Multisets of finite intervals and a universal category of poset representations
Henning Krause, Balduin Stoye

Abstract
. For any finite totally ordered set, the multisets of intervals form an abelian category. Various classes of subcategories admit natural combinatorial descriptions, and counting them yields familiar integer sequences. Surprisingly, in some cases new integer sequences arise. The formulation of this counting problem leads to a universal construction which assigns to any poset a finitely cocomplete additive category; it is abelian when the poset is finite and does not depend on the choice of any ring of coefficients. For a general poset the universal category of representations is abelian if and only if for the lattice of ideals the meet of two compact elements is again compact.

16G20 (primary); 06A11 (secondary)

1.
Introduction

The study of quiver representations involves the analysis of
subcategories that are defined by natural closure operations; see for
instance
[
2
,
5
,
7
,
8
,
11
]
. It is an
obvious task to count them when there are only finitely many. In this
note we provide a systematic account for the quiver of type
A_{n}
with
linear orientation (
n\geq 1
). This may be formulated as a purely
combinatorial problem. To this end we consider for any finite totally
ordered set the category of multisets of intervals
(Definition
2
). This is an abelian category which can
be obtained from a universal construction. In fact, we construct for
any poset
P
a universal functor
P\to\operatorname{rep}P
into a finitely
cocomplete category with a zero object
(Proposition
8
). When
P
is finite the category

\operatorname{rep}P
is actually a
length category
, so an abelian category
such that each object has a finite composition series. We note that
the construction of
\operatorname{rep}P
does not depend on the choice of any ring
of coefficients. Thus
\operatorname{rep}P
may be thought of as a
universal
category of poset representations
. It seems to be an interesting
project to study properties of
P
in terms of
\operatorname{rep}P
. A striking
example is a recent result of Iyama and Marczinzik showing that a
finite lattice
P
is distributive if and only if
\operatorname{rep}P
is the
module category of an Auslander regular algebra
[
6
]
. We end
this note by characterising the posets such that
\operatorname{rep}P
is an
abelian category (Proposition
16
). Along the way we
characterise the posets such that all objects in
\operatorname{rep}P
are either
noetherian or of finite length (Corollary
12
).

2.
Finite intervals

Let
\mathbb{Z}
denote the set of integers and let
\mathbb{F}_{2}
denote the field
with two elements. We consider categories that are

\mathbb{F}_{2}
-linear
, so the morphism sets are
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-linear
spaces and the composition maps are bilinear. An
interval of
length
l
is a pair of integers
a\leq b
such that
b-a=l
.

Definition 1
.

For
n\geq 1
let
\mathcal{I}_{n}
denote the category of
intervals
of type
n
. The objects are given by

\operatorname{Ob}\mathcal{I}_{n}:=\{[a,b]\in\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}\mid 1\leq a\leq b\leq n\}.

For objects

x=[a,b]
and
y=[c,d]
set

\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{I}_{n}}(x,y):=\begin{cases}\mathbb{F}_{2}&\text{if }a\leq c\leq b\leq d,\\
\{0\}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

In case
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{I}_{n}}(x,y)\neq 0
we write
\alpha_{xy}
for the non-zero morphism

x\to y
. The composition of morphisms in
\mathcal{I}_{n}
is determined by two
rules:

\alpha_{yz}\circ\alpha_{xy}=\alpha_{xz}

when

x=[a,b],\,y=[c,d],\,z=[e,f]\qquad\text{and}\qquad a\leq c\leq e\leq b\leq d\leq f.

And
\psi\circ\phi=0
for any other pair of morphisms

\phi\colon x\to y
and
\psi\colon y\to z
.

Next we consider multisets of intervals, and we use the suggestive
notation

\oplus_{i=1}^{r}x_{i}:=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}\}

for a finite multiset of intervals
x_{i}=[a_{i},b_{i}]
.

Definition 2
.

For
n\geq 1
let
\mathcal{A}_{n}
denote the category of
mutisets of
intervals of type
n
. The objects are given by

\operatorname{Ob}\mathcal{A}_{n}:=\{\oplus_{i=1}^{r}x_{i}\mid x_{i}\in\mathcal{I}_{n}\text{ for }1\leq i\leq r\}

and the morphism sets are
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-linear spaces given by

\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{A}_{n}}(\oplus_{i}x_{i},\oplus_{j}y_{j}):=\bigoplus_{i,j}\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{I}_{n}}(x_{i},y_{j}).

The composition of morphisms in
\mathcal{A}_{n}
is given by

(\psi_{jk})\circ(\phi_{ij}):=\left(\sum_{j}\psi_{jk}\circ\phi_{ij}\right)

for
(\phi_{ij})\colon\oplus_{i}x_{i}\to\oplus_{j}y_{j}
and

(\psi_{jk})\colon\oplus_{j}y_{j}\to\oplus_{k}z_{k}
.

Lemma 3
.

The category
\mathcal{A}_{n}
is abelian. The direct sum of objects is given
by the disjoint union, and the full subcategory of indecomposable
objects identifies with
\mathcal{I}_{n}
. Every object admits a finite
composition series, and the composition length of an interval of
length
l
equals
l+1
.

Proof.

We consider the quiver

A_{n}\colon\quad 1\longrightarrow 2\longrightarrow\cdots\longrightarrow n

and write

\operatorname{rep}(A_{n},\mathbb{F}_{2})
for the category of
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-linear

contravariant representations
of
A_{n}
that are finite
dimensional. This is an
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-linear abelian category. Each object
decomposes essentially uniqueley into indecomposable representations.
Up to isomorphism, the indecomposable representations are given by
intervals
x=[a,b]\in\mathcal{I}_{n}
and are of the form

M_{x}\colon\quad 0\longrightarrow\cdots\longrightarrow 0\longrightarrow\mathbb{F}_{2}\xrightarrow{\ 1\ }\cdots\xrightarrow{\ 1\ }\mathbb{F}_{2}\longrightarrow 0\longrightarrow\cdots\longrightarrow 0

such that

M_{x}(i):=\begin{cases}\mathbb{F}_{2}&\text{if }a\leq i\leq b,\\
\{0\}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

The assignment
\oplus_{i}x_{i}\mapsto\bigoplus_{i}M_{x_{i}}
yields an equivalence
\mathcal{A}_{n}\xrightarrow{\raisebox{-1.20552pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle{\sim}$}}\operatorname{rep}(A_{n},\mathbb{F}_{2})
, and from this the assertion of the lemma follows.
∎

3.
Counting subcategories

For
n\geq 1
we consider full subcategories
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{n}
that are
closed under finite direct sums and summands. Such a subcategory is
determined by
\mathcal{A}\cap\mathcal{I}_{n}
. There are several classes of
subcategories which are defined by natural closure
properties. We consider the following operations and combinations thereof:

(Q)

taking quotients,

(S)

taking subobjects,

(C)

taking cokernels,

(K)

taking kernels,

(E)

taking extension.

In each case we compute the number of such
subcategories, depending on
n
. This yields an integer
sequence, which we denote by

\#(X,Y,Z)

when
the relevant operations are (X), (Y), and (Z).
We provide the first six terms and the formula for
computing the
n
-th term in case it is known.

Not all combinations of the above operations need to be considered,
because some operations imply each other and some others are dual to
each other. For instance, any cokernel is a quotient, or a cokernel is
the dual of a kernel. Observe that
\mathcal{A}_{n}
is equivalent to its
opposite category
\mathcal{A}_{n}^{\mathrm{op}}
via the involution of
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}

taking
i
to
n-i+1
.

Remark 4
.

(1) Subcategories are partially ordered by inclusion, and counting
them often amounts to an isomorphism of posets when one identifies
them with some other combinatorial structure. The subcategories
satisfying any choice of the above closure properties are closed under
intersections; so they form a lattice.

(2) Let
\mathbb{F}
be any field. Then one may consider the category

\operatorname{rep}(A_{n},\mathbb{F})
of
\mathbb{F}
-linear representations of the quiver

A_{n}
. It is not difficult to show that the lattice of subcategories
satisfying any choice of the above closure properties does not depend on

\mathbb{F}
. We have chosen
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{F}_{2}
in order to emphasise the
combinatorial nature of the counting problem.

(3) Some choices are more natural than others. For instance, the
finite colimit closed subcategories seem natural, given that
for any ring
A
the category
\operatorname{mod}A
of finitely presented
A
-modules
is the universal finitely cocomplete category over
A
; cf. Proposition
8
.

Serre subcategories (Q,S,E)

Serre subcategories
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{n}
correspond to sets of simple
objects, by taking the simple objects that are contained in
\mathcal{A}
.
There are
n
simple objects, and therefore the subsets form a
Boolean lattice of cardinality given by:

2,4,8,16,32,64,\ldots\qquad 2^{n}

Thick subcategories (C,K,E)

The thick subcategories are precisely the exact abelian and extension
closed subcategories. Each thick subcategory of
\mathcal{A}_{n}
is generated
by an exceptional sequence, and from this fact it follows that the lattice of thick
subcategories identifies with the lattice of non-crossing partitions

[
5
]
. Thus the number is given by the
(n+1)
-th Catalan
number:

2,5,14,42,132,429,\ldots\qquad\frac{1}{n+2}\binom{2n+2}{n+1}

Quotient and extension closed subcategories (Q,E)

Subcategories that are closed under quotients and extensions are
precisely the torsion classes that correspond to torsion pairs. The
lattice of such subcategories identifies with the Tamari lattice

[
11
]
. Thus the number is given by the
(n+1)
-th Catalan
number:

2,5,14,42,132,429,\ldots\qquad\frac{1}{n+2}\binom{2n+2}{n+1}

Of particular interest are the tilting torsion classes. These are
torsion classes that are generated by a tilting object, and this sets
up a bijection with the basic tilting objects, which are counted by
the
n
-th Catalan number
[
4
]
and yield another Tamari
lattice
[
2
]
.

Quotient and subobject closed subcategories (Q,S)

The quotient and subobject closed subcategories
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{n}
correspond
to ideals of the path algebra
\mathbb{F}_{2}A_{n}
, by taking the ideal of
elements that annihilate all objects of
\mathcal{A}
. These ideals are given by
sequences of integers
a_{1}\leq a_{2}\leq\cdots\leq a_{n}
such that

0\leq a_{i}\leq i
for each
i
, and they correspond to Dyck paths with
their lattice structure. Thus the number is given by the
(n+1)
-th
Catalan number:

2,5,14,42,132,429,\ldots\qquad\frac{1}{n+2}\binom{2n+2}{n+1}

Quotient closed subcategories (Q)

The quotient closed subcategories of
\mathcal{A}_{n}
correspond to the elements
of the symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}
with the sorting order

[
1
,
8
]
. In fact, the lattice equals the product of

n+1
chains of length
1,2,\ldots,n+1
; see also
[
3
]

where such subcategories are called pretorsion classes. Thus the
number is given by:

2,6,24,120,720,5040,\ldots\qquad(n+1)!

Extension closed subcategories (E)

The extension closed subcategories are precisely the fully exact and
idempotent complete subcategories. For the number of these
subcategories no formula is known
[
10
]
. The first terms of
the sequence are:

2,7,34,199,1308,9300,\ldots

In order to indicate the nature of the counting problem, let us
provide a criterion for a subcategory
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{n}
to be
extension closed. An elementary calculation shows that it suffices to
check the extensions of indecomposable objects. Fix a pair of
intervals
x=[a,b]
and
x^{\prime}=[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]
.

Lemma 5
.

There is a non-zero element
\eta\in\operatorname{Ext}_{\mathcal{A}_{n}}^{1}(x^{\prime},x)
if and
only if
a\leq a^{\prime}
,
b\leq b^{\prime}
, and
a^{\prime}\leq b+1
. In this case
\eta
is given by
an exact sequence

0\longrightarrow x\longrightarrow y\oplus y^{\prime}\longrightarrow x^{\prime}\longrightarrow 0

where
y=[a,b^{\prime}]
and
y^{\prime}=[a^{\prime},b]
, with convention
[b+1,b]=0
.∎

Thus it suffices to check that
y,y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}
provided that

x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}
.

Exact abelian subcategories (C,K)

The exact abelian subcategories are precisely the subcategories
that are closed under finite limits and colimits. For the number of
these subcategories no formula is known
[
10
]
. The first
terms of the sequence are:

2,6,22,86,345,1411,\ldots

Counting the exact abelian subcategories amounts to counting
subcategories that are closed under taking kernels and cokernels. We
treat both cases separately, and because of the duality it suffices to
consider subcategories that are closed under cokernels.

Finite colimit closed subcategories (C)

These are the additive subcategories closed under cokernels, because
any finite colimit is given by the cokernel of a morphism between
finite coproducts. For the number of these subcategories no formula is
known
[
10
]
. The first terms of the sequence are:

2,7,37,261,2284,23777,\ldots

The counting problem can be translated into a combinatorial
problem. This is based on the following criterion for a subcategory

\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{n}
to be closed under taking cokernels.

Lemma 6
.

For
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{n}
to be closed under taking cokernels, it
suffices to be closed under taking cokernels of morphisms

\phi\colon x\to y
, where
x
is indecomposable and
y
is either
indecomposable or a direct sum of two indecomposable objects. Let

x=[a,b]
,
y=[c,d]
or
y=[c,d]\oplus[c^{\prime},d^{\prime}]
. Assuming that each
summand of
\phi
is non-zero, the cokernel equals
[b+1,d]
in the
first case, and

[b+1,\operatorname{min}(d,d^{\prime})]\oplus[\max(c,c^{\prime}),\max(d,d^{\prime})]

in the second case, with convention
[b+1,b]=0
.

Proof.

First, note that we can indeed assume that
x
is indecomposable,
say
x=[a,b]
. We can also assume that for
y=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{r}y_{i}

with
y_{i}=[c_{i},d_{i}]
the morphisms
x\to y_{i}
are non-zero. Next, we
look at the case where
r=2
and
d_{1}\leq d_{2},c_{1}\leq c_{2}
. It is
convenient to work in
\operatorname{rep}(A_{n},\mathbb{F}_{2})
, using the equivalence

\mathcal{A}_{n}\xrightarrow{\raisebox{-1.20552pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle{\sim}$}}\operatorname{rep}(A_{n},\mathbb{F}_{2})
that identifies
x
with
M_{x}
. Let us
decompose
M_{y}
as follows. We choose elements
e_{i}(j)
such that

M_{y_{i}}(j)=\mathbb{F}_{2}e_{i}(j)
for
i=1,2
and
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Set

v_{1}(j):=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }1\leq j\leq c_{1}-1\text{ or }d_{1}+1\leq j\leq n,\\
e_{1}(j)&\text{if }c_{1}\leq j\leq c_{2}-1,\\
e_{1}(j)+e_{2}(j)&\text{if }c_{2}\leq j\leq d_{1},\\
\end{cases}

and

v_{2}(j):=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }1\leq j\leq c_{2}-1\text{ or }d_{2}+1\leq j\leq n,\\
e_{2}(j)&\text{if }c_{2}\leq j\leq d_{2}.\\
\end{cases}

Then

M_{y}=(\mathbb{F}_{2}v_{1}(n)\to\cdots\to\mathbb{F}_{2}v_{1}(1))\oplus(\mathbb{F}_{2}v_{2}(n)\to\cdots\to\mathbb{F}_{2}v_{2}(1))

and the image of
\phi
lies in
the first of the above summands. Thus the cokernel is the direct sum
of
M_{y_{2}}
and the cokernel of
M_{x}\to M_{y_{1}}
. Now consider the
general case
r\geq 2
. We may arrange the summands such that

d_{1}\leq\cdots\leq d_{r}
and by the above argument for
r=2
we can
also assume that
c_{1}\geq\cdots\geq c_{r}
. In this case the

v_{i}(j)=\sum_{k=i}^{r}e_{k}(j)
yield a basis of the cokernel and therefore

\operatorname{Coker}\phi=[b+1,d_{1}]\oplus\left(\bigoplus_{i=2}^{r}[c_{i-1},d_{i}]\right).

It is easily checked that each indecomposable direct summand also
arises from the cokernel of
a morphism of the form
x\to y_{1}\oplus y_{2}
, which proves the lemma.
∎

Additive subcategories

The additive subcategories correspond to sets of indecomposable
objects. The cardinality of
\mathcal{I}_{n}
equals
\frac{1}{2}(n^{2}+n)
, and therefore the subsets of

\mathcal{I}_{n}
form a Boolean lattice of cardinality given by:

2,8,64,1024,32768,1146419,\ldots\qquad 2^{\frac{1}{2}(n^{2}+n)}

4.
A universal category of poset representations

Let
P=(P,\leq)
be a poset. We propose a canonical construction which
embeds
P
into a finitely cocomplete additive category; it is abelian
when
P
is finite. Recall that a category is
finitely
cocomplete
if it admits finite colimits. It is convenient to view

P
as a category: the objects are the elements of
P
and there is a
unique morphism
x\to y
if and only if
x\leq y
.

A universal category of representations

We begin by adjoining to
P
a distinguished
zero object
, so an
object which is both initial and terminal. The new category
P_{*}
is
given by

\operatorname{Ob}P_{*}:=\operatorname{Ob}P\sqcup\{*\}

and

\operatorname{Hom}_{P_{*}}(x,y):=\begin{cases}\mathbb{F}_{2}&\text{if }x\leq y\text{ in }P,\\
\{0\}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Taking for
x\leq y
the unique morphism
x\to y
in
P
to the
non-zero morphism in
P_{*}
yields a canonical functor
P\to P_{*}
.

Lemma 7
.

The category
P_{*}
is
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-linear and
*
is the unique and
therefore distinguished zero
object. Any functor
P\to\mathcal{C}
to a category with a distinguished zero
object
extends uniquely to a functor
P_{*}\to\mathcal{C}
that preserves distinguished zero
objects.

Proof.

The first assertion is clear. We may view
P
as a subcategory of

P_{*}
and any morphism not in
P
is a zero morphism. Thus a
functor
P\to\mathcal{C}
extends uniquely to
P_{*}
by sending zero morphisms
to zero morphisms.
∎

The lemma implies that the category
P_{*}
is
preadditive
, so
enriched over the category
\mathrm{Ab}
of abelian groups. For such
categories we consider functors that are additive. Recall that a
functor
F
is
additive
if for any pair of objects
x,y
the
induced map

\operatorname{Hom}(x,y)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Hom}(F(x),F(y))

is a group homomorphism.

Let
\operatorname{Mod}P_{*}
denote the category of additive functors

P_{*}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathrm{Ab}
into the category of abelian groups. For a pair of
objects
F,G
in
\operatorname{Mod}P_{*}
we write
\operatorname{Hom}_{P_{*}}(F,G)
for the set of
morphisms
F\to G
which are given by the natural transformations.
The Yoneda embedding

P_{*}\longrightarrow\operatorname{Mod}P_{*},\qquad x\mapsto h_{x}:=\operatorname{Hom}_{P_{*}}(-,x)

provides
the
free cocompletion
of
P_{*}
, so the universal functor
from
P_{*}
to a cocomplete category. Corestricting to the full
subcategory of finitely presented functors yields the
finite
cocompletion

P_{*}\longrightarrow\operatorname{mod}P_{*}.

Thus
\operatorname{mod}P_{*}
is obtained from
P_{*}
by adding finite coproducts
and cokernels, keeping in mind that any finite colimit in an additive
category can be written as the cokernel of a morphism between finite
coproducts.

The above constructions do not depend on the choice of any ring of
coefficients, even though
\mathbb{F}_{2}
appears. We write

\operatorname{rep}P:=\operatorname{mod}P_{*}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\operatorname{Rep}P:=\operatorname{Mod}P_{*}

and one may
think of these as
universal categories of poset
representations
.

Assume that
P
is finite. Then we consider the
incidence
algebra

\mathbb{F}_{2}P:=\bigoplus_{x,y\in P}\operatorname{Hom}_{P_{*}}(x,y)

with coefficients in

\mathbb{F}_{2}
and multiplication induced by the composition of morphisms in

P_{*}
. We write
\operatorname{mod}\mathbb{F}_{2}P
for its category of finitely
presented modules.

Proposition 8
.

For a poset
P
consider the universal functor
P\to\operatorname{rep}P

into a finitely cocomplete category with a zero object. Then

\operatorname{rep}P
is an
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-linear additive category with finite dimensional
morphism spaces. When
P
is finite there is a canonical
equivalence
\operatorname{rep}P\xrightarrow{\raisebox{-1.20552pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle{\sim}$}}\operatorname{mod}\mathbb{F}_{2}P
and
\operatorname{rep}P
is abelian.

Proof.

From the construction it follows that
\operatorname{rep}P
is an
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-linear
additive category with finite dimensional morphism spaces. Now
assume that
P
is finite. For
F
in
\operatorname{rep}P
there is a canonical
right action of
\mathbb{F}_{2}P
on
\bigoplus_{x\in P}F(x)
. This yields
a functor
\operatorname{rep}P\to\operatorname{mod}\mathbb{F}_{2}P
. Now fix a (multiplicative) basis of
\mathbb{F}_{2}P

consisting of elements
\alpha_{xy}\in\operatorname{Hom}_{P_{*}}(x,y)
. Taking an

\mathbb{F}_{2}P
-module
M
to the representation of
P
given
by

x\mapsto M(x):=M\alpha_{xx}

and for
x\leq y
the linear map

M(\alpha_{xy})\colon M(y)\longrightarrow M(x)

given by right multiplication with
\alpha_{xy}
yields a
quasi-inverse. It remains to note that
\operatorname{rep}P
is abelian since the
algebra
\mathbb{F}_{2}P
is finite dimensional.
∎

We continue with further remarks, in particular to clarify the
universality of the functor
P\to\operatorname{rep}P
.

Remark 9
.

(1) The assignment
P\mapsto\operatorname{rep}P
is functorial. Thus any poset morphism

P\to Q
induces a right exact functor
\operatorname{rep}P\to\operatorname{rep}Q
. Also,
for any finitely cocomplete category
\mathcal{C}
with a zero object, the induced functor

\operatorname{Fun}(\operatorname{rep}P,\mathcal{C})\longrightarrow\operatorname{Fun}(P,\mathcal{C})

yields an equivalence when restricted to the full subcategory of
functors that preserve finite colimits and zero objects.

(2) We are not claiming that our universal construction lands in
finitely cocomplete additive categories. This may be confusing, as

\operatorname{rep}P
is an additive category, but a functor between additive
categories that preserves finite colimits and zero objects need not be
additive. An example arises for any field
\mathbb{F}
of characteristic
different from
2
by extending the obvious map
\mathbb{F}_{2}\to\mathbb{F}
to
finite dimensional vector spaces.

(3) Given posets
P
and
Q
, there is an isomorphism
P\xrightarrow{\raisebox{-1.20552pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle{\sim}$}}Q
if and
only if there is an equivalence
\operatorname{rep}P\xrightarrow{\raisebox{-1.20552pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle{\sim}$}}\operatorname{rep}Q
. One direction is
clear, and to see that
\operatorname{rep}P
determines
P
one notes that

P\to\operatorname{rep}P
yields a bijection between the elements of
P
and the
isoclasses of indecomposable projective objects in
\operatorname{rep}P
. Moreover,

x\leq y
in
P
if and only if
\operatorname{Hom}_{P_{*}}(h_{x},h_{y})\neq 0
.

Example 10
.

For
n\geq 1
consider
P_{n}=\{1,\ldots,n\}
with the natural
ordering. The assignment
i\mapsto[1,i]
for
1\leq i\leq n
yields a
fully faithful functor
(P_{n})_{*}\to\mathcal{A}_{n}
which induces an equivalence

\operatorname{rep}P_{n}\xrightarrow{\raisebox{-1.20552pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle{\sim}$}}\mathcal{A}_{n}
. A quasi-inverse maps an interval
[a,b]
to the
quotient
h_{b}/h_{a-1}
.

Linear representations

Let
\mathbb{K}
be a commutative ring and write
\operatorname{Mod}\mathbb{K}
for the category
of
\mathbb{K}
-modules. The forgetful functor
\operatorname{Mod}\mathbb{K}\to\mathrm{Set}
admits a
left adjoint that takes a set
X
to the free
\mathbb{K}
-module
\mathbb{K}X

with basis
X
. For any category
\mathcal{C}
let
\mathbb{K}\mathcal{C}

denote its
\mathbb{K}
-linearisation
which is obtained by setting

\operatorname{Ob}\mathbb{K}\mathcal{C}:=\operatorname{Ob}\mathcal{C},

and for any pair of objects
x,y

\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{K}\mathcal{C}}(x,y):=\mathbb{K}\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(x,y).

The
\mathbb{K}
-linear representations
of
\mathcal{C}
are by definition
functors
\mathcal{C}\to\operatorname{Mod}\mathbb{K}
, and they identify with additive
functors
\mathbb{K}\mathcal{C}\to\mathrm{Ab}
.

For a poset
P
we obtain categories of
contravariant
\mathbb{K}
-linear representations

by setting

\operatorname{rep}(P,\mathbb{K}):=\operatorname{mod}\mathbb{K}P\qquad\text{and}\qquad\operatorname{Rep}(P,\mathbb{K}):=\operatorname{Mod}\mathbb{K}P.

For
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{F}_{2}
the inclusion
\mathbb{F}_{2}P\to P_{*}
yields identifications

\operatorname{rep}P=\operatorname{rep}(P,\mathbb{F}_{2})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\operatorname{Rep}P=\operatorname{Rep}(P,\mathbb{F}_{2}).

There is a tensor product

\operatorname{Rep}(P,\mathbb{K})\times\operatorname{Mod}\mathbb{K}\longrightarrow\operatorname{Rep}(P,\mathbb{K})

which is defined as follows. For
F\in\operatorname{Rep}(P,\mathbb{K})
and
M\in\operatorname{Mod}\mathbb{K}

one defines
F\otimes_{\mathbb{K}}M
by setting for
x\in P

(F\otimes_{\mathbb{K}}M)(x):=F(x)\otimes_{\mathbb{K}}M.

For
G\in\operatorname{Rep}(P,\mathbb{K})
there is a natural isomorphism

(4.1)

\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{K}P}(F\otimes_{\mathbb{K}}M,G)\cong\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{K}}(M,\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{K}P}(F,G)).

This is clear when
M=\mathbb{K}
and the
general case follows by taking a free presentation of
M
.

5.
Coherent posets

Let
P=(P,\leq)
be a poset and fix a field
\mathbb{F}
. In this section we
add some further aspects concerning the interplay between
P
and its
category of
\mathbb{F}
-linear representations
\operatorname{rep}(P,\mathbb{F})
. The
universal category
\operatorname{rep}P
arises as a special case when

\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{F}_{2}
. Specifically, we look at the lattice of subobjects for
objects in
\operatorname{rep}(P,\mathbb{F})
. This yields a description of the posets
such that
\operatorname{rep}(P,\mathbb{F})
is abelian. It is interesting to note that
these results do not depend on the field
\mathbb{F}
.

Subobjects

An
ideal
of
P
is a subset
I\subseteq P
that is downward
closed, so
x\leq y
in
P
and
y\in I
implies
x\in I
. The ideals
of
P
are partially ordered by inclusion and form a complete
lattice. Note that the join is given by taking the union of subsets,
and the meet is given by intersections. Thus the lattice of ideals is distributive.

Given
x\in P
, we consider the

principal ideal

\downarrow\!x:=\{y\in P\mid y\leq x\}

and compare this with the representable functor

h_{x}:=\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{F}P}(-,x)

in
\operatorname{Rep}(P,\mathbb{F})
. For
y\in P
we have

h_{y}\subseteq h_{x}\quad\iff\quad\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{F}P}(h_{y},h_{x})\neq 0\quad\iff\quad y\leq x.

Proposition 11
.

The assignments

\downarrow\!x\supseteq I\mapsto\sum_{y\in I}h_{y}\subseteq h_{x}\qquad\text{and}\qquad h_{x}\supseteq F\mapsto\{y\in P\mid F(y)\neq 0\}\subseteq{\downarrow\!x}

yield mutually inverse and order preserving bijections between

(1)

the lattice of ideals of

\downarrow\!x
, and

(2)

the lattice of additive subfunctors of
h_{x}
.

In particular, both lattices are distributive.

Proof.

The maps are well defined. Then the assertion follows from the adjunctions

F\subseteq\sum_{y\in I}h_{y}\quad\iff\quad\{y\in P\mid F(y)\neq 0\}\subseteq I

and

\sum_{y\in I}h_{y}\subseteq F\quad\iff\quad I\subseteq\{y\in P\mid F(y)\neq 0\}.\qed

The proposition shows that chain conditions on subobjects in
\operatorname{rep}(P,\mathbb{F})

translate into chain conditions on the lattice of ideals of
P
.

Corollary 12
.

For a poset
P
and a field
\mathbb{F}
the following holds.

(1)

The category
\operatorname{rep}(P,\mathbb{F})
is abelian and all objects are of finite length if
and only if for each
x\in P
the set
\downarrow\!x
is finite.

(2)

The category
\operatorname{rep}(P,\mathbb{F})
is abelian and all objects are noetherian if
and only if for each
x\in P
all ascending chains of ideals of
\downarrow\!x
stabilise.

Proof.

For any ring there are well known characterisations in terms of
chain conditions on right ideals such that the category
of finitely presented right modules is an abelian
category consisting either of finite length or of noetherian
objects. The proof for module categories carries over to our
setting, by applying
Proposition
11
. For (1) one uses in addition that a distributive
lattice satisfies both chain conditions if and only if it is finite.
∎

Remark 13
.

A subrepresentation of
F\in\operatorname{rep}(P,\mathbb{F})
need not be finitely
presented. Thus the lattices of subobjects in
\operatorname{rep}(P,\mathbb{F})
and in

\operatorname{Rep}(P,\mathbb{F})
do not agree in general. In fact, they agree
precisely in the noetherian case, so when for each
x\in P
all ascending
chains of ideals of
\downarrow\!x
stabilise.

Coherent posets

For rings there is the following hierarchy:

\text{right artinian}\quad\implies\quad\text{right
noetherian}\quad\implies\quad\text{right coherent}.

The
condition ‘right coherent’ means that the category of finitely
presented right modules is abelian. In our context we wish to characterise
the posets such that
\operatorname{rep}(P,\mathbb{F})
is abelian. To this end we need to
recall some finiteness conditions.

An element
x
in a lattice is
compact

if
x\leq\bigvee_{\alpha\in A}x_{\alpha}
implies
x\leq\bigvee_{\alpha\in B}x_{\alpha}
for
some finite subset
B\subseteq A
.

We continue with conditions on objects in a Grothendieck category and
refer to
[
9
]
for details. An object
X
is
finitely
generated
if
X=\sum_{\alpha\in A}X_{\alpha}
for any family of subobjects

X_{\alpha}\subseteq X
implies
X=\sum_{\alpha\in B}X_{\alpha}
for some finite
subset
B\subseteq A
. A finitely generated object
X
is

finitely presented
if for any epimorphism
X^{\prime}\to X
from a
finitely generated object
X^{\prime}
the kernel is finitely generated. A
finitely presented object is
coherent
if every finitely
generated subobject is finitely presented. For example, any noetherian
object is coherent because all subobjects are finitely generated.

Lemma 14
.

For a Grothendieck category with a generating set of objects that are
finitely generated and projective, the following are equivalent.

(1)

All finitely generated projective objects are coherent.

(2)

The finitely presented objects form an abelian category.

Proof.

Adapt the proof for the category of modules over a ring.
∎

We propose the following definition and the subsequent result
justifies the terminology.

Definition 15
.

A poset
P
is called
coherent
if for every diagram
y\leq x\geq y^{\prime}
there are finitely many diagrams
y\geq z_{\alpha}\leq y^{\prime}
such that for each
diagram
y\geq z\leq y^{\prime}
there is an index
\alpha
with
z\leq z_{\alpha}
.

Proposition 16
.

For a poset
P
the following are equivalent.

(1)

The poset
P
is coherent.

(2)

For each
x\in P
the compact elements in
\downarrow\!x
are
closed under finite meets.

(3)

For each
x\in P
the object
h_{x}\in\operatorname{Rep}(P,\mathbb{F})
is coherent.

(4)

The category
\operatorname{rep}(P,\mathbb{F})
is abelian.

Proof.

(1)
\Leftrightarrow
(2): The ideals of a poset form a distributive
lattice, and an ideal
I
is compact if and only if

I=\bigcup_{\alpha}\downarrow\!x_{\alpha}
for some finite set of elements
x_{\alpha}
.
Thus to check condition (2) it suffices to show that
\downarrow\!y\cap{\downarrow\!y^{\prime}}
is compact for each pair
y,y^{\prime}\leq x
, and
this is precisely condition (1).

(2)
\Rightarrow
(3): For a morphism

\phi\colon\bigoplus_{\alpha\in A}h_{y_{\alpha}}\to h_{x}
given by a finite set
of elements
y_{\alpha}\in{\downarrow\!x}
we need to show that its
kernel is finitely generated. For
z\in P
set

V_{z}:=\operatorname{Ker}\left(\bigoplus_{\alpha\in A}h_{y_{\alpha}}(z)\to h_{x}(z)\right).

The inclusion

V_{z}\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{\alpha\in A}h_{y_{\alpha}}(z)=\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{F}P}(h_{z},\bigoplus_{\alpha\in A}h_{y_{\alpha}})

corresponds via (
4.1
)
to a morphism

\psi_{z}\colon h_{z}\otimes_{\mathbb{F}}V_{z}\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{\alpha\in A}h_{y_{\alpha}}.

Using the adjunction (
4.1
) it follows that

\phi\psi_{z}=0
and any morphism

\psi\colon h_{z}\to\bigoplus_{\alpha\in A}h_{y_{\alpha}}
satisfying

\phi\psi=0
factors through
\psi_{z}
.

Using the coherence of
P
there is for each subset
B\subseteq A
a
finite subset
B^{\prime}\subseteq P
satisfying

\bigcap_{\alpha\in B}\downarrow\!y_{\alpha}=\bigcup_{z\in B^{\prime}}\downarrow\!z.

This yields a morphism

\bigoplus_{B\subseteq A}\bigoplus_{z\in B^{\prime}}h_{z}\otimes_{\mathbb{F}}V_{z}\xrightarrow{\,(\psi_{z})\,}\bigoplus_{\alpha\in A}h_{y_{\alpha}},

where
B\subseteq A
runs through all subsets, and its
image equals
\operatorname{Ker}\phi
by construction. In fact, for any

\psi\colon h_{z}\to\operatorname{Ker}\phi
choose
B=\{\alpha\in A\mid\psi_{\alpha}\neq 0\}
. Then

z\leq z^{\prime}
for some
z^{\prime}\in B^{\prime}
and
\psi
factors through
\psi_{z^{\prime}}
.

(3)
\Rightarrow
(1): A diagram
y\leq x\geq y^{\prime}
yields a morphism
h_{y}\oplus h_{y^{\prime}}\to h_{x}
, and its kernel is
given by diagrams
y\geq z\leq y^{\prime}
. Suppose the kernel is
a quotient of
\bigoplus_{\alpha}h_{z_{\alpha}}
for some finite set of elements

z_{\alpha}\in\downarrow\!x
. This yields finitely many diagrams
y\geq z_{\alpha}\leq y^{\prime}
such that for each
diagram
y\geq z\leq y^{\prime}
there is an index
\alpha
with
z\leq z_{\alpha}
.

(3)
\Leftrightarrow
(4): This follows from
Lemma
14
, once we observe that coherent objects are
closed under finite direct sums and summands.
∎

Corollary 17
.

A poset is coherent if and only if for the lattice of ideals the
meet of two compact elements is again compact.∎
