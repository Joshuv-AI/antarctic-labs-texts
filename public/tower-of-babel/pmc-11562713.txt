Adaptable Plasmonic Membrane Sensors for Fast and Reliable Detection of Trace Low-Micrometer Microplastics in Lake Water
Authors: Ziyan Wu, Sarah E Janssen, Michael T Tate, Haoran Wei, Mohan Qin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn freshwater environments, low-micrometer microplastics (LMMPs) have captured significant attention due to their prevalence and toxicity. Yet, rapid detection of LMMPs (1–10 μm) at the single-particle level within complex freshwater matrices remains a hurdle. We developed an adaptable plasmonic membrane sensor for fast detection of individual LMMPs in eutrophic lake waters. The plasmonic membrane sensor functions both as a membrane filter and as a sensor for LMMP collection and analysis. Among the four types of membrane sensors, polycarbonate track-etch (PCTE) membrane sensors exhibit superior imaging quality for LMMPs due to their flat and homogeneous surfaces. Besides the significantly improved imaging contrast and reduced background interferences, the Raman intensity of LMMPs is enhanced by 48% ± 25% on PCTE membrane sensors compared to unmodified membranes. The increased Raman intensities of a chemical probe with an increasing gold layer thickness and a decreasing membrane pore size suggest a surface-enhanced Raman scattering effect from the membrane sensors. The membrane sensors achieve a detection limit of 1 μg/L and an ultrafast scanning time of 0.01 s for individual LMMPs across natural eutrophic lake water. The developed membrane sensors offer an adaptable tool for the swift and reliable detection of individual LMMPs in complex environmental matrices.Keywords: low-micrometer microplastics, plasmonic membrane, membrane sensor, Raman microspectroscopy, freshwater, dark-field imaging

Introduction

Plastic pollution has become a global concern in the aquatic environment in recent decades.1,2 More than 19 million tons of plastic waste enters global aquatic ecosystems annually,1 with 109 million tons of plastic debris accumulated in freshwater systems as of 2019.3 The degradation of plastic debris in freshwater systems may generate microplastics (MPs) (<5 mm), raising substantial environmental concerns due to their pervasive occurrence and potential toxicity to human health.4,5 Recently, increased attention has been directed to small MPs, particularly low-micrometer MPs (LMMPs) in the size range of 1–10 μm, which have been identified as the predominant form of MPs in treated drinking water and may negatively affect the growth and reproductive rates of aquatic species.6,7 Previous studies have indicated that LMMPs can penetrate cellular membranes, resulting in serious potential health risks.8,9 Despite their potential toxicity and prevalence in freshwater ecosystems, it remains challenging to reliably detect LMMPs due to their small sizes and interferences from naturally occurring particulate matter of similar dimensions.10

The necessity of developing reliable and fast tools to detect LMMPs in freshwater samples is increasingly recognized. Various techniques, such as Fourier transform infrared microspectroscopy (μ-FTIR), Raman related spectroscopic techniques including Raman microspectroscopy (μ-Raman) and surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS), and pyrolysis-gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (Py-GC-MS), have been commonly employed for LMMP detection.11−16 Particularly, μ-Raman has exhibited multiple advantages over the others for LMMP quantification and characterization in both deionized (DI) water and environmental samples, including simple sample preparation, high spatial resolution, and high sensitivity.14,17,18 To detect LMMPs at environmentally relevant levels, μ-Raman based techniques often need a membrane filtration process to separate and concentrate the targeted LMMPs in water samples.17,19,20 To minimize the complexity of the sample pretreatment processes and mitigate potential contamination, it would be ideal to directly visualize and characterize LMMPs on membrane surfaces. This is, unfortunately, often limited by the interferences from both the membranes underneath and the coexisting naturally occurring particulate matter.

Multifunctional membranes that are specifically designed to synergistically separate and detect LMMPs in freshwater samples are highly desirable.21 Despite previous efforts to improve the membrane filters for vibrational spectroscopic imaging of MPs,22,23 significant challenges still hinder their practical applications. A silicon filter with 10 μm holes was prepared using photolithography and applied to analyze high-density polyethylene, polypropylene, polyethylene terephthalate, and polybutylene terephthalate MP films.22 Due to its mid-infrared light transparency, this innovative filter enables the FTIR imaging of MPs using the transmission mode over a broad window of 600–4000 cm–1, which is not achievable with conventional polymeric and Anodisc filters. However, the fabrication of this filter involves complex processes, including expensive reactive ion etching, and its potential for LMMP analysis has not been explored. Another technique employed a nanofiltration membrane, which was prepared by syringe filtration of silver nanowires (AgNWs) onto filter paper.23 This membrane filter efficiently retained and detected polystyrene (PS) micro- and nanoplastics down to 50 nm, leveraging its small pore size and surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS) effect.23 However, the membrane requires the complex synthesis of the AgNWs and a subsequent ligand-exchange step to reduce interferences from the polyvinylpyrrolidone coatings on AgNW surfaces during micro- and nanoplastic analysis. Additionally, the nonadjustable pore size of these membrane filters precludes the fractionation of MPs with varying sizes, which is essential for reliable Raman spectroscopic imaging.14 Thus, developing membrane filters with simple fabrication, tunable pore sizes, rapid detection capabilities, and robust anti-interference properties for analyzing LMMPs in environmentally relevant levels within freshwater samples remains a significant challenge.

In this study, we developed adaptable plasmonic membranes that function both as efficient filters for LMMP fractionation and as sensors for fast and accurate LMMP detection. These membrane sensors were created by sputtering a gold layer onto a polymeric membrane substrate. This gold coating significantly mitigates interference from the underlying membrane with target LMMP analysis via enhancing the dark-field imaging contrast and minimizing Raman scatterings from the membranes. In addition, the pore rims on the gold-coated polymeric membranes support localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR), thereby increasing the Raman intensities of the targeted LMMPs. To verify the adaptability of this method and assess the impact of membrane properties on sensor performance, we used four types of polymeric membrane substrates, i.e., cellulose acetate (CA), polycarbonate track-etch (PCTE), poly(ether sulfone) (PES), and polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes, with different pore sizes. PS microspheres were used as model LMMPs to evaluate the detection accuracy and operational robustness and to optimize the membrane sensors for LMMP detection. We also investigated the fundamental mechanisms governing the performance of the membrane sensors for LMMP analysis, with a particular focus on the SERS effect. Finally, we validated the improved performance of the optimized membrane sensors in analyzing the LMMP in natural waters collected from a eutrophic lake. Advanced imaging procedures and data processing algorithms were developed to minimize interference from lake water matrices, which facilitated LMMP analysis at environmentally relevant concentrations, down to 1 part per billion (ppb). The development and optimization of methods for the detection of LMMPs in natural waters are critical steps in further assessing the distribution and risk associated with these plastics in freshwater environments.

Methods and Materials

Materials

PCTE (pore size: 10, 1, 0.4, 0.2, and 0.1 μm) and CA membranes (pore size: 0.2 μm) with a diameter of 47 mm were purchased from Sterlitech (Auburn, WA). PES membranes (pore size: 0.2 μm) with a diameter of 47 mm were purchased from Millipore (Rockville, MD). PVDF membranes (pore size: 0.45 μm) with a diameter of 25 mm were purchased from Millipore (Rockville, MD). Two suspensions of PS microspheres with average sizes of 1.5 and 8 μm were purchased from Phosphorex (Hopkinton, MA). PS microspheres were selected as model LMMPs to evaluate and validate the performance of the developed membrane sensors due to their well-defined morphology and size distribution, commercial availability, and ubiquitous presence in freshwater environments.14,24−28 4-Mercaptobenzoic acid (4-MBA) was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA).

Membrane Sensor Fabrication and Characterization

Membrane sensors were fabricated by sputter coating gold onto various polymeric membrane substrates. Four types of polymeric membranes (i.e., PCTE, CA, PES, and PVDF) were used as the substrates. The sputter coating duration and the current for the membrane sensors was typically set at 30 s and 40 mA, respectively. To investigate the influence of sputter coating duration on both the chemical sensing and imaging performance of the membrane sensor, 0.4 μm PCTE membranes were sputter-coated for varying durations of 5, 10, 15, 20, 30, and 40 s to fabricate PCTE membrane sensors with gold layers of different thicknesses. A scanning electron microscope (Zeiss GeminiSEM 450, Zeiss, Germany) and an optical microscope equipped with a confocal Raman spectrometer (Horiba XploRA PLUS) were used to characterize the surface morphology of both gold-coated and pristine membranes by capturing and comparing images acquired at various magnification levels. The hydrophilicity of the membrane surfaces was assessed by measuring the contact angles of water droplets on both gold-coated and unmodified PCTE membranes using an optical contact angle goniometer (OCA 15 plus, Dataphysics, NC). The water flux of both the 0.4 μm PCTE membrane sensor and the 0.4 μm pristine PCTE membrane was measured by calculating the flow rate of 100 mL of DI water through the membrane sensors and unmodified membranes.

Verification of the SERS Effect from Gold-Coated Membranes

To investigate the SERS effect of PCTE membrane sensors with varying pore sizes (i.e., 10, 1, 0.4, 0.2, and 0.1 μm) and different sputter coating times (i.e., 0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 30, and 40 s), each membrane was immersed in 20 mL of 4-MBA solution (50 μM) for 24 h, followed by air drying and Raman spectrum collection. For each membrane sample, Raman spectra from 48 different locations across the sample surface were acquired, averaged, and plotted as the average Raman spectra of 4-MBA. Raman spectra were acquired under 532 nm laser excitation. The acquisition time and accumulation for each Raman spectrum were 0.1 and 2, respectively. To quantify the SERS effect, the intensity of the 4-MBA Raman band at 1065 cm–1 was normalized by the Raman band of the PCTE membrane at 876 cm–1. Unmodified PCTE membranes with different pore sizes (i.e., 1, 0.4, 0.2, and 0.1 μm) were also tested by using the same procedures to serve as negative controls for the SERS effect.

Membrane Filtration of LMMPs

Standard suspensions containing single-sized LMMPs of 8 and 1.5 μm were prepared by diluting purchased PS suspensions with DI water. A 100 mL aliquot of PS suspensions at concentrations of 10 and 0.2 mg/L was filtered through either a membrane sensor or an unmodified membrane using a vacuum filtration system consisting of a glass filter holder, a glass flask, and the corresponding membrane. Details of the filtration setup were documented in a previous study.14 CA, PES, and PVDF membrane sensors, along with the unmodified membranes, were used to capture 1.5 μm PS LMMPs from the water samples. PCTE membrane sensors and unmodified membranes were used to collect both 8 and 1.5 μm LMMPs. After filtration, the membrane samples with adhered PS LMMPs were air-dried for 24 h prior to Raman spectroscopic imaging. All membrane sensors and unmodified membranes were thoroughly washed before use to avoid potential contamination.29

Dark-Field and Raman Spectroscopic Imaging of LMMPs

After filtration, membrane filters retaining LMMPs were analyzed using a confocal Raman microscope. For each membrane sample, regular optical (bright-field) images and dark-field images were captured at five different locations on the membrane surface using a 100× objective. At each location, the microscope focus was carefully adjusted to obtain optimal imaging quality of LMMPs (Figure S1). Both 8 and 1.5 μm PS particles on the PCTE membrane sensors and unmodified membranes were characterized through single-point and area-scanning Raman spectroscopic analysis. For single-point analysis, individual Raman spectra of 10 particles were recorded and averaged. The averaged Raman spectra, along with the upper and lower bounds of 1 standard deviation (1 SD), were baseline-corrected and plotted. Raman mapping analysis was performed at three different areas on one membrane sample prior to collecting bright-field and dark-field images for each area. Three Raman maps of each area were generated by tracking the intensities of three PS Raman bands at 996, 1597, and 3053 cm–1, following baseline correction for all the Raman spectra within each map. The generated Raman maps were compared with the optical images to verify the accuracy of the Raman analysis and evaluate the performance of the membrane sensors and unmodified membranes. Dark-field images of a 0.4 μm PCTE membrane sensor and a pristine 0.4 μm PCTE membrane without LMMPs were captured as a negative control, confirming the absence of particles on both the membrane sensor and the unmodified membrane (Figure S2a,b). Raman spectra of the 0.4 μm PCTE membrane sensors without LMMPs were acquired to confirm minimal interference from the membrane signals during PS LMMPs analysis (Figure S2c). To validate the reproducibility of the sensor’s performance, 1.5 μm PS LMMPs were imaged on four 0.4 μm PCTE membrane sensors. The consistent results confirm the excellent reproducibility of the membrane sensor fabrication process (Figure S3).

Validation of Membrane Sensor Performance Using Eutrophic Lake Water Samples

Water from Lake Mendota, a eutrophic lake in Madison, WI, was used to investigate the impact of a complex freshwater matrix on the performance of the membrane sensors for LMMP analysis. Water samples were collected from the lakeshore of Lake Mendota in the summer (July) and autumn (November) of 2023. After sampled from the lake, the lake water was stored at 4 °C before use. The water matrix parameters, including dissolved organic carbon (DOC), pH, and conductivity, were measured using a total organic carbon analyzer (TOC-L, Shimadzu Corporation, Columbia, MD) and a pH/conductivity meter (Orion Versa Star Pro, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). The conductivity, pH, and DOC concentration were similar between summer (474.0 μS cm–1, 7.8 and 4.3 mg L–1, respectively) and winter (490.3 μS/cm, 7.9 and 4.3 mg L–1, respectively) sampling events. Total suspended solids (TSSs) were measured using Standard Method 2540D.30 The TSS concentrations of the lake water collected in the summer and winter were 1 and 0.8 mg/L, respectively. Three liters of the lake water samples was filtered through a 0.4 μm PCTE membrane to remove suspended particles similar in size to the target LMMPs, while the other 3 L was preserved as raw lake water. The filtered lake water (passed through a 0.4 μm membrane) was reserved to assess the impact of the dissolved organic matter in the lake water on LMMP detection via the membrane sensor. The remaining portion of the sampled lake water was designated as raw lake water to investigate the combined effect of particulate and dissolved organic matter on LMMP detection via the membrane sensor. The prefiltered lake water samples were spiked with 1.5 μm PS LMMPs at a concentration of 0.2 mg L–1, and the raw lake water samples were spiked with LMMPs at concentrations of 0.001, 0.01, and 0.2 mg L–1. The spiked lake water samples with LMMPs were then filtered through the PCTE membrane sensors for analysis following the procedure outlined in the previous section. All lake water samples were characterized through single-point and area-scanning Raman spectroscopic analysis with a scanning area of 23 × 33 μm. To further demonstrate the capability of the membrane sensor for rapid LMMP detection, an expanded map (100 × 100 μm) was acquired for lake water sample with a LMMP concentration of 0.01 mg L–1. Details of the data processing procedure for Raman maps collected in lake water are illustrated in Text S2.

Results and Discussion

Improved LMMP Imaging on Gold-Coated Polymeric Membranes

To evaluate the impact of the sputter coating process on membrane morphology and pore size, dark-field images were collected for unmodified PCTE, CA, PES, and PVDF membranes and the corresponding membrane sensors (Figure S4). Based on visual inspection, the morphology of all four types of membranes showed little to no change after a layer of gold was sputter-coated onto the membrane surface. This observation was also confirmed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images (Figure 1a–d) and the consistent water flux of PCTE membrane sensors and PCTE unmodified membranes (Figure S5). Similar to the unmodified membranes, the gold-coated CA, PES, and PVDF membranes retained their fibrous and inhomogeneous surfaces, while the PCTE membrane maintained its flat surface and homogeneous pores.

The dark-field images of PVDF, PES, and CA and the bright-field image of PCTE membrane sensors are presented in Figure S6.

To investigate the influence of the gold layer coating on LMMP imaging, 1.5 μm PS microspheres were used as model LMMPs to validate our analytical method. Compared to the unmodified membranes (Figure 1e–h), gold-coated membranes presented significantly improved imaging quality for LMMPs regardless of the membrane type, as indicated by the enhanced contrast between PS LMMPs and membrane substrates (Figure 1i–l). The thin gold film on the membrane sensors reduced the detected photons from the membrane due to the high reflectivity of gold, which results in a darker background and increases the contrast between the background and the targeted LMMPs.31 Another plausible explanation is that the LSPR of gold nanorings enhanced the light scattering from the LMMPs. The enhanced visualization of LMMPs on the gold-coated membrane surfaces transforms these membranes into sensors for in situ quantification of LMMPs, particularly when combined with Raman spectroscopy and commercial particle-finding software with enhanced speed and reliability.

Compared to bright-field images, dark-field images exhibited similar contrast for CA, PES, and PVDF membrane sensors but much better contrast for PCTE membrane sensors (Figures 1 and S6). Membrane sensors with flat and homogeneous surfaces exhibited greatly enhanced imaging quality for LMMPs compared with those with fibrous structures and rough surfaces. PVDF-, CA-, and PES-based membrane sensors have fibrous structures and inhomogeneous membrane surfaces (Figure 1a–c), which complicates the imaging process, as some LMMPs may penetrate the membrane surfaces and fall into the voids beneath. This leads to inconsistent focal planes for different particles, consequently yielding blurred images of LMMPs (Figure S1). In contrast, PCTE membrane sensors with flat and homogeneous membrane surfaces provide a clean image background and pronounced contrast between LMMPs and the substrate (Figure 1l). This phenomenon aligns with findings in pathogen detection, where the smooth and glass-like membrane surface of PCTE membranes provides a consistent focal plane, generating more accurate Raman imaging compared to silver membranes.32 Similarly, LMMPs retained on PCTE membrane sensors tend to be at the same focal plane, resulting in a much better imaging quality. These results highlight the critical role of the membrane surface morphology in LMMP imaging, which is expected to be even more pronounced as the particle size decreases.

Improved Raman Spectroscopic Analysis for LMMPs on PCTE Membrane Sensors

Given its superior imaging quality for LMMPs compared to other polymeric membrane sensors, the PCTE membrane sensor was selected for further tests of polymer identification. PS LMMPs were filtered through 0.4 μm PCTE membrane sensors and further analyzed by a confocal Raman spectrometer.

For 1.5 μm PS LMMPs, a notable enhancement in Raman intensity was observed on the membrane sensor compared to the unmodified membrane (Figure 2a,f). Specifically, the Raman intensity of the PS band at 996 cm–1 was enhanced by 48% ± 25% (1 SD) on the membrane sensor. The membrane sensor also demonstrated improved reliability in Raman mapping of 1.5 μm PS LMMPs. While the unmodified membrane can provide a reliable Raman map by tracking the dominant PS Raman bands at 996 and 1597 cm–1 (Figure 2c,e), the quality of the Raman maps tracking the other prominent band at 3053 cm–1 was poor due to increased background interference with a short integration time of 0.1 s (Figure 2d). The membrane sensor effectively addressed this challenge by simultaneously reducing background interference and enhancing the Raman intensity of the LMMPs. Consequently, the Raman maps of 1.5 μm PS on the membrane sensor exhibited a substantially cleaner background compared to the unmodified membrane (Figure 2h–j). For 8 μm PS LMMPs, the contrast between the PS LMMPs and the membrane background in Raman maps tracking the PS Raman bands at 996, 3053, and 1597 cm–1 were slightly improved using the PCTE membrane sensor (Figure S7), further demonstrating the sensor’s reliability for multiband analysis of LMMPs. For 1.5 μm PS LMMPs, even with a further reduced integration time of 0.01 s, the membrane sensor maintained its capability to deliver high-quality Raman maps (Figure S8). Reducing the integration time from 0.1 to 0.01 s equates to a 10-fold reduction in the mapping time, indicating that the membrane sensors enable ultrafast and reliable Raman spectroscopic analysis of LMMPs. With an integration time of 0.01 s, 30% of the area of a 47 mm PCTE membrane sensor can be analyzed within 8 h at a spatial resolution of 10 μm and within 0.08 h at a resolution of 100 μm.

Mechanisms Behind the Improved Performance for LMMP Detection

In this section, we aim to evaluate how the sputter coating process affects membrane properties and membrane sensor performance for LMMP analysis and unveil the mechanisms behind the improved performance for LMMP analysis.

Since the hydrophilicity of membranes may affect the distribution of retained LMMPs, it is critical to understand the influence of the sputter coating process on the hydrophilicity of the membranes. The gold coating of the PCTE membrane sensor slightly changed the hydrophilicity of the membranes (Figure S9). However, the marginal change in hydrophilicity did not impact the particle distribution of PS LMMPs on the membrane sensors, thereby supporting the accurate and reliable quantification of LMMPs (Figures S10 and 11). Further discussion is presented in Text S1.

Previous studies suggest that sputter coating of a gold layer on membrane surfaces could produce SERS effect.33 To verify if our PCTE membrane sensors also possess SERS effect, we prepared membrane sensors with different gold layer thicknesses on 0.4 μm PCTE membranes by varying the sputter coating time from 0 to 40 s. The SERS effect of these membrane sensors was evaluated using 4-MBA, a model Raman compound with a high affinity toward gold.34−37 The thickness of the gold layer greatly affected the SERS intensity of the 4-MBA Raman band at 1065 cm–1. In the absence of the gold layer, the unmodified membrane lacked the chemical sensing ability for 4-MBA. As the gold layer became thicker, the color of the membrane sensors changed from light blue to dark gold (Figure S12), accompanied by an increasing SERS intensity of 4-MBA (Figure 3a,c). This supports our hypothesis that the PCTE membrane sensors exhibited the SERS effect, which became more pronounced as the gold layer thickness increased. The morphology and surface nanostructure of a SERS substrate are critical for SERS enhancement.38 As the thickness of the gold layer increases, localized surface plasmon resonance on the gold surface appears to be stronger, potentially increasing the density of SERS hot spots and leading to enhanced Raman intensities from the adsorbed chemicals.39 To balance the SERS effect with the cost effectiveness, we set the optimal sputter coating time at 30 s for the following experiments.

In addition to the nanoscale intrusions on gold surfaces, the formed gold rings around the pores of PCTE membranes may also contribute to the SERS effect. To verify this hypothesis, PCTE membranes with pore sizes of 10, 1, 0.4, 0.2, and 0.1 μm were utilized to fabricate membrane sensors. As shown in Figure 3b, the pore size of the membrane sensors played a critical role in the SERS intensities of 4-MBA. A decrease in membrane pore size from 10 to 0.1 μm led to an increasingly pronounced SERS effect, as demonstrated by the stronger intensity of the 4-MBA Raman band at 1065 cm–1 (Figure 3d). As a control experiment, in the absence of the gold layer, the alternation of membrane pore size did not lead to any observable 4-MBA Raman bands (Figure S13), ruling out the possibility of PCTE membranes themselves possessing any SERS effect. The increasingly pronounced SERS effect of the membrane sensors with a diminishing membrane pore size could be attributed to plasmonic nanorings formed by gold and the membrane pores during the sputter coating process. We note that the coated gold layer did not block the membrane pores (Figures 1d, S4, and S5). Instead, it adhered along the rims of the membrane pores, forming submicrometer-sized rings or nanorings on the membrane surfaces. Plasmonic ring-shaped cavities have been proven to be SERS effective, especially when their dimensions are reduced to nanoscale,40 which supports our observations. Future studies will be needed to fully characterize the electromagnetic field distribution around the gold nanorings and investigate their interactions with LMMPs on the membrane sensors, with the goal of potentially improving the membrane sensors’ detection performance.

Impacts of Lake Water Matrix on Membrane Sensors

The dissolved organic matter of lake water did not affect the imaging and sensing performance of the membrane sensors for LMMPs, as evidenced from the high-quality dark-field images and Raman maps of LMMPs in filtered lake water (Figure 4a–d). The dark-field images of LMMPs show that the contrast between the targeted plastic particles and the image background in filtered lake water remained high and comparable to that in DI water regardless of the dissolved chemical constituents (Figures 4a and S14a). Similarly, Raman maps of PS LMMPs in filtered lake water, processed identically to DI water samples, display a clean background of the membrane substrate and a high contrast between PS LMMPs and the background, further illustrating that the chemical characterization of LMMPs on the membrane sensors was also not affected by the dissolved organic matter in the lake water (Figures 4b and S14b). These results confirmed the robustness and reliability of the membrane sensors for LMMP detection in complex aqueous matrices.

Within raw water samples, dark-field images of LMMPs showed interference with incidental natural particles, but LMMPs could still be delineated in the Raman maps generated by the developed algorithm. Spiked LMMPs and similarly sized suspended particles from raw lake water, retained on the membrane sensor, were visualized in the dark-field images, exhibiting pronounced contrast against the background regardless of the water collection date (Figures 4c and S14c). Since the dark-field images effectively delineate the morphologies and boundaries of the retained particles regardless of their identities, it is necessary to integrate chemical characterization with the optical images to precisely differentiate the targeted LMMPs from incidental particles in the lake water. Raman analysis was performed by tracking the predominant Raman band of PS at 996 cm–1 to generate Raman maps within the imaging area, using an identical data processing procedure for DI water samples. This targeting approach effectively eliminates non-PS particles, isolating PS LMMPs with significant contrast in the Raman maps (Figures 4d and S14d). Despite the higher TSS concentration (1 mg L–1) compared to PS LMMNPs (0.2 mg L–1), the locations of PS LMMPs in the Raman maps matched those in the dark-field images, demonstrating the high accuracy and reliability of the membrane sensor for LMMP detection in the environmental water matrix.

Using the established protocols and data processing procedure in the previous sections, the PCTE membrane sensor precisely and reliably detected PS LMMPs in raw lake water at concentrations as low as 1 μg L–1. PS LMMPs were spiked in raw lake water at concentrations of 10 and 1 μg L–1 to simulate environmentally relevant concentrations of LMMPs in fresh water. Despite the higher concentration of suspended particles originated from raw lake water, the spiked PS LMMPs were distinctly identified in optical images, attributed to the membrane sensor’s capability to clearly delineate particle morphology (Figure 4e,g). Raman maps of PS LMMPs further confirmed the chemical composition of the spotted LMMPs (Figure 4f,h). Notably, the precision of the Raman maps for PS LMMPs remained uncompromised even at 1 μg/L, despite the presence of numerous natural suspended particles in lake water (Figure 4h). The precise Raman maps of PS LMMPs on the membrane sensor can be used to quantify and verify LMMP concentrations using the previously developed method.14 These results collectively demonstrate the potential of the membrane sensors for detecting LMMPs at environmentally relevant concentrations in real samples.

Using advanced data processing, LMMPs spiked in raw lake water were rapidly and accurately separated and spotted on the membrane sensor across expanded scanning areas. The increase in scanning area introduced additional interferences to Raman analysis, including an elevated number of incidental particles and instability of the Raman stage. To mitigate these interferences, we developed an advanced algorithm incorporating baseline correction, peak finding, and peak multiplication to process the Raman maps for targeted LMMPs. When the scanning area was expanded approximately 12-fold to 100 × 100 μm, the dark-field image clearly delineated the morphology of incidental particles and the targeted LMMPs (Figure 4i). Despite the increased presence of incident particles, the advanced algorithm allowed the Raman map to accurately capture the locations and particle numbers of PS LMMPs on the membrane sensor (Figure 4j). These findings, illustrated by the high-quality optical images and Raman maps at low concentrations and expanded scanning areas (Figure 4), underscored the reliability and sensitivity of the membrane sensors for detecting LMMPs at environmentally relevant concentrations, showing the great potential of adapting the membrane sensor to detect LMMPs in natural freshwater environments.

Environmental Implications

LMMPs pose a growing concern in aquatic ecosystems due to their ubiquity and potential toxicity.4,5 To better understand the presence and toxicity of LMMPs in aquatic systems, fast and reliable tools to detect LMMPs at the single-particle level are urgently needed. In this study, we developed a novel plasmonic membrane sensor integrating membrane filtration with the SERS effect for single-particle detection of LMMPs by sputter coating a layer of gold onto a polymeric membrane. The dual-function membrane sensor enables the in situ morphological and chemical characterization of LMMPs in water, eliminating the need for pre- or post-treatment processes, reducing the analysis time, and minimizing the risk of contamination during sample handling and analysis. No additional training beyond the operation of membrane filtration units and Raman spectroscopy is required to deploy the membrane sensors, which reduces user barriers and facilitates broad adoption. Notably, the developed membrane sensors, particularly PCTE membrane sensors with flat and homogeneous surfaces, outperformed unmodified membranes and demonstrated exceptional imaging ability for 1.5 μm LMMPs in water samples. PCTE membrane sensors can reliably characterize individual PS LMMPs within 0.01 s and theoretically construct a 100 × 100 μm Raman map within 1 min, demonstrating its potential for swift detection and immediate monitoring of LMMPs in environmental water. Our study revealed a direct enhancement of the chemical sensing ability of membrane sensors with increasing gold layer thickness and decreasing membrane pore size. These findings provided insights to aid in the further optimization of membrane sensors for the targeted detection of LMMPs in diverse water samples.

To assess the stability and robustness of the membrane sensor in freshwater, PS LMMPs were spiked into natural lake waters and detected using the plasmonic membrane sensor. The membrane sensors effectively isolated and concentrated LMMPs from lake water, enabling the precise single-particle detection of LMMPs. The imaging and chemical sensing ability of the membrane sensors remained uncompromised in the complex water matrices, demonstrating the reliability and stability of the membrane sensor and highlighting their potential for deployment in real-world applications. Despite the presence of organic and inorganic particulate and dissolved organic matter in raw lake water, the membrane sensor distinctly captured the morphology of the spiked LMMPs, which is critical as the morphology may correspond to the toxicity and uptake of LMMPs in aquatic ecosystems.5,41 By providing a fast and reliable tool to clearly delineate the morphology of LMMPs, the membrane sensor can facilitate future studies for the investigation of the environmental impact and biological interaction of LMMPs in aquatic ecosystems. The membrane sensor exhibited high sensitivity and rapid detection of spiked LMMPs in lake water. However, LMMPs recovered from environmental water may have biofilms or surface weathering that alters their surface chemistry compared with spiked samples. Future studies are needed to validate the membrane sensor’s detection efficacy for LMMPs recovered from natural water bodies, such as lakes and rivers.

To increase the sensitivity and reduce the cost of the membrane sensors, further optimization of the membrane sensor may be achieved through modifying the fabrication process of the membrane sensors or alternating the properties of the membrane substrate. While the plasmonic membrane sensors demonstrated ultrafast speed and reliability for single-particle detection of LMMPs, the effectiveness of the membrane sensors for smaller particles like NPs has yet to be determined. The tunable pore size of the developed membrane sensor makes it a promising tool for separating and detecting NPs in water samples. Further study will be needed to evaluate the detection performance of the plasmonic membrane sensors for NPs in complex environmental matrices.

Remarkably, the plasmonic membrane sensor achieved precise imaging and characterization of individual LMMPs in lake water at concentrations as low as 1 μg L–1, surpassing or matching the detection limit of existing analytical methods for MPs and NPs.23,26,42 Enhanced detection limit of the plasmonic membrane sensor is attainable by increasing the filtration volume of the water sample, leveraging the capability of the membrane sensor for ultra-low LMMP concentration. The consistent detection accuracy and reliability of the membrane sensor across various lake water matrices at concentrations as low as 1 μg L–1 demonstrated its viability for single-particle detection of LMMPs in natural water samples. Notably, the advanced data processing algorithm to process Raman maps that was developed in this paper accurately captured the location and number of the targeted LMMPs across expanded scanning areas, paving the road for automatically detecting individual LMMPs in complex natural waters.
