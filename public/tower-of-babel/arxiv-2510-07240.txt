Shedding light on classical shadows: learning photonic quantum states
Hugo Thomas, Ulysse Chabaud, Pierre-Emmanuel Emeriau

Abstract
Efficient learning of quantum state properties is both a fundamental and practical problem in quantum information theory. Classical shadows have emerged as an efficient method for estimating properties of unknown quantum states, with rigorous statistical guarantees, by performing randomized measurement on a few number of copies. With the advent of photonic technologies, formulating efficient learning algorithms for such platforms comes out as a natural problem. Here, we introduce a classical shadow protocol for learning photonic quantum states via randomized passive linear optical transformations and photon-number measurement. We show that this scheme is efficient for a large class of observables of interest. We experimentally demonstrate our findings on a twelve-mode photonic integrated quantum processing unit. Our protocol allows for scalable learning of a wide range of photonic state properties and paves the way to applying the already rich variety of applications of classical shadows to photonic platforms.

I
Introduction

Characterizing unknown quantum states is a fundamental challenge in
quantum information science. Quantum state tomography aims to reconstruct the
full density matrix of a state
\rho
, but it suffers from an unavoidable
scaling: the number of copies required grows quadratically with Hilbert space
dimension. As ever-bigger experimental systems become accessible thanks to
recent quantum hardware development

Nigmatullin et al. 2025
;
Larsen et al. 2025
;
Liu et al. 2025
;
Sales Rodriguez et al. 2025
, full-fledged quantum
state tomography rapidly becomes intractable, as it becomes hopeless to even
store the density matrix in classical memory. The question is no longer on how
to reconstruct the full state, but rather about how much useful information can
be extracted from a limited number of copies.

A breakthrough in this direction came with shadow tomography.
Aaronson
Aaronson 2018
first showed that for many tasks, e.g.,
predicting physical properties, reconstructing the entire density matrix is
unnecessary. Instead, a well-chosen, lightweight description of the quantum
state — a
shadow
of the state — suffices. Huang et
al.
Huang et al. 2020
subsequently introduced the notion of

classical shadows
, produced from randomized measurements on only a few
state copies and classical post-processing. These shadows allow efficient
prediction of a wide variety of quantum features, including expectation values,
fidelities, and entropies. Since then, the framework has been widely adopted and
refined, incorporating error
mitigation
Jnane et al. 2024
;
Hu et al. 2025
;
Chen et al. 2021
,
adaptation to hardware
constraints
West et al. 2025
;
Koh and Grewal 2022
;
Zhao et al. 2021
;
Wan et al. 2023
;
Sauvage and Larocca 2024
;
Ippoliti 2024
;
Low 2024
,
and improvements of the protocol
itself
Nguyen et al. 2022
;
Helsen and Walter 2023
;
Zhou and Liu 2023
;
Chen et al. 2024
;
Fawzi et al. 2024
.
As a result, classical shadows have emerged as a practical tool across domains,
from
benchmarking
Levy et al. 2024
;
Kunjummen et al. 2023
;
Zhu et al. 2022
;
Elben et al. 2020

to error correction
Conrad et al. 2025
, Hamiltonian
simulation
Hadfield et al. 2022
;
McNulty et al. 2023
;
Dutt et al. 2023
;
Huang et al. 2022

and quantum machine
learning
Huang et al. 2021
;
Jerbi et al. 2024
;
Haug et al. 2023
.

Despite this prompt progress, most developments of shadow tomography have
focused on qubit-based architectures. Yet, photonics has emerged as one of the
most promising candidates for scalable quantum information
processing
Knill et al. 2001
;
Bartolucci et al. 2023
;
de Gliniasty et al. 2024
.
Photonic qubits are inherently modular and naturally suited for networking, as
they can be routed, multiplexed and distributed with minimal cross-talk

Wang et al. 2020
. Integrated photonics offers a path to large-scale
by leveraging semiconductor fabrication techniques. Moreover, small-scale
photonic devices are already publicly accessible
Maring et al. 2024
,
and photonics is a strong candidate for quantum advantage demonstration through
boson sampling
Aaronson and Arkhipov 2011
. Experimental realizations of
boson sampling have been reported with increasing scale and
sophistication
Spring et al. 2013
;
Broome et al. 2013
;
Loredo et al. 2017
;
Wang et al. 2017
;
Tillmann et al. 2013
;
Hoch et al. 2022
;
Hoch et al. 2025
,
motivating algorithms that explicitly exploit the hardness of simulating linear
optics
Salavrakos et al. 2025a
.
However, throughout these advances in photonic quantum information,
characterizing multiphoton states in many modes remains a central bottleneck.
While randomized benchmarking protocols have been introduced to assess the gate
quality of discrete variable bosonic systems

Arienzo et al. 2025
;
Wilkens et al. 2024
, full tomography remains
prohibitively demanding
Banchi et al. 2018
.

Here, we bridge this gap by introducing a classical shadow protocol tailored to
photonic platforms in a practically motivated setting. This setting consists of
passive linear optics combined with photon-number resolving (PNR) detectors, an
architecture that is both experimentally mature and conceptually distinct from
qubit systems. This setting imposes three constraints: transformations are
limited to passive linear-optical transformation, states are encoded in Fock
states rather than qubits, and measurements are restricted to photon-number
outcomes. At first sight, these restrictions appear to preclude the versatility
of shadow tomography.

We show that, in fact, scalable shadow protocols are still feasible under these
conditions. The main obstacle arises from PNR detection: it erases coherence
between states of different photon number, implying that measurements are
tomographically complete only within fixed-number
sectors
Banchi et al. 2018
. However, this limitation aligns
naturally with most photonic applications, which operate at fixed photon number,
including linear optical operations and dual-rail qubit encodings for universal
computation. What is more, we prove that our protocol also applies to states
produced by nonlinear processes. This means that a non-universal, linear-optical
device can suffice to characterize highly complex states produced by a universal
photonic machine such as that envisioned in the Knill–Laflamme–Milburn
scheme
Knill et al. 2001
. To support our findings, we demonstrate an
experimental implementation of the protocol on a 12-mode 6-photon linear optical
quantum computer. We show that many physical properties of a Fock state can be
efficiently and accurately predicted, with applications to benchmarking and
certification, as illustrated in
fig.

1
.

II
Background: classical shadows

Let
\rho
be a trace-one, positive-semidefinite linear operator on a

d
-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
and denote by
\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{H})
the space of linear
operators on
\mathcal{H}
. Classical shadows provide lightweight classical descriptions
of an unknown quantum state
\rho
, enabling the estimation of a collection of
properties of that state: linear properties, i.e., expectation values, or
nonlinear properties such as sub-system entropies. The protocol consists in
three steps: 1) the data acquisition phase, 2) the classical post-processing
phase and 3) the property prediction phase. More formally, given a set
\mathcal{U}
of
unitary matrices equipped with a probability measure
\mu
, the copies of
\rho

are evolved by drawing independently at random
U\sim_{\mu}\mathcal{U}
, and measuring
in a basis
\mathcal{B}
defined by a set of projectors
\{\outerproduct{b}{b}\}_{b\in\mathcal{B}}
.
This procedure is equivalent to applying a quantum channel
\mathscr{M}
to
\rho
,
which for instance takes the form of a global depolarising channel for Clifford
measurements or of a tensor product of local depolarising channels for Pauli
measurements
Huang et al. 2020
. In particular, the classical shadow
protocol ensures that for a tomographycally complete set of measurement,

\rho=\mathop{\underset{U,b}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathscr{M}^{-1}(U^{\dagger}\outerproduct{b}{b}U)\right]}.

(1)

More precisely, this channel is determined by both the distribution
\mu
and
the measurement basis
\mathcal{B}
. Its inverse is in general not physical as it might
not be completely positive, but can it be applied to the measurement outcome
during the classical post-processing phase.

A powerful tool for quantifying the amount of information that can be recovered
from a choice of
\mu
and
\mathcal{B}
is the
visible space
formalism

Kirk et al. 2022
. The visible space associated to
\mathcal{U}
,
\mu

and
\mathcal{B}
,
\text{VS}(\mathcal{U},\mu,\mathcal{B})
, is the span of
U^{\dagger}\outerproduct{b}{b}U
for every

U\in\mathcal{U}
with nonzero measure and
b\in\mathcal{B}
. The triplet
(\mathcal{U},\mu,\mathcal{B})

is said to be informationally complete (or tomographically complete) if and only
if
\text{VS}(\mathcal{U},\mu,\mathcal{B})=\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{H})
.

For a given unitary matrix
U\in\mathcal{U}
and a measurement outcome
b\in\mathcal{B}
, we
denote by
\hat{\rho}_{(U,b)}=\mathscr{M}^{-1}(U^{\dagger}\outerproduct{b}{b}U)
a

snapshot
of
\rho
—we might drop the subscript and use only
\hat{\rho}

when the explicit mention of
(U,b)
is irrelevant, and we call the collection
of
N
snapshots

\textsf{S}(\rho,N)=\{\hat{\rho}_{(U_{1},b_{1})},\cdots,\hat{\rho}_{(U_{N},b_{N})}\}

(2)

a
classical shadow
of
\rho
of size
N
.

By linearity of both the trace and the expectation value, linear functions of
the input state satisfy

o=\tr{O\rho}=\mathop{\underset{U,b}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\tr{O\hat\rho_{(U,b)}}\right]}=\mathop{\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{o}\right]}.

(3)

The goal, then, simply boils down to computing an empirical estimate of
\mathop{\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{o}\right]}
from the classical shadow
\textsf{S}(\rho,N)
and bounding the
approximation error coming from the finite statistics. Remarkably, to estimate
the expectation values of a set of
T
observables
\{O_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
to within
additive error
\varepsilon
with constant failure probability, one only needs

N=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\log(T)\varepsilon^{-2}\max_{t}\mathrm{Var}(\hat{o}_{t})\right)
copies of the
unknown state
\rho
, where
\hat{o}_{t}=\tr{O_t \hat\rho}
is the classical
shadow estimate of
o_{t}
.

Here, we extend the classical shadow framework to the aforementioned setting of
passive linear optics with photon-number resolving (PNR) measurement.

III
Photonic classical shadows

Protocol.
We now describe how to perform shadow tomography of
an unknown
m
-mode Fock state
\rho
, i.e., how to produce a classical
representation
\hat{\rho}
that behaves like
\rho
in expectation value. The
only a priori knowledge we assume is
m
, the number of modes. We do not fix the
number of photons. In particular, we allow for coherent superposition of
different photon-number states, and even state not prepared by passive linear
optical transformations on Fock basis states (e.g., by adaptive linear optics or
post-selection

Chabaud et al. 2021
;
Park et al. 2023
;
Monbroussou et al. 2025
). As
we will show, the visible space in this setting is delineated by the
measurements that project onto a fixed photon-number subspace.

The
m
-mode Fock-Hilbert space is
\mathcal{F}_{m}=\bigoplus_{n\geqslant 0}\mathcal{H}_{m}^{n}
, where

\mathcal{H}_{m}^{n}
is the
n
-photon subspace of dimension
\binom{n+m-1}{n}
spanned by

m
-tuples of nonnegative integers summing to
n
, which we denote
\Phi_{m,n}
.
The set of possible
m
-mode passive transformations is identified with the
unitary group
\mathbb{U}(m)
. It acts on
\mathcal{F}_{m}
via the unitary representation

\varphi_{m}
, whose irreducible representations (
irreps
for short) are exactly
those acting on each photon-number subspace
Aniello et al. 2006
.

The data collection process is similar to the classical shadow protocol for
qubits. A snapshot is obtained by drawing a unitary matrix
U
at random from
the Haar measure (which can easily be done numerically

Mezzadri 2007
), applying the transformation
\omega_{m}(U):\rho\mapsto\varphi_{m}(U)\rho\varphi_{m}^{\dagger}(U)
by letting
\rho
evolve through the
linear network described by
U
, and measuring in the computational basis with
PNR detection. Upon measuring a total of
n
photons, the measurement outcome is

\bm{s}\in\Phi_{m,n}
with probability
\tr{\omega_m(U)(\rho)\ketbra{\s}}
.

The protocol therefore produces a description of an arbitrary Fock input state
in the form of a convex combination of unitarily evolved Fock basis states, for
which efficient classical approximation algorithms are known

Aaronson and Hance 2014
;
Lim and Oh 2025
.

The average mapping (over the choice of unitary and measurement outcome, and
henceforth photon-numbers) of the input state
\rho
naturally takes the form of
a quantum channel
\mathscr{M}
. As unitary transformations preserve the number of
photons and photon-number detection projects onto one of the subspaces of

\mathcal{F}_{m}
, the measurement channel is not tomographically complete. In particular,
it is clear that applying
\mathscr{M}
yields a block diagonal operator, i.e., we have

\mathscr{M}=\bigoplus_{n\geqslant 0}{\mathscr{M}^{(n)}}\circ\mathcal{P}^{(n)},

(4)

where
\mathcal{P}^{(n)}
projects onto the
n
-photon sector. Using the decomposition
of
\omega_{m}
into irreducible representations, we give in Supplementary Information
a closed form for the channel, whose derivation follows from Schur’s lemma.
It follows that the visible space associated with this channel also has a
block diagonal structure, and that for instance the states produced by
measurement-based nonlinearity with fixed photon-number belong to the visible
space.

Using the self-adjointness property of the measurement channel, we find that for
each number sector of the Hilbert space,

\tr{O\rho^{(n)}}=\mathop{\underset{U,{\bm{s}}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\bra{\s}\varphi^{m}_{n}(U)\mathscr{M}^{(n)^{-1}}\!\!\left(O\right)\varphi^{m\dagger}_{n}(U)\ket{\s}\right]}\!,

(5)

where
\rho^{(n)}
is the projection of the input state
\rho
onto the

n
-photon sector. Hence, linear functions of a quantum states can be learned by
averaging the elements of a classical shadow
\textsf{S}(\rho,N)
.

Complexity of the protocol.
Next, we analyze the sample complexity of
the photonic classical shadows protocol, i.e., the number of samples required
for successfully learning quantum features of the input state. The
block-diagonalization of both the state and the observable, respectively

{\rho^{(n)}\simeq\bigoplus_{\lambda}\rho^{(n)}_{\lambda}}
and
{O\simeq\bigoplus_{\lambda}O_{\lambda}}
, lets us write

\tr{O\rho^{(n)}}=\sum_{\lambda}\ \tr{\rho^{(n)}_\lambda O_\lambda},

(6)

where
\lambda
runs over the irreps of
\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{H}_{m}^{n})
. This highlight the role
of the irreps supporting both the state and the observable. Importantly, we show
that whether
O_{\lambda}
is zero depend on the degree of
O
in the creation and
annihilation operators products
E_{i,j}=a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j}
. It follows that the
sample complexity of the scheme is dictated by the degree of the observables of
interest.

To that end, we introduce an analogue to the qubit shadow-norm

Huang et al. 2020
, which we call the
photonic shadow-norm
and
denote
\|\cdot\|_{\mathscr{P}}
. This is summarized by the following result (see the
Supplementary Information for a formal generalization):

Theorem 1

(Sample complexity of photonic classical
shadows)
.

A collection of
T
arbitrary linear
functions
\tr(O_1\rho),\cdots,\tr(O_T\rho)
can be collectively estimated
to within additive precision
\varepsilon
with a classical shadow of size

\max_{t}\big\|O_{t}-\frac{\tr{O_t}}{\dim{\mathcal{H}_{m}^{n}}}\mathbb{I}\big\|_{\mathscr{P}}^{2}\log T/\varepsilon^{2}
with constant success
probability.

We provide a detailed analysis in the Supplementary Information showing that
learning low-degree observable features of photonic quantum state is
surprisingly efficient. This analysis is supported by experimental results
exposed in
section

IV
. While full tomography requires high-degree
observables
Banchi et al. 2018
, low-degree observables are
nonetheless central to quantum information processing with photons. Example of
such observables include low-order correlation functions

Mayer et al. 2011
with applications in verification and certification

Walschaers et al. 2016
;
Giordani et al. 2018
;
Flamini et al. 2020
,
a large family of linear optical Lie-algebraic invariants

Parellada et al. 2024
. Remarkably, this aligns with the locality condition
for Pauli-based qubit classical shadows
Huang et al. 2020
, as
low-weight Pauli observables in dual-rail qubit encoding have low-degree.

Beyond their sample complexity, classical shadows protocols also rely on heavy
classical post-processing
Huang et al. 2020
. As such, it is important
to ensure that the corresponding running time is not prohibitive for practical
applications. In our photonic classical shadows protocol, computing a snapshot,
i.e., the evolution of a Fock basis state through a linear interferometer, takes
exponential time because it requires computing
n\times n
matrix permanents

Aaronson and Arkhipov 2011
. And while for simple yet interesting
observables, direct computation of their expectation value is possible

Mayer et al. 2011
, this task takes in general exponential time.
Luckily, classical Monte Carlo algorithms can be used to estimate to within
additive precision the estimator of
eq.

5
from the pair

(U,{\bm{s}})

Seron et al. 2024
;
Lim and Oh 2025
, saving us the
calculation of the snapshots.

The computational complexity is summarized by the following result, which shows
in particular that our protocol is also computationally efficient for learning
low-degree observables:

Theorem 2

(Computational complexity of photonic classical
shadows)
.

Given classical access to the channel

\mathscr{M}^{(n)}
, a collection of
T
arbitrary linear functions
\tr(O_1\rho),\cdots,\tr(O_T\rho)
can be collectively estimated to within additive
precision
\varepsilon
in time
\mathcal{O}\!\left({\mathsf{poly}}(n,m^{\max_{t}\deg(O_{t})})\max_{t}\|O_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\log T/\varepsilon^{2}\right)
with constant
success probability.

The calculation of the matrix-form of the channel is a computationally demanding
task that is discussed in Methods.
theorems

1
and
2

provide pessimistic upper bounds on the complexity of photonic classical
shadows. In practice, the sample complexity and running time of the protocol can
be significantly lower, as we demonstrate experimentally in the next section.

Moreover, as the protocol allows one to express highly complex photonic state as
a linear combination of Fock basis state evolved unitarily,

theorems

1
and
2
also hold for state produced e.g.,
by adaptive linear optics.

We report an experimental implementation of the classical
shadow protocol based on passive linear optical transformations and
photon-number resolving measurements for predicting physical properties of the
input state.

The experimental setup consists in multiphoton states (up to 6 photons) produced by a
time-demultiplexed semiconductor quantum dot based on demand single-photon source evolved through a 12-mode
universal photonic circuit made of an array of fully programmable Mach-Zehnder
interferometers. Photons are detected at the output of the chip by
superconducting nanowire single-photon detectors (SNSPDs) and detection times
are processed by a correlator
Maring et al. 2024
. As we only have
access to threshold detectors, we emulate PNR measurement with pseudo-PNR
measurements. The pseudo-PNR measurement allows us to perform multi-shot
estimation for free
Zhou and Liu 2023
. The experimental setup is
illustrated in
fig.

1
and the measurement technique is
detailed in the Supplementary Information.

In order to demonstrate the versatility of the scheme, we illustrate how a range
of already demonstrated experiments, all relying on different setups, can be
obtained from the same experimental setup with tailored classical
post-processing. We consider three following illustrative applications which consist
in evaluating
(a)
low-order correlation functions

Mayer et al. 2011
,
(b)
Lie-algebraic linear optical
invariants
Parellada et al. 2024
, and
(c)
binned-probability
distributions
Seron et al. 2024
. We briefly explain the theoretical
background behind each experiment and report in
fig.

2
the
experimental results. Finally, we discuss the scaling of the approach.

Application (a)

(Low-order correlators)
.

Low-order quantum correlators, whose purpose are to quantify the correlations of
detection events, comprise a large amount of information about many-body states.
They are at the core of numbers of applications, including bosonic quantum
walks
Mayer et al. 2011
, the study of statistical properties of
many-particle interferences for certifying Boson Sampling experiments

Walschaers et al. 2016
;
Giordani et al. 2018
;
Flamini et al. 2020
,
or the measurement of many-particle entanglement
Brunner et al. 2022
.

One of the simplest mode correlators is the two-mode correlator

C_{i,j}=\expectationvalue{\hat n_i\hat n_j}-\expectationvalue{\hat n_i}\!\!\expectationvalue{\hat n_j},

(7)

where
\hat{n}_{k}=\hat{a}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{k}
is the
k
-th mode number operator.
Higher-order correlators can be obtained from higher-degree products of the mode
number operators by extending
eq.

7
.

Application (b)

(Lie-algebraic linear optical invariants)
.

Linear optical invariants are quantities (or, properties of a state) that remain
constant under linear optical evolution. On the theoretical side, such
invariants can be used to derive no-go theorems for Fock state to Fock state
transformations. Moreover, the experimental observation of Lie-algebraic
invariant, as achieved in
Rodari et al. 2025
, plays a crucial role
for the characterization of integrated photonic processors. For instance, they
allow for the detection of ongoing nonunitary effects, as the measured quantity
are invariant under unitary evolution. We demonstrate here that the classical
shadow protocol can be used to obtain their numerical values.

Let
\{O_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m^{2}}
be a basis of the space of linear optical Hamiltonians
over
m
modes and
\rho
denote an arbitrary
m
-mode Fock state.
Ref.
Parellada et al. 2024
introduces three linear-optical invariants. The
first and arguably simplest is

I(\rho)=\sum_{i=1}^{m^{2}}\expectationvalue{O_i}^{2}.

(8)

The second is the spectrum of

\rho_{T}=\sum_{i=1}^{m^{2}}\expectationvalue{O_i}O_{i},

(9)

which we write
\lambda(\rho_{T})
. The third is the spectrum of the covariance
matrix
\Gamma(\rho)
with entries

\left(\Gamma(\rho)\right)_{i,j}=\expectationvalue*{O_i}\!\!\!\expectationvalue*{O_j}-\frac{1}{2}\expectationvalue*{O_iO_j - O_jO_i},

(10)

which we write
\lambda(\Gamma(\rho))
. Note that
\lambda(\rho_{T})
and

\lambda(\Gamma(\rho))
are of size
\binom{n+m-1}{n}
and
m^{2}
respectively.
Measuring the latter directly is challenging experimentally as it requires measuring
degree-two observables.

Application (c)

(Binned probabilities)
.

Binned distributions, i.e., output distribution of photons distributing in
partitions of the output modes, are yet another tool to validate the correct
functioning of linear optical devices
Seron et al. 2024
which is
experimentally feasible
Anguita et al. 2025
. Probing the total
variation distance between an actual experiment binned-distribution and that of
the expected distribution allows to one to detect fallacious boson samplers.

The
m
output modes are clustered into
K
bins
{\mathcal{K}=\{\mathcal{K}_{1},\cdots,\mathcal{K}_{K}\}}
. To each bin
\mathcal{K}_{i}
we associate the bin-number operator
\hat{N}_{i}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{K}_{i}}\hat{n}_{j}
and denote by
{\bm{N}_{\mathcal{K}}=(\hat{N}_{1},\cdots,\hat{N}_{K})}
the vector of bin-number operators.

Binned-probabilities are given by the multidimensional discrete Fourier
transform of the characteristic function
x_{\rho}
defined as

x_{\rho}(\bm{\eta})=\tr{e^{\imath\bm\eta\cdot{\bm{N}}_\Kc}\rho}.

(11)

Importantly, binned-distribution can be classically approximated to within small
total variation distance if the number of bins
K
is constant

Seron et al. 2024
. In this setting, the computation of the expected
distribution can be performed classically and used to validate the experimental
setup in hand.

We report in

implementation of the classical shadow protocol for these three

,
(b)
and

. The

to estimate all these properties.

For the two-mode correlators (

the correlation matrix
(C_{i,j})_{0\leqslant i,j,\leqslant m}

0.03

For the Lie-algebraic linear optical invariants (

results are compatible with the theoretical results, despite a slightly larger

For the binned distribution (

Several factors that we identify explain these small deviations. The first factor is the TVD with respect to the true distribution at the data-collection
level due to Pseudo-PNR. We explain in the Supplemental Material that this can
be mitigated by simply taking more samples. Second, the
deviations originates from typical experimental imperfections of the apparatus,
whose main representative are partial photon distinguishability, chip
characterization instability over time, and detector efficiency. Lastly, the
prediction precision can indeed be improved by considering a classical shadow of
larger size.

The main point of classical shadows is to lighten the workload on the quantum
device and transfer it to a classical computer. This indeed raises the question
of the running time complexity of the post-processing. While for the experiment
we compute the full snapshot’s density matrix, we argue in the Supplemental
Material that classical algorithms tailored to simulate linear optics can be
used to estimate the state’s properties from the pairs
\{(U_{i},\bm{s}_{i})\}_{i}

rather than
\hat{\rho}_{U_{i},{\bm{s}}_{i}}
. That is to say, computing density matrices
is not necessary and the protocol can instead rely on classical simulation
algorithms such as

running time of the classical post-processing also depends on the degree of the
observables. Another classical-time consuming step is the computation of the
explicit matrix form of the channel
\mathscr{M}
. Luckily, this computation only has to
be done once and for all and stored in memory.

V
Conclusion and outlook

In this work, we adapt the classical shadow framework to the setting of passive
linear optics with photon-number resolving (PNR) measurements and provide an
experimental demonstration of the scheme. We prove that meaningful and scalable
property learning is possible, thereby extending the realm of classical shadows
to experimentally and publicly accessible photonic platforms.

As a proof-of-concept validation, we show that the scheme can be incorporated at
different levels in three types of experiments. To demonstrate its versatility,
we reproduce parts of each experiment with the same classical shadow by
manipulating three single photons in an integrated photonic platform with twelve
modes. The input state generation rate arguably constitutes the primary
limitation to experimental realizations. The reason is that, as of today,
spatially encoded multiphoton states are produced from a single
time-demultiplexed single-photon source. Considerable research efforts are
directed towards using multiple single-photon sources

Pont et al. 2024
, as this is an important building-block of
photonic fault-tolerant architectures. Along with experimental demonstrations of
measurement-based state preparation

Prevedel et al. 2007
;
Thiele et al. 2024
, this suggests that
highly-complex state will shortly be within reach.

Beyond predicting physical properties, another promising venue for classical
shadows obtained from a quantum computer are machine learning applications

Huang et al. 2021
;
Jerbi et al. 2024
;
Cho and Kim 2024
. For instance,
shadow tomography can be used to construct a classical representation of the
output of the parameterized circuit that is then employed during the classical
classification phase. Photonic variational quantum circuits have recently
attracted a lot of attention, both theoretically

Gan et al. 2022
;
Pappalardo et al. 2025
and experimentally

Salavrakos et al. 2025b
. At the same time, classical simulation
algorithms are expected to fail remaining efficient when the input states are
produced from measurement processes

Hoch et al. 2025
;
Monbroussou et al. 2025
. But as each individual
snapshot is a Fock basis state evolved unitarily, the post-processing step of
the classical shadow protocol fulfils the constraints imposed by the classical
simulation algorithms on the input state
Lim and Oh 2025
. Therefore,
even in this case, such algorithms would still fit in the protocol. This opens
the door to shadow-based photonic machine learning applications relying on
sophisticated input states, beyond the reach of classical simulation, with low
workload imposed on the quantum device.

Additionally, it will be crucial to study the effect of noise on the protocol at
larger scale. Photonic architectures are subject to characteristic types of
noise, namely, photon losses and photon indistinguishability. On this basis,
incorporating error mitigation techniques tailored to linear optics

Mills and Mezher 2024
can be considered.

The development of integrated photonic technologies enabled the fabrication of
large scale and publicly available machines. The framework of classical shadows
is a powerful tool that benefits from an ever-growing number of refinements. In
this context, this work naturally opens the door to a wealth of new applications
for linear optical computation via classical shadows before fault-tolerance.

A.2
Classical shadows

In this section, we briefly recall the classical shadow formalism. For a more
thorough introduction we refer the reader to

Huang et al. 2020
;
Elben et al. 2023
. We consider an unknown

n
-qubit quantum state. The aim is to estimate expectation values

T

\{O_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
. The protocol
consists in two steps: 1) the data acquisition phase and 2) a classical
post-processing. Given a set of unitary matrices
\mathcal{U}
and a probability measure

\mu

\mathcal{U}

\{\Pi_{b}\}_{b\in\mathcal{B}}

\mathscr{M}(\rho)=\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}\int_{\mathcal{U}}\mathrm{d}\mu(U)\tr{U\rho U^\dagger\ketbra{b}}U^{\dagger}\outerproduct{b}{b}U.

(15)

For each choice of pair

snapshot
of

is defined

\hat{\rho}_{(U,b)}=\mathscr{M}^{-1}(U^{\dagger}\outerproduct{b}{b}U).

(16)

In the rest, we may omit the subscript and simply denote a snapshot
\hat{\rho}
.
The inverse of
\mathscr{M}
might not be physical but exists and is unique if the
ensemble of unitary transformations defines a tomographically complete set of
measurements

tomographically complete,
\mathscr{M}^{-1}
denotes the Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse of

\mathscr{M}
. The collection of all snapshot one obtains is referred to as a

classical shadow
of
\rho
. In practice, it will be more convenient to
store in memory the pair
(U,b)
rather than
\hat{\rho}
. A closed form
for
\mathscr{M}
follows from representation theory of the unitary group (and its
sub-groups thereof) and Weingarten calculus

— see
(
García-Martín et al. 2024

for a detailed
introduction. For instance,
\mathscr{M}
is a depolarising channel for shadows based on
random Clifford measurements
Huang et al. 2020
and other choices of

\mathcal{U}
induce other measurement channels, see e.g.,

West et al. 2025
;
Sauvage and Larocca 2024
.

Remarkably, to estimate the expectation value of a set of
T
observables to

\varepsilon

needs

N=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{\log(T)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\max_{t}\mathrm{Var}(\hat{o}_{t})\right)

(17)

copies of the unknown state
\rho
, where

is the

Beyond expectation values of observables, nonlinear functions such as Rényi
entropies or fidelities can also be efficiently estimated within this framework.
The image of

is called in

. More formally, it is defined as

\text{VS}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{B})=\text{span}\left\{U^{\dagger}\outerproduct{b}{b}U\right\}_{U\in\mathcal{U},b\in\mathcal{B}}.

(18)

Given an observable

, the authors show that
\hat{o}
is unbiased if

. Otherwise, the observable can be written as

and
O_{\bar{v}}\notin\text{VS}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{B})

is said to be

information that can be recovered with the protocol we introduce.

Appendix B
Shadow tomography for linear optics

In this section, we describe how to perform shadow tomography of an unknown

m

\rho

\hat{\rho}
that behaves
like

do not fix the number of photons. In particular, we allow for coherent

;
Monbroussou et al. 2025

visible space in this setting is constrained by the measurements that project
onto a fixed photon-number subspace.

B.1
Data collection in linear optics

The data collection process is similar to the classical shadow protocol for
qubits. A

is obtained by drawing a unitary matrix at random from
the Haar measure (which can easily be done numerically

of the unknown state
\rho

U

, where

is the
number of measured photons.

The average mapping (over the choice of unitary and measurement outcome, and
henceforth photon-numbers) of the input state

takes the form of a quantum

\bigoplus_{n\geqslant 0}\mathop{\mathbb{E}\left[\omega_{m}^{n}(U)^{\dagger}(\outerproduct{\s}{\s})\right]}=\mathscr{M}(\rho).

(32)

A closed-form for

is given in the next section.

B.2
A closed form for the channel

As unitary transformations preserve the number of photons and photon-number
detection projects onto one of the subspaces of

Theorem 3

Let

where

\rho=\mathbb{E}_{U,\bm{s}}\left[\mathscr{M}^{(n)^{-1}}\big(\omega_{m}^{n}(U)^{\dagger}[\outerproduct{\bm{s}}{\bm{s}}]\big)\right],

Recall from

where each fixed-photon-number channel is

\displaystyle\mathscr{M}^{(n)}

C.3
Variance of the estimator

In this section, we state the second main result of this work. We claim that the

Lemma 1

(Bound on estimators variance)

Fix

(54)

where the photonic shadow-norm only depends on the measurement primitive.

Proof.

Importantly, at most finite number of photons

will be detected in

has no off-block-diagonal terms. First, the

{\tr{\sigma^{\dagger}\Mc(\rho)}=\tr{\Mc(\sigma^{\dagger})\rho}}

\displaystyle\hat{o}-\mathop{\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\hat{o}\right]}=\tr{O\hat\rho^{(n)}}-\tr{O\rho^{(n)}}=\tr{O_0\hat\rho^{(n)}}-\tr{O_0\rho^{(n)}},

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left[\hat{o}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathop{\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[(\hat{o}-\mathop{\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{o}\right]})^{2}\right]}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\tr{O_0\hat\rho^{(n)}}^{2}\right]}-\tr{O_0\e{\hat\rho^{(n)}}}^{2}

57

Indeed, one can check that the photonic shadow norm

Appendix D
Pseudo-photon number resolving measurement

Principle of Pseudo-PNR measurement.

The protocol we present assumes access to photon-number
detectors, i.e., detectors that output the occupation number of the measured
mode. However, the complexity of building such detectors grows with the maximal
photon-number they are expected to resolve. Simpler detectors – threshold
detectors – distinguishing between the vacuum and one or more photons are more
widespread. It is possible to mimic the behaviour of PNR detectors using
threshold detectors in a larger interferometer as follows. A small
p
-mode
interferometer
\mathcal{F}_{p}
is appended to each of the
m
output modes, thus forming
a new
M=mp
mode interferometer

\tilde{U}=(\mathcal{F}_{p})^{\otimes m}P(U\otimes\mathbb{I}_{(m-1)p}),

(74)

where
P
is a suitable permutation that ensures that each output modes of
U

becomes the top mode of one of the
\mathcal{F}_{p}
. The
(m-1)p
additional modes are
filled with the vacuum, so that the new
M
-mode input state is
\rho\otimes\ket{0}^{(m-1)p}
. An example is shown in
.

As threshold detectors only distinguish whether the mode is occupied, the
purpose of
\mathcal{F}_{p}
is to maximize the spread of the input photons among the
p

modes. Formally, for an input state
\ket{n}\otimes\ket{0}^{\otimes p-1}
, the
goal is for
\mathcal{F}_{p}
to maximize

g_{p,n}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{b}\in\{0,1\}^{p}\\
|\bm{b}|=n\end{subarray}}\left|\!\matrixelement*{\bs b}{\varphi_p^n(\Fc_p)}{n, 0, \cdots, 0}\right|^{2},

(75)

where the sum ranges over all detection events where the threshold detectors
clicked for a single photon. This indeed enforces the constraint
p\geqslant n
. In
the case
p=n
,
g_{p,n}
is maximised by the interferometer implementing the
discrete Fourier transform defined as

\mathcal{F}_{p}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}(\omega^{kl})_{0\leqslant k,l\leqslant p-1},

(76)

with
\omega=e^{-2\imath\pi/p}
is a primitive
p
-th root of unity

Aaronson and Hance 2014
. We use this interferometer even for the cases

p\neq n
,
in which case according to
eq.

25

g_{p,n}=\binom{p}{n}\frac{n!}{p^{n}},

(77)

Each PNR detector is therefore emulated by many threshold detectors and a
Fourier interferometer. Upon measuring a bit-string
\bm{b}
at the output of the
Fourier interferometer, the occupation for the mode corresponding to this
pseudo-PNR circuit is
|\bm{b}|
. However, the fact that
g_{p,n}\neq 1
implies
that some measurement outcomes are discarded as threshold detectors cannot
resolve all occupations. This results in a distribution – which refer to as the
pseudo-PNR distribution – that is biased with respect to the actual
distribution. We now described how this bias can be mitigated.

With PPNR, however, we can only reconstruct a single block
\mathcal{P}^{(n)}(\rho)

which induces an additional constraint on the photon-number. This constraint is
a result of the experimental apparatus we use for the demonstration rather than
a limitation of the protocol itself. More precisely, if
\rho
is a
superposition of different photon-number states, the only-block one can
construct using pseudo-PNR is that corresponding to the maximal photon-number.
This comes from the fact that with pseudo-PNR, it is not possible to distinguish
between the measurement of an actual
(n-1)
-photon Fock state and two photons
clicking the same threshold detector (yielding

n
