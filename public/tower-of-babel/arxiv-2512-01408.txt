Bayesian Distributionally Robust Merton Problem with Nonlinear Wasserstein Projections
Jose Blanchet, Jiayi Cheng, Hao Liu, Yang Liu

Abstract
We revisit Merton’s continuous-time portfolio selection through a data-driven, distributionally robust lens. Our aim is to tap the benefits of frequent trading over short horizons while acknowledging that drift is hard to pin down, whereas volatility can be screened using realized or implied measures for appropriately selected assets. Rather than time-rectangular distributional robust control—which replenishes adversarial power at every instant and induces over-pessimism—we place a single ambiguity set on the drift prior within a Bayesian Merton model. This prior-level ambiguity preserves learning and tractability: a minimax swap reduces the robust control to optimizing a nonlinear functional of the prior, enabling Karatzas and Zhao [ 24 ] -type’s closed-form evaluation for each candidate prior. We then characterize small-radius worst-case priors under Wasserstein uncertainty via an explicit asymptotically optimal pushforward of the nominal prior, and we calibrate the ambiguity radius through a nonlinear Wasserstein projection tailored to the Merton functional. Synthetic and real-data studies demonstrate reduced pessimism relative to DRC and improved performance over myopic DRO–Markowitz under frequent rebalancing. MSC 2020 subject classifications: Primary 49K45; Secondary 49Q22, 91G10, 90C31. Keywords : Portfolio selection, distributionally robust stochastic control, reduction of over-pessimism, constrained distributional optimization, nonlinear Wasserstein projection

Introduction

This paper revisits Merton’s classical continuous-time portfolio selection model through a data-driven, distributionally robust lens. Our goal is to tap the benefits of frequent trading over short horizons (days to weeks) while acknowledging that drift is difficult to pin down over such horizons. In contrast, volatility can often be stabilized by screening via realized or implied measures appropriately chosen assets
1
1

1

We acknowledge that this screening can limit the investing universe; we discuss ways to incorporate volatility uncertainty in the conclusion.
. We therefore focus on the robustness of the drift and explicitly separate uncertainty modeling: we treat volatility as pre-estimated (e.g., realized/implied volatility) within the Bayesian filtering setup, and we robustify only the drift by placing a single ambiguity set on the drift prior.

Our starting point is the observation that, while data-driven robust portfolio selection performs competitively, it is static and myopic; it does not leverage the value of frequent rebalancing that Merton’s continuous-time model affords. In particular, for example, myopic DRO–Markowitz policies do not capitalize on information revealed over time except through rolling re-estimation. At first sight, it may be surprising that DRO–Markowitz often outperforms dynamic investment strategies in practice (as illustrated in
[
8
]
). As explained there, sophisticated dynamic decisions rely heavily on model assumptions; when these are violated, errors compound over time, undermining dynamic strategies—especially under unpredictable non-stationarities. By contrast, the Merton framework provides a principled way to exploit frequent trading—provided we handle model uncertainty in a way that does not induce excessive pessimism.

The robust control literature (DRC/DRMDP) places time-rectangular ambiguity on the data-generating process and derives policies via dynamic programming; see, e.g.,
[

Intuitively, in a one-dimensional drift-shift toy example, a rectangular adversary can depress the drift at each instant, compounding pessimism over time; by contrast, a prior-level ambiguity perturbs the drift distribution once. Nevertheless, rectangularity is widely used because it preserves time consistency and the dynamic-programming structure (Bellman equations), which confers strong tractability and algorithmic scalability—the very benefit delivered by the “replenishing” mechanism. Our design choice is different: instead of time-rectangular uncertainty, we adopt a
prior-level
ambiguity in a Bayesian Merton model. We place a single ambiguity set around the drift prior - primarily a Wasserstein ball, though KL balls are also covered. The volatility is assumed to be constant and therefore easy to estimate in continuous-time. This
distributionally robust Bayesian control
(DRBC) design reduces pessimism and preserves the learning structure of the Bayesian Merton formulation.

Technically, an important tractability lever is a minimax swap (Sion-type) that holds for broad prior-level ambiguity sets (including Wasserstein and KL). This swap allows us to evaluate, for any fixed prior in the ambiguity set, the optimal Bayesian Merton value and policy in closed form using the formulas of
[
24
]
. As a result, the DRBC game reduces to a constrained distributional optimization over the drift prior: we optimize a nonlinear functional of the prior that arises from Karatzas and Zhao
[
24
]

Even if the minimax swap is difficult to justify, one may start with the formulation in which the adversary moves first. While this is not the most natural formulation (because the adversary typically models an environment that occurs after the manager makes its decision), still, it may still be a pragmatic way to induce robustness while mitigate overconservative policies.

Optimizing over a Wasserstein ball is subtle here because the resulting objective is highly nonlinear in the prior; standard Wasserstein DRO tools do not apply off-the-shelf. We derive small-radius asymptotics for non-linear functionals and apply these results to the worst-case prior. We obtain a
constructive
approximation: an explicit asymptotically optimal pushforward perturbation of the nominal prior that realizes the first-order effect. Beyond optimization, we also address calibration: we select the ambiguity radius by general
nonlinear Wasserstein projections
. Then, we tailor these general results to the Merton functional, extending linear RWPI-style projection ideas to this nonlinear setting. We highlight that this nonlinear projection perspective may be of independent interest, given the broad and growing use of Wasserstein projections
[
36
,
14
]
.

We complement the theory with evidence in synthetic and real-data settings. In synthetic experiments, we simulate an environment consistent with Merton’s assumptions: volatilities are known (or well-estimated), while asset drifts are unknown but deterministic and time-varying, generated from sinusoidal bases spanning a wide range of oscillatory frequencies. We compare two empirical strategies to construct the nominal drift prior from data: (i) batched, disjoint time windows (e.g., days or weeks) that form an empirical prior from window-level average returns; and (ii) day-of-week aggregation within larger windows (e.g., averages of “Mondays,” “Tuesdays,” etc.). Perhaps surprisingly, the batched-window prior performs slightly better even under periodic drifts, and we adopt it in our real-data study. Across both synthetic and real data, DRBC exhibits reduced pessimism compared to DRC under matched radii and improves performance over myopic DRO–Markowitz when frequent rebalancing is possible.

Contributions.

Our main contributions are as follows.

•

Duality for prior-level ambiguity.
We prove a minimax swap for drift-prior ambiguity sets (Wasserstein and KL), reducing DRBC–Merton to optimizing a nonlinear functional of the prior while preserving closed-form evaluation via
[
24
]
.

•

Constructive worst-case prior sensitivity under Wasserstein and non-linear functionals.
For small ambiguity radii, we derive a first-order expansion of the robust objective and give an explicit asymptotically optimal push-forward perturbation of the nominal prior. These results are of independent interest since they are derived for the evaluation of worst-case non-linear functions of probabilities.

•

Calibration via nonlinear Wasserstein projection.
We introduce a projection-based, data-driven method to select the ambiguity radius tailored to the Merton functional, generalizing linear RWPI-style projections to a nonlinear manifold. Again, the results involve general nonlinear projections in Wasserstein geometry which are of independent interest.

•

Empirical validation and reduced pessimism.
Synthetic and real-data experiments demonstrate reduced over-conservatism relative to DRC and improved performance over myopic DRO–Markowitz under frequent rebalancing.

In the end, we emphasize that our contributions can still be interpreted within a Bayesian lens. What we offer is a systematic approach to infuse robustness and tractability in the choice of the prior, situating our framework within the scope of contemporary tools of distributionally robust decision making. From this perspective, the statistician may note that the prior’s choice may induce a bias that is relatively small as time increases. But this is not the environment we have in mind. In our setting, the investment horizon (which we denote as
T
) is fixed. This time horizon is long enough so that the manager may take advantage of multi-stage, even frequent, decisions but short enough that the volatility and drift are roughly constant. With this in mind, volatility can be set fixed and drift is unknown, so a Bayesian setting is natural but with a prior that requires robust calibration. This is precisely the mindset that motivates our development.

Throughout the rest of the paper, we will choose cost
function
c(u,v)=||v-u||_{2}^{2}
when
B
is compactly supported. The discussion of other choices of the cost functions will be discussed in supplementary materials.

To rigorously define the DRBC formulation, we need to make sure only the distribution of
B
is changed and all other problem structures (e.g., the adaptedness of the controls, the distribution of the other sources of randomness, independence structures, and integrability conditions) are kept the same.
To preserve the structure of the original partial observation problem, we keep track only of the joint law of
(B,W)
and maintain their independence. We place the model on the canonical product space
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{d}\times C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})
equipped with the Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}(\Omega)
, with canonical coordinates
(B,W)
and write
S=S(B,W)
for the price process. We let
\mathbb{P}_{W}
be the Wiener measure on the space
C\left([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)
and denote the prior distribution of
B
as
\mathbb{P}_{0}
(satisfying Assumption
2
). The observation filtration is fixed once and for all as

\mathcal{F}^{S}(t):=\sigma\big(S(u):0\leq u\leq t\big)\vee\mathcal{N},

where
\mathcal{N}
collects the null sets, and it does not depend on the choice of the nominal prior
\mathbb{P}_{0}
(nor on
\mu
in the ambiguity set). The reason is that the distributions of
Y(t;b)=bt+\sigma^{-1}W(t)
share the same sets of measure zero for each
b
in the canonical space. Admissible controls are processes that are progressively measurable with respect to
\{\mathcal{F}^{S}(t)\}_{t\in[0,T]}
and satisfy the usual integrability/positivity conditions. This setup ensures that robustness only changes the prior on
B
while leaving the information structure unchanged.

Now we define
\mathbb{P}:=\mathbb{P}_{0}\otimes\mathbb{P}_{W}
.
The Wasserstein uncertainty set with a radius
\delta>0
is defined as:

\mathcal{U}^{\text{OT}}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{0}):=\left\{\mathbb{Q}=\mu\otimes\mathbb{P}_{W}:D_{c}(\mu,\mathbb{P}_{0})\leq\delta\text{ and $\mu$ satisfies Assumption \ref{ass:compact_B}}\right\},

where we assume in the following that the prior measure and all adversarial measures have a compact support.
We now define the admissible controls by the stochastic process
\pi:[0,T]\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

•

(a)
\pi
is
\mathcal{F}^{S}
-progressively measurable with càdlàg paths.

•

(b) The SDE

dX(t)=rX(t)dt+\pi(t)^{\top}\left(B-r\mathbf{1}\right)dt+\pi(t)^{\top}\sigma dW(t)

admits a unique weak solution such that
X(t)>0
for any
t\in[0,T]
.

•

(c)
\int_{0}^{T}\|\pi(t)\|_{2}^{2}dt<\infty

\mathbb{P}
-a.s.

The collection of all admissible controls is denoted as
\mathcal{A}(x_{0})
for the case when
X(0)=x_{0}
. The DRBC problem is defined by

V(x_{0})=\sup_{\pi\in\mathcal{A}(x_{0})}\inf_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{U}^{\text{OT}}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{0})}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[U^{\pi}(X(T))\right]

(10)

and denote the optimal solution of Problem (
10
) as
\pi_{\text{DRBC}}
.

As discussed in
[
9
]
, Problem (
10
) is typically not directly tractable in its original form. To solve the problem,
[
9
]
discusses several techniques, such as discretizing the prior distribution in the context of KL-uncertainty sets or applying simulation techniques that may be time-consuming. However, exploiting the special structure of the Merton problem, under reasonable assumptions, we can derive the following theorem, which will significantly simplify approximating the solution to Problem (
10
).

Theorem 2

(Min-Max Equality)
.

Under Assumptions
1
and
2
, for any initial wealth
x_{0}>0
, the following min-max equality holds:

\sup_{\pi\in\mathcal{A}(x_{0})}\inf_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{U}^{\text{OT}}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{0})}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[U(X^{\pi}(T))]=\inf_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{U}^{\text{OT}}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{0})}\sup_{\pi\in\mathcal{A}(x_{0})}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[U(X^{\pi}(T))].

Proof.

We prove the theorem in four steps, leveraging the compact support of
B
and the structure of the utility function.

Step 1: Weak duality.
The inequality
\sup_{\pi}\inf_{\mathbb{Q}}\leq\inf_{\mathbb{Q}}\sup_{\pi}
holds by definition (weak duality). To upgrade the reverse inequality, we introduce the subset

\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(x_{0}):=\Big\{\pi\in\mathcal{A}(x_{0}):d(\pi,0)<\infty\Big\},\text{ where }d(\pi,\pi^{\prime}):=\sup_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{U}^{\text{OT}}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{0})}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\|\pi(t)-\pi^{\prime}(t)\|^{2}dt\right]\right)^{1/2},

which equips the class of
L^{2}
-admissible policies with the topology induced by the
L^{2}
-norm. As
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(x_{0})\subset\mathcal{A}(x_{0})
, we have

\sup_{\pi\in\mathcal{A}(x_{0})}\inf_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{U}^{\text{OT}}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{0})}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[U(X^{\pi}(T))]\;\geq\;\sup_{\pi\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(x_{0})}\inf_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{U}^{\text{OT}}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{0})}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[U(X^{\pi}(T))].

In the next steps, we establish the corresponding reverse inequality for
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(x_{0})
. The final step (discussed later) will verify that the optimizer obtained at the end of the analysis belongs to
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(x_{0})
, thereby closing the gap.

Step 2: Variation of constants and continuity in the model.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the compact support guaranteed by Assumption
2
, fix
\pi\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(x_{0})
, and note that the linear wealth dynamics yield the variation-of-constants formula

X^{\pi}(T;b,W)=x_{0}e^{rT}+e^{rT}\int_{0}^{T}e^{-rs}\pi(s)^{\top}(b-r\mathbf{1})\,ds+e^{rT}\int_{0}^{T}e^{-rs}\pi(s)^{\top}\sigma\,dW(s),

(11)

which is affine in
b
for every realization of
W
.
For
b\in K
, define

g_{\pi}(b):=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{W}}\left[U\left(X^{\pi}(T)\right)\mid B=b\right],

where the conditional expectation is taken only with respect to the Brownian motion
W
.
Equation (
11
) shows
b\mapsto X^{\pi}(T;b,W(\omega))
is continuous for every
\omega
, and therefore the same holds for
b\mapsto U(X^{\pi}(T;b,W(\omega)))
.

We now justify interchanging limit and expectation. Because
\pi\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(x_{0})
, there exists
M_{\pi}<\infty
such that
\sup_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{U}^{\text{OT}}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{0})}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\!\left[\int_{0}^{T}\|\pi(s)\|^{2}ds\right]\leq M_{\pi}.

Using the variation-of-constants formula (
11
), Jensen’s inequality, and Itô’s isometry, we obtain a constant
C_{\pi}>0
, independent of
b\in K
, such that

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\!\left[\left|X^{\pi}(T;b,W)\right|^{2+\varepsilon}\right]\leq C_{\pi}\qquad\text{for some }\varepsilon>0,

(12)

uniformly over
\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{U}^{\text{OT}}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{0})
and
b\in K
. The compactness of
K
ensures

\sup_{b\in K}\|b-r\mathbf{1}\|<\infty
, so the deterministic term in (
11
) is uniformly controlled in
b
, while the stochastic integral does not depend on
b
. Moreover, since the wealth dynamics are linear with bounded coefficients (in view of Assumption
2
), standard estimates for linear SDEs imply that, for any
m>0
, there exists
C_{\pi,m}<\infty
such that

\sup_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{U}^{\text{OT}}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{0})}\ \sup_{b\in K}\ \mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\!\left[\bigl(X^{\pi}(T;b,W)\bigr)^{-m}\right]\leq C_{\pi,m}.

(13)

In particular, combining (
12
) and (
13
) with the max-type growth in Assumption
1
, we may choose
p>0
and a constant
C_{U}>0
such that

|U(x)|\leq C_{U}\bigl(1+x^{p}+x^{-p}\bigr),\qquad x>0,

and hence there exists an integrable random variable
Y_{\pi}
(depending on
\pi
but not on
b
) with

|U\bigl(X^{\pi}(T;b,W)\bigr)|\leq Y_{\pi}\qquad\text{for all }b\in K,\ \mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{U}^{\text{OT}}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{0}).

Therefore, if
b_{n}\to b
in
K
, the pointwise continuity of
b\mapsto X^{\pi}(T;b,W)
and the dominated convergence theorem yield

g_{\pi}(b_{n})\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\!\left[U\bigl(X^{\pi}(T;b_{n},W)\bigr)\mid B=b_{n}\right]\;\longrightarrow\;g_{\pi}(b)\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\!\left[U\bigl(X^{\pi}(T;b,W)\bigr)\mid B=b\right].

Thus
g_{\pi}
is continuous on the compact set
K
.
Every
\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{U}^{\text{OT}}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{0})
shares the same law for
W
and has a marginal distribution of
B
supported in
K
. Hence, for fixed
\pi
,

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\!\left[U(X^{\pi}(T))\right]=\int_{K}g_{\pi}(b)\,\mathbb{Q}_{B}(db),

and the right-hand side depends continuously on
\mathbb{Q}_{B}
under the weak topology because
g_{\pi}
is continuous and bounded on
K
.
This proves the desired continuity in the model variable.

Step 3: Concavity and continuity in the control.

For fixed
\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{U}^{\text{OT}}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{0})
, the map

\pi\mapsto\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[U(X^{\pi}(T))]
is concave because
U
is concave
and the state equation is affine in
\pi
.

To show continuity on
(\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(x_{0}),d)
, let
\pi_{n}\to\pi
in the topology
induced by
d
, and set
\Delta\pi(t)=\pi_{n}(t)-\pi(t)
. Subtracting the
representations (
11
) for
\pi_{n}
and
\pi
, taking quadratic moments,
and using
(a_{1}+a_{2})^{2}\leq 2a_{1}^{2}+2a_{2}^{2}
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\!\big[|X^{\pi_{n}}(T)-X^{\pi}(T)|^{2}\big]\;\leq\;C\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\!\left[\int_{0}^{T}\|\Delta\pi(s)\|^{2}ds\right],

where
C=2e^{2rT}\Big(T\sup_{b\in K}\|b-r\mathbf{1}\|^{2}\;+\;\|\sigma\sigma^{\top}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\Big)

is independent of
\mathbb{Q}
. Since
d(\pi_{n},\pi)\to 0
, the right-hand side
converges to zero uniformly over
\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{U}^{\text{OT}}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{0})
.
Hence
X^{\pi_{n}}(T)\to X^{\pi}(T)\quad\text{in }L^{2}(\mathbb{Q})
(and thus in probability)
uniformly over
\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{U}^{\text{OT}}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{0})
.

Next we establish uniform integrability.
Because
\pi\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(x_{0})
and
d(\pi_{n},\pi)\to 0
, there exists

M<\infty
such that

\sup_{n\geq 1}\ \sup_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{U}^{\text{OT}}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{0})}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\!\left[\int_{0}^{T}\|\pi_{n}(s)\|^{2}ds\right]\leq M.

(14)

Indeed, for each
n
,

\int_{0}^{T}\|\pi_{n}(s)\|^{2}ds\;\leq\;2\int_{0}^{T}\|\pi(s)\|^{2}ds\;+\;2\int_{0}^{T}\|\Delta\pi(s)\|^{2}ds,

and the two terms on the right-hand side are uniformly bounded in
\mathbb{Q}
,
with the second one vanishing as
n\to\infty
by the definition of
d
.
Using (
14
), the compactness of
K
, and the linear
wealth dynamics (
11
), standard estimates for linear SDEs with bounded
coefficients yield the existence of
\varepsilon>0
and, for each
m>0
,
constants
C_{+,m},C_{-,m}<\infty
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{n\geq 1}\ \sup_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{U}^{\text{OT}}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{0})}\ \sup_{b\in K}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\!\big[|X^{\pi_{n}}(T;b,W)|^{2+\varepsilon}\big]

\displaystyle\leq C_{+,2+\varepsilon},

(15)

\displaystyle\sup_{n\geq 1}\ \sup_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{U}^{\text{OT}}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{0})}\ \sup_{b\in K}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\!\big[(X^{\pi_{n}}(T;b,W))^{-m}\big]

\displaystyle\leq C_{-,m}.

(16)

In particular, by the max-type growth in Assumption
1
, there
exist
p>0
and
C_{U}>0
such that

|U(x)|\leq C_{U}\big(1+x^{p}+x^{-p}\big),\qquad x>0,

and combining this with (
15
)–(
16
)
shows that the family
\{U(X^{\pi_{n}}(T)):n\geq 1\}
is uniformly integrable
under every
\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{U}^{\text{OT}}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{0})
.
Since
X^{\pi_{n}}(T)\to X^{\pi}(T)
in probability and
\{U(X^{\pi_{n}}(T))\}_{n\geq 1}

is uniformly integrable, the continuity of
U
implies

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[U(X^{\pi_{n}}(T))]\;\longrightarrow\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[U(X^{\pi}(T))]
,
\forall\,\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{U}^{\text{OT}}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{0}).

Thus, for each fixed
\mathbb{Q}
, the map

\pi\mapsto\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[U(X^{\pi}(T))]
is continuous on

(\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(x_{0}),d)
.

Step 4: Apply Sion’s min-max theorem on
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(x_{0})
.
The space
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(x_{0})
is convex because the wealth dynamics are linear in
\pi
, and
\mathcal{U}^{\text{OT}}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{0})
is weakly compact since each admissible
B
-marginal is supported in the compact set
K
and the Wasserstein/KL balls are weakly closed (Prokhorov’s theorem).
Moreover, the map
(\pi,\mathbb{Q})\mapsto\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[U(X^{\pi}(T))]
satisfies:

•

Concavity in
\pi
: For fixed
\mathbb{Q}
,
\pi\mapsto\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[U(X^{\pi}(T))]
is concave by the previous step.

•

Convexity in
\mathbb{Q}
: For fixed
\pi
,
\mathbb{Q}\mapsto\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[U(X^{\pi}(T))]
is linear (hence convex).

•

Continuity in
\pi
: For fixed
\mathbb{Q}
,
\pi\mapsto\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[U(X^{\pi}(T))]
is continuous on
(\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(x_{0}),d)
(Step 3).

•

Continuity in
\mathbb{Q}
: For fixed
\pi
,
\mathbb{Q}\mapsto\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[U(X^{\pi}(T))]
is continuous under the weak topology on
\mathcal{U}^{\text{OT}}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{0})
by the argument in Step 2.

By Sion’s min-max theorem applied to
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(x_{0})\times\mathcal{U}^{\text{OT}}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{0})
, we conclude:

\sup_{\pi\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(x_{0})}\inf_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{U}^{\text{OT}}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{0})}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[U(X^{\pi}(T))]=\inf_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{U}^{\text{OT}}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{0})}\sup_{\pi\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(x_{0})}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[U(X^{\pi}(T))].

Step 5: Return to
\mathcal{A}(x_{0})
.

Fix
b\in K
and consider the optimal policy
\pi^{*,b}
supplied by
Theorem
1
when the prior is the Dirac mass at
b
.
Writing
Y^{b}(t)=\sigma^{-1}(b-r\mathbf{1})t+W(t)
and

\displaystyle\Theta(t,y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}I\!\left(\frac{\mathcal{K}(x_{0})e^{-rT}}{F(T,z+y)}\right)\,\varphi_{T-t}(z)\,dz,

\displaystyle\Xi(t,y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\nabla_{z}F(T,z+y)}{F(T,z+y)^{2}}\,I^{\prime}\!\left(\frac{\mathcal{K}(x_{0})e^{-rT}}{F(T,z+y)}\right)\,\varphi_{T-t}(z)\,dz,

the optimal fraction from Theorem
1
reads

\frac{\pi^{*,b}(t)}{X^{*,b}(t)}=\left(\sigma^{\top}\right)^{-1}(-\mathcal{K}(x_{0})e^{-rT})\,\frac{\Xi(t,Y^{b}(t))}{\Theta(t,Y^{b}(t))}.

To bound the ratio, note that for any
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

F(T,u)=\int_{K}L_{T}(\tilde{b},u)\,\mu(d\tilde{b}),\qquad\nabla_{u}F(T,u)=\int_{K}\sigma^{-\top}(\tilde{b}-r\mathbf{1})L_{T}(\tilde{b},u)\,\mu(d\tilde{b}).

Because
K
is compact and
\sigma^{-\top}(\tilde{b}-r\mathbf{1})
is continuous in
\tilde{b}
,
there exists
C_{K}>0
such that

\left\|\frac{\nabla_{u}F(T,u)}{F(T,u)}\right\|\leq C_{K}\qquad\forall u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(17)

Moreover,
L_{T}(\tilde{b},u)
is bounded from above and below by two-sided exponentials in

\|u\|
, uniformly in
\tilde{b}\in K
, so there exists
c_{0}>0
with

c_{0}^{-1}e^{-c_{0}\|u\|}\;\leq\;F(T,u)\;\leq\;c_{0}e^{c_{0}\|u\|},\qquad\left|\frac{1}{F(T,u)}\right|\leq c_{0}e^{c_{0}\|u\|}.

(18)

Recall that
Assumption
1
guarantees that for some
C_{I},p_{I}>0
and
c_{I}>0
,

|I(x)|+|I^{\prime}(x)|\;\leq\;C_{I}\bigl(1+x^{p_{I}}+x^{-p_{I}}\bigr),\qquad I(x)\;\geq\;c_{I}x^{-p_{I}},\qquad x>0.

(19)

Applying (
19
) to

x=\mathcal{K}(x_{0})e^{-rT}/F(T,z+y)

and using (
18
) yields exponential bounds

\Big|I\!\left(\tfrac{\mathcal{K}(x_{0})e^{-rT}}{F(T,z+y)}\right)\Big|+\Big|I^{\prime}\!\left(\tfrac{\mathcal{K}(x_{0})e^{-rT}}{F(T,z+y)}\right)\Big|\;\leq\;C^{\prime}e^{c^{\prime}\|z+y\|},

and crucially, the
lower bound

\Theta(t,y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}I\!\left(\tfrac{\mathcal{K}(x_{0})e^{-rT}}{F(T,z+y)}\right)\varphi_{T-t}(z)\,dz\;\geq\;c^{\prime}\,\exp\!\bigl(-c^{\prime\prime}\|y\|\bigr)

(20)

for suitable constants
c^{\prime},c^{\prime\prime}>0
(using
I(x)\geq c_{I}x^{-p_{I}}
and the Gaussian tail of
\varphi_{T-t}
).

Similarly,

|\Xi(t,y)|\leq C_{2}e^{c_{2}\|y\|}

for constants
C_{2},c_{2}>0
.

Combining these,

\left\|\frac{\Xi(t,y)}{\Theta(t,y)}\right\|\;\leq\;a\,e^{c\|y\|}\qquad(t,y)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Because
Y^{b}
has continuous paths,

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|Y^{b}(t)\|<\infty

a.s. Since
Y^{b}
is a Brownian motion with bounded drift,

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|Y^{b}(t)\|

admits finite exponential moments. Therefore,

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left\|\frac{\pi^{*,b}(t)}{X^{*,b}(t)}\right\|\;\leq\;a\exp\!\left(c\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|Y^{b}(t)\|\right)<\infty\qquad\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.}

Finally,
X^{*,b}
has continuous paths and is strictly positive, so

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|\pi^{*,b}(t)\|<\infty

a.s., and hence

\int_{0}^{T}\|\pi^{*,b}(t)\|^{2}dt<\infty

a.s.
The constants above are uniform in
b\in K
, and thus for every

\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{U}_{\delta}^{\mathrm{OT}}(\mathbb{P}_{0})
,

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{T}\|\pi^{*}(t)\|^{2}dt\right)^{1/2}\right]<\infty.

This proves
\pi^{*}\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(x_{0})\subset\mathcal{A}(x_{0})
.
Together with the inequalities at the beginning of the proof, this yields the
desired min–max equality on
\mathcal{A}(x_{0})
.

∎

The value of Theorem
2
is that for a fixed probability measure
\mathbb{Q}
, by Theorem
1
, the inner problem
\sup_{\pi\in\mathcal{A}(x_{0})}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[U(X(T))\right]
has a closed-form solution in terms of the distribution of the drift
B
.
In other words, the DRBC problem is equivalent to solving a constrained distributional optimization problem

\displaystyle\inf_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{U}^{\text{OT}}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{0})}\sup_{\pi\in\mathcal{A}(x_{0})}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[U(X^{\pi}(T))\right]=\inf_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{U}^{\text{OT}}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{0})}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}F(T,z)U\left(I\left(\frac{\mathcal{K}(x_{0})e^{-rT}}{F(T,z)}\right)\right)\varphi_{T}(z)dz,

(21)

where

\mathcal{U}^{\text{OT}}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{0})

is the uncertainty set that is only concerned with the distribution of
B
since the objective function in (
21
) no longer contains the Brownian motion
W
. Hence, it suffices to optimize over the nonlinear functional and obtain the extreme probability measure
\mathbb{Q}^{*}
(denote the functional on the right-hand side of Eq. (
21
) by a specific
J(\mathbb{Q})
; see Corollary
1
for details):

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}^{*}=\arg\min_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{U}^{\text{OT}}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{0})}{J}(\mathbb{Q}).

(22)

We will first give a sensitivity analysis of this distributional optimization problem in greater generality for
J
, and then Problem (
22
) can be solved easily in an approximate sense by the following Corollary
1
.

Theorem 3

(Nonlinear optimal perturbations)
.

Fix
1<q<\infty
and let
p
be its Hölder conjugate.
Let
\|\cdot\|_{q}
be a norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\|\cdot\|_{p}
be its dual norm.
Consider the quadratic transport cost
c(x^{\prime}-x):=\tau\,\|x^{\prime}-x\|_{q}^{2}
with
\tau>0

and let
D_{c}
denote the corresponding optimal-transport divergence

D_{c}(\mu,\nu):=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\big[c(X^{\prime}-X)\big].

For
\delta>0
, set
V_{\delta}:=\sup_{D_{c}(\mu,\nu)\leq\delta}J(\mu)
, where the functional

J:\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies:

(i)

For all
\mu
in a neighbourhood of
\nu
, there exists a measurable

J^{\prime}_{\mu}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, differentiable in
x
with gradient
\nabla J^{\prime}_{\mu}(x)
,
such that for every coupling
\pi
of
(X^{\prime},X)\sim(\mu,\nu)
and

\nu_{t}:=(1-t)\nu+t\mu
,

J(\mu)-J(\nu)=\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\big[J^{\prime}_{\nu_{t}}(X^{\prime})-J^{\prime}_{\nu_{t}}(X)\big]\,dt.

(ii)

(Regularity and growth of the Wasserstein gradient)
The map
(\mu,x)\mapsto\nabla J^{\prime}_{\mu}(x)
is jointly continuous in the product
topology (weak topology on
\mu
, Euclidean on
x
) in a neighbourhood
of
(\nu,\cdot)
.
Moreover, there exist
1\leq r\leq 2
and
C>0
such that for all such
\mu
and all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\|\nabla J^{\prime}_{\mu}(x)\|_{p}\leq C\big(1+\|x\|^{r-1}\big),

and the reference law
\nu
has finite
2r
-moment:

\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\|X\|^{2r}<\infty.

Let
g(x):=\nabla J^{\prime}_{\nu}(x)
and assume

\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\|g(X)\|_{p}^{2}<\infty
.
Then, as
\delta\downarrow 0
,

V_{\delta}=J(\nu)+\sqrt{\frac{\delta}{\tau}}\,\Big(\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\big[\|g(X)\|_{p}^{2}\big]\Big)^{1/2}+o(\sqrt{\delta}).

Moreover, there exists an asymptotically optimal Monge-type perturbation

T_{\delta}
of the form

T_{\delta}(X)=X+\sqrt{\frac{\delta}{\tau}}\,\bar{\Delta}(X)\,\big(1+o_{p}(1)\big),

where for each
x
with
g(x)\neq 0
, we choose

u(x)\in\arg\max_{\|u\|_{q}=1}g(x)\cdot u,

and define

Step 1: Coupling class

Spatial term.

For any

Step 4: Lower bound via a deterministic extremal Monge map.

For each

and hence
\pi_{\delta}\in\mathcal{C}_{\delta}

\mu_{\delta}

\liminf_{\delta\downarrow 0}\frac{V_{\delta}-J(\nu)}{\sqrt{\delta}}\geq\sqrt{\frac{1}{\tau}}\,K.

Combining the upper bound from Step 3 with the lower bound from Step 4,

\sqrt{\frac{1}{\tau}}\,K\leq\liminf_{\delta\downarrow 0}\frac{V_{\delta}-J(\nu)}{\sqrt{\delta}}\leq\limsup_{\delta\downarrow 0}\frac{V_{\delta}-J(\nu)}{\sqrt{\delta}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{\tau}}\,K.

and the Monge map

and of the stated form.

Corollary 1

(Asymptotic non-linear optimal perturbation under Wasserstein distance)

Suppose Assumptions
1
and
2

J(\mathbb{Q})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}F_{\mathbb{Q}}(T,z)\,U\!\left(I\!\left(\frac{\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{Q}}(x_{0})e^{-rT}}{F_{\mathbb{Q}}(T,z)}\right)\right)\varphi_{T}(z)\,dz,\qquad\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{OT}}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{0}),

and the uncertainty set

Define the influence function

is the unique solution to the budget constraint under
\mathbb{P}_{0}

\|H\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{0})}:=\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}\big[\|H(B)\|_{2}^{2}\big]\right)^{1/2}.

Then, as
\delta\to 0
, an asymptotically optimal adversarial perturbation is given by the pushforward
\mathbb{Q}^{*}_{\delta}=(I+\Delta^{*}_{\delta})_{\#}\mathbb{P}_{0}
, where

\Delta_{\delta}^{*}(b)=-\sqrt{\delta}\,\frac{H(b)}{\|H\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{0})}}+o(\sqrt{\delta})\quad\text{in }L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{0}).

Furthermore, the corresponding asymptotically optimal value is

\inf_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{OT}}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{0})}J(\mathbb{Q})=J(\mathbb{P}_{0})-\sqrt{\delta}\,\|H\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{0})}+o(\sqrt{\delta}).

We show that
J
satisfies the assumptions of Theorem
3

with

, and then apply the theorem

Consider the perturbation

and write

Remark 2

(Connection to Dual Problem)
.

The conjugate function
I(y)=(U^{\prime})^{-1}(y)
appears naturally in the dual formulation of the robust optimization problem. In particular, the optimal density tilt under
KL divergence is proportional to
I(\lambda L_{T}(b,y))
for some Lagrange multiplier
\lambda
. However, under Wasserstein distance and compact support, we do not need to solve the dual — the first-order condition suffices.

If
\mathbb{P}_{0}=\mathbb{P}_{n}
for some empirical measure, then we can compute
\Delta^{*}
by replacing
\mathbb{P}_{0}
by
\mathbb{P}_{n}
conditioned on these samples, rather than viewing them as random measures. We are going to construct i.i.d. samples of
B^{(k)}
in Section
4
, and then we construct the optimal measure for the original problem via the perturbed empirical measure

\mathbb{P}_{n}(dx)\;=\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\delta_{C^{(k)}}(dx),

where for each
k
,
C^{(k)}=B^{(k)}+\Delta^{*}
. Therefore, this gives a way of solving the Wasserstein constrained distributional optimization problem when
\delta>0
is small.

4
Data-Driven Formulation and Choice of Model Parameters

In this section, we first describe the data-driven version of the DRBC formulation of the stochastic control problem since it is natural to inform the choice of the prior
\mathbb{P}_{0}
from the data, and an appropriate empirical measure is the natural candidate. Next, we provide the prescription to choose an uncertainty radius
\delta
using an asymptotically optimal (as data collected increases) approach. The idea is that this choice should also be based on observed data. We calibrate the distributional ambiguity set to the smallest size that makes the oracle-optimal portfolio (i.e., the one chosen if the true distribution were known) statistically plausible at the desired confidence level. The prescription is then obtained from an asymptotical statistical result with projecting Wasserstein distance on a nonlinear manifold.

Recall that we assume each asset follows a geometric Brownian motion with random drift:

\frac{dS_{i}(t)}{S_{i}(t)}\;=\;B_{i}\,dt\;+\;\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sigma_{ij}\,dW_{j}(t),\qquad i=1,\dots,d.

Therefore, we have the explicit closed-form formula

\log S_{i}(t)-\log S_{i}(0)=\Big(B_{i}-\tfrac{1}{2}\|\sigma_{i\cdot}\|^{2}\Big)\,t\;+\;\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sigma_{ij}\,W_{j}(t),

where
\|\sigma_{i\cdot}\|^{2}:=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sigma_{ij}^{2}
.

We adopt a data-driven approximation in which the ground-truth distribution of
B
is fixed, but at the beginning of the
k
-th nonoverlapping window of length
\tilde{t}
, the realization
B^{(k)}
of return vectors is redrawn i.i.d. from such ground-truth distribution and constant during the window. Within the
k
-th window, we observe prices of the stock
S
on a grid
0,h,2h,\dots,mh=\tilde{t}
.

Asset
i
in window
k
has return
B_{i}^{(k)}
. An unbiased estimator of
B_{i}^{(k)}
(conditional on
B^{(k)}
) is obtained from the endpoint log-return:

\widehat{B}^{(k)}_{i}\;=\;\frac{\log S_{i}^{(k)}(\tilde{t})-\log S_{i}^{(k)}(0)}{\tilde{t}}\;+\;\tfrac{1}{2}\|\sigma_{i\cdot}\|^{2},\qquad\mathbb{E}\!\left[\widehat{B}^{(k)}_{i}\,\middle|\,B^{(k)}\right]=B^{(k)}_{i}.

Equivalently, averaging one-step log-returns over the window yields

\widehat{B}^{(k)}_{i}\;=\;\frac{1}{mh}\sum_{\ell=0}^{m-1}\log\!\left(\frac{S_{i}^{(k)}((\ell+1)h)}{S_{i}^{(k)}(\ell h)}\right)\;+\;\tfrac{1}{2}\|\sigma_{i\cdot}\|^{2}.

Collecting
k\in\{1,...,n\}
windows yields i.i.d. estimates
B^{(1)},\dots,B^{(n)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We are abusing notation here because the estimates that we obtained are not exactly the realized
B^{(k)}
’s but noisy versions. Strictly speaking, we should apply a deconvolution method, which we did but the results did not change significantly and this approach is much easier to implement. The distributional robustness should absorb the noise that is still present in our estimate of
B^{(k)}
and in case
\tilde{t}
is hard to estimate. So, ultimately, we take the nominal prior as the empirical measure

\mathbb{P}_{n}(dx)\;=\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\delta_{B^{(k)}}(dx).

The choice of the key parameter
\delta
is crucial. If
\delta
is too large, there is too much model ambiguity, and the available data becomes less relevant. If
\delta
is too small, the effect of robustification is negligible. Therefore, the choice of
\delta
should not be exogenously defined; rather, it should be endogenously informed by the data. Before presenting the methodology, we first present some technical assumptions. We denote
k^{*}=\mathcal{K}(x_{0})
as the optimal Lagrangian multiplier in Eq. (
8
).

In order to choose an appropriate
\delta=\delta_{n}
, here we follow the idea behind the RWPI approach introduced in
[
11
]
. Intuitively,
\delta
should be chosen such that the set
\mathcal{U}^{\text{OT}}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{n})
contains all the probability measures that are plausible variations of the data represented by
\mathbb{P}_{n}
.
According to Theorem
1
and the assumption that the real underlying ground-truth is
\mathbb{P}^{*}
, we have that the formula for such an optimal policy is unique with a Lagrangian multiplier
k^{*}
as a pre-committed strategy. We restate here (real optimal policy
\pi^{*}
and
X^{*}(T)
has one-to-one correspondence)

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}\left[\hat{\Lambda}(T)X^{*}(T)\right]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}I\left(\frac{k^{*}e^{-rT}}{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}\left[L_{T}(B,y)\right]}\right)\varphi_{T}(y)dy=x_{0}e^{rT}.

We can see that the optimal policy
\pi^{*}
and the Lagrangian multiplier
k^{*}
also have a one-to-one correspondence. Thus, without loss of generality, we assume
k>0
is the decision variable and choose the optimal
\delta
based on whether the optimal
k^{*}
is covered. Similar to the notation in
[
11
]
, we define

\mathcal{F}_{k}=\left\{\mathbb{P}:\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}I\left(\frac{ke^{-rT}}{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[L_{T}(B,y)\right]}\right)\varphi_{T}(y)dy=x_{0}e^{rT}\right\}.

Now, for a fixed
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{U}^{\text{OT}}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{n})
, the set
\left\{k>0:\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{F}_{k}\right\}
contains all parameter choices that are optimal from the decision maker’s point of view. This motivates the definition of the following set

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{n})

\displaystyle=\left\{k>0:\mathcal{F}_{k}\cap\mathcal{U}^{\text{OT}}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{n})\neq\varnothing\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{k>0:\text{there exists }\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{U}^{\text{OT}}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{n})\text{ such that }\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}I\left(\frac{ke^{-rT}}{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[L_{T}(B,y)\right]}\right)\varphi_{T}(y)dy=x_{0}e^{rT}\right\},

which corresponds to all the plausible estimates of
k^{*}
. Thus, it includes all the parameter choices that are collected by the decision maker as optimal for some distribution in the uncertainty set. As a result,
\Lambda_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{n})
is a natural confidence region for
k^{*}
. Therefore,
\delta>0
should be chosen as the smallest
\delta^{*}_{n}
such that
k^{*}
belongs to this region with a given confidence interval. Namely,

\delta^{*}_{n}=\min\left\{\delta>0:\mathbb{P}^{*}\left(k^{*}\in\Lambda_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{n})\right)\geq 1-\delta_{0}\right\},

where
\delta_{0}
is the user-defined confidence level (typically
\delta_{0}=0.05
).

However, by the mere definition, it is hard to compute
\delta^{*}_{n}
. We now provide a simpler representation for
\delta^{*}_{n}
via an auxiliary function called the robust Wasserstein profile (RWP) function:

\displaystyle R_{n}(k)

\displaystyle:=\inf_{\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{F}_{k}}D_{c}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{P}_{n})

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{D_{c}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{P}_{n}):\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}I\left(\frac{ke^{-rT}}{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[L_{T}(B,y)\right]}\right)\varphi_{T}(y)dy=x_{0}e^{rT}\right\}.

Compared with the linear projection in
[
11
]
, here the RWP function is defined as a projection of the empirical measure to a nonlinear manifold, so the behavior of the nonlinear RWP function is much complicated.

In the following theorem, we derive the nonlinear projection asymptotics with great generality, and the specific choice of the uncertainty radius can be obtained by a corollary.

Theorem 4

(Non-linear projection asymptotics)
.

Let
\nu
be a probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and let

\kappa:\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
be a functional with
\kappa(\nu)=0
.
Assume that
\kappa
admits a first variation
\kappa^{\prime}_{\mu}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}

such that:

•

For any probability measure
\mu
and any coupling

(X^{\prime},X)\sim(\mu,\nu)
, with the linear interpolation

\nu_{t}:=(1-t)\nu+t\mu
, we have

\kappa(\mu)-\kappa(\nu)=\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\kappa^{\prime}_{\nu_{t}}(X^{\prime})-\kappa^{\prime}_{\nu_{t}}(X)\right]\,dt.

•

For
\mu
in a neighborhood of
\nu
, the map

x\mapsto\kappa^{\prime}_{\mu}(x)
is
C^{1}
, and its gradient

g_{\mu}(x):=\nabla_{x}\kappa^{\prime}_{\mu}(x)
is jointly continuous
in
(\mu,x)
near
(\nu,\cdot)
. Moreover, there exists
an envelope
G\in L^{2}(\nu)
such that

\|g_{\mu}(x)\|_{2}\leq G(x)\quad\text{for all $\mu$ in a neighborhood of $\nu$ and all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

and

g_{\nu}\in L^{2}(\nu;\mathbb{R}^{d}),\qquad\|g_{\nu}\|_{L^{2}(\nu)}>0.

Let
c:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty]
be a transport cost satisfying:

(i)

There exist
\tau>0
,
r_{0}>0

and a function
\eta:(0,r_{0}]\to[0,\infty)
with
\eta(r)\to 0
as
r\to 0
such that

\bigl|c(x,y)-\tau\|x-y\|_{2}^{2}\bigr|\;\leq\;\eta(\|x-y\|_{2})\,\|x-y\|_{2}^{2}\qquad\text{whenever }\|x-y\|_{2}\leq r_{0}.

(29)

(ii)

There exist constants
C_{1}>0
,
C_{2}\geq 0
,
R>0

such that

c(x,y)\;\geq\;C_{1}\|x-y\|_{2}^{2}-C_{2}\qquad\text{whenever }\|x-y\|_{2}\geq R.

Let

D_{c}(\nu,\mu):=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\nu,\mu)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}c(x,y)\,\pi(dx,dy)

denote the optimal transport divergence induced by
c
. For
z
in a neighborhood
of
0
, define the projection cost

R(z):=\inf_{\mu:\kappa(\mu)=z}D_{c}(\nu,\mu).

Then, as
z\to 0
,

R(z)=\frac{\tau z^{2}}{\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\|g_{\nu}(X)\|_{2}^{2}]}+o(z^{2})=\frac{\tau z^{2}}{\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\|\nabla\kappa^{\prime}_{\nu}(X)\|_{2}^{2}]}+o(z^{2}).

Moreover, there exists an asymptotically optimal Monge-type perturbation
of the form

T_{z}(x)=x+\Delta_{z}(x),\qquad\Delta_{z}(x)=\frac{z}{\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\|g_{\nu}(X)\|_{2}^{2}]}\,g_{\nu}(x)+o(z)\quad\text{in }L^{2}(\nu),

which satisfies
\kappa(T_{z\#}\nu)=z+o(z)
and attains the above
cost up to
o(z^{2})
.

Proof.

Fix
\nu
with
\kappa(\nu)=0
. For
z
small, we seek
\mu
such that

\kappa(\mu)=z
and
D_{c}(\nu,\mu)
is minimal.

Step 1: Linearization of the constraint.

By the assumptions on the first variation and the regularity of

\kappa^{\prime}_{\mu}
, one obtains that
\kappa
is
W_{2}
–differentiable
at
\nu
with derivative
g_{\nu}
. In particular, for any small

\Delta\in L^{2}(\nu)
, letting
\mu=(I+\Delta)_{\#}\nu
,

\kappa(\mu)-\kappa(\nu)=\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\!\left[\langle g_{\nu}(X),\Delta(X)\rangle\right]+o\bigl(\|\Delta\|_{L^{2}(\nu)}\bigr).

Imposing the constraint
\kappa(\mu)=z
and recalling
\kappa(\nu)=0
, we
obtain

\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\!\left[\langle g_{\nu}(X),\Delta(X)\rangle\right]=z+o\bigl(\|\Delta\|_{L^{2}(\nu)}\bigr).

(30)

In particular, any admissible perturbation satisfies

\|\Delta\|_{L^{2}(\nu)}=O(|z|)
as
z\to 0
.

Step 2: Quadratic approximation of the cost.

For
\mu=(I+\Delta)_{\#}\nu
, consider the coupling

(X^{\prime},X)=(X+\Delta(X),X)
with
X\sim\nu
. Then

D_{c}(\nu,\mu)\leq\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\!\big[c\big(X,X+\Delta(X)\big)\big].

By the uniform local expansion (
29
),

c\big(X,X+\Delta(X)\big)=\tau\|\Delta(X)\|_{2}^{2}+r(\Delta(X)),

where

|r(h)|\leq\eta(\|h\|_{2})\,\|h\|_{2}^{2},\qquad\eta(r)\to 0\text{ as }r\to 0.

Since
\|\Delta\|_{L^{2}(\nu)}=O(|z|)
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\|\Delta(X)\|_{2}^{2}]=O(z^{2})
and
\|\Delta(X)\|\to 0
in probability.
Using the uniform bound, we obtain

\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\,|r(\Delta(X))|\,]\;\leq\;\left(\sup_{\|h\|\leq r_{0}}\eta(\|h\|)\right)\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\|\Delta(X)\|_{2}^{2}=o\bigl(\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\|\Delta(X)\|_{2}^{2}\bigr)=o(z^{2}).

Thus

D_{c}(\nu,\mu)\leq\tau\,\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\|\Delta(X)\|_{2}^{2}+o(z^{2}).

(31)

Next, we prove the lower bound.
Let
\mu
satisfy
\kappa(\mu)=z
, and let
\pi
be an optimal coupling
between
\nu
and
\mu
, with
(X,Y)\sim\pi
and displacement
D:=Y-X
.
By the first-variation representation and the envelope bound on
g_{\mu}
,

z=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\!\left[\langle g_{\nu}(X),D\rangle\right]+o\bigl(\|D\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}\bigr).

Hence
\|D\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}=O(|z|)
.

By the local expansion (
29
), for
\|D\|\leq r_{0}
,

c(X,Y)=\tau\|D\|_{2}^{2}+r(D),\qquad|r(D)|\leq\eta(\|D\|)\|D\|_{2}^{2},

while for
\|D\|>r_{0}
, the quadratic coercivity (ii) yields

c(X,Y)\;\geq\;C_{1}\|D\|_{2}^{2}-C_{2}.

As
\|D\|_{L^{2}}=O(|z|)
, the region
\{\|D\|>r_{0}\}
has probability

O(z^{2})
by Markov’s inequality. Combining these bounds,

D_{c}(\nu,\mu)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[c(X,Y)]\;\geq\;\tau\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\|D\|_{2}^{2}-o\!\bigl(\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\|D\|_{2}^{2}\bigr)=\tau\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\|D\|_{2}^{2}+o(z^{2}).

Next define the conditional mean displacement

\Delta(x):=\mathbb{E}[D\mid X=x]
. Then

\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\langle g_{\nu}(X),D\rangle=\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\langle g_{\nu}(X),\Delta(X)\rangle,\qquad\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\|D\|_{2}^{2}\;\geq\;\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\|\Delta(X)\|_{2}^{2}.

Thus every admissible
\mu
induces a perturbation

\Delta\in L^{2}(\nu)
with

\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\langle g_{\nu}(X),\Delta(X)\rangle=z+o(z),\qquad D_{c}(\nu,\mu)\geq\tau\,\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\|\Delta(X)\|_{2}^{2}+o(z^{2}).

Hence we conclude that
for admissible
\mu
,

D_{c}(\nu,\mu)=\tau\,\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\|\Delta(X)\|_{2}^{2}+o(z^{2}).

Step 3: Solving the quadratic optimization problem.

The leading-order problem is therefore

\inf_{\Delta\in L^{2}(\nu;\mathbb{R}^{d})}\Bigl\{\tau\,\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\|\Delta(X)\|_{2}^{2}]:\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\langle g_{\nu}(X),\Delta(X)\rangle]=z\Bigr\},

where we may ignore the
o(\|\Delta\|_{L^{2}})
term in
(
30
) at order
z^{2}
. This is a convex quadratic
optimization with a single linear constraint in the Hilbert space

L^{2}(\nu;\mathbb{R}^{d})
. The Lagrangian is

\mathcal{L}(\Delta,\lambda)=\tau\,\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\|\Delta(X)\|_{2}^{2}]+\lambda\Bigl(z-\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\langle g_{\nu}(X),\Delta(X)\rangle]\Bigr).

Taking the variational derivative with respect to
\Delta
and setting
it to zero gives, for
\nu
-a.e.
x
,

2\tau\,\Delta(x)-\lambda g_{\nu}(x)=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\Delta(x)=\frac{\lambda}{2\tau}\,g_{\nu}(x).

Substituting into the constraint,

\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\!\left[\Big\langle g_{\nu}(X),\frac{\lambda}{2\tau}g_{\nu}(X)\Big\rangle\right]=\frac{\lambda}{2\tau}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\|g_{\nu}(X)\|_{2}^{2}]=z,

so

\lambda=\frac{2\tau z}{\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\|g_{\nu}(X)\|_{2}^{2}]}.

Thus the optimal perturbation at leading order is

\Delta_{z}(x)=\frac{\lambda}{2\tau}\,g_{\nu}(x)=\frac{z}{\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\|g_{\nu}(X)\|_{2}^{2}]}\,g_{\nu}(x).

(32)

Clearly
\|\Delta_{z}\|_{L^{2}(\nu)}=O(|z|)
, so the linearization error in
(
30
) is
o(|z|)
, and

\kappa((I+\Delta_{z})_{\#}\nu)=z+o(z)
.

Step 4: Computing the minimal cost.

Plugging (
32
) into the quadratic cost term,

\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\|\Delta_{z}(X)\|_{2}^{2}]=\frac{z^{2}}{\bigl(\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\|g_{\nu}(X)\|_{2}^{2}]\bigr)^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\|g_{\nu}(X)\|_{2}^{2}]=\frac{z^{2}}{\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\|g_{\nu}(X)\|_{2}^{2}]}.

Therefore,

R(z)=\tau\,\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\|\Delta_{z}(X)\|_{2}^{2}]+o(z^{2})=\frac{\tau z^{2}}{\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\|g_{\nu}(X)\|_{2}^{2}]}+o(z^{2}),

which is the desired expansion. The Monge map
T_{z}(x)=x+\Delta_{z}(x)

is asymptotically optimal and satisfies
\kappa(T_{z\#}\nu)=z+o(z)
,
as claimed.
∎

In the following corollary, we provide an asymptotic result
nR_{n}(k^{*})\Rightarrow\Upsilon
, so that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}^{*}\left(R_{n}(k^{*})\leq\frac{\eta_{1-\delta_{0}}}{n}\right)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}^{*}\left(nR_{n}(k^{*})\leq\eta_{1-\delta_{0}}\right)=\mathbb{P}^{*}\left(\Upsilon\leq\eta_{1-\delta_{0}}\right)=1-\delta_{0},

where we define
\eta_{1-\delta_{0}}
is the
(1-\delta_{0})
-quantile of
\Upsilon
.

Corollary 2
.

Suppose Assumption
1
and
2
hold. Define the RWP function for the Merton budget constraint as

R_{n}(k)=\inf\left\{D_{c}(\mathbb{P}_{n},\mathbb{P}):\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}I\left(\frac{ke^{-rT}}{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[L_{T}(B,y)]}\right)\varphi_{T}(y)dy=x_{0}e^{rT}\right\}.

Then with the cost function
c(x,y)=||x-y||_{2}^{2}
,

nR_{n}(k^{*})\Rightarrow\Upsilon,\quad\text{as }n\to\infty,

where
\Upsilon
is a non-negative random variable given by

\Upsilon=\frac{Z^{2}}{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}[\|\nabla\kappa^{\prime}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}(B)\|_{2}^{2}]},

(33)

with
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
and

\sigma^{2}=\text{Var}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g^{\prime}(F(y))L_{T}(B,y)\varphi_{T}(y)dy\right),

(34)

where
F(y)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}[L_{T}(B,y)]
,

g^{\prime}(F)=-I^{\prime}\!\left(\frac{k^{*}e^{-rT}}{F}\right)\frac{k^{*}e^{-rT}}{F^{2}},

and

\nabla\kappa^{\prime}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}(b)=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}I^{\prime}\left(\frac{k^{*}e^{-rT}}{F(y)}\right)\cdot\frac{k^{*}e^{-rT}}{F(y)^{2}}\cdot\nabla_{b}L_{T}(b,y)\varphi_{T}(y)dy.

Proof.

We apply Theorem
4
to the DRBC Merton setting.
Recall

\kappa_{k}(\mathbb{P})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}I\!\left(\frac{ke^{-rT}}{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[L_{T}(B,y)]}\right)\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy-x_{0}e^{rT},\qquad\mathcal{F}_{k}=\{\mathbb{P}:\kappa_{k}(\mathbb{P})=0\},

and

R_{n}(k)=\inf\{D_{c}(\mathbb{P}_{n},\mathbb{P}):\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{F}_{k}\}.

Let
\mathbb{P}^{*}
be the true model and
k^{*}
the associated multiplier with

\kappa_{k^{*}}(\mathbb{P}^{*})=0
.

Step 1: Wasserstein derivative of
\kappa_{k^{*}}
.

As in Corollary 1, consider
\mathbb{P}^{\epsilon}=(1-\epsilon)\mathbb{P}^{*}+\epsilon\delta_{b}
and write

F(y)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}[L_{T}(B,y)]
.
Differentiating under the integral (justified by Assumptions
1
–
2
) yields

\frac{\delta\kappa_{k^{*}}}{\delta\mathbb{P}^{*}}(b)=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}I^{\prime}\!\left(\frac{k^{*}e^{-rT}}{F(y)}\right)\frac{k^{*}e^{-rT}}{F(y)^{2}}L_{T}(b,y)\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy,

and therefore the Wasserstein gradient is

\nabla\kappa^{\prime}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}(b)=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}I^{\prime}\!\left(\frac{k^{*}e^{-rT}}{F(y)}\right)\frac{k^{*}e^{-rT}}{F(y)^{2}}\,\nabla_{b}L_{T}(b,y)\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy.

(35)

Step 2: Linearization and CLT for
\kappa_{k^{*}}(\mathbb{P}_{n})
.

Since
\mathbb{P}\mapsto\kappa_{k^{*}}(\mathbb{P})
is Wasserstein differentiable at
\mathbb{P}^{*}
, the
functional delta method gives

\sqrt{n}\big(\kappa_{k^{*}}(\mathbb{P}_{n})-\kappa_{k^{*}}(\mathbb{P}^{*})\big)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\delta\kappa_{k^{*}}}{\delta\mathbb{P}^{*}}(B^{(i)})+o_{P}(1)\Rightarrow Z,

where
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
with

\sigma^{2}=\text{Var}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}\!\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g^{\prime}(F(y))\,L_{T}(B,y)\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy\right),\qquad g^{\prime}(F)=-I^{\prime}\!\left(\frac{k^{*}e^{-rT}}{F}\right)\frac{k^{*}e^{-rT}}{F^{2}}.

Write

z_{n}:=\kappa_{k^{*}}(\mathbb{P}_{n})=\frac{Z}{\sqrt{n}}+o_{P}(1/\sqrt{n}).

Step 3: Application of the local projection law.

Theorem
4
gives, for any
\nu
near
\mathbb{P}^{*}
with
\kappa_{k^{*}}(\nu)=z
,

R(z)=\frac{z^{2}}{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}\!\big[\|\nabla\kappa^{\prime}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}(B)\|_{2}^{2}\big]}+o(z^{2}).

Taking
\nu=\mathbb{P}_{n}
and substituting
z_{n}
,

nR_{n}(k^{*})=nR(z_{n})=\frac{Z^{2}}{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}\!\big[\|\nabla\kappa^{\prime}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}(B)\|_{2}^{2}\big]}+o_{P}(1),

hence

nR_{n}(k^{*})\Rightarrow\Upsilon:=\frac{Z^{2}}{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}\!\big[\|\nabla\kappa^{\prime}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}(B)\|_{2}^{2}\big]}.

Since
Z^{2}
is a scaled
\chi^{2}_{1}
variable,
\Upsilon
is nonnegative.
This completes the proof.
∎

Based on the above discussion, we can give the following recipe for computing the optimal DRBC policies with Wasserstein uncertainty set with choosing the optimal
\delta
based on the data (for a certain time window). This can be viewed as a nonlinear extension of the RWPI method (e.g. see
[
8
,
14
]
).

•

(1) Collect return data
\{B^{(i)}\}_{i=1,\ldots n}
.

•

(2) Use the collected data
\{B^{(i)}\}_{i=1,\ldots n}
to solve the equation
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}I\left(\frac{ke^{-rT}}{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{n}}\left[L_{T}(B,y)\right]}\right)\varphi_{T}(y)dy=x_{0}e^{rT}
(this corresponds to estimating
\mathcal{K}(x_{0})
). Denote the solution as
\hat{k}
.

•

(3) Obtain independent samples
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{N}
from
\mathcal{N}(0,TI_{d})
. Compute
\hat{F}(Y_{i})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{n}}[L_{T}(B,Y_{i})]
for each sample using the collected data
\{B^{(i)}\}_{i=1,\ldots n}
, and then compute
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{n}}[\|\nabla\kappa^{\prime}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}(B)\|_{2}^{2}]
as an estimate of the denominator of
\Upsilon,
where
\nabla\kappa^{\prime}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}(b)
is computed by Monte Carlo method with
\hat{F}(Y_{i})
,
L_{T}(b,Y_{i})
, and
\hat{k}
derived in Step (2).

•

(4): Estimate (
34
) by the same Monte Carlo method as in Step (3) and denote the estimated variance as
\hat{\sigma}
. Define
\hat{\Upsilon}=\frac{\hat{Z}^{2}}{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{n}}[\|\nabla\kappa^{\prime}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}(B)\|_{2}^{2}]}
, where
\hat{Z}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\hat{\sigma}^{2})
.

•

(5) Obtain independent samples
\Upsilon_{1},\ldots\Upsilon_{K}
from
\hat{\Upsilon}
. Let
\hat{\eta}_{.95}
be the
95\%
quantile of the sample collections
\Upsilon_{1},\ldots\Upsilon_{K}
. (
K
is a sequence such that
K\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
, for example,
K=\log n
; see Algorithm 1 in
[
14
]
).

•

(6) Set
\delta=\frac{\hat{\eta}_{.95}}{n}
and approximate the solution to problem (
21
) using Corollary
1
and get a worst case probability
\mathbb{Q}^{*}
for the drift
B
.

•

(7) Plug in this
\mathbb{Q}^{*}
into the closed form formula of the optimal fraction with the observed stock prices and interest rates in Theorem
1
replacing
\mu
by
\mathbb{Q}^{*}
.

Synthetic Experiment

5.1
Understanding Model Parameters

In this section, we generate synthetic data in a high-dimensional setting and understand how parameters affect the performance of different models. We let the ground-truth drift to be

B_{it}=\frac{B_{0}}{2}\left(1+2\cos(2\pi\kappa_{i}t)\right)

(36)

where
i
represents the stock number. Each stock’s
\kappa_{i}
is sampled from the same Gaussian distribution. We set the total number of stocks to be 20. The synthetic stock data is generated using (
2
) with drift (
36
), and we set the volatility matrix to be diagonal for simplicity. Throughout the paper, we always assume time 1 to be 1 year, and the unit time is
dt
. Given our model is a continuous-time model,
dt
should be very granular. The trading rule is simple: for every 22
dt
, calculate data-driven parameters and portfolio weights based on data of the previous 2520
dt
on a rolling basis and trade. The Bayesian Merton and DRBC have different portfolio weights for every
dt
, yet others are static. We evaluate the performance using the Sharpe ratio with interest rate
r=1\%
on the last 252
dt
’s wealth. We vary
B_{0},dt
and the distribution to sample
\kappa
to understand the models better, and we would like to use the intuition here to guide our experiments on real data.

All experiment procedures follow algorithm in
4
. To be more precise, we estimate
\mathbb{P}_{0}
from the data using Consecutive Drift approach. Then, use the projection method to estimate
\delta
. After that, we compute
\Delta(B)
, which is not a projection, but rather a perturbation according to Theorem
3
. Then we get the modified prior
B^{*}=B+\Delta(B)
, then apply Formulae in
1
with the modified prior distributions to get portfolio weights. Experiment results are shown in Table
1
, which is the average of 100 simulations (We use Sharpe Ratio here as performance metric. Table with terminal utility can be found in appendix
3
). Every simulation we use a different seed to sample both
\kappa_{i}
and all the random numbers used in Theorem
1
. Value for
1/(dt\times 252)
means how many periods we divide a day into. Notice the ground-truth drift is a periodic function with periodicity
\frac{1}{\kappa}
. The drift distribution is estimated as follows: update of drift estimation happens every 30
dt
, each time we use previous 2520
dt
to estimate drift distribution with batched, disjoint time windows. Specifically, we split the 2520
dt
into 10 no-overlapping periods, and estimate annualized return with each stock’s period cumulative return as in Section
4
. We call this "Consecutive Drift". We call the market condition smooth when
\kappa\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and is volatile when
\kappa\sim\mathcal{N}(12,10)
. During smooth market conditions, models with frequent rebalancing, like Bayesian Merton, DRBC, and DRC, benefit from finer time trading resolution, while DRMV shows worse results. During volatile times, such benefits are not significant. In other words, if one believes the economy will grow steadily with no crisis for a long time, yet they also wants to avoid huge downside risk caused by noise, DRBC could be a reasonable choice.

The experiment is done with rolling time window of one month. We use 5 years of previous data as the training set for DRBC to get uncertainty radius

6.1.1
DRMV

We include the Wasserstein-robust mean-variance model of
[
8
]
.
Given empirical distribution

P_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{R_{i}}
,
they consider all return distributions inside the Wasserstein ball

\mathcal{U}_{\delta}(P_{n})=\{P:D_{c}(P,P_{n})\leq\delta\},\qquad c(u,v)=\|u-v\|_{q}^{2}.

The robust Markowitz problem is

\min_{\phi}\;\;\max_{P\in\mathcal{U}_{\delta}(P_{n})}\phi^{\top}\operatorname{Var}_{P}(R)\phi\quad\text{s.t. }\;\mathbf{1}^{\top}\phi=1,\;\min_{P\in\mathcal{U}_{\delta}(P_{n})}\mathbb{E}_{P}[R]^{\top}\phi\geq\bar{\alpha}.

(38)

A key result is that (
38
) is
exactly equivalent
to a regularized empirical problem:

\displaystyle\min_{\phi}

\displaystyle\phi^{\top}\hat{\Sigma}\,\phi+\sqrt{\delta}\,\|\phi\|_{p},

(39)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}^{\top}\phi=1,\qquad\hat{\mu}^{\top}\phi\geq\bar{\alpha}+\sqrt{\delta}\,\|\phi\|_{p},

where
\hat{\mu}=\mathbb{E}_{P_{n}}[R]
,
\hat{\Sigma}=\operatorname{Var}_{P_{n}}(R)
, and
1/p+1/q=1
.

Thus, Wasserstein robustness leads to a theoretically justified norm penalty
\|\phi\|_{p}
, and the ambiguity radius
\delta
and target
\bar{\alpha}
are chosen via data-driven Wasserstein profile inference.

We use two sets of DRMV algorithm, the original one in
[
8
]
and the one with risk free asset. For the second one, we view the interest rate as the last entry of the return vector, and choose the
\delta
in the same way as
[
8
]
, and change the annual target return
\rho
from
10\%
to
10.5\%
. To avoid trivial results, we manually add a small noise to the interest rate.

6.1.2
DRC

Classical DRC formulations
[
22
,
23
]
introduce an adversary who, at every time
t
, perturbs the model by selecting a worst–case probability measure within a
\phi
–divergence ball.
Given a baseline model
P
, at each time step the adversary selects
Q\ll P
satisfying

D_{\phi}(Q\|P)\;\leq\;\delta,

where
D_{\phi}
is the
\phi
–divergence generated by a convex function
\phi
with
\phi(1)=0
.
The controller then solves the dynamic game

\sup_{\pi\in\mathcal{A}(x_{0})}\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{U}_{\delta}^{\text{DRC}}}\;\mathbb{E}_{Q}\!\left[u(X_{T})\right],

(40)

where
\mathcal{U}_{\delta}^{\text{DRC}}
denotes the time replenished uncertainty set: the adversary is allowed to choose a new worst–case distribution at every instant.

Here, DRC is a high-dimensional implementation of
[
9
]
, where an optimization problem induced by the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman-Isaacs (HJBI) equation is solved to get the drift estimation, and then the Merton portfolio weight formula (
37
) is used to get the portfolio weights. For the same practical concern in shorting stocks as in DRBC, we also assume that the short position of each stock cannot surpass half of the wealth. For the radius in DRC, we directly use the radius from DRBC for simplicity. Note that this is not the optimal radius for DRC.

6.1.3
DRBC

To calculate empirical
\hat{B}
and
\hat{\delta}
, we use the previous 5 years of data. We use Ledoit-Wolf
[
28
]
algorithm to estimate the covariance matrix, and we calibrate the items in the inverse of the volatility matrix to be within a range for numerical stability. As directly scaling up the daily return to annually might lead to unreasonable values, we also calibrate on the empirical center to keep outliers in a limit. Since
B
can be roughly regarded as log return plus volatility, the limit is chosen based on knowledge of financial markets. Besides, given the difficulty to naked short a stock in practice, we assume that the short position of each stock cannot surpass half of wealth.

7
Conclusions and Future Work

In this paper, we revisited Merton’s continuous-time portfolio selection problem through a distributionally robust Bayesian control lens. By placing a single ambiguity set on the drift prior, rather than adopting time-rectangular uncertainty on the data-generating process, which tends to be too pessimistic. We preserved the Bayesian learning structure intended to mitigate the over-pessimism often induced by dynamic robust control. A Sion-type minimax swap reduced the DRBC game to a non-linear distributional optimization problem over the drift prior, allowing us to retain the Karatzas–Zhao
[
24
]
’s closed-form characterization of the value function and optimal policy for each candidate prior. Even if the Sion minimax swap induces a duality gap, weak duality still generates a valid bound which we believe is useful specially in dynamic optimization settings, in which over-pessimism tends to arise more often compared to static adversarial formulations. Therefore, the approach that we propose in this paper, we believe, could be broadly applicable to Bayesian control formulations in which the prior is imposed on certain model parameters.

There are natural directions for future work. One of them is precisely to investigate our proposed approach, as we hinted in the previous paragraph, in distributionally robust Bayesian control settings. This paper provides a blue-print in the setting of Merton’s model because it is both elegant and of significant interest (both academically and practically). A broader extension would need to be more algorithmic, dealing with efficient methods for solving the Bayesian control problem jointly with the evaluation of the required sensitivities (which we characterize in this paper). However, we believe that such an extension is worth pursuing.

7.1
Discussions for Real Data Experiment

In Section
6
, we mention the short constraint we add to make sure every single stock’s short position cannot exceed half of the wealth. This is reasonable in practice, yet we want to investigate how many times this constraint is triggered in DRC, DRBC and Bayesian methods, as well as the leverage condition among these methods.

Across all simulations with different data generating parameters, the short constraint happens in more than 90% of trades for DRC method, which is a common disadvantage for Merton-like problems since it always gives extreme values due to the estimation error of average return and variance matrix and the unconstrained optimization. Short constraint appears in about 70% trades in Bayesian setting and about 40% in DRBC setting, illustrating Bayesian methods by
[
24
]
can mitigate extreme weights in Merton-like problems and DRBC can further decrease extreme weights.

We also check the overall leverage ratio for three algorithms. DRC on average uses about 13x leverage, with outliers like 31x. Bayesian uses about 7x and DRBC 5x in average. The leverage ratio highly depend on how drift B is estimated, which depends on the training period. If finer constraints on both short and long positions are added, it will give you results with less leverage, yet cannot fully reveal the strength of DRBC method.

Supplementary Material: Bayesian Distributionally Robust Merton
Problem with Nonlinear Wasserstein Projections

This material is companion to the paper main body of the paper Distributionally Robust Merton Problem Via Nonlinear Wasserstein Projection. In these sections we adapt the results developed in the main body to the case of non-compactly supported prior distributions for power utilities.” And then just present the development.

Appendix A
Sub-Gaussian Extensions of the Main Results

In Sections
B
,
C
, and
D
, we study a commonly used
utility function in the case where the random drift
B
is no longer
compactly supported. This shows that the compactness assumption in the
main text is not essential for the validity of our results; it is
imposed there mainly to avoid lengthy technical arguments involving
exponential moment bounds and Gaussian integrals.

Throughout these sections we fix a CRRA utility

u(x)=\frac{x^{\alpha}}{\alpha},\qquad x>0,\quad\alpha<1,\ \alpha\neq 0,

(41)

and work under a sub-Gaussian assumption on the prior distribution of

B
. More precisely, we assume:

Assumption 3

(Sub-Gaussian prior on
B
)
.

There exists
\gamma_{0}>0
such that

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}\!\left[\exp\bigl(\gamma^{2}\|B\|_{2}^{2}\bigr)\right]<\infty\qquad\text{for all }\gamma<\gamma_{0}.

This condition is standard: it requires finite exponential moments of

\|B\|^{2}
and will be used repeatedly to control various Gaussian
integrals arising from the likelihood ratio
L_{T}(B,y)
and its
derivatives.

Appendix B
Generalization of Minimax Theorem with Non-compact Support

We first explain how the Minimax Theorem extends to the sub-Gaussian setting when the utility is given by
(
41
). The goal of this subsection is
only
to
highlight where compactness is used and how Assumption
3

replaces it; the detailed sub-Gaussian estimates are of the same style
as those developed later in Sections
C
and
D
, and we
do not repeat them here.

Recall that in the compact case the proof proceeds in five
steps. Steps 2 and 3 establish continuity and concavity properties of
the map

(\pi,\mathbb{Q})\mapsto\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[U(X^{\pi}(T))],

while Step 5 uses the closed-form structure of the Karatzas–Zhao
solution to construct an optimal feedback control and to verify that it
lies in the admissible set
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(x_{0})
.

Step 2 and Step 3 (moment bounds and continuity).

In the compact-support proof, compactness assumption is used to obtain uniform
bounds on the drift term in the wealth process and on the likelihood
ratio
L_{T}(b,y)
, uniformly over
b\in K
. These bounds, together with
the polynomial growth utility, yield
uniform positive and negative moment bounds for
X^{\pi}(T)
and hence
integrability of
U(X^{\pi}(T))
; dominated convergence then gives the
required continuity in the model and in the control.

Under Assumption
3
, these arguments remain
valid after replacing compactness by exponential moment estimates. The
key observation is that the Gaussian likelihood
L_{T}(B,y)
and its
derivatives grow at most exponentially in
\|B\|
and
\|y\|
. Combined
with Assumption
3
, one can bound the relevant
expectations by standard Gaussian integrals and show that

\sup_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{OT}}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{0})}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\bigl[|X^{\pi}(T)|^{2+\varepsilon}\bigr]<\infty,\qquad\sup_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{OT}}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{0})}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\bigl[|X^{\pi}(T)|^{-m}\bigr]<\infty

for suitable
\varepsilon,m>0
and all
\pi\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(x_{0})
. The
rest of Steps 2 and 3 (continuity in
\mathbb{Q}
and in
\pi
) then
follow exactly as in the compact-support proof; only the bounds used to
justify dominated convergence change, and these are handled by the same
type of sub-Gaussian computations that we carry out in detail for nonlinear perturbation and projection theorems in Sections
C
and
D
, respectively.

Step 4 (use of Sion’s Theorem).

In the sub-Gaussian extension in Section
C
and

D
(as shown later), the optimal transport cost is no longer the quadratic cost

\|\Delta\|_{2}^{2}
used in the main text.
Instead, following the Gaussian-integrability estimates derived in this
appendix, the natural cost becomes

c_{\tau}(\Delta)\;:=\;e^{\,\tau\,\|\Delta\|_{2}^{2}}\;-\;1,

(42)

where the parameter
\tau>0
depends explicitly on

\alpha
,
T
, and
\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}
through the sub-Gaussian
tail bounds of the prior distribution of
B
.

We emphasize that this change of transport cost does
not
affect
the minimax argument.
The function
\Delta\mapsto c_{\tau}(\Delta)
is convex, and therefore
the divergence ball

\mathcal{U}_{\delta}^{c_{\tau}}:=\bigl\{\mathbb{Q}:D_{c_{\tau}}(\mathbb{Q}\,\|\,P_{0})\leq\delta\bigr\}

is convex.
Moreover, because
c_{\tau}(\Delta)
grows superlinearly in

\|\Delta\|_{2}
, the corresponding OT balls are tight and relatively
compact under the topology induced by optimal transport.
Since the payoff functional is linear in
\mathbb{Q}
and concave in the
control
\pi
, all assumptions of Sion’s minimax theorem remain valid.
Hence the change of cost from
\|\Delta\|_{2}^{2}
to
c_{\tau}(\Delta)
does
not alter the validity of the min–max swap in
Theorem
B
.

Step 5 (feedback form and admissibility).

In Step 5 of the main-text proof, the compactness of
K
was used to
obtain two-sided exponential bounds on the function
F(T,u)
and its
gradient, and polynomial growth condition on the utility function was used to control the
ratio

\frac{\Xi(t,y)}{\Theta(t,y)}

in terms of
\exp\{c\|y\|\}
. In the present CRRA setting
(
41
), the inverse marginal utility
I=(U^{\prime})^{-1}
and
its derivative admit explicit power-type bounds, so the lower bound on

I
required in Step 5 is automatic. Under
Assumption
3
, the same exponential estimates for

F(T,u)
and
1/F(T,u)
as in the compact case are recovered by
computing Gaussian integrals involving
L_{T}(B,u)
and using the
sub-Gaussian tails of
B
. Consequently, the ratio

\Xi(t,y)/\Theta(t,y)
is again controlled by an exponential in

\|y\|
, and the feedback control
\pi^{*,b}
satisfies

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\Biggl(\int_{0}^{T}\|\pi^{*,b}(t)\|^{2}\,dt\Biggr)^{1/2}<\infty,\qquad\forall\,\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{OT}}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{0}),

so that
\pi^{*,b}\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(x_{0})
.

Appendix C
Generalization of Nonlinear Perturbation Theorem with Non-compact Support

In this section, we give a full detailed analysis of how the proofs of the Nonlinear Perturbation Theorem goes without the compact assumption. The proof skills used here are the same as those estimates for Section
B
.

After the swapping theorem, with the specific power utility, the distributional optimization problem becomes

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}^{*}=\begin{cases}\arg\min_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{U}_{\delta,B}^{D}(\mathbb{P}_{0})}\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{Q}),&\text{if }\alpha\in(0,1);\\
\arg\max_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{U}_{\delta,B}^{D}(\mathbb{P}_{0})}\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{Q}),&\text{if }\alpha<0,\end{cases}

(43)

where

\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{Q})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[L_{T}(B,z)\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}\varphi_{T}(z)dz.

We impose a natural sub-Gaussian assumption on
B
, and interestingly, the cost function for the optimal transport uncertainty set should be changed to the following: for a displacement
\Delta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and scale
\tau>0
, the cost function for the optimal transport is defined via

\ c_{\tau}(\Delta)\;:=\;e^{\,\tau\,\|\Delta\|_{2}^{2}}\;-\;1.

The main reason of this definition is to ensure the integrability conditions and the use of swapping orders of integration, differentiation, or limit, etc, which are easy to achieve in the compact case.

We remark that the parameter
\tau
depends on
\alpha,T,\left\|\sigma^{-1}\right\|_{F}^{2}
. We also assume that

\tau>\max\left\{4T\left\|\sigma^{-1}\right\|_{F}^{2},2T\,(4\beta^{2}-2\beta)\,\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}\right\},

where
\beta=\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}
. The precise sub-Gaussian assumption is given below.

Assumption 4
.

Suppose there exists
\gamma_{0}>0
such that

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}\big[\exp(\gamma^{2}\|B\|_{2}^{2})\big]<\infty\quad\text{for every }\gamma<\gamma_{0}.

with

\frac{\gamma_{0}^{2}}{\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}}\;>\;T\max\left\{\frac{1}{2}\Big(\beta^{2}-\beta+2\;+\;\sqrt{\,(\beta^{2}-\beta+2)^{2}+8(\beta^{2}+\beta)\,}\Big)\;,8,\frac{\tau\cdot 2T\,(4\beta^{2}-2\beta)\,}{\tau-2T\,(4\beta^{2}-2\beta)\,\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}}\right\}.

We begin with the nonlinear perturbation theorem in this setting and stating several technical lemmas.

Theorem 5
.

Assume that for the fixed
\alpha
, we have the corresponding cost function and
\tau
as in the definition. We define

H(b)\;:=\;\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla_{b}L_{T}(b,y)\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}[L_{T}(B,y)]^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\;\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy

(44)

and

\|H\|_{L^{2}_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{0})}:=\Big(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}\|H(B)\|_{2}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}.

Then under Assumption
4
, when
\alpha\in(0,1)
, as
\delta\to 0
, an asymptotically optimal perturbation is the deterministic pushforward

\Delta^{*}_{\delta}(b)\;=-\;\sqrt{\frac{\delta}{\tau}}\,\frac{H(b)}{\|H\|_{L^{2}_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{0})}}+o(\sqrt{\delta})

and

\inf_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{U}_{\delta,B}^{\text{OT}}(\mathbb{P}_{0})}\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{Q})=\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{P}_{0})-\sqrt{\frac{\delta}{\tau}}\|H\|_{L^{2}_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{0})}+o(\sqrt{\delta}).

(45)

When
\alpha<0
, as
\delta\to 0
, an asymptotically optimal perturbation is the deterministic pushforward

\Delta^{*}_{\delta}(b)\;=\;\sqrt{\frac{\delta}{\tau}}\,\frac{H(b)}{\|H\|_{L^{2}_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{0})}}+o(\sqrt{\delta})

and

\sup_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{U}_{\delta,B}^{\text{OT}}(\mathbb{P}_{0})}\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{Q})=\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{P}_{0})+\sqrt{\frac{\delta}{\tau}}\|H\|_{L^{2}_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{0})}+o(\sqrt{\delta}).

(46)

The proof essentially contains two parts. The first part is Lemma
2
, giving the required integrability conditions, and the second part is to linearize the problem and then solve the constrained linear problem, similar to the case in the main body.

Lemma 2
.

Under Assumption
4
, then

•

(1) for any
\alpha<1
and
\alpha\neq 0
,
the vector field of Eq. (
44
) is well-defined
\mathbb{P}_{0}
almost surely and satisfies
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}[\left\|H(B)\right\|_{2}^{2}]<\infty
.

•

(2) for any
s\in[0,1]
, set

F_{s}(B,\Delta)\;:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla_{b}L_{T}(B+s\Delta,y)\;\Big(\mathbb{E}\big[L_{T}(B+s\Delta,y)\big]\Big)^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\;\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy.

Then there exist constants
C(B)>0
and finite
\mathbb{P}_{0}
almost surely and
C_{1}\leq\tau
(
\tau
depends on
\alpha
) such that,
\mathbb{P}_{0}
almost surely,

\|F_{s}(B,\Delta)\|_{2}^{2}\;\leq C(B)\exp(C_{1}\left\|\Delta\right\|_{2}^{2}).

(47)

Proof.

We first prove part (1) and assume
\alpha<0
and denote
\beta:=\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}
. Define
A(y):=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}[L_{T}(B,y)]
where
B
is a random vector on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with law
\mathbb{P}_{0}
.
Direct computations give

\nabla_{b}L_{T}(b,y)\;=\;L_{T}(b,y)\,\sigma^{-T}\!\big(y-T\,a(b)\big).

and

L_{T}(b,y)^{2}\,\varphi_{T}(y)\;=\;\exp\!\big(T\|a(b)\|_{2}^{2}\big)\;\varphi_{T}\!\big(y-2T\,a(b)\big).

By the Jensen inequality,

A(y)\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}\big[e^{\langle a(B),y\rangle-\frac{T}{2}\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}}\big]\;\geq\;\exp\!\Big(\langle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}[a(B)],y\rangle-\tfrac{T}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}\left[\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}\right]\Big).

Raising to the negative power
\beta<0
reverses the inequality and gives

A(y)^{\beta}\;\leq\;C_{a}\,\exp\!\big(\beta\,\langle v,y\rangle\big),\qquad C_{a}:=\exp\!\Big(-\tfrac{\beta T}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}\left[\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}\right]\Big),\ \ v:=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}[a(B)].

(48)

Since

\|\nabla_{b}L_{T}(B,y)\|_{2}^{2}=L_{T}(B,y)^{2}\,\|\sigma^{-T}(y-Ta(B))\|_{2}^{2}\leq\,\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}\,L_{T}(B,y)^{2}\,\|y-Ta(B)\|_{2}^{2},

we use
\|u+v\|_{2}^{2}\leq 2(\|u\|_{2}^{2}+\|v\|_{2}^{2})
and have

\|\nabla_{b}L_{T}(B,y)\|_{2}^{2}\;\leq\;C\,\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}\,L_{T}(B,y)^{2}\,\big(1+\|y\|_{2}^{2}+T^{2}\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}\big),

where
C>0
is a constant (we use
C
to absorb constants if there is no confusion). Therefore,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}A(y)^{\beta}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}\big[\|\nabla_{b}L_{T}(B,y)\|_{2}^{2}\big]\;\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy

\displaystyle\leq\ C\,\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}\!\Big[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}L_{T}(B,y)^{2}\,(1+\|y\|_{2}^{2}+T^{2}\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2})\,e^{\beta\langle v,y\rangle}\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy\Big]

\displaystyle=\ C\,\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}\!\Big[e^{T\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1+\|y\|_{2}^{2}+T^{2}\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2})\,e^{\beta\langle v,y\rangle}\,\varphi_{T}\!\big(y-2Ta(B)\big)\,dy\Big].

The inner integral is

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bigl(1+\|y\|_{2}^{2}+T^{2}\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}\bigr)\,e^{\beta\langle v,y\rangle}\,\varphi_{T}\bigl(y-2T\,a(B)\bigr)\,dy

\displaystyle=\exp\!\Big(2T\beta\,\langle v,a(B)\rangle+\tfrac{T}{2}\|\beta v\|_{2}^{2}\Big)\,\Big[\,1+Td+\|2Ta(B)+T\beta v\|_{2}^{2}+T^{2}\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}\,\Big].

For a fixed
\varepsilon>0
(which will be chosen later), by the Young inequality,

2T\beta\,\langle v,a(B)\rangle\leq 2T\beta\,\left|\langle v,a(B)\rangle\right|\leq\varepsilon\left\|a(B)\right\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{T^{2}\beta^{2}\left\|v\right\|_{2}^{2}}{\varepsilon}.

Hence,

e^{\,2T\beta\langle v,a(B)\rangle+\frac{T}{2}\|\beta v\|_{2}^{2}}\leq C_{\mathrm{tilt}}(\varepsilon)\,e^{\,\varepsilon\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}},

with

C_{\mathrm{tilt}}(\varepsilon):=\exp\!\Big(\tfrac{T}{2}\|\beta v\|_{2}^{2}+\tfrac{T^{2}\beta^{2}\|v\|_{2}^{2}}{\varepsilon}\Big).

Using the Young inequality in the quadratic bracket, we have

\|2Ta+T\beta v\|_{2}^{2}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\,(2T)^{2}\|a\|_{2}^{2}+\Big(1+\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\Big)T^{2}\beta^{2}\|v\|_{2}^{2},

and further get

1+Td+\|2Ta(B)+T\beta v\|_{2}^{2}+T^{2}\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}\leq C_{0}(\varepsilon)+C_{1}(\varepsilon)\,\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2},

where
C_{0}(\varepsilon):=1+Td+\Big(1+\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\Big)T^{2}\beta^{2}\|v\|_{2}^{2},
and
C_{1}(\varepsilon):=T^{2}\big(5+4\varepsilon\big).

Therefore, the inner integral is upper bounded by

C_{\mathrm{tilt}}(\varepsilon)\,\bigl(C_{0}(\varepsilon)+C_{1}(\varepsilon)\,\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}\bigr)\,e^{\,\varepsilon\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}}.

Therefore, there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}A(y)^{\beta}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}\big[\|\nabla_{b}L_{T}(B,y)\|_{2}^{2}\big]\;\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy

\displaystyle\leq C\,\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}\,C_{\mathrm{tilt}}(\varepsilon)\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}\!\Big[\bigl(C_{0}(\varepsilon)+C_{1}(\varepsilon)\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}\bigr)\,e^{\,(T+\varepsilon)\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}}\Big].

Let

\varepsilon_{*}:=\tfrac{1}{2}\!\left(\frac{\gamma_{0}^{2}}{2\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}}-T\right)\quad\text{(well-defined and $>0$ if }\penalty\ \gamma_{0}^{2}>2T\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}).

Since
a(B)=\sigma^{-1}B-m
, we have
\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}\leq 2\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}\|B\|_{2}^{2}+2\|m\|_{2}^{2}
. Hence,

\bigl(C_{0}(\varepsilon_{*})+C_{1}(\varepsilon_{*})\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}\bigr)\,e^{\,(T+\varepsilon_{*})\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}}\;\leq\;K_{m}\,(1+c\|B\|_{2}^{2})\,e^{\,C^{\prime}\|B\|_{2}^{2}},

(49)

for constants
K_{m},c>0
and

C^{\prime}:=2\,(T+\varepsilon_{*})\,\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}=T\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{\gamma_{0}^{2}}{2}\;<\gamma_{0}^{2}.

To see (
49
), recall that
a(B)=\sigma^{-1}B-m
, and denote

\kappa_{1}:=2\,\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2},\qquad\kappa_{0}:=2\,\|m\|_{2}^{2}.

Then

\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}\;=\;\|\sigma^{-1}B-m\|_{2}^{2}\;\leq\;2\|\sigma^{-1}B\|_{2}^{2}+2\|m\|_{2}^{2}\;\leq\;\kappa_{1}\|B\|_{2}^{2}+\kappa_{0},

and

\displaystyle(T+\varepsilon_{*})\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(T+\varepsilon_{*})\big(\kappa_{1}\|B\|_{2}^{2}+\kappa_{0}\big)=\underbrace{2(T+\varepsilon_{*})\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}}_{=:\penalty\ C^{\prime}}\|B\|_{2}^{2}\;+\;2(T+\varepsilon_{*})\|m\|_{2}^{2},

where

C^{\prime}=2(T+\varepsilon_{*})\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}=T\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{\gamma_{0}^{2}}{2}\;<\;\gamma_{0}^{2}.

Hence,

e^{(T+\varepsilon_{*})\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}}\;\leq\;e^{\,2(T+\varepsilon_{*})\|m\|_{2}^{2}}\;e^{\,C^{\prime}\|B\|_{2}^{2}}.

For the polynomial prefactor,

\displaystyle C_{0}(\varepsilon_{*})+C_{1}(\varepsilon_{*})\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{0}(\varepsilon_{*})+C_{1}(\varepsilon_{*})\big(\kappa_{1}\|B\|_{2}^{2}+\kappa_{0}\big)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\big(C_{0}(\varepsilon_{*})+C_{1}(\varepsilon_{*})\kappa_{0}\big)}_{=:\penalty\ K_{1}}\;+\;\underbrace{C_{1}(\varepsilon_{*})\kappa_{1}}_{=:\penalty\ K_{2}}\,\|B\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=K_{1}\Big(1+\frac{K_{2}}{K_{1}}\,\|B\|_{2}^{2}\Big)=K_{1}\big(1+c\,\|B\|_{2}^{2}\big),

where

c:=\frac{C_{1}(\varepsilon_{*})\kappa_{1}}{C_{0}(\varepsilon_{*})+C_{1}(\varepsilon_{*})\kappa_{0}}\;>\;0.

Therefore, we get (
49
) with

K_{m}\;:=\;K_{1}\,e^{\,2(T+\varepsilon_{*})\|m\|_{2}^{2}}\;=\;\big(C_{0}(\varepsilon_{*})+C_{1}(\varepsilon_{*})\kappa_{0}\big)\,e^{\,2(T+\varepsilon_{*})\|m\|_{2}^{2}}.

Since
C^{\prime}<\gamma_{0}^{2}
, we pick any
\gamma
such that
C^{\prime}<\gamma^{2}<\gamma_{0}^{2}
.
For
t\geq 0
and
\delta:=\gamma^{2}-C^{\prime}>0
, the elementary bound

1+ct\;\leq\;\Big(1+\frac{c}{e\,\delta}\Big)\,e^{\delta t}

implies

(1+c\,\|B\|_{2}^{2})\,e^{\,C^{\prime}\|B\|_{2}^{2}}\;\leq\;K\,e^{\,\gamma^{2}\|B\|_{2}^{2}},\qquad K:=1+\frac{c}{e(\gamma^{2}-C^{\prime})}.

Therefore, under the sub-Gaussian assumption

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}\big[e^{\gamma^{2}\|B\|_{2}^{2}}\big]<\infty
for all
\gamma<\gamma_{0}
,

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}\!\Big[\bigl(C_{0}(\varepsilon_{*})+C_{1}(\varepsilon_{*})\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}\bigr)\,e^{(T+\varepsilon_{*})\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}}\Big]\;\leq\;K_{m}\,K\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}\big[e^{\gamma^{2}\|B\|_{2}^{2}}\big]\;<\;\infty.

In order to show the well-definedness of
H
, we need to show

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}A(y)^{\beta}\varphi_{T}(y)dy<\infty

(50)

since from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, for
\mathbb{P}_{0}
almost every
b
,

\displaystyle\left\|H(b)\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}A(y)^{\beta}\left\|\nabla_{b}L_{T}(b,y)\right\|_{2}\,\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\|\nabla_{b}L_{T}(b,y)\right\|_{2}^{2}A(y)^{\beta}\,\varphi_{T}(y)dy\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}A(y)^{\beta}\,\varphi_{T}(y)dy\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

where the first term is finite
\mathbb{P}_{0}
almost surely. If we take square and then take expectation with respect to
\mathbb{P}_{0}
and use Tonelli’s theorem, then the proof is complete.

When
\alpha<0
, we recall Eq. (
48
) and have

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}A(y)^{\beta}\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy\;\leq\;C_{a}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{\beta\langle v,y\rangle}\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy.

Completion of squares gives

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{\beta\langle v,y\rangle}\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy=(2\pi T)^{-d/2}\!\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp\!\left(\beta\langle v,y\rangle-\frac{\|y\|_{2}^{2}}{2T}\right)dy=\exp\!\left(\frac{T}{2}\|\beta v\|_{2}^{2}\right).

Indeed,

\beta\langle v,y\rangle-\frac{\|y\|_{2}^{2}}{2T}=-\frac{1}{2T}\Big\|\,y-T\beta v\,\Big\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{T}{2}\|\beta v\|_{2}^{2}.

Hence,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}A(y)^{\beta}\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy\;\leq\;C_{a}\,\exp\!\left(\frac{T}{2}\|\beta v\|_{2}^{2}\right)<\infty,\qed

which finishes the proof of the case when
\alpha<0
.
Next we divide the positive
\alpha
into two cases.

Case 1:
0<\beta\leq 1
(equivalently
0<\alpha\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
).

Let
\mu(dy):=\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy
; then
\mu
is a probability measure since

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy=1
. Set
f(y):=A(y)=\mathbb{E}[L_{T}(B,y)]\geq 0
and

\phi(x):=x^{\beta},\qquad x\geq 0.

For
0<\beta\leq 1
, the map
\phi
is concave and increasing, so the Jensen
inequality for concave functions gives

\phi\!\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(y)\,\mu(dy)\right)\;\geq\;\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(f(y))\,\mu(dy),

i.e.

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}A(y)^{\beta}\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy\;\leq\;\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}A(y)\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy\Big)^{\beta}.

(51)

It remains to compute
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}A(y)\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy
. By the Fubini theorem,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}A(y)\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}\!\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}L_{T}(B,y)\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy\right]=1

from a completion of squares argument. Thus, plugging into (
51
) yields the bound

\ \int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}A(y)^{\beta}\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy\;\leq\;1,\qquad 0<\beta\leq 1.\

Case 2:
\beta>1
(equivalently
\tfrac{1}{2}<\alpha<1
).

Since the map
x\mapsto x^{\beta}
is convex on
(0,\infty)
, the Jensen inequality yields

A(y)^{\beta}=\big(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}[L_{T}(B,y)]\big)^{\beta}\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}\big[L_{T}(B,y)^{\beta}\big].

Integrating and exchanging expectation and integral,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}A(y)^{\beta}\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy\;\leq\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}\!\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}L_{T}(B,y)^{\beta}\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy\right].

For fixed
b
, completing the square gives

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}L_{T}(b,y)^{\beta}\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy=\exp\!\left(\frac{T}{2}(\beta^{2}-\beta)\,\|a(b)\|_{2}^{2}\right).

Therefore

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}A(y)^{\beta}\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy\;\leq\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}\!\left[\exp\!\left(\frac{T}{2}(\beta^{2}-\beta)\,\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}\right)\right].

Using
\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}\leq 2\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}\,\|B\|_{2}^{2}+2\|m\|_{2}^{2}
, we obtain

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}A(y)^{\beta}\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy\;\leq\;\exp\!\Big(T(\beta^{2}-\beta)\,\|m\|_{2}^{2}\Big)\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}\!\left[\exp\!\Big(T(\beta^{2}-\beta)\,\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}\,\|B\|_{2}^{2}\Big)\right].

(52)

Hence, under the sub-Gaussian moment assumption

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}\!\left[e^{\gamma^{2}\|B\|_{2}^{2}}\right]<\infty\quad\text{for all }\gamma<\gamma_{0},

the right-hand side of Eq. (
52
) is finite whenever

\ T(\beta^{2}-\beta)\,\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}\;<\;\gamma_{0}^{2}.

(53)

Next, we focus on the bound when
\alpha\in(0,1)
.
Define

\mathcal{I}_{\beta}\;:=\;\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}A(y)^{\beta}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}\!\big[\|\nabla_{b}L_{T}(B,y)\|_{2}^{2}\big]\;\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy.

We first consider the case when
\beta\in(0,1)
.
Write

G(y):=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}\!\big[\|\nabla_{b}L_{T}(B,y)\|_{2}^{2}\big].

Then

\mathcal{I}_{\beta}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}A(y)^{\beta}\,G(y)\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy.

By the Hölder inequality with exponents
p=\frac{1}{\beta}
and
q=\frac{1}{1-\beta}
, we have

\mathcal{I}_{\beta}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big(A(y)G(y)\big)^{\beta}\,G(y)^{1-\beta}\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy\;\leq\;\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}A(y)\,G(y)\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy\Big)^{\!\beta}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G(y)\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy\Big)^{\!1-\beta}.

(54)

Set

I_{0}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G(y)\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy,\qquad I_{1}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}A(y)\,G(y)\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy.

It suffices to show
I_{0}<\infty
and
I_{1}<\infty
under some sub-Gaussian assumptions.

Recall that there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\|\nabla_{b}L_{T}(B,y)\|_{2}^{2}\;\leq\;C\,\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}\,L_{T}(B,y)^{2}\,(1+\|y\|_{2}^{2}+T^{2}\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}).

Using the Tonelli theorem,

\displaystyle I_{0}

\displaystyle\leq C\,\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}\,\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}_{0}}\!\Big[e^{T\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}}\!\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\!(1+\|y\|_{2}^{2}+T^{2}\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2})\,\varphi_{T}(y-2Ta(B))\,dy\Big].

For the inner integral,
fix
a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and set the notation

J(a):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bigl(1+\|y\|_{2}^{2}+T^{2}\|a\|_{2}^{2}\bigr)\,\varphi_{T}(y-2Ta)\,dy.

Let
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,TI_{d})
. Since
\varphi_{T}(y-2Ta)\,dy
is the law of
Y:=Z+2Ta
, we have

J(a)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[1+\|Y\|_{2}^{2}+T^{2}\|a\|_{2}^{2}\right]=1+\mathbb{E}\left[\|Z+2Ta\|_{2}^{2}\right]+T^{2}\|a\|_{2}^{2}=1+dT+5T^{2}\|a\|_{2}^{2}.

With
a=a(B)
, the inner integral in
I_{0}
equals
1+dT+5T^{2}\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}
, and therefore

I_{0}\leq C\,\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}\!\Big[e^{T\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}}\big(1+dT+5T^{2}\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}\big)\Big].

The estimate for
I_{0}
requires

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}\!\left[\exp\!\big(T\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}\big)\right]<\infty,

which is actually a sub-Gaussian assumption on
B
with condition

2T\,\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}\;<\;\gamma_{0}^{2}.

Next, we upper bound
I_{1}
. By the Young inequality
\langle u,v\rangle\leq\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\|u\|_{2}^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2\varepsilon}\|v\|_{2}^{2}
with a fixed
\varepsilon>0
(which will be chosen later),

A(y)\;\leq\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}\!\Big[e^{-\frac{T-\varepsilon}{2}\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}}\Big]\;\exp\!\Big(\frac{\|y\|_{2}^{2}}{2\varepsilon}\Big)\;=\;C_{A}(\varepsilon)\,e^{c\|y\|_{2}^{2}},\qquad c:=\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\in\Big(0,\frac{1}{2T}\Big).

Here
C_{A}(\varepsilon)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}\!\big[e^{\frac{\varepsilon-T}{2}\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}}\big]
.
Hence, by the Tonelli theorem,

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle\leq C\,\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}\,C_{A}(\varepsilon)\,\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}_{0}}\!\Big[e^{T\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1+\|y\|_{2}^{2}+T^{2}\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2})e^{c\|y\|_{2}^{2}}\varphi_{T}(y-2Ta(B))\,dy\Big].

Fix
a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
c\in(0,\frac{1}{2T})
. Let

K_{c}(a):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bigl(1+\|y\|_{2}^{2}+T^{2}\|a\|_{2}^{2}\bigr)\,e^{c\|y\|_{2}^{2}}\,\varphi_{T}(y-2Ta)\,dy.

With
Y\sim\mathcal{N}(2Ta,TI_{d})
we have
K_{c}(a)=\mathbb{E}[(1+\|Y\|_{2}^{2}+T^{2}\|a\|_{2}^{2})e^{c\|Y\|_{2}^{2}}]
.
Define

F(c,a):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{c\|y\|_{2}^{2}}\,\varphi_{T}(y-2Ta)\,dy=(1-2cT)^{-d/2}\exp\!\Big(\frac{4T^{2}c}{1-2cT}\,\|a\|_{2}^{2}\Big).

Then

\frac{\partial}{\partial c}F(c,a)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|y\|_{2}^{2}e^{c\|y\|_{2}^{2}}\varphi_{T}(y-2Ta)\,dy=F(c,a)\!\left(\frac{dT}{1-2cT}+\frac{4T^{2}\|a\|_{2}^{2}}{(1-2cT)^{2}}\right),

so

\displaystyle K_{c}(a)

\displaystyle=(1+T^{2}\|a\|_{2}^{2})\,F(c,a)+\frac{\partial}{\partial c}F(c,a)

\displaystyle=F(c,a)\!\left[1+T^{2}\|a\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{dT}{1-2cT}+\frac{4T^{2}\|a\|_{2}^{2}}{(1-2cT)^{2}}\right]\;\leq\;C_{1}(c,T,d)\,\bigl(1+\|a\|_{2}^{2}\bigr)\,F(c,a).

Therefore, with
c=\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\in(0,\frac{1}{2T})
,

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle\leq C\,\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}\,C_{A}(\varepsilon)\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}\!\Big[e^{T\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}}\,K_{c}\big(a(B)\big)\Big]

\displaystyle\leq C(\varepsilon,T,d,\sigma)\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}\!\Big[(1+\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2})\,\exp\!\Big(\Big(T+\frac{2T^{2}}{\varepsilon-T}\Big)\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}\Big)\Big],

where

C(\varepsilon,T,d,\sigma)=C\;\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}\;C_{A}(\varepsilon)\;\Big(1-\tfrac{T}{\varepsilon}\Big)^{-d/2}\left(1+\frac{dT}{1-\tfrac{T}{\varepsilon}}+T^{2}+\frac{4T^{2}}{\big(1-\tfrac{T}{\varepsilon}\big)^{2}}\right).

To make the bound for
I_{1}
finite, we only need an
\varepsilon>T
such that

\frac{\varepsilon-T}{2}\;<\;\frac{\gamma_{0}^{2}}{2\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad T+\frac{2T^{2}}{\varepsilon-T}\;<\;\frac{\gamma_{0}^{2}}{2\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}}.

These two inequalities simultaneously hold whenever

4T^{2}\,\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{4}\;+\;2T\,\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}\,\gamma_{0}^{2}\;<\;\gamma_{0}^{4},

which is equivalent to

\gamma_{0}^{2}\;>\;(1+\sqrt{5})\,T\,\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}.

Under this condition, an explicit admissible choice is

\varepsilon^{*}\;:=\;T+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{2T^{2}}{\frac{\gamma_{0}^{2}}{2\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}}-T}+\frac{\gamma_{0}^{2}}{\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}}\right)\;>\;T.

For this
\varepsilon^{*}
we have

C_{A}(\varepsilon^{*})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}\!\left[\exp\!\left(\frac{\varepsilon^{*}-T}{2}\,\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}\right)\right]<\infty,\qquad\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}\!\left[(1+\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2})\,\exp\!\left(\Big(T+\frac{2T^{2}}{\varepsilon^{*}-T}\Big)\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}\right)\right]<\infty,

by the sub-Gaussian integrability above. Hence,
I_{1}<\infty
.

Finally, we upper bound
\mathcal{I}_{\beta}
when
\beta>1
.
By convexity of the map
x\mapsto x^{\beta}
when
\beta>1
and the Jensen inequality,

A(y)^{\beta}\;=\;\big(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}[L_{T}(B,y)]\big)^{\beta}\;\leq\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}\!\big[L_{T}(B,y)^{\beta}\big].

Fix
\varepsilon>\beta^{2}T
and use the Young inequality

\langle u,v\rangle\leq\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\|u\|_{2}^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2\varepsilon}\|v\|_{2}^{2}
with
u=\beta a(B)
,
v=y
:

L_{T}(B,y)^{\beta}=\exp\!\Big(\beta\langle a(B),y\rangle-\tfrac{\beta T}{2}\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}\Big)\leq\exp\!\Big(\tfrac{\varepsilon-\beta T}{2}\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}\Big)\,e^{c\|y\|_{2}^{2}},\quad c:=\frac{\beta^{2}}{2\varepsilon}\in\Big(0,\frac{1}{2T}\Big).

Taking expectation in
B
yields

A(y)^{\beta}\;\leq\;C_{\beta}(\varepsilon)\,e^{c\|y\|_{2}^{2}},\quad C_{\beta}(\varepsilon):=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}\!\Big[\exp\!\Big(\tfrac{\varepsilon-\beta T}{2}\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}\Big)\Big].

Thus, exactly as in the
I_{1}
bound for
\beta\in(0,1)
, we obtain

\mathcal{I}_{\beta}\;\leq\;C(\varepsilon,T,d,\sigma)\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}\!\Big[(1+\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2})\,\exp\!\Big(\Big(T+\frac{2T^{2}\beta^{2}}{\varepsilon-\beta^{2}T}\Big)\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}\Big)\Big],

for some finite constant
C(\varepsilon,T,d,\sigma)
whenever
c\in(0,1/(2T))
.
Therefore, to have
\mathcal{I}_{\beta}<\infty
it suffices to pick
\varepsilon>\beta^{2}T
such that

\frac{\varepsilon-\beta T}{2}\;<\;\frac{\gamma_{0}^{2}}{2\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad T+\frac{2T^{2}\beta^{2}}{\varepsilon-\beta^{2}T}\;<\;\frac{\gamma_{0}^{2}}{2\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}}.

These two inequalities simultaneously hold precisely when

\frac{\gamma_{0}^{2}}{\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}}\;>\;\frac{T}{2}\Big(\beta^{2}-\beta+2\;+\;\sqrt{\,(\beta^{2}-\beta+2)^{2}+8(\beta^{2}+\beta)\,}\Big)\;.

Under the displayed condition, choose any

\varepsilon\in\Big(\,\beta^{2}T+\frac{2T^{2}\beta^{2}}{\frac{\gamma_{0}^{2}}{2\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}}-T}\,,\;\beta T+\frac{\gamma_{0}^{2}}{\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}}\,\Big),

which is a nonempty interval; then both parts of
(\star)
hold and the expectation above is finite. Hence
\mathcal{I}_{\beta}<\infty
for all
\beta>1
under the stated sub-Gaussian slack.

For the proof of part (2), we first notice that there exists

\gamma_{0}^{\,2}\;>\;\frac{\tau\cdot 2T\,(4\beta^{2}-2\beta)\,\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}}{\tau-2T\,(4\beta^{2}-2\beta)\,\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}}.

such that

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}[e^{\gamma^{2}\|B\|_{2}^{2}}]<\infty
for all
\gamma<\gamma_{0}
by Assumption
4
.
By Cauchy–Schwarz inequality in
y
and
\beta=\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}
,

\displaystyle\|F_{s}(B,\Delta)\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\|u\|_{2}=1}\left\langle u,\;\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla_{b}L_{T}(B+s\Delta,y)\,\big(\mathbb{E}[L_{T}(B+s\Delta,y)]\big)^{\beta}\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sup_{\|u\|_{2}=1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle u,\nabla_{b}L_{T}(B+s\Delta,y)\rangle\,\big(\mathbb{E}[L_{T}(B+s\Delta,y)]\big)^{\beta}\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\|u\|_{2}=1}\Bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle u,\nabla_{b}L_{T}(B+s\Delta,y)\rangle^{2}\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy\Bigg)^{\!1/2}\Bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big(\mathbb{E}[L_{T}(B+s\Delta,y)]\big)^{2\beta}\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy\Bigg)^{\!1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\Bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|\nabla_{b}L_{T}(B+s\Delta,y)\|_{2}^{2}\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy\Bigg)^{\!1/2}\Bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big(\mathbb{E}[L_{T}(B+s\Delta,y)]\big)^{2\beta}\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy\Bigg)^{\!1/2}.

Recall that

\|\nabla_{b}L_{T}(b,y)\|_{2}^{2}\;\leq\;\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}\,L_{T}(b,y)^{2}\big(1+\|y\|_{2}^{2}+T^{2}\|a(b)\|_{2}^{2}\big).

With the identity
L_{T}(b,y)^{2}\varphi_{T}(y)=e^{T\|a(b)\|_{2}^{2}}\,\varphi_{T}(y-2Ta(b))
and a Gaussian
moment bound,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|\nabla_{b}L_{T}(B+s\Delta,y)\|_{2}^{2}\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy\;\leq\;C\,\big(1+\|a(B+s\Delta)\|_{2}^{2}\big)\,e^{T\|a(B+s\Delta)\|_{2}^{2}}.

Since
a(B+s\Delta)=a(B)+s\,\sigma^{-1}\Delta
,

\|a(B+s\Delta)\|_{2}^{2}\leq 2\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}+2\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}\,\|\Delta\|_{2}^{2},

we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|\nabla_{b}L_{T}(B+s\Delta,y)\|_{2}^{2}\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy\;\leq\;C(B)\,\exp\!\big(C_{1}\,\|\Delta\|_{2}^{2}\big),

with
C_{1}:=2T\,\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}
and

C(B):=C\,(1+2\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2})\,e^{2T\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}}
.
With a sub-Gaussian assumption

4T\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}<\gamma_{0}^{2}

and

\mathbb{E}[\exp(\gamma^{2}\|B\|_{2}^{2})]<\infty
for all
\gamma<\gamma_{0}
.
Using
\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}\leq 2\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}\|B\|_{2}^{2}+2\|m\|_{2}^{2}
,
we obtain

C(B)\;\leq\;K\,(1+\|B\|_{2}^{2})\,\exp\!\big(\kappa\|B\|_{2}^{2}\big),\qquad K:=C\,c_{0}\,e^{4T\|m\|_{2}^{2}},\ \ \kappa:=4T\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}.

Hence
C(B)
is finite
\mathbb{P}_{0}
almost surely.

Next, we upper bound the second term. We define

I_{\beta}(s)\;:=\;\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big(\mathbb{E}\big[L_{T}(B+s\Delta,y)\big]\Big)^{2\beta}\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy.

When
\beta<0
(i.e.
\alpha<0
), set

a_{s}(B):=\sigma^{-1}(B+s\Delta)-m.

By the Jensen inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[L_{T}(B+s\Delta,y)\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\exp\!\Big(\langle a_{s}(B),y\rangle-\frac{T}{2}\|a_{s}(B)\|_{2}^{2}\Big)\right]

\displaystyle\geq\exp\!\Big(\langle\mathbb{E}[a_{s}(B)],y\rangle-\frac{T}{2}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\|a_{s}(B)\|_{2}^{2}\right]\Big).

Since
2\beta<0
, raising both sides to the power
2\beta
reverses the inequality:

\Big(\mathbb{E}[L_{T}(B+s\Delta,y)]\Big)^{2\beta}\;\leq\;\exp\!\Big(2\beta\,\langle\mathbb{E}[a_{s}(B)],y\rangle-\beta T\,\mathbb{E}\left[\|a_{s}(B)\|_{2}^{2}\right]\Big).

Integrating against
\varphi_{T}
, we obtain

I_{\beta}(s)\;\leq\;\exp\!\Big(\big(2\beta^{2}-\beta\big)\,T\,\mathbb{E}\left[\|a_{s}(B)\|_{2}^{2}\right]\Big)<\infty.

When
\beta>0
, we first fix
r>1
with
r\geq 2\beta
. By the Lyapunov and Jensen inequalities,

I_{\beta}(s)\;\leq\;\Bigg(\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}L_{T}(B+s\Delta,y)^{\,r}\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy\Big]\Bigg)^{\!2\beta/r}.

Completing the square in
y
gives the closed form

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}L_{T}(b,y)^{\,r}\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy\;=\;\exp\!\Big(\tfrac{T}{2}\,(r^{2}-r)\,\|\sigma^{-1}b-m\|_{2}^{2}\Big).

Therefore, with

c_{r}\;:=\;\frac{T}{2}\,(r^{2}-r)>0,

we obtain

I_{\beta}(s)\;\leq\;\Big(\mathbb{E}\left[\,e^{\,c_{r}\,\|a_{s}(B)\|_{2}^{2}}\right]\Big)^{2\beta/r}.

Since

\|a_{s}(B)\|_{2}^{2}=\|\sigma^{-1}(B+s\Delta)-m\|_{2}^{2}\leq 2\|\sigma^{-1}(B+s\Delta)\|_{2}^{2}+2\|m\|_{2}^{2}\leq 4\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}\big(\|B\|_{2}^{2}+\|\Delta\|_{2}^{2}\big)+2\|m\|_{2}^{2},

then

\mathbb{E}\left[\,e^{\,c_{r}\,\|a_{s}(B)\|_{2}^{2}}\right]\;\leq\;e^{\,2c_{r}\|m\|_{2}^{2}}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\!\left(4c_{r}\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}\big(\|B\|_{2}^{2}+\|\Delta\|_{2}^{2}\big)\right)\right].

Let
p>1
and
q:=\frac{p}{p-1}
. By the Hölder inequality,

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\!\left(4c_{r}\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}\big(\|B\|_{2}^{2}+\|\Delta\|_{2}^{2}\big)\right)\right]\;\leq\;\Big(\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\,ap\,\|B\|_{2}^{2}}\right]\Big)^{\!1/p}\;\Big(\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\,aq\,\|\Delta\|_{2}^{2}}\right]\Big)^{\!1/q},

where
a:=4c_{r}\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}=2T(r^{2}-r)\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}.

Putting the pieces together,

I_{\beta}(s)\;\leq\;\underbrace{\exp\!\big(2\beta T(r-1)\|m\|_{2}^{2}\big)\;\Big(\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\,ap\,\|B\|_{2}^{2}}\right]\Big)^{\!2\beta/(rp)}}_{=:\penalty\ C_{\beta,r,p}}\;\cdot\;\underbrace{\Big(\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\,aq\,\|\Delta\|_{2}^{2}}\right]\Big)^{\!2\beta/(rq)}}_{=:\penalty\ M_{\Delta}(\theta)^{\,2\beta/(rq)}},

where
\theta:=aq=2T(r^{2}-r)\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}\,q.

Recall the sub-Gaussian assumption: there exists
\gamma_{0}>0
such that

\mathbb{E}[e^{\gamma^{2}\|B\|_{2}^{2}}]<\infty
for all
\gamma<\gamma_{0}
. Then
C_{\beta,r,p}<\infty
implies

\;ap\;=\;2T(r^{2}-r)\,\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}\,p\;<\;\gamma_{0}^{\,2}\;

and
M_{\Delta}(\theta)^{\,2\beta/(rq)}<\infty
implies

\theta\leq\tau
.

Let
S:=\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}
. Taking the minimal admissible
r=2\beta
, we have

a(\beta)\;:=\;2T\,(r^{2}-r)\,S\;=\;2T\,(4\beta^{2}-2\beta)\,S.

For a fixed cost scale
\tau>0
, the Hölder coefficients
q=\dfrac{\tau}{a(\beta)}
and
p=\dfrac{q}{q-1}=\dfrac{\tau}{\tau-a(\beta)}
(valid when
\tau>a(\beta)
) yield the
B
-side requirement

\ \tau\;>\;a(\beta)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\gamma_{0}^{\,2}\;>\;\frac{\tau\,a(\beta)}{\tau-a(\beta)}.

Equivalently, in terms of
\beta
,

\ \tau\;>\;2T\,(4\beta^{2}-2\beta)\,S\qquad\text{and}\qquad\gamma_{0}^{\,2}\;>\;\frac{\tau\cdot 2T\,(4\beta^{2}-2\beta)\,S}{\tau-2T\,(4\beta^{2}-2\beta)\,S}.

Proof of Theorem
5
.

Now we are ready to prove Theorem
5
.
We recall that the cost function is
c(u,v)=e^{\tau||u-v||_{2}^{2}}-1
and first do the case when
\alpha<0
and solve the concave optimization problem

\arg\max_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{U}_{\delta,B}^{\text{OT}}(\mathbb{P}_{0})}\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{Q}).

To begin with, we define, for fixed
\delta
and
q
, the collection of couplings

C_{\delta}:=\left\{\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}):\pi(.,\mathbb{R}^{d})=\mathbb{P}_{0},\int\left(e^{\tau||x-y||_{2}^{2}}-1\right)\pi(dx,dy)\leq\delta\right\}.

These couplings play an important role.
In our case, we can write
B
and

as the couplings, and
\Delta
is the non-deterministic transport (may have randomness that is not from
B
, and we write only
\mathbb{E}
to represent the non-deterministic coupling).

From the Jensen inequality,

\exp\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\tau\left\|\Delta\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]\right)\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\tau\left\|\Delta\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)\right]\leq\delta+1.

Therefore, as
\delta\to 0
,

\left\|\Delta\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\Delta\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]\leq\tfrac{1}{\tau}\log(1+\delta)=\frac{\delta}{\tau}+o(\delta).

With the Taylor expansion and the Fubini theorem (valid by Lemma
2
), for
\delta>0
,

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{U}_{\delta,B}^{\text{OT}}(\mathbb{P}_{0})}\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{Q})-\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{P}_{0})

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\Delta:\pi\in C_{\delta}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[L_{T}(B+\Delta,y)\right]^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}-\mathbb{E}\left[L_{T}(B,y)\right]^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}\right)\varphi_{T}(y)dy

\displaystyle=\sup_{\Delta:\pi\in C_{\delta}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\left[L_{T}(B+s\Delta,y)\right]^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle\nabla_{b}L_{T}(B+s\Delta,y),\Delta\right\rangle\right]ds\varphi_{T}(y)dy

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{\delta}{\tau}+o(\delta)}\sup_{\Delta:\pi\in C_{\delta}}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla_{b}L_{T}(B+s\Delta,y)\mathbb{E}\left[L_{T}(B+s\Delta,y)\right]^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\varphi_{T}(y)dy\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}ds,

where the last step is by the Hölder inequality.

Any choice of
\pi^{\delta}\in C_{\delta}
(and the corresponding
\Delta_{\delta}
) converges to the pushforward measure of
\mathbb{P}_{0}
under the map
x\mapsto(x,x)
on
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
in the topology induced by the map
(x,y)\mapsto e^{\tau||x-y||_{2}^{2}}-1
.

By Lemma
2
, there exists constants
C(B)>0
and finite
\mathbb{P}_{0}
almost surely and
C_{1}\leq\tau
such that

\left\|\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla_{b}L_{T}(B+s\Delta,y)\mathbb{E}\left[L_{T}(B+s\Delta,y)\right]^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\varphi_{T}(y)dy\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq C(B)\exp(C_{1}\left\|\Delta\right\|_{2}^{2}).

for all fixed
s\in[0,1]
, small
\delta
, and almost every
B
and
y
.

Fix a sequence
\delta_{n}\downarrow 0
and pick any
\pi^{\delta_{n}}\in C_{\delta_{n}}
with a
law of
(B,B+\Delta_{\delta_{n}})
. Define, for
s\in[0,1]
,

X_{\delta_{n}}(s)\ :=\ \Big\|\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla_{b}L_{T}(B+s\Delta_{\delta_{n}},y)\Big(\mathbb{E}[L_{T}(B+s\Delta_{\delta_{n}},y)]\Big)^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy\Big\|_{2}^{2}.

By Lemma
2
, there exist
C(B)>0
and finite
\mathbb{P}_{0}
almost surely and
C_{1}\leq\tau
such that, for all
s\in[0,1]
and all admissible
\Delta
,

X_{\delta_{n}}(s)\ \leq\ C(B)\,\exp\!\big(C_{1}\|\Delta_{\delta_{n}}\|_{2}^{2}\big)\qquad\mathbb{P}_{0}\text{-a.s.}.

Moreover,
\mathbb{E}[e^{\tau\|\Delta_{\delta_{n}}\|_{2}^{2}}]\leq 1+\delta_{n}
as
n\to\infty
.

Choose any
\eta>0
with
2(1+\eta)C_{1}\leq\tau
(possible since
C_{1}<\frac{\tau}{2}
).
Using the Hölder inequality,

\sup_{n}\mathbb{E}\big[X_{\delta_{n}}(s)^{1+\eta}\big]\ \leq\ \left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}\big[C(B)^{\,2(1+\eta)}\big]\,\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\sup_{n}\mathbb{E}\exp\!\big(2(1+\eta)C_{1}\|\Delta_{\delta_{n}}\|_{2}^{2}\big)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}<\ \infty,

where
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}[C(B)^{2(1+\eta)}]<\infty
holds under the sub-Gaussian assumption (recall the proof of Lemma
2
). Hence,
\{X_{\delta_{n}}(s)\}_{n}
is uniformly integrable.
Since
\Delta_{\delta_{n}}\to 0
in
L^{2}
(hence in probability) and

b\mapsto\nabla_{b}L_{T}(b,y)
is continuous, we have

X_{\delta_{n}}(s)\to X_{0}(s)
in probability, where

X_{0}(s)\ =\ \Big\|\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla_{b}L_{T}(B,y)\Big(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}[L_{T}(B,y)]\Big)^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy\Big\|_{2}^{2}.

Thus, by Vitali’s theorem,

\mathbb{E}\big[X_{\delta_{n}}(s)\big]\ \longrightarrow\ \mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}\big[X_{0}(s)\big]\qquad\text{for every }s\in[0,1].

We also have the

–uniform integrable bound

\mathbb{E}\big[X_{\delta_{n}}(s)\big]\ \leq\ \Big(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}[C(B)^{1+\eta}]\Big)^{\frac{1}{1+\eta}}\Big(\mathbb{E}e^{\tau\|\Delta_{\delta_{n}}\|_{2}^{2}}\Big)^{\frac{\eta}{1+\eta}}\ \leq\ \Big(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}[C(B)^{1+\eta}]\Big)^{\frac{1}{1+\eta}}(1+\delta_{n})^{\frac{\eta}{1+\eta}},

independent of

. Therefore, by dominated convergence theorem in

,

\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\big[X_{\delta_{n}}(s)\big]^{1/2}\,ds\ \longrightarrow\ \int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}\big[X_{0}(s)\big]^{1/2}\,ds,

which completes the upper bound argument and gives

\sup_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{U}_{\delta,B}^{\text{OT}}(\mathbb{P}_{0})}\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{Q})\leq\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{P}_{0})+\sqrt{\frac{\delta}{\tau}}\|H\|_{L^{2}_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{0})}+o(\sqrt{\delta}).

Next we prove the lower bound by a deterministic coupling. From Lemma
2
, fix a deterministic function
h
. Then by the integral form of Taylor expansion, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}((\mathrm{Id}+h)_{\#}\mathbb{P}_{0})-\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{P}_{0})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}_{0}}\left[L_{T}(B+h(B),y)\right]^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}\left[L_{T}(B,y)\right]^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}\right)\varphi_{T}(y)dy

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}\left[L_{T}(B+sh(B),y)\right]^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}\left[\left\langle\nabla_{b}L_{T}(B+sh(B),y),h(B)\right\rangle\right]ds\varphi_{T}(y)dy.

Define the deterministic map

h_{\delta}(b)\;=\;t_{\delta}\,H(b),\qquad t_{\delta}\;=\;\sqrt{\frac{\delta}{\tau\,\|H\|_{L^{2}_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{0})}^{2}}}\,(1+o(1))\quad(\delta\downarrow 0).

Then
h_{\delta}
is feasible for the exponential cost budget

\mathbb{E}\big[e^{\tau\|h_{\delta}(B)\|_{2}^{2}}-1\big]\leq\delta
for all sufficiently small
\delta
, and

\mathcal{J}\big((\mathrm{Id}+h_{\delta})_{\#}\mathbb{P}_{0}\big)-\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{P}_{0})\;=\;\sqrt{\frac{\delta}{\tau}}\|H\|_{L^{2}_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{0})}\,\;+\;o\!\big(\sqrt{\delta}\big).

Consequently, the deterministic class yields the sharp lower bound matching the upper bound’s rate and constant.
To see this, set
h_{t}(b):=t\,H(b)
. For fixed
y
, let

\psi_{y}(t)\;:=\;\Big(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}[L_{T}(B+h_{t}(B),y)]\Big)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}.

Then the above Taylor expansion becomes

\mathcal{J}\big((\mathrm{Id}+h_{t})_{\#}\mathbb{P}_{0}\big)-\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{P}_{0})\;=\;t\,\|H\|_{L^{2}_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{0})}^{2}\;+\;o(t)\qquad(t\downarrow 0).

Since
\|h_{t}(B)\|_{2}^{2}=t^{2}\|H(B)\|_{2}^{2}
and
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}_{0}}\left[\|H(B)\|_{2}^{2}\right]<\infty
, we have

\mathbb{E}\big[e^{\tau\|h_{t}(B)\|_{2}^{2}}\big]\;=\;1+\tau t^{2}\,\|H\|_{L^{2}_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{0})}^{2}+o(t^{2})\qquad(t\downarrow 0).

Thus there exists
t_{\delta}=\sqrt{\delta/(\tau\|H\|_{L^{2}_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{0})}^{2})}\,(1+o(1))
such that

\mathbb{E}[e^{\tau\|h_{t_{\delta}}(B)\|_{2}^{2}}]\leq 1+\delta
for all sufficiently small
\delta
.
Plugging
t=t_{\delta}
into the expansion above yields

\mathcal{J}\big((\mathrm{Id}+h_{t_{\delta}})_{\#}\mathbb{P}_{0}\big)-\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{P}_{0})\;=\;\|H\|_{L^{2}_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{0})}^{2}\,t_{\delta}+o(t_{\delta})\;=\;\|H\|_{L^{2}_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{0})}\,\sqrt{\frac{\delta}{\tau}}\;+\;o\!\big(\sqrt{\delta}\big),

as claimed.

Since Lemma
2
provides the estimates needed for the case when
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the proof of the case when
\alpha\in(0,1)
and the solution of the convex optimization problem

\arg\min_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{U}_{\delta,B}^{\text{OT}}(\mathbb{P}_{0})}\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{Q})

is almost verbatim and we only need to notice that the optimal direction is on the opposite of the previous case.
∎

Appendix D
Generalization of Nonlinear Projection Theorem with Non-compact Support

In this section, we derive a stochastic asymptotic upper bound of the RWPI under the nonlinear projection when

is not compactly supported. We will use a method that does not depend on Wasserstein geometry. Several technical lemmas are presented at the end of this section. Recall that we consider the case when
\alpha<1
and
\alpha\neq 0
with the cost function
c_{\tau}(\Delta)\;:=\;e^{\,\tau\,\|\Delta\|_{2}^{2}}\;-\;1
for a displacement
\Delta
.
B^{(i)}
are i.i.d. (calibrated) samples of

from a distribution
\mathbb{P}^{*}
, and they are used to constitute an empirical measure

, where

and

represents the optimal Lagrangian multiplier. We still make a sub-Gaussian assumption on

(only the sub-Gaussian parameters are different), thus we omit some proofs for simplicity.

Assumption 5
.

Suppose there exists
\gamma_{0}>0

\frac{\gamma_{0}^{2}}{\left\|\sigma^{-1}\right\|_{F}^{2}}>T\max\Big\{\,4\beta^{2}-2\beta,\ \tfrac{2}{\beta-2},\ 16,\ 8\beta+8\,\Big\}

and
\beta=\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}
.

Theorem 6
.

We denote
\alpha(y)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}\left[L_{T}(B,y)\right]
for each fixed
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Under Assumption
5
, as
n\to\infty
, we have the asymptotic stochastic upper bound:

nR_{n}(k^{*})\lesssim_{D}L:=\frac{\tau Z^{2}}{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}\left[\left\lVert\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g_{k^{*}}^{\prime}(\alpha(y))\nabla_{b}L_{T}(B,y)\varphi_{T}(y)dy\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right]},

where

Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,h(\mathbb{P}^{*}))

and the functional
h
is defined by

h(\mathbb{P}^{*})=\int\int g_{k^{*}}^{\prime}(\alpha(y_{1}))g_{k^{*}}^{\prime}(\alpha(y_{2}))\text{Cov}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}\left(L_{T}(B,y_{1}),L_{T}(B,y_{2})\right)\varphi_{T}(y_{1})\varphi_{T}(y_{2})dy_{1}dy_{2}<\infty.

The proof of Theorem
6
consists of six parts. First, we use Taylor expansions to separate terms in
R_{n}(k^{*})
and obtain Eq. (
55
). Second, we estimate some remainder terms (
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
in Eq. (
55
)). Third, we compute the optimal deterministic coupling in Eq. (
56
). Fourth, we establish various senses of convergence to be used in the main statement. Fifth, we continue to compute the remainder term. Sixth, we summarize all the required assumptions and conclude the asymptotic result.

D.1
Part I

To begin with, the uniqueness of
k^{*}
is easy to see. In order to prove Theorem
6
, we define a functional
J
on the space of probability measures such that (for notational convenience)
g(x)=g_{k^{*}}(x)
and

J(\mathbb{P})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[L_{T}(B,y)\right]\right)\varphi_{T}(y)dy.

In particular,

g^{\prime}(x)=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\left(\frac{e^{rT}}{k^{*}}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}x^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}},

and

g^{\prime\prime}(x)=\frac{\alpha}{(1-\alpha)^{2}}\left(\frac{e^{rT}}{k^{*}}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}x^{\frac{2\alpha-1}{1-\alpha}},

We notice that
L_{T}
and
g
are both twice continuously differentiable functions in each argument. Therefore, the RWP function becomes

\displaystyle R_{n}(k^{*})

\displaystyle:=\inf_{\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{F}_{k^{*}}}D_{c}(\mathbb{P}_{n},\mathbb{P})

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{D_{c}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{P}_{n}):\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}I\left(\frac{k^{*}e^{-rT}}{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[L_{T}(B,y)\right]}\right)\varphi_{T}(y)dy=x_{0}e^{rT}\right\}

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{D_{c}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{P}_{n}):J(\mathbb{P})=J(\mathbb{P}^{*})\right\}.

Given the empirical measure
\mathbb{P}_{n}
, we define the perturbed empirical measure
\mathbb{P}_{n}^{\Delta}
by pushing each atom
B^{(i)}
to
B^{(i)}+\Delta_{i}n^{-1/2}
for
\Delta_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We want to show that for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
, for all sufficiently large
n
, with probability at least
1-\varepsilon
, there exists a correction
\Delta
with
\left\|\Delta\right\|_{n}=O_{p}(1)
and
J(\mathbb{P}_{n}^{\Delta})=J(\mathbb{P}^{*})
, where we use the notation for
\gamma\in\mathbb{Z}
,
\left\|\Delta\right\|_{n}^{\gamma}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\|\Delta_{i}\right\|^{\gamma}.

For each
y
and

, set
h_{i}:=\Delta_{i}/\sqrt{n}
.
the Taylor theorem in the

L_{T}(B^{(i)}+h_{i},y)=L_{T}(B^{(i)},y)+\nabla_{b}L_{T}(B^{(i)},y)\!\cdot\!h_{i}+\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)\,h_{i}^{\top}\nabla_{b}^{2}L_{T}(B^{(i)}+th_{i},y)\,h_{i}\,dt.

Defining
m_{\mathbb{P}}(y)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[L_{T}(B,y)\right]
for a probability measure
\mathbb{P}
yields

m_{\mathbb{P}_{n}^{\Delta}}(y)=m_{\mathbb{P}_{n}}(y)\;+\;A_{1}(y)\;+\;A_{2}(y),

with

A_{1}(y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla_{b}L_{T}(B^{(i)},y)\cdot\Delta_{i}

and

A_{2}(y)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)\,\frac{\Delta_{i}^{\top}}{\sqrt{n}}\,\nabla_{b}^{2}L_{T}\!\Big(B^{(i)}+t\tfrac{\Delta_{i}}{\sqrt{n}},y\Big)\,\frac{\Delta_{i}}{\sqrt{n}}\,dt.

Another Taylor expansion gives

\displaystyle g(m_{\mathbb{P}_{n}^{\Delta}}(y))

\displaystyle=g(m_{\mathbb{P}_{n}}(y))+g^{\prime}(m_{\mathbb{P}_{n}}(y))(A_{1}(y)+A_{2}(y))

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)g^{\prime\prime}(m_{\mathbb{P}_{n}}(y)+t(A_{1}(y)+A_{2}(y)))(A_{1}(y)+A_{2}(y))^{2}dt,

which implies that

J(\mathbb{P}_{n}^{\Delta})-J(\mathbb{P}^{*})=J(\mathbb{P}_{n})-J(\mathbb{P}^{*})+\int g^{\prime}(m_{\mathbb{P}_{n}}(y))A_{1}(y)\varphi_{T}(y)dy+R_{1}+R_{2},

(55)

where

R_{1}=\int g^{\prime}(m_{\mathbb{P}_{n}}(y))A_{2}(y)\varphi_{T}(y)dy,

and

R_{2}=\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)\int g^{\prime\prime}(m_{\mathbb{P}_{n}}(y)+t(A_{1}(y)+A_{2}(y)))(A_{1}(y)+A_{2}(y))^{2}\varphi_{T}(y)dydt.

If we define

C_{i}(n)=\int g^{\prime}(m_{\mathbb{P}_{n}}(y))\nabla_{b}L_{T}(B^{(i)},y)\varphi_{T}(y)dy,

then

\int g^{\prime}(m_{\mathbb{P}_{n}}(y))A_{1}(y)\varphi_{T}(y)dy=\frac{1}{n^{1/2}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}C_{i}(n)\cdot\Delta_{i}.

with

Lemma 4
.

In the context of proof of Theorem
6
, under Assumption

, when
\alpha<1
and
\alpha\neq 0

such that

|R_{2}|=O_{p}\left(\frac{\left\|\Delta\right\|_{n}^{2}}{n}\exp\left(C\max_{1\ \leq i\leq n}\frac{\left\|\Delta_{i}\right\|^{2}}{n}\right)\right)+O_{p}\!\left(\frac{\|\Delta\|_{n}^{2}}{n}\cdot\frac{\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\|\Delta_{i}\|^{2}}{n}\;\exp\!\Big(C\,\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\frac{\|\Delta_{i}\|^{2}}{n}\Big)\right).

Proof.

Bounding
R_{2}
is equivalent to bound these two terms:

|R_{2}|\leq I_{1}+I_{2},

where

I_{1}=2\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)\int\left|g^{\prime\prime}(m_{\mathbb{P}_{n}}(y)+t(A_{1}(y)+A_{2}(y)))\right|A_{1}(y)^{2}\varphi_{T}(y)dydt

and

I_{2}=2\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)\int\left|g^{\prime\prime}(m_{\mathbb{P}_{n}}(y)+t(A_{1}(y)+A_{2}(y)))\right|A_{2}(y)^{2}\varphi_{T}(y)dydt.

Recall that
\beta=\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}
and
g^{\prime\prime}(x)=C_{\alpha}\,x^{\beta-1}
for a constant
C_{\alpha}
. Cauchy–Schwarz inequality yields

\displaystyle|A_{1}(y)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\nabla_{b}L_{T}(B^{(i)},y)\|_{2}^{2}\Big)^{\!1/2}\!\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\Delta_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\Big)^{\!1/2}=\sqrt{\frac{\|\Delta\|_{n}^{\,2}}{n}}\;\Big(\overline{\Xi^{2}}(y)\Big)^{\!1/2},

with

\overline{\Xi^{2}}(y):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\nabla_{b}L_{T}(B^{(i)},y)\|_{2}^{2}\ \leq\ C_{1}\Big(\|y\|_{2}^{2}\,\overline{L^{2}}(y)\;+\;T^{2}\,\overline{A^{2}L^{2}}(y)\Big),

where

\overline{L^{2}}(y):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{T}(B^{(i)},y)^{2},\qquad\overline{A^{2}L^{2}}(y):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|a(B^{(i)})\|_{2}^{2}\,L_{T}(B^{(i)},y)^{2},

and therefore,

|A_{1}(y)|^{2}\;\leq\;\frac{\|\Delta\|_{n}^{\,2}}{n}\;C_{1}\Big(\|y\|_{2}^{2}\,\overline{L^{2}}(y)\;+\;T^{2}\,\overline{A^{2}L^{2}}(y)\Big).

Recall that
h_{i}:=\Delta_{i}/\sqrt{n}
. Define the shifted mixture

m_{\mathbb{P}_{n}}^{(h)}(y):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{T}(B^{(i)}+h_{i},y).

Therefore

x_{t}(y):=(1-t)\,m_{\mathbb{P}_{n}}(y)+t\,m_{\mathbb{P}_{n}}^{(h)}(y)=m_{\mathbb{P}_{n}}(y)+t\big(A_{1}(y)+A_{2}(y)\big).

Let
p:=\beta-1=\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}-1
.
We write
m:=m_{\mathbb{P}_{n}}(y)>0
and
m^{(h)}:=m_{\mathbb{P}_{n}}^{(h)}(y)>0
to lighten notation, so

x_{t}=(1-t)m+tm^{(h)}
.

When
p<0
or
p\geq 1
,
the function
f(x)=x^{p}
on
(0,\infty)
is convex. Therefore

x_{t}^{p}=f\big((1-t)m+tm^{(h)}\big)\;\leq\;(1-t)f(m)+tf(m^{(h)})=(1-t)m^{p}+t(m^{(h)})^{p}\;\leq\;m^{p}+(m^{(h)})^{p}.

When
p\in(0,1)
, the map
f(x)=x^{p}
is increasing and concave. Since
x_{t}\leq m+m^{(h)}
and
f
is increasing, then

x_{t}^{p}\ \leq\ (m+m^{(h)})^{p}\ \leq\ m^{p}+(m^{(h)})^{p},

where the last step uses the subadditivity
(a+b)^{p}\leq a^{p}+b^{p}
for
a,b\geq 0
and
0<p\leq 1
.

Therefore,

|g^{\prime\prime}(x_{t})|=|C_{\alpha}|\,x_{t}^{p}\leq|C_{\alpha}|\big(m^{\beta-1}+(m^{(h)})^{\beta-1}\big).

Hence

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=2\!\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)\!\int|g^{\prime\prime}(x_{t})|\,A_{1}^{2}(y)\,\varphi_{T}(y)dy\ \leq\ \frac{C}{n}\,\|\Delta\|_{n}^{\,2}\,\Big(J_{1}+J_{2}+J_{1}^{(h)}+J_{2}^{(h)}\Big),

where

J_{1}:=\int\|y\|^{2}m_{\mathbb{P}_{n}}^{\beta-1}\,\overline{L^{2}}\,\varphi_{T},\qquad J_{2}:=\int m_{\mathbb{P}_{n}}^{\beta-1}\,\overline{A^{2}L^{2}}\,\varphi_{T},

J_{1}^{(h)}:=\int\|y\|^{2}(m_{\mathbb{P}_{n}}^{(h)})^{\beta-1}\,\overline{L^{2}}\,\varphi_{T},\qquad J_{2}^{(h)}:=\int(m_{\mathbb{P}_{n}}^{(h)})^{\beta-1}\,\overline{A^{2}L^{2}}\,\varphi_{T}.

We first focus on the case when
p:=\beta-1<0
, by the Jensen inequality,

m_{\mathbb{P}_{n}}(y)\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{n}}\big[e^{\langle a(B),y\rangle-\frac{T}{2}\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}}\big]\;\geq\;\exp\!\Big(\langle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{n}}[a(B)],y\rangle-\tfrac{T}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{n}}\left[\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}\right]\Big).

Raising to the negative power
p<0
reverses the inequality, giving

m_{\mathbb{P}_{n}}(y)^{p}\;\leq\;C_{a}\,\exp\!\big(p\,\langle v,y\rangle\big),\qquad C_{a}:=\exp\!\Big(-\tfrac{pT}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{n}}\left[\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}\right]\Big),\ \ v:=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{n}}[a(B)].

We denote
c_{i}:=\sigma^{-1}h_{i}
, then

m_{\mathbb{P}_{n}}^{(h)}(y)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{T}(B^{(i)}+h_{i},y)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\exp\!\Big(\,\big\langle a(B^{(i)})+c_{i},\,y\big\rangle-\tfrac{T}{2}\|a(B^{(i)})+c_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\Big).

Introduce the notations

v_{h}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(a(B^{(i)})+c_{i}\big),\qquad s_{2,h}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|a(B^{(i)})+c_{i}\|_{2}^{2},\qquad H_{n}:=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\|c_{i}\|_{2}.

By Jensen’s inequality,

m_{\mathbb{P}_{n}}^{(h)}(y)\ \geq\ \exp\!\Big(\,\langle v_{h},y\rangle-\tfrac{T}{2}\,s_{2,h}\Big).

Since
p<0
, raising both sides to the power
p
reverses the inequality:

\big(m_{\mathbb{P}_{n}}^{(h)}(y)\big)^{p}\ \leq\ \exp\!\Big(\,p\,\langle v_{h},y\rangle-\tfrac{pT}{2}\,s_{2,h}\Big).

Set

s_{2}:=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{n}}\!\big[\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}\big],\qquad C_{a}:=\exp\!\Big(-\tfrac{pT}{2}\,s_{2}\Big).

Hence

J_{1}\ \leq\ \frac{C_{a}}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int\|y\|_{2}^{2}\,e^{\,p\langle v,y\rangle}\,L_{T}(B^{(i)},y)^{2}\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy.

Write
a_{i}:=a(B^{(i)})
and note
L_{T}(B^{(i)},y)^{2}=\exp(2\langle a_{i},y\rangle-T\|a_{i}\|_{2}^{2})
.
Let
Y\sim N(0,TI_{d})
so
\varphi_{T}
is its density. Then, with

\lambda_{i}:=2a_{i}+p\,v\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

\int\|y\|_{2}^{2}\,e^{\,p\langle v,y\rangle}\,L_{T}(B^{(i)},y)^{2}\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy=e^{-T\|a_{i}\|_{2}^{2}}\,(Td+T^{2}\|\lambda_{i}\|_{2}^{2})\,e^{\frac{T}{2}\|\lambda_{i}\|_{2}^{2}}.

Therefore

J_{1}\ \leq\ \frac{C_{a}}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(Td+T^{2}\|\lambda_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\Big)\,\exp\!\Big(\tfrac{T}{2}\|\lambda_{i}\|_{2}^{2}-T\|a_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\Big).

By the inequality
(\|u+z\|_{2}^{2}\leq 2\|u\|_{2}^{2}+2\|z\|_{2}^{2})
with
u=2a_{i}
,
z=pv
:

\|\lambda_{i}\|_{2}^{2}=\|2a_{i}+pv\|_{2}^{2}\ \leq\ 8\|a_{i}\|_{2}^{2}+2p^{2}\|v\|_{2}^{2},

hence

\frac{T}{2}\|\lambda_{i}\|_{2}^{2}-T\|a_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\ \leq\ 3T\|a_{i}\|_{2}^{2}+Tp^{2}\|v\|_{2}^{2}.

Also
\|\lambda_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\leq 8\|a_{i}\|_{2}^{2}+2p^{2}\|v\|_{2}^{2}
implies

Td+T^{2}\|\lambda_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\leq C\big(1+\|a_{i}\|_{2}^{2}+\|v\|_{2}^{2}\big)

for a constant
C=C(T,p,d)
.
Therefore

\displaystyle J_{1}\

\displaystyle\leq\ C\,C_{a}\,e^{Tp^{2}\|v\|_{2}^{2}}\,\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(1+\|a_{i}\|_{2}^{2}+\|v\|_{2}^{2}\big)\,e^{3T\|a_{i}\|_{2}^{2}}.

Set

A_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{3T\|a_{i}\|_{2}^{2}},\qquad B_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|a_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\,e^{3T\|a_{i}\|_{2}^{2}}.

Then

J_{1}\ \leq\ C\,C_{a}\,e^{Tp^{2}\|v\|_{2}^{2}}\,\Big[(1+\|v\|_{2}^{2})\,A_{n}+B_{n}\Big].

Since
a(B)=\sigma^{-1}B-m
, we have

\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}\leq 2\|\sigma^{-1}B\|_{2}^{2}+2\|m\|_{2}^{2}
,
and thus

\mathbb{E}\big[e^{3T\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}}\big]\ \leq\ e^{6T\|m\|_{2}^{2}}\,\mathbb{E}\big[e^{6T\|\sigma^{-1}B\|_{2}^{2}}\big]\ \leq\ e^{6T\|m\|_{2}^{2}}\,\mathbb{E}\big[e^{6T\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}\|B\|_{2}^{2}}\big].

Hence
\,\mathbb{E}[e^{3T\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}}]<\infty\,

\mathbb{E}[\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}e^{3T\|a(B)\|_{2}^{2}}]<\infty
.

and

.

. The bound of

\displaystyle J_{1}\

\displaystyle\leq\ \Big(\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int\|y\|^{2s}L_{T}(B^{(i)},y)^{2s}\varphi_{T}(y)dy\Big)^{\!1/s}

Similarly,

J_{1}^{(h)}=\int\|y\|^{2}\,(m_{\mathbb{P}_{n}}^{(h)}(y))^{p}\,\overline{L^{2}}(y)\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy\ \leq\ \Big(\int m_{\mathbb{P}_{n}}^{(h)}(y)\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy\Big)^{\!p}\Big(\int\|y\|^{2s}\,\overline{L^{2}}(y)^{\,s}\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy\Big)^{\!1/s}.

When

Completing the square (as in the

|\text{Cov}(X,Y)|\leq\sqrt{\text{Var}(X)\text{Var}(Y)}\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]}
, thus

|h(\mathbb{P}^{*})|\;\leq\;\Bigg(\int|g^{\prime}\!\big(\alpha(y)\big)|\,\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}\!\big[L_{T}(B,y)^{2}\big]}\;\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy\Bigg)^{\!2}.

Another application of Cauchy–Schwarz inequality gives

|h(\mathbb{P}^{*})|\;\leq\;\Bigg(\int g^{\prime}\!\big(\alpha(y)\big)^{2}\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy\Bigg)\Bigg(\int\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}\!\big[L_{T}(B,y)^{2}\big]\;\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy\Bigg).

Hence it suffices to show that the first integral is finite.

Recall that
g^{\prime}(\alpha)=\kappa_{\alpha}\,\alpha^{\beta}
with
\beta=\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}
, then

\int g^{\prime}(\alpha(y))^{2}\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy=\kappa_{\alpha}^{2}\int\alpha(y)^{2\beta}\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy.

For
\beta\geq\tfrac{1}{2}
, the Jensen inequality yields
\alpha(y)^{2\beta}\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}[L_{T}(B,y)^{2\beta}]
.
For
0<\beta<\tfrac{1}{2}
, use
x^{2\beta}\leq C_{\beta}\,(1+x^{2\beta}+x^{2})
for
x\geq 0
.
In both cases,

\int\alpha(y)^{2\beta}\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy\;\leq\;C_{\beta}\Bigg(1+\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}\!\int L_{T}(B,y)^{2\beta}\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy\;+\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}\!\int L_{T}(B,y)^{2}\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy\Bigg).

Again by the Gaussian-tilt bound, for all
b
,

\int L_{T}(b,y)^{2\beta}\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy\;\leq\;K_{2\beta}\,\exp\!\big(u_{2\beta}\|b\|^{2}\big),

with
K_{2\beta}<\infty
and
u_{2\beta}>0
depending on
T,\sigma,m,\beta

(a safe choice is
u_{2\beta}=2(1+\beta)T\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}
).
Therefore

\int g^{\prime}(\alpha(y))^{2}\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy\;\leq\;\kappa_{\alpha}^{2}\,C_{\beta}\Big(1+K_{2\beta}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}e^{u_{2\beta}\|B\|^{2}}+K_{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}e^{u_{2}\|B\|^{2}}\Big),

which is finite provided
u_{2\beta}<\gamma_{0}^{2}
and
u_{2}<\gamma_{0}^{2}
.
Combining above cases yields
h(\mathbb{P}^{*})<\infty
.
A convenient single sufficient condition is

\gamma_{0}^{2}\;>\;8\,T\,\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}\,\max\{\,1+\beta,\,2\,\},

which ensures
u_{2\beta}<\gamma_{0}^{2}
and
u_{2}<\gamma_{0}^{2}
.

Denote
\mu=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}\left[L_{T}(B,.)\right]\in H
. By Lemma
9
, the map
\mathcal{T}:H\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathcal{T}(h)=\int g(h(y))\varphi_{T}(y)dy
is Hadamard differentiable at
\mu
if
\beta\leq 1
and is Hadamard differentiable at
\mu
tangential to
L^{1+\beta}
if
\beta>1
. Thus, from the functional Delta theorem and its weaker version with tangential Hadamard differentiability, we have

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\left(\mathcal{T}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{n}}\left[L_{T}(B,.)\right]\right)-\mathcal{T}(\mu)\right)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{n}\left(\int g(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{n}}\left[L_{T}(B,y)\right])\varphi_{T}(y)dy-\int g(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}\left[L_{T}(B,y)\right])\varphi_{T}(y)dy\right)

\displaystyle=J(\mathbb{P}_{n})-J(\mathbb{P}^{*})\Rightarrow\mathcal{T}^{\prime}_{\mu}(G),

where
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}_{\mu}
is the Hadamard derivative at
\mu
. By Lemma
9
, the limiting distribution becomes

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{\prime}_{\mu}(G)

\displaystyle=\int g^{\prime}(\alpha(y))G(y)\varphi_{T}(y)dy\sim\mathcal{N}(0,h(\mathbb{P}^{*})).

∎

Lemma 9
.

In the context of Lemma
8
, the map
\mathcal{T}
is Hadamard differentiable at
\mu
if
\beta\leq 1
and is Hadamard differentiable at
\mu
tangential to
L^{1+\beta}
if
\beta>1
. In particular, for a fixed direction
v\in H
(or
v\in L^{1+\beta}
in the tangential case),

\mathcal{T}^{\prime}_{\mu}(v)=\int g^{\prime}(\alpha(y))v(y)\varphi_{T}(y)dy.

Proof.

We first focus on the case when
\beta>0
and fix
v\in H
and any perturbations
v_{t}\in H
with
v_{t}\to v
in
H
as
t\downarrow 0
.
We must show

\frac{\mathcal{T}(\alpha+tv_{t})-\mathcal{T}(\alpha)}{t}\;\longrightarrow\;\int g^{\prime}(\alpha)\,v\,\varphi_{T}\quad\text{as }t\downarrow 0.

By the fundamental theorem of calculus,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{T}(\mu+tv_{t})-\mathcal{T}(\mu)}{t}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{g(\mu(y)+tv_{t}(y))-g(\mu(y))}{t}\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g^{\prime}\big(\mu(y)+stv_{t}(y)\big)\,v_{t}(y)\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy\,ds.

Add and subtract
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g^{\prime}(\mu(y))\,v(y)\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy
:

\frac{\mathcal{T}(\mu+tv_{t})-\mathcal{T}(\mu)}{t}-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g^{\prime}(\mu)\,v\,\varphi_{T}=\underbrace{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g^{\prime}(\mu)\,(v_{t}-v)\,\varphi_{T}}_{=:A_{t}}+\underbrace{\int_{0}^{1}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\!\big[g^{\prime}(\mu+stv_{t})-g^{\prime}(\mu)\big]\,v_{t}\,\varphi_{T}\,dy\,ds}_{=:B_{t}}.

By Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

|A_{t}|\;\leq\;\|g^{\prime}(\mu)\|_{L^{2}(\varphi_{T})}\,\|v_{t}-v\|_{L^{2}(\varphi_{T})}\;\longrightarrow\;0,

since
g^{\prime}(\mu)\in L^{2}(\varphi_{T})
(see the estimate in proof of Lemma
8
) and
v_{t}\to v
in
L^{2}(\varphi_{T})
. Hence
A_{t}\to 0
.

Fix
\delta\in(0,\min\{\beta,1\})
. Using the elementary inequality

|x^{\beta}-y^{\beta}|\;\leq\;\frac{\beta}{\delta}\,|x-y|^{\delta}\,\big(x^{\beta-\delta}+y^{\beta-\delta}\big)\qquad(x,y\geq 0),

applied with
x=\mu(y)+st|v_{t}(y)|
,
y=\mu(y)
and
g^{\prime}(m)=\kappa_{\alpha}m^{\beta}
, we obtain

|B_{t}|\;\leq\;C\,|t|^{\delta}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\!\Big(\mu(y)^{\beta-\delta}+(\mu(y)+st|v_{t}(y)|)^{\beta-\delta}\Big)\,|v_{t}(y)|^{1+\delta}\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy.

Using
(a+b)^{\beta-\delta}\leq C\,(a^{\beta-\delta}+b^{\beta-\delta})
and
|t|^{\beta-\delta}\leq 1
for small
t
,

|B_{t}|\;\leq\;C\,|t|^{\delta}\!\left\{\underbrace{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mu(y)^{\beta-\delta}\,|v_{t}(y)|^{1+\delta}\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy}_{I_{t,1}}\;+\;\underbrace{|t|^{\beta-\delta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v_{t}(y)|^{1+\beta}\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy}_{I_{t,2}}\right\}.

For the first term, apply the Hölder inequality with
p=\tfrac{2}{1+\delta}
and
q=\tfrac{2}{1-\delta}
:

I_{t,1}\;\leq\;\|\mu^{\beta-\delta}\|_{L^{q}(\varphi_{T})}\,\|v_{t}\|_{L^{2}(\varphi_{T})}.

Under the sub-Gaussian parameter

\gamma_{0}^{2}\;>\;2(1+\beta)\,T\,\|\sigma^{-1}\|_{F}^{2},

we have

\|\mu^{\beta-\delta}\|_{L^{q}(\varphi_{T})}<\infty
for any small
\delta\in(0,1)
, and since
v_{t}\to v
in
L^{2}(\varphi_{T})
,

\|v_{t}\|_{L^{2}(\varphi_{T})}
is uniformly bounded. Hence
I_{t,1}\leq C
uniformly in
t
.

For the second term, we consider two cases.
When
0<\beta\leq 1
, because
1+\beta\leq 2
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v_{t}|^{1+\beta}\,\varphi_{T}\;\leq\;1+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v_{t}|^{2}\,\varphi_{T}\;\leq\;C,

uniformly in
t
. Choose
\delta=\beta
(so
\delta\leq 1
). Then

|B_{t}|\;\leq\;C\,\big(|t|^{\delta}+|t|^{\delta}\big)\;=\;C\,|t|^{\beta}\ \longrightarrow\ 0.

When
\beta>1
, suppose that
v\in L^{1+\beta}(\varphi_{T})\ \text{and }v_{t}\to v\text{ in }L^{1+\beta}
, then

\sup_{0<t\leq 1}\ \|v_{t}\|_{L^{1+\beta}(\varphi_{T})}\ <\ \infty.

Therefore
I_{t,2}\leq C\,|t|^{\beta-\delta}=C\,|t|^{\beta-1+\varepsilon}\to 0
as
t\downarrow 0
. Hence

|B_{t}|\ \leq\ C\,|t|^{\delta}\,I_{t,1}\;+\;C\,|t|^{\delta}\,I_{t,2}\ \leq\ C\,|t|^{\delta}\;+\;C\,|t|^{\beta-1+\varepsilon}\ \xrightarrow[t\downarrow 0]{}\ 0,

since
\delta=1-\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
\beta-1+\varepsilon>0
.

When
\beta<0
, let
t:=\sigma^{-T}B
and pick any
R>0
; then

\mu(y)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\exp\!\left(\langle t,y\rangle-\tfrac{T}{2}\|t\|^{2}\right)\right]\;\geq\;\mathbb{P}(\|t\|\leq R)\,\exp\!\left(-R\|y\|-\tfrac{T}{2}R^{2}\right)=:c_{0}\,e^{-R\|y\|}.

Hence, for
\varepsilon\in(0,c_{0})
,

\{\mu<\varepsilon\}\ \subset\ \Big\{\|y\|>\tfrac{1}{R}\log\!\tfrac{c_{0}}{\varepsilon}\Big\}.

Since
\varphi_{T}
is a nondegenerate Gaussian measure, there are
C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that

\varphi_{T}\{\mu<\varepsilon\}\ \leq\ \varphi_{T}\!\Big(\|y\|>\tfrac{1}{R}\log\!\tfrac{c_{0}}{\varepsilon}\Big)\ \leq\ C_{1}\,\exp\!\Big(-C_{2}\,(\log(1/\varepsilon))^{2}\Big).

We will also use that for any
a>0
,

\int_{\{\|y\|>L\}}e^{a\|y\|}\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy\ \xrightarrow[L\to\infty]{}\ 0,\quad\text{since }\ e^{a\|y\|}\ll e^{c\|y\|^{2}}\ \text{for Gaussian tails}.

For
\varepsilon>0
define

g^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(m):=\kappa_{\alpha}\,(m\vee\varepsilon)^{\beta},\qquad g_{\varepsilon}(0):=g(0),\quad g_{\varepsilon}(m):=g(0)+\int_{0}^{m}g^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(u)\,du,

and set
\mathcal{T}_{\varepsilon}(h):=\int g_{\varepsilon}(h)\,\varphi_{T}
.
For fixed
\varepsilon>0
,
g^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}
is bounded and Lipschitz on
[0,\infty)
, so the standard

chain rule gives

\frac{\mathcal{T}_{\varepsilon}(\mu+tv_{t})-\mathcal{T}_{\varepsilon}(\mu)}{t}\ \longrightarrow\ \int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(\mu(y))\,v(y)\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy=:\mathcal{T}^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(\mu)[v]\quad\text{as }t\downarrow 0,\ \ v_{t}\to v\text{ in }H.

For any
v_{t}\to v
in
H
as
t\downarrow 0
,

\frac{\mathcal{T}(\mu+tv_{t})-\mathcal{T}(\mu)}{t}-\int g^{\prime}(\mu)\,v\,\varphi_{T}=E_{1,\varepsilon,t}+E_{2,\varepsilon,t}+E_{3,\varepsilon},

where

E_{1,\varepsilon,t}:=\frac{\mathcal{T}(\mu+tv_{t})-\mathcal{T}_{\varepsilon}(\mu+tv_{t})}{t},\ \ E_{2,\varepsilon,t}:=\frac{\mathcal{T}_{\varepsilon}(\mu+tv_{t})-\mathcal{T}_{\varepsilon}(\mu)}{t}-\mathcal{T}^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(\mu)[v],\ \ E_{3,\varepsilon}:=\mathcal{T}^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(\mu)[v]-\mathcal{T}^{\prime}(\mu)[v].

For fixed
\varepsilon>0
,
E_{2,\varepsilon,t}\to 0
as
t\downarrow 0
.

On
\{\mu+tv_{t}\geq\varepsilon\}
,
g_{\varepsilon}=g
; on
\{\mu+tv_{t}<\varepsilon\}
and for
m\leq\varepsilon
,
a direct computation shows
|g(m)-g_{\varepsilon}(m)|\leq C\,\varepsilon^{\beta+1}
.
Hence

|E_{1,\varepsilon,t}|\ \leq\ \frac{C\,\varepsilon^{\beta+1}}{|t|}\,\varphi_{T}(\mu+t|v_{t}|<\varepsilon).

Using
\{\mu+t|v_{t}|<\varepsilon\}\subset\{\mu<2\varepsilon\}\cup\{|t|\,|v_{t}|>\varepsilon\}
and Chebyshev,

\varphi_{T}(\mu+t|v_{t}|<\varepsilon)\ \leq\ \varphi_{T}(\mu<2\varepsilon)+\frac{|t|^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\,\|v_{t}\|_{2}^{2}.

Therefore

|E_{1,\varepsilon,t}|\ \leq\ C\left\{\frac{\varepsilon^{\beta+1}}{|t|}\,e^{-C_{2}(\log(1/\varepsilon))^{2}}+\frac{|t|}{\varepsilon^{1-\beta}}\right\}+o(1)\qquad(t\downarrow 0).

Choose
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(t):=|t|^{k}
with any
k\in\big(0,\,\tfrac{1}{1-\beta}\big)
(possible since
\beta<0
).
Then
\frac{|t|}{\varepsilon^{1-\beta}}=|t|^{\,1-k(1-\beta)}\to 0
, and the term with
e^{-C_{2}(\log(1/\varepsilon))^{2}}
also vanishes.

Next,

|E_{3,\varepsilon}|=\left|\int\big((\mu\vee\varepsilon)^{\beta}-\mu^{\beta}\big)\,v\,\varphi_{T}\right|\ \leq\ \|v\|_{2}\,\Big\|\big((\mu\vee\varepsilon)^{\beta}-\mu^{\beta}\big)\mathbf{1}_{\{\mu<\varepsilon\}}\Big\|_{2}.

On
\{\mu<\varepsilon\}
we have
(\mu\vee\varepsilon)^{\beta}=\varepsilon^{\beta}\leq\mu^{\beta}
(since
\beta<0
), so

\Big\|\big((\mu\vee\varepsilon)^{\beta}-\mu^{\beta}\big)\mathbf{1}_{\{\mu<\varepsilon\}}\Big\|_{2}\ \leq\ \|\mu^{\beta}\,\mathbf{1}_{\{\mu<\varepsilon\}}\|_{2}=\left(\int_{\{\mu<\varepsilon\}}\mu(y)^{2\beta}\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy\right)^{\!1/2}.

Using
\mu(y)\geq c_{0}e^{-R\|y\|}
, we get
\mu(y)^{2\beta}\leq c_{0}^{2\beta}\,e^{\,|2\beta|R\|y\|}
.
Together with
\{\mu<\varepsilon\}\subset\{\|y\|>(1/R)\log(c_{0}/\varepsilon)\}
,

\int_{\{\mu<\varepsilon\}}\mu^{2\beta}\,\varphi_{T}\ \longrightarrow\ 0\qquad(\varepsilon\downarrow 0),

hence
E_{3,\varepsilon}\to 0
.

Therefore with all cases,

\frac{\mathcal{T}(\mu+tv_{t})-\mathcal{T}(\mu)}{t}\ \longrightarrow\ \int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g^{\prime}(\mu(y))\,v(y)\,\varphi_{T}(y)\,dy,

which proves Hadamard differentiability of
\mathcal{T}
at
\mu
with derivative
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}_{\mu}(v)
as claimed.
∎

Lemma 10
.

We define
\beta=\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}
, then the sub-Gaussian assumption to make all the lemmas in Section
D
all hold is there exists
\gamma_{0}>0
such that

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}\big[\exp(\gamma^{2}\|B\|_{2}^{2})\big]<\infty\quad\text{for every }\gamma<\gamma_{0}.

with

\frac{\gamma_{0}^{2}}{\left\|\sigma^{-1}\right\|_{F}^{2}}>T\max\Big\{\,4\beta^{2}-2\beta,\ \tfrac{2}{\beta-2},\ 16,\ 8\beta+8\,\Big\}.

Proof.

It suffices to reduce the maximum

\max\left\{2(2\beta^{2}-\beta),2s(2-1),2p^{2}-p,16,8(1+\beta)\right\}

with
s=\frac{1}{p-1}
and
p=\beta-1
, where
\beta=\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}
.

Rewrite each term in
\beta

2(2\beta^{2}-\beta)=4\beta^{2}-2\beta,\qquad 2s(2-1)=\frac{2}{\beta-2},\qquad 2p^{2}-p=2(\beta-1)^{2}-(\beta-1)=2\beta^{2}-5\beta+3,

\qquad 8(1+\beta)=8\beta+8.

Hence

M(\beta)=\max\Big\{\,4\beta^{2}-2\beta,\ \tfrac{2}{\beta-2},\ 2\beta^{2}-5\beta+3,\ 16,\ 8\beta+8\,\Big\}.

Note that

(4\beta^{2}-2\beta)-(2\beta^{2}-5\beta+3)=2\beta^{2}+3\beta-3.

The RHS is
\geq 0
for
\beta\geq\beta_{0}:=\frac{-3+\sqrt{33}}{4}\approx 0.686
. For
0<\beta\leq\beta_{0}
,

2\beta^{2}-5\beta+3\leq 3<16

(since
2\beta^{2}-5\beta+3
is strictly decreasing on
(0,\beta_{0}]
).
Therefore
2\beta^{2}-5\beta+3
never attains the maximum on
\beta>0
, and we may simplify to

M(\beta)=\max\Big\{\,4\beta^{2}-2\beta,\ \tfrac{2}{\beta-2},\ 16,\ 8\beta+8\,\Big\}.

∎

Finally, suppose
X_{n}
and
Y_{n}
are sequences of random variables such that

0\leq X_{n}\leq Y_{n}
on the events
E_{n}
which is true with probability 1 as
n\to\infty
. Hence, for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(X_{n}\geq\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(X_{n}\geq\varepsilon,E_{n}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(X_{n}\geq\varepsilon,E_{n}^{c}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{n}\geq\varepsilon,E_{n}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(E_{n}^{c}\right).

Hence,
Y_{n}=o_{p}(1)
implies
X_{n}=o_{p}(1)
and the same is for
O_{p}(1)
. If we let
X_{n}=n\,R_{n}(k^{*})
and
Y_{n}=\tau\ \frac{n\,F(0)^{2}}{g_{n}}\ +\ o_{p}(1)
, then for any
t\geq 0
,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(X_{n}>t\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{n}>t\right).

Hence, the proof of the asymptotic stochastic upper bound is completed.
