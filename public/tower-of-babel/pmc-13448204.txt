Verbal fluency and attention and processing speed associated with clinical recovery in patients with schizophrenia: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Ryo Asada, Kiyohiro Yasumatsu, Hitoshi Iida, Leo Gotoh, Hikaru Hori
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We investigated differences in cognitive and social functioning between patients with schizophrenia who met criteria for recovery or remission and healthy controls. This cross-sectional study included 55 patients with schizophrenia who met recovery or remission criteria and 20 healthy controls. Cognitive function was assessed using the Japanese version of the Brief Assessment of Cognition in Schizophrenia (BACS-J), and social function was assessed using the Japanese version of the Social Functioning Scale. Executive function scores did not differ significantly among the three groups; however, the other primary and composite scores of the BACS-J differed significantly among the three groups. In the post hoc analysis, verbal fluency (VF) and attention and processing speed (AP) were higher in the recovery group compared with the remission group (VF: p = 0.043; AP: p = 0.045). All domains of social functioning differed significantly among the three groups. In the post hoc analysis, significant differences between the recovery and remission groups were observed in interpersonal communication (p = 0.002), prosocial activity (p = 0.005), employment/occupation (p < 0.001), and total score (p = 0.005). These results indicate that VF and AP may be important cognitive domains related to clinical recovery in patients with schizophrenia. Furthermore, social functioning domains that require interaction with others may be more important for recovery than daily living skills.

Introduction

Approximately half of patients with schizophrenia achieve symptomatic remission after antipsychotic treatment during the acute phase (Lally et al., 2017). Clinical recovery is defined as a state that includes symptomatic remission and achievement of normative levels of functioning in psychosocial domains, including degree of independence, educational and vocational functioning, and peer relationships (Santesteban-Echarri et al., 2017). Although symptomatic remission has long been considered the primary treatment goal for schizophrenia, there is growing interest in achieving clinical recovery from the perspectives of patients and their families, clinicians, and researchers. However, the rate of clinical recovery was 13.5%, which is lower than the remission rate in patients with schizophrenia, and many patients continue to experience functional disability (Jaaskelainen et al., 2013). It was also been reported that patients with schizophrenia who had multiple psychotic episodes had lower rates of employment and clinical remission than those who had a first psychotic episode (Huxley et al., 2021). Thus, clinical recovery is a more difficult treatment goal for schizophrenia compared with achieving remission.

Cognitive impairment is one of the core features of schizophrenia. Previous studies reported that cognitive impairment in schizophrenia was more severe than in other psychotic disorders and bipolar disorders (Jonas et al., 2022; Li et al., 2020). Cognitive impairment is often recognized prior to the onset of illness (Jonas et al., 2022), and it has been shown to be associated with social functioning and specific cognitive domains, such as processing speed, attention, and working memory (Bowie et al., 2008). A meta-analysis showed that certain cognitive domains in treatment-responsive schizophrenia were better preserved than in treatment-resistant schizophrenia (Millgate et al., 2022). Thus, differences in treatment response among patients with schizophrenia may be influenced by cognitive function.

There is a substantial gap in functional outcomes between clinical recovery and remission in patients with schizophrenia, leading to increased interest in the differences between recovery and remission in this population. Cognitive function is considered to be a recovery-related to factor, and changes in cognition were shown to influence changes in functioning (Winter et al., 2025). In addition, specific cognitive domains were shown to be predictors of good functional outcomes in patients with schizophrenia (Ventura et al., 2011) and previous studies demonstrated the importance of cognitive function in achieving recovery and remission (Dubreucq et al., 2022; Faber et al., 2011; Flores et al., 2025). Another study reported that several cognitive domains in the recovered schizophrenia group were higher than those in the non-recovered group but remained lower than those of healthy controls (Kopelowicz et al., 2005). Therefore, functional outcomes, including cognitive function, in patients with schizophrenia who achieve recovery may not reach the same level as those of healthy individuals. We previously reported that cognitive function in patients with schizophrenia in symptomatic remission was impaired across multiple cognitive domains compared with healthy controls (Asada et al., 2024). However, differences in cognitive function between recovery and symptomatic remission in patients with schizophrenia have not been clearly established.

We hypothesized that there would be differences in the profile of cognitive function among patients with schizophrenia in states of recovery and remission and healthy controls, and that specific cognitive domains might be associated with social functioning. Therefore, the primary aim of the present study was to compare cognitive function among patients with schizophrenia in recovery or remission and healthy controls. The secondary aim of the present study was to evaluate the association between cognitive function and social functioning in patients with schizophrenia in states of recovery and remission.

Methods

Participants

This cross-sectional study recruited patients with schizophrenia and healthy controls from five psychiatric institutions in Japan (Fukuoka University Hospital, Aburayama Hospital, Rainbow and Sea Hospital, Kawano Clinic, and UNB Sumiyoshi Jinja Mae Clinic) between 2021 and 2025. A total of 55 patients with schizophrenia and 20 healthy controls were enrolled in this study. The inclusion criteria for patients with schizophrenia were as follows: 1) a diagnosis of schizophrenia based on the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) criteria (American Psychiatric Association, 2013) made by a psychiatrist; 2) age 20–65 years; 3) no change in antipsychotic dosage for at least the past 3 months; 4) no use of anticholinergic medications; and 5) provision of written informed consent. The exclusion criteria for patients with schizophrenia were as follows: 1) serious physical illness, organic brain disease, or a history of head trauma with loss of consciousness for 10 min or longer; and 2) a diagnosis of substance-related or addictive disorder based on DSM-5 criteria (American Psychiatric Association, 2013) made by a psychiatrist. Healthy controls were screened using the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM Disorders, non-patient edition (First, 2002). The inclusion criteria for healthy controls were as follows: 1) age 20–65 years; 2) attending school or working for at least half a day; 3) maintaining interaction with friends at least once per week; 4) living independently without supervision; and 5) provision of written informed consent. The exclusion criteria were as follows: 1) a personal or family history of psychiatric illness; 2) serious physical illness, organic brain disease, or a history of head trauma with loss of consciousness for 10 min or longer; and 3) a history of alcohol or drug abuse.

Demographic data were collected through interviews with participants and from medical records.

Definition of recovery and remission

Recovery was defined according to the established criteria proposed by Liberman et al. (2002). Recovery was required to be sustained for at least the past 2 years and to encompass four domains as follows: 1) symptoms: scores related to positive and negative symptoms (grandiosity, suspiciousness, unusual thought content, hallucinations, conceptual disorganization, bizarre behavior, self-neglect, blunted affect, and emotional withdrawal) were 4 (moderate) or lower on the Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale (BPRS) (Overall and Gorham, 1988); 2) vocational functioning: successful paid employment, academic study, or age- and culturally appropriate volunteer activities for more than half of each week; 3) independent living: management of daily living tasks, such as financial management, meal preparation, laundry, personal grooming, and schedule organization, without supervision; and 4) peer relationships: social interaction (e.g., meetings, meals, phone calls, or recreational activities) with friends other than family members.

Symptomatic remission was evaluated according to the criteria proposed by the Remission in Schizophrenia Working Group (Andreasen et al., 2005): a score of 3 or lower on the BPRS for items associated with positive symptoms (grandiosity, suspiciousness, hallucinatory behavior, and unusual thought content), disorganization (conceptual disorganization and mannerisms and posturing), and negative symptoms (blunted affect). Patients with schizophrenia who simultaneously met the criteria for both recovery and remission were classified as the recovery group. Because this was a cross-sectional study, the examiner determined from medical records whether these required conditions had been met during the specified period.

Evaluation of cognitive function

Cognitive function was assessed using the Japanese version of the Brief Assessment of Cognition in Schizophrenia (BACS-J) (Kaneda et al., 2007). The BACS-J consists of six cognitive domains: list learning (verbal learning), digit sequencing (working memory), token motor task (motor function), verbal fluency (VF), symbol coding (attention and processing speed (AP)), and the Tower of London test (executive function).

The list learning task involved orally presenting participants with a list of 15 words and asking them to recall as many words as possible. This test was repeated 5 times without changing words. In the digit sequencing task, participants were presented with the cluster and asked to sort the numbers from lowest to highest values. In the token motor test, participants were provided with 100 plastic tokens placed on the table and were required to insert two tokens at the same time into a box as many times as possible with 60 s. The verbal fluency test was composed two components. In the first, participants were asked to state as many words as possible belonging to a designated semantic category (animals) within 60 s. In the second, participants were asked to state as many words as possible beginning with a specified letter within 60 s, this test repeated one time after changing letter. In the symbol coding task, participants were required to write the number matching to the symbol from 9 different number and symbol pairs within 90 s. In the Tower of London test, participants were showed two different illustrations at the same time, which showed three balls of different colors resting on 3 bars. The participants were asked to state the minimum number of moves needed to transform one arrangement into the other within 20 s. 20 tests were conducted. If participants answered all tests correctly, they were given additional more two tests.

The BACS-J assessment requires approximately 30 min to complete. The primary outcome measures for each BACS-J subtest were standardized into z-scores. The mean score of the healthy controls was set to zero, and the standard deviation was set to one. The healthy control dataset used to calculate the z-scores was obtained from a previous study (Kaneda et al., 2007). A composite score was calculated as the mean of the z-scores of the six primary outcomes. To control for age-related effects, age-matched controls were used to calculate the BACS-J z-scores for individuals with schizophrenia in the previous study. Higher scores indicated better cognitive function.

Evaluation of social function

Social function was evaluated using the Japanese version of the Social Functioning Scale (SFS-J) (Nemoto et al., 2008). This scale has been widely used to measure social functioning in patients with schizophrenia over the past 3 months and consists of seven subscales (Ochoa et al., 2015; Bellido-Zanin et al., 2015): (1) withdrawal (time spent alone, conversational inhibition, and social avoidance), with a subscore ranging from 0 to 15; (2) interpersonal communication (number of friendships, presence of a romantic relationship, and quality of communication), with a subscore ranging from 0 to 12; (3) independence–performance (actual performance of daily living skills), with a subscore ranging from 0 to 39; (4) independence–competence (capacity to carry out tasks required for independent living), with a subscore ranging from 0 to 39; (5) recreation (participation in common hobbies, interests, or pastimes), with a subscore ranging from 0 to 65; (6) prosocial activity (engagement in typical social activities such as sports, attending parties, and trips), with a subscore ranging from 0 to 66; and (7) employment/occupation (involvement in productive work or a structured daily activity program), with a subscore ranging from 0 to 10. The total SFS-J score ranges from 0 to 226, with higher scores indicating better social functioning.

Psychiatric symptoms and depressive symptoms

Symptoms in patients with schizophrenia were assessed using the BPRS (Overall and Gorham, 1988) and the Calgary Depression Scale for Schizophrenia (CDSS) (Addington et al., 1993). The BPRS consists of 18 items, each rated on a 7-point scale ranging from 1 (not present) to 7 (extremely severe). Symptom evaluation was based on clinical observation of symptoms (tension, emotional withdrawal, mannerisms and posturing, motor retardation, and uncooperativeness) and participants' reports of symptoms (conceptual disorganization, unusual thought content, anxiety, guilt, grandiosity, depressive mood, hostility, somatic concern, hallucinatory behavior, suspiciousness, and blunted affect). The total BPRS score ranges from 18 to 126, with higher scores indicating greater severity of psychiatric symptoms.

The CDSS is a tool used to assess depressive symptoms independently of negative symptoms in patients with schizophrenia and consists of nine items: depression, hopelessness, self-deprecation, guilty ideas of reference, pathological guilt, morning depression, early awakening, suicide, and observed depressive symptoms. Each item is rated on a 4-point scale ranging from 0 (absent) to 3 (severe). The total CDSS score ranges from 0 to 27, with higher scores indicating more severe depressive symptoms.

General function

The Global Assessment of Functioning (GAF) scale is used to evaluate both symptom severity and functional impairment in mental illness (Startup et al., 2002). The GAF score ranges from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better functioning and milder symptoms.

Premorbid intelligence quotient

Premorbid IQ was estimated using the Japanese Adult Reading Test (JART) (Matsuoka et al., 2006), which is the Japanese version of the National Adult Reading Test. The JART is widely used to estimate premorbid IQ by asking participants to read aloud 50 irregularly pronounced Kanji (Japanese characters) words. Higher scores on the JART indicate higher premorbid IQ.

Ethics

All participants, including patients with schizophrenia and healthy controls, provided written informed consent prior to enrollment in this study. This study was approved by the Fukuoka University Ethics Review Board (approval number: U21-11-018) and was conducted in accordance with the latest version of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Statistical analysis

The Statistical Package for the Social Sciences, version 27.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA), was used for data analysis. Categorical variables, including sex, handedness, smoking status, and marital status, were analyzed using the chi-square test. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Age, body mass index (BMI), and the JART score were normally distributed and were analyzed using analysis of variance. The BACS composite and primary scores, SFS scores, number of admissions, duration of illness, age at onset, years of education, and employment status were not normally distributed and were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test or the Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. Spearman's rank correlation coefficient was used to examine the relationships between the BACS composite or primary scores and SFS scores. A p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Demographic data

Table 1 shows the clinical characteristics of the three groups. There were no significant differences in BMI, duration of employment, duration of illness, or CDSS scores among the groups. Discussion

The present study showed that patients with schizophrenia who achieved recovery performed better on tests of VF and AP than patients with schizophrenia in a state of remission. In addition, compared with patients with schizophrenia in the recovery group, healthy controls demonstrated higher performance in verbal learning, working memory, motor function, and AP.

VF and AP were higher in patients with schizophrenia in recovery than in those with symptomatic remission. To the best of our knowledge, no previous studies have directly compared cognitive function between recovery and remission in patients with schizophrenia. A previous study comparing recovered and non-recovered patients with schizophrenia reported that verbal learning, executive function, VF, and verbal working memory were significantly higher in the recovery group (Kopelowicz et al., 2005), and these findings are partially consistent with the present results. Compared with that study, the present study may have reduced heterogeneity by including only participants with schizophrenia. Furthermore, the present study minimized the influence of pharmacotherapy by excluding patients who were taking anticholinergic medications and by including only those whose antipsychotic dosage had not changed for at least 3 months. The VF test requires not only word productivity but also cognitive strategies, such as switching (shifting from one category to another) and clustering (producing words within semantic categories). A recent study reported that the clustering component score was associated with social functioning in patients with schizophrenia (Wada et al., 2025). Although switching and clustering scores were not measured in the present study, the observed difference in VF scores between the recovery and remission groups may be related to differences in cognitive strategy.

In the comparison of social functioning between the recovery and remission groups, the present study demonstrated that scores for interpersonal communication, prosocial activity, and vocational function were significantly higher in the recovered schizophrenia group than in the remission group. To the best of our knowledge, no previous study has evaluated detailed aspects of social functioning between recovery and remission in patients with schizophrenia. The differences in criteria between recovery and remission in this study involved vocational function, independent living, and maintenance of peer relationships. The results of the present study suggest that improvement in vocational function and social interaction skills may be more important than independent living skills in distinguishing these two states. Regarding the correlations between social functioning and cognitive function, previous studies have shown that interpersonal communication is significantly associated with VF (Shimada et al., 2022; Iwata et al., 2017). From the perspective of social functioning, language-related cognitive skills, such as VF, may be important for achieving recovery. On the other hand, negative correlations between motor function and withdrawal were observed in both the recovery and healthy control groups. Most participants in the recovery and healthy control groups were independent and did not require support from others. Therefore, they may have had fewer opportunities for daily communication with others, including family members, than participants in the remission group. We consider that this difference may be related to the paradoxical findings. In addition, the duration of illness in recovery group was longer and a greater number of admissions than remission group, instead of higher score of GAF in recovery group than remission. Regarding this seemingly contradictory result, we reasoned that a longer duration of illness indicated a longer period of treatment, potentially leading to a higher number of hospital admissions, and that the high level of functioning was achieved as a result of long-term treatment or support.

First, the present study is novel in that it focuses on differences in cognitive domains between patients with recovered and remitted schizophrenia. We suggest that VF and AP may be important cognitive functions for achieving recovery in patients with schizophrenia. In longitudinal studies, VF scores did not show significant changes from baseline to 1- or 2-year follow-up (Luperdi et al., 2024; Grimes et al., 2021). Therefore, improving VF skills may require a long period, potentially several years. Regarding AP, some studies have reported improvements in these scores after switching from antipsychotic polypharmacy to monotherapy or after administration of cognitive remediation (Hori et al., 2013; Grynszpan et al., 2011). Thus, AP may represent cognitive domains that can be improved through pharmacotherapy or psychotherapy within a relatively short period. In addition, some cognitive domains remained significantly lower in the schizophrenia recovery group than in healthy controls. These findings suggest that certain cognitive domains are particularly important for achieving recovery in schizophrenia, whereas others may be less critical. Promoting recovery in schizophrenia may require the development of interventions targeting specific cognitive domains, such as VF and AP. Second, the present study showed that scores for several cognitive domains were significantly lower in the recovery group than in healthy controls. These results indicate that even among patients who have achieved recovery, some cognitive domains do not reach the same levels as those of healthy individuals. Improving these cognitive functions may represent an important therapeutic target for further enhancement of recovery in patients with schizophrenia.

In the present study, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, because of the cross-sectional design, it was not possible to determine causal relationships between recovery and cognitive or social functioning. Second, the relatively small sample size may limit the generalizability of the findings, and the findings should be considered preliminary; therefore, future studies with larger cohorts are needed to confirm these results. Third, social cognition was not assessed. Social cognition is impaired in schizophrenia (Green et al., 2015), and a meta-analysis reported that social cognition mediates the relationship between neurocognition and functional outcomes (Halverson et al., 2019). Future studies should evaluate differences in social cognition between remitted and recovered patients with schizophrenia. Fourth, we used healthy controls as subjects to exclude to influence of potential psychiatric disorder for evaluating cognitive function. However, this approach may maximalize the difference comparison of schizophrenia subject and healthy controls, more so than the subjects consist of community controls.

In conclusion, the present study demonstrated cognitive and social differences among recovered and remitted patients with schizophrenia and healthy controls. VF was significantly correlated with social functioning only in the recovered schizophrenia group. Longitudinal assessments of cognitive function and intervention studies targeting specific cognitive domains are warranted.
