Complex symmetric, self-dual, and Ginibre random matrices: Analytical results for three classes of bulk and edge statistics
Gernot Akemann, Noah Aygün, Mario Kieburg, Patricia Päßler

Abstract
Recently, a conjecture about the local bulk statistics of complex eigenvalues has been made based on numerics. It claims that there are only three universality classes, which have all been observed in open chaotic quantum systems. Motivated by these new insights, we compute and compare the expectation values of k k pairs of complex conjugate characteristic polynomials in three ensembles of Gaussian non-Hermitian random matrices representative for the three classes: the well-known complex Ginibre ensemble, complex symmetric and complex self-dual matrices. In the Cartan classification scheme of non-Hermitian random matrices they are labelled as class A, AI † and AII † , respectively. Using the technique of Grassmann variables, we derive explicit expressions for a single pair of expected characteristic polynomials for finite as well as infinite matrix dimension. For the latter we consider the global limit as well as zoom into the edge and the bulk of the spectrum, providing new analytical results for classes AI † and AII † . For general k k , we derive the effective Lagrangians corresponding to the non-linear σ \sigma -models in the respective physical systems. Interestingly, they agree for all three ensembles, while the corresponding Goldstone manifolds, over which one has to perform the remaining integrations, are different and equal the three classical compact groups in the bulk. In particular, our analytical results show that these three ensembles have indeed different local bulk and edge spectral statistics, corroborating the conjecture further.

Introduction

It is one of the goals of statistical mechanics to derive simple, analytical models that describe universal aspects of physical systems in an approximative way. One class of such models is given by ensembles of random matrices. Their application in physics is two-fold. Firstly, they may model physical operators such as the Hamilton and the Dirac operator. Secondly, they typically belong to the wider class of static Coulomb gases with logarithmic interaction. From this point of view and at specific values of the inverse temperature
\beta

2.1
Definition of the ensembles of random matrices of class A, AI
†
and AII
†

To get an idea of our techniques and results as well as have very explicitly formulas, we consider the expectation value of a product of two characteristic polynomials. One characteristic polynomial depends on the non-Hermitian random matrix
J
and the other one on its complex conjugate
J^{*}
, i.e., the main object will be

D_{N}(z,w^{*}):=\langle\det[z\,\mathbf{1}_{d_{N}}-J]\det[w^{*}\,\mathbf{1}_{d_{N}}-J^{*}]\rangle,

(2.1)

with
z,w\in\mathbb{C}
fixed,
\mathbf{1}_{d_{N}}
is the identity matrix of matrix dimension
d_{N}
, and
J
is drawn from a Gaussian probability density

P(J)=\frac{1}{Z_{N}}\exp[-{\rm Tr}\,(JJ^{\dagger})],

(2.2)

where the partition function
Z_{N}
(normalisation constant) is specified below. The latter certainly strongly depends on from which non-Hermitian matrix space
J
is taken. The average over an observable
\mathcal{O}(J)
is denoted for all ensembles by

\langle\mathcal{O}(J)\rangle:=\frac{1}{Z_{N}}\int[dJ]\,\mathcal{O}(J)\exp[-{\rm Tr}\,(JJ^{\dagger})],

(2.3)

where
[dJ]
denotes the flat Lebesgue measure
over all independent complex matrix elements. We underline that the measure
[dJ]
looks different for the three ensembles when writing it out explicitly, as the possible symmetries relate the matrix entries. This leads to
N^{2}
complex independent elements for class
A
,
N(N+1)/2
for class AI
†
and
2N^{2}-N
for class AII
†
. The measure of a complex number
z=x+iy
is chosen as
d^{2}z=dxdy
. The Hermitian conjugate of
J
is denoted by
J^{\dagger}
.

The first and simplest ensemble, we study, has been introduced by Ginibre
[
1
]
. It is given by
N\times N
random matrices with independent identically normal distributed complex matrix elements,
J_{ij}\in\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(0,1)
for
i,j=1,\ldots,N
. We adapt the notation of
[
24
,
29
,
2
]
and label it with the Cartan class notation A. Its partition function reads

Z_{N}^{\rm A}:=\int[dJ]\exp[-{\rm Tr}\,(JJ^{\dagger})]=\prod_{i,j=1}^{N}\int d^{2}J_{ij}\exp[-|J_{ij}|^{2}]=\pi^{N^{2}}.

(2.4)

The second ensemble we consider consists of complex symmetric
N\times N
matrices
J=J^{T}
. As in the complex Ginibre ensemble, the requirement of invariance under the group action
J\to UJU^{T}
for any unitary matrix
U\in{\rm U}(N)
fixes the weight up to a scaling, when assuming that all independent matrix elements are Gaussian. This however also implies that the diagonal and off-diagonal matrix elements have a different variance. We label this ensemble with AI
†
, see
[
24
,
29
,
2
]
, and its partition function is

Z_{N}^{\rm AI^{\dagger}}:=\!\int[dJ]\exp[-{\rm Tr}\,(JJ^{\dagger})]=\prod_{l=1}^{N}\int d^{2}J_{ll}\exp[-|J_{ll}|^{2}]\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\int d^{2}J_{ij}\exp[-2|J_{ij}|^{2}]=\pi^{N}\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right)^{\frac{N(N-1)}{2}}\!\!\!\!.

(2.5)

The third ensemble we consider is that of a complex self-dual
2N\times 2N
random matrix
J
. In a
2N
-dimensional complex representation it consists of 3 complex non-Hermitian matrices
A,B,C
of size
N\times N
, where the latter two are anti-symmetric,
B^{T}=-B
,
C^{T}=-C
:

J=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}A&B\\
C&A^{T}\\
\end{array}\right),\quad\mbox{with}\ J^{\dagger}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}A^{\dagger}&-C^{*}\\
-B^{*}&A^{*}\\
\end{array}\right).

(2.6)

Choosing the following embedding of the second Pauli matrix

\Sigma_{y}:=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&-i\mathbf{1}_{N}\\
i\mathbf{1}_{N}&0\\
\end{array}\right),\quad\Sigma_{y}^{-1}=\Sigma_{y}\ ,

(2.7)

we have the following symmetry on
J

\Sigma_{y}J^{T}\Sigma_{y}=J.

(2.8)

This symmetry is called self-duality, and we follow
[
24
,
29
,
2
]
by labelling the ensemble with AII
†
. The corresponding partition function, thus, reads

\displaystyle Z_{N}^{\rm AII^{\dagger}}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\int[dJ]\exp[-{\rm Tr}\,(JJ^{\dagger})]

(2.9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{i,j=1}^{N}\int d^{2}A_{ij}\exp[-2|A_{ij}|^{2}]\prod_{1\leq n<l\leq N}\int d^{2}B_{nl}d^{2}C_{nl}\exp[-2|B_{nl}|^{2}-2|C_{nl}|^{2}]=\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right)^{2N^{2}-N}

It is not difficult to see from the definition of the expectation value (
2.3
), that for
zw^{*}\to\infty
the right hand side of
(

) asymptotically becomes
\sim(zw^{*})^{N}
for the Ginibre and AI

) while it will be
\sim(zw^{*})^{2N}
for the AII
†
ensemble because the matrices have dimension

.

In order to take the large-
N
limit later, we will adjust the normalisation, where we divide (

) by its value at vanishing arguments,

D_{N}(0,0)=\langle\det[J]\det[J^{*}]\rangle.

(2.10)

It is some natural choice of a reference point, despite that it will not cancel all

2.2
Grassmann variables and special functions

Below we will employ anticommuting complex Grassmann variables
\xi,\psi
, i.e.,
\{\xi,\psi\}=\xi\psi+\psi\xi=0
, in order to write determinants as integrals.
We follow the conventions of Haake
[
60
]
, and we refer to
[
40
]
for an introduction into superalgebra and superanalysis.
Complex conjugation is chosen to satisfy the following relations

(\xi^{*})^{*}=-\xi
for single and
(\xi\psi)^{*}=\xi^{*}\psi^{*}
for a product of Grassmann variables. Complex conjugated variables anticommute equally,
\{\xi,\xi^{*}\}=\{\xi,^{*}\psi\}=0
. The integration convention of each Grassmann variable is chosen to be

\int d\xi=0\ ,\quad\int d\xi\ \xi=1.

(2.11)

For a complex Grassmann vector of size
N
,
\xi^{T}=(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{N})
we, then, obtain for the determinant of an arbitrary
N\times N
matrix
C

\det[C]=\int[d\xi]\exp[\xi^{\dagger}C\xi].

(2.12)

The integration measure is defined as
[d\xi]=\prod_{i=1}^{N}d\xi_{i}d\xi_{i}^{*}
.
A second identity is needed for the Pfaffian determinant of an arbitrary even dimensional antisymmetric matrix
D
of size
2N\times 2N
,

\Pf[D]=(-1)^{N(N+1)/2}\int[d\xi]\exp\left[\frac{1}{2}(\xi^{T},\xi^{*\,T})D\left(\!\begin{array}[]{c}\xi\\
\xi^{*}\\
\end{array}\!\right)\right].

(2.13)

Finally, we would like to introduce some special functions that we will encounter repeatedly.
It is well-known
[
1
]
that the truncated exponential

E_{N}(x):=\sum_{j=0}^{N}\frac{x^{j}}{j!}=e^{x}\frac{\Gamma(N+1,x)}{\Gamma(N+1)}=e^{x}Q(N+1,x)\ ,

(2.14)

plays an important role in the complex Ginibre ensemble (class A). It actually has its appearance for the other two ensembles studied by us.
The truncated exponential can be rewritten in terms of the upper incomplete Gamma-function
[
61
, 8.4.10]

\Gamma(a,x):=\int_{x}^{\infty}dtt^{a-1}e^{-t}\ ,

(2.15)

as well as its normalised version

Q(a,x):=\frac{\Gamma(a,x)}{\Gamma(a)}\ .

(2.16)

At the edge of the complex Ginibre ensemble,
one encounters the complementary error function

\erfc(x)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{x}^{\infty}dte^{-t^{2}},

(2.17)

whose relation to the error function
\erf
is

\erfc(y)=1-\erf(y)=1+\erf(-y)=2-\erfc(-y),

(2.18)

and to the imaginary error function

\erfi(z)=-i\erf(iz)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{z}ds\,e^{s^{2}}.

(2.19)

Those functions can be also found in the case of complex symmetric matrices (AI
†
) while for the case of complex self-dual matrices (AII
†
) we
encounter Owen’s
T
-function
[
62
]
defined as follows,

T(x,a):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{a}dt\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2}x^{2}(1+t^{2})}}{1+t^{2}},\quad x,a\in\mathbb{R}.

(2.20)

We actually need the function analytically continued to the imaginary axis in its second argument,

T\left(x,ib\right)=\frac{i}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{b}du\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2}x^{2}(1-u^{2})}}{1-u^{2}},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}\quad{\rm and}\quad b\in(-1,1).

(2.21)

When going beyond the specified integral one needs to be aware of the emerging branch cuts on
b\in\mathbb{R}\setminus(-1,1)
. We will not run into this problem, though, as we avoid those cuts.

We will mention some properties of all these special functions where necessary in the computations.

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dr\int_{0}^{\infty}ds\exp\left[-r-s\right]\left(4x^{2}+4x(r+s)+(r-s)^{2}\right)^{N},

(2.96)

after changing to polar coordinates for
q
and
p
.

It is useful to introduce so-called light cone variables at this stage, the sum and difference of the integration variables,

\displaystyle v:=r+s,\quad u:=r-s,\quad\mbox{with}\ v\geq 0,\ u\in[-v,v].

(2.97)

The Jacobian is
dr\,ds=\frac{1}{2}dv\,du
so that we get, due to Fubini’s theorem,

\displaystyle f_{N}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}dv\ e^{-v}\int_{-v}^{v}du\left(4x^{2}+4xv+u^{2}\right)^{N}.

(2.98)

We note in passing that this integral satisfies
an inhomogenous first order differential equation as is explained in Appendix
A
.

The integral can be further simplified as follows. Using the evenness of the integrand with respect to variable
u\to-u
,

\displaystyle f_{N}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dv\int_{0}^{v}du\ e^{-v}\left(4x^{2}+4xv+u^{2}\right)^{N}

(2.99)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}du\int_{u}^{\infty}dv\ e^{-v}\left(4x^{2}+4xv+u^{2}\right)^{N}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}du\int_{0}^{\infty}dv\ e^{-v-u}\left(4xv+(2x+u)^{2}\right)^{N}.

In the second equality we reordered the integrals and in the third one we shifted
v\to v^{\prime}=v-u
.

This expression (
2.99
) leads to a simple summation formula at finite-
N
, as well as to the large-
N
asymptotic limits performed in the next section. From a simple expansion of the binomial we obtain

\displaystyle f_{N}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}du\int_{0}^{\infty}dv\ e^{-v-u}\sum_{j=0}^{N}\frac{N!}{j!(N-j)!}(4xv)^{N-j}(2x+u)^{2j}

(2.100)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{2x}^{\infty}du^{\prime}\ e^{-u^{\prime}+2x}\sum_{j=0}^{N}\frac{N!}{j!}(4x)^{N-j}(u^{\prime})^{2j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{2x}\sum_{j=0}^{N}\frac{N!}{j!}(4x)^{N-j}\,\Gamma(2j+1,2x),

after shifting again
u^{\prime}=2x+u
and using the definition of the incomplete Gamma-function (
2.15
).

At
x=zw^{*}=0
we only have a contribution from
j=N
, and hence with
\Gamma(2j+1,0)=(2j)!
we obtain

f_{N}(0)=(2N)!

(2.101)

and, thus, together with (
2.95
)

D_{N}^{\rm AII^{\dagger}}(0,0)=\frac{(2N)!}{4^{N}}.

(2.102)

Inserting this into the definition on the left hand side of (
2.24
) together with (
2.16
) and (
2.14
), this proves both right hand sides of (
2.24
).

Asymptotic global and local large-

3.1
Scaling with
N
and global limit for a single pair

In this subsection, we start exploring the possible large-
N
limits.
First, we present plots for the rescaled characteristic polynomials at different values of
N
over the entire range of the spectrum, helping us in identifying different scaling regimes. In particular, we present the global limit for all three classes, before zooming into the local edge and bulk regime in the subsequent subsections. To deal with those in a unifying way, it is useful to introduce the dimension

d_{N}=N\quad{\rm for}\quad{\rm A}\ {\rm and}\ {\rm AI}^{\dagger}\qquad{\rm and}\qquad d_{N}=2N\quad{\rm for}\quad{\rm AII}^{\dagger}\ ,

(3.1)

and the scaling

s=1\quad{\rm for}\quad{\rm A}\qquad{\rm and}\qquad s=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\quad{\rm for}\quad{\rm AI}^{\dagger}\ {\rm and}\ {\rm AII}^{\dagger}.

(3.2)

The scaling

properly normalises the limiting support of the expectation value (
2.1
) as well as the eigenvalues to the unit disc for all three ensembles. The latter we have checked numerically which also implies that the local mean level spacing of the eigenvalues is normalised to the same distance so that the local statistics become comparable.

Our results for the global limit can be summarised as follows.

3.1
Global limit for a pair of characteristic polynomials.

Let
r\geq 0
. For the rescaled characteristic polynomials

F_{N}(r):=\left.\exp[-d_{N}zw^{*}]\frac{D_{N}\left(\sqrt{d_{N}}sz,\sqrt{d_{N}}sw^{*}\right)}{D_{N}(0,0)}\right|_{zw^{*}=r^{2}},

(3.3)

cf., Proposition
2.1
, we obtain the following global limits

\displaystyle D_{\rm global}^{\rm A}(r)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}F_{N}^{\rm A}(r)\ \ \ =\Theta(1-r)\ ,

\displaystyle D_{\rm global}^{\rm AI^{\dagger}}(r)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}F_{N}^{\rm AI^{\dagger}}(r)\ =(1-r^{2})\Theta(1-r)\ ,

\displaystyle D_{\rm global}^{\rm AII^{\dagger}}(r)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}F_{N}^{\rm AII^{\dagger}}(r)=\frac{1}{1-r^{2}}\Theta(1-r)\ ,

(3.4)

where
\Theta(1-r)
is the Heaviside step-function restricting the support to the interval
[0,1]
.

Class A:
Anew, we begin with the derivation for the complex Ginibre ensemble, where it is well known that it converges to the circular law, since
F_{N}^{\rm A}(r)
is up to a normalisation factor the global spectral density of the eigenvalues in the complex plane when

[

The exponential factor in (
2.22

The global limit of this function
is obtained from the convergence of the normalised lower incomplete Gamma-function

D_{\rm global}^{\rm A}(r)=\Theta(1-r).

(3.6)

The corrections are exponentially suppressed except when

[

, 8.11.12]
.
Later, in the local edge scaling limit the step of the function

at

Class AII

The case of the complex self-dual ensemble is more involved.
We start like before with properly scaling the spectral variables

and then removing the exponential prefactor in (

\displaystyle F_{N}^{\rm AII^{\dagger}}(r)=\sum_{j=0}^{N}\frac{N!(2N-2j)!}{(2N)!(N-j)!}(4Nr^{2})^{j}Q(2N-2j+1,2Nr^{2}).

We have renamed the summation index

. One can even relax the fixed condition of

Summarising, we obtain a geometric series

\displaystyle D_{\rm global}^{\rm AII^{\dagger}}(r)

\displaystyle\approx

[

Furthermore, perturbation theoretic methods

We conclude that although we have to emphasise that the expectation value of two characteristic polynomials does

3.2
Complex Ginibre ensemble: Edge and bulk scaling of the density revisited

In this subsection, we briefly recall the edge and bulk scaling limit of the complex Ginibre ensemble, which is a determinantal point process with kernel
K_{N}(z,w^{*})
. We choose the convention that
K_{N}(z,w^{*})
denotes the polynomial part of the kernel, without the corresponding weight functions, which is also know as pre-kernel. In this form it equals the normalised expectation value of two conjugate characteristic polynomials.

The following relation is known between this kernel at finite-
N
and the characteristic polynomials, e.g., see
[
1
,
46
]
,

K_{N}(z,w)=\frac{D_{N}^{\rm A}(z,w^{*})}{D_{N}^{\rm A}(0,0)}=E_{N}(zw^{*})=e^{zw^{*}}Q(N+1,zw^{*}).

(3.12)

The eigenvalue density is given by the kernel at equal arguments, times the weight function of the Ginibre ensemble,
w(z)=\exp[-|z|^{2}]
,

R_{1,N}(z)=e^{-|z|^{2}}K_{N}(z,z^{*})=Q(N+1,|z|^{2}).

(3.13)

We would like to point out that this density is normalised to
N
.

In the global large-
N
limit it converges to the circular law on a disc of radius
\sqrt{N}
in this normalisation,

R_{1,N}(z)\sim\Theta(\sqrt{N}-|z|).

(3.14)

A further rescaling
z\to\sqrt{N}z
maps the support to the unit disc.

The local bulk scaling limit is defined as follows. We pick a point
\sqrt{N}z_{0}
in the bulk of the spectrum, that is with
|z_{0}|<1
. The vicinity of this bulk point is then parametrised as follows

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{N}z_{0}+\chi,\quad w^{*}=\sqrt{N}z_{0}^{*}+\eta^{*},

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle zw^{*}=N|z_{0}|^{2}+\sqrt{2N}\,y+\chi\eta^{*},\quad y:=(\chi z_{0}^{*}+\eta^{*}z_{0})/\sqrt{2}\ .

(3.15)

The limiting kernel in the bulk is, then, obtained by replacing the truncated sum
E_{N}(zw^{*})
in (
3.12
) by an exponential, leading to

\displaystyle K_{\rm bulk}^{\rm A}(\chi,\eta^{*})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}K_{N}(\sqrt{N}z_{0}+\chi,\sqrt{N}z_{0}^{*}+\eta^{*})\Big|_{|z_{0}|<1}\sim\exp\left[N|z_{0}|^{2}+\sqrt{2N}\,y+\chi\eta^{*}\right].\qquad

(3.16)

Strictly speaking, the pre-kernel does not have a limit.
Only after multiplying with the weight function,
e^{-\frac{1}{2}(|z|^{2}+|w|^{2})}K_{N}(z,w^{*})
and after cancelling cocycles that drop out in the determinant of the
k
-point correlation function, it does lead to the limiting Ginibre kernel.
This is why below we will remove the exponential factor in (
3.12
) when taking limits. Nevertheless, the bulk asymptotic (
3.16
) and edge asymptotic below will be very useful when comparing results for averages over more than one pair of characteristic polynomials, cf., Appendix
B
.

Turning to the local edge scaling limit, we will choose the same rescaling as in (
3.15
), but require now that the point we zoom in is located on the unit circle, that is
|z_{0}|=1
.
For the edge scaling we rewrite the truncated exponential function in the following way,

E_{N}(x)=e^{x}Q(N+1,x)=e^{x}(1-P(N+1,x)),

(3.17)

where

P(N+1,x)=\frac{\gamma(N+1,x)}{\Gamma(N+1)}

(3.18)

is the regularised lower incomplete Gamma-function

\gamma(a,x)=\Gamma(a)-\Gamma(a,x)
. It enjoys the following asymptotic
[
61
, 8.11.10]
for large-
N
, with the edge rescaling (
3.15
) at
|z_{0}|=1
,

P(N+1,x)=\frac{1}{2}\erfc(-y)+\mathcal{O}(N^{-\frac{1}{2}}),

(3.19)

with the complementary error function (
2.17
). Once, we employ the identity (
2.18
), we find

Q(N+1,x)=\frac{1}{2}\erfc(y)+\mathcal{O}(N^{-\frac{1}{2}}),

(3.20)

and hence the edge scaling asymptotic becomes

\displaystyle K_{\rm edge}^{\rm A}(\chi,\eta^{*})=\left.\lim_{N\to\infty}K_{N}(\sqrt{N}z_{0}+\chi,\sqrt{N}z_{0}^{*}+\eta^{*})\right|_{|z_{0}|=1}\sim\frac{1}{2}\exp\left[N+\sqrt{2N}\,y+\chi\eta^{*}\right]\erfc(y).\quad

(3.21)

After multiplying it with the weight function and removing cocycles, it leads to the universal complementary error function kernel (sometimes called Faddeyeva plasma-kernel
[
66
]
).

3.3
Local edge scaling limit for one pair of characteristic polynomials

Because only one of the three ensembles we consider is a determinantal point process, the complex Ginibre ensemble, we do not have the freedom to remove
N
-dependent cocycles from the limiting characteristic polynomials which are proportional to the kernel, nor can we argue why to multiply with a weight function as in the Ginibre class A, which removes the leading contribution
\exp[N|z_{0}|^{2}]
from the expected characteristic polynomials in that case.

Therefore, we choose a different route in defining the limiting characteristic polynomials, by removing the exponential prefactor
\exp[zw^{*}]
that appears in all three ensembles, see eqs. (
2.22
), (
2.23
) and (
2.24
), respectively. Furthermore, as in the expressions for finite matrix dimension
N
given in Proposition
2.1
, we divide by the value of the respective characteristic polynomials at the origin,
D_{N}(0,0)
. It turns out that for classes AI
†
and AII
†
an additional rescaling in powers of
N^{\,\pm 1/2}

is necessary.

3.2
Local limits of a pair of characteristic polynomials at the spectral edge.

We recall the definitions (
3.1
), (
3.2
) as well as Owen’s
T

2.21
) and the results of Proposition
2.1
. The vicinity of edge points is defined as

z=\sqrt{d_{N}}sz_{0}+s\chi\ ,\quad w^{*}=\sqrt{d_{N}}sz_{0}^{*}+s\eta^{*},\quad zw^{*}=s^{2}(d_{N}+\sqrt{2d_{N}}\,y+\chi\eta^{*}),

(3.22)

with
|z_{0}|=1
and
y=(z_{0}^{*}\chi+z_{0}\eta^{*})/\sqrt{2}
. With this rescaling we have

\displaystyle D_{\rm edge}^{\rm A}(\chi,\eta^{*})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}e^{-zw^{*}}\frac{D_{N}^{\rm A}(z,w^{*})}{D_{N}^{\rm A}(0,0)}=\frac{1}{2}\erfc(y),

(3.23)

\displaystyle D_{\rm edge}^{\rm AI^{\dagger}}(\chi,\eta^{*})

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\sqrt{N}\,e^{-2zw^{*}}\frac{D_{N}^{\rm AI^{\dagger}}\left(z,w^{*}\right)}{D_{N}^{\rm AI^{\dagger}}(0,0)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-y^{2}}-\frac{y}{\sqrt{2}}\erfc(y),

(3.24)

\displaystyle D_{\rm edge}^{\rm AII^{\dagger}}(\chi,\eta^{*})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2N}}\,e^{-2zw^{*}}\frac{D_{N}^{\rm AII^{\dagger}}(z,w^{*})}{D_{N}^{\rm AII^{\dagger}}(0,0)}

\displaystyle\left(-\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{4}\erfc\left(y\right)\erfi(y/\sqrt{2})-i\sqrt{\pi}\,T\left(\sqrt{2}y,i/\sqrt{2}\right)\right)e^{-y^{2}/2}.

The corresponding left and right tail asymptotic read (for real

\displaystyle D_{\rm edge}^{\rm A}(\chi,\eta^{*})

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}y}\,e^{-y^{2}},&{\rm for}\ y\to+\infty,\\
\displaystyle 1+\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}y}\,e^{-y^{2}},&{\rm for}\ y\to-\infty,\\

\displaystyle D_{\rm edge}^{\rm AI^{\dagger}}(\chi,\eta^{*})

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{8\pi}\,y^{2}}\,e^{-y^{2}},&{\rm for}\ y\to+\infty,\\
\displaystyle-\sqrt{2}\,y+\frac{1}{\sqrt{8\pi}\,y^{2}}\,e^{-y^{2}},&{\rm for}\ y\to-\infty,\\
\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle D_{\rm edge}^{\rm AII^{\dagger}}(\chi,\eta^{*})

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{8\pi}\,y^{2}}e^{-y^{2}},&{\rm for}\ y\to+\infty,\\
\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\,y}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\,y^{3}},&{\rm for}\ y\to-\infty.\\
\end{array}\right.

The asymptotic (
3.24

[

for the eigenvector statistics as the initial expression (

From the results (
3.23

D_{\rm edge}^{\rm A}(0,0)=\frac{1}{2},\qquad D_{\rm edge}^{\rm AI^{\dagger}}(0,0)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\qquad{\rm and}\qquad D_{\rm edge}^{\rm AII}(0,0)=\frac{{\rm arctanh}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}})}{\sqrt{4\pi}}.

(3.35)

For AII

We need the values (

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{r}\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}z}e^{-z^{2}}\left(1-\frac{1}{2z^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(z^{-4})\right),\quad\mbox{for}\ z\to+\infty,\\
\displaystyle 2+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}z}e^{-z^{2}}\left(1-\frac{1}{2z^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(z^{-4})\right),\quad\mbox{for}\ z\to-\infty,\\
\end{array}\right.

where we give the next-to-leading order, as well.

Class AI
†
:
For the complex symmetric ensemble, it is preferable to write
E_{N-1}(x)=\partial_{x}E_{N}(x)=\partial_{x}(e^{x}Q(N+1,x))
in (
2.23
). Let us mention again that the scaling with
s=1/\sqrt{2}
guarantees the unit disc as the limiting support.
Proceeding as for the Ginibre ensemble, we multiply (
2.23
) with
e^{-2zw^{*}}
to cancel the exponential and set
x=2zw^{*}=N+\sqrt{2N}y+\chi\eta^{*}
,

\displaystyle e^{-2zw^{*}}\frac{D_{N}^{\rm AI^{\dagger}}\left(z,w^{*}\right)}{D_{N}^{\rm AI^{\dagger}}(0,0)}=Q(N+1,x)-\frac{xe^{-x}}{N+1}\partial_{x}\left(e^{x}Q(N+1,x)\right).

(3.39)

With help of the edge rescaling (
3.22
) at
|z_{0}|=1
and the asymptotic (
3.20
) with
y=(z_{0}^{*}\chi+z_{0}\eta^{*})/\sqrt{2}
, we arrive at

\displaystyle e^{-2zw^{*}}\frac{D_{N}^{\rm AI^{\dagger}}\left(z,w^{*}\right)}{D_{N}^{\rm AI^{\dagger}}(0,0)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\erfc(y)+\mathcal{O}(N^{-\frac{1}{2}})

(3.40)

\displaystyle-\frac{N+\sqrt{2N}y+\chi\eta^{*}}{N+1}\left(1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2N}}\partial_{y}\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}\erfc(y)+\mathcal{O}(N^{-\frac{1}{2}})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{y}{\sqrt{2N}}\erfc(y)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi N}}e^{-y^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{N}\right).

The two order one terms cancel after expanding the ratio in
1/N
. Eventually, we multiply with
\sqrt{N}
to find the result (
3.24
).

For the tail asymptotic we insert (
3.3
) into (
3.24
) and see that the leading order Gaussian decay cancels for the right tail,
y\to+\infty

D_{\rm edge}^{\rm AI^{\dagger}}(\chi,\eta^{*})\sim-y\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}y}e^{-y^{2}}\left(1-\frac{1}{2y^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(y^{-4})\right)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-y^{2}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{8\pi}\,y^{2}}e^{-y^{2}}\left(1+\mathcal{O}(y^{-2})\right).

(3.41)

The errors are uniform for any compact set where
y
can be drawn from so that the derivative can indeed be interchanged with the limit
N\to\infty
.

For the left tail with
y\to-\infty
, we obtain from the corresponding asymptotic (
3.3
) of the complementary error function,

\displaystyle D_{\rm edge}^{\rm AI^{\dagger}}(\chi,\eta^{*})

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-y^{2}}-\frac{y}{\sqrt{2}}\left(2+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}y}e^{-y^{2}}\left(1-\frac{1}{2y^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(y^{-4})\right)\right)

(3.42)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sqrt{2}y+\frac{1}{\sqrt{8\pi}\,y^{2}}e^{-y^{2}}\left(1+\mathcal{O}(y^{-2})\right),

a linear rise in
y
(as
y<0
) as the leading term.

Class AII
†
:
For the remaining ensemble of complex self-dual matrices, we cannot use standard asymptotic results. Our starting point is the integral representation (
2.99
) of (
2.24
) in the derivation for finite
N
, that we repeat for the readers’ convenience:

\displaystyle\frac{D_{N}^{\rm AII^{\dagger}}(z,w^{*})}{D_{N}^{\rm AII^{\dagger}}(0,0)}\biggl|_{x=zw^{*}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2N)!}f_{N}(x),\quad f_{N}(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}dv\int_{0}^{\infty}du\ e^{S(u,v)},\quad

\displaystyle S(u,v)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle-u-v+N\log\left(4xv+(2x+u)^{2}\right).

(3.43)

This calls for a Laplace approximation at large
N
. Any critical point
(u_{*},v_{*})
satisfies

\displaystyle 0=\partial_{v}S(u_{*},v_{*})=-1+\frac{4Nx}{4xv_{*}+(2x+u_{*})^{2}}\quad{\rm and}\quad 0=\partial_{u}S(u_{*},v_{*})=-1+\frac{2N(2x+u_{*})}{4xv_{*}+(2x+u_{*})^{2}}.\quad

(3.44)

Inserting the first into the second equation yields
u_{*}=0
, and reinserted into the first equation gives
v_{*}=N-x
.

To figure out whether the point
(u_{*},v_{*})
is the global maximum of
S(u,v)
we need to compare it with the extrema along the two boundaries. For the boundary
u=0
and
v\geq 0
,

0=\partial_{v}S(0,v)=-1+\frac{N}{v+x},

(3.45)

we find the same point
(u_{*},v_{*})
, whereas along the boundary
u\geq 0
and
v=0

0=\partial_{u}S(u_{0},0)=-1+\frac{2N}{2x+u_{0}},

(3.46)

we find
(u_{0},v_{0})=(2(N-x),0)
. Taking the second derivative confirms that both extrema are indeed local maxima.
However, when inserting the scaling limit (
3.22
) with
x=N+\sqrt{N}y+\chi\eta^{*}/2
, since
d_{N}=2N
and
s=1/\sqrt{2}
, we see that both maxima
(v_{*},u_{*})
and
(u_{0},v_{0})
are only within the integration domain
v,u\geq 0
for negative values
y<0
. This can be expected as this is the direction into the bulk of the spectrum which becomes immediate when setting
z=w
.

What is more important is that the leading order of both extrema is
\sqrt{N}
which is the same scale as the standard deviations of the Gaussians once we would expand about these points. This means that even an expansion about the origin would cover all relevant contributions from this integral. To pursue this direction we rescale
v=\sqrt{N}v^{\prime}
and
u=2\sqrt{N}u^{\prime}
,

f_{N}(x)=(2N)^{2N+1}\int_{0}^{\infty}dv^{\prime}\int_{0}^{\infty}du^{\prime}\exp\left[-\sqrt{N}v^{\prime}-2\sqrt{N}u^{\prime}+N\log\left(\frac{xv^{\prime}}{N^{3/2}}+\left(\frac{x}{N}+\frac{u^{\prime}}{\sqrt{N}}\right)^{2}\right)\right].

(3.47)

The logarithm can be expanded as follows,

\begin{split}&\log\left[\frac{xv^{\prime}}{N^{3/2}}+\left(\frac{x}{N}+\frac{u^{\prime}}{\sqrt{N}}\right)^{2}\right]\\
=&\log\left[1+\frac{v^{\prime}+2u^{\prime}+2y}{\sqrt{N}}+\frac{1}{N}\left(yv^{\prime}+(y+u^{\prime})^{2}+\chi\eta^{*}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{v^{\prime}+u^{\prime}}{N^{3/2}}\right)\right]\\
=&1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}(v^{\prime}+2u^{\prime}+2y)-\frac{1}{N}\left(\frac{(v^{\prime})^{2}}{2}+(u^{\prime})^{2}+y^{2}+2v^{\prime}u^{\prime}+v^{\prime}y+2u^{\prime}y-\chi\eta^{*}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{(v^{\prime})^{3}+(u^{\prime})^{3}}{N^{3/2}}\right).\end{split}

(3.48)

This expansion can be stopped at the second order as long as
(v^{\prime})^{3}+(u^{\prime})^{3}\ll\sqrt{N}
because the logarithm is multiplied with
N
. This restriction is, nonetheless, not a problem since already for
v^{\prime}+u^{\prime}\geq N^{\varepsilon}
for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
the integrand in (
3.47
) times
e^{-2\sqrt{N}y}
is exponentially small which we omit to show here. It is essentially due to the fact that the maximum of the integrand in the region
v^{\prime}+u^{\prime}\geq N^{\varepsilon}
is attained on the boundary
v^{\prime}+u^{\prime}=N^{\varepsilon}
, because the directional derivative of
S(u,v)
is always negative when the direction vector is non-zero and has non-negative entries, cf. the partial derivatives (
3.44
) which are negative in this region.

Summarising, it is asymptotically

f_{N}(x)\sim(2N)^{2N+1}e^{2\sqrt{N}y-y^{2}+\chi\eta^{*}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dv\int_{0}^{\infty}du\,e^{-u^{2}-\frac{1}{2}v^{2}-y(v+2u)-2vu},

(3.49)

when
N\to\infty
.
Consequently, the full expression together with the scaling (
3.22
) reads to leading order

\frac{e^{-2zw^{*}}}{\sqrt{2N}}\frac{D_{N}^{\rm AII^{\dagger}}(z,w^{*})}{D_{N}^{\rm AII^{\dagger}}(0,0)}\sim\frac{(2N)^{2N+1}}{\sqrt{2N}(2N)!}e^{-2N-y^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dv\int_{0}^{\infty}du\,e^{-u^{2}-\frac{1}{2}v^{2}-y(v+2u)-2vu}.

(3.50)

After we exploit the Stirling formula
(2N)!\approx\sqrt{2\pi}(2N)^{2N+1/2}e^{-2N}
, we are allowed to take the large-
N
limit leading to

\displaystyle D_{\rm edge}^{\rm AII^{\dagger}}(\chi,\eta^{*})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-y^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dv\int_{0}^{\infty}du\,e^{-u^{2}-\frac{1}{2}v^{2}-y(v+2u)-2vu}.

(3.51)

We can simplify this expression further when substituting
v^{\prime}=(v+y)/\sqrt{2}
and
u^{\prime}=u+v+y

\displaystyle D_{\rm edge}^{\rm AII^{\dagger}}(\chi,\eta^{*})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}e^{-y^{2}/2}\int_{y/\sqrt{2}}^{\infty}dv^{\prime}\,e^{+\frac{1}{2}v^{\prime 2}}\int_{\sqrt{2}v^{\prime}}^{\infty}du^{\prime}\,e^{-u^{\prime 2}}=\frac{1}{2}e^{-y^{2}/2}\int_{y/\sqrt{2}}^{\infty}dv\,e^{v^{2}}\erfc(\sqrt{2}v),

(3.52)

where we employed the definition (
2.17
) of the complementary error function. To get a closed expression as stated in (
3.25
), we make the ansatz

g^{\prime}(v)=-\frac{1}{2}\erfc(\sqrt{2}v)\,e^{v^{2}},

(3.53)

cf., Appendix
A
, which would simplify the integral (
3.52
) as follows

D_{\rm edge}^{\rm AII^{\dagger}}(\chi,\eta^{*})=e^{-y^{2}/2}(g(y/\sqrt{2})-g(+\infty)).

(3.54)

This approach is motivated by the fact that the original integral (
2.98
) satisfies an inhomogenous first order differential equation, as derived in the Appendix
A
. Here, we directly solve the differential equation (
3.53
) for
g(v)
.

For this purpose, we make use of Owen’s
T
-function with an imaginary number in its second entry (
2.21
). Its derivative in its first entry is explicitly given by

\partial_{x}T\left(x,ib\right)=-\frac{ix}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{b}du\ e^{-\frac{1}{2}x^{2}(1-u^{2})}=-\frac{i}{\sqrt{8\pi}}e^{-x^{2}/2}{\rm erfi}\left[\frac{bx}{\sqrt{2}}\right].

(3.55)

Thence, when choosing
x=2v
and
b=1/\sqrt{2}
, the derivative of

g(v)=-\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{4}\erfc\left(\sqrt{2}\,v\right)\erfi(v)-i\sqrt{\pi}T\left(2v,\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}\right)

(3.56)

solves the differential equation (
3.53
).

We still have to determine
g(+\infty)
.
While the asymptotic of the complementary error function is given in (
3.3
), for the error function at imaginary argument we have
[
67
]

\erfi(v)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}\,v}e^{+v^{2}}\left(1+\frac{1}{2v^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(1/v^{4})\right),

(3.57)

which is even valid for both
y\to\pm\infty
.
Therefore, for the first term in (
3.56
) we obtain

-\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{4}\erfc\left(\sqrt{2}\,v\right)\erfi(v)\sim\left\{\begin{array}[]{r}\displaystyle-\frac{1}{4\sqrt{2\pi}v^{2}}e^{-v^{2}}\left(1+\frac{1}{4v^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(v^{-4})\right),\quad\mbox{for}\ v\to+\infty,\\
\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2v}e^{+v^{2}}\left(1+\frac{1}{2v^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(v^{-4})\right),\quad\mbox{for}\ v\to-\infty,\\
\end{array}\right.

(3.58)

implying that it vanishes for
v\to+\infty
.
For the tail asymptotic, we also give the expansion for
v\to-\infty
.

We did not find the asymptotic of Owen’s
T
-function in the literature. Hence, we present a short derivation. Starting from (
2.21
), the substitution
z=(1-u^{2})v^{2}
leads to

\displaystyle-i\sqrt{\pi}T\left(2v,i/\sqrt{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{8\pi}\,v^{2}}e^{-v^{2}}\int_{0}^{v^{2}}dz\,{e^{-z}}\left(1+\frac{z}{v^{2}}\right)^{-1}\left(1-\frac{z}{v^{2}}\right)^{-1/2}

(3.59)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{8\pi}\,v^{2}}e^{-v^{2}}\int_{0}^{v^{2}}dz\,{e^{-z}}\left[1-\frac{1}{2}\frac{z}{v^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{z^{2}}{v^{4}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{8\pi}\,v^{2}}e^{-v^{2}}\left[1-\frac{1}{2v^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{v^{4}}\right)\right],

where in the last step we used elementary integrals. The expansion of the integrand is only uniform when cutting off at
z\leq v^{\varepsilon}\ll v^{2}
for any constant
\varepsilon\in(0,2)
. Like before this is not a problem as the integrand is uniformly bounded from above by
e^{-v^{\varepsilon}}/\sqrt{1-z/v^{2}}
when

, whose integral thus vanishes exponentially for
v\to\infty
.

Let us remark that the expansion in
1/v
holds for both

; in particular, it vanishes in both limits.
As a result, the asymptotic yields
g(+\infty)=0
so that the full solution for the edge scaling limit from (

) is given by (
3.25
).

After having evaluated the asymptotic of

already in great detail, we can immediately give the tail estimates. For

, it is

D_{\rm edge}^{\rm AII^{\dagger}}(\chi,\eta^{*})\sim e^{-y^{2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{8\pi}\,y^{2}}\left(1-\frac{5}{2y^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(y^{-4})\right),

(3.60)

where both terms in (

) contribute to the same order. For

) gives the leading contribution while Owen’s

(3.61)

which completes the derivation of (
3.2
). We note that for negative
y
this is a positive function.

All three limiting characteristic polynomials at the edge are plotted in Fig.
2
. The difference is most pronounced at large negative

. While class A leads to a constant (density),
in class AI
†

\sim y

3.4
Bulk scaling limit for one pair of characteristic polynomials

The limiting expressions for the rescaled characteristic polynomials in the bulk can be defined in a similar way as at the edge.

3.3
Local limits of a pair of characteristic polynomials in the spectral bulk.

Recalling Proposition
2.1
, the local scaling in the bulk, defined as

as well as the definitions (

(3.63)

(3.64)

(3.65)

All three results are consistent with the respective global limit eqs. (
3.6
), (

) and (

) with

. In particular, they give unity at the origin, by setting

Class A:
Looking at (
2.22

Class AI
†
:
Likewise, the result (

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}e^{-2zw^{*}}\left(E_{N}(2zw^{*})-\frac{2zw^{*}}{N+1}E_{N-1}(2zw^{*})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\frac{N|z_{0}|^{2}+\sqrt{2N}\,y+\chi\eta^{*}}{N+1}

as both truncated exponentials give unity.
Evidently, the subleading term contains non-trivial information about the local spectral fluctuation
y

Class AII
†
:
Let us turn to the ensemble of complex self-dual matrices, where we return to (
3.44
) determining the critical points. We note that this time the bulk scaling (
3.62
) is equal to
x=zw^{*}=N|z_{0}|^{2}+\sqrt{N}y+\chi\eta^{*}/2

(u_{*},v_{*})=(0,N-x)
(and maximum at the boundary

We need to compare the integrand in

), at these two points to decide which of those is the global maximum. For this purpose, we ask for the leading asymptotic of the integrand
\exp[S(u,v)]
, with

(3.67)

at these two points which is

\displaystyle e^{S(u_{*},v_{*})}\sim(2N|z_{0}|)^{2N}\exp[-N(1-|z_{0}|^{2})]\quad{\rm and}\quad e^{S(u_{0},v_{0})}\sim(2N)^{2N}\exp[-2N(1-|z_{0}|^{2})].

(3.68)

Thus, we obtain for the difference

S(u_{*},v_{*})-S(u_{0},v_{0})\sim N(1-|z_{0}|^{2}+2\log|z_{0}|)

The function
h(a)=1-a+\log a
is strictly
increasing
for
a=|z_{0}|^{2}\in(0,1)
and takes its maximum at

which is
h(1)=0
. Therefore, it is
S(u_{*},v_{*})<S(u_{0},v_{0})
for suitable large
N
and
(u_{0},v_{0})=(2(N-x),0)

Consequently, we substitute
u=2N(1-|z_{0}|^{2})+2\sqrt{N}u^{\prime}
while the variable
v
remains unchanged. Plugging this into the integral (
3.43
) we arrive at

\displaystyle f_{N}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!2\sqrt{N}\!\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!dv\!\int_{-\sqrt{N}(1-|z_{0}|^{2})}^{\infty}\!\!\!du^{\prime}e^{-2N(1-|z_{0}|^{2})-2\sqrt{N}u^{\prime}-v}\!\left[4xv+(2x+2N(1-|z_{0}|^{2})+2\sqrt{N}u^{\prime})^{2}\right]^{N}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\frac{(2N)^{2N+1}}{\sqrt{N}}e^{-2N(1-|z_{0}|^{2})}\int_{0}^{\infty}dv\int_{-\sqrt{N}(1-|z_{0}|^{2})}^{\infty}du^{\prime}e^{-2\sqrt{N}u^{\prime}-v}\left[1+\frac{2}{\sqrt{N}}(u^{\prime}+y)\right.

Here, we have inserted the bulk scaling (

In the next step, we have to exponentiate the bracket to the

\displaystyle e^{-2x}\frac{f_{N}(x)}{(2N)!}

\displaystyle\frac{(2N)^{2N+1}e^{-2N}}{\sqrt{N}(2N)!}\int_{0}^{\infty}dv\int_{-\sqrt{N}(1-|z_{0}|^{2})}^{\infty}du^{\prime}\exp\left[-v(1-|z_{0}|^{2})-(u^{\prime}+y)^{2}\right]

A similar statement would also allow us to extend the integral to

\displaystyle e^{-2x}\frac{f_{N}(x)}{(2N)!}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dv\exp\left[-v(1-|z_{0}|^{2})\right]\left[1+\frac{vy}{\sqrt{N}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{v^{2}}{N}\right)\right].

(3.72)

Here, we carried out the integral over

yielding

up to order

which is

\displaystyle e^{-2x}\frac{f_{N}(x)}{(2N)!}=\frac{1}{1-|z_{0}|^{2}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\frac{y}{(1-|z_{0}|^{2})^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(N^{-1}).

in (

4
Expectation values for
k
pairs of characteristic polynomials

As the classes AI
†
and AII
†
lack the insight of an explicit joint probability density of the complex eigenvalues we need to circumvent this obstacle. The aim is to derive an effective Lagrangian representation, similar to those for real eigenvalue statistics of Hermitian random matrix ensembles. Those were very helpful in explaining the applicability of random matrix theory to quantum field theories
[
68
]
and condensed matter theory
[
69
]
.

In these applications, the main object is usually an average over products or ratios of characteristic polynomials. For instance, we consider the quantity

\displaystyle D_{N,k}(Z,W^{*})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{\left\langle\prod_{j=1}^{k}\det[J-z_{j}\mathbf{1}_{d_{N}}]\det[J^{\dagger}-w^{*}_{j}\mathbf{1}_{d_{N}}]\right\rangle}{\left\langle\left|\det[J-\sqrt{d_{N}}sz_{0}\mathbf{1}_{d_{N}}]\right|^{2k}\right\rangle}

(4.1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\left\langle\det[J\otimes\mathbf{1}_{k}-\mathbf{1}_{d_{N}}\otimes Z]\det[J^{\dagger}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{k}-\mathbf{1}_{d_{N}}\otimes W^{*}]\right\rangle}{\left\langle\left|\det[J\otimes\mathbf{1}_{k}-\sqrt{d_{N}}sz_{0}\mathbf{1}_{d_{N}}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{k}]\right|^{2}\right\rangle},

with
Z={\rm diag}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})
and
W^{*}={\rm diag}(w^{*}_{1},\ldots,w^{*}_{k})
.
We recall the definitions (
3.1
) and (
3.2
), in particular that the dimension
d_{N}
and the scaling
s
depend on the ensemble. We employed tensor notation to write everything in a compact form, and we normalised the expectation value to simplify the calculations below so that we can easily keep track of the normalisation constants; especially it holds
D_{N,k}(\sqrt{d_{N}}sz_{0}\mathbf{1}_{d_{N}},\sqrt{d_{N}}sz_{0}\mathbf{1}_{d_{N}})=1
.

In the physics literature, e.g., see
[
68
,
69
,
70
]
, expectation values as those in the numerator of (
4.1
) can be usually cast for large-
N
into the form

\left\langle\det[J\otimes\mathbf{1}_{k}-\mathbf{1}_{d_{N}}\otimes Z]\det[J^{\dagger}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{k}-\mathbf{1}_{d_{N}}\otimes W^{*}]\right\rangle\sim C_{N}\int_{\mathcal{S}}d\mu(U)\exp[-\mathcal{L}_{\rm eff}(U,Z,W^{*})],

(4.2)

where
C_{N}(Z,W^{*})
is some
N
-dependent constant,
\mathcal{S}
is a saddle point manifold (also known as Goldstone manifold) whose size depends on
k
but is independent of
N
,
d\mu(U)
is the uniform measure on
\mathcal{S}
, and
\mathcal{L}_{\rm eff}(U,Z,W^{*})
is the effective Lagrangian depending on the source variables
Z
and
W^{*}
as well as on
U
. The spurion analysis
[
70
]
gives some low order expansion for
\mathcal{L}_{\rm eff}(U,Z,W^{*})
in terms of its arguments. We note that the set
\mathcal{S}
as well as the Lagrangian usually depend on the points where one zooms into. In our case, this zooming is given by

\displaystyle z_{j}=\sqrt{d_{N}}sz_{0}+s\chi_{j}\qquad{\rm and}\qquad w^{*}_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{d_{N}}sz_{0}+s\eta^{*}_{j}\ ,

(4.3)

with
\chi=\mbox{diag}(\chi_{1},\ldots,\chi_{k})
and
\eta=\mbox{diag}(\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{k})
. We will indeed see that both, the saddle point manifold and the Lagrangian, change depending on whether
z_{0}
lies in the bulk or at the spectral edge. Here,
z_{0}
(fixed in
N
) is chosen to be either at the edge of the spectrum when
|z_{0}|=1
, or in the bulk of the spectrum

|z_{0}|<1
.
The parameters
\chi_{j}
and
\eta^{*}_{j}
are also fixed and probe the spectral fluctuations on the scale of the local mean level spacing.
We recall that we average over Gaussian random matrices of the form
\exp[-{\rm Tr}\,JJ^{\dagger}]
for any symmetry class. Hence, we need to adjust
d_{N}
and
s
for any of those.

This procedure works rather well in quantum field theory and condensed matter theory. It is also very effective for a zero-dimensional field theories which are essentially random matrix theories. We will see in the derivations of Theorems
4.1
and
4.2
that all three ensembles we consider, with the normalisation (
4.1
), exhibit the very same effective Lagrangian
in the bulk (
|z_{0}|<1
)

\mathcal{L}_{\rm eff}^{\rm bulk}(U,Z,W^{*})=\sqrt{d_{N}}{\rm Tr}\,(z_{0}^{*}\chi+z_{0}\eta^{*})+\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}\,({\rm diag}(\chi,\chi)U^{\dagger}{\rm diag}(\eta^{*},\eta^{*})U)

(4.4)

as well as at the edge (
|z_{0}|=1
)

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{\rm eff}^{\rm edge}(A,Z,W^{*})=&\sqrt{d_{N}}{\rm Tr}\,(z_{0}^{*}\chi+z_{0}\eta^{*})-\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}\,[(z_{0}^{*}\chi)^{2}+(z_{0}\eta^{*})^{2}]\\
&-\frac{z_{0}^{*}}{\sqrt{2}}{\rm Tr}\,A^{\dagger}A\,{\rm diag}(\chi,\chi)-\frac{z_{0}}{\sqrt{2}}{\rm Tr}\,AA^{\dagger}\,{\rm diag}(\eta^{*},\eta^{*})-\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}\,(A^{\dagger}A)^{2}.\end{split}

(4.5)

Only the saddle point manifolds are varying from ensemble to ensemble which are the most important indicator that the spectral statistics are different for the three ensembles.
At the edge the set
\mathcal{S}
is actually a vector space so that the uniform measure is the Lebesgue measure on this vector space, while in the bulk we will see that the saddle point manifolds are classical Lie groups.

We would like to emphasise that we have checked and compared with existing literature
[
35
,
20
,
36
]
that the real and quaternion Ginibre ensemble yield the very same Lagrangians and saddle point manifolds as the complex one (class A) when staying away from the real axis. Only when zooming in on the real axis
\mathcal{S}
will be different from class
A
though the Lagrangian still looks the same. We will, however, not show this here and postpone it to future work where we want to discuss all symmetry classes of non-Hermitian random matrices in this way.

Coming back to the three ensembles we discuss here, we would like to remark that the normalisation with respect to the denominator of (
4.1
) helps to keep track of the normalisation though it is not enough to have a well-defined limit
N\to\infty
. This can be seen in the resulting Lagrangians (
4.4
) and (
4.5
), due to the
d_{N}
-dependent term. To take care of that we normalise the ratio as follows

\widehat{D}_{N,k}(Z,W^{*}):=\frac{D_{N,k}(Z,W^{*})}{\prod_{j=1}^{k}D_{N,1}(z_{j},w_{j}^{*})}.

(4.6)

The pairings into arguments
(z_{j},w_{j}^{*})
in the product of expectation values in the denominator is introduced
ad hoc
but becomes natural when choosing
w_{j}^{*}=z_{j}^{*}
. The latter choice gives the source variables an interpretation of some complex conjugate pairs of “test-charges” that explore the spectrum of the random matrix
J
.

Bulk limit for

We recall the definitions (
4.1
) and (
4.6
). In the bulk limit (
4.3
) with
|z_{0}|<1

we obtain the following
expressions for

\begin{split}D_{{\rm bulk},k}^{\rm R}(\chi,\eta^{*}):=&\lim_{N\to\infty}\widehat{D}_{N,k}^{\rm R}(Z,W^{*})\\
=&\exp\left[-{\rm Tr}\,\chi\eta^{*}\right]\int_{\mathcal{G}}d\mu(U)\exp\left[\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}\,({\rm diag}(\chi,\chi)U^{\dagger}{\rm diag}(\eta^{*},\eta^{*})U)\right].\end{split}

(4.7)

In all three cases, the saddle point manifolds are Lie groups given by
\mathcal{G}={\rm U}(k)
for class R=A (complex Ginibre),
\mathcal{G}={\rm USp}(2k)
for class R= AI
†
(complex symmetric), and
\mathcal{G}={\rm O}(2k)
for class R=AII
†
(complex self-dual), where
d\mu(U)
is their normalised Haar measure. For class A, we embed
{\rm U}(k)
into the
2k\times 2k
matrices as follows,
U={\rm diag}(\widetilde{U},\widetilde{U})
with
\widetilde{U}\in{\rm U}(k)
, to write the result for all three classes in a unifying way.

The expression (
4.7
) is translation and rotation invariant, particularly it is

D_{{\rm bulk},k}^{\rm R}(\chi,\eta^{*})=D_{{\rm bulk},k}^{\rm R}(e^{i\vartheta}\chi+\lambda\mathbf{1}_{k},e^{-i\vartheta}\eta^{*}+\lambda^{*}\mathbf{1}_{k})

(4.8)

for any fixed
\vartheta\in\mathbb{R}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
. As aforementioned, this is one of the main features of a bulk
point. Actually the symmetry group is even larger in this particular case as the invariance even holds for
(\chi,\eta^{*})\to(e^{i\vartheta}\chi+\lambda\mathbf{1}_{k},e^{-i\vartheta}\eta^{*}+\mu^{*}\mathbf{1}_{k})
for any
\vartheta,\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{C}
. This is due to the analyticity in both arguments,
\chi
and
\eta^{*}
, individually.

The result (
4.7
) for class A was previously derived in
[
54
, Theorem 1.15]
. It was shown in
[
53
]
that away from the real axis the same result prevails for the real and quaternion Ginibre ensemble. We have checked this statement independently using the same technique as below. The group integral is actually the Harish-Chandra–Itzykson–Zuber integral
[
71
,
59
]
which is explicitly known

\begin{split}D_{{\rm bulk},k}^{\rm A}(\chi,\eta^{*})=&\exp\left[-{\rm Tr}\,\chi\eta^{*}\right]\int_{{\rm U}(k)}d\mu(U)\exp\left[{\rm Tr}\,(\chi\widetilde{U}^{\dagger}\eta^{*}\widetilde{U})\right]\\
=&\left(\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}j!\right)\frac{\exp\left[-{\rm Tr}\,\chi\eta^{*}\right]}{\Delta_{k}(\chi)\Delta_{k}(\eta^{*})}\det[\exp(\chi_{a}\eta_{b}^{*})]_{a,b=1,\ldots,k},\end{split}

(4.9)

where

\Delta_{k}(x)=\prod_{1\leq a<b\leq k}(x_{b}-x_{a})

(4.10)

is the Vandermonde determinant. This agrees with a derivation based on planar orthogonal polynomials presented in Appendix
B
.

The group integrals in (
4.7
) for AI
†
and AII
†
are the other two Itzykson-Zuber integrals
[
59
]
which are not known explicitly, and only recurrence relations exist
[
72
]
. In particular, the authors observed that the recurrence relations are incompatible with determinantal or Pfaffian structures.

Class A:
We start with the derivation for the complex Ginibre ensemble, in order to be self-contained. Let us recall that it is
d_{N}=N
and
s=1
in this case.

To deal with the characteristic polynomials in (
4.1
) we introduce two
N\times k
dimensional complex Grassmann matrices

\xi=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\xi_{11}&\cdots&\xi_{1k}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
\xi_{N1}&\cdots&\xi_{Nk}\end{array}\right]\qquad{\rm and}\qquad\psi=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\psi_{11}&\cdots&\psi_{1k}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
\psi_{N1}&\cdots&\psi_{Nk}\end{array}\right]

(4.11)

via the standard identity

\begin{split}&\det[J\otimes\mathbf{1}_{k}-\mathbf{1}_{N}\otimes Z]\det[J^{\dagger}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{k}-\mathbf{1}_{N}\otimes W^{*}]\\
=&\int[d\xi][d\psi]\exp\left[{\rm Tr}\,J\xi\xi^{\dagger}+{\rm Tr}\,J^{\dagger}\psi\psi^{\dagger}+{\rm Tr}\,Z\xi^{\dagger}\xi+{\rm Tr}\,W^{*}\psi^{\dagger}\psi\right]\end{split}

(4.12)

with
[d\xi][d\psi]=\prod_{l=1}^{N}\prod_{j=1}^{k}d\xi_{lj}d\xi^{*}_{lj}d\psi_{lj}d\psi^{*}_{lj}
and
\psi^{\dagger}
being the transpose and complex conjugate of
\psi_{l}
which is the generalisation of (
2.12
). We would like to underline that we have exploited the anti-commutation relations of Grassmann variables when interchanging the matrices
\psi
and
\psi^{\dagger}
.
For the determinant in the denominator in (
4.1
) we only need to set
\chi_{j}=\eta_{j}=0
in the above expression.

For the complex Ginibre ensemble the matrix
J
does not satisfy any symmetries so that we can take the expectation of it without further ado,

\begin{split}&D_{N,k}^{\rm A}(Z,W^{*})=\\
=&\frac{\int[d\xi][d\psi]\exp\left[{\rm Tr}\,\xi\xi^{\dagger}\psi\psi^{\dagger}+{\rm Tr}\,Z\xi^{\dagger}\xi+{\rm Tr}\,W^{*}\psi^{\dagger}\psi\right]}{\int[d\xi][d\psi]\exp\left[{\rm Tr}\,\xi\xi^{\dagger}\psi\psi^{\dagger}+\sqrt{N}z_{0}{\rm Tr}\,\xi^{\dagger}\xi+\sqrt{N}z_{0}^{*}{\rm Tr}\,\psi^{\dagger}\psi\right]}\\
=&\frac{\int[d\xi][d\psi]\int_{\mathbb{C}^{k\times k}}[dA]\exp\left[-N{\rm Tr}\,A^{\dagger}A+i\sqrt{N}{\rm Tr}\,A\psi^{\dagger}\xi+i\sqrt{N}{\rm Tr}\,A^{\dagger}\xi^{\dagger}\psi+{\rm Tr}\,Z\xi^{\dagger}\xi+{\rm Tr}\,W^{*}\psi^{\dagger}\psi\right]}{\int[d\xi][d\psi]\int_{\mathbb{C}^{k\times k}}[dA]\exp\!\left[-N{\rm Tr}\,A^{\dagger}A+i\sqrt{N}{\rm Tr}\,A\psi^{\dagger}\xi+i\sqrt{N}{\rm Tr}\,A^{\dagger}\xi^{\dagger}\psi+\sqrt{N}z_{0}{\rm Tr}\,\xi^{\dagger}\xi+\sqrt{N}z_{0}^{*}{\rm Tr}\,\psi^{\dagger}\psi\right]},\end{split}

(4.13)

where we used the anti-commutativity of the Grassmann variables and applied the Hubbard-Stratonovich transformation
[
73
]
in the second equality. The linearisation in the dyadic matrices
\psi^{\dagger}\xi
and
\xi^{\dagger}\psi
allows us to integrate over all Grassmann variables again as they are simple Gaussians, i.e.,

\begin{split}\int[d\xi][d\psi]\exp\left[i\sqrt{N}{\rm Tr}\,A\psi^{\dagger}\xi+i\sqrt{N}{\rm Tr}\,A^{\dagger}\xi^{\dagger}\psi+{\rm Tr}\,Z\xi^{\dagger}\xi+{\rm Tr}\,W^{*}\psi^{\dagger}\psi\right]=&{\det}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}Z&i\sqrt{N}A^{\dagger}\\
i\sqrt{N}A&W^{*}\end{array}\right]^{N},\end{split}

(4.14)

which leads to

\begin{split}D_{N,k}^{\rm A}(Z,W^{*})=\frac{\int_{\mathbb{C}^{k\times k}}[dA]\exp\left[-N{\rm Tr}\,A^{\dagger}A\right]\det\left[ZW^{*}/N+A^{\dagger}(W^{*})^{-1}AW^{*}\right]^{N}}{\int_{\mathbb{C}^{k\times k}}[dA]\exp\left[-N{\rm Tr}\,A^{\dagger}A\right]\det\left[|z_{0}|^{2}\mathbf{1}_{k}+A^{\dagger}A\right]^{N}}.\end{split}

(4.15)

To simplify the determinant we have exploited (
2.42
).

When taking the large-
N
limit, we need to maximise the leading order Lagrangian

\mathcal{L}=-{\rm Tr}\,A^{\dagger}A+{\rm Tr}\,\log\left(|z_{0}|^{2}\mathbf{1}_{k}+A^{\dagger}A\right)

(4.16)

in
A
. This is evidently uniquely given when

A^{\dagger}A=(1-|z_{0}|^{2})\mathbf{1}_{k}\ ,

(4.17)

which follows from differentiating
\mathcal{L}
in
A^{\dagger}A
.

We expand about this solution by first going over to the polar decomposition
A=\widetilde{U}H
with
\widetilde{U}\in{\rm U}(k)
a unitary matrix and
H=H^{\dagger}\geq 0
a positive semi-definite matrix. The measure transforms (up to a normalisation) as follows

[dA]\propto\det(H\otimes\mathbf{1}_{k}+\mathbf{1}_{k}\otimes H)[dH]d\widetilde{\mu}(\widetilde{U}),

(4.18)

where
d\widetilde{\mu}(\widetilde{U})
is the normalised Haar measure on
{\rm U}(k)
. The Jacobian can be computed by performing first a singular value decomposition of
A
and then reverse the eigenvalue decomposition of
H
. In this way, the term
\det(H\otimes\mathbf{1}_{k}+\mathbf{1}_{k}\otimes H)
is reminiscent of the fact that the singular value decomposition yields a square of a Vandermonde determinant in the squared singular values of
A
as well as a factor
\det H
. The singular values are, however, the eigenvalues of
H
whose eigenvalue decomposition only needs a square of a Vandermonde determinant in the eigenvalues, and not their squares which is corrected by the non-trivial term
\det(H\otimes\mathbf{1}_{k}+\mathbf{1}_{k}\otimes H)
.

After this transformation we
set
H=\sqrt{1-|z_{0}|^{2}}\mathbf{1}_{k}+\delta H/\sqrt{N}
and expand in
1/\sqrt{N}
, showing that the term
\det(H\otimes\mathbf{1}_{k}+\mathbf{1}_{k}\otimes H)
only yields a constant in leading order. For the determinant, we use

\begin{split}\det\left[\frac{ZW^{*}}{N}+A^{\dagger}(W^{*})^{-1}AW^{*}\right]=&{\det}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\displaystyle z_{0}\mathbf{1}_{k}+\frac{\chi}{\sqrt{N}}&\displaystyle\left(\sqrt{1-|z_{0}|^{2}}\mathbf{1}_{k}+\frac{\delta H}{\sqrt{N}}\right)\widetilde{U}^{\dagger}\\
\displaystyle-\widetilde{U}\left(\sqrt{1-|z_{0}|^{2}}\mathbf{1}_{k}+\frac{\delta H}{\sqrt{N}}\right)&\displaystyle z_{0}^{*}\mathbf{1}_{k}+\frac{\eta^{*}}{\sqrt{N}}\end{array}\right]\\
=&\det\left[\mathbf{1}_{2k}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\!\left[\!\begin{array}[]{cc}z_{0}^{*}\mathbf{1}_{k}&-\sqrt{1-|z_{0}|^{2}}\widetilde{U}^{\dagger}\\
\sqrt{1-|z_{0}|^{2}}\widetilde{U}&z_{0}\mathbf{1}_{k}\end{array}\!\right]\!\!\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\chi&\delta H\widetilde{U}^{\dagger}\\
-\widetilde{U}\delta H&\eta^{*}\end{array}\right]\!\right]\end{split}

(4.19)

which can be found by going back to the structure to the determinant in (
4.14
) with the matrix in its block form. This leads to the expansion

\begin{split}&\exp[-N{\rm Tr}\,A^{\dagger}A]\det\left[\frac{ZW^{*}}{N}+A^{\dagger}(W^{*})^{-1}AW^{*}\right]^{N}\\
=&\exp\biggl[-Nk(1-|z_{0}|^{2})+\sqrt{N}{\rm Tr}\,\left(z_{0}^{*}\chi+z_{0}\eta^{*}\right)-\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}\,\left[(z_{0}^{*}\chi)^{2}+(z_{0}\eta^{*})^{2}-2(1-|z_{0}|^{2})\chi\widetilde{U}^{\dagger}\eta^{*}\widetilde{U}\right]\\
&\quad\quad-2(1-|z_{0}|^{2}){\rm Tr}\,\delta H^{2}-2\sqrt{1-|z_{0}|^{2}}{\rm Tr}\,\delta H(z_{0}^{*}\chi+z_{0}\widetilde{U}^{\dagger}\eta^{*}\widetilde{U})+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{||\delta H||^{3}}{\sqrt{N}}\right)\biggl],\end{split}

(4.20)

where
||X||=\sqrt{{\rm Tr}\,XX^{\dagger}}
is the Hilbert-Schmidt norm of a matrix. This highlights that we obtain a Gaussian approximation in
\delta H
uniformly when
||\delta H||\ll N^{1/6}
. One can, however, show that the integral restricted to
||\delta H||\gg N^{\varepsilon}
for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
is exponentially small compared to the main contribution at
\delta H=0
so that a restriction to say
||\delta H||\leq N^{1/12}
is sufficient. We will not work out these mathematical details as they
do not illuminate the problem further.

The Gaussian integral in
\delta H
we obtain after these expansions
are integrated out and we arrive at

\begin{split}D_{N,k}^{\rm A}(Z,W^{*})=\exp[\sqrt{N}{\rm Tr}\,\left(z_{0}^{*}\chi+z_{0}\eta^{*}\right)]\int_{{\rm U}(k)}d\widetilde{\mu}(\widetilde{U})\exp\left[{\rm Tr}\,(\chi\widetilde{U}^{\dagger}\eta^{*}\widetilde{U})\right]\left[1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\right)\right].\end{split}

(4.21)

We note that for
k=1
it is trivially

\prod_{l=1}^{k}D_{N,1}^{\rm A}(z_{l},w^{*}_{l})\sim\exp\left[\sqrt{N}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left(z_{0}^{*}\chi_{j}+z_{0}\eta_{j}^{*}\right)\right]\exp\left[\sum_{l=1}^{k}\chi_{l}\eta_{l}^{*}\right],

(4.22)

which shows how the exponential prefactor comes about for class
A
.
Finally, we bring it into the unifying form (
4.7
) by choosing
U={\rm diag}(\widetilde{U},\widetilde{U})
and
d\mu(U)=d\widetilde{\mu}(\widetilde{U})
.

We turn to the complex symmetric and complex self-dual random matrices which can be dealt with in parallel. We recall that the scaling is for both ensembles

J^{T}=TJT

T=\left\{\begin{array}[]{clll}\mathbf{1}_{N},&J\ {\rm is\ symmetric},&d_{N}=N,&\mbox{class R=AI}^{\dagger}\\
\tau_{2}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{N}=\Sigma_{y},&J\ \text{is\ self-dual},&d_{N}=2N,&\mbox{class R=AII}^{\dagger},\end{array}\right.

(4.23)

where
\tau_{2}
is the second Pauli matrix.
This matrix has a dual one of the form

\hat{T}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cll}\tau_{2}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{k},&J\ {\rm is\ symmetric},&\mbox{class R=AI}^{\dagger},\\
\mathbf{1}_{2k},&J\ \text{is\ self-dual},&\mbox{class R=AII}^{\dagger},\end{array}\right.

(4.24)

which are both of dimension
2k
.
Using these two matrices, we may rewrite the determinants in terms of Pfaffians

\det[J\otimes\mathbf{1}_{k}-\mathbf{1}_{d_{N}}\otimes Z]\det[J^{\dagger}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{k}-\mathbf{1}_{d_{N}}\otimes W^{*}]=\pm{\rm Pf}[TJ\otimes\hat{T}-T\otimes\tilde{z}\hat{T}]{\rm Pf}[TJ^{\dagger}\otimes\hat{T}-T\otimes\tilde{w}\hat{T}],

(4.25)

with
\tilde{z}={\rm diag}(Z,Z)
and
\tilde{w}={\rm diag}(W^{*},W^{*})
. The overall sign is, however, not a problem as it is cancelled in the ratio (
4.1
).
The two Pfaffians can be expressed as Gaussian integrals over two
d_{N}\times 2k
real Grassmann matrices

\xi=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\xi_{11}&\cdots&\xi_{1\,2k}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
\xi_{d_{N}1}&\cdots&\xi_{d_{N}\,2k}\end{array}\right]\qquad{\rm and}\qquad\psi=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\psi_{11}&\cdots&\psi_{1\,2k}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
\psi_{d_{N}1}&\cdots&\psi_{d_{N}\,2k}\end{array}\right],

(4.26)

similar to (
2.13
), namely

\begin{split}&{\rm Pf}[TJ\otimes\hat{T}-T\otimes\tilde{z}\hat{T}]{\rm Pf}[TJ^{\dagger}\otimes\hat{T}-T\otimes\tilde{w}\hat{T}]\\
=&\int[d\xi][d\psi]\exp[{\rm Tr}\,TJ\xi\hat{T}\xi^{T}+{\rm Tr}\,TJ^{\dagger}\psi\hat{T}\psi^{T}+{\rm Tr}\,\xi^{T}T\xi\tilde{z}\hat{T}+{\rm Tr}\,\psi^{T}T\psi\tilde{w}\hat{T}].\end{split}

(4.27)

Let the superscript R be either AI
†
or AII
†
to point out the ensemble in the ensuing computation. Once we carry out the Gaussian average over
J
we arrive at

\begin{split}D_{N,k}^{\rm R}(Z,W^{*})=&\frac{\int[d\xi][d\psi]\exp\left[{\rm Tr}\,\xi\hat{T}\xi^{T}T\psi\hat{T}\psi^{T}T+{\rm Tr}\,\xi^{T}T\xi\tilde{z}\hat{T}+{\rm Tr}\,\psi^{T}T\psi\tilde{w}\hat{T}\right]}{\int[d\xi][d\psi]\exp\left[{\rm Tr}\,\xi\hat{T}\xi^{T}T\psi\hat{T}\psi^{T}T+\sqrt{\frac{d_{N}}{2}}z_{0}{\rm Tr}\,\xi^{T}T\xi\hat{T}+\sqrt{\frac{d_{N}}{2}}z_{0}^{*}{\rm Tr}\,\psi^{T}T\psi\hat{T}\right]}\\
=&\frac{\int[d\xi][d\psi]\int_{\mathcal{M}}[dA]\exp\left[\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(A,\xi,\psi,\tilde{z},\tilde{w})\right]}{\int[d\xi][d\psi]\int_{\mathcal{M}}[dA]\exp\left[\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(A,\xi,\psi,z_{0}\mathbf{1}_{2k},z_{0}^{*}\mathbf{1}_{2k})\right]},\end{split}

(4.28)

with

\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(A,\xi,\psi,\tilde{z},\tilde{w})=-\frac{d_{N}}{2}{\rm Tr}\,AA^{\dagger}+i\sqrt{\frac{d_{N}}{2}}\left({\rm Tr}\,A\hat{T}\psi^{T}T\xi+{\rm Tr}\,A^{T}\xi^{T}T\psi\hat{T}\right)+{\rm Tr}\,\xi^{T}T\xi\tilde{z}\hat{T}+{\rm Tr}\,\psi^{T}T\psi\tilde{w}\hat{T}.

(4.29)

The Hubbard-Stratonovich transformation has been carried out with the help of the set
\mathcal{M}
of
2k\times 2k
quaternion (if
J
has been symmetric) or real (if
J
has been self-dual) matrices
A
. The different matrix spaces come into play as we need the property
A^{*}=\hat{T}A\hat{T}
to guarantee an integrable Gaussian in
A
where the completion of squares, especially the shift
A\to A+i\sqrt{\frac{2}{d_{N}}}\xi^{T}T\psi\hat{T}
, works out.

The integrals over
\xi
and
\psi
are again a Pfaffian determinant,

\begin{split}&\int[d\xi][d\psi]\exp\left[i\sqrt{\frac{d_{N}}{2}}\left({\rm Tr}\,A\hat{T}\psi^{T}T\xi+{\rm Tr}\,A^{T}\xi^{T}T\psi\hat{T}\right)+{\rm Tr}\,\xi^{T}T\xi\tilde{z}\hat{T}+{\rm Tr}\,\psi^{T}T\psi\tilde{w}\hat{T}\right]\\
=&{\rm Pf}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}T\otimes\tilde{z}\hat{T}&\displaystyle i\sqrt{\frac{d_{N}}{2}}\,T\otimes\hat{T}A^{T}\\
\displaystyle i\sqrt{\frac{d_{N}}{2}}\,T\otimes A\hat{T}&T\otimes\tilde{w}\hat{T}\end{array}\right]=\det\left[\tilde{z}\tilde{w}+\frac{d_{N}}{2}A^{\dagger}\tilde{w}^{-1}A\tilde{w}\right]^{d_{N}/2}.\end{split}

(4.30)

The square root of the determinant is chosen such that the sign agrees with the Pfaffian. The root is actually not a problem for the class AII
†
since
d_{N}=2N
is even so that no root is taken. In contrast, for class AI
†

=&\frac{\pi^{2k^{2}}}{\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}[(2j+1)!]^{2}}{\rm Pf}\left[(b-a)\int_{0}^{\infty}ds_{1}s_{1}^{a+b-2}e^{-s_{1}^{2}}\right]_{a,b=1}^{2k}.\end{split}

(4.56)

The remaining integral in the Pfaffian determinant gives
\Gamma[(a+b-1)/2]/2
. After multiplying this result with
(2\pi)^{k/2}
originating from (

) we arrive at (
4.37
).

:
Like before we start from

\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2k\times 2k}}[dA]\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}\,(A^{\dagger}A)^{2}\right]=&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2k\times 2k}}[dA]\exp\left[-{\rm Tr}\,(A^{\dagger}A)\right]\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2k\times 2k}}[dA]\exp\left[-{\rm Tr}\,(A^{\dagger}A)^{2}/2\right]}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2k\times 2k}}[dA]\exp\left[-{\rm Tr}\,(A^{\dagger}A)\right]}\\

(4.57)

where the singular value decomposition is now

with
U,V\in{\rm O}(2k)
and

. The integral in the denominator is another special case of the Selberg integral in
[

, Eq. (17.6.5)]
and the numerator can be dealt with the identity

]

|\Delta_{2k}(S)|=\Delta_{2k}(S){\rm Pf}[{\rm sign}(s_{b}-s_{a})]_{a,b=1,\ldots,2k}

with

. Applying

]

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2k\times 2k}}[dA]\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}\,(A^{\dagger}A)^{2}\right]\\
=&\frac{\pi^{2k^{2}}(2k)!}{\prod_{j=0}^{2k-1}2\Gamma[(j+3)/2]\Gamma[(j+1)/2]/\sqrt{\pi}}{\rm Pf}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}ds_{1}ds_{2}\ {\rm sign}(s_{2}-s_{1})s_{1}^{a-3/2}s_{2}^{b-3/2}e^{-(s_{1}^{2}+s_{2}^{2})/2}\right]_{a,b=1}^{2k}.\end{split}

The product of Gamma-functions can be simplified by Legendre’s duplication formula by multiplying factors with odd and even

The integral in the Pfaffian is feasible when, first, ordering

and, then, going over to polar coordinates

We first substituted

in

The difference of this expression with
a
and

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2k\times 2k}}[dA]\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}\,(A^{\dagger}A)^{2}\right]=\frac{\pi^{2k^{2}}}{\prod_{l=0}^{k-1}2^{-4l}[(2l)!]^{2}}\\
&\hskip 28.45274pt\times{\rm Pf}\left[2^{-1/4}\Gamma\left[\frac{a+b-1}{2}\right]\left(\frac{2^{b/2}}{2a-1}\,{{}_{2}F_{1}}\left(\frac{2a-1}{4},\frac{5-2b}{4};\frac{2a+3}{4};\frac{1}{2}\right)-\{a\leftrightarrow b\}\right)\right]_{a,b=1}^{2k}.\end{split}

When pulling the factor

coming from (

).

5
Conclusions and open problems

We provided analytical results for three ensembles of non-Hermitian random matrices, the complex Ginibre ensemble (Cartan class A), complex symmetric matrices (Cartan class AI
†
) and complex self-dual random matrices (Cartan class AII
†
).
Choosing
expectation values of
k
pairs of conjugate characteristic polynomials as correlations functions, we proved that indeed all three ensembles lead to different local bulk and edge statistics for the complex eigenvalues. The explicit expressions for
k=1
pair in class AI
†
and AII
†
, see Proposition
2.1
, show a remarkably similar structure to the well known respective answer for the complex Ginibre ensemble
for finite-
N
, being given in terms of sums of truncated exponentials. This allowed us to derive asymptotic results in the local bulk and edge scaling limit, as well as for the global large-
N
limit, with different results in all three limits for each ensemble, cf., Propositions
3.1
and
3.3
and Theorem
3.2
.
In particular, the resulting properties at the edge are compatible with the expected behaviour of the spectral density based on an approximate Coulomb gas description with three different values of inverse temperature
\beta
.

For general
k
we have identified the effective Lagrangians in the local bulk (Theorem
4.1
) and in the edge scaling limit (Theorem
4.2
). In both limits the respective Lagrangians agree for all three classes. However, the integration domain differs for each ensemble. In the bulk it is given by the compact unitary, unitary symplectic and orthogonal group for class A, AI
†
and AII
†
, respectively. At the edge, integrals over complex, quaternion and real valued matrices result. Only in class A these group integrals can be performed for
k>1
and mapped to known results. Also here, from the known properties of these integrals it is clear that all three ensembles differ. We expect that these effective Lagrangians as well as Goldstone manifolds will be universal and should be shared with physical systems with the corresponding symmetries.
The effective Lagrangians (
4.4
) and (
4.5
) would then appear as the potential part of the corresponding non-linear
\sigma
-models.

Several open questions prevail for these three representatives of local bulk and edge statistics. Is it possible to access more detailed spectral information in the two classes AI
†
and AII
†
, despite the lack of a joint eigenvalue density?
Can we analytically prove universality in these two cases by deforming the Gaussian ensembles considered so far?
More generally, is it possible to prove that all other known non-Hermitian symmetry classes fall into these three classes, away form their symmetry axes and symmetry points?
We
plan
to come back to some of these questions in future work.

Appendix A
Differential equation for class AII
†

In this appendix, we derive a differential equation for the integral representation for the expected characteristic polynomials in class AII
†

We will also show how this could be employed to derive the edge scaling limit in this class.

From the above integral we obtain for

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial x}f_{N}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}dv\ e^{-v}\int_{-v}^{v}duN(8x+4v)\left(4x^{2}+4xv+u^{2}\right)^{N-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}dv\ e^{-v}\int_{-v}^{v}duN\left(4x+\frac{4x^{2}+4vx+u^{2}}{x}-\frac{u^{2}}{x}\right)\left(4x^{2}+4xv+u^{2}\right)^{N-1}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}dv\ e^{-v}\int_{-v}^{v}du\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial v}+\frac{N}{x}-\frac{u}{2x}\frac{\partial}{\partial u}\right)\left(4x^{2}+4xv+u^{2}\right)^{N}.

and

For the third term differentiated with respect to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}dv\ e^{-v}\left\{\left[-\frac{u}{2x}\left(4x^{2}+4xv+u^{2}\right)^{N}\right]_{-v}^{v}+\int_{-v}^{v}du\frac{1}{2x}\left(4x^{2}+4xv+u^{2}\right)^{N}\right\}

\displaystyle 0

see (
