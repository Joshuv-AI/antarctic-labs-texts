Effective and ineffective psychological adjustment in breast cancer patients before receiving neoadjuvant chemotherapy: insights from a cohort study
Authors: Majid Omari, Salima Kriya, Sara Nadi, Ghita Zerhouni, Chaymae El Abbas, Achraf El Asri, Zineb Benbrahim, Nawfel Mellas, Karima El Rhazi, Mohammed El Amine Ragala, Jaouad El Hilaly, Karima Halim, Lamiae Amaadour, Btissame Zarrouq
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe timing of a breast cancer (BC) diagnosis has a significant psychological impact on patients. However, it reported that those eligible for treatment regimens based on neoadjuvant chemotherapy may experience high levels of depression, anxiety and distress. To cope with this situation, patients deploy psychological coping strategies. The aim of this study is to explore effective and ineffective psychological adjustment mobilized by Moroccan patients newly diagnosed with BC and before receiving neoadjuvant chemotherapy, as well as to identify their associations to socio-demographic and clinical determinants.Materials and methodsA total of 209 patients were recruited in outpatient in oncology department in the public oncology hospital of Fez city, between 2021 and 2023. Ethics approval was obtained for this study and patients has signed an informed consent form. The questionnaire interview included socio-economic and clinical variables and the Arabic version of Mini-Mental Adjustment to Cancer (Mini-MAC) scale, which measures the strategies of mental adjustment to disease, as fighting spirit (FS), hopelessness/helplessness (HH), anxious preoccupations (AP), cognitive avoidance (CA), fatalism (FA), “maladaptive coping” and “adaptive coping”. Bivariate analysis of psychological adjustment strategies and sociodemographic and clinical variables was carried out using statistical tests according to the nature of the variables, to their normality and the homogeneity of their variances. Univariate and multivariate linear regression modelling was carried out using a forward “enter” regression procedure.ResultsA mean age of 47.43 ± 9.45 was found among participants. Assessment of mental adjustment to cancer using Mini-MAC scores revealed that psychological adjustment strategies with a higher mean score were FA, FS and “adaptive coping” strategies. Based mainly on multiple linear Regression, monthly family income was negatively associated with HH, and FS and positively linked to FA and “adaptive coping” strategies. Moreover, the left side as BC laterality was negatively associated with AP and “maladaptive coping” strategies. Furthermore, positive ER status was negatively associated with HH and “maladaptive coping” strategies and high Ki-67 levels were positively linked to AP.ConclusionPrograms to strengthen psychological adjustment in patients with BC candidates for NACT, taking into consideration the determinants found associated in this study, are of great importance since the first oncology consultation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-024-02211-9.Keywords: Psychological adjustment, Breast cancer, Neoadjuvant chemotherapy, Socio-demographic determinants, Clinical determinants

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) in Morocco is known as the most common type of cancer in women. Breast cancer (BC) in Morocco is the most common cancer. There are 11,747 new women diagnosed in 2020, and the incidence rate is 56.4 cases per 100,000 women. Around 630,000 BC deaths occur worldwide each year, and about 3700 deaths have been reported in Morocco in 2020 [1].

A recent study showed that advanced BC represents 45.8% among a total of 1973 patients recruited during the period 2008 to 2017 at two oncology centers in the city of Casablanca and the city of Rabat [2]. Regarding to the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) anatomic staging system which is widely considered as a reference for assessing the stage of BC at diagnosis, BC is generally considered advanced if it is classified as stage IIB or higher [3]. Among Advanced BC subtypes, locally advanced BC (LABC) occurs when the cancer does not have distant metastases, and it cannot be realistically resected surgically without neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) [4]. LABC represents in low and middle income countries, between 40% and 60% of all women suffering from BC [5–7], whereas in high income countries, its constitute just 4 to 8% of all cases [6].

In LABC, NACT is usually used to reduce the tumor size, followed by surgery, radiotherapy, and systemic therapy [5, 7–9]. By using NACT, surgical options can be improved and treatment responses assessed. NACT also provides a means of detecting chemotherapy-predictive markers [10]. However, NACT is sometimes recommended for some women with early stage BC, especially patients with stage II BC, due to special medical reasons [11, 12].

Nevertheless, it’s important to note that patients recently diagnosed with BC and considered as candidates for neoadjuvant chemotherapy face psychological shock and present various psychological alterations [13], including anxiety, fear, depression, psychological distress, as well as other family and social problems [14]. In this respect, several studies have examined these alterations, highlighting their impact. Researchers recently found that distress reached its peak before the start of NACT, and gradually decreased over the course of NACT [15]. According also to Taira’s study, 42% of participants suffered from distress at baseline [16]. Oh et al. [17] showed that depression prior to NACT administration accounted for 46%, and anxiety could affect 54% of patients.

From another angle, women adopt psychological adjustment strategies in response to LABC. The psychological effects of this diagnosis require women to cope with diagnosis, the symptoms and adapt to their new circumstances [18, 19]. Psychological adjustment strategies are used by patients in a variety of ways. Patients who were diagnosed at a different stage of BC use various coping strategies in their journey, as identified in a systematic review [20]. Among the most frequently employed strategies are seeking social support, adopting a positive outlook, reevaluating behaviors, practicing religion an improving spirituality, expressing emotions, avoidance [20]. In a qualitative study of 19 Iranian women recently diagnosed with BC and who had undergone unilateral mastectomy, interviews were conducted to explore their coping mechanisms in the face of the disease. The results revealed that the women mainly used a religious approach, a positive or negative attitude towards cancer, acceptance of the reality of their illness, social and cultural factors, and seeking support from others [21].

We consider it valuable to highlight that there is a research gap on studying psychological adjustment strategies in BC patients undergoing NACT. Really, we identified a this area a single study [22] which was conducted among 53 patients, and it revealed several important findings. First, the finding reported a significant decrease in “resigned coping” and increase in “social support coping” during NACT. Furthermore, before the start of treatment, “fighting spirit coping” was negatively associated to “resigned coping”, and was positively related to “social support”. Therefore, psychological adjustment is an important modulator of specific individual variability in terms of the psychosocial consequences and life quality following a diagnosis of BC, and can play an important role in patient observance and therapeutic benefit [23]. Psychological adjustment, or coping is defined as “the responses a person develops in order to overcome the adverse effects of events perceived as stressful” [24]. In the last few years, there has been a growing tendency to focus on understanding the psychological adjustment associated with cancer [25, 26]. It could be assessed through multiple cognitive, emotional, behavioral and psychological strategies [24, 27]. In the field of psycho-oncology, we note a considerable interest to consider the model proposed by Watson and colleagues, which categorizes psychological adjustment on five strategies (22). These strategies are assessed by the “Mental Adjustment to Cancer Scale” (MAC) and revolves around the fighting spirit (FA), anxious preoccupation (AP), helplessness/hopelessness (HH), Cognitive avoidance (CA), and fatalism (FA) [25]. The “maladaptive coping” strategy is derived from the HH and AP strategies, all of whom are considered to be an ineffective form of psychological adjustment. While the “adaptive coping” strategy consists of FS, CA and FA. However, these strategies are considered effective for psychological adjustment [28, 29].

In response to previous publications, including a recent systematic review [30], it has become essential to understand the psychological adjustment of BC patients, to conduct longitudinal studies to assess the dynamics of these strategies at each stage of patient management, and to examine related socio-demographic and clinical factors. To this end, we are conducting a cohort study of Moroccan BC patients receiving neoadjuvant chemotherapy.

However, in the present article, our objective consists to explore effective and ineffective psychological adjustment mobilized by women newly diagnosed with BC and before receiving neoadjuvant chemotherapy, as well as to identify their associations to socio-demographic and clinical determinants.

Materials and methods

Study design

Based on the baseline cohort, this study was a cross-sectional aimed to explore psychological adjustment strategies mobilized by women newly diagnosed with BC and before receiving NACT, and to identify socio-demographic and clinical determinants associated with each coping strategies.

A total of 209 patients were recruited in outpatient in oncology departments in the public oncology hospital of Fez city, between 2021 and 2023. Ethics approval was obtained for this cohort study by the hospital university ethics committee (N°24/18) associated to University of Sidi Mohamed Ben Abdellah. A specific authorization from the Director of the Hassan II Hospital Center has been received.

Inclusion criteria were: (i) women over 18 years of age; (ii) diagnosed as having BC, without distant metastases, and should begin selected to receive NACT; iii) has signed an informed written consent form. While, exclusion criteria were patients with psychiatric illnesses before to be diagnosed with BC; and those suffering from other malignant tumors.

Sociodemographic and clinical variables

Socio-demographic variables involved were age, educational status, marital status, employment status, having children, residency, ethnic origin, medical insurance, patient’s monthly income, and monthly family income.

For clinical variables, we included the laterality of BC, status of menopause, period since the discovery of the first symptoms, history of family cancer and the presence of other chronic illness. In addition, histological types, lymph node, size of tumor, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), level of Ki-67, the estrogen receptor (ER) and progesterone receptor (PR). For molecular types, luminal A (ER + and/or PR+, HER2-), luminal B (ER + and/or PR+, HER2+), triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), HER2 enriched (ER-, PR-, HER2+), and (ER-, PR-, HER2-) were involved.

Mini-MAC

Coping strategies for cancer were assessed using the Mini-Mental Adjustment to Cancer (Mini-MAC) [25, 31]. The original and brief version of the Mini Mental Adjustment to Cancer questionnaire, abbreviated Mini-MAC, consists of 29 items measuring five emerging coping strategies [32]. In the present study, we administered the Arabic version of the Mini-MAC scale, which has been validated and shown reliability among Moroccan breast cancer patients, in the same oncology department where our study took place [33]. To our knowledge, this Arabic version of the Mini-MAC is the only one that has been validated in Arab cancer patients. It comprises 24 items covering five different coping strategies used by patients with a cancer diagnosis [33]. It is a four-point Likert-type scale (from 1 “not at all” to 4 “completely”). The scale consists of five subscales for each meta-category: FS (4 items), HH (7 items), AP (7 items), CA (3 items) and FA (3 items) [33]. To get a score of “maladaptive coping”, we added the two mean scores of HH and AP, while “adaptive coping” is obtained by the sum of the FS, CA and FA [28, 29]. Higher scores indicate greater use of that particular type of coping strategy [32, 34]. This version of the Arabic Mini-MAC was used before the NACT was received by the participants in our study. The reliability and validity of the Arabic version of the Mini-MAC were established in a previous study [28]. In this study, all subscales had high internal consistency (HH (Cronbach’s α 0.94), AP (Cronbach’s α = 0.95), FS (Cronbach’s α = 0.91), CA (Cronbach’s α = 0.83), and FA (Cronbach’s α = 0.80)).

Procedure

the study participants were recruited using a convenience sampling technique. Data were collected individually for research purposes only and processed anonymously. After explaining the aims of this project and providing written consent before starting the investigation, all patients were asked to complete an informed consent form, which was attached to the measurement questionnaires to guarantee data anonymity.

Statistical analysis

EpiInfo version 7.2.6.0 was used to enter Data which was later analyzed statistically by SPSS version 25 to evaluate the overall information provided by the questionnaires, and make a comparison of the scores obtained. Skewness and kurtosis were used as indicators of a normal distribution of responses for subscale for each strategy [35]. A normal distribution was assessed using Kim’s rules [35] for medium size samples (50 < N < 300), where absolute value of Z was less than 3.29. Z-values are obtained by dividing the raw skewness and kurtosis scores by the standard error. As a result, AP and “maladaptive coping” variables followed a normal distribution, whereas the distribution of other variables deviated from a normal distribution. Regarding the homogeneity of variances, we used the Levene test to compare the standard deviations of the different quantitative variables between the groups. Thus, we selected to select the appropriate tests to interpret the results of the study.

The bivariate analysis of the HH, FS, AC, FA and adaptive coping strategy and the socio-demographic and clinical variables with two categories were carried out using the Mann Whitney test, while the Kruskal Wallis Test was used for variables with more than two categories. AP and “maladaptive coping” were investigated using several statistical tests such as independent samples t test, Welsh test, ANOVA test, and Kruskal-Wallis test. Previously, the Levene test was used to compare the standard deviations of the different quantitative variables between the groups. A 95% Confidence Interval was obtained with a significance level of 0.05. Both univariate and multivariate regression linear modeling were conducted, with selected determinants with p ≤ 0.2 in the bivariate tests, using a forward “enter” regression procedure. Variables with over two categories such as “monthly family income”, “molecular subtypes” and “period since the discovery of the first symptoms” were dummy-coded for this analysis.

It’s also worth noting that 15.6% of patients came from families with no income at all, while 71.7% of patients belonged to families with a monthly income of less than 250$. For HH, bivariate analysis showed significant associations with monthly family income (p = 0.001) as socio-demographic variables, and with other clinical variables like breast Tumor laterality (p = 0.03), lymph node status (p = 0.01) and ER (p = 0.01) (Supplementary file) Univariate linear regression (Supplementary file) confirmed the negative association of HH with monthly family income less than 250 $ (B= -0.73; 95% CI [-1.05;-0.41]; p ≤ 0.001), and with family income more than 250 $ (-0.72; 95% CI [-1.15;-0.28]; p = 0.001). Moreover, HH was associated with laterality of BC (B= -0.266; 95% CI [-0.50; -0.03]; p = 0.02), and positive lymph node status (B = 0.25, 95% CI [0.01;0.50]; p = 0.03). Univariate analysis suggests also that there is a significant negative relationship between HH and positive status of ER (B= -0.29; 95% CI [-0.53;-0.05]; p = 0.015). Inclusion of variable eligible for the final multivariate model (Supplementary file) showed that HH was negatively associate with monthly family income less than 250$ (B= -0.69; 95% CI [-1.01;-0.38]; p ≤ 0.001), and with family income more than 250$ (B= -0.67; 95% CI [-1.09; -0.24]; p = 0.002), in comparison with family without monthly income. This means that, on average, for every one unit increase in “monthly family income less than 250$”, the dependent variable HH is expected to decrease by 0.69 units, and that for every one unit increase in “monthly family income” more than 250$, the dependent variable HH is expected to decrease by 0.67 units. In addition, a negative relationship was found between HH and the positive status of ER (B= -0.40; 95% CI [-0.75; -0.06]; p = 0.02), suggesting that for each unit increase in positive status of ER, HH is expected to decrease by 0.40 units (Supplementary file).

Bivariate analysis showed that AP strategy was significantly associated with age (p = 0.01), BC laterality (p = 0.014), estrogen receptor ER (p = 0.003), progesterone receptor PR (p = 0.001), and molecular subtype (p = 0.005) (Supplementary file). For the univariate analysis, it revealed that the PA strategy was less adopted in patients over 50 than in younger patients (B= -0.34; 95% CI [-0.60; -0.08]; p = 0.01), in women with left-sided BC (B= -0.32; 95% CI [-0.57; -0.06]; p = 0.01) and those with another chronic disease (B= -0.35; 95% CI [-0.66; -0.03]; p = 0.02). In addition, positive ER status (B= -0.40; 95% CI [-0.66; -0.14]; p = 0.003) and positive PR (B= -0.42, 95% CI [-0.67; -0.16]; p = 0.001) were also associated with low PA levels (Supplementary file). While multivariate analysis showed that patients with left BC adopted the AP strategy less (B= -0.37; 95% CI [-0.64; -0.11]; p = 0.005), and those with a high KI67 (more than 20%) were more likely to adopt AP (B = 0.58; 95% CI [0.06;1.09]; p = 0.02) (Supplementary file).

For maladaptive coping, bivariate analysis (Supplementary file) showed significant association with age at diagnosis (p = 0.01), laterality of BC (p = 0.009), lymph node status (p = 0.03), ER (p = 0.002), PR (p = 0.007) and molecular subtype (p = 0.02). Including variables in simple linear analysis showed a significant association with age at diagnosis (B= -4.17; 95% CI [-7.65; -0.68]; p = 0.019); left laterality of BC (B = -4.56; 95% CI = [-7.94; -1.17]; p = 0.009), positive lymph node status (B = 3.89; 95% CI [0.38;7.39]; p = 0.03), positive ER (B= -5.48; 95% CI [-8.94; -2.03]; p = 0.002) and positive PR (B= -4.66; 95% CI [-8.06; -1.26]; p = 0.007) (Supplementary file). Multiple linear regression analysis revealed two significant associations. First, patients with left-sided BC were less likely to have high maladaptive coping strategy scores (B= -4.83; 95% CI [-8.37; -1.29]; p = 0.008) than patients with right-sided BC. Secondly, the maladaptive coping score was found to be lower in patients with the positive status of ER (B = -9.25; 95% CI [-18.33;-0.16]; p = 0.008) (Supplementary file).

As far as FS’s strategy is concerned, the bivariate analysis revealed that it was associated with Monthly family income (p ≤ 0.0001) (Supplementary file). The univariate analysis (Supplementary file) showed that the FS was significantly associated with monthly family income below 250$ compared to other income categories (B = 0.47; 95% CI [0.22; 0.73]; p < 0.001), and being employed (B = 0.30; 95% CI [0.02; 0.58]; p = 0.03) (Supplementary file). Multiple regression linear confirmed that patients with 250$ family incomes were more likely to adopt the FS strategy (B = 0.44; 95% CI [0.18; 0.70]; p = 0.01) (Supplementary file).

Concerning the CA, a single significant association was found in the bivariate analysis with the period since the discovery of the first symptoms to diagnosis (p = 0.03) (Supplementary Table S1 and Table S2). Following the results of the univariate analysis, a significant association was shown with patients’ residence in an urban area (B = 0.21; 95% CI [0.01; 0.41]; p = 0.04) (Supplementary file). However, the multiple linear regression model used found no significant association with the variables included in the model (Supplementary file).

Regarding FA, Monthly family income (p = 0.001) was only variables associated in bivariate analysis (Supplementary file). In simple linear regression, it showed that patients with monthly family income below 250$ (B = 0.22; 95% CI [0.09;0.35]; p = 0.001) and above 250$ (B = 0.27; 95% CI [0.09;0.45]; p = 0.003) were positively associated with the FA strategy compared with patients living in families with no income (Supplementary file). The multiple linear analysis confirms these results. it revealed therefor that family monthly income above 250$ was positively associated with FA strategy (B = 0.20; 95% CI [0.06;0.35]; p = 0.04), and that family monthly income above 250$ was also positively associated with FA strategy (B = 0.26; 95% CI [0.06;0.46]; p = 0.009) (Supplementary file).

Bivariate analysis (Supplementary file) showed that the monthly family income was related to “adaptive coping”, which was further confirmed by the simple linear regression. Thus, it was found that patients whose monthly family income was less than 250 $ were 4.00 more likely to adopt adaptive coping strategy (p = 0.001; 95% CI= [1.64;6.37]). In addition, patients living in families with more than 250 $ per month were 3.90 more likely to use adaptive coping (p = 0.017; 95% CI= [0.70; 7.11]) (Supplementary file). In the multivariate analysis, monthly family incomes of less than 250$ were also 3.84 times more likely to have a high adaptive coping score (p = 0.002; 95% CI= [1.47; 6.21]). In addition, patients with a monthly income more than 250$ were 3.92 likely to mobilize the Adaptive coping (p = 0.018; 95% CI= [0.67; 7.16]) (Supplementary file).

Discussion

In light of our ongoing cohort project looking at the psychological adjustment of BC patients during the neoadjuvant chemotherapy treatment period (NACT), this article presents a cross-sectional study describing psychological adjustment strategies in women with BC before starting NACT. We also aim to explore the socio-demographic and clinical factors associated with each psychological coping strategy that characterizes the first period of patient care. This period extends from the announcement of the diagnosis by gynecological doctors, which includes the first information about the treatment, to the first oncology consultation. During this consultation, patients have the opportunity to get more information about their BC and is a crucial time to help them make the decision about neoadjuvant chemotherapy. During this period, patients face high levels of distress, anxiety, and psychological distress [36]. Therefore, it is important to explore how they respond to these psychological challenges and how they mobilize psychological adjustment strategies to cope with these difficulties.

This study revealed that the coping strategies most commonly used by cancer patients were FS, FA and “adaptive coping”, while AP and HH were less common. The results are consistent with a prospective study investigating psychological adjustment in BC patients using MAC [37]. This study found that patients often use approaches such as combative spirit and acceptance at the time of BC diagnosis [37]. However, a study conducted in Iran found that Muslim women recently diagnosed with BC use similar strategies to cope with the diagnosis, including religious approaches and positive thinking in accepting the disease [19]. It is possible that the Moroccan patients in our study accept this period of diagnosis, and actively engage in coping with its consequences, as well as preparing for chemotherapy. The support and backing of their families at this stage could also contribute to increasing their level of inner strength and adaptability in the face of the disease. Given that the patients are of Muslim faith, we take into account the fact that their high level of religiosity could also help them to accept divine destiny more easily, and to take the initiative to overcome any difficulties encountered during this period. With this in mind, they are positively involved in carrying out all the necessary medical consultations, as well as biological and radiological examinations in preparation for neoadjuvant chemotherapy. The follow-up study of psychological coping strategies throughout our cohort project enables us to assess changes in each strategy at each stage of the patients’ journey, as well as to verify the factors that predict them.

In our study, based mainly on multiple linear Regression we found that HH strategy is influenced by two socio-demographic factors. We observed that monthly family income was a negative predictor of HH strategy, and was positively associated to effective strategies as FS, FA and “adaptive coping”. While patient income has no significant correlation of any strategies, bearing in mind that 89.0% of our population have no monthly income. As a result, a patient who is part of a family with a monthly income would have a better chance of dealing with the impact of diagnosis announcement and NACT plan. Furthermore, this shows that family financial support is beneficial for LABC patients to meet care-related expenses. As observed by Liamputtong and Suwankhong [38], we consider our patients, in many cases, were dependent on the income of their children, husbands or other members of the family. The level of family income also has a significant impact on coping [39]. The financial burden of BC patients and caregivers is eased when the family income is substantial [40], in turn, increased the self-efficacy for coping. A study of Qatari BC patients found a significant relationship between participants’ hopelessness scores and household income [41]. But other studies have also highlighted the involvement of patient’s income. For example, Zhang et al. [42] demonstrated a significant association between the level of hope and the monthly income of BC patients.

Our study also revealed that left-sided BC was negatively associated with the AP and “Maladaptive coping” strategy, while right-sided BC was positively related. Given the lack of similar results in the literature, we believe that BC side involvement is generally related to the right patient’s dominant hand. In this case, given that the use of the right hand is culturally privileged for professional and domestic activities, we consider that the involvement of the right breast by the tumor would have an overwhelming impact on the patient, particularly on psychological aspects. It should be noted that a study [43] of BC patients showed statistically significant evidence that women who underwent right-breast surgery had poorer indicators of sexual functioning than those who underwent left-breast surgery.

In terms of biological variables, our results indicated an association between a KI-67 level above 20 and ineffective strategies, notably, AP. Furthermore, we observed a negative relationship between positive ER status, and the HH strategy and “maladaptive coping”. To our knowledge, the literature has not dealt sufficiently with such an association between clinical variables of BC and coping strategies. However, a study attempted to study it, but found no tangible proof [44]. It is important to note that these variables are biological variables that determine a patient’s prognosis of BC. Accordingly, it is important that patients have access to more information about biological characteristics of BC, and to be better informed about the treatments planned for their care pathway, such as targeted chemotherapy and hormone therapy. As a result, they are less likely to resort to ineffective strategies such as HH and “maladaptive coping”. In principle, this explanation is supported by the results of a systematic review of coping strategies and clinical and socio-demographic variables in cancer [30]. Despite the explanations we have put forward in an attempt to understand these associations, we believe that the patients participating in our study did not benefit from sufficient information on BC prognostic variables due to their low level of education. Implementing information programs tailored to patients’ cognitive levels about the clinical features of BC, and their role in NACT decision-making will enable patients to reduce their use of ineffective coping strategies.

Finally, the present study had certain limitations. First, although we identified significant associations between psychological coping strategies, it is essential to note that this is a cross-sectional study from our cohort project. To further our understanding of psychological adjustment to advanced breast cancer, our cohort project merits completion. This will allow us to establish causal relationships between mental coping strategies and sociodemographic, clinical, and psychological variables, while assessing their evolution during the different phases of treatment. Secondly, this study is conducted in a public hospital attended by patients with low monthly incomes. Indeed, those with a profession generally choose the private sector for cancer treatment [45]. It follows that the present study population does not represent the Moroccan LABC patients as a whole, but rather is representative of women treated by public health authorities. It would be worth extending this study to other private cancer treatment centers. This would make it possible to generalize the results of the study to a larger population, and to make comparisons between patients treated in various public and private cancer treatment centers.

Conclusion

This cross-sectional study from the baseline results of cohort study drew significant conclusions regarding psychological adjustment strategies in breast cancer patients selected to receive neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT). These patients showed a tendency to mobilize effective strategies such as FS, FA and “adaptive coping” during this phase.

The results of the present study also showed that the impact of monthly family income on the adoption of effective strategies, as well as the role of clinical prognostic determinants of breast cancer, may lead patients to choose ineffective strategies. These associations need to be taken into account when implementing psychological interventions from the earliest oncology consultations. We therefore recommend that these interventions focus on reinforcing effective coping strategies to better prepare patients for neoadjuvant chemotherapy sessions.

However, it is important to emphasize that psychological adjustment remains dynamic and evolving. Consequently, we consider that our cohort project remains significant, relevant and worth pursuing in order to better understand patients’ choices of psychological adjustment strategies.
