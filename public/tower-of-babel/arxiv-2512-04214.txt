Phase mixing and the Vlasov equation in cosmology
Martin Taylor, Renato Velozo Ruiz

Abstract
We consider the Vlasov equation on slowly expanding isotropic homogeneous tori, described by the Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker cosmological spacetimes. For expansion rate t q t^{q} , with 0 < q < 1 2 0<q<\frac{1}{2} (excluding certain exceptional values), we show that the spatial density decays at the rate t − 6 ​ q t^{-6q} and that, when the spatial average is removed, the density decays at an enhanced rate due to a phase mixing effect. This enhancement is polynomial for Sobolev initial data and super-polynomial, but sub-exponential, for real analytic initial data. We further show that, when the expansion rate is the borderline t 1 2 t^{\frac{1}{2}} — the rate which describes a radiation filled universe — a degenerate phase mixing effect results in a logarithmic enhancement for Sobolev initial data and a super-logarithmic enhancement (in fact, a gain of exp ⁡ ( − μ ​ ( log ⁡ t ) ϵ ) \exp(-\mu(\log t)^{\epsilon}) for some μ , ϵ > 0 \mu,\epsilon>0 ) for analytic initial data. The proof is based on a collection of commuting vector fields, and certain combinatorial properties of an associated collection of differential operators. The vector fields are not explicit, but are shown to have good properties when t t is large with respect to the momentum support of the solution. A physical space dyadic localisation is employed to treat non-compactly supported (in particular, non-trivial real analytic) but suitably decaying solutions.

Introduction

Phase mixing effects for kinetic equations, and the phenomenon of Landau damping, have been famously exhibited in homogeneous settings in plasma physics
[
47
]
, and around inhomogeneous isolated systems in gravitational physics
[
49
,
50
]
. This article concerns a related mixing effect which occurs, in the gravitational context, in homogeneous cosmology. For more on the Vlasov equation in cosmology, see
[
21
,
22
,
23
,
61
,
69
]
. The main results of this article concern the Vlasov equation on a Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker (FLRW) family of spacetimes. Each member of this family describes a homogeneous, isotropic cosmology, evolving from a big bang singularity at
t=0
and expanding indefinitely as
t\to\infty
. We exhibit a phase mixing effect when the expansion rate is suitably slow.

1.1
The main results

For each given
q>0
, the FLRW spacetime
(\mathcal{M},g)
, with expansion rate
t^{q}
, is given by

\mathcal{M}=(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{T}^{3},\qquad g=-dt^{2}+t^{2q}\big((dx^{1})^{2}+(dx^{2})^{2}+(dx^{3})^{2}\big),

(1.1)

where
(x^{1},x^{2},x^{3})
denote Cartesian coordinates on
\mathbb{T}^{3}
. Each such spacetime describes a homogeneous isotropic universe.
The Vlasov equation on (
1.1
) takes the form

\partial_{t}f+\frac{p^{i}}{p^{0}}\partial_{x^{i}}f-\frac{2q}{t}p^{i}\partial_{p^{i}}f=0,\qquad p^{0}=\sqrt{1+t^{2q}|p|^{2}},

(1.2)

where
|p|^{2}=(p^{1})^{2}+(p^{2})^{2}+(p^{3})^{2}
. The summation convention is adopted throughout, whereby repeated Latin indices indicate a summation over
1,2,3
, and repeated Greek indices indicate a summation over
0,1,2,3
. For given
f\colon(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\to[0,\infty)
, the corresponding spatial density
\rho\colon(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{T}^{3}\to[0,\infty)
is defined by

\rho(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(t,x,p)dp.

Each solution of (
1.2
) describes an ensemble of collisionless, unit mass particles, evolving with respect to the fixed FLRW gravitational background (
1.1
).

1.1.1
Phase mixing on slowly expanding FLRW spacetimes

The first main result of this article concerns a mixing effect in solutions of (
1.2
) when
0<q<\frac{1}{2}
.

Theorem 1.1

(Phase mixing for the Vlasov equation on slowly expanding FLRW spacetimes)
.

Consider
0<q<\frac{1}{2}
such that
\frac{1}{2q}
is not an integer (i.e.
q\neq\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{6},\frac{1}{8},\ldots
),
k\geq 2
, and some
f_{1}\in H^{k}_{q}(\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
. Let
f
be the unique solution of equation (
1.2
) on
[1,\infty)\times\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
such that
f(1,x,p)=f_{1}(x,p)
. The spatial density of
f
satisfies, for all
t\geq 1
,

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{3}}\big|\rho(t,x)-\overline{\rho}(t)\big|\lesssim\frac{\|f_{1}\|_{H^{k}_{q}}}{t^{6q+k(1-2q)}},\qquad\text{where}\quad\overline{\rho}(t)=\frac{1}{t^{6q}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f_{1}(x,p)dpdx.

(1.3)

If
f_{1}
lies in the analytic space
f_{1}\in H^{\omega}_{q}(\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
(see Section
2.1.3
) then the spatial density satisfies, for all
t\geq 1
,

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{3}}\big|\rho(t,x)-\overline{\rho}(t)\big|\lesssim\frac{\|f_{1}\|_{H^{\omega}_{q}}}{t^{6q}}e^{-\mu\,t^{\frac{1-2q}{10}}},

(1.4)

for some
\mu>0
.

The spaces
H^{k}_{q}(\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
are
p
-weighted versions of the standard Sobolev spaces (with
p
weights depending on
q
), and
H^{\omega}_{q}(\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
is an associated space of analytic functions. The constant
\mu
is related to a radius of convergence for the analytic norm. See Section
2.1.3
below.

1.1.2
Degenerate phase mixing on the radiation FLRW spacetime

The spacetime (
1.1
) with
q=\frac{1}{2}
is known as the
radiation FLRW spacetime
. The second main result concerns a degenerate mixing effect for solutions of (
1.2
) when
q=\frac{1}{2}
.

Theorem 1.2

(Degenerate phase mixing for the Vlasov equation on the radiation FLRW spacetime)
.

Consider some
k\geq 2
and
f_{1}\in H^{k}_{\log}(\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
. Let
f
be the unique solution of equation (
1.2
), with
q=\frac{1}{2}
, on
[1,\infty)\times\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
such that
f(1,x,p)=f_{1}(x,p)
. The spatial density of
f
satisfies, for all
t\geq 1
,

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{3}}\big|\rho(t,x)-\overline{\rho}(t)\big|\lesssim\frac{\|f_{1}\|_{H^{k}_{\log}}}{t^{3}(\log(1+t))^{k}},\qquad\text{where}\quad\overline{\rho}(t)=\frac{1}{t^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f_{1}(x,p)dpdx.

(1.5)

If
f_{1}
lies in the analytic space
f_{1}\in H^{\omega}_{\log}(\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
then the spatial density satisfies, for all
t\geq 1
,

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{3}}\big|\rho(t,x)-\overline{\rho}(t)\big|\lesssim\frac{\|f_{1}\|_{H^{\omega}_{\log}}}{t^{3}}e^{-\mu(\log(1+t))^{\frac{1}{58}}},

(1.6)

for
\mu=(\lambda(f_{1})/2)^{\frac{1}{58}}
(with
\lambda(f_{1})
defined in Section
2.1.3
below).

The spaces
H^{k}_{\log}(\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
are, again,
p
-weighted versions of the standard Sobolev spaces and
H^{\omega}_{\log}(\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
is an associated space of analytic functions, in which every
\partial_{x}
derivative is now weighted by an additional
\log(2+|p|)
factor. See Section
2.1.3
below.

1.1.3
Remarks on the main theorems

See Figure
1
for an illustration of the phase mixing phenomena in the
q=0
(i.e. the standard, non-expanding) case. Particles initially at the origin, with momentum
p
, evolve to position
tp
after time
t
, and thus eventually, by periodicity, re-enter the picture on the left and right of the torus. The support of a solution
f
of (
1.2
), with
q=0
, which is initially localised around
\{x=0\}
will therefore “mix” in evolution, as depicted in Figure
1
. For general
q>0
, the vectors
t^{-q}\partial_{x^{i}}
and
t^{-q}\partial_{p^{i}}
, for
i=1,2,3
, have unit length and so particles initially at the origin, with momentum
p
, evolve to position
(t-1)\cdot t^{-q}p
at time
t
. Since the the torus is now expanding at rate
t^{q}
, a version of the above effect may occur if there are
t>1
such that
t^{q}\leq t^{1-q}
, i.e. if
q\leq\frac{1}{2}
. See Figure
2
.

For each
q>0
, (
1.1
) describes a cosmological spacetime which evolves from a big bang singularity as
t\to 0^{+}
— at which the Kretschmann scalar

R^{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}R_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}=\frac{12}{t^{4}}q^{2}(2q^{2}-2q+1),

blows up at rate
t^{-4}
— and expands indefinitely as
t\to\infty
. The spacetimes (
1.1
) arise as solutions of the Einstein equations

Ric(g)_{\mu\nu}-\frac{1}{2}R(g)g_{\mu\nu}=T_{\mu\nu},

(1.7)

for various matter models. Some notable examples include

•

Einstein–Euler:
\frac{1}{3}\leq q\leq\frac{2}{3}
;

•

Einstein–scalar field:
q=\frac{1}{3}
;

•

Einstein–nonlinear scalar field
q>0
;

•

Einstein–massless Vlasov:
q=\frac{1}{2}
;

•

Einstein–massless Boltzmann:
q=\frac{1}{2}
;

•

Einstein–massive Vlasov:
q\sim\frac{2}{3}
,

which are described in detail in Appendix
A
. Many of these examples fall within the range of

covered by Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.2
. In particular the case
q=\frac{1}{2}
, considered in Theorem
1.2
, describes a radiation filled universe and arises as a solution of Einstein–Euler with
radiation equation of state
and also of Einstein–massless Vlasov. Accordingly, the case

is typically used to describe the

of the early universe
[

,
45
]
.

Remark 1.3

(Decay due to expansion and the case
q>\frac{1}{2}
)
.

Theorem
1.1
in particular implies that
\rho(t,x)
decays at the rate
t^{-6q}
. This
t^{-6q}
decay rate is purely due to the expansion of the FLRW spacetime (
1.1
). It is the enhanced decay rate for for
\rho(t,x)-\overline{\rho}(t)
of Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.2
, which is due to the phase mixing effect discussed above. Though the mixing effect of Theorem
1.1
is not present in the case

, the
t^{-6q}
decay due to expansion remains present.

Remark 1.4

(The cases
q=\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{6},\frac{1}{8},\ldots
)
.

Theorem
1.1
contains the assumption that the expansion rate
q
is such that
\frac{1}{2q}
is not an integer (i.e.
q\neq\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{6},\frac{1}{8},\ldots
). This assumption is made for a technical reason in order to simplify the proof. Indeed, the proof of Theorem
1.1
relies on a collection of vector fields which are used to commute the Vlasov equation (
1.2
) (see the discussion in Section
1.2
below). The vector fields used are smooth (in fact real analytic) if
\frac{1}{2q}
is not an integer, but not in
C^{\frac{1}{2q}+1}
if
\frac{1}{2q}
is an integer. There is another collection of smooth vector fields (see Remark
3.16
) which can be used, in particular in these remaining cases, but, in order to simply the proof, these vector fields and these exceptional values of
q
are not considered here. The vector fields used do, however, give a proof of a version of (
1.3
) in these exceptional cases (provided (
2.6
) below is suitably defined) for
k\leq\frac{1}{2q}
.

Remark 1.10

(Non-relativistic limit)
.

Equation (
1.9
) arises as a non-relativistic analogue of equation (
1.2
). Indeed, in equation (
1.1
) the speed of light has been normalised to unity. Restoring the speed of light

\mathcal{M}=(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{T}^{3},\qquad g=-c^{2}dt^{2}+t^{2q}\big((dx^{1})^{2}+(dx^{2})^{2}+(dx^{3})^{2}\big).

The appropriately normalised
mass shell
takes the form

\mathcal{P}=\{(t,x,p)\in T\mathcal{M}\mid g(p,p)=-c^{2}\},

and thus the Vlasov equation, for functions
f\colon\mathcal{P}\to[0,\infty)
, takes the form

\partial_{t}f+\frac{p^{i}}{\sqrt{1+c^{-2}t^{2q}|p|^{2}}}\partial_{x^{i}}f-\frac{2q}{t}p^{i}\partial_{p^{i}}f=0.

Equation (
1.9
) thus arises in the formal limit
c\to\infty
.

The analogue of Theorem
1.1

1.9

For

Though it can be helpful for gaining intuition, this representation formula does not feature in the proof of Theorem

The main ingredients in the proof of Theorem

•

Conservation laws:

For any solution

•

Commutation vector fields:

) for each multi-index

•

•

1.2.3
The relativistic free transport equation

The main difference between the non-relativistic problems discussed in Section
1.2.1
and Section
1.2.2
and the problem (
1.2
) is the fact that the vector fields which commute with the equation (
1.2
) are more complicated than (
1.13
). This fact means that the derivative relation (
1.15
) is no longer so simple.

In this section, in order to illustrate some of these new difficulties in a considerably simpler setting, the relativistic free transport equation is considered (which is obtained by setting
q=0
in (
1.2
)). In order to make the notation easier to navigate, we restrict here to the case of one spatial dimension (though the proof, of course, generalises to higher dimensions). Thus, for
f\colon\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
, the equation takes the form

\partial_{t}f+\frac{p}{\sqrt{1+p^{2}}}\partial_{x}f=0,\qquad\rho(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(t,x,p)dp.

(1.24)

Theorem 1.15

(Phase mixing for the relativistic free transport equation)
.

Consider some
k\geq 2
and
f_{1}\in H_{\mathrm{exp}}^{k}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R})
. Let
f
be the unique solution of equation (
1.24
) on
[1,\infty)\times\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}
such that

t\geq 1
,

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{T}}\big|\rho(t,x)-\overline{\rho}\big|\lesssim\frac{\|f_{1}\|_{H^{k}_{\mathrm{exp}}}}{t^{k}},\qquad\text{where}\quad\overline{\rho}=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f_{1}(x,p)dpdx.

(1.25)

If
f_{1}
lies in the analytic space
f_{1}\in H_{\mathrm{exp}}^{\omega}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R})
then the spatial density satisfies, for all
t\geq 1
,

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{T}}\big|\rho(t,x)-\overline{\rho}(t)\big|\lesssim\|f_{1}\|_{H^{\omega}_{\mathrm{exp}}}e^{-\mu\,t^{\frac{1}{11}}},

(1.26)

for some
\mu>0
.

The spaces
H_{\mathrm{exp}}^{k}
and
H_{\mathrm{exp}}^{\omega}
are exponentially weighted versions of the standard Sobolev spaces. See Section
2.1.3
for definitions.

The main ingredients in the proof of Theorem
1.15
are the conservations laws (
1.12

1.14

1.15

•

The vector field

1.24
).

•

Derivative relation:

Spatial derivatives of
\rho

1.27

\partial_{x}\rho(t,x)=\frac{1}{t}\int Mf(t,x,p)+3p(1+p^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}f(t,x,p)dp.

Proposition 1.16

For each

(1.29)

Moreover

•

Each
C^{i}_{jkm}

0\leq C^{i}_{jkm}\leq(4i)!\qquad\text{for all}\quad j,k,m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

(1.30)

•

C^{i}_{jkm}\neq 0\qquad\Rightarrow\quad 0\leq j\leq i,\quad 0\leq k\leq i,\quad 0\leq m\leq 3i.

•

The non-vanishing
C^{i}_{jkm}

C^{i}_{jkm}\neq 0\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad j+m+2k\leq 2i.

In what follows a collection of vector fields

Define also, for
p\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
,

p^{I}=p^{i_{1}}p^{i_{2}}\ldots p^{i_{k}},

where

p^{(1,0,0)}=p^{1},\qquad p^{(0,1,0)}=p^{2},\qquad p^{(0,0,1)}=p^{3}.

For any function
h(t,x,p)
, define

\big(L+h(t,x,p)\,p\big)^{I}=\big(L^{i_{1}}+h(t,x,p)\,p^{i_{1}}\big)\big(L^{i_{2}}+h(t,x,p)\,p^{i_{2}}\big)\ldots\big(L^{i_{k}}+h(t,x,p)\,p^{i_{k}}\big).

For such
I
, define also
|I|=k
.

For
J=(j_{1},j_{2},j_{3})\in(\mathbb{N}_{0})^{3}
, define
|J|=j_{1}+j_{2}+j_{3}
and, for
p\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
,

p^{J}=(p^{1})^{j_{1}}(p^{2})^{j_{2}}(p^{3})^{j_{3}}.

Given a multi-index
K=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})
with
i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\in\{(1,0,0),(0,1,0),(0,0,1)\}

K-e_{i}=\begin{cases}(i_{2},\ldots,i_{k})&\text{if }i_{1}=e_{i}\text{ and }k\geq 2,\\

K+e_{i}=(e_{i},i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}).

If
0=(0,0,0)

(p^{1},p^{2},p^{3})^{0}=1,\qquad L^{0}=1.

(2.1)

2.2.1
Sobolev inequality

The first functional inequality is a standard
L^{\infty}
–
L^{2}
Sobolev inequality.

Proposition 2.1

(
L^{\infty}
–
L^{2}
Sobolev inequality)
.

For any smooth function
\phi\colon\mathbb{T}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
, and any
k\geq 2
,

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{3}}|\phi(x)-\overline{\phi}|\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\sum_{|I|=k}\|\partial_{x}^{I}\phi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{3})}.

Proof.

The function
\phi
can be Fourier decomposed

\phi(x)=\sum_{\xi\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}\hat{\phi}(\xi)e^{2\pi i\xi\cdot x},\qquad\hat{\phi}(\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\phi(x)e^{-2\pi i\xi\cdot x}dx,

and so

|\phi(x)-\overline{\phi}|\leq\sum_{|\xi|\neq 0}|\hat{\phi}(\xi)|\leq\Big(\sum_{|\xi|\neq 0}|\xi|^{-2k}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big(\sum_{\xi\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}|\xi|^{2k}|\hat{\phi}(\xi)|^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}=\Big(\sum_{|\xi|\neq 0}|\xi|^{-2k}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{|I|=k}\|\partial_{x}^{I}\phi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{3})},

by the Cauchy–Schwarz Inequality and the Plancherel Theorem. The proof follows from the fact that
|\xi|^{-2k}
is summable for
k\geq 2
and satisfies

\sum_{\xi\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}\smallsetminus\{0\}}|\xi|^{-2k}\lesssim\frac{1}{k}.

∎

2.2.2
Interpolation inequality

The next functional inequality is an interpolation inequality concerning the
L^{2}
norm of the momentum average of a function
h\colon\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
and certain weighted
L^{2}
norms of
h
.

Proposition 2.2

(Interpolation inequality)
.

For any suitably decaying function
h\colon\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}|\rho_{h}(x)|^{2}dx\lesssim\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|p|^{2}|h(x,p)|^{2}dpdx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|p|^{4}|h(x,p)|^{2}dpdx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

where

\rho_{h}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}h(x,p)dp.

Proof.

Note first that the integral

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{1}{|p|^{2}+|p|^{4}}dp,

is finite. Thus, for any suitably decaying function
h\colon\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}|\rho_{h}(x)|^{2}dx\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(|p|^{2}+|p|^{4})|h(x,p)|^{2}dpdx.

(2.3)

Defining now

h_{\lambda}(x,p)=h(x,\lambda p),

it follows that

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}|\rho_{h_{\lambda}}(x)|^{2}dx=\lambda^{-6}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}|\rho_{h}(x)|^{2}dx,\qquad\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|p|^{2}|h_{\lambda}(x,p)|^{2}dpdx=\lambda^{-5}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|p|^{2}|h(x,p)|^{2}dpdx,

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|p|^{4}|h_{\lambda}(x,p)|^{2}dpdx=\lambda^{-7}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|p|^{4}|h(x,p)|^{2}dpdx.

Applying (
2.3
) to
h_{\lambda}
then gives

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}|\rho_{h}(x)|^{2}dx\lesssim\lambda\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|p|^{2}|h(x,p)|^{2}dpdx+\lambda^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|p|^{4}|h(x,p)|^{2}dpdx.

The proof follows from setting

\lambda=\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|p|^{2}|h(x,p)|^{2}dpdx\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|p|^{4}|h(x,p)|^{2}dpdx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

∎

2.2.3
Combinatorial results

This section contains some combinatorial results which will be used in the proof of the main results.

Proposition 2.3

(Stirling’s formula)
.

There is a sequence
\{r_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
such that
r_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
and, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\frac{n!e^{n}}{n^{n}\sqrt{2\pi n}}=e^{r_{n}}.

Proof.

There are many proofs of this well known fact — see
[
60
]
, for example.
∎

Proposition 2.4

(Products of factorials and polynomials)
.

For any
n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
,

(n_{1}k)!(n_{2}k)!\leq((n_{1}+n_{2})k)!,

for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

Suppose, without loss of generality, that
n_{1}\leq n_{2}
. Then

\frac{((n_{1}+n_{2})k)!}{(n_{2}k)!}=(n_{2}k+1)\times(n_{2}k+2)\times\ldots\times(n_{2}k+k)\times(n_{2}k+k+1)\times\ldots\times(n_{2}k+n_{1}k)\geq(n_{1}k)!,

and the proof follows.
∎

Lemma 2.5

(Summation Lemma)
.

For any integer
k\geq 1
,

\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(1+j)^{k}e^{-j}\lesssim k!\cdot k^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Proof.

Note that the the function
f(x)=(1+x)^{k}e^{-x}
agrees with the summand whenever
x
is an integer, and has a global maximum at
x=k-1
. It follows that the summation is uniformly bounded by

\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(1+j)^{k}e^{-j}\lesssim\int_{0}^{k}(1+x)^{k}e^{-x}dx+\int_{k}^{\infty}x^{k}e^{1-x}dx.

Now Proposition
2.3
implies that

\int_{0}^{k}(1+x)^{k}e^{-x}dx\leq kk^{k}e^{1-k}\lesssim k!\cdot k^{\frac{1}{2}},

and, after integrating by parts
k
times,

\int_{k}^{\infty}x^{k}e^{-x}dx=k^{k}e^{-k}\sum_{l=0}^{k}\frac{k!}{(k-l)!k^{l}}\lesssim k!\cdot k^{\frac{1}{2}},

and the result follows.
∎

2.3
The functions
G_{q}
and
H_{q}

In this section, functions
G_{q}
and
H_{q}
are introduced, which will appear in the commutation vector fields introduced in Section
3
below. Various analyticity properties of these functions are also collected.

Consider
0<q<\frac{1}{2}
such that
\frac{1}{2q}
is not an integer (i.e.
q\neq\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{6},\frac{1}{8},\ldots
). Let
N_{q}
be the unique positive integer such that

\frac{1}{2(N_{q}+2)}<q<\frac{1}{2(N_{q}+1)}.

(2.4)

Define
G_{q}\colon[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
by

G_{q}(s)=\int_{0}^{s}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\tilde{s}^{4q}+\tilde{s}^{2q}}}d\tilde{s}+X_{q},

(2.5)

where

X_{q}=-\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{s^{4q}+s^{2q}}}ds+\sum_{n=0}^{N_{q}}\frac{a_{n}}{1-2q(n+1)}-\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{s^{2q}}\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+s^{-2q}}}-\sum_{n=0}^{N_{q}}a_{n}s^{-2qn}\Big)ds,

(2.6)

and

a_{0}=1,\qquad a_{n}=\frac{(-1)^{n}\prod_{l=1}^{n}(2l-1)}{2^{n}\,n!}\text{ for }n\geq 1.

The coefficients
a_{n}
have the property that

\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+x}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}x^{n},\qquad\text{for }|x|<1,

(2.7)

and so it follows that the integrals in (
2.6
) are convergent (note, in particular, that the property (
2.4
) of
N_{q}
implies that
2q(n+1)>1
for all
n\geq N_{q}+1
). This precise form of
X_{q}
is chosen so that
G_{q}(s)
takes the form (
2.11
) below, and thus admits the expansion (
2.12
).

Remark 2.6

(Explicit expressions for
G_{q}
)
.

For certain values of
q
the function
G_{q}
can be expressed explicitly. For example when
q=\frac{1}{3}
,
G_{q}
takes the explicit form

G_{\frac{1}{3}}(s)=3\sqrt{1+s^{\frac{2}{3}}},

(2.8)

and when
q=\frac{1}{4}
(a case otherwise not considered in the present work),
G_{q}
takes the explicit form

G_{\frac{1}{4}}(s)=X_{\frac{1}{4}}+2\sqrt{s+s^{\frac{1}{2}}}-\log\Big(1+2s^{\frac{1}{2}}+2\sqrt{s+s^{\frac{1}{2}}}\Big).

For general
q
, however,
G_{q}
cannot be expressed explicitly.

Define also
H_{q}\colon[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
by,

H_{q}(0)=\frac{1}{1-2q},\qquad H_{q}(s)=\frac{G_{q}(s^{-\frac{1}{2q}})}{s^{1-\frac{1}{2q}}}\text{ for }s\neq 0.

(2.9)

so that

H_{q}(s^{2})=\frac{G_{q}(s^{-\frac{1}{q}})}{s^{2-\frac{1}{q}}}=G_{q}(s^{-\frac{1}{q}})s^{\frac{1-2q}{q}}.

Proposition 2.7

(Analyticity of
H_{q}
)
.

Consider
0<q<\frac{1}{2}
such that
\frac{1}{2q}
is not an integer (i.e.
q\neq\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{6},\frac{1}{8},\ldots
). The function
H_{q}\colon[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
defined by (
2.9
) is real analytic (in fact,
H_{q}
extends to a real analytic function
H_{q}\colon(-1,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
). Moreover, for any
0\leq s<1
,

H_{q}(s)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}b_{n}s^{n},\qquad b_{0}=\frac{1}{1-2q},\qquad b_{n}=\frac{a_{n}}{1-2q(n+1)}\text{ for }n\geq 1.

(2.10)

Proof.

Note that
H_{q}
satisfies the ordinary differential equation

\frac{d}{ds}H_{q}(s)+\frac{2q-1}{2q}\frac{H_{q}(s)}{s}=-\frac{1}{2qs\sqrt{1+s}}.

It follows from the Cauchy–Kovalevskaya Theorem that
H_{q}
is real analytic on
(0,\infty)
. Suppose now that
0<s<1
. Note first that, for
s>1
, using the expression (
2.6
) for
X_{q}
,

G_{q}(s)=\sum_{n=0}^{N_{q}}\frac{a_{n}s^{1-2q(n+1)}}{1-2q(n+1)}-\int_{s}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\tilde{s}^{2q}}\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\tilde{s}^{-2q}}}-\sum_{n=0}^{N_{q}}a_{n}\tilde{s}^{-2qn}\Big)d\tilde{s},

(2.11)

where the fact that that the property (
2.4
) implies that
2q(n+1)<1
for all
0\leq n\leq N_{q}
has been used. It follows that, for
0<s<1
,

H_{q}(s)=\frac{G_{q}(s^{-\frac{1}{2q}})}{s^{1-\frac{1}{2q}}}=\sum_{n=0}^{N_{q}}\frac{a_{n}s^{n}}{1-2q(n+1)}-\frac{1}{s^{1-\frac{1}{2q}}}\int_{s^{-\frac{1}{2q}}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\tilde{s}^{2q}}\bigg[\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\tilde{s}^{-2q}}}-\sum_{n=0}^{N_{q}}a_{n}\tilde{s}^{-2qn}\bigg]d\tilde{s},

Consider some
N\geq N_{q}
. The property (
2.7
) implies that

\bigg|H_{q}(s)-\sum_{n=0}^{N}b_{n}s^{n}\bigg|\leq\frac{s^{N+1}}{2q(N+2)-1},\qquad b_{0}=\frac{1}{1-2q},\qquad b_{n}=\frac{a_{n}}{1-2q(n+1)}\text{ for }n\geq 1.

Thus, letting
N\to\infty
, the expansion (
2.10
) holds for any
0\leq s<1
and so
H_{q}
is analytic around
0
(in fact,
H_{q}
extends to a real analytic function
H_{q}\colon(-1,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
).
∎

Remark 2.8

(Expansion for
G_{q}
)
.

It follows from the proof of Proposition
2.7
that
G_{q}
admits the expansion, for large
s
,

G_{q}(s)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{a_{n}s^{1-2q(n+1)}}{1-2q(n+1)}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}b_{n}s^{1-2(n+1)q},\qquad\text{for }s>1.

(2.12)

The following proposition is used later when relating combinations of vector fields at
t=1
to the standard
\partial_{x}
and
\partial_{p}
derivatives appearing in the spaces
H^{k}_{q}
.

Proposition 2.9

(
H_{q}
for large
|p|
)
.

There are real analytic functions
\psi_{q},\phi_{q}\colon(-1,1)\to\mathbb{R}
such that, for all
|p|>1
,

H_{q}(|p|^{2})=X_{q}|p|^{\frac{1-2q}{q}}+\psi_{q}(|p|^{-1}),\qquad 2H_{q}^{\prime}(|p|^{2})=Y_{q}|p|^{\frac{1-4q}{q}}+\phi_{q}(|p|^{-1}),

where
Y_{q}=\frac{1-2q}{q}X_{q}
and

\psi_{q}(s)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}s^{2n+1},\qquad\phi_{q}(s)=-\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(2n+1)c_{n}s^{2n+3},\qquad c_{n}=\frac{a_{n}}{1+(2n-1)q},\quad\text{ for }n\geq 0.

(2.13)

Proof.

Recall that

G_{q}(s)=X_{q}+\int_{0}^{s}\frac{1}{\tilde{s}^{q}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\tilde{s}^{2q}}}d\tilde{s},

and, recalling the expansion (
2.7
), it follows as in the proof of Proposition
2.7
that
G_{q}
admits the expansion, for small
s
,

G_{q}(s)=X_{q}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}s^{1+(2n-1)q},\qquad\text{ for }|s|<1,

with
c_{n}
defined by (
2.13
). Thus

H_{q}(s^{2})=X_{q}s^{\frac{1-2q}{q}}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}s^{-(2n+1)},\qquad\text{ for }|s|<1,

and the proof for
H_{q}
follows. Differentiating gives

2sH_{q}^{\prime}(s^{2})=\frac{1-2q}{q}X_{q}s^{\frac{1-3q}{q}}-\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(2n+1)c_{n}s^{-(2n+2)},\qquad\text{ for }|s|<1,

and the proof for
2H_{q}^{\prime}
also follows.
∎

The following proposition contains various functions, defined in terms of
H_{q}
, which also appear in the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Proposition 2.10

(Analyticity of
1/H_{q}
,
B_{q}/H_{q}
, and
\Phi_{q}
)
.

There exists
\delta>0
such that the functions

\frac{1}{H_{q}},\,\frac{1}{H_{q}^{\prime}},\,\frac{B_{q}}{H_{q}},\,\Phi_{q}\colon[0,\delta)\to\mathbb{R},

where the latter two are defined by

B_{q}(s)=\frac{2H_{q}^{\prime}(s)}{2sH_{q}^{\prime}(s)+H_{q}(s)},\qquad\Phi_{q}(s)=-\frac{2}{H_{q}(s)}\Big(\frac{H_{q}^{\prime}(s)}{H_{q}(s)}-sB_{q}(s)\frac{H_{q}^{\prime}(s)}{H_{q}(s)}+sB_{q}^{\prime}(s)+2B_{q}(s)\Big),

(2.14)

are well defined and real analytic (or, rather, extend to an real analytic functions on
(-\delta,\delta)
).

Proof.

Note that
H_{q}^{\prime}
is real analytic and

H_{q}^{\prime}(s)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(n+1)b_{n+1}s^{n}.

Since
H_{q}(0)=\frac{1}{1-2q}
and
H_{q}^{\prime}(0)=b_{1}=-\frac{1}{2(1-4q)}
, it follows that there exists
\delta>0
such that
H_{q}(s)\neq 0
and
H_{q}^{\prime}(s)\neq 0
for all
(-\delta,\delta)
. The fact that
\frac{1}{H_{q}}
,
\frac{1}{H_{q}^{\prime}}
,
\frac{B_{q}}{H_{q}}
, and
\Phi_{q}
are real analytic on

then follows from standard theory of analytic functions. Moreover, the coefficients of the corresponding power series expansions around

can be expressed in terms of
\{b_{n}\}
. See, for example, the textbook of Krantz–Parks
[
46
]
.
∎

2.4
Conservation laws

In this section some conservation laws satisfied by solutions of (
1.2
) are given.
The first conservation law concerns the spatial average of
\rho
.

Proposition 2.11

(Conservation law for spatial average of
\rho
)
.

For any
q>0
, if
f
solves (
1.2
), then the average of
\rho
satisfies the conservation law

t^{6q}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\rho(t,x)dx=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f_{1}(x,p)dpdx,

for all
t\in(0,\infty)
, where
f_{1}(x,p)=f(1,x,p)
.

Proof.

After integrating equation (
1.2
) with respect to both
x
and
p
, the second term on the left hand side vanishes and the third term can be integrated by parts in
p
to give

\partial_{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(t,x,p)dpdx+\frac{6q}{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(t,x,p)dpdx=0,

from which the result follows.
∎

It follows from Proposition
2.11
that, for any solution
f
of (
1.2
),

\overline{\rho}(t)=\frac{1}{t^{6q}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f_{1}(x,p)dpdx.

The next conservation law concerns
p
-weighted
L^{2}
norms of solutions.

Proposition 2.12

(Weighted
L^{2}
conservation law for
f
)
.

For any
q>0
, if
f
solves (
1.2
), then, for any
s\geq 0
,

t^{2q(s+3)}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|p|^{s}|f(t,x,p)|^{2}dpdx=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|p|^{s}|f_{1}(x,p)|^{2}dpdx,

for all
t\in(0,\infty)
, where
f_{1}(x,p)=f(1,x,p)
.

Proof.

A simple computation gives

\partial_{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|p|^{s}|f(t,x,p)|^{2}dpdx=-2\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|p|^{s}f\Big(\frac{p^{i}}{p^{0}}\partial_{x^{i}}f-\frac{2q}{t}p^{i}\partial_{p^{i}}f\Big)dpdx\\
=-\frac{2q(s+3)}{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|p|^{s}|f(t,x,p)|^{2}dpdx,

from which the result immediately follows.

\displaystyle=G_{q}\big(t\,|t^{2q}p|^{-\frac{1}{q}}\big)|t^{2q}p|^{\frac{1-2q}{q}}\bigg(\delta_{k}^{i}+\frac{1-2q}{q}\frac{t^{4q}p^{i}p^{k}}{|t^{2q}p|^{2}}\bigg)-\frac{1}{q}\frac{t^{1-q}}{\sqrt{t^{2q}+|t^{2q}p|^{2}}}\frac{t^{4q}p^{i}p^{k}}{|t^{2q}p|^{2}}

(3.38)

\displaystyle=t^{1-2q}\Big[H_{q}\big(t^{-2q}|t^{2q}p|^{2}\big)\delta_{k}^{i}+2t^{-2q}H_{q}^{\prime}(t^{-2q}|t^{2q}p|^{2})t^{4q}p^{i}p^{k}\Big].

Note that, for
q=\frac{1}{3}
, the function
G_{\frac{1}{3}}
is explicit (see (
2.8
)) and the vector fields (
3.37
)–(
3.38
) reduce to the vector fields (
3.4
)–(
3.5
) introduced in Section

. Unlike in the previous sections, however, the vector fields (

)–(
3.38
) are only considered when

is sufficiently large and the behaviour of Proposition
2.10
can be utilised. For the remaining region, the standard coordinate derivatives
\partial_{x^{k}}
are used.

Proposition 3.14

(Commuting vector fields for equation (
1.2
))
.

The vector fields
L_{k}
, for
k=1,2,3
, defined by (
3.37
)–(
3.38
), satisfy

\Big[\partial_{t}+\frac{p^{i}}{\sqrt{1+t^{2q}|p|^{2}}}\partial_{x^{i}}-\frac{2q}{t}p^{i}\partial_{p^{i}},L_{k}\Big]=0,\qquad k=1,2,3.

Moreover, the standard coordinate derivatives
\partial_{x^{k}}
also satisfy

\Big[\partial_{t}+\frac{p^{i}}{\sqrt{1+t^{2q}|p|^{2}}}\partial_{x^{i}}-\frac{2q}{t}p^{i}\partial_{p^{i}},\partial_{x^{k}}\Big]=0,\qquad k=1,2,3.

Proof.

The proof is a direct computation.

Proposition 3.15

(Inverse of the matrix
A
)
.

There is a constant
\upsilon>1
such that, if
t^{q}\geq\upsilon|t^{2q}p|
, the matrix
{A_{k}}^{i}(t,p)
, defined by (
3.38
), is invertible. The inverse takes the form

\displaystyle{(A^{-1})_{k}}^{i}(t,p)=\

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t^{1-2q}}\frac{1}{H_{q}(t^{-2q}|t^{2q}p|^{2})}\Big(\delta_{k}^{i}-t^{-2q}B_{q}(t^{-2q}|t^{2q}p|^{2})t^{4q}p^{k}p^{i}\Big)\qquad i,k=1,2,3,

where
B_{q}
is as in Proposition
2.10
.

Proof.

Note that if

{A_{k}}^{i}(t,r)=h(t,r)\Big(\delta_{k}^{i}+\frac{r^{i}r^{k}}{b(t,r)-|r|^{2}}\Big),

for some functions
h(t,r)
,

, then

{(A^{-1})_{k}}^{i}(t,p)=\frac{1}{h(t,r)}\Big(\delta_{k}^{i}-\frac{r^{i}r^{k}}{b(t,r)}\Big),\qquad i,k=1,2,3.

The proof follows from setting

h(t,r)=t^{1-2q}H_{q}\big(t^{-2q}|t^{2q}p|^{2}\big),\qquad b(t,r)=|r|^{2}+\frac{t^{2q}H_{q}\big(t^{-2q}|r|^{2}\big)}{2H_{q}^{\prime}\big(t^{-2q}|r|^{2}\big)},

noting that

\frac{1}{b(t,r)}=t^{-2q}B_{q}(t^{-2q}|t^{2q}p|^{2}),

and recalling (see Proposition
2.10
) that
1/H_{q}
and
B_{q}/H_{q}
are well defined (in fact real analytic) on
(-\upsilon^{-2},\upsilon^{-2})
if
\upsilon
is sufficiently large.
∎

3.3.2
A binomial theorem for the commutation vector fields

Consider some
t\geq 1
and
p\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
such that
t^{q}\geq\upsilon|t^{2q}p|
, where
\upsilon>1
is as in Proposition
3.15
. Define

r=t^{2q}p.

and define the operators

M_{i}=t^{1-2q}{(A^{-1})_{i}}^{k}(t,p)L_{k}=\frac{1}{H_{q}(t^{-2q}|r|^{2})}\Big(\delta^{k}_{i}-t^{-2q}B_{q}(t^{-2q}|r|^{2})r^{k}r^{i}\Big)L_{k},\qquad i=1,2,3.

A computation gives

\frac{t^{1-2q}}{t^{2q}}\partial_{p^{i}}{(A^{-1})_{i}}^{k}=\Phi_{q}(t^{-2q}|r|^{2})t^{-2q}r^{k},

where
\Phi_{q}\colon(-\upsilon^{-2},\upsilon^{-2})\to\mathbb{R}
is defined by (
2.14
).
As will be seen in the proof of Proposition
3.18
below, it follows that, for any multi-index
I
,

(t^{1-2q}\partial_{x})^{I}\rho(t,x)=\int\big(M+\Phi_{q}(t^{-2q}|r|^{2})\,t^{-2q}\,r\big)^{I}f(t,x,p)dp.

The following proposition is a Binomial Theorem-type result which relates the operator appearing on the right side to combinations of the commutation vector fields.

Proposition 3.17

(A binomial theorem for the commutation vector fields)
.

If
\upsilon
is sufficiently large then, for each multi-index
|I|\geq 1
, there are
d^{I}_{JKl}\in\mathbb{R}
, for
l\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and multi-indices
K
, and
J\in(\mathbb{N}_{0})^{3}
, such that, for all
t^{q}\geq\upsilon|r|
,

\big(M+\Phi_{q}(t^{-2q}|r|^{2})\,t^{-2q}\,r\big)^{I}=\sum_{K,l}\sum_{|J|=0}^{\infty}d^{I}_{JKl}t^{-lq}(t^{-q}r)^{J}L^{K}.

(3.39)

Moreover

•

There are constants
C,\tilde{C}>0
such that each
d^{I}_{JKl}
satisfies

|d^{I}_{JKl}|\lesssim\tilde{C}^{|I|}C^{|J|}(1+|J|)^{4|I|}\qquad\text{for all}\quad l\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\quad J\in(\mathbb{N}_{0})^{3},\quad\text{and multi-indices }K.

(3.40)

•

The non-vanishing
d^{I}_{JKl}
satisfy

\displaystyle d^{I}_{JKl}\neq 0\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad

\displaystyle 0\leq|K|\leq|I|,\quad 0\leq l\leq|I|.

(3.41)

Proof.

By Proposition
2.10
,
1/H_{q}
,
B_{q}/H_{q}
and
\Phi_{q}
are real analytic on
(-\upsilon^{-2},\upsilon^{-2})
and thus there are
h_{N},b_{N},\phi_{N}\in\mathbb{R}
, for each
N\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3}
,
satisfying

|h_{N}|\lesssim C^{|N|},\qquad|b_{N}|\lesssim C^{|N|},\qquad|\phi_{N}|\lesssim C^{|N|},

(3.42)

for some constant
C>0
, such that

\frac{1}{H_{q}(t^{-2q}|r|^{2})}=\sum_{|N|=0}^{\infty}h_{N}(t^{-q}r)^{N},\quad\frac{B_{q}(t^{-2q}|r|^{2})}{H_{q}(t^{-2q}|r|^{2})}=\sum_{|N|=0}^{\infty}b_{N}(t^{-q}r)^{N},\quad\Phi_{q}(t^{-2q}|r|^{2})=\sum_{|N|=0}^{\infty}\phi_{N}(t^{-q}r)^{N},

for all
t^{q}>\upsilon|r|
. It follows that (
3.39
) holds for
I=e_{i}
with

d^{e_{i}}_{J,0,e_{k}}=h_{J}\delta^{i}_{k},\qquad d^{e_{i}}_{J+e_{j_{1}}+e_{j_{2}},0,e_{k}}=-b_{J}\delta^{i}_{j_{1}}\delta_{k,j_{2}},\qquad d^{e_{i}}_{J+e_{j},1,0}=\phi_{J}\delta^{i}_{j},\qquad d^{I}_{JKl}=0\text{ otherwise}.

The estimates (
3.40
), for
I=e_{i}
, follow from (
3.42
).
Suppose now that (
3.39
) holds for some
|I|\geq 1
for
d^{I}_{JKl}
satisfying (
3.40
)–(
3.41
). Then

\displaystyle\big(M+\Phi_{q}(t^{-2q}|r|^{2})\,t^{-2q}\,r\big)^{I+e_{i}}=\sum_{K,l}\sum_{|J|=0}^{\infty}d^{I}_{JKl}\Big[t^{-(l+1)q}\sum_{|N|=0}^{\infty}h_{N}j_{i}(t^{-q}r)^{J+N-e_{i}}L^{K}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-|J|t^{-(l+1)q}\sum_{|N|=0}^{\infty}b_{N}(t^{-q}r)^{J+N+e_{i}}L^{K}+t^{-lq}\sum_{|N|=0}^{\infty}h_{N}j_{i}(t^{-q}r)^{J+N}L^{K+e_{i}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-t^{-lq}\sum_{c=1}^{3}\sum_{|N|=0}^{\infty}b_{N}(t^{-q}r)^{J+N+e_{i}+e_{c}}L^{K+e_{c}}+t^{-(l+1)q}\sum_{|N|=0}^{\infty}\phi_{N}(t^{-q}r)^{J+N+e_{i}}L^{K}\Big],

and so
(M+\Phi_{q}(t^{-2q}|r|^{2})t^{-2q}r)^{I+e_{i}}
takes the form (
3.39
) with

\displaystyle d^{I+e_{i}}_{JKl}=\

\displaystyle(j_{i}+1)\sum_{J^{\prime}=0}^{J+e_{i}}d^{I}_{J^{\prime},K,l-1}h_{J-J^{\prime}+e_{i}}-(|J|-1)\sum_{J^{\prime}=0}^{J-e_{i}}d^{I}_{J^{\prime},K,l-1}h_{J-J^{\prime}-e_{i}}+\sum_{J^{\prime}=0}^{J}d^{I}_{J^{\prime},K-e_{i},l}h_{J-J^{\prime}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{c=1}^{3}\sum_{J^{\prime}=0}^{J-e_{i}-e_{c}}d^{I}_{J^{\prime},K-e_{c},l}b_{J-J^{\prime}-e_{i}-e_{c}}+\sum_{J^{\prime}=0}^{J-e_{i}}d^{I}_{J^{\prime},K,l-1}\phi_{J-J^{\prime}-e_{i}},

where
\sum_{J^{\prime}=0}^{J}
is interpreted component-wise, and
\sum_{J^{\prime}=0}^{J}=0
if
j_{c}<j_{c}^{\prime}
for some
c=1,2,3
. Moreover the inductive assumption (
3.40
), together with the estimates (
3.42
), implies that

\displaystyle|d^{I+e_{i}}_{JKl}|

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{C}^{|I|}\Big(2(1+|J|)\sum_{J^{\prime}=0}^{J+e_{i}}(1+|J^{\prime}|)^{4|I|}C^{|J^{\prime}|}C^{|J|-|J^{\prime}|+1}+5\sum_{J^{\prime}=0}^{J}(1+|J^{\prime}|)^{4|I|}C^{|J^{\prime}|}C^{|J|-|J^{\prime}|}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{C}^{|I|}C^{|J|}(1+|J|)^{4|I|}\big(2C(1+|J|)^{4}+5(1+|J|)^{3}\Big)\leq\tilde{C}^{|I|+1}C^{|J|}(1+|J|)^{4(|I|+1)},

where the latter follows if
\tilde{C}\geq 2C+5
. Thus
d^{I+e_{i}}_{JKl}
satisfies (
3.40
). Finally, (
3.41
) again follows by induction from the above recursion.

∎

3.3.3
Dyadic decomposition of
f

Let
\chi\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a smooth cut off function satisfying
|\chi|\leq 1
,

\chi(z)=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }|z|\leq\frac{1}{2},\\
0&\text{ if }|z|\geq 1,\end{cases}

and, for all
k\geq 0
,

\sup_{z}|\partial_{z}^{k}\chi(z)|\lesssim(2k)!,

(3.43)

and define, for each
n\geq 1
, and
z\geq 0
,

\chi_{n}(z)=\chi\Big(\frac{z}{2^{n+1}}\Big)-\chi\Big(\frac{z}{2^{n}}\Big),\qquad\chi_{0}(z)=\chi\Big(\frac{z}{2}\Big).

Note that,

\mathrm{supp}(\chi_{0})\subset\{|z|\leq 2\},

and, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
z\geq 0
,

\mathrm{supp}(\chi_{n})\subset\{2^{n-1}\leq|z|\leq 2^{n+1}\},\qquad\sum_{m=0}^{n}\chi_{m}(z)=\chi\Big(\frac{z}{2^{n+1}}\Big)

Define then

f^{n}(t,x,p)=f(t,x,p)\,\chi_{n}\big(|t^{2q}p|\big).

Note that each
f^{n}
solves the Vlasov equation (
1.2
), and, for
n\geq 1
,

\mathrm{supp}(f^{n})\subset\{(t,x,p)\mid 2^{n-1}\leq|t^{2q}p|\leq 2^{n+1}\},\qquad\mathrm{supp}(f^{0})\subset\{(t,x,p)\mid 0\leq|t^{2q}p|\leq 2\}.

(3.44)

Define also the corresponding average,

\rho^{n}(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f^{n}(t,x,p)dp,

and initial condition

f^{n}_{1}(x,p)=f^{n}(1,x,p)=f_{1}(x,p)\,\chi_{n}\big(|p|\big).

Note that

\sum_{n=0}^{N}\rho^{n}\to\rho,\qquad\text{uniformly as }N\to\infty.

3.3.4
Derivative relations

For each
n
, the
\partial_{x}
derivatives of
\rho^{n}
can be related to the above vector fields applied to
f^{n}
.

Proposition 3.18

(Derivatives of
\rho
and vector fields)
.

For
\upsilon
sufficiently large, there is a constant
C>1
such that, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, for all
k\geq 0
, and for any
t^{q}\geq\upsilon\,2^{n+1}
,

\sum_{|I|=k}\big|\partial_{x}^{I}\rho^{n}(t,x)|\lesssim\frac{C^{k}(4k)!}{t^{(1-2q)k}}\sum_{|I|=0}^{k}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|L^{I}f^{n}(t,x,p)|dp.

Proof.

From the assumption that
t^{q}\geq\upsilon\,2^{n+1}
, and the support property (
3.44
), it follows that
t^{q}\geq\upsilon\,|r|
in
\mathrm{supp}(f^{n})
. The results of Proposition
3.17
therefore apply. Now, for any function
g
and any
i=1,2,3
,

\displaystyle t^{1-2q}\partial_{x^{i}}\int g(t,x,p)dp

\displaystyle=\int t^{1-2q}{(A^{-1})_{i}}^{j}L_{j}g(t,x,p)-t^{1-4q}{(A^{-1})_{i}}^{j}\partial_{p^{j}}g(t,x,p)dp

\displaystyle=\int\Big(t^{1-2q}{(A^{-1})_{i}}^{j}L_{j}+t^{1-4q}\partial_{p^{j}}{(A^{-1})_{i}}^{j}\Big)g(t,x,p)dp

\displaystyle=\int\Big(M_{i}+\Phi_{q}(t^{-2q}|r|^{2})\,t^{-2q}\,r^{i}\Big)g(t,x,p)dp.

Applying the above repeatedly, it follows that, for any multi-index
I
,

(t^{1-2q}\partial_{x})^{I}\rho^{n}(t,x)=\int\big(M+\eta(t,r)\,r\big)^{I}f^{n}(t,x,p)dp.

Proposition
3.17
implies that

\displaystyle\sum_{|I|=k}\big|\big(M+\eta(t,r)\,r\big)^{I}f^{n}\big|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{K,l}\sum_{|J|=0}^{\infty}|d^{I}_{JKl}|t^{-lq}\upsilon^{-|J|}|L^{K}f^{n}|

\displaystyle\leq k\,\tilde{C}^{k}\sum_{|K|\leq k}|L^{K}f^{n}|\sum_{|J|=0}^{\infty}(C/\upsilon)^{|J|}(1+|J|)^{4|I|}\lesssim C^{k}(4k)!\sum_{|K|\leq k}|L^{K}f^{n}|,

(3.45)

where the latter follows from Lemma
2.5
, provided
C/\upsilon\leq e^{-1}
, and the proof follows.
∎

3.3.5
Vector fields at
t=1

The main result of this section is the following proposition, which relates
L^{2}
norms of the vector fields applied to
f^{n}
, at
t=1
, to the
H^{k}_{q}
norms of
f_{1}
(see Section
2.1.3
).

Proposition 3.19

(Vector fields at
t=1
)
.

There is a constant
C>1
such that, for any
k\geq 0
, and any
n\geq 0
,

\sum_{|I|\leq k}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(|p|^{2}+|p|^{4})\big|L^{I}(f^{n}(t,x,p))|_{t=1}\big|^{2}dp\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
\lesssim C^{k}(5k)!\sum_{|I|+|J|\leq k}\Big(\int_{2^{n-1}\leq|p|\leq 2^{n+1}}(|p|^{2}+|p|^{4})(1+|p|)^{|I|(\frac{1-2q}{q})}\big|\partial_{x}^{I}\partial_{p}^{J}f_{1}(x,p)\big|^{2}dp\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

The main parts of the proof of Proposition
3.19
is divided into the following two lemmas. The first relates
L^{I}|_{t=1}
to
\partial_{x}
and
\partial_{p}
on compact sets.

Lemma 3.20

(
L^{I}|_{t=1}
on compact sets)
.

For any
p_{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, there exists
\epsilon>0
,
B,C>0
, and constants
d^{I}_{JMN}\in\mathbb{R}
, satisfying

|d^{I}_{JNM}|\leq C^{|J|}(B+|J|)^{2|I|}|I|!,

(3.46)

such that

L^{I}|_{t=1}=\sum_{|N|+|M|\leq|I|}\sum_{|J|=0}^{\infty}(p-p_{*})^{J}d^{I}_{JNM}\partial_{x}^{N}\partial_{p}^{M},\qquad\text{for}\qquad|p-p_{*}|<\epsilon.

(3.47)

Proof.

By Proposition
2.7
the matrix components
{A_{k}}^{i}(1,p)
are real analytic functions, and thus, for each
p_{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, there is a
C>0
,
\epsilon>0
such that

{A_{k}}^{i}(1,p)=\sum_{|J|=0}^{\infty}a^{ki}_{J}(p-p_{*})^{J},

where
a^{ki}_{J}
satisfy

|a^{ki}_{J}|\lesssim C^{|J|},

for all
J
. It follows that, for
I=e_{i}
,
L_{i}|_{t=1}
has the form (
3.47
) with

d^{e_{i}}_{J,e_{k},0}=a^{ki}_{J},\qquad d^{e_{i}}_{J,0,e_{l}}=\delta_{l}^{i},\qquad d^{e_{i}}_{JKL}=0\quad\text{otherwise}.

Suppose now that
L^{I}|_{t=1}
takes the form (
3.47
) for some
|I|\geq 1
, with
d^{I}_{JKL}
satisfying

|d^{I}_{JNM}|\leq C^{|J|}(B+|J|)^{2|I|}\frac{(|I|+|J|)!}{|J|!},

(3.48)

for all
J
,
N
,
M
. Then

\displaystyle L_{i}L^{I}|_{t=1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{|J|=0}^{\infty}\sum_{|N|+|M|\leq|I|}d^{I}_{JKM}\Big(a_{J^{\prime}}^{i,k}(p-p_{*})^{J+J^{\prime}}\partial_{x}^{N+e_{k}}\partial_{p}^{M}+j_{i}(p-p_{*})^{J-e_{i}}\partial_{x}^{N}\partial_{p}^{M}+(p-p_{*})^{J}\partial_{x}^{N}\partial_{p}^{M+e_{i}}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{|J|=0}^{\infty}\sum_{|N|+|M|\leq|I|}(p-p_{*})^{J}\Big(\sum_{J^{\prime}=0}^{J}a_{J-J^{\prime}}^{i,k}d^{I}_{J^{\prime},N-e_{k},M}+(j_{i}+1)d^{I}_{J+e_{i},N,M}+d^{I}_{J,N,M-e_{i}}\Big)\partial_{x}^{N}\partial_{p}^{M},

where
0\leq J^{\prime}\leq J
is interpreted component wise. Thus (
3.47
) holds for
I+e_{i}
, with

d^{I+e_{i}}_{J,N,M}=\sum_{J^{\prime}=0}^{J}a_{J-J^{\prime}}^{i,k}d^{I}_{J^{\prime},N-e_{k},M}+(j_{i}+1)d^{I}_{J+e_{i},N,M}+d^{I}_{J,N,M-e_{i}}.

Suppose now inductively that, for some
I
, the estimate (
3.48
) holds for all
J,K,L
. Then

\displaystyle|d^{I+e_{i}}_{J,N,M}|

\displaystyle\leq(B+|J|)^{2|I|}\Big(\sum_{J^{\prime}=0}^{J}C^{|J|-|J^{\prime}|}C^{|J^{\prime}|}\frac{(|I|+|J^{\prime}|)!}{|J^{\prime}|!}+(|J|+1)C^{|J|+1}\frac{(|I|+|J|+1)!}{(|J|+1)!}+C^{|J|}\frac{(|I|+|J|)!}{|J|!}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq(B+|J|)^{2|I|}C^{|J|}\frac{(|I|+1+|J|)!}{|J|!}\Big(\frac{(|J|+1)^{3}}{|I|+1+|J|}+C+\frac{1}{|I|+1+|J|}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq(B+|J|)^{2(|I|+1)}C^{|J|}\frac{(|I|+1+|J|)!}{|J|!},

where the latter inequality holds provided
B\geq C+2
, and the fact that

\frac{(|I|+|J^{\prime}|)!}{|J^{\prime}|!}\leq\frac{(|I|+|J|)!}{|J|!},

for each
0\leq|J^{\prime}|\leq|J|
has been used in the previous step. Thus (
3.48
) holds for
I+e_{i}
, and hence for all
I
by induction. Now (
3.48
) implies that (
3.46
) holds, for new constants. Indeed, for all
I,J
,

\frac{(|I|+|J|)!}{|J|!}=|I|!\frac{(|I|+|J|)!}{|I|!|J|!}=|I|!\binom{|I|+|J|}{|I|}\leq 2^{|I|+|J|}|I|!.

∎

The next lemma relates
L^{I}|_{t=1}
to
\partial_{x}
and
\partial_{p}
for large

.

Lemma 3.21

(
L^{I}|_{t=1}
for large
p
)
.

For any
R>1
and all
p\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
with
|p|\geq R
, there exists
C>0
, and constants
d^{I}_{klJMN}\in\mathbb{R}
, satisfying

|d^{I}_{klJNM}|\leq C^{k+|I|}(1+k)^{|I|}|I|!,

(3.49)

and

d^{I}_{klJNM}\neq 0\quad\Rightarrow\quad|J|\leq k,\quad l\leq|N|,\quad|N|+|M|\leq|I|,\quad|J|\leq 2|I|,

(3.50)

such that

L^{I}|_{t=1}=\sum_{l,J,N,M}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}p^{J}d^{I}_{klJNM}|p|^{l(\frac{1-2q}{q})-k}\partial_{x}^{N}\partial_{p}^{M}.

(3.51)

Proof.

By Proposition
2.9
there are
h_{k}
and

Appendix A
FLRW solutions of the Einstein equations

There are many examples of matter models for which the Einstein equations (
1.7
) admit solutions of the form (
1.1
) — often with
q
in the range covered by Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.2
. Some notable examples are listed here.

Einstein–Euler:
\frac{1}{3}\leq q\leq\frac{2}{3}

The Euler equations on a given spacetime
(\mathcal{M},g)
concern a fluid four velocity — a vector field
u\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathcal{M})
, normalised so that
g_{\mu\nu}u^{\mu}u^{\nu}=-1
— and fluid pressure and density
p,\rho:\mathcal{M}\to[0,\infty)
. The pressure and density are typically related by an equation of state, which we assume here to be linear:

p=c_{s}^{2}\rho,

(A.1)

for some
c_{s}^{2}\in[0,1]
. The constant
c_{s}
is known as the
speed of sound
. The Euler equations result from equating to zero the spacetime divergence of the energy momentum tensor

T^{\mu\nu}=(\rho+p)u^{\mu}u^{\nu}+pg^{\mu\nu}.

(A.2)

The
Einstein–Euler system
— namely the Euler equations coupled to the Einstein equations (
1.7
) with this definition of
T
— admits the FLRW solution,

g=-dt^{2}+t^{\frac{4}{3\gamma}}\big((dx^{1})^{2}+(dx^{2})^{2}+(dx^{3})^{2}\big),\qquad u^{\mu}=\delta^{\mu}_{0},\qquad\rho=\frac{4}{3\gamma^{2}}t^{-2},

(A.3)

on
\mathcal{M}=(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{T}^{3}
, where
\gamma=1+c_{s}^{2}\in[1,2]
.

Einstein–scalar field:
q=\frac{1}{3}

The Einstein–scalar field system on a spacetime
(\mathcal{M},g)
consists of the scalar wave equation

\Box_{g}\psi=0,

(A.4)

coupled to the Einstein equations (
1.7
) via

T_{\mu\nu}=\partial_{\mu}\psi\partial_{\nu}\psi-\frac{1}{2}g_{\mu\nu}g^{\alpha\beta}\partial_{\alpha}\psi\partial_{\beta}\psi.

(A.5)

The system admits an FLRW solution of the form

g=-dt^{2}+t^{\frac{2}{3}}\big((dx^{1})^{2}+(dx^{2})^{2}+(dx^{3})^{2}\big),\qquad\psi=\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}\log t.

(A.6)

The Einstein–scalar field system arises, as an appropriate special case, from the above Einstein–Euler system when the fluid is
irrotational
, and
stiff
, so that
c_{s}=1
.

Einstein–nonlinear scalar field:
q>0

Given
V\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, consider the scalar field equation with potential on a given spacetime
(\mathcal{M},g)
,

\Box_{g}\psi-V^{\prime}(\psi)=0.

(A.7)

Define

T_{\mu\nu}[\psi]=\partial_{\mu}\psi\partial_{\nu}\psi-\Big(\frac{1}{2}g^{\alpha\beta}\partial_{\alpha}\psi\partial_{\beta}\psi+V(\psi)\Big)g_{\mu\nu}.

(A.8)

Note that
T
is divergence free if
\psi
satisfies (
A.7
). The Einstein–nonlinear scalar field system then consists of the Einstein equations (
1.7
) coupled to (
A.7
). Given
q>0
, if
V
takes the exponential form

V(\psi)=q(3q-1)e^{-\psi\sqrt{\frac{2}{q}}},

(A.9)

then system admits an FLRW solution of the form

g=-dt^{2}+t^{2q}\big((dx^{1})^{2}+(dx^{2})^{2}+(dx^{3})^{2}\big),\qquad\psi=\sqrt{2q}\log t.

(A.10)

Such solutions are often known as
power law inflationary
spacetimes. See, for example,
[
34
,
38
,
56
,
12
]
. Note that, when
q=\frac{1}{3}
, the potential (
A.9
) vanishes, equation (
A.7
)–(
A.8
) reduces to the previous (
A.4
)–(
A.5
), and the solution (
A.10
) is equal to the previous solution (
A.6
).

Einstein–massless Vlasov:
q=\frac{1}{2}

For a fixed particle mass
m\geq 0
, the Einstein–Vlasov system consists of the Vlasov equation

p^{0}\partial_{t}f+p^{i}\partial_{x^{i}}f-p^{\mu}p^{\nu}\Gamma_{\mu\nu}^{i}\partial_{p^{i}}f=0,\qquad g_{\mu\nu}p^{\mu}p^{\nu}=-m^{2},\qquad\Gamma_{\mu\nu}^{\alpha}=\frac{g^{\alpha\beta}}{2}\big(\partial_{\mu}g_{\beta\nu}+\partial_{\nu}g_{\mu\beta}-\partial_{\beta}g_{\mu\nu}\big),

(A.11)

for
f\colon P\to[0,\infty)
, where
P=\{(t,x,p)\in T\mathcal{M}\mid g(p,p)=-m^{2}\}
, coupled to the Einstein equations (
1.7
) with

T^{\mu\nu}(t,x)=\int_{P_{(t,x)}}f(t,x,p)p^{\mu}p^{\nu}\frac{\sqrt{-\det g}}{-p_{0}}dp^{1}dp^{2}dp^{3},

(A.12)

When the particle mass
m=0
, the system is known as the Einstein–massless Vlasov system. In this case, there is an infinite dimensional family of explicit FLRW solutions, where the metric
g
takes the form (
1.1
) with
q=\frac{1}{2}
.
Indeed, for any smooth, sufficiently decaying function
F:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to[0,\infty)
satisfying the integral conditions

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}F(p)p^{i}dp=0,\quad i=1,2,3,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}F(p)\frac{p^{i}p^{j}}{|p|}dp=\frac{\varrho}{3}\delta^{ij},\quad i,j=1,2,3.

such that
F\not\equiv 0
, the metric and particle density

g=-dt^{2}+a(t)^{2}\big((dx^{1})^{2}+(dx^{2})^{2}+(dx^{3})^{2}\big),\qquad f(t,x,p)=F(a(t)^{2}p),

where

a(t)=t^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{4\varrho}{3}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}},\qquad\varrho=\int|p|F(p)dp,

solve the Einstein–massless Vlasov system.

Einstein–massless Boltzmann:
q=\frac{1}{2}

The Einstein–massless Boltzmann system consists of the Boltzmann equation

p^{0}\partial_{t}f+p^{i}\partial_{x^{i}}f-p^{\mu}p^{\nu}\Gamma_{\mu\nu}^{i}\partial_{p^{i}}f=Q(f,f),\qquad g_{\mu\nu}p^{\mu}p^{\nu}=0,
