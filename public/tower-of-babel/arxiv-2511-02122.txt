Matrix Sensing with Kernel Optimal Loss: Robustness and Optimization Landscape
Xinyuan Song, Jiaye Teng, Ziye Ma

Abstract
In this paper we study how the choice of loss functions of non-convex optimization problems affects their robustness and optimization landscape, through the study of noisy matrix sensing. In traditional regression tasks, mean squared error (MSE) loss is a common choice, but it can be unreliable for non-Gaussian or heavy-tailed noise. To address this issue, we adopt a robust loss based on nonparametric regression, which uses a kernel-based estimate of the residual density and maximizes the estimated log-likelihood. This robust formulation coincides with the MSE loss under Gaussian errors but remains stable under more general settings. We further examine how this robust loss reshapes the optimization landscape by analyzing the upper-bound of restricted isometry property (RIP) constants for spurious local minima to disappear. Through theoretical and empirical analysis, we show that this new loss excels at handling large noise and remains robust across diverse noise distributions. This work offers initial insights into enhancing the robustness of machine learning tasks through simply changing the loss, guided by an intuitive and broadly applicable analytical framework.

Introduction

Noisy low-rank matrix optimization arises in numerous settings, including matrix sensing
Recht et al. 2010
, matrix completion
Candès and Recht 2009
, and robust PCA
Candès et al. 2011
. In practical applications such as recommender systems
Koren et al. 2009
, motion detection
Fattahi and Sojoudi 2020
;
Anderson and Sojoudi 2019
, phase synchronization/retrieval
Singer 2011
;
Boumal 2016
;
Shechtman et al. 2015
, and power system estimation
Zhang et al. 2017
, one often encounters a positive semidefinite matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
of rank at most
r
, along with a noise vector
w\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
that can be drawn from an unknown distribution. A standard formulation of this problem is

\min_{M\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}}f(M,w)\quad\text{subject to}\quad\operatorname{rank}(M)\leq r,\quad M\succeq 0,

(1)

where
f
represents a loss function evaluated at
(M,w)
. When the goal is to recover or estimate
M
, the noise
w
is typically unknown and potentially unbounded, coming from processes such as mechanical failures, inclement weather, or cyber-intrusions
Jin et al. 2019a
.

A widely used option for
f
is the mean squared error (MSE) loss:

f(M)=\tfrac{1}{2}\|\mathcal{A}(M)-\tilde{b}\|_{F}^{2},

(2)

where
\|\cdot\|_{F}
is the Frobenius norm and
\mathcal{A}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a linear operator defined by
\mathcal{A}(M)=\bigl(\langle A_{1},M\rangle,\ldots,\langle A_{m},M\rangle\bigr)^{\top}
. The vector
\tilde{b}
is often modeled as
\tilde{b}=\mathcal{A}(M^{*})+w
, where
M^{*}
is the ground truth matrix. Although the MSE objective is convenient and effective when the noise is Gaussian, it can be unreliable if the noise distribution is heavier-tailed, contaminated by outliers, or generally non-Gaussian
Barron 2019
;
Ghosh et al. 2017
;
Hampel et al. 1986
;
Huber 1981
.

Because
M
is required to be low-rank and positive semidefinite, many approaches employ the Burer–Monteiro (BM) factorization
Burer and Monteiro 2003
, writing
M=XX^{\top}
for some
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
. The constrained problem in (
1
) then transforms to an unconstrained form:

\min_{X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}}f\bigl(XX^{\top},w\bigr),

(3)

which is known to be non-convex. An active research direction
Li et al. 2020
;
Park et al. 2018
;
Ge et al. 2017
;
Ma et al. 2023
studies the landscape of (
3
), seeking to characterize local minima, global minima, and convergence rates of local search methods.

While MSE-based losses have been central to many analyses, they are sensitive to large deviations. Robustness to noise is a fundamental property for good loss function used in high-dimensional statistical estimation, especially in settings where the data may contain outliers or heavy-tailed noise distributions. To accommodate unknown or non-Gaussian errors, we consider a robust formulation that draws on nonparametric regression. In nonparametric regression
Breiman 2017
;
Breiman 2001
;
Cheng 1984
;
Devroye et al. 1994
;
Nadaraya 1964
;
Watson 1964
;
Hall and Huang 2001
;
Fan and Gijbels 2018
;
Schumaker 2007
;
Berlinet and Thomas-Agnan 2011
;
Lv et al. 2018
, one estimates an unknown function
g
given i.i.d. samples
\{(\mathbf{X}_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
. Rather than assuming a specific distribution for the noise, we adopt a kernel-based density estimator
\hat{f}(\cdot)
, and maximize the estimated log-likelihood:

\hat{g}=\underset{g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}}{\arg\max}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\hat{f}\bigl(Y_{i}-g(\mathbf{X}_{i})\bigr).

(4)

The loss proposed by
Wang et al. 2023
satisfies Lipchitz continuity, and preserves flexibility by not specifying the error distribution in advance. When the noise is in fact Gaussian,
\hat{g}
is effectively the same as the estimator from the MSE loss. However, it remains robust in more general or adversarial settings. When
w_{i}
deviates from a normal distribution or has heterogeneous variances, we provide a detailed loss study on the optimization landascape of the new robust loss (
4
).

In this work, we apply (
4
) to the BM factorized formulation (
3
) and analyze its optimization landscape and influence of random corruption, focusing on how the restricted isometry property (RIP) constants influence the global recovery of ground truth under noise. In particular, we investigate the behavior of this robust loss under both large and small
\delta
-RIP scenarios, and we study theoretical convergence for local search methods. Finally, we present numerical experiments on matrix recovery under several noise distributions, illustrating that this robust method can stabilize estimation errors and improve convergence.

Our main contributions are as follows: 1) we offer a theoretical analysis of the recovery guarantees for
M^{*}
and convergence behavior under the kernel optimal loss (
4
) in the context of the BM factorization. The theoretical results indicate that for BM factorized matrix sensing, the new loss (
4
) could augment robustness of the problem under heavy-tailed and varying noise distributions. 2) We demonstrate that when the underlying noise distribution is Gaussian, the robust loss aligns with MSE-based approaches, thereby retaining efficiency and better property. 3) We illustrate through empirical results that the new loss achieves accurate recovery and stable convergence, under highly corrupted settings.

Preliminaries

2.1
Proposed Kernel Loss

We estimate
\hat{g}
with the new proposed kernel loss function:

\hat{g}=\arg\min_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\widehat{R}_{n}(g)=\arg\min_{g\in\mathcal{G}}n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(-\log\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}K_{h}\big(Y_{j}-g(X_{j}),Y_{i}-g(X_{i})\big)\right).

(5)

This loss function is based on the log-likelihood formulation of a kernel density estimator using a kernel function
K_{h}
, applied to the residuals. Unlike the mean squared error (MSE) loss, which lacks adaptability to varying noise distributions, the proposed loss function provides robustness by accommodating different noise settings. This characteristic ensures that the estimator remains statistically reliable in the large-sample regime. The proposed
\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{n}(g)
involves a tuning parameter
h
. We use exponential kernel for this
K_{h}
, and we use
\mathcal{A}(XX^{\top})
and we get the loss function
\hat{g}

\hat{g}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(-\log\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\exp\left(-\frac{((Y_{j}-(\mathcal{A}(XX^{\top})_{j}))-(Y_{i}-\mathcal{A}(XX^{\top})_{i}))^{2}}{h^{2}}\right)\right).

(6)

Then problem (
1
) has a new formula:

\displaystyle\min_{M\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}}

\displaystyle\hat{g}(M,w)\ \ \text{s.t.}\mathrm{rank}(M)\leq r,M\succeq 0,

(7)

\displaystyle\min_{X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}}\hat{g}(XX^{\top},w)

Here
\hat{g}
is our proposed new kernel loss function.

2.2
RIP conditions

This line of work usually assumes the Restricted Isometry Property (RIP) for the problem, which is defined below:

(1-\delta_{p})\|X\|_{F}^{2}\leq\|\mathcal{A}(X)\|_{F}^{2}\leq(1+\delta_{p})\|X\|_{F}^{2},

(8)

here the
\|\cdot\|_{F}
denotes the Frobenius norm,
\mathcal{A}
is the linear operator, and
\delta_{p}
is the RIP-constant, which is usually simplified as
\delta
in the following content.

2.3
Lemmas on noise

Assumption 2.1
.

The noise

has a finite influence on the gradient and Hessian of the objective function in the sense that there exist two constants

and
\zeta_{2}\geq 0

\bigl|\langle\nabla_{M}f(M,w)-\nabla_{M}f(M,0),K\rangle\bigr|\leq\zeta_{1}\|w\|_{2}\|K\|_{F},

\bigl|\bigl[\nabla_{M}^{2}f(M,w)-\nabla_{M}^{2}f(M,0)\bigr](K,L)\bigr|\leq\zeta_{2}\|w\|_{2}\|K\|_{F}\|L\|_{F},

\|\langle\nabla_{X}\hat{g}(X+w)-\nabla_{X}\hat{g}(X),K\rangle\|

\|\langle\nabla^{2}_{X}\hat{g}\bigl(X+w\bigr)-\nabla^{2}_{X}\hat{g}\bigl(X\bigr),(K,L)\rangle\|

\|X\|,\|K\|,\|L\|,\text{and}\|w\|
. In addition to Assumption

,

, and

.

)

with respect to the first argument

is

restricted Lipschitz continuous, meaning that:

for all matrices

The proof is provided in Section
C
.
Lemma
2.2
establishes that the gradient of the function

with respect to the variable

Lemma 2.3
.

Gradient Lipschitz Continuity with Respect to Noise:

\|\nabla_{M}\hat{g}(M,w_{1})-\nabla_{M}\hat{g}(M,w_{2})\|_{F}\leq\lambda_{1}\|w_{1}-w_{2}\|_{2}.

(12)

Hessian Lipschitz Continuity with Respect to Noise:

\|\nabla_{M}^{2}\hat{g}(M,w_{1})-\nabla_{M}^{2}\hat{g}(M,w_{2})\|_{F}\leq\lambda_{2}\|w_{1}-w_{2}\|_{2}.

(13)

The proof is provided in Section
D

\hat{g}
with respect to

w

\lambda_{2}
. Together, these conditions ensure that both first- and second-order derivatives of
\hat{g}
vary in a controlled manner as a function of the noise, which is essential for stability and convergence guarantees in noise-sensitive optimization problems.

Conclusion

We compared the MSE loss and a kernel-based loss for low-rank matrix recovery in the presence of heavy-tailed noise. The exponential decay term in the proposed kernel-based loss reduces the impact of large outliers, whereas the MSE loss exhibits a linearly growing gradient. Our theoretical findings show that the kernel-based loss has more favorable upper and lower bounds in noisy regimes, and a combined formulation further balances these properties. Empirical results support these conclusions, indicating that the kernel-based and combined losses perform reliably under varying noise levels and values of
\delta
.

Appendix G
Relationship between different loss

To analyze the behavior and robustness of alternative loss functions under noisy observations, we present the following theorem, which compares covariance-based and kernel-based loss formulations to the standard mean squared error (MSE) criterion.

Theorem G.1
.

Let
w=(w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{n})
be a noise vector, and suppose
w
follows a centered distribution. That is, for residuals

r_{i}=Y_{i}+w_{i}-\mathcal{A}(M)_{i},\quad\text{we have}\quad\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}r_{j}=0.

(107)

Define the
covariance loss
by

L_{\mathrm{cov}}(r_{i})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{(r_{i}-r_{j})^{2}}{h^{2}}.

(108)

Under the centered noise assumption,
L_{\mathrm{cov}}
reduces to the mean squared error (MSE) loss. Next, define the
exponential kernel loss
by

L_{\mathrm{exp}}(r_{i})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\exp\Bigl(-\bigl(\tfrac{r_{i}-r_{j}}{h}\bigr)^{2}\Bigr).

(109)

This exponential kernel loss aligns with the following form of an optimal loss:

\hat{g}(M,w)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Biggl(-\log\Bigl[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{\bigl((Y_{j}+w_{j}-\mathcal{A}(M)_{j})-(Y_{i}+w_{i}-\mathcal{A}(M)_{i})\bigr)^{2}}{h^{2}}\Bigr)\Bigr]\Biggr).

(110)

As the noise amplitude
\|w\|
increases, the exponential kernel loss is suppressed, and the effective residual tends to be small within clusters. In particular, the weighted average of the residual
r_{i}
is

\overline{r}_{i}=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}r_{j}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{(r_{j}-r_{i})^{2}}{h^{2}}\Bigr)}{\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{(r_{j}-r_{i})^{2}}{h^{2}}\Bigr)},

(111)

and

\frac{\partial L_{i}}{\partial r_{i}}=-\frac{2}{h^{2}}\frac{1}{nZ_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(r_{j}-r_{i})\exp\Bigl(-\frac{(r_{j}-r_{i})^{2}}{h^{2}}\Bigr),

(112)

which indicates that
L_{\mathrm{exp}}
is not sensitive to large residuals.

Finally, if the loss function is not centered, then both the kernel loss and the covariance loss may converge to a suboptimal solution that does not match the ground truth. In that setting, even the optimal loss described above can perform worse than the MSE loss.

The proof is provided in Section
G.1
. Theorem
G.1
establishes a theoretical relationship between the traditional mean squared error (MSE) loss and a kernel-based robust alternative by analyzing the behavior of residual-dependent loss functions under a centered noise assumption. Specifically, it shows that the covariance loss, defined by normalized pairwise squared differences of residuals, reduces to the MSE when the noise vector
w
is centered, i.e., when the empirical mean of the residuals is zero.

The theorem then introduces the exponential kernel loss, which applies a Gaussian-type weighting to the residual differences. This loss aligns with the structure of the proposed robust objective
\hat{g}(M,w)
, where the inner exponential acts to suppress the contribution of large residuals. As the noise amplitude increases, the exponential weights diminish for outlier points, making the loss function focus on local, more coherent clusters of residuals. The weighted mean
\overline{r}_{i}
and the derivative
\frac{\partial L_{i}}{\partial r_{i}}
further demonstrate that this kernel loss downweights large deviations and is inherently more robust to noise than the MSE.

However, the theorem also cautions that this robustness depends critically on the centering of the noise. When the noise is not centered, both the covariance and kernel-based losses may fail to identify the correct minimizer, potentially performing worse than the MSE. This highlights an important limitation of these methods: their effectiveness relies on structural assumptions about the data distribution, particularly the unbiasedness of the residuals.

Now we want to calculate the lower bound for
\delta

We start from the second‐order Taylor expansion around the optimum

(179)

we have

Assume that in a neighborhood of

the function

is twice differentiable and its Hessian satisfies the following two conditions:

(Strong convexity) There exists

(181)

(182)

Then the Taylor expansion yields the following two-sided bounds for small

: Using strong convexity we obtain

\hat{g}(M,w)-\hat{g}(M^{\ast},w)\geq\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{\min}\|\Delta\|_{F}^{2}+o(\|\Delta\|_{F}^{2}).

Neglecting the higher‐order term for sufficiently small

(184)

Using the smoothness (an upper bound on the Hessian) we similarly obtain

(185)

so that for sufficiently small

(186)

Thus, the lower bound for

(187)

Here

I.6.1
Optimization Landscape
\|M-M^{\ast}\|_{F}
case

We are given that for any matrix M the linear operator
\mathcal{A}
satisfies the Restricted Isometry Property (RIP)

(1-\delta_{p})\|M\|_{F}^{2}\leq\|\mathcal{A}(M)\|_{2}^{2}\leq(1+\delta_{p})\|M\|_{F}^{2}.

(188)

The function is

f(M)=\|Y+w-\mathcal{A}(M)\|_{2}^{2}.

(189)

r(M)=Y+w-\mathcal{A}(M),
the function can be written as
f(M)=\langle r(M),r(M)\rangle.
Taking the gradient with respect to M yields
\nabla f(M)=-2\mathcal{A}^{\ast}\bigl(r(M)\bigr),

and then the Hessian (the second derivative with respect to
M
) is
\nabla^{2}f(M)=2\mathcal{A}^{\ast}\mathcal{A}.

Notice that this Hessian is independent of the value of
M
. In particular, at
M=M^{\ast}
, we have
\nabla^{2}f(M^{\ast},w)=2\mathcal{A}^{\ast}\mathcal{A}.

For any matrix R,

\langle R,\mathcal{A}^{\ast}\mathcal{A}(R)\rangle=\langle\mathcal{A}(R),\mathcal{A}(R)\rangle=\|\mathcal{A}(R)\|_{2}^{2}.

(190)

Thus, for the Hessian we have

\langle R,\nabla^{2}f(M^{\ast},w)[R]\rangle=2\|\mathcal{A}(R)\|_{2}^{2},

(191)

and consequently,

2(1-\delta_{p})\|R\|_{F}^{2}\leq\langle R,\nabla^{2}f(M^{\ast},w)[R]\rangle\leq 2(1+\delta_{p})\|R\|_{F}^{2}.

(192)

The smallest eigenvalue is thus at least

\lambda_{\min}\bigl(\nabla^{2}_{M}f(M^{\ast},w)\bigr)\geq 2(1-\delta_{p}).

(193)

Then we have:

f(M,0)-f(M^{\ast},0)\geq\delta\|M-M^{\ast}\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{1-3\delta}{2}\|M-M^{\ast}\|_{F}^{2}.

(194)

Now, since the full difference is a sum of the noise–free part plus a noise correction we may write

\displaystyle f(M,w)-f(M^{\ast},w)

\displaystyle=\Bigl[f(M,0)-f(M^{\ast},0)\Bigr]+E(w),

(195)

Now we want to calculate
E(w)

f(M,w)=\|Y+w-\mathcal{A}(M)\|_{2}^{2},

(196)

and in the noise–free case

f(M,0)=\|Y-\mathcal{A}(M)\|_{2}^{2}.

(197)

We set:

u_{1}=Y-\mathcal{A}(M)\quad\text{and}\quad u_{2}=Y-\mathcal{A}(M^{\ast}).

(198)

Then, we set:

\displaystyle f(M,w)

\displaystyle=\|Y-\mathcal{A}(M)\|_{2}^{2}+2\langle Y-\mathcal{A}(M),w\rangle+\|w\|_{2}^{2},

(199)

\displaystyle f(M^{\ast},w)

\displaystyle=\|Y-\mathcal{A}(M^{\ast})\|_{2}^{2}+2\langle Y-\mathcal{A}(M^{\ast}),w\rangle+\|w\|_{2}^{2}.

Consider the difference
f(M,w)-f(M^{\ast},w)
:

\displaystyle f(M,w)-f(M^{\ast},w)

\displaystyle=\Bigl[\|Y-\mathcal{A}(M)\|_{2}^{2}-\|Y-\mathcal{A}(M^{\ast})\|_{2}^{2}\Bigr]

(200)

\displaystyle+2\Bigl[\langle Y-\mathcal{A}(M),w\rangle-\langle Y-\mathcal{A}(M^{\ast}),w\rangle\Bigr]

\displaystyle+\Bigl[\|w\|_{2}^{2}-\|w\|_{2}^{2}\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\Bigl[f(M,0)-f(M^{\ast},0)\Bigr]+2\Bigl[\langle Y-\mathcal{A}(M),w\rangle-\langle Y-\mathcal{A}(M^{\ast}),w\rangle\Bigr].

(201)

\langle Y-\mathcal{A}(M),w\rangle-\langle Y-\mathcal{A}(M^{\ast}),w\rangle=\langle\mathcal{A}(M^{\ast})-\mathcal{A}(M),w\rangle.

(202)

Thus, we obtain

E(w)=2\langle\mathcal{A}(M^{\ast})-\mathcal{A}(M),w\rangle.

(203)

For low rank recovery, we still have:

\bigl\|f(M,w)-f(M^{\ast},w)\bigr\|\geq\frac{1-\delta}{2}\|M-M^{\ast}\|_{F}^{2}-\bigl\|E(w)\bigr\|.

(204)

Using the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality we have

|E(w)|=2\Bigl|\langle\mathcal{A}(M^{\ast})-\mathcal{A}(M),w\rangle\Bigr|\leq 2\|\mathcal{A}(M^{\ast})-\mathcal{A}(M)\|_{2}\|w\|.

(205)

Again applying the RIP we obtain

\|\mathcal{A}(M^{\ast})-\mathcal{A}(M)\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{1+\delta}\|M-M^{\ast}\|_{F}.

(206)

Thus,

|E(w)|\leq 2\sqrt{1+\delta}\|w\|\|M-M^{\ast}\|_{F}.

(207)

Taking absolute values and using the triangle inequality we conclude that

\displaystyle\bigl\|f(M,w)-f(M^{\ast},w)\bigr\|

\displaystyle\leq\Bigl\|f(M,0)-f(M^{\ast},0)\Bigr\|+\|E(w)\|

(208)

\displaystyle\leq(1+\delta)\|M-M^{\ast}\|_{F}^{2}+2\sqrt{1+\delta}\|w\|\|M-M^{\ast}\|_{F}.

With the previous result:

\|M-M^{\ast}\|^{2}_{F}\leq\frac{2}{\lambda_{\min}}\Bigl[f(M,w)-f(M^{\ast},w)\Bigr].

(209)

\lambda_{\min}\bigl(\nabla^{2}_{M}f(M^{\ast},w)\bigr)\geq 2(1-\delta_{p}),

(210)

and we also have derived an upper bound on the function‐difference:

f(M,w)-f(M^{\ast},w)\leq(1+\delta)\|M-M^{\ast}\|_{F}^{2}+2\sqrt{1+\delta}\|w\|\|M-M^{\ast}\|_{F}.

(211)

\|M-M^{\ast}\|_{F}^{2}\leq\frac{2}{\lambda_{\min}}\Bigl[(1+\delta)\|M-M^{\ast}\|_{F}^{2}+2\sqrt{1+\delta}\|w\|\|M-M^{\ast}\|_{F}\Bigr].

(212)

Since
\|M-M^{\ast}\|_{F}\geq 0
and
\delta_{p}>0
(typically small) the left‐side is negative. Multiplying by

(2(1+\delta)-\lambda_{\min})\|M-M^{\ast}\|_{F}^{2}-4\sqrt{1+\delta}\|w\|\|M-M^{\ast}\|_{F}\leq 0.

(213)

\|M-M^{\ast}\|_{F}\leq\frac{\sqrt{1+\delta_{p}}\|w\|}{\delta_{p}}.

(214)

\|M-M^{\ast}\|_{F}=O(\|w\|).

N.5
, since
q<\frac{1/3-\delta}{\zeta_{2}+2\zeta_{\alpha}/3}
, we know that
K>0
. Finally, we choose
C=(\frac{2(1+\delta+\zeta_{2}\epsilon)}{1-\delta-(\zeta_{2}+\zeta_{D})\epsilon}\|M^{\ast}\|^{2}_{F})^{1/4}
and invoke Lemmas 6-8 to complete the proof of Theorem
N.5
. Note the
\epsilon
here is the same
\epsilon
appeared in the statement of Theorem
N.5
.

S.1
Theoretical Study

Theorem S.1

(Combined Loss Bound)
.

Let
r_{i}=Y_{i}-\mathcal{A}(XX^{\top})_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,n,
and assume there is a constant
B>0
such that
r_{i}^{2}\leq B
for all
i.
Define the combined loss:

L_{\mathrm{combined}}(X)=(1-\lambda)\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigl[-\log\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\exp\Bigl(-\tfrac{\bigl((r_{j}-r_{i})^{2}\bigr)}{h^{2}}\Bigr)\Bigr)\Bigr]+\lambda\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}^{2}.

(623)

Then consider a matrix sensing or low-rank recovery problem where
Y=\mathcal{A}(M^{*})+w,
and
\mathcal{A}
satisfies a restricted isometry property (RIP)–like condition: there exists
\delta\in(0,1)
such that, for every symmetric matrix
E
of rank at most
2r,
then with probability at least
\mathbb{P}(\|w\|_{2}\leq\epsilon)
we have:

\|XX^{\top}-M^{*}\|_{F}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\delta}}\sqrt{\frac{L_{\mathrm{combined}}^{*}}{\lambda}+\frac{1}{n}\epsilon^{2}}.

(624)

The detailed proof and simplified analysis are provided in Appendix
S.2
. We can use similar results as above to show the result. More detailed analysis will be provided in the later version.

Proof of Theorem

Proof.

The composite loss with random noise is given as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{L_{\mathrm{combined}}}(X)

(625)

\displaystyle=(1-\lambda)\cdot n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(-\log\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\exp\left(-\frac{((Y_{j}-(\mathcal{A}(XX^{\top})_{j}))-(Y_{i}-\mathcal{A}(XX^{\top})_{i}))^{2}}{h^{2}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle+\lambda\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl(Y_{i}-\mathcal{A}(XX^{\top})_{i}\bigr)^{2}.

The composite loss is more robust to different setting. For example, when the
\|w\|
is small (MSE is better, or when
\|w\|
is medium, (ours is large).

r_{i}=Y_{i}-\mathcal{A}(XX^{\top})_{i},\quad i=1,\dots,n.

(626)

For convenience, assume that there exists a constant
B>0
such that
r_{i}^{2}\leq B,\quad\text{for all }i=1,\dots,n.
We recall that the loss is defined as:

\displaystyle L_{\mathrm{combined}}(X)

\displaystyle=(1-\lambda)\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigl[-\log\Bigl(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{\bigl((r_{j}-r_{i})^{2}\bigr)}{h^{2}}\Bigr)\Bigr)\Bigr]

(627)

\displaystyle+\lambda\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}^{2}.

We now bound each term separately. Since
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}^{2}\leq B,
it follows that
\lambda\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}^{2}\leq\lambda B.
For any
i
and
j
, because
r_{i}^{2}\leq B
we have
|r_{j}-r_{i}|\leq|r_{j}|+|r_{i}|\leq\sqrt{B}+\sqrt{B}=2\sqrt{B}.

(r_{j}-r_{i})^{2}\leq(2\sqrt{B})^{2}=4B.
Then, for every
i
and
j
,
\exp\left(-\frac{(r_{j}-r_{i})^{2}}{h^{2}}\right)\geq\exp\left(-\frac{4B}{h^{2}}\right).

Hence, for each fixed
i
we obtain:

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\exp\left(-\frac{(r_{j}-r_{i})^{2}}{h^{2}}\right)\geq\frac{1}{n}\cdot n\exp\left(-\frac{4B}{h^{2}}\right)=\exp\left(-\frac{4B}{h^{2}}\right).

(628)

Taking the negative logarithm yields:

-\log\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\exp\left(-\frac{(r_{j}-r_{i})^{2}}{h^{2}}\right)\right)\leq-\log\left(\exp\left(-\frac{4B}{h^{2}}\right)\right)=\frac{4B}{h^{2}}.

(629)

Since this bound holds for each
i
, averaging over
i
gives:

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[-\log\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\exp\left(-\frac{(r_{j}-r_{i})^{2}}{h^{2}}\right)\right)\right]\leq\frac{4B}{h^{2}}.

(630)

Putting the bounds (
20
) into the combined loss (
627
), we conclude that:

L_{\mathrm{combined}}(X)\leq(1-\lambda)\frac{4B}{h^{2}}+\lambda B.

(631)

Assuming that we are in a matrix sensing/low‐rank recovery setting. In such settings one assumes Assumption
13
and
2.1
. The observed vector is modeled as
Y=\mathcal{A}(M^{*})+w,

where
w
is a noise vector. The combined loss is given by

\displaystyle L_{\mathrm{combined}}(X)

(632)

\displaystyle=(1-\lambda)\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Biggl[-\log\Bigl(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{\bigl((Y_{j}-\mathcal{A}(XX^{\top})_{j})-(Y_{i}-\mathcal{A}(XX^{\top})_{i})\bigr)^{2}}{h^{2}}\Bigr)\Bigr)\Biggr]

\displaystyle+\lambda\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigl(Y_{i}-\mathcal{A}(XX^{\top})_{i}\Bigr)^{2}.

Further assume that
\mathcal{A}
satisfies an RIP–like property; that is, there exists
\delta\in(0,1)
such that for every symmetric matrix
E
of rank at most
2r
one has
(1-\delta)\|E\|_{F}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{n}\|\mathcal{A}(E)\|_{2}^{2}\leq(1+\delta)\|E\|_{F}^{2}.
In our setting we set
E=XX^{\top}-M^{*}.
Denote the quadratic term in the loss by:

f_{\mathrm{quad}}(X)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigl(Y_{i}-\mathcal{A}(XX^{\top})_{i}\Bigr)^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\|\mathcal{A}(XX^{\top}-M^{*})-w\|_{2}^{2}.

(633)

For simplicity (and without loss of generality for a bound) imagine that the optimization is such that the loss is nearly achieved; then the quadratic term is small. In particular, note that:

\frac{1}{n}\|\mathcal{A}(XX^{\top}-M^{*})\|_{2}^{2}\leq f_{\mathrm{quad}}(X)+\frac{1}{n}\|w\|_{2}^{2}.

(634)

If the noise is controlled (or even absent) and if the combined loss is small near a stationary point (say, bounded above by
L_{\mathrm{combined}}^{*}
), then in particular

\frac{1}{n}\|\mathcal{A}(XX^{\top}-M^{*})\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{L_{\mathrm{combined}}^{*}}{\lambda}+\frac{1}{n}\|w\|_{2}^{2}.

(635)

(In the combined loss the quadratic part appears weighted by
\lambda
.) For clarity we define

\varepsilon^{2}:=\frac{L_{\mathrm{combined}}^{*}}{\lambda}+\frac{1}{n}\|w\|_{2}^{2}.

(636)

Thus,

\frac{1}{n}\|\mathcal{A}(XX^{\top}-M^{*})\|_{2}^{2}\leq\varepsilon^{2}.

(637)

The RIP property (
8
) guarantees that:

(1-\delta)\|XX^{\top}-M^{*}\|_{F}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{n}\|\mathcal{A}(XX^{\top}-M^{*})\|_{2}^{2}\leq\varepsilon^{2}.

(638)

Recalling the definition of
\varepsilon
, this yields

\|XX^{\top}-M^{*}\|_{F}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\delta}}\sqrt{\frac{L_{\mathrm{combined}}^{*}}{\lambda}+\frac{1}{n}\|w\|_{2}^{2}}.

(639)

So we have:

\|XX^{\top}-M^{*}\|_{F}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\delta}}\sqrt{\frac{L_{\mathrm{combined}}^{*}}{\lambda}+\frac{1}{n}\epsilon^{2}}.

(640)

with probability at least
\mathbb{P}(\|w\|_{2}\leq\epsilon)
.
