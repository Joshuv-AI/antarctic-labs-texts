The emerging tick-borne Yezo virus - current knowledge, challenges, and perspectives
Authors: Nathan M Markarian, Levon Abrahamyan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTicks are external parasites that can carry and transmit a diverse range of pathogens, including bacteria, parasites, and viruses. In recent years, factors such as climate change, deforestation, and human activities have contributed to the expansion of tick populations along with the pathogens they harbour. These pathogens can cause serious disease in both humans and animals, such as Lyme disease and Crimean-Congo hemorrhagic fever (CCHF). In 2021, a novel tick-borne orthonairovirus, Yezo virus (YEZV) was reported in Hokkaido, Japan, in two patients presenting with acute febrile illness characterized by thrombocytopenia and leukopenia. Subsequent cases of YEZV have been reported in China, and it has been isolated in ticks collected from migratory birds in Japan, indicating its potential to spread further. As this virus emerges, critical gaps remain in understanding the mechanisms of YEZV pathogenesis, transmission, and prevention. This review aims to provide a comprehensive understanding of YEZV since its discovery, with a focus on its biology, pathogenesis, reported patient cases, treatments, and prevention strategies. By examining the current literature, this review seeks to identify key directions for future research and public health initiatives to enhance control measures for this emerging disease. Ultimately, adopting cross-sectoral One Health approaches will improve early warning, prevention, and preparedness for a potential global health emergency caused by YEZV.Keywords: Yezo virus, Tick-borne disease, Zoonosis, Arbovirus, Emerging virus

Introduction

In the last century, humans have experienced several viral pandemics and witnessed the emergence and re-emergence of zoonotic diseases. Deforestation, urbanization, tourism, wildlife exploitation, and climate change have brought humans into proximity with sylvatic fauna, exposing them to the natural reservoirs of pathogens capable of causing disease [1]. Among those reservoirs, ticks are the arachnids that survive by feeding on the blood of animals and humans. Ticks obtain blood either by pool feeding, cutting skin and blood vessels to drink pooled blood, or by piercing and sucking, using specialized mouthparts to cannulate vessels and draw blood directly. During these feeding processes, ticks can both acquire pathogens from infected vertebrates and transmit them to susceptible hosts [2]. The complete life cycle of ticks is usually measured in years, and their remarkable longevity allows for the persistence of tick-borne pathogens over a prolonged time [3]. These pathogens could lead to tick-borne diseases (TBDs), which may be of bacterial (e.g., Borrelia spp., Ehrlichia spp., Anaplasma spp., and Rickettsia spp.), parasitic (e.g., Babesia spp.), or viral (e.g., Tick-Borne Encephalitis Virus (TBEV), Powassan virus (POWV), Crimean-Congo Hemorrhagic Fever Virus (CCHFV), Severe Fever with Thrombocytopenia Syndrome Virus (SFTSV), Heartland virus (HRTV), and Bourbon virus (BRBV)) origin [4–6]. One of the challenges of TBDs is that it is difficult to distinguish the etiological agents responsible for the disease, as many of the tick-borne pathogens cause similar symptoms [4]. For this reason, molecular testing has been of great importance in the discovery of novel pathogens [4]. Furthermore, the number of tick species affecting a geographical region could rise with the increasing global temperatures, and acaricide-resistant strains have emerged, making tick control a significant public health and livestock issue [7]. Amongst the tick-borne viruses, CCHFV is the most widespread tick-borne virus in humans, reported in more than 30 countries over Asia, Africa, southeastern Europe, and the Middle East and causing severe viral hemorrhagic fever outbreaks with a case fatality rate of 5 to 30% [3]. In the case of livestock, the Nairobi sheep disease virus (NSDV), a zoonotic, tick-borne virus, is one of the most pathogenic tick-borne viruses affecting small ruminants [8]. With a case fatality rate of 90%, NSDV has historically circulated in East Africa and recently emerged in Asia as the Ganjam virus [8]. In 2021, a novel tick-borne virus, belonging to the genus Orthonairovirus – the same genus as CCHFV and NSDV - was discovered on Hokkaido, Japan’s largest island [9]. It was named Yezo virus (YEZV) after its historical name. YEZV, or Orthonairovirus yezoense, is a novel emerging tick-borne virus causing a febrile illness, alerting local public health authorities to an endemic infection affecting several patients [9]. Phylogenetically, YEZV is a bunyavirus in the family Nairoviridae and is related to Sulina virus, discovered in Ixodes ricinus ticks collected in Romania, and to the central Asian Tamdy virus (TAMV) [9]. YEZV antibodies have been detected in wild animals including Hokkaido sika deer and raccoons, suggesting potential reservoir hosts, though their role in viral amplification and transmission requires further investigation. Evidence suggests it is transmitted by the Ixodes persulcatus tick [9–11]. With the recent emergence of YEZV, there is an increase in reported YEZV cases, raising questions of its prevalence and endemicity [10]. Thus, there are several knowledge gaps regarding the virology, pathogenesis, epidemiology, treatments, and prevention strategies. In this review, we will analyze the latest literature on YEZV with the goal of providing a platform for future research focused on improving mitigation strategies and enhancing patient and community health outcomes.

Epidemiology, disease & natural reservoir

Thus far, a total of 24 confirmed human infections have been reported in Hokkaido Japan as well as in Inner Mongolia and Heilongjiang provinces in China [9, 12–14]. Among those patients, the majority were found to develop symptoms within 4 to 9 days following a tick bite [9, 12–14]. All hospitalized YEZV-infected patients were eventually discharged from the hospital with no deaths reported, though not all required hospitalization, although coinfection with Borrelia miyamotoi was noted in four patients from Hokkaido, Japan [9, 12–15]. All reported cases were found to experience fever with most of them showing signs of malaise [9, 12–14]. Laboratory serum findings among patients generally revealed leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, and elevated liver enzymes, suggesting liver damage [9, 12–14]. Given the limited information regarding the past medical history of patients infected with YEZV and the predominance of male cases, further investigation is needed to determine the immunological and genetic predispositions to YEZV infection [9, 12–14]. Questions remain as to whether co-infection with bacterial or parasite pathogens exacerbates clinical morbidity and mortality, a matter that can be assessed through enhanced surveillance and reporting. Specifically, Kodama et al. analyzed samples collected in Hokkaido since 2014 and identified five positive cases. These patients presented with thrombocytopenia and acute leukopenia, with two cases co-infected with Borrelia burgdorferi sensu lato [9]. Similarly, Chen et al. conducted a retrospective analysis of cases from 2012 to 2016 and identified four infections [16]. Three cases were mild, while one severe case involved leukocytosis, lymphocytopenia, thrombocytopenia, and elevated alanine aminotransferase, optic nerve disorder, and abnormal cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) findings. All cases recovered with the severe case requiring 14-day hospitalization before recovery [16].

In terms of the natural reservoir of YEZV, Kodama et al. identified Hokkaido sika deer (Cervus nippon yesoensis) (6 out of 785 samples) and raccoons (Procyon lotor) (3 out of 182 samples) in Hokkaido to be positive for YEZV antibodies [9]. Three major tick species - Haemaphysalis megaspinosa, Ixodes ovatus, and Ixodes persulcatus - were found to be positive for YEZV RNA using RT-PCR, with the highest detection rate of 3.8% in H. megaspinosa [9]. For patient 2 (P2) in Table 1, the tick responsible for the bite was identified as Ixodes persulcatus, the same species that bit patient 4 (P4) [9, 17]. In this case, YEZV RNA was detected in both the tick emulsion and the patient’s serum, providing strong evidence that Ixodes persulcatus may serve as the primary vector of YEZV [17]. Zhang et al. also found that, using RT-PCR, the amplified YEZV sequences from Ixodes persulcatus were identical to those derived from the 18 patients (P6-P23 in Table 1) [13]. Further, a study analyzing 281 ticks collected between March 2021 and May 2023 in Hulun Buir, Inner Mongolia, China, found YEZV in Ixodes persulcatus ticks, with a 0.38% prevalence [18]. In a subsequent study by Chen et al., YEZV was found to primarily infect I. persulcatus (31.4%) and Dermacentor nuttalli (10.5%) in northern China, as well as Rhipicephalus microplus (1.8%) among 868 ticks sampled from nine sites across eight Chinese provinces [16]. Another study showed that YEZV were found in the Dermacentor silvarum and Haemaphysalis japonica tick species, across mainland China [19]. Moreover, a recent study analyzed 2,534 ticks collected during 2020–2021 from migratory birds traveling from Sakhalin and the Kuril Islands to Hokkaido, Japan. The findings revealed that YEZV- positive pools were exclusively associated with I. persulcatus ticks, particularly those collected from black-faced buntings (Emberiza spodocephala) [11]. Based on the latest research, it can be concluded that YEZV is likely to be transmitted by ticks, mainly by I. persulcatus, using wild animals such as Hokkaido sika deer and raccoons as potential intermediate hosts [10]. It remains to be determined whether YEZV is present in other parts of the world and whether additional hosts are involved.

YEZV virology & pathogenesis

As a bunyavirus, YEZV is negative single stranded RNA virus with a genome consisting of three unique segments: large (L), medium (M), and small (S), measuring approximately 12.143, 4.280, and 1.701 kilobases (kb) respectively [6]. These segments encode different proteins as follows - L segment: RNA-directed RNA polymerase (RdRp); M segment: glycoprotein precursor (GPC); S segment: N protein at around 3,939; 1,357 and 503 amino acids, respectively [9] YEZV virions are oval to spherical in shape, with an average diameter around 100–200 nm [9]. As an orthonairovirus, YEZV is proposed to possess a membrane envelope with glycoprotein spikes composed of Gn and Gc subunits and ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complexes consisting of genomic RNA segments bound to N protein, along with the L protein, within the viral particle [20]. To date, the viral replication cycle of YEZV has not been elucidated, and information regarding its host cell receptor and cell tropism remains unavailable. However, it is plausible to propose that it is similar to other nairoviruses [20]. Briefly, YEZV would utilize viral surface glycoproteins to bind to yet unknown host cell receptor(s) and enter the cell through an endosomal pathway, resulting in a pH-dependent fusion and release of the virion RNP complex into the cytoplasm [20]. During primary transcription, the YEZV L protein generates viral mRNAs, which are capped using host-derived primers (cap-snatching) [20]. Subsequently, the mRNAs from the L and S segments are translated by free ribosomes, while M segment mRNA is translated by membrane-bound ribosomes. The resulting glycoprotein precursor (GPC) is subsequently cleaved co- and post-translationally by host proteases and glycosylated by cellular glycosyltransferases, yielding Gn, Gc, and occasionally non-structural glycoproteins [20]. The L protein also synthesizes the antigenome, which would serve as a template for genomic RNA replication. Secondary transcription enhances mRNA production and genome replication. During morphogenesis, Gn and Gc accumulate in the Golgi apparatus, where modified host membranes are acquired, and virions bud into the Golgi cisternae, ultimately leading to virion egress [20]. Interestingly, YEZV encodes viral ovarian tumor-like cysteine proteases (vOTUs), a class of deubiquitinases (DUBs) capable of cleaving both ubiquitin and ISG15, a critical component of the antiviral host response. In an overexpression system, these vOTUs exhibit deISGylation activity, though with a narrower capacity compared to CCHFV during natural infection [16].

To elucidate the pathogenesis of YEZV, Kodama et al. intraperitoneally inoculated the YEZV-infected patient plasma into AG129 mice, which are double knockout mice lacking interferon-ɑ/β and -ɣ receptors [9]. Among the four inoculated mice, two showed no clinical signs up to 14 days post-infection (dpi) but exhibited significant IgG ELISA titers. In contrast, the remaining two mice developed a splenomegaly by 5 dpi [9]. In a subsequent study, Ariizumi et al. investigated the pathogenicity of a human-isolated YEZV strain in mice. Wild-type BALB/c, C57BL/6 mice intraperitoneally infected with 104 focus-forming units (FFU) of YEZV did not develop severe clinical signs [21]. In contrast, immunodeficient AG129 mice, infected at the same dose, developed severe disease, with a 100% fatality rate within 14 days. Serum analysis revealed that viremia peaked at 2 dpi, with viral RNA levels remaining consistent at 2, 4 and 6 dpi [21]. Notably, serum liver enzyme levels, including ALT and AST, were elevated at both 2 and 6dpi [21]. Histopathological examination revealed evidence of tissue damage, inflammation and the presence of YEZV in liver hepatocytes and Iba (+) macrophages in the spleen [21]. Additionally, the virus was isolated from the kidney and heart tissues, although no evidence of tissue damage was observed. Based on these findings, the authors suggested that YEZV is hepatotropic [21], a characteristic consistent with the human cases reported to date [9, 12–14].

Diagnosis, treatments & countermeasure strategies

To diagnose YEZV infection, reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) has been employed to detect viral RNA in blood samples [9, 13, 14, 16, 17], while enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) has been used with YEZV nucleocapsid (N) protein-expressing cells serving as the antigen [9]. An indirect immunofluorescence assay (IFA) has also been successfully used to detect YEZV [13]. At present, no specific treatments or vaccines are available for YEZV infection. Antivirals such as ribavirin and ganciclovir have been administered to affected patients; although their efficacy in alleviating symptoms remains unclear [13]. In cases where the causative agent of infection had not yet been determined, empiric treatments targeting Borrelia miyamotoi, Lyme disease, rickettsioses and tularemia have been applied [9]. Additionally, glycyrrhizin was used to decrease liver damage [12]. To identify potential treatments, Ariizumi et al. evaluated the antiviral activity of favipiravir (T-705), a purine analog, and ribavirin, a synthetic guanosine analog, using Vero cells. The IC50 and IC90 values for favipiravir were 3.51 µM and 8.79 µM, respectively, while those for ribavirin were 12.32 µM and 25.47 µM [21]. Based on these findings, AG129 mice were challenged with YEZV and immediately treated with favipiravir, given once daily from 1 h post-infection to 3 dpi [21]. It was shown that favipiravir-treated AG129 mice had survival rates of 57% and 100%, when administered at doses 100 mg/kg/day and 300 mg/kg/day, respectively [21]. The observed improvements in mice survival and reductions in liver and spleen pathology suggest that favipiravir exerts significant antiviral effects against YEZV in vivo [21]. To identify a potential YEZV vaccine candidate, Rahman et al. used an immunoinformatic approach to predict the antigenicity of T- and B-cell epitopes from YEZV proteins and to design a vaccine construct [22].

With the rising tick population and with the extension of their geographical distribution, responsible for the emergence of several tick-borne diseases, it is crucial to develop effective strategies to prevent future YEZV infections. One approach involves advising individuals to take appropriate self-protective measures, when visiting tick-endemic areas, such as wearing light-colored clothing, long-sleeved shirts, and long trousers tucked into boots or socks, as well as rubber boots [23]. To minimize tick exposure, it is also recommended to avoid brushing against vegetation and to shower and inspect typical attachment sites—such as the knees, armpits, and groin—after returning home [23]. The use of tick/insect repellents containing DEET, picaridin, or EBAAP is advised. Pets should also be routinely screened for ticks given their role as sentinels for vectors and pathogens of veterinary and zoonotic importance [24]. This could be facilitated by an application similar to the Tekenscanner, which allows identification of ticks collected from pets, assessment of their pathogen status, and monitoring possible geographical tick infestations [24]. Additionally, clothing should be washed or dried at 60 °C to eliminate any attached ticks [23]. In the context of public health, routine diagnostic testing for YEZV should be implemented in Northern China and Japan, particularly because YEZV presents with symptoms like those of other tick-borne diseases. Genetic sequences of YEZV should be archived in public databases to facilitate the monitoring of YEZV mutations that may give rise to virulent strains. Indeed, Zhang et al. discovered two YEZV clades harboring important mutations at three sites within the RdRp domain [13]. Moreover, Nishino et al. already reported genetic reassortment events in YEZV using a recombination analysis program, suggesting possible viral transmission between China and Hokkaido, Japan [11]. Along with reassortment, several other driving forces could be responsible for the growing genetic diversity of YEZV. For instance, intrinsic genetic properties such as its mutations rate and segmented genome structure, as well as ecological and epidemiological factors, i.e., its geographical distribution, the number of intermediate and reservoir hosts, frequent cross-species transmission, seasonal patterns, and anthropogenic influences such as interactions with ticks, deforestation and climate change [25]. An example of this is provided by a study that examined YEZV seroprevalence in wild animals and tick loads in regions near Sapporo, Hokkaido [26]. The study found that YEZV seropositivity was correlated with tick load, and that anti-YEZV antibody positivity in raccoons increased with age and the proportion of forested area, but was negatively associated with the length ratio of water edge [26]. Thus, it is crucial to monitor the emergence of new strains of YEZV. Like in the case of CCHFV, serosurveys of domestic and wildlife animal species should be implemented to inform public health authorities about both the geographic distribution and relative prevalence of YEZV circulation [27].

Conclusion

In summary, Yezo virus (YEZV) is a recently identified zoonotic, tick-borne orthonairovirus that has caused illness in multiple patients, with no fatalities reported to date. The combination of YEZV’s zoonotic potential, the genetic and ecological drivers of its emergence, and the recent discovery of related tick-borne orthonairoviruses: such as Songling virus (SGLV), Tacheng tick virus-1 (TcTV-1) and the Tamdy virus (TAMV) highlights its importance as an emerging public health concern [28–30]. Understanding the ecology and biology of YEZV and related viruses remains critical for the development of specific molecular diagnostic tools, effective antiviral therapies, and preventive measures, all of which are currently lacking. This review has outlined recent advancements in YEZV research, emphasizing the urgent need for further studies in several key areas: (i) development of immunocompetent animal models to better understand pathogenesis and immune responses; (ii) screening of antiviral compounds and evaluation of their efficacy against YEZV; (iii) design and testing of vaccine candidates; (iv) implementation of routine diagnostic testing for YEZV in endemic regions of Northern China and Japan; (v) establishment of serosurveys in domestic and wildlife species to map viral distribution; and (vi) strengthening genomic surveillance to monitor viral evolution and reassortment events. Adopting a comprehensive One Health approach at both local and global levels will be essential to ensure a coordinated, effective, and timely response to this emerging public health threat.
