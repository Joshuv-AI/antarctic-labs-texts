Abstract
We probabilistically bound the error of a solution to a radial network topology learning problem where both connectivity and line parameters are estimated. In our model, data errors are introduced by the precision of the sensors, i.e., quantization. This produces a nonlinear measurement model that embeds the operation of the sensor communication network into the learning problem, expanding beyond the additive noise models typically seen in power system estimation algorithms. We show that the error of a learned radial network topology is proportional to the quantization bin width and grows sublinearly in the number of nodes, provided that the number of samples per node is logarithmic in the number of nodes.

\Delta>0

II
Problem formulation

II-A

Communication model

We wish to recover an unknown vector of line parameters
\boldsymbol{w}_{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
from
m\geq d

quantized measurements
of the form

p_{i}\triangleq\mathcal{Q}(\left\langle\boldsymbol{a}_{i},\boldsymbol{w}_{\star}\right\rangle),\quad i=1,\ldots,m,

(1)

where
\mathcal{Q}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a nonlinear
quantization function
and
\left(p_{i},\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\right)
,
i=1,\ldots,m
are measurements collected from smart meters (both spatially
and
temporally). Vectors
\left\{\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\right\}
are rows of a particular sensing matrix
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
and contain pairwise voltage differences measured co-temporally with active power injection
p_{i}
. We will define matrix
\boldsymbol{A}
explicitly in Section
III-A
as a consequence of linearizing the power flow equations. In the same way, we will argue that the sparsity pattern of
\boldsymbol{w}_{\star}
represents the network topology (cf.
[
7
, Sec. IV-B]
).

Concretely, we focus on the setting in which coarsely quantized measurements (
1
) are generated from a uniformly dithered quantization function (cf.
[
3
,
6
]
) with bin width
\Delta>0
. These measurements take the form

p_{i}=\Delta\cdot\left(\left\lfloor\frac{\left\langle\boldsymbol{a}_{i},\boldsymbol{w}_{\star}\right\rangle+\tau_{i}}{\Delta}\right\rfloor+\frac{1}{2}\right)\quad i=1,\ldots,m,

(2)

where
\tau_{i}\sim\operatorname{\sf Uniform}\left(-\frac{\Delta}{2},\frac{\Delta}{2}\right)
, and
\operatorname{E}\left[p_{i}\right]=\left\langle\boldsymbol{a}_{i},\boldsymbol{w}_{\star}\right\rangle
. The
dither

\tau_{i}
is a purposely applied random noise component that is generated by a sensor and added to an input signal prior to
quantization. This technique is rather well-established and commonly used by sensors in practice, and by statisticians in theory; see
[
10
,
3
]
.

II-B

Statistical tools

Suppose that
\boldsymbol{w}_{\star}
lies in a convex constraint set
\mathcal{K}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
that encodes some sort of
known structure
of the parameter
\boldsymbol{w}_{\star}
, e.g., knowledge of where certain lines are located (but not their switching status), or whether the network is operated radially. We can solve for a constrained estimate
\hat{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
that obeys this structure via the program (
3
), known as the
generalized LASSO
:

\hat{\boldsymbol{w}}=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{K}}\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\left\langle\boldsymbol{a}_{i},\boldsymbol{w}\right\rangle-p_{i}\right)^{2}.

(3)

The quality of the estimate produced by the program (
3
) can be quantified with a statistical tool known as the
Gaussian width
. We introduce this tool briefly and refer the reader to
[
11
]
,
[
12
, Ch. 7.5]
and
[
5
,
6
]
for further technical information. Formally, the Gaussian width
\omega(\mathcal{T})
of a set
\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is defined as

\omega(\mathcal{T})\triangleq\operatorname{E}\left[\sup_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{T}}\left\langle\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{g}\right\rangle\right],

(4)

where
\boldsymbol{g}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I})
is a vector of i.i.d. standard Gaussians. The Gaussian width
\omega
is a useful tool for predicting the behavior of structured convex problems with random input data
[
11
]
. It represents the “size” of a standard Gaussian process over
\mathcal{T}
, which we can use as a stochastic
comparison
for our structured problem. Two classic examples of structured constraint sets with well-behaved Gaussian widths are sparse and low-rank constraint sets
[
11
]
.

The squared Gaussian width,
\omega^{2}(\mathcal{T})
, provides a measure of the effective dimension of the set
\mathcal{T}
; informally, this represents the minimum number of orthogonal directions required to capture the variation or structure within
\mathcal{T}
. We will also need another object for our analysis—the
tangent cone
of a set
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
at
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which is defined as

\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{K},\boldsymbol{x})\triangleq\left\{\lambda\boldsymbol{u}\>:\>\lambda\geq 0,\,\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{K}-\boldsymbol{x}\right\},

(5)

where
\mathcal{K}-\boldsymbol{x}
is the set
\mathcal{K}
translated by
\boldsymbol{x}
. The tangent cone (
5
) generalizes the idea of the tangent space to a nonlinear surface that may have non-differentiable points.

II-C

Network and power flow model

The abstract quantities in (
1
) and (
2
) can be applied to the Linear Coupled Power Flow (LCPF) model
[
7
,
13
]
. As the focus of this work is on sample complexity, we make the following simplifying assumption on the reactive power.

Assumption 1

(Fixed power factor)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be active power injections.
Assume that reactive power injections satisfy
\boldsymbol{q}=\kappa\boldsymbol{p}
, where
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
is a known constant.

Assumption
1
is equivalent to defining a fixed power factor
\phi\in(0,1]
such that
\kappa=\pm\phi^{-1}\sqrt{1-\phi^{2}}
, where
\operatorname{sign}(\kappa)=1
if the injections are inductive and
\operatorname{sign}(\kappa)=-1
if the injections are capacitive. For more information on the consequences of this assumption, see
[
14
,
15
]
.

II-C
1
Linear Coupled Power Flow (LCPF) model

For a radial network with
n+1
nodes, let
\boldsymbol{C}\in\left\{-1,0,1\right\}^{n\times n}
be the invertible, reduced, branch-to-node incidence matrix of a tree graph with the column corresponding to the slack node removed. Let
\left\{\left(\boldsymbol{v}_{t},\boldsymbol{p}_{t}\right)\right\}_{t=1}^{m}
be a sequence of nodal voltage magnitude and active power measurements. Under Assumption
1
, the voltage magnitudes satisfy
[
7
,
13
]
:

\boldsymbol{v}_{t}-\boldsymbol{1}=\left(\boldsymbol{R}+\boldsymbol{X}\cdot\kappa\right)\boldsymbol{p}_{t},\quad t=1,\ldots,m

(6)

where
\boldsymbol{R}+\boldsymbol{X}\kappa\triangleq\boldsymbol{Z}
is the (reduced)
equivalent impedance matrix
. We can write
\boldsymbol{Z}
as the inverse of a
real-valued
equivalent admittance matrix
\boldsymbol{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, where

\boldsymbol{Z}=\boldsymbol{C}^{-1}\operatorname{diag}\left(\boldsymbol{r}+\kappa\boldsymbol{x}\right)\boldsymbol{C}^{-\top}\triangleq\boldsymbol{Y}^{-1},

(7)

where
\boldsymbol{z}\triangleq\boldsymbol{r}+\kappa\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
are the line impedances (scaled). The matrix
\boldsymbol{C}^{-1}\in\left\{-1,0\right\}^{n\times n}
is a lower triangular matrix where the non-zero entries of each column
j
represent the descendants of node
j
in the tree. In particular, over the indices
i,j=1,\ldots,n
, we have
\left(\boldsymbol{C}^{-1}\right)_{ij}=-1
if
i=j
or
i
is a descendant of
j
, and
\left(\boldsymbol{C}^{-1}\right)_{ij}=0
otherwise.

II-C
2
Lifting to the complete graph

Echoing
[
9
]
, we can view the problem of recovering
\boldsymbol{w}_{\star}
as
sparsifying
a complete undirected graph
K_{n+1}
with
n+1
nodes and
{n+1\choose 2}
lines. Accordingly, let
\boldsymbol{\tilde{Y}}(\cdot)
be the
admittance matrix operator
that sends line parameters
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1\choose 2}
to a corresponding (non-reduced)
(n+1)\times(n+1)
admittance matrix:

\boldsymbol{\tilde{Y}}(\boldsymbol{w})=\boldsymbol{\tilde{C}}^{\top}\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{w})\boldsymbol{\tilde{C}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}\boldsymbol{E}_{ij}w_{ij}.

(8)

Here,
\boldsymbol{E}_{ij}\triangleq\left(\boldsymbol{e}_{i}-\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\right)\left(\boldsymbol{e}_{i}-\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\right)^{\top}
is an
elementary Laplacian matrix
, where
\boldsymbol{e}_{i}
is the
i
-th standard basis vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. The matrix
\boldsymbol{\tilde{C}}\in\left\{-1,0,1\right\}^{{n+1\choose 2}\times n+1}
is the incidence matrix that corresponds to a complete graph. We can now represent the set of all radial networks as the set of all line parameter vectors
\boldsymbol{w}
that correspond to a connected radial network with
n+1
nodes. This set takes the form

\mathcal{R}=\left\{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1\choose 2}\>:\>\left|\left|\boldsymbol{w}\right|\right|_{0}=n,\ \ \lambda_{2}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{Y}}(\boldsymbol{w}))>0\right\}.

(9)

The sparsity condition
\left|\left|\boldsymbol{w}\right|\right|_{0}=n
ensures
n
lines, and the condition on the second smallest eigenvalue
\lambda_{2}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{Y}}(\boldsymbol{w}))>0
means the graph is connected, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\tilde{Y}}(\boldsymbol{w})\boldsymbol{x}>0
for all
\boldsymbol{x}\perp\boldsymbol{1}
. The constraint set (
9
) is intractable; however, in Section
III-A
we provide a relaxation that performs well both experimentally and theoretically. Hereafter, we implicitly remove the slack node column of
\boldsymbol{\tilde{C}}
in our calculations, and refer to the network as having
n
nodes.

III
Main result

III-A

Topology learning problem

Let
\boldsymbol{P},\boldsymbol{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times s}
be data matrices whose columns are
s
samples of active power and voltage magnitude measurement vectors, respectively, across all
n
nodes. We seek to recover an
n
-sparse vector

\boldsymbol{w}_{\star}
of line parameters, where
w_{\star,i}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{z_{\star,i}}}
if
z_{\star,i}\neq 0
, and
0
otherwise. This problem can be written as a sparse recovery problem over the
complete graph
by defining the measurement system

\boldsymbol{p}=\mathcal{Q}\left(\operatorname{\sf vec}\left(\boldsymbol{P}\right)\right)=\mathcal{Q}\left(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{w}_{\star}\right),\quad\boldsymbol{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{sn}.

(10)

The
sn\times{n+1\choose 2}
sensing matrix is

\boldsymbol{A}\triangleq\boldsymbol{V}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\tilde{C}}^{\top}\odot\boldsymbol{\tilde{C}}^{\top},

where
\odot
is the Khatri-Rao matrix product.

III-B

Bounding the error of the parameter estimate

Taking advantage of the sparsity, we can characterize the Gaussian width of the tree set (
9
).

Lemma 1
.

Define the set
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{R},\boldsymbol{w}_{\star}}\triangleq\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R},\boldsymbol{w}_{\star})\cap\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
as the intersection of the tangent cone (
5
) of constraint set
\mathcal{R}
with the unit shell
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\triangleq\left\{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\>:\>\left|\left|\boldsymbol{u}\right|\right|_{2}=1\right\}
,
d={n+1\choose 2}
. The squared Gaussian width of the set of radial networks (
9
) satisfies
\omega^{2}\left(\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{R},\boldsymbol{w}_{\star}}\right)\leq 2n\log\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)+\frac{3}{2}n.

Proof.

The ground truth
\boldsymbol{w}_{\star}
corresponds to a spanning tree of the complete graph with
n+1
nodes. Since this tree must have
n
edges, vector
\boldsymbol{w}_{\star}
must be
n
-sparse, i.e.,
\left|\left|\boldsymbol{w}_{\star}\right|\right|_{0}=n
. Dropping connectivity, define the relaxation
\mathcal{K}\supseteq\mathcal{R}
as

\mathcal{K}\triangleq\left\{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{{n+1\choose 2}}:\left|\left|\boldsymbol{w}\right|\right|_{1}\leq\left|\left|\boldsymbol{w}_{\star}\right|\right|_{1}\right\},

(11)

i.e., the
\ell_{1}
-norm ball of radius
\left|\left|\boldsymbol{w}_{\star}\right|\right|_{1}
.
It is well known (cf.
[
11
,
6
]
) that, as
\boldsymbol{w}_{\star}
is
n
-sparse, we can conclude that
\omega^{2}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{R},\boldsymbol{w}_{\star}})\leq\omega^{2}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{K},\boldsymbol{w}_{\star}})\leq 2n\log(\frac{n+1}{2})+\frac{3}{2}n
.
∎

Using Lemma
1
, the error of the topology learned by (
3
) with constraint set (
11
) and bin width
\Delta>0
is bounded and can be quantified.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose that
s=\frac{m}{n}
samples with bin width
\Delta>0
are collected throughout an
n
-node distribution network, and suppose that the
\left\{\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\right\}
are any i.i.d. sub-Gaussian random vectors. Then, there exist constants
C,c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that the error of the topology estimate from solving (
3
), with probability at least 0.99 (i.e., 99%) is bounded as

\left|\left|\hat{\boldsymbol{w}}-\boldsymbol{w}_{\star}\right|\right|_{2}\leq C\Delta\cdot\sqrt{\frac{2\log\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)+\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}{s}},

(12)

provided the number of samples
s\geq\mathcal{O}(\log n).

Proof.

Combining Lemma
1
with (
13
) and applying
[
6
, Thm. III.1]
, there exists a constant
C>0
such that the minimizer
\hat{\boldsymbol{w}}
of the program (
3
) satisfies, with probability at least 0.99,

\displaystyle\left|\left|\hat{\boldsymbol{w}}-\boldsymbol{w}_{\star}\right|\right|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\Delta\cdot\frac{\omega\left(\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{K},\boldsymbol{w}_{\star}}\right)}{\sqrt{m}},

(13a)

\displaystyle\leq C\Delta\cdot\sqrt{\frac{2\cancel{n}\log(\frac{n+1}{2})+\frac{3}{2}\cancel{n}}{s\cancel{n}}},

(13b)

provided the total number of measurements
m=sn\geq c_{1}\omega^{2}\left(\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{K},\boldsymbol{w}_{\star}}\right)+c_{2}
for some absolute constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
. Due to Lemma
1
, if the number of samples per node
s\geq c_{1}(2\log\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)+\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}})+c_{2}=\mathcal{O}(\log n)
we satisfy this measurement requirement.
∎

IV
Conclusion

We provided a framework to
predict the error of a learned distribution network topology—before solving any optimization problem.
By exploiting the radial structure, we achieved an error
sublinear
in the number of nodes, up to constant factors determined by the precision of the sensor and the underlying probability distributions. Formally, we showed that the topology
and
line parameters of a radial network can be recovered up to a relative error of
\mathcal{O}(\Delta\sqrt{\nicefrac{{\log n}}{{s}}})
, given a collection of
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
samples from every node with quantization bin width
\Delta>0
. Thus, before collecting data, one can size quantization and sampling budgets to hit a target topology error. Note that (
13
) is determined by the bin width
\Delta
. Deriving bounds involving communication bit rates would require assumptions on the variance of the underlying measurement being quantized.
