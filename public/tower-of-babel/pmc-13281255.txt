The effect of concept mapping–based self-care education on quality of life in patients with rheumatoid arthritis: a non-randomized quasi-experimental study
Authors: Navid Dadashi, Nasrin Rasoulzadeh, Raoofeh Karimi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveIn addition to pharmacological treatments, self-care education is crucial for managing chronic conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis. Concept mapping, a visual and learner-centered educational strategy, has shown potential in improving self-care engagement. However, evidence for its effectiveness in rheumatoid arthritis populations remains limited. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the effect of concept map–based self-care education on quality of life in patients with rheumatoid arthritis.ResultsFollowing the intervention, participants in the experimental group showed significantly better outcomes compared to the control group in overall quality of life (P < 0.001) and most domain-specific measures, including Activities, Movement, and Energy (P = 0.007); Mood/Emotions (P < 0.001); and Physical Contact (P = 0.015), with moderate to large effect sizes. However, no significant difference was observed in the Self-Care domain (P = 0.300). Within-group analyses revealed significant improvements in quality of life across all domains in the intervention group (all P < 0.05), whereas no changes were observed in the control group (all P > 0.05). Therefore, concept mapping, as a structured and visual educational strategy, may enhance patients’ understanding of complex self-care information and improve quality of life among individuals with rheumatoid arthritis.Trial registration: The protocol of this clinical trial has been registered in the Iranian Clinical Trial Registration Center (registration code: IRCT20230405057828N1). Registered 23 June 2023- registered while recruiting, https://www.irct.ir/.

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic, systemic inflammatory autoimmune disorder that primarily affects synovial joints and is clinically characterized by symmetrical polyarticular pain, swelling, prolonged morning stiffness, and fatigue [1, 2]. Persistent synovitis, as the pathological hallmark of RA, leads to progressive cartilage destruction, bone erosion, and irreversible structural joint damage when inadequately managed [3, 4]. Beyond joint involvement, RA is a multisystem disease with extra-articular manifestations such as rheumatoid nodules, interstitial lung disease, pericarditis, peripheral neuropathy, vasculitis, and hematologic abnormalities, all of which can substantially compromise overall health status and survival [5, 6]. Globally, the prevalence of RA has been estimated at approximately 460 cases per 100,000 individuals between 1980 and 2019 [7, 8]. In Iran, the incidence of RA is reported to affect 0.37% of the total population [9].

The chronic and progressive nature of RA, alongside its clinical burden and long-term functional consequences, including severe impairments in physical function, psychological well-being, and social participation, leads to substantial reductions in health-related quality of life (HRQoL) [10–13]. Therefore, the adoption of comprehensive management strategies, extending beyond pharmacological interventions, is critical to reduce disability and the long-term burden of the disease [14]. Among non-pharmacological approaches, self-care plays a pivotal role in the management of chronic diseases. Self-care encompasses individuals’ abilities to maintain health, prevent disease progression, and manage symptoms independently or in collaboration with healthcare professionals. Unlike conventional patient education, which primarily focuses on information delivery, self-care emphasizes active patient engagement. This involvement helps patients develop the confidence, autonomy, and position them as valuable contributors to their own care and decision-making processes [15, 16]. Evidence from other chronic neurological and inflammatory conditions, such as multiple sclerosis, indicates that educational interventions focused on self-care can improve quality of life and psychological resilience [17].

To optimize the effectiveness of such interventions, they should be grounded in robust and innovative pedagogical frameworks, such as Ausubel’s theory of meaningful learning. According to this theory, meaningful learning occurs when new information is actively integrated with pre-existing cognitive structures, with prior knowledge serving as an anchor for the assimilation of new concepts. In this context, concept mapping represents an instructional strategy that operationalizes meaningful learning [18, 19].

Concept mapping, originally developed by Novak in the 1980s, consists of graphical representations composed of nodes and connecting lines. Each node represents a concept, while the linking lines illustrate the relationships and propositions between concepts. A concept map may include multiple interconnected nodes and links organized in a hierarchical structure, in which more general and inclusive concepts are positioned at the top or center, and more specific concepts are arranged at lower levels. This structured organization facilitates the integration of prior knowledge with new information, thereby promoting meaningful learning, enhancing critical thinking and problem-solving skills, and supporting deeper knowledge assimilation [20–22].

Despite the profound multidimensional impact of RA on HRQoL, there is limited evidence on the integration of concept map–based self-care education into clinical practice for this population. The scarcity of rigorous interventional studies examining this instructional strategy represents a critical gap in nursing science, particularly regarding its potential to enhance the effectiveness of self-care education and improve quality of life outcomes. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the effect of concept map–based self-care education on quality of life in patients with rheumatoid arthritis.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study adopted a quasi-experimental design with intervention and control groups. The study was conducted at the rheumatology clinic of Imam Khomeini Hospital, affiliated with Tehran University of Medical Sciences, Tehran, Iran, from September 2023 to December 2023.

Study population and eligibility criteria

The study population consisted of all patients with a confirmed diagnosis of rheumatoid arthritis who attended the rheumatology clinic of Imam Khomeini Hospital during the study period.

Participants were eligible for inclusion if they were aged 18–60 years, possessed sufficient literacy to engage with the educational materials, had a confirmed diagnosis of RA by a licensed rheumatologist, were able to use CD-based educational resources, and provided voluntary informed consent to participate in the study.

Participants were excluded if they had a diagnosis of other chronic medical conditions (e.g., cardiovascular disease, asthma, or cancer), had a documented psychiatric disorder, were unwilling to continue participation, or failed to attend the scheduled educational sessions.

Sample size and sampling procedure

The sample size was determined based on a 95% confidence level, 80% statistical power, and a 5% precision margin [23]. According to these parameters, the required sample size was calculated to be 31 patients per group. To account for a 10% attrition rate, a total of 35 patients were enrolled in each group. A consecutive sampling strategy was employed throughout the study. Participants for the control group were initially recruited using convenience sampling in accordance with the predefined inclusion criteria. After the target sample size for the control group was reached and data collection was completed, the same sampling approach was used to recruit participants for the intervention group. This sequential, non-random allocation design was implemented to reduce the risk of contamination between groups. By completing recruitment and data collection for the control group before initiating enrollment for the intervention group, the potential for crossover effects and information exchange between groups was minimized.

Data collection instruments

Data were collected using two instruments: a demographic characteristics questionnaire and the Rheumatoid Arthritis Quality of Life (RAQoL) questionnaire.

Demographic characteristics questionnaire

Participants’ demographic and clinical characteristics were collected using a researcher-developed questionnaire, including age, sex, level of education, marital status, employment status, perceived economic status, duration of disease, frequency of hospitalization due to RA, and medications used.

Rheumatoid Arthritis Quality of Life (RAQoL) questionnaire

Quality of life was assessed using the RAQoL questionnaire, a validated disease-specific instrument comprising 30 dichotomous (yes/no) items. The RAQoL, originally developed by Jasani et al. [24], is designed to evaluate the impact of RA on multiple aspects of daily functioning. In this instrument, a response of “yes” indicates that a need or problem is present and is scored as 1, whereas a response of “no” indicates that the need is met and is scored as 0. Total scores range from 0 to 30, with higher scores indicating poorer quality of life. The questionnaire encompasses four domains: activities, movement, and energy (10 items); self-care (8 items); mood and emotions (8 items); and physical contact (4 items). The validity and reliability of the RAQoL have been well documented. Zlatkovic-Svenda et al. [25] reported a correlation coefficient of 0.94, supporting its validity in patients with RA. The Persian version of the RAQoL Questionnaire has previously been translated and culturally adapted for the Iranian population with RA. Furthermore, its psychometric properties, including reliability and construct validity, have been rigorously confirmed [26]. In the present study, content validity was evaluated by a panel of 10 faculty members from the Tehran School of Nursing and Midwifery, and their feedback was incorporated into the final version of the instrument. Reliability was assessed using a test–retest approach in 15 patients with RA, with a 10-day interval between administrations. Internal consistency, assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, was 0.704, and the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was 0.889, indicating acceptable internal consistency and strong temporal stability.

Intervention

All required institutional permissions and ethical approvals were obtained before the initiation of data collection. Participants were provided with a detailed explanation of the study objectives and procedures, and written informed consent was obtained in accordance with ethical guidelines.

For the control group, baseline data (pretest) were collected before the intervention period. Participants then received routine education as part of standard clinical care during regular outpatient visits, delivered by physician assistants. This education primarily addressed immediate clinical concerns, such as symptom management and medication instructions, and was not delivered within a structured or comprehensive educational framework. In addition, patients received routine printed educational materials, including pamphlets and brochures related to RA. No scheduled or interactive educational sessions were conducted for the control group. One month after the pretest, post-intervention data were collected.

Following completion of recruitment and data collection for the control group, enrollment of the intervention group commenced. After baseline assessment (pretest), participants in the intervention group received a self-care education programme based on concept mapping. The intervention was delivered in two individual, face-to-face sessions, each lasting 45–60 min, with a two-week interval between sessions. Individual delivery was chosen to facilitate active engagement, tailored explanations, and responsiveness to participants’ questions. Educational content was presented using a laptop and delivered in a step-by-step manner at an appropriate pace to support comprehension. All sessions were conducted in a quiet, pre-arranged setting to minimise distractions. Throughout the intervention, participants’ questions and concerns regarding their condition were addressed to promote effective learning.

The first session (45 min) focused on disease awareness, clinical manifestations, diagnostic processes, and general corrective and therapeutic approaches. The second session (60 min) addressed a broader range of self-care topics, including nutrition, sleep and rest, physical activity and rehabilitation, the influence of weather on symptoms, use of hot and cold compresses, marriage and family life, pregnancy and breastfeeding, vaccination, travel considerations, anxiety and depression, and strategies for ongoing disease management and continuity of care (Table 1).Table 1content of self-care training sessions for RA patientsSessionDurationTopic145 minUnderstanding the disease, clinical manifestations, diagnosis of the disease, and various corrective and therapeutic methods260 minPrinciples of nutrition, sleep and rest, exercise, rehabilitation, the effect of weather on the disease, the use of hot and cold compresses, marriage, pregnancy, breastfeeding, vaccination, travel, anxiety and depression and how to control and continuously care for the disease

The educational programme consisted of 20 concept maps developed by the researcher using Mindomo software and organised in a hierarchical structure. Seven concept maps addressed disease recognition, clinical manifestations, diagnosis, and therapeutic approaches, while 13 concept maps focused on self-care and lifestyle-related topics, including nutrition, rest, exercise, rehabilitation, environmental factors, psychosocial issues, and long-term disease management. The content and structure of the concept maps were reviewed by three faculty members from the School of Nursing and Midwifery with expertise in patient education and rheumatology nursing, and their feedback was incorporated to enhance clarity and content validity.

At the conclusion of the two educational sessions, participants in the intervention group received the concept map-based educational materials in CD format. Post-intervention data for the intervention group were collected one month after completion of the final educational session. The extended interval in the intervention group was necessary to allow sufficient time for participants to engage with the educational materials and potentially translate acquired knowledge into practice before outcome assessment. During the follow-up period, participants were contacted weekly via scheduled telephone calls to encourage review of the educational materials, respond to questions, and support continued engagement. Brief questions were used to confirm participation and understanding. Participants were also encouraged to contact the researcher by telephone if additional questions or concerns arose.

At the end of the study, the educational CD was provided to participants in the control group to ensure equitable access to the educational content, in accordance with ethical principles. Due to the nature of the educational intervention, blinding of participants and the researcher was not feasible. However, efforts were made to minimise potential bias through standardised data collection procedures and consistent follow-up across study groups.

Ethics consideration

All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations (Declaration of Helsinki). This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Tehran University of Medical Sciences (Ethics code: IR.TUMS.FNM.REC.1401.187). Participants were informed about the purpose of the study, the voluntary nature of participation, and the confidentiality of their personal information. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection.

Trial registration

The protocol of this clinical trial has been registered in the Iranian Clinical Trial Registration Center (registration code: IRCT20230405057828N1). Registered 23 June 2023- registered while recruiting, https://www.irct.ir/.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using SPSS software (version 16.0). Both descriptive and inferential statistical methods were employed. Descriptive statistics included frequency distributions for categorical variables and mean and standard deviation for continuous variables. For inferential analyses, the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate, was used to assess baseline homogeneity of categorical variables between the control and intervention groups. Before conducting parametric analyses, the normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. The results indicated that quality-of-life total scores and domain-specific scores were approximately normally distributed in both groups at baseline and post-intervention (p > 0.05), supporting the use of parametric tests. Independent-samples t-tests were conducted to compare quality of life scores and their domain-specific dimensions between groups at baseline and post-intervention. Within-group changes over time were examined using paired-samples t-tests. In addition to inferential statistics, effect size (Cohen’s d) and their 95% confidence intervals were reported. For between- group comparisons, Cohen’s d was calculated using pooled standard deviations. For within-group comparisons, Cohen’s d for dependent samples (dz) was computed based on the paired t statistic. A significance level of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Results

In the intervention group, 45 participants were initially recruited. Of these, 35 participants (77.8%) completed both educational sessions and the post-intervention assessment, while 10 participants (22.2%) discontinued participation before completion of the educational sessions. In the control group, 37 participants were enrolled, of whom 35 (94.6%) completed the post-test; two participants (5.4%) withdrew during the study period. The reported reasons for withdrawal were personal and non–study-related, including time constraints, family obligations, transportation difficulties, and lack of availability to attend scheduled sessions. Notably, no withdrawals were attributed to dissatisfaction with the intervention, adverse effects, or disease-related complications. Paired-samples t-test analyses indicated no statistically significant within-group changes in the control group across overall quality of life or any domain-specific measures between baseline and post-intervention assessments (all P > 0.05). In contrast, the intervention group demonstrated statistically significant reductions across overall quality of life (P < 0.001, d = 1.41, 95% CI [1.03, 1.79]) and all domain-specific measures following the intervention, including activities, movement, and energy (P < 0.001, d = 0.97, 95% CI [0.62, 1.32]), self-care (P < 0.001, d = 0.75, 95% CI [0.41, 1.09]), mood/emotions (P < 0.001, d = 1.12, 95% CI [0.77, 1.47]), and physical contact (P < 0.001, d = 0.86, 95% CI [0.51, 1.21]). Discussion

This study was conducted to evaluate the effect of concept map–based self-care education on quality of life in patients with RA. Findings indicate that the intervention group demonstrated significantly better overall quality of life and most domain-specific outcomes (activities, movement, and energy; mood/emotions; and physical contact) compared with the control group, whereas differences in self-care were not significant. Within-group analyses further confirmed significant improvements in overall quality of life and most domain-specific measures in the intervention group following concept mapping–based education, compared with those in the control group who received routine education. Collectively, these findings add to the expanding body of evidence underscoring the importance of self-care educational interventions in improving quality of life for individuals living with chronic conditions such as RA.

The observed improvements in physical and psychosocial dimensions of QoL are consistent with prior evidence demonstrating the beneficial effects of structured self-care education in RA and other chronic conditions. For instance, Mohammed et al. (2023) reported significant improvements in health status, QoL, and reduced pain level following self-care education among women with RA, underscoring the empowering role of patient education in chronic disease management [27]. Similarly, Baratzadeh et al. (2021) found that self-care education enhanced functional status in RA patients [28]. Beyond corroborating these findings, the present study extends existing literature by emphasizing the potential added value of concept mapping as an instructional strategy, rather than education alone. Concept mapping differs from traditional didactic approaches by visually organizing information and explicitly illustrating relationships between concepts, thereby facilitating deeper cognitive processing and knowledge retention, features that may be particularly advantageous for complex, multifaceted conditions such as RA [20, 28, 29].

The results extend beyond statistical significance, demonstrating meaningful improvements in quality of life among patients with RA following a concept mapping–based self-care educational intervention. The observed effect sizes were moderate (Cohen's d = 0.60–0.69) across most subscales, with a particularly robust effect on overall quality of life (d = 0.86), thereby underscoring the practical relevance and value of this educational program. These findings suggest that concept mapping is an effective and practical clinical tool for patient education that can lead to meaningful improvements in quality of life. Such robust effect sizes are particularly notable in the management of chronic conditions, where enhancing patient well-being is both critical and challenging. Furthermore, these findings support the shift toward active and visual-based learning strategies in patient education to optimize clinical outcomes.

The effectiveness of concept map–based education can be interpreted by the theoretical assumptions introduced earlier, Ausubel’s theory of meaningful learning, which emphasizes the integration of new information into existing cognitive structures. It can be stated that concept mapping may facilitate a deeper understanding of self-care–related information and, by promoting critical thinking, self-regulatory capacity, and behavioral self-management [20, 30–32], may contribute to improvements in quality of life among individuals with chronic conditions. Evidence supporting the adaptability of this approach across chronic conditions has been reported by Bozorgzad et al. (2013), who demonstrated that visual concept mapping improved QoL and disease control in children with asthma [33]. The present findings suggest that similar mechanisms may operate in adult patients with RA.

Interestingly, despite significant within-group improvements, no statistically significant between-group difference was observed in the self-care domain of the RAQoL. Notably, the present findings differ from Gurjar (2024) [34], who reported significant improvements in self-care behaviors following nurse-led educational interventions in arthritis populations. Several methodological and theoretical factors may explain the lack of significant between-group differences in the self-care domain. First, the RAQoL primarily reflects the physical capacity to perform personal care activities (e.g., dressing, washing, or mobility-related tasks) rather than actual self-care behaviors, adherence practices, or disease-management strategies. These items are strongly influenced by joint damage, pain severity, and functional limitations, factors that are unlikely to be substantially modified through a brief educational intervention. Therefore, the absence of a significant between-group difference may not necessarily indicate ineffectiveness of the educational content, but rather reflects the specificity and functional orientation of the measurement instrument. Second, although concept mapping–based education may have improved patients’ understanding of self-care principles, the short duration of the intervention and follow-up may have been insufficient to translate cognitive gains into sustained behavioral changes, consistent with previous evidence suggesting that self-care behaviors require prolonged practice to become routine [35]. Third, standard educational advice provided during routine care may have been adequate to maintain stability in this domain, limiting the observable differences between groups. Finally, characteristics of the study population, including a high proportion of homemakers with limited prior exposure to structured educational technologies, may have necessitated additional time and practice to effectively engage with concept mapping and apply learned strategies. These findings underscore the importance of tailoring educational interventions to patients’ baseline competencies, learning preferences, and contextual constraints, an issue of particular relevance for nursing-led education in diverse clinical settings.

Limitation

Several limitations must be considered when interpreting the results. First, the quasi-experimental design with non-random allocation introduces the possibility of selection bias, which limits internal validity. To mitigate this, baseline comparability and strict inclusion criteria were applied. Second, the sequential sampling method, with the control group recruited before the intervention group, may have introduced temporal threats, such as history and maturation effects. Despite efforts to stabilize treatment protocols, these factors could have influenced the findings. Third, attention bias may have occurred, as the intervention group received weekly telephone reminders, potentially leading to a Hawthorne effect, where increased attention and perceived support improved outcomes. Fourth, unequal time intervals between pre- and post-assessments (six weeks for the intervention group vs. four weeks for the control group) represent another methodological limitation. Fifth, the small sample size (35 patients per group) and single-center design limit the generalizability of the findings. Sixth, the brief educational program (two sessions) may not have been sufficient for sustained behavioral change, though it enhances clinical applicability. Seventh, the lack of long-term follow-up prevents conclusions about the durability of quality of life improvements. Lastly, reliance on self-reported data introduces potential response bias.

Future studies should incorporate randomized controlled trials with standardized follow-up intervals and alternative sampling strategies (e.g., random or stratified sampling). Including an attention-control group would help isolate intervention-specific effects from the potential Hawthorne effect. Larger, multicenter studies with longer follow-up and objective self-care measures, such as adherence indices and validated self-management scales, are needed to assess the sustainability of improvements and differentiate cognitive gains from changes in physical functional capacity.

Conclusion

This quasi-experimental study demonstrated that concept mapping–based self-care education significantly improved overall quality of life and several psychosocial and functional domains in patients with RA. However, the absence of a significant between-group difference in the self-care domain warrants cautious interpretation. These findings highlight the potential value of concept mapping as a structured, visual educational strategy to enhance patients’ understanding of complex self-care information, which may contribute to improvements in quality of life. Integrating concept mapping into routine patient education by healthcare professionals, including nurses, could provide a practical approach to empower patients and foster active engagement in disease management. Given the study’s limitations, future research should employ randomized controlled designs, simultaneous sampling strategies, longer interventions, and extended follow-up to evaluate the durability and clinical significance of these effects.
