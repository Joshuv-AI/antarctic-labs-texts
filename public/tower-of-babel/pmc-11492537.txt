Synoviocyte detachment: an overlooked yet crucial histological aspect in rheumatoid arthritis
Authors: Bihua Wang, Jianbin Li, Yiping Huang, Rui Wu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveRheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a prevalent autoimmune disorder that leads to chronic joint inflammation, deformity, disability, and systemic complications. This study aimed to analyze the clinical characteristics and synovial pathology of RA patients with synoviocyte detachment, and explore the factors associated with this phenomenon.MethodsThis was a retrospective cohort study included RA patients who underwent synovial biopsy at our center from April to September 2023. Demographic, clinical, laboratory, and synovial histological data were retrospectively collected from medical records at the time of joint synovial biopsy in patients. Microscopic examination of hematoxylin and eosin (HE)-stained synovial tissue sections categorized the samples into synoviocyte detachment and no-synoviocyte detachment groups. Clinical characteristics and synovial pathological changes were compared between the two groups, and the factors associated with synoviocyte detachment were explored through logistic regression analysis.ResultsFifty-five RA patients were enrolled; 45 were females, and the mean age was 53.4 ± 11.8 years. Nine RA patients exhibited synoviocyte detachment. A total of 46 RA patients in the no-synoviocyte detachment group (15 with a normal lining layer and 31 with synovial cell proliferation) were included. Compared with the no-synoviocyte detachment group, the synoviocyte detachment group presented higher RF, ESR, CRP and DAS28-CRP levels (P < 0.05). The synoviocyte detachment group exhibited more prominent neovascularization (P < 0.05). ESR, DAS28-CRP and synovial neovascularization were risk factors associated with synoviocyte detachment in RA patients.ConclusionRA patients with synoviocyte detachment exhibit elevated clinical disease activity, marked by pronounced synovial pathology featuring increased neovascularization and less inflammatory cell infiltration. A significant reduction in lymphocyte count compared with patients with synovial cell proliferation was also observed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-024-07935-8.Keywords: Rheumatoid arthritis, Synoviocyte Detachment, Synovial pathology

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a prevalent autoimmune disorder that affects approximately 0.5-1% of adults worldwide, with a relatively high incidence in women and typical onset during middle age [1]. This disorder poses significant health risks, leading to chronic joint inflammation, deformity, disability, and systemic complications. The characteristic features of RA include synovial inflammation and proliferation, alongside cartilage and bone destruction. These hallmark traits contribute to the chronic and progressive nature of the disease, leading to joint deformities and functional impairment [2, 3]. Understanding these pathological processes is crucial for developing targeted therapeutic interventions aimed at halting or slowing disease progression and improving the quality of life of individuals affected by RA [4].

The synovium, positioned between joints, tendons, and bursae, plays a pivotal role in joint health and function. It comprises two main layers: the lining layer and the sublining layer. The lining layer is primarily composed of 1–3 layers of synovial cells and matrix [5]. These synovial cells, including fibroblast-like synoviocytes and macrophages, are responsible for secreting synovial fluid, which lubricates and nourishes the joint, as well as for maintaining the integrity of the synovial membrane [6]. The sublining layer, located beneath the lining layer, contains immune cells, blood vessels, and connective tissue elements.

Inflammation induces profound changes in the synovial structure of individuals with RA. As inflammation occurs, an influx of inflammatory cells infiltrates the sublining layer of the synovium, causing thickening of the lining layer [7]. This thickening is accompanied by the proliferation of synovial cells, contributing to the cell proliferation observed in the RA synovium. Persistent inflammation exacerbates these changes, promoting neovascularization within the sublining layer. The newly formed blood vessels supply oxygen and nutrients to proliferating synovial cells, further fueling synovial proliferation and exacerbating joint damage. Neovascularization and synovial proliferation play crucial roles in perpetuating the inflammatory cascade and perpetuating joint destruction in RA [8]. Characteristic manifestations of the RA synovium include synovial cell proliferation, matrix-degrading enzymes activation, neovascularization, and inflammatory cells infiltration.

Traditionally, synovial lining hyperplasia has been acknowledged as a key pathological feature in RA and is characterized by the proliferation of synovial cells that invade adjacent tissues and promote joint damage. This proliferation often results in an increase in the number of layers within the synovial lining, with cells expanding to 6–10 layers in thickness. Proliferating synovial cells play a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of RA by instigating synovial inflammation and contributing to bone erosion through the production of cytokines and proteases [6, 9].

In contrast to synovial cell proliferation, synoviocyte detachment has historically received less attention in the understanding of synovial pathology in RA. Synoviocyte detachment is commonly regarded as a pathological phenomenon that occurs in the late stages of RA and is often referred to as “burn-out” synovium [10]. To date, the clinical characteristics of RA patients with synoviocyte detachment remain inadequately understood, and this phenomenon has not received sufficient attention in RA research. Therefore, we conducted a retrospective analysis of RA patients who underwent synovial pathological examination at our center. The aim of this study was to comprehensively analyze the clinical features and synovial pathological changes in RA patients with synoviocyte detachment and explore potential factors influencing this phenomenon.

Patients and methods

Study population

This retrospective study was conducted at the Rheumatology and Immunology Department of the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University in China, spanning from April 2023 to September 2023. We analyzed data from a cohort of 55 patients with rheumatoid arthritis (RA) who underwent synovial biopsy during this period. Eligible patients were adults who met the 2010 American College of Rheumatology/European League Against Rheumatism (ACR/EULAR) diagnostic criteria for RA [11]. Ethical approval for the research protocol was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University (ethics number IIT [2023] and Clinical Ethics Review No.011) Written consent was obtained from all participants prior to synovial biopsy. Detailed demographic and clinical information, including sex, age, disease duration, medication history, and relevant laboratory assessments such as rheumatoid factor (RF), anti-citrullinated protein antibody (ACPA), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and C-reactive protein (CRP) within two weeks before and after synovial biopsy, was collected for each RA patient. Furthermore, joint X-rays were obtained to complement the clinical assessments, and the Steinbrocker stage classification were used to evaluate the X-ray staging in RA patients [12, 13].

Device and procedure

The novel synovial biopsy device is composed of a sampling outer needle with diameters ranging from 1.1 mm to 1.7 mm and lengths ranging from 50 mm to 120 mm, featuring a sampling port length of 4 mm. This device is patented under number 202310572174.4. Its structure and procedure are depicted in Supplementary Figure S1. Synovial tissue samples were obtained from swollen joints, including 43 knee joints (78.18%), 8 wrist joints (14.54%), 2 elbow joints (3.64%), and 2 ankle joints (3.64%), respectively. We selected joints based on the biopsy joint hierarchy proposed by Kelly et al [14]. According to this hierarchy, the first choice should be a medium or large joint, with grade 3 synovial thickening, followed by a joint of any size with synovitis grade ≥ 2, a medium or large joint with synovitis grade ≥ 1, and finally, a small joint with any degree of synovitis [14, 15].

Synovial tissue processing and scoring method

The harvested synovial tissue was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde buffered with PBS for 48 h, dehydrated and embedded in paraffin. The paraffin-embedded tissue blocks were subsequently sectioned into 3–4 μm-thick slices, stained with HE, and observed under a light microscope for synovial structure evaluation. The assessment was scored on a scale of 0 to 3 and involved semiquantitative synovial component grading, including lining layer cell proliferation and detachment, matrix activation, neovascularization, and inflammatory infiltration [16, 17]. Neovascularization scoring was performed by counting the number of blood vessels in synovial tissue (0, 0–3; 1, 4–9; 2, 10–15; 3, ≥ 16 vessels under low magnification (20×)). Capillaries, venules, and arterioles were weighted equally for this score.

By examining the synovial tissue slices stained with HE under a microscope, we observed that the lining layer of synovial tissue became thinner, the number of synovial cells decreased and the single-layer arrangement became loose in some RA cases. We defined this phenomenon as synoviocyte detachment. This categorization distinguished patients into the synoviocyte detachment group (those with synoviocyte detachment) and the no-synoviocyte detachment (those with a normal lining layer or synovial cell proliferation) groups (Fig. 1). Immunohistochemistry was performed to detect the distributions of T cells (CD3), B cells (CD20), plasma cells (CD138), and macrophages (CD68). The tissue sections were incubated with mouse-specific primary antibodies against human CD3, CD20, CD68, and CD138. The negative controls were analyzed via similar procedures without primary antibodies, and the slides were counterstained and fixed with hematoxylin. Two reviewers independently scored each section, and any discrepanices in scoring were resolved through joint review to reach a consensus.

Clinical activity assessment

RA patients rated their pain via the visual analog scale (VAS). Pain intensity was scored from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating greater pain. TJC28 represents the number of tender joints out of 28, whereas SJC28 represents the number of swollen joints out of 28. Experienced clinicians counted these cases on the basis of the patient’s clinical symptoms. Disease activity was assessed via the Disease Activity Score in 28 joints on the basis of C-reactive protein (DAS28-CRP) [18]. The formula for DAS28-CRP was as follows: DAS28-CRP = [0.56 * sqr (TJC)] + [0.28 * sqr (SJC)] + [0.36 * ln (CRP + 1)] + (0.014 * VAS). Clinical remission was defined as DAS28-CRP < 2.6, mild disease activity as 2.6 ≤ DAS28-CRP < 3.2, moderate disease activity as 3.2 ≤ DAS28-CRP ≤ 5.1, and severe disease activity as DAS28-CRP > 5.1.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed via SPSS 26.0. The normal distribution of the data was assessed via the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Quantitative data that conformed to a normal distribution are expressed as the mean and standard deviation (SD) and were compared via a Student’s t-test. Quantitative data that did not conform to a normal distribution are expressed as the median and interquartile range (IQR) and were compared via the Mann‒Whitney U test. Qualitative data are expressed as counts and percentages and were assessed via the Pearson chi-square test or Fisher’s exact probability method. In this cohort, we used exploratory logistic regression to explore factors independently associated with the RA patients with synoviocyte detachment. Significant indicators in the univariable logistic regression analysis along with age and duration were introduced into the multivariable logistic regression model. The results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs). Two-sided P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics of RA patients with and without synoviocyte detachment

The majority of the cohort were women, accounting for 89.1% of the 55 participants (Table 1). The mean age at the time of synovial biopsy was 53.4 ± 11.8 years, with a median disease duration of 72 months (interquartile range [IQR] 24–132). Synoviocyte detachment was observed in 9 out of 55 patients (16.4%). This detachment is characterized by the synovial lining layer thinning, synoviocyte reduction, and a loose, single-layer arrangement or complete disappearance (Fig. 2A-F). There were no significant differences between the synoviocyte detachment and no-synoviocyte detachment group in terms of sex, age, disease duration, or X-ray staging. However, X-ray changes (Fig. 2G) indicated instances of synoviocyte detachment in the joints of RA patients, even in the early to middle stages. Morever, RA patients in the synoviocyte detachment group presented significantly higher levels of RF, ESR, CRP, and DAS28-CRP values than did those in the no-synoviocyte detachment group (P < 0.05).

Comparison of clinical and synovial histopathological characteristics between RA patients with synoviocyte detachment and those with synoviocyte proliferation

A total of 31 (56.4%) patients exhibited synovial cell proliferation, whereas 9 (16.4%) exhibited synoviocyte detachment. There were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of sex, age, disease duration, or X-ray staging. However, RA patients in the synoviocyte detachment group presented significantly greater RF, ESR, CRP, and DAS28-CRP levels than did those in the synoviocyte proliferation group (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3A-D). In terms of synovial histopathological changes, RA patients with synoviocyte detachment displayed a substantial reduction in synovial infiltration of CD3 and CD20 cells than those with synovial cell proliferation (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3E-H).

Factors influencing synoviocyte detachment in RA patients

Univariable and multivariable binary logistic regression models were employed to identify potential factors influencing synoviocyte detachment in RA patients. Firstly, important indicators with P values < 0.05 were identified in univariate logistic regression, including ESR, CRP, DAS28-CRP, and neovascularization. Since CRP is included in DAS28-CRP, we omited CRP and used DAS28-CRP for multivariate analysis. Due to the significant linear correlation between ESR, DAS28-CRP, and neovascularization, their individual effects may be diluted or masked when included simultaneously in a regression model. Therefore, we adopted a adjusted analysis strategy. We separately included ESR, DAS28-CRP, and neovascularization in the multivariable logistic regression model, adjusting for age and disease duration. The results revealed that elevated levels of ESR (OR 1.028; 95% CI 1.002–1.055, P = 0.033), DAS28-CRP (OR 3.061; 95% CI 1.094–8.562, P = 0.033), and synovial neovascularization (OR 5.241; 95% CI 1.220–22.508, P = 0.026), significantly influenced for synoviocyte detachment in RA patients (Table 3).

Discussion

The synovial membrane is characteristically slender with a thickness of approximately 5 mm and embodies a mesenchymal framework consisting of both a lining and a sublining layer. The synovial lining in a healthy joint is typically 20–40 μm thick and comprises 1–3 layers of specialized cells known as synoviocytes, which are embedded within an extracellular matrix [5]. However, RA dramatically alters this landscape by causing an inflammatory cascade that triggers excessive proliferation of synovial cells and causes the synovial lining to thicken to 6–10 layers of cells. This proliferation is largely driven by the action of inflammatory cytokines action and neovascularization within the synovial tissue [19, 20].

RA causes the accumulation of immune cells that release a wide array of cytokines and proteases [21]. These biochemical mediators, such as TNF-α and IL-6, induce cell cycle arrest and apoptosis, thereby promoting synovial cell proliferation [22]. Furthermore, these inflammatory processes initiate and sustain angiogenesis, or the formation of new blood vessels in the sublayer [23]. This neovascularization is critical for supplying oxygen and nutrients to the expanding population of synovial cells, further fueling synovial cell proliferation and exacerbating joint destruction. Studies have shown that inhibiting angiogenesis reduces synovial cell proliferation, suggesting that angiogenesis significantly contributes to synovial cells proliferation in RA [24].

Thickening of the lining layer and proliferation of synovial cells represent the most common pathological manifestations of RA synovitis. This feature was present in 31 out of 55 RA patients (56.4%) in this study. However, approximately 15% − 20% of RA patients exhibited thinning of the synovial lining layer, synovial cells reduction, and a sparse single-layer arrangement. We have termed this phenomenon ‘synoviocyte detachment’. Although synoviocyte detachment is often considered a pathological occurrence in the late stages of RA [10] that is referred to as ‘burnt synovium’, our analysis of joint X-ray manifestations during synovial biopsy indicated that synoviocyte detachment is also present in the joints of RA patients in the early and middle stages. Notably, there was no significant difference in X-ray staging between the groups with and without synoviocyte detachment in our study. These findings suggest that synoviocyte detachment may not be solely a late-stage manifestation following synovial cell proliferation but instead is a distinct subtype parallel to synovial cell proliferation. Due to the lack of X-ray staging data for 8 patients, the sample size included in the final X-ray staging statistical analysis was relatively small, which serves as an exploratory result.

We observed that RA patients with synoviocyte detachment presented higher levels of autoantibodies (RF) and activity indicators (ESR, CRP, and DAS28-CRP) than those without synoviocyte detachment. Logistic regression analysis demonstrated that the ESR and DAS28-CRP are risk factors influencing synoviocyte detachment. Therefore, synoviocyte detachment is a result of heightened systemic inflammation, and the degree of inflammation may even surpass that of the synovial cell proliferation group.

Notably RA patients with synoviocyte detachment presented greater systemic inflammation (Fig. 3A-D) but demonstrate less synovial inflammation in local synovial tissue (Fig. 3E-H) than those in the synovial cell proliferation group. Systemic inflammation in synoviocyte detachment was inconsistent with local inflammation, and the reason for this finding is not clear. Some studies have proposed the ‘burnt out’ hypothesis, suggesting that it may result from inflammatory destruction. However, the underlying mechanisms are not fully understood, especially when this phenomenon occurs in patients with late stage RA.

The underlying mechanism causing synoviocyte detachment has not been documented in the literature, despite numerous studies on synovial cell proliferation mechanisms. Based on the mechanisms of synovial cell proliferation, we speculate that synoviocyte detachment may occur due to a relative decrease in immune cell infiltration and cytokine release, leading to the absence of factors promoting synovial proliferation. However, we are uncertain whether synoviocyte detachment is associated with a deficiency in cytokines. The conclusion can be made that synoviocyte detachment and synovial cell proliferation involve entirely different pathological mechanisms.

We observed significant neovascularization in the synovium of patients with synoviocyte detachment, and logistic regression analysis revealed a notable correlation between neovascularization and synoviocyte detachment. While previous studies have established an association between angiogenesis and synovial cell proliferation [25, 26], our research suggests that the link between synoviocyte detachment and neovascularization may be even stronger than that between synovial cell proliferation and neovascularization. The pathological mechanisms of neovascularization are likely to differ considerably between the synoviocyte detachment and synovial cell proliferation groups. In the synovial proliferation group, synovial neovascularization was driven predominantly by synovial inflammatory cells, which secrete proangiogenic factors that stimulate endothelial cells and fibroblasts to produce vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF), and other angiogenesis-promoting factors [23, 27, 28]. However, the synovial tissue lacked inflammatory cells in the synoviocyte detachment group, thus making it challenging to attribute synovial neovascularization solely to synovial inflammatory cells. To date, no specific mechanisms related to this phenomenon have been reported. Therefore, we can only speculate that RA patients with a distinct subset of synoviocyte detachment may possess unique pathological mechanisms within the synovium.

In summary, our findings reveal, for the first time, that approximately 15–20% of RA patients experience synoviocyte detachment. Additionally, we have observed significant neovascularization within the synovium of these patients. Systemic inflammation and disease activity appear to be more severe in those with synoviocyte detachment. Conversely, local synovial inflammatory cell infiltration is comparatively lower than in individuals with synovial cell proliferation. Further exploration of these findings at the genetic and cellular molecular levels may reveal novel pathological mechanisms and therapeutic targets.

Our study had several limitations that warrant consideration. As a single-center, small-sample, retrospective study, selection bias was potentially present. Therefore, the results of logistic regression analysis of the factors associated with synoviocyte must be interpreted with caution. Further validation of our results with a larger sample size and further exploration of related mechanisms causing RA are still needed.
