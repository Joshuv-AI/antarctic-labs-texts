Physicochemical and structural properties of myofibrillar proteins in abalone (Haliotis discus hannai): Effects of pulsed electric fields
Authors: Zhao-Ming Lin, Hai-Hong Wu, Jia-Yin Huang, Kang Liu, Min-Jie Cao, Duanquan Lin, Le-Chang Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study explored how pulsed electric field (PEF) treatment at voltages of 10 − 30 kV affects the properties of abalone myofibrillar protein (MP). As voltage increased, compared with control, solubility, fragmentation index, carbonyl content of MP, and ozone content in solution kept rising, while Ca2+-/Mg2+-ATPase activities decreased. At 10 kV, the solubility, total and reactive sulfhydryl group, surface hydrophobicity, zeta-potential, and particle size of MP peaked. PEF treatment altered the apparent viscosity but minimally affected the temperature ramp of MP with optimized dispersion and minimized aggregation. In addition, its protein molecular weight distribution was unchanged by PEF treatment. The secondary structures of MP showed an increase in the α-helix content, a decrease in the β-fold, β-turn and random curl and an increase in the disulfide bonding after PEF treatment. Overall PEF treatment significantly changed the physicochemical and structural properties of abalone MPs, providing theoretical guidance for PEF in abalone processing.

Introduction

Abalone (Haliotis discus hannai) is a type of coastal marine herbivorous mollusk with delicious taste and high nutrition. It has been reported that abalone has many physiological benefits including fatigue recovery, refection, detoxification (Kim et al., 2008). It is usually eaten fresh as a highly valued seafood by Chinese residents, but in non-coastal areas, most of them can only eat frozen abalone. Low-temperature freezing is the main preservation method for long-distance transportation to slow down deterioration of abalone. However, protein denaturation, lipid oxidation, and changes in functional and gelling properties are the problems that can occur during abalone freezing (Zhang et al., 2022). Compared with freezing, refrigeration storage reduces protein denaturation and tissue damage because no ice crystals are formed during this process. However, due to microorganism enrichment, abalone stored in refrigerated conditions is highly susceptible to spoilage.

As a cost-effective and environment-friendly technology, pulsed electric field (PEF) has been demonstrated to improve food quality in refrigeration. PEF not only kills microorganisms by electric breakdown cell membrane but applies the extra electric field forces. These physical forces can induce orientation and perturbation of targeted amino acid residues, bend, and exposure of active atoms/residues along the carbon backbone, incipient induction regarding the unfolding and realignment state of protein to enhance hydrophobic interaction for aggregation was encouraged (Hu et al., 2024). The application of PEF in food preservation has been reported in raw meat, fruit, vegetable and aquatic products, as well as improving extraction rates and accelerating fermentation (Dziadek et al., 2019; Manurung, Sulaeman, Kurniadi, Suharto, and IEEE, 2021; Zhang et al., 2022). Currently, most of the PEF research in aquatic products focuses on the extraction of substances and the thawing time. There are few studies on the changes in protein in aquatic products during PEF treatment, and the high protein content of aquatic products necessitates further investigation.

Myofibrillar protein (MP), one of the major proteins in abalone muscle. There is a close relationship between MP and physicochemical properties, such as muscle contraction, texture, and water holding capacity. During the PEF treatment, proteins undergo conformational changes, due to stretching and elongation, that may influence their functional characteristics. Recent studies have demonstrated that PEF processing has different effects on MP depending on species and treatment conditions. For example, PEF treatment of MP in pale, soft, and exudative (PSE)-like chicken breast meat, led to significant changes in the tertiary and secondary structures of MP, exposing the sulfhydryl groups and improving solubility; the increase in α-helix content enhanced gel strength, indicating improved functional properties (Dong et al., 2020). However, the surface hydrophobicity and rheological properties of porcine MP decreased after PEF treatment with different times (0, 1, 5, 15, and 30 min) at an electric field strength of 2.1 kV/cm using exponential decay pulses with a pulse width of 100 μs and a pulse frequency of 5 Hz at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C) (Y. J. Kim, & D. M. Shin et al., 2024). For venison, PEF treatment showed no significant effect on the shear force and myofibrillar fragmentation index, but an increase in calpain activity and proteolysis (Bhat et al., 2019). These findings indicated different effects of PEF among MP in various species. PEF generates ozone during high-voltage discharge, a reactive species that oxidizes proteins via amino acid modification and peptide chain cleavage (Wang et al., 2025). This ozone-induced protein oxidation has not been thoroughly explored in a marine matrix like abalone. Therefore, studying the effect of PEF on MP in abalone can help to understand the effect of PEF on the quality of abalone.

In this study, Haliotis discus hannai was taken as the research object to investigate the effects of PEF on physicochemical and structural properties of abalone MP under different voltages, in order to expand the application of PEF in the field of aquatic food processing and contribute to the process of the industrialization of the use of PEF.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

Live cultured abalones (Haliotis discus hannai) with an average body weight of 80–90 g were purchased from a local aquaculture market in Xiamen, China, and then transported to the laboratory alive in clean oxygenated water. All other chemicals were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China).

Extraction of abalone myofibrillar proteins

Haliotis discus hannai is a widely cultivated edible shellfish in China, which is not an endangered or specially protected species. All methods used in this study were conducted in accordance with the NIH (National Research Council) guide for the care and use of laboratory animals.

Abalones were exposed and immersed in ice/water slurry (temperature = 0 ± 1 °C) until no prolegs movements were observed. Then, the hepatopancreas of abalone was carefully peeled under low temperature to obtain abalone muscle.

The following operations were performed at 4 °C. Abalone muscle was obtained from fresh abalone after shucking and gutting, followed by cutting into small pieces. The muscle tissues were then homogenized with 10 volumes of 20 mmol/L Tris-HCl and (pH 7.0). After filtering the unmashed tissues and fascia using gauze, the supernatant was collected by centrifugation at 4 °C using a centrifuge (Avanti J-26S XP, Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA). After centrifugation at 8000 ×g for 15 min, the supernatant was discarded and the upper soft part of the precipitate was collected. The precipitate was then mixed with 10 volumes of 20 mmol/L Tris-HCl (pH 7.0), followed by centrifuging at 8000 ×g for 15 min (4 °C), and then the upper soft fraction of precipitate was collected after discarding the supernatant. This step was repeated twice. The resulted precipitate is abalone myofibrillar proteins.

PEF treatment

Abalone myofibrillar proteins were dissolved in 20 mmol/L Tris-HCl with 0.5 mol/L NaCl (pH 7.0), and the protein concentration was adjusted to 3 mg/mL. Then 600 mL aliquot of the protein solution was transferred into the PEF treatment chamber (MSZGYC02-Smart, Lannuo Optoelectornic Technology Co., Ltd., Changzhou, China) which was pre-cleaned with deionized water and cooled at 4 °C. The protein solution was treated at different voltages (0 kV, 10 kV, 20 kV, and 30 kV), with a frequency of 10 kHz, a pulse width of 60 milliseconds, using square pulse for 60 s.

Physicochemical properties of myofibrillar protein

Solubility

The protein solubility was measured according to the method described by (Dong et al., 2020) with slight modification. Myofibrillar protein samples after different voltage treatments were diluted to a protein concentration of 1 mg/mL using 20 mmol/L Tris-HCl with 0.5 mol/L NaCl (pH 7.0) and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant protein concentration and uncentrifuged protein concentration were then measured using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) as the following Eq. Measurement of ozone content

Using an ozone test kit (Huankai Microbial, Guangdong, China) and referring to instructions, the myofibrillar protein solution was applied by aspirating 15 mL immediately after PEF treatment. Measure the blank value using an equal volume of untreated 20 mmol/L Tris-HCl with 0.5 mol/L NaCl (pH 7.0) and subtract it. The relative values of ozone content was determined by UV–vis at 525 nm.

Carbonyl content

According to (Zhang et al., 2015) with some modifications. After adding 1 mL 1.0 mg/mL of myofibrillar protein to 1 mL10 mM/L of 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (DNPH) (solubilized using 2 mol/L of HCl), the reaction was carried out for 1 h, protected from light, followed by the addition of 1 mL 20% TCA to terminate reaction. Centrifugation at 10,000 ×g and 4 °C for 10 min was performed to obtain the precipitate. The precipitate was washed three times with ethanol: ethyl acetate mixture (v:v = 1:1) to remove excess DNPH, then it was added to 3 mL of 6 mol/L guanidine hydrochloride and incubated at 37 °C for 20 min, followed by centrifugation to obtain the supernatant, and the absorbance was measured at 370 nm. The carbonyl content was calculated using an extinction coefficient of 22,400/M/cm and reported as nanomole carbonyl per milligram protein.

Myofibrillar fragmentation index (MFI)

The determination of the myofibrillar fragmentation index was performed according to Culler's methods (Culler, Parrish, Smith, and Cross, 1978). Myofibrillar protein samples under different voltage treatments were diluted using 20 mmol/L Tris-HCl with 0.5 mol/L NaCl (pH 7.0) to the protein concentration of 0.5 mg/mL, and the absorbance was measured at 540 nm by spectrophotometer (759 s, Lengguang technology Ltd., Shanghai, China). The MFI was calculated as the following Eq. (2):(2)MFI = A540 × 150

A540: absorbance at 540 nm.

Ca2+ − ATPase and Mg2+ −ATPase activities measurement

Ca2+-ATPase and Mg2+-ATPase activities were determined according to the method described by (Lee et al., 2007). Ca2+-ATPase activities of PEF-treated myofibrillar protein were measured in a reaction mixture containing 5 mmol/L CaCl2, 100 mmol/L KCl, 25 mmol/L Tris-maleate (pH 7.0), 1 mmol/L ATP and 0.2 mg myofibrillar protein, with reacting at 25 °C for 10 min, then the reaction was terminated after adding 20% trichloroacetic acid (TCA) to the final concentration of 5%. Similarly, Mg2+-ATPase activities were measured in a reaction mixture containing 2 mmol/L MgCl2, 100 mmol/L KCl, 25 mmol/L Tris-maleate (pH 7.0), 1 mmol/L ATP and 0.2 mg myofibrillar protein, and other conditions were the same. Ca2+-ATPase activity and Mg2+-ATPase activity were expressed as the amount of inorganic phosphorus produced per minute.

Zeta potential and particle size measurement

The zeta-potential and particle size of the particles were measured, according to the methods described by (Liu et al., 2020). The protein concentration was adjusted to 1 mg/mL to avoid multiple scattering. A nanoparticle size and potential analyzer (NS-90Z, Omec, Zhuhai, China) was used. The measurement temperature was 25 °C, and the equilibration time was 60 s. Each sample was scanned 11 times, and the scanning was repeated 3 times. The equilibrium time for zeta potential measurements was 60 s, the measurement temperature was 25 °C, and each sample was scanned after three repetitions of 20 scans per round.

Apparent viscosity and temperature ramp

The apparent viscosity and temperature ramp were measured, according to the method described in our previous study (Lin et al., 2024). A hybrid rheometer (Discovery HR-2, TA Instruments, USA) was used. The concentration of the myofibrillar protein solution was adjusted to 50 mg/mL, and the sample was loaded in the center of the sample stage, using a 40 mm parallel aluminum plate fixture with a spacing of 1 mm. The experimental temperature was 25 °C, and a shear rate was set from 0.1 s−1 to 100 s−1, and the variation of the apparent viscosity (η) with the shear rate (γ) was recorded. Similarly, silicone oil was used to seal the gap between the sample and the fixture to prevent water evaporation during the heating process of the measurement. The measurement temperature range was 20–90 °C, the heating rate was 2 °C/min, the oscillation frequency was 0.1 Hz, the strain was 1%, and the G' of the sample was recorded.

Sodium dodecyl sulfate polypropylene gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE)

SDS-PAGE was performed as described by (Laemmli, 1970). 1 mg/mL protein samples were dissolved at a 3:1 ratio with an volume of 4 × SDS sample buffer (20 mL 1 mol/L Tris HCI (pH 6.8), 8 g SDS, 0.4 g bromophenol blue, 20 mL glycerol, dilute to 100 mL with ultrapure water) and then heated at 95 °C for 10 min. Reduced SDS-PAGE with additional 1.5% β-mercaptoethanol. 8 μL of the sample was applied to a 15% polypropylene separator gel. After sampling, electrophoresis was performed using an electrophoresis apparatus (Mini-PROTEIN, Bio-Rad, USA) with a constant current of 15 mA. After electrophoresis, the gel was stained with dye, decolorized with water, and photographed and analyzed by a gel imager (G: BOX, Sungene, England).

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

Surface morphological characteristics were observed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Phenom-World Pro, Eindhoven, Netherlands). The concentration of MP solution was adjusted to 50 mg/mL, and it was filled as in a 10 mL beaker, avoiding air bubbles when filling. The gel was formed by heating at 90 °C for 30 min and placed at 4 °C overnight. The gels were cut into thin slices of 2–3 mm thickness, and the slices were flooded with 2.5% glutaraldehyde and fixed for 24 h. The fixed slices were washed with buffer solution 3 times, each time for 10 min. After that, the washed slices were subjected to gradient dehydration using 20% - 100% ethanol. The dehydrated gel was dried in a fume hood for 24 h. Then, samples were sputter sprayed with gold and microscopically observed at a beam addition voltage of 5 kV and standard beam current. The fractal dimension was calculated using Image J (1.54f, National Institues of Health, USA). The image was first converted to 8-bit binary, and then Fractal Box Count in Tools was used to get the fractal dimension (D).

Structure of myofibrillar protein

Surface hydrophobicity

The surface hydrophobicity of myofibrillar protein samples was determined using 8-anilino-1-naphthalene sulfonic acid (ANS) as a fluorescent probe, according to the method described by (Xu et al., 2018). After adjusting protein concentration to 0.1 mg/mL, 20 μL of 8 mmol/L ANS was added and mixed thoroughly, and then the reaction was carried out for 20 min at room temperature in the dark. After centrifuging at 12000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, the fluorescence intensity of the supernatant was measured using 390 excitation wavelength, 470 emission wavelength and 10 nm slit width by a fluorescence spectrophotometer (FP-8200, Jasco, Japan).

Total and reactive sulfhydryl content

Total and reactive sulfhydryl groups were determined according to (Chen et al., 2019) with minor modifications. After adjusting the protein concentration to 1 mg/mL, 0.5 mL was taken and added to 2 mL of 20 mmol/L Tris-HCl containing 0.5 mol/L NaCl and 8 mol/L urea, pH 7.0. After shaking well, 0.5 mL of 10 mmol/L DNTB was added, and the reaction was kept away from light for 25 min for the determination of the total sulfhydryl groups. Similarly, 0.5 mL of protein samples was taken and added into 2 mL of 20 mmol/L Tris-HCl containing 0.5 mol/L NaCl (pH 7.0). After shaking well, 0.5 mL of 10 mmol/L DNTB was then added, followed by reacting for 25 min away from light to determine the active sulfhydryl groups. The sulfhydryl content was calculated as the following Eq. (3):(3)Sulfhydryl contentnmol/mg=A×n×106ε×ρ

A: absorbance at 412 nm; n: total protein dilution; ε: molar extinction coefficient 13,600 M−1 cm−1; ρ: protein mass, mg.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR)

The FT-IR was determined according to the method described by (Zhang et al., 2023). Myofibrillar proteins were freeze-dried, and samples were mixed with dried potassium bromide, 1:100 (w/w), followed by grounding into powder. The samples were then scanned on the fourier infrared spectroscopy instrument (Vector-22, Bruker, Switzerland) after making thin slices. The scanning range was 4000–400 cm−1, resolution was 4 cm−1, and the number of scans was 16 to obtain the myofibrillar protein infrared spectrogram. Using PeakFit v4.12, Gaussian fitting and deconvolution were performed on the 1600–1700 cm−1 peaks of the spectra to obtain the area occupied by each peak, and then ratios of secondary structure of myofibrillar protein were calculated.

Raman spectroscopy

According to (Wang et al., 2024) with some changes, the protein concentration was adjusted to 1 mg/mL, and 8 μL drops were aspirated onto tin foil for measurement by Raman spectroscopic instruments (RMS1000, OCEANHOOD, Shanghai, China). The power was set to 300 mW, the integration time was 3000 ms, and the Raman shift range was 2000–400 cm−1.

Far-UV CD spectra analysis

Far-UV CD spectra analysis of myosin was carried out using a Chirascan circular dichroism (CD) spectrometer (Applied Photophysics Ltd., Surrey, UK), according to a method described by Dong et al. (2020). MP samples were adjusted to 0.1 mg/mL and placed in a Hellma 0.5-mm light path microcell. Samples were measured from 200 to 260 nm at 25 °C with operating parameters of scan rate at 100 nm/min, interval of 0.25 s, and bandwidth of 1.0 nm. The CD spectra of buffer were averaged and set as baseline and subtracted from the average spectra of the samples. The measurement was performed in triplets.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were carried out in triplicate. One-way ANOVA was carried out with IBM SPSS Statistics 27. The difference between least-squares means was determined using Duncan's multiple range test (p < 0.05). Origin 2024 was used to make graphs.

Results and discussion

Solubility and surface hydrophobicity

Solubility is one of the important physicochemical properties of proteins and is also the basis for other functional properties, such as emulsification and foaming. The solubility of proteins is determined by their hydrophobic and hydrophilic groups. As shown in Fig. 1A, there was a significant effect of voltage intensity on the solubility of the myofibrillar protein. After pulsed electric field treatment, solubility of MPs were all higher than those of the untreated group (68.34%), suggesting that proteins undergo different degrees of elongation and aggregation during PEF processing. As shown in Fig. 1B, surface hydrophobicity of MP increased and then decreased, probably because that under physicochemical stresses, protein structure altered, affecting its spatial structure, and then the redistribution of amino acid residues in proteins affected charge distribution, hydrophilicity and hydrophobicity of proteins (Zhao et al., 2014).Fig. 1Effect of PEF on the solubility (A), surface hydrophobicity (B), total (C) and reactive (D) sulfhydryl of abalone MP. The solubility of MP reached the highest at 10 kV (80.02%), which was 17.09% higher than that of the control. When the voltage continued to increase, the solubility of MP showed a decrease. The enhancement of MP solubility with the application of PEF might be because when MP was exposed to a strong electric field, some polar groups absorbed energy and generate free radicals, which could disrupt several interactions between protein molecules and thus enhanced the interaction between protein molecules and water (Taha et al., 2022). When the voltage continued to increase, protein molecules were polarized at low PEF strengths, and as the electric field strength increased, hydrophobic amino acids were exposed to the solvent, and unfolded proteins end up forming aggregates connected by weakly covalent and noncovalent bonds at higher electric field strengths. Similar results were obtained by (Wang et al., 2018), in which HPP combined with low concentrations (< 40 mM) of CaCl2 synergistically increased the solubility and surface hydrophobicity of MP.

Total sulfhydryl and reactive sulfhydryl

Changes in total and reactive sulfhydryl are closely related to the degree of protein food oxidation and denaturation. Reactive sulfhydryl groups are one of the most active groups in proteins and play a crucial role in the functional properties of proteins. The total and active sulfhydryl content of abalone MP after PEF treatment both showed increasing trends and then decreased in Fig. 1C and D. A similar situation was observed in the MP of pork with modified atmosphere packaging treated by ultrasound (Jiang et al., 2022). The active sulfhydryl content decreased when voltage continued to rise. The MP-10 showed a 24.84% increase in total sulfhydryl content and a 14.29% increase in active sulfhydryl content as compared to the control. The PEF treatment induced the generation of free radicals from the protein ammonia polar groups, which could affect intramolecular interactions within protein molecules, including hydrophobic and electrostatic interactions, disulfide bridges, hydrogen bonds, salt bridges, and Van der Waals forces, causing them to change structure (Taha et al., 2022). It indicates that at 10 kV, the protein mainly underwent the phenomenon of unfolding, which allowed the exposure of -SH in amino acid. Ozone and free radicals in solution could cause highly exposed sulfhydryl to form cystines with disulfide bonds by oxidizing two neighboring Cys. Cys could be oxidized by ozone to form cysteine sulfonic acid (Cys-SO3H) and cysteine sulfinic acid (Cys-S(=O) OH) (Fang et al., 2021). These reactions intensified with the increased voltage, resulting in a decrease in total and active sulfhydryl content values at voltages higher than 10 kV.

Ozone content, carbonyl content of MP and MFI

Ozone is a substance produced by oxygen under high-voltage discharge conditions. It has been reported that the by-products of pulsed electric arc discharge at atmospheric pressure are gaseous ozone, hydrogen peroxide and nitrate ions (Masaoka, 2007). Ozone dissolved in solution has the highest absorption peak at 525 nm under the reaction with N, N-diethyl-p-phenylenediamine (DPD). As shown in Fig. 2A, the absorbance of the solution at 525 nm increased with increasing voltage, indicating that ozone content in the solution was rising. Ozone molecules had dipolar electrophilic and nucleophilic properties, so the oxidative degradation of proteins by ozone involves both direct and indirect oxidation. Ozone oxidation led to changes in protein conformation, resulting from amino acid modification, peptide chain breakage and cross-linking of proteins (Kelly & Mudway, 2003). Oxidation also led to protein aggregation, which affected the gel and water-holding properties of proteins (Zhang et al., 2015). Research has revealed that during pulsed electric field treatment of conductive samples, the anode undergoes electrochemical oxidation of water. Through a multi-step electron transfer process, multiple reactive oxygen species (ROS) are generated—including (OH∙, O∙∙, and HO∙). These ROS interact to form 1O₂. These electrogenerated ROS oxidize proteins in solution (such as bovine serum albumin), attacking their aliphatic amino acid side chains, triggering radical chain reactions, and causing substantial oxidative damage including protein carbonylation (Vahalová et al., 2023).Fig. 2Effect of PEF on the ozone content in abalone MP solutions (A), carbonyl content (B) and MFI (C) of abalone MP. Functional groups in the side chains of amino acids are easily oxidized to form carbonyl compounds, so protein carbonyl content is often used to express the degree of protein oxidation. As shown in Fig. 2B, the carbonyl content of MP increased from 2.03 nmol/mg to 4.43 nmol/mg as the treatment voltage increased from 0 V to 30 kV, indicating that the PEF treatment could lead to protein oxidation and the degree of oxidation increased with the increasing of treatment voltage. This might be resulted from the increase in ozone dissolved in the solution as the voltage increases. Ozone dissolved in water to form ozonated water, which was unstable and produced very strong oxidizing agents, including molecular oxygen (O) and hydroxyl (OH) units. These radicals could make a nucleophilic attack on the methylene carbon of proteins and could capture the hydrogen on the carbon to initiate a radical chain reaction, resulting in the formation of a large number of carbonyl groups (Gonçalves, 2009). Similar results were obtained by (Liu et al., 2021), in which MP of grass carp was rinsed by ozone water, and there was a positive association between the degree of MP oxidation and the concentration of ozone in water.

MFI represents the integrity of myofibrillar proteins, or the degree of MP degradation, with higher MFI values representing a greater degree of MP impairment (Wang et al., 2024). Fig. 2C showed the variation of MFI values of abalone MP under different PEF voltage treatments. The MFI of samples were positively correlated with the treatment voltage. At the end of the PEF treatment, the MFI of MP increased significantly to 20.3 (10 kV), 26.45 (20 kV), and 32.1 (30 kV). This suggested that PEF treatment under these conditions significantly harmed the structural integrity of MP. This was in contrast to previous studies in which the effect of PEF on MFI in venison was insignificant (Bhat et al., 2019; Bhat, Morton, Mason, and Bekhit, 2019), which might be due to differences between PEF processing units, as well as the way samples were handled. Previous researchers had treated dairy beef and venison whole for PEF and placed the treatment chamber electrodes in direct contact with the sample, and this operation reduced the air content between gaps and the ozone content in the sample. The samples treated in this study were abalone MP solutions and there was a 1 cm air gap between the electrodes and the liquid surface. This induced ozone production and dissolution in the MP solution. Ozone caused the oxidation of protein-peptide backbones, peptide bond breaks, protein cross-linking and a range of amino acid side chain modifications (Kelly & Mudway, 2003).The attraction of positive and negative PEF poles to the amino acids at the protein band sites leads to molecular polarization and rearrangement (Giteru et al., 2018). The synergistic effect of these two factors might be responsible for the increase in MFI values of abalone MP.

Ca2+-ATPase and Mg2+-ATPase activities

The Ca2+-ATPase activity is related to the globular head structure in myosin, and its magnitude reflects the degree of protein aggregation and degeneration, and actin-stimulated Mg2+-ATPase activity appears to play a role in the interaction between myosin and actin (Lee et al., 2007). Fig. 3A and B show the activities of Ca2+-ATPase and Mg2+-ATPase of abalone MP, respectively. Both Ca2+-ATPase and Mg2+-ATPase of MP decreased significantly with increasing voltage under different PEF voltage treatments. The activity of the Ca2+-ATPase decreased from 0.0227 μmol (pi)/mg (pro)/min (0 kV) to 0.0218 μmol (pi)/mg (pro)/min (30 kV). Its activity decreased with increasing voltage at 0 kV, 10 kV, and 20 kV, with no significant difference in activity after treatment at 20 kV and 30 kV (p > 0.05). Mg2+-ATPase activity decreased from 0.137 μmol (pi)/mg (pro)/min (0 kV) to 0.118 μmol (pi)/mg (pro)/min (30 kV), with enzyme activity declining as voltage increased. It had been previously shown that PEF has an inactivating effect on biological enzymes (Naliyadhara et al., 2022). In particular, the enzyme was an amphipathic biomolecule characterized by a dipole moment and a net charge that was sensitive to external electric fields, which led to varying degrees of alteration in its functional properties (Wang, Li, et al., 2018). During treatment in the PEF unit, the electric field effect and the strong oxidizing effect of ozone combined to change structure of the Ca2+-ATPase and Mg2+-ATPase, leading to a decrease in enzyme activities. 3.4 Zeta-potential and particle size

When the protein is dispersed in the solvent, the intermolecular electrostatic repulsion is an important force to maintain the dispersion stability of the solution. Fig. 4A showed that the zeta potentials of abalone MP after PEF treatment were all higher than those of the control, and their absolute values showed an increasing and then decreasing trend. Similar results were obtained by (Kim & Shin et al., 2024), which showed that zeta potential of porcine MP was increased during short time PEF treatment (< 15 min), and then decreased under 30 min. Among them, the absolute value of the zeta potential of MP-10 was the highest, indicating that its dispersion in solution was the most stable. It also demonstrates the best solubility after 10 kV treatment. In this case, a large number of homophilic charges repel each other, and the repulsive force decreased the occurrence of molecular aggregation and reduced the hydrophobic groups to be buried, which made the hydrophobic groups more exposed to the surface, resulting in the increase of hydrophobicity on the protein surface (Zhang et al., 2017). Rheology behavior

The apparent viscosities of abalone MP treated with different voltages are shown in Fig. 5A. Except for the control, which showed uniform shear thinning, all the treated MP showed shear thickening behavior followed by shear thinning. The speed of shear thinning was MP-30 > MP-20 > MP-10 in descending order. Myosin was an important component in MP and was considered to be an important part of MP gel formation and rheological properties. Since myosin tails exhibited a typical rod-modified chain structure, which was oriented to move under shear force (Guo et al., 2019). At lower shear rates, the rod-like tails of myosin were prone to overlap and entanglement, generating high viscosity through the spatial site resistance and internal friction of the protein chain. When the shear rate increased, the entanglement and overlap between myosin molecular chains were destroyed, and asymmetrically dispersed molecules were more likely to be aligned with the shear surface, thus weakening the friction and leading to a decrease in apparent viscosity (Villamonte et al., 2015).Fig. 5Effect of PEF on apparent viscosity (A) and temperature ramp (B) of abalone MP solution. In (A), the abscissa variable was shear rate (s−1, 0.1–100 s−1) and the ordinate was apparent viscosity (mPa·s); In (B), the abscissa variable was temperature (°C, 20–90 °C) and the ordinate was storage modulus G' (Pa). All experiments were performed in triplicate.Fig. 5

However, after further PEF treatment, myofibrillar proteins were fragmented, meanwhile, intact and fragmented myofibrillar proteins were presented in the MP solution. Under low-speed shear, the MP was also subjected to the positional obstruction and friction of the fragmented MP in the space. The distribution of the fragmented MP was irregular, and they existed in the interspaces between the entangled and overlapped intact MP, which hindered the relative motion, thus leading to an increase in viscosity at low shear speeds. It had been reported that a mixture of proteins with different structures hinders the movement between the proteins (Onwulata et al., 2014). As the shear rate increases, both intact and fragmented MP are more likely to align with the shear plane, thus reducing friction and decreasing apparent viscosity. Since the number of intact proteins in the treated MP was lower than that in the control group, the apparent viscosity decreased at a faster rate than control group.

The G´ reflects the energy stored during heat-induced gel formation. A higher G´ indicates better protein gelation ability, tighter internal structure, and improved elasticity. In the temperature ramp results (Fig. 5B) realistically, though the MFI kept rising in treatment, there was not much difference in G' between the control and treated groups after gelation. Gel-forming properties of surimi were influenced by salt-soluble muscle proteins, hydrophilic and hydrophobic interactions, and reactive sulfhydryl content during the milling process (Zhang et al., 2015). Enhanced interactions of structural domains and the formation of disulfide bonds contribute to the increase in G'. Some degree of oxidation exposed more sulfhydryl groups, improved surface hydrophobicity, increased disulfide bonding content, and facilitated gel formation. Raman spectroscopy and surface hydrophobicity results indicate that treated MP exhibits increased disulfide bonds and high surface hydrophobicity, which enhances the gel network structure. Fragmented MP may serve as a filler for gel network gaps. These factors may balance the disruption of gel integrity caused by MP fragmentation, hence G' remains unaffected.

Non-reduced and reduced SDS-PAGE

Fig. 6A shows the non-reduced SDS-PAGE of abalone MP after treatment with different voltages of PEF. The results showed that PEF had almost no effect on the molecular weight of abalone MP, which was similar to that of a previous study that found no significant band changes in PSE-like chicken breast meat MP as the intensity and frequency of the PEF treatment increased (Dong et al., 2020). However, it was worth noting that the protein staining on the loading well of lanes 2, 3, and 4 was gradually enhanced with the increase of the treatment voltage, which proved that the protein aggregation phenomenon occurred with the increase of voltage. The deepening of the bands between 35 and 45 kDa might be due to the slight oxidation of MP (Fang et al., 2021). The reduced SDS-PAGE of abalone MP after different PEF treatments was shown in Fig. 6B. With the addition of β-mercaptoethanol, the disulfide bonds in abalone MP were broken and the protein aggregation phenomenon on the loading wall was weakened, so it could be deduced that the main force for the aggregation phenomenon of MP after PEF treatment was the disulfide bonds. Though disulfide bonds play a primary role, minor contributions from hydrophobic interactions and electrostatic forces cannot be excluded. As discussed in Section 3.1, PEF treatment initially exposes hydrophobic amino acid residues, and at higher voltages, unfolded protein molecules may undergo hydrophobic association as a secondary cross-linking mechanism. Additionally, Section 3.4 shows that zeta potential decreases at voltages >10 kV, which reduces the electrostatic barrier between MP molecules and facilitates weak electrostatic attraction. Together, these observations indicate that MP aggregation is a synergistic result of dominant disulfide bonding and minor hydrophobic/electrostatic interactions.

Structural characteristics of MP

Tertiary structure of MP

Surface hydrophobicity (H0) is an indicator of the number of active sites on the surface of a protein and is associated with the formation and stabilization of protein tertiary structures (Dong, Li, et al., 2017). The surface hydrophobicity of all PEF-treated abalone MPs increased, indicating that more hydrophobic groups were exposed by the PEF treatment, as described in Fig. 1B. The H0 of MP treated with 10 kV voltage was the highest, which was attributed to the fact that the MP unfolded under this intensity, resulting in the reaction of the fluorescent probe ANS with the previously embedded hydrophobic groups, which increased the H0 (Dong et al., 2020). However, with the increase of voltage, the H0 decreased, which was probably due to the change of electrostatic interactions caused by the voltage change, and the aggregation of the unfolded proteins occurred, which re-embedded the exposed hydrophobic groups, reducing the reaction with ANS and the hydrophobicity decrease (Liu et al., 2018; Zhao et al., 2014).

Raman spectroscopy (RS) is a type of scattering spectroscopy caused by molecular vibrations and rotations. Currently, RS is considered a very promising technique for studying the spatial structure of biomolecules, especially biomolecules in solution. Because the environment of peptide bonds (ammonia bonds) is a reflection of the backbone conformation of proteins, the specific vibrational bands of peptide bonds can be used as indicators to infer the backbone conformation of proteins (Li, 2012). The phenylalanine displays a characteristic Raman peak at wavelengths 1003–1006 cm−1, which can be used for normalization of protein Raman spectra, due to the respiratory vibration of the ring and the insensitivity of this Raman band to conformation or microenvironment. Usually, the bands located near the wavelengths of 500–550 cm−1 are identified as vibrations of disulfide bond stretching. In Fig. 8C, after normalizing the characteristic peaks of disulfide bonding and β-fold, the I540 /I1003 of the control, MP-10, MP-20, and MP-30 were 0.913, 1.58, 1.69, and 1.84, respectively, which indicated that the disulfide bond content of MP increased with increasing PEF voltage. This result was consistent with the protein deposition in the lanes of non-reduced SDS-PAGE (Fig. 6A).

The pulsed electric field equipment mainly consisted of a control system and a treatment chamber, in which parameters such as voltage, frequency, and processing time were set, and the distance between the liquid level in the processing room and the pulsing electrode was adjusted (maintaining a 1 cm air gap), which was the key factor for ozone/ROS generation during high-voltage discharge. This was the main difference from previous studies on PEF treatment of terrestrial animal muscle, where the electrode was in direct contact with the sample and the ozone production was negligible. During processing, electric field polarization and ozone/ROS oxidation were the core drivers of MP structural and physicochemical changes.

At the moderate voltage of 10 kV, the electric field was the dominant factor: the external electric field disrupted the intramolecular interactions of MP, promoting protein unfolding and exposing the buried hydrophobic groups and free sulfhydryl groups. This structural change was directly reflected in the significant increase of solubility, surface hydrophobicity and zeta-potential absolute value of MP, and the decrease of particle size (Fig. 1A-B, Fig. 4). Meanwhile, the low-level ozone/ROS generated at 10 kV only induced mild peptide chain cleavage, which did not cause severe protein oxidation, thus maintaining the functional properties of MP.

With the increase of PEF voltage (>10 kV), the ozone/ROS content in the solution increased significantly (Fig. 2A), and ozone oxidation became the dominant factor, which was confirmed by the strong positive correlation between voltage and ozone content/carbonyl content (Fig. 9B). Ozone/ROS induced severe oxidation of MP, including amino acid modification, peptide chain breakage and cross-linking, leading to a significant increase in MFI (Fig. 2C) and a decrease in total/reactive sulfhydryl content (Fig. 1C-D). The exposed sulfhydryl groups were oxidized to form intermolecular disulfide bonds, which was verified by the Raman spectroscopy results (I540/I1003 ratio increase, (Fig. 8C) and reduced SDS-PAGE (aggregation weakened after β-mercaptoethanol addition, (Fig. 6B). Meanwhile, the electric field-induced polarization weakened the electrostatic repulsion between MP molecules (zeta-potential absolute value decrease, (Fig. 4A), and the unfolded/fragmented MP molecules cross-linked to form aggregates through disulfide bonds, hydrophobic interactions and weak electrostatic attraction, resulting in the increase of particle size (Fig. 4B) and the decrease of solubility (Fig. 1A). In addition, the synergistic effect of electric field and ozone oxidation altered the spatial structure of Ca2+-ATPase and Mg2+-ATPase (the globular head structure of myosin), leading to a significant decrease in enzyme activity (Fig. 3), and the strong negative correlation between voltage and ATPase activity (Fig. 9B) confirmed the structural damage of the enzyme active center.

Limitations and future perspectives

Despite the promising findings, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the experiments were conducted using purified abalone MP solutions rather than intact abalone muscle tissue. As a result, the matrix influence from endogenous components such as lipids, carbohydrates, connective tissues, and other soluble substances in natural abalone muscle was not considered. These matrix components may interfere with electric field distribution, affect ozone generation and diffusion, and alter protein–matrix interactions, which could lead to different responses of MP to PEF treatment in real food systems. Second, only a limited range of voltage levels (10–30 kV) and fixed processing parameters were investigated; the effects of other PEF conditions such as frequency, pulse width, and treatment time remain unclear. Third, the detailed oxidation sites and molecular structural changes induced by ozone and reactive oxygen species (ROS) under PEF were not fully identified at the molecular level.

Future research is recommended to address these limitations. Studies should be performed using intact abalone muscle to evaluate and compensate for matrix effects under PEF processing. Moreover, systematic optimization of PEF parameters (voltage, frequency, pulse width, and treatment duration) is needed to maximize modification efficiency while minimizing excessive protein oxidation. In addition, advanced structural characterization such as proteomics and molecular dynamics simulation could be applied to reveal the exact oxidation sites and conformational evolution of MP induced by PEF coupled with ozone-mediated oxidation. These efforts will improve the practical application reliability of PEF technology in abalone and other aquatic product processing.

Conclusion

In this study, PEF treatment was applied to MP isolated from Haliotis discus hannai. The intensity of PEF treatment had a significant effect on physicochemical and structural properties of MP. Results showed that appropriate PEF strength increased solubility, surface hydrophobicity, sulfhydryl content and zeta-potential of MP. Under strong PEF treatment, the solubility, hydrophobicity, total and reactive sulfhydryl content of MP and zeta potential decreased, but the particle size increased, which was due to the increase in the number of aggregates formed by mutually attractive polar protein molecules. Therefore, the potential application of medium-intensity pulsed electric fields (≈10 kV) in seafood processing can enhance solubility while controlling oxidation.

The PEF treatment increased the α-helix of MP and the content of disulfide bonds. The treatment had little effect on the network structure and rheological properties formed in the MP gels. Interestingly, the fracture of MP treated by the device was attributed to the synergistic effect of electric field action and ozone oxidation. We will further investigate the changes in abalone MP structure by ozone and the analysis of oxidation sites. In conclusion, the PEF system is a promising technology for abalone MP treatment, which expands the marketing range of fresh abalone and provides a theoretical basis for the application of PEF in aquatic product reprocessing.
