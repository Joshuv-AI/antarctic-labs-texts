Characterization of the genetic and clinical landscapes of DCTN1 gene in neurodegenerative diseases: a series of large case-control study
Authors: Xiaorong Hou, Xuxiong Tang, Yuwen Zhao, Ziqin Liu, Jiajian Zhang, Ziwei Gong, Zhineng Kang, Ziwen Li, Han Chen, Junling Wang, Beisha Tang, Xiaoxia Zhou, Lifang Lei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractImpairment of axonal transport has been emphasized as a common feature in a series of neurodegenerative diseases (NDs). Variations in DCTN1 have been reported in NDs such as Parkinson’s disease (PD), Perry syndrome (PS) and Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS). The overall objective of this study was to investigate the contribution of DCTN1 variants in different NDs and to explore the correlation between DCTN1 variants and disease phenotypes. We identified a previously published mutation p.G71E in three unrelated PS families. In the PD cohort, 30 putative deleterious variants (PDVs) were identified in DCTN1. Gene-based burden analysis showed a nominal association between DCTN1 rare PDVs and PD (uncorrected p = 0.042); however, this association did not remain statistically significant after multiple testing correction (FDR-corrected p = 0.084). In the ALS cohort, 10 PDVs were all rare damaging missense variants, and the PDVs were not enriched in ALS patients. Our findings first provide the independent evidence that PDVs in DCTN1 may be a risk factor for PD, but do not support the genetic involvement of DCTN1 in ALS of Asian ancestry.Subject terms: Clinical genetics, Genotype, Mutation, Neurological disorders, Risk factors

Introduction

Neurodegenerative diseases (NDs) are chronic and irreversible conditions that affect tens of millions of individuals worldwide each year, but with no effective prevention or cure approaches1. The prevalence of NDs is projected to rise continuously with the increasing elderly population, posing a tremendous burden to patients, caregivers, and health systems2. Despite being distinct in origin, there is a growing recognition that considerable mechanisms and pathologies overlap across different NDs3. The common cellular perturbations shared in NDs include protein mishandling, organelle trafficking, RNA processing, synaptic health, and neuroinflammation4. Impairment of axonal transport, a highly regulated process essential for maintaining neuronal homeostasis, has been emphasized as a common feature in neurodegeneration over the last two decades by the identification of mutations in motor proteins that drive cargo trafficking in patients with NDs and has emerged as a promising target for potential early interventions5,6.

Dynactin, an activator of dynein, is a conserved multiprotein complex that directly binds to the dynein intermediate chain via its component p150Glued, functioning as an indispensable cofactor to trigger the motility of dynein. Protein p150Glued, the largest polypeptide subunit of dynactin assembly, contains a CAP-Gly motif at the N terminus that engages microtubule binding and two coiled-coiled domains enabling interactions with dynein and association with other dynactin subunits7,8. Dynactin-1 (DCTN1) is the encoding gene for p150Glued, and the mutations in this gene were first connected to motor neuron disease (MND) in 2003 and later identified as the causative gene for Perry syndrome (PS) in 20099,10. PS is a rare autosomal dominant neurodegenerative disorder characterized by atypical Parkinsonism associated with apathy/depression, weight loss, and central hypoventilation. It is rapidly progressive, with death usually within 5.5 years from onset. 11The abnormal deposition of transactive response DNA binding protein of 43 kDa (TDP-43) was established as a typical neuropathological feature of PS12. Additional to TDP-43 pathology, dynactin p50 inclusions were also observed in PS patients13. Due to the rapid development and widespread application of genetic sequencing technology, it has become clear that rare DCTN1 variants can exhibit more complicated neurodegeneration phenotypes than previously thought, involving Parkinson’s disease (PD), amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), frontotemporal dementia (FTD), and progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP)-like syndrome14–18. Functional studies have shown that the depletion of p150Glued leads to progressive degeneration of midbrain nigral dopaminergic neurons and accumulation of α-synuclein in aged p150Glued conditional knockout (cKO) mice, implying a link between DCTN1 and parkinsonism19. Furthermore, Yu et al. discovered a late-onset, selective degeneration of spinal motor neurons in p150Glued cKO mice20. Farrer et al. identified five disease-segregating DCTN1CAP-Gly domain substitutions in 8 families that diminish microtubule binding and lead to intracytoplasmic inclusions, including the TDP-43 inclusions and the p150glued inclusions10. Subsequently, Deshimaru et al. revealed that DCTN1 binds to TDP-43, and the dysregulation of DCTN1-TDP-43 interactions induces TDP-43 mislocalization and aggregation, establishing the correlation of DCTN1 with TDP-43 proteinopathies21. This body of bioinformatic and biological evidence suggests that further investigation into the role of DCTN1 variation in NDs is warranted.

To our current knowledge, most past studies have only focused on mutation screening of DCTN1 in patients with PD and ALS18,22–25. Hence, there remains a scarcity of large-scale case-control studies to confirm the true genetic association between DCTN1 variants and NDs. The overall objective of this study was to investigate the role of DCTN1 variants in different NDs and to explore the correlation between DCTN1 variants and disease phenotypes. In this work, we used both whole-exome sequencing (WES) and whole-genome sequencing (WGS) to perform a comprehensive screening for putative damage variants in the DCTN1 gene in patients with NDs (including large PD and ALS cohorts), and further conducted association analysis to validate the contribution of DCTN1 variations in NDs in the Chinese population. Additionally, we reported the clinical and genetic findings of our three PS patients carrying pathogenic DCTN1 mutations, then investigated the genetic variability of DCTN1 across different NDs (PS, PD, and ALS) and proceeded to characterize the clinical features of these patients carrying DCTN1 putative deleterious variants (PDVs) to explore genotype-phenotype correlations.

Results

Identification of DCTN1 mutations in PS patients

These three probands had the mean age of 47.67 ± 4.04 years and mean age at onset (AAO) of 43.00 ± 4.00 years. The variants of the three probands had been verified by Sanger sequencing, and the results were shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. Family 1: the pedigree contained 20 members including the spouses, spanning four generations, with three affected individuals (Fig. 1. F1). The proband (III-4), who was diagnosed with definite PS, began with bradykinesia, postural instability, and backward falls at the age of 48-year-old. As the disease progressed, he displayed cognitive impairment, reduced facial expression, staring off, slow speech with dysarthria, and difficulty falling asleep. Over the next year, he developed apathy, and severe depression with suicidal ideation and he continued to lose approximately 10 kg in weight. His caregivers subsequently noted that he had an abnormal breathing pattern with rapid shallow breathing. At age 49, his neurological examination revealed predominant axial akinetic-rigid Parkinsonism with diminished postural reflexes. Examination of gaze showed impaired vertical pursuits. The brain MRI had no obvious abnormalities. The proband was unable to finish respiratory function tests and a battery of neuropsychological tests, including the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), and Hamilton Anxiety Scale (HAMA). The treatment with L-Dopa (levodopa equivalent dose 137.5 mg/d) resulted in no motor gains. His mother (II-3) suffered from cognitive deficits and upper limb tremor, and she died four years after the onset. His grandmother (I-1) committed suicide when she was 40 years old (details unavailable).

The DCTN1 mutation segregated with the disease: patient III-4 carried the mutation, whereas 2 asymptomatic relatives II-2 (older than 60 years) and III-6 (40-year-old), did not (supplementary Fig. 2). The DNA of patients II-3 and I-1, who were putative carriers, was unavailable. In addition, the proband’s father (II-4) declined to provide a blood sample for genetic testing.

Family 2: a forty-year-old right-handed woman reported a 1-year history of progressive slowed movements, imbalance, and anxiety (Fig. 1. F2). She had no anosmia, autonomic disturbances, hallucinations, or vivid dreams, but difficulty in getting to sleep. Examination revealed hypomimia, bradykinesia, reduced arm swing, rigidity in all limbs, dysarthria, and normal cognition (MMSE score 28/30). Her motor symptoms were bilateral and worse on the right. The presynaptic dopamine transporter brain PET imaging showed asymmetric reduced uptake in the basal ganglia (caudate nucleus and putamen), more marked on the left. The clinicians started levodopa, which improved her Parkinsonism partially. In the most recent follow-up assessment after two years from onset, she had developed marked end-of-dose wearing off spells, with the Hoehn-Yahr (H&Y) stage being 3.0, and yet no hypoventilation or weight loss.

Genetic testing revealed the c.212 G > A, p.G71E mutation in exon 2 of the DCTN1 gene in the proband III-2. Her mother, grandfather, and aunt all presented with uniform phenotypes and died of the disease at the ages of 54, 50, and 55. However, detailed information on their disease courses was inaccessible, and these affected members did not seek medical diagnosis or treatment. Meanwhile, the DNA of II-3 and II-4 was unavailable. The proband’s two children were well. Collectively, we diagnosed her with definite PS.

Family 3: a 48-year-old female was in good health until the age of 43 (Fig. 1. F3), when she developed evident Parkinsonism (bradykinesia and asymmetrical rest tremor combining postural tremor, worse on the right side, lead-pipe rigidity in all limbs), depression, sleep difficulties, daytime somnolence, and autonomic dysfunction. She had a masked face, and the MMSE score was 23/30. She had felt unsteady while resting and asleep, with a very strong urge to move her legs. The pedigree indicated an autosomal dominant inheritance: her mother and aunt displayed similar symptoms, and both passed away in their fifth decade of life, but we were unable to gain any detailed information about these two affected family members. Her 43-year-old sibling had complained of upper limb tremors and slowed movements for two years. In the latest follow-up visit, the proband died at the age of 50, while her sibling died at the age of 46. Genetic tests were negative for any known pathogenic mutation of PD, but positive for DCTN1, p.G71E mutation. She fulfilled the definite criteria for diagnosing PS.

Overall, 68 cases were identified as carriers of rare nonsynonymous variants in the DCTN1 gene, with 30 cases harboring PDVs (28 rare damaging missense (Dmis) variants and 2 loss of function (LoF) variants) (Supplementary Table 1) (Fig. 2). In gene burden analysis, rare PDVs (Dmis plus LoF variants) in the DCTN1 demonstrated a potential association with total PD patients (30 vs. 11, p = 0.042) that was no longer significant after FDR correction (p* = 0.084) (Table 3, Fig. 3). We also performed exploratory subgroup analyses within the PD cohort, which included the EoPD&FPD and LoPD subgroups. A nominally significant association of DCTN1 PDVs with PD was found in the EoPD & FPD cohort (20 vs. 7, p = 0.049), which did not remain significant after correction (FDR p* = 0.098). No such association was detected in the LoPD cohort (10 vs. 4, p = 0.422, p* = 0.422).(Table 3, Fig. 3). Given that mutations within distinct domains are likely to be related to different molecular events through a varied protein interactome, we further investigated the associations of rare PDVs located in different functional domains of DCTN1 with PD risk (CAP domain, CC1 domain, and CC2 domain, respectively). However, no correlations were observed in our analysis (p = 0.191 for CAP-domain, p = 0.118 for CC1 domain, p = 0.941 for CC2 domain) (Supplementary Table 2). In addition, we performed a burden analysis on synonymous variants in the PD cohort. The result demonstrated that synonymous variants in the DCTN1 were not associated with the risk of PD in any of the analyzed cohorts (Supplementary Table 3). Additionally, all variants predicted as PDVs have been verified by Sanger sequencing, and the results are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. In the UKB cohort, burden analysis at the entire gene level did not reach significant level. (Supplementary Table 4)

Particularly, 2 PDVs in exon 2 of DCTN1 (namely p.K56R and p.G71E, both documented point mutations in PS) were identified in our EoPD & FPD cohort. We then performed follow-up phone calls on these two patients. Except for the known Parkinsonism manifestations, we carefully inquired if they developed any additional PS-related symptoms, including apathy or depression, respiratory problems, unexplained weight loss, or rapid disease progression within 5 years of onset. We also verified whether they had a positive family history of Parkinsonism or respiratory symptoms. After thorough questioning, we confirmed that they still did not present any of the other core symptoms of PS above; hence, we diagnosed them with PD at this time. This may be owing to the incomplete penetrance of the DCTN1 p.K56R and p.G71E mutations, or it could be because the clinical symptoms have not yet fully emerged, needing long-term follow-up for a definitive final diagnosis. Detailed clinical characteristics in PD patients with DCTN1 PDVs were shown in Supplementary Table 5. Furthermore, we performed genotype-phenotype association analyses to investigate potential modifying effects of DCTN1 PDVs on PD phenotypes. However, our results did not demonstrate any statistically significant association between DCTN1 PDVs and clinical phenotypic manifestations in PD (Supplementary Table 6).

Identification of DCTN1 variants in ALS cohorts

We analyzed the rare variants of DCTN1 gene in 988 ALS patients of Chinese ancestry. A total of 22 rare variants (minor allele frequency, MAF < 0.001) were identified, including 4 splicing site variants and 18 missense variants, among which 10 were predicted as PDVs by Reve. Unlike PS, we found none of these 10 PDVs are inside exon 2. Instead, they are dispersed throughout exons 14 to 26. PDVs at the gene level were not enriched in ALS patients (10 vs 7, p = 0.089, p* = 0.119) (Table 3, Fig. 3). The location and pathogenicity of the 10 PDVs identified in this study are shown in Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 7. Equally, all variants predicted as PDVs identified in ALS cohort have been verified by sanger sequencing, and the results are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. In addition, burden analysis was also performed in the Project MinE cohort. A total of 91 rare variants (MAF < 0.001) were identified, among which 28 were predicted as PDVs by Reve. The burden analysis was performed (Supplementary Table 4) to further indicate that DCTN1 may not be an ALS risk gene.

To explore the genotype-phenotype associations, we comprehensively reviewed the clinical manifestations of these ten unrelated ALS patients with DCTN1 PDVs. All these ten patients carrying DCTN1 PDVs were sporadic, with six having spinal onset, three presenting bulbar onset and the remaining one exhibiting multifocal onset of both spinal and bulbar. The mean AAO was 55.40 ± 10.88 years old, with a sex ratio of 1.5:1 (6 men: 4 women). By the last follow-up, 6 patients had died, 1 were lost to follow-up, and 3 were still alive. The median survival time was 27.00 ± 13.97 months. According to the Edinburgh Cognitive and Behavior ALS Screen (ECAS) and MMSE scores, about 60.0% (6/10) of the patients had cognitive impairment. Compared to the other ALS patients in our cohort who did not have DCTN1 PDVs, there were no significant differences in the AAO (55.40 years vs. 54.11 years), proportion of male involvement (40.00 vs 35.48%), or the revised version of the ALS functional rating scale (ALSFRS-R) scores. No specific clinical characteristics were observed in ALS patients with DCTN1 PDVs, detailed in Supplementary Tables 8 and 9.

Discussion

Genetic analyses and function studies confirmed axonal transport defects in NDs26. In hereditary spastic paraplegia, point mutations in KIF5A can affect the activity of the kinesin motor protein and anterograde transport27; In PD, mutated LRRK2 causes disrupted axonal transport of mitochondria28; ALS-related SOD1 variants inhibited kinesin-based fast axonal transport29. DCTN1, which encodes dynactin subunit 1 (also known as p150glued) critical for retrograde axonal transport in neuron, has been linked to several NDs. However, large-scale case-control studies are needed to verify the association between DCTN1 variants and NDs. Our overall goal was to, by analyzing genetic variants and clinical presentations, determine the contribution of DCTN1 variants in NDs, and whether there is a specific genotype-phenotype correlation of DCTN1 variations.

Mutations in exon 2 of the DCTN1 gene were established as the genetic cause of PS, a rare autosomal dominant neurodegenerative disorder characterized by weight loss, Parkinsonism, central hypoventilation, and severe depression30. To date, all PS-related DCTN1 mutations have been found inside or near the GKNDG motif (from p.F52L to p.Y78C/H, plus an additional missense mutation p.Q93H)22,31,32. Herein, we have reported three Chinese PS families shared a known pathogenic DCTN1 mutation p.G71E, with significant interfamilial variability in clinical manifestations, including typical PS (family 1) and isolated Parkinsonism (family 2 and family 3). Parkinsonism in PS differed from that seen in PD, as it tended to be symmetrical, progressed more rapidly and was less responsive to L-Dopa31. We summarized the clinical characteristics of the previously reported cases of PS carrying the p.G71E mutation in the DCTN1 gene (Supplementary Table 10), and found that rigidity and bradykinesia was the most prevalent Parkinsonian symptom, and tremor might be lacking16,30. Even while the AAO of PS patients was comparatively early in our study, some research has shown that the AAO of the PS patients might be later than 5016,30. Infrequently, patients with PS had typical symptoms of other neurodegenerative disorders, such as frontotemporal dementia (FTD)31. Similarly, clinical heterogeneity was also observed in different families carrying the p.G59S mutation in DCTN133. The varied phenotype of patients with the same DCTN1 mutation may be partly explained by oligogenic or polygenic background. Although these three families were not known to be related, we cannot definitively exclude a founder effect for this mutation. Our identification of three new cases of PS in a small regional population highlights that DCTN1 mutations should be considered in patients presenting with early-onset Parkinsonism, particularly when there is a family history of Parkinsonism, psychiatric disturbances, or an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern.

A range of mutation analyses have linked DCTN1 genetic variability to more complex neurodegenerative phenotypes: PD, ALS/FTD, and rarer PSP-like presentations16,17,34,35. This is the first and largest study to conduct a comprehensive clinical and genetic analysis of DCTN1 variants in the independent case-control series of PD and ALS in the Chinese population, involving 5032 patients and 2931 healthy controls. We screened all participants for putative, deleterious DCTN1 variants and found that the PDVs of DCTN1 are very rare and do not associate with ALS susceptibility, which is consistent with previous studies15,24. In addition, we report all coding and splicing variants with a CADD score >20 that are observed exclusively in cases within each cohort (Supplementary Table 11). Gene-level burden analysis identified a suggestive association between PDVs in DCTN1 and PD risk in Chinese population, particularly within EoPD&FPD subtypes. However, this finding was not replicated in the UK Biobank cohort, which consists predominantly of individuals of European ancestry36,37. The discrepancy may be attributed to the following factors. First, differences in genetic background across populations represent a major contributing factor; both the allele frequency of DCTN1 variants and the genetic context in which they exert effects are likely population-specific. Second, differences in cohort composition play a critical role. Our cohort is enriched for EoPD&FPD, which are generally influenced by stronger genetic factors, whereas the UK Biobank cohort consists largely of late-onset sporadic cases, a context that may dilute the observable genetic effect size of DCTN1. In our ALS cohort, we discovered ten PDVs in DCTN1 gene, among which p.R639W was reported in a sporadic ALS patient from southern China in 201938. This work, together with earlier DCTN1 mutation analyses, confirms the low prevalence of DCTN1 variants in different ethnic groups of ALS patients, making the DCTN1 gene an unclear genomic risk factor for ALS15,23,25,38.

P150Glued, encoded by the full-length DCTN1 gene, contains tandem microtubule-binding domains at its N-terminus: the cytoskeleton-associated protein and glycine-rich (CAP-Gly) domain and the basic domain, followed by the coiled-coil 1 (CC1) and CC2 domains7,39. PS-related pathogenic DCTN1 variants have been demonstrated to alter microtubule binding ability and disrupt the motility of dynein-p150Glued co-complex40. Unlike the pathogenic mechanism of PS caused by DCTN1 mutations in the CAP-Gly domain, DCTN1 depletion has been shown to induce the loss of midbrain dopaminergic neurons and spinal motor neurons in mice, resembling the pathology of PD and ALS. In our cohort, the distribution of DCTN1 variants identified in ALS patients does not overlap with that in PS patients, supporting the distinct molecular processes underlying PS and other DCTN1-related neurodegeneration.

However, this study still has several limitations. First, although we could conclude that these three PS families have definite PS according to Mishima’s criteria, there are no autopsy data in our cohort. Second, we did not conduct the copy number variant analysis in our study. Lastly, we used two different sequencing methods, including WGS and WES. We analyzed these two datasets separately, as it is reasonable to perform analyses in the same case-control dataset, since they were sequenced in the same technical manner in the same dataset; however, a comparison between the two datasets might not be appropriate. Finally, the p values identified in our PD cohort would not reach statistical significance if in a genome-wide study. However, burden analysis in genome-wide studies did not always identify all known PD causative or risk genes as PD-associated genes. In a word, it requires more studies to validate the association of DCTN1 with PD. The current study represents a comprehensive and systematic screening of DCTN1 variants in large cohorts of Chinese patients with PD and ALS, expanding the genotype and phenotype spectrum of DCTN1 variants in NDs. Although our findings demonstrate that variants in DCTN1 are not common risk factors for ALS, the identification of PDVs of DCTN1 in patients with NDs suggests that the disruption of axonal transport is a unifying event in neurodegeneration.

Methods

Recruitment and clinical data collection

Study participants with PS (nPS = 3), PD (nPD = 4044), and ALS (nALS = 988) were of Chinese origin and recruited from the Department of Neurology, Xiangya Hospital and the Third Xiangya Hospital (Central South University), as well as other centers from PD & Movement Disorders Multicenter Database and Collaborative Network in China (PD-MDCNC, http://pd-mdcnc.com/)41. All patients were diagnosed based on their phenotypic presentation and whether they met the diagnostic criteria, involving the latest PS diagnostic criteria for PS cases42, the Movement Disorders Society (MDS) clinical diagnostic criteria for PD cases43, the current Gold Coast criteria for ALS cases44. The EoPD is defined as the AAO ≤ 50 years old; The LoPD is defined as the AAO > 50 years old. A positive family history was defined by at least one first- or second-degree relative manifesting the phenotypically concordant clinical manifestations with the proband. We recruited 2,931 healthy individuals matched for age and ancestry as control subjects. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to check for genetic ancestry, and only unrelated subjects of Chinese descent were retained. In addition, we also included the data in the Project MinE cohort (data freeze 2), including 6596 ALS patients and 2454 controls45 and in the UK Biobank, including 2959 PD patients and 340,018 controls46–48.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xiangya Hospital and the Ethics Committee of the Third Xiangya Hospital. All procedures were conducted conforming to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed written consents were obtained from all participants enrolled.

Clinical assessment in the PD patients was carried out using Unified PD Rating Scale (UPDRS) motor subsection III and H&Y scale. The ALSFRS-R was applied to assess disease severity in ALS cases. All participants experienced the cognitive function evaluation utilizing the ECAS (only for ALS cases) and/or the MMSE and/or MoCA. Meanwhile, the PS and PD patients were evaluated HAMA. Clinical and demographic features for our study cohorts, which have previously been described in other recent papers49,50, are shown in Table 2.

Genetic sequencing

Genomic DNA was isolated from peripheral blood leukocytes using standard phenol–chloroform extraction protocols. Each obtained DNA sample was quantified with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer 1000 (Thermo Scientific). The PS cohort was sequenced by WES following the manufacturer’s instruction on the HiSeq X10 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA). The PD cohort was divided into two cohorts: the EoPD &FPD cohort and the LoPD cohort. The EoPD & FPD cohort included 2082 patients and the LoPD cohort included 1962 patients. The EoPD & FPD cohort, ALS cohort and 1652 matched controls were also sequenced by WES following the manufacturer’s instruction on the HiSeq X10 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA), while the LoPD cohort and 1279 matched controls were sequenced by whole genome sequencing (WGS) as directed by the manufacturer’s instruction on the HiSeq Nova platform. The WES Library Preparation is performed using MGIEasy Universal DNA Library Prep Set (MGI, China). Genomic DNA is randomly fragmented and selected to a certain average size. Then, DNA fragments are processed sequentially with end repair reaction, A-tailing reaction, and adapter ligation reaction. After connecting the library adapters, the products are amplified with PCR reaction. Next, a certain amount of PCR products is taken for hybridization by SureSelectXT Human All Exon V6 kit (Agilent USA). The exome targeted probes are labeled with biotin and mixed with PCR products in hybridization reaction solution. By the principle of nucleic acid base complementarity, the probe binds to the target DNA segment. Subsequently, the hybridization mixture is combined with streptavidin-coated magnetic beads, allowing the biotin-labeled probes to firmly bind to the streptavidin-coated magnetic beads, thus the targeted exome regions DNA segments are captured. After further purification to remove non-specifically bound DNA from the beads, the targeted exome DNA segments are enriched. The final exome-enriched library is amplified through a PCR reaction and subjected to a library quality control process. The WGS Library preparation is performed by MGIEasy PCR-Free DNA Library Prep Set (MGI-Shenzhen, China). A certain amount of genomic DNA is taken and subjected to fragmentation processing. Fragmented samples are size selected through magnetic beads. Then the size-selected DNA fragments are converted to blunt-end DNA with an end repair reaction. A single adenosine is added on the 3' end of DNA through the A tailing reaction. Later, the library adapters are connected to the two ends of the DNA by adapter ligation. Finally, the library products are subjected to a quality control process. The resulting data were aligned to the human reference genome GRCh37/hg19, and variants calling were performed using the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK), proceeding to annotate genetic variants with the ANNOVAR software51 and Varcards252. Details on genotyping and quality control standards for both WES and WGS data have been outlined in our previous study49,53.

All nonsynonymous variants in the coding and splicing region of DCTN1, including missense, stop gain/loss, frame-shift insertion and deletion, in-frame insertion and deletion, and splice site mutations, were considered. Variants annotated as stop gain/loss, frameshift, or splicing mutations (±2 bp) were categorized as LoF. Variants with MAF in the Genome Aggregation Database (GnomAD)-genome-East Asian (GnomAD-EAS), gnomAD_exome_EAS and the Exome Aggregation Consortium-East Asian (ExAC_EAS) less than 0.001 were defined as rare variants. The ReVe score was used to predict the pathogenicity of rare missense variants54. In this study, Lof mutations and rare damaging missense variants (Dmis, rare missense variants with Reve score > 0.7) were classified as putative deleterious variants (PDVs). These PDVs were selected for downstream analysis.

Gene-based burden analysis

Gene-level burden analysis was conducted in our series of case-control studies. To address multiple testing concerns, we pre-specified two primary analytical endpoints: (1) the association between DCTN1 rare deleterious variant burden and PD in the overall PD cohort, and the association in the ALS cohort. Then, all subgroup analyses within the PD cohort (including EoPD & FPD and LoPD subgroups) were explicitly designated as exploratory analyses to generate hypotheses for future investigation.

We performed gene-level burden analysis in our case-control cohorts using the Firth logistic regression55 (implemented via the logistf package in R version 4.4) to assess the association between rare variants (MAF < 0.001) in DCTN1 and PD/ALS risk. This approach effectively addresses small-sample bias and complete separation issues in rare variant analysis. All models were adjusted for age and gender to account for potential population stratification.

Genotype–phenotype association analysis

Patients in both the PD and ALS cohorts were classified into carrier and non-carrier groups according to their genetic variant status. For the PD cohort, we examined associations between carrier status and various motor/non-motor scales, including AAO, disease duration, UPDRS-III scores, H&Y stage, and MMSE scores. Similarly, in the ALS cohort, we analyzed relationships between carrier status and clinical measures comprising AAO, disease duration, ALSFRS, and ECAS scores. All analyses were performed using generalized linear models (GLM) with adjustment for gender and age as covariates, and p-values were corrected for multiple comparisons using the with false discovery rate (FDR).

Statistical analysis

Values were expressed as the mean ± SD (standard deviation) for continuous variables and the median ± SD for categorical variables. Differences between the two compared groups were assessed by Student’s t-test, χ2-tests, or Fisher’s exact test where appropriate. All tests were two-tailed, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed with SPSS version 26.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).
