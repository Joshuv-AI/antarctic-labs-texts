Associations of daily sedentary time with mental health and sleep quality in adolescents: analyses of interaction and moderation effects
Authors: Weiwei Xiang, Lijuan Zheng, Chenghao Zhou, Xiang Liu, Li Dai, Xinwen Min, Handong Yang, Jun Chen, Jishun Chen, Dongfeng Li, Hao Xu, Wenwen Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundProlonged sedentary time and poor mental health are both associated with poorer sleep quality in adolescents, but how they interact to influence sleep quality is unclear. This study examined the interaction between daily sedentary time and mental health on adolescents’ sleep quality and assessed the moderating role of mental health.MethodsThis cross-sectional study recruited 10,623 adolescents from 13 schools in Shiyan City, China, from October 2023 to January 2024. Data on demographics (age, sex), daily sedentary time, sleep quality, mental health, and other related factors (e.g., father’s smoking status, mode of commuting to school) were collected via self-report questionnaires. Blood pressure was measured using standard protocols. Mental health was assessed with the validated Kessler Psychological Distress Scale, and sleep quality was assessed with the validated Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index. Multivariate logistic regression was used to examine the associations of daily sedentary time and mental health with sleep quality, as well as the additive and multiplicative interactions between daily sedentary time and mental health on sleep quality. Subsequently, the moderation of the daily sedentary time–sleep quality relationship by mental health was assessed.ResultsThe prevalence of poor sleep quality was 40.14%. Adolescents with ≥ 10 h of daily sedentary time had a higher risk of poor sleep quality (OR = 1.308, 95% CI: 1.169–1.465) than those with < 10 h. Those with poor mental health had a significantly elevated risk (OR = 7.434, 95% CI: 6.535–8.456) compared to those with good mental health. A multiplicative interaction was observed between daily sedentary time and mental health on adolescents’ sleep quality (p < 0.05, OR = 0.791, 95% CI: 0.651–0.961), but no additive interaction (RERI = − 0.052, 95% CI: −4.033–3.930; AP = − 0.007, 95% CI : −0.526 − 0.512; S = 0.992, 95% CI : 0.549–1.794). Mental health levels moderated the relationship between daily sedentary time and sleep quality.ConclusionsDaily sedentary time and mental health were independently associated with sleep quality, and mental health moderated this relationship. These findings suggest that strategies targeting both factors may hold potential for improving adolescent sleep.

Introduction

Adequate sleep is positively associated with better health status and health-related behaviours in adolescents [1]. By contrast, insufficient sleep not only impairs cardiovascular and metabolic functions and neurocognitive development, but also adversely affects mental health [2, 3]. Insufficient sleep also adversely affects academic performance, subjective well-being, interpersonal relationships and broader psychological and social functioning [4, 5]. A nationally representative cross-sectional survey of American adolescents has found that the average sleep duration among adolescents has declined across all subgroups over the past two decades [6]. Similarly, another study has documented a continuing decline in sleep duration among children and adolescents over the past 103 years [7]. Previous research has shown that sleep disorders during adolescence may have a lasting impact on sleep patterns in adulthood. Among adolescents who experienced sleep disorders at age 16, about 33% continued to report persistent sleep disturbances at age 23, and 10% still had sleep disorders at age 42 [8]. Therefore, it is essential to systematically examine the key factors influencing sleep disorders in adolescents and their underlying mechanisms in order to improve sleep quality.

The World Health Organization (WHO) released the 2020 WHO Guidelines on Physical Activity and Sedentary Behaviour [9], which define sedentary behaviour (SB) as sitting, reclining or lying awake with an energy expenditure of ≤ 1.5 metabolic equivalents. Adolescents are reported to spend substantial amounts of time in SB, with educational activities contributing the largest proportion. Time spent in SB for educational activities increases with grade level, whereas social and transportation-related activities contribute only a small proportion of total SB. Previous research has indicated that prolonged SB is a significant risk factor for physical conditions, including obesity [10] and cardiometabolic abnormalities [11], and psychological disorders, including depression, anxiety [12, 13] and suicidal behaviours or ideation [14] in adolescents. With growing research on SB, the association between SB and sleep quality in children and adolescents has received increasing attention. Previous research has shown that prolonged SB is associated with an increased risk of sleep disorders in adolescents [15]. Experimental studies have found that interventions aimed at reducing or regularly interrupting SB can help improve sleep quality in this population [16]. Although previous studies have demonstrated the adverse effects of SB on sleep disorders in adolescents, the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood.

Childhood and adolescence are critical stages of individual development and have been described as ‘crisis periods’ [17]. During this time, individuals experience rapid psychological development, and their sense of self is still developing. They are exposed to major physiological and psychological changes as well as pressures from family, school and society, increasing their vulnerability to mental health problems, including depression and anxiety. Previous studies have demonstrated that mental health is closely associated with sleep quality in children and adolescents [18, 19].

Although previous studies have examined the effects of SB and mental health on sleep disorders in adolescents separately, few studies have integrated these factors within a single analytical framework or investigated their underlying mechanisms in depth. This study aims to investigate the interaction between SB and mental health on adolescents’ sleep quality and the moderating role of mental health in this association. The research findings may provide a theoretical foundation and practical implications for managing adolescents’ sleep health, potentially contributing to improvements in sleep quality.

Method

Participants

This study used a convenience sampling method to recruit students from 13 schools in Shiyan City, China, as participants from October 2023 to January 2024. After the study purpose was explained, written informed consent was obtained from all participants and their legal guardians. Participants were required to have sufficient cognitive ability to accurately complete the questionnaire independently. The sample size was calculated using the following formula: N = Z²pq/d² [20], where Z = 1.96 for a 95% confidence interval, p = the detection rate of poor sleep quality, p = 1 − p, and d = the absolute allowable error. Based on previous research, the detection rate of poor sleep quality was hypothesized to be 26.9% (d = 0.01). The minimum required sample size was calculated to be 7,555 participants. A total of 10,629 students participated in the survey. After excluding six participants with incomplete questionnaire or physical measurement data, 10,623 participants were ultimately included in the study. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Humanities Research Ethics Committee of the Hubei University of Medicine (Approval No. 2023-RE-027).

Data collection

Data were collected at the participating schools through two components: a self-administered questionnaire and physical measurements. The questionnaire was completed by the adolescents in their classrooms under the supervision of trained research assistants. It primarily collected information on sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle behaviours, and sleep quality. Physical measurements, including blood pressure (BP), were conducted at the same school setting by trained medical personnel from the research team.

Sociodemographic characteristics

Sociodemographic data were self-reported by the adolescents via the questionnaire. The data included age (years), school period (junior high school, senior high school), gender (male, female), ethnicity (Han, other), relationship with parents (better, normal), academic performance (better, normal), traits of character (introversion, neutral, extroversion) and father’s smoking status (yes, no).

Lifestyle and BP

Lifestyle data were collected, including mode of transportation to school (boarding school, commuting on foot, commuting by vehicle, or commuting by other means), daily screen time, daily sedentary time and daily exercise time (0 min, < 30 min, 30–60 min, > 60 min). Daily sedentary time was categorized into ≤ 10 h and > 10 h per day based on the median value of the sample distribution. Given the absence of universally accepted cut-off points for sedentary time in adolescents, this sample-based categorization was adopted to facilitate statistical analysis and interpretation. Screen time was dichotomized as ≤ 6 h and > 6 h per day. This cut-off was specified a priori, taking into account that Chinese adolescents are exposed to electronic screen-based blackboards during classroom instruction, which contributes substantially to total daily screen time alongside recreational use. The 6-hour threshold was therefore intended to identify adolescents with exceptionally high overall screen exposure.

In addition, trained medical personnel in the research team measured BP, including systolic and diastolic measurements, using a calibrated Omron HEM-1020 automatic electronic BP monitor. Participants were instructed to avoid vigorous physical activity for at least 1 h prior to BP measurement and remain at rest for at least 5 min before assessment. The BP cuff was positioned at the level of the brachial artery to ensure accurate measurement. Two BP readings were taken, with an interval of 1–2 min between measurements. The average of the two readings was recorded. If the difference between systolic or diastolic pressure exceeded 5 mmHg, a third reading was taken, and the average of all three measurements was used. Elevated BP in adolescents was defined according to China’s national reference standard: Reference for Screening Elevated Blood Pressure in Children and Adolescents Aged 7–18 Years (WS/T 610–2018) [21]. Systolic and/or diastolic BP at or above the 95th percentile for adolescents of the same sex, age and height was considered elevated.

Mental health

The 10-item Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K10) was used to assess participants’ mental health status by measuring non-specific psychological distress, reflecting an individual’s level of nervousness, agitation, psychological fatigue, and depression over the preceding four weeks. The scale was developed in 1992 by Kessler and Mroczek at the University of Michigan and has since been widely used to assess mental health in populations nationally and internationally [22]. The scale consists of 10 items assessing the frequency of psychological anxiety and stress experienced over the preceding four weeks. Each item is scored on a 5-point Likert scale, with ‘almost never’ = 1, ‘occasionally’ = 2, ‘sometimes’ = 3, ‘most of the time’ = 4 and ‘all the time’ = 5. The total score range of this scale is 10 to 50 points. An individual’s mental health level is assessed based on the total score of the scale. Scores of 10 to 15 points is the first level, indicating good mental health; 16 to 21 points is the second level, indicating good mental health; 22 to 29 points is the third level, indicating poor mental health; 30 to 50 points is the fourth level, indicating relatively poor mental health. In the present sample, the K10 demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.91.

Sleep quality

The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) was used to assess the sleep quality of the study subjects over the past month [23]. The PSQI was developed by Buysse et al. in 1989. The Chinese version employed in this study was translated by Liu et al. in 1996. The PSQI comprises 19 items across seven components: subjective sleep quality, sleep onset latency, sleep duration, sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of hypnotic medication and daytime dysfunction. Each PSQI component is scored from 0 to 3 points, and the sum of all component scores constitutes the total PSQI score, which ranges from 0 to 21. Higher scores indicate poorer sleep quality. The original PSQI cutoff score of ≥ 5 was developed and validated for adult populations. However, adolescents have unique sleep patterns, circadian rhythms, and lifestyle characteristics. To avoid overestimation of poor sleep prevalence and improve diagnostic specificity in adolescents, a higher cutoff has been recommended. A recent large-scale psychometric evaluation of the Chinese version of the PSQI among non-clinical Chinese adolescents found, through receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, that a score of 6.5 was the optimal cutoff for identifying sleep disturbance [24]. Accordingly, a cutoff of ≥ 7 has been adopted in studies of Chinese adolescent populations [25]. Based on this evidence, a PSQI score < 7 was defined as good sleep quality, and a score ≥ 7 was defined as poor sleep quality in the present study. The Cronbach’s α coefficient for the PSQI in this study was 0.751.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 26.0 and Stata. Continuous variables were reported as means with standard deviations, and comparisons between groups were made using t-tests. Categorical variables were expressed as percentages, with the χ² test used for group comparisons. The multiplicative interaction between daily sedentary time and mental health status was assessed using logistic regression models. Given the absence of a well-established consensus on definitive confounders for the associations among sedentary time, mental health, and sleep quality in adolescents, a data-driven approach was adopted for covariate selection: variables that showed significant associations with sleep quality in univariate analyses (p < 0.05) were entered simultaneously into the multivariable logistic regression model using the enter method. The moderating effect of mental health on the relationship between daily sedentary time and sleep quality was tested using the PROCESS 4.1 macro in SPSS. Simple slope analysis was conducted using linear regression with continuous PSQI scores as the outcome to visualize the interaction. Additive interactions between daily sedentary time and mental health were analysed using Excel 2019, following the method described by Andersson et al. [26]. Results

Characteristics of the participants

A total of 10,623 adolescents were included in the study, primarily secondary school students aged 12–17 years old, with a mean age of 13.83 ± 1.41 years. Among them, 5,644 (53.1%) were male, and 4,979 (46.9%) were female. In terms of SB, 6,131 (57.7%) students reported a daily sedentary time of ≤ 10 h, whereas 4,492 (42.3%) reported > 10 h. Regarding mental health status, 7,328 (69.0%) students were classified as having good mental health, whereas 3,295 (31.0%) as having poor mental health. The prevalence of poor sleep quality differed significantly across multiple sociodemographic and behavioural characteristics (all p < 0.05, Table 1). Poor sleep quality was more prevalent among females, senior high school students, those reporting a “normal” relationship with parents, those with “normal” academic performance, introverted individuals, those whose fathers smoked, boarders, those with daily sedentary time > 10 h, those with less daily exercise time, and those with poor mental health. Older age was also associated with a higher prevalence of poor sleep quality. No statistically significant differences in sleep quality were observed for ethnicity, daily screen time, or elevated blood pressure (all p > 0.05). Detailed data are presented in Table 1.

Association of daily sedentary time and mental health with sleep quality in adolescents

With sleep quality as the dependent variable, daily sedentary time and mental health were the primary independent variables. Covariates with p < 0.05 in univariate analysis, including age, school period, gender, relationship with parents, traits of character, father’s smoking status, mode of commuting to school, and daily exercise time, were entered simultaneously into the multivariable logistic regression model. The results showed that longer daily sedentary time (> 10 h) (OR = 1.215, 95% CI: 1.105–1.335) and poor mental health (OR = 6.723, 95% CI: 6.096–7.414) were significantly associated with poorer sleep quality. Among covariates, age, female gender, senior high school period, and normal relationship with parents were associated with poorer sleep quality, while neutral traits of character, extroversion traits of character, non-smoking father, walking to school, commuting by vehicle, other commuting modes, and daily exercise time (30–60 min and > 60 min) were protective factors (all p < 0.05). Analysis of the multiplicative interaction between daily sedentary time and mental health on sleep quality

A multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted to examine the interaction between daily sedentary time and mental health in relation to sleep quality, with sleep quality as the dependent variable. The independent variables included daily sedentary time, mental health and their interaction term (daily sedentary time × mental health), adjusting for covariates that showed significant associations with sleep quality in the univariate analyses (p < 0.05), including age, school period, gender, relationship with parents, traits of character, father’s smoking status, mode of commuting to school and daily exercise time. The results indicated that adolescents with daily sedentary time exceeding 10 h per day were significantly more likely to experience poor sleep quality compared with those with daily sedentary time ≤ 10 h (OR = 1.308, 95% CI: 1.169–1.465). Similarly, adolescents with poor mental health were at a substantially higher risk of poor sleep quality than those with good mental health (OR = 7.434, 95% CI: 6.535–8.456). Importantly, a significant multiplicative interaction was observed between daily sedentary time and mental health (p < 0.05, OR = 0.791, 95% CI: 0.651–0.961). Analysis of the additive interaction between daily sedentary time and mental health on sleep quality

Using adolescents’ sleep quality as the dependent variable and daily sedentary time and mental health as independent variables, a multivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted. The model was adjusted for potential confounders, including age, school period, gender, relationship with parents, traits of character, father’s smoking status, mode of commuting to school and daily exercise time. Adolescents with daily sedentary time ≤ 10 h and good mental health were used as the reference group. The results indicated that students with daily sedentary time > 10 h and poor mental health had a 7.688-fold higher risk of poor sleep quality than the reference group. Additive interaction analysis indicated no significant interaction between daily sedentary time and mental health on sleep quality, with RERI = − 0.052 (95% CI: −4.033–3.930), AP = − 0.007 (95% CI: −0.526–0.512), and S = 0.992 (95% CI: 0.549–1.794). The moderating role of mental health between daily sedentary time and sleep quality

The results of Model 3 indicated that, after adjusting for age, gender, school period, relationship with parents, traits of character, father’s smoking status, mode of commuting to school and daily exercise time, the OR for daily sedentary time was 1.308 (95% CI: 1.169–1.465), and the OR for mental health was 7.434 (95% CI: 6.535–8.456). The OR for the interaction term between daily sedentary time and mental health was 0.791 (95% CI: 0.651–0.961), indicating that mental health significantly moderated the association between daily sedentary time and sleep quality. To further investigate the moderating effect, an interaction plot was generated for different daily sedentary times (daily sedentary time > 10 h = 1; daily sedentary time ≤ 10 h = 0) (Fig. 1). The slope of each line in the plot represents the strength of the association between daily sedentary time and sleep quality. To facilitate interpretation, simple slope analysis was conducted using linear regression with continuous PSQI scores as the outcome. Consistently, simple slope analyses showed that longer daily sedentary time was positively associated with higher PSQI scores (indicating poorer sleep quality) only among adolescents with poor mental health (simple slope = 0.371, p < 0.001) (Table 6), whereas no significant association was observed among those with good mental health (simple slope = 0.003, p > 0.05). Thus, mental health moderated the sedentary time–sleep quality relationship, and the detrimental association of sedentary time with sleep quality was evident only in the context of poor mental health.

Discussion

The results of this study revealed that the detection rate of poor sleep quality among adolescents in Shiyan City, China, was 40.14%. This proportion was lower than the detection rate reported among Turkish high school students (53.0%) [22] but higher than the 38.8% reported among Japanese adolescents [23]. These findings further indicated that poor sleep quality was a widespread problem among adolescents, with certain differences observed across countries and regions.

Associations of daily sedentary time and mental health with sleep quality

The findings of this study indicated that daily sedentary time and mental health status were independently associated with sleep quality. Specifically, compared with those whose daily sedentary time was < 10 h, adolescents with a sedentary duration > 10 h had a markedly higher likelihood of experiencing poor sleep quality, a result that was consistent with findings of previous studies [27]. Several factors may be related to this association. Firstly, prolonged SB was often accompanied by increased screen and social media use. In addition, most schools had adopted digital teaching methods, which required students to spend extended periods in front of electronic screens during school hours, further increasing their screen exposure. Previous studies had consistently shown that screen-based SB was closely linked to multiple dimensions of sleep quality [28]. Secondly, SB may be related to sleep through changes in hormone levels. McPherson et al. reported that the duration of sedentary behaviour was negatively correlated with melatonin secretion levels [29]. Moreover, given the limited amount of time available each day, excessive SB may be associated with reduced time available for sleep, which is related to sleep deprivation.

Logistic regression results indicated that adolescents with poor mental health had a 6.434-fold higher risk of sleep disorders compared with those with good mental health. These findings suggested that mental health problems were significantly associated with sleep quality in adolescents. On the one hand, psychological problems may be related to disruptions in normal sleep processes, including increased sympathetic nervous system excitability and activated stress responses, and are associated with difficulties such as sleep onset delay or nighttime awakenings [30]. On the other hand, prolonged exposure to negative emotions and stress may be associated with altered secretion of sleep-regulating hormones, such as melatonin, which is related to disruptions in normal circadian rhythms [31].

Daily sedentary time and mental health status only exhibited a negative multiplicative interaction effect on sleep quality, with no additive interaction effect observed. Analysis of this interaction suggested that the combined effect of the two factors was not a simple linear sum but rather a buffering effect. Specifically, given that the OR was < 1, the combined effect appeared to attenuate the negative impact on sleep quality compared with the individual effects of each factor. This phenomenon may have arisen from the activation of regulatory or adaptive responses by the body under the combined influence of multiple adverse factors [32]. From a physiological perspective, prolonged daily SB may induce metabolic dysregulation and alterations in hormone secretion, whereas mental health problems involve complex psychophysiological processes, including neurotransmitter imbalances and emotional stress responses. When these two factors act simultaneously, the body may engage regulatory mechanisms to maintain internal homeostasis, resulting in a combined effect on sleep quality that deviates from a simple additive or multiplicative model.

The relatively large effect size observed for poor mental health should be interpreted with caution. Although the K10 and the PSQI assess largely distinct constructs, some degree of conceptual overlap and shared method variance cannot be entirely excluded. In addition, the magnitude of the association may be influenced by residual confounding from unmeasured factors, such as academic stress, family environment, or other lifestyle behaviours. Therefore, the observed association should be interpreted as indicative of a strong relationship rather than a precise estimate of an independent effect.

Moderating effect of mental health status on the relationship between daily sedentary time and sleep quality

Previous evidence has demonstrated that sedentary behaviour is associated with poorer mental health outcomes among adolescents, including increased symptoms of depression and anxiety [33]. Given the well-established relationship between mental health and sleep quality, these findings provide a theoretical basis for the interconnected pathways examined in the present study.

In the present study, the examination of the moderating effect of mental health revealed that, after controlling for age, gender, school period, relationship with parents, traits of character, father’s smoking status, mode of commuting to school and daily exercise time, the relationship between daily sedentary time and sleep quality was significantly moderated by mental health status. For adolescents with poor mental health, an increase in daily sedentary time was associated with progressively poorer sleep quality. By contrast, for those with good mental health, daily sedentary time was not significantly associated with sleep quality. This finding was consistent with the results of Endrighi et al., who reported that under conditions of psychological stress, the physiological stress responses induced by sedentary behaviour, such as elevated inflammatory levels, are more strongly associated with sleep disturbances [34]. The underlying reasons for this finding were complex and likely multifactorial. Firstly, individuals with poorer mental health generally experienced higher levels of emotional stress and psychological distress, which may be related to attentional difficulties and reduced ability to modulate the physiological effects associated with prolonged SB. Previous studies have indicated that prolonged SB can elevate levels of inflammatory biomarkers, such as IL-6, potentially triggering chronic inflammation. In addition, SB may activate the transcription factor NF-kB, thereby promoting the expression of inflammation-related genes and inducing systemic inflammatory responses [35]. Such inflammatory responses not only posed potential threats to overall health but also adversely affected sleep quality, and poorer mental health may be associated with more negative consequences of these physiological processes. Secondly, the impact of prolonged SB on hormonal regulation was substantial. Extended periods of sitting are associated with reductions in basal metabolic rate and changes in glucose regulation and insulin sensitivity, which potentially result in hormonal imbalances [36]. These hormonal alterations may be associated with reduced emotional stability in individuals with poor mental health, greater susceptibility to anxiety and depression, and poorer sleep quality. These findings further support the plausibility of mental health acting as a modifying factor in the relationship between sedentary behaviour and sleep outcomes.

This study was the first to investigate the relationships among sedentary time, mental health and sleep quality; however, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences, and future prospective cohort studies are needed to verify these findings. Second, daily screen time, daily exercise time, and daily sedentary time were self-reported, which may have introduced information bias. In addition, daily sedentary time was dichotomized based on the sample median. Although sample-based categorization is common in the absence of universally accepted thresholds, this approach may result in loss of information and may not reflect clinically meaningful cut-offs. Future studies should incorporate objective measurements where possible and consider data-driven approaches such as ROC analysis or dose–response modelling to identify more precise sedentary time thresholds. Third, the use of convenience sampling may limit generalizability. Participants were recruited from selected schools based on accessibility rather than random sampling, and caution is therefore warranted when applying these results to other populations. Fourth, parent–child relationship and academic performance were each assessed with a single self-reported dichotomous item (“better” vs. “normal”) without a negative response option (e.g., “poor”). This may have led adolescents with genuinely negative experiences to endorse the “normal” option, introducing misclassification and likely underestimating the associations of these factors with sleep quality. Moreover, academic performance was not a primary outcome in this study; future studies could incorporate it as an outcome and use neurocognitive assessment tools (e.g., attentional network tests) to examine the mechanisms through which sedentary time, mental health, and sleep quality jointly influence academic functioning. Fifth, mental health was assessed using the K10, which captures non-specific psychological distress rather than disorder-specific symptoms. Some degree of conceptual overlap (e.g., fatigue-related items) and shared method variance with the PSQI cannot be excluded. The large effect size observed for mental health should therefore be interpreted with caution, as residual confounding from unmeasured factors (e.g., academic stress, family environment, circadian chronotype, social media use) may also have contributed. Future studies should consider incorporating disorder-specific instruments (e.g., PHQ-9, GAD-7) and more comprehensive confounder measurement.

Conclusion

The prevalence of poor sleep quality among adolescents was relatively high. Improving sleep quality in this population requires attention to both daily sedentary time and mental health. For adolescents with poor mental health, psychological counseling and limiting sedentary time are recommended. For adolescents with good mental health, promoting healthy lifestyles, encouraging regular physical activity, and balancing screen time are important to reduce the risks associated with excessive sedentary behavior.
