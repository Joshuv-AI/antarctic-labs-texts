Perceptions and lived experiences with abemaciclib and endocrine therapy for early breast cancer: a rapid communication
Authors: Helena Harder, Val Jenkins, Lesley Fallowfield
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsThis study explored perceptions and initial experiences of abemaciclib, a CDK4/6 inhibitor, plus endocrine therapy (ET) as adjuvant targeted therapy for early breast cancer with high risk of recurrence (EBC). Early onset diarrhea is the most common side-effect, but its impact is often under-recognized and may affect treatment adherence.MethodsThree serial interviews were conducted with five women with EBC recruited from four UK breast units. The baseline interview took place before the start of treatment, follow-up interviews 4 and 8 weeks later. Transcripts were analyzed following the framework approach to thematic analysis.ResultsDiarrhea at follow-up was reported by 4 women, and in 3 this was the most bothersome symptom. It was usually managed by anti-diarrheal drugs and/or dietary modifications. One woman discontinued treatment due to its effect on day-to-day living. All patients said they adhered to treatment. Beliefs that the drug offered a lower risk of recurrence helped some to tolerate side-effects, alongside support from their clinical teams.ConclusionThese initial findings showed that abemaciclib plus ET was generally manageable. Regular follow-up in this patient cohort could support early detection of symptoms. Further longitudinal real-world research into patients’ experiences with abemaciclib plus ET for EBC is warranted.Trial registrationNCT04584853 (06/07/2020)KEYWORDS: Abemaciclib, side-effects, diarrhea, early breast cancer, management, patient experience

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most frequently diagnosed cancer in women worldwide, with hormone receptor-positive (HR+), HER2-negative (HER2-) the most common subtype, accounting for approximately 65–75% of all diagnoses [1,2]. Most cases are diagnosed at an early stage, and potentially curable. However, patients remain at high risk (ranging from 10 to 41% depending on nodal status and tumor grade) for both early and late recurrences within the first 20 years post-diagnosis [3]. New targeted therapies for early breast cancer (EBC), including cyclin-dependent kinase 4 and 6 (CDK4/6) inhibitors, have shown promising results in reducing the risk of recurrences.

The first CDK4/6 inhibitor to be approved as an adjuvant treatment option for high-risk HR+, HER2-, node positive EBC was abemaciclib [4,5]. The addition of 2 years of abemaciclib to standard endocrine therapy (ET) reduced the likelihood of developing invasive and distant disease recurrence [6,7]. The combination of these two oral continuous drugs requires optimal adherence for the expected therapeutic benefits to be realized. This could potentially be compromised as both treatments are associated with side effects (SEs). The most common SEs of ET include vasomotor symptoms, joint stiffness or pain, mood changes and sexual problems [8]. The safety profile of abemaciclib showed an increased risk of gastrointestinal SEs, in particular early-onset diarrhea [9,10]. This is usually managed and sometimes prevented with supportive care, such as early proactive treatment with antidiarrheal drugs (at the first signs of loose stools) or dose adjustments, with long-term patient-reported outcomes (PROs) showing maintenance of overall quality of life (QoL) [11]. However, current clinical trial data lack PROs of the first treatment months (when diarrhea is more prominent) and SEs are usually assessed using the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), a toxicity scoring system that assigns severity grades [12]. While the CTCAE provides an established framework for the classification of SEs, it may not fully capture the subjective experience and individual variations in how patients perceive and cope with them. Moreover, there is often discordance between patient- clinician-symptom severity ratings, with patients generally reporting SEs earlier and more frequently, and healthcare providers underestimating their severity [13–15]. This can result in poor symptom management and early treatment withdrawal.

As the number of patients receiving CDK4/6 inhibitors will likely increase, a more comprehensive understanding of how SEs affect patients’ daily lives is crucial for tailoring care and improving QoL. An increasing number of studies have used qualitative methods to investigate the experiences of breast cancer patients receiving CDK4/6 inhibitors, but this research is only cross-sectional and conducted with advanced or metastatic patients [16–20]. The current preliminary study employed thematic analysis to explore the perceptions and lived experiences of women with high-risk EBC during the first 8 weeks of treatment with abemaciclib combined with ET. It aimed to capture information on health beliefs and attitudes, their preferences and needs, SEs and management, and the impact of treatment on QoL and day-to-day life. This information could be used by clinicians to better support patients in managing treatment related SEs.

Methods

Ethics statement

The study received ethical approval (London Chelsea Research Ethics Committee, 21/PR/0853) and signed informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Study design

A qualitative longitudinal study design was chosen to capture patients’ perceptions and early experiences over time. This approach focuses on the temporality and allows for a comprehensive exploration of contexts and outcomes at an individual level [21].

Population and setting

Participants were identified at 4 UK breast units recruiting for a clinical trial randomizing high-risk EBC patients to abemaciclib and ET (clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04584853), as the study started before global regulatory approval was granted. Eligible women were 18 years of age or over and English-speaking. Patients received study information from the site staff. Contact details of those willing to be contacted were recorded and sent to the research team who approached the patient and discussed the interview study by phone.

Data collection

Three telephone interviews were conducted with each participant between December 2021 and June 2023. The first interview took place before the start of treatment and follow-up interviews were conducted 4 and 8 weeks later. A semi-structured interview schedule (with open-ended and probing questions) was developed by the research team. Average interview length was 24 min (range: 14–37 min). Audio-recorded interviews were transcribed verbatim and de-identified.

Data analysis

Interview transcripts were analyzed iteratively using the framework approach to thematic analysis, a frequently used method in healthcare research [22]. Central to this approach is an open-ended exploration and the development of a thematic framework specific to the study objectives. The framework and themes were discussed within the study team. Direct quotes were used to illustrate the findings. This brief report follows the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) format (Supplement material) [23].

Results

Recruitment and participants

A total of 5 women aged between 54 and 76 years were enrolled. Four were partnered. Three women had retired, and two were in a phased return to full-time work. Patients were between 4 and 12 months post-diagnosis. All had surgery, 3 received radiotherapy, and 1 had chemotherapy. All women had abemaciclib in combination with an aromatase inhibitor (letrozole, anastrozole). Dose modifications (suspension/reduction) were required in 4 participants due to neutropenia, diarrhea or COVID-19; one woman discontinued treatment (abemaciclib only) because of persistent diarrhea. At 8 weeks, 2 patients received abemaciclib at the standard 150 mg twice daily dose.

Perceptions and expectations prior to treatment

Table 1 displays an overview of patients’ experiences before the start of treatment. All showed a basic understanding of the treatment, mainly from the trial information provided by the hospital (quote [Q]1, Q2). They recalled discussions about SEs (Q4), including diarrhea. Main reasons for clinical trial participation were associated with altruism (e.g., helping others) and the potential for personal (therapeutic) benefit (Q3). Although there was unease about feeling unwell and managing SEs, particularly diarrhea (Q4-6). Some also expressed concerns about having to take multiple medications and the possibility of unintentional non-adherence (Q5).

Treatment experiences, impact and management

Patients’ experiences with abemaciclib combined with ET, including self-reported SEs and symptoms and clinical management, are summarized in Tables 2 and 3. During the follow-up interviews, all but one reported diarrhea, ranging from mild or manageable (Q7) to moderate-severe (Q8). Women also noted other gastrointestinal symptoms, including bloating, abdominal cramps or discomfort (Q9), indigestion (Q10), nausea (Q11) and loss of appetite (Q12). Fatigue was also reported by women in the discussions about SEs (Q13), together with reduced stamina (Q14). Symptoms associated with ET were frequently stated (Table 3), including mood swings, vasomotor symptoms, joint pain, weight changes and vaginal symptoms (Q15-19).

Diarrhea was reported as the most bothersome symptom by three women, followed by fatigue and heartburn. Impact of treatment on day-to-day living varied, but some felt restrained in their social (Q20) or family life (Q21). Treatment also interfered with relationships (Q22) and work commitments (Q23).

Prescribed (anti-diarrheal) medication or over-the-counter drugs (Q24-25, Q29) were frequently taken to control gastrointestinal symptoms. This was followed by dietary changes, including avoiding certain foods (Q26) or reducing food intake. Some women took precautionary steps and carried incontinence products when going out (Q27). Treatment beliefs (e.g., reduced risk of disease recurrence) helped some to cope with SEs (Q28), and patient nonadherence was not reported. Women generally felt supported by the clinical team (Q29-30) and encouraged regular contact for those starting the treatment.

Discussion

CDK4/6 inhibition is an important step-change in the treatment of HR+, HER2-EBC at high risk of recurrence [6,7]. However, early detection of symptoms and SEs and regular follow-up to see how patients manage these is important, as is shown in this preliminary small qualitative longitudinal research. Participants believed in the benefits of the treatment and understood the risks. They also valued initial conversations about potential SEs in preparation for treatment. During the first few weeks of treatment, diarrhea was the most prominent SE, followed by fatigue. This had a detrimental effect on personal relationships (family, friends and partnership), work responsibilities, QoL and day-to-day living during the first months of treatment. Symptom and SE management involved anti-diarrheal drugs and dietary modifications.

Previous qualitative research on patients’ experiences of CDK4/6 inhibitors confirmed that treatment with abemaciclib combined with ET is associated with profound diarrhea in subsets of patients, with significant consequences for patients’ roles and responsibilities and everyday life [19,20]. One report highlighted differences in perceptions of symptom burden, with patients perceiving symptoms as more burdensome compared to healthcare providers [17]. Other studies focused on adherence barriers and showed that knowledge, medication beliefs, a trusted provider–patient relationship, strong medical and social support, and minimal drug costs were important for optimal adherence [16,18]. It was suggested that assessing these factors at the start of treatment, and periodically thereafter, can help to identify issues earlier and address them sooner. However, all studies included (mixed groups of) patients with advanced or metastatic disease. When dealing with incurable disease, patients often make trade-offs and could be more willing to accept a higher symptom and SE burden for the potential benefit of prolonged survival [24,25]. Moreover, research showed that CDK4/6 inhibitors have a higher rate of toxicity in patients with advanced or metastatic breast cancer, probably due to their poorer general condition and longer duration of treatment [26]. This makes a comparison to our findings difficult.

Clinical implications

Consistent with the toxicity profile of abemaciclib in EBC [9], early-onset diarrhea was the most reported and bothersome SE, causing early treatment discontinuation (abemaciclib only) for one participant. Notably, participants also described a range of adjuvant ET concerns, including vasomotor symptoms, joint pain, mood swings and sexual health concerns, particularly vaginal dryness. Similar results were reported in a recent qualitative study into the lived experiences of breast cancer survivors concerning ET-induced SEs within the initial year of treatment [27]. The authors emphasized the need for personalized support for the diverse challenges faced and stressed the significance of the first year of ET in determining (long-term) adherence and overall compliance.

The risks and benefits of adjuvant abemaciclib and ET (including SEs and early prevention strategies) should therefore be thoughtfully and proactively discussed before the start of treatment, alongside frequent follow-ups (starting at the point of prescription), and accessible support and close symptom monitoring.

A patient-centered approach that incorporates PROs and QoL assessments can help clinicians gain a more complete understanding of impact of SEs and tailor their care. This can be delivered as a remote pathway as was shown in a recently conducted service evaluation in a large UK comprehensive cancer center [28]. This service included a centrally delivered, regional, remote adjuvant abemaciclib clinic, utilizing electronic PROs measures, blood close to home, medication prescribing/dispensing, and telephone consultations for clinical and toxicity management. It showed that care could be delivered efficiently and safely with high patient satisfaction.

Study limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results. Although not unusual for longitudinal interview studies [29], our sample size is small. Recruitment was hampered by starting a clinical study during the COVID-19 pandemic when many trials experienced disruptions, such as delayed enrollment and temporary recruitment pauses with restarts [30]. The patients in our study also participated in a clinical trial with strict eligibility criteria and are therefore a highly selected subset of patients. As part of the trial, they were closely monitored and dose modifications were handled as per study protocol to improve drug tolerability, which may not reflect a real-world situation. All together, these factors limit our preliminary findings. It is therefore important to conduct further comprehensive research in a real-world setting outside a clinical trial setting. This could, along with investigations how to best manage SEs, ensure successful treatment outcomes. Future studies should also include a longer follow-up period to consider treatment adherence, which is more likely to become an issue for patients who are on medication for longer periods. Such data are of value to health providers for whom the potential toxicity associated with abemaciclib combined with ET is a concern, and could also be used to develop interventions to alleviate SEs and optimize the adherence to this adjuvant oral treatment.

Conclusions

Despite these limitations, this preliminary qualitative interview study did provide some important insights of early treatment-related SEs and its management and patients’ concerns about possible restrictions to day-to-day activities. Early detection, assessment of patients’ needs and interventions for SEs is important, and further research is needed.
