Outcomes of Revision Modified Broström Procedure with Periosteal Flap Augmentation in Patients with Recurrent Chronic Ankle Instability
Authors: Jong Jin Moon, Myeong Jun Song, Jun Young Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough the modified Broström procedure (MBP) is widely accepted as the standard surgical treatment for chronic ankle instability (CAI), recurrent instability remains a clinical challenge, particularly in patients with poor tissue quality or high functional demands. To overcome these limitations, periosteal flap augmentation has been proposed as a biological alternative for patients with recurrent CAI after failed MBP. This study evaluated the clinical and radiographic outcomes of revision MBP with periosteal flap augmentation.MethodsFourteen patients (mean age, 29.5 years; range, 18–45 years) who underwent revision MBP combined with periosteal flap augmentation between 2018 and 2023 were retrospectively reviewed. The mean follow-up duration was 23.7 months (18–32 months). Clinical outcomes were assessed using the visual analog scale (VAS), American Orthopaedic Foot and Ankle Society (AOFAS) score, and Karlsson–Peterson score. Radiographic evaluation included talar tilt angle and anterior talar displacement.ResultsAt a mean follow-up of 23.7 months (18–32 months), all clinical scores improved significantly (VAS: 5.9 to 1.6; AOFAS: 64.1 to 90.6; Karlsson–Peterson: 50.7 to 90.0; p < 0.001). Radiographic stability also improved significantly (talar tilt: 10.7° to 4.9°; anterior displacement: 8.9 mm to 4.1 mm; p < 0.001). No recurrent instability was observed. Three patients had minor wound complications, all of which resolved with conservative treatment.ConclusionsRevision MBP with periosteal flap augmentation showed favorable short-term clinical and radiographic outcomes in patients with recurrent CAI after failed MBP. This technique may serve as a useful autologous reinforcement option in selected revision cases.

Chronic ankle instability (CAI) is a common complication after ankle sprains, especially among young and physically active individuals, and may lead to recurrent sprains, pain, functional limitation, and progressive degenerative change if left untreated. The modified Broström procedure (MBP) is widely accepted as the standard surgical treatment for CAI because it offers anatomic reconstruction with reliable long-term outcomes and minimal morbidity.123) However, recurrent instability may still occur in some patients, leading to failed MBP. Reported failure rates up to 10%–15% have been described, and revision surgery remains challenging.456)

Failure of the MBP may result from several reasons, including additional trauma, poor patient compliance, poor soft-tissue quality, inadequate postoperative protection, or poor surgical technique.4) In such situations, revision surgery is often technically demanding because the residual ligament tissue may be attenuated or scarred, and the surrounding soft tissue may be compromised. Several revision strategies have been introduced, including autologous tendon grafts, allograft tendon reconstruction, and synthetic augmentation such as suture tape. However, these procedures have limitations such as donor-site morbidity, increased cost, and foreign body reaction.789)

The periosteum has recently gained attention as a biologically favorable autologous material. It possesses considerable tensile strength, contains multipotent progenitor cells, and serves as a natural scaffold that promotes ligamentous healing.1011) Prior studies in primary CAI reported favorable outcomes using periosteal flap augmentation when combined with MBP.1213141516) However, to our knowledge, there has been no study specifically focused on revision MBP with periosteal flap augmentation after failed MBP.4)

Therefore, the purpose of this study was to evaluate the clinical and radiographic outcomes of revision MBP with periosteal flap augmentation in patients with recurrent CAI after failed MBP. We hypothesized that this procedure would provide favorable stability and functional recovery while avoiding donor-site complications or foreign body reactions associated with tendon grafts or synthetic materials.

METHODS

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Chosun University Hospital (IRB No. CHOSUN 2025-09-021). The requirement for informed consent was waived by the IRB due to the retrospective design and the use of de-identified data.

Between March 2018 and December 2023, 14 consecutive patients (14 ankles) with recurrent CAI after a failed MBP underwent revision MBP with periosteal flap augmentation by a single surgeon (JYL) at a single center (Table 1). The initial MBPs were performed at different hospitals by various surgeons. Most of the index procedures were performed through a longitudinal incision over the anterolateral ankle, and suture anchors were used for ligament repair (Table 2). Detailed information regarding anchor configuration, concomitant calcaneofibular ligament (CFL) treatment, and postoperative rehabilitation after the index procedure was incomplete because the primary surgeries had been performed at outside institutions.

Failure of the index MBP was defined as persistent or recurrent subjective ankle instability, including repeated sprains and giving-way episodes during activity, together with objective instability on physical examination and abnormal stress radiographic findings (talar tilt angle of 15° or more compared with the contralateral ankle) despite at least 6 months of conservative management such as peroneal muscle strengthening. Patients with generalized ligamentous laxity,15) osteochondral lesions requiring microfracture, or cavovarus deformity were excluded. There were 4 males and 10 females with a mean age of 29.5 years (18–45 years) and a mean follow-up duration of 23.7 months (18–32 months). The mean time between the initial surgery and the revision surgery was 34.3 months (9–96 months).

Surgical Technique

All procedures were performed under general or spinal anesthesia with the patient in the lateral decubitus position. A 5 cm curvilinear incision was made along the anterolateral aspect of the distal fibula, taking care to avoid the superficial peroneal nerve. The scar tissue surrounding the lateral ligament complex was carefully dissected, and the attenuated remnant of the anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL) and capsule was identified and mobilized. Two bioabsorbable suture anchors (MiniLok QuickAnchor Plus; DePuy, Mitek) were inserted at the fibular attachment site of the ATFL, and the residual ATFL and capsular tissue were advanced and repaired to the distal fibula with the ankle held in neutral dorsiflexion and 5° valgus.17)

The CFL was evaluated preoperatively using magnetic resonance imaging and intraoperatively by gross inspection and varus stress testing. Isolated CFL reconstruction was not routinely performed, and overall lateral ankle stability was managed primarily through ATFL repair with periosteal flap augmentation. Additional CFL reconstruction would have been considered in the presence of definite CFL insufficiency, persistent varus laxity after repair, or marked rotational instability.

Inferior extensor retinaculum (IER) reinforcement was attempted in all cases. When sufficient tissue quality and mobility were present, additional Gould-type reinforcement was performed. When the IER was attenuated or scarred from the index surgery, periosteal flap augmentation served as the primary reinforcement method. After repair of the lateral ligament complex, a fibular periosteal flap measuring approximately 1 × 3 cm was elevated from the distal fibula while preserving its distal attachment (Fig. 1). The flap was turned distally over the repaired ATFL and secured using absorbable mattress sutures (Fig. 2). Stability was checked after augmentation, and the wound was closed in layers.

Postoperative Management

A short-leg cast was applied with the ankle in the neutral position for 4 weeks after surgery, followed by an ankle brace for an additional 2 months. Partial weight-bearing began at 4 weeks, and full weight-bearing was allowed at 6 weeks. Range of motion exercises, peroneal strengthening, and proprioceptive rehabilitation were started after cast removal.

Clinical and Radiographic Evaluation

Clinical outcomes were assessed using the visual analog scale (VAS), the American Orthopaedic Foot and Ankle Society (AOFAS) ankle-hindfoot functional scores, and the Karlsson–Peterson scores.18) These measures were selected because they were the standard clinical outcome measures routinely collected at our institution during the study period and have also been widely used in prior periosteal flap augmentation studies. More contemporary patient-reported outcome measures, such as the Foot and Ankle Outcome Score (FAOS) and the Foot and Ankle Ability Measure (FAAM), were not consistently available for all patients in this retrospective cohort.

Stress radiographs were obtained using a standardized Telos device (TELOS). Talar tilt angle and anterior talar displacement were measured using the digital picture archiving and communication system (G7 PACS, INFINITT).19) Measurements were compared preoperatively and at last follow-up. To reduce measurement bias, each radiographic value was independently measured by 2 observers (JJM and MJS), who were not part of the operative team, and the mean value was used for analysis. Statistical analysis used paired t-tests with significance at p < 0.05. Mean differences and 95% CIs were calculated for all outcomes.

RESULTS

All patients demonstrated significant improvement in clinical scores at final follow-up. The mean VAS score decreased from 5.9 preoperatively to 1.6 postoperatively, the mean AOFAS score improved from 64.1 to 90.6, and the Karlsson–Peterson score increased from 50.7 to 90.0 (all p < 0.001) (Table 3).18) All patients returned to their previous activity level, and no recurrence of instability was observed. Radiographic parameters also improved significantly. The mean talar tilt angle decreased from 10.7° preoperatively to 4.9° at final follow-up, and the mean anterior talar displacement improved from 8.9 mm to 4.1 mm (all p < 0.001) (Table 3). Three patients had superficial wound problems that resolved with local wound care; no nerve injuries, graft failures, or recurrent instability occurred during follow-up.

DISCUSSION

This study suggests that revision MBP augmented with a periosteal flap can provide favorable short-term clinical and radiographic outcomes in patients with recurrent CAI after failed MBP.4) No recurrent instability was observed at a mean follow-up of 23.7 months, and both functional scores and stress radiographic parameters improved significantly. These findings support the use of periosteal flap augmentation as a useful autologous reinforcement option in selected revision settings.413)

Although the periosteum is generally regarded as a biologically active tissue,20) its biological contribution in the present technique should be interpreted cautiously. Because the periosteal flap is elevated and reflected, the cambium layer may not directly face the ligament repair interface, which could reduce its intrinsic regenerative effect at the repair site. Therefore, periosteal flap augmentation in this revision setting may be better understood as a hybrid strategy that provides immediate mechanical reinforcement while potentially offering supplementary biologic support through autologous tissue integration and preserved vascularity.

The management of the CFL also warrants discussion. Although the CFL contributes to rotational and subtalar stability, isolated CFL reconstruction was not routinely performed in the present series because satisfactory clinical stability was obtained through revision ATFL repair and periosteal flap augmentation. However, in revision cases with definite CFL insufficiency, persistent varus laxity, or marked rotational instability, additional CFL reconstruction may be appropriate.

Previous studies of periosteal flap augmentation in non-revision settings have reported favorable outcomes in patients with poor quality ligamentous tissue, large subfibular ossicles, generalized ligamentous laxity, high-demand activity, obesity, and previous failed surgery.131416212223) These studies suggest that periosteal flap augmentation may be a practical reinforcement strategy when standard repair alone is not sufficient. Our study extends this concept specifically to the revision setting after failed MBP.

The current literature on revision surgery after failed MBP remains limited. Previous revision studies have mainly focused on tendon graft reconstruction or suture tape augmentation. In particular, revision anatomical reconstruction augmented with suture tape has shown favorable intermediate-term outcomes and reliable restoration of mechanical stability.12) However, tendon graft reconstruction may require greater surgical invasiveness, graft harvest or allograft use, and higher cost, whereas suture tape augmentation introduces synthetic material and implant-related concerns.78912) In contrast, periosteal flap augmentation uses local autologous tissue through the same operative field and avoids both distant donor-site morbidity and synthetic implants. In this respect, periosteal flap augmentation may be considered a biologically favorable and technically simple reinforcement strategy for selected revision cases in which residual tissue is inadequate for standard repair alone, but formal tendon reconstruction is not considered necessary.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was small (n = 14), which limits statistical power and the ability to generalize the results. Second, the retrospective design is subject to selection bias and may not fully capture all confounding variables. Third, more contemporary patient-reported outcome measures such as FAOS and FAAM were not consistently available in this retrospective cohort. Fourth, the absence of a control group prevents direct comparison with other revision strategies such as tendon graft reconstruction or suture tape augmentation. Fifth, detailed information regarding the index MBP was incomplete and formal interobserver and intraobserver reliability coefficients for the stress radiographic measurements were not calculated. Finally, the follow-up period was relatively short (18–32 months), and quantitative biomechanical testing was not performed. Future research including extended follow-up of this study and comparative analyses against suture tape or tendon graft reconstructions may help establish the durability and broader clinical value of this technique.

Revision MBP with periosteal flap augmentation showed favorable short-term clinical and radiographic outcomes in patients with recurrent CAI after failed MBP. This technique may serve as a useful autologous reinforcement option in selected revision cases by combining anatomic repair with additional structural support. Further comparative studies with larger cohorts and longer follow-up are needed to define its role relative to tendon graft reconstruction and suture tape augmentation.
