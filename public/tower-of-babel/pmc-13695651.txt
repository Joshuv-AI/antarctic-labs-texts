QoQ-Med3: a multimodal reasoning foundation model for clinical analysis
Authors: David Dai, Jeannie She, Jiaee Cheong, Xing Han, Carl Harris, Haowen Wei, Farzan Vahedifard, Suchi Saria, Robert Stevens, Paul Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Multimodal reasoning-based foundation models (MRFMs) hold considerable promise for addressing key challenges in medical practice, yet their readiness for real-world deployment remains insufficiently explored. To bridge this gap, we developed two MRFMs (QoQ-Med3 and QoQ-Med3-MIMIC) and systematically evaluated their (i) generalizability to previously unseen clinical modalities and tasks, (ii) transferability to held-out datasets collected across different clinical sites, and (iii) robustness to real-world challenges like cross-site heterogeneity. Our results demonstrate that these models can learn transferable representations across modalities, tasks, and heterogeneous clinical datasets. QoQ-Med3 achieves an overall balanced accuracy of 71.3% and a F1 of 0.349, superceding all open-source and closed-source models, including GPT-4o, with particularly pronounced gains in understudied modalities such as ultrasound and mammography. The model trained on public clinical data only generalized to both the held-out MIMIC-IV and the private JHU PMAP dataset collected at Johns Hopkins University hospital. In addition, the extrinsic hallucination rates are reduced by 44.4 percent after training. Collectively, our findings highlight both the potential of multimodal reasoning-based clinical foundation models and the critical next steps required to make them robust and reliable for real-world deployment.

Introduction

AI for clinical diagnosis has evolved significantly over the past decade, with numerous computational models developed to assist clinicians in organizing patient records1,2, formulating diagnoses3,4, interpreting clinical images5,6, and other clinical tasks7. These advancements have substantially improved healthcare efficiency and accuracy across multiple specialties8,9. Recently, the rise of foundation models has enabled quick adaptation of pretrained models in the radiology10, psychiatry11, and ophthalmology12 domains. However, these methods still suffer from two main limitations. Firstly, clinical data spans multiple modalities across 1D (ECG, EEG), 2D (Chest X-ray, dermoscopy, mammography), and 3D (CT Scans, MRI). While existing models like BiomedGPT13 and MedFlamingo14 include vision, no existing model has been able to integrate both 1D sensor data with 2D/3D images. The heterogeneity across specialties and modalities15,16 often leads to settings where modalities compete rather than synergize, leading to three performance17–20. This necessitates careful retraining or fine-tuning strategies to balance heterogeneous distributions while enriching these models with clinical knowledge.

Secondly, despite the proliferation of foundation models for EHR, they remain predictive “black box” models that generate a single, definitive answer without revealing their underlying analytical process21–23. This significantly impedes their practical adoption in clinical settings, as healthcare professionals might hesitate to trust diagnostic suggestions without understanding the reasoning that produced them24. Transparency in the decision-making process is not merely a preference but a necessary component for responsible clinical implementation, regulatory compliance, and effective human-AI collaboration in healthcare environments25–27. This problem is exacerbated since models often fail silently in the face of heterogeneous temporality28,29, long context windows30, and cross-site heterogeneity in clinical workflows and coding practices31 within non-stationary healthcare environments that change data distributions both across time and population32,33. Recent benchmarking efforts have begun to address these gaps by evaluating fairness and interpretability across diverse populations34 and proposing clinically meaningful evaluation suites beyond ICU-restricted cohorts35, yet most models remain trained on narrowly-scoped datasets that provide limited insight into real-world utility30.

To tackle these two challenges of heterogeneity and interpretability, we leverage recent advances in multimodal reasoning foundation models (MRFMs) integrating diverse clinical modalities including imaging, time series, and textual records13,36–40. The representations learned by multimodal foundation models have proven effective for a variety of downstream healthcare-related data and tasks, including leveraging MRFMs to analyze highly heterogeneous Electronic Health Records (EHRs) and radiology data30,35,41,42. Secondly, GPT-543 and Deepseek R144 have inspired efforts to create specialized clinical reasoning systems45–47 capable of answering complex clinical questions and generating comprehensive clinical reports13,36. Reasoning allows models to think explicitly in a more logical and systematic way with evidence from the inputs and their own knowledge48, all of which are essential for robust clinical diagnosis and doctor-in-the-loop usage49,50.

Wornow et al. found that most models are trained on small, narrowly-scoped clinical datasets (e.g., MIMIC-III) or broad, public biomedical corpora (e.g., PubMed) and are evaluated on tasks that do not provide meaningful insights on their usefulness to health systems and further released a 141M parameter clinical foundation model pretrained on the structured EHR data of 2.57M patients30.Yu et al. presented a comprehensive benchmark that evaluates the performance, fairness, and interpretability of foundation models, both as unimodal encoders and as multimodal learners, using the publicly available MIMIC-IV database34. Zhu et al. trained a foundation model to predict the risk of Alzheimer’s disease using EHRs51. Sivarajkumar et al. introduced a multimodal foundation model that natively encodes structured EHR time series via a CNN-Transformer encoder and fuses it with unstructured EHRs through cross-modal attention into an LLaMA-based decoder.40 However, the biggest model size till date are no greater than 20B parameter. In contrast, our model is currently the largest open clinical MLLM in the field at a size of 32B. It is also the only model till date that can take in 1D, 2D (Chest X-ray, mammography, dermoscopy, and histopathology) and 3D (ultrasound, MRI, and CT scan) inputs. Moreover, by leveraging a stronger backbone, the different modalities are better aligned, which thus allows it to learn better representations across the different heterogeneous modalities, thus setting it apart from previous medical foundation models.

Renc et al. combined a system with foundation models to compute dynamic, personalized risk probabilities for clinician-defined critical events using MIMIC-IV42. Guo et al. attempted to improve the temporal robustness of EHR models via pre-training32. Pange et al. proposed a suite of clinically meaningful tasks spanning patient outcomes, early prediction of acute and chronic conditions, including desiderata for robust evaluations using EHR data consisting of 5 million patients from Columbia University Irving Medical Center (CUMC)35. Compared to existing works, our model is the only model that takes in time series data and output its thinking process, along with the bounding-box annotation highlighting the salient region made during the thinking process which are deemed essential for real-world deployment52–54. As a result of our stronger backbone and novel architecture choices, our model is better able to produce step-by-step explanations that link clinical knowledge to specific image features and patient data. This makes its predictions easier to trace and audit, as intermediate reasoning steps reveal how different modalities are combined. In contrast to earlier medical foundation models that often rely on implicit representations or shallow fusion, our models thus provide more transparent and structured justifications, allowing clinicians to better assess the validity of its conclusions and identify potential failure modes.

By marrying the two ideas, of multimodal fusion and cross-modality reasoning, we introduce a new set of multimodal clinical reasoning models that integrates visual, time series, and textual data for comprehensive analysis of clinical records and are capable of facilitating more holistic diagnostic reasoning37,55. As an example of our model’s capabilities in Fig. 1, we see that such models also advance model interpretability whilst allowing clinicians to assess the model’s diagnosis with ease. Building upon domain-aware relative policy optimization (DRPO), a reinforcement learning algorithm to enable reasoning over highly heterogeneous multimodal data, we present QoQ-Med3, an MRFM building on top of Qwen3-VL that sets state-of-the-art clinical diagnostic performance across seven clinical modalities, including ECG, CT scan, chest X-ray, fundus, ultrasound, mammography, and pathology. Upon QoQ-Med3, we aim to investigate: "how can large-scale MRFMs be effectively leveraged to improve performance and generalizability across diverse, multimodal downstream clinical tasks across real-world conditions and out-of-distribution settings?” We do so by answering the following research questions (RQs):RQ 1: Do MRFMs pretrained on different datasets reflect increased generalization and robustness to new clinical modalities and tasks? To answer this, we further design, propose and conduct experiments using new models, QoQ-Med3 and QoQ-Med3-MIMIC, and publicly release our model, training pipeline, and reasoning traces generated by the model across 2.61 million question-answer pairs at this link. This marks one of the largest resources for transparent and reproducible multimodal reasoning in the clinical domain. We further conduct modality ablation and real-world clinician analysis to understand how the different modalities contribute to the eventual reasoning-based clinical outcome. Our findings show that QoQ-Med3 trained with DRPO achieves an overall balanced accuracy of 71.3% and an F1 of 0.349, a 14.7 percentage point improvement over the strongest baseline, with particularly pronounced gains in understudied modalities such as ultrasound and mammography. RQ 2: Are the findings from foundation models pretrained on non-EHR data generalizable to and capable of improving medical diagnosis on EHR data collected from different clinical sites and settings? To answer this RQ, we investigate how the performance of a medical foundation model pretrained on CLIMB, a non-EHR collection of medical datasets, transfers to MIMIC-IV, a widely used benchmark for EHR-based clinical prediction. Further, we systematically compare model performance across the MIMIC-IV and Johns Hopkins University (JHU) datasets, examining scalability and generalizability across institutions, clinical specialties, and care settings. Our results show that QoQ-Med3 pretrained on non-EHR data (CLIMB) achieves 3.4-fold higher BioBERT similarity and 8.1% higher accuracy than the base model on MIMIC-IV in a zeroshot setting, with further gains when combined with MIMIC-IV finetuning. These improvements generalize to the held-out JHU dataset, as models trained on MIMIC or CLIMB exhibit superior performance on the JHU dataset as well.

RQ 3: How robust are medical foundation models to failure modes such as model hallucinations, and how well do they perform when evaluated on outcomes beyond prediction? To address this question, we conduct experimental analyses to examine model robustness, assess potential biases across diverse patient populations through subgroup analyses, and systematically evaluate the prevalence and characteristics of model hallucinations. In particular, we curate an exhaustive criteria to judge incorrect model outputs in terms of intrinsic and extrinsic hallucinations, and identify substantial concordance amongst two human annotators and one LLM-as-a-judge (Fleiss’ κ = 0.792). We see that the number of extrinsic hallucinations reduces by 44.4% after training on CLIMB and MIMIC datasets, thereby enforcing that domain-specific training strongly improves model reasoning performance.

Performance on CLIMB

CLIMB, introduced by ref. 55, is a large-scale clinical diagnosis dataset across 13 modalities. In the training of QoQ-Med3, we utilize eight modalities, including ECG, CT scan, chest X-ray, fundus, ultrasound, mammography, pathology, and MRI. Table 2 presents a comprehensive comparison of QoQ-Med and QoQ-Med3, our latest model, against several critic-free RL training methods across eight medical imaging modalities. Baselines include ReMax56, Reinforce++57, RLOO58, GRPO59, which are popular MRFM post-training methods that are known to elicit the model’s reasoning capabilities. QoQ-Med3, trained with DRPO, is a novel training method that is optimized specifically for highly heterogeneous multimodal data. We note that the overall F1 is macro-averaged across all classes, including rare conditions with very few positive samples, which produces lower absolute values than micro-averaged or binary metrics. In this setting, relative improvements are more informative. QoQ-Med3 achieves a 72.8% relative F1 improvement over the strongest baseline (0.349 vs. 0.202 for RE++). We examined when QoQ-Med3-MIMIC hallucinated on MIMIC-IV data in order to investigate its performance on demographic disparities in a clinical context. Considering that we infer only from the hallucination analysis completed by the LLM-as-a-judge, these observations should not be interpreted as evidence of systematic bias in model behavior, but rather potential insight and mere exploration into an unintentional model skew.

The model was not provided with race or insurance information at any stage of training or evaluation. By excluding these features, we were able to examine demographic differences in model performance independent of explicit demographic conditioning. In Fig. 6b, e, hallucination rates are higher for less prevalent racial groups in the training data. Asian patients show hallucination rates 14.5 percentage points above those of White patients, while the remaining aggregated racial groups exceed the White rate by 1.76 percentage points on average. This pattern suggests that under-representation in the training distribution may be associated with elevated hallucination risk. A similar, but weaker, trend is observed for insurance status in Fig. 6d, g, where less prevalent insurance categories—Medicaid and Other—exhibit hallucination rates comparable to those of more common insurance types, despite their lower representation in the training data.

In contrast, gender, which is an attribute explicitly provided as part of a patient’s history to the model, does not exhibit this disparity. As shown in Fig. 6c, f, hallucination rates for Female and Male patients closely mirror their respective prevalence in the training dataset. This alignment indicates no measurable gender-specific amplification of hallucinations and suggests that explicitly modeled demographic attributes may be less susceptible to representation-driven hallucination effects.

Discussion

As in RQ 1, we explored the question “RQ 1: Do multimodal foundation models pretrained on different datasets reflect increased generalization and robustness to new clinical modalities and tasks?” Our results demonstrate that large-scale multimodal clinical pretraining provides a strong foundation that enhances downstream task performance after domain-specific finetuning. Specifically, we found that vision and text contribute more to the final accuracy and F1 scores than ECG. The model also demonstrates a sophisticated understanding of imaging physics. In the brain tumor cases in Fig. 1d, the model correctly discusses T1 and T2 signal characteristics, noting that tumors often appear “hyperintense on T2” due to associated edema and altered tissue properties. It identifies the tumor as “round, well-circumscribed” with a “clear boundary” from surrounding tissue and “clearly distinct from the surrounding brain tissue”, which are all clinically relevant observations that support the diagnosis. Notably, in the negative case shown in the Supplement, the model appropriately considers meningioma characteristics but ultimately reaches the correct “No Tumor” conclusion despite some reasoning about tumor features, demonstrating appropriate calibration.

To evaluate the clinical utility and real-world readiness of QoQ-Med3’s reasoning process, we collaborated with clinicians to annotate reasoning traces on randomly selected validation samples, with phrases marked by relevance to diagnosis: high (pink), low (yellow), or none (aqua). In general, we see around 45.3% of reasoning traces rated as highly relevant, and 100% of the rated samples contain highly relevant reasoning traces. Fig. 1 presents representative examples. Clinician review confirmed that the majority of reasoning steps corresponded to diagnostically relevant evidence with minimal unsupported content.

While the clinician annotation study presented here provides initial evidence of clinical utility, we acknowledge that this represents a retrospective evaluation on held-out validation data. The generalization to the held-out JHU PMAP dataset, collected independently at Johns Hopkins University across 845,904 patients and 8.3 million encounters, provides evidence of cross-site robustness that is a necessary precondition for prospective evaluation. Our hallucination analysis further establishes the safety characterization needed before clinical deployment, showing a 78.3% reduction in extrinsic hallucinations after training. We view the current work as establishing the technical foundation, with prospective clinician-in-the-loop evaluation as the natural next step. Fig. 1.

e further highlights the model’s ability to combine multimodal inputs for complex clinical predictions. To estimate hospital length of stay, it integrates chest X-ray findings (“no obvious acute pathology”), ECG interpretation (“unremarkable... no signs of acute heart failure”), and laboratory values (“normal... no life-threatening abnormalities”) to predict a short stay. This kind of holistic reasoning across modalities is beyond the capability of unimodal models.

As in RQ 2, we explored the question “RQ 2: Are the findings from foundation models pretrained on non-EHR data generalizable to and capable of improving medical diagnosis on EHR data collected from different clinical sites and settings?” Our findings suggest that clinical knowledge acquired through large-scale multimodal pretraining on non-EHR data CLIMB, which encompasses diverse diagnostic reasoning tasks, transfers effectively to downstream EHR data collected from different clinical sites and settings across several clinical prediction tasks including diagnosis, survival prediction, and length-of-stay estimation. Importantly, the strong zeroshot transfer results suggest that the model does not merely memorize dataset-specific patterns but acquires generalizable clinical reasoning capabilities. QoQ-Med3-ZeroShot, having never seen any MIMIC-IV data during training, still substantially outperforms the base model across all MIMIC-IV task types (Fig. 3). This indicates that the CLIMB training corpus, while large, serves primarily to teach transferable multimodal clinical reasoning.

We hypothesize that reasoning-enabled models perform better because they can explicitly recall and apply domain-specific clinical knowledge during inference. For instance, in Fig. 1a, the model recalls that hemorrhage appears differently by age and type—hypodense (dark) regions suggest acute bleeding, while hyperdense (bright) areas indicate older clots. It then examines the scan and notes that “the tissue appears uniformly gray, without any obvious bright or dark areas,” correctly predicting no hemorrhage. This step-by-step link from general knowledge to image-specific evidence provides an interpretable explanation. Similarly, in Fig. 1b, the model applies dermoscopic criteria to distinguish diagnoses. It describes the lesion as “slightly raised” and “pinkish,” with a “rough, scaly surface,” consistent with sun-damaged skin in a sun-exposed area. It then systematically evaluates alternatives—melanoma, nevus, BCC, and AKIEC—before selecting BCC based on the accumulated evidence. This structured reasoning mirrors clinical decision-making and makes the model’s conclusions more transparent.

The full trace in the Supplement reveals the model systematically considers each differential diagnosis—melanoma, nevus, BCC, and AKIEC, before converging on BCC based on the cumulative evidence. This structured elimination process mirrors clinical decision-making and further provides transparency into how the model weighs competing hypotheses.

As in RQ 3, we explored the question “how robust are medical foundation models to failure modes such as model hallucinations, and how well do they perform when evaluated on outcomes beyond prediction?” Although the model usually produces clinically relevant observations, it is not always robust and can hallucinate. It sometimes mistakes a past admission for the current admission, follows a faulty reasoning trace, or fails to integrate its findings into the final prediction. For instance, in Fig. 1c, the model correctly notes that “the x-ray shows a pacemaker, which is a medical device,” and even recognizes this as a normal finding. However, it still predicts “No finding” instead of “support devices,” indicating a disconnect between reasoning and classification. The full trace in the Supplement shows further issues: the model hallucinates findings such as “increased lung density” and “pleural effusion,” and spends considerable effort on irrelevant details like patient positioning and radiographic markers. This example shows that identifying individual features does not ensure a correct diagnosis if they are not properly weighted and combined.

In addition, the model struggles with fine-grained distinctions between similar conditions. For example, in lung ultrasound cases, it predicts “pneumonia” when the ground truth is “COVID-19.” While incorrect, its reasoning identifies relevant features—"hyperechoic areas,” “consolidation,” and patterns typical of pneumonia. Because COVID-19 and bacterial pneumonia often share ultrasound findings, distinguishing them usually requires clinical context (e.g., exposure history or lab markers) not available in the image. This suggests that some errors reflect inherent diagnostic ambiguity rather than flawed reasoning. Such nuances are not captured by standard accuracy metrics but become visible through reasoning traces. Prospective clinician-in-the-loop evaluation is needed to assess real-world safety and performance under a distribution shift.

As highlighted in our hallucination analysis, extrinsic hallucinations remain the principal bottleneck for reliable clinical deployment and real-world readiness. While this suggests limitations of the current models, the judge model itself may also exhibit inaccuracies—in this experiment, we used GPT-5.5. The judge model may wrongly claim some part of the reasoning process is “hallucinated” due to its own limited understanding of clinical contexts. Additionally, our two human annotators comprise a small sample size of human review.

Finally, all judges in this analysis viewed only text data for rapid evaluation, which may skew the hallucination judgment. Future works should explore annotating the hallucination with clinical experts, stronger models, and multimodal input, in order to provide more accurate classification evaluation before real-world deployment.

While QoQ-Med3 represents a first step towards multimodal reasoning models across vision and time series, future works could explore better architectures, data, or training methods that better balances the power of each modalities. The annotated traces also reveal patterns of low-relevance content generation. Across multiple examples, the model produces introductory statements (“To analyze the image, let’s go through the reasoning process step by step”) and generic explanations of imaging modalities that, while technically accurate, do not contribute to the specific diagnosis. We emphasize that clinician annotations consistently mark these segments in yellow (low relevance), suggesting opportunities for training refinements that encourage more focused, diagnostically pertinent reasoning. In particular, future work could explore how to best annotate reasoning traces at scale and then use the resulting data to guide the model toward reasoning most efficiently, for instance by penalizing generic introductory statements so that the reasoning concentrates on diagnostically pertinent evidence.

A further design consideration concerns our use of a frozen ECG-JEPA encoder for the time series modality. Keeping the encoder frozen ensures a robust and stable representation of the time series input, as no additional gradient updates are applied that could degrade or destabilize the features learned during large-scale ECG pretraining. The trade-off is that the time series representation is not explicitly aligned with the downstream diagnostic objective: unlike the vision and text pathways, the 1D features are not natively learned during multimodal training and may therefore be less tailored to the final clinical understanding than if they were optimized jointly with the rest of the model. This reflects a tension between representational robustness and task-specific alignment. Future work should explore jointly training the time series encoder together with the main model, for instance, through gradual unfreezing or low learning-rate fine-tuning, to better align the 1D representation with downstream reasoning while preserving the robustness afforded by pretraining.

While QoQ-Med3 exhibits a lower hallucination rate than other open-source models, and demonstrates the best diagnostic performance against commercial models, we caution that the model still hallucinates some answers throughout the validation set. Mitigating intrinsic hallucinations in particular is a critical step for prospective model deployment. These results strengthen the case for exploring how changes in architecture, refinement of the task definition, adjustments in training objective, or selection of feature exclusion, masking, or aggregation during training may affect the downstream behavior of the model, particularly in the case of either amplifying or reducing perpetuated biases in demographic disparities in healthcare.

In conclusion, we developed two multimodal reasoning-based foundation models and tested them on a held-out MIMIC-IV and a private JHU PMAP dataset collected at Johns Hopkins University Hospital. Results indicate large-scale multimodal clinical pretraining provides a strong foundation that enhances downstream robust task performance after domain-specific finetuning. Further, knowledge acquired through large-scale multimodal pretraining on non-EHR data is largely transferable to EHR data analysis using our model. Our reasoning mechanism also enhanced model interpretability and led to a reduction in hallucination behavior.

Methods

With our training method and unified multimodal model, we design two variants of the model: QoQ-Med3 and QoQ-Med3-MIMIC. We train QoQ-Med3 on CLIMB55, a combination of 33 multimodal datasets spanning across 1D (ECG), 2D (chest X-ray, mammography, dermoscopy, histopathology, fundus), and 3D (ultrasound, MRI, CT scan) data with a total size of 2.61 samples. With QoQ-Med3, we analyze the model’s trained performance handling diverse multimodal data for basic diagnosis of diseases. With QoQ-Med3-MIMIC, on the other hand, we test the model’s ability to integrate highly multimodal data and long context EHR, and the ability to conduct complex multistep reasoning under minimal supervision.

For multi-label tasks, predictions are judged with an order-independent match: a response is deemed correct if all ground truth labels are present in the answer, and no other labels are present, irrespective of label ordering. This criterion accommodates scenarios where several conditions may be present simultaneously and listed in any sequence. In addition, a subset of the clinical modalities presented in CLIMB are labeled as the following three categories:

(1) Novel tasks. Novel tasks are defined as new diseases or conditions not previously present. Examples include COVID X-ray and COVID ultrasound.

(2) Understudied modalities. Building on the criteria set forth in the original study, we characterize each modality along two axes: (i) research attention, estimated by the number of Google Scholar hits returned for standardized queries such as “[modality] classification,” and (ii) public data availability, counted as the total number of openly released samples. Based on both criteria, mammography and ECG appear understudied in the literature, whereas ultrasound is chiefly limited by data scarcity.

(3) Underrepresented regions. The underrepresented regions are defined as datasets collected from regions historically underrepresented regions (like Africa, South America and Parts of South and Southeast Asia), or regions with less economic development (like India, Vietnam, Iraq, and Brazil).

These categories demonstrates datasets with particular clinical significance, due to their limited representation in prior studies.

As shown in Fig. 9, we initialize QoQ-Med3 from a large pretrained vision-language model comprising an image encoder, a linear projection that maps each visual patch embedding into the backbone LLM’s token space, and the LLM. To ingest temporal data, we prepend a pretrained time series encoder, namely ECG-JEPA66, whose outputs are passed through a newly initialized linear projection of matching dimension. At inference, the projected image patches, time series tokens, and tokenized text are interleaved in their original temporal order and fed to the LLM. The LLM autoregressively generates a free-text chain-of thought, bounding-box tokens that localize the evidence identified in that reasoning, and outputs a short diagnosis. This design supports heterogeneous modality combinations, allowing the model to skip missing channels while preserving positional consistency across the multimodal sequence. Comparing with the previous generation model QoQ-Med, QoQ-Med3 in this work features updated backbone, Qwen3-VL-8B, allowing it to reach better performance than the previous generation. We adopt ECG-JEPA as a pretrained time series encoder for ECG representation extraction. Our framework enables integrating these representations with vision and text through the LLM backbone for cross-modal reasoning.Fig. 9Overview of QoQ-Med3.The training corpus spans 11 clinical domains, including structured waveforms (e.g., ECG), diverse imaging modalities, electronic health records, and curated clinical QA pairs. Training process

Training proceeds in two stages.

Stage 1: Modality alignment. Since we initialize the projection layer from scratch, we first train and align the ECG encoder, the projection layer, and the LLM. To encourage high-quality reasoning outputs from the beginning, we use the same DRPO training as in Stage 2.

Stage 2: Multimodal fine-tuning with DRPO. We train on the full multimodal corpus with domain-aware relative policy optimization (DRPO), a reinforcement learning training method that balances training across different samples in various domains and difficulty. In this stage, we aim to simultaneously improve the diagnostic accuracy and reasoning quality. In this work, we further tune the model with a downstream task with MIMIC-IV, providing a detailed analysis of its performance with complex tasks requiring multistep reasoning.

QoQ-Med3-8B trains in approximately 48 h on 8 × NVIDIA A100 GPUs with a per-device batch size of 4 and rollout batch size of 512. The 32B variant requires approximately 2 weeks on 8 × H200 GPUs. Compared to PPO-based alternatives that require a separate value network of comparable size to the policy, DRPO adds negligible overhead to the standard GRPO training loop: as shown in Supplementary Table 4, DRPO requires 429 seconds per training step compared to 425 seconds for GRPO, an increase of less than 1%. The total compute for the 8B model is ~768 A100-GPU-hours, making the approach practical for academic research groups with standard cluster access.

Domain-aware relative policy optimization (DRPO)

Group relative policy optimization (GRPO) is a reinforcement learning method that gained prominence following the success of DeepSeek-R1. Unlike proximal policy optimization (PPO), which relies on a separate value network to estimate advantages, GRPO directly computes the advantage \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\hat{A}}_{(q,i,t)}$$\end{document}A^(q,i,t) for each response within a group of rollouts G(q, t) at a given training iteration. A rollout refers to a single sampled trajectory or response generated by the policy in reaction to a prompt. The advantage quantifies how much better a particular rollout is compared to others generated for the same prompt, enabling the policy to prioritize relatively high-quality responses without requiring an explicit estimate of expected return. Measures

For all experiments on multiple-choice questions, we use balanced accuracy and F1 scores as performance metrics. For open-ended questions, we use BioBERT similarity as the primary measure of performance. A detailed description of metrics is included in the Supplement.

Failure mode analysis

When a model evaluates multiple-choice tasks in a validation setting, model outputs are fully determined by its parametric knowledge. Under this condition, any incorrect model output necessarily reflects one of two outcomes: either a misjudgment of the admission to be evaluated in the patient’s context (intrinsic hallucination), or a gap in the model’s knowledge (extrinsic hallucination). We apply these two categories exhaustively to incorrect responses to the multiple-choice tasks in order to characterize failure modes.

Starting with our validation dataset, we filter out open-ended tasks and objectively correct model outputs, resulting in a total of 2495 incorrect, multiple-choice, validation items across all four models. To investigate the failure modes of QoQ-Med-MIMIC against the other three model variants, we utilize GPT-5.5 to perform downstream hallucination analysis on all 2495 items, and randomly select 100 samples encompassing all model variants for two human annotators to evaluate on the same criteria.

The criteria is defined as follows:

(i) Intrinsic hallucination: The model answered using or referencing the wrong admission in the patient’s history—e.g., it grounded its answer in a prior admission or unrelated history instead of the current admission. The error is in the admission identity.

(ii) Extrinsic hallucination: The model correctly identified the current admission and its details, but drew an incorrect clinical conclusion from them, or fabricated a detail not present in the current admission. The error is in the reasoning.

To encourage improved accuracy by the LLM-as-a-judge, the prompt includes instructions to perform 2–3 sentences worth of reasoning before producing a final one-word label. The full system prompt is included in the Supplement.
