Association of thromboelastogram hypercoagulability with postoperative deep venous thrombosis of the lower extremity in patients with femur and pelvic fractures: a cohort study
Authors: Peiyao Cheng, Bo Cheng, Linqin Wu, Hui Zhang, Yitong Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe relationship between thromboelastogram (TEG) hypercoagulation status and perioperative deep vein thrombosis (DVT) in patients with femoral and pelvic fractures is not well understood. We aimed to investigate the relationship between hypercoagulation status identified by thromboelastography and postoperative DVT formation in patients with femoral and pelvic fractures, as well as to evaluate the role of thromboelastography in assessing hypercoagulation status and predicting postoperative DVT formation.MethodsData from 2,065 patients with femoral and pelvic fractures who underwent surgical treatment at a hospital in China between May 2018 and December 2023 were retrospectively analysed. Hypercoagulable TEG was defined as reaction time (R) < 5 min, coagulation time (K) < 1 min, alpha angle (α) > 72 degrees, maximum amplitude (MA) > 70 mm, and/or coagulation index (CI) > 3. The correlation between preoperative hypercoagulability identified by TEG and postoperative DVT formation was assessed using multivariate logistic regression. Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed to control for confounding factors.ResultsCompared to the non-DVT group, the DVT group had decreased R and K values, while the α, MA, and CI values significantly increased (P < 0.05). Multivariate logistic regression analysis demonstrated that hypercoagulable TEG findings were predictive of postoperative DVT formation. PSM, using a 0.1 calliper value, matched 296 patients from the hypercoagulation and non-hypercoagulation groups in a 1:1 ratio. Before PSM, hypercoagulable TEG was associated with DVT in femoral and pelvic fractures (P < 0.001, odds ratio [OR]:1.860, 95% confidence interval: 1.389–2.492). After PSM, these two variables remained correlated (P = 0.001, OR = 1.878, 95% confidence interval:1.301 − 2.709).ConclusionsThe hypercoagulable state identified by TEG can predict thromboembolic events in patients with femoral and pelvic fractures.Trial registrationThe study was registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Register (https://www.chictr.org.cn/bin/home) on April 16, 2024, with registration number ChiCTR2400083135.Keywords: Fracture, Deep vein thrombosis, Thromboelastography, Propensity score matching

Background

Venous thromboembolism (VTE), encompassing deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE), is a common complication in individuals with bone injuries. Despite thrombus prophylaxis, the perioperative incidence of DVT in patients with femoral neck fractures remains high, at 28% [1]. Conventional coagulation tests (CCT) are used to monitor the coagulation status of patients with bone trauma. However, CCT cannot comprehensively evaluate the coagulation process, diagnose hypercoagulation status, or predict DVT formation [2, 3].

A thromboelastogram (TEG) is a graph drawn by a thromboelastometer that can dynamically monitor the process from blood coagulation to fibrinolysis; it provides a detailed and accurate reflection of the coagulation state in patients with traumatic injuries [4]. TEG can be used to monitor hypercoagulability in patients undergoing surgery to guide personalised blood transfusion and perioperative coagulation monitoring [5, 6]. However, the correlation between TEG parameters and perioperative DVT remains controversial. Assab et al. and Parameswaran et al. reported that TEG is unable to distinguish between patients with and without VTE [7, 8], while studies by Mao et al., Brown et al., and Tsantes et al. confirmed that TEG can predict thrombotic complications [9–11]. However, recent studies on the correlation between these two factors have shown contradictory results. Therefore, we aimed to conduct a retrospective cohort study to determine if TEG-identified hypercoagulability was associated with postoperative DVT formation. We hypothesised that patients with hypercoagulable trauma identified by TEG would have a higher incidence of DVT than those in the non-hypercoagulable group.

Methods

Data collection

A retrospective analysis was conducted on patients with femoral and pelvic fractures who underwent surgical treatment at The First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University between May 2018 and December 2023. The inclusion criteria were: (1) age > 18 years, (2) surgical intervention for trauma-induced femoral and pelvic fractures, and (3) availability of complete medical records. Exclusion criteria were: (1) long-term anticoagulant use; (2) blood system disease or coagulation dysfunction; (3) pregnancy; (4) severe heart, liver, kidney, or brain diseases preventing surgery; and (5) a history of DVT.

Clinical data of patients were obtained from the electronic medical record and surgical anaesthesia systems. All participants underwent preoperative and postoperative pulsed Doppler ultrasonography of both lower limbs using a C5-1 linear probe and IU22 system (Philips ATL, Bothwell, WA, USA). The positive criteria for DVT included venous incompressibility, intravascular filling defects, and absence of Doppler signals. Routine scans included the common femoral, superficial femoral, deep femoral, popliteal, posterior tibial, peroneal, and intermuscular veins. Proximal thrombus is defined as a clot located in the popliteal, femoral, or iliac veins. Distal thrombus is defined as a clot in the lower leg veins located below the knee (excluding popliteal veins) or limited to the lower leg veins (peroneal, anterior tibial, posterior, and intermuscular veins). Mixed thrombus is defined as the presence of both distal and proximal thrombus. The surgery outcomes included new postoperative DVT formation or extended preoperative DVT progression were the outcome events. Primary study variables included preoperative TEG indicators. Possible DVT-related covariates were sex, age, body mass index (BMI), smoking history, drinking history, American Society of Anaesthesiologists (ASA) rating, and other basic data such as diabetes, hypertension, coronary heart disease, renal insufficiency, liver disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), pulmonary infection, malignant tumour, osteoporosis, hypoproteinaemia, anaemia, and other complications; trauma type (multiple injuries, high-energy trauma), fracture type (femoral neck fracture, intertrochanteric fracture, pelvic fracture, and femoral shaft fracture), time from trauma to surgery, preoperative anticoagulation, and perioperative blood transfusion. The results of conventional coagulation function tests, including prothrombin time (PT), international normalized ratio (INR), fibrin degradation products (FDP), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), fibrinogen (FIB), D-dimer, and platelet count, were obtained.

TEG

Before surgery, 1 mL of fasting arterial blood was collected from all participants. The blood samples were utilised for standard citrated kaolin-activated TEGs, conducted using the Thrombelastograph® Analyzer, Model 5000 (Haemonetics Corporation, Braintree, MA, USA). Proficient personnel conducted all TEG tests and recorded values, including reaction time (R), coagulation time (K), alpha angle (α), maximum amplitude (MA), and coagulation index (CI).

The R-value refers to the duration from the initiation of the trial until early blood clot formation, reflecting the cumulative impact of coagulation factors. The normal range for R is 5–10 min, with a value exceeding 10 min indicating a hypocoagulable state and a value below 5 min indicating a hypercoagulable state. The K value refers to the time from the completion of coagulation response time detection to the attainment of a 20 mm amplitude, reflecting the combined action of fibrinogen and some platelets during initial clot formation. The normal range for the K value is 1–3 min; >3 min indicates a hypocoagulable state, while < 1 min suggests a hypercoagulable state. The alpha angle refers to the arc of the curve, which represents the angle between the tangent line and the horizontal line when the amplitude reaches 20 mm. It serves as an indicator of blood clot formation rate and is influenced by fibrinogen, clotting factors, and platelet activity. In normal cases, the alpha angle ranges from 53°−72°. An alpha angle > 72° indicates a high condensation state, while an angle < 53° suggests a low condensation state. The K value is closely associated with the alpha angle and primarily reflects fibrinogen levels, coagulation factor activity, and platelet function. The MA value refers to the maximum vertical distance of the blood clot formation curve, primarily indicating the peak intensity of blood clotting and providing insights into platelet function and count. Normally, the maximum amplitude should range between 50 and 70 mm. Values exceeding 70 mm suggest potential increases in platelet count or hyperfunction, while values below 50 mm indicate possible decreases in platelet count or impaired function. The CI value serves as an indicator of clotting status, reflecting the comprehensive strength of clots resulting from intricate interactions among all coagulation factors, platelets, and fibrinogen. In normal circumstances, the optimal range for the CI value is between − 3.0 and 3.0, with values exceeding 3.0 indicating a heightened state of coagulation while values below − 3.0 suggest a diminished state of coagulation. This study defined TEG hypercoagulability as R < 5 min, K < 1 min, α > 72°, MA > 70 mm, and/or CI > 3.

Statistical methods

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 22.0 software. Chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests were employed to analyse the categorical data, with the outcomes presented as percentages (%). The Shapiro − Wilk test was used to evaluate normal distribution, while the mean ± standard deviation (x ± s) was obtained through an independent sample t-test. For non-normally distributed measurements, the Mann − Whitney U test was used, with results presented as median (quartile) [M (IQR)]. Binary logistic regression analysis was used to identify risk factors for postoperative DVT and calculate odds ratios (OR) with a 95% confidence interval (95% CI). Variables with a p-value < 0.05 were included in multiple regression analysis to account for confounders and determine any correlation between TEG-detected hypercoagulable state and postoperative DVT occurrence.

Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed using the R plugin PSM in SPSS to control for confounding factors. PSM was used to establish a logistic regression model, matching the hypercoagulable and non-hypercoagulable groups based on preoperative TEG results. DVT-related variables were used as covariates, and the hypercoagulable and non-hypercoagulable groups were matched according to the nearest neighbour matching method, with 0.1 as the calliper value. The matching effect of PSM was assessed using the standardised difference method, and the standard mean difference (SMD) was calculated. An SMD absolute value < 0.1 indicated a good matching effect. Adjusted OR and 95% CI were calculated using binary logistic regression to evaluate the correlation between TEG parameters and DVT occurrence. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

A total of 2,065 patients were enrolled in the study based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. A retrospective follow-up showed that 517 patients (25.04%) did not undergo preoperative TEG examination, and 642 patients (31.09%) did not undergo postoperative venous ultrasonography of the lower limbs, resulting in 981 patients with incomplete perioperative examination. The patients were divided into two groups: incomplete examination (981 patients) and complete examination (1,084 patients). Comparison between the two groups showed no statistically significant difference in general characteristics, underlying diseases, anticoagulation, and other factors (P > 0.05), indicating balanced patient distributions in the two groups. Therefore, 981 patients with incomplete preoperative TEG examinations and those with no postoperative lower-extremity venous ultrasonography findings were excluded. A total of 1,084 patients, comprising 455 males and 629 females, with a mean age of 68.32 ± 18.52 years (range: 19–112 years), were included in this study. Research flow chart

The total incidence of perioperative DVT was 45.76% (496/1084), with a preoperative incidence of 29.80% (323/1084) and a postoperative incidence of 25.37% (275/1084). Among the 275 cases of postoperative thrombosis, there were 173 new cases and 102 advanced cases. The incidence of intramuscular vein thrombosis was the highest at 87.2%. The type of thrombus in patients with postoperative deep vein thrombosis included proximal thrombus in 11 cases (4.00%), distal thrombus in 237 cases (86.18%), and mixed thrombus in 27 cases (9.82%). Pulmonary embolism occurred in seven patients, with six having distal thrombus and one having mixed thrombus.The study included 414 patients with femoral neck fracture, 339 with intertrochanteric fracture, 208 with pelvic fracture, and 123 with femoral shaft fracture. The incidence rates of DVT were 18.4% (76/414) for femoral neck fracture, 30.7% (104/339) for intertrochanteric fracture, 24.5% (51/208) for pelvic fracture, and 35.8% (44/123) for femoral shaft fractures.

Clinical features of the DVT and non-DVT groups

Of the 1,084 patients, 275 (25.4%) were included in the DVT group and 809 (74.6%) in the non-DVT group. The clinical characteristics of the patients in the DVT and non-DVT groups are shown in Table 1. The average age of patients in the DVT group was higher than that of the non-DVT group. The incidence of coronary heart disease, pulmonary infection, preoperative anaemia, perioperative blood transfusion, and hypoproteinaemia was higher in the DVT group compared to the non-DVT group, while the incidence of malignant tumours and osteoporosis was lower in the DVT group than in the non-DVT group, with statistical significance. However, there were no statistically significant differences between the two groups in terms of sex, BMI, smoking history, drinking history, time from trauma to surgery, hypertension, diabetes, COPD, renal insufficiency, liver disease, preoperative anticoagulation, trauma type (multiple traumas or high-energy trauma), or ASA grade.

Logistic regression analysis

Binary logistic regression (Fig. 2) indicated that age, ASA grade, coronary heart disease, pulmonary infection, malignant tumour, osteoporosis, fracture type, anaemia, perioperative blood transfusion, and hypoproteinaemia were correlated with postoperative thrombosis (P < 0.05). After adjusting for the above factors in the multivariate logistic regression analysis, high-coagulation TEG was found to have predictive value for postoperative DVT formation (P = 0.001, OR:1.669, 95% CI: 1.230–2.263). Traditional coagulation function indicators (PT, INR, FDP, APTT, FIB, D-dimer, platelet) were included in the multiple logistic regression analysis, and only APTT was correlated with postoperative DVT formation (P = 0.002, OR: 0.954, 95%CI: 0.934 − 0.985), the remaining indexes were not associated with postoperative thrombosis (P > 0.05).

Scoring method based on propensity matching

Using a calliper value of 0.1, covariates included age, sex, BMI, ASA rating, trauma to surgery time, smoking history, drinking history, hypertension, diabetes, coronary heart disease, renal insufficiency, liver disease, malignant tumour, osteoporosis, COPD, pulmonary infection, multiple injuries, high-energy trauma, hypoproteinaemia, perioperative blood transfusion, anaemia, preoperative anticoagulation, and fracture type. Comparison of DVT-related covariates between the two groups before and after matching

Before PSM, 784 patients (72.3%) were in the non-hypercoagulable group, and 300 patients (27.7%) were in the hypercoagulable group. There were no significant differences in sex, age, BMI, smoking history, drinking history, diabetes, hypertension, coronary heart disease, renal insufficiency, malignant tumour, osteoporosis, hypoproteinaemia, COPD, pulmonary infection, ASA grade, or trauma to surgery time between the two groups (P > 0.05). After calliper matching of the inter-group data using the propensity score method, there were 296 cases in each group, and none of the 23 DVT-related covariates showed statistical significance between the two groups (P > 0.05), indicating balanced and comparable data (Table 3).

Incidence of DVT before and after matching

Before PSM, the incidence of postoperative DVT was 25.4% (275/1084), with 34.3% (103/300) in the hypercoagulable group and 21.9% (172/784) in the non-hypercoagulable groups. Correlation analysis between preoperative TEG hypercoagulable state and DVT formation before and after matching

Before PSM, preoperative TEG hypercoagulation was associated with postoperative DVT formation in 1,084 patients with femoral and pelvic fractures (P < 0.001, OR: 1.860, 95%CI: 1.389 − 2.492). After matching, preoperative TEG hypercoagulability was identified as an independent risk factor for DVT in these patients (P = 0.001), and the risk of DVT was 1.878 times higher in patients with hypercoagulability compared to those without (95%CI: 1.301 − 2.709).

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the role of TEG hypercoagulability in the risk assessment of lower limb DVT formation in patients with femoral and pelvic fractures following surgery. This retrospective study showed that hypercoagulable TEG was associated with a significantly higher incidence of lower-limb DVT in both univariate and multivariate models.

According to the Virchow triad (1840), thrombus formation is influenced by three factors: venous stasis, vascular endothelial injury, and hypercoagulability [12]. However, despite 150 years since Virchow’s study, there is limited knowledge about the incidence and clinical relevance of hypercoagulability, and postoperative thrombotic complications remain prevalent, consuming significant medical resources [13, 14]. Both elective surgery and unplanned surgeries (such as those due to trauma) can induce hypercoagulability. The mechanisms of thrombosis include platelet surface activation, reduced fibrinolysis, and decreased endogenous anticoagulant function, such as decreased antithrombin III [15–18]. Factors such as advanced age, a prolonged course of the disease, intraoperative fixation, vascular traction, use of general anaesthesia, blood transfusion, and bone cement can affect vascular endothelium and blood flow to varying degrees, disrupting the balance of coagulation and fibrinolysis, and promote perioperative DVT formation in patients with bone trauma [19, 20]. Therefore, patients with bone trauma are most prone to VTE. Although 66% of the patients in this study received preoperative anticoagulation therapy to prevent thrombosis, the incidence of postoperative DVT remained high at 25.4%, with 87.2% (240/275) having intermuscular vein thrombosis. This finding is consistent with a previous study, which reported that the intermuscular vein is the most easily involved site of DVT after hip and knee replacement, with an incidence of 71% [21].

CCTs include prothrombin time, activated partial thromboplastin time, platelet count, fibrinogen levels, thrombin time, and D-dimer levels. Coagulation status is typically monitored using CCT, which can detect exogenous and endogenous coagulation pathways, fibrinogen status, and specific stages or components of the coagulation process. However, it is difficult to evaluate the entire process of blood coagulation and fibrinolysis. In this study, the CCT index was included in the multiple logistic regression analysis, and only APTT was correlated with postoperative DVT formation (P = 0.002, OR:0.954, 95%CI: 0.934 − 0.985), while the remaining indexes were not correlated with postoperative thrombosis (P > 0.05). Our findings suggest that the use of traditional coagulation indicators to predict thromboembolic events lacks reliability and objectivity. Chopard et al. [22] also found CCT to be inaccurate, with low sensitivity and reliability. D-D levels can reflect the fibrinolytic process well, with a sensitivity of 96%; however, the specificity was only 40%, and its positive predictive value was just 48%. Therefore, we need a more effective method to objectively evaluate the clotting status of patients to help clinicians diagnose, effectively treat, and prevent DVT formation earlier. TEG measures changes in blood viscosity during blood coagulation to capture the entire process, including platelet aggregation, coagulation, and fibrinolysis. Whole blood samples with or without citrate were incubated in a cup at 37 °C. A fixed needle connected to the overhanging wire, which monitored movement, was immersed in the sample and vibrated six times per min. As coagulation began, fluid resistance increased, and the sensor connected to the needle detected changes in viscoelasticity, recording the formation of a clotting elastic pattern [6]. In 1948, Hartert first used TEG to monitor the entire blood coagulation process. Currently, TEG is widely used in clinical fields such as liver transplantation, cardiothoracic surgery, trauma, and intensive care to monitor blood coagulation and guide transfusion management [6, 23]. Aside from its use in patients with low coagulation and bleeding tendencies [24], several studies have shown that TEG can effectively detect hypercoagulation in patients with malignant tumours [25, 26], pregnancy [27, 28], trauma [29], and surgery [30].

Hypercoagulability increases the risk of DVT and is associated with the occurrence of DVT [31, 32]. Recently, researchers reported that TEG-detected hypercoagulability can predict a patient’s risk of developing VTE. Gary et al. studied over 1,800 patients with severe limb fractures and found that higher MA values detected by TEG are associated with an increased risk of in-hospital DVT and PE. VTE risk increased by 3.6-fold and 6.7-fold when MA values were ≥ 68 mm and ≥ 72 mm, respectively [33]. Another study of 983 patients with trauma found that TEG suggests a two-fold increase in DVT risk for patients with hypercoagulable states [34]. However, Parameswaran et al. examined the preoperative TEG parameters of 101 patients who underwent knee or hip replacements and found that the hypercoagulable state suggested by TEG before surgery is unable to predict the formation of postoperative DVT, and only one of six patients with postoperative DVT had abnormal TEG values before surgery [8]. We observed significant differences in preoperative R, K, α, MA, and CI values between patients with DVT and those without. A decrease in R and K values or an increase in α, MA, and CI confirmed the hypercoagulability tendency in patients with DVT. Additionally, patients with preoperative hypercoagulable TEG had a significantly higher incidence of postoperative DVT compared to those with non-hypercoagulable TEG.

Logistic regression analysis and PSM revealed that hypercoagulable TEG was predictive of postoperative DVT formation (P = 0.001, OR:1.669, 95% CI: 1.230–2.263). Before PSM, hypercoagulation detected by TEG was found to be associated with DVT in patients with femoral and pelvic fractures (P < 0.001, OR:1.860, 95% CI: 1.389–2.492). Further PSM analysis for recognised and probable DVT-related risk factors, along with sensitivity analysis for confounding variables, showed that the association persisted (P = 0.001, OR = 1.878, 95% CI:1.301 − 2.709). Moreover, the risk of DVT in patients with femoral and pelvic fractures with hypercoagulable TEG was 1.878 times higher than that in non-hypercoagulable patients. Patients with TEG-detected hypercoagulability may have one or more risk factors for DVT, which could explain why pre-matching data analysis suggested an association between the two. However, we reached the same conclusion after controlling for the confounding variables of DVT using PSM, which further explained the independence of the two correlations.These comprehensive results suggest that the hypercoagulable state identified by TEG is correlated with postoperative DVT formation in patients with femoral and pelvic fractures and can predict the formation of DVT.

The incidence of deep venous thrombosis in the lower extremities remains high despite patients receiving standard anticoagulant therapy. Perioperative TEG results can guide adjustments to anticoagulants and their dosages for more effective clot prevention. When TEG results indicate a high MA value (indicating thrombocytosis), consideration should be given to antiplatelet drugs such as aspirin, and platelet function inhibition should be assessed using platelet mapping. In cases where the R value is low (indicating an increase in clotting factors), replacement of anticoagulant drugs such as rivaroxaban may be necessary, with close observation of subcutaneous ecchymosis. Ideally, quantification of specific values beyond the normal reference range for R, K, MA, and CI values from TEG returns would allow for more accurate dose adjustment plans based on relevant anticoagulant drug instructions, facilitating the development of individualised standards for preventing deep vein thrombosis after orthopaedic surgery and significantly reducing its clinical risk. However, further clinical trials are required to verify the effectiveness and feasibility of this approach. In conclusion, dynamic adjustment of anticoagulant therapy under TEG monitoring has the potential to reduce DVT incidence; nevertheless, additional clinical studies and trial data are needed to support and clarify its objectivity and accuracy.

This study had some limitations. First, it was a single-centre retrospective study with a relatively small sample size. Although we used PSM to control for the bias from 23 confounding factors, potential risk factors not included in the PSM analysis may have influenced our results. Larger prospective studies are required to determine the potential clinical application of TEG in predicting DVT formation. Second, the cut-off values for TEG were determined based on manufacturer recommendations and laboratory ranges; therefore, other cut-off values may yield different predictions. Third, the study only analysed preoperative TEG parameters in patients with femoral and pelvic fractures, with no follow-up data available. Monitoring changes in TEG values before and after surgery, as well as at various time points from admission to discharge, and comparing these parameters would provide more meaningful insights.

Conclusions

The TEG test is a standardised assessment performed upon admission to comprehensively evaluate a patient’s blood clotting status. This cohort study aimed to explore the association between TEG hypercoagulability and the development of DVT after surgery in patients with fractures in the femoral and pelvic regions. The results indicate that an increased risk of DVT is significantly associated with TEG hypercoagulability, suggesting its potential as a crucial indicator for predicting DVT risk in such patients. Future research could consider expanding the sample size to include individuals from diverse age groups and undergoing different types of surgeries, thereby further validating the accuracy and reliability of TEG in predicting postoperative DVT among individuals with bone trauma. Additionally, by monitoring TEG parameters, further investigation can be conducted to optimise anticoagulation therapy according to changes observed in patients’ TEG results. This approach aims to determine optimal preventive treatment plans for individuals identified as having a hypercoagulable state by TEG, ultimately reducing the incidence of DVT and bleeding risks associated with treatment interventions.
