Dielectric and temperature-dependent optimization of an asymmetric gate–drain engineered core–shell dopingless nanotube tunnel FET for analog and RF performance
Authors: Ranjith Kumar T., Lakshmi Priya G.
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This work presents a comprehensive study of a core–shell Asymmetric Dopingless Nanotube Tunnel Field Effect Transistor (A-DL-NT-TFET), designed to overcome the limitations of conventional TFET architectures. The device employs asymmetric gate design to enhance tunneling, doping-less operation to remove abrupt junctions and ensure uniform control, and a cylindrical nanotube geometry to improve electrostatics while minimizing parasitic effects. 3-D Sentaurus TCAD simulations were used to compare the core–shell A-DL-NT-TFET with its equivalent nanowire (NW) counterpart (A-DL-NW-TFET). The nanotube achieves an ON-state current of 8.49 × 10−7 A µm−1, which is approximately 1.43 times higher than that of the nanowire. Moreover, its OFF-state current is suppressed to 5.92 × 10−21 A µm−1, nearly 6.1 × 103 times lower than that of the nanowire (3.61 × 10−17 A µm−1). Consequently, the device attains an impressive ION/IOFF ratio of 1.43 × 1014. Device performance is further analyzed using various gate dielectric materials (air, SiO2, Al2O3, ZrO2, and HfO2) and operating temperatures (200–500 K). The results show that high-k dielectrics enhance tunneling efficiency and ON current, while SiO2 at 300 K offers the best trade-off, sustaining a minimum SS of 57.1 mV dec−1 alongside favorable OFF leakage and threshold stability. The device exhibits a superior analog and RF performance with a peak transconductance of 1.18 µS and a low output conductance in the 10−7 S range. It also achieves a cut-off frequency of 24.2 GHz, a GBP of 6.29 GHz, a GFP of 242 GHz, and a THz-class GTFP of 2.38 THz. This establishes the proposed core–shell A-DL-NT-TFET as a strong candidate for ultra-low-power communication systems.

Introduction

The continuous miniaturization of CMOS transistors, long sustained by Moore's law, has driven exponential growth in computing capability for more than five decades.1 However, with aggressive device scaling, conventional MOSFETs now encounter fundamental roadblocks, including Short Channel Effects (SCEs), elevated leakage currents, and degraded Subthreshold Swing (SS), all of which threaten power efficiency at nanometer nodes. In particular, the thermionic emission limit restricts the SS to ≥60 mV dec−1 at room temperature, preventing further supply voltage reduction and posing a bottleneck for future ultra-low-power technologies.2–4

Tunneling Field Effect Transistors (TFETs) have therefore been explored as potential replacements, as they exploit Band-to-Band Tunneling (BTBT) rather than thermionic injection. This mechanism enables TFETs to surpass the MOSFET limit, achieving sub-60 mV dec−1 SS and enhanced ON/OFF ratios, making them promising for energy efficient logic and sensing applications.5–7 A broad range of TFET architectures have been proposed to date to address the challenges of low ON current and parasitic effects, including Double Gate (DG) TFETs,8–10 Gate-All-Around (GAA) TFETs11,12 and nanowire TFETs,13,14 Heterojunction TFETs (HJ-TFETs),15–17 vertical TFETs,18,19 dielectric and work function engineered TFETs,20,21 Junctionless (JL) TFETs22 and Negative Capacitance (NC) TFETs.23–25 Despite these innovations, issues such as low drive current, ambipolar leakage, and sensitivity to process variations persist.

Recent research has highlighted the effectiveness of asymmetric gate engineering in mitigating OFF-state leakage and boosting ON-state tunneling. For example, our earlier work on asymmetric Uniform TFETs (UTFETs)26 for biosensing confirmed that longer tunnel gates and shorter auxiliary gates sharpen band bending at the source while suppressing Drain Induced Barrier Lowering (DIBL), leading to superior RF and analog performance compared to symmetric designs.27 To further simplify fabrication and minimize variability, doping-less (DL) or charge-plasma TFETs have gained attention. Unlike junction-based or junctionless TFETs (UTFETs), DL-TFETs eliminate the need for abrupt dopant profiles and high-temperature processing, thereby improving scalability, suppressing trap-assisted tunneling, and offering CMOS compatibility.28–30

Among the geometries proposed, nanotube channels provide a clear advantage over planar and nanowire designs. Their cylindrical core–shell configuration enhances electrostatic control, reduces screening, and increases the tunneling junction area, leading to higher ON current and improved SS.31 Several notable studies have explored nanotube TFETs: researchers have demonstrated a junctionless silicon nanotube TFET with improved electrostatic integrity, though limited by modest ON currents (10−7–10−6 A µm−1).32 InAs/Si heterojunction nanotube TFETs have been reported to boost drive current by leveraging bandgap engineering, but such designs suffer from interface defects and lattice mismatch issues.33 Core–shell InAs–GaSb nanotube TFETs have also been investigated, achieving strong tunneling efficiency but facing challenges from material complexity and trap states at the heterointerface.18 Si/Ge heterostructure nanotube TFETs demonstrated improved SS but remained limited by high OFF-state leakage due to band-offset induced tunneling leakage.34 More recent studies have analyzed Gate-All-Around (GAA) core–shell (CS),35 Dual Gate (DG),36 and Electrostatic Doped Nanotube (ED-NT) TFETs,37 showing significant improvements in tunneling control and RF potential, but at the cost of increased structural complexity and susceptibility to interface trap charges. Junctionless nanotube TFET studies under temperature variation38 and twin-source nanotube TFETs39 have further demonstrated enhanced drive current and RF behavior yet consistently reported limitations in achieving simultaneous record-high ON current with ultra-low OFF leakage.40,41

These studies collectively reinforce the importance of nanotube geometries but also highlight unresolved drawbacks such as leakage, parasitic capacitances, and fabrication complexity. Building upon these findings, the present work proposes and analyzes a core–shell Asymmetric Dopingless Nanotube TFET (A-DL-NT-TFET), which synergistically combines (i) asymmetric gate engineering for reduced leakage and sharper turn-on, (ii) doping-less operation for process simplicity and trap suppression, and (iii) nanotube geometry for superior electrostatics and larger tunneling cross-section.

In this study, the core–shell A-DL-NT-TFET is initially calibrated against an equivalent Si based core–shell Nanotube TFET to validate the structure in Section 2 followed by the models used for the simulation in Section 3. Electrical characteristics (transfer characteristics, energy band diagrams, electric field, and potential profiles) are then compared in Section 4, followed by a detailed analysis of ON-state current, OFF-state current, ON/OFF ratio, transconductance, output conductance, and SS under variations of dielectric constant, temperature, and gate length in Section 5. Finally, RF and analog Figures of Merit (FOM) including fT, GBP, TGF, GFP, and GTFP are evaluated in Section 6, demonstrating the capability of the proposed core–shell A-DL-NT-TFET to deliver record-low leakage, high drive current, and THz-class RF efficiency for next generation low power communication systems.

Device structure and operation

For comparison, the 3-D nanowire counterpart of the proposed structure is shown in Fig. 1(a), with its corresponding cross-sectional view in Fig. 1(b). The proposed 3-D core–shell Asymmetric Dopingless Nanotube Tunnel Field Effect Transistor (A-DL-NT-TFET) is illustrated in Fig. 1(c), while its corresponding cross-sectional view is presented in Fig. 1(d). Similar to previously reported charge-plasma-based dopingless TFETs,42 the proposed device relies on localized electrostatically induced carrier accumulation near the source–channel interface for tunneling junction formation, rather than requiring abrupt physical doping or complete gate coverage across the entire source region. The structure comprises a hollow cylindrical silicon channel of thickness TSi = 10 nm as mentioned in previously reported nanotube TFET literature studies,42 which is intentionally maintained well below the effective Debye screening length (∼129 nm for a uniformly low background doping of 1 × 1015 cm−3) to ensure efficient electrostatic carrier induction in accordance with the charge-plasma operating principle, encapsulated by a gate dielectric (Tox = 1 nm) and surrounded by an outer cylindrical gate. The source, channel, and drain lengths are LS = 40 nm, LC = 20 nm and LD = 40 nm, respectively. The core and shell gate asymmetry is achieved by employing a long tunneling gate of length LTG = 15 nm toward the source side and a short auxiliary gate of length LAG = 5 nm near the drain. Metal work functions are chosen such that ΦTG = 4.9 eV induces a P type source and ΦAG = 5.2 eV ensures proper channel modulation, while a mid-gap contact metal forms the N type drain. This asymmetry enhances tunneling efficiency at the source while suppressing Drain-Induced Barrier Lowering (DIBL) and ambipolar leakage, thereby yielding higher drive current and ultra-low OFF-state current. The hollow cylindrical geometry further increases the tunneling junction area and strengthens gate-to-channel coupling compared to solid nanowire TFETs, enabling steeper band bending and reduced tunneling length. Models used in the TCAD simulation

Device simulations were carried out using Sentaurus TCAD in a three-dimensional framework, with sub-nanometer mesh refinement at the source–channel junction and along the oxide interfaces to capture the sharp tunneling field distribution. Carrier transport was modeled using the drift–diffusion framework with Fermi–Dirac statistics to account for carrier degeneracy under high electric fields. The dominant conduction mechanism was nonlocal band-to-band tunneling (BTBT), implemented using Kane's model with dynamic path integration. The tunneling generation rate, obtained from ref. 43 is expressed as follows:1where n is the field exponent (2.5 for indirect tunneling), Ap (Apath1) = 4 × 1014 is the material-dependent BTBT Prefactor, Bp (Bpath1) = 1.9 × 107 V cm−1 is the tunnelling coefficient, F is the electric field across the tunneling region and F0 is the reference electric field (1 V cm−1). Temperature influences the tunneling process through its effect on carrier distribution and phonon-assisted transitions embedded in the TCAD framework. As temperature increases, Fermi–Dirac tail broadening enhances the probability of band-to-band tunneling, leading to increased OFF-state current and degraded subthreshold behavior.

Carrier mobility was modeled using concentration dependent mobility (CONMOB) and field-dependent mobility (FLDMOB) to account for mobility degradation at high carrier density and strong electric field conditions. Shockley–Read–Hall (SRH) and Auger recombination models were included to represent trap-assisted and high injection recombination mechanisms. Density-Gradient (DG) quantum correction was applied to capture confinement effects in the 10 nm silicon nanotube body. Source and drain regions were realized using metal work function boundary conditions under the charge-plasma concept, and Schottky contact models were employed to represent metal–semiconductor injection accurately. The simulations capture nonlocal band-to-band tunneling and electrostatic effects that dominate nanoscale TFET operation. The study is therefore focused on intrinsic device behavior and performance trends arising from asymmetric gate–drain engineering under dielectric and temperature variations. Also, this calibrated physical framework provides reliable prediction of the transfer characteristics, subthreshold swing, ON/OFF current ratio, and intrinsic RF figures of merit such as transconductance, cutoff frequency, and gain bandwidth product.

Results and discussion

Electrical performance analysis of the proposed A-DL-NW-TFET and core–shell A-DL-NT-TFET devices

Transfer characteristics comparison

At VD = 1 V, as shown in Fig. 5, it can be observed that the core–shell Asymmetric Dopingless Nanotube TFET (A-DL-NT-TFET) demonstrates clear superiority over its nanowire counterpart (A-DL-NW-TFET). The nanotube delivers an ON current of 8.49 × 10−7 A µm−1 and an ultra-low OFF current of 5.92 × 10−21, corresponding to an ION/IOFF ratio of 1.43 × 1014. In contrast, the core–shell A-DL-NW-TFET with identical TG/AG configuration but only an outer gate attains ION = 4.99 × 10−7 A µm−1 and IOFF = 3.61 × 10−17 A µm−1 , yielding a much lower ratio of about 1.38 × 1010. The substantial improvement arises from the nanotube's core–shell dual-gate geometry, where the additional core gate provides uniform circumferential control, enlarges the tunneling junction cross-section, and strengthens the local electric field during ON bias while widening the barrier in the OFF state.

Transfer characteristics of the proposed A-DL-NW-TFET and core–shell A-DL-NT-TFET with other conventional Si based nanotube TFETs.

The broader comparative analysis in Fig. 3 at the same applied voltages further validates this conclusion. Relative to previously reported devices such as the Dual-Gate All-Around Core–Shell Nanotube TFET36, the Doping-Less Silicon Nanotube TFET (DL-Si-NT-TFET)44, and the Junctionless Silicon Nanotube TFET (JL-Si-NT-TFET) for resistive temperature detection38, the proposed core–shell A-DL-NT-TFET consistently delivers the highest ON current in the 10−7 A µm−1 regime, while simultaneously suppressing the OFF-state leakage to the 10−21 A µm−1 level. This positions the proposed architecture as the state of the art among silicon nanotube TFETs, combining record-high current drivability with record-low leakage.

Energy band diagram

The corresponding energy band diagrams in Fig. 6 provide direct physical insight into these characteristics. In the OFF state (VG = 0 V), the nanowire exhibits a narrower tunneling barrier at the source–channel interface because of weaker electrostatic confinement, which permits residual band-to-band tunneling and explains its higher leakage of 10−17 A µm−1. In contrast, the nanotube's core–shell dual-gate geometry enforces more uniform circumferential channel control and stronger gate–channel coupling, which widen the tunneling barrier and increase the conduction–valence band separation, thereby driving the leakage down to the 10−21 A µm−1 range. In the ON state (VG = 1.5 V), the nanotube produces sharper band bending and a reduced tunneling path length at the source–channel junction. The longer tunneling gate strengthens the electrostatic field near the source, while the shorter auxiliary gate suppresses drain-induced barrier lowering, collectively enhancing the tunneling efficiency. A smaller tunneling length and larger band bending in the nanotube reduce the exponential term, yielding significantly higher tunneling probability than the nanowire. This explains the earlier turn-on, higher drive current in the 10−7 A µm−1 regime, and steeper subthreshold slope of the nanotube relative to the nanowire.

Electric field and electrostatic potential

Fig. 7(a) and (b) present the electric field and electrostatic potential distributions for the core–shell A-DL-NT-TFET and the ADL-NW-TFET at VG = 1.5 V and VD = 1 V. The dominant electric-field peak occurs at the source–channel tunneling interface, with the nanotube reaching 6.59 × 105 V cm−1 compared to 6.34 × 105 V cm−1 for the nanowire. The higher peak field in the nanotube arises from the dual-gate core–shell geometry, which strengthens circumferential gate control and focuses the field at the tunneling junction, thereby exponentially increasing BTBT probability. Beyond the primary tunneling region, a weaker secondary electric-field component appears toward the drain side. In the nanowire, this tail remains more prominent because the planar gate exerts weaker electrostatic control near the drain. In contrast, the nanotube's core–shell geometry provides stronger gate coupling and suppresses drain-side field penetration, resulting in a more confined and uniform tunneling profile. The electrostatic potential increases from 0.3 V at the source contact to above 1.2 V near the drain. Around the tunneling junction, a distinct steep rise is visible, corresponding to sharp band bending that shortens the tunneling path. The nanotube exhibits a steeper potential gradient than the nanowire, reflecting stronger source-side gate control, while toward the drain side both devices show a gradual potential increase consistent with the applied bias. The combination of higher peak field at the source interface, suppressed drain-side field penetration, and steeper potential modulation in the nanotube underpins its superior ON-state tunneling efficiency and OFF-state leakage control.

(a) Electric field of the proposed A-DL-NW-TFET and core–shell A-DL-NT-TFET devices. (b) Electrostatic potential of the proposed A-DL-NW-TFET and core–shell A-DL-NT-TFET devices.

Fig. 8 illustrates the proposed fabrication sequence of the core–shell A-DL-NT-TFET. The process begins with the definition of a lightly doped silicon body (1 × 1015 cm−3) on a silicon substrate, followed by electron-beam lithography and selective etching to define the nanotube geometry (steps 1–3). Subsequently, the SiO2 gate dielectric is deposited by Atomic Layer Deposition (ALD), and the asymmetric shell gate metals corresponding to the Tunneling Gate (TG) and Auxiliary Gate (AG) are patterned over the channel region (steps 4–5). After outer oxide encapsulation, the temporary gate materials are removed and Low-Pressure Chemical Vapour Deposition (LPCVD) SiO2 is deposited, followed by sacrificial oxide deposition and planarization (steps 6–9).

Fabrication flow of the proposed core–shell A-DL-NT-TFET.

Selective etching is then performed to define the recessed drain and central trench, after which the inner sidewall dielectric is deposited to electrically isolate the core gate (steps 10–15). The asymmetric core gate is subsequently formed together with the inner spacer oxide (steps 16–17). Finally, metallic source, gate, and drain contacts are deposited to complete the proposed core–shell A-DL-NT-TFET (step 18). The proposed fabrication methodology follows substrate-supported top-down processing adapted from previously reported nanotube TFET fabrication approaches,32,39,46 with modifications introduced to realize the asymmetric drain and split-gate architecture of the proposed device.

Performance analysis of the core–shell A-DL-NT-TFET device

On state current analysis

For LG = 20 nm, the ON-state current of the proposed core–shell A-DL-NT-TFET exhibits a clear dependence on both dielectric constant and temperature, as given in Fig. 9. The current increases systematically from air (1.25 × 10−7 A µm−1 at 300 K) to SiO2 (1.40 × 10−7 A µm−1) and further to HfO2 (1.54 × 10−7 A µm−1), with all dielectrics reaching ∼5 × 10−7 to 6 × 10−7 A µm−1 at 500 K. This increase follows the Kane/WKB tunneling relation, IBTBT ∝ FP·exp (−B/F). Higher-k dielectrics enhance gate-to-channel capacitance, strengthen the tunneling field, and shorten the effective tunneling path. Elevated temperature further promotes tunneling by broadening the Fermi tail and enabling phonon-assisted transitions. Since the proposed charge-plasma architecture employs a uniformly low background doping concentration of 1 × 1015 cm−3, impurity-scattering-dominated mobility degradation is less significant compared with that in heavily doped conventional devices, while the observed temperature dependence is primarily governed by thermally assisted tunneling and carrier distribution effects.

ON state current (ION) of the proposed core–shell A-DL-NT-TFET with respect to changes in temperature and the dielectric material.

Although HfO2 yields the highest nominal ON current, its larger parasitic capacitance causes minor degradation in analog and RF performance. Therefore, SiO2 at 300 K provides the most balanced operating conditions, ensuring high drive current with stable threshold voltage and low leakage, consistent with earlier nanotube TFET optimization studies.

OFF-state current analysis

The OFF-state current of the core–shell A-DL-NT-TFET exhibits a strong dependence on both temperature and dielectric constant as depicted in Fig. 10. For all dielectrics, IOFF increases almost exponentially with temperature, ranging from the 10−25–10−24 A µm−1 regime at 200 K up to 10−13–10−12 A µm−1 at 500 K. In silicon TFETs, tunneling is dominated by the indirect bandgap. The rise in OFF current with temperature results from enhanced phonon-assisted tunneling, caused by Fermi–Dirac broadening and increased phonon activity at higher temperatures. OFF state current (IOFF) of the proposed core–shell A-DL-NT-TFET with respect to changes in temperature and gate length for different gate dielectrics: (a) air, (b) SiO2, (c) Al2O3, (d) ZrO2, and (e) HfO2.

Across dielectrics, OFF current decreases with increasing dielectric constant, following the order: air > SiO2 > Al2O3 > ZrO2 > HfO2. High-k dielectrics improve gate-to-channel coupling, widen the tunneling barrier in the OFF state, and thereby suppress leakage. For example, at 300 K, air produces IOFF of nearly 10−21 A µm−1, while HfO2 achieves values closer to 10−23 A µm−1. At elevated temperatures (T ≥ 450 K), however, the benefit of high-k materials diminishes as phonon-assisted tunneling dominates, and all dielectrics converge in the 10−14–10−12 A µm−1 range. An oxide thickness of 1 nm is adopted in accordance with previously reported nanotube and gate-all-around TFET studies in the literature35,47 to ensure strong electrostatic control at scaled dimensions. Under these conditions, the OFF-state behavior is primarily governed by source–channel band-to-band tunneling, which primarily influences the observed leakage current trends. Gate-length dependence is weaker: shorter gates (LG = 20 nm) allow slightly higher leakage than longer gates (LG = 60 nm) due to reduced tunneling length, but dielectric engineering remains the dominant factor. Overall, SiO2 at 300 K offers the most balanced trade-off between OFF-current suppression, ON-current retention, and threshold voltage control, while HfO2 achieves the lowest absolute leakage at cryogenic to moderate temperatures.

ON/OFF current ratio analysis

The extracted ION/IOFF ratio as plotted in Fig. 11 clearly reflects the combined influence of dielectric constant, gate length, and temperature. At 300 K, values lie in the range of 1014–1018, with HfO2 as gate oxide devices achieving the highest suppression of leakage and thus the largest ratio. The ratio decreases almost exponentially with temperature because the rapid rise in IOFF dominates the overall current behavior. In contrast, ION remains nearly constant, consistent with BTBT-dominated transport. Gate-length variation also affects the ratio: shorter gates (LG = 20 nm) exhibit lower ION/IOFF due to stronger leakage paths, whereas longer gates (LG = 60 nm) maintain higher ratios by effectively widening the tunneling barrier. Importantly, even under worst-case high-temperature operation (500 K), ratios remain above 107, while at room temperature the optimized device achieves beyond 1015, surpassing many previously reported silicon TFETs. This establishes that dielectric engineering combined with asymmetric nanotube geometry provides robust ON/OFF performance across a wide operating window.

For BTBT-driven TFETs, gm quantifies how effectively the gate modulates the tunneling field at the source–channel junction. The results from Fig. 12 indicate values in the range of 1.0 × 10−6 S, for SiO2 and air, rising slightly to ∼1.5 × 10−6 S for high-k dielectrics such as HfO2 and ZrO2. This modest enhancement arises from improved electrostatic coupling, which increases the tunneling rate sensitivity to gate bias. However, with rising temperature, gm decreases consistently because thermal broadening and leakage currents degrade the steepness of the transfer characteristics.

Gate-length scaling shows negligible impact, highlighting the strong inherent electrostatics of the nanotube configuration. Importantly, when overall metrics such as ION/IOFF, subthreshold swing, and threshold voltage are considered together, SiO2 at 300 K (room temperature) emerges as the optimum dielectric choice, balancing leakage suppression with adequate transconductance for both analog and RF applications.

The simulated results in Fig. 13 indicate that gds lies in the 10−7–10−6 S range and shows a consistent rise with increasing temperature due to enhanced phonon-assisted tunneling and stronger drain-induced barrier modulation. Shorter gates (LG = 20 nm) exhibit the highest gds values because of stronger electrostatic coupling between the drain and channel, whereas longer gates (40–60 nm) effectively suppress this effect. Subthreshold swing analysis

The subthreshold swing (SS) is defined by eqn (6):486

It is a key figure of merit indicating switching steepness in TFETs. In the proposed core–shell A-DL-NT-TFET, SS shows strong dependence on both dielectric constant and temperature, as observed in Fig. 14. At lower temperatures (200–300 K), all dielectrics exhibit sharp SS in the 40–70 mV dec−1 range, validating tunneling-dominated conduction and efficient gate electrostatics. As temperature increases above 350 K, SS degrades progressively, exceeding 200 mV dec−1 at 450–500 K due to Fermi–Dirac tail broadening and phonon-assisted tunneling, which enable carriers to leak under weak gate bias.49 Higher-k dielectrics (HfO2 and ZrO2) yield lower SS by enhancing gate control and narrowing the tunneling barrier, while SiO2 at 300 K provides the most practical compromise with a subthreshold swing of 57.1 mV dec−1 alongside favorable ION/IOFF and threshold stability confirming its efficient steep-slope switching behavior. These results establish reliable steep-slope performance up to 350 K, beyond which thermal effects dominate. RF and analog performance of the core–shell A-DL-NT-TFET

The advancement of RF/analog front-ends for high-frequency communication, sensing, and biomedical applications demands devices with steep switching, high transconductance efficiency, and minimized parasitic delay. Conventional CMOS FETs are restricted by the 60 mV dec−1 thermal limit, constraining their RF performance and making it challenging to achieve THz-class figures of merit (FOMs). In contrast, the core–shell Asymmetric Dopingless Nanotube TFET (A-DL-NT-TFET) harnesses BTBT in a cylindrical geometry, where superior gate electrostatics and reduced leakage collectively enable enhanced high-frequency response. To benchmark this capability, key FOMs such as cut-off frequency (fT), Gain Bandwidth Product (GBP), Transconductance Generation Factor (TGF), Gain Frequency Product (GFP), and Gain Transconductance Frequency Product (GTFP) are systematically evaluated.

Cut-off frequency (fT) and gain bandwidth product (GBP)

The intrinsic speed limit of a transistor is quantified by eqn (7):527where gm is the transconductance and Cgg is the total gate capacitance. The cut-off frequency is evaluated using the standard small-signal analytical formulation based on transconductance and total gate capacitance, representing the unity current-gain operating frequency of the device.

As shown in Fig. 15, fT exhibits a sharp rise beyond VGS = 0.7V, attaining a peak of 24.2 GHz at VGS = 0.9V. This corresponds to the onset of strong tunneling in the nanotube channel, where the steep subthreshold slope translates into a rapid increase in gm relative to gate capacitance. At lower biases (<0.6 V), the absence of sufficient tunneling suppresses fT, while beyond 1.0 V, parasitic capacitances and quasi-saturation effects in the cylindrical channel reduce the FOM. The well-defined peak highlights the bias-tunable frequency scalability of the device.

The extracted FOMs – fT = 24.2 GHz, GBP = 6.29 GHz, TGF = 172 V−1, GFP = 242 GHz, and GTFP = 2.38 THz position the proposed core–shell A-DL-NT-TFET as a superior candidate for RF/analog circuits compared to conventional MOSFETs and planar TFETs. The cylindrical core–shell geometry enhances electrostatic integrity, enabling bias-dependent tunability of high-frequency performance. Importantly, the steep subthreshold slope and high TGF at low bias ensure energy-efficient RF operation, a critical requirement for next-generation communication systems and biosensor interfaces.

Conclusion

This work demonstrates that the core–shell Asymmetric Dopingless Nanotube TFET (A-DL-NT-TFET) provides a substantial improvement over its nanowire counterpart by leveraging cylindrical electrostatics, charge-plasma operation, and asymmetric gate engineering. The nanotube design achieves over three orders of magnitude lower OFF current while sustaining higher ON current, resulting in a higher ION/IOFF ratio in the order of 1014. Unlike previously reported junctionless and heterojunction nanotube TFETs, which often faced trade-offs between leakage and drivability, the proposed device retains both ultra-low leakage and strong current capability within a CMOS-compatible silicon platform. The analysis across dielectric materials and temperatures confirms that high-k oxides can boost tunneling efficiency, while SiO2 at 300 K offers the most balanced compromise between ON/OFF ratio, subthreshold slope, and threshold voltage. RF performance further establishes the device's suitability for next-generation analog front-ends, with cut-off frequency and GTFP values reaching the GHz–THz regime. These findings validate the A-DL-NT-TFET as a scalable pathway for ultra-low-power, high-frequency applications, spanning advanced communication systems to biosensing interfaces.
