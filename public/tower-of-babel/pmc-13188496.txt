Evolution with age of the relationship between sex and post-COVID symptoms: a population-based cohort study
Authors: Anne Pastorello, Cécile Vuillermoz, Laurence Meyer, Camille Davisse-Paturet, Joël Coste, Nathalie Bajos, Olivier Lambotte, Nicolas Noël, Junko Kose, Josiane Warszawski, Alexandra Rouquette
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIt is unclear why women more frequently experience post-COVID symptoms than men. Sex and age-dependent immune dysregulation triggered by SARS-CoV-2 infection might be involved in this association. We assessed whether age modified the association between sex and post-COVID symptoms, and whether this effect modification might be attributable to SARS-CoV-2 infection.MethodsFrom the French population-based prospective cohort study with random sampling EpiCov (Spring 2020–Autumn 2022), we included two groups of participants aged ≥ 17: those with a first SARS-CoV-2 confirmed infection during the Omicron wave in 2022 (infected participants, N = 10,618), and those with no SARS-CoV-2 infection history (uninfected participants, N = 28,716). In both groups, we assessed effect modification by age on the association between sex and any self-reported persistent symptom in Autumn 2022 using modified Poisson regressions.ResultsRespectively, 10.0% (95% CI: 9.2%, 10.8%) infected participants and 3.1% (95% CI: 2.8%, 3.4%) uninfected participants reported persistent symptoms. In both groups, women more frequently reported persistent symptoms than men (adjusted prevalence ratios (aPRs) [95% CI]: 2.06 [1.70, 2.49] and 1.55 [1.25, 1.92], respectively). However, in infected participants, this association was stronger before than after 35 years (aPRs [95% CI]: 3.54 [2.12, 5.91], 1.77 [1.32, 2.37]; 1.65 [1.20, 2.26]; 1.74 [1.15, 2.63] for 17–34, 35–49, 50–64 and ≥ 65-year-olds, respectively, interaction P = 0.041). We found no effect modification by age in uninfected participants.ConclusionsWomen are a target population in post-COVID symptoms management, especially among younger adults. Sex and age-dependent mechanisms attributable to SARS-CoV-2 infection may be involved in post-COVID symptoms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-026-04826-8.

Background

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), post-COVID symptoms encompass a wide range of symptoms present 3 months after severe acute respiratory syndrome-coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) infection and lasting for at least 2 months with no other explanation [1]. There are currently few evidence-based therapies for these symptoms [2], despite their often significant impact on daily life and activities [1]. Female sex is one of the main risk factors for post-COVID symptoms, along with age, preexisting physical or mental comorbidities, severe acute SARS-CoV-2 infection, higher Body Mass Index (BMI), lower socio-economic status, and no history of vaccination against COVID-19 [3–5]. Mechanisms underlying the higher risk of post-COVID symptoms in women than in men are currently unknown. They may involve biological differences in immune response and/or social disparities in living conditions or health behaviours between men and women [6, 7].

In this context, investigating whether age modifies the association between female sex and post-COVID symptoms might contribute to its understanding. Indeed, immune response, which may be involved in post-COVID symptoms [8, 9], is age and sex dependent [10]. Several studies documented a higher T- and B-cell count and a higher antibody production in women [10, 11]. However, changes with age in the production of sex hormones may affect the immune system of women in many ways [10]. Namely, the production of type-I interferons (IFN type-I), that are associated with persistent symptoms after SARS-CoV-2 infection [8, 9], decreases after the menopause [11].

Age was found to modify the association between female sex and post-COVID symptoms in several studies, but results on the strength or direction of this effect modification were inconsistent [12–15]. Indeed, most studies on the topic had limitations: two of them excluded persons with non-severe forms of SARS-CoV-2 infection [12, 13], and one retrospectively assessed post-COVID symptoms using diagnosis codes partly recorded before a standardized clinical definition was given [14].

In addition, no study so far assessed effect modification by age on the association between sex and persistent symptoms in a control group of uninfected persons. Thus, it is unclear whether this effect modification by age on sex is attributable to SARS-CoV-2 infection, given that most post-COVID symptoms are non-specific and common in the general uninfected population [16]. Indeed, sex and age variations in the risk of post-COVID symptoms might be mediated by non-specific determinants of health, including age- and sex-dependent social characteristics or health behaviours [7].

We used data from a population-based cohort study with random sampling to investigate whether the association between sex and post-COVID symptoms evolves with age, and whether this evolution might be attributable to SARS-CoV-2 infection. We assessed effect modification by age on the association between sex and persistent symptoms, separately in participants with a confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection in 2022, i.e. during the Omicron wave, and in a control group of participants with no SARS-CoV-2 infection history. In both groups, we accounted for the potential mediating role of non-specific determinants of health (social characteristics, health behaviours and preexisting conditions) in the association between sex and persistent symptoms.

Methods

EpiCov design and data collection

Epicov sampling design and data collection have been described elsewhere [17]. Briefly, in April 2020, persons aged 15 or older living in France were randomly selected from the national Fichiers démographiques sur les logements et les individus (FIDELI) tax and administrative database. FIDELI covers more than 96% of the general population living in France, including immigrants residing in France on a legal basis. Of 371,000 initially selected persons, 134,391 (36.2%) participated in baseline (Spring 2020). Among them, 65,423 participated in all EpiCov follow-ups (Autumn 2020, Summer 2021 and Autumn 2022). All participants or their legally authorized representatives provided informed consent to participate in this study.

At baseline and at each follow-up, depending on the participant’s preferences, a questionnaire, written in French, was self-administered online, or administered by an investigator during telephone interviews. The questionnaire included data on living conditions, health state, health behaviours, history of testing for SARS-CoV-2 infection, and on the tests results. At EpiCov last follow-up, people were namely asked about the year and month of their first and their last positive tests, and about the year and month of onset, current presence and duration (less than 1 month/1 month/2–6 months/6 months–1 year/more than 1 year) of new and unusual symptoms. The exact questions asked on test history and symptoms are presented in Additional File 1: Supplementary Materials 1 and 2. The completion of the questionnaire allowed missingness.

Study population: infected and uninfected participants

Our analysis focused on persons who participated at all EpiCov data collection times, from baseline (Spring 2020) to the last follow-up (Autumn 2022). EpiCov participants were all aged 17 or older by last follow-up.

Infected participants included people who had reported a first positive SARS-CoV-2 polymerase chain reaction (PCR) or antigenic test done in 2022 (see Additional File 1: Supplementary Material 1 for additional details). At that time, Omicron variant was dominant, and most persons living in France were vaccinated against SARS-CoV-2 [18]. Uninfected participants included people who had reported no positive SARS-CoV-2 PCR or antigenic tests by last follow-up.

Outcome: 6-month persistent symptoms

In infected as well as in uninfected participants, the main outcome was the self-reported presence of any persistent symptom, i.e. any symptom appeared in 2022 and lasting for at least 6 months at third follow-up (Autumn 2022). Symptoms assessed in the third follow-up questionnaire (see Additional File 1: Supplementary Material 2) included fever, headache, cough, runny nose, fatigue, muscle pain, joint pain, dyspnoea, taste/smell loss, thoracic pain, digestive disorders, anxiety/depression/sleep problems, and brain fog/memory loss. For infected participants, we only considered symptoms that had appeared less than 1 month before the first positive test, or after it, to ensure that they could be related to SARS-CoV-2 infection.

Sex and age

For all participants, the FIDELI database included information on legal sex, corresponding to sex assigned at birth for the majority of the French general population [24], as well as on year of birth, used to compute age at third follow-up.

The distribution of age at third follow-up in infected and uninfected participants is presented in Additional File 2: Fig. S1. This variable was categorized into four classes according to the shape of its association with persistent symptoms in infected and uninfected men and women (see Additional File 2: Fig. S2): 17–34, 35–49, 50–64 and ≥ 65 years.

Potential mediators

Potential mediators assessed in the present study included known socio-demographic and health-related risk factors for post-COVID symptoms that are also sex-dependent [3–5]. Socio-economic status was approached by household income level per capita and educational level. Migratory status was identified using the participants’ nationality and country of birth, as well as the ones of their parents (majority population/first-generation European immigrants/second-generation European immigrants/first-generation non-European immigrants/second-generation non-European immigrants). BMI was calculated using self-reported height and weight (< 25 kg/m2/ ≥ 25 kg/m2). Health behaviours included smoking status (daily smoker/non-daily smoker/non-smoker) and vaccination (vaccinated: at least two doses of BNT162b2, nRNA-1273 or AD1222 vaccines, or one single-dose of Ad26.COV2.S vaccine). Potential mediators also included the presence of preexisting physical and/or mental conditions (see Additional File 1: Supplementary Material 3 for additional details).

To account for social disparities in living conditions or in attitudes towards health between men and women [7, 25, 26], we also included age of the youngest child in the household (< 6 years/6–12 years/ < 12 years/no child/lives alone), employment status (workers with a permanent contract / workers with a temporary contract/self-employed workers/non-workers, including students and retired participants), and perceived health state (very good/good/good enough/bad or very bad) as potential mediators in the association between sex and persistent symptoms.

Household income level per capita was obtained from the FIDELI database, and educational level was self-reported at baseline. All other potential mediators were self-reported at second follow-up (Summer 2021), i.e. before the first positive test for infected participants and before the onset of persistent symptoms for all included participants.

We used a directed acyclic graph (DAG), computed via Dagitty [27], to represent our assumptions on the causal relationships between our interest variables (Additional File 2: Fig. S3) [28].

Missing data

To account for EpiCov sampling design and for non-participation bias, we used, in all our analyses, weights derived from non-participation models and from calibration on margins [17, 29]. These weights, specifically designed to account for initial non-participation as well as for attrition until EpiCov last follow-up, were estimated via logit models and algorithms of statistical learning theory, using auxiliary variables available in the FIDELI sampling frame or obtained from previous EpiCov questionnaires (see Additional File 1: Supplementary Material 4 for additional details) [17, 29–32].

Respectively 1183 infected and 3104 uninfected participants followed up until Autumn 2022 had missing data on persistent symptoms (6.7% infected participants and 5.6% uninfected participants) or on other interest variables (highest missing rate: income level; 5.5% infected participants and 5.0% uninfected participants). The characteristics of participants with and without missing data are compared in Additional File 3. We performed multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) with 30 imputed datasets to handle missing data (see Additional File 1: Supplementary Material 5 for additional details) [33–38].

Statistical analysis

In the main analysis, we used univariable and multivariable modified Poisson regressions to estimate Prevalence Ratios (PRs) of persistent symptoms according to sex, overall and stratified by age, separately for infected and uninfected participants. Crude PRs obtained by univariable models provided estimates of the total effect of sex on persistent symptoms in each subgroup, whether this effect was mediated or not by non-specific health determinants [39]. Adjusted PRs obtained by multivariable models enabled us to assess the direct effect of sex on persistent symptoms (i.e. the part of the association between sex and persistent symptoms that was not mediated by the health determinants assessed in our study) [39]. We controlled our models for multicollinearity by checking the variance inflation factor of independent variables.

We then tested effect modification by age on the association between sex and persistent symptoms, separately for infected and uninfected participants. In a regression model adjusted for all the potential mediators cited above, we specified an interaction term between sex and age in years, modelled using restricted cubic splines with knots placed at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles [40]. This choice aimed at accounting for non-linearity in the association between age and persistent symptoms, while ensuring sufficient statistical power to the interaction test [41, 42].

In a secondary analysis, we assessed the prevalence by sex and age (17–34, 35–49, 50–64 and ≥ 65) of each symptom, with its 95% logit transformation-based confidence interval, separately for infected and uninfected participants.

All analyses were conducted on imputed datasets, obtained via the mice package [43], and using the survey [44] package on R software (4.1.0 version) [45]. Prevalences, prevalence ratios, as well as their confidence intervals obtained from Horvitz-Thompson type standard errors, were pooled across the imputed datasets using Rubin’s Rules [38]. The equality to zero of regression coefficients, including interaction terms, was tested with the Wald method. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and the type I error was set at 5% for all tests.

Sensitivity analyses

In our main analysis, as mentioned above, we used a specific timing criterion (i.e. symptom onset less than 1 month before the SARS-CoV-2 positive test, or after it) to define persistent symptoms among infected participants, which could have partly explained differences found between infected and uninfected subjects. To explore potential information bias linked to this definition, we reproduced our main analysis among infected participants using the same primary outcome as for uninfected participants (i.e. presence of any symptom appeared in 2022 and lasting for at least 6 months at third follow-up, regardless of its precise date of onset).

Moreover, to account for the higher risk of severe acute SARS-CoV-2 infection in men than in women [46], that might mediate the association between sex and post-COVID symptoms (see the study DAG in Additional File 2: Fig. S3), we reproduced our main analysis among infected participants after having excluded those who were hospitalized for SARS-CoV-2 infection in 2022 (0.7%).

Discussion

In this French national population-based study conducted among people aged 17 or older during the Omicron wave, women more frequently reported persistent symptoms than men, both in SARS-CoV-2 infected and uninfected participants. However, infected women were 3.5 times more likely to report persistent symptoms than infected men before 35 years old, versus less than twice afterwards. This effect modification by age was observed for most persistent symptoms assessed. Contrastingly, we found no effect modification by age on the association between sex and persistent symptoms in uninfected participants.

The effect modification by age observed among infected participants is in line with the findings of two cohorts including adults previously hospitalized for SARS-CoV-2 in Italy [13] and in the UK [12]. In these studies, women had a higher risk of post-COVID symptoms than men after discharge, especially at younger ages. Contrastingly, in two population-based cohort studies, conducted respectively in Sweden [14] and in the United States of America (USA) [15], the association between female sex and post-COVID symptoms was stronger among middle-aged participants [14]. However, they used other ways to identify post-COVID symptoms than in our study. The Swedish study [14] retrospectively assessed post-COVID symptoms via medical records using diagnosis codes partly recorded before a standardized clinical definition of post-COVID symptoms was given. This limitation may have led to differential information bias, due to sex and/or age disparities in healthcare seeking [47] or to differential diagnosis habits in healthcare professionals [48]. In the study conducted in the USA [15], post-COVID symptoms assessed did not include anxiety or depression, which seemed to largely contribute to differences between men and women observed in our study, namely before 35. In addition, a French cross-sectional population-based study conducted in 2022 found no effect modification by age on the association between sex and post-COVID symptoms [49]. However, analyses were conducted on a sample of moderate size (N = 1448 infected participants versus N = 10,618 in our study), and they therefore might be limited by a lack of power.

Our population-based study, conducted on a large random sample of participants aged 17 or older, therefore highlights women as a target population in the management of post-COVID symptoms, especially among younger people. In addition, it adds the information that the effect modification by age on the relation between sex and post-COVID symptoms is at least partly attributable to SARS-CoV-2 infection, as it was not observed in uninfected participants. Moreover, this effect modification by age persisted after accounting for potential mediators related to living conditions, preexisting health state and health behaviours. Our findings therefore suggest that non-specific sex disparities in health determinants do not entirely mediate the higher risk of post-COVID symptoms in women, especially among younger adults. They rather point to the possible role of sex- and age-dependent mechanisms triggered by SARS-CoV-2 infection, such as immune dysregulation, autoimmunity and/or chronic inflammation, in post-COVID symptoms [8, 9]. Indeed, immune response evolves with age in different ways among men and women [10, 11]. In particular, among women, changes in levels of estradiol and progesterone during pregnancies, as well as after the menopause, may affect both innate and adaptative immune responses [10, 11]. Namely, estradiol might influence the production of INF type-I [10, 11], that decreases more rapidly with age in women than in women [50]. High levels of IFN-I, that are involved in several autoimmune diseases such as Systematic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE) [9], were found to be associated with persistent symptoms months after SARS-CoV-2 infection [8, 9].

While the underlying mechanisms of post-COVID symptoms remain unknown, our study therefore highlights the need to further investigate the role of hormonal and immune phenomena in their development, especially in younger women. This investigation might also contribute to the understanding of post-viral infection syndromes that might share common causes with post-COVID symptoms [51].

To our knowledge, this study is the first to investigate the effect modification by age on the association between sex and persistent symptoms separately in SARS-CoV-2 infected and uninfected participants. We used data from a large national population-based cohort study with random sampling, including a majority of participants with no SARS-CoV-2 hospitalization history, whereas several studies on the topic only included persons who had been hospitalized for SARS-CoV-2 [12, 13]. We limited non-response and attrition bias, usual in population-based studies with random sampling characterized by low participation rates [52–54], by the implementation of non-response adjustment weights, using demographic and socio-economic indicators available for all eligible persons in the sampling frame. We accounted for the potential mediating role of several non-specific health determinants in the association between sex and post-COVID symptoms. These determinants included the main socio-demographic and health-related risk factors for post-COVID symptoms [3, 5], as well as indicators of disparities between men and women in living conditions and health behaviours [7, 26].

However, this study has limitations. First, we focused on persons with a first SARS-CoV-2 infection in 2022, i.e. when the Omicron variant was dominant [18]. Therefore, the effect modification by age found in this study may not concern people infected with other SARS-CoV-2 variants, or people reinfected by SARS-CoV-2. However, our results are consistent with the findings of several studies conducted at the beginning of the pandemic, before the Omicron variant became dominant [12, 13]. Second, EpiCov sampling frame (the FIDELI database) only included information on legal sex. Therefore, we were not able to account for the potential specificities of participants whose legal sex was different from sex assigned at birth. Third, test history and persistent symptoms were collected retrospectively, i.e. at EpiCov last follow-up. However, potential recall bias was probably limited by the short time-lapse between infection or symptom onset and data collection (i.e. less than 1 year). Fourth, as due to EpiCov questionnaires, we considered only symptoms persisting 6 months or more after SARS-CoV-2 infection, i.e. a longer duration than long COVID as defined by the WHO [1]. Last, we might have overestimated the prevalence of persistent symptoms among uninfected participants, and especially among women, due to the presence of false negatives in this group. Indeed, there is evidence that the sensitivity of SARS-CoV-2 antigenic tests decreased during the Omicron wave [55], and both PCR and antigenic tests were found to have a lower sensitivity in women than in men [55, 56]. However, to our knowledge, there is currently no evidence that the sensitivity of SARS-CoV-2 PCR or antigenic tests evolves differently with age among men and women. Therefore, it is unlikely that this limitation might explain our main result, i.e. an effect modification by age on sex found in SARS-CoV-2 infected participants only.

Conclusions

In this population-based study conducted among participants aged 17 or older during the French Omicron wave, women were 3.5 times more likely than men to report persistent symptoms after a first SARS-CoV-2 infection before 35 years old, versus less than twice afterwards. This effect modification by age was probably attributable to SARS-CoV-2 infection, as it was not observed in uninfected participants, and as it persisted after accounting for the potential mediating role of non-specific health determinants. Our study therefore highlights women as a target population in the management of post-COVID symptoms, especially among younger adults. Further research is needed to determine which age- and sex-dependent mechanisms related to SARS-CoV-2 infection are at stake in their vulnerability to post-COVID symptoms.
