A retrospective comparative study comparing sensory outcomes of free arterialized forearm vs medial plantar venous flaps following finger pulp defect reconstruction
Authors: Lingling Bai, Feiya Zhou, Xian Zhang, Xue Zhang, Wenwen Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeSensation is essential for finger pulp functionality. Our objective was to compare the sensory outcomes of forearm vs. medial foot arterialized venous flap reconstruction of finger pulp defects.MethodsWe conducted a study involving 26 arterialized venous flap reconstruction in 25 patients for finger pulp damage. The patients were categorized into two groups based on the donor site: the forearm group (n = 14) and the medial foot group (n = 12).ResultsAll flaps in both the forearm and medial foot groups survived without complications. Patients in the medial foot group experienced significantly improved sensory recovery compared to those in the forearm group. The majority of cases in the forearm group exhibited venous congestion and bullae formation within 5–10 days postoperatively. Conversely, in the medial foot group, all cases were free from venous congestion or bullae formation.ConclusionsOur findings indicate that arterialized venous flaps from the medial foot for finger pulp defect reconstruction yield superior sensory recovery outcomes compared to those from the forearm. Non-sensate arterialized venous flaps from the medial foot can provide satisfactory sensation for finger pulp coverage.Clinical relevanceLevel III. Clinical trial number: not applicable. Keywords: Arterialized venous flap, Sensation recovery, Finger-pulp reconstruction, Forearm, Medial foot

Introduction

The finger pulp, frequently referred to as the “eyes” of the fingers, is of paramount importance for hand functionality. Sensation is indeed crucial for the function of the finger pulp, as it is integral to the fine motor skills and tactile discrimination that the hand provides [1]. Traumatic injuries of finger pulps are very common in the upper extremity at the emergency department. The optimal treatment should be based on a simpleand reliable strategy in order to minimize the donor site morbidities with the best functional and cosmetic outcomes [1].

In terms of repairing hand and digit injuries, the arterialized venous flap (AVF) has become increasingly popular because of its ease of design, harvest and dependable quality [2]. Moreover the AVF has the advantage of being thin, malleable without the limitations of the donor site and does not require the loss of a major artery of the donor site [3]. AVFs have also been reported for finger pulp reconstructions harvested from the forearm or medial foot. However, there are no reports comparing the sensibility outcomes between the forearm AVFs and medial foot AVFs in literature [4–8]. Based on the anatomical similarity between medial foot skin and finger pulp tissue, we hypothesized that non-sensate arterialized venous flaps harvested from the medial foot would achieve better sensory recovery, less cold intolerance, and higher patient satisfaction than those harvested from the forearm, even without nerve coaptation. Therefore, this study aimed to compare the clinical outcomes of forearm and medial foot AVFs for finger pulp defect reconstruction, with particular emphasis on sensory recovery.

Methods

Patients

Institutional review board approval was obtained, with a waiver of informed consent per institutional protocol. Inclusion criteria: (1) traumatic finger pulp defect with exposure of flexor tendon and/or distal phalanx; (2) defect size requiring flap coverage; (3) intact digital nerves proximal to the defect; (4) primary reconstruction within 7 days after injury. Exclusion criteria: (1) concomitant digital nerve defect requiring nerve graft; (2) history of peripheral neuropathy or diabetes mellitus; (3) infection at the recipient site; (4) refusal to participate. Although the patient group included both isolated and circumferential injuries, all shared the common feature of full-thickness pulp loss with intact proximal digital nerves. To reduce heterogeneity, patients with nerve defects, prior finger surgery, or systemic neuropathy were excluded.

Twenty-six AVFs were retrospectively reviewed for the treatment of finger pulp defects in twenty-five patients between June 2012 and December 2017 in our hospital. It can be a simple pulp soft tissue defect or a circumferential finger avulsion injury with pulp defect. They were divided into two groups depending on the donor site, including the forearm group (n = 14) and the medial foot group (n = 12). In the forearm group, all patients sustained single finger pulp injury including thumb (n = 6), index finger (n = 2), middle finger (n = 3) and ring finger (n = 3), respectively. In the medial foot group, ten patients sustained single finger injury and one patient suffered double finger injuries. All included defects were full-thickness pulp defects with exposure of the distal phalanx and/or flexor tendon, requiring flap reconstruction. All patients presented with full-thickness pulp skin loss. Isolated pulp defects without deeper structure exposure were not included. Specifically, distal phalanx exposure was present in all cases, and flexor tendon exposure was present in 22 of 26 flaps (forearm group: 12; medial foot group: 10). No patient had isolated pulp defect without tendon or bone exposure. Injury mechanisms were classified as crush-type (e.g., machine-related, heavy object compression) or avulsion-type (e.g., ring avulsion, traction injury). Among the 25 patients, crush injuries accounted for 20 cases (forearm group: 12; medial foot group: 8), and avulsion injuries accounted for 5 cases (forearm group: 2; medial foot group: 3). No statistically significant difference in injury type was observed between the two groups (p > 0.05).

The patients in this study are consecutive AVFs cases and all the patients sustained local full thickness finger pulp skin defects and the digital nerves proximal to defects were intact. No flap was innervated in this series. All the flaps were obtained from the forearm or medial foot (Table 1).

Surgical methods

The procedure for harvesting a medial foot vein flap [9] involves first marking an axis line from 3.5 cm below the medial malleolus apex to the first metatarsal head as the flap’s central reference. The flap dimensions are outlined according to the recipient defect size. During dissection on the medial plantar aspect, subcutaneous dorsal foot veins with oblique proximal orientation become visible, demonstrating prominent venous engorgement when proximal outflow is occluded. One or two superficial veins matching the recipient vessel caliber are selected as the flap’s main venous trunks, with flap design adjusted according to both the defect characteristics and arterial positioning. The skin and superficial fascia are incised along the markings, and the flap is elevated at the deep subcutaneous plane containing the veins while preserving only the skin and a thin venous-adipose layer. Meticulous dissection avoids direct exposure of the main venous trunks, with vascular pedicle length harvested 20–30% longer than required to facilitate tension-free anastomosis. The venous outflow-to-inflow ratio is maintained at 1:2 or 1:3 through selective branch ligation.The vein flap of forearm group [10] was designed on the radial side of the forearm, which included 2 veins. The ratio of the afferent and efferent veins to be anastomosed was 1:1 or 1:2. The relatively smaller vein was used as the afferent vein, while the larger vein was used as the efferent vein.

In both groups, the donor vessels of the harvested flaps were anastomosed to the recipient digital proper arteries and dorsal digital veins with matched calibers.

Outcome evaluation

Sensory recovery, pinch power, cold intolerance and time of return to work were reviewed at the final follow-up observation conducted by 2 trained blinded hand surgeons. To test for sensory recovery [11], moving two-point discrimination (m-2PD), static two-point discrimination (s-2PD) and Semmes Weinstein monofilament test (SWM test) [12] were used. A Touch-Test two-point discriminator (Shanghai RuiShi Biological Technology Co., Ltd, Shanghai, China). As for Cold intolerance [13], the patients themselves rated subjectively based on how they feel in everyday life with the classification of none, slight, moderate or severe. Their subjective satisfaction was evaluated by a 10-point scoring system: 0 being very dissatisfied, 10 very satisfied.

Postoperative treatment

Postoperatively, the patients were asked to elevate the arm above the heart level in order to reduce venous congestion for about 3–5 days. Capillary refilling and the color of the flaps were observed carefully until the flap reached a normal state. The patients were also encouraged to perform active and passive range-of-motion exercises. The time of hospital stay was about 7–14 days depending on the healing condition of the flap. Complications were also recorded and managed accordingly.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were first assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test because of the small sample size. Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation and were compared using the independent-samples t-test. Non-normally distributed variables are presented as median with range or interquartile range and were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages and were compared using the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Ordinal variables, including cold intolerance, were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Exact P values were reported whenever possible, and 95% confidence intervals were calculated for between-group differences in the main outcomes.

Because this was a retrospective study of a relatively uncommon reconstructive procedure, an a priori sample size calculation was not feasible. Therefore, a post hoc sensitivity power analysis was performed based on the available sample size. With 14 flaps in the forearm group and 11 flaps available for final sensory assessment in the medial foot group, the study had 80% power at a two-sided alpha level of 0.05 to detect a large standardized between-group difference. Therefore, the present study was considered exploratory, and the results should be interpreted with caution. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 20.0 software.

As showed in Table 1, There were no significant differences between the two groups in demographic characteristics(p > 0.05). The two groups were comparable. In the forearm group, the defect size was from 20 × 25 mm to 60 × 30 mm and in the medial foot group from 20 × 25 mm to 45 × 25 mm. The average sizes of flaps 35 × 25 mm and 30 × 21 mm harvested from the forearm group and the medial foot group respectively. No statistically significant difference was observed in operative time between the two groups (p > 0.05). Most cases in the forearm group presented venous congestion and bulla formation for 5–10 days after the operation, which without special care subsided gradually: in contrast, all cases in the medial foot group survived completely without signs of venous congestion or bulla formation. All but one the patients were followed up at an average of 16.8 month (11–29 months). Three weeks post-surgery, 1 patient in the medial foot group was lost during the period of follow-up. In both groups none of these flaps required additional surgery for thinning or any complications. Close to full range of motion were achieved in all patients of both groups. According to the Hand Surgery clinical assessment criteria of the American Society, both groups achieved fair sensory recovery, with static two-point discrimination (s-2PD) ranging from 6 to 12 mm in the forearm group (mean ± SD: 8.86 ± 1.96 mm) and 4–9 mm in the medial foot group (6.55 ± 1.86 mm). The medial foot group demonstrated significantly better sensory recovery than the forearm group, with a mean difference in s-2PD of 2.31 mm (95% CI: 0.71 to 3.91 mm, p = 0.006). For moving two-point discrimination (m-2PD), the forearm group had a mean of 8.36 ± 1.55 mm, while the medial foot group had a mean of 5.73 ± 1.42 mm; the mean difference was 2.63 mm (95% CI: 1.38 to 3.88 mm, p < 0.001). Meanwhile, Semmes-Weinstein monofilament (SWM) test results also showed that patients in the medial foot group had significantly better sensory recovery than those in the forearm group (median difference: 0.8, 95% CI: 0.3 to 1.3, p = 0.009; Table 2).

Most cases of the forearm group showed cold intolerance with none in two patients, slight in two patients, moderate in eight patients and severe in two patients, while in the medial foot group, six patients reported slight cold intolerance, one patient reported moderate cold intolerance and the remainder did not report any cold intolerance. There was significant difference between the two groups (p < 0.05). The decline of the pinch power of the treated fingers was not significant among the two groups. The patients in the medial foot group returned to work on average 4 weeks earlier than those in the forearm group (p < 0.05). Case report

Case 1

A 50-year-old man suffered a crush injury by a machine with the defect of right thumb pulp. A forearm AVF was harvested on the right palmar forearm. The ratio of inflow and outflow of flap configuration was 1:3. The flap survived well postoperatively. S-2PD of the flap was 12 mm with a follow-up time of 18-month. The reconstructed thumb was described as satisfactory by the patient in terms of function and appearance. But he mentioned slight cold intolerance during the follow-up. After nearly 3 months, he returned to work. (Figures 1 and 2).

A 55-year-old woman suffered a crush injury by a machine with right middle and ring fingers defects. Two medial foot AVFs measuring 40 × 30 mm were transferred from the medial aspect of bilateral feet with the ratio of inflow and outflow of flap configuration was 1:2 (Fig. 3). The flap survived postoperatively. The patient returned to work after nearly 2 months and was very satisfied with the recovery condition. Discussion

Although recent advances in microsurgical and plastic surgery, patients are still seeking more aesthetic and sensory results [14]. The vascular course of the forearm flap is consistent, it is relatively thin, and it provides a large skin area, making it especially suitable for repairing facial and hand defects. However, because the forearm is an exposed area, even after suturing or skin grafting, the wound may still leave an unsightly scar. The aim of our study was to compare the flaps between the medial foot and the forearm, and determine which one is the most favorable for the reconstruction of the fingertip injuries in terms of sensation, cold intolerance, and patient satisfaction. Both the forearm AVFs and the medial foot AVFs have been reported, with good results in literatures, for the reconstruction of finger pulp. However, due to the inherent differences in venous network and tissue structures, there must be something different in reconstructive outcomes and comparing these two flaps seemed to be the only way to reach to a conclusion. Based on the results of s-2PD, m-2PD and SWM, the differences were significant between the two groups. The medial foot group showed better sensory recovery than the forearm group and the medial foot group demonstrated significantly less cold intolerance than the forearm group.

According to a systematic review and meta-analysis of the clinical use of arterialized venous flaps (AVFs), 92.2% show complete or nearly complete viability [15], indicating the reliability of these non-conventional flaps are comparable with the traditional ones. The AVF is a skin flap, which comprises of arterial inflow through an afferent vein perfusing the flap and then venous outflow through the efferent veins [16, 17]. In addition, arterialized venous flaps have the advantage of being useful in the repair of compound tissue defects [17, 18].

Yang et al. [19] reported the surgical procedures and efficacy of using medial plantar venous flaps for the repair of soft tissue defects of the fingers in twenty-nine cases, including reconstructions of twenty-two finger pulp or fingertip. All the flaps survived eventually. After the repair of defects in the fingertip and pulp, fingerprints appeared, and the protective sensation was restored. They suggested that the soft tissue defects of the fingers can be satisfactorily repaired with medial plantar venous flaps, and little damage was caused to the donor area.

Nonetheless, the reconstruction of digital pulp has been a challenge because of its unique anatomical structure in finger pulp. A great effort is being made by hand and plastic surgeons to find the optimal flap for the reconstruction of finger pulp defects [20]. Very few reports have been published in detail on sensory recovery of venous flaps.

Dr. Yan [3] report that the vein flaps are thinner than those of conventional arterial flaps because they consist only of skin, venous plexus and subcutaneous fat, which may theoretically facilitate a good sensation recovery through nerve regeneration surrounding the recipient site. Dr. Liu [21] found that Satisfactory sensory recovery can be obtained using non-innervated flaps for covering finger defects, especially in younger patients, with sensory recovery mainly depending on the following aspects. First, the ingrowth of the nerve ending from the peripheral and the wound bed could provide good sensory recovery when the flap is thin and narrow. Second, the finger is a highly innervated area and contains numerous nerve endings for regeneration. Third, relatively young patients show improved regeneration and recovery.

Restoring the sensory function, without any doubt, of the affected finger may play a role in the selection of these flaps for the reconstruction of finger pulp.

It is intuitive that finger pulp reconstruction may be a favorable option to be reconstructed using sensate flaps to satisfy its function and in our previous study we compared a forearm sensate flap with an insensate arterialized venous flap.15 As expected, most of the flaps achieved normal sensation in the sensate group. However, poor results were shown commonly in the insensate group. In the present study, we achieved similar results as noted in our previous study [22] in terms of sensation recovery for the patients in the forearm group reconstructed using insensate AVFs, while the patients in the medial foot group presented similar results as observed in patients treated with sensate AVFs. This shows that it is possible to obtain good sensation recovery with the medial foot flap despite being a non-sensate flap. We also noted that patients in the medial foot group were much more satisfied in general than those in the forearm group.

The exact mechanism underlying the observed difference in sensory recovery remains unclear. Several hypotheses may be considered, though none can be confirmed without further histological or neurophysiological investigation. First, it is possible that compensatory ingrowth of small nerve fibers from the surrounding recipient bed contributed to sensory reinnervation, as no nerve coaptation was performed. Second, the structural similarity between medial foot skin and finger pulp tissue—both being relatively thick, glabrous, and with low mobility—might theoretically facilitate better sensory integration compared to the loose, non-glabrous forearm skin. However, these explanations remain speculative and require validation through histological analysis of nerve regeneration and neurophysiological studies. Then, they are thinner than those of conventional arterial flaps because they consist only of skin, venous plexus and subcutaneous fat, which may theoretically facilitate a good sensation recovery through nerve regeneration surrounding the recipient site.

There are several limitations to this study. First, the retrospective nature of the study may introduce biases related to data collection and patient selection. Second, the follow-up times varied among patients, with a relatively short mean follow-up period, which may affect the accuracy of the long-term outcomes assessment. Third, the study included different surgeons and observers, which could potentially introduce inter-observer variability and biases. Additionally, the number of patients in the two groups was not equal, and patients were not matched for confounding factors, which may influence the comparability of the results. Finally, the small sample size limits the statistical power of the study, despite a post-hoc power of 0.82 for the primary outcome, the risk of type II error for secondary outcomes remains. Future research should address these limitations through larger, prospective, multicenter studies with standardized follow-up protocols and matched patient cohorts to better assess the advantages and disadvantages of using arterialized venous flaps for finger pulp reconstruction. Furthermore, this study did not include histological examination of the flaps (e.g., nerve fiber density, mechanoreceptor distribution), neurophysiological assessments (e.g., nerve conduction studies, quantitative sensory testing), or cortical sensory mapping (e.g., somatosensory evoked potentials or functional MRI). These investigations would be valuable in future research to clarify the mechanisms of sensory recovery following non-sensate arterialized venous flap reconstruction.

Conclusion

In this retrospective comparative study, non-sensate arterialized venous flaps from the medial foot yielded significantly better sensory recovery, less cold intolerance, and higher patient satisfaction than those from the forearm for finger pulp defect reconstruction. These findings suggest that the structural and histological similarity between medial foot skin and finger pulp tissue may facilitate neural ingrowth and sensory reinnervation, even without direct nerve coaptation. However, the exact mechanism remains unclear, and the absence of histological, neurophysiological, and cortical mapping studies is a major limitation. Given the small sample size, larger prospective studies are needed to validate these results. Clinically, the medial foot arterialized venous flap represents a favorable alternative for finger pulp reconstruction when optimal sensory recovery is desired.
