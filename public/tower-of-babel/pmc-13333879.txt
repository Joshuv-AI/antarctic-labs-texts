Influence of shallow loess crack patterns on meso-pore morphology and distribution
Authors: Luqing Zhao, Zhen Liu, Cuiying Zhou, Xuerong Chen, Guangjun Cui, Jianbing Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFrequent shallow loess disasters pose severe threats to line engineering and public safety. Millimeter-scale cracks serve as key controlling factors in the initial stage of shallow loess disaster development. Such cracks can alter the morphology and distribution of mesoscopic pores within loess to influence permeability and mechanical behavior, consequently reducing the stability of shallow loess masses. Therefore, this study investigates the mesoscale initial damage state of loess specimens containing millimeter-scale cracks with different patterns through laboratory experiments. Uniaxial compression tests coordinated with CT scanning were conducted to record damage state images. Machine learning methods were employed to establish three-dimensional models of the meso-pore structure, allowing systematic quantitative analysis of pore size, arrangement, and morphology. Experimental results indicate that specimens containing cracks reach the initial damage state at lower stresses. Cracks at different depths reduced the initial damage stress by approximately 68%, cracks with different inclinations reduced it by about 72%, and cracks with different combinations reduced it by about 83%. Under the damage state, small pores increased while large pores decreased. Near-horizontal and near-vertical pores suffered severe damage. Spherical pores exhibited more regular morphology, and irregular pores showed more complex morphology. Damage pores primarily distribute in clustered patterns along the extension directions of cracks at different depths, in striped patterns along the inclination directions of different inclined crack tips, and in concentrated patterns between different combined cracks. Under the localized concentration of tensile and shear stresses at crack tips and sides, pore structures collapsed and particles rearranged within the affected soil, leading to the formation of small pores and the disappearance of large pores. Introduction

Frequent shallow disasters on loess slopes seriously threaten the safety of engineering structures and local residents1–5. Millimeter-scale initial cracks formed under external environmental factors present a critical structural element in the disaster mechanism of shallow loess slope failures3,6. Soil with millimeter-scale initial cracks experiences a progressive failure process involving initial damage, crack propagation, and complete failure7–10. Initial damage is a critical indicator of soil failure, research on the damage caused by millimeter-scale cracks to meso-pores is essential for understanding the chain reaction process of shallow disaster in loess slopes3,11,]12.

Engineering geological problems in loess regions are related to the microstructure of loess13–16. The properties and behavior of loess under different environmental conditions are closely related to its microstructure and composition. Tang17 investigated soil cracking mechanisms under evaporative conditions. Water evaporation-induced volume contraction generates non-uniform tensile stress fields within the soil matrix. When tensile stresses exceed inter-particle cohesive forces, particle separation leads to soil cracking. Zhao18 explored loess cracking behavior under freeze-thaw cycles. The expansion forces generated when water freezes into ice crystals alter the arrangement of soil particles, and the resulting loose, large-pore structure promotes cracking. Jiang et al.19 observed the microstructure of loess before and after triaxial tests through SEM and MIP, illustrating that intergranular pores impact soil deformation. Li et al.13 studied the microstructural evolution of intact loess under varying stress levels and stress paths. They noted that the disintegration of clay aggregates and the destruction of carbonate cement and other bonding cause damage to soil structure under loading.

The presence of millimeter-scale cracks influences soil mechanical properties. Under loading, existing cracks initiate new cracks, which further expand and connect, ultimately causing soil failure20–22. Griffith23 analyzed the relationship between crack parameters and soil failure using the principle of energy dissipation. Ahmadi and Molladavoodi24 developed a micromechanical damage model to analyze crack-induced failure and deformation patterns in soils. Amini et al.25 employed acoustic emission analysis to examine the damage process in cracked concrete, quantitatively identifying the initial stress threshold for material rupture. Qi26 visualized crack morphologies in cracked rock specimens using image correlation (DIC) techniques, validating and describing crack outcomes through PFC2D modeling. Researches have extensively investigated fracture damage in geomaterials at both micro- and macro-scales20,21,27,22,28,29, however, limited attention has focused on the impact of millimeter-scale cracks on the meso-pores within loess.

Based on field investigations of the depth, inclination, and combinations of millimeter-scale cracks in natural shallow loess, this study conducted uniaxial compression tests coordinated with CT scanning on loess specimens containing different patterns of millimeter-scale cracks. Machine learning methods were employed to extract the three-dimensional structure of the meso-pore network. The research systematically quantified changes in pore structure morphology and distribution before and after damage, providing an in-depth understanding of the damage mechanisms of different millimeter-scale crack patterns on the micro-pore structure. These findings provide scientific insights for studying the behavior of cracked loess and contribute to furthering research on the mechanisms of shallow soil disasters.

Materials and methods

Materials description

The loess on the southern plateau of Jingyang is characterized as eolian sedimentary soil, located in Jingyang County, Shaanxi Province, in the southern part of China’s Loess Plateau (Fig. 1a). The Jingyang southern plateau covers a total area of approximately 70 km², with an elevation ranging from 420 to 490 m, sloping from east to west. Shallow disasters such as debris flow and collapse frequently occur on loess slopes along the plateau’s edge (Fig. 1b). An in-situ investigations revealed widespread millimeter-scale cracks in the shallow layer of loess slopes. These cracks separate the shallow soil into unstable blocks, which are highly susceptible to detachment and collapse under external disturbances, leading to shallow disasters. These millimeter-scale cracks are primarily distributed within the top 3 m of the slope surface, with widths ranging from 0.1 mm to 0.3 mm and dip angles predominantly between 0° and 90° (Fig. 1c). This distribution and geometric characteristics are consistent with the systematic survey results of millimeter-scale cracks in the Loess Plateau reported by6. The shallow soils on the slope’s free face are subject to negligible constraints from adjacent soil pressures. Therefore, uniaxial compression testing was employed in this study to simulate the stress-damage process of shallow slope soils. The intact Q3 loess blocks, approximately 400 mm × 200 mm × 200 mm in size, were collected from a depth of 3 m within a slope on the southern plateau of Jingyang (Fig. 1d). To preserve the natural state of the sample, the block was sealed in plastic film with its in-situ orientation clearly marked. After being placed in a wooden box filled with flexible material, the samples were transported to the laboratory.

Sample preparation

Based on the geometric morphology and distribution characteristics of millimeter-scale cracks in shallow loess, intact loess specimens with different characteristic cracks were prepared. First, appropriately sized soil masses were cut from block samples, then trimmed with a wire saw into cylinders of 100 mm diameter and 50 mm height. The cylinder samples were placed into tubular containers with pre-marked cracks designed from transparent PVC material. Artificial cracks were then cut into the loess samples using wire saw, carving knife, and specialized tools, following the pre-marked crack patterns. The through-cracks measured 2 mm in width and 20 mm in length.

Figure 3 displays loess specimens with different characteristic crack. Specimens can be categorized as follows: by distribution depth, top-crack specimens (T), middle-crack specimens (M), and bottom-crack specimens (B); by dip angle, 0° inclination crack specimens (M-0), 45° inclination crack specimens (M-45), and middle 90° inclination crack specimens (M-90); by combination characteristics: top-middle crack specimens (TM), top-bottom crack specimens (TB), and middle-bottom crack specimens (MB). Tubular-like channels extending inward into the sample were observed on the specimen surface. These pore channels are distinctive secondary pore structures within intact loess, primarily comprising plant root channels and insect burrows, which are widely found in loess.

Experimental setup and testing process

Uniaxial compression tests were conducted using the YYW-2 strain-controlled unconfined compression apparatus. A specimen holder was designed to contain the specimen and maintain it at a specific deformation state during CT scanning. The CT holder, constructed from transparent acrylic material, enables real-time observation of specimen failure. A positioning arrangement beneath the CT holder ensures specimens are scanned from the same orientation during different deformation stages, enabling continuous analysis of deformation processes from a consistent viewpoint. According to the Standards for Geotechnical Testing Methods, when axial strain is less than 3% during loading, the readings of the axial stress gauge and axial displacement gauge shall be recorded once for every 0.5% increase in axial strain. When axial strain exceeds 3%, readings from the axial stress gauge and axial displacement gauge should be recorded once per 1% axial strain increment. Testing should cease after an additional 3%-5% axial strain compression when the axial force stress gauge reading reaches its peak or stabilizes. Previous studies indicate that when microcracks appear on the specimen surface during loading, the specimen has entered an initial damage state30,31.

CT scanning tests were performed with the nanoVoxel-2000 micro-CT scanner. The device features a high-performance imaging system with an enclosed X-ray source, a sample chamber accommodates large-sized specimens, and supports multiple scanning modes including spiral, offset, and finite-angle. It offers pixel detail resolution of 0.05 μm and high spatial resolution of 0.5 μm. CT scanning was performed on loess samples in their original state and initial damage state, with a resolution of 72 μm. The structural units visible in the CT images belong to the mesoscale.

The testing process is illustrated in Fig. 4. The cracked loess specimens were placed into CT holders, which were then positioned in the unconfined compression apparatus for uniaxial compression testing. When the planned uniaxial compression was reached, the specimen was restrained in the specific deformation state with the CT holder’s immobilization device. The holder was moved into the CT sample chamber following the positioning arrangement at its base, then CT scanning was performed on samples to image their internal structures. Following pre-reconstruction and hardening calibration of CT images, three-dimensional reconstruction was performed using Dragonfly 3D image processing software. Quantitative analysis of the three-dimensional structure of the loess specimens was conducted after image segmentation and processing32–34.

Data processing

A machine learning method employing U-Net network as model architecture was used to identify and extract the internal structure of loess specimens at the original state and the initial damage state. Machine learning methods can integrate morphological features and grayscale data to identify different object, and the identification accuracy can be improved through reasonable manual adjustments. Both pore and crack areas appear black in CT images, and their grayscale ranges have high overlap, making it difficult to distinguish them using traditional threshold segmentation methods. Therefore, this study employs machine learning methods to effectively distinguish between cracks and pores.

The training process of the machine learning method is illustrated in Fig. 5. To begin with, a region exhibiting typical loess matrix, pores, and cracks was selected within any X-Y plane CT image. Through threshold segmentation, these elements were individually identified and marked with distinct colors, and a training dataset comprising loess matrix, pore, and crack samples was established. The U-net deep learning model was adopted as the discrimination model, trained in 2.5D dimensions. Labeled samples were segmented into 64 × 64-pixel training data with a segmentation stride of 16 pixels. The loss function employed was Ors Dice Loss, and the optimization algorithm was Adadelta. After inputting the training data into the discrimination model, 100 iterations of optimization training were conducted. The trained model was applied to detect loess matrix, cracks, and pores within any other X-Y plane CT image. Incorrect identifications were corrected and incorporated as new training data to repeat the training process. This iterative process continued until the validation metric curve stabilized below 0.1, confirming the establishment of a deep learning model capable of distinguishing loess matrix, cracks, and pores. In this study, an average of 300 training dataset was used for each loess specimens.

Results

Initial damage state

The stress-strain curves and initial damage patterns of loess specimens containing millimeter-scale cracks with different characteristics are shown in Fig. 7. Both intact specimens and cracked specimens exhibit uniaxial compression stress-strain curves with an initial rise followed by a decline. Specimen I begin to develop damage at an axial pressure of approximately 65 kPa, with damage appearing on one side of the specimen top (Fig. 7a). Specimens T, M, and B exhibit damage at axial pressures of approximately 28 kPa, 18 kPa, and 16 kPa, respectively (Fig. 7b). Cracks located at the center and lower regions of the specimens are more prone to initiating damage. The damage crack in Specimen T develop from the pre-existing crack at the top and propagate downward, accompanied by vertically extended damage cracks within the specimen. In Specimen M, the damage crack develops from both ends of the pre-existing crack in the middle and propagated toward the specimen ends in a direction nearly parallel to the pre-existing crack. In Specimen B, the damage crack propagates from the side of the pre-existing crack at the bottom toward the specimen’s lateral surface. Damage crack distribution correlates with pre-existing crack depth.

Uniaxial stress-strain curves and initial damage states of loess specimens containing millimeter-scale cracks. (a) Intact specimen, (b) specimen with cracks at different depths, (c) specimen with cracks at different angles, (d) specimen with different crack combinations.

Specimens M-0, M-45, and M-90 develop damage at axial pressures of approximately 14 kPa, 23 kPa, and 18 kPa, respectively (Fig. 7c). Cracks inclined at 0° and 90° are more prone to initiating damage. The damage crack in Specimen M-0 develop at the end of the pre-existing crack, propagating both upward and downward at an inclination. Similarly, the damage crack in Specimen M-45 initiate at the end of the pre-existing crack and propagate upward and downward. In Specimen M-90, the damage crack propagates nearly in the same direction from both ends of the pre-existing crack.

Specimen TM, TB, and MB develop damage at axial pressures of approximately 7 kPa, 13 kPa, and 13 kPa, respectively (Fig. 7d). Cracks forming both at the top and in the middle are more likely to cause damage. In specimen TM, the damage cracks develop from the edges of pre-existing cracks at the top and in the middle, extending downward from the top crack edge and upward from the middle crack edge, respectively, showing a tendency to penetrate each other. In specimen TB, damage cracks develop from the end of the pre-existing bottom crack and propagated upward, with no clearly visible damage cracks forming near the pre-existing top crack. In specimen MB, damage cracks propagate in the same direction from both ends of the pre-existing middle crack, interconnecting with damage cracks propagating in the same direction from the end of the pre-existing bottom crack.

Loess is a porous material characterized by randomly distributed large-pore structures. Under compressive loading, pores are damaged within the specimens, resulting in changes of pore size distribution, as shown in Fig. 8. Specimen I shows a reduction in pores near its top. Specimen T shows an increase in large pores and clustered distribution beneath the top crack. Specimen M shows an increase in large pores near damage cracks initiated at the upper and lower edges of the middle crack, with dense, striped distribution along the damage cracks. Specimen B shows an increase in large pores near the bottom crack. Specimen M-0 shows an increase in large pores on both sides of the middle crack. Specimen M-45 shows an increase in large pores near damage cracks initiated at the upper and lower edges of the 45° crack, with dense, striped distribution along the damage cracks. Specimen M-90 is the same specimen as Specimen M. Specimen TM shows large pores mainly distributed between the top crack and the middle crack. Specimen TB shows large pores mainly distributed near the bottom crack. Specimen MB shows large pores mainly distributed between the middle crack and the bottom crack.

Figure 9 illustrates the distribution characteristics of pore volume at the initial damage stage. At the original state T0 and initial damage state T1, the number of pores within specimens I, T, M, and B first increases and then decreases with increasing volume (Fig. 9a). At T0 state, the proportions of pores in the small volume range (0–1E + 6 μm³) are approximately 21.4%, 19.5%, 23.3%, and 26.2%, respectively, while those in the large volume range (1E + 12–1E + 14 μm³) are approximately 0.0008%, 0.0009%, 0, and 0.0006%, respectively. After reaching T1 state, the number of small-volume pores changes by -1.8%, + 2.4%, -2%, and + 5.4%, respectively, while the large-volume pore distribution changes by -0.00003%, -0.00023%, -0.00009%, and − 0.00034%, respectively. Under axial compression, Specimen B shows the greatest increase in small-volume damage pores and the most destruction of large-volume pores, followed by Specimen T.

Pore volume distribution.

Specimens M-0, M-45, M-90, TM, TB, and MB show similar patterns in pore volume (Fig. 9b,c). After reaching T1 state, for specimens with different inclination cracks, the proportion of small-volume damage pores increases by 5.6%, 5.4%, and − 2%, respectively, while the proportion of large-volume pores decreases by 0.00034%, 0.0001%, and 0.00009%, respectively. The 0° crack generates more small-volume damage pores while also destroying more large-volume pores. For specimens with different combinations of cracks, the number of small-volume pores increases by 11.4%, 9.9%, and 6.8%, respectively, while the number of large-volume pores changes by -0.0004%, -0.00023%, and + 0.00003%, respectively. The top-middle crack combination generates more small-volume damage pores and more large-volume pores.

In the initial damage state, damage of the pore structure primarily involves the formation of small-volume pores and the disappearance of large-volume pores. The flatter the crack angle, the more small-volume damage pores are generated and the more large-volume pores are destroyed. Combined cracks lead to a greater formation of small-volume damage pores and a greater destruction of large-volume pores. This is because local stress destroys the particle structure and the separation of particles, causing the formation of small-volume damage pores and the breaking of large-volume pores16,35.

By the T1 state, the number of large-surface-area pores in specimens with different inclinations decreases by 0.00034%, 0.0001%, and 0.0001%, respectively, whereas more large-surface-area pores are destroyed in the 0°-inclined crack specimen. The reduction in large-area pores is due to axial loading causing pores to be squeezed and broken into smaller pores, with some broken pores forming partial damage cracks.

Meso-pore arrangement

Figure 12 shows the distribution of pore space inclination angle Phi at initial damage. At the T0 state, the highest number of pores with Phi between 0–15° is found in specimens I, M-45, and TB, while the highest number of pores with Phi between 75–90° is observed in specimens T, B, M-0, M-90 (M), TM, and BM. This indicates that meso-pores in intact loess primarily distribute along subhorizontal and subvertical orientations. Reaching the T1 state, the changes of Phi in specimens T, B, M-0, M-90(M), and TM are low, within 5%. Specimens I, M-45, TB, and MB show significant increases in Phi, up to 28%. This may be attributed to the generation of numerous small damage pores and large broken pores in specimens M-45, TB, and MB under axial pressure loading (as shown in Fig. 9b, c).

Meso-pore shape

Figure 14 shows the distribution of pore sphericity during initial damage. At the T0 state, the number of pores in all specimens increases with increasing sphericity. Over 68% of pores exhibit sphericity between 0.9 and 1. Most pores exhibited relatively smooth shapes. This is attributed to the formation of numerous naturally spheroidal pores during the sedimentation process of native loess, while some secondary pores, such as irregular insect burrows and root channels, present lower sphericity36,6. Arriving at the T1 state, the number of pores with sphericity below 0.9 reduces, while those within the 0.9–1 range increases, with over 70% of pores exhibiting sphericity exceeding 0.9. Axial compression enhances pore sphericity.

Pore sphericity distribution.

During the T0 and T1 states, pores in specimens I, T, M, and B show a positive correlation between number and sphericity (Fig. 14a). Arriving at the T1 state, the number of pores with sphericity between 0.9 and 1 increases by 1.32%, 3.45%, 2.79%, and 3.89%, respectively. The number of pores with sphericity below 0.9 decreases. Pre-existing cracks at the top and bottom positively influence the improvement in pore sphericity. Specimens M-0, M-45, M-90, TM, TB, and MB exhibit similar patterns in pore sphericity (Fig. 14b, c). By the T1 state, the proportion of spherical pores (sphericity > 0.9) increased by 3.88%, 4.48%, and 2.79% for specimens with different crack inclinations, respectively. Among specimens with different combination cracks, the proportion of spherical pores (sphericity > 0.9) increases by 1.8%, 2.06%, and 1.02%, respectively. The 45° inclined cracks and top-bottom combination cracks are more effective in altering pore morphology.

Among the specimens with cracks of different depths, specimen B exhibited the greatest decrease in connected porosity, approximately 1.81%, while specimen M exhibited the greatest increase in isolated porosity, approximately 0.66%. Among the specimens with cracks at different inclinations, specimen M-45 exhibited the greatest decrease in connected porosity, approximately 1%, while specimen M-90 showed the greatest increase in isolated porosity, approximately 0.66%. Among the specimens with cracks in different combinations, specimen MB exhibited the greatest decrease in connected porosity, approximately 2.91%, while specimen TM showed the greatest increase in isolated porosity, approximately 0.48. Discussion

Due to the unique substable structure, the deformation mechanism of loess is complex, with its deformation process and failure characteristics dominated by the spatiotemporal evolution of its pore structure. The pore network of loess is formed by cementing materials (primarily clay minerals and calcium carbonate) bonding together silt and sand particles. The high bonding strength of clay minerals and inter-particle friction determine the stability of the substable structure. When stresses acting on the pore structure exceed the initial yield strength, weak cementation chains and contact points fail to resist the stress intensity, leading to cracks along relatively weak cementation bonds. This causes localized pore collapse. Accompanied by aggregate disintegration, bonding failure, and particle rearrangement, larger pores transform into smaller ones, with pore shapes changing. Localized damage typically forms along these weak cementation and contact zones within specimens12. The presence of cracks provides structural defects, which tend to concentrate stresses at both ends, consequently inducing damage26,37.

Scholars have employed fracture mechanics methods to investigate the cracking mechanisms of rock and soil bodies38,39. A small plastic zone exists at the crack tip under compression, where stress concentration readily occurs. Chen Chi’s realistic blunted tip crack model clarifies that tensile stress is primarily concentrated at the crack tip under compression40. According to the maximum stress release criterion and crack propagation criterion41,42, once the crack has formed, the internal tensile stress perpendicular to the existing crack plane has been released. The new direction of maximum tensile stress must be parallel to the existing crack plane. Therefore, a zone of maximum tensile stress concentration exists on the side of the existing crack. Meanwhile, loess materials usually behave as brittle fracture failures. Due to the anisotropic properties of soil structures, the actual stress conditions within the soil are complex, and crack tips are also subjected to shear stresses. As shown in Fig. 16, under axial compressive loading, the crack tip is primarily subjected to tensile and shear stress, while the crack side is mainly subjected to tensile stress. The tensile stress distributed at the crack tip initiate edge-extended cracks and form large damage pores around the tip. The shear stress at the crack tip initiate edge-wing crack and create striped patterns of damage pores along the crack path. The tensile stress at the crack flank initiates lateral-wing crack and form striped patterns of damage pores along the crack path.

Damage pore distribution patterns.

Figure 17 illustrates the layer-by-layer surface porosity of specimens with different crack patterns after damage. The specimens are divided into approximately 1400 CT slice images from top to bottom. The most pronounced pore changes occurred in the middle-lower region of Specimen T, in the areas above and below the middle crack of Specimen M, and in the region above the bottom crack of Specimen B. This indicates that cracks at different depths significantly affect pore damage along their propagation direction. This phenomenon relates to the development of extended damage cracks at various depths. The greatest pore changes are observed around the cracks in specimens M-0, M-45, and M-90. This is attributed to the damage wing cracks at the crack tips, where damaged pores distribute in a striped pattern along the wing-crack around the tip. The porosity around the cracks in specimen M-0 also experiences significant variation. This may be attributed to stress concentration at both ends of the 0° cracks, causing the pore structure to be deformed by external forces in these regions. In specimen TM, the most significant pore distribution changes are observed between the top and middle cracks. In specimen TB, the greatest pore changes occur above the bottom crack. In specimen MB, noticeable pore changes are found above both the middle and bottom cracks. Compared to single cracks, combinational cracks cause greater damage to pores. This may be due to the susceptibility of combinational cracks to developing through-crack damage.

Layer-by-layer surface porosity.

The deformation of loess is a progressive process, with the internal structure at different locations continuously changing under loading and wetting. During loading and wetting, the damage of the initial pore structure, the weakening of cementation, the disintegration of clay, and the rearrangement of particles have been demonstrated to reduce strength and promote deformation development43,16. Changes in large pores promote the deformation process of soil44,45. Wei et al.12 reported that the pore structure responds most sensitively to loess deformation, with significant local variations. The collapse in pore structure indicates that the specimens have entered the initial stage of structural failure, followed by a phase of large-scale structural collapse. Changes in pore structure signal a period of accelerated strength decline. As soil strength rapidly decreases, irreversible damage cracks develop within the soil body. The emergence and interpenetration of these cracks directly lead to rapid soil failure. Variations in pore structure can serve as early structural indicators of loess failure. Timely identification of loess pore morphology and distribution will advance the prediction of soil failure processes.

Additionally, the clustered or striped distribution of damage pores promotes rapid infiltration of rainwater and irrigation water. Typically, higher loess porosity, larger average pore size, and longer or irregularly shaped connected pores are correlated with better permeability and faster water migration rates46. Loess pores distribute in clusters within the vertical plane. These pores feature strong vertical connectivity but weaker connectivity in the other two directions. The concentrated distribution of damaged pores in the vertical plane further enhances vertical water migration within the soil mass, which negatively impacts slope stability. Under the effects of unloading and gravity, damaged pore accumulates within the soil mass of cracked slope. These pores will rapidly transfer rainwater and irrigation water into the internal slope. The moisture content of lower soil quickly increases to saturation, and pore water pressure rises. Under the soil-water interaction, the shear strength of the soil is degraded. This creates a potential sliding surface within the slope, triggering landslide disasters (as shown in Fig. 18).

Conclusion

This study conducted uniaxial compression experiments with CT scanning on loess specimens containing millimeter-scale cracks. The influence of crack depth, inclination, and combination on the morphology and distribution of mesoscale pores at the initial damage state was systematically quantified. The impact of mesoscale damage pores on shallow disaster in loess slopes was discussed. Based on the obtained results, the following conclusions can be drawn:

Specimens containing cracks enter the initial damage state at lower stresses. Compared to the initial damage stress of intact specimens, crack depth reduces the initial damage stress by approximately 68%, crack inclination reduces it by about 72%, and crack combinations reduce it by roughly 83%. Bottom cracks, 0°-inclined cracks, and top-mid combined cracks most severely impact the initial damage state.

Natural loess features a large-pore structure. In the initial damage state, smaller pores increase while larger pores decrease. Subhorizontal and subvertical pores suffer severe damage. The morphology of spherical pores becomes more regular, while tubular pores become more irregular.

For cracks at different depths, damaged pores are primarily distributed in clustered pattern along the crack extension direction. For cracks with different inclinations, damaged pores mainly form striped distribution near the damaged wing-cracks at their tips. For combined cracks, damaged pores are predominantly distributed between the cracks. Damaged pores are also distributed on both sides of the cracks. Top, bottom, and 0°-inclined cracks show the greatest influence on pore size, and combined cracks significantly change pore arrangement.

Loading induces stress concentration zones in the soil surrounding the existing crack edges and sides. Under tensile and shear stresses, the intergranular cementation bonds fracture, the pore structure collapses, and particles rearrange. This results in the formation of new small pores and the destruction of large pores. The changes in pore structure trigger and exacerbate the damage state of the soil.

A key contribution of this study is the mesoscopic investigation of initial damage states under the influence of millimeter-scale cracks. The damage evolution of loess mesostructure demonstrates a strong correlation with crack patterns, which provides insights into the disaster behavior of shallow cracked loess. This manuscript currently investigates only the initial damage state under uniaxial compression, with subsequent failure processes not yet explored. To better reflect actual loading conditions, the authors will conduct future research on the complete failure process under complex stress conditions.
