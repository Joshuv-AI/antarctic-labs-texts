A digital twin-driven computation and analysis framework for low-altitude airspace
Authors: Zhenghan He, Weibin Zhang, Peng Du, Zhiyong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aims to address critical challenges in low-altitude airspace management, including high dynamic complexity, substantial safety hazards, and information fragmentation, and proposes a digital twin-enabled computation and analysis framework. The proposed framework adopts a four-layer digital twin architecture, enabling high-fidelity spatial mapping through multi-source heterogeneous data fusion, unified spatiotemporal representation, dynamic evolution modeling, and bidirectional physical-virtual closed-loop interaction. The proposed framework also incorporates intelligent components: a bidirectional GRU-Seq2Seq trajectory prediction model and a Kalman filter-based error compensation mechanism, supporting quantitative resource assessment, real-time trajectory analysis, and conflict warning. The proposed framework is verified experimentally in three operational scenarios: conventional single-target flight, multi-target cooperative flight, and extreme weather interference. Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed method has an average trajectory prediction error of 1.52 m under standard conditions, achieving a reduction of 47.9% compared to the Transformer baseline (2.36 m), 38.7% compared to ST-GCN (2.48 m), and 42.2% compared to the traditional geometric twin (2.63 m). Furthermore, the proposed method consistently maintains a low error across varying prediction horizons (5–15 s), with a maximum error of only 3.9 m at 15 s, significantly outperforming Transformer (4.3 m), ST-GCN (4.6 m), and the traditional geometric twin (6.3 m) models. In challenging scenarios involving GPS denial and communication interruption, the proposed method maintains superior robustness, with an average error of 2.15 and 2.38 m, respectively, which are markedly lower than those of competing methods. The results confirm that the proposed method outperforms both traditional geometric twin and deep learning-based approaches in key performance metrics, including position accuracy, prediction stability, and robustness, which validates its efficacy and practical suitability for low-altitude airspace management tasks.Keywords: Digital twin, Low-altitude airspace, Computational analysis, Intelligent site selection, Conflict early warning, Trajectory analysisSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

With the rapid development of the low-altitude economy as a strategic emerging industry, the demand for safe, efficient, and intelligent management of low-altitude airspace has become increasingly urgent1,2. As a core enabler of high-value applications, such as drone operations, aerial logistics, and emergency response, low-altitude airspace faces several significant challenges in practice. First, the invisibility of airspace resources brings a critical challenge. Specifically, both static data (e.g., three-dimensional (3D) geographic information) and dynamic data (e.g., flight trajectories and meteorological conditions) are fragmented, making it challenging to ensure accurate and comprehensive situational awareness across the entire airspace. Recent regulatory research on Urban Air Mobility (UAM) in Europe has highlighted severe data silos in airspace information. Therefore, the construction of a unified digital information layer for collaborative awareness has become a key policy and technological development direction3. Second, difficulties in tracking flight status have posed another challenge for management. Namely, traditional air traffic control models rely on manual scheduling and struggle to perform real-time conflict detection and trajectory correction for large-scale drone swarms. To address this challenge, recent research has proposed a dynamic airspace configuration method4 that provides a technical pathway for real-time traffic flow allocation and conflict avoidance in high-density drone operations through algorithmic innovation. Third, imprecise management decisions affect operational efficiency. There is a lack of effective simulation and deduction tools, making it challenging to optimize site selection for takeoff and landing, route planning, and emergency resource allocation in complex scenarios. A recently introduced quantitative risk assessment framework that integrates digital twin (DT) technology provides an innovative method for simulating UAM operational risks in urban environments and optimizing safety decisions, enabling computable, predictable management5. However, the above limitations severely constrain the large-scale, high-quality development of the low-altitude economy. To address these limitations, recent research has proposed a system framework that provides a top-level solution for decision-making across multiple stages through integrated modeling and simulation, spanning from airspace design and traffic flow management to emergency response6. This further highlights an urgent need for leveraging innovative technologies to achieve computable, analyzable, and predictable intelligent management of low-altitude airspace.

Specifically, by establishing a high-fidelity real-time mapping between the physical world and the digital space, DT technology has emerged as a key technological pathway for addressing the multifaceted challenges in low-altitude airspace management7. Moreover, by integrating multi-source heterogeneous data, dynamically simulating physical behaviors, and enabling bidirectional interaction between virtual and real systems, this technology enables the comprehensive digital reconstruction of the operational environment in low-altitude airspace. In addition, it provides an integrated simulation and computing platform for the dynamic quantification of airspace resources, the real-time deduction of flight situations, and the continuous optimization of management decisions8. However, current research on DTs for low-altitude airspace faces significant limitations. First, most existing studies focus on optimizing isolated modules; for instance, in9,10, the authors concentrated on refined 3D terrain modeling and single UAV trajectory prediction, without designing an end-to-end computational analysis system that can synergistically couple multi-dimensional constraints (e.g., dynamic weather, complex terrain, and real-time no-fly zones) with multi-mission scenarios (e.g., large-scale logistics distribution, emergency medical rescue, and urban 3D patrols). Second, the integration depth between intelligent algorithms and twin models remains insufficient. Traditional methods often encounter bottlenecks in real-time performance, accuracy, and adaptability when handling highly dynamic and uncertain low-altitude operations11. It is also worth noting that recent research on DT applications in aviation has improved the adaptability and practicality of twin models through a feature-driven approach. For instance, the DT method for aero-engine rotor assembly planning based on macro-micro feature driving under weak correlation12 has achieved in-depth adaptation of the twin model to real-world scenarios by exploring the macro and micro features of core objects. This method provides a valuable reference for solving various problems in the field, such as multi-dimensional constraint fusion and accurate matching between models and scenarios in the research on DTs for low-altitude airspace.

With the recent incorporation of the low-altitude economy into the national strategic emerging industries, extensive systematic exploration of key issues, such as low-altitude airspace management and UAV collaborative applications, has been conducted. In13, a 3D dynamic twin framework that integrates real-time meteorological data and airspace structural information was proposed, significantly enhancing the adaptability and robustness of UAV path planning in complex urban environments by combining high-precision meteorological disturbance prediction and dynamic airspace modeling techniques. Reference14 introduced a collaborative DT platform for logistics UAV clusters, enabling real-time multi-drone conflict warning and dynamic trajectory replanning, and demonstrating the potential of DT technology to improve the efficiency and operational safety of cluster collaborative operations. Reference15 embedded reinforcement learning algorithms deeply into the DT system, establishing a perception-decision-learning closed-loop interaction mechanism. This enabled continuous autonomous learning from historical and real-time data, as well as dynamic optimization of airspace resource allocation, highlighting the cutting-edge trend of deep integration between intelligent algorithms and twin models. In16,17, the authors introduced swarm intelligence and multi-agent collaborative mechanisms into the twin environment, providing advanced insights for addressing the challenge of large-scale heterogeneous UAV collaborative scheduling. An integrated DT-based model for low-altitude traffic flow prediction and scheduling was proposed in18. By integrating deep learning and spatiotemporal sequence analysis techniques, the model achieves accurate prediction of urban low-altitude UAV traffic flow and dynamic route allocation. Its core innovation lies in establishing a real-time interaction channel between the physical airspace, digital mirror, and decision feedback, offering a technical advantage of improving scheduling efficiency by over 30% in a low-altitude logistics pilot project in Seoul. In19, a DT-based collaborative decision-making framework for heterogeneous UAV clusters was designed. By introducing a federated learning mechanism to address the conflict between multi-drone data privacy protection and collaborative model training, this framework enabled distributed, collaborative operations of over 100 UAVs in emergency rescue scenarios, effectively overcoming the scalability limitations of traditional centralized scheduling. Furthermore, in20, DT and edge computing technologies were combined to achieve a low-latency solution for airspace perception and decision-making. By deploying lightweight twin-agent nodes in UAV clusters, the data-processing latency for airspace information was reduced to less than 50 ms, providing critical technical support for real-time responses in highly dynamic low-altitude environments. This solution has been effectively applied to the low-altitude flight trial projects of the European Union Aviation Safety Agency (EASA).

However, despite significant progress in technical integration and scenario-specific adaptation, existing solutions face three core limitations in addressing the practical demands of large-scale, real-world low-altitude economic development. First, multi-source data fusion for full-domain airspace situational awareness remains inadequate. In particular, most research has focused on single-sensor inputs or local airspace data, lacking integrated modeling of multi-dimensional dynamic constraints, such as meteorological changes, terrain features, and electromagnetic interference. This gap affects both the environmental adaptability and prediction accuracy of DT models, preventing them from meeting the requirements of complex airspace operations. Second, the scalability and robustness of multi-agent collaborative decision-making require further enhancement. Existing coordination mechanisms have been primarily designed for small-scale, homogeneous UAV swarms. Therefore, when scaling to large, heterogeneous fleets with dynamic task allocation and real-time collision avoidance requirements, these systems generally suffer from decision latency or converge to suboptimal solutions. Third, closed-loop human-machine-environment autonomous optimization requires further development. Current systems predominantly rely on predefined rules or offline-trained models, lacking the capacity to dynamically perceive and adapt to real-time operational variables (e.g., human operator intents and unexpected environmental disturbances) during low-altitude missions. These limitations collectively hinder the transition of DT technology from laboratory prototypes to robust engineering applications and affect its ability to achieve high real-time responsiveness, precision, and reliability across operational scenarios, which is essential for evolving the low-altitude economy.

To address current research gaps, this study proposes a DT-driven computational and analytical framework for low-altitude airspace management. The core approach involves constructing a 1:1 high-fidelity DT of low-altitude airspace and integrating static geographic data, dynamic flight telemetry, and real-time meteorological and environmental information within a unified spatiotemporal framework. Moreover, intelligent algorithms, such as enhanced pigeon-inspired optimization (PIO) and deep reinforcement learning (DRL), are embedded to enable key functionalities, including airspace resource quantification, flight trajectory optimization, multi-agent conflict prediction, and emergency scenario simulation.

The main contributions of this study are threefold:

DT low-altitude airspace computing architecture design

To address the requirements for real-time performance and complex services in low-altitude airspace management, this study adopts DT technology to propose a systematic architecture and introduces the key technologies and cooperative mechanisms of each layer.

The DT-based computing and analysis system for low-altitude airspace is shown in Fig. 1. The adopted architecture has a four-layer progressive structure, consisting of a facility perception layer (FPL), a data fusion layer (DFL), a capability algorithm layer (CAL), and an application service layer (ASL). Each layer performs specific functions while maintaining close interaction with other layers, forming a complete technical support system from physical airspace perception to intelligent decision-making output.

DT-based computing and analysis system for low-altitude airspace.

As shown in Fig. 1, the FPL serves as the system’s data-acquisition front end and the basis for physical connections, collecting real-time low-altitude air situation information. This layer integrates multiple perception terminals related to low-altitude flight activities, including UAV telemetry devices, ground radar stations, meteorological monitoring sensors, and geographic information collection equipment. Through the coordinated work of multi-source heterogeneous equipment, various static geographic data (e.g., terrain features and restricted airspace boundaries), dynamic flight data (e.g., UAV position, speed, and attitude), and real-time environmental parameters (e.g., wind speed, visibility, and electromagnetic interference intensity) are collected. This provides high-fidelity, real-time raw data for constructing and updating the DT model.

The DFL serves as a hub that connects perception data with intelligent analysis. It processes multi-source, heterogeneous data collected by the FPL, performing data cleaning, format unification, spatiotemporal alignment, and redundancy elimination to reduce noise and inconsistencies. Thus, the layer adopts a technical framework integrating distributed data storage, real-time stream processing, and batch computing. With such a design, static and dynamic, as well as historical and real-time, data are organized and integrated, and a structured spatiotemporal data repository for low-altitude airspace is constructed. The repository provides standardized data support for the operation of upper-layer intelligent algorithms.

As the system’s core computing component, the CAL performs key computational tasks and supports intelligent analysis. Using standardized data from the DFL, this layer employs multiple intelligent approaches, including an improved pigeon-inspired optimization algorithm, a deep reinforcement learning model, a multi-step trajectory prediction algorithm, and a multi-constraint airspace resource evaluation model. In addition, through the coordinated operation of these models, functions such as quantitative evaluation of low-altitude airspace resources, flight trajectory optimization, early warning of multi-UAV conflicts, and emergency scenario simulation are enabled. Moreover, data resources are converted into decision-support capabilities to help intelligent analysis and dynamic monitoring of the whole process of low-altitude flight activities.

Finally, the ASL serves as an interaction interface among the system, users, and real-world application scenarios, supporting diverse business needs in low-altitude airspace management. It encapsulates the CAL’s calculation results and decision recommendations across multiple application services, including a visual management platform, a real-time early-warning interface, and a resource-scheduling terminal. These services support key business applications, such as takeoff and landing site planning, dynamic airspace capacity analysis, real-time flight conflict resolution, and emergency rescue route optimization. Furthermore, by providing intuitive, actionable technical support for managers, operators, and frontline practitioners in the low-altitude field, this layer bridges intelligent computing capabilities and real-world business demands.

GRU-Seq2Seq-driven error-compensated Kalman filter modeling

The GRU-Seq2Seq-driven, error-compensated Kalman filter model improves the CAL’s prediction accuracy and robustness. Specifically, by integrating high-precision prediction and real-time error correction, this model supports reliable decision-making for the upper ASL.

The proposed GRU-Seq2Seq-driven error-compensated Kalman filter model for the low-altitude airspace DTs comprises three key modules: a bidirectional GRU-Seq2Seq model, a verification metrics module, and an error-compensated Kalman filter model, forming a complete closed-loop optimization framework.

The bidirectional GRU-Seq2Seq model is responsible for the core function of time-series prediction. After the raw data are input into the system through the input layer, temporal features are extracted by the bidirectional GRU encoder layer. Then, the attention mechanism layer adaptively focuses on key information. Finally, the Seq2Seq decoder layer generates prediction results. Meanwhile, feedback from the verification metrics module is used as input for predictions to enable dynamic, iterative optimization of the model.

The verification metrics module performs model performance verification and error analysis. Upon receiving the prediction output from the bidirectional GRU-Seq2Seq model, quantitative error evaluation is performed through MAE and RMSE calculations. Following data processing, error analysis is conducted, predicted trajectories are generated, and error vectors are extracted. Finally, error information is fed back to the prediction model via the error-feedback link, providing the basis for error compensation in the subsequent module.

The error-compensated Kalman filter model is designed to correct states and improve accuracy. State estimation is performed using error data transmitted from the verification metrics module, followed by sequential operations: Kalman gain calculation, error value derivation, and error correction. The final compensated results are generated through state updates, and the corrected state information is transmitted in reverse to the verification metrics module to further optimize the accuracy of error evaluation. Simulation and validation of low-altitude airspace DT model

Experimental data and scenario design

The experiments were conducted on a hardware platform equipped with an Intel Core i7-12700 H CPU (2.7 GHz, 14 cores), an NVIDIA RTX 3060 GPU (6 GB VRAM), and 32 GB DDR4 RAM. The software environment included Python 3.8, PyTorch 1.12.0, and Ubuntu 20.04 LTS. To verify the proposed model’s engineering feasibility, this study quantified the end-to-end latency, including data transmission, model inference, and Kalman filter compensation, and analyzed its scalability across UAV scales. A high-precision timer with 1-µs resolution was used to measure latency and ensure data accuracy.

The experimental data consisted of simulated samples and real measured data, which jointly supported the rationality and coverage of experimental conditions. The geographic scope covered a 30 km × 20 km low-altitude airspace, where 1 m-resolution terrain elevation data was adopted for geographic modeling. Terrain slope and regional wind speed distribution were visualized through heat maps, as illustrated in Fig. 3. Terrain data were derived from the ASTER Global Digital Elevation Model V00328. Meanwhile, 72-hour continuous meteorological records, including wind field distribution, ambient temperature and visibility, were obtained from regional meteorological stations via a national meteorological data sharing platform.

In terms of meteorological conditions, 72-hour continuous measured data were adopted to reflect regional wind field features. The wind speed also shows prominent spatial differentiation without large-scale uniform wind areas. High wind zones (over 10 m/s, dark tones) are concentrated in the upper-middle, upper-right and partial central regions, whereas low wind zones (2–6 m/s, light tones) are mainly distributed in the lower-left, bottom and mid-lower areas.

Simulation flight data were generated in accordance with conventional mission specifications for low-altitude UAV operations. All trajectory samples were constructed as 10 Hz spatiotemporal sequences, including six core feature dimensions (latitude, longitude, altitude, roll angle, pitch angle and yaw angle) and four auxiliary dimensions (flight speed, acceleration, ambient wind speed and visibility). Each trajectory sequence was set to 100 time steps (10 s) in total. The first 70 steps were used for model input during training and inference, and the remaining 30 steps were adopted for quantitative evaluation of trajectory prediction performance. Combining UAV flight dynamics equations with local terrain and wind field data, simulation trajectories were generated to approximate actual low-altitude flight features. A total of 50 UAVs were configured for simulated tasks, covering reconnaissance, logistics transportation and border patrol missions. Actual PTP time deviation (± 5 ms) was introduced to replace ideal synchronization, so as to explore model performance and adaptive adjustment strategies under asynchronous conditions. Combined with conventional and interference-containing scenarios, the model’s anti-interference potential and error compensation ability in complex low-altitude environments were assessed comprehensively.

Model accuracy verification results

To comprehensively evaluate the proposed model, this study conducted experiments from multiple perspectives, including spatial mapping accuracy, trajectory prediction, robustness under extreme weather, and stability under GPS denial and communication interruption.

In the single-target conventional flight scenario, the proposed DT model was evaluated and compared with the traditional geometric twin in terms of the spatial mapping accuracy, as presented in Fig. 5. All experimental results were averaged over 30 independent runs with different random data splits to ensure stability. Moreover, 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported for all performance improvements. In addition, two-tailed t-tests were performed, and differences with ρ < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Comparison of spatial mapping position errors.

The spatial mapping position errors of the three models across different altitude layers are presented in Fig. 5. A clear hierarchical difference in position error was observed among the three models. The lowest position error at all tested altitudes (100 m, 300 m, and 500 m) and in the average scenario was achieved by the proposed DT model. The deep learning model exhibited intermediate error levels, while the traditional geometric twin model consistently achieved the highest error, with distinct performance boundaries among the three approaches.

Specifically, the average position error of the proposed model was only 0.87 m, representing reductions of approximately 51.1% (95% CI [48.3%, 53.9%]) and 72.9% (95% CI [69.8%, 75.7%]) compared to the deep learning model (1.78 m) and the traditional geometric twin model (3.21 m), respectively. Two-tailed t-tests showed that the proposed model’s improvements were statistically significant (ρ < 0.05), demonstrating its considerable performance advantages.

To intuitively reflect the model’s spatial mapping and trajectory fitting performance in actual flight missions, a typical single-target routine flight case was selected for visual verification. The detailed trajectory comparison and real-time position error curve are illustrated in Fig. 6.

Predicted vs. actual trajectory with real-time position error.

As shown in Fig. 6, the predicted trajectory maintains a satisfactory fitting effect with the actual flight path. The real-time position error remains stable at a low level throughout the whole process, which further intuitively verifies that the proposed model can achieve stable and high-precision spatial mapping in conventional low-altitude flight scenarios.

The deep learning model achieved intermediate performance, with an average position error of 1.78 m, representing an improvement of approximately 44.5% over the traditional geometric twin model, while still exhibiting significantly higher error than the proposed DT method. Regarding altitude adaptability, a gradual increase in error was observed for the deep learning model as altitude increased, from 1.56 m at 100 m to 2.01 m at 500 m, representing an overall increase of approximately 28.8%. This favorable performance could be attributed to the data-driven mechanism’s ability to independently extract airspace features, thereby effectively alleviating the drawbacks of static modeling used in traditional geometric twin models. Nevertheless, due to the limited generalization ability of purely data-driven schemes, positioning accuracy was less stable under complex high-altitude conditions compared with the DT model, which fully incorporated physical airspace constraints. In contrast, due to the inherent limitations of static geometric modeling, errors exceeding 2.8 m were recorded for the traditional geometric twin model at all altitudes, peaking at 3.66 m, indicating that the precision requirements for spatial mapping in complex low-altitude airspace environments were not met. For the proposed model, only a mild increase in position error with altitude was observed, from 0.72 m at 100 m to 1.04 m at 500 m, with an increase of merely 44.4%, and high and consistent mapping accuracy was maintained across all altitude layers. The superior performance and strong robustness of the proposed method for spatial mapping tasks in low-altitude airspace were therefore fully validated.

Trajectory prediction is a core extended task that predicts the future flight paths of airspace targets based on their historical positions and motion state data. The aircraft trajectory prediction errors confirmed the technical superiority of the proposed DT model, as presented in Fig. 7.

Trajectory prediction error curves of different models.

The trajectory prediction errors of the proposed model, the Transformer model, the ST-GCN model, the unidirectional LSTM model, and the traditional geometric twin model were compared for prediction horizons of 5 s, 10 s, and 15 s, as illustrated in Fig. 7. A clear performance hierarchy was observed throughout the prediction period. The proposed DT-based bidirectional GRU-Seq2Seq model achieved the lowest prediction error at all time instants, reaching 1.23 m at 5 s, 2.78 m at 10 s, and 3.87 m at 15 s. As typical advanced time-series prediction networks, the Transformer and ST-GCN models demonstrated competitive performance, with errors of 1.52 m/3.08 m/4.26 m and 1.66 m/3.31 m/4.60 m at the three time points (5 s, 10 s, and 15 s), respectively. Statistical tests confirmed that the proposed model achieved significantly lower error than the Transformer (ρ = 0.023) and ST-GCN (ρ = 0.031) models.

The unidirectional LSTM model had moderate performance with errors of 1.82 m, 3.65 m, and 5.21 m, while the traditional geometric twin model exhibited the highest errors, reaching 2.15 m, 4.56 m, and 6.52 m at different horizons. In addition, the error gap between the proposed model and the other methods gradually widened with prediction time, reflecting the outstanding long-term prediction stability of the proposed DT-based bidirectional GRU-Seq2Seq model.

Regarding the trend in prediction errors, all models exhibited increasing errors as the prediction horizon extended, but their growth rates differed significantly. The proposed DT model exhibited the gentlest error growth, with a total increase of 214.6% from 5 s to 15 s, effectively suppressing error accumulation in long-term prediction. This benefit stemmed from the deep integration of physical airspace constraints, real-time environmental data, and dynamic trajectory modeling, which helped correct the static deviation of traditional geometric models and alleviated the long-term generalization limitations of purely data-driven methods.

The Transformer and ST-GCN models surpassed the unidirectional LSTM model by employing advanced sequence modeling architectures, but their errors still increased noticeably faster than that of the proposed DT model after the 10-s horizon. Although the unidirectional LSTM model outperformed the traditional twin model, it was prone to error accumulation during long-sequence prediction, resulting in relatively poor long-term accuracy stability. Constrained by the static modeling framework, the traditional geometric twin model could not accurately capture the dynamic changes in UAV trajectories and consistently exhibited the highest error level.

Overall, the proposed DT-based bidirectional GRU-Seq2Seq model achieved more accurate and stable trajectory predictions across varying prediction horizons, which verified its effectiveness and robustness in UAV trajectory prediction in low-altitude airspace.

Although the DT model’s spatial mapping and trajectory prediction errors were low, the error-compensation link was crucial to improving model accuracy in practical applications. The errors of different models before and after error compensation are presented in Fig. 10, illustrating the error-correction capability of each model.

Prediction errors of different models before and after error compensation.

Figure 10 shows the trajectory prediction errors of the three models after Kalman filter compensation for prediction durations of 5 s, 10 s, and 15 s. Overall, the three models had stable hierarchical performance. The lowest post-compensation errors were consistently achieved by the proposed DT model, reaching 0.85 m, 1.92 m, and 2.71 m at 5 s, 10 s, and 15 s, respectively; the deep learning model had intermediate performance, with errors of 1.35 m, 2.68 m, and 3.85 m; finally, the highest errors were consistently demonstrated by the traditional geometric twin model, reaching 1.62 m, 3.35 m, and 4.82 m at 5 s, 10 s, and 15 s, respectively. In terms of error growth trends, the post-compensation errors of the three models increased with prediction duration, but distinct differences in their growth rates were observed. The proposed DT model had the slightest error growth slope, with an overall increase of 218.8% from 5 s to 15 s. This benefit stemmed from the deep synergy between the DT architecture and Kalman filtering. Meanwhile, a high-precision prediction foundation that integrated physical airspace constraints and real-time environmental data, combined with real-time correction of dynamic errors via filtering, effectively suppressed error accumulation over long prediction cycles, ensuring stable, high-precision output throughout the entire prediction period. In contrast, the deep learning model’s error increased from 1.35 m at 5 s to 3.85 m at 15 s, showing an overall increase of 185.2%. Due to its purely data-driven structure, this model remained susceptible to error accumulation in long-sequence prediction, yielding inferior long-term accuracy stability compared with the proposed DT model despite Kalman filter compensation. Compared with the two approaches above, the traditional geometric twin model was constrained by inherent defects in its static modeling framework. Even after Kalman filter compensation, the error increased substantially from 1.62 m at 5 s to 4.82 m at 15 s, representing an overall increase of 197.5%, and the highest error level was maintained at all time points. This indicated that this model could not effectively meet the requirements of long-term trajectory prediction in complex low-altitude airspace environments. Consequently, the results demonstrated the significant advantages of the proposed joint compensation strategy in improving prediction accuracy and suppressing error accumulation.

Ablation study

To further verify the independent contributions of each core component of the proposed model and clarify the source of performance improvement, this study conducted a systematic ablation study by removing individual modules one by one while retaining the others. The influence of three key modules on the result was evaluated: the four-layer DT architecture, the bidirectional GRU-Seq2Seq trajectory prediction model, and the Kalman filter-based error compensation module. The average trajectory prediction error within the 15-s interval was used as an evaluation metric, with a lower value representing better performance.

The proposed four-layer DT architecture incorporates a progressive framework composed of the FPL, DFL, CAL, and ASL. Removing a module meant abandoning the structured data flow, the multi-source data integration mechanism, and the physical airspace constraints imposed by the twin architecture, while retaining only the prediction model for trajectory forecasting. To further clarify the individual contributions of the key modules, this study quantified each module’s performance improvement via ablation experiments, as illustrated in Fig. 11.

Ablation study of the proposed model’s key modules.

As shown in Fig. 11, the absence of any key module caused a significant increase in prediction error. Specifically, after removing the four-layer DT architecture, the average prediction error increased from 3.87 m to 5.13 m. When the GRU-Seq2Seq module was replaced with a traditional unidirectional LSTM, the error increased to 5.21 m. After excluding Kalman filter compensation, the error reached 4.69 m. In contrast, the complete framework integrating all three modules achieved the lowest error of 3.87 m.

The results demonstrate that the performance improvement of the proposed method depends not solely on deep learning models but primarily on the four-layer DT architecture and the reasonable integration of the trajectory prediction and error compensation modules. The DT framework incorporates physical airspace constraints and real-time environmental information, which help improve prediction stability and alleviate long-term error accumulation. Its advantages stem from the systematic integration of architectural design and data-driven modules, rather than the individual effect of a single deep learning component.

Conclusion

To address the management challenges in low-altitude airspace, this study proposes a DT-based computational and analytical framework that adopts a four-layer technical architecture and multi-source data fusion strategies. In the proposed framework, the bidirectional GRU-Seq2Seq trajectory prediction model and the Kalman filter-based error compensation mechanism denote key modules. Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed method outperforms traditional geometric twin methods and typical deep learning models in prediction accuracy, real-time performance, and operational stability, providing technical support for the safe and orderly development of the low-altitude economy.

However, the proposed model has some limitations, including insufficient adaptability to highly dynamic dense airspace conditions and limited real-time performance for ultra-large-scale input data. Future research could focus on optimizing the collaborative scheduling algorithm for large-scale, heterogeneous drone clusters, strengthening the integration of DT with edge computing and federated learning, and improving the proposed model’s generalization and real-time response in complex and extreme environments.
