Comparing the Effect of Semi‐Immersive Virtual Reality, Computerized Cognitive Training, and Traditional Rehabilitation on Cognitive Function in Multiple Sclerosis: A Randomized Clinical Trial
Authors: Maria Grazia Maggio, Desiree Latella, Lilla Bonanno, Paulo Cezar Rocha dos Santos, Marcos Maldonado‐Díaz, Meir Plotnik, Rosaria De Luca, Angelo Quartarone, Rocco Salvatore Calabrò
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundCognitive impairment is a common non‐motor symptom in Multiple Sclerosis (MS), negatively affecting autonomy and Quality of Life (QoL). Innovative rehabilitation strategies, such as semi‐immersive virtual reality (VR) and computerized cognitive training (CCT), may offer advantages over traditional cognitive rehabilitation (TCR), particularly in terms of engagement, specificity, adaptability, and ecological validity. This study aimed to compare the efficacy of semi‐immersive VR, CCT using the ERICA platform, and TCR on cognitive outcomes and depressive/anxiety symptoms in individuals with MS.MethodsEighty‐seven patients with MS and mild to moderate cognitive impairment were randomly assigned to three groups: VR‐based training, CCT, or TCR. Each group underwent 24 individual sessions delivered regularly three times per week over 8 weeks. Neuropsychological assessments were conducted at baseline (T0) and post‐intervention (T1), evaluating information processing speed (SDMT), memory (SRT, SPART), executive functions (WLG), emotional status (BDI, HRS‐A), and QoL (MSQOL‐54).ResultsAll groups showed significant improvements in most cognitive and depressive (BDI)/anxiety symptoms (HRS‐A) after the intervention. However, the VR group demonstrated significantly greater gains in executive functions, working memory, and QoL (MSQOL‐54 Physical and Mental Composite scores) compared to both the CCT and TCR groups (p < 0.001). While the CCT group showed selective improvements in mood and working memory, its overall efficacy did not significantly differ from that of TCR.ConclusionsSemi‐immersive VR represents an effective and engaging approach to cognitive rehabilitation in MS, likely due to its capacity to deliver multisensory, adaptive, and ecologically valid stimulation. Nonetheless, a tailored hybrid model integrating VR with conventional and computer‐based strategies may further optimize outcomes and support functional independence in individuals with MS.

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) affects both physical and cognitive functioning. Indeed, up to 70% of individuals with MS experience cognitive deficits, which may include deficits in attention, processing speed, executive function, and memory [1]. These cognitive changes may occur early in the course of the disease and often progress in parallel with motor symptoms, with growing evidence indicating a reciprocal relationship between cognitive decline and physical disability [2]. The interaction between cognitive and motor dysfunction has important implications for rehabilitation, as cognitive deficits may interfere with motor learning, therapeutic adherence, and functional recovery [3]. Consequently, there is growing interest in designing integrated interventions that address both domains. Our study is grounded in an experience‐dependent neuroplasticity framework, whereby rehabilitation gains are maximized when training is sufficiently intense and repetitive, task‐oriented, and supported by salient, timely feedback and active engagement. Within this framework, semi‐immersive VR may operationalize these principles more effectively by integrating multisensory input, embodied interaction, and real‐time performance feedback. Such features may enhance attentional allocation and information processing speed through increased activation of frontoparietal networks, which are frequently compromised in MS. Moreover, the combination of cognitive demands with goal‐directed motor actions may strengthen executive functions and working memory by promoting functional coupling between prefrontal and sensorimotor circuits. The simulation of ecologically valid, real‐life scenarios may further support cognitive flexibility and divided attention, facilitating transfer to everyday functioning. Semi‐immersive VR is expected to implement these principles more effectively than CCT or traditional approaches by coupling cognitive demands with goal‐directed actions in an enriched, multisensory context, potentially increasing the effective training dose and facilitating transfer to everyday functioning. In MS, this rationale aligns with cognitive reserve models, suggesting that enriched and motivating activities may partially buffer disease‐related cognitive vulnerability. Moreover, motivational factors (e.g., autonomy, competence, and enjoyment) may be more strongly elicited by interactive VR environments than by repetitive drill‐based tasks, potentially improving adherence. Accordingly, we hypothesize that semi‐immersive VR will yield larger cognitive gains than CCT or TCR, particularly in attention and information processing speed.

In recent years, cognitive rehabilitation has evolved significantly with the integration of advanced technologies such as VR and CCT [1, 4, 5]. These approaches are employed in clinical settings to address cognitive impairments resulting from conditions such as stroke, traumatic brain injury, and neurodegenerative diseases [6]. Compared to traditional paper‐and‐pencil methods, VR and CCT offer enhanced ecological validity, adaptability, and engagement, which may result in improved functional outcomes [7]. In this context, ecological validity refers to the use of interactive, goal‐directed tasks embedded in simulated real‐world scenarios requiring simultaneous motor and cognitive engagement, thus approximating everyday functional demands.

Enhanced levels of engagement in VR are likely explained by the involvement of higher cortical control, such as the prefrontal cortex [8]. Not surprisingly, studies have highlighted VR effectiveness in enhancing executive function, attention, memory, and visuospatial abilities in different neurological patients, such as those affected by stroke [8, 9]. Similarly, VR interventions have shown promise in enhancing cognitive functions in individuals with MCI and dementia, suggesting moderate to large improvements in global cognition, attention, memory, and motor performance [10]. However, limitations of these tools include high costs, technical complexity, and potential for cybersickness or sensory overload, especially in older adults or patients with severe impairments.

In terms of CCT, this modality consists of structured cognitive exercises administered through software platforms. CCT is highly accessible, can be used remotely, and often provides automated feedback and performance tracking [11]. It supports repeated, intensive training of specific cognitive domains, with evidence showing significant improvements in working memory, attention, and global cognition [11]. Nevertheless, a primary limitation is its limited ecological validity, as tasks are often abstract and less representative of real‐world activities. Additionally, long‐term engagement may decrease due to the repetitive and less immersive nature of many programs [12, 13].

While traditional rehabilitation remains fundamental in treating cognitive deficits, integrating technologies like VR and CCT offers promising enhancements [14]. Comparative studies suggest that these technological approaches provide additional advantages, such as increased patient motivation and the ability to precisely monitor progress [4, 15, 16]. Notably, VR‐based rehabilitation has been shown to promote neuroplastic changes even in the chronic phase of ischemic stroke, indicating its potential effectiveness in long‐term recovery [9].

Although several rehabilitation approaches have shown positive effects on cognitive outcomes in individuals with MS, direct comparisons between different modalities remain limited. This gap in the literature hinders the ability to identify the most effective strategies to address cognitive dysfunction in MS. Given the heterogeneous nature of cognitive impairment in this population and the fragmentation of evidence between different intervention types, comparative studies are essential to guide clinical decision‐making and optimize personalized rehabilitation programs.

Therefore, the present study aims to compare the efficacy of three distinct rehabilitation approaches: virtual reality‐based semi‐immersive rehabilitation, CCT, and TCR. This comparison aims to identify which modality may be more effective in improving specific cognitive functions in MS patients undergoing rehabilitation. Although we hypothesized that semi‐immersive VR would yield larger cognitive gains, the primary objective was to compare the relative effectiveness of three active interventions rather than to conduct a formal superiority trial.

Material and Methods

Study Population and Study Design

This study represents the first phase of a broader randomized controlled trial (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT06838975) aimed at evaluating the effects of different rehabilitation modalities on cognitive impairment across distinct neurological populations. The present study specifically focused on individuals with mild‐to‐moderate cognitive impairment due to MS and compared the efficacy of three rehabilitation approaches: semi‐immersive VR‐based rehabilitation (Nirvana system), computerized cognitive training (CCT), and traditional cognitive rehabilitation (TCR).

Participants were recruited at IRCCS Centro Neurolesi “Bonino‐Pulejo” between January and May 2025. Given the retrospective extraction of baseline clinical information from routine records, disease duration (years) and detailed disease‐modifying therapy (DMT) exposure were not consistently available for all participants; therefore, these variables were not included in baseline comparisons or covariate‐adjusted analyses (see Limitations). Participants were assigned in a 1:1:1 ratio to VR‐based rehabilitation (n = 29), CCT (n = 29), or TCR (n = 29) using a computer‐generated block randomization sequence created through the Sealed Envelope online randomization service (https://www.sealedenvelope.com). Allocation concealment was ensured by the electronic system, which generated group assignments only after participant enrolment and baseline assessment, preventing foreknowledge of treatment allocation. Outcome assessments were conducted by evaluators blinded to group assignment.

The required sample size was determined through an a priori power analysis conducted using G*Power 3.1. The analysis was based on a repeated‐measures ANOVA with a within–between interaction (Group × Time), assuming three independent groups (VR, CCT, TCR) and two measurement time points (baseline and post‐intervention). The parameters used were: medium effect size (f = 0.20), alpha level = 0.05, statistical power = 0.90, correlation among repeated measures = 0.5, and no sphericity correction ε = 1. Under these assumptions, the minimum sample size required to detect statistically significant interaction effects was 72 participants (24 per group). To account for a potential dropout rate of approximately 21%, the final target sample size was increased to 87 participants (29 per group), ensuring sufficient power and balanced group allocation. All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrolment. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the IRCCS Centro Neurolesi “Bonino‐Pulejo” (approval no. 19/2024) and conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

This investigation constitutes the first part of a larger trial. A subsequent phase, not included in the present manuscript, will involve different patient populations and alternative technologies, including immersive VR and advanced computerized platforms.

Eligibility Criteria

The inclusion criteria were: (i) a diagnosis of relapsing–remitting multiple sclerosis (RRMS) according to the 2017 Revision of McDonald criteria [17]; (ii) a stable pharmacological treatment for at least 6 months; (iii) mild‐to‐moderate cognitive impairment at baseline as defined by a Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) screening score between 20 and 25, adjusted for education according to standard MoCA scoring procedures; (iv) and the absence of severe medical or psychiatric conditions interfering with the VR training. MoCA was administered exclusively as an eligibility screening tool and was not considered an outcome measure. This threshold was adopted to exclude individuals with severe impairment, who may be unable to adequately engage in rehabilitation tasks and comply with protocol requirements.

Exclusion criteria were: (i) age < 18 years or > 75, (ii) presence of severe medical conditions likely to interfere with participation and/or cognitive performance, (iii) severe psychiatric disorders according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 5th Edition (DSM‐V) [18] including psychotic disorders and bipolar disorder, or any psychiatric condition requiring acute treatment/hospitalization or judged by the study clinicians to compromise adherence and safety, (iv) MS clinical and/or neuroradiological relapse in the 6 months before enrolment; (iv) Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) > 7.

Alcohol/substance use disorders and recent misuse, as well as psychotropic medication stability, were not systematically assessed and are therefore acknowledged as potential confounders (see Limitations).

Intervention Protocol

For each of the three proposed rehabilitation strategies (VR, CCT, TCR), the intervention consisted of 24 individual sessions, administered regularly three times per week over 8 weeks, with each session lasting approximately 60 min. All sessions were delivered one‐to‐one in a controlled clinical setting by trained clinicians with expertise in neurorehabilitation (neuropsychologists/therapists routinely involved in MS cognitive rehabilitation). Task selection and progression were individualized based on baseline cognitive profile and ongoing performance. For the VR and CCT arms, difficulty progression was additionally supported by the platforms' built‐in adaptive features, with clinician oversight to adjust task type and difficulty when needed. During the study period, participants did not engage in any additional structured cognitive rehabilitation programs or psychological treatments. All patients received exclusively the assigned intervention while maintaining stable pharmacological therapy. The VR‐based rehabilitation was conducted using the BTS‐Nirvana system, a semi‐immersive virtual reality system that provides cognitive stimulation through real‐time movement tracking and interaction with virtual objects. The system includes cognitive exercises targeting executive functions, visuospatial navigation, and memory, with adaptive difficulty levels tailored to each patient's performance. VR training was performed in a controlled clinical setting under therapist supervision.

The CCT was delivered using the ERICA platform, a clinician‐guided, computer‐assisted cognitive rehabilitation system designed for structured training of attention, memory, executive functions, and visuospatial processing. The platform includes standardized cognitive exercises organized into modules targeting specific domains and increasing levels of difficulty. Tasks comprised word retrieval and lexical access exercises, category‐based naming, numerical ordering, working memory tasks, and dual‐task paradigms. Exercise difficulty was adaptively adjusted according to the patient's performance, with task levels increasing or decreasing based on the number of errors and execution time. This adaptive mechanism allowed individualized training while preserving a standardized intervention structure. Each session was supervised by a trained clinician, who selected and monitored the exercises in accordance with the rehabilitation protocol. Visual and auditory feedback was provided in real time to reinforce performance, sustain attention, and enhance engagement throughout the training (Figure 2).

Interface of the ERICA computerized cognitive training platform. The system includes domain‐specific modules and adaptive exercises whose difficulty is adjusted according to execution time and number of errors, under clinician supervision.

Conceptually, the ERICA platform is based on structured, domain‐specific cognitive stimulation delivered through screen‐based exercises targeting discrete cognitive functions (e.g., attention, working memory, lexical retrieval). Training is primarily drill‐based and focuses on repetition and graded task difficulty within specific cognitive domains [19]. In contrast, the semi‐immersive VR intervention is grounded in principles of embodied cognition and ecological validity, integrating cognitive demands with real‐time motor interaction in a multisensory environment. Practically, ERICA relies on two‐dimensional screen interaction without sensorimotor engagement, whereas the VR system involves body movements, spatial exploration, and dual‐task scenarios that simultaneously activate cognitive and motor networks. These conceptual and operational differences reflect distinct rehabilitation models: domain‐specific CCT versus integrated cognitive‐motor stimulation within an ecologically enriched context.

Participants in the TCR group received therapist‐led, paper‐and‐pencil cognitive training, including structured exercises on memory recall, problem‐solving, and language tasks. This conventional approach followed standard neurorehabilitation guidelines and was conducted in individual sessions with a trained neuropsychologist [20].

Treatment Fidelity

All interventions were delivered individually by trained clinicians following standardized procedures routinely adopted in the Neurorehabilitation Unit. Session attendance and completion (number of sessions delivered) were recorded for all participants to ensure that the planned dose (24 sessions) was implemented. Although adherence was not a primary outcome, it was generally excellent, with the majority of participants attending all scheduled sessions and no dropouts due to lack of motivation or other adherence‐related issues. All statistical analyses were conducted according to an intention‐to‐treat (ITT) principle, including all randomized participants in the models regardless of session attendance.

Outcome Measures

Neuropsychological assessment was conducted at baseline (T0) and post‐intervention (T1) by trained evaluators blinded to group allocation. The Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) was administered at baseline exclusively as an eligibility screening tool to identify patients with mild‐to‐moderate cognitive impairment and was not included among the longitudinal outcome measures. Cognitive outcomes were assessed using the Brief Repeatable Battery of Neuropsychological Tests (BRB‐N).

Primary Cognitive Outcome

The primary outcome measure was the change from T0 to T1 in information processing speed/attention as measured by the Symbol Digit Modalities Test (SDMT).

Secondary Cognitive Outcomes

Secondary outcomes included measures included changes from T0 to T1 in verbal memory, which was assessed using the Selective Reminding Test, including indices of long‐term storage (SRT‐LTS), consistent long‐term retrieval (SRT‐CLTR), and delayed recall (SRT‐D). Visuospatial memory was evaluated with the 10/36 Spatial Recall Test and its delayed recall version (SPART and SPART‐D). Attention and information processing were measured through the Paced Auditory Serial Addition Test at both 3‐ and 2‐s intervals (PASAT 3 and PASAT 2). Executive functioning was examined via the Word List Generation (WLG) task.

Secondary Non‐Cognitive Outcomes

Secondary non‐cognitive outcomes included affective symptoms and Quality of Life (QoL). Affective status was assessed using the Hamilton Rating Scale for Anxiety (HRS‐A) [21] for anxiety symptoms, and the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) [22] for depressive symptoms. Finally, QoL was evaluated using the Multiple Sclerosis QoL‐54 questionnaire [23], including both the Physical and Mental Composite scores (MSQOL‐54 PC and MSQOL‐54 MC). Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were computed for all demographic and clinical variables. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of data distributions. Intra‐group comparisons (T0 vs. T1) were performed using the paired Student's t‐test for normally distributed variables or the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test for non‐parametric data. For all intra‐group comparisons, effect sizes were calculated using Cohen's d (for parametric tests) or matched rank biserial correlation (for non‐parametric tests) and interpreted according to conventional thresholds: small (0.21–0.50), medium (0.51–0.79), and large (> 0.79). A negative effect size indicates that scores at T1 were lower than at T0, which may reflect a positive clinical change when lower scores denote improvement. Baseline comparability across the three treatment arms (VR‐based rehabilitation, CCT, and TCR) was examined using one‐way ANOVA and, as a distribution‐robust confirmatory approach, Kruskal–Wallis tests. Given the presence of baseline imbalances in several clinical and cognitive measures, post‐intervention group effects were primarily evaluated using covariate‐adjusted ANCOVA models with T1 as the dependent variable:

T1 ~ Group + T0 + Age + Education (+ Sex/Gender when available).

Estimated marginal means were obtained for Group and tested with prespecified planned contrasts (VR–TCR, CCT–TCR, and VR–CCT), reporting adjusted mean differences, 95% confidence intervals, and p‐values. The primary endpoint was the Symbol Digit Modalities Test (SDMT); secondary endpoints included cognitive outcomes (PASAT‐3, PASAT‐2, SPART, SPART‐D, SRT‐CLTR, SRT‐D, WLG), affective outcomes (BDI, HRS‐A), and QoL (MSQOL‐54 Physical and Mental). Given baseline differences in affective measures, a sensitivity ANCOVA was performed for all non‐affective outcomes by additionally adjusting for baseline depression and anxiety (BDI_T0 and HRS‐A_T0) to test whether cognitive and quality‐of‐life findings were independent of affective baseline status. To control multiplicity, family‐wise Bonferroni correction was applied within outcome domains (cognitive secondary outcomes: m = 7; affective outcomes: m = 2; QoL: m = 2) using p_bonf = min (p × m, 1). The primary endpoint was interpreted at nominal α = 0.05 and presented separately from family‐wise correction. Finally, to quantify longitudinal effects and directly test whether change over time differed across interventions, two‐way repeated‐measures ANOVA models were conducted for each outcome with Time (T0 vs. T1) as within‐subject factor and Group (VR, CCT, TCR) as between‐subject factor, focusing on main effects of Time and the Time × Group interaction. For interpretability of change direction, oriented change scores (Δ_oriented) were also computed so that positive values consistently indicated improvement across all outcomes (T1−T0 for outcomes where higher scores indicate better functioning; T0−T1 for outcomes where lower scores indicate better functioning, such as BDI and HRS‐A). All analyses were performed in R (version 4.2.2), using two‐sided tests, α = 0.05, and 95% confidence intervals.

Intra‐Group Analysis

Intra‐group comparisons between T0 and T1 were conducted for each group (VR‐based Group, CCT, and TRC) across a range of neuropsychological and QoL (Table 4). In the VR‐based Group, all variables showed statistically significant improvements (p < 0.001, d range = −1.20–1.31). Similarly, the CCT group showed significant differences between T0 and T1 in nearly all measures, BDI (p < 0.001, r = 1.0), HRS‐A (p < 0.001, r = 1.0), SPART (p < 0.001, d = −0.86), SPART‐D (p < 0.001, d = −0.72), SRT‐LTS (p < 0.001, d = −0.84), SRT‐CLTR (p = 0.04, r = −1.0), SDMT (p = 0.001, d = −0.66), PASAT‐3 (p = 0.01, r = −0.72), PASAT‐2 (p = 0.03, d = −0.42), SRT‐D (p = 0.002, d = −0.64), WLG (p < 0.001, d = −0.81), and both physical (p < 0.001, r = −0.90) and mental components of MSQOL‐54 (p < 0.001, and d = −1.52). For the TCR group showed a more selective pattern of improvement. Significant intra‐group differences were observed for BDI (p < 0.001, r = 1.0), HRS‐A (p < 0.001, r = 1.0), SPART‐D (p < 0.001, d = 1.87), SRT‐LTS (p < 0.001, d = −0.81), SRT‐CLTR (p = 0.04, d = −0.39), and both physical (p < 0.001, r = −1.01) and mental components of MSQOL‐54 (p < 0.001, r = −1.0). No significant differences were found for SPART (p = 0.28), SDMT (p = 0.12), PASAT‐3 (p = 0.15), PASAT‐2 (p = 0.33), SRT‐D (p = 0.49), and WLG (p = 0.3).

Baseline and Post‐Intervention Group Comparisons

An inter‐group analysis was conducted on 29 participants in the VR‐based Group, 29 in the CCT group, and 29 TCR (Table 3). At baseline (T0), one‐way ANOVA revealed significant between‐group differences in several cognitive domains, including SPART‐D (p < 0.001), SRT‐LTS (p = 0.02), SRT‐D (p < 0.001), and WLG (p < 0.001). Post hoc Tukey tests showed that SPART‐D scores differed significantly across all comparisons, with the VR‐based group outperforming both the CCT group (p = 0.001, d = 1.78, 95% CI [1.16, 2.38]) and the TCR group (p < 0.001, d = 2.44, 95% CI [1.75, 3.12]), and the CCT group performing better than the TCR group (p < 0.001, d = 1.47, 95% CI [0.88, 2.05]). Significant differences were also found in SRT‐LTS scores, with both the VR‐based group (p = 0.04, d = −0.72, 95% CI [−1.25, −0.19]) and the CCT group (p = 0.04, d = −0.64, 95% CI [−1.17, −0.11]) performing better than the TCR group. Regarding SRT‐D, scores were markedly lower in the TCR group compared to both the CCT group (p = 0.001, d = 2.73, 95% CI [2.00, 3.45]) and the VR‐based group (p < 0.001, d = 2.76, 95% CI [2.03, 3.47]), suggesting poorer delayed recall performance. WLG scores followed a similar trend, with the TCR group performing significantly worse than both the CCT group (p < 0.001, d = 3.55, 95% CI [2.71, 4.37]) and the VR‐based group (p < 0.001, d = 3.46, 95% CI [2.63, 4.28]).

Non‐parametric Kruskal–Wallis tests were conducted to account for non‐normal distributions. At T0, significant group differences emerged for BDI (p < 0.001), HRS‐A (p < 0.001), PASAT‐2 (p = 0.002), and the MSQOL‐54 Mental Component (p < 0.001). Dunn's post hoc tests revealed that BDI scores were significantly higher in the VR‐based group than both the TCR (p < 0.001, r = 0.93, 95% CI [0.87, 0.96]) and CCT groups (p < 0.001, r = 0.95, 95% CI [0.92, 0.97]), indicating greater baseline depressive symptoms in the VR group. HRS‐A scores were also higher in the CCT versus TCR (p < 0.001, r = −1.00, 95% CI [−1.00, −1.00]) and in the VR‐based group versus TCR (p < 0.001, r = 0.72, 95% CI [0.55, 0.84]). PASAT‐2 scores were significantly higher in both the CCT (p = 0.02, r = −0.40, 95% CI [−0.62, −0.12]) and VR‐based group (p = 0.003, r = 0.52, 95% CI [0.27, 0.70]) compared to TCR. Mental health‐related QoL (MSQOL‐54 Mental) was significantly higher in the VR and CCT groups than in TCR (all p < 0.001), with large effect sizes (r range = −1.00–1.00).

At T1, At T1, group differences were observed. ANOVA showed significant differences in HRS‐A (p < 0.001), SPART (p = 0.001), SPART‐D (p < 0.001), SRT‐LTS (p < 0.001), SRT‐D (p < 0.001), and WLG (p < 0.001). Specifically, for HRS‐A, scores were significantly lower, better, in both the CCT (p < 0.001, d = −4.32, 95% CI [−5.26, −3.36]) and VR‐based groups (p < 0.001, d = −1.23, 95% CI [−1.78, −0.66]) compared to TCR, and lower in the CCT versus VR‐based group (p = 0.009, d = 0.67, 95% CI [0.14, 1.20]). For SPART, both the VR‐based versus TCR (p = 0.001, d = −0.93, 95% CI [−1.47, −0.39]) and VR‐based versus CCT (p = 0.02, d = −0.69, 95% CI [−1.22, −0.16]) comparisons favored the VR group at post‐intervention. Similarly, SPART‐D, SRT‐D, and WLG scores were higher at post‐intervention in the VR‐based and CCT groups than TCR (all p < 0.001), with effect sizes consistently above 2.0. For example, SPART‐D in VR‐based group versus TCR had d = 2.53 (95% CI [1.83, 3.22]); SRT‐D had d = 2.77 (95% CI [2.04, 3.49]); and WLG had d = 3.58 (95% CI [2.74, 4.41]). For SRT‐LTS, both VR and CCT groups showed higher post‐intervention scores compared to TCR (p < 0.001), with large effect sizes (d = −1.13 and d = −1.40, respectively).

Kruskal–Wallis tests at T1 also showed significant between‐group differences in BDI (p < 0.001), PASAT‐3 (p = 0.01), PASAT‐2 (p < 0.001), and both MSQOL‐54 components (Physical and Mental, p < 0.001). Post hoc comparisons indicated PASAT‐2 scores in both VR and CCT groups compared to TCR, with moderate to large effect sizes (r range: −0.49 to −0.73), and also significant differences between VR and CCT (p < 0.001, r = 0.28, 95% CI [−0.02, 0.53]). For BDI, better scores were observed in the VR‐based group versus both TCR (p < 0.001, r = 0.62, 95% CI [0.40, 0.77]) and CCT (p < 0.001, r = 0.71, 95% CI [0.53, 0.83]). VR‐based participants also outperformed TCR in PASAT‐3 (p < 0.001, r = 0.41, 95% CI [0.14, 0.63]). Finally, both physical and mental components of MSQOL‐54 were significantly improved in CCT and VR groups compared to TCR, with large effect sizes (r = −1.00 and r = 1.00).

The main conclusions therefore rely on the covariate‐adjusted repeated‐measures and ANCOVA models reported in Sections 3.4, 3.6 and refer specifically to short‐term post‐intervention effects, given the absence of long‐term follow‐up data.

Repeated‐Measures ANOVA (Time and Time × Group Effects)

Across outcomes, repeated‐measures ANOVA showed a significant main effect of Time for all major cognitive, affective, and quality‐of‐life measures, indicating overall improvement from baseline to post‐intervention. Importantly, significant Time × Group interactions were detected for each reported endpoint, supporting differential treatment‐related change trajectories. For the primary cognitive outcome (SDMT), there was a significant Time effect (F(1,84) = 17.09, p < 0.001) and a significant Time × Group interaction (F(2,84) = 4.98, p = 0.009). Significant Time × Group interactions were also found for BDI (F(2,84) = 57.30, p < 0.001) and HRS‐A (F(2,84) = 16.88, p < 0.001). Cognitive outcomes showed significant interaction effects, including SPART (F(2,84) = 8.84, p < 0.001), SPART‐D (F(2,84) = 124.74, p < 0.001), SRT‐LTS (F(2,84) = 11.52, p < 0.001), SRT‐CLTR (F(2,84) = 12.62, p < 0.001), PASAT‐3 (F(2,84) = 5.71, p = 0.005), PASAT‐2 (F(2,84) = 5.28, p = 0.007), SRT‐D (F(2,84) = 11.30, p < 0.001), and WLG (F(2,84) = 5.74, p = 0.005). QoL (MSQOL‐54 Physical and Mental Composite scores) improved substantially, with significant Time effects and Time × Group interactions for both MSQOL‐54 Physical (Time: F(1,84) = 64.35, p < 0.001; interaction: F(2,84) = 16.00, p < 0.001) and MSQOL‐54 Mental (Time: F(1,84) = 144.22, p < 0.001; interaction: F(2,84) = 26.86, p < 0.001). Overall, these interaction effects indicate that VR‐based and CCT interventions produced greater gains over time compared with traditional rehabilitation.

Covariate‐Adjusted ANCOVA (Primary Adjusted) With Within‐Family Bonferroni

In baseline‐adjusted ANCOVA models (T1 adjusted for T0 and covariates), the primary endpoint SDMT showed a significant post‐intervention difference for VR compared with TCR (estimate = 5.23, 95% CI [1.31, 9.15], p = 0.0096). The contrasts CCT versus TCR (p = 0.275) and VR versus CCT (p = 0.113) were not significant.

Among cognitive secondary outcomes (Bonferroni family size m = 7), several effects remained significant after correction. VR showed significantly higher adjusted post‐intervention scores compared to TCR on PASAT‐3 (estimate = 6.53, p_bonf = 0.005), PASAT‐2 (estimate = 7.28, p_bonf = 0.001), and SPART (estimate = 3.74, p_bonf < 0.001). VR also showed higher adjusted post‐intervention scores compared to CCT on SPART (estimate = 3.09, p_bonf = 0.004) and SRT‐D (estimate = 1.70, p_bonf < 0.001). Significant between‐group differences were also observed for SPART‐D, with CCT versus TCR (estimate = −10.68, p_bonf < 0.001) and VR versus CCT (estimate = 9.88, p_bonf < 0.001).

For affective outcomes (m = 2), VR showed lower (i.e., improved) adjusted post‐intervention BDI scores versus TCR (estimate = −1.90, p_bonf < 0.001) and versus CCT (estimate = −1.58, p_bonf < 0.001). For HRS‐A, VR versus CCT was significant after correction (estimate = −2.38, p_bonf < 0.001), whereas VR versus TCR did not survive family‐wise correction (p_bonf = 0.07).

For QoL (m = 2), both MSQOL‐54 components showed significant adjusted group differences. MSQOL‐54 Physical was higher in VR versus TCR (estimate = 15.29, p_bonf < 0.001) and in CCT versus TCR (estimate = 9.98, p_bonf = 0.005), while VR versus CCT was not significant after correction. MSQOL‐54 Mental was higher in both VR versus TCR (estimate = 22.75, p_bonf < 0.001) and CCT versus TCR (estimate = 21.30, p_bonf < 0.001), with no difference between VR and CCT after correction. Covariate‐adjusted estimates and Bonferroni‐corrected p‐values for all outcomes are summarized in Table 5. Taken together, these findings suggest that, although the CCT group showed within‐group improvements in mood and working memory (BDI and HRS‐A: both p < 0.001; PASAT‐3: p = 0.01; PASAT‐2: p = 0.03), its covariate‐adjusted post‐intervention effects did not significantly differ from those of TCR on the primary endpoint (SDMT: CCT vs. TCR, p = 0.27) and was largely comparable on most cognitive and affective secondary outcomes (e.g., PASAT‐3 p_bonf = 0.66; PASAT‐2 p_bonf = 0.61; SPART p_bonf = 0.99; SRT‐CLTR p_bonf = 0.99; SRT‐D p_bonf = 0.76; WLG p_bonf = 0.99; BDI p_bonf = 0.50; HRS‐A p_bonf = 0.99), with the notable exception of SPART‐D, where CCT showed higher adjusted post‐intervention scores compared to TCR (p_bonf < 0.001).

Sensitivity ANCOVA (Additional Adjustment for Baseline BDI and HRS‐A)

Sensitivity models adjusting for baseline affective status (BDI at T0 and HRS‐A at T0) confirmed the robustness of the primary endpoint: VR versus TCR remained significant for SDMT (estimate = 7.72, 95% CI [1.88, 13.57], p = 0.01). Within the cognitive secondary family (m = 7), VR continued to show significantly greater post‐intervention improvements compared to TCR on PASAT‐3 (p_bonf = 0.001), PASAT‐2 (p_bonf = 0.002), SPART (p_bonf < 0.001), and SRT‐CLTR (p_bonf < 0.001), as well as on SRT‐D (p_bonf = 0.005). Additional VR versus CCT contrasts became significant after correction for some outcomes, including SRT‐D (p_bonf < 0.001), WLG (p_bonf = 0.007), and SPART‐D (p_bonf < 0.001), suggesting that the observed post‐intervention differences were not attributable to baseline affective differences. For QoL, sensitivity ANCOVA showed significant differences for MSQOL‐54 Physical and Mental with higher post‐intervention scores in VR and CCT compared to TCR, and also significant VR versus CCT contrasts (Physical: p_bonf = 0.02; Mental: p_bonf = 0.03). Covariate‐adjusted estimates for the primary models are reported in Table 5. Sensitivity analyses additionally adjusting for baseline depression and anxiety (BDI and HRS‐A) were consistent with the pattern of findings observed at post‐intervention for the primary and secondary outcomes (Table S1).

Direction of Change (Δ_oriented)

Oriented change scores supported the adjusted findings, showing larger descriptive short‐term improvements in the VR group for SDMT (mean Δ_oriented = 6.69), affective symptoms (BDI Δ_oriented = 4.62; HRS‐A Δ_oriented = 4.97), and QoL (MSQOL‐54 Physical Δ_oriented = 16.58; Mental Δ_oriented = 18.33), while the TCR group showed smaller mean improvements across domains. By construction, positive Δ_oriented values indicate improvement for all outcomes, including measures where lower scores denote clinical benefit (BDI and HRS‐A).

Discussion

The primary objective of this study was to compare the effects of three different cognitive rehabilitation modalities in MS patients: a CCT program using ERICA, a TCR protocol, and a semi‐immersive VR‐based approach with BTS Nirvana. MS represents an ideal clinical model to investigate integrative rehabilitation strategies, as it is characterized by the co‐occurrence of motor and non‐motor symptoms, including behavioral problems, executive dysfunction, and attentional deficits [3]. These impairments often interact, leading to reduced autonomy and increased risk of functional decline [24]. The results indicate that while all three modalities led to cognitive improvements, semi‐immersive VR appeared to be associated with larger gains across several key outcomes, as reflected by consistently large effect sizes. However, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary and hypothesis‐generating rather than as definitive evidence of comparative efficacy. The observed statistical interaction suggests differential trajectories of change across groups, but it does not allow firm conclusions regarding the superiority of VR over conventional interventions. In contrast, CCT with the ERICA software and TCR showed more modest and comparable improvements across multiple measures.

These findings confirm the central role of multimodal stimulation in enhancing neural plasticity and increasing treatment adherence [25]. The efficacy of semi‐immersive VR in improving cognitive functions and QoL in MS patients may be attributed to multiple neurophysiological and motivational factors. Real‐time interaction with virtual environments enriched with multisensory stimuli promotes the simultaneous activation of multiple neural circuits involved in attention, memory, and executive control [26, 27]. Notably, exposure to cognitive and motor tasks in virtual environments has been shown to stimulate neural plasticity, with measurable effects observed through functional neuroimaging [28]. A fundamental advantage of VR over traditional approaches is its ability to dynamically modulate task difficulty in real time based on patient responses, creating a highly personalized rehabilitation experience [20, 29]. This dynamic adaptation not only enhances cognitive performance but also directly influences patient engagement and motivation, two critical factors in adherence to rehabilitation programs [4]. Several studies have highlighted that VR increases the sense of presence and interactivity, which contribute to greater activation of dopaminergic circuits, thereby improving positive reinforcement and learning capacity [6, 30, 31, 32, 33, 34, 35].

The integration of motor tasks within a cognitive training framework appears to potentiate executive function, memory, and attention by increasing functional connectivity between sensorimotor and cognitive control networks [36] This is particularly relevant for MS patients, in whom balance impairments and cognitive deficits often co‐exist, affecting overall functional independence [37]. VR‐based intervention reinforces higher‐order cognitive skills such as working memory, cognitive flexibility, and processing speed, which are crucial for daily‐life adaptation [14]. Furthermore, the real‐time feedback and adaptive difficulty levels of VR tasks optimize task difficulty to the patient's abilities, fostering greater cognitive engagement and motivation, which in turn enhances learning retention and functional outcomes [4, 5]. The real‐time adaptability of VR allows continuous adjustment of task demands based on individual performance, offering a dynamic response to fluctuating cognitive capacity with greater improvements in learned skills and daily activities [9, 38]. The integration of cognitive and motor demands within a single training protocol, commonly known as dual‐task training, has shown remarkable potential in MS rehabilitation, as it improves attentional control and cognitive flexibility during complex activities of daily living [39]. In our study, the semi‐immersive VR system allowed patients to engage in ecologically valid dual‐task scenarios, such as responding to stimuli while performing movement‐based tasks, thus simulating real‐world challenges such as walking while conversing or moving in dynamic environments. Therefore, our findings suggest that semi‐immersive VR may represent a promising and feasible adjunct to standard rehabilitation approaches, rather than a replacement, to promote both cognitive and motor recovery in MS through enriched, multisensory, and interactive experiences.

Another important finding of this study is that CCT using the ERICA platform and TCR led to comparable cognitive improvements in patients with MS across several domains, including global cognition (MOCA), depressive and anxiety symptoms (BDI, HRS‐A), and verbal learning and recall (SRT LTS, SRT D). Both interventions yielded significant within‐group improvements; however, between‐group comparisons generally did not reveal statistically significant differences, except in a few cases where CCT showed modest advantages, such as in working memory and processing speed [13, 40]. Nevertheless, TCR demonstrated comparable efficacy, particularly in domains that benefit from flexible and therapist‐guided adaptation.

Importantly, the absence of consistent superiority of CCT over TCR, despite its technological structure and standardized delivery, warrants specific consideration. One possible explanation is that TCR represented an active, structured, and therapist‐mediated intervention rather than a passive control condition. The direct clinician–patient interaction inherent to TCR allows continuous real‐time adjustment of task difficulty, pacing, and strategy use, as well as immediate motivational reinforcement and error correction. These relational and adaptive components may enhance metacognitive awareness and strategy generalization beyond the trained tasks, thereby reducing the incremental advantage that a computerized platform might otherwise provide.

One key advantage of traditional rehabilitation lies in the real‐time interaction between patient and clinician, allowing immediate modulation of task difficulty, motivational reinforcement, and personalized goal‐setting [41]. This adaptability may account for TCR's comparable performance across most domains, despite its lack of the technological sophistication found in computerized platforms.

The absence of consistent superiority of CCT over TCR, observed both here and in other studies [42, 43], may be attributed to structural limitations of CCT programs. While ERICA and similar platforms offer standardized and replicable content, they may lack dynamic responsiveness to individual patient needs. Moreover, these systems typically require a higher level of patient autonomy and familiarity with digital interfaces, which may pose accessibility barriers for individuals with cognitive or executive dysfunction [40].

An additional limitation of CCT, particularly when compared to immersive VR‐based interventions, is the lack of sensorimotor engagement [44]. Unlike VR, which combines visual, motor, and cognitive stimulation to activate multiple neural systems simultaneously, CCT is based on screen‐based exercises, offering limited interaction with the environment and potentially lower levels of engagement and generalization [45]. This aspect is particularly relevant in patients with motor impairment and spasticity, as structured goal‐oriented and dual‐task proprioceptive training has been associated with improvements in balance, mobility, and selected cognitive domains in neurological populations, including individuals with MS, through the integration of motor and cognitive demands [45].

In line with this evidence, the significant within‐group improvements observed in the TCR group in depressive symptoms (BDI) and anxiety symptoms (HRS‐A), verbal memory, and QoL in our study further support the clinical value of therapist‐guided traditional rehabilitation.

Finally, although covariate‐adjusted analyses were performed and sensitivity ANCOVA confirmed the robustness of the effects, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded. Several potentially relevant variables (e.g., disease duration, fatigue, DMT status) were only partially available and could not be included without substantial case‐wise deletion. Moreover, baseline imbalances across cognitive, affective, and QoL domains highlight the importance of controlled comparisons, and future randomized studies are warranted to further address these factors. Furthermore, several non‐cognitive symptoms commonly associated with multiple sclerosis, such as bladder dysfunction, chronic pain, fatigue, and sleep disturbances, were not systematically assessed or controlled for in the present study. These MS‐related comorbidities are known to influence cognitive performance, emotional regulation, and quality of life, particularly in the case of bladder dysfunction [46], and may therefore have acted as additional confounding factors. Future studies should incorporate a more comprehensive evaluation of MS‐related symptom burden to better isolate intervention‐specific effects.

To sum up, while CCT represents a structured, scalable, and effective approach for cognitive rehabilitation, its superiority over TCR is not consistent. These findings support the adoption of a complementary model, in which CCT and TCR are integrated within multimodal rehabilitation programs, combining technological innovation with therapist‐guided adaptability to enhance both cognitive and motivational outcomes in patients with MS. Despite these limitations, a growing body of evidence supports the utility of CCT, particularly in telerehabilitation settings. Systems such as RehaCom and BrainHQ have demonstrated positive effects on executive functions, memory, and processing speed in neurological populations, offering a scalable and accessible alternative to conventional in‐person interventions [40, 47, 48, 49]. In addition, our previous study suggests that the effectiveness of CCT strongly depends on patient‐specific characteristics and the clinical context in which it is applied [40]. This variability in outcomes could be attributed to differences in treatment protocols.

Our findings underscore the importance of a personalized approach in selecting rehabilitation strategies, rather than a generalized adoption of software‐based or conventional programs. Digital tools may be particularly beneficial for patients with strong autonomy and familiarity with technology, whereas traditional rehabilitation remains a preferable option for those requiring more flexible and therapist‐driven support. A hybrid approach that combines the adaptability of conventional rehabilitation with the technological advantages of digital software may represent the most effective solution for maximizing therapeutic benefits and ensuring better adaptation to the individual needs of each patient.

Clinical Implications

The findings of this study highlight the potential of VR as a preferred approach for cognitive and motor rehabilitation in patients with MS. The integration of cognitive and motor stimulation within a highly engaging environment facilitates widespread neural network activation, enhancing neuroplasticity processes and functional recovery [49, 50]. The ability of VR to simulate real‐life scenarios and engage cognitive functions within a multisensory context may contribute to greater generalization of learning compared to TCR [51]. However, a uniform and generalized application of VR‐based rehabilitation is not appropriate for all patients. In addition, practical considerations such as equipment costs, required physical space, staff training, and technological infrastructure may limit the widespread implementation of VR‐based rehabilitation in certain clinical settings. Accessibility of disparities between specialized rehabilitation centers and community‐based services should also be considered. Future cost‐effectiveness analyses and implementation studies are needed to determine the sustainability and scalability of VR interventions in routine MS care.

Personalization of rehabilitation interventions is essential to ensure optimal clinical outcomes [20]. In this regard, TCR remains a valid option, particularly for patients requiring direct therapist interaction, those with limited access to digital technologies, or those less familiar with digital tools [52].

A critical aspect emerging from this study is the role of CCT, which, despite being effective for certain cognitive domains with a subtle advantage for TCR, we interpret that this advantage is not clinical and superior to TCR. This finding is consistent with previous literature, which reports variable efficacy of CCT, depending on factors such as the level of cognitive impairment, patient motivation, and treatment duration [40, 42, 43]. Moreover, software‐CCT such as ERICA, while providing modular and adaptable training for specific cognitive domains, does not incorporate a sensorimotor component, which may explain their lower efficacy compared to VR in enhancing transferable daily‐life skills [53].

Study Limitations and Future Directions

While this study provides evidence on the potential efficacy of VR in cognitive and motor rehabilitation for MS patients, several limitations should be acknowledged.

First, the sample size, although statistically adequate to detect group differences, may limit the generalizability of the findings. In addition, the study included individuals with mild‐to‐moderate cognitive impairment, and the generalizability of these findings to patients with more severe cognitive deficits or progressive MS phenotypes remains uncertain. Moreover, the absence of a long‐term follow‐up prevents the assessment of the durability of the observed improvements and their transferability to everyday functioning. Finally, disease duration was not consistently available across participants and could not be systematically included in the analyses. In addition, patients were not stratified by age or other individual variables that may influence treatment response, such as fatigue or digital literacy. Motor impairment severity and spasticity were not systematically quantified or controlled across groups, which may have influenced cognitive and functional outcomes, particularly in interventions involving motor engagement. Importantly, several potentially relevant covariates were not systematically controlled for, including disease‐modifying therapy, psychiatric comorbidities, and alcohol or other substance use. Given their known impact on cognitive performance, emotional regulation, and treatment responsiveness, these factors may have contributed to variability in outcomes, and residual confounding cannot be excluded.

Similarly, the stability and dosage of psychotropic medications were not formally assessed, further limiting causal attribution of the observed effects to the rehabilitation of modality alone.

Finally, potential confounding factors such as novelty effects, differential therapist involvement, and variations in patient motivation were not formally quantified. Although all interventions were delivered under supervised conditions, the possibility that the immersive nature of VR or therapist‐related factors influenced engagement and adherence cannot be entirely excluded.

Several promising avenues for future research emerge from the findings and limitations of this study. First, long‐term follow‐up assessments are essential to determine whether the cognitive and motor improvements observed after VR‐based rehabilitation are maintained over time and effectively transferred to daily life.

Another important direction concerns the role of individual variability in shaping treatment outcomes. Cognitive impairment severity, anxiety/depression symptoms, fatigue, gender, and age‐related factors may all influence response to rehabilitation. Future studies should account for these differences through greater stratification and using adaptive interfaces and pre‐screening tools to tailor interventions and improve adherence.

Personalization of rehabilitation could be further enhanced by integrating predictive models based on machine learning and neurophysiological biomarkers. The use of real‐time EEG and fMRI, for example, may allow dynamic adjustment of task difficulty in response to individual brain activation patterns and behavioral performance.

Advances in digital technology also represent a key opportunity. Improvements in immersion, interactivity, and user experience may enhance motivation and ecological validity.

Finally, future protocols may benefit from combining the strengths of different approaches. Integrating the realistic, embodied experiences offered by VR with the modular precision of CCT could yield synergistic effects, especially in patients with mild‐to‐moderate cognitive impairment. A multimodal strategy that leverages both ecological validity and cognitive specificity may maximize rehabilitation outcomes across a wider range of individuals.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides preliminary evidence that semi‐immersive VR may be a promising and feasible modality for cognitive rehabilitation in individuals with MS. Participants undergoing VR training showed significantly greater short‐term improvements in global cognition and QoL compared to those treated with computerized or traditional methods. However, these differences do not establish the superiority of VR‐based rehabilitation, as uncontrolled covariates such as DMT, disease duration, psychiatric status, and substance use may have influenced treatment responsiveness and functional outcomes. These findings highlight the benefits of immersive, adaptive, and multisensory environments in promoting engagement and short‐term cognitive and patient‐reported outcomes. Nonetheless, personalization remains the cornerstone of effective rehabilitation. VR should currently be viewed as a potentially useful complementary intervention rather than a replacement for standard rehabilitation. Integrating semi‐immersive VR within a flexible, hybrid rehabilitation model tailored to patient‐specific cognitive, motor, and emotional needs may represent a theoretically promising direction. However, this approach was not directly tested in the present study and should therefore be considered hypothesis‐generating. Future randomized controlled trials with adequate control of key clinical and behavioral covariates are needed to clarify their potential role in optimizing rehabilitation outcomes in MS.
