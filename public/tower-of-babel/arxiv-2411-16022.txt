General Geronimus Perturbations for Mixed Multiple Orthogonal Polynomials
Manuel Mañas 1, Miguel Rojas 2

Abstract
. General Geronimus transformations, defined by regular matrix polynomials that are neither required to be monic nor restricted by the rank of their leading coefficients, are applied through both right and left multiplication to a rectangular matrix of measures associated with mixed multiple orthogonal polynomials. These transformations produce Christoffel-type formulas that establish relationships between the perturbed and original polynomials. Moreover, it is proven that the existence of Geronimus-perturbed orthogonality is equivalent to the non-cancellation of certain τ \tau -determinants. The effect of these transformations on the Markov–Stieltjes matrix functions is also determined. As a case study, we examine the Jacobi–Piñeiro orthogonal polynomials with three weights.

Introduction

Multiple orthogonal polynomials (MOPs) form a general class of polynomials associated with multiple weight functions, unlike classical orthogonal polynomials tied to a single weight. MOPs play essential roles in numerical analysis, approximation theory, and mathematical physics, owing to their capacity to address complex problems involving simultaneous orthogonality conditions.

MOPs are historically linked to Hermite–Padé approximations and constructive function theory. For a detailed introduction, Nikishin and Sorokin’s book

[

Mixed-type MOPs, along with the corresponding Riemann–Hilbert problems, find applications in contexts such as Brownian bridges and non-intersecting Brownian motions

In 1858, the German mathematician Elwin Christoffel
[
26
]
studied Gaussian quadrature rules, aiming to find explicit formulas relating orthogonal polynomial sequences under different measures. Specifically, he investigated the Lebesgue measure
\mathrm{d}\mu=\mathrm{d}x
and a modified measure
\operatorname{d}\hat{\mu}(x)=p(x)\operatorname{d}\mu(x)
, where
p(x)=(x-q_{1})\cdots(x-q_{N})
is a polynomial with roots outside the support of
\mathrm{d}\mu
. Christoffel sought to understand the distribution of zeros, or nodes, in such quadrature rules
[
47
]
. The resulting Christoffel formula is discussed in classical textbooks on orthogonal polynomials, such as
[

,

These transformations extend beyond measures to involve linear functionals

,

These perturbations, including Christoffel and Geronimus, belong to the broader category of Darboux transformations, initially introduced in the context of integrable systems

The canonical Christoffel transformations are closely related to the

\mu

In the context of a moment functional

The first author and collaborators have previously studied Christoffel and Geronimus transformations in the context of matrix polynomials. The research began with the application of Christoffel transformations to monic matrix orthogonal polynomials, as detailed in
[
5
]

[
7
]
, where spectral techniques were introduced for monic perturbations, while the non-monic case was addressed without employing spectral methods. In
[
8
]
, the Geronimus–Uvarov framework was explored, with a particular focus on its connections to non-Abelian Toda lattices.

Moreover, in
[
17
]
, the Christoffel and Geronimus perturbations of two weights for non-mixed MOPs was studied. It was presented connection formulas between type II multiple orthogonal polynomials, type I linear forms, and vector Stieltjes–Markov functions. The perturbation matrix polynomials in this case were not necessarily monic but belonged to a restricted class.

In
[
40
]
, we explored general Christoffel transformations for mixed multiple orthogonal polynomials. By applying regular matrix polynomials—neither required to be monic nor constrained by the rank of their leading coefficients—through both right and left multiplication to a rectangular matrix of measures, we derived Christoffel-type formulas that relate the perturbed polynomials to the original ones. Using the divisibility theorem for matrix polynomials, we also established a criterion for the existence of perturbed orthogonality, characterized by the non-cancellation of specific
\tau
-determinants.

In this paper, we extend our investigation to examine general Geronimus transformations. These transformations, defined by regular matrix polynomials that are neither required to be monic nor limited by the rank of their leading coefficients, are applied via both right and left multiplication to a rectangular matrix of measures associated with mixed multiple orthogonal polynomials. The resulting transformations lead to Christoffel-type formulas that connect the perturbed polynomials to the original ones. Additionally, we prove that the existence of Geronimus-perturbed orthogonality is equivalent the non-cancellation of certain
\tau
-determinants.
The impact of these transformations on the Markov–Stieltjes matrix functions is also analyzed. As a case study, we focus on the Jacobi–Piñeiro orthogonal polynomials with three weights.

1.1.
Mixed Multiple Orthogonal Polynomials on the Step Line

For
p,q\in\mathbb{N}
, consider the setup where a rectangular matrix of measures is defined:

\operatorname{d}\mu=\begin{bNiceMatrix}\operatorname{d}\mu_{1,1}&\Cdots&\operatorname{d}\mu_{1,p}\\
\Vdots&&\Vdots\\
\operatorname{d}\mu_{q,1}&\Cdots&\operatorname{d}\mu_{q,p}\end{bNiceMatrix},

with each
\mu_{b,a}
being a measure supported on the interval
\Delta_{a,b}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
. The support of the matrix of measures is said to be
\Delta\coloneq\cup_{a,b}\Delta_{a,b}
. In this paper we use the notation
\mathbb{N}\coloneq\{1,2,\dots\}
and
N_{0}\coloneq\{0,1,2,\dots\}
.

For
r\in\mathbb{N}
, we define the matrix of monomials:

X_{[r]}(x)=\begin{bNiceMatrix}I_{r}\\
xI_{r}\\
x^{2}I_{r}\\
\Vdots\end{bNiceMatrix},

and the moment matrix as:

\mathscr{M}\coloneq\int_{\Delta}X_{[q]}(x)\operatorname{d}\mu(x)X_{[p]}^{\top}(x).

If all leading principal submatrices
\mathscr{M}^{[k]}
are invertible, a
LU
factorization exists:

\mathscr{M}=\mathscr{L}^{-1}\mathscr{U}^{-1},

where
\mathscr{L}
and
\mathscr{U}
are nonsingular lower and upper triangular matrices. This factorization is unique up to

\mathscr{L}\to\mathscr{d}^{-1}\mathscr{L},\quad\mathscr{U}\to\mathscr{U}\mathscr{d},

for
\mathscr{d}
any nonsingular diagonal matrix.

Two normalizations are of importance:

•

Left normalization sets
\mathscr{L}
as unitriangular, resulting in triangular matrices
S
and
\mathscr{U}_{L}
.

•

Right normalization sets
\mathscr{U}
as unitriangular, yielding matrices
\mathscr{L}_{R}
and
\bar{S}^{\top}
.

The
LU
factorization can be rewritten as:

\mathscr{M}=S^{-1}H\bar{S}^{-\top},

where
S
,
\bar{S}
, and
H
are lower unitriangular and invertible diagonal matrices, respectively.

Next, we define matrix polynomials associated with this factorization:

\displaystyle B(x)

\displaystyle=\mathscr{L}X_{[q]}(x),

\displaystyle A(x)

\displaystyle=X_{[p]}^{\top}(x)\mathscr{U},

where
B(x)
is monic, i.e.,

\displaystyle B(x)

\displaystyle=SX_{[q]}(x),

\displaystyle A(x)

\displaystyle=X_{[p]}^{\top}(x)\bar{S}^{\top}H^{-1}.

The polynomial entries of
B
and
A
are:

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=\begin{bNiceMatrix}B^{(1)}_{0}&\Cdots&B^{(q)}_{0}\\[2.0pt]
B^{(1)}_{1}&\Cdots&B^{(q)}_{1}\\[2.0pt]
B^{(1)}_{2}&\Cdots&B^{(q)}_{2}\\
\Vdots&&\Vdots\end{bNiceMatrix},

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{NiceMatrix}A^{(1)}_{0}&A^{(1)}_{1}&A^{(1)}_{2}&\Cdots\\
\Vdots&\Vdots&\Vdots&\\
A^{(p)}_{0}&A^{(p)}_{1}&A^{(p)}_{2}&\Cdots\end{NiceMatrix}\right].

We will make use of the following notation,

\displaystyle B_{n}(x)

\displaystyle\coloneq\begin{bNiceMatrix}B^{(1)}_{n}(x)&\Cdots&B^{(q)}_{n}(x)\end{bNiceMatrix},

\displaystyle A_{n}(x)

\displaystyle\coloneq\begin{bNiceMatrix}A_{n}^{(1)}(x)\\
\Vdots\\
A_{n}^{(p)}(x)\end{bNiceMatrix}.

The polynomials
B(x)
and
A(x)
satisfy the biorthogonality condition:

\displaystyle\int_{\Delta}B(x)\,\operatorname{d}\mu(x)\,A(x)

\displaystyle=I,

\displaystyle\int_{\Delta}\sum_{b=1}^{q}\sum_{a=1}^{p}B^{(b)}_{n}(x)\operatorname{d}\mu_{b,a}(x)A^{(a)}_{m}(x)

\displaystyle=\delta_{n,m}.

The
LU
factorization yields:

\displaystyle\int_{\Delta}B(x)\,\operatorname{d}\mu(x)X_{[p]}^{\top}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathscr{U}^{-1},

\displaystyle\int_{\Delta}X_{[q]}(x)\,\operatorname{d}\mu(x)A(x)

\displaystyle=\mathscr{L}^{-1},

which gives rise to diagonal orthogonality relations:

\displaystyle\int_{\Delta}x^{l}\sum_{a=1}^{p}\operatorname{d}\mu_{b,a}(x)A_{n}^{(a)}(x)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle\in\{1,\dots,q\},

\displaystyle l

\displaystyle\in\left\{0,\dots,\left\lceil\frac{n-b+2}{q}\right\rceil-1\right\},

\displaystyle\int_{\Delta}\sum_{b=1}^{q}B_{n}^{(b)}(x)\operatorname{d}\mu_{b,a}(x)x^{l}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle\in\{1,\dots,p,\},

\displaystyle l

\displaystyle\in\left\{0,\dots,\left\lceil\frac{n-a+2}{p}\right\rceil-1\right\}.

Consequently, the existence of the
LU
factorization is equivalent to the existence of the orthogonality.

For
r\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, the shift matrix is:

\Lambda_{[r]}\coloneq\left[\begin{NiceMatrix}0_{r}&I_{r}&0_{r}&\Cdots\\
0_{r}&0_{r}&I_{r}&\Ddots\\
0_{r}&0_{r}&0_{r}&\Ddots\\
\Vdots&\Ddots[shorten-end=5pt]&\Ddots[shorten-end=10pt]&\Ddots[shorten-end=15pt]\end{NiceMatrix}\right],

with
\Lambda_{[1]}=\Lambda
and
\Lambda_{[r]}=\Lambda^{r}
. These matrices satisfy:

\Lambda_{[r]}X_{[r]}(x)=xX_{[r]}(x).

The moment matrix
\mathscr{M}
obeys the symmetry:

\Lambda_{[q]}\mathscr{M}=\mathscr{M}\Lambda_{[p]}^{\top},

leading to:

T=\mathscr{L}\Lambda_{[q]}\mathscr{L}^{-1}=\mathscr{U}^{-1}\Lambda_{[p]}^{\top}\mathscr{U},

implying that
T
is a
(p,q)
-banded matrix with
p
subdiagonals and
q
superdiagonals. The recurrence relations for mixed multiple orthogonal polynomials are:

(1)

\displaystyle TB(x)

\displaystyle=xB(x),

\displaystyle A(x)T

\displaystyle=xA(x),

where
T
is the recurrence matrix. The leading principal submatrices take the form

T^{[n]}=\begin{bNiceMatrix}T_{0,0}&\Cdots&&T_{0,q}&0&\Cdots&&0\\
\Vdots&&&&\Ddots&\Ddots&&\Vdots\\
T_{p,0}&&&&&&&\\
0&\Ddots&&&&&&0\\
\Vdots&\Ddots&&&&&&T_{n-q,m}\\
&&&&&&&\Vdots\\
&&&&&&&\\[6.0pt]
0&\Cdots&&&0&T_{n,n-p}&\Cdots&T_{n,n}\end{bNiceMatrix}.

So that we have the inductive limit
T=\lim_{n\to\infty}T^{[n]}
.

1.2.
Christoffel–Darboux Kernels

We now turn our attention to key elements essential for the constructions in this paper: the kernel polynomials.

Definition 1.1
.

Let us introduce the Christoffel–Darboux (CD) kernel
p\times q
matrix polynomial,
K^{[n]}(x,y)
:

(2)

\displaystyle K^{[n]}(x,y)

\displaystyle\coloneq A^{[n]}(x)B^{[n]}(y)=\begin{bNiceMatrix}A_{0}^{(1)}(x)&\Cdots&A_{n}^{(1)}(x)\\
\Vdots&&\Vdots\\
A_{0}^{(p)}(x)&\Cdots&A_{n}^{(p)}(x)\end{bNiceMatrix}\begin{bNiceMatrix}B_{0}^{(1)}(y)&\Cdots&B_{0}^{(q)}(y)\\
\Vdots&&\Vdots\\
B_{n}^{(1)}(y)&\Cdots&B_{n}^{(q)}(y)\end{bNiceMatrix},

(3)

\displaystyle K^{[n]}_{a,b}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n}A^{(a)}_{i}(x)B_{i}^{(b)}(y).

This kernel polynomial satisfies several interesting properties, similar to the properties of the kernel polynomial in the scalar case.

Proposition 1.2
.

For any given matrix polynomial of degree
N
, i.e.,

P(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{N}P_{i}x^{i},\quad P_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{p\times p},

the following projection property holds:

(4)

\displaystyle\int_{\Delta}K^{[n]}(x,t)\operatorname{d}\mu(t)P(t)

\displaystyle=P(x),

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle\geq Np+p-1.

Proof.

Using the orthogonality relations, for
n\geq Np+p-1
, it is straightforward to verify that:

\int_{\Delta}\begin{bNiceMatrix}B_{n}^{(1)}(t)&\Cdots&B_{n}^{(q)}(t)\end{bNiceMatrix}\operatorname{d}\mu(t)P(t)=0.

Consequently, the result is independent of
n
:

A^{[n]}(x)\int_{\Delta}B^{[n]}(t)\operatorname{d}\mu(t)P(t)=A^{[Np+p]}(x)\int_{\Delta}B^{[Np+p]}(t)\operatorname{d}\mu(t)P(t)=A(x)\int_{\Delta}B(t)\operatorname{d}\mu(t)P(t).

Now, we prove the projection property for
n\rightarrow\infty
,

\displaystyle A(x)\int_{\Delta}B(t)\operatorname{d}\mu(t)P(t)=X_{[p]}^{\top}(x)\bar{S}^{\top}H^{-1}S\int_{\Delta}X_{[q]}(t)\operatorname{d}\mu(t)X_{[p]}^{\top}(t)\begin{bNiceMatrix}P_{0}\\
\Vdots\\
P_{N}\\
0_{p}\\
\Vdots\end{bNiceMatrix}=X_{[p]}^{\top}(x)\begin{bNiceMatrix}P_{0}\\
\Vdots\\
P_{N}\\
0_{p}\\
\Vdots\end{bNiceMatrix}=P(x).

To achieve the stated result, we have used the definition of the moment matrix and its Gauss–Borel factorization.
∎

6

for mixed multiple orthogonality. For another type of Christoffel–Darboux formulas for mixed multiple orthogonality, see

For the banded recurrence matrix

\displaystyle\mathscr{T}^{[n,>n]}

\displaystyle\coloneq\begin{bNiceMatrix}T_{n+1-q,n+1}&0&\Cdots&0\\
\Vdots&\Ddots&\Ddots&\Vdots\\
&&&0\\
T_{n,n+1}&\Cdots&&T_{n,n+q}\end{bNiceMatrix},

\displaystyle\mathscr{T}^{[>n,n]}

\displaystyle\coloneq\begin{bNiceMatrix}T_{n+1,n+1-p}&\Cdots&&T_{n+1,n}\\
0&\Ddots[shorten-start=-1pt,shorten-end=-25pt]&&\\
\\[-16.0pt]
\Vdots&\Ddots[shorten-start=-25pt]&&\Vdots\\

where
\mathscr{T}^{[n,>]}
and
\mathscr{T}^{[>,n]}

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}^{[n]}

\displaystyle\coloneq\begin{bNiceMatrix}A^{(1)}_{n+1-q}&\Cdots&A^{(1)}_{n}\\
\Vdots&&\Vdots\\
A^{(p)}_{n+1-q}&\Cdots&A^{(p)}_{n}\end{bNiceMatrix},

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}^{[>n]}

\displaystyle\coloneq\begin{bNiceMatrix}A^{(1)}_{n+1}&\Cdots&A^{(1)}_{n+p}\\
\Vdots&&\Vdots\\
A^{(p)}_{n+1}&\Cdots&A^{(p)}_{n+p}\end{bNiceMatrix},

\displaystyle\mathscr{B}^{[n]}

\displaystyle\coloneq\begin{bNiceMatrix}B^{(1)}_{n+1-p}&\Cdots&B^{(q)}_{n+1-p}\\
\Vdots&&\Vdots\\
B^{(1)}_{n}&\Cdots&B^{(q)}_{n}\end{bNiceMatrix},

\displaystyle\mathscr{B}^{[>n]}

\displaystyle\coloneq\begin{bNiceMatrix}B^{(1)}_{n+1}&\Cdots&B^{(q)}_{n+1}\\
\Vdots&&\Vdots\\
B^{(1)}_{n+q}&\Cdots&B^{(q)}_{n+q}\end{bNiceMatrix},

where
\mathscr{A}^{[n]}
,

,

T=\left[\begin{NiceArray}\right],

Theorem 1.3
.

The following matrix Christoffel–Darboux formula holds:

(x-y)K^{[n]}(x,y)=\mathscr{A}^{[>n]}(x)\mathscr{T}^{[>n,n]}\mathscr{B}^{[n]}(y)-\mathscr{A}^{[n]}(x)\mathscr{T}^{[n,>n]}\mathscr{B}^{[>n]}(y).

Proof.

From (
1
), we have
(A(x)T)^{[n]}=xA^{[n]}(x)
and
(TB(y))^{[n]}=yB^{[n]}(y)
. Expanding these:

\displaystyle xA^{[n]}(x)

\displaystyle=(A(x)T)^{[n]}=A^{[n]}(x)T^{[n]}+A^{[>n]}T^{[>n,n]},

\displaystyle yB^{[n]}(y)

\displaystyle=(TB(y))^{[n]}=T^{[n]}B^{[n]}(y)+T^{[n,>n]}B^{[>n]}(y).

Thus, we obtain:

\displaystyle(x-y)K^{[n]}(x,y)

\displaystyle=A^{[>n]}(x)T^{[>n,n]}B^{[n]}(y)-A^{[n]}(x)T^{[n,>n]}B^{[>n]}(y)

\displaystyle=\mathscr{A}^{[>n]}(x)\mathscr{T}^{[>n,n]}\mathscr{B}^{[n]}(y)-\mathscr{A}^{[n]}(x)\mathscr{T}^{[n,>n]}\mathscr{B}^{[>n]}(y).

∎

1.3.
Cauchy Transforms

Another crucial set of objects in this construction are the Cauchy transforms of the matrix polynomials under consideration.

Definition 1.4
.

The Cauchy transforms of the orthogonal polynomials
A(x)
and
B(x)
are defined as follows:

\displaystyle C(z)

\displaystyle\coloneq\int_{\Delta}\frac{\operatorname{d}\mu(x)}{z-x}A(x),

\displaystyle D(z)

\displaystyle\coloneq\int_{\Delta}B(x)\frac{\operatorname{d}\mu(x)}{z-x}.

Remark 1.5
.

The entries of the Cauchy transform matrices are holomorphic in
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Delta
.

Proposition 1.6
.

C(z)
and
D(z)
can also be expressed in terms of the Gauss–Borel matrices,
S
and
\bar{S}
:

\displaystyle C(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{z}X_{[q]}^{\top}(z^{-1})S^{-1},

\displaystyle D(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{z}H\bar{S}^{-\top}X_{[p]}(z^{-1}),

whenever
\lvert z\rvert>\mathrm{sup}\{\lvert x\rvert:x\in\Delta\}
.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle C(z)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Delta}\frac{\operatorname{d}\mu(x)}{z-x}A(x)=\frac{1}{z}\int_{\Delta}\frac{\operatorname{d}\mu(x)}{1-\frac{x}{z}}A(x)=\frac{1}{z}\int_{\Delta}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{x}{z}\right)^{i}\operatorname{d}\mu(x)A(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{z}X_{[q]}^{\top}(z^{-1})\int_{\Delta}X_{[q]}(x)\operatorname{d}\mu(x)X^{\top}_{[p]}(x)\bar{S}^{\top}H^{-1}=\frac{1}{z}X_{[q]}(z^{-1})S^{-1}.

The expansion of
(1-\frac{x}{z})^{-1}
in a power series is valid since
\lvert z\rvert>\mathrm{sup}\{\lvert x\rvert:x\in\Delta\}
. A similar proof can be given for
D(z)
.
∎

Let us now introduce two additional families of CD kernels.

Definition 1.7
.

The mixed Christoffel–Darboux kernels are defined as follows:

\displaystyle K^{[n]}_{C}(x,y)

\displaystyle\coloneq C^{[n]}(x)B^{[n]}(y)=\int_{\Delta}\frac{\operatorname{d}\mu(t)}{x-t}K^{[n]}(t,y),

\displaystyle K^{[n]}_{D}(x,y)

\displaystyle\coloneq A^{[n]}(x)D^{[n]}(y)=\int_{\Delta}K^{[n]}(x,t)\frac{\operatorname{d}\mu(t)}{y-t}.

We now derive Christoffel–Darboux formulas for the mixed kernels. To this end, we introduce

\displaystyle\mathscr{C}^{[n]}(x)

\displaystyle\coloneq\int_{\Delta}\frac{\mu(t)}{x-t}\mathscr{A}^{[n]}(t),

\displaystyle\mathscr{C}^{[>n]}(x)

\displaystyle\coloneq\int_{\Delta}\frac{\mu(t)}{x-t}\mathscr{A}^{[>n]}(t),

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}^{[n]}(y)

\displaystyle\coloneq\int_{\Delta}\mathscr{B}^{[n]}(t)\frac{\mu(t)}{y-t},

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}^{[>n]}(y)

\displaystyle\coloneq\int_{\Delta}\mathscr{B}^{[>n]}(t)\frac{\mu(t)}{y-t}.

Theorem 1.8
.

The mixed Christoffel–Darboux kernels satisfy the following Christoffel–Darboux-type formulas:

\displaystyle(x-y)K^{[n]}_{C}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\big(\mathscr{C}^{[>n]}(x)-\mathscr{C}^{[>n]}(y)\big)\mathscr{T}^{[>n,n]}\mathscr{B}^{[n]}(y)-\big(\mathscr{C}^{[n]}(x)-\mathscr{C}^{[n]}(y)\big)\mathscr{T}^{[n,>n]}\mathscr{B}^{[>n]}(y),

\displaystyle(x-y)K^{[n]}_{D}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\mathscr{A}^{[n]}(x)\mathscr{T}^{[n,>n]}\big(\mathscr{D}^{[>n]}(x)-\mathscr{D}^{[>n]}(y)\big)-\mathscr{A}^{[>n]}(x)\mathscr{T}^{[>n,n]}\big(\mathscr{D}^{[n]}(x)-\mathscr{D}^{[n]}(y)\big).

Proof.

We prove only the first formula, as the second follows similarly.

We start with

K^{[n]}_{C}(x,y)=\int_{\Delta}\frac{\operatorname{d}\mu(t)}{x-t}K^{[n]}(t,y),

and use the Christoffel–Darboux formula:

∎

2.
Geronimus Perturbation

2.2.
Eigenvalues of Arbitrary Multiplicity

We now address the case of a perturbation matrix with arbitrary multiplicity. In this context, the most general measure is expressed as follows:

\operatorname{d}\check{\mu}(x)=\operatorname{d}\mu(x)R^{-1}(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sum_{j=1}^{s_{i}}\sum_{k=0}^{\kappa_{i,j}-1}\bm{\xi}_{i,j,k}(x)\left(\sum_{l=0}^{k}\frac{(-1)^{l}}{l!}\bm{v}_{i,j;k-l}^{L}\delta^{(l)}(x-x_{i})\right),

where the first summation accounts for the different roots, the second and third summations account for the number of vectors in the canonical set of Jordan chains (associated with each eigenvalue), and the final summation correctly expands the action of each Jordan chain. The total number of distinct masses is
Np-r
. Many of the results derived in the simple eigenvalue scenario can be extended to this case, provided that the full theory of Jordan chains for
R(x)
is applied. However, the different limits evaluated in the proofs of Proposition
2.7
and Proposition
2.11
require more careful consideration. Before proceeding, we establish a useful lemma.

Lemma 2.16
.

For a holomorphic matrix function
f(x)
(with holomorphic entries) and a matrix polynomial
R(x)
, both of order
p
, the following holds:

\sum_{l=0}^{k}\frac{1}{l!}\bm{v}_{i,j;k-l}^{L}\frac{\operatorname{d}^{l}}{\operatorname{d}x^{l}}\left[R(x)f(x)\right]_{x=x_{i}}=0,

where
\bm{v}_{i,j;k-l}
is a Jordan vector and
k\leq\kappa_{i,j}-1
. The same result holds if we apply this expression on the left to the combination
f(x)R(x)
.

Proof.

Differentiating
l
times gives:

Proposition 2.17

The following limits:

Remark 2.22

Let us now extend the proof of Theorem

R(x)\begin{bNiceMatrix}\mathbb{K}^{[n-1],(1)}(x)+\mathbb{P}^{(1)}(x)&\Cdots&\mathbb{K}^{[n-1],(M)}(x)+\mathbb{P}^{(M)}(x)\end{bNiceMatrix}\\

\Vdots&\Ddots&\Ddots[shorten-end=-8pt]&\Vdots\\

\times\begin{bNiceMatrix}\mathbb{D}_{n-Np+r}^{(1)}-\mathbb{W}_{n-Np+r}^{(1)}&\Cdots&\mathbb{D}_{n-Np+r}^{(M)}-\mathbb{W}_{n-Np+r}^{(M)}\\

\mathbb{D}_{n-1}^{(1)}-\mathbb{W}_{n-1}^{(1)}&\Cdots&\mathbb{D}_{n-1}^{(M)}-\mathbb{W}_{n-1}^{(M)}\end{bNiceMatrix}.

Assuming that orthogonality holds even when
\tau_{n-1}=0
, there exists a vector
\bm{c}
of size
Np-r
, such that by studying points where
\det R(x)\neq 0

\begin{bNiceMatrix}\mathbb{K}^{[n-1],(1)}(x)+\mathbb{P}^{(1)}(x)&\Cdots&\mathbb{K}^{[n-1],(i)}(x)+\mathbb{P}^{(i)}(x)&\Cdots&\mathbb{K}^{[n-1],(M)}(x)+\mathbb{P}^{(M)}(x)\end{bNiceMatrix}\bm{c}=0.

Let us now examine
\mathbb{P}^{(i)}(x)
in closer detail. The vector contains derivatives of
\frac{1}{x-y}
evaluated at

, which are poles whose multiplicity depends on the order of the derivative. Specifically, we have:

\mathbb{P}^{(i)}(x)=\begin{bNiceMatrix}\frac{\bm{v}_{i,1;0}^{R}}{x-x_{i}}&\Cdots&\sum_{l=0}^{\kappa_{i,1}-2}\frac{\bm{v}_{i,1;\kappa_{i,1}-2-l}^{R}}{(x-x_{i})^{l+1}}&\sum_{l=0}^{\kappa_{i,1}-1}\frac{\bm{v}_{i,1;\kappa_{i,1}-1-l}^{R}}{(x-x_{i})^{l+1}}&\Cdots&\frac{\bm{v}_{i,s_{i};0}^{R}}{x-x_{i}}&\Cdots&\sum_{l=0}^{\kappa_{i,s_{i}}-1}\frac{\bm{v}_{i,s_{i};\kappa_{i,s_{i}}-1-l}^{R}}{(x-x_{i})^{l+1}}\end{bNiceMatrix}.

Multiplying the expression by
(x-x_{i})^{\kappa_{i,j}-1}
, where
\kappa_{i,j}
is the largest partial multiplicity, we integrate the relation over the complex plane using a counterclockwise contour
C_{i}
around
x_{i}
(and not any other eigenvalue):

\oint_{C_{i}}(z-x_{i})^{\kappa_{i,j}-1}\begin{bNiceMatrix}\mathbb{K}^{[n-1],(1)}(z)+\mathbb{P}^{(1)}(z)&\Cdots&\mathbb{K}^{[n-1],(M)}(z)+\mathbb{P}^{(M)}(z)\end{bNiceMatrix}\bm{c}\>\operatorname{d}z.

The only nonzero terms will correspond to those involving poles of multiplicity
\kappa_{i,j}
, which are associated with Jordan chains of length
\kappa_{i,j}
. Without loss of generality, assume that the partial multiplicities for
j\in\{1,\dots,s_{i}\}
are the same. Then:

2\pi\operatorname{i}\begin{bNiceMatrix}0&\Cdots&0&\bm{v}_{i,1;0}^{R}&0&\Cdots&0&\bm{v}_{i,s_{i};0}^{R}&0&\Cdots[shorten-end=8pt]\end{bNiceMatrix}\bm{c}=0.

The nonzero entries correspond to positions
\kappa_{i,1},\dots,\kappa_{i,s_{i}}

\bm{v}_{i,1;0}^{R}c_{\kappa_{i,1}}+\bm{v}_{i,2;0}^{R}c_{\kappa_{i,2}}+\dots+\bm{v}_{i,s_{i};0}^{R}c_{\kappa_{i,s_{i}}}=0.

However, since the vectors
\bm{v}_{i,j;0}^{R}
are linearly independent, it follows that the only possible conclusion is

. We then multiply the preceding expression by

, along with the terms immediately preceding them. At this juncture, we could also consider the Jordan chains associated with a partial multiplicity equal to
\kappa_{i,j}-1

\kappa_{i,j}-1

Consequently, we would systematically deduce that all entries of

2.3.
Left Multiplication

We previously described Geronimus perturbations as a right multiplication of the perturbed measure. Here, we generalize the result by considering the case of a left multiplication, as follows:

L(x)\,\mathrm{d}\check{\mu}(x)=\mathrm{d}\mu(x),

where

is a matrix polynomial of degree
N

\displaystyle L(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=0}^{N}L_{l}x^{l},

\displaystyle L_{l}\in\mathbb{C}^{q\times q},

\displaystyle L_{N}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0_{r\times(q-r)}&0_{r}\\

\displaystyle L_{N-1}

2.4.
Markov–Stieltjes Functions

The Markov–Stieltjes function of the matrix of measures
\operatorname{d}\mu
is given by

F(z)=\int_{\Delta}\frac{\operatorname{d}\mu(x)}{z-x},

which is the Cauchy transform of the identity matrix and plays a crucial role in the spectral theory of orthogonal polynomials. In
[
49
]
, linear spectral transformations of the Markov–Stieltjes function for standard orthogonal polynomials were studied in the form

F(z)\longrightarrow\frac{A(z)F(z)+B(z)}{C(z)F(z)+D(z)},

where
A,B,C,
and
D
are polynomials.

Zhedanov proved that Christoffel transformations generate a more general linear spectral transformation of the form

F(z)\longrightarrow A(z)F(z)+B(z).

He further proved that the composition of
N
successive Geronimus transformations generates a more general linear spectral transformation of the form

F(z)\longrightarrow\frac{F(z)+B(z)}{D(z)},

where
\deg B=N-1
and
\deg D=N
.

We now prove that similar statements hold for Geronimus transformations in this mixed multiple scenario.

Here, we consider a Markov–Stieltjes
q\times p
-matrix function

F(z)\coloneq\int_{\Delta}\frac{\operatorname{d}\mu(x)}{z-x}.

Proposition 2.30
.

The Geronimus transformation described in Equation (
7
) amounts to a matrix linear spectral transformation of the form

\check{F}(z)=\big(F(z)+S(z)\big)R^{-1}(z),

where
\deg S=\deg R-1
with matrix polynomial coefficients given by

\displaystyle S_{k}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Delta}\operatorname{d}\check{\mu}(x)(R_{k+1}+xR_{k+2}+\cdots+x^{N-k-1}R_{N}),

\displaystyle k

\displaystyle\in\{0,\dots,N-1\}.

Proof.

This follows from the relationship
\operatorname{d}\check{\mu}(x)R(x)=\operatorname{d}\mu(x)
. Indeed, we have the identity

which is a polynomial of degree

∎

\check{A}_{0}^{(1)}\neq 0
, the term
\check{A}_{Np-r}^{(p-r+1)}
must have degree
N-1
. Below these terms, for
p-r+1<a\leq p
, there are no terms of degree
N-1
on the left-hand side, implying that
\check{A}_{Np-r}^{(a)}
can have degree up to
N-2
. Repeating this analysis
r
times for subsequent columns of Equation (
29
), completes the degree structure for
\mathcal{\check{A}}_{N-1}
.

Next, let us examine the
r+1
-th column and focus on the terms of degree
N
:

\Omega_{Np,r}\begin{bNiceMatrix}\check{A}_{Np}^{(1)}(x)\\
\Vdots\\
\check{A}_{Np}^{(p)}(x)\end{bNiceMatrix}=\begin{bNiceMatrix}A_{r}^{(r+1)}\\
0\\
\Vdots\\
0\end{bNiceMatrix}x^{N}+O(x^{N-1}).

Clearly,
\check{A}_{Np}^{(1)}(x)
must have degree
N
, whereas the remaining terms can have a degree of at most
N-1
. By iterating this reasoning as described, we can conclude that up to
n=Np+p-r
, all vector polynomials possess the required degree structure.

Thus, the proof is complete. Under the assumption
\tau_{n}\neq 0
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
and the existence of the orthogonality conditions for
B_{i}(x)
, we derive certain orthogonality relations for
\check{A}(x)
as well as their degree structure.
∎

Observe that assuming the orthogonality exists up to
n={Np-r}-1
does not guarantee its existence for all degrees, as it may truncate at some point. However, we have demonstrated that, provided the
\tau
-determinants are nonzero, the orthogonality does not truncate.

It is now evident that the existence of perturbed orthogonality, provided that the
\tau
-determinants are nonzero, is related to the formulas for
\check{A}(x)
and
\check{B}(x)
in the case where
n<{Np-r}
. Let us examine the case for
n<{Np-r}
in greater detail. The connection formulas for
\check{B}(x)
, as presented in Proposition
2.3
, are given by:

(30)

\check{B}^{(b)}_{n}(x)=B^{(b)}_{n}(x)+\Omega_{n,n-1}B^{(b)}_{n-1}(x)+\cdots+\Omega_{n,0}B^{(b)}_{0}(x),

and, as noted in Remark
2.15
, the method employed throughout this work does not provide explicit information about the
\Omega
components when
n<{Np-r}
. However, under the assumption of the existence of perturbed orthogonality, the following relation holds:

(31)

\displaystyle\int_{\Delta}\check{B}_{n}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\check{\mu}(x)\,\left(X_{[p]}^{\top}(x)\bm{e}_{l}\right)=0,\quad l\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}.

Definition 3.2
.

We introduce the following notation:

(32)

\mathbb{I}_{i,l}\coloneq\int_{\Delta}B_{i}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\check{\mu}(x)\,\left(X_{[p]}^{\top}(x)\bm{e}_{l}\right).

Referring to Equation (
7
) and (
32
), we have for the case of simple zeros
x_{i}

\mathbb{I}_{i,l}=\int_{\Delta}B_{i}(x)\left(\operatorname{d}\mu(x)R^{-1}(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{M}\bm{\xi}_{i}(x)\bm{v}_{i}^{L}\delta(x-x_{i})\right)\operatorname{d}\check{\mu}(x)\,\left(X_{[p]}^{\top}(x)\bm{e}_{l}\right).

The adjugate matrix
\operatorname{adj}R(x)
of the matrix polynomial
R(x)
is a matrix polynomial such that

R^{-1}(x)=\frac{1}{\det R(x)}\operatorname{adj}R(x).

Recalling the partial fraction decomposition

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\det R(x)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{M}\frac{C_{j}}{x-x_{j}},

\displaystyle C_{j}\coloneq\prod_{k\neq j}(x_{j}-x_{k}),

we can write, cf.
[
47
]
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{I}_{i,l}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Delta}B_{i}(x)\sum_{j=1}^{M}\left(\operatorname{d}\mu(x)\frac{C_{j}}{x-x_{j}}\operatorname{adj}R(x)+\bm{\xi}_{j}(x)\bm{v}_{j}^{L}\delta(x-x_{j})\right)\left(X_{[p]}^{\top}(x)\bm{e}_{l}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{M}C_{j}\int_{\Delta}B_{i}(x)\frac{\operatorname{d}\mu(x)}{x-x_{j}}\operatorname{adj}R(x)X_{[p]}^{\top}(x)\bm{e}_{l}+\sum_{j=1}^{M}B(x_{j})\bm{\xi}_{j}(x_{j})\bm{v}_{j}^{L}X_{[p]}^{\top}(x_{j})\bm{e}_{l}.

This applies to the case of simple eigenvalues. It can be noted that the generalization to cases with higher multiplicities involves merely considering a more general partial fraction decomposition.

Definition 3.3
.

We define new
\tilde{\tau}
-determinants as follows:

\displaystyle\tilde{\tau}_{n}

\displaystyle\coloneq\begin{vNiceMatrix}\mathbb{I}_{0,0}&\Cdots&\mathbb{I}_{0,n}\\
\Vdots&&\Vdots\\
\mathbb{I}_{n,0}&\Cdots&\mathbb{I}_{n,n}\end{vNiceMatrix},

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle\in\{0,\dots,{Np-r}-1\}.

Assuming that these determinants are nonzero, substituting Equation (

) into (

) yields:

-\mathbb{I}_{n,l}=\begin{bNiceMatrix}\Omega_{n,0}&\Cdots&\Omega_{n,n-1}\end{bNiceMatrix}\begin{bNiceMatrix}\mathbb{I}_{0,l}\\
\Vdots\\
\mathbb{I}_{n-1,l}\end{bNiceMatrix}.

Taking into account that
l\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}
, we obtain:

-\begin{bNiceMatrix}\mathbb{I}_{n,0}&\dots&\mathbb{I}_{n,n-1}\end{bNiceMatrix}\begin{bNiceMatrix}\mathbb{I}_{0,0}&\Cdots&\mathbb{I}_{0,n-1}\\
\Vdots&&\Vdots\\
\mathbb{I}_{n-1,0}&\Cdots&\mathbb{I}_{n-1,n-1}\end{bNiceMatrix}^{-1}=\begin{bNiceMatrix}\Omega_{n,0}&\Cdots&\Omega_{n,n-1}\end{bNiceMatrix}.

Next, let us study the orthogonality conditions when

. In this case:

\int_{\Delta}\check{B}_{n}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\check{\mu}(x)\,\left(X_{[p]}^{\top}(x)\bm{e}_{n}\right)=\alpha_{n}\neq 0,

and substituting the

\mathbb{I}_{n,n}-\begin{bNiceMatrix}\mathbb{I}_{n,0}&\dots&\mathbb{I}_{n,n-1}\end{bNiceMatrix}\begin{bNiceMatrix}\mathbb{I}_{0,0}&\Cdots&\mathbb{I}_{0,n-1}\\

\mathbb{I}_{n-1,n}\end{bNiceMatrix}=\frac{\tilde{\tau}_{n}}{\tilde{\tau}_{n-1}}=\alpha_{n}\neq 0.

Therefore, the orthogonality relations are satisfied provided
\tilde{\tau}_{n-1}
and
\tilde{\tau}_{n}
are nonzero.

When
n={Np-r}
, the orthogonality conditions become:

-\begin{bNiceMatrix}\mathbb{I}_{{Np-r},0}&\dots&\mathbb{I}_{{Np-r},{Np-r}-1}\end{bNiceMatrix}\begin{bNiceMatrix}\mathbb{I}_{0,0}&\Cdots&\mathbb{I}_{0,{Np-r}-1}\\

This result, together with Proposition

, establishes that the non-cancellation of

is equivalent to the non-cancellation of
\tau_{{Np-r}-1}
. As the
\tau
-determinants were not defined for

, and disregarding

as a condition, we simplify the notation by dropping the

and referring to all determinants as

for

Theorem
2.12
extends to these
\tau
-determinants for

Proposition 3.4
.

If perturbed orthogonality exists, then
\tau_{n}\neq 0
for
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

Proof.

Assume orthogonality exists despite
\tau_{n}=0
for some
n\in\{0,\dots,{Np-r}-2\}
. Since
\tau_{n}=\alpha_{n}\tau_{n-1}
and
\tau_{n-1}\neq 0
, the only possibility is
\alpha_{n}=0
. However,

\int_{\Delta}\check{B}_{n}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\check{\mu}(x)\,\left(X_{[p]}^{\top}(x)\bm{e}_{n}\right)=\alpha_{n},

and orthogonality would not be satisfied.
∎

The assumption in Theorem
3.1
regarding the existence of certain orthogonality conditions for
B_{i}(x)
can be reformulated as the non-cancellation of the
\tau
-determinants for
n\in\{0,\dots,{Np-r}-2\}
. Consequently:

Theorem 3.5
.

If
\tau_{n}\neq 0
for
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, then the perturbed orthogonality exists.

Proof.

The assumption of non-cancellation of these determinants ensures the desired biorthogonality relations for
\check{B}_{n}(x)
when

, satisfying the conditions of Theorem
3.1
.
∎

Conclusions and Outlook

In this work, we have extended our previous study on general Christoffel transformations
[
40
]
to explore general Geronimus transformations for mixed multiple orthogonal polynomials. These transformations, characterized by regular matrix polynomials that are neither required to be monic nor constrained by the rank of their leading coefficients, were applied via both right and left multiplication to a matrix of measures. This approach led to Christoffel-type formulas that effectively describe how the perturbed orthogonal polynomials are related to the original ones. Importantly, we have also proven that the existence of the perturbed orthogonality is equivalent to the non-cancellation of certain
\tau
-determinants associated with the non-perturbed orthogonality.
The influence of these transformations on the Markov–Stieltjes matrix functions is also explored. As an example, we study the Jacobi–Piñeiro orthogonal polynomials with three weights.

A promising avenue for further exploration is the study of Uvarov transformations, which can be understood as a hybrid of Christoffel and Geronimus transformations, potentially offering a more comprehensive framework for perturbing orthogonality. We are also investigating the impact of these Uvarov transformations on the Markov–Stieltjes matrix functions associated with the corresponding matrix of measures.
