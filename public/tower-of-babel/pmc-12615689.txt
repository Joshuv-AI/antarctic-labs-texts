Influence of process parameters on surface roughness of laser powder bed fusion NiTi
Authors: Adriano Cebrian Carcavilla, Shrinivas Bojanampati, Wael Zaki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This paper investigates the influence of process parameters on the surface roughness of NiTi samples fabricated using laser powder bed fusion. The investigation uses a comprehensive full factorial design of experiments across wide ranges of scanning speed and laser power. The goal is to establish a direct correlation between surface finishings and manufacturing parameters. The coefficients obtained from the analytical fittings provide valuable insights into how roughness changes with processing parameters, highlighting the major change that is only observed with a significant variation in laser power. By leveraging machine learning algorithms to analyse experimental data, the limitations of traditional analytical methods are highlighted. The machine learning models trained on a subset of experimental data successfully predict the roughness of various NiTi specimens, enabling more efficient optimization of surface quality in additive manufacturing. The greater efficiency of machine learning models is emphasized, offering a significant advantage over conventional approaches and facilitating easier deployment of predictive tools. Moreover, an analysis of the results indicates that input energy density is not generally a reliable predictor of surface quality for the fabricated samples.

Introduction

Additive manufacturing (AM) has emerged as a transformative approach for fabricating NiTi (Nickel-Titanium) components, enabling the production of complex geometries and architected materials1,2 that are challenging to achieve through traditional subtractive methods, or even powder metallurgy3. This capability is particularly significant for NiTi, a material known for its unique shape memory and superelastic properties, as AM circumvents issues such as excessive tool wear caused by the material’s high hardness4. Recent studies have explored various aspects of additively manufactured NiTi, including phase transformation behaviours5, functional6,7 and structural8 fatigue, and the influence of processing parameters on material behaviour9,10. Notably, the formation of metastable R-phase during manufacturing has been identified as a critical factor affecting NiTi’s performance11,12. Despite advancements in AM technologies, the fabrication of NiTi components remains complex13, with process parameters such as laser power, scanning speed, hatch spacing, and layer thickness significantly impacting the quality of as-built specimens14,15. Surface roughness, in particular, is a critical consideration for applications where fatigue resistance is paramount, as smoother surfaces reduce stress concentrations and enhance structural durability, even when dealing with additively manufactured components of different materials, which exhibit unique roughness characteristics that can impact performance16,17. While post-processing techniques like laser polishing18,19 and electropolishing20,35 have been proposed to improve surface quality, they may inadvertently expose internal defects, potentially compromising fatigue life21,22. Thus, optimizing process parameters to achieve minimal roughness directly during fabrication is a key challenge.

Previous studies on the surface roughness of NiTi in additive manufacturing (AM) have provided valuable insights but are often limited in scope. For instance,20 investigated the influence of channel orientation in laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) NiTi actuators on roughness, finding that lower energy density enhances smoothness after electropolishing. Similarly,33 demonstrated that high laser power and scanning speed can reduce both roughness and porosity, suggesting optimal conditions for producing B2-NiTi. Other researchers have explored post-processing techniques to improve surface quality. In34, chemical etching was used to reduce the roughness of AM NiTi parts. Studies on related alloys, such as36, examined the roughness of AM Inconel 718 using a single set of parameters and employed electropolishing for surface improvement. While post-processing techniques like laser polishing18,19 and electropolishing20,35 have been proposed to improve surface quality, they may inadvertently expose internal defects, potentially compromising fatigue life21,22. Thus, optimizing process parameters to achieve minimal roughness directly during fabrication is a key challenge. In contrast to these prior efforts, which often rely on limited parameter sets or post-processing, this study adopts a comprehensive approach by employing a full factorial design of experiments. This method systematically investigates the effects of laser power and scanning speed on NiTi surface roughness across a wide range of parameters. By capturing complex interactions between these variables, the study reveals novel dependencies, such as the occurrence of minimal roughness at intermediate scanning speeds due to enhanced melt pool stability. It also challenges the conventional reliance on energy density as a predictor of surface quality, demonstrating its unreliability in this context. Furthermore, this work achieves lower surface roughness values across a broad parameter range compared to previous studies, which typically focused on single parameter sets. This broadens the operational window for AM NiTi, allowing for the optimization of other critical properties, such as phase transformation temperatures, without compromising surface quality. This study advances the field through three key novelties. First, it introduces predictive machine learning algorithms to analyze experimental data from AM NiTi, significantly enhancing predictive accuracy and efficiency compared to traditional analytical methods. This is evidenced by models trained on a subset of data that successfully predict roughness across diverse processing conditions. Second, it establishes a robust framework for optimizing process parameters, reducing the need for extensive and costly experimental testing by providing predictive tools applicable to NiTi and potentially other materials. These advancements culminate in the third novelty: a thorough identification of the interplay between processing parameters and their impact on surface roughness, offering deeper insights into the mechanisms governing AM NiTi fabrication.

Materials and methods

Nickel-rich NiTi powder of particle size between 15 and 53 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\upmu$$\end{document}m was procured from TLS Technik GmbH & Co. Spezialpulver KG. The chemical composition of the powder consists of 55.6 wt.% Ni, 0.008 wt.% C, 0.04 wt.% O, 0.002 wt.% N, 0.02 wt.% Fe, 0.0008 wt.% H, and a balance of Ti. The NiTi powder was used to fabricate dense NiTi samples, of cylindrical shape, using a metal 3D printer as laser powder bed fusion (L-PBF). This is an additive manufacturing technique that uses a laser to selectively melt and fuse metal powder spread evenly across a build platform within an inert gas environment to minimize oxidation. A layer of metal powder is deposited onto the build plate using a recoater blade. The laser then scans the powder bed, melting the material according to the cross-sectional geometry of the layer, allowing it to solidify and bond with the underlying substrate or previous layers. After each layer is completed, the build platform lowers, and a new layer of powder is applied. This cycle is repeated until the part is fully formed. A post-process step to remove the samples is usually carried out.

The fabricated samples are nominally 6 mm in height and 5 mm in diameter, and were printed on a NiTi substrate. The choice of the size of the samples is based on practical constraints at the time of printing. Due to the limited size of the substrates and the available NiTi powder, the number of printed samples was maximized to enable the identification of statistically meaningful trends across a comprehensive dataset. Spacing was maintained between samples to mitigate the risk of detached fragments interfering with adjacent samples, which could potentially halt the entire build process within the machine. The decision to use a NiTi substrate, rather than the commonly used stainless steel, was based on its superior metallurgical compatibility with the printed NiTi parts, particularly in terms of lattice structure and thermal expansion behavior. Preliminary experiments using a Ti-6Al-4V substrate resulted in sample detachment, likely due to residual stresses arising from lattice and thermal mismatch. While stainless steel substrates offer easier post-build removal, they pose risks of further thermal incompatibility and residual stresses, which can compromise the structural integrity of the printed parts. The NiTi substrate was therefore selected to ensure stronger interfacial bonding and more reliable build quality. Wire electro-discharge machining (EDM) was used for the removal of the samples from the substrate. Surface roughness evaluation

Roughness measurements were carried out on the top surface of each sample, normal to the build direction. Even though the lateral surface of the cylindrical samples is relevant for service conditions, the scope of this work is focused on the top surface. This is justified by both technical and scientific considerations. The curved geometry of the lateral surface presents significant challenges for accurate roughness measurement using the optical profilometer, which relies on overfocusing and underfocusing to capture surface topology effectively on flat surfaces. A smaller region could be considered, since it appears flatter, to minimize the noticeable effect of curvature. However, analyzing a small region of the curved surface would likely produce unrepresentative results. In contrast, the flat top surface, aligned parallel to the build plate, enables precise measurements and directly reflects the impact of laser powder bed fusion process parameters on layer quality, which is indicative of the bulk material properties.

The observed trend is explained by improved melting of the powder with increased energy input. However, it is observed that the influence of energy density is not as strong as the influence of laser power, if the other parameters are fixed in each case. In fact, for the highest laser powers, both roughness parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_a$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_a$$\end{document} reach their minimum values. The influence of energy density on roughness is significantly lower at these highest laser powers. Keeping a constant laser power at the higher values, roughness is marginally dependent on energy density, with only minor variations observed. This is based on the almost-horizontal line that is seen in Figs. 7 and 8. Moreover, Figs. 7 and 8 show that input energy does not fully determine roughness, as confirmed by the clear vertical spread in data points associated with the same energy density. Instead, roughness depends on the specific combination of laser power and scanning speed rather than energy density alone. In particular, the data suggest that laser power can be used as a modulator of roughness at a given energy density. This suggests that higher laser powers deepen the melt pool, remelting previous layers32 and smoothing the surface, while maintaining its influence on the roughness of top layers. At the same time, high scanning speeds may reduce this effect by limiting melt pool spread. At lower powers, insufficient energy restricts this remelting, resulting in higher roughness regardless of scanning speed adjustments.

Predictive models

Given the nature of the data, several models have been proposed to predict the roughness levels of AM NiTi based on various input parameters. These models aim to provide phenomenological insights into the impact of processing parameters on surface roughness.

On the one hand, an analytical approach is used, where a single functional form is employed to fit all models, with different parameters tailored for different input variables. To assess the feasibility of this approach, one specific case with a fixed energy density is studied first. On the other hand, different machine learning regression models are explored to evaluate their predictive capabilities through a supervised learning approach. In this way, the analytical method is compared to the data-driven machine learning approach.

The error metrics used to evaluate these models are the coefficient of determination, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2$$\end{document}, and the mean absolute error (MAE). These metrics are defined as per Eq. 8 and Eq. 9, respectively:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} R^2=1 - \frac{\sum _{i=1}^{n}(y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}{\sum _{i=1}^{n}(y_i - \bar{y})^2} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} MAE=\frac{1}{n} \sum _{i=1}^{n} |y_i - \hat{y}_i| \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where n is the number of observations, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y_i$$\end{document} is the observed value of the observation i, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{y}_i$$\end{document} is the predicted value for observation i and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\bar{y}$$\end{document} is the mean of the observed values y. The coefficient of determination is a metric that shows the proportion of the variance of the dependent variable that can be predicted from the independent variable. The MAE is the mean of the differences, in absolute value, between the actual values of a distribution and the predicted values. For the model with three sine terms, the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2$$\end{document} is 0.8155, and the MAE is 1.5736. In fact, the MAE is lower for the simpler model with only three sine terms, making it a more advantageous option for prediction due to both its fewer parameters and lower MAE. However, while analytical models demonstrate good performance, they still require calibration with multiple parameters. Despite their accuracy and robustness, these models can struggle to generalize to unseen data, and also fail to scale if dimensionality grows. In contrast, machine learning models offer significant advantages by automatically adapting to complex data patterns, without the need for rigid, predefined functional forms. Unlike analytical models, which are limited by their fixed structures and struggle to adapt to new data or scale effectively, machine learning excels in generalization. This allows the model to perform well on unseen data and easily scale to larger datasets. These advantages make machine learning a powerful alternative to traditional analytical methods, offering far greater flexibility, scalability, and performance in real-world applications.

Machine learning models

For this approach, the whole dataset is considered in an aggregated manner, i.e., all the information collected is used to train the machine learning models, in the way described below. The inputs to the model are the processing parameters: laser power, scanning speed and energy density. These are the most common variables reported in the literature, so the deployment of the models would be straightforward for the community. The consideration of three input parameters justifies the use of machine learning algorithms, with the intention of building a unique model to predict roughness in all the cases presented in the previous sections. This is an advantage over having only one input variable, like in the analytical approach. As aforementioned, the ranges of the processing parameters employed in this work include most of the successful NiTi prints reported in the literature. In addition, each sample has been printed independently from one another. The response is the roughness variable \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_a$$\end{document}. However, this can readily be extended to include roughness \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_a$$\end{document} as well.

Conclusions

The investigation into surface roughness of NiTi samples fabricated via laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) reveals the complex interplay of laser power, scanning speed, and energy density, and their collective influence on surface quality. This study demonstrates that roughness, measured as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_a$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_a$$\end{document}, is not dictated by a single parameter but by non-linear relationships and specific parameter combinations. For instance, roughness decreases sharply with increasing laser power, from approximately 32 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\upmu$$\end{document}m at 40 W to 5 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\upmu$$\end{document}m at 80 W, before stabilizing between 80 W and 200 W, with a similar trend at intermediate scanning speeds (400–1000 mm/s). Beyond these ranges, roughness rises due to melt pool instability or incomplete melting. Energy density alone fails to predict roughness uniquely, as identical values (e.g., 83.33 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {J/mm^3}$$\end{document}) yield varying outcomes depending on the balance of power and scanning speed.

The distinct roles of laser power and scanning speed are evident in melt pool dynamics. Optimal roughness (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim$$\end{document}5 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\upmu$$\end{document}m) occurs at 80–200 W and 400–1000 mm/s, where a stable, deep melt pool enhances surface smoothing via remelting. Conversely, high power with high speed (e.g., 240 W at >1000 mm/s) causes balling, while low power with low speed (e.g., 40 W at 135 mm/s) leads to incomplete fusion and higher roughness (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim$$\end{document}25 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\upmu$$\end{document}m). Analytical models, such as polynomial and harmonic fittings, highlight a dominant linear trend with laser power, yet require higher-order terms to capture thresholds (e.g., the drop up to 80 W or rise beyond 200 W). Machine learning models excel here, accurately predicting roughness across parameter combinations and reducing reliance on extensive testing.

These findings offer actionable guidance for LPBF NiTi fabrication. Parameter ranges of 80–200 W and 400–1000 mm/s consistently achieve minimal roughness (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim$$\end{document}5 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\upmu$$\end{document}m), providing a flexible window for optimizing surface quality and throughput. At fixed values of energy density, multiple combinations within this range yield comparable results, allowing process tailoring. However, exceeding 240 W or 1000 mm/s risks defects, necessitating precise control. Fixed parameters, like hatch spacing at 80 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\upmu$$\end{document}m, layer thickness at 30 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\upmu$$\end{document}m, suggest further optimization potential. A smaller hatch spacing could improve smoothness at low powers, while thicker powder layers might exacerbate instability at high speeds. By leveraging these insights and predictive models, manufacturers can minimize trial-and-error, targeting parameters for scalable, high-quality NiTi production.
