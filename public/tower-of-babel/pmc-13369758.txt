Subchronic toxicity of 222 nm ultraviolet irradiation in rats for safe human translation and application guidelines
Authors: Jin Li, Yuxin Su, Chun Xu, Qiang Wei, Feng Gao, Qiuhua Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the long-term safety of 222 nm far-ultraviolet light, a promising technology for continuous disinfection that is believed to be safe for human exposure. To address concerns about its practical efficacy and potential long-term effects, an accelerated 90-day toxicological study was conducted on rats. The animals were exposed to graded doses of 222 nm light: high (5000 mJ/cm2/day), medium (1000 mJ/cm2/day), and low (500 mJ/cm2/day). The results were dose-dependent. The high-dose group exhibited significant adverse effects, including skin damage (inflammatory cell infiltration, thickening), systemic weight loss, and thymic atrophy. In stark contrast, the low and medium-dose groups showed no abnormal clinical symptoms, behavioral changes, or weight abnormalities throughout the entire study. Comprehensive analysis of hematological, biochemical, and histopathological indicators confirmed no toxicologically relevant changes at these lower doses. Based on these findings, the study established a No Observed Adverse Effect Level (NOAEL) of 1000 mJ/cm2/day for subchronic exposure in rats. This indicates that continuous exposure to 222 nm far-UV light at or below this dose level is not associated with any detectable adverse biological effects, supporting its potential for safe use in occupied spaces.Keywords: 222nm far-UVC, Toxicity safety, Germicidal efficacySubject terms: Diseases, Drug discovery, Environmental sciences, Medical research

Introduction

Limiting airborne disease transmission in occupied public spaces remains a critical public health challenge, particularly in light of lessons from the COVID-19 pandemic, seasonal influenza risks, and persistent threats such as tuberculosis. Improved engineering controls can significantly reduce transmission rates. Among these, the use of UVC light to directly inactivate airborne pathogens is one of the most promising approaches. Conventional germicidal UVC at 254 nm was first successfully applied for air disinfection in the 1940s1,2. However, its implementation was restricted to upper-room installations to avoid human overexposure, thereby limiting its widespread adoption.

In search of safer and more effective alternatives, scientists have recently explored far-UVC wavelengths within the 200–235 nm range. Initial studies using 222 nm Krypton Chloride (KrCl) excimer lamps demonstrated that far-UVC could inactivate pathogens more effectively than 254 nm lamps3, while causing minimal harm when appropriate optical filtering is used4,5. The Project Research Center for Nosocomial Infectious Diseases at Hiroshima University reported that, at an irradiation distance of 24 cm, doses of 1 and 3 mJ/cm2 of 222-nm UV-C irradiation (0.1 mW/cm2 for 10 and 30 s respectively) achieved 88.5% and 99.7% reductions in viable SARS-CoV-2, as measured by the TCID50 assay6.The research team from Columbia University demonstrated that low-doses of 1.7 and 1.2 mJ/cm2 inactivated 99.9% of aerosolized human coronaviruses 229E and OC43, respectively. Given that all human coronaviruses have similar genomic sizes, far-UVC light would be expected to show similar inactivation efficiency against other human coronaviruses, including SARS-CoV-2. Based on the beta-HCoV-OC43 results, continuous far-UVC exposure in occupied public locations at the current regulatory exposure limit of 3 mJ/cm2 per hour would result in 90% viral inactivation in 8 min, 95% in 11 min, 99% in 16 min and 99.9% inactivation in 25 min7.Although extensive experimental data demonstrate that low-dose 222 nm ultraviolet light can effectively inactivate bacteria and viruses, practical application scenarios require significantly higher irradiation doses than laboratory measurements to achieve rapid pathogen inactivation due to factors including installation constraints of disinfection fixtures, variations in ambient temperature and humidity, differences in material surface properties, and overall energy consumption considerations.

Factors including installation constraints of disinfection fixtures, variations in ambient temperature and humidity, differences in material surface properties, and overall energy consumption collectively require operational doses substantially exceeding experimental values.

Recent years have witnessed a growing focus on the research and application of medium-to-high dose 222 nm ultraviolet radiation. Although its disinfection efficacy is well-recognized, toxicological safety continues to be a paramount concern. A recent study from the Department of Ophthalmology at the Faculty of Medicine of Shimane University provided critical toxicological insights. Using high-output KrCl excimer lamps in rat model, the researchers determined that the minimum photokeratitis threshold doses for 222 nm light ranges between 3500 and 5000 mJ/cm2. Furthermore, they reported that cyclobutane pyrimidine dimers (CPDs) observed in the corneal epithelium following 222-nm UV-C irradiated were no longer detected within 12 h8. Another simulation study using Monte Carlo radiation transfer codes and a 5- layer skin model indicated that the 222 nm wavelength does not result in CPDs formation in the basal layer of the epidermis, results suggest that 222 nm UV-C solely does not cause DNA damage in the basal layer of the epidermis but does with UV-C ranging between 230 and 280 nm9. Although the American Conference of Governmental Industrial Hygienists (ACGIH) has raised the Threshold Limit Value (TLV) for 222 nm ultraviolet radiation from 22 mJ/cm2 to 160 mJ/cm210–12, a critical need remains for further toxicological evaluation. Given the prospective use of far-UVC as a germicidal irradiation (UVGI) technology, it is important to re-evaluate the damage thresholds of 222 nm radiation on skin and eyes, as well as its potential risks to deeper organs from subchronic, cumulative exposure.

This study employed low, medium, and high gradient doses of 222 nm ultraviolet radiation to conduct a continuous 90-day exposure experiment on Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats. Through systematic observation of effects on skin, blood, organs, and other physiological systems, we revealed the low-hazard nature of 222-nm UV-C. Our findings further support the potential safety of employing higher irradiation doses in future applications.

Materials and methods

Design

This prospective observational study included clinical, ophthalmological, hematological, and blood biochemical examinations, gross autopsy, and histopathological evaluation of SD rats. All experimental protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Zhongguancun International Medical Inspection and Certification Technology Co., Ltd. and were conducted in accordance with GB/T 42,011 − 2022 Laboratory animals—General code of animal welfare, GB/T 35,892 − 2018 Laboratory animal—Guideline for ethical review of animal welfare.

Individual body weights were maintained within ± 20% of the gender-specific average. The experiment comprised five dose groups: a low-dose group (500 mJ/cm2), a medium-dose group (1000 mJ/cm2), a high-dose group (5000 mJ/cm2), a positive control group (500 mJ/cm2), and a blank group (0 mJ/cm2).

Due to the absence of a standardized methodology for phototoxicity assessment, the design of dose groups was based on the safety threshold for dermal irradiation with 222 nm ultraviolet light, as recommended by ACGIH, which stipulates a maximum daily exposure limit of 478 mJ/cm2. Furthermore, by extrapolating animal experimental data to human exposure scenarios and applying a safety factor of 10, the highest dose group was consequently established at 5000 mJ/cm2 (Table 1).

UV-C irradiation device design

The irradiation apparatus was designed to ensure consistent physical parameters including light source type, irradiation method, and mounting configuration. The distance between the UV sources and the cage floor was adjustable to compensate for potential output attenuation over time(Fig. 1).

Irradiation device.

Concurrently, we designed the distribution of the light source and simulated its irradiation effects to ensure uniformity within the irradiation zone throughout the experiment(Fig. 2).

Simulated radiation irradiance distribution diagram.

Clinical observation and parameter monitoring

Animals were acclimatized for 5 days pre-irradiation under controlled conditions: ambient temperature 20–26 °C, relative humidity 30–70%, and a 12 h:12 h light-dark cycle. They were housed by sex, five per cage, with ad libitum access to food and water.

All groups were monitored for 90 consecutive days. Daily clinical examinations documented all toxicological manifestations, including onset, severity (graded 1–4), and duration. Body weight and feed consumption were recorded weekly. Ophthalmic exams were conducted pre-irradiation and after 90 days. On day 90, blood samples were collected with anticoagulant for hematological and biochemical analyses. A complete gross necropsy was performed on all animals. Histopathological assessments were conducted on irradiated skin and multiple organs.

Statistics

Data were analyzed using SPSS 22.0. Normality (Shapiro-Wilk) and homogeneity of variance (Levene’s test) were assessed. For parametric data, One-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s t-test was used; for non-parametric data, the Dunnett’s T3 test was employed. The NOAEL was determined based on statistical results, physiological relevance, toxicological significance, and dose-response relationships.

Results

Clinical observation

The irradiation of animals in this experiment began on November 20, 2024.From day 5, some animals in the high-dose and positive control groups exhibited skin darkening with a light yellow hue. By day 9, all animals in these groups were affected. From day 13, localized desquamation occurred on one animal in the positive control and high-dose group; Eventually, all animals in this group exhibited recurrent peeling until day 90.From day 21, two females in the medium-dose group also showed similar changes. No obvious symptoms were observed on the skin of rats in the low-dose group.Since SD rats lack melanin, the discoloration is likely due to protein photodegradation or oxidation of skin lipids into yellow peroxides.

All dose groups showed reduced activity, feeding, and drinking during irradiation, normalizing shortly after daily exposure.The images comparing the skin symptoms of animals in different dosage groups and the positive control group with the blank control group during the experiment are shown in Fig. 3.No deaths occurred in any animals during the experiment.

Blood biochemical examination results

Compared to the blank control group, the groups after irradiation showed the following statistically significant changes:

Low-dose group

Male rats exhibited a significant decrease in blood urea nitrogen levels (Urea; **p < 0.01) and a significant increase in sodium levels (Na; *p < 0.05).

Medium-dose group

Female rats showed a significant increase in potassium levels (K; **p < 0.01), while males demonstrated a significant increase in chloride levels (Cl; *p < 0.05) and a significant decrease in potassium levels (K; **p < 0.01).

High-dose group

Females had a significant decrease in albumin levels (ALB; *p < 0.05) and glucose levels (Glu; *p < 0.05), along with increased in aspartate aminotransferase levels (AST; **p < 0.01).

Males showed significantly decreased in albumin levels (ALB; *p < 0.05) and in total bilirubin levels (TBil; *p < 0.05), and increased in AST levels (*p < 0.05).

Positive control group

Females displayed significantly decreased in ALB levels (**p < 0.01) and Glu levels (*p < 0.05), and significantly increased alanine aminotransferase levels (ALT; **p < 0.01) and AST levels (*p < 0.05).

Males exhibited significantly decreased ALB levels (**p < 0.01), TBil levels (**p < 0.01), and in total protein levels (TP; *p < 0.05), along with increased in AST levels (*p < 0.05) (Table 4).

Discussion

Recent studies have demonstrated that 222-nm far-UVC irradiation exhibits considerable potential for inactivating pathogens-including SARS-CoV-2-on surfaces and in air, with accumulating evidence supporting its high degree of biological safety for skin and ocular tissues at exposure levels within current guidelines. However, further investigation is necessary to elucidate the maximum safe exposure thresholds for long-term application to optimize practical implementation13,14.

A subchronic (90-day) irradiation toxicity test was conducted on SD rats using a 222-nm UV-C generator to identify potential target organs and establish no-observed-adverse-effect levels (NOAELs). The experimental design included low, medium, and high dose groups (500 mJ/cm2/day, 1000 mJ/cm2/day, and 5000 mJ/cm2/day), along with a blank control group and a positive control group. Each group consisted of 20 SD rats with equal gender distribution (half male and half female).

The results demonstrated that no clinically significant changes were observed in all hematological, biochemical, and histopathological indicators in the low-dose and medium dose groups. Only the high-dose group and positive control group showed toxicologically significant alterations in parameters including body weight, ophthalmological examination, white blood cell count, thymus organ weight, histopathological assessment, and dermatopathological evaluation. Under the conditions of this test, the no-observed-adverse-effect level (NOAEL) of the 222-nm UV-C ultraviolet generator in the subchronic (90-day) irradiation toxicity test on SD rats was determined to be 1000 mJ/cm2/day.

During the experimental design phase, relevant animal studies conducted by other institutions were thoroughly reviewed and referenced. For instance, Buonanno et al. exposed hairless mice to 254-nm UV-C and 222-nm UV-C radiation at a dose of 157 mJ/cm2, evaluating cyclobutane pyrimidine dimers (CPDs) and 6 − 4 photoproducts as biomarkers of DNA damage. The results demonstrated that CPDs and 6 − 4 photoproducts were exclusively generated following 254-nm UV-C irradiation, confirming the induction of DNA damage5. Another study similarly reported that even subchronic exposure to 222-nm UV-C at 450 mJ/cm2/day for 10 consecutive days did not induce DNA damage in mice15. Furthermore, research from the Kobe University Graduate School of Medicine confirmed that 222-nm UV-C irradiation at 500 mJ/cm2 did not elicit erythema16. Building upon these existing research findings and aligning with the objectives of the current study, three radiation dose groups were established.

This study has verified a no-observed-adverse-effect level (NOAEL) of 1000 mJ/cm2/day for 222-nm UV-C radiation in a 90-day subchronic toxicity test using SD rats. However, systemic alterations—including thymic atrophy—were observed. It remains unclear whether these effects stem from direct radiation exposure or are secondary to localized skin injury, due to the lack of validated biomarkers for specific target organ exposure. Further investigation into the penetration properties of 222-nm ultraviolet light is warranted to elucidate its underlying mechanisms of action. Additional experiments are required to determine the safety threshold for radiation doses between 1000 and 5000 mJ/cm2/day. Moreover, practical applications must consider factors such as the spectral purity of the 222-nm light source and the structural differences between SD rat and human skin.

Currently, 222-nm UV-C is used at doses below the current ACGIH TLV. However, if higher exposure doses of 222-nm UV-C were permitted, its germicidal efficacy would increase and its application scene might become more widespread. It will be necessary to reconsider the far-UVC TLV based on these current studies to permits more effective utilization of far-UVC radiation. We hope that our results will play an important role in this updating process.
