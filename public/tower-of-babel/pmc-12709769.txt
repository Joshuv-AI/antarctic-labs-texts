Impact of a 25-minute nap on cognitive variables and reactive management of disruptive behaviour in trainee secondary school physical education teachers
Authors: Talel Maddeh, Inès Souid, Hamdi Snoussi, Raouf Hammami, Mert Kurnaz, Mustafa Altınkök
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbstractSleep deprivation in trainee teachers (TTs) increases the risk of cognitive disorders specific to teaching. The aim of this study was to examine the effects of a 25-minute nap on certain cognitive faculties in these teachers, with a view to creating a learning climate more conducive to the transfer of knowledge. Sixty male TT (21.77 ± 0.63 years ; height : 1.79 ± 0.03 m) were randomly divided into two groups: an experimental group (ExpGr ; n = 30) and a control group (ContGr ; n = 30). A 25-minute nap protocol was administered to the ExpGr. An experimental design (3 months) with repeated measures (3 samplings: T1, T2, T3) was adopted to measure sustained attention (d2 test), complex reaction times (CRT: BlazePod test) and the choice of reactive management interventions (Sieber model, 2001) against student disruptive behavior (DB) during teaching. A significant increase in attention for ExpGr vs. ContGr following an improvement in the global performance index (GPI) for P < 0.001. The same was true for the CRT variable, reporting a significant increase in favour of ExpGr for p < 0.001. The progression of ExpGr is even more marked in relation to the use of reactive management interventions against disciplinary acts. The new pattern of interventions adopted by ExpGr is characterised by a significant reduction (p < 0.001) in those involving Remontrance, clarifications and indirect interventions, thus generating a significant reduction (p < 0.001) in the number of fractures in the pedagogical act in favour of its continuity during teaching. A 25-minute nap led to significant improvements in the TT’s cognitive faculties (CRT and attention) during teaching, which made it possible to better choose the most appropriate interventions to manage the DB during teaching and to create a better learning climate for the transfer of knowledge characterised by the maintenance of pedagogical continuity.Trial registrationThis trial was retrospectively registered with the International Standard Randomised Controlled Trial Number (ISRCTN) on 21/08/2025 (Registration Number: ISRCTN95971464).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03673-1.Keywords: Trainee teachers, Nap, Cognitive faculties, Managing indiscipline, Physical education

Introduction

Teaching is considered to be one of the hardest professions [1, 2] because of the many demands that have to be managed in a limited space of time, such as preparing lessons, managing time and space, and managing the various interactions within the class group, the pupils and their socio-economic contexts [3–5]. All these demands are classified under the umbrella of classroom management, which is often a challenge in teachers’ professional lives [6, 7]. Falling under the fifth dimension of classroom management [7], the management of indiscipline or disruptive behaviour (DB) sees itself as one of the first tools whose degree of effectiveness can have positive consequences on the transfer of learning during pedagogical acts [8, 9].

This is why it often requires experienced teachers and trainee teachers (TT) to be intensely involved in choosing the most appropriate response to an apparent act of indiscipline. Sieber [10] presents a method of intervention called reactive management for dealing with DB when it occurs. This is subdivided into five dimensions or generic forms : remontrance, clarification, indirect management, diversion and ignoring, which can be described as strategic [7] (appendix 3). These generic forms can be used by teachers depending on the nature and intensity of the indisciplinary behaviour that arises during lessons, but also according to the circumstances and in consideration of the characteristics and background of the students concerned. This reactive management then implies a differentiated management of undisciplined behaviour, which is perhaps more difficult for the trainee teacher [8] or for the supply teacher because they cannot rely as strongly as the regular teacher on the dimension of historicity [9, 11]. This differentiated aspect of reactive management consists in the fact of taking into consideration, when choosing the intervention, the degree of intrusion (the extent to which it can disturb the smooth running of the teaching) of the undisciplined behaviour, its source, the context, and then weighing up the consequences of the choice of whether or not to break up or disrupt the teaching. This is why this type of DB management requires beginner or experienced teachers to put a great deal of effort into cognitive functions such as concentration, vigilance and reflective analysis [12]. These cognitive functions are geared towards capturing the facts and choosing the intervention to be carried out against DBs at the right moments when they appear, with the aim of intervening effectively against undisciplined pupils [13–15]. In this context of managing indiscipline, physical education (PE) classes become more complex as a result of their nature, which is based on bodily engagement combining close proximity between pupils and an aggressive competitive nature [16].

In relation to the specificity of the five forms of reactive management [13, 14], remontrance, clarification and indirect management involve creating a break in pedagogical continuity, which proponents of the classroom ecology approach [17] call an interruption in the vector of instruction (vector 1). Each interruption constitutes a risk that TT need to weigh up carefully, as it entails stopping processes of commitment whose restart is always uncertain [11], very often affecting their feelings of personal self-efficacy when they take up their vocations [18]. This is why TT may be tempted to favour the continuity of the instructional vector by adopting less intrusive intervention techniques such as diversion or strategic ignoring in the hope of diverting pupils’ attention from the undisciplined behaviour(s) displayed while preserving their engagement in learning situations [10, 13].

In order to overcome these challenges, TT are often confronted with sleep deprivation [19] caused by the multiple focus of managing their responsibilities in parallel with their university studies [20]. Based on the principle that sleep deprivation negatively affects human cognitive faculties [21], such as concentration, attention or vigilance, the possibility of failure during teaching is increased [19, 22–25] and a decrease in the general well-being of the teacher [26]. Several observations made by the pedagogical unit at the Tunis Higher Institute for Sport and Physical Education reveal signs of sleepiness among TT during active supervision of their teaching courses. This observation supports the hypothesis that the introduction of a nap protocol for PEs might lead to improvements in their cognitive faculties with regard to the choice of interventions when managing indiscipline.

For further clarification, reactive management, more specifically intervention against disruptive behaviour [10, 18] in the school context, is frequently compromised by sleep deprivation, a common problem among pre-service teachers [19, 20]. While a great deal of research has examined the impact of sleep deprivation on general cognitive functions [19, 23, 27], there is a notable gap in the specific study of the effects of napping on cognitive skills relating to classroom management, in particular those relating to the fifth dimension, namely the management of disruptive behaviour during teaching. Furthermore, the majority of studies focus mainly on the immediate effects [21, 22, 28] and little on the potential benefits of a regular nap intervention in the long term. It is therefore crucial to fill this gap by investigating in more detail how a nap protocol planned over a school year could influence vigilance, attention and the ability to manage classroom behaviour effectively, as well as its direct impact on the learning climate. It is from this new perspective that this study aims to explore these little-studied dimensions in order to enrich our understanding of the interactions between sleep, cognitive abilities and the management of disruptive behaviour.

In view of the above, the aim of this study was to explore the effects of the 25 min nap on cognitive abilities - more specifically sustained attention, complex reaction time (CRT) and the realisation of intervention choices against disruptive behaviour during teaching sessions - in trainee physical education teachers.

Materials and methods

Participants

This study was conducted in compliance with the CONSORT guidelines. Our sample consisted of sixty male trainee physical education teachers (21.77 ± 0.63 years; height : 1.79 ± 0.03 m) who had been trained (60 h; appendix 1 and 2) according to Sieber’s [10] model of indiscipline management [13] (Fig. 1). This choice was imposed by the conditions of the vocational training programme, since only male students were assigned to the establishments, which were located in working-class neighbourhoods known for their frequent disruptive behaviour. Female students were not assigned, in order to protect them, at least during their first years of experience. TT’s benefit from a period of preparation for working life, designed as a complete experience that fully simulates the role of a full teacher. During this period, they take on the full range of classroom management responsibilities, such as leading lessons, managing pupil behaviour and implementing teaching activities. Their responsibilities cover an entire school year, including planning teaching cycles, drawing up the annual calendar, and formative and summative assessment of pupils. The aim is to strengthen their autonomy and their ability to organise and run a school effectively, while developing their pedagogical and administrative skills. This highly demanding pedagogical approach is chosen because of the growing complexity of the education system, incorporating increased optional requirements to ensure the training of highly competent teachers, able to meet the contemporary challenges of education.

For reasons of representativeness, a minimum sample size of 59 was determined from an a priori statistical power analysis using G*Power (Version 3.1, University of Dusseldorf, Dusseldorf, Germany) based on the t-test family (means: the difference between two dependent means). The analysis output showed that a sample size of 59 subjects would be sufficient to identify significant differences (effect size = 0.48, power (1 - β) = 0.95 with an actual power of 95.20) in this study. This sample was randomly subdivided into two groups : an experimental group (ExpGr; n = 30) and a control group (ContGr; n = 30) (Fig. 1). In order not to bias the results, our selection criteria stipulated acceptance of non-smokers, non-drinkers of alcoholic beverages and those who gave their consent not to drink caffeine during the experiment. Sleep diaries were collected (bedtime, from 22 :30 to 6 :30 ± 1 :00), and only trainees with a score ≤ 5 according to the Pittsburgh sleep quality index [29] were included. On the other hand, the same successful candidates agreed to align themselves with the time they went to bed at night (22 :30) and the time they woke up (6 :30 in the morning). These selected volunteers followed a practical training course in a secondary school environment during the last year of their 2022/2023 university studies (Bachelor-Master-Doctorate regime). These two groups were assigned to three secondary schools in the same neighbourhood that could be described as difficult. All the participants were informed beforehand of the aims of the study, which were to cover the two seasons 2021/2022 and 2022/2023, and of the measures taken to preserve their anonymity and the confidentiality of the data collected.

A total of 1680 secondary school pupils (age : 13.43 ± 0.49 years), 806 of whom were male (47.9%) and 874 female (52.1%), were observed during the study, with an average of 28 pupils per class. Written parental consent was obtained from all the parents of the pupils involved in the study. They were all involved in individual sports activities within their colleges. In addition, the present study was conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki. The protocol was approved by the Research Unit Physical Activity, Sport and Health – UR18JS01 National Sports Observatory – Tunis Research Ethics Committee (CPP No. 01136/2022).

Experimental protocol

D2 test

The d2 test for the measurement of sustained attention is a test of discernment of detail that is independent of the intelligence of the person being treated. This test is applicable to ages ranging from 9 to 90 years. Its aim is to test an individual’s visual attention and ability to concentrate. It is characterised by the speed and accuracy with which the results are analysed during the test. The strength of the d2 test lies in the fact that it takes no longer than eight minutes to administer to the people concerned, and can be taken either individually or in a group. It consists of 14 lines of 47 letters, each formed by the letters (d) and (p). The test consists of crossing out the letter (d) accompanied by an English quotation mark (“) placed above or below the letter, or an apostrophe (‘) placed above or below the same letter. All other structures of these two letters must be ignored, within a time limit of 20 seconds per line. Given the meticulous execution of this test, the subjects often find themselves in a conflicting situation combining speed of execution and the need not to make mistakes. It is in this context that the ability to concentrate is highlighted by the d2 test. The first is called ‘GZ’, which represents the quantitative performance index. This correspond to the total number of characters processed, regardless of whether they are characters that have to be selected, or decisive characters, or characters that do not have to be selected, or non-decisive characters performed. The second, called F, represents the gross error index, which is the total sum of all the errors made during the d2 test. Note here that F is equal to the sum of the ‘F1’ errors designating the determining characters that were not identified and the ‘F2’ errors relating to the non-determining characters ticked by mistake, raising the problem of confusion among the subjects performing the test. The last variable in the d2 test is the overall performance index (IPG), which mathematically consists of GZ - (F1 + F2) and refers to the overall quality of the subject’s performance. For the purposes of this article, we have used this global performance index to determine the quality of the subject’s performance.

BlazePod test

The BlazePod test was used to measure the complex reaction time (CRT) of TT in the two study groups. The BlazePod consists of a test that provides total stimulation of the body and brain, based on the stimulus/response process. This process is ensured by two essential elements that cannot function without each other : the BlazePod application (data collection) accompanied by its light ‘Pods’ (visual stimulation devices using light signals). Connected via Bluetooth to a smartphone (or tablet), the Pods are controlled from the BlazePod application, which provides a number of exercises predefined by its exploration system. Acting as a touch-sensitive visual cue, the Pods trigger the body’s natural response to sensory stimulation according to the programme chosen by the operator. For our article, the test used to measure CRT consisted of each TT trying to touch the BlazePod sensor as quickly as possible when one of the four sensors used in the test lit up red. The starting position for the TT was to stand in front of a wall on which the four square sensors, 30 centimetres wide and 1.5 metres high, had been placed. The test consisted of the TTconcerned using his or her strong hand to try to touch the sensor that lit up in as short a time as possible once the red light came on. In total, five repetitions were performed by each TT for a random delay of 2000 milliseconds (ms) programmed into the device. The results were instantly stored in milliseconds (ms) on a tablet linked by wifi to the target sensor. The average of the five times was used in our research. This test has a confidence level of 95% [30].

[13–15], evolves with an interventionist tendency, allowing for a dual objective, the first consisting of producing a classification of learners’ acts of indiscipline and the second summarising the strategic choices intended for those involved in their management. The model was designed as a pedagogical aid for teachers, with a view to managing indiscipline in the classroom, taking into account the fact that pupils’ behavioural problems arise mainly during school activities and must therefore be dealt with as a priority in this specific context. One of the main strengths of this model lies in the fact that it has not been specifically contextualised culturally, which broadens its methodology and facilitates its application in various educational environments. As a model of differentiated management of indiscipline, it is based on an approach combining three strategies : preventive, to anticipate and reduce undesirable behaviour; organisational, to structure the classroom and establish a climate of trust; and reactive, to intervene appropriately when incidents occur, which is the subject of this study. This flexible, integrated approach makes it possible to maintain a calm school environment that is conducive to learning, while being adapted to the different situations encountered in the educational environment.

To carry out these measurements, we used the technique of deferred observation (analysis of video recordings) using an observation grid (Appendix 3) derived from Sieber’s [10] model representing a matrix cross-referencing the different apparent DBs with the forms of reactive management used by the trainee teacher at each moment when a DB appears during teaching. The protocol used stipulates that in order to be counted, each instance of indiscipline should last between 5 and 30 s. If the incident is perceived by the trainee, it is entered directly into the observation grid with its respective code. The second approach consists of inserting the form of reactive management used by the same trainee to counteract it. If this is not the case (DB not perceived by the trainee), the fact is deemed non-codable.

Experimental procedure

The experiment was conducted over three months (from February 1 to the end of April) using a longitudinal model, with three intakes for each individual (T0 : February1, T1 : March 15 and T2 : end of April). Each experimental day began with an information session at midday, followed by a standardised lunch. Participants in the ExpGr group took a 25-minute nap in a dedicated room to improve cognitive recovery, while participants in the ContGr group rested under normal conditions. Both groups then underwent tests of attention (d2 test) and SRT (BlazePod test), followed by participation in supervised teaching sessions. During these sessions, the DB variables and the reactive management interventions of the trainee teachers were collected using six camcorders (Sony, Model; Handcam 4 K) with a built-in projector and six wireless microphones with transmitting receivers (BoomTone DJ, VHF 10HL F4 Micro H.F) with a range of 50 m to capture their verbal interventions of the filmed teachers.

A total of 180 sessions of 40-minute group sports activities were filmed and 180 grids of the d2 test collected at the three sampling times (T0, T1 and T2 respectively) were analysed with the averages of the complex reaction times of each trainee teacher. Figure 2 summarises the experimental phase, which began at midday after a standardised lunch for the two study groups. The test for the measurement of complex reaction time was carried out first, followed by that of d2 for the measurement of attentional variation and finally a collection of data through the use of delayed observation concerning the modalities of their choice of interventions against the apparent DBs during their teaching lessons.

Data analysis and processing

Referring to the technique of off-line observation [13–15], the analysis of our data was done by means of a matrix representing Sieber’s [10] observation grid crossing the 4 categories of disruptive behaviours with the five forms of reactive management, thus allowing the capture of research data. These data were then analysed statistically using SPSS version 24 software. The analysis consisted of counting the absolute frequencies (f) of the four categories of DBs and the different forms of reactive management according to the grid used. The normality of the distribution of the various variables in our study was verified using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests. The two-factor ANOVA test (group x time interaction) was used to compare changes in the means of the various forms of reactive management, the global performance index (GPI) of the d2 test and the complex reaction time (CRT), between the three sampling times. The intragroup comparison of the same variables mentioned above between the three sampling times taken in pairs (T0 versus T1, T0 versus T2 and T1 versus T2) was carried out using the repeated measures ANOVA test with Bonferroni adjustment. The significance threshold was set at p < 0.05.

Inter-coder reliability

To ensure inter-coder reliability, three investigators were specially trained in the coding procedure for the observation grid derived from Sieber’s model [10]. The calculation of the probability based on the comparison of the perceptions of the different results following the coding of three teaching sessions taken at random from the 180 that were filmed, gave an average concordance of 82% on the 71 disciplinary incidents retained. According to Fortin and Gagnon [31], this inter-coder agreement rate is considered reliable. In order to better appreciate the concordance between them, the Fleiss Kappa (KFleiss) was used in the background to compare the coding of the same three independent analysts on 71 DB chosen at random from all the empirical material corresponding to the four categories of Sieber’s [10] indiscipline management model. The results obtained indicate that we can be confident in the coding fidelity since KFleiss= 0.7689 >(SE = 0.051; lower 95% asymptotic CI bound = 0.689; upper 95% asymptotic CI bound = 0.895). Assuming that there are no rules of thumb [32] to judge the quality of the KFleiss value. It must therefore take into account the marginal distributions used to calculate the proportion of chance agreement. Here, the strength of agreement based on Cohen’s Kappa value is between 0.68 and 0.8, which is considered good according to Altman’s [33] guidelines.

Effect of the 25-minute nap sustained attention

The results in Table 1 show that there was no significant difference between ExpGr and ContGr at T0 for the d2 test of sustained attention (F = 0.078, p = 0.922). This proves that the two groups had the same characteristics at the start of the experiment (T0). The results at T1 (F = 3376.822, p < 0.001) and T2 (F = 18131.124, p < 0.001) show significant differences in favour of the ExpGr compared with the ContGr. In addition, the mean GPIs seemed to have progressed better from T0 to T2 on the ExpGr side (respectively T0 = 141.0 ± 2.7, T1 = 256.0 ± 8.7, T2 = 343.0 ± 4.41) compared with those of the ContGr (T0 = 142.0 ± 2.7, T1 = 140.0 ± 3.3, T2 = 145.0 ± 7.6). These results show a clear improvement in ExpGr compared with ContGr caused by the addition of the nap before work. The increase in GPI proves that the nap gave the ExpGr the alternative of increasing the quantitative performance index (GZ) and reducing the number of F faults (F1 + F2) during the d2 test. This necessarily points to an improvement in the sustained concentration of these same trainee teachers following the practice of the nap before carrying out the pedagogical acts. A more detailed analysis of the different sampling times taken in pairs confirms once again that the ExpGr improved significantly from T0 to T1, from T0 to T2 and from T1 to T2 (p ≤ 0.001), unlike the ContGr, which did not improve for the same sampling times taken in pairs (Table 1).

Effect of the 25-minute nap on complex reaction time

The results in Table 2 shows that at the start of the experiment (T0), the differences between ExpGr and ContGr were non-significant (F = 0.56 and p = 0.832). This highlights the fact that these two groups had the same CRT characteristics. The differences became significant between the two groups at T1 (F = 821.212, p = < 0.001) and T2 (F = 1421.235, p < 0.001). The ExpGr improved its performance by decreasing its CRT from T0 = 466 ± 12 ms to T1 = 353 ± 6ms and T2 = 256 ± 13ms. These results are totally opposed to those of the ContGr, which recorded non-significant results reporting a stagnation of the CRT (T0 = 468 ± 13 ms; T1 = 432 ± 10 ms; T2 = 408 ± 11 ms). The results reported using repeated measures ANOVA still demonstrate that the progressions only affected the ExpGr compared with the ContGr for values of p ≤ 0.001 (T0 versus T1, T1 versus T2 and T0 versus T2). This argue in favour of a positive impact following the addition of our nap protocol to the ExpGr, as illustrated by their significant progress during the sampling times taken in pairs. We can therefore say that the nap reduced the ability of the experimental group (Versus ContGr) to analyse the apparent DBs more quickly, by enabling them to decide more quickly on the targeted intervention to solve the problem.

Effect of the 25-minute nap on the reactions emitted by the stagires to the encostern of disturbing behaviour

The results in Table 3 shows that, at the start of the T0 experiment, the averages for the use of the different forms of reactive management for ExpGr vs. ContGr were not significant, and vice versa for the forms of remonstrance, clarification, indirect, diversion and ignorance. This confirms that there was no difference between the two study groups with regard to the ability to make strategic choices on the different forms of reactive management (as opposed to DB’s) before the addition of our nap protocol. The results become significant in T1 and T2, indicating differences between ExpGr and ContGr in the average frequency of use of the different forms of reactive management. In this context, it appears that ExpGr had a greater reduction in the use of the reprimand form, clarification and the so-called indirect form. For the diversionary form, the results show that it was still the ExpGr that increased the average use of this form. Finally, the same was true for the form of ignorance. The results reported using the repeated measures ANOVA still confirm that it was the ExpGr that managed to minimise the use of the forms by remonstrance, clarification and indirect fors valus of p ≤ 0.001 (T0 versus T1, T1 versus T2 and T0 versus T2) compared with those of the ContGr which did not record significant results between the three sampling times compared two by two. The same test also confirmed the significant increase in the use of forms by diversion and ignorance only for the ExpGr for p ≤ 0.001 (T0 versus T1, T1 versus T2 and T0 versus T2) compared with the ContGr. All these results demonstrate that the addition of our nap protocol generated a change in the patern of strategic choices of interventions against the DB apparent and realised by the ExpGr vs. ContGr.

Effect of the 25-minute nap on the frequency of use of interventions leading to fractures and continuities in trainees in the control and experimental groups

The results in Table 4 shows non-significant results at the starting point of the T0 experiment for the averages of the use of interventions causing fractures (F = 0.446, p = 0.832) and continuities (F = 0.435, p = 0.512) of the pedagogical action for ExpGr vs. ContGr. This confirms that there was no difference between the two study groups in the strategic choice of reactive management interventions, taking into account their repercussions on the pedagogical act at the time of teaching. The results become significant for the use of fractures, reporting a more marked decrease in their T1 averages in ExpGr (avg = 20.22, p < 0.001) vs. ContGr (avg = 25.92, p < 0.001). This reduction was further consolidated at T2 for ExpGr (avg = 10.92, p < 0.001) vs. ContGr (avg = 25.49, p < 0.001). The results reported using the repeated measures ANOVA further confirm that it was ExpGr that managed to significantly minimize the use of reactive management interventions provoking fractures in the pedagogical act during teaching for values of p ≤ 0.001 (T0 versus T1, T1 versus T2 and T0 versus T2) compared with those of ContGr, which failed to record significant results between the three sampling times compared two by two.

Discussion

The aim of this study was to test the impact of a 25-minute nap on attention, complex reaction time and choice of intervention strategies compared with apparent DBs derived from Sieber’s reactive management model [10] in trainee PE teachers. The study showed a clear improvement in the experimental group compared with the control group in terms of cognitive faculties (complex reaction time and attention), thus encouraging more appropriate choices to be made in response to disruptive behaviour occurring during teaching.

The choice of intervention strategy on the part of the teacher to manage disruptive behavior is a very delicate one, given the consequences this maneuver can have on the ecology of the classroom [6, 7]. These very crucial choices for teachers come as a result of a great influence that can affect their stress and burnout [34], their sense of self-efficacy [18] as well as the learning climate that governs the group/class [8, 9, 35]. The addition of a nap to the training routines of physical education teachers is therefore seen as an innovative approach geared to circumventing challenges that require attention, rapid information processing dependent on a strategic choice of the intervention best suited to the circumstances that arise and intended for a better approach leading to the installation of the best learning climate for students.

Our analyses of the different variables show a number of significant results highlighting the benefits that adding a nap to the daily routine of PE teachers can have on their cognitive aspects. The first finding of our study shows that trainee teachers who underwent the nap protocol recorded better results for the sustained attention variable than those who did not. More specifically, the results are reflected in an improvement in ExpGr quantitative performance on the d2 test, arguing in favor of better concentration. This is further consolidated by the results of the overall performance index (GPI) for the d2 test, which more specifically reflects a clear improvement in the pace of the test (GZ) and a reduction in the gross error index (F) for the test. This points more explicitly to an improvement in ExpGr concentration, reflected in better attention [27] and vigilance [36, 37] on their part. Our comments also corroborate studies by Huang and Zhang [38], Ruth et al. [28], Dutheil [36] and Stevens and Bavelier [39], which highlight the positive effects of nap administration on selective attention and vigilance in humans.

The second finding of our research argues for the improvement of ExpGr vs. ContGr on the variable of complex reaction time. It shows that this same group has lower average complex reaction times than the ContGr. This shows that ExpGr individuals take less time to process information when choosing which interventions to carry out against indiscipline. This also underlines an improvement in their active cognitive processes [36], characterized by better adaptation to new situations and appropriate choices, for faster problem-solving in situations requiring creativity in their management. Our findings are confirmed by McHill et al. [40], who report that napping helps individuals overcome sleep deprivation by minimizing loss of attention and reaction time, thus reducing the risk of error.

The third finding of our study is the improvement in the characteristics of interventions called “reactive management” in favor of the ExpGr in comparison with the ContGr. Indeed, unlike the ContGr, the ExpGr managed to minimize interventions of the admonishing, clarifying and indirect types. Assuming [10] that these three intervention modalities have a character emphasizing the DB sender through direct confrontation with the undisciplined pupil, our results underline the ExpGr’s improvement in the choice of interventions to be made against DBs. It also shows that this same group is better able to strategically minimize oppositional and aggressive interventions that focus on the DB and his sender, thus inducing teacher/teacher tension. Our findings are in line with those of Killgore et al. [41] and Thomas et al. [42], who report that napping helps to circumvent sleep deprivation by minimizing riskier, more aggressive behavior during human intervention.

On the other hand, our results show an increase in the use of interventions of the type, diversion and ignoring, with the character of a gentle intervention with the specificity of intervening on the task and not on the DB or its sender. These results show that ExpGr individuals do indeed try to strategically dodge direct confrontation with students by issuing verbal interventions in relation to the tasks performed by the students, thus diverting their attention from the apparent DB. The aim of this approach is to maintain a learning climate conducive to the acquisition of knowledge, while maintaining positive social interactions within the class group. Our comments concur with those of Dutheil et al. [36] and McDevitt et al. [43], demonstrating that napping promotes an increase in the ability to evaluate situations experienced by the person following sufficient sleep, consolidated by a better capacity for verbal communication supported by a more correct formulation of meaningful sentences and known under the tutelage of the “verbalization of thoughts” [44] or “verbal memory” [45].

The fourth and final findings of our study report that it is indeed the ExpGr trainees who tend to minimize interventions that lead to a fracture (Romantrance, clarification and indirect) in teaching in favor of those that ensure continuity (Diversion and ignorance). His results are only significant for trainees in this same group. This suggests that these trainees are trying to opt for less direct and risky interventions to maintain the pedagogical continuity of their PE lessons. This is confirmed by a closer look at “ignorance” and “diversion”, whose significant increases are found only in this same group, arguing in favour of making more appropriate choices against DBs, or again, called strategic ignorance, to ensure the same continuity invoked above. Contrary to its results, the ContGr recorded insignificant results, which largely consolidates our lead for the ExpGr. The reduction in interventions that break pedagogical continuity (remontrance, clarifications and indirect interventions), still only significant on the ExpGr side, brings us back to the point that it’s still these same trainees who try to use the least risky interventions for the installation of teacher/learner conflicts, while avoiding reactions against DBs whose aggressive nature [41, 42] always engenders a break in the pedagogical act. This assumes, on the other hand, that the nap has brought about an improvement affecting ExpGr trainee teachers on the principle of their adoption of new procedures for managing indiscipline engendering social interactions [46] aimed at establishing a good classroom climate conducive to the transfer of learning by trying to dodge the action putting the blame on the DB’s sender pupil often having a willingness oriented towards the principle of testing the teacher’s limits [7].

Limitations and suggestions for future studies

To say that our research did not come up against certain limitations is surely derisory. It is in this context that we would like to point out, for future studies, that we should try to adopt mixed experimental designs, the aim of which would be to provide clarification between the results reported for the structure of off-line observation (indirect observation on the videos) and the reasons for the action of the trainees’ intervention. In this way the researchers will be able to get round the problem of what they think they are observing on the video recordings and the exact intention of this intervention. This problem can only be solved if researchers use interviews based on the video feedback protocol [47]. It would also be wise to consider the use of actigraphy or polysomnography, which could support the results of this study.

On the other hand, we think it would be very interesting for future research to use a crossover model, in which the same participants would experience napping and not napping in random order. This within-subjects model would be better able to control for individual differences in motivation, physical condition, basic cognitive ability and sleep rhythm. Finally, it would be desirable to test a scientific approach using a linear mixed model (LMM), which takes into account the intra-subject correlation in time and directly tests the group interaction in relation to time. This model would perhaps have more impact on the management of unequal measurement intervals, missing data and the numerous observation variables per participant, while allowing a certain broadening of the research by incorporating covariates such as previous nocturnal sleep or caffeine consumption.

Practical implications

This study, carried out in the Tunisian context, provides a range of knowledge that contributes positively to understanding the significant contribution of the nap to cognitive skills and the choice of interventions against DB among trainee PE teachers in their 3rd year of university studies. Our results argue in favour of adding the nap for its positive repercussions, which depend on better concentration and vigilance against indiscipline when this occurs. It is therefore essential that our comments are taken into consideration by the teaching staff and official bodies in order to improve the learning climate for teachers. We were also able to prove that the addition of the nap led to a marked improvement in the know-how and interpersonal skills of our trainee PE teachers. These results would undoubtedly favour the improvement of the learning climate established on their part and characterised by didactic interventions more convenient and appropriate to the episodes of indiscipline encountered by our Tunisian PE trainee teachers. Finally, it is imperative to advance that the results of our research report a greater influence of the experimental condition (internship + nap) on the use of interventions by the trainees against the DB compared to the reference condition (internship only).

Conclusion

This study specifically demonstrates that the principle of exposing these trainees to a 25-minute nap between the morning and afternoon sessions led to significant improvements in their CRTs, their attention and the making of more strategically appropriate choices for better interventions in DB management during teaching, enabling the establishment of a better learning climate characterised by the maintenance of pedagogical continuity during the transfer of knowledge.
