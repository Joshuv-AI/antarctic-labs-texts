Research note: ELISA kit replacement leads to false negatives in ALV eradication—A case study in Chinese yellow-feathered broilers
Authors: Ruiqiang Geng, Mengyu Song, Zhihao Ren, Wenping Cui, Yixin Wang, Shuang Chang, Peng Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The ELISA kit for detecting the p27 antigen of avian leukosis virus (ALV) is a cornerstone reagent in avian leukosis (AL) eradication programs. Its analytical sensitivity is a key determinant of successful eradication. During an AL eradication campaign in a Chinese yellow-feathered broiler breeder flock, an unexpected shift in detection positivity rates occurred following a change in ELISA kit. The new positivity rate was inconsistent with the flock’s clinical presentation, raising concerns that the lower sensitivity of the new kit may have resulted in false-negative results. In this study, we selected 200 breeding hens from the affected flock. Aseptically collected anticoagulated blood samples were subjected to virus isolation, and the cell culture supernatants were evaluated using two commercial ELISA kits (designated Kit A and Kit B) and ALV magnetic-particle chemiluminescent antigen detection kit. Kit B, the kit currently in use at the facility, yielded negative results for all 200 samples. In contrast, both Kit A and the magnetic-particle chemiluminescent kit consistently detected 12 positive samples, all of which corresponded to the same individuals. Further sequencing analysis of the env gene from positive plasma samples confirmed avian leukosis virus subgroup K (ALV-K), a strain with low replication capacity. This finding indicates that the insufficient analytical sensitivity of Kit B rendered it ineffective for detecting this particular subgroup. Collectively, this case study underscores the critical importance of pre-evaluating diagnostic kits for ALV eradication—especially when targeting low-replication strains such as ALV-K. For such strains, more sensitive detection platforms should be prioritized to ensure accurate identification and effective control.

Introduction

AL is a neoplastic and immunosuppressive infectious disease caused by avian leukosis virus (ALV), posing a significant threat to the global poultry industry. Based on differences in the antigenicity of the viral env protein, host range, and genomic sequence characteristics, ALV is classified into 11 subgroups (A through K) (Payne and Nair, 2012). Notably, in recent years, the detection rate of ALV-K has been increasing annually among local chicken breeds, yellow-feathered broilers, and certain conservation flocks in China (Zhang et al., 2025).

First isolated and identified from the Chinese local Luhua chicken breed, ALV-K possesses biological characteristics distinct from ALV-J (Cui et al., 2014). It exhibits a reduced in vitro replication capacity, attributed to the weaker promoter activity of its long terminal repeat sequence (Guo et al., 2023). This characteristic is further influenced by the lower binding affinity of the gp85 protein in certain ALV-K strains to the Tva receptor (Chen et al., 2022). Common laboratory methods for ALV detection include virus isolation, ELISA, and indirect immunofluorescence assay. Among these approaches, the p27 antigen ELISA is the most widely adopted method in current ALV eradication initiatives (Yun et al., 2013). However, a multitude of commercial ALV p27 antigen ELISA kits exist, each differing significantly in terms of sensitivity and specificity. Consequently, the selection of detection kits can result in divergent detection outcomes, which in turn exerts a significant impact on the effectiveness of AL eradication programs.

This study was performed on a Chinese yellow-feathered broiler flock with a long history of AL eradication. By comparing detection results from different diagnostic kits at the late eradication stage, this study highlights the critical role of high-sensitivity kits in improving the efficacy of ALV-K eradication.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

The animal care and use protocol was approved by the Shandong Agricultural University Animal Care and Use Committee (SDAUA-2016-002). Care and maintenance of all experimental animals throughout the experiments were in strict accordance with the ethics and biosecurity guidelines approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Shandong Agricultural University.

Background of chicken flocks

A yellow-feathered broiler breeder flock (designated QJM) in Guangxi, China, has undergone continuous AL eradication efforts. The virus isolation positivity rate in pre-laying chickens declined steadily from 1.80% in 2017 to 0.15% in 2022. All isolates obtained during this period were identified as ALV-J, and all p27 antigen ELISA kits employed were sourced from IDEXX Laboratories (Westbrook, ME, USA). In 2022, the diagnostic kit was switched to another commercial ALV p27 antigen ELISA kit. After the switch, no ALV was isolated from the flock for two consecutive years. However, p27 antigen-positive results persisted in egg white samples. Furthermore, a marked decline in the quality of hatched chicks marketed after 2024 was observed, raising concerns about potential false negatives during the eradication process.

Virus, cells, and animals

The reference strain used as positive control was the ALV-K strain JS11C1 (GenBank No. KF746200.1). This strain was isolated, identified, and preserved by our laboratory in 2012 from a conservation flock of a local chicken breed in Jiangsu Province, China. The DF-1 cell was purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Specific-pathogen-free (SPF) chickens were purchased from Jinan Sais Poultry Co., Ltd., Jinan, China.

ALV antigen detection kit used in the experiment

ALV p27 antigen ELISA kits were purchased from two distinct Chinese manufacturers. As designated by the manufacturers, these kits were labeled as Kit A and Kit B, both of which are commercial products. The Biotek Synergy H1 microplate reader (model: H1M) was purchased from Darui Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China. The ALV magnetic-particle chemiluminescent antigen detection kit was developed using monoclonal antibodies derived from the hybridoma cell line 7H7H2 (specific for ALV) and conjugated to carboxylated microspheres. This kit is intended for the detection of ALV p27 antigen from different sources. It is a commercial kit co-developed by our laboratory and HeXu Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Zhengzhou, China, and has been approved as a new veterinary drug by the Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs of the People's Republic of China. The magnetic-particle chemiluminescence analyzer (Model: Shine i1910), a fully automated chemiluminescent immunoassay platform, was procured from Shenzhen IncreCare Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

Virus isolation and identification in chicken flocks

A total of 200 hens were randomly selected from the described flock for further analysis. Each hen was housed individually and assigned a unique identifier. Anticoagulated blood was collected aseptically from all chickens, centrifuged at 2,000 rpm for 2 min, and plasma samples were seeded into 24-well plates. After 7 days of incubation, cell culture supernatants were harvested and stored at -80°C until analysis. These supernatants were tested with two commercial ALV p27 antigen ELISA kits (Kit A and Kit B) and the ALV magnetic-particle chemiluminescent antigen detection kit, following the manufacturers' protocols. DF-1 cells inoculated with the ALV-K strain JS11C1 and plasma from SPF chickens served as positive and negative controls, respectively, for virus isolation.

Sequencing of the isolated ALV strains

Viral RNA was extracted from the plasma of the positive chickens using a commercial kit (Omega Bio-Tek, Norcross, GA, USA). The complete env gene was amplified by PCR with previously established laboratory primers(Primers used: ALV-F 5′-GCTGCCATCGAGAGGTTACT-3′, ALV-R 5′-AGTTGTCAGGGAATCGAC-3′) (Wang and Cui, 2006). PCR products were gel-purified, cloned into the pMD-19T vector (TaKaRa Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Dalian, China), and positive clones were identified and subjected to Sanger sequencing (Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). The resulting sequences were analyzed using the NCBI BLAST tool to identify the viral subgroup.

The same samples were also tested with the Kit B. Based on the manufacturer's cut-off (S/P value > 0.2 designated as positive), all 200 hens were negative for virus isolation. None of these samples exceeded the positive threshold for Kit B, with a maximum S/P value of only 0.071 (Table 1). Kit B has been employed for ALV eradication surveillance in this flock since 2022. These findings demonstrate that Kit B possesses substantially lower detection sensitivity than Kit A, which likely resulted in missed detections during the eradication program.

The same samples were also tested with the ALV magnetic-particle chemiluminescent antigen detection kit. Using the manufacturer's cut-off (S/P value > 0.2 designated as positive), 12 hens were positive for ALV isolation (Table 1), matching the exact individuals identified by ELISA Kit A. Compared to Kit A, the chemiluminescent kit produced substantially higher S/P value, demonstrating markedly stronger signal intensity and offering clearer result interpretation. In all experiments, the positive control showed a strong signal, while the negative control yielded near-zero readings, confirming the absence of non-specific interference and supporting the reliability of the results.

Sequencing of the env gene and subgroup identification

Viral RNA was extracted from plasma samples and subjected to env gene amplification, followed by cloning and Sanger sequencing. Notably, only two samples (QJM004 and QJM011) yielded faint electrophoretic bands following PCR amplification. Subsequent cloning and sequencing confirmed that these two samples possessed an identical nucleotide sequence. This consensus sequence was submitted to the NCBI database for BLAST analysis. The BLAST results showed that the ALV strain identified in this flock shared high nucleotide homology (95.36%–98.72%) with ALV-K reference strains (Table 2). Among these, the highest homology (98.72%) was observed with the ALV-K strain DT190903 (GenBank accession number: MT319754.1). Risks of false negatives in ALV eradication using low-sensitivity ELISA

In ALV eradication programs, p27 antigen-targeted detection methods serve as the cornerstone of surveillance (Payne et al., 1993; Weiss, 2006). However, significant variations in detection sensitivity exist between different products. In the present study, two distinct commercial group-specific ALV p27 antigen ELISA kits yielded starkly divergent results when testing the same batch of cell culture supernatants: Kit B failed to detect any positive samples, whereas Kit A identified 12 ALV-positive specimens. Here, Kit A detected ALV-K-positive samples missed by Kit B. This case provides a cautionary illustration: switching from the high-sensitivity kit (e.g., the original IDEXX product) to the less sensitive Kit B likely yielded false-negative results in virus isolation, even as the virus remained persistent in the flock. Therefore, ELISA kits should not be regarded as interchangeable commodities in ALV eradication programs.

Virus isolation and sequencing confirmed ALV-K as the causative agent in this study. Because of low in vivo replication efficiency and viremia titers, the feature poses a direct challenge to virus isolation—the gold-standard method for ALV eradication. In this case, the declining positive rate doesn’t indicate the absence of infection; instead, it is more likely because of the difficulty of capturing low-titer ALV-K. Furthermore, the low replicative capacity of ALV-K directly leads to relatively low in vivo viremia levels. This also explains why direct PCR amplification of viral sequences from plasma yielded only faint electrophoretic bands. To overcome the false-negative limitations of traditional ELISAs, this study employed the magnetic-particle chemiluminescent antigen detection kit, which exhibited a marked sensitivity advantage. This platform consistently identified the same 12 positive samples as high-sensitivity Kit A, with enhanced signal intensity that simplified result interpretation. Our findings indicate that low-sensitivity ELISA kits are insufficient for ALV eradication, particularly in the late phase, targeting low-replication strains like ALV-K. We recommend incorporating multiple high-sensitivity detection methods for parallel testing and confirmatory validation.

Worldwide relevance of kit sensitivity differences in ALV eradication

Although this case study was conducted in a Chinese broiler flock, its implications are directly relevant to the global poultry industry. ALV and the associated neoplastic diseases, immunosuppression, and production losses pose a common endemic threat to commercial poultry farming worldwide, and ALV eradication is also a key priority for breeding companies globally (Dou et al., 2023). In China, as in many other poultry operations worldwide that implement ALV eradication programs, commercial p27 antigen ELISA kits are routinely used under the assumption that different kits provide comparable sensitivity. However, our head-to-head comparison reveals substantial performance differences among commercial p27 antigen ELISA kits—differences that can lead to false-negative results. This finding is particularly critical for late-stage eradication programs, where a declining positivity rate may be misinterpreted as evidence of successful elimination when, in fact, an insensitive assay merely masks persistent low-level viral circulation, ultimately leading to continued production losses. This consequence is not unique to the Chinese production system but represents a common risk for any flock undergoing ALV eradication. Furthermore, the magnetic‑particle chemiluminescence platform introduced in our study provides clearer threshold discrimination and offers a practical solution. Therefore, this study suggests that in ALV eradication programs targeting low‑replication strains such as ALV‑K, breeding companies should evaluate the sensitivity of their diagnostic kits and consider incorporating more sensitive detection technologies to avoid false negatives caused by kit variability.
