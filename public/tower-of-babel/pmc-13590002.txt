Single-Nucleus Transcriptomic Mapping Reveals Correlative Microglial Changes Associated with rTMS in the Motor Cortex After Spinal Cord Injury
Authors: Kunlong Zhang, Xinjiang Yang, Ruibin Hou, Rui Zhao, Dongjin Li, Hancheng Liao, Fei Tian, Xiaolong Sun, Hua Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSpinal cord injury (SCI)-associated neuropathic pain (NP) is a severely disabling complication with limited treatment options. Although spinal microglial activation is well documented in SCI-NP, the functional role of microglia in the primary motor cortex (M1) and their response to repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) remain poorly understood.MethodsSingle-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) was performed on M1 tissues from Sham, SCI, and SCI+rTMS mice to identify transcriptionally distinct microglial states. Differential expression analysis, GO/KEGG pathway enrichment, and CellChat-based cell-cell communication mapping were used to characterize phenotypic changes, key signaling pathways, and intercellular crosstalk underlying microglial responses to SCI and rTMS. Real Time Quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) and immunofluorescence were employed to validate microglial marker expression. Behavioral assessments included the Basso Mouse Scale (BMS) for locomotor function, as well as von Frey and Hargreaves tests to evaluate mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia.ResultsSCI did not alter overall cellular composition in the M1 cortex but was associated with microglial transcriptional changes suggesting transformation toward a pro-inflammatory state, with the CX3C pathway identified as a potential mediator. Whereas rTMS was correlated with a shift toward an anti-inflammatory/repair state. These findings were validated by iNOS/Arg1 and Aif1/Cx3cr1 immunofluorescence, RT-qPCR, and behavioral assays, which together were consistent with SCI severity and rTMS-associated analgesia.ConclusionOur snRNA-seq analysis identifies M1 cortical microglial transcriptional and communication changes that correlate with SCI-induced NP. rTMS correlates with reduced pro-inflammatory microglial signaling and enhanced repair-associated transcriptional programs. However, as these findings are correlational and do not establish causation, they provide a hypothesis-generating preclinical foundation for future mechanistic studies targeting cortical microglia with rTMS.

Introduction

Spinal cord injury (SCI) is a devastating neurological disorder that often leads to long-term disability, with neuropathic pain (NP) affecting up to 70% of patients, severely impairing quality of life.1 Current therapeutic options, including pharmacotherapy and invasive interventions, are limited by poor efficacy and adverse effects, highlighting an urgent need to elucidate the underlying mechanisms and identify targeted strategies.2 Neuroinflammation is a key driver of SCI-NP, with microglial activation serving as a central event in the initiation and maintenance of pain hypersensitivity.3,4

Most existing studies on SCI-NP have focused on microglial inflammation within the spinal cord, where resident microglia become activated following injury and release pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines that sensitize dorsal horn neurons; but emerging evidence suggests remote cortical regions, including the primary motor cortex (M1), contribute to the development of SCI-induced central sensitization and NP.5,6 The M1 is critical for motor control and pain modulation, and its dysfunction post-SCI may exacerbate neuroinflammatory cascades.7,8 However, the role of microglia in the M1 region of SCI-NP remains uncharacterized, and the molecular mechanisms governing their activation and communication with other cell types are unclear. Notably, the CX3C chemokine pathway, a key mediator of neuron-microglia crosstalk, is implicated in neuroinflammation, but its role in M1 cortical networks post-SCI has not been defined.9

Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) is a non-invasive neuromodulatory technique that shows promise for treating neuroinflammatory disorders and NP.8 Clinical evidence supports the use of high-frequency rTMS targeting the M1 cortex to reduce NP, particularly in patients with SCI.10 A recent meta-analysis confirmed that rTMS produces significant analgesic effects across various NP etiologies.11 Emerging evidence suggests that rTMS also exerts anti-inflammatory actions, including decreasing pro-inflammatory cytokines and the pan-activated microglia marker Iba-1, while restoring the homeostatic marker Cx3cr1.12–14 However, its effects on M1 microglial function and intercellular communication in SCI remain unknown. Clarifying whether rTMS targets M1 microglial inflammatory signaling could establish a mechanistic basis for its clinical application.

Here, we used single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq), intercellular communication analysis, and in vivo validation to investigate M1 microglial roles in SCI-NP and the regulatory effects of rTMS. This work maps the transcriptional landscape of supraspinal microglial responses to SCI and rTMS, generating testable hypotheses about the CX3C-microglial axis. The correlative findings presented here do not establish causality but provide a framework for future mechanistic studies to optimize rTMS-based therapies.

Materials and Methods

Animal

Adult male C57BL/6J mice, with an average body weight of 20 ± 2.5 g, were obtained from the Experimental Animal Center of the Fourth Military Medical University (Xi’an, Shaanxi, China). All animals were housed under a 12-h light-dark cycle (8:00 a.m. to 8:00 p.m.) with free access to standard chow and water. Male mice were exclusively used in this study to minimize confounding effects associated with sex differences.15,16 A total of 27 mice were used in this study. Eighteen mice (n=6 per group) were randomly assigned to Sham, SCI, and SCI+rTMS groups for behavioral assessments, with 3 of these 6 subsequently allocated to snRNA-seq and the remaining 3 to RT-qPCR. An additional 9 mice (n=3 per group) were included for immunofluorescence validation. All animal experiments were performed in compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Establishment of the Spinal Cord Injury (SCI) Model

The mouse SCI model was established with minor modifications based on previously described protocols.17 Mice were anesthetized intraperitoneally with pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg) prior to surgical procedures.18 A laminectomy was performed at the T9–T11 vertebral level to expose the underlying spinal cord segments. Acute compressive SCI was induced at the T10 level using a microvascular clip (30 g force; ST2 64–0195, Harvard Apparatus) under visualization with a stereomicroscope (SZ 60, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). The dura mater and spinal nerve roots were left intact, and the clip was maintained in place for 30 seconds before removal. Incisions were then sutured layer by layer. Sham-operated mice underwent identical laminectomy without spinal cord compression. Manual bladder expression was performed twice daily until reflex bladder function was restored (7–10 days post-SCI).

Repetitive Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation (rTMS)

The rTMS was delivered using a dedicated stimulator (YRD CCY-II, YIRUIDE, Wuhan, China) equipped with a 2-cm diameter rodent-specific coil.19 The rTMS protocol was based on that of a previous study, and mice received daily rTMS sessions starting 3 days after SCI surgery for a total of 12 consecutive days.20 The stimulation parameters were set as follows: frequency of 20 Hz, stimulation duration of 2 seconds, interstimulus interval of 10 seconds, total pulse number of 1600, and total daily stimulation time of 8 minutes per mouse. Mice in the SCI group (sham rTMS) received sham stimulation, where the TMS coil was positioned parallel to the skull to expose mice to the acoustic artifact without delivering magnetic stimulation.

Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) Staining

Mouse T9 spinal cord segments were collected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, then dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4 µm thickness. Sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, stained with hematoxylin for 5 min, differentiated in acid alcohol, blued in warm water, and counterstained with eosin for 30s to 3 min.21 After dehydration and clearing, sections were mounted with neutral gum.

Behavioral Assays

All behavioral assessments were performed by investigators blinded to the experimental group assignments. Group codes were only decoded after data collection and analysis were completed to minimize observer bias. For each group, n = 6 mice were used for behavioral assessments.

Basso Mouse Scale (BMS)

Locomotor function of mice was evaluated using the BMS, which consists of a score (0–9 points) and a sub-score (0–11 points) to comprehensively assess motor recovery after SCI.

Mechanical Allodynia

Baseline (pre-SCI) behavioral evaluations were conducted 1 day before injury. Post-injury evaluations were performed at 2 weeks after SCI to confirm the development of NP. Mechanical hypersensitivity was assessed using von Frey monofilaments to determine the paw withdrawal mechanical threshold (PWMT).22 To mimic clinical manifestations of SCI-induced NP, both hind paws were tested individually, and the paw with more severe hypersensitivity was selected for statistical analysis.

Thermal Hyperalgesia

Hargreaves test was evaluated using a thermal testing apparatus (PL-200, Chengdu Techman Software, China).23 The paw withdrawal thermal latency (PWTL), defined as the time from the onset of thermal irradiation to paw withdrawal, was recorded. Each paw was tested three times, with a 5-minute interval between consecutive tests to avoid tissue damage, and the average PWTL was used for statistical analysis.

Nucleus Isolation for Single-Nucleus RNA Sequencing (snRNA-Seq)

In each group, three mice were randomly selected and humanely euthanized through CO2 asphyxiation, in strict accordance with the guidelines for laboratory animal welfare and ethics and the 3R principles. Fresh-frozen mouse cerebral cortex tissue was used to isolate nuclei from the M1 region (n = 3 per group). Approximately 10 mg of M1 tissue was dissected on dry ice using sterile disposable scalpels. Tissue samples were homogenized in ice-cold homogenization buffer (0.25 M sucrose, 5 mM CaCl2, 3 mM MgAc2, 10 mM Tris-HCl [pH 8.0], 0.1 mM EDTA, 1× protease inhibitor cocktail, and 1 U/μL Ribolock RNase inhibitor) using a glass Dounce homogenizer. Homogenates were filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer to collect the nuclear fraction. The nuclear fraction was mixed with an equal volume of 50% iodixanol and layered onto a 30–33% iodixanol gradient, followed by centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 20 minutes at 4°C. After removing the myelin layer from the top of the gradient, nuclei were collected from the 30–33% iodixanol interface. Nuclei were resuspended in nuclear wash and resuspension buffer (0.04% bovine serum albumin, 0.2 U/μL Ribolock RNase inhibitor, 500 mM mannitol, and 0.1 mM PMSF in phosphate-buffered saline) and pelleted by centrifugation at 500 × g for 5 minutes at 4°C. The nuclear pellet was filtered through a 40 μm cell strainer to remove debris and large clumps. Nuclear concentration was determined manually using trypan blue staining and a hemocytometer. The concentration was adjusted to 700–1200 nuclei/μL, and libraries constructed immediately following the 10X Genomics® Single Cell Protocol.

SnRNA-Seq Data Processing and Analysis Pipeline

Raw gene expression matrices for each sample were generated using the 10X Genomics Cell Ranger pipeline (v8.0.0, https://github.com/10XGenomics/cellranger) aligned to the mouse reference genome Ensembl_release113. Quality control (QC) was performed using the DoubletFinder R package (v2.0.4, https://github.com/chris-mcginnis-ucsf/DoubletFinder) to remove doublets from each sample, followed by further filtering to retain high-quality cells: cells with fewer than 500 or more than 7000 detected genes, UMI counts greater than 29,000, or mitochondrial gene expression >5% were excluded. The filtered gene expression matrices were then analyzed using the Seurat R package (v5.1.0) in R v4.3.2. To integrate datasets and correct for batch effects, we used the Harmony algorithm (v1.2.0, https://github.com/immunogenomics/harmony). Subsequent dimensionality reduction was performed via principal component analysis (PCA), and cells were clustered and visualized in two-dimensional space using the t-SNE algorithm. Cell clusters were annotated based on the expression of canonical marker genes (listed in Figure 1C), including established markers for neurons, microglia, astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, and other central nervous system cell types. Differentially Expressed Gene (DEG) Analysis

DEGs among the three groups in the M1 cortex were identified using the “limma” package in R software on pseudo-bulk expression profiles (aggregated by cell cluster and group). Gene expression profiles were log-transformed to normalize data distribution. Genes with an adjusted P-value < 0.05 and |log2 fold change (FC)| > 0.36 were defined as significantly differentially expressed. This threshold was selected to balance sensitivity to biologically relevant changes and specificity against technical noise, consistent with snRNA-seq analytical best practices.24

Functional Enrichment Analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) term enrichment and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis of DEGs were performed using the R package clusterProfiler v3.16.1. GO analysis included three categories: biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF). Pathways and GO terms with an adjusted P-value < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched and visualized using bar plots.

Cell-Cell Communication (CCC) Analysis

The CellChat V2.1.0 software was used to analyze intercellular communication based on the single-nucleus gene expression matrix.25 Ligand-receptor pairs and their expression patterns between cell subpopulations were identified, and a ligand-receptor level cell interaction network was constructed to predict potential intercellular communication relationships.

Real Time Quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

The remaining three mice in each group were humanely euthanized through CO2 asphyxiation. Total RNA was extracted from the M1 of mice in Sham, SCI, and SCI+rTMS groups (n = 3 per group) using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, USA). RT-qPCR was performed on a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) to detect Aif1 and Cx3cr1 expression (GAPDH as internal reference), using intron-spanning primers in a 20 μL system (10 μL 2×SYBR Green Premix, 0.4 μL each primer, 2 μL cDNA, 7.2 μL RNase-free water). The amplification program included pre-denaturation at 95°C for 3 min, 40 cycles of 95°C for 15s and 60°C for 30s, followed by melting curve analysis for specificity; each sample was run in triplicate. Relative expression was calculated via the 2^(-ΔΔCt) method, with the Sham group as reference.

The primer sequences are listed in Table S1.

Immunofluorescence

Immunofluorescence was performed on an additional cohort of mice (n=3 per group) that were processed in parallel with the main experimental groups. For immunofluorescence staining, brain sections were incubated with primary antibodies against Iba-1 (1:500; Abcam), Cx3cr1 (1:200; Immunoway), iNOS (1:500; Proteintech) and Arg1 (1:500; Proteintech), followed by the corresponding secondary antibodies. Images were acquired using a confocal microscope (Olympus FV3000). Histological and immunofluorescence analyses were conducted by investigators blinded to the experimental group assignments. Group codes were only decoded after data acquisition and analysis were completed.

Statistical Analysis

The sample size estimation was based on our primary outcome measures (alleviation of NP). To achieve a desired power of 80% and with a type I error set at 0.05, we calculated 5–6 mice per group were sufficient to detect biologically meaningful differences. Therefore, 6 mice per group was used for behavioral tests. For snRNA-seq analyses, n=3 per group was employed, which is common in exploratory single-nucleus studies. Data analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0. Quantitative data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) from at least three independent experiments. Unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test was used for comparisons between two independent groups. Data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test for RT-qPCR, and immunofluorescence quantification. For longitudinal repeated-measures data (eg, pain behaviors, BMS), two-way repeated-measures ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test was used to assess group, time, and interaction effects.26 Values of P < 0.05 were considered a statistically significant difference.

Results

SCI and rTMS Do Not Alter Cellular Composition of the M1 as Revealed by snRNA-Seq

To characterize the cellular landscape of M1 in response to SCI and rTMS intervention, we performed snRNA-seq on the M1 region from Sham, SCI, and SCI+rTMS mice (Figure 1A). Based on canonical marker gene and unsupervised clustering analysis, a total of 9 major cell types were identified from all 38 clusters: glutamatergic neurons (Glut), GABAergic neurons (GABA), oligodendrocytes, astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPC), fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and pericytes (Figure 1B and C). T-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE) dimensionality reduction visualized the clustering pattern of all cells across experimental groups (Figure 1D). Quantitative analysis of relative cell abundance revealed no statistically significant differences in the percentage of any cell type across the three groups (Figure 1E, F and Table S2). In addition, Pearson correlation and PCA confirmed high sample reproducibility, absence of outliers, and clear biological separation among groups (Figures S1 and S2), supporting the reliability of our snRNA-seq dataset. These data indicate that SCI and rTMS do not alter the cellular composition of M1, prompting us to focus subsequent analyses on functional changes in intercellular communication and gene expression, particularly those linked to neuroinflammation and central sensitization.

rTMS is Associated with Reduced Microglia-Mediated Inflammatory Crosstalk in the M1 Cortex Post-SCI via Modulation of Ligand-Receptor Signaling

To delineate the pathological disorder of the cell communication network after SCI and the regulatory effects of rTMS, we systematically analyzed intercellular communication networks and ligand-receptor signaling pathways using CellChat (Figure 2A and B). Based on the hypothesis that the pathological effects of SCI and the therapeutic effects of rTMS are antagonistic, we focused on screening cell subsets exhibiting reverse regulatory characteristics in intercellular communication. At the level of ligand-receptor pair numbers, the numbers of ligand-receptor pairs in microglia, GABA, and OPC were significantly increased after SCI and decreased following rTMS, whereas those in fibroblasts and pericytes were decreased after SCI and increased after rTMS (Figure 2C). Further statistical analysis of the percentage changes of interactions strength revealed that microglia exhibited the most significant alteration in communication strength among all cell subsets (Figure 2D), suggesting that it may be the cell type with notable communication changes mediating SCI pathological disturbances and the reparative effects of rTMS.

To dissect the molecular pathways driving this inflammatory network, we analyzed the top 5 significantly enriched ligand-receptor pathways in the Sham vs SCI group (Figure 2E). Among these, the CX3C signaling pathway exhibited robust communication between glutamatergic neurons (the primary source of the CX3C ligand) and microglia (which express the CX3C receptor), as confirmed by pathway heatmaps (Figure 2E). Following rTMS intervention (Figure 2F), dysregulated CX3CL1-CX3CR1 signaling was diminished, while neuroprotective and reparative signaling pathways including the SLIT and ADGRB pathways were enhanced. Notably, SLIT2 signaling, a pathway implicated in axon guidance, contributes to anti-inflammatory and repair-promoting effects in CNS injury. These cell-cell communication changes are further illustrated in hierarchical communication maps: the CX3C signaling network (Figure 2G) highlights the inflammatory crosstalk between neurons and microglia under baseline conditions, while the SLIT and ADGRB networks (Figure 2H and I) reveal thin, multi-colored connections between neurons/multiple cell types and microglia after rTMS. This identifies CX3C signaling as a critical link between neuronal activity and microglial activation, underscoring microglia’s role in propagating inflammatory crosstalk and central sensitization within the M1 cortex, which is consistent with a phenotypic transition of microglia from pro-inflammatory to anti-inflammatory/repair state following rTMS intervention.

Collectively, these findings suggest that SCI may coincide with the activation of microglia as a transcriptionally responsive cell type of inflammation-associated gene expression in the M1 cortex via the CX3C pathway, while rTMS coincided with a shift in microglial communication toward anti-inflammatory/repair pathways, correlating with reduced neuroinflammation.

Transcriptional Remodeling in M1 Microglia Links Neuroinflammation to Central Sensitization and is Reversed by rTMS

To further delineate microglial contributions to neuroinflammation, we integrated single-nucleus transcriptomic data with our earlier intercellular communication network findings. In the Sham vs SCI comparison, microglia exhibited robust transcriptional remodeling, with 37 genes significantly upregulated and 69 genes significantly downregulated (Figure 3A). Among these, Fkbp5, a critical modulator of stress responses and neuroinflammation,27 was the most prominently upregulated gene in microglia and across multiple cell types (Figure 3B), suggesting that SCI coincided with inflammatory stress responses throughout the M1 region of cells. This aligns with our observation of CX3C inflammatory pathway activation in intercellular communication analyses, consistent with SCI-associated pro-inflammatory transcriptional changes. Concurrently, downregulation of heat shock proteins such as Hsp90ab1 (a negative regulator of inflammatory signaling) further supported a shift toward a pro-inflammatory microglial phenotype. In the SCI vs SCI+rTMS comparison, rTMS elicited more pronounced transcriptional changes in microglia, with 300 genes significantly upregulated and 109 genes significantly downregulated (Figure 3C). To clarify the molecular basis of this phenotypic reversal, we analyzed the top 5 upregulated and downregulated genes in microglia post-rTMS (Figure 3D). Upregulated genes included Per3 (an NF-κB inflammation inhibitor), Rps5 (which enhances anti-inflammatory protein synthesis), Pfdn5 (a chaperone facilitating repair protein folding), and Sncb (a regulator of synaptic repair), which collectively supported the shift of microglia to an anti-inflammatory/repair phenotype. Conversely, downregulated genes included Ccnd3 (which inhibits pro-inflammatory microglial proliferation), Sft2d1 (attenuating endoplasmic reticulum stress-induced inflammation), H2bc6 (suppresses epigenetic activation of pro-inflammatory genes), and Mir124a-1hg (reduces miR-124-mediated inhibition of resting microglial markers), all of which further suppress microglial pro-inflammatory activity. GO Enrichment Analysis Reveals Functional Switch from Inflammatory Activation to Anti-Inflammatory Repair in Microglia

To characterize the functional implications of transcriptomic changes, we performed GO enrichment analysis in microglia. In the Sham vs SCI comparison, DEGs were significantly enriched in biological process (BP) terms, including response to stimulus and immune system process. This finding suggests that microglia-mediated innate immune activation and inflammatory responses were observed following SCI. In terms of molecular function (MF), enrichments in terms such as binding and catalytic activity reflect the engagement of inflammatory receptors on the microglial surface and the catalytic function of pro-inflammatory enzymes. For cellular components (CC), the enrichment of protein-containing complex points to the assembly and activation of diverse signaling complexes, among which are the inflammasome complexes that are pivotal to neuroinflammatory cascades in SCI. To identify key signaling pathways driving microglial phenotypic changes, we performed KEGG pathway enrichment analysis on the DEGs in the M1. These pathway shifts are consistent with our prior observations of rTMS-induced suppression of CX3C signaling, and activation of SLIT/ADGRB repair pathways, which is consistent with the possibility that rTMS may promote neuroprotective and repair-associated transcriptional programs to suppress neuroinflammation. Behavioral and Molecular Validation Confirm rTMS-Mediated Analgesia and Suppression of Microglial Neuroinflammation

To validate the functional relevance of our mechanistic findings, we constructed a SCI mouse model and conducted behavioral tests (Figure 7A). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of spinal cord tissue revealed prominent structural damage and inflammatory infiltration at the injury site, verifying the efficacy of the SCI surgery (Figure 7B). Consistent with histological changes, BMS scoring demonstrated severe locomotor dysfunction in SCI mice immediately post-injury, with only partial spontaneous recovery observed over the 2-week observation period (SCI: 1D, 0.17±0.16; 2W, 2.67±0.21; P<0.0001). Furthermore, rTMS treatment significantly improved motor function compared to the untreated SCI group (Figure 7C; SCI: 2.67±0.21; SCI+rTMS: 3.83±0.31; P=0.001). We then performed NP behavioral assessments in mice. Using von Frey filament and Hargreaves tests, we found that SCI mice exhibited significant reductions in PWMT (Sham: 0.73±0.05; SCI: 0.09±0.01; P<0.0001) and PWTL (Sham: 21.34±0.37; SCI: 9.52±0.58; P<0.0001) compared to Sham mice, confirming the development of SCI-induced NP (Figure 7D and E). Following 12 consecutive days of rTMS intervention, both PWMT (SCI: 0.09±0.01; SCI+rTMS: 0.32±0.03; P=0.0016) and PWTL (SCI: 9.52±0.58; SCI+rTMS: 13.58±0.86; P=0.0165) were significantly increased compared to those in SCI mice, demonstrating that rTMS alleviates SCI-associated NP and provides functional analgesia in vivo. The impact of rTMS on microglial phenotypic switching and neuroinflammation was further examined by RT-qPCR analysis of key polarization markers in the M1 cortex. In SCI mice, the homeostatic microglial marker Cx3cr1 was significantly downregulated (Figure 7F; Sham: 0.98±0.06; SCI: 0.67±0.04; P=0.0002), indicating a loss of the resting surveillance phenotype, whereas Aif1 (encoding Iba-1), a pan-microglial activation marker, was markedly upregulated (Figure 7G; Sham: 0.94±0.03; SCI: 1.27±0.08; P=0.0033). Notably, rTMS treatment restored both Cx3cr1 (Figure 7F; Sham: 0.98±0.06; SCI+rTMS: 0.89±0.03; P=0.2543) and Aif1 (Figure 7G; Sham: 0.94±0.03; SCI+rTMS: 0.84±0.05; P=0.4796) expression to levels approaching those observed in Sham mice. These changes are consistent with a phenotypic shift along the microglial activation continuum, rather than a discrete M1-to-M2 “switch”, in agreement with recent single-nucleus studies that challenge the classical binary polarization model.

Moreover, the Arg1⁺/iNOS⁺ ratio, a direct measure of the pro-inflammatory versus anti-inflammatory balance, confirmed that rTMS was associated with a shift of microglia from a pro-inflammatory toward an anti-inflammatory/repair state (Figure 8E; SCI: 0.62±0.10; SCI+rTMS: 2.54±0.29; P=0.0006). Discussion

SCI-associated NP remains a debilitating clinical challenge, with current therapies limited by incomplete mechanistic understanding and poor efficacy.28 Here, our integrated analyses suggest that intercellular communication analyses, single-nucleus transcriptomics, functional enrichment, and in vivo validation indicate that microglia in the M1 may serve as a transcriptionally responsive cell type of inflammation-associated gene expression in SCI-induced NP, and that rTMS correlated with reduced pro-inflammatory activation. These findings advance our understanding of remote cortical regulation of SCI-induced neuroinflammation and generate testable hypotheses for rTMS as a targeted therapeutic strategy.

A series of microglia-focused studies have confirmed that, as resident immune cells in the central nervous system, microglia are core regulators of neuroinflammation induced by SCI.29 The resting microglia are rapidly activated after SCI and dynamically shift from a pro-inflammatory toward an anti-inflammatory/repair-associated transcriptional state. Microglia adopting a pro-inflammatory transcriptional state exacerbate neuroinflammation and impede neural repair by activating signaling pathways such as NF-κB and NLRP3 inflammasome, releasing pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β), and inducing pyroptosis.30 In contrast, microglia adopting an anti-inflammatory/repair-associated state exert neuroprotective effects, promoting tissue repair and neural functional recovery by secreting anti-inflammatory factors (IL-4, IL-10) and facilitating the clearance of myelin debris.31,32 However, most current therapeutic strategies for regulating microglial function and intervening in neuroinflammation focus on local intervention at the SCI site, including targeted drug delivery, local inhibition of pro-inflammatory signaling pathways, and regulation of local microglial polarization,33–36 with insufficient attention paid to the regulatory role of remote cortical regions in neuroinflammation after SCI.

A major finding of this study is the identification of M1 microglia as a candidate supraspinal inflammatory mediator in SCI-induced NP, challenging the traditional focus on spinal cord inflammation. By integrating CellChat analyses, single-nucleus transcriptomics, and GO/KEGG enrichment, we observed a consistent pattern: SCI is accompanied by CX3C pathway alterations that may drive microglial pro-inflammatory gene expression through Fkbp5 upregulation and suppression of homeostatic markers such as Cx3cr1. This transcriptional remodeling coincides with a functional shift toward inflammatory and phagocytic pathways, collectively amplifying cortical neuroinflammation and contributing to NP.37 Critically, rTMS correlates with reduced activity in this axis, shifting microglial intercellular communication, transcriptional profiles, and functional pathways toward an anti-inflammatory/repair state—effects validated by behavioral recovery, RT-qPCR, and immunofluorescence analyses of Aif1 and Cx3cr1. While we employed iNOS and Arg1 as classical markers associated with pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory states, respectively, we recognize that microglial phenotypes exist along a continuum. Our snRNA-seq data support this nuanced perspective, leading us to interpret our findings as shifts along this continuum rather than discrete categorical switches. Together, this multi-layered evidence suggests a cortical basis for SCI-induced NP and points to molecular pathways that may underlie rTMS’s analgesic effects.

Our findings carry substantial translational implications for SCI-associated NP. Previous studies have demonstrated the therapeutic efficacy of focal repetitive trans-spinal magnetic stimulation for SCI.38,39 However, this approach carries definite contraindications for patients with spinal instrumentation or metallic implants.40 The M1 cortex, accessible via non-invasive rTMS, emerges as a promising alternative target for suppressing neuroinflammation and alleviating pain without the risks of direct spinal intervention.13,41 By showing that rTMS is associated with modulation of cortical microglial phenotype and function, our study provides preclinical rationale supporting the clinical translation of rTMS for SCI-induced NP, with potential applicability to other neuroinflammatory pain conditions. Moreover, the markers we identified—particularly Cx3cr1 and Aif1—could be explored in future human studies as candidate biomarkers for predicting or monitoring treatment response, potentially informing precision medicine strategies. Mechanistically, the downregulation of Cx3cr1 after SCI may reflect disruption of the CX3CL1-CX3CR1 neuron–microglia communication axis, which is essential for maintaining microglial homeostasis and limiting excessive inflammation. Accordingly, rTMS-associated restoration of Cx3cr1 expression may represent re-establishment of homeostatic surveillance, rather than simply reflecting a shift toward “anti-inflammatory” polarization.

We acknowledge several inherent limitations of this exploratory work. Our snRNA-seq analysis used n=3 biological replicates per group—a standard setup in discovery-oriented transcriptomics, supported by over 90,000 high-quality nuclei ensuring robust cell-level statistical power—yet this cannot fully capture inter-individual variance. Importantly, all transcriptomic and CellChat analyses are inherently correlative; without targeted functional perturbations, our observations serve as hypothesis-generating tools and require follow-up validation with pathway-specific interventions. Mechanistically, microglia lack excitable membranes and cannot respond directly to magnetic stimulation, so the observed phenotypic shifts are likely indirect, mediated by rTMS-modulated neuronal signaling, which will require cell-type-specific genetic manipulations in future work. Additionally, the exclusive use of male mice restricts generalizability to females, and the absence of a Sham+rTMS group limits our ability to fully discriminate injury-specific therapeutic effects from baseline rTMS activity. Despite these constraints, our multi-layered data provide a foundational framework for future translational research aimed at optimizing rTMS interventions for SCI-NP.

Conclusion

In summary, our combined snRNA-seq and experimental validation data are consistent with a role for M1 microglial transcriptional changes in SCI-induced NP, and raise the hypothesis that rTMS may modulate microglial states. These findings advance our understanding of remote cortical neuroinflammation and provide a preclinical basis for rTMS as a targeted, non-invasive therapy for SCI-associated NP. By bridging cellular, molecular, and functional analyses, our work lays the foundation for future translational studies to optimize rTMS protocols and validate its clinical efficacy in patients with SCI.
