Visual Acuity Influences Visual Outcome in Endophthalmitis: A 10-Year Retrospective Observational Cohort Analysis
Authors: Yvonne Nowosielski, Charlotte Erlacher, Sarah Maier, Teresa Rauchegger, Alexander Franchi, Matus Rehak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionEndophthalmitis is a severe, sight-threatening intraocular infection that may follow ophthalmic procedures or arise endogenously. Since the Endophthalmitis Vitrectomy Study (EVS, 1995), surgical and antimicrobial strategies have evolved. This study aimed to evaluate treatment approaches, microbial spectrum, and outcomes of endophthalmitis cases at the University Eye Clinic Innsbruck over the past decade.MethodsA retrospective observational cohort analysis of all patients treated for endophthalmitis between January 2013 and December 2023 was conducted. Cases were categorized as exogenous, endogenous, or keratitis-related. Data on etiology, treatment, time to intervention, microbial isolates, and best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA, logMAR) were assessed. Treatment included tap and inject, pars plana vitrectomy (PPV) within 24 h after presentation, or delayed surgery. Regression analyses identified predictors of visual outcome and re-intervention.ResultsA total of 118 eyes from 110 patients (median age 72 years) were included. Exogenous cases accounted for 60.2%, endogenous for 28.8%. Median BCVA at diagnosis was 2.3 logMAR, improving to 1.0 logMAR at 6 months. Pathogens were isolated in 58.5% of cases—mainly Staphylococcus epidermidis (57%) in exogenous and Candida albicans (20.6%) in endogenous infections. Median time to intervention in exogenous cases was 3.5 h. BCVA at diagnosis and timepoint were significantly associated with visual outcome over time (p < 0.001, respectively; exogenous cases only). Re-intervention was required in 78 eyes; hazard was significantly higher after delayed surgery in exogenous cases (HR of 5.7, 95% CI [2.9–11.1], p < 0.001 (n = 71, 39 re-interventions).ConclusionsVisual acuity remains the main prognostic factor for visual recovery. In exogenous endophthalmitis, delayed surgery is associated with a high re-intervention rate, which may be reduced by initial tap-and-inject treatment. Prospective studies are needed to establish optimal timing and treatment strategies.Keywords: Endophthalmitis, Time to intervention, Endophthalmitis rate, Germ spectrum

Introduction

Endophthalmitis remains a vision-threatening emergency that arises exogenously after ocular procedures or trauma and endogenously via hematogenous spread. The Endophthalmitis Vitrectomy Study (EVS) established intravitreal broad-spectrum antibiotics as first-line therapy and suggested primary vitrectomy only for patients presenting with light-perception vision after post-cataract surgery endophthalmitis; it also found no benefit to the systemic antibiotics used at that time [1]. However, the generalizability of EVS to contemporary practice is uncertain given major changes since the 1990s in prophylaxis, microbiology, diagnostics, and surgery.

Post-EVS, implementation of intracameral antibiotic prophylaxis at cataract surgery reduced postoperative endophthalmitis incidence at a population level, albeit with regional differences in agents and practices [2]. At the same time, the exponential growth of intravitreal anti-VEGF injections created a new dominant source of exogenous endophthalmitis, with incidence estimates generally lower than after cataract surgery but with a distinct microbiologic spectrum characterized by higher rates of virulent oral streptococci and poorer visual outcomes when streptococci are involved [3–5]. For bleb-related cases, streptococci and Gram-negative organisms remain overrepresented and outcomes are typically guarded [5].

Antimicrobial recommendations have also evolved. Intravitreal vancomycin plus a Gram‑negative agent (commonly ceftazidime) remains standard empiric therapy for acute bacterial cases, while concerns about aminoglycoside retinal toxicity have limited routine use of amikacin [5]. Awareness of hemorrhagic occlusive retinal vasculitis (HORV) has curtailed the intraoperative prophylactic use of vancomycin in cataract surgery, though this syndrome is not a contraindication to therapeutic intravitreal vancomycin when infection is present [6, 7]. The role of adjunctive systemic antibiotics and corticosteroids remains unsettled in the modern era, with heterogeneous practices and limited high‑quality comparative data [5, 8, 9].

Diagnostic yield beyond conventional culture has improved with molecular methods, which increase organism detection, reduce time to identification, and can assist in culture‑negative cases [10, 11]. Parallel advances in vitreoretinal surgery (small‑gauge PPV, high‑speed cutters, widefield visualization, chandelier illumination) have enhanced the safety and completeness of early vitrectomy and the ability to remove infective load and biofilm-laden material. Several contemporary series suggest that earlier and more complete vitrectomy-particularly for fulminant presentations (e.g., streptococcal infection, dense vitreous opacification, or rapid clinical deterioration)-may yield better anatomic and visual outcomes than tap and inject alone, challenging the strict application of EVS criteria to today’s patients [5, 8].

Despite these advances, practice patterns vary widely, and real‑world outcomes across procedure types (cataract surgery, vitrectomy, intravitreal injection), causative organisms, and initial management strategies have not been consistently reported from Western European tertiary centers in the last decade. The aim of this study therefore was to review a consecutive 10‑year cohort at a single academic eye hospital to characterize current management strategies, microbiologic spectrum (including culture‑negative cases), and visual outcomes, and to identify clinical features associated with the choice of early vitrectomy versus tap‑and‑inject.

Methods

Study Cohort

This was a single-center retrospective observational cohort study. All patients diagnosed with and treated for endophthalmitis at the University Hospital of Innsbruck during the ten-year period between January 2013 and December 2023 were included in this study. Patients who were diagnosed with endophthalmitis but did not undergo treatment at Innsbruck University Eye Hospital were excluded from the study. Patients under the age of eighteen or diagnosed with a different disease were also excluded from the study. Cases were identified using our electronic patient record. Causes of endophthalmitis, underlying disease, treatment modality, time to intervention, germ spectrum, and visual acuity at diagnosis and at 6-month follow-up were assessed. Time to intervention was defined as the period from the time of diagnosis in the emergency department to the time of surgery. Best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was converted from Snellen to logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (logMAR) for purposes of analysis. In cases where follow-up visual acuity was under 0.1 Snellen, but not further defined, values were imputed with the mean value of other patients with a defined visual acuity < 0.1 and then converted to logMAR (three imputed cases). Visual acuity measurements below the level of standard chart testing (counting fingers, hand motion, light perception) were converted to logMAR according to published methods [12, 13]: counting fingers = 1.9 logMAR, hand motion = 2.3 logMAR, and light perception = 2.7 logMAR. BCVA was assessed at diagnosis and at 7 days, 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months thereafter.

Treatment

Treatment choices could be divided into three arms: firstly “tap and inject” with anterior chamber specimen collection, administration of intracameral cefuroxime (1 mg/ml) and intravitreal injection of cefuroxime (2.25 mg/ml) and vancomycin (1.0 mg/0.1 ml), or followed by intravenous antibiotics (fosfomycin 8 mg and ceftazidime 2 g twice a day) for 1 week; secondly “pars plana vitrectomy” with anterior and posterior chamber specimen collection with intracameral cefuroxime (1 mg/ml) and intravitreally administered cefuroxime (2.25 mg/ml) and vancomycin (1.0 mg/0.1 ml), followed by intravenous antibiotics (fosfomycin 8 mg and ceftazidime 2 g twice a day) for 1 week; Thirdly, “delayed surgery” group with intravenous fosfomycin 8 mg and ceftazidime 2 g twice daily starting on the day of diagnosis, continued for 1 week. An eye was classified as “delayed surgery” if no surgical procedure was performed within 24 h after diagnosis. For presumed or confirmed fungal endophthalmitis, patients received systemic voriconazole (6 mg/kg IV every 12 h for two loading doses, then 4 mg/kg IV every 12 h or 200 mg orally every 12 h, adjusted for drug interactions and hepatic function). When vitritis or sight‑threatening retinal lesions were present, intravitreal amphotericin B deoxycholate (5–10 µg/0.1 ml) was administered at presentation, with repeat injections every 48-72 h as indicated. Pars plana vitrectomy was performed at the treating surgeon’s discretion based on media opacity and response to therapy. Therapy was continued for at least 4-6 weeks and until clinical resolution. All treatment groups received topical antibiotic treatment while in-house. Surgical procedures were performed in retrobulbar or peribulbar anesthesia with monitored anesthesia care.

Analysis

Molecular microbiological diagnostics were performed using the commercially available SepsiTest™-Universal Microbial Detection (UMD) Kit (Molzym, Bremen, Germany), a panbacterial/panfungal PCR assay based on universal 16S and 18S rRNA primers. Positive PCR products subsequently underwent sequencing and species identification by database alignment.

Statistics

Data are presented as medians (interquartile ranges (IQR)) or absolute numbers (percentages). Generalized estimating equations (GEE) were used to assess the associations between visual acuity at diagnosis, time to intervention, type of intervention and visual outcome over time, including timepoint as a continuous covariate. The GEE model was fitted with patient as the clustering subject and repeated measures defined by eye number and timepoint, assuming a normal distribution with an identity link and an exchangeable working correlation structure. All available observations were included (three values were imputed). Regression coefficients (β) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) and p values were reported.

Cox proportional hazards regression was used to analyze time to re-intervention within 3 months after initial intervention. Hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) and p values were reported. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 30.0.0.0., release 2024; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA).

Ethical approval

Patient involvement was not part of this study. The patients were part of a retrospective observational cohort study that was approved by the Institutional Ethical Board of the Medical University of Innsbruck (1399/2023). Due to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of routinely collected clinical data, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the ethics committee. The study was conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration of 1964 and its later amendments.

To minimize selection bias, all consecutive patients diagnosed and treated for endophthalmitis during the study period were included. Information bias was reduced by using standardized electronic medical records and predefined data extraction criteria. Microbiological results and visual acuity measurements were based on routine clinical protocols, limiting measurement variability. To address confounding, multivariable regression analyses were performed including clinically relevant covariates. However, as a retrospective single-center study conducted at a tertiary referral center, referral bias and residual confounding cannot be fully excluded.

Causes of Referred Cases for Exogenous Endophthalmitis

Most cases from referred cases causing exogenous endophthalmitis resulted from phacoemulsification of cataracts (65.7%), followed by intravitreal injections (25.7%), MIGS (5.7%) and combined phacoemulsification with pars plana vitrectomy (2.9%, Table 3).

Causes for Endogenous Endophthalmitis

Thirty-four patients had endogenous endophthalmitis. Six patients (17.6%) who were diagnosed with endogenous endophthalmitis had a confirmed infectious disease; 17 (50%) patients were immunosuppressed and had an infectious disease simultaneously, nine (26.5%) patients had cancer and were immunosuppressed at the time of diagnosis, one (2.94%) patient had cardiac disease and one (2.9%) patient had an unknown underlying condition.

Germ Spectrum

In 58.5% of cases a germ could be isolated; 60.9% showed Gram-positive, 5.8% Gram negative, 29.0% fungal and 4.3% mixed infections (Table 2). The most common germ isolated in exogenous endophthalmitis was Staphylococcus epidermidis (n = 20, 57%) followed by Streptococcus oralis (n = 2, 5.6%), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (n = 1, 2.9%) and Enterococcus faecalis (n = 2, 5.7%) for both in-house and referred cases. For endogenous endophthalmitis most frequent infection was caused by fungal species being Candida albicans (n = 7, 20.6%), Aspergillus fumigatus (n = 5, 14.7%) and Malassezia (n = 2, 5.9%). 5.9% (n = 2) of cases were caused by infection with Staphylococcus aureus (Table 4).

Interventions and Re-Interventions

From 118 cases, 17 were first treated with tap and inject, 40 with vitrectomy and 61 with delayed surgery. Re-intervention was lost to follow-up in two cases. From 116 cases re-intervention was necessary in 78 eyes. Importantly the type of first treatment was associated with re-intervention risk: In detail 95% needed a 2nd intervention after delayed surgery. Analyzing exogenous cases only, Cox regression for a 2nd intervention after delayed surgery revealed a HR of 5.7, 95% CI [2.9–11.1], p < 0.001 (n = 71, 39 re-interventions). Sensitivity analysis including only one eye per patient (n = 70) revealed similar results (HR 5.9; 95% CI 3.0–11.7; p < 0.001).

Exploratory analysis comparing first-line tap-and-inject with delayed surgery showed a reduced hazard of re-intervention in exogenous cases only (HR 0.38; 95% CI 0.15–0.96; p = 0.040; n = 34, 28 re-interventions).

Discussion

This retrospective study investigated the clinical features, causative organisms, management and visual outcome of endophthalmitis over the last 10 years at the university hospital of Innsbruck, Austria.

The majority of cases (60.2%) were due to exogenous endophthalmitis. For the in-house cases the calculated endophthalmitis rates after phacoemulsification of cataract, intravitreal injections and vitrectomy were within the range of published literature and therefore reflect the given knowhow of endophthalmitis management within our clinic. Staphylococcus epidermidis was the most frequent bacterium isolated in exogenous endophthalmitis in both, in-house and externally referred cases. This is in accordance to past and current literature reporting Gram-positive, coagulase-negative bacteria to be the most frequent germ isolated in postoperative endophthalmitis [1, 14]. This fact highlights the importance of compliance to eye and skin disinfection exposure time before starting to operate [15]. Despite the use of polymerase chain reaction (PCR) analysis, no pathogens were detected in 40% of cases. This result is unexpected, as PCR should, in principle, identify residual genetic material from microorganisms even if they are no longer viable. In the literature this finding was addressed by the French institutional endophthalmitis study group who found that PCR was superior to culture in vitrectomy specimens from post-IVI associated endophthalmitis (70% vs. 9%) [16]. Although PCR offers significant advantages over conventional culture techniques, a key methodological limitation remains: PCR can detect only those organisms whose genomic sequences are specifically targeted by the assay. Recent advances, however, are addressing this limitation through the application of deep DNA sequencing, which enables the identification of most known bacterial species as well as phages, viruses, and previously uncharacterized organisms [16, 17]. Depending on the pathogen, the infection can take a fulminant course. Therefore, one would expect that rapid action is of utmost importance once the diagnosis has been made. Interestingly in our study we did not find an influence of time to intervention and the visual outcome in cases of exogenous endophthalmitis. This result is unexpected, as one would anticipate that during the acceleration phase of infection-characterized by rapid visual decline, ocular pain, and visible purulent accumulation, typically the stage at which patients present to the emergency department [18]-timely intervention would be critical. Our result also contrasts with the European Society of Cataract and Refractive Surgeons (ESCRS) guidelines for the management of postoperative endophthalmitis published in 2013 [19], which recommend immediate complete three-port vitrectomy as the “gold standard.” In our study, the only significant factor influencing visual outcome was visual acuity at presentation. Visual outcome at baseline has also been found to be an important factor in distinguishing between different surgical procedures: the randomized controlled EVS study published in 1995 suggested immediate vitrectomy for vision with light perception whereas recent evidence increasingly supports early vitrectomy for many patients to improve visual outcomes [1, 20]. To the best of our knowledge, however, these studies did not investigate the specific time interval from diagnosis to surgery. While our finding may reduce some of the urgency placed on the operating surgeon and facilitate more effective triage of multiple surgical emergencies, it is important to note that many of our patients were referred from external ophthalmologists, introducing an unknown delay between the initial diagnosis and presentation to the emergency department, which may have influenced the final outcomes. Regarding treatment strategies for exogenous endophthalmitis our cohort showed that delayed surgery was associated with a higher hazard of re-intervention (HR 5.7; 95% CI 2.9–11.1; p < 0.001; n = 71, 39 re‑interventions) and an exploratory analysis suggested a lower hazard with first-line tap-and-inject compared with delayed surgery (HR 0.38; 95% CI 0.15–0.96; p = 0.040; n = 34, 28 re-interventions). However, this observational study is subject to unmeasured confounding, including possible confounding by indication. The reasons for postponing surgery (clinical versus logistical) were not consistently documented, limiting our ability to distinguish indication-driven decisions from operational delays. Consequently, these associations should not be interpreted as evidence of a causal effect, particularly given the small sample size of the exploratory comparison. Rather, they should be considered hypothesis-generating and require confirmation in prospective studies with more comprehensive documentation of the clinical decision-making process. The high rate of delayed surgery may partly be explained by the unavailability of an anesthesiologist on the day of diagnosis, as operating on an inflamed eye can be painful and adequate pain control or sedation is essential. In our opinion, however, at a minimum, a tap-and-inject procedure should have been performed within 24 h, as endophthalmitis represents an ophthalmic emergency. In these cases, patients initially received systemic therapy only, yet the majority nevertheless required an ocular intervention on the following day. These findings suggest that postponing ocular intervention in patients with an apparently less severe clinical presentation may not be justified and could instead increase the risk of substantial disease progression and clinical deterioration. In endogenous cases, the treatment approach often depends on the patient’s overall health and clinical stability, making surgery a secondary consideration.

Looking forward, several strategies may further reduce incidence and improve outcomes. First, prevention through optimized perioperative antisepsis remains foundational: standardized povidone–iodine protocols to the conjunctival sac and periocular skin with adequate exposure times, together with careful lash isolation and draping, and, where appropriate, intracameral antibiotics, have been associated with lower postoperative risk [2, 21, 22]. Second, even earlier diagnosis and expedited therapy can be supported by streamlined access pathways, patient education about warning symptoms, and rapid diagnostics. Beyond conventional culture, broad‑range PCR and metagenomic next‑generation sequencing can accelerate pathogen identification in culture‑negative cases and help target therapy sooner [17, 23, 24]. Third, antimicrobial strategies continue to evolve: intravitreal vancomycin plus ceftazidime remains standard for suspected bacterial disease (with amikacin as an alternative when indicated), while fungal endophthalmitis typically requires systemic azoles (e.g., voriconazole) with intravitreal antifungals (amphotericin B deoxycholate or voriconazole) when vitritis or sight‑threatening lesions are present; drug selection should consider susceptibility patterns and ocular pharmacokinetics. [25–27] Fourth, individualized (“customized”) intravitreal approaches that account for ocular parameters such as axial length and vitreous volume may optimize intraocular drug concentrations, particularly in small eyes or post‑vitrectomy eyes, and merit further study. [28, 29] Finally, intravitreal povidone–iodine (e.g., 0.625%) has been proposed as an adjunct in refractory cases in small series from East Asia; while promising as salvage therapy, broader safety and efficacy data are needed before routine adoption. [30, 31]

Limitations

Due to the single-center retrospective design and the unequal distribution of patients among treatment groups, particularly the small tap-and-inject cohort, comparisons between treatment modalities should be interpreted with caution. The limited sample size reduces the robustness of the estimated hazard ratios and increases the uncertainty of the observed associations. Furthermore, accurate determination of symptom onset would have added important information to the analysis of the interval between diagnosis and surgical intervention. This gap highlights the need for future prospective studies to collect such data systematically. As a tertiary referral center, our cohort likely overrepresents severe or complex endophthalmitis and includes many externally referred patients with variable, often undocumented delays and prior treatments before presentation, introducing selection and timing biases that may limit generalizability and potentially confound associations between initial management and outcomes.

Conclusions

Our data indicate that delayed surgery in exogenous cases of endophthalmitis is associated with a high rate of re-intervention. This rate can be reduced by 63% when tap-and-inject is used as the initial treatment approach. Among patients presenting to the emergency department with postoperative endophthalmitis, visual acuity was the only factor predictive of visual outcome. Despite these findings, there remains a strong need for prospective studies to better define optimal timing and treatment strategies in endophthalmitis.
