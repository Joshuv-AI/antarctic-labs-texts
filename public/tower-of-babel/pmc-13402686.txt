Mast cell–driven remodeling of pulmonary immune and stromal landscapes in COVID-19
Authors: D. A. Atiakshin, O. A. Burgasova, A. A. Kostin, A. V. Alekhnovich, A. T. Prikhodko, V. A. Galieva, M. A. Ignatyuk, M. M. Mirolaev, I. D. Klabukov, D. S. Baranovskii, M. V. Taranova, D. Elieh-Ali-Komi, I. Buchwalow, M. Tiemann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The role of mast cells (MCs) in the pathogenesis of COVID-19 remains poorly understood. In the present study, we profiled the spatial phenotype of MC interaction with the pulmonary immune and stromal landscapes. The study included lung tissue samples from 12 COVID-19 patients and 12 patients with undifferentiated community-acquired pneumonia (CAP). Histochemical, monoplex, and multiplex immunohistochemical staining with spatial phenotyping, profiling, and mapping of MCs, as well as interactions with the immune and stromal landscape of the lungs, including elastic and collagen fibers, were used. Compared with other respiratory infections, SARS-CoV-2-associated pneumonia caused an increase in the pool of MCs in the lungs simultaneously with the activation of targeted secretion of tryptase and the formation of significant differences in the spatial phenotype of colocalization of MCs with immunocompetent cells and fibers. The intensity of juxtacrine and paracrine interactions of MC with cytotoxic lymphocytes and monocytes increased, initiating the polarization of macrophages into type 2. In this case, MCs and type 2 macrophages formed extended multicellular chains in the form of a functional syncytium, creating profibrotic niches of the local tissue microenvironment. Under COVID-19 conditions, an unexpected increase in MC interaction with elastic extracellular matrix (ECM) fibers and localized tryptase release revealed new insights into tissue tensometry and ECM remodeling. New spatial patterns of interaction between MCs and the immune and stromal landscape may have beneficial effects in developing therapeutic solutions for fibrotic change prevention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47864-1.

Introduction

Mast cells (MCs) are cKIT+ /FcεRI+ cells of innate immunity that develop from CD34 + hematopoietic progenitors released from bone marrow1. Profiling of patterns recognition receptors (PRRs) in MCs has shown that these cells are not only equipped with surface receptors to detect microorganisms, but also possess a rich array of cytoplasmic and endosomal receptors-primarily RIG-I-like receptors (RLRs), and Toll-like receptors (TLRs)- capable of recognizing viral components, including single- and double-stranded viral RNA (i.e. TLR7 and TLR-3, respectively) or single-stranded DNA (recognized by TLR9)2,3.

MC involvement in the pathology of COVID-19 has been studied in different cellular and molecular aspects and a pilling number of studies using lung tissues of COVID-19 patients reported that: a) MCs accumulate in different anatomical sites of lungs in large numbers, b) MCs degranulation is frequently observed in lung tissues, c) tryptase is elevated in serum of these patients, d) lung tissue damage is correlated to MC density, e) MCs produce pro-inflammatory cytokines driving cytokine storm4–9.

MC accumulation in the lungs during SARS-CoV-2 infection contributes to rapid lung damage by releasing proteases, histamine, and other mediators that exacerbate epithelial and endothelial injury7,10–14.

One of the negative effects of COVID-19 is intraorgan fibrosis; its intensity correlates with the state of MCs7. MC interaction with fibroblasts through the exosomal release of local mediators is a unique mechanism for the production of excess collagen15. It is not surprising that the highest activity of genes responsible for the expression of membrane receptors of immunocompetent cells, determining signaling cascades that transmit intercellular signals correlating with the severity of COVID-19 symptoms, was found in the pulmonary MCs during coronavirus infection16.

To date, MCs are considered as a promising pharmacological target in the treatment of SARS-CoV-2 PNA, in particular, to block pro-inflammatory mediators, including histamine and tryptase17–25.

However, despite the progress in the study of COVID-19 pathogenesis, the spatial phenotype of MC interactions with immune cells and the extracellular matrix (ECM) remains unaddressed. Only one study indirectly interpreted the potential MC involvement in intercellular signaling based on transcriptome analysis4. In this context, the present study, employing spatial proteomics technologies, provides an important contribution to the currently available findings on the mechanisms of mast cell involvement in the regulation of the spatial architecture of the immune and stromal landscape of the lung in COVID-19.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethical aspects

This was a retrospective, single-center, case–control morphological study. The investigation was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (World Medical Association, 2013 revision) and was approved by the Local Ethics Committee of the Infectious Clinical Hospital No. 1 of the Moscow City Health Department (Protocol dated December 28, 2022, Moscow, Russia). Informed consent was obtained from all patients or their next of kin. The studied lung tissue samples were qualified as excess clinical material, were depersonalized, and were not linked to patients’ personal data.

Characteristics of the main group

The main group consisted of 12 patients (n = 12) with a first-time diagnosis of COVID-19 coronavirus infection (U07.1 according to ICD-10), hospitalized at the Infectious Clinical Hospital No. 1 of the Moscow City Health Department between November 17, 2020, and February 3, 2021. In 100% of cases, the diagnosis was confirmed by clinical and laboratory data, including the detection of SARS-CoV-2 RNA by real-time RT-PCR in biological material. The gender distribution was 5 males (41.67%) and 7 females (58.33%). The median age was 67.00 years [52.00; 91.00]. Patients were hospitalized between days 1 and 10 of the illness. The length of hospitalization ranged from 1 to 17 bed-days, with a median of 10 days. The cause of death in 100% of cases was acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS).

The severity of COVID-19 was assessed in accordance with the Temporary Guidelines of the Ministry of Health of the Russian Federation "Prevention, Diagnosis, and Treatment of Novel Coronavirus Infection (COVID-19)," version 6 of April 28, 202026, and the World Health Organization’s clinical management guidelines for severe acute respiratory infection when COVID-19 is suspected (March 13, 2020)27. Treatment was administered in accordance with these documents. The structure of comorbidities in the main group is presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Characteristics of the control group

The control group (comparison group) consisted of 12 patients (n = 12) with a clinical diagnosis of community-acquired pneumonia (J18.9 according to ICD-10), hospitalized at the Infectious Clinical Hospital No. 1 of the Moscow City Health Department between 2018 and 2019 inclusive (prior to the COVID‑19 pandemic). Bacteriological examination of bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) was performed during autopsy for all patients. In 2 out of 12 cases (16.67%), Streptococcus pneumoniae was identified as the etiological agent. The gender distribution was 8 males (66.67%) and 4 females (33.33%). The median age was 63.00 years [54.00; 84.00]. Patients were hospitalized between days 1 and 9 of the illness. The length of hospitalization ranged from 1 to 19 bed-days, with a median of 9 days.

The severity of pneumonia was assessed in accordance with the clinical guidelines of the Russian Respiratory Society and the Interregional Association for Clinical Microbiology and Antimicrobial Chemotherapy "Community-acquired Pneumonia in Adults"26, as well as the recommendations of the European Respiratory Society (ERS/ESICM/ESCMID/ALAT, 2023)27. Treatment was administered in accordance with these guidelines. The structure of comorbidities in the control group is presented in Supplementary Table 2.

Group comparability

To assess the comparability of the main and control groups with respect to demographic and clinical parameters, the Mann–Whitney U test (for quantitative variables with non-normal distribution) and Fisher’s exact test (for categorical variables with expected frequencies < 5) were applied (Supplementary Table 3). Statistical data processing was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (v. 26.0). Quantitative data are presented as median (Me) with range [min; max] or interquartile range [Q1; Q3]. The Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare quantitative variables between the two independent groups. Fisher’s exact test was used to compare qualitative variables. The threshold for statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. No statistically significant differences were found between the groups regarding sex, age, length of hospitalization, or comorbidity structure (all p > 0.05), confirming the comparability of the groups and enabling a valid intergroup comparison of the results (Supplementary Table 3).

Tissue probe staining

The study material consisted of lung tissue samples obtained during autopsy of 24 deceased patients (12—main group, 12—control group). Tissue samples were fixed in 4% buffered formaldehyde, routinely embedded in paraffin, and processed using standard protocols28. Histological sections of 5 μm thickness were prepared for histochemical staining, while 2.5 μm sections were used for monoplex and multiplex immunohistochemical staining following current standard operating procedures29.

Consistent with our earlier protocol recommendations, the step for blocking endogenous Fc receptors was intentionally excluded before primary antibody application30. After antigen retrieval and, when required, quenching endogenous peroxidase, sections were immunoreacted with primary antibodies. Controls

Control incubations were the samples lacking the primary antibodies or with substitution of primary antibodies by the same IgG species (Dianova, Hamburg, Germany) at the same final concentration as the primary antibodies. The exclusion of either the primary or the secondary antibody from the immunohistochemical reaction and the substitution of primary antibodies with the corresponding IgG at the same final concentration resulted in a lack of immunostaining. The specific and selective staining of different cells with the use of primary antibodies from the same species on the same preparation is, by itself, sufficient control for the immunostaining specificity.

Image acquisition

For the study of microsections stained according to histochemical and immunohistochemical protocols for light microscopy, stained tissue sections were observed using ZEISS AxioImager.Z2 equipped with Zeiss alpha Plan-Apochromat objective 100 × /1.46 OilDICM27, Zeiss Objective Plan-Apochromat 150 × /1.35 GlycDICCorrM27 and ZEISS Axiocam 712 color digital microscope camera and ZEISS Axiocam 712 mono digital microscope camera (Carl Zeiss Vision, Jena, Germany). Photomicrographs were obtained in some cases with a Nikon D-Eclipse C1 Si confocal microscope based on Nikon Eclipse 90i (Tokyo, Japan). The Mantra 2 Quantitative Pathology Imaging System for multiplex visualization (Akoya Biosciences, Marlborough, MA, USA) based on an Olympus BX43 microscope equipped with a scientific-grade multispectral 12-bit monochrome high-sensitivity CCD camera with a liquid crystal tunable spectral filter was used to determine the profile of specific proteases of mast cells, immune and stromal landscape of tumor microenvironment using the list of different OPAL fluorochromes.

Quantitative analysis

Quantitative analysis of the studied criteria involved total sections of lung fragments measuring at least 1.5 × 1.5 cm. Planimetric analysis was performed to identify the number of search cells (MCs, immune cells, and stromal cells) per unit area of the sections, the absolute and relative number of MCs and other cells, elastic fibers, and collagen fibers using the QuPath v0.5.1 (developed by the University of Edinburgh, UK)31.

Mapping and imaging of stained sections were performed after scanning the entire histological section using a digital pathology slide scanner (fluorescence) KF-FL-005 (Ningbo, Zhejiang, China), the × 40 objective of ScanScope CS (Leica Biosystems, Deer Park, IL, USA), and the Mantra 2 Quantitative Pathology Imaging System (Akoya Biosciences, Marlborough, MA, USA) based on Olympus BX43 microscope.

QuPath version 0.5.1 software31 was used to analyze the entire histological slide images. Stardist extension was used to perform nuclear segmentation based on the DAPI signal32. Further classification of segmented detections by phenotype was achieved by training a neural network with point annotations and subsequent iterative verification by a specialist. Autofluorescence was minimized using a dedicated reagent kit and by optimising exposure time during scanning. The maximum distance for contacting cells was defined as the sum of the major semi-axes of the nuclei, averaged ≤ 10 μm. Distance categories for further analyses were defined as follows: < 10 μm, contacting cells; 10–20 μm, cells within the paracrine interaction zone; > 20 μm, non-interacting cells. The minimum Euclidean distance between the centroids of classified cells was calculated using the "Detection centroid distances" function built into QuPath. The resulting Euclidean distances were categorized to determine the frequency of cell contacts, paracrine interactions, and instances where cells were located outside the interaction zone. At each iteration, results were reviewed by a specialist and parameters adjusted as necessary. Quantitative data were exported from QuPath v0.5.1 to R for further visualization and analysis33. Heatmaps were generated in R using the ggplot2 and hexbin packages. Each hexagonal bin (hexagon) represents an area of 8660 µm2. The density of interactions between cells of different populations was assessed based on the midpoints of the line segments connecting the centroids of the nuclei.

Mapping of structures (elastic fibers, collagen fibers) and mast cells was performed by training the pixel classifier built into QuPath, followed by exporting the segmentation masks to ImageJ for further processing/visualization.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using the SPSS software package (Version 13.0, IBM, Armonk, New York, United States). The results are presented as the mean (M) ± m (standard error of the mean). To assess the significance of the differences between the two groups, Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test in the case of a nonparametric distribution was used. Significance was considered as *—p < 0.05 compared to the community-acquired pneumonia (CAP), **—p < 0.01 compared to the CAP.

Results

MCs localized in the bronchial and vascular structures had larger secretory granules; they were adequately visualized even using classical toluidine blue staining (Fig. 1C, E, L). MCs could infiltrate rather large areas of the lung parenchyma, in which alterative signs, parenchyma destruction, diffuse damage to the alveoli, the formation of inflammatory infiltrates were observed. Diplex staining of tryptase and CD68 revealed active expression of macrosialin in a certain pool of MCs, indicating active phagocytic activity of MCs (Fig. 3A, B, C, G). Notably, macrosialin was characterized by a uniform distribution throughout the MC cytoplasm and was localized in some secretory granules (Fig. 3K-M). The number of CD68 + granules in MCs varied. Calculation of the phenotype of MCs Tryptase + CD68 + and Tryptase + CD68- showed that SARS-CoV-2 caused an increased number of MCs positive for macrosialin (Fig. 3G). It is also necessary to note the higher content of CD68 in mast cells. Despite a certain pattern of dominating CD68 localization in granules, macrosialin was also detected in the intergranular compartments of the MC cytoplasm (Fig. 3K, L). In COVID-19, the content of CD68 + in MCs was higher compared to CAP (Fig. 3G). In SARS-CoV-2 PNA, CD68 + was more frequently detected on the surface of MCs and the extracellular matrix when compared to CAP. The study of the profile and spatial landscape of immunocompetent cells demonstrated that SARS-CoV-2 PNA causes an increase in the number of T-lymphocytes, primarily cytotoxic (Fig. 2A, D). In terms of spatial distribution though, we determined the uneven distribution of CD8 + lymphocytes in the structural components of the respiratory region of the lungs, which revealed extremely pronounced signs during SARS-CoV-2 PNA (Fig. 2A).

T-lymphocytes, including cytotoxic, often formed numerous groups (Fig. 2F), were detected in close connection with the microcirculatory bed (Fig. 2E, H, I), and were found in the microenvironment of plasma cells (Fig. 2G). No difference in B-lymphocyte content was detected, while the number of plasma cells increased (Fig. 2D, L). In SARS-CoV-2 PNA, plasma cells formed a rather heterogeneous spatial landscape, accumulating in fairly clearly identified foci of lung parenchyma (Fig. 2B).

Furthermore, in SARS-CoV-2 PNA, a significant increase in the pool of CD14 + cells and CD163 + was observed, while the number of CD68-positive cells decreased compared to CAP (Fig. 2D, J). A significant integral increase in type 2 macrophages in the lungs was characterized by the features of the spatial phenotype of CD163 expression, primarily in the formation of loci with a high content (Fig. 4B, D). For example, in some patients, the number of macrophages increased sharply in limited tissue loci 100–200 μm in area, where they could become the dominant cell population compared to other cells (Fig. 4B). In addition, it is worth noting that in SARS-CoV-2 PNA, the number of endothelial cells with features of intraorgan distribution was increased (Fig. 2D, M). CD31 + cell mapping revealed that, in SARS-CoV-2 PNA, there were formed lung areas with the excessive endothelial content, the fact indicating excess angiogenesis in certain lung areas and no angiogenesis in other lung areas (Fig. 2C, D, M-P). The formation of areas with high and low microvascular development was particularly noticeable in lung areas with impaired alveolar structure of the respiratory tract.

Analysis of the MC interaction with other immunocompetent cells demonstrated both similar and different signs of intercellular signaling in CAP and SARS-CoV-2 PNA. The general trend of MC behavior in pneumonia of any genesis demonstrated their increased number in the loci of inflammatory changes.

Even with monoplex immunophenotyping of tryptase, it was possible to frequently observe images of MCs surrounded by 3–7 other cells, resembling other immunocompetent cells in their external morphological features. In some cases, MCs acted as a certain point of unification of other cells, with each of which intercellular interactions were detected (Fig. 2E).

The structural features of MCs were, firstly, the formation of cytoplasmic processes positive for tryptase (Figs. 1K-N, 5B), and a large number of immature granules types I and II presenting along with large mature secretory granules in the cytoplasm (Fig. 1G). Immature granules were in contact with large mature granules, which indicated an active exchange of secretome components. Secretory mechanisms were accompanied not only by the activation of exosomal transport but also by the release of autonomous large secretory granules into the intercellular matrix, visible even with metachromatic staining (Figs. 1C, N, 3D, 4N, P, 5B, E, G, I, 6C, K). Despite the individual features of the MC intercellular signaling phenotype in the lungs of each studied patient, some general regularities could be identified, thus indicating a fundamental difference in immunogenesis during SARS-CoV-2 infection due to MC participation. These regularities primarily concerned a strategic change in the activity of interaction with monocytes and macrophages as key components of the innate immune system involved in the regulation of the initiation, development and completion of numerous inflammatory diseases. Indeed, in SARS-CoV-2 PNA, if compared with CAP, MCs more actively interacted with type 2 macrophages (CD68 + CD163 +), monocytes (CD14 + CD68 +), cytotoxic lymphocytes and plasma cells (CD138 + CD38 +), as well as endothelial cells of the microcirculatory bed (CD31 +). In contrast, juxtacrine and paracrine effects with type 1 macrophages (CD68 + CD163 −) were reduced (Fig. 3H-J). The phenotype of MC interaction with type 2 macrophages obtains spatial features (Fig. 4F, G, H). We convincingly demonstrated the tryptase secretion with penetration into the cytoplasm and then into the nuclei of monocytes and type 2 macrophages (Fig. 4G).

Localization of specific MC protease in macrophages is well captured in micrographs taken using confocal microscopy transformed to 3D models.

It is also interesting that MCs and macrophages were formed in areas with the most significant morphogenetic rearrangements, in which these cells formed the spatial architecture of a multicellular network, or “functional” syncytium. Thus, in pneumonia, one can observe numerous evidence of juxtacrine MC contacts with macrophages; at significant distances, their alternation resembled the phenotype of multicellular chains creating morphogenetic networks in fairly large areas.

The same patterns of MC interaction with macrophages, but with greater severity, could be observed in COVID-19 (Fig. 4 M-P). We observed MC transformation into a special phenotype, which could be elongated in shape, as well as the formation of fairly long cytoplasmic outgrowths over significant distances (Supplementary 14). It should be noted that in such cases, MCs could affect a larger number of macrophages and other cells in the tissue microenvironment of the lungs. This allows us to assume that in COVID-19 infections, MCs have the potential to create conditions for contact network effects on numerous neighboring cells, thus, being able to function in certain ranges with specific morphogenetic effects of increased inflammation or regenerative activity and increased formation of connective tissue.

Similar patterns can be observed in interactions with type 1 macrophages. Typically, one MC could interact with several CD68 + cells (Fig. 3). However, the spatial phenotype of MC interactions with monocytes was predominantly local interactions, without visible signs of network formation affecting large areas of the tissue microenvironment.

Discussion

Following the emergence of COVID-19, MCs quickly drew attention as key immune players, with their potential contributions to disease pathology and symptomatology prompting numerous studies aimed at deciphering their role in the pathophysiology of the infection. Additionally, even after the introduction of a variety of vaccines, the exacerbation of allergic reactions such as hives in patients with MC-dependent diseases like urticaria once again put MCs under the spotlight35. In COVID-19, MCs are frequently activated, and the presence of degranulated mast cells in lung tissue is a common histopathological finding10.

Tryptase participates immediately in the proinflammatory background formation of the local tissue microenvironment of the lungs. Spatial patterns of tryptase secretion upon MC activation are the most important pathogenetic event for the development of the inflammatory process, the pronounced intensity or chronic nature of this process inevitably leads to the formation of profibrogenic niches. During progression, these loci of fibrotic changes can combine and capture much larger areas of the lungs. Chronic MC activation can induce persistent increased sensitivity to a number of components of the specific tissue microenvironment due to the restructuring of its proper receptor apparatus, which provokes higher secretion of biogenic amines, cytokines, and specific proteases with proinflammatory activity36,37. Tryptase secretion may play a critical role in disrupting the homeostasis of the tissue microenvironment of the airways and respiratory tract by altering the regulation of the cell growth or cell death processes38.

MC tryptase is characterized by high biological activity affecting the state of multiple cellular and non-cellular components of the tissue microenvironment39,40. A number of studies have demonstrated the active tryptase involvement in angiogenesis41 that is combined with connective tissue remodeling, secretion of growth factors, cytokines and chemokines, and matrix metalloproteinases42.

Although our study does not provide definitive evidence of a causal relationship between the increased number of mast cells and the heightened density of the microvasculature, a certain probability of tryptase-mediated angiogenic effects may be hypothesized. Such effects have been demonstrated in a number of tumors43,44, in the choroid in wet age-related macular degeneration45, in oral mucosal inflammation46, and in the growth of inflammatory odontogenic cysts47, among others.

From a pathobiological point of view, fibrosis is a protective reaction of the body aimed at isolating the inflammation site from the surrounding tissues and the systemic blood flow. Fibrous replacement of tissues results in a gradual loss of their specific functions and, upon reaching a certain scale, integral dysfunction of the organ.

The increase in tryptase-positive MCs, as well as the active tryptase secretion, detected in our study directly evidences the tryptase participation in the pathogenesis of fibrotic changes in the lungs, since the tryptase effects on increasing the migratory activity of fibroblasts, stimulating their mitotic division and activating the synthesis of collagen proteins have been widely known48. The statistically significantly more frequent colocalization of mast cells with collagen and elastic fibers in COVID-19 compared to community-acquired pneumonia provides objective evidence of the important contribution of mast cells to the development and progression of pulmonary fibrotic changes accompanying coronavirus infection49.

Tryptase potentiates the development of inflammation by interacting with Protease-Activated Receptor 2 (PAR-2) receptors on various phenotypes of tissue microenvironment cells42. As known, tryptase causes a stable increase in PAR-2 receptor expression in target cells, enhancing the effects of inflammation41,50,51.

Recently, the epigenetic effects of tryptase on target cells have been widely discussed52–55. Studies on the fundamental importance of tryptase transport into cell nuclei have demonstrated the ability of the protease to process core histones in the N-terminal tail and alter transcription processes52,54,55. In this case, the enzymatic activity of tryptase in the nucleus is stabilized by DNA molecules, which allows for long-term regulation of the state of histones52,55,56. This principle to regulate epigenetic modification of histones in nuclei represents specific functions of MC Tryptase55. Thus, the obtained results suggest the active use of epigenetic mechanisms in the development of tryptase biological effects on immunocompetent lung cells both in the extracrine and paracrine effects of MCs on monocytes, macrophages, lymphocytes, and other cells.

In our study, we first performed the profiling of the functional architecture of the MC interaction with immune cells of the lungs in pneumonia and coronavirus infection COVID-19 and displayed the key mechanisms of MC involvement in the development of intraorgan inflammation. The results suggest that COVID-19 significantly changes the MC interaction with other subpopulations of the immune cells of the lung tissue microenvironment, despite the existing individual features of each patient. The main differences related to a significantly decreased frequency of MC contact with type 1 macrophages and its increased interaction with monocytes, type 2 macrophages, cytotoxic lymphocytes, and endothelium.

As evidenced, macrophages play a key role in tissue repair and regeneration57–59. Notably, the balance between M1 and M2 types of macrophages is crucial in determining the trend and severity of pulmonary fibrosis60. The developing "acute respiratory distress syndrome" in COVID-19 is accompanied by an increased formation of CD163-expressing macrophages with a pro-fibrotic transcriptional phenotype from monocytes61,62. Type 2 macrophages contribute to fibrotic changes in ECM by increasing the production of specific inflammatory cytokines via the TLR-7/IRAK4/NF-κB signaling pathway63.

It is possible to assume direct MC involvement in this polarization of type 2 macrophages from monocytes in the new coronavirus infection COVID-1961. Our results verify the hypothesis that the juxtacrine and paracrine MC effects on monocytes contribute to their transformation into type 2 macrophages and, thus, creating a morphological basis for the pro-fibrotic status of the tissue microenvironment. Due to spatial phenotyping and profiling of the intensity of MCs and CD163 + type 2 macrophages interaction, MCs can be regarded to provide the formation of a specific architecture of functional intercellular networks of a syncytial nature contributing to excessive production of the ECM components. Apparently, by binding type 2 macrophages to each other, MCs can coordinate their secretory potential and enhance their pro-fibrotic effects creating tissue niches with a special status of an integrative-buffering metabolic environment in large areas of the lung tissue microenvironment, sized 200–300 μm or more. A close correlation between CD14⁺ monocytes and CD163⁺ macrophages and the severity of fibrosis has been demonstrated in various organs, including the liver64. Our results correlate with the data obtained in the study discussing the effect of trypsin, tryptase, and thrombin on polarized macrophages towards a pro-fibrotic M2a phenotype65.

Despite the decrease in type I macrophages with the CD68 + CD163 − tryptase − phenotype in the lungs of patients with SARS-CoV-2 PNA, it is necessary to note the increased pool of MCs containing macrosialin. The predominant location of CD68, in the cell is in late endosomes, and one of its functions is known as a role in peptide transport or antigen processing and ensuring phagocytosis66–68. In addition, CD68 is critical in diverse physiological and pathological processes, including inflammation69. CD68 can rapidly translocate between the plasma membrane and endosomes70, accounting for the diffuse cytoplasmic staining of MCs detected in our study, along with the accumulation of macrosialin in secretory granules. The increase in CD68 may indicate a more active MC involvement in antigen processing or presentation in COVID-19.

An increase in CD8 + T cells during the development of COVID-19 has been shown in several studies71. Our findings of increased juxtacrine and paracrine MC interactions with CD8 + T lymphocytes assume the MC involvement in the activation of cytotoxic lymphocytes via perforin and granzyme. The results are consistent with the known immunomodulatory effects of MCs, including activation and proliferation of antigen-specific CD8 + T cells via direct immune contact with subsequent major histocompatibility (MHC) class I-dependent antigen presentation72. MC effects on CD8 + T cells might be mediated by increased secretion of IL-2, IFN-γ, and macrophage inflammatory protein-1α (MIP-1α).73.

Recent studies have manifested the close involvement of CD8 + lymphocytes in the implementation of the fibrotic changes program, including the lung tissue74,75. Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis is accompanied not only by an increased number of intraorgan CD8 + lymphocytes, but also by their activity, indicating an important contribution of cytotoxic lymphocytes to the progression of excessive ECM formation. It has been demonstrated that CD8 + lymphocytes have special transcriptional features in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) indicating high cytotoxicity74. This state is likely to be achieved by juxtacrine and paracrine MC effects on T cells and epigenetic effects of tryptase53,55,56,76,77. Thus, on the one hand, the increase in the intrapulmonary population of CD8 + T cells that we detected may be a consequence of increased migration from the microcirculatory bed due to more active MC interaction with endothelial cells. On the other hand, it is necessary to take into account that the targeted MC interaction with CD8 + lymphocytes is accompanied by an increased cytotoxicity and an increase in their profibrotic effects.

Multidimensional morphological analysis enabled us to map lung mast cells in relation to ECM fibrosis, supporting the assessment of collagen fibrillogenesis. Compared with CAP, COVID-19 is associated with an increased number of tissue niches exhibiting conditions favorable for excessive ECM formation. This was evidenced not only by a more pronounced increase in the number of mast cells within specific loci of the lung microenvironment showing signs of active secretome degranulation, but also by the frequency of their interactions with type 2 macrophages, cytotoxic T cells, and the fibrous component. It is possible that these phenomena may be, to some extent, related to the administration of mechanical ventilation in the supportive care of COVID-19 patients, in which mechanical stress is recognized as a risk factor for fibrosis development78.

In addition, we first performed triple labeling with simultaneous identification of collagen fibers, elastic network, and MCs with digital spatial profiling of the distribution density of profibrotic niches of the tissue microenvironment. The results of imaging and histoprofiling using artificial intelligence technology showed that COVID-19 is accompanied by an increased content of the fibrous component of the ECM (elastic and collagen framework, including reticular fibers) in the lungs, and the frequency of juxtacrine and paracrine MC effects on both collagen fibers and elastic fibers. The observed findings may provide informative evidence of mast cell involvement in the development of fibrotic changes in COVID-19, partly attributable to their active participation in collagen fibrillogenesis48. To our surprise, we found a close relationship between MCs and elastic fibers, which may reflect their physiological function in regulating the tensometry of the tissue microenvironment. It is known that elastic fibers confer elasticity and resilience and may also play an important role in inducing biochemical reactions in cells and regulating cellular behavior in response to mechanical forces originating from the tissue microenvironment79,80. This may explain why the effect of mast cells on elastic fibers could, on one hand, lead to disruption of tissue niche cell integration into the elastic scaffold of the tissue. On the other hand, the results of our study may indicate the involvement of MCs in elastogenesis and the formation of excessive elastic fibers in the lungs of COVID-19 patients. We observed more frequent localization of MCs with abnormal elastic fibers in the skin in Marfan syndrome81,82; however, the different biological context of the pathogenesis of this hereditary disease precludes a direct analogy with the mechanisms of elastic scaffold remodeling in COVID-19 and requires further investigation.

There is evidence of the role of tryptase in adipose tissue fibrosis83,84. It is possible that the active MC participation in the formation of fibrotic changes in SARS-CoV-2 PNA resulted not from the tryptase secretion solely, but also from other specific proteases mainly carboxypeptidase A3 (CPA3) and chymase, as evidenced by the results reported in several studies10,85. Increased levels of all three specific proteases closely correlated with the severity of the clinical picture86. It was demonstrated that a higher number of mast cells expressing CPA3 correlated with the developing fibrotic changes in the lungs8. This indirectly confirms our data on the increased number of connective tissue-type MCs in the respiratory region of the lungs, which are known to have a triad of specific proteases (tryptase, chymase, and CPA3), regardless of the organ location87. One of the possible mechanisms for the production of excess collagen in the ECM of the lungs may be exosomes of MCs containing mediators with profibrogenic effects15,88,89.

Thus, our study has first stated the spatial patterns of MC integration into the lung stromal landscape with the formation of multicomponent morphogenetic clusters. Likely that SARS CoV-2 causes increased communication between MCs and cells of the lung stromal landscape through the forming of tryptase-containing exosomes, tryptase-positive secretory granules to α-SMA-positive and CD31-positive cells, as well as a fibrous component, thereby stimulating the formation of fibrous niches in the lungs.

Conclusion

The revealed regularities of juxtacrine and paracrine MC interactions with other lung cell populations and ECM components in SARS-CoV-2 PNA manifest novel mechanisms of targeted induction of inflammatory and fibrotic changes in the local tissue microenvironment. The detected immunomodulatory mechanism of MCs goes beyond the biological effects of soluble mediators and includes interactions with other cell populations of both innate and acquired immunity, as well as the fibrous component of the ECM.
