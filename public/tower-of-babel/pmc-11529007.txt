Total knee arthroplasty combined with simultaneous open reduction and internal fixation in the treatment of knee osteoarthritis with patellar fracture: a case report and review of the literature
Authors: DongFeng Cai, JiMo Li, Song Hong
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with severe knee osteoarthritis combined with patellar fracture are rare, and it is unclear whether single-stage surgery affects the enhanced recovery after surgery.Case presentationThis case report describes two patients of Han nationality. A 69-year-old male patient was previously diagnosed with severe knee osteoarthritis of the left knee joint and was treated conservatively with long-term oral nonsteroidal antiinflammatory drugs and intraarticular injection of sodium hyaluronate. He was hospitalized for acute left knee injury owing to a fall from a height. He was diagnosed with knee osteoarthritis of the left knee joint and patellar fracture. Another 74-year-old female patient was previously diagnosed with severe knee osteoarthritis, long-term oral nonsteroidal antiinflammatory drugs and topical Chinese medicine to relieve the pain. A month ago, she fell down the stairs and fractured the patella in her left knee, she was diagnosed with severe left knee osteoarthritis combined with an old left patella fracture. Both patients were successfully treated by single-stage primary total knee arthroplasty and fixation of the patellar fracture. They both carried out early functional exercise normally and successfully achieved enhanced recovery.ConclusionSuccessful single stage total knee replacement with patella management can not only reduce the number of operations, but also enable patients to achieve rapid postoperative recovery. However, single stage may increase the risk of local surgical complications. Therefore, for most patients, especially those with low risk of anesthesia related complications, standard of treatment remains first fixing the patellar fracture and when the fracture is healed proceed with the total knee arthroplasty.Keywords: TKA, ORIF, Knee osteoarthritis, Patellar fracture

Introduction

Knee osteoarthritis (KOA) is a common degenerative disorder of the knee joint, and total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is currently the only effective treatment for end-stage KOA [1]. In recent years, with advancements in surgical techniques and enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) protocols, patients with TKA can achieve significant pain relief and good functional recovery early on [2–7]. While the concept of patients with end-stage KOA requiring TKA treatment has gained widespread acceptance, there is still a lack of unified treatment strategies for patients with end-stage KOA with concomitant patellar fractures, as these cases are relatively rare in clinical practice. For patients with significantly displaced patellar fractures that are unsuitable for conservative treatment, staging the open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) of the patellar fracture and TKA would require two separate surgeries. If patellar fracture ORIF and TKA are performed simultaneously, early postoperative rehabilitation exercises become a crucial and challenging issue. When the patient actively or passively flexes and extends the knee joint, the patella is subjected to the tension from the quadriceps muscle and the stress from the anterior aspect of the femoral prosthesis. If the patellar fracture fragments cannot be securely fixed, the knee joint requires immobilization with a cast or brace for several weeks postoperatively to prevent redisplacement of the patellar fracture [8]. Early postoperative knee joint immobilization precludes early rehabilitation after TKA, which, to a certain extent, affects early knee flexion and extension function and hinders rapid recovery [9]. Therefore, when combining TKA with simultaneous patellar fracture ORIF, it is crucial to achieve two aspects of success intraoperatively: (1) satisfactory knee joint flexion and extension gap balancing and joint prosthesis alignment, and (2) adequate reduction and secure initial internal fixation of the patellar fracture. In this article, we followed up on two patients who concurrently had end-stage KOA and ipsilateral transverse patellar fractures with significant displacement and impairment of the extensor mechanism. We performed simultaneous patellar fracture ORIF and TKA for them. Postoperatively, rehabilitation exercises were guided according to the standard TKA postoperative rehabilitation strategy, achieving early recovery and satisfactory treatment outcomes.

Case 1

A 69-year-old Han male patient was admitted to the hospital 10 hours after falling from a height of 4 m, injuring his left knee joint. The patient had severe KOA (stage IV) in the left knee joint before the injury and had been receiving conservative treatment with long-term oral nonsteroidal antiinflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and intra-articular injections of sodium hyaluronate. In the past year, the pain had significantly worsened, and pain relief from oral medications and sodium hyaluronate injections was not significant. Upon physical examination, we found that the patient’s left knee joint was swollen and had obvious tenderness. He was unable to actively flex or extend his left knee joint. X-rays suggested severe KOA in the left knee joint, accompanied by a left patellar fracture. The left patella had a transverse fracture with separation and displacement, and osteoporosis was present (Fig. 1A, B). The patient had type II diabetes and hypertension, which were well-controlled with oral metformin and nifedipine sustained-release tablets, respectively. We decided to perform simultaneous TKA combined with patellar fracture ORIF. The surgical approach utilized the conventional left knee joint anterior midline incision with a medial parapatellar approach. The medial parapatellar incision was at least 1 cm from the medial edge of the patella. The patella was not everted, and only slight traction was applied to expose the surgical area. A minimal amount of the infrapatellar fat pad was excised to provide adequate exposure of the surgical site. We used the measured osteotomy method, in which the femur was osteotomized at 6° of lateral valgus and 3° of external rotation according to preoperative measurements, and the the tibial component is placed in external rotation. The center of the tibial component was in the same line as the medial third of the tibial tuberosity and the center of the ankle joint. Intramedullary localization was performed on the femur side and extramedullary localization on the tibia side. After the tibial and femur prostheses were installed, we straightened the knee joint, the patellar fracture ends were reduced under direct vision, and two 2.0 mm Kirschner wires were passed through the patella parallel to its longitudinal axis. Tension band wiring was performed in a figure-of-eight and circular pattern to fix the patella, with the wires wrapped around the patellar edges beneath the soft tissues. The ends of the Kirschner wires and the junctions of the wires were bent and buried within the soft tissues to ensure that they would not scrape or impact the knee joint prosthesis during joint movement. We slightly everted the patella in the knee extension position to expose the articular surface of the patella. Then, we used a pendulum saw to trim the articular surface of the patella and remove osteophytes, and we denervated the patella by electrocautery. Intraoperative “no thumb test” showed a satisfactory patellar tracking, with no displacement of the patellar fracture surfaces during extreme flexion and extension of the knee joint. The medial joint capsule and extensor retinaculum were reconstructed using absorbable sutures. A drainage tube was placed, and the incision was closed in layers. Intraoperative periarticular injection cocktail. Patient controlled analgesia (PCA) combined with oral NSAIDs was used for postoperative analgesia. The drain was removed the next day. The patient was encouraged to perform ankle pump exercises on the day of surgery. On the second postoperative day, passive flexion up to 100° was achieved with the assistance of a continuous passive motion (CPM) machine, and the patient could walk with weight-bearing using a walker. On the third postoperative day, the patient could independently perform straight leg raises. At 5 months postoperatively, the patellar fracture had healed, and the patient could walk normally. The left knee joint had a flexion of 110° and an extension of 0° (Fig. 2), with no symptoms of extensor lag. X-rays showed satisfactory healing of the patellar fracture, as well as satisfactory joint spaces and prosthesis alignment (Fig. 1C, D).

Case 2

The second patient was a 74-year-old Han female who had been diagnosed with left KOA 10 years prior. She had been taking oral NSAIDs and applying traditional Chinese medicine externally to relieve her left knee pain. In the past 3 months, the pain had worsened. Then, 1 month ago, she accidentally fell while walking downstairs, exacerbating the pain in her left knee and limiting her mobility. Physical examination revealed a flexion and varus deformity of the left knee joint, with a varus angle of approximately 5°. Active flexion could reach 100°, but she was unable to actively extend her knee, with a passive extension of −20°. She had a 10-year history of hypertension, which was well-controlled with nifedipine sustained-release tablets. Preoperative cardiac ultrasound revealed chronic heart failure. X-rays indicated severe KOA in the left knee, accompanied by an old left patellar fracture. The patella had a transverse fracture with separation and displacement; the fracture ends were sclerotic. Moderate to severe osteoporosis was present (Fig. 3A, B). We performed simultaneous left patellar fracture ORIF combined with left TKA using an anterior midline incision of the knee joint and a medial parapatellar approach. Intraoperatively, we observed a transversely separated and displaced patellar fracture, with tearing of the anterior soft tissues. Only some soft tissue connections remained on the medial and lateral sides of the fracture surfaces, and fibrous tissue filled the fracture gap. The fracture ends were sclerotic, and moderate to severe osteoporosis was present. We first performed a left TKA, with the medial parapatellar incision at least 1 cm from the medial edge of the patella. During the operation, the patella was not everted, and only slight traction was applied to expose the surgical area. A minimal amount of the infrapatellar fat pad was excised to provide adequate exposure of the surgical site. Similar to case 1, a TKA was performed using the measured osteotomy method, in which the femur was osteotomized at 5° of lateral valgus and 3° of external rotation according to preoperative measurements. The installation principles of the femoral and tibial components were similar to those in case 1. Then, we straightened the knee joint and cleared the fibrous tissue from the patellar fracture surfaces and debrided the sclerotic bone until bleeding bone was encountered. The patellar fracture ends were reduced under direct vision, and two 2.0 mm Kirschner wires were passed through the patella parallel to its longitudinal axis. Tension band wiring was performed in a figure-of-eight and circular pattern to fix the patella, with the wires wrapped around the patellar edges beneath the soft tissues. The ends of the Kirschner wires and the junctions of the wires were bent and buried within the soft tissues to ensure that they would not scrape or impact the knee joint prosthesis during joint movement. The treatment of the patellar surface was similar to that of case 1. Intraoperative “no thumb test” showed a satisfactory patellar tracking, with no displacement of the patellar fracture surfaces during extreme flexion and extension of the knee joint. The medial joint capsule and extensor retinaculum were reconstructed using absorbable sutures. A drainage tube was placed, and the incision was closed in layers. The analgesic and postoperative rehabilitation programs were similar to those of case 1. On the second postoperative day, the patient could actively flex the left knee up to 100° and walk with weight-bearing using a walker. On the third postoperative day, she could independently perform straight leg raises, with an extension of −5°. There were no symptoms of extensor lag (Fig. 4). X-rays showed satisfactory reduction of the patellar fracture, with satisfactory joint spaces and prosthesis alignment (Fig. 3C, D).

Discussion

Patients with severe KOA and concomitant patellar fractures are relatively rare in clinical practice, and there are currently no standardized guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of such patients. If the patellar fracture displacement is less than 4 mm or the articular step-off is less than 3 mm, and the extensor mechanism is intact, conservative treatment can be considered. This typically involves immobilization with a cast or brace for 4–6 weeks, followed by gradual joint mobilization and weight-bearing as appropriate. Numerous studies have demonstrated that conservative treatment can yield satisfactory outcomes [10–12]. Although these studies primarily focused on patients with isolated patellar fractures or periprosthetic patellar fractures, we believe that even in patients with concomitant end-stage OA, if the patellar fracture meets the criteria for conservative treatment, it can be managed conservatively first, followed by TKA after fracture healing. This approach allows patients to undergo only one surgery. However, when a patellar fracture is not suitable for conservative treatment, staged treatment for the patellar fracture and osteoarthritis of the knee is the standard of treatment. One staged surgery may lead to higher postoperative complications especially when the patient has a severe comminuted patellar fracture, or extremely severe osteoporosis that prevents reliable internal fixation, or an open patellar fracture [13, 14]. These patients are more suitable for fixing the patellar fracture and then receiving TKA after the fracture has healed, which can minimize the risk of complications caused by patellar fracture surgery. However, staged management of the patellar fracture and knee osteoarthritis inevitably increases the number of surgeries for the patient and the overall hospital stay. This is especially concerning for patients with multiple comorbidities, such as heart disease, diabetes, or pulmonary conditions, as staged surgeries undoubtedly increase the anesthesia-related risks. Moreover, studies have reported that previous ORIF of knee fractures may increase the risk of postoperative infection following subsequent TKA [15]. A history of prior knee surgery may also affect the postoperative function after subsequent TKA, which may be related to the formation of periarticular soft tissue scarring [16, 17]. Therefore, if patients with more comorbid underlying disease and higher anesthetic risk with transverse patellar fractures unsuitable for conservative treatment and concomitant end-stage KOA, simultaneous TKA combined with patellar fracture ORIF may be a treatment strategy to consider.

However, compared with simple primary TKA, performing simultaneous TKA and patellar fracture ORIF may increase the uncertainty of postoperative knee joint functional recovery. For patients undergoing simple primary TKA, early postoperative functional exercises mainly include active and passive knee flexion and extension, straight leg raises, and ankle pump exercises to maximize the restoration of knee joint function. When we conduct early postoperative rehabilitation training for patients undergoing simultaneous TKA and patellar ORIF, the patella is subjected to the pulling force from the quadriceps tendon and the stress from the femoral component trochlea during knee flexion. This may lead to redisplacement of the patellar fracture ends and even disruption of the extensor mechanism [18, 19]. If the patient has poor patellar bone quality or unreliable internal fixation, the risk of patellar fracture displacement is further increased during early postoperative functional exercises. To prevent this from occurring, surgeons often recommend immobilizing the knee joint with a cast or brace for 4 to 6 weeks postoperatively before gradually resuming functional exercises. However, early postoperative knee immobilization may affect early or long-term functional recovery after TKA [20–22]. Additionally, early postoperative immobilization after TKA is not conducive to shortening hospital stays, alleviating pain, and may lead to quadriceps atrophy, increase the risk of urinary and pulmonary complications, and increase the risk of pressure sores and osteoporosis [12, 23]. Therefore, for these patients, achieving appropriate and secure fixation of the patellar fracture intraoperatively is crucial for early postoperative rehabilitation exercises and preventing postoperative complications.

Common internal fixation methods for patellar fractures include tension band wiring, cannulated screws, or nonabsorbable sutures. However, currently, no single fixation method has demonstrated clear superiority [12]. Among these, the tension band construct is a classic and widely used technique, with commonly used materials including Kirschner wires, steel wires, cannulated screws, and nonabsorbable sutures. This construct converts the longitudinal pulling force generated by the quadriceps muscle into compressive stress on the patellofemoral joint surface, which helps counteract the pulling force of the quadriceps and maintain fracture reduction. However, Meng et al. found a complication rate as high as 31.6% with tension band constructs [24]. It is worth noting that in their study, the fixation method with the highest failure rate was the tension band construct using Kirschner wires combined with figure-of-eight titanium cables, while the construct using compression screws combined with circular titanium cables reduced the complication rate by nearly half (16.7%). Bai et al. found that the complication rate of Kirschner wires or double-headed compression screws combined with circular wire tension band constructs was lower than that of Kirschner wires or double-headed compression screws combined with figure-of-eight wire tension band constructs. On the other hand, some scholars believe that cannulated screws may provide stronger stability and compression than Kirschner wires, and cannulated screw tension band constructs may achieve better clinical outcomes [25–28]. However, there are reports in literature that the protruding portion of cannulated screws cannot be well-buried and may cause clinical symptoms leading to internal fixation failure [29]. In a cyclic loading experiment, protrusion of the screw tail may increase local stress or reduce the ability of the internal fixation construct to resist fracture separation [30]. Buried double-headed compression screws can avoid complications related to screw protrusion and increase fixation strength. However, it is uncertain whether they have the same fixation effect in patients with poor patellar bone quality [18]. Martin et al. found that headless screw constructs had a higher failure rate compared with headed screw constructs [31]. In summary, the tension band construct for patellar fractures is widely applicable, but the appropriate internal fixation materials and specific construct design should be selected based on the patient’s patellar bone quality.

Rao et al. reported two patients, one of whom had an intact patellar retinaculum and an undisplaced patella. After completing TKA, conservative treatment was chosen for the patella. The other patient underwent simultaneous TKA combined with patellar fracture ORIF, using Semitendinosus, Fiber wire, and Fiber tape for patellar fixation [32]. This patient began functional rehabilitation 3 months after surgery with the knee joint immobilized in a cast, and the range of motion was 10–70° at 4 months postoperatively. Surendran et al. used non-absorbable sutures to fix the patella in their reported case [33]. The patient was passively flexed to 100° at 4–6 weeks postoperatively and started weight-bearing at 6 weeks. The two patients with patellar fractures treated surgically by Rao and Surendran had good bone quality and relatively intact extensor retinaculum, making non-absorbable suture fixation of the fracture ends a potentially good choice. Patel reported a case of a patient with a chronic patellar fracture. After completing TKA, they performed simultaneous Kirschner wire combined with figure-of-eight wire tension band fixation of the patellar fracture, achieving good results [34]. This demonstrates that Kirschner wire and wire tension bands can be used for the treatment of patellar fractures in such patients. Moreover, Zhu and Agarwal et al. also found that tension band constructs with a combination of figure-of-eight and circular configurations could provide a higher fixation success rate [35, 36]. Both of the patients we reported in this article had varying degrees of osteoporosis and rupture of the extensor retinaculum. To enhance fixation, we used Kirschner wires combined with figure-of-eight wire tension band and circular wire cerclage fixation. Both patients achieved early recovery of knee joint function with significant pain relief, without internal fixation-related complications.

For simple primary TKA, postoperative patellofemoral complications remain a challenging problem affecting up to 8% of patients [37, 38]. The causes of patellofemoral complications may be related to patient-, surgeon- and implant-related factors [39]. The implant-related patellofemoral joint deserves even more caution in patients undergoing one staged TKA with patellar fracture ORIF. In both patients, a well-reduced articular surface of the patella and solid initial fixation were equally important as good alignment of the knee prosthesis. The femoral condyle was osteotomized at 3°of external rotation, which is thought to be beneficial in restoring normal patellofemoral tracking [40]. Moreover, we took the middle third of the tibial tubercle and the center of the ankle joint as anatomical reference points, and installed the tibial platform with appropriate external rotation to avoid the internal rotation of tibial components leading to pain in the medial or anterior knee joint after TKA [41]. Overstufffing, another important cause of anterior knee pain, is also associated with increased patellofemoral pressure and decreased knee motion [42]. This may increase the risk of redisplacement of the fracture in patients with patellar fractures. To reduce overstufffng, the thickness of the femoral bone cuts should match the thickness of the prosthesis, and the right component size should be chosen [39]. We used posterior-referenced systems to accurately measure the osteotomy of the femur so that the osteotomy surface of the anterior femoral condyle was in the same plane as the anterior cortex. And then, we chose patellar denervation with electrocautery, which is thought to reduce the incidence of anterior knee pain [43].

Avascular necrosis of the patella is a serious complication following TKA, the prevalence is at 0.05%–2% [44]. Patellar ischemia leading to patellar necrosis may be associated with secondary infection or nonunion [12, 45, 46], which may increase the risk of postoperative complications. Therefore, protecting the patellar blood supply is of utmost importance [47]. The patellar blood supply originates from the extrapatellar circumferential anastomotic ring, with intraosseous vessels flowing out from this ring. The traditional approach for total knee arthroplasty is the anterior medial parapatellar approach, which may completely interrupt the medial blood supply to the patella, leaving only the lateral blood supply. Thus, we should avoid lateral patellar retinacular release intraoperatively [48]. Fortunately, good intraoperative patellar trajectory was obtained in these two patients, and lateral patellar retinacular release was not required [49]. Moreover, considering that the superior genicular artery lies within the vastus medialis obliquus muscle, some researchers recommend that the medial parapatellar incision should be at least 1 cm from the patellar edge to protect this vascular pathway [48]. Additionally, intraoperative excision of the infrapatellar fat pad should be minimized to preserve the inferior polar vessels and the transverse infrapatellar branches. Furthermore, Stofel et al. used Doppler ultrasound and found that everting the patella may exacerbate patellar ischemia, while slight traction on the patella to expose the surgical site can effectively avoid interference with the patellar blood supply [50]. Therefore, we strictly adhered to these principles intraoperatively, protecting the patellar blood supply while exposing the surgical site.

TKA combined with simultaneous ORIF in the treatment of KOA with patellar fracture allows patients to avoid two surgeries. However, compared with staged surgery, a one-stage surgery brings some other uncertainties. For example, loosening of the internal fixation and collision or friction of the internal fixation against the knee prosthesis; patella fracture not healing; patellofemoral joint abnormalities. In addition, patients undergoing a one-stage procedure are not candidates for intraoperative patellar resurfacing because of the presence of a patellar fracture, and patellar resurfacing may offer benefits to some patients [51, 52]. Thus, for most patients, first fixing the patellar fracture and when the fix is healed proceeding with the TKA is still a standard treatment.

Our study has some limitations. For example, we cannot confirm whether simultaneous surgery increases blood loss or requires a longer single operative time compared with staged surgery. The two patients reported in this study had satisfactory short-term follow-up results, but the long-term efficacy of this treatment approach needs to be further confirmed with larger sample sizes and longer-term follow-up studies.

Conclusion

Preoperative severe knee osteoarthritis combined with patellar fracture is rare in knee surgery patients. For patients with high risk of anesthesia related complications owing to many coexisting diseases, successful single stage TKA with patella management can reduce the number of operations for patients, reducing the risk of anesthesia complications and facilitating early and rapid recovery. However, single stage surgery may increase the risk of complications related to the patellofemoral joint and internal fixation. Therefore, for most patients, first fixing the patellar fracture and when the fix is healed proceed with the TKA is still a standard treatment.
