Adsorption-assisted removal of chlorinated pollutants using a magnetically recoverable Fe3O4/CeO2 nanocomposite: kinetics, machine learning and first-principle insights
Authors: Monika Dubey, Sunita Saharan, Monika Joshi, Piyush Kuchhal, Ranjit Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The development of efficient and recyclable catalysts for persistent organic pollutant removal remains a major challenge in environmental nanotechnology. Herein, a mesoporous Fe3O4/CeO2 nanocomposite was synthesized and systematically characterized, confirming a particle size of (20 ± 10 nm) with a high surface area (44.6 m2 g−1), magnetic recoverability, and abundant oxygen vacancies. The catalyst exhibited excellent adsorption-assisted removal of chlorobenzene (CB) and 2,4-dichlorophenol (2,4-DCP), achieving ∼100% removal within 70 min at near-neutral pH, with ∼98% stability after five cycles. Scavenger experiments revealed that hydroxyl radicals (˙OH) are the dominant reactive species, with additional contribution from superoxide radicals (O2˙−), confirming adsorption-assisted removal pathway. Kinetic analysis followed pseudo-first order (kpfo = 0.072 min−1 for CB and 0.0399 min−1 for 2,4-DCP) behaviour and showed strong agreement with Langmuir–Hinshelwood and Runge–Kutta models (R2 ≈ 0.99). An ANFIS model, trained on 380 datasets, accurately predicted adsorption-assisted removal under varying conditions. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations demonstrated that the Fe3O4/CeO2 (1 : 1) system possesses the most negative binding energy, indicating superior interfacial stability. PDOS analysis revealed enhanced overlap of Fe 3d, Ce 4f, and O 2p orbitals. This study highlights a synergistic experimental–theoretical–AI approach for advanced environmental remediation.

Introduction

Chlorinated organic pollutants (COPs) such as chlorobenzene (CB) and 2,4-dichlorophenol (2,4-DCP) are widely utilized in chemical manufacturing, pesticides, pharmaceuticals and dye industries.1,2 Because of their high chemical stability, toxicity and resistance to natural biodegradation, these compounds persist in aquatic environments.3–10 These compounds pose severe risks to ecosystems, marine life and human health.11 Their long-time accumulation in the environment can be responsible for bioaccumulation, endocrine disruption and carcinogenic effects. Therefore, their removal from industrial wastewater is an urgent environmental priority.12

Conventional treatment approaches such as biological degradation, chemical oxidation and physical adsorption have been widely explored for COP removal.10,13–15 However, these methods often suffer from several limitations, such as slow removal kinetics, incomplete mineralization, high operational cost, a complex separation process and the generation of secondary pollutants.16,17 Consequently, there is a need to develop advanced multifunctional nanomaterials capable of simultaneously facilitating adsorption and removal to enhance pollutant removal efficiency.18

Among various nanomaterials, magnetic metal-oxide nanocomposites have attracted considerable attention due to their unique physicochemical attributes like high surface area, tunable redox activity and ease of magnetic separation from aqueous systems.19 Magnetite (Fe3O4) nanoparticles are promising materials on account of their excellent magnetic responsiveness, chemical stability and capability to participate in electron transfer reactions. Fe3O4 possesses a mixed-valence iron structure (Fe2+/Fe3+) which can decompose various organic contaminants. These radicals can effectively disintegrate complex organic molecules, together with chlorinated compounds, dyes, pharmaceuticals and pesticides into less harmful or mineralized products like CO2 and H2O. Another important advantage of Fe3O4 nanoparticles is their superparamagnetic behaviour.20 Because of that, the rapid magnetic separation of the catalyst from treated water using an external magnetic field significantly simplifies catalyst recovery and reuse.19 So, the remediation process can be more economical and environmentally sustainable. However, pure Fe3O4 nanoparticles tend to aggregate because of strong magnetic interactions, which can minimize their active surface area and catalytic efficiency. Cerium oxide (CeO2), a rare earth metal oxide with a fluorite crystal structure, has emerged as a highly attractive catalytic material owing to its remarkable oxygen storage capacity and reversible Ce3+/Ce4+ redox cycling.21–24 The presence of a mesoporous structure further enhances the catalytic performance of CeO2 by providing a large specific surface area, uniform pore distribution and more abundant active sites.25,26 When combined with magnetic Fe3O4, CeO2 can form synergistic heterostructures that enhance interfacial electron transfer, improve catalytic activity and provide magnetic recoverability.24 The integration of Fe3O4 with mesoporous CeO2 can also improve surface area and dispersion of active sites, which are critical for adsorption-assisted removal processes. Although Fe3O4/CeO2 composites have previously been investigated for Fenton-like degradation of chlorophenols, our study focuses on adsorption-assisted removal and oxidative transformation of chlorobenzene and 2,4-dichlorophenol under near-neutral aqueous conditions and integrates experimental characterization with kinetic, numerical, DFT and ANFIS analyses. The objective is therefore not to claim novelty of the Fe3O4/CeO2 composition itself, but to provide an integrated assessment of pollutant removal, transformation behaviour and structure property relationships. Despite recent progress in magnetic oxide nanocomposites, the integration of adsorption-assisted catalysis with predictive modelling and artificial intelligence approaches remains relatively unexplored.27 The combination of advanced nanomaterials with first principle theory (Density Functional Theory-DFT: a quantum mechanical modelling method)28 and machine learning tools can significantly improve the understanding and optimization of the integration of nanomaterials and pollutant removal systems.

Herein, we report the successful synthesis of a magnetically recoverable mesoporous Fe3O4/CeO2 nanocomposite with mesoporous architecture for the efficient removal of chlorinated pollutants from aqueous media. DFT analysis has been performed to study the stability of synthesized nanocomposite. The structural, morphological and magnetic properties of the synthesized nanocomposite were systematically characterized and analyzed. The adsorption-assisted removal performance toward CB and 2,4-DCP was investigated under various experimental conditions. Furthermore, the adsorption kinetics were evaluated using pseudo-first order and Langmuir–Hinshelwood models. The adsorption behaviour was numerically simulated using the Runge–Kutta (RK4) method. To further enhance predictive capability, an Adaptive Neuro-Fuzzy Inference System (ANFIS) was implemented to model the influence of operational parameters on pollutant removal efficiency. The integration of nanomaterial engineering, kinetic modelling, machine learning prediction, and first-principles calculations provides a comprehensive framework for understanding and optimizing catalytic wastewater treatment systems. The results demonstrate that the Fe3O4/CeO2 nanocomposite offers a highly efficient, recyclable and scalable platform for environmental remediation of persistent organic pollutants.

Materials and methods

Materials

All chemicals of analytical grade were purchased from Merck Chemicals (India). Chlorobenzene (CB), 2,4-dichlorophenol (2,4-DCP), cerium ammonium nitrate-CAN (NH4)2Ce(NO3)6, NaOH, KOH, acrylamide, (CH2OH)2, FeCl2, HNO3, FeCl3, isopropanol (IPA), benzoquinone (BQ) and ethylene di amine tetra acetic acid (EDTA) were used without any purification.

Synthesis of Fe3O4/CeO2 nanocomposite

Mesoporous CeO2 was synthesized via a hydrothermal approach using cerium ammonium nitrate (CAN) as the cerium precursor.29 In this procedure, 1.25 g of CAN was dissolved in 30 mL of deionized water, followed by the addition of 10 mL of ethylene glycol drop by drop under continuous stirring. Subsequently, 500 mg of acrylamide was introduced to the mixture and stirring was maintained for 2 h. The pH was adjusted to 11 by the dropwise addition of 1 M NaOH solution. The precipitate was transferred into an autoclave and subjected to hydrothermal treatment at 150 °C for 48 h. The product was washed, dried at 100 °C for 6 h and calcined at 300 °C for 3 h. The resulting white powder corresponded to mesoporous cerium(iv) oxide.

Magnetic mesoporous Fe3O4/CeO2 nanocomposites were fabricated using an ultrasonically assisted co-precipitation method. Initially, a 0.2 M FeCl3 solution was added to 0.1 M urea, and the mixture was stirred at 90 °C for 2 h. Following this, 0.1 M FeCl2 was introduced and stirring continued for 25 min. Co-precipitation was initiated by the dropwise addition of 0.3 M NaOH until the pH reached 11. Subsequently, 500 mg of as-prepared CeO2 was incorporated in a 1 : 1 mass ratio with Fe3O4. The resulting dispersion was ultrasonicated (30 °C, 150 W, 40 kHz) for 1 h and allowed to age undisturbed for 15 h. The product was repeatedly washed and magnetically separated, followed by drying at 100 °C for 6 h. The pH played a critical role in regulating the morphology and interfacial interaction within the Fe3O4/CeO2 nanocomposite. The precipitation of Fe(OH)3 and Fe(OH)2 commenced around pH 9, whereas incorporation of CeO2 at pH 11 ensured that the mixed valence states of Ce3+/Ce4+ remained unaffected by Fe2+ ions, as represented in eqn (1):1

Ultrasonic irradiation facilitated stable magnetic Fe3O4/CeO2 hybrid on account of intense microturbulence, and shear forces generated at the ceria nanoparticles surface, promoting nucleation and subsequent growth of Fe3O4 nanoparticles.30 The collapse of cavitation bubbles produced abundant reactive H+ and OH− species, which can enhance nucleation rates and ensure high product yield.31 However, direct chemical coupling at the Fe3O4/CeO2 interface may not be conclusively established solely from the synthesis procedure. A schematic representation (Fig. 1a) illustrates the proposed formation mechanism, highlighting the uniform distribution of Fe3O4 nanoparticles within the mesoporous CeO2 framework, leading to improved structural integrity and synergistic catalytic functionality. Fe3O4 nanoparticles were synthesized under identical conditions as the composite, but without the addition of CeO2, to enable comparative analysis.

Simulation and characterization

The unit cells of CeO2, Fe3O4 and nanocomposites were constructed in QuantumATK. Geometry optimization of the unit cells was realized via the Limited-Memory Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno (L-BFGS) algorithm. A maximum force tolerance of 0.02 eV Å−1 and a stress error tolerance of 0.1 GPa were applied for all structural parameters. A gamma-centred 5 × 5 × 1 k-point mesh of the Brillouin zone with a density mesh cut off 2000 eV and a temperature of 300 kelvin (K) was employed to perform geometry optimization.32 Generalized gradient approximation (GGA) with predefined functional Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) integration with Grimme DFT-D3 (van der Waals corrections) and energy exchange–correlation and DFT calculations were evaluated for different ratios of CeO2 and Fe3O4 in nanocomposites such as (1 : 1, 1 : 2, 2 : 1). The projected density of states (PDOS) of the 1 : 1 Fe3O4/CeO2 heterostructure was calculated using a spin-polarized DFT+U approach. The on-site Coulomb interaction parameters were set to U = 5 eV for Ce 4f states and U = 4 eV for Fe 3d states. A density mesh cut-off of 250 Ry was employed to ensure accurate representation of the charge density.

The adsorption process was modelled using an Adaptive Neuro-Fuzzy Inference (ANFI) System based on a Sugeno-type fuzzy logic framework and implemented in MATLAB (R2025a). The ANFIS architecture comprises a five-layer hybrid neuro-fuzzy network, in which each layer performs a specific computational function essential for fuzzy inference.33 In this study, the system inputs consisted of key operational parameters influencing adsorption pH, initial COP concentration, contact time, Fe3O4/CeO2 dosage and stirring rate. These variables were transformed into fuzzy sets through first-order Sugeno membership functions in the fuzzification layer.34 The rule-generation layer (third layer) established the logical relationships between the input membership functions to form the rule base governing system behaviour. The resulting fuzzy outputs were subjected to defuzzification in the subsequent layer, where output membership functions were used to convert the fuzzy rule consequences into numerical estimates of the adsorption efficiency. In the final layer, all normalized firing strengths and corresponding outputs were aggregated into a single node that represented the predicted adsorption percentage. The overall ANFIS configuration used in this work is illustrated schematically in Fig. S1, demonstrating the interaction between input variables, fuzzy rule sets, and the model's predictive output.

XRD confirmed crystalline phases of both Fe3O4 and CeO2 and validated the formation of the Fe3O4 and CeO2 for the 2θ range 20–70°. A scanning electron microscope (SEM; ZEISS EVO-18) with energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) analyser was used to evaluate the structure and morphology of Fe3O4, and CeO2 and a transmission electron microscope ThermoFisher Talos F200X (S) TEM, was used for Fe3O4/CeO2 high resolution micrograph. BET (Brunauer, Emmett and Teller) surface analyser, and BJH (Barrett–Joyner–Halenda) model were applied for surface area analysis and porosity. VSM magnetization curves demonstrated strong magnetic behaviour suitable for rapid separation. The magnetic characteristics of synthesized iron oxides and nanocomposites have been studied by measuring room temperature magnetization (emu g−1) versus magnetic field (Oe) variation using VSM (Model number-PAR 155). Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) (wavenumber range 4000–400 cm−1) was done using Nicolet iS5, ThermoScientific, India. FTIR spectroscopy established surface functional groups and pollutant catalyst interactions. To determine the chemical composition of Fe3O4/CeO2 X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS) Prevac XPS was used. The UV absorbance spectra of adsorption in an aqueous solution were measured using UV-vis spectrophotometer UV-1800 (Shimadzu's Co., UV-Probe 2.42). High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis was performed to monitor the residual concentration of chlorinated organic pollutants during the treatment process. A C18 reversed-phase column (250 × 4.6 mm, 5 µm) was used for chromatographic separation. For chlorobenzene (CB), the mobile phase consisted of acetonitrile/water/acetic acid (50 : 48 : 2, v/v/v), with an isocratic flow rate of 1.0 mL min−1 and UV detection at 280 nm. For 2,4-dichlorophenol (2,4-DCP), methanol/water (70 : 30, v/v) was employed as the mobile phase at a flow rate of 1.0 mL min−1, and detection was carried out at 265 nm.

Adsorption-assisted removal experiments and kinetics

The pH point of zero charge-pHpzc was investigated using the pH drift method. Briefly, 50 mg of catalyst was added to 50 mL of 29 mg NaCl solution with an initial pH adjusted between 2 and 12. The suspensions were stirred for 24 h at room temperature (RT), and the final pH was noted. The pHpzc was evaluated at the point where ΔpH (pH_final − pH_initial) equals zero.

To elucidate the dominant reactive species responsible for the adsorption-assisted removal of CB and 2,4-DCP, radical scavenger experiments were performed under optimized conditions (100 ppm pollutant, 100 mg per L catalyst, pH ≈ 7). Results and analysis

Structural and magnetic characterization

XRD patterns confirmed the coexistence of spinel Fe3O4 and fluorite CeO2 phases. The powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) profiles of calcined CeO2, Fe3O4, and the Fe3O4/CeO2 nanocomposite are presented in Fig. 1b. The diffraction pattern of Fe3O4 displays well-defined reflections at (220), (311), (400), (422), (511) and (440), characteristic of the inverse cubic spinel structure and consistent with the standard JCPDS File No. 85-1436.38 CeO2 exhibits distinct and narrow diffraction peaks indexed to the (111), (200), (220) and (311) planes of the fluorite-type face-centered cubic phase (JCPDS No. 34-0394),39,40 affirming the high crystallinity and phase purity of the material within limit of XRD. No impurity phases were detected. The diffraction pattern of the Fe3O4/CeO2 nanocomposite contains the full set of characteristic peaks from both constituent phases. The slight shift in the CeO2 peaks in the composite can be attributed to interfacial strain from the lattice mismatch between the Fe3O4 and CeO2 domains, and/or limited substitution of Fe3+ (0.645 Å) for Ce4+ (0.97 Å) at the interface, which shrinks the CeO2 cell. The sharper peaks in the composite is possibly a result of ultrasonic treatment of the reaction mixture, as acoustic cavitation promotes interparticle collision and oriented attachment of the nanocrystalline building blocks, and the alkaline aging promotes partial dissolution–reprecipitation of the smallest, most defective crystallites. The longer aging time could have promoted larger crystallite size. In addition, repeated washing and magnetic separation retain only the CeO2 associated with Fe3O4, which can bias the retained fraction toward larger crystallites.

The SEM (Fig. 2a and b) and EDS (Fig. 2c) analyses confirm the CeO2 nanoparticles with an average particle size of approximately 40 nm (Fig. 2d). Energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) analysis (Fig. 2c) was performed to verify the elemental composition of the synthesized Ce and O in CeO2 nanocomposite. The SEM micrograph (Fig. 2e and f) illustrates a cluster of Fe3O4/CeO2 nanocomposite quasi-spherical nanoparticles with a characteristic size distribution of approximately 20 ± 10 nm (Fig. 2h). The EDS spectrum (Fig. 2g) clearly shows characteristic peaks corresponding to oxygen (O), iron (Fe) and cerium (Ce), confirming the successful integration of both oxide phases. The strong peak at approximately 0.52 keV corresponds to the O Kα transition, indicating the presence of lattice oxygen within the oxide structure. The signals observed at around 0.71 keV and 6.40 keV are attributed to Fe Lα and Fe Kα transitions, respectively, confirming the presence of magnetite nanoparticles. Additionally, distinct peaks at approximately 4.84 keV and 5.26 keV correspond to Ce Lα and Ce Lβ emissions, which verify the incorporation of cerium oxide within the composite structure. Quantitative analysis indicates that Fe, Ce, and O are the dominant elements with no detectable impurity peaks, suggesting the high purity of the synthesized material. The presence of these characteristic peaks collectively confirms the successful formation of the Fe3O4/CeO2 nanocomposite, which is expected to enhance catalytic activity due to the synergistic interaction between magnetite and ceria phases. The porous interconnected morphology, nanoscale dimensions and uniform particle size distribution are expected to provide a high surface area and abundant active sites, making the material highly suitable for applications in catalysis and environmental remediation.

Nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms (Fig. 4a) for Fe3O4/CeO2 are characteristic of type-IV profiles, confirming mesoporosity. The steep uptake in the low relative pressure region (p/p0 = 0.1–0.4) further supports the presence of well-developed mesopores. The specific surface area is calculated as 44.6 m2 g−1. In our previous work BET analysis yielded a specific surface area of 270.2 m2 g−1 for synthesized CeO2.29 Fe3O4 often has a lower surface area than mesoporous CeO2. In the nanocomposite, CeO2 may contribute to the high surface area, but Fe3O4 may partially block pores or increase particle growth. Thus, the composite surface area is lower than that of pure mesoporous CeO2.

The magnetic characteristics of Fe3O4 and Fe3O4/CeO2 were evaluated via room-temperature magnetization (M–H) hysteresis (Fig. 4b). Fe3O4 exhibits clear ferromagnetic behavior with a saturation magnetization (Ms) of 40.2 emu g−1 (1.5µB), consistent with exchange interactions between Fe ions occupying tetrahedral and octahedral sites. The Fe3O4/CeO2 composite retains ferromagnetic ordering, although with a reduced Ms value of 21.04 emu g−1 (0.5µB), which can be attributed to the non-magnetic (diamagnetic) nature of CeO2 and partial aggregation of Fe3O4 nanoparticles on the CeO2 surface. Here, it is noted that composite reached magnetic saturation at a relatively low applied field (∼5000 Oe), suggesting facile magnetic response. This behavior enables rapid magnetic separation, as demonstrated in the digital images showing the dispersion before and after exposure to an external magnet (Fig. 4b, insets A and B). VSM measurements demonstrated excellent magnetic saturation, facilitating effortless recovery of the catalyst from aqueous media.

The XPS spectrum (Fig. 5a) authenticates the presence of Fe, Ce and O elements in the synthesized Fe3O4/CeO2 nanocomposite without noticeable impurities. The high-resolution Fe 2p spectrum (Fig. 5b) shows two characteristic peaks at approximately 710.6 eV and 724.2 eV corresponding to Fe 2p3/2 and Fe 2p1/2 respectively, with a satellite feature around 718 eV, confirming the Fe3O4 phase. The Ce 3d spectrum (Fig. 5c) exhibits multiple components associated with the coexistence of Ce3+ and Ce4+ states. The Ce3+/(Ce3+ + Ce4+) ratio is ∼0.38, confirming substantial oxygen vacancy formation. Deconvoluted into three components: lattice O2− (529.4 eV, ∼42%), defect/oxygen-vacancy oxygen (531.0 eV, ∼36%), and adsorbed O2/C–O–C (532.8 eV, ∼22%). The defect-oxygen fraction (∼36%) is consistent with the Ce3+ fraction (∼38%), providing cross-validated evidence for oxygen vacancies. These results substantiate the successful formation of the Fe3O4/CeO2 nanocomposite with abundant oxygen vacancies.

Optimization of adsorption-assisted removal parameters

The temporal profiles for CB, 2,4-DCP removal at varying initial concentrations (50, 100 and 200 ppm) using catalysts as 100 mg L−1 of Fe3O4/CeO2 nanocomposite are presented in Fig. 6a and b. All reactions were performed at ambient temperature, and the solution pH remained close to neutral (pH ∼7). In the absence of stirring, catalyst addition alone did not induce measurable adsorption-assisted removal. Under catalytic conditions, nearly complete removal of CB was achieved within 50 min for an initial concentration of 50 ppm and within 70 min for 100 ppm. Almost complete removal of 200 ppm CB required approximately 80 min, whereas only ∼72% removal was achieved for 500 ppm after the same reaction duration. These results highlight the concentration-dependent kinetics of CB degradation. As the initial CB concentration increases while the catalyst loading and the density of active zones remain constant, competition for adsorption sites intensifies, thereby slowing surface-mediated reactions and reducing overall removal efficiency. Consequently, lower pollutant concentrations facilitate higher removal efficiencies under identical loading conditions. Based on these findings, an initial CB concentration of 100 ppm was decided for subsequent optimization experiments. The removal efficiency of COPs is strongly influenced by the availability of active sites on the catalyst surface, where electrostatic interactions play a significant role. At lower pollutant concentrations, sufficient active sites are available, facilitating efficient adsorption and subsequent removal.41 However, as the concentration of COPs increases, competition among pollutant molecules for the limited adsorption sites becomes more pronounced, leading to reduced removal efficiency. Consequently, higher pollutant concentrations require longer contact time to achieve comparable removal levels.

The observed decline in removal percentage at elevated concentrations can be attributed to the saturation of active sites on the adsorbent surface. Therefore, controlling the initial concentration of pollutants is essential to achieve optimal performance.42 Based on these observations, an initial concentration of 100 ppm was selected for subsequent experiments. Under optimized conditions, complete removal of COPs (100 ppm) was achieved within 70 min at pH ∼7 using a catalyst dosage of 100 mg L−1 with continuous stirring at 400 rpm.

pH is another critical factor governing both adsorption behavior pathways.11 To evaluate its influence, removal reactions were conducted at pH values of 3, 5, 7, 9, and 11 using 100 ppm CB and 2,4-DCP using 100 mg L−1 of Fe3O4/CeO2 (Fig. 6c). pH adjustments were performed using 1 M KOH or 10% HNO3. At pH ∼7, the system achieved nearly complete (∼100%) adsorption-assisted removal within 70 min. In contrast, removal efficiencies were substantially lower in acidic media ∼45% at pH 3 and ∼56% at pH 5 and only moderately improved in alkaline conditions, reaching ∼72% at pH 9 and ∼68% at pH 11. CB is a neutral, non-ionizable chlorinated aromatic compound under the experimental pH range. Therefore, its pH-dependent removal is primarily associated with changes in the surface charge state of the Fe3O4/CeO2 nanocomposite and the availability of surface adsorption sites rather than pollutant ionization. In contrast, 2,4-DCP is a weakly acidic compound, and its degree of deprotonation increases with increasing pH. Consequently, its interaction with the catalyst surface is affected by both the surface charge of Fe3O4/CeO2 and the changing speciation of 2,4-DCP. At low and higher the pHpzc, electrostatic interactions between the increased charge at Fe3O4/CeO2 surface and 2,4-DCP species can become less favourable, potentially reducing adsorption. Therefore, the observed pH dependence of 2,4-DCP removal represents a combined effect of surface charge, pollutant speciation, adsorption, and subsequent surface-associated transformation, rather than a simple electrostatic interaction. These observations indicate that a neutral environment most effectively supports the adsorption-assisted removal process, likely due to favorable surface charge interactions and optimal oxidation states of the Fe3O4/CeO2. Thus, pH ∼7 was identified as the optimum working condition.

The influence of catalyst dosage was further examined by varying the Fe3O4/CeO2 concentration from 50 to 200 mg L−1 for a 100 ppm COPs solution (Fig. 6d). Increasing catalyst loading enhanced removal efficiency by providing a greater number of accessible adsorption and reaction sites. However, beyond 100 mg L−1, further increases produced only marginal improvements, indicating that active-site saturation or light/diffusion limitations may occur. Accordingly, 100 mg L−1 was designated as the optimal catalyst dosage for all remaining experiments.

Point of zero pH effect

The point of zero charge (pHpzc ≈ 6.8) indicates that the surface of the Fe3O4/CeO2 nanocomposite remains positively charged at pH values below 6.8 and becomes negatively charged at pH values above this threshold (Table S2). As shown in Fig. 6c, the maximum removal efficiency observed at pH ≈ 7, which is close to the pHpzc, suggests that electrostatic repulsion is minimized under these conditions, allowing other interactions such as hydrophobic forces, hydrogen bonding, π–π interactions, and catalytic oxidation at Ce active sites to dominate.

Kinetic modelling

The combined kinetic and mathematical modelling demonstrates that the adsorption-assisted removal of CB and 2,4-DCP is governed primarily by pseudo-first-order kinetics43 for R2 = 0.99. The H–L model successfully explains the competition for active sites and validates the surface-controlled catalytic mechanism for both CB and 2,4-DCP. The RK4 simulations provide strong numerical validation of the experimentally obtained kinetic parameters. Proposed mechanism

Mesoporous CeO2, magnetite (Fe3O4) nanoparticles, and the ultrasound-assisted magnetic Fe3O4/CeO2 nanocomposite44–49 were evaluated as catalysts for the adsorption-assisted removal of 100 ppm CB and 2,4-DCP. Pure Fe3O4 exhibited an adsorption efficiency of approximately 21% within 70 minutes, which can be attributed to the presence of surface –OH groups capable of generating reactive hydroxyl species. Mesoporous CeO2, with a large specific surface area, contains abundant oxygen vacancies and surface radicals, enabling a higher adsorption-assisted removal efficiency of about 42% under the same conditions as shown in eqn (8).8CB/2,4-DCP(aq) → CB/2,4-DCP(ads)

Among all the tested systems, the Fe3O4/CeO2 nanocomposite demonstrated the most rapid catalytic response, achieving nearly 100% adsorption-assisted removal within 70 minutes at neutral pH (∼7) using 100 mg L−1 of catalyst. The composite synthesized under ultrasonic irradiation possessed an even higher surface area of 44 m2 g−1, together with an increased concentration of oxygen vacancies (O2˙−) and ˙OH radicals. The adsorption step, therefore, acts as a precursor to catalytic oxidation, ensuring efficient electron transfer and rapid adsorption-assisted removal. The exceptional adsorption-assisted catalytic performance of the Fe3O4/CeO2 composite can be ascribed to a strong synergistic interplay between the Fe3O4 and CeO2 phases. The interfacial contact between the two oxides promotes efficient separation of photon or chemically generated electrons, preventing recombination and thereby enhancing redox activity. The UV-vis spectra for CB adsorption-assisted removal (in Fig. S3) show the main absorbance peak at 278 nm. Initially, Fe3O4 captures CB molecules on its surface; subsequently, electron exchange between Fe3+/Fe2+ and Ce4+/Ce3+ pairs accelerate the production of hydroxyl radicals (˙OH). The progressive decline of the 278 nm peak after 50 minutes reflects the oxidation of CB, while the gradual shift and disappearance of π → π* aromatic transitions indicate cleavage of the C–Cl bonds.

After 60 min, depletion at the absorption band 280 nm (n → π* transitions) shows oxidation of the 2,4-DCP.50 Band shift presents π → π* transitions of the aromatic ring disappearing slowly, which presents the breakage of C–Cl bonds. The Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectrum of the calcined Fe3O4/CeO2 nanocomposite exhibits a broad absorption band around 3437 cm−1, which is attributed to surface hydroxyl (–OH) groups. The band observed at approximately 1549 cm−1 is associated with adsorbed water molecules. A distinct peak at 1079 cm−1 corresponds to Fe–Ce–O vibrational modes, indicating the interaction between Fe3O4 and CeO2 within the composite structure. Furthermore, the absorption region between 450–700 cm−1 is assigned to Ce–O stretching vibrations; the broad nature of this band arises from the overlap of Ce–O and Fe–O vibrations, confirming the successful integration of Fe3O4 onto the CeO2 surface. These observations validate the formation of the magnetic Fe3O4/CeO2 nanocomposite. After the adsorption-assisted removal of 2,4-DCP, noticeable changes in the FTIR spectrum were observed (Fig. S4b). The appearance of a new band near 1650 cm−1, along with the broadening of the hydroxyl band around 3400 cm−1, indicates the presence of adsorbed intermediates and increased surface hydroxyl interactions. Additionally, the emergence of a peak at approximately 743 cm−1 is attributed to C–Cl vibrations, suggesting the interaction of 2,4-DCP or its transformation products with the catalyst surface. These spectral changes confirm the involvement of surface functional groups during the catalytic adsorption-assisted removal.34,35 After the catalytic reaction with CB, the FTIR spectrum displays a more pronounced –OH stretching band near 3400 cm−1 and the appearance of a new band at around 746 cm−1, which is characteristic of H–O–H and C–Cl related vibrations, indicating the participation of CB molecules and subsequent structural transformation during adsorption-assisted removal as shown in Fig. S2. HPLC analysis further confirmed the removal of the parent 2,4-DCP and CB (Fig. S5 and S6 in SI) peaks following treatment with Fe3O4/CeO2, together with the appearance of additional chromatographic peaks attributable to possible transformation products. These results support the proposed adsorption-assisted oxidative transformation pathway.

The adsorption-assisted removal efficiency of CB and 2,4-DCP decreased significantly in the presence of scavengers, indicating the crucial role of reactive oxygen species in parity with HPLC results. The addition of IPA resulted in the most pronounced suppression of adsorption-assisted removal efficiency (reduction from ∼100% to ∼45–50%), suggesting that hydroxyl radicals (˙OH) are the primary active species governing the adsorption-assisted removal process. In the presence of benzoquinone, the degradation efficiency decreased to ∼60–65%, confirming the involvement of superoxide radicals (O2˙−) as secondary reactive species. A comparatively smaller reduction (∼75–80%) was observed with EDTA, indicating a limited contribution of surface-generated holes (h+) as shown in Fig. 8b. These results demonstrate that the adsorption-assisted removal mechanism is predominantly driven by ˙OH radicals, with additional contributions from O2˙− species. The generation of these reactive species can be attributed to the synergistic interaction between Fe3O4 and CeO2. The redox cycling between Fe2+/Fe3+ and Ce3+/Ce4+ facilitates electron transfer. This behaviour strongly supports a stepwise oxidative adsorption-assisted removal pathway, as shown in Fig. S7. Magnetic recovery allows efficient reutilization (after washing and drying) without structural collapse.51

Fe3O4/CeO2 nanocomposite is a highly stable composite with ∼100% adsorption-assisted removal efficiency for multiple organic pollutants.52 It has a combination of adsorption and catalytic synergy that boosts overall performance. Magnetic separation allows easy recovery and almost ∼98% reusability was found over repeated cycles. Recyclability tests were performed to assess catalyst stability and practice al applicability. After each adsorption-assisted removal cycle, the magnetic Fe3O4/CeO2 nanocomposite was rapidly separated using an external magnet, washed, and dried at 85 °C for 3 h before reuse. The catalyst maintained high activity over five consecutive cycles (Fig. 8c), demonstrating robust magnetic recoverability and operational durability suitable for repeated environmental applications. The post reaction XRD patterns have been compared with fresh Fe3O4/CeO2 nanocomposite. Almost all peaks are recovered after 5 cycle reaction of Fe3O4/CeO2 with 2,4-DCP and CB as shown in Fig. S10. It represents stability of nanocomposite and very less leaching of material. Synthesized Fe3O4/CeO2 nanocomposite is effectively applicable for the adsorption-assisted removal of bisphenol A (BPA) and crystal violet (CV) dye as shown in Fig. 8d. UV-vis based adsorption-assisted removal results have been shown in Fig. S6 and S7. Overall, Fe3O4/CeO2 has strong potential for industrial-scale wastewater treatment applications. Conclusion

A novel Fe3O4/CeO2 nanocomposite was successfully synthesized and demonstrated exceptional efficiency for the adsorption-assisted removal of chlorobenzene and 2,4-DCP under mild conditions. The composite's high surface area, strong magnetic recoverability and synergistic redox activity enabled ∼100% pollutant removal and excellent stability across repeated cycles. These findings showcase the nanocomposite as a promising material for scalable, high-performance environmental remediation technologies. Kinetic analysis revealed that the adsorption-assisted removal process follows pseudo-first-order behaviour for R2 = 0.99. Langmuir–Hinshelwood model confirmed that the reaction proceeds through a surface-mediated catalytic mechanism. Numerical simulations using the Runge–Kutta method showed excellent agreement with experimental results, validating the reliability of the kinetic parameters. Furthermore, the ANFIS machine learning model successfully predicted the adsorption-assisted removal performance with high accuracy, demonstrating the potential of integrating artificial intelligence with nanomaterial-based remediation strategies. Further research can include pilot-scale continuous-flow reactor evaluations. This study can be extended to other emerging contaminants (e.g., pharmaceuticals, pesticides, dyes and other organic pollutants).
