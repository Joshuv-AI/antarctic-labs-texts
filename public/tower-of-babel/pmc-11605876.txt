SS31 alleviates LPS-induced acute lung injury by inhibiting inflammatory responses through the S100A8/NLRP3/GSDMD signaling pathway
Authors: Peiyao Luo, Quankuan Gu, Jianpeng Wang, Xianyong Li, Nana Li, Wei Yang, Xianglin Meng, Mingyan Zhao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute lung injury/acute respiratory distress syndrome (ALI/ARDS) is an acute, diffuse, inflammatory lung injury caused by various endogenous or exogenous factors. It is currently widely recognized that an excessive inflammatory response resulting from immune imbalance constitutes a crucial pathogenic mechanism in ALI/ARDS. SS31 is a novel mitochondria-targeted antioxidant peptide. This article validates the role of SS31 in lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced ALI.MethodsThe study applied transcriptome sequencing, immunofluorescence, PCR, immunofluorescence and other methods to explore the mechanism of SS31 in LPS induced ALI.ResultsTranscriptome sequencing results indicate that LPS-induced ALI is closely associated with immune regulatory processes, the Toll-like receptor pathway, and the NF-κB signaling pathway. The role of SS31 in acute lung injury is closely related to biological processes, such as immune regulation and cell death. This study demonstrated that SS31 can inhibit the expression of inflammatory factors IL-6, IL-1β, IL-18, and TNF-α, and reduce the expression of pyroptosis-related proteins NLRP3, and GSDMD-N. Further analysis revealed that S100A8 may be a key gene in the effect of SS31. LPS stimulation leads to increased expression of S100A8, while SS31 decreases its expression. Recombinant protein S100A8 can attenuate the inhibitory effect of SS31 on IL-1β, IL-18, NLRP3, and GSDMD-N.ConclusionsThe research results indicate that SS31 may inhibit the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome and suppress inflammatory responses by regulating S100A8, thereby alleviating LPS-induced ALI in mice; this process may be related to pyroptosis.Keywords: SS31, S100A8, NLRP3, Inflammation, Pyroptosis, Acute lung injury

Background

ALI/ARDS is one of the most common diseases in Intensive Care Unit (ICU), characterized by high morbidity and mortality. Studies have shown that the incidence of ARDS in ICU patients is 10.4%; among patients requiring mechanical ventilation, the incidence of ARDS reaches 23.4%; the in-hospital mortality rates for patients with mild, moderate, and severe ARDS are 34.9%, 40.3%, and 46.1%, respectively [1]. The pathological mechanisms underlying the development and progression of ALI/ARDS are complex and have not been fully elucidated despite long-term research efforts. It is currently widely accepted that uncontrolled inflammatory response is a crucial factor leading to the development of ALI/ARDS. This inflammatory cascade involves the activation and release of a series of inflammatory cells and chemokines, which interact with each other to trigger a sustained and amplified inflammatory response [2].

The pathogenesis of ALI/ARDS involves multiple cell death pathways, which are interconnected and collectively exacerbate the inflammatory response [3, 4]. Increasing research has revealed the crucial role of cell death in the development and progression of ALI/ARDS. Currently known pathways involved in cell death in ALI/ARDS include apoptosis, necroptosis, autophagy, ferroptosis, pyroptosis, and necrosis [5]. In the process of pyroptosis, the NLRP3 inflammasome is activated under various stimulatory factors of damage, leading to the activation of Caspase-1. This, in turn, cleaves the GSDMD protein, generating the pore-forming GSDMD-N fragment. Ultimately, this results in the rupture of the cell membrane, the release of large amounts of inflammatory factors (IL-1β and IL-18), and exacerbation of the inflammatory response [6]. During ALI/ARDS, pyroptosis mediated by NLRP3 activation can occur in alveolar macrophages, pulmonary vascular endothelial cells, and alveolar epithelial cells, which is closely associated with the cascading amplification of inflammatory responses [7–10]. In case of uncontrolled inflammatory response, the alveolar–capillary barrier is damaged, leading to impaired gas exchange and triggering a series of physiological abnormalities, which are typical manifestations of ALI/ARDS [11]. The pyroptosis of alveolar epithelial cells can lead to destruction of alveolar structure and alveolar edema, thereby affecting gas exchange. Therefore, inhibiting the pyroptosis of alveolar epithelial cells may be a crucial step in preventing and treating ALI/ARDS.

SS31 is a novel mitochondrial targeted antioxidant peptide that can eliminate mtROS and damaged mtDNA, thereby restoring mitochondrial membrane potential and inhibiting oxidative stress, exerting anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects [12, 13]. However, the role of SS31 in ALI/ARDS remains unclear. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the effects and underlying mechanisms of SS31 in ALI.

Methods

Animal experiments

C57BL/6 male mice (weighing 20 to 30 g) were procured from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China).The experiment was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University. The mice were randomly divided into 4 groups, with 6 mice in each group: control group (CON), SS31 group (SS31, 5 mg/kg), LPS group (LPS, 10 mg/kg), and SS31 treatment group (LPS + SS31). Mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 50 mg/kg pentobarbital sodium. To establish an ALI model, mice were anesthetized and administered 20 μl of LPS (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) via airway instillation. Subsequently, SS31 (Topscience China) was administered through three intraperitoneal injections at 1 h before modeling, 12 h before modeling, and 12 h after modeling. Samples were collected 24 h after modeling.

Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF)

Firstly, the skin of the neck and chest of the mouse is incised and disinfected. Subsequently, a layered dissection is performed to expose the trachea and thoracic cavity, and the trachea is isolated. An incision is made in the trachea between the second and third cartilaginous rings distal to the cricoid cartilage, and a plastic cannula is inserted and secured tightly at the bifurcation of the right and left main bronchi. Finally, 0.8 ml of PBS solution is injected using a 1 ml syringe, left in place for 20–30 s, and then the lavage fluid is slowly withdrawn. This procedure is repeated three times.

Histological evaluation

The lung tissues of the mice were promptly fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded in paraffin blocks for sectioning. Then stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) following standard histological procedures. The stained sections were carefully examined under a light microscope to assess and quantify the pathological changes associated with lung injury.

Lung wet-to-dry ratio

The degree of lung edema was assessed by calculating the wet-to-dry (W/D) ratio. Immediately after collection, the lung tissue was measured for wet weight. Subsequently, it was dried in an oven at 80 °C for 48 h to determine the dry weight, and finally, the wet-to-dry weight ratio was calculated.

Transcriptome analysis

For the RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) study, lung tissue was promptly snap-frozen at − 80 °C for further analysis. Total RNA was extracted from the cells using TRIzol reagent. After quantification of the total RNA samples, mRNA was reverse-transcribed into double-stranded complementary DNA (cDNA). PCR products were purified using AMPure XP beads, resulting in the final library. Once the library was constructed, an initial quantification was performed using a Qubit2.0 Fluorometer, followed by library detection using an Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer before Illumina sequencing. Differential gene expression analysis was conducted using DESeq2, with significance set at |log2FoldChange|≥ 0.8 and P < 0.05. GO functional enrichment analysis and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis were performed on the set of differentially expressed genes using the clusterProfiler software.

Cell culture

Human lung adenocarcinoma epithelial cells (A549) were sourced from Saios Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (China). These cells were maintained in DMEM/F12 medium fortified with 1% penicillin–streptomycin solution and 10% fetal bovine serum, incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Cells were divided into the following four groups: Control group (CON), LPS intervention group (LPS), LPS + SS31 intervention group (LPS + SS31), and LPS + SS31 + rS100A8 intervention group (LPS + SS31 + rS100A8). The specific processing steps are as follows: cells were pre-treated with rS100A8 (20 μg/ml) for 1 h, followed by treatment with SS31 (200 μM) for an additional 1 h, and subsequently treated with LPS (10 μg/ml) for 12 h.

Reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from lung tissue using an RNA extraction kit (TransGen Biotech, China), and RNA samples were then reverse transcribed into cDNA. The expression levels of candidate genes were measured using SYBR Green on a real-time fluorescent quantitative PCR system (Applied Biosystems, USA). The following primer sequences were used in this experiment: IL-6 (F, 5′-CTGCAAGAGACTTCCATCCAG-3′; R, 5′-AGTGGTATAGACAGGTCTGTTGG-3′) and TNF-α (F, 5′-CTGAACTTCGGGGTGATCGG-3′; R, 5′-GGCTTGTCACTCGAATTTTGAGA-3′).

Western blot

Total proteins were harvested using RIPA buffer (Beyotime, China), and protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, China). Equal amounts of protein were electrophoresed on polyacrylamide gels and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk powder for 1 h, followed by incubation with the corresponding primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Afterwards, the membranes were incubated with the respective anti-rabbit/anti-mouse secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Finally, the membranes were inverted onto a developing apparatus, and the grayscale values of the target protein bands were measured and analyzed to calculate protein expression levels. The antibodies used were, NLRP3 (Cell Signaling Technology, USA), S100A8 (Abcam, USA), IL-1β (Proteintech, USA), IL-18 (Proteintech, USA), GSDMD-N (Proteintech, USA), β-Tubulin (Abcam, USA), and GAPDH (Proteintech, USA).

Immunofluorescence

A549 were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and rinsed with PBS; paraffin sections were dewaxed in gradient concentrations of xylene and ethanol, followed by antigen retrieval using antigen retrieval solution and washing with PBS. Subsequently, the cell slides or lung tissue sections were incubated with goat serum blocking buffer at room temperature for 30 min and then incubated with the corresponding primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. The cell slides or tissue sections were washed with antibody washing buffer and incubated with the corresponding fluorescent secondary antibodies in the dark for 1 h. Afterwards, they were incubated with DAPI in the dark for 5 min and observed under a microscope.

Statistical analysis

In this study, quantitative data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. For data analysis and comparison between single groups, the t test method was employed, while for comparisons among multiple groups, one-way ANOVA was used. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

SS31 alleviates LPS-induced ALI

We examined the pathological structure of lung tissue to investigate the effects of SS31 on ALI. HE staining revealed that LPS caused destruction of lung structure, alveolar structural disorder, thickening of alveolar walls, alveolar space collapse, and inflammatory cell infiltration, while SS31 alleviated lung pathological damage. The W/D ratio indicated that SS31 reduced the degree of edema in lung tissue. In addition, SS31 decreased the total cell count in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid. In summary, SS31 attenuated LPS-induced ALI (Fig. 1).

SS31 reduced the degree of edema in lung tissue. ns P > 0.05, *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, **P < 0.0001

SS31 inhibits inflammatory responses and the expression of NLRP3, GSDMD-N

While SS31 alleviated lung injury, we observed that SS31 also reduced the release of inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6, IL-1β, IL-18, and TNF-α. Notably, IL-1β and IL-18 are the end products of pyroptosis. We also observed that LPS treatment led to an elevation in the expression of NLRP3, and GSDMD-N, whereas SS31 inhibited the expression of these proteins (Fig. 2).

Transcriptome sequencing of LPS-induced ALI

To further explore the role of pyroptosis in LPS-induced ALI in mice, we conducted transcriptome sequencing of lung tissue. Differential analysis of sequencing results revealed 3554 differentially expressed genes between the CON and LPS groups, including 1952 upregulated genes and 1602 downregulated genes. GO and KEGG analyses were performed on these genes to further elucidate the mechanisms underlying LPS-induced ALI. We have found that immune system dysregulation may be closely associated with the occurrence of ALI. Toll-like receptors and NF-κB are classic pro-inflammatory signaling pathways, both of which are closely related to the occurrence of ALI (Fig. 3).

Transcriptomic alterations induced by SS31 in LPS-induced ALI

To explore the mechanism of SS31 in ALI, we performed transcriptome sequencing on lung tissue from the LPS + SS31 and LPS groups. Differential analysis revealed 181 differentially expressed genes between the two groups, including 127 upregulated genes and 54 downregulated genes. GO and KEGG analysis results indicated the relevant pathways affected by SS31. In previous sections, we observed that SS31 induced changes in the expression of pyroptosis-related proteins, suggesting that SS31 may be associated with pyroptosis. Therefore, we obtained 534 pyroptosis-related genes from Genecard and performed a cross-analysis with the differentially expressed genes from the two groups, ultimately identifying a total of 7 genes: S100A8, IL6, S100A9, S100A4, ETS1, CASP4, and PYCARD. Among them, S100A8/9 forms a dimer in vivo to interact with TLR4, activating the NF-κB signaling pathway, which subsequently triggers an inflammatory response. Therefore, we examined the expression of S100A8 both in vivo and in vitro and found that LPS can increase the expression of S100A8, while SS31 inhibits its expression. These results suggest that S100A8 may be related to the effects of SS31 (Fig. 4).

In A549, SS31 attenuates inflammation and the expression of NLRP3, and GSDMD-N, while rS100A8 inhibits these effects of SS31

We conducted in vitro experiments to verify whether S100A8 is involved in the role of SS31 in acute lung injury. Initially, we observed that SS31 can inhibit the release of IL-1β, IL-18 and the expression of NLRP3, and GSDMD-N induced by LPS in A549. Next, we administered rS100A8 as an intervention and found that the inhibitory effect of SS31 on the aforementioned proteins was reduced, indicating that S100A8 may be involved in the role of SS31 in acute lung injury (Fig. 5).

Discussion

SS31 has demonstrated potent anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and mitochondrial protective effects in various inflammatory diseases. SS31 can scavenge mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS), inhibit the release of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), prevent the opening of mitochondrial permeability transition pores, accelerate ATP recovery, regulate mitochondrial fusion, preserve mitochondrial integrity, and inhibit inflammation and oxidative stress, thereby protecting tissues and organs [14–17]. SS-31 is capable of inhibiting the mitochondrial translocation of phosphorylated dynamin-related protein 1 (Drp1), restoring mtROS levels, and reducing cell apoptosis. It effectively decreases the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome and the recruitment of GSDMD-N to the mitochondrial membrane, thereby preventing the occurrence of pyroptosis [18, 19]. The results of this experiment validate the protective effect of SS31 on LPS-induced ALI.

In this study, through GO analysis of transcriptome sequencing of lung tissue, it was found that the occurrence and development of lung injury are closely related to immune system regulation, which is consistent with the pathogenesis of ALI. During ALI, in response to lung injury, the immune system initiates an inflammatory response to eliminate pathogens and protect lung tissue [20]. Inflammatory responses play a positive role in eliminating pathogens. However, excessive inflammatory responses will lead to alveolar damage, particularly the enhancement of endothelial and epithelial permeability, which further triggers the accumulation of protein-rich fluid in the alveoli, resulting in increased respiratory work and impaired gas exchange, ultimately leading to acute respiratory failure [21]. Furthermore, KEGG analysis indicates that the Toll-like receptor pathway and NF-kB pathway are involved in the processes of ALI. Toll-like receptors (TLRs) are transmembrane binding receptors that serve as the core of innate and adaptive immune responses. These receptors are activated by recognition of various pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and danger-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), playing a crucial role in inflammatory diseases [22]. Among them, the role of TLR4 in lung diseases has been extensively studied. In LPS-induced ALI, TLR4 is directly activated and interacts with the adaptor protein MyD88, which contains a TIR domain, leading to the activation of NF-κB. This activation results in the release of cytokines and inflammatory mediators, thereby promoting inflammatory responses [23–25]. The activation of the TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway can also activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, leading to the occurrence of pyroptosis [26, 27].

During lung injury, factors that cause damage can activate the NLRP3 inflammasome as PAMPs or DAMPs [28]. The NLRP3 inflammasome, as an intracellular immune sensor, is activated to result in the recruitment and activation of procaspase-1. The activated caspase-1 then prompts the production and release of the inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and IL-18. In addition, Caspase-1 can cleave GSDMD, generating the membrane-permeabilizing GSDMD-N, leading to cell rupture. This further results in excessive release of IL-1β and IL-18, thereby aggravating lung injury [29–31]. Research has shown that alveolar epithelial cells undergo oxidative stress, generating a large amount of ROS and activating the PINK1/Parkin pathway, leading to mitophagy. Excessive mitophagy induces pyroptosis in alveolar epithelial cells through the NLRP3-Caspase-1-GSDMD signaling pathway [32]. Concurrently, endoplasmic reticulum stress can also promote TXNIP upregulation, ROS accumulation, NF-κB nuclear translocation, activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, and induce pyroptosis in alveolar epithelial cells [33]. In this study, through GO analysis, it was found that the effect of SS31 is related to biological processes, such as immune response and cell death. Pyroptosis is closely associated with excessive inflammatory responses. Both in vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrate that SS31 inhibits the release of IL-1β and IL-18, and reduces the expression levels of NLRP3, and GSDMD-N. This suggests that SS31 may improve ALI by blocking inflammation and pyroptosis in alveolar epithelial cells.

We further analyzed the target genes affected by SS31 and identified key genes, including S100A8, IL6, S100A9, S100A4, ETS1, CASP4, and PYCARD. Among them, S100A8, an essential member of the calcium-binding protein family, typically binds specifically with S100A9 to form a heterodimer, exhibiting various biological functions. Intracellularly, S100A8 serves as a Ca2+ sensor, regulating the cytoskeleton, initiating and modulating inflammatory responses, and defending against pathogens. Extracellularly, S100A8 is associated with leukocyte recruitment, promoting the release of cytokines, and regulating cell proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis [34]. Extracellular S100A8 is widely recognized as a DAMP that can interact with various PRRs through paracrine and autocrine mechanisms. This interaction activates proinflammatory signaling pathways, such as MyD88/IKK and MAPK, leading to the production and release of a series of chemokines and proinflammatory cytokines. This process intensifies the inflammatory response and contributes to the development of inflammatory diseases [35]. TLR4, as a PRR, serves as an essential target for the action of S100A8. S100A8 activates the NLRP3 inflammasome and induces pyroptosis in macrophages by regulating the TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway and promoting ROS production [36]. In sepsis, S100A8/A9 induces GSDMD-dependent platelet pyroptosis through the TLR4/ROS/NLRP3/Caspase-1 pathway, leading to the release of oxidized mtDNA, which contributes to the formation of NETs. In turn, NETs release S100A8/A9 and accelerate platelet pyroptosis, forming a positive feedback loop that exacerbates the production of proinflammatory cytokines [37]. Studies have shown that inhibiting the S100A8/A9 signaling pathway can effectively promote the self-renewal and phagocytic function of alveolar macrophages, suppress inflammatory responses, accelerate the resolution of inflammation, reduce vascular permeability, mitigate pulmonary microvascular leakage, and improve pulmonary edema [38, 39]. In our experiments, LPS stimulation led to a significant increase in S100A8 expression, while SS31 inhibited the expression of S100A8. In addition, rS100A8 attenuated the inhibitory effect of SS31 on inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-18, and NLRP3, GSDMD-N. Therefore, we suggest that the role of SS31 in acute lung injury is partially mediated through the regulation of S100A8, which may be associated with the TLR4/NF-Kb/NLRP3 signaling pathway.

In conclusion, this study provides evidence that SS31 mitigates LPS-induced ALI. Specifically, SS31 inhibits NLRP3/GSDMD-mediated inflammatory responses in alveolar epithelial cells. Furthermore, we postulate that the actions of SS31 may be partially mediated through modulation of S100A8.

Conclusion

Through the S100A8/NLRP3/GSDMD pathway, SS31 effectively suppresses inflammation in alveolar epithelial cells, ultimately mitigating LPS-induced ALI in mice. This suggests that SS31 could emerge as a promising therapeutic candidate for the management of ALI/ARDS.
