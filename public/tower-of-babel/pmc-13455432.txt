Cavernous malformations (CMs) are low-flow vascular lesions composed of sinusoidal vascular channels lacking intervening neural parenchyma. Although they most commonly occur within the cerebral hemispheres, brainstem, and spinal cord, involvement of the anterior visual pathway is exceedingly rare.1,2 Optic pathway CMs account for less than 1% of all intracranial CMs and may involve the optic nerves, optic chiasm, or optic tracts.2 Among these lesions, intrinsic optic nerve CMs represent one of the rarest anatomical subtypes reported in the literature.3–9

Patients with optic nerve CMs typically present with progressive or fluctuating visual deterioration, retro-orbital pain, headache, or acute visual decline related to intralesional hemorrhage.2–9 Repeated microhemorrhage and hemosiderin deposition may result in chronic compression and irreversible axonal injury of the optic apparatus. Some patients demonstrate recurrent episodes of visual decline separated by partial recovery periods, a phenomenon described as “optic apoplexy.”2,8,9 Because of nonspecific clinical and radiological findings, diagnosis may be challenging, and these lesions may mimic optic glioma, thrombosed aneurysm, inflammatory optic neuropathy, or hemorrhagic neoplasms.2,5,7,10

Due to the rarity of these lesions, the optimal management strategy and timing of surgery remain controversial. Observation may be considered in asymptomatic or stable patients; however, progressive visual deterioration and recurrent hemorrhage often favor surgical intervention.2,8,9

We present the case of an intrinsic optic nerve CM treated through a transsylvian pterional approach with optic canal unroofing and microsurgical gross-total resection (GTR). The operative strategy, microsurgical anatomy, and technical nuances are discussed together with a review of the relevant literature.

Illustrative Case

A 30-year-old male presented with progressive visual deterioration in the right eye. His symptoms had begun in 2018 with an episode of near-complete right-sided visual loss, followed by substantial spontaneous recovery. Imaging at that time suggested a right optic nerve CM. Because useful visual function had returned and the optimal timing of surgery for this rare lesion was uncertain, conservative follow-up was chosen. During subsequent years, the patient experienced recurrent fluctuations in vision, with alternating periods of partial recovery and deterioration. In the months preceding the current admission, these episodes were followed by sustained visual decline accompanied by headache and dizziness, prompting neurosurgical reevaluation. Given the progressive course and concern for cumulative optic nerve injury related to recurrent intralesional hemorrhage, surgical treatment was reconsidered.

Neuro-ophthalmological examination demonstrated pallor of the right optic disc, predominantly involving the superior and temporal regions. Optical coherence tomography revealed corresponding structural defects within the retinal nerve fiber layer. Visual field testing demonstrated severe temporal and inferior visual field deficits approaching quadrantanopic and hemianopic distribution in the right eye (Fig. 1).

MRI revealed an approximately 1-cm lesion intrinsic to the right optic nerve at the level of the internal carotid artery bifurcation. The lesion demonstrated heterogeneous signal intensity with hypointense regions suggestive of hemosiderin deposits and showed no significant contrast enhancement. The radiological appearance was consistent with CM involving the right optic nerve (Fig. 2). Although a partially thrombosed aneurysm was considered because of the lesion’s proximity to the internal carotid artery bifurcation, MRI demonstrated no flow-related signal, residual patent lumen, or continuity with an adjacent arterial structure; therefore, preoperative catheter angiography was not obtained.

Early postoperative axial (B) and coronal (E) T2-weighted MR images obtained on postoperative day 1, demonstrating GTR of the lesion and decompression of the optic apparatus without residual hemorrhagic mass. Follow-up axial (C) and coronal (F) T2-weighted MR images obtained at 3 months, demonstrating durable complete resection without radiological recurrence.

Given the patient’s progressive visual deterioration and concern for recurrent hemorrhage-related optic injury, microsurgical treatment was recommended. He underwent a right-sided pterional craniotomy through a transsylvian approach under neuronavigation guidance. After proximal sylvian fissure dissection and opening of the carotid and chiasmatic cisterns, the lesion was identified as a violaceous intraneural CM involving the right optic nerve. Hemosiderin staining extending toward the chiasmatic region and gyrus rectus was observed intraoperatively. To improve exposure and minimize traction on the optic apparatus, optic canal unroofing and falciform ligament release were performed. The optic sheath was subsequently opened to facilitate decompression and visualization of the anterior extent of the lesion. Sharp microsurgical dissection was carried out along the cavernoma–optic nerve interface while preserving the surrounding perforating vessels and pial vascular supply of the optic nerve. Intraoperative fluorescein angiography was used to better delineate the relationship between the lesion and adjacent vascular structures. A vascular structure associated with the optic nerve surface was carefully preserved throughout the dissection. Following circumferential mobilization, the CM was resected en bloc. Discussion

Optic nerve CMs are exceptionally rare vascular lesions of the anterior visual pathway. Although CMs account for approximately 10%–15% of intracranial vascular malformations, lesions involving the optic apparatus comprise less than 1% of intracranial CMs, and isolated intrinsic optic nerve involvement represents one of the rarest anatomical subtypes reported in the literature.1,2,8,9 Because of their rarity and highly eloquent anatomical location, diagnosis and management remain challenging, and the available evidence is largely limited to isolated case reports and small surgical series.2,8,9

Observations

The present case illustrates several important clinical and surgical features of isolated intrinsic optic nerve CMs. First, the patient demonstrated a prolonged fluctuating clinical course characterized by recurrent episodes of visual deterioration and partial recovery over more than a decade, suggesting repeated intralesional hemorrhage and progressive hemosiderin-mediated injury to the optic apparatus. Second, despite long-standing symptoms, meaningful postoperative improvement of visual function and visual field deficits was achieved following microsurgical GTR. Third, the transsylvian pterional approach combined with optic canal unroofing and falciform ligament release provided favorable exposure of the opticocarotid region while minimizing manipulation of the optic nerve and surrounding neurovascular structures. Finally, meticulous preservation of the pial vascular network and perforating arteries, assisted by intraoperative fluorescein angiography, represented critical operative principles for minimizing postoperative visual morbidity.

Radiological diagnosis is often challenging because these lesions may mimic optic pathway glioma, a partially thrombosed aneurysm, inflammatory optic neuropathy, or a hemorrhagic neoplasm. Characteristic MRI findings of CMs include heterogeneous signal intensity reflecting blood products of different ages, hemosiderin-related hypointensity, and minimal or absent contrast enhancement. Optic pathway glioma more commonly produces fusiform enlargement and T2 hyperintensity with variable enhancement, whereas inflammatory optic neuropathy typically demonstrates optic nerve enhancement and an acute or subacute inflammatory clinical course without chronic hemosiderin deposition. A partially thrombosed aneurysm was an important consideration in the present case because of the lesion’s proximity to the internal carotid artery bifurcation; continuity with a parent vessel, a residual patent lumen, or flow-related signal would favor this diagnosis. However, catheter angiography was not performed because MRI demonstrated a characteristic intraneural hemorrhagic lesion with hemosiderin deposition and no imaging evidence of a patent vascular component or continuity with an adjacent artery. Although CMs are low-flow lesions and are typically angiographically occult, CTA, MRA, or digital subtraction angiography should be considered when the lesion’s relationship to an adjacent artery remains uncertain or when a partially thrombosed aneurysm cannot be confidently excluded. Nonetheless, definitive diagnosis may require histopathological confirmation.2,5,7,10

Despite the limited number of reported cases, the cumulative literature suggests that symptomatic lesions demonstrating progressive or recurrent visual deterioration may benefit from surgical treatment before irreversible optic nerve injury develops.2,7–10 As summarized in Table 1, most surgically treated patients experienced stabilization or improvement of visual function following resection, although postoperative deterioration has also been reported. In the 3-patient series by Kostic et al., vision improved in 2 patients and worsened in 1, while GTR was achieved in one of the 2 surgically treated patients.7 In the 14-patient series by Srinivasan et al., stable to normal postoperative vision was reported in 70% of patients.8 Conservatively managed lesions demonstrated variable outcomes, ranging from spontaneous recovery to progression to complete visual loss.5,7,9 These findings support consideration of timely intervention in selected symptomatic patients, particularly in the presence of progressive or fluctuating deficits or radiological evidence of repeated hemorrhage. However, conclusions regarding surgical outcomes and optimal timing remain limited by small heterogeneous series, variable follow-up, and incomplete reporting of extent of resection and complications.

The present case further emphasizes that meaningful visual recovery may still be achievable even after a prolonged symptomatic course. Despite more than a decade of fluctuating visual symptoms, our patient demonstrated postoperative improvement in both visual function and visual field deficits following GTR. This observation suggests that viable optic fibers may remain functionally recoverable despite long-standing compression and recurrent hemorrhagic injury, particularly when definitive treatment is performed before complete optic atrophy develops.

Surgical Considerations

The transsylvian pterional approach provided several important advantages in the present case. Extensive sylvian fissure dissection and opening of the basal cisterns allowed early CSF release and minimized fixed frontal lobe retraction. More importantly, the transsylvian corridor provided direct access to the opticocarotid region with excellent visualization of the internal carotid artery, A1 segment, perforating vessels, and the prechiasmatic optic nerve.

Although several operative corridors have been described for anterior visual pathway lesions, including subfrontal, supraorbital, and orbitozygomatic approaches, the transsylvian route remains particularly advantageous for lesions located within the opticocarotid cistern.7–9 In the present case, this approach allowed circumferential microsurgical exposure of the lesion while minimizing manipulation of the optic apparatus.

Optic canal unroofing and falciform ligament release represented additional key operative nuances. These maneuvers improved visualization of the anterior optic nerve and reduced traction during intraneural dissection. Although optic canal decompression is not routinely required for all optic pathway lesions, it may be particularly beneficial in prechiasmatic or intracanalicular optic nerve cavernomas where operative maneuverability is limited.

Preservation of the pial vascular network surrounding the optic nerve was considered a critical surgical principle throughout the procedure. The vascular anatomy of the anterior visual pathway is highly delicate, and injury to perforating vessels may result in irreversible ischemic optic injury despite technically successful lesion removal. In the present case, meticulous sharp arachnoid dissection and circumferential microsurgical mobilization allowed preservation of surrounding perforators and a vascular structure associated with the optic nerve surface.

Another notable technical aspect was the use of intraoperative fluorescein angiography to better delineate the relationship between the lesion and adjacent vascular structures. Although fluorescein angiography is more commonly discussed in vascular neurosurgery, its use in this case facilitated preservation of critical vascular anatomy within a highly eloquent operative corridor.

The lesion additionally demonstrated a distinct hemosiderin-stained dissection plane intraoperatively, facilitating separation of cavernoma-associated gliotic tissue from functional optic nerve fibers. Similar findings have been described in previous reports and may aid safe microsurgical dissection in selected lesions.

Timing of Surgery and Visual Outcomes

The optimal timing of surgery for optic nerve CMs remains controversial because of the rarity of these lesions and the absence of prospective studies. Nevertheless, the available literature increasingly supports surgical treatment in symptomatic patients with progressive visual decline, recurrent hemorrhage, or fluctuating visual symptoms.2,7–10

Repeated hemorrhage may result in cumulative axonal injury through direct compression, ischemia, and hemosiderin-related toxicity.2,8,9 Accordingly, prolonged observation in a patient with recurrent or progressive visual symptoms may reduce the potential for postoperative recovery once irreversible optic nerve degeneration has occurred. Timely microsurgical treatment should therefore be considered when objective visual deterioration, recurrent hemorrhagic episodes, or structural evidence of optic nerve injury is documented.2,8–10 In retrospect, earlier intervention could reasonably have been considered once recurrent visual episodes became established in our patient. However, his substantial initial recovery, preservation of useful vision, and the absence of an evidence-based threshold for intervention supported the initial decision for observation. His subsequent course highlights the importance of serial objective neuro-ophthalmological assessment and prompt reconsideration of surgery when fluctuating symptoms evolve into sustained deterioration.

In the present case, partial postoperative recovery of visual deficits was achieved despite a prolonged symptomatic history. This finding reinforces the concept that visual improvement remains possible even in long-standing lesions when viable optic fibers persist. However, the progressive clinical course observed in this patient further supports consideration of early surgical intervention before complete functional deterioration develops.

Role of Radiosurgery

The role of radiosurgery in optic nerve CMs remains limited. Unlike CMs located in less eloquent regions, radiation treatment near the optic apparatus carries a substantial risk of radiation-induced optic neuropathy. Furthermore, radiosurgery does not provide immediate decompression and may not prevent recurrent hemorrhage during the latency period before treatment effect occurs. For these reasons, microsurgical resection remains the preferred treatment modality for symptomatic surgically accessible optic nerve CMs.2,8,9

Lessons

Isolated intrinsic optic nerve CMs are exceptionally rare lesions that should be considered in patients presenting with recurrent or progressive visual deficits associated with hemorrhagic pathology of the anterior visual pathway.3–10 The cumulative literature suggests that symptomatic patients may benefit from early microsurgical treatment before irreversible optic nerve injury develops.2,7–10 A transsylvian approach combined with optic canal unroofing can provide a favorable operative corridor for selected lesions while facilitating preservation of the vascular supply of the optic apparatus and surrounding perforating arteries.7–9
