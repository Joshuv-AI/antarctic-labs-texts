Radial Müntz-Szász Networks: Neural Architectures with Learnable Power Bases for Multidimensional Singularities
Gnankan Landry Regis N’guessan 1,2,3 Bum Jun Kim 4 rnguessan@aimsric.org bumjun.kim@weblab.t.u-tokyo.ac.jp 1 Axiom Research Group 2 Department of Applied Mathematics and Computational Science, NM-AIST, Arusha, Tanzania 3 African Institute for Mathematical Sciences (AIMS), Research and Innovation Centre, Kigali, Rwanda 4 Graduate School of Engineering, The University of Tokyo, Japan

Abstract
Radial singular fields—such as 1 / r 1/r , log ⁡ r \log r , and crack-tip profiles—are difficult to model for coordinate-separable neural architectures. We show that any C 2 C^{2} function that is both radial and additively separable must be quadratic, establishing a fundamental obstruction for coordinate-wise power-law models. Motivated by this result, we introduce Radial Müntz-Szász Networks (RMN), which represent fields as linear combinations of learnable radial powers r μ r^{\mu} , including negative exponents, together with a limit-stable log-primitive for exact log ⁡ r \log r behavior. RMN admits closed-form spatial gradients and Laplacians, enabling physics-informed learning on punctured domains. Across ten 2D and 3D benchmarks, RMN achieves 1.5 × \times –51 × \times lower RMSE than MLPs and 10 × \times –100 × \times lower RMSE than SIREN while using 27 parameters, compared with 33,537 for MLPs and 8,577 for SIREN. We extend RMN to angular dependence (RMN-Angular) and to multiple sources with learnable centers (RMN-MC); when optimization converges, source-center recovery errors fall below 10 − 4 10^{-4} . We also report controlled failures on smooth, strongly non-radial targets to delineate RMN’s operating regime.



Keywords

Scientific machine learning, singular functions, Müntz-Szász theorem, physics-informed neural networks, structure-matched architectures, radial basis functions, power-law singularities, Green’s functions

Introduction

Many physical singularities in multiple spatial dimensions are radial: they depend primarily on the distance to a source. For Laplace’s equation in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the fundamental solution scales as
\left\|\bm{x}\right\|^{2-d}
for
d\geq 3
and as
\log\left\|\bm{x}\right\|
for
d=2

(
15
)
. This distance-to-source scaling underlies the Coulomb and gravitational potentials, line-charge fields, and fracture-mechanics crack-tip asymptotics, where stress fields scale as
r^{-1/2}
while displacement fields scale as
r^{1/2}

(
46
)
. Boundary layers in fluid dynamics can exhibit similarly steep, fractional-power behavior.

These radial singularities pose fundamental challenges for neural network approximation. Standard multilayer perceptrons (MLPs) with smooth activation functions exhibit a spectral bias toward low-frequency components
(
33
)
, so capturing singular behaviors such as
1/r
or
\log r
typically requires large models and long training. In our benchmarks, a 4-layer, width-128 MLP with 33,537 parameters approaches an accuracy that Radial Müntz-Szász Networks (RMN) achieve with only 27 parameters. Sinusoidal representation networks (SIREN)
(
39
)
broaden frequency support for smooth implicit representations, but they still lack an explicit mechanism for power-law divergence near singularities and require 8,577 parameters to reach accuracy comparable to RMN’s 27.

In recent work, we introduced Müntz-Szász Networks (MSN)
(
30
)
, which replace fixed activation functions with learnable power-law bases. The classical Müntz-Szász theorem
(
29
;
40
)
characterizes when systems of monomials
\{x^{\mu_{k}}\}
form complete bases in continuous function spaces, providing a theoretical foundation for power-law neural architectures. MSN improves approximation for one-dimensional singular functions. However, extending MSN to multiple dimensions by applying it coordinate-wise requires an additively separable model:

\phi_{\text{MSN-coord}}(\bm{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{k=1}^{K}a_{ik}|x_{i}|^{\mu_{k}}.

(1)

Such separable functions fundamentally cannot represent radial structure. We prove a separability obstruction theorem: any
C^{2}
function that is both radial and additively separable must be quadratic (Theorem
3.15
). This explains why coordinate-wise MSN systematically fails on radial singularities by factors of 72
\times
to 1,652
\times
, regardless of capacity.

This observation motivates RMN, which directly parameterizes radial power functions:

\phi_{\text{RMN}}(\bm{x})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}a_{k}\left\|\bm{x}\right\|^{\mu_{k}}+c_{0}\psi_{\log}(r;\mu_{\log})+b_{0},

(2)

where
r=\left\|\bm{x}\right\|=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}^{2}}
,
\{\mu_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
are learnable exponents in
[\mu_{\min},\mu_{\max}]
,
\psi_{\log}(r;\mu_{\log})
is a log-primitive with a learnable log-exponent
\mu_{\log}
with the limit
\mu_{\log}\to 0
recovering
\log r
, and
b_{0}
is a bias term. Crucially, RMN allows negative exponents:
1/r=r^{-1}
requires
\mu=-1
, which coordinate-wise approaches with positive exponents cannot achieve.

Related Work

RMN draws on and extends several research threads: physics-informed neural networks (PINNs), specialized architectures for implicit representations, classical approximation theory, and numerical methods for handling singularities. We position our contribution relative to each.

2.1
PINNs

PINNs
(
34
)
embed partial differential equation (PDE) residuals directly into the loss function, enabling mesh-free solutions to forward and inverse problems. This framework has been extensively reviewed
(
21
;
12
)
and implemented in libraries such as DeepXDE
(
27
)
. However, PINNs with standard MLP architectures struggle near singularities due to spectral bias
(
33
)
: these networks preferentially learn low-frequency components, requiring many parameters and training iterations to accurately capture sharp gradients.

Several approaches have been developed to address PINN training difficulties.
43
analyzed gradient pathologies and proposed adaptive weighting schemes. The Deep Ritz method
(
47
)
reformulates PDEs variationally, while the Deep Galerkin Method
(
38
)
uses random sampling for high-dimensional problems. Recent work by
2
specifically addresses boundary layer singularities in fluid mechanics through tailored network architectures. Although these methods improve training performance, they do not address the fundamental architectural mismatch between smooth basis functions and singular targets.

RMN’s contribution

Rather than modifying training procedures, RMN modifies the architecture itself to match the target structure. The learnable power-law basis directly represents singularities that MLPs can only approximate inefficiently.

2.2
Specialized Architectures for Coordinate Networks

The spectral bias problem has motivated architectures with broader frequency support. SIREN
(
39
)
uses periodic activations
\sin(\omega x)
with careful initialization, achieving strong results in implicit neural representations. Fourier feature networks
(
41
)
map inputs through random Fourier features before a standard MLP, enabling high-frequency learning. Multiplicative filter networks
(
17
)
combine sinusoidal and Gabor filters for improved expressiveness.

Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks (KAN)
(
25
;
24
)
replace linear weights with learnable univariate functions on edges, inspired by the Kolmogorov-Arnold representation theorem. KAN demonstrates improved interpretability and efficiency in scientific problems, with the learned edge functions revealing the underlying structure.

Rational neural networks
(
8
;
7
)
represent another approach to learning singular behavior by parameterizing networks with rational functions. These networks can approximate functions with poles more efficiently than polynomial-based activations, providing a complementary strategy to RMN’s power-law basis.

RMN’s contribution

While SIREN and Fourier features address frequency content, they do not capture power-law structure. KAN’s learnable edge functions are related to our approach, but RMN specializes in radial power laws with learnable exponents, enabling the exact representation of fundamental solutions that coordinate-based methods cannot efficiently approximate, as shown in Theorem
3.15
. Unlike rational networks, which approximate singularities via poles, RMN directly parameterizes the singularity structure.

2.3
Neural Operators

Neural operators learn mappings between function spaces rather than pointwise evaluations. The Fourier Neural Operator (FNO)
(
22
)
parameterizes integral kernels in the Fourier space, achieving discretization-invariant learning. DeepONet
(
26
)
uses branch and trunk networks based on the universal approximation theorem for operators.

These methods excel at learning solution operators for families of PDEs but inherit the spectral properties of their underlying architectures. Near singularities, both FNO and DeepONet require fine discretization or many modes.

RMN’s contribution

RMN operates at the pointwise level with built-in singularity representation. For problems with a known radial structure, RMN provides a more parameter-efficient representation compared to discretizing singular kernels.

2.4
Radial Basis Function Networks

Radial basis function (RBF) networks
(
32
;
9
;
31
)
represent functions as weighted sums of radially symmetric basis functions centered on data points:

f(\bm{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{j}\varphi(\left\|\bm{x}-\bm{c}_{j}\right\|),

(3)

where
\varphi
is typically a Gaussian, multiquadric, or thin-plate spline. RBF networks have strong approximation properties
(
45
)
and have connections to Gaussian processes.

RMN’s contribution

Classical RBF networks use fixed functional forms
\varphi
, for example
\varphi(r)=e^{-r^{2}}
or
\varphi(r)=r^{2}\log r
. RMN instead learns the functional form itself through learnable exponents. This enables adaptation to the specific singularity structure of the target, rather than relying on a predetermined basis.

Numerical Methods for Singularities

Classical numerical analysis has developed specialized techniques for singular problems. Extended finite element methods (XFEM)

Mesh-free methods
(

completely avoid mesh generation, with some variants incorporating singular enrichments. Boundary element methods naturally handle fundamental solution singularities through careful quadrature
(

RMN’s contribution

These classical methods require the a priori specification of the singularity structure. RMN learns the appropriate exponents directly from data, automatically discovering singularity orders. This is particularly valuable for inverse problems where the singularity structure is unknown or for complex problems where multiple types interact.

Equivariant and Geometric Neural Networks

Equivariant neural networks
(

RMN’s contribution

2.7
Approximation Theory

Universal approximation theorems for neural networks
(
13
;
20
)
guarantee that sufficiently wide networks can approximate any continuous function. However, these results do not address the efficiency regarding the required number of parameters for a given accuracy.

Barron’s theorem
(
4
)
provides efficiency guarantees for functions with bounded Fourier moments, but singular functions violate these regularity assumptions. The Müntz-Szász theorem
(
29
;

)
characterizes which exponent sequences yield dense spans in

RMN’s contribution

We extend Müntz-Szász theory to neural network architectures with learnable exponents, negative powers for singularities, and multidimensional radial structure. We establish density in radial

3
Foundations: Preliminaries and Separability Obstruction

This section establishes the mathematical framework for analyzing radial singularities and their approximation. We work primarily in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with the Euclidean norm
\left\|\bm{x}\right\|=(\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}^{2})^{1/2}
, review the calculus and integrability of radial powers, and then prove the obstruction result used to justify RMN.

3.1
Radial Functions in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

Definition 3.1

(Radial function)
.

A function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is called radial, also called spherically symmetric, if there exists a function
h:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
such that
f(\bm{x})=h(\left\|\bm{x}\right\|)
for all
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We write
f(\bm{x})=h(r)
where
r=\left\|\bm{x}\right\|
.

Radial functions are invariant under orthogonal transformations:
f(O\bm{x})=f(\bm{x})
for all
O\in O(d)
. This invariance serves as both a geometric constraint and a source of efficiency, because radial functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
are effectively one-dimensional.

Calculus of radial functions

For a differentiable radial function
f(\bm{x})=h(r)
:

\displaystyle\nabla f(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=h^{\prime}(r)\frac{\bm{x}}{r},

(4)

\displaystyle\Delta f(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=h^{\prime\prime}(r)+\frac{d-1}{r}h^{\prime}(r).

(5)

The Laplacian formula shows that the
(d-1)/r
term couples the radial derivative to the dimension. For
h(r)=r^{\mu}
:

\Delta r^{\mu}=\mu(\mu-1)r^{\mu-2}+(d-1)\mu r^{\mu-2}=\mu(\mu+d-2)r^{\mu-2}.

(6)

Corollary 3.2
.

The radial power
r^{\mu}
is harmonic
\Delta r^{\mu}=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
if and only if
\mu=0
or
\mu=2-d
.

Thus
r^{-1}
is harmonic in
d=3
and corresponds to the Coulomb and Newtonian potential,
r^{0}=1
is trivially harmonic everywhere, and
\log r
is harmonic in
d=2
as the limit
\mu\to 0
of
(r^{\mu}-1)/\mu
.

3.2
Integrability and Singular Orders

Not all radial singularities are equally severe. The integrability of
r^{\alpha}
near the origin determines whether the singularity is mild or strong, i.e., integrable or non-integrable.

Proposition 3.3

(
L^{p}
integrability of radial powers)
.

Let
B_{1}=\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\left\|\bm{x}\right\|\leq 1\}
be the unit ball. Then
r^{\alpha}\in L^{p}(B_{1})
if and only if
\alpha>-d/p
.

Proof.

Switching to polar coordinates,

\int_{B_{1}}|r^{\alpha}|^{p}d\bm{x}=\omega_{d}\int_{0}^{1}r^{\alpha p}r^{d-1}dr=\omega_{d}\int_{0}^{1}r^{\alpha p+d-1}dr.

This integral converges if and only if
\alpha p+d-1>-1
, that is,
\alpha>-d/p
.
∎

For
p=2
, corresponding to the mean squared error (MSE) loss, this criterion yields dimension-dependent thresholds. In
d=2
,
r^{\alpha}\in L^{2}(B_{1})
if and only if
\alpha>-1
, so
\log r\in L^{2}(B_{1})
but
r^{-1}\notin L^{2}(B_{1})
. In
d=3
,
r^{\alpha}\in L^{2}(B_{1})
if and only if
\alpha>-3/2
, so
r^{-1}\in L^{2}(B_{1})
and the Coulomb potential is square-integrable in 3D.

This distinction matters for training: when the
L^{2}
norm diverges, minimizing the unweighted MSE on the full domain is ill-posed. In our benchmarks, we therefore fit and evaluate on punctured domains
\{\bm{x}:\left\|\bm{x}\right\|\geq r_{\min}\}
with
r_{\min}>0
, which ensures that the loss is well-defined.

Remark 3.4

(Punctured Domains and Evaluation Protocol)
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and define the punctured domain
\Omega_{\varepsilon}=\{\bm{x}:\left\|\bm{x}\right\|\geq\varepsilon\}
. Such punctured domains are not merely a numerical convenience but a mathematical necessity: for singularities with
\alpha\leq-d/2
, the
L^{2}
norm
\|f\|_{L^{2}(B_{1})}
diverges, so standard MSE training is ill-defined on the full domain. For example, fitting
r^{-1}
in 2D with
L^{2}
loss on
B_{1}
asks for a finite approximation to an infinite quantity. In our experiments, we take
\varepsilon=r_{\min}=0.01
. We sample all training points uniformly from
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
, evaluate on a separate test grid within the same punctured domain, and report the root mean squared error (RMSE) computed on the punctured test domain. This is standard practice in singular function approximation and matches evaluation protocols in XFEM
(
28
)
and related literature. Physically, this is also realistic: real systems have finite extent, with no true point charges and finite crack-tip radii.

3.3
The Müntz-Szász Theorem

The classical Müntz-Szász theorem characterizes when power functions form a complete basis for continuous functions.

Theorem 3.5

(Müntz-Szász
(
29
;
40
)
)
.

Let
0<\mu_{1}<\mu_{2}<\cdots
be a strictly increasing sequence of positive real numbers. The linear span of
\{1,x^{\mu_{1}},x^{\mu_{2}},\ldots\}
is dense in
C[0,1]
equipped with the supremum norm if and only if

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\mu_{k}}=\infty.

(7)

This remarkable theorem shows that even sparse sequences of exponents can yield universal approximation, provided the exponents do not grow too quickly.

Example 3.6

(Satisfying and violating the Müntz condition)
.

Consider several representative exponent sets. For standard polynomials,
\Lambda=\{0,1,2,3,\ldots\}
, the harmonic series
\sum_{k}1/k
diverges, so Eq.
7
holds, and density is consistent with the Weierstrass approximation. Likewise, for half-integer exponents
\Lambda=\{0,0.5,1,1.5,2,\ldots\}
, one has
\sum_{k}2/(k+1)=\infty
, and the span is dense in
C[0,1]
. In contrast, for squared exponents
\Lambda=\{0,1,4,9,16,\ldots\}=\{k^{2}\}
, the series
\sum_{k}1/k^{2}=\pi^{2}/6
converges, so this set is not dense and cannot approximate functions such as
x^{1/2}
. A similar failure occurs for
\Lambda=\{0,2,4,8,16,\ldots\}=\{2^{k}\}
, since
\sum_{k}2^{-k}=2<\infty
.

Limitations of Müntz-Szász theorem for singular approximation.

The Müntz-Szász theorem, while foundational, has several limitations for our purposes. First, it concerns positive exponents on
[0,1]
, whereas singular functions require negative exponents. Second, the theorem guarantees density but not efficiency. Third, the logarithm
\log x
cannot be represented as a finite linear combination of powers. Finally, the theorem is one-dimensional.

We now establish a density result for radial Müntz-type expansions that addresses these limitations, extending the classical theorem to multidimensional radial
L^{2}
spaces with negative exponents.

Definition 3.7

(Radial Müntz space)
.

Let
\Lambda=\{\mu_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\subset\mathbb{R}
be a sequence of distinct real exponents. Define the radial Müntz space on the annulus
A_{a,b}=\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:a\leq\left\|\bm{x}\right\|\leq b\}
with
0<a<b
as:

\mathcal{M}_{\Lambda}^{\mathrm{rad}}(A_{a,b})=\overline{\operatorname{span}\{r^{\mu_{k}}:k\in\mathbb{N}\}}^{L^{2}_{\mathrm{rad}}(A_{a,b})},

(8)

where
L^{2}_{\mathrm{rad}}(A_{a,b})=\{f\in L^{2}(A_{a,b}):f(\bm{x})=h(\left\|\bm{x}\right\|)\text{ a.e.}\}
denotes the closed subspace of radial functions.

Theorem 3.8

(Radial Müntz-Szász Density)
.

Let
0<a<b<\infty
and let
\Lambda^{+}=\{\mu_{k}^{+}:k\in\mathbb{N}\}
and
\Lambda^{-}=\{\mu_{k}^{-}:k\in\mathbb{N}\}
be sequences of positive and negative exponents, respectively, with
\mu_{k}^{+}\to+\infty
and
\mu_{k}^{-}\to-\infty
. Define
\Lambda=\Lambda^{+}\cup\Lambda^{-}\cup\{0\}
. If

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\mu_{k}^{+}}=\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{|\mu_{k}^{-}|}=\infty,

(9)

then
\mathcal{M}_{\Lambda}^{\mathrm{rad}}(A_{a,b})=L^{2}_{\mathrm{rad}}(A_{a,b})
.

Proof.

We reduce the multidimensional radial problem to the classical Müntz-Szász theorem through successive changes of variables.

First, for a radial function
f(\bm{x})=h(r)
with
r=\left\|\bm{x}\right\|
, the
L^{2}
norm on the annulus
A_{a,b}
satisfies

\|f\|_{L^{2}(A_{a,b})}^{2}=\omega_{d}\int_{a}^{b}|h(r)|^{2}r^{d-1}\,dr,

(10)

where
\omega_{d}=2\pi^{d/2}/\Gamma(d/2)
is the surface area of
S^{d-1}
. Thus
L^{2}_{\mathrm{rad}}(A_{a,b})
is isometrically isomorphic to the weighted space
L^{2}([a,b],r^{d-1}dr)
.

Next, the substitution
r=a+(b-a)t
maps
[a,b]
to
[0,1]
, and the power
r^{\mu}
becomes
(a+(b-a)t)^{\mu}
. Density of the powers
\{r^{\mu}\}
in
L^{2}([a,b],r^{d-1}dr)
is equivalent to the density of
\{(a+(b-a)t)^{\mu}\}
in
L^{2}([0,1],w(t)dt)
for a strictly positive continuous weight
w(t)=(a+(b-a)t)^{d-1}(b-a)
.

We then observe that on
[0,1]
with any continuous positive weight, density questions reduce to the unweighted case by standard arguments
(
3
, Chapter II)
. The binomial expansion of
(a+(b-a)t)^{\mu}
shows that each such function lies in the closed span of
\{t^{n}:n\geq 0\}\cup\{t^{\mu}:\mu\in\Lambda\}
over
[0,1]
.

More directly, we apply the substitution
t=x^{\gamma}
for an appropriate
\gamma>0
to reduce to the classical setting. The key observation is that, on any interval
[a,b]
with
0<a<b
, the system
\{r^{\mu}:\mu\in\Lambda\}
is complete if and only if the bilateral Müntz condition Eq.
9
holds. This follows from the classical Müntz-Szász theorem applied separately to the positive and negative exponent subsequences, using the fact that on
[a,b]
with
a>0
, negative powers are bounded continuous functions
(
3
;
1
)
.

Finally, by hypothesis Eq.
9
, the exponent sequence
\Lambda
satisfies the bilateral Müntz condition. Hence
\{r^{\mu_{k}}\}
is complete in
L^{2}([a,b],r^{d-1}dr)
, and therefore, in
L^{2}_{\mathrm{rad}}(A_{a,b})
.
∎

Remark 3.9

(Relation to the classical theorem)
.

When restricted to positive exponents and
d=1
, Theorem
3.8
recovers the classical Müntz-Szász theorem on
[a,b]\subset(0,\infty)
. The extension to negative exponents is essential for singular approximation: the condition
\sum 1/|\mu_{k}^{-}|=\infty
ensures that arbitrarily strong singularities can be approximated. The annular domain
a>0
avoids integrability issues at the origin.

Remark 3.10

(Role of negative exponents)
.

The bilateral condition Eq.
9
requires both positive and negative exponents to diverge slowly enough. This is essential for approximating singular functions: positive exponents alone cannot efficiently represent
r^{-1}
near zero, while negative exponents alone cannot capture smooth growth at large
r
. The RMN architecture learns exponents from both regimes, automatically discovering the appropriate balance for the target function.

Remark 3.11

(Logarithmic terms)
.

Theorem
3.8
does not directly include
\log r
. However, for any
\mu\neq 0
, the identity
(r^{\mu}-1)/\mu\to\log r
as
\mu\to 0
shows that
\log r
lies in the
L^{2}
closure of Müntz spans with exponents accumulating at zero. In practice, we include a dedicated log-primitive
\psi_{\log}(r;\mu_{\log})
for numerical stability, rather than relying solely on this limiting argument.

Corollary 3.12

(Density for RMN hypothesis class)
.

Fix
0<a<b
. Suppose that, in the large-
K
limit, the learnable exponents can realize a sequence
\Lambda=\{\mu_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
whose positive and negative subsequences satisfy the hypotheses of Theorem
3.8
, in particular,
\mu_{k}^{+}\to+\infty
,
\mu_{k}^{-}\to-\infty
, and Eq.
9
holds. This requires that the allowable exponent range is not fixed a priori and permits arbitrarily large
|\mu|
. Then the RMN-Direct hypothesis class is dense in
L^{2}_{\mathrm{rad}}(A_{a,b})
.

Proof.

Fix
0<a<b
. By assumption, in the large-
K
limit, the learnable exponent set of the RMN can realize a sequence
\Lambda=\{\mu_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
satisfying the hypotheses of Theorem
3.8
, in particular the bilateral Müntz condition Eq.
9
.
For each
K
, consider the RMN-Direct model with
c_{0}=0
and exponents
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{K}
. As the coefficients
a_{1},\ldots,a_{K}
and the bias
b_{0}
vary freely, the resulting hypothesis class contains
\operatorname{span}\{1,r^{\mu_{1}},\ldots,r^{\mu_{K}}\}
.
Therefore, the
L^{2}_{\mathrm{rad}}(A_{a,b})
-closure of the union over
K
contains

\overline{\operatorname{span}\{1,r^{\mu_{k}}:k\in\mathbb{N}\}}^{L^{2}_{\mathrm{rad}}(A_{a,b})}=\mathcal{M}_{\Lambda\cup\{0\}}^{\mathrm{rad}}(A_{a,b}),

which equals
L^{2}_{\mathrm{rad}}(A_{a,b})
by Theorem
3.8
.
∎

3.4
Spherical Harmonics

For functions with angular dependence, we employ spherical harmonics. In
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the spherical harmonics
\{Y_{\ell}^{m}\}_{\ell\geq 0,|m|\leq\ell}
form an orthonormal basis for
L^{2}(S^{d-1})
.

Any function defined on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
can be expanded as

f(\bm{x})=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}f_{\ell m}(r)Y_{\ell}^{m}(\hat{\bm{x}}),

(11)

where
\hat{\bm{x}}=\bm{x}/\left\|\bm{x}\right\|
is the unit direction and
f_{\ell m}(r)
are the radial coefficient functions.

2D specialization and mode counting

In
d=2
,
S^{d-1}=S^{1}
and the angular basis reduces to Fourier modes. Writing
\bm{x}=(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)
, we expand

f(r,\theta)=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}f_{m}(r)e^{im\theta}\approx\sum_{m=-M_{\max}}^{M_{\max}}f_{m}(r)e^{im\theta}.

This symmetric truncation contains
2M_{\max}+1
angular modes. If
f
is real-valued, then
f_{-m}(r)=f_{m}(r)^{\ast}
, equivalently, a cosine–sine representation with
2M_{\max}+1
real angular components.

3.5
Separability Obstruction

This subsection establishes the core theoretical result: coordinate-separable architectures are fundamentally incompatible with non-quadratic radial functions.

3.5.1
Coordinate-Separable Architectures

Definition 3.13

(Additive separability)
.

A function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is additively separable if there exist functions
g_{i}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

f(\bm{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}g_{i}(x_{i}).

(12)

Definition 3.14

(Multiplicative separability)
.

A function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is multiplicatively separable if there exist functions
g_{i}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

f(\bm{x})=\prod_{i=1}^{d}g_{i}(x_{i}).

(13)

Many neural architectures produce separable representations: coordinate-wise MSN, generalized additive models, tensor product bases, and the first layer of MLPs.

3.5.2
The Obstruction Theorem

Theorem 3.15

(Separability Obstruction)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb{R}
be
C^{2}
. Suppose that
f
is radial, that is,
f(x,y)=h(r)
for some
h:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
with
r=\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}
, and also additively separable, that is,
f(x,y)=g(x)+k(y)
for some
g,k:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
.
Then
h(r)=cr^{2}+d
for some constants
c,d\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

From radiality, we compute the mixed partial derivative using the chain rule:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}

\displaystyle=h^{\prime}(r)\frac{\partial r}{\partial x}=h^{\prime}(r)\frac{x}{r},

(14)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial x\partial y}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(h^{\prime}(r)\frac{x}{r}\right).

(15)

Applying the product rule, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial x\partial y}

\displaystyle=h^{\prime\prime}(r)\frac{\partial r}{\partial y}\cdot\frac{x}{r}+h^{\prime}(r)\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(\frac{x}{r}\right)

(16)

\displaystyle=h^{\prime\prime}(r)\frac{y}{r}\cdot\frac{x}{r}+h^{\prime}(r)\cdot x\cdot\left(-\frac{1}{r^{2}}\right)\frac{y}{r}

(17)

\displaystyle=\frac{xy}{r^{2}}h^{\prime\prime}(r)-\frac{xy}{r^{3}}h^{\prime}(r)

(18)

\displaystyle=\frac{xy}{r^{2}}\left(h^{\prime\prime}(r)-\frac{h^{\prime}(r)}{r}\right).

(19)

From additive separability,
f(x,y)=g(x)+k(y)
implies

\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial x\partial y}=\frac{\partial}{\partial y}g^{\prime}(x)=0.

Combining these results: for all
(x,y)
with
xy\neq 0
,

\frac{xy}{r^{2}}\left(h^{\prime\prime}(r)-\frac{h^{\prime}(r)}{r}\right)=0.

(20)

Because
xy\neq 0
and
r>0
, we conclude
h^{\prime\prime}(r)-h^{\prime}(r)/r=0
for all
r
such that there exists
(x,y)
with
x^{2}+y^{2}=r^{2}
and
xy\neq 0
.

Now we extend to all
r>0
. For any
r>0
, we can choose
x=y=r/\sqrt{2}
, which satisfies
x^{2}+y^{2}=r^{2}
and
xy=r^{2}/2\neq 0
. Thus the ordinary differential equation (ODE)
h^{\prime\prime}(r)=h^{\prime}(r)/r
holds for all of
(0,\infty)
. Alternatively, since
h\in C^{2}
, both
h^{\prime\prime}(r)
and
h^{\prime}(r)/r
are continuous on
(0,\infty)
. Two continuous functions that agree on a dense subset must agree everywhere on their common domain.

This is a second-order linear ODE. Let
\psi(r)=h^{\prime}(r)
. Then
\psi^{\prime}(r)=\psi(r)/r
, which gives:

\displaystyle\frac{d\psi}{\psi}

\displaystyle=\frac{dr}{r},

\displaystyle\log|\psi(r)|

\displaystyle=\log r+C_{1},

\displaystyle\psi(r)

\displaystyle=Ar,

for some constant
A
. Integrating,
h(r)=\frac{A}{2}r^{2}+B=cr^{2}+d
.
∎

Remark 3.16
.

The quadratic
r^{2}=x^{2}+y^{2}
is the only nontrivial radial function that is additively separable. This is because
x^{2}+y^{2}=g(x)+k(y)
with
g(x)=x^{2}
and
k(y)=y^{2}
.

This result formalizes the empirical observation that coordinate-wise networks systematically introduce axis-aligned artifacts when approximating radial structure. Figure

illustrates a representative example.

3.5.3
Extension to Multiplicative Separability

Proposition 3.17
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}\to(0,\infty)
be
C^{2}
, radial, and multiplicatively separable. Then there exist constants
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
such that

f(x,y)=\exp(a(x^{2}+y^{2})+b).

Proof.

Since
f>0
, we can take logarithms:
\log f(x,y)=\log g(x)+\log h(y)
. Thus
\log f
is additively separable. Because
f
is radial,
\log f
is also radial. By Theorem
3.15
,
\log f(x,y)=a(x^{2}+y^{2})+b
for some constants
a,b
. Hence
f(x,y)=e^{b}\cdot e^{ar^{2}}=\exp(ar^{2}+b)
.
∎

Remark 3.18
.

Gaussian-type functions
\exp(ar^{2})
are smooth at the origin and do not exhibit singular behavior. Thus multiplicative separability cannot represent radial singularities such as
r^{-1}
,
r^{1/2}
, or
\log r
. This complements Theorem
3.15
: neither additive nor multiplicative separability can capture non-quadratic radial structure.

3.5.4
Extension to Higher Dimensions

Proposition 3.19
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb{R}
be
C^{2}
, radial, and additively separable:
f(\bm{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}g_{i}(x_{i})
. Then
f(\bm{x})=c\left\|\bm{x}\right\|^{2}+d
for constants
c,d
.

Proof.

For any pair of indices
i\neq j
, consider the restriction of
f
to the
(x_{i},x_{j})
-plane. This restriction is both radial in the 2D sense and additively separable. By Theorem
3.15
,
f(x_{i},x_{j})=c_{ij}(x_{i}^{2}+x_{j}^{2})+d_{ij}
. Consistency across all pairs requires
c_{ij}=c
and
d_{ij}=d
for all
i,j
, giving
f(\bm{x})=c\left\|\bm{x}\right\|^{2}+d
.
∎

3.5.5
Implications for Neural Approximation

The separability obstruction has immediate consequences for neural network architectures:

Coordinate-wise MSN cannot efficiently approximate radial singularities

Because MSN-coord produces additively separable functions, Theorem
3.15
implies that it can only exactly represent quadratic radials. The failure is structural, not capacity-limited. Adding more parameters in MSN-coord does not resolve the obstruction. The obstruction quantifies the capacity gap. To approximate
\log r
using coordinate-wise bases, the required number of terms grows as
\varepsilon^{-\gamma}
for a tolerance
\varepsilon
. RMN with its log-primitive achieves a numerically exact representation through a stable limiting mechanism.

4
RMN: Architecture and Variants

We first define RMN-Direct and its exponent parameterization, then introduce the log-primitive and numerical stabilization, and finally present two extensions: RMN-Angular for angular dependence and RMN-MC for multiple singularity centers.

4.1
RMN-Direct: Architecture and Exponent Parameterization

Definition 4.1

(RMN-Direct)
.

For an input
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the RMN-Direct network computes:

\boxed{\phi_{\text{RMN}}(\bm{x})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}a_{k}r^{\mu_{k}}+c_{0}\psi_{\log}(r;\mu_{\log})+b_{0}},

(21)

where
r=\left\|\bm{x}\right\|
is the radial coordinate,
\{a_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
are learnable coefficients,
\{\mu_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}\subset[\mu_{\min},\mu_{\max}]
are learnable exponents including negative values,
c_{0}
is the coefficient for the log-primitive,
\mu_{\log}
is a learnable log-exponent,
\psi_{\log}(r;\mu_{\log})
is the log-primitive (Definition
4.3
), and
b_{0}
is a bias term.

Parameter count

RMN-Direct has
K
coefficients
+K
exponents
+1
log coefficient
+1
log exponent
+1
bias
=2K+3
parameters. For the default
K=12
, this gives 27 parameters, compared to 33,537 for a 4-layer MLP with 128 neurons per layer.

Exact Representability

We record two basic exact-representability propositions in Appendix
A.3
. These facts show that RMN has exact representation capacity for finite power sums, unlike MLPs, which must approximate.

Exponent Parameterization

We parameterize exponents using a cumulative-gap scheme that ensures (1) exponents remain in
[\mu_{\min},\mu_{\max}]
, (2) exponents are ordered, and (3) gradients flow smoothly. Given raw parameters
\{s_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
, we compute:

\displaystyle\delta_{k}

\displaystyle=\softplus(s_{k})+\epsilon,\epsilon=0.01,

(22)

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\delta_{j},

(23)

\displaystyle u_{k}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{k}/\sigma_{K},

(24)

\displaystyle\mu_{k}

\displaystyle=\mu_{\min}+(\mu_{\max}-\mu_{\min})u_{k}.

(25)

The default exponent range is
[\mu_{\min},\mu_{\max}]=[-2,4]
, which covers singularities from
r^{-2}
to regular functions like
r^{4}
.

4.2
Logarithmic Singularities: The Log-Primitive

Proposition 4.2
.

The function
\log r
cannot be represented as a finite sum
\sum_{k}a_{k}r^{\mu_{k}}
for any choice of
\mu_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Suppose
\log r=\sum_{k=1}^{K}a_{k}r^{\mu_{k}}
. Differentiating:
1/r=\sum_{k}a_{k}\mu_{k}r^{\mu_{k}-1}
. The left side is
r^{-1}
; the right side is a finite sum of terms
r^{\mu_{k}-1}
. For equality, we need exactly one
\mu_{j}=0
with
a_{j}\mu_{j}=1
, but
\mu_{j}=0
gives
a_{j}\cdot 0=0\neq 1
. Contradiction.
∎

We include an explicit log-primitive based on the identity:

\lim_{\mu\to 0}\frac{r^{\mu}-1}{\mu}=\log r.

(26)

Definition 4.3

(Log-primitive)
.

For
r>0
, define

\psi_{\log}(r;\mu)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{r^{\mu}-1}{\mu}&\text{if }\mu\neq 0,\\[8.0pt]
\displaystyle\log r&\text{if }\mu=0.\end{cases}

(27)

For numerical stability when
|\mu|
is small, we evaluate this expression using its Taylor expansion and switch to that series below a threshold
\epsilon_{\log}=10^{-4}
.

Proposition 4.4

(Pointwise convergence)
.

For all
r>0
,
\psi_{\log}(r;\mu)\to\log r
as
\mu\to 0
.

Proof.

Write
r^{\mu}=e^{\mu\log r}=1+\mu\log r+\frac{\mu^{2}(\log r)^{2}}{2}+O(\mu^{3})
. Then

\frac{r^{\mu}-1}{\mu}=\log r+\frac{\mu(\log r)^{2}}{2}+O(\mu^{2})\to\log r\text{ as }\mu\to 0.

∎

Numerical stability

Direct computation of
(r^{\mu}-1)/\mu
near
\mu=0
suffers from catastrophic cancellation. The Taylor expansion provides a numerically stable alternative. We use a smooth sigmoid-based blend between the two formulas to ensure differentiability.

Learning logarithmic behavior

In practice, we include a separate learnable exponent
\mu_{\log}
for the log-primitive, initialized near zero. The network can learn to either (a) keep
\mu_{\log}\approx 0
to represent
\log r
, or (b) move
\mu_{\log}
away from zero to represent a power law
(r^{\mu_{\log}}-1)/\mu_{\log}
.

4.3
Optimization and Numerical Stability

For small
r
and negative
\mu
, direct computation of
r^{\mu}
can overflow. We therefore compute
r^{\mu}
in the stable form
r^{\mu}=\exp(\mu\log r)
and floor
r
at
r_{\mathrm{floor}}=10^{-12}
. The exponent gradient satisfies
\partial_{\mu_{k}}r^{\mu_{k}}=r^{\mu_{k}}\log r
, which can be large when
r
is small; to control this, we train on bounded, punctured domains and use gradient clipping. We initialize exponents uniformly in
[\mu_{\min},\mu_{\max}]
, sample coefficients from
\mathcal{N}(0,1/K)
, and initialize the log-exponent as
\mu_{\log}=0.1
. Optimization is performed using Adam with a learning rate of
2\times 10^{-3}
in 2D or
10^{-3}
in 3D for 5,000–8,000 iterations.

4.4
Closed-Form Derivatives for Physics-Informed Learning

For physics-informed applications, RMN provides closed-form spatial derivatives.

Proposition 4.5

(Gradient and Laplacian of RMN)
.

For
\phi_{\text{RMN}}(\bm{x})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}a_{k}r^{\mu_{k}}+c_{0}\psi_{\log}(r;\mu_{\log})+b_{0}
:

\displaystyle\nabla\phi_{\text{RMN}}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}a_{k}\mu_{k}r^{\mu_{k}-2}+c_{0}r^{\mu_{\log}-2}\right)\bm{x},

(28)

\displaystyle\Delta\phi_{\text{RMN}}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}a_{k}\mu_{k}(\mu_{k}+d-2)r^{\mu_{k}-2}+c_{0}(\mu_{\log}+d-2)r^{\mu_{\log}-2}.

(29)

Proof.

Fix
\bm{x}\neq 0
and write
r=\left\|\bm{x}\right\|
. Each term in
\phi_{\text{RMN}}
is radial, so we apply the identities Eq.
4
–Eq.
5
.
For
h(r)=r^{\mu_{k}}
we have
h^{\prime}(r)=\mu_{k}r^{\mu_{k}-1}
and
h^{\prime\prime}(r)=\mu_{k}(\mu_{k}-1)r^{\mu_{k}-2}
, hence

\nabla r^{\mu_{k}}=\mu_{k}r^{\mu_{k}-2}\bm{x},\qquad\Delta r^{\mu_{k}}=\mu_{k}(\mu_{k}+d-2)r^{\mu_{k}-2},

consistent with Eq.
6
.
For the log-primitive term, for
h(r)=\psi_{\log}(r;\mu_{\log})
we have
h^{\prime}(r)=r^{\mu_{\log}-1}
and
h^{\prime\prime}(r)=(\mu_{\log}-1)r^{\mu_{\log}-2}
for
\mu_{\log}\neq 0
; the same formulas hold at
\mu_{\log}=0
by taking the limit
\mu\to 0
, recovering
h(r)=\log r
. Therefore

\nabla\psi_{\log}(r;\mu_{\log})=r^{\mu_{\log}-2}\bm{x},\qquad\Delta\psi_{\log}(r;\mu_{\log})=(\mu_{\log}+d-2)r^{\mu_{\log}-2}\quad(r>0).

Summing the contributions and multiplying by the coefficients
a_{k}
and
c_{0}
yields Eq.
28
–Eq.
29
.
∎

The expressions above hold pointwise for
r>0
. In the special case
\mu_{\log}=0
and
d=2
,
\log r
is harmonic for
r>0
but contributes a distributional term proportional to the Dirac delta at the origin, consistent with its role as a Green’s function for the Laplacian.

Remark 4.6

(Clarification on Closed-Form Derivatives)
.

The spatial derivatives
\nabla_{\bm{x}}\phi
and
\Delta_{\bm{x}}\phi
in Eq.
28
–Eq.
29
are computed analytically using the formulas provided above, without invoking automatic differentiation. This provides computational efficiency for evaluating PDE residuals in physics-informed learning. However, the parameter gradients used for training,
\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\theta
with
\theta=\{a_{k},\mu_{k},c_{0},\mu_{\log},b_{0}\}
, still use standard backpropagation through the computational graph. The efficiency gain is in forward-mode evaluation of the physics constraints, not in eliminating backpropagation entirely.

4.5
RMN-Angular: Spherical Harmonic Extension

Definition 4.7

(RMN-Angular)
.

For an input
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
r=\left\|\bm{x}\right\|
and
\hat{\bm{x}}=\bm{x}/r
. The RMN-Angular network computes:

\phi_{\text{RMN-ang}}(\bm{x})=\underbrace{\sum_{k=1}^{K_{r}}a_{k}r^{\mu_{k}}}_{\text{radial terms}}+\underbrace{\sum_{\ell=1}^{L_{\max}}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}\sum_{j=1}^{K_{a}}c_{\ell mj}r^{\lambda_{j}}Y_{\ell}^{m}(\hat{\bm{x}})}_{\text{angular terms}}+c_{0}\psi_{\log}(r;\mu_{\log}),

where
Y_{\ell}^{m}
are real spherical harmonics, and
L_{\max}
is the maximum angular mode. We exclude the constant harmonic
\ell=0
from the angular branch, since it is purely radial and can be absorbed into the radial terms and bias.

2D convention

In
d=2

(\ell,m)

\bm{x}=(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)
we use the symmetric truncation
m=-M_{\max},\ldots,M_{\max}
,

\phi_{\text{RMN-ang}}(r,\theta)=\sum_{k=1}^{K_{r}}a_{k}r^{\mu_{k}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m=-M_{\max}\\
m\neq 0\end{subarray}}^{M_{\max}}\sum_{j=1}^{K_{a}}c_{mj}r^{\lambda_{j}}e^{im\theta}+c_{0}\psi_{\log}(r;\mu_{\log}),

with the real-valued implementation given by the equivalent cosine–sine basis; the
m=0
component is purely radial and is absorbed into the radial branch and bias, so the angular coefficient count uses
n_{\text{ang}}=2M_{\max}
.

Physical interpretation

RMN-Angular is a learned multipole expansion with fractional orders. Its applications include crack-tip fields with
\sigma\sim r^{-1/2}f(\theta)
, dipole potentials with
\phi=z/r^{3}=r^{-2}Y_{1}^{0}(\hat{\bm{x}})
, and corner singularities with
r^{\pi/\alpha}\sin(\pi\theta/\alpha)
.

4.5.1
Half-Integer Angular Basis for Crack-Tip Fields

The Williams stress field expansion
(
46
)
for crack-tip singularities takes the form:

\sigma_{ij}(r,\theta)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}A_{n}r^{(n-1)/2}f_{ij}^{(n)}(\theta),

(31)

where the angular functions involve
\cos((2n+1)\theta/2)
and
\sin((2n+1)\theta/2)
, which have half-integer periodicity. Standard spherical harmonics use integer angular frequencies and therefore cannot exactly represent these functions.

Definition 4.8

(RMN-Angular with Half-Integer Basis)
.

For 2D crack-tip problems, we extend RMN-Angular with a half-integer angular basis:

\phi_{\text{RMN-crack}}(\bm{x})=\sum_{k=1}^{K_{r}}a_{k}r^{\mu_{k}}+\sum_{n=0}^{N_{\max}}\sum_{j=1}^{K_{a}}\left[c_{nj}^{c}r^{\lambda_{j}}\cos\left(\frac{(2n+1)\theta}{2}\right)+c_{nj}^{s}r^{\lambda_{j}}\sin\left(\frac{(2n+1)\theta}{2}\right)\right],

(32)

where
\theta=\arctan(y/x)
is the polar angle measured from the crack line.

Improvement over standard RMN-Angular

On the crack-tip benchmark
f=r^{1/2}\cos(\theta/2)
: RMN-Angular with integer harmonics, using 51 parameters, achieves an RMSE of
2.40\times 10^{-2}
, while RMN-Angular with the half-integer basis, using 59 parameters, achieves an RMSE of
1.20\times 10^{-2}
. The half-integer basis provides a 2
\times
improvement by exactly matching the Williams series structure.

When is angular structure necessary?

Our experiments show that RMN-Direct fails on the 2D crack-tip benchmark without angular terms, achieving an RMSE of 0.227, while the MLP achieves 0.00435. RMN-Angular with
M_{\max}=4
reduces the error to 0.024, still worse than the MLP but structurally correct. This illustrates a key trade-off: structure-matched architectures excel when the target matches the assumed structure but may underperform when it does not.

The 2D crack-tip benchmark

MLP achieves the lowest RMSE,
4.35\times 10^{-3}
, but uses 33,537 parameters. RMN-Direct uses 27 parameters but fails without angular terms, with RMSE 0.227. RMN-Angular uses 51 parameters and reduces error to
2.40\times 10^{-2}

\times
improvement over RMN-Direct, and matches SIREN, which uses 8,577 parameters, at RMSE

MLP’s advantage is expected: the crack-tip angular factor
\cos(\theta/2)

captures only a finite set of integer modes; increasing
M_{\max}
would improve RMN-Angular at the cost of additional parameters.

In practice, use RMN-Angular when angular structure aligns with truncated harmonic bases, when parameter efficiency is critical, or when interpretability of angular modes is desired. Use MLP when angular structure is unknown or complex.

The 3D dipole field
\phi=z/r^{3}
initially showed optimization instability for RMN-Angular, with RMSE varying by 100
\times
across seeds. We traced this to two factors. First, the field has a large dynamic range:
|\phi|\sim 10^{2}
near poles and
|\phi|\sim 10^{-2}
near the equator. Second, gradients are large near the origin, where
\nabla(z/r^{3})\sim r^{-4}

\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}w_{i}(\phi(\bm{x}_{i})-f(\bm{x}_{i}))^{2}
with
w_{i}\propto r_{i}^{2}
to down-weight near-origin points, and a verification step that initializes coefficients to the ground truth and checks RMSE
<10^{-10}
before full training.

With these modifications, RMN-Angular achieves stable convergence on the dipole benchmark with mean RMSE over five seeds equal to
3.2\times 10^{-2}
, comparable to SIREN at
2.8\times 10^{-2}
.

6
Discussion and Conclusion

6.1
Limitations and Scope

RMN is not a universal approximator, and its strengths come with clear constraints:
RMN assumes singularities are radial or can be decomposed into radial components, so intrinsically non-radial or anisotropic singularities, for example, edges, interfaces, and corners, require different inductive biases. In the multi-center setting, RMN-MC is sensitive to center initialization and can encounter local minima, especially in high dimensions or with many centers. Our main experiments fit function values directly; for PINN settings, incorporating PDE residuals can better exploit RMN’s closed-form derivatives. We demonstrate RMN in 2D and 3D, but scaling to higher dimensions remains challenging due to sampling complexity, and exponent optimization can make RMN slower per iteration than an MLP, even when it reaches target accuracy in fewer iterations.

6.2
Conclusion

We introduced Radial Müntz-Szász Networks (RMN), a structure-matched architecture for multidimensional fields with radial singularities. RMN represents solutions as linear combinations of learnable radial powers
r^{\mu}
including negative exponents together with a limit-stable log-primitive, and our separability obstruction theorem clarifies why coordinate-separable power models cannot express non-quadratic radial functions. Closed-form gradients and Laplacians make RMN a natural building block for physics-informed learning on punctured domains.

Across ten 2D and 3D benchmarks, RMN achieves 1.5
\times
–51
\times
lower RMSE than a 33,537-parameter MLP and 10
\times
–100
\times
lower RMSE than an 8,577-parameter SIREN while using only 27 parameters. The learned exponent spectrum is physically interpretable e.g.,
\mu\to-0.997
for
r^{-1}
; RMN-Angular matches SIREN on angular problems with orders-of-magnitude fewer parameters, and RMN-MC can recover two-source centers to
<10^{-4}
when optimization succeeds. Overall, RMN’s advantage comes from matching architecture to a physically meaningful function class rather than increasing capacity.

RMN is most effective when the target exhibits known or discoverable radial singular structure; conversely, smooth or strongly non-radial, oscillatory targets remain better served by MLPs or sinusoidal networks. We delineate predictable failure modes, including crack-tip and smooth controls, and higher-variance regimes in harder multi-source settings. Promising directions include physics-informed RMN incorporating PDE residuals, improved multi-center optimization and adaptive center placement, anisotropic extensions, time-dependent variants, and hybrid singular–smooth architectures combining RMN with MLPs.

We provide the full derivation of the mixed partial for completeness.

From radiality
f(x,y)=h(r)
where

\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}=h^{\prime}(r)\frac{\partial r}{\partial x}=h^{\prime}(r)\frac{x}{r}.

Differentiating again yields the mixed partial derivative:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial y\partial x}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left[h^{\prime}(r)\frac{x}{r}\right]

(44)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial h^{\prime}(r)}{\partial y}\cdot\frac{x}{r}+h^{\prime}(r)\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(\frac{x}{r}\right)

(45)

\displaystyle=h^{\prime\prime}(r)\frac{y}{r}\cdot\frac{x}{r}+h^{\prime}(r)\cdot x\cdot\left(-\frac{1}{r^{2}}\right)\frac{y}{r}

(46)

\displaystyle=\frac{xy}{r^{2}}h^{\prime\prime}(r)-\frac{xy}{r^{3}}h^{\prime}(r)

(47)

\displaystyle=\frac{xy}{r^{2}}\left[h^{\prime\prime}(r)-\frac{h^{\prime}(r)}{r}\right].

(48)

If instead

is additively separable, then:

\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial y\partial x}=\frac{\partial}{\partial y}g^{\prime}(x)=0.

Therefore, for
xy\neq 0
we must have

h^{\prime\prime}(r)-\frac{h^{\prime}(r)}{r}=0.

Let
\psi(r)=h^{\prime}(r)

\psi^{\prime}(r)=\psi(r)/r

\displaystyle\frac{d\psi}{\psi}

\displaystyle=\frac{dr}{r},

\displaystyle\log|\psi(r)|

\displaystyle=\log r+C_{1},

\displaystyle\psi(r)

\displaystyle=Ar,

and hence

We prove

Appendix D
Additional Experiments: 3D Poisson with a Point Charge under Dirichlet Boundary Conditions

We include this experiment as a supplementary evaluation of RMN in a bounded-domain, physics-informed setting. We study the 3D Poisson equation on
\Omega=[-1,1]^{3}
with a single point charge and homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions. In contrast to the free-space Coulomb benchmark in Section
5.2
, the boundary breaks radial symmetry and induces a non-radial harmonic correction. We therefore treat this experiment primarily as a stress test of PINN stability and physics constraint satisfaction, rather than as a setting where a purely radial model should dominate global
L^{2}
error.

We consider two complementary training paradigms:
supervised fitting, where models are trained directly on a numerically computed ground truth solution
u^{\star}
, and full PINN training, where models are trained using only physics-based losses, PDE residual, boundary conditions, and Gauss flux constraints, without access to
u^{\star}
during training.

Note on hyperparameters.

Unlike the function-fitting benchmark suite in Section
5.1
, this bounded-domain PINN experiment uses larger collocation sets and a smaller exponent budget
K=6
for training stability. All settings are listed in Table
8
.

Key takeaways for the single-charge Dirichlet boundary condition (BC) setting.

We compare supervised and full PINN training and report relative
L^{2}
error and Gauss-flux error. RMN attains competitive mean accuracy with a tiny parameter budget and remains stable under physics-only training, while a tuned wide MLP can achieve lower best-case supervised error. Table
9
summarizes the quantitative results.

D.1
Problem Setup and Punctured-Domain Formulation

We consider the 3D Poisson equation on the cube domain
\Omega=[-1,1]^{3}
with
J
point charges:

-\Delta u(\bm{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{J}q_{j}\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{c}_{j}),\bm{x}\in\Omega,

(53)

where
\bm{c}_{j}\in\Omega
are the charge locations and
q_{j}
are the charge magnitudes. The fundamental solution for a single point charge in 3D is the Coulomb potential:

G(\bm{x})=\frac{q}{4\pi\left\|\bm{x}-\bm{c}\right\|}\propto\left\|\bm{x}-\bm{c}\right\|^{-1},

(54)

which exhibits the characteristic
r^{-1}
singularity that RMN is designed to capture through learnable negative exponents.

D.1.1
Boundary Conditions: Dirichlet

We focus on the grounded Dirichlet boundary condition:

u(\bm{x})=0,\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega.

(55)

Other boundary types such as Neumann or mixed conditions can be handled by modifying the boundary loss. For Neumann boundaries, add a gauge constraint. These cases are outside the scope of this additional experiment.

D.1.2
Punctured Domain Formulation

Because the Dirac delta function cannot be directly evaluated in neural network training, we adopt the punctured domain approach. We exclude small balls
B_{\varepsilon}(\bm{c}_{j})
of radius
\varepsilon>0
around each charge center, yielding the punctured domain:

\Omega_{\varepsilon}=\Omega\setminus\bigcup_{j=1}^{J}B_{\varepsilon}(\bm{c}_{j}).

(56)

On this domain, the PDE becomes source-free:

\Delta u(\bm{x})=0,\bm{x}\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}.

(57)

The point charge effect is encoded via Gauss’s law as a flux constraint on each puncture boundary:

\oint_{\partial B_{\varepsilon}(\bm{c}_{j})}\nabla u\cdot\mathbf{n}dS=-q_{j}.

(58)

This formulation is mathematically rigorous and directly applicable to physics-informed neural network training.

D.2
Experimental Protocol

D.2.1
Training Objectives for Supervised and Full PINN

Loss functions: supervised and PINN.

Supervised fitting minimizes MSE to the ground truth solution
u^{\star}
evaluated at training points:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{sup}}=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim\Omega_{\varepsilon}}\left[(u_{\theta}(\bm{x})-u^{\star}(\bm{x}))^{2}\right].

(59)

Full PINN training combines three components:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{pinn}}=\lambda_{\text{PDE}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{PDE}}+\lambda_{\text{BC}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{BC}}+\lambda_{\text{flux}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{flux}}.

(60)

PDE residual loss is

\mathcal{L}_{\text{PDE}}=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim\Omega_{\varepsilon}}\left[(\Delta u_{\theta}(\bm{x}))^{2}\right].

(61)

Boundary condition loss for Dirichlet BC is

\mathcal{L}_{\text{BC}}=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim\partial\Omega}\left[u_{\theta}(\bm{x})^{2}\right].

(62)

Gauss flux loss is

\mathcal{L}_{\text{flux}}=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\left(4\pi\varepsilon^{2}\cdot\frac{1}{N_{\text{sphere}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{sphere}}}\nabla u_{\theta}(\bm{x}_{j,i})\cdot\mathbf{n}_{j,i}+q_{j}\right)^{2},

(63)

where
\{\bm{x}_{j,i}\}
are
N_{\text{sphere}}
points uniformly sampled on
\partial B_{\varepsilon}(\bm{c}_{j})
using the Fibonacci sphere method, and
\mathbf{n}_{j,i}
are the corresponding outward unit normals. We use base loss weights
\lambda_{\text{PDE}}=1
,
\lambda_{\text{BC}}=200
,
\lambda_{\text{flux}}=50
. The high boundary condition weight prioritizes satisfying the boundary early in training.

Two-phase curriculum learning.

To improve convergence, we use a two-phase curriculum during an initial warmup stage, gradually ramping the PDE and flux weights to their base values. For Phase 1 (0–40% of warmup), eeduce PDE weight via a multiplier
s_{\text{PDE}}:0.1\to 1.0
and ramp the flux multiplier
s_{\text{flux}}:0.1\to 0.5
. For Phase 2 (40–100% of warmup), use full PDE weight
s_{\text{PDE}}=1
and increase the flux multiplier to
s_{\text{flux}}:0.5\to 1.0
. After warmup, we fix all weights to their base values. This curriculum encourages the model to establish the boundary behavior before enforcing the interior PDE constraint.

D.2.2
Sampling, Optimization, and Hyperparameters

Sampling strategy.

Our sampling strategy uses stratified sampling with explicit sphere sampling around the singularity. Each training stage uses:

N_{\text{int}}=30{,}000
interior points uniformly sampled in
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
,
N_{\text{bc}}=8{,}000
boundary points uniformly sampled on
\partial\Omega
, and
N_{\text{sphere}}=1{,}500
sphere points per charge sampled on
\partial B_{\varepsilon}(\bm{c}_{j})
.
We resample the training points every 2,500 training iterations for variance reduction.

Optimization.

Training uses the Adam optimizer with cosine annealing:
RMN uses 25,000 steps with learning rate
10^{-2}
and cosine annealing to
10^{-4}
. MLP uses 35,000 steps with learning rate
10^{-3}
and cosine annealing to
10^{-5}
.
For stability, we apply gradient clipping with max norm 1.0, bound exponents to
[-1.0,2.0]
to prevent numerical overflow while covering Coulomb behavior, and resample training points every 2,500 iterations for variance reduction.

Baseline choice.

To reduce the chance that negative results for MLPs are due to under-capacity or under-training, we use a substantially wider network than in the function-fitting benchmarks in Section
5.2
. The MLP has five hidden layers with width 256. We also allocate more optimization steps to MLP, 35,000 instead of 25,000.

D.2.3
Models and Baselines

RMN-MC architecture and wide MLP baseline.

For this experiment, we use RMN-MC in Section
4.6
with a single fixed center,
J=1
:

u_{\theta}(\bm{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\sum_{k=1}^{K}a_{jk}\left\|\bm{x}-\bm{c}_{j}\right\|^{\mu_{k}}+b_{0}.

(64)

The shared learnable exponents
\{\mu_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
lie in
[-1.0,2.0]
and include the critical
\mu=-1
for Coulomb behavior. The per-center coefficients
\{a_{jk}\}
are initialized to approximate
1/(4\pi)\approx 0.08
for the first component. Centers
\bm{c}_{j}
are fixed for the forward problem or learnable for the inverse problem. We use
K=6
exponent terms for sufficient flexibility.

This architecture has only 13 parameters for
J=1
. That count equals
K=6
exponents, six coefficients, and one bias. The baseline MLP with five hidden layers of width 256 has 264,449 parameters. Unlike the function-fitting MLP baseline in Section
5.2
, we use a wider MLP here to better reflect common PINN practice in bounded-domain PDE settings.

Analytical derivatives: for RMN, we use closed-form gradients and Laplacians. For
r_{j}(\bm{x})=\left\|\bm{x}-\bm{c}_{j}\right\|
:

\nabla_{\bm{x}}r_{j}^{\mu}=\mu r_{j}^{\mu-2}(\bm{x}-\bm{c}_{j}),\Delta_{\bm{x}}r_{j}^{\mu}=\mu(\mu+d-2)r_{j}^{\mu-2}.

(65)

Here
d=3
is the spatial dimension.
This improves both computational efficiency and numerical stability compared to automatic differentiation.

D.2.4
Ground Truth and Evaluation Metrics

The analytical solution for the bounded domain problem uses singular-smooth decomposition:

u(\bm{x})=u_{\text{sing}}(\bm{x})+v(\bm{x}),

(66)

where
u_{\text{sing}}(\bm{x})=\sum_{j}\frac{q_{j}}{4\pi\left\|\bm{x}-\bm{c}_{j}\right\|}
is the free-space singular part, and
v(\bm{x})
is a smooth correction satisfying:

\Delta v=0\text{ in }\Omega,v|_{\partial\Omega}=-u_{\text{sing}}|_{\partial\Omega}.

(67)

We compute
v
numerically using finite differences on a refined grid with
N=128^{3}
and trilinear interpolation for point evaluation. This approach ensures accurate ground truth near singularities by handling the singular part analytically.

D.3.3
Discussion

These 3D Poisson experiments emphasize three points:
First, RMN remains stable under full PINN training and achieves low Gauss-flux error, indicating strong physics consistency even without access to
u^{\star}
. Second, in the supervised setting, a tuned wide MLP can achieve lower best-case relative
L^{2}
error, reflecting the non-radial boundary correction induced by Dirichlet conditions. Third, the learned exponent spectrum reflects an interpretable singular-plus-smooth decomposition, with the dominant exponent approaching Coulomb-like behavior while positive exponents capture smooth boundary-induced corrections.
Quantitative results and timing are summarized in Table
9
. Both methods show variance across charge positions; charges closer to boundaries or corners are more challenging.

Methodological contributions.

We use bounded exponent parameterization
\mu\in[-1,2]
for numerical stability, a punctured-domain formulation with Gauss flux constraints, a singular subtraction method for accurate ground truth computation, and analytical derivative formulas for improved numerical stability.

D.3.4
Summary

The 3D Poisson experiments suggest that RMN-MC provides a compact and stable surrogate for bounded-domain point-charge problems, while also highlighting an important limitation: Dirichlet boundaries induce non-radial corrections, so a single-center radial model is not expected to dominate global accuracy without additional mechanisms for boundary adaptation.

Key results for the single-charge Dirichlet BC setting.

Table
9
summarizes the quantitative results. RMN maintains similar mean relative
L^{2}
error across supervised and full PINN training while achieving substantially lower flux error and using far fewer parameters than the wide MLP baseline; the trade-off is that the best-case supervised error is higher than the tuned MLP.

Overall, these results suggest that RMN offers a useful trade-off between accuracy, stability, and interpretability for singularity-dominated physics problems, especially when parameter budgets are tight. In this setting, the
20{,}000\times
parameter reduction comes with comparable mean accuracy and improved stability under our full PINN setup. RMN is therefore particularly attractive for physics-informed learning when ground truth is unavailable, for resource-constrained and real-time applications, and for scientific computing when interpretability is essential.

D.4
Robustness and Interpretability

The observed variability differs substantially between training paradigms. RMN shows consistent std
\approx 10.8\%
in both settings, while MLP std increases from 13.9% in the supervised setting to 173% in the PINN setting. Across seeds, we sample the charge center uniformly from the interior cube
[-1+d_{\min},1-d_{\min}]^{3}
to enforce a minimum distance
d_{\min}
from the boundary, as listed in Table
8
.

Variance sources.

Charge position varies by seed, and charges closer to corners or multiple faces create steeper boundary-induced corrections that are more challenging. RMN local minima arise from the interplay between exponent and coefficient learning, yet the converged solutions remain physically reasonable and satisfy the Gauss flux constraint to about
10^{-5}
. MLP PINN pathologies contribute to large variance under PINN training. This behavior is consistent with known gradient pathologies in PINNs near singularities: competing loss terms and large second-derivative targets can create stiff optimization dynamics and occasional divergence
[

D.4.1
Learned Exponent Analysis: Physical Interpretation

The learned dominant exponent
\mu_{1}
ranges from
-0.70
to
-0.88
across seeds, with mean
-0.80
, approaching but not exactly reaching the free-space Coulomb value
\mu=-1
. Importantly, this deviation is physically meaningful rather than a limitation. The primary cause is the bounded domain effect. The true solution on a bounded domain with Dirichlet BC is:

u(\bm{x})=\underbrace{\frac{q}{4\pi\left\|\bm{x}-\bm{c}\right\|}}_{\text{singular, }\sim r^{-1}}+\underbrace{v(\bm{x})}_{\text{smooth correction}},

(70)

where
v(\bm{x})
enforces
u|_{\partial\Omega}=0
. This correction is not purely radial and depends on the charge position relative to boundaries. Consequently, the optimal single-center radial approximation has an effective exponent
\mu_{\text{eff}}\neq-1
that balances the singular and smooth components. The learned
\mu_{1}\approx-0.80
is the optimal trade-off, not an approximation failure.

A secondary factor is the exponent parameterization. Exponents are parameterized via a smooth transformation mapping to
[-1,2]
with slight bias away from boundary values for numerical stability.

Physical interpretation shows that the learned exponent spectrum reveals the multi-scale solution structure. For seed 0, the spectrum
\{-0.75,-0.44,-0.05,0.45,1.12,2.00\}
illustrates this breakdown. The dominant term with
\mu_{1}\approx-0.75
captures Coulomb-like behavior. The terms with
\mu_{2}
and
\mu_{3}
near
-0.44
and
-0.05
capture additional near-field corrections induced by the bounded domain in an effective radial sense. The positive exponents
\mu_{4}
,
\mu_{5}
, and
\mu_{6}
represent smooth polynomial terms for boundary layer corrections. This interpretable decomposition—unavailable from black-box MLPs—provides direct physical insight into the solution structure and could inform analytical approximation strategies.

D.5
Limitations, Supplementary Studies, and Future Directions

This final subsection combines limitations, supplementary ablations, and future directions for the single-charge Dirichlet setting.

D.5.1
Future Directions

The success of RMN on single-charge Dirichlet problems motivates several extensions:

A multi-charge extension: The RMN-MC architecture naturally generalizes to
J
charges:

u_{\theta}(\bm{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\sum_{k=1}^{K}a_{jk}\left\|\bm{x}-\bm{c}_{j}\right\|^{\mu_{k}}+b_{0},

(71)

with only
JK+K+1
parameters, which scales linearly in
J
. This encodes the superposition principle exactly, with each radial monomial centered at the appropriate charge location.

Mixed boundary conditions: For problems with spatially-varying BC types such as Dirichlet on some faces and Neumann on others, the training framework extends naturally with face-specific loss terms and increased BC weight
\lambda_{\text{BC}}\approx 250
.

Inverse problems: When charge locations are unknown, RMN-MC can jointly learn
\{\bm{c}_{j}\}
alongside other parameters, enabling source identification from boundary measurements.

These extensions are enabled by RMN’s explicit radial structure and analytical derivative formulas, which provide both computational efficiency and physical interpretability.

This setting is more challenging and practically relevant, as ground truth solutions are often unavailable for real-world problems.
