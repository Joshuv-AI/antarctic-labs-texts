Innovations in Thrombectomy Training: A Systematic Review and Expert Recommendations from the Society of Vascular and Interventional Neurology‐Mission Thrombectomy Initiative
Authors: Fawaz Al‐Mufti, Mohamed Elfil, Abdallah Abbas, Haneen Sabet, Hazem S Ghaith, Ariel Sacknovitz, Ankita Jain, Victor Urrutia, Nabeel Herial, Gabor Toth, Mohamad El‐Ghanem, Krishna Amuluru, Viktor C Szeder, Jonathan Crowe, Karol Budohoski, Zurab Nadareishvili, Kaustubh Limaye, Fazeel Siddiqui, Boris Pabon, Ossama Yassin Mansour, Atilla Ozcan Ozdemir, Houman Khosravani, Hamza Shaikh, Nishita Singh, Hesham Masoud, Sushanth R Aroor, Shashvat Desai, Santiago Ortega‐Gutierrez, Fredrick Echols, Thanh N Nguyen, Pankajavalli Ramakrishnan, Priyank Khandelwal, Dileep R Yavagal, Kaiz Asif

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDMechanical thrombectomy is a critical intervention for patients with acute ischemic stroke with large vessel occlusion. However, significant barriers remain in its widespread implementation, particularly in low‐ to middle‐income countries, including a shortage of trained physicians and limited access to advanced medical technologies. This systematic review and meta‐analysis aimed to comprehensively evaluate current mechanical thrombectomy training methodologies and assess their effectiveness in improving procedural skills among neurointerventional teams.METHODSWe conducted a systematic review following Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta‐Analyses guidelines, searching PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science. Eight studies were included, with 3 studies eligible for meta‐analysis. We assessed training approaches, participant demographics, and procedural outcomes using the Risk of Bias in Non‐randomized Studies of Interventions tool and performed statistical analysis using OpenMetaAnalyst software.RESULTSVarious training modalities, including virtual reality simulations and hands‐on workshops, consistently demonstrated positive effects on procedural skills and professional confidence, demonstrating significant improvements across multiple metrics. Our systematic review and meta‐analysis revealed statistically significant reductions in total procedure time (average decrease of 17.84 minutes, 95% CI: [−22.19 to −13.48]), number of handling errors (decreased by 6.34 errors, 95% CI: [−13.16 to 0.48]), contrast volume (decreased by 27.35 mL, 95% CI: [−45.11 to −9.60]), and fluoroscopy time (reduced by 8.07 minutes, 95% CI: [−10.71 to −5.44]). Participants showed increased procedural steps completed, with an average increase of 6.52 steps (95% CI: [3.99–9.05]).CONCLUSIONStructured, simulation‐based mechanical thrombectomy training programs can significantly enhance procedural skills, clinical decision‐making, and professional confidence among neurointerventional teams, potentially improving stroke care.Keywords: mission thrombectomy, stroke, thrombectomy, training, virtual reality

Mechanical thrombectomy (MT) has revolutionized the treatment of acute ischemic stroke, offering a critical intervention for patients with large vessel occlusion. 1 Despite its proven efficacy in improving patient outcomes and reducing disability, significant challenges remain in the widespread adoption and implementation of this life‐saving procedure, particularly in low‐ to middle‐income countries and regions with limited specialized stroke care. 2 , 3

The primary barriers to MT implementation include the shortage of specialty‐trained physicians, limited access to advanced medical technologies, lack of a well‐defined training pathway, and gaps in procedural knowledge and technical skills among healthcare professionals. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 Recognizing these challenges, medical educators have increasingly innovated training approaches to address the crucial need for skilled MT practitioners. Although the clinical value of MT is well established, the educational infrastructure needed to safely scale these services remains underdeveloped. Simulation‐based MT training programs—whether hands‐on workshops or virtual reality (VR) modules—are promising but often expensive, requiring significant investment from health systems, governments, or nonprofit initiatives such as Mission Thrombectomy. Demonstrating their consistent effectiveness across diverse settings is critical to justifying the cost of their deployment.

Although we searched broadly for educational initiatives related to MT training—including workshops, bootcamps, and curricula, all studies that met inclusion criteria incorporated some form of simulation‐based training. These included either hands‐on simulation using flow models or VR‐based training environments. This consistency across diverse programs allowed us to focus our analysis on the core impact of simulation components in MT education.

Understanding which training approaches consistently improve procedural performance not only guides future curriculum design, it also provides a foundation to advocate for sustainable investment in simulation technology. By showing that even heterogeneous, resource‐variable training programs can produce measurable improvements, we support the rationale for VR simulator acquisition, stipends for workshops, and mobile training units.

This systematic review and meta‐analysis aim to evaluate the effectiveness of simulation‐based MT training in improving procedural skills, confidence, and clinical outcomes among neurointerventional teams. By synthesizing current evidence, we aim to identify best practices and support the case for expanded implementation of simulation‐based training in stroke systems of care.

Search and Eligibility Criteria

PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science were queried from inception until November 27, 2024 using the following search query: ((“Stroke” OR “Ischemic Stroke” OR “Brain Ischemia” OR “Middle Cerebral Artery Infarction” OR “Cerebrovascular Event”) AND (“Mechanical Thrombectomy” OR “Endovascular Treatment” OR “Endovascular Therapy” OR “Neurointerventional Therapy”)) AND (“Simulation” OR “Training” OR “Simulation‐Based Training” OR “Simulat*” OR “Educat*” OR “Cours*” OR “Workshop*” OR “Bootcamp*” OR “Boot‐Camp” OR “Learn*” OR “Experienc*” OR “Curricul*” OR “Framework*” OR “Teach*” OR “Guid*” OR “Coach*” OR “Supervis*” OR “Seminar*” OR “Lectur*” OR “Round*” OR “Tutor*” OR “Mentor*” OR “Program*” OR “Training*” OR “Event*”).

Our inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) study design–all published studies and abstracts from conferences; (2) participants–all eligible (postgraduate) healthcare professionals engaged in clinical practice or training who were involved in MT for treating ischemic stroke; (3) intervention–any type of simulation training related to MT in the management of ischemic stroke; (4) comparisons–no interventions or absence of simulation training (for example, ongoing postgraduate education without any simulation, or no modifications to training programs); and (5) outcomes–we did not define a specific outcome for including studies to ensure a thorough synthesis of evidence concerning simulation training for MT.

Screening and Data Extraction

Data extraction was performed independently by 2 reviewers using a standardized form, with discrepancies resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. The screening process was conducted using Rayyan software in 2 stages: the first stage involved reviewing titles and abstracts, and the second stage focused on full‐text screening. For data extraction, we used spreadsheets 4 to compile information on the following aspects:

Risk of Bias Assessment

The risk of bias was assessed for each included study using the Risk of Bias in Non‐randomized Studies of Interventions tool, 10 evaluating potential biases across 7 key domains. These domains included bias due to confounding (D1), bias in the selection of participants (D2), bias in the classification of interventions (D3), bias due to deviations from intended interventions (D4), bias due to missing data (D5), bias in the measurement of outcomes (D6), and bias in the selection of the reported result (D7). Each domain was rated as having a low, moderate, serious, or critical risk of bias or as having no information when insufficient details were available. The overall risk of bias for each study was determined based on the highest level of risk identified in any domain, following the Risk of Bias in Non‐randomized Studies of Interventions guidance. For instance, a study with a serious risk of bias in 1 domain would receive an overall rating of serious, regardless of its ratings in other domains. This approach ensures that the overall assessment reflects the potential for bias to affect the study's results.

Statistical Analysis

The analysis was conducted using OpenMetaAnalyst software. 11 The pooled mean difference (MD) was evaluated with 95% CIs. This MD represents the data collected before the workshop minus the data collected after the workshop. A correlation coefficient value of 0.2 was used to calculate the change from the baseline using the Meta‐Analysis Accelerator tool, 12 as we were unable to calculate the exact correlation coefficient based on the provided data in the studies, and 0.2 was considered the minimum clinically important correlation. 13

Heterogeneity was assessed using the chi‐square test and the I‐squared statistic (I2). The data were deemed heterogeneous if the P value was <0.1 or I2 was >50%. In cases of heterogeneity, a random‐effects model rather than a fixed‐effects mode was applied. Additionally, a leave‐one‐out analysis was performed to address heterogeneity. Because the number of studies included in the analysis was <10, publication bias could not be assessed. 8

Part 2: Development of Expert Recommendations

Following the evidence synthesis, members of the Society of Vascular and Interventional Neurology‐Mission Thrombectomy initiative conducted structured discussions to identify key takeaways. Expert consensus was reached through iterative feedback among clinicians, researchers, and training program leaders via a series of virtual meetings and written correspondence. Recommendations were grouped into evidence‐supported and consensus‐based categories.

RESULTS

Part 1: Findings from the Systematic Review and Meta‐Analysis

Search and Screening Process

A total of 1721 articles were identified from 3 databases. After removing duplicates, the number was reduced to 1081. Following title and abstract screening, 75 articles remained. Of the 75 full‐text articles assessed for eligibility, 67 were excluded for reasons such as not involving simulation‐based MT training, lack of procedural outcomes, duplicate data from the same cohort, or insufficient methodological detail for analysis. Ultimately, 8 studies were included in our research, 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 with 3 15 , 20 , 21 providing sufficient data to be incorporated into a meta‐analysis (Figure 1).

Summary of Included Studies

The included studies provide insights into geographical locations, participant demographics, and training methods (Table 1). For instance, the training spans different countries, including Jamaica, Germany, Norway, and the United States. The number of participants varies significantly, from small groups n = 5 in Paech et al 19 to larger cohorts n = 99 in Inoa et al. 12 Trainee classifications range from practicing neurointerventionalists and residents to medical students and technicians, emphasizing diverse target groups. The type of training also exhibits variability, incorporating hybrid simulations, VR training, and hands‐on workshops, often supplemented with assessments or skill evaluations. Training duration varies, with some reporting hours (22 hours in Inoa et al 12 ) and others extending to months (5 months in Sovik et al 11 and Schneider et al 10 ).

Risk of Bias Assessment

The majority of studies demonstrated a low risk of bias across most domains (Supplementary Figure 1). However, the most common serious risk of bias was found in the D1 category (bias due to confounding), specifically in Inoa et al 12 and Sovik et al. 11 Additionally, certain studies, such as Schneider et al 10 and Dardick et al, 9 had domains marked with “no information,” particularly in D1 (bias due to confounding), D2 (bias due to the selection of participants), and D3 (bias in the classification of interventions) (Supplementary Figure 1). Notably, a single moderate risk was observed in D6 (bias in the measurement of outcomes) in Dardick et al. 9

Regarding the overall risk of bias, Inoa et al 12 and Sovik et al 11 were rated as having a serious overall risk of bias due to multiple high‐risk domains. In contrast, Schneider et al 10 and Dardick et al 9 had unclear overall risks, primarily because of missing information in certain domains. Other studies, including Paech et al, 19 Patchana et al, 20 and Crossley et al, 8 exhibited a low overall risk of bias.

Total time of procedure from beginning to the end (minutes)

The first case following the workshop demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in time, with an average decrease of 17.84 minutes compared with the time recorded before the workshop (MD: −17.84, 95% CI: [−22.193 to −13.484]). In the second case, this reduction was slightly less, at an average decrease of 13.36 minutes (MD: −13.36 to 95% CI: [−16.26 to −10.46]) (Figure 2). Additionally, 1 study reported the total time for 2 cases after the workshop, which revealed a statistically significant reduction of 2.24 minutes compared with pre‐workshop times (MD: −2.24, 95% CI: [−3.34 to −1.39]) (Figure 2). A fixed‐effect model was applied because the data were homogeneous throughout the analysis.

Total time (in minutes) from the beginning to the end of the procedure. I2 indicates I‐squared statistic; and NA, not applicable.

Number of steps completed

The first case following the workshop showed a statistically significant increase in the number of steps completed, with an increase of 6.52 steps compared with the period recorded before the workshop (MD: 6.52, 95% CI: [3.99–9.05]) (Figure 3). This increase continued in the second case, where the number of steps rose slightly to 9.59 steps (MD: 9.59, 95% CI: [6.11–13.08]). A fixed‐effect model was applied due to the homogeneity of the data.

Steps completed (in numbers) analysis. I2 indicates I‐squared statistic.

Number of handling errors

The first case following the workshop indicated a statistically insignificant reduction in the number of handled errors, showing a decrease of 6.34 errors compared with the period before the workshop (MD: −6.34, 95% CI: [−13.16 to 0.48]) (Figure 4). In the second case, the reduction was slightly greater and statistically significant, with a decrease of 10.73 handled errors (MD: −10.73, 95% CI: [−19.83 to −1.64]). Data were homogenous, so a fixed‐effect model was used.

Handling errors (in numbers) analysis. I2 indicates I‐squared statistic.

Contrast volume (milliliters)

The first case following the workshop demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in contrast volume, with a decrease of 27.35 mL compared with the time recorded before the workshop (MD: −27.35, 95% CI: [−45.11 to −9.60]) (Figure 5). In the second case, the reduction was even greater, amounting to 36.01 mL (MD: −36.01, 95% CI: [−56.84 to −15.19]). A fixed‐effect model was applied due to data homogeneity.

Contrast volume (in millimeters) analysis. I2 indicates I‐squared statistic.

Fluoroscopy time (minutes)

The first case following the workshop demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in fluoroscopy time, decreasing by 8.07 minutes compared with the time recorded before the workshop (MD: −8.07, 95% CI: [−10.71 to −5.44]) (Figure 6). In the second case, the reduction was slightly less, at 7.26 minutes (MD: −7.26, 95% CI: [−9.43 to −5.09]). The data were homogeneous, and a fixed‐effect model was applied.

Fluoroscopy time (in minutes) analysis. I2 indicates I‐squared statistic.

Total time to complete a thrombectomy

Training significantly improved procedural efficiency in several studies. Sovik and colleagues 21 demonstrated substantial reductions in time, with pretest means of 33.5 and 29.4 minutes for 2 cases decreasing to 17.1 and 16.4 minutes, respectively, after training. Similarly, Schneider et al 20 reported that the total time to complete thrombectomy was significantly reduced from 39.4 minutes to 18.4 minutes in case 1 and from 31.5 to 17.2 minutes in case 2. Patchana et al 18 provided detailed posttest times for 3 trials, ranging from 1027 to 2200 seconds, but no pretest comparisons were available. Dardick et al 15 showed a mean reduction of 2.24 minutes, and Crossley et al 14 reported a posttest mean time of 1833.60 seconds, focusing more on the construct validity of VR training than on time reduction. Overall, these findings indicate that training, particularly using VR, can improve efficiency in completing thrombectomy procedures.

Steps completed during the MT procedure after training

The studies revealed improvements in the number of steps completed following MT training. Sovik et al 21 reported increases in steps completed, with pretest means of 25 and 27.2 steps improving to 31.4 and 35.8 steps, respectively, after training. Schneider et al 20 similarly observed an increase, with pretest values of 25.8 and 25.6 steps rising to 32.6 and 38.4 steps. Patchana et al 18 documented posttest values of 27.3, 35, and 33 steps across 3 trials, showing consistent procedural execution over time. Crossley et al 14 endorsed these findings with a posttest mean of 30.7 steps, highlighting the benefit of VR training in enhancing procedural completion rates.

Handling errors

Handling errors, another key outcome, showed mixed results across studies. Sovik and colleagues 21 reported significant reductions in handling errors, with pretest means of 33.1 and 35.1 errors dropping to 22.2 and 17.8 errors, respectively. However, Schneider et al 20 did not observe significant changes, with pretest errors of 25.9 and 23.4 only slightly decreasing to 23.6 and 20.3 errors. Patchana et al 18 documented high error counts across trials, ranging from 86 to 124 errors, indicating room for improvement despite training. 18 Crossley and colleagues 14 observed fewer errors among experienced users, suggesting that VR training is effective in reducing errors for skilled participants.

Contrast volume used

Contrast volume used during procedures also improved with training. Sovik et al 21 noted reductions in contrast volume, with pretest values of 79.7 and 82.7 mL decreasing to 57.2 and 51.1 mL, respectively. Schneider et al 20 similarly reported significant reductions, with pretest volumes of 109.6 and 109.8 mL dropping to 75.1 and 63.5 mL. Crossley et al 14 recorded a posttest mean of 42.45 mL, indicating that training fosters more efficient use of contrast.

Fluoroscopy time

Fluoroscopy time showed significant reductions in studies with pre‐ and posttraining comparisons. Sovik and colleagues 21 found that fluoroscopy time decreased from 14.6 and 14.3 minutes pretest to 7.0 and 7.2 minutes posttest. Similarly, Schneider and colleagues 20 observed decreases from 16.6 and 15.3 minutes to 7.4 and 7.6 minutes, respectively. In contrast, Patchana et al 18 reported prolonged times across 3 trials, ranging from 454 to 1388.6 seconds, reflecting variation in training impact. Crossley and colleagues 14 observed a posttest mean of 826.8 seconds, focusing more on realism and skill validation than on time reduction.

After completing the systematic review and meta‐analysis, the authors developed a set of recommendations based on the findings. These were drafted collaboratively by the study team and refined through group discussions over email. Recommendations were categorized as either evidence based (supported by the included studies) or expert driven (based on the authors’ clinical experience and judgment).

DISCUSSION

The emerging educational landscape for MT training emphasizes several core principles: (1) comprehensive, structured training approaches; (2) integration of advanced simulation technologies; (3) multidisciplinary team‐based learning; (4) continuous performance assessment and feedback; and (5) cultural and linguistic adaptability.

MT is now the gold standard treatment for patients with acute ischemic stroke‐large vessel occlusion who meet certain eligibility criteria. 22 There is an increasing need for well‐trained neurointerventionalists to ensure the availability of MT as a treatment modality, especially in areas with high volumes of stroke. 23 Given the critical nature of MT, researchers have explored various training methodologies to optimize skill acquisition. 24

Neurointerventional workshops focused on MT procedure have been implemented as a training strategy aiming to enhance the procedural skills of the trainees and boost their confidence level to ultimately improve the patient outcome in the clinical setting. 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 19 , 20 , 21 However, there was a wide variability of approaches in the studies that investigated the impact of MT workshops on the trainees’ efficiency and procedural skills. For instance, the VR approach was used by a few studies included in this systematic review. 14 , 15 , 20 , 21 However, other studies relied on the use of flow models using vascular manikins. 16 , 19 Further variability was observed in the pre‐ and postworkshop assessment. For example, although there were studies such as the one conducted by Crossley and colleagues, 14 which was purely focused on the technical aspect of the MT procedure from the vascular access to the safe retrieval of the used device(s) and closure of the access site, there were other studies such as the one conducted by Inoa et al 16 that had a wider approach to assess not only the impact of the simulation training on the procedural skills but also targeted the knowledge aspect including stroke epidemiology, clinical and imaging selection of cases for MT, post‐MT care in the intensive care unit, and stroke systems of care. This methodological diversity underscores the need for a standardized approach to MT training that can be consistently implemented across different healthcare settings.

The variability in methodological approaches highlights both the emerging nature of MT training and the potential for ongoing improvement in educational strategies. Although the studies reviewed demonstrate promising outcomes across different training modalities—including VR simulations and flow models—they collectively underscore the critical need for more standardized, comprehensive training programs. The consistent positive effects observed across studies suggest that structured, simulation‐based workshops have significant potential to enhance neurointerventional skills, improve patient outcomes, and address the growing demand for skilled MT practitioners, particularly in regions with high stroke incidence. As the field continues to evolve, there is a clear imperative to develop more refined, evidence‐based training methodologies that can be consistently implemented across diverse healthcare settings.

Importantly, the wide variability in structure, participant demographics, training duration, and assessment methods raises both challenges and opportunities for generalizability. On one hand, heterogeneity can limit direct comparisons and reduce clarity on which components are essential. On the other hand, the fact that significant improvements were observed across diverse settings suggests that simulation‐based training is robust, adaptable, and effective across a range of contexts. This flexibility enhances the applicability of findings to real‐world scenarios—particularly in low‐ and middle‐income countries or resource‐limited regions where training programs must be tailored to local needs.

For future educational course design, this variability reinforces the value of creating modular, scalable programs that can be customized while still maintaining core components—such as procedural simulation, stepwise feedback, and interdisciplinary learning. Duration and complexity may vary, but foundational elements (eg, hands‐on practice, structured assessment) should be prioritized across all programs. Where possible, future studies should aim to identify which training elements yield the greatest educational benefit, to help streamline and standardize curricula over time.

Although most performance metrics showed consistent improvements, the reduction in handling errors was not observed across all studies. This inconsistency may be related to variability in training methodology. For instance, studies that relied solely on brief workshops or flow‐model‐based sessions without structured feedback may not have provided sufficient opportunities for error recognition and correction. In contrast, studies using VR simulations with real‐time performance metrics and iterative learning cycles appeared to show greater reductions in handling errors. This suggests that training format and feedback mechanisms may play a key role in procedural error mitigation—a hypothesis worth exploring in future comparative studies.

STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS

The main strength of the study is that it provides a comprehensive systematic review and meta‐analysis of the published literature on the effectiveness of training of MT assessed using different metrics. The primary limitations of our study are the limited number of studies and variability in training approaches, which underscore the need for standardized training protocols. Also, there was a lack of detailed description of the simulation cases included in these training workshops. Furthermore, due to the unavailability of the granular data from the included studies, we were unable to assess if the trainee's background (neurosurgery, neurology, or radiology) had an impact on the outcomes of the simulation training. Although the studies included trainees with varying backgrounds, most participants were nonexpert users, and granular data on prior experience were not consistently reported. Therefore, although our findings support improved procedural performance among trainees, further studies are needed to determine the impact of simulation‐based training on experienced practitioners and to validate whether these interventions effectively fill gaps in current advanced training pathways.

FUTURE DIRECTIONS

Future research should focus on longitudinal studies to assess the sustained impact of training and explore the integration of emerging technologies like augmented reality. Despite the heterogeneity observed amongst the studies in terms of their methodology and aims of conducting MT workshops, there is a consistent trend of positive effects of such training sessions on knowledge acquisition, procedural skills, teamwork, and professional confidence. Based on these findings, the following strategic recommendations are proposed to further optimize the impact of MT workshops:

Comprehensive Skill Development: Future MT workshops should expand their scope to comprehensively assess and improve clinical and imaging decision‐making skills. This approach would enhance trainees' ability to critically evaluate patient selection criteria, interpret imaging studies, and make nuanced decisions about MT intervention.

CONCLUSION

This systematic review demonstrated the critical value of comprehensive, simulation‐based training for MT. The available evidence demonstrated that simulation‐based educational interventions can significantly enhance procedural skills, clinical decision‐making, and professional confidence among neurointerventional teams.

Importantly, the measurable impact observed across multiple performance metrics—despite variation in training formats and locations—provides strong justification for the adoption of simulation‐based training programs globally, including in low‐ and middle‐income countries. Our findings support the case for funding high‐cost simulation tools such as VR systems and mobile workshop programs, which may be subsidized by governments, simulator developers, or initiatives such as Mission Thrombectomy.

What distinguishes this work is not only the synthesis of limited but promising evidence but also the collaborative interpretation and response by global leaders in MT. Based on the data, this expert group developed a set of consensus‐driven, actionable recommendations for future MT training curricula. These include strategies for structuring workshops, building progressive learning pathways, integrating team‐based training, and setting standardized evaluation metrics.

By combining quantitative evidence with expert insights, this work offers both proof of benefit and a blueprint for implementation. As the field of neurointervention continues to advance, ongoing research and refinement of training strategies will be essential to optimize patient outcomes and expand access to this life‐saving intervention.
