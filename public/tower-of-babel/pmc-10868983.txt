Familial Gigantic Melanocytosis: A Report of Rare Case
Authors: Veena U. Nair, Meghana Patil, Jayashree Basavaraju, Chandramohan Kudligi, Sharanabasava M. Choukimath
Published: 2023

A 10-year-old boy born out of second-degree consanguineous marriage presented to us with four-year history of gradual onset pigmentation of the skin and silvery grey hair. The pigmentation was not preceded by burning sensation, itching or redness. There was no history of photosensitivity or history suggestive of recurrent infection, bleeding tendency or seizures. Growth and development milestones were normal. None of his family members including his elder brother had similar complaints. Systemic examination and growth indices were unremarkable. Cutaneous examination revealed diffuse hyperpigmentation covering face, upper limbs, upper chest and upper back with interspersed multiple discrete hypopigmented macules [Figure 1]. Palms, soles, nails and mucosae were uninvolved. Complete haemogram, liver function tests and renal function tests were normal. Peripheral blood smear examination showed normochromic and normocytic RBCs. Ultrasonography of the abdomen showed no obvious abnormality. Microscopic examination of the hair shaft showed pigment clumps arranged linearly in the medulla of the hair shaft [Figure 2].

Histopathological examination revealed richly pigmented basal and suprabasal keratinocytes with numerous huge melanocytes, which were longer and larger than the normal melanocytes in the basal layer of the epidermis [Figures 3 and 4]. Fontana-Masson stain showed large melanocytes in the basal layer and basophilic degeneration of collagen fibres with entrapped adnexal structures [Figure 5]. Though various differential diagnosis like Griscelli syndrome, Chediak–Higashi syndrome, Elejalde syndrome, Dyschromatosis universalis hereditaria and familial gigantic melanocytosis were considered, history clinical examination and pathognomonic histopathological features narrowed our diagnosis to familial gigantic melanocytosis.

Discussion

Familial gigantic melanocytosis (FGM) or familial melanopathy is a rare and peculiar familial disorder of pigmentation first described in 1984. It has a very characteristic clinical features and almost pathognomonic histopathology.[1] It presents with diffuse brown hyperpigmentation studded with multiple interspersed rain drop hypopigmentation affecting predominantly photo-exposed area like face, neck, chest, upper arms and sometime trunk. These lesions are neither associated nor preceded by any constitutional or local symptoms like photosensitivity, itching or redness. Affected skin is normal in texture, and scalp hair is always lighter than the unaffected family members. Palms, soles, nails and mucosae remain unaffected. Routine blood investigations, endocrinal assessment, physical, mental and sexual development lie within the normal range. Familial gigantic melanocytosis may clinically mimic disorders such as Griscelli syndrome, Chediak–Higashi syndrome and Elejalde syndrome for its light coloured hair and other conditions like lichen planus pigmentosus, Dyschromatosis universalis hereditaria and reticulate acropigmentation of Kitamura for its pigmentary changes[2] [Table 1]. However, histopathology helps in clinching the correct diagnosis by revealing the hallmark feature of this disorder, which is the presence of melanin filled large melanocytes at the base of the epidermis and the follicular walls. Heavily pigmented keratinocytes are seen in hyperpigmented areas but hypopigmented areas show less melanin in the keratinocytes and fewer giant melanocytes. On electron microscopic examination, melanocytes in both hyperpigmented and hypopigmented areas have large melanosomes, large irregular nuclei with fragmented chromatin and a wide nuclear envelope.[3] The cause of this disorder is unknown; though it is hypothesized that in genetically predisposed individuals, there is a defect in the transfer of melanosomes from the melanocytes to the surrounding keratinocytes. This results in the formation of giant, highly melanized melanosomes and paucity of melanin in the surrounding areas, causing hypopigmented macules to be interspersed within the hyperpigmented areas.[2]

The present case is being reported for its rarity since only nine cases have been reported in the literature [Table 2]. It is also being reported to create awareness about this seemingly benign disorder, which can easily be diagnosed by simple histopathological examination avoiding unnecessary investigations to rule out similar looking conditions like Griscelli syndrome, Chediak–Higashi syndrome and Elejalde syndrome, which are often associated with underlying grave haematological and neurological disorders.
