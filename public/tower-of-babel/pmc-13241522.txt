Z-nucleic acid-mediated PANoptosis in infection, inflammation, and cancer
Authors: Nisha Thapa, Aro Kim, Paritosh Patel, Minh Quan Nguyen, Ein Lee, Rajendra Karki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNucleic acids serve not only as carriers of genetic information but also as potent immunological signals that shape innate and adaptive immune responses. Their immunogenicity is determined by their origin, subcellular localization, structural conformation, and the host sensors that detect them. Within this landscape, Z-nucleic acids, including Z-RNA and Z-DNA, have emerged from biological noise to key regulators of immune activation, particularly through their ability to induce inflammatory cell death pathways. Both host and pathogens can generate Z-nucleic acids, yet the mechanisms governing their formation and regulation remain incompletely understood. In mammals, ZBP1 and ADAR1 are the only known Z-nucleic acid sensors, while some pathogens encode Z-binding proteins to evade detection. Although critical for host defense, dysregulated Z-nucleic acid signaling can drive autoimmunity and chronic inflammation. This review synthesizes current structural and functional principles of Z-nucleic acid recognition and highlights emerging therapeutic opportunities across infectious diseases, inflammatory disorders, and cancer.Subject terms: Immunology, Infection, Immune cell death, Sepsis

Introduction

Nucleic acids (RNA and DNA) are universally recognized as the carriers of genetic information; however, they also serve as important immunological signals. Beyond their canonical roles in transcription and protein synthesis, nucleic acids are continuously scrutinized by the innate immune system as indicators of infection, cellular stress, or tissue damage1,2. Their immunogenicity is shaped by origin, subcellular localization, and structural conformation3,4. Pathogen-derived nucleic acids—including viral double-stranded RNA (dsRNA), single-stranded RNA (ssRNA), 5′-triphosphate RNA, and unmethylated CpG DNA act as classical pathogen-associated molecular patterns5. Self-derived nucleic acids are normally confined to the nucleus or mitochondria. Mislocalized mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), nuclear DNA, or extracellular DNA released from stressed or dying cells function as damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) that drive sterile inflammation3,6,7.

Nucleic acids adopt distinct helical conformations characterized by their chirality. Right-handed helices—such as B-DNA and A-RNA—comprise the predominant physiological structures, whereas left-handed Z-DNA and Z-RNA arise under specific sequence and physicochemical conditions8,9 (Table 1). These conformations critically influence recognition by innate immune receptors distributed across endosomal, cytosolic, and nuclear compartments. Endosomal toll-like receptors TLR3, TLR7/8, and TLR9 detect dsRNA, ssRNA, and CpG DNA, respectively. Cytosolic RNA sensors—including retinoic acid-inducible gene I (RIG-I), melanoma differentiation-associated protein 5 (MDA5), protein kinase R (PKR), and adenosine deaminase acting on RNA 1 (ADAR1)—recognize diverse RNA species. Cytosolic DNA is sensed primarily by cyclic GMP–AMP synthase (cGAS)-stimulator of interferon (IFN) genes (STING), absent in melanoma (AIM2), and IFN-gamma inducible protein 16 (IFI16)10–12. In contrast, Z-RNA and Z-DNA are uniquely recognized by the Z-binding domains of ZBP1 and ADAR1. Engagement of these receptors activates transcriptional programs driven by nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) and IFN regulatory factors (IRFs), leading to type I IFN production, inflammatory cytokine release, inflammasome activation, and induction of programmed cell death10,12,13. Notably, ZBP1 sensing of Z-nucleic acids initiates inflammatory cell death, whereas ADAR1 acts as a critical suppressor of ZBP1-driven responses14–16.

Here, we organize the review around (i) nucleic acid conformations and their immune sensors, (ii) generation and regulation of Z-RNA and Z-DNA, (iii) Z-nucleic acid binding to ZBP1 and ADAR1, (iv) regulation of inflammatory cell death by ZBP1 and ADAR1, and (v) significance of ZBP1 and ADAR1 in infection, inflammation, and cancer.

Nucleic acid conformations and their immune sensors

Nucleic acid helices can be broadly classified by their helical chirality as right-handed (B-DNA, A-DNA, and A-RNA) or left-handed (Z-DNA, Z-RNA) forms8,9. B-DNA, the canonical right-handed duplex, is the principal ligand for DNA sensors, such as cGAS-STING, AIM2, IFI16, and TLR9. A-RNA, the predominant conformation of dsRNA, serves as the primary ligand for RIG-I, MDA5, TLR3, ADAR1, and PKR17–20 (Table 1). In contrast, A-DNA—which arises under non-physiological conditions or within RNA:DNA hybrids—lacks a dedicated sensor but can be variably recognized by cGAS, TLR9, and occasionally by RIG-I or MDA5, reflecting functional overlap among nucleic acid sensors. Left-handed Z-DNA and Z-RNA represent structurally and immunologically distinct helices that are selectively recognized by the Z-nucleic acid-binding domains of ZBP1 and ADAR121 (Table 1). Collectively, sensor specificity is determined not only by nucleic acid sequence and length but also by conformation.

Generation and regulation of Z-nucleic acids (Z-DNA and Z-RNA)

Z-nucleic acids have base pairs alternate between syn (purines) and anti (pyrimidines) conformations, producing a distinctive zigzag sugar-phosphate backbone stabilized by hydration networks and cation interactions22. The formation and stability of Z-DNA and Z-RNA are governed by an interplay of torsional stress, sequence composition, ionic conditions, polyamine availability, chemical modifications, and protein binding (Fig. 1).

Formation and regulation of Z-RNA and Z-DNA.

Left: viral proteins, such as NS1 and ICP27, cause disruption of transcription termination (DoTT) by interfering with host mRNA 3′-end processing factors, particularly the cleavage and polyadenylation specificity factor (CPSF) complex, thereby blocking proper mRNA cleavage and termination. Small molecules, such as JTE-607, similarly target CPSF. This DoTT leads to the formation of endogenous retroelement (ERE)-derived transcripts that form double-stranded RNA structures with an intrinsic propensity to adopt the Z-RNA conformation. Loss of ADAR1 leads to elevated type I interferon (IFN-I) production; in this IFN-rich environment, increased expression of IFN-stimulated gene (ISG) mRNAs that lack sufficient ADAR1-mediated editing further promotes Z-RNA accumulation. In addition, oxidative stress activates JNK signaling, which suppresses ADAR1 expression, thereby amplifying Z-RNA formation. Center: DNA-binding proteins, such as MeCP2, Zscan4, and ZBTB43, promote Z-to-B DNA reversion, thereby limiting Z-DNA persistence and downstream signaling. Right: Z-DNA preferentially forms at GC-rich repeat sequences (GC)n under conditions of negative supercoiling and topological stress. Replication stress, DNA damage, and RNA polymerase II pausing promote Z-DNA formation, whereas topoisomerases I and II, helicases, and RNase H1 resolve Z-DNA and associated R-loop structures to maintain genomic stability.

Negative supercoiling is a principal physiological driver of Z-DNA formation. Transcription-induced torsional stress, particularly behind elongating RNA polymerase, promotes the B-to-Z transition. DNA topology is tightly regulated by topoisomerases, which dynamically modulate the generation, relaxation, and redistribution of supercoils, thereby influencing Z-DNA formation. Topoisomerase I relaxes torsional stress through transient single-strand cleavage and rotational diffusion, thereby dissipating the negative superhelical density that thermodynamically favors Z-DNA formation23. In contrast, topoisomerase II resolves DNA entanglements via ATP-dependent double-strand passage. Within topologically constrained chromatin domains, this activity can redistribute rather than fully relax supercoiling, allowing residual negative superhelical density to persist and creating local conditions permissive to B-to-Z transitions24,25. Beyond its catalytic role, topoisomerase II exhibits a markedly higher affinity for Z-DNA relative to B-DNA. This preference has been attributed to a phylogenetically conserved winged-helix domain (Zτ) that is structurally analogous to the canonical Zα Z-DNA recognition motif26.

In addition to topology, DNA sequence is a critical determinant: GC-rich regions and alternating purine-pyrimidine repeats, such as (CA)n or (GC)n, are classical Z-DNA-forming elements27–31. Protein interactions further shape Z-DNA formation and function by either facilitating or constraining access to Z-prone sequences. Methyl-CpG-binding protein 2 (MeCP2), originally characterized as binding methylated cytosine residues within cytosine-guanine (CG) dinucleotides, has subsequently been shown to bind methylated CH (mCH) [where H is adenine (A), cytosine (C), or thymine (T)], hydroxymethylated CA (hmCA), and methylated or hydroxymethylated CAC sequences32. CA repeats are repetitive microsatellite DNA elements, and alternating (CA)n or (CG)n repeats possess an intrinsic capacity to adopt Z-DNA conformations33.

MeCP2 suppresses Z-DNA formation at CA-repeat loci through two mechanistically linked activities. By specifically recognizing hydroxymethylated CA repeats, MeCP2 maintains these regions in a nucleosome-free state, directly competing with nucleosome assembly and limiting the remodeling-associated torsional stress required for B-to-Z transitions32. Concurrently, MeCP2 promotes large-scale chromatin compaction through regions distinct from its methyl-CpG binding domain, further restraining the torsional dynamics that favor non-B conformations34,35. Loss of MeCP2 disrupts both activities—liberating CA-repeat sequences for nucleosome invasion and permitting transcription-coupled torsional stress32—creating conditions mechanistically sufficient for Z-DNA emergence36. Although direct evidence of increased Z-DNA at these loci in MeCP2-deficient cells has yet to be demonstrated, MeCP2-mediated nucleosome exclusion and chromatin compaction together constitute an indirect but coherent mechanism for suppressing Z-DNA formation at microsatellite loci in neuronal genomes.

Similarly, zinc finger and SCAN domain-containing protein 4 (Zscan4) binds nucleosomal (CA)n microsatellite repeats and protects fragile genomic regions from DNA damage during early embryogenesis, a developmental window characterized by elevated transcriptional activity and genomic stress37. While Zscan4 has not been shown to directly modulate Z-DNA formation, its association with Z-prone repetitive sequences and its role in maintaining genome integrity suggest that it may suppress the accumulation of non-B DNA structures, including Z-DNA, at vulnerable loci. In contrast to proteins that indirectly limit Z-DNA formation, certain factors actively resolve Z-DNA once it is formed. The transcription factor zinc finger and BTB domain-containing protein 43 (ZBTB43) directly recognizes Z-DNA and promotes its reversion to B-DNA conformation, thereby preventing DNA double-strand breaks. By removing Z-DNA, ZBTB43 also facilitates de novo DNA methylation at CG-containing purine–pyrimidine repeats, safeguarding germline genomic and epigenomic integrity38 (Fig. 1).

Biophysical and chemical factors also strongly influence Z-DNA stability. In vitro, high salt concentrations favor Z-DNA by stabilizing syn-anti alternation, while chemical base modifications—including bromination of guanine at C8 or cytosine at C5—can induce Z-conformation even under low-salt conditions39. Polyamines, such as spermine and spermidine, enhance Z-DNA stability by neutralizing the negatively charged phosphate backbone. Accordingly, spermidine/spermine N1-acetyltransferase (SAT1)-mediated polyamine catabolism limits intracellular polyamine pools and restricts Z-DNA accumulation40. Oxidative stress represents an additional endogenous trigger of Z-nucleic acid formation41,42, although the precise molecular mechanisms remain incompletely defined. Notably, amyloid-β-induced oxidative stress promotes mtDNA oxidation and drives a B-to-Z transition43, linking Z-DNA formation to neurodegenerative pathology. Certain chemotherapeutic drugs and polyamines, including spermine and spermidine, undergo metabolism to produce toxic metabolites, like acrolein, which triggers Z-nucleic acid formation via oxidative stress42.

Beyond DNA, RNA can also adopt the Z-conformation, particularly under conditions of torsional stress or during viral replication. Viral proteins, such as influenza A virus NS1 and herpes simplex virus-1 ICP27, disrupt transcription termination by binding cleavage and polyadenylation specificity factors (CPSF), inducing disruption of transcription termination (DoTT)44. DoTT impairs mRNA 3′-end processing, nuclear export, and translation, leading to the accumulation of aberrant host RNAs enriched in inverted repeats and endogenous retroelements (ERE) sequences capable of forming dsRNA and Z-RNA structures44. Certain small molecules, such as JTE-607, can similarly induce DoTT, thereby promoting cellular Z-nucleic acid formation44 (Fig. 1). Immunoprecipitation experiments using anti-Z-nucleic acid antibodies have identified that viral RNAs mapping to the ORF1a and ORF1b regions of the SARS-CoV-2 genome are particularly enriched in the pool of Z-RNA-forming species45. The formation of Z-RNA from these regions may be related to the high levels of RNA synthesis and the potential for topological stress generation during viral genome replication within double-membrane vesicles. Similarly, genomic analyses of SARS-CoV-2 have identified Z-prone sequences, particularly within the Nsp13 helicase region, where the energy generated by processive helicase and polymerase activity may drive conformational flipping of local RNA into the Z-form, providing a mechanistic basis for Z-RNA generation during coronavirus replication46. A similar principle is observed in vaccinia virus infection, where the E3 protein plays a central role in modulating Z-RNA formation. The C-terminal dsRNA-binding domain of E3 associates with A-form dsRNA and facilitates the emergence of Z-RNA structures, likely by stabilizing RNA duplexes and promoting conformational strain that favors the A-to-Z transition. Accordingly, Z-RNA accumulation during vaccinia infection is transcription-dependent and detectable early in the cytoplasm, where vaccinia virus replicates47. Beyond viral systems, endogenous Z-RNA formation is tightly linked to IFN signaling and RNA editing pathways. Z-forming sequences are particularly abundant in the 3′ untranslated regions of mRNAs encoding IFN-stimulated genes (ISGs), which are extensively edited by ADAR148. c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) signaling augments ISG transcription and promotes Z-RNA formation49. Loss or mutation of ADAR1 results in pronounced Z-RNA accumulation14,48; this phenotype is suppressed by IFN-α and β receptor subunit 1(IFNAR1) neutralization, or mitochondrial antiviral signaling protein (MAVS) ablation, implicating RIG-I-like receptor-driven type I IFN signaling in Z-RNA generation48. ADAR1 localization and abundance further modulate Z-RNA levels: the nuclear export inhibitor KPT-330 sequesters ADAR1p150 in the nucleus14, whereas alcohol suppresses ADAR1 protein expression49, both leading to increased cellular Z-RNA accumulation (Fig. 1).

In summary, Z-nucleic acids arise through a tightly regulated, context-dependent interplay of sequence composition, torsional and transcriptional stress, chemical and oxidative modifications, polyamine metabolism, RNA processing pathways, and specialized protein recognition.

Binding of Z-nucleic acid to ZBP1

ZBP1 is encoded on human chromosome 20q13.31 and produces a 429-amino acid protein (~46.3 kDa), with the highest expression in the small intestine and lymphoid tissues50. In mice, Zbp1 is predominantly expressed in the lung, spleen, and liver, sharing ~47% sequence identity with human ZBP151. Both species express long (ZBP1-L) and short (ZBP1-S) isoforms52,53. Structurally, ZBP1 contains two N-terminal Zα domains (Zα1 and Zα2), which bind Z-DNA and Z-RNA, followed by tandem receptor-interacting protein homotypic interaction motifs (RHIMs) and a C-terminal signaling domain (Fig. 2). ZBP1-S contains only the two Zα domains and lacks the RHIMs, rendering it signaling-deficient but capable of ligand binding53. Although Zα1 and Zα2 share conserved folds, their binding modes differ. Zα1 uses both the α3 helix and β-loop to recognize Z-DNA, whereas Zα2 relies primarily on the α3 helix due to a shorter β-loop54. The ZBP1 Zα1 domain shares ~35% amino acid identity with the ADAR1 Zα domain, with slightly lower conservation in Zα215. Binding to Z-nucleic acids is mediated by three conserved residues—Y145, N141, and W162—shared across Zα family proteins. The Zα domains of ZBP1 and ADAR1 recognize Z-nucleic acids (Fig. 2). Upon binding Z-nucleic acids, ZBP1 nucleates the assembly of a multiprotein signaling complex composed of receptor-interacting serine/threonine protein kinase 3 (RIPK3), cysteine-aspartic acid-specific protease (CASP8/6), and apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing caspase recruitment domain (ASC)15,55. ZBP1 associates with RIPK3 via homotypic RHIM–RHIM interactions, resulting in RIPK3 activation and subsequent phosphorylation and oligomerization of mixed lineage kinase domain-like protein (MLKL), which executes necroptosis. In parallel, ZBP1-mediated activation of CASP8 promotes the cleavage and activation of effector caspases CASP3 and CASP7, driving apoptotic cell death. In immune cells, ZBP1 signaling additionally promotes NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome activation, leading to gasdermin D (GSDMD) cleavage, pyroptotic cell death, and the maturation and release of the inflammasome-dependent cytokines interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) and interleukin-18 (IL-18)56–58. Collectively, these pathways converge on a coordinated inflammatory cell death program termed PANoptosis59,60, in which ZBP1 serves as a central upstream sensor, integrating molecular features of pyroptosis, apoptosis, and necroptosis15,56,61,62 (Fig. 2).

In contrast, ZBP1 contains two Zα domains and a RIP homotypic interaction motif (RHIM), which together enable Z-nucleic acid sensing and downstream PANoptotic signaling.

ZBP1 in infection

ZBP1-mediated inflammatory cell death has been implicated in various infectious diseases.

Influenza A virus (IAV)

During IAV infection, type I IFNs strongly induce ZBP1, which recruits RIPK3, CASP8, CASP6, and RIPK1 to assemble the ZBP1-PANoptosome and execute PANoptosis55 (Fig. 3). Genetic studies demonstrate that only combined inhibition of pyroptosis, apoptosis, and necroptosis pathways (Casp1−/−Casp8−/−Ripk3−/− macrophages) blocks IAV-induced cell death, establishing ZBP1 as the upstream integrator55,63. The short isoform ZBP1-S negatively regulates this pathway as its overexpression reduces IAV-induced cell death53 (Fig. 2). ZBP1 induction requires IFNAR1 and IRF1, explaining the resistance of Ifnar1−/− and Irf1−/− cells to IAV-triggered death64. In immune cells, both TLR and RIG-I signaling pathways drive ZBP1 expression, whereas in nonimmune cells, RIG-I alone suffices65 (Fig. 3). Most studies support a protective role for ZBP1 in IAV infection56,66–68; however, some report similar susceptibility of wild-type and Zbp1−/− mice66,68. These discrepancies are largely attributable to differences in IAV challenge dose and route of infection between studies69,70. With respect to route, intranasal challenge renders Zbp1−/− mice more susceptible to IAV compared to heterozygous controls, while intratracheal administration of the same dose results in reduced mortality in Zbp1−/− mice71. This dichotomy likely reflects the distinct cellular targets engaged by each route. Intranasal inoculation predominantly infects upper airway epithelia, where ZBP1-driven PANoptosis facilitates antiviral clearance; whereas intratracheal delivery directly seeds alveolar epithelia, where ZBP1-dependent cell death instead drives fulminant immunopathology, neutrophil recruitment, and DAMP release66. Viral dose introduces an additional layer of complexity. Both ZBP1 and RIPK3 contribute to host protection at sublethal doses, whereas at higher infectious doses, the same molecules may drive immunopathology and increased lethality69,70. Nonetheless, there is a consensus that ZBP1 recruits RIPK3 through RHIM–RHIM interactions during IAV infection. MLKL deficiency does not protect against IAV66,72. Furthermore, CASP8 knock-in (Casp8DA) mice-harboring a mutation that prevents CASP8 autoprocessing required for apoptosis, while preserving its essential survival function during development, remain susceptible72. These findings indicate that ZBP1 can reroute cell death through multiple alternative pathways.

Assembly of ZBP1 PANoptosome during viral infection.

RNA viruses, including influenza A virus (IAV), and DNA viruses, such as herpes simplex virus 1 (HSV-1) are sensed by RIG-I and cGAS, respectively, leading to activation of IRF3 and IRF7 and subsequent production of type I interferons (IFNs). IFNs signal in both autocrine and paracrine manner through IFNAR, activating the ISGF3 complex and inducing IRF1, which transcriptionally upregulates Zbp1. Viral proteins from both IAV and HSV-1 cause disruption of transcription termination (DoTT), promoting the formation of Z-RNA. Z-RNA is sensed by the Zα domains of ZBP1, triggering ZBP1 activation and RHIM-dependent interaction with RIPK3. This interaction promotes recruitment of additional signaling components, including CASP8 and inflammasome-associated proteins (NLRP3, ASC, and CASP1), with CASP6 facilitating ZBP1–RIPK3 complex assembly. The resulting multiprotein complex, termed the ZBP1 PANoptosome, coordinates activation of pyroptosis, apoptosis, and necroptosis (PANoptosis) and is essential for effective host defense against IAV and HSV-1 infection.

Coronaviruses (SARS-CoV-2, MHV)

Progressive coronavirus disease 19 (COVID-19) is associated with increased ZBP1 expression in immune cells, suggesting a pathological role of ZBP156. In mice, Zbp1 deletion reduces PANoptosis, cytokine storm, and lethality after coronavirus infection. ZBP1 loss suppresses production of tumor necrosis factor (TNF) and IFN-γ, key cytokines driving PANoptosis and COVID-19 pathogenesis56. Importantly, ZBP1 deficiency improves survival during combined TNF/IFN-γ challenge, but not TNF alone, implicating ZBP1 specifically mediates IFN-γ-driven inflammation73.

Herpes simplex virus-1 (HSV-1)

In macrophages, HSV-1 triggers assembly of a ZBP1- and pyrin-dependent AIM2–PANoptosome. Zbp1−/−Pyrin−/− mice phenocopy Aim2−/− mice with higher viral titers, indicating that ZBP1 coordinates with pyrin to mediate protective AIM2 responses74. While the HSV-1 genome contains sequences capable of forming Z-DNA, host cells also produce Z-RNA as a result of DoTT caused by HSV-1 ICP27 (Fig. 1). Interestingly, host-derived Z-RNA is required for optimal ZBP1-mediated cell death as a host defense mechanism44.

Cytomegalovirus (CMV)

Murine CMV encodes the RHIM protein M45, which competes with RIPK3 for ZBP1 binding, thereby blocking ZBP1–RIPK3 complex formation and suppressing cell death75.

Vaccinia virus (VACV)

VACV and variola virus encode E3L, a Zα domain protein that binds viral Z-nucleic acids and prevents ZBP1 activation. Loss of E3L or its Zα domain leads to Z-nucleic acid accumulation and robust ZBP1-mediated cell death in IFN-β-primed cells47,76.

Zika virus (ZIKV)

Zbp1−/− mice exhibit increased paralysis and mortality following ZIKV infection. Zbp1−/− neuronal cultures show higher viral replication in a cell-death-independent, metabolism-driven mechanism involving immune responsive gene 1 induction and itaconate production77.

West Nile virus (WNV)

Zbp1−/− mice show increased morbidity and mortality, and neuronal viral loads, indicating impaired viral clearance following WNV infection78.

Collectively, these studies establish ZBP1 as a central sensor-effector that integrates IFN signaling and Z-nucleic acid detection to orchestrate context-dependent inflammatory cell death during infection.

ZBP1 in sterile inflammation

Dysregulated activation of ZBP1 drives diverse forms of sterile inflammation, including cytokine release syndromes, neuroinflammation, inflammatory liver disease, and photosensitivity-associated skin inflammation. These pathologies arise from inappropriate sensing of endogenous Z-nucleic acids and subsequent engagement of inflammatory cell death and cytokine production pathways.

Systemic inflammation and cytokine storm

The clinical term cytokine storm, or cytokine release syndrome, refers to a spectrum of inflammatory states perpetuated by a self-amplifying feedback loop between cytokine signaling and programmed cell death pathways, culminating in life-threatening tissue and organ damage79,80. ZBP1 is a central molecular driver of this process through its Zα domains, which detect Z-nucleic acids, and its RHIM, which enables assembly of cell death-inducing signaling complexes. ZBP1 interacts via RHIM–RHIM interactions with other mammalian proteins, including TRIF, RIPK1, and RIPK3, thereby promoting necroptosis, apoptosis-associated inflammation, and cytokine release. Among these regulators, RIPK1 plays a critical scaffolding role in suppressing excessive inflammation by restraining ZBP1- and RIPK3-mediated cell death. Consistent with this function, postnatal lethality in Ripk1-deficient mice is characterized by systemic inflammation, severe anemia, cytopenia, and neutrophilia81,82—hallmarks of cytokine storm—and is rescued by combined deletion of apoptotic caspase-8 and necroptotic RIPK381,82, underscoring the cooperative contribution of apoptosis and necroptosis to disease pathogenesis. In contrast, mice harboring RHIM-mutant RIPK1 (Ripk1mRHIM) die perinatally due to unchecked ZBP1-dependent necroptosis. Unlike complete RIPK1 deficiency, in which TNF-driven apoptosis and ZBP1-mediated necroptosis both contribute to lethality, the RHIM mutation selectively disrupts RIPK1’s ability to restrain ZBP1, thereby isolating a dominant ZBP1-driven necroptotic pathway. Accordingly, perinatal lethality in Ripk1mRHIM mice is prevented by deletion of RIPK3, MLKL, or ZBP1, demonstrating that spontaneous activation of the ZBP1–RIPK3–MLKL necroptosis axis underlies disease. Mechanistically, in cells expressing RHIM-mutant RIPK1, ZBP1 exhibits enhanced interaction with phosphorylated RIPK3, indicating that the RIPK1 RHIM normally functions to prevent aberrant ZBP1–RIPK3 engagement83,84. Human genetic studies further support this model. Individuals with heterozygous missense mutations in RIPK1 (D324N, D324H, D324V, and D324Y), which prevent caspase-mediated cleavage of RIPK1, develop early-onset periodic fever syndromes accompanied by severe episodic lymphadenopathy. These phenotypes are attributed to impaired caspase-8-dependent cleavage of RIPK1, rendering cells hypersensitive to TNF-induced RIPK3-dependent apoptosis and necroptosis, thereby disrupting inflammatory homeostasis85,86. In parallel, mice with epidermis-specific deletion of RIPK1 develop progressive, ZBP1-dependent skin pathology. In RIPK1-deficient keratinocytes, ZBP1 is upregulated and accompanied by accumulation of endogenous Z-nucleic acids, which drives necroptosis and IL-17–mediated inflammatory responses87. Under homeostatic conditions, the RIPK1-Fas-associated death domain (FADD)-CASP8 axis restrains ZBP1 activation by suppressing spontaneous cell death signaling. Accordingly, loss of FADD or CASP8 in intestinal epithelial cells triggers ZBP1-dependent necroptosis and results in severe inflammatory colitis88. SET domain bifurcated histone lysine methyltransferase 1 (SETDB1), an endogenous histone methyltransferase that primarily mediates gene silencing via trimethylation of histone H3 at lysine 9 (H3K9me3)89, has been reported to suppress spontaneous ZBP1 activation by limiting the availability of immunostimulatory Z-nucleic acids. SETDB1 represses endogenous retroelements and repetitive sequences; its loss in intestinal epithelium leads to ZBP1-mediated inflammation90.

Neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration

ZBP1-mediated inflammation contributes to the progression of neurodegenerative diseases independently of its canonical cell death function. In the 5×FAD mouse model, Aβ-induced oxidative stress damages mtDNA, driving its fragmentation and release into the cytoplasm, where oxidized mtDNA undergoes a conformational transition into Z-DNA that is recognized by ZBP1 in microglia. Genetic deletion of Zbp1 or pharmacological inhibition of RIPK1 attenuates neuroinflammation, reduces Aβ pathology, and improves behavioral outcomes in this model, establishing the ZBP1–RIPK1 axis as a pathogenic driver of microglia-mediated neurodegeneration and a potential therapeutic target in AD. ZBP1 responds to endogenous Z-DNA, engaging RIPK1 kinase-dependent transcription of pro-inflammatory molecules and signaling mediators spanning the type I IFN, TLR, and NLRP3 inflammasome pathways43.

Photosensitivity-driven skin inflammation (SLE and CLE)

ZBP1 expression is markedly upregulated in the epidermis of both adult and pediatric patients with autoimmune photosensitivity. In lupus patient-derived keratinocytes, ultraviolet B (UVB) exposure leads to extensive accumulation of cytosolic Z-DNA. This results from UVB-induced oxidative stress, which promotes 8-oxo-dG formation in mtDNA, favoring the B-to-Z transition. Functionally, ZBP1 is essential for amplifying this response. ZBP1 inhibition abrogates UVB-induced IFN production, whereas ZBP1 overexpression drives spontaneous Z-DNA accumulation and a lupus-like IFN signature even without UV stress91. Together, these findings establish Z-DNA and ZBP1 as key mediators of UVB-induced cutaneous inflammation and demonstrate how chronic IFN-rich environments—characteristic of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and cutaneous lupus erythematosus (CLE)-lower the threshold for photosensitivity and fuel a self-amplifying skin inflammation.

Liver inflammation

Accumulating evidence indicates that ZBP1 promotes liver pathology by engaging multiple regulated cell-death pathways49,92,93. Transcriptomic analyses of livers from patients with alcoholic liver disease (ALD) reveal enrichment of hallmark inflammatory and IFN response pathways compared with healthy controls49. Consistent with this inflammatory milieu, biomarkers of pyroptosis, apoptosis, and necroptosis are markedly elevated in ALD livers. Similarly, ZBP1 expression is significantly increased in hepatocytes from both human and mouse steatotic livers relative to normal livers49,92. Notably, ZBP1 levels are higher in livers from ALD patients than in either healthy subjects or patients with non-ALD93. Genetic ablation of Zbp1 confers protection against both hepatic ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) injury and ALD in mouse models, underscoring a pathogenic role for ZBP1 in distinct forms of liver injury49,92,93.

Despite this shared requirement for ZBP1, the upstream activation mechanisms and downstream cell-death programs differ substantially between steatotic liver I/R injury and ALD. In ALD, ZBP1-mediated liver injury critically depends on its ability to sense Z-nucleic acids49,93, whereas Z-nucleic acids sensing is dispensable for ZBP1-driven liver I/R injury92. During ALD, cooperative signaling between alcohol and IFNs promotes Z-RNA accumulation by suppressing ADAR1 protein expression while increasing ISG transcripts capable of adopting the Z-RNA conformation49. Recognition of Z-RNA by ZBP1 triggers the coordinated activation of pyroptosis, apoptosis, and necroptosis, collectively driving hepatocyte death and liver inflammation49,93. Importantly, inhibition of any single cell-death pathway is insufficient to prevent liver injury or disease progression in ALD, highlighting the redundancy and integration of ZBP1-driven death programs49,93. In contrast, during liver I/R injury—such as that occurring during transplantation—ZBP1 is activated through a Z-nucleic acid–independent mechanism. Excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated during I/R induce ZBP1 aggregation, leading to its activation92. Activated ZBP1 promotes the recruitment and activation of RIPK1, thereby enhancing both caspase-8-dependent apoptosis and apoptosis-independent inflammatory signaling, ultimately exacerbating liver damage92.

Collectively, ZBP1 functions as a context-specific amplifier of liver injury, coupling Z-RNA sensing to inflammatory cell death in ALD and ROS-driven signaling to apoptosis during ischemia/reperfusion.

ZBP1 in cancer

The role of ZBP1 in cancer is highly context-dependent, varying with tumor type and stage. Necroptosis, a downstream effect of ZBP1, can suppress tumor growth and enhance anti-tumor immunity by releasing danger signals and neoantigens94. Consistent with this, high ZBP1 expression correlates with increased lymphocyte infiltration48 and a favorable prognosis in melanoma95 and triple-negative breast cancer96. ZBP1-mediated cell death limits tumorigenesis in mouse models of colorectal cancer and melanoma14. In contrast, higher ZBP1 expression associates with poor prognosis in low-grade glioma95, and in breast cancer models, ZBP1-driven cell death can promote metastasis97. Recent studies provide mechanistic insight into these opposing outcomes. ZBP1 participates in the replicative crisis, a tumor-suppressive barrier activated by dysfunctional telomeres98. In this setting, telomeric repeat–containing RNA (TERRA) transcripts are sensed by a ZBP1 splice variant lacking Zα1 (ZBP1-S), which engages MAVS to trigger type I IFN responses and autophagy-dependent cell death99. ZBP1-S thus safeguards against tumor initiation. In contrast, in multiple myeloma, ZBP1 cooperates with IRF3 and IRF4 to induce cell-cycle genes, promoting the proliferation of malignant cells100. Interestingly, a shorter ZBP1 isoform resembling ZBP1-S is detected in these cells, suggesting isoform-specific functions99,100. ZBP1 function in cancer is also shaped by therapeutic interventions. Small molecules such as curaxin (CBL0137) and KPT-330 induce Z-nucleic acids and activate ZBP1-dependent cell death, synergizing with immune checkpoint blockade or IFNs in melanoma models14,48.

Overall, ZBP1 functions as a double-edged regulator in cancer, acting as a tumor suppressor or a tumor promoter depending on the cellular context.

Binding of Z-nucleic acid to ADAR1

ADAR1 prevents aberrant innate immune activation. Structurally, the protein contains a catalytic deaminase domain followed by three double-stranded RNA-binding domains (dsRBD). Notably, the third dsRBD harbors a nuclear localization sequence (NLS)101,102 (Fig. 2). There are two isoforms of ADAR1, ADAR1p110 and ADAR1p150. While both isoforms contain a Zβ domain, the p150 isoform contains the Zα domain, which has a nuclear export sequence (NES)103 (Fig. 2). Due to the presence of both NES and NLS, ADAR1p150 shuttles between the nucleus and cytoplasm. ADAR1p110 is constitutively expressed in the nucleus, but ADAR1p150 is induced by IFNs102,104. Three conserved residues, asparagine 173 (N173) and tyrosine 177 (Y177) in the α helix and tryptophan 195 (W195) in the β sheet of the human ADAR1 Zα domain, play central roles in the interaction with Z-nucleic acids, and a W195A substitution causes a complete loss of binding to Z-nucleic acids54.

ADAR1 in infection

Influenza A virus (IAV)

ADAR1 exerts isoform-specific and opposing effects during IAV infection. The cytoplasmic ADAR1p150 isoform dampens sustained RIG-I-like receptor signaling, thereby supporting efficient viral replication and demonstrating proviral activity105. In contrast, reduced expression of the nuclear ADAR1p110 isoform enhances viral replication, indicating an antiviral role105. Evidence suggests that ADAR1p110 edits viral RNAs within the nucleus to suppress infection106, whereas ADAR1p150 promotes infection by suppressing host innate immune signaling.

Hepatitis B virus (HBV)

ADAR1 regulates HBV replication in a context-dependent manner. In hepatocyte-derived Huh7 cells, both p110 and p150 isoforms suppress HBV RNA accumulation by enhancing the processing of miR-122, a liver-specific microRNA with antiviral activity107. This antiviral effect is editing-independent, as both wild-type ADAR1 and a catalytically inactive mutant (E912Q) similarly reduce HBV RNA levels. By contrast, in hepatocellular carcinoma cells, ADAR1 overexpression enhances HBV DNA synthesis in a deaminase-dependent manner106,107. Together, these findings highlight a dual, cell-type-dependent role of ADAR1 in HBV infection.

Human immunodeficiency viruses (HIV)

ADAR1 acts as a proviral factor in HIV-1 replication via editing-dependent and editing-independent pathways108,109. Overexpression of wild-type ADAR1—but not catalytic mutants—increases capsid protein expression and virion production, while knockdown decreases viral protein synthesis106,108. The p150 isoform promotes viral protein expression through its dsRNA-binding domains, even when catalytically inactive. ADAR1 also interacts with the HIV-1 structural protein p55 Gag110, supporting roles in viral assembly and translation.

Poxviruses

Poxviruses impair ADAR1 activity through viral proteins, such as E3L, which contains a Z domain and an R domain. The carboxy-terminal R domain is essential for inhibiting ADAR1, likely by sequestering RNA substrates106,111. Z-DNA binding by the N-terminal E3L is tightly linked to viral pathogenicity. Mutations that reduce Z-DNA binding of E3L decrease VACV virulence112.

Other viral infections

ADAR1 displays a proviral activity with vesicular stomatitis virus, chikungunya virus, Venezuelan equine encephalitis virus, yellow fever virus, dengue, and Zika virus. ADAR1 has antiviral properties against the Ebola virus106.

ADAR1 in sterile inflammation

Autoinflammation

ADAR1 deficiency leads to profound inflammatory phenotypes characterized by spontaneous cytokine production, including type I IFN. In humans, loss-of-function mutations or dominant-negative variants of ADAR1 cause Aicardi–Goutières syndrome (AGS), a severe encephalopathy characterized by chronic IFN-driven inflammation113. ADAR1 mutations have also been linked to bilateral striatal necrosis and dyschromatosis symmetrica hereditaria114. In mice, global ADAR1-deficiency leads to embryonic lethality, with embryos displaying strong IFN induction before resorption. Deletion of the ADAR1p150 isoform yields similarly severe autoinflammatory phenotypes, underscoring the critical role of p150 in limiting IFN production and maintaining homeostasis115. The autoinflammation in the absence of ADAR1 can be explained by the accumulation of unedited, cellular dsRNAs, which aberrantly activate innate dsRNA sensors, including MDA5, PKR, oligoadenylate synthases, and ZBP1116,117. These pathways converge on IFN production and inflammatory cell death, providing insights into the mechanisms underlying ADAR1 dysfunction-associated immunopathology. Some AGS patients carry compound heterozygous ADAR1 mutations: one allele with a Zα-domain variant (P193A, corresponding to P195A in mice) and the other with a deaminase-domain mutation118,119. In mice, combining Zα-domain mutations with loss of ADAR1 or ADAR1p150 causes early postnatal lethality and severe inflammation, which is largely rescued by ZBP1 deletion or disruption of its Zα domains. Mechanistically, Z-RNAs accumulated in ADAR1-deficient cells activate ZBP1, thereby triggering cell death and inflammation14,120. For details on how ADAR1 regulates ZBP1, refer to the section “Regulation of ADAR1 and ZBP1.”

Neuroinflammation

ADAR1 contributes to central nervous system homeostasis and has been implicated in several neurological disorders, including schizophrenia, suicidal depression, Alzheimer’s disease, and Parkinson’s disease121. Analyses of human brain tissue and primary astrocyte cultures show that ADAR1 expression declines with age and is reduced in Alzheimer’s disease122. Both ADAR1 isoforms, ADAR1P150 and ADAR1P110, are upregulated in astrocytes following middle cerebral artery occlusion. In experimental stroke models, Adar1+/− mice, which are viable and exhibit reduced ADAR1 expression and activity, show improved neurobehavioral outcomes compared to wild-type controls. These benefits correlate with reduced activation of glial cells123.

ADAR1 in cancer

ADAR1 influences cancer development and therapeutic responses through its diverse functions in adenosine-to-inosine (A-to-I) RNA editing, regulation of endogenous RNA abundance, and modulation of innate immune signaling. ADAR1 can either promote or suppress tumorigenesis depending on the context.

ADAR1 promotes malignant progression in several cancers by editing transcripts that regulate proliferation and survival. A prominent example is the A-to-I editing of antizyme inhibitor 1 (AZIN1), which produces a hyperactive protein that binds antizyme more efficiently, preventing degradation of polyamine biosynthetic enzymes and thereby enhancing tumor growth124,125. In T cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia, ADAR1 sustains leukemic-initiating cells by editing and sequestering endogenous dsRNAs, preventing activation of cytoplasmic RNA sensors, such as MDA5 and PKR126. Loss of ADAR1 derepresses these pathways, triggering PKR-dependent cell death and impairing leukemia maintenance. ADAR1 is frequently upregulated in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), where it promotes proliferation, survival, and progression to advanced disease stages. ADAR1 deficiency sensitizes HCC cells to oxidative stress, leading to increased ROS accumulation, elevated kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (Keap1), suppression of the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) antioxidant pathway, and induction of apoptosis127. Clinically, high ADAR1 expression correlates with poor prognosis and more advanced tumor stages127. ADAR1 is increasingly recognized as a barrier to anti-tumor immunity. Genetic or pharmacologic inhibition of ADAR1 enhances tumor immunogenicity by activating the MDA5–PKR axis, increasing inflammatory signaling, promoting immune-cell infiltration, and sensitizing tumors to cytotoxic T cell-mediated killing128. Notably, ADAR1 loss synergizes with immune checkpoint blockade, offering a strategy to overcome resistance to programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1)—or cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4)-targeted therapies128.

Regulation of ADAR1 and ZBP1

Various factors tightly regulate ADAR1 and ZBP1 to maintain homeostasis and prevent pathological inflammation. ZBP1 activity is modulated by tripartite motif-containing 34 (TRIM34). TRIM34 interacts with ZBP1 and promotes its K63-linked polyubiquitination, enhancing the ability of ZBP1 to recruit RIPK3 and initiate necroptosis (Fig. 2). During IAV infection, TRIM34 amplifies ZBP1-mediated programmed cell death, restricting viral replication while preventing excessive inflammation. ZBP1 detects viral RNA via its Zα2 domain and recruits TRIM34 through its N-terminal region. TRIM34 also augments NF-κB-dependent inflammatory signaling, facilitating nuclear translocation and secretion of IL-6 and TNF-α. Knockdown of TRIM34 reduces these effects in a ZBP1-dependent manner, positioning TRIM34 as a positive regulator upstream of ZBP1129. Although many IFN-stimulated genes, including ZBP1, require signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 (STAT1), ADAR1p150 induction remains intact in STAT1-deficient cells, whereas STAT2-deficient or janus kinase 1 (JAK1)-deficient cells show impaired expression, confirming STAT2–JAK1 dependency for ADAR1 expression130,131.

Despite both proteins binding to Z-nucleic acids, ZBP1 and ADAR1 exert antagonistic effects. In ADAR1 deficiency, Alu-derived dsRNAs accumulate in Z-RNA conformations, activating ZBP1 and triggering caspase-8-mediated apoptosis and MLKL-driven necroptosis132,133. Genetic studies reinforce this interplay: AdarZα/− Zbp1+/+ mice die within 2 days due to intestinal necrosis, whereas ZBP1-mutant AdarZα/− mice survive up to 18 weeks, highlighting ZBP1 as the driver of pathology115. Cells lacking ADAR1 exhibit heightened susceptibility to IFN-β and nuclear export inhibitors, with increased ZBP1 expression and cell death. Inhibiting ADAR1 nuclear export using KPT-330 triggers activation of apoptotic caspases, necroptotic MLKL phosphorylation, and pyroptotic pathways through caspase-1 cleavage and GSDME activation. These findings demonstrate that ADAR1 suppresses multiple forms of programmed cell death, whereas ZBP1 activation in the absence of ADAR1 drives uncontrolled apoptosis, necroptosis, and pyroptosis14.

Summary and future directions

Z-nucleic acids represent a distinct class of immunologically active nucleic acid structures. Their generation is tightly linked to transcriptional stress, repetitive genomic elements, oxidative damage, aberrant RNA processing, and viral infection, placing them at the intersection of host defense and cellular stress responses. This review highlights how Z-nucleic acids are selectively sensed by two evolutionarily conserved host proteins—ZBP1 and ADAR1—which together form a regulatory axis controlling immune activation and cell fate. ZBP1 acts as a bona fide innate immune sensor that translates Z-nucleic acid recognition into the assembly of PANoptosomes, thereby integrating pyroptosis, apoptosis, and necroptosis into a unified inflammatory cell death program. In contrast, ADAR1 functions predominantly as a suppressor of aberrant Z-nucleic acid signaling by editing and masking endogenous RNAs, preventing inappropriate activation of ZBP1 and other RNA sensors. Z-nucleic acid signaling is implicated across diverse pathological contexts. During viral infection, ZBP1-mediated responses can limit viral replication but may also promote immunopathology. In sterile inflammatory diseases, endogenous Z-nucleic acids act as danger signals that drive chronic inflammation when regulatory mechanisms fail. In cancer, ZBP1 and ADAR1 exhibit context-dependent roles, functioning either as tumor suppressors through immunogenic cell death or as facilitators of tumor growth and immune evasion. Together, these findings establish nucleic acid conformation as a fundamental determinant of immunogenicity and position the Z-nucleic acid–ZBP1–ADAR1 axis as a unifying framework linking structure, innate sensing, and disease.

Despite rapid advances, several key questions in Z-nucleic acid biology remain unresolved. The cellular sources, kinetics, and spatial organization of Z-RNA and Z-DNA formation in vivo are still incompletely defined, underscoring the need for high-resolution, conformation-specific probes and imaging tools. In addition, the molecular determinants that bias ZBP1 activation toward protective immunity versus pathological inflammation remain unclear, including the roles of cell type, cytokine milieu, ZBP1 isoforms, and downstream signaling pathways. Although isoform-specific functions of ZBP1 and ADAR1 are emerging, largely from mouse studies, their relevance in human physiology and disease is poorly understood. Moreover, how Z-nucleic acid signaling integrates with other classical nucleic acid-sensing pathways, such as cGAS-STING, RIG-I–MDA5, and inflammasomes, requires further investigation. Finally, while targeting this pathway holds therapeutic promise in cancer, infection, and inflammatory disease, a deeper mechanistic understanding will be essential to harness its benefits without provoking excessive inflammation.
