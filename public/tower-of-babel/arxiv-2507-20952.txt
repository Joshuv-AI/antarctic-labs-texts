Abstract
This paper presents a comprehensive mathematical model to characterize the energy dynamics of batteryless IoT sensor nodes powered entirely by ambient energy harvesting. The model captures both the energy harvesting and consumption phases, explicitly incorporating power management tasks to enable precise estimation of device behavior across diverse environmental conditions. The proposed model is applicable to a wide range of IoT devices and supports intelligent power management units designed to maximize harvested energy under fluctuating environmental conditions. We validated our model against a prototype batteryless IoT node, conducting experiments under three distinct illumination scenarios. Results show a strong correlation between analytical and measured supercapacitor voltage profiles, confirming the proposed model’s accuracy.

I
Introduction

The large-scale deployment of
IoT
(
IoT
) devices is a fundamental requirement to allow accurate and ubiquitous sensing and monitoring in diverse environments
[
1
]
. However, as the number of devices deployed increases, so does the number of batteries that contain hazardous substances, limiting the sustainability score of
IoT
networks and introducing environmental concerns
[
2
]
. To address this, low-power, batteryless IoT sensors that harvest ambient energy reduce battery dependence and environmental impact.

Batteryless
IoT
nodes rely on energy harvested from ambient sources such as
RF
(
RF
), light, thermal gradients, and kinetic motion
[
3
]
. These nodes leverage
PM
(
PM
) units with highly efficient DC/DC conversion and
MPPT
(
MPPT
) to make efficient use of the energy harvested from the environment
[
4
]
. In large-scale IoT deployments, it is essential to ensure that batteryless
IoT
sensor nodes are carefully designed and optimized before production, which is crucial to maximize energy efficiency and minimize costs
[
2
,
5
]
. This key step in the development of batteryless nodes involves the analysis of ambient energy dynamics in the environment and the detailed understanding of the node’s energy behavior. This knowledge enables accurate performance evaluation across diverse environments and operating conditions
[
3
]
.

Recent works have highlighted the importance of mathematical modeling of energy dynamics in
IoT
devices to optimize energy management and ensure reliable operation under intermittent and varying energy conditions. Focusing on NB-IoT, studies have proposed analytical
[
6
]
and stochastic
[
7
]
models that capture device behaviors and energy consumption patterns, addressing critical aspects such as power-saving mechanisms (eDRX, PSM), idle-state transitions, and uplink transmissions. Validated through experiments and NS-3 simulations, these models support battery sizing, lifetime prediction, and parameter tuning, all crucial for energy-efficient
IoT
deployments. Although these efforts focused on battery-powered nodes, they offer valuable insights and underscore the importance of research into energy modeling for
IoT
.

For batteryless
IoT
nodes employing low-power communication methods like
BLE
(
BLE
) and LoRaWAN, analytical modeling has evolved to address the fluctuating nature of energy sources. Sabovic et al.
[
8
]
modeled the energy harvesting and management circuit as a current generator, incorporating sensing and communication costs while exploring the effects of sensing frequency and capacitor sizing. Their work, assuming static energy sources and lacking advanced power management, was extended by Delgado et al.
[
9
]
. The latter formalized three capacitor-based electrical models, including parasitic effects (ESR, EPR), to predict task completion without power failure. However, their model did not account for real-world dynamic energy use for sensing or complex power management.
More recently, probabilistic and diffusion-based models
[
10
]
have been proposed to better capture non-exponential energy arrivals and consumption patterns, improving predictions of depletion events and system uptime.
Furthermore, studies on light-powered
IoT
nodes
[
11
]
have emphasized the incorporation of empirical irradiation data to optimize harvester sizing and minimize outages.

Together, the state-of-the-art highlights the importance of accurate energy modeling of
IoT
devices within the energy constraints of real-life deployments. However, existing models overlook power management, which is critical to optimizing the operation of batteryless
IoT
nodes and aligning models with practical deployments. This gap drives our work to develop an analytical model that integrates energy management into harvesting and supply processes.

Our contribution expands existing research by introducing a comprehensive mathematical model for batteryless
IoT
sensor nodes powered by dynamic ambient energy. We begin with an in-depth analysis of the
EHU
(
EHU
) (comprising the ambient
EH
(
EH
),
PM
module, and a
SCap
(
SCap
)-based energy buffer) and subsequently model the energy dynamics of the
CL
(
CL
). These components are integrated into a unified model that captures the interactions between active and sleep cycles, enabling simulation of energy flow and voltage variations in the energy buffer. This model is generalizable to a wide range of batteryless systems, enabling energy profiling and performance optimization before physical deployment.

The main contributions of this work are twofold:

•

We analyze a batteryless
IoT
sensor node designed for environmental sensing, describing its architectural components and developing a mathematical model of its dynamic energy variations. This model uses an electrical circuit representation of the
EH
,
PM
,
SCap
,
CL
, along with the node’s functional states (sensing, communication, and sleeping). We also detail the energy charge/discharge behavior of the
SCap
in each of these states.

•

We validate the model using experimental data from a light-powered batteryless
IoT
prototype, assessing the correlation between simulated and real-world results under three lighting conditions (300 lx, 500 lx, and 700 lx), including variations to show when the energy consumed by the node is either equal to, greater than, or less than the energy harvested. Our results demonstrate that the model accurately predicts the real-world
SCap
energy behavior, with deviations of around 1% in energy.

The

.

IAQ

.

II-B

Energy Constraints for Continuous Batteryless Operation

In our previous work
[
2
]
, we defined the critical energy constraints necessary for designing reliable batteryless
IoT
sensor nodes capable of continuous operation. We considered a constant illumination scenario to guarantee continuous operation and sensor readings at a fixed amount of time. Moreover, to achieve maximum sensing efficiency with the available energy, our solution adopted a power management strategy based on the periodic switching between active and sleep cycles. Our previous analysis considers all the energy sources and energy consumed by the batteryless
IoT
node and, based on the energy conservation principle, outlines that the energy produced in a total operational time
T
should be greater than or equal to the energy consumed, which is described in the following inequality:

E_{buf}\!+\!\underbrace{\int_{0}^{T}\!P_{harv}(\tau)d\tau}_{E_{harv}(T)}\geq\underbrace{\int_{0}^{T}\!P_{dev}(\tau)d\tau}_{E_{dev}(T)},

(1)

where
\tau
is used as the time variable and
E_{buf}
represents the minimal energy in the buffer (in Joules), to ensure that the node stays operational.
P_{harv}(\tau)
is the harvested power for an instant of time
\tau
in Watts, and
P_{dev}(\tau)
is the power consumed by the node for an instant of time
\tau
in Watts. In addition,
E_{harv}(T)
and
E_{dev}(T)
represent the energy harvested and consumed (in Joules), respectively, during a complete duty cycle denoted by
T
. A simplified version of the energy flow model is

E_{harv}(T)\geq\!E_{dev}(T),\forall T\in[0,\infty).

(2)

In other words, at each duty cycle, we aim to harvest more or equal energy than what the node consumes for the active and sleep cycles. Therefore, the inequality described in (
2
) models the energy of an
IoT
node that is always active. However, to provide a detailed view of the energy produced and consumed during sleep and active cycles, we divide the duty cycle time
T
into active and sleeping times,
T_{a}
and
T_{s}
, such that
T
=
T_{a}
+
T_{s}
. Consequently, we divide the energy consumed by the node
E_{dev}(T)
into energy consumed during active and sleeping times,
E_{deva}(T_{a})
and
E_{devs}(T_{s})
, being
E_{dev}(T)=E_{deva}(T_{a})+E_{devs}(T_{s})
. Therefore, the energy conservation of a batteryless
IoT
node with low-power management is modeled as

E_{harv}(T_{a}+T_{s})\geq\!E_{deva}(T_{a})+E_{devs}(T_{s}).

(3)

The obtained mathematical expression is applied to design and implement batteryless
IoT
sensor nodes under constant illumination scenarios. Specifically, it allows us to compute the sleeping period
T_{s}
that satisfies (
3
), enabling an active/sleep low-power management strategy for the batteryless
IoT
nodes. This will allow us to evaluate and model the energy dynamics of the node in different illumination scenarios.

III
Energy Modeling of Batteryless IoT Sensors

To accurately model the behavior of batteryless
IoT
sensor nodes powered by ambient energy harvesting, we first analyze their energy harvesting and consumption stages. This enables a detailed characterization of node performance and supports the development of a mathematical model capturing its real-world operation. While this work focuses on
BLE
-enabled environmental sensing nodes, the model is generalizable to any batteryless
IoT
device, offering valuable insights for network optimization and design.

The analysis begins with the
EH
, which converts ambient energy (
RF
, light, thermal, kinetic) into electrical energy following a technology-specific transduction curve
[
3
,
14
]
. The
PM
then manages this power via DC/DC conversion and
MPPT
, optimizing energy transfer under dynamic conditions
[
4
]
. Harvested energy is stored in a
SCap
, which buffers and stabilizes supply to the
CL
. The
PM
regulates the voltage and current delivered to the
CL
to ensure stable operation. Finally, the
CL
consumes energy based on the node’s actions determined by the different
states
of the finite state machine governing the node’s behavior. In this section, we first model each component individually, then derive the governing equations for currents and voltages across the circuit model, depicted in Fig.
2
.

III-A

Description of the circuit components

Fig. 2:
Equivalent electrical model of the node: the left-hand side circuit models the behavior of the voltage at the
SCap
, while the right-hand side circuit models the
CL
.

EH
and PM

The
EH
gathers energy from the environment to charge the
SCap
and power the
CL
. The harvester can be modeled as an ideal constant current source with a parallel inner resistance. The current generated by the
EH
, as well as the output voltage, depend on the specific technology used to harvest energy from the environment and on the environmental conditions. For example in light, the energy harvested from a
PV
cell depends on the light (50 - 1000 lx), temperature, and humidity. The
PM
adapts the load connected to the
EH
to guarantee maximum power transfer to the circuit, i.e., to the
SCap
and
CL
. Moreover, the
PM
controls the maximum voltage of the
SCap
.

The combination of
EH
and
PM
can be modeled as the series of an ideal voltage generator, with nominal voltage
E_{eh}
, and a resistance
R_{eh}
, which varies with the voltage
V_{c}(t)
at the
SCap
. Accordingly, the overall power transferred to the circuit remains equal to
P_{eh}(t)
. Mathematically, we have

P_{eh}(t)=V_{c}(t)I_{eh}(t)=V_{c}(t)\frac{E_{eh}-V_{c}(t)}{R_{eh}}\,,

(4)

and hence
R_{eh}
can be expressed as

R_{eh}=V_{c}(t)\frac{E_{eh}-V_{c}(t)}{P_{eh}(t)}\,,

(5)

where
I_{eh}(t)
is the current generated by
E_{eh}
and
V_{c}(t)
.
It is important to note that when
P_{eh}(t)=0
or
V_{c}(t)=E_{eh}
, the model is no longer valid because, in these cases, the
PM
will disconnect the
EH
from the
SCap
to prevent inefficient power transfer or potential overcharging. We model these two limit situations by setting
E_{eh}=0
and
R_{eh}=R_{c}
, where
R_{c}
is the leakage
resistance of the
SCap
that accounts for the self-discharging of the
SCap
in the absence of harvested power.

SCap

The
SCap
is modeled as an ideal capacitor with capacitance
C
that closes the circuit. An additional parallel resistance
R_{c}
models the self-discharging in the absence of harvested power. The current
I_{c}(t)
is positive when the
SCap
is charging, i.e., storing energy from the
EH
, and negative when the
SCap
is discharging, i.e., powering the
CL
.

PM and
CL

The resistance
CL
on the right-hand side circuit represents the equivalent load of the other components of the boards, including
MC
(
MC
), radio transceiver, and sensors, and its value depends on the operations performed by these components. This resistance dissipates a power
P_{cl}
that depends on the operational state of the node. Since a node can only operate in a finite number of states, the power
P_{cl}
typically takes values in a finite set
\mathcal{P}_{cl}=\{\ell_{0},\dots,\ell_{M-1}\}
.

For proper operation, digital components typically require a stable voltage source. The role of the
PM
is to stabilize the voltage used to power the
CL
, fixing it to a nominal value
E_{pm}
. We model it as a quadripole, where the left-hand side circuit consists of a variable resistance
R_{pm}
and the right-hand side circuit consists of an ideal voltage generator
E_{pm}
. This is modeled in series with its inner resistance
R_{in}
, and a switch
sw
, which controls the energy supply to the
CL
.

The resistance
R_{pm}
on the right-hand-side circuit represents the equivalent load presented by the
PM
to the
EH
and the
SCap
, whereas the inner resistance
R_{in}
on the right-hand side circuit accounts for the power loss due to voltage scaling. Therefore, the power
P_{pm}
dissipated by
R_{pm}
is expressed as

\displaystyle P_{pm}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P_{cl}w_{pm},\quad\hbox{with}\quad w_{pm}=\frac{1}{1+\frac{R_{in}}{R_{cl}}}\,.

(6)

where the coefficient
w_{pm}\geq 1
accounts for the inefficiency of voltage scaling, which is a characteristic of the
PM
.

In a certain state of the
CL
, such as sleeping,
P_{cl}
is constant because of the fixed energy consumption
E_{devs}
. On the other hand, the power dissipated by the resistance
R_{pm}
is given by

P_{pm}=\frac{V_{c}(t)^{2}}{R_{pm}}\,.

(7)

Therefore, to have
P_{pm}=P_{cl}w_{pm}
the resistance
R_{pm}
needs to vary with the voltage
V_{c}(t)
as follows

R_{pm}=\frac{V_{c}(t)^{2}}{P_{pm}}=\frac{V_{c}(t)^{2}}{w_{pm}P_{cl}}\,.

(8)

Eq. (
8
) provides the value of the equivalent resistance
R_{pm}
for a given operational state of the circuit, when varying the voltage of the capacitor.

It is worth noting that if the voltage at the
SCap
(which is proportional to the stored energy) drops below a certain threshold
V_{\rm off}
, the
PM
will no longer be able to keep the output voltage to
E_{pm}
, and it will switch to an OFF state. In this state, the
CL
is no longer powered, and the electronic components of the board (
MC
, transceiver, sensors, volatile memories, etc) will be shut off. In the OFF state, the switch in the right-hand side circuit is open (
sw=0
), which means
P_{pm}=0
, and consequently
R_{pm}=\infty
.

In general, the reactivation of the circuit after an OFF state may involve a transient period before re-establishing the normal operational conditions. In this period, the node may consume a non-negligible amount of energy, according to the characteristics of the board
[
2
,
14
]
.

III-B

SCap voltage behavior over time

In this subsection, we derive the mathematical expressions that govern the electrical circuit in the different states (sensing, communication, and sleeping), focusing in particular on
V_{c}(t)
, i.e., the behavior over time of the
SCap
voltage. Our analysis assumes
P_{eh}(t)=p_{h}
and
R_{cl}(t)=\rho_{h}
for
t\in[0,T)
, for an operational time
T
. In other words, we assume the charging and load conditions of the circuit remain fixed in an operational time interval. Moreover, we denote by
V_{c}(0)
the voltage of the capacitor at the beginning of the operational time interval.

In the following, we consider all possible configurations that require a specific analysis.

III-B
1
OFF state, EH disconnected

In OFF state, the right-hand side circuit does not consume energy. Consequently,
I_{pm}
is zero, which implies
\rho_{h}=\infty
.
If the
EH
does not produce enough energy, the
PM
disconnects it from the
SCap
, so that
p_{h}=0
, while
R_{eh}=R_{c}
. In this condition, the
SCap
slowly discharges over time, due to the leakage current. The voltage hence varies in time according to the standard discharging law of a capacitor with time constant
\tau_{disch}~=~C\cdot R_{c}
:

V_{c}(t)=V_{c}(0)e^{-\frac{t}{CR_{c}}}\,.

(9)

III-B
2
OFF state, EH connected

If the
PM
is in OFF state but the energy provided by the
EH
is positive,
p_{h}>0
, then the
SCap
gets charged according to the following differential equation:

\frac{{\rm d}V_{c}(t)}{{\rm d}t}=\frac{I_{c}(t)}{C}=\frac{1}{C}\frac{E_{pm}-V_{c}(t)}{R_{eh}}=\frac{1}{C}\frac{p_{h}}{V_{c}(t)}\;,

(10)

where, using (
5
) and solving the differential equation, we get

V_{c}(t)=\sqrt{\frac{2p_{h}t}{C}+V_{c}(0)^{2}}\,.

(11)

It is important to note that if the circuit configuration remains unchanged, the capacitor voltage will continue to rise until it reaches the threshold
V_{on}
. At this point, the
PM
switches are in the ON state, powering the
CL
, and the behavior of
V_{c}(t)
will change accordingly.

III-B
3
ON state, EH disconnected

We now consider the situation where the
PM
is in ON state, so that the
CL
dissipated a certain power
P_{cl}
.
If
p_{h}=0
, the energy required to power the
CL
is taken from that stored in the
SCap
, which is connected in parallel to
R_{c}
and
R_{pm}
. The voltage then changes according to the following differential equation

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}V_{c}(t)}{{\rm d}t}

\displaystyle=\frac{I_{c}(t)}{C}=-\frac{V_{c}(t)}{C}\left(\frac{1}{R_{c}}+\frac{1}{R_{pm}}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{V_{c}(t)}{C}\left(\frac{1}{R_{c}}+\frac{w_{pm}P_{cl}}{V_{c}(t)^{2}}\right).

(12)

where, once again, we replace
R_{pm}
with its expression given by (
5
). Solving the differential equation we find

V_{c}(t)=\sqrt{\alpha e^{-2t/(CR_{c})}-w_{pm}P_{cl}R_{c}}

(13)

where
\alpha=V_{c}(0)^{2}+w_{pm}P_{cl}R_{c}\,
. To be observed that (
13
) holds for
t\leq\frac{CR_{c}}{2}\ln\left(\frac{\alpha}{w_{pm}P_{cl}R_{c}}\right)
, since the voltage of the
SCap
cannot become negative.

III-B
4
ON state, EH connected

This is the normal operational condition of the node, where the load dissipates power
P_{cl}
and the
EH
transfers a positive power
p_{h}>0
to the circuit. In this case, the voltage generator on the left-hand side circuit provides a constant voltage
E_{eh}
, while the series resistance
R_{eh}
varies as for (
5
).
The differential equation that determines the time evolution of the
SCap
voltage is then given by

\frac{{\rm d}V_{c}(t)}{{\rm d}t}=\frac{1}{C}\left(\frac{E_{eh}-Vc(t)}{R_{eh}}-\frac{V_{c}(t)}{R_{pm}}\right)=\frac{P_{eh}-w_{pm}P_{cl}}{CV_{c}(t)}\,,

(14)

where the right-most expression is obtained by replacing
R_{eh}
and
R_{pm}
with their expressions given by (
5
) and (
8
), respectively. From (
14
), we can immediately realize that, when the power provided by the
EH
exceeds that required by the
CL
, the
SCap
will accumulate the extra energy, while in the contrary case, the energy stored in the
SCap
will be used to meet the demand of the
CL
. Solving (
14
) we get

V_{c}(t)=\sqrt{2\frac{P_{eh}-w_{pm}P_{cl}}{C}t}+V_{c}(0)^{2}\,.

(15)

Here,
V_{c}(t)
is the voltage in the
SCap
at any time
t
during the operational time
T
, when the
EH
and/or the
SCap
are supplying energy to the
CL
. This expression allows us to model in detail the energy in the
SCap
, precisely its charge because of energy harvesting and its discharge due to the
CL
.

Conclusions and future work

In this work, we presented an analytical model for batteryless IoT sensor nodes powered by ambient energy harvesting. The model integrates energy harvesting, power management, and supply stages, providing a detailed representation of the
SCap
voltage over a given operational time
T
. Experimental results under three illumination levels (300 lx, 500 lx, and 700 lx) confirm that the model accurately captures
SCap
’s energy behavior across sensing, communication, and sleeping states. Simulations closely match measurements from our batteryless prototype, demonstrating the model’s robustness to illumination variations, with estimated deviations around 1% over an eight-hour operation. Future work could focus on leveraging this model for adaptive sleep mechanisms and its integration into digital twins to improve resource utilization and reliability in sustainable IoT systems.
