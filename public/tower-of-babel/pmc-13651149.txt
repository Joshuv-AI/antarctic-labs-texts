Development and preliminary evaluation of a group adaptation of the Unified Protocol for Transdiagnostic Treatment of Emotional Disorders in adolescents (UP-A) in a Spanish public mental health center
Authors: Lucía Cortés, Julia García-Escalera, Irene Casanova, Alberto Segura, Rosa M. Valiente, Paloma Chorot
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnxiety and depression frequently co-occur during adolescence. The Unified Protocol for Transdiagnostic Treatment of Emotional Disorders in Adolescents (UP-A) targets shared emotional mechanisms and has shown promise in individual formats. This study adapted the UP-A to a group format and evaluated its feasibility, acceptability, and preliminary effectiveness in a public outpatient mental health setting in Spain.MethodsThirty-one adolescents (M = 14.03; 87.1% female) participated in a 16-session group intervention, which included 3 group sessions for parents.ResultsA total of 80.65% of participants completed the treatment. Adolescents and parents reported high satisfaction and therapeutic alliance. Significant improvements were observed in adolescent-reported emotional symptoms and emotion regulation strategies, along with parent-reported reductions in depressive symptoms. Clinician ratings indicated reduced diagnostic severity and improved functioning.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that the group-based UP-A is feasible, well accepted, and associated with clinical improvement in public mental health care. Further randomized studies are needed to confirm these results.Trial registration number (ClinicalTrials.gov)NCT06894368. Date of registration: March 12, 2025 (retrospectively registered).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-05472-8.

Introduction

Anxiety and depression are highly prevalent disorders among adolescents, constituting a growing concern for public health. According to recent normative data from a representative sample of 2,235 Spanish adolescents aged 12 to 18 years, 11.1% of participants presented severe anxiety symptoms and 4.4% reported severe depressive symptoms [1]. Additionally, it has been reported that around 50% of patients who present emotional symptoms, whether related to anxiety or depression, also report symptoms of another emotional disorder [2]. One of the arguments that can explain the co-occurrence between anxiety and depression is the existence of common factors that influence various disorders, usually referred to as transdiagnostic factors. Carlucci et al. [3] define these transdiagnostic factors as psychological processes that play a key role in the development and maintenance of various disorders and that can be evaluated both in people with clinical disorders as well as in those without a clinical diagnosis.

Some of the most studied transdiagnostic constructs in anxiety and depression include neuroticism, high negative affect, anxiety sensitivity and emotional avoidance [4]. Among these, negative affect and neuroticism deserve particular attention, as they are closely intertwined: neuroticism has been conceptualized as a core temperamental dimension reflecting an enduring tendency to experience negative affect [5]. Yet neuroticism is defined not only by how often negative emotions occur, but also by how individuals react to them. At the core of neuroticism lies a heightened and recurrent reactivity to negative emotions, together with beliefs of uncontrollability and unpredictability of emotional experiences, which often drive avoidant coping [6]. Such patterns of reactivity and avoidance have been associated with the onset and maintenance of anxiety and depression, the so-called “emotional disorders” [6, 7]. The term “emotional disorders” refers to anxiety, depressive, and related disorders (e.g., obsessive-compulsive and trauma-related disorders) that share the frequent experience of intense negative emotions, aversive reactions to these emotions, and maladaptive attempts to avoid or escape them [6, 7].

The Unified Protocol for Transdiagnostic Treatment of Emotional Disorders (UP) is a modular transdiagnostic cognitive-behavioral intervention that was developed to address neuroticism by targeting maladaptive responses to negative emotions and promoting more adaptive emotion regulation strategies [5, 6]. The UP teaches a sequence of adaptive emotion regulation skills such as awareness and tolerance of emotion-related physical sensations through interoceptive exposure; cognitive flexibility; mindful (i.e., present-focused and nonjudgmental) emotion awareness; the identification of maladaptive emotional behaviors (e.g., avoidance and other emotion-driven behaviors) and their replacement with alternative, opposite actions; and the consolidation of all these skills through situational emotion exposures [8]. The UP has since been developmentally adapted for adolescents (UP-A; [9]), with a parallel version available for children (UP-C; [10]). The UP-A follows this same skill sequence but tailors it to the developmental stage: contents and materials are presented in an adolescent-friendly format, and parents are involved at two levels. Throughout treatment, caregivers are kept informed about the skills their adolescents are learning so that they can support their use at home; in addition, a specific parenting module targets emotional parenting behaviors (criticism, overprotection, inconsistency, and excessive modeling of intense emotions and avoidance) that may inadvertently reinforce the adolescent’s emotional responses, helping caregivers to monitor and replace them with more supportive opposite parenting behaviors [9, 10].

The UP-A was designed as a manualized, individual in-person therapy, with most studies evaluating its effectiveness following this one-to-one presentation format [4, 11]. However, recent studies have adapted the UP-A for group-based preventive interventions. In Spain, our research group adapted the UP-A as a universal preventive intervention to be administered in group format within school settings [12–14], concluding that symptom reduction was only observed in adolescents with more severe symptoms. Our group also developed a self-administered online version of the UP-A [15], providing significant evidence on its efficacy and clinical utility in samples of adolescents diagnosed with anxiety and depressive disorders [16, 17] and adolescents at risk of suffering from anxiety and depression [18]. Recently, in Spain as well, the UP-A has been adapted for telehealth delivery as an individual treatment for adolescents with anxiety and depressive disorders [19] and as a group-based online preventive intervention for adolescents presenting with subclinical symptoms of anxiety and depression, with promising results from a randomized controlled trial (RCT) [20]. Finally, a third Spanish study recently adapted and administered the UP-A as an in-person, school-based group prevention program led by trained school psychologists and aimed at adolescents with subclinical symptoms of anxiety and depression [21].

Beyond these preventive and subclinical applications, recent research has also begun to explore the UP-A in group-based interventions with clinical samples [4, 11, 22]. For instance, Maleki et al. [23] conducted a quasi-experimental trial in a university hospital setting in Iran with 31 adolescents meeting diagnostic criteria for emotional disorders, who received 14 in-person group sessions of the UP-A either alone (n = 15) or combined with an adjunctive mindfulness component (n = 16), reporting significant symptom reductions in both conditions. Similarly, Zemestani et al. [24] conducted a waitlist RCT with 54 Iranian adolescents diagnosed with comorbid anxiety disorders, recruited through school counseling centers, who received 9 weekly 60-minute in-person group sessions of a culturally adapted UP-A at an outpatient clinic, reporting significant improvements relative to the waitlist condition. Milgram et al. [25], in turn, examined early treatment response in 95 children and adolescents with emotional disorders who received the UP-C/UP-A at the University of Miami specialty research clinic where these protocols were originally developed by Ehrenreich-May and colleagues; however, most participants were treated individually, and only a small subgroup (n = 4; 4.2%) received the group UP-A so the evidence this study provides regarding group delivery should be interpreted with caution. More recently, Pan et al. [26] conducted a single-arm pilot study in which 24 adolescents, together with at least one of their parents, completed a group adaptation of the UP-A consisting of 12 weekly 120-minute sessions at the outpatient clinic of a public psychiatric hospital in China (Peking University Sixth Hospital), providing preliminary evidence of its feasibility and efficacy in a real-world, publicly funded setting. In addition, Weintraub and colleagues have applied group-based adaptations of the UP-A to adolescents at high risk of developing serious mental illness, such as psychosis and bipolar disorder, recruited from specialty outpatient programs at a university research clinic in the USA (UCLA Semel Institute), in all cases across 9 weekly 90-minute group sessions: first in an open pilot trial with 10 adolescents [27]; subsequently in an open trial of an app-enhanced version (group sessions supported by a mobile app for between-session skill practice) with 31 adolescents and their parents [28]; and most recently in a RCT with 60 adolescents comparing the app-enhanced and standard group versions delivered via telehealth [29]. Overall, studies evaluating the UP-A adapted to a group format have shown promising evidence of feasibility, acceptability, and improvements in emotional and functional outcomes. However, to the best of our knowledge, no studies have examined the feasibility or efficacy of the UP-A as a group intervention for clinically diagnosed adolescents in Europe.

Unlike the adolescent protocol, the adult UP [8] has demonstrated adequate feasibility and acceptability when adapted as a group treatment within the Spanish public health system [30]. In the most recent report of this multicenter RCT, Peris-Baquero and Osma [30], compared the UP delivered in group format (n = 277) with individually delivered, disorder-specific cognitive behavioral therapy (treatment as usual, TAU; n = 256) in 533 adult users of Spanish public specialized mental health services, and found that the group-based UP was statistically noninferior to TAU at 15 months after treatment onset, with significant improvements in anxiety and depressive symptoms, as well as in quality of life, maintained over time.

Adapting and administering the UP-A as a transdiagnostic group intervention could represent an effective strategy to optimize public mental health resources for adolescents, as it would allow for the simultaneous treatment of patients with different disorders, increasing the number of patients treated per clinician and per time slot, and thereby potentially helping to reduce waiting lists and the cost per patient [26, 30]. Additionally, this approach may be particularly suitable during adolescence, a developmental stage in which emotional disorders often present with overlapping and still poorly differentiated symptoms [31].

The primary objectives of this study were twofold. The first aim was to adapt the Spanish version of the UP-A [9, 10] to a group format treatment within a public mental health context (a mental health center associated with Hospital Puerta de Hierro in Madrid), ensuring it met the practical and clinical needs of this setting. The second objective was to evaluate the clinical utility and feasibility of the group-based UP-A in an uncontrolled trial with 3- and 6-month follow-ups. This involved assessing adolescents’ and parents’ perspectives on anxiety and depressive symptoms, transdiagnostic variables related to emotional disorders, and engagement and satisfaction with the program. Clinicians’ perspectives on participants’ progress in general and clinical functioning were also included.

Methods

Participants

The study was carried out between 2023 and 2024, with adolescents who were active patients of the Mental Health Center (Centro de Salud Mental, i.e., CSM, in Spanish) based in Majadahonda (Madrid, Spain). The inclusion criteria were: (a) aged 11–18 years; (b) a primary diagnosis of an emotional disorder1; (c) fluency in Spanish and ability to attend treatment sessions; (d) at least one parent or guardian also fluent in Spanish and able to participate in the parent sessions; and (e) if taking psychiatric medication, being on a stable dose for at least one month prior to and during treatment and follow-up. Exclusion criteria were: (a) moderate to severe self-harm or suicidal ideation; (b) diagnoses of intellectual disability, psychotic disorders, bipolar disorder, severe emotional disorders or substance use disorders (except cannabis, caffeine, or nicotine); (c) clinical presentation requiring individualized treatment; (d) concurrent psychological treatment; (e) severe medical illness interfering with participation; and (f) refusal to provide informed consent. Inclusion and exclusion criteria were assessed by the participants’ treating clinicians at the CSM (clinical psychologists and/or psychiatrists) based on clinical judgment, the routine clinical evaluations conducted at the center, and the information available in the patients’ clinical records; no standardized instruments or predefined severity thresholds were used. In particular, the criteria of moderate to severe self-harm or suicidal ideation and severe emotional disorders were operationalized through clinical judgment, the latter referring to clinical presentations whose severity, in the clinicians’ judgment, required individualized, more intensive, or other types of treatment.

A total of 31 adolescents between the ages of 11 and 17 (M = 14.03; SD = 1.56; 87.1% were female; all participants identified as cisgender) were included in the study, all of whom were students. Of the total, only 2 (6.4%) were born outside of Spain (specifically, in South America and Eastern Europe), and both had been living in Spain for more than five years. As for their parents, 29.0% (n = 9) of the fathers and 32.3% (n = 10) of the mothers were not born in Spain. A large proportion of parents (60%; n = 18) were married, 26.7% (n = 8) were separated or divorced, 6.7% (n = 2) had a partner and 6.7% (n = 2) were single. More than three quarters of the families (76.7%; n = 23) included at least one parent with a university degree. Regarding the income of the family unit, 35.7% (n = 10) reported a low income (<€25,000), 42.9% (n = 12) a medium income (€25,000–75,000), and 21.4% (n = 6) a high income (>€75,000). Concerning their living arrangements, 74.2% (n = 23) of the adolescents were living with both of their parents and 25.8% (n = 8) only with their mother. Involved parents, a designation referring to those caregivers who completed all relevant questionnaires and contributed to the evaluation process, were mostly mothers (n = 24; 77.4%). Sociodemographic information on parents and family characteristics was not available for all families; the corresponding percentages are therefore based on the number of valid responses for each variable (ns = 28–30). Procedure

The Ethics Committees of Hospital Puerta de Hierro in Madrid and National University of Distance Education (UNED) approved the study (references PI 219/2022 and 1-PSI-2023, respectively). All participants were active patients who were already receiving clinical care (psychological and/or psychiatric follow-up) at the CSM when they were referred to the program. The CSM’s clinicians (I.C., a child and adolescent clinical psychologist, and A.S., a child and adolescent psychiatrist) selected those adolescents who were suitable for the program and were responsible for contacting the families to obtain informed consent for study participation. After patients or their legal guardians provided written informed consent, clinicians at the hospital shared sociodemographic and clinical information with the UNED research team. This information included the primary and secondary DSM-5 diagnoses recorded in the CSM clinical database, which had been assigned by the adolescents’ treating clinicians (clinical psychologists and/or psychiatrists) on the basis of routine clinical evaluation, medical records and multidisciplinary consensus within the mental health center team, rather than through structured diagnostic interviews. During the first and last session, an additional half an hour was dedicated for adolescents and their parents to complete several evaluation questionnaires through a link to the Qualtrics platform, including the sociodemographic questionnaire at baseline, which was completed mostly by the parents. The follow-up assessments took place 3 and 6 months after completing the intervention. At both follow-up assessments the researchers contacted the families by telephone to schedule a call that took place after the adolescent and the involved parent had answered the follow-up questionnaires on the Qualtrics platform (through links that were sent through e-mail to the involved parent). During the follow-up call, the adolescent had the chance to rate their “Top Problems” (see Measures section), and both the adolescent and the parent were encouraged to continue using the therapeutic skills learned in the program.

Intervention

The intervention consisted of a group adaptation of the Spanish version of the Unified Protocol for Transdiagnostic Treatment of Emotional Disorders in Adolescents (UP-A; [9, 10]). The contents of the UP-A were covered over 16 sessions, each lasting 90 min, delivered once a week in person at the Mental Health Center during regular business hours. Appendix A summarizes the adolescent group sessions, which were structured according to the treatment modules and their main contents. Three consecutive group interventions took place between 2023 and 2024. From now on, we will refer to the three groups as CSM-I, CSM-II and CSM-III, respectively. The groups ranged from 8 to 13 adolescents (CSM-I: n = 8; CSM-II: n = 13; CSM-III: n = 10). The groups were facilitated by two UP-A–trained psychologists from UNED: L.C. (a general psychologist and doctoral researcher) facilitated Group 1 (CSM-I), and J.G.E. (a permanent faculty member at UNED and certified UP-C and UP-A trainer) facilitated Groups 2 and 3 (CSM-II and CSM-III), each together with one clinical psychologist (I.C.) from the CSM. Additionally, Groups 1 and 2 also included a youth psychiatrist (A.S.) from the CSM, and Group 3 included a psychologist in clinical training at the mental health center (B.R.R.). Coauthor L.C. received UP-A training through an online workshop (University of Miami), followed by preparatory meetings to become familiar with the protocol prior to group implementation and weekly supervision meetings to monitor treatment progress, all conducted by coauthor J.G.E. Previously, L.C. had also conducted a 12-session group adaptation of the UP-A program in a Madrid high school as a pilot program for the present study. In addition to these sessions, the intervention included three one-hour in-person group sessions for the parents that concurred with the beginning, middle and end of the intervention for the adolescents. The table containing the content of the parent sessions can be found in Appendix B. Throughout the three group interventions (CSM-I, CSM-II and CSM-III), a series of variations of the UP-A were introduced to adapt it to the group format and the therapeutic context (public health in outpatient format). Adaptations made to the content of the adolescents’ sessions to accommodate the group delivery format are detailed in Appendix C and described following the FRAME framework [32], in line with Kennedy and colleagues [33].

Measures

At various points in time (before and after the intervention at 3- and 6-month follow-ups), adolescents completed several self-report instruments through the Qualtrics platform. Primary outcomes were assessed using the following self-report measures:

Self-report measures

Revised child anxiety and depression scale-30 (RCADS-30; [34])

It is a scale that evaluates symptoms of anxiety and depression in children and adolescents and was adapted to Spanish by Sandín et al. [35]. The scale consists of 30 items, rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“Never or almost never”) to 3 (“Very often or almost always”), and divided into 6 subscales that measure symptoms of major depressive disorder, panic disorder, social phobia, separation anxiety disorder, generalized anxiety disorder, and obsessive-compulsive disorder. Higher scores indicate greater symptom severity. The RCADS-30 has demonstrated good psychometric properties with both non-clinical and clinical populations [35, 36], with internal consistency values (Cronbach’s α) that vary between 0.66 and 0.86 for the subscales, between 0.86 and 0.88 for the total scale, and between 0.82 and 0.90 for the general anxiety scale. The reliability (α coefficient) for the RCADS-30 in the present study was (pre/post/3-month/6-month): 0.94/0.93/0.94/0.94 (RCADS-30 total); 0.91/0.78/0.86/0.90 (MDD); 0.85/0.83/0.85/0.88 (PD); 0.92/0.88/0.86/0.89 (SP); 0.62/0.75/0.65/0.63 (SAD); 0.82/0.87/0.88/0.88 (GAD); 0.81/0.71/0.77/0.85 (OCD).

Depression and anxiety interference scale for children and adolescents (EIDAN; [37])

It is a 10-item self-reported instrument that evaluates the interference of emotional symptoms in the adolescent’s daily functioning over the previous weeks across multiple contexts (school, family and peers). Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (not at all) to 4 (a lot), and higher scores indicate greater levels of interference. This instrument has shown high internal consistency, with α values ranging from 0.84 to 0.86 [12]. For the EIDAN, the reliability (α coefficient) obtained in the present study was (pre/post/3-month/6-month): 0.87/0.84/0.81/0.85 (EIDAN total); 0.82/0.82/0.76/0.85 (EIDAN school); 0.73/0.76/0.58/0.75 (EIDAN family); 0.90/0.78/0.59/0.84 (EIDAN peers).

Emotion regulation strategies questionnaire (CERE-II)

Sandín et al. [38] adapted this 28-item questionnaire to Spanish, which evaluates 7 subscales of the “emotion regulation” construct. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“Never or almost never”) to 5 (“Always or almost always”), and higher scores indicate a greater use of the corresponding emotion regulation strategy. Below, the subscales are presented with their corresponding internal consistency index [39]: attention/awareness/understanding (α = 0.82), acceptance/tolerance (α = 0.76), cognitive reappraisal (α = 0.67), self-instructions (α = 0.82), suppression (α = 0.77), rumination (α = 0.62) and distraction (α = 0.73). The reliability found for the CERE in the present study was (pre/post/3-month/6-month): 0.85/0.77/0.92/0.95 (attention/awareness/understanding); 0.86/0.85/0.91/0.92 (acceptance/tolerance); 0.65/0.71/0.71/0.77 (cognitive reappraisal); 0.80/0.86/0.78/0.84 (self-instructions); 0.73/0.72/0.81/0.86 (suppression); 0.67/0.55/0.34/0.58 (rumination); 0.85/0.86/0.81/0.95 (distraction).

Top Problems measure

As recommended in the UP-A Therapist Manual, the adolescent version of the Top Problem Assessment (TPA; [40]) was used to operationalize the concerns that bring patients to therapy and enhance motivation. With the therapist’s guidance, adolescents were encouraged to formulate each Top Problem by describing both the emotional experience and the emotional behavior associated with it (i.e., what they typically do in response to the emotion), including overt and covert avoidance strategies. Adolescents identified one to three main concerns, rating their impact on daily functioning (0–10 scale, where higher scores indicate greater interference of the problem) at five time points: session 2 (baseline), session 8 (mid-treatment), session 16 (end of treatment), and at 3- and 6-month follow-ups.

Parent-reported measures

In both the pre and posttreatment conditions and at the 3, and 6-month follow-up, parents answered two self-report questionnaires using the Qualtrics platform. One of them was the parent version of the RCADS [35]. For this questionnaire, the reliability (α coefficient) was as follows (pre/post/3-month/6-month): 0.91/0.90/0.95/0.94 (RCADS-30 total); 0.79/0.84/0.85/0.79 (MDD); 0.79/0.82/0.87/0.84 (PD); 0.84/0.82/0.88/0.91 (SP); 0.84/0.76/0.89/0.86 (SAD); 0.72/0.68/0.79/0.80 (GAD); 0.70/0.66/0.82/0.71 (OCD). The other one was the parent version of the EIDAN [37]. The reliability (α coefficient) for the EIDAN-P was 0.78/0.88/0.89/0.93 (EIDAN total); 0.90/0.89/0.93/0.95 (EIDAN school); 0.23/0.56/0.58/0.69 (EIDAN family); 0.70/0.90/0.84/0.87 (EIDAN peers).

Clinician-rated measures

Both in the pretreatment, posttreatment and follow-ups, the therapists involved in the study assessed the clinical evolution of the participants using three scales:

The children’s global assessment scale (CGAS; [41])

The Spanish version by the Research Unit on Family Care and Intervention was used (n. d.). It is a clinician-estimated measure of the adolescent’s overall level of functioning in the last month. Scores range from 100 − 91 (optimal functioning in all areas) to 10 − 1 (constant need for supervision). For the analyses, the original CGAS scores were recoded into a 1–10 ordinal scale by grouping the standard CGAS ranges into ten 10-point intervals (100–91 = 10, 90–81 = 9, 80–71 = 8, 70–61 = 7, 60–51 = 6, 50–41 = 5, 40–31 = 4, 30–21 = 3, 20–11 = 2, 10–1 = 1). Accordingly, the CGAS values reported in the Results and in Table 3 are expressed on this recoded 1–10 scale.

The clinical global impression (severity) scale (CGI-S; [42])

Using this scale, the clinician estimated the severity of the adolescent’s anxiety and depression symptoms. Scores range from 1 (“Normal, not ill at all”), to 7 (“Extremely ill”).

The clinical global impressions (improvement) scale (CGI-I; [42])

This scale was completed by clinicians during posttreatment and follow-ups. Adolescent’s improvement was scored on a scale from 1 to 7, with 1 being “very much improved” and 7 being “very much worse.”

Measures of satisfaction with the program and the therapeutic alliance

Finally, in the posttreatment evaluation, adolescents and involved parents completed a 12-item questionnaire that evaluates satisfaction with the program and the quality of the relationship with the therapist [43]. Items are rated on a 0–10 scale, with higher scores indicating greater satisfaction and a stronger therapeutic alliance. The specific items included in this questionnaire are provided in Appendices F and G.

Sample size

To estimate the sample size, version 3.1 of the G*Power program was used [44]. Based on the results of previous studies that have administered the UP-A protocol [3, 12–14, 45], an effect size of 0.30 was estimated. Assuming a statistical power of 0.80, a repeated measures ANOVA type statistical analysis (with 4 evaluation moments), a single experimental group, and α = 0.05, the G*Power program estimated that 24 participants would be necessary. The waitlist RCT conducted by Ehrenreich-May et al. [45] reported a 17.64% dropout rate. Taking that into consideration, an initial sample size of 29 participants was calculated for this study, anticipating an 18% dropout.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using linear mixed models (LMMs) implemented in SPSS (version 30.0), using full-information maximum likelihood estimation to assess change across the four time points (pretreatment, posttreatment, 3-month follow-up, and 6-month follow-up). Each outcome was analyzed in a separate model, with Time included as a fixed factor and random intercepts specified for participants. Alternative covariance structures (e.g., compound symmetry and first-order autoregressive) were initially considered, but these more complex structures led to model convergence problems given the limited sample size and number of observations per subject; an identity covariance structure was therefore used across all models to reduce model complexity and ensure convergence. Effect sizes were quantified using marginal R² to estimate the proportion of variance explained by fixed effects [46]. Standardized regression coefficients (β), p-values and R2 are reported.

Results

Dropout and attendance rates

Of the 31 participants who initially enrolled, a total of 25 (80.65%) completed the treatment, defined as remaining in the group until the last (16th) session and completing the posttreatment assessment (no minimum attendance threshold was predefined; dropout = 19.35%) (see Fig. 1 – flow diagram). At the 3-month follow-up, data were available for 23 adolescents (74.19%) and 20 (64.52%) at the 6-month follow-up. On average, the adolescents who finished the treatment missed a total of 1.68 sessions (SD = 1.49; range: 0–6) and parents missed a mean of 0.36 sessions (SD = 0.64; range: 0–2). Note that the sample sizes reported in Tables 2 and 3 differ from those shown in the flow diagram because the former reflect the number of participants with valid questionnaire data at each assessment point, after excluding the two participants removed from the analyses for no longer meeting the study criteria (see Table 1; Fig. 1). In addition, at some assessment points a parent completed the questionnaires while the adolescent did not, or vice versa, so adolescent- and parent-reported ns may also differ.

Treatment outcomes

Self-report measures

Linear mixed models revealed significant improvements over time in most of the self-report measures. Specifically, significant changes were observed in all subscales of the RCADS (all ps < 0.05); changes were significant across all assessment points in the CERE subscales of Attention/Awareness/Understanding and Cognitive Reappraisal (all ps < 0.05), and at least at one point for each of the following CERE subscales: Acceptance/Tolerance (pre to 3-month follow-up: β = 3.17, p = .004; to 6-month follow-up: β = 5.26, p < .001), Self-Instructions (pre to 3-month follow-up: β = 2.23, p < .001; to 6-month follow-up: β = 1.98, p = .001), and Suppression (pre to post: β = -1.44, p = .014). In contrast, no significant changes were observed in EIDAN family (from pre to posttreatment and to 6-month follow-up), Acceptance/Tolerance (pre to posttreatment), Self-Instructions (pre to posttreatment), Suppression (from pretreatment to 3- and 6-month follow-ups), and Rumination and Distraction (at any time point) (all ps > 0.05). Descriptive statistics, along with beta coefficients, p values and R² from the pre–post linear mixed model analyses, are presented in Table 2, and the full set of model estimates (β, SE, p, 95% CI, R² Marginal) of RCADS, EIDAN and CERE scales for all comparisons (pre–post, pre–3 months, pre–6 months) is provided in Appendix D.

In the Top Problems measure, linear mixed model analyses indicated significant changes over time in the intensity of the main problems (P) reported by adolescents. For P1, symptom intensity decreased significantly at all time points (mid: β = − 1.54; post: β = − 2.56; 3 m: β = − 3.65; 6 m: β = − 4.08; all ps < 0.001). For P2, reductions were also consistent across evaluations (mid: β = − 1.25; post: β = − 2.65; 3 m: β = − 3.58; 6 m: β = − 3.59; all ps ≤ 0.05). Similarly, P3 showed significant improvements across assessments (mid: β = − 1.15; post: β = − 2.56; 3 m: β = − 2.76; 6 m: β = − 2.86; all ps ≤ 0.05). Descriptive statistics, along with beta coefficients, p values and R2 from the pre-post linear mixed model analyses, are presented in Table 3. Although the Top Problems is an adolescent-reported measure, it is presented in Table 3 rather than in Table 2 because its assessment schedule differed from that of the remaining self-report instruments: it was administered with therapist guidance and included an additional mid-treatment assessment (session 8).

Parent-reported measures

According to parent-reported measures, linear mixed model analyses revealed significant improvement over time in some outcomes. Specifically, significant changes were observed in the RCADS total score (pre vs. posttreatment and pre vs. 6-month follow-up), in the MDD subscale (pre vs. posttreatment and pre vs. 6-month follow-up), and in the SAD subscale (pre vs. 6-month follow-up). Additionally, a significant improvement was found in the EIDAN total score from pretreatment to 6-month follow-up. In contrast, no significant changes were found in the remaining subscales of the RCADS (PD, SP, GAD, OCD), nor in any time-point comparisons for the EIDAN School, Family, and Peers subscales. Descriptive statistics for all assessment points and results from pre–post linear mixed models are reported in Table 2, and the full set of model estimates (β, SE, p, 95% CI, R² Marginal) for all comparisons (pre–post, pre–3 months, pre–6 months) is provided in Appendix E.

Clinician-rated measures

According to clinician-rated measures, linear mixed model analyses showed significant improvements from pre to posttreatment in both the primary and secondary diagnoses’ severity levels. Specifically, for the CGI-I scale, a significant decrease in scores (indicating greater clinical improvement) was observed from posttreatment to the 6-month follow-up (β = -0.54, p < .001), whereas the decrease between the 3- and 6-month follow-ups approached, but did not reach, statistical significance (β = -0.35, p = .053). No significant difference was found between posttreatment and the 3-month follow-up (β = -0.15, p = .233). Linear mixed model analyses also indicated significant improvements in clinician-rated functioning and symptom severity over time. For the CGAS, functioning scores increased significantly from pretreatment to all subsequent time points: to posttreatment (β = 0.73, p < .001); to 3-month follow-up (β = 0.93, p < .001), and to 6-month follow-up (β = 1.23, p < .001). Similarly, for the Clinical Global Impression – Severity (CGI-S), symptom severity significantly decreased across all time points (β = -1.38, p < .001; β = -1.85, p < .001; β = -2.08, p < .001). Table 3 displays descriptive statistics across all time points and the results of pre-post linear mixed model analyses.

Measures of satisfaction with the program and the therapeutic alliance

On a 0–10 scale, adolescents reported high acceptability of the program during the posttreatment assessment. Average ratings for learning, perceived effectiveness, enjoyment, and likelihood of recommending the program to peers ranged from 7.04 to 7.96. Self-reported emotion regulation skills showed a marked improvement, with average ratings increasing from 3.52 (pretreatment) to 7.57 (posttreatment). The therapeutic alliance was also positively evaluated by adolescents, with average ratings ranging from 8.00 to 9.22. On the same 0–10 scale, parents reported high levels of satisfaction with the program, with average ratings ranging from 7.00 to 8.55. Ratings for the usefulness of session summaries and parent sessions were 7.35 and 8.17, respectively. Parents also observed improvements in their child’s emotion regulation skills, with average ratings increasing from 2.82 (baseline) to 7.00 (posttreatment). The therapeutic alliance was similarly rated highly by parents, with average ratings ranging from 7.09 to 9.30. Further details are available in Appendices F and G.

Discussion

This uncontrolled repeated-measures study evaluated the effects of the UP-A delivered in a group format in a Spanish public mental health setting to adolescents diagnosed with anxiety and/or depressive disorders. The intervention consisted of 16 weekly sessions, with assessments conducted at pretreatment, posttreatment, and at 3- and 6-month follow-ups. Significant improvements were observed over time in clinician-rated severity of primary and secondary emotional diagnoses, general functioning, and symptomatology. Adolescent-reported outcomes showed significant reductions in most emotional symptoms and increases in the use of several adaptive emotion regulation strategies. However, some indicators did not change significantly, and several effect sizes were small. Parent-reported changes, while generally in the same direction, were less consistent. Additionally, the intervention was rated as highly acceptable by both adolescents and their parents. These promising findings are consistent with previous research applying the adult UP in group settings within Spanish public care [30], as well as with recent studies conducted in the USA, Iran and China that have adapted the UP-A to a group format for adolescents with clinical conditions [23, 25–29].

Beyond anxiety and depression symptom reduction, the sustained improvements in emotion regulation found in the present study, as assessed with the CERE scale (enhanced emotional awareness, increased cognitive flexibility, and reductions in behavioral avoidance), suggest that the UP-A may be impacting this core transdiagnostic mechanism implicated in the development and maintenance of emotional disorders [45]. The emotion regulation skills showing the most consistent and significant improvements (Attention/Awareness/Understanding, Cognitive Reappraisal, and Acceptance/Tolerance) represent the strategies most explicitly targeted in the UP-A sessions. For Acceptance/Tolerance, the improvement did not reach statistical significance from pre to posttreatment (β = 1.80, p = .088) but became significant at the 3- and 6-month follow-ups. These gains align with the core intervention principles of the UP-A, which emphasize enhanced emotional awareness, reduced experiential avoidance through emotion-focused practices, greater cognitive flexibility, and the use of reappraisal and exposure techniques to address maladaptive emotional responses [45].

In contrast, the limited improvement in the rumination, suppression, and distraction strategies (no significant changes for rumination or distraction, and only a transient reduction in suppression at the 3-month follow-up) may reflect the challenges of addressing these more covert, internally driven processes in psychological therapy, especially in a group format. Rumination has been linked to more severe symptoms and poorer response to cognitive-behavioral treatment, suggesting it may require more individualized attention [47]. Similarly, suppression and distraction (which, like rumination, also tend to operate outside of conscious awareness and are less readily observable) may likewise be difficult to modify in a group context. This is consistent with prior findings indicating that some emotion regulation strategies, such as expressive suppression, tend to show more limited change over the course of group-based interventions when compared to other strategies like cognitive reappraisal, even among treatment responders [48]. An additional, complementary explanation relates to how these strategies were assessed. In the present study, rumination, suppression, and distraction were explicitly assessed through the corresponding subscales of the CERE-II; however, self-report questionnaires may have a limited capacity to capture these covert, internally driven processes, particularly among adolescents. Indeed, the internal consistency of the rumination subscale was low at several assessment points in our sample (α ranged from 0.34 to 0.67), so the absence of significant change on these subscales should be interpreted with caution. Regarding treatment delivery, these three strategies were explicitly addressed in the program: they were presented as cognitive avoidance strategies and the Top Problems procedure allowed each adolescent to identify the specific emotional behaviors that were most problematic for them, including those of a cognitive nature. Nevertheless, it is possible that the intensity and explicitness with which these covert strategies can be targeted in a group format (where opportunities to individualize examples are more limited and which partly depend on clinicians’ skills) were not sufficient to produce measurable change. Future group applications of the UP-A may benefit from complementing self-reports with multi-method assessments of these strategies and from systematically ensuring that the examples used in group sessions cover the full range of emotional behaviors, including rumination, suppression, and distraction.

Finally, outcome measures based on parent reports showed less consistent significant changes compared to adolescent self-reports. This aligns with prior findings showing poor adolescent–parent agreement across internalizing disorders, particularly for anxiety-related symptoms [49]. As noted in broader reviews of multi-informant assessments, such discrepancies are to be expected when evaluating internalizing problems, which are less observable and therefore more difficult for external informants to detect accurately [50]. The closest values to statistical significance in parent reported measures—apart from depression (which did show significant change in most comparisons)—were found in the “EIDAN peers” subscale, which measures interference of anxious-depressive symptoms in peer relationships. One possible explanation is that the UP-A group emphasizes exposure techniques focused on social functioning, the effects of which may be more observable to parents than changes in internal emotional states.

This study has several limitations. The absence of an active control group prevents firm conclusions about UP-A’s specific efficacy. Some constructs relied solely on self-report, and no structured diagnostic interviews were used, limiting diagnostic accuracy; similarly, exclusion criteria such as moderate to severe self-harm or suicidal ideation and severe emotional disorders were operationalized through clinical judgment rather than through standardized instruments or thresholds. Although improvements were maintained at 6 months, long-term effects remain unclear. Additionally, the small sample size limits generalizability and power to detect subtle effects. However, linear mixed-effects models may have helped compensate for this. Finally, because the study was conducted in a single public mental health center, the results may not be generalizable to other contexts or to other public mental health settings. Notwithstanding this limitation, we would like to emphasize the importance of conducting research in real-world clinical contexts, as effectiveness studies carried out in routine care settings are essential to inform the implementation of evidence-based treatments in public mental health systems.

A notable strength of this study is that program modifications were systematically documented using the FRAME model [32], a methodology previously employed by Kennedy et al. [33] to report adaptations to the UP-C/A in partial hospitalization settings. Treatment adherence can also be considered a modest strength of the study. Only 6 of the 31 participants (19.35%) discontinued the intervention between the pre and posttreatment assessments, a dropout rate in line with those reported by other group-based UP-A studies with clinical samples, which have ranged from approximately 10% [23] to over 20% [28], including 16.67% in a Chinese public mental health sample [26]. This figure is also well within the range of attrition rates reported across UP trials with clinical samples (0–62%; [3]), and far below its upper bound. Although the present study was not designed to examine determinants of treatment adherence, several implementation features may have contributed to participant retention, including the delivery of the intervention free of charge within participants’ usual mental health center, the group format that may have promoted cohesion and peer support, the active involvement of parents through parallel sessions and session summaries, and the co-facilitation of the groups by clinicians from the CSM itself, which may have additionally strengthened engagement and the therapeutic alliance.

Future research should continue to explore mechanisms of change using longitudinal designs and mediation/moderation analyses, as well as extend to comparative evaluations of different UP-A group delivery formats. Recent group-based studies have varied considerably in structure. For instance, Pan et al. [26] delivered a 12-session version with enhanced parental involvement; Maleki et al. [23] used a 14-session program; Zemestani et al. [24] and Weintraub et al. [27], [28], [29] implemented briefer, 9-session formats; and the present study employed a 16-session protocol that followed the Therapist Manual’s recommendation for group delivery [10] and incorporated three parallel group sessions for parents. Identifying the optimal number of sessions and the degree of caregiver involvement that maximizes adherence and treatment response therefore represents an important direction for future research.

Lastly, several practical lessons emerged from the implementation of the UP-A in a group format within a public mental health setting. First, covert emotion regulation strategies, such as rumination, suppression, and distraction, should be explicitly addressed using a wide range of examples during group sessions. In addition, conducting individual and family assessments prior to treatment, including structured diagnostic interviews and the collaborative identification of each adolescent’s Top Problems, may improve treatment efficiency.
