Hydrophobic deep eutectic solvent-based DLLME for simultaneous determination of triazole fungicides in grain and water samples
Authors: Fatemeh Sadat Fatemi, Abolfath Shahsavani, Zolfaghar Aladaghlo, Ali Reza Fakhari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aims to develop a dispersive liquid-liquid micro-extraction (DLLME) method for simultaneous determination of triazole fungicides (TFs). A hydrophobic molecular deep eutectic solvent (DES), composed of diphenylamine (DPA) and benzophenone (BZP) in a 1:1 molar ratio, was prepared as an extraction solvent for the adsorption of TFs. The rapid injection of a dispersion solvent containing the extraction solvent (DES) facilitated the extraction process, and phase separation was further accelerated through centrifugation. Under the optimal conditions, the proposed method provided a correlation coefficient greater than 0.993, with intra-day and inter-day RSDs ranging from 2.5% to 4.8% and 5.5% to 7.1%, respectively. The limits of detection and quantitation were found to be in the range of 0.5-1.0 ng mL− 1 and 1.6–3.3 ng mL− 1, respectively. Enrichment factors ranged from 593 to 1380. Ultimately, the proposed extraction method successfully detected residual TFs, achieving relative recoveries between 84.6% and 109.6% in water, wheat, and rice samples. Furthermore, this study provides comparative insights into the presence of TFs in bran compared to hulled wheat samples. The findings demonstrate that the proposed DLLME method, based on a tailored hydrophobic DPA–BZP-1:1 DES, provides a simple, low-solvent, and selective sample preparation strategy, offering an effective methodological alternative for the routine monitoring of triazole fungicide residues in agricultural products and environmental samples.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51500-3.

Introduction

Triazole fungicides (TFs) are one of the significant classes of pesticides that can lead to the devastation of crops and fruits1. Due to their high chemical and photochemical stability coupled with slight biodegradability, prolonged utilization of TFs allows them to accumulate in soil and water, which can cause environmental contamination and damage to organisms. Moreover, previous studies have indicated that TFs may disrupt endocrine functions, leading to increased toxicity in both animals and humans2. On this point, the determination of acceptable levels for pesticide residues in food and biological samples, aiming to mitigate their impact on humans and the environment, is typically based on the maximum residue limits (MRLs). The EU Commission Regulation (EC) No 396/2005 has set the standards and regulations for MRLs. For example, for wheat, these are 0.01 mg kg−1 for hexaconazole, propiconazole, and triticonazole, and 0.3 mg kg−1 for difenoconazole and tebuconazole, respectively3. Thus, it is crucial to detect and isolate TFs as indispensable measures to safeguard organisms and ecosystems.

Accordingly, there is an inevitable requirement to explore simple, rapid, and sensitive methods for the determination of TFs. Over recent years, various techniques have been utilized to investigate these fungicides in environmental and food samples. These methods encompass gas chromatography (GC)4, high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)5, and liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS)6. Despite their notable sensitivity and high-throughput separation capability, many analytical methods remain susceptible to matrix interference from analytical samples. Utilizing sample preparation methods to enhance and pre-concentrate the analytes in the sample matrix is critical. These chromatographic methods are typically coupled with traditional sample pretreatment techniques. Two primary method classifications exist for sample preparation employed in the extraction and determination of pesticides. The first one involves employing an adsorbent, with extraction methods such as solid-phase extraction (SPE)7 and solid-phase microextraction (SPME)8 and sustainable sorptive extractions such as fabric phase sorptive extraction (FPSE)9, cationic paper sorptive extraction (CPSE)10, and disposable in-tip cellulose paper device (DICP)11. The second category of pesticides extraction methods is solvent-based and includes techniques such as liquid-liquid extraction (LLE)12,13, liquid phase microextraction (LPME)14, and dispersive liquid-liquid microextraction (DLLME)15.

In the realm of conventional extraction methods, DLLME has long served to isolate and concentrate analytes due to the various offering advantages, including a miniaturized analyte extraction process, requiring a small volume of solvents and cost-effective procedure. This method incorporates the dispersion of an extraction solvent, leading to shortened extraction times. Swift injection ensures that adsorption sites are readily accessible to target analytes by dispersing fine droplets of extraction solvent throughout the entire cloudy sample solution. Consequently, small droplets are separated manually by centrifugation16–18.

Extensive attention has been attributed to ionic liquids (ILs) as a versatile solvent class, which includes advantageous features like low melting points, exceptionally low vapor pressures, high thermal stability, and customizable molecular properties19,20. A different class of solvents called deep eutectic solvents (DESs), are formed by harmonious blends of two solids acting as hydrogen bond donor (HBD) and acceptor (HBA)21,22. Strong interactions between the components result in DESs having much lower melting point than those of the sole constituents. Cost-effectiveness, higher biodegradability, and simplified preparation are particular characteristics of a DES that can be considered as additional superiorities over ILs23–26. The DES proves to be an economical solvent for extracting non-ionic compounds in addition to ionic compounds. The development of hydrophobic DESs has significantly expanded the potential applications of these solvents, particularly in aqueous systems where traditional hydrophilic DESs encounter limitations. By carefully selecting precursors, researchers have successfully created hydrophobic DESs with unique characteristics that give them an advantage over their hydrophilic counterparts27. One notable advantage of hydrophobic DESs is their ability to form non-covalent interactions, such as π-π interactions and hydrogen bonding. These interactions enable hydrophobic DESs to effectively extract non-ionic compounds. In the context of DLLME, hydrophobic DESs have shown great promise as an appropriate candidate due to their preferential affinity for organic compounds and excellent extraction capabilities28,29. The adsorption of TFs using hydrophobic DESs based on fatty acids, phenols and terpenoids has been documented in environmental water and wine samples30,31. In this study, a hydrophobic molecular DES, composed of diphenylamine and benzophenone in a 1:1 molar ratio29, was prepared as an extraction solvent for the adsorption of environmental TFs from water, wheat, and rice samples. Hydrophobic DESs are often prepared from quaternary ammonium salts, which are relatively expensive, thermally unstable under moderate heating, and present biodegradability concerns32. In contrast, the DPA: BZP-1:1 DES is prepared from inexpensive, commercially abundant aromatic compounds and exhibits high thermal stability. In consideration of previously reported methods for the extraction of triazole fungicides, the hydrophobic DES-DLLME method developed in this study demonstrates several notable advantages. By employing a low volume of a simply synthesized aryl-based hydrophobic DES and eliminating the need for dispersers or auxiliary devices, the proposed approach significantly reduces solvent consumption and operational cost while enhancing extraction efficiency toward triazole fungicides33. These features contribute to improved greenness, minimized use of hazardous organic solvents, and enhanced analytical performance compared with existing methods. In conventional DLLME-based methods, solubility-related limitations often arise from partial dissolution of the extraction solvent in the aqueous phase or insufficient affinity between the extractant and target analytes, leading to reduced enrichment factors and compromised extraction efficiency. In the present study, these limitations are effectively overcome by employing a hydrophobic DES with negligible solubility in water and tailored physicochemical properties. Improving extraction selectivity for specific analytes remains a major challenge in microextraction techniques. The aryl-based DES is capable of providing favorable interactions with triazole fungicides through a synergistic combination of hydrogen bonding, π–π stacking, and hydrophobic effects. The aromatic structures of the DES components may facilitate π–π interactions with phenyl-containing triazole moieties, while the amino group of DPA and the carbonyl group of BZP can participate in hydrogen bonding with the heteroatoms (N and O) present in triazole fungicides. Compared to other tested pesticide classes, these interactions were found to be more pronounced for triazole compounds, resulting in rapid and efficient partitioning of the target analytes into the DES phase, even at a low extraction solvent volume28,29,32. The hydrophobic DPA: BZP in 1:1 molar ratio structure demonstrated effective TFs extraction, highlighting the superior adsorption capabilities of DESs such as tailored DPA: BZP in 1:1 molar ratio.

Experimental

Materials

Six triazole fungicide analytical standards (PESTANAL®, Sigma-Aldrich), each with a purity greater than 99.0%, were used in this study. The compounds and their corresponding CAS numbers are as follows: hexaconazole (CAS 79983-71-4), diniconazole (CAS 83657-24-3), tebuconazole (CAS 107534-96-3), propiconazole (CAS 60207-90-1), triticonazole (CAS 131983-72-7), and difenoconazole (CAS 119446-68-3). Diphenylamine (99.0%), and benzophenone (99.0%) were obtained from Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, United States). Acetone (chromatographic grade, 99.8%), acetonitrile (ACN, chromatographic grade, 99.9%), methanol (chromatographic grade, 99.9%), 2-propanol (analytical grade, 99.8%), sodium hydroxide (analytical grade, 99.0%), hydrochloric acid (analytical grade, 37.0% wt.) from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany), and ethanol (EtOH, analytical grade, 99.8%) from Kimia Alcohol (Zanjan, Iran) were used. A stock solution containing TFs at 1000 mg L− 1 was prepared by dissolving an appropriate amount of TFs in methanol. Daily preparation of working standard solutions included serially diluting the stock solution with deionized water. Methanol was utilized for the preparation of standard solutions for subsequent usage in gas chromatography-flame ionization detection (GC-FID) instrument calibration.

Instrumentation

Comprehensive information about the instruments utilized are presented in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM).

Chromatographic condition

Target analytes were monitored with the utilization of an Agilent 7890 A GC (USA) coupled to a split/splitless injector, flame ionization detector, carrying a BP-5 (5% Biphenyl + 95% poly dimethyl siloxane) fused-silica capillary column (30 m long, 0.32 mm internal diameter and 0.25 mm film thickness). Ultra-high pure nitrogen with a flow rate of 1.1 mL min-1 was employed as carrier gas. The following parameters were used in GC separation: injection temperature set at 280 °C in splitless mode, initial temperature starting at 80 °C programmed to increase to 200 °C at a rate of 30 °C min-1, set at 270 °C for 1 min; then programmed to achieve 290 °C at a rate of 15 °C min-1 and held at 290 °C for 5 min; detector temperature set at 300 °C. For sample introduction, 1 µL of sample solution was injected into GC-FID.

Sampling

In order to assess the applicability of the method, three different samples of each rice and wheat were acquired from random stores in Tehran, Iran. Furthermore, river water samples were obtained along the Jajrood river in Tehran, Iran. To accurately represent water quality variations, river water samples were collected from three locations along the river, then combined into a single homogenized sample.

Sample preparation

Rice samples were first ground into a fine powder and screened through an 80-mesh sieve; then, an appropriate amount of the TFs standard solution was spiked into 1 g of accurately weighed ground rice, which was soaked in 10 mL of acetonitrile for 10 min. After the maceration process, the mixture was placed in the ultrasonic water bath for 30 min at 35 °C and then centrifuged for 5 min at 10000 rpm2. The supernatant was transferred to a different conical test tube and evaporated under a nitrogen gas stream until dryness was achieved. Finally, the residue was dissolved in 50 mL of ultrapure water. The wheat samples were analyzed using the same method as described for the preparation of rice samples, with the exception that the bran was first removed, and the hulled wheat was then powdered. Both the bran and the powdered hulled wheat were analyzed separately and compared for the presence of TFs. Water samples were filtered through a 0.45-µm membrane and then stored in amber glass bottles at 4 °C without further pretreatment. An appropriate amount of the 1000 mg L− 1 stock standard solution was spiked into the samples to obtain final concentrations of 20, 100, and 200 ng mL− 1.

Preparation of DES

The DES was prepared by combining each component in 1:1 accurate molar ratio. After mixing DPA and BZP at room temperature, the mixture was placed in an ultrasonic water bath at 35 °C for 1 min, until a homogeneous yellow eutectic liquid was obtained.

DES-DLLME procedure

A 50 mL aqueous solution of samples/standards at the desired concentration with a pH of 3.0 was added to a conical test tube. Afterward, 1500 µL of dispersion solvent (methanol) containing 25 µL of extraction solvent (DES) was injected quickly via a 3.0 mL syringe into the same tube and was shaken manually for 1 min. Increased contact between target analytes and adsorptive sites in DES is achieved through the fast injection of solvent; subsequently, boosted mass transfer will be achieved. Followed by the injection and fine dispersion of particles, a cloudy solution was formed. In the next step, the solution was centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 5 min, and phase separation was achieved by decantation. The remaining aqueous solution was carefully removed using a syringe, leaving only a yellow droplet at the bottom of the test tube. Finally, 1 µL of the droplet was injected into GC-FID to measure triazole fungicides.

Results and discussion

Characterization of DES

To investigate the interactions between the DES precursors (DPA and BZP), FT-IR spectra of the components and the prepared DES were compared. As shown in Fig. 1, the N–H stretching vibrations of DPA appearing at 3406 and 3381 cm−1 shifted to 3374 cm−1 in the DES and became significantly broader, which is indicative of the formation of intermolecular hydrogen bonds. Furthermore, the carbonyl stretching bands of BZP (1646 and 1624 cm−1) experienced a clear redshift after DES formation, consistent with hydrogen-bond stabilization and increased conjugation within the DPA–BZP network. Characteristic benzene ring vibrations were observed in all spectra at 900–1000 cm−1, reflecting the presence of π–π stacking interactions among the aromatic rings of DPA and BZP. Importantly, these spectral changes fully agree with the interaction patterns previously reported for this DES system in the literature29,34, confirming that the DES synthesized in this work exhibits the same hydrogen-bonding and non-covalent assembly characteristics.

Optimization of the extraction process

In this study, the DLLME method was utilized for the extraction and preconcentration of TFs from standards and samples, which were subsequently determined through GC-FID. Three parallel replicates (n = 3) were conducted for each factor to determine the optimal conditions. Various factors influencing the extraction process, such as solution pH, the amount of extraction solvent, dispersion solvent type and volume, and centrifugation conditions underwent examination and optimization.

The effect of pH

The pH value emerges as a crucial factor influencing adsorption efficiency. The pH level plays an important role in altering surface charge, ionization degree, and dissociation for both analytes and adsorbents. A 50 mM phosphate buffer solution was used to adjust the pH in the experiment. Investigating the effects on TFs extraction efficiencies from pH 2.0 to 8.0 indicated that efficiencies increased with rising pH from 2.0 to 3.0 (Fig. S1). The acidity and basicity of DESs originate from their Brønsted acid–base component. Thus, the HBD/HBA pair determines the system’s pH. At mildly acidic pH, the DES maintains a stable hydrogen-bond network that favors extraction. However, at higher pH values, excess OH⁻ ions capture protons from the HBD and partially disrupt the DES hydrogen-bond structure, altering its polarity and reducing its affinity for the analytes. Consequently, extraction efficiency decreases above the optimal pH35,36.

The effect of the extraction solvent amount

As the extraction efficiency of the sample solution was highly dependent on the amount of extraction solvent, a series of extractions was studied in the range of 8–42 µL of DES to achieve maximum efficiency. It was difficult to separate the DES from the solution when using less than 25 µL of DES. Thus, 25 µL was the appropriate amount. Results in Fig. S2 demonstrated that the extractions of the TFs reached a maximum at 25 µL of DES, and exceeding this amount showed a decline in extraction performance. This can be attributed to saturation of the adsorption sites, and the excessive amount would dissolve in an aqueous solution.

The effect of the type and volume of the dispersion solvent

The extraction process relies on the dispersion solvent as a key parameter to effectively disperse the extraction solvent in the sample, forming a cloud of fine particles. The rapid injection of the dispersion solvent leads to increased contact between target analytes and adsorptive sites in DES, and subsequently, boosted mass transfer will be achieved. As the chosen solvent must include miscibility with the sample matrix for easy DES release, dissolve the extraction solvent, and have no interference with target analyte extraction, various solvents such as acetone, 2-propanol, acetonitrile, methanol, and ethanol were utilized. TFs were analyzed using 1000 µL of each solvent containing 25 µL of DES. Based on the results in Fig. S3 methanol showed the maximum extraction efficiency. To evaluate the dispersion solvent volume effect, a series of extractions were carried out in the range of 500 to 2500 µL of each solvent containing 25 µL of DES. No droplet was observed when 500 µL of solvent was used. Results presented in Fig. S4 illustrated a steady rise until the volume reached 1500 µL and a reduction afterward due to the increased solubility of DES along with the increase in the dispersion solvent. Hence, 1500 µL methanol (25 µL DES) was selected as the optimal dispersion solvent31.

The effect of the centrifuge rate and time

Centrifugation is another critical parameter affecting the separation of TFs by collecting them from the sample solution. The centrifuge rate optimization involved a series of extractions between 4000 and 10,000 rpm. Results in Fig. S5 exhibited increased extraction efficiencies when the centrifuge rate was up to 5000 rpm, after which no significant alteration was observed, and extractions were almost constant. Additionally, varying centrifuge time from 4.0 to 10 min revealed a gradual enhancement in extraction performance from 4.0 to 5.0 min, and after that extraction remained nearly unaltered (Fig. S6). Hence, the optimal centrifuge rate and time were set to 5000 rpm and 5.0 min, respectively.

Method validation

The analytical performance of the presented approach was assessed under optimized conditions (Table 1). The linearity of all TFs was obtained with the DLLME method before analysis with GC-FID using several concentrations of TFs within the range of 1.6 − 1000 ng mL− 1. Correlation coefficients were found to be in the range of 0.993 to 0.996. Relative standard deviations (RSDs%) ranged from 2.5 to 4.8% for intra-day (n = 5) and 5.5 to 7.1% for inter-day (n = 5 × 3 days), determined at a concentration level of 200 ng mL−1. Limits of detection (LODs), calculated at a signal-to-noise ratio of 3 (S/N = 3), were found to be in the range of 0.5-1.0 ng mL− 1. Limits of quantitation (LOQs), calculated at the S/N = 10, were in the range of 1.6 to 3.3 ng mL− 1. EFs were determined according to Eq. 1 and ranged from 593 to 1380.

Table 3 presents a comparison of the analytical characteristics and figures of merit of the proposed method with previously reported studies.Notably, the developed method shows LODs and RSDs similar to those of previous studies. However, the present method indicates superior performance regarding EFs compared to most reported methods16,30,31,37–43. It should be noted that many previously reported DESs are predominantly hydrophilic and therefore exhibit limited affinity and ineffective interactions toward analytes such as triazole fungicides. In contrast, the hydrophobic nature of the synthesized DES in this study provides favorable non-covalent interactions with triazole compounds, leading to significantly enhanced EFs. The high EF values obtained clearly indicate strong affinity between the extraction solvent and triazole fungicides, confirming the suitability of the proposed DES for their selective extraction. A key feature of the proposed method is the minimal consumption of DES (only 25 µL), avoiding the use of large volumes of hazardous organic solvents. Additionally, the simplicity of DES synthesis eliminates the need for costly chemical materials, while the method’s approach enables rapid dispersion and a short extraction time (1 min) without relying on energy-intensive devices such as agitators, heaters, stirrers, vortexers, or sonicators. Furthermore, compared with other reported approaches, the proposed method uses a moderate amount of solvent (1500 µL), while some methods consume much larger volumes (1850–5000 µL ) or, although a few use smaller amounts (30–100 µL), they require additional steps such as specialized devices, highlighting the practical advantage of the present approach in terms of operation time, reagent cost, and instrument simplicity. The approach also reduces solvent consumption when coupled with GC-FID analysis compared to LC. Owing to the rapid partitioning and selectivity provided by the synthesized DES mediated by non-covalent interactions such as hydrogen bonding and π–π interactions the six targeted fungicides were efficiently concentrated and separated within the DES droplet. The method requires only a brief centrifugation step and uses an extraction solvent that is not recyclable. Although solvent recyclability could further enhance economic efficiency in future optimizations, its absence does not compromise the method’s overall performance.

Greenness evaluation of DES-DLLME method

The environmental friendliness of the proposed method was assessed using the Analytical GREEnness tool, which evaluates the entire analytical workflow against the 12 Principles of Green Analytical Chemistry. AGREE generates a quantitative score ranging from 0 representing least green to 1 representing most green, complemented by a visual color spectrum where red indicates substantial environmental impact and dark green signifies optimal alignment with green chemistry principles and minimal ecological footprint. The resulting AGREE assessment, shown in Fig. S7 achieved a score of 0.56, reflecting greenness that is acceptable for analytical applications. This score demonstrates the method’s environmental sustainability through minimal solvent use and simplified instrumentation. Although the current DLLME setup involves direct injection of DES into the GC system, limiting solvent recyclability, its greenness could be improved in future studies. One possible approach is immobilizing the hydrophobic DES onto magnetic nanoparticles, allowing rapid magnetic separation instead of centrifugation and enabling reuse of the DES phase after analyte desorption. This strategy would significantly improve the greenness profile of the method by minimizing solvent waste and avoiding energy-intensive separation steps. The graphical representation provides clear visual guidance for targeted improvements in future method development [44].

Conclusion

In the current study, a DES-DLLME method utilizing a hydrophobic DES (DPA:BZP in 1:1 molar ratio) as a novel approach was used for the preconcentration and determination of TFs in wheat, rice, and river water samples. This research reported a comparison of the presence of TFs between bran and hulled wheat for the first time, involving the usage of a dispersing solvent to enable rapid formation of fine droplets in order to increase contact between extraction solvent and target analytes. The advantages of this approach include good linearity, LODs (0.5-1.0 ng mL-1), LOQs (1.6–3.3 ng mL-1), relatively low RSDs (< 7.1%) and favorable enrichment factors (593–1380). Compared with classical LLE protocols, which generally require 10–20 mL of organic solvents per extraction, the DES-DLLME system developed in this work uses only 25 µL of the DPA: BZP DES together with 1.5 mL of methanol as the dispersion solvent. This miniaturized procedure results in a 90–98% reduction in solvent consumption and waste generation. The drastic decrease in organic solvent usage, confirms the improved environmental compatibility of the proposed method in accordance with green analytical chemistry principles. Our research underscores the potential for reliable application of DESs in trace detection of TFs in food and water commodities.
