Abstract
. Let ℍ \mathbb{H} be the finite direct sums of H 2 ​ ( 𝔻 ) H^{2}(\mathbb{D}) . In this paper, we give a characterization of the closed subspaces of ℍ \mathbb{H} which are invariant under the shift, thus obtaining a concrete Beurling-type theorem for the finite index shift. This characterization presents any such a subspace as the finite intersection, up to an inner function, of pre-images of a closed shift-invariant subspace of H 2 ​ ( 𝔻 ) H^{2}(\mathbb{D}) under “determinantal operators” from ℍ \mathbb{H} to H 2 ​ ( 𝔻 ) H^{2}(\mathbb{D}) , that is, continuous linear operators which intertwine the shifts and appear as determinants of matrices with entries given by bounded holomorphic functions. With simple algebraic manipulations we provide a direct proof that every invariant closed subspace of codimension at least two sits into a non-trivial closed invariant subspace. As a consequence every contraction with finite defect has a nontrivial closed invariant subspace.

1.
Introduction

Let
H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
be the classical Hardy space on the unit disc
\mathbb{D}
. Beurling’s theorem
[
5
,
6
]
states that every closed subspace of
H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
invariant under the shift are of the form
\phi H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
, where
\phi
is either identically
0
or an inner function.

Beurling’s Theorem is a cornerstone in the theory of function spaces. It has been extended to broader classes of operators, with significant advancements achieved through the development of sophisticated tools in related fields such as Harmonic Analysis, Function Theory, and finite-dimensional Linear Algebra within the framework of Operator Theory (see,
e.g.
,
[
9
,
7
,
18
,
1
,
2
,
17
,
16
,
14
,
15
]
).

The importance of understanding the lattice of invariant subspaces for the shift operators, besides its intrinsic interest, lies in the universality property of their adjoints (see,
e.g.
,
[
18
, Section 1.5]
). Specifically, every continuous endomorphism of defect
\delta
in a Hilbert space can be modeled (up to a constant) through the restriction to an invariant subspace of any backward shift of index at least
\delta
.
From Beurling’s theorem, it is straightforward to understand the lattice of invariant subspaces of the shift of index one. Via the universality property, this provides an affirmative solution to the invariant subspace problem for bounded linear operators with defect
\leq 1
.

Beurling’s Theorem can be viewed as stating that every non-trivial closed invariant subspace for the shift in
H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
is the image of an isometry which commutes with the shift. From this perspective, Lax
[
13
]
(for finite index shifts), along with Halmos
[
12
]
and Rovnyak
[
19
]
(for infinite index shifts), extended Beurling’s theorem to what is now known as the Beurling-Lax Theorem (see also
[
18
, Section 1.12]
). They proved that every closed subspace of a Hilbert space, invariant under a shift, is the image of a quasi-isometry that commutes with the shift.

While the Beurling-Lax Theorem fully characterizes closed invariant subspaces for the shift in any Hilbert space, its generality and abstraction can pose challenges when working with specific cases.

For instance, directly from this theorem, it seems difficult to obtain information about maximal invariant subspaces for the shift, even for finite index shifts. In fact, by a result of Atzmon
[
4
]
, it is known that maximal invariant subspaces for finite index shifts have codimension one—this also follows from Guo, Hei and Hou
[
11
]
, who proved a similar statement for the restriction of the multiplication on the Bergman space to a finite index invariant subspace, and hence the result follows by the “universality” of the lattice of invariant subspaces of such an operator
[
3
, Corollary 3.4]
. However those proofs are non constructive and could be very difficult to generalize to infinite index shifts.

In this paper, we present a novel approach to describing closed invariant subspaces for the finite index shift on the direct sum of Hardy spaces. This approach proves to be “effective’ in the sense that, starting from this characterization, we can easily demonstrate through simple algebraic manipulations that the only maximal invariant subspaces have codimension one.

In order to state our main result, we need to introduce some notations (see Section
3
for details and precise statements). Let
d
be a positive integer and let
\mathbb{H}
be the Hilbert space given by the direct sum of
d
copies of the Hardy space
H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
. The elements of
\mathbb{H}
are
d
-tuple of functions in
H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
, and we write
F\in\mathbb{H}
if
F(z)=f_{1}(z){\sf e}_{1}+\ldots+f_{d}(z){\sf e}_{d}
,
z\in\mathbb{D}
and
f_{j}\in H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
. Let
S:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
be the
shift operator
, defined as
S(F)(z)=zF(z)=zf_{1}(z){\sf e}_{1}+\ldots+zf_{d}(z){\sf e}_{d}
,
z\in\mathbb{D}
. We write
S_{1}:=S
if
d=1
.

The basic observation is that if
R:\mathbb{H}\to H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
is a bounded linear operator such that
R\circ S=S_{1}\circ R
and
\phi
is a inner function, then
M:=R^{-1}(\phi H^{2}(\mathbb{D}))
is an
S
-invariant closed subspace of
\mathbb{H}
. Our main result is that every closed
S
-invariant subspace of
\mathbb{H}
are, up to an inner function, finite intersections of subspaces like
M
. From this perspective, our approach also makes sense for
d=\infty
. Indeed, the basic question is whether the same conclusion holds in such a case (possibly with infinitely many intersections). In fact, we also specify the form of the operators
R
, which turns out to be what we call “determinantal operators”, and we now describe them in detail.

Let
1\leq m\leq d
be an integer number and let
A=(a_{jk})
be a
m\times m
matrix whose entries are bounded holomorphic functions in
\mathbb{D}
(that is, elements of
H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
). Let
1\leq s_{1}<\ldots<s_{m}\leq d
. Let
j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
. A
determinantal operator
is any linear operator
L:\mathbb{H}\to H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
of the form

L(f_{1}{\sf e}_{1}+\ldots+f_{d}{\sf e}_{d}):=\det\left(\begin{matrix}a_{11}&\ldots&a_{1m}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
a_{j-1,1}&\ldots&a_{j-1,m}\\
f_{s_{1}}&\ldots&f_{s_{m}}\\
a_{j+1,1}&\ldots&a_{j+1,m}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
a_{m1}&\ldots&a_{mm}\\
\end{matrix}\right).

Let
V\subseteq H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
be a closed
S_{1}
-invariant subspace. Hence, there exists
\varphi
which is either identically zero or a inner function, such that
V=\varphi H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
. A
determinantal brick
Q_{\varphi}
based on
\varphi
is any set of the form

Q_{\varphi}:=L^{-1}(\varphi H^{2}(\mathbb{D}))=\{F\in\mathbb{H}:L(F)\in\varphi H^{2}(\mathbb{D})\},

where
L
is a determinantal operator. The set
Q_{\varphi}
is a closed
S
-invariant subspace of
\mathbb{H}
. A
determinantal space
\mathcal{Q}_{\varphi}
based on
\varphi
is the intersection of a finite number of determinantal bricks based on
\varphi
, that is,

\mathcal{Q}_{\varphi}=Q^{1}_{\varphi}\cap\ldots\cap Q^{n}_{\varphi},

where
Q^{j}_{\varphi}
is a determinantal brick based on
\varphi
,
j=1,\ldots,n
. With these definitions at hand, the main result of the paper can then be stated as follows:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
N\neq\{0\}
be a closed subspace of
\mathbb{H}
. Then
N
is
S
-invariant if and only if there exist inner functions
\varphi,\phi
and determinantal subspaces
\mathcal{Q}_{\varphi}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{0}
such that either

N=\phi\left(\mathcal{Q}_{\varphi}\cap\mathcal{Q}_{0}\right),

or

N=\phi\mathcal{Q}_{\varphi}.

In the previous statement, it might happen that either
\mathcal{Q}_{\varphi}=\mathbb{H}
or
\mathcal{Q}_{0}=\mathbb{H}
(or both, in case
N=\mathbb{H}
). Note also that, in case
d=1
, the previous theorem reduces to the classical Beurling’s Theorem. Indeed, in that case, the only determinantal operator is the identity operator
L(f_{1}{\sf e}_{1})=f_{1}
and hence the determinantal bricks are of the form
Q_{\varphi}=\varphi H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
and
Q_{0}=\{0\}
.

Theorem
1.1
follows at once from Theorem
6.1
(where a more precise statement is given). The starting point of the proof is the Beurling-Lax theorem (see Section
5
): any closed
S
-invariant subspace of
\mathbb{H}
is the image of a so-called
S
-inner operator of
\mathbb{H}
. Such an operator is actually given by the a
d\times d
matrix with entries in
H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
. This is the matrix from which we define the determinantal subspaces associated to a closed
S
-invariant subspace. The two different cases in the theorem depends on whether the determinant of such a matrix is identically zero or not.

The concrete form given by Theorem
1.1
allows us to prove directly the following result (cfr.
[
4
]
):

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
N\subsetneq\mathbb{H}
be a closed
S
-invariant subspace. Suppose that
\dim N^{\perp}\geq 2
. Then there exists a closed
S
-invariant subspace
M\subsetneq\mathbb{H}
such that
N\subsetneq M
.

This theorem follows immediately from Proposition
4.1
, Proposition
4.2
and Theorem
6.1
.

As we stated in the introduction, Theorem
1.2
allows to give an affirmative answer to the invariant subspace problem for contractions with finite defect (for an account history and circle of ideas related to the subspace invariant problem see,
e.g.
,
[
8
,
10
]
). Namely, let
H
be a separable Hilbert space, and let
T:H\to H
be a contraction, namely,
\|T\|\leq 1
. The
defect of
T
is

\delta(T):=\dim\overline{(I-T^{\ast}T)H}.

Roughly speaking,
\delta(T)
“measures” how much far from an isometry
T
is. By the universality of the shifts (see,
e.g.
,
[
18
, Section 1.5]
), every contraction
T:H\to H
with
\delta(T)\leq d
is unitarily equivalent to the restriction of the backward shift
S^{\ast}
to some of its invariant closed subspaces. By Theorem
1.2
, the backward shift
S^{\ast}:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
does not have non-trivial minimal invariant closed subspaces of dimension greater than
1
. Hence, we have

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
H
be a separable Hilbert space. Let
T:H\to H
be a contraction such that
\delta(T)<\infty
. Then there exists a closed subspace
M\subsetneq H
,
M\neq\{0\}
such that
T(M)\subseteq M
.

2.
Preliminaries

Let
d
be a positive integer and let
\mathbb{H}
be the Hilbert space given by the direct sum of
d
copies of the Hardy space
H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
. It is convenient to write

\mathbb{H}=H^{2}(\mathbb{D}){\sf e}_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus H^{2}(\mathbb{D}){\sf e}_{d},

where
{\sf e}_{j}=(0,\ldots,0,1,0,\ldots,0)
, with
1
in the
j
-th position,
j=1,\ldots,d
.

The elements of
\mathbb{H}
are given by
\sum_{j=1}^{d}f_{j}{\sf e}_{j}
where
f_{j}\in H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
,
j=1,\ldots,d
. The vector space
\mathbb{H}
is a (separable) Hilbert space with Hermitian product given by

\langle\sum_{j=1}^{d}f_{j}{\sf e}_{j},\sum_{j=1}^{d}g_{j}{\sf e}_{j}\rangle:=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\langle f_{j},g_{j}\rangle_{H^{2}(\mathbb{D})}.

The shift operator
S:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
is defined as

S(f_{1}(z){\sf e}_{1}+\ldots+f_{d}(z){\sf e}_{d}):=zf_{1}(z){\sf e}_{1}+\ldots+zf_{d}(z){\sf e}_{d}.

As a convenient notation, throughout the paper the shift operator for
d=1
,
i.e.
,
\mathbb{H}=H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
, will be denoted by
S_{1}
,
i.e.
,
S_{1}(f(z)):=zf(z)
,
f\in H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
.

As customary, if
X
is a Hilbert space and
T:X\to X
is a bounded linear operator, we say that a closed subspace
Y\subset X
is
T
-invariant if
T(Y)\subseteq Y
.

Given a bounded function
g\in H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
, if
V\subset\mathbb{H}
is a closed subspace, we denote by
gV
the image of
V
via the continuous linear operator of
\mathbb{H}
into
\mathbb{H}
given by

f_{1}{\sf e}_{1}+\ldots+f_{d}{\sf e}_{d}\mapsto gf_{1}{\sf e}_{1}+\ldots+gf_{d}{\sf e}_{d}.

In general,
gV
is not closed. However, if
g
is
inner
(that is,
g:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}
is a holomoprhic function such that
\lim_{r\to 1^{-}}|g(re^{i\theta})|=1
for a.e.
\theta\in[0,2\pi]
), then the operator
\mathbb{H}\ni F\mapsto gF
is an isometry and if
V
is closed subspace of
\mathbb{H}
, then so is
gV
.

The algebra of inner functions is well studied (see,
e.g.
,
[
7
]
and references therein). As customary, we say that a inner function
\phi
is
invertible
or
constant
if
\phi(z)=\lambda
for all
z\in\mathbb{D}
and some unimodular constant
\lambda
. We say that an inner function
\phi
divides a inner function
\varphi
provided
\frac{\phi}{\varphi}
is a inner function. Given any family
\{\phi_{j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{I}}
of inner functions, there exists a inner function
\phi
, unique up to multiplication by a unimodular constant, such that
\phi
divides
\phi_{j}
for all
j\in\mathcal{I}
and, if
\varphi
is a inner function that divides all elements of
\{\phi_{j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{I}}
, then
\varphi
divides
\phi
. The function
\phi
is called the
greatest common divisor
of
\{\phi_{j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{I}}
—strictly speaking,
\phi
is not unique, which introduces some ambiguity in the choice. However, since it is unique up to multiplication by a unimodular constant, this ambiguity is inconsequential. Two inner functions are
coprime
if their greatest common divisor is
1
.

By the classical Beurling theorem
[
5
]
(or see,
e.g.
,
[
7
,
9
]
), a closed subspace
V
of
H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
is
S_{1}
-invariant if and only if there exists
\phi\in H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
an inner function, such that
V=\phi H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
.

The following lemma is well known, and we omit the proof:

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\varphi
be an inner function. Suppose that
\dim(\varphi H^{2}(\mathbb{D}))^{\perp}\geq 2
. Then there exists a not constant inner function
\tilde{\varphi}
such that
\tilde{\varphi}
divides
\varphi
and
\varphi
does not divide
\tilde{\varphi}
. In particular,
\varphi H^{2}(\mathbb{D})\subsetneq\tilde{\varphi}H^{2}(\mathbb{D})\subsetneq H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
.

We will make use of the inner-outer factorization of a holomorphic function (see,
e.g.
,
[
9
]
):

Definition 2.2
.

For any
f\in H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
,
f\not\equiv 0
, we write
f=I(f)O(f)
for the inner-outer factorization, where
I(f)
is inner and
O(f)\in H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
is outer.

For the aim of this paper, the only relevant property of an outer function is that it is
S_{1}
cyclic. That is, if
O\in H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
is an outer function,

\overline{\hbox{span}\{S^{n}(O):n\in\mathbb{N}\}}^{H^{2}(\mathbb{D})}=\overline{\{pO:p\hbox{ polynomial}\}}=H^{2}(\mathbb{D}).

In the following sections, we will need the lemma below, for which we provide a proof due to the lack of a direct reference.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{m}
be inner functions. Let
\phi
be the (inner function) greatest common divisor of
\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{m}
. Then there exist
m
sequences
\{h^{j}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
,
j=1,\ldots,m,
such that
\{\sum_{j=1}^{m}h^{j}_{n}\phi_{j}\}
converges to
\phi
in
H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
.

Proof.

We have that
V:=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\phi_{j}H^{2}(\mathbb{D})\subset\phi H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
. Thus
\overline{V}^{H^{2}(\mathbb{D})}\subseteq\phi H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
. Since
V
is
S_{1}
-invariant, by Beurling Theorem there exists a inner function
\varphi
such that
\overline{V}^{H^{2}(\mathbb{D})}=\varphi H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
. Hence,
\varphi
divides
\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{m}
. In particular,
\varphi
divides
\phi
. Thus,

\overline{V}^{H^{2}(\mathbb{D})}\subseteq\phi H^{2}(\mathbb{D})\subseteq\varphi H^{2}(\mathbb{D})=\overline{V}^{H^{2}(\mathbb{D})},

and the result follows.
∎

3.
Closed
S
-invariant spaces associated to matrices

In this section we associate to every
d\times d
matrix whose entries are in
H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
a closed
S
-invariant subspace of
\mathbb{H}
. We start with some general preliminaries.

Definition 3.1
.

For positive integers
m,n
, we denote by
\mathcal{M}_{\infty}(m\times n)
the set of all
m\times n
matrices whose entries are in
H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
. In other words,
B\in\mathcal{M}_{\infty}(m\times n)
if there exist
b_{jk}\in H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
,
j=1,\ldots,m
,
k=1,\ldots,n
, such that

B=\left(\begin{matrix}b_{11}&\ldots&b_{1n}\\
\ldots&\ldots&\ldots\\
b_{m1}&\ldots&b_{mn}\\
\end{matrix}\right).

For square matrices we can associate certain operators which will be crucial for our characterization of closed
S
-invariant spaces:

Definition 3.2
.

Let
m\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
. Let
j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
and let
J=\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{m}\}
with
1\leq j_{1}<\ldots<j_{m}\leq d
. Let
B\in\mathcal{M}_{\infty}(m\times m)
. The
(j,J)
-determinantal operator associated to
B
is the operator
L_{B,j,J}:\mathbb{H}\to H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
defined as follows:

L_{B,j,J}(f_{1}{\sf e}_{1}+\ldots+f_{d}{\sf e}_{d}):=\det\left(\begin{matrix}b_{11}&\ldots&b_{1m}\\
\ldots&\ldots&\ldots\\
b_{(j-1)1}&\ldots&b_{(j-1)m}\\
f_{j_{1}}&\ldots&f_{j_{m}}\\
b_{(j+1)1}&\ldots&b_{(j+1)m}\\
\ldots&\ldots&\ldots\\
b_{m1}&\ldots&b_{mm}\\
\end{matrix}\right)

In case
m=d
(hence
J=\{1,\ldots,d\}
) we simply denote
L_{B,j,J}
by
L_{B,j}
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
m\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
. Let
B\in\mathcal{M}_{\infty}(m\times m)
. For every
j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
and
J=\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{m}\}
with
1\leq j_{1}<\ldots<j_{m}\leq d
, the operator
L_{B,j,J}:\mathbb{H}\to H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
is continuous and
L_{B,j,J}\circ S=S_{1}\circ L_{B,j,J}
.

Proof.

Let
F_{1},F_{2}\in\mathbb{H}
. Since the determinant is
m
-multilinear on the rows, it follows that
L_{B,j,J}(F_{1}+F_{2})=L_{B,j,J}(F_{1})+L_{B,j,J}(F_{2})
and
L_{B,j,J}(zF_{1})=zL_{B,j,J}(F_{1})
. Therefore the operator
L_{B,j,J}
is linear and intertwines
S
and
S_{1}
. Finally, using the Laplace expansion of the determinant with respect to the
j
-th row, we see that, if
F=f_{1}{\sf e}_{1}+\ldots+f_{d}{\sf e}_{d}\in\mathbb{H}
,

(3.1)

L_{B,j,J}(F)=\sum_{k=1}^{d}(-1)^{k+j}f_{j_{k}}\det(B^{jk}),

where
B^{jk}
is the
(m-1)\times(m-1)
matrix obtained from
B
by removing the
j
-th row and the
k
-th column. Note that
\det(B^{jk})
is a polynomial in the
b_{lm}
’s. Since
b_{lm}\in H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
, it follows that there exists
C>0
such that
\|\det(B^{jk})\|_{\infty}\leq C
for all
k=1,\ldots,m
. Therefore,

\|L_{B,j,J}(F)\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{D})}\leq C\sum_{k=1}^{m}\|f_{j_{k}}\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{D})}\leq C\sqrt{m}\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{m}\|f_{j_{k}}\|^{2}_{H^{2}(\mathbb{D})}}\leq C\sqrt{m}\|F\|_{\mathbb{H}},

hence
L_{B,j,J}
is bounded.
∎

A first consequence of this lemma, recalling the definition of determinantal subspaces from the introduction is the following:

Corollary 3.4
.

Every determinantal subspace of
\mathbb{H}
is closed and
S
-invariant.

Now we go on with our construction:

Definition 3.5
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{M}_{\infty}(d\times d)
. Let
\mathcal{A}(A)=\{a_{jk}:a_{jk}\not\equiv 0\}
. If
A
is not the zero matrix—hence
\mathcal{A}(A)\neq\emptyset
—we denote by
\phi_{A}
the (inner function) greatest common divisor of
\{I(a_{jk}):a_{jk}\in\mathcal{A}(A)\}
. We call
\phi_{A}
the
inner greatest common divisor of
A
.

Finally for every
j,k\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
let

\hat{a}_{jk}:=\frac{a_{jk}}{\phi_{A}}.

Note that
\hat{a}_{jk}\in H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
for all
j,k=1,\ldots,d
.

Definition 3.6
.

We call the matrix
\hat{A}:=(\hat{a}_{jk})_{j,k=1,\ldots,d}\in\mathcal{M}_{\infty}(d\times d)
the
reduced matrix
of
A
.

3.1.
Case
\det A\not\equiv 0

Let
A\in\mathcal{M}_{\infty}(d\times d)
and assume
\det A\not\equiv 0
. Clearly
\det\hat{A}\not\equiv 0
. Hence, it is well defined

\varphi_{A}:=I(\det\hat{A}),

the inner factor of
\det\hat{A}
.

Recall that, if
L:\mathbb{H}\to H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
is a linear operator and
V\subseteq H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
, the fiber of
L
over
V
is defined as

L^{-1}(V):=\{F\in\mathbb{H}:L(F)\in V\}.

Clearly, if
L
is continuous and
V
is closed in
H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
, then
L^{-1}(V)
is closed in
\mathbb{H}
.

Definition 3.7
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{M}_{\infty}(d\times d)
, and assume
\det A\not\equiv 0
. We let

\mathcal{N}_{A}:=\phi_{A}\left(\bigcap_{j=1}^{d}L_{\hat{A},j}^{-1}(\varphi_{A}H^{2}(\mathbb{D}))\right),

and call it the
closed
S
-invariant space associated to
A
.

With the notation introduced in the Introduction,

\mathcal{N}_{A}=\phi_{A}\mathcal{Q}_{\varphi_{A}},

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{M}_{\infty}(d\times d)
,
\det A\equiv 0
. Suppose
A
is not the identically zero matrix. Let
J
be a good multi-index of
\hat{A}
. Then
\dim(\mathcal{N}_{A,J})^{\perp}=\infty
and there exists a closed
S
-invariant subspace
M\subsetneq\mathbb{H}
such that
\mathcal{N}_{A,J}\subsetneq M
.

Proof.

Let
A=(a_{jm})
,
j,m\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
and
a_{jm}\in H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
.

Let
k\in\{1,\ldots,d-1\}
be the rank of
A
–and hence of
\hat{A}
. In order to simplify notation, and without loss of generality, we can assume that
J=\{1,\ldots,k\}
is a good multi-index of
\hat{A}
and that
J\in\mathcal{J}_{J}(\hat{A})
. Thus,
\det\hat{A}^{J,J}\not\equiv 0
.

Now, for every
\theta\in H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
,

(4.1)

\det\left(\begin{matrix}0&\ldots&0&\theta\\
\hat{a}_{11}&\ldots&\hat{a}_{1k}&\hat{a}_{1d}\\
\vdots&\ldots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\hat{a}_{k1}&\ldots&\hat{a}_{kk}&\hat{a}_{kd}\end{matrix}\right)=(-1)^{k}\theta\det\hat{A}^{J,J}.

In particular, if
\theta\not\equiv 0
, by (
3.3
),
\theta{\sf e}_{d}\not\in\mathcal{R}(A)
and, hence
\mathcal{R}(A)\neq\mathbb{H}
. Note that this also implies that
\dim(\mathbb{H}/\mathcal{R}(A))=\infty
—hence,
\dim(\mathcal{R}(A))^{\perp}=\infty
and hence
\dim(\mathcal{N}_{A,J})^{\perp}=\infty
—because
[\theta{\sf e}_{d}]=[0]
in
\mathbb{H}/\mathcal{R}(A)
if and only if
\theta\equiv 0
and thus
H^{2}(\mathbb{D})\ni\theta\mapsto[\theta{\sf e}_{d}]\in\mathbb{H}/\mathcal{R}(A)
is a linear injective operator.

We know that
\mathcal{N}_{A,J}\subseteq\phi_{A}\mathcal{R}(A)
and, clearly,
\phi_{A}\mathcal{R}(A)\subseteq\mathcal{R}(A)
. Thus, if
\mathcal{N}_{A,J}\neq\mathcal{R}(A)
, we can take
M=\mathcal{R}(A)
and we are done since
\mathcal{R}(A)
is a closed
S
-invariant subspace of
\mathbb{H}
. Therefore, we can assume that

\mathcal{N}_{A,J}=\mathcal{R}(A).

Now, let
\theta
be an inner function and consider the matrix
B\in\mathcal{M}_{\infty}(d\times d)
given by

B:=\left(\begin{matrix}0&\ldots&0&\theta\\
\hat{a}_{11}&\ldots&\hat{a}_{1,d-1}&\hat{a}_{1d}\\
\vdots&\ldots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\hat{a}_{d-1,1}&\ldots&\hat{a}_{d-1,d-1}&\hat{a}_{d-1,d}\end{matrix}\right).

By (
4.1
), the rank of
B
is
k+1
.

1. Case

.

We claim that
\mathcal{R}(A)\subsetneq\mathcal{R}(B)\subsetneq\mathbb{H}
. If this is so, taking into account that
\mathcal{R}(B)
is closed and
S
-invariant, we can take
M=\mathcal{R}(B)
and we are done. Indeed, arguing as before, we see that
\mathcal{R}(B)\subsetneq\mathbb{H}
. On the other hand, let
f_{1}{\sf e}_{1}+\ldots+f_{d}{\sf e}_{d}\in\mathcal{R}(A)
. By (
3.3
), we have for
m=k+1,\ldots,d-1
,

\det\left(\begin{matrix}f_{1}&\ldots&f_{k}&f_{m}&f_{d}\\
0&\ldots&0&0&\theta\\
\hat{a}_{11}&\ldots&\hat{a}_{1k}&\hat{a}_{1m}&\hat{a}_{1d}\\
\vdots&\ldots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\hat{a}_{k1}&\ldots&\hat{a}_{kk}&a_{km}&\hat{a}_{k,d}\end{matrix}\right)=(-1)^{k}\theta\det\left(\begin{matrix}f_{1}&\ldots&f_{k}&f_{m}\\

\vdots&\ldots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\hat{a}_{k1}&\ldots&\hat{a}_{kk}&a_{km}\end{matrix}\right)\equiv 0,

which, by (
4.1
) and Kronecker’s Theorem (see Remark
3.9
) implies that

\hbox{rank}\left(\begin{matrix}f_{1}&\ldots&f_{d-1}&f_{d}\\
0&\ldots&0&\theta\\
\hat{a}_{11}&\ldots&\hat{a}_{1,d-1}&\hat{a}_{1d}\\
\vdots&\ldots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\hat{a}_{d-1,1}&\ldots&a_{d-1,d-1}&\hat{a}_{d-1,d}\end{matrix}\right)=k+1,

that is,
f_{1}{\sf e}_{1}+\ldots+f_{d}{\sf e}_{d}\in\mathcal{R}(B)
. Hence,
\mathcal{R}(A)\subset\mathcal{R}(B)
. It is clear that
\mathcal{R}(A)\neq\mathcal{R}(B)
, since
\theta{\sf e}_{d}\in\mathcal{R}(B)
but
\theta{\sf e}_{d}\not\in\mathcal{R}(A)
.

1. Case

.

Since
k=d-1
, we have that
J=\{1,\ldots,d-1\}
(and we are assuming

). Let
\theta
be a non-invertible inner function coprime with the inner part
I(\det\hat{A}^{J,J})
of

(for instance, if

and for some

, we can take
\theta(z)=\frac{z_{0}-z}{1-\overline{z_{0}}z}
,

). Let

is a determinantal space, so it is closed and
S
-invariant. We have

Case 2.
For all
j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
either
\theta_{j}
is a Blaschke product with a simple pole or
\theta_{j}
is invertible.

Since
L_{\hat{A},d}^{-1}(\varphi_{A}H^{2}(\mathbb{D}))\neq\mathbb{H}
, there exists at least one
j
such that
\theta_{j}
is not invertible. To simplify readability, we assume that
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{m}
are Blaschke products with a simple pole and
\theta_{m+1},\ldots,\theta_{d}
are invertible, for some
1\leq m\leq d
(the other cases are similar).

Let
Q:=L_{\hat{A},d}^{-1}(\varphi_{A}H^{2}(\mathbb{D}))
. By (
4.4
),
Z\subseteq Q
. Since
Z
is an orthogonal direct sum, it is easy to see that there is a natural isometrical isomorphim between
\mathbb{H}/Z
and

E:=\left(H^{2}(\mathbb{D})/(\theta_{1}H^{2}(\mathbb{D}))\right){\sf e}_{1}+\ldots+\left(H^{2}(\mathbb{D})/(\theta_{m}H^{2}(\mathbb{D}))\right){\sf e}_{m},

given by

E\ni[f_{1}]{\sf e}_{1}+\ldots+[f_{m}]{\sf e}_{m}\mapsto[f_{1}{\sf e}_{1}+\ldots+f_{m}{\sf e}_{m}]\in\mathbb{H}/Z.

Since
\theta_{j}
is a Blaschke product with a simple pole it follows that
H^{2}(\mathbb{D})/(\theta_{j}H^{2}(\mathbb{D}))
is a one-dimensional space for
j=1,\ldots,m
,. Hence,
\dim\mathbb{H}/Z=\dim E=m
. Taking into account that
\mathbb{H}/Z
is isometrically isomorphic to
Z^{\perp}
, it follows that
\dim Z^{\perp}=m
.

From
Z\subseteq Q
, we have that
Q^{\perp}\subseteq Z^{\perp}
, hence,
\dim Q^{\perp}\leq m
. Note that
Q^{\perp}\neq\{0\}
(because
Q\neq\mathbb{H}
) and
Q^{\perp}
is
S^{\ast}
-invariant (because
Q
is
S
-invariant).

Therefore,
S^{\ast}|_{Q^{\perp}}:Q^{\perp}\to Q^{\perp}
is a linear endomorphism of a finite dimensional space and
2\leq\dim Q^{\perp}\leq m
. Hence, there exists a subspace
V\neq\{0\}
such that
V\subsetneq Q^{\perp}
and
S^{\ast}(V)\subseteq V
. Given such a
V
, we let
M=V^{\perp}
. Thus,
M
is a closed
S
-invariant subspace of
\mathbb{H}
,
Q\subsetneq M
and
M\neq\mathbb{H}
.
∎

5.
The Beurling-Lax matrix of an invariant subspace

Let
M\subset\mathbb{H}
be a closed
S
-invariant subspace. By the Beurling-Lax Theorem
[
5
,
13
]
(see also,
e.g.

[
18
, Section 1.12]
), there exists a bounded linear operator
Q:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
(called an
S
-inner operator
) such that:

(1)

there is a closed
S
-invariant orthogonal decomposition
\mathbb{H}=I_{Q}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\perp}}{{\oplus}}\ker Q
,

(2)

A|_{I_{Q}}:I_{Q}\to Q(I_{Q})=Q(\mathbb{H})=M
is an isometry,

(3)

Q\circ S=S\circ Q
.

The following proposition can be deduced also from
[
18
, Theorem B Section 1.15]
, but we give a proof both for the sake of completeness and in order to fix some notations.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
M\subset\mathbb{H}
be a closed
S
-invariant subspace. Then there exist
a_{jk}\in H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
, with
\|a_{jk}\|_{\infty}\leq 1
,
j,k=1,\ldots,k
, such that

\begin{split}M&=\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{d}h_{j}(a_{j1}{\sf e}_{1}+\ldots+a_{jd}{\sf e}_{d}):h_{j}\in H^{2}(\mathbb{D})\right\}\\
&=\overline{\hbox{span}\{S^{n}(a_{11}{\sf e}_{1}+\ldots+a_{1d}{\sf e}_{d}),\ldots,S^{n}(a_{d1}{\sf e}_{1}+\ldots+a_{dd}{\sf e}_{d}):n\in\mathbb{N}\}}^{\mathbb{H}}.\end{split}

Proof.

Let
Q
be a
S
-inner operator such that
M=Q(\mathbb{H})
. Let
k=1,\ldots,d
. Since
Q\circ S=S\circ Q
, it turns out that for every polynomial
q
,

Q(q{\sf e}_{k})=qQ({\sf e}_{k}).

If
f\in H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
and
\{q_{j}\}
is a sequence of polynomials converging to
f
in
H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
, since
Q
is a
S
-inner operator, we have

\begin{split}\lim_{j\to\infty}\|q_{j}Q({\sf e}_{k})-Q(f{\sf e}_{k})\|_{\mathbb{H}}&=\lim_{j\to\infty}\|Q(q_{j}{\sf e}_{k}-f{\sf e}_{k})\|_{\mathbb{H}}\\
&\leq\lim_{j\to\infty}\|q_{j}{\sf e}_{k}-f{\sf e}_{k}\|_{\mathbb{H}}=\lim_{j\to\infty}\|q_{j}-f\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{D})}=0.\end{split}

Therefore
\{q_{j}Q({\sf e}_{k})\}
converges to
Q(f{\sf e}_{k})
in
\mathbb{H}
.

Now, as a matter of notation, if
F\in\mathbb{H}
, we write
F=p_{1}(F){\sf e}_{1}+\ldots+p_{d}(F){\sf e}_{d}
. Note that, if
g\in H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
and
gF\in\mathbb{H}
then
p_{m}(gF)=gp_{m}(F)
,
m=1,\ldots,d
.

Since
\{q_{j}Q({\sf e}_{k})\}
converges to
Q(f{\sf e}_{k})
in
\mathbb{H}
it follows that
\{p_{m}(q_{j}Q({\sf e}_{k}))\}
converges in
H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
to
p_{m}(Q(f{\sf e}_{k}))
,
m=1,\ldots,d
. Therefore,
\{p_{m}(q_{j}Q({\sf e}_{k}))\}
converges uniformly on compacta to
p_{m}(Q(f{\sf e}_{k}))
.

Now we show that
\{p_{m}(q_{j}Q({\sf e}_{k}))\}
converges uniformly on compacta of
\mathbb{D}
to
p_{m}(fQ({\sf e}_{k}))
,
m=1,\ldots,d
, from which it follows that
Q(f{\sf e}_{k})=fQ({\sf e}_{k})
. Indeed, let
K\subset\subset\mathbb{D}
. Let
C_{m}:=\max_{\zeta\in K}|p_{m}(Q({\sf e}_{k}))(\zeta)|
,
m=1,\ldots,d
. Since
\{q_{j}\}
converges uniformly to
f
on
K
, we have

\begin{split}&\max_{z\in K}|p_{m}(q_{j}(z)Q({\sf e}_{k})(z))-p_{m}(f(z)Q({\sf e}_{k})(z))|\\
&=\max_{z\in K}|q_{j}(z)p_{m}(Q({\sf e}_{k})(z))-f(z)p_{m}(Q({\sf e}_{k})(z))|\leq C_{m}\max_{z\in K}|q_{j}(z)-f(z)|\to 0.\end{split}

Therefore,

(5.1)

Q(f{\sf e}_{k})=fQ({\sf e}_{k})\quad\forall f\in H^{2}(\mathbb{D}),k=1,\ldots,d.

Now, for
j=1,\ldots,d
, we write

Q({\sf e}_{j})=a_{j1}{\sf e}_{1}+\ldots+a_{jd}{\sf e}_{d}.

By (
5.1
), for all
f\in H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
,

p_{1}(Q(f{\sf e}_{1}))=fa_{11}.

Hence, taking into account that
Q
is
S
-inner we have for every
f\in H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
,

(5.2)

\begin{split}\|fa_{11}\|^{2}_{H^{2}(\mathbb{D})}&\leq\|fa_{11}\|^{2}_{H^{2}(\mathbb{D})}+\ldots+\|fa_{1d}\|^{2}_{H^{2}(\mathbb{D})}=\|Q(f{\sf e}_{1})\|^{2}_{\mathbb{H}}\\
&\leq\|f{\sf e}_{1}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{H}}=\|f\|^{2}_{H^{2}(\mathbb{D})}.\end{split}

Therefore, the multiplication operator
H^{2}(\mathbb{D})\ni f\mapsto a_{11}f\in H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
is continuous and with (operator) norm
\leq 1
. By a standard result,
a_{11}\in H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
and
\|a_{11}\|_{\infty}\leq 1
. A similar argument works for
a_{jk}
,
j,k\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
.

Now, every
f\in M
is given by
f=Q(h)
for some
h\in\mathbb{H}
(actually,
h\in I_{A}
). If we write
h=h_{1}{\sf e}_{1}+\ldots+h_{d}{\sf e}_{d}
, we have

f=Q(h)=h_{1}Q({\sf e}_{1})+\ldots+h_{d}A({\sf e}_{d})=\sum_{j=1}^{d}h_{j}(a_{j1}{\sf e}_{1}+\ldots a_{jd}{\sf e}_{d}),

and this proves the first part of the formula. As for the second, by Beurling theorem,

\mathbb{H}=\overline{\hbox{span}\{S^{n}({\sf e}_{1}),\ldots,S^{n}({\sf e}_{d}):n\in\mathbb{N}\}}^{\mathbb{H}}.

Since
Q\circ S=S\circ Q
, we have

Q(\mathbb{H})=\overline{\hbox{span}\{S^{n}(Q({\sf e}_{1})),\ldots,S^{n}(Q({\sf e}_{d})):n\in\mathbb{N}\}}^{\mathbb{H}},

and we are done.
∎

There are a couple of interesting corollaries we need. Before that, we need a definition.
Let

\mathbb{H}_{\infty}:=\{f_{1}{\sf e}_{1}+\ldots+f_{d}{\sf e}_{d}:f_{j}\in H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D}),j=1,\ldots,d\}.

Corollary 5.2
.

Let
M,N
be two closed
S
-invariant subspaces of
\mathbb{H}
. Then
M\subseteq N
if and only if
M\cap\mathbb{H}_{\infty}\subseteq N
.

Proof.

Assume first that
M\cap\mathbb{H}_{\infty}\subseteq N
. Let
\{a_{jk}\}_{j,k=1,\ldots,d}
be the bounded functions given by Proposition
5.1
for
M
. Hence
a_{j1}{\sf e}_{1}+\ldots+a_{jd}{\sf e}_{d}\in N
for
j=1,\ldots,d
. Since
N
is
S
-invariant and by Proposition
5.1
, we have

M=\overline{\hbox{span}\{S^{n}(a_{11}{\sf e}_{1}+\ldots+a_{1d}{\sf e}_{d}),\ldots,S^{n}(a_{d1}{\sf e}_{1}+\ldots+a_{dd}{\sf e}_{d}):n\in\mathbb{N}\}}^{\mathbb{H}}\subseteq N.

The other implication of the corollary is trivial.
∎

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
M
be a
S
-invariant subspace of
\mathbb{H}
. Let
O\in H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
be outer. Then

\overline{OM}^{\mathbb{H}}=M.

Proof.

First we show that
\overline{OM}^{\mathbb{H}}\subseteq M
. Since
M
is closed, it is enough to show that
OM\subseteq M
. Let
F\in M
. By Proposition
5.1
, there exist sequences of polynomials
\{p_{n,j}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
,
j=1,\ldots,d
such that
F
is the limit in
\mathbb{H}
of
the sequence
\{Q_{n}:=\sum_{j=1}^{d}p_{n,j}(a_{j1}{\sf e}_{1}+\ldots+a_{jd}{\sf e}_{j})\}
. Now, again by Proposition
5.1
,

OQ_{n}=O\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}p_{n,j}(a_{j1}{\sf e}_{1}+\ldots+a_{jd}{\sf e}_{j})\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}p_{n,j}\left(O(a_{j1}{\sf e}_{1}+\ldots+a_{jd}{\sf e}_{j})\right)\in M,

therefore
OQ_{n}\in M
for every
n
. Since
\|OQ_{n}-OF\|_{\mathbb{H}}\leq\|O\|_{\infty}\|Q_{n}-F\|_{\mathbb{H}}
, it follows that
\{OQ_{n}\}\subset M
converges to
OF
in
\mathbb{H}
, hence
OF\in M
, and we are done.

Now we prove that
M\subseteq\overline{OM}^{\mathbb{H}}
. Since the multiplication by
O
commutes with
S
, we have that
OM
is
S
-invariant, and so is
\overline{OM}^{\mathbb{H}}
. Therefore, by Corollary
5.2
it is enough to show that
M\cap\mathbb{H}_{\infty}\subseteq\overline{OM}^{\mathbb{H}}
. To this aim, let
F\in M\cap\mathbb{H}_{\infty}
. Since
O
is outer, hence a cyclic vector for
S_{1}
, there exists a sequence of polynomials
\{p_{n}\}
such that
\{p_{n}O\}
converges to
1
in
H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
. Since
(p_{n}O)F=O(p_{n}F)
and
p_{n}F\in M
, it follows that
p_{n}OF\in OM
for all
n
. Now, write
F=f_{1}{\sf e}_{1}+\ldots+f_{d}{\sf e}_{d}
, with
f_{j}\in H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
,
j=1,\ldots,d
. Let
C>0
be such that
\|f_{j}\|_{\infty}<C
for
j=1,\ldots,d
. Hence,

\begin{split}\|p_{n}OF-F\|^{2}_{\mathbb{H}}&=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\|(p_{n}O-1)f_{j}\|^{2}_{H^{2}(\mathbb{D})}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{d}\|f_{j}\|^{2}_{\infty}\|p_{n}O-1\|^{2}_{H^{2}(\mathbb{D})}\\
&\leq dC^{2}\|p_{n}O-1\|^{2}_{H^{2}(\mathbb{D})}.\end{split}

Since
\lim_{n\to\infty}\|p_{n}O-1\|^{2}_{H^{2}(\mathbb{D})}=0
, it follows that
\{p_{n}OF\}
converges to
F
in
\mathbb{H}
. But
\{p_{n}OF\}\subset OM
, hence
F\in\overline{OM}^{\mathbb{H}}
, and we are done.
∎

Definition 5.4
.

Let
M\subset\mathbb{H}
be a closed
S
-invariant subspace, and let
\{a_{jk}\}_{j,k=1,\ldots,d}
be given by Proposition
5.1
. The matrix
A_{M}=(a_{jk})\in\mathcal{M}_{\infty}
is a
Beurling-Lax matrix
of
M
.

6.
The structure theorem

Let
N\subset\mathbb{H}
be a closed
S
-invariant subspace. Let
A
be a Beurling-Lax matrix associated to
N
(see Definition
5.4
). Note that
A
is the zero matrix if and only if
N=\{0\}
.

In case
A
is not the zero matrix, and
\det A\not\equiv 0
, we can associate to
A
the closed
S
-invariant subspace
\mathcal{N}_{A}
(see Definition
3.7
). In case
\det A\equiv 0
we can associate the closed
S
-invariant subspace
\mathcal{N}_{A,J}
for every good multi-index of
\hat{A}
(see Definition
3.16
).

The main result of this section (from which Theorem
1.1
follows at once) is the following:

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
\{0\}\subsetneq N\subsetneq\mathbb{H}
be a closed
S
-invariant subspace. Let
A
be a Beurling-Lax matrix associated to
N
. Then,

•

if
\det A\not\equiv 0
,

(6.1)

N=\mathcal{N}_{A}.

•

While, if
\det A\equiv 0
,

(6.2)

N=\mathcal{N}_{A,J},

for every good multi-index
J
of
\hat{A}
. In particular,
N_{A,J}=N_{A,J^{\prime}}
for every
J,J^{\prime}
good multi-indices of
\hat{A}
.

Proof.

Since
N\neq\{0\}
,
A
is not the matrix identically zero. Let
\hat{A}
be the reduced matrix of
A
(see Definition
3.6
). Hence, by Proposition
5.1

\begin{split}N&=\overline{\hbox{span}\{S^{n}(a_{11}{\sf e}_{1}+\ldots+a_{1d}{\sf e}_{d}),\ldots,S^{n}(a_{d1}{\sf e}_{1}+\ldots+a_{dd}{\sf e}_{d}):n\in\mathbb{N}\}}^{\mathbb{H}}\\
&=\overline{\hbox{span}\{\phi_{A}S^{n}(\hat{a}_{11}{\sf e}_{1}+\ldots+\hat{a}_{1d}{\sf e}_{d}),\ldots,\phi_{A}S^{n}(\hat{a}_{d1}{\sf e}_{1}+\ldots+\hat{a}_{dd}{\sf e}_{d}):n\in\mathbb{N}\}}^{\mathbb{H}}.\end{split}

Let

W:=\overline{\hbox{span}\{S^{n}(\hat{a}_{11}{\sf e}_{1}+\ldots+\hat{a}_{1d}{\sf e}_{d}),\ldots,S^{n}(\hat{a}_{d1}{\sf e}_{1}+\ldots+\hat{a}_{dd}{\sf e}_{d}):n\in\mathbb{N}\}}^{\mathbb{H}}.

Bering in mind that the multiplication by
\phi_{A}
is an isometry in
\mathbb{H}
, we have

N=\phi_{A}W.

Case A:
\det A\not\equiv 0
.

Fix
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
. Let
L_{A,j}
be the determinantal operator associated to
A
(see Definition
3.2
). Since
S_{1}\circ L_{A,j}=L_{A,j}\circ S
, it follows that, for
k\in\{1,\ldots,d\}\setminus\{j\}
,

L_{A,j}\left(\hbox{span}\{S^{n}(\hat{a}_{k1}{\sf e}_{1}+\ldots+\hat{a}_{kd}{\sf e}_{d}):n\in\mathbb{N}\}\right)=\{0\},

hence, since
L_{A,j}
is continuous,

L_{A,j}(W)=L_{A,j}\left(\overline{\hbox{span}\{S^{n}(\hat{a}_{j1}{\sf e}_{1}+\ldots+\hat{a}_{jd}{\sf e}_{d}):n\in\mathbb{N}\}}^{\mathbb{H}}\right).

Now, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have

L_{A_{j}}(S^{n}(\hat{a}_{j1}{\sf e}_{1}+\ldots+\hat{a}_{jd}{\sf e}_{d}))=S_{1}^{n}(\det\hat{A}).

Recall that, by definition (and since
\det\hat{A}\not\equiv 0
)
\varphi_{A}
is the inner part of
\det\hat{A}
. We let
O
to be the outer part of
\det\hat{A}
. Therefore, by Beurling theorem,

\begin{split}\overline{L_{A_{j}}(W)}^{H^{2}(\mathbb{D})}&=\overline{L_{A,j}\left(\overline{\hbox{span}\{S^{n}(\varphi_{A}O):n\in\mathbb{N}\}}^{\mathbb{H}}\right)}^{H^{2}(\mathbb{D})}\\
&=\overline{\{\hbox{span}\{S_{1}^{n}(\varphi_{A}O):n\in\mathbb{N}\}}^{H^{2}(\mathbb{D})}=\varphi_{A}H^{2}(\mathbb{D}).\end{split}

From this it follows that
W\subseteq L_{A,j}^{-1}(\varphi_{A}H^{2}(\mathbb{D}))
and then
N\subseteq\phi_{A}L_{A,j}^{-1}(\varphi_{A}H^{2}(\mathbb{D}))
,
j=1\ldots,d
, that is,
N\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{A}
.

Now, we show that
\mathcal{N}_{A}\subseteq N
. Since
N=\phi_{A}W
and
\mathcal{N}_{A}=\phi_{A}\bigcap_{j=1}^{d}L_{A,j}^{-1}(\varphi_{A}H^{2}(\mathbb{D}))
, it is enough to show that

Z:=\bigcap_{j=1}^{d}L_{A,j}^{-1}(\varphi_{A}H^{2}(\mathbb{D}))\subseteq W.

To this aim, let
F=f_{1}{\sf e}_{1}+\ldots f_{d}{\sf e}_{d}\in\mathbb{H}
. By (
3.1
),
F\in Z
if and only if

L_{A,j}(F)=\sum_{k=1}^{d}(-1)^{k+j}f_{k}\det(\hat{A}^{jk})\in\varphi_{A}H^{2}(\mathbb{D}),\quad j=1,\ldots d.

Since by Beurling theorem the closure of
\hbox{span}\{S_{1}^{n}(\varphi_{A}O):n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is
\varphi_{A}H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
, there exist sequences of polynomials
\{p_{nj}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
,
j=1,\ldots,d
, such that

(6.3)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|\varphi_{A}Op_{nj}-\sum_{k=1}^{d}(-1)^{k+j}f_{k}\det(\hat{A}^{jk})\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{D})}=0,\quad j=1,\ldots,d.

Taking into account that
\hat{a}_{jk}\in H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
,
j,k\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
—and hence if a sequence
\{h_{n}\}\subset H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
converges to
h
in
H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
then
\{\hat{a}_{jk}h_{n}\}
converges to
\hat{a}_{jk}h
in
H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
—from (
6.3
) we have for every
m=1,\ldots,d

(6.4)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|\sum_{j=1}^{d}\hat{a}_{jm}\varphi_{A}Op_{nj}-\sum_{k=1}^{d}f_{k}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}(-1)^{k+j}\hat{a}_{jm}\det(\hat{A}^{jk})\right)\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{D})}=0.

Now we claim that

(6.5)

\sum_{j=1}^{d}(-1)^{k+j}\hat{a}_{jm}\det(\hat{A}^{jk})=\begin{cases}\varphi_{A}O&k=m\\
0&k\neq m\end{cases}

Assuming the claim for the moment, from (
6.4
) we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|\varphi_{A}\left(O\sum_{j=1}^{d}p_{nj}\hat{a}_{jk}-Of_{m}\right)\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{D})}=0,\quad m=1,\ldots,d.

Since
\varphi_{A}
is inner, this implies that
\{O\sum_{j=1}^{d}p_{nj}\hat{a}_{jm}\}
converges to
Of_{m}
in
H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
,
m=1,\ldots,d
. In other words, if we let

Q_{n}:=p_{n1}(\hat{a}_{11}{\sf e}_{1}+\ldots+\hat{a}_{1d}{\sf e}_{d})+\ldots+p_{nd}(\hat{a}_{d1}{\sf e}_{1}+\ldots+\hat{a}_{dd}{\sf e}_{d}),

then
\{OQ_{n}\}
converges to
O(f_{1}{\sf e}_{1}+\ldots+f_{d}{\sf e}_{d})=OF
in
\mathbb{H}
. Note that
Q_{n}\in W
, so that
OQ_{n}\in OW
for all
n
. By Proposition
5.3
,
OW\subseteq W
, hence,
OQ_{n}\in W
for all
n
. It follows that
OF\in W
. By the arbitrariness of
F
, this means that
OZ\subset W
and, since
W
is closed,
\overline{OZ}^{\mathbb{H}}\subseteq W
. Since
Z
is
S
-invariant, by Proposition
5.3
we have
Z=\overline{OZ}^{\mathbb{H}}
, hence
Z\subset W
as wanted.

We are thus left to prove (
6.5
). Let

U:=\{z\in\mathbb{D}:\det\hat{A}(z)\neq 0\}.

Since
\det A\not\equiv 0
—hence
\det\hat{A}\not\equiv 0
—the set
U
is open, connected and dense in
\mathbb{D}
. Let
B
the
d\times d
matrix whose
(j,k)
entry is
(-1)^{j+k}\det\hat{A}^{kj}
.
Then it follows from classical linear algebra that, for all
z\in U

\hat{A}(z)\cdot B(z)=(\det\hat{A}(z)){\sf Id},

where
{\sf Id}
is the
d\times d
identity matrix. Expanding the previous equality one obtains (
6.5
) for all
z\in U
. By analytic extension, it holds thus in
\mathbb{D}
and we are done.

Case B:
\det A\equiv 0
.

Let

T(A):=\left(\bigcap_{S\in\mathcal{J}_{J}(\hat{A}),j=1,\ldots,k}L^{-1}_{\hat{A}^{S,J},j,J}(\varphi_{J,A}H^{2}(\mathbb{D}))\right)\cap\mathcal{R}(A).

Since

\mathcal{N}_{A,J}=\phi_{A}T(A)

(see Definition
3.16
) and
N=\phi_{A}W
, it is enough to show that
W=T(A)
.

To simplify the readability, we can assume that
J=\{1,\ldots,k\}
. This would not affect the proof since, switching the columns
j
and
m
in
\hat{A}
corresponds to see the problem under the isometric automorphism of
\mathbb{H}
which switches the coefficients of
{\sf e}_{j}
and
{\sf e}_{m}
.

Now, we claim that
W\subseteq T(A)
. To this aim, because of the (multi-)linearity of the determinant, it is enough to show that
\underline{\hat{a}}_{m}:=\hat{a}_{m1}{\sf e}_{1}+\ldots+\hat{a}_{md}{\sf e}_{d}\in T(A)
for
m=1,\ldots,d
. So, fix
m\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
. Obviously,
\underline{\hat{a}}_{m}\in\mathcal{R}(A)
. Next, let
S=\{1\leq s_{1}<\ldots<s_{k}\leq d\}\in\mathcal{J}_{J}(\hat{A})
and
j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
. We need to show that
L_{\hat{A}^{S,J},j,J}(\underline{\hat{a}}_{m})\in\varphi_{J,A}H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
. Now,

L_{\hat{A}^{S,J},j,J}(\underline{\hat{a}}_{m})=\det\left(\begin{matrix}\hat{a}_{s_{1}1}&\ldots&\hat{a}_{s_{1}k}\\
\ldots&\ldots&\ldots\\
\hat{a}_{s_{j-1}1}&\ldots&\hat{a}_{s_{j-1}k}\\
\hat{a}_{m1}&\ldots&\hat{a}_{mk}\\
\hat{a}_{s_{j+1}1}&\ldots&\hat{a}_{s_{j+1}k}\\
\ldots&\ldots&\ldots\\
\hat{a}_{s_{k}1}&\ldots&\hat{a}_{s_{k}k}\\
\end{matrix}\right).

There are two possibilities: either the determinant is identically zero—so that
L_{\hat{A}^{S,J},j,J}(\underline{\hat{a}}_{m})\in\varphi_{J,A}H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
—or it is not identically zero. In the latter case it means that, up to reordering in increasing way, the multi-index
\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{j-1},m,s_{j+1},\ldots,s_{k}\}\in\mathcal{J}_{J}(\hat{A})
. Hence, by definition of
\varphi_{J,A}
, it follows that
\varphi_{J,A}
divides the inner part of
L_{\hat{A}^{S,J},j,J}(\underline{\hat{a}}_{m})
, that is,
L_{\hat{A}^{S,J},j,J}(\underline{\hat{a}}_{m})\in\varphi_{J,A}H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
.

In order to show that
T(A)\subseteq W
, let
S=\{1\leq s_{1}<\ldots<s_{k}\leq d\}\in\mathcal{J}_{J}(\hat{A})
and let

\hat{A}^{S,J}=\left(\begin{matrix}\hat{a}_{s_{1}1}&\ldots&\hat{a}_{s_{1}k}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\hat{a}_{s_{k}1}&\ldots&\hat{a}_{s_{k}k}\\
\end{matrix}\right).

Since
\det\hat{A}^{S,J}\not\equiv 0
and
\det\hat{A}^{S,J}\in H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
, the inner part
\varphi_{S}:=I(\det\hat{A}^{S,J})
of
\det\hat{A}^{S,J}
is well defined (and
\varphi_{J,A}
divides
\varphi_{S}
by definition).

We first prove that

(6.6)

T_{S}:=\left(\bigcap_{j=1,\ldots,k}L_{\hat{A}^{S,J},j,J}^{-1}(\varphi_{S}H^{2}(\mathbb{D}))\right)\cap\mathcal{R}(A)\subset W.

Let
f_{1}{\sf e}_{1}+\ldots+f_{d}{\sf e}_{d}\in T_{S}
. Hence,

(6.7)

\hbox{rank}\left(\begin{matrix}f_{1}&\ldots&f_{d}\\
\hat{a}_{s_{1}1}&\ldots&\hat{a}_{s_{1}d}\\
\vdots&\ldots&\vdots\\
\hat{a}_{s_{k}1}&\ldots&\hat{a}_{s_{k}d}\end{matrix}\right)=k.

Let
U_{S}
be the open dense set of
\mathbb{D}
such that
\det\hat{A}^{S,J}(z)\neq 0
. Then, for every
z\in U_{S}
, by classical linear algebra, there exist
\lambda_{j}(z)\in\mathbb{C}
,
j=1,\ldots,k
such that

(6.8)

f_{j}(z)=\lambda_{1}(z)\hat{a}_{s_{1}j}(z)+\ldots+\lambda_{k}(z)\hat{a}_{s_{k}j},\quad j=1,\ldots,d.

Since
\det(\hat{A}^{S,J}(z))\neq 0
(for
z\in U_{S}
), it follows by Cramer’s rule that for
j=1,\ldots,k
,

\det(\hat{A}^{S,J}_{S}(z))\lambda_{j}(z)=\det\left(\begin{matrix}\hat{a}_{s_{1}1}&\ldots&\hat{a}_{s_{1}k}\\
\vdots&\ldots&\vdots\\
\hat{a}_{s_{j-1}1}&\ldots&\hat{a}_{s_{j-1}k}\\
f_{1}&\ldots&f_{k}\\
\hat{a}_{s_{j+1}1}&\ldots&\hat{a}_{s_{j+1}k}\\
\vdots&\ldots&\vdots\\
\hat{a}_{s_{k}1}&\ldots&\hat{a}_{s_{k}k}\end{matrix}\right).

Since
\hat{a}_{jm}\in H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
and
f_{j}\in H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
(for all indices
j,m
), the right hand side of the previous equation is in
H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
and, since
L_{\hat{A}^{S,J},j,J}(f_{1}{\sf e}_{1}+\ldots+f_{d}{\sf e}_{d})\in\varphi_{S}H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
for
j=1,\ldots,k
by hypothesis, it follows that it is actually in
\varphi_{S}H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
. Thus, if
O_{S}\in H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
denotes the outer part of
\det\hat{A}^{S,J}
, we have that
O_{S}\lambda_{j}\in H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
for
j=1,\ldots,k
.

Therefore, from (
6.8
), we have that

O_{S}(f_{1}{\sf e}_{1}+\ldots+f_{d}{\sf e}_{d})=\sum_{m=1}^{k}O_{S}\lambda_{m}(\hat{a}_{s_{m}1}{\sf e}_{1}+\ldots+\hat{a}_{s_{m}d}{\sf e}_{d})\in W.

In particular, it follows that
O_{S}T_{S}\subset W
. Taking into account that
T_{S}
is a closed,
S
-invariant subspace of
\mathbb{H}
by Lemma
3.3
and Lemma
3.12
, equation (
6.6
) follows at once from Corollary
5.3
.

Hence,

P:=\overline{\sum_{S\in\mathcal{J}_{J}(\hat{A})}T_{S}}^{\mathbb{H}}\subseteq W.

On the other hand, note that
P
is closed and
S
-invariant (since it is the closure of the sum of
S
-invariant spaces). Also, note that
\underline{\hat{a}_{m}}:=\hat{a}_{m1}{\sf e}_{1}+\ldots+\hat{a}_{md}{\sf e}_{d}
belongs to some
T_{S}
(indeed, if
m
is part of some multi-index
S\in\mathcal{J}_{J}(\hat{A})
then
\underline{\hat{a}_{m}}\in T_{S}
, while, if
m
does not belong to any multi-index
S\in\mathcal{J}_{J}(\hat{A})
then
L_{\hat{A}^{S,J},j,J}(\underline{\hat{a}_{m}})\equiv 0
for all
S\in\mathcal{J}_{J}(\hat{A})
and
j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
, so that
\underline{\hat{a}_{m}}\in T_{S}
for all
S\in\mathcal{J}_{J}(\hat{A})
). Thus,
W\subseteq P
and therefore

P=W.

We are thus left to show that

T(A)\subseteq P.

By Corollary
5.2
, it is enough to show that
T(A)\cap\mathbb{H}_{\infty}\subseteq P
. To this aim, let
F:=f_{1}{\sf e}_{1}+\ldots+f_{d}{\sf e}_{d}\in T(A)\cap\mathbb{H}_{\infty}
. For every
S\in\mathcal{J}_{J}(\hat{A})
let
\theta_{S}
be the inner function (unique up to multiplication by a unimodular constant) such that
\varphi_{S}=\varphi_{J,A}\theta_{S}
. Note that the (inner function) greatest common divisor of
\{\theta_{S}\}_{S\in\mathcal{J}_{J}(\hat{A})}
is
1
(for otherwise
\varphi_{J,A}
would not be the greatest common divisor of the
\varphi_{S}
’s).

Fix
S\in\mathcal{J}_{J}(\hat{A})
and
j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
. Since by hypothesis
L_{\hat{A}^{S,J},j,J}(F)\in\varphi_{J,A}H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
, it follows by the (multi-)linearity of the determinant that

L_{\hat{A}^{S,J},j,J}(\theta_{S}F)\in\varphi_{S}H^{2}(\mathbb{D}).

Thus,
\theta_{S}F\in P
and, in particular, since
F\in\mathbb{H}_{\infty}
, we have
h\theta_{S}F\in P
for all
h\in H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
.
Hence, if for each
S\in\mathcal{J}_{J}(\hat{A})
we take a function
h^{S}\in H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
, we have

\left(\sum_{S\in\mathcal{J}_{J}(\hat{A})}h^{S}\theta_{S}\right)F\in P.

Now, let
\{h^{S}_{n}\}\subset H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
be the sequences given by Lemma
2.3
such that
\{\sum_{S\in\mathcal{J}_{J}(\hat{A})}h_{n}^{S}\theta_{S}\}
converges to
1
in
H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
, and let

F_{n}:=\left(\sum_{S\in\mathcal{J}_{J}(\hat{A})}h_{n}^{S}\theta_{S}\right)F\in P.

Taking into account that
F\in\mathbb{H}_{\infty}
, it follows that
\{F_{n}\}
converges to
F
in
\mathbb{H}
and hence
F\in P
, and we are done.
∎
