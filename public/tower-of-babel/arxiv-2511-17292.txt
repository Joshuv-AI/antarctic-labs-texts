The Experimental Unit Information Index: Balancing Evidentiary Value and Sample Size of Adaptive Designs
Leonhard Held 1 , Fadoua Balabdaoui 2 Samuel Pawel 1

Abstract




Abstract
:

Reducing the number of experimental units is one of the
three pillars of the 3R principles (Replace, Reduce, Refine) in animal
research. At the same time, statistical error rates need to be controlled to
enable reliable inferences and decisions. This paper proposes a novel measure
to quantify the evidentiary value of one experimental unit for a given study
design. The experimental unit information index (EUII) is based on power,
Type-I error and sample size, and has attractive interpretations both in terms
of frequentist error rates and Bayesian posterior odds. We introduce the EUII
in simple statistical test settings and show that its asymptotic value depends
only on the assumed relative effect size under the alternative. We then extend
the definition to adaptive designs where early stopping for efficacy or
futility may cause reductions in sample size. Applications to group-sequential
designs and a recently proposed adaptive statistical test procedure show the usefulness of
the approach when the goal is to maximize the evidentiary value of one
experimental unit.

Key Words
: Adaptive designs; Diagnostic odds ratio; Likelihood ratio; 3R Principles; Bayesian updating

1
Introduction

Standard statistical hypothesis testing is based on the control of the Type-I
error rate at some pre-defined significance level
\alpha
. “Control” means that the actual
Type-I error rate is not allowed to be larger than
\alpha
, but it can be
smaller. A comparison of different statistical tests that control the Type-I
error rate is then based on the power to detect a certain effect size of
interest. The greater the power, the better the test. However, power comparisons
are rarely meaningful if the Type-I error rates of the tests are different, as a
greater power can typically be traded-off with an increase in Type-I error rate.

Over the last decades, various adaptive designs have been developed where an
experiment can be stopped early, either for efficacy (a convincing result has
already been obtained) or for futility (the results are so unconvincing that
there is only little sense in continuing the experiment). Stopping an experiment
early ensures that the number of animals included in an experiment
does not become too large. This reflects the second pillar of the 3R principles
“Replace, Reduce, Refine” in animal research
(
Russell and Burch 1959
)
,
described as

“
to use methods for obtaining comparable levels of information from
the use of fewer animals in scientific procedures, or for obtaining more
information from the same number of animals
”
(
swiss3rcc.org/3rs-for-the-public/
)

The 3R principles are now widely adopted to improve the design and analysis of
animal studies

(
Reynolds 2023
;
Reynolds 2024a
;
Reynolds 2024b
;
Townsend et al. 2025
)
. However, the
focus of standard statistical theory on error rates ignores that different adaptive designs may
have different (expected) sample sizes. Furthermore, a comparison of methods with desired Type-I error rate
control with alternative methods that may have more power but also Type-I error
rate inflation can be difficult.
To address the latter issue, optimising the trade-off between Type-I
and Type-II error rates has been recently considered

Mudge et al. 2012
;
Walley and Grieve 2021
;
Grieve 2024
. The goal is
here to optimize a weighted sum of error rates based on prior
probabilities of the null and the alternative hypothesis and the
relative costs of Type-I and Type-II errors. An alternative approach is to adjust power for the
corresponding Type-I error rate using the theory of receiver
operating characteristic curves
(
Lloyd 2005
;
Cavus et al. 2019
)
.
However, all these methods do not take sample size into account.

In this paper we propose a new measure of statistical information that takes
into account Type-I error rate, power, and sample size.
This measure is related to the diagnostic odds ratio to quantify
diagnostic accuracy
(
Glas et al. 2003
)
, which is widely used in
clinical epidemiology and which also has attractive interpretations
when applied to statistical hypothesis tests. First, it has a
frequentist interpretation as the ratio of the rejection odds under
the alternative and under the null hypothesis. Second, it also has a
Bayesian interpretation as the ratio of the posterior odds of the
alternative hypothesis given a significant versus nonsignificant
results. Finally, the diagnostic odds ratio allows for a normalization
with respect to the (expected) sample size to obtain a measure of the
evidentiary value of one unit/animal/patient, the experimental unit
information index (EUII).

This article is organized as follows: Section
2
reviews
likelihood ratios and diagnostic odds ratios and applies them to statistical
hypothesis tests. Section
3
then defines the experimental unit
information index for fixed sample size and studies its performance in standard
statistical test settings. The extension to adaptive tests with different
expected sample size of significant and nonsignificant findings is described in
Section
4
. Applications to group-sequential designs (Section

4.1
) and “constrained sample augmentation” (Section

4.2
), an adaptive test procedure recently proposed by

Reinagel 2023
, are described. We close with some discussion in Section

5
.

2
Methodology

2.1
Likelihood ratios and the diagnostic odds ratio

Sensitivity and specificity are traditional measures of diagnostic test accuracy
(
Pepe 2004
)
. Sensitivity is defined as the probability to obtain a positive
test result from a diseased patient. Specificity is defined as the probability
of a negative test result from a healthy patient.

In a diagnostic context,
H_{1}
represents the hypothesis that a patient has the
disease whereas
H_{0}
represents that the patient does not have the disease.
Likelihood ratios (LRs) are commonly used in diagnostic testing to quantify the
(prior to posterior) change in odds
\mathsf{Odds}(H_{1})=\Pr(H_{1})/\Pr(H_{0})
after having
observed a positive or negative test result
(
Deeks and Altman 2004
)
:

\displaystyle\left.\begin{array}[]{c}{\mathsf{Odds}(H_{1}\,|\,\mbox{test positive})}\\
{\mathsf{Odds}(H_{1}\,|\,\mbox{test negative})}\end{array}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\mbox{LR}^{+}\\
\mbox{LR}^{-}\end{array}\right\}\,\cdot\,{\mathsf{Odds}(H_{1})}.

LRs are useful because they do not depend on the pre-test odds of having the
disease (typically the prevalence), which may be unknown or differ across
populations.

Positive and negative likelihood ratios directly relate to sensitivity and
specificity,

positive LR:

\displaystyle\mbox{LR}^{+}=

\displaystyle\frac{\Pr(\text{test positive}\,|\,\text{diseased})}{\Pr(\text{test positive}\,|\,\text{non-diseased})}=\frac{\mbox{Sensitivity}}{1-\mbox{Specificity}}

negative LR:

\displaystyle\mbox{LR}^{-}=

\displaystyle\frac{\Pr(\text{test negative}\,|\,\text{diseased})}{\Pr(\text{test negative}\,|\,\text{non-diseased})}=\frac{1-\mbox{Sensitivity}}{\mbox{Specificity}}

and the diagnostic odds ratio
(
Glas et al. 2003
)
(DOR) is simply the ratio of
the two likelihood ratios:
\mbox{DOR}=\mbox{LR}^{+}/\mbox{LR}^{-}
.

Useful diagnostic tests have positive likelihood ratio
\mbox{LR}^{+}>1
and negative likelihood ratio
\mbox{LR}^{-}<1
, where one of the
two inequalities implies the other one. Equation (
2.1
)
shows that a positive result from a useful test increases the odds (and
therefore also the probability) of having the disease whereas a
negative result decreases it. The diagnostic odds ratio is thus a
single indicator of diagnostic test accuracy, the larger, the
better. We will describe its properties in more detail in the
following, when we apply it to statistical hypotheses tests.

2.2
The diagnostic odds ratio for hypotheses testing

It has been argued that likelihood ratios can also be applied to statistical
hypothesis tests
(
Bayarri et al. 2016
;
Huang and Trinquart 2024
)
, where a positive or
negative diagnostic test result is now replaced by a significant or
nonsignificant hypothesis test result. The positive and negative likelihood
ratios are then known as the (pre-experimental) rejection ratio

(
Bayarri et al. 2016
)
and nonrejection ratio
(
Phelan 2018
)
, respectively.

Huang and Trinquart 2024
apply the positive and negative
likelihood ratio to hypothesis tests and consider

positive LR:

\displaystyle\mbox{LR}^{+}=

\displaystyle\frac{\Pr(\mbox{significant}\,|\,H_{1})}{\Pr(\mbox{significant}\,|\,H_{0})}=\frac{\mbox{Power}}{\mbox{T1E rate}},

(6)

negative LR:

\displaystyle\mbox{LR}^{-}=

\displaystyle\frac{\Pr(\mbox{nonsignificant}\,|\,H_{1})}{\Pr(\mbox{nonsignificant}\,|\,H_{0})}=\frac{1-\mbox{Power}}{1-\mbox{T1E rate}}.

(7)

For a significant finding, the positive likelihood ratio directly relates to the

positive predictive value

\Pr(H_{1}\,|\,\mbox{significant})

(
Browner 1987
)
via

\mathsf{Odds}(H_{1}\,|\,\mbox{significant})=\mbox{LR}^{+}\cdot\mathsf{Odds}(H_{1}).

(8)

Similarly, the negative likelihood ratio
\mbox{LR}^{-}
directly relates to the

negative predictive value

\Pr(H_{0}\,|\,\mbox{nonsignificant})=1-\Pr(H_{1}\,|\,\mbox{nonsignificant})
:

\mathsf{Odds}(H_{1}\,|\,\mbox{nonsignificant})=\mbox{LR}^{-}\cdot\mathsf{Odds}(H_{1}).

(9)

As for diagnostic tests, useful statistical tests have positive likelihood ratio

\mbox{LR}^{+}>1
and negative likelihood ratio
\mbox{LR}^{-}<1
. This means
that a significant test result increases the odds (and the probability) of the
alternative hypothesis
H_{1}
being true, whereas a nonsignificant result
increases the odds of the null hypothesis
H_{0}
. In what follows we describe the
approach for standard statistical hypothesis tests but note that the approach
can also be applied to other inferential approaches such as dual-criterion
designs
(
Roychoudhury et al. 2018
)
or Bayesian
methods
(
Spiegelhalter et al. 2004
)
, as long as these methods provide a
dichotomous test result in the form of accepting either
H_{1}

(“significant”) or not (“not significant”).

The diagnostic odds ratio of a statistical test can now
be defined as the ratio of the positive likelihood ratio (
6
) and
the negative likelihood ratio (
7
):

\mbox{DOR}=\frac{\mbox{LR}^{+}}{\mbox{LR}^{-}}=\frac{{\mbox{Power}}/{\mbox{T1E rate}}}{(1-{\mbox{Power}})/(1-{\mbox{T1E rate}})}.

(10)

Rearranging this equation gives the frequentist interpretation of the DOR as the
ratio of the odds of significance under
H_{1}
(“Power Odds”) to the odds of
significance under
H_{0}
(“T1E Odds”):

DOR

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mbox{Power Odds}}{\mbox{T1E Odds}}

(11)

where

\displaystyle\mbox{Power Odds}=\frac{\mbox{Power}}{1-\mbox{Power}}

and

\displaystyle\mbox{T1E Odds}=\frac{\mbox{T1E rate}}{1-\mbox{T1E rate}}.

DOR thus describes the evidentiary value of a statistical test in discriminating
between
H_{1}
and
H_{0}
.
The DOR ranges from 0 to infinity and is larger than 1 for a useful statistical
test.
If
\mbox{DOR}=1
, the power is equal to the T1E rate, so the probability of
significance is the same under
H_{1}
and
H_{0}
and the test is useless.

The DOR can also be written as the ratio of the odds of
H_{1}
given a
significant result relative to the odds of
H_{1}
given a nonsignificant result:

DOR

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathsf{Odds}(H_{1}\,|\,\mbox{significant})}{\mathsf{Odds}(H_{1}\,|\,\mbox{nonsignificant})}

(12)

This formulation directly follows from division of (
8
) with
(
9
) and is particularly remarkable, because it is a
statement about posterior odds of the alternative
H_{1}
without specification of
the prior probability
\Pr(H_{1})
or the corresponding prior odds (the prior odds

\mathsf{Odds}(H_{1})
cancel in the division of (
8
) with
(
9
)). So the DOR quantifies how much larger the odds of
the alternative
H_{1}
are among significant results compared to nonsignificant
results. A similar rearrangement of Bayes’ theorem led to the crucial
understanding that the odds ratio in a retrospective case-control study can be
interpreted prospectively
(
Cornfield 1951
;
Breslow 1996
)
. Note that
(
12
) can also be formulated in terms of odds of the null hypothesis

H_{0}
:

DOR

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathsf{Odds}(H_{0}\,|\,\mbox{nonsignificant})}{\mathsf{Odds}(H_{0}\,|\,\mbox{significant})}.

(13)

To summarize, the DOR quantifies how large the odds of
H_{1}
of significant test results are
compared to nonsignificant test results. Equivalently, DOR tells us how large
the odds of
H_{0}
of nonsignificant test results are compared to significant
test results.
The DOR has therefore useful interpretations both in terms of frequentist error
rates (
11
) and Bayesian posterior odds (
12
)
and (
13
).

3
The experimental unit information index

To adjust the DOR for a pre-specified sample size
n
, we propose
the experimental unit information index as the
n
th root of the
diagnostic odds ratio:

\mbox{EUII}=\frac{\left(\mbox{LR}^{+}\right)^{1/n}}{\left(\mbox{LR}^{-}\right)^{1/n}}=\mbox{DOR}^{1/n}.

(14)

The motivation to take the
n
th root comes from equation (
8
)
and (
9
), respectively. These two equations show that
likelihood ratios act multiplicatively on the odds of
H_{1}
,
transforming prior odds to posterior odds. Both likelihood ratios are based on
data from
n
experimental units, so evey unit contributes on average an amount
of
({\mbox{LR}^{-}})^{1/n}
and
({\mbox{LR}^{+}})^{1/n}
, respectively, to the
likelihood ratios, because

\mbox{LR}=\underbrace{{\mbox{LR}}^{1/n}\times{\mbox{LR}}^{1/n}\times\ldots\times{\mbox{LR}}^{1/n}}_{\mbox{$n$ times}}.

This representation emphasizes the coherence of Bayesian (prior to posterior)
updating based on increasingly available data. Taking the ratio of

({\mbox{LR}^{-}})^{1/n}
and
({\mbox{LR}^{+}})^{1/n}
leads to the definition
(
14
) of the experimental unit information index (EUII). Note that
this derivation assumes that the experimental units are independent. The EUII
hence represents the average contribution of one experimental unit to the DOR of
a statistical test, where “average” is to be understood as the geometric
average. A useful statistical test has EUII > 1, so on average each additional
experimental unit provides information that helps to discriminate between the
null hypothesis (
H_{0}
) and the alternative hypothesis (
H_{1}
).

Taking the logarithm of (
14
),

\log\mbox{EUII}=\frac{1}{n}\log\mbox{DOR},

shows that the log EUII can be interpreted as the average contribution of each
observation to the log diagnostic odds ratio. Further, writing the
\log\mbox{EUII}
as

\log\mbox{EUII}=\frac{1}{n}\left\{\log\left(\frac{1}{1-\mbox{Power}}-1\right)+\log\left(\frac{1}{\mbox{T1E rate}}-1\right)\right\}

shows explicitly that two tests can have the same EUII for different
combinations of power, T1E rate, and sample size. This means that if
less than two out of three quantities are fixed, there is possibly no unique
statistical test that has the highest EUII. Fixing the sample size and the
T1E rate results in the classical uniformly most powerful test emerging
as the test with the highest EUII. Conversely, fixing the power and T1E
rate, the test with the lowest sample size emerges as the optimal test that
maximizes the EUII. Finally, fixing the sample size and the power, the test with
the lowest T1E rate is the optimal test that maximizes the EUII. All of
these three cases are in line with common sense.

3.1
Empirical evaluation

We will now evaluate the EUII for both
z
and
t
-tests.

3.1.1
Standard
z
-test

For simplicity we consider a standard two-sided
z
-test based on one sample, where there
is a closed-form expression for the required sample size to achieve a power
1-\beta
to detect a relative effect
size
\delta

(
Kirkwood and Sterne 2003
)

n=\frac{(u+v)^{2}}{\delta^{2}}

(15)

with Type-II error (T2E) rate
\beta
,
u=\Phi^{-1}(1-\beta)
and

v=\Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha/2)
. The required sample size
n
is usually
rounded up to the next integer and then the actual power will be
slightly larger than
1-\beta
. However, we will work in the following
with the unrounded sample size from (
15
) to keep the power
exactly at the desired value.

For example, suppose we design a study to achieve a power of
1-\beta=80\%
to detect the
relative effect size
\delta=0.5
at the (two-sided) significance
level of
\alpha=5\%
. We then have
\mbox{LR}^{+}={\mbox{80\%}}/{\mbox{5\%}}=16
,
\mbox{LR}^{-}={\mbox{20\%}}/{\mbox{95\%}}=4/19
and so
\mbox{DOR}=16/(4/19)=76
. Alternatively, the DOR can be computed as the ratio of the

\mbox{Power Odds}={\mbox{80\%}}/{\mbox{20\%}}=4
and the
\mbox{T1E Odds}={\mbox{5\%}}/{\mbox{95\%}}=1/19
, which then also gives
\mbox{DOR}=4\cdot 19=76
. The required sample size (
15
) to achieve 80% power to detect

\delta=0.5
with a
one-sample
t
-test is
n=31.4
. The EUII is therefore

\mbox{EUII}_{1}=76^{1/31.4}=1.15,

which means that every experimental unit in the sample increases the evidentiary value of the
statistical test on average by 15%.

Table
1
gives the DOR and the EUII also for other values of the
power and Type-I error rate. We can see that a test with 90%
power at significance level 5% has more evidentiary value
than a test with 80% power at significance level
2.5% (DOR = 171 vs. 156).
The increase in DOR is parallelized by an increase in the required sample size

n
to detect the standardized effect
\delta=0.5
, so the
corresponding values of EUII are nearly the same, 1.13 and 1.14,
respectively. The EUII takes its largest value 1.15 if both Type-I and Type-II error have the largest value
(5% and 20%, respectively). We will see later, that the asymptotic value of
EUII (for fixed T1E rate and increasing sample size
n
) is
\exp(\delta^{2}/2)=1.133
, so the finite sample size values of EUII
in Table
1
are all close to this asymptotic value.

If the design would have been instead based on two equally-sized samples and

\delta
represents the standardized difference to be detected, then the
required total sample size
n=125.6
at the 5% significance level and 80% power is
four times as large
(
Kirkwood and Sterne 2003
)
,
and we obtain

\mbox{EUII}_{2}=76^{1/125.6}=1.035.

The average evidentiary increase per unit is now
3.5%. Note that
\mbox{EUII}_{1}=({\mbox{EUII}_{2}})^{4}
which means that one unit in a one-sample study contributes
as much information as four units in a (perfectly balanced) two-sample trial, if
the goal is to detect the same standardized effect.

Note that we take
n
to be the total sample size and not the sample size per
group. This enables us to adjust EUII for unequal sample sizes. Unequal sample
sizes are quite common in clinical trials even if allocation is based on simple
1:1 randomisation. Furthermore, unequal allocation (e.g., 2:1) can provide
investigators with greater experience of a new treatment and may even encourage
recruitment to the trial.

The effect of unequal sample sizes on the power of the
z
-test has been
discussed in
Matthews 2006
. If we have
n=126

patients in total, the power will decrease to

1-\beta=\Phi\left(\delta\sqrt{\frac{n_{1}\,n_{2}}{126}}-v\right)

where
n_{1}
patients are allocated to one group and
n_{2}=126-n_{1}
to the other. Unequal sample sizes will thus decrease the power odds, the
DOR and hence also the EUII, because the total sample size
n=126

remains fixed. This is illustrated in Figure
1
.

n=42

For example, if the sample size in the smaller group is
n_{1}=42

(corresponding to a 2:1 allocation ratio), then EUII reduces from
1.035 to slightly below 1.033. This
confirms that the information loss of unequal randomisation in terms of EUII is
small if the allocation ratio is not greater than 2:1
(
Matthews 2006
)
.

3.2
Z
-test and
t
-test with increasing sample size

Consider now the standard one-sample one-sided
t
-test to test the
null hypothesis
H_{0}\colon\mu=0
versus the alternative
H_{1}\colon\mu>0
. We let the sample size
n
vary between

2^{3}=8
and

2^{13}=8192
and compute the power
to detect a standardized effect size
\delta
at significance
level
\alpha=5\%
. Figure
2
shows how
the resulting EUII varies with
\delta
and
n
. Not surprisingly,
EUII increases with increasing
\delta
, because the power to detect

\delta
increases with increasing
\delta
(for fixed
n
). If

\delta=0.1
and
n=8
, EUII is smaller than 1 because the power to
detect such a small effect with such a small sample size is in fact
smaller than the T1E rate. Such a test is called
biased
in
the mathematical statistics literature
(
Lehmann 1959
)
.

Figure 2:

one-sided
t
-and
z
-test as a function of sample size and effect size. The
asymptotic value
\exp(\delta^{2}/2)
for the different values of the standardized effect size
\delta
is
indicated by the dotted lines.

Note that the power also increases with increasing
n
, but the EUII index
adjusts for the sample size
n
and is able to compensate for this increase. It first drops and
then increases to the finite limiting value

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mbox{EUII}_{1}=\exp\left(\frac{\delta^{2}}{2}\right)

(16)

as shown in Appendix
A
. For comparison, Figure
2
also
shows EUII for the corresponding
z
-test, which is slightly larger for
small
n
, but converges to the same limit.

In the two-sample case with equal group sizes, the limiting value of EUII is the
fourth root of (
16
),
i. e
.

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mbox{EUII}_{2}=\exp\left(\frac{\delta^{2}}{8}\right).

(17)

Thus the asymptotic evidentiary gain from one additional unit is lower
for a two-sample compared to a one-sample test. Some rearrangement of
(
16
) and (
17
) shows that the effect size

\delta
in the two-sample setting needs to be twice as large as in
the one-sample setting to obtain the same asymptotic evidentiary value
of one experimental unit.

4
The experimental unit information index for adaptive designs

In adaptive trial designs, a trial can often be stopped preliminary if the
results are sufficiently compelling at an interim analysis, for example, if
there is strong evidence for treatment benefit or harm
(
Kairalla et al. 2012
)
.
This means that the sample size is random and typically differs under
H_{0}
and

H_{1}
. In the following, we denote a random sample size by
N
to distinguish it
from a fixed sample size
n
. An adaptive trial can then be characterized by the
pair of the sample size
N
at which the trial stops and the indicator
S
of
whether the results were (non)significant. The expected sample sizes for
significant (
+
) and nonsignificant (
-
) are then then defined by the
conditional expectations
\E(N_{+})=\E(N\,|\,S=\text{significant})
and

\E(N_{-})=\E(N\,|\,S=\text{nonsignificant})
, respectively.

Differences in sample sizes make it difficult to compare operating
characteristics (such as power and T1E rate) of different trial designs. The
fixed sample size definition (
14
) suggests a way to generalize the EUII to settings
with random sample size:

\displaystyle\mbox{EUII}=\frac{\big(\mbox{LR}^{+}\big)^{1/\E(N_{+})}}{\big(\mbox{LR}^{-}\big)^{1/\E(N_{-})}}.

(18)

The rationale for the generalization (
18
) is that the
computation of posterior predictive odds with likelihood ratios is either based
on significant (equation (
8
)) or nonsignificant findings
(equation (
9
)). It is therefore natural to normalize the
numerator
\mbox{LR}^{+}
in (
18
) with the expected sample size
of significant findings
\E(N_{+})
and the denominator with the expected sample
size of nonsignificant findings
\E(N_{-})
.

For designs with fixed sample size

n=\E(N_{+})=\E(N_{-})
, this generalization reduces correctly
to (
14
). Mathematically, equation (
18
) can be justified by a first-order Taylor approximation of the terms

\E(1/N_{+})\approx 1/\E(N_{+})\mbox{ and }\E(1/N_{-})\approx 1/\E(N_{-})

in the expected log EUII

\E(\log\mbox{EUII})=\E(1/N_{+})\log\mbox{LR}^{+}-\E(1/N_{-})\log\mbox{LR}^{-},

where
\log\mbox{LR}^{+}
and
\log\mbox{LR}^{-}
are assumed to be fixed. However, a first-order Taylor approximation may be inaccurate.
The second order Taylor approximations

\E(1/N_{+})\approx\frac{1+\mathsf{CV}(N_{+})^{2}}{\E(N_{+})}\mbox{ and }\E(1/N_{-})\approx\frac{1+\mathsf{CV}(N_{-})^{2}}{\E(N_{-})}

also incorporates the coefficient of variation

\mathsf{CV}(N_{+})=\frac{\sqrt{\Var(N_{+})}}{\E(N_{+})}\mbox{ and }\mathsf{CV}(N_{-})=\frac{\sqrt{\Var(N_{-})}}{\E(N_{-})},

respectively, and hence the variance of the sample size, leading to the second-order approximation

\widetilde{\mbox{EUII}}=\frac{\left(\mbox{LR}^{+}\right)^{\left(1+\mathsf{CV}(N_{+})^{2}\right)/\E(N_{+})}}{\big(\mbox{LR}^{-}\big)^{\left(1+\mathsf{CV}(N_{-})^{2}\right)/\E(N_{-})}}.

(19)

One can view
\E(N_{\bullet})/(1+\mathsf{CV}(N_{\bullet})^{2})
(where
\bullet\in\{+,-\}
) as an adjusted expected
sample size that also takes the coefficient of variation into
account. The larger the coefficient of variation, the smaller the
adjusted expected sample size will be. The difference between (
18
) and
(
19
) will be small if
\mathsf{CV}(N_{+})
and
\mathsf{CV}(N_{+})
are
small. We will study the difference in the application described in Section
4.2
.

A computational complication arises because significant and nonsignificant results can occur
both under the null and alternative hypothesis. Monte Carlo simulation can often
be used to obtain the distribution of the sample sizes
N_{0}
and
N_{1}
under the
null and alternative hypothesis, respectively. We can then compute the expected
sample size for significant results separately under the null (
\E(N_{0+})
) and
alternative (
\E(N_{1+})

To summarize, in the case of varying sample size the EUII
(
18
) depends on the expected sample size for
significant and nonsignificant findings. A generalization also
depends on the coefficient of variation. Both expectation and
coefficient of variation depend on an additional input variable, the
prior probability
\Pr(H_{1})
(or
\Pr(H_{0})=1-\Pr(H_{1})
) of the
alternative (null) hypothesis. Specification of this probability
depends on the scientific context of the experiment. For example, in
clinical research a number of 10% for the percentage of effective
treatments has been suggested
(
Staquet et al. 1979
;
Simon 1982
)
. In
discovery-oriented research this probability is expected to be even
lower
(
Ioannidis 2005
)
.

\delta=0.46

n_{\scriptsize\texttt{Max}}=50

displays the EUII for the different methods
(top), relative to the fixed design (middle), and the maximum sample

\delta
between

increases with increasing
\delta
but also depends on the assumed
prior probability of
H_{1}
, chosen to be either 0.01, 0.1 or 0.5. In
particular, for
\Pr(H_{1})=.01
the EUII depends less on the effect
size
\delta
under the alternative, because the second term
\E(N_{1+})
(that depends on
\delta
) of the
expected sample size (
20
) for significant findings receives less weight.

All group-sequential designs have a
larger EUII than the fixed design. The improvement is around 1-11% for the
Pocock method, and around 0-7% for O’Brien-Fleming and Haybittle-Peto. These
results indicate that Pocock should be the preferred group-sequential method if
the goal is to maximize the evidentiary value of one experimental unit.

Figure
3
also shows the expected sample size of
significant findings under the two hypotheses, for the different
values of the standardized effect size. With the exception of Pocock,
the expected sample size of significant findings is always larger
under
H_{0}
than under
H_{1}
. Only for Pocock it can be the other way
round if the standardized effect size is less than
\delta=0.46
, which corresponds to the power being
below 90%. This is a consequence of the fact that, under
H_{0}
, a
significant finding with Pocock’s method is more likely to be obtained
at the first interim analysis and less at the second and so
forth. This is not the case under
H_{1}
, where a significant finding
at the second interim analysis is more likely than at the first. This
peculiar feature is caused by the constant and relatively large
nominal significance levels (see Table
3
) and has been
one of the criticisms of the Pocock method in clinical trials, where
– under the
H_{0}
– it is less difficult that a trial is stopped
early than for competing methods
(
Simon 1994
;
Matthews 2006
)
. However, this
feature has been described as an adventage in animal trials where the
goal is to minimize animal numbers
(
Blotwijk et al. 2022
)
and our
analysis confirms that smaller expected sample sizes under
H_{0}
lead
to a larger evidentiary value per animal of the Pocock method compared
to the other ones. For example, the EUII is smaller for
Haybittle-Peto, where the nominal significance levels (with the exception of the last one) are also
constant, but smaller. Significant findings are then more likely to be obtained
at the last analysis and the expected sample size under
H_{0}

increases accordingly. In sum, the EUII allowed us to compare
sequential designs with differing power, Type-I error rate, sample
sizes, and interim analyses time points.

4.2
N
-hacking and constrained sample augmentation

The term
N
-hacking was recently introduced by

Reinagel 2023
to describe an adaptive procedure where
more data is collected in an effort to achieve statistical
significance, if only a “trend to significance” is observed so far.
Without appropriate adjustment of the
significance level
\alpha
(usually 5% for a two-sided test), such a
“peeking” procedure is known to inflate the Type-I error rate

(
Ludbrook 2003
)
. However,
Reinagel 2023
argues that
with an additional stop for futility if
p>0.1
, the Type-I error rate
is only moderately increased while the positive predictive value

\Pr(H_{1}\,|\,\mbox{significant})
, the probability that a
significant result is true positive, is substantially larger than for
a fixed design with significance level
\alpha
. In addition, the mean
final sample size is smaller compared to fixed sample size experiments
with the same Type-I error rate and power. This aligns with the
“Reduce” pillar of the 3R principles.

Reinagel specifically considers a modification of
N
-hacking,
so-called “constrained sample augmentation”, to include an
additional stop for futility: First, data are collected until 8
observations are collected, 4 in each group. If an independent sample
Student’s
t
-test produces two-sided
p\leq 0.05
, significance is
achieved, whereas
p>0.1
leads to a futility stop of the
experiment. If
0.05<p\leq 0.1
, another four observations are
added and the updated
p
-value is used again to decide whether to
stop for significance, stop for futility, or to continue with another
four observations. This procedure is continued until a total of

n_{\scriptsize\texttt{Max}}\in\{12,16,20,24,28,32\}
observations
are collected.

The usage of group-sequential methods in preclinical experiments has
been advocated for in
Neumann et al. 2017
and

Blotwijk et al. 2022
. In a simulation study we compare the
approach based on the EUII with three standard group-sequential
methods from clinical trials, the Pocock, the O’Brien-Fleming and the
Haybittle-Peto stopping rule, as described in the previous
section. The first two work with more stringent nominal significance
levels at interim to control the Type-I error rate exactly at
\alpha

in large samples. These results have been extended to also achieve
exact T1E control in the
t
-test with small samples

(
Nikolakopoulos et al. 2016
;
Rom and McTague 2020
)
, but for simplicity we use the
standard significance levels for large samples available in the
R
package

rpact
. The slightly increased T1E rate is automatically
taken into account in the computation of EUII, as described in
Appendix
B
, which gives additional details on the design of
the simulation study.

Additional stopping rules for futility has been reported to lead
to the saving of resources of up to 30% compared to standard group-sequential
methods
(
Neumann et al. 2017

. Here we allow for an additional stop for futility
for the group-sequential methods based on the concept of predictive power (PP)

(
Spiegelhalter et al. 1986

. Specifically, we stop the
experiment for futility, if the preditive power to achieve significance

(with four additional observations) is
below 30%. The predictive power approach takes into account the uncertainty of
the current effect estimate in contrast to standard conditional power
calculations
(

. A threshold on predictive power translates
to thresholds on the

-hacking to investigate the effect of a varying
p
-value
threshold for futility compared to Reinagel’s approach, where the futility
threshold is always 0.1.

Looking at the methods without futility stopping (top panel), we can
see that Pocock always has a larger EUII than O’Brien-Fleming, both

4.1
. If

is relatively large,

-hacking (without the additional futility stop of Reinagel’s

O’Brien-Fleming. The effect of the prior probability
\Pr(H_{1})
on
EUII is relatively small.

throughout. The underlying values of
\E(N_{\bullet})
and

for significant and nonsignificant

the case for the coefficient of variation. Without futility stopping,
the coefficient of variation of significant findings can take values

n_{\scriptsize\texttt{Max}}
are large.

interesting that the approach by Reinagel often performs better in terms of

for futility. The bottom panels of Figure

now somewhat larger, in particular for Haybittle-Peto and O’Brien-Fleming. The

increase, with the Pocock or the Haybittle-Peto method now performing best.
Pocock has fairly constant values of EUII and is always among the top two

is large, but can be
worse than all other methods if
n_{\scriptsize\texttt{Max}}
is small.
O’Brien-Fleming is better than Reinagel for a small effect size, but for a
large effect size it is the other way round. Finally
N
-hacking plus
predictive power is very similar to Reinagel’s approach, slightly better for

\mbox{SMD}=1
and slightly worse otherwise.

In summary, the EUII allowed us to compare designs with different
Type-I error rates, power, and expected sample sizes on a scale with
direct practical value – the evidentiary value of an additional
experimental unit. The second-order approximation gives slightly larger values
of EUII, but qualitatively very similar results.

5
Discussion

We have introduced a new measure to quantify the evidentiary value of one
experimental unit, the experimental unit information index. Properties in
standard statistical test procedures have been described and the corresponding
asymptotic values have been derived. Two application have shown the benefits of
this measure to compare more complex statistical procedures with efficacy and
futility stopping. It would be interesting to investigate more systematically
the optimal choice for efficacy and futility stopping in terms of EUII. In the
adaptive designs considered in Sections
4.1
and
4.2
, we
have used a standard overall significance level of 2.5% (one-sided) and 5%
(two-sided) to determine the nominal efficacy thresholds of the different
adaptive designs considered. Other overall significance values may lead to
different (and perhaps larger) values of EUII.

Future work could apply the proposed methodology to
dual-criterion designs
(
Fisch et al. 2015
;
Roychoudhury et al. 2018
;
Goodman et al. 2019
;
Rosenkranz 2021
;
Zhao et al. 2023
)
,
where success of an experiment requires not only significance, but also
relevance of the estimated effect size. Furthermore, Bayesian sequential designs

(
Gsponer et al. 2014
;
Gerber and Gsponer 2016
;
Pourmohamad and Wang 2022
;
Kang et al. 2025
)
could be
considered, where the implied sample size of a prior distribution could be taken
into account in the calculation of EUII. Methods to quantify a prior
distribution in terms of sample size have been recently
proposed
(
Wiesenfarth and Calderazzo 2020
;
Neuenschwander et al. 2020
)
. This aspect is also
relevant for Bayesian borrowing methods based on historical data

(
Gravestock and Held 2017
;
Gravestock and Held 2018
)
. Another possible area of application are
more complex experimental designs such as randomized complete block design to
incorporate cage effects
(
Townsend et al. 2025
)
.

There are also interesting possible extensions of the methodology proposed. For
example, we may average the power over plausible values of the effect size
rather than taking a certain fixed value, as proposed for the pre-experimental
rejection ratio
(
Bayarri et al. 2016
)
. For example, in the absence of precise
information on the clinically relevant difference we may use predictive power in
the calculation of DOR and EUII. Similarly, we may average the power according
to the distribution on the sample sizes of the two groups, because increasing
imbalance in sample size leads to increasing loss in power. Finally, in most
realistic applications, the EUII must be estimated using simulation. Further
research is needed to develop a formula for the Monte Carlo standard error

(
Koehler et al. 2009
)
that can quantify the uncertainty associated with the
estimate.
