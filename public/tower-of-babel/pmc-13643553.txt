Novel Caval Valve Implantation in a Patient With Symptomatic Tricuspid Regurgitation and Right Heart Failure

Severe tricuspid regurgitation (TR) is a frequent condition in patients with advanced heart failure, causing clinical signs of right ventricular (RV) dysfunction, particularly peripheral edema and ascites. In these patients, interventional strategies for tricuspid valve repair or replacement have gained increasing prominence, particularly in those ineligible for surgery. Tricuspid transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (T-TEER) has become widely adopted, demonstrating favorable outcomes in terms of safety, TR reduction, and quality of life, as the TriCLASP study,1 the PASTE registry,2 and the TRILUMINATE Pivotal trial3 showed. However, T-TEER is not suitable for all patients, particularly when anatomical constraints like absent cusp coaptation are present. As a result, orthotopic transcatheter tricuspid valve replacement (TTVR) has emerged as an alternative in recent years, enabling treatment irrespective of preexisting valve anatomy. Nevertheless, patients with large annuli are ineligible for TTVR.4 Concurrently, heterotopic transcatheter approaches are under active investigation. One such innovation is the TricValve, a transcatheter caval valve implantation (CAVI) system consisting of 2 self-expanding biological valves implanted simultaneously in the superior vena cava (SVC) and inferior vena cava (IVC).5

TRICENTO is a transcatheter bicaval valved stent graft that represents a novel heterotopic treatment option6 for severe TR in patients ineligible for surgery or transcatheter repair.7 The second-generation TRICENTO G2 prosthesis (MEDIRA GmbH) was developed recently and is not yet approved for the market. It has undergone major engineering changes compared to the previous version (Figure 1), which was withdrawn from the market because of stent fractures, although positive effects on functional state and RV remodeling were observed.6 Since fractures were mainly caused by increased right atrial (RA) pressure, the second generation was designed to withstand up to 45 mm Hg. The lateral aperture area was enlarged, and the IVC sealing zone was reduced. Unlike the custom-made first generation, the TRICENTO G2 is an off-the-shelf prosthesis that is available in 2 sizes. Before the intervention, the anatomical selection criteria were reviewed via a full-cycle computed tomography scan, measuring the diameters of the SVC (22.9-37.1 mm) and IVC (27.9-42.9 mm), RA height (55-80 mm), and distance to the hepatic veins (>5 mm).Figure 1Comparison of the TRICENTO G2 to the First-Generation TRICENTOMajor engineering changes indicated between the previous (left) and the current version (right). In this case report, we describe the successful implantation of the TRICENTO G2 system in a patient with severe TR who was deemed ineligible for surgical or transcatheter repair after failure of conventional therapies. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first published case of TRICENTO G2 transcatheter heart valve implantation. The aim is to evaluate the feasibility of this novel approach, highlight the specific challenges it presents, and discuss the potential clinical benefits for patients with symptomatic TR without other options.

Case Summary

This report details the case of an 81-year-old man with a complex medical history of recurrent cardiac decompensation who had previously undergone surgical mitral valve repair and T-TEER with 3 TriClips. He was admitted to our University Hospital in November 2025 because of progressive right heart failure symptoms, particularly stasis dermatitis and pleural effusion. Conservative pharmacologic therapy was initiated during hospitalization, and multiple pleural punctures were performed. Transesophageal echocardiography revealed severe TR, but a detachment of the previously implanted clips was excluded. Furthermore, extensive RV dilatation with impaired RV function was present. Regarding the patient's poor prognosis with conventional treatment, a transcatheter approach was pursued. Preprocedural assessment included right heart catheterization, and comprehensive imaging revealing the diameters of the SVC (36 mm) and IVC (41 mm). The patient was deemed ineligible for orthotopic TTVR because of prior T-TEER. Given the patient's recurrent cardiac decompensation and severe RV dysfunction and the absence of suitable options, TRICENTO G2 transcatheter heart valve implantation was pursued as compassionate use therapy.

The procedure was conducted under sedation and hemodynamic monitoring, and the patient tolerated the procedure well. After establishing femoral venous access, an AL2 catheter was placed at the bottom of the RA in order to mark the junction of the IVC and RA. The prosthesis (large size) was then advanced into position using a delivery system; the attached radiopaque gold markers served for orientation under fluoroscopy. The caudal markers were aligned with the AL2 catheter, and the valve element was rotated toward the tricuspid valve. Once the appropriate position was found, the self-expanding prosthesis was deployed by activating the release mechanism and anchored in the caval veins because of an oversizing of the stent graft in the sealing zone. Finally, the position and function of the prosthesis were verified by angiography and transesophageal echocardiography. Postprocedurally, neither significant paraprosthetic leakage nor valvular regurgitation was observed (Videos 1 and 2). Notably, systolic flow reversal in the hepatic veins was resolved (Figure 2). Following the procedure, third-degree atrioventricular (AV) block was observed, and a pacemaker was required. In this case, short lasting episodes of third-degree AV block had already occurred during right heart catheterization before the implantation. Thus, it is likely that the AV conduction disturbances were preexisting and were not caused by the heterotopic prosthesis. Higher degree AV block is not common after heterotopic tricuspid interventions,3 thus there is scarce experience on subsequent pacemaker implantation. Orthotopic TTVR may be associated with AV block given its anatomical proximity and potential mechanical compression of the right bundle branch. After TTVR, implantation of a conventional transtricuspid pacing lead is not recommended, as prosthesis dysfunction can occur.4 Therefore, alternative pacing strategies are required, for example leadless transcatheter pacemaker systems that can be inserted in the RV without continuous traversion of the tricuspid valve.8 Simultaneously, we decided to implant the leadless pacemaker Micra TPS (Medtronic) in order to avoid interference with the implanted TRICENTO G2 prosthesis by pacemaker leads.

The femoral vein was punctured, and the RV was accessed using a steerable delivery catheter under fluoroscopy. The atrium was entered through the valved stent graft, and the passage of the prosthesis went smoothly without disturbance. The device was positioned in the septal region (Figure 5). Once the optimal position was achieved, the device was fixed and released by cutting the tethers to the delivery catheter. The following parameters were measured at the end of the procedure: sensing 4.2 mV, threshold 0.38 V, impedance 710 Ω. The vital signs remained stable, and transcutaneous pacing was not required throughout the procedure. Finally, the patient's subjective well-being improved. He was transferred to his local hospital, where he received a pleural catheter, and a prolonged hospitalization was necessary. However, no major adverse cardiovascular events, including myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke, or all-cause-death, occurred during the 90-day follow-up period. An external follow-up transthoracic echocardiography performed 30 days after the intervention showed correct prosthesis positioning with adequate opening and closing function. No relevant regurgitation and no evidence of a large thrombus of the prosthesis was observed (Video 4, Video 5, Video 6). The follow-up transthoracic echocardiography further revealed a TAPSE of 13 mm and an RV FAC of 30%. Novelty

To the best of our knowledge, this represents the first published case of TRICENTO G2 transcatheter heart valve implantation, which was conducted in a patient with severe TR and no remaining options, since he was ineligible for approved transcatheter strategies, whereas the heterotopic position of the prosthesis allows for valve replacement irrespective of the preexisting valve anatomy. Bicaval valve implantation with the TricValve was not possible in this case given insufficient oversizing of the IVC component in the landing zone. The TRICENTO G2 might fill a treatment gap in the future given its larger anchoring zone and the continuous stent frame. Furthermore, the TRICENTO G2 device functionally isolates the RA from regurgitant RV pressure, in contrast to the TricValve system. Whether this will translate to a more complete protection of systemic backflow and improved venous congestion needs further investigation.

Future Directions

Through implementation of the TRICENTO G2, we may expand our armamentarium for interventional treatment, enabling tailored therapies. However, its impact on cardiovascular outcome and hospitalization rates will need to be investigated in future registries.

Conclusions

This case describes a successful TRICENTO G2 implantation as compassionate therapy in an 81-year-old patient with severe TR and symptomatic right heart failure after conventional therapy had failed and no other interventional strategies were available. The procedure was performed without major complications, and a short-term functional improvement in RV function under altered loading was observed. To the best of our knowledge, it represents the first published case of TRICENTO G2 transcatheter heart valve implantation. In comparison to T-TEER, the coaptation gap and, unlike the TTVR, the annulus size are irrelevant for TRICENTO G2. However, measurements of the caval veins and the RA are necessary, and in particular an IVC diameter of 45 mm must not be exceeded. The continuous architecture of the prosthesis might enable therapy in cases where CAVI is not suitable. Furthermore, this case shows that implantation of a leadless pacer was feasible without major challenges. According to the manufacturer, implantation of transvenous pacemaker leads through the lateral valve element is principally possible; however, we think that leadless pacing should be preferred to avoid long-term interaction and impaired functionality of the prosthesis. The TRICENTO G2 should currently be reserved for carefully selected patients in experienced centers with structured follow-up, since this single case cannot inform prognosis.
