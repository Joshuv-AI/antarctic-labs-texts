Measurement and impact analysis of social vulnerability to geological disasters in Guizhou Province, China
Authors: Yan Jia, Shuo Jia, Ruiqiu Pang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractInvestigating social vulnerability is a crucial aspect of attaining an in-depth understanding of disasters and disaster risk. This study developed a measurement framework for social vulnerability to geological disasters, obtained a comprehensive social vulnerability index, and compared the spatial distribution patterns of fatalities, social vulnerability, and geological disaster-prone zones. These efforts assisted in determining the strength and character of the relationship between geological disaster fatality risk and social vulnerability at the county level. The findings show that: (1) the social vulnerability to geological disasters index varies significantly between counties. High social vulnerability is primarily concentrated in Bijie City, Guizhou province, which shows concentrated clustering. Conversely, low social vulnerability is mainly concentrated in Guiyang City: (2) the spatial distribution patterns reveal the limitations of disaster exposure in explaining fatality risk and demonstrate the ability of social vulnerability to amplify disaster risk; (3) the zero-inflated negative binomial regression model fit the data significantly better than the negative binomial regression model, suggesting that for every one unit increase in the social vulnerability index, there is a 6.33% increase in the risk of fatalities, holding all other variables constant. This study establishes a specific framework for measuring social vulnerability to geological disasters and provides new evidence of how social vulnerability influences disaster risk intensity. The county-level spatial measurement results can further assist disaster management personnel in formulating more localized mitigation strategies. The findings will further support the identification of core remediation sites and the implementation of management and relocation (avoidance) projects during Guizhou Province’s 14th Five-Year Plan for geological disaster prevention and control.Subject terms: Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences, Natural hazards

Introduction

Geological disasters are characterized by sudden onset and subtle signs that are difficult to detect, which may instantaneously cause widespread fatalities and property loss. Despite technological advancements, forecasting these events remains difficult1. The increasing frequency of extreme weather incidents activates the triggering factors of geological disasters, making measurement harder2. According to data from the Global Disaster Platform, over 58 countries have suffered severe geological disasters in the last 20 years. China is among the hardest-hit countries, with an average of over 2340 casualties per year. The challenge of forecasting geological disasters is also evident in the statistical information from previous incidents. For instance, 68% of geological disasters in Guizhou Province, China, occurred outside of the sites monitored and forecasted in 20203. Existing technologies are unsatisfactory in meeting disaster response requirements, and exclusive consideration of technology inevitably leads to the pitfalls of the technocratic paradigm4. For example, the landslide that occurred in Xingren City, Guizhou in 2021 highlighted the limitations of technology as evidenced by the deaths of a deaf mute family who were unable to receive evacuation instructions. Tragedies such as this demonstrate that technology is not a panacea.

The inherent complexity and dynamic nature of social systems pose challenges to measuring social vulnerability5. Many approaches have evolved from pre-existing disaster risk or vulnerability paradigms in an attempt to develop innovative structures to measure social vulnerability. First among these, deduction based on theoretical derivation carefully structures social vulnerability, identifies the elements involved in a development and how they interact, and then adopts appropriate characterization indicators to measure6–8. Second, induction based on practical experience furnishes a comparatively broad range of indicators that can be selected when constructing a framework9,10. Currently, many social vulnerability frameworks adopt an integrated strategy involving both deduction and induction, which entails constructing an indicator system based on social vulnerability theories and adjusting it based on regional experience11,12. Cutter et al. developed the Social Vulnerability Index (SoVI)8, in a famous case that measured social vulnerability to environmental hazards using 17 concepts in county-level units in the United States. This framework was built on the social vulnerability components outlined in Cutter’s “hazards-of-place model.”13. After collecting a substantial number of literature-validated indicators, the most robust ones were identified using census data from the study area to construct a framework to measure social vulnerability. The SoVI is widely used largely due to its reliance on openly accessible statistical data, which has encouraged scholars from diverse national and regional contexts to engage in social vulnerability research. However, the development of the SoVI was primarily driven by general environmental hazards and census data specific to the United States, giving rise to limitations. First among these, the indicators have shown the characteristics of the region, making it harder to effectively transfer the contents to new geographic units. Second, the SoVI lacks the sensitivity required to establish direct connections with specific disasters. Consequently, researchers in different regions or in varying disaster contexts often adjust the indicators to meet their specific research needs. For instance, Mendes et al. incorporated infrastructure indicators into their social vulnerability measurement to align with the requirements of Portugal’s municipal civil protection plans9. Ye et al. included indicators such as port cargo throughput to reflect the exposure characteristics of islands14. Li et al. emphasized the connection between the indicator system and flooding by adding hydraulic engineering-related indicators to their flood social vulnerability measurement15. Ruan et al. enhanced their natural disaster social vulnerability measurement by including indicators that reflect the urban-rural development gap through the capture of regional development characteristics16. These studies retained conceptual and statistical features of the SoVI but adjusted its indicators. As research demands continue to evolve, an increasing number of researchers are refining indicators to better suit various spatial scales and different types of disasters, such as hurricanes17,18, floods19,20, and earthquakes21,22. Inspired by these researchers, we deemed developing a social vulnerability measurement framework that accounts for regional differences crucial to effectively mitigate geological disaster risks. Additionally, the new framework needs to establish a closer connection with the specific characteristics of geological disasters. This is necessary for regions such as Guizhou that frequently experience geological disasters.

According to Wisner, disaster risk is a composite function of natural hazards and the features of exposed population in a particular spacetime (social vulnerability). This can be summarized in the formula Risk = Hazard*Vulnerability23. The United Nations International Strategy for Disaster Reduction (UNISDR) has extended the principle of risk to include human-induced disasters, which suggests that disaster risk increases due to the interaction of natural or technological disasters with vulnerability, resulting in negative outcomes24,25. Social vulnerability, as a key component of disaster risk, has propelled research progress in risk assessment. However, several crucial issues continue to obstruct the practical application of social vulnerability to disaster mitigation planning. For instance, databases based on detailed geological disaster investigations have primarily focused on geological environmental surveys while neglecting socioeconomic environments. Without quantifying the impact of social vulnerability, problems such as the disaster avoidance challenges deaf families face cannot be fully addressed.

Previous studies have primarily focused on physical vulnerability of geological disaster, emphasizing susceptibility through the measurement of geological and environmental factors (e.g., slope gradient) and triggering factors (e.g., rainfall)26–29. These well-developed methods and frameworks have significantly advanced disaster prevention and mitigation efforts. However, a robust system for assessing social vulnerability to geological disasters remains underdeveloped30. As part of the risk assessment matrix, vulnerability measurement typically relies on traditional indicators such as “population density,” “transportation network density,” and “housing density”26,27,29. For example, Liu et al. conducted a geological disaster risk measurement for Bangor County, selecting population density, buildings, and transportation infrastructure as the three key factors of vulnerability, with a focus on measuring susceptibility to geological disasters26. As a result, the quantification of social vulnerability in disaster risk assessments continues to influence the scientific validity of disaster management measures31. Hou et al. approached social vulnerability to geological disasters as an independent topic, rather than as part of the risk assessment, addressing the challenge of using statistical data to quantify the complex of social vulnerability to geological disasters based on the SoVI32. However, their research mainly applied economic methods to analyze vulnerability factors, placing less emphasis on disaster information and the impact of vulnerability. Currently, compared to controlling geological disasters, managing vulnerability offers a simpler and more feasible approach in risk management, yet discussions on this topic remain limited31, and its role has not been fully highlighted in disaster prevention and mitigation practices. In response, this study builds on the use of statistical data by expanding the measurement framework of social vulnerability to geological disasters through an inductive approach, incorporating key indicators such as the population at risk from geological disaster sites33,34. This could strengthen the connection between the indicator system and specific disasters and regional background. Finally, verifying the impact of the social vulnerability index on geological disaster risk using statistical disaster data may offer new evidence into the critical role of social vulnerability in the management of geological disasters.

Geological disasters are widespread across China, particularly in the mountainous regions of the southwest. In recent years, rapid urbanization has led to the sprawling expansion of disaster-bearing bodies, such as populations and infrastructure, into geological disaster-prone zones35. Guizhou Province, a typical example of a southwestern region, is characterized by steep and bedding slopes, and many villages and towns are built on mountainsides. This geographic reality means that numerous residents face long-term threats from geological disaster hazards.

The objective of the study was to develop a social vulnerability to geological disasters index (SVI) in Guizhou Province, based on the SoVI. We integrated mathematical models such as principal component analysis (PCA) to access a wider range of result formats and identified key components of social vulnerability to geological disasters. Using historical disaster data from Guizhou to verify the impact weightings of social vulnerability on disaster risk. On the one hand, this approach may directly draw geological disaster management agencies’ attention to social vulnerability and serve as a valuable decision-support tool. On the other hand, although regional differences may limit the full transfer of the indicator system, the ability to link historical disaster data, hazard exposure, and social vulnerability indicators is practically significant for other regions similarly affected by geological disasters. This connection offers a meaningful pathway for reducing geological disaster risks.

Sect “Methods” shows the framework design and models. Sect “Results and analysis” presents the results and a brief analysis. Sect “Discussion” details requiring more investigation, followed by Sect “Conclusion”.

Study area and data sources

Study area

The southwest Chinese province of Guizhou has nine jurisdictions, namely Guiyang, Zunyi, Liupanshui, Anshun, Bijie, Tongren, Qiandongnan, Qiannan, and Qianxinan, totaling 88 county-level units. Guizhou covers the latitudes 24°37′ to 29°13′N and the longitudes 103°36′ to 109°35′E. It was once the focus of national poverty alleviation efforts because of its high incidence of severe and widespread poverty.

Natural conditions

Guizhou is in the slope zone of the Yunnan-Guizhou Plateau. Over 92.5% of the territory is mountainous and hilly, and the landscape has been affected by river erosion and is characterized by medium to high mountains, low mountains, hills, intermountain basins, deep valleys, etc36. The region is intersected by numerous folds and faults, resulting in a highly complex geological structure. Extensive carbonate rock formations, coupled with fragmented surface bedrock, contributing to a fragile geological environment. Deeply incised river valleys and significant elevation gradients drive intense erosion processes, serving as a primary catalyst for geological disasters35. Guizhou has a subtropical monsoon climate, which is further influenced by the landscape, leading to climate variation. In summer, especially in the western region, high-intensity rainfall events form frequently when the cool and warm air masses meet frequently because of in the zone of transition zone between the southeast and southwest monsoons, which are the essential weather factors that lead to the occurrence of geological disasters in Guizhou.

The main geological disasters in the region include landslides, collapses, mudslides, ground collapses, ground fissures, and unstable slopes, with landslides and collapses being very common37. According to historical records, as of 2021, 8383 geological disaster risk sites were documented (Fig. 1) as potentially threatening over 2 million people. Guizhou experienced over 1800 geological disasters between 2011 and 2020, of which 65 were classified as major disasters. In that decade, there were over 500 fatalities and economic losses totaling approximately 1.7 billion yuan35. Therefore, adequate disaster reduction still seems to be a distant notion.

Data sources

A dataset for social vulnerability to geological disasters in Guizhou was established with reference to scholars Cutter et al.8 and Mendes et al.9 Socioeconomic statistics were based on the 2020 census data. After calibration with 2010 census data, these were the foundation for the 2020 social vulnerability to geological disasters measurement. The statistics were obtained from the National Bureau of Statistics. The geological disaster risk site records were updated until early 2021, and the fatality statistics were extracted from the years 2010 to 2020. Both datasets were obtained from the Guizhou Provincial Government Data Open Platform. The geological disaster-prone zone map of Guizhou province was obtained from the Guizhou Geological Environment Monitoring Institute.

Methods

Study design

Despite the availability of several approaches to measure social vulnerability across single and multiple disasters, developing a framework remains a challenge due to the various county-level statistical data and the complex geological environments. We conducted empirical analysis of geological disasters and adopted induction to choose strongly characterized indicators. Using ArcGIS to visually analyze social vulnerability, disaster exposure, and related fatalities. This method entailed intuitively judging the need to analyze social vulnerability during risk measurement. Finally, applying the negative binomial regression model (NB) and the zero-inflated negative binomial regression model (ZINB) to verify the impact of social vulnerability based on variables. Measuring social vulnerability

Indicator selection is crucial for ensuring reliable results when measuring the social vulnerability to geological disasters index (SVI)38. We considered the following main aspects: (1) geological disasters exposure, (2) inherent sensitivity of pre-disaster social systems, and (3) support capacity of social systems in times of disaster. To describe the measurement variables, we used two dimensions: sensitivity and support capacity12. Our selection of indicators covers the main aspects of social vulnerability, prioritizing those the existing literature has validated. Support capacity (Sc)

Support capacity refers to the physical foundation that regions employ to prevent and resist disasters. It is negatively correlated with social vulnerability. Sixteen indicators were selected to characterize support capacity (Table 1).

Economic support16,40,41. Regional economic strength is represented by per capita gross domestic product (GDP) and fiscal revenue. A region’s economic strength is a source of critical economic support to ensure the availability of supplies during disasters. Relatedly, standard of living is represented by per capita income and plays a crucial role in self-rescue.

Labor support16,41. The workforce furnishes human resources to mount a comprehensive emergency disaster response. For this indicator, we used the percentage of the population aged 15–59 years.

Social infrastructure supports12,40. During disasters, issuing alarm information promptly via communication channels such as radio and television is crucial. Social facilities such as health and service organizations can provide temporary shelter, offering accommodation, necessities, and immediate health care. When disasters strike, extensive social infrastructure coverage enhances the efficiency of local response efforts.

Social security support42. Social security payments reflect recovery capacity by transferring the burden of disaster risk from individuals to the collective, thereby enhancing resilience.

Variable selection for verifying the impact of social vulnerability

To verify the impact of social vulnerability on disaster risk, we selected fatalities as the dependent variable to represent disaster risk, and the SVI as the independent variable. Additionally, it is necessary to incorporate geological and environmental factors as control variables. These factors are complex and mainly concern the geological conditions, weather patterns, and human engineering activities in the region. Geological conditions are direct factors in the occurrence of disasters, whereas heavy rainfall and engineering activities are triggering factors37. Introducing geological and environmental factors into the model could have led to the omission of important variables. To avoid that, we adopted geological disaster-prone zones as control variables. Zoning is assessed based on various data obtained from topography, rock and soil mass, geological formation, hydrological conditions, engineering activities, and field surveys. It serves as a crucial reference for disaster prevention efforts35. Vector information on geological disaster-prone zones is eventually transformed into area indices of geological disaster-prone zones in Guizhou counties. Due to the absence of non-prone zones, we chose the high and low area indices of prone zones as control variables to balance geological disaster exposure. Sensitivity analysis

The calculation of composite indices is often informed by the researchers’ judgment rather than universally accepted scientific principles, making sensitivity analysis an essential step47. The principal component analysis in this study involved uncertainties in the choice of methods for data standardization, principal component rotation, and aggregation, necessitating a process of self-validation. It is important to note that the data structure of the SVI has a significant impact on the results, both in the analysis of spatial variability and in subsequent regression models. Hence, the sensitivity analysis focused on changes in county rankings and the correlation between results derived from different methods.

The first pathway in the sensitivity analysis investigated the key steps in the PCA calculation. To evaluate the influence of different data standardization methods, both Z-score and Min-Max normalization were applied to the raw data for SVI computation. The results demonstrated minimal impact, with no changes in county rankings, indicating that the choice of standardization method had only a minor influence. Additionally, the impact of principal component rotation methods was assessed using both Promax and Varimax rotations. The average ranking change was 2.82 positions, indicating that rotation method choice had a minor influence. The aggregation method was also tested by substituting the original subtraction aggregation with division aggregation, resulting in an average ranking change of only 1.32 positions. This finding demonstrate that the choice of aggregation method exerted minimal influence on the results. Overall, SVI ranking changes ranged from 0 to 2.82 positions, suggesting a high degree of robustness in the measurement model.

The second pathway involved a comparison between the results obtained using the arithmetic mean method and those derived from the PCA method. The Pearson correlation coefficient between the two approaches was 0.96, demonstrating strong statistical significance. This high degree of correlation indicates that the PCA method effectively captures the information in the raw data, thereby reinforcing the validity and reliability of the SVI calculated through this approach.

Multicollinearity test

Multicollinearity is a widespread phenomenon characterized by strong connections between independent variables. Numerous scenarios may give rise to the challenge of multicollinearity, such as inappropriate variable selection stemming from cognitive constraints or modeling with cross-sectional data, resulting in a lack of meaningful interpretations of the model parameters48. Collinearity (or near collinearity) among independent variables can severely impact parameter estimation, invalidating the results. This problem can also obscure the significance of individual variables, and in some cases, may even lead to parameter signs that contradict the actual relationships (e.g., positive or negative effects appearing reversed)49. To avoid this, before modeling, we tested the variables for multicollinearity and obtained the variance inflation factor (VIF) for all variables (Table 6). A VIF value below 10 is considered good for a variable. In this study, the VIF values were close to 1, indicating a lack of significant correlations among the variables and confirming the appropriateness of incorporating them into the model for regression estimation.

Judgment of fitting results

The fatality data in this study exhibited overdispersion (variance exceeding the mean), which necessitated the use of the NB model. However, This model is limited when processing dependent variables with many of zeros. It assumes that the zeros are true zeros, meaning no fatalities were recorded due to the absence of geological disasters. To address this, the ZINB model was applied for comparative analysis, first estimating the probability of fatality occurrence and then predicting the number of fatalities. Even with this approach, the issue of excessive zeros (superinflation)50 still required careful consideration. In response, this study used the 10-year average fatality data from 2010 to 2020 across Guizhou counties as the dependent variable. Additionally, exposure variables were defined as the population affected by geological disasters rather than the total county population, helping to correct for errors associated with jurisdiction size.

With reference to the log-likelihood values, G test statistics were used to estimate the significance level of the NB model51, G = −2(LLnull−LLmodel) = 25.198. According to the chi-square distribution and a degrees of freedom (df) value of 5 (independent variables), the probability of estimating the test statistic value was below 0.001. It can be inferred that the variables exerted an important impact on the geological disaster fatality rates. Similarly, using G test statistics to estimate the ZINB model, G = −2(LLnull−LLmodel) = 16.378. According to the chi-square distribution, the probability of estimating the test statistic was below 0.01, indicating that the variables in the ZINB model also significantly impacted on the geological disaster fatality rates.

Analysis of the ZINB model results

The ZINB model offered two potential explanations for regions with zero fatalities. The first is that the dependent variable, geological disaster fatalities, had a value of 0 due to the absence of geological disasters in certain counties (true zeros). The other purports that although geological disasters have occurred in some counties, the absence of fatalities can be attributed to the resilience of the social systems, resulting in the dependent variable also taking a value of 0 (sampling zeros). The logit part estimated the probability of true zeros, such as the likelihood of counties not experiencing geological disasters. Fitting the zero-inflation component with the independent variable LAI was appropriate because counties with a high proportion of low-prone zones are less likely to experience geological disasters. This could be a major factor contributing to the zero-inflation phenomenon observed in the dataset. The model showed that the baseline odds of counties not experiencing geological disasters was 0.0759, which may be attributed to the fact that in the selected dataset, 30 counties without low-prone zones accounted for as much as 67.25% of the total deaths. The odds of geological disasters not occurring increase by 6.646 for each unit increase in the LAI. The results (Table 7) from the NB part were statistically significant: SVI (p < 0.01) and HR (p < 0.05). The baseline fatality risk was 0.00047, showing a good predictiveness. Each unit increase in SVI results in a 6.33% increase in fatality risk (given by Exp (1.0633)). An increase in HAI increases the fatality risk by 14.52%. Inferred from the coefficients that the control variables showed a clear trend that aligns with common knowledge and effectively controlled the influence of other factors in this model. The ZINB model established a separate regression model for the excess of 0 values, enhancing the robustness of the regression results for the count component compared to those of the NB model. This makes the regression coefficients of the SVI more meaningful.

Discussion

This study established a framework for measuring social vulnerability to geological disasters and verified its impact on disaster risk. The index measurement is supported by an existing methodological framework (SoVI). To capture the characteristics of counties in Guizhou, the index system incorporates a support capacity dimension. A sensitivity analysis was conducted from two perspectives: the principal component calculation process and a comparison of results obtained using different methods, to assess the robustness of the SVI results. The verified section of the SVI indicates that the ZINB model’s fitting performance is superior to that of the NB model. Overall, social vulnerability to geological disasters in Guizhou is generally high, significantly amplifying disaster risk. We observed a strong correlation between the risk of fatalities, representing geological disaster risk, and social vulnerability. The results effectively captured the situation in counties such as Dafang (SVI = 4.97), Qianxi (SVI = 4.53), Zhijin (SVI = 5.05), and Weining (SVI = 5.28), where very high social vulnerability correlated with an elevated risk of fatalities. The extent of this amplification varied across counties, and addressing the root causes of very high social vulnerability may provide a practical approach to reducing geological disaster risks. Significantly, however, there are anomalous regions in the findings. For example, despite relatively favorable geological conditions and moderate social vulnerability, Fuquan exhibited a high risk of fatalities. This anomaly could be linked to the younger cities and population and to its low frequency of disasters, which may have resulted in residents having a reduced awareness of risk and inexperience with disaster responses. This highlights the potential influence of personal cognitive behaviors such as disaster recognition and response capabilities. However, owing to the limited availability of data, this aspect warrants further investigation in future studies.

The study revealed significant spatial differences in the social vulnerability to geological disasters index. Counties with low social vulnerability are primarily concentrated near the economic center and secondary centers of Guizhou. Contrastingly, high social vulnerability to geological disasters is clustered in Bijie. However, according to Guizhou Province’s 13th Five-Year Plan for geological disaster prevention and control, the proportion of disaster management and relocation projects in Bijie has been relatively low (10.43% and 13.41%, respectively), despite Bijie accounting for over 40% of geological disaster fatalities in Guizhou over the past decade. These findings challenge reliance on disaster exposure as the primary basis for formulating geological disaster management strategies. By revealing the components of social vulnerability in terms of sensitivity and support capacity, the study aim to enhance the influence of social vulnerability in geological disaster risk management and encourage its positioning as a crucial tool for informed decision-making.

These results, integrated with the spatial variability of social vulnerability, provide additional tools for disaster risk reduction to support Guizhou Province’s 14th Five-Year Plan for Geological Disaster Prevention and Control.

The resultant approach is expected to better balance the traditional approach of categorizing priority, sub-priority, and general prevention zones based on disaster exposure. Elevating prevention and control measures in areas with high and very high social vulnerability is essential. Disaster management plans should adjust strategies based on the varying dimensions of social vulnerability. For instance, in regions with very high vulnerability, especially those facing significant geological disaster risk sites, it is crucial not only to monitor disasters in real time but also to establish additional information dissemination channels to strengthen early warning systems and enhance residents’ risk perception. In such zones, the population often has a reduced capacity to respond to disasters, and the entire process of receiving, comprehending, and acting on disaster information can become a critical vulnerability during disaster response. Therefore, during the disaster risk survey phase, information about highly vulnerable groups should be prioritized and recorded within disaster monitoring and evaluation systems, which could help facilitate effective early warning and rescue efforts for different population groups during disaster response. At the provincial level, it is important to conduct a comprehensive assessment of the feasibility of population relocation in areas where very high vulnerability coincides with significant geological disaster risk sites. Implementing incentive policies and systematically guiding populations to relocate to lower-risk areas could greatly reduce the strain on emergency management departments during disaster response. Given the substantial financial investment disaster management and relocation (avoidance) projects require, selecting sites urgently needing remediation for engineering projects is critical. Integrating the SVI with the spatial variability of geological disaster susceptibility provides a more accurate site selection basis.

This study had several limitations. First, incorporating institutional indicators that reflect support capacity, such as the percentage of geological disaster risk sites treated or relocated per completed or projected government plans, into the social vulnerability measurement system is challenging in terms of transforming the indicators into available data. We hope to acquire more high-quality data to refine the analysis of social vulnerability.

Second, when using statistical indicators as proxies to measure social vulnerability, setting weights is a common issue. We used an equal weighting strategy to minimize the subjective effect of weight assignment in the measurement of the SVI. This has limitations in its rationale, as emphasized in the reviews by Mendes et al. concerning the SoVI9. Addressing this may require an in-depth investigation across the disaster process.

Third, the data we used incorporate geospatial information. However, the NB and ZINB models both belong to the globally homogeneous regression technique, which restricts the ability to analyze spatial relationships. Future research should introduce spatial regression models to establish local regression models for each county, thereby enhancing the reliability of the results.

Fourth, although using administrative divisions as the analysis units is beneficial for collecting and processing statistical data (a key reason for the effective replication and reference of the SoVI), its application to geological disaster emergency management is limited, particularly regarding the precise localization of vulnerable populations, due to the low spatial resolution. This limitation has long challenged the refinement of social vulnerability. Some researchers have attempted to reduce the spatial scale by introducing disaster buffer zones, but the lack of accessible demographic data within these buffers often results in significant discrepancies between the measurement outcomes and the actual situation. Future efforts can focus on building a population information database around geological hazard sites. Social vulnerability measurement can then be conducted based on key disaster points and their impact areas, as identified by geological experts, to improve measurement precision. This approach could enhance measurement granularity, rendering the results more useful for emergency management.

Finally, the availability of historical disaster information, especially in less developed regions such as Guizhou, has limited the analysis of temporal trends in disaster fluctuations and the dynamic development of social vulnerability. To overcome this issue, future work could leverage global databases with longer time spans, such as the Global Human Settlement Layer and Land Use/Land Cover, to extract variables that better represent geological disaster risks.

Conclusion

Guizhou Province has experienced over ten huge geological disasters since 2010. These disasters have resulted in numerous fatalities, placing Guizhou among China’s most disaster affected regions. Although technologies such as InSAR, LiDAR, and drones are used to investigate hidden dangers, each has inherent limitations. Therefore, the work of preventing, reducing, and responding to disasters cannot solely rely on technological applications. Accurately recognizing social vulnerability is also essential. This study developed a regional paradigm to measure social vulnerability to geological disasters in two dimensions: sensitivity and support capability. The SVI was obtained through dimensionality reduction calculations based on indicators. Visualizing disaster exposure, disaster losses, and the SVI revealed an inconsistency in the correlation between fatality numbers and geological disaster-prone zones across counties, which reaffirms the limitations of measuring fatality risk based solely on disaster exposure. This study presents an approach for measuring and verifying the SVI, dedicated to serving the goal of a safer environment.
