Time-frequency localization in the Fourier Symmetric Sobolev space Thanks: The author was supported by Grant 334466 of the Research Council of Norway.
Denis Zelent

Abstract
. We study concentration operators acting on the Fourier symmetric Sobolev space ℋ \mathcal{H} consisting of functions f f such that ∫ ℝ | f ⁡ ( x ) | 2 ​ ( 1 + x 2 ) ​ 𝑑 x + ∫ ℝ | f ^ ​ ( ξ ) | 2 ​ ( 1 + ξ 2 ) ​ 𝑑 ξ < ∞ \int_{\mathbb{R}}|f(x)|^{2}(1+x^{2})dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\hat{f}(\xi)|^{2}(1+\xi^{2})d\xi<\infty . We find that the Bargmann transform is a unitary operator from ℋ \mathcal{H} to a weighted Fock space. After identifying the reproducing kernel of ℋ \mathcal{H} , we discover an unexpected phenomenon about the decay of the eigenvalues of a two-sided concentration operator, namely that the plunge region is of the same order of magnitude as the region where the eigenvalues are close to 1, contrasting the classical case of Paley–Wiener spaces.



1.
Introduction

The Fourier symmetric Sobolev space
\mathcal{H}
is defined to be

\mathcal{H}=\{f:f,\hat{f}\in\mathcal{H}_{1},\|f\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}=\|f\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}+\|\hat{f}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}\},

where
\mathcal{H}_{1}
is the Sobolev space of functions
f
on
\mathbb{R}
such that

\|f\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\hat{f}(\xi)|^{2}(1+\xi^{2})d\xi<\infty,

with
\hat{f}(t)=\mathcal{F}f(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)e^{-2\pi itx}dx.

Since
\mathcal{H}
treats time and frequency in a symmetric way and is invariant under the Fourier transform, it may reflect interesting aspects of the uncertainty principle. For example, the Balian–Low theorem states that there is no function
g\in\mathcal{H}
such that the Gabor system
\{e^{2\pi imbx}g(x-na)\}_{m,n\in\mathbb{Z}}
with
ab=1
forms an orthonormal basis for
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
(see e.g.
[
7
, §8.4]
).
In addition,
\mathcal{H}
provides a natural setting for an analytic study of Fourier interpolation, as shown in the recent paper
[
8
, Thm. 1.3 and the following discussion]
.
It also appears in the study of pseudodifferential operators as one of the Shubin–Sobolev spaces (also called Shubin classes), which turns out to be one of the modulation spaces on
\mathbb{R}
, namely

\displaystyle M_{v_{1}}^{2}(\mathbb{R})=\left\{f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}):||f||_{M_{v_{1}}^{2}}=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|V_{g}f(x,\omega)|^{2}(1+x^{2}+\omega^{2})dxd\omega\right)^{1/2}<\infty\right\},

where
g(t)=2^{1/4}e^{-\pi t^{2}}
is the normalized gaussian and

\displaystyle V_{g}f(x,\omega)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(t)\overline{g(t-x)}e^{-2\pi i\omega t}dt

is the definition of the short-time Fourier transform, see e.g.
[
1
, §2.2]
,
[
2
]
,
[
12
]
and
[
14
, §25.3]
.

It is known from
[
8
]
that
\mathcal{H}
is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space, but the kernel has not been identified.
We will find an explicit formula for this kernel based on the discovery that the scaled Hermite functions form an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}
. Another proof of this, using that the Hermite functions are eigenfunctions of the Hamiltonian operator of the classical quantum harmonic oscillator, appeared in
[
16
]
after the announcement of the first version of the present paper. We obtain this result by showing that the Bargmann transform is a unitary operator from
\mathcal{H}
to the weighted Fock space with

||F||^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{C}}|F(z)|^{2}e^{-2|z|^{2}}\left(2\pi-\frac{1}{2}+2|z|^{2}\right)dA<\infty.

We will use the reproducing kernel
K_{t}
of
\mathcal{H}
to study the problem of maximizing the ratio

(1)

\displaystyle\frac{\int_{I}|f(x)|^{2}(1+x^{2})dx+\int_{J}|\hat{f}(\xi)|^{2}(1+\xi^{2})d\xi}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f(x)|^{2}(1+x^{2})dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\hat{f}(\xi)|^{2}(1+\xi^{2})d\xi},

where
I,J\subset\mathbb{R}
are measurable.
The inspiration for our study is the classical Paley–Wiener case (see e.g.
[
9
,
10
,
11
,
15
]
), where one seeks to maximize

\displaystyle\frac{\int_{I}|f(x)|^{2}dx}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f(x)|^{2}dx}

for
f
in the Paley–Wiener space. By standard arguments, we are in this case led to consider the eigenvalues of the Hilbert–Schmidt operator
S_{I}
defined by
S_{I}f(t)=\int_{I}f(x)\overline{k_{t}(x)}dx
, where
k_{t}(x)
is the reproducing kernel of the Paley–Wiener space. If
\lambda_{n}(I)
denotes the eigenvalues of
S_{I}
in decreasing order, one obtains
\sum_{n}\lambda_{n}(I)\sim\sum_{n}\lambda_{n}^{2}(I)
as
|I|\to\infty
, where as usual
f(x)\sim g(x)
means
\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=1.

This implies that the eigenvalues are at first very close to
1
, then there is a short plunge region where they decay, and afterwards they are very close to
0
. This behavior allows us, for example, to establish critical densities for sampling and interpolation, as was done in
[
10
]
.

In our case, the problem of maximizing (
1
) leads to the consideration of the eigenvalues of the operator
T_{I,J}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
defined as

T_{I,J}f(t)=\int_{I}f(x)\overline{K_{t}(x)}(1+x^{2})dx+\int_{J}\hat{f}(x)\overline{\hat{K_{t}}(x)}(1+x^{2})dx.

Interestingly, we discover that in this case, the plunge region is much longer. Indeed, if
\lambda_{n}
denotes the
n
th eigenvalue of
T_{I,I}
for
I=[-R,R]
, then
\lambda_{n}\in(0,1)
and

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\lambda_{n}

\displaystyle\sim 2\pi R^{2},\quad\sum_{n}\lambda_{n}^{2}\sim(2\pi-2)R^{2}.

We have not found a complete description of the asymptotic behavior of these eigenvalues, but the following theorem shows that many of them will cluster close to
1
.

Theorem 1
.

Fix
\epsilon>0
and let
I=[-R,R],J=[-T,T]
. Then there are

•

at least
4RT-O(\log R)-O(T)
eigenvalues of
T_{I,J}
larger than
1-\epsilon
,

•

at most
2\pi(R^{2}+T^{2})+o(R^{2})+o(T^{2})
eigenvalues of
T_{I,J}
larger than
\epsilon
,

where
R,T\to\infty
.

We conjecture that the number of these eigenvalues larger than
1-\epsilon
is also at most
4RT+o(RT)
. Using the
4RT-O(\log R)-O(T)
eigenvalues of
T_{I,J}
which are close to
1
, we may be able to improve the second part of the theorem to at most
2\pi(R^{2}+T^{2})-4RT+o(R^{2})+o(T^{2})
eigenvalues of
T_{I,J}
larger than
\epsilon
. However, the proof would need to be more complicated, and there is no reason to believe that the new bound would be optimal.

To prove Theorem
1
, we consider the analogous problem of concentration in the Paley–Wiener space
PW_{T}(1+x^{2})
of functions
f
with Fourier transform supported in
[-T,T]
and with the norm

||f||^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f(x)|^{2}(1+x^{2})dx<\infty.

Here we are interested in maximizing the ratio

\displaystyle\frac{\int_{-R}^{R}|f(x)|^{2}(1+x^{2})dx}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f(x)|^{2}(1+x^{2})dx},

which leads to considering the eigenvalues of the operator
W_{R}:PW_{T}(1+x^{2})\to PW_{T}(1+x^{2})
defined as

\displaystyle W_{R}f(t)=\int_{-R}^{R}f(x)\overline{L_{t}(x)}(1+x^{2})dx,

where
L_{t}
is the reproducing kernel of
PW_{T}(1+x^{2})
. For this operator, we obtain

Theorem 2
.

For any
\epsilon>0
there are
4RT+O(\log R)+O(T)
eigenvalues of
W_{R}
larger than
1-\epsilon
as
R,T\to\infty
.

Finally, we consider a one-sided concentration problem in
\mathcal{H}
, namely that of maximizing the ratio

(2)

\displaystyle\frac{\int_{I}|f(x)|^{2}(1+x^{2})dx}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f(x)|^{2}(1+x^{2})dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\hat{f}(\xi)|^{2}(1+\xi^{2})d\xi},

where
I\subset\mathbb{R}
measurable.
In this case, for the eigenvalues of the corresponding operator
T_{I}:~\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
defined as

T_{I}f(t)=\int_{I}f(x)\overline{K_{t}(x)}(1+x^{2})dx

we get

Theorem 3
.

Fix
0<\epsilon<\frac{1}{4}
and let
I=[-R,R]
. Then there are

•

at most
o(R^{2})
eigenvalues of
T_{I}
larger than
0.5+\epsilon
,

•

2\pi R^{2}+o(R^{2})
eigenvalues of
T_{I}
between
0.5-\epsilon
and
0.5+\epsilon
,

•

at most
o(R^{2})
eigenvalues of
T_{I}
between
\epsilon
and
0.5-\epsilon
,

where
R\to\infty.

It is interesting that most of the eigenvalues of
T_{I}
larger than
\epsilon
cluster around
\frac{1}{2}
, since, in general,
\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f(x)|^{2}(1+x^{2})dx
and
\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\hat{f}(\xi)|^{2}(1+\xi^{2})d\xi
may differ considerably for a “generic”
f\in\mathcal{H}
. Theorem
3
also shows that the plunge region for the eigenvalues of
T_{I}
is negligible.

The results of this paper exhibit a clear distinction from the well-studied Gabor–Toeplitz localization operator, which for a nonnegative, bounded function
a
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is defined as

\displaystyle T_{a}f(t)=\iint\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}a(x,\omega)V_{g}f(x,\omega)g(t-x)e^{2\pi it\omega}dxd\omega.

See e.g.
[
3
,
5
,
4
,
6
]
for the study of
T_{a}
.
When, in particular,
a
is the indicator function of
[-R,R]\times[-T,T]
, Theorem 4.1 in
[
3
]
shows that for the Gabor–Toeplitz localization operator
T_{a}
, the size of the plunge region is small compared with the size of the region where the eigenvalues are close to 1. Note that the concentration operator
T_{[-R,R],[-T,T]}
studied in this paper also measures the time-frequency concentration of
f
on
[-R,R]\times[-T,T]
in accordance with (
1
), but produces a different eigenvalue behaviour.

Theorem
3
sheds some light on this difference. Consider, for example,
I=[-R,R]
,
J=[-\epsilon,\epsilon]
for some fixed
R
. By decreasing the
\epsilon
, the eigenvalues of the Gabor–Toeplitz operator with
a
being the indicator function of
I\times J
will necessarily decrease to
0
. At the same time,

\displaystyle\frac{\int_{I}|f(x)|^{2}(1+x^{2})dx+\int_{J}|\hat{f}(\xi)|^{2}(1+\xi^{2})d\xi}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f(x)|^{2}(1+x^{2})dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\hat{f}(\xi)|^{2}(1+\xi^{2})d\xi}\geq\frac{\int_{I}|f(x)|^{2}(1+x^{2})dx}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f(x)|^{2}(1+x^{2})dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\hat{f}(\xi)|^{2}(1+\xi^{2})d\xi},

and by Theorem
3
there are many eigenvalues of
T_{I,J}
around
0.5
.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we find the closed formula for the reproducing kernel of
\mathcal{H}
. In Section 3, we discuss the concentration problem (
1
). In Section 4, we turn our attention to the concentration operator in the weighted Paley–Wiener space
PW_{T}(1+x^{2})
. In Section 5, we return to the space
\mathcal{H}
and examine the concentration problem (
2
). Finally, in Section 6, we prove Theorem
1
.

2.
The reproducing kernel of
\mathcal{H}

In this section, we find the reproducing kernel of
\mathcal{H}
, which was done as part of the author’s master’s thesis
[
18
]
. For the convenience of the reader, we include this computation here.

Since a reproducing kernel can be expressed using an orthonormal basis, we want to find a suitable orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}
. We will show that such a basis consists of scaled Hermite functions

e^{-\pi x^{2}}H_{n}(\sqrt{2\pi}x),
where
H_{n}
denotes the
n
th Hermite polynomial.
We begin with the following lemma.

Lemma 1
.

If
n>m+2
,
n,m\geq 0
, then

\int_{\mathbb{R}}H_{n}(\sqrt{2\pi}x)H_{m}(\sqrt{2\pi}x)e^{-2\pi x^{2}}(1+x^{2})dx=0.

Proof.

The lemma follows by using
H_{n}(x)=(-1)^{n}e^{x^{2}}\frac{d^{n}}{dx^{n}}e^{-x^{2}}
and
n
integrations by parts:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}H_{n}(\sqrt{2\pi}x)H_{m}(\sqrt{2\pi}x)e^{-2\pi x^{2}}(1+x^{2})dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(-1)^{n}\left(\frac{d^{n}}{dx^{n}}e^{-x^{2}}\right)H_{m}(x)\left(1+\frac{x^{2}}{2\pi}\right)dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\frac{d^{n}}{dx^{n}}H_{m}(x)\left(1+\frac{x^{2}}{2\pi}\right)\right)e^{-x^{2}}dx=0,

as
H_{m}(x)
is a polynomial of degree
m
, and therefore
\frac{d^{n}}{dx^{n}}H_{m}(x)\left(1+\frac{x^{2}}{2\pi}\right)=0
for
n>m+2
.
∎

Lemma 2
.

The Hermite functions
H_{n}(\sqrt{2\pi}x)e^{-\pi x^{2}}
are pairwise orthogonal in
\mathcal{H}
.

Proof.

We will use the fact that the Hermite functions are eigenvalues of the Fourier transform:
\mathcal{F}(H_{n}(\sqrt{2\pi}x)e^{-\pi x^{2}})(\xi)=(-i)^{n}H_{n}(\sqrt{2\pi}\xi)e^{-\pi\xi^{2}}
. For any
n,m\geq 0
we have

\displaystyle\langle H_{n}(\sqrt{2\pi}x)

\displaystyle e^{-\pi x^{2}},H_{m}(\sqrt{2\pi}x)e^{-\pi x^{2}}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}H_{n}(\sqrt{2\pi}x)e^{-\pi x^{2}}\overline{H_{m}(\sqrt{2\pi}x)e^{-\pi x^{2}}}(1+x^{2})dx

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{F}(H_{n}(\sqrt{2\pi}x)e^{-\pi x^{2}})(\xi)\overline{\mathcal{F}(H_{m}(\sqrt{2\pi}x)e^{-\pi x^{2}})(\xi)}(1+\xi^{2})d\xi

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}H_{n}(\sqrt{2\pi}x)H_{m}(\sqrt{2\pi}x)e^{-2\pi x^{2}}(1+x^{2})dx+

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathbb{R}}(-i)^{n}H_{n}(\sqrt{2\pi}\xi)e^{-\pi\xi^{2}}(\xi)\overline{(-i)^{m}}H_{m}(\sqrt{2\pi}\xi)e^{-\pi\xi^{2}}(1+\xi^{2})d\xi

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}H_{n}(\sqrt{2\pi}x)H_{m}(\sqrt{2\pi}x)e^{-2\pi x^{2}}(1+x^{2})dx(1+(-i)^{n}i^{m}).

Note that if
n,m
differs by one, then
H_{n}H_{m}
is an odd function, and so the integral is zero. If
n,m
differ by two, then
(1+(-i)^{n}i^{m})=0
. If
n,m
differ by more than two, then the integral is zero by Lemma
1
. Thus, if
n\neq m
, then
\langle H_{n}(\sqrt{2\pi}x)e^{-\pi x^{2}},H_{m}(\sqrt{2\pi}x)e^{-\pi x^{2}}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=0
.
∎

To normalize the functions, we need to find the norms of
H_{n}(\sqrt{2\pi}x)e^{-\pi x^{2}}
.

Lemma 3
.

For every
n\geq 0
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}H_{n}^{2}(\sqrt{2\pi}x)e^{-2\pi x^{2}}(1+x^{2})dx=2^{n-\frac{3}{2}}n!\left(n+2\pi+\frac{1}{2}\right)\frac{1}{\pi}.

Thus

\|H_{n}(\sqrt{2\pi}x)e^{-\pi x^{2}}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}=2^{n-\frac{1}{2}}n!\left(n+2\pi+\frac{1}{2}\right)\frac{1}{\pi}.

Proof.

Notice first, that using again
H_{n}(x)=(-1)^{n}e^{x^{2}}\frac{d^{n}}{dx^{n}}e^{-x^{2}}
and
n
integrations by parts, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{n}H_{n}(\sqrt{2\pi}x)e^{-2\pi x^{2}}dx

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-\frac{n+1}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{n}H_{n}(x)e^{-x^{2}}dx=(2\pi)^{-\frac{n+1}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{n}(-1)^{n}\frac{d^{n}}{dx^{n}}e^{-x^{2}}dx

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-\frac{n+1}{2}}n!\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-x^{2}}dx=(2\pi)^{-\frac{n+1}{2}}n!\sqrt{\pi},

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{n+2}H_{n}(\sqrt{2\pi}x)e^{-2\pi x^{2}}dx

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-\frac{n+3}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{n+2}(-1)^{n}\frac{d^{n}}{dx^{n}}e^{-x^{2}}dx

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-\frac{n+3}{2}}\frac{(n+2)!}{2!}\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{2}e^{-x^{2}}dx=(2\pi)^{-\frac{n+3}{2}}\frac{(n+2)!}{2}\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2},

for any
m<n

\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{m}H_{n}(\sqrt{2\pi}x)e^{-2\pi x^{2}}dx=0.

Combining this with the fact that
H_{n}
can be written as

H_{n}(\sqrt{2\pi}x)=n!\sum_{m=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\frac{(-1)^{m}}{m!(n-2m)!}(2\sqrt{2\pi}x)^{n-2m},

we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}H_{n}^{2}(\sqrt{2\pi}x)e^{-2\pi x^{2}}(1+x^{2})dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left((2\sqrt{2\pi})^{n}x^{n}-\frac{n!}{(n-2)!}(2\sqrt{2\pi}x)^{n-2}\right)H_{n}(\sqrt{2\pi}x)e^{-2\pi x^{2}}(1+x^{2})dx

\displaystyle=(2\sqrt{2\pi})^{n}(2\pi)^{-\frac{n+1}{2}}n!\sqrt{\pi}+(2\sqrt{2\pi})^{n}(2\pi)^{-\frac{n+3}{2}}\frac{(n+2)!}{2}\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}-\frac{(2\sqrt{2\pi})^{n-2}n!}{(n-2)!}(2\pi)^{-\frac{n+1}{2}}n!\sqrt{\pi}

\displaystyle=2^{n}(2\pi)^{-\frac{3}{2}}n!\sqrt{\pi}\left(n+2\pi+\frac{1}{2}\right).

∎

It remains to show that the Hermite functions constitute a complete sequence in
\mathcal{H}
.
The idea of doing so is based on
[
13
]
. To this end we introduce the Bargmann transform

\mathfrak{B}:f\to F(z)=(\mathfrak{B}f)(z)=\frac{2^{1/4}}{\pi^{3/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\right)e^{2tz-z^{2}-t^{2}/2}dt.

If we let
\mathcal{B}_{\beta}
be the space of all entire functions satisfying

||F||^{2}_{\mathcal{B}_{\beta}}=\int_{\mathbb{C}}|F(z)|^{2}e^{-2|z|^{2}}\left(2\pi-\frac{1}{2}+2|z|^{2}\right)^{\beta}dA<\infty,

where
dA
denote the Lebesgue area measure on
\mathbb{C}
, then by
[
13
]

\mathfrak{B}
is, up to a constant factor, a unitary linear operator from
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
onto
\mathcal{B}_{0}
, and a bounded invertible mapping from
\mathcal{H}
onto
\mathcal{B}_{1}
. We will improve this result by showing that
\mathfrak{B}
is also a unitary operator from
\mathcal{H}
onto
\mathcal{B}_{1}
.
Proving that it sends an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{B}_{1}
to the orthonormal set of Hermite functions, we will conclude the proof.

Remark.
It is worth noting that the above are the only two cases when the Bargmann transform can be a unitary operator between the Schwartz scale
\chi_{\beta}
consisting of functions such that

\displaystyle||f||^{2}_{\chi_{\beta}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f(x)|^{2}(1+x^{2\beta})dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\hat{f}(\xi)|^{2}(1+\xi^{2\beta})d\xi<\infty,

and the corresponding space
\mathcal{B}_{\beta}
.

Lemma 4
.

The system
\left\{\sqrt{\frac{2^{n+1}}{n!(n+2\pi+1/2)\pi}}z^{n},n\geq 0\right\}
forms an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{B}_{1}
.

Proof.

Orthogonality is a trivial computation, while completeness follows from analyticity of functions in
\mathcal{B}_{1}
(any
f\in\mathcal{B}_{1}
can be written as
f(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}z^{n}
, and so
\langle f,z^{m}\rangle_{\mathcal{B}_{1}}=0
for every
m
implies that
a_{m}=0
for every
m
). Now,

\displaystyle\|z^{n}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{B}_{1}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{C}}|z|^{2n}e^{-2|z|^{2}}\left(2\pi-\frac{1}{2}+2|z|^{2}\right)dA=2\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{2n+1}e^{-2r^{2}}\left(2\pi-\frac{1}{2}+2r^{2}\right)dr

\displaystyle=2\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}(t/2)^{n+\frac{1}{2}}e^{-t}(2\pi-\frac{1}{2}+t)\frac{dt}{4\sqrt{t/2}}=\frac{2\pi}{4\cdot 2^{n}}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{n}e^{-t}(2\pi-\frac{1}{2}+t)dt

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{2^{n+1}}\left(\left(2\pi-\frac{1}{2}\right)n!+(n+1)!\right)=\frac{\pi n!}{2^{n+1}}(n+2\pi+1/2),

which concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 5
.

We have

\mathfrak{B}(H_{n}(\sqrt{2\pi}x)e^{-\pi x^{2}})(z)=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\pi^{1/4}}(2z)^{n},

and therefore

\mathfrak{B}^{-1}\left(\sqrt{\frac{2^{n+1}}{n!(n+2\pi+1/2)\pi}}z^{n}\right)=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2^{n-\frac{1}{2}}n!\left(n+2\pi+\frac{1}{2}\right)}}H_{n}(\sqrt{2\pi}x)e^{-\pi x^{2}}.

Proof.

The first part follows by
H_{n}(x)=(-1)^{n}e^{x^{2}}\frac{d^{n}}{dx^{n}}e^{-x^{2}}
and
n
integrations by parts:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{B}(H_{n}(\sqrt{2\pi}x)e^{-\pi x^{2}})(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{1/4}}{\pi^{3/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}H_{n}(x)e^{-x^{2}/2}e^{2xz-z^{2}-x^{2}/2}dx

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{1/4}}{\pi^{3/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(-1)^{n}\left(\frac{d^{n}}{dx^{n}}e^{-x^{2}}\right)e^{2xz-z^{2}}dx

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{1/4}}{\pi^{3/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-x^{2}}(2z)^{n}e^{2xz-z^{2}}dx

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{n+1/4}}{\pi}z^{n}.

This means that

\mathfrak{B}^{-1}(z^{n})=\frac{\pi}{2^{n+1/4}}e^{-\pi x^{2}}H_{n}(\sqrt{2\pi}x),

and thus

\displaystyle\mathfrak{B}^{-1}\left(\sqrt{\frac{2^{n+1}}{n!(n+2\pi+1/2)\pi}}z^{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2^{n+1}}{n!(n+2\pi+1/2)\pi}}\frac{\pi}{2^{n+1/4}}e^{-\pi x^{2}}H_{n}(\sqrt{2\pi}x)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2^{n-\frac{1}{2}}n!\left(n+2\pi+\frac{1}{2}\right)}}H_{n}(\sqrt{2\pi}x)e^{-\pi x^{2}}.

∎

Theorem 4
.

The Bargmann transform is a unitary operator from
\mathcal{H}
onto
\mathcal{B}_{1}
.
Thus, the system
\left\{\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2^{n-\frac{1}{2}}n!\left(n+2\pi+\frac{1}{2}\right)}}H_{n}(\sqrt{2\pi}x)e^{-\pi x^{2}},n\geq 0\right\}
forms an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}
.

Proof.

Our lemmas prove that for any
n,m\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\left\langle\sqrt{\frac{2^{n+1}}{n!(n+2\pi+1/2)\pi}}z^{n},\sqrt{\frac{2^{m+1}}{m!(m+2\pi+1/2)\pi}}z^{m}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{B}_{1}}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\mathfrak{B}^{-1}\left(\sqrt{\frac{2^{n+1}}{n!(n+2\pi+1/2)\pi}}z^{n}\right),\mathfrak{B}^{-1}\left(\sqrt{\frac{2^{m+1}}{m!(m+2\pi+1/2)\pi}}z^{m}\right)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}.

Since
\left\{\sqrt{\frac{2^{n+1}}{n!(n+2\pi+1/2)\pi}}z^{n},n\geq 0\right\}
forms an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{B}_{1}
, this proves that
\mathfrak{B}^{-1}
is a unitary transformation
\mathcal{B}_{1}\to\mathcal{H}
. By lemma
5
, since a unitary map sends an orthonormal basis to an orthonormal basis,
\left\{\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2^{n-\frac{1}{2}}n!\left(n+2\pi+\frac{1}{2}\right)}}H_{n}(\sqrt{2\pi}x)e^{-\pi x^{2}},n\geq 0\right\}
is an orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{H}
.
∎

Having obtained an orthonormal basis, we can now turn our attention to finding the reproducing kernel of
\mathcal{H}
.

Theorem 5
.

The reproducing kernel
K_{x}(y)
of
\mathcal{H}
is given by

K_{x}(y)=\sqrt{2}\pi e^{-\pi(x^{2}+y^{2})}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{t^{2\pi-1/2}}{\sqrt{1-t^{2}}}e^{2\pi\frac{2xyt-(x^{2}+y^{2})t^{2}}{1-t^{2}}}dt.

Proof.

Using the orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}
from Theorem
4
we find that

\displaystyle K_{x}(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{H_{n}(\sqrt{2\pi}x)e^{-\pi x^{2}}H_{n}(\sqrt{2\pi}y)e^{-\pi y^{2}}\pi}{2^{n-\frac{1}{2}}n!\left(n+2\pi+\frac{1}{2}\right)}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2}\pi e^{-\pi(x^{2}+y^{2})}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{H_{n}(\sqrt{2\pi}x)H_{n}(\sqrt{2\pi}y)}{2^{n}n!(n+2\pi+1/2)}.

To get the integral representation we will use Mehler’s Hermite polynomial formula
[
17
]
,

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{H_{n}(\sqrt{2\pi}x)H_{n}(\sqrt{2\pi}y)}{2^{n}n!}t^{n}=(1-t^{2})^{-1/2}e^{2\pi\frac{2xyt-(x^{2}+y^{2})t^{2}}{1-t^{2}}}.

Multiplying both sides by
t^{2\pi-1/2}
and then integrating from 0 to 1 gives

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{H_{n}(\sqrt{2\pi}x)H_{n}(\sqrt{2\pi}y)}{2^{n}n!(n+2\pi+1/2)}=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{t^{2\pi-1/2}}{\sqrt{1-t^{2}}}e^{2\pi\frac{2xyt-(x^{2}+y^{2})t^{2}}{1-t^{2}}}dt,

which concludes the proof.
∎

The Fourier transform of the kernel, which will also be used later, can be found in a similar way and is given in the following theorem.

Theorem 6
.

The Fourier transform of the reproducing kernel
K_{x}(y)
of
\mathcal{H}
is given by

\widehat{K_{x}}(y)=\sqrt{2}\pi e^{-\pi(x^{2}+y^{2})}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{t^{2\pi-1/2}}{\sqrt{1+t^{2}}}e^{2\pi\frac{-2xyti+(x^{2}+y^{2})t^{2}}{1+t^{2}}}dt.

Proof.

First, we will again use the facts that

K_{x}(y)=\sqrt{2}\pi e^{-\pi(x^{2}+y^{2})}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{H_{n}(\sqrt{2\pi}x)H_{n}(\sqrt{2\pi}y)}{2^{n}n!(n+2\pi+1/2)},

and that the Fourier transform of

e^{-\pi x^{2}}H_{n}(\sqrt{2\pi}x)
is equal to
(-i)^{n}e^{-\pi\xi^{2}}H_{n}(\sqrt{2\pi}\xi).

This gives

\widehat{K_{x}}(y)=\sqrt{2}\pi e^{-\pi(x^{2}+y^{2})}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-i)^{n}\frac{H_{n}(\sqrt{2\pi}x)H_{n}(\sqrt{2\pi}y)}{2^{n}n!(n+2\pi+1/2)}.

Multiplying the Mehler’s Hermite polynomial formula by
t^{2\pi-1/2}
as before, but now integrating from
0
to
-i
, we obtain

\displaystyle\widehat{K_{x}}(y)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2}\pi e^{-\pi(x^{2}+y^{2})}(-i)^{-2\pi-1/2}\int_{0}^{-i}\frac{t^{2\pi-1/2}}{\sqrt{1-t^{2}}}e^{2\pi\frac{2xyt-(x^{2}+y^{2})t^{2}}{1-t^{2}}}dt

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2}\pi e^{-\pi(x^{2}+y^{2})}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{t^{2\pi-1/2}}{\sqrt{1+t^{2}}}e^{2\pi\frac{-2xyti+(x^{2}+y^{2})t^{2}}{1+t^{2}}}dt,

where in the last equality we used the change of variable
t\to-it
.
∎

3.
Eigenvalue problem for two-sided concentration

Knowing the expression for the reproducing kernel allows us to study various concentration problems. The most natural one for the space
\mathcal{H}
is the problem of maximizing the ratio

\displaystyle\lambda=\frac{\int_{I}|f(x)|^{2}(1+x^{2})dx+\int_{J}|\hat{f}(\xi)|^{2}(1+\xi^{2})d\xi}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f(x)|^{2}(1+x^{2})dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\hat{f}(\xi)|^{2}(1+\xi^{2})d\xi}

where
I,J\subset\mathbb{R}
are measurable, which leads to the eigenvalue problem

\lambda_{n}f_{n}(t)=\int_{I}f_{n}(x)\overline{K_{t}(x)}(1+x^{2})dx+\int_{J}\hat{f_{n}}(\xi)\overline{\hat{K_{t}}(\xi)}(1+\xi^{2})d\xi,

where
K_{t}
is the reproducing kernel of
\mathcal{H}
.
We define thus an operator
T_{I,J}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
as

T_{I,J}f(t)=\int_{I}f(x)\overline{K_{t}(x)}(1+x^{2})dx+\int_{J}\hat{f}(x)\overline{\hat{K_{t}}(x)}(1+x^{2})dx.

We want to show that
T_{I,J}
is a self-adjoint Hilbert–Schmidt operator and study its eigenvalues, which corresponds to the next best possible concentrations
\lambda
obtained by orthogonal functions in the space.

Lemma 6
.

For any measurable
I,J
of finite Lebesgue measure,
T_{I,J}
is a self-adjoint Hilbert–Schmidt operator.

Proof.

First, we show that
T_{I,J}
is bounded. By definition

\displaystyle||T_{I,J}f||^{2}=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\int_{I}f(t)\overline{K_{x}(t)}(1+t^{2})dt+\int_{J}\widehat{f}(t)\overline{\widehat{K_{x}}(t)}(1+t^{2})dt\right|^{2}(1+x^{2})dx

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\int_{I}f(t)\widehat{K_{x}}(t)(1+t^{2})dt+\int_{J}\widehat{f}(t)\overline{K_{x}(t)}(1+t^{2})dt\right|^{2}(1+x^{2})dx.

Using
|z|^{2}=z\overline{z}
, multiplying out and using the reproducing property of the kernel we obtain

\displaystyle||T_{I,J}f||^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{I}\int_{I}f(t)\overline{f(y)}K_{y}(t)(1+y^{2})(1+t^{2})dydt+\int_{J}\int_{J}\widehat{f}(t)\overline{\widehat{f}(y)}K_{y}(t)(1+y^{2})(1+t^{2})dydt

\displaystyle+\int_{I}\int_{J}f(t)\overline{\widehat{f}(y)}\widehat{K_{y}}(t)(1+y^{2})(1+t^{2})dydt+\int_{J}\int_{I}\widehat{f}(t)\overline{{f}(y)\widehat{K_{y}}(t)}(1+y^{2})(1+t^{2})dydt.

Since the kernel and its Fourier transform are bounded, the above is bounded and

||T_{I,J}f||\leq c\max{(|I|,|J|)}^{3}||f||.

To show that
T_{I,J}
is Hilbert–Schmidt, we compute
\sum_{n}||T_{I,J}e_{n}||^{2}
, where
\{e_{n}\}_{n}
is the orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}
from Theorem
4
. Doing the same as above with the additional facts that
\langle\widehat{K_{y}},\widehat{K_{t}}\rangle=\langle K_{y},K_{t}\rangle=K_{y}(t)
,
\widehat{e_{n}}(t)\overline{\widehat{e_{n}}(y)}=(-i)^{n}e_{n}(t)\overline{(-i)^{n}}e_{n}(y)=e_{n}(t)e_{n}(y)
,
\sum_{n}e_{n}(t)e_{n}(y)=K_{y}(t)
and
\sum_{n}e_{n}(t)\widehat{e_{n}}(y)=\widehat{K_{y}}(t)
gives

\displaystyle\sum_{n}||T_{I,J}e_{n}||^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{I}\int_{I}K_{y}(t)^{2}(1+y^{2})(1+t^{2})dydt+\int_{J}\int_{J}K_{y}(t)^{2}(1+y^{2})(1+t^{2})dydt

\displaystyle+2\int_{I}\int_{J}|\widehat{K_{y}}(t)|^{2}(1+y^{2})(1+t^{2})dydt.

As
I,J
have finite Lebesgue measures, the above is finite, and so
T_{I,J}
is a Hilbert–Schmidt operator.
To prove that
T_{I,J}
is self-adjoint, one simply writes down
\langle Tf,g\rangle
and
\langle f,Tg\rangle
, and notices that they are equal using the reproducing property of the kernel.
∎

This lemma allows us to study the eigenvalues of
T_{I,J}
.

Theorem 7
.

Let
\lambda_{n}
be the
n
th eigenvalue of
T_{I,J}
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\lambda_{n}

\displaystyle=\int_{I}K_{x}(x)(1+x^{2})dx+\int_{J}K_{x}(x)(1+x^{2})dx,

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\lambda_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{I}\int_{I}K_{y}(t)^{2}(1+y^{2})(1+t^{2})dydt

\displaystyle+\int_{J}\int_{J}K_{y}(t)^{2}(1+y^{2})(1+t^{2})dydt

\displaystyle+2\int_{I}\int_{J}|\widehat{K_{y}}(t)|^{2}(1+y^{2})(1+t^{2})dydt.

Proof.

By the previous lemma,
T_{I,J}
is a compact self-adjoint operator, and thus admits an orthonormal basis
\{f_{n}\}_{n}
of
\mathcal{H}
such that
T_{I,J}f_{n}=\lambda_{n}f_{n}
. As
T_{I,J}
is also Hilbert–Schmidt,
\sum_{n}||T_{I,J}e_{n}||^{2}=\sum_{n}||T_{I,J}f_{n}||^{2}
for any two orthonormal bases
\{e_{n}\}_{n},\{f_{n}\}_{n}
, and so the second formula follows from the proof of the previous lemma as

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\lambda_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\lambda_{n}^{2}||f_{n}||^{2}=\sum_{n}||T_{I,J}f_{n}||^{2}=\sum_{n}||T_{I,J}e_{n}||^{2}.

For the first formula, we simply compute that

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}=\langle T_{I,J}e_{n},e_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{I}\langle K_{x},e_{n}\rangle e_{n}(x)(1+x^{2})dx+\int_{J}\langle K_{x},\widehat{e_{n}}\rangle\widehat{e_{n}}(x)(1+x^{2})dx

\displaystyle=\int_{I}\overline{e_{n}}(x)e_{n}(x)(1+x^{2})dx+\int_{J}\overline{\widehat{e_{n}}(x)}\widehat{e_{n}}(x)(1+x^{2})dx,

and thus

\sum_{n}\lambda_{n}=\int_{I}K_{x}(x)(1+x^{2})dx+\int_{J}K_{x}(x)(1+x^{2})dx.

∎

To get some information now about the size of both sums, we need to obtain an approximate formula for the reproducing kernel, as the original formula of Theorem
5
is difficult to use in practice. Since the kernel will clearly be very small if
y\notin(x-1,x+1)
, it is enough for us to check the kernel’s behavior around the diagonal.

Lemma 7
.

For
y\in(x-1,x+1)
we have

K_{x}(y)\sim\frac{\pi}{x}e^{-\pi|x^{2}-y^{2}|},\quad x\to\infty.

Proof.

First, we know from Theorem
5
that

\displaystyle K_{x}(y)=\sqrt{2}\pi e^{\pi(x-y)^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{t^{2\pi-1/2}}{\sqrt{1-t^{2}}}e^{-2\pi xy\frac{1-t}{1+t}-2\pi\frac{(y-x)^{2}}{1-t^{2}}}dt.

For simplicity assume that
y\in(x,x+1)
(the other case is analogous).
Since the maximum of
-xy\frac{1-t}{1+t}-\frac{(y-x)^{2}}{1-t^{2}}
is attained at
t=\frac{x}{y}
, we take the Taylor expansions around
t=\frac{x}{y}
and get

\displaystyle K_{x}(y)

\displaystyle\sim\sqrt{2}\pi e^{\pi(x-y)^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)^{2\pi-1/2}}{\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)^{2}}}e^{2\pi y(x-y)-2\pi\frac{y^{4}}{y^{2}-x^{2}}(t-\frac{x}{y})^{2}}dt

\displaystyle\sim\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{1-\frac{x}{y}}}e^{\pi(x^{2}-y^{2})}\int_{0}^{1}e^{-2\pi\frac{y^{4}}{y^{2}-x^{2}}(t-\frac{x}{y})^{2}}dt

\displaystyle\sim\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{1-\frac{x}{y}}}e^{\pi(x^{2}-y^{2})}\sqrt{\frac{y^{2}-x^{2}}{2y^{4}}}\sim\frac{\pi}{x}e^{\pi(x^{2}-y^{2})}.

∎

Lemma 8
.

We have

\displaystyle\widehat{K_{x}}(y)\sim\frac{e^{-2\pi ixy}}{x^{2}+y^{2}},\quad x^{2}+y^{2}\to\infty.

Proof.

Theorem
6
gives us that

\displaystyle\widehat{K_{x}}(y)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2}\pi\int_{0}^{1}\frac{t^{2\pi-1/2}}{\sqrt{1+t^{2}}}e^{\pi\frac{-4xyti+(x^{2}+y^{2})(t^{2}-1)}{1+t^{2}}}dt.

We notice that the mass of the integrand is concentrated around
t=1
. By using suitable Taylor expansions around
t=1
, we therefore obtain

\displaystyle\widehat{K_{x}}(y)

\displaystyle\sim\sqrt{2}\pi\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}e^{-2\pi ixy}e^{\pi(x^{2}+y^{2})(t-1)}dt

\displaystyle\sim\frac{e^{-2\pi ixy}}{x^{2}+y^{2}}.

∎

This now allows us to study the eigenvalues of
T_{I,J}
.

Theorem 8
.

For
I=[-R_{1},R_{1}]
,
J=[-R_{2},R_{2}]
we have the following for the trace of
T_{I,J}
:

\sum_{n}\lambda_{n}\sim\pi R_{1}^{2}+\pi R_{2}^{2},\quad R_{1},R_{2}\to\infty.

Proof.

By Lemma
7
,
K_{x}(x)\sim\frac{\pi}{x}
. Thus, the conclusion follows trivially from Theorem
7
.
∎

Theorem 9
.

For
I=[-R_{1},R_{1}]
,
J=[-R_{2},R_{2}]
we have the following for the sum of squares of eigenvalues of
T_{I,J}
as
R_{1},R_{2}\to\infty
:

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\lambda_{n}^{2}\sim

\displaystyle\frac{\pi}{2}R_{1}^{2}+\frac{\pi}{2}R_{2}^{2}+2((R_{1}^{2}+R_{2}^{2})\arctan(R_{1}/R_{2})-R_{1}R_{2}

\displaystyle-R_{1}^{2}\arctan(R_{1})+2R_{1}^{2}\arctan(R_{2}/R_{1})).

Proof.

First,

\displaystyle\int_{I}\int_{I}K_{y}(t)^{2}(1+y^{2})(1+t^{2})dydt\sim 4\int_{1}^{R_{1}}\int_{t}^{t+1}K_{y}(t)^{2}(1+y^{2})(1+t^{2})dydt

\displaystyle\sim 4\int_{1}^{R_{1}}\int_{t}^{t+1}\frac{\pi^{2}}{yt}e^{2\pi(t^{2}-y^{2})}(1+y^{2})(1+t^{2})dydt\sim\pi\int_{1}^{R_{1}}t(1-e^{-2\pi(2t+1)})dt\sim\frac{\pi}{2}R_{1}^{2}.

Second, using
\int\frac{t^{2}}{(y^{2}+t^{2})^{2}}dt=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\arctan(\frac{t}{y})}{y}-\frac{t}{t^{2}+y^{2}}\right)
,
\int y\arctan(\frac{R}{y})dy=\frac{1}{2}((R^{2}+y^{2})\arctan(\frac{R}{y})+Ry)
and
\int\frac{Ry^{2}}{R^{2}+y^{2}}dy=R(y-R\arctan(\frac{y}{R})))
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{I}\int_{J}|\widehat{K_{y}}(t)|^{2}(1+y^{2})(1+t^{2})dydt\sim 4\int_{1}^{R_{1}}\int_{1}^{R_{2}}\frac{1}{(y^{2}+t^{2})^{2}}(1+y^{2})(1+t^{2})dydt

\displaystyle\sim(R_{1}^{2}+R_{2}^{2})\arctan(R_{1}/R_{2})-R_{1}R_{2}+R_{1}+R_{2}-R_{1}^{2}\arctan(R_{1})

\displaystyle-R_{2}^{2}\arctan(1/R_{2})+2R_{1}^{2}\arctan(R_{2}/R_{1})-2R_{1}^{2}\arctan(1/R_{1}).

Thus, the conclusion follows from Theorem
7
.
∎

As we can see now, if
I=J=[-R,R]
, then the above simplifies to

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\lambda_{n}

\displaystyle\sim 2\pi R^{2},

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\lambda_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle\sim(2\pi-2)R^{2}.

This means that the eigenvalues do not decay as quickly as we initially expected them to do. It seems like concentrating the sum of both the function and its Fourier transform is the reason for that. We do not think it has anything to do with the weight in the norm - as we can see below, in the case of a weighted Paley–Wiener space, the general behavior of eigenvalues did not change.
This leaves us with the question of how many of the eigenvalues are close to
1
, partially answered in Theorem
1
.

To prove this theorem, we will consider a subspace of
\mathcal{H}
, the weighted Paley–Wiener space. This space is easier to study and will already give us the desired number of large eigenvalues. Thus, we divert our focus to this space for now, before coming back to the proof of Theorem
1
.

4.
Eigenvalue problem for the Paley–Wiener space with
(1+x^{2})
weight

Consider the Paley–Wiener space
PW_{T}(1+x^{2})
of functions
f
with Fourier transform supported on
[-T,T]
and with the norm

||f||^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f(x)|^{2}(1+x^{2})dx<\infty.

Note that it is a subspace of our space
\mathcal{H}
.
It is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space, and to find the kernel
L_{t}(y)
we want to solve

\displaystyle f(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(y)\overline{L_{t}(y)}(1+y^{2})dy.

Using Plancherel’s theorem we obtain the differential equation

\displaystyle\widehat{L_{t}}(y)-\frac{\widehat{L_{t}}^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(y)}{4\pi^{2}}=e^{-2\pi iyt}\mathbbm{1}_{[-T,T]}(y),\quad\widehat{L_{t}}(-T)=\widehat{L_{t}}(T)=0,

and solving it we get

\displaystyle\widehat{L_{t}}(y)=\frac{e^{2\pi T}(e^{-2\pi itT}e^{4\pi T}-e^{2\pi itT})(e^{2\pi y}-e^{2\pi(2T-y)})}{(1-e^{8\pi T})(1+t^{2})}+\frac{e^{-2\pi ity}-e^{-2\pi itT}e^{2\pi(T-y)}}{1+t^{2}},

which in turn gives

\displaystyle L_{t}(y)=\frac{(1+ty)\sin(2\pi T(t-y))}{\pi(t-y)(1+t^{2})(1+y^{2})}+\frac{(1+e^{8\pi T})\cos(2\pi T(t-y))-2e^{4\pi T}\cos(2\pi T(t+y))}{\pi(1-e^{8\pi T})(1+t^{2})(1+y^{2})}.

If we consider now a problem of maximizing the ratio

\displaystyle\lambda=\frac{\int_{-R}^{R}|f(x)|^{2}(1+x^{2})dx}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f(x)|^{2}(1+x^{2})dx},

we will get the corresponding compact trace class operator

\displaystyle W_{R}f(t)=\int_{-R}^{R}f(y)\overline{L_{t}(y)}(1+y^{2})dy.

The eigenvalues
\lambda_{n}
of
W_{R}
, sorted as usual in decreasing order, satisfy

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\lambda_{n}=\int_{-R}^{R}L_{t}(t)(1+t^{2})dt,

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\lambda_{n}^{2}=\int_{-R}^{R}\int_{-R}^{R}|L_{t}(y)|^{2}(1+t^{2})(1+y^{2})dtdy.

Since

\displaystyle L_{t}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{2T}{1+t^{2}}+\frac{(1+e^{8\pi T})-2e^{4\pi T}\cos(4\pi Tt)}{\pi(1-e^{8\pi T})(1+t^{2})^{2}},

we see that

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\lambda_{n}=4RT+O\left(\frac{1}{1+R}\right).

Now,

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\lambda_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{-R}^{R}\int_{-R}^{R}|L_{t}(y)|^{2}(1+t^{2})(1+y^{2})dtdy

\displaystyle\geq\int_{-R}^{R}\int_{-R}^{R}\left(\frac{(1+ty)\sin(2\pi T(t-y))}{\pi(t-y)(1+t^{2})(1+y^{2})}\right)^{2}(1+t^{2})(1+y^{2})dtdy

\displaystyle-\int_{-R}^{R}\int_{-R}^{R}\left(\frac{c}{(1+y^{2})(1+t^{2})}\right)^{2}(1+t^{2})(1+y^{2})dtdy

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\int_{-R}^{R}\int_{-R}^{R}\left(\frac{\sin(2\pi T(t-y))}{t-y}\right)^{2}\frac{(1+ty)^{2}}{(1+t^{2})(1+y^{2})}dtdy-\frac{c}{(1+R)^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{4}{\pi^{2}}\int_{4}^{R}\int_{y/2}^{y}\left(\frac{\sin(2\pi T(t-y))}{t-y}\right)^{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{2ty}\right)dtdy-\frac{c}{(1+R)^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{4}{\pi^{2}}\int_{4}^{R}\left(\pi^{2}T-\frac{2}{y}-\frac{\pi^{2}T-\frac{2}{y}}{y^{2}}\right)dy-\frac{c}{(1+R)^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq 4RT-O(\log R)-O(T).

Using standard arguments that take into account the fact that
0<\lambda_{n}<1
and considering
\sum_{n}\lambda_{n}(1-\lambda_{n})
, the above facts prove Theorem
2
. We refer to the proof of Theorem
3
, which will carry out an analogous computation.

5.
Eigenvalue problem for one-sided concentration

Intrigued by the behavior of the eigenvalues of
T_{I,J}
, we study what would happen in the case of one-sided concentration, which leads to Theorem
3
.
Here we consider the problem of maximizing the ratio

\lambda=\frac{\int_{I}|f(x)|^{2}(1+x^{2})dx}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f(x)|^{2}(1+x^{2})dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\hat{f}(\xi)|^{2}(1+\xi^{2})d\xi},

where
I\subset\mathbb{R}
is measurable.
This leads to the eigenvalue problem

\lambda_{n}f_{n}(t)=\int_{I}f_{n}(x)\overline{K_{t}(x)}(1+x^{2})dx,

where
K_{t}
is the reproducing kernel of
\mathcal{H}
.
Define an operator
T_{I}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
as

T_{I}f(t)=\int_{I}f(x)\overline{K_{t}(x)}(1+x^{2})dx.

By computations analogous to the two-sided case, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{n}||T_{I}e_{n}||^{2}=\int_{I}\int_{I}K_{y}(t)^{2}(1+y^{2})(1+t^{2})dydt

and

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\langle T_{I}e_{n},e_{n}\rangle=\sum_{n}\int_{I}\overline{e_{n}(x)}e_{n}(x)(1+x^{2})dx=\int_{I}K_{x}(x)(1+x^{2})dx.

Thus if
I=[-R,R]
, then as
R\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\lambda_{n}\sim\pi R^{2},

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\lambda_{n}^{2}\sim\frac{\pi}{2}R^{2}

by the computation done in the proof of Theorem
9
.
Now, to obtain the third moment needed for the proof of Theorem
3
, we analyze

\displaystyle T_{I}^{3}f(t)=\int_{I}\int_{I}\int_{I}f(x_{1})\overline{K_{x_{2}}(x_{1})}(1+x_{1}^{2})dx_{1}\overline{K_{x_{3}}(x_{2})}(1+x_{2}^{2})dx_{2}\overline{K_{t}(x_{3})}(1+x_{3}^{2})dx_{3},

which gives

\displaystyle\langle T_{I}^{3}e_{n},e_{n}\rangle=\int_{I}\int_{I}\int_{I}e_{n}(x_{1})K_{x_{2}}(x_{1})K_{x_{3}}(x_{2})\overline{e_{n}(x_{3})}(1+x_{1}^{2})(1+x_{2}^{2})(1+x_{3}^{2})dx_{1}dx_{2}dx_{3},

and thus

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\langle T_{I}^{3}e_{n},e_{n}\rangle=\int_{I}\int_{I}\int_{I}K_{x_{1}}(x_{3})K_{x_{2}}(x_{1})K_{x_{3}}(x_{2})(1+x_{1}^{2})(1+x_{2}^{2})(1+x_{3}^{2})dx_{1}dx_{2}dx_{3}.

Using that mass will be concentrated around the diagonal and using the approximation formula for the kernel (Lemma
7
), we find that as
R\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\int_{I}\int_{I}\int_{I}K_{x_{1}}(x_{3})K_{x_{2}}(x_{1})K_{x_{3}}(x_{2})(1+x_{1}^{2})(1+x_{2}^{2})(1+x_{3}^{2})dx_{1}dx_{2}dx_{3}

\displaystyle\sim 2^{3}\int_{1}^{R}\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{1}+1}\int_{x_{2}}^{x_{2}+1}K_{x_{1}}(x_{3})K_{x_{2}}(x_{1})K_{x_{3}}(x_{2})x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{2}x_{3}^{2}dx_{3}dx_{2}dx_{1}

\displaystyle\sim(2\pi)^{3}\int_{1}^{R}\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{1}+1}\int_{x_{2}}^{x_{2}+1}e^{-2\pi(x_{3}^{2}-x_{1}^{2})}x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}dx_{3}dx_{2}dx_{1}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{(2\pi)^{3}}{4\pi}\int_{1}^{R}\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{1}+1}e^{-2\pi(x_{2}^{2}-x_{1}^{2})}x_{1}x_{2}dx_{2}dx_{1}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{(2\pi)^{3}}{(4\pi)^{2}}\int_{1}^{R}x_{1}dx_{1}\sim\frac{(2\pi)^{3}}{(4\pi)^{2}}\frac{R^{2}}{2}=\frac{\pi}{4}R^{2}.

We are now prepared to prove Theorem
3
.

Proof of Theorem
3
.

Consider the sum
\sum_{n}\lambda_{n}(\lambda_{n}-\frac{1}{2})^{2}.
By the previous computations of the moments it is

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\lambda_{n}\left(\lambda_{n}-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\left(\lambda_{n}^{3}-\lambda_{n}^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\lambda_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{4}R^{2}-\frac{\pi}{2}R^{2}+\frac{\pi}{4}R^{2}+o(R^{2})=o(R^{2}).

Since all eigenvalues are necessarily between
0
and
1
, this already shows that there are at most
o(R^{2})
eigenvalues larger than
\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon
and between
\epsilon
and
\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon
.
Now, it is clear that there are at most
2\pi R^{2}+o(R^{2})
eigenvalues between
\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon
and
\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon
(as
\sum_{n}\lambda_{n}=\pi R^{2}+o(R^{2})
). If there would be only
(2\pi-\delta)R^{2}
of them for some
\delta>0
, then to still have
\sum_{n}\lambda_{n}\sim\pi R^{2}
we would need to have
\sum_{\lambda_{n}<\epsilon}\lambda_{n}\sim\delta R^{2}
(as we already know that there are
o(R^{2})
eigenvalues greater than
\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon
and between
\epsilon
and
\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon
). But then we would have
\sum_{\lambda_{n}<\epsilon}\lambda_{n}^{2}<\epsilon\delta R^{2}
,
\sum_{\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon<\lambda_{n}<\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon}\lambda_{n}^{2}<(\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon)^{2}(2\pi-\delta)R^{2}<\frac{1}{4}(2\pi-\delta)R^{2},
and so in total
\sum_{n}\lambda_{n}^{2}
would be smaller than
(\frac{\pi}{2}-\frac{\delta}{4}+\epsilon\delta)R^{2}+o(R^{2})
. This is a contradiction with
\sum_{n}\lambda_{n}^{2}=\frac{\pi}{2}R^{2}+o(R^{2})
for any
\epsilon<\frac{1}{4}
, which concludes the proof.
∎

\displaystyle\frac{\int_{-R}^{R}|f(x)|^{2}(1+x^{2})dx}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f(x)|^{2}(1+x^{2})dx}>1-\epsilon.

\mathcal{H}

(3)

\begin{split}&\frac{\int_{-R}^{R}|f(x)|^{2}(1+x^{2})dx+\int_{-T}^{T}|\hat{f}(x)|^{2}(1+x^{2})dx}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f(x)|^{2}(1+x^{2})dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\hat{f}(x)|^{2}(1+x^{2})dx}\\

&\geq 1-\epsilon.\end{split}

We therefore have an
N(R,T)
dimensional subspace of
\mathcal{H}
of functions satisfying (

2

To prove that there are at most
2\pi(R^{2}+T^{2})+o(R^{2})+o(T^{2})

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\lambda_{n}\langle g_{n},g_{m}\rangle=\langle T_{I}g_{n},g_{m}\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}T_{I}g_{n}(x)\overline{g_{m}(x)}(1+x^{2})dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widehat{T_{I}g_{n}}(x)\overline{\widehat{g_{m}}(x)}(1+x^{2})dx
