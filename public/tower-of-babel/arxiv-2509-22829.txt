Exact shape of an ideal heavy spring suspended at both ends.
Cyril Belardinelli Kantonsschule Solothurn Switzerland

Abstract
. In the present article we determine the exact shape of an ideal spring suspended at both ends. Historically, this goes under the name of Elastica problem. The total energy of the suspended spring is a functional depending on two independent functions: on one hand the function describing the shape of the hanging spring, on the other hand a non-negative function describing the mass distribution along the elongated spring. Calculus of Variations provides a rather short and elegant solution. This method does not appear to be widely known in the literature.

2.
The
Elastica
Problem revisited by Calculus of Variations

The catenary is the solution to the problem of minimizing the potential gravitational energy of an unextendable (but flexible) chain hanging under its own weight when supported only at
its ends. In the present paper we study a natural extension of the problem which historically goes under the name of
Elastica
. Instead of a flexible but unextendable chain we consider an elastic spring hanging under its own weight (See Fig.
1
).

In this case the total energy stored in the spring is given by the sum of the potential gravitational energy
E_{\text{gravity}}
and the elastic energy
E_{\text{elastic}}
due to the deformation of the spring. The shape of the hanging spring (in its equilibrium position) will be the one that minimizes the total energy. It is therefore very natural to study the problem in the context of Calculus of Variations.

The total energy is a functional
E_{\text{total}}[y,\sigma]
depending on two independent functions
y(x)
and
\sigma(x)
where the function
y(x)
describes the shape of the hanging spring and
\sigma(x)
denotes the mass distribution along the hanging spring defined as
\sigma(x)=d{m}/d{s}
where
x
is a point on the abscissa and
d{s}
is an infinitesimal line element of the spring (Fig.
1
).

The spring’s mass is
m
and its elastic constant is denoted by
k
. The unstretched spring has an equilibrium length
\ell_{0}
and a mass density
\sigma_{0}=m/\ell_{0}
.

The total energy functional of the spring is then formally;

(2.1)

\displaystyle E_{\text{total}}[y,\sigma]=E_{\text{gravity}}[y,\sigma]+E_{\text{elastic}}[y,\sigma]

The gravitational potential energy is given by the following integral;

\displaystyle E_{\text{gravity}}[y,\sigma]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g\int_{x_{0}}^{x_{1}}y(x)\,\sigma(x)\sqrt{1+y^{\prime 2}}\,d{x}

Whereas the functional of the elastic energy can be formulated in the following way. (For a derivation see App.
A
)

\displaystyle E_{\text{elastic}}[y,\sigma]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{k\ell_{0}}{2}\int_{x_{0}}^{x_{1}}\quantity[\frac{\sigma_{0}}{\sigma(x)}-2+\frac{\sigma(x)}{\sigma_{0}}]\sqrt{1+y^{\prime 2}}\,d{x}

We must minimize the energy functional of Eq.
2.1
under the constraint of fixed mass
m
. In addition, we demand the linear mass density
\sigma(x)
to be non-negative.

\displaystyle m=\int_{x_{0}}^{x_{1}}\sigma(x)\sqrt{1+y^{\prime 2}}\,d{x}\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma(x)\geqslant 0\quad\forall x\in[x_{0},x_{1}]

In order to take into account the first of these constraints one introduces a
Lagrange-Multiplier

\mu
which leads to the functional
H[y,\sigma]
;

\displaystyle H[y,\sigma]=E_{\text{total}}[y,\sigma]+\mu\int_{x_{0}}^{x_{1}}\quantity[\sigma(x) \sqrt{1+y^{\prime 2}}\, -m]d{x}\equiv\int_{x_{0}}^{x_{1}}\mathcal{F}(x)\,d{x}

where the equivalence ”
\equiv
” means: ”equal up to irrelevant constant terms”.

The Lagrange density
\mathcal{F}(x)
is;

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(x)=\quantity[g y(x)\sigma(x)+\mu\sigma(x) +\frac{k \ell_{0}\sigma_{0}}{2\sigma(x)}+\frac{k \ell_{0}\sigma(x)}{2\sigma_{0}}-k \ell_{0}]\sqrt{1+y^{\prime 2}}

The variation of H with respect to
y
and
\sigma
leads to two coupled differential equations; i. e. the
Euler-Lagrange equations
;

\displaystyle\derivative{x}\partialderivative{F}{y^{\prime}}-\partialderivative{F}{y}=0

\displaystyle\derivative{x}\partialderivative{F}{\sigma^{\prime}}-\partialderivative{F}{\sigma}=0

An explicit evaluation leads to the following system of equations;

\displaystyle\derivative{x}\quantity{\qty[g\sigma(y+\mu/g)+\frac{k \ell_{0}}{2}\qty(\frac{\sigma_{0}}{\sigma}+\frac{\sigma}{\sigma_{0}}-2)]\frac{y^{\prime}}{\sqrt{1+y^{\prime 2}}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g\sigma\sqrt{1+y^{\prime 2}}

\displaystyle\alpha y+\frac{2\mu\sigma_{0}}{k\ell_{0}}+\quantity(1-\frac{\sigma_{0}^2}{\sigma^{2}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

where we introduced the parameter
\alpha
defined as;

\alpha=\frac{2g\sigma_{0}}{k\ell_{0}}

Note that the parameter
\alpha
has dimension
[\alpha]=\text{m}^{-1}
.

We rewrite the system by introducing the following variables;

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle y+\mu/g

\displaystyle\tilde{\sigma}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma}{\sigma_{0}}

which gives;

(2.2)

\displaystyle\derivative{x}\quantity{\qty[(1+\alpha z)\tilde{\sigma}+\frac{1}{\tilde{\sigma}}-2]\frac{z^{\prime}}{\sqrt{1+z^{\prime 2}}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha\tilde{\sigma}\sqrt{1+z^{\prime 2}}

(2.3)

\displaystyle 1+\alpha z

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tilde{\sigma}^{2}}

Elimination of the reduced density
\tilde{\sigma}
from the first equation (I) leads to a
2\text{nd}
order differential equation for the variable
z
alone;

(2.4)

z^{\prime\prime}\quantity(1+\alpha z-\sqrt{1+\alpha z})=\frac{\alpha}{2}\quantity(1+z^{\prime 2})

It is convenient to cast the differential equation in a dimensionless form. For that purpose we choose the variables;

(2.5)

\displaystyle\tilde{z}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+\alpha z

(2.6)

\displaystyle\tilde{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha x

We get then the following
universal
differential equation (DE);

(2.7)

\ddot{\tilde{z}}\quantity(\tilde{z}-\sqrt{\tilde{z}})=\frac{1+\dot{\tilde{z}}^{2}}{2}

We can draw an important conclusion from the latter differential equation: On one hand it is evident from physical reasons that
\ddot{\tilde{z}}(0)>0
. On the other hand we see that the r.h.s. of Eq.
2.7
is
\geqslant\frac{1}{2}
. We therefore conclude that
\tilde{z}(0)>1
.

Since
\tilde{z}=\frac{1}{\tilde{\sigma}}
applies and the linear density
\tilde{\sigma}
assumes its lowest value at
\tilde{x}=0
, we have necessarily;

\tilde{z}(\tilde{x})>1\iff\tilde{\sigma}(\tilde{x})<1\quad\forall\,\tilde{x}\in\mathbb{R}

Moreover, we conclude from Eq.
2.7
;

\lim_{\tilde{z}(0)\to 1^{+}}\ddot{\tilde{z}}(0)=+\infty

The limit
\ddot{\tilde{z}}(0)\rightarrow+\infty
means that the radius of curvature in
\tilde{x}=0
(i. e. in the lowest point of the curve
\tilde{z}(\tilde{x})
where
\dot{\tilde{z}}(0)=0
) is approaching zero (i. e. infinite curvature). This happens when the distance between the two suspension points of the spring goes to zero. (In the limit where they coincide one would have two vertically and infinitesimally adjacent hanging springs connecting at the bottom)

2.1.
Solving the differential equation

In the present section we provide an exact solution of the following Initial value problem (IVP) ;

(2.8)

\begin{split}\ddot{\tilde{z}}\quantity(\tilde{z}-\sqrt{\tilde{z}})&=\frac{1+\dot{\tilde{z}}^{2}}{2}\\
\tilde{z}(0)&=\tilde{z}_{0}\\
\dot{\tilde{z}}(0)&=0\end{split}

First, we introduce a new variable
p
;

p(z)=\derivative{\tilde{z}}{\tilde{x}}

With this variable we can write;

\ddot{\tilde{z}}=\dot{p}p

The differential equation Eq.
2.8
transforms into;

\log{\qty(1+p^2)}+C=\int_{\tilde{z}_{0}}^{\tilde{z}}\frac{d{\tilde{z}^{\prime}}}{\tilde{z}^{\prime}-\sqrt{\tilde{z}^{\prime}}}

The latter integral can be calculated;

\int_{\tilde{z}_{0}}^{\tilde{z}}\frac{d{\tilde{z}^{\prime}}}{\tilde{z}^{\prime}-\sqrt{\tilde{z}^{\prime}}}=2\log{\underbrace{\frac{r-1}{r_{0}-1}}_{>1}}>0

where

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\tilde{z}}

\displaystyle r_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\tilde{z}_{0}}

By applying the initial value
p(0)=\dot{\tilde{z}}(0)=0
we conclude that
C=0
.

With this we obtain;

p(z)=\derivative{\tilde{z}}{\tilde{x}}=\pm\sqrt{\quantity(\frac{r-1}{r_{0}})^{2}-1}

This leads to a further integration;

\pm\int_{r_{0}}^{r}{\frac{2rd{r}}{\sqrt{\quantity(\frac{r-1}{r_{0}})^{2}-1}}}=\int_{0}^{\tilde{x}}{d{\tilde{x}^{\prime}}}

By the variable change
\eta=\frac{r-1}{r_{0}}
the latter equation transforms to;

2\quantity(r_{0}-1)\int_{1}^{\eta}{\frac{d{\eta^{\prime}}}{\sqrt{\eta^{\prime 2}-1}}}+\quantity(r_{0}^2-1)\int_{1}^{\eta}{\frac{2\eta^{\prime}d{\eta^{\prime}}}{\sqrt{\eta^{\prime 2}-1}}}=\pm\tilde{x}

These integrals are readily evaluated. the result is;

(2.9)

\pm\tilde{x}=2\quantity(r_{0}-1)^{2}\sqrt{\eta^{2}-1}+2\quantity(r_{0}-1)\cosh^{-1}{\eta}

where

\eta=\frac{r-1}{r_{0}-1}=\frac{\sqrt{\tilde{z}}-1}{\sqrt{\tilde{z}_{0}}-1}

The inverse of hyperbolic cosine can be expressed in the following way;

\cosh^{-1}(\eta)=\log(\eta+\sqrt{\eta^2-1})\qquad 1\leqslant\eta<\infty

Therefore, we may also write;

\pm\tilde{x}=2\quantity(r_{0}-1)^{2}\sqrt{\eta^{2}-1}+2\quantity(r_{0}-1)\log(\eta+\sqrt{\eta^2-1})

2.2.
Limiting cases of soft and hard springs

in order to perform the extreme limits of hard and soft springs we have to go back to Eq.
2.4
. For the sake of clarity we rewrite the equation here;

z^{\prime\prime}\quantity(1+\alpha z-\sqrt{1+\alpha z})=\frac{\alpha}{2}\quantity(1+z^{\prime 2})

2.2.1.
limiting case of a hard spring

In this limit one must consider the limit;

\alpha=\frac{2g\sigma_{0}}{k\ell_{0}}\rightarrow 0.

By using the following approximation (Taylor expansion up to
1\text{st}
order);

\sqrt{1+\alpha z}=1+\frac{1}{2}\alpha z+\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{2})

and by retaining terms up to first order in
\alpha
DE Eq.
2.4
becomes (after cancelling out
\alpha
);

(2.10)

\displaystyle z^{\prime\prime}z=\quantity(1+z^{\prime 2})

By taking the derivative on both sides of Eq.
2.10
we obtain;

\displaystyle z^{\prime\prime\prime}z+z^{\prime\prime}z^{\prime}=2z^{\prime\prime}z^{\prime}

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle z^{\prime\prime\prime}z=z^{\prime\prime}z^{\prime}

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle z^{2}\quantity(\frac{z^{\prime\prime}}{z})^{\prime}=0

This leads to the solution
z_{1}(x)=0
which is the trivial solution of Eq.
2.10
. The non-trivial solution denoted
z_{2}(x)
fullfills;

\displaystyle\quantity(\frac{z_{2}^{\prime\prime}}{z_{2}})^{\prime}=0

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle z_{2}^{\prime\prime}=c\cdot z_{2}

The
catenary
, i. e. the
hyperbolic cosine
is the well-known physically relevant solution to the latter equation.

2.2.2.
limiting case of a soft spring

In this case we have to consider the limit;

\alpha=\frac{2g\sigma_{0}}{k\ell_{0}}\rightarrow\infty.

In this limit we get for Eq.
2.4
;

\displaystyle z^{\prime\prime}z=\frac{1+z^{\prime 2}}{2}

Once again, we take the derivative on both sides;

\displaystyle z^{\prime\prime\prime}z+z^{\prime\prime}z^{\prime}=z^{\prime\prime}z^{\prime}\iff

\displaystyle z^{\prime\prime\prime}z=0

Once again, we have a trivial solution
z_{1}(x)=0
. The non-trivial solution is determined by the equation;

(2.11)

z_{2}^{\prime\prime\prime}(x)=0

A general solution to Eq.
2.11
is a parabola. The wrong conjecture that the catenary is identical to a parabola reappears here but only in the limit of a very soft spring (i. e. in the limit
k\rightarrow 0
).

Appendix A
Elastic Energy

The elastic energy of a spring, also known as elastic potential energy, is the energy stored within a spring when it is stretched or compressed from its equilibrium position. We assume a general non-homogeneous deformation. The stored energy is then a functional
E_{\text{elastic}}
depending on the linear mass distribution
\sigma(x)
along the spring.

To begin with we consider an infinitesimal line element
d{s}
of the spring which stores an (infinitesimal) amount
d{E}_{\text{elastic}}
of elastic energy;

d{E}_{\text{elastic}}=\frac{1}{2}{k}_{0}\cdot(d{s}-d{s}_{0})^{2}

where
d{s}_{0}
is the equilibrium length of
d{s}
and
{k}_{0}
is the spring’s elastic constant of the infinitesimal spring (of length
d{s}_{0}
).
The elastic constant of
d{s}_{0}
is;

k_{0}=k\cdot\frac{\ell_{0}}{d{s}_{0}}\\

Since

\displaystyle\sigma(x)d{s}=d{m}=\sigma_{0}d{s}_{0}\iff d{s}_{0}=\frac{\sigma(x)}{\sigma_{0}}d{s}

we have;

d{E}_{\text{elastic}}=\frac{1}{2}k\ell_{0}\frac{\sigma_{0}}{\sigma(x)}\quantity(1-\frac{\sigma(x)}{\sigma_{0}})^{2}d{s}

Finally, with the following relation between
d{x}
and
d{s}
;

d{s}=d{x}\sqrt{1+y^{\prime 2}}

we obtain the total elastic energy in integral form;

E_{\text{elastic}}=\frac{1}{2}k\ell_{0}\int_{x_{0}}^{x_{1}}\frac{\sigma_{0}}{\sigma(x)}\quantity(1-\frac{\sigma(x)}{\sigma_{0}})^{2}\sqrt{1+y^{\prime 2}}\,d{x}
