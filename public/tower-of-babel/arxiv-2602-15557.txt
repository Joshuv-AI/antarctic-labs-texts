Stochastic Games on Large Sparse Graphs
Eyal Neuman, Sturmius Tuschmann

Abstract
We introduce a framework for stochastic games on large sparse graphs, covering continuous-time and discrete-time dynamic games as well as static games. Players are indexed by the vertices of simple, locally finite graphs, allowing both finite and countably infinite populations, with asymptotics described through local weak convergence of marked graphs. The framework allows path-dependent utility functionals that may be heterogeneous across players. Under a contraction condition, we prove existence and uniqueness of Nash equilibria and establish exponential decay of correlations with graph distance. We further show that global equilibria can be approximated by truncated local games, and can even be reconstructed exactly on subgraphs given information on their boundary. Finally, we prove convergence of Nash equilibria along locally weakly convergent graph sequences, including sequences sampled from hyperfinite unimodular random graphs.

Introduction

Dynamic network games are non-cooperative games in which players make strategic decisions within a dynamic system, with their mutual dependencies encoded by a connectivity network modeled as a graph. Each player’s strategy influences not only their own payoff but also the dynamics of the environment. These games are useful for modeling strategic behavior in a range of competitive systems, such as autonomous driving
[
22
]
, real-time bidding
[
41
]
, and dynamic production management
[
30
]
. From a mathematical standpoint, dynamic network games are challenging to analyze because interactions are typically heterogeneous, populations can be very large, and the feedback effects between players’ actions and the evolving environment are often nonlinear.

Lovász and Szegedy’s seminal work on the convergence of dense graph sequences paved the way for the development of graphon games, which serve as infinite-population approximations of finite-player network games. They showed that in dense graph sequences (where the individual node degrees tend to infinity as the total number of nodes increases) subgraph densities converge to a natural limit. This limit is represented by a graphon, i.e., a symmetric measurable function
W:[0,1]^{2}\to[0,1]
that encodes these densities (see
[
9
,
10
,
31
]
).

Building on this graphon formalism,
Parise and Ozdaglar 2023
introduced static graphon games as an infinite-population approximation for large finite static network games. In these games, a continuum of players interacts according to a graphon
W
, where
W(u,v)
specifies the strength of interaction between players
u
and
v
. Crucially, the Nash equilibria of these graphon games provide approximate Nash equilibria for large finite network games
[
16
,
36
]
.
Dynamic network games are analytically more involved than static ones, since each player’s payoff depends not only on the others’ actions but also on an evolving system state whose dynamics are jointly driven by all actions. Dynamic graphon games with Markovian state dynamics have been studied in
[
4
,
14
,
15
,
19
,
24
,
38
,
43
]
, among others, while non-Markovian formulations were studied in
[
32
,
33
]
.

While graphon models provide a powerful limit theory for dense graphs, most large-scale real-world networks are sparse. Games on sparse networks are thus particularly important, as they more accurately reflect the structure of such real-world networks, where players face cognitive, economic, or technological constraints that limit the number of connections they can maintain. In these settings, the individual node degrees typically remain bounded or grow slowly with network size, making dense models less realistic. As a result, sparse models are often better suited for studying strategic behavior, efficiency, and policy design in large networks arising in economic, social, and technological applications. While the limit theory for dense graphs is well established, the sparse case remains comparatively less understood.

One approach to scaling limits of sparse graph sequences is local weak convergence, introduced by
Benjamini and Schramm 2001
and independently by
Aldous and Steele 2004
. For sequences of locally finite graphs, local weak convergence captures the distributional convergence of rooted neighborhoods around a uniformly chosen node. That is, it amounts to the weak convergence of the associated laws on the space of locally finite, connected, countable, rooted graphs equipped with the local topology. A central concept in this theory is that of unimodular probability measures, which play a role analogous to graphons as limit objects for dense graph sequences
[
1
,
7
,
8
]
.

Local weak convergence has recently found applications as a limit theory for locally interacting dynamic systems. In particular,
[
17
,
18
,
21
,
25
,
26
,
27
,
28
,
34
,
35
]
, among others, have studied large-scale interacting diffusions and Markov chains on sparse graphs. However, extending this paradigm to stochastic games poses significant challenges, as it integrates dynamic interactions on sparse networks with game-theoretic effects. Some special cases have been studied by
Lacker and Soret 2022
, who considered linear-quadratic stochastic differential games on the specific class of transitive graphs and established convergence of Markovian Nash equilibria under weak convergence of the associated graph spectral measures. However, as pointed out in
[
23
,
39
]
, a general framework for stochastic games on locally finite graphs, including an intrinsic infinite-player model that allows the approximation of large finite-player models, has been missing. Addressing this challenge is the main focus of this work.

Concretely, we introduce a general formulation for stochastic games on large sparse graphs that accommodates continuous-time and discrete-time dynamic games as well as static ones. Players are indexed by the nodes of a simple, locally finite graph, allowing for both finite and countably infinite populations, while asymptotics are encoded through local weak convergence of marked graphs, where each graph node is equipped with an associated mark that carries the player’s control. Our framework permits path-dependent goal functionals that are heterogeneous across players, and may include both idiosyncratic and common noise. More precisely, we work with continuously differentiable, strongly concave utility functionals, which provides a general yet tractable setting in which we can characterize Nash equilibria and study their structural properties.

Our main contributions.

In Theorem
4.3
, we show that for any simple, locally finite graph, the game admits a unique Nash equilibrium, obtained as the fixed point of the best-response operator. Having established existence and uniqueness, we then investigate structural properties of this equilibrium and prove its convergence.

(i)

Correlation decay.
Assuming that the players’ heterogeneity processes (i.e., their individual noise terms) are independent, Proposition
4.6
proves that the equilibrium strategies exhibit exponential decay of correlations with graph distance. This result is established through the interplay between the Picard approximation of the Nash equilibrium and the rate at which information propagates through the graph.

(ii)

Local approximation.
We formalize the notion of approximating a player’s equilibrium action using only information from a local neighborhood of that player. Proposition
4.8
makes this precise and yields exponential bounds on the convergence rate. Building on this, Proposition
4.11
constructs global
\varepsilon
-Nash equilibria from local equilibrium approximations and derives exponential bounds on the resulting utility gap relative to the global Nash equilibrium.

(iii)

Local reconstruction.
Proposition
4.13
shows that if the Nash equilibrium is known on the vertex boundary of an induced subgraph, then the equilibrium in the interior can be recovered exactly by iterating the best-response operator while keeping the boundary actions fixed.

(iv)

Convergence.
For a sequence of finite random graphs converging locally weakly to a random rooted graph, Theorem
4.16
establishes convergence of the associated Nash equilibria. This result requires the convergent graph sequence to be specified in advance and is proved by combining analytic fixed-point arguments with tools from local weak convergence theory. We prove this both for convergence in distribution in the local weak sense and for the stronger notion of convergence in probability in the local weak sense, with the latter requiring refined techniques.

We also introduce a sampling scheme for a hyperfinite unimodular random graph
G
, producing an increasing sequence of finite random subgraphs
(G_{n})_{n\geq 1}
that converges locally weakly to
G
. In Corollary
4.21
we show that the Nash equilibria of the games on the sampled graphs converge to the Nash equilibrium on
G
.

Collectively, these results establish a unified framework for analyzing strategic interactions on large sparse networks. Beyond a general existence–uniqueness result, we show that equilibrium behavior is intrinsically local: correlations decay exponentially with graph distance, local computations yield global approximate equilibria, and interior equilibrium actions on subgraphs can be exactly recovered from boundary values. Complementing these locality results, we connect finite and infinite populations by proving equilibrium convergence under local weak convergence, including convergence under graph sampling.

Structure of the paper.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. Section
2
reviews sparse graphs, their local weak convergence, and the corresponding limit objects. Section
3
introduces our general framework for stochastic games on large sparse graphs, along with several examples. Section
4
presents our main results, including existence and uniqueness of equilibria, correlation decay of equilibrium actions, local approximation and local reconstruction of equilibria, and convergence of equilibria. Sections
5
–
7
are dedicated to the proofs of our results. Additional results can be found in the appendix.

Preliminaries

In this section we collect the basic notions needed to describe sparse graphs and their convergence. In particular, we define local weak convergence of (random) graph sequences, as introduced by
Benjamini and Schramm 2001
and independently by
Aldous and Steele 2004
. We also consider the concept of unimodular probability measures, which will serve as limit objects in the spirit of graphons for dense graph sequences. Following
[
1
,
7
,
8
,
27
,
44
]
, we work with both unmarked and marked graphs.

2.1
Graphs

Throughout, we will work with graphs
G=(V,E)
that always consist of a finite or countably infinite vertex set
V
and an edge set
E
. All graphs are assumed to be simple (i.e., they are undirected and have no loops or multiple edges) and locally finite (that is, the degree
\deg_{G}(v)
of each vertex
v\in V
is finite). For a vertex
v\in V
, let
N_{v}(G):=\{u\in V:\{u,v\}\in E\}
denote the set of all neighbors of
v
in
G
and write
u\sim v
when
u\in N_{v}(G)
. For two vertices
u,v\in V
, denote by
d_{G}(u,v)
the graph distance between
u
and
v
, that is, the length of the shortest path in
G
from
u
to
v
. If
H=(V_{H},E_{H})
is a graph with
V_{H}\subset V
and
E_{H}\subset E
, we call
H
a subgraph of
G
and write
H\subset G
.
We call
H
an induced subgraph if
E_{H}=\{\{u,v\}\in E:\ u,v\in V_{H}\}
, that is,
H
contains all edges of
G
between vertices in
V_{H}
. Given a subgraph
H\subset G
and
k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, we define its
k
-neighborhood
B_{k}(H):=\{u\in V(G):\min_{v\in V_{H}}d_{G}(u,v)\leq k\}
, where
\min\emptyset:=\infty
.
For two subgraphs
H,H^{\prime}\subset G
, we define their graph distance by
d_{G}(H,H^{\prime}):=\min\{d_{G}(u,v):\ u\in V_{H},\ v\in V_{H^{\prime}}\}
. With a slight abuse of notation, we sometimes write
v\in G
to mean
v\in V
, and similarly set
|G|:=|V|
for the cardinality of the vertex set. We denote by
\mathcal{G}
the set of all finite graphs.

2.2
Local Convergence of Rooted Graphs

A rooted graph is a triple
G=(V,E,o)
consisting of a (simple, locally finite) graph
(V,E)
with a distinguished vertex
o\in V
, called the root. We write
G=(G,o)
when we wish to emphasize the distinguished root
o
, and we denote by
[[G]]
the corresponding unrooted graph. Given a rooted graph
G=(V,E,o)
and
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we write
B_{k}(G)
for the induced rooted subgraph consisting of all vertices
v\in V
whose graph distance
d_{G}(o,v)
from the root
o
is at most
k
. Two rooted graphs
G=(V,E,o)
and
G^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime},o^{\prime})
are said to be isomorphic if there exists a bijection
\varphi:V\to V^{\prime}
with
\varphi(o)=o^{\prime}
such that

\{u,v\}\in E\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\{\varphi(u),\varphi(v)\}\in E^{\prime},\quad\text{for all }u,v\in V.

In this case we write
G\cong G^{\prime}
and say that
\varphi
is an isomorphism from
G
to
G^{\prime}
. We denote by
I(G,G^{\prime})
the set of all isomorphisms from
G
to
G^{\prime}
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{\ast}
denote the set of isomorphism classes of connected, countable, rooted graphs. A sequence
\{G_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{G}_{\ast}
is said to converge locally to
G\in\mathcal{G}_{\ast}
if for every
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
there exists
n_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
B_{k}(G_{n})\cong B_{k}(G)
for all
n\geq n_{k}
.

Remark 2.2
.

The set
\mathcal{G}_{\ast}
can be endowed with a natural metric. For two rooted graphs
G,G^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}_{\ast}
, define

d_{\ast}(G,G^{\prime}):=\inf_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\left\{2^{-k}:B_{k}(G)\cong B_{k}(G^{\prime})\right\}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}2^{-k}\mathds{1}_{\{I(B_{k}(G),B_{k}(G^{\prime}))=\emptyset\}}.

(2.1)

This turns
(\mathcal{G}_{\ast},d_{\ast})
into a complete and separable metric space (see
[
2
]
, Section 2.2). If one works with graphs of uniformly bounded degree,
(\mathcal{G}_{\ast},d_{\ast})
is compact (see
[
31
]
, Chapter 18.3.1).

We now add a further layer of structure by considering marked graphs, in which each vertex carries an associated mark. In the context of a game, vertices represent players, and the marks encode both player-specific noise and individual actions.

Let
(\mathcal{X},d)
be a metric space. An
\mathcal{X}
-marked rooted graph is a pair
(G,x)=(G,o,x)
, where
G=(V,E,o)\in\mathcal{G}_{\ast}
is a connected, countable, rooted graph and
x=(x_{v})_{v\in V}\in\mathcal{X}^{V}
is a family of vertex marks taking values in
\mathcal{X}
. For
(G,x)
and
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we denote by
B_{k}(G,x)
the induced
\mathcal{X}
-marked rooted subgraph obtained by restricting
G
to the ball
B_{k}(G)
around the root and equipping each vertex
v\in B_{k}(G)
with its mark
x_{v}
.
Two
\mathcal{X}
-marked rooted graphs
(G,x)
and
(G^{\prime},x^{\prime})
are called isomorphic if there exists an isomorphism
\varphi:G\to G^{\prime}
such that
x_{v}=x^{\prime}_{\varphi(v)}
for all
v\in V(G)
. In this case we write
(G,x)\cong(G^{\prime},x^{\prime})
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
(\mathcal{X},d)
be a metric space. Denote by
\mathcal{G}_{\ast}[\mathcal{X}]
the set of isomorphism classes of
\mathcal{X}
-marked rooted graphs. A sequence
\{(G_{n},x^{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{G}_{\ast}[\mathcal{X}]
is said to converge locally to
(G,x)\in\mathcal{G}_{\ast}[\mathcal{X}]
if for every
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
n_{k,\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geq n_{k,\varepsilon}
there is an isomorphism
\varphi:B_{k}(G_{n})\to B_{k}(G)
with

\max_{v\in B_{k}(G_{n})}d\big(x^{n}_{v},\,x_{\varphi(v)}\big)<\varepsilon.

Remark 2.4
.

As in the unmarked case, the space
\mathcal{G}_{\ast}[\mathcal{X}]
can be equipped with a metric
d_{\ast}
corresponding to this notion of convergence. Following
[
24
]
, one convenient choice extending (
2.1
) is

d_{\ast}\big((G,x),(G^{\prime},x^{\prime})\big):=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}2^{-k}\Big(1\wedge\inf_{\varphi\in I(B_{k}(G),B_{k}(G^{\prime}))}\max_{v\in B_{k}(G)}d\big(x_{v},\,x^{\prime}_{\varphi(v)}\big)\Big),

(2.2)

where
\inf\emptyset:=\infty
. If
(\mathcal{X},d)
is complete and separable, then
(\mathcal{G}_{\ast}[\mathcal{X}],d_{\ast})
is also complete and separable (see
[
8
]
, Lemma 3.4).

Remark 2.5
.

We will also consider graphs that not only have vertex marks in a metric space
\mathcal{X}
, but also edge marks in another metric space
\mathcal{Y}
. Namely, an
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
-marked rooted graph is a triple
(G,x,y)
, where
G=(V,E,o)\in\mathcal{G}_{\ast}
is a connected, countable, rooted graph,
x=(x_{v})_{v\in V}\in\mathcal{X}^{V}
is a family of vertex marks, and
(y_{e})_{e\in E}\in\mathcal{Y}^{E}
is a family of edge marks. We follow
[
3
,
8
,
29
]
in assigning one
\mathcal{Y}
-valued mark to each undirected edge, rather than two as, for instance, in
[
1
,
7
,
44
]
. The corresponding notion of isomorphism and the associated local metric are defined analogously to the
\mathcal{X}
-marked case, and we denote by
\mathcal{G}_{\ast}[\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}]
the set of isomorphism classes of
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
-marked rooted graphs.

2.3
Local Weak Convergence

Up to now, we have worked with connected graphs, but for local weak convergence one often starts from a possibly disconnected graph and then simply views it through the rooted connected component of a chosen vertex. We make this concept precise following
[
24
,
31
,
44
]
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph and
v\in V
a vertex. We write
C_{v}(G)
for the (isomorphism class of the) connected component of
v
, that is, the rooted graph obtained by taking all vertices
u\in V
that are connected to
v
by a path, and viewing
v
as the root. In particular,
C_{v}(G)\in\mathcal{G}_{\ast}
. For a finite graph
G
, let
U
be a uniformly random vertex of
G
and denote by
C_{U}(G)
the corresponding
\mathcal{G}_{\ast}
-valued random variable.
In the marked setting, for an
\mathcal{X}
-marked rooted graph
(G,x)
, we analogously set
C_{v}(G,x):=(C_{v}(G),\,x_{C_{v}(G)})
and
C_{U}(G,x):=(C_{U}(G),\,x_{C_{U}(G)})
, where
x_{C_{v}(G)}
and
x_{C_{U}(G)}
denote the corresponding restriction of the marks.

All the graph sequences we considered so far were deterministic. Throughout the rest of this section, we work with random graph sequences defined on a probability space with probability measure
\mathbb{Q}
. Just as for random variables, one typically considers convergence in distribution, convergence in probability, and almost sure convergence. Denote by
C_{b}(\mathcal{G}_{\ast})
the set of continuous, bounded functions on
\mathcal{G}_{\ast}
. Recall that
\mathcal{G}
denotes the set of all finite (simple, possibly disconnected) graphs.

Definition 2.6
.

Let
\{G_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{G}
be a sequence of finite random graphs
G_{n}=(V_{n},E_{n})
and let
G
be a random rooted graph in
\mathcal{G}_{\ast}
. Then:

1.

We say that
\{G_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges in distribution in the local weak sense to
G
if

{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\Big[\frac{1}{|V_{n}|}\sum_{v\in V_{n}}h\big(C_{v}(G_{n})\big)\Big]\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\big[h(G)\big],\quad\text{for all }h\in C_{b}(\mathcal{G}_{\ast}).

If
U^{n}
is a uniformly random vertex of
G_{n}
, then, equivalently,
\{C_{U^{n}}(G_{n})\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
converges in distribution to
G
in
\mathcal{G}_{\ast}
.

2.

We say that
\{G_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges in
\mathbb{Q}
-probability in the local weak sense to
G
if

\frac{1}{|V_{n}|}\sum_{v\in V_{n}}h\big(C_{v}(G_{n})\big)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\ \text{prob.}\ }{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\big[h(G)\big],\quad\text{for all }h\in C_{b}(\mathcal{G}_{\ast}).

3.

We say that
\{G_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges
\mathbb{Q}
-almost surely in the local weak sense to
G
if

\frac{1}{|V_{n}|}\sum_{v\in V_{n}}h\big(C_{v}(G_{n})\big)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\text{a.s.}}{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\big[h(G)\big],\quad\text{for all }h\in C_{b}(\mathcal{G}_{\ast}).

Remark 2.7
.

The three notions of local weak convergence above form a hierarchy:

\mathbb{Q}
-almost sure convergence implies convergence in
\mathbb{Q}
-probability, which in turn
implies convergence in distribution. For deterministic graph sequences, however, these distinctions disappear. If the sequence
\{G_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is deterministic, all randomness lies in the limit object
G
, and all three notions of convergence reduce to the single requirement

\frac{1}{|V_{n}|}\sum_{v\in V_{n}}h\big(C_{v}(G_{n})\big)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\big[h(G)\big],\quad\text{for all }h\in C_{b}(\mathcal{G}_{\ast}).

Remark 2.8
.

The concept of local weak convergence can be extended to the marked setting. Let
(\mathcal{X},d)
be a metric space. For
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
(G_{n},x^{n})
be a finite random graph
G_{n}=(V_{n},E_{n})
equipped with random vertex marks
x^{n}=(x_{v}^{n})_{v\in V_{n}}\in\mathcal{X}^{V_{n}}
. Let
(G,x)
be a random rooted graph
G
together with random marks
x=(x_{v})_{v\in G}
, that is, a
\mathcal{G}_{\ast}[\mathcal{X}]
-valued random variable. Then Definition
2.6
carries over simply by replacing
C_{v}(G_{n})
with the marked component
C_{v}(G_{n},x^{n})
and
h\in C_{b}(\mathcal{G}_{\ast})
with
h\in C_{b}(\mathcal{G}_{\ast}[\mathcal{X}])
. For example, convergence in distribution in the local weak sense of the marked graphs
\{(G_{n},x^{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
to
(G,x)
means that

{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\Big[\frac{1}{|V_{n}|}\sum_{v\in V_{n}}h\big(C_{v}(G_{n},x^{n})\big)\Big]\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\big[h(G,x)\big],\quad\text{for all }h\in C_{b}\big(\mathcal{G}_{\ast}[\mathcal{X}]\big).

The concepts presented next in Sections
2.4
and
2.5
are only needed for Corollary
4.21
, where we construct a locally weakly convergent sequence of finite games by sampling from a fixed random rooted graph. They are not used elsewhere in the paper, and may be skipped without affecting the readability of the other results.

2.4
Limits of Sparse Graph Sequences

The limits of locally weakly convergent graph sequences are random rooted graphs in
\mathcal{G}_{\ast}
, or, equivalently, probability measures on
\mathcal{G}_{\ast}
. So far, however, we have not put any measure-theoretic structure on
\mathcal{G}_{\ast}
itself. Recall that
d_{\ast}
makes
(\mathcal{G}_{\ast},d_{\ast})
a complete and separable metric space, and denote by
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{G}_{\ast})
the associated Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
\mathcal{G}_{\ast}
. An important observation is that not every probability measure on
(\mathcal{G}_{\ast},\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{G}_{\ast}))
arises as a local weak limit. More precisely, since the root in Definition
2.6
is chosen uniformly at random, any limiting law must satisfy a corresponding invariance property. This requirement is captured by the concept of unimodularity, which we introduce following
[
1
,
8
]
.

A doubly-rooted graph
G=(V,E,o_{1},o_{2})
consists of a (simple, locally finite) graph
(V,E)
and two roots
o_{1},o_{2}\in V
. We write
G=(G,o_{1},o_{2})
when we wish to emphasize the distinguished roots
o_{1}
,
o_{2}
. Two doubly-rooted graphs
G=(V,E,o_{1},o_{2})
and
G^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime},o_{1}^{\prime},o_{2}^{\prime})
are said to be isomorphic if there exists a bijection
\varphi:V\to V^{\prime}
with
\varphi(o_{1})=o_{1}^{\prime}
and
\varphi(o_{2})=o_{2}^{\prime}
such that

\{u,v\}\in E\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\{\varphi(u),\varphi(v)\}\in E^{\prime},\quad\text{for all }u,v\in V.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{\ast\ast}
denote the set of isomorphism classes of connected, countable, doubly-rooted graphs. As for rooted graphs,
\mathcal{G}_{\ast\ast}
can be equipped with a local metric
d_{\ast\ast}
that gives rise to a Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
\mathcal{G}_{\ast\ast}
.

Definition 2.9
.

A probability measure
\sigma
on
(\mathcal{G}_{\ast},\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{G}_{\ast}))
is called unimodular if it obeys the following mass-transport principle: for all Borel-measurable
F:\mathcal{G}_{\ast\ast}\to[0,\infty]
,

\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\sum_{v\in G}F(G,o,v)\sigma(d(G,o))=\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\sum_{v\in G}F(G,v,o)\sigma(d(G,o)).

(2.3)

If
G\in\mathcal{G}_{\ast}
is a random rooted graph with law
\sigma=\mathcal{L}(G)
and
\sigma
is a unimodular distribution, we say that
G
is a unimodular random graph.

As observed by
Benjamini and Schramm 2001
, any random rooted graph that is the local weak limit of a finite graph sequence is unimodular, as stated in the following lemma.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
\{G_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\subset\mathcal{G}
be a sequence of finite random graphs, and let
G\in\mathcal{G}_{\ast}
be a random rooted graph. If
\{G_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges in distribution in the local weak sense to
G
, then
G
is unimodular.

2.5
Hyperfiniteness and Sampling

While unimodularity is a necessary condition for a random rooted graph
G\in\mathcal{G}_{\ast}
to arise as the local weak limit of a sequence of finite graphs, it is not sufficient in general
[
11
,
12
]
. A convenient additional assumption ensuring sufficiency and thereby guaranteeing approximability of
G
by finite graphs is hyperfiniteness
[
1
,
42
]
. We follow the presentation of
[
3
,
29
]
, which aligns best with our framework. Recall the definition of vertex-edge-marked graphs from Remark
2.5
.

Definition 2.11
.

Let
G=(V,E,o)\in\mathcal{G}_{\ast}
be a unimodular random graph. A percolation
\xi
on
G
is a
\{0,1\}
-valued vertex-edge marking
\xi=(x,y)
such that the random rooted vertex-edge-marked graph
(G,x,y)
with
x=(x_{v})_{v\in V}\in\{0,1\}^{V}
and
(y_{e})_{e\in E}\in\{0,1\}^{E}
is unimodular. That is, the law of
(G,x,y)
satisfies Definition
2.9
with
\mathcal{G}_{\ast}
,
\mathcal{G}_{\ast\ast}
, and
F:\mathcal{G}_{\ast\ast}\to[0,\infty]
replaced by
\mathcal{G}_{\ast}[\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}]
,
\mathcal{G}_{\ast\ast}[\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}]
, and
F:\mathcal{G}_{\ast\ast}[\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}]\to[0,\infty]
for
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{Y}=\{0,1\}
, respectively. Here, we assume consistency of vertex and edge marks in the sense that an edge can be marked
1
only if both its endpoints are marked
1
, i.e.,
y_{\{u,v\}}=1
implies
x_{u}=x_{v}=1
for every
\{u,v\}\in E
.

It may be more intuitive to view a percolation
\xi
as specifying a random subgraph
H_{\xi}\subset G
where precisely those vertices and edges with mark
1
are kept. We say that
\xi
is finitary if all connected components of
H_{\xi}
are finite
\mathbb{Q}
-almost surely.

Definition 2.12
.

A unimodular random graph
G\in\mathcal{G}_{\ast}
is called hyperfinite if there exists a sequence
(\xi_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of finitary percolations on
G
such that the associated random subgraphs form an increasing sequence
H_{\xi_{1}}\subset H_{\xi_{2}}\subset\ldots
with
\bigcup_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}H_{\xi_{n}}=G
,
\mathbb{Q}
-almost surely. In this case,
\{H_{\xi_{n}}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is called a finitary exhaustion.

Percolations and hyperfiniteness can be analogously defined for vertex-marked graphs. The main point for us is that hyperfiniteness yields an approximation by finite unimodular
random graphs
\{H_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
(see
[
29
]
, Corollary 4.2).

Lemma 2.13
.

Let
G=(G,o)\in\mathcal{G}_{\ast}
be a hyperfinite unimodular random graph with finitary exhaustion
\{H_{\xi_{n}}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
. Then the sequence of finite unimodular random graphs
\{H_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\subset\mathcal{G}_{\ast}
given by
H_{n}:=C_{o}(H_{\xi_{n}})
converges
\mathbb{Q}
-almost surely and hence also in distribution to
G
in
(\mathcal{G}_{\ast},d_{\ast})
.

Lemma
2.13
yields a sequence of finite unimodular random graphs
\{H_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
that converges in distribution in
\mathcal{G}_{\ast}
to a given hyperfinite unimodular random graph
G
. Since
\{H_{\xi_{n}}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is finitary, the mass-transport principle implies that the root is uniformly distributed on its connected component
H_{n}=C_{o}(H_{\xi_{n}})
(see
[
3
]
, Lemma 3.1). Therefore, by Definition
2.6
, forgetting the root yields an unrooted sequence of finite random graphs converging to
G
in distribution in the local weak sense.

Corollary 2.14
.

Let
G=(G,o)\in\mathcal{G}_{\ast}
be a hyperfinite unimodular random graph with finitary exhaustion
(H_{\xi_{n}})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
. Then the sequence of finite random unrooted graphs
\{G_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\subset\mathcal{G}
given by
G_{n}:=[[C_{o}(H_{\xi_{n}})]]
converges
in distribution in the local weak sense to
G
.

Remark 2.15
.

Lemma
2.13
and Corollary
2.14
can be extended to the marked setting. Let
(\mathcal{X},d)
be a metric space. If
(G,x)=(G,o,x)\in\mathcal{G}_{\ast}[\mathcal{X}]
is a hyperfinite unimodular random
\mathcal{X}
-marked graph with finitary exhaustion
\{H_{\xi_{n}}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, then the sequence of finite unimodular random
\mathcal{X}
-marked graphs
\{(H_{n},x_{H_{n}})\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
given by
H_{n}:=C_{o}(H_{\xi_{n}})
converges in distribution to
(G,x)
in
\mathcal{G}_{\ast}[\mathcal{X}]
. Similarly, by Lemma 3.1 of
[
3
]
, this implies that the sequence of finite random unrooted
\mathcal{X}
-marked graphs
\{(G_{n},x_{G_{n}})\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
given by
G_{n}:=[[C_{o}(H_{\xi_{n}})]]
converges in distribution in the local weak sense to
(G,x)
.

3
Model Setup

In this section we present a general framework for stochastic games on large sparse graphs, which includes both continuous-time and discrete-time games and allows heterogeneous, path-dependent utility functionals. We also provide several examples.

3.1
Formulation of the Game

Let
T>0
denote a finite deterministic time horizon and fix a filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},{\mathbb{F}}:=(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T},\mathbb{P})
. We consider a dynamic stochastic game on a fixed (locally finite, simple) graph
G=(V,E)
, where each player interacts with their finitely many neighbors. Here we allow both finite and countably infinite graphs and thus define the finite-player and infinite-player game at the same time. We label the players by
v\in V
(or
v\in G
). Define the set of admissible actions

\mathcal{A}:=\left\{\tilde{a}:\Omega\times[0,T]\to{\mathbb{R}}\,\Big|\,\tilde{a}\textrm{ is }\mathbb{F}\textrm{-prog.~measurable, }\,\|\tilde{a}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{A}}:={\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}\Big[\int_{0}^{T}\tilde{a}(t)^{2}dt\Big]<\infty\right\},

(3.1)

where throughout, variables with a tilde denote generic elements in
\mathcal{A}
. The set
\mathcal{A}
can be turned into a Hilbert space by equipping it with the inner product

\big\langle\tilde{a}_{1},\tilde{a}_{2}\big\rangle_{\mathcal{A}}:={\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\tilde{a}_{1}(t)\tilde{a}_{2}(t)dt\right],\quad\tilde{a}_{1},\tilde{a}_{2}\in\mathcal{A}.

(3.2)

Remark 3.1
.

Our framework also includes discrete-time stochastic games. Namely, for a fixed finite partition of
[0,T]
given by
\mathbb{T}:=\{0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\ldots<t_{N}=T\}
with
N\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, one can analogously define the set of admissible actions by

\mathcal{A}:=\bigg\{\tilde{a}:\Omega\times\mathbb{T}\to{\mathbb{R}}\,\Big|\,\tilde{a}(t_{j})\ \text{is }\mathcal{F}_{t_{j}}\text{-measurable for each }j,\,\,\|\tilde{a}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{A}}:={\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}\Big[\sum_{j=0}^{N}\tilde{a}(t_{j})^{2}\Big]<\infty\bigg\},

(3.3)

with corresponding inner product rendering it a Hilbert space given by

\big\langle\tilde{a}_{1},\tilde{a}_{2}\big\rangle_{\mathcal{A}}:={\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}\Big[\sum_{j=0}^{N}\tilde{a}_{1}(t_{j})\tilde{a}_{2}(t_{j})\Big],\quad\tilde{a}_{1},\tilde{a}_{2}\in\mathcal{A}.

(3.4)

In particular, the case where
T=0
and
N=0
then yields a static game. Throughout the paper, we have both the continuous-time and the discrete-time formulation in mind, and all results and proofs hold in either setting.

Definition 3.2
.

An action profile is a family
a=(a_{v})_{v\in G}
of actions
a_{v}\in\mathcal{A}
. Define the set of feasible action profiles as

\mathcal{A}^{G}:=\Big\{a=(a_{v})_{v\in G}\,\Big|\,a_{v}\in\mathcal{A},\ \|a\|_{\mathcal{A}^{G}}:=\sup_{v\in G}\|a_{v}\|_{\mathcal{A}}<\infty\Big\}.

(3.5)

rendering
\mathcal{A}^{G}
a Banach space.

Each player
v\in G
selects an action
a_{v}
from their individual set of admissible actions
\mathcal{A}_{v}\subset\mathcal{A}
, which may be heterogeneous across the players.
Define the corresponding set of admissible action profiles as

\mathcal{A}_{ad}^{G}:=\left\{a\in\mathcal{A}^{G}\Big|a_{v}\in\mathcal{A}_{v}\textrm{ for all }v\in G\right\}.

(3.6)

In the sparse setting, each player interacts with at most finitely many other players. The interaction effects experienced by player
v\in G
are defined as the local aggregate

z_{v}(a):=(\boldsymbol{G}a)(v):=\frac{\mathds{1}_{\{\deg_{G}(v)>0\}}}{\deg_{G}(v)}\sum_{u\sim v}a_{u},\quad a\in\mathcal{A}^{G},\ v\in G,

(3.7)

where we use the convention that
0/0:=0
and note that the denominator in (
3.7
) is finite, since
G
is locally finite by assumption.

Remark 3.3
.

Note that the local aggregate operator
\boldsymbol{G}
on
\mathcal{A}^{G}
from (
3.7
) is linear. Its operator norm given by

\|\boldsymbol{G}\|_{\text{op}}:=\sup\left\{\|\boldsymbol{G}a\|_{\mathcal{A}^{G}}:a\in\mathcal{A}^{G},\|a\|_{\mathcal{A}^{G}}\leq 1\right\}

(3.8)

is equal to 1 (unless
G
has no edges, then
\|\boldsymbol{G}\|_{\text{op}}=0
), thus,
\boldsymbol{G}
is bounded. We could have also defined
\boldsymbol{G}
in another way. For instance, if the vertex degrees of
G
are uniformly bounded by
D\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the alternative definition

(\boldsymbol{G}a)(v):=\frac{1}{D}\sum_{u\sim v}a_{u},\quad a\in\mathcal{A}^{G},\ v\in G,

yields a linear bounded operator with
\|\boldsymbol{G}\|_{\text{op}}\leq 1
, and all the subsequent analysis carries over with minimal modifications.

Next, let
\theta=(\theta_{v})_{v\in G}\in\mathcal{A}^{G}
be a heterogeneity profile consisting of processes
\theta_{v}\in\mathcal{A}
. These can contain both idiosyncratic and common noise and thereby allow us to incorporate heterogeneity among the players. Consider a universal utility functional

U:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}\to{\mathbb{R}},

(3.9)

acting directly on player actions, local aggregates, and heterogeneity processes. Let the individual utility functional of player
v
on
\mathcal{A}_{v}
be given by

a_{v}\mapsto U\left(a_{v},z_{v}(a),\theta_{v}\right).

(3.10)

In line with
[
33
,
37
]
, we denote this game by
\mathbb{G}(\mathcal{A}_{ad}^{G},U,\theta,G)
.

Definition 3.4
.

An admissible action profile
(\bar{a}_{v})_{v\in G}\in\mathcal{A}_{ad}^{G}
is called a Nash equilibrium of the game
\mathbb{G}(\mathcal{A}_{ad}^{G},U,\theta,G)
if for every
v\in G
the action
\bar{a}_{v}
satisfies

\bar{a}_{v}=\argmax_{\tilde{a}\in\mathcal{A}_{v}}\ U\left(\tilde{a},z_{v}(\bar{a}),\theta_{v}\right).

(3.11)

We will also need the more general notion of approximate Nash equilibria.

Definition 3.5
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
.
An admissible action profile
(a_{v})_{v\in G}\in\mathcal{A}_{ad}^{G}
is called an
\varepsilon
-Nash equilibrium of the game
\mathbb{G}(\mathcal{A}_{ad}^{G},U,\theta,G)
if for every
v\in G
the action
a_{v}
satisfies

U(a_{v},z_{v}(a),\theta_{v})\geq\sup_{\tilde{a}\in\mathcal{A}_{v}}U\left(\tilde{a},z_{v}(a),\theta_{v}\right)-\varepsilon.

(3.12)

3.2
Examples

Note that our formulation does not model state variables explicitly. Instead, each player’s payoff is specified through the universal functional
U
in (
3.9
), which acts directly on progressively measurable processes. This yields a flexible setup that encompasses, for instance, the following classes of games.

Example 3.6
.

A basic class of games is given by functionals of the form

U(\tilde{a},\tilde{z},\tilde{\theta})={\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}\!\left[\int_{0}^{T}f_{U}\big(\tilde{a}(t),\tilde{z}(t),\tilde{\theta}(t)\big)\,dt\right],

where
f_{U}:{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\to{\mathbb{R}}
specifies a running payoff. By considering a discrete time grid
\mathbb{T}
equipped with the counting measure in the sense of Remark
3.1
, one recovers discrete-time games.

Example 3.7
.

A further important family with various applications is given by linear–quadratic Volterra functionals,

U(\tilde{a},\tilde{z},\tilde{\theta})=\langle\tilde{a},\boldsymbol{A}_{1}\tilde{a}\rangle_{\mathcal{A}}+\langle\tilde{a},\boldsymbol{A}_{2}\tilde{z}\rangle_{\mathcal{A}}+\langle\tilde{z},\boldsymbol{A}_{2}\tilde{a}\rangle_{\mathcal{A}}+\langle\tilde{z},\boldsymbol{A}_{3}\tilde{z}\rangle_{\mathcal{A}}+\langle\tilde{a},\tilde{\theta}\rangle_{\mathcal{A}}+\langle\tilde{z},\tilde{\theta}^{\ast}\rangle_{\mathcal{A}},

where
\boldsymbol{A}_{1},\boldsymbol{A}_{2},\boldsymbol{A}_{3}
are Volterra operators on
L^{2}([0,T])
extended to
\mathcal{A}
in the natural way and
\tilde{\theta}^{\ast}\in\mathcal{A}
is an exogenous input (e.g. common noise). The Volterra structure naturally encodes memory, allowing
U
to depend on the past of
\tilde{a}
and
\tilde{z}
in a non-Markovian way. In the finite-player setting, explicit Nash equilibria for this class were derived in
[
32
]
.

Example 3.8
.

A large class of games with linear state variables can be rewritten in the above state-free form by explicitly solving the state equation and substituting the solution into the objective. Let
G
be any simple, locally finite graph and fix square-integrable Volterra kernels
K,L:[0,T]^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}
together with idiosyncratic noise processes
\eta=(\eta_{v})_{v\in G}\in\mathcal{A}^{G}
.
Given an action profile
a=(a_{v})_{v\in G}\in\mathcal{A}^{G}
, suppose each player
v\in G
has a controlled state process
X_{v}\in\mathcal{A}
following the linear dynamics

X_{v}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}K(t,s)X_{v}(s)ds+\int_{0}^{t}L(t,s)a_{v}(s)ds+\eta_{v}(t),\quad t\in[0,T].

(3.13)

Define the corresponding local aggregate state
Z_{v}\in\mathcal{A}
by

Z_{v}:=z_{v}(X)=(\boldsymbol{G}X)(v),\quad\text{i.e.,}\quad Z_{v}(t)=\frac{\mathds{1}_{\{\deg_{G}(v)>0\}}}{\deg_{G}(v)}\sum_{u\sim v}X_{u}(t),\quad t\in[0,T].

Let
R
denote the resolvent of the kernel
(-K)
and set

(R\star L)(t,s):=\int_{0}^{t}R(t,u)L(u,s)du,\quad t,s\in[0,T].

Then (
3.13
) admits the explicit representation

X_{v}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\big(L-(R\star L)\big)(t,s)a_{v}(s)ds-\int_{0}^{t}R(t,s)\eta_{v}(s)ds+\eta_{v}(t),\quad t\in[0,T],

(3.14)

by Chapter 9.3 of
[
20
]
. Consider payoffs of the classical form

J_{v}(a_{v};(a_{u})_{u\neq v})={\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}f_{J}\big(t,X_{v}(t),Z_{v}(t),a_{v}(t)\big)dt+g_{J}\big(X_{v}(T),Z_{v}(T)\big)\right],

(3.15)

with running payoff
f_{J}:{\mathbb{R}}^{4}\to{\mathbb{R}}
and terminal payoff
g_{J}:{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}
. Solving for the state variables via (
3.14
) and substituting into (
3.15
) yields a representation of the form

J_{v}(a_{v};(a_{u})_{u\neq v})=U\big(a_{v},z_{v}(a),\theta_{v}\big),

for a suitable universal functional
U
and a suitable heterogeneity profile
(\theta_{v})_{v\in G}\in\mathcal{A}^{G}
. Hence, games with linear state dynamics can be embedded into our general framework once the associated state equations are solved explicitly.

4
Main Results

This section presents our main results, including the existence and uniqueness of equilibria, correlation decay of equilibrium actions, local approximation and local reconstruction of equilibria, and convergence of equilibria along locally weakly convergent graph sequences.

4.1
Existence and Uniqueness

In line with
[
33
,
37
]
, we focus on continuously differentiable and strongly concave utility functionals to study the Nash equilibrium of the game and its properties.

Assumption 4.1
.

For all
\tilde{z},\tilde{\theta}\in\mathcal{A}
, the utility functional
U(\tilde{a},\tilde{z},\tilde{\theta})
in (
3.9
) is continuously Gâteaux differentiable in
\tilde{a}
and strongly concave in
\tilde{a}
with strong concavity constant
\gamma_{U}>0
, that is,
\smash{U(\tilde{a},\tilde{z},\tilde{\theta})+\frac{\gamma_{U}}{2}\|\tilde{a}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{A}}}
is concave in
\tilde{a}
. Moreover,
\smash{\nabla_{\tilde{a}}U(\cdot,\tilde{z},\tilde{\theta})}
is Lipschitz continuous in
\tilde{z},\tilde{\theta}
with constants
\ell_{U},\ell_{\theta}
, and it holds that

\rho_{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny\eqref{eq:rho}}}:=\ell_{U}/\gamma_{U}<1.

(4.1)

Assumption 4.2
.

For each player
v\in G
, the set of admissible actions
\mathcal{A}_{v}
from (
3.6
) is nonempty, convex, and closed. Moreover, there exist
\tilde{z}^{0},\tilde{\theta}^{0}\in\mathcal{A}
such that

\big(\argmax_{\tilde{a}\in\mathcal{A}_{v}}U(\tilde{a},\tilde{z}^{0},\tilde{\theta}^{0})\big)_{v\in G}\in\mathcal{A}^{G}.

We note that the second part of Assumption
4.2
always holds in the finite-player case. The following theorem establishes existence and uniqueness of a Nash equilibrium as introduced in Definition
3.4
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Suppose that Assumptions
4.1
and
4.2
hold. Then the game
\mathbb{G}(\mathcal{A}_{ad}^{G},U,\theta,G)
admits a unique Nash equilibrium for any simple, locally finite graph
G
.

The proof of Theorem
4.3
is given in Section
5
.

Remark 4.4
.

The condition
\rho_{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny\eqref{eq:rho}}}<1
will be essential throughout the paper. It ensures that the optimization problem can be cast via a contraction principle: the Nash equilibrium is characterized as the unique fixed point of an appropriate best-response operator, with
\rho_{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny\eqref{eq:rho}}}
acting as a contraction constant. This contraction property not only yields existence and uniqueness via Banach’s fixed-point theorem, but also provides geometric error bounds for the associated Picard iterates. Such bounds allow for efficient approximation of the Nash equilibrium and will serve as a central mechanism in the proofs of our other results (see Propositions
4.6
,
4.8
,
4.11
,
4.13
, and Theorem
4.16
).

4.2
Correlation Decay

The following result shows that, under independence of the players’ heterogeneity processes, the equilibrium actions exhibit exponential decay of correlations with graph distance.

Assumption 4.5
.

There exists a constant
M_{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny\eqref{eq:A_M}}}>0
such that for every
v\in G
, the set of admissible actions
\mathcal{A}_{v}
introduced before (
3.6
) satisfies

\mathcal{A}_{v}\subset\mathcal{A}_{M_{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny\eqref{eq:A_M}}}}:=\big\{\tilde{a}\in\mathcal{A}\,\big|\,\|\tilde{a}\|_{\mathcal{A}}\leq M_{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny\eqref{eq:A_M}}}\big\}.

(4.2)

For a graph
G
and a subgraph
H\subset G
, denote by
a_{H}\in\mathcal{A}^{H}
the restriction of an action profile
a\in\mathcal{A}^{G}
to
H
. Consistent with (
3.5
), endow
\mathcal{A}^{H}
with the supremum norm
\|a\|_{\mathcal{A}^{H}}:=\sup_{v\in H}\|a_{v}\|_{\mathcal{A}}
. Similarly, equip the associated path space
\mathcal{L}^{H}:=(L^{2}([0,T]))^{H}
with the norm
\|\psi\|_{\mathcal{L}^{H}}:=\sup_{v\in H}\|\psi_{v}\|_{L^{2}}
for
\psi\in\mathcal{L}^{H}
and let
d_{H}
be the induced metric

d_{H}(\psi,\psi^{\prime}):=\sup_{v\in H}\|\psi_{v}-\psi^{\prime}_{v}\|_{L^{2}}.

(4.3)

For a function
f:\mathcal{L}^{H}\to{\mathbb{R}}
that is bounded and Lipschitz with respect to
d_{H}
, define the bounded Lipschitz norm

\|f\|_{\text{BL}}:=\max\big\{\|f\|_{\infty},\mathrm{Lip}(f)\big\},

(4.4)

where
\|f\|_{\infty}
is the supremum norm and
\mathrm{Lip}(f)
is the Lipschitz constant of
f
. Recall the definition of the graph distance
d_{G}
from Section
2.1
.

Proposition 4.6
.

Suppose that Assumptions
4.1
,
4.2
, and
4.5
hold. Assume that the heterogeneity processes
(\theta_{v})_{v\in G}
are independent under
\mathbb{P}
and that
\smash{\argmax_{\tilde{a}\in\mathcal{A}_{v}}U(\tilde{a},\tilde{z},\theta_{v})}
is
\sigma(\tilde{z},\theta_{v})
-measurable for all
\tilde{z}\in\mathcal{A}
and
v\in G
. Let
\bar{a}\in\mathcal{A}^{G}
denote the unique Nash equilibrium of
\mathbb{G}(\mathcal{A}_{ad}^{G},U,\theta,G)
. For
i=1,2
, let
H_{i}\subset G
be a finite induced subgraph and let
f_{i}:\mathcal{L}^{H_{i}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be bounded and Lipschitz with respect to
d_{H_{i}}
. Then,

\big|\mathrm{Cov}_{\mathbb{P}}\big(f_{1}(\bar{a}_{H_{1}}),f_{2}(\bar{a}_{H_{2}})\big)\big|\leq 2\rho_{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny\eqref{eq:rho}}}^{k}M_{\raisebox{-0.90417pt}{\tiny\eqref{eq:A_M}}}(|H_{1}|+|H_{2}|)\,\|f_{1}\|_{\text{BL}}\|f_{2}\|_{\text{BL}},

where

k=\big\lceil d_{G}(H_{1},H_{2})/2\big\rceil.

(4.5)

The proof of Proposition
4.6
is given in Section
6
.

Figure 1
:
Left: the circle graph with 30 vertices
G=C_{30}
with two induced subgraphs
H_{1}\subset G
(red) and
H_{2}\subset G
(green). It holds that
d_{G}(H_{1},H_{2})=9
and thus
k=5
in (
4.5
). Right: an excerpt of the 4-regular tree
G^{\prime}=T_{4}
with two induced subgraphs
H^{\prime}_{1}\subset G^{\prime}
(red) and
H^{\prime}_{2}\subset G^{\prime}
(green). It holds that
d_{G^{\prime}}(H^{\prime}_{1},H^{\prime}_{2})=4
and thus
k=2
in (
4.5
).

Remark 4.7
.

Proposition
4.6
establishes that correlations in the Nash equilibrium decay exponentially fast with respect to graph distance. Importantly, this is a statement for the equilibrium controls on the full time horizon. That is, the covariance is taken between functionals of entire trajectories of
(\bar{a}_{u})_{u\in H_{i}}
, rather than describing a decay of correlations in time. This is conceptually different from correlation decay results for interacting particle systems (see
[
27
]
, Lemmas 5.1 and 5.2).

4.3
Local Approximation

It can be desirable to approximate the equilibrium action of a player
v\in G
using only local information in a neighborhood of
v
. The next result formalizes such a local approximation of the Nash equilibrium.

For
v\in G
and
k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, denote the
k
-ball around
v
by
B_{k}(G,v)
. Given a local action profile
a=(a_{u})_{u\in B_{k}(G,v)}\in\mathcal{A}^{B_{k}(G,v)}
, define the truncated local aggregate by

z^{(v,k)}_{u}(a):=(\boldsymbol{G}^{(v,k)}a)(u):=\frac{\mathds{1}_{\{\deg_{G}(u)>0\}}}{\deg_{G}(u)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\sim u\\
w\in B_{k}(G,v)\end{subarray}}a_{w},\quad u\in B_{k}(G,v).

(4.6)

Note that we normalize by
\smash{\deg_{G}(u)}
and not by
\smash{\deg_{B_{k}(G,v)}(u)}
in (
4.6
) when restricting to the neighborhood
B_{k}(G,v)
. Recalling (
3.8
), the operator
\boldsymbol{G}^{(v,k)}
on
\mathcal{A}^{B_{k}(G,v)}
satisfies
\|\boldsymbol{G}^{(v,k)}\|_{\operatorname{op}}\leq 1
. We refer to this truncated local game on
B_{k}(G,v)
as
\mathbb{G}^{(v,k)}(\mathcal{A}_{ad}^{G},U,\theta,G)
. Under the assumptions of Theorem
4.3
, existence and uniqueness follow as for the game on the original graph
G
.

Proposition 4.8
.

Suppose that Assumptions
4.1
,
4.2
, and
4.5
hold. Let
\bar{a}\in\mathcal{A}^{G}
be the unique Nash equilibrium of
\mathbb{G}(\mathcal{A}_{ad}^{G},U,\theta,G)
, and fix
v\in G
and
k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
. Let
\smash{\bar{a}^{(v,k)}\in\mathcal{A}^{B_{k}(G,v)}}
be the unique Nash equilibrium of the truncated local game
\mathbb{G}^{(v,k)}(\mathcal{A}_{ad}^{G},U,\theta,G)
. Then,

\|\bar{a}_{v}-\bar{a}^{(v,k)}_{v}\|_{\mathcal{A}}\leq 2\rho_{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny\eqref{eq:rho}}}^{k}M_{\raisebox{-0.90417pt}{\tiny\eqref{eq:A_M}}}.

(4.7)

The proof of Proposition
4.8
is given in Section
6
.

Remark 4.9
.

Proposition
4.8
quantifies a tradeoff between locality and accuracy. To approximate the global equilibrium action
\bar{a}_{v}
of a given player
v\in G
within a prescribed tolerance
\delta>0
, it suffices to solve the truncated local game on a ball of radius

k\geq\left\lceil\frac{\log(\delta/(2M_{\raisebox{-0.60275pt}{\tiny\eqref{eq:A_M}}}))}{\log\rho_{\raisebox{-0.60275pt}{\tiny\eqref{eq:rho}}}}\right\rceil,

since then
2\rho_{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny\eqref{eq:rho}}}^{k}M_{\raisebox{-0.60275pt}{\tiny\eqref{eq:A_M}}}\leq\delta
in (
4.7
). That is, increasing the radius
k
enlarges the ball
B_{k}(G,v)
, but yields an exponentially more accurate local approximation of
\bar{a}_{v}
.

Proposition
4.8
provides a local approximation of the global Nash equilibrium action profile
\bar{a}\in\mathcal{A}^{G}
in the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{A}}
. For the related notion of
\varepsilon
-Nash equilibria introduced in Definition
3.5
, we are interested in closeness in terms of the players’ utility functionals rather than their controls. This is established in Proposition
4.11
below.

Assumption 4.10
.

Under Assumption
4.5
, assume there exist constants
L_{a},L_{z}\geq 0
such that for all
\tilde{a},\tilde{a}^{\prime},\tilde{z},\tilde{z}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}_{M_{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny\eqref{eq:A_M}}}}

and all
\tilde{\theta}\in\mathcal{A}
,

\big|U(\tilde{a},\tilde{z},\tilde{\theta})-U(\tilde{a}^{\prime},\tilde{z}^{\prime},\tilde{\theta})\big|\leq L_{a}\|\tilde{a}-\tilde{a}^{\prime}\|_{\mathcal{A}}+L_{z}\|\tilde{z}-\tilde{z}^{\prime}\|_{\mathcal{A}}.

(4.8)

Proposition 4.11
.

Suppose Assumptions
4.1
,
4.2
,
4.5
, and
4.10
hold. Fix

. For each
v\in G
, let
\bar{a}^{(v,k)}\in\mathcal{A}^{B_{k}(G,v)}
be the unique Nash equilibrium of the truncated local game
\mathbb{G}^{(v,k)}(\mathcal{A}_{ad}^{G},U,\theta,G)
on
B_{k}(G,v)
, and define the global action profile
\hat{a}^{(k)}\in\mathcal{A}^{G}
by

\hat{a}^{(k)}_{v}:=\bar{a}^{(v,k)}_{v},\quad v\in G.

Then

is an
\varepsilon_{k}
-Nash equilibrium of the global game
\mathbb{G}(\mathcal{A}_{ad}^{G},U,\theta,G)
with

\varepsilon_{k}:=2\rho_{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny\eqref{eq:rho}}}^{k}M_{\raisebox{-0.90417pt}{\tiny\eqref{eq:A_M}}}(L_{a}+2L_{z}).

(4.9)

In particular,

as

.

The proof of Proposition

is given in Section

Remark 4.12
.

Proposition
4.11
shows that a global approximate Nash equilibrium can be constructed from purely local computations. Indeed, for
k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
and each vertex
v\in G
, one solves the truncated local game on the ball

and uses the resulting local equilibrium action at

-Nash equilibrium of the global game with

to ensure that

is an

.

4.4
Local Reconstruction

The previous subsection showed that equilibrium actions can be approximated from local information. The next result shows that if the Nash equilibrium is known exactly on the vertex boundary of a subgraph, then it can be reconstructed exactly in the interior by running best-response Picard iterates while keeping the boundary actions fixed.

Let
H\subset G
be an induced subgraph. Define its boundary and interior by

\partial H\,:=\,\Big\{u\in H:\ \exists\,w\notin H\text{ with }w\sim u\Big\},\quad H^{\circ}\,:=\,H\setminus\partial H.

Note that if
u\in H^{\circ}
, then every neighbor of
u
lies in
H
. Next, given
b=(b_{u})_{u\in\partial H}\in\mathcal{A}^{\partial H}
and
a=(a_{u})_{u\in H^{\circ}}\in\mathcal{A}^{H^{\circ}}
, define the clamped profile
a\oplus b\in\mathcal{A}^{H}
by

(a\oplus b)_{u}:=\begin{cases}a_{u},&u\in H^{\circ},\\
b_{u},&u\in\partial H.\end{cases}

For
b\in\mathcal{A}^{\partial H}
define the corresponding clamped local aggregate on the interior by

z_{u}^{H,b}(a):=\frac{\mathds{1}_{\{\deg_{G}(u)>0\}}}{\deg_{G}(u)}\sum_{w\sim u}(a\oplus b)_{w},\quad a\in\mathcal{A}^{H^{\circ}},\ u\in H^{\circ}.

(4.10)

Moreover, define the clamped best-response operator
\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{H,b}_{\theta}:\mathcal{A}^{H^{\circ}}\to\mathcal{A}^{H^{\circ}}
by

\big(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{H,b}_{\theta}(a)\big)(u):=\argmax_{\tilde{a}\in\mathcal{A}_{u}}U\big(\tilde{a},\,z_{u}^{H,b}(a),\,\theta_{u}\big),\quad a\in\mathcal{A}^{H^{\circ}},\ u\in H^{\circ}.

(4.11)

Starting from
a^{H,b,(0)}\equiv 0
on
H^{\circ}
, define the clamped Picard iterates in
\mathcal{A}^{H^{\circ}}
by

a^{H,b,(k)}:=\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{H,b}_{\theta}\big(a^{H,b,(k-1)}\big),\quad k\geq 1.

Proposition 4.13
.

Suppose that Assumptions
4.1
,
4.2
, and
4.5
hold. Let
\bar{a}\in\mathcal{A}^{G}
be the unique Nash equilibrium of
\mathbb{G}(\mathcal{A}_{ad}^{G},U,\theta,G)
. Let
H\subset G
be an induced subgraph and assume that the boundary actions are known exactly, that is,
b=\bar{a}_{\partial H}\in\mathcal{A}^{\partial H}
is given. Then:

(i)

The operator
\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{H,b}_{\theta}
is a contraction on
\mathcal{A}^{H^{\circ}}
with contraction constant
\rho_{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny\eqref{eq:rho}}}<1
and hence admits a unique fixed point
a^{H,b,\ast}\in\mathcal{A}^{H^{\circ}}
.

(ii)

This fixed point coincides with the restriction of the global Nash equilibrium to the interior, that is,
a^{H,b,\ast}=\bar{a}_{H^{\circ}}
. In particular, if
v\in H^{\circ}
, then
\smash{a^{H,b,\ast}_{v}=\bar{a}_{v}}
.

(iii)

The clamped Picard iterates converge geometrically:

\sup_{u\in H^{\circ}}\big\|a^{H,b,(k)}_{u}-\bar{a}_{u}\big\|_{\mathcal{A}}\leq\rho_{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny\eqref{eq:rho}}}^{k}M_{\raisebox{-0.90417pt}{\tiny\eqref{eq:A_M}}},\quad k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}.

The proof of Proposition
4.13
is given in Section
6
.

4.5
Convergence

Next, given a sequence of finite graphs that converges in the local weak sense to a random rooted graph, we study the convergence of the associated Nash equilibria. The formulation of local weak convergence involves rooted graphs, however, their root is merely a bookkeeping device and does not endow the corresponding player with any special role in the game. Recall the notion of (marked) local weak convergence from Definition
2.6
and Remark
2.8
.

Convention 4.15

(Randomness of marked graphs)
.

Throughout the rest of this section, we allow both the underlying graphs and their vertex marks to be random.
We consider two sources of randomness:

1.

Graph randomness.
Let
\mathbb{P}^{\prime}
be a probability measure on the space of connected, countable, rooted graphs
\mathcal{G}_{\ast}
, equipped with the Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{G}_{\ast})
. A random graph
G\in\mathcal{G}_{\ast}
is then distributed according to
\mathbb{P}^{\prime}
.

2.

Vertex mark randomness.
Given
G
, each vertex
v\in G
carries a stochastic process
x^{v}\in\mathcal{A}
, where
\mathcal{A}
is the Hilbert space defined in (
3.1
). The family of processes
(x^{v})_{v\in G}
is sampled according to its conditional law given
G
.

The joint law of marked graphs
(G,(x^{v})_{v\in G})
is then denoted by the probability measure
\mathbb{Q}
on
(\mathcal{G}_{\ast}[\mathcal{A}],\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{G}_{\ast}[\mathcal{A}]))
, and is obtained by first sampling
G
according to
\mathbb{P}^{\prime}
and then sampling the vertex processes
(x^{v})_{v\in G}
according to their conditional laws given
G
.

Unless stated otherwise, in the following all statements below are understood in the quenched sense:
We fix a realization of the graph under
\mathbb{P}^{\prime}
and then the game is analyzed conditional on this realization. Accordingly, throughout the remainder of this section we treat graphs, the induced games,
and the associated Nash equilibria as random objects with respect to
\mathbb{P}^{\prime}
, and we do not always indicate this dependence explicitly in the notation for readability. In particular, the terms “random” and “deterministic” will henceforth refer to randomness with respect to
\mathbb{P}^{\prime}
.

Theorem 4.16
.

Consider
(G,\theta)\in\mathcal{G}_{\ast}[\mathcal{A}]
, that is, a random rooted graph
G\in\mathcal{G}_{\ast}
with marks
(\theta_{v})_{v\in G}\in\mathcal{A}^{G}
. Let
\mathbb{G}(\mathcal{A}_{ad}^{G},U,\theta,G)
be the associated game, and assume that
\mathcal{A}_{v}=\mathcal{A}_{0}
for some
\mathcal{A}_{0}\subset\mathcal{A}
and all
v\in G
. Suppose that Assumptions
4.1
,
4.2
, and
4.5
hold, and denote by
\bar{a}\in\mathcal{A}^{G}
the corresponding unique Nash equilibrium. Consider a sequence
\{G_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\subset\mathcal{G}
of finite graphs with marks
(\theta^{n}_{v})_{v\in G_{n}}\in\mathcal{A}^{G_{n}}
. For

, let
\bar{a}^{n}\in\mathcal{A}^{G_{n}}
be the unique Nash equilibrium of the game
\smash{\mathbb{G}(\mathcal{A}_{ad}^{G_{n}},U,\theta^{n},G_{n})}
with

for all

. Then,

(i)

Remark 4.18

(5.4)

\displaystyle\leq\rho\,\|\boldsymbol{G}\|_{\operatorname{op}}\,\|a-a^{\prime}\|_{\mathcal{A}^{G}}

\displaystyle\leq\rho\,\|a-a^{\prime}\|_{\mathcal{A}^{G}}.

Since

is a contraction on

\mathcal{A}^{G}
. By Banach’s fixed point theorem (see
[
5
]
, Chapter 1.12, Theorem 1.50), it admits a unique fixed point

\bar{a}\in\mathcal{A}^{G}
, which by construction lies in
\mathcal{A}_{ad}^{G}
and is therefore the unique Nash equilibrium.
∎

Proof of Proposition
4.6
.

Recalling the definition of the local aggregate operator
\boldsymbol{G}
from (

) and the best-response operator
\boldsymbol{B}_{\theta}
from (
5.1
), define the sequence of Picard iterates
\{a^{(k)}\}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}\subset\mathcal{A}^{G}
by
a^{(0)}\equiv 0
and

a^{(k)}:=\boldsymbol{B}_{\theta}\boldsymbol{G}a^{(k-1)},\quad k\geq 1.

(6.1)

Recall the notation for subgraphs introduced in Section
2.1
and before Proposition
4.6
. By assumption, the processes
(\theta_{v})_{v\in G}
are independent under
\mathbb{P}
and
\smash{\argmax_{\tilde{a}\in\mathcal{A}_{v}}U(\tilde{a},\tilde{z},\theta_{v})}
is
\sigma(\tilde{z},\theta_{v})
-measurable for all
\tilde{z}\in\mathcal{A}
and
v\in G
. For
i=1,2
, let
H_{i}\subset G
be finite induced subgraphs and let
f_{i}:\mathcal{L}^{H_{i}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be bounded and Lipschitz with respect to
d_{H_{i}}
(see (
4.3
)). Then, it follows inductively from the definition of the iterates (
6.1
) that, for every
k\geq 1
, the family of strategies
\smash{a^{(k)}_{H_{i}}}=\smash{(a^{(k)}_{v})_{v\in{H_{i}}}}
is measurable with respect to
\sigma(\theta_{u}:\ u\in B_{k-1}(H_{i}))
and independent of
\sigma(\theta_{u}:\ u\notin B_{k-1}(H_{i}))
. Consequently, if
2k-2<d_{G}(H_{1},H_{2})
, then
B_{k-1}(H_{1})\cap B_{k-1}(H_{2})=\emptyset
, and it follows that
\smash{a^{(k)}_{H_{1}}}
and
\smash{a^{(k)}_{H_{2}}}
are independent, hence

\mathrm{Cov}_{\mathbb{P}}\big(f_{1}(a^{(k)}_{H_{1}}),\,f_{2}(a^{(k)}_{H_{2}})\big)=0.

(6.2)

Next, recalling (
5.4
), it holds that

\|\boldsymbol{B}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{G}a)-\boldsymbol{B}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{G}a^{\prime})\|_{\mathcal{A}^{G}}\leq\rho\,\|a-a^{\prime}\|_{\mathcal{A}^{G}},

hence
\boldsymbol{B}_{\theta}\boldsymbol{G}
is a contraction on
\mathcal{A}^{G}
with constant
\rho<1
. Since
\bar{a}
is its unique fixed point,

\displaystyle\|a^{(k)}-\bar{a}\|_{\mathcal{A}^{G}}

\displaystyle=\|(\boldsymbol{B}_{\theta}\boldsymbol{G})^{k}a^{(0)}-(\boldsymbol{B}_{\theta}\boldsymbol{G})^{k}\bar{a}\|_{\mathcal{A}^{G}}

(6.3)

\displaystyle\leq\rho^{k}\,\|a^{(0)}-\bar{a}\|_{\mathcal{A}^{G}},

where
(\boldsymbol{B}_{\theta}\boldsymbol{G})^{k}
denotes the
k
-fold composition of the operator
\boldsymbol{B}_{\theta}\boldsymbol{G}
. By Assumption
4.5
and the fact that
a^{(0)}\equiv 0
it follows that

\|a^{(0)}-\bar{a}\|_{\mathcal{A}^{G}}\leq M
. Therefore,

\sup_{v\in G}\|a^{(k)}_{v}-\bar{a}_{v}\|_{\mathcal{A}}\leq M\rho^{k},\quad k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}.

(6.4)

In particular, recalling (
4.3
), by the linearity of expectation, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, and (
6.4
), it holds for any finite induced subgraph
H\subset G
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}\big[d_{H}(a^{(k)}_{H},\bar{a}_{H})\big]

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}\Big[\sum_{v\in H}\|a^{(k)}_{v}-\bar{a}_{v}\|_{L^{2}}\Big]

(6.5)

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in H}{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}\big[\|a^{(k)}_{v}-\bar{a}_{v}\|_{L^{2}}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{v\in H}\|a^{(k)}_{v}-\bar{a}_{v}\|_{\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle\leq|H|\big(\sup_{v\in H}\|a^{(k)}_{v}-\bar{a}_{v}\|_{\mathcal{A}}\big)

\displaystyle\leq|H|M\rho^{k}.

Now, fix finite induced subgraphs
H_{1},H_{2}\subset G
and choose
k:=\lceil d_{G}(H_{1},H_{2})/2\rceil
, so that
2k-2<d_{G}(H_{1},H_{2})
.
Set
\smash{\Delta_{i}:=f_{i}(\bar{a}_{H_{i}})-f_{i}(a^{(k)}_{H_{i}})}
for
i=1,2
. Then, by the linearity of covariance and (
6.2
),

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}_{\mathbb{P}}\big(f_{1}(\bar{a}_{H_{1}}),f_{2}(\bar{a}_{H_{2}})\big)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Cov}_{\mathbb{P}}\big(\Delta_{1},f_{2}(\bar{a}_{H_{2}})\big)+\mathrm{Cov}_{\mathbb{P}}\big(f_{1}(a^{(k)}_{H_{1}}),f_{2}(\bar{a}_{H_{2}})\big)

(6.6)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Cov}_{\mathbb{P}}\big(\Delta_{1},f_{2}(\bar{a}_{H_{2}})\big)+\mathrm{Cov}_{\mathbb{P}}\big(f_{1}(a^{(k)}_{H_{1}}),\Delta_{2}\big).

We thus obtain from (
6.6
) that

\Big|\mathrm{Cov}_{\mathbb{P}}\big(f_{1}(\bar{a}_{H_{1}}),f_{2}(\bar{a}_{H_{2}})\big)\Big|\leq 2\|f_{2}\|_{\infty}\,{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}[|\Delta_{1}|]+2\|f_{1}\|_{\infty}\,{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}[|\Delta_{2}|].

(6.7)

By the Lipschitz continuity of the
f_{i}
and (
6.5
), it holds that

{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}[|\Delta_{i}|]\leq{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}\big[\mathrm{Lip}(f_{i})\,d_{H_{i}}(\bar{a}_{H_{i}},a^{(k)}_{H_{i}})\big]\leq M\rho^{k}\,|H_{i}|\,\mathrm{Lip}(f_{i}),\quad i=1,2.

(6.8)

Finally, combining (
6.7
) and (
6.8
) and recalling (
4.4
) yields

\displaystyle\Big|\mathrm{Cov}_{\mathbb{P}}\big(f_{1}(\bar{a}_{H_{1}}),f_{2}(\bar{a}_{H_{2}})\big)\Big|

\displaystyle\leq 2M\,\rho^{k}\Big(\|f_{2}\|_{\infty}\,\mathrm{Lip}(f_{1})|H_{1}|+\|f_{1}\|_{\infty}\,\mathrm{Lip}(f_{2})|H_{2}|\Big)

(6.9)

\displaystyle\leq 2M\,\rho^{k}\,(|H_{1}|+|H_{2}|)\|f_{1}\|_{\text{BL}}\,\|f_{2}\|_{\text{BL}},

as claimed.
∎

The map

\displaystyle B:\mathcal{G}_{\ast}[\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}]

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{G}_{\ast}[\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}],

(7.5)

\displaystyle(G,(a_{v})_{v\in G},(\theta_{v})_{v\in G})

\displaystyle\mapsto\big(G,\big(\beta_{\theta_{v}}(\frac{\mathds{1}_{\{\deg_{G}(v)>0\}}}{\deg_{G}(v)}\sum_{u\sim v}a_{u})\big)_{v\in G},(\theta_{v})_{v\in G}\big),

is continuous with respect to the metric
d_{\ast}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
7.1
and
7.3
,
B
is given by a composition of continuous maps.
∎

The final ingredient we need for the proof of Theorem
4.16
is the following statement.

Lemma 7.5
.

Let
(\mathcal{X},d)
be a metric space. Let
\{X_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
and
X
be
\mathcal{X}
-valued random variables on a probability space
(\tilde{\Omega},\tilde{\mathcal{F}},\mu)
. For any
\varepsilon>0
, assume that there are
\mathcal{X}
-valued random variables
\{X^{\varepsilon}_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
and
X^{\varepsilon}
such that

1.

d(X_{n},X_{n}^{\varepsilon})\leq\varepsilon
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
d(X,X^{\varepsilon})\leq\varepsilon
,
\mu
-almost surely.

2.

(X_{n}^{\varepsilon})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
converges in distribution to
X^{\varepsilon}
.

Then
\{X_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
converges in distribution to
X
.

Proof.

Let
K\subset\mathcal{X}
be closed. For
\delta>0
define the closed sets

K^{\delta}:=\{x\in\mathcal{X}:\ d(x,K)\leq\delta\}.

Now
d(X_{n},X_{n}^{\varepsilon})\leq\varepsilon
implies
\{X_{n}\in K\}\subset\{X_{n}^{\varepsilon}\in K^{\varepsilon}\}
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and thus

\mu(X_{n}\in K)\leq\mu(X_{n}^{\varepsilon}\in K^{\varepsilon}),\quad n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

(7.6)

By assumption,
X_{n}^{\varepsilon}\Rightarrow X^{\varepsilon}
. Since
K^{\varepsilon}
is closed, the Portmanteau lemma yields

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mu(X_{n}^{\varepsilon}\in K^{\varepsilon})\leq\mu(X^{\varepsilon}\in K^{\varepsilon}).

(7.7)

Combining (
7.6
) and (
7.7
) gives

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mu(X_{n}\in K)\leq\mu(X^{\varepsilon}\in K^{\varepsilon}).

(7.8)

Next,
d(X,X^{\varepsilon})\leq\varepsilon
implies that
\{X^{\varepsilon}\in K^{\varepsilon}\}\subset\{X\in K^{2\varepsilon}\}
, and thus

\mu(X^{\varepsilon}\in K^{\varepsilon})\leq\mu(X\in K^{2\varepsilon}).

(7.9)

Combining (
7.8
) and (
7.9
) yields

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mu(X_{n}\in K)\leq\mu(X\in K^{2\varepsilon}).

(7.10)

Since
K
is closed, we have
K^{2\varepsilon}\downarrow K
as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
. By continuity
\mu
,

\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\mu(X\in K^{2\varepsilon})=\mu(X\in K).

Thus taking the limit over
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
in (
7.10
) we conclude that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mu(X_{n}\in K)\leq\mu(X\in K).

By the Portmanteau lemma,
X_{n}\Rightarrow X
, as claimed.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
4.16
. Note that we have only worked with local convergence of
\mathcal{A}
-marked rooted graphs (see Definition

) so far. Now we will also consider local weak convergence of
\mathcal{A}
-marked unrooted graphs (see Definition
2.6
and Remark

).

Proof of Theorem
4.16
.

Recall Convention
4.15
and suppose that the assumptions of Theorem
4.16
hold.
Given a random rooted graph
G\in\mathcal{G}_{\ast}
, let

(G,(a^{(0)}_{v})_{v\in G},(\theta_{v})_{v\in G})\in\mathcal{G}_{\ast}[\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}]

be defined by
a^{(0)}_{v}=0
for all
v\in G
. Similar to the proof of Proposition
4.6
, inductively define the Picard iterates in
\mathcal{G}_{\ast}[\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}]
given by

(G,(a^{(k)}_{v})_{v\in G},(\theta_{v})_{v\in G}):=B\big(G,(a^{(k-1)}_{v})_{v\in G},(\theta_{v})_{v\in G}\big),\quad k\geq 1,

(7.11)

where

is defined in (
7.5
). Recall that
\rho=\ell_{U}/\gamma_{U}
. Then, by Lemma
7.2
, it holds for all

\displaystyle\|a^{(k)}_{v}-\bar{a}_{v}\|_{\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle=\|\beta_{\theta_{v}}(\frac{\mathds{1}_{\{\deg_{G}(v)>0\}}}{\deg_{G}(v)}\sum_{u\sim v}a^{(k-1)}_{u})-\beta_{\theta_{v}}(\frac{\mathds{1}_{\{\deg_{G}(v)>0\}}}{\deg_{G}(v)}\sum_{u\sim v}\bar{a}_{u})\|_{\mathcal{A}}

(7.12)

\displaystyle\leq\rho\ \|\frac{\mathds{1}_{\{\deg_{G}(v)>0\}}}{\deg_{G}(v)}\sum_{u\sim v}(a^{(k-1)}_{u}-\bar{a}_{u})\|_{\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle\leq\rho\ \sup_{u\in G}\|a^{(k-1)}_{u}-\bar{a}_{u}\|_{\mathcal{A}},

and thus by iteration and Assumption
4.5
for all
v\in G
that

\displaystyle\|a^{(k)}_{v}-\bar{a}_{v}\|_{\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle\leq\rho^{k}\sup_{u\in G}\|a^{(0)}_{u}-\bar{a}_{u}\|_{\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle=\rho^{k}\sup_{u\in G}\|\bar{a}_{u}\|_{\mathcal{A}}

Now consider a sequence
\{G_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\subset\mathcal{G}
of finite graphs with marks
(\theta^{n}_{v})_{v\in G_{n}}\in\mathcal{A}^{G_{n}}

(G,\theta)\in\mathcal{G}_{\ast}[\mathcal{A}]
. For

\bar{a}^{n}\in\mathcal{A}^{G_{n}}
be the unique Nash equilibrium of the game
\smash{\mathbb{G}(\mathcal{A}_{ad}^{G_{n}},U,\theta^{n},G_{n})}

\mathcal{A}_{v}=\mathcal{A}_{0}
for all

\bar{a}\in\mathcal{A}^{G}

\smash{\mathbb{G}(\mathcal{A}_{ad}^{G},U,\theta,G)}

\mathcal{A}_{v}=\mathcal{A}_{0}

(i) First, assume that
\{(G_{n},\theta^{n})\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
converges in distribution in the local weak sense to
(G,\theta)
. By Remark
2.8
, this means that

{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\Big[\frac{1}{|G_{n}|}\sum_{v\in G_{n}}h\big(C_{v}(G_{n},\theta^{n})\big)\Big]\;\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\;}{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\big[h(G,\theta)\big],\quad\text{for all }h\in C_{b}\big(\mathcal{G}_{\ast}[\mathcal{A}]\big).

(7.14)

Let
\smash{a^{(0),n}_{v}=0}
for all

and

, and
a^{(0)}_{v}=0
for all
v\in G

denote the (continuous) inclusion adding a first coordinate equal to zero. Then setting

{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\Big[\frac{1}{|G_{n}|}\sum_{v\in G_{n}}\hat{h}\big(C_{v}(G_{n},a^{(0),n},\theta^{n})\big)\Big]\;\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\;}{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\big[\hat{h}(G,a^{(0)},\theta)\big],\quad\text{for all }\hat{h}\in C_{b}\big(\mathcal{G}_{\ast}[\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}]\big).

(7.15)

Recall that
B
is defined in (
7.5

, define the Picard iterates on

(G_{n},(a^{(k),n}_{v})_{v\in G_{n}},(\theta^{n}_{v})_{v\in G_{n}}):=B\big(G_{n},(a^{(k-1),n}_{v})_{v\in G_{n}},(\theta^{n}_{v})_{v\in G_{n}}\big),\quad k\geq 1.

(7.16)

Then, by Lemma
7.4
and (
7.11
), (
7.15
), (
7.16
), given
\hat{g}\in C_{b}\big(\mathcal{G}_{\ast}[\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}]\big)
and

, setting
\smash{\hat{h}:=\hat{g}\circ B^{k}}
in (

{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\Big[\frac{1}{|G_{n}|}\sum_{v\in G_{n}}\hat{g}\big(C_{v}(G_{n},a^{(k),n},\theta^{n})\big)\Big]\;\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\;}{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\big[\hat{g}(G,a^{(k)},\theta)\big],\quad\text{for all }\hat{g}\in C_{b}\big(\mathcal{G}_{\ast}[\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}]\big).

(7.17)

Now given
g\in C_{b}\big(\mathcal{G}_{\ast}[\mathcal{A}]\big)
, and letting

{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\Big[\frac{1}{|G_{n}|}\sum_{v\in G_{n}}g\big(C_{v}(G_{n},a^{(k),n})\big)\Big]\;\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\;}{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\big[g(G,a^{(k)})\big],\quad\text{for all }g\in C_{b}\big(\mathcal{G}_{\ast}[\mathcal{A}]\big),

(7.18)

i.e., the sequence of
k
-th Picard iterates
\{(G_{n},a^{(k),n})\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
corresponding to
(G_{n},\theta^{n})
converges in distribution in the local weak sense to the
k
-th Picard iterate
(G,a^{(k)})
corresponding to
(G,\theta)
. Equivalently, by Definition
2.6
, given an independent uniform
\mathbb{Q}
-random variable

on the vertex set of

\mathcal{G}_{\ast}[\mathcal{A}]

Now, recalling Assumptions
4.1
and
4.5
, given any

, choose

.
Then, using (

) applied to

and

), it holds that

d_{\ast}\big(C_{U}(G_{n},\bar{a}^{n}),C_{U}(G_{n},a^{(k),n})\big)\leq\varepsilon,\quad d_{\ast}\big((G,\bar{a}),(G,a^{(k)})\big)\leq\varepsilon,\quad\mathbb{Q}\text{-a.s.~for all }n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

(7.19)

Therefore, we can apply Lemma

\{C_{U}(G_{n},\bar{a}^{n})\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}

. This is equivalent to convergence of
\{(G_{n},\bar{a}^{n})\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
to
(G,\bar{a})
in distribution in the local weak sense, completing the proof of (i).

(ii) Second, assume that
\{(G_{n},\theta^{n})\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
converges in
\mathbb{Q}
-probability in the local weak sense to
(G,\theta)
. By Remark

, this means that

\frac{1}{|G_{n}|}\sum_{v\in G_{n}}h\big(C_{v}(G_{n},\theta^{n})\big)\;\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\ \text{prob.}\ }\;{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\big[h(G,\theta)\big],\quad\text{for all }h\in C_{b}(\mathcal{G}_{\ast}[\mathcal{A}]).

(7.20)

By the same line of argument as used in (
7.15
), (
7.17
), (
7.18
), we obtain for any
k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
that

\frac{1}{|G_{n}|}\sum_{v\in G_{n}}g\big(C_{v}(G_{n},a^{(k),n})\big)\;\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\ \text{prob.}\ }\;{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\big[g(G,a^{(k)})\big],\quad\text{for all }g\in C_{b}\big(\mathcal{G}_{\ast}[\mathcal{A}]\big),

(7.21)

i.e., the sequence of
k
-th Picard iterates
\{(G_{n},a^{(k),n})\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
corresponding to
(G_{n},\theta^{n})
converges in
\mathbb{Q}
-probability in the local weak sense to the
k
-th Picard iterate
(G,a^{(k)})
corresponding to
(G,\theta)
.

For
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, given
G_{n}
, let

,
U_{2}^{n}
be two independent random variables that are uniformly distributed on the vertex set of

and set
\smash{C_{i}^{(k),n}:=C_{U_{i}^{n}}(G_{n},a^{(k),n})}
for

. Then, recalling that

in
\mathbb{Q}
-probability, by Lemma 2.8 in
[

]
, (

) is equivalent to

{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}[f_{1}(C_{1}^{(k),n})f_{2}(C_{2}^{(k),n})]\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}[f_{1}(G,a^{(k)})]\ {\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}[f_{2}(G,a^{(k)})],\quad\text{for all }f_{1},f_{2}\in C_{b}(\mathcal{G}_{\ast}[\mathcal{A}]).

Since

is complete and separable, we may equivalently replace

in (

\operatorname{BL}_{1}(\mathcal{G}_{\ast}[\mathcal{A}]):=\{f:\mathcal{G}_{\ast}[\mathcal{A}]\to{\mathbb{R}}\mid\|f\|_{\text{BL}}\leq 1\},

where

Fix

Then, by the definition of

Moreover, by (

) applied to

, it holds that

d_{\ast}\big((G,\bar{a}),(G,a^{(k)})\big)\leq\varepsilon,\quad\mathbb{Q}\text{-a.s.}

Since

are Lipschitz with respect to

from (

) with

and
\operatorname{Lip}(f_{j})\leq 1

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\Big|{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\big[f_{1}(\bar{C}_{1}^{n})f_{2}(\bar{C}_{2}^{n})\big]-{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\big[f_{1}(C_{1}^{(k),n})f_{2}(C_{2}^{(k),n})\big]\Big|

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\Big[\big|f_{1}(\bar{C}_{1}^{n})-f_{1}(C_{1}^{(k),n})\big|\cdot|f_{2}(\bar{C}_{2}^{n})|\Big]+{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\Big[|f_{1}(C_{1}^{(k),n})|\cdot\big|f_{2}(\bar{C}_{2}^{n})-f_{2}(C_{2}^{(k),n})\big|\Big]

\displaystyle\Big|{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}[f_{1}(G,a^{(k)})]{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}[f_{2}(G,a^{(k)})]-{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}[f_{1}(G,\bar{a})]{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}[f_{2}(G,\bar{a})]\Big|

Again, by Lemma 2.8 in
[

]
and the equivalence between testing against

\frac{1}{|G_{n}|}\sum_{v\in G_{n}}g\big(C_{v}(G_{n},\bar{a}^{n})\big)\;\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\ \text{prob.}\ }\;{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\big[g(G,\bar{a})\big],\quad\text{for all }g\in C_{b}(\mathcal{G}_{\ast}[\mathcal{A}]),

\{(G_{n},\bar{a}^{n})\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
converges in

. This completes the proof of (ii), and hence of Theorem

.
∎

.

Proof of Corollary

.

Appendix A
Additional Results

The following result provides sufficient conditions on a filtered probability space ensuring that the associated Hilbert space of square-integrable, progressively measurable processes is separable. Related arguments appear in various forms in the literature, however, we could not locate a statement matching our framework exactly, so we include a self-contained proof for completeness. For simplicity, we omit the tilde in the notation for elements in
\mathcal{A}
from (
3.1
) from now on.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},{\mathbb{F}}:=(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]},\mathbb{P})
be a filtered probability space with left-continuous filtration such that
\mathcal{F}_{t}
is separable for every
t\in[0,T]
(in the sense of Definition
4.19
). Then the Hilbert space of square-integrable, progressively measurable processes

\mathcal{A}=\left\{a:\Omega\times[0,T]\to{\mathbb{R}}\,\Big|\,a\textrm{ is }\mathbb{F}\textrm{-prog.~measurable, }\,\|a\|^{2}_{\mathcal{A}}:={\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}\Big[\int_{0}^{T}a(t)^{2}dt\Big]<\infty\right\}

(A.1)

is separable.

Proof.

Let
\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}}
denote the norm on
L^{2}(\Omega\times[0,T],\mathcal{F}\otimes\mathcal{B}([0,T]),\mathbb{P}\otimes dt)
that agrees with
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{A}}
on
\mathcal{A}\subset L^{2}(\Omega\times[0,T])
. Fix
a\in\mathcal{A}
and
\varepsilon>0
. Define the bounded truncations
a^{m}\in\mathcal{A}
,

a^{m}:=(-m)\vee a\wedge m,\quad m\in{\mathbb{N}}.

(A.2)

Let
\{\pi_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
denote the sequence of dyadic partitions
\pi_{n}=(q_{j}^{n})_{j=0}^{2^{n}}
of
[0,T]
given by

0=q_{0}^{n}<q_{1}^{n}<\ldots<q_{2^{n}}^{n}=T,\quad q_{j}^{n}:=\frac{jT}{2^{n}}\text{ for }j=0,1,\ldots,2^{n}.

(A.3)

For
m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, define the corresponding step process

a^{m,n}_{t}:=\sum_{j=0}^{2^{n}-1}c^{m,n}_{j}\,\mathds{1}_{(q_{j}^{n},q_{j+1}^{n}]}(t),\quad c^{m,n}_{j}:={\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}\!\bigg[\frac{1}{q_{j+1}^{n}-q_{j}^{n}}\int_{q_{j}^{n}}^{q_{j+1}^{n}}a^{m}_{s}\,ds\ \Big|\ \mathcal{F}_{q_{j}^{n}}\bigg].

(A.4)

Then
c^{m,n}_{j}
is
\mathcal{F}_{q_{j}^{n}}
-measurable with
|c^{m,n}_{j}|\leq m
. Since
t\in(q_{j}^{n},q_{j+1}^{n}]

implies
\mathcal{F}_{q_{j}^{n}}\subset\mathcal{F}_{t}
, the process
a^{m,n}
is adapted, so being piecewise constant in
t
, it is
progressively measurable, hence
a^{m,n}\in\mathcal{A}
. Moreover, define
b^{m,n}\in L^{2}(\Omega\times[0,T])
by

b^{m,n}_{t}:=\sum_{j=0}^{2^{n}-1}\Big(\frac{1}{q_{j+1}^{n}-q_{j}^{n}}\int_{q_{j}^{n}}^{q_{j+1}^{n}}a^{m}_{s}\,ds\Big)\,\mathds{1}_{(q_{j}^{n},q_{j+1}^{n}]}(t),

(A.5)

so that
a^{m,n}_{t}={\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}[b^{m,n}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{q_{j}^{n}}]
for
t\in(q_{j}^{n},q_{j+1}^{n}]
.

Claim 1:
For fixed
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
b^{m,n}\to a^{m}
in
L^{2}
as
n\to\infty
.

Proof of Claim 1:

Indeed, recalling (
A.2
) and (
A.5
), for a.e.
\omega
, the path
t\mapsto a^{m}_{t}(\omega)
lies in
L^{2}([0,T])
and
b^{m,n}(\omega)
is the step function on
\pi_{n}
averaging over its subintervals. Therefore, (
A.3
) implies

\int_{0}^{T}|a^{m}_{t}(\omega)-b^{m,n}_{t}(\omega)|^{2}\,dt\to 0,\quad\text{for a.e.\ }\omega.

Moreover
|a^{m}-b^{m,n}|\leq 2m
, hence dominated convergence yields
\|a^{m}-b^{m,n}\|_{L^{2}}\to 0
.

Claim 2:
For all
m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

\|a^{m}-a^{m,n}\|_{\mathcal{A}}^{2}\leq 2\int_{0}^{T}{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}\Big[\,\big|a^{m}_{t}-{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}[a^{m}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{\underline{t}^{n}}]\big|^{2}\,\Big]\,dt\;+\;2\|a^{m}-b^{m,n}\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

(A.6)

where
\underline{t}^{n}:=\max\{q^{n}_{j}\in\pi_{n}:\ q^{n}_{j}<t\}
for
t\in(0,T]
, and
\underline{0}^{n}:=0
.

Proof of Claim 2:

Fix
t\in(0,T]
and let
j=j(t)
be such that
t\in(q^{n}_{j},q^{n}_{j+1}]
, so that
\underline{t}^{n}=q^{n}_{j}
.
By (
A.4
) and (
A.5
), we have

a^{m,n}_{t}={\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}[b^{m,n}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{q^{n}_{j}}]
.
Hence

a^{m}_{t}-a^{m,n}_{t}=\big(a^{m}_{t}-{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}[a^{m}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{q^{n}_{j}}]\big)+{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}[a^{m}_{t}-b^{m,n}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{q^{n}_{j}}].

Using
(x+y)^{2}\leq 2x^{2}+2y^{2}
and the conditional Jensen inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}\big[|a^{m}_{t}-a^{m,n}_{t}|^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq 2\,{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}\Big[\,\big|a^{m}_{t}-{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}[a^{m}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{q^{n}_{j}}]\big|^{2}\,\Big]+2\,{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}\Big[\,\big|{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}[a^{m}_{t}-b^{m,n}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{q^{n}_{j}}]\big|^{2}\,\Big]

\displaystyle\leq 2\,{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}\Big[\,\big|a^{m}_{t}-{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}[a^{m}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{q^{n}_{j}}]\big|^{2}\,\Big]+2\,{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}\big[|a^{m}_{t}-b^{m,n}_{t}|^{2}\big].

Integrating over
t\in[0,T]
and noting that
q^{n}_{j}=\underline{t}^{n}
on
(q^{n}_{j},q^{n}_{j+1}]
yields (
A.6
).

Claim 3:
Fix
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Then

\int_{0}^{T}{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}\Big[\,\big|a^{m}_{t}-{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}[a^{m}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{\underline{t}^{n}}]\big|^{2}\,\Big]\,dt\;\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\;0.

(A.7)

Proof of Claim 3:

Fix
t\in(0,T]
. Since
(\pi_{n})_{n}
is dyadic, the set
\{\underline{t}^{n}:n\in{\mathbb{N}}\}
is a subset of
[0,t)
with
\underline{t}^{n}\uparrow t
.
Hence, by monotonicity of the filtration,

\bigvee_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mathcal{F}_{\underline{t}^{n}}=\sigma\Big(\bigcup_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mathcal{F}_{\underline{t}^{n}}\Big)=\sigma\Big(\bigcup_{s<t}\mathcal{F}_{s}\Big)=\mathcal{F}_{t-}.

By left-continuity,
\mathcal{F}_{t-}=\mathcal{F}_{t}
.
Since
a^{m}
is progressively measurable,
a^{m}_{t}
is
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-measurable and
|a^{m}_{t}|\leq m
, hence
a^{m}_{t}\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
.
By Corollary 2.4 in Chapter II of
[
40
]
,

{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}[a^{m}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{\underline{t}^{n}}]\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{a.s.}{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}[a^{m}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}]=a^{m}_{t}.

Moreover, since
|a^{m}_{t}|\leq m
implies
|{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}[a^{m}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{\underline{t}^{n}}]|\leq m
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we have

\big|a^{m}_{t}-{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}[a^{m}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{\underline{t}^{n}}]\big|^{2}\leq 4m^{2},\quad t\in[0,T],\ n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

(A.8)

so that dominated convergence on
\Omega
implies

{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}\big[|a^{m}_{t}-{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}[a^{m}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{\underline{t}^{n}}]|^{2}\big]\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0,\quad t\in(0,T].

Moreover, by (
A.8
) we have

{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}\big[\,\big|a^{m}_{t}-{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}[a^{m}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{\underline{t}^{n}}]\big|^{2}\,\big]\leq 4m^{2},\quad t\in[0,T],\ n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

Hence dominated convergence on
[0,T]
implies (
A.7
).

By Claims 1–3, for each fixed
m
we have
\|a^{m}-a^{m,n}\|_{\mathcal{A}}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.
Choose
m
so large that
\|a-a^{m}\|_{\mathcal{A}}<\varepsilon/4
, and then choose
n
so large that

\|a^{m}-a^{m,n}\|_{\mathcal{A}}<\varepsilon/4
. Then, by the triangle inequality,

\|a-a^{m,n}\|_{\mathcal{A}}\leq\|a-a^{m}\|_{\mathcal{A}}+\|a^{m}-a^{m,n}\|_{\mathcal{A}}<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

(A.9)

Next, we need to approximate the random coefficients of the step processes in (
A.4
). For each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
j=0,1,\dots,2^{n}-1
, separability of
\mathcal{F}_{q_{j}^{n}}

implies that
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{F}_{q_{j}^{n}},\mathbb{P})
is separable, hence we may fix a countable dense subset

D_{j}^{n}\subset L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{F}_{q_{j}^{n}},\mathbb{P}).

Let
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathcal{A}
be the set of all dyadic step processes of the form

s_{t}=\sum_{j=0}^{2^{n}-1}\zeta_{j}\,\mathds{1}_{(q_{j}^{n},q_{j+1}^{n}]}(t),\quad\zeta_{j}\in D_{j}^{n},\ n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

Since for each fixed
n
the set
\prod_{j=0}^{2^{n}-1}D_{j}^{n}
is a finite product of countable sets (hence countable), and
\mathcal{S}
is a countable union over
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
of such sets, it follows that
\mathcal{S}
is countable. Finally, to show that
\mathcal{S}
is dense in
\mathcal{A}
, write
a^{m,n}
as in (
A.4
):

a^{m,n}_{t}=\sum_{j=0}^{2^{n}-1}c^{m,n}_{j}\,\mathds{1}_{(q_{j}^{n},q_{j+1}^{n}]}(t),\quad c^{m,n}_{j}\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{F}_{q_{j}^{n}},\mathbb{P}).

(A.10)

For each
j
, choose
\zeta_{j}\in D_{j}^{n}
such that

{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}\big[|c^{m,n}_{j}-\zeta_{j}|^{2}\big]<\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4T2^{n}}.

(A.11)

Define
s\in\mathcal{S}
by

s_{t}:=\sum_{j=0}^{2^{n}-1}\zeta_{j}\,\mathds{1}_{(q_{j}^{n},q_{j+1}^{n}]}(t).

(A.12)

Then by (
A.10
), (
A.11
), and (
A.12
),

\displaystyle\|a^{m,n}-s\|_{\mathcal{A}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{2^{n}-1}(q_{j+1}^{n}-q_{j}^{n})\,{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}\big[|c^{m,n}_{j}-\zeta_{j}|^{2}\big]

(A.13)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{2^{n}-1}T\cdot\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4T2^{n}}=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4},

so
\|a^{m,n}-s\|_{\mathcal{A}}<\varepsilon/2
. Combining this with (
A.9
) and applying the triangle inequality yields

\|a-s\|_{\mathcal{A}}\leq\|a-a^{m,n}\|_{\mathcal{A}}+\|a^{m,n}-s\|_{\mathcal{A}}<\varepsilon.

Therefore,
\mathcal{S}
is a countable dense subset of
\mathcal{A}
, so
\mathcal{A}
is separable.
∎
