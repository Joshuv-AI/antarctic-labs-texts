Evolution of CAR‐T Therapy: Functional Engineering, Manufacturing Innovation, and Future In Vivo Strategies
Authors: Yukimi Sakoda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTChimeric antigen receptor (CAR)‐T cell therapy has transformed the treatment of hematologic malignancies and established a new paradigm for engineered cellular immunotherapy. However, its broader clinical application remains constrained by complex autologous manufacturing, high cost, limited scalability, variable product quality, suboptimal efficacy in solid tumors, and treatment‐related toxicities. To address these challenges, recent advances in CAR‐T cell therapy can be viewed across four interconnected dimensions: functional engineering, manufacturing innovation, alternative cell sources, and in vivo CAR programming. Functional engineering includes cytokine‐armored, checkpoint‐resistant, multi‐targeting, and modular or switchable CAR platforms designed to enhance efficacy, persistence, and safety. In parallel, manufacturing innovations such as shortened culture protocols, automated closed systems, and preservation of less differentiated T‐cell states aim to improve product potency while reducing production time and complexity. Alternative cell sources, including allogeneic donor‐derived and induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)‐derived platforms, may further enable standardized, scalable, and potentially off‐the‐shelf therapies. Finally, in vivo CAR programming has emerged as a fourth dimension in which endogenous immune cells are directly engineered within patients using viral or non‐viral gene delivery systems. This review summarizes these interconnected advances and discusses how they may collectively shape the next generation of CAR‐based immune therapies.

Introduction

Over the past decade, chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)‐T cell therapy has emerged as one of the most successful forms of adoptive cellular immunotherapy, achieving unprecedented clinical responses in refractory hematologic malignancies [1, 2]. Autologous CD19‐targeted CAR‐T products have induced durable remissions in patients with relapsed or refractory B‐cell leukemia and lymphoma, while subsequent CAR‐based therapies have expanded therapeutic benefits to multiple myeloma [3]. These achievements have established genetically engineered T cells as a transformative therapeutic modality and accelerated efforts to broaden CAR‐T cell therapy to additional diseases and patient populations [2, 4, 5].

Despite these clinical successes, several major challenges continue to limit the broader impact of CAR‐T cell therapy. Although remarkable efficacy has been achieved in hematologic malignancies, translation to solid tumors is substantially more challenging due to limited trafficking, antigen heterogeneity, physical stromal barriers, and a highly immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [4, 6]. In addition, treatment‐related toxicities—including cytokine release syndrome (CRS), immune effector cell‐associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS), and on‐target off‐tumor effects—remain important clinical concerns [7].

Conventional CAR‐T cell therapy is also associated with substantial manufacturing and logistical constraints. Most approved CAR‐T products continue to rely predominantly on individualized autologous manufacturing workflows requiring leukapheresis, ex vivo T‐cell activation, gene transfer, cell expansion, quality testing, and subsequent infusion [8]. This multi‐step process is labor‐intensive, costly, and often requires several weeks, during which patients with aggressive disease may clinically deteriorate. Moreover, prolonged ex vivo culture can promote T cell differentiation and exhaustion, thereby compromising proliferative capacity, persistence, and antitumor efficacy after infusion.

To provide an integrated perspective on these developments, we consider the evolution of CAR‐T cell therapy across four interconnected dimensions (Figure 1). The first is functional engineering, in which CAR design and cellular programming are optimized to enhance efficacy, persistence, and safety. Representative strategies include cytokine‐armored CAR‐T cells, checkpoint‐resistant constructs, multi‐targeting strategies, and modular or switchable systems designed to overcome tumor escape and immune suppression [4]. The second is manufacturing innovation, which seeks to preserve favorable T‐cell states while improving production efficiency and accessibility through approaches such as shortened culture protocols, automated closed systems, and decentralized manufacturing. The third is the expansion of alternative cell sources, including allogeneic donor‐derived and induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)‐derived platforms, which aim to enable more standardized, scalable, and potentially off‐the‐shelf CAR‐based therapies [5, 8]. Recently, in vivo CAR programming has emerged as a distinct fourth dimension, shifting genetic engineering from ex vivo cell processing to the direct programming of endogenous immune cells within patients through targeted gene delivery systems [9]. These dimensions address different barriers and may evolve along distinct but intersecting paths, with selected principles developed ex vivo also informing emerging in vivo strategies.

Together, these dimensions represent distinct but interacting paths: functional engineering can be combined with manufacturing innovation and alternative cell sources in ex vivo products, while selected design and safety principles for ex vivo CAR‐T may also inform in vivo programming. A distinctive feature of this review would be its comprehensive and integrated examination of the evolution of ex vivo CAR‐T technologies and the emerging development of in vivo CAR programming through these four‐dimensional perspectives.

Functional and Manufacturing Evolution of Ex Vivo CAR‐T Cell Therapy

Cytokine‐Armored CAR‐T Cells: From Concept to Clinical Translation

Although conventional CAR‐T cell therapy has transformed outcomes in hematologic malignancies, limited persistence, dysfunction under chronic antigen exposure, and immunosuppressive tumor microenvironments continue to constrain efficacy, particularly in solid tumors [6]. Armored CAR‐T cells seek to overcome these barriers by incorporating immunomodulatory payloads that enhance intrinsic T‐cell fitness or reshape the surrounding immune milieu. Among these approaches, cytokine armoring is one of the most clinically advanced, with the potential to promote CAR‐T‐cell persistence, recruit endogenous immune cells, and remodel the tumor microenvironment [10]. This subsection focuses on the clinical translation and design trade‐offs of cytokine‐armored CAR‐T cells.

IL‐12 provided an early proof of concept for cytokine armoring. Preclinical studies showed that IL‐12‐secreting CAR‐T cells enhanced cytotoxicity, activated innate immune cells, and remodeled suppressive tumor microenvironments, supporting the use of CAR‐T cells as localized cytokine‐delivery vehicles [11]. However, clinical experience with NFAT‐inducible IL‐12‐engineered tumor‐infiltrating lymphocytes revealed substantial systemic toxicity, indicating that activation‐responsive expression alone may not adequately restrict cytokine exposure [12]. Current IL‐12 engineering therefore emphasizes tighter spatial or activation‐dependent control, including antigen‐inducible and membrane‐anchored formats [10, 13].

IL‐15, a homeostatic cytokine supporting memory CD8+ T cells and NK cells, is among the most clinically advanced payloads for enhancing CAR‐T‐cell survival and proliferation [14]. Tethered IL‐15 prolonged persistence and enriched stem cell memory‐like subsets in preclinical models [15]. In Phase I studies involving 12 patients with R/R GPC3‐positive solid tumors, IL‐15‐armored GPC3‐targeted CAR‐T cells showed greater in vivo expansion than non‐armored counterparts and achieved an ORR of 33% and a DCR of 66% [16]. However, CRS requiring immunomodulatory treatment was more frequent than with non‐armored CAR‐T cells (relative risk, 3.3); iC9 activation rapidly improved symptoms in three patients with prolonged or severe CRS [16]. Together, these findings illustrate the activity–toxicity trade‐off of IL‐15 armoring and the potential value of built‐in safety mechanisms.

Modeled on T‐cell zones of secondary lymphoid tissues, where fibroblastic reticular cells co‐produce IL‐7 and CCL19, IL‐7/CCL19 armoring combines IL‐7‐mediated support of transferred CAR‐T‐cell survival with CCL19‐mediated recruitment of endogenous T cells and dendritic cells into tumors [17, 18]. This approach, also termed PRIME (Proliferation‐Inducing and Migration‐Enhancing) CAR‐T cells (Figure 2), induced complete regression of established tumors with marked endogenous immune‐cell infiltration in our initial immunocompetent syngeneic models [17]. Subsequent studies from our group demonstrated epitope spreading in syngeneic models [19] and antitumor activity in orthotopic, patient‐derived xenograft, and patient‐derived tumor organoid models across multiple solid tumors [20, 21].

Antitumor mechanisms of PRIME CAR‐T cells (IL‐7/CCL19 armored CAR‐T cells). Compared with conventional CAR‐T cells, PRIME CAR‐T cells facilitate accumulation of endogenous T cells and dendritic cells, thereby enhancing endogenous antitumor immunity and overcoming the immunosuppressive nature of the tumor microenvironment. Independent groups have also evaluated this approach in early clinical studies. In a multicenter Phase I and expansion‐phase study involving 39 patients with R/R large B‐cell lymphoma, CD19‐targeted CAR‐T cells with inducible IL‐7/CCL19 expression achieved a 3‐month ORR of 79.5%; Grade 3 CRS and Grade ≥ 3 ICANS occurred in 12.8% and 10.3% of patients, respectively [22]. In a preliminary 6‐patient cohort with GPC3‐ or mesothelin‐positive solid tumors, one CR, one PR, and two cases of SD were reported, with no Grade 2–4 adverse events or major complications [23]. These findings provide initial clinical evidence for IL‐7/CCL19‐based engineering across hematologic and solid tumors, although further investigation is necessary.

Other cytokine‐ and chemokine‐based strategies have also entered clinical evaluation. CLDN18.2‐targeted CAR‐T cells co‐expressing IL‐7 and XCL1 to support T‐cell persistence and recruit XCR1+ dendritic cells achieved an ORR of 20% and a DCR of 80% in 10 evaluable patients with advanced digestive tract cancers [24]. IL‐18 armoring can engage innate and adaptive immunity, broaden bystander antitumor responses, and remodel suppressive myeloid compartments [25, 26, 27]. In patients with B‐cell lymphoma after prior CD19 CAR‐T‐cell failure, huCART19‐IL18 achieved a 3‐month ORR of 81% and a complete response rate of 52% [28]; in the Phase I CLEAR‐AML trial, CD371‐targeted IL‐18‐secreting CAR‐T cells showed in vivo expansion and antileukemic activity in heavily pretreated patients with R/R acute myeloid leukemia (Table 1) [29]. Together, these approaches extend cytokine armoring beyond support of the infused CAR‐T cells themselves by seeking to engage endogenous immunity, a strategy that may help address tumor heterogeneity [10].

Beyond the strategies with emerging clinical evidence, additional approaches involving synthetic IL‐9 signaling, IL‐21/CXCL9 co‐expression, or IL‐23 have enhanced CAR‐T‐cell function in preclinical studies, further broadening the range of cytokine payloads under investigation [30, 31, 32].

Collectively, the available evidence highlights a central trade‐off of cytokine armoring: enhanced CAR‐T‐cell expansion, persistence, and immune activation inside tumor tissues may improve antitumor activity but can also increase systemic inflammatory toxicity. The therapeutic window is likely to depend on both the cytokine selected and its mode of expression, underscoring the importance of inducible or context‐dependent payload expression and complementary safety mechanisms.

Manufacturing Innovation as a Determinant of CAR‐T Cell Quality and Accessibility

Beyond CAR design, manufacturing is an important determinant of CAR‐T cell phenotype and function. Conventional autologous production involves leukapheresis, ex vivo engineering and expansion, and centralized workflows, with vein‐to‐vein times often extending over several weeks [33]. Prolonged culture can reduce stem‐like properties and promote dysfunctional or exhausted phenotypes, potentially limiting in vivo expansion and persistence [34]. To limit culture‐induced differentiation and preserve T‐cell fitness, current manufacturing strategies include enrichment of naïve, central memory, or stem cell memory subsets, abbreviated culture, IL‐7/IL‐15–based cytokine support, and modulation of differentiation‐associated pathways such as PI3K/AKT signaling [33, 34, 35, 36, 37].

Among these approaches, clinically evaluated accelerated manufacturing platforms include Novartis' T‐Charge (YTB323 and PHE885), Bristol Myers Squibb's NEX‐T (BMS‐986354), and Gracell/AstraZeneca's FasTCAR (GC007F and GC012F/AZD0120) (Table 2). These platforms shorten manufacturing to < 2 days, 5–6 days, and approximately 1 day, respectively, while limiting T‐cell differentiation. YTB323 and BMS‐986354, which use the same CAR constructs as the conventionally manufactured counterparts—tisagenlecleucel and the investigational orvacabtagene autoleucel, respectively—preserved naïve/stem‐like or central memory phenotypes and achieved comparable in vivo exposure at approximately 25‐fold and 10‐fold lower infused cell doses [38, 43]. Early clinical studies reported activity across disease settings, with durable responses to YTB323 in R/R DLBCL; high response rates and in vivo expansion or persistence with BMS‐986354 and PHE885 in R/R multiple myeloma; sustained disease control with GC012F/AZD0120 in high‐risk NDMM at a median follow‐up of 32.3 months; and rapid MRD‐negative remissions with GC007F in R/R B‐ALL [38, 39, 40, 41, 42, 43]. However, Grade 3 CRS and Grade 3–4 ICANS occurred in 24% and 28% of patients, respectively, with GC007F [41]. Although cross‐study comparisons are limited, these findings support the clinical feasibility of accelerated manufacturing while indicating that product‐ and indication‐specific dose and safety assessment remain important; mechanisms permitting rapid control of CAR‐T‐cell activity may also merit consideration for products with high proliferative capacity [8, 44].

Abbreviated manufacturing has also begun to converge with functional armoring. The IL‐18‐secreting huCART19‐IL18, discussed in Section 2.1, was produced using a 3‐day manufacturing process [28]. In a distinct preclinical approach, our recent study integrated IL‐7/CCL19 armoring with an approximately 4‐day manufacturing process that omitted the post‐transduction expansion phase, preserving memory‐associated phenotypes, maintaining function after repeated antigen stimulation, and mediating antitumor activity at low cell doses in solid‐tumor models [45]. These examples illustrate the convergence of abbreviated manufacturing and armoring, for which payload activity and, where appropriate, regulated or context‐dependent payload expression warrant consideration.

In parallel, automation is advancing through closed all‐in‐one platforms and interoperable modular workflows that reduce hands‐on processing and variability and may support both centralized high‐throughput and decentralized point‐of‐care production. Validation of automated workflows, adaptability to different products and processes, and consistent quality control across manufacturing sites remain important implementation challenges [46, 47].

Together, these developments highlight manufacturing as an integral component of CAR‐T design, influencing product fitness, scalability, and accessibility.

Expansion of CAR‐T Cell Sources: Allogeneic and iPSC‐Derived Platforms

To overcome limitations associated with autologous CAR‐T manufacturing, standardized off‐the‐shelf platforms using healthy donor‐derived allogeneic CAR‐T cells have been developed and advanced into clinical‐stage investigational products [5]. However, major challenges associated with these approaches include graft‐versus‐host disease (GVHD) and host‐versus‐graft rejection. Genome editing therefore serves primarily as an enabling tool to establish allogeneic compatibility through disruption of endogenous T‐cell receptor (TCR) and modification of HLA‐associated pathways [48, 49]. More recent approaches increasingly incorporate immune‐evasive engineering to improve persistence and resistance to host immunity, although extensive multiplex editing raises concerns regarding genomic integrity and manufacturing robustness [5, 50, 51].

Clinical studies of donor‐derived platforms, including cema‐cel, CTX110, and ALLO‐715, have demonstrated antitumor activity in B‐cell malignancies and multiple myeloma, with cema‐cel advancing into pivotal Phase II evaluation (Table 3) [52, 53, 54]. Limited persistence, host rejection, infectious complications, and dependence on lymphodepletion nevertheless remain important challenges. Repeat dosing has been clinically explored, with a two‐infusion regimen of CTX110 showing a signal of improved response durability, although whether repeated administration can compensate for limited in vivo persistence remains uncertain.

In contrast, induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) provide a renewable, clonally defined master cell source that can be extensively engineered before immune‐cell differentiation [51, 59]. FT819 has progressed from initial evaluation in B‐cell malignancies to clinical development in autoimmune diseases, including Phase II evaluation in refractory lupus nephritis [55, 56, 57], while the iPSC‐derived CAR‐NK product FT596 has also provided clinical proof‐of‐concept in B‐cell lymphoma (Table 3) [58]. Healthy donor‐derived CAR‐T cells retain mature T‐cell biology and established effector and memory programs but remain affected by donor variability, host rejection, and the complexity of editing mature cells. In contrast, iPSC‐derived platforms offer greater control over clonal selection, product standardization, and complex engineering, but face distinct challenges in differentiation, genomic stability, tumorigenicity, and generation of durable functional memory [51].

Across both donor‐derived and iPSC‐derived platforms, next‐generation engineering is extending beyond basic allogeneic compatibility toward functional enhancement. Clinical‐stage donor‐derived examples include Regnase‐1/TGFBR2 disruption in zugo‐cel and PD‐1 disruption in vispa‐cel, designed to enhance cellular fitness and resistance to immunosuppressive signals [60, 61]. The clonal nature of iPSC platforms further facilitates increasingly complex designs, including immune‐evasive, cytokine‐supporting, and multi‐antigen targeting strategies. In preclinical studies from the authors' group, IL‐7/CCL19‐armored allogeneic CAR‐T cells promoted epitope spreading and enhanced antitumor activity despite rapid donor‐cell clearance, suggesting a potential role for immune‐recruiting armoring in maintaining antitumor immunity despite limited allogeneic CAR‐T persistence [19]. Whether these functional enhancements translate into clinical benefit without compromising long‐term safety remains to be established.

Collectively, donor‐derived and iPSC‐derived platforms represent distinct strategies for achieving off‐the‐shelf CAR therapy: the former builds on mature T cell biology, whereas the latter emphasizes clonal standardization and programmable engineering. The relative importance of persistence may also depend on the therapeutic setting: sustained cellular activity may be more important for durable tumor control, whereas transient but effective depletion of pathogenic B‐cell populations may be sufficient to induce immune resetting in some autoimmune diseases. These disease‐dependent requirements between persistence and controllability are also relevant to the emerging in vivo CAR programming strategies discussed below.

Toward In Vivo CAR Programming: Direct Engineering of Immune Cells in Patients

Following advances in manufacturing innovation and off‐the‐shelf cellular platforms, in vivo CAR programming has emerged as a strategy to engineer endogenous immune cells directly in patients. Current clinical approaches are based mainly on targeted integrating lentiviral vectors and non‐viral mRNA–lipid nanoparticle (LNP) systems.

Lentiviral vectors can support stable gene transfer and sustained CAR expression but have limited reversibility and require consideration of genomic integration. In contrast, mRNA‐LNPs provide temporally restricted expression without genomic integration [9]. These properties frame distinct questions regarding target‐cell selectivity, vector immunogenicity, durability of effect, and dosing strategy. Targeted lentiviral vectors are designed to generate expanding, potentially persistent CAR‐T cells through stable gene transfer. In the Phase I ESO‐T01 study, four of five patients with multiple myeloma responded, including three stringent complete responses, at a median follow‐up of 6.0 months [64]. However, Grade ≥ 3 adverse events occurred in all five patients, Grade 3 cytokine release syndrome (CRS) in three, and one patient died following disease‐related spinal cord compression. The study was stopped early in 2025. Conference‐reported Phase I data for KLN‐1010 included a larger cohort of 18 treated patients, with responses reported in all evaluable patients; CRS was limited to Grade 1–2 and ICANS occurred in two patients, including one Grade 3 event, although median follow‐up was only 2.8 months [65]. JY231 extends the lentiviral evidence to autoimmune disease: in 16 patients with refractory neurologic autoimmune disorders, in vivo CAR‐T‐cell expansion was accompanied by complete B‐cell depletion and preliminary clinical improvement [62]. Collectively, these studies support the feasibility of lentiviral in vivo CAR‐T programming. However, as the currently available evidence is limited to early‐phase clinical trials, larger patient cohorts and longer follow‐up will be required to establish the durability of clinical benefit and long‐term safety.

For mRNA‐LNP platforms, clinical evidence also remains at an earlier stage and centers on pharmacodynamic evidence of rapid, transient programming. Repeat dosing has been incorporated into several early studies to renew CAR expression (Table 4). In B‐cell malignancies, GT801 and WGb‐0301 generated CAR‐expressing T cells within hours of administration and showed early signs of antitumor activity. However, response data remain limited to Week‐4 assessments of three patients in GT801 and three out of seven evaluable patients in WGb‐0301 [67, 68]. In five patients with refractory systemic lupus erythematosus, HN2301 generated CD19 CAR‐T cells through mRNA‐LNP delivery and rapidly reduced circulating B cells; disease activity was lower at the 3‐month assessment [69]. In solid tumors, early clinical outcomes have been reported with MT‐302, which programs TROP2‐directed CAR myeloid cells in vivo. Among 18 patients with target‐lesion assessments, one confirmed partial response and seven cases of stable disease were reported [70]. CAR+ myeloid cells were detected within tumors together with increased T‐cell infiltration [70], providing early evidence of intratumoral immune programming, although broader clinical efficacy remains to be established.

The distinct expression kinetics of these platforms may also affect the timing of inflammatory toxicity. In the ESO‐T01 study, lentiviral administration was associated with a biphasic inflammatory response, comprising an acute phase within 24 h of vector infusion and a later phase coinciding with CAR‐T‐cell expansion [64], In contrast, mRNA‐LNP platforms generate CAR‐expressing T cells within hours of administration [67, 68, 69]. Severe adverse events have also been reported with mRNA‐LNP approaches, including Grade ≥ 3 CRS with GT801 [67] and Grade 4 ICANS at the highest MT‐302 dose [70]. Detailed time‐to‐onset data remain limited, precluding conclusions about platform‐specific toxicity patterns.

Target‐cell selectivity and biodistribution remain significant challenges of in vivo CAR‐T: intravenously administered LNPs can accumulate in the liver, whereas lentiviral vectors need to use retargeted envelopes to reduce transduction of non‐T cells [9]. Integrating vectors may sustain CAR expression, but integration in unintended cells raises long‐term safety concerns, and anti‐vector immunity may limit delivery or redosing. Conversely, transient mRNA‐LNP expression may require repeat dosing, which could be constrained by immune responses to the delivery vehicle; whether either approach yields durable clinical benefit remains uncertain [9]. Looking forward, in vivo CAR programming may increasingly incorporate functional engineering strategies developed through ex vivo CAR‐T research. Multi‐targeting has already entered clinical evaluation with in vivo dual‐target CAR approaches such as LB2501 [66], while cytokine armoring, regulated signaling, and other context‐dependent programs may represent further directions. Such advances may be particularly important in solid tumors, where in vivo CAR programming must address not only efficient immune‐cell engineering but also antigen heterogeneity, limited tumor infiltration, and the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [71, 72].

Conclusion and Future Perspectives

Although conventional autologous CAR‐T cell therapy has demonstrated transformative clinical efficacy, its broader application remains constrained by manufacturing complexity, cost, treatment delays, and limited efficacy in solid tumors. Advances across four interconnected dimensions—functional engineering, manufacturing innovation, alternative cell sources, and in vivo CAR programming—are collectively expanding the scope and potential of CAR‐based therapies. Their continued convergence, together with advances in genome editing and biomaterials science, may transform CAR‐based therapy from a resource‐intensive, bespoke cellular intervention into a more scalable and programmable therapeutic platform applicable across cancer and autoimmune diseases.
