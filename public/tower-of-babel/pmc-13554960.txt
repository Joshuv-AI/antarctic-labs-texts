Population Pharmacokinetic Analysis of Brensocatib in Adult and Adolescent Patients With Non‐Cystic Fibrosis Bronchiectasis
Authors: Helen Usansky, Anthony Cammarata, ChongHua Li, Sam Au Yeung, Christopher M Rubino, Lin Wang, Ariel Teper, Carlos Fernandez, Kevin Mange
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBrensocatib, a competitive and reversible oral DPP1 inhibitor, at 10 and 25 mg significantly reduced pulmonary exacerbation burden, and 25 mg also reduced lung function decline vs. placebo in patients with non‐cystic fibrosis bronchiectasis, hereafter bronchiectasis, in ASPEN (NCT04594369). A population pharmacokinetic (PPK) model was developed to support brensocatib dose selection for adults and adolescents. Data from 11 clinical trials were pooled for analysis. The covariate model was developed via a stepwise approach. Clinical relevance of statistically significant covariates was assessed by simulated brensocatib exposure at steady state in various subgroups with different characteristics relative to a reference subject (healthy White male, 49 years, 70 kg, fasted, no concomitant medications). Covariate effects on brensocatib PK were evaluated. From 291 healthy participants and 807 patients, 11,643 brensocatib plasma concentrations were used for PPK model construction. Body weight was integrated using standard allometric scaling coefficients of 0.75 on total and intercompartment clearance (CL/F and Q/F) and 1.0 on central and peripheral volume of distribution (Vc/F and Vp/F). Statistically significant covariates included hepatic impairment, sex, race, and co‐medication use (strong CYP3A and Pgp modulators) on CL/F; age on Vc/F; food effect on Ka; and co‐medications (strong CYP3A and Pgp inhibitors) on relative bioavailability. Disease populations, renal impairment, and tablet formulations were not statistically significant covariates. The PPK model provided a robust fit to observed brensocatib concentration time profiles in bronchiectasis patients. No identified covariates were clinically relevant. This analysis supported brensocatib dosage of 10 and 25 mg in patients with bronchiectasis ≥ 12 years without dose adjustment.Keywords: clinical trials, covariates, population pharmacokinetics

Introduction

Non‐cystic fibrosis bronchiectasis, hereafter bronchiectasis, is a chronic and progressive inflammatory lung disease characterized by localized, irreversible dilatation of bronchi and bronchioles, which may lead to obstructed breathing caused by abnormal mucus production. The severe pulmonary dysfunction and airway wall thickening associated with bronchiectasis are due to chronic inflammation and/or infection [1].

Neutrophil‐mediated inflammation is central to the self‐perpetuating cycle of inflammation, impaired mucociliary clearance, structural airway damage, and recurrent infection, collectively known as the vicious vortex, that promotes progression of bronchiectasis [2]. Chronic airway inflammation in bronchiectasis is characterized by excessive release of neutrophil serine proteases (NSPs), which overwhelm endogenous protease inhibitors [3, 4]. NSP precursors are converted to functional enzymes during neutrophil maturation in the bone marrow by dipeptidyl peptidase 1 (DPP1) and are released upon neutrophil activation [5].

Brensocatib is a competitive and reversible inhibitor of DPP1 [6]. In healthy participants, patients with bronchiectasis, and patients with cystic fibrosis, once‐daily dosing of brensocatib at 10 to 40 mg resulted in dose‐ and exposure‐dependent reduction of the NSPs neutrophil elastase, cathepsin G, and proteinase 3 [5, 7, 8]. Brensocatib was approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration for the treatment of non‐cystic fibrosis bronchiectasis in patients aged 12 years or older (10‐ and 25‐mg once‐daily tablets) and by the European Medicines Agency and the Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency for the treatment of bronchiectasis in patients aged 12 years or older who have had 2 or more exacerbations in the past 12 months (25‐mg once‐daily tablets). The approval of brensocatib was based on the efficacy and safety demonstrated in the WILLOW (NCT03218917) and ASPEN (NCT04594369) studies [9, 10, 11, 12, 13].

Safety, tolerability, and pharmacokinetics (PK) of brensocatib were investigated in several studies (Phase 1) covering a single dose with a range of 5–120 mg and 10–40 mg after 28 days of once‐daily dosing [5, 8, 14]. In these studies, brensocatib exposure was dose dependent with approximately 2‐fold accumulation at steady state and low to moderate intersubject variability after single or multiple daily dosing. The elimination half‐life (t ½) of brensocatib was approximately 20–30 h, with approximately 20% urinary excretion at steady state [5, 14, 15]. Brensocatib was moderately metabolized following a single dose of radiolabeled brensocatib in healthy participants. Clinical data showed that brensocatib exposure is not significantly altered when coadministered with strong CYP3A or P‐glycoprotein (Pgp) inhibitors or with strong CYP3A inducers (Data on file) despite being a substrate of CYP3A4 and CYP3A5, Pgp, and breast cancer resistance protein (BCRP) [14, 16]. Participants with mild, moderate, and severe renal or hepatic impairment showed no clinically meaningful alterations in brensocatib elimination and systemic exposure, suggesting that dose adjustment of brensocatib is not necessary in participants with various degrees of renal or hepatic impairment [15, 17]. In these Phase 1 studies, brensocatib was well tolerated with any treatment‐emergent adverse events resolved by the end of the study [5, 14, 15, 17].

In a previous population pharmacokinetic (PPK) model based on limited PK data from fewer participants, results showed that age and renal function had a mild effect on brensocatib PK (~20%) and were not considered clinically meaningful [7]. The objectives of this analysis were to develop a PPK model using the PK data collected from Phase 1, Phase 2, and Phase 3 studies to characterize the absorption and disposition kinetics of brensocatib and to identify statistically significant intrinsic and extrinsic factors that could affect the PK of brensocatib. The results from this evaluation were used to support the dose‐adjustment rationale for brensocatib to treat patients with bronchiectasis.

Methods

Participants, Studies, and PPK Data Set

The PPK data set comprised brensocatib concentrations from 11 clinical trials (eight Phase 1 trials [NCT05927597, NCT05673603, NCT05355935, NCT05517525, NCT05826574, NCT05965570, NCT02303574, and NCT02653872], two Phase 2 trials [NCT05090904 and WILLOW, NCT03218917], and one Phase 3 trial [ASPEN, NCT04594369]) with dose ranges at 5–120 mg for single dose and 10 to 40 mg for once‐daily dosing. The PK samples were collected with intensive sampling schemes from the Phase 1 studies and sparse sampling schemes from the Phase 2 and Phase 3 studies. A total of 11,643 brensocatib plasma concentrations collected from 783 bronchiectasis patients (including 31 adolescents aged 12 to < 18 years), 24 patients with cystic fibrosis, and 291 healthy volunteers were included in the PPK data set (Figure S1).

Bioanalytical Methods for PK Plasma Samples

The plasma concentrations of brensocatib were analyzed using two validated liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) methods: One method had a quantitation range of 0.250–150 ng/mL, and the second method had a quantitation range of 0.841–841 ng/mL. Data from a cross validation showed that the 2 methods produced consistent concentration results, and the variability of the results was within acceptable range per applicable regulatory guidance [18].

PPK Analysis and Data Handling

The PPK analysis was conducted using NONMEM version 7.4.4 (ICON Development Solutions, Ellicott City, MD) implementing the first‐order conditional estimation method with η‐ε interaction. Additional software such as SAS v9.4 and R v4.0.4 was used for data handling, statistical analysis, and graphing.

Below limit of quantification concentration values were set as missing in the dataset and ignored by the analysis program. Concentration data without dosing or sampling times, without required covariate information (e.g., demographic and laboratory) or those determined as outliers were excluded from the analysis. An outlier was defined as an aberrant observation that substantially deviated from the rest of the observations within an individual or across all study subjects. Suspected outlier plasma concentration observations were tested and, if justified, excluded. The previously developed PPK models for brensocatib [7] were used to inform structural model development for this PPK model and were refined using the full pooled dataset.

The covariate model was developed via a stepwise approach. The covariates assessed included age, body weight, height, body surface area, ideal body weight, body mass index, albumin, creatinine clearance (CrCl), dose, renal/hepatic impairment, formulation, food effects, CYP3A inhibitor/inducer, Pgp inhibitor, sex, race, country of origin, and population/study phase. CrCl was determined using the Cockcroft and Gault equation [19] and then normalized to body surface area [20]. The covariates of renal or hepatic impairment were applied to participants with mild, moderate, and severe impairment in the brensocatib renal impairment [17] and hepatic impairment [15] studies. After testing for potentially significant covariates, the resultant model was deemed the final PPK model and was subjected to model qualification procedures. Allometric scaling using body weight was evaluated, including estimation of the allometric exponents for clearance and distribution parameters. However, estimation of these exponents resulted in a substantial worsening of model fit (ΔOFV = +2348) and model instability; therefore, standard theoretical exponents (0.75 for CL/F and Q; 1 for Vc/F and Vp/F) were applied in the final model.

The Bayesian post hoc PK parameter estimates obtained from the fit of the final PPK model to the pooled data were used to generate individual predicted steady‐state concentration‐time profiles for each subject and to estimate brensocatib exposures. The clinical relevance of the statistically significant covariates was assessed as “forest” plots, in which simulated brensocatib exposure at steady state—represented by the area under the plasma concentration‐time curve from time zero to 24 h at steady state (AUC24) and the maximum (peak) plasma concentration (C max)—was compared across various subgroups (n = 1000 subjects per subgroup) with different characteristics relative to a reference subject (healthy White male, 49 years old, 70 kg, no hepatic impairment, fasted, and no concomitant medications).

Ethics

All studies were performed in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, the Good Clinical Practice guidelines of the International Council for Harmonization, and applicable regulatory requirements. All participants or their legally authorized representative provided written informed consent.

Brensocatib PPK Model

The PPK structural model deemed to best represent the system was a simple 2‐compartment mammillary model with absorption described via a depot compartment followed by 3 transit compartments. A previous PPK model was used as the base model for fitting the pooled dataset of the 11 brensocatib studies. The base model covariate relationships included CrCl and body weight on apparent oral clearance (CL/F), age and body weight on the apparent volume of the central compartment (Vc/F), body weight on the volume of the peripheral compartment (Vp/F), fed status on the absorption rate constant (Ka), and dose dependence on the relative bioavailability (relF) best described by a maximum obtainable percent change from baseline (E max) function. The residual variability model found to best represent the system was best described with an additive and proportional error model distinguished by study phase.

Covariate evaluation using a forward addition and backward elimination approach identified several significant PK covariates associated with Ka, CL/F, Vc/F, Q/F, Vp/F, and relF. Other inhibitors and inducers of CYP3A and Pgp used as co‐medications in the Phase 3 study (mostly mild and moderate) were further evaluated on brensocatib PK and did not show any apparent relationships on the exposure of brensocatib. In addition, renal functions (e.g., CrCl) and disease populations (bronchiectasis, CF, or healthy) were not statistically significant PK covariates.

The performance of the final model was verified using goodness‐of‐fit plots (Figure 1) and a prediction‐corrected visual predictive check (PC‐VPC) plot (Figure 2). The goodness‐of‐fit plots demonstrated the adequacy of the model fit across the pooled dataset because the overall coefficient of determination was 0.95 based on observed vs. individual predicted plasma concentrations. The various conditional weighted residuals (CWRES) plots showed balanced scatter around the line of identity (i.e., CWRES of zero), indicating a lack of substantial bias in fit when examined across the predicted concentration range and the time since last dose. The PC‐VPC plot showed good agreement between median simulated plasma concentrations using the final PPK model and the median observed plasma concentrations for the pooled dataset. Overall, the final model was expected to provide robust and reliable estimates of brensocatib plasma exposure in healthy subjects and in patients with CF or bronchiectasis.

Impact of Covariates on Brensocatib Exposures

As statistically significant predictors of the variability in clearance and volume parameters, body weight and age could affect the systemic exposure of brensocatib. Figure S3 shows that brensocatib exposure in bronchiectasis patients decreased with increasing body weight. In adolescent and elderly (aged > 65 years) patients with bronchiectasis, the AUC24, C max, and C min were slightly higher than those in adults (18–65 years) but overlapped (Figure S4). Based on the covariate analyses, age was also found to be a significant predictor of the variability in brensocatib Vc/F. Using the final PPK model and individual concentration data, brensocatib PK parameters at steady state were determined for adult and adolescent patients enrolled in the Phase 3 study (Table 3). Overall, the magnitude of the increased exposure in patients with bronchiectasis with low body weight, in adolescents, and in elderly participants is considered not clinically meaningful based on the safety profile of the drug at the dose levels of 10 and 25 mg.

Discussion

Brensocatib is a competitive and reversible DPP1 inhibitor. It is a substrate of CYP3A, Pgp, and BCRP but not a clinically meaningful inhibitor of CYP isozymes and transporters [14]. In this study, the PPK of brensocatib was evaluated using pooled data from 11 Phases 1–3 clinical studies. The outcome of this study was used to support brensocatib dose adjustment rationale for treatment of bronchiectasis.

Using pooled PK data from 1098 participants (healthy, or with bronchiectasis, or with CF), a robust PPK model to best describe brensocatib PK was developed, which examined the full range of mixed covariate effects. The final PPK model consisted of 2 systemic compartments coupled with a 3‐compartment transit chain followed by first‐order absorption. The residual variability was best described with an additive and proportional error model distinguished by study phase.

Covariate evaluation using a forward addition and backward elimination approach identified several significant PK covariates associated with Ka, CL/F, Vc/F, Q/F, Vp/F, and relF. However, none of them were considered clinically meaningful given the magnitude of the covariate impact on brensocatib PK and the clinical safety and efficacy data at 10 and 25 mg in patients with bronchiectasis. The covariate effects detected by the PPK analysis are consistent with the observed data from individual clinical studies. In a Phase 1 study [14], Caucasian and Japanese participants received a single dose of brensocatib after overnight fasting or immediately after a high‐fat and high‐calorie breakfast. In both ethnic groups, the presence of food slightly delayed brensocatib absorption with time to maximum plasma concentration increased by 0.7 to 1.7 h, but it had no significant effect on brensocatib exposure (C max and AUC24). In a renal impairment study [17] and a hepatic impairment study [15], the alteration in brensocatib C max and AUC24 was less than 30% compared with participants with normal renal or hepatic functions. In drug–drug interaction studies, brensocatib C max and AUC24 increased by 68% and 55%, respectively following concomitant administration with clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily for 6 days; brensocatib C max and AUC24 increased by 53% and 32%, respectively, following concomitant administration with verapamil 240 mg once daily for 5 days; brensocatib C max and AUC24 were unchanged following concomitant administration with esomeprazole (a proton pump inhibitor) 40 mg once daily for 4 days [9].

In the presence of multiple covariates, the highest exposures were predicted in low‐body‐weight females (e.g., 12‐year‐old, 30‐kg individuals) receiving CYP3A‐ or Pgp‐inhibiting co‐medication (clarithromycin or verapamil), where the predicted C max and AUC24 at steady state with 25 mg could increase more than 2‐fold and reach ~1000 ng/mL and 10,000–12,000 ng h/mL (approximately 3‐fold above the reference), respectively. These results may reflect the combined effects of reduced clearance, increased bioavailability, and smaller volume of distribution. In contrast, the lowest exposures were predicted in high‐body‐weight Black or Asian males receiving CYP3A‐inducing co‐medication (rifampin) who had hepatic impairment, where multiple factors contributed to increased clearance and volume of distribution. Across the remaining simulated covariate combinations, changes in AUC24 and C max were generally modest, with body weight and sex having the largest individual contributions and most scenarios remaining within a 2‐fold range relative to the reference population. Importantly, even at the highest predicted exposures in the most vulnerable subgroups, brensocatib was well tolerated at doses up to 40 mg once daily across completed Phase 1 and Phase 2 studies, and the predicted exposures in these extreme scenarios remain well below the safety margins established from the toxicological assessment, supporting the conclusion that no dose adjustment is warranted. Although fixed theoretical allometric exponents were applied, body weight remained a significant predictor of exposure, and the model adequately captured the increased exposure observed in lower‐body‐weight individuals, including adolescents. Attempts to estimate allometric exponents resulted in model instability and worsening of fit, indicating that the available data did not support refinement beyond conventional scaling. Therefore, the use of fixed exponents is not expected to bias the interpretation of body‐weight effects in this population.

Across completed Phase 1 and Phase 2 studies, brensocatib was well tolerated at doses up to 40 mg once daily. The overall safety profile of brensocatib 10 and 25 mg in the Phase 3 ASPEN study has been reported previously [12]. A detailed evaluation of the relationship between brensocatib exposure and safety outcomes is the subject of a separate manuscript currently in preparation (H. Usansky, in preparation). In addition, brensocatib exposures at the level of C max 1000 ng/mL and AUC24 10,000 ng h/mL are much lower than the established safety margins based on the toxicological assessment [9]. The effects of metabolic inhibition and organ impairment on CL/F and V/F were incorporated during covariate model development. Given the absence of major metabolites and the mild magnitude of drug–drug interactions, additional mechanistic modeling was not expected to add value for exposure‐response analyses of efficacy and safety.

Although age, sex, race, food intake, use of inducing co‐medication, and/or hepatic impairment were identified as statistically significant covariates, the impact of these intrinsic and extrinsic factors on brensocatib systemic exposure is relatively minor and not clinically meaningful. Additionally, renal impairment, disease status, and oral formulations showed no statistically significant effect on brensocatib PK.

It is important to note that the interpretation of covariate effects in adolescents is limited by the imbalance in the number of adolescent versus adult participants, potentially limiting the robustness of estimates in this subgroup. However, the observed trends were directionally consistent with those in adults and are therefore considered unlikely to impact the overall conclusions.

In conclusion, a robust PPK model to best describe brensocatib PK was developed. The impact of all intrinsic and extrinsic factors evaluated on brensocatib exposures was either no effect or limited but not clinically relevant. Thus, no dose adjustment is necessary in adolescent, adult, and elderly patients with bronchiectasis, supporting the dosing regimen of 10 and 25 mg in bronchiectasis patients ≥ 12 years.
