AI-assisted phage formulation delivered via injectable hydrogels for localized control of implant-associated infections
Authors: Fereshteh Bayat, Martin Stark, Sara Rahmani, Cindy Sun, Arman Khani, Yassaman Ommi, Lei Tian, Fiorelle C Aguilar Lopez, Jeffrey I Weitz, Tohid F Didar, Zeinab Hosseinidoust
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBacteriophages are emerging as highly effective antimicrobials for the treatment of persistent bacterial infections, offering precision-targeted action ideal for personalized medicine. Here, we present a scalable phage therapy platform specifically engineered for implant infections. Using an unsupervised machine learning-assisted clustering model, we selected phage combinations for enhanced antibacterial synergy that were delivered using a minimally invasive, shelf-stable composite hydrogel. The hydrogel was composed of laponite nanoclay and carboxymethyl cellulose, delivering a binary Pseudomonas aeruginosa phage cocktail. The phage cocktail-loaded composite nanoclay hydrogel achieved a 5-log bacterial reduction in a biofilm model, while in vivo studies in a murine implant infection model showed 100% survival of treated mice compared to 60% in controls. The composite phage-nanoclay hydrogel also demonstrated a 50% reduction in friction, maintained shelf-life of up to 18 months, and sustained phage release in vivo. Our results demonstrate the power of AI-assisted phage formulation paired with multifunctional biomaterials as a customizable and effective strategy for precision treatment of bacterial infections, advancing the clinical potential of phage therapy.Keywords: Biofilm disruption, Phage synergism, Osteointegration, Controlled release, AMR

Introduction

Implant associated infections (IAIs) are a major contributor to morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs worldwide [1]. Over 50 % of the roughly 2 million annual healthcare-associated infections are linked to medical devices, with implantable devices representing the largest category [2]. IAIs are often chronic or recurrent and require repeat surgical interventions and extended antibiotic therapy due to biofilm formation and antibiotic resistance [3]. Treatment is particularly challenging with orthopedic implants because of bacterial biofilm formation on bone surfaces, combined with the bone's dense structure and limited blood supply from insufficient vascularization, which together make bone infections especially difficult to manage [4]. Current treatment strategies involve surgical debridement, extended antibiotic therapy and potential removal of the implant [5]. Repeat surgical interventions increase the risk of recurrent infection, and can be associated with delayed healing and functional impairment. In addition, the increasing prevalence of antibiotic resistance challenges can complicate the treatment of IAIs [6,7].

Mounting evidence supports the efficacy of bacteriophages as potent antimicrobials for the treatment of persistent human infections (a practice known as phage therapy), including IAI's and bone and joint infections [[8], [9], [10], [11], [12]]. Bacteriophages, or phages for short, are viruses that target bacteria with high specificity, thus minimizing off-target effects and disturbances to host microbiota making them particularly suitable for personalized therapy. Phages replicate at the site of infection in the presence of a bacterial host resulting in sustained activity and reducing the need for repeat dosing. The obvious need and the growing evidence supporting safety and efficacy have promoted continued streamlining of the regulatory process for phage therapy. However, widescale clinical adoption is hampered by practical challenges at every stage, from phage selection and formulation to administration and long-term stability [[13], [14], [15], [16]].

We have previously highlighted the potential of bioengineering to address key bottlenecks in phage therapy, such as redesigning phage biobanks to be more scalable and accessible [17]. We have also demonstrated that integrating materials technologies with bacteriophages can produce shelf-stable, multifunctional biomaterials with enhanced properties [[18], [19], [20], [21]] Building on this experience, we envision a scalable bench-to-clinic workflow for phage-biomaterial combinations by integrating artificial intelligence (AI) to assist with phage formulation. AI represents the next frontier in precision medicine and has therefore attracted significant interest from the phage community, with applications ranging from host prediction to the design of synthetic phage genomes and virions [[22], [23], [24]]. Using an unsupervised machine learning-assisted clustering model, we categorized a subset of Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa) phages and validated the hypothesis that phage combinations selected from divergent genomic clusters outperform intra-cluster pairs in antibacterial activity.

Guided by published clinical evidence indicating that the best outcomes are achieved when bacteriophages are administered directly at the site of infection [24], we developed an injectable, shear-thinning composite hydrogel composed of Laponite nanoclay and carboxymethyl cellulose for localized delivery (Fig. 1a). The composite hydrogel is designed for minimally invasive administration (e.g., intra-articular injection) and enables sustained in situ release of therapeutic phages. Localized delivery is especially beneficial for IAIs, as poor blood flow and biofilms can shield bacteria from both antibiotics and immune cells, while systemic phages may be rapidly cleared before reaching the infection site. Sustained release was a critical design consideration, as phages delivered in vivo may be rapidly cleared if sufficient bacterial replication niches are not present [25]. In addition, the shear thinning rheology offers on site lubrication supporting joint compatibility and the hydrated nature supported long shelf life and phage preservation at room temperature, enabling broader distribution and storage in low-resource settings. In vivo performance was assessed in a dual-hit murine model of P. aeruginosa titanium implant infection. This work presents a scalable pipeline that integrates AI-assisted phage selection with a multifunctional biomaterial delivery platform. By addressing key challenges related to phage formulation and delivery, this approach aims to support the development of phage-based strategies for orthopedic and implant-associated infections. Continued refinement, including expansion to larger and more diverse phage datasets and further biological validation, will be important for broader translational applicability.

Results and discussion

Physical characterization of the composite hydrogel

Laponite nanoclay (NC), is a synthetic nanoclay and a biocompatible, non-toxic, biologically active biomaterial that has been widely used in cosmetics and personal care products for decades and is extensively studied for biomedical applications [[26], [27], [28], [29]]. NC is a two-dimensional phyllosilicate composed of stacked tetrahedral and octahedral sheets in a 2:1 ratio. A positive charge is created at the edges by a charge imbalance caused by magnesium/lithium atoms in the octahedral layer, while the surface is negatively charged due to the presence of silicate tetrahedral [30,31] (Fig. 1b). With a diameter of 20-50 nm and a thickness of 1–2 nm, these nanosheets have a permanent negative charge on the surface and a positive charge along the edges, making them capable of forming house-of-cards structures when dispersed in water at sufficiently high concentrations [26]. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) of NC confirms the 2D sheet structure (Fig. 1b). Due to the opposite charges on the NC and the presence of hydroxyl and silanol groups on its crystals, laponite is expected to interact with other anionic, cationic, and neutral polymers through electrostatic, covalent and hydrogen bonding [32,33].

We prepared a shear-thinning composite hydrogel by incorporating NC and carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC), an anionic biopolymer. Based on their chemical structure, CMCs are expected to interact with laponite through electrostatic and hydrogen bonding to form a crosslinked hydrogel with reversible bonds that can break and reform in response to shear force (Fig. 1c). We prepared the composite hydrogels with three different laponite concentrations (1.5, 2, 2.5 w/v%) and 1 w/v% CMC to form a crosslinked composite hydrogel. The final hydrogel formulations were labeled as 1.5% NC, 2%NC, and 2.5%NC to reflect the laponite concentration.

CMC can electrostatically interact with the positive edges of the NC, as well as form hydrogen bonds between silanol groups of laponite (Si-O) and hydroxyl groups (-OH) of CMC [33]. As a result, CMC can be absorbed onto the surface of NC and can interact with the positive edges through electrostatic and hydrogen bonds. Results of Dynamic light scattering (DLS), shown in Fig. S1, reveal an initial hydrodynamic size of 60 nm for 0.2 w/v% NC. Hydrodynamic size progressively increased to 137 nm, 293 nm, 632 nm, and 1370 nm by adding 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, and 0.5 w/v% CMC, respectively. Simultaneously, the standard deviations increased, indicating the nonuniform formation of NC and CMC aggregates. The anisotropic charge distribution on the surface and edges of laponite can result in reversible electrostatic interactions between nanoclay particles and the CMC polymers, providing shear-thinning injectability properties. Fig. 1c shows the resultant hydrogel, demonstrating hydrogel integrity when extruded through a needle and highly controllable injectability. The shear-thinning behavior also enables a desirable self-healing capability, allowing the hydrogel to reassemble its structure after injection and remain localized at the injection site (Fig. 1d). Furthermore, as shown in Fig. 1e, the composite NC-CMC hydrogel exhibited high spreadability, potentially enabling it to fill irregular structures and cavities and uniformly coat surfaces. This property is advantageous for targeting difficult-to-reach areas around implants where bacteria often persist. High spreadability can also promote more effective therapeutic distribution, reducing the risk of untreated zones where biofilms can form.

Composite hydrogel exhibits shear-thinning behaviour, lubricating performance, and mechanical resilience

Free-standing composite hydrogels were prepared by mixing NC suspension and CMC solution, which led to instant gelation (Fig. 2a). As seen in the scanning electron micrographs of freeze-dried hydrogels, the 2.5% NC composite hydrogel formed a microporous structure with irregular pore shapes (Fig. 2b). Micrographs of the 1.5 and 2% NC hydrogels showed similar microstructure, with slightly larger feature sizes (Fig. S2). Swelling tests revealed high absorption ratios (35-45 g/g) for all formulations, confirming a highly porous structure (Fig. S3a). The dried aerogels absorbed PBS to their full capacity after 2 h, and the swelling ratio remained constant for up to 24 h. The swelling ratio increased by lowering the NC concentration from 2.5 to 1.5 w/v%, and there was no significant difference between 2.5% NC and 2% NC formulations. The composite hydrogels showed a positive weight change (swelling) up to 28 days, after which they began to lose weight, signifying degradation. By day 84, approximately 89% of the initial weight of the 1.5% NC hydrogel was lost. By day 112, the 2%NC and 2.5%NC composite hydrogels had degraded by approximately 91% and 90%, respectively (Fig. S3b).

Fig. 2c shows the results of Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy of laponite, CMC and composite aerogel containing both NC and CMC. The broad band at around 3427 cm−1 on the laponite spectrum is due to –OH stretching vibration of the surface hydroxyl groups, and 1631 cm−1 is attributed to H-OH flexion (absorbed water) [34]. The bands seen at 973 and 641 cm−1 of the laponite spectrum can be attributed to bending and asymmetric stretching of Si–O and vibration of Mg–O bonds [34]. In the FTIR spectrum of CMC, the characteristic peaks seen at 3281 cm−1, 2891 cm−1, 1319 cm−1, and 1020 cm−1 are attributed to -OH, CH stretching, CH bending, C-O bonds, respectively [35]. The bonds at 1595 cm−1, 1441 cm−1 are also indicators of symmetric and asymmetric C=O stretching vibrations of carboxylate ions, respectively. In the NC-CMC spectrum, the broadening and displacement of 3427 cm−1 to 3395 cm−1 and the displacement of the H-OH flexion pick from 1631 cm−1 to 1595 cm−1 can be attributed to intramolecular hydrogen bonding interactions between laponite and CMC [34]. Similarly, the Si-O band has been displaced from 973 to 983 cm−1 and also broadened, along with displacement of asymmetric C=O stretching vibrations of carboxylate ions and C-O bonds, when comparing the laponite spectrum to NC-CMC hydrogel, indicating the interactions between Si–O groups in laponite and the C-O, C=O groups in CMC.

Quantitative measurements of viscosity and storage and loss moduli support shear-thinning behavior and injectability of the composite phage-NC hydrogel. In shear thinning behavior, the viscosity of a hydrogel decreases with increasing shear rate, indicating that the material becomes less resistant to flow under mechanical stress. This is beneficial for lubrication because during the dynamic conditions (e.g., joint movement), the hydrogel's viscosity decreases, allowing it to spread more easily and form a thin, low friction lubricating layer. This dynamic viscosity reduction minimizes wear and friction between bone contacting surfaces. As seen in Fig. 2d, in all three hydrogel formulations, the viscosity decreased by increasing the shear rate, indicating the presence of shear-thinning behavior. By increasing the percentage of NC, relatively higher viscosities were observed possibly because of increased electrostatic interactions and hydrogen bonding between NC and CMC polymer chains. The viscosity stayed constant in 1w/v% CMC solution with no NC and did not change with increasing shear rate, indicating a Newtonian behavior. The CMC solution also showed significantly lower viscosity than the hydrogels containing both NC and CMC. To further evaluate the lubricating effect of the phage-NC hydrogels, a tribology test was conducted using the pin-on-disk method with biomedical-grade titanium balls and disks to measure the coefficient of friction (COF) in the presence of hydrogel, compared to a PBS control (Fig. 2e). The hydrogels reduced the COF from approximately 0.8 to 0.4, demonstrating their ability to effectively lubricate the joint interface, an advantageous property for applications involving intra-articular injection. All hydrogel formulations exhibited similar lubricating effects with no significant differences in their COF values (Fig. 2f).

The storage (Gʹ) and loss (Gʺ) moduli of a material in response to oscillatory shear measure its ability to either absorb (solid-like behavior) or dissipate the energy (liquid-like behavior), respectively. The amplitude sweep results revealed that in all hydrogel formulations, Gʹ exceeded Gʺ, indicating the formation of a free-standing hydrogel network with solid-like behavior (Fig. 2g). As expected, the increase in NC concentrations resulted in an increased storage modulus. A strain of 0.2%, within the linear viscoelastic (LVE) region-where Gʹ and Gʺ are independent of applied stress-was selected and used for the frequency sweep to investigate the viscoelastic properties of a hydrogel over a range of low to high frequencies. Fig. 2h shows how the hydrogel responds to different rates of deformation or stress. Across the frequency range of 0.01-100 Hz, Gʹ consistently exceeded Gʺ (Gʹ>Gʺ), signifying a solid-like behavior maintained at all frequencies. This behavior suggests strong interactions between polymer chains and NC particles within the hydrogel network, maintaining its solid-like behavior across the low to high frequency range. As the oscillation frequency increased, both Gʹ and Gʹʹ showed an increase (frequency dependency). This phenomenon has been reported to occur when there are physical entanglements within the hydrogel network, non-covalent interactions, such as hydrogen bonds or electrostatic forces [36]. The stress sweep test further showed that the hydrogel transitions from linear elastic to nonlinear viscoelastic behavior as the applied stress increases (Fig. S4). The point at which the hydrogel transitions from a linear to a nonlinear region is called yield stress. Beyond this threshold, the hydrogel may begin to flow or exhibit plastic deformation. The results of the stress sweep test conducted at a constant frequency of 10 Hz indicated an increase in the yield stress with increased NC concentration, with values measured at 138.5 Pa, 167.2 Pa, and 244.9 Pa for the 1.5%, 2%, and 2.5% NC hydrogels, respectively (Fig. 2i). This is likely due to increased interactions between NC and CMC with higher NC concentration. As expected, increasing the NC content also resulted in a higher storage modulus (Gʹ), with values of 446.5 ± 41 Pa, 573.6 ± 60 Pa, and 977.2 ± 88.4 Pa for the 1.5%, 2%, and 2.5% NC hydrogels, respectively. The loss modulus (Gʺ) of the hydrogels was significantly lower than the storage modulus (Gʹ) (Fig. 2j). The system containing 2.5% NC displayed the highest yield strength and storage modulus. It's worth noting that NC concentrations exceeding 2.5% were not investigated further due to the excessively high viscosity of the mixture, which made it impractical to achieve uniform and homogeneous hydrogels.

Another significant feature of shear-thinning hydrogels is their capacity for rapid recovery after injection. This can be assessed by subjecting them to cyclic low and high strains and monitoring changes in Gʹ and Gʹʹ over time. As seen in Fig. 2k, at lower strain levels within the LVE region (0.2%), Gʹ surpasses Gʹʹ, indicating the presence of a solid-like structure. However, when subjected to a high strain outside the LVE region (500%), the hydrogel network becomes compromised, with Gʹʹ surpassing Gʹ, resulting in a loss of structural integrity. By lowering the applied strain to 0.2% again, there is a swift recovery of the hydrogel network as shown by the immediate increase of Gʹ to levels higher than Gʹʹ. These successive low and high strain cycles demonstrate the capacity of the hydrogel to recover after complete disintegration, a crucial attribute for shear-thinning injectable hydrogels. Furthermore, Furthermore, the injectability of the hydrogel was assessed by injecting the formulation through a 25G needle using a mechanical tester (Fig. S5). The injection force test revealed that the required force to extrude the hydrogel linearly increased until it reached a plateau, the maximum injection force for each formulation (Fig. S6). As expected, the increase in the laponite concentration led to higher injection force (Fig. 2l). All hydrogel formulations exhibited an injection force well below the maximum recommended limit for injectable medical materials (males: 95.4 N, females: 64.1 N) [37].

Phage cocktail design using unsupervised machine learning-assisted clustering model

In the context of phage therapy, phage cocktails are usually preferred over monotherapy to decrease frequency of bacterial resistance and broaden spectrum of activity [38,39]. However, not all phage cocktails are equally effective, and well-designed formulations can better eradicate bacteria and minimize the emergence of resistance. We developed an unsupervised, machine learning-assisted computational pipeline to cluster phages into genetically distinct groups (Fig. 3a). This approach builds on reports that phage cocktails composed of genetically distinct phages targeting different bacterial receptors exhibit enhanced bacterial killing efficiency [40].

Unsupervised machine-learning-driven computational pipeline for phage cocktail design. a, Schematic of the workflow beginning with phage genome sequences, which undergo dual preprocessing: functional annotation using Pharokka and sequence embedding via DNABERT. Following standardization and dimensionality reduction with UMAP, clustering was performed using K-means on 24 phages. UMAP visualization of clustering results, along with silhouette scores, Davies-Bouldin indices, and elbow method analyses for b, Pharokka-derived features; c, DNABERT embeddings; and d, the combination of Pharokka-derived features and DNABERT embeddings.

The computational framework integrates genome-level characteristics, functional annotations, and sequence-based embeddings to systematically analyze phage genomes. Functional annotations were obtained using Pharokka [41], which captures predicted protein functions, virulence-associated genes, and basic genomic features. In parallel, sequence-derived representations were generated using DNABERT, enabling contextual embedding of nucleotide sequences. DNABERT provides a pretrained, k-mer-based representation that captures both local and long-range sequence patterns [42]. While originally trained on human genomic sequences, it has not been specifically optimized for phage genomes, which may limit its ability to capture phage-specific sequence characteristics. However, its ability to learn contextual representations directly from nucleotide sequences makes it useful for analyzing phage genomes, where many genes remain unannotated and sequence similarity to known proteins is often limited [41]. In addition, DNABERT embeddings are high-dimensional and not directly interpretable in biological terms, as the resulting latent representations reflect sequence patterns rather than explicit functional annotations.

To leverage both biologically interpretable features and sequence-derived information, DNABERT embeddings were combined with features derived from Pharokka annotations, as well as basic genomic properties including genome length, GC content, and coding density. This feature integration enables complementary evaluation of functional genomic information and sequence-level patterns for the design of genetically diverse phage cocktails.

We applied this framework to 24 Pseudomonas aeruginosa phages (Table S1) using genomic sequences retrieved from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) public repository. To assess the contribution of different feature types to phage clustering performance, three feature-integration strategies were evaluated: (i) Pharokka-derived features alone (Fig. 3b), (ii) DNABERT embeddings alone (Fig. 3c), and (iii) a combined feature set integrating Pharokka features with DNABERT embeddings (Fig. 3d). The Pharokka-derived feature set included functional annotation counts together with basic genomic characteristics such as genome length, GC content, and coding sequence (CDS) density.

UMAP visualizations together with the corresponding Silhouette score, Davies-Bouldin Index, and inertia values for K = 2-5 are presented in Fig. 3. The NCBI accession codes of all phages and their cluster assignments for each clustering configuration are provided in Tables S2, S3, and S4. Across the tested clustering configurations, K = 2 produced the highest Silhouette score and the lowest Davies-Bouldin Index, suggesting improved cluster cohesion and separation compared to higher K values. The inertia plot also showed a noticeable elbow around K = 2, further supporting the presence of two major clusters in this dataset. Pharokka-based clustering produces a moderate silhouette score (0.55 at K = 2) and a higher Davies-Bouldin index (0.63) (Fig. 3b). DNABERT embeddings yield slightly higher silhouette scores (0.59) and lower Davies-Bouldin (0.53) at K = 2, reflecting improved cluster cohesion and separation compared to Pharokka alone, capturing sequence-level similarities (Fig. 3c). Combined Pharokka-derived features and DNABERT embeddings achieve a silhouette score of 0.58 and Davies-Bouldin index of 0.56 at K = 2, representing a balance between functional and sequence-based information (Fig. 3d).

Notably, cluster assignments varied slightly depending on the features used, with assignment of PP7 varying across models. Clustering based on DNABERT embeddings and the combined DNABERT embeddings and Pharokka-derived features produced identical results, whereas clustering based solely on Pharokka-derived features assigned PP7 to the alternate cluster. The remaining 23 phages showed consistent cluster assignments across all three clustering configurations (Tables S2, 3, and 4). UMAP visualizations of clustering results at higher K values (3-5) for the three feature configurations are presented in Fig. S7–S9.

A naïve clustering was also performed with genome length and GC content as a baseline. UMAP visualizations for K = 2-5 for Naïve clustering are shown in Fig. S10a–d. Clustering based solely on genome length and GC content produced superficially favorable validation metrics, with a high silhouette score (0.79) and a low Davies-Bouldin index (0.21), and an elbow at K = 2 (Fig. S10e and f). However, the resulting assignments grouped most phages into a single cluster, with only three outliers at K = 2 (Table S5). Notably, the silhouette scores for K = 2-5 were relatively similar (0.79, 0.69, 0.76, and 0.78), suggesting that the clustering outcome is not strongly sensitive to the number of clusters. This indicates that GC content and genome length, while potentially informative, are insufficient to fully capture the underlying biological differences among phages, particularly those related to receptor binding and host interaction.

To test the hypothesis that cross-cluster phage combinations exhibit greater bacterial killing efficiency than within-cluster combinations, we selected a subset of five P. aeruginosa phages from the 24 phage collection, including vB_Pae-TbilisiM32 (P32), JG004, PO4, PP7, and E79, and evaluated their efficacy against the P. aeruginosa PAO1 strain using in vitro infectivity assays. As shown in Fig. S11, bacterial cultures infected with individual phages exhibited an increase in OD600 after ∼8 h, indicating selection for phage-resistant mutants. Among the 10 binary phage combinations tested against PAO1, some combinations achieved higher bacterial growth suppression than others, preventing secondary growth in liquid cultures for up to 24 h. Specifically, JG004-P32, E79-P32, E79-PO4, and JG004-PO4 achieved ∼99% suppression index (Fig. 4b–e), while JG004-PP7 and E79-PP7 showed 91% and 83% suppression indices, respectively (Fig. 4f and g). Other combinations, including JG004-E79, PO4-PP7, P32-PP7, and P32-PO4, showed lower suppression indices of ∼66, 65, 53, and 62%, respectively (Fig. 4h–k). Clustering results at K = 2 (Fig. 3b–d) revealed that JG004 and E79 were consistently in the opposite cluster from P32 and PO4. Accordingly, inter-cluster combinations (JG004-P32, E79-P32, E79-PO4, and JG004-PO4) showed the highest bacterial growth suppression (∼99%, Fig. 4b–e), whereas intra-cluster combinations (JG004-E79 and P32-PO4) resulted in lower suppression (66% and 62%, respectively, Fig. 4h–k). PP7 displayed cluster-dependent effects as it clustered with P32 and PO4 based on Pharokka-derived features, but with E79 and JG004 when using DNABERT or combined features. Consequently, PP7 achieved high suppression when paired with JG004 or E79 (91% and 83%, Fig. 4f and g) but lower suppression when paired with P32 or PO4 (53% and 65%, Fig. 4i and j).

For PP7, a short genome ssRNA phage (3588 bp), functional annotation features may better capture biologically relevant traits than sequence-based embeddings alone, likely because its shorter genomes provide fewer windows for embedding generation, which may reduce the robustness of genome-level embeddings and contribute to discrepancies between clustering predictions and experimental observations [43]. When DNABERT embeddings are combined with Pharokka-derived features, the DNABERT component may dominate the resulting feature representation. The DNABERT embeddings have a dimensionality of 768, which is substantially larger than the 12 Pharokka-derived features. Consequently, the higher-dimensional sequence embeddings can disproportionately influence the combined feature space and potentially obscure the contribution of the functional annotation features, particularly after dimensionality reduction when the overall characteristics are compressed into two components. Nevertheless, the overall agreement between clustering results based on Pharokka-derived features and DNABERT embeddings suggests that the DNABERT model is able to capture genome-level differences that broadly correspond to functional characteristics. Setting aside PP7, our data suggest that combining phages across clusters, i.e., inter-cluster combinations, can achieve higher bacterial suppression than intra-cluster combinations, consistent with reports that cocktails of genetically distinct phages targeting different bacterial receptors enhance killing efficiency [40]. This supports the broader concept that integrating genome-level characteristics, functional annotations, and sequence embeddings within a computational framework may help guide the systematic selection of complementary phages for cocktail design, potentially reducing the number of experimental combinations that need to be tested in the laboratory.

These findings are based on a small proof-of-concept dataset, and further evaluation with larger experimental datasets and additional phages will be necessary to draw definitive conclusions. The clustering is intended as a hypothesis-generating approach, and any functional interpretations including potential complementarity or synergy require experimental validation through receptor profiling, adsorption assays, or host-range studies. Consequently, final phage selection should rely on biological evidence rather than clustering results alone.

Considering both in silico and in vitro results, we selected the P32-JG004 combination for the development of composite phage-NC hydrogel. From this point on, in all composite phage-NC hydrogel formulations, the term 'phage' refers to the P32-JG004 combination.

Composite Phage-NC hydrogels exhibit potent antibacterial and antibiofilm activity

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was used to image the PAO1 strain, and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was used to image two selected P. aeruginosa phages (Fig. 5a). To assess the antibacterial efficacy of our phage pair, PAO1 cultures were exposed to a range of MOIs (Multiplicity of Infection, defined as the ratio between the phage plaque-forming units and the bacterial colony-forming units) by changing the phage concentrations. Fig. 5b shows that the P32-JG004 pair at MOI = 1 effectively suppressed bacterial growth, whereas using individual P32 and JG004 phages led to the emergence of resistant mutants after 8-10 h. At all other examined MOIs (10, 1, 0.01, 0.001, 0.0001), a noticeable and effective reduction in bacterial growth was observed compared to the control and single-phage infections (Fig. S12). The resultant hydrogel contained a final phage concentration of approximately ∼5 × 109 PFU/mL (Plaque Forming Units per milliliter). When this composite phage-NC hydrogel was injected onto the surface of a bacterial lawn, a clear zone of bacterial clearance was observed around the injected hydrogel after overnight incubation at 37 °C, demonstrating its effectiveness in killing bacterial cells (Fig. 5c). Long-term stability of composite phage-NC hydrogel was evaluated qualitatively after 3, 12, and 18 months of storage at room temperature in a sealed syringe. As shown in Fig. S13, the cleared zones around the injected hydrogel on a PAO1 bacterial lawn remained nearly consistent across all time points from 3 to 18 months, indicating that phage activity was preserved even after 18 months of storage at room temperature. It is important to consider that phages are inherently diverse entities with varying widely in stability under harsh conditions [17,44], and so for different phages, we recommend evaluation of shelf-life prior to use.

The phage release profile from the phage-NC hydrogel was evaluated over a period of 5 days. The hydrogels were immersed in sterile PBS, with samples collected at designated time points to measure phage release using a standard plaque assay. After each sampling, the buffer was replaced with fresh PBS. Fig. 5d shows the cumulative phage release profile. There was an immediate release of approximately 103 to 104 PFU/mL at time zero, followed by a burst release of ∼106-107 PFU/mL after 2 h that was maintained till day 5. The incremental phage release profile shows a consistent release of 105-106 PFU/mL every day after the initial burst release of phage (Fig. S14), which suggests that the hydrogel can provide a sustained supply of phage at the site of infection.

The effectiveness of composite phage-NC hydrogels in preventing and dispersing biofilms was quantified next. In the biofilm prevention assay, hydrogels were first placed within each well of a multi-well plate, which was subsequently seeded with bacteria (Fig. 5e). Comparing the CFU counts for the PAO1 blank (no hydrogel) and the 2% NC (no phage hydrogel, negative control) in Fig. 5f revealed that the no-phage hydrogel did not exhibit any bactericidal effect. In contrast, the composite phage-NC hydrogel reduced the bacterial load by approximately 5 logs compared to the blank (no hydrogel) and the negative control (2%NC). Further verification was done by spotting the planktonic phase from each well onto MacConkey agar. Samples from biofilms incubated with negative control led to plates densely covered with bacteria. In contrast, composite phage-NC hydrogels led to the growth of only a few colonies (Fig. 5g). The metabolic activity of bacterial was significantly reduced when challenged with composite phage-NC hydrogels compared to negative control in both the planktonic and sessile phase, indicating reduced bacterial metabolic activity (Fig. 5h). The turbidity of the supernatant (planktonic phase) was also measured for each well and as shown in Fig. S15, composite phage-NC hydrogels resulted in lower OD600 values, confirming that the presence of phages in the hydrogels effectively prevented bacterial growth and biofilm formation.

For the biofilm dispersion assay, a 24-h biofilm was established before introducing composite phage-NC hydrogels into each well (Fig. 5i). After 24 h of incubation, the colony counts indicated a significant decrease in sessile and planktonic bacterial load (approximately 6 logs) in biofilms treated with composite phage-NC hydrogels when compared to the blank and negative control (Fig. 5j). Representative images of the overnight growth on MacConkey agar plates are shown in Fig. 5k, confirming the effectiveness of phage hydrogels. Compared to the negative control, a significant decrease in bacterial metabolic activity in both the planktonic and sessile bacteria was observed in the presence of composite phage-NC hydrogels. Quantified results and the representative images of the wells for biofilm dispersion challenge after conducting XTT assay are shown in Fig. 5l. Turbidity measurements of the sessile phase in each well agree with the findings from CFU counts and the XTT assay (Fig. S16).

An important characteristic of phages as bactericidal agents is their self-dosing ability, meaning they can replicate in the presence of a bacterial host at the site of infection. In both the biofilm prevention and dispersion assays, the phage concentration was quantified by PFU counts. The initial phage concentration loaded in the composite phage–NC hydrogel was approximately 109 PFU/mL. Based on the phage release profile, about 106–107 PFU were cumulatively released into the medium within the first 24 h. Despite this relatively low initial release, the phage concentration in the medium increased to ∼109 PFU/mL in the biofilm prevention assay and ∼1011 PFU/mL in the biofilm dispersion assay by the 24-h endpoint (Fig. S17). This demonstrates the self-amplifying capacity of the phages, resulting in a concentration increase of approximately 3–5 orders of magnitude compared to the initial amount released.

Composite phage-NC hydrogels exhibit in vitro cytocompatibility and in vivo biocompatibility

The cytocompatibility of the composite phage-NC hydrogels was assessed using an indirect method. SaOS-2 osteoblast cells were cultured in standard complete medium supplemented with or without extracts from the composite phage-NC hydrogels. These extracts were obtained by incubating the hydrogels in standard complete medium for 5 and 7 days, allowing degradation products to accumulate. As shown in Fig. S18a, the hydrogels demonstrated good cytocompatibility, with no evidence of cytotoxicity. Even cells exposed to extracts collected after 7 days, which contained higher concentrations of degradation products, showed no signs of cytotoxicity. We further quantified the alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity of the SaOS-2 cells after a 3-day incubation with composite phage-NC hydrogels. The ALP activity increased by 34%, 48%, and 50% in the presence of 1.5%, 2%, and 2.5% NC hydrogels, respectively (Fig. S18b). With a formulation of (Na+0.7[(Si8Mg5.5Li0.3) O20(OH)4]−0.7, laponite degradation products include magnesium, orthosilicic acid, and lithium, which are beneficial for growth factor-free bone regeneration [26,45]. ALP serves as an early marker of osteogenic differentiation in osteoblast cells and is expressed on the extracellular matrix and stimulates bone mineralization by releasing phosphorus [46]. Thus, an increase in ALP activity suggests a potential for bone regeneration when these phage hydrogels are used for infection control in severe cases like osteomyelitis, commonly associated with bone loss.

Given the comparable cytocompatibility, potential for bone regeneration, and anti-biofilm performance between the three formulations, we chose the composite phage-NC hydrogel with 2% NC for investigation in a murine model of chronic subcutaneous implant-associated infection. First, the in vivo biocompatibility of the purified phage suspension and a no-phage NC hydrogel were evaluated separately by subcutaneous injection and monitoring the clinical traits of mice during the 14-day study (Note 1 in the SI file). The control group received a subcutaneous injection of sterile PBS. At the endpoint, histological analysis was performed on the spleen, liver, kidney, and the subcutaneous tissues. H&E staining revealed no evidence of altered tissue architecture or inflammation (Fig. S19). No significant weight loss was observed in any group, and all mice showed normal weight gain over the 14-day study period (Fig. S20a) and the overall health score of all groups remained unaffected, and there were no significant changes in attitude and appearance of the mice across all three groups (Fig. S20b). The homogenized liver, kidney, spleen and subcutaneous tissues were cultured on McConkey agar plates, and no bacterial growth was observed (Fig. S21). Homogenized tissue samples were serially diluted and plated on a bacterial lawn to evaluate the fate of phage released from phage suspension receiving group (Fig. S22 and S23). At the study endpoint, phages were detected in homogenized spleen samples from 7 out of 10 mice and in the subcutaneous tissue of 2 out of 10 mice that received the phage suspension. (Fig. S23a). In contrast, no phage was detected in the liver, kidney, and subcutaneous tissue as shown in Fig. S22a and b and S23b, respectively (limit of detection for assay is 100 PFU/mL equal to 1 PFU/mg of the homogenized sample). The average phage titer in homogenized spleen was 285 PFU per mL of the homogenized sample in PBS, equivalent to 2.85 PFU per mg of the spleen (homogenized tissue concentration was set to 100 mg/mL in PBS and kept the same for all samples), which is a low concentration of phage. Phages have been reported to have higher persistence in the spleen than in the liver and kidneys [47]. The presence of phage in the spleen agrees with its role as the largest and most significant secondary lymphoid organ in the body, which filters the blood to remove pathogens and abnormal cells [48].

Composite phage-NC hydrogel prevents mortality in an implant infection mouse model

A subcutaneous implant infection model was established in mice to evaluate the antibacterial efficacy of the composite phage-NC hydrogel. Sterile titanium coupons were surgically implanted in the subcutaneous tissue, and the incisions were subsequently closed with sutures. The sutures were removed once the incision site had fully healed. A two-hit infection model with P. aeruginosa PAO1 was utilized to establish a chronic infection around the implant. The two-hit infection model can mimic a chronic infection [49] and prevent the mouse immune system to clear the infection before phage action. The first hit activates the immune response and leads to inflammation and tissue damage. The second hit often leads to immunosuppression, where a prolonged inflammatory state weakens the immune system [50]. This increases the risk of secondary infections, which are more difficult to treat since the immune system's support in clearing infections is compromised. A two-hit model is particularly relevant in scenarios where phage therapy is introduced at later stages as a last resort after the patient has failed to respond to antibiotic treatment, to manage chronic infections, or in immunocompromised patients. Our two-hit infection involved administering an initial low dose of 107 CFU per mouse, followed by a 2nd dose of 5 × 108 CFU per mouse three days later, both delivered subcutaneously at the implant site. Two infection groups of 10 mice each received (i) composite phage-NC hydrogel (treatment group) or (ii) 2%NC hydrogel, no phage (negative control) as direct injection subcutaneously to the implanted site area right after the 2nd dose of infection. The third group (blank) was not inoculated with bacteria but received the composite phage-NC hydrogel at the same time as the other two groups.

Fig. 6a illustrates the study timeline. At day 4, one mouse was found dead in the negative control group and three other mice in that group were euthanized due to a significant decrease in their combined health scores (Fig. 6b–d). Significantly, 100% of mice in the treatment group survived (Fig. 6b) and despite some weight loss on day 4, managed to recover weight. By day 8, all these mice had comparable weight and combined health score to the uninfected mice in the blank group (Fig. 6c and d). It is noteworthy that the two-hit model is a harsh infection model; severity of the infection resulted in histological analysis of the organs (Liver, kidney, and spleen) with H&E to be inconclusive. This further underscores the potency of the composite phage-NC hydrogels for controlling implant infections.

At end pint, organs and subcutaneous tissue were harvested. Bacterial colonies were isolated and cultured separately for growth monitoring (Fig. 6e). Phage was present in all harvested organs as well as recovered coupons from the treatment group (Fig. 6f). We also isolated phage from the uninfected group (blank) but at significantly lower concentration likely due to the absence of host bacteria and, consequently, the lack of on-site phage amplification (Fig. S24). The presence of phages in both groups can be attributed to their sustained release from the hydrogel over time, which is expected to protect them from rapid clearance by the immune system [51]. We have previously observed that in the absence of phage-biomaterial combinations providing protection and controlled release, phage is rapidly cleared in vivo [52].

The bacterial burden in the treatment group compared to the negative control was significantly lower locally (in both the coupon and subcutaneous tissue) (Fig. 6g), but not significantly different in the organs, namely liver and spleen (Fig. 6g) and kidney (Fig. S25). This led us to investigate other possible mechanisms contributing to the decrease in mortality in the treatment group. Clear evidence of phage activity was observed in the process of colony counts, where serial dilutions of tissue samples isolated from the treatment group lacked viable bacterial colonies at lower dilutions (Fig. S26). In comparison, the bacterial colonies became evident in higher dilutions, signalling potential phage activity in the samples was diminished by dilution. We further observed evidence of possible fitness diversification in the colonies isolated from the treatment group, with these colonies exhibiting a wide range of growth rates and yield (quantified as AUC in Fig. 6h and Fig. S27) compared to colonies isolated from negative control group, with some colonies exhibiting impaired growth, either at slower rate or no growth at all. This suggests possible selection for a bacterial population with lower fitness, with phage-resistant bacteria becoming more susceptible to antibiotics and immune system-mediated clearance, leading to a successful clinical outcome [[53], [54], [55], [56]]. Notably, the colonies retrieved from the treatment group were mostly smaller and had irregular shapes compared to the spherical colonies of the no phage group (Fig. S26). Minor colony variants have been reported in the literature to evolve as a result of bacteriophage infection [53].

We observed another indication of reduced virulence when examining the regrown colonies isolated from the treatment and negative control groups. The colonies recovered from mice in the negative control group were surrounded by a greenish-blue color absent in the colonies from the treatment group. This pattern was consistent across bacteria recovered locally (from the coupon and subcutaneous tissue) and from the liver, spleen, and kidney, as shown in Fig. S26. The greenish blue hue of P. aeruginosa colonies on nutrient agar is due to pyocyanin production [57]. Pyocyanin is a redox-active secondary metabolite and toxin secreted by P. aeruginosa, and is a major contributor to death and sepsis in immunocompromised patients [58,59]. Thus, the decreased production of this virulence factor can contribute to improved clinical outcomes in phage therapy patients [54,55]. This reduced fitness and virulence may have partially contributed to the significant decrease in mortality in our treatment group over negative control, despite the non-significant reduction in bacterial load in organs and the lack of complete bacteria eradication locally.

Conclusion

In this study, we present a workflow for precision phage therapy by combining materials technology and artificial intelligence. We targeted P. aeruginosa, a critical-priority pathogen on the World Health Organization’s antibiotic-resistant priority list, and developed a machine learning-assisted computational model to cluster P. aeruginosa phages based on genetic diversity. Our experimentally validated results showed that inter-cluster combinations outperform intra-cluster pairs in terms of antibacterial efficacy. Our work demonstrates the potential power of AI-assisted computational approaches to facilitate the design of effective phage cocktails. The resulting clusters provided a rational basis for selecting phages with complementary genomic profiles for formulation within the composite hydrogel delivery platform. Importantly, this AI-assisted clustering framework is not intended as a clinical prediction tool, but rather as an exploratory representation-learning approach that leverages transformer embeddings to capture higher-order genomic relationships. The current pipeline is an unsupervised, exploratory computational approach and requires substantial refinement and biological validation before broader application. It is important to emphasize that these results represent an initial proof-of-concept toward a generalizable framework for phage cocktail design. Development of a more robust and widely applicable system will require significantly larger and more diverse phage genome datasets across multiple bacterial hosts, extensive in vitro and in vivo validation, and integration of additional genomic, proteomic, and phenotypic features. Achieving this goal will necessitate interdisciplinary collaboration across phage biology, bioinformatics, machine learning, and clinical microbiology. Despite these limitations, our findings suggest that AI-assisted genomic clustering can reduce the experimental burden associated with cocktail formulation using random phage pairing, thereby improving scalability and streamlining development. With continued validation and collaborative refinement, such computational approaches have the potential to meaningfully advance and transform phage therapy design.

To further streamline the personalized therapy aspect, the selected and verified phage combination was formulated into a shear-thinning hydrogel designed for minimally invasive treatment of implant-associated infections by localized injection. Given the well-documented restriction of blood flow at site of implant-associated infection, localized phage delivery directly to the infection site is expected to significantly improve clinical outcomes. Although phage suspensions can be administered directly to the infection site, phages are rapidly cleared if they do not encounter adequate concentrations of host bacteria for replication. However, our phage-biomaterial combination provided a sustained release of phage in vivo even in the absence of host bacteria. This means that even if the initial injection of phage-NC hydrogel does not immediately encounter a high concentration of bacteria, phage will be sustained in the local microenvironment as the infection progresses. The controlled release, combined with lubrication ability, self-healing nature, and potential for enhanced osteointegration, make our phage-NC composite hydrogels particularly well suited for bone and joint infections. Furthermore, the phage-biomaterial combination format offered shelf stability, conformed by enabled long term preservation of phage activity at room temperature. This feature allows for formulations to be transported between hospitals around the world or used in low resource settings and ultimately contributing to accessibility of phage therapy.

Phage therapy has traditionally relied on direct elimination of pathogenic bacteria. However, our results show that mice treated locally with phage-NC hydrogels had improved clinical outcomes, even though the local bacterial load was only modestly reduced. We observed diversification within the bacterial population isolated from the treatment group. We hypothesize that the emergence of a subpopulation with reduced fitness following bacteriophage infection may contribute to the effectiveness of the composite phage-NC hydrogel in improving clinical outcomes, although further investigation is required to validate this hypothesis. While the in vitro evidence for potential bone healing benefits requires further investigation in bone injury models, the demonstrated cyto- and biocompatibility of the composite hydrogels, along with their strong in vivo performance in a high-burden infection model, position them as a promising candidate for precision infection management.

Rheological analysis. Rheological properties were assessed using a Discovery HR-2 hybrid rheometer (TA Instruments, USA) with a cone-plate geometry with 40 mm diameter, 1°cone angle, and 50 μm truncation gap. To prevent evaporation, mineral oil was applied around the circumference of the plates in all measurements to obtain consistent results. The hydrogels were equilibrated for 2 min prior testing under steady shear of 0.2% strain at 10 Hz to remove the mechanical history of handling the hydrogel. Frequency sweeps were conducted from 0.01 to 100 Hz at 37 °C, and strain sweeps were performed from 0.01% to 1000% strain at 10 Hz at 37 °C by 10 points per decade measurements. Recovery test was performed at 10 Hz 37 °C under 500% strain for 60 s, a value outside the linear viscoelastic region, followed by applying a 0.2% strain for 120 s. The low and high strain cycle was repeated 6 times for a cyclic strain test to assess the ability of the hydrogel to recover itself. The flow ramp was performed at shear rates from 0 to 50 s−1 over 150 s at 25 °C.

Bacterial culture and phage propagation. Overnight cultures of bacteria were prepared by streaking a frozen glycerol stock of Pseudomonas aeruginosa PAO1 strain (stored at −80 °C) and inoculating LB media, followed by incubation under 180 rpm at 37 °C for 14-16 h. Pseudomonas aeruginosa phages including JG004 (NCBI: NC_019450.1) [60], vB_Pa2-TbilisiM32 (P32 for short, NCBI: KX711710.1) [61], PO4 (NCBI: PX259653.1) [62], PP7 (NCBI: NC_001628.1) [63], and E79 (HER 359, NCBI: MH536736.1) [55] were propagated on their host (PAO1), by introducing a single plaque of each phage to a bacterial subculture at mid-exponential phase of growth (OD600 = 0.3) and incubating at 37 °C at 180 rpm for 6 h. Crude phage lysates were then pelleted down at 7000 rcf, at 4 °C for 15 min, the solution on top was decanted to a new tube, and the pellet containing bacterial debris was discarded. The phage containing solutions were then sterile filtered using 0.45 μm and 0.22 μm syringe filters consecutively. To further concentrate the phage solutions in some cases 30 KDa, and 10 KDa Amicon ultra filters were used.

Bacterial concentration was determined by performing 10-fold serial dilutions of the bacterial suspension in sterile PBS, followed by plating onto LB agar plates (1.5% agar, 25 g/L LB media) or MacConkey agar plates. The plates were incubated overnight at 37 °C in a stationary incubator. Colonies were counted the next day, and the bacterial concentration was reported as colony-forming units per milliliter (CFU/mL).

To determine phage concentration, the conventional phage overlay assay was performed [64]. Briefly, phage samples were serially diluted in sterile PBS buffer (10-fold dilution). The dilutions were plated in triplicate on LB agar plates (1.5% agar, 25 g/L LB media) containing a bacterial lawn. The bacterial lawn was prepared by spreading 5 mL of melted soft agar (0.6% agar, 25 g/L LB media) cooled to 50 °C and mixed with 150 μL of PAO1 overnight culture onto the LB agar plates. The plates were incubated overnight at 37 °C in a stationary incubator. Phage-mediated clear zones, known as plaques, formed on the bacterial lawn and were counted to calculate the phage concentration in plaque-forming units per milliliter (PFU/mL).

Phage cocktail design

Computational model architecture and hyperparameters. The proof-of-concept AI-assisted clustering pipeline for phage genome analysis was developed as an unsupervised machine learning framework integrating genome composition, functional annotation, and sequence-based embeddings. Genome sequences were embedded using a k-mer transformer pipeline implemented in our public repository. Specifically, we used the pretrained DNABERT-6 model, which tokenizes sequences into 6-mers. Each genome was segmented into overlapping windows of ∼515 bp (510 consecutive 6-mers), with window start positions advanced by 250 bp. Contextual embeddings were extracted from the CLS token for each window using the pretrained transformer model. Window-level embeddings were then averaged to generate a single 768-dimensional genome-level representation.

In addition to sequence embeddings, genomic descriptors and functional annotation-derived features were incorporated. Genome-level descriptors included genome length, GC content, and CDS coding density. Functional features were derived from genome annotations generated by Pharokka. These included the tRNA count (pharokka_tRNA), the number of PHROG-annotated coding sequences (PHROG_CDS), and counts of genes assigned to major PHROG functional categories: connector proteins (PHROG_connector), DNA/RNA and nucleotide metabolism (PHROG_DNA_RNA_and_nucleotide_metabolism), head and packaging proteins (PHROG_head_and_packaging), integration and excision functions (PHROG_integration_and_excision), moron/auxiliary metabolic and host takeover genes (PHROG_moron_auxiliary_metabolic_gene_and_host_takeover), tail proteins (PHROG_tail), and transcription regulation proteins (PHROG_transcription_regulation).

To evaluate the contribution of different feature classes to clustering performance, multiple feature sets were constructed using combinations of Pharokka-derived functional features and genomic descriptors, and DNABERT embeddings. In addition, a baseline feature set consisting of simple genomic heuristics (genome length and GC content) was included. These basic descriptors served as a baseline to evaluate whether functional annotations and AI-derived sequence embeddings provide improved clustering resolution beyond simple genome statistics.

For all configurations, features were standardized prior to clustering. Dimensionality reduction for clustering and visualization was performed using UMAP with parameters n_neighbors = 10 and min_dist = 0.4. Phages were subsequently clustered using K-means (n_init = 10, random_state = 42) to group genomes based on genomic and functional similarity. Cluster validity was evaluated using silhouette score, Davies-Bouldin index and Elbow method. This framework was applied to a dataset of 24 Pseudomonas aeruginosa phages, listed in Table S1.

In vitro phage cocktail testing. Phage Cocktails were tested in vitro by measuring turbidity of phage infected cultures compared to un-infected cultures using OD600 assay. Binary combinations of phages (including P32-JG004, P32-PO4, P32-PP7, P32-E79, PO4-JG004, PO4-PP7, PO4-E79, JG004-PP7, PP7-E79) were introduced to PAO1 cultures at OD600 ∼0.1 at MOI = 10 (multiplicity of infection or phage to bacteria ratio), at total volume of 200 μL in a sterile flat bottom transparent 96 well plate. The plate was incubated for 24 h at 37 °C under orbital shaking inside a Synergy Neo2 BioTek plate reader and absorbance was recorded every 5 min at 600 nm. Phage pairs which successfully supressed the bacterial growth and had absorbance values similar to media controls were introduced as effective combinations. The selected phage pair (P32-JG004) were further analyzed by repeating the above explained experiments at different MOIs including 10, 1, 0.1, 0.01, 0.001. The final OD600 results were graphed using GraphPad Prism 10 software.

Phage release test. The phage release profile was assessed by immersing hydrogels in sterile PBS buffer and collecting samples at various time points over 5 days (0, 2, 4, 6, 7, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h). Hydrogels loaded with a phage cocktail (P32-JG004) at a final concentration of 5 × 109 PFU/mL were prepared in quadruplicate. At each time point, an eluate sample was collected, serially diluted, and plated onto PAO1 bacterial lawns on agar plates to determine the phage concentration by counting the plaques. The entire buffer was then replaced with fresh PBS to continue monitoring phage release. The cumulative release profile was plotted by summing the phage released at each time point.

In vitro biological evaluation

In vitro antibacterial assessment. Antibacterial performance of the composite phage-NC hydrogel was assessed using diffusion assay by injecting it on a PAO1 bacterial lawn. The lawn was prepared by adding 100 μL of PAO1 overnight into melted LB soft agar (0.6% agar) and spreading on a LB agar plate. Having injected the composite phage-NC hydrogel on the prepared lawn, the plate was incubated inside a stationary incubator at 37 °C overnight.

In order to evaluate the biofilm prevention ability of the composite phage-NC hydrogel, quadruplicates of composite phage-NC hydrogel with 3 different formulations (1.5%NC, 2%NC, 2.5%NC + phage) with a final concentration of 109 PFU/mL of phage cocktail containing P32,JG004 (1:1 v/v) and a hydrogel formulation without phage (2%NC-no phage) as a negative control were placed in a sterile untreated flat bottom 24 well plate. LB media only wells and PAO1 only wells were also used controls. Hydrogel volume was kept constant at 400 μL for all conditions. Having added 1 mL of bacterial cultures of PAO1 at 105 CFU/mL to each well, bacterial biofilms were grown for 24 h under 120 rpm agitation at 37 °C. At the end of 24 h biofilm growth, optical density of 200 μL of the supernatant was measured at 600 nm (OD600) in a 96 well plate, comparing the turbidity of the samples. Afterwards, CFU counts of the supernatant was calculated by taking samples from the wells containing hydrogels with and without phage and serially diluted in sterile PBS solution (10 folds serial dilutions) and plating on MacConkey agar (selective for gram negative bacteria) plates to obtain CFU/mL. The metabolic activity of the planktonic bacterial cells was also measured by adding 100 μL of solution from each well and added to 100 μL of XTT solution in a 96 well plate and incubated for an hour. The absorbance was then recorded at 490 nm using Synergy Neo2 BioTek plate reader. The metabolic activity of the adhered biomass was also evaluated by carefully removing all the planktonic bacteria and adding 1500 of XTT solution, followed by 1 h incubation in the dark under 120 rpm agitation at 37 °C. The absorbance was recorded for all samples by adding 200 μL of the XTT containing solutions from each well to a 96 well plate and recording the absorbance at 490 nm.

For assessing the biofilm dispersing ability of the injectable hydrogels, 24-h biofilms were first seeded inside a sterile 24 well plate under 120 rpm agitation at 37 °C in a total volume of 1000 μL per well by initial concentration of PAO1 at 105 CFU/mL. Having removed 500 μL from each well at the end of 24-h incubations, and replacing it with 500 μL of fresh media, different hydrogel formulations with and without phage cocktails, were introduced to each well in quadruplicates. The plates were then incubated for another 24 h, followed by measuring the turbidity of the supernatant (OD600), metabolic activity of the supernatant and the adhered biomass using XTT assay, CFU counts, and turbidity measurements as discussed above. Synergy Neo2 BioTek plate reader was used to record absorbance data in both XTT and OD600 assays.

The phage concentration was evaluated at the endpoint of each assay as well. The serially diluted samples were plated on PAO1 bacteria lawn made on LB agar plates. The plaques were counted, and phage concentration was reported for both biofilm prevention and biofilm dispersion assays.

Cell culture and cytotoxicity assay. Cytotoxicity of the composite phage-NC hydrogel was evaluated using an indirect method. First, hydrogels with three different Lapointe concentrations (1.5, 2, 2.5% NC) loaded with a mixture of P32-JG004 phages at a final concentration of 109 PFU/ml were emersed in 700ul SaOS-2 growth medium (McCoy's 5A medium supplemented with fetal bovine serum (FBS)) and intubated at 37 °C in 5% CO2 for 3, 5, and 7 days. In the meanwhile, SaOS-2 cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well into each well of a tissue culture treated 24-well plate. The SaOS-2 cells were exposed to hydrogel extracts by replacing the complete media in cell seeded wells with 700 μL of the extract form each hydrogel group. The cells were incubated for an additional 48 h in elution medium containing hydrogel extracts. The cells exposed to fresh complete media were regarded as a control group. After 48h of incubation, cytotoxicity assay was conducted using PrestoBlue™ HS Cell Viability Reagent (Invitrogen P50200) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, having removed the medium from each well of the 24-well plate containing cells, 10-fold dilution of the cell viability reagent in cell culture medium was added to each well. The plate was incubated for 3 h at 37 °C in 5% CO2. After incubation, 200 μL of the solution was added to black well plates (Greiner Bio-One 655086) and fluorescence measurements were taken at excitation and emission wavelengths of 560 ± 5 nm and 590 ± 5 nm, respectively using Synergy Neo2 BioTek plate reader. To assess the ALP activity of cells exposed to hydrogels, SaOS-2 cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well into each well of a tissue culture treated 24-well plate. Hydrogels with 1.5, 2, 2.5% NC loaded with phage cocktail (P32, JG004) were placed onto seeded wells and incubated for 3 days. The ALP activity was measured at the end of incubation in the supernatant of each sample using Alkaline Phosphatase Assay Kit (ab138887, Abcam.com), based on manufacturer's instructions. All measurements were performed in quadruplicates.

In vivo mouse model of subcutaneous implant infection

All protocols and procedures described in this manuscript were approved by McMaster University's Animal Research Ethics Board (AREB) under Animal Utilization Protocol (AUP) 21-10-32.

In vivo Biocompatibility. To assess cytotoxicity effects, individual components of the bacteriophage hydrogel were injected subcutaneously into the dorsal skin of mice. For this procedure, 30 female C57BL/6J mice (The Jackson Laboratory) were divided into three groups. The first group received 100 μL of sterile hydrogel without any bacteriophages (2%NC). The second group was injected with 100 μL of phage suspension in PBS at a concentration of 5 × 109 PFU (Phage-suspension). The final group received 100 μL of sterile PBS as a control. Post-injection, the mice were monitored daily for 14 days, and their overall health was scored based on the endpoint monitoring criteria listed in Note 1 (supplementary information).

After the 14-day monitoring period, blood was collected from the mice to test for the presence of phage and bacteria. Subsequently, the mice were ethically euthanized by cervical dislocation under isoflurane anesthesia, and their organs were collected for histological examination.

Composite phage-NC hydrogel in subcutaneous implant infection in a mouse model

Surgical implantation of titanium disks

An infected implant animal model was developed to evaluate the efficacy of the phage hydrogel as a therapeutic approach. Before starting any experiments, the mice were given a week's time to acclimate to their new environment. During this time, the mice were monitored daily to obtain a health and wellness baseline (Note 1 of the Supplementary Information) and to ensure the mice were fit for the procedures. The experiment timeline can be observed in Fig. 4a. First, the surgical implantation of the titanium disks was performed 7 days prior to the induction of the infection and 10 days before the injection of the hydrogels, to allow the mice to heal and minimize the influence of the surgery on the infection model results.

The surgical procedures for the mice were adapted from Stravakis et al. [65] Briefly, the mice were administered 0.05 mL of slow-release buprenorphine for analgesia 1 h before surgery. After this preparatory period, the mice were anesthetized via inhalation of isoflurane (2%), shaved, and prepared using a three-step disinfecting protocol before being transferred to a sterile working area. A small incision was made in the mid-lower dorsal region, followed by gentle tissue undermining to create a subcutaneous (SQ) pocket using a blunt dissection with the iris scissors. A sterile titanium disk (5 mm) was introduced into the pocket, and the incision site was sutured closed.

The mice were placed in a sterile cage to recover from anesthesia before being returned to their housing cage. Following disk implantation, the mice were monitored daily for 7 days, allowing the incision site to heal. After this 7-day period, the sutures were removed, and the mice were prepared for the induction of the infection, their health was monitored daily until the end of the experiment.

Infection induction and Phage–Hydrogel in vivo procedure

To evaluate the efficacy of the phage hydrogel, 30 female C57BL/6J mice were divided into three groups post-disk implantation.

•Group I: Received the phage-hydrogel (2%NC + phage) but no bacteria (uninfected).

Group I: Received the phage-hydrogel (2%NC + phage) but no bacteria (uninfected).

•Groups II: Received the phage-hydrogel (2%NC + phage) and the two-shot bacteria regimen (described below).

Groups II: Received the phage-hydrogel (2%NC + phage) and the two-shot bacteria regimen (described below).

•Group III: Received the hydrogel without phage (2%NC) and the two-shot bacteria regimen.

Group III: Received the hydrogel without phage (2%NC) and the two-shot bacteria regimen.

A two-hit infection models was adopted to mimic a chronic infection model [49]. Here a lower bacterial load (107 CFU) was introduced 3-days earlier at the implant site to activate the immune response, induce inflammation and tissue damage. The second, more aggressive bacterial load (5 × 108 CFU) was used at day zero to overload the immune system, and mimic a prolonged inflammatory state [50]. Following the two-hit bacterial infections, a 100 μL treatment injection: either a 2%NC or a 2%NC + phage hydrogel was introduced at the implant site. The mice were monitored daily for signs of illness of if they reached an early endpoint as described in Note 1 (supplementary information). At the endpoint, blood was collected via the facial vein while under a light isoflurane anesthesia. The mice were then humanely euthanized by cervical dislocation under isoflurane anesthesia, after which the titanium disc, liver, kidney, and spleen samples were collected to assess bacterial and bacteriophage viability.

Tissue homogenization for PFU and CFU counts. To assess the fate of phage and translocation of bacteria, 100 mg of tissue samples from the spleen, liver, and kidneys, and the subcutaneous skin tissue surrounding the hydrogel was added in 1 mL of sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing sterile stainless-steel beads. The samples were homogenized using a bullet blender homogenizer. The homogenized samples were then serially diluted in PBS and plated on McConkey agar plates for CFU counts and on PAO1 lawn on LB agar plates for the PFU counts. All plates were incubated in a stationary incubator at 37 °C for 16 h. CFU and PFU numbers were calculated as previously described. One mouse from group III (2% NC) showed no bacterial colonies in any of the collected samples and was therefore excluded from the final analysis.

Monitoring growth behaviour of retrieved colonies from mice. Homogenized spleen, liver, kidney, and subcutaneous tissue, along with bacteria isolated from coupons, were plated on MacConkey agar and incubated overnight at 37 °C to allow colony growth. The growth behavior of bacterial colonies retrieved from mice was assessed by inoculating fresh LB media with selected colonies from both groups (2%NC + phage and 2% NC) in a 96-well plate, with each well containing a single colony. For each tissue sample, 5–12 colonies were selected per mouse. Growth was monitored by reading absorbance at 600 nm (OD600) every 15 min for up to 24 h at 37 °C using Synergy Neo2 BioTek plate reader. As a comparison, colonies from a streaked plate of wild-type PAO1 (PAO1-W) were grown under the same condition. The growth curves for individual colonies were then graphed separately and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated per colony using built-in feature of GraphPad Prism Version 10 software to facilitate the comparison.

Statistical analysis. Data generated in this study are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (version 10.0.5). Data were evaluated using unpaired t-tests, one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with multiple comparison tests, the log-rank (Mantel–Cox) test for Kaplan–Meier survival analysis, and mixed-effects analysis for longitudinal measurements such as body weight and daily health scores. A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.
