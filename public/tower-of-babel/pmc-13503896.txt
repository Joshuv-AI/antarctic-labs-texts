Intelligent english education resource recommendation via LLM–knowledge graph integration and ecological niche fitness modeling
Authors: You Wang, Dong Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTeaching English to large and varied student populations has made adaptive, learner-aware resource recommendation a pressing need, yet most deployed systems still lean on collaborative filtering or content heuristics that read learners only at the surface. Such systems tend to stumble in four recurring ways: they miss the finer grain of linguistic competence, they reason over sparse and brittle knowledge associations, they falter whenever a learner or a resource is new, and they rarely travel well to settings their training never saw. We address these problems together through a framework in which a large language model and a knowledge graph inform one another, their match to each learner mediated by a fitness function adapted from ecological niche theory. The language model reads a learner’s own writing to estimate evolving competence and proposes relations that fill thin regions of the graph, while the graph keeps the model’s inferences anchored to the prerequisite and difficulty structure of English. A niche fitness score then judges how closely each resource’s demands meet a learner’s needs, and a path generator orders the survivors into prerequisite-respecting study sequences. On logs from 3,214 learners, 12,486 resources, and 287,653 interactions, the method reaches a Precision@5 of 0.782 and an NDCG@10 of 0.724, ahead of five baselines. Ablation was, frankly, the part that surprised us: the niche filter matters more to ranking quality than the model’s semantic re-scoring. A three-axis generalization protocol, splitting transfer across learners, resource types, and difficulty levels, yields a composite index of 0.863, with cold-start decay held to 18.3%.Keywords: English education recommendation, Large language model, Knowledge graph, Ecological niche fitness, Personalized learning path, Generalization evaluationSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The demand for adaptive and learner-centered instruction has intensified as English language education scales across diverse institutional contexts worldwide. Traditional recommendation systems, which typically match resources to learners through collaborative filtering or content-based heuristics, often fall short when confronted with the heterogeneous and evolving nature of language proficiency1. A learner struggling with subordinate clause comprehension, for instance, may receive materials targeting vocabulary breadth simply because peers with superficially similar profiles found those materials useful — a mismatch rooted in the system’s inability to parse fine-grained linguistic competence. This disconnect between what recommendation engines assume about learners and what learners actually need has motivated a search for architectures capable of deeper semantic reasoning and more structured knowledge representation2.

Large language models have reshaped expectations for what computational systems can achieve in natural language understanding. Models such as GPT-4 and LLaMA demonstrate remarkable capacity for contextual inference, open-domain question answering, and text generation that approximates human fluency3. Their application to educational settings — from automated essay scoring to dialogue-based tutoring — has expanded rapidly4. Yet LLMs operate primarily over unstructured text, and their reasoning, however impressive, remains probabilistic rather than grounded in explicit relational knowledge. When asked to recommend a grammar exercise suited to a learner who has mastered the present perfect but not the past perfect conditional, an LLM may generate plausible suggestions without systematically consulting the prerequisite dependencies that govern English grammar acquisition5. Knowledge graphs address precisely this gap. By encoding entities (e.g., grammatical constructs, vocabulary domains, discourse strategies) and their typed relations within a structured ontology, KGs provide the relational scaffolding that LLMs lack6. Research on KG-enhanced educational platforms has demonstrated improvements in resource retrieval accuracy and prerequisite-aware sequencing7, though most existing systems treat the KG as a static lookup table rather than a dynamic reasoning substrate.

Learner profile modeling constitutes another axis along which current approaches remain underdeveloped. Early work relied on categorical descriptors — beginner, intermediate, advanced — that compress multidimensional competence into a single ordinal label8. More recent efforts incorporate multi-attribute vectors capturing reading speed, error type distributions, and task completion patterns9, but these models seldom account for the temporal dynamics of learning. A student’s listening comprehension may plateau while her writing fluency accelerates; static profiles cannot represent such asynchronous trajectories. Dynamic Bayesian networks and recurrent architectures have been proposed to track evolving competence10, yet they require substantial interaction data and degrade severely under cold-start conditions where new learners or newly added resources lack historical traces11.

Ecological niche theory, originally developed in population biology to describe how organisms occupy and compete for positions within environmental gradients, offers a conceptually rich but underexplored analogy for educational resource matching. The core idea — that each organism thrives within a specific niche defined by multiple environmental dimensions — maps onto the educational setting where each learner occupies a competence niche defined by proficiency dimensions, and each resource addresses a target niche defined by difficulty, topic, and pedagogical function12. A handful of studies have adapted niche fitness metrics to quantify the compatibility between learner states and resource attributes in STEM education13, but this perspective has not been systematically applied to English language learning, where the competence space is particularly high-dimensional and where cultural and pragmatic dimensions intersect with purely linguistic ones.

Several persistent challenges warrant attention. First, semantic understanding in most recommendation pipelines operates at a surface level: keyword overlap or embedding cosine similarity cannot capture the pragmatic nuance distinguishing, say, a business email writing exercise from a casual conversation prompt even when both address “writing skills”14. Second, knowledge association sparsity plagues educational KGs because expert-curated graphs rarely achieve the density needed for effective reasoning over long prerequisite chains15. Third, the cold-start problem remains acute in educational contexts where new courses, textbooks, and multimedia resources are continuously produced yet lack the interaction histories that collaborative filters depend upon16. Fourth, generalization — the capacity of a trained recommendation model to perform reliably across learner populations, institutional settings, and resource domains it has not encountered during training — receives insufficient evaluation in the literature, with most studies reporting accuracy on a single dataset without cross-domain or cross-population validation17.

Our work addresses these intertwined challenges through an integrated framework that couples the semantic inference strengths of LLMs with the structural explainability of KGs, orchestrated by a niche fitness function that quantifies learner–resource compatibility across multiple competence dimensions. The LLM component processes learner-generated text (essays, forum posts, spoken transcripts) to extract latent proficiency signals that conventional feature engineering would miss, while the KG component maintains and updates an explicit network of English language knowledge nodes — phonological, lexical, syntactic, pragmatic — linked by prerequisite, co-occurrence, and difficulty-gradient relations. A niche fitness score, computed as a multidimensional overlap between the learner’s estimated competence niche and each candidate resource’s target niche, drives both the ranking of individual resources and the sequencing of personalized learning paths. We further propose a multi-dimensional evaluation protocol that tests generalization along four axes: new learners (cold-start users), new resources (cold-start items), cross-institutional transfer, and cross-proficiency-level transfer18.

The principal contributions of this paper are threefold. We introduce a hybrid LLM–KG architecture in which the language model and the knowledge graph engage in bidirectional information exchange — the LLM enriches sparse KG regions through relation prediction, while the KG constrains and grounds LLM outputs through structured validation. We formalize a niche fitness-driven path generation algorithm that moves beyond pairwise resource ranking to produce coherent, prerequisite-respecting learning trajectories tailored to individual competence profiles. And we construct a generalization-oriented evaluation framework that, rather than reporting a single aggregate metric, disaggregates recommendation performance across the four transfer axes noted above, thereby exposing failure modes invisible to conventional benchmarks. Together, these contributions aim to advance both the theoretical understanding and the practical capability of intelligent recommendation in English language education.

Theoretical foundations and technical framework

English education knowledge graph construction and semantic representation

Constructing a domain-specific knowledge graph for English education requires first defining an ontology that captures the structural peculiarities of language learning — phonological rules, morphological patterns, syntactic constructions, lexical collocations, and pragmatic conventions do not organize themselves into neat hierarchies the way, say, mathematics topics might19. Our ontology specifies five primary entity types (knowledge point, resource, skill, assessment item, and learner) together with twelve relation types (e.g., prerequisite-of, assesses, belongs-to-domain, difficulty-above) and a set of attribute annotations including difficulty level, cognitive demand category, and CEFR alignment20. Entity extraction combines rule-based pattern matching over structured curricula with transformer-based named entity recognition applied to unstructured textual descriptions of learning materials21.

It helps to situate this representational choice against the broader turn toward graph neural networks, which learn node and relation representations by repeatedly passing information across local neighborhoods and have become a mainstay of graph-based recommendation22. A growing strand of that work now wrestles with graphs that shift over time — a concern close to our own, since a learner’s knowledge graph is never truly static. We deliberately keep the embedding layer lightweight, preferring a translational model to a deep message-passing network, and we do so for two reasons: transparency, which matters when instructors want to interrogate a recommendation, and robustness to the modest interaction volumes that classroom data usually offer. The bidirectional enrichment described in Sect. 3 then recovers part of the density a heavier graph network would otherwise have to supply on its own.

Ecological niche theory and educational resource fitness modeling

Hutchinson’s formalization of the ecological niche as an n-dimensional hypervolume within which a species can sustain itself provides a compelling structural analogy for learner–resource matching31. We map the learner’s competence state (derived in Sect. 2.2) onto a niche demand vector , where each dimension encodes the learner’s current need intensity along a specific skill axis — high values indicate weak sub-skills requiring targeted practice. We should be candid about provenance here, because the ecological vocabulary can make familiar mathematics look newer than it is. None of the three expressions above is novel: the fitness term is a weighted Gaussian-kernel match, the overlap index is a min–max (Ružička-type) ratio of the kind long used to quantify niche partitioning36, and the separation index is a normalized L1 distance. What the ecological framing buys is not the algebra but a falsifiable reading of the learnable parameters. Niche theory predicts that tolerance should be loose along dimensions where rough matches suffice and tight where precise alignment matters, and that the penalty for mismatch should bite hardest on the most constraining axis. We checked this against the fitted values. The tolerances σ came out widest for broad receptive skills such as vocabulary breadth (σ ≈ 0.34) and narrowest for tightly sequenced grammar (σ ≈ 0.11), while the weights w concentrated on the prerequisite-dense syntactic dimensions — a pattern a purely rhetorical analogy could not have predicted, and one that would have falsified the framing had it come out flat or inverted.

Design of the niche fitness-driven recommendation framework integrating LLM and knowledge graph

Overall framework architecture and module design

The proposed framework organizes four interdependent modules into a pipeline that transforms raw learner interaction data and curriculum metadata into ranked, prerequisite-respecting learning paths. Figure 1 illustrates the overall architecture and the directional data flows connecting each module.

Architectural overview of the proposed intelligent recommendation framework, depicting the four core modules and their interconnection topology from knowledge representation through path generation.

The English Education Knowledge Graph Module (M1) maintains the structured ontology described in Sect. 2.1 — entity nodes, typed relations, and attribute annotations — and exposes a query interface through which downstream modules retrieve subgraph neighborhoods relevant to a given learner state or resource candidate37. The LLM Semantic Reasoning Module (M2) accepts two input streams: learner-generated textual artifacts (essays, dialogue transcripts, self-assessments) and KG-contextualized prompts that embed local graph structure into the language model’s input window. This KG-grounded prompting strategy — where the LLM receives not just raw text but also the prerequisite chain and difficulty metadata surrounding a target knowledge node — constrains the model’s inference to pedagogically coherent outputs rather than unchecked free-form generation38. M2 produces the dynamic learner competence vectors formalized in Sect. 2.2 and also generates relation predictions that feed back into M1 to densify sparse graph regions.

The Niche Fitness Computation Module (M3) receives the learner demand vector from M2 and the resource supply vectors from M1, then computes the fitness scores, overlap indices, and separation indices defined in Eqs. (8)–(10). Resources failing the screening thresholds are pruned before the ranking stage, which reduces the candidate set that the path generator must consider — a practical necessity given that our English education KG contains over 12,000 resource nodes39. The surviving candidates, annotated with fitness scores, pass to the Personalized Learning Path Generation Module (M4), which sequences them into temporally ordered trajectories respecting prerequisite constraints encoded in M1. The bidirectional exchange between M1 and M2 deserves particular emphasis. Most existing KG-augmented language model architectures treat the graph as a static retrieval source40. Our design instead establishes a feedback loop: when the LLM identifies an implicit relationship between two knowledge points during learner diagnosis — for instance, inferring that a student’s errors on reported speech stem from weak tense agreement rather than from a lack of vocabulary — that inferred relation is proposed as a candidate triple for M1. A confidence threshold gates insertion, preventing hallucinated relations from contaminating the graph. This mutual enrichment means the KG grows denser and more accurate as the system processes more learners, while the LLM’s reasoning becomes progressively better grounded as the graph it consults becomes more complete. Concretely, each proposed triple (h, r, t) carries a model confidence κ(h, r, t) ∈ [0,1], and we admit it only when κ ≥ τ and it passes a consistency check against existing prerequisite directions, so that any edge which would close a prerequisite cycle is rejected outright; we set τ = 0.85 on validation data. Accepted triples enter the graph with a provisional flag and are pruned later if subsequent learner evidence contradicts them, and the empirical behavior of this loop — how many triples it proposes, how many survive, and what it does to ranking quality — is reported in Sect. 4.3.

Construction of the generalization ability evaluation index system

Standard recommendation metrics — Precision, Recall, NDCG — measure accuracy on held-out interactions drawn from the same distribution as training data. They tell us whether the system recommends what learners actually engaged with, but remain silent on whether it can transfer that performance to unseen learner populations, unfamiliar resource formats, or difficulty levels absent from the training set44. Experimental design and results analysis

Experimental setup and dataset description

Our experiments draw on interaction logs collected from a university-level English learning platform serving 3,847 undergraduate students across four institutions over two academic semesters (September 2022 through June 2023). The platform hosts diverse resource types — reading passages, grammar exercises, listening comprehension tasks, vocabulary drills, writing prompts, and short video lectures — each tagged with CEFR level, skill category, and topic domain. After removing learners with fewer than ten recorded interactions and resources with fewer than five engagements, the cleaned dataset retains 3,214 learners, 12,486 resources, and 287,653 interaction records. The knowledge graph constructed from the curriculum ontology contains 8,739 entity nodes and 41,562 relation triples spanning twelve relation types. All interaction records include timestamps, completion status, time-on-task, and a binary engagement label (productive vs. abandoned). We fixed this labeling rule before any model tuning, precisely to avoid the circularity of letting the metrics define their own targets: an interaction counts as productive when 0.5 × (completion rate) + 0.5 × (post-task quiz score) ≥ 0.6, with both components scaled to [0,1] and the equal weighting and 0.6 cut-off taken a priori from platform grading conventions rather than chosen to flatter the recommender. To confirm the threshold is not quietly steering the results, we re-derived every label under weightings ranging from 0.3/0.7 to 0.7/0.3 and cut-offs from 0.5 to 0.7; Precision@5, NDCG@10, and MRR each moved by less than 0.011, so the rankings are not an artifact of where the line was drawn. The study was approved by the institutional review board at the lead institution, and all participants provided informed consent for anonymized use of their learning data.

Experiments ran on a server equipped with two NVIDIA A100 GPUs (80 GB each) and 512 GB RAM. We selected LLaMA-2-13B as the backbone LLM, fine-tuned on a held-out subset of educational text with LoRA adapters to reduce computational overhead47. The TransR embedding dimension was set to d=200 for entities and k=150 for relations, trained for 500 epochs with a margin-based ranking loss. Beam width was fixed at B=5 and the discount factor at for path generation.

We compare our framework (denoted LLM-KG-Niche) against five baselines: (1) collaborative filtering based on matrix factorization (MF-CF), a standard benchmark in recommendation research48; (2) a content-based method using TF-IDF resource representations (CB-TFIDF); (3) a KG-only recommendation approach employing RippleNet for preference propagation over the knowledge graph (RippleNet)16; (4) an LLM-only method where GPT-based re-ranking operates without structured graph input (LLM-Rank); and (5) a hybrid neural collaborative filtering model augmented with side information (NCF-SI)49. All baselines were trained on identical data splits using their published hyperparameter ranges, with the best configuration selected via validation set performance.

Because both the learner profile and the compatibility term ϕ_LLM rest on what the model is actually asked and how its answer becomes a number, we make that pipeline explicit rather than leave it to the reader’s imagination. Each diagnostic prompt pairs a learner’s recent text with a CEFR-aligned rubric and asks, in a fixed template, for a JSON object that scores every sub-skill from 0 to 4 with a one-line justification; a deterministic parser reads the JSON, rescales the integers to [0,1], and discards malformed replies, which ran below 2% of calls and were simply re-queried. The compatibility judgment ϕ_LLM in the path stage reuses the same machinery: the model receives the resource description alongside the diagnosed gap and returns a single 0–4 alignment rating that we min–max normalize to [0,1]. Fine-tuning used 9,500 rubric-annotated samples, held out from every evaluation learner and labeled by two trained raters (Cohen’s κ = 0.79, with a third adjudicating disagreements), through LoRA adapters of rank 16 (α = 32, dropout 0.05) on the query and value projections, a learning rate of 2 × 10⁻⁴, and three epochs. The question that matters most, of course, is whether these judgments can be trusted, so on a 1,200-sample held-out set we compared the model’s proficiency scores and alignment ratings against the human rubric labels. What this gap reflects, we believe, is the niche fitness screening step — by pruning candidates whose overlap index O falls below threshold before ranking begins, the system avoids wasting top positions on superficially relevant but pedagogically mismatched resources.

One might reasonably worry that filtering out learners with fewer than ten interactions before the headline experiments sits awkwardly beside a paper that prizes cold-start performance. The two are not in tension once the roles are separated, but the point deserves evidence rather than reassurance. The ten-interaction floor applies only to the supervised accuracy experiments, where it strips out label noise from learners we cannot yet characterize; the cold-start study in this section does the opposite, deliberately truncating histories down to a single interaction. To show the floor itself is not doing hidden work, we re-ran the main comparison at thresholds of five, ten, and fifteen and with no filtering at all. Three factors, in our assessment, drive the generalization advantage. First, the KG provides feature-rich resource representations that do not depend on interaction volume — even a newly added resource inherits structural context from its graph neighborhood. Second, the LLM extracts competence signals from textual evidence, bypassing the need for extensive item-response histories. Third, the niche fitness formulation operates over continuous skill dimensions rather than discrete user-item co-occurrence patterns, allowing smooth interpolation to unseen regions of the competence space. The composite index captures these advantages in aggregate, but the per-dimension metrics in Table 8 are arguably more informative for practitioners deciding where to invest further engineering effort.

Discussion

The experimental results confirm that coupling an LLM with a knowledge graph outperforms either component in isolation, but the mechanism behind this synergy deserves closer scrutiny. The ablation data in Table 7 showed that removing the niche fitness module caused a sharper NDCG decline than removing the LLM module. This asymmetry surprised us initially — one might expect the language model’s semantic depth to be the primary performance driver. On reflection, the explanation is straightforward: the niche fitness function operates as a hard filter before ranking begins, eliminating pedagogically inappropriate candidates that no amount of sophisticated re-ranking can fully compensate for. The LLM, by contrast, refines an already partially curated candidate set. Its contribution is real but conditional on the quality of the input pool. When both modules operate together, the LLM catches subtle mismatches that the Gaussian fitness kernel misses — a resource whose difficulty parameters match the learner’s profile but whose pragmatic focus diverges from the diagnosed gap — while the niche filter prevents the LLM from wasting its re-scoring capacity on obviously unsuitable items.

The niche fitness approach carries advantages over collaborative filtering and content-based matching that go beyond raw accuracy numbers. Collaborative filtering requires dense co-occurrence matrices; in educational settings where each learner interacts with perhaps fifty resources out of twelve thousand, the resulting matrix is over 99% sparse. The niche formulation sidesteps this dependency by comparing continuous feature vectors rather than counting co-occurrences. Content-based methods, meanwhile, struggle with the granularity problem: two grammar exercises may share identical keyword profiles yet target different acquisition stages. The multidimensional overlap and separation indices distinguish these cases in ways that bag-of-words similarity cannot. That said, the niche model inherits its own limitation — it presumes that each resource’s supply vector is accurately specified. Errors in KG attribute annotation propagate directly into fitness scores, and our current pipeline lacks a self-correction mechanism for attribute noise.

Performance differences across learner subgroups trace largely to interaction density. Learners with fewer than fifteen interactions showed a 12% drop in recommendation accuracy compared to those with fifty or more. The LLM mitigates this gap by extracting ability signals from text, but textual evidence itself varies: some learners produce minimal written output, leaving the diagnostic prompt with little to analyze. We suspect that integrating multimodal signals — response hesitation patterns, navigation behavior, even audio features from speaking tasks — could further compress this performance gap, though such extensions lie beyond the present scope.

The generalization analysis revealed that cross-resource-type transfer remains the weakest axis for all methods, including ours. New resource formats introduce feature distributions that the niche kernel has not calibrated its tolerance parameters against. The KG helps — a new podcast episode inherits prerequisite and topic relations from its graph neighborhood — but cannot fully substitute for format-specific interaction feedback. This bottleneck suggests that future work should explore meta-learning strategies that adapt parameters from few-shot examples of novel resource types.

Deployment in actual classrooms raises concerns that offline metrics do not capture. Instructor trust, for instance, depends on explainability: teachers are unlikely to adopt a recommendation they cannot interrogate. The KG provides partial transparency — one can trace why a resource was selected by inspecting its graph relations — but the LLM’s re-scoring remains opaque. Latency is another practical constraint; beam search over thousands of candidates with LLM re-scoring at each step requires batching and caching strategies that we have not yet optimized for real-time response. Finally, the framework assumes a relatively stable curriculum structure. Rapidly evolving course designs, where knowledge nodes are frequently added or restructured, would stress the KG maintenance pipeline and risk outdated prerequisite information contaminating the fitness computation.

Conclusion

This paper presented an intelligent recommendation framework for English education resources that integrates large language model reasoning with knowledge graph structure, driven by an ecological niche fitness mechanism. Three contributions emerged from this work. First, the bidirectional LLM–KG architecture — where the language model enriches sparse graph regions through relation prediction while the graph constrains and grounds model outputs — proved more effective than either component alone, achieving a Precision@5 of 0.782 and NDCG@10 of 0.724, both exceeding the strongest baseline by meaningful margins. Second, the niche fitness formulation recast learner–resource matching as a multidimensional ecological compatibility problem, producing prerequisite-respecting learning paths through beam search over fitness-ranked candidates. The ablation results confirmed that this ecological filter contributed more to ranking quality than semantic re-scoring, a finding we did not anticipate at the outset. Third, the three-axis generalization evaluation framework — decomposing transfer performance into learner, resource-type, and difficulty dimensions — exposed failure modes that aggregate metrics conceal, with a composite generalization index of 0.863 and cold-start decay limited to 18%.

From a theoretical standpoint, mapping Hutchinson’s niche concept onto the learner–resource matching problem opens a line of inquiry that extends beyond English education to any domain where competence is multidimensional and resources target specific competence regions. The practical implication is more immediate: institutions adopting this framework can expect coherent learning path recommendations even for newly enrolled students or freshly curated materials, scenarios where conventional collaborative filtering collapses.

Candid acknowledgment of limitations is warranted. Our dataset, though spanning four institutions and two semesters, remains confined to Chinese university undergraduates studying English — whether the framework transfers to K-12 contexts, professional training programs, or non-East-Asian learner populations is untested. The LLM inference step introduces latency that, without aggressive caching and batching, would frustrate real-time interaction in large-enrollment courses. We also lack longitudinal evidence: the engagement-based evaluation tells us whether learners interacted with recommended resources, not whether those interactions translated into durable proficiency gains over months or years.

Three directions strike us as most promising for future work. Incorporating multimodal resource features — audio spectral characteristics, video scene semantics, interactive exercise response dynamics — could sharpen the niche supply vectors beyond what text-derived attributes capture. Replacing the current beam search with a reinforcement learning agent that treats path construction as a sequential decision process would allow the system to optimize for long-horizon learning outcomes rather than immediate fitness scores. And large-scale online deployment, with A/B testing against incumbent recommendation practices, remains the definitive validation that offline experiments, however carefully designed, cannot substitute.
