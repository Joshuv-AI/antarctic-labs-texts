Quantifying agricultural resilience under climate variability: a data-driven climate resilience index for European cereal systems
Authors: Anil Utku, Magdalena Radulescu, Abdulkadir Barut, Hind Alofaysan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractClimate variability and extreme weather events pose an increasingly serious threat to agricultural productivity in Europe. Although many studies have focused on the mean yield response to climate change, relatively few have attempted to offer a comprehensive, multi-dimensional view of agricultural resilience, separating long-term productivity trends from short-term climate-induced fluctuations. In this paper, a data-driven Climate Resilience Index is proposed to assess the resilience of European cereal systems under climate stress. Annual yield time series are decomposed into trend and anomaly components to identify interannual deviations driven by climate variability. Machine learning and deep learning algorithms, including RF, CatBoost, CNN, LSTM, TCN, and a TCN-LSTM hybrid model, are used to assess the predictive validity of climate extreme indices and persistence patterns. Among the evaluated models, the TCN-LSTM architecture achieved the highest predictive performance with 0.8347 R2, outperforming standalone temporal and ensemble-based models. The PCA-based exposure framework explained 68.4% of the total climate variability within the first principal component, indicating a highly concentrated climate-stress structure. Feature importance analyses further revealed that lagged yield anomalies and rolling volatility indicators were the dominant predictors of resilience dynamics. The findings show that temporal models perform better than traditional ensemble methods, emphasizing the significance of multi-year recovery mechanisms in anomaly evolution. To provide a consistent measure of exposure, principal component analysis is used to reduce correlated climate indicators into a multi-dimensional climate stress index. The CRI captures these three essential elements: climate exposure, yield variability, and recovery. The results at the country level show substantial variability across Europe, indicating that while exposure intensity is important, recovery dynamics are also critical to understanding resilience. The results also show that there are structurally distinct resilience profiles, and robustness tests indicate that country rankings are not sensitive to different weights and recovery functions. The results clearly show that recovery and adaptive capacity are critical to agricultural sustainability amid growing climate uncertainty. The CRI developed in this paper provides a clear, empirically valid framework for comparing resilience in agricultural systems.Keywords: Agricultural sustainability, Climate resilience, Yield anomalies, Cereal production, Deep learning, Principal component analysisSubject terms: Climate sciences, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Climate change has recently appeared as one of the most important structural issues facing the global agricultural sector1. Climate change is described by the rise in temperature, the occurrence of weather-related disasters, changes in precipitation, and an increase in droughts, which are currently influencing crop productivity variability in different continents2. In Europe, the growing of cereals, especially wheat, barley, and maize, is of prime significance for ensuring food security, livestock, and agro-industrial value chains3. Consequently, the resilience of cereal production systems to climate change has become a topical subject in scientific and policy circles.

Although different studies have investigated the effect of climate change on agricultural production, the majority of these studies have focused on the average effect of temperature or precipitation change on agricultural production4,5. While these studies are extremely enlightening, they tend to ignore the distinction between technological change and climate-related variability in the short term6. Moreover, resilience is usually quantified solely based on exposure metrics, which tend to ignore the multidimensional nature of recovery processes. Thus, there is a need to adopt an integrated approach that considers climate exposure, production sensitivity, and recovery processes in a unified quantitative framework7,8.

In this study, resilience is defined as production resilience, meaning the ability of cereal production systems to withstand any climatic disturbances, maintain functional yield stability, and restore themselves following yield disturbances. While the theoretical model for the study is based on the exposure-sensitivity-adaptive capacity approach of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, it defines resilience in terms of climate exposure, interannual yield volatility, and recovery from yield disturbance.

This paper addresses these research gaps by developing a Climate Resilience Index (CRI) for European cereal production systems based on a multi-step analytical framework. First, annual yield series are decomposed into trend and anomaly components to isolate climate-driven deviations from technological change. This detrending approach ensures that the resilience index is calculated from interannual variability instead of productivity change. Second, machine learning and deep learning approaches are employed to validate the predictive accuracy of climate-extreme variables and analyze the persistence of yield shocks. Third, a Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is used on the multivariate climate variables to build a statistically consistent exposure indicator. Fourth, the exposure indicator, yield variability, and recovery capacity are combined into a normalized composite indicator of agricultural resilience for each country.

The key contributions of the current research could be summarized as follows. Firstly, the proposed approach distinguishes between the long-term technological trend and the short-term climate-induced anomalies using an anomaly-based decomposition. Secondly, machine learning and deep learning methods were integrated to highlight the significance of extreme climatic events and their temporal dynamics. Thirdly, the PCA-based multivariate climate exposure index was estimated, and climate indicators were condensed into a statistically sound representation of climate stress. Lastly, the CRI approach integrates all three dimensions (exposure, volatility, and recovery) into a single resilience assessment framework.

In contrast to research that focuses on exposure as the key driver of vulnerability, this research emphasizes the critical role of recovery capacity and stability of production in determining resilience outcomes. The findings show that the adaptive mechanisms can reduce climate stress, which underlines the significance of improving institutions, technology, and infrastructure in agricultural policies.

The combination of anomaly decomposition, machine learning modeling, PCA, and composite index construction into a unified framework represents an advancement in the state-of-the-art of quantitative resilience analysis in agricultural systems. The CRI framework developed in this research is a novel, data-informed, and policy-relevant approach to assessing agricultural sustainability in the context of rising climate uncertainty. This section provides a review of the most pertinent literature on yield variability due to climate change, resilience assessment in agriculture, and climate-yield modeling using machine learning techniques, which underlie the methodology proposed in this paper.

Related works

The study on the effect of climate change on crop yield has been an ongoing process for the last two decades. The first large-scale study was conducted to understand the average yield response to temperature and precipitation change. Lobell and Field9 calculated the global yield response to warming and found that warming has a very negative effect on cereal yields. Lobell et al.10 concluded that heatwave events during the critical growth stages are responsible for a large share of yield reduction, particularly for wheat and maize. Similar findings were derived by Asseng et al.11, who concluded that wheat yields are very sensitive to temperatures above the optimal level during the growing stage.

Apart from average yield response, there has been a rising trend in the interest of interannual variability and yield anomalies. Ben-Ari and Makowski12 analyzed global crop yield variability and concluded that crop yields are not normally distributed but follow region-specific patterns affected by climatic extremes. Ray et al.13 further concluded that yield variability contributes to a large share of food production system instability, thus emphasizing the importance of anomaly modeling instead of average yield response.

Another area of research is the study of agricultural vulnerability and resilience using composite index methods. Smit and Wandel14 conceptualized vulnerability as a function of exposure, sensitivity, and adaptive capacity, which was later measured using composite indices. Wiréhn et al.15 analyzed the vulnerability of agriculture to climate change in Europe with composite indicators and found that weighting decisions have a significant impact on the outcome of ranking results. Williges et al.16 analyzed the adaptive capacity of European agriculture and found that institutional and structural differences have a significant impact on the differences in drought resilience among EU countries. These studies suggest the complexity of drought resilience and the need for appropriate index formulation and robustness analysis.

More recently, data-driven approaches have been explored to improve yield forecasting and climate impact analysis. Jeong et al.17 demonstrated that machine learning models, namely Random Forests (RF), are superior to regression models in capturing nonlinear climate-yield relationships on a continental domain. Khaki and Wang18 demonstrated that deep learning models, such as convolutional and recurrent neural networks, could improve the accuracy of yield forecasting by considering temporal and spatial dependencies.

The application of temporal deep learning models is particularly pertinent to the analysis of yield persistence and recovery. Shan et al.19 demonstrated that Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCNs) are generally more effective than traditional recurrent neural networks for most sequence modeling tasks, as they can effectively capture long-term effective memory using dilated convolutions. Hochreiter and Schmidhuber20 first introduced Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks to overcome long-term dependency issues in sequential data. Since then, LSTMs have been widely adopted in agricultural time series modeling.

Despite the increasing interest in applying machine learning to yield prediction, most existing studies tend to emphasize prediction accuracy rather than structural resilience evaluation. Few studies attempt to integrate anomaly decomposition, multidimensional exposure modeling, and composite resilience index computation into a unified framework. Moreover, although the vulnerability of European agriculture has been explored with indicator approaches15,16, most existing studies tend not to involve machine learning validation to evaluate the relevance of climate extremes and persistence.

Existing research typically concentrates on two categories: climate-yield forecasting or multi-dimensional vulnerability assessments. Nevertheless, very few have attempted to include the anomaly decomposition of yields, multivariate climate exposure assessment, temporal validation of deep learning algorithms, and the construction of resilience indices within a single coherent framework17,18. This paper aims to fill this knowledge gap by bringing together all four methodologies into a single climate resilience assessment framework for European cereals.

Material and method

This section presents the methodological framework that has been developed to construct and validate the CRI for European cereal production systems. The methodological framework is structured in a step-by-step procedure that integrates time series decomposition, machine learning modeling, multidimensional climate exposure definition, and composite index construction. First, annual cereal yield time series are decomposed to identify standardized yield deviations, thereby separating short-term climate variability from long-term technological change. Second, ensemble and deep learning models are employed to quantify the predictive weight of climate extreme indicators and temporal persistence patterns. Third, a PCA is carried out on correlated climate variables to derive a statistically consistent exposure index. Finally, exposure, yield variability, and recovery capacity are combined to construct a standardized composite CRI, and clustering and robustness analysis are carried out to identify structural resilience profiles and index stability. This integrated framework enables a dynamic, data-driven, and policy-oriented analysis of agricultural resilience to climate variability.

Dataset

This analysis will employ a harmonized panel dataset of European cereal production and climate variables from 1990 to 202221. The agricultural production dataset was collected at the country-crop-year level, and it includes annual yield (YIELD) data for the key cereal crops. The dataset includes three key cereals that are produced in Europe, and these are wheat, barley, and maize (corn). The dataset includes data for several European countries, and it is annual. Data preprocessing and hyperparameter configuration

Before proceeding with the estimation of the models, a preprocessing procedure was established to ensure consistency and comparability of data over time and across countries. The yield data was matched with the climate indicators at the country-crop-year level, and a harmonized panel dataset was created. Missing values in the yield data were deleted to maintain the integrity of the time series at the country level, while the climate indicators were maintained as reported in the WB_CCKP database. As the dependent variable was specified as standardized yield anomaly after trend decomposition, no additional transformation was required for the target variable. Predictor variables were scaled using statistics calculated solely from the training data to avoid look-ahead bias. These scaling parameters were then used to standardize validation and test datasets to maintain strict out-of-sample prediction. Outliers were deliberately retained, as extreme negative values correspond to climate shock events of primary interest in resilience analysis.

The data were temporally divided to maintain causal order. The model was trained on data from 1990 to 2016, validated on data from 2017 to 2019, and tested on data from 2020 to 2022. This approach avoids information leakage and allows the model to be evaluated based on its performance in a real-world forecasting scenario, rather than a random cross-sectional split.

Hyperparameters of ensemble models were determined using randomized search algorithms and validation-based optimization. In the RF model, a large number of trees were employed to reduce the variance associated with ensemble averaging, and tree depth was allowed to be sufficiently flexible to represent nonlinear relationships between climate and yield without over-pruning. In gradient boosting models, such as XGBoost and CatBoost, learning rates were set conservatively to improve generalization performance, and tree depths were moderately constrained to avoid overfitting. Subsampling methods were used in boosting models to improve robustness, and early stopping based on validation loss was employed to limit the number of boosting iterations.

Deep learning models were trained using the Adam optimizer with mean squared loss as the objective function. Model training was performed for a maximum number of epochs with early stopping when validation loss did not improve for a specified number of epochs. This ensured that the model converged to a set of optimal parameters without overfitting. The batch sizes were chosen to ensure a balance between computation and the stability of the gradient.

For sequence models, the rolling time windows were chosen to identify multi-year patterns of anomaly behavior. The sequence size was chosen to identify the medium-term effects of agricultural memory without going into the high-dimensional domain. The input variables were normalized to ensure stable gradient updates and faster convergence of the neural network.

The Convolutional Neural Network architecture was selected to detect local temporal information with one-dimensional convolutional layers and causal padding, followed by pooling and dense layers for regression tasks. The LSTM architecture was selected to detect long-term temporal patterns with multiple recurrent layers, along with dropout to mitigate overfitting. The Temporal Convolutional Network architecture employed dilated convolutions and residual connections to expand the receptive field. The hybrid TCN-LSTM model combined hierarchical feature extraction using dilated convolutions with recurrent memory processing to facilitate multi-scale temporal representation and long-term dependency modeling.

In all deep learning architectures, regularization methods such as dropout and validation-based early stopping were used. The choice of hyperparameters was based on validation performance rather than test-set optimization to avoid bias in the assessment. The approach to preprocessing and hyperparameter selection ensures methodological transparency, replicability, and comparability of ensemble and deep learning models with respect to the temporal nature of yield anomaly dynamics.

Trend decomposition and yield anomaly definition

To separate the short-term climate effects from the long-term technological change in agricultural production, the annual yield data were decomposed into trend and residual components. While STL can be effectively used to estimate seasonal cycles in seasonal data, the locally weighted regression approach used in STL makes it an effective means of separating out smooth, nonlinear trend elements from rough yearly fluctuations in annual data as well. The current analysis is not concerned with using STL to extract seasonality, but rather with separating the underlying long-term technology-driven trends from climate-induced short-term fluctuations in yield time series. Linear detrending may fail in this aspect, since trends can follow any pattern over time as long as technology, policies, and other factors continue to change.

PCA-based climate exposure index construction

To measure the climate exposure component of agricultural resilience, a composite climate exposure index was created through PCA. The aim was to distill the multidimensional character of climate extreme variables into a simple, interpretable form at the country level. The WB_CCKP dataset contains several climate indices that measure temperature and precipitation variability, drought-related variables, and extreme weather events. Since these variables are highly correlated, it would be redundant to include all of them in the composite index. PCA was used on the standardized country averages of all climate variables.

Before applying PCA, the climate variables were standardized to give equal weight to each variable. The cumulative explained variance of the principal components is represented in Fig. 7. The findings reveal that a few principal components explain most of the variation in the climate data. In particular, PC1 accounts for 68.4% of the total variance, indicating that one underlying factor for climate stress explains most of the variance among the climate variables. This implies that the exposure model is concentrated and can be represented in a lower-dimensional space. In combination, the first three principal components accounted for about 89.7% of the total variance, validating the effective representation of the multidimensional structure of the climate exposure in lower dimensions.

The cumulative explained variance of the principal components.

To understand the interpretation of PC1, the loadings were analyzed (Fig. 8). The largest absolute loadings are associated with indicators of climate extremes (e.g., temperature anomalies, drought indices, and precipitation variability). This state is further evidence that PC1 primarily reflects a common “climate stress” factor rather than individual climate fluctuations.

PC1 loadings.

Thus, the scores of PC1 were employed as the country-level climate exposure variable. A higher score indicates higher exposure to climate variability and disasters, while a lower score indicates relatively stable climate conditions. There are three key advantages of this PCA-based approach: dimensionality reduction, data-driven weighting, and cross-country comparability. In dimensionality reduction, a group of related climate variables is condensed into a single climate exposure variable. In data-driven weighting, the weights of the climate variables are assigned based on a data-driven statistical pattern, rather than human judgment. In cross-country comparability, standardization ensures that exposure scores are comparable between climatically diverse countries. The CRI benefits from the inclusion of PCA-based climate exposure, enabling a statistically significant and interpretable assessment of multidimensional climate risk.

To assess the stability of the exposure configuration over time, another robustness test was performed using a split-sample approach. Two separate principal component models were estimated using data from the years 1990 to 2005 and 2006 to 2022. The factor loadings and climate stress configurations were fairly similar across these two sub-periods, suggesting that the dominant exposure dimension, represented by the first principal component, remains stable despite changes in climate regimes. Although the gradual increase in temperature might influence the intensity of climate stress through time, the correlation structure among climate extremes remained fairly constant.

Climate resilience index

The CRI is intended to offer a country-level composite indicator of agricultural resilience to climate variability35. The index combines three different but complementary dimensions: climate exposure, yield volatility, and recovery capacity36. Climate exposure is the degree to which a country is affected by climate variability and related extreme events. Yield volatility is the degree of interannual yield variability. Recovery capacity is the ability of the agricultural system to recover from negative yield shocks. These three dimensions encompass the widely used exposure-sensitivity-adaptation capacity framework of resilience. The CRI is an indicator of resilience in agricultural production systems, but not socio-economic or ecological resilience. In essence, the CRI measures the resilience of cereal production systems by their capacity to cope with climate variability, maintain yield stability, and bounce back from shocks driven by climate change.

A negative yield shock was identified as a standardized anomaly . Z < − 1 was chosen because it reflects a deviation of more than one standard deviation below the average yield for each country-crop, thus reflecting significant shocks as opposed to mere variations within the time series. To ensure that the CRI did not rely heavily on this cut-off value, sensitivity tests were performed by applying two other criteria to define negative shocks: Z < − 0.5 and Z < − 1.5. The first defines shocks more broadly, while the second reflects only serious yield shocks. For each shock, the recovery path was analyzed for the following years.

Recovery capacity was quantified as the average number of years required for the standardized anomaly to return to zero after a negative shock. This element captures the system’s adaptability.

To facilitate comparability across different scales, the three dimensions of exposure, volatility, and recovery were standardized using z-scores. This standardization eliminates scale effects and ensures that all variables have a mean of zero and a unit variance.

Results

This section discuss the empirical findings of the research, focusing on the statistical characteristics of yield anomalies, the accuracy of machine learning and deep learning models, and the structural interpretation of feature importance. The findings will provide quantitative evidence for the climate sensitivity of European cereal production and will help to build the CRI.

Yield anomaly characteristics

The distributional properties of the standardized yield anomalies are presented in Fig. 9. The histogram indicates that the anomalies are symmetric around zero, as required, with moderate symmetry and strong tails. This is yet another sign that the detrending procedure was successful in removing the technological growth component without removing the interannual variability. About 82.6% of all the standardized outliers fell between − 1 and 1, but 6.8% of all the cases were characterized by strong negative disturbances (Z < − 1). The analysis shows that although significant yield shocks are rare, they are nonetheless crucial for assessing crop resilience.

The distributional characteristics of the standardized yield anomalies.

The co-presence of positive and negative tail variables suggests that European cereal crop yields are subject to strong short-run dynamics. The negative tails (for example, Z < − 1) represent strong yield shocks that can be linked to droughts, heatwaves, or rainfall events. The presence of tail events in each country further supports the use of a resilience strategy instead of a simple average productivity analysis.

The tail variables also suggest that extreme negative shocks are less likely but still possible, which is consistent with stress events caused by climate change and not structural failure. It is also important to recognize that the smooth distribution suggests that most country-year data points lie within one standard deviation, further supporting the use of anomaly-based shock identification thresholds.

The critical role of lagged anomaly variables also reinforces the hypothesis’s assumption about the persistence and memory properties of agricultural resilience processes. Specifically, the findings point to the importance of disturbances in past yields for future anomaly behavior, suggesting that climate shocks can persist for many years in succession. Moreover, the high importance of drought severity, temperature anomalies, and soil moisture deficit corroborates the multi-faceted character of climate-related stressors in the European cereal farming systems.

The above CRI results highlight the vital role of adaptive capacity and response systems in distinguishing between resilient and non-resilient agricultural systems. The resilience index presents a systematic approach to identify countries where adaptation investments, technological progress, or risk management policies may potentially provide the greatest resilience gains.

On the other hand, according to the silhouette coefficient analysis, the most suitable number of clusters is also four, considering the best trade-off between similarity within each cluster and dissimilarity among different clusters.

Robustness and sensitivity analysis

To test the methodological robustness of the CRI, a set of robustness and sensitivity tests was performed. The goal of these tests was to determine whether country rankings and structural results are sensitive to (i) alternative definitions of recovery and (ii) alternative weights.

First, the recovery capacity was redefined using an alternative anomaly definition that accounts for post-shock slope behavior and recovery timing. The new CRI formula was then compared to the baseline formula. The correlation between the baseline and alternative CRI rankings is presented in Fig. 16. The high correlation between the two rankings confirms that the overall resilience structure is not dependent on a particular definition of recovery.

The correlation between the baseline and alternative CRI rankings.

Second, a weight-sensitivity analysis was conducted by adjusting the weights assigned to the exposure, volatility, and recovery components. Several reasonable weight combinations were explored to represent varying policy emphases (e.g., focusing on exposure mitigation versus enhancing adaptive resilience). Within these alternative specifications, the derived CRI measures showed strong correlations and few significant reversals in rankings for top- and bottom-ranked countries. The results of the robustness analysis reveal three key implications: structural robustness, recovery importance, and policy robustness.

Furthermore, the influence of the CRI on the shock identification threshold was analyzed. Three thresholds were considered: Z < –0.5, Z < –1.0, and Z < –1.5. All these variations led to similar results concerning the ranking and scores of the resilience index in the selected countries. This shows that the identified major trends are not dependent on any arbitrary choice of shock threshold. The selected threshold, Z < –1.0, was retained for its ability to define a balanced concept by ignoring small changes while maintaining a reasonable number of shocks.

Structural robustness indicates that country rankings are not highly sensitive to moderate changes in the weights used to create the index. Recovery importance means that, regardless of weight changes, recovery capacity remains a key distinguishing factor in resilience profiles. Policy robustness indicates that CRI is a robust analytical tool for comparative resilience analysis and is not a function of arbitrary parameter adjustments. The results of the sensitivity analysis above further confirm the methodological soundness of the CRI approach. The resilience patterns that emerge consistently across alternative specifications lend strong support to the reliability of policy inferences and comparisons.

Further quantitative insights into the relative importance of methodology for CRI results have been obtained through another global sensitivity analysis using parameter perturbation tests similar to those used in elementary-effect sensitivity analysis techniques. Specifically, three major aspects of the methodology, namely (i) shock identification threshold, (ii) weighting of exposure, volatility, and recovery components, and (iii) trend smoothing setting in the process of anomaly decomposition, have been explored.

The results demonstrated that recovery-weighting options had the most pronounced impact on country ranking in the context of CRI scores, whereas shock identification threshold came next. Finally, moderate adjustments to trend-smoothing settings resulted in minimal changes to resilience rankings. However, irrespective of the parameter values used, the general resilience categories and relative country groups remained consistent.

Thus, the presented quantitative evidence demonstrates that the introduced CRI methodology is robust in its structure, and the observed resilience patterns are not simply a consequence of a particular parameter setting. Furthermore, the results highlight the critical importance of recovery dynamics in determining resilience to climate variation.

Discussion

As part of the further investigation into the external validity of the newly-proposed CRI framework, the scores for countries’ CRIs were compared with widely used indices for measuring countries’ resilience and vulnerability, such as the ND-GAIN index and the RIMA framework from FAO. It is worth noting that while both frameworks are more holistic and include indicators for socio-economic and governance aspects beyond agricultural production systems, there still appears to be a moderate positive correlation between the CRI and the benchmark resilience indices.

The correlation demonstrates that countries that score relatively high on resilience according to the CRI are expected to have higher overall scores in broader adaptation and resilience assessments. Nonetheless, the difference is significant, as the CRI is oriented towards measuring climate-induced crop yield variation, production instability, and the country’s capacity to recover from shocks.

The results of this study add to the existing body of knowledge on climate resilience in agricultural systems by combining anomaly-based analysis, machine-learning validation, and composite index construction within a single framework. The results show that agricultural resilience across European countries is extremely heterogeneous and cannot be explained solely by vulnerability to climate variability.

First, the anomaly-based decomposition shows that yield variability is large and episodic rather than structurally persistent. This is consistent with the existing literature, which shows that climate change is increasingly expressed through extreme events and variability rather than through uniform productivity declines. The decomposition of trend and anomaly components in this study helps to distinguish between technological change and climate sensitivity, thus providing a clearer understanding of resilience dynamics.

Second, the findings of the machine learning analysis offer empirical confirmation of the relevance of climate extremes and persistence effects. The success of temporal deep learning models, especially the hybrid TCN-LSTM model, indicates that the recovery process takes several years and has persistent patterns. This result is consistent with the dynamic view of resilience rather than a static one. The importance of lagged anomalies and volatility variables also suggests that vulnerability has a path-dependent component, such that countries that have recently experienced turbulence are more likely to experience future shocks.

Third, the PCA-based exposure part of the analysis confirms that climate stress is a multidimensional phenomenon. Instead of focusing on individual indicators (such as temperature alone), the exposure variable captures the interrelated patterns of extremes and variability. This aligns with the resilience framework, which focuses on the accumulation of systemic stress rather than individual hazard variables.

The CRI outcomes show that resilience is not exclusively geographically defined. Countries with similar levels of exposure can differ significantly in overall resilience due to differences in volatility and recovery capacity. This state confirms the view that adaptive capacity, especially the capacity to recover quickly from negative shocks, is a critical determinant of agricultural sustainability in a climate-stressed world. In certain instances, effective recovery mechanisms can partially offset high exposure, suggesting that adaptation spending can significantly reduce vulnerability.

The cluster analysis also emphasizes the structural typologies of resilience. Rather than analyzing countries one by one, analyzing them according to their exposure-volatility-recovery profiles can be a more practical approach to regional policy formulation. The presence of a structurally vulnerable group of countries with high exposure, high volatility, and low recovery capacity indicates that resilience gaps are not random but systemic.

The robustness tests also show that arbitrary parameter settings do not drive the CRI. The fact that country rankings remain stable across different definitions of recovery and weight settings enhances the index’s credibility. This state is especially important because composite indices are highly sensitive to normalization and weighting settings.

In general, the results are consistent with the resilience literature that highlights the importance of three interrelated processes: exposure reduction, sensitivity management, and adaptive recovery. Nevertheless, the results indicate that recovery capacity is disproportionately important in distinguishing resilient systems from vulnerable ones. It can be noted that the finding aligns with resilience theory in its approach to resilience, which is perceived not only as the absence of exposure but also as the ability of a system to endure disturbance, reorganize, and keep functioning despite shocks. In agriculture systems, in particular, it becomes extremely difficult to avoid exposure to climate variability because of its effects across vast geographical areas. As a result, the differences between resilient and non-resilient cases do not depend much on exposure; rather, they relate to the capacity of agricultural systems to recover, adapt, and stabilize their performance after being subjected to a shock. The concept of recovery capacity, viewed through a systems lens, concerns the availability of adaptive responses within a production system. Among such responses are technology flexibility, irrigation facilities, institutional backing, agricultural techniques, and agricultural knowledge. In general, all these responses are associated with the ability to diminish the impact of climate shock persistence. These results highlight the importance of shifting the focus from hazard protection to enhancing institutional, technological, and infrastructural capabilities that enable swift adjustment in the aftermath of the shock.

Conclusion

This research proposed a CRI to assess the resilience of European cereal production systems to climate variability. By using decomposition of yield trends, anomaly-based shock definition, machine-learning validation, principal component-based exposure measurement, and composite index construction, this research presents a comprehensive framework for assessing resilience. The hybrid TCN-LSTM model, among the architectures tested, achieved the highest accuracy (R2 = 0.8347, RMSE = 0.7816), highlighting the significance of temporal dependency in the study of agricultural resilience.

The empirical evidence presented in this research confirms that yield variability in Europe is episodic and large, thereby reiterating the need for interannual variability analysis rather than focusing on long-term productivity trends. The anomaly-based method effectively distinguishes climate-driven deviations from technological change, thereby offering a more robust framework for resilience analysis. The machine learning results confirm that climate extremes and persistence dynamics drive yield anomalies, and the deep learning architecture further identifies that recovery dynamics exhibit multi-year temporal structures. The analysis of feature importance also revealed that the most explanatory variable was the first-lagged anomaly at 0.327, followed by rolling yield volatility at 0.128, highlighting the significance of persistence and recovery effects in the generation of anomalies.

The PCA-based exposure element emphasizes that climate risk is, by nature, multidimensional, incorporating the correlations between temperature and precipitation extremes within a single framework. In isolation, the first principal component accounted for 68.4% of the climate’s variability, suggesting that the multidimensional climate stress structure could be captured by a low-dimensional exposure framework. Nevertheless, the CRI results clearly show that exposure alone is not a determining factor in resilience outcomes. Countries with similar levels of climate-related pressures can have vastly different levels of resilience, depending on their yield stability and recovery abilities. More specifically, recovery ability is shown to be a critical discriminating factor between resilient agricultural systems and inherently vulnerable ones. This has several implications for the role of adaptive mechanisms and post-shock dynamics in agricultural resilience over the long term.

The robustness and sensitivity tests also offer evidence that the CRI approach is independent of the selection of arbitrary parameters. The country rankings are robust to different definitions of recovery and weight adjustments, which is important for the methodological validity of the index. Ranking correlations from the sensitivity analysis ranged from 0.87 to 0.91 across various threshold values and weighting schemes, indicating the structural robustness of the new index. This is particularly important from a policy point of view, as composite indices have been criticized for their sensitivity to normalization and weighting decisions.

From a policy-making perspective, the results indicate that building adaptive capacity could be more advantageous for long-term agricultural resilience than focusing exclusively on exposure reduction. While it might not always be possible to avoid being exposed to climate variability, it is clear that resilience depends significantly more on absorbing shocks, maintaining stability, and recovering rapidly than on exposure to climate variability in European cereal production systems.

In practice, the results show that, despite exposure to the same climatic pressures, nations may exhibit very distinct resilience responses based on their capacity for recovery and stability. The findings imply the significance of institutional preparation, technology, and risk management in building resilience. Improving water-use efficiency, adopting climate-smart agricultural practices, diversifying crops, developing drought-resistant seeds, and implementing insurance systems and early warning mechanisms may help minimize long-term vulnerability.

Moreover, the significant predictive effect of persistence factors indicates that climate shocks can create multi-year impacts on agricultural output. In this regard, agricultural resilience strategies must consider more than just immediate disaster prevention; they should also enhance post-disaster recovery measures and adaptive capabilities.

Such insights indicate that sustainable agriculture amid climate unpredictability is not only contingent on minimizing climate vulnerability but also on enhancing the system’s ability to recover, restructure, and adapt after climate disruption. Hence, future agricultural policy could consider prioritizing adaptation and resilience development over climate protection.

While this research provides a broad and comparative framework, future research might profit from the further extension of this framework by the addition of sub-national data, socio-economic adaptation strategies, and further information on climate data. The research on strategies of causal modeling could also help to further disentangle the effect of climate change from agricultural structural trends. In summary, the CRI framework presented in this research provides a clear, empirically grounded, and policy-relevant framework for analyzing agricultural resilience in the face of increasing climate uncertainty. Furthermore, the results indicate that resilience outcomes depend not only on exposure levels but also on recovery capabilities and the persistence of climatic stresses in the agricultural sector over time. By integrating exposure, volatility, and recovery into a unified framework, the CRI improves the quantitative analysis of agricultural resilience.
