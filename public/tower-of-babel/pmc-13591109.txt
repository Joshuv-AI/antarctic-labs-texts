From Innovation to Implementation: A Scoping Review of Simulation in Otolaryngology Education
Authors: Michael T Yim, Mackenzie Latour, Srivatsa Surya Vasudevan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesTo systematically map the otolaryngology simulation literature according to learner stage, primary educational objective, and depth of outcome measurement, and to propose a structured implementation framework for integrating emerging technologies and developing standardized, national simulation curricula.MethodsA scoping review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA‐ScR). PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, and Web of Science were searched for studies published from January 2010 through August 2026 describing simulation used for education, training, or assessment in otolaryngology. Included studies were charted across three domains: learner stage (medical students, residents, practicing otolaryngologists), primary educational objective (exposure, acquisition, refinement, assessment), and outcome depth (face/content validity, pre–post or construct validity, behavior/clinical correlation). Evidence from adjacent surgical specialties and emerging technologies was synthesized narratively to inform a stepwise implementation framework.ResultsOf 9081 records identified, 979 unique studies met inclusion criteria, yielding 1142 learner entries. Simulation efforts were heavily concentrated in resident education (56.0% of learner entries) and skill acquisition (53.0%), with skill refinement concentrated among practicing otolaryngologists and almost no studies designed primarily for assessment. Face or content validity remained the most common highest measured outcome (53.7%); behavior‐level outcomes were rare (1.1%). These distributions demonstrate insufficient alignment between learner stage, educational intent, and validation depth. Lessons from adjacent surgical specialties, together with emerging technologies such as artificial intelligence and extended reality, offer a practical pathway toward scalable, objective assessment.ConclusionsOtolaryngology is poised to advance simulation education beyond historically fragmented initiatives toward a deliberately designed simulation ecosystem aligned with learner level, educational intent, and outcome depth. A coordinated national framework that leverages emerging technologies while building on existing infrastructure, implemented through phased pilot programs and attentive to cost, faculty, and technical barriers, can position the field as a leader in surgical education.Keywords: curriculum development, otolaryngology education, scoping review, simulation‐based training, surgical simulation

Introduction

Simulation in otolaryngology has rapidly expanded over the past decade, driven by the growing diversity of otolaryngologic procedures and the rapid evolution of technology. Advances in both 3D printing and computer‐based systems have enabled higher fidelity training activities. Traditional apprenticeship models are increasingly insufficient in an era of rapidly evolving medicine and surgical techniques [1]. Despite this growth, simulation efforts within otolaryngology often lack alignment between learner level, educational intent, and outcome measurement, which has limited the overall impact.

Simulation is now a widely implemented training tool across various nonmedical industries. Flight simulation represents one of the most mature and well‐documented applications of how simulation can deliver measurable benefits in safety, cost reduction, improved training effectiveness, and optimization [2]. Other industries have demonstrated that simulation utility expands when embedded within a coordinated infrastructure rather than deployed as isolated technology [3]. In otolaryngology, realizing this potential will similarly require intentional design and integration.

Simulation studies are typically evaluated across four domains of validity: face (perceived realism), content (inclusion of essential components), construct (ability to distinguish between novice and expert performance), and predictive (translation to clinical competence). Training effectiveness is also frequently assessed, often described using the Kirkpatrick framework. While higher level outcomes such as behavioral change or clinical impact are ideal, they are often impractical due to confounding variables and resource constraints. A realistic and achievable standard for most studies is demonstration of pre‐post performance improvement and, when feasible, construct validity. Despite this, much of the existing literature remains limited to subjective and descriptive outcomes such as participant satisfaction or perceived realism, oftentimes regarding the model rather than the skill or task at hand. This discrepancy reflects a broader issue within the described simulation literature: misalignment between the intended educational purpose and the methods used to evaluate it.

This scoping review was therefore undertaken with three objectives: (1) to systematically map the published otolaryngology simulation literature according to learner stage, primary educational objective, and depth of outcome measurement; (2) to identify translatable lessons from adjacent surgical specialties that have implemented simulation at a national scale; and (3) to propose a structured implementation framework, including anticipated barriers, timelines, and pilot program designs to guide the next phase of simulation in otolaryngology.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

A scoping review design was selected because the purpose of this study was to characterize the breadth and organization of the otolaryngology simulation literature, mapping how simulation is deployed across learner stages, educational objectives, and outcome measures, rather than to aggregate simulator characteristics or pool effect sizes. The review was conducted according to the five‐stage framework described by Arksey and O'Malley [4] and is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA‐ScR) (Table S1) [5].

Eligibility Criteria

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they (1) were peer‐reviewed original investigations published in English from January 1, 2010 through August 2026; (2) described a simulation modality, including synthetic or physical task trainers, 3D‐printed models, cadaveric models, computer‐based or extended reality systems, or hybrid approaches, applied to an otolaryngologic procedure or skill; (3) involved human learners at the level of medical student, resident or fellow, or practicing otolaryngologist; and (4) reported at least one educational or assessment outcome. Studies were excluded if they were review articles, editorials, commentaries, or case reports; were conference abstracts without an associated full text; described patient‐facing rather than learner‐facing simulation; addressed nonotolaryngologic procedures; included multiple learner groups without reporting outcomes separately for each group, precluding attribution of results to a specific learner stage; or reported no educational or assessment outcome.

Information Sources and Search Strategy

A search strategy was developed combining controlled vocabulary and free‐text terms for otolaryngology and its constituent procedures with terms for simulation, training models, and education. PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, and Web of Science were searched using equivalent, database‐adapted syntax. The full search strategy for each database is provided in Table S2. Reference lists of included studies and relevant prior reviews [6, 7] were hand‐searched to identify additional eligible articles.

Selection of Sources of Evidence

Records were exported into Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia) and duplicates were removed. Titles and abstracts were screened against the eligibility criteria, and full texts were reviewed when the abstract was insufficient to determine eligibility. To mitigate the risk of single‐reviewer selection bias, all records were screened in two independent passes separated by a two‐week interval, with discrepant decisions resolved by full‐text review.

Data Charting

A standardized charting form was used to code each included study across three domains. Learner stage was categorized as medical student, resident, or practicing otolaryngologist. Primary educational objectives were categorized as exposure (orientation to a procedure or anatomy), acquisition (development of new technical skills), refinement (improvement of existing skills), or assessment (measurement of competency). Outcome depth was categorized as face/content validity, pre–post performance improvement or construct validity, or behavior/clinical correlation, and was classified according to the highest level explicitly measured. Behavior/clinical correlation, the highest tier, was assigned only when a study measured transfer to, or correlation with, real‐world surgical performance or patient care. For example, operating‐room performance formally assessed after simulation training, correlation of simulated performance with intraoperative findings or postoperative imaging, or patient‐specific simulation used to plan or rehearse an actual operation that was subsequently performed. Self‐reported confidence or perceived readiness for clinical practice was not accepted as evidence of behavior‐level outcome. Because this tier carries the greatest evidentiary weight, every candidate behavior‐level assignment was individually re‐verified against the source abstract or full text before final classification. Studies that included multiple learner groups were coded separately for each learner group represented, allowing the analysis to reflect the full distribution of simulation efforts across training levels. Charting was performed primarily at the abstract level, with full texts consulted when abstracts were incomplete or ambiguous.

Synthesis of Results

Consistent with scoping review methodology, no formal critical appraisal of individual sources was performed [5]. Descriptive statistics (absolute counts and percentages) were calculated for each domain, and two‐dimensional heatmaps were constructed to visualize the distribution of studies across learner stage, educational objective, and outcome depth. Temporal trends were examined by tabulating annual publication counts stratified by outcome depth, and the annual proportion of studies reporting objective outcomes (pre–post/construct or behavior/clinical correlation) was calculated to assess whether validation depth has changed over time. Analyses were performed in GraphPad PRISM version 10; consistent with scoping review methodology, no inferential statistical testing was undertaken. Evidence regarding national simulation programs in adjacent surgical specialties and emerging simulation technologies was synthesized narratively to contextualize the mapping results and inform the proposed implementation framework.

Results

Study Selection

The database searches identified 9081 records (PubMed/MEDLINE, n = 2958; Embase, n = 3014; Web of Science, n = 3109); hand‐searching of reference lists identified no additional eligible studies. After removal of 4704 duplicates, 4377 records underwent title and abstract screening, and 3001 were excluded. The remaining 1376 full‐text articles were assessed for eligibility, of which 397 were excluded, most commonly because educational outcomes were not stratified by learner group (unstratified learners, n = 190), followed by noneducational review articles (n = 99), conference abstracts without an associated full text (n = 65), and case reports (n = 43). A total of 979 unique studies met inclusion criteria. Because studies that included multiple learner groups were coded separately for each learner level represented, the final analytic dataset consisted of 1142 learner entries. Distribution Across Learner Stage, Educational Objective, and Outcome Depth

Simulation efforts were heavily concentrated in resident education (640 of 1142 learner entries; 56.0%), followed by practicing otolaryngologists (324; 28.4%) and medical students (178; 15.6%). The dominant educational objective overall was skill acquisition (605 entries; 53.0%), followed by skill refinement (306; 26.8%) and exposure or orientation (230; 20.1%), with only a single entry directed primarily toward assessment (Figure 2). Acquisition‐focused work was concentrated among residents (416 of 605 acquisition entries), whereas skill refinement was the leading objective among practicing otolaryngologists (176 of 324 practicing‐otolaryngologist entries), reflecting dissection courses, patient‐specific rehearsal, and continuing professional development.

2D heatmap of learner stage and educational objective in otolaryngology simulation.

Outcome depth analysis demonstrated that face or content validity remained the most common highest measured outcome (613 entries; 53.7%). A substantial subset evaluated pre–post performance improvement or construct validity (516; 45.2%), most frequently within resident populations (275 entries). Behavior‐level or clinical‐correlation outcomes remained rare across all learner groups (13 entries; 1.1%) (Figure 3).

Temporal Trends in Publication Volume and Validation Depth

Annual publication volume increased more than fourfold over the study period, from 23 studies in 2010 to a peak of 101 studies in 2025, with a further 78 studies indexed through August 2026 (Figure 4). This growth was driven predominantly by face/content‐level reports, which constituted the largest outcome stratum nearly every year. Behavior‐level or clinical‐correlation outcomes never exceeded two studies in any single year. The annual proportion of studies reporting objective outcomes (pre‐post/construct or behavior‐level) ranged between 27% and 58% without a sustained trend for most of the period, although highest in recent years (52% in 2025 and 59% in 2026 to date).

Discussion

This scoping review mapped more than 16 years of published otolaryngology simulation literature and found that, while simulation has expanded substantially in volume, its deployment remains concentrated in early instructional phases. Simulation in otolaryngology encompasses a wide range of modalities, including synthetic task trainers, computer‐based systems, cadaveric models, and hybrid approaches, and these efforts have been supported by initiatives such as regional boot camps and increasing representation at national meetings [6, 7]. However, expansion has often been uncoordinated, with technology frequently applied retroactively rather than driven by predefined educational needs. There is significant room for growth in developing simulation intentionally designed for skill refinement and assessment.

Simulation in Other Medical Specialties—Lessons and Potential Adaptations

There are lessons to be learned from adjacent surgical specialties who have already harnessed simulation at a national level. General surgery has long been the leader in both standardization of simulation training and utilizing surgical simulation to assess competency. The Fundamentals of Laparoscopic Surgery (FLS) and Fundamentals of Endoscopic Surgery (FES) programs combine standardized curricula with technical assessment and are required for board eligibility [8, 9]. Where general surgery has excelled in developing a scalable simulation tool with standardized outcomes for benchmark assessment, neurosurgery has excelled in nationalizing a bootcamp curriculum, with the Society of Neurological Surgeons (SNS) launching a comprehensive national curriculum in 2010 [10]. This was an impressive and unprecedented coordinated educational effort that included participation from 94% of all U.S. neurosurgical PGY1 residents in its first year. The success of this program led to the addition of a second national course for PGY2 residents, concentrating on more complex, integrated surgical tasks, emergency scenarios, and nontechnical skills around communication and leadership [11].

Otolaryngology has long been a leader in both technological innovation and surgical education, positioning the specialty to build upon coordinated simulation paradigms established elsewhere while advancing them further. The timing is particularly advantageous, as emerging technologies now offer opportunities to develop more sophisticated and scalable approaches to simulation implementation.

Emerging Technologies and Future Potential

With the rapid evolution and deployment of artificial intelligence, otolaryngology as a field must endeavor to be on the leading edge of harnessing AI to enhance surgical training. This can be accomplished in multiple ways. The first would be to use AI to develop personalized training pathways tailored to individual performance and knowledge gaps. The system would allow for real‐time feedback and adjust difficulty levels based on learner competency. There is some preliminary evidence supporting the use of AI tutoring over traditional instructors, as AI is better able to call attention to learner goals and set measurable performance objectives [12].

For task trainers and technical simulators, AI could soon provide performance analytics through tracking trainee movements, outcomes, and eventually identifying areas of improvement. Machine learning (ML) algorithms can objectively stratify surgical skills by level of experience by learning specific differences between experts and novices. It is not an unreasonable extrapolation that similar ML algorithms be implemented into autonomous simulations soon, leading to self‐directed training with goal‐oriented feedback that is both objective and personalized for trainees. The early studies in this area are predominantly, and unsurprisingly, from general surgery [13] and neurosurgery [14].

Another major category of potential lies in the realm of extended reality (XR) technologies, encompassing augmented reality (AR), virtual reality (VR), and mixed reality (MR). Two recent systematic reviews evaluated the use of XR in surgical and procedural training, with both studies concluding that XR technologies can significantly improve surgical training by increasing technical skill development compared to standard training methods [15, 16]. Importantly, otolaryngology‐specific evidence for these technologies, while early, is accumulating. Virtual reality platforms have shown promise for teaching head and neck anatomy to junior learners [17], and virtually augmented surgical navigation has been shown in a prospective repeated‐measures trial to reduce simulated complications and improve efficiency during endoscopic sinus surgery training [18]. Simulation‐based continuing education has likewise been extended to practicing otolaryngologists for complex frontal sinus surgery [19]. These studies suggest that the technological building blocks for a modern otolaryngology simulation ecosystem already exist within the specialty; what remains is coordinated validation and integration.

When taken together, these established foundational components have evident theoretical benefits in training, in practice, and for educators themselves, and point toward the enormous growth potential for surgical simulation in our field. However, practical and technological capability alone does not ensure educational impact. Without alignment between learner stage, educational objective, and outcome depth, even sophisticated simulators may end up missing the mark.

Barriers to Adoption and Implementation

Realizing this potential requires a candid accounting of the barriers that have historically limited simulation uptake, and that will shape adoption of newer technologies. The first is cost. High fidelity simulators, XR platforms, and AI‐enabled analytics carry substantial acquisition expenses, and the less visible costs of maintenance, consumables, software licensing, dedicated space, and support personnel are recurrent and frequently underestimated. These costs fall unevenly across residency programs, and without deliberate planning risk widening the gap between well‐resourced and smaller programs. The second is fragmented organization. Adoption is hindered by a lack of coordinated leadership and trusted, accessible standards for implementation and evaluation. Lengthy assessments, competing demands on faculty time, and the absence of standardized outcome metrics make participation difficult to sustain, while limited institutional coordination has prevented simulation from becoming an integrated and credible component of surgical education. Assessment fatigue among faculty raters is a related and well‐recognized limitation of scaling structured assessment. The third is technical literacy. AI‐ and XR‐based platforms presuppose a degree of comfort with the underlying technology that cannot be assumed among faculty or trainees, and effective deployment requires onboarding, troubleshooting support, and iterative refinement. Finally, AI‐driven performance analytics introduce considerations of data governance, learner privacy, and algorithmic transparency that training programs have limited experience navigating. These barriers are addressable through shared national resources, faculty development and protected educational time, low‐cost model options, and phased technology adoption, but only if they are anticipated in the design phase rather than discovered during implementation.

Rethinking the Standard in Surgical Training Research: Author's Perspective

It is the opinion of the authors that this scoping review highlights a need to reconsider how simulation research is prioritized, evaluated, and ultimately how it is implemented. Surgical simulation studies are frequently expected to satisfy the highest standards of scientific rigor, including randomized controlled designs, multi‐institutional enrollment, and outcomes spanning multiple levels of educational impact. This expectation stands in contrast to the development and adoption of traditional surgical training. The apprenticeship model, often summarized as “see one, do one, teach one”, has remained the dominant paradigm for more than a century despite never having been required to demonstrate its efficacy through similarly rigorous study. Its continued use largely reflects historical precedent, even though the educational experience it provides varies considerably according to mentorship, subjective assessment, case availability, and local training opportunities.

Simulation should be evaluated rigorously, but the required level of evidence should be proportionate to the decision being made. The level of evidence sought should consider the significance of potential application and adoption, including risk and benefit to others. Demonstration that an intervention improves skill acquisition, provides meaningful educational value, and can be feasibly incorporated into training should generally be sufficient to justify its use in the educational realm. The field must avoid allowing the pursuit of ideal evidence to become a barrier to implementing interventions with clear educational benefit.

More importantly, continued expansion of resource‐intensive validation studies may yield diminishing returns. Multi‐institutional studies requiring continuous enrollment over several years demand substantial coordination and effort yet may still produce inconclusive statistical findings because of small cohorts, institutional heterogeneity, and the practical constraints inherent to surgical education research [20]. These limitations do not necessarily indicate that the intervention lacks value. Rather, they may reflect the difficulty of applying conventional clinical research frameworks to educational questions for which outcomes are complex, sample sizes are limited, and experimental control is often unrealistic.

The central challenge facing surgical simulation is therefore no longer simply determining whether deliberate practice improves performance. As in athletics, repeated, structured practice of a complex motor task would reasonably be expected to improve proficiency, and observational data can and should be accepted [21]. Although meaningful evaluation remains necessary, the field should increasingly direct its finite resources toward the more consequential and less frequently studied questions of implementation: how simulation can be integrated into curricula, adopted across institutions, supported by faculty, delivered efficiently, and sustained over time. The goal should not be to lower the scientific standard for simulation research, but to align research effort with the questions most likely to advance surgical training.

A Structured Implementation Framework

The scoping review identified the core issue with prior efforts: a lack of alignment between what is taught, who is taught, and how trainees are assessed. The first step is to intentionally select the procedures to be simulated, as not all surgical procedures merit simulation. While simple task trainers are beneficial for early trainees, more advanced procedure selection should be based on educational yield such as high‐risk anatomy, steep learning curve, limited case exposure, or poor tolerance for error. Procedures that do not fit any of these categories should not be simulated. Once an appropriate procedure is identified, educational intent must be defined in order to direct simulation development. A single simulator does not need to serve all three learner groups. Similarly, attempting to design a single model which simultaneously addresses exposure, acquisition, refinement, and assessment risks diluting its effectiveness. As such, each simulator should be designed around a single primary intent. Then the fidelity of the simulation should match the targeted learner and intent, rather than assuming higher fidelity is universally beneficial. For example, a simulation targeting medical students for exposure to basic nasal endoscopy skills can be accomplished with a simple task trainer created with gelatin and readily available materials [22]. On the other hand, a simulation targeting residents to acquire skills in a technically challenging procedure such as an endoscopic nasoseptal flap harvest and placement would benefit more from a high‐fidelity model with a simulated cerebrospinal fluid leak that has measurable outcomes [23]. Once the learner, intent, and fidelity are aligned, the next step is to define a standardized and intuitively meaningful outcome measure, such as improvement from baseline or achievement of a predetermined proficiency benchmark, with correlation to clinical performance encouraged when feasible.

There is significant room for growth to elevate the utilization of otolaryngology simulation at both the skill acquisition and assessment levels. At the skill acquisition level, there is already enough preexisting infrastructure in place to quickly ramp up efforts in this realm. One method is to integrate simulation through a standardized national curriculum. With the launch of the Otolaryngology Core Curriculum (OCC) in 2024, this provides an obvious opportunity to expand simulation offerings at the national level through a pre‐existing platform that is currently being used by greater than 90% of otolaryngology programs nationwide. A second method of expanding skill acquisition simulation would be to nationalize current regional boot camp efforts. A national boot camp guideline containing centrally defined curricula, recommended modules and detailed setup instructions, cost considerations, and assessment frameworks would leverage current resources and optimize simulation training offerings throughout the country. Given regional differences, implementation should include flexibility for local sites to adopt and deliver tailored simulation experiences to their trainees' needs. Given the high costs often associated with simulation infrastructure and maintenance, sustainable funding models must also be a priority. A multifaceted strategy including local institutional investment, support from national societies, and collaboration with industry will be essential to ensuring long‐term program viability and access for all residency programs. Proposed Implementation Timeline and Pilot Program Design

To translate this framework into practice, a phased national timeline is proposed. In Phase 1, a national needs assessment would be conducted through the OCC platform and residency program directors to prioritize procedures according to the educational‐yield criteria described above and to inventory existing regional boot camp resources. In Phase 2, a small number of standardized simulation modules, each designed around a single learner stage and a respective educational intent, would be piloted across a purposive sample of 8–10 residency programs selected to represent geographic and resource diversity. Each pilot would prospectively define its standardized endpoint in advance (pre–post performance improvement at minimum, with construct validity where expert comparison groups are available), alongside feasibility endpoints including per‐learner cost, faculty time burden, and learner and faculty acceptability. In Phase 3, modules meeting predefined performance and feasibility thresholds would be scaled nationally with longitudinal tracking of learner progression to begin building the behavior‐level outcome data that the present scoping review found to be largely absent. This deliberately incremental design keeps the scope of study focused, consistent with the proportionate evidentiary standard argued above, while building toward coordinated national infrastructure.

Application of the Implementation Framework: Functional Endoscopic Sinus Surgery

To illustrate how the proposed implementation model might function in practice, functional endoscopic sinus surgery (FESS) is a good example for a representative case study and will be applied to the model in a stepwise fashion.

Procedure Selection

FESS is a core otolaryngologic procedure that satisfies multiple criteria for simulation prioritization based on educational yield. There is high risk anatomy given the proximity to the orbit and skull base. Driving with the endoscope with one hand and operating with the other hand provides a steep early learning curve, as spatial orientation and instrument handling require deliberate practice. And there is low tolerance for error, as complications can be life or sight threatening.

Defining Educational Intent

Under the proposed framework, educational intent should vary by learner stage. For medical students, the primary goal should be exposure and orientation. Simulators should aim to develop spatial understanding of sinonasal anatomy and to familiarize learners with driving a rigid endoscope. The expected outcome is knowledge acquisition and self‐reported confidence. For residents, the goal should be skill acquisition. Here, simulation would develop stepwise dissection skills and encourage endoscopic coordination. Recognition of critical anatomic areas (skull base, orbit) would also apply. For attending physicians, the intent should be geared toward self‐assessment and skill refinement. Objectives would center around improving efficiency and optimization, with specific modules potentially developed for refreshers for rarer or feared surgical situations and for safety in adaptation of new technologies or techniques.

Matching Fidelity to Learner and Intent, and Validation Standards

For medical students, a simple task trainer is sufficient in developing early endoscopic driving skills [24]. Virtual reality simulation can also be helpful to learn head and neck anatomy [17]. For residents, higher fidelity 3D printed models provide excellent face and content value, and pre–post intervention evaluation can provide excellent skill acquisition through repetitive, structured practice [25]. For skill refinement, virtually augmented surgical navigation has been shown to decrease complications during simulated FESS and improve surgical efficiency [18]. For practicing otolaryngologists, high fidelity simulation with expert demonstration is helpful for enhancing skills for complex frontal sinus surgery, enabling experienced surgeons to refine technical judgment and support lifelong learning [19]. The routine implementation of realistic models and intelligent technology throughout surgical training directs future aims toward potential development of an assessment‐oriented FESS simulator which independently measures and guides learning in surgical precision, accuracy, and efficiency.

Embedding FESS Simulation Into Training Infrastructure

The effectiveness of FESS simulation depends less on technological sophistication than on structural integration. Simulation initiatives often underperform because they are implemented as adjunct workshops rather than embedded components of surgical training. In early residency, simulation should function as structured exposure and skill acquisition prior to substantive operative participation, ideally incorporated into introductory rhinology rotations. While FESS is likely too technical of a procedure to include in boot camps, early exposure to nasal endoscopy and adjacent skills such as epistaxis control would set an excellent foundation for subsequent FESS training. As residents progress, simulation would transition toward deliberate practice and performance refinement before advanced cases, with objective metrics used to track progression longitudinally.

Limitations

This review is not without limitations. First, the scoping review characterizes the published literature, not the totality of simulation education in otolaryngology. Many residency programs conduct locally developed simulation activities, boot camp stations, cadaveric courses, and departmental task‐trainer sessions that are never submitted for publication, so the distributions reported here should not be read as a census of simulation activity across training programs nationwide. Second, and relatedly, the very constraints this review highlights in simulation research of small learner cohorts, limited resources, confounded outcomes, and the difficulty of achieving statistical significance in educational studies, may themselves discourage or preclude publication. The resulting publication bias likely skews the visible literature toward novel simulator descriptions with feasibility‐level outcomes and may partially explain the predominance of face and content validity observed. Third, analysis was performed primarily at the abstract level, which may underestimate outcome depth when higher‐level outcomes were reported only within full texts. Finally, the proposed implementation framework is anchored in U.S. training infrastructure, the OCC, regional boot camps, and American board certification structures, and its specific recommendations may not generalize directly to other countries' training systems. The underlying principles of the framework, however; deliberate procedure selection, alignment of fidelity with learner and intent, and proportionate validation standards, are system‐agnostic and adaptable to many international contexts.

Conclusion

The future of otolaryngology education lies in embracing the transformative potential of advanced simulation technologies while learning from the successes and challenges of other medical specialties. The path forward requires coordinated effort through national organizations to overcome existing barriers and create sustainable, innovative training programs. Through systematic implementation of simulation strategies, standardization of assessment protocols, and investment in emerging technologies, the field of otolaryngology is set to define a new forward‐thinking standard for surgical simulation in medical education.
