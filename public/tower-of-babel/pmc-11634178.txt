Applying normative atlases in deep brain stimulation: a comprehensive review
Authors: Bowen Chang, Jay J. Park, Vivek P. Buch
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Deep brain stimulation (DBS) has emerged as a crucial therapeutic strategy for various neurological and psychiatric disorders. Precise target localization is essential for optimizing therapeutic outcomes, necessitating advanced neuroimaging techniques. Normative atlases provide standardized references for accurate electrode placement, enhancing treatment customization and efficacy. This comprehensive review explores the application of normative atlases in DBS, emphasizing their role in target identification, patient-specific electrode placement, and predicting stimulation outcomes. Challenges, such as variability across atlases and technical complexities, are addressed alongside future directions and innovations, including advancements in neuroimaging technologies and the integration of machine learning (ML) and artificial intelligence (AI). Normative atlases play a pivotal role in enhancing DBS precision and patient outcomes, promising a future of personalized and effective therapies in neurology and psychiatry.

Deep brain stimulation (DBS) serves as a therapeutic approach for a range of neurological and psychiatric conditions, utilizing electrode implants to provide targeted electrical stimulation to specific brain regions1. Previously advancements have seen substantial progress in the application of DBS for diseases such as Parkinson’s disease (PD), epilepsy, and obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD)2–4. Achieving precise electrode placement is paramount for optimizing therapeutic outcomes and minimizing adverse effects, necessitating the use of sophisticated neuroimaging and electrophysiological techniques for accurate target localization5,6. Moreover, the variability in brain structures and anatomical details among individuals calls for tailored treatment strategies to maximize efficacy and ensure patient safety7.

In the dynamic domain of DBS therapy, achieving precision and effectiveness is driving the advancement of cutting-edge neuroimaging modalities and computational approaches. The employment of normative atlases represents a significant advancement towards improving target localization accuracy and enhancing treatment customization8. These atlases offer a standardized reference for brain structures, aiding clinicians in accurately identifying the desired target regions for electrode placement and effective stimulation. This paper aims to provide a comprehensive review of the application and challenges associated with the use of normative atlases in DBS therapy.

Literature selection

This comprehensive review of the application of normative atlases in DBS was based on a systematic search of the relevant literature. We searched the following databases: PubMed, Embase, and Scopus. The following keywords were used in our search: ‘deep brain stimulation’, ‘normative atlas’, ‘neuroimaging’, ‘precision medicine’, ‘neuroarchitectural’, and ‘artificial intelligence’. Studies were included if they met the following criteria: 1) Articles published in English; 2) Studies focusing on the application of normative atlases in DBS; 3) Studies involving human participants; 4) Studies published between January 2000 and August 2023. Studies were excluded if they: 1) Were case reports or opinion pieces; 2) Did not provide original data or analysis; 3) Were not peer-reviewed. This systematic search resulted in a comprehensive selection of relevant literature, which formed the basis of our review.

Normative atlases: concepts and development

The advancement of normative atlases marks a significant research endeavor in neuroscience and neuroimaging, offering essential references to decipher the typical anatomy and function of the human brain across various life stages and species. The genesis of normative atlases involves diverse methodologies and applications, from enhancing our grasp of cortical labeling in the human brain to advancing our understanding of structural connectivity. A pivotal contribution in this area is the Mindboggle-101 dataset by Klein and Tourville, distinguished as the most extensive collection of freely accessible, manually annotated human brain images to-date9. Its new cortical labeling protocol, which requires a minimal amount of manual input after the automation of labels and a definitive set of anatomical landmarks, signifies crucial progress in crafting human brain normative atlases.

Firstly, in the field of veterinary science, Milne et al.10 developed a canine brain atlas-based on MRI, emphasizing the broad applicability and importance of atlas-based segmentation techniques across species and promoting neuroimaging research and disease diagnosis beyond humans. Ullmann et al.11 MRI-based nonhuman brain atlas demonstrated the extensive application and crucial role of normative atlases in detailing the anatomy and function of different species’ brains. This involves complex processes such as dataset registration, ontology classification, and segmentation, which are essential for enhancing our understanding in this field.

Additionally, Shi et al.12 introduced computational atlases to examine structural MRI and diffusion tensor imaging data of early postnatal brain development in rhesus monkeys, opening new research avenues for nonhuman primate brain development. These atlases are valuable tools for analyzing MRI data of early developmental stages, broadening the applicability, and relevance of normative atlases. In summary, these efforts highlight the significant role of normative atlases in enhancing our understanding of brain structure and function across different life stages and species13–15. With detailed standard references, researchers can more effectively navigate and manipulate complex brain structures. The continuous expansion of normative atlases is anticipated to make substantial contributions to future neuroscience research and clinical practice.

Based on the advancements in normative atlas research in animals, their application in humans is also flourishing. Rutherford et al.16 developed standardized models to elucidate structural surface area and brain functional connectivity across the lifespan. Their approach demonstrated the utility of using standardized static network atlases to cover various neuroimaging measurements throughout life, highlighting the role of standardized modeling in deepening our understanding of typical brain development and function. Additionally, Varoquaux et al.17 enriched the normative atlas repository by constructing cognitive maps through extensive human brain atlases and consolidating findings from independent task-state functional MRI (fMRI) studies. This approach yielded a comprehensive whole-brain map associated with specific cognitive functions, providing an integrative perspective for precisely interpreting cognitive concepts in new tasks.

Applications of normative atlases in DBS

As normative atlases advance our understanding of brain structure and function across different species and age groups, their practical applications in DBS therapy are becoming increasingly evident, particularly in the realm of target identification, programming, and outcome prediction critical for successful DBS procedures.

Target identification and localization in DBS

The value of normative atlases is evident in their impact on target localization during the preoperative planning stage of DBS, which contributes to the success of the surgery. Accurately targeting crucial brain structures, such as the Globus Pallidus internus (GPi), Subthalamic Nucleus (STN), and Pedunculopontine Nucleus (PPN), is fundamental to DBS effectiveness18–20. The introduction of high-resolution MRI techniques, like the Modified Driven Equilibrium Fourier Transform (MDEFT), has markedly improved the visualization of deep brain structures, enabling more precise targeting in DBS procedures. Leveraging the capacity of MDEFT, Nowacki et al.18 demonstrated the significant variability in target localization among patients. Because normative atlases provide a comprehensive set of anatomical references, they can potentially address the variability in target localization among patients, which is a significant challenge in DBS programming. For example, the shift in DBS programming towards adaptive closed-loop stimulation accentuates the need for precise target identification and validation postimplantation. Hell et al.19 overview of recent research into target structures and strategies emphasizes the movement towards personalized DBS approaches, highlighting normative atlases’ role in initial target identification. Shah et al.21 work introduces a novel approach to enhance DBS targeting through 3D visualization of intraoperative stimulation tests. This method enhances target selection by marrying electric field distribution simulations with patient-specific anatomy and observed symptom improvement, exemplifying normative atlases’ role in melding general anatomical references with individual patient details. The integration of advanced imaging techniques, automated localization, and innovative targeting methods highlights the indispensability of normative atlases in improving DBS targeting accuracy and effectiveness. A significant innovation in this field stems from the introduction of computer-assisted preoperative DBS planning. This method integrates anatomical-clinical atlases with Volume of Tissue Activated (VTA) models to automate the optimization of electrode trajectory and stimulation site selection, with the goal of achieving optimal clinical results22. By emphasizing the integration of normative atlases in the preoperative phase, this approach sets the foundation for enhancing surgical precision and therapeutic efficacy.

Petersen et al. demonstrates that atlas-based localization is critical for postoperative stimulation testing in addition to preoperative electrode localization. In comparison to manual or tractography-based targeting, atlas-based targets were closest to the electrode contacts that were stimulated. Historically, there have been technological barriers to using neuroanatomical data in postoperative stimulation, but emerging open-source software is actively being explored to overcome these challenges23. Additionally, as explored by Black et al., the application of DBS in treating neuropathic pain following spinal cord injury demonstrates its practical value beyond conventional DBS targets. They utilized a standardized fiber orientation distribution (FOD) template to nonlinearly register subject-specific FOD maps in order to assess intergroup differences. This highlighted the importance of normative atlases in identifying neural structural changes for evidence-based target selection24.

Electrode placement and programming

In addition to the preoperative and postoperative stages of DBS treatment, the normative atlas plays a crucial role during the placement and programming of DBS. In DBS, precise electrode placement and programming play pivotal roles in maximizing therapeutic effects and mitigating adverse outcomes25. Recent advancements underscore the crucial significance of normative atlases in optimizing patient-specific electrode positioning and DBS programming, fostering a more tailored and efficacious treatment strategy26. They do mention the use of a normative functional atlas for region-based analysis in the machine learning model. Research by Caparros-Lefebvre et al.27 further stresses the impact of minor variations in electrode placement on ameliorating levodopa-induced dyskinesias. Their findings emphasize the potential role of specific brain structures, indicating that the Centre Median and Parafascicularis complex (CM-Pf) is included in the motor circuitry of the basal ganglia system and receives significant input from the internal globus pallidus. This suggests that normative atlases provide crucial anatomical insights for individualized DBS electrode placement to optimize clinical outcomes.

About normative atlas-assisted DBS programming, the GEPESTIM registry, focusing on pediatric dystonia, offers valuable neuroanatomical perspectives through localized ‘sweetspot’ and distributed network models derived from pediatric neuroimaging data, elucidating DBS-associated clinical effects28. When integrated with normative atlases, this type of neuroimaging-based analysis significantly enhances the precision of targeting and programming in DBS, particularly in complex cases like pediatric dystonia, where variations in brain structures and developmental stages demand a customized approach. In the context of optimizing DBS parameters for neuroimaging-based targets, the introduction of algorithms such as DBS Illumina 3D showcases the potential for seamlessly merging clinical programmer software with advanced image-based anatomy analysis29. This accelerates the determination of stimulation settings that optimize target area activation, once again showcasing the utility of normative atlases in conjunction with sophisticated computational models to fine-tune DBS programming for individual patients.

Collectively, these studies present a compelling narrative on the evolving role of normative atlases in enhancing patient-specific electrode placement and DBS programming. Furnishing a detailed anatomical framework, combined with computational models and neuroimaging data, significantly enhances the precision of DBS interventions, optimizing clinical outcomes, and steering towards a more personalized treatment approach.

Prediction of stimulation outcomes based on normative atlases

Normative atlases have the potential to aid in predicting DBS outcomes, which can assist in patient selection and managing individual expectations. However, challenges remain in fully realizing their predictive capabilities in clinical applications. Using probabilistic mapping and brain overlay analysis has been shown to reveal a significant association between motor outcomes and stimulation volume30. Wang et al.31 further advanced the field by introducing functional connectivity analysis of hemispheric asymmetry to forecast DBS outcomes in PD. Their findings showed stronger correlations between predictive values and actual results in the right hemisphere network, highlighting the role of brain lateralization in DBS response. These insights advocate for a customized approach leveraging normative atlases that account for hemispheric-specific variances to enhance DBS effects.

In addition, connectomics approaches are enriching our comprehension of DBS outcomes. Chen et al.32 identified functional and structural connectivity of brain regions relevant to the VTA and initial STN-DBS outcomes. This underscores the significance of studying the whole-brain network beyond local effects to estimate DBS response accurately. Interpreting these intricate networks can be enhanced through normative atlases, grounding patient-specific analyses in a comprehensive neuroanatomical framework. While these methods show promise, challenges persist in the widespread application of normative atlases for predicting DBS outcomes. Widge et al.33 research points out the limitations of patient-specific connectome models in reliably forecasting outcomes in patients with OCD. This underscores the need for cautious consideration and potential refinement in utilizing normative connectome studies to impact clinical decisions significantly.

The use of normative atlases to forecast DBS outcomes has also expanded into pediatric neurology, as illustrated by Al-Fatly et al.28, shedding light on DBS-related clinical outcomes in pediatric dystonia using neuroimaging surrogate data. This underscores the necessity for adaptable normative atlases that are not only relevant to adults but also to developing brains, broadening the applicability of these models across diverse age groups.

Lastly, Sobesky et al. explored how different DBS target points modulate the PD network. Integrating indirect connectivity metrics and retrospective data, these studies suggest an overall network modulation by DBS, irrespective of specific target points34. This emphasizes the potential role of normative atlases in facilitating a comprehensive mapping of interconnected brain regions while considering DBS target points. The evolving landscape of DBS outcome prediction utilizing normative atlases benefits from advanced probabilistic mapping, connectomics, and functional connectivity analyses. Customizing and refining the development of normative atlases tailored to a range of patient populations and neurological conditions is essential for leveraging these insights in personalized DBS therapy. The trajectory of this research field not only assures enhanced precision in DBS applications but also a deeper understanding of its neurobiological efficacy.

Practical applications of normative atlases in DBS

While normative atlases offer a powerful tool for advancing DBS precision, their practical application is still evolving. Here are some key examples demonstrating their impact. In the treatment of PD, a research study by Nowacki et al.18 demonstrated the value of using normative atlases combined with MDEFT MRI imaging to refine the target localization of the GPi. Their findings revealed that a normative atlas-based approach could significantly improve the accuracy of target identification, leading to more precise electrode placement and potentially better therapeutic outcomes.

Another example of the practical application of normative atlases can be seen in the treatment of dystonia. Shah et al.21 investigated the use of 3D visualization of intraoperative stimulation tests guided by normative atlases to optimize DBS programming. Their findings highlighted the potential of this approach in enhancing target selection and adjusting stimulation parameters based on individual patient anatomy and observed symptom improvements.

In addition, Wang et al.8 explored the use of connectome-based analysis derived from normative atlases to predict DBS outcomes in PD. Their findings suggested that incorporating functional connectivity analysis, particularly focusing on hemispheric asymmetry, could enhance the accuracy of predicting DBS responses. This approach opens promising avenues for individualized treatment planning and the optimization of stimulation parameters based on specific patient characteristics.

Challenges and limitations

While the potential applications of normative atlases in optimizing DBS precision are vast, challenges arise from the variability among these atlases, necessitating a deeper understanding and refinement to ensure consistent and reliable clinical outcomes in DBS procedures. In addition, the challenges in methodology and technical innovation are also important issues to be addressed in the application of normative atlases in DBS.

Variability across normative atlases used in different studies

While normative atlases can help achieve precise targeting and subsequent patient outcome analysis in DBS, the variability across these atlases presents significant challenges and limitations, affecting both clinical and research domains associated with DBS. An illustrative example is through the application of normative atlases in DBS treatment for Tourette syndrome. Johnson et al.35 emphasized the complexity of predicting DBS outcomes based solely on anatomical structures and noted that inconsistencies in atlas-based targets can lead to varying clinical responses. This work highlights the variability of normative atlases across different patients and underscores the importance of addressing this issue. Moreover, the development of species-specific templates for characterizing individual brain differences, as shown in the work by Lv et al.36, also illustrates the necessity of tailored atlases for precise neurological analyses. While this study focuses on the macaque brain, it draws attention to the broader challenge of ensuring that normative atlases accurately reflect the population they are intended to represent. Particularly relevant when considering application in human DBS studies, meticulous consideration of individual patient variability is necessary to optimize clinical outcomes. In a related field, the exploration of normative connectomic comparisons for DBS targeting, as discussed by Morishita et al.37, further enriches discussions around atlas variability. Their case report sheds light on the potential of connectome analysis to provide insights into the structures underpinning positive DBS responses, suggesting that integrating connectomic data within normative atlases could offer a pathway toward resolving the issue of atlas variability. As methodologies and technologies evolve, the need for accurate, universally applicable atlases becomes increasingly evident. The pursuit of more reliable and representative normative atlases promises to enhance both the precision of DBS targeting and the reliability of outcomes across patient populations.

Technical and methodological challenges in normative atlases to DBS

The application of normative atlases in DBS presents significant technical and methodological challenges, including the integration of multimodal imaging data, image segmentation, and registration, and the interpretation of rich high-dimensional datasets. One of the most critical technical challenges in using normative atlases is the accuracy of brain structure segmentation. The work of Ahamed et al.38 highlighted the role of deep learning in improving the accuracy of brain tumor segmentation, which requires similar precision when identifying DBS target areas. Their findings suggest that deep learning can significantly enhance segmentation accuracy, offering promising prospects for normative atlas-based DBS applications. Yousef et al.39 further supported this view, demonstrating that the recurrent residual U-Net model outperformed other state-of-the-art models in brain tumor segmentation, which could directly benefit the optimization of normative atlases used for DBS targeting.

Another key area is integrating multimodal data to inform the development of comprehensive normative atlases. The challenges related to this integration include technical considerations around data harmonization and methodological strategies for effectively combining different imaging modalities. To address these challenges, Ji et al.40 introduced the ACOEC-FD approach, which utilizes ant colony optimization to learn effective connectivity networks from fMRI and diffusion tensor imaging. This method not only demonstrated the feasibility of integrating multimodal imaging data but also highlighted the potential of these approaches to enrich normative atlases with complex details of brain connectivity.

The application of normative atlases in clinical settings also presents unique challenges, particularly considering the individual variability among patients undergoing DBS. Paulo et al.41 emphasized the value of combining atlas-based imaging data with intraoperative neurophysiological testing to personalize electrode placement, potentially enhancing the precision and efficacy of DBS. This work illustrates methodological advances that reconcile the general applicability of normative atlases with the need for individualized treatment plans, paving the way for more personalized and effective DBS interventions. As the field continues to navigate these complex technical and methodological challenges, the overall trajectory suggests that DBS targeting strategies will evolve towards greater complexity and precision, informed by continually refined normative atlases.

Future directions and innovations

Overcoming the variability and technical challenges associated with normative atlases requires advancements in neuroimaging technologies and the integration of machine learning (ML) and artificial intelligence (AI), heralding a new frontier in DBS precision and personalized therapy guided by refined atlas standards.

Advancements in neuroimaging technologies for refining normative atlases

The refinement of normative atlases through advancements in neuroimaging technologies is a significant endeavor. A promising approach in this refinement involves employing magnetic resonance fingerprinting (MRF) techniques42. Choi et al.43 utilized a clinical 3T scanner to create whole-brain MRF T1 and T2 atlases for healthy subjects, offering a quantitative and region-specific baseline for future brain studies and pathology detection. These atlases provide a more precise depiction of cortical regions, marking a substantial advancement in diagnosing and comprehending neurological conditions through imaging data. Another innovative strategy is crafting high-resolution normative atlases tailored to specific population segments. Rajashekar et al.44 introduced high-resolution, age-specific FLAIR (Fluid-Attenuated Inversion Recovery) and noncontrast CT atlases targeting the elderly, pivotal for lesion-related research in this age group. These progressions underscore the significance of demographic-specific standards in enhancing the accuracy and relevance of neuroimaging analyses. Normative atlases are generated based on imaging data from a large number of individuals, representing an ‘average’ brain structure. By incorporating demographic-specific standards, more detailed normative atlases can be developed, such as those stratified by age group, sex, and ethnicity, thereby enhancing the applicability and accuracy of these atlases. Apart from traditional structural imaging, the utilization of neuroelectric brain activity and connectivity pushes the boundaries of improving normative atlases. Krieger et al.45 compiled atlases from a vast human cohort, highlighting the high spatial resolution of neuroelectric measures, even from white matter regions. Such a diverse combination of neuroimaging techniques enriches the comprehension of brain function and the variability between individuals.

While these innovations demonstrate substantial progress in refining normative atlases, challenges persist in implementing and applying these advancements in clinical and research settings. The endeavors by Forkert et al. concerning elderly-specific atlases and the contributions by Choi et al. related to quantitative MRF-based atlases represent pivotal steps toward overcoming these challenges46,47. Sustaining efforts in developing and refining these tools will necessitate a joint commitment from the neuroimaging community. Through establishing quantitative imaging standards, age-specific atlases, and venturing into neuroelectric activity mapping, these advancements promise to elevate our understanding of brain structure and function.

Integration of machine learning and artificial intelligence in normative atlases-based DBS

Integrating ML and AI into normative atlases-based DBS for neurological and psychiatric disorders represents the frontier and future direction of research48,49. Balachandran et al.50 discussed the precision achieved through virtual targets in implementing a novel targeting framework for DBS, achieving sub-millimetric accuracy. This technological advancement underscores the potential of ML algorithms to boost the precision of DBS electrode placement by normative atlases to predict optimal target sites based on individual patient anatomy and disease characteristics. In the clinical arena, neuroimaging-based analyses yield profound insights into the outcomes of DBS in treating pediatric dystonia, as demonstrated by Al-Fatly et al.28. The surgical targeting and postoperative programming of DBS depend on the spatial relationship between the stimulation electrodes and the surrounding anatomical structures, as well as the connections between the electrodes and specific distribution patterns within brain networks. This information is typically gathered through group-level analyses, which rely on the availability of normative imaging resources such as atlases and connectomes. Their research illuminates how ML models leverage normative pediatric neuroimaging data to uncover the neuroanatomical basis of DBS-related outcomes. This progress indicates that we have made advances in utilizing ML algorithms to develop normative maps incorporating detailed neuroimaging data, thereby enabling the customization of DBS therapy based on predictive models tailored to individual responses. Boutet et al.26 developed a machine learning model based on fMRI data from 67 PD patients, achieving an 88% accuracy in distinguishing optimal from nonoptimal DBS settings. This suggests that fMRI brain responses can serve as an objective biomarker for clinical response in PD patients. Future applications of the same algorithm combined with normative maps could potentially enhance the model’s generalizability. In summary, integrating ML and AI into normative atlas-based DBS is still in its early but rapidly developing stages. The harmonious fusion of advancements in targeting technology and normative atlas-based predictive algorithms provides the necessary interdisciplinary approach to realize the full therapeutic potential of DBS. Future research directions include refining algorithms to enhance the precision of normative atlas-based DBS targeting and expanding the application of normative atlas-based models to a range of neurological and psychiatric disorders.

Conclusion

The incorporation of normative atlases in DBS significantly enhances target localization precision and optimizes patient treatment outcomes. These atlases furnish standardized information on brain structures, assisting in accurate electrode placement and efficient stimulation. By comparing patients’ brain structures with normative atlases, personalized treatment plans can be devised. The advancement of normative atlases across species and age groups, combined with strides in neuroimaging technologies, has refined DBS interventions. From target identification to electrode placement and programming tailored to individual anatomical variances, normative atlases heighten clinical outcomes by personalizing treatments. Utilizing normative atlases for predicting stimulation outcomes has shown promise in refining patient selection and expectations. Challenges like variability among normative atlases and the technical complexities of their integration in DBS persist. Tackling these challenges through innovative neuroimaging technologies and the incorporation of AI and ML can further enhance normative atlases and improve DBS precision. Proposed workflow for DBS planning utilizing normative atlases. The key steps involved in applying normative atlases throughout the DBS workflow. First, patient characteristics and anticipated DBS outcomes are considered. Normative atlases are then used to identify the optimal target brain region for DBS implantation. Next, surgeons utilize the atlases to guide electrode placement and programming, optimizing stimulation parameters based on individual patient anatomy and predicted outcomes. Finally, the effectiveness of DBS is evaluated based on the predicted outcomes. This iterative process aims to ensure personalized and effective DBS treatment.

Challenges in utilizing normative atlas in DBS and solution. 1) Variability: Normative atlases can differ in their representation of brain structures due to factors like population variability, disease states, and imaging acquisition techniques. This variability can impact the accuracy and precision of DBS targeting and outcomes; 2) Segmentation accuracy: Precise segmentation of brain structures is essential for accurate DBS targeting. Noise, image artifacts, and individual differences can affect the accuracy of segmentation; 3) Multimodal data integration: Combining information from different neuroimaging modalities, like structural MRI, functional MRI, and diffusion tensor imaging, can provide a more comprehensive understanding of brain structure and function; 4) Individual variability: Patient-specific characteristics, such as age, disease state, and individual anatomy, can influence the effectiveness of DBS.
