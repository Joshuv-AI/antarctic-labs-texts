Ultrasound Renal Stress Test Assessment in Moderate-to-Severe Psoriasis: A Pilot Study
Authors: Stefano Piaserico, Roberto Mazzetto, Jacopo Tartaglia, Marco Bogo, Christian Ciolfi, Federico Nalesso
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPsoriasis is increasingly recognized as a systemic inflammatory disease and has been associated with chronic kidney disease. Whether psoriasis is also linked to subclinical impairment in renal microcirculation remains unclear.ObjectiveTo assess renal functional reserve (RFR) in patients with moderate-to-severe psoriasis using the renal stress test (RST).MethodsFifty-one consecutive adults with psoriasis and 35 age- and sex-matched controls underwent RST. Renal resistive index (RRI) was measured by Doppler ultrasound in an interlobular renal artery at baseline and after abdominal compression with a fluid-filled bag corresponding to 10% of body weight. Intrarenal resistive index variation (IRRIV) was calculated as the percentage difference between baseline and stress values.ResultsPatients with psoriasis showed a lower IRRIV than controls (median 13.8% [10.7–18.6] vs 23.1% [17.8–25.0]; p<0.001). IRRIV differed significantly across controls and Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI) severity strata (p<0.001), but post hoc pairwise comparisons showed that this difference was driven by the comparison between controls and psoriasis patients, with no significant difference between severity strata. In multivariable analysis, psoriasis remained independently associated with lower IRRIV.LimitationsThis was a single-center pilot study with a limited sample size.ConclusionModerate-to-severe psoriasis was associated with impaired renal functional reserve, suggesting subclinical renal microcirculatory dysfunction. Larger studies are needed to confirm these findings and define their clinical relevance.

Introduction

Psoriasis is a chronic immune-mediated skin disorder that affects approximately 2–3% of the global population.1,2 Traditionally considered as a condition affecting only the skin and joints, psoriasis is now recognized as a systemic disease. Research conducted in the past decade has clarified the association between psoriasis and various comorbidities, including diabetes, cardiovascular disease, metabolic syndrome, often independently of conventional risk factors.3–7

Additionally, recent large-scale epidemiological studies have suggested a connection between psoriasis and chronic kidney disease (CKD), and potentially End-Stage Kidney Disease (ESKD), particularly in patients with severe psoriasis. This association appears to be independent of traditional risk factors.8–14 The growing body of evidence indicates that psoriasis may function as an independent risk factor for renal dysfunction, much like its established role in the cardiovascular domain, where it is recognized as an independent risk factor for cardiovascular disease.

Although the precise mechanisms underlying this association remain unclear, one hypothesis centers on the role of cytokines, particularly interleukin-17 (IL-17). In psoriasis, T cells, specifically Th17 cells, are key drivers of the inflammatory response.15 Notably, IL-17 receptors are not only found in skin cells but are also expressed in various renal cell types, suggesting they may play a role in renal pathology.16–18 While the exact mechanisms linking IL-17 to renal dysfunction in psoriasis remain hypothetical, experimental studies have shown that IL-17 can cause kidney changes similar to those observed in CKD, such as increased oxidative stress and fibrosis.19 These findings point to IL-17 as a potential contributor to renal impairment in psoriatic patients.

In light of the growing understanding of the complex link between psoriasis and renal dysfunction, it is crucial to further characterize the functional and microcirculatory changes occurring within the kidneys in psoriasis patients. Recent evidence has shown potential dysfunction in the coronary microcirculation of psoriasis patients,20 leading us to hypothesize that similar vascular abnormalities may occur systemically, including within the renal vasculature. One method for assessing coronary vasodilator capacity is the measurement of coronary flow reserve (CFR) using stress echocardiography. CFR quantifies the ability of coronary arteries to dilate and increase blood flow in response to heightened demand, typically induced by vasodilators or exercise.21 Likewise, renal functional reserve (RFR) reflects the kidneys’ ability to increase glomerular filtration rate (GFR) in response to physiological or pathological stimuli. A low RFR is associated with a higher risk of developing Acute Kidney Injury (AKI) and CKD. RFR is typically measured by calculating the difference between baseline GFR and peak GFR following a protein load or pharmacological stimulation (eg, dopamine or amino acid infusion).22 The dynamic change in GFR therefore remains the gold standard definition of RFR. Recently, the renal stress test (RST) has been introduced as a non-invasive surrogate approach: the RST uses mechanical abdominal compression to reduce renal blood flow, thereby activating the kidney’s autoregulatory mechanism. The resulting variation in the renal resistive index correlates well with RFR measured by protein loading and can be obtained with colour Doppler ultrasound, a safe, reproducible and inexpensive method that has been validated for the assessment of RFR in healthy subjects.23 This process induces afferent arteriolar vasodilation, aimed at maintaining glomerular perfusion despite the decreased blood flow. This vasodilation can be quantified using Doppler ultrasound by measuring changes in the renal resistive index (RRI) before and after stress.24 Although RST has been explored as a routine test for assessing renal function and susceptibility to AKI in patients scheduled for cardiac and abdominal surgery,25,26 its application in chronic inflammatory conditions with potential kidney involvement like psoriasis remains underexplored.

In this pilot study, we assessed RFR in a cohort of patients affected by moderate-to-severe psoriasis.

Materials and Methods

Studied Patients

This prospective interventional study included 51 consecutive participants with psoriasis, aged 18 years or older, who were recruited from March 2021 through October 2023 at the University Hospital of Padova, Italy. A certified dermatologist or rheumatologist diagnosed psoriasis and psoriatic arthritis. Exclusion criteria were an estimated glomerular filtration rate less than 30 mL/min/1.73 m2, the use of NSAIDs in the 10 days before the procedure, known current CVD, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease or asthma, and conditions that increase systemic inflammation such as internal solid or liquid malignancy within the past 5 years, HIV infection, any active infection 72 hours before, major surgery within the previous 3 months, current pregnancy, or lactation.

A nonrandomized control group of 35 normal volunteers recruited from institutional personnel, matched for age and sex, was recruited. All control subjects were asymptomatic with no history of renal or heart disease.

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Padua (CET: 6494/AO/25) and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki; written informed consent was obtained from all participants before study procedures.

Study Protocol

We utilized intrarenal resistive index variation (IRRIV) to assess renal microcirculation during RST. This parameter is not a direct measure of RFR, which is defined by the dynamic change in GFR, but a bedside ultrasound surrogate that correlates closely with it. The intra-parenchymal renal resistive index (RRI) indicates flow resistance within the kidney, making it a valuable tool for assessing changes in renal perfusion. In this context, a variation in RRI, called IRRIV and assessed through Doppler ultrasound in an interlobular artery, can be regarded as an indirect measure of pre-glomerular vasodilation. After exposure to mechanical stress, renal autoregulatory mechanisms are activated, influencing the tone of the afferent arteriole and promoting vasodilation to sustain glomerular perfusion. Thus, the RST allows for a rapid assessment of RFR, providing insights into the kidney’s susceptibility to different stress stimuli and the associated risk of acute kidney injury.

The assessment of IRRIVs followed a previously established ultrasound technique.24,27 All scans were performed by the same operator, trained in Colour and Doppler ultrasound, on an Esaote MyLab Gamma with an AC2541 convex probe (Esaote, Genoa, Italy). With the participant supine and after at least 5 minutes of rest, RRI was measured in interlobular arteries as (peak systolic velocity − end diastolic velocity)/peak systolic velocity. Baseline RRI was the mean of three measurements per kidney. A saline bag weighing 10% of body weight was then placed on the abdomen, and RRI was recorded every minute for 10 minutes; the lowest value obtained was taken as the stress RRI. IRRIV was calculated as (baseline RRI − stress RRI)/baseline RRI × 100.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were represented as median with first and third quartiles (Q1-Q3). Discrete variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. The distribution of the data was analyzed with a 1-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Categorical variables were compared by the χ2 test or the Fisher exact test as appropriate. Continuous data were compared by use of the Mann–Whitney U. Spearman’s rho correlation coefficient was used to assess the relationship between continuous variables. All tests were two-sided and statistical significance was accepted if the null hypothesis could be rejected at p<0.05. Comparisons of IRRIV between controls and psoriasis patients stratified by PASI at the time of measurement were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. PASI cut-offs of 15 and 20 were chosen because they correspond to the 33rd and 67th percentiles of the PASI distribution in the study cohort, yielding three subgroups of comparable size. When the Kruskal–Wallis test was significant, post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed with the Dunn test and p values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni correction. Outliers were defined a priori as values lying beyond 1.5 times the interquartile range below the first or above the third quartile within each group; they were identified for descriptive purposes, retained in all analyses, and the main analyses were repeated after their exclusion as a sensitivity analysis. Univariate and multivariable linear regression analyses were used to assess the relationship between IRRIV and the presence of psoriasis, age, sex and body mass index (BMI). The multivariable model was specified a priori and included all four covariates simultaneously, irrespective of their univariate significance, in order to adjust for the between-group difference in BMI. Regression coefficients are reported as B with standard errors and 95% confidence intervals, together with standardised coefficients, R2, adjusted R2 and the F statistic of the model; collinearity was assessed by variance inflation factors. As a further sensitivity analysis, the multivariable model was repeated after excluding participants with BMI ≥30 kg/m2. Data were analyzed with SPSS software version 29.0 (Chicago, SPSS, Inc., Chicago, Illinois).

Results

Baseline Characteristics

A total of 51 psoriatic patients underwent the RST. Two patients (3.9%) were excluded from the analyses due to technical challenges in measuring IRRIV, attributed to obesity or meteorism. The final psoriasis study cohort comprised 49 subjects with complete data for IRRIV and psoriasis activity and history. The demographic and psoriasis characteristics of the excluded patients did not significantly differ from those of the final participant group.

The studied patients were predominantly male (35, 71.4%) with a median age of 44.0 years (Q1–Q3: 32.0–52.0). The control group comprised 35 individuals without psoriasis or other inflammatory diseases (21 male, 60.0%), with a median age of 36.4 years (Q1–Q3: 29.0–48.1). IRRIV is Reduced in Psoriasis Patients Compared to Normal Controls

Psoriasis patients showed a significantly lower IRRIV than controls (median 13.8% [10.7–18.6] vs 23.1% [17.8–25.0]; mean 15.7 ± 6.4% vs 21.5 ± 5.5%; Mann–Whitney U = 391.0, p<0.001). Additionally, there was a trend towards a negative correlation between IRRIV and disease activity as measured by the PASI index (p=0.09, rho’s coefficient=−0.245) (Figure 1). IRRIV differed significantly across the four groups, from controls to psoriatic patients categorized by PASI scores below 15 (n=15), between 15 and 20 (n=15) and above 20 (n=19) (median 23.1%, 16.7%, 14.3% and 12.5%, respectively; Kruskal–Wallis H=20.01, p<0.001) (Figure 2). Post hoc pairwise comparisons (Dunn test with Bonferroni correction) showed that this difference was driven by the comparison between controls and psoriasis patients, being significant for controls versus PASI 15–20 (p=0.004) and controls versus PASI >20 (p=0.001), but not for controls versus PASI <15 (p=0.253); none of the comparisons between severity strata was significant (all p=1.000). Consistently, a Kruskal–Wallis test restricted to the three severity strata was not significant (H=3.66, p=0.161). No significant association was identified between IRRIV and the duration of psoriasis (Spearman’s rho coefficient=−0.031, p=0.834). Three observations (one control and two psoriasis patients) met the predefined outlier criterion; all were retained in the analyses. Discussion

Multiple studies have demonstrated that severe psoriasis is associated with an increased risk of CKD and ESKD.7,9 In a population-based cohort study by J. Wan et al, the hazard ratio for CKD in psoriasis patients was 1.93.9 Similarly, a systematic review and meta-analysis by Xu Jing et al reported a hazard ratio of 1.37 for ESKD in individuals with severe psoriasis.7 So far, the mechanisms involved in this process are still unclear.

In the present study, we investigated renal microcirculation in patients with psoriasis using RST, a non-invasive test recently validated as an indicator of RFR.24

For the first time, we demonstrated that individuals with moderate-to-severe psoriasis have a significantly reduced IRRIV during RST compared with healthy controls (median 13.8% vs 23.1%, respectively; p<0.001), a finding that we interpret as indicative of impaired RFR and, more broadly, of renal microcirculatory dysfunction. When participants were classified as controls or into three PASI-defined severity strata, IRRIV differed significantly across groups. However, post hoc analyses showed that this difference was driven by the comparison between controls and patients with psoriasis, rather than by a progressive decrease in IRRIV with increasing disease severity. No significant differences were observed among the psoriasis severity subgroups. Therefore, the present data do not support a severity-dependent relationship. A trend toward an inverse association between psoriasis activity and IRRIV was observed, but it did not reach statistical significance.

RFR reflects the kidney’s ability to increase GFR when faced with various biological challenges. RFR is typically assessed by measuring the difference between resting and peak GFR. This peak GFR is induced by dietary protein loading or administering substances like dopamine or a mixture of amino acids. However, these methods are only sometimes effective for patient screening, as they may produce variable or unclear results, potentially limiting their utility in reliably detecting subclinical renal dysfunction. Therefore, more straightforward and reliable methods for evaluating RFR have been developed. One such approach is the determination of RFR through the RST, which has recently gained attention as a non-invasive and easy-to-use predictor of RFR. This method offers a practical alternative for assessing renal function in clinical settings, with greater consistency and ease of application. IRRIV is the percentage difference between baseline RRI and stress RRI, achieved by applying abdominal pressure using a weight on the abdomen (fluid bag equivalent to 10% of the subject’s body weight). The abdominal compression activates renal auto-regulatory mechanisms, resulting in vasodilation of the afferent arteriole, as indicated by variations in RRI.

Studies show that RFR diminishes progressively with CKD and advancing age.28,29 In cases of diabetic nephropathy, where hyperfiltration is already present, GFR may be near its maximum, limiting the potential for further increase in response to protein load.30

Our novel findings support the hypothesis that psoriasis, likely through disease-induced systemic inflammation, may impact renal microcirculation, impairing the kidney’s ability to increase filtration in response to stress. The detrimental effect on renal microcirculation could lead to subclinical damage initially not detectable through clinical indicators such as reduced GFR or elevated creatinine levels.

Until now, the renal response to external stressors via RFR test or RST has not been explored in psoriatic patients. Despite epidemiological evidence linking psoriasis, particularly when severe and accompanied by psoriatic arthritis, to CKD,8–12 the potential for subclinical renal dysfunction detectable through renal stress tests remains unexplored.

However, the connection between systemic inflammation and reduced renal responsiveness to stress has been examined in other chronic conditions, including Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD). A study by C. Chen et al specifically investigated the impact of systemic inflammation on RFR in COPD patients, demonstrating a correlation between elevated TNF-alpha levels and impaired RFR.31 Elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines may be associated with systemic vascular dysfunction, affecting the renal vasculature and other vascular beds.

In psoriasis, latent vascular damage is recognized as a potential contributor to atheromatous plaque formation and dysfunction in the cardiac microcirculation.32 Recent evidence from a study conducted on a large cohort of 503 patients highlights the role of psoriasis in coronary microcirculation impairment.33 The study revealed that 31.5% of patients exhibited coronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD). Factors independently associated with CMD included higher PASI scores, prolonged disease duration, and psoriatic arthritis.

In both cardiac and renal microvascular vessels, systemic inflammation induced by psoriasis may reduce the availability of nitric oxide (NO), a critical molecule responsible for maintaining endothelial function. NO plays a vital role in vasodilation and vascular homeostasis. A decrease in NO availability can lead to endothelial dysfunction, a key factor in the pathogenesis of cardiovascular and renal complications.34 In particular, TNF-α interferes with endothelial NOS activation, triggers degradation of NOS mRNA, disrupts vasomotor function, and induces oxidative stress, collectively influencing NO modulation.34,35 This could negatively impact renal microcirculation, impairing the kidney’s ability to increase filtration in response to stress.

In addition, IL-17 might also play a role, as demonstrated in preclinical models where IL-17A overexpression in T cells led to impaired endothelium-dependent vascular relaxation through increased phosphorylation of endothelial NOS Thr495, resulting in decreased NO production and vasodilation.34,36,37

Beyond this endothelial effect, psoriasis-related inflammation also involves targets that are specific to the kidney.38 Receptors for IL-17 are constitutively expressed by resident renal cells, including mesangial cells, tubular epithelial cells and podocytes, so that circulating IL-17A can act directly on the glomerular and tubulointerstitial compartments.16–18 Ligation of IL-17RA recruits the adaptor ACT1 and, through TRAF6, activates NF-κB and MAPK signalling in resident renal cells.39 In cultured mesangial cells, IL-17A and IL-17F induce chemokine production and act in synergy with TNF-α and IL-1β, sustaining the recruitment of neutrophils and of additional Th17 cells and establishing a self-amplifying inflammatory loop within the kidney.16,38 Th17-driven glomerular inflammation has been demonstrated experimentally, and in tubular epithelial cells the Th17/IL-17 axis upregulates TGF-β and promotes epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition, oxidative stress and interstitial fibrosis.17,19

In contrast to what has been reported for the coronary microcirculation,33 we did not observe a significant relationship between disease activity, measured by PASI, and IRRIV across severity strata. Similarly, we did not find a correlation between the duration of psoriasis and reduced IRRIV values. This discrepancy could be attributed to the relatively small sample size or to intrinsic differences in the mechanisms governing renal vasculature, which may distinguish it from cardiac microcirculation.

The present study’s primary limitation is its small sample size. In addition, IRRIV is a surrogate marker of RFR that has been validated mainly in healthy volunteers, and RFR was not concurrently measured by protein or amino acid loading; our findings should therefore be interpreted as reflecting renal microcirculatory reserve rather than a direct measurement of RFR. More extensive studies are necessary to validate these findings and clarify their clinical implications for psoriasis patients.

Conclusion

This study is the first to evaluate renal functional reserve in patients with moderate-to-severe psoriasis. Our study offers new insights into the relationship between psoriasis and renal dysfunction, proposing that systemic inflammation may contribute to renal microcirculation dysfunction, leading to subclinical alterations not evident through abnormal GFR values.
