Activated microglial exosomes enhance α-Synuclein internalization and accelerate neurodegeneration in lewy body dementia
Authors: Minjie Zhang, Tongyao You, Min Guo, Yichen Zhao, Weiwei Shen, Yingzhe Wang, Yanfeng Jiang, Qiang Dong, Jintai Yu, Mei Cui, Yanxin Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLewy body dementia (LBD), which includes Parkinson’s disease dementia and dementia with Lewy bodies, is characterized by progressive cognitive decline, α-synuclein (α-Syn) aggregation, and concurrent microglial activation and neuroinflammation. The nucleus basalis of Meynert (NBM), the primary source of cortical cholinergic input, plays a key role in cognitive function and is particularly affected in LBD. Cholinergic neurons within the NBM are highly vulnerable to Lewy body pathology, yet the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood. In this study, we employed a mouse model with PFF injections into the NBM to investigate how microglia and their exosomes influence α-Syn pathology. We found that exosomes derived from activated microglia exacerbate α-Syn deposition and cognitive deficits, whereas microglial depletion mitigates these pathological changes. In vitro, activated microglial exosomes enhanced the uptake of exogenous α-Syn by cholinergic neurons. Mechanistically, we discovered that microglial exosomes can transfer bioactive membrane receptors to neurons. Focusing on lymphocyte-activation gene 3 (LAG3), a receptor critical for α-Syn internalization, we demonstrated that LAG3 is abundant in exosomes from activated—but not resting—microglia. These exosomes deliver LAG3 to neuronal membranes via an endosomal recycling pathway, a process facilitated by elevated cholesterol content, thereby promoting α-Syn uptake. Together, these findings indicate that microglial exosomes function not only as carriers of signaling molecules but also as vectors for receptor transfer, reshaping neuronal membrane composition. Our results provide a mechanistic explanation for the heightened vulnerability of NBM cholinergic neurons in LBD and reveal a novel pathway in which LAG3-rich microglial exosomes drive neuronal α-Syn internalization, advancing our understanding of neurodegeneration and identifying potential therapeutic targets.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-025-03838-5.Keywords: Lewy body dementia, Nucleus basalis of meynert, Exosome, Microglia

Introduction

Lewy body dementia (LBD), which includes dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB) and Parkinson’s disease dementia (PDD), is a prevalent neurodegenerative disorder characterized by the accumulation of α-Synuclein (α-Syn) in both neuronal and non-neuronal cells, leading to Lewy body (LB) pathology [1, 2]. However, the etiology of LBD and α-Syn spread remains unclear, with hypotheses suggesting either a “Body first” or “Brain first” origin [3, 4]. The basal forebrain, particularly the basal nucleus of Meynert (NBM), serves as a major cholinergic hub and the primary source of cortical cholinergic innervation, playing a crucial role in arousal, attention, memory, and visuospatial abilities [5–7]. Studies have shown that LBD patients exhibit significant degeneration of cholinergic neurons in the NBM, accompanied by a marked reduction in cortical cholinergic activity [8–11]. Additionally, extensive LB pathology within cholinergic nuclei in the NBM is well-documented [12]. Given its key role in cognitive function, NBM degeneration has been identified as a potential predictive marker for LBD [11, 13]. However, the mechanisms underlying its selective vulnerability to α-Syn remain largely unknown.

Excessive extracellular α-Syn uptake leads to abnormal deposition of insoluble α-Syn within neurons, driving neurodegeneration [14]. While α-Syn spreads via trans-synaptic transmission and extracellular vesicles, receptor-mediated endocytosis is a predominant pathway under pathological conditions [15]. The interaction of α-Syn with cell surface receptors facilitates its internalization and aggregation. However, the receptor subtypes mediating α-Syn uptake in NBM cholinergic neurons and their dynamic regulation in disease states remain unknown.

Microglia-neuron crosstalk forms a critical pathological basis of many neurodegenerative diseases, including LBD [16, 17]. Pathological proteins, including α-Syn, can activate and recruit microglia. Clinical evidence shows that microglial activation in pivotal areas related to LB pathology occurs in the early stages of LBD [18, 19]. However, the activation dynamics and phenotypic characteristics of microglia within the NBM region of the basal forebrain remain to be systematically elucidated. Besides, whether pathologically activated microglia in NBM have unique regulatory mechanisms on cholinergic neurons remains unclear in current studies. Abnormal microglial states have long-lasting effects on neuronal function through their secretion of cytokines or through exosomes that act as intercellular cargo transport vehicles. Our previous findings demonstrated that α-Syn-activated microglia excessively release exosomes, which carry cargoes derived from microglia to mediate material transfer to neurons [20].

Exosomes, a subset of extracellular vesicles (EVs) with diameters ranging from 30 to 150 nanometers, play critical roles in both physiological homeostasis and pathological processes [21]. As pivotal mediators of intercellular communication, exosomes transport proteins, lipids, metabolites, nucleic acids, and cell-specific pathological protein seeds to neighboring or distant recipients, eliciting specific biological responses [20, 22, 23]. Nonetheless, as lipid bilayer structures, exosome membranes harbor numerous receptors and transporters. A groundbreaking dimension of exosome biology may lie in their capacity to transfer functional membrane proteins, including receptors and transporters, to recipient cells. Exosomal membranes, derived from their parental cells, retain surface proteins in their native orientation, enabling direct integration into the recipient cell. After internalization, membrane proteins may be retained, degraded in lysosomes, or recycled back to the cell membrane [24]. Previous studies observed that tumor-derived exosomes transfer oncogenic receptors like EGFR to recipient cells [25]. PD-L1-containing exosomes regulate immune surveillance by delivering PD-L1 to other cells in the tumor microenvironment [26, 27]. These findings support the hypothesis that exosomes derived from parent cells facilitate the transfer of functional receptors to recipient cells, where they remain biologically active and contribute to neuronal physiology.

As the principal phagocytic cells in the central nervous system (CNS), microglia mediate the clearance of extracellular α-Syn aggregates through active uptake mechanisms. The LAG3 (lymphocyte activation gene 3) encodes an immunosuppressive checkpoint protein that plays a critical role in tumor immunity by modulating the proliferation and activation of CD4⁺ T cells. Some existing research revealed that LAG3 is predominantly expressed in microglia within the brain, with expression levels markedly elevated compared to other neural cell types [28–30]. In addition, LAG3 is proven to functionally mediate the neuronal cellular uptake of α-syn PFF and initiates subsequent transmission and toxicity [31]. Given that activated microglia exhibit robust exosome-releasing capacity and these exosomes can transport cell-type-specific proteins and membrane receptors, an intriguing question arises: Could microglial exosomes serve as intercellular vehicles for LAG3 receptor transfer, thereby potentiating α-synuclein internalization in cholinergic neurons and ultimately contributing to their selective vulnerability in LBD?

In this study, we explored the potential impact of exosomes derived from activated microglia, as well as exosome-borne α-Syn-specific internalization receptor LAG3, on the function and viability of cholinergic neurons in NBM. Our results showed that activated microglia within the NBM secrete LAG3-rich exosomes. The translocation of LAG3 from exosomes to the neuronal membrane promoted the uptake of α-Syn by cholinergic neurons, ultimately culminating in neuronal dysfunction and cognition decline. Our discovery of α-Syn propagation via exosomes derived from activated microglia provides one alternative explanation for α-Syn spreading in CNS. Furthermore, we shed light on the susceptibility of the basal forebrain cholinergic nuclei in LBD for the first time. As a potential intervention target, microglia-derived exosomes in the NBM region may provide a new perspective for early treatment strategies.

Materials and methods

Participants

For this cross-sectional study, 38 people with Parkinson’s disease who met the UK Brain Bank clinical diagnostic criteria and had no dementia were included in the PDND group. According to available clinical information, PDD was diagnosed if dementia developed in the setting of well-established PD and at least one year after the onset of Parkinsonian motor signs, while DLB was diagnosed when cognitive symptoms developed precedes and within one year from the onset of motor symptoms. We consolidated 34 patients into the PDD/DLB group in this study based on their similar neuropathological characterization. Neurologists collected demographic data (age, gender, educational level) from all participants and conducted extensive neuropsychological testing to assess overall cognition, memory, visuospatial abilities, language, attention, and executive functions. Global cognitive function was evaluated using the MMSE and MOCA. The Logical Memory Test was employed to assess narrative episodic memory. The ROCFT was used to evaluate patients’ visuospatial abilities and working memory. The VFT was administered to test patients’ language and semantic memory, while the Boston Naming Test was used to measure confrontational word retrieval. The Digit Span Test was used to assess patients’ verbal working memory, attention, cognitive control, and executive function. The SDMT was employed to evaluate patients’ executive functions, cognitive processing speed, and attention [32]. This study obtained ethical approval from the Research Ethics Committee of Huashan Hospital, Shanghai, China, and written informed consent from each participant.

Clinical neuro-image acquisition and analysis

All MRI examinations for enrolled patients were conducted at the Department of Radiology of Huashan Hospital, Fudan University, using a 3 T GE Discovery MR750 scanner (Milwaukee, WI, USA) equipped with a circular polarized eight-channel head matrix coil. High-resolution T1-weighted 3D anatomical brain images were obtained using an inversion recovery prepared fast spoiled gradient recalled sequence. Preprocessing of MRI images was performed using Statistical Parametric Mapping 12 software (The Wellcome Department of Neurology, London, UK) implemented in Matlab 2022a (Mathworks Inc.). The DICOM data exported from the scanner were converted to NII format, recognizable by SPM, using the MRIcron software package. Subsequently, tissue segmentation, registration, spatial normalization, and spatial smoothing (with an 8 mm Gaussian kernel) were performed. Four regions of interest (ROIs) were obtained using the atlas built into the SPM 12 Anatomy toolbox, targeting the basal forebrain: left CH123, right CH123, left NBM (CH4), and right NBM. Two- sample t-tests were conducted on the gray matter images of the ROIs using SPM12, with sex, age, and total intracranial volume (TIV) as covariates, to compare the differences between the two groups. Statistical results were corrected for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate (FDR), with voxel-level significance set at P < 0.001 and FDR-corrected P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Reporting and mapping of results were conducted using the XJVIEW software package. CH4 volume was extracted by the DPABI package (DPABI_V8.2, https://rfmri.org/DPABI). Pearson’s correlation analysis was performed to assess the correlation between the ROI brain regions and cognitive scores of the patients, with P < 0.05 indicating statistical significance.

For the clinical subjects undergoing TSPO PET imaging, the retention of TSPO was measured 40 min after the intravenous injection of 370 MBq of [18F] DPA-714 and lasted for 20 min. All PET scans were performed using a Siemens mCT flow edge 128 (Siemens, Germany) in three-dimensional (3D) mode. All participants remained awake during the uptake and scanning procedures. PET image processing was conducted using PMOD image analysis software (version 3.7; PMOD Technologies Ltd, Zurich, Switzerland). The PET images were first spatially normalized to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space based on the PMOD template. The ROI for the NBM region was delineated and evaluated for quality by two physicians with over five years of experience in neuroimaging data processing.

Primary cholinergic neuron culture

Primary cholinergic neurons in the basal forebrain were isolated and cultured according to a previously established protocol with modifications [33]. Briefly, the brains of E17-18 mouse embryos were scooped out of the skull. After removing the olfactory tubercles and meninges, the hemispheres were bluntly spread apart along the longitudinal fissure to expose the septum. Next, meninges-free septa were dissected, subjected to trypsin-EDTA (Gibco) digestion for 30 min in a shaker, and then dispersed in complete DMEM with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco). The dissociated cells were plated onto poly-L-lysine- coated glass-bottom cell culture dishes (NEST) or 100 mm dishes at a density ranging from 3 to 5 × 105 cells/ml. Four hours later, medium was exchanged into serum-free Neurobasal™ (Gibco) Medium supplemented with 2% B27 (Gibco), 2 mM L-glutamine (Gibco), and penicillin/streptomycin (Life Technologies). The cultured neurons were utilized for in vitro studies on Day 7 after exchanging half of the medium every 48 h. Cell purity was confirmed by immunofluorescence staining using ChAT as a specific marker for neurons.

Primary microglia culture and activation

Primary microglia were isolated from postnatal Day 1–3 (P1-3) C57BL/6 neonatal pups following previously established protocol [19]. In brief, meninges-free cortices were dissected and subjected to trypsinization. The isolated cells were then cultured in complete DMEM/F12 (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS and penicillin/streptomycin. Murine monocyte colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF, 10 ng/ml, Peprotech) was introduced into the medium six days post-plating. After 15 days, microglia were harvested by vigorous shaking (4 h, 260 rpm) to detach them from the culture. The purity of the isolated microglia was confirmed by immunofluorescence staining using Iba-1 as a specific marker. Primary microglia were activated by treatment with 100ng/ml LPS (Sigma-Aldrich) plus 100ng/ml IFN-γ (Gibco) for 24 h. After stimulating microglia with IFN-γ and LPS for 24 h to induce activation, the supernatant was discarded. The cells were washed three times with sterile PBS, and the culture medium was replaced with standard medium. After 24 h, this supernatant was collected as conditioned medium (CM) for neuronal treatment.

Exosomes purification and identification

Exosomes were isolated and purified by ultracentrifugation. In brief, to obtain primary microglia derived exosomes, cells were cultured in advance in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Before extracting exosomes, the standard medium was switched to one containing 10% exosome-free serum (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After 15 min of ATP (InvivoGen) stimulation, the culture medium supernatant was collected in a centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 2000 g at 4 ℃ for 10 min to remove cell debris. The supernatant was put into a new centrifuge tube and centrifuged again at 10,000 g at 4 ℃ for 30 min. The supernatant was then collected carefully and transferred to a new centrifuge tube with an ultra-fast rotor selected and centrifuged for 70 min at 120,000 g at 4 °C and repeated twice. After discarding the supernatant, the precipitate was resuspended using 100ul of precooled PBS to obtain pure microglia-derived exosomes. The exosome suspension was diluted 1:100-1:500. Samples were tested for particle size using NanoSight NS300 (NanoSight Ltd.) and repeated at least three times. The number of particles was analyzed using Nanoparticle Size Tracking Analysis 3.0 software. For electron microscopy, the exosome suspension was diluted 1:50. The exosomes were adsorbed on the copper net for 90 s, stained negatively with uranyl acetate for 30 s, dried and observed by transmission electron microscopy.

Exosomes labelling

Exosomes were labeled for tracking using the PKH26 Red Fluorescent Cell Linker Mini Kit (Sigma-Aldrich). The PKH26 linker dye was diluted with Diluent C to prepare a working solution at a final concentration of 100 µM. This working solution was added to the exosome suspension (50 µl of the working solution per 100 µg of exosomal protein), vortexed for 1 min, and incubated for 10 min. After incubation, 10 ml of PBS was added to the GDEs-PKH26 complex and mixed thoroughly. The exosomes were then purified again by ultracentrifugation to remove excess dye, and the pellet was resuspended in 200 µl of PBS, yielding PKH26-labeled GDEs. For tracking PKH26-labeled GDEs, primary neurons in good condition were incubated with the labeled exosomes for certain hours. The intracellular distribution of red fluorescence was observed using confocal microscopy (ZEISS).

Preparation of α-Syn-PFF and α-Syn-biotin PFF

PFF were prepared by incubating 5 mg/ml a-Syn monomer (Proteos, USA) with an orbital mixer (Eppendorf) at 1000 rpm for 7 days at 37 °C, and then aliquoted and kept at − 80 °C. Before treatment, the α-Syn aggregates were sonicated at power level 2 for 30 s (QSonica XL-2000). To distinguish exogenous α-Syn-PFF from endogenous protein, α-Syn-biotin PFF were prepared by recombinant α-Syn monomers conjugated with sulfo-NHS-LC-Biotin (Thermo Scientific) according to the accompanying protocol [34].

Western blotting

Neurons and microglia were lysed on ice using RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Beyotime Biotechnology, Beijing, China). For the analysis of α-synuclein, soluble and insoluble cell fractions were prepared as previously described [35]. Cells were incubated on ice with TX-100 lysis buffer (150 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris, 1% Triton X-100 in TBS) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors for 25 min. The lysates were then briefly sonicated and centrifuged at 13,000 g for 15 min. The supernatant, referred to as the “TX-100 soluble fraction,” was collected. The remaining pellet was extracted with 2% SDS in TBS, referred to as the “TX-100 insoluble fraction.” Exosomes from treated microglia were purified from the culture medium, lysed with RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors, and sonicated before loading. After quantifying the proteins, SDS-PAGE was performed, and the proteins were transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore). Following blocking with 5% non-fat milk (Merck), the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: total α-Syn antibody (1:1000; Abcam, ab13850), p-α-Syn antibody (1:1000; Abcam, ab51253), anti-biotin (1:1000; Abcam, ab1227), anti-TSG101 (1:1000; Abcam, ab125011), anti-LAG3 (1:1000; Cell Signaling, E5S8V), anti-β-actin (1:1000; Abcam, ab1801), and anti-FLAG (1:5000; Millipore, MAB3118). The membranes were then incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Jackson ImmunoResearch) at room temperature for 1 h. Protein bands were visualized using a chemiluminescence ECL kit and imaged with a chemiluminescence imaging system. Band intensity was quantified using ImageJ software.

Ach ELISA

Ach ELISA kit (BioVision, USA) was utilized to detect Ach levels in cell supernatant and brain tissue homogenates. After rinsing the reaction wells with wash solution, standard working solution and specimens were added to each reaction well, respectively. Biotin-conjugated Ach antibody was then added and incubated at 37 °C for 45 min. The samples were discarded and washed 4 times with the wash solution. HRP-labeled streptavidin was added to each well, and after incubation at 37 °C for 30 min, the mixture was washed four times. Wells were measured by substrate reagents for color reaction and incubating at 37 °C in the dark for 15 min, the reaction termination solution was added, shaken and mixed until the color of the sample was developed and then placed in a microplate reader to record the absorbance at 450 nm. The concentration of Ach contained in the samples was calculated according to the standard curve.

RT-qPCR assay

Total RNA was isolated from cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA library construction was performed using the Prime-Script RT Master Mix Kit (TaKaRa, Kyoto, Japan). RT-qPCR was conducted on the Mx-3000P Quantitative PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA) using the SYBR Green Master Mix Kit (TaKaRa). The primers used in this study were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China), with sequences as follows: Lag3 (F: AGGAGGCCAGGCTCCTTGCT, R: CCTTATAGTCCTTATCATCGTC). Gapdh was used as the housekeeping gene.

Immuno-electron microscopy (IEM)

According to the protocol previously described [36], fixed exosomes at the optimal concentration were placed onto 400-mesh carbon/formvar-coated grids and allowed to adhere for at least 1 min. Subsequently, grids with adsorbed exosomes were incubated in blocking buffer for 1 h to block non-specific binding. The grids were then incubated with primary antibodies (anti-LAG3, 1:100, Abcam, ab209238) overnight at 4 °C. After washing the grids with PBS, they were incubated with secondary antibodies conjugated to gold nanoparticles at room temperature for 2 h. After washing and air-drying, the grids were stained with uranyl acetate for contrast. The labeled exosomes were analyzed and imaged using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) with a Hitachi microscope from Japan. For cryo-IEM, neurons were fixed in 4% PFA/0.1% glutaraldehyde solution for 1 h at room temperature away from light, scraped, and pellets resuspended. Cell pellets were cooled and cleaned in the pre-cooled 0.1 MPB (pH 7.4) at 4℃ and then wrapped in the 2% agarose. After dehydration, resin embedding and polymerization, the resin blocks were cut to 80 nm thin, and floated out onto the 150 meshes nickel grids with formvar film prior to labelling with anti-LAG3 (1:100; Abcam, ab209238) and proteinA-gold (1:50, Abcam, ab270555). Sections were stained with 2% uranyl acetate and observed under TEM, and images were taken.

Construction and transfection of lentiviral vectors

The lentiviral vectors used for Lag3 overexpression in this study were prepared by Gene-Chem (Shanghai, China). The construction of stable cell lines was briefly described as follows. BV2 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 10^4 cells per well. The appropriate volume of viral supernatant was added to the culture plates (MOI = 50), and the cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 8–16 h. Subsequently, 1.5 µg/ml puromycin (Thermo Fisher) was added to each well for selection of stable cell lines.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining of cells was performed in confocal dishes. Briefly, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 20 min, and blocked with 3% BSA for 1 h. The dishes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-LAG3 (1:100, Abcam, ab209238), anti-p-a-syn (1:1000, Abcam, ab51253), anti-Map-2 (1:2000, Abcam, ab5392), anti-ChAT (1:800, ThermoFisher, MA5-31382), anti-Iba1 (1:1000, Abcam, ab107159), anti-EEA-1 (1:500, Abcam, ab109110), anti-RAB7 (1:200, Abcam, ab137029), anti-LAMP-1 (1:100, Abcam, ab208943), and anti-RAB11 (1:50, Cell Signaling Technology, 5589 S). The cells were then incubated with fluorescent secondary antibodies for 1 h at 25 °C. After washing with PBS, the cells were mounted using an antifade solution containing DAPI (Abcam). IF staining of brain slices was performed using standard protocols. Brain tissues were cryo-sectioned at 12 μm. The sections were first permeabilized with 0.05% Triton X-100 for 30 min, followed by incubation in blocking buffer (5% donkey serum and 0.05% Triton X-100 in TBS) for 2 h. After removing the blocking buffer, sections were incubated with the primary antibodies: anti-ChAT (1:800, Invitrogen, MA5-31382), anti-CD68 (1:100, Abcam, ab283654), anti-p-a-Syn (1:500, Abcam, ab59264), and anti-FLAG (1:1000, Millipore, MAB3118) overnight at 4 °C. The sections were then washed in TBS and incubated with secondary antibodies conjugated to Alexa Fluor 488, 647, or Cy3 for 1 h at room temperature. The secondary antibodies were sourced from Jackson ImmunoResearch. Finally, sections were counterstained with DAPI for 5 min and mounted in fluorescence mounting medium (Abcam). Images were captured using a confocal microscope (ZEISS).

Immunohistochemistry assay

The immunohistochemical staining method for mouse brain sections is briefly described as follows. After euthanizing the mice, the brain tissue was quickly extracted and immediately fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at 4 °C for 24 h. The fixed brain tissue was then dehydrated through a series of sucrose solutions and embedded in paraffin. Using a rotary microtome, the paraffin-embedded brain tissue was sectioned into 15-micron thick serial sections, which were mounted onto glass slides. The brain sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated through xylene and graded ethanol solutions, followed by a wash in distilled water. For antigen retrieval, the sections were placed in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) or EDTA buffer (pH 8.0) and heated in a microwave oven on high power for 10–20 min. After cooling to room temperature, the sections were washed with PBS. The sections were blocked at room temperature for 1 h with 5–10% donkey serum to reduce nonspecific staining. After removing the blocking solution, appropriately diluted primary antibodies anti-p-a-Syn (1:500, Abcam, ab59264) were applied, and the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C. The appropriate secondary antibodies were then applied, followed by incubation at room temperature for 1 h and DAB chromogenic development. Finally, hematoxylin was used for counterstaining, and mounting medium (such as neutral resin) was applied before covering with a coverslip. The stained sections were observed under a light microscope(Nikon, Japan), and images were captured and analyzed.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay

The co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays were performed utilizing the Pierce Classic Magnetic IP/Co-IP Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) to investigate protein interactions according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, cells from each experimental group were washed with PBS and lysed on ice for 30 min using the specialized Co-IP lysis buffer. Following lysis, the samples were centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was transferred to a new EP tube. Anti-FLAG-conjugated magnetic beads were then added to each supernatant sample, and the mixture was incubated overnight at 4 °C with gentle rotation. The magnetic beads were collected using a magnetic rack to obtain the immunoprecipitates. The bound protein complexes were subsequently analyzed using Western blot to detect specific protein bands.

Animals

The P1-3d C57BL/6 neonatal mice and E14-16d pregnant C57BL/6 mice used for primary isolation were purchased from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. 6–8-week-old male WT C57BL/6 mice were obtained from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. The animals were housed in the animal facility of Shanghai Tenth People’s Hospital, with appropriate density, free access to food and water, and regular replacement of irradiated sterilized bedding. The animal facility maintained a constant light/dark cycle (12/12 hours) with a room temperature of 25 ± 2 °C throughout the day. For mice requiring induction of microglial depletion, a diet containing 1200 mg/kg PLX5622 (Abmole, USA) was provided for 4 weeks prior to surgery. Control groups were fed with control chow without PLX5622. All animal experiments in this study were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee.

Stereotaxic injection

After anesthesia of mice with 1% pentobarbital sodium, mice were secured in a stereotaxic apparatus. Following hair removal and disinfection, the skin was incised along the sagittal suture to expose the anterior fontanelle. NBM injection (AP: −0.6 mm; ML: ±2.1 mm; DV: 4.4–4.6 mm) was performed using a 10 µl syringe (Hamilton) loaded with a glass pipette, at an injection rate of 0.1 µl/min. The needle was left in place for 10 min on each side after injection.

Morris watermaze

Circular pool filled with opaque water mixed with non-toxic white paint was divided into 4 quadrants, with a transparent plastic platform submerged 1 cm beneath the water surface in the fourth quadrant. Visual cues were positioned around the perimeter of the pool. The place navigation test spanned 5 days. Mice were placed into the pool facing the wall from midpoint along the pool wall in either quadrant and allowed to freely swim for 60 s or until they staying on the platform. The time taken to find the platform within 60 s and swimming paths were recorded. The average latency to find the platform over four training trials per day was calculated to present the daily learning performance. After completing daily trials, mice were dried and returned to their cages. In the spatial probe test, spatial memory for the platform location was assessed with the platform removed. Swimming paths and the number of platform entries were recorded.

Open field

Mice were placed in a behavioral chamber measuring 40 × 40 × 15 cm, with a central area of 20 × 20 cm demarcated using computer software. The test duration was 5 min. Horizontal activity of the mice, including total distance traveled and time spent in the central area, was detected by cameras within the chamber. After the test, analysis of these two parameters was conducted using ANYmaze behavioral software.

Novel object recognition

Mice were pre-adapted to the environment of a square behavioral chamber measuring 44 × 44 cm. The task, lasting for 2 days, consisted of three trials. On the first day, mice were individually placed into the chamber and allowed to freely explore for 5 minutes. After exploration, the mice were removed and returned to their housing cages. The chamber was then cleaned to eliminate any odors. This habituation trial was conducted once in the morning and once in the afternoon. In the familiarization trial on the second day, mice were allowed to explore two identical objects (1 and 1’) placed in two opposite areas of the chamber for 10 minutes. After a 1-hour interval, the test trial was conducted: object 1 remained unchanged (old object), while object 1’ was replaced with object 2 (novel object). Mice were then placed back into the chamber to explore for 10 min. The experimental results of the mice were recorded using ANYmaze software. The percent novel object exploration was calculated according to the formula: percent novel object exploration = ((novel object exploration time)/(novel object exploration time + familiar object exploration time)) × 100%.

Rotarod

For three consecutive days prior to the experiment, mice were trained once a day at a fixed time using fixed parameters (4r/min). During the formal experiment, the rotation speed was set to increase gradually from 4 r/min to 40 r/min within 300 s. The time until the mouse fell off was recorded, followed by a 5-minute rest period. Three trials were repeated and the average latency to fall was calculated.

Pole test

Before testing, mice were trained for two consecutive days. Mice were placed at the top of a test pole with heads facing upward. The time was recorded when the animal began to turn around and when it reached the bottom of the pole. Each group of mice underwent the test three times, and the average time was calculated. If the animal stopped during the descent, the data for that group were excluded, and the test was repeated.

Grip strength

Neuromuscular strength was measured by the maximum grip strength generated by the mice. Mice were allowed to grip a metal grid with their forelimbs and/or hindlimbs. Then, the tail of the mouse was slowly pulled horizontally until it released its grip from the grid, and the maximum pulling force displayed on the sensor was recorded. Each group of mice underwent the test three times, and the average was calculated.

Exosomal lipidomics analysis

Lipids were extracted from samples using a modified Bligh/Dyer method (dual extraction). Reconstituted samples in isotope-labeled internal standard mixtures were analyzed on an Exion UPLC-QTRAP 6500 Plus system (Sciex) with electrospray ionization (ESI) under the following conditions: curtain gas = 20, ion spray voltage = 5500 V, temperature = 400 °C, ion source gas 1 = 35, ion source gas 2 = 35. Polar lipid separation was achieved using a Phenomenex Luna silica column. Polar lipids were identified and quantified via mass spectrometric multiple reaction monitoring (MRM). For neutral lipid characterization, reverse-phase RP-HPLC-MS/MS was employed for qualitative and quantitative analysis. Free cholesterol, sterols, and corresponding esters were analyzed using HPLC-MS/MS with atmospheric pressure chemical ionization (APCI).

Modulation of exosomal cholesterol content

Cholesterol was depleted from A-MG-EXO membranes using methyl-β-cyclodextrin (MβCD). Briefly, exosomes were resuspended in PBS and treated with MβCD. MβCD was added to 1 mg of A-MG-EXO to achieve a final concentration of 4 mM. Samples were incubated at 37 °C for 2 h to facilitate cholesterol removal. After incubation, the sample was centrifuged at 140,000 × g for 30 min. The resulting pellet was carefully collected, washed with 1 ml of PBS, and subjected to successive ultracentrifugation at 100,000 × g for 70 min at 4 °C. The final exosome pellet was resuspended for subsequent experiments.

Statistics

Differences between groups were analyzed using independent-sample t-tests, one-way or two-way ANOVA, followed by Newman-Keuls post hoc tests for pairwise comparisons using R version 4.2.1 and GraphPad Prism version 9.0. The null hypothesis was rejected at a P-value of less than 0.05. Data are presented as Mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Results

Volumetric loss and microglia activation within the NBM region in LBD patients

To examine the brain structure changes of NBM, we compared grey matter volume between patients with nondemented Parkinson’s disease (PDND) and those with PDD/DLB. The NBM region of interest was delineated using the established probabilistic atlas, JuBrain Anatomy Toolbox (https://www.fz-juelich.de/en/inm/inm-7/resources/jubrain-anatomy-toolbox/jubrain-toolbox). According to Meynert’s original description and Mesulam’s nomenclature, Ch1-Ch4 respectively correspond to the four groups of cholinergic neurons in basal forebrain. In the selected atlas, Ch4_L and Ch4_R refer to left and right NBM, respectively [7].

Demographics and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. No significant differences were observed in gender distribution and education between PDND and PDD/DLB participants, then the age of PDND was younger than PDD/DLB patients (p < 0.001). All subjects underwent comprehensive neuropsychological examinations involving multiple cognition domains. As expected, individuals with cognitive impairment exhibited poorer scores across various cognitive domains (Table 1). After removing the confounding factors of the following four covariates: age, gender, TIV, and education, compared to PDND, volumetric reductions in CH4 (NBM) were evident in PDD/DLB group (Fig. 1a). Pearson correlation analysis showed that, smaller Ch4 volume correlated with lower Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) (R = 0.445.; p < 0.001) and Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) scores (R = 0.405; p < 0.001), which represent global cognitive function. As for cognition domains, Rey–Osterrieth Complex Figure Test (ROCFT) (for immediate recall, R = 0.423; p < 0.001) and Verbal Fluency Test (VFT) for language function (R = 0.326; p = 0.005) and Symbol Digit Modalities Test (SDMT) for attention/executive function (R = 0.330; p = 0.005) exhibited positive association with Ch4 volume (Fig. 1b-f).

Translocator protein (TSPO) PET holds promise as a valuable tool for detecting active CNS inflammation, given TSPO’s upregulation in activated microglia [37]. We explored tracer uptake patterns in the NBM region with the novel TSPO tracer [18 F] DPA-714 in both groups of patients. The results showed that compared to the PDND group, patients with PDD/DLB generally exhibited higher isotope uptake signals in the NBM region. Here, we presented PET images of a 68-year-old DLB patient with mild cognitive impairment as an example, which exhibited elevated focal tracer uptake in CH4 region (Fig 1 g). This finding suggests the presence of microglial activation within this brain region, where cortical cholinergic innervation originates. Activated microglia may be involved in the neurodegenerative pathology of the NBM.

Microglial depletion alleviates cognitive impairment and neuronal α-Syn pathology in PFF NBM-injected mice

Previous study confirmed that the activation of microglia was associated with α-Syn deposition and neurodegenerative changes in synucleinopathies [38]. Whether pathologically activated microglia drive the degeneration of NBM is largely unknown. To further investigate the impact of activated microglia in the NBM region on neuronal internalization of α-Syn, we first established a mouse model of NBM α-Syn preformed fibrils (PFF) injection and observed the behavioral manifestations of cognitive dysfunction in this unreported animal model, then further examined the activation status of microglia in NBM region. Stereotaxic injection of PFF (2 µg/µL) was administered bilaterally into the NBM of 6–8-week-old wild-type C57BL/6J mice (1µL/side). Given that the NBM is a discrete nucleus with no distinct boundaries, we initially injected fluorescent dye and observed that green fluorophores appeared where choline acetyltransferase (ChAT) positive cells were grouped (Fig. 2a, left panel). After 2 weeks of injection, visible colocalization was observed between ChAT positive cholinergic neurons and α-Syn positive neurons, indicating the uptake of exogenous α-Syn by cholinergic neurons in vivo (Fig. 2a, right panel). We further investigated the cholinergic neuronal loss and α-Syn deposition at various time points after PFF injection. Results indicated a gradual loss of cholinergic neurons, concomitant with an increasing trend in of p-α-Syn staining intensity in the NBM region at 0, 2, 4, 6 and 8 weeks post-injection (Extended Data Fig. 1a). Subsequently, cognitive behavioral experiments, including water maze, open field test, and novel object recognition, were conducted to assess cognitive impairment progression. Water maze results showed a decline in learning and memory abilities from 4 weeks after PFF injection, although not significantly different. Distinct impairments in spatial learning and memory abilities were evident at 6 weeks post-injection (Extended Data Figure. 2a-d). No statistical difference was observed in the open field test (Extended Data Figure. 2e-h). Consistent with findings from watermaze, we observed a significant decline in object recognition memory at 6 weeks (Extended Data Figure. 2i-j). To mitigate potential confounding effects of motor ability differences on the cognitive behavioral tests, additional assessments, including the pole test and climbing test, were conducted. Measurement of musculature strength in the mice’s forelimbs and hindlimbs revealed no significant differences between the groups of mice compared to the 0wpi group (Extended Data Figure. 3a-d).

After validating the behavioral and pathological changes in the PFF NBM-injected model, we proceeded to quantify acetylcholine (Ach) levels in various brain regions. Fresh brain tissues were extracted from the mice and systematically isolated regions, including the NBM, cortex (CTX), medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), hippocampus (Hippo), and caudate putamen (CPU) guided by anatomical atlases and injection needle tracing. The ELISA results revealed that beyond 2 weeks, Ach levels in these regions gradually decreased over time, exhibiting significant differences compared to the control group (Extended Data Figure. 4a-c). Conversely, no significant changes were observed in Ach levels within the hippocampus and caudate putamen tissues of the mice (Extended Data Figure. 4d-e). These results indicate that a significant reduction in NBM Ach levels not only coincides with behavior and pathological alterations but also extends to the NBM projecting brain regions in the NBM PFF-injection model.

Pathological α-Syn has been shown to induce the upregulation of CD68 in the mouse brain, enabling discrimination between surveilling and activated microglia. Immunofluorescence results of brain sections showed the accumulation of CD68-positive microglia (Iba-1+) around the NBM region after injection (Extended Data Figure. 1b). To further investigate the impact of microglia activation on Lewy body pathology formation and degeneration progression of cholinergic nucleus in the NBM region, we administered chow containing PLX5622 to feed the mice for four consecutive weeks before PFF injection and throughout the entire experimental period (Fig. 2b). PLX5622, a colony-stimulating factor 1 receptor inhibitor, has been shown to selectively deplete microglia in the mouse brain after three weeks of continuous feeding [39, 40]. Immunofluorescence staining in Extended Data Fig. 1c demonstrates the depletion of microglia in the mouse brain following PLX5622 diet administration.

After 6 weeks post-injection, behavioral tests and Ach-ELISA were conducted. Immunohistochemical staining was utilized to examine the α-Syn deposition within the NBM. Results revealed that microglia depletion (PFF + PLX) remarkably alleviated the cognitive deficits induced by PFF injection (Fig. 2c-g). Additionally, PFF + PLX group exhibited notably increased Ach levels in the NBM, CTX and mPFC regions compared to the PFF group. No significant changes in Ach levels were observed in the hippocampus and striatum among the groups (Fig. 2h). More importantly, following microglia depletion, the deposition of α-Syn in the neurons within the NBM was significantly reduced (Fig. 2i). In summary, the above results demonstrated that PFF injection induced α-Syn pathology in NBM which led to decreased cognitive function and Ach release. Depletion of microglia exhibited a neuroprotective effect by mitigating neurobehavioral damage and rescuing the neuronal somatic pathological α-Syn deposition in NBM.

A-MG-EXO enhances the ability of α-Syn uptake of primary cholinergic neurons and results in synaptic damage

In Parkinson’s disease and other related conditions, various neurotoxins, including α-Syn and lipopolysaccharide (LPS), have been shown to activate microglia [41]. The activated microglia further exacerbate neurotoxicity and promote α-Syn spreading [42]. Our previous study has shown that after treatment with PFF, activated microglia release exosomes, inducing the aggregation of insoluble α-Syn in recipient neurons [20]. To investigate whether activated microglia could influence the uptake of pathological seeds in recipient neurons through alternative mechanisms in addition to exosomal microglia-to-neuron transmission, we used a treatment involving the addition of 100 ng/ml LPS and 100 ng/ml IFN-γ to activate primary microglia instead of PFF to exclude interference from seed sources originating from microglial exosomes. We confirmed the expression of specific markers of activated microglia through immunofluorescence staining, revealing Cox-2 and iNOS in activated microglia (Extended Data Figure.5a). The isolation and culture of primary cholinergic neurons followed established protocols, and immunoblotting and immunofluorescence experiments confirmed the immunoreactivity of ChAT (Extended Data Figure.5b, Figure. 6).

To assess the impact of activated microglia on the internalization of synuclein fibers in cholinergic neurons. We incubated the cholinergic neurons with conditioned medium from normal cultured (MG-CM) or activated microglia (A-MG-CM) for 2 days before PFF were added. PFF were further incubated for 1 day to allow internalization of synuclein fibers to occur. TX-100 insoluble α-Syn aggregation in the recipient neurons, as assessed using an antibody against total α-Syn or an antibody against p-Ser129 α-Syn, was dramatically increased in the A-MG-CM group compared to the MG-CM group (Fig. 3a), indicating that the conditioned medium of activated microglia can promote the internalization of synuclein fibers in cholinergic neurons.

To further explain the components in the conditioned media of activated microglia that contribute to the aggregation of insoluble α-Syn in cholinergic neurons, we employed an alternative approach outlined in Fig. 3b. Initially, cell culture medium derived from activated microglia underwent centrifugation at low speed to eliminate cellular debris. This resulted in a clarified conditioned medium (A-MG-CM), which still retained exosomes and cytokines. Subsequently, the clarified conditioned medium underwent ultra-centrifugation, and the supernatant was collected as exosome-free conditioned medium (EF-CM). Following this, the conditioned medium from active microglia (A-MG-CM), purified exosome fractions from activated microglia (A-MG-EXO), or exosome-free conditioned medium (EF-CM) were individually added to cultured cholinergic neurons and then incubated for 2 days before PFF were added. Western blot analysis showed that in the A-MG-CM and A-MG-EXO groups, there was a significant increase in TX-100-insoluble α-Syn aggregation in the recipient neurons, whereas this effect was largely absent in the exosome-free conditioned medium (Fig. 3c). Treatment with A-MG-EXO resulted in elevated levels of pathological p-α-Syn, and total α-Syn in the TX-100-insoluble fraction. It is proposed that exosomes from activated microglia promote the accumulation of pathogenic aggregates in neurons, which is chiefly driven by the exogenous PFF uptake process.

To further understand the impact of activated microglial exosomes on the synaptic morphology and neurotransmitter release of cholinergic neurons, we isolated the exosomes derived from microglia. Transmission electron microscopy and nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) analysis revealed that extracellular vesicles exhibit a cup-shaped morphology and a size distribution ranging from 30 to 150 nm (Extended Data Figure.5c-d). Cholinergic neurons were then incubated with exosomes isolated from normal culture (MG-EXO) or activated microglia (A-MG-EXO) before α-Syn PFF was added, the level of Ach and morphology of neurons were subsequently evaluated. Compared to MG-EXO, exosomes secreted by activated microglia significantly exacerbated the decrease in synaptic branches and lengths induced by PFF(Fig. 3d-f). This finding is consistent with the results of Ach levels (Fig. 3g), indicating the exosomes secreted by activated/non-activated microglia have markedly different effects on the morphology and function of recipient neurons. Overall, these findings demonstrated that the exosomes derived from activated microglia promote synaptic damage in cholinergic neurons, which may be associated with their facilitation of neuronal uptake of exogenous α-Syn fibers.

Exosomes derived from activated microglia exacerbate a-Synuclein deposition in NBM and behavioral impairment

To further investigate the potential role of microglial exosomes in mediating NBM neurodegeneration and α-Syn pathology in vivo, we isolated exosomes from both non-activated and LPS-activated microglia, and then stereotaxically injected these exosomes into the bilateral NBM region of mice 3 days after PFF injection. All mice fed on the PLX5622 diet to deplete endogenous microglia. Morris water maze tests were conducted at 6 weeks post-injection, after which all animals were sacrificed for subsequent biological experiments (Fig. 4a). The animals in the PLX + PFF + A-MG-EXO group exhibited distinctly worse cognitive function (Fig. 4b-f). ELISA results revealed a significant decrease in the levels of Ach in the NBM and projecting cortical areas of mice after A-MG-EXO combined with PFF injection, with statistically noticeable differences compared to the other groups (Fig. 4g). Using p-α-Syn antibodies, we performed immunohistochemical analysis in NBM after injection to map out α-Syn pathology. After 6 weeks, few p-α-Syn was detected in mice injected with PBS; however, more pronounced LB pathology was detected in the NBM region of mice injected with exosomes derived from LPS-activated microglia (A-MG-EXO) compared to those injected with non-activated microglia exosomes (MG-EXO) (Fig. 4h).

LAG3 expression changes in recipient cholinergic neurons potentially implicated in extracellular pathogenic α-Syn uptake

To ascertain whether the pathological aggregates in recipient neurons are attributable to the uptake of extracellular PFF seeds rather than endogenous α-Syn aggregation, we employed recombinant α-Syn conjugated to biotin to form α-Syn-biotin, followed by aggregation over seven days. After sonication, the morphology and size of α-Syn-biotin were confirmed using transmission electron microscopy (Fig. 5a). Subsequently, cholinergic neurons were treated with α-Syn-biotin for 24 h, followed by immunoblotting using an anti-biotin antibody to detect exogenous α-Syn in lysates extracted from 2% SDS (TX-100-insoluble) fractions. Neuronal α-Syn-biotin levels were significantly increased after incubation with exosomes from LPS-activated microglia, but this alteration was not observed in the MG-EXO group (Fig. 5b), suggesting that exosomes derived from activated microglia facilitate the internalization of exogenous α-Syn seeds by cholinergic neurons via a distinct mechanism.

Previous studies by Mao et al. have revealed the high-affinity binding of LAG3 to α-Syn PFFs, orchestrating the transmission and toxicity of α-Syn PFF [43]. Despite widespread expression in peripheral immune cells and microglia, the expression of LAG3 on neurons remains contentious. Our analysis of LAG3 expression in primary cholinergic neurons unveiled its near absence in non-transgenic primary cholinergic neurons (Extended Data Figure.6b). Notably, exposure to A-MG-EXO resulted in a significant increase in LAG3 expression in cholinergic neurons, a phenomenon not observed with MG-EXO treatment (Fig. 5c-d). Subsequent q-PCR aimed to evaluate the LAG3 mRNA levels in neurons exposed to these two exosomes, with glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase serving as a reference gene for measuring gene transcripts. Surprisingly, A-MG-EXO intervention failed to increase LAG3 mRNA expression (Fig. 5e), suggesting that the modulation of LAG3 protein expression in cholinergic neurons is independent of transcriptional changes.

Previous single-cell sequencing findings and related basic research have solidified the notion that LAG3 is predominantly expressed in microglia within the brain. Our data also confirmed the expression of LAG3 in LPS-activated primary microglia (Extended Data Figure.6a). Given the divergent effects observed with exosomes released from non-active/LPS-activated microglia, we isolated exosomes derived from two groups of microglia and performed Western blot validation to assess the expression of exosomal LAG3. We observed a pronounced upregulation of LAG3 expression in the A-MG-EXO, while it was almost undetectable in the MG-EXO group using a specific antibody (Fig. 5f). These results indicate that activated microglia release a mass of LAG3-rich exosomes after LPS stimulation. Moreover, the translocated LAG3 from activated microglial exosomes to neurons - rather than genomic alteration- may largely influence the disparate internalization of α-Syn PFF in cholinergic neurons.

Microglia-derived LAG3 experiences membrane translocation from A-MG-EXO to cholinergic neurons via the early endosome-recycling endosome-membrane migration pathway

Based on the previous observations, we propose that after being taken up by neurons, A-MG-EXO can translocate LAG3 from microglial exosomes to recipient neurons. To validate this hypothesis, we first performed exosome immunoelectron microscopy to determine whether LAG3 is located on the exosome membrane or enclosed within vesicles as cargo. Immunoelectron microscopy revealed the presence of LAG3 on cup holder-like exosomes, as indicated by gold-conjugated antibodies labeling (Fig. 6a). It was evident that LAG3 clustered on the membrane of the exosomes (Fig. 6a). Further elucidating the subcellular distribution of the LAG3 in recipient neurons at the ultrastructural level, cholinergic neurons exposed to A-MG-EXO for two days were fixed and immunolabeled for detection under immunoelectron microscopy. As shown in Fig. 6b, immunogold-labeled particles were abundantly observed in both the neuronal membrane and plasma pretreated by A-MG-EXO instead of MG-EXO.

To confirm the microglial origin of the elevated LAG3 expression on neuronal membranes after A-MG-EXO intervention, we further constructed stable transfectants of LAG3 overexpressing mouse microglial tumor cells. Our aim was to track the translocation of FLAG-labeled LAG3 from microglia to neurons via exosomes, ultimately confirming the microglial origin of the increased LAG3 on cholinergic neuronal membranes. We first performed Western blot analysis using a specific anti-FLAG antibody to validate the expression of FLAG-labeled LAG3 in microglia and their exosomes in both control (NC) and LAG3 overexpression group (OE). The results showed that FLAG-labeled LAG3 was absent in BV2 cells of the NC group, while the OE group exhibited robust expression of FLAG-labeled LAG3 (Fig. 6c). In exosomes, despite the overexpression of FLAG-labeled LAG3 in non-activated microglial cells, the expression level of LAG3 in their exosomes remains limited, only the exosomes secreted by activated LAG3-overexpressing BV2 cells (OE-A-EXO) carried a significant amount of FLAG-labeled LAG3 (Fig. 6d).

Subsequently, we established a time gradient of OE-A-EXO intervention on cholinergic neurons. Neurons were fixed and immunolabeled with anti-FLAG antibodies after exposure to exosomes for 1, 3, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h to detect the expression of LAG3-FLAG derived from microglia. Immunofluorescence results showed that after 6 h of OE-A-EXO intervention, FLAG-labeled LAG3 translocated from the cytoplasm back to the cell membrane. Subsequently, the signal of LAG3-FLAG remained stable on the neuronal membrane after 24 h (Fig. 6e). To assess the time-dependent uptake of PFF, we measured PFF-biotin levels at multiple time points using both Western blot analysis and immunofluorescence staining (Extended Data Fig. 8a–b). The results demonstrated a time-dependent increase in PFF-biotin uptake within 24 h following treatment with microglial exosomes. To determine whether LAG3 translocated to cholinergic neurons retains its biological activity in mediating α-Syn internalization, we performed co-immunoprecipitation (CO-IP) experiments to assess the interaction between LAG3-FLAG and α-Syn (Fig. 6f). The results showed that LAG3, after translocation, can interact with α-Syn, while treatment with neutralizing antibody against LAG3 significantly reduced the binding between a-Syn to LAG3. These results indicate that leveraging exosomes as an intermediary, activated microglia transfer the LAG3 receptor to cholinergic neurons via membrane translocation, thereby indirectly facilitating α-Syn transmission.

According to previous studies, membrane proteins undergo recycling processes to ensure the efficiency of cell life activities. Following internalization, membrane proteins are either retained in endosomes, transported to lysosomes for degradation, or recycled back to the cell membrane. Does this process also apply to exogenous proteins from exosomes, after exosomes are internalized by recipient cells and fused with their own membranes? To further investigate the internalization of microglia-derived exosomes and their fate of either degradation or recycling back to the cell membrane with LAG3 membrane translocation, we examined the subcellular co-localization of microglial exosomes and selected organelles associated with endosome system using confocal immunofluorescence microscopy. After 6 h of incubation with OE-A-EXO, LAG3-FLAG appears on the membrane. Therefore, we selected this time point for subsequent research. At 4 h, the membrane ingredient of exosomes from activated microglia (A-MG-EXO) is predominantly co-localized with markers of recycling endosomes RAB11, molecules that define the endocytic recycling compartment, suggesting the potential possibility of their membrane structures being recycled through the endocytic recycling pathway. On the contrary, exosomes derived from non-activated microglia (MG-EXO) showed greater co-localization with late endosome marker RAB7 and lysosome marker LAMP1, suggesting their exosomal membrane proteins are directed towards degradation pathways (Fig. 6g). With the prolongation of intervention time, we observed markedly different intracellular co-localization patterns of exosomes from activated/non-activated microglia within neurons (Fig. 6h-j). This indicates that membrane components from different origins might have divergent fates, and the stably integrated transmembrane protein LAG3 localized in and followed with the functional membrane microdomains towards a specific direction.

Differences in lipid composition influence the divergent fates of exosomes after uptake by recipient cells

The composition and abundance of lipid components exert a critical influence on membrane dynamics. To investigate the cause of the aforementioned divergent exosomal fates, we performed lipidomic analysis on exosomes derived from both activated microglia and non-activated microglia. Concurrently, we explored the comprehensive targeted lipidomics data of these two exosome types using multivariate statistical analysis. Orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) of the minor lipid components in these exosomes revealed a marked separation between the two sample groups in the OPLS-DA score scatter plot (Fig. 7a). Furthermore, an unsupervised clustering heatmap provided a clear visualization of the differences in lipid composition between the two exosome types (Fig. 7b). Based on the variable importance in the projection (VIP) values obtained from the OPLS-DA model for all lipid components, we identified 10 lipids significantly elevated in A-MG-EXO (VIP > 1, p < 0.05, and Log2FC > 0.25). Among these upregulated lipids, free cholesterol exhibited the highest VIP value (Fig. 7c-d). The differential lipid distribution is also displayed in a Z-score plot to better illustrate the differences in lipidomic profiles between the two exosome groups (Fig. 7e). Subsequently, enrichment analysis of the differential lipids using MetaboAnalyst 6.0 revealed that the lipids elevated in A-MG-EXO were primarily enriched in metabolic pathways such as steroid biosynthesis. This finding aligns with the observed increase in free cholesterol within A-MG-EXO (Fig. 7f). Existing research indicates that exosomal cholesterol content can determine the pathway of its internalization into recipient cells. To further investigate whether elevated free cholesterol content influences the fate of microglia-derived exosomes within recipient cells, we reduced free cholesterol levels in A-MG-EXO by depleting cholesterol from the exosomal membrane using methyl-β-cyclodextrin (MβCD). Following MβCD treatment, internalized A-MG-EXO exhibited a greater propensity to traffic to lysosomes rather than via the recycling endosome pathway in neurons (Fig. 7g-i). Therefore, abundant cholesterol content is an essential requirement for the internalization of A-MG-EXO and its mediation of LAG3 membrane translocation.

In summary, we propose a potential mechanism whereby microglia activation in the NBM region impacts the progression of synucleinopathy (Fig. 7j). A large number of cho-riched exosomes containing abundant LAG3 released from activated microglia are ingested by cholinergic neurons. During this process, microglia-originated LAG3 translocates to the neuronal membrane through the endosome-recycling system, and remains biologically active, facilitating the uptake of α-Syn into cholinergic neurons. These interesting findings suggest that LAG3 may bind to extracellular α-Syn fibrils, participate in the initiation of α-Syn endocytosis and delivery, and participate in α-Syn-induced cholinergic neuron loss and neurotoxicity in LBD. Consequently, LAG3 originating from activated microglia may serve a pivotal role in α-Syn transmission and neurodegenerative diseases.

Discussion

Exosomes are known to encapsulate a wide range of biomolecules, including proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids, which they deliver to recipient cells to modulate cellular functions. In recent years, extracellular vesicles have been proposed as crucial mediators for intercellular α-Syn transmission. Studies have confirmed the presence of abundant α- Syn in exosomes derived from plasma or cerebrospinal fluid of PD patients, and microglial activation is implicated in the transmission of α-Syn via the exosome pathway [44]. Our previous study revealed that microglia phagocytosed pathological α- Syn and were activated by this stimulation, then secrete a large number of exosomes containing α-Syn, thus mediating the propagation of α-Syn to distant brain regions [20]. The aforementioned studies collectively concur that exosomes participate in propagation serving as natural carriers for cargo α-Syn. However, the hypothesis that “exosomes derived from microglia act as spreading seeds” has certain limitations. Firstly, it only partially elucidates the genesis of LB pathology. Secondly, it overlooks whether exosomes generated by microglia in diverse activation states yield differing effects on recipient neurons. Lastly, this theory fails to address whether microglial exosomes influence the neuronal uptake of α-Syn from the extracellular spaces. In this study, we synthesized α-Syn-biotin to distinguish extracellular fibrils from endogenous aggregates and demonstrate uptake of α-Syn fibrils. On this basis, we identified that exosomes derived from LPS and IFN-γ activated microglia (to rule out the possibility of direct exosomal transmission of α-Syn), as a key component of microglia culture supernatant, also promoted the internalization of exogenous α-Syn fibrils by cholinergic neurons. These results provide further evidence that microglial activation and resultant exosomes facilitate the seeding-propagation of extracellular α-Syn aggregates to secondary neurons with diverse mechanisms.

According to prevailing perspectives, extracellular α-Syn released from affected neurons is considered the primary cellular source of pathological α-Syn generation and spread. Several research report that specific cell surface receptors bind and then mediate uptake of α-Syn fibrils, with LAG3 demonstrating notably high affinity and specificity in this process [31, 43, 45]. It is widely accepted that LAG3 is highly expressed in microglia. Our investigation also revealed abundant LAG3 expression on primary mouse microglia, with minimal expression observed in primary cholinergic neurons, consistent with prior findings. Interestingly, only activated microglia-derived exosomes expressed LAG3 in large quantities. This is not seen in the exosomes of non-activated microglia. This suggests the context-dependent role of exosomal cargoes, where the state of microglia dictates whether exosomes act as “guardians of homeostasis” or “agents of disruption”. Furthermore, we explored the role of exosomes as facilitators of LAG3 translocation from microglia to neuronal membranes, and confirmed this phenomenon. As a consequence of LAG3 translocation, we observed an enhanced ability of neurons to uptake extracellular α-Syn fibrils. Cholinergic neurons lack endogenous LAG3 expression; however, in pathological conditions, the functional significance of LAG3 in neurons may be attributed to its translocation from microglia to neurons, facilitated by exosomes derived from activated microglia.

The existence of α-Syn pathology is often accompanied by neuroinflammation characterized by microglia activation, as α-Syn is also a potent activator of microglia. While the susceptibility of the NBM to α-Syn and neurodegenerative changes is recognized, evidence regarding the involvement of microglial activity in the NBM region in the early onset and progression of LBD is lacking. Microglia exhibit heterogeneity and functional diversity across brain regions and pathological conditions [46]. TSPO PET is utilized to measure and track the activity of microglia in the brain, since TSPO is considered as a biomarker specifically expressed in activated microglia in vivo. This study utilized a highly specific second-generation TSPO tracer, [18F]DPA-714, enabling precise assessment of microglial activity in vivo. PET supports the regional activation of microglia consistent with the brain region-related pathological invasion of synucleinopathy, providing initial insight into microglial activity in NBM. Further, using NBM PFF injection mice model to mimic the pathological microenvironment with a-Syn burden, we observed microglia activation and pathological deposition of α-Syn in the NBM region. Meanwhile, microglia depletion significantly improved α-Syn deposition and cognitive function, highlighting the significance of microglia in the pathogenesis of NBM. While it’s widely believed that neuroinflammation-associated neurodegeneration is mainly mediated by activated microglia and their secreted cytokines [47]. Our further exploration of the cellular biological processes mediated by activated microglia in vitro revealed that only the presence of exosomes, rather than the exosome-free conditioned medium containing high levels of cytokines, notably promoted intraneuronal α-Syn aggregation when applied to neurons. Subsequently, for in vivo experiments, we performed whole-brain microglial depletion to eliminate the influence of cytokines before NBM PFF injection. Subsequent administration of activated microglia-derived exosomes accelerated α-Syn deposition and cognitive dysfunction in mice. This underscores the overlooked yet significant role of activated microglia in α-Syn aggregation, potentially mediated by exosomes from activated microglia, rather than cytokine-related immunological synergistic effects.

The impact of disease-associated microglia on the neuronal microenvironment is profound. Their secreted exosomes deliver protective or destructive payloads to remodel the neuronal microenvironment. Exosomes are not merely passive carriers but active participants in intercellular communication, capable of transferring functional membrane proteins between cells. Typically, exosomes are internalized by recipient cells via endocytosis and subsequently sequestered within endocytic early compartments. Tubular membrane protrusions extending towards the cytosol form from the vacuolar membrane of early endosomes. Following fission, these tubules give rise to tubular-vesicular transport carriers, known as recycling endosomes. Cargo is thereby transported back to the cell surface via membrane flux, diverting it away from the endosomal vacuole and the associated fate of lysosomal degradation. The repeated formation of tubules implies extensive membrane fusion and membrane flux. Given the impact of lipids on membrane properties, it is reasonable to infer that when alterations occur in the membrane composition of exosomes derived from activated microglia, particularly in their lipid profile, the membrane flux fate of these exosomes undergoes a corresponding shift. Evidence suggests that high cholesterol content in exosomal membranes is essential for facilitating fusion with recipient cell membranes [48]. Furthermore, phosphatidylserine (PS) and phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) are implicated in promoting lysosomal escape, potentially by destabilizing endolysosomal membranes or altering their physical properties [49]. In the context of neurodegenerative diseases, microglial lipid metabolism is often dysregulated, leading to alterations in the lipid composition of secreted exosomes. These metabolic changes may significantly impact the post-uptake fate of exosomes in recipient cells, influencing their functional outcomes. Our observations reveal distinct fates for exosomes derived from activated versus resting microglia. Specifically, exosomes originating from activated microglia demonstrate a notable ability to escape lysosomal degradation and exhibit a higher propensity for membrane fusion with recipient cells. This membrane fusion is critical for receptor transfer. Focusing on LAG3 as the subject of research on membrane transport mechanisms, we found that microglial exosomes are prone to enter the recycling endosome pathway and subsequently fuse with the cell membrane, where they remain biologically active and contribute to neuronal physiology. While our study focused solely on LAG3 translocation via microglia-derived exosomes, it is noteworthy that this phenomenon underscores the multifaceted nature of exosomes, which not only act as cargoes but also harbor a diverse array of receptors and transporters. In further study, deciphering the lipid signatures of microglial exosomes may not only elucidate neurodegenerative mechanisms but also pave the way for precision therapies that recalibrate microglial output toward neuroprotection.

This finding indicates that exosomes have the potential to facilitate receptor translocation between different neural cell types. Further investigation into this phenomenon is imperative and warrants continued attention. Several challenges remain, First, the mechanisms underlying the selective sorting of receptors into exosomes are not fully understood and warrant further investigation. Second, the functional longevity of transferred receptors is unclear. We observed efficient LAG3 transfer remained active for hours post-uptake, but neuronal membrane turnover rates (e.g., synaptic vesicle recycling) might limit receptor persistence. In addition, the functional consequences of receptor transfer in vivo, particularly in the context of neurodegenerative diseases, need to be elucidated.

Injection of pathological seeds generated from recombinant α-Syn into specific intracerebral regions has been demonstrated to induce synucleinopathy in animal models, mimicking the spreading patterns of pathological seeds and clinical motor functions symptoms. However, models reported by previous research were rarely verified for whether and how the cognitive function was impaired, neglecting systematic and comprehensive exploration of pathologic regions relevant to cognition. LBD has similar pathological features to Lewy bodies as the main component appears in the cortex and subcortex, accompanied by varying degrees of dysfunction in the cholinergic neurotransmitter system [50]. Cholinergic neurons in NBM are also susceptible to α-Syn, as evidenced by LBs formation and neuronal loss observed in postmortem examinations of LBD patients. Clinical evidence corroborates the close association between the death or dysfunction of cholinergic neurons in the NBM region and the onset and severity of LBD [51]. In this study, we selected NBM as the intracerebral inoculation target of α-Syn for the first time, and established a novel rodent model of synucleinopathy with cognitive impairment. The NBM injection model not only addresses the limitations of current methods and animal models but also provides a valid disease model for LBD, laying a foundation for further research.

In this study, we provide a paradigm-shifting perspective on how exosomal membrane proteins drive pathological protein propagation. Specifically, we demonstrate that microglial activation reprograms exosomal cargo to include pathogenic receptors like LAG3, which act as molecular “keys” to unlock neuronal vulnerability to α-Syn. This discovery extends beyond LBD, offering insights into other synucleinopathies such as Parkinson’s disease and multiple system atrophy, where exosome-mediated receptor transfer may similarly underpin selective neurodegeneration. In conclusion, our study presents a novel perspective that the membranes of exosomes contain specific transporters capable of altering the membrane protein architecture of recipient cells through translocation. This functional aspect of exosomes deserves careful consideration in future discussions.
