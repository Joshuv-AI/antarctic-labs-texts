Red-Bandit: Test-Time Adaptation for LLM Red-Teaming via Bandit-Guided LoRA Experts
Christos Ziakas, Nicholas Loo, Nishita Jain, Alessandra Russo

Abstract
Automated red-teaming has emerged as a scalable approach for auditing Large Language Models (LLMs) prior to deployment, yet existing approaches lack mechanisms to efficiently adapt to model-specific vulnerabilities at inference. We introduce Red-Bandit, a red-teaming framework that adapts online to identify and exploit model failure modes under distinct attack styles (e.g., manipulation, slang). Red-Bandit post-trains a set of parameter-efficient LoRA experts, each specialized for a particular attack style, using reinforcement learning that rewards the generation of unsafe prompts via a rule-based safety model. At inference, a multi-armed bandit policy dynamically selects among these attack-style experts based on the target model’s response safety, balancing exploration and exploitation. Red-Bandit achieves state-of-the-art results on AdvBench under sufficient exploration (ASR@10), while producing more human-readable prompts (lower perplexity). Moreover, Red-Bandit’s bandit policy serves as a diagnostic tool for uncovering model-specific vulnerabilities by indicating which attack styles most effectively elicit unsafe behaviors.

Introduction

Despite significant advances in alignment to human values
[
1
,
2
]
, Large Language Models (LLMs) remain vulnerable to adversarial prompts that bypass safety measures
[
3
,
4
]
.
Wei et al. 2023
attributes this vulnerability to competing objectives within LLMs and the generalization gap between safety and capabilities, indicating fundamental limitations of current architectures and training approaches. As LLMs are increasingly integrated with external tools and real-world applications
[
6
,
7
]
, the breadth and severity of safety risks are expected to grow—from individual harms to large-scale societal impacts
[
8
]
—highlighting the need for reliable auditing and red-teaming methods prior to deployment
[
9
]
.

Existing LLM evaluations often rely on human-annotated datasets to detect failure cases, such as toxicity
[
10
]
, hallucinations
[
11
]
, and adversarial prompts
[
12
]
, but these datasets are limited in size and subject to selection biases. Human red-teaming, though effective, remains resource-intensive and limited in scalability
[
13
]
. Therefore, automated red-teaming
[
14
,
15
]
has emerged as a promising strategy, aiming to uncover unsafe outputs before deployment without requiring human oversight
[
16
]
.

Adversarial prompt-optimization methods exploit privileged access, using gradients (white-box) or log-probabilities (gray-box), to craft jailbreaks
[
3
]
, with recent work improving readability and computational efficiency
[
17
,
18
]
. However, while prompts from these methods transfer effectively to proprietary LLMs (black-box)
[
3
,
17
]
, they lack the ability to identify and exploit the target model’s vulnerabilities at test time. Iterative search and mutation methods refine candidate prompts online based on the target model’s responses, providing test-time adaptability but at the cost of being highly query-intensive
[
19
,
20
,
21
]
. RL-based approaches post-train LLMs to efficiently generate human-readable adversarial prompts, but their diversity remains limited (mode collapse) due to the challenge of balancing multiple objectives
[
22
]
and diversity
[
23
]
in reward design.

In this work, we introduce Red-Bandit, a black-box red-teaming method that employs a multi-armed bandit to identify and exploit the most effective attack styles (e.g., role-playing, historical scenarios) for a given target model. Red-Bandit (i) trains a diverse set of LoRA attack-style experts using GRPO and reward on prompt-level safety, leveraging in-context conditioning to generate style-specific adversarial instructions, and (ii) dynamically selects among these attack-style experts at inference via a bandit policy guided by response-level safety. Red-Bandit leverages the efficiency of RL-trained LLM attackers while balancing exploration (stylistic diversity) and exploitation (attack success) at test time. Red-Bandit outperforms state-of-the-art approaches on the AdvBench dataset, achieving higher attack success rates and lower perplexity (human-readable prompts) under a sufficient exploration horizon. In addition, Red-Bandit provides a useful diagnostic tool for understanding model-specific vulnerabilities by revealing which attack styles each model is most susceptible to.

We summarize our key contributions as follows:

•

We introduce Red-Bandit, a black-box red-teaming framework that employs a multi-armed bandit algorithm to identify effective attack styles at test time, dynamically balancing exploration (stylistic diversity) and exploitation (attack success).

•

We propose an RL post-training pipeline that trains LoRA experts for each attack style using GRPO with prompt-level content safety rewards, leveraging in-context conditioning to generate human-readable and style-aligned adversarial prompts.

•

We demonstrate that Red-Bandit outperforms state-of-the-art methods on AdvBench across both open-source and proprietary LLMs under a sufficient exploration horizon, while also serving as a diagnostic tool for identifying model-specific vulnerabilities across attack styles.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
RL Post-Training with GRPO

Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
[
24
]
is widely used for RL post-training of LLMs due to its stability at scale
[
1
]
. While effective, PPO is resource-intensive, requiring both a reference model for KL computation and training of a value model for advantage estimation, which significantly increases training overhead. Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
[
25
]
reduces this cost by eliminating the value model and estimating advantages via group-wise reward normalization. In our work, we adopt a variation of GRPO with LoRA
[
26
]
for parameter-efficient fine-tuning of attack-style experts.

2.2
Multi-Armed Bandits

The multi-armed bandit problem formalizes the exploration–exploitation trade-off in sequential decision-making under uncertainty
[
27
]
. At each step, an agent selects an action (arm) with an unknown reward distribution and receives stochastic feedback, aiming to maximize cumulative reward over time while balancing exploration of uncertain arms and exploitation of high-reward ones. The
\varepsilon
-greedy algorithm implements this balance by selecting the empirically best arm with probability
1-\varepsilon
and a random arm with probability
\varepsilon

[
28
]
. The Upper Confidence Bound (UCB) algorithm
[
27
]
instead applies optimism under uncertainty, selecting the arm with the highest upper confidence estimate of its expected reward. UCB favors exploration of less-explored arms, which receive wider confidence intervals, while exploiting well-sampled arms that yield higher empirical returns.
In our setting, Red-BANDIT instantiates the multi-arm bandit problem by treating each attack style (e.g., role play, slang, hypothetical) as an arm. The reward for choosing an arm is determined by the content safety of the target LLM’s response to a prompt generated in that style, as measured by a rule-based reward model.

3
Methodology

3.1
Automated Red-teaming

Given a target LLM
\mathcal{T}
and a prompt space
\mathcal{P}
, automated
red-teaming is formulated as a search for prompts that elicit harmful behavior.
Formally, we seek the prompt
p^{*}\in\mathcal{P}
that maximizes:

p^{*}=\arg\max_{p\in\mathcal{P}}r\!\left(\mathcal{T}(p)\right)

where
r(\mathcal{T}(p))
is a scalar reward measuring the harmfulness of the
model’s response to
p
(e.g., computed via a rule-based verifier, a learned
classifier, or human judgment).

3.2
Post-Training of LoRA Experts

We post-train LoRA-based attacker policies with reinforcement learning, using a
rule-based prompt-safety reward and style-conditioned prompts.

3.2.1
RL Algorithm

We post-train attacker policies using a reinforcement learning algorithm based on a variant of GRPO
[
29
]
(see Section
2.1
), which removes response-length and question-difficulty biases while retaining a group baseline for stable optimization. Following
Rastogi et al. 2025
, we omit the
KL penalty for efficiency. For samples
(x,y)\sim\pi_{\theta}
, the clipped
policy-gradient loss is

\small\mathcal{L}_{\theta}=-\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}\!\left[\min\!\left(r_{\theta}\hat{A},\,\text{clip}(r_{\theta},\,1-\epsilon,\,1+\epsilon)\,\hat{A}\right)\right]

(1)

where
r_{\theta}=\tfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y|x)}
and

\epsilon
is the clipping threshold. The advantage estimate
\hat{A}
is

\small\hat{A}=R(x,y)-\tfrac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^{G}R(x,y_{j})

(2)

with
R(x,y)
the rule-based reward and
G
the number of responses per prompt.

3.2.2
Reward Function Design

Our method relies on a rule-based reward function to identify unsafe content. In our formulation, the reward function is a binary
R(x,y)
that scores prompts directly based on predefined rules, where a reward of 1 indicates the prompt x is unsafe. This approach allows training to proceed in a fully black-box setting without querying the target model, which substantially reduces computational cost and promotes attacker policies that generalize across downstream LLMs. For comparison, we also study alternative setups where the reward signal is derived from the target model’s response and from a combination of both prompt and response signals in a large-scale RL setup described in Section
4.3.2
.

Training pipeline of Red-Bandit. Multiple LoRA attack-style experts are trained with GRPO using rule-based rewards on prompt safety.

3.2.3
Attack-Style LoRA Experts

We implement each attacker policy using LoRA
[
26
]
, a parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) method that injects trainable low-rank adapters into frozen weight layers. Concretely, we train a separate LoRA expert for each of the 10 attack style categories defined in Rainbow Teaming
[
21
]
:
Slang, Technical Terms, Role Play, Authority Manipulation, Misspellings, Word Play, Emotional Manipulation, Hypotheticals, Historical Scenario,
and
Uncommon Dialects
. Each expert is fine-tuned on prompts that explicitly include the attack style as an input token, leveraging in-context learning to generate style-specific adversarial instructions. Training follows the rule-based safety reward described previously, producing lightweight, style-conditioned attacker policies that can be adaptively selected at inference time. Appendix
A
provides examples of generated prompts given a behavior for each attack style.

3.3
Bandit-Guided Inference

At inference, Red-Bandit treats each LoRA expert as an arm in a multi-armed bandit problem. Let
\mathcal{A}=\{1,\dots,K\}
denote the set of
K
arms,
corresponding to different attack styles. At round
t
, the agent selects an
arm
A_{t}\in\mathcal{A}
and observes a reward
X_{A_{t},t}
from the rule-based
model that scores the safety of the target LLM’s response. The
performance of a bandit policy is measured by its expected cumulative regret:

\small R(T)=T\mu^{*}-\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}X_{A_{t},t}\right]

(3)

where
\mu^{*}=\max_{i\in\mathcal{A}}\mu_{i}
is the mean reward of the optimal arm. Our framework is agnostic to the choice of bandit algorithm. We evaluate the policy with UCB, chosen for its strong theoretical guarantees and robustness in balancing exploration and exploitation
[
27
]
, and with
\varepsilon
-greedy, which adopts a more exploitative strategy as described in Section
2.2
. Red-Bandit can be viewed as a post-hoc hard routing instantiation of a mixture-of-experts architecture
[
31
,
32
,
33
]
, where a test-time bandit policy performs discrete expert selection among pre-trained LoRA modules based on response feedback from the target model, instead of learning a router jointly during training.

4.1
Experiment Setup

4.1.1
Implementation Details

We use Mistral-7B
[
34
]
as the base model for prompt generation, selected for its minimal alignment fine-tuning and open-weight availability. Training follows the prompt template described in Appendix
D
. The reward model is instantiated with Llama Guard
[
35
]
, an instruction-tuned safeguard LLM that classifies prompts and responses into predefined safety risk levels and outputs binary safety decisions. We use Llama Guard-8B for RL training and Llama Guard-1B for bandit algorithms at inference time. Each attack-style expert is trained for one epoch using a GRPO variant, following the reinforcement learning pipeline described in Section
3.2
, with eight generations per step and LoRA adaptation. Full training, inference, and LoRA configurations, along with all hyperparameters, are detailed in Appendix
C
.

4.1.2
Evaluation Setup

Following prior work such as AdvPrompter
[
18
]
and Atoxia
[
17
]
, we use the AdvBench dataset
[
3
]
, which consists of 520 harmful instructions. We adopt the same 80/20 train–test split as in
[
17
]
to ensure fair comparability across methods. Following prior work
[
18
,
17
]
, we report the attack success rate (ASR), defined as the fraction of adversarial prompts that elicit harmful responses from the target LLM. We measure ASR under two sampling settings:
ASR@1, where only a single adversarial prompt is sampled per harmful instruction, and ASR@10, where ten adversarial prompts are sampled and a trial is counted as successful if at least one of them succeeds. Success is determined using keyword matching against a predefined list of refusal words, adopted by Atoxia and AdvBench, ensuring consistency and comparability with prior baselines. GPT-4o evaluations
[
17
]
frequently misclassified successful attacks as non-toxic when the responses followed non-toxic stylistic patterns; therefore, we exclude these results, as detailed in Appendix
E
. In addition, we evaluate prompt fluency using perplexity computed under our trained model. We report the average perplexity across generations, where lower values indicate more fluent and human-like text.

4.3.2
Reward Design in RL Post-Training

We evaluate alternative reward formulations to assess their impact on large-scale RL post-training for LLM prompt generators in attack style, following the DeepSeek-R1 training recipe
[
41
]
. In addition to our proposed prompt-based reward, we consider response-based and hybrid reward variants. Prompt-only reward yields more efficient and diverse training, outperforming the other designs in terms of training time and VRAM utilization. It also achieves higher ASR@20 on the HarmBench benchmark
[
12
]
against Llama-3.1-8B and Qwen3-8B
[
42
]
, while performing on par with hybrid training. We provide the full analysis in Appendix

.

5
Related Work

5.1
Prompt Optimization Methods

Adversarial prompt-optimization methods leverage privileged model access to craft effective jailbreaks, using gradients (white-box) or log-probabilities (gray-box). GCG (Greedy Coordinate Gradient)
[
3
]
optimizes adversarial suffixes directly via gradient information from the target model, achieving high attack success rates but producing low-readable prompts.
Jones et al. 2023
similarly explores automated prompt search for eliciting toxic outputs, but their approach is limited by the computational cost of discrete optimization. To address these issues, recent gray-box methods focus on improving human readability and efficiency. AdvPrompter
[
18
]
trains an auxiliary LLM through alternating optimization to generate coherent, human-readable suffixes, while Atoxia
[
17
]
employs reinforcement learning to synthesize adversarial queries and openings conditioned on a target harmful response. Although both methods achieve strong transferability to black-box models, their attack policies are trained offline, preventing them from adapting to a target model’s vulnerabilities at test time.

5.2
Iterative and Search-Based Methods

Black-box iterative methods refine adversarial prompts at test time by conditioning on the target model’s responses. PAIR
[
19
]
performs feedback-driven refinement through attacker–target interaction, while TAP
[
44
]
employs tree search with pruning to discard suboptimal candidates. GAP
[
45
]
generalizes this paradigm using graph-structured search to maintain global context across attack trajectories. AutoDAN
[
20
]
and GPTFuzzer
[
46
]
apply genetic and fuzzing-inspired mutations to evolve semantically coherent adversarial prompts, whereas CipherChat
[
47
]
leverages ciphered or non-natural text to bypass safety filters. Rainbow Teaming
[
21
]
frames adversarial prompt generation as a quality–diversity optimization problem, performing open-ended search using MAP-Elites to populate an archive of prompts spanning diverse attack styles and risk categories. While effective, these methods are computationally expensive at inference, requiring many queries to generate and evaluate candidate prompts for a single successful attack. In contrast, Red-Bandit enables efficient test-time adaptation through online selection among pre-trained attack-style experts as the bandit’s exploration–exploitation trade-off stabilizes.

5.3
RL-Based Methods

RL-based approaches post-train LLMs to generate human-readable adversarial prompts but are prone to mode collapse, limiting prompt diversity
[
15
]
. To mitigate this, recent work introduces curiosity-driven exploration
[
48
]
, GFlowNet fine-tuning with MLE smoothing
[
49
]
, and multi-step RL with auto-generated rule-based rewards
[
23
]
. While these methods improve diversity, they remain constrained by the inherent difficulty of modeling multiple objectives and stylistic variation in reward design
[
22
]
. Moreover, prior RL-based approaches do not adapt their attack strategy to specific target models at test time. Red-Bandit overcomes this limitation by introducing diversity through multiple style-specific LoRA experts and enabling model-specific adaptation via a test-time bandit policy guided by response feedback. SoC
[
50
]
proposed a multi-armed bandit framework for generating jailbreak attacks, where the bandit learns to select context-switching query categories that progressively weaken model safeguards. In contrast, Red-Bandit performs test-time adaptation at the attack-style level, selecting among pre-trained LoRA experts trained via reinforcement learning. Beyond adaptation, we interpret the learned bandit policy as a diagnostic tool that identifies model-specific vulnerabilities to particular attack styles.

6
Discussion, Limitations, and Future Work

6.1
Discussion

We demonstrate that exploiting model vulnerabilities through targeted attack styles is highly effective, outperforming existing methods in both attack success rate given sufficient exploration and human-readability. Efficient RL post-training with LoRA further improves attack effectiveness over in-context conditioning by enabling style-aligned prompt generation. Beyond enhancing attack performance, the multi-armed bandit also improves interpretability: by analyzing the distribution of selected attack styles, Red-Bandit exposes model-specific weaknesses and behavioral biases, offering insights into how different LLMs respond to distinct adversarial framings. Beyond enhancing attack performance, Red-Bandit can be used as a diagnostic tool: by analyzing the distribution of selected attack styles, it reveals model-specific weaknesses and behavioral biases, offering insights into how different LLMs respond to distinct attack style patterns.

6.2
Limitations

Our approach introduces additional computational overhead during inference compared to transferable prompt-based methods, as it requires access to a rule-based reward model (e.g., Llama Guard 1B). While post-training remains efficient due to parameter-efficient fine-tuning, overall training complexity still scales with the number of attack styles, since each style-specific expert must be post-trained separately. Finally, extending the framework to custom datasets or behavioral domains may be challenging when LLM-based prompt generators cannot reliably condition on attack-style instructions.

6.3
Future Work

Red-Bandit’s bandit-based test-time adaptation framework is method-agnostic and readily extends beyond LoRA-based experts to other LLM red-teaming approaches, including even human red-teamers. The framework can also be used to discover and exploit model vulnerabilities tied to specific attack primitives, not merely attack styles. A further direction is to compose multiple attack styles within a single attempt or to leverage partial gray-box access to improve adaptability and performance.

6.4
Potential Risks and Societal Impact

While the Red-Bandit framework is designed to advance LLM safety, we acknowledge its dual-use nature. The same techniques used to identify safety vulnerabilities could, in principle, be misused by malicious actors to generate harmful content or develop more effective jailbreaks. The adversarial prompts produced by our method are intended solely for developers and researchers to audit and strengthen model safeguards.

Impact of Reward Design in Large-Scale RL Post-Training

Following the large-scale RL training recipe of DeepSeek-R1
[
41
]
, we initialize the LLM prompt generator from a base model via supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on red-teaming exemplars that pair harmful behaviors with concrete attack prompts. Specifically, we use 80,000 prompts from the extended open-source HarmBench corpus
[
51
]
to reduce refusal rates and teach the mapping from behavior descriptions to adversarial prompt templates, substantially improving stability for subsequent RL fine-tuning. Following SFT, we post-train the LLM attacker with a variant of GRPO
[
29
]
on AdvBench-v4
[
52
]
, extending AdvBench to 4,948 distinct harmful behaviors. Rewards are provided by our rule-based reward model (
Llama Guard
-8B) and applied in three configurations: prompt-only (rewarding the attacker’s prompts), response-only (rewarding the target model’s responses), and hybrid (an equal-weight combination of both). This setup enables controlled ablations over reward design.

Evaluation follows the HarmBench benchmark
[
12
]
on 200 diverse harmful behaviors. For each behavior, the attacker generates up to 20 independent adversarial prompts, each submitted separately to the target model without conversational carry-over. A behavior is considered successfully attacked if any attempt elicits a harmful response, where harmfulness is determined by the HarmBench pretrained classifier applied to the target model’s outputs. This yields the Attack Success Rate (ASR@20). Our SFT and RL corpora partially overlap with the HarmBench evaluation set; while this may inflate absolute ASR values, the overlap is consistent across all training configurations, ensuring that relative comparisons between reward functions remain fair. To assess diversity, we compute Self-BLEU
[
53
]
across 20 generations per behavior, where lower scores indicate more diverse attack strategies. We also record VRAM usage and wall-clock training time to compare the efficiency of different reward configurations.

shows that prompt-based rewards provide the most effective and scalable training signal. They achieve the highest attack success on Qwen-8B (82.0%), the best diversity (lowest Self-BLEU), and substantially lower computational cost compared to response-based or hybrid setups. While hybrid rewards yield a minor gain on Llama-3.1-8B, they require more resources and reduce diversity, making prompt-based feedback the preferable choice for large-scale adversarial training.

Limitations of GPT-4o Evaluations

Automated evaluations with GPT4o used in Atoxia
[
17
]
can occasionally produce misclassifications, particularly when responses include informative content that implicitly fulfills unsafe intents. Example

In our setting, GPT-4o evaluators often misclassify successful attacks as non-toxic when the responses adopt an attack style that appears non-toxic in form. This failure mode has two implications. First, relying on LLM-based evaluations can produce false negatives (unsafe responses classified as safe), which undermines the validity of large-scale safety evaluations. Second, target LLMs may produce content that is informative enough to satisfy a malicious intent while avoiding explicit phrasing that triggers rule-based or instruction-following detectors.
