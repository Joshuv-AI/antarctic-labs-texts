A tri-specific EGFR/cMet/VEGF antibody demonstrates potent multi-mechanistic activity in preclinical triple-negative breast cancer models
Authors: Pu Pu, Ying Jin, Songling Zhang, Yuqiang Xu, Peng Chen, Fulai Zhou, Ping Sun, Hao Jiang, Sheng Huang, Limin Chen, Meixia Fu, ZhengXia Zha, Maria P. MacWilliams, Mark L. Chiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Triple negative breast cancer (TNBC) is a cancer with significant unmet medical needs and comprises 10 to 15% of all breast cancers and 15 to 20% of advanced breast cancers due to the limited therapeutic options. Many TNBC tumors are driven by aberrant activities of epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), mesenchymal epithelial transition factor (cMet), and vascular endothelial growth factor. We demonstrated how TAVO412, a tri-specific antibody that recognized cMet, dual epitopes of EGFR, and vascular endothelial growth factor could elicit antitumor activity in TNBC. TAVO412 showed inhibition of EGFR- and cMet-mediated tumor proliferation, enhanced Fc-mediated effector functions, and suppression of angiogenesis. The avidity of the dual-epitope based anti-EGFR and anti-cMet design had better signaling inhibition and cytotoxicity against EGFR-low expressing tumor cell lines than the JNJ-61186372 analog, a marketed EGFR/cMet bispecific antibody. Furthermore, TAVO412 exhibited anti-tumor activities in multiple TNBC cell line-derived xenograft models. Overall, TAVO412 demonstrated great preclinical utility against TNBC.

Triple negative breast cancers (TNBCs) are aggressive subtypes of breast cancers lacking the estrogen and progesterone receptors and display low-levels of human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (1, 2, 3). TNBCs account for 10 to 15% of all breast cancers and 15 to 20% of advanced breast cancers, and is associated with rapid progression, early relapse, and limited therapeutic options. Current first-line therapies rely mainly on combination chemotherapy or immune checkpoint blockade, yet TNBC often progresses more rapidly than other subtypes, leading to poor outcomes and limited long-term control (1, 4, 5).

A major challenge in TNBC is the high degree of molecular heterogeneity and the rapid emergence of drug-resistant subclones. Single-target therapies—including tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) and monospecific antibodies-often produce only transient responses before resistance develops through target loss, mutation, or compensatory pathway activation. In contrast, multi-targeting agents can suppress parallel oncogenic pathways and reduce the likelihood of simultaneous resistance-conferring mutations, offering a more durable therapeutic approach (6).

Many TNBC patients have tumors with increased levels of EGFR, and cMet (7, 8, 9, 10). Concomitantly, the activation of the EGFR signaling pathway enhances vascular endothelial growth factor type A (VEGF-A) secretion that then promotes angiogenesis and tumor growth, resistance to EGFR directed therapies such as cetuximab (anti (α)-EGFR mAb), and gefitinib (EGFR TKI), and shorter survival in TNBC patients (11, 12, 13, 14, 15). Similarly, cMet overexpression and hepatocyte growth factor (HGF)–mediated activation contribute to resistance to EGFR TKIs and are associated with inferior clinical outcomes (16, 17, 18, 19). High-level co-expression of EGFR and cMet in TNBC is associated with significantly worse disease-free survival than expression of EGFR alone. Combined inhibition of EGFR and cMet signaling—either pharmacologically or with bispecific antibodies—has demonstrated synergistic suppression of TNBC growth in preclinical models (16, 18). the bispecific EGFR/cMet antibody JNJ-61186372 has demonstrated strong efficacy in non-small cell lung cancer. However, such agents have not been extensively evaluated in TNBC, and they do not address the VEGF-driven angiogenic component of TNBC biology.

Angiogenesis represents another key driver of tumor progression. TNBC tumors are characterized by high micro vessel density, largely driven by excessive VEGF-A produced by tumor and stromal cells (20). Elevated VEGF levels are associated strongly with poor outcomes in advanced TNBC. (7, 21). While approved anti-VEGF/ vascular endothelial growth factor receptor (VEGFR) agents can mitigate angiogenesis, systemic toxicity often limits their use (22). Moreover, cMet- and VEGFR-mediated signaling synergistically sustains angiogenic and invasive phenotypes, and concurrent targeting of these pathways could enhance efficacy compared with single-agent blockade (23).

Given the extensive crosstalk between aberrant levels of EGFR, cMet, and VEGFR/VEGF-A pathways in TNBC, a therapeutic capable of simultaneously inhibiting all three could provide a more comprehensive blockade of TNBC growth and survival mechanisms. Results

TAVO412 showed stronger binding to TNBC cell lines

TAVO412 binding affinity to the respective monomeric recombinant human EGFR extracellular domain (ECD), cMet ECD, and VEGF (VEGF-165) domains were determined from biolayer interferometry measurements. TAVO412 bound to the rh EGFR ECD with a mean KD of 2.45 ± 0.39 nM, to rh cMet ECD with a mean KD of 13.36 ± 2.03 nM, and to VEGF-165 with a mean KD of 1.42 ± 0.29 nM (Table S1). By comparison, the JNJ-61186372 analog bound to rhEGFR with KD of 10.80 ± 0.06 nM and to cMet with KD of 1.29 ± 0.20 nM. The bivalent bevacizumab analog bound to VEGF with a KD of 0.58 ± 0.02 nM. Furthermore, biolayer interferometry confirmed that TAVO412 could bind to EGFR ECD, rh cMet ECD, and rh VEGF simultaneously, confirming that its binding arms function independently and without steric interference. (Fig. S1).

Avidity measurements using ELISA revealed sub-nanomolar effective concentration (EC50) values for EGFR (59.0 pM), cMet (234 pM), and VEGF-165 (84.7 pM), reflecting the enhanced multivalent interactions expected when antigens are clustered on surfaces. (Fig. S2, Table S2 listed the binding avidity values with 95% confidence intervals). These potencies were comparable to the JNJ-61186372 analog for EGFR (57.1 PM) but lower for cMet (41.8 PM). The bevacizumab analog exhibited stronger VEGF-165 avidity (29.2 PM), consistent with its bivalent α-VEGF design, whereas TAVO412 contained a monovalent α-VEGF domain.

Mutant variants of TAVO412 lacking individual binding arms (α-EGFR, α-cMet, or α-VEGF replaced with inert arms) retained unaltered affinities for the remaining domains, indicating that epitope binding for each arm acted independently (Figs. S3 and S4).

Flow-cytometric analysis confirmed high-avidity cell binding of TAVO412 to TNBC cell lines expressing varying EGFR and cMet receptor densities (Fig. 1C–E; Table S3). TAVO412 bound MDA-MB-231 with EC50 of 0.367 nM, HCC70 with EC50 of 0.832 nM, and BT-20 with EC50 of 0.114 nM. In contrast, the JNJ-61186372 analog exhibited weaker binding to the same cell lines (1.37, 2.54, and 0.636 nM, respectively). Notably, TAVO412 displayed 3–5-fold stronger avidity than the JNJ-61186372 analog across all three lines, despite comparable EGFR affinity and weaker cMet affinity. The overall binding efficiency correlated positively with receptor density (Table S4) but remained comparable to or better than the bispecific comparator across all models. This enhanced avidity correlated with receptor density and likely reflected the dual-epitope EGFR engagement mechanism.

TAVO412 inhibited EGFR, cMet, and VEGFR signaling. Ligand-blocking assays showed that TAVO412 inhibited EGF binding to EGFR (inhibitory concentration (IC50) = 1.53 nM), HGF binding to cMet (IC50 = 8.05 nM), and VEGF binding to VEGFR (IC50 = 14.81 nM) (Fig. S5). The negative control antibody (α-gp120 hIgG1) exhibited no blocking activity. Compared to TAVO412, the JNJ-61186372 analog inhibited EGF binding to EGFR less potently (IC50 = 14.4 nM), maintained moderate inhibition of HGF-cMet interactions, but showed no VEGF-blocking activity. The bevacizumab analog had stronger inhibition of VEGF binding to VEGFR (IC50 = 5.86 nM) because of stronger avidity of bevacizumab with bivalent VEGF binding arms as compared to the monovalent VEGF binding arm of TAVO412.

Cell-based phosphorylation (time-resolved fluorescence and resonance energy transfer) assays demonstrated that TAVO412 suppressed HGF and EGF ligand-induced receptor phosphorylation. In MDA-MB-468 cells with high EGFR density, TAVO412 inhibited EGFR Y1063 phosphorylation (IC50 = 8.5 nM) more potently than the JNJ-61186372 analog (IC50 = 25.2 nM) (Fig. 2A) (Table S4). In MDA-MB-231 cells, which expressed higher cMet levels, TAVO412 inhibited cMet Y1234/Y1235 phosphorylation (IC50 = 0.453 nM) less potently than the JNJ-61186372 analog (IC50 = 0.134 nM) (Fig. 2B). Using a VEGFR reporter assay, TAVO412 effectively blocked VEGF-VEGFR activation (IC50 = 4.75 nM) but with lower potency than the bevacizumab analog (IC50 = 0.35 nM) (Fig. 2C), attributable to the latter’s bivalent configuration. These data demonstrated that TAVO412 inhibited multiple signaling axes relevant to TNBC biology.

TAVO412 mediated strong antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity, ADCP, and CDC Fc effector functions

TAVO412 incorporated Fc-enhancing F243 L/R292P/Y300 L/V305I/P396 L (LPLIL) mutations to strengthen ADCC, ADCP, and CDC. (25). ELISA Binding assays confirmed TAVO412 increased affinity for Fc gamma receptor (FcγR) FcγRIIIa, FcγRIIa, and C1q relative to a wild-type IgG1 control, consistent with enhanced effector recruitment (Fig. S6, Table S5). JNJ-61186372 had low fucose levels to boost Fc effector function. Both TAVO412 (EC50 = 0.46 nM) and the JNJ-61186372 analog (EC50 = 0.51 nM) had stronger FcγRIIIa (CD16 A) binding affinity than that of the inert IgG1 control (EC50 = 3.7 nM). TAVO412 (EC50 = 0.16 nM), the JNJ-61186372 analog (EC50 = 0.08 nM), and the inert arm IgG1 control (EC50 = 0.12 nM) had comparable FcγRI (CD64) binding affinity. However, the TAVO412 FcγRIIa (CD32 A) (EC50 = 2.90 nM) was more potent than the JNJ-61186372 analog (EC50 = 10.3 nM), and the inert IgG1 Fc (EC50 = 14.4 nM). Thus, TAVO412 was expected to have a stronger ADCP effect. C1q engagement was similar in potency across all molecules (TAVO412 with EC50 = 14.2 nM), JNJ-61186372 analog with EC50 = 11.2 nM, and inert IgG1 control with EC50 = 14.1 nM). However, TAVO412 had stronger C1q binding efficacy (as shown in the higher range of y axis span) which portended stronger CDC activity.

In primary peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC)-based ADCC assays, TAVO412 induced stronger tumor cell lysis than the JNJ-61186372 analog across all TNBC lines tested: MDA-MB-231 (Fig. 3A), HCC70 (Fig. 3B), and BT-20 (Fig. 3C). The isotype control gp120 hIgG1 displayed minimal ADCC lysis. Macrophage-driven ADCP was likewise more efficacious (larger range in y axis showed higher Emax), particularly in MDA-MB-231 (Fig. 3D) and HCC70 (Fig. 3E). Although both antibodies had limited ADCP activity in BT-20 (low receptor density), TAVO412 still achieved higher maximal efficacy (Emax) (Fig. 3F). The isotype control gp120 hIgG1 displayed minimal ADCP killing. Although TAVO412 and the JNJ-61186372 analog had similar C1q binding affinity, TAVO412 triggered potent CDC activity in MDA-MB-231 cells, whereas the JNJ-61186372 analog elicited no CDC response in any line (Fig. 3G). The isotype control gp120 hIgG1 displayed minimal CDC lysis. TAVO412 demonstrated broad antitumor activity in TNBC xenograft models

TAVO412 produced substantial tumor growth inhibition (TGI) across multiple TNBC xenograft models representing a range of EGFR and cMet expression levels (Table S4).

In MDA-MB-231 tumors, 10 mg/kg TAVO412 achieved 62% TGI without body-weight loss, outperforming the 10 mg/kg JNJ-61186372 analog (40% TGI) (Fig. 4, A and B; Fig. S7A) and matching the efficacy of 4 mg/kg doxorubicin but without its toxicity (Fig. 4, A and B; Fig. S7A). Excised tumors from TAVO412-treated mice showed visibly reduced vascularization, consistent with VEGF pathway inhibition. Combining TAVO412 with doxorubicin provided an additive effect (71% TGI). In the HCC70 model (moderate EGFR/cMet expression), 10 mg/kg TAVO412 produced 26% TGI, comparable to JNJ-61186372 (21%) but enhanced when co-administered with doxorubicin (61% TGI) (Fig. 4, C and D; Fig. S7B). Doxorubicin alone gave 45% TGI but caused weight reduction. Likewise, following TAVO412 treatment, the representative HCC70 tumor samples were smaller and had less red coloring demonstrating lower levels of vascularization (Fig. 4D).

In the BT-20 tumor model, characterized by low EGFR and cMet expression, 10 mg/kg TAVO412 achieved a striking 74% TGI without body weight loss, whereas the JNJ-61186372 analog (13% TGI) and bevacizumab analog (39% TGI) showed less activity (Fig. 4, E and F, Table S4, Fig. S7C). This model highlighted the advantage of tri-specific targeting: even when Fc-effector functions may be attenuated by low receptor density, VEGF blockade and multi-pathway inhibition remained effective.

The effect of avidity of the TAVO412 binding domains on the in vivo %TGI was determined by replacing each domain with an inert arm while maintaining the same framework structure of TAVO412. The control molecules with the inert arms retained the binding of the unmodified domains as confirmed by ELISA (Fig. S4). Domain-inactivation variants revealed that all three binding arms and the enhanced Fc domain contributed to maximal efficacy. Removing the VEGF arm reduced TGI from 74% to 17%, removing the EGFR arm reduced TGI to 65%, removing the cMet arm reduced TGI to 66%, and introducing an Fc-silencing LALA mutation reduced TGI to 47%. (Fig. 4, I–L), these findings underscored the cooperative, multi-mechanistic design of TAVO412.

TAVO412 (74% TGI) had stronger TGI than bevacizumab analog (39% TGI) and even the combination of the bevacizumab analog (a-VEGF antibody) and the EGFR x cMet bispecific antibody, JNJ-61186372 analog (Fig. 4, E and I) (47 %TGI).

Finally, TAVO412 induced degradation of EGFR and cMet in vivo. Western blot analysis of MDA-MB-231 tumors collected after two doses showed significantly reduced total receptor levels, consistent with receptor internalization and downregulation following multivalent engagement. (Fig. 4, G and H). Collectively, TAVO412 exhibited superior efficacy over the bevacizumab and JNJ-61186372 analogues in the MDA-MB-231, BT-20, and HCC70 TNBC cell line derived xenograft (CDX) models, including those with low receptor expression, underscoring its broad therapeutic potential for TNBC.

Discussion

TNBC remains one of the most therapeutically challenging malignancies due to its molecular heterogeneity and lack of defined targets. Despite improvements in combined chemotherapy and immunotherapy, durable clinical benefit remains limited, underscoring the need for new treatment strategies that can simultaneously intercept multiple oncogenic pathways and overcome adaptive resistance mechanisms.

TAVO412 was engineered to address this challenge by integrating three mechanistic components—EGFR blockade, cMet inhibition, and VEGF neutralization—into a single tri-specific antibody. This design enables coordinated suppression of proliferative signaling, invasive behavior, and angiogenesis, while also enhancing Fc-mediated immune effector functions. Although the TAVO412 affinity and ligand blocking parameters, and pharmacokinetic properties have been described for lung cancer cell lines (26), the present study extends it characterization TNBC including the basal-like (BL) and the mesenchymal stem-like (MSL) subgroups (27).

The MSL subtype, exemplified by MDA-MB-231, exhibits high angiogenic activity and elevated VEGF levels. (7). In the MDA-MB-231 CDX model, the TAVO412 showed superior results compared to the JNJ-61186372 analog, possibly due to the presence of the anti -VEGF arm that inhibited angiogenesis to promote TGI.

HCC70 and BT-20 belong to the BL subtype that has higher levels of EGFR expression which is a predictor of poor patient survival (54%) independent of nodal status and tumor size when compared to patients with non-BL tumors (11%) (28). Compared to luminal cell lines, the BL subtypes have higher expression levels of EGFR and cMet which could be the focus of targeted therapies (10, 29, 30). In BL models, TAVO412 maintained strong activity despite differences in receptor density, demonstrating that its avidity-driven design supports robust engagement even when EGFR and cMet levels vary (31). These biological distinctions provided an opportunity to evaluate the multi-pathway targeting capacity of TAVO412 across diverse TNBC contexts.

We confirmed TAVO412 binding to three representative TNBC lines (Fig. 1, C–E) with comparable EC50 binding potency values. However, the efficacy of binding as reflected in the saturation values on the y axes of these binding curves correlated to the EGFR and cMET receptor density levels (Table S4). Hence, we compared side-by-side determination of EGFR and cMET receptor density levels. The receptor density in BT-20 confirmed the presence of EGFR and cMet albeit at lower levels than the other two TNBC cell lines, MDA-MB-231 and HCC70 (29, 30).

TAVO412 demonstrated several advantageous mechanistic features. First, its α-EGFR arm contains dual non-overlapping epitope binders that confer 4.4-fold higher affinity than the EGFR/cMet bispecific analog JNJ-61186372. Second, although its α-cMet binding affinity was roughly one 10th in monovalent form, overall tumor-cell avidity increased through cooperative engagement between the EGFR and cMet arms. Third, the monovalent α-VEGF arm effectively inhibited VEGF-VEGFR signaling while contributing to tumor targeting and localization, reducing the risk of systemic toxicity associated with conventional anti-VEGF therapies. Importantly, the three binding domains operated independently without steric hindrance, enabling simultaneous target engagement.

Consistent with these mechanistic attributes, TAVO412 demonstrated potent inhibition of EGFR and cMet phosphorylation. TAVO412’s strong cell-surface avidity (32), which promotes optimal Fc presentation as well as enhanced FcγR binding (33)—driven by LPLIL Fc mutations (34)—supported superior ADCC, ADCP, and CDC activities across multiple TNBC cell lines, including those with low EGFR/cMet expression. TAVO412 triggered CDC in MDA-MB-231 cells where the JNJ-61186372 analog did not. These findings suggest that both the avidity gained from multi-epitope engagement and improved FcγR interactions synergistically enhance immune cell-mediated killing.

In vivo, TAVO412 exhibited broad antitumor efficacy across diverse TNBC xenograft models representing BL and MSL molecular subtypes. The BT-20 xenograft model provided a particularly stringent test of TAVO412’s capabilities. BT-20 cells express low levels of both EGFR and cMet—below typical thresholds for strong Fc-effector activation—yet TAVO412 achieved 74% TGI, whereas the JNJ-61186372 analog was largely ineffective. This result underscores the importance of simultaneously blocking EGFR, cMet, and VEGF signaling, especially in tumors where individual pathways may be insufficiently dominant to support single-target therapies. Control variants lacking individual functional arms or an active Fc confirmed that all components contribute collectively to tumor suppression. Moreover, the observed reduction of EGFR and cMet protein levels in treated tumors indicates that downstream receptor degradation complements signaling inhibition.

Nonetheless, the Fc effector functions demonstrated in vivo were still muted since we utilized nude mice with attenuated immune systems. Hence, such CDX models showed the potential level for Fc effector functions without demonstrating the full range of such responses in the clinical setting.

Domain-inactivation studies further confirmed that all three binding arms and the enhanced Fc domain contribute to maximal antitumor activity. Removal of any single arm reduced TGI, with the most pronounced loss observed when the VEGF arm was removed. These results highlight the cooperative nature of TAVO412’s design and support the concept that multi-pathway blockade is necessary to overcome TNBC’s complex resistance mechanisms.

In the BT20 CDX model at equivalent dose levels, TAVO412 at 10 mg/kg had a significantly better antitumor (74% TGI) activity when compared to the bevacizumab analog at 5 mg/kg (45% TGI). The anti-tumor activity of TAVO412 was greatly decreased when an inert arm replaced the α-VEGF arm. While the bevacizumab had stronger binding avidity to VEGF and better blocking of the VEGF/VEGFR interactions, TAVO412 was able to use the α-EGFR and α-cMet arms to guide the α-VEGF domain to the tumor tissue. The TAVO412 unique design allowed for strong avidity of EGFR and cMet binding while projecting the α-VEGF arm away from the target cell surface. Such architecture could increase the potential blocking of VEGF from engaging the VEGFR on the TNBC cell line surface that resulted in a stronger anti-tumor effect than that of the bevacizumab analog alone or in the combination of the bevacizumab and JNJ-61186372 analogues. Notwithstanding, the TAVO412-induced antitumor growth activity in the EGFR and cMet low-expressing BT-20 tumor model could be due to both EGFR-dependent and cMet-dependent proliferation inhibition.

Mechanistically, TAVO412 brought together three orthogonal therapeutic actions: (1) blockade of mitogenic EGFR/cMet signaling, (2) suppression of VEGF-driven angiogenesis, and (3) recruitment of immune effector cells for direct cytolysis and phagocytosis. Combining these mechanisms within a single molecule reduced dependence on simultaneous administration of multiple antibodies, minimized potential pharmacokinetic mismatches, and enabled localized VEGF targeting through EGFR/cMet-guided tumor binding.

As TNBC is heterogeneous, identifying patients who will benefit most from trispecific therapy will depend on appropriate biomarkers reflecting EGFR, cMet, and VEGF pathway activation. We could not determine which mechanism was dominant since each CDX model was different in regard to their reliance distinct mechanisms linked to tumor growth. Transcriptomic profiling in related preclinical studies has helped establish candidate markers that could inform patient selection for clinical evaluation of TAVO412 (35). Additionally, preliminary combination data with doxorubicin indicate that standard chemotherapeutics may further strengthen its efficacy (26).

Overall, TAVO412 exemplified a next-generation engineered antibody capable of integrating growth-factor blockade with immune engagement. Collectively, the data demonstrated that TAVO412 integrated multi-target signaling inhibition, anti-angiogenic activity, and potent Fc-effector functions to achieve broad and durable antitumor responses across TNBC subtypes. Its ability to suppress tumor growth even in low-receptor-density models suggested potential clinical utility in heterogeneous patient populations. These findings supported further development of TAVO412 as a promising therapeutic candidate for TNBC.

Experimental procedures

Protein expression and purification

TAVO412 is a trispecific antibody in a human IgG1 backbone with knob-in-hole mutations (24). All antibody design mutation references followed the EU numbering system (36, 37). The F243 L/R292P/Y300 L/V305I/P396 L mutations were introduced to enhance Fc-mediated effector functions (25). The JNJ-61186372 analog, an EGFR x cMet BsAb (sequences referred to World Health Organization Proposed INN List 121) was generated in-house by using the controlled Fab-arm exchange process (38). These antibodies were labeled as analogues since they were not obtained as the pharmaceutical agents. The low fucose parental antibodies to generate the JNJ-61186372 analog were expressed in Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells by adding 100 μM of 2F-Peracetyl-Fucose (Sigma) into the cell culture media. Bio Mix (Sepax) chromatography was performed to analyze the levels of BsAb content by comparing JNJ-61186372 analog to the respective parental antibodies. The elution peaks corresponding to the BsAb were well resolved from the elution peaks of the respective parental antibodies. The negative control antibody (α-gp120 hIgG1) was used as an isotype control with a human IgG1 Fc domain. TAVO412 and all comparator antibodies—including the JNJ-61186372 analog, domain-inactivated TAVO412 variants, and isotype control gp120 hIgG1 were expressed in CHO cells (ExpiCHO-S, Thermo Fisher Scientific) using the standard transient transfection protocol. The antibodies were purified by standard Protein A affinity chromatography followed by ion exchange chromatography. Characterization of the purified antibody by size exclusion chromatography (39) capillary electrophoresis (24) demonstrated that the molecule was monomeric, monodisperse with purity above 95%. The anti (α)-VEGF bevacizumab analog was purchased from MedChemExpress. Doxorubicin was purchased from Shanxi Pude Pharma. The inert arm control molecules for TAVO412 were generated by replacing the entire targeting arm of the α-EGFR, α-cMET, and α -VEGF with the corresponding non-targeting anti-gp120 antibody, thereby creating functionally inert EGFR, cMET, and VEGF arms, respectively (Fig. S3). Additionally, the Fc-silenced control variant was engineered by introducing the L234 A and L235 A (LALA) mutations into the CH2 domain of Fc to minimize the binding to Fc gamma receptors (FcgR) and C1q (40).

Recombinant human EGFR ECD, cMet ECD, and VEGF-165 were purchased commercially and used for affinity and avidity assays.

Tumor cell lines and peripheral blood mononuclear cells

The in vitro and in vivo models utilized TNBC cell lines such as BT-20, HCC70, and MDA-MB-231, obtained from the American Type Culture Collection. These cells had the following various genetic alterations: BT-20 with p16 inactivation, p14ARF inactivation, EGFR amplification, PIK3CA mutation, and p53 mutation; HCC70 with a p53 mutation; and MDA-MB-231 with E-cadherin inactivation, p16 inactivation, p14ARF inactivation, KRAS G12D, BRAF mutation and p53 mutation (41, 42). All cells were obtained from Cobioer, cultured using the American Type Culture Collection -recommended media in a humidified incubator at 37 °C, with 5% CO2, and routinely evaluated for mycoplasma using the Myco-Lumi Luminescent Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Beyotime). Frozen humanPBMCs) were purchased from AllCells and SAILYBIO. The cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. The receptor density for EGFR and cMet was quantified using calibrated flow cytometry as described earlier (26).

Binding kinetics and affinity assays

The binding kinetics and affinity of TAVO412 and the comparator JNJ-61186372 analog for their target proteins were determined on the Octet Qke (Sartorius) as noted in Table S1. Recombinant human EGFR extracellular domain (ECD), cMet ECD, and VEGF-165 were purchased commercially and used for affinity and avidity assays. Affinity values measured engagement with a single antigen. Avidity values referred to engagement with more than one antigen. For instance, avidity referred to binding of EGFR, cMET, both, VEGF when presented for ELISA or on a cell surface.

Antibodies were diluted with kinetic buffer (KB) containing 1 × phosphate buffered saline (PBS), at pH 7.4 or 6.0, 0.05% (w/v) Tween-20 and 0.1% (w/v) bovine serum albumin (BSA to a final concentration of 5 μg/ml, then were captured onto Protein A biosensor (Sartorius). Subsequently, 2-fold serially diluted recombinant antigens ranging from 100 to 1.562 nM were injected. Association rate constants (kon) and dissociation rate constants (koff) were calculated using the Forte Bio data analysis software version 12.2.0.20. The equilibrium dissociation constant (KD) was calculated as the quotient of koff/kon.

In simultaneous binding assays, TAVO412 antibody, which was diluted to 1 μg/ml in KB, was captured onto Protein A biosensors to reach a binding threshold of 1 nm. After the sensor rinsing step in KB for 100 s, the association profiles of VEGF-A (more potent isoform used for binding VEGF-165, also known as VEGF-A165) (43, 44), EGFR, and cMet antigens at a concentration of 50 nM were sequentially measured for 180 s, respectively. KD values represented the mean values ± standard deviation of at least three independent experiments.

ELISA binding and ligand blocking assays

The antigen binding and the Inhibition of ligand binding were measured using ELISA. To assess avidity under conditions mimicking antigen clustering, plates were coated with recombinant EGFR ECD, cMet ECD, or VEGF-165 - Streptavidin (SA) coated ELISA plates were incubated with 0.05 μg biotinylated antigens at room temperature for 1 h. After washing three times with PBS, serially diluted antibodies were added into the ELISA plates. After incubation at room temperature for 1 h, horse radish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat α-human IgG was applied for 30 min followed by a 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate reaction for 5 min. Stop solution was added to terminate the reaction and color development was quantified by measuring optical densities at 450 nm using Spark multimode microplate reader (TECAN).

Blocking assays measured the prevention of human EGF binding to EGFR, human HGF binding to cMet, and VEGF binding to VEGFR. Serial dilutions of TAVO412, JNJ-61186372 analog, and inert arm hIgG1were applied to ELISA wells coated with the respective ECD domains of EGFR, cMet, and VEGFR. The respective ELISA blocking assays used a serial concentration of TAVO412, using JNJ-61186372 analog as a benchmark molecule for comparison and α-gp120 hIgG1 as a negative isotype control. For the determination of TAVO412 dose-response blocking of EGF, ELISA plates were coated with SA at a concentration of 0.5 μg/ml, and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After washing and blocking with 3% (w/v) BSA in PBS, biotinylated human EGFR was added at a concentration of 0.5 μg/ml. After a 3-step wash procedure, TAVO412 and controls were serially diluted in antibody dilution buffer, and added to the plate, and incubated for 1 h. After the washing step, EGF was added at a concentration of 0.3 μg/ml. After a 1 h incubation, the plates were washed and the primary detection antibody, rabbit α-human EGF pAb, was added (with 1:1000 dilution), and incubated for 1 h. After a wash step, the second antibody, goat-α-rabbit IgG (H + L), HRP was added (1:5000 dilution) and incubated for 30 min. TMB solution was then added and after a 4 min incubation, the plate was read for absorbance at 450 nm. The A450 nm was determined for each drug/concentration, and a dose response curve was generated. The cMet blocking assays used a similar experiment above but with 0.6 μg/ml HGF (NoVo protein #CJ72). Likewise, the VEGF blocking used the same assay as above but had VEGF165 adsorbed on the plate and assessed with 1.2 μg/ml VEGFR2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific #MA5-35963). For domain-inactivation studies, TAVO412 variants lacking individual binding arms were tested in parallel.

Cellular binding assays

The binding of TAVO412 to TNBC cells including MDA-MB-231, HCC70, and BT-20 was evaluated using flow cytometry. TNBC target cells at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well were incubated with serially diluted antibodies in the dark at 4 °C for 1 h. After washing in fluorescence activated cell sorting (FACS) buffer for three times, AF647 goat α -human IgG1 Fc (1:5000 dilution) was added. After 30 min, the cells were washed in FACS buffer three times and then resuspended in 150 μl FACS buffer for analysis via flow cytometry. Experiments were performed in triplicate.

The mean fluorescence intensity of the cells was plotted against the antibody concentrations The half-maximal EC50 values in nM with a 95% confidence intervals as well as the Emax value (Y axis span value)were determined by nonlinear regression fitting using a Variable Slope (log(agonist); Hill Slope; 4 parameters).

Time-resolved fluorescence and resonance energy transfer EGFR and cMet phosphorylation assays

Cells were stimulated with EGF, HGF, or both, and then treated with antibodies for 15 min. Lysates were analyzed using time-resolved fluorescence and resonance energy transfer (TR-FRET) kits specific for phosphorylated EGFR (Y1068) or cMet (Y1234/Y1235). TNBC cells were seeded at a density of 4 × 104 cells per well in 96-well plates and starved in non-FBS medium overnight. After starvation, cells were treated with serially diluted antibodies at 37 °C for 1 h. After stimulation with 1650 ng/ml EGF and/or 816 ng/ml HGF for 5 min, the MDA-MB-231 cells were lysed. The levels of EGFR and cMet phosphorylation were detected using the Thunder Phospho-Met (Y1234/1235) TR-FRET kit and Phospho-EGFR (Y1069) TR-FRET kit following the manufacturer’s protocols (Bioauxilium). The half-maximal IC50 values were determined as the concentrations of inhibitor that resulted in one-half of the maximal responses. The data were plotted using GraphPad Prism 9.3.1. and the dose-response curves to calculate IC50 values were fitted using nonlinear regression model with a sigmoidal dose response. The phosphorylation rate (%) = (Signal Test article-Signal detection buffer control)/(Signal None treated- Signal detection buffer control) × 100%.

Recombinant human HGF and recombinant human EGF were purchased from NoVo protein.

Fc gamma receptors and C1q binding assays

FcγRIIIa, FcγRIIa, FcγRI, and C1q binding were measured by ELISA. Plates coated with recombinant FcγRs or C1q were incubated with serial dilutions of antibodies. Bound antibody was detected using HRP-conjugated anti-human Fc. EC50 values were determined by nonlinear regression. Briefly, 100 μl (per well) of 0.5 μg/ml streptavidin were added into a 96-well microplate and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The following day after the washing and the blocking steps, 100 μl of 0.5 μg/ml biotinylated recombinant human (rh) CD16a, rhCD32a, rhCD64, and rhC1q were added to a 96 well plate and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. After washing three times with PBS, the serially diluted antibodies were added and then incubated at room temperature for 1 h. After washing three times in PBS, a 1:5000 dilution of the horse radish peroxidase-conjugated goat-α-human IgG1 antibody was added, incubated for 30 min at room temperature, and then reacted with the TMB substrate reaction for 5 min. The stop solution was added to terminate the reaction and color development was quantified by measuring optical densities at 450 nm in a Spark multimode microplate reader (TECAN).

ADCC primary cell killing assays

PBMC - driven killing assays measured the levels of in vitro ADCC activities on TNBC cells. Primary human PBMCs from three healthy donors were used as effector cells. TNBC target cells were labeled with CFSE and incubated with antibodies at varying concentrations. After 4 h, cell lysis was quantified using flow cytometry. Percent cytotoxicity was calculated relative to untreated controls. In brief, frozen PBMC were thawed in a 37 °C water bath then transferred to a T75 flask, followed by incubation overnight at 37 °C. TNBC target cells at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well were co-incubated with PBMCs at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well in the presence of serially diluted antibodies for 6 h. The levels of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) released into the supernatants were detected by a Roche LDH kit following the protocols in the manufacturer manual.

ADCP primary cell killing assays

Human monocyte-derived macrophages were co-cultured with CFSE-labeled TNBC cells in the presence of antibodies. After 24 h, phagocytosis was quantified by flow cytometry as the percentage of macrophages containing CFSE-positive material.

Monocytes, isolated from frozen human PBMCs using EasySep Human Monocyte Enrichment Kit (StemCell), were induced into macrophages by adding macrophage colony stimulating factor (StemCell) and IFNγ (StemCell). CFSE-labeled tumor target cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well. Monocyte-derived macrophages at the density of 1 × 105 cells per well were co-cultured with target cells in the presence of serially diluted antibodies for 24 h. Then cells were harvested by Accutase and stained by Alexa647-labeled CD14 and CD11b antibodies (R&D). CSFE positive cells and Alexa 647 positive cells were detected by flow cytometry. The killing percentage values were calculated by the following equation: = 100∗((average %FITC + APC-A- of [lowest mAb] for each antibody -%FITC + APC-A -sample)/(average %FITC + APC-A - of [lowest mAb] for each antibody) (45).

CDC killing assays

TNBC cells were incubated with antibodies and human complement serum for 1 h. Cell lysis was quantified using LDH release assays. Percent CDC was calculated relative to maximum lysis controls. MDA-MB-231 cells were grown in DMEM medium and passaged every 3 days. After being harvested in Enzyme-Free Dissociation Buffer (Gibco), MDA-MB-231 cells were seeded at the density of 2.0 × 104 per well in 50 μl of RPMI1640 medium without phenol red. Serially diluted antibodies were added to the cells and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Then 50 ml aliquot of a 1:4 diluted rabbit complement (Cedarlane) in RPMI1640 was added into each well followed by an incubation at 37 °C for 4 h. Cytotoxicity was determined by LDH released into the supernatant using LDH kit (Roche).

In vivo CDX models

CDX models in female BALB/c nude mice were conducted to measure in vivo TGI. All animals were maintained under pathogen-free conditions in the Experimental Animal Center of GenePharma. All animal related experiments were approved by the GenePharma Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) prior to conducting the studies. The use of animals for this study complied with the ARRIVE guidelines (46). Tumor cells were harvested and counted with a cell counter (Countstar by ALIT Life Sciences). HCC70 (1.0 × 107), BT-20 (3.0 × 106) and MDA-MB-231 (5.0 × 106) cells mixed with matrigel (Corning #354234) (at a 1:1 ratio) were implanted subcutaneously into the right flank of female BALB/c nude mice. Once the mean tumor volume reached 100 to 200 mm3, the mice were randomly assigned to treatment groups (n = 6/group). The first day of treatment was denoted as day 0. All the antibodies and doxorubicin were intraperitoneally injected twice a week for 2 or 3 weeks. The tumor volumes and body weights were measured twice a week. For all in vivo studies, the tumor volume in mm3 was calculated using the following formula: (width)2 x length/2 (47). TGI (%) was calculated based on the data of the last day. Animals were euthanized when tumor volume exceeded 2000 mm3 or when the percentage of body weight loss exceeded 20%. At the end of the study, the tumors of all groups were excised from the mice and photographed.

Measurement of total EGFR and cMet in tumor tissue

Nude mice bearing MDA-MB-231 xenografts were treated with two doses of TAVO412 or polyclonal human IgG (HaoKeSaiYe, Beijing) at 10 mg/kg twice every week. Twenty-four hours after the second dose, the tumors were isolated and lysed in RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) containing protease inhibitor (Thermo Fisher Scientific#1860932) and phosphatase inhibitor (Roche #04906837001) using a homogenizer. Protein samples were detected for EGFR and cMet by Western blotting. Protein samples were loaded into an SDS-PAGE gel (GenScript) and then transferred onto Immobilon-P Polyvinylidene Fluoride membrane. Membranes were blocked with 5% (w/v) BSA in Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% (w/v) Tween-20 detergent (TBST) for 2 h, and subsequently incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies (1:500 diluted): α-phospho-EGFR Y1173 (CST, 4407S), α-EGFR (CST, 2085S), α-phospho-cMet Y1349 (CST, 3121S), α-cMet (CST, 4560S), α-β-Actin (CST, 8457S).

Statistical analyses

ELISA and cell binding avidity values were calculated from triplicate measurements. The values of absorbance or g mean fluorescence intensity were plotted as a “best fit” curve with GraphPad Prism version 9.3.1 using a Variable Slope agonist model. Nonlinear regression was used for the analysis. Half-maximal effective concentration (EC50) value was determined as the concentration of the test article that gives a half-maximal response. The half-maximal activity concentration (IC50) values were determined as the concentration of inhibitor that gave a half-maximal response. Maximal response and minimal response values were based on the plateau values in the units of the Y axis. For the ligand blocking measurements, the dose-response curves were fit using nonlinear regression model for dose responses [log(inhibitor concentration) versus response -- Variable slope model (four parameters)].

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism software version 9.3.1 (GraphPad Software, Inc.). A comparison between two groups was performed using the Student’s t test. Multiple group comparisons used a parametric one-way ANOVA followed by post hoc test (Tukey’s test or Dunn’s test). p values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.
