Pulsed radiofrequency alleviates neuropathic pain by upregulating MG53 to inhibit microglial activation
Authors: Yuanxin Huang, Yuanyue Huang, Xianglong Lv, Zilong Yu, Yue Qin, Xingyue Yang, Songsong An, Chunxin Wo, Lin Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatients with neuropathic pain (NP) have significantly lower quality of life. Because the pathophysiology of NP is not fully understood, there is a lack of effective treatment for it in clinic. This study set out to investigate the precise mechanism by which pulsed radiofrequency (PRF) alleviated NP.MethodThe rat models of chronic constriction injury of the sciatic nerve (CCI) were established to simulate the occurrence of NP, following with measuring MWT and TWL to evaluate the pain of the rats. HE staining was utilized to observe the rat spinal cord tissue pathology. The expression of MG53, ATF4 and CHOP was evaluated by qRT-PCR and WB, while the expression of inflammatory factors was measured by ELISA. In addition, immunofluorescence assay was used to detect the expression of MG53 and Iba-1.ResultPRF treatment alleviated NP in CCI rats, as well as upregulating the expression of MG53 and inhibiting microglial activation. After MG53 knockdown, the remission of NP by PRF was significantly weakened, but microglial activation and endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS) exhibited enhancement. Therefore, PRF inhibited microglial activation by upregulating MG53. After injection of ERS inducer in CCI rats, the inhibition effect of overexpressed MG53 on microglial activation and its alleviation effect on NP were reversed. Consequently, MG53 played a role in suppressing microglial activation by mediating the inhibition of ERS.ConclusionPRF attenuated microglial activation by upregulating MG53 to inhibit ERS, resulting in the alleviation of NP in CCI rats.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-024-02134-6.

Introduction

Neuropathic pain (NP) is pain directly caused by lesions or diseases affecting the somatosensory system, including pain syndromes with various peripheral or central disorders [1, 2]. Furthermore, NP exhibits a spectrum of severity, ranging from moderate to excruciating levels, significantly impairing the patient's quality of life. In extreme cases, patients suffering from NP may necessitate amputation. The disease affects up to 5% of the population, 70% of patients with advanced cancer and inflammation, and 95% of patients with spinal cord injury [3]. Antidepressants, calcium channel α2-δ ligands, topical local anesthetics, and opioid analgesics are the main treatments for NP [4]. However, tolerability and side effects of drug therapy make the efficacy of drug therapy for NP still far from satisfactory, with less than half of patients achieving significant efficacy [5]. Consequently, there is still a need to develop new and more effective ways to alleviate NP.

Pulsed radiofrequency (PRF) is a new technique developed on the basis of continuous radiofrequency for the treatment of chronic pain, which has the advantages of less complications and tissue damage. In addition, clinical data have implied that PRF is effective in the treatment of a variety of chronic pain [6]. In recent years, PRF has become a mainstream clinical treatment for NP [7–10]. Nevertheless, the mechanism of PRF in treating NP remains unclear.

Microglia are a type of glial cells, which are distributed throughout the brain and spinal cord. Substantial data have demonstrated the critical role that signal transmission plays in NP between glial cells, particularly spinal microglia, and peripheral sensory neurons [11, 12]. A significant factor in the formation and progression of NP is the activation of microglia and the pro-inflammatory response it triggers [13]. PRF has been found to inhibit microglial activation in NP [14, 15]. However, it is unknown how PRF prevents the activation of microglia.

Endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS) is the obstruction of a cell's ability to properly fold and modify secreted and transmembrane proteins after translation in the endoplasmic reticulum, resulting in the accumulation of misfolded proteins in organelles [16]. ERS restores protein homeostasis by activating an adaptive unfolded protein response [17]. The existing literature has confirmed that ERS is closely related to neuronal cell death [18]. Additionally, ERS plays a regulatory role in microglial activation, as evidenced by Ilandarage’s study [19].

Mitsugumin 53 (MG53), also known as tripartite motif protein 72 (TRIM72), is a recently identified protein belonging to the tripartite motif protein (TRIM) family that is essential for mending damage of cell membranes and stimulating tissue regeneration [20]. MG53 has been reported to play a role in inhibiting microglial activation and alleviating neuroinflammation [21–23]. In spite of that, whether MG53 affects NP by regulating microglial activation remains to be explored. Thus, NP model was constructed to explore the specific mechanism of PRF alleviating NP in this study. The results of the research elucidated that PRF mitigated NP through MG53-mediated the suppression of microglial activation. These findings provided new insights into the pathogenesis and the search for effective ways of NP.

Materials and methods

Animals

The Sprague Dawley (SD) male rats, aged 6 weeks, were acquired from Beijing Spafu Biotechnology Co., LTD (Beijing, China). The rats were fed in a Specific Pathogen Free (SPF) animal laboratory at 21–23 °C and 60%–65% humidity for 1 week, during which they ate and drank freely. The experiment began one week after the rats had acclimated. All animal experiments in this study were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Affiliated Hospital of Guizhou Medical University. The dead rats in the experiment were properly disposed in accordance with the Operating Procedures for Disposal of Laboratory Animal Carcasses of Guizhou Medical University. Animal carcasses were sealed in a special biosafety bag and stored in a temporary refrigerator at −20 ℃ for centralized freezing storage, which finally were incinerated by a professional company for harmless treatment.

Chronic constriction injury of the sciatic nerve (CCI) rat model

The 40 rats were randomly divided into 4 groups: control group, Sham group, CCI group and CCI + PRF group. After the rats were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection, the left sciatic nerve in the middle thigh of the rats was exposed by blunt muscle stripping. The nerves were then isolated from the adhesive tissue adjacent to the sciatic nerve, following with ligating at four sites 1 mm apart above the trigeminal branch of the sciatic nerve. The tightness of the ligature was adjusted to an appropriate level where slight twitching of the surrounding muscles was able to be observed. The same procedure was performed on the Sham group of rats, but only the left sciatic nerve was exposed with no ligating. To investigate the effect of PRF on NP, PRF treatment was performed as previously described at the sciatic nerve ligation site in CCI rats at the day after surgery [7]. In brief, PRF treatment lasted for 300 s at a pulse frequency of 2 Hz, a temperature of 42 °C and an output voltage of 45 V. During PRF treatment, the right thigh of the rats exhibited a slight twitch. During CCI modeling, 3 out of 40 rats died. There were no deaths in the Control group and the Sham group, 2 deaths in the CCI group and 1 death in the CCI + PRF group.

Afterwards, to explore the effect of MG53 knockdown on NP, 40 rats were randomly divided into CCI group, CCI + PRF group, CCI + PRF + AAV-sh-NC group and CCI + PRF + AAV-sh-MG53 group. The latter two groups were, respectively, treated with intrathecal injection of AAV-sh-NC or AAV-sh-MG53 at 6 weeks before CCI modeling. The rats were treated with molding and PRF according to the above methods. In addition, in order to investigate the role of upregulation of MG53 in NP, 30 rats were randomly divided into 3 groups for CCI modeling. Two out of three groups, respectively, received intrathecal injection of AAV-NC or AAV-MG53 before modeling. In order to clarify the mechanism of MG53 regulating microglial activation, another 30 rats were randomly divided into 3 groups and CCI modeling was performed as above. Two groups out of them were, respectively, treated with CCI after intrathecal injection of AAV-MG53 or AAV-MG53 + tunicamycin. During this period, nine rats died, one each from the CCI + PRF group, the CCI + PRF + AAV-sh-MG53 group, the CCI + AAV-NC group, the CCI + AAV-MG53 group and the CCI + AAV-MG53 + tunicamycin group, as well as two each from CCI + PRF + AAV-sh-NC group and CCI + AAV-MG53 group. Euthanasia of the rats was performed as previously [24]. Briefly, 14 days after surgery, the rats were killed by cervical dislocation companied with inhalation of isoflurane (0.41 mL/min). Spinal cord tissues from the euthanized rats were isolated for further testing. The instrument of PRF treatment was RFE4 series 4 channels radiofrequency (RF) Lesion Generator (Beijing Neo Science, Beijing, China), including RF electrode CD2201(OD = 0.4 mm, L = 50 mm) and RF cannula 240,100 (22G-50 mm, −4 mm, straight).

Mechanical withdrawal threshold (MWT) test

MWT tests were performed at 2 days before CCI surgery and 1, 3, 7, and 14 days after surgery. The plantar mechanical pain test system was used to stimulate the center of the plantar on both sides of the rats with 16 different intensity stimuli, 10 times for each intensity, according to the intensity from small to large. The intensity of the stimulus, in which more than 5 times paw withdrawal responses were observed in rats, was identified as the threshold for the response of rats to mechanical stimuli.

Thermal withdrawal latency (TWL) test

TWL tests were also performed at 2 days before CCI surgery and 1, 3, 7, and 14 days after surgery. The procedure was as follows: the rats were placed in a glass box containing a smart hot-plate pain meter, in which the rats were able to move their whole body freely. After the rats stood for 10 min, the left and right heels of the rats were illuminated with a strong light of the same intensity. The time from the onset of light to the withdrawal of the rat's feet was subsequently recorded and measured every 5 min for a total of three measurements. The average of the three results was taken to evaluate the thermal hyperalgesia of the rats.

Cell culture

Rat primary microglia (Cybercon Biotechnology Co., LTD, Shanghai, China) were cultured in primary astrocyte medium containing 5% fetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin streptomycin and 1% primary astrocyte culture additive. The conventional culture conditions of the cells were 37 °C and 5% CO2.

Cell transfection

Primary rat microglia were cultured until the number of cells had increased by about 70%–80%, following with the removing of previous medium. After washing the cells twice with PBS, the cells were cultured overnight in a new serum-free and antibiotic-free medium. After dilution with serum-free Dulbecco's modified eagle medium (DMEM), primary rat microglia were transfected with si-MG53-1, si-MG53-2, si-MG53-3 and si-NC plasmid (2.5 μg) (Genepharma, Suzhou, Jiangsu, China) applying Lipofectamine 2000 (4 μL) (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). After an additional 6 h of incubation, the supernatant was aspirated and the medium was replaced. Subsequently, the cells continued to be cultured for 48 h. Microglia were divided into Control group, si-NC group and si-MG53 group.

Hematoxylin–eosin staining (HE)

Spinal cord tissue of rats fixed with 4% polyformaldehyde was dehydrated and paraffin embedded. Afterwards, the intact tissue was cut into wax sheets with a thickness of 4 µm, which were then applied and dried. Subsequently, the sections were stained with hematoxylin dye (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 8 min, following with staining with eosin dye (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 5 min. The pathological injury of rat spinal cord was observed by optical microscope after sealing the stained tissue sections.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The expression levels of TNF-α, IL-6 and IL-1β were detected by ELISA kits (Elabscience, Wuhan, Hubei, China). Under the condition of adding protease inhibitors, the spinal cord tissue was fully ground and ultrasonically broken on ice. The treated tissue was then centrifuged and the supernatant is placed on ice for use. The cells of logarithmic growth stage were collected and washed 3 times with cold PBS. Then, the cells were broken by repeated freezing and thawing after suspension. The cell extract was centrifuged at 2–8 °C for 10 min to obtain the supernatant for use. At the same, the extract of tissue or cell was analyzed according to the instructions of the ELISA kit. Finally, enzyme-labeled analyzer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was used to measure the absorbance of each hole in sequence. ELISA kits for rat TNF-α, IL-6 and IL-1β were purchased from Wuhan Ilerite Biological Co., LTD.

Western blotting (WB)

The total protein extracted was quantified for protein concentration utilizing the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) kit (Biosharp, Guangzhou, Guangdong, China). The protein samples underwent electrophoresis on sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) polyacrylamide gel. Subsequently, the proteins were transferred from the gel to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane and blocked with 5% BSA for a duration of 2 h at ambient temperature. The membrane was then subjected to incubation with primary antibodies for MG53 (1:1,000, ab307593; Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA), ATF4 (1:1,000, ab270980; Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA), CHOP (1:1,000, A20987; ABclonal Technology, Wuhan, Hubei, China) and β-actin (1:1,000, ab8226; Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA) overnight at 4 °C. After washing three times with TBST buffer, the membrane was incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:5000, Ab0101; Abways Technology, Shanghai, China) and HRP-labeled goat anti-mouse IgG (1:5000, Ab0102; Abways Technology, Shanghai, China) secondary antibody for 2 h at 37 °C. The relative expression of the target protein was analyzed by Image J v1.8.0 software.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted by adding TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) in each group, and the extracted total RNA was reversely transcribed into cDNA according to the instructions of PrimeScript RT reagent Kit (Takara, Tokyo, Japan), and then the cDNA was amplified. The primers were designed by Primer-BLAST software and synthesized by Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA), and the primer sequences and product lengths were shown in Table 1. Homologous double label immunofluorescence (IF) analysis

After deparaffinization and rehydration, tissue slides were treated with 100 μL endogenous peroxidase blockers for 10 min. Then, tissue slides were incubated with monoclonal primary antibody against Iba-1 (1:200, Ab178846; Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA) overnight at 4 °C. Subsequently, HRP-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:5000, Ab0101; Abways Technology, Shanghai, China) secondary antibody was applied to the sections at room temperature for 50 min, following with tyramide signal amplification (TSA) stain. After antigen repair of the sections again, another polyclonal primary antibody against MG53 (1:200, ab307593; Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA) was added to the sections overnight at 4 °C. Afterwards, the sections were incubated again with HRP-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody in the dark at room temperature for 50 min. Next, the slides were counterstained with 4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Servicebio Biotechnology, Wuhan, Hubei, China) and then were visualized using microscope.

Statistical analysis

The data from this chapter of the study were statistically analyzed using SPSS 23.0 and the results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD). Statistical methods were one-way ANOVA, Chi-square test, or non-parametric test depending on the data characteristics of the different comparison groups. Differences were considered statistically significant if P < 0.05.

Results

PRF alleviated NP in CCI rats by inhibiting the activation of microglia

In comparison to the Sham group, the CCI model group exhibited significantly reduced MWT and TWL values on days 1, 3, 7, and 14, indicating the successful establishment of the CCI rat models. Following with PRF treatment, the TWL value normalized by day 7 and the MWT value approached normal levels by day 14, suggesting that PRF effectively mitigated NP in CCI rats (Fig. 1A, B). Histological examination of the spinal dorsal horn revealed that tissue rupture and notable neuronal atrophy were presented in the CCI group compared to the Sham group, with PRF treatment causing a restoration of tissue integrity and neuronal structure (Fig. 1C). Besides, the upregulation of inflammatory factors TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β, alongside the downregulation of MG53 expression, were observed in the CCI rat models. Treatment with PRF resulted in the inhibition of inflammatory factors expression and the upregulation of MG53, as evidenced by ELISA, qRT-PCR and WB analysis (Fig. 1D–F). Furthermore, IF analysis demonstrated that PRF treatment significantly suppressed the expression of the microglial activation marker Iba-1 in CCI rats, while also inducing an increase in the expression of MG53 (Fig. 1G–I). In addition, the findings from qRT-PCR and WB analysis indicated a decrease in the expression of ERS-related proteins ATF4 and CHOP in CCI rats following PRF treatment (Fig. 1J, K). These findings suggested the presence of ERS and microglial activation in CCI rats. PRF inhibited the activation of microglia in CCI rats by upregulating MG53

The knockdown of MG53 resulted in a significant reduction in the impact of PRF treatment on MWT and TWL values in CCI rats (Fig. 2A, B). Analysis through qRT-PCR and WB assays indicated a marked decrease in the expression of MG53 in the CCI + PRF + AAV-sh-MG53 group compared to the CCI + PRF + AAV-sh-NC group (Fig. 2C, D). Furthermore, the results of HE staining verified that the knockdown of MG53 led to the re-injury of spinal dorsal horn tissue and neuronal structure that had previously been repaired by PRF treatment (Fig. 2E). In comparison to the PRF treatment group, the IF detection revealed an increase in the expression of the microglial activation marker Iba-1 (Fig. 2F, G). Additionally, by ELISA, qRT-PCR and WB detection, it was observed that compared with the PRF treatment group, the expression levels of ATF4, CHOP and inflammatory factors were higher in the MG53 knockdown group (Fig. 2H–J). The findings of qRT-PCR and WB analysis displayed a significant elevation in the levels of MG53 mRNA and protein in CCI rats with overexpressed MG53 compared to the control group (Fig. 4C, D). Furthermore, histological analysis using HE staining confirmed that the overexpression of MG53 mitigated and repaired spinal dorsal horn tissue damage and neuronal atrophy in CCI rats (Fig. 4E). IF analysis revealed a reduction in the expression of the microglial activation marker Iba-1 in CCI rats as a result of overexpressed MG53 (Fig. 4F, G). Moreover, ELISA, qRT-PCR and WB analysis demonstrated that elevated levels of MG53 resulted in decreased levels of TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, ATF4 and CHOP in CCI rats (Fig. 4H–J). Discussion

NP is a chronic pain that is particularly difficult to manage and leads to severe disability and poor quality of life [25]. In addition, NP is associated with many diseases [5]. With the development of aging population, the prevalence of NP will further increase [26, 27]. At present, the unclear pathogenesis of NP is an important factor limiting its treatment. This study revealed the specific mechanism by which PRF alleviated NP through upregulated MG53-mediated microglial activation.

Microglia are the basis of NP and are mediators of neuroinflammation [13]. Microglia make up 5%-10% of the cells in the central nervous system and are widely considered to be outpost cells of the brain [28]. Under healthy conditions, microglia defend against infection and tissue damage by secreting pro-inflammatory and pro-fading mediators [29]. However, under pathological conditions, microglia cause persistent and destructive neuroinflammation [29, 30]. Existing literature has confirmed that microglial activation triggers the synthesis and release of several pro-inflammatory factors, including IL-6, TNF-α and IL-1β, thereby reshaping the spinal cord circuit closely associated with NP [29–31]. Moreover, a series of studies have verified that widespread targeting of microglial activation with tetracycline antibiotics inhibits pro-inflammatory cytokines and reduces neuropathic pain [32–37]. These studies further reveal the relationship between NP and microglial activation and its induced pro-inflammatory response. Because Iba-1 expression is specific and is expressed in both reactive and stationary microglia, Iba-1 has been widely used to study microglia [38]. Besides, several studies have identified Iba-1 as a marker to measure the activation of microglia [39]. Microglial activation and its induced pro-inflammatory responses were observed in our CCI rat model, as measured by significantly increased expression of Iba-1, TNFα, IL-6 and IL-1β, consistent with literature reports. After PRF treatment, the expression of Iba-1 and pro-inflammatory factors dropped, indicating that PRF alleviated NP in CCI rats via preventing microglial activation.

MG53 is a multifunctional protein involved in important physiological and pathological processes of multiple organs, with classical membrane repair functions and anti-inflammatory capabilities [20]. MG53 has been reported to reduce LPS-induced neurotoxicity and neuroinflammation by reducing the production of IL-1β and IL-6 and inhibiting microglial activation [21]. Additionally, MG53 plays a therapeutic role in attenuating LPS-induced neuroinflammation due to its role in inhibiting microglial activation and the expression of TNF-α, TLR4, NLRP3, cleave-caspase-1 and IL-1β [22]. One study has presented that the MG53/GMs/HA-Dex neural scaffold significantly improves motor function in SCI mice, as well as inhibits microglial activation and neuroinflammation [23]. Consistent with this evidence, overexpressed MG53 inhibited Iba-1, TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β expression in CCI rats in this work. These results hinted that upregulation of MG53 alleviated NP in CCI rats by prohibiting microglial activation. Furthermore, MG53 regulates a variety of signaling pathways, such as Nrf2/HO-1 signaling pathway [40], AMPK signaling pathway [41, 42], PI3K–AKT signaling pathway [43], NF-kB signaling pathway [44], etc. Among them, a recent study has reported that MG53-mediated Nrf2/HO-1 signaling pathway alleviates NP and neuroinflammation in rats [40]. Based on these and the findings of this study, MG53 is expected to be a new target for the treatment of neuropathic pain. Other researchers have demonstrated that the LKB1–AMPK signaling pathway, TLR4/NF-kB signaling pathway and TLR4/NF-kB signaling pathway are involved in microglial activation and its mediated neuroinflammation [45–47]. Therefore, which signaling pathway plays a role in the regulation of microglial activation by MG53 deserves further attention.

ERS is a state in which protein folding is slowed down due to the disturbance of many homeostasis processes in cells [48]. Transcription factors involved in ERS include ATF4 and CHOP, whose interaction triggers an unfolded protein response [49–51]. Furthermore, it has been shown by numerous researchers that ERS activates the PEPK/ATF4/CHOP pro-apoptotic pathway, which results in apoptosis [52–57]. ATF4 and CHOP expression were observed to be lowered by PRF in this investigation, indicating that PRF therapy prevented ERS activation in CCI rats. Future research on the impact of PRF on PEPK/ATF4/CHOP signaling during PRF relieving NP is warranted, given the regulation of apoptosis by ERS activation. In addition, Samidurai M et al. have displayed that inhibition of ERS activation attenuates microglial activation and inflammatory responses [58–61]. In our CCI rat models, PRF-induced inhibition of ERS was accompanied by reduced microglial activation, which was consistent with literature studies. Moreover, the experimental data in the present study confirmed that upregulation of MG53 inhibited the expression of ATF4, CHOP, Iba-1 and pro-inflammatory factors, which was reversed by ERS agonist treatment. These results indicated that PRF suppressed the activation of microglia by upregulating MG53 to mediate the inhibition of ERS.

Because of resource, time, and other constraints, the sample size of this study was relatively small, as well as the investigation was only conducted in the CCI-induced NP model. Whether PRF therapy has the same performance in other NP models has not been verified. In our CCI models, PRF treatment was performed at the sciatic nerve ligation site in rats, which ensured the precise target of PRF treatment. However, PRF is clinically often performed on the dorsal root ganglion of the patients [62]. In the studies of other scholars, there is also no evidence on the more ideal treatment site in the same NP model or in different NP models [63, 64]. Therefore, it is meaningful to further identify more effective therapeutic targets of PRF for the clinical application of PRF. Additionally, the pain of CCI rats treated with PRF in this study presented gradual relief rather than immediate relief. Based on this fact and the differences between the small animal model and the human body, it will take some time to wait for the effect of PRF in the PRF therapy clinical process in NP, which may be another challenge for clinicians and patients.

The biological pathways involved in PRF treatment of NP are multiple, including ion channels, neurotransmitters, immune activity and synaptic function and its receptors [65]. This work revealed the mechanism by which PRF regulated microglial activation in neuro immunity. The results of several studies have confirmed that ERS is closely related to the activation of NF-κB, PI3K/AKT and AMPK signaling pathways [66–69]. As well, these pathways are regulated by MG53 in different states [41–44]. However, whether MG53 is able to regulate ERS to influence biological processes is unclear. Previous studies have implied that MG53 exhibits the inhibition of microglial activation in LPS-induced mice when exploring the mechanism by which MG53 alleviates neuroinflammation [21, 22]. Nevertheless, whether MG53 regulates ERS or microglial activation in NP has not been reported. The findings of this research answered these questions. MG53 might play a potential anti-inflammatory role in NP therapy by regulating neuroinflammation-related pathways. However, due to the lack of more animal or human data on the performance of MG53 in NP therapy, targeting MG53 in NP therapy for humans may remain difficult. This work clearly elucidated the mechanism of PRF relieving NP. PRF repressed the activation of microglia by upregulating MG53-mediated ERS inhibition, resulting in the remission of NP in CCI rats. Furthermore, it will be necessary in the future to identify the signaling pathways that play a role in this mechanism and the roles they play. This will provide help for further elucidating the pathogenesis and exploring drug targets for the treatment of NP.

Conclusion

In conclusion, PRF alleviated NP in CCI rats by upregulating MG53-mediated microglial activation. Moreover, MG53 restrained microglial activation through inhibiting ERS. Therefore, PRF attenuated microglial activation through upregulation of MG53-mediated ERS inhibition, leading to the remission of NP in CCI rats. This study provided a theoretical basis for the clinical treatment of PRF alleviating NP, as well as some new insights for searching drug targets of NP.
