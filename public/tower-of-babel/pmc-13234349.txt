Site-specific wastewater-based surveillance in early detection of COVID-19 new cases and prediction of mass testing outcomes in long-term care facilities
Authors: Jiabi Wen, Ken K Peng, Bonita E Lee, Rhonda J Rosychuk, Tiejun Gao, Judy Y Qiu, Michael Y Li, Eleanor Risling, Lorie A Little, Christopher Sikora, Xiaoli Lilly Pang, Arto Ohinmaa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLong-term care facilities (LTCFs) were disproportionately impacted during the COVID-19 pandemic. Site-specific wastewater-based surveillance (WBS) offers a non-invasive alternative to traditional mass testing by capturing collective viral loads in wastewater. This study assessed the effectiveness of WBS in detecting new COVID-19 cases in nine Edmonton LTCFs from January 2021 to May 2023. We used constrained distributed lag models to identify critical windows when wastewater viral loads were significantly associated with new cases. Using this critical window, we evaluated the predictive accuracy of wastewater samples for mass testing outcomes. Fisher’s exact test and Mann-Whitney U test compared WBS accuracy for predicting resident vs. staff cases and examined whether factors like sample type, quantity, outbreak duration, or collection timing influenced accuracy. Among 2,515 wastewater samples, 909 were positive, alongside 825 COVID-19 cases identified from 18,226 clinical specimens. Before the clinical testing scale-down in 2022, eight of nine facilities had critical windows within three days. Wastewater collected three days in advance predicted 85% of negative and 60% of positive mass testing outcomes. WBS more accurately predicted resident cases than staff cases (74% vs. 33%, p=0.02). Other factors did not significantly affect prediction accuracy. Introduction

Residents of long-term care facilities (LTCFs) are among the most vulnerable populations affected by COVID-19. Globally, pooled infection and case-fatality rates were 32.6% and 22.7%, respectively, more than ten times higher than in the general population (all ages)1. This disproportionate burden reflects the characteristics of LTCF residents, who are typically older, frailer and more medically complex than the broader elderly population2. Congregate living environments and long-standing overcrowding problems further compound the risk by increasing the transmission of various infectious pathogens3. Early identification of cases is therefore critical: undetected transmission can escalate quickly in settings where residents have limited physiological reserve to withstand severe outcomes4.

To protect LTCF residents, jurisdictions worldwide adopted comprehensive prevention strategies, with the most essential measures being widespread surveillance, early detection and response, and strict infection prevention and control protocols such as restricted access and visits, minimized staff rotation, and rapid response for quarantine cases5. Given the high proportion of asymptomatic cases, symptom-based screening alone has proven insufficient. Thus, mass testing of residents and staff regularly was deemed necessary for early diagnoses5, 6. In a retrospective cohort study focusing on LTCFs in the United States, greater surveillance testing among staff members was associated with clinically meaningful reductions in COVID-19 cases and deaths among residents7. However, mass testing presents practical challenges, including a long turnaround time for results, especially when community testing demands are high8, decreased consent to swabbing over time, and a significant resource and financial burden to healthcare systems due to repeated testing9.

Site-specific wastewater-based surveillance (WBS) emerged as a complementary tool for monitoring COVID-19 transmission within individual buildings or facilities, informing the need for investigation and targeted testing10, 11, 12, 13, 14. This approach collects wastewater samples from a facility’s sewer system to measure the collective viral load of the population residing or working at the facility. This application has shown effectiveness in various settings, such as university dormitories10, 14 and correctional centres13. Our prior research15 demonstrated that wastewater signals in LTCFs can function as an early warning system for emerging infections in LTCFs when no outbreak has been identified, facilitating early detection and potentially preventing escalation. However, the utility of WBS extends beyond pre-outbreak surveillance; a critical and underexplored question is whether wastewater signals remain informative during active outbreaks, when the stakes for vulnerable LTCF residents are highest.

Interpreting wastewater viral loads during active outbreaks in LTCFs presents a distinct challenge: unlike other congregate settings where confirmed cases are isolated elsewhere, LTCF residents typically remain on-site, meaning positive wastewater signals may reflect ongoing viral shedding from existing cases rather than new infections. Despite this complexity, no study has systematically evaluated the lead time of WBS signals relative to clinical case detection during active outbreaks or assessed whether wastewater could inform the use of mass testing. Whether WBS could reduce the burden of large-scale clinical testing while maintaining effective surveillance represents an important and unresolved public health question.

Given the vulnerability of the LTCF populations, the resource-intensive and invasive nature of mass testing, and the critical gap in evidence regarding the use of site-specific WBS to guide mass testing during active outbreaks, we leveraged 28-month longitudinal data to address the evidence gap. Specifically, we examined whether wastewater viral signals provided early warning of clinical cases during outbreaks, and assessed their predictive accuracy for mass testing outcomes, thereby minimizing unnecessary testing while maintaining effective surveillance.

Methods

Context

Edmonton is the capital city of the Canadian province of Alberta, with a population of 1.1 million in 2024 and 92 LTCFs. During the pandemic, Alberta mandated daily symptom checks among residents and staff in LTCFs, starting in April 202016. Symptomatic individuals were tested using reverse transcription and polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) on nasopharyngeal swabs, and a confirmed case led to contact tracing, isolation and testing of close contacts. As the pandemic progressed, rapid antigen testing became increasingly available and was incorporated into outbreak protocols in late 2021 for screening. COVID-19 outbreak at LTCF was defined as having one confirmed COVID-19 case17 and later modified to requiring two epidemiologically linked cases, with the interval between cases shortening from 14 to 7 days between September 2021 and July 202218, 19. Mass testing of all residents and staff was required within 72 hours after the start of an outbreak20. Additional rounds of mass testing were at the discretion of outbreak investigators, depending on the situation before an outbreak was declared over (no new COVID-19 cases appeared in 28 days). The public health mandate for mass testing of all individuals was rescinded in February 2022, and the operational guidance on universal testing was removed in May 2022. However, actual practice varied among different facilities. As testing requirements were scaled back, only symptomatic individuals were tested at some sites21.

Between Jan 2021 and May 2023, 12 LTCFs participated in the site-specific WBS program in Edmonton. Three were excluded in Apr 2022 due to shared sewer systems with other non-LTCF operations in the buildings within the same complex at the sampling point; thus, nine facilities were included in the analysis15. Detailed sample collection and analysis methods were described in previous studies15, 22. EPCOR Water Services collected 24-hour composite 500 mL wastewater samples twice weekly from January to September 2021 and three times weekly from October 2021 to May 2023 using ISCO GLS autosamplers, shipping them to the University of Alberta laboratories. When composite sampling was not possible (e.g., due to weather conditions), grab samples were taken. Viruses were concentrated from 100 mL wastewater via ultrafiltration, followed by nucleic acid extraction and duplicate RT-quantitative-PCR for SARS-CoV-2 N1 and N2 detection. Results were reported as genome copies per 100 mL15, 22. Wastewater viral load results were emailed to public health officials within 24 hours of sample collection. A positive wastewater result would prompt a review of the facility’s outbreak status; however, there were limited wastewater-determined outbreak control strategies, such as increasing or reducing testing. More details of the study design were reported in our prior publication15. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Health Research Ethics Board and Environment, Health and Safety Office, University of Alberta (number Pro00106422). Only retrospective, aggregate patient data were analyzed, and the University of Alberta Research Ethics Board reviewed and approved the study, explicitly waiving the requirement for individual informed consent given the anonymous and aggregate nature of the data.

Data

Daily new case data and the number of clinical samples tested daily were provided by the Alberta Health Services communicable disease outbreak management team and the Alberta Provincial Laboratory for Public Health, respectively. Specimen collection dates were used to track daily new cases and testing numbers.

Since wastewater samples were not collected daily, linear interpolation was used to impute wastewater viral signals for days with no sample collection. Under high-frequency measurements, linear interpolation could yield comparable results to more complex interpolation methods23. Wastewater sample collection dates were used to record wastewater viral signals.

Conceptual relationship between wastewater viral loads and clinical cases

Underlying infection refers to all existing SARS-CoV-2 infections in the population, including symptomatic, presymptomatic, and asymptomatic individuals. Presymptomatic and asymptomatic individuals are less likely to be detected clinically; therefore, the underlying infection is an unobservable metric. Wastewater viral loads reflect this underlying infection burden, whereas reported daily new cases emerging from the underlying infection pool depend on the outbreak management and testing strategies. There are two possible mechanisms by which wastewater signals can precede confirmed case counts. The first is a biological lead: infected individuals shed SARS-CoV-2 into the wastewater (e.g., via faeces) before symptom onset or before being clinically tested, allowing wastewater to capture infections prior to their identification. Previous research applied daily longitudinal RT-PCR testing and found viral shedding starting before symptom onset24. The second is a surveillance lead: wastewater signals reflect the overall infection burden within a facility, including presymptomatic and asymptomatic individuals who are less detectable compared to symptomatic individuals. These unidentified individuals continue to transmit the virus, giving rise to new reported cases in subsequent days. The second mechanism is similar to the ability of municipal wastewater in signalling the community COVID-19, where lead time can span from 1-4 weeks25. Exploratory analysis of wastewater lead time

We investigated the temporal relationship between wastewater viral signals and COVID-19 cases through an exploratory analysis. First, we modelled the expected viral shedding contribution from underlying infections at each facility, hereafter referred to as the estimated infection burden. This was constructed by summing the decay-weighted shedding from existing resident cases and the cumulative daily new cases detected up to days after each wastewater sample collection date. This approach relied on the comprehensive testing strategies implemented in LTCFs, especially during early pandemic phases, where facilities attempted to identify all cases as early as possible, making confirmed case counts a reasonable source for approximate the actual infection burden. Existing staff cases were excluded as they would be restricted from working and would therefore stop contributing to the site-based wastewater. For existing resident cases, we applied an exponential decay function based on the time since case confirmation, with decay parameters derived from known faecal shedding patterns26. For new resident and staff cases, the decay factor was assumed to be 1. To account for individual variability in viral shedding duration (21-34 days)26, we conducted 1,000 Monte Carlo simulations, randomly sampling shedding durations for each case (full details in Appendix 1). To identify potential lead time in wastewater signals, we created 21 different time series by progressively including cases detected 0-20 days after each wastewater sample collection date. Next, we calculated Spearman’s rank correlations between these estimated infection burden series and observed wastewater viral loads, treating all facility-date pairs as independent observations in the correlation analysis. If wastewater truly leads to case detection by a given number of days, the correlation between this estimated infection burden and observed wastewater viral loads would be expected to peak at the corresponding accumulation window. This analysis was conducted separately for three distinct periods: Pre-Omicron (before December 2021), Omicron with maintained testing protocol (December 2021 – May 2022), and Reduced testing (June 2022 onward). During the first two phases, the correlation strength would theoretically peak when the accumulation time window corresponds to the true lead time of wastewater signals. During the reduced testing phase, we expected a weaker overall correlation and possibly no clear peak, as clinical case counts no longer reliably approximated the true underlying infection burden.

Constrained distributed lag model confirming the lead time

We conducted regression analyses for each facility to evaluate the relationship between wastewater viral loads and daily new cases. A distributed lag model was applied to capture the influence of both same-day wastewater viral loads and those from previous dates (i.e., the existence of lead time). This method has been previously used to analyze the municipal wastewater data for predicting community COVID-19 burden27, 28. Prior research suggested the effect of the viral load on the real number of active cases had a logarithmic shape29. Therefore, the viral load was introduced with a logarithmic transformation. The number of clinical tests was included as a covariate, as daily new cases were influenced by the number of RT-PCR tests and the test results. Additionally, the number of cases identified “yesterday” was included to address serial correlation in residuals, as detected by the Durbin-Watson test. We combined Pre-Omicron and Omicron with testing-maintained periods into a single period, as Pre-Omicron cases were limited for estimation, and no distinct differences were observed in lead time patterns during the exploratory analysis. Two regression models were fit for each facility. To account for variability in the timing of facility-specific testing reduction, a facility-specific cutoff was applied and determined by the Akaike information criterion (AIC) (Appendix 2). Given the high autocorrelation in interpolated wastewater viral loads, the coefficients were constrained to follow a second-degree polynomial function to mitigate potential multicollinearity issues. This approach is commonly used in analyses involving distributed lag models, with the polynomial lag originating from Almon30. A second-degree polynomial constraint was applied to ensure the lag coefficients exhibit a downward trend toward the end of the lag window, reflecting the expectation that older wastewater signals become progressively less predictive of current case counts. This quadratic form accommodates two plausible patterns: a monotone decline across the entire window, or an initial increase followed by a decrease, the latter capturing a peak lead time within the window.

Given the two possible schemes described in the conceptual relationship by which wastewater could signal the clinical cases, we varied in the regression model from 2 to 30 for different facilities. To determine the optimal that best captures the relationship between wastewater viral loads and clinical cases, we examined whether the trend of values followed the expected downward trend. Additionally, we sought to avoid the spurious effect where at earlier dates became significantly negative, which could artificially inflate the significance of samples collected closer to the specimen collection date and lead to misleading results (Appendix 3).

Once the optimal lead time range was determined, we identified the critical window—the period within this range where wastewater viral loads were statistically significantly associated with clinical cases. This critical window represents the timeframe during which WBS provides plausible early signals of emerging cases.

Predicting mass testing outcomes based on lead time information

Considering the 24-hour turnaround time for wastewater results, we evaluated the accuracy of wastewater results in predicting mass testing outcomes, using wastewater collected during a critical window, while excluding wastewater samples collected on the same date as the clinical specimen collection. Predictions were considered accurate if they met one of the following criteria: (1) no positive wastewater signals within the lead time window and no cases identified through mass testing, or (2) at least one positive wastewater sample within the lead time window and cases identified during mass testing. Inaccurate predictions occurred when the wastewater signal and mass testing outcomes did not align. We evaluated testing scales ranging from 100% down to 10% of a facility’s units. To evaluate factors influencing prediction accuracy, we used Fisher’s exact and Mann-Whitney U tests. The factors considered included: 1) whether the wastewater signal predicted the absence or presence of cases, 2) the source of cases (residents or staff), and 3) various wastewater operational factors: the number of wastewater samples collected within the lead-time window, the duration between wastewater sample and the date of mass testing, the timing of mass testing during an outbreak, the type of wastewater sample (24-hour composite sample or grab sample), and the magnitude of the viral load on predictive accuracy.

Results

Sample characteristics

During the study period from January 2021 to May 2023, a total of 18,226 clinical tests were performed across nine LTCFs. 825 cases were identified, of which 507 were residents. The number of confirmed cases and test-positivity ratio were lowest during the pre-Omicron phase (up to December 2021) (124 cases, 1%), suggesting extensive clinical testing was conducted. Staff cases were primarily detected in the Pre-Omicron phase and Omicron phase when testing was maintained, with 305 out of 318 cases identified. A total of 2,515 wastewater samples were collected, with 909 showing positive SARS-CoV-2 viral load (36%). The proportion of positive samples was 8% up to December 2021, 28% up to June 2022, and 36% of all-time wastewater samples, suggesting an increase in positive samples over the three periods (Table 1).

Exploratory analysis

The association between estimated infection burden and wastewater viral loads exhibited the expected patterns across the three periods. During the Pre-Omicron period and the Omicron period with testing maintained, the association peaked at a lead time of 3 days and 4 days, respectively, with a moderate Spearman’s correlation: 0.420 (interquartile range: 0.415-0.425) and 0.488 (interquartile range: 0.486-0.490), respectively. The overall association was stronger during the Omicron phases, likely due to greater dynamic range in both wastewater viral loads and case counts during this period, which provided more informative variation. During the Pre-Omicron phase, sparser case occurrence limited the correlation estimates, though the peak lead time remained consistent with the Omicron period (Table 1). During the testing-reduced period, as only symptomatic cases were detected which underestimated the burden of infection, the overall association weakened, and no distinct lead-time pattern was observed (Fig. 2).

Distributed lag model

Positive and significant associations between viral loads and clinical cases were detected for all facilities during the testing-maintained periods (Pre-Omicron and Omicron phases). The critical window, where statistically significant leading effects occurred, was within a five-day time relative to clinical specimen collection, with eight of nine facilities occurring within a three-day timeframe. One log10 (base 10 logarithm) increase in wastewater viral loads during the critical window was associated with an expected increase of 0.013 (Site 6, 95% CI: [0.001, 0.025]) to 0.146 (Site 4, 95% CI: [0.079, 0.213]) cases across facilities, while holding other covariates constant. During the testing-reduced phase, not all facilities exhibited a critical window. Site 2, 3, 6, and 14 maintained significant associations within a three-day window, and Site 6 showed statistically significant associations at days 7 and 8. The magnitude of regression coefficients decreased. One log10 increase in wastewater viral loads across the significant wastewater leading days would lead to an expected increase of 0.002 (Site 2, 95% CI: [0.0004, 0.004]) - 0.054 (Site 14, 95% CI: [0.029, 0.078]) cases, across facilities, holding other covariates as constant (Table 2, Fig. 3).

Predicting mass testing outcomes

We identified 198 instances of mass testing (testing at least 10% of a facility’s units). 179 (90%) were conducted during an outbreak, 77 (39%) identified cases, and 27 (14%) tested 100% of a facility’s units. Most mass testing occurred between January and July 2021 (Appendix 4). Wastewater samples were available for 1-3, 1-4, and 1-5 days before clinical specimen collection in 140 (71%), 167 (84%), and 185 (93%) instances, respectively.

Predictive performance differed between wastewater collected 4-5 days in advance and 1–3 days in advance, although the difference did not reach conventional statistical significance (p = 0.09). Different testing scales (ranging from 10% to 100%) were used to classify mass testing. While testing scale did not affect the accuracy of predicting positive mass testing outcomes, prediction of negative outcomes was significantly lower (p = 0.01) at testing scales of 10–19% compared to testing scales of ≥20% (Appendix 5). Accordingly, subsequent analyses focused on testing scale ≥20% and predicted by wastewater collected within 1–3 days prior to testing.

At a testing scale of ≥20%, the prediction accuracy of wastewater collected up to three days in advance was 85% for negative mass testing outcomes and 60% for positive outcomes, respectively. When mass testing yielded cases, prediction accuracy was higher for having resident cases (74%) compared to staff-only outcomes (33%), with the difference being statistically significant (p = 0.02). No statistically significant difference in prediction accuracy was observed between composite and grab samples, although grab samples numerically showed higher accuracy for both positive (48% vs. 33%, p = 0.53) and negative (92% vs. 81%, p = 0.30) mass testing outcomes. Similarly, no statistically significant differences were observed across the number of wastewater samples collected within the lead time window (all p = 1.00 for predicting positive, negative, and combined outcomes), the timing of wastewater collection within the three-day lead time window (p = 1.00, p = 0.45, and p = 0.22 for predicting positive, negative, and combined outcomes, respectively), or the timing of mass testing during an outbreak (p = 0.8). Compared with positive wastewater that correctly predicted positive mass testing outcomes, those did not correspond to positive mass testing outcomes had lower wastewater viral loads (median [interquartile range] 2.2 [1.9-2.3] vs. 2.5 [2.0-3.4]), but the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.31) (Table 3).

Discussion

WBS has emerged as a valuable tool to complement COVID-19 monitoring, both in the communities31 and within specific facilities13. LTCFs house a particularly vulnerable population, yet limited research has explored the benefits of WBS in this context. Our group previously demonstrated that WBS could effectively signal the presence of outbreaks at LTCFs before the outbreak investigation15. Building on this foundation, the current study extends the evidence by evaluating whether the wastewater remain informative during active outbreaks. Specifically, we quantified the lead time across pandemic phases, evaluated the ability of wastewater to predict mass testing outcomes, and assessed operational factors influencing prediction accuracy. These findings further support the use of WBS for early detection and for informing targeted interventions, such as mass testing.

The ability of wastewater as a leading indicator of COVID-19 burden in site-specific contexts is determined by epidemiological, biological, and health system factors32. We articulated two possible mechanisms by which wastewater could precede case detection: a biological lead, whereby wastewater measures viral shedding that occurs before individuals are clinically identified, and a surveillance lead, whereby wastewater reflects the overall infection burden, including undetected cases who contribute to future transmission. Our findings revealed a consistent significant lead time of up to five days across facilities when testing was maintained, with eight of nine facilities showing a lead time of three days. This supports the biological lead as the dominant mechanism during testing-maintained phases. This lead time aligns closely with published estimates of SARS-CoV-2 incubation periods33, which decreased from 5 days for the Alpha strain to 3.4 days for the Omicron strain. Given that outbreak investigations and frequent mass testing took place during the testing-maintained phase, the likelihood of a large pool of hidden infections was minimal, leaving little room for the surveillance lead to operate. During the testing-reduced phase, symptomatic testing likely underestimated true infections, allowing the “surveillance lead” mechanism to play a larger role; thus, one site showed extended lead time. However, reduced clinical ascertainment weakens the observable relationship between wastewater viral loads and reported cases, which likely explains the attenuated correlations and the absence of a detectable lead-time peak in several facilities.

Building on the lead time findings, we further assessed the predictive ability of wastewater on mass testing outcomes. Population-scale testing with strengthened infection prevention and control measures has been recognized as an effective strategy for controlling SARS-CoV-2 transmission, particularly during the early stages of the pandemic34. At the community level, individual testing often falls short of capturing the true epidemiology of COVID-19. WBS has been proposed as a valuable tool to bridge this gap35. In the LTCF setting, where outbreak investigations were conducted in full force in addition to the large-scale clinical testing, the combined outcomes provided a unique opportunity for the evaluation of wastewater predictions. The high accuracy for negative mass testing outcomes (85%) suggests that wastewater could serve as a reliable tool for ruling out active facility-level transmission, potentially reducing the frequency of resource-intensive and invasive mass testing during periods of low viral signal. The more modest accuracy for positive mass testing outcomes (60%) reflects the inherent uncertainty in using a facility-level aggregate signal to predict the need for mass testing. A false negative, wastewater negative, but cases present, carries a higher cost than a false positive, given the vulnerability of LTCF residents. This reinforces not to rely on wastewater as a sole trigger. When decomposing positive mass testing outcomes by population group reveals that wastewater was substantially more accurate in predicting resident cases (74%) than staff cases (33%). This asymmetry is biologically expected, as residents live on-site and contribute continuously to facility wastewater. This suggests that staff surveillance may require complementary approaches, such as rapid antigen testing or symptom screening, which are not contingent on wastewater signal.

We also evaluated a set of key wastewater operational factors influencing the prediction accuracy. First, we examined the sample collection strategy. A 24-hour composite sampling strategy is generally recommended for wastewater monitoring due to its ability to reduce the impact of seasonal and diurnal variability of targets in wastewater, including viral load36. We did not find significant differences in prediction accuracy between composite sampling and grab sampling. The absence of significance may reflect limited statistical power rather than true equivalence. While previous studies have reported consistent viral load measurements across sampling strategies in municipal wastewater contexts37, whether this holds in small-scale facility-level surveillance warrants further investigation. Formal equivalence testing in an adequately powered study would be required before concluding that the two sampling strategies perform similarly. We then examined the timing of mass testing across different stages of the outbreak. In later stages of an active outbreak, wastewater signal may be less informative, as they can be influenced by convalescent viral shedding from recovered individuals38. However, our analysis did not show a statistically significant association between timing within the outbreak stage and wastewater prediction accuracy. Similarly, we explored whether correct predictions of positive mass testing outcomes were associated with higher wastewater viral loads, and no significant differences were observed. The lack of a significant difference may be attributed to variations in viral shedding dynamics—likely influenced by individual shedding levels, time since infection, and environmental factors—which were not explored in this study. These findings highlight the challenge of interpreting LTCF wastewater data and the importance of considering inherent biological variability when designing surveillance and testing protocols.

While site-specific WBS offers significant public health benefits for protecting vulnerable LTCF populations, its implementation in congregate settings should be guided by established ethical principles to promote the common good, equity, respect for persons, and good governance39. Given the typically small size of LTCFs, facility-level surveillance is more likely to generate data that could identify individual residents and staff members, necessitating robust data governance frameworks that ensure confidentiality, limit data use to public health purposes, and involve facilities and residents in surveillance planning. In this study, individual privacy was protected through aggregate facility-level reporting, and data collection was conducted under institutional ethics approval with informed consent waived given the public health nature of the surveillance program.

There are several limitations to this study. Firstly, the determination of whether a facility had cases might be imprecise due to untested infected individuals. We used testing proportions ranging from 100% down to smaller scales as a proxy for the facility’s actual status, which might not truly reflect the absence of cases. However, we still observe 85% of accuracy in predicting the negative mass testing outcomes. Secondly, we did not have access to data on residents’ use of adult briefs or the exact work shifts of individual staff members relative to wastewater collection times. The presence of visitors, especially in the later months of the pandemic, could have influenced wastewater composition, as these individuals also used restrooms at the sites. The lack of information limits us to describing the actual population contributing to the wastewater at the sampling time, affecting an accurate estimate of the COVID-19 burden within the facility. Thirdly, the SARS-CoV-2 viral shedding dynamics vary across strains and individuals40; however, our analysis only stratified the timeframe by testing strategy. During the testing-maintained period, three variants, namely Alpha, Delta, and Omicron, were dominant at different times. Since the Omicron phase had the highest number of cases, the lead time results were likely more influenced by Omicron, and the estimated lead time was indeed more similar to Omicron’s incubation period. Additionally, we assumed similar shedding patterns for all individuals. While this assumption is reasonable for large-scale population studies, such as those focusing on municipal wastewater, our study involved smaller-scale settings, with one or two cases occurring at a site on some dates. This highlights the need for future research to incorporate individual-level modelling to better capture shedding variability and explain the relationship between wastewater and cases under this site-specific WBS system.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated the effectiveness of WBS in signalling new cases in LTCFs, even in the context of comprehensive on-site outbreak investigations and testing. These findings highlight the potential of WBS as a valuable tool for monitoring specific vulnerable populations during future pandemics, especially in the early stages when comprehensive symptom detection and outbreak identification are challenging. Site-specific WBS may help guide the targeted testing in facilities with ongoing transmissions, thereby improving efficiency and conserving public health resources.
