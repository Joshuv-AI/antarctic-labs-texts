Prevalence and occupational risk factors of dry eye symptoms among Chinese healthcare workers
Authors: Yanying Zhang, Jiayu Zhang, Zhigang Sun, Chang Su, Jieying Guan, Jingjing Xie, Shujuan Chen, Su Liu, Haibo Huang, Jingchun Lv, Qinhua Yu, Qian Zhang, Chunzhi Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Healthcare workers (HCWs) in China are at a high risk of self-reported dry eye symptoms due to occupational exposures, yet population-based epidemiological data on this issue among Chinese HCWs remain scarce. This multicenter cross-sectional study aimed to quantify the prevalence of frequent dry eye symptoms, their diagnostic status, and independent occupational risk factors among Chinese HCWs. From September 2024 to December 2025, 269 active HCWs from 5 tertiary hospitals in Guangdong completed anonymous questionnaires covering demographics, occupational/lifestyle factors, and ocular symptoms. Multivariate logistic regression identified independent risk factors for frequent dry eye symptoms (≥3 times/wk). The prevalence of frequent dry eye symptoms among participants (mean age = 38.5 ± 8.2 years, 59.9% female, 74.4% were physicians) was 54.3%, but only 6.3% had a formal clinical diagnosis. Daily screen time of 7 hours or more emerged as the strongest modifiable risk factor (odds ratio [OR] = 2.31, P = .005). Other independent risk factors included perceived dry work environment (OR = 1.98), frequent night work (OR = 2.15), and myopia (OR = 1.89; all P < .01). A striking gap between knowledge, belief, and behavior was observed: 92.2% recognized screen-use risks, yet only 2.6% performed regular eye exercises. Chinese HCWs face a high burden of frequent dry eye symptoms, which remain severely underdiagnosed. The identified screen-time threshold (≥7 hours/d) is lower than conventional occupational health recommendations and should be considered a modifiable target for intervention. Importantly, current occupational health checkups for HCWs lack ocular surface screening, representing a systemic gap. Given that data were collected in the post-pandemic era (2024–2025), our findings likely reflect the new normal of digitally intensive healthcare work. Workplace-oriented interventions – such as incorporating symptom screening into annual exams, electronic medical record rest reminders, and eye care training – are urgently needed to bridge the awareness-behavior gap and protect HCWs’ ocular health. Our findings are based on self-reported symptoms rather than clinical diagnosis, highlighting the need for objective screening in routine occupational health checkups.

1. Introduction

Dry eye syndrome (DES) is a multifactorial ocular surface disorder characterized by tear film instability and ocular surface neurosensory abnormalities, affecting a substantial global population with prevalence rates ranging from 10% to 50%.[1,2]

With population aging, widespread use of electronic devices, and environmental changes, the disease burden has progressively increased. It not only causes ocular discomfort and fluctuating vision but also severely impacts patients’ quality of life and work efficiency, becoming a significant public health issue.[3–5]

Among various occupational groups, healthcare workers (HCWs) are considered a high-risk population for occupational DES due to their unique work environment and behavioral patterns.

Their occupational exposure is multifaceted. First, to complete medical record documentation, image interpretation, and information retrieval, HCWs typically require prolonged staring at video display terminals, which significantly reduces blink frequency and accelerates tear film evaporation.[6,7] Second, hospital environments commonly employ air conditioning to control infections and temperature, which paradoxically reduces workplace humidity, exacerbating tear evaporation.[8–10] In addition, circadian rhythm disruption from shift work, altered local microenvironments due to prolonged wearing of protective gear (such as masks and goggles),[11] and high-intensity work stress may further impair lacrimal gland secretion and ocular surface health.[12,13] International studies indicate that symptom-based DES prevalence among HCWs can reach up to 62%, correlating with diminished work performance.[14,15]

Although a preliminary consensus exists regarding the high risk faced by HCWs, significant gaps remain in the available evidence. Most studies focus on describing symptom burden, lacking detailed epidemiological data on the association between specific, quantifiable occupational exposure factors (such as graded screen time and subjective environmental dryness) and DES risk. Particularly in China, large-scale, multicenter investigations on DES among HCWs remain scarce. Concurrently, there is a lack of systematic assessment regarding the current diagnostic status, treatment patterns, and the gap between risk awareness and preventive behaviors for DES within this high-risk population. This information deficit hinders the development of targeted, evidence-based workplace health intervention strategies.

Therefore, this multicenter cross-sectional study aims to: quantify the prevalence of dry eye symptoms and diagnostic gaps among Chinese HCWs; and quantitatively analyze the independent association between definable occupational exposures and frequent dry eye symptoms to identify key intervention targets, thereby providing evidence for advancing precise occupational eye health protection measures.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design and setting

This multicenter cross-sectional survey was conducted from September 1, 2024, to December 1, 2025, at 5 tertiary general hospitals in Guangdong Province, China. Subjects included active medical staff across clinical departments, comprising physicians, nurses, and medical technologists. Participants were randomly selected from departmental duty rosters using a computer-generated random number sequence applied to the complete rosters within each hospital. This approach was implemented consistently across all 5 sites to minimize selection bias. This study was reviewed and approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Guangzhou Health Science College (approval number: GZWS202402). All participants provided informed consent prior to enrollment.

2.2. Study size

The sample size was determined a priori using the standard formula for cross-sectional studies: n = Z2 × P × (1 − P)/d2, where Z = 1.96 for 95% confidence, P is the expected prevalence of frequent dry eye symptoms, and d is the margin of error.[16] As there were no prior data on dry eye symptom prevalence specifically among Chinese HCWs, an expected prevalence “P” of 50% was conservatively assumed to maximize the sample size. With an allowable error “d” of 6% (recommended when P ranges between 10% and 90%), the minimum required sample size was calculated to be approximately 267 participants. To account for a potential 10% nonresponse rate, the target sample was set at 294 participants.

2.3. Participants

A total of 300 HCWs were initially approached from the 5 hospitals. Inclusion criteria were age ≥18 years and currently employed. Exclusion criteria were: history of major ocular surgery (e.g., corneal transplantation, cataract surgery) or active ocular disease (excluding DES); presence of systemic autoimmune disease (excluding documented Sjögren syndrome). Applying these criteria, 15 individuals were excluded (major ocular surgery, n = 8; systemic autoimmune disease, n = 7). Of the remaining 285 eligible participants, 16 declined to participate or did not return the questionnaire, yielding a final sample of 269 participants (response rate 94.4%). The flow of participants through the study is described in the text above.

2.4. Data sources and measurement

The questionnaire was developed based on a comprehensive literature review and consultation with ophthalmology experts. A pilot test was conducted with 20 HCWs who were not included in the final study sample to evaluate the clarity and comprehensibility of the questionnaire items. Based on pilot feedback, minor wording adjustments were made to improve item clarity. The internal consistency of the symptom-related items was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, which yielded a value of 0.82, indicating acceptable reliability.

The self-administered structured questionnaire was distributed via an electronic survey platform and included the following dimensions.

Demographic and occupational characteristics

Age, gender, occupational category, department affiliation, and years of service.

Ocular symptom assessment

Referencing the Diagnostic and Evaluation Working Group criteria for DES from the Tear Film and Ocular Surface Society,[2] participants were asked about the frequency (none/occasional/frequent) and subjective severity (tolerable/intolerable) of 4 common dry eye symptoms (dryness, itching, fatigue, and foreign body sensation) over the past 3 months. “Frequent dry eye symptoms” was defined as experiencing any symptom ≥3 times weekly as the primary outcome variable. We also inquired about symptom burden using a format analogous to the Ocular Surface Disease Index (OSDI) questionnaire approach.[17]

Occupational and environmental exposure assessment

Screen time was assessed as average daily work-related screen usage (≤3, 4–6, 7–9, ≥10 hours). Perceived environmental humidity was assessed using a 5-point Likert scale (from “very dry” to “very humid”). Other exposures included air conditioning usage and exposure to intense light.

Lifestyle and eye care practices

Frequency of night shifts (overnight work), regularity of eye exercises or warm compresses, and daily water intake.

Ocular history and treatment

Refractive status, presence of DES confirmed by a physician, prior ophthalmic visits due to dry eye symptoms, and types of eye drops used (e.g., artificial tears, fluorometholone, cyclosporine A).

Risk awareness and impact

Understanding of common risk factors for DES, preferred preventive measures, and the impact of subjective symptoms on work and daily life.

2.5. Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS statistical software (version 31.0; IBM Corp.). Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies (percentages). Between-group comparisons were conducted using the chi-square test. To identify independent factors associated with frequent dry eye symptoms, univariate analysis was first performed. Variables with P < .10 from the univariate analysis, along with clinically significant variables such as age and gender, were then entered into the final multivariate logistic regression model. Results were presented as adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and their 95% confidence intervals. A P-value < .05 was considered statistically significant. All responses were mandatory in the electronic questionnaire, resulting in complete data with no missing values.

To assess the performance of the final multivariate logistic regression model, multicollinearity was evaluated using variance inflation factors. All variance inflation factor values were below 10, indicating no significant multicollinearity among the independent variables. Model calibration was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, which yielded a P value of .43, indicating acceptable model fit.

2.6. Bias

To reduce recall bias, the questionnaire used structured, closed-ended questions and was completed in the presence of a physician. Selection bias was minimized by randomly selecting participants from departmental duty rosters. Multivariate logistic regression was used to adjust for potential confounders. Subgroup analyses were not performed due to the limited sample size (n = 269), which was only marginally above the minimum requirement. No sensitivity analyses were conducted.

3. Results

The mean age of the participants was 38.5 ± 8.2 years, with the highest proportion (77.3%) falling within the 31 to 50 age group. Females accounted for 59.9%, while males constituted 40.1%. The predominant occupation was physician (74.4%), followed by nurse (13.0%). Departmental distribution showed a higher proportion in internal medicine (37.0%) and traditional Chinese medicine (14.5%). 3.2. Ocular symptoms, diagnosis, and medical consultation

Ocular discomfort symptoms were prevalent, with results presented in Table 2. The prevalence of occasional symptoms such as dry eyes, eye fatigue, itchy eyes, and foreign body sensation was 80.3%, 91.4%, 73.6%, and 71.7%, respectively. Most participants rated their symptoms as “tolerable.” Despite the high symptom burden, only 6.3% (17/269) reported a physician-made diagnosis of DES, and only 14.1% had visited an ophthalmologist for dry eye symptoms. Overall, 54.3% of participants experienced at least 1 dry eye-related symptom in the past 3 months.

3.3. Occupational exposure, lifestyle, and current treatment status

Overall, 60.1% of participants spent 4 to 9 hours per day working on screens, with 30.5% exceeding 7 hours. The vast majority (88.1%) worked in air-conditioned environments, and 43.5% perceived their workplace as “dry” or “very dry.” Regarding lifestyle habits, 48.7% of participants reported frequently staying up late (working night shifts). Adherence to eye care practices was poor, with only 2.6% regularly performing eye exercises. As for current treatment status, 39.8% of participants had used eye drops to alleviate symptoms. The most commonly used medications included fluorometholone (used by 23.1% of users) and Pearl Brightening Eye Drops (18.9%). The usage rate of cyclosporine A was relatively low (4.8%), but its self-reported improvement rate (80%) was slightly higher than that of artificial tears (75%).

3.4. Perception of risk

Awareness of key risk factors was relatively high: 92.2% of participants identified prolonged screen use as a risk factor, while 78.4% recognized the impact of air-conditioned environments.

3.5. Multivariate logistic regression analysis of dry eye symptoms

As shown in Table 3, after adjustment for age, sex, and occupational category, daily screen time ≥7 hours, perceived dryness of the work environment, frequent night work, and myopia were independently associated with frequent dry eye symptoms (all P < .01). Female gender showed a trend toward higher risk but did not reach statistical significance (adjusted OR = 1.58, 95% confidence interval: 0.96–2.61, P = .07).

4. Discussion

This study reveals a striking occupational health paradox among Chinese HCWs: while 54.3% suffer from frequent dry eye symptoms – a burden comparable to that reported in high‑risk industrial workers[7,8] – the formal diagnosis rate is a mere 6.3%. This “symptom‑diagnosis gap” is not merely a clinical oversight; it signals a systemic failure in occupational health surveillance. Three main findings emerge from our multicenter cross‑sectional survey. First, the prevalence of frequent dry eye symptoms among HCWs is alarmingly high, yet the vast majority remain undiagnosed and untreated. Second, we quantified for the first time in a Chinese HCW population the independent association of daily screen time ≥7 hours (OR = 2.31), perceived dry work environment (OR = 1.98), frequent night shifts (OR = 2.15), and myopia (OR = 1.89) with frequent dry eye symptoms. Third, we identified a striking “high awareness – low action” gap: 92.2% recognized screen‑use risks, but only 2.6% performed regular eye exercises. Together, these findings underscore an urgent need to reframe occupational dry eye symptoms as a preventable and manageable condition, rather than an inevitable consequence of medical work. It is important to emphasize that our prevalence estimates reflect self-reported symptoms, not clinically confirmed dry eye disease. Throughout this discussion, we use “dry eye symptoms’’ to refer to our measured outcome and reserve “dry eye syndrome” for the clinically diagnosed condition.

Why, then, are so many symptomatic HCWs systematically undiagnosed? Three intertwined reasons emerge. First, occupational “tolerance”: HCWs often attribute ocular discomfort to work fatigue rather than disease, leading to low help‑seeking behavior. In our sample, only 14.1% had ever visited an ophthalmologist for dry eye symptoms. This mirrors the phenomenon of “presenteeism” in healthcare, where workers normalize physical complaints as part of the job.[12] Second, a blind spot in occupational health examinations: standard health checkups for Chinese HCWs include visual acuity but omit validated dry eye screening tools such as the OSDI or tear film breakup time (TBUT).[6] Consequently, even when HCWs report symptoms, no systematic mechanism captures them. Third, the subjective‑objective disconnect: dry eye symptoms often correlate poorly with clinical signs[8,18]; some individuals with severe symptoms show minimal signs, and vice versa. This complicates early identification, especially in the absence of routine screening. Ironically, those who diagnose others’ diseases are themselves systematically undiagnosed for their own occupational illness. Breaking this cycle requires integrating DES screening into annual HCW health checkups – a low‑cost, high‑impact policy change.

Beyond the diagnostic gap, which specific occupational exposures drive these symptoms, and how do they damage the ocular surface? Rather than reiterating all ORs, we focus on the integrated framework illustrated in Figure 1. The left panel summarizes the modifiable risk factors ranked by effect strength; the middle panel shows the core pathophysiological pathways (tear film instability, evaporative dry eye, and ocular surface inflammation); and the right panel outlines a 3‑tier prevention strategy targeting these pathways. The 3 strongest modifiable risk factors act through these routes. Daily screen time ≥7 hours reduces blink rate from the normal 15–20 to 5–7 blinks per minute, with a marked increase in incomplete blinks. This prevents uniform spreading of meibomian gland lipids, disrupting tear film stability.[19] Notably, the threshold we identified (≥7 hours/d) is associated with increased symptom risk. This finding should be interpreted as an association that warrants further investigation, rather than a definitive causal intervention target. Perceived dry work environment (OR = 1.98) accelerates tear evaporation. Although hospitals maintain low humidity (30%–40%) for infection control, our data show that subjective dryness itself is an independent risk signal. Even when objective humidity cannot be easily raised, individual protective measures (e.g., humidifying goggles, desktop humidifiers) may help. Frequent night shifts (OR = 2.15) disrupt circadian rhythms, leading to abnormal cortisol and reduced melatonin secretion, which impair lacrimal gland function and activate inflammatory pathways.[14,20] Sleep disturbance, often comorbid with night work, independently shortens tear film break‑up time.[21] Myopia (OR = 1.89) and female sex (trend, OR = 1.58) add further risk, a pattern consistent with findings from other Asian populations,[22] but the 3 factors above offer the most immediate targets for prevention. In addition, individuals with myopia may have longer daily screen time, further compounding the risk.[23]

Yet awareness of these risks does not automatically translate into protective behavior. The contradiction between 92.2% awareness of screen risks and 2.6% regular eye exercises is not a failure of knowledge – it is a failure of behavioral design. In high‑pressure medical environments, individual willpower is a scarce resource. Traditional health education (lectures, posters) relies on this willpower and is therefore ineffective. To overcome this, we propose shifting to “nudge” strategies that embed protective behaviors into existing workflows, drawing on behavioral economics.[24] Three concrete examples: technical reinforcement (mandatory “20‑20‑20” reminders that pop up in the electronic medical record system every 20 minutes, turning a desired behavior into a default interruption); environmental reinforcement (placing artificial tear dispensers at nurse stations and physician offices as a “default option”); and management reinforcement (integrating micro‑breaks for blinking exercises into departmental quality metrics). These low‑cost, scalable interventions directly address the “high awareness, low action” dilemma and should be tested in pragmatic workplace trials as proposed strategies rather than evidence directly supported by the current study. These strategies align with the secondary prevention (nudge) and tertiary prevention (stepwise treatment) components of the framework in Figure 1 (right panel).

Several limitations warrant consideration before translating these findings into practice. First, the cross‑sectional design precludes causal inference. The observed associations may be subject to reverse causality – for example, HCWs with preexisting dry eye symptoms might be more sensitive to perceived dryness, biasing the estimate. Second, unmeasured confounding is likely. We did not assess anxiety or depression, which are prevalent among HCWs and known to both exacerbate and be exacerbated by dry eye.[25] Third, although multicenter, all 5 hospitals were in Guangdong Province, and our sample had limited representation from high‑risk units such as operating rooms. Moreover, the sample size (n = 269) was only marginally above the minimum requirement, which precluded more detailed subgroup analyses (e.g., examining risk differences across departments). Thus, generalizability to other regions or different healthcare systems is uncertain. Fourth, our outcome was “symptomatic dry eye” based on self‑report, not clinical confirmation (e.g., TBUT, meibomian gland imaging). Subjective symptoms and objective signs often correlate poorly, so our prevalence estimates may not align with clinical diagnostic criteria. Fifth, we relied on subjective perception of workplace humidity rather than objective measurements, which may introduce recall bias. Sixth, all exposure variables and outcomes were self-reported, making the study susceptible to recall and information bias. Finally, the study population was predominantly composed of physicians (74.4%), which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other HCW groups such as nurses and technicians.

Future research should address these gaps. Priorities include conducting prospective cohort studies to establish temporality and causality; incorporating objective ocular surface tests to validate symptom‑based findings; designing and evaluating multicomponent nudge interventions in real‑world hospital settings; and expanding sampling to diverse geographic regions and departments to improve generalizability.

5. Conclusions and implications

In conclusion, China’s HCWs bear a high burden of dry eye symptoms, yet the condition remains significantly underdiagnosed. Key modifiable occupational factors associated with frequent dry eye symptoms include daily screen time ≥7 hours, dry work environments, frequent night shifts, and myopia. In response to the core risk factors and pathological mechanisms identified in Figure 1, we propose the following multilevel recommendations to achieve comprehensive management spanning from “etiological exposure” to “clinical outcomes”:

At the policy level (corresponding to Fig. 1: secondary prevention – screening and education): dry eye screening should be considered for incorporation into the annual occupational health checkups of HCWs, using the OSDI questionnaire combined with subjective and objective assessment of TBUT, and focused monitoring could be carried out in high-risk departments (internal medicine and operating rooms).

Protecting the ocular health of HCWs is essential to safeguarding the core productivity of the medical system. We advocate elevating the management of occupational DES to a strategic level of equal importance as the prevention of needlestick injuries and protection against musculoskeletal disorders. Through multidisciplinary collaboration, we aim to establish a closed-loop management system encompassing “screening-intervention-follow-up,” thereby providing dual safeguards for the physical and mental well-being of healthcare professionals and patient safety.
