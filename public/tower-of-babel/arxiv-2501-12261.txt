A Framework for the Design of Efficient Diversification Algorithms for Packing and Covering Problems
Waldo Gálvez, Mayank Goswami, Arturo Merino, GiBeom Park, Meng-Tsung Tsai, Victor Verdugo

Abstract
There has been considerable recent interest in computing a diverse collection of solutions to a given optimization problem. Given a classical optimization problem Π \Pi (e.g., minimum spanning tree, maximum independent set) with input size n n and an integer k ≥ 1 k\geq 1 , the goal is to generate a collection of k k maximally diverse solutions to Π \Pi . This diverse-X paradigm not only allows the user to generate very different solutions, but also helps make systems more secure and robust by handling uncertainty and achieving energy efficiency. For several problems Π \Pi solvable in polynomial time, there are efficient poly ​ ( n , k ) \text{poly}(n,k) -time exact or approximation algorithms available for their diverse-solution variants. In contrast, only FPT algorithms are known for NP-complete problems such as vertex covers and independent sets. In the worst case, these algorithms run in time exp ⁡ ( ( k ​ n ) a ) \exp((kn)^{a}) for some a > 0 a>0 . In this work, we address this gap and give a general framework for poly ​ ( n , k ) \text{poly}(n,k) -time bi-criteria PTAS for the diverse variants of several NP-hard problems such as Knapsack , Maximum Weight Independent Set (MWIS) and Minimum Weight Vertex Cover (MWVC) in planar graphs. More precisely, given a maximization (or, minimization) problem Π \Pi , an integer k ≥ 1 k\geq 1 , user-specified quality threshold c ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) c\in(0,1) , and user-controlled error parameters δ , ε ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) \delta,\varepsilon\in(0,1) , our bi-approximation algorithm returns a collection of k k solutions S 1 , S 2 , … , S k S_{1},S_{2},\ldots,S_{k} that are ( 1 − δ ) ​ c (1-\delta)c -optimal and whose diversity is at least a ( 1 − ε ) (1-\varepsilon) -fraction of the maximum diversity achievable by any collection of k k c c -optimal solutions. To complement our positive results, we prove that the diverse-solution variants of Knapsack and MWIS mentioned above do not admit standard single-criterion PTASs. In particular, we show that under the assumption P ≠ NP {\mathrm{P}\neq\mathrm{NP}} , both c c , the solution-quality threshold, and the diversity guarantee must be approximated to achieve a polynomial time algorithm. Moreover, even under such a simultaneous relaxation, none of the two problems admits a bi-criteria FPTAS.

1. There are

Therefore, in order for the diverse and nice optimization to be well-defined, we allow a multiset of solutions.

Related Work and Challenges

Despite considerable research, there are
no polynomial-time algorithms
with approximation guarantees known for the diverse variants of
any NP-hard optimization problem
.
All existing results either give polynomial (in
n
and
k
) time approximations for problems solvable in polynomial time, or FPT algorithms (that are exponential in
n
in the worst case) for NP-hard problems. We highlight some relevant ones below.

For knapsack, the concept of diversity has been explored
within a solution
in
[
25
]
, where items have colors, and one wants to pack solutions satisfying certain diversity constraints on the color distribution of the items in the solution. However, we have not seen any work on obtaining diverse collections of knapsack solutions, nor for the geometric knapsack versions.

Finding diverse MWISs and MWVCs has, on the other hand, received considerable attention with several FPT results. This line of work focuses on
c=1
, i.e., algorithms that return
optimal
solutions and maximize the diversity
exactly
. While it is clear that such algorithms cannot be polynomial in
n
and
k
for NP-complete problems like MWISs in (planar) graphs
[
27
]
, even the problem such as finding a diverse pair of maximum matchings is hard
[
46
]
. Thus the work in this area focuses on fixed-parameter-tractable algorithms that avoid an exponential dependence on the input size
n
; see, e.g.,
[
7
,
6
,
21
,
22
,
23
,
39
,
45
,
42
,
4
,
19
]
.

The works on the
Diverse Vertex Cover
problem are the most relevant to us. The algorithm in
[
7
]
runs in time
2^{k\psi}n^{O(1)}
where
\psi
denotes the
size of each solution
(e.g., the size of a minimum vertex cover or a maximum independent set), and the algorithm in
[
6
]
runs in time
2^{\omega k}\psi^{O(k)}n^{O(1)}
, where
\omega
represents the

of the input graph.

While the above
2^{k\psi}n^{O(1)}
and
2^{\omega k}\psi^{O(k)}n^{O(1)}
results are impressive and important in the FPT context, in our setting there is a limitation: planar graphs can have large treewidth and large independent sets or vertex covers, i.e.,
\psi
or
\omega
could be
n^{\Omega(1)}
. Even if
k=2
, this translates to a runtime of
2^{n^{\Omega(1)}}
, which could be prohibitive for many applications.

Recalling our motivating example of diverse dominating sets in unit-disk graphs,

shows that the size of a minimum dominating set in a sensor network deployed on a

In addition to scalability issues, since these algorithms cannot take

Two related frameworks for diverse solutions to problems solvable in polynomial time were presented in
[
29
,
26

.
Hanaka et al.’s framework
[
29
]
only allows to compute diverse solutions satisfying a cardinality constraint (i.e.,

cannot be input by the user) but guarantees
\max\{\frac{1}{2},\frac{k-1}{k+1}\}
of optimal diversity and PTASs for some problems.
Gao et al.’s approach allows the user to specify a

Note that not only do we want the best of both worlds (
c
as input, and
\max\{\frac{1}{2},\frac{k-1}{k+1}\}
as a diversity factor), but, more importantly, we also want our framework to apply to NP-hard problems.

Is diversification possible without exploring the full space of solutions?

There is a good reason for the lack of polynomial time apx. algorithms for diversification of NP-hard problems. Since such an algorithm has to return
k
many
c
-optimal solutions, an obvious foundation is some existing apx. algorithm for obtaining
one
solution. Traditionally, such algorithms have worked by simplifying the input or ignoring parts of it, while still allowing for good approximation guarantees. Baker’s strategy on planar graphs (See
Section

6
for details), or other “thinning” or “sparsifying” procedures are standard in such algorithms. Therefore, existing algorithms for finding one solution restrict the solutions to have a simpler, or more tractable form.

However, simplifying or ignoring parts of the input can have a drastic effect on diversity. This means that existing algorithms for finding one approximate solution cannot be used directly, because the simpler, or more tractable subspace of solutions may not have enough diversity compared to the unrestricted optimal set of
k
solutions.

In this paper, we give the first bi-criteria PTASs (Polynomial Time Approximation Schemes) for several NP-hard problems such as
Knapsack
,
MWIS
and
MWVC
in planar graphs.
In the process of developing these approximation algorithms, we develop a general framework, that we hope can be applied to other problems of interest in the future.

3.1
Our Algorithm

For brevity, we henceforth write
A-a+b\coloneqq(A-\{a\})\cup\{b\}
for any multiset
A
, where
A-\{a\}
removes one occurrence of
a
and

One of the main goals of our algorithm is to efficiently implement the local search algorithm by Cevallos et al.
[
11
]
.
Given a search space
{\mathcal{X}}
, this local search algorithm starts with an arbitrary subset
X=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\}\subseteq{\mathcal{X}}
of
k
elements, and then finds a pair of elements
x_{\text{in}}\in X
and
x_{\text{out}}\in{\mathcal{X}}-X
such that exchanging these two maximizes the diversity in
X
, i.e.,
(x_{\text{in}},x_{\text{out}})=\argmax_{(x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})}\sum_{x_{i},x_{j}\in(X-x^{\prime}+x^{\prime\prime})}|x_{i}\bigtriangleup x_{j}|
, where
x^{\prime}\in X
and
x^{\prime\prime}\in{\mathcal{X}}-X
.
Indeed, they prove that
O(k\log k)
swaps are enough to obtain
k
solutions with diversity at least
\max\{\frac{1}{2},\frac{k-1}{k+1}\}
-fraction of maximum diversity.

Note, however, that this algorithm might run in time
\exp(n)
if the search space is implicitly given (e.g., spanning tree).
Hanaka et al. (2023) showed a strategy to overcome the aforementioned issue. Their approach is the following.
Let
{\mathcal{F}}
be the family of all feasible solutions. Given
{\mathcal{S}}=\{S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}\}\subseteq{\mathcal{F}}
, define an objective function
r(\cdot)
as

r_{{\mathcal{S}}-S_{\text{in}}}(e)=\sum_{S\in{{\mathcal{S}}-S_{\text{in}}}}(\mathbf{1}(e\not\in S)-\mathbf{1}(e\in S)),

(1)

and
r(S)=\sum_{e\in S}r(e)
.
Intuitively, a feasible subset
S
with a large value of

tends to contain elements underrepresented in
{\mathcal{S}}
.
Then, they argue that finding

and

that maximize the diversity of
{\mathcal{S}}-S_{\text{in}}+S_{\text{out}}
is equivalent to finding

{\mathcal{S}}-S_{\text{in}}
, i.e.,

S_{\text{out}}=\argmax_{S\in{\mathcal{F}}}\sum_{S^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{S}}-S_{\text{in}}}|S\bigtriangleup S^{\prime}|.

(2)

In other words, the diverse optimization problem can be approximated via maximization problem. Therefore, with correctly designed farthest insertion algorithm, by making at most
k
guesses for
S_{\text{in}}
we may compute the best swapping pair
(S_{\text{in}},S_{\text{out}})
.
Since

by executing farthest insertion

times, we may compute a collection of solutions with diversity at least
\max\{\frac{1}{2},\frac{k-1}{k+1}\}
of maximum.

We note here that the approach of Cevallos et al. (2019) still holds when duplicate solutions are allowed. Their local search algorithm relies on the fact that the collection of all subsets of
{\mathcal{X}}
with size at most
k
forms a matroid. One can duplicate every element in
{\mathcal{X}}

k
times, and the same argument holds for the collection

with the duplicates.

Multiple-choice Multidimensional Knapsack

[

In MMK, each item has several incarnations, each represented by a multidimensional weight vector, and at most one incarnation may be selected per item.
The goal is to maximize the total profit subject to a capacity constraint in each dimension.
For a fixed number of dimensions, MMK admits a PTAS.
Roughly speaking, the PTAS enumerates all possible choices of high-profit incarnations and, for each choice, formulates the residual problem as an integer program (IP), solves its linear programming (LP) relaxation, and rounds all fractional variables down.
Since MMK contains only packing constraints, this rounding preserves feasibility; the algorithm then returns the most profitable rounded solution over all guesses.

Our setting is not exactly MMK, since
Diverse
c
-Knapsack
has both packing and covering constraints.
In particular, rounding fractional variables down may violate the profit-covering constraints.
We address this issue by guessing a bounded number of high-profit incarnations and capping the profits of all remaining items.
For a fixed solution
S_{t}
, if an optimal solution contains at least

\lceil 4k/\delta\rceil
incarnations with profit at least
\frac{c\delta\widehat{P}}{4k}
, then the guessed incarnations alone satisfy the profit requirement.
Otherwise, all such incarnations can be guessed, and capping does not change the profit contribution of the residual optimal solution.

.

Let
\{w_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
and
\{p_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be positive weights and profits of
n
items in a classical knapsack problem with capacity
W>0
.
Given integers
k\geq 1
,
d_{min}\geq 0
and
P>0
, there is an algorithm that runs in time
O\Bigl((n+1)(d_{min}+1)^{\binom{k}{2}}(W+1)^{k}(P+1)^{k}2^{k}k^{2}\Bigr)
and returns
k
feasible solutions
S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}
that maximize
\sum_{i\neq j}|S_{i}\Delta S_{j}|
subject to
|S_{i}\Delta S_{j}|\geq d_{min}
for all
1\leq i<j\leq k
and
p(S_{i})\geq P
for every
i\in[k]
, if they exist. Otherwise, the algorithm reports an error without affecting the overall running time.

Proof.

Our algorithm uses a dynamic programming approach.
At each step (for a given item), it enumerates all possible
2^{k}
assignments of including or excluding that item in each of the
k
solutions.
At each step, it keeps track of (i) how many items we have considered so far, (ii) how much
distance
for each pair of solutions is still required, (iii) how much
capacity
remains for each of the
k
knapsacks, and (iv) how much
profit
is still required for each of the
k
solutions.
We then show how to compute the dynamic programming table (DP) within the desired time bound and reconstruct the actual
k
solutions.
Define

\text{DP}\Bigl[h,\;d_{1,2},\ldots,d_{i,j},\ldots,d_{k-1,k},\;W^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,W^{\prime}_{m},\ldots,W^{\prime}_{k},\;P^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,P^{\prime}_{m},\ldots,P^{\prime}_{k}\Bigr]

(16)

to be the maximum sum of pairwise distances of any
k
partial solutions
S^{\prime}_{1},\dots,S^{\prime}_{m},\ldots,S^{\prime}_{k}
such that for every
m\in[k]
and for all
1\leq i<j\leq k
:

S^{\prime}_{m}\subseteq\{1,2,\dots,h\},\quad\lvert S^{\prime}_{i}\,\Delta\,S^{\prime}_{j}\rvert\,\geq\,d^{\prime}_{i,j},\quad w(S^{\prime}_{m})\,\leq\,W^{\prime}_{m},\quad\text{and}\quad p(S^{\prime}_{m})\,\geq\,P^{\prime}_{m}.

(17)

For brevity, let us use
\{d^{\prime}_{i<j}\}_{i,j\in[k]}
for
d^{\prime}_{1,2},\ldots,d_{i,j},\ldots,d^{\prime}_{k-1,k}
, and similarly for
\{W^{\prime}_{m}\}_{m=1}^{k}
and
\{P^{\prime}_{m}\}_{m=1}^{k}
.
Then, among all possible
2^{k}
possibilities of assigning the current item to none, some, or all of the
k
partial solutions, the maximum total of pairwise distances of the
k
partial solutions can be obtained as follows:

\begin{split}\text{DP}\Bigl[h,\;&\{d^{\prime}_{i<j}\}_{i,j\in[k]},\;\{W^{\prime}_{m}\}_{m=1}^{k},\;\{P^{\prime}_{m}\}_{m=1}^{k}\Bigr]\\
&=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\{0,1\}^{k}\\
W^{\prime}_{m}\geq w_{h}\cdot x_{h}\;\forall m\in[k]\end{subarray}}\text{DP}\Bigl[h-1,\;\{d^{\prime\prime}_{i<j}\}_{i,j\in[k]},\;\{W^{\prime\prime}_{m}\}_{m=1}^{k},\;\{P^{\prime\prime}_{m}\}_{m=1}^{k}\Bigr]+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbf{1}(x_{i}\neq x_{j}),\end{split}

where
x_{i}
represents the
i
-th bit of
x
,
d^{\prime\prime}_{i,j}=\max\{0,d^{\prime}_{i,j}-\mathbf{1}(x_{i}\neq x_{j})\}
,
W^{\prime\prime}_{m}=W^{\prime}_{m}-(w_{h}\cdot x_{h})
and
P^{\prime\prime}_{m}=\max\{0,P^{\prime}_{m}-(p_{h}\cdot x_{h})\}
.
Here, the distance and the profit are “clamped” to zero once the needed distance or the profit has been achieved, meaning that from the next item onward, the program no longer tracks whether the distance or the profit has gone beyond the requirement—it is simply recorded as fully satisfied.
The constraint
W^{\prime}_{m}\geq w_{h}\cdot x_{m}
for all
m\in[k]
ensures that we do not consider assignments that would exceed the weight capacity in any of the
k
solutions. Such a case would result in a weight-infeasible assignment, where including the
h
-th item in the
m
-th solution causes its total weight to exceed the remaining capacity
W^{\prime}_{m}
.

Also,
Equation

16
requires some error and base cases to be specified.
When
h=0
, if
P^{\prime}m>0
for some
m
, or if
d^{\prime}_{i,j}>0
for some
i<j
, we define the DP value in
Equation

16
to be
-\infty
, to indicate a profit-infeasible or diversity-infeasible assignment,
and we let the DP value to be zero when
h=0
and
P^{\prime}m=d^{\prime}{i,j}=0
for all
m
and
i<j
.

Note that the desired total of pairwise distances is then stored properly in the cell

\text{DP}[n,\{d_{min}\}_{1\leq i<j\leq k},\{W\}_{m=1}^{k},\{P\}_{m=1}^{k}].

(18)

Since each required pairwise distance
d^{\prime}_{i,j}
can range from
0
to
d_{min}
, each remaining capacity
W^{\prime}_{m}
from
0
to
W
and similarly for
P^{\prime}_{m}
, there are at most

(d_{min}+1)^{\binom{k}{2}}(W+1)^{k}(P+1)^{k}

possible states for each
h\in\{0,\dots,n\}.

Since at each state the algorithm considers
2^{k}
ways of assigning the current item to the
k
solutions and each such assignment requires
O(k^{2})
time to update the current state, computing
Equation

18
takes

O\Bigl((n+1)(d_{min}+1)^{\binom{k}{2}}(W+1)^{k}(P+1)^{k}2^{k}k^{2}\Bigr).

Finally, we mention that constructing the actual solutions can be done by bookkeeping. In each DP cell, store the chosen bit-vector
x^{*}
that yielded the maximum diversity. When we reach the final cell, we follow its stored pointer back to the cell for
h-1
, etc. Each time we see
x^{*}_{m}=1
, it means item
h
was included in solution
S_{m}
. Tracing back from
h=n
down to
h=1
recovers all choices, giving the final subsets
S_{1},\dots,S_{k}
. This completes the construction of
k
solutions with the desired constraints.
∎

Baker’s Technique.

Finding one maximum independent set (MIS) and one minimum vertex cover (MVC) in planar graphs is NP-complete
[
27
]
, and an influential work by Baker
[
5
]
provided a PTAS for both problems.
A planar graph
G=(V,E)
can be embedded in the plane and the
levels
of vertices can be computed in linear time
[
33
,
41
]
.
A vertex is at level 1 if it is on the exterior face. In general, the vertices on the exterior face after all vertices at levels up to
i-1
have been removed are said to be at level
i
. We refer to the collection of all vertices at a certain level as a
layer
.

Figure 1
:
Illustration of decomposition of
G
. Here,
G
consists of 8 layers and
\ell=2
.
The left one denotes a part of
G
with the level indicating to the left of each layer. The middle one is a collection of
\ell
-outerplanar graphs constructed by removing
H^{(0)}_{3}(G)
from
G
. The right one is a collection of
(\ell+2)
-outerplanar graphs constructed by duplicating
L^{(0)}_{3}(G)
.

Given an approximation factor
(1-\gamma)
, let
\ell=(1/\gamma)-1
, and let
p\in\{0,\ldots,\ell\}
. The
p
-th strata of
G
, denoted
H^{(p)}_{\ell+1}
(or simply
H^{p}
when clear from the context), is the set of all vertices in
G
that are at levels congruent to
p
modulo
\ell+1
, i.e., the collection of every
(\ell+1)
-st layer from the
p
-th layer; see
Figure

1
. A planar graph is said to be
\ell
-outerplanar if it has at most
\ell
layers. Baker’s technique proceeds in two steps:

1.

Baker shows that there exists an MIS
S^{\prime}
and a
p\in\{0,\ldots,\ell\}
such that removing the
p
th strata from
S^{\prime}
results in an independent set
S
such that
|S|\geq(1-\gamma)|S^{\prime}|
. That is, ignoring the vertices in the strata does not decrease the size of an MIS by a factor more than
(1-\gamma)
. For MVCs, Baker shows that there exist layers such that duplicating them does not increase the size too much.

2.

Removal of such a
p
th strata now decomposes
G
into a collection of several
\ell
-outerplanar graphs
G_{1},\cdots,G_{q}
, for
\ell=(1/\gamma)-1
. Baker then provides a dynamic programming based exact algorithm for independent sets and vertex covers in
\ell
-outerplanar graphs running in time
2^{O(\ell)}n
.
The final solution is the union of all optimal solutions for the
\ell
-outerplanar graphs
G_{1},\cdots,G_{q}
.
For the MIS problem, by 1 above, the returned solution is at least
(1-\gamma)
of optimal.

Using the notation in
Section

3
with
c=1-\gamma
, if we let
\mathcal{A}_{(1-\gamma)}
denote Baker’s algorithm above, then the solutions output by
\mathcal{A}_{(1-\gamma)}
belong in a smaller class
{\mathcal{F}}^{{}^{\prime\prime}}_{1-\gamma}\subseteq{\mathcal{F}}_{(1-\gamma)}
defined as:

{\mathcal{F}}_{1-\gamma}^{{}^{\prime\prime}}\coloneqq\left\{\begin{aligned} S\subseteq V:&\exists p\in\{0,\ldots,\ell\}\ \text{s.t.}\ S\cap H^{p}=\emptyset,\\
&\text{and }S\text{ is an MIS in }G[V-H^{(p)}]\end{aligned}\right\},

where
G[A]
denotes the graph induced by some
A\subseteq V
.

In other words, the space of solutions is restricted in that the independent sets returned by Baker’s algorithms will be missing vertices from a certain strata. By ignoring a strata we lose some
c
-optimal solutions from the solution space. Thus any generalization of Baker’s algorithm is likely to be insufficient for solving DNO problem over
{\mathcal{F}}_{c}
.

Nice Tree Decomposition.

Our algorithm runs on a nice tree decomposition of an
\ell
-outerplanar graph. We first formally define a
tree decomposition
of a graph and define the “nice” version of it.

Definition 23

(Tree decomposition
[
38
]
)
.

A
tree decomposition
(TD) of a graph
G=(V,E)
consists of a tree
T
and a subset
V_{t}\subseteq V,
called the
bag
, associated with each node
t
of
T
, such that the ordered pair
(T,\{V_{t}:t\in T\})
must satisfy the following three properties:

(a)

Every node of
G
belongs to at least one bag
V_{t}
.

(b)

For every edge
e
of
G
, there is some bag
V_{t}
containing both ends of
e
.

(c)

Let
t_{1}
,
t_{2}
and
t_{3}
be three nodes of
T
such that
t_{2}
lies on the path from
t_{1}
to
t_{3}
. Then, if a vertex
v
of
G
belongs to both
V_{t_{1}}
and
V_{t_{3}}
, it also belongs to
V_{t_{2}}
.

Remark.

To avoid confusion, we use the term
a vertex
for
v\in V
and
node
for a vertex
t
of a tree decomposition of
T
of
G
.
The width of a tree decomposition
T
is defined as
\max_{t\in T}(|V_{t}|-1)
, and the
treewidth
of a graph
G
is the minimum width over all tree decompositions of
G
. Unless stated otherwise,
\omega
denotes the width of a tree decomposition.

Now, we define a nice tree decomposition of a graph.

Definition 24

(Nice TreeDecomposition,
[
9
]
)
.

A tree decomposition
T
is called a “nice” tree decomposition if each node
t
is one of the following four types.

1.

(Leaf) A node
t
is leaf of
T
, and
|V_{t}|=1
.

2.

(Introduce) A node
t
has exactly one child, say
t^{\prime}
, and there is a vertex
v\in V
such that
V_{t}=V_{t^{\prime}}\cup\{v\}
and
v\not\in V_{t^{\prime}}
.

3.

(Forget) A node
t
has exactly one child, say
t^{\prime}
, and there is a vertex
v\in V
such that
V_{t^{\prime}}=V_{t}\cup\{v\}
and
v\not\in V_{t}
.

4.

(Join) A node
t
has exactly two children, say
t^{\prime}
and
t^{\prime\prime}
and
V_{t}=V_{t^{\prime\prime}}=V_{t^{\prime\prime}}
.

Remark.

Since every
\ell
-outerplanar graph has a treewidth of at most
3\ell-1

[
10
]
, using the algorithms in Lemma 7.4 and Theorem 7.18 of
[
13
]
, any
\ell
-outerplanar graph can be transformed in time
2^{O(\ell)}n^{2}
into a nice tree decomposition with width of
O(\ell)
and
O(\ell n)
nodes.
Since these running times do not affect the overall running time of our algorithm, we assume throughout this section that any
\ell
-outerplanar graph can be transformed into a nice tree decomposition without any additional running time.

Lemma 25
.

[Existence of Marginal Strata]

Given a planar graph
G=(V,E)
,
c,\delta,\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and an integer

\ell\geq 4k\delta^{-1}+2\varepsilon^{-1}-1

(i)

(ii)

\sum_{i<j}|(S_{i}\cap H^{p})\triangle(S_{j}\cap H^{p})|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\sum_{i<j}|S_{i}\triangle S_{j}|
.

Proof.

The proof is a simple application of the union bound. Fix any independent sets
S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}
of
G

\ell\geq 4k\delta^{-1}+2\varepsilon^{-1}-1

Assume for contradiction that every

(i

|S_{h}\cap H^{p}|>\frac{\delta}{4}|S_{h}|\text{ for some $h\in[k]$.}

(ii
′
)

\sum_{i<j}|(S_{i}\cap H^{p})\triangle(S_{j}\cap H^{p})|>\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\sum_{i<j}|S_{i}\triangle S_{j}|
.

For each
h\in[k]

We claim that

\begin{split}|S_{h}|&=\sum_{p\in\{0,\ldots,\ell\}}|S_{h}\cap H^{p}|\geq\sum_{p\in A_{h}}|S_{h}\cap H^{p}|\\
&>\sum_{p\in A_{h}}\frac{\delta}{4}|S_{h}|\geq{4\delta}^{-1}\left(\frac{\delta}{4}\cdot|S_{h}|\right)=|S_{h}|,\end{split}

which is a contradiction. Similarly, define

as the set of all

Then,

Therefore, it follows that
|A_{1}|+\cdots+|A_{k}|+|A_{div}|<4{k}{\delta}^{-1}+2{\varepsilon}^{-1}\leq\ell+1,

which is a contradiction because every

9
Conclusion and Future Work

In this work, we provide a general framework for designing bi-criteria approximation algorithms that compute diverse solutions to packing and covering problems. As applications of this framework, we obtain bi-PTASs for the diverse variants of several classical problems, including
Knapsack
, MWIS and MWVC. We also establish complementary lower bounds, showing that
Diverse
c
-Knapsack
and
Diverse
c
-MWIS-PG
do not admit a standard PTAS or a bi-criteria FPTAS.

A gap remains between our upper and lower bounds. A natural direction for future work is therefore to determine whether these problems admit bi-criteria EPTASs (efficient polynomial-time approximation schemes).

Another promising direction is to investigate whether our framework extends to other packing and covering problems. One interesting candidate is the
Minimum Dominating Set
(MDS) problem on unit-disk graphs. Since this problem admits a PTAS
[
34
]
, techniques similar to those used in our framework for planar graphs may also be applicable in this setting. Given the fundamental role of MDS on unit-disk graphs in theory and practice, we believe that this direction merits further investigation.
