Comparative transcriptome profiling reveals a role of 24-epibrassinolide in flavonoid biosynthesis in Gleditsia sinensis
Authors: Zisiye Mu, Shuai Liu, Qiaoyuhui Xue, Jing Yang, Heying Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIt has been shown that 24-epibrassinolide (EBR), an active by-product of brassinolide biosynthesis, can stimulate different plant metabolic processes, including photosynthesis and protein and nucleic acid biosynthesis. EBR plays an important role in plant growth and development, especially in regulating the synthesis of secondary metabolites. The flavonoid-rich thorns of Gleditsia sinensis are used as a traditional Chinese medicine. Little is known about the molecular mechanism of flavonoid synthesis changes under EBR treatment. To elucidate the underlying molecular mechanism, we conducted comparative transcriptome analysis of plants treated with four different EBR concentrations (0, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5 mg/L) using RNA-Seq.ResultsA total of 1176 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were found between the EBR treatment groups. Among the DEGs, PAL and 4CL among flavonoid biosynthesis genes were significantly up-regulated under 1.0 mg/L EBR treatment. Additionally, CHS, F3H, and FLS were significantly up-regulated under 0.5 and 1.5 mg/L EBR treatments. Furthermore, C4H and CHI had higher expression under all EBR treatments than under control conditions, but DFR and ANR were down-regulating under all EBR treatments. In additional, important transcription factors (MYB-related, NAC, and WRKY) were activated under different EBR concentrations. Functional enrichment analysis suggested that genes associated with the metabolic process, response to stimulus, response to stress, plant hormone signal transduction, and flavonoid biosynthesis pathways were significantly enriched under EBR treatment.ConclusionOur study first provides a comprehensive understanding of genes involved in the response to EBR at the transcriptome level, especially the changes in the flavonoid pathway. These results elucidate the molecular mechanism of the EBR response in G. sinensis and also provide new insights and candidate genes for the synthesis of flavonoids under EBR treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07939-z.Keywords: Gleditsia sinensis, Flavonoid biosynthesis, 24-epibrassinolide, RNA-Seq

Background

Gleditsia sinensis Lam. is a woody species and widely distributed owing to its value and adaptability to harsh environmental conditions in China [1]. The seeds of G. sinensis are rich in galactomannan, which has potential industrial uses, and the thorns of G. sinensis are used as medicine to treat diseases that include skin ulcers, acute tonsillitis, and cancer, among other medical conditions [2–4]. The medical value of G. sinensis is likely owing to its richness in secondary metabolites.

So far, five classes of secondary metabolites from G. sinensis have been isolated and elucidated: triterpene, sterol, flavonoid, phenolics, and alkaloids. Among them, flavonoids are involved in various biological activities and responses to abiotic stressors. For example, two novel flavonoids, (2R,3R,7″R,8″S)-5″-methoxyl-silychristin A and (2R,3R,7″S,8″R)-5″-methoxyl-silychristin A, were isolated from G. sinensis thorns in 2021, and (2R,3R,7″S,8″R)-5″-methoxyl-silychristin A exhibited inhibitory effects on apoptosis [5]– [6].

Brassinosteroids (BRs) are a novel class of phytohormones that play numerous important roles in plant growth and development [7]– [8]. In 1941, Mitchell and Whitehead first discovered that can extract of rapeseed pollen that could promote the stem growth of soybeans. Subsequently, researchers isolated and characterized 37 new BRs from rape pollen [9–11]. Now, more than 70 natural analogues of BRs have been identified among 37 angiosperm species and 5 gymnosperm species [12–17]. BR signaling has been shown to modulate flavonoid biosynthesis by regulating key enzyme genes. For instance, Xi et al. sprayed different concentrations of EBR on grapes during the veraison stage and found that the activities of PAL and UDP-glucose enzymes were significantly enhanced. Additionally, the contents of total phenols, tannins, flavonoids, and anthocyanins were substantially increased [18]. In BR-deficient conditions, the suppressor BKI1 inhibits BRI1 receptor activity. BR binding releases BKI1, allowing the BAK1 co-receptor to enhance BRI1 kinase activity via phosphorylation. Downstream kinases BSK1/BSU1 inactivate the negative regulator BIN2 through dephosphorylation, thus preventing BIN2-mediated phosphorylation of transcription factors BZR1/BES1. Activated BZR1/BES1 then translocates to the nucleus to regulate target genes [19].

Among the BR family members, 24-epibrassinolide (EBR) is particularly highly bioactive. EBR, as an active by-product of brassinolide biosynthesis, can stimulate different plant metabolic processes, such as photosynthesis [20] and protein and nucleic acid biosynthesis [14]. EBR not only plays a key role in plant growth and development, but also has crucial regulatory effects on the production of plant secondary metabolites [21]– [22]. For example, Cao et al. found that EBR significantly activated the enzymes associated with phenylpropanoid metabolism, which slowed down pectin degradation and elevated the increase of total phenols, flavonoids, anthocyanin and lignans during post-harvest storage in blueberries [23]. The synthesis of flavonoids, an important class of secondary metabolites, involves the phenylpropanoid metabolic pathway. Phenylalanine ammonia lyase (PAL) catalyzes the deamination reactions of phenylalanine to coumaric acid, which in turn first undergoes cinnamate 4-hydroxylase (C4H) oxidation to 4-coumaric acid and then activation to p-coumaroyl-CoA (4CL) by the addition of a coenzyme A (CoA). Chalcone synthase (CHS) is responsible for the production of chalcone from one p-coumaroyl-CoA and 3-malonyl-CoA molecules, and this reaction is a key step in the downstream flavonoid formation pathway [24]– [25]. Chalcone isomerase (CHI), cinnamate 4-hydroxylase (C4H), anthocyanidin synthase (ANS), leucoanthocyanidin dioxygenase (LDOX), leucoanthocyanidin reductase (LAR), dihydroflavonol reductase (DFR), flavanone 3-hydroxylase (F3H), flavonol synthase (FLS), flavonoid 3′-hydroxylase (F3′H), flavonoid 3′5′-hydroxylase (F3′5′H), and flavonoid 3-O-glucosyltransferase (UFGT) are also key enzymes that control the biosynthesis of flavonoids [26]. The biosynthesis of anthocyanins occurs downstream of the flavonoid metabolic pathway, and the final products catalyzed by the corresponding enzymes are rutin and anthocyanins [27]. The biosynthesis of flavonoids is regulated by many factors, including light, hormones, temperature, etc. Meanwhile, transcription factors (TF) also exert regulatory effects on the synthesis of flavonoid compounds [28–30]. Ye et al. found that inhibition of CsMYB67 expression led to a substantial decrease in CsFLS transcript levels, resulting in a significant increase in the contents of both total catechins and total anthocyanidins. This indicated that CsMYB67 plays a critical regulatory role in the downstream pathway of flavonoid biosynthesis in summer tea leaves [31]. Furthermore, flavonoid biosynthesis has been demonstrated to be an adaptive mechanism of G. sinensis seedlings to low nitrogen conditions. Specifically, previous research has demonstrated that low nitrogen stress specifically activates phenylpropanoid metabolism and the flavonoid/flavonol biosynthetic pathways; the upregulation of key genes (including PAL17.1, PAL2, CYP73A, CHS, and FLS) promotes the accumulation of naringenin, luteolin, kaempferol, and quercetin [32].

During the growth and development of plants, they respond to biological and abiotic stresses by synthesizing various secondary metabolites. Additionally, as an important class of secondary metabolites, flavonoids play a crucial role in plant growth, development, and stress resistance. However, the biosynthesis of flavonoids can be regulated by some hormones, and the application of exogenous hormones can be utilized to regulate flavonoid biosynthesis in plants. Thus, investigating the effects of EBR on the flavonoid metabolic pathway in G. sinensis is of particular importance for exploring the growth and development of this plant species.

Results

Quality analysis and sequence assembly of RNA-seq data

A total of 73.02 Gb clean data were generated. All samples yielded clean sequencing data, with each sample reaching at least 5.94 Gb, Q30 base percentages over 94.63%, and GC content percentages exceeding 42.42% (Table 1). The assembly included a total of 81,981 unigenes, among which 20,730 exceeded 1 kb in length and 40,427 had at least one database annotation. Functional annotation of unigenes yielded annotation results for 40,427 unigenes. The Unigene sequences were aligned and annotated against the NR, Swiss-Prot, GO, COG, KOG, eggNOG, Pfam, and KEGG databases using DIAMOND software. Among each of these databases, 6887 to 38,625 genes were annotated (Table 2). To validate the accuracy of the RNA-seq data, RT-qPCR was performed on four genes randomly selected from among the identified DEGs (identified by gene expression differences with |fold change| > 2 and P-value < 0.05). RNA-seq data and RT-qPCR showed a similar trend in Fig. S1, suggesting that the transcriptome data were indeed reliable (Fig. S1).

Quantitative analysis of DEGs

Among the DEGs in the CK_/EBR0.5_, CK_/EBR1.0_, CK_/EBR1.5_, EBR0.5_/EBR1.0_, EBR1.0_/EBR1.5_ comparisons, 2496 (52.10%), 1692 (50.15%), 2769 (50.31%), 1988 (52.69%), and 1996 (46.95%) genes were down-regulated, while 2295 (47.90%), 1682 (49.85%), 2735 (49.69%), 1785 (47.31%), and 2255 (53.05%) genes were up-regulated (Fig. 1). Among the five comparisons, a total of 1176 different DEGs were found between the EBR treatment groups with different concentrations. As shown in the Venn diagram in Fig. 2, there were DEGs that occurred in one or more comparisons between the different EBR treatments (Fig. 2).

Gene correlation networks

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) identified a total of 18 modules with similar gene expression patterns from the dynamic hierarchical tree (Fig. 3a). Among the 18 modules, seven modules (blue, brown, lightcyan, pink, red, turquoise, and yellow in Fig. 3b) contained more than 1000 genes, and different numbers of TFs were identified in each modules (Fig. 3c).

Gene network analysis results. a Dendrogram showing modules identified by the weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) and a clustering dendrogram of expressed genes. b The number of genes contained in each module. c The number of transcription factors (TFs) present in each module. d Module-trait correlation analysis

The analysis of eigengenes showed that green, yellow, purple, and magenta modules showed a highly positive association with the control (CK) treatment, while the darkturquoise module had a weakly negative association with the CK treatment (Fig. 3d). The blue modules showed a highly positive association with the 0.5 mg/L EBR treatment, while the darkturquoise module had a weakly negative association with the 0.5 mg/L EBR treatment. The cyan, salmon, and midnightblue modules showed a highly positive association with the 1.0 mg/L EBR treatment, while the lightcyan module had a weakly negative association with the 1.0 mg/L EBR treatment. In addition, the pink modules showed a higher positive association with the 0.5 and the 1.5 mg/L EBR treatments. Thirty-seven genes (including DN35152_c0_g1, DN7582_c0_g1, and DN35152_c0_g1) were identified as possibly related to flavonoid biosynthesis in G. sinensis. Eleven genes (including DN37721_c0_g1, DN2238_c0_g1, and DN16251_c0_g1) associated with flavonoid biosynthesis were identified in the pink module. Four genes (including DN19618_c0_g1, DN35152_c0_g1, and DN13332_c0_g3) associated with flavonoid biosynthesis were identified in the blue and lightcyan modules.

Functional enrichment analysis of DEGs

To explore the functions of these DEGs, Gene Ontology (GO) annotation and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genome (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were performed. Among the GO terms associated with each treatment, in terms of the biological process (BP) category, DEGs were significantly enriched for annotations to cellular process and metabolic process (Fig. 4). In terms of the cellular component (CC) category, DEGs were significantly enriched for annotations to cellular anatomical entity. In terms of the molecular function (MF) category, DEGs were significantly enriched for annotations to binding and catalytic activity. Among these top processes, the DEGs related to flavonoid biosynthesis in the CK and 0.5 mg/L EBR comparison included metabolic process (GO:0019748), response to stimulus (GO:0050896), nitrogen compound metabolic process (GO:0006807), regulation of metabolic process (GO:0019222), and chloroplast (GO:0009507). Additionally, in the CK and 1.0 mg/L EBR comparison, the processes included biosynthetic process (GO:0009058), response to abiotic stimulus (GO:0009628), apoplast (GO:0048046), regulation of metabolic process (GO:0019222), and chloroplast (GO:0009507). GO terms that were enriched for DEGs related to flavonoid biosynthesis in the CK and 1.5 mg/L EBR comparison included response to stress (GO:0006950), response to wounding (GO:0009611), UDP-glycosyltransferase activity (GO:0008194), and heme binding (GO:0020037) (Fig. S2, Fig. S3, Fig. S4).

KEGG enrichment analysis was performed on both treatment-specific DEGs and co-expressed DEGs shared across all treatments. Both co-expressed and treatment-specific DEGs were significantly enriched for the plant–pathogen interaction and plant hormone signal transduction pathways (Fig. 5). In the CK and 1.0 mg/L EBR comparison, the most significantly enriched pathways were plant–pathogen interaction (map04626), MAPK signaling pathway–plant (map04016), amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism (map00520), and carbon metabolism (map01200). The most significantly enriched pathways for DEGs involved in the response to hormones in the CK and 1.5 mg/L EBR comparison included endocytosis (map04144), photosynthesis (map00195), glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism (map00630) and flavonoid biosynthesis (map00941), among others. These results suggest that the functions of DEGs were mostly related to response to stimulus (GO:0050896), phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (map00940), flavonoid biosynthesis (map00941), signal transduction (GO:0007165), response to hormone (GO:0009725), plant hormone signal transduction (map04075), cellular response to hormone stimulus (GO:0032870), hormone-mediated signaling pathway (GO:0009755), and plant–pathogen interaction (map04626) (Fig. S2, Fig. S3, Fig. S4). Under different EBR concentrations, the DEGs were enriched in annotations for plant hormone signal transduction pathways, which indicating that EBR may play a crucial role during plant growth and development.

Transcription factors (TFs) are critical proteins that govern gene expression by binding to specific DNA sequences. Among the three EBR treatment groups, we further analyzed the expression of TFs (including WRKY, MYB, and NAC members), which have been reported to be involved in the regulation of plant hormones (Fig. 6).

In our study, we characterized some WRKY TFs under EBR treatments, and the expression level of some WRKY members were significantly increased under 1.5 mg/L EBR. Based on WGCNA, nine WRKY genes (including DN7115_c0_g1, DN9570_c0_g1, and DN4829_c4_g1) and eleven genes (including DN37721_c0_g1, DN2238_c0_g1, and DN16251_c0_g1) related to flavonoid biosynthesis were grouped in the pink module. Similar to the results for WRKY members, some MYB members were significantly increased under 1.5 mg/L EBR treatments. The WGCNA results also indicated fifteen MYB genes (including DN101901_c0_g3, DN16254_c0_g1, and DN29_c1_g3) and eleven genes (includingDN37721_c0_g1, DN2238_c0_g1, and DN16251_c0_g1) associated with flavonoid biosynthesis were grouped in the pink module. The expression pattern of MYB (DN11811_c0_g1) significantly increased in the 0.5 and 1.5 mg/L EBR treatments. In terms of NAC TFs, DN504_c0_g1 was significantly increased under 1.0 mg/L EBR. The observed expression differences in the expression levels of these TFs under EBR treatments suggest that these TFs may play important roles in the response to physiological processes, especially in terms of flavonoid biosynthesis, in G. sinensis.

Key DEGs in the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway

The accumulation of flavonoid plays an important role in the process of plant growth and development, and key DEGs were identified in the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway based on WGCNA results. Two PAL genes (DN14335_c0_g1 and DN656_c0_g1) upstream of the pathway were upregulated under 1.0 mg/L EBR relative to the other treatments (Fig. 7). For instance, the DN14335_c0_g1 gene showed 1.6-, 9.2-, and 1.7-fold higher expression in the 1.0 mg/L EBR treatment compared to the CK, 0.5 mg/L, and 1.5 mg/L treatment groups, respectively. C4H (DN2064_c0_g2) had a higher expression under all EBR treatment relative to CK conditions. The 4CL gene (DN4233_c0_g1) expression level was the highest under 1.0 mg/L EBR, and CHS (DN7582_c0_g1) was highly expressed in the 0.5 and 1.5 mg/L EBR treatments. For example, under 0.5 and 1.5 mg/L EBR treatments, the expression of DN7582_c0_g1 was 7.3- and 8.3-fold higher, respectively, compared to the CK group.

CHI encodes an enzyme that can catalyze the stereo-specific cyclization of chalcones into naringenins or flavanones, and F3H (flavanone 3-hydroxylase) encodes an enzyme that can catalyze the hydroxylation of flavanones to form dihydrokaempferol. CHI and F3H are both important genes in the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway. The expression of CHI (DN37721_c0_g1) was upregulated under all EBR treatments. F3H (DN2238_c0_g1) expression was significantly higher under the 0.5 and 1.5 mg/L EBR treatments. The expression of FLS (DN35152_c0_g1), which acts downstream of the pathway, was significantly increased under the 0.5 and 1.5 mg/L EBR treatments. For instance, under 0.5 and 1.5 mg/L EBR treatments, the expression of DN35152_c0_g1 was 10.9- and 10.7-fold higher, respectively, compared to the CK group. However, DFR (DN8199_c0_g1) and ANR (DN6454_c0_g1) were significantly decreased under all EBR treatment. For example, the DN6454_c0_g1 gene showed 2.1-, 1.5-, and 2.3-fold higher expression in CK conditions compared to the 0.5, 1.0, and 1.5 mg/L treatment groups, respectively.

Discussion

EBR is one of the most biologically active brassinosteroids, and it can significantly promote plant growth, enhance stress resistance, and improve crop yield and quality when applied at appropriate concentrations [33–35]. In a previous study, Shaf et al. confirmed the potential of pre-soaking seeds in EBR solution to neutralize the toxic effects of heavy metals in Zea mays [36]. Luo et al. found that EBR can effectively regulate sugar and acid metabolism, increase flavonoid content, and influence fruit aroma, demonstrating its potential application in improving tomato fruit quality [37]. Exogenous EBR at optimal concentrations enhances the net photosynthetic rate and modulates endogenous hormone levels in Ginkgo biloba [38]. However, the current understanding of the molecular mechanism plant responses to EBR treatment remains incomplete. In our study, we revealed the potent role of BRs in the regulation of key flavonoid biosynthesis genes at the transcriptome level, and the current findings provide the groundwork for elucidating the mechanism of BRs-induced the regulation of genes in G. sinensis in particular. Our research could also provide a reference for understanding the relationship between EBR and flavonoid biosynthesis for other related woody plants.

In our study, we identified 4791 DEGs, including 2295 up-regulated and 2496 down-regulated genes in the CK vs. 0.5 mg/L EBR treatment comparison. Meanwhile, in the CK vs. 1.0 mg/L EBR treatment comparison there were 3374 DEGs, including 1682 up-regulated and 1692 down-regulated DEGs. We also identified 2735 up-regulated and 2769 down-regulated genes in the CK vs. 1.5 mg/L EBR treatment comparison. The significant enrichment of DEGs between different concentrations of EBR treatment with annotations for plant–pathogen interaction (map04626), plant hormone signal transduction (map04075), signal transduction (GO:0007165), starch and sucrose metabolism (map00500), response to hormone (GO:0009725), and MAPK signaling pathway–plant (map04016), reflect the mechanism by which G. sinensis respond to exogenous hormones [39]. BRI1 genes (DN8290_c0_g2, DN5057_c0_g1, and DN9652_c1_g1) involved in BR signal transduction were significantly upregulated under all EBR treatments (Fig. S5), which may indicate that exogenous EBR is likely recognized by the receptor kinase BRI1 and its co-receptor BAK1, thus triggering mutual phosphorylation between BRI1 and BAK1. The activation of BRI1 leads to its dissociation from the inhibitory protein BKI1 as well as the phosphorylation of membrane-anchored kinases BSK1 and CDG1 [40]. Concurrently, the RBOH gene (DN58791_c0_g1) was also significantly upregulated under all EBR treatments (Fig. S6). The synergistic interaction between BR signaling and reactive oxygen species (ROS) production induces RBOH expression. The NADPH oxidase encoded by this gene triggers apoplastic ROS accumulation, which subsequently activates the MAPK signaling pathway to enhance plant stress tolerance [41]. The upregulation of these genes may indicate that EBR acts as a signal to activate the stress tolerance response mechanisms in G. sinensis.

Plant hormones are essential bioactive substances that regulate various physiological and biochemical processes throughout the plant life cycle. MYB, WRKY, and NAC encode members of the largest family of plant TFs, and they play crucial roles in plant hormone responses. Numerous studies have reported the involvement of these three TFs in plant hormone response mechanisms. In Arabidopsis, EBR mediates BrWRKY8-induced leaf senescence delay [42]. Additionally, in maize, a MYB TF can respond to EBR signaling and increase the expression of Lhcb5 by binding to its promoter region [43]. We identified 34 WRKY members, 28 MYB members, and 19 NAC members were significantly up-regulated under different concentrations of EBR. In previous studies, overexpression of BnaNAC56 in Brassica napus induced massive ROS accumulation, thus triggering cell death and defense responses [44]. Among the NAC members that we have identified, DN504_c0_g1, DN3877_c0_g1, and DN13856_c0_g1 were significantly upregulated under the 1.0 mg/L EBR treatment. We thus hypothesized that NAC members may participant in defense responses. WRKY TFs participate in the biosynthesis of flavonoids metabolic products, ultimately contributing to the optimal adaptive regulation of plants under stress conditions [45]. We observed that DN67_c0_g1, DN7253_c0_g1, and DN17702_c0_g1 were significantly upregulated under the 1.5 mg/L EBR, suggesting that the TFs in these families have an important role to regulate in the response to stress.

Flavonoids are a group of secondary metabolites with antioxidant activities and medicinal value, and they also play important roles in plant pigmentation and abiotic stress responses [46]. Flavonols function as ROS scavengers to maintain cellular homeostasis, ensuring effective signal transduction while preventing ROS from accumulating to detrimental levels. These compounds also play a crucial protective role in plant responses to environmental stress [47]. Our study demonstrates that the significant up-regulation of key genes (PAL, C4H, and 4CL) in the phenylpropanoid pathway may lead to the accumulation of p-coumaric acid and coumaroyl, which then induce the biosynthesis of flavonoids. Additionally, other key genes (CHS, CHI, F3H, FLS) acting upstream were also significantly upregulated, but DFR (DN8199_c0_g1) and ANR (DN6454_c0_g1), acting in the downstream, were significantly downregulated. These results suggest that EBR may be related to the synthesis of flavonols, quercetin, and kaempferol.

WGCNA identified 102, 40, and 36 candidate genes in the pink module potentially associated with plant–pathogen interaction, plant hormone signal transduction, and the MAPK signaling pathway–plant, respectively. DN58791_c0_g1 and DN9652_c1_g1 were identified in the NR database as participating in signal transduction. Thus, the coordinated involvement of these genes might serve as key regulatory hubs in plant hormone signal transduction, plant–pathogen interaction, and the MAPK signaling pathway. In plants, the MAPK signaling pathway mediates the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, signal transduction, and transport of plant hormones [48]. In the present study, EBR treatment promoted the expression of BRI1 and RBOH, which may subsequently activate the MAPK signaling pathway to enhance plant stress tolerance. Furthermore, we identified fifteen MYB members (including DN16254_c0_g1 and DN29_c1_g3) and nine WRKY members (including DN7115_c0_g1 and DN4829_c4_g1) in the pink module in Fig. 3, and eleven potential genes (including DN37721_c0_g1 and DN2238_c0_g1) associated with flavonoid biosynthesis were also identified in this module. Weerawanich et al. reported that DzMYB1 can activate pDzCHI, pDzCHS, and pDzF3H, which encode proteins involved in the early portion of the flavonoid pathway [49]. In Gossypium hirsutum, Xiao et al. found that the GhWRKY41 homodimer directly activates the expression of GhC4H and Gh4CL, which then results in the synthesis and accumulation of flavonoids [50]. In the present study, 0.5 and 1.5 mg/L EBR treatments promoted the expression of WRKY and MYB, which encode TFs that may bind to structural genes involved in flavonoid biosynthesis and promoting the accumulation of flavonoids to enhance plant stress tolerance.

Some studies have shown that exogenous hormone application can restrict plant growth and development to different degrees. In the present study, we comprehensively analyzed G. sinensis under different concentrations of EBR, which provides valuable information and genetic resources for exploring flavonoid biosynthesis. Exogenous EBR likely acts as a stress signal that activates key genes associated with plant–pathogen interaction, plant hormone signal transduction, and the MAPK signaling pathways (via BRI1 and RBOH) through signal transduction. These signaling genes subsequently interact with TFs involved in stress responses (such as WRKY and MYB). Then, these TFs bind to structural genes involved in flavonoid biosynthesis, leading to the accumulation of flavonoids and ultimately activating a stress resistance mechanism in G. sinensis. These present results indicate that 0.5 and 1.5 mg/L EBR optimally activated the expression of genes related to flavonoid biosynthesis. Compared to the present findings on the flavonoid accumulation mechanism that were reported by Zou et al., who posited that flavonoid accumulation acts as a metabolic strategy for G. sinensis to sustain growth under nitrogen deficiency; our research indicates that EBR acts as a stress signal that enhances transcriptional programs associated with flavonoid biosynthesis in G. sinensis [32]. Future research should conduct functional validation of these key genes and elucidate their regulatory network of interactions. The present understanding of the regulatory mechanisms may provide a reference for further research investigating the role of EBR in other plant flavonoid pathways.

Conclusions

Our study first systematically elucidated the molecular regulatory network of G. sinensis under EBR treatment through via transcriptome sequencing. The important DEGs under EBR treatment were mainly enriched for involvement in metabolic process, response to stimulus, response to stress, plant hormone signal transduction and flavonoid biosynthesis pathways. Transcriptome analysis revealed that EBR treatment up-regulated BRI1 and RBOH, which are involved in plant hormone signaling. Additionally, different members of the MYB, WRKY, and NAC gene families were significantly up- and down-regulated under various EBR treatments. In addition, some DEGs (PAL and 4CL) among the flavonoid biosynthesis genes were significantly up-regulated under 1.0 mg/L EBR treatment. Similarly, CHS, F3H, and FLS were significantly up-regulated under 0.5 and 1.5 mg/L EBR treatments. Furthermore, C4H and CHI had higher expression under all EBR treatments relative to CK conditions, while DFR and ANR were down-regulating under all EBR treatments. All of these key DEGs may play crucial roles in flavonoid biosynthesis under EBR treatment, and the present results provide a new insights and candidate genes involved in the synthesis of flavonoids under EBR treatment.

Materials and methods

Plant material

The test materials consisted of open-pollinated (half-sib) seedling progeny of G. sinensis, accessions obtained Tianhetan Agricultural Tourism Research and Learning Pilot Demonstration Base (Guiyang, China). The seeds were stored at 4 °C before sowing and seedling cultivation. Routine management was conducted at the experimental base of the west campus of Guizhou University, Guiyang, Guizhou, China. Following the methods of a previous study, a total of 120 healthy and uniformly growing three-year-old G. sinensis plants were selected as experimental materials [51]. We employed a completely randomized design. Four different EBR concentrations (0, 0.5, 1.0, and 1.5 mg/L) were utilized to evaluate the effects of EBR on G. sinensis plants. Each treatment consisted of three biological replicates with 10 plants per replicate. The first treatment of G. sinensis leaves was conducted on October 21, 2024, with 4 mL of EBR solution sprayed per plant, while the control group was sprayed with an equal amount of distilled water (containing 0.045% anhydrous ethanol). Spraying was performed every three days, for a total of five applications (Table. S1). Mixed sampling of G. sinensis leaves was conducted six days after the final spraying. All G. sinensis leaves samples were collected at the same time and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen. The handling of all plant materials complied with institutional and international guidelines for experimental use of cultivated plants.

RNA extraction and RNA-seq

Total RNA was extracted from the freeze-dried samples using a Trizol reagent kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA quality was assessed with an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA). The RNA extraction quality and concentration of all samples passed the quality inspection, and the mRNA was enriched with Oligo (dT) magnetic beads. Fragmentation buffer was added to randomly fragment the mRNA into small fragments. Under the action of reverse transcriptase, single-stranded cDNA was reversely synthesized using mRNA as a template, and then double-stranded cDNA was synthesized. The double-stranded cDNA was purified, end-repaired, A-tailed, and ligated to sequencing adapters. After the library construction was completed, an initial quantification was performed using a Qubit 3.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), with the concentration required to exceed 1 ng/µL. The insert size of the library was then assessed using the Qsep400 High-Throughput Analysis System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Once the insert size was confirmed to be as required, the effective concentration of the library (requiring >2 nM) was accurately quantified using the Q-PCR method to ensure library quality. After passing these quality checks, paired-end sequencing was performed on the Illumina HiSeq sequencing platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). The raw reads were subjected to quality control with fastp to remove adapter sequences and filter out low-quality reads [52]. Subsequently, the clean reads were assembled using Trinity software to obtain a de novo transcriptome assembly. Trinity successfully identified and reconstructed potential transcripts from the clean reads, which were then utilized for downstream analysis [53].

Data processing and analysis

The PlantTFDB database was used to identify TFs [54]. All assembled unigenes were annotated using several databases, including the NCBI NR (http://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), GO (http://www.geneontology.org), KEGG (http://www.kegg.jp), Pfam (http://pfam.xfam.org/), and COG (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/COG/) databases. Genes of interest were subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis to predict the potential functions of the identified genes. Gene expression levels were estimated as fragments per kilobase per transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM values). We performed differential expression analysis between transcriptomes obtained under the various EBR treatments using the DESeq R package, which allowed for statistical analysis using the negative binomial distribution model [55]. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between different treatments were identified based on thresholds of p-value < 0.05 and |fold change| > 2. The weighted co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) package in R [56] was used to obtain the gene co-expression networks with the following parameters: power = 8, minModuleSize = 30, MEDissThres = 0.25.

Validation of DEGs by real-time quantitative PCR

RT-qPCR primers were designed using Beacon Designer 7.0 (Table S2). Differences in expression among EBR treatments for enzyme-encoding genes were verified by quantitative real-time PCR using the G. sinensis EIF5A gene as a reference. The thermal cycling program for the qRT-PCR was as follows: 95 °C for 5 min; 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s, 57 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s. The real-time RT-PCR volume (10 µl) included 5 µl of 2× SYBR Green qPCR Mix, 2 µl of cDNA, 0.25 µl of each primer, and 2.5 µl of ddH2O. The qPCR using SYBR Green qPCR Mix (Biosharp, Beijing, China) was repeated at least three times, and the data were analyzed by the 2−(∆∆Ct) method to obtain relative expression data [57].

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted with a minimum of three independent biological replicates. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) data were analyzed using SPSS statistical software (version 22.0; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Statistical significance was determined by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Fisher’s protected least significant difference (LSD) test, with a significance threshold set at p < 0.05.
