Occupational health challenges among university security personnel in a low-resource setting: a qualitative exploratory study
Authors: Michael Afari Baidoo, Ansir Ahmed Omar Saasong, Desmond Kofi Kwawuvi, Nana Borsah Ackah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionUniversity security personnel in low-resource settings are among the most occupationally vulnerable non-academic staff yet they remain largely absent from occupational health research. This study investigated how university security officers in Ghana perceive and navigate occupational health challenges associated with burnout and shift-related stress.MethodsA qualitative exploratory study rooted in interpretive phenomenology was conducted between July and August 2025 at a public university in southern Ghana. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with 20 purposively selected security personnel with at least 5 years of service. Data were analysed using Braun and Clarke’s reflexive thematic analysis approach.ResultsFive themes emerged: (I) occupational workload and burnout driven by chronic understaffing; (II) perceptual burden and emotional strain from disrespectful student interactions; (III) night duty as a significant stressor through disrupted sleep and environmental hazards; (IV) improvised individual and social coping strategies; and (V) inadequate institutional support and structural resource gaps as predictors of sustained stress and burnout.ConclusionBurnout and occupational stress among university security personnel in low-resource settings reflect institutional and systemic failures rather than individual shortcomings. Sustainable improvements require adequate staffing, psychosocial support, gender-responsive scheduling and improved infrastructure. Future research should employ longitudinal and multisite designs to capture temporal and contextual variation.Keywords: Accidents, Occupational; Environmental Exposure; Occupational Medicine; Public Health

Introduction

University security personnel, defined as trained, uniformed staff employed by institutions of higher education to safeguard lives, property and campus order, constitute a vital yet frequently unrecognised segment of the university workforce. In the broader literature, the terms ‘security guard’ and ‘security personnel’ are used interchangeably. However, in this study, ‘security personnel’ is the preferred term because it better describes the institutional, professional and quasi-regulatory roles these workers play in a university setting, including both frontline officers and supervisors.

The safety and operational continuity of universities worldwide depend significantly on the vigilance and dedication of their security personnel.1,2 Nevertheless, these frontline professionals, tasked with protecting lives and property, often grapple with profound occupational health challenges, including sleep disturbances, fatigue; anxiety from sustained vigilance; trauma-related conditions such as post-traumatic stress disorder; burnout and shift-related stress.1,2 This critical issue extends beyond individual well-being, impacting the overall effectiveness of campus security and, by extension, the academic environment itself.3,4

The importance of this problem is underscored by the global expansion of the security profession and evidence of health impairments linked to occupational stress,2,5 with studies indicating that security work is inherently more stressful than many other occupations due to exposure to aggression, irregular working hours and high-stakes responsibility. These stressors lead to a range of adverse health effects, such as cardiovascular disease, diabetes and metabolic syndrome.2 In Serbia, a study of security guards revealed a high prevalence of dyslipidaemia, hypertension and metabolic syndrome directly linked to professional stressors.2 Similarly, a study in Shimoga, Karnataka, India, found that a significant majority of security guards experienced moderate to severe occupational stress, emphasising the necessity of effective stress management programmes.6

Burnout, which is characterised by emotional exhaustion, depersonalisation and reduced personal accomplishments, is a prevalent and debilitating syndrome among security personnel.7,8 It is a psychological response to prolonged occupational stress, particularly common in professions involving intense interaction with people in emotionally challenging situations.8 A multicentred, cross-sectional study in Central Serbia found high levels of emotional exhaustion and depersonalisation among private security staff.8 This phenomenon extends to academic settings: security guards at North-West University’s Potchefstroom campus in South Africa face unique challenges, including verbal abuse from students, irregular shifts disrupting family life and monotony.9 A study at a public university in Brazil found a notable prevalence of health problems and reduced work capacity among security guards, linked to sedentary work and demanding schedules.10

Shift-related stress compounds these challenges further. Shift work, particularly night shifts, disrupts the body’s natural circadian rhythms, leading to sleep disturbances and an increased risk of anxiety and depression.11,12 Studies on general shift workers13 and specifically on public safety personnel such as police officers in North America show that irregular schedules, long shifts (≥11 hours) and insufficient sleep significantly increase the risk of burnout.14 In developing contexts such as Nigeria, healthcare workers exposed to shift work also experience heightened mental health challenges due to disrupted sleep patterns.15

There are diverging perspectives on the primary drivers of occupational stress and the most effective interventions. Some research emphasises the role of organisational support in mitigating stress,1,16 while others focus on individual coping mechanisms.17,18 Studies highlight the negative consequences of work intensity on burnout in higher education19 alongside the importance of supportive leadership and resources.20 Ongoing debate also exists regarding whether low internal control beliefs or specific occupational stressors primarily drive psychological distress in high-risk occupations.21 These perspectives emphasise the complex nature of burnout and suggest a need for integrated approaches that consider both systemic and individual factors.22

Despite this growing body of evidence, limited research exists on occupational health among university security personnel in sub-Saharan Africa. Research on occupational health among university security personnel in sub-Saharan Africa and in low-income settings more broadly remains sparse. Existing studies have largely been conducted in high-income Western or Asian contexts, which limits the relevance of their findings to institutions operating under significantly different resource, cultural and organisational conditions. In Ghana, where universities face chronic underfunding, staffing shortfalls and limited psychosocial infrastructure, understanding the lived occupational realities of security personnel represents both a research gap and a policy imperative.

In this study, we examined the psychosocial experiences of security officers in a university environment within a low-income context. Specifically, we asked, “How do university security personnel perceive and navigate the occupational health challenges associated with burnout and shift-related stress?” From this perspective, we aimed to extend current knowledge on occupational health by foregrounding the often-invisible labour of workers responsible for maintaining security in higher education settings.

Materials and methods

Study design

We conducted a qualitative exploratory study rooted in interpretive phenomenology, using semi-structured interviews with open-ended questions guided by the shift-work and occupational-burnout literature23,24 to examine university security officers’ lived experiences of stress and burnout associated with shift work. Interpretive phenomenology was selected because it is particularly well-suited to capturing the subjective meanings individuals attach to their lived experiences within their occupational and social contexts.23 Unlike purely descriptive phenomenology, the interpretive (hermeneutic) approach acknowledges the researcher’s active role in meaning-making and allows for analysis that moves beyond description towards interpretive engagement with participants’ accounts, an approach uniquely appropriate for understanding the nuanced, culturally embedded experiences of a marginalised occupational group in a low-resource setting.

Study context

This study was conducted between 1 July 2025 and 31 August 2025 at a renowned public university in the southern part of Ghana. The university employs a total of 1784 staff members, including 120 security personnel stationed across its three sub-campuses.

Participants

A total of 20 participants were recruited for this study. The study’s participants consist of university-employed security personnel with a minimum of 5 years of experience as university security staff. The 5-year employment requirement was adopted to ensure that participants had sufficient experience and long-term exposure to the demands of security work, allowing them to provide informed, reflective insights into its cumulative occupational health effects.

Sample size justification

A sample of 20 participants was deemed sufficient for this qualitative study using an interpretive phenomenological approach, where the aim was in-depth exploration rather than statistical generalisation. Participants were purposively selected to achieve maximum variation in characteristics such as gender, rank and years of service, ensuring a wide range of experiences was represented. Data collection continued until thematic saturation was reached, with no new themes emerging in the final interviews. This sample size is consistent with established guidance for qualitative studies seeking rich, detailed insights into lived experiences.23,25

Data collection process

Individual face-to-face interviews were conducted in individual offices within the Security Directorate, which provided a neutral and familiar setting for participants. Sessions lasted between 15 and 25 min, depending on participants’ time availability and motivation. 15 interviews were conducted in Twi or Fante, and 5 in English, according to participant preference. The principal interviewer (the first author, MAB), who is fluent in both local languages and English, conducted all interviews to ensure uniformity. Audio recordings were supplemented with rich field notes documenting nonverbal conduct, interruptions and affective responses, which are considered essential for interpretive validity in qualitative health research.26 Following each interview, participants were debriefed and invited to offer reflections or clarifications.

Translation and linguistic rigour

To minimise the risk of semantic loss in translation, the following procedures were adopted: (1) all interviews conducted in Twi or Fante were transcribed verbatim in the original language by the lead researcher (MAB), who is a native speaker of Fante; (2) transcripts were then translated into English by the same researcher; (3) a bilingual research team member independently reviewed a random sample of five transcripts (25%) to verify translation accuracy, with discrepancies resolved through discussion; and (4) where culturally specific expressions or idioms had no direct English equivalent, the original phrase was retained in the transcript alongside a contextual explanation. This process ensured that culturally specific constructions of burnout, stress and occupational distress were preserved rather than flattened into Western terminological conventions.

Data collection tool

The study used a semi-structured interview guide organised into six thematic sections: background information, experiences of burnout, shift-related stress, occupational health and well-being, coping mechanisms, institutional support and closing reflections. The tool was designed with open-ended questions and targeted prompts to elicit rich, in-depth accounts of participants’ lived experiences while maintaining consistency across interviews (see online supplemental material for the full questionnaire). Content validity was ensured through alignment with existing literature on occupational stress and burnout and expert review, while clarity and relevance were refined through pre-testing. Reliability was enhanced by the structured sequencing of questions, the use of consistent probes and the application of a uniform interview procedure across all participants.

Data analysis

Interviews were transcribed verbatim in the native language and translated into English to preserve nuance by the lead researcher (MAB), who is fluent in the local language (Fante). Transcripts were analysed using Braun and Clarke’s24 reflexive thematic analysis approach, which involves six sequential steps: (1) close reading and notetaking; (2) line-by-line coding in ATLAS V.24; (3) initial theme generation through team discussion; (4) cross-validation of themes using the full dataset; (5) iterative refinement and naming of themes and (6) identification of key quotations. Two researchers (MAB and AAOS) independently coded the first five transcripts, with disagreements resolved through discussion to ensure analytic rigour.25 Analysis proceeded in parallel with data collection, allowing preliminary findings to inform subsequent interviews.

Reflexivity and positionality

The research team acknowledged that their positionality may influence data collection, analysis and interpretation. The lead researcher (MAB) is a public health academic with professional familiarity with university environments in Ghana. While this insider knowledge facilitated rapport with participants and informed culturally sensitive probing, it also required deliberate reflexive practice to guard against confirmatory interpretation. Reflexive memos were maintained throughout the study to document emerging interpretations, analytical decisions and positionality-related reflections. These memos were shared among team members to support transparent and balanced analysis. The research team had no supervisory relationship with participants and took care to emphasise the voluntary, confidential nature of participation to minimise any perceived institutional obligation to respond in particular ways.

Ethical considerations

All study procedures and protocols were approved by the University Institutional Review Board. Our ethical processes conformed to the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki27 and the Ghana Health Service research ethics policy. Voluntary participation and informed consent were obtained prior to each interview, and participants were reminded of their right to withdraw at any point without penalty. Interviews were scheduled outside working hours to prevent conflict with work expectations. Confidentiality was maintained through the anonymous use of pseudonyms and the removal of all identifying details from transcripts and reports. Due to the sensitive nature of work-related stress, referral to counselling services was offered to all participants, irrespective of whether they requested it.

Confidentiality and anonymity

Participants’ identities were protected through pseudonyms and identification codes, and no personally identifiable information was included in transcripts or reports. Data were securely stored on password-protected devices and handled in accordance with ethical research guidelines to ensure privacy and confidentiality throughout the study.

Patient and public involvement

Security personnel were not involved in study design or participant recruitment. However, a subgroup of five officers contributed to the interpretation of preliminary findings to ensure that themes accurately reflected their lived experiences and operational realities.

Results

Participant characteristics

Table 1 summarises the socio-demographic and occupational characteristics of the participants (N=20). The sample was predominantly male (80%), with females representing 20% of participants. The largest proportion of respondents (40%) were aged 38–47 years, with the remainder evenly distributed between 28–37 years (30%) and 48–57 years (30%). Respondents were relatively evenly distributed across service categories (35% with 5–9 years, 30% with 10–14 years and 35% with 15–23 years). The majority held frontline officer positions (75%), while 25% were supervisors. Most were married (60%), and 70% reported dependent children. Educationally, 85% had secondary education, and 15% held a diploma. Concerning shift assignment, 40% mainly worked night shifts, 35% alternated between day and night shifts, and 25% worked irregular shifts or covered staffing shortages.

Five main themes were identified in the thematic analysis

Theme I: occupational workload and burnout

Subtheme 1.1: chronic workload and understaffing

Security staff experienced significant pressure arising from chronic workload and understaffing, resulting in extensive physical and emotional fatigue. Lengthy shifts and insufficient staffing levels typified high-pressure conditions, cumulatively leading to emotional and physical exhaustion.

Yes, we are sometimes short-staffed, and for that reason, it winds up making the job very demanding. And in extreme cases, we call on the other stations to come and help. (E1)

Oh, yeah. As I said, we're undermanned, so you might have about two or three people working at the same time. (E1)

Our job is stressful. You wake up at about 4:00 AM and come back maybe around 7:00 PM. (E6)

Yes, night duty is hard. Because of shortages, too, we don't have enough time to rest. (E4)

The stress was not confined to physical exertion alone. Managing interactions with students added another dimension to the chronic workload:

Yes. Some students are rude and insult us when we give them instructions. Some also see security as illiterate. (E3)

Subtheme 1.2: perceptual burden and emotional strain leading to burnout

The majority of participants articulated burnout in terms of total physical and mental exhaustion:

It is when you are exhausted. (E2)

Burnout to me means feeling completely exhausted, both physically and mentally. Yes, I've felt that, especially after working long hours without adequate rest. “Like, continuous work can get you so tense and demoralised.” (E7)

YES. Some students are very disrespectful and may insult us when we provide them with instructions. "Some students perceive the security officers as illiterate. (E3)

It’s not easy. Yes, definitely. Especially when there was a demonstration, the behavior of the students towards us was very poor. To manage them is not easy. If circumstances are unfavourable, the students may physically assault you. (E5)

We security personnel experience significant stress when intruders enter the premises. The stress also increases, particularly when we have hall elections. (E11)

Not all participants experienced burnout uniformly. E8 showed relative detachment, saying “This is my job, so it doesn't bother me”, while E12 stated unfamiliarity with the term “burnout,” suggesting individual differences in coping capacity or resilience.

Theme II: night duty as a significant stressor

Night shifts imposed a distinctive constellation of stressors, including relentless patrolling demands, environmental hazards, heightened security vigilance and profoundly disrupted sleep patterns.

When on night duty, especially near the bungalows, check that all doors are shut and outside items are secured. You also check if vehicles are well locked, and if you find any unlocked vehicles, you report the vehicle number to management to inform the owner. You are also to patrol every hour. (E9)

Night duty is challenging for me; you have to patrol all the time. (E4)

Night duty is cumbersome to me, and mosquito bites are also there. (E4)

“At night, while inspecting the premises outdoors, mosquitoes swarm and avoid swatting.” Those who go to the hospital are granted two days off. I currently have about two excused duties. (E5)

Another officer directly associated mosquito exposure with a recurring medical outcome: “Yes. Because of mosquito bites I sometimes contract fever and malaria. Our shared disease is malaria.” The threat of intruders further amplified night-duty stress:

The night shift is more difficult and stressful. If you're not very alert, you'll be robbed, and since we're understaffed, it makes it harder for us. (E5)

Sleep disruption was a consistent and significant consequence of night duty:

When we work night duties. Because whenever you are sleeping in the house at nighttime and whenever you close from night duty, sleeping during the day, the hours that you will sleep in the night are much more compared to the day duties. And that one will cause you either a headache or … (E8)

When I am on night duties, I do not sleep more than two hours. (E12)

Theme III: improvised individual and social strategies to cope with stress and burnout

Participants described a range of individual and social coping strategies developed largely without institutional facilitation.

It depends on the individual. The rotation system allows me to cope with the stress because when you remain in the same place for too long, it is stressful. (E1)

“I believe that physical training is essential; as a security officer, you should engage in daily exercise.” You will be fit to do the work. You should eat well and exercise. (E1)

I take some time to rest. (E6)

I talk to my mates to cope with stress. (E10)

Social humour and peer interaction were also featured as relief mechanisms, with one officer noting a momentary reduction in tension due to social distractions during patrol. There was variation in perceived institutional support: while some participants reported management responsiveness as helpful, others identified stress management as an individual responsibility:

Because it is your work, you must take responsibility to do it well. (E10)

Theme IV: inadequate institutional support and structural resource gaps as predictors of stress and burnout

The renaming of this theme from 'liminal predictors’ to ’structural predictors’ is intended to improve conceptual clarity and avoid ambiguity.

Despite the provision of basic health screening services, participants identified pervasive deficiencies in preventative measures, infrastructure and operational resources:

Because of staff shortages, too, we don't have enough time to rest. (E4)

Night shift is more difficult and stressful. If you're not very careful, you'll get theft cases, and we're short-staffed, making it harder. (E5)

The institution should be fenced well. This will make intruders fearful when entering. (E17)

We don't have any shade. We don't have raincoats, so when it rains, it becomes difficult for us. There are no walkie-talkies. We use our phones. (E5)

Despite these deficits, salary remained a significant motivation for continued service:

Salary is one of the important factors. It is useful. (E10)

The basic health screening offered by the university represents a reactive rather than preventative response, addressing symptoms without targeting the structural sources of occupational distress.

Discussion

This study examined how university security personnel in a low-resource Ghanaian setting perceive and navigate occupational health challenges associated with burnout and shift-related stress. The findings reveal a convergence of chronic workload, inadequate staffing, emotional strain and physiological night-duty burden that collectively generate conditions in which burnout is structural rather than incidental. We discuss these findings in relation to established theoretical frameworks and the broader occupational health literature.

The experiences described by participants closely align with the Job Demands–Resources (JD-R) model,20,28 which posits that burnout occurs when job demands chronically exceed available resources. In our sample, demands such as extended working hours, constant patrolling, crowd management and exposure to confrontation remained persistently high, while key resources like adequate staffing, protective equipment, mental health support and infrastructure were severely lacking. This imbalance maps directly onto the JD-R model’s burnout pathway and extends its applicability to a population not yet studied within this framework in sub-Saharan Africa.

The chronic workload and understaffing align with global security profession observations. Health impairments among security guards in Serbia2 have a direct link to high occupational stress, which is a consequence of demanding work. The perception of being overworked resonates with studies on District Armed Reserve police in Karnataka, India, where officers performing the work of multiple individuals endure continuous demands and a lack of adequate rest.17 Work overload generally leads to increased job stress, burnout, emotional exhaustion and negative health outcomes such as sleep disturbances and cardiovascular problems.29

The emotional burden borne by participants, arising from disrespectful student interactions, crowd management risk and the psychological weight of protecting lives, also resonates with the Effort–Reward Imbalance (ERI) model, which identifies a mismatch between high occupational effort and low reward (including low status, insufficient pay and lack of recognition) as a key driver of burnout. Security officers in our study exerted considerable emotional labour, yet they reported low institutional recognition, inadequate compensation relative to their responsibilities and minimal psychosocial support. This imbalance is consistent with the high burnout rates documented among private security staff in Central Serbia8 and reflects a pattern where occupational role and social status are fundamentally misaligned.

Night duty significantly exacerbated stressors for university security guards, a finding consistent with extensive research on shift work’s occupational health impact. A study at AIIMS Bibinagar, India, demonstrated that night shifts severely compromise security personnel’s sleep architecture and mental well-being, leading to decreased sleep efficiency and elevated systolic blood pressure.30 Brazilian research on security workers similarly indicates that night work contributes to physical and psychological fatigue, stress, and sleep dysregulation.31 In Italy, a study on security guards indicated that night-shift work disrupts circadian rhythms and activates stress responses.32 These patterns mirror experiences reported by Iranian hospital security personnel due to unwanted shift schedules, which similarly disrupt their circadian rhythms and contribute to increased stress levels.

Participants’ coping strategies, rotation adaptation, physical exercise, peer support, humour and religious practice can be understood through the lens of the Conservation of Resources (COR) theory, which posits that individuals strive to acquire, retain and protect resources to manage stress.20 The improvised, individually driven nature of these strategies in our study suggests that institutional resource provision is insufficient to replenish occupational resource deficits, leaving officers to manage stress through personal resourcefulness. This is consistent with scoping review evidence on police officers’ coping mechanisms33 and self-reported public safety personnel strategies,34 while also highlighting a gap in empirically validated resilience programmes for security staff in low-income settings.35

Inadequate institutional support and resource deficits emerged as structural predictors of sustained stress and burnout. This mirrors findings from police organisations, where organisational social support, leadership culture and resource availability are critical determinants of perceived discomfort.36 The stigma around mental health help-seeking further constrains support usage.37 The absence of protective equipment, inadequate infrastructure (fencing, shelter, communication devices) and heavy reliance on personal mobile phones for operational communication underscore the extent of systemic neglect. While salary provides a residual motivation for continued service, financial compensation cannot substitute for environmental safety and psychosocial support.

Methodological limitations

When interpreting the findings, one should consider the several methodological limitations of this study.

First, data collection was conducted within offices of the Security Directorate—a setting that may have constrained participants’ willingness to critically discuss institutional policies, management conduct or experiences of conflict, potentially introducing social desirability bias and institutional power asymmetry. Despite efforts to maintain confidentiality, the proximity to participants’ workplace may have affected the candour of their responses.

Second, restricting recruitment to participants with a minimum of 5 years of service may have introduced a survivor effect, excluding newer or more transient personnel who may exhibit distinct stress profiles, higher attrition tendencies or more acute burnout-related exits. Early-career vulnerability may therefore be under-represented.

Third, interviews were conducted in multiple languages and translated by the principal investigator. Although steps were taken to preserve semantic fidelity (including bilingual verification of a subset of transcripts), translation inevitably risks losing culturally specific constructions of burnout, stress and occupational distress that may not have precise equivalents in English.

Finally, the manuscript does not engage with established occupational stress or burnout theoretical frameworks beyond the phenomenological approach. Explicit engagement with the JD-R model, ERI model or COR theory would have enhanced the interpretive depth of the findings and their theoretical generalisability.

Implications

Our findings have several implications for universities and security management. First, addressing chronic understaffing by hiring adequate personnel and implementing fair workload distribution is paramount. Second, providing training on de-escalation techniques and conflict resolution can help security personnel manage disrespectful interactions with students. Third, recognising and mitigating the unique challenges of night duty through better scheduling, adequate rest facilities and environmental protections (eg, insect repellents and improved shelters) is crucial. Fourth, universities must invest in robust institutional support, including mental health services, improved infrastructure and appropriate equipment, to foster healthier and more sustainable working conditions for security staff.

Conclusion

University security personnel in low-resource settings navigate multiple occupational health challenges that demand urgent institutional attention. Chronic understaffing, emotional strain from contested authority and the physiological burden of night shifts combine to create conditions where burnout is structural rather than incidental. The officers demonstrated resilience through improvised coping strategies, including peer support, humour, physical exercise and religious practice; however, these individual strategies cannot substitute for systemic reform. Climate stressors—including heat, rainfall and mosquito exposure—further compound occupational distress during night duties. Evidence-based interventions, including appropriate staffing levels, psychosocial support services, gender-responsive shift scheduling and improved infrastructure, are essential investments rather than optional provisions. Future research should employ longitudinal and multisite designs to capture temporal dynamics and enhance transferability across contexts.
