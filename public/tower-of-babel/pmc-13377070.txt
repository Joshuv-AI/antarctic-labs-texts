Catalytic and regulatory basis of tRNA t6A modification by the KEOPS complex
Authors: Li Zhou, Zelin Zhang, Mengqi Jin, Dengmiao Xie, Han Wen, Eric Westhof, Dongsheng Lei, Wenhua Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
N6-threonylcarbamoyladenosine (t6A) at tRNA position 37 is essential for translational fidelity and cellular homeostasis. The multi-subunit KEOPS complex catalyzes t6A formation in Archaea and Eukarya. Here we present cryo-EM structures of C. elegans KEOPS in its apo and tRNA-bound states. tRNA binding induces concerted conformational rearrangements, distorting the anticodon loop to project A37 into the Kae1 active site. Kae1 recognizes the conserved G10–C25 pair and 36-UAA-38 motif. Bud32 directly contacts the anticodon and acceptor arms, coupling ATP hydrolysis to t6A catalysis in the distant Kae1 active site through long-range conformational changes. Cgi121 enhances catalytic efficiency through cooperative binding with Bud32 and the tRNA 3’ CCA. Pcc1 stabilizes the anticodon loop and mediates KEOPS dimerization, enhancing tRNA binding and t6A activity. GAMOS-associated mutations cluster at functional hotspots within the KEOPS–tRNA complex. This study provides a structural framework for understanding KEOPS mechanism and its cellular roles.

Introduction

Transfer RNAs (tRNAs) require a diverse set of post-transcriptional modifications for their maturation, structure and function1–3. Dedicated modifications enzymes install distinct chemical groups to defined atoms of specific nucleobases at various positions of tRNA molecules3–6. Notably, a high density of modifications is found at positions 34 and 37 within the anticodon loop of tRNAs where they play pivotal roles in accurate decoding and translational fidelity1,2,7–12. The threonylcarbamoylation of adenosine 37 of tRNA to N6-threonylcarbamoyladenosine (t6A) is one of the universal core modifications found in all three domains of life10,13,14. The t6A modification strengthens the first codon-anticodon pair in codon triplets beginning with an adenine (ANN-codons, N being A, U, C, or G)14–17. In humans, misregulation of the tRNA t6A modification is linked to several diseases14, including the autosomal-recessive Galloway-Mowat syndrome (GAMOS)18,19, cancers20, and type 2 diabetes21,22. The loss of t6A modification leads also to misrecognition of the AUG codon and +1 frameshifting23–26, thereby causing translational dysregulation21,27, with effects ranging from early lethality in unicellular organisms to impaired fitness in higher eukaryotes24,28–30.

The biosynthesis of tRNA t6A proceeds through two conserved steps, catalyzed by members of two last universal common ancestor (LUCA) protein families—TsaC/Sua5 and TsaD/Kae1/Qri7—with the assistance of varying auxiliary components depending on the organisms14,24,28,31–33. As depicted in the reaction scheme (Fig. 1a), TsaC/Sua5 utilizes L-threonine, bicarbonate and ATP to generates a threonylcarbamoyl-adenylate (TC-AMP) intermediate; TsaD/Kae1/Qri7 transfers its threonylcarbamoyl (TC) moiety to the N6 atom of A37 in tRNA. For example, while mitochondrial Qri7/OSGEPL1 proteins perform the TC-transfer reaction, cytosolic TsaD/Kae1/OSGEP proteins do not function alone but assemble into structurally and mechanistically diverse TC-transfer machineries14,32. In bacteria, TsaD associates with TsaB to form a binary complex that supports tRNA binding and a basal t6A-catalytic activity that is critically enhanced by TsaE38–43. In yeast and humans, Kae1/OSGEP is integrated into the multi-subunit KEOPS complex that adopts a conserved linear arrangement of Gon7/GON7–Pcc1/LAGE3–Kae1/OSGEP–Bud32/PRPK–Cgi121/TPRKB (Fig. 1b)19,44–46. Notably, the Gon7/GON7 subunits are only presently found in certain fungi and metazoans31,44,46,47. In archaea and most eukaryotes, the four-subunit KEOPS dimerizes to form a winged architecture through a conserved Pcc1–Pcc1 interface48–51. Recently, a Pcc2 subunit has been identified as a paralog of Pcc1 in some archaea and functions analogously to Gon7/GON750,52. The structural integrity of KEOPS complexes is essential for the t6A modification function, which also depends on an ATP-to-ADP hydrolysis by the Bud32 subunits29,51,53–55. The recently reported cryo-EM structure of the archaean M. jannaschii KEOPS–tRNA complex was solved in a non-productive state with the modification base A37 of tRNA positioned outside the Kae1 catalytic pocket within the monomeric KEOPS complex56.

The 36-UAA-38 motif within the anticodon loop is universally required for recognition by TsaD/Kae1/Qri7 enzymes and t6A modification of full-length tRNAs (Fig. 1c)15,26,51,55,57. KEOPS exhibits additional sequence specificities on its tRNA substrates14. Mutations in the D-stem (G10–C25 base pair), anticodon loop (C32, U33, 36-UAA-38) or 3’ CCA end of tRNAs abolish t6A modification by the KEOPS complex32,51,55,56. Except for the predominant binding of the tRNA 3’ CCA end to Cgi12155,56, the structural basis for other key recognition elements—including G10C11 motif, C32, and 36-UAA-38—has remained unresolved. Despite the recent structural and functional insights into the KEOPS complex14,51,56, the molecular mechanisms that coordinate tRNA recognition, catalytic complex assembly, and Bud32 ATP hydrolysis to regulate Kae1 t6A catalysis remain to be elucidated.

Here, we report cryo-EM structures of the apo-form KEOPS dimer and its tRNA-bound complex, capturing a productive-like tRNA conformation with its modification site A37 positioned within the Kae1 active site through a distributed interaction network involving all four subunits. We show that tRNA binding triggers concerted structural rearrangements, including a large-scale inter-lobe rotation in Bud32 that activates t6A catalysis by Kae1. This study establishes Kae1, Bud32 and Pcc1 as the minimal catalytic core, with Cgi121 stabilizing tRNA binding and enhancing catalytic efficiency. The dimeric architecture of KEOPS creates additional contacts between the opposing Kae1 protomer and the tRNA anticodon loop, thereby priming its active conformation and boosting the t6A activity. Together, our findings reveal how the KEOPS complex engages tRNA and catalyzes t6A modification that is critically regulated by Bud32, providing a structural framework for understanding its essential cellular functions and the pathogenic mechanisms underlying disease-associated mutations.

Results

Symmetric architecture of the apo KEOPS dimer

We investigated the apo and tRNA-bound states of the Caenorhabditis elegans (Ce) KEOPS complex, which comprises four evolutionarily conserved subunits: KAE1 (K), BUD32 (B), CGI121 (C) and PCC1 (P) (Supplementary Fig. 1). Recombinant CeKEOPS and in vitro transcribed (IVT) CetRNAAsnGUU bearing an additional GG doublet to its 5’ terminus (generated during the transcription) were purified (Supplementary Fig. 2a–c) and used to reconstitute a functional t6A modification assay with the intermediate TC‑AMP generated from L-threonine, bicarbonate and ATP by CeYRDC (Fig. 1a; Supplementary Fig. 2d–f). To obtain a stable complex for structural analysis, CeKEOPS and CetRNAAsnGUU were incubated at a 1:2 molar ratio, and the assembled complex was isolated by size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) and the presence of four KEOPS subunits and tRNAAsnGUU was confirmed by Gel analyses (Fig. 1d). Single-particle cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) analysis of the SEC-purified sample yielded two distinct structures: an apo-form KEOPS dimer and a partial KEOPS dimer in complex with a single tRNA, at global resolutions of ~3.1 Å and ~3.4 Å, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 3; Fig. 1e–h). The cryo-EM structure of the apo KEOPS dimer reveals a symmetric P2K2B2C2 architecture (Fig. 1e–f), consistent with the ~200 kDa molecular weight estimated by SEC (Supplementary Fig. 2b). The dimer adopts a V-shaped architecture with two protomers related by a two-fold symmetry axis at the PCC1–PCC1 interface (Fig. 1f). KAE1, BUD32, and PCC1 exhibit high structural conservation across orthologs from H. sapiens (Hs), A. thaliana (At), S. cerevisiae (Sc), and M. jannaschii (Mj) (Supplementary Fig. 4a–c), with backbone root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) values for aligned Cα atoms typically below 2.5 Å. In contrast, CeCGI121 exhibits pronounced conformational divergence from its orthologs (Supplementary Fig. 4d). The overall subunit arrangements and interaction interfaces are conserved with those in MjKEOPS (Supplementary Fig. 4e) and AtKEOPS (Supplementary Fig. 4f). A notable divergence is observed in the relative positioning of the Bud32 C-lobes with respect to Kae1 (Supplementary Fig. 4e–f). The CeKEOPS dimer cannot be superimposed as a complete unit onto the MjKEOPS dimer (Supplementary Fig. 4g)48,49,56 or the AtKEOPS dimer (Supplementary Fig. 4h)51, highlighting divergence in quaternary assembly and structural diversity of the KEOPS complex across species.

Asymmetric tRNA binding to a partial KEOPS dimer lacking one BUD32–CGI121 module

The cryo-EM structure of the KEOPS–tRNAAsnGUU complex reveals a stoichiometrically defined assembly in which a single tRNA molecule binds to a partial KEOPS dimer lacking one BUD32–CGI121 module (Fig. 1g). The map resolves the overall architecture of CetRNAAsnGUU, including its acceptor arm, D-arm, anticodon arm, T-arm (Fig. 1h). Within the complex, the tRNA is nestled in a deep, composite binding cleft formed jointly by the six subunits of the KEOPS dimer (Fig. 1h). Specifically, the anticodon loop is cradled between two subdomains of KAE1, with additional support from PCC1 and KAE1 of the opposing protomer; the D-stem contacts KAE1; the acceptor arm directly interacts with BUD32; and the 3’ CCA end docks into a hydrophobic pocket of CGI121. A detailed analysis of these interactions, including residue–based distances and conservation patterns, is provided in Supplementary Fig. 5a and Supplementary Table 1. The total buried surface area between the bound tRNA and the KEOPS monomer is ~2808 Å2 in CeKEOPS–tRNAAsnGUU, increasing to ~2971 Å2 upon inclusion of the opposing protomer (Fig. 1h). This interface is nearly twice as large as the ~1426 Å2 interfacial area reported for the non-productive MjKEOPS–tRNALysUUU structure (Supplementary Fig. 5b)56, indicating substantially enhanced stability and specificity of tRNA binding in the CeKEOPS–tRNAAsnGUU complex. In the KEOPS–tRNA complex, the single BUD32–CGI121 dimer rearranges

tRNAAsnGUU binds along a positively charged, belt-like concave surface formed by phylogenetically conserved residues from all four subunits of the KEOPS (Fig. 2b), suggesting a conserved mechanism of tRNA recognition by the KEOPS complexes. Comparative structural analysis between the apo and tRNA-bound states reveals that tRNA engagement induces substantial conformational rearrangements throughout the complex (Fig. 2c). Most notably, BUD32 itself undergoes large-scale inter-lobe rearrangements, with its secondary structure elements translating by up to ~7 Å and rotating by ~40° (Fig. 2d). Nonetheless, two BUD32–CGI121 modules from the apo and the tRNA-bound states are well aligned with an RMSD of 1.5 Å over 284 Cα atoms (Fig. 2e), suggesting a concerted relocation of the BC module relative to the KAE1 upon tRNA binding. In contrast, only minimal structural changes are observed in the MjKEOPS complex upon tRNA binding (RMSD ≈ 0.66 Å over 301 Cα atoms)56. Structural comparison indicates that the distinct inter-lobe conformational change in BUD32 primarily mediates the pre-catalytic KEOPS–tRNA assembly, underscoring that we have captured a functionally critical state in the CeKEOPS–tRNAAsnGUU structure.

To dissect the functional roles of the individual subunits within the KEOPS complex, we purified recombinant PCC1, CGI121, and the subcomplexes that include PCC1–KAE1–BUD32 (PKB), PCC1–KAE1 (PK), and BUD32–CGI121 (BC) (Fig. 2f, g): PCC1 formed a stable homodimer and mediated the dimerization of both PKB and PK, yielding P2K2B2 and P2K2 complexes, respectively; BC formed a stable binary complex, whereas CGI121 alone was monomeric. We next evaluated the tRNA-binding properties of these KEOPS proteins using electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSA) with 6-Carboxyfluorescein (FAM)-labeled CetRNAAsnGUU (FAM-tRNAAsn) (Supplementary Fig. 2g; Fig. 2h). The full KEOPS complex bound FAM-tRNAAsn with a dissociation constant (Kd) of ~2.0 μM. Subcomplexes P2K2B2 and P2K2 exhibited comparable affinities (Kd ≈ 2.5 µM and 1.8 µM, respectively), while the binding affinity of the PCC1 dimer was weaker (Kd ≈ 6.0 μM). BC exhibited significantly strong tRNA binding affinity (Kd ≈ 0.5 μM), whereas CGI121 alone was incapable of tRNA binding (Supplementary Fig. 2g–i). These results indicate that both P2K2 and BC can independently bind tRNA, and that CGI121 acts cooperatively with BUD32 to enhance tRNA binding to the KEOPS complex. The P2K2B2 retained ~40% of the t6A activity of the full KEOPS complex, while the P2K2 was completely inactive (Fig. 2i, j). Together, these findings establish that PCC1 and BUD32 are essential for KAE1 catalytic function and that CGI121, although dispensable for basal tRNA binding, stabilizes the active conformation of the KEOPS–tRNA complex and potentiates t6A catalysis.

To functionally validate these interfaces, we generated mutations in KAE1 (R41L, R168A, R325A), BUD32 (K71A/K75A/R79A, K181A), and PCC1 (R54A/R55A, K79A/K83A) within the full KEOPS complex. The mutated complexes were purified to homogeneity and assessed for their tRNA binding and t6A modification activities (Supplementary Fig. 7a–f). We also designed and transcribed tRNAAsnGUU variants with selected mutations (G30, C32, U33, U36 and A38) or truncations (D-loop, T-loop, and 3’ CCA end), which were analyzed for folding by SEC and for their competence in t6A modification by CeKEOPS (Supplementary Fig. 8a, b). Combined with previous mutational validations (Supplementary Table 2), our mutational analyses identify key previously unrecognized determinants of t6A modification (Fig. 3a–c), including a critical interaction between KAE1 R41 and the tRNA anticodon loop (U35 and A38), and a primary engagement of the tRNA acceptor arm by the α1 helix of BUD32 (K71/K75/R79). Deletion of either the D-loop or the T-loop prevented proper folding of tRNA (Supplementary Fig. 8a), thereby leading to almost no t6A modification (Fig. 3c). Notably, the G30C mutation within the G30–C40 base pair also prevented proper folding of tRNAAsnGUU (Supplementary Fig. 8a). Compensatory double mutations G30C/C40G and G30U/C40A sustained proper folding, yet only G30U/C40A retained weak t6A modification (Fig. 3c). Removal of the 3’ CCA end reduced the t6A activity by ~40% (Fig. 3c), paralleling the effect of CGI121 depletion (Fig. 2j). Mutations of KAE1 R325A (contacting U28), BUD32 K181A (contacting G49) or PCC1 R54A/R55A (contacting C29G30G31) severely reduced t6A activity without affecting tRNA binding (Fig. 3b; Supplementary Fig. 7f). C32A caused a moderate reduction in t6A activity, while C32G had a negligible effect (Fig. 3c).

The extensive KEOPS–tRNA interaction network led us to examine how tRNA sequence variation influences modification efficiency. We therefore generated a panel of IVT ANN-decoding tRNAs (Fig. 3d), each confirmed to be properly folded by SEC (Supplementary Fig. 8c). Modification assays were carried out for 60 minutes, a time point at which the reaction had not reached full completion (Supplementary Fig. 2f), enabling comparative assessment of modification efficiencies across tRNA species. Their t6A modification activities varied substantially, ranging from ~35% for tRNAAsnGUU to ~5% for tRNAIleAAU, with corresponding Kd values spanning 2.0–7.6 μM (Fig. 3e). The generally low modification activities observed are likely due to the absence of other native modifications in these IVT tRNAs, which might contribute to stabilizing tRNA tertiary structure and facilitating recognition by KEOPS. Integrating these functional measurements with our structural and sequence analyses (Fig. 3a–e) and prior mutagenesis data (Supplementary Table 2) allowed us to delineate specific interaction pairs likely to modulate tRNA binding and t6A catalysis. These include G10/C11–D156/S205 (KAE1), G26–R165 (KAE1), U33–R50/Q51 (KAE1’ from the opposing protomer), A44–K230/E233 (BUD32), G49–K181 (BUD32) and G73–N95 (CGI121). Additionally, G30–C40 and G31–C39 base pairs appear to stabilize the anticodon stem, thereby promoting modification efficiency. Together, these results establish that KEOPS-dependent t6A modification is enabled by a distributed network of interactions across the tRNA structure, which collectively fine-tune binding affinity and catalytic output.

A distorted architecture of the tRNA anticodon hairpin in the KEOPS complex

The cryo-EM density of CetRNAAsnGUU is absent for U33, poor for G34 and U36, but well-defined for U35 and A37 within the 36-UAA-38 motif (Fig. 4a), a key determinant for t6A modification (Fig. 3c). In this motif, U36 and A37 are flipped out, while A38 adopts an inwardly folded orientation (Fig. 4a). Consistent with an open, fan-like configuration of the anticodon loop, no clear intra-loop H bonds could be determined at such resolutions. Nevertheless, a potential interaction may occur between the N6 of A38 and the N3/O2 of C32 (within a distance of 3.0 Å) (Fig. 4a), a pairing commonly observed in tRNA anticodon loops58. Structural comparison reveals distinct anticodon loop conformations among CetRNAAsnGUU, free MjtRNALysUUU55, MjKEOPS-bound tRNALysUUU56. Notably, A37 adopts a flipped-out conformation uniquely in the CeKEOPS-bound tRNAAsnGUU (Supplementary Fig. 5c). Despite overall similarity in tRNA tertiary structure and binding mode to KEOPS, local steric clashes are evident in the anticodon hairpins and 3’ CCA ends (Supplementary Fig. 5d), underscoring mutual conformational changes in both the KEOPS complex and tRNA. In both KEOPS-bound tRNA structures, a pronounced kink originates at the G30–C40 base pair, propagates through the anticodon loop, and results in a bent conformation with a fan-like extrusion of nucleotides from U33 to A37 (Fig. 4a; Supplementary Fig. 5c). This remodeling dislodges A37 from the loop and inserts its Watson–Crick edge into the KAE1 active site (Fig. 4b). Structural superposition of our CeKEOPS–tRNA structure with the MjKEOPS–tRNA structure reveals that a conserved loop (residues 35–41) connecting β3 and α1 in KAE1 would sterically clash with the anticodon loop of the aligned tRNALys (Fig. 4b). Notably, this β3–α1 loop in MjKae1 is disordered when bound to a near native tRNA but became structured upon binding a tRNA with a distorted anticodon loop56, suggesting an active role in anticodon loop remodeling. Asymmetric conformational rearrangements in one KAE1–PCC1 dimer promotes tRNA anticodon loop recognition

The cryo-EM densities for the dimerizing interfaces in both the apo and tRNA-bound states were well resolved and enabled a comparison of structural changes in two KAE1–PCC1 modules upon engagement of the tRNA anticodon arm (Fig. 4c). The PCC1’–KAE1’ module rotates by ~12° and translates up to 6 Å away from the tRNA-binding KAE1–PCC1 module; concomitantly, the bent α2 helix of PCC1 straightens, and its β3 and α1–β2 connecting loop (residues 50–57) remodel to accommodate the anticodon stem (Fig. 4c). These rearrangements position the nucleotides C32–U35 at the interface formed by α1 helix of KAE1, α2 helix of PCC1 and α1’ helix of KAE1’ (Fig. 4d). Notably, several specific residues of KAE1 and PCC1 subunits are reconfigured to coordinate the anticodon loop, including KAE1 Phe40, Arg41, Thr43 and Gln47, PCC1 Arg54, Lys83 and Asn87, and KAE1’ Arg50 and Gln51 (Fig. 4d). Specifically, Thr43 and Arg41 from the β3–α1 loop of KAE1 participate in stabilizing the U35 phosphate and A38 ribose, respectively; KAE1 Phe40 stacks against the base of A37; PCC1 Arg54 and Lys83 are repositioned to stabilize U35 base and U33 phosphate; KAE1’ Arg50 and Gln51 engage the backbone phosphate of U33. In this reconfigured state, U35 and A37 are positioned near the entrance to the KAE1 active site, at the interface between its β3–α1 and β4–α3 loops; the base of U35 protrudes into a cavity formed by the KAE1 α3 helix and the PCC1 α1–β2 loop; U36 is oriented toward a void between KAE1 and PCC1; A37 inserts into the KAE1 active site, where it is sandwiched between Asn161 and Phe40, and is further stabilized by a metal ion. Collectively, the asymmetric rearrangements within the KAE1–PCC1 modules generate a binding geometry that cradles nucleotides U33–A37, with reconstituted contacts from both subunits stabilizing the loop and positioning A37 deep within the KAE1 active site.

Structural basis for t6A catalysis by KAE1 through coordinated recognition of tRNA A37 and TC-AMP

In our cryo-EM maps, we observed a distinct density peak adjacent to residues His111, His115, S134 and Asp296 of KAE1 (Supplementary Fig. 9a, b), a conserved metal-binding sphere in all TsaD/Kae1/Qri7 enzymes (Supplementary Fig. 4a). Modeling a divalent cation such as Zn2+, Fe2+ or Mg2+ into this density did not yield satisfactory metal–ligand coordination geometry59. Given the presence of KCl in the sample buffer, we tentatively assigned this density to a potassium ion (K+) to reflect the presence of a bound metal ion. Our KEOPS–tRNA complex structure was obtained in the absence of the TC-AMP intermediate. We computationally docked TC-AMP into the KAE1 active site (Supplementary Fig. 9c) using the coordinates of PDB 6Z8137. The resulting model positions TC-AMP perpendicular to tRNA A37 within the KAE1 active site without introducing steric clashes (Fig. 5a, b). TC-AMP occupies a deeply buried concave region, while tRNA A37 is situated near the positively-charged entrance of the pocket (Supplementary Fig. 9d). This arrangement is compatible with a sequential binding model for the two substrates. Notably, the N6 atom of A37 is positioned within ~2.4 Å of the TC carbonyl carbon of TC-AMP (Fig. 5b), suggesting a catalytically competent binding configuration. Structural comparison reveals that tRNA A37 binding was accompanied by rearrangements in the metal coordination sphere and a repositioning of the metal ion toward the N6 atom of A37 (Supplementary Fig. 9e–f). The spatial proximity of His111, Ser134, Asp296, and the metal ion suggests a collective role in stabilizing the reaction intermediate, potentially facilitating nucleophilic attack on the TC-AMP carbonyl carbon during catalysis (Fig. 5c). Our mutagenesis studies confirmed the functional importance of these residues (Fig. 5d). BUD32 allosterically regulates t6A-catalysis of KAE1 via a distant ATP hydrolysis-driven structural remodeling

Previous studies have established that both the ATPase activity and the C-terminal tail of Bud32 are essential for t6A catalytic function of KEOPS (Supplementary Table 2), yet the mechanistic link of ATP hydrolysis in Bud32 to t6A catalysis in Kae1 has remained unclear. Since neither of our CeKEOPS structures (apo or tRNA-bound) contained a bound ATP or ADP in the BUD32 active site, we computationally docked an ATP analog AMPPNP in CeBUD32 active site using the coordinates PDB 6WQX (Supplementary Fig. 4b)61. The model shows that ATP can be compatibly bound in the BUD32 active site (Supplementary Fig. 9g; Fig. 5e), which is ∼32 Å away from the t6A catalytic site in KAE1 (Fig. 5a; Supplementary Fig. 9h). This long range suggests allosteric regulation rather than direct chemical participation of ATP hydrolysis in stimulating t6A catalysis. Comparative structural analysis indicates that tRNA binding induces large-scale conformational shift in BUD32 (Fig. 2d), repositioning its ATP-binding site closer to the α8 and α9 helices KAE1 (Supplementary Fig. 9g). This rearrangement enables KAE1 residues—Glu245, Glu248, Arg249 in α8 and Arg282 in α9—to approach the remodeled KAE1/BUD32 interface (Fig. 5e), consistent with Kae1’s proposed role in stimulating Bud32 ATPase activity51,54,56. Structural comparison further reveals that the C-terminal α7 helix of BUD32 undergoes a marked conformational change upon tRNA binding (Supplementary Fig. 9h). Lys237 and Lys240 within α7 helix likely contact the tRNA bases of U27 and U28 (Fig. 5f), implicating this helix in stabilizing the tRNA anticodon stem. Additionally, the structurally disordered C-terminal tail (241-RDMIG-245) extending from α7 points toward the t6A catalytic site of KAE1 (Supplementary Fig. 9h; Fig. 5f). This conserved C-terminal tail may interact with residues Thr324, Gln324 and Arg325 in the α11–α12 loop of KAE1, located at the periphery of the t6A catalytic center (Fig. 5f). The functional importance of this region is supported by findings that deletion of the equivalent C-terminal tail (Gln373–Asp386) in yeast Kae1 leads to growth defects48. Thus, the BUD32 α7 helix and its flexible C-terminal tail could serve as a structural conduit, coupling conformational changes driven by ATP hydrolysis in BUD32 to the t6A catalytic site in KAE1.

To test this model, we generated mutants targeting the ATP-binding site (KBD153A/N158ACP), the α7 C-terminal tail (KBR239ACP, KBR239stopCP), and the α1 helix (KBK71A/K75A/R79ACP) (Supplementary Fig. 7a). Using an in-solution NADPH-coupled ATPase assay, we measured initial ATP hydrolysis rates (V0) and derived kinetic parameters―maximum velocity (Vmax), Michaelis constant (Km), turnover number (kcat) and catalytic efficiency (kcat/Km)―for each mutant in the presence and absence of tRNAAsnGUU (Fig. 5g), confirming the effect of mutation on ATP hydrolysis (Supplementary Fig. 9i). The t6A modification activities of these mutants corroborated their functional defects (Fig. 5h). The ATPase-dead mutant KBD153A/N158ACP exhibited a severe reduction of ~80% in t6A catalytic activity. Notably, the C-tail truncation mutant KBR239stopCP retained ATPase activity comparable to wild-type KEOPS but showed a complete loss of t6A activity, uncoupling ATP hydrolysis from productive catalysis. While tRNA binding enhanced the ATPase activity of KEOPS, PKB, and KBR239stopCP, it failed to stimulate the KBK71A/K75A/R79ACP mutant (Fig. 5g). This result indicates that tRNA stimulation requires interaction between the tRNA acceptor arm and BUD32 α1 helix, specifically through Arg79, which directly contacts tRNA C66 (Fig. 5f).

To probe energy coupling more directly, we established a simplified assay using 2 μM KEOPS, 20 μM IVT tRNAAsnGUU and ~20 μM pre-synthesized TC-AMP isolated from the YRDC-mediated assay (Supplementary Fig. 2d), supplemented with 200 μM ATP, ADP, or AMPPNP (Supplementary Fig. 7g; Fig. 5i). While the t6A levels were lower than in full reconstitution assays, likely due to insufficient supply and instability of TC-AMP62, ATP boosted modification from ~3% (no nucleotide) to ~12%. This stimulation was absent in the ATPase-dead mutant (KBD153A/N158ACP). Neither ADP nor AMPPNP enhanced activity; instead, both slightly inhibited the basal modification level (Fig. 5i). These results indicate that the free energy from ATP hydrolysis, rather than mere binding, is required to drive the conformational remodeling essential for efficient t6A catalysis.

A conserved dimeric four-helical bundle in TsaD/Kae1/Qri7 enzymes mediates tRNA anticodon loop recognition

The t6A catalytic subunits of the TsaD/Kae1/Qri7 family engage evolutionarily distinct partners—TsaD–TsaB in bacteria, Kae1–Pcc1 in archaea and eukaryotic cytosol, or a Qri7 homodimer in fungal mitochondria—through a structurally conserved four-helical interface14,33. Disruption of this interface universally abolishes t6A activity, underscoring its essential role across diverse systems49,50,54,57,63. Remarkably, this requirement has been overcome by the monomeric OSGEPL1 in human mitochondria64,65. Our CeKEOPS–tRNA structure reveals that this conserved interface serves as the platform for the tRNA anticodon arm engagement (Fig. 4c, d). We generated structural models for bacterial TsaDB–tRNA (Fig. 6g) and fungal Qri7–tRNA (Fig. 6h) by superposing CeKAE1–tRNA onto EcTsaDB heterodimer39 and ScQri7 homodimer63, respectively. Both models position the tRNA molecule in a catalytically compatible arrangement, with A37 inserted into the respective active sites and the anticodon loop contacting the conserved interface. In TsaDB, the TsaD helix α8 (Ser212–Asp224) likely engages the D-stem, while the extended C-terminal loop of TsaB potentially contacts the anticodon loop (Fig. 6g). In the Qri7 homodimer, one protomer’s helix α6 (Ala259–Lys270) likely contacts the D-stem, while the opposing protomer’s β3–α1 loop is positioned to interact with the anticodon loop (Fig. 6h), consistent with an asymmetrical binding of one molecule of tRNA to a Qri7 homodimer63. To functionally validate these predictions, we purified EcTsaDB heterodimer and ScQri7 homodimer (Supplementary Fig. 10f) and evaluated their t6A modification activity toward CetRNAAsnGUU and its anticodon loop variants (Supplementary Fig. 10g, h). Both EcTsaDB (plus TsaE) and ScQri7 modified CetRNAAsnGUU efficiently (Fig. 6i, j). Notably, mutations that impaired CeKEOPS activity—G30U/C40A, C32A, and G30C/C40G—produced similar defects in EcTsaDB and ScQri7. These results establish that the four-helical interface mediates a universal tRNA recognition mechanism across domains of life, with higher eukaryotes representing an evolutionary exception where mitochondrial OSGEPL1 has diverged to function independently.

To define the structural framework of GAMOS pathogenesis, we analyzed the conservation of the clinically identified GAMOS-associated mutations across eukaryotes (Supplementary Table 3) and mapped the conserved sites onto our CeKEOPS–tRNA structure (Fig. 7b). This analysis reveals that pathogenic mutations are not randomly distributed; instead, they cluster at key evolutionarily conserved functional hotspots: the catalytic sites of KAE1 and BUD32, the tRNA 3’ CCA end binding pocket of CGI121, and critical subunit interfaces. Integrating our structural analysis with previous mutational validations on KEOPS function (Supplementary Table 2), we find that GAMOS mutations likely exert their pathogenic effects through multiple converging mechanisms: destabilizing inter-subunit interactions, compromising tRNA binding affinity, disrupting active site architecture of OSGEP and PRPK, and uncoupling the allosteric communication between PRPK’s ATPase activity and OSGEP’s t6A catalytic function (Fig. 7b).

Discussion

We present cryo-EM structures of the apo KEOPS dimer and a partial dimer captured in a pre-catalytic, tRNA-bound state. The cryo‑EM densities for several nucleotide bases within the anticodon loop of the bound tRNAAsn are limited. Specifically, the densities for C32, U33, G34, U36, and A38 are weak or absent, precluding unambiguous determination of their precise conformations (Fig. 4a). To enable structural interpretation, we modeled the complete anticodon loop using a prediction‑based template and validated the key interactions through extensive mutational analyses. Despite these local-resolution constraints, the overall architecture enabled us to define the precise roles of each subunit and to decipher the catalytic mechanism of Kae1, including its allosteric regulation by Bud32’s ATPase activity. We demonstrate how a conserved four-helical interface enables universal tRNA recognition, reveal that a distributed interaction network governs substrate discrimination, establish CGI121 as a modular efficiency enhancer with species-dependent essentiality, and provide a structural model for understanding GAMOS pathogenesis. Integrated with comprehensive biochemical validation, these structures establish a unified framework for t6A modification by the conserved KEOPS complex, reconciling prior biochemical and genetic data (Supplementary Table 2).

Critically, our CeKEOPS–tRNAAsn structure captures a productive-like binding mode in which the tRNA engages the KEOPS dimer with its anticodon loop distorted, flipping U33–A37 outward and positioning A37 deep within the KAE1 active site (Fig. 4a; Fig. 5b). The backbone phosphate of U35 contacts Thr43, while G34 is accommodated in a spacious cleft between KAE1 and PCC1 (Fig. 4d). This configuration explains the tolerance for diverse chemical modifications at the wobble position, clarifying how t6A modification proceeds on a broad set of ANN-decoding tRNAs1,3,10,13,14. Within the KAE1 active site, A37 is stabilized by Phe40, Asn161, and a bound metal ion (Fig. 4d). Docking of TC-AMP positions its carbonyl carbon atom within ~2.4 Å of the N6 atom of tRNA A37, consistent with a catalytically competent arrangement poised for nucleophilic attack (Fig. 5b). Given that the exocyclic N6 amino group of tRNA A37 is not protonated under physiological conditions, TC-transfer may proceed without proton abstraction from A37 or protonation of the departing AMP. Instead, catalysis is likely driven by precise substrate positioning, metal coordination, and electrostatic stabilization of the transition state. Asp296 contributes to metal coordination and active site electrostatics, while Ser134 may stabilize a tetrahedral intermediate. Consistent with this model, H111A/H115A and D296A mutations abolish t6A activity (Fig. 5d), in agreement with the t6A catalytic dependence on the conserved metal-coordination HHD motif of the TsaD/Kae1/Qri7 family enzymes (Supplementary Table 2).

A crucial finding is the inter-lobe rotation of BUD32 upon tRNA binding (Fig. 2d), which repositions its ATP-binding cleft toward KAE1 and enables interaction between the γ-phosphate of ATP and KAE1’s α8/α9 helices (Fig. 5e; Supplementary Fig. 9g). This provides a structural basis for the well-documented observations that tRNA binding potentiates Bud32’s ATPase activity and that Kae1 is required for this activation51,54–56. The α7 helix of BUD32 directly contacts the tRNA anticodon stem, while its unresolved C-terminal tail points toward the KAE1 active site (Supplementary Fig. 9h). This orientation, together with the severe catalytic defect upon tail truncation (Fig. 5h), supports its proposed role as a structural conduit for energy transduction51,55,56. The ATPase-dead mutant (KBD153A/N158ACP) loses most activity (Fig. 5h), and only ATP—not ADP or AMPPNP—enhances modification under TC-AMP-limited conditions (Fig. 5i), indicating that hydrolysis energy, not mere binding, is required. Given the ~32 Å separation between the BUD32 and KAE1 active sites (Fig. 5a; Supplementary Fig. 9h), this energy likely drives a conformational reset—possibly facilitating t6A-tRNA release or active site reconfiguration—via the C-terminal tail, coupling the ATPase cycle to catalytic turnover.

The four-helical bundle interface between KAE1 and PCC1 stabilizes the tRNA anticodon loop, an architectural principle conserved across bacterial TsaD–TsaB and fungal Qri7 homodimer (Fig. 6g–j), with human mitochondrial OSGEPL1 as a monomeric outlier65. This illustrates striking functional convergence: evolutionarily distinct partners have adapted to present the tRNA anticodon loop to the catalytic core14,31. PCC1-mediated dimerization is functionally cooperative: the opposing KAE1’ provides stabilizing contacts to the bound tRNA (Fig. 6c), explaining why dimerization is essential for full activity in CeKEOPS (Fig. 6f), AtKEOPS51, and PaKEOPS50. The five-subunit yeast and human KEOPS complexes may use Gon7/GON7 analogously19,45. Our biochemical data indicate that all four subunits of AtKEOPS are present at equimolar ratios in the presence of tRNA (Fig.1d), and our model supports a 2:2 stoichiometry for tRNA binding to a fully assembled KEOPS dimer (Fig. 6a). However, the cryo-EM structure captured only one tRNA molecule bound to a partial dimer lacking one BUD32–CGI121 module (Fig. 1g). This asymmetry likely results from dissociation of the tRNA-bound BUD32–CGI121 binary complex during sample preparation, as also observed in the AtKEOPS dimer structure51. Nonetheless, in the ScQri7 homodimer bound to only a single tRNA molecule (Fig. 6h), one protomer is catalytically active while the other provides essential structural support63. Thus, such asymmetry may reflect intrinsic conformational dynamics and represent a transient catalytic intermediate during the KEOPS catalytic cycle.

KEOPS recognizes full-length and properly folded tRNA through a distributed network where each subunit contacts specific domains (Fig. 3a), ensuring precise selection, stable binding, and correct anticodon loop positioning. We validate key determinants (Fig. 3c, Supplementary Table 1): the β3–α1 loop of KAE1 uses Arg41 to contact A38 and U35, remodeling the anticodon loop and flipping A37 into the active site; the conserved G10–C25 base pair is specifically coordinated by conserved Asp156 and Arg165 of KAE1; the essential G30–C40 base pair is recognized by PCC1 Arg54, linking dimerization to substrate engagement; BUD32 α1 helix engages acceptor stem C66 via Arg79, stabilizing tRNA and mediating ATPase stimulation (Fig. 5f–g). These findings mechanistically explain two long-established determinants—the G10–C25 base pair and the 36-UAA-38 motif15,26.

The role of Cgi121 in KEOPS function presents a compelling case of evolutionary adaptation. While the PCC1–KAE1–BUD32 core supports basal tRNA binding, catalysis, and ATP hydrolysis, CGI121 acts as an efficiency factor by engaging the 3’ CCA end (Fig. 2h) and promoting both BUD32’s ATPase activity and overall t6A catalytic output (Fig. 5g; Fig. 2j). Consistent with structural divergence among Cgi121/CGI121 orthologs (Supplementary Fig. 4c), the functional dependence of KEOPS on this subunit varies markedly: MjKEOPS strictly requires Cgi12155,56; PaKEOPS, ScKEOPS, and AtKEOPS retain partial activity without51,53,54,63. Most strikingly, the Drosophila KEOPS complex―composed solely of Kae1, Bud32 and Pcc1―has evolved to function entirely without a Cgi121 ortholog29,31. The moderate activity reduction in Cgi121-deficient complexes likely becomes limiting under high translational demand or stress, explaining why yeast Cgi121 is dispensable in optimal growth but essential under stress44,53. This context-dependent essentiality provides an explanation for why mutations in human TPRKB can lead to the tissue-specific neurological disorder GAMOS (Fig. 7b; Supplementary Table 3). Conversely, the complete loss of a Cgi121 ortholog in Drosophila implies compensatory adaptations. Thus, Cgi121 is a modular efficiency enhancer whose essentiality reflects organism-specific kinetic and regulatory demands.

Our human KEOPS–tRNA model validates conserved subunit interactions (Supplementary Table 2) and suggests the disordered GON7 C-terminus may contact the tRNA anticodon arm (Fig. 7a). GAMOS-associated mutations map to evolutionarily conserved residues that cluster at key functional hotspots: the KAE1 and BUD32 active sites, the CGI121 tRNA-binding pocket, and critical interfaces (Fig. 7b; Supplementary Table 3). These observations provide a unified structural framework for GAMOS pathogenesis, wherein diverse mutations converge to impair KEOPS–tRNA assembly, allosteric coordination, or catalytic capability. Even modest reductions in t6A modification efficiency caused by mutations in human KEOPS subunits—tolerable in less metabolically active tissues—may become limiting in neurons, where precise and rapid translation of codon-biased mRNAs is critical. Over time, this translational deficit could impair the production of key proteins required for neuronal survival and function, culminating in the microcephaly and neurological deficits characteristic of GAMOS19,66.

Methods

Cloning and site-directed mutagenesis

The chemically synthesized DNAs encoding Caenorhabditis elegans (Ce) YRDC (UniProt: Q9NAF8), CeKAE1 (UniProt: Q9BL28), CeBUD32 (UniProt: B5WWL2), CeCGI121 (UniProt: O44566) and CePCC1 (UniProt: Q21019) were inserted into the expression vectors pET21a and pET24a using ligation cloning. We constructed plasmids of pET21a–CeYRDC-6His, pET24a–CePCC1–BUD32–CGI121–KAE1-6His, pET21a–CePCC1–BUD32–KAE1-6His, pET21a–CePCC1–KAE1-6His, pET21a–CeBUD32–CGI121-6His, pET21a–CeCGI121-6His and pET21a–CePCC1-6His, which express six histidine (6His)-tagged YRDC, KEOPS, PCC1–KAE1–BUD32, KAE1–PCC1, BUD32–CGI121, CGI121 and PCC1, respectively. Site-directed mutagenesis was performed using the Ready-to-Use Seamless Cloning Kit (Sangon Biotech) following the manufacturer’s instructions. All constructs were verified by DNA sequencing. The primers for site-directed mutagenesis are listed in Supplementary Table 4.

Overexpression and purification of proteins

The plasmids were transformed into E. coli Rosetta (DE3) pLysS or BL21 (DE3) pLysS for protein expression. The transformed cells were grown at 37 °C to OD600 0.6–0.8 and induced by the addition of 0.5 mM IPTG for 2.5–3.5 h. Cells were harvested by centrifugation, the pellet was resuspended in lysis buffer containing 20 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 200 mM NaCl and 5 mM β-Mercaptoethanol. Chilled cells were lysed by sonification. The lysate was centrifuged at 15,000 × g. The supernatant was applied to Ni2+-NTA resins for immobilized metal affinity chromatography (IMAC) purification. Ni2+-NTA-bound recombinant proteins were eluted out upon addition of lysis buffer in the presence of increasing concentration (100–400 mM) of imidazole. The proteins in the eluted fractions were analyzed by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE). The fractions containing pure recombinant proteins were applied to size exclusion chromatography (SEC) on a Hiload 16/600 Superdex 200 pg column (GE Healthcare) with lysis buffer. SEC fractions containing proteins were analyzed by SDS–PAGE and concentrated using centrifugal filters (Amicon). ScKEOPS, AtKEOPS complex and EcTsaDB (and TsaE) were from our lab stock which were expressed and purified from E. coli Rosetta (DE3) cells43,51. ScQri7 was expressed in E. coli Rosetta (DE3) cells using a chemically synthesized plasmid pET28a–ScQri7-6His. The concentrations of proteins were spectroscopically measured by NanoDrop2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Purified proteins were used immediately or stored at –80 °C.

In vitro run-off transcription and purification of tRNAs

Gene ID and DNA sequences of Caenorhabditis elegans tRNAs were retrieved from the NCBI database and listed in Supplementary Table 5. We performed an in vitro T7 run-off transcription of CetRNAs using chemically synthesized DNAs. Forward and reverse DNA primers were designed and synthesized based on the tRNA sequences. The forward primer contained the T7 RNA polymerase recognition sequence (TAATACGACTCACTATA) followed by a GGG triplet (including 5’ Gs in the native sequence) to promote transcription initiation, which generates an extra G, GG, or GGG to the 5’ terminus of the transcript. The tRNA transcription template was prepared by PCR amplification using these primers. Run–off transcription was carried out using T7 RNA polymerase at 30 °C for 8 h in a reaction mixture containing 120 ng/μL tRNA transcription template, 40 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 5 mM NTP mix, 5 mM Dithiothreitol (DTT), 1 mM spermidine, 0.5% (v/v) Triton X-100, 33 mM MgCl2, 3 μM T7 RNA polymerase, 10 μM pyrophosphatase and 15 mM GMP. The mixture was applied to SEC purification using a Superdex 75 Increase 10/300 GL column (GE Healthcare) in refolding buffer contained 50 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 5 mM MgCl₂ and 100 mM KCl. The folded fractions were collected and precipitated with 75% (v/v) ethanol. The pellet was centrifuged, air-dried and resuspended in refolding buffer. The tRNA concentration was determined using Nanodrop2000. tRNAs were used immediately or stored at –80 °C.

Cryo-EM data collection, processing, model building, and refinement

3.5 μL of the SEC-purified CeKEOPS–tRNAAsnGUU complex at a concentration of 100 μg/mL was meticulously dispensed onto an ANT Au 300 R1.2/1.3 grid that was treated with glow discharge. Using a Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with the chamber humidity set at 100% and temperature maintained at 4 °C, the sample underwent blotting for 20 s before being rapidly plunged into liquid ethane. Data acquisition was performed on a Thermo Fisher Scientific Titan Krios 300 kV transmission electron microscope equipped with a Gatan Bioquantum K3 direct electron camera. The EPU 1.24.2.2782 software was employed to conduct operations in super-resolution mode, with a magnification of 105 kx, a pixel size of 0.83 Å, and a total of 40 frames captured with an exposure time of 3 seconds, and the defocus were set within the range of -0.8 to -2.2 μm.

All raw images were processed using cryoSPARC v4.267,68. First, MotionCor2 was employed for motion correction with a binning factor set to 269. Subsequently, CTFFIND4 was utilized to perform contrast transfer function (CTF) fitting70. Ultimately, 18,984 images were retained for the subsequent single-particle reconstruction. Approximately 100 particles were manually selected for 2D classification, and the superior classes were used as templates for automated particle picking, resulting in the selection of ~6,712,533 particles. Through multiple rounds of 2D classification, ~1,045,693 high-quality particles were screened out to generate a low-resolution initial model. Subsequent 3D classification using ~241,539 and ~123,388 particles yielded a 3D reconstruction for an apo-form KEOPS complex and a 3D reconstruction for a tRNA-bound KEOPS complex, respectively. Finally, each structure underwent an additional round of 3D auto-refinement to generate the final density maps. All cryo-EM maps produced during this process were evaluated for resolution using the gold-standard Fourier shell correlation (FSC) criterion of 0.143.

The AlphaFold3 prediction models of KAE1 (AF ID: Q9BL28), BUD32 (AF ID: B5WWL2), CGI121 (AF ID: O44566), PCC1 (AF ID: Q21019), and tRNAAsnGUU (sequence-based prediction model)71 were used to dock into the cryo-EM maps of the apo-form KEOPS complex and the tRNA-bound KEOPS complex using Dock-in-Map in PHENIX 1.20.1 suite72, followed by iterative manual model building with Coot 0.9.873 and real space refinement in PHENIX 1.20.1 suite72. The regions with poor cryo-EM densities were modeled with reference to the structures of HsKEOPS19,61, AtKEOPS51, ScKEOPS45 and MjKEOPS48,56. The cryo-EM density map of the KEOPS–tRNA complex enabled template-guided modeling of the backbone and most nucleobases of CetRNAAsnGUU. However, densities for several regions—including nucleotides U16–A17 and G19–C20 in the D-arm, G31 and U33 in the anticodon stem, U55–C56 in the elbow region, and C61–C62–A63 in the T-arm—were weak and could not be unambiguously interpreted. These nucleobases were instead modeled using coordinates derived from a structural prediction template. Final models were validated using Molprobity in PHENIX 1.20.1 suite72.

Enzymatic reconstitution of tRNA t6A and quantification of t6A modification

TC-AMP was generated in a 10 μL reaction mixture containing 2 μM CeYRDC, 2 mM L-threonine, 5 mM NaHCO3, 1 mM ATP, 5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM TECP, 200 mM NaCl and 20 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5. Reactions proceeded at 25 °C for 10 min, with TC-AMP production and efficiency monitored by liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS). For preparation of TC-AMP compound, multiple injections of 100 μL TC-AMP reaction mixtures were chromatographed by HPLC using a C18 column (250 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm, Agilent). The collected fractions containing TC-AMP were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and subjected to freeze-drying in a lyophilizer (Beijing Sihuan Technology). The stability of TC-AMP compound was verified by LC–MS.

To establish the tRNA t6A enzymatic reaction system, 20 μM tRNA substrate and 2 μM CeKEOPS complex were added to the TC-AMP reaction mixture containing 5 μM CeYRDC, followed by incubation at 30 °C for 1 hour. tRNAs were isolated using 12% polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis supplemented with 8 M UERA to remove proteins and other excess reactants, and extracted with 0.5 M NaAc pH 5.2. The purified tRNAs were digested into mononucleotides using 0.1 U/mL nuclease P1 (Sigma) and 0.1 U/mL alkaline phosphatase (Sigma) at 37 °C overnight. The digestion samples were heated at 95 °C for 5 min and centrifuged to remove the enzymes and applied to LC–MS for of t6A analysis. Nucleosides were separated on a C18 column (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm, Agilent) using mobile phase A (0.1% trifluoroacetic acid in water) and mobile phase B (methanol). The elution protocol was as follows: 5% B for 5 min, followed by a linear gradient from 5 to 40% B over 15 min, and a subsequent gradient from 40 to 98% B over 5 min. UV absorbance at 254 nm was used to detect eluted nucleosides, whose retention time and mass-to-charge ratios (m/z) were recorded using OpenLAB CDS ChemStation (Agilent). Peak areas were integrated and used to quantify the molar amounts of A, U, C, G and t6A, using standard curves generated from the calibrations using standards of A, U, C, G and t6A. The percentage of t6A was calculated as the molar ratio of t6A to A (t6A/A), normalized to the total number of A in the tRNA substrate (NA − 1). The values of t6A modification efficiency were additionally normalized using the average molar content of C, U and G as the internal controls. Final t6A modification efficiency was reported as the mean from four independent replicate.

Electrophoretic mobility shift assay

For electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA), we chemically synthesized CetRNAs with a 6-carboxyfluorescein (6-FAM) group attached to the 5’ G1, leading to FAM-tRNAs, which were refolded before use. 0.5 μM FAM-tRNA was incubated with varying concentrations of CeKEOPS protein (0–10 μM) in electrophoresis buffer (50 mM Tris–base pH 8.5, 50 mM glycine) containing 20% (v/v) glycerol. Samples were resolved on 2% (w/v) non-denaturing agarose gels prepared in the same buffer. Electrophoresis was carried out at 100 V for 30 minutes at 4 °C in pre-chilled buffer. FAM-tRNA was visualized using a Pharos FX Molecular Imager (Bio-Rad) with 488 nm excitation. Band intensities of free and protein-bound tRNA were quantified using ImageJ 1.52a. The ratio of bound tRNA to total tRNA was plotted against protein concentration, and dissociation constant (Kd) was calculated by fitting the data to a single-site binding isotherm using nonlinear least squares regression equation. All measurements were performed independently in triplicate.

ATPase assay

The hydrolysis of ATP to ADP by KEOPS complex was measured using an NADH-coupled ATPase assay in which the consumption of NADH is proportional to the production of ADP. The reaction mix contained 2 μM KEOPS or variants, 0–50 μM ATP, 10 μM tRNAAsnGUU, 10 mM MgCl2, 4 mM phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP), 6 U/mL pyruvate kinase (PK), 9 U/mL lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), 0.5 mM NADH in buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 50 mM KCl and 1 mM DTT). Reactions were performed in 96-well plates at 25 °C. The consumption of NADH at absorbance at 340 nm was recorded at 30-second intervals for 60 minutes using a MULTISKAN GO microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The initial reaction rate of ATP to ADP hydrolysis was determined as the slope based on a standard curve with known concentrations of ADP and their corresponding absorbance changes at 340 nm. The initial reaction rates of KEOPS at varying concentrations of ATP were fitted to the Michaelis–Menten equation to determine kinetic parameters (Vmax and Km). The catalytic turnover number (kcat) was calculated by dividing Vmax by the protein concentration. All measurements were performed in triplicates.

Bioinformatics, sequence analysis and structure representations

Protein sequence alignments were performed using Clustal Omega74. C. elegans tRNA sequences and secondary structure annotations for were retrieved from the GtRNAdb 2.0 database75. Sequence logos for tRNA alignments were generated with WebLogo (http://weblogo.berkeley.edu). Cryo-EM density maps were visualized, and figures were prepared using UCSF ChimeraX 1.976 and PyMOL 3.1.6.1 (Schrödinger, LLC). Structural superpositions and root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) calculations were performed with PyMOL 3.1.6.1. Electrostatic potential surfaces were calculated and displayed using UCSF ChimeraX 1.9. Residue conservation was mapped onto molecular surfaces using the ConSurf server77.
