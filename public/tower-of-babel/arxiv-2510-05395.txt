On Hardy spaces, univalent functions and the second coefficient
Martin Chuaqui, Iason Efraimidis, Rodrigo Hernández

Abstract
. We consider normalized univalent functions with prescribed second Taylor coefficient a 2 a_{2} . For convex functions f f we study the Hardy spaces to which f f and f ′ f^{\prime} belong, refining in particular on a theorem of Eenigenburg and Keogh, and give a sharp asymptotic estimate and an explicit uniform bound for their coefficients. Relating the lower order of a convex function to the angle at infinity of its range we deduce that its range lies always in some sector of aperture | a 2 | ​ π |a_{2}|\pi . We give sharp smoothness conditions on the boundary for convex functions with prescribed second coefficient. We find the sharp Hardy space estimates for f f and f ′ f^{\prime} when f f belongs to other geometric subclasses, such as those of starlike, close-to-convex, convex in one direction, convex in the positive direction and typically real funtions. We extend a theorem of Lohwater, Piranian and Rudin, in which a univalent function whose derivative has radial limits almost nowhere is constructed, by showing that this pathological behavior can be obtained for any prescribed value of the second coefficient, in particular, manifesting itself arbitrarily close to the Koebe function.

Let
{\mathbb{D}}
denote the unit disk and

f(z)=z+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n},\qquad z\in{\mathbb{D}}.

In view of the implications of Bieberbach’s inequality
|a_{2}|\leq 2
in covering, growth and distortion theorems, among others, it is of interest to study constraint extremal problems having
|a_{2}|
as a prescribed parameter. Results on problems of this kind appeared as early as 1920 with Gronwall’s paper
[

]
, while a list of references, inevitably incomplete, is
[

The integral means of a function

are typically denoted by

M_{p}(r,f)=\left\{\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}|f(re^{i\theta})|^{p}d\theta\right\}^{1/p},\qquad p>0,

and its maximum modulus by

M_{\infty}(r,f)=\max_{|z|=r}|f(z)|.

The Hardy space
H^{p}
is defined as the space of holomorphic functions
f
in
{\mathbb{D}}
satisfying

\sup_{r<1}M_{p}(r,f)<\infty,

for
0<p\leq\infty
. The study of the integral means of univalent functions and their derivatives is a classical topic. For example, in 1927 Prawitz showed that
S\subset H^{p}
for all
p<1/2
. One can find a wealth of information on the growth of the means of functions in
S
in Pommerenke’s book
[
40

, while Baernstein’s celebrated inequality

M_{p}(r,f)\leq M_{p}(r,k),\qquad r\in(0,1),

for all
p\in(0,\infty)
and
f\in S
, where
k(z)=\frac{z}{(1-z)^{2}}
is the Koebe function, can be found in Chapter 7 of Duren’s book
[
10
]
. Further references on this subject, including characterizations of univalent functions in Hardy spaces in terms of geometric properties of their range, as well as applications to Operator Theory, can be found in
[
3
,
7
,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

.

In this article we go a step further by analyzing the integral means of univalent functions with
prescribed

|a_{2}|
, a question not addressed in the literature. We provide a comprehensive study of Hardy space estimates for
f
and
f^{\prime}
when

On the other hand, we show that certain pathological behavior of the derivative of a univalent function, first shown to exist by Lohwater, Piranian and Rudin, can be realized arbitrarily close to the Koebe function.

Convex functions, Hardy spaces and coefficients.
Our starting point is a theorem of Eenigenburg and Keogh
[
12
]
, stating that if
f
is in
C
, the subclass of
S
consisting of functions whose range is a
convex
set, and if
f
is not a rotation of the half-plane function
z\mapsto\frac{z}{1-z}
then there exists
\delta>0
for which
f^{\prime}\in H^{\frac{1}{2}+\delta}
. Our first result makes this precise in terms of
|a_{2}|
.

Theorem 1
.

If
f\in C
then
f^{\prime}\in H^{p}
for all
p<\frac{1}{1+|a_{2}|}
and
f\in H^{q}
for all
q<\frac{1}{|a_{2}|}
. Both estimates are sharp.

The sharpness comes from the sector function, with
\alpha\in[0,1]
, given by

s_{\alpha}(z)=\frac{1}{2\alpha}\left[\left(\frac{1+z}{1-z}\right)^{\alpha}-1\right]=z+\alpha z^{2}+\frac{1+2\alpha^{2}}{3}z^{3}+\ldots

(1)

(understood as
z\mapsto\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{1+z}{1-z}
when
\alpha=0
), which maps
{\mathbb{D}}
onto a sector of opening
\alpha\pi
.

For the coefficients of functions in
C
we find their sharp order of growth and an explicit bound. Recall that
|a_{2}|\leq 1
with equality only for rotations of the half-plane function
s_{1}(z)=\frac{z}{1-z}
.

Theorem 2
.

If
f\in C
then
a_{n}\in O(n^{|a_{2}|-1})
, which is sharp, and

|a_{n}|\leq\exp\left(\frac{|a_{2}|^{2}-1}{2}\right),\qquad n\geq 2.

Following Cruz and Pommerenke
[
8
]
we define the
lower order
of a function
f
in
{\mathbb{D}}
as

\beta=\inf_{\zeta\in{\mathbb{D}}}|A_{f}(\zeta)|,

where the expression

A_{f}(\zeta)=\frac{1}{2}(1-|\zeta|^{2})\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(\zeta)}{f^{\prime}(\zeta)}-\overline{\zeta},\qquad\zeta\in{\mathbb{D}},

is the second coefficient of the Koebe transform of
f
, given by

F_{\zeta}(z)=\frac{f\left(\frac{\zeta+z}{1+\overline{\zeta}z}\right)-f(\zeta)}{(1-|\zeta|^{2})f^{\prime}(\zeta)}=z+A_{f}(\zeta)z^{2}+\ldots,\qquad\zeta,z\in{\mathbb{D}}.

(2)

Clearly,
0\leq\beta\leq|a_{2}|
, while we will prove in Section
3
that

\beta=|a_{2}|
if and only if either
a_{2}=0
or
f
is a rotation of
s_{\alpha}
.

We have the following improvement of Theorem
1
.

Theorem 3
.

If
f\in C
then
f^{\prime}\in H^{p}
for all
p<\frac{1}{1+\beta}
and
f\in H^{q}
for all
q<\frac{1}{\beta}
.

Convex functions and the angle at infinity.
We define the
angle at infinity
of a convex domain
\Omega
as

\Theta(\Omega)=\inf\{\,\theta\in[0,\pi]\;:\;\Omega\;\text{is contained in a sector of aperture}\;\theta\;\};

(3)

see
[
5
, §3]
. An equivalent definition in terms of half-tangents is given in Section
4
. We regard a sector of aperture zero as an infinite strip. Clearly,
\Theta=0
for bounded domains, infinite half-strips and strips. Note that the infimum in (
3
) is not always attained. For example,
\Theta=0
for a parabolic region but no parallel strip contains it. Simple modifications provide similar examples for other values of
\Theta
in
(0,\pi)
. On the other hand, if
\Theta=\pi
then this value is always attained by any of the supporting half-planes of
\Omega
. In fact, we will prove that in this case
\Omega
itself must be a half-plane.

Our main theorem relates the angle at infinity with the lower order as follows.

Theorem 4
.

If
f\in C
then
\Theta\big(f({\mathbb{D}})\big)=\beta\pi
.

From this, we deduce some corollaries. We will be needing the following theorem of Chuaqui and Osgood
[
6
]
and Fournier, Ma and Ruscheweyh
[
17
]
. We provide a new proof of this in Section
8
.

Theorem A

(
[
6
,
17
]
)
.

If
f\in C
has
a_{2}=0
then
f
is either bounded or a rotation of
s_{0}(z)=\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{1+z}{1-z}
.

We now deduce our first corollary from Theorem
4
.

Corollary 1
.

If
f\in C
then its image
f({\mathbb{D}})
lies in some sector of aperture
|a_{2}|\pi
.

Indeed, if
a_{2}=0
then the corollary holds for the two cases given in Theorem
A
. If
a_{2}\neq 0
then, by the aforementioned characterization of the case when
\beta
equals
|a_{2}|
, we see that
f
is either a rotation of a sector function
s_{\alpha}
or
\Theta<|a_{2}|\pi
, again, two cases for which the corollary holds.

Corollary
1
is sharp in view of the functions
s_{\alpha}
. Moreover, the aperture
|a_{2}|\pi
cannot be improved to
\beta\pi
since, as discussed earlier, the infimum in (
3
) is not always attained.

By subordination, if
f
is holomorphic in
{\mathbb{D}}
and its range lies in some sector of aperture

then
f\in H^{q}
, for
q<\frac{\pi}{\theta}
; see Exercise 2 in
[
9
, Chap. 1]
. Hence, from Corollary
1
we deduce the following, which is the Hardy space estimate for the function
f
in Theorem
1
, thus given an alternative geometric proof.

Corollary 2
.

If
f\in C
then
f\in H^{q}
for all
q<\frac{1}{|a_{2}|}
.

Finally, since for functions in
C
we have
|a_{2}|=1
only for half-plane mappings, we obtain the following.

Corollary 3
.

If
f\in C
with
\Theta\big(f({\mathbb{D}})\big)=\pi
then
f
is a half-plane mapping.

Convex functions and smoothness at the boundary.
Let
\Lambda_{t}
denote the class of functions
\phi
defined on
[0,2\pi]
which satisfy the Lipschitz condition of order
t\in(0,1]
:

|\phi(x)-\phi(y)|\leq c\,|x-y|^{t},

for some
c>0
. Clearly,
s<t
implies
\Lambda_{s}\supset\Lambda_{t}
. Moreover, we consider the wider class
\Lambda_{t}^{p}
, for
t\in(0,1]
and
p\geq 1
, of functions
\phi\in L^{p}(0,2\pi)
that satisfy the integral Lipschitz condition

\left(\int_{0}^{2\pi}|\phi(\theta+h)-\phi(\theta)|^{p}d\theta\right)^{1/p}\leq c\,h^{t},\quad\;\text{for}\;\;h>0,

where
c>0
is some constant. It is easy to see that if
s<t
then
\Lambda_{s}^{p}\supset\Lambda_{t}^{p}
, while also
p<q
implies
\Lambda_{t}^{p}\supset\Lambda_{t}^{q}
. Here is our main theorem.

Theorem 5
.

If
f\in C
is not a rotation of the half-plane function
s_{1}(z)=\frac{z}{1-z}
then
f(e^{i\theta})\in\Lambda_{t}^{p}
with
t=\frac{1}{p}-|a_{2}|
, for every
1\leq p<\frac{1}{|a_{2}|}
. This is sharp in the sense that for every
\alpha\in[0,1)
there exists
f\in C
with
\alpha=|a_{2}|
, for which
f(e^{i\theta})\notin\Lambda_{t}^{p}
with
t=\frac{1}{p}-\alpha+\varepsilon
, for every
1\leq p<\frac{1}{\alpha}
and
\varepsilon>0
.

In the case
a_{2}=0
we are able to prove a stronger Lipschitz condition on the boundary.

Theorem 6
.

Let
f\in C
with
a_{2}=0
. If
f
is not a rotation of the parallel strip function
s_{0}(z)=\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{1+z}{1-z}
then
f(e^{i\theta})\in\Lambda_{t}
for
t=\frac{1-3|a_{3}|}{3(1+|a_{3}|)}
.

Other geometric subclasses of
S
and Hardy spaces.
Theorem
1
takes the following form in
S^{*}
, the subclass of
S
consisting of functions whose range is

with respect to the origin. This refines another theorem of Eenigenburg and Keogh

.

Theorem 7
.

If
f\in S^{*}
then
f^{\prime}\in H^{p}
for all
p<\frac{2}{4+|a_{2}|}
and
f\in H^{q}
for all

For the subclass
K

functions, a theorem of Leung
[
29

provides the sharp estimate: if

then the derivative

belongs to

Theorem 8

Let

(i)

if
a_{2}=0

f^{\prime}\in H^{1/3}

f\in H^{1/2}
, while

(ii)

f^{\prime}\in H^{p}

and

for all
q<1/2

All four estimates are sharp.

Let
R
denote the subclass of
S
consisting of functions

whose range is
convex in one direction
, that is, there exists a direction
\theta\in[0,\pi)
such that for each line

\{te^{i\theta}:t\in{\mathbb{R}}\}

is either empty or a connected set. Domains that are convex in one direction have been of importance in the theory of harmonic mappings; see
[

Let

denote the class of functions

in

whose range is

It is well known that

apply to functions in

and

. Moreover, these estimates are optimal when

. However, for
a_{2}=0
the optimal exponent exhibits a jump.

Theorem 9
.

Let
f\in S
and assume that
a_{2}=0
. We have that

(i)

if
f\in R
then
f^{\prime}\in H^{p}
for all
p<1/2
and
f\in H^{q}
for all
q<1
, while

(ii)

if
f\in R^{+}
then
f^{\prime}\in H^{p}
for all
p<1
and
f\in H^{q}
for all
q<\infty
.

All four estimates are sharp.

We now show that the improvement of the Hardy space estimate for close-to-convex functions given in Theorem
8
for the case
a_{2}=0
does not persist in the class
S
, in fact, not even in the class
S_{\mathbb{R}}
, the subclass of

consisting of functions that have

. On the other hand, it is not difficult to see that Prawitz’ Hardy space estimate for functions in

also holds in the class of

Theorem 10
.

It holds that
T\subset H^{p}
for all
p<1/2
. For every
\alpha\in[0,2]
there exists
f\in S_{\mathbb{R}}
with
a_{2}=\alpha
and such that
f\notin H^{1/2}
.

Integrability of the derivative of univalent functions.
Recall that the
Nevanlinna class

N
consists of holomorphic functions
f
in
{\mathbb{D}}
having bounded characteristic:

\sup_{r\in(0,1)}\,\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log^{+}|f(re^{i\theta})|d\theta<\infty,

where
\log^{+}x=\max\{0,\log x\}
. It is known that
N
is wider than all Hardy spaces, that is,
\cup_{p>0}H^{p}\subset N
; see
[
9
, §2.1]
.

The Bloch-Nevanlinna conjecture, asserting that
f^{\prime}\in N
whenever
f\in N
, has been proven false by various authors. A striking counterexample was provided by Lohwater, Piranian and Rudin
[
32
]
. They showed that for a suitably chosen increasing sequence
\{n_{p}\}
of positive integers, the function

\Phi(z)=\int_{0}^{z}\exp{\bigg(\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{p=1}^{\infty}w^{n_{p}}\bigg)}dw

is holomorphic in
{\mathbb{D}}
, continuous and injective in
\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}
, and for almost all
\theta
it satisfies

0=\liminf_{r\to 1}|\Phi^{\prime}(re^{i\theta})|\,<\,\limsup_{r\to 1}|\Phi^{\prime}(re^{i\theta})|=+\infty

and

-\infty=\liminf_{r\to 1}\,{\rm arg}\,\Phi^{\prime}(re^{i\theta})\,<\,\limsup_{r\to 1}\,{\rm arg}\,\Phi^{\prime}(re^{i\theta})=+\infty.

This means that
\Phi\in H^{\infty}\cap S_{\mathbb{R}}
but
\Phi^{\prime}\notin N
.

It’s easy to see that
a_{2}(\Phi)=\frac{1}{4}
(it relies on the choice
n_{1}=1
made in
[
32
]
). We mention that the construction of
\Phi
can be modified so that the second coefficient takes any value in the interval
[0,\frac{\pi}{8})
, but we will not supply a proof for this. Instead, composing the Koebe function with an integral transform of the function
\Phi
, we will prove the following.

Theorem 11
.

For every
\alpha\in[0,2)
there exists
f\in S_{\mathbb{R}}
such that
a_{2}=\alpha
and
f^{\prime}\notin N
.

It is interesting to note that in view of this theorem, the pathological behavior of the function
\Phi
can be encountered arbitrary close to the Koebe function
k
, whose derivative
k^{\prime}(z)=\frac{1+z}{(1-z)^{3}}
belongs to
H^{p}
for all
p<1/3
, therefore to
N
as well.

2.
Preliminaries

Hardy spaces.
We will make frequent use of the fact that

z\mapsto\frac{1}{(1-z)^{a}}\;\,\text{is in}\;\,H^{p}\quad\text{ if and only }\quad p<\frac{1}{a}

(see Exercise 1 in
[
9
, Chap. 1]
) and of the following two classical theorems, the first one of Hardy and Littlewood (see
[
9
, §5.6]
) and the second of Prawitz (see
[
9
, §3.7]
or
[
10
, §2.10]
).

Theorem B
.

If
f^{\prime}\in H^{p}
for some
p<1
then
f\in H^{q}
for
q=p/(1-p)
.

Theorem C
.

If
f\in S
and
p\in(0,\infty)
then

M_{p}^{p}(r,f)\leq p\int_{0}^{r}\frac{1}{t}M_{\infty}^{p}(t,f)dt,\qquad r\in(0,1).

A direct corollary of Theorem
C
, via the growth theorem, is that
S\subset H^{p}
for all
p<1/2
.

The Carathéodory class.
Let
{\mathcal{P}}
denote the Carathéodory class of functions
h(z)=1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_{n}z^{n}
which are analytic in
{\mathbb{D}}
and satisfy
{\rm Re}\,h(z)>0
for all
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
. It is well known that
{\mathcal{P}}\subset H^{p}
for all
p<1
; see Exercise 2 in
[
9
, Chap. 1]
. According to the Herglotz representation, for each function
h
in
{\mathcal{P}}
there exists a unique probability measure
\mu
supported on
{\mathbb{T}}=\partial{\mathbb{D}}
, such that

h(z)=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{1+\lambda z}{1-\lambda z}d\mu(\lambda),\quad z\in{\mathbb{D}}.

A theorem of Carathéodory states that
|c_{n}|\leq 2
, for all
n\geq 1
. We will be needing the case of equality only for
n=1
: we have that
|c_{1}|=2
only for
h(z)=\frac{1+\lambda z}{1-\lambda z}
, for
\lambda\in{\mathbb{T}}
. See
[
10
, §1.9 and §2.5]
.

3.
Convex functions, Hardy spaces and coefficients

To prove the Hardy space estimate in
C
we will combine Prawitz’ theorem with the following theorem of Gronwall, which gives growth and distortion bounds in
C
in terms of
|a_{2}|
. Although Gronwall
[
18
]
provided no proof, it was subsequently proved by Finkelstein
[
16
]
(via the Schwarz-Pick Lemma) and by Szynal
et. al.

[
45
]
(via Löwner theory).

Theorem D

(
[
18
,
16
,
45
]
)
.

If
f\in C
and
\alpha=|a_{2}|
then

\ell_{\alpha}(r)\leq|f(z)|\leq s_{\alpha}(r),\qquad r=|z|,

and

\ell^{\prime}_{\alpha}(r)\leq|f^{\prime}(z)|\leq s^{\prime}_{\alpha}(r),\qquad r=|z|,

where
s_{\alpha}
is given in (
1
) and

\ell_{\alpha}(z)=\frac{i}{2\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}}\log\frac{1-\lambda z}{1-\overline{\lambda}z},\qquad\lambda=\alpha+i\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}},

maps onto a shifted infinite vertical strip.

We now prove Theorem
1
, which states that if
f\in C
then
f^{\prime}\in H^{p}
for all
p<\frac{1}{1+|a_{2}|}
and
f\in H^{q}
for all
q<\frac{1}{|a_{2}|}
.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

In view of Theorem
D
, we have that

|f^{\prime}(z)|\leq s^{\prime}_{\alpha}(r)=\frac{1}{(1-r)^{1+\alpha}(1+r)^{1-\alpha}},\qquad r=|z|,

where
\alpha=|a_{2}|
. By Alexander’s theorem (see
[
10
, §2.5]
), the function
g(z)=zf^{\prime}(z)
is strarlike. Applying Prawitz’ Theorem
C
to
g
we obtain

M_{p}^{p}(r,g)\leq p\int_{0}^{r}\frac{dt}{t^{1-p}(1-t)^{p(1+\alpha)}}.

Therefore, we have that

\lim_{r\to 1^{-}}M_{p}^{p}(r,g)\leq p\int_{0}^{1}\frac{dt}{t^{1-p}(1-t)^{p(1+\alpha)}},

which is finite since
1-p<1
and
p(1+\alpha)<1
, so that
g\in H^{p}
. Hence,
f^{\prime}(z)=g(z)/z
is also in
H^{p}
. The estimate for
f
follows from Theorem
B
.

Finally, both estimates are sharp for each
\alpha\in[0,1]
in view of the sector function (
1
).

∎

An alternative proof of the estimate for the function
f
could be given by using the Schwarz-Pick Lemma in order to show that

(1-|z|^{2})\left|\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(z)}{f^{\prime}(z)}\right|\leq 2(1+|a_{2}|),\qquad z\in{\mathbb{D}},

and then invoking a result of Kim and Sugawa
[
26
]
to deduce that
f\in H^{q}
for
q<\frac{1}{|a_{2}|}
. We leave the details of this to the interested reader.

We turn to the coefficients of functions in
C
and prove Theorem
2
.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

We first prove the asymptotic estimate
a_{n}\in O(n^{|a_{2}|-1})
. As in the proof of Theorem
1
, we see that the starlike function
g(z)=zf^{\prime}(z)
satisfies

|g(z)|\leq\frac{1}{(1-|z|)^{1+|a_{2}|}},\qquad z\in{\mathbb{D}},

for some
c>0
, in view of Theorem
D
. Writing
g(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_{n}z^{n}
we see from Pommerenke’s
[
39
, Theorem 3]
that the coefficients
b_{n}=na_{n}
satisfy
b_{n}\in O(n^{|a_{2}|})
. From this the estimate follows.

For the sharpness we see from the sector function (
1
), that the estimate cannot be replaced by
o(n^{|a_{2}|-1})
. Indeed, for
\alpha=0
we have that

s_{0}(z)=\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{1+z}{1-z}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{2k+1}}{2k+1},

so that
na_{n}\nrightarrow 0
, while for
\alpha\in(0,1]
it has been computed in
[
5
]
that the coefficients of
s_{\alpha}
satisfy

\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{1-\alpha}a_{n}=\frac{2^{\alpha-1}}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}>0,

where
\Gamma
denotes the gamma function.

We now prove the bound
|a_{n}|\leq\exp\left(\frac{|a_{2}|^{2}-1}{2}\right)
. Recall that according to the Marx-Strohhäcker theorem if
f\in C
then
f
is starlike of order
1/2
,
i.e.
, it satisfies

{\rm Re}\,\frac{zf^{\prime}(z)}{f(z)}>\frac{1}{2},\qquad z\in{\mathbb{D}}

(see exercise 23 in
[
10
, Ch. 2]
). We set

h(z)=2\,\frac{zf^{\prime}(z)}{f(z)}-1=1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_{n}z^{n},\qquad z\in{\mathbb{D}},

which belongs to the class
{\mathcal{P}}
. We have that
c_{1}=2a_{2}
. We write

\left(\log\frac{f(z)}{z}\right)^{\prime}=\frac{h(z)-1}{2z},

which upon integration gives

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n+1}z^{n}=\frac{f(z)}{z}=\exp\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{c_{n}}{2n}z^{n}\right).

An application of the third Lebedev-Milin inequality
[
10
, §5.1]
yields

|a_{n+1}|^{2}\leq\exp\left(\sum_{n=1}^{n}\frac{|c_{k}|^{2}-4}{4k}\right),\qquad n\geq 1.

(4)

Observe that, by Carathéodory’s theorem, each summand is non-positive and, therefore, we have that

|a_{n+1}|^{2}\leq\exp\left(\frac{|c_{1}|^{2}-4}{4}\right)=\exp\left(|a_{2}|^{2}-1\right).

The proof is complete.
∎

Note that keeping more terms in (
4
) would result in a stronger, though less elegant inequality.

We make a brief remark regarding summability. In view of Theorem
2
, the coefficients
a_{n}
of functions in
C
converge to zero, except for the case of the half-plane function and its rotations. It follows from a result of Lewis
[
31
]
that, in general, the sequence of coefficients
(a_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
will not belong to any
\ell^{p}
space. Indeed, he showed that the polylogarithm function

z\mapsto\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{z^{n}}{n^{t}}

belongs to
C
for all
t\geq 0
; observe that
(n^{-1/p})_{n=1}^{\infty}\notin\ell^{p}
for
p>0
. However, by a theorem of Pommerenke
[
39
]
we have that
(a_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{1}
for bounded convex functions. This is best possible since Lewis’ example is bounded for
t>1
.

We will be needing the following lemma.

Lemma 1
.

If
f\in C
then
|a_{3}|\leq\frac{1+2|a_{2}|^{2}}{3}
. Equality holds only for rotations of the sector function (
1
).

Proof.

By a result of Hummel
[
21
]
(see also Trimble
[
47
]
for a simpler proof), we have that

|a_{3}-a_{2}^{2}|\leq\frac{1-|a_{2}|^{2}}{3}.

(5)

A simple application of the triangle inequality

|a_{3}|\leq|a_{3}-a_{2}^{2}|+|a_{2}|^{2}\leq\frac{1+2|a_{2}|^{2}}{3}

(6)

gives the desired inequality. It is easy to see that equality holds for rotations of the sector function (
1
), but some more work is needed in order to show that there are no other extremal functions.

Let
f
be an extremal function for the statement. We associate with
f
a function
h(z)=1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_{n}z^{n}
in
{\mathcal{P}}
by

h(z)=1+\frac{zf^{\prime\prime}(z)}{f^{\prime}(z)},\qquad z\in{\mathbb{D}},

(see
[
10
, §2.5]
) and write the relations for the initial coefficients

c_{1}=2a_{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad c_{2}=6a_{3}-4a_{2}^{2}.

We see that (
5
) is equivalent to

\left|c_{2}-\frac{c_{1}^{2}}{2}\right|\leq 2-\frac{|c_{1}|^{2}}{2},

which is equivalent to the second Carathéodory-Toeplitz determinant of
h
being non-negative. Equality holds here only if the support of the Herglotz measure of
h
consists of at most 2 atoms (see Theorem IV.22 in
[
48
, Ch. IV, §7]
). Hence, we have that

h(z)=t\frac{1+\lambda z}{1-\lambda z}+(1-t)\frac{1+\mu z}{1-\mu z},\qquad t\in[0,1],\;\lambda,\mu\in{\mathbb{T}}

(these correspond to extremals that have not been considered by Trimble). Without loss of generality we may assume that
t\in[\frac{1}{2},1]
. The coefficients of
h
are
c_{n}=2t\lambda^{n}+2(1-t)\mu^{n}
, while a simple calculation gives us that

6(a_{3}-a_{2}^{2})=c_{2}-\frac{c_{1}^{2}}{2}=2t(1-t)(\lambda-\mu)^{2}

and

a_{2}^{2}=\frac{c_{1}^{2}}{4}=\big[\mu+t(\lambda-\mu)\big]^{2}.

The case when
\lambda=\mu
corresponds to rotations of the half-plane function
s_{1}(z)=\frac{z}{1-z}
. Otherwise, if
\lambda\neq\mu
we have that equality in the the triangle inequality of (
6
) shows that these two expressions must have the same argument and therefore

0<\frac{\big[\mu+t(\lambda-\mu)\big]^{2}}{(\lambda-\mu)^{2}}=\left(t+\frac{1}{\lambda\overline{\mu}-1}\right)^{2},

from which it follows that
\lambda\overline{\mu}\in{\mathbb{R}}
, so that
\lambda\overline{\mu}=-1
. Hence, we have that

\big(\log f^{\prime}(z)\big)^{\prime}=\frac{h(z)-1}{z}=\frac{2t\lambda}{1-\lambda z}-\frac{2(1-t)\lambda}{1+\lambda z},

which yields

f^{\prime}(z)=\frac{1}{(1-\lambda z)^{(1+\alpha)}(1+\lambda z)^{(1-\alpha)}},

readily showing that
f(z)=\overline{\lambda}s_{\alpha}(\lambda z)
, with
\alpha=2t-1
.
∎

We turn to the lower order
\beta
of functions in
C
and first prove the following.

Lemma 2
.

If
f\in C
then
\beta=|a_{2}|
if and only if either
a_{2}=0
or
f
is a rotation of
s_{\alpha}
.

Proof.

It is evident that if
a_{2}=0
then
\beta=0
as well. We see that the sector function
s_{\alpha}
satisfies
\frac{s_{\alpha}^{\prime\prime}(z)}{s_{\alpha}^{\prime}(z)}=\frac{2(\alpha+z)}{1-z^{2}}
, so that after a straightforward calculation we get that

|A_{s_{\alpha}}(z)|^{2}=\alpha^{2}+\frac{4(1-\alpha^{2})y^{2}}{|1-z^{2}|^{2}},\qquad z=x+yi.

Hence
|A_{s_{\alpha}}(z)|\geq\alpha
and therefore
\beta=\alpha
for
s_{\alpha}
. Clearly the same is true for rotations of
s_{\alpha}
.

On the other hand, for an arbitrary
f\in C
and for
z=re^{i\theta}
we find that

|A_{f}(z)|^{2}=|a_{2}|^{2}+2r{\rm Re}\big[(3a_{3}\overline{a_{2}}-2a_{2}^{2}\overline{a_{2}}-a_{2})e^{i\theta}\big]+O(r^{2}),\qquad r\to 0.

If
\beta=|a_{2}|
then
|A_{f}(z)|\geq|a_{2}|
for every
\theta
, so that
3a_{3}\overline{a_{2}}-2a_{2}^{2}\overline{a_{2}}-a_{2}=0
. It follows that either
a_{2}=0
or equality holds in Lemma
1
, that is,
f
is a rotation of a sector function.
∎

Now we prove Theorem
3
, according to which for
f\in C
we have that
f^{\prime}\in H^{p}
for all
p<\frac{1}{1+\beta}
and
f\in H^{q}
for all
q<\frac{1}{\beta}
. Its proof relies on Theorem
1
.

Proof of Theorem
3
.

Let
p_{0}<\frac{1}{1+\beta}
and write
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{p_{0}}-(1+\beta)>0
. Let
\zeta\in{\mathbb{D}}
be such that
|A_{f}(\zeta)|<\beta+\varepsilon
and write
F_{\zeta}
for the corresponding Koebe transform (
2
). By Theorem
1
,
F^{\prime}_{\zeta}
belongs to
H^{p}
for every
p<(1+|A_{f}(\zeta)|)^{-1}
. Since

|f^{\prime}\circ\psi(z)|\leq 4|f^{\prime}(\zeta)|\,|F^{\prime}_{\zeta}(z)|,\qquad\text{where}\qquad\psi(z)=\frac{\zeta+z}{1+\overline{\zeta}z},

we have that
f^{\prime}\circ\psi
belongs to the same Hardy spaces as
F^{\prime}_{\zeta}
. An appeal to the Corollary in
[
9
, §2.6]
shows that
f^{\prime}\in H^{p}
for every
p<(1+|A_{f}(\zeta)|)^{-1}
. Since
p_{0}=\frac{1}{1+\beta+\varepsilon}
lies in this range, the first assertion in the statement has been proved. The second assertion follows immediately from Theorem
B
.

∎

4.
Convex functions and the angle at infinity

We begin by giving an equivalent definition for the angle at infinity by making use of that fact that half-tangents exist at all boundary points of a convex domain; see
[
41
, §3.5]
. Let
\Omega
be an unbounded convex domain,
f
be one of its Riemann mappings and assume that
f(e^{it_{0}})=\infty
for some
t_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}
. We set

\vartheta^{\pm}=\lim_{t\to t_{0}^{\pm}}\arg f(e^{it})

(7)

to be the argument of the two half-tangents at infinity. We will prove that the difference
\Delta\vartheta=\vartheta^{+}-\vartheta^{-}
is equal to the angle at infinity.

Lemma 3
.

\Theta=\Delta\vartheta

Proof.

We may translate in order to have
0\in\Omega
and rotate so that

\vartheta:=\vartheta^{+}=-\vartheta^{-}\in[0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}],

in which case
\Delta\vartheta=2\vartheta
.

Step (i):
\Theta\leq\Delta\vartheta
. Let
\varepsilon>0
and consider the sectors

P_{x}=\left\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:|\arg(z-x)|<\vartheta+\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\right\},\qquad x<0.

We will show that there exists
x_{0}<0
sufficiently small so that
\Omega\subset P_{x_{0}}
. If this is not true then let
w
be the point leftmost in
\partial\Omega\cap\partial P_{x}
, with
{\rm Im}\,w>0
, and see that the half-line
\ell
that extends from
w
to infinity along
\partial P_{x}
cannot lie entirely within
\Omega
since

\lim_{\ell\ni z\to\infty}\arg z=\vartheta+\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}

is larger than
\vartheta
. Hence, by convexity, we have that
\Omega\cap\ell
must be a single open line segment. Now it is evident that sliding the sector to the left we can obtain
P_{x_{0}}\supset\Omega
for some
x_{0}<0
sufficiently small. Hence,
\Theta\leq\Delta\vartheta+\varepsilon
and letting
\varepsilon\to 0
we get that
\Theta\leq\Delta\vartheta
.

Step (ii):
\Theta\geq\Delta\vartheta
. We begin with the case
\Theta=0
and assume that
\vartheta>0
in order to get a contradiction. Then
\Omega
lies in some symmetric with respect to the real line sector
P
of aperture
\Delta\vartheta/3
. But this is impossible since for points
z
in the upper half plane

\lim_{\partial P\ni z\to\infty}\arg z=\tfrac{\vartheta}{3}<\vartheta=\lim_{\partial\Omega\ni z\to\infty}\arg z.

Hence
\vartheta=0
in this case.

We assume that
\Theta\in(0,\pi]
. If
\vartheta=0
then we work as in step (i) in order to find some sector of aperture
\Theta/2
that contains
\Omega
, a contradiction. Hence,
\vartheta>0
. Let
\varepsilon>0
and consider

Q=\left\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:|\arg z|<\vartheta-\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\right\}.

Now if the ray
\partial Q\cap\{{\rm Im}\,z>0\}
intersects
\partial\Omega
then, by convexity, this can only occur at a unique point, so that the ray lies outside
\Omega
at a neighborhood of infinity. But this is impossible since
\arg z=\vartheta-\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}
on this ray, a contradiction. Hence
Q\subset\Omega
, so that
\Delta\vartheta-\varepsilon\leq\Theta
, which gives us the desired conclusion upon letting
\varepsilon\to 0
.
∎

Let
f\in C
and see that by the Herglotz formula there exists a unique probability measure
\mu
, supported on
{\mathbb{T}}
, such that

1+\frac{zf^{\prime\prime}(z)}{f^{\prime}(z)}=\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\frac{1+\lambda z}{1-\lambda z}d\mu(\lambda).

(8)

If
\lambda_{0}\in{\mathbb{T}}
is mapped by
f
to infinity then
\mu
has a point mass there with
\mu(\lambda_{0})\geq\frac{1}{2}
, a fact that is usually attributed to Paatero
[
34
]
. By convexity, there can be either none, one or two preimages of infinity, the latter only in the case of an infinite strip.

It is well known that the boundary rotation,
i.e.
, the net change of the direction angle of the tangent, corresponding to an arc
I
on
{\mathbb{T}}
under a function
f\in C
equals
2\pi\mu(I)
. To see this, write
I=(a,b)
and compute the limit of

\arg\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}f(re^{i\theta})\bigg|_{a}^{b}=\theta+\arg f^{\prime}(re^{i\theta})\bigg|_{a}^{b}

as
r\to 1^{-}
by making use of either formula (6) in
[
12
]
or formula (14) in
[
41
, page 62]
.

Lemma 4
.

Let
f\in C
with associated measure
\mu
and assume that
f(\lambda_{0})=\infty
for some
\lambda_{0}\in{\mathbb{T}}
. Then

\Theta\big(f({\mathbb{D}})\big)=\big(2\mu(\lambda_{0})-1\big)\pi.

Proof.

In case the range of
f
is an infinite strip the desired equality is evident since
\Theta=0
and
\mu(\lambda_{0})=\frac{1}{2}
. We assume that
\lambda_{0}
is the unique preimage of infinity. The boundary rotation of
f
on the finite plane is equal to

2\pi\mu\big({\mathbb{T}}\backslash\{\lambda_{0}\}\big)=2\pi\big(1-\mu(\lambda_{0})\big).

On the other hand, in view of the half-tangents (
7
), the boundary rotation of
f
on the finite plane is equal to

\vartheta^{-}-(\vartheta^{+}-\pi)=\pi-\Delta\vartheta=\pi-\Theta,

by Lemma
3
. Equating the two the desired equality follows.

∎

We now prove Theorem
4
, according to which
\Theta\big(f({\mathbb{D}})\big)=\beta\pi
for
f\in C
.

Proof of Theorem
4
.

If
f
is bounded then
\beta=0
by a result of Pommerenke
[
42
]
and, moreover, we clearly have that
\Theta=0
, so that the desired conclusion holds in this case. We assume that
f
is unbounded and, after applying a rotation, we may further assume that
f(1)=\infty
. Using (
8
) we compute

\displaystyle A_{f}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(1-|z|^{2})\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(z)}{f^{\prime}(z)}-\overline{z}

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\frac{(1-|z|^{2})\lambda}{1-\lambda z}\,d\mu(\lambda)-\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\overline{z}\,d\mu(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\frac{\lambda-\overline{z}}{1-\lambda z}\,d\mu(\lambda).

Applying the triangle inequality twice we obtain

\displaystyle|A_{f}(z)|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{1-\overline{z}}{1-z}\,\mu(1)+\int_{{\mathbb{T}}\backslash\{1\}}\frac{\lambda(1-\overline{\lambda z})}{1-\lambda z}\,d\mu(\lambda)\right|

\displaystyle\geq\mu(1)-\left|\int_{{\mathbb{T}}\backslash\{1\}}\frac{\lambda(1-\overline{\lambda z})}{1-\lambda z}\,d\mu(\lambda)\right|

\displaystyle\geq\mu(1)-\mu({\mathbb{T}}\backslash\{1\})

\displaystyle=2\mu(1)-1

\displaystyle=\frac{\Theta}{\pi},

by Lemma
4
. Now, for
x\in(0,1)
we have that

A_{f}(x)=\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\frac{\lambda-x}{1-\lambda x}\,d\mu(\lambda)=\mu(1)+\int_{{\mathbb{T}}\backslash\{1\}}\frac{\lambda-x}{1-\lambda x}\,d\mu(\lambda),

where the integrand in the last expression is uniformly bounded and for each
\lambda\in{\mathbb{T}}\backslash\{1\}
it converges to
-1
when
x\to 1^{-}
. By the dominated convergence theorem we have that

\lim_{x\to 1^{-}}A_{f}(x)=\mu(1)-\mu({\mathbb{T}}\backslash\{1\})=\frac{\Theta}{\pi}.

The proof is complete.

∎

We mention here, without providing the details, that a slightly more involved proof of Theorem
4
would be more revealing: the Koebe transform of
f
, as its parameter tends to the preimage of infinity, converges locally uniformly in
{\mathbb{D}}
to a sector mapping of aperture
\Theta
, whose second coefficient is equal to
\frac{\Theta}{\pi}
. We chose the proof we presented in favor of simplicity.

5.
Convex functions and smoothness at the boundary

We will be needing a theorem of Hardy and Littlewood, according to which for a function
f\in H^{p}
we have that

f(e^{i\theta})\in\Lambda_{t}^{p}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad M_{p}(r,f^{\prime})\in O\big((1-r)^{t-1}\big),\quad r\to 1;

(9)

see Theorem 5.4 in
[
9
, §5.2]
.

Proof of Theorem
5
.

As in the proof of Theorem
1
, we use the distortion estimate from Theorem
D
and apply Prawitz’ Theorem
C
to the starlike function
g(z)=zf^{\prime}(z)
in order to deduce

M_{p}^{p}(r,g)\leq p\int_{0}^{r}\frac{1}{t}M_{\infty}^{p}(t,g)dt\leq p\int_{0}^{r}\frac{t^{p-1}\,dt}{(1-t)^{p(1+|a_{2}|)}}\leq\frac{c}{(1-r)^{p(1+|a_{2}|)-1}},

for some constant
c>0
. Hence

M_{p}(r,f^{\prime})\in O\left(\frac{1}{(1-r)^{1+|a_{2}|-\frac{1}{p}}}\right),\qquad r\to 1.

An application of (
9
) yields the desired result since for
t=\frac{1}{p}-|a_{2}|
,
p<1/|a_{2}|
implies
t>0
, while
p\geq 1
implies
t\leq 1
.

For the sharpness we fix
\alpha\in[0,1)
and
1\leq p<\frac{1}{\alpha}
, and see that the boundary function of
s_{\alpha}
, the sector function (
1
), cannot belong to
\Lambda_{t}^{p}
, with
t=\frac{1}{p}-\alpha+\varepsilon
, for any
\varepsilon>0
. If it did, then by (
9
) we would have that

M_{p}(r,s_{\alpha}^{\prime})\leq\frac{c_{1}}{(1-r)^{1-\frac{1}{p}+\alpha-\varepsilon}},

for some
c_{1}>0
. Since

s_{\alpha}^{\prime}(z)=\frac{1}{(1-z)^{1+\alpha}(1+z)^{1-\alpha}},

we see that the above would imply

\displaystyle\frac{c_{1}^{p}}{(1-r)^{(1+\alpha-\varepsilon)p-1}}

\displaystyle\geq\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\theta}{|1-z|^{(1+\alpha)p}|1+z|^{(1-\alpha)p}},\qquad z=re^{i\theta}

\displaystyle\geq c_{2}\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{d\theta}{|1-z|^{(1+\alpha)p}}

\displaystyle=c_{2}\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{d\theta}{[(1-r)^{2}+4r\sin^{2}(\theta/2)]^{\frac{(1+\alpha)p}{2}}}

\displaystyle\geq c_{2}\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{d\theta}{[(1-r)^{2}+r\theta^{2}]^{\frac{(1+\alpha)p}{2}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{2}}{(1-r)^{(1+\alpha)p-1}}\int_{0}^{\frac{\sqrt{r}\pi}{1-r}}\frac{dt}{\sqrt{r}[1+t^{2}]^{\frac{(1+\alpha)p}{2}}}

\displaystyle>\frac{c_{2}}{(1-r)^{(1+\alpha)p-1}}\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{1-r}}\frac{dt}{[1+t^{2}]^{\frac{(1+\alpha)p}{2}}},

after the change of variabless
\sqrt{r}\theta=(1-r)t
, for
r
sufficiently close to 1 and for some
c_{2}>0
. But this leads to

0<\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{1-r}}\frac{dt}{[1+t^{2}]^{\frac{(1+\alpha)p}{2}}}\leq c_{3}(1-r)^{\varepsilon p}\to 0,\qquad\text{as}\;\;r\to 1,

for some
c_{3}>0
, a contradiction.
∎

We now assume that
a_{2}=0
and first prove the following lemma. Recall that, in this case,
|a_{3}|\leq 1/3
by Lemma
1
.

Lemma 5
.

Let
f\in C
with
a_{2}=0
. Then

|f^{\prime}(z)|\leq(1-|z|)^{-\frac{2(1+3|a_{3}|)}{3(1+|a_{3}|)}},\qquad z\in{\mathbb{D}}.

Proof.

Let
\gamma=3|a_{3}|
. According to Lemma
1
, we have that
\gamma=1
only for rotations of the function
s_{0}(z)=\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{1+z}{1-z}
, which satisfy the statement.

We assume that
\gamma<1
. The well-known characterization of the class
C
tells us that

1+\frac{zf^{\prime\prime}(z)}{f^{\prime}(z)}=\frac{1+z\varphi(z)}{1-z\varphi(z)},\qquad z\in{\mathbb{D}},

for some analytic
\varphi:{\mathbb{D}}\to\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}
. Writing

\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(z)}{f^{\prime}(z)}=\frac{2\varphi(z)}{1-z\varphi(z)}

we see that
\varphi(0)=a_{2}=0
, so that
\varphi(z)=z\psi(z)
for some
\psi:{\mathbb{D}}\to\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}
. A standard calculation shows that
\psi(0)=3a_{3}
. Upon integrating and taking real parts we obtain

\log|f^{\prime}(z)|=2\,{\rm Re}\int_{[0,z]}\frac{\zeta\psi(\zeta)}{1-\zeta^{2}\psi(\zeta)}d\zeta\leq 2\int_{[0,z]}\left|\frac{\zeta\psi(\zeta)}{1-\zeta^{2}\psi(\zeta)}\right||d\zeta|,\qquad z=re^{i\theta}.

A simple consequence of the Schwarz-Pick lemma is that

|\psi(\zeta)|\leq\frac{\gamma+\rho}{1+\gamma\rho},\qquad\rho=|\zeta|<1,

(10)

which leads to

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\zeta\psi(\zeta)}{1-\zeta^{2}\psi(\zeta)}\right|\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\rho|\psi(\zeta)|}{1-\rho^{2}|\psi(\zeta)|}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\rho(\gamma+\rho)}{(1-\rho)[1+(1+\gamma)\rho+\rho^{2}]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3+\gamma}\left(\frac{1+\gamma}{1-\rho}-\frac{1+\gamma+2\rho}{1+(1+\gamma)\rho+\rho^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\frac{1+\gamma}{(3+\gamma)(1-\rho)}

Therefore, we get that

\log|f^{\prime}(z)|<\frac{2(1+\gamma)}{3+\gamma}\int_{0}^{r}\frac{d\rho}{1-\rho}=\frac{2(1+\gamma)}{3+\gamma}\log\frac{1}{1-r},

from which the desired inequality follows.

∎

Proof of Theorem
6
.

If
f
is not a rotation of
s_{0}(z)=\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{1+z}{1-z}
then
\gamma=3|a_{3}|<1
, hence
t=\frac{1-\gamma}{3+\gamma}\in(0,\frac{1}{3}]
, and the statement follows by a direct application of Lemma
5
and
[
9
, Theorem 5.1 in §5.2]
.

∎

6.
The Hardy space for other geometric subclasses

Recall that
S^{*}
stands for the class of functions in
S
whose range is starlike with respect to the origin. We prove Theorem
7
: if
f\in S^{*}
then
f^{\prime}\in H^{p}
for all
p<\frac{2}{4+|a_{2}|}
and
f\in H^{q}
for all
q<\frac{2}{2+|a_{2}|}
.

Proof of Theorem
7
.

By Alexander’s theorem we may write
f(z)=zh^{\prime}(z)
for some
h\in C
. We compute
f^{\prime}(z)=h^{\prime}(z)\big[1+z\frac{h^{\prime\prime}(z)}{h^{\prime}(z)}\big]
. Also, we write
h(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_{n}z^{n}
and find that
a_{2}=2c_{2}
. Setting
\alpha=|a_{2}|
and applying Hölder’s inequality with the conjugate parameters

s=\frac{4+\alpha}{2+\alpha}\qquad\text{and}\qquad t=2+\frac{\alpha}{2}

we get

\int_{0}^{2\pi}|f^{\prime}(e^{i\theta})|^{p}\tfrac{d\theta}{2\pi}\leq\left(\int_{0}^{2\pi}|h^{\prime}(e^{i\theta})|^{ps}\tfrac{d\theta}{2\pi}\right)^{\frac{1}{s}}\left(\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left|1+e^{i\theta}\frac{h^{\prime\prime}(e^{i\theta})}{h^{\prime}(e^{i\theta})}\right|^{pt}\tfrac{d\theta}{2\pi}\right)^{\frac{1}{t}}.

Now the first term is finite in view of Theorem
1
and the fact that
ps<\frac{2}{2+\alpha}=\frac{1}{1+|c_{2}|}
, while the second term is finite since
pt<1
and the integrand has positive real part. The estimate for
f
follows from Theorem
B
.

For the sharpness, we see that if
h=s_{\gamma}
is a sector function (
1
) with
\gamma\in[0,1]
then the corresponding starlike function via Alexander’s theorem is

f(z)=\frac{z}{(1-z)^{1+\frac{\alpha}{2}}(1+z)^{1-\frac{\alpha}{2}}},

with
a_{2}=\alpha=2\gamma
. This functions does not belong to
H^{\frac{2}{2+\alpha}}
and its derivative is

f^{\prime}(z)=\frac{1+\alpha z+z^{2}}{(1-z)^{2+\frac{\alpha}{2}}(1+z)^{2-\frac{\alpha}{2}}},

which does not belong to
H^{\frac{2}{4+\alpha}}
.
∎

Geometrically, the starlike function proving the sharpness in Theorem
7
maps
{\mathbb{D}}
onto the complement of two slits symmetric with respect to the real axis lying on the rays
\arg w=\pm\frac{\pi}{4}(2+\alpha)
. The modulus of the endpoints of these two rays is
(2-\alpha)^{-\frac{2-\alpha}{4}}(2+\alpha)^{-\frac{2+\alpha}{4}}
.

We turn to the class
K
of functions
f
in
S
which are close-to-convex,
i.e.
, that satisfy

{\rm Re}\left(\lambda\frac{f^{\prime}(z)}{g^{\prime}(z)}\right)>0,\qquad z\in{\mathbb{D}},

(11)

for some
g\in C
and
\lambda\in{\mathbb{T}}
. It is known that the class
K
coincides with the class of
linearly accessible
functions, that is, functions whose range is the complement of the union of mutually disjoint half-lines; see
[
10
, §2.6]
.

Using Baernstein’s star function, Leung
[
29
]
proved that every
f\in K
satisfies

M_{p}(r,f^{\prime})\leq M_{p}(r,k^{\prime}),\qquad r<1,\,p>0,

where
k
is the Koebe function (see also
[
10
, §7.5]
). Hence, for every
f\in K
, the derivative
f^{\prime}
belongs to
H^{p}
for
p<1/3
, and this is sharp since
k^{\prime}(z)=\frac{1+z}{(1-z)^{3}}
does not belong to
H^{1/3}
. The inclusion
K\subset H^{p}
for
p<1/2
, derived from Prawitz’ theorem, is sharp because
k\in K
. We now prove Theorem
8
, according to which the above Hardy space estimates are sharp whenever
a_{2}\neq 0
, while they can be improved to
f^{\prime}\in H^{1/3}
and
f\in H^{1/2}
in the case when
a_{2}=0
.

Proof of Theorem
8
.

In view of (
11
) we may write
H=\lambda f^{\prime}/g^{\prime}
and
H(0)=\lambda=a+ib
, and see that
a\in(0,1]
. We normalize so that
h=(H-ib)/a
belongs to the Carathéodory class
{\mathcal{P}}
. Setting

f(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n},\qquad g(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_{n}z^{n}\qquad\text{and}\qquad h(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}z^{n},

we get the relation
a_{2}=b_{2}+a\overline{\lambda}c_{1}/2
between the first non-trivial coefficients.

The estimates for the case
a_{2}\neq 0
have already been discussed and so it remains to prove their sharpness. We let
\lambda=1
,
g
to be the half-plane function
s_{1}
and
h
to be a function whose Herglotz measure has point masses
t
and
1-t
, for
t\in(0,1]
, at the points 1 and
-1
, respectively. Hence, we have that

g(z)=\frac{z}{1-z}\qquad\text{and}\qquad h(z)=t\,\frac{1+z}{1-z}+(1-t)\,\frac{1-z}{1+z},

which shows that
b_{2}=1
and
c_{1}=4t-2
, so that
a_{2}=2t\in(0,2]
. A simple calculation gives

f^{\prime}(z)=t\,\frac{1+z}{(1-z)^{3}}+(1-t)\,\frac{1}{1-z^{2}},

which does not belong to
H^{1/3}
. An integration shows that

f(z)=t\,\frac{z}{(1-z)^{2}}+\frac{1-t}{2}\,\log\frac{1+z}{1-z},

(12)

which does not belong to
H^{1/2}
.

We turn to claim (i), the case
a_{2}=0
. It holds that
c_{1}=-2\lambda b_{2}/a
. We distinguish two cases. First, if
|b_{2}|=1
, say
b_{2}=\mu\in{\mathbb{T}}
, then
|c_{1}|=2/a\geq 2
and since, also,
|c_{1}|\leq 2
by Carathéodory’s theorem, we get that
a=1
,
\lambda=1
and, therefore,
g
and
h
must be the half-plane functions

g(z)=\frac{z}{1-\mu z}\qquad\text{and}\qquad h(z)=\frac{1-\mu z}{1+\mu z}.

It follows that
f^{\prime}(z)=\frac{1}{1-\mu^{2}z^{2}}
, which belongs to
H^{p}
for all
p<1
. For the case
|b_{2}|<1
, we set
\beta=|b_{2}|
and use Hölder’s inequality

\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}|f^{\prime}|^{p}\leq\left(\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}|g^{\prime}|^{pr}\right)^{1/r}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}|H|^{ps}\right)^{1/s}

with the conjugate exponents

r=\frac{2+\beta}{1+\beta}\qquad\text{and}\qquad s=2+\beta.

The right-hand side is bounded whenever
p<\frac{1}{2+\beta}
, since
ps<1
and
pr<\frac{1}{1+\beta}
, so that Theorem
1
can be applied. Since the exponent
p=1/3
lies in this range, it follows that
f^{\prime}\in H^{1/3}
. Now the claim that
f\in H^{1/2}
follows from Theorem
B
.

To show the sharpness of (i) we let
\lambda=1
,
g=s_{\beta}
with
\beta\in[0,1)
, and
h\in P
with a measure consisting of three point-masses:
t,t
and
1-2t
at the points
\mu=e^{i\theta},\overline{\mu}
and 1, respectively, given by

h(z)=t\,\frac{1+\mu z}{1-\mu z}+t\,\frac{1+\overline{\mu}z}{1-\overline{\mu}z}+(1-2t)\frac{1+z}{1-z},\qquad t\in\left(0,\tfrac{1}{2}\right),\,\theta\in(0,\pi).

We compute
c_{1}=4t\cos\theta+2(1-2t)
and

f^{\prime}(z)=\frac{1}{(1-z)^{1+\beta}(1+z)^{1-\beta}}\left[t\,\frac{1+\mu z}{1-\mu z}+t\,\frac{1+\overline{\mu}z}{1-\overline{\mu}z}+(1-2t)\frac{1+z}{1-z}\right].

(13)

Assume in order to get a contradiction that
f
belongs to
H^{q}
for some
q\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
and recall the growth estimate (see
[
9
, §3.2]
):

|f(z)|\leq\frac{c}{(1-r)^{1/q}},\qquad r=|z|,

for some
c>0
. Now the bound

|f^{\prime}(z)|\leq\frac{\hat{c}}{(1-r)^{1+1/q}},\qquad r=|z|,

for some
\hat{c}>0
, follows from the Cauchy integral formula. Note that
b_{2}=\beta
and let
\beta
be such that
\frac{1}{2}<\frac{1}{1+\beta}<q
. We ensure that
a_{2}=0
by choosing, say,
t=\frac{3+\beta}{8}
and
\cos\theta=-\frac{1+3\beta}{3+\beta}
, so that

c_{1}=-2\beta=-2b_{2}

is satisfied. We see from (
13
) that the order of growth of
f^{\prime}
at
z=1
is
\beta+2>1+1/q
, which together with the above estimate for
f^{\prime}
leads to a contradiction.

Finally, if
f^{\prime}\in H^{p}
for some
p>1/3
then by Theorem
B
we would have that
f\in H^{q}
for
q=\frac{p}{1-p}>1/2
, a contradiction, as we just saw.

∎

Recall from the introduction the subclasses
R
and
R^{+}
of
S
consisting of functions whose range is convex in one direction and convex in the positive direction, respectively. Since
R^{+}\subset R\subset K
the estimates in Theorem
8
apply to functions in
R^{+}
and
R
and, as already mentioned, when
a_{2}\neq 0
these are optimal (they are
f^{\prime}\in H^{p}
for
p<1/3
and
f\in H^{q}
for
q<1/2
). This can be seen from the function (
12
), used in the proof of Theorem
8
, which has
a_{2}=2t\in(0,2]
and maps
{\mathbb{D}}
onto the complement of two horizontal slits at heights
\pm(1-t)\frac{\pi}{4}
whose real part extends from
-\infty
to some point
x_{0}=x_{0}(t)
and, thus, belongs to
R^{+}
.

We now prove Theorem
9
, according to which if a function
f\in S
satisfies
a_{2}=0
then

(i)

if
f\in R
then
f^{\prime}\in H^{p}
for all
p<1/2
and
f\in H^{q}
for all
q<1
, while

(ii)

if
f\in R^{+}
then
f^{\prime}\in H^{p}
for all
p<1
and
f\in H^{q}
for all
q<\infty
.

Proof of Theorem
9
.

For the case (i), let
f\in R
have
a_{2}=0
and apply a rotation, if necessary, in order to get that
f({\mathbb{D}})
is convex in the vertical direction. Then, by a theorem of Royster and Ziegler
[
44
]
there exist parameters
\mu\in{\mathbb{T}}
and
n\in[0,\pi]
such that

{\rm Re}\big[-i\mu(1-2\cos(n)\overline{\mu}z+\overline{\mu}^{2}z^{2})f^{\prime}(z)\big]\geq 0,\qquad z\in{\mathbb{D}}.

Consider the function
g(z)=\overline{\mu}f(\mu z)
. We set

h(z)=-i\mu(1-2\cos(n)z+z^{2})g^{\prime}(z)

(14)

and note that it satisfies
{\rm Re}\,h\geq 0
in
{\mathbb{D}}
. Set
\mu=a+ib
. Since
h(0)=-i\mu=b-ia
we get that
b\in[0,1]
. If
b=0
then by the open mapping theorem
h\equiv-ia
and we find that

g^{\prime}(z)=\frac{1}{1-2\cos(n)z+z^{2}}.

Since
g^{\prime\prime}(0)=0
we see that
\cos(n)=0
, hence
g^{\prime}(z)=\frac{1}{1+z^{2}}
, which belongs to
H^{p}
for
p<1
.

Assume now that
b>0
. It follows from (
14
) that
h^{\prime}(0)=2i\mu\cos(n)
. Since
(h+ia)/b
is in
{\mathcal{P}}
, by Carathéodory’s theorem we have that
|h^{\prime}(0)|\leq 2b
, and therefore that
|\cos(n)|\leq b
. We distinguish two cases. First, if
|\cos(n)|=1
then also
b=1
, hence
a=0
and
\mu=i
. By the case of equality in Carathéodory’s theorem we get that

h(z)=\frac{1+\lambda z}{1-\lambda z}=1+2\lambda z+\ldots

for some
\lambda\in{\mathbb{T}}
. Hence,
\lambda=-\cos(n)=\pm 1
. In view of (
14
) we have that

h(z)=(1+\lambda z)^{2}g^{\prime}(z)

and, therefore,

g^{\prime}(z)=\frac{1}{1-\lambda^{2}z^{2}}=\frac{1}{1-z^{2}}.

Now clearly
g^{\prime}\in H^{p}
for all
p<1
. For the remaining case of
|\cos(n)|<1
we write

1-2\cos(n)z+z^{2}=(\nu-z)(\overline{\nu}-z),\qquad\text{with}\qquad\nu=e^{in}\in{\mathbb{T}}\backslash\{\pm 1\},

which shows that this expression cannot be a perfect square. Therefore, by the Cauchy-Scwharz inequality we have that

\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}|g^{\prime}|^{p}\leq\left(\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}|h|^{2p}\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\frac{1}{|\nu-z|^{2p}|\overline{\nu}-z|^{2p}}\right)^{1/2}<\infty

whenever
p<1/2
. Therefore, we have that
g^{\prime}\in H^{p}
for
p<1/2
in all subcases of case (i), hence the same is true for
f^{\prime}
. By Theorem
B
we get the desired estimate for
f
.

The sharpness of case (i) can readily be seen from the function

f(z)=\frac{z}{1+z^{2}}=z-z^{3}+\ldots,

whose image domain is
{\mathbb{C}}\backslash\{x:|x|\geq 1/2\}
, and which does not belong to
H^{1}
, nor
f^{\prime}(z)=\frac{1-z^{2}}{(1+z^{2})^{2}}
belongs to
H^{1/2}
. Also, clearly,
f\in R\backslash R^{+}
.

Turning to case (ii), we let
f\in R^{+}
with
a_{2}=0
. For each
z
in
{\mathbb{D}}
the limit

\tau=\lim_{t\to\infty}f^{-1}\big(f(z)+t\big)

exists, belongs to
{\mathbb{T}}
and is independent of
z
; see
[
14
, p.440]
, for example. It is well known that the function

g(z)=(\tau-z)(1-\overline{\tau}z)f^{\prime}(z)

satisfies
{\rm Re}\,g(z)>0
; see
[
15
, §3.5]
. Since
g(0)=\tau=a+ib
, with
a\in(0,1]
, we may normalize to get that

h(z)=\frac{g(z)-ib}{a}=1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_{n}z^{n}

belongs to
{\mathcal{P}}
. Relating the first coefficients we find that
ac_{1}=2\tau a_{2}-2=-2
, since
a_{2}=0
. Now,
2=a|c_{1}|\leq|c_{1}|\leq 2
, which implies that
a=1
and
c_{1}=-2
. Hence, we have that
h(z)=\frac{1-z}{1+z}
by the case of equality in Carathéodory’s theorem. It is therefore clear that

f^{\prime}(z)=\frac{1}{1-z^{2}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad f(z)=\frac{1}{2}\,\log\frac{1+z}{1-z},

which proves our claim and its sharpness.

∎

Let
S_{\mathbb{R}}
denote the class of functions in
S
that have real coefficients and let
T
denote the class of typically real functions,
i.e.
, functions
f
which are holomorphic in
{\mathbb{D}}
, are normalized by
f(0)=f^{\prime}(0)-1=0
, have real values on the interval
(-1,1)
and non-real values elsewhere in the disk. Clearly
S_{\mathbb{R}}\subset T
. We now prove Theorem
10
, which states that
T\subset H^{p}
for all
p<1/2
, and that this cannot be improved in
S_{\mathbb{R}}
, for any
|a_{2}|\in[0,2]
.

Proof of Theorem
10
.

Let
f\in T
and use Rogosinski’s representation
f(z)=\frac{z}{1-z^{2}}\,h(z)
, where
h\in{\mathcal{P}}
and has real coefficients; see
[
9
, §2.8]
. An application of the Cauchy-Scwharz inequality shows that

\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}|f|^{p}\leq\left(\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\frac{1}{|1-z^{2}|^{2p}}\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}|h|^{2p}\right)^{1/2}<\infty

whenever
p<1/2
.

For the second claim and the case
\alpha\in(0,2]
we can consider the function (
12
). However, we provide a proof for the whole interval
[0,2]
by using the extremal functions in Jenkins’
[
22
]
solution of the Gronwall problem and an observation of Hayman
[
20
, p.262]
: for each
\alpha\in[0,2]
there exists
f\in S_{\mathbb{R}}
with
a_{2}=\alpha
and such that

\lim_{r\to 1}(1-r)^{2}M_{\infty}(r,f)=\gamma,

where

\gamma=4\lambda^{2}e^{2-4\lambda}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lambda=\frac{1}{2-\sqrt{2-\alpha}};

see also
[
10
, §5.5]
. If it were true that
f\in H^{1/2}
then

\int_{0}^{1}M^{1/2}_{\infty}(r,f)dr<\infty

by an inequality of Hardy and Littlewood (see
[
3
, Theorem A]
for the optimal version of this inequality). However, this is readily contradicted by the fact that
\gamma>0
, which finishes the proof.

∎

7.
Integrability of the derivative of univalent functions

We now prove Theorem
11
, according to which for every
\alpha\in[0,2)
there exists
f\in S_{\mathbb{R}}
such that
a_{2}=\alpha
and its derivative
f^{\prime}
does not belong to the Nevanlinna class
N
.

Proof of Theorem
11
.

We keep the notation given in the introduction for the function
\Phi\in H^{\infty}\cap S_{\mathbb{R}}
constructed in
[
32
]
, whose derivative
\Phi^{\prime}
has radial limits almost nowhere, so that
\Phi^{\prime}\notin N
. Recall that the second coefficient of
\Phi
is equal to
1/4
.

For
\alpha=0
we consider the square root transformation
f(z)=\sqrt{\Phi(z^{2})}
which produces an odd function in
S_{\mathbb{R}}
. If
f^{\prime}
had radial limits in some open subset of
{\mathbb{T}}
then so would
\Phi^{\prime}
since
f(z)f^{\prime}(z)=z\Phi^{\prime}(z^{2})
, a contradiction. Hence
f^{\prime}\notin N
.

For
\alpha\in(0,2)
we consider the composition
f=k_{r}\circ g
, where
k_{r}(z)=\frac{1}{r}k(rz)
, with
r<1
, is a dilation of the Koebe function
k(z)=\frac{z}{(1-z)^{2}}
, and
g
is the integral transform

g(z)=\int_{0}^{z}\Phi^{\prime}(\zeta)^{\varepsilon}d\zeta,\qquad z\in{\mathbb{D}},

which is univalent for
0<\varepsilon\leq 1/4
by a theorem of Pfaltzgraff
[
35
]
. To show that the composition is well defined we prove that for each
r<1
there exists
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small so that the range of
g
lies in the disk
|z|<1/r
. Indeed, by the distortion theorem (see
[
10
, §2.3]
) we have that

\displaystyle|g(z)|

\displaystyle\leq|z|\int_{0}^{1}|\Phi^{\prime}(tz)|^{\varepsilon}\,dt

\displaystyle\leq|z|\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(1+t|z|)^{\varepsilon}}{(1-t|z|)^{3\varepsilon}}\,dt

\displaystyle<2^{\varepsilon}|z|\int_{0}^{1}\frac{dt}{(1-t|z|)^{3\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{\varepsilon}}{1-3\varepsilon}\,\big[1-(1-|z|)^{1-3\varepsilon}\big]

\displaystyle<\frac{2^{\varepsilon}}{1-3\varepsilon}.

Since the last expression is increasing with
\varepsilon
there exists a unique
\varepsilon_{0}=\varepsilon_{0}(r)>0
for which
\frac{2^{\varepsilon_{0}}}{1-3\varepsilon_{0}}=\frac{1}{r}
. It follows that if the parameters lie in

\Omega=\big\{(r,\varepsilon)\,:\,r\in(0,1),\;0<\varepsilon\leq\min\{\varepsilon_{0}(r),\tfrac{1}{4}\}\big\}

we have that
f
is a well defined function in
S
. Moreover,
f\in S_{\mathbb{R}}
since all functions involved have real coefficients. In view of

f^{\prime}(z)=k^{\prime}\big(rg(z)\big)\Phi^{\prime}(z)^{\varepsilon}

it is clear that
f^{\prime}
has radial limits almost nowhere, hence
f^{\prime}\notin N
.

It remains to show that the equation

a_{2}(f)=2r+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}=\alpha

has a solution in
\Omega
for each
\alpha\in(0,1)
. It is not hard to give explicit solutions, but for our purpose it suffices to see that
\varepsilon_{0}
is strictly decreasing to
\varepsilon_{0}(1)=0
since

\varepsilon_{0}^{\prime}(r)=-\frac{2^{\varepsilon_{0}}}{3+r2^{\varepsilon_{0}}\log 2}<0,

so that
\Omega
is a connected set, and note that

\inf_{\Omega}a_{2}(f)=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sup_{\Omega}a_{2}(f)=2,

after which the existence of a solution follows from the intermediate value theorem.

∎

8.
Appendix: Convex functions and probability measures

In view of Theorem
A
convex functions with vanishing second coefficient are very specific:

If
f\in C
has
a_{2}=0
then
f
is either bounded or a rotation of
s_{0}(z)=\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{1+z}{1-z}
.

The proof of Theorem
A
relies on the Schwarzian derivative, the fact that
C
is included in the Nehari class and on Lemma 4 from
[
6
]
. Alternatively, it follows from Theorem 3 in
[
17
]
, which provides a structural formula for unbounded convex functions. In Theorem
12
we provide an alternative statement and proof of Theorem
A
using the language of probability measures.

Let
f\in C
and
\mu
be its associated measure via the Herglotz formula (
8
). Note that
a_{2}=\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\lambda d\mu(\lambda)
and recall that a point
\lambda_{0}\in{\mathbb{T}}
is mapped by
f
to infinity if and only if
\mu
has a point mass there with
\mu(\lambda_{0})\geq\frac{1}{2}
. Also, let us denote by
\delta_{\lambda}
the Dirac measure on
\lambda
.

Theorem 12
.

If a probability measure
\mu
on
{\mathbb{T}}
has vanishing first moment and has a point mass at
\lambda_{0}
with
\mu(\lambda_{0})\geq 1/2
then

\mu=\frac{1}{2}(\delta_{\lambda_{0}}+\delta_{-\lambda_{0}}).

Proof.

Applying a rotation we may assume that
\lambda_{0}=1
. Since
\mu
is a probability measure we have that

\int_{{\mathbb{T}}\backslash\{1\}}d\mu(\lambda)=1-\mu(1).

Its first moment is zero, hence

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle={\rm Re}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\lambda d\mu(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\mu(1)+\int_{(-\pi,\pi]\backslash\{0\}}\cos\theta\,d\mu(\theta)

\displaystyle=\mu(1)+\int_{(-\pi,\pi]\backslash\{0\}}\big[2\cos^{2}(\theta/2)-1\big]d\mu(\theta)

\displaystyle=2\mu(1)-1+\int_{(-\pi,\pi]\backslash\{0\}}2\cos^{2}(\theta/2)\,d\mu(\theta)

\displaystyle\geq 2\mu(1)-1.

It follows that
\mu(1)=1/2
.

Once again, the vanishing of the first moment yields

0={\rm Re}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\lambda d\mu(\lambda)=2\int_{(-\pi,\pi]}\cos^{2}(\theta/2)\,d\mu(\theta)-1.

Hence,

\frac{1}{2}=\int_{(-\pi,\pi]}\cos^{2}(\theta/2)\,d\mu(\theta)=\mu(1)+\int_{(-\pi,\pi]\backslash\{0\}}\cos^{2}(\theta/2)\,d\mu(\theta),

so that

\int_{(-\pi,\pi]\backslash\{0\}}\cos^{2}(\theta/2)\,d\mu(\theta)=0.

Since the integrand is non-negative, the measure
\mu
on
(-\pi,\pi]\backslash\{0\}
can only be supported where
\cos(\theta/2)
vanishes, that is, at
\theta=\pi
. Hence,
{\rm supp}(\mu)=\{\pm 1\}
and the proof is complete.

∎
