Vielbein Lattice Boltzmann approach for fluid flows on spherical surfaces
Victor E. Ambru s ,, Elisa Bellantoni, Sergiu Busuioc, Alessandro Gabbana, Federico Toschi

Abstract
In this paper, we develop a lattice Boltzmann scheme based on the vielbein formalism for the study of fluid flows on spherical surfaces. The vielbein vector field encodes all details related to the geometry of the underlying spherical surface, allowing the velocity space to be treated as on the Cartesian space. The resulting Boltzmann equation exhibits inertial (geometric) forces, that ensure that fluid particles follow paths that remain on the spherical manifold, which we compute by projection onto the space of Hermite polynomials. Due to the point-dependent nature of the advection velocity in the polar coordinate θ \theta , exact streaming is not feasible, and we instead employ finite-difference schemes. We provide a detailed formulation of the lattice Boltzmann algorithm, with particular attention to boundary conditions at the north and south poles. We validate our numerical implementation against two analytical solutions of the Navier-Stokes equations derived in this work: the propagation of sound and shear waves. Additionally, we assess the robustness of the scheme by simulating the compressible flow of an axisymmetric shockwave and analyzing vortex dynamics on the spherical surface.



I
Introduction

Fluid flows on curved manifolds are ubiquitous to several physical systems in nature,
ranging from interface rheology in foams
[
1
]
, the dynamics of confined active matter
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
, geophysical flows of oceanic and atmospheric circulation
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
up to exotic applications in the study of the motion of electrons in 2D materials
[
8
]
or quark-gluon plasma formed in heavy-ion collisions
[
9
,
10
]
.

The spherical geometry holds particular relevance for geophysical flow modeling,
as complex three-dimensional flows in atmospheres and oceans are typically
modeled assuming planetary surfaces can be approximated as spheres.
Global atmospheric circulation on Earth and large planets is commonly
modeled by two-dimensional Euler or Navier-Stokes equations (often in the
vorticity-stream function formulation
[
11
,
12
,
13
]
)
coupled to classic thermodynamics, quasi-geostrophic equations, and rotating shallow water equations
[
14
]
.
Notably, spherical geometry has been identified as the optimal testing ground
for exploring two-dimensional turbulence through means of numerical simulations
[
15
]
.
These flows are characterized by large vortex structures due to the inverse energy cascade,
combined with scale filaments due to the enstrophy cascade, and it is well known that, under
real-world parameter ranges, the energy spectrum extends over several orders of magnitude,
making a direct numerical simulation (DNS) approach computationally unfeasible.
This calls for the development of new, efficient and accurate numerical methods leveraging
the ever-growing computational power available on modern massively parallel architectures.
In addition, analytical solutions for fluid flows on the sphere are rare
[
16
]
, which makes it generally difficult to benchmark and compare the performances of various numerical schemes.

The contributions provided in this work are twofold: i) we define two benchmarks for axisymmetric flows
on the sphere, presenting exact analytic solutions of the Navier-Stokes equations, and ii) we
introduce a lattice Boltzmann method formulation based on differential geometry.

LBM has emerged as a highly efficient and flexible mesoscopic approach
for the simulation of many different fluid flows
[
17
]
.
Although its standard formulation is based on the use of Cartesian grids,
in the past decades several formulations have been proposed to extend the
applicability of the method to curvilinear coordinates.
One first approach
[
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
]

consists in moving the metric dependence from the streaming part of the lattice Boltzmann equation
to the forcing and collision part, adding additional terms that can be identified by performing
a Chapman-Enskog expansion to match the target macroscopic equations.
The advantage of this approach is that exact streaming is preserved; however, the resulting scheme typically supports only mildly curved surfaces.
An alternative approach consists of making the advection velocity space coordinate dependent.
No extra source terms are required. However, the price to pay is the loss of exact streaming,
hence requiring interpolation for the implementation of the advection step, which introduces
extra numerical dissipation. Most of these schemes are based on finite difference or
finite volume methods
[
22
,
23
,
24
,
25
]
.
Recently, this approach has been employed in an elegant formulation based on differential
geometry and vielbein fields, closely related to the definition of kinetic theory
in general relativity
[
26
]
, which allows for
advection velocities to become coordinate independent
[
27
,
28
]
, in principle simplifying the task of handling more complex geometries.

In this work, we adopt the vielbein approach to define an LBM for describing
fluid flows on the spherical surface. We assume radial fluid motion
is suppressed by internal or external centripetal forces
(e.g., attraction between a spherical membrane and biological fluid above it),
allowing the description of vector fields and their gradients using differential geometry.
The vielbein fields separate the local geometry from the definition of vector field components.
This is convenient when describing the local phase space.
The conceptual simplicity of the vielbein scheme makes it a promising candidate for tackling more complex geometries,
such as those relevant to biomembrane modelling
[
29
]
, including the case of time-dependent surfaces
[
30
,
31
]
.

For example, the square of the particle
velocity shows an explicit geometry dependence when expressed with respect to the spherical
coordinate basis,
\mathbf{v}^{2}=(v^{\theta})^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta(v^{\varphi})^{2}
. On the contrary,
with respect to the vielbein components
(v^{\hat{\theta}},v^{\hat{\varphi}})=(v^{\theta},\sin\theta v^{\varphi})
, the square of the velocity becomes coordinate independent:

\mathbf{v}^{2}=(v^{\hat{\theta}})^{2}+(v^{\hat{\varphi}})^{2}
.
We validate our scheme through numerical benchmarks, examining the dynamics of sound
and shear waves while comparing the accuracy and convergence order with different interpolation
methods for the advection step. We further test our scheme in compressible flows using
a Sod shock tube-like setup that produces propagating and reflecting shock waves.
We solve these axisymmetric problems using both axisymmetric and rotated grids,
requiring fully 2D simulation for the latter, in order to validate our scheme’s isotropy.
We conclude by studying two-vortex dynamics on a spherical surface.

In Section

Readers primarily interested in the numerical results may proceed directly to Section
IV

V
.
Finally, concluding remarks and future directions are summarized in Section
VI

II.1
Preliminaries: The vielbein formalism

Vectors and tensors in curved spaces are generally treated by employing curvilinear coordinates and differential geometry.
These allow us to define fundamental notions such as distances, derivatives and integrals in a non-flat geometry, which are necessary to describe physical phenomena.
First, a curvilinear coordinate system that parametrizes the curved space is chosen.
Vector fields, such as the velocity field
\mathbf{u{(x)}}
, can be expressed in
a curvilinear coordinate system
as:

\mathbf{u{(x)}}=u^{a}{(q^{b})}{\bf\it\partial}_{a},

(1)

where
q^{b}
represent the curvilinear coordinates chosen to parametrize the surface,

u^{a}{(q^{b})}
represent the velocity field components and
{\bf\it\partial}_{a}
represent the basis vectors.
Hereafter, we adopt Einstein’s summation convention over repeated indexes.
The spherical surface can be seen as a two-dimensional manifold embedded in a three-dimensional space, so that
a,b=\{1,2\}
.

The squared norm of the velocity field
\mathbf{u}
is given by:

\mathbf{u}^{2}=g_{ab}u^{a}u^{b}.

(2)

The metric tensor
g_{ab}
can be read off from the line element
{\differential s}^{2}=dx^{2}+dy^{2}+dz^{2}
,
which is expressed with respect to the coordinates
\{q^{a}\}
parametrizing the surface via

{\differential s}^{2}=g_{ab}\differential q^{a}\differential q^{b},\qquad g_{ab}=\delta_{ij}\partialderivative{x^i}{q^a}\partialderivative{x^j}{q^b},

(3)

with
i,j=\{1,2,3\}
referring to the Cartesian coordinates and
a,b
referring
to the curvilinear coordinates.

Next, we define a set of vector fields
\mathbf{e}_{\hat{a}}=e^{a}_{\hat{a}}{\bf\it\partial}_{a}

and use it as an orthonormal basis to rewrite vector quantities
so that the velocity field becomes:

\mathbf{u}=u^{\hat{a}}{\bf\it e}_{\hat{a}}.

(4)

The vielbein vector fields (frame) and its dual vielbein one-form fields (co-frame),
defined by
{\bf\it\omega}^{\hat{a}}=\omega^{\hat{a}}_{a}{\bf\it\differential q}^{a}
,
satisfy the following relations
[
32
]
:

g_{ab}e^{a}_{\hat{a}}e^{b}_{\hat{b}}=\delta_{\hat{a}\hat{b}},\quad\omega^{\hat{a}}_{a}e^{a}_{\hat{b}}=\delta^{\hat{a}}_{\hat{b}},\quad\omega^{\hat{a}}_{a}e^{b}_{\hat{a}}=\delta^{b}_{a},

(5)

where hatted indices refer to the vielbein frame components and
non-hatted indices to coordinate space components.
It follows that the components of the vector field
\mathbf{u}
can be expressed as

u^{\hat{a}}=\omega^{\hat{a}}_{a}u^{a},\quad u^{a}=e^{a}_{\hat{a}}u^{\hat{a}},

(6)

with squared norm

\mathbf{u}^{2}=\delta_{\hat{a}\hat{b}}u^{\hat{a}}u^{\hat{b}}.

(7)

We now introduce the Cartan coefficients and the connection coefficients.
The Cartan coefficients
c^{{\mathchoice{\makebox[11.11115pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[11.11115pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[11.11115pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[11.11115pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}{\hat{c}}}}_{{{\hat{a}\hat{b}}\mathchoice{\makebox[5.55557pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[5.55557pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[5.55557pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[5.55557pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}
are defined as the contraction
between the co-frame one-form
{\bf\it\omega}^{\hat{c}}
and the commutator of the frame vector field

[{\bf\it e}_{\hat{a}},{\bf\it e}_{\hat{b}}]:=(e^{a}_{\hat{a}}\partial_{a}e^{c}_{\hat{b}}-e^{b}_{\hat{b}}\partial_{b}e^{c}_{\hat{a}}){\bf\it\partial}_{c}\equiv c_{\hat{a}\hat{b}}{}^{\hat{c}}e_{\hat{c}}^{c}\boldsymbol{\partial}_{c}.

(8)

Therefore, the Cartan coefficients can be expressed as:

c^{{\mathchoice{\makebox[11.11115pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[11.11115pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[11.11115pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[11.11115pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}{\hat{c}}}}_{{{\hat{a}\hat{b}}\mathchoice{\makebox[5.55557pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[5.55557pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[5.55557pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[5.55557pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}:=\omega^{\hat{c}}_{c}(e^{a}_{\hat{a}}\partial_{a}e^{c}_{\hat{b}}-e^{b}_{\hat{b}}\partial_{b}e^{c}_{\hat{a}}).

(9)

The connection coefficients
\Gamma^{{{\hat{d}}\mathchoice{\makebox[11.11115pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[11.11115pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[11.11115pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[11.11115pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}_{{\mathchoice{\makebox[5.55557pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[5.55557pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[5.55557pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[5.55557pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}{\hat{b}\hat{c}}}}
directly relate to the space curvature and the choice of curvilinear coordinates.
They are linked to the Cartan coefficients via:

\Gamma^{{{\hat{d}}\mathchoice{\makebox[11.11115pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[11.11115pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[11.11115pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[11.11115pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}_{{\mathchoice{\makebox[5.55557pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[5.55557pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[5.55557pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[5.55557pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}{\hat{b}\hat{c}}}}=\delta^{\hat{a}\hat{d}}\Gamma_{\hat{a}\hat{b}\hat{c}},\quad\Gamma_{\hat{a}\hat{b}\hat{c}}=\frac{1}{2}(c_{\hat{a}\hat{b}\hat{c}}+c_{\hat{a}\hat{c}\hat{b}}-c_{\hat{b}\hat{c}\hat{a}}).

As usual, it is possible to lower and raise indices in the vielbein frame via the Kronecker delta

\delta_{\hat{a}\hat{b}}
and its inverse
\delta^{\hat{a}\hat{b}}
,
e.g.

c_{\hat{b}\hat{c}\hat{d}}=\delta_{\hat{a}\hat{d}}c^{{\mathchoice{\makebox[11.11115pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[11.11115pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[11.11115pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[11.11115pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}{\hat{a}}}}_{{{\hat{b}\hat{c}}\mathchoice{\makebox[5.55557pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[5.55557pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[5.55557pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[5.55557pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}=c^{{\mathchoice{\makebox[11.11115pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[11.11115pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[11.11115pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[11.11115pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}{\hat{d}}}}_{{{\hat{b}\hat{c}}\mathchoice{\makebox[5.55557pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[5.55557pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[5.55557pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[5.55557pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}.

(11)

From the definition above, one can define the

\nabla_{\hat{a}}{F}=e^{a}_{\hat{a}}\partial_{a}{F}.

(12)

Moreover, the covariant derivative of a vector field

is:

\nabla_{\hat{b}}u^{\hat{a}}=e^{b}_{\hat{b}}\partial_{b}u^{\hat{a}}+\Gamma^{{{\hat{a}}\mathchoice{\makebox[11.11115pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[11.11115pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[11.11115pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[11.11115pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}_{{\mathchoice{\makebox[5.55557pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[5.55557pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[5.55557pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[5.55557pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}{\hat{c}\hat{b}}}}u^{\hat{c}},\ \nabla_{\hat{b}}u_{\hat{a}}=e^{b}_{\hat{b}}\partial_{b}u_{\hat{a}}-\Gamma^{{{\hat{c}}\mathchoice{\makebox[11.11115pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[11.11115pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[11.11115pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[11.11115pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}_{{\mathchoice{\makebox[5.55557pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[5.55557pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[5.55557pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[5.55557pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}{\hat{a}\hat{b}}}}u_{\hat{c}},

\mathbf{u}
is:

\nabla_{\hat{a}}u^{\hat{a}}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\partial_{a}(\sqrt{g}e^{a}_{\hat{a}}u^{\hat{a}}),

(15)

with

,
which we parametrize using the spherical polar coordinates
\theta\in[0,\pi]
and
\varphi\in[0,2\pi)

The position vector

spherical surface, shown in Fig.

is expressed in terms of the spherical coordinates via

x=R\sin{\theta}\cos{\varphi},\quad y=R\sin{\theta}\sin{\varphi},\quad z=R\cos{\theta}.

(16)

and

II.3
Hydrodynamics in spherical coordinates

In this section, we derive the hydrodynamic equations for a fluid on a spherical
surface. The starting point is given by the continuity equation and Navier-Stokes equations in covariant vielbein formalism:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\rho}+\nabla_{\hat{a}}(\rho u^{\hat{a}})

\displaystyle=0,

(25a)

\displaystyle\rho\dfrac{\mathrm{D}u^{\hat{a}}}{\mathrm{D}t}-\nabla_{\hat{b}}{\tau^{\hat{a}\hat{b}}}

\displaystyle=0,

(25b)

where
\mathrm{D}/\mathrm{D}{t}:=\partial_{t}+u^{\hat{b}}\nabla_{\hat{b}}
is the convective derivative,
\rho
is the fluid density, and
u^{\hat{a}}
is the fluid velocity. The stress tensor
\tau^{\hat{a}\hat{b}}
of a viscous ideal gas is

\tau^{\hat{a}\hat{b}}=-p\delta^{\hat{a}\hat{b}}+\sigma^{\hat{a}\hat{b}},

(26)

with pressure

is the particle mass,

is the Boltzmann constant. The viscous stress tensor

(27)

\displaystyle\tau^{\hat{\theta}\hat{\varphi}}

), a tedious calculation leads to the Navier-Stokes equations without external forces:

\rho\pqty{ \partial_t u^{\hat{\theta}}
+ \dfrac{u^{\hat{\theta}}}{R} \partial_{\theta} u^{\hat{\theta}}
+ \dfrac{u^{\hat{\varphi}} \partial_{\varphi} u^{\hat{\theta}}}{R \sin{\theta}} - \dfrac{u_{\hat{\varphi}}^2}{R\tan{\theta}} }=-\frac{\partial_{\theta}p}{R}\\
+\frac{\eta+\zeta}{R^{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left[\frac{\partial_{\theta}(\sin\theta u^{\hat{\theta}})}{\sin\theta}\right]+\frac{2\eta}{R^{2}}u^{\hat{\theta}}+\frac{\eta\partial^{2}_{\varphi}u^{\hat{\theta}}}{R^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}\\
+\frac{\zeta}{R^{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left(\frac{\partial_{\varphi}u^{\hat{\varphi}}}{\sin\theta}\right)-\frac{2\eta}{R^{2}}\cos\theta\partial_{\varphi}u^{\hat{\varphi}},

(33b)

\rho\pqty{ \partial_t u^{\hat{\varphi}}
+ \dfrac{u^{\hat{\theta}}}{R} \partial_{\theta} u^{\hat{\varphi}}
+ \dfrac{u^{\hat{\varphi}} \partial_{\varphi} u^{\hat{\varphi}}}{R \sin{\theta}} + \dfrac{u^{\hat{\theta}} u^{\hat{\varphi}}}{R\tan{\theta}} }=-\dfrac{\partial_{\varphi}p}{R\sin{\theta}}\\
+\frac{(\eta+\zeta)\partial^{2}_{\varphi}u^{\hat{\varphi}}}{R^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}+\frac{\zeta\partial_{\theta}(\sin\theta\partial_{\varphi}u^{\hat{\theta}})}{R^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}+\frac{2\eta\partial_{\varphi}u^{\hat{\theta}}}{R^{2}\sin\theta\tan\theta}\\
+\frac{\eta}{R^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left[\sin^{3}\theta\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left(\frac{u^{\hat{\varphi}}}{\sin\theta}\right)\right],

(33c)

where we assumed that the dynamical and bulk viscosities,
\eta
and
\zeta
, are coordinate-independent.

II.4
Axisymmetric flows

For benchmarking purposes, we will consider the case when the flow configuration is axisymmetric. In this case, the derivatives with respect to
\varphi
vanish and the Navier-Stokes equations (
33
) reduce to:

\partial_{t}\rho+\frac{\partial_{\theta}(\rho\sin\theta u^{\hat{\theta}})}{R\sin\theta}=0,

(34a)

\rho\pqty{ \partial_t u^{\hat{\theta}}
+ \dfrac{u^{\hat{\theta}}}{R} \partial_{\theta} u^{\hat{\theta}}
- \dfrac{u_{\hat{\varphi}}^2}{R\tan{\theta}} }=-\frac{\partial_{\theta}p}{R}\\
+\frac{\eta+\zeta}{R^{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left[\frac{\partial_{\theta}(\sin\theta u^{\hat{\theta}})}{\sin\theta}\right]+\frac{2\eta}{R^{2}}u^{\hat{\theta}},

(34b)

e\rho\pqty{ \partial_t u^{\hat{\varphi}}
+ \dfrac{u^{\hat{\theta}}}{R} \partial_{\theta} u^{\hat{\varphi}}
+ \dfrac{u^{\hat{\theta}} u^{\hat{\varphi}}}{R\tan{\theta}} }=\\
\frac{\eta}{R^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left[\sin^{3}\theta\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left(\frac{u^{\hat{\varphi}}}{\sin\theta}\right)\right].

(34c)

III
Numerical Method: vielbein lattice Boltzmann on the sphere

In this section, we describe the steps required to define the discrete Boltzmann equation
in the vielbein formalism on the sphere.
The reader not concerned with the details can skip directly to Sec.
III.8
, where we give a summary of the method.
The starting point is given by the covariant Boltzmann equation
[
32
,
27
]
, which we consider in the absence of external forces:

\partialderivative{f}{t}+\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\partialderivative{q^{b}}\pqty{ v^{\hat{a}} e^{b}_{\hat{a}}f \sqrt{g} }-\Gamma^{\hat{a}}{}_{\hat{b}\hat{c}}\frac{\partial(v^{\hat{b}}v^{\hat{c}}f)}{\partial v^{\hat{a}}}=C[f],

(35)

where
f\equiv f(q^{a},v^{\hat{b}},t)
is the single-particle distribution function,
v^{\hat{a}}
are the vielbein components of the particle velocity and
C[f]
is the collision term.
The third term on the left-hand side of Eq. (
35
) shows that the connection coefficients give rise to forcing terms, whose role is to enforce the propagation of fluid particles along the curved surface. They are thus related to the intrinsic curvature of the chosen geometry or the employment of curvilinear coordinates.

In the case of the spherical geometry described in Sec.
II.2
, Eq. (
35
) becomes:

\partialderivative{f}{t}+\dfrac{v^{\hat{\theta}}}{R\sin{\theta}}\partialderivative{(f\sin{\theta})}{\theta}+\dfrac{v^{\hat{\varphi}}}{R\sin{\theta}}\partialderivative{f}{\varphi}\\
+\dfrac{\cos{\theta}}{R\sin{\theta}}\bqty{v^{\hat{\varphi}}\pdv{(f v^{\hat{\varphi}})}{v^{\hat{\theta}}} - v^{\hat{\theta}}\pdv{(f v^{\hat{\varphi}})}{v^{\hat{\varphi}}}}=C[f].

(36)

This is the equation that we need to discretize in order to derive the lattice Boltzmann equation and construct our vielbein lattice Boltzmann method for the simulation
of fluid flows on the surface of a sphere.

In the remainder of this section, we present all the steps required for the construction of the lattice Boltzmann algorithm for the solution of Eq. (
36
). In Subsec.
III.1
, we discuss the discretization of the velocity space using the Gauss-Hermite quadrature.
Subsec.
III.2
presents the construction of the collision term, for which we employ the BGK model
[
34
]
.
Subsec.
III.3
describes the computation of the derivatives with respect to the velocity, which are required to implement the curvature terms.
Subsections
III.4
and
III.5
present the space discretization and the flux-based, finite-difference advection scheme, while boundary conditions are discussed in Subsec.
III.6
.
Finally, Subsec.
III.7
presents the time-stepping algorithm, for which we employ the Runge-Kutta explicit time stepping.

III.1
Velocity discretization

One key aspect in the derivation of a lattice Boltzmann method is the discretization of the velocity space.
To achieve this, we introduce the tensor Hermite polynomials,
denoted as
\mathcal{H}^{(\ell)}_{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{\ell}}(\mathbf{v})
and defined as
[
35
,
36
]
:

\mathcal{H}^{(\ell)}_{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{\ell}}(\mathbf{v})=\dfrac{(-1)^{\ell}}{\omega(\mathbf{v})}\frac{\partial^{\ell}\omega(\mathbf{v})}{\partial v_{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\partial v_{\alpha_{\ell}}},\quad\omega(\mathbf{v})=\dfrac{e^{-\mathbf{v}^{2}/2}}{(2\pi)^{D/2}}.

(37)

For a
D
-dimensional space, the indices satisfy
1\leq\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{\ell}\leq D
,
and an expansion up to order
Q
includes polynomials of orders
0\leq\ell<Q
.
The first four Hermite polynomials are:

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{(0)}(\mathbf{v})=1,\quad\mathcal{H}^{(1)}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{v})=v_{\alpha},\quad\mathcal{H}^{(2)}_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{v})=v_{\alpha}v_{\beta}-\delta_{\alpha\beta},

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{(3)}_{\alpha\beta\gamma}(\mathbf{v})=v_{\alpha}v_{\beta}v_{\gamma}-\pqty{\delta_{\alpha\beta} v_{\gamma}+\delta_{\beta\gamma} v_{\alpha}+\delta_{\gamma\alpha} v_{\beta}}.

(38)

Following a Gauss-Hermite quadrature rule, the roots of the one-dimensional Hermite polynomial
H_{Q}(v_{k_{\alpha}})
(
1\leq k_{\alpha}\leq Q
)
define the Cartesian components
v_{k_{\alpha}}
of the discrete velocity set along axis
\alpha
.
The vielbein formalism ensures that these components remain valid even in non-Cartesian geometries.

The quadrature weights for order
Q
along axis
\alpha
are given by:

w_{k_{\alpha}}=\dfrac{Q!}{H^{2}_{Q+1}(v_{k_{\alpha}})}.

(39)

The overall quadrature weights are
w_{\mathbf{k}}=w_{k_{\alpha_{1}}}\dots w_{k_{\alpha_{\ell}}}
.

A Gauss-Hermite quadrature of order
Q

[
37
,
36
]
exactly recovers all moments up to order

Q-1
of the equilibrium distribution function
f^{\mathrm{eq}}
, by projecting
f^{\mathrm{eq}}

onto the space of Hermite polynomials (see Subsec.
III.2
).
Therefore, conserved moments can be computed exactly via discrete summation over
f_{\mathbf{k}}
:

n=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}f_{\mathbf{k}},\quad n\mathbf{u}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}f_{\mathbf{k}}\mathbf{v_{k}},

(40)

with the discrete distribution function
f_{\mathbf{k}}
related to the Boltzmann distribution
f
via:

f_{\mathbf{k}}=\dfrac{w_{\mathbf{k}}}{\omega(\mathbf{v_{k}})}f(\mathbf{v_{k}}).

(41)

The exact recovery of the first three moments of the distribution function is essential for any kinetic model that aims to capture the correct hydrodynamic equations. The transition from the mesoscopic kinetic description to the macroscopic Navier-Stokes equations framework is achieved via a Chapman-Enskog expansion in the Knudsen number
[
37
]
(see Appendix
A.4
).

III.2
Collision term discretization

In this paper, we consider the Bhatnagar-Gross-Krook (BGK) model for the collision operator,

C[f]=-\frac{1}{\tau}(f-f^{\mathrm{eq}}),

(42)

where
\tau
is the relaxation time, and
f^{\mathrm{eq}}
is the local equilibrium distribution,
modeled using the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution,

f^{\mathrm{eq}}=n\left(\frac{m}{2\pi k_{B}T}\right)^{D/2}\exp\left[-\frac{m(\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{u})^{2}}{2k_{B}T}\right].

(43)

In what follows, we consider isothermal flows at
T=T_{0}
,
and adopt a non-dimensionalization of velocities based on the thermal reference speed

c_{\rm ref}=\sqrt{k_{B}T/m}
, such that the expression for the local equilibrium simplifies down to

f^{\rm eq}=\frac{n}{(2\pi)^{D/2}}e^{-(\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{u})^{2}/2}.

(44)

In order to discretize the collision operator, we consider an expansion of the
equilibrium distribution in terms of Hermite polynomial
[
35
,
36
]
:

f^{\mathrm{eq}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v},t)=\omega(\mathbf{v})\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\ell!}a^{\mathrm{eq},(\ell)}_{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{\ell}}(\mathbf{x},t)\mathcal{H}^{(\ell)}_{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{\ell}}(\mathbf{v}),

(45)

where a summation over the repeated Greek indices is implied,
and the expansion coefficients are defined as:

a^{\mathrm{eq},(\ell)}_{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}}(\mathbf{x},t)=\int\differential{\vb{v}}f^{\mathrm{eq}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v},t)\mathcal{H}^{(\ell)}_{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{\ell}}(\mathbf{v}).

(46)

It follows that the first four coefficients are given by:

\displaystyle a^{\mathrm{eq},(0)}=n,\quad a^{\mathrm{eq},(1)}_{\alpha}=nu_{\alpha},

\displaystyle a^{\mathrm{eq},(2)}_{\alpha\beta}=nu_{\alpha}u_{\beta},\quad a^{\mathrm{eq},(3)}_{\alpha\beta\gamma}=nu_{\alpha}u_{\beta}u_{\gamma}.

(47)

Since the expansion coefficients coincide with the hydrodynamic quantities of interest,
it directly follows that considering an expansion of
f^{\mathrm{eq}}
truncated at the order
N=Q-1

f^{Q,\mathrm{eq}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v},t)=\omega(\mathbf{v})\sum_{\ell=0}^{Q-1}\frac{1}{\ell!}a^{\mathrm{eq},(\ell)}_{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{\ell}}\mathcal{H}^{(\ell)}_{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{\ell}}(\mathbf{v}),

(48)

all the moments of
f^{\rm eq}
up to order
N=Q-1
are preserved exactly.

Next, following the discretization of the velocity space, the continuous distribution
f^{\rm eq}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v},t)

is replaced by a discrete set of distributions
f^{\mathrm{eq}}_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{x},t)
,
which are related to the original continuous distribution as shown in Eq. (
41
), that is,

f^{\mathrm{eq}}_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{x},t)=\frac{w_{\mathbf{k}}}{\omega(\mathbf{v}_{\mathbf{k})}}f^{\rm eq}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v}_{\mathbf{k}},t).

(49)

In conclusion, the truncated discrete equilibrium distribution can be expressed as:

f^{Q,\mathrm{eq}}_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{x},t)=\omega(\mathbf{v})\sum_{\ell=0}^{Q-1}\frac{1}{\ell!}a^{\mathrm{eq},(\ell)}_{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{\ell}}\mathcal{H}^{(\ell)}_{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{\ell}}(\mathbf{v_{k}}).

(50)

Full expressions for the cases
Q=3
and
Q=4
can be found in Appendix
A
.

III.3
Velocity gradients discretization

We now discuss the strategy for computing the velocity gradients
\partialderivative*{f}{v^{\hat{\theta}}}
and
\partialderivative*{(fv^{\hat{\varphi}})}{v^{\hat{\varphi}}}
appearing in Eq. (
36
),
by projecting these derivatives onto the space of the corresponding one-dimensional Hermite polynomials.
First, we consider the Hermite expansion of
f(v^{\hat{\theta}},v^{\hat{\varphi}})
with respect to either
v^{\hat{\theta}}
or
v^{\hat{\varphi}}
:

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle=\omega(v^{\hat{\theta}})\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\ell!}\mathcal{F}^{\theta}_{\ell}(v^{\hat{\varphi}})H_{\ell}(v^{\hat{\theta}})

\displaystyle=\omega(v^{\hat{\varphi}})\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\ell!}\mathcal{F}^{\varphi}_{\ell}(v^{\hat{\theta}})H_{\ell}(v^{\hat{\varphi}}).

(51)

The velocity derivatives of
f
can be taken using the following relations:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial[\omega(v^{\hat{1}})H_{\ell}(v^{\hat{1}})]}{\partial v^{\hat{1}}}

\displaystyle=-\omega(v^{\hat{1}})H_{\ell+1}(v^{\hat{1}}),

(52a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial[\omega(v^{\hat{1}})v^{\hat{1}}H_{\ell}(v^{\hat{1}})]}{\partial v^{\hat{1}}}

\displaystyle=-\omega(v^{\hat{1}})[\ell H_{\ell}(v^{\hat{1}})

\displaystyle+(\ell+1)(\ell+2)H_{\ell+2}(v^{\hat{1}})].

(52b)

This leads to the following results:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f}{\partial v^{\hat{1}}}

\displaystyle=-\omega(v^{\hat{1}})\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{\ell}{\ell!}\mathcal{F}_{\ell-1}(v^{\hat{2}})H_{\ell}(v^{\hat{1}}),

(53a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial(fv^{\hat{1}})}{\partial v^{\hat{1}}}

\displaystyle=-\omega(v^{\hat{1}})\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\ell!}[\ell\mathcal{F}_{\ell}+\ell(\ell-1)\mathcal{F}_{\ell-2}]H_{\ell}(v^{\hat{1}}).

(53b)

The expansion coefficients
\mathcal{F}^{\theta}_{\ell}(v^{\hat{\varphi}})
and
\mathcal{F}^{\varphi}_{\ell}(v^{\hat{\theta}})
can be evaluated by exploiting the orthogonality of the Hermite polynomials,

\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\differential{x}\omega(x)H_{\ell}(x)H_{\ell^{\prime}}(x)=\ell!\delta_{\ell\ell^{\prime}},\ \omega(x)=\dfrac{e^{-x^{2}/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}},

(54)

giving

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\theta}_{\ell}(v^{\hat{\varphi}})

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dv^{\hat{\theta}}\,f\,H_{\ell}(v^{\hat{\theta}}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\varphi}_{\ell}(v^{\hat{\theta}})

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dv^{\hat{\varphi}}\,f\,H_{\ell}(v^{\hat{\varphi}}).

(55)

After the velocity space discretization,
\mathcal{F}^{\theta}_{\ell}(v^{\hat{\varphi}})
and
\mathcal{F}^{\varphi}_{\ell}(v^{\hat{\theta}})
are replaced by

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\theta}_{\ell,k_{\varphi}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k_{\theta}=1}^{Q}H_{\ell}(v_{k_{\theta}})f_{k_{\theta},k_{\varphi}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\varphi}_{\ell,k_{\theta}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k_{\varphi}=1}^{Q}H_{\ell}(v_{k_{\varphi}})f_{k_{\theta},k_{\varphi}}.

(56)

In an analogue way, we project and discretize the velocity gradients of
f
:

\displaystyle\pqty{ \pdv{f}{v^{\hat{1}}}}_{\mathbf{k}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k^{\prime}_{1}=1}^{Q}\mathcal{K}^{H}_{k_{1},k^{\prime}_{1}}f_{k^{\prime}_{1},k_{2}}

(57a)

\displaystyle\pqty{ \pdv{(f v^{\hat{1}})}{v^{\hat{1}}} }_{\mathbf{k}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k^{\prime}_{1}=1}^{Q}\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}^{H}_{k_{1},k^{\prime}_{1}}f_{k^{\prime}_{1},k_{2}},

(57b)

where the kernels
\mathcal{K}^{H}_{k_{\theta},k^{\prime}_{\theta}}
,
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}^{H}_{k_{\varphi},k^{\prime}_{\varphi}}
have the following expressions
[
38
,
32
]
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}^{H}_{k,k^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=-w_{k}\sum_{\ell=0}^{Q-1}\dfrac{1}{\ell!}H_{\ell+1}(v_{k})H_{\ell}(v_{k^{\prime}}),

(58a)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}^{H}_{k,k^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=-w_{k}\sum_{\ell=0}^{Q-1}\dfrac{1}{\ell!}H_{\ell+1}(v_{k})\bqty{ H_{\ell+1}(v_{k'}) + \ell H_{\ell-1}(v_{k'}) }.

(58b)

The explicit expressions of the objects introduced in this section for the quadrature orders
Q=3
and
Q=4
are found in Appendix
A
.

III.4
Space discretization

The discretization of the velocity space via the Gauss-Hermite quadrature procedure seen above leads to a discrete version of the Boltzmann equation which, for the sphere, corresponds to:

\partialderivative{f_{\vb{k}}}{t}+\dfrac{1}{R\sin{\theta}}\bqty{ \pdv{ (f_{\vb{k}} v_{k_\theta} \sin{\theta}) }{\theta} + \pdv{ (f_{\vb{k}} v_{k_\varphi}) }{\varphi} }\\
+\dfrac{\cos{\theta}}{R\sin{\theta}}\bqty{ v^2_{k_\varphi} \pqty{ \pdv{f}{v^{\hat{\theta}}} }_{\vb{k}} - v_{k_\theta} \pqty{ \pdv{ (f v^{ \hat{\varphi}}) }{ v^{\hat{\varphi}}} }_{\vb{k}} }\\
=-\dfrac{1}{\tau}\bqty{ f_{\vb{k}} - f^{\mathrm{eq}}_{\vb{k}} }.

(59)

We now discretize the space via an equidistant grid along the
\theta
and
\varphi
coordinates. Since
\theta\in[0,\pi]
and
\varphi\in[0,2\pi)
,
we take
N_{\theta}
points along
\theta
and
N_{\varphi}
points along
\varphi
such that:

\displaystyle\theta_{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{N_{\theta}}(s-1/2)\qquad(1\leq s\leq N_{\theta}),

(60a)

\displaystyle\varphi_{q}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi}{N_{\varphi}}(q-1/2)\qquad(1\leq q\leq N_{\varphi}).

(60b)

In this two-dimensional coordinate space, each cell
(s,q)
has four interfaces which we write as
(s+1/2,q),(s-1/2,q),(s,q+1/2)
and
(s,q-1/2)
. For simplicity, we will assume here equidistant cells along both
\theta
and
\varphi
, thus

\variation{\theta}=\pi/N_{\theta}
and
\variation{\varphi}=2\pi/N_{\varphi}
, respectively. Note, however, that in general it is desirable to consider the more general case where a non-uniform discretization is employed along
\theta
to avoid accumulation of grid points in the proximity of the poles.

III.5
Advection

To complete the spatial discretization of the method, we write the advection term appearing in the parentheses on the first line of (
59
) as:

\Bqty{ \dfrac{1}{R \sin{\theta}} \bqty{ \pdv{\theta}( f_{\vb{k}} v_{k_\theta} \sin{\theta} ) + \pdv{\varphi} ( f_{\vb{k}} v_{k_\varphi} ) } }_{s,q}=\\
\dfrac{1}{R\sin{\theta_s}}\bqty{
\dfrac{\mathcal{F}^{\theta}_{s + \frac{1}{2},q} - \mathcal{F}^{\theta}_{s - \frac{1}{2}, q }}{\delta\theta} +
\dfrac{\mathcal{F}^{\varphi}_{s, q + \frac{1}{2}} - \mathcal{F}^{\varphi}_{s, q - \frac{1}{2}}}{\delta\varphi}
}.

(61)

Here,
\mathcal{F}
is used to represent the fluxes evaluated at the interfaces between adjacent cells (hence the
s\pm 1/2
and
q\pm 1/2
at the subscript) so that, for instance,
\mathcal{F}^{\theta}_{s+1/2,q}
is the flux of
f
advected with velocity
v_{k_{\theta}}
through the interface between the cell centered in
(s,q)
and that centered in
(s+1,q)
as in Ref.
[
39
]
.

The choice of a fifth-order weighted essentially non-oscillatory scheme for the advection part of the Boltzmann equation was motivated by its high precision
[
40
]
, as well as its shock-capturing properties
[
41
,
42
]
. However, lower-order schemes may also be used, giving some advantages in terms of computational cost. To improve the accuracy near the poles, we support these lower-order models with a different definition of the fluxes in these areas corresponding to
[
43
]
:

\hskip-12.0pt\bqty{\dfrac{1}{R \sin{\theta}} \pdv{\theta}( f_{\vb{k}} v_{k_\theta} \sin{\theta} ) }_{s,q}=-\bqty{\dfrac{1}{R} \pdv{\cos{\theta}} ( f_{\vb{k}} v_{k_\theta} \sin{\theta} ) }_{s,q}\\
\simeq\dfrac{1}{R}\bqty{
\dfrac{\mathcal{F}^{\theta}_{s + \frac{1}{2}, q} \sin{\theta_{s + \frac{1}{2}}} - \mathcal{F}^{\theta}_{s - \frac{1}{2}, q} \sin{\theta_{s - \frac{1}{2}}}}{\cos{{\theta_{s - \frac{1}{2}}}} - \cos{\theta_{s + \frac{1}{2}}}} }.

(62)

Here, the
\sin{\theta}
terms on the right-hand-side represent:

\displaystyle\sin{\theta_{s + \frac{1}{2}}}

\displaystyle=\sin\pqty{ (s+1) \var{\theta}},

\displaystyle\sin{\theta_{s - \frac{1}{2}}}

\displaystyle=\sin\pqty{ s \var{\theta}},

(63a)

\displaystyle\cos{\theta_{s + \frac{1}{2}}}

\displaystyle=\cos\pqty{ (s+1) \var{\theta}},

\displaystyle\cos{\theta_{s - \frac{1}{2}}}

\displaystyle=\cos\pqty{ s \var{\theta}}.

(63b)

Here and in what follows, we refer to the approach in Eqs. (
62
)–(
63
) as the “Komissarov” scheme. A known drawback of the Komissarov scheme is that, due to the addition of the geometrical factors of
\sin\theta_{s\pm\frac{1}{2}}
next to the actual flux functions, the method cannot exceed second-order accuracy
[
43
,
27
]
.

We implement the fluxes in an upwind-biased manner, using a selection of schemes: the first-order (U1), second-order (U2) and third-order (U3) upwind schemes, as well as the fifth-order weighted essentially non-oscillatory scheme. Taking for definiteness the flux
\mathcal{F}^{\theta}_{s+\frac{1}{2},q}
for the case when the advection velocity
V
is positive, with
V=v_{k_{\theta}}\sin\theta
for Eq. (
61
) and
V=v_{k_{\theta}}
for the Komissarov scheme in Eq. (
62
), the fluxes for the upwind schemes are:

\displaystyle\text{U1}:\mathcal{F}^{\theta}_{s+\frac{1}{2},q}

\displaystyle=Vf_{s,q},

(64a)

\displaystyle\text{U2}:\mathcal{F}^{\theta}_{s+\frac{1}{2},q}

\displaystyle=V\left(\frac{3}{2}f_{s,q}-\frac{1}{2}f_{s-1,q}\right),

(64b)

\displaystyle\text{U3}:\mathcal{F}^{\theta}_{s+\frac{1}{2},q}

\displaystyle=V\left(\frac{1}{3}f_{s+1,q}+\frac{5}{6}f_{s,q}-\frac{1}{6}f_{s-1,q}\right).

(64c)

In the case of the WENO-5 scheme, we write
\mathcal{F}_{s+1/2}\equiv\mathcal{F}^{\theta}_{s+\frac{1}{2},q}
and henceforth drop the
\theta
superscript, as well as the
q
index. The flux is computed as an average over three stencils,
\mathcal{F}_{s+1/2}=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\overline{\omega}_{i}\mathcal{F}^{i}_{s+\frac{1}{2}}
, with interpolating functions

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{1}_{s+\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=V\left(\frac{1}{3}f_{s-2}-\frac{7}{6}f_{s-1}+\frac{11}{6}f_{s}\right),

(65a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{2}_{s+\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=V\left(-\frac{1}{6}f_{s-1}+\frac{5}{6}f_{s}+\frac{1}{3}f_{s+1}\right),

(65b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{3}_{s+\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=V\left(\frac{1}{3}f_{s}+\frac{5}{6}f_{s+1}-\frac{1}{6}f_{s+2}\right).

(65c)

The weighing factors
\overline{\omega}_{i}=\tilde{\omega}_{i}/(\sum_{j=1}^{3}\tilde{\omega}_{j})
, with
\tilde{\omega}_{j}=\delta_{j}/\sigma_{j}^{2}
, are given in terms of the ideal weights,

\delta_{1}=\frac{1}{10},\quad\delta_{2}=\frac{6}{10},\quad\delta_{3}=\frac{3}{10},

(66)

as well as the smoothness functions

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{13}{12}(f_{s-2}-2f_{s-1}+f_{s})^{2}+\frac{1}{4}(f_{s-2}-4f_{s-1}+3f_{s})^{2},

(67a)

\displaystyle\sigma_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{13}{12}(f_{s-1}-2f_{s}+f_{s+1})^{2}+\frac{1}{4}(f_{s-1}-f_{s+1})^{2},

(67b)

\displaystyle\sigma_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{13}{12}(f_{s}-2f_{s+1}+f_{s+2})^{2}+\frac{1}{4}(3f_{s}-4f_{s+1}+f_{s+2})^{2}.

(67c)

III.6
Boundary Conditions

Figure 2:
North pole view (with the letter N indicating the pole) of the boundaries defined by Eq. (
69
) for

\theta=0
in the case of positive velocity. The black arrows represent the advection velocities at different grid points.
In red we highlight the components of a velocity vector traveling towards the north pole.
Notice that the “translated” advection velocities have the opposite sign compared to
those of the population residing at the same node (black arrows).

For the implementation of the fluxes discussed in the previous subsection, the distribution function
values to the left and/or right of the computation point must be utilized.
Consequently, it is necessary to define the distribution function beyond the limits of the grid

1\leq s\leq N_{\theta}
,
1\leq q\leq N_{\varphi}
. Since the sphere is a periodic manifold,
the distribution function outside this grid can be mapped within it.

This is straightforward for boundary conditions in the azimuthal (
\varphi
) coordinate:

\displaystyle f(\theta_{s},\varphi_{N_{\varphi}+q};\mathbf{v})

\displaystyle=f(\theta_{s},\varphi_{q};\mathbf{v}),

(68a)

\displaystyle f(\theta_{s},\varphi_{-q};\mathbf{v})

\displaystyle=f(\theta_{s},\varphi_{N_{\varphi}-q};\mathbf{v}).

(68b)

For the polar angle (
\theta
), the boundary conditions require a more careful treatment.
To illustrate this, let us consider a trajectory reaching the north pole at a fixed
azimuthal angle
\varphi
(cf. Fig.
2
).
As it crosses to the opposite side, the point with coordinates
(-\theta,\varphi)
is mapped to
(\theta,\varphi\pm\pi)
, ensuring that
\varphi
remains within the domain
[0,2\pi)
.
Additionally, a velocity vector with components
(v^{\hat{\theta}},v^{\hat{\varphi}})

undergoes inversion upon crossing the pole.
Therefore, we formulate the polar boundary conditions as

\displaystyle f(-\delta\theta,\varphi;\mathbf{v})

\displaystyle=f(\delta\theta,\varphi\pm\pi;-\mathbf{v}),

(69a)

\displaystyle f(\pi+\delta\theta,\varphi;\mathbf{v})

\displaystyle=f(\pi-\delta\theta,\varphi\pm\pi;-\mathbf{v}),

(69b)

where we choose
\delta\theta>0
for definiteness.

III.7
Time stepping

The introduction of finite difference schemes for the advection makes it necessary to employ finite difference schemes also for the time-marching part of the algorithm. In this work, a total variation diminishing third-order Runge-Kutta algorithm was used to approximate
\partial_{t}{f}
at step
l

[
38
,
27
]
:

\displaystyle f_{l}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=f_{l}+\variation{t}\mathcal{L}{[f_{l}]}

(70a)

\displaystyle f_{l}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{3}{4}f_{l}+\dfrac{1}{4}f_{l}^{(1)}+\dfrac{1}{4}\variation{t}\mathcal{L}{[f_{l}^{(1)}]}

(70b)

\displaystyle f_{l+1}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{3}f_{l}+\dfrac{2}{3}f_{l}^{(2)}+\dfrac{2}{3}\variation{t}\mathcal{L}{[f_{l}^{(2)}]},

(70c)

assuming a uniform discretization of
t
with step
\variation{t}
and
f_{l}\equiv f(t_{l})
. The term
\mathcal{L}{[f_{l}]}
is given by:

\mathcal{L}{[f_{l}]}=-\dfrac{1}{R\sin{\theta}}\bqty{ \pdv{ (f_l v_{\theta} \sin{\theta}) }{\theta} + \pdv{ (f_l v_{\varphi}) }{\varphi} }\\
-\dfrac{\cos{\theta}}{R\sin{\theta}}\bqty{ v^2_{\varphi} \pqty{ \pdv{f}{v^{\hat{\theta}}} } - v_{\theta} \pqty{ \pdv{ (f v^{ \hat{\varphi}}) }{ v^{\hat{\varphi}}} } }-\dfrac{1}{\tau}\bqty{ f_l - f^{\mathrm{eq}}_l }.

(71)

III.8
Summary

In this section, we summarize the key components required to implement the method described above.
The first step is to select an appropriate quadrature order, based on the characteristics of the fluid flow under
consideration. The
Q=3
quadrature may in general be sufficient for weakly compressible, isothermal flows,
whereas
Q=4
offers a more robust and stable approach allowing for the description of more complex flows.
A choice of advection scheme must also be made from those presented in Section
III.5
.

The algorithm then proceeds using the multi-stage time-stepping procedure outlined in Section
III.7
,
where the central computational task is the evaluation of the
\mathcal{L}[f_{l}]
operator.
This process can be summarized as follows:

•

Angular derivatives in
\theta
and
\varphi
(from Eq.(
71
)) are evaluated using the flux-based
discretization in Eq.(
61
), or alternatively using Eq.(
62
) for
the Komissarov-type advection scheme. The corresponding fluxes are defined in Eqs.(
64
)–(
67
).

•

Velocity-space derivatives on the second line of Eq.(
71
) are computed via matrix multiplication as shown
in Eqs.(
57
), using the differentiation kernels given in Eqs. (
98
) and (
100
) for quadrature orders
Q=3
and
Q=4
, respectively.

•

Macroscopic quantities such as density
n
and velocity
\mathbf{u}
are obtained from the distribution function moments
using Eq. (
40
).

•

Equilibrium distributions
f^{\rm eq}_{\mathbf{k}}
are then computed via Eqs. (
97
) and (
99
), depending on the chosen quadrature order.

The problem described above is studied analytically with full details in Appendix

Riemann problem

In this section, we consider the spherical analogue of the classical Sod shock-tube problem.
This test is used to assess the robustness of our numerical method in the presence of shock waves traveling at supersonic speed.

We take into consideration the following initial conditions:

\displaystyle\rho(\theta)=\begin{cases}\rho_{1},&\theta<\theta_{\rm disc},\\
\rho_{2},&\theta>\theta_{\rm disc},\end{cases},

(88)

with
\rho_{1}=1
,
\rho_{2}=0.125
and
\theta_{\rm disc}=\pi/10
. At the inital time (
t=0
) the fluid is at rest, with
u^{\theta}=u^{\varphi}=0
.
The initial discontinuity in the density profile will give rise to a shock wave propagating towards the south pole,
with the discontinuity expected to remain sharp in the inviscid (perfect fluid) limit.

Since no closed-form solution exists for shock wave propagation on a sphere, we employ two different approaches
to validate the accuracy of our LBM solver. First, we solve numerically the inviscid Euler equations on the sphere using
a flux vector splitting method (details provided in Appendix
D
), yielding a high-resolution reference
for the zero-viscosity limit.
Second, and as a novel contribution of this work, in what follows we derive a semi-analytical estimate of the
shock front propagation by applying the Rankine-Hugoniot jump conditions to an axisymmetric discontinuity on the sphere.

We denote with
g(t,\theta)
a generic physical quantity with a moving discontinuity at
\theta=\theta_{d}(t)
:

g(t,\theta)=\Theta(\theta_{d}-\theta)g_{N}(t,\theta)+\Theta(\theta-\theta_{d})g_{S}(t,\theta),

(89)

where
\Theta
is the Heaviside step function, and
g_{N/S}
are the values north/south of the discontinuity.
The time and angular derivatives of
g(t,\theta)
read:

\displaystyle\dot{g}

\displaystyle=\Theta(\theta_{d}-\theta)\dot{g}_{N}+\Theta(\theta-\theta_{d})\dot{g}_{S}+\dot{\theta}_{d}\delta(\theta_{d}-\theta)(g_{N}-g_{S}),

\displaystyle g^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\Theta(\theta_{d}-\theta)g^{\prime}_{N}+\Theta(\theta-\theta_{d})g^{\prime}_{S}-\delta(\theta_{d}-\theta)(g_{N}-g_{S}),

(90)

where
\delta(x)
is the Dirac delta function.

Integrating the Euler equations (
192
) over an infinitesimal region around
\theta_{d}
yields
the Rankine-Hugoniot jump conditions:

\displaystyle\rho_{S}(u_{S}-R\dot{\theta}_{d})

\displaystyle=\rho_{N}(u_{N}-R\dot{\theta}_{d}),

(91a)

\displaystyle\rho_{S}(u_{S}^{2}+c_{s}^{2}-R\dot{\theta}_{d}u_{S})

\displaystyle=\rho_{N}(u_{N}^{2}+c_{s}^{2}-R\dot{\theta}_{d}u_{N}),

(91b)

where
R\dot{\theta}_{d}
denotes the shock front velocity.
Solving these equations gives:

u_{N}-u_{S}=\pm c_{s}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\rho_{N}}{\rho_{S}}}-\sqrt{\frac{\rho_{S}}{\rho_{N}}}\right),

(92a)

with the sign chosen based on the shock propagation direction. The shock velocity is

R\dot{\theta}_{d}=\frac{\rho_{N}u_{N}-\rho_{S}u_{S}}{\rho_{N}-\rho_{S}}.

(92b)

For the primary shock, where
u_{S}=0
,
u_{N}>0
, and
\rho_{N}>\rho_{S}
, this reduces to:

R\dot{\theta}_{d}=\frac{\rho_{N}u_{N}}{\rho_{N}-\rho_{S}},\quad u_{N}=c_{s}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\rho_{N}}{\rho_{S}}}-\sqrt{\frac{\rho_{S}}{\rho_{N}}}\right).

(93)

Given the shock velocity
R\dot{\theta}_{d}
(which we extract from numerical data) and the known
density
\rho_{S}=n_{2}
, the quantities north of the discontinuity can be computed as:

u_{N}=\frac{(R\dot{\theta}_{d})^{2}-c_{s}^{2}}{R\dot{\theta}_{d}},\quad\rho_{N}=\rho_{S}\left(\frac{R\dot{\theta}_{d}}{c_{s}}\right)^{2}.

(94)

These relations allow modeling the shock front dynamics, which we overlay in Fig.
8
as
solid gray lines for both velocity and density.

For LBM, we use a 1D spherical grid with resolution
N_{\theta}=1024
,
N_{\varphi}=1
, employing the fifth-order weighted essentially non-oscillatory
advection scheme and a time step
\Delta t=5\times 10^{-6}
.

Figure
8
shows the time evolution of the density and velocity, normalized
with respect to the initial density
\rho_{1}
and sound speed
c_{s}
.
The top row corresponds to early times
t\in\{3,6,9,12\}
, capturing the rarefaction wave collapse and formation of
a reverse shock. The bottom row shows late-time snapshots
t\in\{15,18,21,24\}
, including the shock front
arrival at the south pole and its reflection.

The results of our LBM solver (marked with dots) are in perfect agreement with the numerical solution of the
inviscid Euler equations (dotted lines), and both are also consistent with the modeling of the shock front
described by the solid gray lines.
These results convincingly demonstrate that our method remains robust and accurate even in the presence
of strong shocks and compressible effects.

In this section, we provide a brief overview of the numerical model’s performance.
We consider an implementation of vLBM using double-precision arithmetic and standard
optimization techniques
[

]
for GPU acceleration using
the OpenACC programming model.

Figure

presents the performance, measured in million lattice updates per second (MLUPS),
for the different advection schemes discussed above.
Performance data was obtained from the sound-wave benchmark on a fully 2D grid of size

1024\times 1024
, running on a single Nvidia V100 and a single Nvidia A100 GPU.

Our results show that increasing the advection scheme from first-order upwind
to third-order upwind incurs a negligible computational cost.
However, the WENO-5 scheme introduces an overhead of approximately
\approx 20\%
in the case of the A100 card.
Nevertheless, for applications involving shock waves and supersonic flows,
WENO-5 significantly enhances stability, making this overhead justifiable.

On average, the performance increases by about a factor of two going from the Nvidia V100 to the A100, consistent with the difference in peak computational capabilities between these architectures.

Compared to a standard on-grid LBM, the computational cost is approximately an order of magnitude higher.
For reference, a similarly optimized isothermal D2Q17 model with a BGK collision operator achieves
\approx 1600

MLUPS on an Nvidia A100 GPU. This additional cost arises from the Runge-Kutta time integration and off-grid advection scheme.

Notably, the Komissarov scheme has a negligible impact on the overall computational cost.
Therefore, an optimal trade-off between performance and accuracy is achieved using
a third-order upwind scheme combined with the Komissarov scheme for enhanced stability.
For simulations involving shock waves and supersonic flows, the WENO-5 scheme provides
the necessary stability, albeit at a moderate overhead.

VI
Conclusions

In this paper, we have developed a lattice Boltzmann model, based on the
vielbein formalism, for the simulation of fluid flows on spherical surfaces.
The vielbein fields encode all the geometric properties of the underlying
manifold, allowing the velocity space to be parametrized with respect to the
velocity components
(v^{\hat{\theta}},v^{\hat{\varphi}})
expressed with
respect to the vielbein.
This formulation trades the advantage of perfect streaming in traditional LBM
schemes for greater flexibility, enabling the description of fluid flows on
generic curved surfaces, as well as increased numerical stability.
We leverage Gauss quadrature techniques to ensure the exact preservation of the
low-order moments of the particle distribution function, thereby retaining
one of the key properties of standard LBM formulations. This is in contrast
to finite difference discretizations of the Navier-Stokes equations or
discrete velocity methods, where such moment preservation is in general not guaranteed.

A key contribution of this work is the derivation of exact solutions to the Navier-Stokes equations for sound and shear wave dynamics on the sphere. These analytical solutions, presented in Appendix
B
, were used to validate and benchmark our numerical vLBM implementation. Additionally, they provide a valuable reference for future comparisons between different numerical solvers.

After successfully validating our model, we examined the propagation of an axisymmetric shock wave. As the shock travels from the north to the south pole, it is reflected upon reaching the latter. Simultaneously, a rarefaction wave depletes the north pole, leading to the formation of a reverse shock, both of which are captured in our simulations.
To support the correctness of our results, we compared them with numerical solutions of the inviscid Euler equations and with a semi-analytic estimate of the shock front based on the Rankine-Hugoniot conditions. This benchmark highlights the capability of the solver to sustain the propagation of shock waves, also in the presence of sizeable compressibility effects.
We have also assessed the isotropy of the solver, comparing numerical results on an axisymmetric grid and a
90^{\circ}
-tilted configuration, finding excellent agreement.

As a final, more qualitative test, we examined the evolution of two vortices initialized on the spherical surface, following the setup suggested by Yang et al.
[
44
]
. We found a good qualitative agreement between our results and those reported therein.

Finally, we have reported a quick overview of the computational performances achieved by implementing the solver targeting modern GPU architectures. Our analysis suggests that an optimal trade-off between performance and accuracy is obtained using a third-order upwind scheme for advection. On the other hand, in the presence of flows subject to shock waves and supersonic speeds, the WENO-5 scheme may provide the necessary stability at a moderate overhead.

In future work, we plan to address key challenges in studying turbulence on
manifolds. To enable DNS at high Reynolds numbers, we will develop a
multi-GPU implementation to efficiently handle high-resolution grids
and large-scale simulations. Additionally, the current
explicit method imposes a time-step restriction due to the
Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy (CFL) condition, as well as the
\delta t<\tau
limitation typical for explicit schemes, due to the stiffness of the collision term. To overcome this,
we will incorporate implicit-explicit (IMEX) methods
[
47
,
48
]
or semi-Lagrangian implicit
approaches
[
49
]
to enable coarser time steps, even for
high-order advection schemes.
This will pave the way for large-scale simulations of turbulent flows on spherical surfaces.
We also plan to explore the method’s performance beyond the hydrodynamic regime, leveraging its
kinetic foundation.

A.3
Conservation equations

The recovery of the mass and momentum conservation equations, Eqs. (
25a
) and (

), requires the integration of Eq. (
35

1

, over the velocity space. The discretization of the velocity space must ensure the exact recovery of the following integrals of the distribution function:

\displaystyle\int d\mathbf{v}\,f=n,\quad\int d\mathbf{v}\,f\mathbf{v}=n\mathbf{u},

\displaystyle m\int d\mathbf{v}\,fv^{\hat{a}}v^{\hat{b}}=\rho u^{\hat{a}}u^{\hat{b}}-\tau^{\hat{a}\hat{b}}.

(101)

The structure of
\tau^{\hat{a}\hat{b}}
and its connection to the constitutive relation for the Newtonian fluid is addressed in Subsec.
A.4
. In both the
Q=3
and
Q=4
quadrature models, the projection of
f
onto the Hermite space, together with the Gauss integration rules, ensures the preservation of the integrals appearing above.

Within a model of order
Q
, the continuous distribution function
f(\mathbf{v})
is replaced by the discrete sets
f_{\mathbf{k}}
via Eq. (
41
), which allow for a Hermite series representation as given in Eq. (
51
), truncated at
\ell_{\theta}=\ell_{\varphi}=Q-1

f(\mathbf{v})\to f_{\mathbf{k}}=w_{\mathbf{k}}\sum_{\ell_{\theta},\ell_{\varphi}=0}^{Q-1}\frac{\mathcal{F}_{\ell_{\theta},\ell_{\varphi}}}{\ell_{\theta}!\ell_{\varphi}!}H_{\ell_{\theta}}(v^{\hat{\theta}}_{k_{\theta}})H_{\ell_{\varphi}}(v^{\hat{\varphi}}_{k_{\varphi}}),

(102)

which guarantees the recovery of the moments of the following type:

\int d\mathbf{v}\,f(\mathbf{v})v_{\hat{\theta}}^{\ell_{\theta}}v_{\hat{\varphi}}^{\ell_{\varphi}}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}f_{\mathbf{k}}v_{k_{\theta}}^{\ell_{\theta}}v_{k_{\varphi}}^{\ell_{\varphi}},

(103)

for any
0\leq\ell_{\theta},\ell_{\varphi}<Q
. Eqs. (
101
) require moments with
0\leq\ell_{\theta},\ell_{\varphi}\leq 2
, hence our
Q=3
and
Q=4
quadratures are sufficient.

Moreover, the following integrals of the velocity gradients of
f
must be recovered:

\displaystyle\int d\mathbf{v}\frac{\partial(v^{\hat{b}}v^{\hat{c}}f)}{\partial v^{\hat{a}}}

\displaystyle=0,

(104)

\displaystyle\int d\mathbf{v}\frac{\partial(v^{\hat{b}}v^{\hat{c}}f)}{\partial v^{\hat{a}}}v^{\hat{d}}

\displaystyle=-\delta^{\hat{d}}_{\hat{a}}\int d\mathbf{v}\,fv^{\hat{b}}v^{\hat{c}}.

(105)

Specifically, in spherical coordinates, the following integrals must be ensured:

\displaystyle\int d\mathbf{v}\frac{\partial f}{\partial v^{\hat{\theta}}}v_{\hat{\varphi}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int d\mathbf{v}\frac{\partial f}{\partial v^{\hat{\theta}}}v_{\hat{\varphi}}^{3}=0,

(106a)

\displaystyle\int d\mathbf{v}\frac{\partial f}{\partial v^{\hat{\theta}}}v_{\hat{\varphi}}^{2}v^{\hat{\theta}}

\displaystyle=-\int d\mathbf{v}\,fv_{\hat{\varphi}}^{2},

(106b)

\displaystyle\int d\mathbf{v}\frac{\partial(fv^{\hat{\varphi}})}{\partial v^{\hat{\varphi}}}v^{\hat{\theta}}

\displaystyle=\int d\mathbf{v}\frac{\partial(fv^{\hat{\varphi}})}{\partial v^{\hat{\varphi}}}v^{2}_{\hat{\theta}}=0,

(106c)

A.4
Chapman-Enskog analysis

In subsections
A.1
and
A.2
, we presented the quadratures of orders
Q=3
and
Q=4
. We discussed the recovery of the mass and momentum conservation equations within these discrete models in Sec.
A.3
. We now discuss the recovery of the Navier-Stokes equations, for which we consider the Chapman-Enskog expansion, following Ref.
[
32
]
.

The constraint that
\psi\in\{1,\mathbf{v}\}
represents collision invariants of the BGK model implies that the deviation
\delta f=f-f^{\rm eq}
of the distribution function from equilibrium satisfies:

\int d\mathbf{v}\,\delta f=\int d\mathbf{v}\,\delta f\,v^{\hat{a}}=0.

(109)

Considering that the fluid is close to equilibrium,
\delta f
can be taken as a small quantity, of the order of the relaxation time
\tau
. From Eq. (
35
),
\delta f
can be obtained to leading order as:

\delta f\simeq-\tau\Bigg[\frac{\partial f^{\rm eq}}{\partial t}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\frac{\partial}{\partial q^{b}}\left(v^{\hat{a}}e_{\hat{a}}^{b}f^{\rm eq}\sqrt{g}\right)\\
-\Gamma^{\hat{a}}_{\hat{b}\hat{c}}\frac{\partial(v^{\hat{b}}v^{\hat{c}}f^{\rm eq})}{\partial v^{\hat{a}}}\Bigg],

(110)

In the case of the spherical geometry, the vielbein vectors
e^{b}_{\hat{a}}
and the connection coefficients
\Gamma^{\hat{a}}_{\hat{b}\hat{c}}
are given in Eqs. (
19
) and (
23
), respectively. Employing these expressions in Eq. (
110
) allows
\delta f
to be obtained as

\delta f\simeq-\tau\Bigg[\frac{\partial f^{\rm eq}}{\partial t}+\frac{v^{\hat{\theta}}\partial_{\theta}(f^{\rm eq}\sin\theta)}{R\sin\theta}+\frac{v^{\hat{\varphi}}\partial_{\varphi}f^{\rm eq}}{R\sin\theta}\\
+\frac{\cos\theta}{R\sin\theta}\left(v_{\hat{\varphi}}^{2}\frac{\partial f^{\rm eq}}{\partial v^{\hat{\theta}}}-v^{\hat{\theta}}\frac{\partial(v^{\hat{\varphi}}f^{\rm eq})}{\partial v^{\hat{\varphi}}}\right)\Bigg].

(111)

We are interested in the form of the viscous part
\sigma^{\hat{a}\hat{b}}=\tau^{\hat{a}\hat{b}}+p\delta^{\hat{a}\hat{b}}
of the stress tensor, obtained from
\delta f
via

\sigma^{{\hat{a}}{\hat{b}}}=m\int d\mathbf{v}\,\delta f\,v^{\hat{a}}v^{\hat{b}}.

(112)

Multiplying Eq. (
111
) by
v^{\hat{a}}v^{\hat{b}}
and integrating with respect to the velocity space shows that the recovery of
\sigma_{{\hat{a}}{\hat{b}}}
requires various moments of the equilibrium distribution,
f^{\rm eq}
, which we list below:

\displaystyle\int d\mathbf{v}\,f^{\rm eq}=n,\quad\int d\mathbf{v}\,f^{\rm eq}v^{\hat{a}}=nu^{\hat{a}},

\displaystyle m\int d\mathbf{v}\,f^{\rm eq}v^{\hat{a}}v^{\hat{b}}=\rho u^{\hat{a}}u^{\hat{b}}+p\delta^{\hat{a}\hat{b}},

\displaystyle m\int d\mathbf{v}\,f^{\rm eq}v^{\hat{a}}v^{\hat{b}}v^{\hat{c}}=\rho u^{\hat{a}}u^{\hat{b}}u^{\hat{c}}+p(u^{\hat{a}}\delta^{\hat{b}\hat{c}}+u^{\hat{b}}\delta^{\hat{c}\hat{a}}+u^{\hat{c}}\delta^{\hat{a}\hat{b}}).

(113)

While the above relations are exactly satisfied by the
Q=4
quadrature, in the case when
Q=3
, we find discrepancies for the third-order moment:

m\sum_{\mathbf{k}}f_{\mathbf{k}}^{Q=3,{\rm eq}}v_{\mathbf{k}}^{\hat{a}}v_{\mathbf{k}}^{\hat{b}}v_{\mathbf{k}}^{\hat{c}}=p(u^{\hat{a}}\delta^{\hat{b}\hat{c}}+u^{\hat{b}}\delta^{\hat{c}\hat{a}}+u^{\hat{c}}\delta^{\hat{a}\hat{b}}),

(114)

indicating that the
O(\mathbf{u}^{3})
term is not captured. This omission is easily understood from the fact that the equilibrium distribution
f_{\mathbf{k}}^{Q=3,\mathrm{eq}}
, given in Eq. (
97
), contains no terms cubic in the velocity
\mathbf{u}
. Nevertheless, as is well known within the LBM community, the missing contribution
O(\mathrm{Ma}^{3})
has a negligible impact in the low-Mach number regime.

In addition, the curvature term requires the recovery of the following moments of the velocity gradients of
f^{\rm eq}
:

\displaystyle\int d\mathbf{v}\frac{\partial f^{\rm eq}}{\partial v^{\hat{\theta}}}v_{\hat{\varphi}}^{4}

\displaystyle=\int d\mathbf{v}\frac{\partial(f^{\rm eq}v^{\hat{\varphi}})}{\partial v^{\hat{\varphi}}}v_{\hat{\theta}}^{3}=0,

\displaystyle m\int d\mathbf{v}\frac{\partial f^{\rm eq}}{\partial v^{\hat{\theta}}}v_{\hat{\varphi}}^{3}v^{\hat{\theta}}

\displaystyle=-\rho u_{\hat{\varphi}}^{3}-3pu^{\hat{\varphi}},

\displaystyle m\int d\mathbf{v}\frac{\partial f^{\rm eq}}{\partial v^{\hat{\theta}}}v_{\hat{\varphi}}^{2}v_{\hat{\theta}}^{2}

\displaystyle=m\int d\mathbf{v}\frac{\partial(f^{\rm eq}v^{\hat{\varphi}})}{\partial v^{\hat{\varphi}}}v^{\hat{\theta}}v_{\hat{\varphi}}^{2}

\displaystyle=-2\rho u_{\hat{\varphi}}^{2}u^{\hat{\theta}}-2pu^{\hat{\theta}},

\displaystyle m\int d\mathbf{v}\frac{\partial(f^{\rm eq}v^{\hat{\varphi}})}{\partial v^{\hat{\varphi}}}v_{\hat{\theta}}^{2}v^{\hat{\varphi}}

\displaystyle=-\rho u^{\hat{\varphi}}u_{\hat{\theta}}^{2}-pu^{\hat{\varphi}}.

(115)

Again, these relations are exactly satisfied in the case of the
Q=4
quadrature. When
Q=3
, we find discrepancies in the following cases:

\displaystyle m\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\left(\frac{\partial f^{\rm eq}}{\partial v^{\hat{\theta}}}\right)_{\mathbf{k}}^{Q=3;{\rm eq}}v_{k_{\varphi}}^{3}v_{k_{\theta}}^{2}

\displaystyle=-3pu^{\hat{\varphi}},

(116)

\displaystyle m\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\left(\frac{\partial f^{\rm eq}}{\partial v^{\hat{\theta}}}\right)_{\mathbf{k}}^{Q=3;{\rm eq}}v_{k_{\varphi}}^{2}v_{k_{\theta}}^{2}

\displaystyle=-2pu^{\hat{\theta}},

(117)

\displaystyle m\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\left(\frac{\partial(f^{\rm eq}v^{\hat{\varphi}})}{\partial v^{\hat{\varphi}}}\right)_{\mathbf{k}}^{Q=3;{\rm eq}}v_{k_{\theta}}v_{k_{\varphi}}^{2}

\displaystyle=-2pu^{\hat{\theta}},

(118)

\displaystyle m\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\left(\frac{\partial(f^{\rm eq}v^{\hat{\varphi}})}{\partial v^{\hat{\varphi}}}\right)_{\mathbf{k}}^{Q=3;{\rm eq}}v^{2}_{k_{\theta}}v_{k_{\varphi}}

\displaystyle=-pu^{\hat{\varphi}},

(119)

therefore again missing the
O(\mathbf{u}^{3})
terms.

Summarizing the above discussion, we have
\sigma^{\hat{a}\hat{b}}_{Q=4}=\sigma^{\hat{a}\hat{b}}_{\rm NS}
, with
\sigma^{\hat{a}\hat{b}}_{\rm NS}
being the Navier-Stokes viscous stress tensor given in Eq. (
27
) with
\zeta=\mu=\tau p
, while the viscous stress tensor obtained with
Q=3
deviates from this expression via

\sigma^{\hat{a}\hat{b}}_{Q=3}=\sigma^{\hat{a}\hat{b}}_{\rm NS}+\frac{\tau}{R\sin\theta}\left[\partial_{\theta}(\rho u^{\hat{a}}u^{\hat{b}}u^{\hat{\theta}})+\partial_{\varphi}(\rho u^{\hat{a}}u^{\hat{b}}u^{\hat{\varphi}})\right]\\
-\frac{\tau\cos\theta}{R\sin\theta}\left[\rho u_{\hat{\varphi}}^{2}(\delta^{\hat{a}}_{\hat{\theta}}u^{\hat{b}}+\delta^{\hat{b}}_{\hat{\theta}}u^{\hat{a}})-\rho u^{\hat{\varphi}}u^{\hat{\theta}}(\delta^{\hat{a}}_{\hat{\varphi}}u^{\hat{b}}+\delta^{\hat{b}}_{\hat{\varphi}}u^{\hat{a}})\right].

(120)

Due to the above inaccuracy, we consider the
Q=4
quadrature in the main text, however it is interesting to note that, aside from the shock wave problem considered in Sec.
IV.3
, the
Q=3
model should provide qualitatively satisfactory results for all tests considered in this paper.

Appendix B
Solutions for Axis-symmetrical Flows

In this appendix section, we provide full details on the analytic derivation of the solutions for the axisymmetric flows presented in Sec
IV
. The solution for the sound waves propagation, considered in Sec.
IV.1
, is presented in Subsections
B.1
. The solution for the shear waves, considered in Sec.
IV.2
, is presented in Subsec.
B.2
.

B.1
Sound waves

We consider the propagation of longitudinal waves of infinitesimal amplitude along the polar (
\theta
) direction. We assume that the velocity along the azimuthal direction vanishes,
u^{\hat{\varphi}}=0
. Writing

\rho=\rho_{0}(1+\delta\rho),\ P=P_{0}(1+\delta P),

(121)

where
\rho_{0}
and
P_{0}
are the density and pressure of the background (undisturbed) fluid, and considering that
\delta\rho
,
\delta P
and
u^{\hat{\theta}}\equiv\delta u^{\hat{\theta}}
are infinitesimal perturbations, Eqs. (
34a
) and (
34b
) reduce to:

\partial_{t}\delta\rho+\frac{\partial_{\theta}(\delta u^{\hat{\theta}}\sin\theta)}{R\sin\theta}=0,\hfill

(122a)

\partial_{t}\delta u^{\hat{\theta}}+\frac{c_{s}^{2}}{R}\partial_{\theta}\delta\rho=\frac{\nu+\nu_{v}}{R^{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left[\frac{\partial_{\theta}(\sin\theta\delta u^{\hat{\theta}})}{\sin\theta}\right]+\frac{2\nu}{R^{2}}\delta u^{\hat{\theta}},

(122b)

where
\nu=\eta/\rho
and
\nu_{v}=\zeta/\rho
are the kinematic shear and bulk viscosities and we took into account that the pressure perturbations can be linked to the density perturbations via

\delta P=\frac{\rho_{0}}{P_{0}}\frac{\partial P}{\partial\rho}\delta\rho,

(123)

with
\partial P/\partial\rho=c_{s}^{2}
being the speed of sound squared.

Inviscid fluid.
We first consider the case of a perfect fluid, corresponding to vanishing kinematic viscosities
\nu=\nu_{v}=0
. In this case, Eq. (
122b
) reduces to

\partial_{t}\delta u^{\hat{\theta}}+\frac{c_{s}^{2}}{R}\partial_{\theta}\delta\rho=0.

(124)

Combining Eqs. (
122a
) and (
124
), we arrive at the single differential equation

\frac{\partial^{2}\delta u^{\hat{\theta}}}{\partial t^{2}}-\frac{c^{2}_{s}}{R^{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left[\frac{1}{\sin{\theta}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left(\delta u^{\hat{\theta}}\sin\theta\right)\right]=0.

(125)

The above equation can be solved using the separation of variables of the form

\delta u^{\hat{\theta}}\to\frac{U_{n}(t)F_{n}(\theta)}{\sin\theta},

(126)

where
n
is used to identify the different possible solutions of Eq. (
125
). This leads to a system of two decoupled equations in
t
and
\theta
:

\displaystyle\derivative[2]{U_n}{t}+\lambda^{2}_{n}\dfrac{c^{2}_{s}}{R^{2}}U_{n}

\displaystyle=0,

(127a)

\displaystyle\sin{\theta}\derivative{\theta}\left(\dfrac{1}{\sin{\theta}}\partialderivative{F_n}{\theta}\right)+\lambda^{2}_{n}F_{n}

\displaystyle=0.

(127b)

We easily recognize that (
127a
) has the form of a harmonic oscillator equation with the solution:

U_{n}(t)=u_{n}\cos(\omega_n t + \varphi_n),

(128)

where
u_{n}
and
\varphi_{n}
represent arbitrary integration constants, while the angular frequency obeys

\omega_{n}=\frac{\lambda_{n}c_{s}}{R}.

(129)

For the angular equation, we seek even and odd solutions with respect to
\theta\rightarrow\pi-\theta
,

F^{e}_{n}(\theta)\equiv f^{e}_{n}(\zeta),\quad F^{o}_{n}(\theta)\equiv\cos\theta\,f^{o}_{n}(\zeta),

(130)

where we take
\zeta=\cos 2\theta
to be the argument of the reduced functions
f^{e/o}_{n}
. In this case, Eq. (
127b
) becomes

\displaystyle(1-\zeta^{2})\frac{\partial^{2}f^{e}_{n}}{\partial\zeta^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}(1-\zeta)\frac{\partial f^{e}_{n}}{\partial\zeta}+\frac{\lambda_{e;n}^{2}}{4}f^{e}_{n}

\displaystyle=0,

(131)

\displaystyle(1-\zeta^{2})\frac{\partial^{2}f^{o}_{n}}{\partial\zeta^{2}}+\frac{3}{2}(1-\zeta)\frac{\partial f^{o}_{n}}{\partial\zeta}+\frac{\lambda_{o;n}^{2}}{4}g_{n}

\displaystyle=0.

(132)

The regular solutions of the above equations can be written in terms of the Jacobi polynomials
P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)
, which satisfy

\bigg[(1-x^{2})\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+[\beta-\alpha-(\alpha+\beta+2)x]\frac{d}{dx}\\
+n(n+\alpha+\beta+1)\bigg]P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)=0.

(133)

For future reference, we give below the series expansion for the Jacobi polynomials:

P^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}(\zeta)=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+n+1)}{n!\Gamma(\alpha+\beta+n+1)}\\
\times\sum_{m=0}^{n}\binom{n}{m}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta+n+m+1)}{\Gamma(\alpha+m+1)}\left(\frac{\zeta-1}{2}\right)^{m},

(134)

as well as the orthogonality relation

\int_{-1}^{1}dx\,(1-x)^{\alpha}(1+x)^{\beta}P_{m}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)\\
=\frac{2^{\alpha+\beta+1}}{2n+\alpha+\beta+1}\frac{\Gamma(n+\alpha+1)\Gamma(n+\beta+1)}{\Gamma(n+\alpha+\beta+1)n!}\delta_{nm}.

(135)

Comparing Eqs. (
131
), (
132
) and (
133
), it can be seen that the angular functions
F^{e/o}_{n}
are given by

\displaystyle F^{e}_{n}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}_{e;n}P^{(-1,-1/2)}_{n}(\cos 2\theta),

(136a)

\displaystyle F^{o}_{n}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}_{o;n}\cos\theta P^{(-1,1/2)}_{n}(\cos 2\theta),

(136b)

where
n=0,1,2,\dots
must be a non-negative integer. The eigenvalues
\lambda^{e/o}_{n}
are related to
n
via

\lambda^{e}_{n}=\sqrt{2n(2n-1)},\quad\lambda^{o}_{n}=\sqrt{2n(2n+1)}.

(137)

The normalization constants
\mathcal{N}^{e/o}_{n}
appearing in Eqs. (
136
) must be found by imposing unit norm with respect to a suitable inner product for the acoustic modes (“ac”),

\langle F^{e}_{n},F^{e}_{n^{\prime}}\rangle_{\rm ac}=\langle F^{o}_{n},F^{o}_{n^{\prime}}\rangle_{\rm ac}=\delta_{nn^{\prime}},

(138)

with
\langle F^{e}_{n},F^{o}_{n^{\prime}}\rangle_{\rm ac}=0
automatically, by symmetry considerations. The relevant inner product can be found as follows.

First, we multiply Eq. (
127b
) by
F_{n^{\prime}}/\sin\theta
, with
F_{n^{\prime}}
being also a solution of Eq. (
127b
). From the result, we subtract an equivalent expression with
n
and
n^{\prime}
interchanged, leading to

\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left[\frac{(F_{n^{\prime}}\overleftrightarrow{\partial_{\theta}}F_{n})}{\sin\theta}\right]+\frac{\lambda_{n}^{2}-\lambda_{n^{\prime}}^{2}}{\sin\theta}F_{n^{\prime}}(\theta)F_{n}(\theta)=0,

(139)

where
f\overleftrightarrow{\partial_{\theta}}g=f\partial_{\theta}g-(\partial_{\theta}f)g
is the billateral derivative.
Integrating the above with respect to
\theta
between
0
and
\pi
and assuming that the term inside the square brackets vanishes sufficiently fast around the spherical poles at
\theta=0
and
\pi
, we arrive at

(\lambda_{n}^{2}-\lambda_{n^{\prime}}^{2})\langle F_{n},F_{n^{\prime}}\rangle_{\rm ac}=0,

(140)

where

\langle\varphi,\chi\rangle_{\rm ac}=\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{d\theta}{\sin\theta}\varphi(\theta)\chi(\theta).

(141)

Substituting
\alpha=-1
and
\beta=-1/2
into Eq. (
135
) and taking into account that the interval
[-1,1]
for
x=\cos 2\theta
covers only half of the sphere, we have

\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{d\theta}{\sin\theta}P_{m}^{(-1,-1/2)}(\cos 2\theta)P_{n}^{(-1,-1/2)}(\cos 2\theta)\\
=\frac{2n-1}{n(4n-1)}\delta_{nm},

(142)

\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{d\theta}{\sin\theta}\cos^{2}\theta\,P_{m}^{(-1,1/2)}(\cos 2\theta)P_{n}^{(-1,1/2)}(\cos 2\theta)\\
=\frac{2n+1}{n(4n+1)}\delta_{nm},

(143)

such that the normalization constants are

\mathcal{N}^{e}_{n}=\sqrt{\frac{n(4n-1)}{2n-1}},\quad\mathcal{N}^{o}_{n}=\sqrt{\frac{n(4n+1)}{2n+1}}.

(144)

From the above, it can be seen that the solutions with
n=0
remain unviable since they cannot be normalized. Taking into account that
P^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{0}(z)=1
, the velocity profile corresponding to the
n=0
solutions is either
u^{\hat{\theta}}\sim 1/\sin\theta
(even) or
u^{\hat{\theta}}\sim\cos\theta/\sin\theta
(odd), both versions diverging near the poles at
\theta=0
and
\pi
.

Thus, the even and odd solutions
F^{e/o}_{n}(\theta)
of the angular problem for the propagation of acoustic (sound) modes read:

\displaystyle F^{e}_{n}(\theta)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n}\sqrt{\frac{n(4n-1)}{2n-1}}P^{(-1,-1/2)}_{n}(\cos 2\theta),

(145a)

\displaystyle F^{o}_{n}(\theta)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n}\cos\theta\sqrt{\frac{n(4n+1)}{2n+1}}P^{(-1,1/2)}_{n}(\cos 2\theta),

(145b)

with the corresponding eigenvalues given in Eq. (
137
). Before listing the full solution
u^{\hat{\theta}}(t,\theta)
, we discuss below the inclusion of dissipative effects.

Dissipative fluid.
In order to incorporate the effect of the dissipative term on the right-hand side of Eq. (
122b
), we first write out explicitly the time dependence of our normal modes:

\delta\rho=\sum_{n}e^{-i\alpha_{n}t}\widetilde{\delta\rho}_{n}(\theta),\quad u^{\hat{\theta}}=\sum_{n}e^{-i\alpha_{n}t}\tilde{u}^{\hat{\theta}}_{n}(\theta),

(146)

where
\widetilde{\delta\rho}_{n}
and
\tilde{u}^{\hat{\theta}}_{n}
depend only on the polar coordinate
\theta
. The continuity equation (
122a
) gives

\widetilde{\delta\rho}_{n}=-\frac{i}{R\alpha_{n}\sin\theta}\frac{\partial(\tilde{u}^{\hat{\theta}}_{n}\sin\theta)}{\partial\theta}.

(147a)

Plugging this into Eq. (
122b
) gives

\left(-iR^{2}\alpha_{n}+2\nu\right)\tilde{u}^{\hat{\theta}}_{n}=\\
\left(\frac{ic_{s}^{2}}{\alpha_{n}}+\nu+\nu_{v}\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left[\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\frac{\partial(\tilde{u}^{\hat{\theta}}_{n}\sin\theta)}{\partial\theta}\right].

(147b)

The above equation looks just like Eq. (
127b
) appearing in the case of the perfect fluid, which admits the mode solutions
\tilde{u}^{\hat{\theta}}\to F_{n}(\theta)/\sin\theta
.

Considering
\tilde{u}^{\hat{\theta}}\to F_{n}(\theta)/\sin\theta
in Eq. (
147b
) and using Eq. (
127b
) to eliminate the differential operator leads to the following equation for the frequencies
\alpha_{n}
:

\alpha^{2}_{n}+\frac{i\nu\lambda_{n}^{2}}{R^{2}}\left(\frac{\lambda_{n}^{2}-2}{\lambda_{n}^{2}}+\frac{\nu_{v}}{\nu}\right)-\frac{c_{s}^{2}\lambda_{n}^{2}}{R^{2}}=0.

(148)

In general, we have two solutions given by

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}^{\pm}=-i\gamma_{n}\pm\omega_{n}(\nu),\quad\gamma_{n}=\frac{\nu\lambda_{n}^{2}}{2R^{2}}\left(\frac{\lambda_{n}^{2}-2}{\lambda_{n}^{2}}+\frac{\nu_{v}}{\nu}\right),

\displaystyle\omega_{n}(\nu)=\sqrt{\omega^{2}_{n}(0)-\gamma_{n}^{2}},

(149)

where
\omega_{n}(0)=c_{s}\lambda_{n}/R
is the frequency encountered in the dissipationless case [see Eq. (
129
)]. In the absence of dissipation (
\nu=\nu_{v}=0
), we recover the frequency encountered in the inviscid case,
\alpha_{n}\to\omega_{n}(0)=\pm c_{s}\lambda_{n}/R
. It is remarkable that in the absence of bulk viscosity (
\nu_{v}=0
) the first even mode for which
\lambda^{e}_{1}=\sqrt{2}
is dissipationless.

The general solution for the sound wave propagation problem reads

u^{\hat{\theta}}(t,\theta)=\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[U^{e}_{n}(t)F^{e}_{n}(\theta)+U^{o}_{n}(t)F^{o}_{n}(\theta)\right],

(150)

where the time-dependent amplitudes
U^{*}_{n}(t)
(
*\in\{e,o\}
) are given by

U^{*}_{n}(t)=u^{*}_{n}e^{-\gamma^{*}_{n}t}\cos[\omega^{*}_{n}(\nu)t+\varphi^{*}_{n}],

(151)

where the dissipation coefficient
\gamma^{*}_{n}
and the angular velocity
\omega^{*}_{n}
are given in Eqs. (
149
).
The constants
u^{*}_{n}
and
\varphi^{*}_{n}
can be obtained from the initial density gradient,
\delta\rho^{\prime}_{0}\equiv\partial_{\theta}\delta\rho(t=0,\theta)
, and initial velocity profile,
u^{\hat{\theta}}_{0}\equiv u^{\hat{\theta}}(t=0,\theta)
, via

\displaystyle u^{*}_{n}(\gamma_{n}^{*}\cos\varphi_{n}^{*}-\omega_{n}^{*}\sin\varphi_{n}^{*})

\displaystyle=-\frac{c_{s}^{2}}{R}\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\,\delta\rho^{\prime}_{0}F^{*}_{n}(\theta),

(152a)

\displaystyle u^{*}_{n}\cos\varphi_{n}^{*}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\,u^{\hat{\theta}}_{0}F^{*}_{n}(\theta).

(152b)

B.2
Shear waves

We now consider the dynamics of a shear wave
u^{\hat{\varphi}}\equiv u^{\hat{\varphi}}(t,\theta)
, governed by the
\varphi
component of the Cauchy equation (
34c
). Considering
u^{\hat{\varphi}}\to\delta u^{\hat{\varphi}}
as an infinitesimal perturbation gives

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\frac{u^{\hat{\varphi}}}{\sin\theta}\right)=\frac{\nu}{R^{2}\sin^{3}\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left[\sin^{3}\theta\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left(\frac{u^{\hat{\varphi}}}{\sin\theta}\right)\right].

(153)

To solve this equation, we use separation of variables and consider normal modes of the form

u^{\hat{\varphi}}\rightarrow\sin\theta e^{-\kappa_{n}t}G_{n}(\theta),

(154)

such that
G_{n}(\theta)
satisfies

\frac{1}{\sin^{3}\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left(\sin^{3}\theta\frac{\partial G_{n}}{\partial\theta}\right)+\chi_{n}^{2}G_{n}=0,

(155)

where
\chi_{n}=R\sqrt{\kappa_{n}/\nu}
represent the eigenvalues of the differential operator appearing above.

We seek for odd and even solutions of Eq. (
155
) with respect to
\theta\rightarrow\pi-\theta
,
G^{e}_{n}
and
G^{o}_{n}
, defined as

G^{e}_{n}(\theta)\equiv g^{e}_{n}(\zeta),\quad G^{o}_{n}(\theta)\equiv\cos\theta g^{o}_{n}(\zeta),

(156)

where we introduced the variable
\zeta=\cos(2\theta)
. The functions
g^{e/o}_{n}
satisfy the following differential equations:

\displaystyle(1-\zeta^{2})\frac{\partial^{2}g^{e}_{n}}{\partial\zeta^{2}}-\frac{1}{2}(3+5\zeta)\ \frac{\partial g^{e}_{n}}{\partial\zeta}+\frac{\chi^{2}_{e;n}}{4}g^{e}_{n}=0,

(157)

\displaystyle(1-\zeta^{2})\frac{\partial^{2}g^{o}_{n}}{\partial\zeta^{2}}-\frac{1}{2}(1+7\zeta)\ \frac{\partial g^{o}_{n}}{\partial\zeta}+\left(\frac{\chi^{2}_{o;n}}{4}-1\right)g^{o}_{n}=0.

(158)

Comparing the above to the differential equation (
133
) satisfied by the Jacobi polynomials
P^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}(x)
,
we see that
(\alpha,\beta)=(1,-1/2)
and
(1,1/2)
for the even and odd solutions, respectively, such that

\displaystyle g^{e}_{n}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}^{e}_{n}P^{(1,-1/2)}_{n}(\cos 2\theta),

\displaystyle\chi_{e;n}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2n(2n+3)},

(159a)

\displaystyle g^{o}_{n}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}^{o}_{n}P^{(1,-1/2)}_{n}(\cos 2\theta),

\displaystyle\chi_{o;n}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2(2n+1)(n+2)},

(159b)

with
n=0,1,2,\dots
being a non-negative integer and
\mathcal{C}^{e/o}_{n}
normalization constants.

In order to fix the normalization constants
\mathcal{C}^{e/o}_{n}
appearing in Eqs. (
159
), we will employ a suitable inner product
\langle\varphi,\chi\rangle_{\rm sh}
, such that

\langle G^{e}_{n},G^{e}_{n^{\prime}}\rangle_{\rm sh}=\langle G^{o}_{n},G^{o}_{n^{\prime}}\rangle_{\rm sh}=\delta_{n,n^{\prime}},

(160)

while
\langle G^{e}_{n},G^{o}_{n^{\prime}}\rangle_{\rm sh}=0
by symmetry considerations.
The inner product compatible with the differential equation (
155
) for a generic solution
G_{n}
can be obtained by multiplying this equation by
G_{n^{\prime}}\sin^{3}\theta
, with
G_{n^{\prime}}
being another solution of the same equation. Subtracting an equivalent relation with
n\leftrightarrow n^{\prime}
gives

\partial_{\theta}\left[\sin^{3}\theta\left(G_{n^{\prime}}\overleftrightarrow{\partial_{\theta}}G_{n}\right)\right]+(\chi_{n}^{2}-\chi_{n^{\prime}}^{2})\sin^{3}\theta G_{n^{\prime}}G_{n}=0.

(161)

Integrating the above equation with respect to
\theta
between
0
and
\pi
provides the natural definition for the inner product, i.e.

\displaystyle(\chi_{n}^{2}-\chi_{n^{\prime}}^{2})\langle G_{n},G_{n^{\prime}}\rangle_{\rm sh}=0,

\displaystyle\langle\varphi,\chi\rangle_{\rm sh}\equiv\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\,\sin^{3}\theta\varphi(\theta)\chi(\theta).

(162)

On the other hand, the orthogonality relation (
135
) for the Jacobi polynomials for the cases
(\alpha,\beta)=(1,-1/2)
and
(1,1/2)
reads as follows:

\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\,\sin^{3}\theta\,P^{(1,-\frac{1}{2})}_{n}(\cos 2\theta)P^{(1,-\frac{1}{2})}_{m}(\cos 2\theta)\\
=\frac{4(n+1)\delta_{nm}}{(2n+1)(4n+3)},

(163)

\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\,\sin^{3}\theta\,\cos^{2}\theta P^{(1,\frac{1}{2})}_{n}(\cos 2\theta)P^{(1,\frac{1}{2})}_{m}(\cos 2\theta)\\
=\frac{4(n+1)\delta_{nm}}{(2n+3)(4n+5)}.

(164)

Thus, the normalization constants
\mathcal{C}^{e/o}_{n}
satisfy

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{e}_{n}=\sqrt{\frac{2(n+\frac{1}{2})(n+\frac{3}{4})}{n+1}},\quad\mathcal{C}^{o}_{n}=\sqrt{\frac{2(n+\frac{3}{2})(n+\frac{5}{4})}{n+1}}.

(165a)

In summary, the mode solutions of Eq. (
155
) for the angular part of the shear waves problem read

\displaystyle G^{e}_{n}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{(2n+1)(4n+3)}{4(n+1)}}P^{(1,-\frac{1}{2})}_{n}(\cos 2\theta),

(166a)

\displaystyle G^{o}_{n}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{(2n+3)(4n+5)}{4(n+1)}}\cos\theta\,P^{(1,\frac{1}{2})}_{n}(\cos 2\theta),

(166b)

while their corresponding eigenvalues are

\chi^{e}_{n}=\sqrt{4n(n+\tfrac{3}{2})},\quad\chi^{o}_{n}=\sqrt{2(2n+1)(n+2)}.

(167)

The general solution for the shear wave problem can be written as

u^{\hat{\varphi}}(t,\theta)=\sin\theta\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left[V_{n}^{e}(t)G^{e}_{n}(\theta)+V_{n}^{o}(t)G^{o}_{n}(\theta)\right],

(168)

where the time-dependent amplitudes are

V_{n}^{e}(t)=v^{e}_{n}e^{-\kappa^{e}_{n}t},\quad V_{n}^{o}(t)=v^{o}_{n}e^{-\kappa^{o}_{n}t}.

(169)

The damping coefficients
\kappa^{e/o}_{n}
read:

\displaystyle\kappa^{e}_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{\nu}{R^{2}}\chi_{e;n}^{2}=\frac{\nu}{R^{2}}2n(2n+3),

(170a)

\displaystyle\kappa^{o}_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{\nu}{R^{2}}\chi_{o;n}^{2}=\frac{2\nu}{R^{2}}(2n+1)(n+2).

(170b)

The constant amplitudes
v^{e/o}_{n}
appearing in Eqs. (
169
) can be obtained from the initial velocity profile
u^{\hat{\varphi}}_{0}(\theta)\equiv u^{\hat{\varphi}}(t=0,\theta)
via

v^{e/o}_{n}=\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\,\sin^{2}\theta\,u^{\hat{\varphi}}_{0}(\theta)G_{n}^{e/o}(\theta).

(171)

Appendix C
Isotropy test

In this appendix section, we test the isotropy preservation of our proposed vielbein lattice Boltzmann scheme by considering the axisymmetric flows described in Sec.
B
in a rotated reference frame. To be specific, we consider a new coordinate frame given by the spherical coordinates (
\theta^{\prime},\varphi^{\prime}
), obtained by applying a clockwise rotation of angle
\alpha
with respect to the
y
axis on the initial coordinate system. With respect to the initial coordinate axes
Ox
,
Oy
and
Oz
, having unit vectors
\mathbf{i}
,
\mathbf{j}
and
\mathbf{k}
, respectively, the new axes have the unit vectors

\mathbf{i}^{\prime}=\mathbf{i}\cos\alpha+\mathbf{k}\sin\alpha,\quad\mathbf{j}^{\prime}=\mathbf{j},\quad\mathbf{k}^{\prime}=-\mathbf{i}\sin\alpha+\mathbf{k}\cos\alpha.

(172)

Consider a point
P
on the sphere, having the position vector
\mathbf{x}(P)=x\mathbf{i}+y\mathbf{j}+z\mathbf{k}
expressed with respect to the original coordinate system. In the new coordinate system, the same vector can be decomposed as
\mathbf{x}(P)=x^{\prime}\mathbf{i}^{\prime}+y^{\prime}\mathbf{j}^{\prime}+z^{\prime}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}
, with

\begin{pmatrix}x^{\prime}\\
y^{\prime}\\
z^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}=R_{2}(\alpha)\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\\
z\end{pmatrix},\ R_{2}(\alpha)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\alpha&0&\sin\alpha\\
0&1&0\\
-\sin\alpha&0&\cos\alpha\end{pmatrix}.

(173)

The above relations allow the spherical coordinates
(\theta,\varphi)
and
(\theta^{\prime},\varphi^{\prime})
with respect to the original and rotated coordinate frames to be related via

\displaystyle\sin\theta^{\prime}\cos\varphi^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\cos\alpha\sin\theta\cos\varphi+\sin\alpha\cos\theta,

(174a)

\displaystyle\sin\theta^{\prime}\sin\varphi^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\sin\theta\sin\varphi,

(174b)

\displaystyle\cos\theta^{\prime}

\displaystyle=-\sin\alpha\sin\theta\cos\varphi+\cos\alpha\cos\theta.

(174c)

The inverse transformation reads:

\displaystyle\sin\theta\cos\varphi

\displaystyle=\cos\alpha\sin\theta^{\prime}\cos\varphi^{\prime}-\sin\alpha\cos\theta,

(175a)

\displaystyle\sin\theta\sin\varphi

\displaystyle=\sin\theta^{\prime}\sin\varphi^{\prime},

(175b)

\displaystyle\cos\theta

\displaystyle=\sin\alpha\sin\theta^{\prime}\cos\varphi^{\prime}+\cos\alpha\cos\theta^{\prime}.

(175c)

The above relations are sufficient to relate a scalar field, e.g., the density, between the two frames:

\rho^{\prime}(\theta^{\prime},\varphi^{\prime})=\rho(\theta,\varphi).

(176)

When considering the components of a vector field, e.g., the velocity, we employ the relation

\begin{pmatrix}u^{\theta^{\prime}}\\
u^{\varphi^{\prime}}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\partial\theta^{\prime}/\partial\theta&\partial\theta^{\prime}/\partial\varphi\\
\partial\varphi^{\prime}/\partial\theta&\partial\varphi^{\prime}/\partial\varphi\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}u^{\theta}\\
u^{\varphi}\end{pmatrix}.

(177)

Using the relations in Eqs. (
174
), we arrive at

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}u^{\prime\hat{\theta}^{\prime}}\\
u^{\prime\hat{\varphi^{\prime}}}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\begin{pmatrix}\cos\alpha\sin\theta^{\prime}-\sin\alpha\cos\theta^{\prime}\cos\varphi^{\prime}&-\sin\alpha\sin\varphi^{\prime}\\
\sin\alpha\sin\varphi^{\prime}&\cos\alpha\sin\theta^{\prime}-\sin\alpha\cos\theta^{\prime}\cos\varphi^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}u^{\hat{\theta}}\\
u^{\hat{\varphi}}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sin\theta^{\prime}}\begin{pmatrix}\cos\alpha\sin\theta+\sin\alpha\cos\theta\cos\varphi&-\sin\alpha\sin\varphi\\
\sin\alpha\sin\varphi&\cos\alpha\sin\theta+\sin\alpha\cos\theta\cos\varphi\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}u^{\hat{\theta}}\\
u^{\hat{\varphi}}\end{pmatrix},

(178)

where we used the relations
u^{\hat{\theta}}=u^{\theta}
and
u^{\hat{\varphi}}=\sin\theta\,u^{\varphi}
between the tetrad and coordinate components of the velocity vector field, while
\sin\theta
and
\sin\theta^{\prime}
can be obtained via

\displaystyle\sin^{2}\theta

\displaystyle=\sin^{2}\theta^{\prime}\sin^{2}\varphi^{\prime}+(\cos\alpha\sin\theta^{\prime}\cos\varphi^{\prime}-\sin\alpha\cos\theta^{\prime})^{2},

\displaystyle\sin^{2}\theta^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\sin^{2}\theta\sin^{2}\varphi+(\cos\alpha\sin\theta\cos\varphi+\sin\alpha\cos\theta)^{2}.

(179)

The inverse relations read

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}u^{\hat{\theta}}\\
u^{\hat{\varphi}}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sin\theta^{\prime}}\begin{pmatrix}\cos\alpha\sin\theta+\sin\alpha\cos\theta\cos\varphi&\sin\alpha\sin\varphi\\
-\sin\alpha\sin\varphi^{\prime}&\cos\alpha\sin\theta+\sin\alpha\cos\theta\cos\varphi\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}u^{\prime\hat{\theta^{\prime}}}\\
u^{\prime\hat{\varphi^{\prime}}}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\begin{pmatrix}\cos\alpha\sin\theta^{\prime}-\sin\alpha\cos\theta^{\prime}\cos\varphi^{\prime}&\sin\alpha\sin\varphi^{\prime}\\
-\sin\alpha\sin\varphi^{\prime}&\cos\alpha\sin\theta^{\prime}-\sin\alpha\cos\theta^{\prime}\cos\varphi^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}u^{\prime\hat{\theta}^{\prime}}\\
u^{\prime\hat{\varphi}^{\prime}}\end{pmatrix}.

(180)

Let us now consider the problem of extracting the amplitudes
U^{e/o}_{n}
and
V^{e/o}_{n}
of the harmonics in the sound and shear wave setups. These amplitudes are extracted in the frame where the flow is axisymmetric, as shown in Eqs. (
151
) and (
169
), via the formulas

\displaystyle U^{e/o}_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\varphi}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\,u^{\hat{\theta}}(t,\theta)F^{e/o}_{n}(\theta),

(181)

\displaystyle V^{e/o}_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\varphi}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\,\sin^{2}\theta\,u^{\hat{\varphi}}(t,\theta)G^{e/o}_{n}(\theta),

(182)

where we inserted an integration with respect to
\varphi
. We now switch the integration variables
d\Omega=\sin\theta d\theta d\varphi
to
d\Omega^{\prime}=\sin\theta^{\prime}d\theta^{\prime}d\varphi^{\prime}
, where the Jacobian is unity as
d\Omega
is an invariant integration measure (this can be checked by an explicit calculation). Thus, we arrive at the expressions

\displaystyle U^{e/o}_{n}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\varphi^{\prime}}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{d\theta^{\prime}\sin\theta^{\prime}}{\sin^{2}\theta}[(\cos\alpha\sin\theta^{\prime}-\sin\alpha\cos\theta^{\prime}\cos\varphi^{\prime})u^{\prime\hat{\theta}^{\prime}}+\sin\alpha\sin\varphi^{\prime}u^{\prime\hat{\varphi}^{\prime}}]F^{e/o}_{n}(\theta),

(183)

\displaystyle V^{e/o}_{n}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\varphi^{\prime}}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta^{\prime}\sin\theta^{\prime}[-\sin\alpha\sin\varphi^{\prime}u^{\prime\theta^{\prime}}+(\cos\alpha\sin\theta^{\prime}-\sin\alpha\cos\theta^{\prime}\cos\varphi^{\prime})u^{\prime\hat{\varphi}^{\prime}}]G^{e/o}_{n}(\theta),

(184)

where
\theta
and
\varphi
can be obtained from Eq. (
175
).

Appendix D
Euler solver

In this section of the appendix, we discuss the Flux Vector Splitting method for the numerical solution of the
Euler Eqs. in the axisymmetric case, obtained by setting
\eta=\zeta=0
in Eqs. (
34a
)–(
34b
):

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial_{\theta}(\rho u^{\hat{\theta}}\sin\theta)}{R\sin\theta}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial(\rho u^{\hat{\theta}})}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial_{\theta}[\rho(u^{2}_{\hat{\theta}}+c_{s}^{2})\sin\theta]}{R\sin\theta}

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho c_{s}^{2}}{R\tan\theta},

(192)

where we took into account that the pressure is
p=c_{s}^{2}\rho
for an isothermal gas.

Considering the vector
U=(\rho,\rho u^{\hat{\theta}})^{T}
of primary variables, we consider the following abstract form of the Euler equations:

\partial_{t}\mathbf{U}+\frac{\partial_{\theta}(\mathbf{F}\sin\theta)}{R\sin\theta}=\mathbf{S}_{\rm inv},

(193)

where the flux vector and the inviscid source vector read

\mathbf{F}=\begin{pmatrix}\rho u^{\hat{\theta}}\\
\rho(u_{\hat{\theta}}^{2}+c_{s}^{2})\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathbf{S}_{\rm inv}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
(\rho c_{s}^{2}/R)\cot\theta\end{pmatrix}.

(194)

The Jacobian matrix
\mathbb{A}=\partial\mathbf{F}/\partial\mathbf{U}
reads

\mathbb{A}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
c_{s}^{2}-u_{\hat{\theta}}^{2}&2u^{\hat{\theta}}\end{pmatrix},

(195)

having the eigenvalues
\lambda_{\pm}=u^{\hat{\theta}}\pm c_{s}
. We take the corresponding right- and left-eigenvectors as

\mathbf{R}_{\pm}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
u^{\hat{\theta}}\pm c_{s}\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathbf{L}_{\pm}=\frac{1}{2c_{s}}\begin{pmatrix}c_{s}\mp u^{\hat{\theta}}\\
\pm 1\end{pmatrix},

(196)

satisfying
\mathbb{A}\mathbf{R}_{\pm}=\lambda_{\pm}\mathbf{R}_{\pm}
and
\mathbf{L}_{\pm}\mathbb{A}=\lambda_{\pm}\mathbf{L}^{T}_{\pm}
. We then introduce the matrices
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{L}
of right- and left-eigenvectors via

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{R}_{+}&\mathbf{R}_{-}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
u^{\hat{\theta}}+c_{s}&u^{\hat{\theta}}-c_{s}\end{pmatrix},

(197a)

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{L}^{T}_{+}\\
\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\mathbf{L}^{T}_{-}\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{2c_{s}}\begin{pmatrix}c_{s}-u^{\hat{\theta}}&1\\
c_{s}+u^{\hat{\theta}}&-1\end{pmatrix},

(197b)

satisfying
\mathbb{A}\mathbb{R}=\mathbb{R}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
and
\mathbb{L}\mathbb{A}=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbb{L}
, with
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}={\rm diag}(\lambda_{+},\lambda_{-})
being the diagonal form of the Jacobian matrix
\mathbb{A}
.

We solve the Euler equations using the third-order Runge-Kutta time-stepping method introduced in Sec.
III.7
. The advection is computed at each point
s
(
1\leq s\leq N_{\theta}
), having polar coordinate
\theta_{s}
, using a Komissarov-type scheme:

\left[\frac{\partial_{\theta}(\mathbf{F}\sin\theta)}{R\sin\theta}\right]_{s}=\frac{\mathbf{F}_{s+\frac{1}{2}}\sin\theta_{s+\frac{1}{2}}-\mathbf{F}_{s-\frac{1}{2}}\sin\theta_{s-\frac{1}{2}}}{R\delta\theta\sin\theta_{s}},

(198)

with
\mathbf{F}_{s\pm 1/2}
being the fluxes at the two interfaces
s\pm 1/2
of cell
s
.
For brevity, we focus on the flux through the interface at
s+1/2
. Here, we require the Jacobian matrix
\mathbb{A}_{s+1/2}
, computed for the primitive variables at the cell interface, defined as
\mathbf{U}_{s+1/2}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{U}_{s}+\mathbf{U}_{s+1})
. Then, the flux
\mathbf{F}_{s+1/2}
is reconstructed using the matrix of right eigenvectors
\mathbb{R}_{s+1/2}
,

\mathbf{F}_{s+1/2}=\mathbb{R}_{s+1/2}\mathbf{Q}_{s+1/2},

(199)

with the vector of characteristic fluxes
\mathbf{Q}_{s+1/2}\equiv(Q^{+}_{s+1/2},Q^{-}_{s+1/2})^{T}
computed in an upwind-biased manner using the WENO-5 advection scheme, described in Eqs. (
65
)–(
67
). The values
\mathbf{Q}_{s^{\prime}}
(
s-3\leq s^{\prime}\leq s+3
) required for the WENO-5 stencils in Eqs. (
65
) are computed using the matrix of left eigenvectors, as follows:
\mathbf{Q}_{s^{\prime}}=\mathbb{L}_{s+1/2}\mathbf{F}_{s^{\prime}}
. The upwind direction is given for each flux
Q^{\pm}_{s+1/2}
by the corresponding eigenvalue,
\lambda^{\pm}_{s+1/2}
. For the ghost nodes outside the grid, we employ
(\rho_{1-s^{\prime}},u^{\hat{\theta}}_{1-s^{\prime}})=(\rho_{s^{\prime}},-u^{\hat{\theta}}_{s^{\prime}})
and
(\rho_{N_{\theta}+s^{\prime}},u^{\hat{\theta}}_{N_{\theta}+s^{\prime}})=(\rho_{N_{\theta}+1-s^{\prime}},-u^{\hat{\theta}}_{N_{\theta}+1-s^{\prime}})
, with
1\leq s^{\prime}\leq 3
.
