On Quantum Large Sieve inequalities and operator recovery from incomplete information
Luís Daniel Abreu, Michael Speckbacher, Erling Svela

Abstract
. We obtain large sieve type inequalities for the Rayleigh quotient of the restriction of phase space representations of higher rank operators, via an operator analogue of the short-time Fourier transform (STFT). The resulting bounds are referred to as quantum large sieve inequalities . Building on foundational work of Donoho and Stark, we demonstrate that these inequalities guarantee the recovery of an operator whose phase-space information is missing or unobservable over a ’measure-sparse’ region Ω \Omega , by solving an L 1 L^{1} -minimization program. This is an operator version of what is commonly known as ‘Logan’s phenomenon’. Moreover, our results can be viewed as a deterministic, continuous variable version of sparse matrix recovery, which itself can be regarded as an operator version of compressive sensing. Our results depend on an abstract large sieve principle for operators with integrable STFT and on a non-commutative analogue of the local reproducing formula in rotationally invariant domains (first stated by Seip for the Fock space of entire functions). As an application, we obtain concentration estimates for Cohen’s class distributions and the Husimi function. We motivate the paper by comparing with Nicola and Tilli’s Faber-Krahn inequality for the STFT, illustrating that norm bounds on a domain Ω \Omega , obtained by large sieve methods, introduce a trade-off between sparsity and concentration properties of Ω \Omega : If Ω \Omega is sparse, the large sieve bound may significantly improve known operator norm bounds, while if Ω \Omega is concentrated, it produces worse bounds.

Introduction

While signal analysis typically deals with vectors and functions, quantum
physics often requires matrices or density operators of
higher rank
to represent
mixed states
. Since
any normalized function
f
can be interpreted as a
rank-one
density operator via the mapping
f\mapsto f\otimes f
, signal analysis of functions can be regarded as a particular instance of signal analysis of quantum states.

The purpose of this paper is to obtain
large sieve type inequalities for the Rayleigh quotient of the restriction of phase space representations of higher
rank operators. The resulting bounds, which will be referred to as
quantum large sieve inequalities
, provide guarantees for an operator-level analogue of Logan’s phenomenon
[
40
,
16
,
16
]
: the possibility of
recovering an operator whose phase space values
are missing or impossible to observe in a ‘measure-sparse’ region
\Omega
, by solving an
L^{1}
-minimization program in the complementary region
\Omega^{c}
.

The large sieve principle has its origins in number theory
[
45
]
, where it provides asymptotic averages of arithmetic functions on integers
constrained by congruences modulo sets of primes. It has been used in a
number of different mathematical fields. In particular, it found important
applications in signal analytic settings, first for band-limited
functions
[
16
]
and, more recently, for the short-time Fourier
[
2
]
and wavelet transforms
[
3
]
, and band-limited spherical harmonics expansions
[
33
]
. The large sieve
inequalities considered in
[
16
,
2
,
3
,
33
]

consist of norm bounds of localization operators on a domain
\Omega
,
which introduce a
trade-off
between
sparsity
and
concentration
properties of
\Omega
. If
\Omega
is sparse, the large
sieve bound may significantly improve known operator norm bounds, while if
\Omega
is
concentrated, it produces worse bounds. Before presenting our results in the operator setting, we illustrate the key principles of our approach in Section
1.1
by revisiting Nicola and Tilli’s ‘Faber-Krahn inequality’ for the STFT
[
46
]
. While this inequality is optimal for arbitrary sets, we demonstrate that it can be significantly sharpened for sets whose Lebesgue measure is ‘well-spread’ across the time-frequency plane.

To put the ideas of the previous paragraph on a firm mathematical foundation, we first present several definitions and illustrative examples.
First, we define the concept of
R
-measure
(or
maximum Nyquist density
) of a Euclidean set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2d}

[
2
]
:

\nu(\Omega,R)=\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}}|\Omega\cap D_{R}(z)|\text{.}

The
R
-measure
is close to the Lebesgue measure of the disk for a ‘well-concentrated’ set
\Omega
, but it can be much smaller for a ‘spread-out’
\Omega
. Examples to support this claim can be easily constructed; see, e.g., the set (
11
). Clearly, for a fixed
R>0
, and any given
\epsilon,\mu>0
, one can always construct a
set
\Omega
with
|\Omega|=\mu
and
\nu(\Omega,R)<\epsilon
. We will loosely refer to ‘
R
-sparse
’ sets, as those for which
\nu(\Omega,R)/\pi R^{2}
is small, but we will not specify any threshold for this
quantity, since it does not enter our results directly.

Let
f,g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
g\neq 0
. For
z=(x,\omega)\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
, the time-frequency shift of
g
is
\pi(z)g(t)=g(t-x)e^{2\pi i\omega\cdot t}
. The
short-time Fourier transform (STFT)
of

f
with window
g
is defined as

(1)

V_{g}f(z)=\langle f,\pi(z)g\rangle\text{.
}

In the terminology of signal analysis and physics, the operator
V_{g}
transforms a function
f(t)
, defined over time (in signal analysis) or position (in physics) with
t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, into a function
V_{g}f(z)
, where
z=(x,\omega)\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
represents the joint
time-frequency
domain in signal analysis or the
phase space
in physics.
In
[
2
]
, large sieve
inequalities were obtained for the STFT by estimating the Rayleigh
quotient of the Daubechies-type localization operator
[
14
,
46
]
,

(2)

\Phi_{\Omega,g}^{(p)}(f)=\frac{\int_{\Omega}|V_{g}f(z)|^{p}dz}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}|V_{g}f(z)|^{p}dz},

in terms of
\nu(\Omega,R)
. In addition to the case
p=1
, estimates for the concentration problem with
p=2
are also of particular interest, as they provide eigenvalue estimates for the Daubechies localization operator
A_{\Omega}^{g}

[
14
,
15
,
13
]
, defined weakly as

\displaystyle A_{\Omega}^{g}f=\int_{\Omega}V_{g}f(z)\pi(z)g\,dz.

By the Courant-Fischer theorem, the value of the supremum of (
2
) is the largest eigenvalue of
A_{\Omega}^{g}
, a well-known connection which has been exploited with great success in the past; see, for instance,
[
1
,
46
]
.

Given a ‘window operator’
\gamma\in\mathcal{S}^{2}
(where
\mathcal{S}^{2}
denotes the space of Hilbert-Schmidt
operators), we define the operator STFT of
\rho\in\mathcal{S}^{2}
as in
[
19
]
:

\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho(z):=\gamma^{\ast}\pi(z)^{\ast}\rho,\qquad z\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}\text{.}

This transform preserves several of the convenient properties of the
classical STFT (
1
).
We provide some information in Section
1.2
and a more detailed exposition in Section
2
. The main goal of this paper is to obtain large sieve
inequalities for the following natural operator analogue of the Rayleigh
quotient (
2
),

(3)

{\Phi}_{\Omega,\gamma}^{(p)}(\rho)=\frac{\int_{\Omega}\left\|\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho(z)\right\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}^{p}dz}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\left\|\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho(z)\right\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}^{p}dz}\text{,}

in terms of
\nu(\Omega,R)
.
Let
\mathfrak{M}^{1}\subset\mathcal{S}^{2}
be the space of operators whose operator STFT has finite
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})
norm. As for the classical STFT case, we will see that once the condition
\sup_{\rho\in\mathfrak{M}^{1}}{\Phi}_{\Omega,\gamma}^{(1)}(\rho)<\frac{1}{2}
is met -
something that our inequalities assure if
\nu(\Omega,R)/\pi R^{2}
is small enough - then
L^{1}
-minimization is guaranteed to exactly recover
\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho(z)
for all
z\in\Omega
, even when the values of
\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho(z)
over the region
\Omega
are missing and only those in the complement

\Omega^{c}
are observed. Precisely, we have:

\rho=\argmin_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{M}^{1}}\left\|\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\sigma\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})},\qquad\text{ subject to }\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\sigma|_{\Omega^{c}}=\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho|_{\Omega^{c}}.

More general recovery scenarios, involving the presence of noise, can be
considered. They will be
gathered in Section
7
.

The paper is structured as follows: This introduction is extended by two
subsections. In Section
1.1
, we revisit the large sieve for the (rank-one) STFT, and compare it to
the sharp Faber-Krahn
inequality
[
46
]
for different levels of
R
-sparsity of the concentration domain. This is mostly intended to
provide intuition for Section
1.2
, where we present our main results
regarding the estimation of (
3
). In most cases we present a particular form of our
results in
d=1
, to facilitate a first reading. Section
2
contains the required
background. In Section
3
we study the best concentration problem in the case
p=2
and show that the solutions on
\mathcal{S}^{2}
coincide with those on
L^{2}
. The proof of the operator large sieve is given in Section
4
. Section
5
provides the proof of a novel kind of local reproducing formula, which holds for polyradial operators of higher rank. This formula is then combined with explicit special function estimates, in order to obtain fully explicit large sieve inequalities. Then, Section
6
considers two applications of the large sieve principle, first to the Husimi function, and then for distributions in the Cohen class. Finally, in
Section
7
, we show how ‘Logan’s phenomenon’ and other
L^{1}

-minimization reconstruction scenarios, can be adapted to the operator setting
[
16
]
.

1.1.
Large sieve inequalities for the STFT

To simplify the presentation and to enable direct comparison with
[
46
]
we will fix the 1-dimensional Gaussian window
g(t)=h_{0}(t)=2^{1/4}e^{-\pi t^{2}},\ t\in\mathbb{R},
in (
1
) throughout this section. With this choice, we can identify the image of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
under the STFT with the Fock
space of entire functions. Exploiting this structure, Nicola and Tilli

[
46
]
established a
sharp
estimate for the Rayleigh quotient (
2
):

(4)

\Phi_{\Omega,h_{0}}^{(p)}(f)\leq 1-e^{-p|\Omega|/2},\qquad\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}\text{.}

This is a special case of the results in
[
46
]
, which confirmed a
conjecture of two of us in
[
2
, Conjecture 1]
. Estimates for the Rayleigh quotient are of particular interest for signal recovery methods when
p=1
.
Assume, for example, that the
certificate condition

(5)

\sup_{f\in M^{1}(\mathbb{R})}\Phi_{\Omega,h_{0}}^{(1)}(f)<1/2

is satisfied (where
M^{1}(\mathbb{R})
denotes the modulation space
[
23
]

of functions with
L^{1}
-integrable STFT, also known as the Feichtinger algebra
[
22
]
), then one may perfectly recover
V_{h_{0}}f
on the whole phase space from only observing

V_{h_{0}}f(z)

outside

\Omega
. In particular, we have

(6)

f=\argmin_{{}_{h\in M^{1}(\mathbb{R})}}\|V_{h_{0}}h\|_{1}\text{,
\ \ \ \ \ subject to }V_{h_{0}}h|_{\Omega^{c}}=V_{h_{0}}f|_{\Omega^{c}}\text{,}

In the setting
of bandlimited functions (
[
40
]
,
[
18
, Theorem 9]
), this
remarkable property is known as ‘Logan’s phenomenon’, an embryonic idea of
the theory of compressive sensing
[
24
,
9
,
47
]
, a
powerful signal analytic tool extended to discrete density operators in
[
31
,
30
]
.

The condition (
5
) is difficult to assure in general. Even the sharp result (
4
) requires the restrictive condition
|\Omega|<2\log 2
. But
large sieve methods can produce better bounds than (
4
) for
R
-sparse sets. The following observation, contained in
[
2
]
, is
the rank-one case of (
13
) in the next section.
Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
g\in M^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and set

I_{R}(V_{h_{0}}f)(z):=\int_{D_{R}(z)}V_{h_{0}}f(w)\langle\pi(w)h_{0},\pi(z)h_{0}\rangle dw,

where
D_{R}(z)
denotes the disk of radius
R
and center
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Then the
following inequality holds

(7)

\Phi_{\Omega,h_{0}}^{(1)}(f)\leq\sup_{f\in M^{1}(\mathbb{R})}\frac{\left\|V_{h_{0}}f\right\|_{1}}{\left\|I_{R}(V_{h_{0}}f)\right\|_{1}}\cdot\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\Omega\cap D_{R}(z)}|V_{h_{0}}h_{0}(z-w)|dw\text{.}

The
local reproducing
formula
(see
[
52
]
for the Gaussian and
[
2
]
for the extension to
general Hermite windows)

(8)

V_{h_{0}}f(z)=\frac{1}{1-e^{-\pi R^{2}}}\int_{D_{R}(z)}V_{h_{0}}f(w)\langle\pi(w)h_{0},\pi(z)h_{0}\rangle dw\text{, \ \ }R>0,

gives
\big(1-e^{-\pi R^{2}}\big)^{-1}
for the first factor in (
7
). Since
|V_{h_{0}}h_{0}(z-w)|=e^{-\pi|z-w|^{2}/2}
, a change of variables
in (
4
) leads to a sharp estimate for the second factor in (
7
):

(9)

\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\Omega\cap D_{R}(z)}|V_{h_{0}}h_{0}(z-w)|dw\leq 2(1-e^{-\nu(\Omega,R)/2})\text{.}

This results in the following large sieve inequality, which seems to have been
hitherto unnoticed:

(10)

\Phi_{\Omega,h_{0}}^{(1)}(f)\leq\frac{2(1-e^{-\nu(\Omega,R)/2})}{1-e^{-\pi R^{2}}}\text{.}

For
R
-concentrated sets (i.e.,
|\Omega|\approx\nu(\Omega,R)
), (
10
) is worse than (
4
), but for
R
-sparse
sets, (
10
) can provide a significant improvement upon (
4
). For example, if
\Omega=D(0,R)
, then (
10
) gives
\Phi_{\Omega,h_{0}}^{(1)}(f)\leq 2
,
while, from (
4
) we get
\Phi_{\Omega,h_{0}}^{(1)}(f)\leq 1-e^{-\pi R^{2}/2}
. In contrast, for

(11)

\Omega=\bigcup\limits_{k=0}^{N-1}D\left(2kR,\frac{R}{2}\right)\text{,}

we have
\nu(\Omega,R)=\pi R^{2}/4
, while
|\Omega|=N\pi R^{2}/4
(which
includes the case
N=\infty
). In this case, (
4
) gives
\Phi_{\Omega,h_{0}}^{(1)}(f)\leq 1-e^{-N\pi R^{2}/8}
, requiring
NR^{2}<\frac{8\log 2}{\pi}
to ensure the certificate condition (
5
). On the other hand, (
10
)
leads to

\Phi_{\Omega,h_{0}}^{(1)}(f)\leq\frac{2(1-e^{-\pi R^{2}/8})}{1-e^{-\pi R^{2}}}\text{,}

which is less than
1/2
for
R<1/9
and arbitrary
N
(even when
N=\infty
and
|\Omega|=\infty
).
It is actually easy to construct domains for which the Faber-Krahn bound is arbitrarily close to one while the large sieve bound is arbitrarily close to zero.

1.2.
A large sieve inequality for the STFT of density operators

We will now describe
the main results of the paper, which extend the observations of the previous section to density
operators.
This requires extending the STFT from rank-one operators (functions) to higher rank operators. Here, we consider the
operator STFT
introduced in
[
19
,
21
]
which uses ideas from quantum harmonic analysis, an emerging field inspired by Werner’s work
[
55
]
. In this extension
the phase space is
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})
with the
Bochner inner product

\langle F,G\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\langle F(z),G(z)\rangle_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}dz.

To gain some intuition on the definition of the operator STFT, we first interpret the classical STFT as a rank-one operator. Let
\gamma=g\otimes e
and
\rho=f\otimes e
,
e,f,g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\|e\|_{2}=1
. The
standard STFT can then be defined as the rank-one operator

\gamma^{\ast}\pi(z)^{\ast}\rho=V_{g}f(z)\,(e\,\otimes\,e).

This motivates the following definition of an operator STFT

\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho(z):=\gamma^{\ast}\pi(z)^{\ast}\rho,\qquad\gamma,\rho\in\mathcal{S}^{2},\ z\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}.

The operator STFT can be seen as a non-commutative extension (for the algebra of operators instead of functions) of the classical STFT and shares many properties with the classical STFT, which is recovered in the rank-one case, as shown above. The range of the operator STFT space
\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}(\mathcal{S}^{2})\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})

is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space with kernel

K_{\gamma}(z,w)=\|\gamma\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}^{-2}\,\gamma^{\ast}\pi(z)^{\ast}\pi(w)\gamma
, and
if
\gamma
is normalized in
S^{2}
, then it follows that
\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}:\mathcal{S}^{2}\to\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}(\mathcal{S}^{2})
is an isometry. We denote
by
\mathfrak{M}^{p}
the space of operators
\rho
with
\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})
.

The operator STFT encompasses a wide range of well-known time-frequency distributions. Beyond the classical STFT, recovered when both

\gamma
and

\rho
are rank-one operators, familiar objects also arise when only one of them is assumed to be rank-one. For instance, if

\rho=f\otimes e
, then the pointwise Hilbert-Schmidt norm satisfies

\|\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}(f\otimes e)\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}^{2}=Q_{\gamma\gamma^{*}}f(z),

which is a distribution in Cohen’s class
[
42
, Section 7]
. Similarly, if
\gamma=h_{0}\otimes h_{0}
and
\rho
is a general density operator, then

\big\|\mathfrak{V}_{h_{0}\otimes h_{0}}\sqrt{\rho}\big\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}^{2}=\langle\rho\pi(z)h_{0},\pi(z)h_{0}\rangle,

which corresponds to the Husimi function of the quantum state
\rho

[
34
]
. Consequently, our main results can be interpreted in terms of familiar phase space representations. As we will see in Section
6
, the special structure of these two representations simplifies the analysis of the involved constants in the large sieve principle.

Similarly to the classical STFT, in order to establish a large sieve estimate we study the STFT concentration problem over
\Omega
:

(12)

\displaystyle{\Phi}_{\Omega,\gamma}^{(p)}(\rho)\leq\sup_{\rho\in\mathfrak{M}^{p}}\frac{\int_{\Omega}\|\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho(z)\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}^{p}dz}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\|\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho(z)\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}^{p}dz}.

Before considering the case
p=1
, let us note that just like for the classical STFT, there is a connection between the concentration problem and the spectrum of a special type of operator, namely the (operator-valued)
mixed-state localization operator
A_{\Omega}^{\gamma\gamma^{\ast}}
, defined as

\displaystyle A_{\Omega}^{\gamma\gamma^{\ast}}\rho=\int_{\Omega}\pi(z)\gamma\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho(z)\,dz.

The rank-one case
\rho=f\otimes e
recovers the function-valued mixed-state localization operator

\displaystyle A_{\Omega}^{\gamma\gamma^{\ast}}(f\otimes e)=\left(\chi_{\Omega}\star\gamma\gamma^{*}\right)\left(f\right)\otimes e=\left(A_{\Omega}^{\gamma\gamma^{\ast}}f\right)\otimes e,

which is known to be related to the best concentration problem for Cohen’s class
[
42
, Proposition 8.2]
. Thus, we can think of the concentration problem (
12
) as a generalization of the concentration problem

\displaystyle\sup_{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\frac{\int_{\Omega}\|\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}f(z)\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}^{2}dz}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\|\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}f(z)\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}^{2}dz}=\sup_{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\frac{\int_{\Omega}Q_{\gamma\gamma^{*}}f(z)dz}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}Q_{\gamma\gamma^{*}}f(z)dz}.

While the problem in
L^{2}
might seem more restrictive than the
\mathfrak{M}^{p}
one, we will show that for
p=2
, the optimal values of the two Rayleigh quotients actually coincide. This will be the topic of Section
3
, where it will be shown that

\sup_{\rho\in\mathcal{S}^{2}}\frac{\int_{\Omega}\|\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho(z)\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}^{2}dz}{\|\rho\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}^{2}}=\sup_{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\frac{\int_{\Omega}\|\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}f(z)\|_{2}^{2}dz}{\|f\|_{2}^{2}}.

In Section
4
, we prove an abstract large sieve type result of the form (
13
) (stated in full generality in Proposition
4.1
). If we define the integral operator

I_{\mathcal{K}}(\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho)(z):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\mathcal{K}(z,,w)\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho(w)dw

for some adequately chosen kernel
\mathcal{K}(z,w)\in\mathcal{S}^{2}
, then for
1\leq p<\infty

(13)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\Omega,\gamma}^{(p)}(\rho)\leq\theta_{\mathcal{K}}\cdot\sup_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\int_{\Omega}\left\|\mathcal{K}(z,w)\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}dz,

with

\theta_{\mathcal{K}}:=\sup_{\rho\in\mathfrak{M}^{1}}\left(\frac{\|\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}}{\|I_{\mathcal{K}}(\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}}\right).

In Section
5
, we then provide explicit bounds for
\theta_{\mathcal{K}}
for different choices of
\gamma
and
\mathcal{K}
.
If, for example, we choose
d=1
and
\gamma
a finite rank polyradial operator,

(14)

\gamma=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\lambda_{n}(h_{n}\otimes h_{n}),

(with
h_{n}
denoting the
n
-th Hermite function)
then we obtain the following fully explicit operator large sieve estimate for the Rayleigh quotient of the operator-valued mixed-state localization operator.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
1\leq p<\infty,

\gamma\in\mathfrak{M}^{1}
be defined as (
14
), such that
\|\gamma\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}=1
, and let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be
measurable. If
\pi R^{2}=\alpha N
for
\alpha\geq 5
, then for every
\rho\in\mathfrak{M}^{p}
it holds

\Phi_{\Omega,\gamma}^{(p)}(\rho)\leq\frac{\nu(\Omega,R)}{1-\alpha^{2N}e^{N(2-\alpha)-\log(2)}}.

Choosing
R
according to the rank
N
is
reminiscent of the original large sieve principle for the Paley-Wiener space
[
16
]
where the parameter
R
is chosen according to the bandwidth.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
is divided in three parts among two different sections, since the first two parts are required for other results. First, as already mentioned, we prove an abstract large sieve in Proposition
4.1
. Then we prove Proposition
5.4
, an operator analogue of the local reproducing formula involving a multiplier. Finally, we estimate the multiplier in Example
5.7
, among other illustrative cases. Since the operator analogue
of the local reproducing formula is considerably different from the rank-one case (
8
), we state it here for polyradial operators in a simplified form (see Proposition
5.4
for the full details):
If
\gamma
is normalized in
\mathcal{S}^{2}
and, as in (
14
), we have

\int_{D_{R}(z)}K_{\gamma}(z,w)\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho(w)dw=\mathfrak{V}_{\widetilde{\gamma}}\rho(z),\qquad z\in\mathbb{R}^{2},

where
D_{R}(z)
is the disk of radius
R
centered at
z
, the operator
\widetilde{\gamma}
is defined by

\widetilde{\gamma}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}A_{n}(R)(h_{n}\otimes h_{n}),

and
A_{n}(R)
is given explicitly in terms of sums of integrals of generalized Laguerre polynomials which do not seem to admit relevant simplification.
The estimate required in Theorem
1.1
follows from a lower bound on
A_{n}(R)
.

Remark 1.2
.

Note that, while the non-commutativity of the setting is not visible in the formulation of the concentration problem, it can manifest itself in subtle ways. The local
reproducing formula stated above, for example, is a
non-commutative analogue of (
8
) in the sense that
\widetilde{\gamma}
is in general not a scalar multiple of
\gamma
, unless it is a rank-one operator. While in rank-one settings, the local reproducing formula is equivalent to double orthogonality in concentric domains (
[
52
,
2
,
3
]
), this is not the case for higher rank (see
[
54
]
for double orthogonality in the operator STFT case). In the discrete variables
setting of matrix recovery, the ‘low rank’ of the matrix stands as a
non-commutative analogue (for the algebra of matrices) of ‘sparsity’ of
vectors
[
31
,
30
]
. See also
[
20
, Section 5]
for a related notion of sparsity for operators. It may be
interesting to observe that, in our continuous setting, sparsity for phase
space representations of operators, is measured in exactly the same way as
sparsity for phase space representations of functions.

In the cases discussed so far, we were able to obtain good estimates of the
constant
\theta_{\mathcal{K}}
, but obtaining precise estimates for the
resulting second term in (
13
) seems to be out of reach in most
instances of higher rank density operators. But for operators with the
spectral decomposition (the square root of the so-called
thermal
states,
see Section
5.1
for details and for the proof of Theorem
1.3
below)

(15)

\gamma=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+a}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\left(\frac{a}{a+1}\right)^{n/2}(h_{n}\otimes h_{n})\text{,}

we can set
\theta_{\mathcal{K}}=1
and obtain a good operator norm
estimate for
{\Phi}_{\Omega,\gamma}^{(p)}
, by bounding the
second factor of (
13
) in terms of the Hilbert-Schmidt norm of
{K}_{\gamma}(z,w)
and using the Weyl correspondence. This leads to

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
a>0
and
\gamma
as in (

) and
1\leq p<\infty
. For every

\rho\in\mathfrak{M}^{p}
,

\Phi_{\Omega,\gamma}^{(p)}(\rho)\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+2a}}\cdot\sup_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\Omega}e^{-\frac{\pi}{2(1+2a)}|z-w|^{2}}\leq 2\sqrt{1+2a}\Big(1-e^{-\frac{|\Omega|}{2(1+2a)}}\Big)\text{.}

Preliminaries

2.1.
Notation

Without any subscripts
\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle
will denote the inner product on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with its corresponding norm
\|\cdot\|_{2}
. The other
L^{p}
-norms are denoted by
\|\cdot\|_{p}
,
1\leq p\leq\infty
.
We use the convention
f\otimes g=\langle\,\cdot\,,g\rangle f
for a rank-one operator and denote the space of Schatten-
p
operators acting on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
by
\mathcal{S}^{p}
,
1\leq p\leq\infty
, where
\mathcal{S}^{\infty}=\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
. We use
\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for the functions in Schwartz class, and define
the space of Schwartz operators
\mathfrak{S}
as the space of integral operators on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with integral kernel
k\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
. We denote its dual space, the tempered operators, by
\mathfrak{S}^{\prime}
. If an operator
\rho
is positive semi-definite, we write
\rho\succeq 0
.

2.2.
Basics of time-frequency analysis and Hermite functions

We give a very brief overview of the most important concepts in time-frequency analysis, for further details see, e.g.,
[
29
]
.
Given
f,g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
g\neq 0
, the
short-time Fourier transform (STFT)
of
f
using the window
g
is defined by

\displaystyle V_{g}f(z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(t)\overline{g(t-x)}e^{-2\pi i\omega\cdot t}dt,\qquad z=(x,\omega)\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}.

If we define the time-frequency shift of
g
as

\pi(z)g(t)=\pi(x,\omega)g(t)=g(t-x)e^{2\pi i\omega\cdot t},

we may write
V_{g}f(z)=\langle f,\pi(z)g\rangle.
A fundamental property of the STFT is
Moyal’s identity
:

(16)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}V_{g_{1}}f_{1}(z)\overline{V_{g_{2}}f_{2}(z)}dz=\langle f_{1},f_{2}\rangle\overline{\langle g_{1},g_{2}\rangle},\qquad f_{1},f_{2},g_{1},g_{2}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

As a direct consequence, one may deduce that
f
can be reconstructed from
V_{g}f
via

\displaystyle f=\frac{1}{\|g\|^{2}_{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}V_{g}f(z)\pi(z)gdz,

where the integral has to be interpreted weakly. Another implication of (
16
) is that
V_{g}f:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
is an isometry if
\|g\|_{2}=1
. Moreover, the adjoint of
V_{g}
is given by

V_{g}^{*}F=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}F(z)\pi(z)gdz,

where again the integral is to be interpreted weakly. If
g\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then the definition of the STFT extends to a tempered distribution
f\in\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
via the pairing
V_{g}f(z)=\langle f,\pi(z)g\rangle_{\mathscr{S}^{\prime},\mathscr{S}}
.

Let
h_{0}(t)=2^{d/4}e^{-\pi|t|^{2}}
denote the standard Gaussian. We define the
modulation space

M^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
1\leq p\leq\infty
, as

M^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):=\left\{f\in\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):\ \|f\|_{M^{p}}:=\|V_{h_{0}}f\|_{p}<\infty\right\}.

The
(cross-)Wigner distribution
of
f,g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is defined by

\displaystyle W(f,g)(x,\omega)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\Big(x+\frac{t}{2}\Big)\overline{g\Big(x-\frac{t}{2}\Big)}e^{-2\pi i\omega\cdot t}dt.

We write
W(f)=W(f,f)
. Given a tempered distribution
F
on
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
one can define an operator
L_{F}
, called the
Weyl transform
of
F
, via the pairing

\displaystyle\langle L_{F}f,g\rangle=\langle F,W(g,f)\rangle,\qquad f,g\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Given an operator
T
, if there is a tempered distribution
F
on
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
such that the relation

\displaystyle\langle Tf,g\rangle=\langle F,W(g,f)\rangle,\qquad f,g\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

holds, we call
F
the
Weyl symbol
of
T
.

The Hermite functions, defined by

\displaystyle h_{n}(t)=\frac{2^{1/4}}{\sqrt{n!}}\left(\frac{-1}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\right)^{n}e^{\pi t^{2}}\frac{d^{n}}{dt^{n}}\left(e^{-2\pi t^{2}}\right),\qquad t\in\mathbb{R},

play an important role in time-frequency analysis and many related areas of mathematics. They are, for example, the eigenfunctions of the harmonic oscillator and form an orthonormal basis for
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.
In higher dimensions we consider the tensor product Hermite functions, defined by

h_{k}(t_{1},t_{2},\dots,t_{d})=\prod_{j=1}^{d}h_{k_{j}}(t_{j}),\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}.

Next, we define the
generalized Laguerre polynomials
by

(17)

\displaystyle L_{k}^{\alpha}(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}(-1)^{j}\binom{k+\alpha}{k-j}\frac{t^{j}}{j!},\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\ \alpha,t\geq 0.

Although the definition of the
generalized Laguerre polynomials via (
17
) is only given for
\alpha\geq 0
, it may be extended for negative
\alpha
using the reflection identity

(18)

\displaystyle\frac{(-t)^{n}}{n!}L_{j}^{n-j}(t)=\frac{(-t)^{j}}{j!}L_{n}^{j-n}(t),

and we will use this property throughout this paper to simplify notation. Like with the Hermite functions, we will consider tensor product Laguerre polynomials, defined by

\displaystyle L_{k}^{\alpha}(t_{1},t_{2},\dots,t_{d})=\prod_{j=1}^{d}L_{k_{j}}^{\alpha_{j}}(t_{j}),\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d},\,\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{d}.

The Laguerre polynomials provide us with a formula for the STFT of a Hermite function with respect to another Hermite function.

Lemma 2.1

(Laguerre connection)
.

Let
n,k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Then

(19)

\displaystyle V_{h_{k}}h_{n}(z)=V_{h_{k}}h_{n}(x,\omega)=\sigma_{n,k}(r)e^{i(k-n)\theta}e^{-\pi ixw},\qquad(x,\omega)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

where
\sigma_{n,k}(r)=\sqrt{\frac{k!}{n!}\pi^{n-k}}r^{n-k}L_{k}^{n-k}(\pi r^{2})e^{-\pi r^{2}/2}
, and
x=r\cos(\theta),\;\omega=r\sin(\theta)
.

Due to the tensor product structure of the Hermite and Laguerre functions the above formula naturally extends to higher dimensions.

2.3.
The operator STFT

In this paper, we are mostly concerned with concentration inequalities for the following analogue of the STFT for Hilbert-Schmidt operators.
Given
\gamma,\rho\in\mathcal{S}^{2}
, we define
\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho
as the
\mathcal{S}^{2}
-valued function

(20)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho(z):=\gamma^{*}\pi(z)^{*}\rho,\qquad z\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}.

The operator STFT was first studied in
[
21
]
. Like the usual STFT, the
operator STFT
represents a signal (an operator acting on functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
) by a function on the phase space
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
. However, unlike the usual STFT, the operator STFT is operator-valued. Nevertheless, the operator STFT inherits many useful properties from the STFT. We collect the ones we need below. For further details we refer to
[
19
]
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\sigma,\rho,\gamma,\eta\in\mathcal{S}^{2}
, and
F\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})
. Then the following properties hold:

(1)

Moyal’s identity:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\langle\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho(z),\mathfrak{V}_{\eta}\sigma(z)\rangle_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}dz=\langle\eta,\gamma\rangle_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}\langle\rho,\sigma\rangle_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}.

(2)

The operator
\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}:\mathcal{S}^{2}\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})
is a multiple of an isometry,
and its adjoint
\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}^{\ast}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})\to\mathcal{S}^{2}
is given by

\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}^{\ast}F=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\pi(z)\gamma F(z)dz,

where the integral is to be understood weakly in
\mathcal{S}^{2}
.

(3)

Inversion:

\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}^{*}\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\pi(z)\gamma\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho(z)dz=\|\gamma\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}^{2}\,\rho.

(4)

The range
\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}(\mathcal{S}^{2})
is an
\mathcal{S}^{2}
-valued reproducing kernel Hilbert space with reproducing kernel

K_{\gamma}(z,w)=\|\gamma\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}^{-2}\,\gamma^{*}\pi(z)^{*}\pi(w)\gamma.

Using the singular value decomposition of a normalized
\gamma=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}(f_{n}\otimes g_{n})
, where
\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{g_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are two orthonormal sets, we may write the reproducing kernel
\gamma^{*}\pi(z)^{*}\pi(w)\gamma
as

(21)

\displaystyle\gamma^{*}\pi(z)^{*}\pi(w)\gamma=\sum_{m,n=0}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}\lambda_{m}\langle\pi(z)f_{n},\pi(w)f_{m}\rangle(g_{m}\otimes g_{n}).

If one considers operators of the form
\rho=f\otimes g
, where
g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is normalized and fixed, the operator STFT evaluates to
\gamma^{*}\pi(z)^{*}(f\otimes g)=(\gamma^{*}\pi(z)^{*}f)\otimes g.

It is therefore natural to define the STFT of a function
f
with respect to an operator
\gamma
as the first term in the operator STFT of its corresponding rank-one operator
f\otimes g
. Indeed, we define
\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}f
as the
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
-valued function

(22)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}f(z):=\gamma^{*}\pi(z)^{*}f,\qquad z\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}.

See
[
19
]
and
[
53
]
for a detailed analysis of this transform and note that the properties of Proposition
2.2
also hold with minor adjustments.

As we are mainly interested in concentration estimates, we note that it follows from the basic
identity
\|\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}f(z)\|_{2}=\|\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}(f\otimes g)(z)\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}
that

(23)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\|\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}f(z)\|_{2}^{p}d\mu(z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\|\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}(f\otimes g)(z)\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}^{p}d\mu(z),

where
1\leq p\leq\infty
, and
\mu
is an arbitrary measure on
\mathbb{R}^{2d}.

Therefore, all results that we derive for the operator-valued STFT immediately carry over to the function-valued STFT.

2.4.
Quantum harmonic analysis and mixed-state localization operators

On several occasions in this paper we will encounter convolutions between operators, and between operators and functions. These are the central objects of the quantum harmonic analysis framework, first studied by Werner
[
55
]
. We recall the basic notions here. For details and connections to time-frequency analysis we refer to
[
42
]
.

For
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
and
T\in\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
we define the
translation of an operator

T
by

\alpha_{z}(T)=\pi(z)T\pi(z)^{\ast},

and the
involution of an operator

T
via

\widecheck{T}=PTP,

where
P
is the parity operator
Pf(t)=f(-t).

For future reference, we note that

\alpha_{z}(\alpha_{w}(T))=\alpha_{z+w}(T),\qquad(\widecheck{T})\,\widecheck{\ }=T\qquad\text{and}\qquad\alpha_{-z}(\widecheck{T})=P\alpha_{z}(T)P.

The latter property follows directly from the simple observations that
P\pi(z)=\pi(-z)P
and
\pi(z)^{\ast}P=P\pi(-z)^{\ast}
.
The
convolution of two operators

S,T\in\mathcal{S}^{1}

is the function
S\star T\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
defined as

S\star T(z)=\text{tr}(S\alpha_{z}(\widecheck{T})),\qquad z\in\mathbb{R}^{2d},

while the convolution of a function
F\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
and an operator
S\in\mathcal{S}^{1}
is the operator
F\star S\in\mathcal{S}^{1}
given by

F\star S=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}F(z)\alpha_{z}(S)dz.

We will later need that the convolution between two operators can be interpreted as the ordinary convolution between their respective Weyl symbols, i.e., for
F,G\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d}),

(24)

\displaystyle L_{F}\star L_{G}(z)=F\ast G(z),\qquad z\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}.

Although initially only defined for
L^{1}
-functions and trace class operators, the convolutions extend to the other Lebesgue spaces and Schatten classes via the
Werner-Young inequalities
: (
[
42
, Proposition 3.6]
or
[
55
]
)

(25)

\displaystyle\|S\star T\|_{r}\leq\|S\|_{\mathcal{S}^{p}}\|T\|_{\mathcal{S}^{q}},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\|F\star S\|_{\mathcal{S}^{r}}\leq\|F\|_{p}\|S\|_{\mathcal{S}^{q}},

where
1\leq p,q,r\leq\infty
satisfy
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1+\frac{1}{r}
. By duality, the two convolutions may also be extended to tempered distributions and operators in
\mathfrak{S}^{\prime}
, see
[
36
]
, or
[
42
, Proposition 3.16]
.

The operator-operator convolution is connected to the Hilbert-Schmidt norm of the operator STFT in the following way:

\displaystyle\|\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\left(\gamma^{*}\pi(z)^{*}\rho\rho^{*}\pi(z)\gamma\right)=\mathrm{tr}\left(\pi(z)\gamma\gamma^{*}\pi(z)^{*}\rho\rho^{*}\right)

(26)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\left(\alpha_{z}(\gamma\gamma^{*})\rho\rho^{*}\right)=\widecheck{\gamma\gamma^{*}}\star\rho\rho^{*}(z).

For future reference, we state this computation as a lemma.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\gamma,\rho\in\mathcal{S}^{2}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\big\|\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho(z)\big\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}=\sqrt{\widecheck{\gamma\gamma^{*}}\star\rho\rho^{*}(z)},\qquad z\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}.

For a positive operator
\sigma
with
\|\sigma\|_{\mathcal{S}^{1}}=1
, we will also
consider the so-called
mixed-state localization operators
defined as
A_{\Omega}^{\sigma}=\chi_{\Omega}\star\sigma

[
42
]
. If we write
\sigma=\gamma\gamma^{\ast}
, then
it was shown in

[
19
, Section 4.3]
that the mixed-state localization operator with respect to
\sigma
is unitarily equivalent to
T_{\Omega}
, the Toeplitz operator on the quantum Gabor space
\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}(\mathcal{S}^{2})
with symbol
\chi_{\Omega}
:

\chi_{\Omega}\star(\gamma\gamma^{\ast})=\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}^{\ast}{T}_{\Omega}\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma},

where
T_{\Omega}:\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}(\mathcal{S}^{2})\to\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}(\mathcal{S}^{2}),

T_{\Omega}F=\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}^{\ast}(\chi_{\Omega}\cdot F).

In particular, any bound on the first eigenvalue of the Toeplitz operator
T_{\Omega}
provides a bound for
\chi_{\Omega}\star(\gamma\gamma^{\ast})
as well. This corresponds to
p=2
in our main results, see Remark
4.4
.

2.5.
Modulation spaces of operators

We define the class of
admissible operators
as the following subset of the Hilbert-Schmidt operators

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}^{1}=\left\{\gamma\in\mathcal{S}^{2}\colon\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\gamma\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})\right\}.

\mathfrak{M}^{1}
is a special case of the
\mathfrak{A}_{v}
-classes which were introduced in
[
19
]
for the study of co-orbit spaces of operators.
\mathfrak{M}^{1}
contains all operators that are nuclear from
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to
M^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),
and is also closed under linear combinations and convolutions with functions in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
. For our purposes, it turns out that
\mathfrak{M}^{1}
is the correct operator analogue of Feichtinger’s algebra
M^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Keeping this analogy in mind, we now define the operator co-orbit spaces
\mathfrak{M}^{p}

[
21
,
20
]
. They will be the operator analogues of the modulation spaces
M^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
p\in[1,\infty]
,
h_{0}
be the standard
d
-dimensional Gaussian, and let
\gamma_{0}=h_{0}\otimes h_{0}
. We define the space
\mathfrak{M}^{p}
by

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}^{p}=\left\{\rho\in\mathfrak{S}^{\prime}\colon\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma_{0}}\rho\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})\right\},

where
\mathfrak{S}^{\prime}
denotes the tempered operators, i.e., the operators with Weyl symbols in the space of tempered distributions.

The spaces
\mathfrak{M}^{p}
are Banach spaces,
\mathfrak{M}^{2}=\mathcal{S}^{2}
, and the inversion formula (Proposition
2.2
(3)) extends to these spaces, see
[
19
, Section 5]
for proofs and a detailed discussion. In addition, the rank-one operator
f\otimes g
belongs to
\mathfrak{M}^{p}
if and only if
f\in M^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),
and
g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, so we can consider the classical modulation space
M^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
as embedded inside
\mathfrak{M}^{p}
. Furthermore,
\mathfrak{M}^{p}
inherits many of the useful properties of
M^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. For instance, replacing the operator
\gamma_{0}
with any other admissible operator defines an equivalent norm on
\mathfrak{M}^{p}
.

Proposition 2.5

(Special case of Proposition 5.13 in
[
19
]
)
.

Any operator
\gamma\in\mathfrak{M}^{1}
defines an equivalent norm on
\mathfrak{M}^{p}
. That is,

\displaystyle\|\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}=\|\gamma^{*}\pi(z)^{*}\rho\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}\asymp\|\rho\|_{\mathfrak{M}^{p}}

for every
p\in[1,\infty]
.
Furthermore, since
\|V_{h_{0}}f\|_{p}=\|\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma_{0}}(f\otimes h_{0})\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})},
any
\gamma\in\mathfrak{M}^{1}
defines an equivalent norm on
M^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

\displaystyle\|\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}f\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))}=\|\gamma^{*}\pi(z)^{*}f\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))}\asymp\|f\|_{M^{p}}.

Lastly, we will need that
\mathfrak{M}^{p}
is an interpolation space when interpolating between
\mathfrak{M}^{1}
and
\mathfrak{M}^{\infty}
, which can be proven using similar arguments as in
[
41
, Theorem 6.8]
.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
p\in(1,\infty)
and
\theta=1-\frac{1}{p}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\big[\mathfrak{M}^{1},\mathfrak{M}^{\infty}\big]_{\theta}=\mathfrak{M}^{p}

Proof.

By Proposition 3.16 and Lemma 4.3 in
[
41
]
, we have

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}^{p}\cong\mathcal{F}W\big(\mathcal{F}L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d},L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})),L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\big).

Interpolation between
\mathfrak{M}^{p}
-spaces thus corresponds to interpolation between suitable Wiener amalgam spaces. As interpolation in Wiener amalgam spaces can be carried out separately in the local and global components, we get

\displaystyle\big[W\big(\mathcal{F}L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},L^{2}),

\displaystyle L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\big),W\big(\mathcal{F}L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d},L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})),L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\big)\big]_{\theta}

\displaystyle=W\big(\big[\mathcal{F}L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})),\mathcal{F}L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d},L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))\big]_{\theta},\big[L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\big]_{\theta}\big)

\displaystyle=W\big(\mathcal{F}L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d},L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})),L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\big),

since
\big[L^{1}\big(\mathbb{R}^{d},L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\big),L^{\infty}\big(\mathbb{R}^{d},L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\big)\big]_{\theta}=L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d},L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
, just like in the scalar-valued case
[
6
]
. The interpolation result for the
\mathfrak{M}^{p}
spaces now follows by using the fact that the Fourier transform is an isomorphism between
\mathfrak{M}^{p}
and
W\big(\mathcal{F}L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d},L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})),L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\big),
see, for instance,
[
23
]
.

2.6.
Reinhardt domains

In this section, we recall some basic properties of Reinhardt domains, for further details, see
[
28
]
.
Reinhardt domains are generalizations of radially symmetric subsets of the plane. A set
\Delta\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is radially symmetric if and only if any rotation leaves
\Delta
invariant. In other words, if we let the circle
\mathbb{T}
act on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
by
e^{i\theta}(x,\omega)=(\cos(\theta)x,\sin(\theta)\omega)
, then
e^{i\theta}(x,\omega)\in\Delta
for any
e^{i\theta}\in\mathbb{T}
and
(x,\omega)\in\Delta
. For
d>1
we consider the action of the torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}
on
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
given by

e^{i\theta}(x,\omega)=\big(\cos(\theta_1)x_{1},\cos(\theta_2)x_{2},\dots,\cos(\theta_d)x_{d},\sin(\theta_1)\omega_{1},\sin(\theta_2)\omega_{2},\dots,\sin(\theta_d)\omega_{d}\big).

We say that a set
\Delta\subset\mathbb{R}^{2d}
is a
Reinhardt domain
if for any
e^{i\theta}\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
and
(x,\omega)\in\Delta
we have
e^{i\theta}(x,\omega)\in\Delta
. In other words, we have

e^{i\theta}\Delta=\Delta,\qquad\text{ for all }e^{i\theta}\in\mathbb{T}^{d}.

Reinhardt domains have a nice description in terms of polyradial coordinates:
x_{i}=r_{i}\cos(\theta_i),

\omega_{i}=r_{i}\sin(\theta_i)
. It then turns out that any Reinhardt domain
\Delta
may be written as
W\times\mathbb{T}^{d}
, where
W\subset\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{d}
. The set
W
is uniquely determined by
\Delta
and vice versa, and we will refer to
W
as the
Reinhardt shadow
of
\Delta

[
11
]
.
Many familiar domains are Reinhardt domains. Below we collect some examples and their Reinhardt shadows.

Example 2.7
.

•

The ball in
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
with radius
R
is a Reinhardt domain. Its shadow is
\{r\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{d}\colon\sum_{j=1}^{d}r_{j}^{2}\leq R^{2}\}
.

•

The polydisk in
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
with polyradius
R=(R_{1},R_{2},\dots,R_{d})
is a Reinhardt domain. Its shadow is
\prod_{j=1}^{d}[0,R_{j}]\subset\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{d}
.

•

In general, let
p\in(0,\infty]
. Balls in the
p
-(semi)norm centered at the origin are Reinhardt domains. Their shadows are given by
\{r\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{d}\colon\sum_{j=1}^{d}r_{j}^{p}\leq R^{p}\}
.

3.
Eigenvalues of vector-valued time-frequency localization operators

In this section, we discuss some basic properties of the spectrum of the mixed-state localization operator
A_{\Omega}^{\gamma\gamma^{\ast}}
defined on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and on
\mathcal{S}^{2}
respectively, and show that, for
p=2
, the solutions to the optimal concentration problems (
2
) and (
3
) on
\mathcal{S}^{2}
and on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
coincide. In other words, the concentration problem on operators reduces to the same concentration problem on pure states.

Recall our standing assumption
\|\gamma\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}=1
.
First, we note that if
\Omega
is compact, then
A_{\Omega}^{\gamma\gamma^{\ast}}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a trace class operator. To see this consider the singular value decomposition of
\gamma=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\lambda_{n}(g_{n}\otimes f_{n})
and let
\{e_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be any orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then

\displaystyle\text{trace}(A_{\Omega}^{\gamma\gamma^{\ast}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\langle A_{\Omega}^{\gamma\gamma^{\ast}}e_{m},e_{m}\rangle=\sum_{n,m\in\mathbb{N}}\int_{\Omega}|\lambda_{n}|^{2}\big\langle\pi(z)(g_{n}\otimes g_{n})\pi(z)^{\ast}e_{m},e_{m}\big\rangle dz

\displaystyle=\sum_{n,m\in\mathbb{N}}\int_{\Omega}|\lambda_{n}|^{2}\big|\big\langle\pi(z)g_{n},e_{m}\big\rangle\big|^{2}dz=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\int_{\Omega}|\lambda_{n}|^{2}dz=|\Omega|.

If
\gamma
satisfies certain additional localization assumptions then it can be shown that the eigenvalue profile of
A_{\Omega}^{\gamma\gamma^{\ast}}
behaves similar to the classical time-frequency localization operators. See
[
43
]
for a detailed discussion of that matter.

Proposition 3.1
.

The mixed-state localization operators
A_{\Omega}^{\gamma\gamma^{\ast}}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
A_{\Omega}^{\gamma\gamma^{\ast}}:\mathcal{S}^{2}\to\mathcal{S}^{2}
have the same eigenvalues. Moreover, every eigenvalue of
A_{\Omega}^{\gamma\gamma^{\ast}}:\mathcal{S}^{2}\to\mathcal{S}^{2}
has infinite multiplicity and the corresponding eigenvectors have rank one
unless the corresponding eigenvalue of
A_{\Omega}^{\gamma\gamma^{\ast}}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
has multiplicity greater than one. Finally,

(27)

\displaystyle\sup_{\rho\in\mathcal{S}^{2}}\frac{\int_{\Omega}\|\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho(z)\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}^{2}dz}{\|\rho\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}^{2}}=\sup_{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\frac{\int_{\Omega}\|\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}f(z)\|_{2}^{2}dz}{\|f\|_{2}^{2}},

and the left-hand side is maximized by a rank-one operator.

Remark 3.2
.

Note that
A_{\Omega}^{\gamma\gamma^{\ast}}:\mathcal{S}^{2}\to\mathcal{S}^{2}
is not compact since every nonzero eigenvalue has infinite multiplicity. The equality (
27
) is therefore not a mere consequence of the min-max-theorem and the
observation that the point spectra of
A_{\Omega}^{\gamma\gamma^{\ast}}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
A_{\Omega}^{\gamma\gamma^{\ast}}:\mathcal{S}^{2}\to\mathcal{S}^{2}
are equal.

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

Let
\{\lambda_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be the collection of nonzero eigenvalues of
A_{\Omega}^{\gamma\gamma^{\ast}}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
in non-increasing order, and
\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be an orthonormal family such that

A_{\Omega}^{\gamma\gamma^{\ast}}f_{n}=\lambda_{n}f_{n}.

Then for every
g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):

A_{\Omega}^{\gamma\gamma^{\ast}}(f_{n}\otimes g)=\big(A_{\Omega}^{\gamma\gamma^{\ast}}f_{n}\big)\otimes g=(\lambda_{n}f_{n})\otimes g=\lambda_{n}(f_{n}\otimes g).

In other words,
f_{n}\otimes g
is an eigenvector in
\mathcal{S}^{2}
for the eigenvalue
\lambda_{n}
. Since
g
is arbitrary, it clearly follows that the multiplicity of
\lambda_{n}
is infinite for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

If
\{g_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is an orthonormal basis of
\overline{\text{span}}^{\bot}(\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}})
and
\{e_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then we may write every
\rho\in\mathcal{S}^{2}
as

(28)

\rho=\sum_{k,n\in\mathbb{N}}\alpha_{n,k}(f_{n}\otimes e_{k})+\sum_{k,n\in\mathbb{N}}\beta_{n,k}(g_{n}\otimes e_{k}),

where
\{\alpha_{n,k}\}_{(n,k)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}},\{\beta_{n,k}\}_{(n,k)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}^{2}).
In particular,
\{g_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is is a basis of
\text{ker}(A_{\Omega}^{\gamma\gamma^{\ast}})
.
Now assume that
\rho
is an eigenvector of
A_{\Omega}^{\gamma\gamma^{\ast}}
with corresponding eigenvalue
\nu\neq 0
. Then

\nu\rho=A_{\Omega}^{\gamma\gamma^{\ast}}\rho=\sum_{k,n\in\mathbb{N}}\alpha_{n,k}(A_{\Omega}^{\gamma\gamma^{\ast}}f_{n}\otimes e_{k})+\sum_{k,n\in\mathbb{N}}\beta_{n,k}(A_{\Omega}^{\gamma\gamma^{\ast}}g_{n}\otimes e_{k})=\sum_{k,n\in\mathbb{N}}\lambda_{n}\alpha_{n,k}(f_{n}\otimes e_{k}),

which is only possible if
\beta_{n,k}=0
and

\alpha_{n,k}=\frac{\lambda_{n}}{\nu}\alpha_{n,k}
for every
(n,k)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
. In particular,
\nu=\lambda_{n^{\ast}}
for some
n^{\ast}\in\mathbb{N}
and
\{\alpha_{n,k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}=0
if
\lambda_{n}\neq\lambda_{n^{\ast}}
. If
\lambda_{n^{\ast}}
has multiplicity one, then it follows that
\text{rank}(\rho)=1.

To prove (
27
) we note that from (
28
)

\displaystyle\sup_{\rho\in\mathcal{S}^{2}}\frac{\int_{\Omega}\|\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho(z)\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}^{2}dz}{\|\rho\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\rho\in\mathcal{S}^{2}}\frac{\langle A_{\Omega}^{\gamma\gamma^{\ast}}\rho,\rho\rangle_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}}{\|\rho\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\alpha,\beta\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}^{2})}\frac{\sum_{n,m\in\mathbb{N}}|\alpha_{n,k}|^{2}\langle A_{\Omega}^{\gamma\gamma^{\ast}}f_{n},f_{n}\rangle+|\beta_{n,k}|^{2}\langle A_{\Omega}^{\gamma\gamma^{\ast}}g_{n},g_{n}\rangle}{\|\alpha\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}+\|\beta\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\frac{\langle A_{\Omega}^{\gamma\gamma^{\ast}}f,f\rangle}{\|f\|_{2}^{2}}=\sup_{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\frac{\int_{\Omega}\|\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}f(z)\|_{2}^{2}dz}{\|f\|_{2}^{2}}.

∎

Remark 3.3
.

Equation (
27
) has interesting consequences when one considers the problem of optimal concentration among all concentration domains of fixed measure. As already discussed in the introduction, Nicola and Tilli showed in their landmark paper
[
46
]
that the following Faber-Krahn inequality holds for the STFT with Gaussian window
h_{0}

(29)

\sup_{{|\Omega|=C}}\ \sup_{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\frac{\int_{\Omega}|V_{h_{0}}f(z)|^{2}dz}{\|f\|_{2}^{2}}\leq 1-e^{-C},\qquad\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2},

with equality occurring if and only if (up to measure zero perturbations)
\Omega=D_{\sqrt{C/\pi}}(z^{\ast})
and
f=\pi(z^{\ast})h_{0}
for some
z^{\ast}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Note that a stable version of this inequality was introduced in
[
27
]
.
If we consider
\gamma=h_{0}\otimes g
for an arbitrary
g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
with
\|g\|_{2}=1
, then we deduce from a combination of (
27
) and (
29
) that

\sup_{|\Omega|=C}\sup_{\rho\in\mathcal{S}^{2}}\frac{\int_{\Omega}\|\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho(z)\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}^{2}dz}{\|\rho\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}^{2}}\leq 1-e^{-C},\qquad\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2},

where equality holds if and only if one has
\Omega=D_{\sqrt{C/\pi}}(z^{\ast})
(up to sets of measure zero) and
\rho=(\pi(z^{\ast})h_{0})\otimes f
for some
z^{\ast}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
with
\|f\|_{2}=1
.

4.
A large sieve principle for the operator STFT

We now prove an operator-valued version of the large sieve principle presented in
[
2
]
. We will start by proving the result in the case where both
\gamma
and
\rho
are admissible. The result can then be extended to
\rho\in\mathfrak{M}^{p}
by interpolation.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\gamma,\rho\in\mathfrak{M}^{1}
with
\|\gamma\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}=1
and let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2d}
be measurable.
If
\mathcal{K}:\mathbb{R}^{2d}\times\mathbb{R}^{2d}\to\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
is such that

(30)

I_{\mathcal{K}}(\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\mathcal{K}(\,\cdot\,,w)\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho(w)dw

is bounded and boundedly invertible on
\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}(\mathfrak{M}^{1})\subset L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})
, then we have

\displaystyle\frac{\|\chi_{\Omega}\cdot\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}}{\|\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}}\leq\theta_{\mathcal{K}}\cdot\sup_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\int_{\Omega}\left\|\mathcal{K}(z,w)\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}dz,

with

\theta_{\mathcal{K}}:=\sup_{\rho\in\mathfrak{M}^{1}}\left(\frac{\|\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}}{\|I_{\mathcal{K}}(\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}}\right).

Proof.

We adapt the proof of
[
2
, Proposition 1]
to our operator-valued setting. The assumption on
\mathcal{K}
implies that for each
\rho\in\mathfrak{M}^{1}
there exists a unique
\widetilde{\rho}\in\mathfrak{M}^{1}
such that

\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho(z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\mathcal{K}(z,w)\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\widetilde{\rho}(w)dw

for almost every
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left\|\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho(z)\right\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}dz

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left\|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\mathcal{K}(z,w)\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\widetilde{\rho}(w)dw\right\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}dz

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\left\|\mathcal{K}(z,w)\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\widetilde{\rho}(w)\right\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}dwdz

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\int_{\Omega}\left\|\mathcal{K}(z,w)\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\big\|\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\widetilde{\rho}(w)\big\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}dzdw

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\int_{\Omega}\left\|\mathcal{K}(z,w)\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}dz\cdot\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\big\|\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\widetilde{\rho}(w)\big\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}dw

\displaystyle=\frac{\big\|\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\widetilde{\rho}\big\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}}{\|\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}}\cdot\sup_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\int_{\Omega}\left\|\mathcal{K}(z,w)\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}dz\cdot\big\|\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}{\rho}\big\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}

\displaystyle\leq\theta_{\mathcal{K}}\cdot\sup_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\int_{\Omega}\left\|\mathcal{K}(z,w)\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}dz\cdot\big\|\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}{\rho}\big\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}.

The second inequality is due to the ideal property of
\mathcal{S}^{2}
, i.e.,
\|AB\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}\leq\|A\|_{\mathrm{op}}\|B\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}
.
∎

We now state the general large sieve principle for the operator STFT.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
p\in[1,\infty)
,
\gamma\in\mathfrak{M}^{1}
with
\|\gamma\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}=1
and let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2d}
be measurable. If
\mathcal{K}
is as in Proposition
4.1
, then we have for
\rho\in\mathfrak{M}^{p}

\displaystyle\frac{\|\chi_{\Omega}\cdot\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}^{p}}{\|\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}^{p}}\leq\theta_{\mathcal{K}}\cdot\sup_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\int_{\Omega}\left\|\mathcal{K}(z,w)\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}dz.

Proof.

Proposition
4.1
proves the case
p=1
. For
p=\infty
we clearly have

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\|\chi_{\Omega}(z)\cdot\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho(z)\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}=\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\chi_{\Omega}(z)\cdot\|\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho(z)\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}\leq\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\|\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho(z)\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}.

Thus we also have an estimate for
\mathfrak{M}^{\infty}.
Now we apply complex interpolation (Proposition
2.6
) to get the result.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

This proof strategy also works when the characteristic function
\chi_{\Omega}
is replaced by a general weight function
F\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
. In this case, however, one picks up an additional constant on the right-hand side, since

\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\|F(z)\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho(z)\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}\leq\|F\|_{\infty}\cdot\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\|\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho(z)\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}.

In particular, the correct inequality is as follows

\displaystyle\frac{\|F\cdot\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}^{p}}{\|\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}^{p}}\leq\theta_{\mathcal{K}}\cdot\|F\|_{\infty}^{p-1}\cdot\sup_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\left\|\mathcal{K}(z,w)\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}F(z)dz.

Remark 4.4
.

From the observation (
23
), we infer that Proposition
4.2
also provides the following estimate for
f\in M^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

\displaystyle\frac{\|\chi_{\Omega}\cdot\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}f\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))}^{p}}{\|\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}f\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))}^{p}}\leq\theta_{\mathcal{K}}\cdot\sup_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\int_{\Omega}\left\|\mathcal{K}(z,w)\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}dz.

Finally, the discussion in Section
3
shows that the first eigenvalue of the
mixed-state localization operator
A_{\Omega}^{\gamma\gamma^{\ast}}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfies

\lambda_{1}\big(A_{\Omega}^{\gamma\gamma^{\ast}}\big)\leq\theta_{\mathcal{K}}\cdot\sup_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\int_{\Omega}\left\|\mathcal{K}(z,w)\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}dz.

This estimate holds for quite general sets
\Omega
, operators
\gamma
and kernels
\mathcal{K}
and as such, it is not optimal.

So far, our large sieve estimate is rather abstract. The following section will therefore be concerned with studying different kernels
\mathcal{K}
and deriving concrete estimates of the corresponding constant
\theta_{\mathcal{K}}
.

5.
Estimating the constant
\theta_{\mathcal{K}}

5.1.
Proof of Theorem
1.3

We first note that, if we use the reproducing kernel
K_{\gamma}
as the integral kernel, we immediately derive the following result.

Corollary 5.1
.

Let
p\in[1,\infty)
,
\gamma\in\mathfrak{M}^{1}
with
\|\gamma\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}=1
and let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2d}
be measurable. For
\rho\in\mathfrak{M}^{p}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\|\chi_{\Omega}\cdot\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}^{p}}{\|\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}^{p}}\leq\sup_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\int_{\Omega}\left\|\gamma^{*}\pi(z)^{*}\pi(w)\gamma\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}dz.

Proof.

If we use
\mathcal{K}=K_{\gamma}
, then
\theta_{\mathcal{K}}=1
and the statement follows from Proposition
4.2
.
∎

Now, observe that if the operator window is a rank-one operator
g\otimes g
with
g\in M^{1}
, then the operator norm of the kernel simplifies to the absolute value of the kernel of the scalar valued STFT, i.e.,

\displaystyle\frac{\|\chi_{\Omega}\cdot\mathfrak{V}_{g\otimes g}\rho\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}^{p}}{{\|\mathfrak{V}_{g\otimes g}}\rho\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}^{p}}\leq\sup_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\int_{\Omega}|\langle\pi(w)g,\pi(z)g\rangle|dz=\sup_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\int_{\Omega}|K_{g}(z,w)|dz.

We note that although the operator norm of the kernel gives the best estimate, it is in many situations easier to estimate the right-hand side of Proposition
4.2
by a Schatten norm since one can exploit the trace properties to get results that are easier to interpret or are even explicit. In this case, estimating the right-hand side using the Hilbert-Schmidt norm, and writing
\sigma=\gamma\gamma^{*}
, we have by Lemma
2.3

(31)

\|\gamma^{*}\pi(z)^{*}\pi(w)\gamma\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\|\gamma^{*}\pi(z)^{*}\pi(w)\gamma\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}=\|\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\gamma(z-w)\|_{\mathcal{S}_{2}}=\sqrt{\widecheck{\sigma}\star{\sigma}(z-w)},

an estimate that we will leverage in the following example, which completes the proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Example 5.2
.

Let
d=1
and consider the Weyl transform
L_{g_{a}}
of the Gaussian
g_{a}
,
a>0
, defined by
g_{a}(t)=\frac{2}{1+2a}e^{-\frac{2\pi}{1+2a}|t|^{2}},\ t\in\mathbb{R}^{2},
which has the spectral decomposition

\displaystyle L_{g_{a}}=\frac{1}{a+1}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\left(\frac{a}{a+1}\right)^{n}(h_{n}\otimes h_{n}).

Note that the normalization of
g_{a}
is chosen such that
\|g_{a}\|_{1}=\|L_{g_{a}}\|_{\mathcal{S}^{1}}=1.

Now take
\gamma=\sqrt{L_{g_{a}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+a}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\big(\frac{a}{a+1}\big)^{n/2}(h_{n}\otimes h_{n})
, so that
\gamma\gamma^{*}=L_{g_{a}}
gives us the Cohen’s classes
W(f,f)*g_{a}
, which are popular choices in the literature, due to their positivity
[
29
,
8
,
56
]
.
Instead of trying to compute the operator norm of the expansion (
21
) we instead use (
31
). Together with (
24
) and the semigroup property of the Gaussian this yields the following nice explicit expression for the Hilbert-Schmidt norm of the reproducing kernel

\displaystyle\|\gamma^{*}\pi(z)^{*}\pi(w)\gamma\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\widecheck{L_{g_{a}}}\star{L_{g_{a}}}(z-w)}=\sqrt{L_{g_{a}}\star L_{g_{a}}(z-w)}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{g_{a}*g_{a}(z-w)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+2a}}e^{-\frac{\pi}{2(1+2a)}|z-w|^{2}}.

Setting
w=0
above, we see that
\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\gamma\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathcal{S}^{2})
, that is
\gamma
is admissible and we may apply Proposition
4.1

to derive the estimate

\displaystyle\frac{\|\chi_{\Omega}\cdot\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathcal{S}^{2})}^{p}}{\|\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}^{p}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+2a}}\cdot\sup_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\Omega}e^{-\frac{\pi}{2(1+2a)}|z-w|^{2}}dz\leq{2\sqrt{1+2a}\left(1-e^{-\frac{|\Omega|}{{2(1+2a)}}}\right)},

where in the last step we used that the Gaussian integral is maximized for
\Omega
being a ball centered at
w
. In other words, we have established Theorem
1.3
.

5.2.
Local reproducing formulas for operators

In order to interpret Proposition
4.2
as a measure of the sparsity of
\Omega
we will derive and employ local reproducing formulas for the operator STFT.
Before we prove the result,
we recall a result from
[
54
, Lemma 5.2]
which generalized
[
2
, Proposition 6]
to higher dimensions.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
W\subset\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{d}
be bounded, let
\Delta\subset\mathbb{R}^{2d}
be a Reinhardt domain with Reinhardt shadow
W
, and let
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
. Then, the short-time Fourier transforms of the Hermite functions
\{V_{h_{m}}h_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}
are orthogonal with respect to
\chi_{\Delta}(z)dz
, i.e.,

(32)

\displaystyle\int_{\Delta}V_{h_{m}}h_{n}(z)\overline{V_{h_{m}}h_{k}(z)}dz=C_{n,m}(\Delta)\cdot\delta_{n,k}\qquad n,k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d},

where

(33)

\displaystyle C_{n,m}(\Delta)=(2\pi)^{d}\frac{m!}{n!}\pi^{|n-m|}\int_{W}r^{2n-2m+1}e^{-\pi|r|^{2}}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{d}L_{m_{j}}^{n_{j}-m_{j}}(\pi r_{j}^{2})\right)^{2}dr.

The above lemma shows that the following identity holds weakly

\int_{\Delta}\langle h_{n},\pi(w)h_{m}\rangle\pi(w){h_{m}}dw=C_{n,m}(\Delta)h_{n}.

If we then argue as in
[
2
, Theorem 1]
, we deduce that

(34)

\int_{z+\Delta}\langle\pi(z)h_{n},\pi(w)h_{m}\rangle\pi(w){h_{m}}dw=C_{n,m}(\Delta)\cdot\pi(z)h_{n}

holds weakly. Therefore,
we deduce the following local reproducing formula

(35)

V_{h_{n}}f(z)=\frac{1}{C_{n,m}(\Delta)}\int_{z+\Delta}V_{h_{m}}f(w)\langle\pi(w)h_{m},\pi(z)h_{n}\rangle dw,\qquad z\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}.

Note that here, the value of the STFT with window
h_{n}
at a point
z
is recovered from the values of the STFT with window
h_{m}
in a neighborhood of
z
. Compare this to
[
2
, Theorem 1]
where the
V_{h_{n}}f(z)
is recovered from the local information on
V_{h_{n}}f
around
z
.

We are now ready to state our operator analogue of the local reproducing formula for the STFT. Unlike the classical case, where we reproduce the STFT precisely, the quantum local reproducing formula yields the value of an operator STFT of the same signal, but with a different operator window. For the classical STFT, the Hermite functions are the particular windows that admit a local reproducing formula. In the operator case, the Hermite functions are replaced by the class of
polyradial operators
, that is, the operators that admit a diagonal decomposition in terms of the Hermite basis:
\gamma=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}\lambda_{n}(h_{n}\otimes h_{n})
.

Proposition 5.4

(Quantum local reproducing formula)
.

Let
\Delta\subset\mathbb{R}^{2d}
be a Reinhardt domain, and let
\gamma=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}\lambda_{n}h_{n}\otimes h_{n}
be a polyradial operator with
\|\gamma\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}=1
. For any
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
, we have

(36)

\displaystyle\int_{z+\Delta}K_{\gamma}(z,w)\gamma^{\ast}\pi(w)^{\ast}dw=\big(\widetilde{\gamma}(\Delta)\big)^{\ast}\pi(z)^{\ast},

where

\widetilde{\gamma}(\Delta)=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}\lambda_{m}A_{m}(\Delta)(h_{m}\otimes h_{m}),

and

\displaystyle A_{m}(\Delta)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}|\lambda_{n}|^{2}C_{m,n}(\Delta),

and
C_{m,n}(\Delta)
is given by (
33
). In particular, for every
\rho\in\mathcal{S}^{2}
one has

(37)

\mathfrak{V}_{\widetilde{\gamma}(\Delta)}\rho(z)=\int_{z+\Delta}K_{\gamma}(z,w)\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho(w)dw.

Proof.

Let us first write out
K_{\gamma}(z,w)\gamma^{\ast}\pi(w)^{\ast}
explicitly:

\displaystyle K_{\gamma}(z,w)\gamma^{\ast}\pi(w)^{\ast}

\displaystyle=\gamma^{*}\pi(z)^{\ast}\pi(w)\gamma\gamma^{\ast}\pi(w)^{\ast}

\displaystyle=\gamma^{*}\pi(z)^{\ast}\pi(w)\left(\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}|\lambda_{n}|^{2}h_{n}\otimes(\pi(w)h_{n})\right)

\displaystyle=\gamma^{*}\left(\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}|\lambda_{n}|^{2}\big(\pi(z)^{\ast}\pi(w)h_{n}\big)\otimes\big(\pi(w)h_{n}\big)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}\overline{\lambda_{m}}|\lambda_{n}|^{2}\langle\pi(w)h_{n},\pi(z)h_{m}\rangle\big(h_{m}\otimes(\pi(w)h_{n})\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}\overline{\lambda_{m}}\,h_{m}\otimes\left(\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}|\lambda_{n}|^{2}\langle\pi(z)h_{m},\pi(w)h_{n}\rangle\,\pi(w)h_{n}\right).

The last equality holds due to our convention of the tensor product being antilinear in the second argument. Integrating over
z+\Delta
thus yields by (
34
)

\displaystyle\int_{z+\Delta}K_{\gamma}(z,w)\gamma^{\ast}\pi(w)^{\ast}dw

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}\overline{\lambda_{m}}\,h_{m}\otimes\left(\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}|\lambda_{n}|^{2}\int_{z+\Delta}\langle\pi(z)h_{m},\pi(w)h_{n}\rangle\,\pi(w)h_{n}dw\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}\overline{\lambda_{m}}\,h_{m}\otimes\left(\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}|\lambda_{n}|^{2}C_{m,n}(\Delta)\pi(z)h_{m}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}\overline{\lambda_{m}}A_{m}(\Delta)\,h_{m}\otimes\pi(z)h_{m}=\big(\widetilde{\gamma}(\Delta)\big)^{\ast}\pi(z)^{\ast}.

The identity (
37
) follows if we multiply both sides of (
36
) from the right by
\rho.

∎

Remark 5.5
.

While the operator window
\widetilde{\gamma}(\Delta)
is in general neither equal to nor a scalar multiple of
\gamma
, it is still a polyradial operator. In the special case where
\gamma=h_{n}\otimes h_{n}
is a rank-one operator, then
A_{m}(\Delta)=\delta_{n,m}C_{m,n}(\Delta)
and
\widetilde{\gamma}(\Delta)=C_{n,n}(\Delta)\gamma
. This leads to a local reproducing formula reminiscent of the original version obtained in
[
2
]

(38)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{V}_{h_{n}\otimes h_{n}}\rho(z)=\frac{1}{C_{n,n}(\Delta)}\int_{z+\Delta}\langle\pi(w)h_{n},\pi(z)h_{n}\rangle\mathfrak{V}_{h_{n}\otimes h_{n}}\rho(w)dw.

In this case, the constant
A_{m}(\Delta)
is the eigenvalue of the classical localization operator
A_{\Delta}^{h_{n}}=\chi_{\Delta}\star h_{n}\otimes h_{n}
corresponding to the
m
-th Hermite function. In general,
A_{m}(\Delta)
coincides with the eigenvalue of the mixed-state localization operator
A_{\Delta}^{\gamma\gamma^{*}}=\chi_{\Delta}\star\gamma\gamma^{*}
that corresponds to the
m
-th Hermite function.

In order to apply Proposition
4.2
we need to compute an upper bound of

(39)

\theta_{\mathcal{K}}=\sup_{\rho\in\mathfrak{M}^{1}}\frac{\|\mathfrak{V}_{{\gamma}}\rho\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}}{\|\mathfrak{V}_{\widetilde{\gamma}(\Delta)}\rho\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}}

where the denominator can be written as

\displaystyle\|\mathfrak{V}_{\widetilde{\gamma}(\Delta)}\rho\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\left\|\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}\overline{\lambda_{m}}A_{m}(\Delta)\mathfrak{V}_{h_{m}\otimes h_{m}}\rho(z)\right\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}dz

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\left(\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}|\lambda_{m}|^{2}A_{m}(\Delta)^{2}\|\mathfrak{V}_{h_{m}\otimes h_{m}}\rho(z)\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}^{2}\right)^{1/2}dz.

There is no one-fits-all recipe for how to bound (
39
) as the spectral structure of
S
critically influences the value of
\theta_{\mathcal{K}}
. Nevertheless, we present several strategies that may be useful.
First, for

(40)

B(\Delta)=\inf\big\{A_{m}(\Delta):\ m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d},\ \lambda_{m}A_{m}(\Delta)\neq 0\big\},

one deduces that

\displaystyle\|\mathfrak{V}_{\widetilde{\gamma}(\Delta)}\rho\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}

\displaystyle\geq B(\Delta)\cdot\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\left(\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}|\lambda_{m}|^{2}\|\mathfrak{V}_{h_{m}\otimes h_{m}}\rho(z)\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}^{2}\right)^{1/2}dz

\displaystyle=B(\Delta)\cdot\|\mathfrak{V}_{{\gamma}}\rho\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})},

and consequently

(41)

\displaystyle\theta_{\mathcal{K}}\leq B(\Delta)^{-1}.

Note that in general
B(\Delta)
might be zero. If
\gamma
is a finite rank polyradial operator, however, then
B(\Delta)>0
is guaranteed.
In the following, we consider particular examples of how to lower bound
B(\Delta)
and discuss cases when (
41
) does not provide a good estimate.

Example 5.6
.

Let
d=1
and
\Delta=D_{R}(0)
. The simplest example of a polyradial operator with rank greater than one is the rank-two operator
\gamma=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(h_{0}\otimes h_{0}+h_{1}\otimes h_{1})
. In this setting we can compute

\displaystyle B(D_{R}(0))

\displaystyle=\min_{m\in\{0,1\}}A_{m}(D_{R}(0))

explicitly.
Indeed,

\displaystyle A_{0}(D_{R}(0))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(C_{0,0}+C_{0,1})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\pi R^{2}}e^{-t}dt+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\pi R^{2}}te^{-t}dt=1-\frac{2+\pi R^{2}}{2}e^{-\pi R^{2}},

while

\displaystyle A_{1}(D_{R}(0))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(C_{1,0}+C_{1,1})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\pi R^{2}}te^{-t}dt+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\pi R^{2}}\big(1-t\big)^{2}e^{-t}dt

\displaystyle=1-\frac{2+\pi R^{2}+\pi^{2}R^{4}}{2}e^{-\pi R^{2}}.

Since
A_{0}(D_{R}(0))-A_{1}(D_{R}(0))=\frac{\pi^{2}R^{4}}{2}e^{-\pi R^{2}}>0
for all
R>0
we conclude that

B(D_{R}(0))=A_{1}(D_{R}(0))=1-\frac{2+\pi R^{2}+\pi^{2}R^{4}}{2}e^{-\pi R^{2}}.

Example 5.7
.

Again assume that
d=1
,
\Delta=D_{R}(0)
, and consider
\gamma=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\lambda_{n}(h_{n}\otimes h_{n})
, for some fixed
N\in\mathbb{N}
. In this example, we show how to lower bound
B(D_{R}(0))
when
R
is chosen according to the rank
N
.

Let
m\geq n
. By orthonormality,

\displaystyle 1=\frac{n!}{m!}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{m-n}\big(L_{n}^{m-n}(t)\big)^{2}e^{-t}dt,

and we deduce that

C_{n,m}(D_{R}(0))=\frac{n!}{m!}\int_{0}^{\pi R^{2}}t^{m-n}\big(L_{n}^{m-n}(t)\big)^{2}e^{-t}dt=1-\frac{n!}{m!}\int_{\pi R^{2}}^{\infty}t^{m-n}\big(L_{n}^{m-n}(t)\big)^{2}e^{-t}dt.

It is therefore sufficient to provide an upper bound for the integral on the right-hand side.

The following basic estimate was shown in
[
32
, Lemma 2.4]

(42)

|L^{m-n}_{n}(t)|\leq\frac{t^{n}}{n!},\qquad\text{for }\ t\geq\frac{n+m}{2}-1+\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{4}+(n-1)(m-1)}.

If
\pi R^{2}\geq 2N\geq m+n
, for
m,n\leq N
, then

(43)

\frac{(\pi R^{2})^{k}}{k!}=\frac{\pi R^{2}}{k}\,\frac{(\pi R^{2})^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}\geq\frac{(\pi R^{2})^{k-1}}{(k-1)!},\qquad k\in\{1,...,m+n\}.

Using the recurrence relation
\Gamma(s+1,x)=s\Gamma(s,x)+x^{s}e^{-x}
for the upper incomplete gamma function we observe that

(44)

\Gamma(m,t)=(m-1)!\,e^{-t}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\frac{t^{k}}{k!},\qquad m\in\mathbb{N}.

Note that for
t=\pi R^{2}\geq 2N
, the condition in (
42
) is satisfied, which allows us to combine (
42
) (
43
) and (
44
) to show that for
\pi R^{2}\geq 2N

\displaystyle\frac{n!}{m!}\int_{\pi R^{2}}^{\infty}t^{m-n}\big(L_{n}^{m-n}(t)\big)^{2}e^{-t}dt

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n!m!}\int_{\pi R^{2}}^{\infty}t^{m+n}e^{-t}dt=\frac{\Gamma(n+m+1,\pi R^{2})}{n!m!}

\displaystyle=\frac{(n+m)!}{n!m!}e^{-\pi R^{2}}\sum_{k=0}^{n+m}\frac{(\pi R^{2})^{k}}{k!}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(n+m)!}{n!m!}e^{-\pi R^{2}}(m+n+1)\frac{(\pi R^{2})^{m+n}}{(m+n)!}

\displaystyle\leq(n+m+1)e^{-\pi R^{2}}\frac{(\pi R^{2})^{m+n}}{n!m!}\leq(2N+1)e^{-\pi R^{2}}\frac{(\pi R^{2})^{2N}}{(N!)^{2}}.

Altogether we thus have

\displaystyle B(D_{R}(0))

\displaystyle=\min_{m\in\{1,...,N\}}A_{m}(D_{R}(0))=\min_{m\in\{1,...,N\}}\sum_{n=0}^{N}|\lambda_{n}|^{2}C_{n,m}(D_{R}(0))

\displaystyle\geq\min_{m\in\{1,...,N\}}\sum_{n=0}^{N}|\lambda_{n}|^{2}\Big(1-(2N+1)e^{-\pi R^{2}}\frac{(\pi R^{2})^{2N}}{(N!)^{2}}\Big)

\displaystyle=1-(2N+1)e^{-\pi R^{2}}\frac{(\pi R^{2})^{2N}}{(N!)^{2}}.

The final expression is not positive for all
\pi R^{2}\geq 2N
. However, if we set
\pi R^{2}=\alpha N
, then we obtain from an application of the pointwise Stirling bound
n!\geq\sqrt{2\pi n}\left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^{n}
(see, e.g.,
[
50
]
) that

\displaystyle B(D_{R}(0))

\displaystyle\geq 1-(2N+1)e^{-\alpha N}\frac{(\alpha N)^{2N}}{(N!)^{2}}\geq 1-\frac{(2N+1)}{2\pi N}\alpha^{2N}e^{N(2-\alpha)}\geq 1-\frac{\alpha^{2N}}{2}e^{N(2-\alpha)},

which is greater than zero for
\alpha\geq 5
. With these calculations we have thus proven Theorem
1.1
.

Example 5.9
.

Let again

As

Example 5.10
.

Estimating
\theta_{\mathcal{K}}
via a lower bound on

often leads to rather bad estimates. Take, for example, a small perturbation of the rank-one operator
\gamma_{0}=h_{0}\otimes h_{0}
, i.e., consider
\gamma=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{d}_{0}}\lambda_{n}(h_{n}\otimes h_{n})
with
\lambda_{0}=\sqrt{1-\varepsilon}
and
\lambda_{k}=\sqrt{\varepsilon/N}
for

,
\#\mathcal{N}=N
, and
\lambda_{k}=0
otherwise. For small

one has that

\|\mathfrak{V}_{\widetilde{\gamma}(\Delta)}\rho\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}\approx C_{0,0}(\Delta)\|\mathfrak{V}_{{\gamma}}\rho\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})},

while

which can be significantly smaller than

, in particular, if
\mathcal{N}
contains an element

large.

For
\Delta,\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the maximum Nyquist density of
\Omega
, first introduced in
[

, is given by

\nu(\Omega,\Delta)=\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}}|\Omega\cap(z+\Delta)|.

When

is a disk, then

is the

can in general be thought of as a measure of the sparsity of

, see the discussion in Section
1.1
. With the preparatory computations in place, we are now ready to apply Proposition

in order to connect the best concentration problem to the maximum Nyquist density.

Corollary 5.11
.

Let
\gamma=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}\lambda_{n}(h_{n}\otimes h_{n})\in\mathfrak{M}^{1}
be such that

and let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2d}
be measurable. Moreover, let
\theta_{\mathcal{K}}
be given by (

). Then for

, it holds

\displaystyle\frac{\|\chi_{\Omega}\cdot\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}^{p}}{\|\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}^{p}}

\displaystyle\leq\theta_{\mathcal{K}}\cdot\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\int_{\Omega\cap z+\Delta}\left\|\gamma^{*}\pi(z)^{*}\pi(w)\gamma\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}dw

\displaystyle\leq\theta_{\mathcal{K}}\cdot\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\int_{\Omega\cap z+\Delta}\left(\sum_{m,n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}|\lambda_{n}|^{2}|\lambda_{m}|^{2}|\langle h_{n},\pi(z-w)h_{m}\rangle|^{2}\right)^{1/2}dw

\displaystyle\leq\theta_{\mathcal{K}}\cdot\nu(\Omega,\Delta).

Proof.

The first inequality is a direct consequence of Proposition
4.2
applied for
\mathcal{K}(z,w)=K_{\gamma}(z,w)\cdot\chi_{z+\Delta}(w)
.
To obtain the second inequality, we recall that that
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}
and observe that by (

\|K_{\gamma}(z,w)\|_{\mathcal{S}_{2}}^{2}=\sum_{m,n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}|\lambda_{n}|^{2}|\lambda_{m}|^{2}|\langle\pi(w)h_{n},\pi(z)h_{m}\rangle|^{2}.

The third inequality is due to the bound

\displaystyle\sum_{m,n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}|\lambda_{n}|^{2}|\lambda_{m}|^{2}|\langle\pi(w)h_{n},\pi(z)h_{m}\rangle|^{2}\leq\sum_{m,n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}|\lambda_{n}|^{2}|\lambda_{m}|^{2}=1.

∎

When

Corollary

recovers
[

, Theorem 3]
, as expected.

6.
Applications of the quantum large sieve principle

In this section we will consider two special cases of the operator STFT, both of which are popular phase space representations of operators and functions, respectively. We will see how the large sieve principle applies to both cases.

6.1.
The Husimi function

Given a positive trace class operator
\rho
, the assignment

\displaystyle H_{\rho}(z):=\langle\rho\,\pi(z)h_{0},\pi(z)h_{0}\rangle

is called the
Husimi Q-function
of
\rho

[
34
,
44
,
51
]
, the
coherent state transform
of
\rho

[
26
]
or the
Berezin transform
of
\rho

[
5
,
13
]
. In physics, it is a common way to represent a quantum state on phase space.
Naturally, we interpret the integral

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}H_{\rho}(z)dz

as the time-frequency localization of the state
\rho
on the domain
\Omega
.
An application of Parseval’s theorem shows that the Husimi function of
\rho
equals the convolution
\rho\star(h_{0}\otimes h_{0})
. Keeping in mind that
(h_{0}\otimes h_{0})^{2}=h_{0}\otimes h_{0}
, it is also clear that

\displaystyle\big\|\mathfrak{V}_{h_{0}\otimes h_{0}}\sqrt{\rho}(z)\big\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\big\langle(h_{0}\otimes h_{0})\pi(z)^{\ast}\sqrt{\rho},(h_{0}\otimes h_{0})\pi(z)^{\ast}\sqrt{\rho}\big\rangle_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\text{tr}\big(\sqrt{\rho}\pi(z)(h_{0}\otimes h_{0})\pi(z)^{\ast}\sqrt{\rho}\,\big)=\text{tr}(\rho\,\alpha_{z}(h_{0}\otimes h_{0}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}\langle\rho\,\pi(z)h_{0},e_{n}\rangle\langle e_{n},\pi(z)h_{0}\rangle=H_{\rho}(z).

Proposition
4.2
therefore provides an estimate for the Husimi function’s concentration on
\Omega

(45)

\displaystyle\frac{\int_{\Omega}H_{\rho}(z)^{\frac{p}{2}}dz}{\|H_{\rho}\|_{p/2}^{p/2}}\leq\theta_{\mathcal{K}}\cdot\sup_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\int_{\Omega}\left\|\mathcal{K}(z,w)\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}dz.

Using our results we can get an estimate of the local Wehrl entropy in terms of the reproducing kernel, or even in terms of the maximum Nyquist density.
For instance, setting
p=2
yields

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}H_{\rho}(z)dz\leq\theta_{\mathcal{K}}\cdot\sup_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\int_{\Omega}\left\|\mathcal{K}(z,w)\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}dz\cdot\|\rho\|_{\mathcal{S}^{1}},

where we used Proposition
2.2
and the fact that
\|\sqrt{\rho}\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}^{2}=\|\rho\|_{\mathcal{S}^{1}}
.
Applying Corollaries
5.1
and
5.11
to the estimate above, we can explicitly write

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}H_{\rho}(z)dz\leq\sup_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\int_{\Omega}e^{-\pi\frac{|z-w|^{2}}{2}}dz\cdot\|\rho\|_{\mathcal{S}^{1}},

and

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}H_{\rho}(z)dz\leq C_{0,0}(\Delta)^{-1}\cdot\nu(\Omega,\Delta)\cdot\|\rho\|_{\mathcal{S}^{1}}.

The Husimi function therefore distributes an operator

\rho

in phase space in precisely the same way that the spectrogram with a Gaussian window distributes a function, see
[
2
]
.

For
p\geq 2
, the estimate (
45
) can be interpreted as a local bound on the ’generalized Wehrl entropy’ for
\rho
. The standard sharp bounds given in
[
25
,
26
]
are of the following form: for
\Phi:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
convex one has

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\Phi(H_{\rho}(z))dz\leq\int_{0}^{1}\Phi(s)\frac{ds}{s},\qquad\rho\succeq 0,\ \text{tr}(\rho)=1,

with the inequality being sharp.
This was first established by Lieb and Solevej
[
38
]
for rank-one operators and later extended by Frank
[
25
]
for density operators. For pure states
f\otimes f
, a sharp local estimate that extended the results from
[
46
]
was given in
[
37
, Theorem 5.3]
.
For
\Phi(s)=s^{p/2}
, (
45
) gives a new way to locally bound the Wehrl entropy which, in case that the maximum Nyquist density of
\Omega
is small, can significantly improve upon the bounds in
[
37
]
while also allowing for general density operators
\rho
.

6.2.
Concentration estimates for Cohen’s class distributions

In time-frequency analysis, particular attention is devoted to quadratic time-frequency distributions that are covariant and weakly continuous at the origin, that is to distributions
Q
that satisfy

\displaystyle Q(\pi(z_{0})f)(z)

\displaystyle=Q(f)(z-z_{0}),

\displaystyle|Q(f)(0)|

\displaystyle\leq\|f\|_{2}^{2},

see for instance
[
7
,
48
,
49
,
35
,
12
]
.
The collection of these time-frequency distributions, which is called
Cohen’s class
, is closely connected to quantum harmonic analysis. In fact, for any distribution
Q
in Cohen’s class there is a tempered operator
\eta\in\mathfrak{S}^{\prime}
such that

\displaystyle Q(f)=Q_{\eta}(f)=(f\otimes f)\star\widecheck{\eta}.

Cohen’s class has an important connection to the mixed-state localization operators. Given a function
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2d}
we have

\displaystyle\langle\chi_{\Omega}\star\eta(f),f\rangle=\int_{\Omega}Q_{\eta}(f)(z)\,dz.

So by the min-max principle, the eigenfunctions of the operator
\chi_{\Omega}\star\eta
are the stationary points of the best concentration problem
\displaystyle\sup_{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\|f\|_{2}^{-2}{\int_{\Omega}Q_{\eta}f(z)\,dz}
. In particular, when
\|\gamma\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}=1
and
\eta=\gamma\gamma^{*}
, then the eigenfunctions of the mixed-state localization operator
A_{\Omega}^{\gamma\gamma^{\ast}}
are the stationary points of the best concentration problem for the positive Cohen’s class distribution
Q_{\gamma\gamma^{*}}f
.

Let
g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\|g\|_{2}=1
, and
\eta=\gamma\gamma^{*}
. Since
(f\otimes g)(f\otimes g)^{*}=f\otimes f,
we get that by Lemma
2.3

\displaystyle Q_{\gamma\gamma^{*}}(f)(z)

\displaystyle=(f\otimes f)\star\widecheck{\gamma\gamma^{*}}=\big((f\otimes g)(f\otimes g)^{*}\big)\star\widecheck{\gamma\gamma^{*}}

(46)

\displaystyle=\|\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}(f\otimes g)(z)\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}^{2}=\|\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}f(z)\|_{2}^{2}.

We can thus use the large sieve principle to obtain concentration estimates for Cohen’s class distributions, at least in the case when
\gamma
is admissible. Note that the large sieve approach works even in the case
p\neq 2
, unlike the min-max approach. Since the operator STFT of an admissible operator defines an equivalent norm on
M^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we get the following corollary for Cohen’s class on modulation spaces.

Corollary 6.1
.

Let
p\in[1,\infty)
,
\gamma\in\mathfrak{M}^{1}
with
\|\gamma\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}=1
and let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2d}
be measurable. If
\mathcal{K}
is as in Proposition
4.1
, then for
f\in M^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\|Q_{\gamma\gamma^{*}}(f)\|_{p/2}^{p/2}}\int_{\Omega}\left(Q_{\gamma\gamma^{*}}(f)(z)\right)^{\frac{p}{2}}dz\leq\theta_{\mathcal{K}}\cdot\sup_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\int_{\Omega}\left\|\mathcal{K}(z,w)\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}dz.

Remark 6.2
.

If
\gamma=g\otimes g
with
\|g\|_{L^{2}}=1
, then
Q_{\gamma\gamma^{*}}(f)(z)=|V_{g}f(z)|^{2}
and we recover the original large sieve principle for the STFT
[
2
]
.

If we want to derive a concentration bound for Cohen’s class of admissible operators
\eta
that are not necessarily positive, we can use

\displaystyle|Q_{\eta}f(z)|

\displaystyle=\big|\big\langle\eta\pi(z)^{\ast}f,\pi(z)^{\ast}f\big\rangle\big|\leq\big\|\eta\pi(z)^{\ast}f\big\|_{2}\,\big\|\pi(z)^{\ast}f\big\|_{2}=\big\|\mathfrak{V}_{\eta^{\ast}}f(z)\big\|_{2}\,\|f\|_{2}.

to get a simpler bound which is structurally similar to a local Lieb’s inequality
[
39
]
, although with worse bounds.

Proposition 6.3
.

Let
p\in[2,\infty)
,
\eta^{\ast}\in\mathfrak{M}^{1}
with
\|\eta\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}=1
and
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2d}
measurable. For
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, it holds

\displaystyle\frac{\int_{\Omega}|Q_{\eta}f(z)|^{p}dz}{\|f\|^{2p}_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\theta_{\mathcal{K}}\cdot\sup_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\int_{\Omega}\|\mathcal{K}(z,w)\|_{\mathrm{op}}dz.

Proof.

Let us assume that
\|f\|_{2}=1
. Since
\|\mathfrak{V}_{\eta^{\ast}}f(z)\|_{2}\leq\|\eta^{\ast}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\|f\|_{2}\leq 1
, it then follows that for
p\geq 2

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|Q_{\eta}f(z)|^{p}dz

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}\|\mathfrak{V}_{\eta^{\ast}}f(z)\|_{2}^{p}dz

\displaystyle\leq\theta_{\mathcal{K}}\cdot\sup_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\int_{\Omega}\|\mathcal{K}(z,w)\|_{\mathrm{op}}dz\cdot\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\|\mathfrak{V}_{\eta^{\ast}}f(z)\|_{2}^{p}dz

\displaystyle\leq\theta_{\mathcal{K}}\cdot\sup_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\int_{\Omega}\|\mathcal{K}(z,w)\|_{\mathrm{op}}dz\cdot\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\|\mathfrak{V}_{\eta^{\ast}}f(z)\|_{2}^{2}dz

\displaystyle=\theta_{\mathcal{K}}\cdot\sup_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\int_{\Omega}\|\mathcal{K}(z,w)\|_{\mathrm{op}}dz.

Rescaling
f
then yields the stated result.
∎

An immediate consequence of Corollary
6.1
is a variant of an uncertainty principle for Cohen’s class distributions given in
[
42
, Proposition 7.7]
which can be reformulated as follows: If
\eta
is a bounded operator with
\|\eta\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 1
,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2d}
is a measurable subset and
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
\|f\|_{2}=1
, is such that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|Q_{\eta}f(z)|dz\geq(1-\varepsilon),

then

\displaystyle 1-\varepsilon\leq\|\eta\|_{\mathrm{op}}|\Omega|.

So heuristically, if a large part of the signal’s energy is concentrated on a domain
\Omega
, then that domain must also be large. While the above inequality is true for any bounded operator
\eta
, the total energy
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}|Q_{\eta}f(z)|\,dz
will in general only be finite in the case when
\eta\in\mathcal{S}^{1}
. Moreover,
Q_{\eta}f
is only positive if
\eta
is positive definite, so the above inequality really only behaves like a proper uncertainty principle in this case. In the sequel we will therefore only consider the ”physical” case when
\eta\in\mathcal{S}^{1}
, and
\eta\succeq 0.
Under these reasonable assumptions we extend the result above to
p\neq 2
and obtain an uncertainty principle that actually measures the concentration of the total energy on
\Omega.

Proposition 6.4
.

Let
p\in[1,\infty),\ \eta\in\mathcal{S}^{1}
be a positive operator with
\|\eta\|_{\mathcal{S}^{1}}=1
and
\sqrt{\eta}\in\mathfrak{M}^{1}
. Let
f\in M^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be such that
\|{Q_{\eta}f}\|_{p/2}=1
, and let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2d}
and
\varepsilon\geq 0
. If

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left(Q_{\eta}f(z)\right)^{\frac{p}{2}}dz\geq 1-\varepsilon,

then we have

\displaystyle 1-\varepsilon\leq\sup_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\int_{\Omega}\big\|\sqrt{\eta}\pi(z)^{*}\pi(w)\sqrt{\eta}\,\big\|_{\mathrm{op}}dz\leq\|\eta\|_{\mathrm{op}}|\Omega|\leq|\Omega|.

Proof.

Using Corollary
6.1
with
\gamma=\sqrt{\eta}
and
\mathcal{K}=K_{\gamma}
, we get

\displaystyle 1-\varepsilon

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}\left(Q_{\eta}f(z)\right)^{\frac{p}{2}}dz\leq\sup_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\int_{\Omega}\big\|\sqrt{\eta}\pi(z)^{*}\pi(w)\sqrt{\eta}\,\big\|_{\mathrm{op}}dz.

The result then follows immediately from
\|\sqrt{\eta}\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}=\|\eta\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\|\eta\|_{\mathcal{S}^{1}}.

∎

While the large sieve principle improves the uncertainty principle in the physical case, it cannot improve
[
42
, Proposition 7.7]
in all cases, due to the admissibility assumption.

7.
Signal recovery from operator STFT measurements

Following the ideas of Donoho and Logan
[
16
]
, the inequalities established in the preceding sections can be applied to derive signal recovery results. In our case the signals are operators, so the signal recovery task can be thought of as an infinite dimensional matrix recovery problem. Restricting our attention to positive operators with trace
1
, the task becomes a problem of quantum state reconstruction. We assume that we observe some noisy version of a signal we want to recover. Under certain structural assumptions on the noise we will derive estimates for signal recovery via minimization in the
1
-norm. This is reminiscent of theory of compressed sensing
[
24
,
17
,
10
]
. As in
[
2
]
we consider two scenarios, although we believe that several other recovery results for functions can be adapted to our setting.

First, we consider a scenario where the noise is supported on some domain
\Omega
but is allowed to have arbitrary finite
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})
-norm. Concretely, we assume that we observe the operator-valued function

(47)

\displaystyle G(z)=\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho(z)+N(z),

where
\gamma,\rho\in\mathfrak{M}^{1}
,
\|\gamma\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}=1
,
\|N\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}<\infty
, and
\mathrm{supp}(N)\subseteq\Omega
. Under certain assumptions on
\Omega
, perfect signal recovery is possible. If
N
is only approximately concentrated on
\Omega
, i.e.,
\|\chi_{\Omega^{c}}\cdot N\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}\leq\varepsilon
, then it is possible to approximately recover
\rho
. The abstract tool that we will use is the following Donoho-Stark type result for Bochner spaces.

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
B_{1}\subset L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})
be a Banach space,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2d}
and write

\delta(\Omega)=\sup_{F\in B_{1}}\frac{\int_{\Omega}\|F(z)\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}dz}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\|F(z)\|_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}dz}.

Consider
G=F+N
where
F\in B_{1}
and
N\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})
with
\|\chi_{\Omega^{c}}\cdot N\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}\leq\varepsilon
. If
\delta(\Omega)<\frac{1}{2}
and
\beta(G)
is any solution to the minimization problem

(48)

\displaystyle\argmin_{H\in B_{1}}\|H-G\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})},

then

\|F-\beta(G)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}\leq\frac{2\varepsilon}{1-2\delta(\Omega)}.

Moreover, if
\emph{supp}(N)\subseteq\Omega
, then
F
is perfectly recovered as the unique solution of (
48
).

Proof.

We will give the proof for the sake of completeness, but note that it is done exactly as in the scalar valued case
[
16
,
18
]
.

First, we assume that
F=0
. Since
\delta(\Omega)<\frac{1}{2}
we get using the reverse triangle inequality

\displaystyle\|H

\displaystyle-N\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}=\left\|\chi_{\Omega}\cdot(H-N)\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}+\left\|\chi_{\Omega^{c}}\cdot(H-N)\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}

\displaystyle\geq\left\|\chi_{\Omega}\cdot N\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}-\left\|\chi_{\Omega^{c}}\cdot N\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}-\left\|\chi_{\Omega}\cdot H\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}+\left\|\chi_{\Omega^{c}}\cdot H\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}

\displaystyle\geq\left\|N\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}-2\varepsilon+\left\|H\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}(1-2\delta(\Omega)).

If
N
is supported on
\Omega
, i.e.,
\varepsilon=0
, we immediately see that
H=0
has to be the unique solution to the minimization problem.

For
\varepsilon>0
we note that for any optimizer
\beta(G)
one trivially has
\|\beta(G)-N\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}\leq\|N\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}
and therefore, after combining this inequality with the estimate above, we arrive at

\|\beta(G)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}\leq\frac{2\varepsilon}{1-2\delta(\Omega)}.

To prove the result in general, we note that

\displaystyle\min_{H\in B_{1}}\|G-H\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}

\displaystyle=\min_{H\in B_{1}}\|F+N-H\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}=\min_{H\in B_{1}}\|N-H\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})},

and any minimizer of the problem on the left-hand side can be written as a translation by
F
of a minimizer on the right-hand side and vice versa.
∎

Since
N
is an arbitrary function in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},S^{2})
we can take, in Proposition
7.1
,
F(z)=\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho(z)
and
N(z)=-\chi_{\Omega}\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho(z)
. This results in the operator version of Logan’s
phenomenon mentioned in the introduction.

Corollary 7.2
.

Let
\gamma\in\mathfrak{M}^{1}
and
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2d}
be such that
{\Phi}_{\Omega,\gamma}^{(1)}(\rho)<\frac{1}{2}
for every
\rho\in\mathfrak{M}^{1}
. Then:

\rho=\argmin_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{M}^{1}}\left\|\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\sigma\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})},\qquad\text{subject to }\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\sigma|_{\Omega^{c}}=\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho|_{\Omega^{c}}\text{.}

In the second case we again observe a version of our signal that is corrupted by noise, but in addition parts of the observation are lost. Compared to the first recovery scenario we now assume that the (operator-valued) perturbation is small in the
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})
sense. Thus our observation is

(49)

\displaystyle G(z)=\begin{cases}\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho(z)+N(z),&z\in\Omega,\cr 0\,,&z\notin\Omega,\end{cases}

with
\gamma,\rho
and
\Omega
as before.
In general, we cannot hope to recover the signal perfectly, but the error that we make is proportional to the
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})
-norm of the noise. We rely on another generalized Donoho-Stark result.

Proposition 7.3
.

Let
B_{1}\subset L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})
be a Banach space. For
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2d}
we define
\delta(\Omega)
as before. Assume
\delta(\Omega)<1
and consider

\displaystyle G(z)=\begin{cases}(F+N)(z),&z\notin\Omega\cr 0\,,&z\in\Omega\end{cases},

where
F\in B_{1}
. If
\beta(G)
is any solution of the minimization problem

(50)

\displaystyle\argmin_{H\in B_{1}}\|\chi_{\Omega^{c}}\cdot(G-H)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})},

then

\displaystyle\|F-\beta(G)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}\leq\frac{2}{1-\delta(\Omega)}\|N\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}.

Proof.

Let
\beta(G)
be a solution to (
50
),
then

\displaystyle\|F

\displaystyle-\beta(G)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}=\|\chi_{\Omega}\cdot(F-\beta(G))\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}+\|\chi_{\Omega^{c}}\cdot(F-\beta(G))\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}

\displaystyle\leq\delta(\Omega)\|F-\beta(G)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}+\|\chi_{\Omega^{c}}\cdot(F-G)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}+\|\chi_{\Omega^{c}}\cdot(G-\beta(G))\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}

\displaystyle\leq\delta(\Omega)\|F-\beta(G)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}+2\|\chi_{\Omega^{c}}\cdot N\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}

which concludes the proof.
∎

Combining this with our large sieve estimates we get conditions for recovery from noisy operator STFTs.

Corollary 7.4
.

Let
B_{1}=\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}(\mathfrak{M}^{1})
,
G=\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho+N
where
\gamma,\rho\in\mathfrak{M}^{1}
and
N\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})
. If

\alpha(\Omega):=\theta_{\mathcal{K}}\cdot\sup_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\int_{\Omega}\|\mathcal{K}(z,w)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\,dz<\frac{1}{2},

\|\chi_{\Omega^{c}}\cdot N\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}\leq\varepsilon
, and
\beta(G)
is a solution to the
L^{1}
-minimization problem (
48
), then

{\big\|\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho-\beta(G)\big\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}\leq\frac{2\varepsilon}{1-2\alpha(\Omega)}.}

If
\alpha(\Omega)<1
, and
\beta(G)
is any solution of the
L^{1}
-minimization problem (
50
), then

\displaystyle{\|\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho-\beta(G)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}\leq\frac{2}{1-\alpha(\Omega)}\|N\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})}.}

Proof.

This is a restatement of Propositions
7.1
and
7.3
for the case
B_{1}=\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}(\mathfrak{M}^{1})
, plus an application of the operator STFT inversion formula. The large sieve principle guarantees that
\delta(\Omega)\leq\theta_{\mathcal{K}}\cdot\sup_{w}\int_{\Omega}\|\mathcal{K}(z,w)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\,dz
.
∎

Remark 7.5
.

Since
\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}^{\ast}
is bounded from
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\mathcal{S}^{2})
to
\mathfrak{M}^{1}
, it follows that Corollary
7.4
gives sufficient conditions for recovery of
\rho
, not just
\mathfrak{V}_{\gamma}\rho.

We note here that in Corollary
7.4
we replaced the condition on
\delta(\Omega)
with a condition on
\alpha(\Omega)=\theta_{\mathcal{K}}\cdot\sup_{w}\int_{\Omega}\|\mathcal{K}(z,w)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\,dz
which gives an easier to verify, but less optimal, stability estimate. We extensively discussed the question of how to further bound
\alpha(\Omega)
in
Section
5
.

As a last remark, we make a brief mention of the implications of the results to the problem of quantum state tomography, which was one of our inspirations. Our results provide theoretical guaranties for the recovery of a state represented by a density operator, from incomplete information in the phase space. The low rank condition of
[
31
]
is replaced by the measure-sparsity discussed in the introduction. But it should be stressed that, at this point, potential applications are stylized, due to the continuity of the phase space and the errors resulting from the infinite-dimensional nature of the spaces involved. Further research would be required to make a decisive statement regarding the feasibility of this approach in realistic settings.
