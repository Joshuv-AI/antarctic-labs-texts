Differential Modulation of Wheat Transcriptional Programs Driven by Native Endophytic Fungi
Authors: Sylwia Salamon, Polina Havrysh, Piotr Banachewicz, Katarzyna Mikołajczak, Lidia Błaszczyk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fungal endophytes are increasingly recognised as key modulators of plant physiology, however their molecular impact on major crops such as wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) remains underexplored. In this study, we analyzed the transcriptomic response of wheat seedlings to treatment with native endophytic fungi representing four species: Penicillium olsonii, Chrysosporium pseudomerdarium, Sarocladium spinificis, and Sarocladium strictum. Using high-resolution RNA-sequencing, we reveal that each endophyte induces a distinct transcriptional landscape in wheat seedlings grown under controlled conditions, reflecting divergent modes of host interaction. Penicillium olsonii and C. pseudomerdarium induced broad transcriptional reprogramming, characterised by repression of chromatin organisation and DNA replication pathways, coupled with selective activation of signalling and metabolic functions—suggesting a state of physiological priming or resource reallocation. Sarocladium spinificis triggered a moderate, predominantly upregulatory response involving circadian rhythm, ABA signalling, and stress-associated pathways, consistent with developmental modulation rather than immune activation. In contrast, S. strictum elicited a negligible transcriptional response, downregulating only a single pathway linked to diterpenoid biosynthesis. This silent interaction may reflect a commensal, latent, or stress-contingent role. Our findings highlight the plasticity of plant-endophyte communication and provide ecological insight into species-specific modes of interaction, with potential exploitation for sustainable agriculture. Understanding these subtle and species-specific responses offers a foundation for harnessing endogenous fungal microbiota in crop resilience strategies, particularly under climate-related stress scenarios.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00248-026-02731-4.

Background

Wheat is among the most widely cultivated crops globally, with annual production approaching 800 million tons [5].

Growing demand for cereal-based products coupled with climate change-driven increases drought, temperature extremes and other anomalies, poses a major challenge to stable wheat production [35, 44]. Moreover, wheat is adversely affected by a wide range of pests and pathogens [46]. Consequently, considerable effort has been devoted to improving crop management strategies, including approaches that exploit natural plant-microorganism interactions, particularly with fungal endophytes [29].

Endophytic fungi are microorganisms that inhabit internal plant tissues without causing visible disease symptoms in the host [30]. They encompass obligate, facultative and opportunistic lifestyles, with most plant-associated endophytes belonging to the latter two categories [16, 19, 32, 40].

The presence and diversity of endophytic fungi in wheat has been demonstrated by numerous authors [7, 22, 38, 39]. In recent years, the development of both culture-dependent and culture-independent methods has also enabled us to understand the complexity and diversity of native fungal communities associated with the wheat endosphere [8, 11, 23, 42, 43]. Inoculation studies further indicate that endophytes can influence host physiology by modulating phytohormone production, immune responses, pathogen suppression and tolerance to abiotic stress [7, 13, 34].

Plant - endophytic fungus interactions are highly context dependent and encompass a wide range of outcomes from commensal to mutualistic and, under certain conditions, pathogenic [14, 16]. The functional roles of most wheat-associated endophytes remain poorly understood. It is not know why certain fungal species are recruited by wheat plants and can colonize their endosphere, and therefore what role they play in this process. Based on numerous studies, there is increasing evidence that the functions of endophytic fungi, and consequently the types of their interactions with plants, are influenced by various abiotic and biotic factors, including environmental conditions, plant genotype, plant tissue type, fungal taxon and strain, as well as the dynamic network of interactions across the plant holobiont [41].

In this study, we investigated how native endophytic fungi, differing in taxonomic affiliation and interaction strategy, shape host transcriptional responses in wheat under controlled conditions. By conducting comparative transcriptomic analyses of wheat seedlings inoculated with four fungal species isolated from internal wheat tissues, we sought to determine whether wheat-endophyte interactions converge on common molecular pathways or elicit distinct, species-specific transcriptional programs. This approach provides insight into the ecological diversity of wheat-associated endophytes and deepens our understanding of how different fungal partners modulate host genome functionality, offering a molecular perspective on the complexity of plant-microbe interactions in the wheat holobiont.

Hence, the present study focused on the transcriptomic profiling of wheat in response to inoculation with four native endophytic fungi, namely Penicillium olsonii, Chrysosporum pseudomerdarium, Sarocladium strictum, and Sarocladium spinificis, previously isolated from internal tissues of wheat. Using RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq), we compared the gene expression profiles of wheat seedlings treated with each fungal strain to those of un-inoculated control plants. The analysis aimed to identify differentially expressed genes and enriched biological pathways associated with wheat colonisation by fungi, providing insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying endophyte-induced modulation of host genome functionality and consequently its phenotype.

Materials and Methods

Plant Material - Seedling Preparation, Growth Conditions and Contamination Control

The winter form of Triticum aestivum L., the bread variety Euforia, was selected for the study. The seed material was obtained directly from the Strzelce Plant Breeding Ltd. The Plant Breeding and Acclimatization Institute GROUP, Poland. The Euforia variety was entered into the national register in 2018 and since then has been widely used as a crop plant with a large amount of protein and gluten, ensuring high yield and winter hardiness, characterized by a high rate of resistance to most chronic wheat diseases.

The Euphoria cultivar seeds were sterilized by soaking in 70% ethyl alcohol [Avanator Performance Materials Poland S.A., Gliwice, Poland] for 30 s, and then in a sodium hypochlorite solution [ACE Classic – Procter and Gamble DS. Polska Sp. z oo, Warsaw, Poland] prepared by mixing with sterile distilled water in a ratio of 1:9, for 2 min. To remove any traces of alcohol and sodium hypochlorite, the seeds were washed with sterile distilled water. The cleaned seeds were placed in sterile conditions on 15 cm diameter glass Petri dishes lined with Whatman No. 1 cellulose paper soaked in water. After 7 days of cultivation at room temperature, healthy looking seedlings without visible signs of fungal contamination with equal lengths of roots and leaves were transplanted into experimental pots. Five seedlings were placed in each 3 × 3 × 12 cm pot filled with sterile quartz sand. Pots with seedlings were placed in sterilized, closed walk-in phytotrons with a system for regulating and maintaining temperature, relative humidity (RH%) and photoperiod at the Greater Poland Center for Advanced Technologies (Poznań, Poland). Walk-in phytotrons were used in two zones: (1) to work with plants inoculated with microorganisms; (2) to work with uninfected (control) plants. To prevent cross-contamination and limit the spread of spores via airborne pathogens, pots were placed in separate plastic containers. Additionally, plants were watered manually to avoid contamination through the use of automated irrigation and fertilization systems by flooding the growing tables. To minimize the risk of accidental airborne contamination of plants, procedures were also introduced that followed strict good laboratory practice guidelines, including the use of disposable gloves, protective clothing and footwear, and sequential handling of control and treated plants. Plants were grown in walk-in phytotrons for 7 days until inoculation under the following conditions: photoperiod 12 h light/12 h dark, light/dark temperature 25/18°C ± 1 °C and humidity 40%.

Fungal Material and Cultivation

Four species of fungi previously isolated from the wheat endosphere by Salamon et al., [42] were selected for the study, namely: (1) P. olsonii - strain E68 originated from seeds of Polish cv. Kandela; (2) C. pseudomerdarium - strain E161 originated from stem of Polish cv. Arkadia; (3) S. strictum - strain E22 originated from stem of polish cv. Rusałka; and (4) S. spinificis - strain E11 originated from roots of Polish cv. Rospuda. These species have not previously been identified as natural endophytes of wheat, and their role in wheat tissues has not yet been identified. Initial selection based on microscopic observations and molecular analysis (according to [43]) showed that selected strains of these species are capable of colonizing the rhizosphere and phyllosphere of wheat and/or recolonizing its endosphere (unpublished data). Isolates were stored in 2 ml cryogenic tubes on solid SNA (synthetic agar poor in nutrients, Nirenberg, 1976) medium at 4 °C in the collections of the Institute of Plant Genetics of the Polish Academy of Sciences in Poznań (curator: Lidia Błaszczyk). For reactivation, pure cultures of selected strains were transplanted from the initial slants onto 90 mm diameter Petri dishes on potato dextrose agar medium (PDA, Thermo Scientific™ Oxoid™, Canada). Then, the Petri dishes were kept for 7 days in an incubator Sanyo MIR-254 (SanLab, Poland), under a diurnal photoperiod of 12 h light/12 ​​h dark at 22 °C.

Plant Inoculation and Sampling

Each plant was inoculated with the selected strain by spraying the above-ground parts of the seedling with fungal spores and placing the spore-forming mycelium in the root zone. To inoculate the above-ground parts of 14-day-old wheat seedlings with each strain of the fungus, a mixture of its spores was used at a concentration of approx. 1 × 103 spores mL− 1 sterile water with the addition of Triton X-100 surfactant (Sigma Aldrich, USA) in a ratio 10:1. Spore concentration was determined using a hemocytometer. Plants were sprayed manually with a volume of 1 ml of the spore mixture. The 0.8 mm diameter agar discs containing sporulating mycelium, cut from a 7-day-old culture of each fungus, were used to inoculate the roots of 14-day-old wheat seedlings. One agar disc was placed in the root zone of each wheat seedling. The above-ground parts of control plants were treated only with a mixture of water and surfactant, and a clean agar disc was placed near the roots. After inoculation, the plants were further cultivated in a growth chamber at a day/night temperature of 25/18°C ± 1 °C with a photoperiod of 16/8 h (day/night) and 40% humidity. The experiment was set up in a completely randomized design: 1 cultivar x three pot replicates x five plants per pot replicate (biological replicate) x five treatments (water, P. olsonii E68 strain, C. pseudomerdarium E161 strain, S. strictum E22 strain and S. spinificis E11 strain). Twenty-one-day-old (7dpi) seedlings grown in phytotrons were collected, cleaned of quartz sand and washed in distilled water. The above-ground parts of the seedling were separated from the roots using a sterile scalpel. Each above-ground part was then divided into three successive sections/tissue portions: lower, middle, and apical. The same procedure was followed for each of five plants from one pot replicate (biological replicate). Approximately 20 mg of the same tissue portion was then weighed from each of the five plants from one pot replicate and placed in 2-ml Eppendorf tubes. Each Eppendorf tube therefore contained a total of approximately 100 mg of a mixture of lower, middle, or apical tissue portions from five plants from one pot replicate. The collected material was then frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored in a −80 °C freezer prior to the RNA isolation process.

Verification of Seedling Colonization by Endophytic Fungi

To confirm that the fungi successfully colonized wheat tissues under experimental conditions, a fungal re-isolation test was performed from the collected plant material. For this purpose, the seedlings (roots and above-ground parts) were cut into sections of approximately 2 cm length using a sterile scalpel. The plant fragments prepared in this way were placed in 90 mm diameter Petri dishes filled with potato dextrose agar (PDA, Oxoid Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) medium with the addition of 50 µl of ampicillin (Merck Life Science Sp.z.o.o., Darmstadt, Germany) at a concentration of 100 mg/ml. The control consisted of plants not treated with native endophytic fungi. The plates were maintained as described above in a Sanyo MIR-254 incubator (SanLab, Poland) until mycelium appeared. The observed fungal cultures were passaged several times on 90 mm diameter Petri dishes with PDA medium to obtain single cultures. The collected mycelia were stored in 1.5 ml Eppendorf tubes at − 20 °C. The identity of the re-isolated fungi was confirmed by sequencing the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region according to the protocol described by Salamon et al., [42]). The obtained sequences were compared with reference sequences (NCBI, http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/BLAST/) and with sequences previously generated for the strains [GenBank accession number: (1) P. olsonii E68 strain - MZ447463; (2) C. pseudomerdarium E161 strain - MZ447539; (3) S. strictum E22 strain - MZ447432; and (4) S. spinificis E11 strain - MW713463] used in the present study. Additionally, the presence of fungi in wheat tissues and the assessment of the degree of fungal colonization of roots and above-ground parts of plants were made based on microscopic observations using the Phillips and Hayman method [33] with modifications described by Salamon et al., [42].

RNA Isolation

Total RNA was extracted from 100 mg of leaves using the Direct-zol RNA MiniPrep kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA), which enables the isolation of high-quality RNA. The procedure was supported using Plant Isolation Aid (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and followed the protocol outlined by Smoczynska et al., [48]. Briefly, leaf tissue was pulverised in liquid nitrogen, homogenised in TRI Reagent, and supplemented with Plant RNA Isolation Aid to precipitate interfering compounds. After centrifugation (14,000 × g, 15 min, 4 °C), the supernatant was transferred without disturbing the pellet and clarified by repeated centrifugation as needed. RNA was then bound to Zymo-Spin columns after addition of ethanol, treated with on-column DNase I, washed according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and finally eluted in 50 µL of nuclease-free water. RNA integrity was assessed via electrophoresis on a 1% agarose gel (BioShop, Canada) prepared in 1xTBE buffer (BioShop, Canada), and RNA concentration was determined spectrophotometrically using a NanoDrop ND-1000 device (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

RNA Sequencing

Total RNA extracted from wheat tissues (was used to construct 15 RNA-seq independent libraries, each derived from a distinct biological sample, representing three biological replicates (above-ground parts of 5 potted plants) for each of five experimental conditions (four fungal inoculations and one control). Library preparation and sequencing were outsourced to a commercial provider (Genomed S.A., Warsaw, Poland). Sequencing was carried out on an Illumina HiSeq X platform using paired-end 150 bp reads (PE150). Libraries were uniquely indexed, pooled, and sequenced, yielding approximately 22 million read pairs per sample.

Validation

Validation of RNA-seq data was conducted using the same total RNA samples that were used for NGS library preparation. 500 ng of total RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad Sp. z o.o., Poland). The resulting cDNA was used as a template for qRT-PCR, which was performed with the SsoAdvanced Universal SYBR® Green Supermix (Bio-Rad Sp. z o.o., Poland). Primers for the regions of interest (ROIs) were designed using the PRIMER-BLAST tool [53]. During primer design, functional aspects of the target sequences were considered. Specifically, for transcripts containing introns, the forward primer was designed to partially bind an exon upstream of the intron, while the reverse primer was complementary to a downstream exon. This strategy ensured amplification of cDNA rather than potential genomic DNA contamination. A detailed list of selected target transcripts, reference genes, and primer sequences used for qRT-PCR were provided in Supplementary Material 1. Raw data from qPCR, namely, Ct (threshold cycle) values and reaction efficiency, were used to calculate the 2−ΔΔCt parameter [24] in Microsoft Excel program.

Bioinformatic Analysis

Raw sequencing reads were initially filtered using Cutadapt v3.0 [26] with a quality threshold of q = 25 and a minimum read length of m = 15. Quality control for both raw and trimmed reads was performed using FASTQC v0.11.8 [4]. Reads were then aligned to the reference genome Triticum aestivum (IWGSC RefSeq v1.0, GenBank ID: GCA_900519105.1) using HISAT2 v2.2.0 [20] with the rna-strandness RF option. Due to HISAT2 requirements, an additional length filter (≥ 20 nt) was applied before mapping. Gene-level read counts were generated with HTSeq (Anders, 2014) using strand-specific mode (-stranded=reverse), with gene annotations obtained from Ensembl Plants (release 51) via BioMart [47]. Differential gene expression analysis was performed in R v3.6.3 (R Core Team, [36]) using the DESeq2 package v1.26.0 [25]. Expression estimates were modelled with a gene-wise negative binomial distribution, and significance was assessed using Wald tests. Resulting p-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. Gene Ontology (GO) term enrichment was analysed with topGO v2.38.1 [1], based on DEGs with adjusted p < 0.05. Both the classic and elim algorithms were used for GO categories: molecular function (MF), biological process (BP), and cellular component (CC). Corresponding p-values were reported alongside GO hierarchy diagrams. KEGG identifiers were assigned utilising data from Ensemble BioMart and KEGG databases with ClusterProfiler library in R [55] The network diagram was generated in Python with Dash framework and Dash-cytoscape library [10, 18].

Results

Colonization of Wheat Tissues by Native Strains of Endophytic Fungi

Fourteen-day-old winter wheat seedlings of the Euforia variety, grown in walk-in phytotrons under controlled, sterile conditions were inoculated with native endophytic fungi. Plants were treated independently with P. olsonii E68, C. pseudomerdarium E161, S. strictum E22 and S. spinificis E11 strains. To assess the effectiveness of plant inoculation and the colonization of wheat tissues by the tested endophyte strains, their re-isolation and molecular re-identification were performed. Plants not treated with native endophytic fungi were used as a control. Re-isolation of fungi from wheat seedlings and their molecular identification showed that P. olsonii E68, C. pseudomerdarium E161, S. strictum E22 and S. spinificis E11 strains were able to colonize roots and aerial parts of wheat after spore inoculation. Initially, dominant growth of each species was observed in those cultures on PDA medium where plant fragments inoculated with that species were placed. The taxonomic identity was further confirmed by macroscopic observations of the mycelium in a monoculture of each re-isolate on agar medium. Finally, analysis of the phylogenetic marker sequence (the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region) showed that re-isolates matched the corresponding inoculated endophytes: P. olsonii, Ch. pseudomerdarium, S. spinificis, S. strictum. Additionally, the presence of fungal hyphae and spores in the inoculated plants was detected based on microscopic observations (data not shown).

Differential Expression Analysis of Wheat Transcripts in Response to Chrysosporum Pseudomerdarium Inoculation

To investigate the transcriptional response of wheat to endophytic colonisation by C. pseudomerdarium, we performed a differential gene expression analysis comparing control and inoculated plants. The results (Supplementary Material 2) are visualised in a volcano plot (Fig. 1a). A total of 74,653 transcripts were identified in the studied plants, among which 1,192 were classified as significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the wheat transcriptome (adjusted p ≤ 0.05), including 588 upregulated and 604 downregulated genes. Several transcripts were strongly upregulated following fungal inoculation, exhibiting a log₂ fold change greater than 5 and an adjusted p-value corresponding to –log₁₀(p) values exceeding 30, indicative of highly significant expression changes. Functional enrichment analysis of Gene Ontology (GO) terms in the Biological Process (BP) category identified 53 significantly enriched terms (elim method, p < 0.05) (Supplementary Material 3). Among the most prominent were nucleosome assembly (GO:0006334), microtubule-based movement (GO:0007018), and response to water deprivation (GO:0009414), which are associated with chromatin organisation, intracellular transport, and environmental stress response, respectively. Most genes linked to these processes were downregulated, signalling the repression of structural maintenance and stress resilience functions. Such downregulation may reflect a shift in cellular priorities aimed at conserving resources or modulating host physiology in response to fungal colonisation. Additionally, the oxidation-reduction process (GO:0055114) involved the highest number of DEGs (n = 87), with 55 genes downregulated, indicating potential disturbances in redox homeostasis. In contrast, the carbohydrate metabolic process (GO:0005975) included 76 genes, 46 of which were upregulated, suggesting increased energy mobilisation or metabolic reprogramming.

The Cellular Component (CC) GO enrichment analysis identified nuclear-related structures as the most significantly enriched following inoculation (Supplementary Material 4). The top-ranking terms included nucleosome (GO:0000786) and nucleus (GO:0005634), both with a high proportion of downregulated genes. This pattern points to broad suppression of chromatin-associated components. Other enriched terms, such as plasmodesma, vacuolar membrane, nucleolus, and plasma membrane, further support the idea that endophyte presence affects intracellular structure and communication. The subgraph visualisation (Supplementary Material 5) revealed that these terms are closely related and cluster around chromatin and membrane systems.

In the Molecular Function (MF) category, enriched terms included DNA binding, protein heterodimerization activity, microtubule binding, and glycoside hydrolase activities (Supplementary Material 6). Many of these terms involved large sets of significantly downregulated genes, including 129 out of 155 for DNA binding. These functions are involved in transcriptional regulation, cytoskeletal organisation, and carbohydrate processing, indicating that both structural and metabolic pathways are affected by C. pseudomerdarium colonisation.

Differential Expression Analysis of Wheat Transcripts in Response to Penicillium Olsonii Inoculation

Differential gene expression analysis identified a subset of genes significantly affected by P. olsonii colonisation in wheat leaves (Supplementary Material 7; Fig. 1b). Among the significantly differentially expressed genes (adjusted FDR < 0.05), both upregulation and strong downregulation patterns were observed, with fold changes ranging from log2(FC) approximately − 6.3 to + 4.1. A total of 11,062 genes were significantly impacted. Among these, 4124 genes were upregulated, while 6938 were downregulated, suggesting a predominant trend of gene repression following inoculation. The most strongly upregulated transcripts included TraesCS2B02G192700 with log2FC = 4.09 (fold change approximately 17.0), TraesCS6A02G255600 with log2FC = 4.04 (fold change approximately 16.4), and others such as TraesCS2D02G174200 and TraesCS6D02G236800 with log2FC above 3.7. These genes showed elevated expression in inoculated samples, with normalised counts exceeding 800 to 1300. Several genes were sharply downregulated, including TraesCS3A02G110100 with log2FC = − 6.26 (fold change approximately 0.01), TraesCS3D02G112000 with log2FC = − 4.66, and TraesCS7B02G188000 and TraesCS4B02G290500 with log2FC values between − 2.6 and − 2.7.

Functional enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes in response to P. olsonii inoculation revealed complex transcriptional reprogramming in wheat leaves. Among significantly enriched Gene Ontology (GO) Biological Process terms (elim method, p < 0.05, Supplementary Material 8), the most prominent were related to nucleosome assembly (GO:0006334), chromatin organisation (GO:0006325), and DNA packaging (GO:0006323). These categories exhibited a consistent enrichment of downregulated genes, suggesting repression of chromatin-associated functions. In addition, the subgraph representation (Supplementary Material 9) highlighted a connected cluster of chromatin-associated categories, including nucleosome assembly (GO:0006334), chromatin organisation (GO:0006325), and DNA packaging (GO:0006323). While these terms did not appear among the most statistically significant based solely on p-values, their co-occurrence and functional relatedness in the subgraph suggest a biologically relevant repression of chromatin-related functions.

Several stress- and signalling-related categories, including response to oxidative stress (GO:0006979), carbohydrate metabolic process (GO:0005975), and protein phosphorylation (GO:0006468), were also enriched, signalling metabolic activation and regulatory adjustments following endophytic colonisation.

The Cellular Component enrichment analysis revealed specific transcriptional shifts affecting subcellular structures in wheat leaves upon colonisation by P. olsonii. According to the GO term results (elim method, p < 0.05; Supplementary Material 10), the most statistically significant categories included nucleosome (GO:0000786), chromatin (GO:0000785), and extracellular region (GO:0005576), all with extremely low p-values (< 1e–20). These results point to transcriptional modulation of chromatin- and DNA-associated structures. Additional significant enrichment was observed for the cell wall (GO:0005618), plasmodesma (GO:0009506), microtubule (GO:0005874), and plasma membrane (GO:0005886), suggesting possible changes in structural interfaces and cell-to-cell communication. The integral component of the membrane (GO:0016021) was also highly enriched, involving over 2100 significant genes. Subgraph visualisation (Supplementary Material 11) highlights strong clustering among nuclear, chromatin, and membrane-related components, indicating a coordinated transcriptional response implying intracellular architecture and structural regulation.

The enrichment analysis of molecular functions revealed several significantly overrepresented terms in wheat leaves following P. olsonii colonisation (Supplementary Material 12). The most significant GO categories included microtubule motor activity (GO:0003777), hydrolase activity acting on glycosyl bonds (GO:0004553), peroxidase activity (GO:0004601), protein kinase activity (GO:0004672), ATP binding (GO:0005524), and heme binding (GO:0020037). These functions reflect diverse cellular roles, including intracellular transport, cell wall remodelling, redox regulation, and signal transduction. Microtubule motor activity and microtubule binding (GO:0008017) were both highly enriched and signalled a possible reorganisation of cytoskeletal functions, in line with observations from Biological Process categories. Enrichment of protein kinase activity and ATP binding indicates modulation of signalling pathways and energy-related regulation. Hydrolase activity, particularly those acting on glycosyl bonds, implies active carbohydrate metabolism and potential changes in the plant cell wall. The presence of peroxidase and heme-binding activities among enriched terms supports the involvement of oxidative stress response mechanisms. The subgraph representation (Supplementary Material 13) confirms clustering of motor-related, catalytic, and binding activities, highlighting functional coherence across enriched molecular terms.

Differential Transcriptome Response to Sarocladium Spinificis Exposure

A total of 600 transcripts were identified as significantly differentially expressed between the control condition and the S. spinificis treatment (Supplementary Material 14). Among these, 475 transcripts were upregulated, while 125 were downregulated based on log₂ fold change values. Representative examples of upregulated genes include TraesCS2B02G192700 with a log₂ fold change of 3.26, TraesCS6A02G255600 with a value of 2.99, and TraesCS2D02G174200 with a value of 3.01. Examples of downregulated genes include TraesCS7B02G188000 (log₂FC = − 2.28), TraesCS7D02G295400 (log₂FC = − 1.96), and TraesCS1D02G428600 (log₂FC = − 3.13). These levels of gene expression are characteristic of predominant transcriptional activation in response to S. spinificis exposure.

Functional enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes following interaction with S. spinificis revealed notable transcriptional reprogramming in wheat leaves (Supplementary Material 15). Among the significantly enriched Gene Ontology (GO) Biological Process terms (elim method, p < 0.05), the most prominent categories were circadian rhythm (GO:0007623), response to water (GO:0009415), and embryo development ending in seed dormancy (GO:0009793). These categories were predominantly enriched in upregulated genes, suggesting activation of developmental and environmental response pathways following contact with endophyte. Notably, responses to abscisic acid (GO:0009737) and negative regulation of endopeptidase activity (GO:0010951) were also significantly represented, implying possible modulation of hormonal signalling and protease regulation during colonisation.

Cellular Component enrichment analysis identified transcriptional shifts affecting structural and extracellular compartments (Supplementary Material 16). Among the most significantly enriched terms, anchored component of plasma membrane (GO:0046658), extracellular region (GO:0005576), and actin cytoskeleton (GO:0015629) were most notable. These findings point to reorganisation at the cell periphery, possibly related to signal perception, transport processes, or cytoskeletal remodelling in response to fungal presence.

In the Molecular Function category (Supplementary Material 17), significant enrichment was observed for serine-type endopeptidase inhibitor activity (GO:0004867), hydrolase activity, hydrolyzing O-glycosyl compounds (GO:0004553), and DNA binding (GO:0003677). The presence of both regulatory and enzymatic functions suggests a complex response involving protease inhibition, carbohydrate metabolism, and transcriptional regulation. The strong statistical enrichment of serine protease inhibitor activity (p = 1.7 × 10⁻⁹) may reflect protective or regulatory mechanisms triggered during plant-fungal interaction.

Differential Expression Analysis of Wheat Transcripts in Response to Sarocladium Strictum Treatment

The transcriptomic response of wheat leaves to S. strictum inoculation was minimal. A total of nine transcripts were identified as exhibiting statistically significant differential expression, with five being upregulated and four downregulated (Supplementary Material 18). The very limited number of substantial changes indicates that S. strictum triggered only a weak transcriptional response in the host under the experimental conditions used. Considering the minimal number of differentially expressed transcripts (n = 9) identified in response to S. strictum treatment, GO enrichment analysis was not conducted, as it would lack adequate statistical power and biological significance.

Validation of RNA-seq using qRT-PCR revealed a consistent expression pattern for the majority of the tested wheat transcripts after inoculation with endophytic fungi. Discussion

Despite increasing recognition of the importance of fungal endophytes in plant biology, the molecular mechanisms underlying plant response to diverse fungal partners remain poorly understood. This study addresses this gap by characterising how wheat responds at the transcriptomic level to colonisation by native fungal endophytes isolated from its own tissues. It is well known that endophytic fungi influence plant development, stress responses, and defence signalling, yet their molecular effects remain poorly defined in many crop systems [7]. It should be noted that knowledge about plant-endophyte interactions typically concerns fungal species that have been previously recognized for their beneficial effects on plants and/or that have been isolated from tissues of host plants other than those in the experimental system being studied. However, it seems crucial to understand the role of native endophytes in their host plants, the motive of their associations, and the factors that may influence native endophyte-host plant relationships. Resolving these issues is not only important for recognition, but also necessary to assess the ecological roles and potential applications of endophytes in sustainable agriculture. The research presented here should therefore be considered as an element of understanding the impact of native endophytic fungi on the host plant – common wheat – at the level of its transcriptomic response. The native strains used in the study were P. olsonii E68, C. pseudomerdarium E161, S. strictum E22 and S. spinificis E11, previously isolated from the wheat endosphere by Salamon et al., [42]. The experiments were conducted under controlled conditions with limited access to other microorganisms (seed surface sterilization, use of sterile quartz sand, phytotrons zones, pots in additional plastic boxes, and sterile handling conditions during plant growth). These conditions deviate from natural field conditions and therefore may affect the physiological state of both control plants and plants treated with endophytic fungi. However, they enable the capture of strictly bilateral interactions of wheat plants with fungal inoculant, especially by reference to control plants growing under the same abiotic conditions and with the same reservoir of seed-borne microorganisms as the treated plants. However, in order to assess the potential of the studied endophyte fungi for the management of wheat crops, it is necessary to carry out further studies comparing these interactions in natural scenarios of abiotic and biotic conditions of the agricultural environment.

Wheat - Chrysosporium Pseudomerdarium Interaction

Chrysosporium pseudomerdarium, known for its keratinolytic activity, has so far been isolated from the lung feathers of Pavo cristatus L. rodents and described by some authors as pathogenic to humans [28, 45]. Several strains of C. pseudomerdarium have been isolated from freshwater stream sediments in South Korea, soil and plant debris in Colombia, and sandy soil in a potato field in the Netherlands [12, 31]. In addition, this species has been described as a soybean endophyte that is able to produce gibberellins and promote plant growth [17]. Recently, C. pseudomerdarium was described by Salamon et al., [42] as an endophyte of the stem of the Polish winter wheat cultivar Arkadia. However, the biological function of this species and its role as a wheat endophyte have not yet been elucidated. Therefore, here we provided a detailed insight into the transcriptomic profile of wheat leaves after inoculation with C. pseudomerdarium. We identified over 1,100 differentially expressed genes, including a subset with robust induction, revealing distinct regulatory signatures. This indicates that C. pseudomerdarium elicits a specific and robust transcriptional response, distinct from background variation.Among the transcripts that were particularly strongly induced were TraesCS1B02G249000. Upregulation of TraesCS1B02G249000 described as (1,3/1,4) beta-glucanase (lichenase – 2) may be attributed to the induction of mechanisms related to the defence against fungal infection in wheat, thus focusing attention on C. pseudomerdarium as a biological agent in crop protection. It is known that beta-1,3-glucanases play a key role in plant cell division and material transfer through plasmodesmata [6]. Moreover, β−1,3-glucanases are a family of pathogenesis-related (PR) proteins involved in plant defense against fungal pathogens [52]. These enzymes can act directly on fungal pathogens by degrading β−1,3/1,6-glucans in their cell wall, thus rendering them susceptible to lysis. Recently, wheat β−1,3-glucanase has been shown to inhibit fungal hyphal growth, spore formation, and mycelial morphology of species commonly associated with the wheat kernel (i.e. Fusarium graminearum, Alternaria sp., Aspergillus glaucus, Aspergillus flavus, Aspergillus ochraceus, Aspergillus niger and Penicillium sp. Zhang et al., [56].

GO enrichment analyses further clarified the nature of wheat transcriptional response to C. pseudomerdarium inoculation. We observed a notable repression of regulatory and structural gene categories, including chromatin organisation, nucleosome assembly, and DNA binding. Simultaneously, selective activation of genes involved in carbohydrate metabolism and glycoside hydrolase activity was observed. This combination of downregulated structural functions and upregulated metabolic processes suggests a reorganisation strategy rather than classical immune activation. Such a response may reflect a shift in cellular priorities, enabling the host to accommodate fungal colonisation without triggering energy-intensive defence pathways. This physiological adjustment could facilitate a state of compatibility with the endophyte, allowing for low-cost coexistence while preserving metabolic plasticity. Importantly, although genes related to oxidation–reduction processes were mostly downregulated, their enrichment may indicate that the plant was adjusting its redox balance in a controlled way, rather than reacting with a strong stress response. This supports the idea of a subtle and non-defensive transcriptional adjustment.

KEGG pathway analysis reinforces this interpretation by highlighting the coordinated repression of core cellular processes, including DNA replication (taes03030), mismatch repair (taes03430), and chromatin remodelling (taes03082), which are typically associated with proliferative activity. Concurrently, several metabolic pathways were selectively upregulated, such as ether lipid metabolism (taes00565), taurine and hypotaurine metabolism (taes00430), and indole alkaloid biosynthesis (taes00901), pointing to enhanced metabolic flexibility (Supplementary Material 19). These findings further support the notion that C. pseudomerdarium does not provoke defence mobilisation but instead elicits a host adjustment characterised by resource reallocation and subtle physiological modulation. This shift may facilitate a compatible interaction in which the fungus coexists without triggering canonical immune responses but still induces meaningful transcriptional remodelling within the host.

The coordinated downregulation of chromatin assembly and DNA replication pathways may hint at an epigenetic mechanism by which C. pseudomerdarium modulates wheat gene expression. Recent studies support the notion that fungal symbionts can impact host transcription through changes in chromatin state and DNA methylation [15], and that phytopathogenic fungi can leverage histone modifications and chromatin remodelling to regulate host transcriptional responses [21]. In plants, such epigenetic mechanisms—including histone acetylation, DNA methylation and ncRNA-mediated modulation—are known to fine-tune gene regulation in stress and symbiotic contexts [27]. The presence of plant beneficial Epichloë coenophiala in tall fescue was shown to induce only subtle transcriptomic changes under non-stress conditions (Dinkins et al., 2017). Our results suggest that C. pseudomerdarium triggers a more extensive transcriptional reprogramming, including repression of chromatin-related processes and activation of metabolic pathways. These differences further support the view that host responses to fungal endophytes are species-specific and context-dependent, as also emphasised by Dinkins et al., (2017).

Wheat - Penicillium Olsonii Interaction

Rodolfi et al., [37] initially described P. olsonii as one of the dominant airborne fungal species. More recently, the biocontrol activity and plant growth-promoting potential of various P. olsonii strains have been documented [38, 49, 50]. In the study presented here, P. olsonii isolated from the wheat endosphere induced the most extensive transcriptional response in wheat among all endophytic fungi tested. Most of the 11,062 differentially expressed genes were declared as downregulated, including TraesCS3A02G110100 and TraesCS3D02G112000 reported to encode protodermal factor 1-like protein [2] that is associated with reactive oxygen species (ROS) homeostasis. Another transcript with a sharply downregulated expression was found to be TraesCS7B02G188000 described as the Myb-7B gene binding to the RCC1-3 A/3 promoter and increasing gene expression in response to abiotic stress in wheat [3]. The most strongly upregulated transcripts in P. olsonii included TraesCS2B02G192700. This candidate gene is described to have protein kinase activity, triggering various protective mechanisms such as antioxidant enzymes under drought stress conditions in wheat [51].

Functional analysis of the wheat transcriptome following P. olsonii colonisation revealed a widespread transcriptional reprogramming. GO and KEGG analyses indicated a dual response pattern: global repression of core metabolic and structural pathways accompanied by selective activation of regulatory and transport-related functions. Among the most prominently downregulated categories were DNA replication, chromatin organisation, and primary metabolic routes including glycolysis, amino acid biosynthesis, and secondary metabolite production. These patterns suggest a downscaling of anabolic activity and growth-associated functions. In contrast, upregulated pathways were associated with protein processing, intracellular trafficking (e.g. SNARE vesicle transport), and phosphatidylinositol-mediated signalling. The induction of these regulatory and membrane-related components points to a cellular reorganisation strategy rather than a defence-triggered response. Such a shift may reflect an adaptive, low-cost state of physiological accommodation, enabling the host to coexist with the endophyte while reallocating resources to regulatory stability and signalling preparedness.

These findings imply that wheat perceives P. olsonii not as a pathogen, but as a dynamic stimulus that necessitates structural and metabolic reorganisation. Such a response may reflect a form of controlled stress or metabolic priming, where the plant increases flexibility and readiness without activating full immune defences. The interaction thus exemplifies a non-pathogenic but influential endophyte, capable of reshaping host gene expression in ways that may benefit either partner, depending on environmental context.

Our results are consistent with those reported by [38], who demonstrated that P. olsonii ML37 reduces Fusarium Head Blight severity through local induction of plant defence responses. However, our transcriptomic data reveal a broader and more complex reprogramming of host gene expression, suggesting that P. olsonii is rather as a modulator of host physiology. The simultaneous suppression of growth-related and metabolic pathways, together with the activation of regulatory and membrane-associated signalling functions, may reflect a preparatory state of enhanced plasticity. This primed condition could enable wheat to respond more rapidly and effectively to subsequent stress, as supported by the reduced disease symptoms and toxin accumulation observed in ML37-treated plants by Rojas et al., Thus, P. olsonii appears to trigger a form of adaptive accommodation in wheat, characterised by transcriptional restraint and signalling readiness, which may underlie its biocontrol potential.

The transcriptional pattern observed in wheat upon P. olsonii inoculation — including repression of chromatin structure- and DNA replication-related pathways — further supports the notion that certain endophytes modulate host responses through epigenetic mechanisms, as previously proposed for C. pseudomerdarium and reported in other studies [15].

Wheat – Sarocladium Spinificis Interaction

Sarocladium spinificis has been described as an endophyte of the grass Spinifex littoreus from a marine coastal habitat [54]. Recently, this species was detected in the endosphere of common wheat [42]. Inoculation with one of the S. spinificis isolates obtained by Salamon et al., [42] from the internal tissues of wheat resulted in a moderate but structured transcriptional response, characterised by predominant gene upregulation rather than repression, with over 470 genes upregulated, including categories related to circadian rhythm, water response, seed dormancy, and ABA signalling. Among the transcripts whose expression was significantly increased compared to untreated plants was the TraesCS2B02G192700 discussed above in P. olsonii. It is worth mentioning that the genes involved in protein kinase activity play an important role in regulating protein activity through the phosphorylation process [51]. An example of a downregulated gene is, similarly to P. olsonii, TraesCS7B02G188000 and the TraesCS7D02G295400 — both described as the Myb-7B gene binding to the RCC1-3 A/3 promoter and increasing gene expression in response to abiotic stress in wheat [3].

Enrichment of GO terms related to the plasma membrane, actin cytoskeleton, and serine-type endopeptidase inhibitors further supports the notion of a structured, adaptive response, potentially related to stress acclimation, signal transduction, and cellular protection. The transcriptomic response of wheat to S. spinificis inoculation differed markedly from the patterns observed for P. olsonii and C. pseudomerdarium. Unlike P. olsonii, no strong suppression of primary or secondary metabolic pathways was detected, and typical immune or defence responses remained uninduced, suggesting that S. spinificis engages in a non-immunogenic, developmentally modulatory interaction.

Supporting this, KEGG analysis revealed upregulation of key metabolic pathways, such as glycolysis (taes00010), pyruvate metabolism (taes00620), amino acid biosynthesis (e.g., arginine and proline metabolism – taes00330), and peroxisome-related processes (taes04146) (Supplementary Material 19). This indicates sustained metabolic activity, contrasting with the metabolic suppression seen in P. olsonii-inoculated plants. Collectively, these patterns suggest that S. spinificis may act as a growth-compatible endophyte, possibly enhancing host flexibility to environmental fluctuations without inducing costly defence activation. Its transcriptional profile aligns more closely with developmental priming than immune stimulation, reinforcing its status as a potentially beneficial symbiont.

The transcriptional response of wheat to S. spinificis shows notable similarities to that of perennial ryegrass interacting with the mutualistic endophyte Epichloë festucae (Dupont et al., 2015). In both systems, immune-related genes were not prominently activated, suggesting a non-pathogenic, compatible association. Additionally, GO enrichment for circadian rhythm, ABA signalling, and developmental pathways (e.g. seed dormancy, embryo development) in the S. spinificis treatment mirrors the hormone-mediated developmental modulation reported in the Lolium–Epichloë interaction. However, key differences emerge in the metabolic response. While E. festucae strongly suppressed primary metabolism (photosynthesis, Calvin cycle) and upregulated secondary metabolite biosynthesis, S. spinificis induced glycolysis and pyruvate metabolism, with no major activation of secondary metabolism. This indicates that S. spinificis may exert a lighter physiological impact, avoiding strong trade-offs between growth and defence. Overall, these observations suggest that S. spinificis may function as a low-impact developmental modulator, subtly adjusting hormonal and structural pathways to enhance host environmental adaptability without imposing high metabolic cost. Its role may resemble a facultative symbiont, supporting plant plasticity under fluctuating conditions rather than providing direct defence benefits.

Wheat - Sarocladium Strictum Interaction

Sarocladium strictum is a species repeatedly described as an endophyte of Poaceae plants, including wheat [39]. This species has been documented to limit the in vitro growth of Helminthosporium solani, inhibit the growth of the nematode Meloidogyne incognita in tomato, and demonstrate biological activity against gray mold in tomato and Fusarium head blight in wheat [39]. What seems interesting about the studies presented here is that the transcriptomic response of wheat to S. strictum treatment is extremely negligible, with only nine genes showing significant differential expression. KEGG analysis identified just one significantly affected pathway—downregulation of diterpenoid biosynthesis (tae00904), a pathway associated with secondary metabolism and plant defence. This minimal transcriptional shift suggests a low-impact or passive interaction, potentially indicative of weak colonisation, limited fungal activity, or high compatibility with the host (Supplementary Material 19).

Several non-exclusive hypotheses may explain this muted response. First, S. strictum may exist in a quiescent or metabolically inert state, especially at early stages of colonisation, thereby avoiding activation of host signalling pathways. Second, the host response may be spatially localised—restricted to fungal entry sites—and therefore diluted in whole-tissue transcriptome analysis. Third, timing may play a role: key transcriptional changes could have occurred before or after the sampling window, escaping detection. Finally, it is plausible that wheat perceives S. strictum as a non-threatening or familiar component of its microbiota, leading to immune tolerance or lack of recognition. It is also possible that S. strictum becomes more active under abiotic or biotic stress, when its presence might benefit the host more clearly by enhancing tolerance or suppressing pathogens, as was the case in the literature examples cited earlier, where the fungus inhibited plant pathogens [38].

Taken together, these findings support the interpretation that S. strictum acts as a commensal or latent endophyte, exerting minimal metabolic or signalling pressure on the host. Its suppression of diterpenoid biosynthesis may represent a subtle modulation of basal defence readiness, but the overall absence of developmental, immune, or metabolic reprogramming suggests that S. strictum does not disturb host transcriptional homeostasis under the tested conditions.

Comparative Insights into Endophyte-Induced Host Responses

The results of this study demonstrate that fungal endophytes elicit markedly different transcriptional responses in wheat, depending on fungal species and the nature of the interaction. This observed diversity likely reflects not only species-specific strategies of colonisation and signalling, but also deeper differences in the evolutionary character and functional maturity of each symbiosis — ranging from long-established mutualisms to opportunistic or cryptic associations. For instance, P. olsonii and C. pseudomerdarium induced broad-scale repression of primary metabolism and transcriptional machinery, suggestive of a stress-associated or resource-reallocative state. These responses partially mirror those reported in perennial ryegrass colonised by Epichloë festucae (Dupont et al., 2015), where extensive transcriptomic changes accompanied a shift from growth to defence-related metabolism. However, while the Epichloë–ryegrass interaction reflects a long-term, mutualistic co-evolution, the wheat responses to P. olsonii and C. pseudomerdarium may be more transient, reflecting opportunistic or emergent relationships, potentially lacking the regulatory fine-tuning characteristic of co-adapted symbioses. In contrast, S. spinificis triggered targeted transcriptional activation of developmental and signalling pathways, without engagement of defence-related genes. This suggests a non-threatening, possibly beneficial interaction, consistent with the model of growth–defence decoupling proposed for certain endophytes (Bastias et al., 2021). Meanwhile, S. strictum induced virtually no transcriptional changes, possibly due to low colonisation, immune tolerance, or a latent/quiescent lifestyle, raising the possibility of a commensal or cryptic endophytic role.

The concept of a growth–defence trade-off has long been central to understanding plant responses to biotic stimuli, suggesting that investment in immune function limits growth-related processes and vice versa. However, recent studies have proposed that certain endophytes can uncouple this trade-off, promoting host growth without triggering costly defence activation. Bastias et al. (2021), for example, suggested that some symbiotic fungi—such as Epichloë species—can enhance host performance by modulating hormonal and transcriptional pathways in a way that bypasses the typical antagonism between growth and immunity. Our data partially support this model but also reveal its limitations. While S. spinificis elicited a transcriptional response consistent with stress readiness and developmental signalling—without activation of canonical defence pathways—P. olsonii and C. pseudomerdarium induced stress- or suppression-associated responses, including downregulation of primary metabolism and transcriptional machinery. These patterns suggest not mitigation of the trade-off, but rather reinforcement of resource reallocation away from growth. In the case of S. strictum, the absence of a substantial transcriptomic response may reflect a neutral or inactive interaction, that does not affect the balance between growth and defence.

Consistent with findings by Dinkins et al., [9], who demonstrated that endophyte–plant responses vary widely across genotypes and often operate through subtle “stress priming” mechanisms rather than broad transcriptional shifts, our data reinforce that wheat responses to fungal endophytes are both host- and strain-specific. In particular, the preparatory reprogramming observed in response to P. olsonii and C. pseudomerdarium mirrors the priming effects seen in tall fescue, whereas the silent interaction with S. strictum aligns with the genotype-dependent neutrality reported under non-stressed conditions.

Conclusions

The contrasting transcriptomic profiles induced by the four fungal endophytes point toward functionally diverse modes of interaction with the wheat host, each potentially serving a distinct biological role. Penicillium olsonii and C. pseudomerdarium triggered widespread transcriptional reprogramming, including suppression of core metabolic and chromatin-related pathways, suggesting a role in stress adaptation, metabolic reallocation, or physiological priming. Such a response may reflect a pre-conditioned state of heightened readiness, which could render the host more resilient to subsequent pathogenic attack.

Sarocladium spinificis, by selectively activating developmental and hormone-related processes without engaging immune pathways, may act as a modulator of growth-related signalling under non-stress conditions. In contrast, the near-complete lack of transcriptomic response to S. strictum implies a neutral or quiescent colonisation, which—despite its apparent passivity—may still confer benefit by occupying ecological niches otherwise vulnerable to pathogen invasion.

These diverse outcomes highlight that endophytic fungi influence host physiology in ways not always visible through conventional growth or defence metrics, but nonetheless meaningful. Understanding these nuanced interactions may be key to harnessing endophytes not just as growth promoters or biocontrol agents, but as regulators of plant adaptability in complex environments.
