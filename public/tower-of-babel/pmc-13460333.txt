Real-World Evidence for Oncology Patients With Rare NTRK Gene Fusions: Final Results From the German Multicenter Patient Cohort Study REALTRK
Authors: Karin Potthoff, Sebastian Lange, Thomas Seufferlein, Kathrin Heinrich, Rainer Claus, Annalen Bleckmann, Matthias Zaiss, Corinne Vannier, Sina Grebhardt, Sophie Koszinowski, Larissa E Hillebrand, Kai Ringwald, Benjamin Kasenda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPURPOSETRK inhibitors (TRKis) have transformed the therapeutic landscape for patients with neurotrophic tyrosine receptor kinase (NTRK) gene fusion–positive tumors. However, approval of TRKis is based on evidence derived mainly from small, pooled, single-arm clinical trial cohorts. The REALTRK registry aims to describe real-world molecular diagnostic practices, treatment patterns, and clinical outcomes for adult patients with NTRK fusion–positive cancers.PATIENTS AND METHODSThe REALTRK registry was a multicenter cohort study that included adults with advanced solid tumors harboring NTRK1/2/3 fusions, from Germany and Switzerland. Both retrospective and prospective data were collected from diverse clinical settings. NTRK fusions had to be diagnosed via validated assays.RESULTSOf 88 patients screened, 47 adults with advanced NTRK fusion–positive solid tumors were included in the full analysis set. Across all treatment lines after NTRK fusion diagnosis, 29 patients received TRKi therapy, eight received non-TRKi therapy, and 10 received no therapy. Lung cancer, colorectal cancer, and soft tissue sarcoma were the most common tumor types. Next-generation sequencing was the primary diagnostic method, with a median turnaround time of 2 weeks. After NTRK fusion diagnosis, TRKi therapy was immediately initiated in 26 patients, of whom 13 received TRKi as first-line treatment in the advanced/metastatic setting. About half of the patients responded to TRKi treatment as the first treatment line after NTRK fusion diagnosis (46.2%), with an overall response rate of 46.2% and a disease control rate of 73.1%. The median progression-free survival was 15.7 months, and the overall survival was 27.6 months in TRKi-treated patients.CONCLUSIONThe REALTRK registry provides important real-world insights into the patient path of adult patients with locally advanced or metastatic solid tumors harboring NTRK1/2/3 gene fusions.

INTRODUCTION

Neurotrophic tyrosine receptor kinase (NTRK) gene fusions, involving the NTRK1, NTRK2, or NTRK3 genes, are well-established oncogenic drivers across a wide range of cancers. These fusions result in the formation of constitutively active TRK fusion proteins, which promote ligand-independent activation of downstream signaling pathways, driving tumor growth and survival. While NTRK fusions are rare in most cancers, with an estimated prevalence of 0.1%-0.3% in common malignancies such as colorectal, pancreatic, lung, or breast cancer, they are found with much higher frequencies in certain rare tumor types such as congenital fibrosarcoma, salivary gland cancer, and secretory breast carcinoma.1,2

TRK inhibitors (TRKis), such as larotrectinib and entrectinib, which are first-generation TRKis with activity against TRKA, B, and C, have demonstrated remarkable efficacy in patients with NTRK fusion–positive tumors3 as they both have shown high response rates, durable clinical activity across tumor types, and favorable safety profiles, resulting in tumor-agnostic approvals in Europe.4,5 However, these approvals were based on pooled data from phase I/II trials with relatively small patient cohorts, which limits the generalizability of the findings to real-world settings.6,7 By capturing data across all lines of therapy, both before and after the molecular diagnosis of NTRK fusion–positive cancer, the REALTRK registry aims to provide a comprehensive overview of molecular testing, treatment strategies, and their effectiveness in routine clinical practice for adult patients with advanced solid tumors harboring NTRK1, NTRK2, or NTRK3 gene fusions.

Given the rarity of NTRK fusions, accurate molecular diagnosis is essential. The European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) guidelines stress the importance of reliable testing methods that confirm not only the presence of an NTRK fusion but also the retention of an intact kinase domain, which is essential for TRKi activity.8 However, real-world data on testing strategies and the implementation of testing guidelines into clinical practice are still scarce. The REALTRK registry collected data on the molecular testing used to identify NTRK fusions, the treatment decisions made after diagnosis, and the clinical outcomes achieved in routine clinical practice.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

Study Design and Cohort Definition

The REALTRK registry was an international, retro- and prospective, longitudinal, multicenter cohort study. Patients with advanced (locally advanced and/or metastatic) solid tumors harboring an NTRK1, NTRK2, or NTRK3 fusion (detected according to current ESMO guidelines8) were recruited in different types of study sites (office-based oncologists, outpatient centers, and university hospitals) in Germany and Switzerland. Complete documentation required compliance with established standards for NTRK fusion testing, like a validated assay according to the current ESMO guidelines (ie, preferably RNA-based next-generation sequencing (NGS) assay; for secretory carcinoma of the breast or salivary gland and cellular mesoblastic nephroma, fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) and real-time polymerase chain reaction were also accepted), confirmation of the (likely) intact kinase domain and NTRK as the 3′ fusion partner, and laboratory accreditation. Furthermore, availability of specific variables was mandatory, including NTRK fusion partner, type of NGS assay, sequencing platform, and test panel. If the NTRK fusion test did not meet the required standards, or if mandatory variables were missing, tumor samples could be subjected to central retesting using RNA-based NGS analysis to reliably detect NTRK gene fusions. If the NTRK gene fusion was confirmed, the patient qualified for complete documentation. Molecular pathology reports were uploaded by sites and reviewed centrally by iOMEDICO medical experts to assess eligibility. If a patient was eligible, molecular report data were documented in the database by iOMEDICO medical experts.

Patients could be included at any time point after diagnosis of the NTRK fusion, including deceased patients. This retro- and prospective approach was chosen because of the extreme rarity of NTRK fusion–positive tumors. Data were prospectively assessed for at least 12 months per patient. Participation in REALTRK was open to all patients with advanced tumors that had an NTRK gene fusion, regardless of the treatment regimen. This also included patients who had not received treatment after a diagnosis of NTRK gene fusion. Patients treated with TRKis before September 19, 2019 (Germany), or May 28, 2020 (Switzerland), were excluded from the study.

The study was approved by the responsible ethics committees (Ethics Committee at the General Medical Council of the State of Baden-Wuerttemberg, Germany, and the Ethics Committee Northwest and Central Switzerland) and is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (identifier: NCT04557813). Written informed consent was obtained from all living patients. All procedures performed comply with the current laws in Germany and Switzerland and with the Declaration of Helsinki 1964 and its later amendments.

Objectives

The objectives of the REALTRK registry were to assess patient characteristics, molecular testing, treatment, and effectiveness in adult patients with locally advanced or metastatic solid tumors with a documented NTRK gene fusion.

Statistical Analysis

The full analysis set (FAS) comprised all included patients (excluding screening failures and those with off-label use of TRKi) for whom the required NTRK testing standards were met and all mandatory variables were available. This population was relevant for all analyses but safety analyses. The safety analysis set (SAF) was based on the FAS patients who received TRKi treatment. All safety analyses were based on this population. All variables were analyzed only in a descriptive manner because of the limited sample size. Time-to-event analyses, including progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS), were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method. OS was defined as time from first treatment administration until death from any cause, and PFS was defined as time from first treatment administration until disease progression or death, whichever occurred first. Patients without events were censored at last contact. Overall response rate (ORR) was defined as the proportion of patients with complete response or partial response during treatment. Because we documented the response as reported from routine clinical care, no specific response criteria, for example, RECIST, were required. Disease control rate (DCR) was defined as the proportion of patients with CR, PR, or stable disease during treatment. Patients without response measurement were considered nonresponders. All analyses were performed using R software, version 4.3.2.

RESULTS

Patient and Disease Characteristics

Between December 2020 and December 2023, 88 patients were assessed for eligibility at 47 German and Swiss study sites (Fig 1). Data collection ended on December 31, 2024. Forty-one patients were excluded (required standards for NTRK fusion testing not met: n = 30; screening failure: n = 9; TRKi treatment not according to the summary of medical product characteristics: n = 2).

Main reasons for not meeting the required standards for NTRK fusion testing were FISH testing only (n = 13), nonaccredited pathology laboratory (n = 5), and inactive or unknown kinase activity, for example, because of NTRK being the 3' fusion partner (n = 5). Eleven tumor samples underwent RNA-based NGS retesting by a central pathology laboratory, six originally tested using FISH and five using NGS, which had first been tested in a nonaccredited laboratory. On retesting, all the FISH-tested samples and three of five NGS-tested samples failed to confirm the presence of an NTRK fusion.

A total of 47 patients were eligible for the FAS. Of these patients, 18 were excluded from the SAF (n = 29) as they had either received no therapy (n = 10) or a non-TRKi therapy (n = 8).

Two subgroups of patients from the FAS were defined. Subgroup 1 was used to assess the first therapy after NTRK fusion diagnosis and comprised all patients of the FAS (n = 47). Subgroup 2 was used to analyze the first therapy with a TRKi in any line after NTRK fusion diagnosis and comprised all patients receiving a TRKi therapy (n = 29). Of these 29 patients, nine had received treatment with entrectinib and 22 with larotrectinib; the overlap was due to two patients having been treated sequentially with entrectinib first and then larotrectinib.

Patient and disease characteristics at NTRK fusion diagnosis are shown in Table 1. The median age at NTRK fusion diagnosis was 65.2 years. Overall, 57.4% of patients were male. The Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status at diagnosis was 0 or 1 in 59.6% of patients, 2 in 12.8%, and 3 in 12.8%, with 14.9% having missing ECOG data. At NTRK fusion diagnosis, 53.2% of patients presented with metastasis. At the time of NTRK fusion testing, 59.6% of patients had received no prior treatment line, 19.1% had received one prior treatment line, and 21.3% had received two or more prior treatment lines in the advanced/metastatic setting. The most prevalent tumor types were lung cancer (21.3%), colorectal cancer (CRC) (17.0%), and soft tissue sarcoma (17.0%). TRKi-treated patients appeared to be younger (median 58.9 years) compared with non–TRKi-treated patients (66.3 years). A higher proportion of TRKi-treated patients was male (69.2% v 27.3%) and presented with metastases at NTRK fusion diagnosis (61.5% v 45.5%). Of patients who received TRKi treatment as the first therapy line after NTRK fusion diagnosis, 50% had already received prior therapies in the advanced/metastatic setting: 23.1%, 15.4%, and 11.5% had received one, two, or ≥four prior treatment lines, respectively.

Data on Molecular Testing of NTRK Fusions

NGS was used as a diagnostic method to detect the NTRK gene fusion (Table 2) on a variety of gene panels and sequencing platforms (Appendix Table A2). The median time between the diagnosis of advanced/metastatic disease and NTRK gene fusion diagnosis was 4.0 months. The median time from NTRK fusion diagnosis to the start of the first therapy was 1.1 months. RNA-based (61.7%), DNA-based (31.9%), and hybrid DNA-/RNA-based NGS test panels (34.0%) were used individually or in combination (multiple panels per patient could be used). Testing was performed almost exclusively on formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue (93.6%), and the median NGS turnaround time was 2.0 weeks. About half of the cases (46.8%) were discussed by a molecular tumor board (MTB), and in these cases, 77.3% of the recommendations by the MTB were implemented. The most common tumor type in the registry was lung cancer, and it involved fusion of all three NTRK genes (Appendix Table A3). Colorectal cancers and soft tissue sarcomas were mostly associated with fusions of NTRK1 and NTRK3. NTRK2 fusions were less frequent.

Effectiveness

About half of the patients responded to their first treatment line after NTRK fusion diagnosis (ORR = 48.6%; Table 3). The total DCR was 70.3%. The ORR was 46.2% for TRKi-treated patients and 54.5% for non–TRKi-treated patients. The DCR was 73.1% and 63.6%, respectively. The median PFS from the start of the first treatment line after NTRK fusion diagnosis was 15.7 months, 15.7 months for patients with TRKi treatment and 6.7 months for patients without TRKi treatment. The median OS from the start of the first treatment line after NTRK fusion diagnosis was 27.6 months, 27.6 months with TRKi treatment and 18.2 months without TRKi treatment, respectively (for Kaplan-Meier plots of PFS and OS, see Appendix Fig A1). DISCUSSION

The REALTRK registry offers important real-world insights into the patient path of adult patients with locally advanced or metastatic solid tumors harboring NTRK1/2/3 gene fusions. Identifying and recruiting NTRK fusion–positive patients in real-world clinical practice is a significant challenge because of the rarity of these alterations. To collect comprehensive real-world data not only from major academic hospitals but also from specialized oncology centers, a wide network of sites needs to be involved. The REALTRK registry used several innovative strategies to enhance its effectiveness and reach, such as engaging a large network of cooperating sites with regular screening inquiries, activating sites only when a potential candidate was identified, offering retesting when initial molecular diagnostics were insufficient for inclusion, streamlining administrative processes, and providing targeted support to sites. Thus, it was possible to recruit a substantial number of patients with NTRK fusion–positive tumors within 36 months of recruitment. These efforts show the importance of tailored strategies and collaborative networks in advancing real-world research for rare, molecularly defined populations.

As frequently observed in real-world studies, patients included in REALTRK appear to be older (65.2 years) than patients in clinical trials for entrectinib (57-58 years7,9) or larotrectinib (43-45 years6,10). However, larotrectinib trials often included pediatric patients. In REALTRK, 57.4% of patients were male (69.2% among TRKi-treated) and seemed to have a worse ECOG status compared with clinical trials, with 59.6% having ECOG 1 and 23% having ECOG 2/3, whereas about 45% of clinical trial patients had ECOG 0; in entrectinib trials, 9% had ECOG 2 and patients with ECOG 3 were excluded.6,7,10,11 Moreover, the REALTRK data set comprises patients receiving treatment in later lines and with more advanced disease compared with trial patients.

For NGS testing, a wide variety of panels and platforms were used with a median turnaround time of 2 weeks, which is quite fast for a real-world setting and beneficial for patients with advanced disease who require rapid therapeutic decisions.

Of note, almost half of the patients enrolled in REALTRK could not be included in the FAS, often because NTRK testing did not follow ESMO recommendations, and only FISH testing was applied in tumor entities where NTRK fusions occur at a very low frequency. FISH does not allow for determining whether a gene rearrangement results in a functional fusion protein, and it carries the risk of false-negative results if the fusion break point involves uncommon sites. Therefore, FISH testing alone is only recommended for tumor types known to harbor highly recurrent NTRK fusions.8 This highlights the complexity of NTRK fusion detection and underscores the importance of adhering to established standards to prevent treating patients who are ineligible.

In REALTRK, 26 of 47 patients (55%) received TRKi therapy after a NTRK fusion diagnosis, including 13 as first-line treatment. Meanwhile, 11 patients did not receive a TRKi during the observation period, despite having a confirmed fusion status, and 10 received no treatment at all. This aligns with other real-world data, showing that only around 50% of patients with diagnosed NTRK fusions receive TRKi treatment.12,13

Patients with NTRK fusion–positive tumors have been shown to benefit from targeted therapy, with longer survival and improved time-to-event outcomes.14,15 In our study, among patients receiving TRKi therapy as the first treatment line after NTRK fusion diagnosis, the ORR was 46.2%. This is slightly lower than ORRs reported in clinical trials for entrectinib (57%–61.2%7,9,11) or larotrectinib (66%-79%6,10,16). The lower response rate likely reflects a less selected, more comorbid real-world population with less responsive tumor types. Response rates to TRKis have been shown to vary by tumor type, with many tumor types like sarcoma, thyroid cancer, salivary gland cancer, and non–small cell lung cancer demonstrating superior responses compared with other types like CRC or neuroendocrine tumors.6,9 With 15.4%, patients with colorectal cancer were quite prominently represented in REALTRK, exceeding the proportion in pivotal clinical trials (8.3% for entrectinib and 5% for larotrectinib). This likely reflects real-world testing patterns, where patients with CRC routinely undergo comprehensive molecular testing. In addition, the absolute number of NTRK fusion–positive cases may be higher because of the high overall incidence of CRC.

Reviewing other real-world studies, treatment duration of TRKi seemed to be longer compared with that of non-TRKi in both 1L and 2L therapies.17 In REALTRK, the unadjusted median OS was 27.6 months for TRKi-treated patients versus 18.2 months for non-TRKi patients. In a propensity score–weighted real-world analysis, larotrectinib-treated patients demonstrated significantly longer OS compared with controls.18 In addition, in another data set analysis derived from the US electronic health database, NTRK fusion–positive patients receiving a standard-of-care therapy without TRKi treatment showed a median OS of only 10.2 months.19 As in our study, only descriptive analyses were performed without adjustment for confounding factors, the different treatment groups cannot be directly compared. However, the OS benefit observed across real-world studies supports the clinical value of TRKi therapy in NTRK fusion–positive cancers, highlighting the importance of timely NTRK testing and treatment initiation to optimize patient outcomes.

Early genomic profiling is critical to allow sufficient time for MTB evaluation and implementation of targeted therapy recommendations, particularly in aggressive malignancies where rapid disease progression and clinical deterioration may preclude the use of targeted agents if testing is delayed.20,21 Real-world data demonstrate that testing frequently occurs later in the treatment course after disease progression, potentially missing the optimal therapeutic window.13,17 Half of the REALTRK patients were discussed in a tumor board, followed by a high implementation rate of 77.3% for board-driven treatment recommendations, highlighting the importance of multidisciplinary review in patients with rare genomic alterations.22

Several limitations should be noted for this study. As data were collected in routine clinical practice, bias in patient selection, treatment, and reporting is expected. Irregular response assessments in routine practice may limit the accuracy and comparability of treatment response data. Furthermore, a considerable proportion of patients (approximately 30%) were still receiving TRKi therapy at the end of study observation, resulting in a relatively short follow-up for these patients. This limited follow-up is reflected in the relatively low event rates for OS (43.2%) and PFS (64.9%), with more than half of OS events and approximately one third of PFS events being censored at data cutoff. Notably, identifying eligible patients was challenging and required considerable effort. Easier recruitment of deceased patients might have biased the cohort toward less fit individuals, affecting survival outcomes. Retrospectively enrolled patients also had more missing data because of reliance on existing documentation. The rarity of NTRK fusions necessitated pooling patients with heterogeneous characteristics, including different tumor types and TRKi treatment across various therapy lines. Although this approach aligns with other NTRK studies, it generally restricts subgroup comparability and limits comparison with study populations. Entity-specific effectiveness conclusions are therefore not possible. The sample size of 47, while sizable, given the rarity of NTRK fusions, restricts the generalizability of the results and may not capture all patient experiences and outcomes.

In conclusion, given the rarity of NTRK fusion–positive cancers and the resulting limited prospective clinical trial data, real-world evidence from systematic registries is essential to comprehensively characterize treatment patterns and clinical outcomes in these patients. While most real-world data on NTRK fusion–positive cancers derive from retrospective US chart reviews, systematic data collection and comprehensive follow-up in REALTRK represent a key methodological distinction, which may enhance data completeness and quality compared with data extracted from electronic health records. To our knowledge, REALTRK represents the largest real-world cohort of NTRK fusion–positive patients reported to date, who were enrolled both pro- and retrospectively, providing detailed information on NTRK testing methods, treatment strategies after NTRK gene fusion diagnosis, and clinical outcomes. Our data demonstrate that patients with NTRK fusions treated with TRKi therapy achieved good response and disease control rates, suggesting clinical benefit in real-world populations. Continued integration of real-world data from registries like REALTRK with clinical trial evidence will be essential to complement the limited prospective trial data available for this rare patient population.
