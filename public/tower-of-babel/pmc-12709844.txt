Yield performance and stability of durum wheat varieties in northwestern Ethiopia
Authors: Ahadu Menzir, Yalemtesfa Firew, Mulatu Kassaye, Gebremeskel Mequanint
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA key challenge for plant breeders is to achieve high durum wheat yield in Ethiopia across various locations while minimizing environmental influences. Fourteen varieties were tested using atriplicate RCBD design.The experiment was conducted over two consecutive main cropping seasons (2020–2021) at three different locations: Felege Birhan, Sebshengo, and Felege Selam Farmers Training Center, consisting a total of six test environments.The study aimed to evaluate the yield performance and stability of durum wheat varieties using various stability models in northwestern Ethiopia to provide advanced information to breeders and the local farming community. Data was collected for fifteen key traits and analyzed using modern statistical approaches. The combined analysis of variance exhibited notable variances (p < 0.001) in traits including grain yield, biomass yield, and hectoliter weight due to genotype, environment, and their interaction.The AMMI biplot analysis indicated that the first two interaction principal component axes, IPC1 and IPC2, together explaining a total of 80.83% of the GEI variance. AMMI efficiently distingushes genotypes G10, G14, G13 that display low IPCA scores positioned near the origin, indicating high yielding and stable across all studied environments.GGE displayed six test environments were grouped into two mega environments where E4, followed by E1 and E3, is the most ideal for both discriminating and representing the grain yield performance of durum wheat genotypes. Genotypes G1, G9, G12 and G11 were the vertex genotypes and G1 and G11 were most strongly associated with specific environments. Genotype G10, G5, G2 and G12 demonstrated high yield performance and stability across environments. In the study, Mean vs stability plot showed genotypes G7, G4, G8, G2, G10 are located on the right side and close to ordinary line had excellent yield performance and wonderful stability.The Multi-trait stability analysis result exhibited genotypes G1, G8, G12, G5 had stable and superior yield performance across diverse test environments by integrating multiple agronomic and stability traits.Using all stability evaluation methods genotype G10 was high yield performance and stable across test environments. G1, G8 were selected as stable for considering multiple triats to a specific environment. A significant positive relationship was observed between grain yield ton/ha with important traits, this shows that these traits indirectly contribute significantly to increasing the grain yield of durum wheat.The selected genotype (G10) can serve as a reference for future durum wheat improvement studies and cultivar selection for wide adaptation.The findings provide valuable information to farmers in choosing stable and high-yielding varieties. This contributes to ensuring food security in Ethiopia by developing climate-resilient, stress-tolerant, and superior genotypes, which should be further assessed in on-farm trials.Keywords: Stability, Durum wheat, Variety, Environment, Grain yield

Introduction

Durum wheat (Triticum turgidum L. var. durum) is a tetraploid species (2n = 4x = 28) and a monocotyledon crop belonging to the Poaceae family [19], it possesses an AABB genomic makeup [17]. Durum wheat is commonly cultivated in black clay vertisols under rain-fed conditions and is best suited for regions characterized by hot days and cool nights [51]. It is typically sown late in the growing season to minimize early waterlogging damage, and it also is planted during the dry season by utilizing residual moisture at altitudes between 1800–2800 m.a.s.l. [16]. Durum wheat possesses distinctive characteristics, including hardness, a yellow pigment, and high protein content, making it preferred for the pasta and Macroni manufacturing industries [69]. In addition to its industrial demand in Ethiopia, durum wheat is particularly well-suited for traditional dishes such as biscuits and cookies, kinche (cracked and cooked grain), flat braead (dabo), injera, nifro (boiled whole grain), genfo (porridge), kolo (roasted grain), and dabokolo (ground and seasoned dough that is traditional cookies) (Tidiane et al., 2019) [53, 73].

Durum wheat is a major grain crop and ranks as the second top cultivated wheat species worldwide, succeeding bread wheat [4, 85]. According to Xynias et al. [77], In 2019 nearly seventeen million hectares were covered by durum wheat, its total production was 38.1 million tons globally. There are 2 million hectares of land in East Africa that can grow durum wheat, but unfortunately we only use 630,000 hectares of it [73, 77]. In Ethiopia, durum wheat is the most significant cereal crop and is thought to have its center of diversity in the highlands areas, sometimes called the 'wheat belt' [54]. Durum wheat growers in this region have developed a wide variety of genetically diverse durum wheat landraces suitable for various favorable environments, utilizing local germplasm and their indigenous knowledge [30, 37].

Data on durum wheat production and productivity in Ethiopia is currently inaccurate, as it is often conflated with that of bread wheat. The Central Statistical Agency (CSA) [18], reported that durum wheat cultivation in Ethiopia occupies approximately 24% of the country’s wheat potenitial arable land, with a production rate of 2.74tha−1 ( Tegenu and Nepir, 2019) [87]. In total, 1,897,405 hectares were planted with bread and durum wheat, its average productivity was recorded at 3.046tha−1 [76]. In the Amhara region, the total area cultivated with both bread and durum wheat is 641,170.34 ha with an average productivity of 2.831tha−1. Detecting stable and high-yielding genotypes can encourage the sustainable production and productivity worldwide [10].

Durum wheat cultivates well in the diverse agroecological circumstances of Ethiopia, from lowland to highland environments, which offer favorable conditions for durum wheat cultivation. Durum wheat production at the national level was approximately 2.7474tha−1 tonnes per hectare. This shows that it is significantly less than the production potential of 8 to 10tha−1( Tegenu and Nepir, 2019) [87]. Currently, the state-owned pasta industries have been privatized and are now operating alongside numerous new entrants in the Ethiopian Millers Association. Consequently, pasta producing industries primarily depened on substantial imports of durum wheat grain, a strategy that is unsustainable in the long term due to fluctuating prices and competition for limited foreign currency reserves with other national prioritized issues. In 2019, Ethiopia imported €24,433,488 worth of pasta and €129,113,334 worth of grain [73]. The cultivation of durum wheat in Ethiopia has progressively declined due to the advent of improved and genetically stable modern bread wheat varieties developed by national and international breeding initiatives. This decline is attributed to the use of inherently low-yielding farmer varieties, concerns regarding seed quality, and both biotic and abiotic factors that have confined durum wheat cultivation to marginal areas, despite the rising demand for durum wheat grains in domestic and international markets explained by [47]. Among the two primary wheat species (durum wheat and bread wheat), growers prioritize durum wheat in its different important characteristics. They value durum wheat primarily for its resistance to biotic and abiotic stress, superior food excellence, enhanced weed competition, and increased straw production [54].

Durum wheat production also hindered present challenges, such as variable rainfall distribution, biotic stress, and soil heterogeneity [8]. Multi-environment trials (MET) and the evaluation of multiple traits are vital for estimating superior genotypes intermes of yield performance and stability. This evaluation ensures that the selected durum wheat genotypes perform suitably across diverse environments within the target region, meeting the varied demands of farmers, producers, processors, and consumers in Ethiopia's diverse agro-ecological settings. Ethiopian durum wheat is highly influenced by the combined interaction of genotype, environmental factors, and management practices. Different genotypes respond variabiliy to different environmental conditions, meaning that the best performance of genotypes in one environment may not be an indicative of performance in another, this is the reason why genotype by environment interaction (GEI) complicates to select the finest genotypes. In Ethiopia, where agroecological factors suchas temperature, altitude, rainfall, soil fertility, and disease incidence vary widely, Plant Breeders have excerted considerable effort in evaluating the combined effects of genotype, environment, and their interactions effect of (G × E) to develop superior varieties that yield high outputs and ensure yield stability. This is particularly important in many producing countries, including Ethiopia, where environmental variations are extensive and unpredictable, presenting a significant challenge [44, 64]. Evaluating the stability and genotype-by-environment interaction (GEI) of selected released durum wheat varieties is crucial to ensure their consistent performance across different growing conditions in northwestern Ethiopia. Farmers in this region need varieties that yield reliably across seasons. Stability evaluation helps identify varieties that perform well despite climate variability or soil differences. Although the tested varieties were released over decades, there have been alitle efforts at the study location to evaluate their stability and conduct GEI analysis to choose the best-performing and stable varieties for the specific sites.

Several statistical models are currently available for assessing the stability of genotypes tested across various environments, such as Additive Main Effects and Multiplicative Interaction (AMMI) model [88] and the genotype plus G × E interaction (GGE) biplot [81, 83]. By partitioning the genotype × environment interaction sum of squares into interaction principal component (IPCA) axes, AMMI facilitates an understanding of the extent of genotype-environment interactions (GEI) through one or more IPCAs. This model is particularly used for identifying those that exhibit stability in different environments, making it convenient for initial screenings in multi-environment trials (METs). However the biplot does not effectively provide a clear visual depiction of the relationship between genotypes and environments. In contrast, GGE biplot analysis helps breeders in selecting the best genotypes for particular circumstances by graphically displaying how the performance of genotypes perform in various environments. Additionally, GGE biplots simplify the identification of those that share similar environmental features. Beyond the "which won where" pattern, GGE also clearly distinguishes stable and ideal genotypes, as well as the representative and discriminatory capacity of environments [45, 84]. It also separates total test environments into mega-environments, helping to identify winning genotypes across environments as well as in specific environments.GGE despite their benefits, advantages, biplots may overlook important relationships because they focus too much on the first two principal components. By including all main principal components, this approach offers a potent way to assess average performance and stability, making it useful for analyzing diverse range datasets.

BLUP-based approaches, like Weighted Average Scores based on BLUPs (WAASB), provide exact valuations of genotypic performance [57]. The Multi-Trait Stability Index (MTSI) is also an important statistical tool that improves genotype selection by integrating multiple agronomic and stability traits, assisting researchers pick genotypes that achieve outstanding yield and stability. MTSI is a model used to identify the best durum wheat genotypes for grain yield and quality under variable conditions, taking into account the multiple traits rather than a single trait yield alon to estimate stability ​​and yield performance. However, its effectiveness can depend on the weighting of traits. The Multi-Trait Genotype Ideotype Distance Index (MGIDI) refines genotype selection by considering various tested traits to ensure long-term improvements in key characteristics [56]. Using the models AMMI biplot, GGE biplot, MTSI methods several researchers were identifying stable and high-yielding crop genotypes Sokolović et al. [70] in faba bean, Yue et al.[86] in maize hybirds, Kuru et al. [42] in finger millet. Much of the previous work done so far has focused on genotypic variability and diversity of durum wheat [48](Mengistu et al., 2018). Some work has been done on identifying stable and high yielding durum wheat genotypes using some AMMI stability methods to ensure food security in Ethiopia [43, 52, 54]. However,there are limitations in identifying stable and high-yielding durum wheat genotypes using all AMMI and AMMI-driven stability parameters, BLUP-based approaches and MTSI methods, which is why it was initiated to conduct this research. In conclusion, combining different statistical models creates a robust framework for analyzing the variability of durum wheat yields across different environments. The statistical methods AMMI and GGE biplots contribute to enhance the understanding of GEI and mega-environments, whereas BLUP enhances the accuracy of stability analysis. To improve breeding success, it is crucial to evaluate the stability of durum wheat genotypes that can adapt well to diverse environments. The present investigations aimed to estimate durum wheat varieties with high-yielding and more stable across test environments by using multitrait stability index parameters (MTSI), BLUP-based approaches, AMMI, and GGE biplot models.

Experimental design, procedures and trial management

The field trial was conducted using a randomized complete block design (RCBD) with three replications at Felege Birhan, Sebshengo, and Felege Selem over two consecutive years (2020 and 2021), encompassing six environments. Each location by year combination was considered as a separate environment. The selected locations are recognized as potential sites for regional durum wheat production in the East Gojjam Zone of northwestern Ethiopia, well-representing mid and highland agro-ecologies with substantial durum wheat production. Plot size consisted of six rows, each measuring (1.2 m wide and 2.5 m long), with row spacing 0.2 m, resulting in a net experimental plot area of (0.8 m × 2.5 m = 2m2). The recommended seed rate and fertilizer rate for the site was used. Planting was carried out manually by drilling in August 2020 and 2021. Data were measeured from the four middle rows of every plot, leaving border rows. All other agronomic management practices were undertaken uniformly across the whole plot according to the recommendion of a wheat production package for the site during the croping season throughout the experiment.

Data collected

The data were collected from each experimental unit on both a plant and plot basis.

Data were collected on plot basis

Days to 50% heading (DH): was recorded as the number of days taken from the date of sowing to the date of 50% heading of the plants from each plot by visual observation.

Days to 90% physiological maturity (DM): was determined as the days to 50% heading (DH) plus the date when 90% of the panicles reached physiological maturity that turned to yellow straw color.

Thousand seed weight (TSW): It was measured by weighing 1000-seeds sampled from the net plot using a sensitive balance and the seed was adjusted to 12.5% moisture content.

Aboveground dry biomass (BY): This trait was recorded from plants harvested from the net plot area after sun drying to a constant weight and converted to tha−1.

Grain yield (GY): Grain yield was detremined by harvesting and threshing the grain yield from net plot area. The grain yield was adjusted to 12.5% moisture content.Finally, yield per plot was converted to tha.−1

Harvest index (HI): It was computed as ratio of grain yield per plot to total aboveground dry biomass yield per plot expressed as percent.

Protein content (PC) was estimated by the Kjeldahl digestion approach, which entails digesting the sample in concentrated sulfuric acid [12]. 0.25g of samples were weighted in the digestion tube containing concentrated sulphuric acid and copper tablet catalyst and digested in a block digester at 375°c for 3h. The digested sample was distilled in bunchi 339 distillation units to liberate ammonia using alkali (40% NaOH). The liberated ammonia was trapped in a weak boric acid solution and titrated with a standardized 0.1N sulphuric acid. Then the amount of nitrogen and crude protein were calculated as below.

Hectoliter weight (HLW) is a simple measure of wheat quality that represents the weight of 100 L of wheat. To measure it, a clean, representative sample of wheat seeds (usually 500 g to 1 kg) is poured into a standardized 1-L container, ensuring no air gaps. The filled container is weighed, and after subtracting the container's weight, the net weight of the wheat is multiplied by 100 to give the hectoliter weight in kilograms per hectoliter (kg/hl). This measure is widely used to assess wheat quality and flour yield potential.

Data were measured on plant bases

Plant height (PH): Plant height was recorded from the base to the tip of the spike (excluding awns) of 10 randomly tagged plants from the net plot area at physiological maturity.

Spike length (SL): It was determined from the bottom of the spike to the tip of the spike excluding the awns from 10 randomly tagged spikes from the net plot.

Number of total tillers per plant(NT): Number of total tillers per plant was measured from ten randomly pre-tagged plants per net plot at full tillering stage by counting the number of tillers and then take an average.

Number of productive tillers per plant (ET): it was measured at maturity by counting all spikes bearing kernels from ten randomly pre-tagged plants per net plot at full tillering stage and then calculating an average.

Number of spikelets per spike (NSPS): Ten main tiller plants were taken randomly from the net plot area of each plot and were counted the number of spikelets per spike carefully and then the mean was determined.

Number of kernels per spike (NKPS): Ten main tiller plants were taken randomly from the net plot area of each plot and were counted the number of kernels per spike carefully and then the mean was determined.

Number of kernels per spike (NSPK): Ten kernels per plant were taken randomly from10 plants in net plot area of each plot, thresh it and then counted the number of seeds per kernels carefully and then the mean was determined. All plant based measured traits were measured in plant-basis from 10 randomly chosen plants, excluding border plants, and their mean average values were utilized for statistical analysis.

Data analysis

Analysis of variance

The PROC GLM (General Linear Mixed Model) procedure in SAS was used to perform analysis of variance (ANOVA) for all measured data at each location and their combinations. Homogeneity of error variance was primarily tested using Hartley's F-max test [38] to validate each environmental analysis. After confirming homogeneity of error variance, a combined ANOVA was conducted for the randomized complete block design (RCBD) to evaluate genotype variation across environments. Mean comparisons were carried out using Fisher's Least Significant Difference (LSD) test at a 5% significance level, following the methodology outlined by Gomez and Gomez [35].

Results

Combined ANOVA and Additive Main Effects and Multiplicative Interaction (AMMI) analysis

Prior to conducting the combined ANOVA and AMMI analyses, the homogeneity of error variances was confirmed using Bartlett’s test (p > 0.05). Pooled ANOVA revealed highly significant differences (p < 0.001) among environments, genotypes, and genotype-by-environment interactions (GEI) for traits including days to heading (DH), plant height (PH), spike length (SL), number of seeds per kernel (NSPK), number of seeds per spike (NSPS), grain yield (GY), biomass yield (BY), and hectoliter weight (HLW). All studied traits exhibited highly significant differences (p < 0.001) among genotypes Table 3. Various environments influenced most of the studied durum wheat traits, while traits such as days to maturity (DM), tiller number (TT), number of kernels per spike (NKPS), and harvest index (HI) were not significantly affected.The current result indicated that the variation between replicates for most studied traits were non-significant, it does not significantly affect the overall results or conclusions of the experiments. Mean square values from the combined Additive Main Effects and Multiplicative Interaction (AMMI) analysis revealed significant differences (p ≤ 0.001), indicating substantial distinction due to environments, genotypes, and GEI for grain yield as detailed in Table 4. The environment accounted for 40.53% of the total variance in grain yield (GY), while genotype contributed the largest share to the overall variability (G + E + GEI) for grain yield. Genotype, GEI explained 45.71% and 43.78% of the variance, respectively. Cumulatively, IPCA1 and IPCA2 contributed for 80.85% of the variation in GY. The first three IPCAs were statistically significant for grain yield, collectively explaining 94.14% of the variance. IPCA4 (4.15%), and IPCA5 (1.71%) results showed that there is no statistically difference, indicating these components may not contribute to the detection of genotypic stability or adaptation to the environment. According to the GEI analysis, the variation of GEI is accounted for by IPCA1 (63.5%), IPCA2 (13.35%), IPCA3 (13.31%), IPCA4 (4.15%) and IPCA5 (1.71%), these five IPCAs contribute to a total of 100% of GEI variation respectively.

Discussion

A lot of previous work was mainly concentrated on the diversity and variability of traits among durum wheat genotypes in Ethiopia [9, 20, 48]. However, efforts to develop a stable and high-yielding durum wheat variety are very limited (Mulugeta et al. 2022) [52, 73]. One gab of the previous scholars regarding this issue were did not use all the AMMI stability methods to select stable and high yielding varieties and the other big gab was no one do not use the MTSI method which is used to select stable and high yielding varieties based on all the studied characteristics of durum wheat genotype. What makes the present study offers novel contribution to identifying stable and out performing varieties in Ethiopia using all AMMI methods and AMMI-driven stability methods, BLUP-based aproches and including MTSI, to select a stable and high-yielding variety.Evaluating genotype stability and adaptability to create sustainable agronomic systems by developing resilient varieties that ensure long-term productivity that are capable of maintaining stable yield under variable environmental conditions, and resilient to other factors is vital tasks for plant breeders [60]. Optimizing breeding efficiency by minimizing resource inputs while selecting environmentally stable and adapted genotypes investigators are focusing on this approach. Identifying durum wheat genotypes that perform well and are stable across diverse environmental conditions requires the use of multi environment trials (METs).The present study examined 14 durum genotypes across 15 agronomic traits in northwestern Ethiopia at Sebshengo, Felege Selam and Felege Birhan in two consecutive years 2020 and 2021.In the context of promising candidates may be adapted directly as varieties following successful performance in substantiation trials and can be used as a reference for developing a superior hybrid variety. The current findings highlight the importance of genotype × environment interaction (GEI) and the effectiveness of advanced stability approaches in breeding programs.

Pooled ANOVA revealed highly significant differences (p < 0.001) among environments, genotypes, and genotype-by-environment interactions (GEI) for most traits Table 3.This highlights the importance of both genetic and environmental factors, along with their interaction, in determining yield performance. The combined AMMI ANOVA indicated large genotypic variation as well as GEI for key traits, suggesting the genotypes responded differently to the environments under test Table 4. Further partitioning of the GEI into Interaction Principal Component Axes (IPCA) revealed that IPCA1 through IPCA3 were significant (p < 0.001) for important traits. Whereas IPCA 4 and IPCA 5 did not show significant difference, indicating that may not important contribution to durum wheat yield variation.The current results align with the result of Mekonnen et al. [47], Dinsa and Balcha [24], Mulugeta et al. [52], which conveyed that the pooled analysis of variance showed highly significant variations (P < 0.01) for environments (E), genotypes (G), and their interaction between environments and genotypes (GEI), and the first three principal component axes (IPCA1, IPCA2, and IPCA3) were significant at (p < 0.001) contribute more for the total variation of durum wheat grain yield. Letta [44] reported that split the variance components and it was found that both expected (location) and unexpected (year) environmental variables played a significant influence in the variation.

The present finding in Fig. 2 illustrates that the AMMI2 biplot, which integrates the first principal component axes IPC1 and IPC2 comulatively explains 80.83% of the GEI variance, effectively demonstrates the relationships between genotypes and environments with varying agro-ecological conditions. The highest grain yields observed from genotypes G10, G13, G14, G8, G5, display low IPCA scores, identified as the stable across studied environments. Genotypes were ranked based on their mean performance and Genotype Selection Index (GSI). To further measure yield performance and stability, the Yield Stability Index (YSI), which assimilates mean yield with AMMI-derived stability values, was utilized Table 5. Based on YSI rankings, genotypes G7 (D.2018, YSI = 3), G4 (Alamata, YSI = 8) and G10 (Utuba, YSI = 9) were high performing and stable candidates, while genotypes G9 (Asasa, YSI = 19), G12 (Tsefaye, YSI = 23) and G6 (Foka, YSI = 23) showed poor stability and low yield Table 5.Similarly, several scholars [29, 50, 52, 72] identified genotypes with high-yielding performance and consistent stability across test environments using AMMI biplot statistical approaches.

A robust statistical approach beyond conventional ANOVA is essential for effectively characterizing genotype-by-environment interaction (GEI) and selecting stable, environment-insensitive durum wheat genotypes (Mullualem et al., 2024) [80, 90]. A comprehensive evaluation of genotype stability can be employed using AMMI-derived stability catalogs, including the non-parametric stability statistic index (NP-SSI), parametric stability statistic index (P-SSI), and composite stability statistic index (C-SSI) [3, 7, 28] and [41]. These indices complement conventional AMMI approaches like ASV by addressing distinct analytical objectives: NP-SSI evaluates rank-based stability without distributional assumptions, P-SSI quantifies parametric stability across environments, and C-SSI integrates multi-dimensional stability metrics. To compare and assess their effectiveness in evaluating genotype stability, the stability parameters ASI, ASV, ASTAB, AVAMGE, MASI, MASV, and SIPC were figured out in Table 5. For any stability parameter, a genotype becomes more stable as the score decreases; it becomes less stable as the score increases. Among the study parameters, ASTAB, ASV, AVAMGE, MASI, MASV and WAAS displayed consistent stability rankings, highlighting consistent stable ranking for genotypes G7, G10, G4 and G3 as the most stable across environments. Overall, the analysis confirmed that the genotypes with the highest mean yields were also consistently classified as stable according to all AMMI-based stability indices evaluated, underscoring their suitability for selection in multi-environment trials. In rice research, Kesh et al. [41] selected stable and high-performing genotypes using the same stability approaches. Consistent with the current finding, Kuru et al. [42] noted that these stability indices were important for testing finger millet yield stability and performance.

The GGE (Genotype + Genotype × Environment) biplot is a graphical analysis tool enables for visualizing and interpreting multi-environment trial (MET) data, facilitates the identification of ‘which-won-where’ relationships, and aids in delineating mega-environments [2, , 15, 23, 31, 32, 68, 74, 80, 82, ]. It also helps to identify the genotypes, which are stable across environments and specific environments. The GGE biplot analysis was carried out to estimate the extent of genotypic performance and specific adaptation to the examined environments[10] (Al-Sayaydeh et al., 2023). Based on the fact, the present result indicated six tested environments were divided into two separate mega environments: group 1 included E1, E2, E3, and E4, while group 2 comprise E5 and E6 Fig. 3. The ‘which-won-where’ biplot displayed that genotypes G3, G4, G6, G8 and G10 clusterd close to the biplot origin exhibited a high degree of stability across the test environment. Among them, G10, G3, and G4 were closest to the center, highlighting their superior consistency and broad adaptability. Genotype G1, G9, G12 and G11 were the vertex genotypes situated at the corner of the polygon, indicating that these genotypes are stable in at least one specific environment.

Based on the angle between vectors, scaling with an environment focus of the GGE biplot uses the Average Environment Axis (AEA), Average Environment Coordinate (AEC), and concentric circles to determine the representativeness and discriminatory power of study environments [26, 84]. An ideal test environment exhibits good representativeness and a higher discriminatory capacity of the target environments, characterized by longer vectors and smaller angles, which facilitate better genotype variation. Conversely, shorter vectors indicate limited discriminatory ability. As shown in Fig. 4, environments E5 and E6 had high discriminating capacity and low representation. The reverse is true for environments E2, E3, E4 and E1 for evaluating the grain yield performance and stability of durum wheat genotypes.

Based on discriminativeness vs. representativeness view Fig. 4showed that genotypes G10, G14, G13, and G12 were identified as the most desirable due to their high yield and stability across environments, demonstrating that those genotypes could be suitable for broad adaptation. Conversely, G11, G2, G7, and G4 exhibited high performance only in specific environments but lacked overall stability. This indicates the biplot effectively integrates both environmental differentiation and representativeness, providing a comprehensive framework for selecting both ideal genotypes and optimal testing environments. Previous research has indicated that the length of a location vector and positioned near the arrowhead of the AEC can be used to estimate the high discriminatory capacity of the environment [40]. The current finding in agreement with the reports of Kaya [39] in durum wheat genotypes, Engida et al. [25] in maize, Oladosu et al. [55] in rice genotypes to identify the best and poorest environment for selecting genotypes adapted to the across the whole environment based on discriminative and representative ability. The mean vs stability plot result in Fig. 5 showed that genotype G7, G4, G8, G2, G10 are located the right side and close to ordinate line (AEC) were the best varieties with excellent yield performance and wonderful stability, suggesting ideal genotype can be used as a reference selecting genotypes which were stable and high yielding for wide adaptation. Similar results reported to choice maize hybrids for mean performance and stability of multiple traits [59]. This indicates using this genotype stability ranking method is important for selecting genotypes, which is stable and high yielding.

Best Linear Unbiased Prediction (BLUP) for 15 studied traits across six environments is summarized in Table 6 indicatd that highest heritability was recorded for grain yield, followed by plant height and hectoliter weight as compared to other traits Table 6. This advocates that the studied traits are highly influenced by environmental issues and are less controlled by genetic effects. In contrast, mean-based heritability estimates were higher for the traits GY, PH, HLW, PC%, TSW, and ET were considerably higher, with values of 94%, 88%, 86%, 83%, 80%, and 73%, respectively. These results reflect how well phenotypic selection may work in stable conditions. The selection accuracy (AS) was often high across genotypes and the best accuracy for key traits. This indicates that a larger proportion of phenotypic variation is caused by genetic factors than by measurement inaccuracies or environmental sound. The coefficient of determination for GEI (R2ge) differed depending on the trait. Despite the low overall values, there was evidence of significant environmental modulation. The high genotypic correlations across environments (rge) for PH and GY indicated that these traits performed consistently and remained stable across a variety of environments. The utility of BLUP-based indices for assessing adaptability and multi-trait stability has been demonstrated in various crops including wheat [46, 71] and finger millet [11, 42].Advanced prediction models, particularly linear mixed models, provide superior accuracy for interpreting multi-environment trial data compared to fixed-effect approaches like AMMI (Gauch and Zobel, 1988) [13, 75, 89]. These models enable precise estimation of genetic parameters including broad-sense heritability and trait correlations [57].

BLUP-based ranking is essential in plant breeding because of its strong statistical framework, which enhances the ability to effectively manage unbalanced datasets, evaluate stability, and achieve genetic gain in choosing better genotypes across environments and traits, taking into account both fixed and random effects. To evaluate yield stability, BLUP-based indices such as the Harmonic Mean of Genotypic Values (HMGV), Relative Performance of Genotypic Values (RPGV), and their combination (HMRPGV) were calculated using BLUP-derived grain yield data. These indices integrate both average performance and stability, supporting the selection of genotypes that are not only high yielding but also consistent across diverse environments. Based on these stability metrics, genotypes G7 (D.2018), G4 (Alamata) and G10 (Utuba) were identified as the most stable and high performing across tested environments. Biometric methodologies such as HMGV (harmonic mean of genetic values), RPGV (relative performance of genetic values), and HMRPGV (harmonic mean of relative performance of genetic values) enable robust genetic assessments by quantifying random genotypic effects and facilitating genotype ranking based on predicted genetic merit. Based WAASBY-based BLUP estimates G1 had the highest value. Using this approach effectively reduced data dimensionality while retaining excellent explanatory value. Supporting this findings, several reports have been conveyed in other crops like, Barley under salt stress [61],Maize hybrids [86],Wheat [59] and Rice [36]. The high WAASBY-based stability of selected genotypes underscores their breeding value.

Our study implemented a multi-trait selection approach using stability evaluation methods MTSI, MGIDI used to select superior genotypes by considering multiple traits simultaneously. This index is used to forecast the stability of varieties in different environments and to single out genotypes that consistently perform well under various environmental conditions. This is especially useful when dealing with complex traits like yield, stability, and nutritional content. They help breeders make decisions when selecting for multiple traits without running into problems with multicollinearity. Using MTSI ranking, high-yielding durum wheat genotypes G1, G8, G12, G5 demonstrated consistent performance across various environmental conditions, indicating their stability in multiple settings. The results of AMMI stability selction and MTSI were not consistent, this why AMMI inclines to highlight stable yielding across environments, while multi-trait indexes (MTSI) prioritize genotypes with stable superiority considering important studied agronomic traits simultaneously (phonological, growth, yield related and qualitative traits), not just to evaluate stability for grain yield only. Similarly, Olivoto et al. [58] found that using genotype ideotype distance as a selection index makes comprehension even easier in MTSI. This demonstrates that the MTSI approach offers a distinctive, reliable, and simple selection procedure that facilitates the identification of a genotype's advantages and disadvantages.The genotype with the lowest multi trait-based selection index is chosen because it is closer to the ideotype. These results align with prior studies in wheat [59] and maize [86], confirming MGIDI’s utility for multi-trait selection.

Grain yield kgha−1 showed a significant positive association with key traits Fig. 7, suggesting these traits might have an important contribution for grain yield improvement of durum wheat. Similar to this result indicated by Yagdi & Sozen [78] significant positive correlation was determined between the seed yield, plant height and spike length. Giunta et al. [33] the genotypic correlations estimated showed positive association of grain yield with days for heading, harvest index and kernels number per spike.

Conclusions

The current result indicated that the grain production of durum wheat genotypes across test environments in northwestern Ethiopia was greatly influenced by genotype, environment, and their interaction. The polygon views of the GGE biplot pierced out that there was grouping test environments into two mega environments and identified genotypes which were high yielder or stable across the test environment. Regarding "discrimination vs. representativeness" two environments E5 and E6 have high discriminatory capacity and E1,E2, E3, E4 are more representative for evaluating the performance of durum wheat genotypes.The multi-trait selection approach using stability evaluation methods MTSI, MGIDI genotypes G1, G8, G12 and G5 exhibited the most favorable combination of traits across test environments and were therefore prioritized for selection under 15% selection intensity criterion. Significant positive associations were observed between grain yield and important traits, indicating these traits might have an important indirect role for grain yield improvement of durum wheat. All stability based indices showed geneotype G10 were stable and high yielding across environments, whereas considering multiple traits (MGIDI and MTSI) genotype G1 and G8 illustrated the highest yielding with better stability across test environments. The overall result of the finding using all study parameters genotype G10 were stable and high yielding, suggesting that it can be recommended for all durum wheat growers for the study areas and similar agroecology. These findings critical insights to the scientific community by improving durum wheat yield and could serve as a reference for evaluating the stability and yield performance of other durum wheat genotypes. This is essential to ensure food security and to overcome agricultural challenges through developing climate- resilient, stress tolerance, high yielding and stable genotypes. Additionally, it is advisable that candidate durum wheat varieties could be assessed for their yield performance through on-farm trials in farmers' fields.
