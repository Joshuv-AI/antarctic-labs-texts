Mortality and clinical outcomes in peripartum cardiomyopathy: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Resha Reya Ganthan, Francesco Rotatori
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPeripartum cardiomyopathy (PPCM) is an important cause of maternal cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, characterized by the development of left ventricular systolic dysfunction toward the end of pregnancy or in the months following delivery. We aimed to evaluate all-cause mortality and clinical outcomes in women with PPCM.MethodsWe performed a systematic review and meta-analysis of studies evaluating outcomes in PPCM. PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science were searched from inception through April 2026. Studies reporting all-cause mortality and/or heart failure hospitalization were included. Pooled event rates were estimated using a random-effects model with logit transformation.ResultsA total of 60 studies comprising 44,922 patients were included. The pooled all-cause mortality rate was 7% (95% confidence interval [CI] 5–9%), with substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 96.4%). Reported mortality ranged from 0% to over 25%, reflecting marked variability across geographic regions and clinical settings. Heart failure hospitalization was reported in three studies; the pooled rate was 11% (95% CI 1–63%), although this estimate should be interpreted cautiously given the limited evidence base and substantial heterogeneity.ConclusionsPeripartum cardiomyopathy is associated with a meaningful risk of mortality, with substantial variability across populations. These findings highlight the need for improved recognition, risk stratification, and standardized longitudinal cardiovascular care in women with PPCM.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease remains a leading cause of maternal morbidity and mortality worldwide, with an increasing proportion of adverse events occurring in the postpartum period [1], [2], [3]. While clinical attention has traditionally focused on complications during pregnancy and delivery, the weeks following childbirth—often referred to as the “fourth trimester”—represent a period of significant cardiovascular vulnerability characterized by dynamic hemodynamic and neurohormonal changes [4], [5], [6].

Peripartum cardiomyopathy (PPCM) is characterized by the development of left ventricular systolic dysfunction toward the end of pregnancy or in the months following delivery [7], [8], [9]. In addition to PPCM, heart failure in the postpartum setting may arise in association with hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, pre-existing cardiomyopathies, and cardiometabolic comorbidities, contributing to substantial clinical heterogeneity [10], [11], [12].

Outcomes in peripartum cardiomyopathy remain highly variable across studies and geographic regions. Reported mortality rates range from less than 5% in high-income settings to over 20% in resource-limited environments, reflecting differences in disease severity, access to care, and healthcare infrastructure [13], [14], [15], [16]. Furthermore, recovery of left ventricular function is inconsistent, with many patients experiencing persistent dysfunction and ongoing risk of adverse cardiovascular events [17], [18], [19]. Data on other clinically relevant outcomes, including heart failure hospitalization, are comparatively limited.

Despite increasing recognition of peripartum cardiomyopathy, there remains a lack of comprehensive synthesis quantifying the burden of mortality and clinical outcomes across diverse populations. In this study, we performed a systematic review and meta-analysis to evaluate all-cause mortality and available data on clinical outcomes, including heart failure hospitalization, among patients with peripartum cardiomyopathy. This focus complements prior meta-analyses examining ventricular recovery and outcomes of subsequent pregnancy by specifically characterizing the magnitude and timing of mortality across contemporary and historical PPCM cohorts.

Methods

Search strategy and study selection

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.

A comprehensive literature search was performed across three electronic databases: PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science, from database inception through April 2026. The search strategy incorporated combinations of keywords and controlled vocabulary related to peripartum cardiomyopathy and heart failure.

A total of 3541 records were identified (PubMed: 488; Web of Science: 590; Embase: 2463). After removal of duplicates, 2677 unique records were screened by title and abstract. Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they evaluated adult patients with peripartum cardiomyopathy and reported clinically relevant outcomes, including all-cause mortality and/or heart failure hospitalization. To be included in the quantitative synthesis, studies were required to provide extractable event-level data, including the number of outcome events and total sample size.

Eligible study designs included observational cohort studies, registry-based analyses, and post hoc analyses of randomized controlled trials. Only studies involving human adult populations were considered.

The primary outcome of interest was all-cause mortality, with HF hospitalization evaluated as a secondary clinical outcome where sufficient data were available. LVEF recovery and recurrence or relapse during subsequent pregnancy were not prespecified outcomes of the present review, as the objective was to quantify mortality burden and its variation across reported follow-up intervals rather than to reassess previously synthesized predictors of ventricular recovery or subsequent-pregnancy outcomes.

Studies were excluded if they involved pediatric populations or non-human subjects, were case reports or small case series with fewer than 10 participants, or consisted of reviews, editorials, or conference abstracts without sufficient data for extraction. Studies that did not report relevant clinical outcomes or lacked sufficient data to derive outcome measures were also excluded.

Data extraction and study characteristics

Data were extracted using a standardized approach. Extracted variables included study identifier (author and year), sample size, and number of outcome events.

The primary outcome was all-cause mortality, defined as death from any cause during the reported follow-up period. Where studies reported cause-specific mortality, only all-cause mortality was used for the primary quantitative synthesis. Cardiovascular and non-cardiovascular mortality were not pooled separately because cause-specific mortality was inconsistently reported across the included studies.

Statistical analysis

Meta-analyses were conducted using R with the meta package.

Pooled estimates of proportions were calculated using a random-effects model with logit transformation (PLOGIT). Between-study heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic and Cochran's Q test. Random-effects confidence intervals were calculated using the Hartung–Knapp method.

Sensitivity analyses were performed using a leave-one-out approach to evaluate the influence of individual studies on pooled estimates.

Publication bias was assessed visually using funnel plots and statistically using Egger's regression test. Given that fewer than 10 studies contributed to the HF hospitalization analysis, formal assessment of publication bias for this outcome was not performed because funnel plot asymmetry tests have limited statistical power and are not considered reliable in meta-analyses with small numbers of studies.

To evaluate the influence of outcome-assessment timing, a stratified analysis was performed according to mortality follow-up. Studies were categorized as reporting early mortality (≤6 months) or later mortality (>6 months) based on the reported mortality assessment time point. Studies without a clearly defined mortality follow-up interval were retained in the primary overall mortality analysis but excluded from the timing-stratified analysis. Differences between subgroups were assessed using a formal test for subgroup differences.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

A total of 60 studies were included in the primary meta-analysis of mortality, comprising 44,922 patients and 1408 events. The included studies encompassed a wide range of geographic regions and clinical settings, with variability in study design, sample size, and duration of follow-up. Three studies were included in the secondary analysis evaluating heart failure (HF) hospitalization.

Mortality according to follow-up duration

Among the 60 studies included in the primary mortality analysis, 43 had a clearly defined mortality assessment time point and were included in the timing-stratified analysis. Mortality was significantly higher in studies reporting outcomes beyond 6 months compared with those reporting outcomes at or before 6 months (p for subgroup difference = 0.009). Discussion

Principal findings

In this systematic review and meta-analysis of 60 studies comprising 44,922 patients, the pooled all-cause mortality rate in peripartum cardiomyopathy was 7.0%. Despite this overall estimate, there was substantial variability across studies, with mortality ranging from 0% to over 25%, and marked heterogeneity (I2 = 96.4%). In the secondary analysis, heart failure hospitalization was evaluated in a limited number of studies, and while a pooled estimate was derived, these findings should be interpreted cautiously.

Comparison with existing literature

Peripartum cardiomyopathy (PPCM) is a heterogeneous disorder with widely variable outcomes influenced by geographic region, access to care, and baseline disease severity [1], [2], [3], [4]. Prior studies have reported mortality rates ranging from less than 5% in high-income countries to over 20% in resource-limited settings [2], [5], [6], [7]. The pooled estimate observed in the present analysis is consistent with this spectrum and reflects the inclusion of diverse global populations.

Differences in outcomes are likely multifactorial. Delayed diagnosis, limited access to guideline-directed medical therapy, and more advanced disease at presentation have been reported in low and middle income countries (LMICs) [5], [6], [8]. In contrast, earlier recognition and multidisciplinary care in high-income settings have been associated with improved outcomes [2], [3], [9]. Socioeconomic factors, healthcare infrastructure, and access to advanced therapies further contribute to disparities in outcomes [6], [8]. In addition, emerging data suggest that underlying pathophysiological mechanisms—including vascular dysfunction, inflammation, and oxidative stress—may influence disease severity and recovery, potentially contributing to observed variability across populations [22], [23], [24].

Clinical implications

The findings of this study highlight that peripartum cardiomyopathy remains associated with a meaningful risk of mortality, even in contemporary cohorts [3], [9], [10]. The substantial variation in outcomes underscores the need for improved risk stratification and standardized management strategies. These findings support the integration of cardiovascular evaluation into routine postpartum care pathways.

Recovery of left ventricular function is variable, with a significant proportion of patients experiencing persistent dysfunction [9], [10], [11]. Early identification of high-risk patients is therefore essential, particularly given the potential for adverse outcomes including arrhythmias, thromboembolism, and progressive heart failure [3], [10], [12]. The observed burden of HF hospitalization, although based on limited data, suggests ongoing morbidity in this population.

Importantly, these findings support the concept of the postpartum period as a high-risk window for cardiovascular complications. The “fourth trimester” is characterized by dynamic hemodynamic and neurohormonal changes that may precipitate or exacerbate heart failure in susceptible individuals [13], [14], [25]. Despite this, structured postpartum cardiovascular follow-up remains inconsistent, and opportunities for early intervention may be missed [26], [27]. These results highlight the need for dedicated postpartum surveillance strategies and multidisciplinary care pathways extending beyond delivery [28], [29], [30].

Heterogeneity and study variability

A key finding of this analysis is the high degree of heterogeneity across studies. This likely reflects differences in study design, patient populations, diagnostic criteria, and follow-up duration [15], [16]. The timing of outcome assessment varied substantially, with some studies reporting short-term outcomes and others evaluating longer-term mortality.

Geographic variability is a major contributor. Studies conducted in sub-Saharan Africa and other LMICs frequently report higher mortality rates, consistent with prior literature [5], [6], [8]. In addition, variability in baseline left ventricular function, comorbidities, and access to therapies such as bromocriptine and advanced heart failure interventions may further contribute to outcome differences [2], [9], [17], [22]. The high degree of heterogeneity underscores the need for standardized definitions and reporting in peripartum cardiomyopathy research.

Publication bias and small-study effects

Funnel plot asymmetry and Egger's regression test suggested the presence of small-study effects. However, interpretation of these findings must be approached with caution given the substantial heterogeneity observed. In highly heterogeneous meta-analyses, funnel plot asymmetry may reflect underlying clinical variability rather than true publication bias [18], [19], [20], [23].

An additional source of heterogeneity is the broad temporal span of the included literature. PPCM management has evolved substantially over time, including greater use of contemporary guideline-directed heart failure therapy, improved access to multidisciplinary cardio-obstetric care, and broader availability of temporary and durable mechanical circulatory support. Accordingly, outcomes reported in earlier cohorts may not fully reflect those achievable in contemporary practice. Differences in treatment era and access to advanced heart failure therapies should therefore be considered when interpreting the pooled mortality estimates.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. It represents a large, comprehensive synthesis of over 44,000 patients across diverse clinical settings, providing a broad overview of outcomes in peripartum cardiomyopathy. The use of a random-effects model with logit transformation and robust sensitivity analyses enhances the reliability of the findings [20], [21], [22].

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the high heterogeneity limits the precision of pooled estimates. Second, the observational nature of included studies introduces potential bias and confounding [15], [16]. Third, variability in outcome definitions and follow-up duration may affect comparability across studies. Cause-specific mortality and associations between baseline or follow-up LVEF and mortality could not be quantitatively synthesized because these data were inconsistently defined and reported across the included studies. Future prospective PPCM studies should systematically report cardiovascular and non-cardiovascular causes of death and standardized measures of ventricular function to better characterize determinants of long-term mortality. Finally, the limited number of studies evaluating HF hospitalization restricts the strength of conclusions for this outcome.

Conclusions

In this systematic review and meta-analysis of PPCM, pooled all-cause mortality was approximately 7%, with substantially higher mortality reported beyond 6 months than during earlier follow-up. These findings highlight the importance of sustained longitudinal surveillance beyond the immediate peripartum period and demonstrate that outcome assessment timing is an important source of variability across PPCM studies. Although HF hospitalization data were limited, the available evidence further supports the need for continued cardiovascular follow-up after diagnosis.
