Optimized adiabatic–impulse protocol preserving Kibble–Zurek scaling with attenuated anti–Kibble–Zurek behavior
Han-Chuan Kou, Zhi-Han Zhang, Xin-Hui Wu, Yan Zhou, Gang Chen, Peng Li

Abstract
We propose an optimized adiabatic-impulse (OAI) protocol that achieves much shorter evolution time while preserving the Kibble-Zurek scaling. Near the critical regime, the control field is linearly ramped across the quantum critical point at a rate characterized by a quench time τ Q \tau_{Q} . Away from the critical regime, the evolution is designed to follow the threshold of adiabatic breakdown, which we characterize by an adiabatic coefficient ζ ∝ τ Q α \zeta\propto\tau_{Q}^{\alpha} . As a consequence, the total evolution time exhibits a sublinear power-law dependence on τ Q \tau_{Q} , and the conventional linear quench protocol is recovered in the limit α → ∞ \alpha\rightarrow\infty . We apply the OAI protocol to the transverse Ising chain and numerically determine the minimum value of ζ \zeta . We further investigate the nonequilibrium dynamics in the presence of a noisy field that can induce anti–Kibble–Zurek behavior, leading to more defects for slower ramps. Within the OAI protocol, the optimal quench time that minimizes defects obeys an altered universal power-law scaling with the noise strength. Finally, we generalize the OAI protocol to incorporate nonlinear Kibble-Zurek scaling.



I
Introduction

Quantum phase transitions play a central role in the equilibrium physics and non-equilibrium dynamics
Sachdev 2011
. When a system is driven across a phase transition, nonequilibrium critical dynamics can induce the formation of topological defects, with a density that is predicted by the well-known Kibble-Zurek (KZ) mechanism. This theory was first proposed by Kibble in the context of cosmology
Kibble 1976
;
Kibble 1980
and discussed by Zurek in the thermal phase transitions of condensed matter systems
Zurek 1985
;
Zurek 1993
;
Zurek 1996
. The KZ mechanism has been confirmed in a wide range of theories and experiments
Polkovnikov and Gritsev 2008
;
Zurek 2013
;
Ulm et al. 2013
;
Xu et al. 2014
;
Meldgin et al. 2016
;
Gardas et al. 2018
;
Ulčakar et al. 2018
;
Keesling et al. 2019
;
Weinberg et al. 2020
;
Deng et al. 2025
. In recent decades, the KZ mechanism has been also generalized to quantum phase transitions
Damski 2005
;
Zurek et al. 2005
;
Polkovnikov 2005
;
Dziarmaga 2005
;
Dziarmaga 2010
, and has become an important dynamical tool for measuring critical property in quantum simulation platforms
Keesling et al. 2019
.

The emergence of the KZ mechanism can be attributed to the critical slowing down near the critical point, where the divergence of the relaxation time induces the appearance of topological defects regardless of how slowly the system is driven. The adiabatic-impulse approximation (AIA) framework captures this essential feature and provides a qualitative account of KZ scaling by comparing the relaxation time with the timescale induced by the external driving
Damski and Zurek 2006
;
Uhlmann et al. 2007
;
Polkovnikov et al. 2011
;
Francuz et al. 2016
;
Gardas et al. 2017
;
Chen et al. 2020
;
Tong et al. 2021
;
Roychowdhury et al. 2021
;
Nowak and Dziarmaga 2021
;
Dziarmaga et al. 2022
;
Kou and Li 2022
;
Kou and Li 2023
;
Sokolov et al. 2024a
;
YuRong et al. 2025
. An extended AIA picture
Kou and Li 2023
demonstrates that, as the driving rate increases, the dynamics deviates from the KZ scaling and the defect density enters pre-saturated and saturated regimes
Świsłocki et al. 2013
;
Chesler et al. 2015
;
Ko et al. 2019
;
Zeng et al. 2023
;
Yang et al. 2023
. Reducing defects typically entails slower driving, thereby increasing the total evoulution time. However, a counterintuitive phenomenon, known as anti-Kibble-Zurek (AKZ) behavior, emerges due to the interaction with the environment and stochastic noise, leading to the production of more defects at slower driving rates
Griffin et al. 2012
;
Dutta et al. 2016
;
Gao et al. 2017
;
Puebla et al. 2020
;
Rossini and Vicari 2020
;
Ai et al. 2021
;
Singh and Gangadharaiah 2021
;
Subires et al. 2022
;
King et al. 2023
;
Jara and Cosme 2024
;
Ding and Zhang 2025
;
Iwamura and Suzuki 2024
;
Kou et al. 2025
.

In recent years, a variety of nonequilibrium protocols have been developed to speed up quenches across quantum phase transitions while avoiding additional defect production. Counterdiabatic driving has been proposed as a powerful tool that can, in principle, suppress nonequilibrium defects completely
del Campo et al. 2012
;
Takahashi 2013
;
Saberi et al. 2014
;
Barone et al. 2024
;
Sandeep et al. 2026
. This approach relies on an auxiliary Hamiltonian with nonlocal many-body interactions, which are difficult to implement in experimental platforms
Damski 2014
. Several alternative routes are developed, including truncated counterdiabatic driving
del Campo et al. 2012
and counterdiabatic optimized local driving approach
Čepaitė et al. 2023
;
Barone et al. 2024
.
For isolate system, nonlinear quench (NLQ) protocol can yield a lower defect density than the linear quench (LQ) protocol
Sen et al. 2008
;
Barankov and Polkovnikov 2008
;
Sokolov et al. 2024b
;
Kriel et al. 2026
. In finite-size systems, there exists an optimal NLQ protocol that minimizes the formation of defects
Wu et al. 2015
. By accounting for excited states, a nonadiabatic optimization scheme yields a lower defect density than the optimal NLQ protocol
Grabarits et al. 2025
.
In the quantum annealing regime, a faster annealing schedule was introduced to mitigate nonadiabatic excitations during target-state preparation
Roland and Cerf 2002
, and a corresponding lower bound on the evolution time was derived in later developments
García-Pintos et al. 2023
;
García-Pintos et al. 2025
. The quantum adiabatic brachistochrone provides a time-optimal strategy
Rezakhani et al. 2009
, which can be determined more precisely by solving the corresponding brachistochrone equation
Wang et al. 2015
;
Geng et al. 2016
.

Motivated by these investigations, we further analyze the AIA framework and design an OAI protocol that captures the KZ and nonlinear KZ scaling. The construction is based on comparing the timescale
|\epsilon(t)/\dot{\epsilon}(t)|
with the relaxation time
\tau(t)
, where
\epsilon(t)
denotes a dimensionless distance from the critical point. As illustrated in Fig.
1
, away from criticality the protocol chooses
\epsilon(t)
such that
|\epsilon/\dot{\epsilon}|\propto\tau(t)
, enabling faster ramps farther from the critical point while keeping the dynamics near the threshold of adiabatic breakdown. In the vicinity of the critical point, timescale
|\epsilon(t)/\dot{\epsilon}(t)|
reduces to
|t|
for a linear ramp. As a result, the OAI protocol substantially reduces the total time cost while the final defect density does not exceed the prediction of KZ dynamics. This further implies that, for dynamics governed by a Lindblad equation, the OAI protocol can yield a lower defect density than the LQ and NLQ protocols.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
II
, we formulate the OAI protocol based on the AIA framework. We analytically derive the scaling behavior of the total evolution time and the optimal quench time. The former is obtained as a function of quench time, whereas the latter is discussed in the presence of the noisy field. The OAI protocol is applied to the one-dimensional transverse Ising model in the absence and presence of a noisy field, as discussed in Sec.
III
and Sec.
IV
, respectively. In Sec.
V
, the OAI protocol is generalized to the nonlinear KZ scaling. At last, a brief summary and discussion are given in Sec.
VI
.

II
Optimized impulse-adiabatic protocol

We start from the AIA framework, which is applicable to a variety of systems with second-order phase transitions
Dziarmaga 2010
. We consider a time-dependent Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H(t)=H[g(t)],

(1)

where the control parameter is slowly ramped from
g_{i}\equiv g(t_{i})
to
g_{f}\equiv g(t_{f})
(with
g_{i}>g_{f}
) across the critical point
g_{c}
with quench time
\tau_{Q}
. Here,
t_{i}
and
t_{f}
denote the initial and final time, respectively. A distance from a quantum critical point can be measured with a dimensionless parameter
\epsilon(t)=\frac{g(t)-g_{c}}{g_{c}}
.

In general, the system is initially prepared in the ground state of a simple Hamiltonian and subsequently driven to a final nontrivial state at which the topological defects induced by critical dynamics can be easily counted. For a conventional LQ protocol,

\displaystyle\epsilon(t)=-\frac{t}{\tau_{Q}},

(2)

the AIA picture divides the dynamics into two adiabatic stages and an intermediate impulse stage by comparing the timescale
|\epsilon(t)/\dot{\epsilon}(t)|
with the relaxation time
\tau(t)=|\epsilon(t)|^{-z\nu}
. Here,
z
and
\nu
are the dynamical and correlation length exponents, respectively.
In the adiabatic stages (
|\epsilon(t)|\gg 0
), the system evolves adiabatically. Near the critical point, the critical slowing down causes the diverging relaxation time, so the system inevitably generates the topological defects or quantum excitations. The resulting defect density or excitation density is predicted by the KZ mechanism,

\displaystyle n\propto\left(\frac{J\tau_{Q}}{\hbar}\right)^{-\beta},

(3)

where
J
sets the energy scale and
\beta=\frac{z\nu}{1+z\nu}
is the KZ exponent. Throughout we set
\hbar=1
and
J=1
.

In this work, we propose the OAI protocol based on the AIA picture. In both adiabatic stages, we design
\epsilon(t)
such that the driving timescale
|\epsilon(t)/\dot{\epsilon}(t)|
is approximately proportional to the relaxation time
\tau(t)

\displaystyle|\epsilon(t)/\dot{\epsilon}(t)|\approx\zeta\tau(t)=\zeta|\epsilon(t)|^{-z\nu},~~|\epsilon(t)|\gg 0,

(4)

as shown in Fig.
1
(a).
We refer to
\zeta
as the
adiabatic coefficient
to mark the threshold of adiabatic breakdown.

\zeta
is chosen to be positive and sufficiently large so that the evolution remains adiabatic in both adiabatic stages. In the two adiabatic stages, the time-dependent parameter
\epsilon(t)
can be written as

\displaystyle\epsilon(t)\approx\epsilon_{1}(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\left[\frac{z\nu}{\zeta}(\theta+t)\right]^{-\frac{1}{z\nu}},&t\ll 0,\\
-\left[\frac{z\nu}{\zeta}(\theta-t)\right]^{-\frac{1}{z\nu}},&t\gg 0,\end{array}\right.

where
\theta>0
is a positive coefficient and the detail is presented in App.
A
.

To remain consistent with the KZ dynamics where the final defect density satisfies Eq. (
3
), the system should be linearly driven across the critical point,

\displaystyle\epsilon(t)\approx\epsilon_{2}(t)=-\frac{t}{\tau_{Q}},

\displaystyle~~~|\epsilon(t)|\sim 0,

(7)

Meanwhile, for
t\sim 0
, the part
(\theta\pm t)^{-x}
in Eq. (
II
) can be expanded as
\theta^{-x}\pm x\theta^{-1-x}t+O(t^{2})
. Combining Eqs. (
II
) and (
7
), we obtain the complete formulation of
\epsilon(t)
,

\displaystyle\epsilon(t)

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{1}(t)-\epsilon_{1}(0)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}+\left(\frac{\zeta/z\nu}{\theta+t}\right)^{\frac{1}{z\nu}}-\left(\frac{\zeta}{z\nu\theta}\right)^{\frac{1}{z\nu}},&t<0,\\
-\left(\frac{\zeta/z\nu}{\theta-t}\right)^{\frac{1}{z\nu}}+\left(\frac{\zeta}{z\nu\theta}\right)^{\frac{1}{z\nu}},&t\geq 0,\end{array}\right.

where
\theta=\frac{1}{z\nu}\left(\zeta^{\frac{1}{z\nu}}\tau_{Q}\right)^{\frac{z\nu}{1+z\nu}}
. A detailed derivation of Eq. (
II
) is provided in App.
A
. The term
\epsilon_{1}(0)
should be small enough,

\displaystyle\left.|\epsilon_{1}(0)\right.|=\left(\frac{\zeta}{z\nu\theta}\right)^{\frac{1}{z\nu}}=\left(\tau_{Q}^{-1}\zeta\right)^{\frac{1}{1+z\nu}}\ll 1,

(10)

which ensures that the condition in Eq. (
4
) remains satisfied.
Accordingly, the OAI protocol requires an upper bound on
\zeta
, namely
\zeta\ll\tau_{Q}
. With an appropriate choice of
\zeta
, the adiabaticity in two adiabatic stages is maintained, and the whole nonequilibrium physics still is governed by the critical dynamics. The final defect density is given by KZ scaling, Eq. (
3
).

Eq. (
II
) indicates that the evolution is restricted to

\displaystyle-\theta\leq t_{i}<t_{f}\leq\theta.

(11)

Here, we set the adiabatic coefficient
\zeta
as a power law of
\tau_{Q}
,

\displaystyle\zeta\propto\tau_{Q}^{\alpha},~~\alpha<1.

(12)

When Eq. (
4
) holds, the above equation gives the upper bound of the exponent
\alpha
, while its lower bound is model dependent and must be determined for each system. As an explicit example, we determine the lower bound of
\alpha
for the transverse Ising model in Sec.
III.2
.
Then, the parameter
\theta=\frac{1}{z\nu}\tau_{Q}^{(\alpha+z\nu)/(1+z\nu)}
is determined only by
\tau_{Q}
.
So, regardless of how distant the initial or final system is from the critical point, the total evolution time is bounded,

\displaystyle T_{\alpha}\equiv t_{f}-t_{i}\leq 2\theta=\frac{2}{z\nu}\tau_{Q}^{\frac{\alpha+z\nu}{1+z\nu}}.

(13)

With this bound, the KZ scaling in Eq. (
3
) can be expressed in terms of the total evolution time as
n_{\text{KZ}}\propto T_{\alpha}^{-d\nu/(\alpha+z\nu)}
.

When a noisy field is present, the Hamiltonian defined in Eq. (
1
) is modified to

\displaystyle H_{\gamma}(t)=H[g(t)]+\gamma(t)V,

(14)

where
V
is the noisy part. The resulting AKZ behavior induces more defects for slower driving
Dutta et al. 2016
. Here,
\gamma(t)
is Gaussian white noise with zero mean and the second moment
\langle\gamma(t)\gamma(t^{\prime})\rangle=W^{2}\delta(t-t^{\prime})
. Therefore, shortening the evolution time reduces the noise-induced contribution and hence yields lower defect density in the final state. When the OAI protocol is applied to Eq. (
14
), the final defect density takes the form

\displaystyle n=a\tau_{Q}^{-\beta}+b\gamma T_{\alpha}=a\tau_{Q}^{-\beta}+b^{\prime}\gamma\tau_{Q}^{\frac{\alpha+z\nu}{1+z\nu}},

(15)

where
a
,
b
, and
b^{\prime}
are three constants. There exists an optimal quench time

\displaystyle\tilde{\tau}_{Q}^{\{\text{OAI}\}}=\left(\frac{b^{\prime}\beta\gamma}{a\alpha}\right)^{-\frac{1}{\alpha^{\prime}+\beta}}\propto\gamma^{-\frac{1+z\nu}{(d+z)\nu+\alpha}},

(16)

which exhibits a universal scaling and corresponds to a minimum defect density
n
, where
\alpha^{\prime}=\frac{\alpha+z\nu}{1+z\nu}
.

Finally, we emphasize three points.
First, the total evolution time defined in Eq. (
13
) is rewritten as
T_{\alpha}\propto\tau_{Q}
for
\alpha>1
, corresponding to
\zeta\gg\tau_{Q}
. In Eq. (
12
), we have restricted the range of
\zeta
to ensure the validity of Eq. (
4
). The restriction, however, can be relaxed by allowing
\alpha>1
. Such a relaxation does not affect the adiabaticity of the adiabatic stages, but it renders Eq. (
4
) invalid. Consequently, the resulting total evolution time is no longer the shortest time in the AIA framework.
Second, the LQ protocol is recovered as a limiting case of the OAI protocol. In the limit
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
, we have
\zeta,\theta\rightarrow\infty
and Eq. (
II
) reduces to the LQ protocol by applying the approximation
(1-|t|/\theta)^{-1/z\nu}=1+|t|/(z\nu\theta)
. Detailed derivations supporting the first two points are presented in App
B
.
Third, we can rewrite Eq. (
II
) as

\displaystyle\epsilon(t)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}+\left[\left(\frac{\zeta/z\nu}{\theta+t}\right)^{\frac{1}{z\nu}}-\left(\frac{\zeta}{z\nu\theta}\right)^{\frac{1}{z\nu}}\right]^{r},&t<0,\\
-\left[\left(\frac{\zeta/z\nu}{\theta-t}\right)^{\frac{1}{z\nu}}-\left(\frac{\zeta}{z\nu\theta}\right)^{\frac{1}{z\nu}}\right]^{r},&t\leq 0,\end{array}\right.

In this case, the quench protocol can be reduced to the NLQ protocol near the critical point,
\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\epsilon(t)=-\mathop{\rm sgn}(t)|t/\tau_{Q}|^{r}
. The final defect density then follows the nonlinear KZ scaling,

\displaystyle n\propto\tau_{Q}^{-\frac{rd\nu}{1+rd\nu}}.

(19)

We refer to Eq. (
II

V

III
Transverse Ising Chain under

the OAI Protocol

As a prototypical model for a quantum phase transition, we consider the one-dimensional transverse Ising model,

\displaystyle H=-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(\sigma_{j}^{x}\sigma_{j+1}^{x}+g\sigma_{j}^{z}\right),

(20)

where
\sigma^{a}_{j}
(
a=x,y,z
) are Pauli matrices and the total number of lattice sites
N
is assumed to be even. We impose a periodic boundary condition,
\sigma^{a}_{N+j}=\sigma^{a}_{j}
, and focus on the ferromagnetic case. The strength of the transverse field is measured by
g
. Using the Jordan-Wigner mapping,
\sigma_{j}^{z}=1-2c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{j}
and
\sigma_{j}^{x}=-(c_{j}^{\dagger}+c_{j})\prod_{l<j}\sigma_{l}^{z}
, and the canonical Bogoliubov transformation,
c_{q}=u_{q}\eta_{q}-v_{q}\eta_{-q}^{\dagger}
with the Bogoliubov coefficients
u_{q}
and
v_{q}
, we can arrive at the diagonalized form of the Hamiltonian in the quasiparticle representation,

\displaystyle H=\sum_{q}\omega_{q}(\eta_{q}^{\dagger}\eta_{q}-\frac{1}{2}),

(21)

where
\eta_{q}
is the quasiparticle operator,
q
the quasimomentum, and
\omega_{q}=2\sqrt{1+g^{2}-2g\cos q}
the quasiparticle dispersion.

In the thermodynamic limit
N\rightarrow\infty
and at zero temperature, there is a second-order quantum phase transition from a ferromagnetic state (
|g|<1
) with
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry breaking to a quantum paramagnetic state (
|g|>1
)
Sachdev 2011
. The QCP occurs at
g_{c}=1
or
g_{c}=-1
, where the quasiparticle dispersion becomes a linear one,
\omega_{q}\sim 2|q-q_{c}|
with gap-closing mode
q_{c}=0
or
\pi
, that is responsible for the dynamical exponent
z=1
and implies the correlation length exponent
\nu=1
.

III.1
OAI protocol and KZ scaling

Using the quench protocol in Eq. (
II
), we initialize the system in its ground state and ramp the transverse field from the paramagnetic (
g_{i}>1
) to the ferromagnetic phases (
0\leq g_{f}<1
) across a quantum critical point (

\displaystyle g(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{\zeta}{t+\sqrt{\zeta\tau_{Q}}}-\sqrt{\frac{\zeta}{\tau_{Q}}}+1,&t_{i}\leq t<0,\\[4.0pt]
\frac{\zeta}{t-\sqrt{\zeta\tau_{Q}}}+\sqrt{\frac{\zeta}{\tau_{Q}}}+1,&0\leq t\leq t_{f},\\
\end{array}\right.

where the initial and final times are given by

and

, respectively. In Eq. (
II
), the coefficient
\theta
is given by

. By adjusting the adiabatic coefficient
\zeta
, we maintain adiabaticity in the two adiabatic stages. Near the critical point, Eq. (
III.1
) can be reduced to

\displaystyle g(t)=1-\frac{t}{\tau_{Q}}+O(t^{2}),~~t\rightarrow 0,

(24)

where we adopt the approximation
\frac{1}{(t+C)^{a}}\approx\frac{1}{C^{a}}-\frac{at}{C^{1+a}}+O(t^{2})

1

At the final time

\displaystyle n=n_{\text{KZ}}=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{2\tau_{Q}}}.

(b) demonstrates a power-law behavior between
\zeta
and scaled defect density
n/n_{\text{KZ}}-1
,

\displaystyle n/n_{\text{KZ}}-1=F^{-y}(\zeta,\tau_{Q}),

(29)

where the unknown function
F
depends on
\zeta
and
\tau_{Q}
. To determine
F
, we fix a reference value
n/n_{\text{KZ}}-1=2
, indicated by the horizontal dashed line in Fig.
3
(b). The intersections with the numerical curves then establish the corresponding pairs (
\zeta
,
\tau_{Q}
). The resulting
\zeta
extracted as a function of
\tau_{Q}
is shown in the inset of Fig.
3
(b). A numerical fit indicates that
F
scales as
F\propto\tau_{Q}^{-1/4}\zeta
. As shown in Fig.
3
(c), plotting
n/n_{\text{KZ}}-1
versus the scaling variable
\tau_{Q}^{-1/4}\zeta
yields a good data collapse for different values of
\tau_{Q}
. As a result, in the range of
0\ll\zeta\ll\tau_{Q}^{1/4}
, the defect density takes the form

\displaystyle n=n_{\text{KZ}}\left[1+x(\tau_{Q}^{-1/4}\zeta)^{-y}\right].

(30)

By numerical fitting, we have
x\approx 0.113
and
y\approx 1.732
for
g_{i}=2
and
g_{f}=0
. Therefore, when
\zeta\geq\tau_{Q}^{1/4}
, the function
F
in Eq. (
29
) becomes negligible, and the defect density follows the KZ scaling,
n=n_{\text{KZ}}
. Combining Eqs. (
27
), (
28
), and (
30
), we obtain the full expression of defect density:

\displaystyle n=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}n_{\text{su}},&\zeta=0,\\
n_{\text{KZ}}\left[1+x(\tau_{Q}^{-1/4}\zeta)^{-y}\right],&0\ll\zeta\ll\tau_{Q}^{1/4},\\
n_{\text{KZ}},&\zeta\geq\tau_{Q}^{1/4}.\end{array}\right.

In the inset of Fig.
3
(c), we show the excitation probability
p_{q}
as a function of
q
for
\tau_{Q}=10
and
\zeta=0.8
(i.e.,
\zeta\ll\tau_{Q}^{1/4}
).
The term
n_{\text{KZ}}
in Eq. (
30
) is dominated by excitations of the gap-closing modes, for which the LZ transition,
p_{q\rightarrow 0}=e^{-2\pi\tau_{Q}q^{2}}
, provides an accurate description (blue curve). The remaining part in Eq. (
30
), proportional to
(\tau_{Q}^{-1/4}\zeta)^{-y}
, arises from the contribution of the short wave modes
0\ll q<\pi
, where
p_{q}
exhibits pronounced coherent oscillations. These oscillations for short wave modes are rapidly suppressed as
\zeta
increases.

In Eq. (
12
), we define the adiabatic coefficient as a function of quench time,
\zeta\propto\tau_{Q}^{\alpha}
. If we need a precise KZ scaling, the exponent
\alpha
must be further restricted

\displaystyle\zeta\propto\tau_{Q}^{\alpha},~~\frac{1}{4}\leq\alpha<1,

(34)

which ensures
\zeta\tau_{Q}^{-1/4}\geq 1
and
\zeta\tau_{Q}^{-1}\ll 1
.

On the other hand, when the time-dependent system is driven in the presence of stochastic noise as shown in Eq. (
14
), AKZ behavior induces higher defect density for slower driving results. The defect density is minimized at a finite optimal quench time
\tilde{\tau}_{Q}
. In this noisy field setting, we may take
\zeta
to be a constant
C
(i.e.,
\alpha=0
) and choose
C
sufficiently large,

\displaystyle\zeta=C\gg\tau_{Q}^{1/4},~~\alpha=0.

(35)

In the next section, the OAI protocol is applied in the presence of the noisy field, and we will discuss how
\zeta
influences the scaling behavior of the optimal quench time dependence on the noisy strength.

IV
OAI Protocol in a noisy field

In this section, we consider the system in the presence of the noisy field. The total Hamiltonian reads

\displaystyle H_{\gamma}(t)

\displaystyle=H(t)+\gamma(t)V,

(36)

where
H(t)
is defined in Eq. (
20
) that describes the noise-free Hamiltonian, and
V=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sigma_{j}^{z}
is the noise-dependent part.
\gamma(t)
is a Gaussian white noise with zero mean and the nonzero second moment
\langle\gamma(t)\gamma(t^{\prime})\rangle=W^{2}\delta(t-t^{\prime})
, where
W
characterizes the strength of the noise. Using Novikov’s theorem
Novikov 1965
, the dynamics can be described by the Lindblad-type master equation, which is given by
Dutta et al. 2016

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\rho(t)=-i[H(t),\rho(t)]-\frac{W^{2}}{2}[V,[V,\rho(t)]],

(37)

where
\rho(t)
is the density operator where the initial condition reads
\rho(t_{i})=|\psi(t_{i})\rangle\langle\psi(t_{i})|
.

By a Fourier transform, the density operator can be mapped to momentum space
\rho(t)=\bigotimes_{q>0}\rho_{q}(t)
. In each Fourier subspace, the Lindblad equation reads

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\rho_{q}(t)=

\displaystyle-i[\sigma_{z}h_{q}^{z}(t)+\sigma_{x}h_{q}^{x}(t),\rho_{q}(t)]

\displaystyle-\frac{W^{2}}{2}[\sigma_{z},[\sigma_{z},\rho_{q}(t)]],

(38)

where
h_{q}^{z}(t)=2g(t)-2\cos q
,
h_{q}^{x}=2\sin q
, and
g(t)
is defined in Eq. (
III.1
).

In the presence of noise, the defect generation is governed by two competing mechanisms: (i) noise-induced excitations, which increase with the noise strength
W^{2}
and the total evolution time
T_{\alpha}
, and (ii) non-adiabatic excitations, which are suppressed by raising the quench time scale
\tau_{Q}
. As a result, the final defect density can be written as
Dutta et al. 2016
;
Kou et al. 2025

\displaystyle n=n_{\text{KZ}}+bW^{2}T_{\alpha},

(39)

where
b
is a constant and the total evolution time,

\displaystyle T_{\alpha}=t_{i}-t_{f}\propto\tau_{Q}^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}},

(40)

follows a sublinear power-law dependence on the quench time. This noise-induced contribution,
W^{2}T_{\alpha}
, gives rise to the AKZ behavior. The defect density can be rewritten

\displaystyle n=n_{\text{KZ}}+b\left(W^{\frac{4}{1+\alpha}}\tau_{Q}\right)^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}.

(41)

The defect density exhibits a minimum at a finite optimal quench time,

\displaystyle\tilde{\tau}_{Q}=\tilde{\tau}_{Q}^{\{\text{OAI}\}}\propto W^{-s},

(42)

with
s=\frac{4}{\alpha+2}
. The exponent
\alpha
is restricted to
1/4\leq\alpha<1
, which is numerically demonstrated in Sec.
III.2
.

VI
Summary and Discussion

In summary, we propose an OAI protocol that realizes the much shorter evolution time within the AIA framework for systems whose dynamics is governed by the KZ mechanism. We define an adiabatic coefficient
\zeta
that characterizes the threshold for adiabatic breakdown in the two adiabatic stages. Applying the OAI protocol to the transverse Ising chain in the absence and presence of stochastic noise reveals a new scaling of the optimal quench time. The resulting universal scaling law suggests that the OAI protocol can be generalized, making it a suitable tool for studying nonequilibrium critical dynamics in other integrable and nonintegrable systems.

In D-Wave quantum annealers, a non-adiabatic process during fast evolution prevents the preparation of the adiabatic quantum state
Hauke et al. 2020
;
Crosson and Lidar 2021
;
Fioroni and Savona 2025
. An appropriate evolution protocol can therefore provide an efficient means of satisfying the adiabatic condition during quantum annealing
Gardas et al. 2018
;
Munoz-Bauza and Lidar 2025
.
For a finite system of linear size
L
, the adiabatic condition requires that
\tau_{Q}\sim\frac{1}{\varepsilon}L^{\frac{1+z\nu}{\nu}}
in order to maintain the defect density less than
\varepsilon

Dziarmaga 2010
;
Altshuler et al. 2010
.
Within the OAI protocol, the total evolution time required to satisfy this adiabatic condition follows directly from Eq. (
13
),

\displaystyle T_{\alpha}(L)\sim\varepsilon^{-\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}L^{\frac{(1+z\nu)(1+\alpha)}{2\nu}}.

(49)

This scaling exhibits an analytical route to accelerate driving while suppressing the generation of additional topological defects.
