AlzStack: Forecasting early-onset Alzheimer's with an explainable AI system using multiple data balancing techniques

To the Editor-in-Chief,

Dear Professor Burstyn,

We appreciate the opportunity to make the comments regarding our recent publication. We stand by the original framing and results of the AlzStack framework. The following points provide the technical and clinical justification for the methodology and terminology employed in our study.

The commenter suggests a rigid adherence to the age-based definition of Early-Onset Alzheimer's Disease (EOAD < 65 years). While we acknowledge this specific chronological definition in geriatric psychiatry, the term "early-onset" in the context of Machine Learning and Predictive Analytics is frequently used to describe the temporal onset of detectable symptoms within a longitudinal or cross-sectional cohort. Our study focuses on the early detection of the disease state. In a cohort aged 60–90, identifying patients at the earliest point of clinical presentation is a vital "early-onset" detection task for primary care. We utilized the term to describe the earliness of the diagnosis relative to the disease's progression, which is a standard terminological application in many health informatics papers.

The critique argues that using variables like MMSE and functional assessments makes the model a "classifier" rather than a "forecaster" because these are proximal manifestations of the disease. The use of clinically proximal variables is a deliberate design choice aimed at translational utility. While antecedent biological markers (like CSF or PET imaging) are valuable, they are often inaccessible in high-volume or resource-limited clinical settings. By utilizing functional and cognitive markers—which the commenter notes are "very close to the outcome"—we ensure that AlzStack provides high-confidence decision support where it is needed most: at the point of clinical assessment. High performance using these variables does not invalidate the model; rather, it confirms the model's ability to synthesize complex, overlapping symptomatic data into a reliable diagnostic output.

Regarding the performance metrics in Table 5, the commenter notes that Stacking showed a marginally higher mean AUC than Soft Voting. In Machine Learning, the "best" model is not always the one with the highest decimal-point advantage in a single metric like AUC. Model Parsimony is a core principle of our work. Soft Voting was selected as the superior approach because it achieved identical Recall to Stacking while offering significantly lower computational overhead and greater stability. In a clinical screening tool, avoiding the "black box" complexity of a meta-learner (Stacking) in favor of a more interpretable and stable ensemble (Soft Voting) is a standard architectural preference. Our claim of "superior performance" encompasses this balance of accuracy, stability, and deployment readiness.

The commenter suggests "cross-sectional classifier" is more accurate than "forecasting system." We maintain that AlzStack functions as a forecasting system within the patient's clinical trajectory. For a patient presenting with vague cognitive complaints, the model "forecasts" the formal clinical label of Alzheimer's Disease by analyzing current symptomatic patterns that may be missed by a human observer. Classification and forecasting are not mutually exclusive in medical AI; providing a high-probability classification of a progressive condition effectively serves as a forecast of the patient's immediate clinical status. The AlzStack framework was developed to bridge the gap between complex ensemble learning and practical clinical application. The points raised by the commenter represent a specific clinical perspective on nomenclature; however, they do not challenge the mathematical validity, the explainability results, or the technical rigour of our published work. We believe the paper stands as a robust contribution to the field of interpretable AI in dementia research.
