Study protocol FRENCH-ARRIVE: labor induction in low-risk nulliparous women at 39 weeks of gestation to reduce cesarean births: a randomized trial of induction versus expectant management in France
Authors: Hugo Madar, Jean-Marie Chrétien, Hanane Bouchghoul, Alizée Froeliger, Aline Bibes, Caroline Lacoste, Damien Subtil, Patrick Saulnier, Loïc Sentilhes, the FRENCH-ARRIVE Study Group and the Groupe de Recherche en Obstétrique et Gynécologie (GROG)
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe ARRIVE trial showed that elective induction of labor at 39 weeks for low-risk nulliparous women compared to expectant management reduced cesarean delivery rates with a nonsignificant trend toward decreased perinatal morbidity. However, the generalizability of these findings to other health care systems and obstetric contexts outside of the United States remains uncertain.MethodsThe FRENCH-ARRIVE multicenter, randomized controlled trial with two parallel groups, modeled on the ARRIVE trial, will include 4200 low-risk nulliparous women with singleton pregnancies from 37 weeks 0 days through 38 weeks 6 days of gestation. Women will be randomized in a 1:1 ratio to either elective induction between 39 weeks 0 days and 39 weeks 4 days of gestation or expectant management. The primary outcome will be the incidence of cesarean deliveries after randomization, regardless of indication. This study will have a power of 80% to show a 20% reduction in the cesarean incidence from 15.0% to 12.0%. Secondary outcomes will include indicators of maternal and perinatal morbidity and of health-care resource utilization, defined consistently with those of the ARRIVE trial to allow meaningful comparisons.DiscussionThis large, multicenter, randomized controlled trial, as the first replication of the ARRIVE trial in a different setting, aims to compare the effect of elective induction of labor at 39 weeks of gestation versus expectant management on the incidence of cesarean delivery in low-risk nulliparous women.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov NCT04799912 (March 12, 2021).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-09193-z.Keywords: Labor induction, Low-risk nulliparous women, Expectant management, Cesarean delivery, Neonatal morbidity, Randomized trial, ARRIVE, FRENCH-ARRIVE, Maternal satisfaction

Background

Elective labor induction at 39 weeks to reduce maternal and perinatal morbidity

Numerous observational studies have consistently shown that the incidence of perinatal and maternal complications is lowest at 39 weeks of gestation [1–13]. These findings come from large retrospective cohort studies of low-risk women, most of which used multivariate analyses to control for confounding biases [1–12]. It can thus be reasonably be concluded that 39 weeks represents the optimal gestational age for delivery to minimize the risk of maternal and neonatal complications such as cesarean and operative vaginal deliveries, perinatal death, and encephalopathy [1–12]. A randomized controlled trial (RCT) in the United Kingdom (UK) took place between 2012 and 2015 to evaluate whether labor induction at 39 weeks of gestation might reduce the cesarean rate among nulliparous women aged 35 years or older with uncomplicated pregnancies [14]. The 619 women randomized to either induction or expectant management did not differ significantly for cesarean delivery rates (32% in the induction group and 33% in the expectant-management group, relative risk [RR] 0.99, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.87–1.14) or maternal and neonatal morbidity [14]. Because this trial was powered to detect a high relative difference (36%) in cesarean delivery rates, it can neither rule out the possibility that labor induction has a smaller benefit nor adequately assess its safety [14]. Its external validity has also been called into question because the 33% rates of both cesarean and operative deliveries reported in the expectant-management group appear high even among a population at moderate risk, such as women aged 35 years and older [15]. These high rates may reflect differences in health care practices and organization between the UK and other countries [16]. In 2018, the Eunice Kennedy Shriver National Institute of Child Health and Human Development Maternal–Fetal Medicine Units (MFMU) Network published results from ARRIVE, a multicenter RCT conducted at 41 medical centers across the United States (US) [17]. Low-risk nulliparous women between 38 weeks 0 days and 38 weeks 6 days of gestation were randomized either to labor induction at 39 weeks 0 days to 39 weeks 4 days or to expectant management. The primary outcome was a composite variable of perinatal death or severe neonatal complications, and the main secondary outcome was the cesarean delivery rate [17]. Among the 6106 randomized women, the primary outcome occurred in 4.3% (132/3059) of neonates in the induction group compared to 5.4% (164/3037) in the expectant management group (RR 0.80, 95% CI 0.64–1.00) [17]), a nonsignificant difference. The cesarean delivery rate, however, was significantly lower in the induction than in the expectant management group (18.6% versus 22.2%, RR 0.84, 95% CI 0.76–0.93) [17]. Women assigned to induction of labor were also significantly less likely than their counterparts receiving expectant management to have had hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (RR 0.64, 95% CI 0.56–0.74) but spent more time in the labor and delivery unit ― a median duration of 20 h (interquartile range [IQR] 13–28) versus 14 h (IQR 9–20), (p < 0.001) [17]. Other secondary perinatal and maternal outcomes were similar in both groups. The ARRIVE trial thus demonstrated a notable maternal benefit associated with elective induction of labor at 39 weeks in this population with a nonsignificant reduction of the incidence of adverse perinatal outcomes [17].

Rationale for replication of the ARRIVE trial

Although the ARRIVE trial provided evidence supporting elective induction of labor at 39 weeks in low-risk nulliparous women, the generalizability of its results to populations, contexts, and health care systems outside the US remains uncertain [15, 18–27] for several reasons.

First, in the ARRIVE trial, among the 22,533 eligible women, only 6106 (27%) provided written informed consent and underwent randomization, and the characteristics and outcomes of the eligible women who declined to participate were not reported [17]. As the study population included a quarter of the eligible individuals, it cannot be ruled out that the latter group was not representative of all nulliparous women at low risk [16].

Second, the ARRIVE trial took place exclusively in academic hospital centers with 24-h-in-house obstetrician coverage and suggested the application of a standardized definition of failed induction of labor during the latent phase (waiting at least 12 h after both membrane rupture and initiation of a uterine stimulant before proceeding to cesarean delivery, absent any acute indication) [17]. Generalizing the induction of labor to other settings or defining failed induction differently might not have similar effects on cesarean rates. The plausibility of this hypothesis is reinforced by the 22.2% rate reported in the ARRIVE trial’s expectant management group — much lower than the overall US cesarean rate, around 30% [28]. In addition, differences in health care organization and access, and in the characteristics of both women and clinical management between the US and other countries ― particularly European ― may influence cesarean rates and therefore limit the generalizability of the ARRIVE trial’s findings [27, 29–31]. In particular, mean body mass index, mean maternal age at first child, ethnicity, and socioeconomic status differed between those reported in the US ARRIVE trial and those reported in Dutch and French populations; and cesarean rates in the US are markedly higher than those in many — and specifically in European — countries [16, 27].

Third, translating the findings of an RCT into real-world settings and everyday clinical practice — outside the controlled context of a trial — may produce unexpected or divergent outcomes [32–35]. Several authors have assessed the effects of the ARRIVE trial on obstetric practices and outcomes in this light [26, 36, 37]. In the US, Gilroy et al. [26], examined this question in a population-based retrospective cohort study of low-risk nulliparous women who had begun prenatal care by 12 weeks of gestation, had singleton, non-anomalous pregnancies, and gave birth at or after 39 weeks. They found an increase in labor inductions, a higher proportion of deliveries at 39 weeks, and a reduction in cesarean deliveries in the year following the publication of the ARRIVE trial, but they also identified small but statistically significant increases in some adverse maternal and perinatal outcomes in the post-ARRIVE group, including maternal blood transfusions, maternal intensive care unit admissions, low 5-min APGAR scores, and neonatal assisted ventilation. Nonetheless, the observational design of the study and the potential influence of factors other than changes in induction practices on outcomes prevent the establishment of causality [26]. The same authors reported that the rate of early term induction and delivery before 39 weeks has increased among low-risk nulliparous women since publication of the ARRIVE results [38]. This suggests that clinicians may have extrapolated from the ARRIVE trial findings, even in the absence of data demonstrating the safety or relevance of an increase of labor induction before 39 weeks [16, 38].

Finally, a routine policy of elective induction of labor at 39 weeks of gestation in low-risk nulliparous women is likely to affect health care facilities and systems and women's perceptions and experiences of childbirth [16, 39–43].

Additional evidence is thus needed to support the generalizability of the ARRIVE trial’s findings in settings outside the US before this shift in clinical practice toward elective induction at 39 weeks of gestation for low-risk women can be endorsed; that is, these findings should be replicated in other clinical settings. The Dutch Society of Obstetrics and Gynecology (NVOG), seeking to set up a replication trial in the Netherlands, conducted a study assessing the willingness of nulliparous women in the Netherlands to participate in such an RCT [27] and concluded setting it up would be very difficult. The Groupe de Recherche en Obstétrique et Gynécologie (GROG) [44–49], a French group of gynecologists-obstetricians involved in clinical research, and the French College of Gynecologists and Obstetricians [24] decided to replicate the ARRIVE trial in France. We chose as the primary outcome the incidence of cesarean delivery, because the reduction of cesarean delivery observed in the ARRIVE trial was the main improvement in maternal or perinatal health that might justify this new policy. To facilitate meaningful comparison with the original ARRIVE trial, we chose secondary maternal and perinatal outcomes that closely mirror those used in ARRIVE. Furthermore, to explore potential selection bias related to participation in an RCT in detail, we decided to ask women who decline participation in the FRENCH-ARRIVE trial to join a parallel cohort study to provide comparative outcome data.

The same inclusion and exclusion criteria will apply to the RCT and the prospective observational cohort of women who decline to participate in the trial.

From 34 weeks of gestation onwards, obstetricians and midwives will provide full oral and written information to potentially eligible women who meet the inclusion criteria during prenatal visits. Eligible women will be invited to confirm their participation and provide signed informed consent during a prenatal visit between 37 weeks 0 days and 38 weeks 6 days of gestation, during which a digital cervical examination will be performed to ascertain the Bishop score [51]. Women will be randomized in a 1:1 ratio with variable block sizes to either the elective induction or expectant management group. Randomization will be centralized through a secure web-based system – Ennov Clinical Software managed by the Data Management Unit of Angers University Hospital – and stratified by center. Once a woman has been included, her randomization number will remain assigned even if she withdraws from the study. Blinding of participants and health care providers is not feasible given the nature of the intervention.

Women eligible for the RCT who decline to participate will be asked to participate in a parallel prospective observational cohort and will be included after providing a written attestation that they do not oppose the use of their routinely collected data for this purpose. This particular design (prospective observational cohort alongside an RCT) allows assessment of the bias associated with refusal to participate in an RCT. It is expected to produce a much higher rate of inclusion than in the RCT, where refusal is due mainly to the random treatment assignment. Two studies in obstetrics have already used this design [52, 53]. To ensure continuous cohort enrollment throughout the study and to maintain balance between the RCT and the cohort (especially as cesareans rates may well change in participating centers during the planned study period of least 6 years), cohort recruitment will be stratified by center and conducted in sequential thirds, based on the expected number of trial participants at each center and re-assessed regularly every six months according to the rhythm of inclusions at each center. Each time cohort enrollment reaches one-third of the expected randomized sample size at a given site, cohort recruitment will pause until trial enrollment equals that number; this process will continue until the number of women included in the cohort matches the number randomized in the trial, so that the RCT and the prospective observational cohort include equal numbers of women, all recruited over the same time period.

Interventions

Women randomized to the elective induction group will undergo planned induction of labor between 39 weeks 0 days and 39 weeks 4 days of gestation. Labor will be induced by amniotomy for women with a favorable cervix (Bishop ≥ 6) that day and augmented, if necessary, with intravenous oxytocin. Women with an unfavorable cervix (Bishop < 6) will undergo cervical ripening, initiated by methods at the clinician’s discretion, with amniotomy the following day if the membranes have not ruptured and augmented, if necessary, with oxytocin, in accordance with French guidelines [54]. All other aspects of obstetric management will follow local standard practice.

Women allocated to the expectant management group will receive standard prenatal care until at least 41 weeks and 0 days, without elective induction unless a medical indication arises, as determined by the woman’s physician. Antepartum fetal surveillance will begin at 41 weeks, depending on the policies at each center and the woman’s preference as recommended in the French guidelines [50]. All women will be induced by 42 weeks at the latest, in accordance with these national guidelines [50]. As in the induction of labor arm, if cervical ripening is necessary for women allocated to the expectant management group, its methods will be determined by the clinician.

Adequate time for progression of labor will be allowed to women in both study arms who are undergoing induction of labor before a decision is made to consider the induction as “failed” and to proceed to cesarean delivery. As the ARRIVE trial protocol and French guidelines both suggest [17, 55], clinicians will be advised to consider that induction has “failed” if at least 12 h have elapsed since both rupture of membranes and use of oxytocin and the woman remains in latent labor (< 6 cm of cervical dilatation). However, the decision to perform a cesarean will remain at the discretion of the clinicians in charge of the delivery. Continuous fetal heart rate monitoring will be used throughout labor, and outpatient induction will be not allowed.

Women eligible for the RCT who decline randomization but agree to participate in the prospective cohort will be managed similarly to the women who enter the RCT.

Outcome measures

Primary outcome measure

The trial’s primary outcome will be the incidence of cesarean delivery, defined as any cesarean delivery performed after randomization, regardless of indication. We chose this primary outcome to confirm or refute in a different setting the main findings of the original ARRIVE trial, that is, the significantly lower cesarean rate among low-risk nulliparous women undergoing elective induction of labor at 39 weeks [17].

Statistical analysis

Data analysis and reporting will follow the CONSORT guidelines for RCTs. Analyses will be conducted by the trial statistician and investigators, blinded to group allocation. Baseline maternal and pregnancy characteristics, including the Bishop score at randomization, will be compared between the two groups. All randomized women will be analyzed in their assigned group, excluding those who withdraw consent or are deemed ineligible after randomization, regardless of protocol deviations. The main analysis of the primary and secondary outcomes will be performed in this population on an intention-to-treat basis. A per-protocol analysis will also be conducted, including women in the arm corresponding to the treatment they actually received (elective induction of labor between 39 weeks and 0 days and 39 weeks and 4 days or expectant management).

Descriptive statistics will be used to compare the women’s baseline characteristics. Quantitative variables will be expressed as means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges and compared by Student’s t-test or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, as appropriate. Categorical variables will be compared by Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. The effects of elective labor induction will be reported as mean differences with 95% confidence intervals for continuous variables and as relative risks with 95% confidence intervals for categorical outcomes. Binary outcomes will also be expressed as absolute risk differences with 95% confidence intervals. All tests will be two-sided, with a type I error rate of 5%.

Three prespecified subgroup analyses will explore the effect of elective induction on the primary outcome across key clinical subgroups, that is, according to their Bishop score at randomization (< 6 or ≥ 6), body mass index (< 30 or ≥ 30 kg/m2), or maternal age (< 35 or ≥ 35 years).

Feasibility

Most of the participating centers belong to a national network called the Groupe de Recherche en Obstétrique et Gynécologie (GROG) and have recently participated successfully in several RCTs. All have demonstrated their ability to manage large RCTs assessing perinatal outcomes [44–48, 60–64].

Sample size

We assumed a cesarean delivery rate of 15.0% in the expectant management group, based on recent unpublished data from several French tertiary centers, an estimate consistent with the overall national cesarean delivery rate of approximately 20% among all women, including both low- and high-risk populations [65]. By using a slightly lower — and therefore more conservative — baseline cesarean delivery rate than that reported in the original ARRIVE trial, the study ensures sufficient statistical power to detect a clinically meaningful difference between the two strategies.

To show a 20% relative reduction in the incidence of the primary outcome in the elective induction group — that is, a reduction from 15.0% to 12.0% or less — with α = 0.05, 1 − β = 0.80, and a two-sided test, we require a sample size of 2031 women per group (4062 overall).

Assuming that 3% of enrolled women may withdraw consent or have missing primary outcome data, the study plans to include 2100 women per group, for a total of 4200 women in the trial.

Timetable

Recruitment will take place in 32 French maternity units that account for approximately 106,000 annual deliveries. Based on an estimated 30% prevalence of eligible women, assuming that approximately half of them will be approached about the study and anticipating a 5% participation rate [27], this sample size is considered achievable within the planned six-year inclusion period.

Trial steering committee

A trial steering committee (TSC) will be set up and will be responsible for overall supervision of the trial. It will meet before the trial starts and then at least once per year until the trial is completed. The TSC will meet within a month of every DSMC meeting to consider its recommendations.

Safety consideration

Monitoring for adverse events covers only women enrolled in the RCT and their newborns (until hospital discharge); it does not cover participants in the prospective observational cohort.

All serious adverse events (SAE) (i.e. adverse events resulting in maternal, fetal or neonatal death, being life-threatening, requiring hospitalization or prolongation of hospitalization, causing an inability or significant or long-term disability, resulting in abnormality or congenital malformation) are notified without delay by investigators to the Sponsor using the SAE form.

Expected serious adverse events for this study include third or fourth-degree perineal lacerations, postpartum hemorrhage requiring invasive treatment, maternal infection necessitating surgery, thromboembolic events, and neonatal acidosis. Any other SAE resulting in maternal, fetal, or neonatal death, or which is life-threatening or medically significant, and considered as possibly related to the study procedure by the investigator and/or the Sponsor, will be classified as a Suspected Unexpected Serious Adverse Reactions (SUSAR) and reported by the sponsor to the ANSM (National Drug Safety Agency) and to the Ethic committee.

The DSMC is an independent data and safety monitoring committee made up of experts with no relation to the clinical trial — one epidemiologist, one gynecologist-obstetrician, one pharmacovigilance specialist, one midwife, and one pediatric neurologist. It will meet annually to examine the recruitment process, baseline data, SAE and any other safety issues. If the DSMC judges it necessary, it may recommend temporary suspension or early termination of the trial to the sponsor and the Scientific Committee.

Discussion

Potential and implementation of the findings

Since the publication of the ARRIVE trial in 2018, the rate of elective induction of labor has increased not only in the US but worldwide [36, 65, 66]. Elective induction at 39 weeks in low-risk nulliparous women appears to be one of the most effective strategies for decreasing primary cesarean deliveries: estimated risk reduction ranges from 7 to 24% compared with expectant management [17].

However, the issue of elective induction at 39 weeks extends beyond cesarean prevention. It raises broader ethical and societal concerns including the medicalization of childbirth and women’s autonomy in making decisions about their pregnancies, as well as their subjective experience and perception of childbirth [16]. The validity of extrapolating the ARRIVE results to populations, health care systems, and obstetric practices different from those in the US is unproven and uncertain. More generally, the translation of RCT findings into real-world clinical practice does not always yield the same results and may occasionally produce unintended or harmful effects [34, 35].

Given these uncertainties, further evidence is required to explore the benefits and potential risks of elective induction of labor at 39 weeks in low-risk nulliparous women in settings other than the US. The FRENCH-ARRIVE trial, a large multicenter RCT, aims to determine the effect of induction of labor at 39 weeks versus expectant management on the risk of cesarean delivery in the French obstetric context. In addition to the primary outcome (cesarean delivery), the trial will assess maternal outcomes — including women’s experience of and satisfaction with childbirth — as well as perinatal outcomes and the utilization of medical resources. A prospective observational cohort study of women who decline randomization will provide valuable insight into potential participation bias and will possibly enhance the external validity of the findings of this RCT.
