Changes in cardiovascular risk factors in patients with inflammatory arthritis following screening consultations—a Danish register-based cohort study
Authors: Julie Katrine Karstensen, Sonja Wehberg, Anja Bachmann, Kim Hørslev-Petersen, Jens Søndergaard, Anette Hvenegaard Kjeldgaard, Jette Primdahl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesPatients with chronic inflammatory arthritis (IA) (rheumatoid arthritis, psoriatic arthritis, and axial spondyloarthritis) have an increased risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD). Although the European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) recommends systematic cardiovascular risk (CVR) management in these patients, knowledge about whether CVR management programmes significantly reduce risk factors and, thus, overall CVR in patients with IA remains limited. This study evaluated changes in CVR factors following nurse-led screening consultations with a motivational approach for patients with IA based on the EULAR recommendations.MethodsIn a register-based cohort study, patients who had participated in at least 2 nurse-led screening consultations at the Danish Hospital for Rheumatic Diseases between September 2011 and September 2015 were included. Data on CVR risk factors were collected and analysed for changes from the first to the second screening consultation.ResultsThere were 595 patients included, with a mean of 683 days between the first and second consultations. The odds ratios (ORs) for improvement vs aggravation of CVR risk factors were significant for smoking, OR of 0.41 (95% CI, 0.17-0.92), and body mass index (BMI) ≥ 25 kg/m2, OR of 0.46 (95% CI, 0.25-0.83). Improvements in glycated haemoglobin level, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol level, and systolic blood pressure did not reach statistical significance. Conversely, significant aggravation vs improvement was evident for changes in waist circumference, (OR 1.71; 95% CI, 1.05-2.84) and exercise (OR 1.93; 95% CI, 1.41-2.68).ConclusionsAlthough lifestyle change is difficult, we observed significant improvements in smoking and BMI after patients attended nurse-led screening consultations.

INTRODUCTION

Patients with chronic inflammatory arthritis (IA), such as rheumatoid arthritis (RA), psoriatic arthritis (PsA), and axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA), have a significantly increased risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD) compared with the general population [1]. Patients with RA have double the risk of CVD, similar to patients with diabetes mellitus (DM) [2]. The increased risk is due to systemic inflammation and traditional risk factors for CVD [1]. The European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) recommends cardiovascular risk (CVR) assessments in patients with IA at least every 5 years [1].

In Europe, systematic CVR assessments have been implemented differently for patients with RA. For example, the preventive Cardio-Rheuma Clinic [3] in the rheumatology department [4] is a collaboration between the rheumatologist and the general practitioner (GP) or internist [[5], [6], [7]] or nurse-led screening for comorbidities in general [8] or specific nurse-led CVD screenings [9]. Despite the increased risk for patients with IA, implementing the EULAR recommendations for systematic CVR management is still suboptimal [7,[10], [11], [12]]. Approximately 50% of patients with IA still need initiation or adjustment of antihypertensive or lipid-lowering medications to reduce their CVR [9,10,13], and studies report insufficient CVR management in 60% to 91% of patients with RA with elevated blood lipid levels and 76% to 83% of patients with RA with hypertension [5,7,10].

Although CVR assessment and management in patients with IA may improve risk factors, studies have shown that 74% still have elevated risk factors after follow-up [7,8]. Knowledge of whether CVR management programmes initiate real, lasting change for patients with IA is limited. Heslinga et al [7] described that 42% of patients with RA reported one lifestyle change (exercised more, adapted diet, or lost weight) one year after screening. However, for patients with an indication that pharmacological interventions were required, only one-third of them initiated treatment [7]. In particular, a risk reduction was seen in patients who started antihypertensive or cholesterol-lowering treatment [7]. Gossec et al [8] showed an improvement regarding smoking cessation in patients with RA three years after CVR assessment, with 2% of the population quitting smoking. However, several risk factors were aggravated despite the improved frequency of assessment [8]. Molto et al [14] could not show improved CVR for patients with axSpA one year after a nurse-led comorbidity screening. In a previous register-based study, we identified that 34% of patients with RA, PsA, or axSpA had a high or very high risk for CV death within 10 years based on systematic CVR screening in accordance with the EULAR recommendations. Of these, 75% of patients consulted their GP as recommended, but only 38% received a relevant action to address their CV risk [15]. However, we do not yet know whether the patients actually changed their risk factors and thus decreased their overall CVR based on the systematic CVR assessment and management. We do not expect changes in how patients succeed with a lifestyle change after this registered study.

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate changes in CV risk factors following systematic nurse-led screening consultations based on the EULAR recommendations for patients with RA, PsA, and axSpA.

METHODS

Study design and population

The study was a register-based cohort study based on the follow-up of the same cohort we have published on previously [15]. Patients who had participated in at least two nurse-led screening consultations at the outpatient clinic at the Danish Hospital for Rheumatic Diseases, Sønderborg (formerly: King Christian X’s Hospital for Rheumatic Diseases, Gråsten), Denmark, between September 2011 and September 2015 were included. Patients with known DM were excluded from this study because of the well-established CVR management programme within primary care for this group. Patients with established CVD were included, as an initial screening revealed a lack of treatment for known risk factors [9,13].

Setting

The CVR screening and management followed the EULAR recommendations [1] and national guidelines [16]. The intervention was described previously [9,13,15]. The screening consultations providing data for this study were performed before the publication of the Systematic Coronary Risk Evaluation 2 (SCORE2) the Systematic Coronary Risk Evaluation for older persons (SCORE2-OP) and the national changes to recommended levels of lipids and blood pressure [[17], [18], [19]]. All patients with RA, axSpA, and PsA, aged 18 to 75 years, registered with the outpatient department, were invited to a 30-minute nurse-led screening consultation in addition to the patient’s regular clinical follow-up. Since 2011, patients with RA have been invited for screening consultations, and this was extended to patients with axSpA and PsA in 2012 [13]. Before the consultation, patients had blood tests taken to measure glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) (initially, we used fasting glucose [FG] level), triglycerides, total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol levels. During the consultation, the patient’s blood pressure, height, weight, and waist circumference were measured, and body mass index (BMI) was calculated. Waist circumference is measured midway between the lower rib margin and the iliac crest, 2 cm above the umbilicus [20]. The risk of death due to CVD within 10 years was calculated based on the SCORE system [21] (patients with axSpA or PsA) or the modified SCORE (mSCORE) system for patients with RA. The mSCORE system multiplies the risk-based SCORE system by 1.5 in accordance with the EULAR recommendations from 2016 [1]. Patients aged <40 or >65 years or with known CVD were assessed based on individual risk factors, as the SCORE system did not apply to them [21]. During the screening consultation, a dialogue between the patient and the nurse regarding the patient’s individual risk factors (habits regarding diet, smoking, physical activity, and alcohol) was initiated. Motivational interviewing [22] was applied to initiate lifestyle changes, ensuring agreement with the patient’s values and preferences. Moreover, information about opportunities to receive additional free support for lifestyle changes from the municipal health centres was provided. Written pamphlets about smoking cessation and fat-reduced diets were also offered. The patients entered data regarding lifestyle in the National Danish Quality Registry, DANBIO, before the consultations [23]. During the consultations, these data were validated through dialogue, and a nurse entered data on blood tests, BMI, waist circumference, blood pressure, and results from the blood tests. The patients were offered a printout of the results from the screening consultation, including a visual representation of their actual risk SCORE system to take home. If the patient’s risk was ≥5% or a single risk factor was substantially above the normal levels (systolic blood pressure [SBP] ≥ 145 mmHg, total cholesterol level ≥ 8 mmol/L, LDL cholesterol level ≥ 5 mmol/L, HbA1c level ≥ 42 mmol/mol, or FG level ≥ 6 mmol/L), the patient was encouraged to make a follow-up appointment with their GP. After the consultation, a copy of the note in the medical record was sent electronically to the patient’s GP. This note included data from the screening consultation, the patient’s goals for a lifestyle change, and information on whether the GP was expected to follow up and potentially initiate counselling or treatment. Patients with a risk score ≥ 5% or with known CVD or DM were invited to annual follow-up. Patients with low to moderate risk were invited for follow-up every second year or less frequently if they had no modifiable risk factors. If the patients were younger than 50 years, a screening consultation would be offered every 5 years unless there were modifiable risk factors. Patients who were 80 years old or older were not invited for follow-up unless they opted to continue in the programme.

Registry data

Data from the screening consultations, including self-reported information on known CVD, age, gender, and diagnosis, were retrieved from the DANBIO registry [24]. Data regarding educational level, labour market affiliation, marital status, and income were retrieved from Statistics Denmark (DST). The Danish healthcare system provides equal and universal access for all residents, offering free medical treatments with some exceptions, such as dental care and physiotherapy. In Denmark, 98% of the population is registered with a GP [25], who is responsible for coordinating care and addressing primary healthcare needs. Data from GP consultations or other services provided by the GP for each patient were retrieved from the National Health Service Register (NHSR). Data retrieved from DANBIO were merged with data from DST and NHSR via the unique identification number for each person from the Danish civil registration system [26].

Statistical analyses

Standard descriptive statistics were used to calculate the frequencies and percentages of categorical variables. Patient characteristics were summarised descriptively for all patients, patients with only one screening consultation, and patients with at least two screening consultations (Table 1). The time between the first and second screening consultations was calculated in days and reported with the mean ± SD. The primary outcomes were defined as the change in cardiovascular (CV) risk factors and risk score between baseline (first screening) and follow-up (second screening). In addition, we used the McNemar test to examine the hypothesis of equal probability of change, ie, among patients who changed from baseline to follow-up for each risk factor (Table 3). These analyses were limited to patients who had two screening consultations with no missing measurements regarding relevant risk factors. Ethics

Ethical approval and written informed consent were not required, as the data were available from a quality registry and national registers. Data management was registered by the Research and Innovation Organisation (RIO) (#10.106) at the University of Southern Denmark. Data were stored on servers from DST, and no identifiable information on an individual level was extracted. The study was registered with the Danish Clinical Quality Program—National Clinical Registries, RKKP, but written approval was unnecessary.

We excluded 82 patients due to missing data or living abroad, and 103 patients were excluded due to known DM at baseline. The baseline characteristics of the patients are presented in Table 1 for all 1,264 patients and by the number of screening consultations. The mean age of the total sample was 60.3 (± SD, 13.5) years.

The majority of the participants were women (67.1%) and 80.8% had a RA diagnosis. In the group with only one screening consultation, more patients self-reported CVD, and fewer were living with a partner compared with those who had attended a follow-up screening consultation. However, they were also more likely to engage in unhealthy behaviours such as smoking and excessive alcohol consumption, and more could not exercise due to their condition or did not exercise compared with those who participated in follow-up screening. Among those attending a follow-up screening consultation, the mean time between the screenings was 683 (± SD, 182) days (Table 1). Most patients did not change their risk factors, and the percentage of normal values was high for most risk factors (Table 3). Conditioning on a change in either direction, improvements were observed between the first and second screening consultations across several health parameters (Table 3). The primary outcomes of the study were changes in CV risk factors and risk scores between the first and second screening consultations. The most notable improvements were observed in smoking and BMI. We found a significant difference between the number who ceased smoking vs started smoking between the first and second screening consultations. Among patients who were smokers at the first consultation, 22 (17.4%; OR, 0.41; 95% CI, 0.17-0.92) had ceased smoking by the second consultation, while only 9 (1.9%) of initial nonsmokers had started (Table 3, Table 4). A statistically significant difference in the number of those who reduced vs increased their BMI scores was also observed (OR, 0.46; 95% CI, 0.25-0.83), as 39 out of 404 patients with a BMI > 25 kg/m2 at baseline (13.9%) had reduced their BMI scores to below 25 kg/m2 at the second screening (Table 3). At the same time, 9.5% of those with a BMI score below 25 kg/m2 at baseline had increased their BMI scores to above 25 kg/m2 at follow-up (Table 4).

As shown in the detailed results (Table 3), a significant worsening was observed in waist circumference: 26.6% of patients experienced aggravation, while 6.8% improved to below 80 cm for women and 94 cm for men at the second consultation. For those with measurements in the middle group between 80 and 87 cm (women) and 94 and 101 cm (men) (OR, 1.71; 95% CI, 1.05-2.84), 23 out of 152 patients (15.1%) reached the recommended level, but 24% aggravated it. For patients with a waist circumference of ≥88 cm (women) or ≥102 cm (men) (OR, 1.23; 95% CI, 0.79-1.93), 1.9% reached the recommended levels, and 12.9% had reduced their waist circumference to the middle level (Table 3). Exercise frequency showed deterioration (OR, 1.93; 95% CI, 1.41-2.68), with 118 out of 206 patients (57.3%) reporting that they had decreased their physical activity to less than 5 times a week, while 61 out of 389 (15.7%) had increased their physical activity to at least 5 times a week (Table 3, Table 4).

Regarding glycaemic markers, patients with HbA1c level ≥ 42 mmol/mol or FG level ≥6 mmol/L showed a trend towards improvement, which did not reach significance (OR, 0.64; 95% CI, 0.39-1.03) (Table 3). However, 47 out of the 74 (63.5%) of those who had blood glucose levels in the risk zone (42≤ HbA1c <48 mmol/mol) improved to a normal level, and 6 out of 12 (50%) of those with HbA1c levels ≥ 48 mmol/L (or FG levels ≥ 6 mmol/L) improved to a normal level (Table 4).

No significant improvement was observed in LDL cholesterol levels ≥ 5 mmol/L (OR 0.38; 95% CI, 0.11-1.15) (Table 3). Still, five out of 18 (27%) with LDL cholesterol levels ≥ 5 mmol/L at the first screening consultation had improved to below 5 mmol/L at the second consultation. Five patients out of 566 (0.9%) had increased their LDL-cholesterol level from below to 5 mmol/L or above at the second screening (Table 4).

In total, 48% reduced their SBP from between 145 and 160 mmHg to below 145 mmHg, whereas 10.1% with a normal SBP increased to between 145 and 160 mmHg, and 12 out of 445 (2.7%) aggravated to ≥160 mmHg (Table 3). Of the 54 patients with very high SBP (≥160 mmHg), 17 (31.5%) reached 145≤ SBP <160 and 22 (40.7%) reached a normal SBP < 145 mmHg (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

Implementing lifestyle changes in patients with chronic inflammatory diseases poses significant challenges. Many patients prefer pharmacological treatments over lifestyle modifications, and the coexistence of multiple unhealthy habits exacerbates the difficulty of adopting healthier behaviours [[27], [28], [29], [30]]. This study showed significant improvements in smoking and BMI scores after patients attended nurse-led screening consultations. However, there was aggravation on a group basis in waist circumference and exercise habits. The abdominal weight gain and reduced exercise frequency potentially increase CV risk. Patients participating in follow-up screening consultations seem to be committed to managing their health and are motivated by lifestyle changes, although we could not compare changes in risk factors between those who only attended one screening consultation and those who accepted a follow-up screening. Patients who had only attended one consultation tended to have slightly better risk scores and baseline measurements for some CVR factors, but were more likely to engage in unhealthy behaviours such as smoking and excessive alcohol consumption, and more of them could not exercise due to their condition or did not exercise compared with those who participated in follow-up screening. This may indicate problems in reaching all patients with a high risk of CVD.

Our results revealed that 17% of the patients who were smokers at the first screening consultation had ceased smoking by the second screening consultation. This is interesting, as the Danish Health Authorities have reported that there was no change in the percentage of smokers in the general Danish population from 2011 to 2015 [31]. Rheumatologists may have recommended smoking cessation, as smoking affects, among other things, the efficacy of the pharmacological treatment and the overall prognosis for the patients [1,32]. Therefore, there may be a cumulative effect, but we cannot differentiate between the effect of the intervention and external parameters, as there is no control group. The finding is still noteworthy, given the detrimental effects of smoking on CVR and how difficult it is for people to quit smoking. Smoking cessation has been associated with lower disease activity in patients with RA, more favourable blood lipid profiles, and less CVD [1,33,34]. Another positive outcome of the screening consultations is the indication that patients reduced their BMI score significantly. Some aggravated their waist circumference, while others improved. Unfortunately, we are not able to report the number of patients with aggravation vs improvement in different levels of BMI scores due to low numbers in some of the categories. BMI score and waist circumference are particularly relevant, as patients with RA and PsA often exhibit higher rates of overweight and obesity compared with the general population, contributing to comorbidities such as DM, hypertension, sleep apnoea, and CVD [28,29,[35], [36], [37], [38], [39]]. In addition, obesity is found to be associated with worse disease activity in patients with RA, physical disability, pain, and general health [35].

Most of these patients with IA are less physically active than the general population [9,13,28,29]. This is probably due to the impact of their arthritis. Still, 15% increased their physical activity level by exercising five days or more, as the Danish health authorities recommended at that time. The Danish health authorities reported that in 2021, 58% of adult Danes did not fulfil the adjusted recommendations for a minimum of 30 minutes of physical activity daily [40]. At follow-up, the percentage in our population with IA reached 58.4%, which is similar to the general population. We do not know whether inflammatory activity or other factors influenced the aggravation of the physical activity level in other patients, as we do not have data for disease activity.

Encouraging lifestyle changes to promote weight loss and increased physical activity is crucial, as these measures significantly improve overall health [41]. It is impressive that 72% of those with high levels of LDL cholesterol at the first screening consultation improved to normal levels at follow-up, but this result was only based on 18 participants with follow-up data. Diet plays a critical role in CVR management, with evidence indicating that individually tailored dietary advice, compared with brief recommendations on LDL cholesterol level reduction and healthier food choices, can lead to short-term improvements in lipid profiles among patients with inflammatory joint diseases [42]. This highlights the importance of healthcare professionals providing continuous support and engaging in a biopsychosocial approach to patient care throughout the change process. However, further studies are needed to evaluate the long-term sustainability of such interventions and whether repeated CVR risk assessments may lead to further improvements in lifestyle.

In the context of CVR, nurse-led CVR management programmes have emerged as a promising solution [8,[43], [44], [45], [46], [47]]. Previous studies have shown that 9 out of 10 invited patients actively participate in nurse-led screening consultations, highlighting the feasibility and acceptability of this approach [9,43]. In addition, nurse-led comorbidity assessments in patients with RA in France have demonstrated cost-effectiveness [46]. A French randomised trial comparing nurse-led CVR assessments to standard care found that lipid-lowering therapy initiation was more frequent in the nurse-led group, although there was no significant effect on smoking cessation or diet, and changes in lipid profile were not evaluated [47].

In a previous study on the CVR management programme at the Danish Hospital for Rheumatic Diseases, all eligible patients with RA under 75 years of age were offered CV screening consultations, with an impressive 92% of the 1,149 invited patients participating in the initial screening [43]. However, among those identified as having high to very high risk, only 58% attended a follow-up screening, highlighting the need for improved strategies to engage this subgroup, given their elevated risk [43]. These results highlight the gaps in follow-up. High-risk patients are advised to contact their GP following CVR screening at the Danish Hospital for Rheumatic Diseases, but the responsibility for scheduling an appointment lies with the patient. The Danish Society of Rheumatology's national guidelines state that patients with IA should ask their GP for a CVR assessment every 5 years [16]. This approach can contribute to social inequity in risk assessments and follow-up, as it requires patients to be proactive. Our previous register study showed that only one-third of the patients at high risk contacted their GP to initiate CVR-reducing interventions within 6 weeks, in contrast to 25% of low-risk patients who contacted their GP concerning the CVR assessment, despite no active suggestion or encouragement to do so [15]. This study could not determine why contact with the GP was needed, suggesting that clear communication pathways and strong systems for follow-up are needed. Integrating CVR screening into primary care could address some of these challenges. Weijers et al [6] demonstrated that reminder letters to GPs resulted in 88% of patients with RA undergoing CVR assessments. However, the study did not assess whether risks were estimated according to guidelines, whether treatment was initiated, or whether risk levels changed after the CVR assessments [6]. This study highlights the potential for improved integration of CVR management into GP practices, where clinical nurses trained in motivational interviewing could support patients’ lifestyle changes. In Denmark, patients with chronic diseases can be invited for annual check-ups by their GP. When fully implemented, this may further enhance continuity of care, potentially replacing hospital-based screening consultations with GP consultations for many patients. However, challenges remain in ensuring effective communication and coordination between rheumatology departments, GPs, and patients. Many patients with chronic diseases struggle to navigate the healthcare system, which can hinder continuity of care and contribute to social inequity [48]. Addressing these systemic issues requires improved communication strategies, such as shared electronic health records, standardised referral protocols, and automated follow-up reminders. Additionally, understanding patients’ values, beliefs, and social context is essential, as lifestyle changes are not solely the individual's responsibility but are deeply influenced by social inequities [43,49].

Effective CVR management should result in sustained modifications to risk factors, overall risk, improved health outcomes, and reduced morbidity and mortality [1]. While pharmacological interventions remain a cornerstone of CVR management, a biopsychosocial approach incorporating tailored support, patient education, and system-level improvements is important [50]. Future research and interventions should foster improved communication and collaboration among rheumatology departments, GPs, and community care providers to optimise CVR management for patients with chronic inflammatory diseases. It would be beneficial to have a shared data platform across hospitals and GPs to increase continuity of care. In addition, shared video consultations between the patient, the GP, and the screening staff could help increase continuity and assist patients at high risk in managing their risk further in primary care through a person-centred approach [16,51].

Although conditional logistic regression modelling was planned to adjust for covariates and estimate their association with change, we decided against this, as there were small changes to most risk factors. We expected risk factors with normal values at baseline to remain in the normal range, whereas risk factors with high levels at baseline were expected to change much more frequently. However, in this setting, it is not especially meaningful to test whether the probability of change differs between the two baseline groups.

Strengths and limitations

One strength of this study is that it is a well-defined cohort with systematically collected data since 2011. As we did not expect larger changes in risk profiles from the period 2011-2015 until now, we used the same dataset as in the previous register study. Patients screened early in the inclusion period were more likely to attend a follow-up screening consultation, thus providing more data for the study. High-risk patients were recommended to follow up after one year according to local guidelines, which increased their chances of participating in two consultations and contributing data. However, the actual timing of follow-up screenings in our data may not exactly match these intervals. Delays could have occurred if patients rescheduled or cancelled appointments due to personal reasons or disease-related circumstances. A limitation of this study is the absence of a control group, making it impossible to differentiate the effects of the intervention from natural progression over time. Additionally, limited data on changes in risk profiles in the general population make direct comparisons challenging. More patients who attended only one screening consultation reported known CVD and a more unhealthy lifestyle compared with those who attended follow-up, who tended to have slightly better risk scores, blood sugar levels, and BMI scores. These differences may constitute a bias in the results of our study. Furthermore, measurement of waist circumference may be subject to observer variability, as only a single trained staff member performed each assessment. Although measurements followed a standardised protocol, having multiple independent observers would have reduced potential subjectivity.

The small sample sizes in subgroup analyses, such as those evaluating patients with an HbA1c level ≥ 48 mmol/mol, FG level ≥ 7 mmol/L, or LDL cholesterol level ≥ 5 mmol/L, introduce a risk of a type 2 error [52]. The study may have failed to detect significant differences or associations due to insufficient statistical power. For example, only 12 patients for glycaemic control and 18 for LDL cholesterol level control make it difficult to draw definitive conclusions. Furthermore, the small sample size limited the possibility of performing subgroup analyses, such as stratification by sex or diagnosis (RA vs axSpA), or investigating specific relationships, for example, whether patients who stopped smoking were more likely to experience increases in BMI score or waist circumference. Nevertheless, patient interviews suggest that some individuals use the information and support from the consultations to modify their risk factors [43]. Most importantly, the screenings increased patients’ awareness of their CVR, particularly in light of their diagnosis [43]. Even though treating inflammation is an essential aspect of CVR reduction, we could not provide information about disease activity in our patients, highlighting another study limitation [1]. Furthermore, data on medication were limited, and longitudinal treatment information was therefore incomplete. Additionally, sample size limitations prevented analyses over time; as a result, data on medications are not included. Denmark was considered a CVD high-risk country until 2012 [20]. Consequently, different schemes have been used to estimate the mSCORE system between 2011 and 2015. As these changes were not a focus of this study, we have not included changes in the total mSCORE or SCORE systems. Lastly, hypertension in this study was defined using a threshold of >145 mmHg SBP, based on guidelines at the time of data collection. Current recommendations define hypertension as ≥140 mmHg SBP, which may slightly change the number of patients classified as hypertensive [17,18]. Similarly, CV risk was assessed using the SCORE system, as these were the recommended risk scores during the study period; while the SCORE2 system has since been introduced, the earlier scores remain valid for assessing changes over time and allow comparison with previous studies. Recommendations provided to patients during the screening consultations were also based on these contemporaneous guidelines, reflecting the relevant clinical guidance at the time.

Conclusion

This study highlighted the potential benefits of nurse-led screening consultations in reducing CV risk factors in patients with RA, PsA, and axSpA. The observed increase in smoking cessation and reductions in BMI, blood sugar levels, and LDL cholesterol suggest that a motivational interviewing approach may have contributed to patients’ adopting healthier lifestyles. However, we cannot rule out natural change over time, as there was no control group. These findings highlighted the importance of integrating systematic CVR assessment and management into routine rheumatology care. Systematically targeting lifestyle changes can significantly improve patients’ health outcomes and potentially reduce the long-term CV morbidity associated with these inflammatory conditions.
