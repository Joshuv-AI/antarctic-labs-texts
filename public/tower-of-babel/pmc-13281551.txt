Assessment of parental awareness of diabetic ketoacidosis in children with diabetes: a cross-sectional study at a tertiary referral center in Iran
Authors: Homa Ilkhanipoor, Nima Hosseini, Hamide Barzegar, Hossein Moravej, Leila Salarian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) is a life-threatening complication of type 1 diabetes (T1DM) and a leading cause of pediatric mortality. Prevention depends on caregivers’ ability to recognize early signs and initiate proper sick-day management. However, data on DKA awareness among Iranian parents remains limited. This study aimed to evaluate both subjective self-ratings and objective calculated knowledge regarding DKA among parents of children with T1DM.MethodThis cross-sectional study was conducted among 217 parents at a pediatric diabetes clinic in Shiraz, Iran. A validated questionnaire (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.8), adapted through forward-backward translation, assessed demographics, disease duration, HbA1c, and DKA knowledge. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, Kruskal-Wallis tests, and Spearman’s correlations (rs) to identify predictors of awareness.ResultsMost participants were mothers (80.6%), and 38.7% reported DKA as the initial presentation at diagnosis. Subjective knowledge scores (0–10) were 1.86 ± 3.07 for mothers and 0.89 ± 2.16 for fathers (P = 0.06). Mothers demonstrated significantly higher calculated objective awareness than fathers (P = 0.007). Higher objective knowledge significantly correlated with higher education levels (rs=0.280, P = 0.01), longer disease duration (P = 0.01), and prior DKA admission (P = 0.02). Awareness showed no significant relationship with the child’s most recent HbA1c levels (P = 0.98).ConclusionParental DKA knowledge is critically inadequate. As DKA mortality is preventable through early detection, targeted educational interventions and accessible resources are urgently needed to empower parents and improve clinical outcomes for children with T1DM.

Background

The incidence of autoimmune type 1 diabetes mellitus(T1DM) is increasing [1, 2]. Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), a serious complication of T1DM, occurs when insulin levels drop and counterregulatory hormones rise. This imbalance results in elevated blood sugar, increased osmolality, ketone production, and acid buildup in the blood, which can lead to a life-threatening condition if not treated promptly [3]. DKA is the initial presentation of T1DM in 15–70% of children and occurs in 1–10% of those previously diagnosed with T1DM [4].

DKA can manifest with multiorgan involvements. Common presentations include dehydration, tachypnea, Kussmaul respiration, a fruity odor of ketones on the breath, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain. Most children with DKA experience severe dehydration, often presenting in a state of shock, which may also lead to acute kidney injury [5]. If unrecognized, progressive dehydration and acidosis may cause altered mental status, ranging from lethargy to coma [6].

A study showed that a brief educational intervention and a printed teaching tool successfully improved patients’ and parents’ knowledge of recognizing and managing early signs of ketosis. The intervention significantly improved sick days management and knowledge about DKA symptoms and contributed to a reduction in emergency department visits [7]. This underscores the importance of raising awareness of DKA symptoms, as timely visits to a physician can potentially prevent the onset of DKA or reduce its severity.

While some studies have examined the awareness of adult diabetic patients regarding DKA, the results have been unsatisfactory [8–10]. However, research on parents’ knowledge of DKA remains limited. It is essential to assess awareness levels across various countries to develop effective prevention strategies and improve early recognition of DKA. This study aims to assess the knowledge of parents of children with T1DM regarding diabetic ketoacidosis.

Method

Study population

This study was conducted on pediatric diabetic patients registered at the Pediatric Diabetes Clinic in Shiraz, a referral center in southern Iran which is affiliated with Shiraz University of Medical Sciences. where p = 0.5, q = 0.5, Z = 1.96, and N = 500 (the estimated number of patients visiting the clinic annually). Based on these parameters, the calculated sample size was 217.

The inclusion criteria for this study were parents of pediatric patients with T1DM who were willing to participate in the interview and complete the questionnaire. The exclusion criteria included parents who were unwilling to participate or provided incomplete data (defined as three or more unanswered questions).

Data collection

Data were collected through interviews with the parents to evaluate their knowledge and experiences related to DKA. The questionnaire used in this study was adapted from a previous study [8]. To ensure linguistic and cultural equivalence, a forward-backward translation procedure was performed. First, the questionnaire was translated into Persian by two independent translators fluent in both English and Persian. Subsequently, the Persian version was back-translated into English by a third independent translator to ensure accuracy. Any discrepancies were resolved by a panel of experts.

The questionnaire was evaluated by three endocrinologists and one statistician, ensuring that the items were relevant and representative of the constructs related to DKA awareness. Before the main study, a pilot test was conducted on 15 parents of pediatric patients with T1DM to assess the clarity, understandability, and face validity of the items. Participants in the pilot study confirmed that the questions were clear and easy to understand. These participants were not included in the final sample. The reliability of the questionnaire was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, which measures the internal consistency of the instrument. A Cronbach’s alpha of 0.8 was obtained, indicating good reliability.

The interview was conducted by a trained medical doctor, who also measured the children’s weight and height.

The questionnaire covered several areas: demographic information of both the parent and the child, the duration of the child’s diabetes, and details regarding medication usage. It also included questions about the signs and symptoms of DKA, the parents’ experiences with DKA, their knowledge of appropriate actions during sick days, and recent clinical metrics, such as the most recent HbA1c level, the date of the last physician visit, and the frequency of blood sugar and ketone checks.

Parents’ subjective perception of their DKA knowledge was measured using a single-item self-rating scale. Participants were asked to rate their overall knowledge of DKA on a visual analogue scale ranging from 0 (no knowledge) to 10 (excellent knowledge). This score represented the participants’ self-estimated competence before answering objective questions. Objective awareness was determined using a “Calculated Awareness Score” adapted from the scoring system of Hepprich et al. [8] This score was derived from two specific sections of the questionnaire: DKA symptoms (12 items) and DKA causes (7 items).For every correct typical symptom (e.g., thirst, nausea) or typical cause (e.g., infection, missed insulin) selected, 1 point was awarded. To account for random selection, 1 point was subtracted for every atypical or incorrect symptom (e.g., hearing problems, increased appetite) or cause (e.g., increased physical activity) selected. The net points were summed and normalized to a final scale of 0 to 10, where 10 represented perfect objective knowledge. The questionnaire also inquired about the parents’ willingness to seek additional information on DKA and their preferred methods for obtaining this information.

The questions varied in format, comprising open-ended questions, multiple-choice questions, and binary (yes/no/I don’t know) options. Additionally, the attending physician provided a subjective assessment of each parent’s overall knowledge of T1DM, rated on a scale from 0 (no knowledge) to 10 (excellent knowledge), without additional commentary. Although this was a global clinical impression rather than a rubric-based assessment, reliability was maintained by having a single trained physician conduct all interviews and assign all ratings, thereby eliminating inter-observer bias.

An English version of the final questionnaire is available in the online supplemental material.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered into SPSS version 26. The normality of quantitative variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Descriptive data were analyzed using frequencies and percentages, while quantitative data were analyzed using mean ± standard deviation (SD). Since the awareness scores and self-reported awareness data were not normally distributed, non-parametric tests were used. The total awareness score was calculated based on the correct signs and symptoms selected by the parents. The relationship between parents’ subjective knowledge scores and their calculated objective awareness scores was assessed using Spearman’s correlation to determine the alignment between self-perception and actual knowledge. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics

This study was approved by the Shiraz University of Medical Sciences with the ethics code IR.SUMS.MED.REC.1402.517.

Results

A total of 217 parents participated in the study, of which 175 (80.6%) were mothers and 42 (19.4%) were fathers. The mean age of the parents was 40.43 ± 6.20 years (ranging from 26 to 60 years). Regarding their education levels, 3 (1.4%) were illiterate, 65 (30%) had education below a high school diploma, 77 (35.5%) had a high school diploma, and 72 (33.2%) had education above a high school diploma.

Among the patients, 107 (49.3%) were female and 110 (50.7%) were male. Additionally, 41 parents (18.9%) were unsure whether their child had ever experienced DKA. Most patients (216, 99.5%) were on aspartate and glargine insulin, while 1 (0.5%) used NPH and regular insulin. Regarding the last physician visit, 112 (51.6%) were seen less than 6 months ago, 65 (30%) between 7 and 12 months, 26 (12%) between 13 and 24 months, and 14 (6.5%) more than 2 years ago.

Patient characteristics

Parents rated their knowledge about DKA on a scale from 0 to 10, where 0 represented no knowledge and 10 represented complete knowledge. The mean score was 1.68 ± 2.93 (ranging from 0 to 10). The mean score for mothers was 1.86 ± 3.07 (0–10) and for fathers was 0.89 ± 2.16 (0–8).

Regarding the causes of DKA, 155 (71.4%) parents did not know any cause. Only 2 (0.9%) mentioned infection, 3 (1.3%) reported both infection and uncontrolled blood sugar, 36 (16.6%) mentioned uncontrolled blood sugar, 4 (1.8%) missing a dose of insulin, and 15 (6.9%) mentioned both uncontrolled blood sugar and missing a dose of insulin. The last ketone check was done less than a month ago by 6 (2.8%) parents, less than 3 months ago by 4 (1.8%), less than 6 months ago by 8 (3.7%), less than 12 months ago by 3 (1.4%), more than 12 months ago by 17 (7.8%), and never by 179 (82.5%). Regarding the frequency of checking ketones, none reported doing so weekly or monthly. Seven (3.2%) checked at least once a year, 14 (6.5%) when blood sugar was high, 11 (5.1%) rarely, and 185 (85.3%) did not know.

When asked when they should check ketones, 14 (6.5%) responded during infection or illness, 1 (0.5%) after increased physical activity, 2 (0.9%) after a decreased dose of insulin, and 20 (9.2%) when signs and symptoms of DKA are present. No one chose surgery and 177 (81.6%) said never.

According to the blood sugar level at which they should check ketones, 2 (0.9%) answered 271–360 mg/dL, 11 (5.1%) said more than 360 mg/dL, and 204 (94%) said never. No one selected levels below 270 mg/dL.

When asked how they check ketones at home, 1 (0.5%) said blood, 10 (4.6%) reported urine, and 206 (94.9%) did not check at all.

The mean frequency of daily blood sugar checking was 3.46 ± 1.84 times per day. Two (0.9%) of the patients used a sensor, while the others reported different frequencies of checking blood sugar daily: 12 (5.5%) never checked, 27 (12.4%) checked once, 26 (12%) checked twice, 46 (21.2%) three times, 44 (20.3%) four times, 16 (7.4%) five times, and 44 (20.3%) more than five times per day.

Discussion

This study highlights the significant gaps in parental knowledge regarding DKA symptoms and the necessary actions for managing children with T1DM. The majority of parents were unaware of the correct signs, symptoms, and appropriate actions to take during special conditions, such as illness or high blood sugar levels. Mothers demonstrated slightly higher awareness than fathers, and the overall level of knowledge was low. Importantly, parental awareness was significantly associated with several key factors. We found a moderate positive correlation between education level and self-reported awareness (rs= 0.384, p < 0.001), as well as objective calculated awareness (rs= 0.280, p = 0.01). This suggests that while higher education aids in actual knowledge, it has an even stronger impact on a parent’s confidence in their knowledge. Additionally, the duration of the child’s disease (rs = 0.175, p = 0.01) and the frequency of daily blood sugar monitoring (rs= 0.149, p = 0.03) were positively linked to higher awareness scores, reflecting the learning-by-doing effect over time.

The overall knowledge about DKA in this study was unsatisfactory. This finding is consistent with other studies that report varying levels of parental awareness. For example, Hamed et al. [11] and Alhommod et al. [12] both found that parental awareness was low and suboptimal, respectively. Similarly, Alsaedi et al. found that most parents lacked sufficient knowledge about DKA [13]. In contrast, Al Kaabba et al. reported that the majority of parents had a good understanding of DKA [14].

Another finding in this study was that the vast majority of parents (86.6%) reported utilizing carbohydrate (CHO) calculation for meal planning. This rate is considerably higher than those reported in similar regional studies and likely reflects the intensive educational focus of the Shiraz University of Medical Sciences pediatric diabetes clinic, where nutritional training is a cornerstone of the standard care protocol. However, a high rate of reported use does not necessarily imply high calculation accuracy or mastery. The observed disparity between the high adherence to daily CHO counting and the overall unsatisfactory level of DKA awareness suggests that parental education may be skewed toward routine metabolic management rather than acute emergency readiness. As noted by Alsaedi et al., parents often demonstrate better performance in repetitive daily tasks but lack the critical ‘survival skills’ needed to manage acute complications like DKA [13]. This highlights the urgent need for clinical educators to ensure that nutritional proficiency is balanced with comprehensive training on sick-day rules and the recognition of DKA warning signs.

In our study, several factors were significantly associated with parental awareness of DKA symptoms and management. Specifically, higher education levels showed a significant positive correlation with objective awareness, suggesting that more educated parents may have better access to information and a greater ability to comprehend complex medical guidance. Parents whose children had a longer duration of the disease also demonstrated higher awareness, likely due to their increased exposure to T1DM management over time. A history of DKA admission further heightened awareness, as these parents may become more vigilant following a critical event. Additionally, the frequency of blood sugar monitoring was positively correlated with awareness, indicating that parents actively involved in daily monitoring tend to have a deeper understanding of their child’s condition. Notably, we found no significant relationship between DKA awareness and the child’s most recent HbA1c level, suggesting that theoretical knowledge of DKA does not necessarily guarantee better long-term glycemic control.

These findings underscore the importance of targeted educational interventions, especially for parents with lower education levels or less experience managing the disease. Consistent with our results, factors such as being a mother, having higher education [11], and a previous history of DKA [15] were also associated with greater knowledge in previous literature. However, unlike the findings of Hamed et al. [11], our study did not find a significant association between parental age and awareness. This suggests that in our specific population, educational background and practical experience with the disease are more critical predictors of awareness than the parent’s chronological age. This highlights the need for tailored educational strategies to reach all parents, regardless of their background or experience.

We found no significant relationship between parental DKA awareness and the child’s most recent HbA1c level. This indicates that while parents may possess the theoretical knowledge to identify and manage DKA, this knowledge alone may not be sufficient to improve long-term glycemic control, which is often influenced by a complex array of socioeconomic and physiological factors.

In this study, the majority of participants were mothers, and their knowledge was significantly greater than that of fathers. This disparity in representation may have influenced the results. Consistent with our findings, Alhomood et al. identified being a father as a risk factor for having less knowledge about DKA [12]. Research has shown that, although fathers initially receive attention in educational efforts following the onset and diagnosis of T1DM, they often take on a more peripheral role over time, losing early significance in managing the condition [16]. This transition may explain why some studies report lower overall parenting stress among fathers compared to mothers; however, paternal stress is often qualitatively different, stemming from a perceived lack of competence in managing medical emergencies [17]. Our findings suggest that this lower level of involvement and the resulting lack of hands-on experience directly contribute to the lower DKA awareness scores observed among fathers. This creates a critical gap in the ‘safety net’ at home, as fathers may be less equipped to recognize early warning signs of DKA when the primary caregiver is unavailable. These insights highlight the need for healthcare professionals to focus on the experiences of both parents in managing pediatric diabetes, promoting paternal involvement, and supporting their psychosocial needs.

The importance of raising awareness about DKA is often overlooked. Most parents in this study strongly desired to learn more about DKA, particularly through brochures and online resources. A campaign in Germany over three years successfully reduced the DKA rate in children from 28% to 16% [18]. Similarly, another campaign saw a decrease in DKA rates from 48% in 2010 to 39% in 2014, with cases of severe DKA dropping from 26.1% in 2005–2010 to 15.8%. These examples highlight the critical need for well-planned awareness initiatives to educate parents about DKA [19].

One of the strengths of this study is its focus on an important yet under-explored aspect of pediatric T1DM—parental awareness and knowledge, which plays a critical role in disease management and outcomes. Another strength is the large sample size, which provides statistically robust results and enhances the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, this is the first study in Iran focusing on parents of pediatrics with T1DM.

However, there were several limitations. First, some parents were unwilling to participate, which may have introduced selection bias. Additionally, the study relied on self-reported data, which may have affected the accuracy of parents’ recollections of their experiences and knowledge. Furthermore, the majority of participants were mothers, limiting the generalizability of the findings to fathers, who may have different levels of knowledge and involvement in T1DM management.

For future research, we recommend interviewing both parents to compare their knowledge and involvement in managing their child’s T1DM. This would offer a more comprehensive understanding of family dynamics in disease management. Additionally, implementing awareness campaigns to educate parents about T1DM management and comparing DKA rates before and after the campaign would provide valuable insights into the effectiveness of educational interventions in reducing DKA occurrences. It could also be beneficial to explore how different educational approaches (e.g., online resources vs. in-person sessions) influence parental knowledge and engagement. Finally, conducting longitudinal studies would help evaluate how parental awareness evolves over time and its long-term impact on T1DM outcomes, such as DKA rates and overall disease management.

In conclusion, our findings indicate that parental awareness of DKA is insufficient and requires greater attention and educational efforts. The importance of checking keton and sick days management should be clarified for parents. To address this gap, targeted strategies should be developed to improve parents’ understanding of the signs, symptoms, diagnosis, and treatment of DKA. These strategies could include educational campaigns, easily accessible resources such as brochures and online tools, and routine counseling sessions during medical visits. Enhancing parental awareness is critical to improving early detection and management of DKA, ultimately reducing its incidence and improving outcomes for children with T1DM.
