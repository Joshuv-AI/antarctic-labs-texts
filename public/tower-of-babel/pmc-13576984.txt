Knowledge of early signs and risk factors, and attitude towards breast cancer among women in Dodoma, Tanzania: a community-based cross-sectional study
Authors: James J Yahaya, Advera I Ngaiza, Angelina A Joho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionImprovement of knowledge and attitude helps to improve the prevention of breast cancer (BC).AimThis study aimed to determine the level of knowledge of early signs and risk factors, and attitude toward BC.Materials and methodsThis was a cross-sectional study that was conducted in Dodoma city, Tanzania among women aged ≥18 years. Mean values were used as cutoff points for adequate knowledge and positive attitude. Multivariable analysis was used to determine the predictors of knowledge and attitude. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsA total of 354 women were analyzed. Having 35–49 years was associated with increased chance of having adequate knowledge of early signs and risk factors for BC compared to having ≥50 years of age (adjusted odds ratio (AOR) = 2.11, 95% CI = 1.87–5.09, p = 0.040) and positive attitude toward BC (AOR = 2.87, 95% CI = 1.14–7.27, p = 0.040). Having secondary education had a reduced chance of having adequate knowledge compared to having a tertiary level of education (AOR = 0.84, 95% CI = 0.14–0.97, p = 0.032).ConclusionThis study has shown a moderately low level of adequate knowledge of early signs and risk factors for BC. Also, younger age was associated with adequate knowledge and positive attitude toward BC.Keywords: Breast cancer, knowledge, attitude, early signs, risk factors

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) is the most common malignancy affecting women [1], and it is also ranked second after lung cancer in causing cancer-related deaths globally [2]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) report of 2020, there were 2.3 million women diagnosed with BC and 685,000 deaths worldwide [2,3]. Furthermore, the same report indicated that at the end of 2020, there were 7.8 million women alive who were diagnosed with BC in the past 5 years, which makes BC the world’s most prevalent malignancy [4]. Although the incidence of BC in low-and middle-income countries (LMICs) particularly Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) is lower than that of high-income countries (HICs), the gap has recently been observed to be closing [4–8]. It has been shown that approximately 627,000 BC-related deaths in 2018 globally, a significant proportion of them (15%) were from SSA [9]. In Tanzania, as it is for many other LMICs, the incidence of BC is on increase and it is estimated that by 2030, the burden of the disease is projected to reach 82% [10].

Both knowledge and attitude have an influence on early diagnosis and good prognosis of BC patients. However, low level of knowledge and having negative attitude toward health seeking behavior is attributed to high prevalence and poor outcome of BC patients especially, in LMICs [11]. Since early detection hinges on stage shifting toward earlier stage at diagnosis, lack of awareness and low level of knowledge of BC regarding early signs and risk factors both act as contributing factors to late diagnosis leading to poor prognosis of BC in the majority of LMICs [12,13]. Lack of knowledge of BC has also been found to be attributed to a number of folklores, which lead to increased burden of the disease and high mortality due to diagnostic delays [14]. Furthermore, Positive attitude toward health involves emotions, personality characteristics, and strong intention to engage in health promoting behaviors [15]. Conversely, negative attitude in health refers to strong unfavorable emotions and beliefs toward health-seeking behavior. Positive attitude plays a major role in determining health seeking behavior of an individual [16]. Positive attitude toward health-seeking behavior is significantly affected by the level of knowledge of an individual [17]. Positive attitude among women helps to improve screening practices for BC, which in turn increases the chances of detection of asymptomatic BC [18–20]. Improvement of knowledge equips individuals with facts and understanding of health related matters whereas improvement of positive attitude enables individuals to have strong intention and emotion toward health-seeking behaviors.

There is scarcity of studies addressing the importance of knowledge and attitude for the reason of modifying and improving health seeking behavior especially on BC among women in Tanzania. This contributes to late detection and poor clinical outcomes of patients with BC in the country. Therefore, the current study aimed to determine the level of adequate knowledge of early signs and risk factors for BC and positive attitude toward BC among women. Secondly, the study also determined the factors associated with the two domains.

Materials and methods

Study design, area, and study population

This was a cross-sectional analytical community-based study. The study was conducted in Dodoma city which is located in Dodoma region for six months (July-December 2020). We included women from different streets in Dodoma city. The reason for selecting Dodoma city is that, the city is one of the fastest growing cities in Tanzania after the shifting of the headquarter from Dar-es-salaam to Dodoma in July 2016. This has resulted in increased population as well as lifestyle modification. The women were aged ≥18 years and they were living in the study area for at least 1 year, with no known diagnosis of BC, and agreed voluntarily to participate in the study. All women who were very sick and mentally ill and those who refused to participate in the study were excluded from the analysis.

Sample size and sampling method

The estimated sample size was 354 which was calculated using the Kish Leslie formula as previous [21] based on the following assumptions: standard normal variable (z score) of 1.96 at 95% confidence interval, proportion (p) of women who were knowledgeable on risk factors of BC of 50% in Bangladesh [22] and margin of error of 5%, with an adjustment rate of 15% for non-response. Dodoma city was selected conveniently from the seven districts of the Dodoma region from which 5 wards were selected randomly. A total of 10 streets were selected from the wards using systematic sampling method. From the selected streets, a total of 368 households were selected using systematic sampling method as well. However, the first house was selected randomly and only one participant was selected from every household. The study participants were selected from the households using a simple random sampling method (lottery replacement method). Pieces of paper were cut and labeled with numbers 0 and 1 and placed in a container which had a cover. After vigorously shaking the container to allow mixing up of the pieces of paper, the container was opened and the pieces of paper were placed on the surface to allow the participants to pick. Those who picked papers labeled 0 and 1 were excluded and included, respectively (Figure 1).

A flowchart illustrating the selection of the study participants. The response rate for the study was 96.2% (354/368).

Measurement of variables

Knowledge

Knowledge of early signs of BC was assessed using a total of 7 items which had two responses (“yes/no”). Each correct response was scored 1 point. For internal consistency of the reliability of the tool, factor analysis statistics were performed to reduce the weak items. The Kaiser-Mayer-Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy was 0.885. Bartlett’s test of sphericity was 1010.7 and p-value <0.001. The total variance explained (initial Eigenvalues) had two components with a cumulative percentage of 56.1. Knowledge scores did not assume normal distribution with the Shapiro-Wilk test value of statistic of 0.924 and a p-value <0.001. The mean score was considered to be the cutoff point for those with adequate and inadequate knowledge. Therefore, participants with less than the mean score were considered to have inadequate knowledge and vice versa.

Assessment of knowledge of early signs and risk factors of BC was done using 14 questions with two responses (“Yes/No”). Each correct response was scored 1 point and incorrect response was scored 0. The overall knowledge point was considered for total scores ranging from 0 to 14 points. The mean score was considered to be the cutoff point whereby participants with score equal or above the mean were considered to have “adequate knowledge” and participants who scored below the mean value were labeled as having “inadequate knowledge” as it was done previously [23]. Therefore, participants with less than the mean score were considered to have inadequate knowledge and vice versa. For internal consistency of the reliability of the tool, we performed factor analysis statistics in order to reduce the weak items. The Kaiser-Mayer-Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy was 0.9. Bartlett’s test of sphericity was 2196.0 and p-value <0.001. The total variance explained (initial Eigenvalues) had two components with a cumulative percentage of 54.6. Knowledge scores did not assume normal distribution with the Shapiro-Wilk test value of the statistic of 0.954 and p-value <0.001.

Attitude

Attitude was assessed using 14 items which were measured on 5 points Likert scale (1-strongly agree, 2-agree, 3-neither agree nor disagree, 4-disagree, and 5-strongly disagree). Then we dichotomized the responses “strongly agree” and “agree” as “positive attitude” whereas the responses “neither agree nor disagree”, “disagree”, and “strongly disagree” were regarded as “negative attitude”. Then during analysis, 1 and 0 scores were given for a response indicating “positive” and “negative” attitude, respectively. The mean value was considered to be the cutoff point for categorizing attitude as “positive” and “negative”. The overall attitude was considered for total scores ranging from 14 to 70 points. All participants with scores equal or greater than the mean score were considered to have positive attitude, whereas participants with scores less than the mean value were considered as having negative attitude toward BC as it was done previously [24]. In assessing for internal consistency of reliability of the tool, factor analysis statistics were performed so as to reduce the weak items. The Kaiser-Mayer-Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy was 0.7, Bartlett’s test of sphericity was 495.9, and p-value <0.001. The total variance explained (initial Eigenvalues) had 5 components with a cumulative percentage of 54.1. Attitude scores did not assume normal distribution with the Shapiro-Wilk test value of the statistic of 0.977 and p-value <0.001.

Data collection and research tool

A face-to-face structured interviewer-administered questionnaire was used for data collection. The questionnaire was adapted from a previous study [22] and modified to make it easily understandable including translating it into Swahili local language. A person who is well versed in medical language using both English and Swahili was involved in translating the questionnaire. Cronbach’s alpha coefficients 0.888 and 0.714 were calculated for determining the internal consistency of the reliability of the questionnaire for the assessment of knowledge and attitude, respectively. The obtained Cronbach’s alpha coefficient is acceptable as per Taber [25] study which recommends the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of not less than 0.7 for optimal agreement of the assessed items or questions in a study. Validation of the questionnaire was done after conducting a pilot study of 20 participants from the same areas where study participants were obtained.

The questionnaire had questions on sociodemographic characteristics, and knowledge and attitude toward early signs and risk factors of BC. Two research assistants (registered nurses) were involved in the process of data collection after being familiarized with the process for two days. Face to face interview method was used to collect the required data, which took approximately 20 minutes for each participant. Each participant was interviewed far from the rest of the family members in order to avoid sharing of answers.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS software version 25.0. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize both categorical and continuous variables in frequencies and percentages and mean ± standard deviation (SD), respectively. Inferential statistics involved the use of binary logistic regression analysis in determining the factors associated with knowledge and attitude toward early signs and risk factors of BC. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to determine the factors associated with knowledge and attitude by adjusting for all independent variables. Odds ratios (ORs) at 95% confidence intervals (CI) were determined for assessing the predictability of the independent variables. A two-tailed p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Also, majority 62.7% (222/354) of the study participants had family income below 100,000 Tanzanian shilling (TZS) per month which is equivalent to <1.5 USD per day as per the International Poverty Line criteria.

Participants’ overall knowledge of early signs, and risk factors, and attitude toward of BC

The study participants’ overall knowledge of early signs, risk factors, and attitude toward BC are presented in Figure 2. The Mean score of knowledge of early signs of BC was 2.4 ± 2.3 (range 0–7), and adequate knowledge level of early signs of BC was observed in less than half 43.5% (154/354) of the study participants. Also, the mean score for knowledge level of risk factors of BC was 4.1 ± 3.8 (range 0–14), and less than half 37.3% (132/354) of the participants had adequate knowledge level. Of all the participants, less than half 45.8% (162/354) had adequate overall knowledge level of early signs and risk factors of BC above with mean score of 6.5 ± 4.8 (range 0–21). Concerning attitude toward BC, over half 54.2% (192/354) of the participants had positive attitude toward BC and the mean score was 7.6 ± 2.2 (range 0–14).

Association of sociodemographic characteristics with knowledge of early signs and risk factors of BC

During multivariate regression analysis after adjusting for age, education level, family income, occupation, and marital status; age and education level remained the sociodemographic factors that were associated with overall knowledge of the study participants on early signs and risk factors of BC. The study participants who were aged between 35 and 49 years had increased odds of being knowledgeable on early signs and risk factors of BC compared to women who were ≥50 years old (AOR = 2.11, 95% CI = 1.87–5.09, p = 0.040). Also, women who had secondary level of education had reduced odds of having adequate knowledge of early signs and risk factors of BC compared to women who had attained tertiary level of education (AOR = 0.84, 95% CI = 0.14–0.97, p = 0.032) (Table 5).

Association of sociodemographic characteristics with attitude toward BC

Under multivariate analysis, only age remained statistically significant. Women who were younger than 50 years (18–34 years) were almost 3 times more likely to have a positive attitude toward BC compared to their counterpart women aged ≥50 years (95% CI = 1.14–7.27, p = 0.026). Also, women who were aged 35–49 years were almost 2.5 times more likely to have a positive attitude toward BC compared to women who were aged ≥50 years although the statistical significance was marginal (AOR = 2.46, 95% CI = 0.94–6.40, p = 0.066). Furthermore, women who had inadequate knowledge of BC had reduced odds of having a positive attitude toward BC compared to their counterpart women who had adequate knowledge but the association did not reach statistical significance (AOR = 0.84, 95% CI = 0.55–1.29, p = 0.423) (Table S1).

Discussion

The objective of this study was to determine the levels of adequate knowledge of early signs and risk factors for BC as well as positive attitude toward BC among women from a community in a resource-limited setting. Additionally, we also assessed the factors associated with the two domains. The major finding of our study is that, less than half of the study participants had adequate knowledge and positive attitude.

The level of adequate knowledge of early signs and risk factors for BC in various studies among women has been found to vary considerably. In this study, the level of adequate knowledge of both early signs and risk factors for BC was below 50% which is similar to the findings of knowledge of signs and risk factors for BC of 44% [26], 27.4% [27], and 46.4% [28] which were reported previously. However, a slightly higher level of knowledge of risk factors for BC of 50% was reported in a study done in Bangladesh [22]. The observed discrepancy in the level of adequate knowledge for the compared studies could be due to the difference in the characteristics of the study participants for the compared studies. This is because community-based studies are more likely to report a low level of knowledge compared to hospital-based studies. Also, the difference of the scoring method of knowledge across studies may partly be associated to the difference of the level of adequate knowledge for the compared studies.

Health seeking behavior is significantly influenced by positive attitude for mitigating prevention and early detection [29]. The level of positive attitude among women in Tanzania is still low as it has been seen in this study. This is also similar to the findings reported elsewhere. For instance, in the study of Zahra et al which was done in Iran in which the level of positive attitude among women toward BC was also low [30]. The positive attitude toward BC for the majority of women particularly in LMICs in which Tanzania is included, is generally low. This significantly contributes to low uptake of BC screening. As a result, there is a marked delay in diagnosis contributing to high mortality.

Sociodemographic characteristics determine the level of knowledge of BC among women in a given population. In our study, age was significantly associated with increased level of adequate knowledge of early signs and risk factors of BC. Women with age between 35 and 49 years in our study had increased odds of having adequate knowledge toward BC compared to women who were aged 50 years or above. This is similar to the finding in the study of Garai et al which also showed that young women were more likely to be knowledgeable about the symptoms and risk factors for BC [31]. However, other studies have shown a positive association between old age and knowledge of risk factors of BC. For example, studies of Zuniara et al, Basem et al, Mohamedraed et al, and Umit et al which were conducted in Pakistan, Saudi Arabia, Palestina, and Turkey, respectively reported that old women were associated with an increased level of knowledge of signs and symptoms of BC compared to younger women [32–35]. The positive association between younger age among women and a high level of knowledge of early signs and risk factors of BC has been associated with a high literacy level, which in turn helps to increase the level of knowledge [36]. Although old women are more likely to have low literacy level of knowledge about various aspects of BC, still there is evidence that old women are more likely to have more exposure and experience on BC. This helps them to be capable of identify possible early signs and risk factors as it was reported in study of Elshami et al [34].

Individuals’ education level mainly influences the knowledge on various aspect of life including diseases. In this study, women who did not have tertiary level of education had less chance of having knowledge of early signs and risk factors of BC compared to women with tertiary level of education. This is in agreement with the finding in the studies of Ranjan et al, Umit et al, Alam et al, and Nsaful et al which were done in India, Turkey, Bangladesh and Ghana, respectively [35,37–39]. It has been argued that high level of education is more likely to be linked to high income and health seeking behavior. Conversely, individuals with low level of education are more likely to have low income which in one way or another prevents them from engaging in health activities such as health education programmes [40]. Additionally, there is a possibility that individuals with low education level are likely to reduced access to various sources of information such as television, news papers, and social media platforms.

A positive attitude plays a major role in increasing the levels of the two domains (knowledge and practice) of health-seeking behavior among individuals in a given community [41]. It has been observed that the way how individuals feel about health systems determines their readiness to change so as to take action toward prevention of diseases [42]. In this study, women with younger age had significantly increased chance of having positive attitude toward BC compared to their counterparts. This is similar to the finding in the study of Ulku et al done in Turkey [43]. Also, in the study of George et al which was done in Nigeria it was found that old women had negative attitude toward BC [44]. Also, in the studies of El Asmar et al and Asmare et al it was shown that participants who had good knowledge of BC they were more likely to have positive attitude toward BC compared to their counterparts [45,46].

Limitations of the study

Our study had some limitations including lack of generalizability of the findings due to lack of representativeness because of small sample size caused by insufficient funds to include large sample size. We also acknowledge that by using mean value as a cutoff value for dichotomization of knowledge as “adequate” and “inadequate” and attitude as “positive” and “negative”. This therefore, resulted to loss of nuance in which there was failure to obtain participants with moderate knowledge of early signs and risk factors of BC. Additionally, the responses of the participants were self-reported which might have contributed to recall bias and social desirability bias.

Conclusion

This study reports relatively both low level of good knowledge and positive attitude of early signs and risk factors of BC among women from a community in Tanzania. These low levels of good knowledge and moderate positive attitude were associated with old age (above 50 years) and not having tertiary level of education. This implies that, there is a need of raising awareness among old women who at the same time have low level of formal education regarding early signs and risk factors of BC. This may help to have early detection and diagnosis of BC, which usually helps in downstaging of BC and improve the prognosis of the patients.
