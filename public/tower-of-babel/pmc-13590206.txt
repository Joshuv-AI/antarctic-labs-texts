Short‐Term Safety of Concomitant Meningococcal B and National Immunisation Program Vaccines in Infants Aged < 15 Months: A Survey‐Based Surveillance Study
Authors: Thuy Nguyen, Evelyn C S Tay, Michael Dymock, Thomas L Snelling, Lucy Dawes, Julie Marsh, Yue Wu, Alan Leeb, Nicholas Wood, Lucy Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Concomitant vaccination, defined as administering multiple separate vaccines during a single visit, is a recognised strategy for enhancing vaccine coverage. By reducing the number of healthcare visits, it improves adherence to vaccination schedules, lowers costs and facilitates the timely introduction of new vaccines [1]. It also minimises missed opportunities and incomplete vaccination courses, both of which are common barriers to achieving high vaccination coverage [1]. Concomitant vaccination may also reduce the cumulative burden of transient adverse events that could be more frequently reported if vaccines were administered separately [2].

In Australia, the National Immunisation Program (NIP) Schedule recommends vaccines at specific ages across the lifespan [3]. For children aged under 15 months, these occur at 2, 4, 6 and 12 months. At 2 and 4 months, infants receive the hexavalent vaccine, which protects against diphtheria, tetanus, whooping cough, hepatitis B, polio and Haemophilus influenzae type b (Hib), along with Rotavirus and pneumococcal vaccines. At 6 months, the hexavalent vaccine is given again, and at 12 months, children receive meningococcal serogroups A, C, W and Y (MenACWY), measles–mumps–rubella (MMR) and pneumococcal vaccines [3].

Beyond the standard schedule, the meningococcal B (MenB) vaccine has been made increasingly available for Australian children. MenB vaccines are provided under the NIP to Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children and children with specified medical risk conditions at 2, 4 and 12 months, with an additional 6‐month dose recommended only for those with certain medical conditions (Table S1). The vaccine is also funded for all children in Queensland [4] and South Australia [5], while in other states and territories it remains available privately should parents choose to vaccinate. Current recommendations from the Australian Immunisation Handbook support the co‐administration of MenB vaccine with scheduled NIP vaccines, where eligible [6].

Despite this, uncertainty remains among carers and healthcare providers regarding the safety of concomitant administration, as evidence to date on co‐administration of MenB vaccine with routine childhood vaccines is limited. To address this, we used data from AusVaxSafety, Australia's active vaccine safety surveillance system, to evaluate whether separating MenB vaccines from NIP vaccines reduces the risk of adverse events. We examined short‐term safety outcomes following vaccination at 2, 4, 6 and 12 months, comparing infants who received MenB vaccines concomitantly with NIP vaccines to those who received the vaccines at separate visits.

Methods

Study Design

This is an observational cohort survey study. We analysed data from the AusVaxSafety surveillance system (described elsewhere [7], with further details in Appendix S1) and followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology checklist to ensure all relevant reporting items were included (Table S2).

Study Population

Infants under 15 months of age were included in the analysis if, between 1 April 2022 and 31 March 2025, they either received only the scheduled standard NIP vaccines concomitantly with the MenB vaccine at 2, 4, 6 and 12 months and carers completed the AusVaxSafety survey, or received only the scheduled standard NIP vaccines and MenB vaccine on separate occasions and carers completed AusVaxSafety surveys following both vaccination visits.

Concomitant vaccination was defined as administration of the scheduled standard NIP vaccines and MenB vaccine at the same visit, while separate vaccination was defined as administration of the two at least 3 days apart. MenB vaccine doses were classified to the corresponding schedule timepoint according to predefined time windows (Table S1). Vaccine encounters were linked using unique personal identifiers obtained from participating vaccination sites. As identifiers were site‐specific, vaccine encounters could only be linked within the same site.

Infants who received separate vaccinations with MenB vaccine administered before NIP vaccines were excluded. Infants who received a non‐standard set of NIP vaccines (e.g., those who received only one or two vaccines from the recommended combination of hexavalent, Rotavirus and pneumococcal vaccines at the 2‐ or 4‐month schedule timepoint) were also excluded. These exclusions aimed to maintain a more homogeneous comparison group and reduce potential variability relating to vaccination order. Participant inclusion and exclusion are shown in Figure 1.

Flow diagram for including and excluding infants in the study.

Data Collection

Three days post‐vaccination, participants who had attended AusVaxSafety surveillance sites received a text message or email linking to an online survey with structured questions on adverse events following immunisation (AEFIs) 0–3 days post‐vaccination, any medical review sought (via phone, primary healthcare visit or hospital emergency department visit), impact on daily activities and existing underlying medical conditions. If surveys were incomplete, a reminder was sent on day 4, and only surveys completed by day 7 were included to reduce recall bias.

Vaccination details (vaccine type and date) and demographic information (age, sex, Indigenous status) were retrieved from vaccination records. Sex refers to the biological classification of infants as male or female. Participation was automatic, with consent for surveillance integrated into the vaccination consent process, although carers could opt out.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the respondents reporting any AEFI, defined as at least one adverse event, within 0–3 days of vaccination. Secondary outcomes included: specific adverse events (local reactions, systemic reactions including fever and fatigue, gastrointestinal symptoms, rash beyond injection sites and possible seizure); medical review for an AEFI (defined as advice from a telehealth service, or presentation to primary healthcare or a hospital emergency department); and carer‐reported impact on daily activities (defined as answering ‘Yes’ to the question ‘Did any of the symptoms you reported cause you to miss work, study or normal daily activities?’).

For infants receiving separate vaccinations, outcomes were further classified according to whether events were reported after any of the encounters, one encounter only or after both encounters.

Covariates

Covariates included in the statistical model were infant sex (male or female), Indigenous status (Aboriginal and/or Torres Strait Islander or non‐Indigenous), jurisdiction in which vaccination took place (state or territory), type of vaccination clinic (general practitioner clinic, Aboriginal health service or state health clinic) and presence of at least one underlying medical condition [8].

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were performed on de‐identified survey data using R version 4.5.2. We report a summary of demographic characteristics and report proportions of each outcome using descriptive statistics.

Bayesian multinomial logistic regression [9] was used to model the probabilities of zero, one and two events of the following reported outcomes, within 3 days after concomitant or separate vaccination: (i) any AEFI; (ii) any local reaction; (iii) fever; (iv) medical review sought, via phone, primary healthcare or hospital emergency department; and (v) any days of work or normal daily activities lost by the infant's carer. The primary estimand of the cumulative probability of at least one event was defined by the sum of the mutually exclusive probabilities of one event and two events. Secondary estimands were the probabilities of one event or two events after separate vaccination. The primary contrast is the absolute difference between the posterior probability distributions of at least one event following separate vaccination and of one event following concomitant vaccination.

The model accounted for NIP schedule timepoint, vaccination strategy (concomitant or separate vaccination), individual (as a random effect to account for individuals contributing to multiple schedules), sex, Indigenous status, jurisdiction in which vaccination took place, vaccination clinic type and report of at least one of the underlying medical condition covariates. Missing covariate data were presented as ‘unknown’ in descriptive analyses and excluded from Bayesian modelling.

Reported estimates were marginalised over the empirical joint covariate distribution separately for each schedule. Modelled probabilities, together with 95% credible intervals (CrIs), of each event type are summarised according to outcome, vaccination strategy (concomitant or separate vaccination) and NIP schedule timepoint.

Posterior distributions for model parameters were estimated using Stan [10] via the cmdstanr package (version 0.6.1). Each model was run with eight chains of 1000 iterations after warm‐up and model convergence was assessed using cmdstanr diagnostic tools. For the key model parameters, each model converged as demonstrated by acceptable R‐hat values (all less than 1.1), effective sample sizes (ratios greater than 0.1) and well mixed trace plots (Figure S1).

A sensitivity analysis was performed with independent prior distributions on the concomitant strategy effect (i.e., no hierarchical borrowing). The conditional (non‐marginalised) estimates were also compared with the marginalised model estimates to assess the influence of prior specification and covariate marginalisation. The Supporting Information contains further details of the statistical modelling (Appendix S2) and the sensitivity analysis and conditional estimates (Appendix S3), in addition to the prior predictive distributions (Figure S2) and posterior predictive distributions (Figure S3).

Ethics Statement

The Sydney Children's Hospital Network Human Research Ethics Committee approved the study (HREC/16/SCHN/19).

Result

A total of 45,215 infants received NIP and MenB vaccines either concomitantly or on separate occasions at the 2‐, 4‐, 6‐ and 12‐month schedule timepoints, with or without other vaccines, between 1 April 2022 and 31 March 2025. Of these, 72% (32,751/45,215) received NIP and MenB vaccines concomitantly. After excluding infants who received other vaccine(s), 10,959 infants were included in the analysis, 8749 (80%) of whom received NIP vaccines concomitantly with MenB vaccines and 2210 (20%) of whom received NIP vaccines separately (equivalent to 4420 encounters; Figure 1, Table 1). Infants were uniquely represented within each schedule, but some appeared in more than one schedule, and therefore, the total does not represent unique individuals across the dataset. The overall response rate following concomitant vaccination was 33% (8749/26,124). Under the separate strategy, the response rate was 45% (10,668/23,892) for at least one survey and 20% (4720/23,892) for both surveys. Details of excluded encounters are provided in Table S3.

Sex distribution was comparable across schedule groups, with male infants comprising 43%–53% of the separate group and 49%–52% of the concomitant group (see Table 1 for n and N values). The proportion of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander infants was consistently higher in the concomitant group than in the separate group at 2 months (24% [519/2125] vs. 5.3% [35/654]), 4 months (16% [483/3115] vs. 3.2% [20/630]) and 12 months (20% [478/2352] vs. 4.7% [32/684]) but similar at 6 months (4.9% [57/1157] vs. 3.3% [8/242]), reflecting recommendations to offer MenB vaccination at this age only to Indigenous children with specific medical risk factors. The separate vaccination group also showed the lowest Indigenous representation at the 4‐ and 6‐month schedule timepoints. Chronic medical conditions were rare in both groups, reported by fewer than 1% of participants across all age points (Table 1).

Some data were missing for sex (0.05% [6/10,959]) and Indigenous status (0.06% [7/10,959]), leaving 10,946 records for Bayesian modelling. The proportions of responses that were provided for infants contributing to multiple schedules are as follows: 60% (6545/10,946) contributed 1 response, 31% (3428/10,946, equivalent to 1714 unique infants) contributed 2 responses, 8.9% (969/10,946, equivalent to 323 unique infants) contributed 3 responses and < 0.01% (4/10,946, equivalent to 1 unique infant) contributed 4 responses.

Any AEFI and Specific Symptoms

The proportion of infants reporting at least one AEFI 3 days post‐vaccination varied by schedule timepoint and vaccination strategy (Tables 2 and 3, Figures S4–S6). There were no noticeable differences in unadjusted AEFI rates by demographic characteristics (Table S4). After adjusting for sex, Indigenous status and chronic medical conditions, the risk of any AEFI was 6.7% (95% CrI, 2.2% to 11.5%) lower in the concomitant group at 2 months (mean risk, 29% [95% CrI, 26% to 31%] in the concomitant group vs. 35% [95% CrI, 31% to 40%] in the separate group). The difference in the mean risk of any AEFI between concomitant and separate strategies was not apparent at 4 months (mean risk, 45% [95% CrI, 43% to 48%] in the concomitant group vs. 47% [95% CrI, 43% to 52%] in the separate group; risk difference, −1.9% [95% CrI, −6.5% to 2.6%]), 6 months (mean risk, 45% [95% CrI, 42% to 49%]) in the concomitant group vs. 44% [95% CrI, 38% to 50%] in the separate group; risk difference, 1.4% [95% CrI, −4.8% to 7.8%] or 12 months (mean risk, 47% [95% CrI, 45% to 50%] in the concomitant group vs. 52% [95% CrI, 48% to 56%] in the separate group; risk difference, −4.3% [95% CrI, −9.1, 0.2%]; Table 3, Figure S7). Notably, in the separate group, the adjusted risks of experiencing adverse events at both visits were substantial (2 months, 11% [95% CrI, 8% to 15%]; 4 months, 16% [95% CrI, 13% to 21%], 6 months, 13% [95% CrI, 9% to 17%]; 12 months, 19% [95% CrI, 15% to 24%], Table 3).

Adjusted risk of reporting fever ranged from 18% (95% CrI, 15% to 22%, Table 3) at 2 months to 31% (95% CrI, 27% to 35%) at 12 months in the separate group, and from 18% (95% CrI, 16% to 20%) to 31% (95% CrI, 28% to 33%) in the concomitant group, with no differences between strategies at 2, 4 and 12 months. At the 6‐month schedule timepoint, the risk of fever was 7.5% (95% CrI, 2.0% to 13.0%) higher in the concomitant group. The mean risk of reporting fever following both encounters was estimated at 2.8%–7.6% across schedules in the separate group (Table 3).

Modelled risks for local reactions were 5.1% (95% CrI, 1.6% to 9.0%) lower at 2 months in the concomitant group (16% [95% CrI, 14% to 18%]) compared with the separate group (21% [95% CrI, 18% to 25%]), while differences were not apparent at 4, 6 and 12 months (Table 3).

Fatigue was reported by 16%–33% of infants across schedule timepoints and vaccination strategies (see Table 2 for n and N values). Gastrointestinal symptoms (7%–19%) and rash (< 7%) were less common with little difference between vaccination strategies. Possible seizures were very rare with < 0.5% at any schedule timepoint with either vaccination strategy. No modelled risk estimates were generated for these events.

Unadjusted rates of AEFIs and specific symptoms, especially fever, were higher following the MenB vaccine encounter than the NIP encounter in the separate group, but were slightly lower than the unadjusted rates observed following concomitant vaccination across all schedule timepoints (Table S5). Notably, 93%–96% (see Table S5 for n and N values) of infants were given prophylactic antipyretics (e.g., paracetamol or ibuprofen) at the time of vaccination across schedule timepoints, whether receiving MenB vaccines alone or concomitantly with NIP vaccines.

Medical Review

Modelled risk for medical review within 3 days of vaccination was low across all schedule timepoints in both groups (Table 3, Figure S8). At the 2‐, 4‐ and 6‐month schedule timepoints, risks were low and showed no differences between strategies (2 months, 3.6% [95% CrI, 2.4% to 5.2%] in the separate group vs. 2.3% [95% CrI, 1.7% to 3.0%] in the concomitant group; 4 months, 3.7% [95% CrI, 2.4% to 5.3%] in the separate group vs. 4.2% [95% CrI, 3.5% to 5.0%] in the concomitant group; 6 months, 3.9% [95% CrI, 2.1% to 6.2%] in the separate group vs. 3.4% [95% CrI, 2.4% to 4.4%]) in the concomitant group. At 12 months, however, the risk was 2.2% [95% CrI, 0.4% to 4.3%] lower in the concomitant group (4.1% [95% CrI, 3.4% to 5.0%]) compared with the separate group (6.3% [95% CrI, 4.7% to 8.3%]). Where medical review occurred, presentations were divided between emergency departments and primary care including phone advice, with distribution varying by schedule but without a consistent difference between strategies (Table 2).

Carer Impact

Carer‐reported impact on daily activities was rare at younger ages but more frequently observed at 12 months (Table 3, Figure S9). At the younger schedule timepoints, risk increased with each schedule timepoint, though impact mostly involved 1 day or less of disruption. At 2 months, the risks were 1.1% (95% CrI, 0.7% to 1.6%) in the concomitant group and 1.6% (95% CrI, 0.9% to 2.8%) in the separate group. At 4 months, risks were 2.8% (95% CrI, 2.3% to 3.5%) in the concomitant group and 2.5% (95% CrI, 1.4% to 4.0%) in the separate group. At 6 months, risks were 4.8% (95% CrI, 3.6% to 6.0%) in the concomitant group and 3.9% (95% CrI, 2.1% to 6.7%) in the separate group. At 12 months, impact on daily activities was modelled at 8.5% (95% CrI, 7.3% to 9.7%) in the concomitant group and 8.3% (95% CrI, 6.4% to 11%) in the separate group, with 40% of carers reporting they were affected for 2 or more days (Table 2). There were no apparent differences between vaccination strategies at any schedule timepoint based on the modelled risks (Table 3).

Discussion

Using real‐world AusVaxSafety active vaccine safety surveillance data, we answered decision‐making questions relevant to carers, health practitioners and policymakers by comparing adverse event reporting in infants aged under 15 months following routine NIP vaccines and the MenB vaccine, administered either concomitantly or separately. Overall, the risk of experiencing at least one adverse event was similar or lower when vaccines were given concomitantly compared with when they were given on two separate occasions, with the greatest differences observed at the 2‐month schedule timepoint (Table 3, Figure 2).

Risk of reporting any adverse event, local reaction, fever, medical review and carer impact following concomitant or separate National Immunisation Program and meningococcal B vaccination by schedule timepoint.

Concomitant and separate vaccination showed broadly similar patterns for symptom type, with fever and local reactions being the most frequently reported events. Fever was more prominent following the MenB vaccine dose in the separate group and after concomitant MenB vaccination compared with NIP doses alone, consistent with the known higher reactogenicity of MenB vaccines in young children [11]. This aligns with findings from a multicountry European trial, where infants were likely to experience fever following MenB vaccination regardless of whether it was given alone or concomitantly [12]. That study, however, did not assess the cumulative risk across separate doses. When MenB and NIP vaccines are administered separately, infants are exposed to potential adverse events from both vaccinations on different occasions, effectively doubling the opportunity for AEFI occurrence. In our study, we observed that separating MenB and NIP vaccine doses increased the likelihood of experiencing two episodes of fever, with risks of up to 9%, highlighting the added burden associated with separating the doses. Notably, in our study 93%–96% of infants received prophylactic antipyretics before MenB vaccination, whether administered alone or concomitantly with NIP vaccines, in line with guidance from the Australian Immunisation Handbook [13]. Therefore, the observed increase in fever risk is likely attributable to the vaccination strategy rather than differences in antipyretic use.

For both concomitant and separate vaccine recipients, modelled risks of any AEFI, local reaction, fever, medical review and carer impact increased with age (Table 3, Figure 3), reflecting the broader trend of heightened reactogenicity as children progress through the immunisation series [14]. This pattern was particularly evident at the 2‐ and 4‐month schedule timepoints; when the same NIP vaccines were administered, the modelled risks of any AEFI were higher at 4 months than at 2 months for both strategies.

Unadjusted proportion of participants reporting solicited symptoms following concomitant or separate National Immunisation Program and meningococcal B vaccination by schedule timepoint.

The safety outcomes of greatest clinical significance, namely medical review and carer impact, were infrequently reported at any age. Modelled risks of medical review were below 5% at 2, 4 and 6 months for both strategies. Only at 12 months was the risk of medical review higher in the separate vaccination group compared with the concomitant group. Similarly, carer‐reported impact on daily activities remained uncommon across all infant ages, without differences between strategies. Notably, carer impact increased with age in both groups, which may be due to carers no longer being on parental leave or caring for an older infant being more demanding [15].

These findings have important implications for clinical practice. In our study, only 72.4% of infants received NIP and MenB vaccines concomitantly, which is lower than in other countries such as the United Kingdom (> 90%) [16] and Italy (80%) [17]. While some parents and providers may perceive separating vaccines as a strategy to reduce reactogenicity [18], our data suggest otherwise. Separate administration was associated with equal or higher cumulative probabilities of AEFIs. Concomitant vaccination may therefore not only reduce the healthcare burden, by decreasing the number of visits [2] and missed opportunities for immunisation [1], but also lower the overall likelihood of reactions and stress‐related responses from the vaccination event itself [19].

Practitioners may separate MenB vaccines from routine vaccines, particularly MenACWY, due to potential diminished effects on immune responses to certain antigens (e.g., PorA). However, available evidence indicates that concomitant administration elicits acceptable immune responses and supports its use in routine schedules [20]. These considerations should be interpreted alongside the present safety findings.

Limitations

Limitations in this study should be noted. Ascertainment relied on solicited carer reports, which may be subject to recall or reporting bias. The severity of each symptom was not captured in the surveys, and therefore, we are unable to comment on whether the severity of AEFIs differed with vaccination strategy beyond the use of medical review and carer impact as proxies for severity. Adverse events were recorded at the encounter level; therefore, reactions from concomitant vaccines given at separate sites were not attributable to specific vaccines and were recorded as a single event. Baseline background rates of symptoms such as fever in young children may also contribute to reported events and/or influence the choice of vaccination strategy [21]. Infants receiving non‐standard NIP vaccine schedules were excluded in our analysis; however, if infants with a predisposition to reactogenicity were more likely to receive non‐standard schedules [22], this may have resulted in selection bias. While we were able to link separate dose responses from the same infants within a given schedule timepoint to compare with concomitant recipients, we could not track infants across different schedules. Consequently, we were unable to determine whether a child who received concomitant vaccines at one schedule later received separate doses at another or to make within‐person comparisons across schedules. Furthermore, inclusion in the separate group required carers to complete both surveys, which may have introduced response bias if carers whose children experienced AEFIs were more motivated to report. However, in our study, excluded participants reported similar or slightly higher rates of reactions compared with those included (Table S5). Therefore, our analysis is more likely to underestimate rather than overestimate the cumulative risk of reactions due to the exclusion criteria. Finally, systematic differences between families who chose separate schedules versus families who chose concomitant schedules cannot be excluded, though demographic differences were modest after adjustment.

Conclusion

Our findings indicate that separating MenB vaccines from routine NIP vaccines does not reduce, and may in fact increase, the cumulative reaction burden across visits. These findings support current recommendations favouring concomitant administration of MenB vaccines with infant NIP vaccines.
