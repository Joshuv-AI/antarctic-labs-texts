Uridine metabolism promotes lung adenocarcinoma progression by activating FBL transcription via YBX1
Authors: Xinyi Wang, Liang Chen, Yingge Li, Luojun Chen, Chunmei Hao, Qilu Yan, Shuyang Yu, Cai Zhang, Ruibin Li, Bin Xu, Yi Yao, Qibin Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUridine metabolism serves as a critical compensatory metabolic pathway in tumor cells under glucose deprivation. However, the mechanistic role of uridine metabolism in tumorigenesis and progression has not been elucidated.MethodDistinct uridine metabolism patterns across pan-cancer types were analyzed using the TCGA database. Single-cell RNA sequencing was used to delineate uridine metabolic variations within the tumor microenvironment (TME). Through comprehensive bioinformatics analyses, including enrichment and transcription factor network mapping, YBX1 was identified as a key downstream transcriptional regulator. RNA sequencing, chromatin immunoprecipitation quantitative PCR (ChIP-qPCR), and immunoprecipitation (IP) assays were used to explored the key regulatory targets of the transcription factor. Furthermore, the role of YBX1 in promoting tumor progression via ribosome biogenesis was further investigated.ResultPan-cancer analyses demonstrated a strong correlation between elevated uridine metabolism levels and unfavorable clinical outcomes, particularly in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD). Single-cell analysis revealed that uridine metabolism promoted ribosomal biogenesis and identified YBX1 as the key transcription factor responsible for this regulation. Mechanistically, we found that uridine metabolism led to YBX1 lactylation at the K137 site, thereby stabilizing the YBX1 protein. By integrating RNA-sequencing and ChIP-qPCR assays, we found that YBX1 transactivated FBL expression by directly binding to its promoter, thereby modulating ribosome biogenesis. Functional assays demonstrated that silencing either YBX1 or FBL inhibited tumor cell proliferation, migration, and stemness. In vivo, we validated that knockdown of YBX1 or FBL significantly reduced tumor growth in mice. Moreover, our rescue experiments provided causal evidence that FBL knockdown could abrogate the oncogenic phenotypes of YBX1 overexpression both in vitro and in vivo.ConclusionOur findings not only highlight the uridine metabolism-YBX1-FBL axis as a potential oncogenic mechanism but also provide a framework for developing targeted therapies against uridine metabolic pathways. Clinically, these results potentially enable metabolic profiling-based patient stratification, paving the way for personalized treatment strategies in LUAD management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08034-z.Keywords: Cancer metabolism, YBX1, Lung cancer, Tumor progression, FBL

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the most prevalent malignancy globally [1], demonstrating the highest incidence rates across all cancer types according to GLOBOCAN 2020 data [2]. As the predominant histological subtype, non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for approximately 85% of cases, with adenocarcinoma representing nearly half of all NSCLC diagnoses [3]. While small-molecule targeted therapies, especially EGFR inhibitors, have revolutionized treatment paradigms for lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), current evidence indicates only 10% of patients derive clinical benefit from these precision medicines [4]. Consequently, in-depth exploration of the molecular regulatory mechanism and identification of novel therapeutic targets are paramount for advanced NSCLC treatment.

Uridine is composed of uracil and ribose and plays crucial roles in RNA synthesis, glycogen production, and biomembrane formation [5–7]. Additionally, studies have indicated that uridine is closely associated with systemic homeostasis, regulating vital processes such as glucose homeostasis, lipid metabolism, and amino acid metabolism [5]. Emerging evidence suggests that uridine metabolism plays a beneficial role in mitigating aging, inflammation, and fibrosis [8–11]. It is reported that uridine sustains glycolytic flux in pancreatic cancer during glucose deprivation, suggesting tumor cells may exploit uridine as an alternative energy source [12]. However, the molecular mechanism of uridine metabolism in tumorigenesis and progression remain poorly understood. We analyzed the expression of uridine metabolism-related genes across 32 types of solid tumors and their impact on prognosis using multiple bioinformatics methods, and found that elevated uridine metabolism drives poor prognosis in lung adenocarcinoma.

Accumulating evidence has established a strong correlation between the aberrant expression of transcription factors and the etiology of multiple cancers [13]. In our research, we identified YBX1 as a key transcription factor related to uridine metabolism. YBX1 is overexpressed in various human cancers and recognized as an oncogenic gene associated with poor prognosis [14]. Previous studies have investigated glycosylation modification of YBX1 enhances its intrinsic protein stability and promotes aerobic glycolysis in hepatocellular carcinoma [15]. Subsequent research further demonstrated circKIAA0182 regulates YBX1 expression by modulating its protein stability, thereby promoting the proliferation, migration, invasion, and chemoresistance of lung cancer cells [16]. However, the effect of uridine metabolism on YBX1 has not been reported yet. We found that uridine metabolism modulated the lactylation of YBX1, thereby influencing its stability and function.

We further explored the mechanism of uridine metabolism in promoting lung adenocarcinoma progression using molecular biological experiments. Our study demonstrated that YBX1 activated FBL gene transcription, regulated ribosome biogenesis, and ultimately promoted tumor progression. This discovery not only deepened our understanding of metabolic reprogramming in the pathogenesis of NSCLC, but also established the uridine metabolism-YBX1-FBL axis as a highly attractive therapeutic target. Targeting this pathway provides a crucial direction for developing novel therapeutic strategies and potential preventive measures against NSCLC.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition and preprocessing

We obtained publicly available transcriptome data with the matching clinical data for 32 types of solid tumors from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA, https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). The RNA-seq data were then normalized to Counts Per Million mapped reads and log2 transformed. Uridine-related genes were utilized from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB, https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/) and previous literature [5, 12]. Additionally, the somatic mutation data was obtained from the TCGA database and analyzed by using the R package “maftools”. Furthermore, four lung adenocarcinoma transcriptome datasets (GSE3141, GSE74706, GSE21933, GSE40791) were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database, along with gene sets associated with metabolism from the Reactome and MSigDB databases.

Single-cell analysis

We collected the LUAD single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets (GSE131907, GSE171145 and GSE189357) from the GEO database. The R package “Seurat” was used to analyze the single-cell data. We initially integrated these datasets using the merge function (Seurat R package, v4.4.0), followed by cell renaming via the RenameCells function to ensure unique cellular barcode identifiers. Subsequently, high-quality single cells were selected based on the following criteria: UMI counts more than 300 and less than 100 000 gene feature counts between 500 and 6 000, and mitochondrial gene ratio less than 20%. This quality control pipeline retained 200 045 cells for downstream analysis. Next, the function LogNormalize was performed and the scale factor was set to 10 000. The top 2 000 variable genes were identified for downstream analysis using the FindVariableFeatures function. The R package “harmony” was used to remove batch effects of the transcriptome data from different samples. Subsequently, we selected the top 50 principal components for uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) dimensionality reduction and visualization. Through unbiased clustering analysis, we obtained 16 cell clusters. The function FindAllMarkers was used to identify differentially expressed genes for each cell cluster. Combining reported and publicly available single-cell surface markers, we annotated the cells to determine their cell types. Finally, we used the function AUCell to score the uridine-related gene set.

Enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes

Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) databases store a wealth of gene-related functional information based on different classification concepts. After performing differential analysis using the R package “DESeq2”, we obtained differentially expressed genes between different groups (FDR q < 0.05 and |log2FoldChange|>1). Subsequently, we used the R package “ClusterProfiler” for GO and KEGG analyses.

Cell culture

H1975 and H157 human lung cancer cell lines, HEK293T cell line, and LLC-luc mouse lung cancer cell line were purchased from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. H1975 and H157 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin solution, maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. HEK293T cells and LLC mouse lung cancer cells were cultured in DMEM medium under the same conditions. In experiments with uridine supplementation, cells were cultured in no-glucose RPMI 1640 (Thermo Fisher Scientific Waltham, 11879020) complemented with 10% dialysed FBS (Bioind, C3830), 100 U/ml penicillin-streptomycin and 10 mM of uridine (Sigma, U3003).

Lentivirus packaging and cell infection

The overexpressing plasmids pLV2-CMV-YBX1 (human)-3×FLAG-Puro and pLV3-CMV-Ybx1 (mouse)-3×FLAG-Puro, the shRNA plasmids pLV3-U6-YBX1 (human)-shRNA1-CopGFP-Puro and pLV3-U6-YBX1 (human)-shRNA2-CopGFP-Puro, and HA-Ubiquitin (HA-Ub) were purchased from MiaoLing Biotechnology. The shRNA plasmid shFBL (shRNA1: GCTCGACACCCACACAAATA; shRNA2: GGATGTGATCTTTGCTGATGT) and shFbl(shRNA1: CGCGGAAAGGAGGATGCCCTT; shRNA2: GCCAAGAAGAGGACTAACATT)were purchased from AuGCT Biotech. Subsequently, the H1975 and H157 cells were infected with lentiviruses packaged by HEK293T cells and selected through puromycin (2 ug/ml) to establish the stable cell lines.

Total RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted with Fast RNA Extraction Kit (ABclonal, RK30120). Complementary DNA was synthesized by PrABScript III RT Master Mix (ABclonal, RK20429) and qRT-PCR was subsequently performed with SYBR Green kit (ABclonal, RK21203) following the manufacturer’s instructions. β-actin gene was detected for normalization of data. Fold changes of gene expression were calculated by the 2 − ΔΔCt method, three independent replicates of all biological samples were assessed. The primers used in qRT-PCR were listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Immunoblotting

Total protein was extracted with RIPA buffer (containing 1% SDS), separated by 10% SDS-PAGE, and transferred to nitrocellulose membrane. The protein was blotted with the following antibodies: anti-YBX1 (Proteintech, 20339-1-AP, 1:6000), anti-β-actin (Proteintech, 20536-1-AP, 1:5000), anti-FBL (Proteintech, 16021-1-AP, 1:5000), anti-RPS3 (Proteintech, 11990-1-AP, 1:3000), anti-RPS15 (Proteintech, 14957-1-AP, 1:1500), anti-BOP1 (Proteintech, 82742-3-RR, 1:5000), anti-CD133 (Proteintech, 18470-1-AP, 1:8000), anti-Nanog (Proteintech, 14295-1-AP, 1:3000). The primary antibody was detected by incubating the membrane with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody, followed by visualization with the enhanced chemiluminescence.

Cycloheximide (CHX) Tracing Experiment

Protein degradation assay was performed by treating cells with CHX (MedChemExpress, HY-12320, 20 µg/ml) at indicated time points, then cells were lysed and followed by immunoblotting.

Puromycin metabolic labeling and immunoblotting

Cells were incubated with puromycin (5 µg/ml) for 15 min. Then cells were rinsed with cold PBS and immediately were lysed in RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors. Equivalent amounts of protein from each sample were subjected to immunoblotting analysis.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

Cells were seeded into 96-well plates at an initial density of 4 × 103 cells per well. After different treatments, cells were treated with CCK-8 (Dojindo, CK04) asured at the wavelength of 450 nm using a microplate reader (TECAN).

Migration assays

Cells were harvested and suspended in RPMI 1640 at 4 × 104 cells/ml. The upper chamber of the Transwell (Corning, Inc.) was filled with 200 µl of cell suspension, and the lower chamber was filled with 500 µl of RPMI 1640. Following incubation for 24 h at 37 °C, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and stained with 0.5% crystal violet for 15 min at room temperature. The chamber was then washed with PBS solution, and the cells on the surface of the chamber were wiped of with cotton swabs. The images of stained cells on the lower side were captured by a light microscope from 5 different randomly selected views under 20 × magnification.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) -qPCR assays

ChIP-qPCR assays were performed in H1975 and H157 cells using the SimpleChIP® Kit (Cell Signaling Technology, 9002S) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Immunoprecipitations employed anti-YBX1 antibody (Abcam, ab255606) and normal rabbit IgG. Enriched DNA was subsequently quantified by real-time qPCR.

Immunoprecipitation (IP)

Following 24 h uridine treatment, cells were lysed with 500 µL Nonidet P-40, EDTA, Tris, NaCl Lysis Buffer (containing 1 mM PMSF and protease inhibitor cocktail) under rotary agitation at 4 °C for 20 min. A fraction of lysates was immediately denatured by boiling at 95 °C for 10 min as input controls. Remaining lysates were incubated with 10 µL protein A/G agarose beads coupled to 5 µg anti-YBX1 antibody (Proteintech, 20339-1-AP) or 2 µg control IgG (Santa Cruz, SC-2025) at 4 °C for 12 h. Immunoprecipitated proteins were eluted by boiling beads in SDS-PAGE sample buffer, followed by immunoblotting with anti-lactylation antibody (PTM BioLabs, PTM-1401RM) to assess YBX1 lactylation.

LC-MS for lactylation detection

H1975 cells were exposed to uridine for 24 h. Cell lysates were treated with 1% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) before immunoprecipitation using the YBX1 antibody. SDS-PAGE was performed, followed by Coomassie blue staining and the excision of bands for detection. Mass spectrometry (orbitrap480, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to identify the lactylation of the YBX1 protein.

Experimental animal models

All animal protocols were approved by the Hubei Provincial Center for Disease Control and Prevention (Hubei CDC) under approval number 202510295. In a mouse xenograft tumor model, cells (5 × 10⁶ per mouse) were collected, washed, and suspended in PBS, followed by subcutaneous injection into the right axilla of five-week-old female BALB/c nude mice. Tumor growth was monitored every 5 days, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula V = a × b2/2, where “a” represents the long diameter and “b” the short diameter. On day 25 post-injection, tumors were excised, weighed, and embedded in paraffin for immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis. For the orthotopic LLC tumor model, C57BL/6 mice were anesthetized, and the surgical site was shaved and sterilized. A 5 mm incision was made below the left scapula to expose the underlying intercostal space. A total of 2 × 10⁵ LLC-luc cells suspended in 50 µL of PBS containing 20% Matrigel (Corning) were injected into the left lung lobe via the intercostal space using an insulin syringe with a 29 G needle. On post-operative day 7, in vivo imaging was performed using the IVIS Spectrum system.

Statistics

All experimental data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software. Statistical details of experiments can be found in the figure legends. Unless otherwise noted in figure legends, data are representative of three biologically independent experiments. Continuous variables with a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± SD. Comparisons between two groups were conducted using the independent samples t-test. For comparisons among three or more groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used. Homogeneity of variances was assessed using Levene’s test. If the assumption of homogeneity was met, overall group differences were evaluated by one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s HSD test for post hoc multiple comparisons. For tumor growth curves involving multiple time points, two-way repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to assess the main effects of treatment, time, and their interaction. When a significant interaction was detected, simple effects analysis was conducted using Sidak’s multiple comparisons test to compare treatment groups at each time point. For all the statistical analysis: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001.

Results

The pan-cancer landscape of uridine metabolism

To explore alterations in uridine metabolism-related genes within tumor tissues, we collected 39 uridine metabolism-related genes from mSigDB and existing literature [5, 12, 17]. Subsequently, RNA-seq data and gene copy number variation data for 32 solid tumor types were downloaded from TCGA database for comprehensive analysis. Initially, we observed that most of uridine metabolism-related genes exhibited significantly upregulated expression in tumor tissues compared to normal tissues (Fig. S1A). Subsequently, we characterized the mutational landscape of these genes (Fig. S1B-D), revealing elevated somatic mutation frequencies in CAD, DPYD, and PFKP, with missense mutations as the predominant single nucleotide polymorphism type. Critically, survival analysis revealed that the mutation group had a poor overall survival (OS) (Fig. S1E). Collectively, these findings indicate that uridine metabolism plays a critical role in tumor initiation.

We established a uridine metabolism consensus score by integrating three pathway activity algorithms: ssGSEA, GSVA, and Singscore (Fig. 1A). Based on individual sample scores, 9 652 tumor patients from TCGA database were stratified into three distinct subgroups via machine learning: high metabolism, heterogeneous metabolism, and low metabolism clusters. Notably, kidney renal papillary cell carcinoma (KIRP), lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), and diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBC) were identified as the tumor types exhibiting the most metabolically active profiles (Fig. 1B). Survival analysis demonstrated that patients with active uridine metabolism had a poor OS (Fig. 1C). Subsequent differential analysis comparing the high-metabolism cluster against other clusters(FDR < 0.05, |log2FC|>1)revealed 306 genes upregulated versus heterogeneous-metabolism and 1 170 versus low-metabolism, with 301 overlapping genes (Fig. 1D). GO enrichment analysis revealed that epithelial development was the most significantly enriched pathway (Fig. 1E; Supplementary Table S2), suggesting uridine metabolism may influence tumor progression. Next, ssGSEA and CIBERSORT analyses, used to analyze the impact of uridine metabolism on immune cell infiltration in the tumor microenvironment, showed significantly elevated immune infiltration in high-metabolism clusters (Fig. 1F, Fig. S1G). Additionally, we employed the R package “oncoPredict” to investigate differential sensitivities of commonly used chemotherapeutic drugs in each metabolism cluster. Drugs were clustered based on similarity in IC50 values (Fig. S1F). We observed significantly low IC50 values for nucleoside analogs, including 5-fluorouracil, cytarabine, and gemcitabine in the high-metabolism cluster, indicating heightened therapeutic sensitivity (Fig. 1G). To further determine the tumor types most significantly influenced by the regulation of uridine metabolism, we compared the number of uridine metabolism-related genes present in the differentially expressed genes of 32 types of solid tumor and their matched normal tissues. The result showed that genes had the greatest changes in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), and thymoma (THYM) (Fig. 1H). Using the consensus score, we stratified patients into high-metabolism and low-metabolism groups across distinct tumor types, revealing significant differences in OS. Notably, among the three tumor types exhibiting the most active metabolism and the three encompassing the most differentially expressed uridine metabolism-related genes, LUAD demonstrated the most pronounced survival disparity (Fig. S2, Fig. 1I).

The characteristics of uridine metabolism in LUAD

To investigate the function of uridine metabolism in LUAD progression, we integrated three independent scRNA-seq datasets of LUAD. After rigorous data quality control, transcriptome data from 200 045 single cells were retained. By integrating SingleR automated prediction with manual prediction via previously reported biomarkers, we definitively annotated 16 major cell types, including T/NK cells, B cells, myeloid cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and epithelial cells (Fig. 2A, B; Supplementary Table S3). Next, we used AUCell to construct a uridine metabolism score (UMscore), revealing significantly elevated metabolic activity in epithelial and myeloid cells (Fig. 2C). Intercellular communication analysis via CellChat showed enhanced signaling transmission/reception in the UMscore-High group (Fig. S3A), with specific upregulation of IL6, CXCL, and CCL pathways (Fig. S3B). The CXCL signaling pathway was preferentially received by immune cells in the UMscore-High group (Fig. 2D). Comparative analysis of UMscore between tumor and normal tissues demonstrated significantly higher activity in tumor epithelial cells (Fig. 2E). Then, we extracted epithelial cells and divided them into high and low groups according to UMscore for transcription factor enrichment analysis (Fig. 2F). Subsequently, differential analysis exhibited that YBX1 was the most significant upregulation in the UMscore-High group among all enriched transcription factors (Fig. 2G), suggesting that YBX1 may play a pivotal role in uridine metabolism. We further analyzed the impact of YBX1 on immune cell infiltration within the tumor microenvironment (Figure. S1H). GSEA enrichment analysis demonstrated that ribosome biogenesis-related pathways were significantly enriched in the UMscore-High group (Fig. 2H). Based on these findings, we propose that YBX1 is a key gene associated with uridine metabolism regulation and is closely linked to ribosome biogenesis.

Uridine metabolism enhances the stability of YBX1 protein and promotes ribosome biogenesis

We first examined the impact of uridine supplementation on ribosome function. Under glucose deprivation conditions, uridine supplementation increased global protein translation (Fig. 3A). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) further revealed abnormal nucleolar enlargement and increased numbers in uridine -supplemented H1975 cells, suggesting enhanced ribosome biogenesis activity (Fig. 3B). These findings were consistent with our previous bioinformatics results. We subsequently investigated the effect of uridine metabolism on YBX1. We found that uridine supplementation increased YBX1 protein levels (Fig. 3C), without affecting YBX1 mRNA levels (Fig. 3D). Therefore, we hypothesize that the regulation of YBX1 expression is more likely to occur at the post-transcriptional level. CHX treatment experiments confirmed increased YBX1 protein stability in H1975 cells with uridine treatment (Fig. 3E). Furthermore, we analyzed the correlation between uridine metabolism and other metabolic pathways in lung adenocarcinoma. The results revealed that uridine metabolism was most significant positive correlated with the glycolytic pathway (which produces lactate) (Fig. S4). Importantly, we found that uridine supplementation elevated intracellular lactate levels (Fig. 3F) and enhanced YBX1 lactylation (Fig. 3G). Subsequent studies revealed that the lactate dehydrogenase inhibitor (oxamate) suppressed the aforementioned lactylation levels (Fig. 3H) and attenuated the uridine-induced increase in YBX1 protein stability (Fig. 3I, J). These results indicate that uridine metabolism may enhance YBX1 protein stability through lactylation.

To validate the presence of Kla in the YBX1 protein, we first utilized a prediction website (https://transkla.zzu.edu.cn/), which indicated five potential sites (K58, K64, K81, K92 and K137) (Supplementary Table S4). We then employed LC-MS/MS analysis to experimentally verify the modification sites, which confirmed high-confidence lactylation at residues K92 and K137 (Fig. 3K; Supplementary Table S5). To identify the specific residues targeted for lactylation, we generated point mutants where each of these lysines was substituted with arginine (R) (Supplementary Table S1). Our results demonstrate that the K137R mutation significantly reduced the lactylation level compared to the wild-type YBX1 protein (Fig. 3L). Taken together, these results indicate K137 as the major lactylation site of YBX1 and demonstrate that lactylation at this site plays a key role in promoting YBX1 stability.

To further define how K137 lactylation stabilizes YBX1, we inhibited the proteasome with MG132 in H1975 cells expressing the lactylation-deficient mutant YBX1‑K137R. This treatment restored YBX1 protein levels (Fig. S5A), indicating that YBX1‑K137R is degraded via the proteasome. Furthermore, YBX1‑K137R exhibited higher basal ubiquitination than wild-type YBX1 (Fig. S5B). Collectively, these results suggest that lactylation at residue K137 reduces YBX1 ubiquitination, likely through proteasomal pathway, thereby enhancing YBX1 stability.

YBX1 enhances ribosome biogenesis and promotes the proliferation, migration and stemness of lung cancer cells

To investigate the function role of YBX1 in lung cancer cells, two shRNAs (shYBX1-1 and shYBX1-2) were synthesized to knock down YBX1 in both H1975 and H157 cell lines (Fig. 4A, B). Silencing of YBX1 markedly downregulated ribosome-related proteins (RPS3, RPS15, BOP1) and rRNAs (18S, 28S, 5.8S) (Fig. 4C, D). CCK-8 analysis showed that the proliferation of H1975 and H157 cells was inhibited after YBX1 knockdown (Fig. 4F). Notably, stemness markers CD133 and Nanog were reduced upon YBX1 knockdown (Fig. 4E). Transwell assays further indicated that YBX1 knockdown dramatically inhibited the cell migration ability (Fig. 4G). These results collectively indicate that YBX1 drives ribosome biogenesis and promotes proliferation, migration, and stemness in lung cancer cells. Furthermore, knockdown of YBX1 significantly abrogated the uridine-enhanced sensitivity of lung cancer cells to gemcitabine (Fig. S6).

YBX1 transcriptionally activates the expression of ribosome-related gene FBL

To delineate the downstream regulatory mechanism of YBX1, we performed transcriptomic RNA sequencing in H1975 cells after YBX1 knockdown. We identified 797 downregulated and 1,134 upregulated genes upon YBX1 knockdown (Fig. 5A). We applied GO and GSEA anlysis for the downregulated genes, and found ribosome-associated pathways as the most enriched items (Fig. 5B, C; Supplementary Table S6-7), suggesting a critical role of YBX1 in ribosome biogenesis regulation. By integrating differentially expressed genes, ribosome pathway genes, and published YBX1 ChIP-seq data [18], we pinpointed 74 candidate genes (Fig. 5D, E). Among these genes, FBL exhibited the most significant differential expression. In YBX1 knockdown and overexpressed lung cancer cells, FBL mRNA and protein levels consistently correlated with YBX1 (Fig. 5F; Fig. S7A-B). Then we analyzed publicly available ChIP-seq datasets (ENCSR799GOY_1 and ENCSR799GOY_2) of YBX1. The heatmaps showed all YBX1 binding sites across the whole genomes, and the promoter region of FBL exist the binding peak of YBX1 (Fig. 5G, H). We next used the JASPAR database (http://jaspar.genereg.net/) to predict YBX1 binding sites in the promotor region of FBL (Fig. 5I). Subsequent ChIP-qPCR demonstrated significant enrichment of YBX1 at the FBL promoter region in H1975 and H157 cells (Fig. 5J). These findings establish that YBX1 can directly bind to the promoter region of FBL and activated its transcription in lung cancer cells, thereby promoting FBL expression.

YBX1 enhances ribosome biogenesis and consequently promotes the malignant phenotype of lung cancer cells through upregulated FBL expression

To investigate the functional role of FBL in lung cancer cells, two shRNAs (shFBL-1 and shFBL-2) were designed to knock down FBL in H1975 and H157 cells (Fig. 6A, B). FBL silencing suppressed expression of ribosome related proteins (RPS3, RPS15, BOP1) and rRNAs (18S, 28S, 5.8S), phenocopying the effects of YBX1 knockdown (Fig. 6C, D). Furthermore, CCK-8 assays demonstrated significantly impaired cell proliferation upon FBL depletion (Fig. 6F). Compared to shCtrl groups, FBL knockdown markedly reduced expression of stemness markers (CD133 and Nanog) (Fig. 6E), and transwell migration assays further revealed a substantial decrease in cell migration after FBL silencing (Fig. 6G). We overexpressed YBX1 and knocked down FBL in H1975 and H157 cells to investigate the effects of the YBX1/FBL axis on LUAD (Fig. 6I). qPCR and immunoblotting analyses demonstrated that the upregulation of ribosomal components (proteins RPS3, RPS15, BOP1; rRNAs 18S, 28S, 5.8S) induced by YBX1 overexpression could be reversed by FBL knockdown (Fig. 6H, I). CCK-8 and transwell assays confirmed that overexpression of YBX1 could promote the proliferation and migration of H1975 and H157 cells (Fig. 6J, K). Rescue experiments identified that this enhanced cell proliferation and migration ability induced by YBX1 overexpression could be reversed by FBL knockdown (Fig. 6J, K). Collectively, these data suggest that YBX1 enhances ribosome biogenesis in lung cancer cells by regulating FBL transcription, thereby promoting cell proliferation, migration, and stemness.

YBX1/FBL axis promotes the growth of lung cancer cells in vivo

To validate the function of YBX1 and FBL in lung cancer, we established a LUAD xenograft tumor model in nude mice. The nude mice were subcutaneously injected with H1975 cells stably knocked down for YBX1 (shYBX1) or FBL (shFBL), along with negative controls (shCtrl). Our results showed that tumor volume and tumor weight were drastically reduced following knockdown of either YBX1 or FBL (Fig. 7A-C). Furthermore, IHC analysis revealed that either YBX1 or FBL knockdown reduced the expression of proliferation marker Ki67 and stemness markers CD133 and Nanog (Fig. 7D). These data collectively indicate that high expression of YBX1 and FBL promotes proliferation and stemness in vivo.

To further delineate the functional dependence of the YBX1/FBL axis, we performed rescue experiments in both immunodeficient and immunocompetent backgrounds. In BALB/c nude mice, we inoculated human H1975 cells overexpressing YBX1 with concurrent FBL knockdown; in C57BL/6 mice, we inoculated murine LLC cells with the same genetic modifications. Results from both subcutaneous and orthotopic lung tumor models demonstrated that YBX1 overexpression alone significantly promoted tumor growth, whereas this effect was effectively reversed upon concomitant FBL knockdown (Fig. 7E–G; Fig. S8A, B). Moreover, in nude mice fed with uridine and implanted with either control or YBX1-knockdown cells, the tumor-promoting effect of uridine was abolished by YBX1 knockdown (Fig. S8C-E). These results conclusively demonstrate that the Uridine/YBX1/FBL axis drives lung cancer progression in vivo (Fig. 7H).

Discussion

Metabolic reprogramming represents a fundamental hallmark of cancer. Even under oxygen-sufficient conditions, most tumor cells preferentially utilize glycolysis for energy generation, a phenomenon termed the “Warburg effect” [19]. Recent studies have demonstrated that uridine supplementation in culture media promotes pancreatic cancer cell growth under glucose deprivation, establishing uridine as a nutrient source capable of fueling PDAC progression [12]. Similarly, melanoma and glioma cell lines exhibit high uridine catabolism, utilizing uridine for proliferation under glucose-deprived conditions [17]. To systematically profile uridine metabolism in human cancers, we quantified its activity across 32 solid tumor types from TCGA. Stratification of 9,532 patients based on metabolic profiles identified three metabolically distinct clusters exhibiting significant heterogeneity. Survival analysis revealed that elevated uridine metabolism correlated with poorer prognosis, suggesting its clinical relevance. Pharmacosensitivity profiling further demonstrated that the high-uridine metabolism cohort exhibited enhanced susceptibility to nucleotide analog antineoplastic agents, likely due to interference with uridine metabolic processes. However, the mechanistic link between uridine metabolism and molecular regulation in LUAD remains unclear.

Our single-cell analysis in LUAD revealed the high-uridine metabolism group exhibited enhanced cellular communication, with immune cells demonstrating substantial reception of CXCL signals. CXCL family members promoted tumor-infiltrating immune cells, thereby supporting tumor progression [20].This may account for the enrichment of immune cells observed in the high-uridine metabolic group in our immune infiltration analysis. Furthermore, our study revealed that only tumor epithelial cells exhibited significant uridine metabolic activity upregulation compared to normal cells. SCENIC analysis revealed elevated YBX1 transcription factor activity in high-uridine metabolism epithelial cells, where it showed the highest fold change among all transcription factors. Previous studies have demonstrated that YBX1 is significantly upregulated across multiple cancer types and is correlated negatively with OS in patients with solid tumors [14, 21, 22]. Research has further indicated that YBX1 interacts with MUC1 to enhance the progression and stemness of lung adenocarcinoma [23]. Additionally, it has been established that YBX1 promotes the stemness and metastasis of lung adenocarcinoma by augmenting Nanog’s transcriptional activity [24]. In the present study, we observed significantly increased YBX1 protein levels in lung adenocarcinoma cells supplemented with uridine, whereas no substantial changes were detected at the mRNA level. Moreover, we observed that uridine metabolism is not influenced by YBX1 expression levels (Fig. S9A, B). Subsequent lactylation quantification revealed that uridine metabolism enhanced YBX1 lactylation modification. Previous studies have demonstrated that lactylation modification can influence protein stability. For instance, lactylation at the K70 site of APOC2 stabilizes its protein levels in lung cancer [25]. Lactic acid directly enhances the lactylation modification of DCBLD1, thereby inhibiting its ubiquitination and stabilizing its protein levels in cervical cancer [26]. Based on the previous findings, we hypothesized that uridine metabolism increased the lactylation modification levels of YBX1, thereby enhancing its protein stability. We found that suppressing lactate production reduced the lactylation level and impeded the protein degradation of YBX1. Furthermore, through mass spectrometry analysis and site-directed mutagenesis, we identified K137 as the major lactylation site on YBX1. Functional assays demonstrated that knockdown of YBX1 significantly inhibited the proliferation, migration, and stem-like properties of lung adenocarcinoma cells, while in vivo mouse models showed knockdown of YBX1 reduced tumor volume and weight, establishing YBX1 as a critical NSCLC therapeutic target.

GSEA results indicated that increased uridine metabolism activity in epithelial cells upregulated ribosome-related pathway activity, particularly the ribosome biogenesis pathway. Ribosomes are complex machines composed of rRNA and ribosomal proteins [27]. Recent studies have demonstrated that increased ribosome biogenesis and protein synthesis play a critical role in sustaining tumor cell growth and proliferation [28]. Prior research has associated active ribosomal biosynthesis with poor prognosis in lung cancer [29]. Based on the findings, we hypothesized that uridine metabolism influenced tumor progression by regulating ribosome-related pathways. Intriguingly, our drug sensitivity analysis revealed that patients with high uridine metabolism exhibited higher sensitivity to 5-fluorouracil (5-FU), a compound extensively documented for its ability to inhibit ribosome synthesis [30, 31]. These results further validated the significant association between uridine metabolism and ribosome biogenesis. Separately, a 2025 study reported that YBX1 amplifies oncogenic translation via mRNA recruitment to polysomes [18]. In our study, knockdown and overexpression experiments confirmed YBX1 as a pivotal regulatory factor linking uridine metabolism to ribosomal biosynthesis, mediating increased ribosomal biosynthesis. In summary, our findings enrich the understanding of the regulatory network involving uridine metabolism reprogramming, YBX1, and ribosome biogenesis.

The RNA sequencing results after knockdown of YBX1 revealed that 143 ribosome-related genes were significantly downregulated, among which FBL was the most strongly inhibited. ChIP-qPCR experiments confirmed that YBX1 directly bound to the FBL promoter to regulate its transcription. FBL is a highly conserved, extensively studied nucleolar filamentous protein in eukaryotes that plays a pivotal role in regulating early ribosome biogenesis [32–34]. The oncogenic role of FBL is well-established, as its silencing inhibits proliferation, migration, and invasion in prostate cancer [35], attenuates stem-like properties in acute myeloid leukemia [36], and suppresses proliferation and stemness in hepatocellular carcinoma [37]. Consistently, our data demonstrated that FBL knockdown impaired proliferative, migratory, and stemness capacities in lung cancer cells, with in vivo murine models further corroborating its tumor-suppressive effects via reduced tumor volume and mass. To further validate the regulatory role of YBX1 on FBL, rescue experiments demonstrated that the enhanced proliferative and migratory capacities induced by YBX1 overexpression were reversed upon FBL knockdown. Together, these findings establish YBX1 as a transcriptional activator of FBL and propose the YBX1-FBL axis as a potential therapeutic target in LUAD.

Uridine yields ribose-1-phosphate via UPP1 to fuel both catabolic and biosynthetic metabolism. Previous studies have reported that in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDA), UPP1 is regulated by the KRAS-MAPK signaling pathway, upregulated under nutrient-restricted conditions, and associated with poor prognosis. Additionally, KRAS mutation has been shown to correlate significantly with UPP1 expression levels in lung cancer cell lines [12]. It is plausible that such a genetic context creates a heightened dependency on uridine salvage during metabolic stress. However, it remains to be determined whether this pathway represents a universal “glucose deprivation stress response” or is particularly dominant in LUAD due to its specific genetic landscape.

Our study demonstrates that uridine metabolism promotes lung adenocarcinoma progression via the YBX1-FBL axis. We propose that this axis may also play a critical role in other tumors characterized by high uridine metabolism. This hypothesis is indirectly supported in pancreatic cancer, where both YBX1 and FBL have been reported to be upregulated and to promote tumor progression [38, 39]. Furthermore, our study highlights the translational potential of therapeutic strategies targeting the YBX1-FBL signaling axis in lung adenocarcinoma. Future investigations applying this model to other cancer types—particularly those with and without KRAS mutations—may reveal whether targeting the YBX1-FBL axis represents a broadly applicable strategy or one that is most effective in specific genetic contexts.

However, this study still has certain limitations. Firstly, while our study delineates a clear pathway from uridine metabolism to YBX1 stabilization via lactylation, we acknowledge that cellular metabolism is interconnected. Additional metabolic pathways downstream of or parallel to uridine catabolism may also contribute to the observed effects. Secondly, it should be noted that this study has only validated FBL as a key downstream effector of YBX1, and does not exclude the potential contribution of other YBX1 target genes in this process. Thirdly, this study has not yet directly validated the association between YBX1 lactylation levels or FBL expression and patient prognosis in clinical cohorts, an issue that awaits further investigation. These aspects represent critical directions for our future research.

Conclusions

In summary, this study comprehensively investigated the role of uridine metabolism in LUAD. Our findings illustrated the uridine-YBX1-FBL axis as a critical pathway in LUAD, offering a conceptual framework for future therapeutic targeting, patient stratification and clinical decision-making in LUAD.
