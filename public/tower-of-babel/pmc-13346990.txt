CD22-targeted immunotherapy for B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia progressing following CD19-targeted immunotherapy
Authors: Fengmei Song, Junfang Yang, Mingming Zhang, Shan Fu, Jingjing Feng, Ruimin Hong, Yue Lu, Alex H Chang, Xian Zhang, He Huang, Yongxian Hu, Guoqing Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPatients progressing after CD19-targeted immunotherapy in r/r B-ALL experience poor outcomes. CD22-targeted therapies, including CD22 CAR-T cells and Inotuzumab Ozogamicin, show promise as alternatives, although data in those patients is limited. This study retrospectively analyzed 43 r/r B-ALL patients who had previously received CD19-targeted therapy at two centers in China. Among these patients, 27.9% received blinatumomab, 58.1% received CD19 CAR-T cells, and 14% received both. After CD19-targeted therapy, 34.9% of patients experienced CD19-negative relapse, while the remaining patients maintained CD19 expression. Subsequent treatments included CD22 CAR-T cells (55.8%) and InO (44.2%). The median age was 39 (24–56) years, with an overall CR/CRi rate of 51.1% and 30.2% achieving MRD negativity. Among the 22 patients achieving CR/CRi, 13 (59.1%) experienced relapse. The median relapse-free survival (RFS) was 236 days (95% CI: 132–unreached), and the median OS has not been reached. Multivariate analysis showed similar remission rates and survival for CD22 CAR-T and Inotuzumab Ozogamicin therapies. Patients with extramedullary disease had worse remission rates, and those previously resistant to CD19-targeted therapy had shorter RFS. CD22-targeted therapies offer a potential option for patients progressing after CD19-targeted immunotherapy, but high relapse rates highlight the need for better strategies for lasting remission.Subject terms: Cancer, Immunology, Oncology

Introduction

In recent years, significant clinical success has been achieved with CD19-targeted immunotherapy for the treatment of relapsed or refractory B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (r/r B-ALL), leading to the approval of CD19-targeted products such as blinatumomab and tisagenlecleucel by the US Food and Drug Administration for pediatric and young adult patients with r/r B-ALL. Blinatumomab, a bispecific T-cell engager that targets both CD19 and CD3, has improved the complete remission rate and event-free survival in patients with r/r B-ALL1,2. Similarly, CD19 CAR-T cell therapy has significantly advanced treatment, with complete remission rates reaching 70–90%3–6. However, 10–30% of patients are refractory to CAR-T cells, resulting in a poor prognosis for these individuals. Even in patients who achieve remission, CD19 CAR-T therapy fails to induce durable responses in many patients, with 30–60% relapsing after treatment4,7–10. Previous studies4,11–13, including those conducted at our center14, have shown that CD19 loss or reduced expression is a major cause of relapse following CD19 CAR-T therapy, a pattern also observed with blinatumomab treatments5,15,16. Whether owing to resistance to CD19-targeted immunotherapy or relapse from loss of CD19 expression, alternative therapeutic targets are urgently needed.

CD22 is expressed on leukemic blasts in more than 90% of patients with B-ALL17, making it a viable alternative target for immunotherapy. CD22-targeted treatments primarily include CAR-T cell therapy and antibody-drug conjugates such as inotuzumab ozogamicin that is conjugated to the cytotoxic antibiotic calicheamicin. Compared with standard therapies, Inotuzumab Ozogamicin has shown higher remission and survival rates in patients with r/r B-ALL18. Several studies suggest that CD22-targeted therapies can induce remission in B-ALL patients who are either naive or resistant to CD19-targeted immunotherapy7,19,20. However, systematic data on the effectiveness of CD22-targeted treatments in patients who progress after CD19 immunotherapy remain limited. In this study, we present a multicenter retrospective analysis of CD22-targeted therapy for 43 patients with r/r B-ALL who relapsed after CD19-targeted therapy, focusing on their disease characteristics and the efficacy and safety of CD22-targeted immunotherapy.

Results

Patient characteristics

From August 16, 2019, to June 11, 2024, 43 patients were enrolled. Of these, 24 (55.81%) were treated with CD22 CAR-T cells, while 19 (44.19%) received inotuzumab ozogamicin. According to the grouping based on different combinations of CD19-targeted and CD22-targeted therapies, five distinct groups were identified. The largest group consisted of patients who received CD22 CAR-T therapy after relapse following CD19 CAR-T therapy (n = 21), followed by those who received Inotuzumab Ozogamicin therapy after relapse following blinatumomab therapy (n = 12) (Fig. 1B). The median age was 39 years (IQR 24–56), and 23 (53.49%) patients were male. The median number of bone marrow blasts before infusion was 51% (IQR 10.50–72.50%), with 12 (27.91%) presenting high-risk cytogenetic and molecular profiles. Seventeen patients (39.53%) had extramedullary disease, including 11 (25.58%) with central nervous system involvement. Patients had received a median of seven previous therapy lines (IQR 5–9), and 15 (34.88%) had prior allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT). Regarding prior CD19-directed immunotherapy, 12 (27.91%) patients were treated with blinatumomab only, 25 (58.14%) with CD19 CAR-T cells only, and 6 (13.95%) with both. All patients treated with CD22 CAR-T cells had previously received CD19 CAR-T cell therapy. Only 9 patients (20.93%) exhibited refractory disease to CD19-directed immunotherapy, while the remaining patients experienced relapse. Following CD19-directed immunotherapy, 23 (53.49%) patients experienced CD19-positive relapse, 15 (34.88%) experienced CD19-negative relapse, and 5 (11.63%) experienced dim or partial CD19 expression. Efficacy

The overall CR/CRi rate was 51.1%, with 30.2% achieving minimal residual disease negativity. It is noteworthy that all responders in our study achieved CR/CRi within the first cycle of treatment; no additional remissions were observed in subsequent cycles for those who did not respond by the one-month assessment. Patients who achieved a response to inotuzumab ozogamicin received a median of 3 treatment cycles (range, 1–3). The CR/CRi rate was 50.0% (12/24) in the CD22 CAR-T group and 52.6% (10/19) in the Inotuzumab Ozogamicin group, with no significant difference observed between the two groups (Fig. 1A). Within these responses, CRi was observed in 5 of 24 patients (20.8%) treated with CD22 CAR-T therapy and in 4 of 19 patients (21.1%) treated with inotuzumab ozogamicin. The CR/CRi rates across the groups based on different combinations of CD19-targeted and CD22-targeted therapies ranged from 47.6% to 66.7%, similarly showing no significant differences between the groups (Fig. 1B). Notably, among the three patients who had previously achieved CR following CD22 CAR-T therapy and subsequently relapsed, two achieved MRD-negative CR after receiving inotuzumab ozogamicin in the current study, while one failed to respond. Additionally, among the 15 patients who had prior allo-HSCT, the CR/CRi rates were 60% (3/5) for the CD22 CAR-T group and 70% (7/10) for the inotuzumab ozogamicin group, with no significant difference between the two groups. Multivariate analysis indicated that patients with prior allo-HSCT had higher CR/CRi rates, whereas noncentral nervous system extramedullary disease was associated with lower CR/CRi rates. CR/CRi rate was not significantly affected by other patient, disease, and treatment characteristics, including CD22 CAR T or Inotuzumab Ozogamicin treatment, CD19 immunotherapy response, or CD19/CD22 expression status (Fig. 1C).

Among the 22 patients who achieved CR, 13 (59.1%) experienced relapse, of whom 10 had postrelapse CD22 expression data. Of those 10 patients, 6 (60.0%) were CD22-positive, 2 (20.0%) were CD22-negative, and 2 (20.0%) exhibited dim or partial CD22 expression. Of the 21 patients with refractory disease, 11 had data on CD22 expression following CD22-targeted therapy; among them, 9 (81.8%) were CD22-positive, 1 (9.1%) was CD22-negative, and 1 (9.1%) had dim CD22 expression. Overall, among patients who experienced treatment failure or relapse after CD22-targeted therapy, 4 cases exhibited antigen loss or partial loss, transitioning from CD22-positive to CD22-negative or partial expression.

Among the 22 patients who achieved complete remission (CR) (one patient with an unknown relapse time was excluded from the RFS analysis), the median follow-up time was 19 months. The median relapse-free survival (RFS) was 236 days (95% CI: 132–unreached). According to the univariate analysis, the median RFS for Inotuzumab Ozogamicin and CD22 CAR-T cell therapy were 212 days (95% CI: 132–unreached) and 248 days (95% CI: 108–unreached), respectively, with no significant difference (Fig. 2A). Although multivariate analysis also revealed no significant difference in RFS between the Inotuzumab Ozogamicin and CD22 CAR-T cell therapy groups, survival analysis of adjusted covariates by multivariate regression models suggested a tendency for the CD22 CAR-T group to have longer RFS (Fig. 2C). Notably, patients with prior resistance to CD19-targeted therapy had significantly shorter RFS (Fig. 2D). Additionally, the 2 patients with dim/partial CD19 expression seemed to have worse RFS compared to those with negative or positive expression, however, this was limited by very small numbers—and would not present the Hazard Ratio. The median OS for both groups has not been reached (Fig. 2B). Although three patients underwent bridging hematopoietic stem cell transplantation after achieving CR (Table 1), multivariate analysis showed that bridging HSCT did not have a significant impact on RFS (p = 0.52).

Safety

Our analysis of safety focused on common adverse events, such as hematologic toxicity and infections. Additionally, we examined group-specific side effects, including liver toxicity in the Inotuzumab Ozogamicin group and CRS, ICANS, and GVHD in the CAR-T group. Owing to incomplete data on certain adverse events, the sample size varied across analyses and was sometimes smaller than the total patient population. Grade 3 or higher hematologic adverse events—including anemia (23/35 [77.1%]), neutropenia (33/35 [94%]), and thrombocytopenia (32/35 [91%])—were common in both groups, with no significant differences observed. Similarly, severe infections (grade 3 or higher) occurred in 10.5% (2/19) of the Inotuzumab Ozogamicin group and 12.5% (2/16) of the CAR-T group, with no significant difference between the groups. Group-specific adverse events, such as liver toxicity in the Inotuzumab Ozogamicin group and CRS in the CAR-T group, were infrequent and predominantly mild (grades 1–2). Overall, the safety profiles of both therapies were comparable, with manageable adverse events in most cases (Fig. 3).

Discussion

Although CD19-targeted immunotherapy has significantly improved remission rates in patients with r/r B-ALL, many patients face a high risk of relapse. Relapses can be classified as either antigen-positive or antigen-negative on the basis of the target antigen status on leukemia cells. In this study, 34.88% of patients progressed with negative CD19 expression, a rate exceeding that of several historical reports21,22. This discrepancy is likely explained by the intensive and sequential CD19-targeted pretreatment in our cohort. Specifically, in our study, as high as 58.14% of the patients had received only CD19 CAR-T cell therapy, and 13.95% had received both blinatumomab and CD19 CAR-T cell therapy prior to enrollment. In the context of a median of seven prior therapy lines, this relentless immunological pressure creates a highly selective environment that significantly favors antigen escape through downregulation or lineage switch. For patients with antigen-negative relapse, identifying alternative treatment targets is crucial. Even for patients with CD19-positive relapse, the response rate to a second infusion of CD19 CAR-T cells remains disappointing12,23. In contrast, CD22-targeted immunotherapy achieved a remission rate of 51.1% in this study, suggesting that CD22 may serve as a viable alternative target after CD19 immunotherapy failure. Responses were influenced primarily by the presence of noncentral nervous system extramedullary disease (which is associated with lower remission rates) and were unrelated to Inotuzumab Ozogamicin or CD22 CAR-T cell therapy.

In this study, the RFS remained relatively short, indicating the need for additional combination therapies to strengthen the efficacy of CD22-targeted treatment. While we observed similar RFS and overall survival between the two groups, the divergence between RFS and OS trends in our study is likely a reflection of the heterogeneous immunological exhaustion and treatment history between the two groups (Table 1). The CD22 CAR-T group was composed entirely of patients who had previously failed CD19 CAR-T therapy (100% vs. 36.84% in the InO group, P < 0.001). This ‘ultra-refractory’ status explains why, although CD22 CAR-T provided a longer RFS interval (median 248 vs. 212 days), the OS did not show a corresponding advantage; these patients had fewer salvage ‘escape’ therapies remaining after a second cellular therapy failure. Conversely, the InO group, with significantly higher prior allo-HSCT rates (52.63% vs. 20.83%, P = 0.03) and lower prior CAR-T exposure, likely maintained better sensitivity to subsequent conventional or targeted salvage treatments, which stabilized their OS despite a higher frequency of early relapse events. The relatively small sample size in our cohort may limit the generalizability of these results to all patients who relapse after CD19-targeted therapy. Further prospective studies are needed to determine whether CD22 CAR-T cells or Inotuzumab Ozogamicin are associated with improved disease remission and survival outcomes. Notably, patients with prior resistance to CD19-targeted therapy had significantly shorter RFS when treated with CD22-targeted therapy. It suggests that tumor resistance to immunotherapy may be driven by shared molecular mechanisms rather than being solely related to the target24. A comprehensive understanding of these molecular interactions is essential for the development of new and targeted anticancer therapies.

Common hematologic toxicities and infections following CD22-targeted therapy were expected. Despite our cohort having undergone more extensive prior treatments, the observed rates were similar to those seen after CD19-targeted therapy3,4,25. Severe CRS and ICANS of grade 3 or higher were rare after CD22 CAR-T therapy. Additionally, no instances of liver toxicity, a characteristic adverse event of Inotuzumab Ozogamicin treatment, were observed in our study.

In this study, multivariate analysis revealed that the CD22 expression status did not significantly impact the CR rate or event-free survival. A previous study suggested that inotuzumab treatment in pediatric patients with r/r B-ALL, MRD negativity was correlated with calicheamicin sensitivity in vitro but not with CD22 surface expression, saturation, or internalization26. In our study, 34 patients progressed after treatment, and CD22 expression data were available for 22 of them; only 3 patients had CD22-negative expression at progression, which seems to support this finding. However, several exploratory analyses have indicated that progression is more common in patients with higher percentages of low-level CD22 expression before treatment and those with diminished CD22 site density after treatment19,27–29. Unfortunately, owing to the lack of quantitative analysis, we cannot confirm whether the positive relapses were related to CD22 downregulation. Future research should explore the relationships among baseline CD22 expression, postprogression CD22 antigen density, and treatment outcomes.

In conclusion, our study suggests that CD22-targeted therapies, such as CAR-T and inotuzumab, may hold promise after CD19 treatment failure. However, the relapse rate following CD22-directed immunotherapy remains high, highlighting the need for further research to improve long-term efficacy.

Methods

Study design

Our primary objective was to determine the overall rate of complete remission with or without incomplete count recovery (CR/CRi) at one month following CD22-targeted immunotherapy in adult patients with r/r B-ALL who had previously undergone CD19-targeted immunotherapy. Additionally, clinical responses beyond the first month were monitored to capture any late remissions. Our secondary objective was to report the relapse-free survival, overall survival, and toxicity. We conducted a multicenter, retrospective study across two medical institutions in China, analyzing data from 43 adult patients who received CD22-targeted immunotherapy after progressing or failing CD19-targeted therapy. This analysis included patients treated between August 16, 2019, and June 11, 2024, with CD22-targeted therapies, which comprised CD22 CAR-T cells, administered as part of clinical trials (ChiCTR1800017402, ChiCTR-ONC-17012829), and inotuzumab ozogamicin, sourced from a commercial supply. The data cutoff date for analysis was July 1, 2024. We reviewed the electronic records of all consent-providing patients and documented their baseline characteristics, disease responses, and survival outcomes. This study has been performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. This study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of The First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine, Hangzhou, China, and Hebei Yanda Lu Daopei Hospital, Langfang, China. Informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Clinical outcomes assessment

Clinical outcomes were assessed based on the institution’s definition, capturing the CD19 and CD22 status before and after therapy. Owing to the lack of systematic antigen density quantification, antigen expression was assessed by flow cytometry using two independent parameters: the proportion of leukemic blasts expressing the antigen and relative fluorescence intensity. CD19/CD22 expression was classified by the percentage of leukemic blasts expressing CD19/CD22 above the corresponding isotype control as negative (<20%), partial (20–80%), or positive (>80%). Expression intensity was evaluated separately using internal controls within each sample, including residual normal B cells when present. CD19/CD22 expression was categorized as “bright” when the fluorescence intensity was comparable to or higher than that of normal B cells, and as “dim” when the fluorescence intensity was lower but remained distinguishable from background. CR, no response (NR), and complete remission with incomplete count recovery (CRi) were defined according to the National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines (Version 1.2016). Minimal residual disease (MRD) negativity was defined as less than 0.01% abnormal B cells in the bone marrow, as determined by flow cytometry. CRS was graded using the Lee scale30. Immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) was graded according to the American Society for Transplantation and Cellular Therapy (ASTCT). Graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) was graded using the modified Glucksberg grading standard. Relapse-free survival (RFS) was calculated as the time from achieving CR until confirmed morphological relapse; patients without these events were censored at the date of their last follow-up. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from infusion to last contact or death, whichever occurred first.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were computed to summarize patient and disease characteristics. Frequencies and percentages are reported for categorical variables, and medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) are reported for numerical variables. The comparison of continuous variables was performed using a t-test or Wilcoxon test; categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test. The Kaplan–Meier test was employed to perform time-to-event analyses. Frequencies were reported to describe response rates, and multivariable logistic models were performed to identify significant differences. Regression coefficients with 95% confidence intervals (CI) and p values are provided. A multivariable Cox proportional hazards model was used to detect differences in survival endpoints, with hazard ratios (HR) reported alongside 95% CIs and p values. Adjusted RFS curves were generated using a conditional adjustment method to present expected survival curves based on the Cox model separately for each subpopulation. This method balances covariates by replicating the data, creating a new, balanced dataset for each subgroup without refitting the model, thereby ensuring that survival estimates are adjusted for covariate differences31. All statistical analyses were performed with the Statistical Package for R software V.4.0.3, and p values ≤ 0.05 were considered significant.
