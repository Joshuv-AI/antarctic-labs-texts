Conditional Local Independence Testing for Dynamic Causal Discovery Thanks: Working paper. Contact: liumingzhou@stu.pku.edu.cn
Mingzhou Liu, Xinwei Sun, Yizhou Wang

Abstract
Inferring causal relationships from dynamical systems is the central interest of many scientific inquiries. Conditional Local Independence (CLI), which describes whether the evolution of one process is influenced by another process given additional processes, is important for causal learning in such systems. However, existing CLI tests were limited to counting processes. In this paper, we propose a nonparametric CLT test for Itô processes. Specifically, we first introduce a testing statistic based on the Local Covariance Measure (LCM) by constructing a martingale from the conditional expectation of the process of interest. For estimation, we propose an efficient estimator based on the optimal filtering equation, which can achieve root- N N consistency. To establish the asymptotic level and power of the test, we relax the restrictive boundedness condition to a moment bound condition, which is practical for Itô processes. We verify the proposed test in synthetic and real-world experiments.



Keywords:
Conditional local independence, Itô process, Causal discovery

1
Background and the Testing statistic

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)
be a probability space,
(\mathcal{F}_{t})
,
0\leq t\leq T
, be a nondecreasing family of sub-
\sigma
-algebras (a filtration), and
W=(W_{t},\mathcal{F}_{t})
be a Wiener process.

We first give the definition of Itô process.

Definition 1.1

(Itô process;
Liptser & Shiryaev 2013
Def. 6 (p. 116))
.

The continuous-time random process
X=(X_{t},\mathcal{F}_{t})
,
0\leq t\leq T
, is called an
Itô process
if there exists two adapted processes
\lambda=(\lambda_{t},\mathcal{F}_{t})
and
\sigma=(\sigma(t),\mathcal{F}_{t})
, such that with probability
1
for
0\leq t\leq T
,

X_{t}=X_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\lambda(s,\omega)ds+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(s,\omega)dW_{s}.

(1)

For brevity it is said that the process
X_{t}
has the stochastic differential

dX_{t}=\lambda(t,\omega)dt+\sigma(t,\omega)dW_{t},

(2)

with (
2
) understood as shorthand for the representation given by (
1
).

For the multidimensional case, we let
X=(X_{t},\mathcal{F}_{t})
,
0\leq t\leq T
, be a
d
-dimensional Itô process
X_{t}=(X_{1}(t),...,X_{d}(t))
, having the stochastic differential

dX_{t}=\lambda(t,\omega)dt+\sigma(t,\omega)dW_{t},

(3)

where
W=(W_{t},\mathcal{F}_{t})
,
0\leq t\leq T
, is a
d
-dimensional Wiener process,
W_{t}=(W_{1}(t),...,W_{d}(t))
. The vector
\lambda(t,\omega)=(\lambda_{1}(t,\omega),...,\lambda_{d}(t,\omega))
and matrix
\sigma(t,\omega)=[\sigma_{ij}(t\omega)]
,
i,j=1,...,d
, consist of adaptive processes. In more complete form, (
3
) is written as follows:

dX_{i}(t)=\lambda_{i}(t,\omega)dt+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sigma_{ij}(t,\omega)dW_{j}(t),\quad i=1,...,d.

In this paper, we consider a special class of Itô process, namely the
regular
Itô process, where the drift process
\lambda(t,\omega)
is continuous almost surely and the diffusion coefficient
\sigma(t,\omega)\equiv\sigma
is an invertible matrix. Further, we assume
\lambda(t,\omega)
is bounded in
L_{2}
, that is

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathrm{E}|\lambda(t)|^{2}<\infty.

(4)

We then introduce the definition of conditional local independence. We denote
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\alpha}
be the usual augmentation of the filtration generated by
X_{\alpha}
for
\alpha=1,...,d
. Denote
[d]:=\{1,...,d\}
. For a subset
C\subseteq[d]
, denote
\mathcal{F}^{C}_{t}:=\sigma(\mathcal{F}^{\alpha}_{t};\alpha\in C)
.

Definition 1.2

(Conditional local independence;
Mogensen & Hansen 2022
, Def. 3)
.

We say the process
X_{\beta}
is locally independent with
X_{\alpha}
given
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\beta\cup C}
, and write
\alpha\not\to\beta|\beta\cup C
, if the process

t\mapsto\mathrm{E}(\lambda_{\beta}(t)|\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\beta\cup C})

is a version of

t\mapsto\mathrm{E}(\lambda_{\beta}(t)|\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\alpha\cup\beta\cup C})

Here, the
\mathrm{E}(\cdot|\mathcal{F}_{t})
should be interpreted as the optional projection process (
Rogers & Williams 2000
; Thm. 7.1 (Vol. 2, p. 319)), which is unique up to distinguishability. This is unproblematic in calculation (
Rogers & Williams 2000
; Thm. 7.10 (Vol. 2, p. 320)).

We make two remarks regarding Def.
1.2
. First, conditional local independence holds if the intensity process
\lambda_{\beta}(t)
, which characterizes the change rate of
X_{\alpha}
relative to
t
, does not depend on the sample path of the
\alpha
-coordinate. Second, Def.
1.2
is consistent with Def. 2.1 made by
Christgau et al. 2024
for discrete-time counting process. In
Christgau et al. 2024
, a counting process
N_{t}
is said to be locally independent of another process
X_{t}
given
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{N,Z}
, if the martingale component in the Doob-Meyer decomposition of
N_{t}
is also a
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{N,X,Z}
-martingale. This is equivalent to requiring that the
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{N,Z}
-intensity of
N_{t}
,
\lambda_{t}:=\mathrm{E}[\lambda_{t}^{\text{all}}|\mathcal{F}_{t-}^{N,Z}]
, is a version of the
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{N,X,Z}
-intensity
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t}:=\mathrm{E}[\lambda_{t}^{\text{all}}|\mathcal{F}_{t-}^{N,X,Z}]
, which coincides with the virtue of Def.
1.2
. Def.
1.2
writes
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\beta\cup C}
(resp.
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\alpha\cup\beta\cup C}
) instead of
\mathcal{F}_{t-}^{\beta\cup C}
(resp.
\mathcal{F}_{t-}^{\alpha\cup\beta\cup C}
) because the augmented filtration is continuous (Coro. 7.8,
Karatzas & Shreve 2014
).

To ease the notation, we let
\mathcal{F}_{t}:=\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\beta\cup C}
and
\mathcal{G}_{t}:=\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\alpha\cup\beta\cup C}
hereafter. We denote
\mu_{t}:=\mathrm{E}(\lambda_{\beta}(t)|\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\beta\cup C})
and
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t}:=\mathrm{E}(\lambda_{\beta}(t)|\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\alpha\cup\beta\cup C})
. We also denote
M_{t}:=X_{\beta}(t)-\int_{0}^{t}\mu_{s}ds
and
\mathbf{M}_{t}:=X_{\beta}(t)-\int_{0}^{t}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{s}ds
.

We now extend the Local Covariance Measure (LCM) statistic proposed by
Christgau et al. 2024
to Itô process. Define

G_{t}:=X_{\alpha}(t)-\Pi(t)=X_{\alpha}(t)-\mathrm{E}(X_{\alpha}(t)|\mathcal{F}_{t}),

as the additive residual process of
X_{\alpha}
given
\mathcal{F}_{t}
. Here,
\Pi(t):=\mathrm{E}(X_{\alpha}(t)|\mathcal{F}_{t})
denotes the predictable projection of
X_{\alpha}
to
\mathcal{F}_{t}
(
Rogers & Williams 2000
; Thm. 19.2 (Vol. 2, p. 348)). It is clear that
G_{t}
is caglad and
\mathcal{G}_{t}
-adapted. Further, it satisfies that

\mathrm{E}(G_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t})=0,\quad 0\leq t\leq T.

Definition 1.3

(LCM statistic for Itô process)
.

Define for
t\in[0,T]

\gamma_{t}:=\mathrm{E}(I_{t}),\quad\text{where}\,\,I_{t}:=\int_{0}^{t}G_{s}dM_{s}

whenever the expectation is well-defined. We call the function
t\mapsto\gamma_{t}
the Local Covariance Measure (LCM).

We make the following remark on Def.
1.3
. It can be shown that
M_{t}
is an
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-martingale. Further, under
\mathbb{H}_{0}:\alpha\not\to\beta|\beta\cup C
, we have
M_{t}
is a version of
\mathbf{M}_{t}
, which is a
\mathcal{G}_{t}
-martingale. Therefore, under
\mathbb{H}_{0}
, the integrator in
I_{t}
is a
\mathcal{G}_{t}
-martingale. Since the integrand,
G_{t}
, is a
\mathcal{G}_{t}
-predictable process, we have
I_{t}
is a well-defined stochastic integral and hence enjoys the zero-mean property
\gamma_{t}=\mathrm{E}(I_{t})\equiv 0
. Thus, we can check
\mathbb{H}_{0}
by checking the deviation of
\sup_{t}\gamma_{t}
from zero.

The above remark is formalized as follows.

Proposition 1.4
.

Assume (
4
), then
M_{t}
is a continuous, square-integrable
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-martingale, and
\mathbf{M}_{t}
is a continuous, square-integrable
\mathcal{G}_{t}
-martingale.

Proposition 1.5
.

Under
\mathbb{H}_{0}
, the process
I=(I_{t})
is a
\mathcal{G}_{t}
-local martingale with
I_{0}=0
. If
I
is a martingale, we have
\gamma_{t}=0
for
t\in[0,T]
.

The following proposition explains the name of Local Covariance Measure.

Proposition 1.6
.

For every
0\leq t\leq T
, we have

\gamma_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{cov}(G_{s},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{s}-\mu_{s})ds.

In particular,
\gamma
is the zero-function if and only
\mathrm{cov}(G_{t},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t}-\mu_{t})=0
for almost all
t
.

By Prop.
1.6
, the LCM quantifies deviations from
\mathbb{H}_{0}
in terms of the covariance between the residual process and the difference of the
\mathcal{F}_{t}
- and
\mathcal{G}
-projection of the intensity process.

2
Estimation

In this section, we introduce the estimation procedure of the LCM statistic.

Estimation setup.
We split the time interval
[0,T]
into discrete time grids
\{0,\delta,2\delta,...,n\delta\}
, with
T=n\delta
. We assume that we can only observe data points at the time grids. Let
\rho(t):=k\delta
for
t\in[k\delta,(k+1)\delta)
. Further, we assume the availability of
N
i.i.d samples
X^{1},...,X^{N}
. We split the data into two disjoint subsets
j\in J_{N}
and
j\in J_{N}^{c}
for estimation. Let (with a bit abuse of notation)
N:=|J_{N}|
and
N_{c}:=|J_{N}^{c}|
.

We summarize the estimation procedure in the following algorithm.

Algorithm 1

Estimation for LCM

1:

\hat{\gamma}_{k\delta}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\hat{I}_{k\delta,j}
.

2:

\hat{I}_{k\delta,j}^{(N)}=\sum_{l=1}^{k}\hat{G}_{l\delta,j}(\hat{M}_{l\delta,j}-\hat{M}_{(l-1)\delta,j})
.

3:

\hat{G}_{l\delta,j}=X_{l\delta,j}^{\alpha}-\hat{\Pi}_{l\delta,j}
.

4:

\hat{M}_{l\delta,j}=X_{l\delta,j}^{\alpha}-\hat{\Lambda}_{l\delta,j}
, where
\hat{\Lambda}_{l\delta,j}=\sum_{i=1}^{l}\hat{\mu}_{i\delta,j}\delta
.

5:

Estimate
\hat{\Pi}_{l\delta,j}
and
\hat{\mu}_{i\delta,j}
with the filtering estimators.

The nontrivial part of Alg.
1
lies in the estimation of
\hat{\Pi}
and
\hat{\mu}
(line 5), for which we elaborate below. The processes
\Pi
and
\mu
are both projection processes. Hence, their evolutions follow the
filtering equations
(
Liptser & Shiryaev 2013
; Thm. 8.1 (Vol. 1, p. 318)), which are essentially SDEs with drift and diffusion terms related to
\lambda(\cdot)
and
\sigma(\cdot)
. When the equation is closed, i.e., it does not contain terms that cannot be expressed (in general) as functions of
\Pi
(resp.
\mu
), we can consider the Euler-Maruyama method with estimated
\hat{\lambda}
and
\hat{\sigma}
for approximation. On the other hand, if the equation is not closed, we can find some numerically tractable approximation to the solution. One popular approach of such is of the Monte Carlo type; the so-called
particle filtering methods
(
Budhiraja et al. 2007
; Sec. 4), roughly speaking, propagate a collection of random samples in such a way that the probability of observing these “particles” in a certain set
A
is an approximation of
\Pi_{t}(1_{A})
. For these method, convergence results are known
Crisan & Doucet 2002
.

In this note, for simplicity, we consider the case where the filtering equation is closed; and in particular the case where
\lambda(\cdot)
is linear and
\sigma(\cdot)
is constant, i.e., the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck model that is defined as follows:

Definition 2.1

(Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process;
Liptser & Shiryaev 2013
Def. 7 (p.118))
.

The Itô process
X=(X_{t},\mathcal{F}_{t})
,
0\leq t\leq T
, is called a (time-homogeneous)
diffusion process
if its stochastic differential has

dX_{t}=\lambda(X_{t})dt+\sigma(X_{t})dW_{t}.

(5)

That is, if there exists measurable functions
\lambda(\cdot),\sigma(\cdot)
such that in (
3
),
\lambda(t,\omega)=\lambda(X_{t}(\omega))
and
\sigma(t,\omega)=\sigma(X_{t}(\omega))
. Further, it is called an
Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process
if the function
\lambda(\cdot)
is linear and the function
\sigma(\cdot)
is constant. That is,
X
has the stochastic differential

dX_{t}=\Phi X_{t}dt+\Sigma^{1/2}dW_{t},

(6)

where
\Phi
is a
d\times d
matrix and
\Sigma^{1/2}
is the matrix of the diffusion coefficients. It is assumed that the covariance matrix
\Sigma=[\Sigma^{1/2}][\Sigma^{1/2}]^{\prime}
is positive definite.

The Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process is also known as the (multi-factor) Vasicek model in economics
Vasicek 1977
;
Langetieg 1980
. It also has important applications in physics, chemistry
Van Kampen 1992
, and neuroscience
Wilmer et al. 2010
;
Gilson et al. 2019
.

For the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck model, we can first estimate the parameters, then estimate
\Pi
and
\mu
by plugging-in the filtering equation. In the subsequent two sections, we will introduce the details of these two steps.

2.1
Estimation of the OU model

In this section, we consider estimating the parameter matrix
\Phi
and
\Sigma
of the OU process. As mentioned previously, our data consists of
N_{c}
i.i.d. observations of (
6
) sampled at a time interval
\delta_{c}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{D}^{c}=\{(X_{0}(j),X_{\delta_{c}}(j),...)\}_{j=1}^{N_{c}}
.

For estimation,
Phillips 1972
showed that the discrete-time observation follows the SVAR(1) process:

X_{t}=FX_{t-1}+\varepsilon_{t},\quad t=0,\delta_{c},...

(7)

where
F=e^{\Phi\delta_{c}}
with the matrix exponential definition
e^{\Phi\delta_{c}}:=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k!}(\Phi\delta_{c})^{k}
and
\varepsilon_{t}
is a martingale difference sequence with covariance matrix

\Omega=\mathrm{E}(\varepsilon_{t}\varepsilon_{t}^{\top})=\int_{0}^{\delta}e^{\Phi s}\Sigma e^{\Phi s}ds.

Phillips 1973
showed that
(\Phi,\Sigma)
is identifiable from
(F,\Omega)
if and only if the matrix
\Phi
is identifiable from
F=e^{\Phi\Delta}
. He gave a rank condition for the case of linear homogeneous relations between the elements of a row of
\Phi
. A special case is when
\Phi
is triangular.
Hansen & Sargent 1983
and
Wang 2012
extended this result variously. Here, we assume the following condition.

Assumption 2.2
.

The matrix
\Phi
is identifiable from
F=e^{\Phi\delta}
.

For the process (
7
), it can be shown that:

F=\Gamma(-1)\Gamma(0)^{-1},

where
\Gamma(-1):=EX_{\delta}X_{0}^{\top}
and
\Gamma(0):=EX_{0}X_{0}^{\top}
are the auto-covariance matrices. Hence, we can estimate
F
by:

\hat{F}=C_{N}(-1)C_{N}(0)^{-1},

where
C_{N}(-1):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j}X_{\delta}(j)X_{0}(j)^{\top}
and
C_{N}(0):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j}X_{0}(j)X_{0}(j)^{\top}
are the sample covariance matrices.

To obtain an estimate of
\Phi
from
\hat{F}
, we can consider the Taylor approximation:

F=e^{\Phi\delta_{c}}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\Phi\delta_{c})^{k}}{k!}=I+\Phi\delta_{c}+\frac{\Phi\delta_{c}}{2}(F-I)+\eta,

where
\eta=-\{\frac{(\Phi\delta_{c})^{3}}{2\cdot 3!}+\frac{2(\Phi\delta_{c})^{4}}{2\cdot 4!}+\cdots+\frac{(k-2)(\Phi\delta_{c})^{k}}{2\cdot k!}+\cdots\}
. Consequently, we have:

\Phi=\frac{2}{\delta_{c}}(F-I)(F+I)^{-1}-\frac{2}{\delta_{c}}\eta(F+I)^{-1}.

After neglecting terms smaller than
O(\delta_{c}^{2})
, we obtain an estimator:

\hat{\Phi}=\frac{2}{\delta}(\hat{F}-I)(\hat{F}+I)^{-1}.

In practice, to achieve consistency, we introduce two refinements to the estimation.
First
, we truncate the singular values of the matrix
I+\hat{F}
into the interval
[1,3]
. This truncation bounds the norms of
\hat{F}
and
(I+\hat{F})^{-1}
, which is essential for proving the convergence of
\mathrm{E}\|\hat{\Phi}-\Phi\|^{r},(r\geq 1)
. We justify this step by showing that the singular values of
I+\hat{F}
fall into
[1,3]
almost surely.
Second
, we truncate the entries of
\hat{\Phi}
to
[-U,U]
, where
U
is a variable that goes to infinity with some appropriate rate. This modification controls the tail behavior of
\hat{\Phi}
, ensuring the convergence of the plug-in estimators. In details, since
\hat{\Pi}
and
\hat{\mu}
depend exponentially on
\hat{\Phi}
, bounding
\hat{\Phi}
prevents excessive accumulated error, thereby guaranteeing the convergence of
\mathrm{E}|\hat{\Pi}-\Pi|^{2}
and
\mathrm{E}|\hat{\mu}-\mu|^{2}
.

We summarize the estimation of
\Phi
in the following algorithm.

Algorithm 2

Estimation for OU model

1:

\hat{F}=C_{N}(-1)C_{N}(0)
.

2:

\hat{M}=I+\hat{F}
.

3:

Find
\hat{M}=USV^{\top}
with SVD.

4:

Truncate
S
into
[1,3]
. Call the resulted matrix
\bar{S}
.

5:

\bar{M}=U\bar{S}V^{\top}
.

6:

\bar{F}=\bar{M}-I
.

7:

\bar{\Phi}=\frac{2}{\delta_{c}}(\bar{F}-I)(\bar{F}+I)^{-1}
.

8:

Truncate the entries of
\bar{\Phi}
into
[-U,U]
. Call the resulted estimator
\tilde{\Phi}
.

Furthermore, the covariance matrix
\Omega
of the SVAR(1) process (
7
) can be estimated by the sample covariance of the fitting residual:

\hat{\Omega}=\frac{1}{N-d-1}\sum_{j}\left\{X_{t}(j)-\hat{F}X_{t-1}(j)\right\}\left\{X_{t}(j)-\hat{F}X_{t-1}(j)\right\}^{\top}.

Phillips 1973
introduced a connection between
\Omega
and the diffusion matrix
\Sigma
:

\mathrm{vec}\,\Sigma=\left\{F\otimes F-I\otimes I\right\}^{-1}\left\{\Phi\otimes I+I\otimes\Phi\right\}\mathrm{vec}\,\Omega,

where
\otimes
denotes the Kronecker product.

Therefore, we can estimate
\Sigma
by:

\mathrm{vec}\,\hat{\Sigma}=\left\{\hat{F}\otimes\hat{F}-I\otimes I\right\}^{-1}\left\{\hat{\Phi}\otimes I+I\otimes\hat{\Phi}\right\}\mathrm{vec}\,\hat{\Omega}.

(8)

2.2
Estimation of the Projection processes

We now introduce our proposed method for estimating the projection process
\Pi_{t}
and
\mu_{t}
. As mentioned earlier, our estimator is based on the
optimal filtering equation
, for which we first outline below:

Let
\theta=[\theta_{1},...,\theta_{k}]^{\top},\xi=[\xi_{1},...,\xi_{l}]^{\top}
be a continuous-time diffusion process with

\displaystyle d\theta_{t}=\left\{a_{0}(t,\xi)+a_{1}(t,\xi)\theta_{t}\right\}dt+\sum_{i=1}^{2}b_{i}(t,\xi)dW_{i}(t),

(9a)

\displaystyle d\xi_{t}=\left\{A_{0}(t,\xi)+A_{1}(t,\xi)\theta_{t}\right\}dt+\sum_{i=1}^{2}B_{i}(t,\xi)dW_{i}(t),

(9b)

where
a_{0}(t,x):\mathcal{T}\times\mathcal{X}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{k},A_{0}(t,x):\mathcal{T}\times\mathcal{X}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{l}
are vector-valued functionals,
a_{1}:k\times k,A_{1}:l\times k,b_{1}:k\times k,b_{2}:k\times l,B_{1}:l\times k,B_{2}:l\times l
are matrix-valued functionals, and
W_{1}=[W_{11},...,W_{1k}]^{\top},W_{2}=[W_{21},...,W_{2l}]^{\top}
are vector Wiener processes.

Denote
m_{t}:=\mathrm{E}(\theta_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\xi})=\left[\mathrm{E}(\theta_{1,t}|\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\xi}),...,\mathrm{E}(\theta_{k,t}|\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\xi})\right]^{\top}
as the projection process of
\theta
relative to
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\xi}
. Further, let
\gamma=\mathrm{E}\{(\theta_{t}-m_{t})(\theta_{t}-m_{t})^{\top}|\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\xi}\}
be the conditional covariance process of the projection error.

Theorem 2.3

(Filtering equation;
Liptser & Shiryaev 2013
, Thm. 12.7 (Vol. II, p. 36))
.

Let
(\theta,\xi)
be a random process with differentials given in (
9
a) and (
9
b). Suppose that some regularity conditions for integrability hold, and that
P(\theta_{0}\leq a|\xi_{0})\sim\mathcal{N}(m_{0},\gamma_{0})
is Gaussian. Use the notations:

b\circ b=b_{1}b_{1}^{\top}+b_{2}b_{2}^{\top},\quad b\circ B=b_{1}B_{t}^{\top}+b_{2}B_{2}^{\top},\quad B\circ B=B_{1}B_{1}^{\top}+B_{2}B_{2}^{\top}.

Then,
m_{t}
and
\gamma_{t}
satisfy the equations:

\displaystyle dm_{t}=(a_{0}+a_{1}m_{t})+(b\circ B+\gamma_{t}A_{t}^{-1})(B\circ B)^{-1}\left\{d\xi_{t}-(A_{0}+A_{1}m_{t})dt\right\},

(10a)

\displaystyle\dot{\gamma}_{t}=a_{t}\gamma_{t}+\gamma_{t}a_{1}^{\top}+b\circ b-(b\circ B+\gamma_{t}A_{1}^{\top})(B\circ B)^{-1}(b\circ B+\gamma_{t}A_{1}\top)^{\top},

(10b)

subject to the condition
m_{0}=\mathrm{E}(\theta_{0}|\xi_{0}),\gamma_{0}=\mathrm{E}\left\{(\theta_{0}-m_{0})(\theta_{0}-m_{0})^{\top}|\xi_{0}\right\}
.

Theorem 2.4

(
Liptser & Shiryaev 2013
, Thm. 12.7 (Vol. II, p.36))
.

Let the conditions of Thm.
2.3
be satisfied. Then the system of equations (
10
a)-(
10
b), subject to the initial condition on
m_{0},\gamma_{0}
, has a unique, continuous,
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\xi}
-adapted solution for any
t
,
0\leq t\leq T
.

The significance of Thm.
2.4
is that it ensures the solution obtained from forwarding (
10
a)-(
10
b) is exactly the projection progress
m_{t}
and the covariance process
\gamma_{t}
.

We now introduce our proposed estimator for the projection process
\Pi_{t}
. To simplify the expression, we consider the case of isotropic noise
\Sigma=\sigma^{2}I
as in
Bellot et al. 2022
. For a matrix
\Phi\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and two subsets
I,J\subseteq[d]
, we denote
\Phi_{I,J}=\left[\Phi_{i,j}\right]_{(i,j)\in I\times J}
as the submatrix induced by the
I
-th rows and
J
-th columns of
\Phi
.

In (
9
), substitute with

\displaystyle\quad\qquad\qquad\theta=X_{\alpha\cup D},\quad\xi=X_{\beta\cup C},\quad W_{1}=W_{\alpha\cup D},\quad W_{2}=W_{\beta\cup C},

3
Asymptotic analysis (I)

To establish the asymptotics of the LCM statistic, one of the keys is to show the consistency of the projection estimators
\hat{\Pi}
and
\hat{\mu}
, as required in
Christgau et al. 2024
(Asm. 4.2). In this section, we focus on addressing this problem.

To streamline the discussion, we introduce some notations. For a vector
x
, let
\|x\|
denote its vector
\ell_{2}
-norm. For a matrix
A
, let
\mathrm{vec}(A)
denote its vectorization. We define the entrywise
\ell_{2}
-norm of
A
as

\|A\|:=\|\mathrm{vec}(A)\|=\left(\sum_{i,j}|A_{i,j}|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

We now introduce the condition that we aim to prove. Define

\|X\|:=\left(\mathrm{E}\int_{0}^{T}\|X_{s}\|^{2}ds\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

as the
\ell_{2}
-norm for any (vector) random process
X\in L_{2}(\Omega\times[0,T])
. Then, the condition we aim to prove is:

Condition 3.1
.

Denote
g(N_{c},\delta_{c},U,\delta):=\|\Pi-\hat{\Pi}\|
and
h(N_{c},\delta_{c},U,\delta)=\|\mu-\hat{\mu}\|
. Then, we require that each of the sequences:
g
,
h
, and
N_{c}^{\frac{1}{2}}g\cdot h
converges to zero. That is,

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}N_{c}\to\infty,\delta_{c}\to 0\\
U\to\infty,\delta\to 0\end{subarray}}\max\{g(N_{c},\delta_{c},U,\delta),h(N_{c},\delta_{c},U,\delta),N_{c}^{\frac{1}{2}}g(N_{c},\delta_{c},U,\delta)h(N_{c},\delta_{c},U,\delta)\}=0.

We make the following remark on Condition
3.1
. Compared with
Christgau et al. 2024
that studied discrete-time processes, we here study the continuous-time Itô process. Therefore, the error terms in Cond.
3.1
are also influenced by the granularity of the time grid (
\delta
and
\delta_{c}
). Thus, to vanish the error, we require not only sufficient large sample size
N_{c}\to\infty
, but also refined time partition
\delta\to 0,\delta_{c}\to 0
. This is similarly adopted in
Denis et al. 2020
;
Denis et al. 2021
.

In the subsequence, we first analyze the consistency of the OU estimator introduced in Alg.
2
. Based on this, we then discuss the fulfillment of Cond.
3.1
.

3.1
Consistency of the OU estimator

In this section, we analyze the consistency of the OU estimator proposed in Sec.
2.1
. To this end, we first show that:

Proposition 3.2
.

For every integer
r\geq 1
, there exists a positive constant
K=K(r,\Phi)
such that for sufficiently large
N_{c},U
and sufficiently small
\delta_{c}
:

\mathrm{E}\|\Phi-\tilde{\Phi}\|^{r}\leq K\left(\frac{1}{\delta_{c}^{r}N_{c}^{\frac{r}{2}}}+\delta_{c}^{2r}\right).

The proof is left in Appx.
B.2
. To comprehend, note that we have:

\displaystyle\Phi-\bar{\Phi}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\delta_{c}}\left\{(F-I)(F+I)^{-1}-(\bar{F}-I)(\bar{F}+I)^{-1}\right\}+\nu

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\delta_{c}}(I+F)^{-1}(F-\bar{F})(I+\bar{F})^{-1}+\nu,

where
\nu:=-\frac{2}{\delta_{c}}\eta(F+I)^{-1}
. It then follows that:

\mathrm{E}\|\Phi-\bar{\Phi}\|^{r}\leq 2^{r}\left\{\left(\frac{2}{\delta_{c}}\|(I+F)^{-1}\|\right)^{r}\mathrm{E}\|F-\bar{F}\|^{r}\|(I+\bar{F})^{-1}\|^{r}+\|\nu\|^{r}\right\}.

On the right-hand side, the first term is
O\{1/(\delta_{c}^{r}N_{c}^{\frac{r}{2}})\}
, which follows from the fact that
\mathrm{E}\|F-\bar{F}\|^{r}=O(1/N_{c}^{\frac{r}{2}})
, as similar to the convergence rate of moments for sample mean
Brillinger 1962
;
Ren & Liang 2001
; and that
\|(I+\bar{F})^{-1}\|
is bounded (by
1
) due to the truncation in Alg.
2
(line 4). The second term is
O(\delta_{c}^{2r})
since
\nu=O(\delta_{c}^{2})
.

Further, since
\tilde{\Phi}=\bar{\Phi}
almost surely for sufficiently large
U
, we can show that
\mathrm{E}\|\Phi-\tilde{\Phi}\|^{r}
has a consistent convergence rate with
\mathrm{E}\|\Phi-\bar{\Phi}\|^{r}
.

We also show that:

Proposition 3.3
.

Consider the estimator
\hat{\Sigma}
in (
8
), it holds that:

\hat{\Sigma}\to_{\mathcal{P}}\Sigma

under the asymptotic scheme of Prop.
3.2
.

Proof.

According to
Lütkepohl 2005
(Coro. 3.2.1),
\hat{\Omega}\to_{\mathcal{P}}\Omega
. Since

and
\tilde{\Phi}\to_{\mathcal{P}}\Phi

∎

3.2
Consistency of the Projection estimators

In this section, we discuss the consistency of the projection estimators as required in Cond.
3.1
. To this end, we show that:

Proposition 3.4
.

Consider the estimators
\hat{\Pi}
and
\hat{\mu}
introduced in Sec.
2.2
, we have:

\max\{N_{c}^{\frac{1}{4}}g(N_{c},\delta_{c},U,\delta),N_{c}^{\frac{1}{4}}h(N_{c},\delta_{c},U,\delta)\}\to 0

under the asymptotic scheme of
N_{c}\to\infty,\delta_{c}=\Theta(N_{c}^{-\frac{1}{6}}),U=O(\frac{1}{3kT}\log\frac{1}{6}\log N_{c})
,
\delta=O(N_{c}^{-\frac{2}{3}})
. In this regard, we have Cond.
3.1
is fulfilled.

The proof is left in Appx.
B.3
. We sketch the outline below. Take the convergence of
g
as an example, the error can be decomposed into:

g(N_{c},\delta_{c},U,\delta)=\|m-\hat{m}\|\leq\underbrace{\|m-\tilde{m}\|}_{:=g_{1}(N_{c},\delta_{c},U)}+\underbrace{\|\tilde{m}-\hat{m}\|}_{:=g_{2}(N_{c},\delta_{c},U,\delta)},

(18)

where
\tilde{m}
is the continuous process satisfying:

d\tilde{m}_{t}=(\tilde{A}_{t}\xi_{t}+\tilde{B}_{t}\tilde{m}_{t})dt+\tilde{C}_{t}d\xi_{t}.

On the right-hand side of (
18
), the first term

represents the estimation error induced by plugging-in
\tilde{\Phi}
, and the second term
g_{2}
is the discretization error induced by the Euler-Maruyama approximation.

Using the Gronwall’s lemma, we can show that
g_{1}^{2}
is bounded by:

O\left\{e^{M_{B}^{2}}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathrm{E}\left(\|A_{t}-\tilde{A}_{t}\|^{2}+\|B_{t}-\tilde{B}_{t}\|^{2}+\|C_{t}-\tilde{C}_{t}\|^{2}\right)\right\},

where
M_{B}
is the upper bound of
B(\Phi,t)
over
[-U,U]^{d}\times[0,T]
.

To analyze the rates of
M_{B}
and
\sup_{t}\mathrm{E}\|A_{t}-\tilde{A}_{t}\|^{2}
, we first use the Gronwall’s lemma to show that:

\|y_{t}\|=O\left(e^{\frac{1}{2}T\|\Phi\|}\right),\,\,\|y_{t}-\tilde{y}_{t}\|=O\left(e^{\|y_{t}\|+\|\tilde{y}_{t}\|}\|\Phi-\tilde{\Phi}\|\right).

Since
M_{B}
and
A_{t}-\tilde{A}_{t}
rely on
y_{t}
and
y_{t}-\tilde{y_{t}}
, respectively, we can then show that:

M_{B}=O\left(U^{4}e^{\frac{1}{2}Td^{2}U}\right),\,\,\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathrm{E}\|A_{t}-\tilde{A}_{t}\|^{2}=O\left(e^{e^{\frac{1}{2}Td^{2}U}}\mathrm{E}\|\Phi-\tilde{\Phi}\|^{2}\right).

The rates of
\sup_{t}\mathrm{E}\|B_{t}-\tilde{B}_{t}\|^{2}
and
\sup_{t}\mathrm{E}\|C_{t}-\tilde{C}_{t}\|^{2}
can be similarly obtained. Consequently, we can show that
g_{1}^{2}=O\left(e^{e^{Td^{2}U}}\mathrm{E}\|\Phi-\tilde{\Phi}\|^{2}\right)
. By setting an appropriate rate of

, we can let

converge to zero.

As for the second term

, the standard result on the strong convergence of the Euler-Maruyama shows that

is bounded by
C(N_{c},\delta_{c},U)\sqrt{\delta}
. Hence, we can control
N_{c}^{\frac{1}{4}}g_{2}
to zero as long as
\delta\to 0
in a sufficiently fast way.

4
Asymptotic analysis (II)

In this section, we derive asymptotic results regarding the general LCM estimator as a random process. Recall that the LCM estimator
\hat{\gamma}
constructed in Alg.
1
satisfies that:

\hat{\gamma}_{t}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j}\int_{0}^{t}\hat{G}_{j,s}d\hat{M}_{j,s}.

With this form, we consider the decomposition:

\sqrt{N}\hat{\gamma}=U+R_{1}+R_{2}+R_{3}+D_{1}+D_{2},

where the processes on the right-hand side are given by:

\displaystyle U_{t}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{j}\int_{0}^{t}G_{j,s}d\mathbf{M}_{j,s},

\displaystyle R_{1,t}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{j}\int_{0}^{t}G_{j,s}(\mu_{j,s}-\hat{\mu}_{j,s})ds,

\displaystyle R_{2,t}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{j}\int_{0}^{t}(\hat{G}_{j,s}-G_{j,s})d\mathbf{M}_{j,s},

\displaystyle R_{3,t}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{j}\int_{0}^{t}(\hat{G}_{j,s}-G_{j,s})(\mu_{j,s}-\hat{\mu}_{j,s})ds,

\displaystyle D_{1,t}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{j}\int_{0}^{t}G_{j,s}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j,s}-\mu_{j,s})ds,

\displaystyle D_{2,t}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{j}\int_{0}^{t}(\hat{G}_{j,s}-G_{j,s})(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j,s}-\mu_{j,s})ds.

During the establishment of the decomposition, we use the fact that:

\hat{M}_{s}-\mathbf{M}_{s}=\left\{X_{\beta}(\xi(s))-X_{\beta}(s)\right\}+\left\{\int_{0}^{s}\mathbf{\mu}_{u}du-\int_{0}^{s}\hat{\mu}_{u}du\right\},

and also the fact that:

\int_{0}^{t}\hat{G}_{s}dX_{\beta}(\xi(s))=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\hat{G}_{(i-1)\delta}\left[X_{\beta}(i\delta)-X_{\beta}\left\{(i-1)\delta\right\}\right]=\int_{0}^{t}\hat{G}_{s}dX_{\beta}(s).

We now proceed to show that the remainder terms
R_{1},R_{2},R_{3}
converge to the zero-processes. To this end, we impose the following assumptions:

Assumption 4.1
.

There exists
p>2
such that the process
\mu
and
G
are bounded in
L_{p}
. That is, we assume

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathrm{E}|\mu_{t}|^{p}<\infty,\quad\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathrm{E}|G_{t}|^{p}<\infty.

We also assume the process
\boldsymbol{\mu}
, the estimators
\hat{\mu}
and
\hat{G}
satisfy the same bounds as
\mu
and
G
, respectively.

A similar but stronger assumption on bounding the infinity norms of
\mu
and
G
is required in
Christgau et al. 2024
. This kind of assumption is important to establish the stochastic equicontinuity of the remainder process, and hence achieve convergence uniformly over
t
using the chaining argument. Nonetheless, unlike
Christgau et al. 2024
that require
\mu
and
G
to be bounded, which can be unpractical for Ito processes, we here only bound their moments. These moment bounds can be satisfied by processes whose drift functions are Lipschitz (see Corollary
B.6
).

Furthermore, we also require the estimators
\hat{\mu}
and
\hat{G}
to be consistent, as shown in the following assumption:

Assumption 4.2
.

There exists
r>2
such that
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{r}=\frac{1}{2}
for the
p
in Asm.
4.1
,

\lim_{N\to\infty,\delta\to 0}\max\left\{\|\mu-\hat{\mu}\|_{r},\,\|G-\hat{G}\|_{r},\,\sqrt{N}\|\mu-\hat{\mu}\|_{2}\|G-\hat{G}\|_{2}\right\}=0.

With these assumptions, we can establish that the remainder terms converge uniformly to the zero process.

Proposition 4.3
.

Under Asm.
4.1
and Asm.
4.2
, it holds that

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|R_{i,t}|\to 0\,\,\text{ in probability }

The proof is left in Appx.
C.1

Furthermore, under Asm.

Type I and Type II error rate control.

, where
(W_{t})
is a standard Wiener process. We let
S
denote a random variable with such a distribution and note that its CDF can be written as:

F_{S}(x)=P(S\leq x)=\frac{4}{\Pi}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{2k+1}\exp\left\{-\frac{\pi^{2}(2k+1)^{2}}{8x^{2}}\right\},\quad x>0.

(21)

The p-value for a test of
\mathbb{H}_{0}
equals to
1-F_{S}(\hat{T})
, and since the series in (
21
) converges at an exponential rate, the p-value can be computed with high numerical precision. Furthermore, for a significance level
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the
(1-\alpha)
-quantile of
F_{S}
, denoted as
z_{1-\alpha}
, exists and is unique since the right-hand side of (
21
) is strictly increasing and continuous.

Then, the
Local Covariance Test
(LCT) with significant level
\alpha
is defined by:

\Phi=1\left\{F_{S}(\hat{T})>1-\alpha\right\}=1\left(\hat{T}>1-\alpha\right).

Based on Thm.
4.8
and Prop.
4.9
, we can deduce the asymptotic level of the LCT:

Theorem 5.2
.

Under Asms.
4.1
,
4.2
and
5.1
, we have:

\hat{T}\to_{\mathcal{D}}S

as
N\to\infty,\delta\to 0
. As a consequence, for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
, we have:

\limsup_{N\to\infty,\delta\to 0}P(\Phi=1)\leq\alpha.

In other words, the LCT has an asymptotic level
\alpha
.

We left the proof in Appx.
D.1
.

In general, we can not expect the test to have power against alternatives to
\mathbb{H}_{0}
. This is analogous to other types of conditional independence tests. However, we do have the following result that establishes power against local alternatives in which
\|\gamma\|_{\infty}
decays at an order of at most
N^{-\frac{1}{2}}
:

Theorem 5.3
.

Assume Asm.
4.1
and Asm.
4.2
. Then, for any
0<\alpha<\beta<1
, there exists
c>0
such that:

\liminf_{N\to\infty,\delta\to 0}\inf_{\theta\in\mathcal{A}_{c,N}}P(\Phi=1)\geq\beta,

where
\mathcal{A}_{c,N}:=\left\{\theta:\|\gamma^{\theta}\|_{\infty}\geq cN^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\}
. In other words, the type II error rate of the test can be controlled to arbitrarily small.

We left the proof in Appx.
D.2
.

Proposition

M_{t}:=X_{\beta}(t)-\int_{0}^{t}\mu_{s}ds=\int_{0}^{t}\lambda_{\beta}(s)-\mathrm{E}(\lambda_{\beta}(s)|\mathcal{F}_{s})ds+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sigma_{\beta j}W_{j}(t).

which together mean

is square-integrable.

In the following, we show
\mathrm{E}(M_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{u})=M_{u}
for

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}(A_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{u})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\lambda_{\beta}(s)-\mathrm{E}(\lambda_{\beta}(s)|\mathcal{F}_{s})ds\Big|\mathcal{F}_{u}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{E}(\lambda_{\beta}(s)|\mathcal{F}_{u})-\mathrm{E}(\lambda_{\beta}(s)|\mathcal{F}_{s}|\mathcal{F}_{u})ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{u}\mathrm{E}(\lambda_{\beta}(s)|\mathcal{F}_{u})-\mathrm{E}(\lambda_{\beta}(s)|\mathcal{F}_{s}|\mathcal{F}_{u})ds+\int_{u}^{t}\mathrm{E}(\lambda_{\beta}(s)|\mathcal{F}_{u})-\mathrm{E}(\lambda_{\beta}(s)|\mathcal{F}_{s}|\mathcal{F}_{u})ds,

B.1.1
The Gronwall’s lemma

We first introduce the Gronwall’s lemma that is frequently used in time-series analysis.

Lemma B.1

(Gronwall’s lemma)
.

Let
I
denote an interval of the real line of the form
[a,\infty)
or
[a,b]
or
[a,b)
with
a<b
. Let
\alpha,\beta
and
u
be real-valued functions defined on
I
. Assume that
\beta
and
u
are continuous and that the negative part of
\alpha
is integrable on every closed and bounded subinterval of
I
.

If
\beta
is non-negative and if
u
satisfies the integral inequality

u(t)\leq\alpha(t)+\int_{a}^{t}\beta(s)u(s)ds,\quad\forall t\in I

then

u(t)\leq\alpha(t)+\int_{a}^{t}\alpha(s)\beta(s)\exp\left(\int_{s}^{t}\beta(r)dr\right)ds,\quad t\in I.

Corollary B.2
.

Suppose that
u(t)
satisfies:

u(t)\leq u_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\alpha(s)u(s)ds+\int_{0}^{t}\beta(s)ds,

where
\alpha\geq 0
and
\beta
is integrable, then it holds that:

u(t)\leq u_{0}e^{A(t)}+\int_{0}^{t}\beta(s)e^{A(t)-A(s)}ds,

where
A(t):=\int_{0}^{t}\alpha(s)ds
.

Corollary B.3

(Differential version)
.

Suppose that
u(t)
satisfies that

u(t)^{\prime}\leq a(t)u(t)+b(t)

for some
a,b\in L_{1}([0,T])
. Then,

u(t)\leq e^{A(t)}u(0)+\int_{0}^{t}b(s)e^{A(t)-A(s)}ds,

where
A(t):=\int_{0}^{t}\alpha(s)ds
.

Corollary B.4

(Discrete version)
.

Consider a positive real number sequence
\{u_{n}\}
such that

u_{n}\leq\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\alpha_{k}u_{k}+B_{k},\quad\forall n\geq 0,

where
\{\alpha_{n}\}
and
\{B_{n}\}
are two given real number (sequences), and
\{\alpha_{n}\}
is furthermore positive. Then

u_{n}\leq B_{n}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\alpha_{k}B_{k}\exp\{\sum_{i=k}^{n-1}\alpha_{i}\}.

Proposition B.5
.

B.1.3
Solution of RDE: Properties

In this section, we discuss properties of the solution
y_{t}=y(\Phi,t)
to the RDE (
11
b). We will first derive a upper bound of
\sup_{t}\|y_{t}\|
in terms of
\|\Phi\|

\sup_{t}\|y(\Phi_{1},t)-y(\Phi_{2},t)\|
between two solutions under two parameters

\Phi_{2}

Proposition B.8
.

Consider the RDE

\dot{y}_{t}=ay_{t}+y_{t}a^{\top}+I-y_{t}by_{t},\quad y_{0}=y(0),

(22)

where
a,b,y_{t}
are all
k\times k
matrices. Suppose that
b
is positive semidefinite. Then, the solution
y_{t}
satisfies that:

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|y_{t}\|\leq\frac{1}{2}k\left\{\mathrm{tr}(y_{0})+kT\right\}e^{T\|a\|_{1}},

where
\|\cdot\|_{1}
denotes the matrix entrywise
\ell_{1}

Proof.

Since
b
and the identity matrix

are both positive semidefinite, the condition of Thm. 2-1
Potter 1965
is satisfied. We then have the solution
y_{t}
is positive semidefinite. This implies that:

\left|y_{ij}(t)\right|^{2}\leq y_{ii}(t)y_{jj}(t)\leq\frac{1}{4}\left\{y_{ii}(t)+y_{jj}(t)\right\}^{2},

and therefore

\left|y_{ij}(t)\right|\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}\left\{y(t)\right\}.

Then,

|\mathrm{tr}(ay_{t})|=|\mathrm{tr}(y_{t}a^{\top})|=\left|\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}a_{ij}y_{ji}(t)\right|\leq\frac{1}{2}\|a\|_{1}\mathrm{tr}\left\{y(t)\right\}.

(23)

Since
y_{t}by_{t}
is positive semidefinite, its diagonal elements are nonnegative, and hence

\mathrm{tr}(y_{t}by_{b})\geq 0.

(24)

Taking the trace of both sides of (

23
) and (
24
) yields that:

\frac{d}{dt}\mathrm{tr}(y_{t})\leq\|a\|_{1}\mathrm{tr}(y_{t})+k.

Apply the differential Gronwall’s lemma (Coro.

), we obtain that:

2|y_{ij}(t)|\leq\mathrm{tr}(y_{t})\leq e^{\|a\|_{1}t}\mathrm{tr}(y_{0})+\frac{k}{\|a\|_{1}}(e^{\|a\|_{1}t}-1).

Therefore, we have:

\displaystyle\|y_{t}\|\leq\frac{k}{2}\left\{e^{\|a\|_{1}t}\mathrm{tr}(y_{0})+\frac{k}{\|a\|_{1}}(e^{\|a\|_{1}t}-1)\right\}\leq\frac{1}{2}k\left\{\mathrm{tr}(y_{0})+kT\right\}e^{T\|a\|_{1}},

which concludes the proof.

Proposition B.9
.

Consider the solutions of the RDE under two different parameters:

\displaystyle\dot{y}_{1}=a_{1}y_{1}+y_{1}a_{1}^{\top}+I-y_{1}b_{1}y_{1},

\displaystyle\dot{y}_{2}=a_{2}y_{2}+y_{2}a_{2}^{\top}+I-y_{2}b_{2}y_{2}.

Then, we have

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|y_{1}(t)-y_{2}(t)\|\leq\alpha Te^{\beta T},

where

\displaystyle\alpha=\sup_{t}\|y_{1}(t)\|\cdot\sup_{t}\|y_{2}(t)\|\cdot\|b_{1}-b_{2}\|+2\sup_{t}\|y_{1}(t)\|\cdot\|a_{1}-a_{2}\|,

\displaystyle\beta=\sup_{t}\|y_{1}(t)\|\cdot\|b_{1}\|+\sup_{t}\|y_{2}(t)\|\cdot\|b_{2}\|+2\|a_{1}\|.

Proof.

Denote

z(t):=\|y_{1}(t)-y_{2}(t)\|.

We then have that:

z(t)\leq\int_{0}^{t}2\|a_{1}y_{1}(s)-a_{2}y_{2}(s)\|+\|y_{1}(s)b_{1}y_{1}(s)-y_{2}(s)b_{2}y_{2}(s)\|ds.

Consider the fact that:

\displaystyle\|a_{1}y_{1}(s)-a_{2}y_{2}(s)\|

\displaystyle\leq\|a_{1}y_{1}(s)-a_{1}y_{2}(s)\|+\|a_{1}y_{2}(s)-a_{2}y_{2}(s)\|

\displaystyle=\|a_{1}\|\cdot\|y_{1}(s)-y_{2}(s)\|+\|a_{1}-a_{2}\|\cdot\|y_{2}(s)\|.

Similarly, it can be shown that:

\displaystyle\|y_{1}(s)b_{1}y_{1}(s)-y_{2}(s)b_{2}y_{2}(s)\|

\displaystyle\quad\leq\left\{\|b_{1}\|\cdot\|y_{1}(s)\|+\|b_{2}\|\|y_{2}(s)\|\right\}\|y_{1}(s)-y_{2}(s)\|+\|y_{1}(s)\|\cdot\|y_{2}(s)\|\cdot\|b_{1}-b_{2}\|.

Therefore, we have that:

z(t)\leq\alpha T+\int_{0}^{t}\beta z(s)ds.

Then, use the Gronwall’s lemma, we obtain that:

z(t)\leq\alpha T+\alpha T\beta\int_{0}^{t}e^{\beta(t-s)}ds=\alpha Te^{\beta t}.

Since
t\mapsto e^{\beta t}
is monotonically increasing, we have that:

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}z(t)\leq\alpha Te^{\beta T}.

This concludes the proof.
∎

B.2
Proof of Prop.
3.2

In this section, we introduce the proof of Prop.
3.2
. To this end, we first discuss the consistency of
\hat{F}
as defined in line 1 of Alg.
2
.

Lemma B.10
.

For evert integer
r\geq 1
, there exists a positive constant
K=K(r,\Phi)
such that for sufficiently large
N_{c}
,

\mathrm{E}\|\hat{F}-F\|^{r}\leq KN_{c}^{-\frac{1}{2}r}.

Proof.

We have the decomposition

\displaystyle\hat{F}-F

\displaystyle=C_{N}(-1)C_{N}(0)^{-1}-F

\displaystyle=\left[C_{N}(-1)-\Gamma(-1)-F\left\{C_{N}(0)-\Gamma(0)\right\}\right]C_{N}(0)^{-1}.

Take the matrix norm, we obtain that:

\|\hat{F}-F\|^{r}\leq 2^{r-1}\left\{\|C_{N}(-1)-\Gamma(-1)\|^{r}+\|F\|^{r}\|C_{N}(0)-\Gamma(0)\|^{r}\right\}\|C_{N}(0)^{-1}\|^{r}.

Take expectation and use the Cauchy’s inequality, we have that:

\mathrm{E}\|\hat{F}-F\|^{r}\leq 4^{r}\left\{\mathrm{E}\|C_{N}(-1)-\Gamma(-1)\|^{2r}+\|F\|^{2r}\mathrm{E}\|C_{N}(0)-\Gamma(0)\|^{2r}\right\}^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\mathrm{E}\|C_{N}(0)^{-1}\|^{2r}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Note that for a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, it holds that:

\|A\|^{r}=\left(\sum_{i,j}|A_{i,j}|^{2}\right)^{\frac{r}{2}}\leq d^{r-2}\sum_{i,j}|A_{i,j}|^{r}=d^{r-2}|\mathrm{vec}(A)|_{r}^{r},

where
|\cdot|_{r}
denote the
\ell_{r}
vector norm.

Therefore, we have
\mathrm{E}\|\hat{F}-F\|^{r}
is controlled by:

4^{r}d^{2r-2}\left\{\mathrm{E}|\mathrm{vec}C_{N}(-1)-\mathrm{vec}\Gamma(-1)|^{2r}_{2r}+\|F\|^{2r}\mathrm{E}|\mathrm{vec}C_{N}(0)-\mathrm{vec}\Gamma(0)|^{2r}_{2r}\right\}^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\mathrm{E}\|C_{N}(0)^{-1}\|^{2r}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

In the following, we will show that
\mathrm{E}|\mathrm{vec}C_{N}(-1)-\mathrm{vec}\Gamma(-1)|^{2r}_{2r}
and
\mathrm{E}|\mathrm{vec}C_{N}(0)-\mathrm{vec}\Gamma(0)|^{2r}_{2r}
are controlled by
K_{1}N_{c}^{-r}
for some
K_{1}>0
. We will also show that
\mathrm{E}\|C_{N}(0)^{-1}\|^{2r}
is bounded by some
K_{2}>0
for sufficiently large
N_{c}
.

To be specific, we have

\mathrm{vec}C_{N}(-1)-\mathrm{vec}\Gamma(-1)=\frac{1}{N_{c}}\sum_{j}\mathrm{vec}X_{\delta_{c}}(j)X_{0}(j)^{\top}-\mathrm{E}\mathrm{vec}X_{\delta_{c}}(j)X_{0}(j)^{\top}=:\frac{1}{N_{c}}\sum_{j}Y_{j},

where
\left\{Y_{j}=\mathrm{vec}X_{\delta_{c}}(j)X_{0}(j)^{\top}-\mathrm{E}\mathrm{vec}X_{\delta_{c}}(j)X_{0}(j)^{\top}\mid j\in J_{N}^{c}\right\}
are i.i.d copies of the zero-mean,
L_{r}
-integrable random vector
Y=\mathrm{vec}X_{\delta_{c}}X_{0}^{\top}-\mathrm{E}\mathrm{vec}X_{\delta_{c}}X_{0}^{\top}
.

Hence, apply
Ren & Liang 2001
with
p=r
, we obtain that:

\mathrm{E}|\mathrm{vec}C_{N}(-1)-\mathrm{vec}\Gamma(-1)|^{2r}_{2r}\leq K_{1}N^{-r}_{c}

for some constant
K_{1}>0
.

In a similar way, denote
Z:=\mathrm{vec}X_{0}X_{0}^{\top}-\mathrm{E}\mathrm{vec}X_{0}X_{0}^{\top}
and denote
\{Z_{j}\mid j\in J_{N}^{c}\}
as i.i.d copies of
Z
, we have that:

E|\mathrm{vec}C_{N}(0)-\mathrm{vec}\Gamma(0)|^{2r}_{2r}=\left|\frac{1}{N_{c}}\sum_{j}Z_{j}\right|^{2r}_{2r}\leq K_{2}N^{-r}_{c}

for some constant
K_{2}>0
.

Considering the fact that
\|F\|^{2r}\leq e^{\|\Phi\|^{2r}\delta_{c}^{2r}}
is bounded for
\delta_{c}\leq 1
, we have that:

\left\{\mathrm{E}|\mathrm{vec}C_{N}(-1)-\mathrm{vec}\Gamma(-1)|^{2r}_{2r}+\|F\|^{2r}\mathrm{E}|\mathrm{vec}C_{N}(0)-\mathrm{vec}\Gamma(0)|^{2r}_{2r}\right\}^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq KN^{-\frac{1}{2}r}_{c},

(25)

for some constant
K>0
.

Below, we will show that
\mathrm{E}\|C_{N}(0)^{-1}\|^{2r}
is bounded for sufficiently large
N_{c}
. To this end, we can apply the main theorem of
Bhansali & Papangelou 1991
with
p=1,q=2r
and
A_{j}=X_{0}(j)X_{0}(j)^{\top}
. It is clear that the sequence
\{X_{0}(j)\}_{j\in J_{N}^{c}}
satisfies the conditions (I)-(III) in
Bhansali & Papangelou 1991
. The theorem then states that there exists a number
N_{0}
and a nonnegative random variable
\Lambda_{0}
with
E(\Lambda_{0}^{2r})<\infty
, such that for all sufficiently large
N_{c}>N_{0}
:

\left\|\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j}X_{0}(j)X_{0}(j)^{\top}\right]^{-1}\right\|_{sp}\leq\Lambda_{0},\quad\text{almost surely,}

where
\|\cdot\|_{sp}
denotes the matrix spectral norm.

Since the Lebesgue integral on a zero-measure set is zero, we then have that

\mathrm{E}\left\|\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j}X_{0}(j)X_{0}(j)^{\top}\right]^{-1}\right\|_{sp}^{2r}\leq\mathrm{E}(\Lambda_{0}^{2r})<\infty

is bounded.

Then, use the equivalence of the matrix entrywise
\ell_{2}
-norm and the spectral norm, we obtain that
\left(\mathrm{E}\|C_{N}(0)^{-1}\|^{2r}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}
is bounded for sufficiently large
N_{c}
. Together with (
25
), this result concludes the proof.

In the following, we will introduce a lemma on the Lipschitz continuity of the singular value of a matrix. This result will help us show Prop.
3.2
.

Lemma B.11

(
Golub & Van Loan 2013
Coro. 8.6.2)
.

Denote
\sigma_{i}(A)
as the
i
-th largest singular value of the matrix
A
. If
A
and
A+E
are in
\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}
with
p\geq q
, then for
k=1,...,q

|\sigma_{k}(A+E)-\sigma_{k}(A)|\leq\|E\|_{{sp}},

where
\|\cdot\|_{sp}
denotes the matrix spectral norm.

We now introduce the proof of Prop.
3.2
.

Proposition
3.2
.

For every integer
r\geq 1
, there exists a positive constant
K=K(r,\Phi)
such that for sufficiently large
N_{c},U
and sufficiently small
\delta_{c}
:

\mathrm{E}\|\Phi-\tilde{\Phi}\|^{r}\leq K\left(\frac{1}{\delta_{c}^{r}N_{c}^{\frac{r}{2}}}+\delta_{c}^{2r}\right).

Proof.

The proof consists of three steps. First, we discuss the consistency of
\bar{F}
that is defined in line 6 of Alg.
2
; followed by the consistency of
\bar{\Phi}
(line 7 of Alg.
2
). Finally, we show the consistency of
\tilde{\Phi}
.

First, we discuss the consistency
of
\bar{F}
. We show that

E\|F-\bar{F}\|^{r}=O\left(\frac{1}{N^{\frac{r}{2}}}+\frac{1}{N^{2r}}+\delta_{c}^{4r}\right)

Specifically, denote
\mathcal{A}
as the event “
1<\sigma_{\min}(\hat{M})<3
”, we have the decomposition

\mathrm{E}\|F-\bar{F}\|^{r}=\mathrm{E}(\|F-\bar{F}\|^{r}1_{\mathcal{A}})+\mathrm{E}(\|F-\bar{F}\|^{r}1_{{}^{\neg}\mathcal{A}}).

For the first term, we have there exists
K
such that

\mathrm{E}(\|F-\bar{F}\|^{r}1_{\mathcal{A}})=\mathrm{E}(\|F-\hat{F}\|^{r}1_{\mathcal{A}})\leq\mathrm{E}(\|F-\hat{F}\|^{r})=O(\frac{1}{N^{\frac{r}{2}}}),

due to Lem.
B.10
.

For the second term, we have there exists another
K>0
such that

\|F-\bar{F}\|^{r}\leq 2^{r-1}(\|F\|^{r}+\|\bar{F}\|^{r})\leq 4^{r-1}(\|F\|^{r}+\|\bar{M}\|^{r}+\|I\|^{r})\leq K

since the largest singular value of
\bar{M}
is bounded below
3
(line 4), which implies
\|\bar{M}\|\leq 3
.

It follows that

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}(\|F-\bar{F}\|^{r}1_{{}^{\neg}\mathcal{A}})

\displaystyle\leq KP(^{\neg}\mathcal{A})=KP(\sigma_{\min}(\hat{M})\leq 1\lor\sigma_{\max}(\hat{M})\geq 3)

\displaystyle\leq K\left\{P(\sigma_{\min}(\hat{M})\leq 1)+P(\sigma_{\max}(\hat{M})\geq 3)\right\}.

Use the Markov inequality,

P(\sigma_{\min}(\hat{M})\leq 1)\leq P(|\sigma_{\min}(\hat{M})-2|\geq 1)\leq\mathrm{E}|\sigma_{\min}(\hat{M})-2|^{4r}.

With Lem.
B.11
, we further have:

|\sigma_{\min}(\hat{M})-2|\leq\|\hat{F}-F\|_{{sp}}+\|H\|_{{sp}},

where we define

with
\|H\|_{sp}=O(\delta_{c})
.

As a result, we have:

\mathrm{E}|\sigma_{\min}(\hat{M})-2|^{4r}\leq 2^{4r-1}(\mathrm{E}\|\hat{F}-F\|_{{sp}}^{4r}+\|H\|_{{sp}}^{4r})=O\left(\frac{1}{N^{2r}}+\delta_{c}^{4r}\right).

Therefore, we have
P(\sigma_{\min}(\hat{M})\leq 1)=O\left(\frac{1}{N^{2r}}+\delta_{c}^{4r}\right)
. In a similar way, we can show that
P(\sigma_{\max}(\hat{M})\geq 3)=O\left(\frac{1}{N^{2r}}+\delta_{c}^{4r}\right)
.

Thus, we have
\mathrm{E}(\|F-\bar{F}\|_{1}1_{{}^{\neg}\mathcal{A}})=O\left(\frac{1}{N^{2r}}+\delta_{c}^{4r}\right)
. As a result, we have:

E\|F-\bar{F}\|^{r}=O\left(\frac{1}{N^{\frac{r}{2}}}+\frac{1}{N^{2r}}+\delta_{c}^{4r}\right).

Second, we show the consistency
of
\bar{\Phi}
. Recall that
\Phi
can be written as

\Phi=\frac{2}{\delta_{c}}(F-I)(F+I)^{-1}+\nu,\quad\nu=-\frac{2}{\delta_{c}}\eta(F+I)^{-1},

where

\eta=-\frac{(\Phi\delta_{c})^{3}}{2}\sum_{i=3}^{\infty}\frac{(i-2)(\Phi\delta_{c})^{i-3}}{i!}.

Hence, we have:

\Phi-\bar{\Phi}=\frac{2}{\delta_{c}}\left\{(F-I)(F+I)^{-1}-(\bar{F}-I)(\bar{F}+I)^{-1}\right\}+\nu.

It follows that:

\displaystyle\|\Phi-\bar{\Phi}\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\delta_{c}}\left\|(F-I)(F+I)^{-1}-(\bar{F}-I)(\bar{F}+I)^{-1}\right\|+\|\nu\|

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\delta_{c}}\left\|(I+F)^{-1}(F-\bar{F})(I+\bar{F})^{-1}\right\|+\|\nu\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\delta_{c}}\|(I+F)^{-1}\|\cdot\|F-\bar{F}\|\cdot\|(I+\bar{F})^{-1}\|+\|\nu\|.

Among the four terms on the right-hand side, we have
\|\nu\|=O(\delta_{c}^{2})
. In the following, we will discuss the other three terms
\|(I+F)^{-1}\|
,
\|F-\tilde{F}\|
and
\|(I+\tilde{F})^{-1}\|
, respectively. During the discussion, we sometimes use the entrywise
\ell_{1}
-norm
\|\cdot\|
and the spectral norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{sp}}
interchangeably, as they are equivalent up to a constant.

For

\|(I+F)^{-1}\|_{{sp}}
, we have
\|(I+F)^{-1}\|_{{sp}}=1/\sigma_{\min}(I+F)
equals the inverse of the smallest singular value of
I+F
. Since
F=e^{\Phi\delta}=I+H
, where
\|H\|_{{sp}}=O(\delta_{c})
, we have due to Lem.
B.11
that

|\sigma_{\min}(I+F)-2|\leq\|H\|_{{sp}}=O(\delta_{c}).

Hence, there exists
\delta_{0}>0
such that
\sigma_{\min}(I+F)\geq 1
for all
\delta<\delta_{0}
. Consequently, we have
\|(I+F)^{-1}\|_{{sp}}=1/\sigma_{\min}(I+F)\leq 1
is bounded.

For

\|(I+\bar{F})^{-1}\|_{{sp}}
, we have
\|(I+\bar{F})^{-1}\|_{{sp}}=1/\sigma_{\min}(I+\bar{F})\leq 1
due to Alg.
2
(line 4).

For

\|F-\bar{F}\|
, we have shown that:

E\|F-\bar{F}\|^{r}=O\left(\frac{1}{N^{\frac{r}{2}}}+\frac{1}{N^{2r}}+\delta_{c}^{4r}\right).

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\|\Phi-\bar{\Phi}\|^{r}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{r}\left[\left(\frac{2}{\delta_{c}}\right)^{r}\|(I+F)^{-1}\|^{r}\cdot\mathrm{E}\left\{\|F-\bar{F}\|^{r}\cdot\|(I+\bar{F})^{-1}\|^{r}\right\}+\|\nu\|^{r}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2^{r}\left\{\left(\frac{2}{\delta_{c}}\right)^{r}\mathrm{E}\|F-\bar{F}\|^{r}+\|\nu\|^{r}\right\}

\displaystyle=O\left(\frac{1}{\delta_{c}^{r}N^{\frac{r}{2}}}+\delta_{c}^{2r}\right).

Finally, we show the consistency
of
\tilde{\Phi}
. Denote
\mathcal{E}
as the event of “
|\bar{\Phi}|\leq U
”. We then have the decomposition:

\mathrm{E}\|\tilde{\Phi}-\Phi\|^{r}=\mathrm{E}(\|\tilde{\Phi}-\Phi\|^{r}1_{\mathcal{E}})+\mathrm{E}(\|\tilde{\Phi}-\Phi\|^{r}1_{{}^{\neg}\mathcal{E}}).

For the first term in the decomposition, we have:

\mathrm{E}(\|\tilde{\Phi}-\Phi\|^{r}1_{\mathcal{E}})=\mathrm{E}(\|\bar{\Phi}-\Phi\|^{r}1_{\mathcal{E}})\leq\mathrm{E}\|\bar{\Phi}-\Phi\|^{r}=O\left(\frac{1}{\delta_{c}^{r}N^{\frac{r}{2}}}+\delta_{c}^{2r}\right).

For the second term, we first have:

\|\tilde{\Phi}-\Phi\|\leq\|\tilde{\Phi}\|+\|\Phi\|\leq U+\|\Phi\|.

It follows that:

\mathrm{E}(\|\tilde{\Phi}-\Phi\|^{r}1_{{}^{\neg}\mathcal{E}})\leq(U+\|\Phi\|)^{r}P(^{\neg}\mathcal{E}).

Note that

\displaystyle P(^{\neg}\mathcal{E})

\displaystyle=P(|\bar{\Phi}|>U)=P(|\bar{\Phi}|-|\Phi|>U-|\Phi|)

\displaystyle\leq P(|\bar{\Phi}-\Phi|>U-|\Phi|)\leq\frac{\mathrm{E}|\bar{\Phi}-\Phi|^{r}}{(U-|\Phi|)^{r}}.

Therefore, for sufficiently large
U
, we have that:

\mathrm{E}(\|\tilde{\Phi}-\Phi\|^{r}1_{{}^{\neg}\mathcal{E}})\leq\frac{(U+\|\Phi\|)^{r}}{(U-|\Phi|)^{r}}\mathrm{E}|\bar{\Phi}-\Phi|^{r}=O(\mathrm{E}|\bar{\Phi}-\Phi|^{r}).

As a result, we can show that:

\mathrm{E}\|\tilde{\Phi}-\Phi\|^{r}=O(\mathrm{E}\|\bar{\Phi}-\Phi\|^{r})=O\left(\frac{1}{\delta_{c}^{r}N^{\frac{r}{2}}}+\delta_{c}^{2r}\right),

which concludes the proof.
∎

\mathrm{E}|R_{1,t}-R_{2,t}|^{2}=\mathrm{E}\left|\int_{0}^{t}G_{u}(\mu_{u}-\hat{\mu})_{u})du\right|^{2}\leq(t-s)\int_{s}^{t}\mathrm{E}|G_{u}(\mu_{u}-\hat{\mu}_{u}))|^{2}du.

Use the Hölder inequality and Asm.
4.1
, the right-hand side can be bounded by
C(t-s)^{2}
, where
C=\sup_{t}\mathrm{E}|G_{t}|^{p}\left(\sup_{t}\mathrm{E}|\mu_{t}|^{r}+\sup_{t}\mathrm{E}|\hat{\mu}_{t}|^{r}\right)
. Therefore, we have
\left(\mathrm{E}|R_{1,t}-R_{2,t}|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq C|t-s|
. This concludes the proof.
∎

We are now ready to introduce the proof of Prop.
C.6
.

Proof of Prop.
C.6
.

We have the collection of random variables

\left\{\int_{0}^{t}G_{j,u}(\mu_{j,u}-\hat{\mu}_{j,u})du\mid j\in J_{N}\right\}

are i.i.d conditionally on
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{1}^{c}
. Use this and (
42
), we have
\mathrm{E}(R_{1,t}|\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{1}^{c})=0
.

Therefore,
\mathrm{Var}(R_{1,t})=\mathrm{E}(\mathrm{Var}(R_{1,t}|\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{1}^{c}))
. From this, we obtain that:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(R_{1,t})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}\,\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j}\mathrm{Var}\left\{\int_{0}^{t}G_{j,s}(\mu_{j,s}-\hat{\mu}_{j,s}ds\mid\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{1}^{c})\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}\,\mathrm{E}\left[\left\{\int_{0}^{t}G_{s}(\mu_{s}-\hat{\mu}_{s})ds\right\}^{2}\mid\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{1}^{c}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}\left\{\int_{0}^{t}G_{s}(\mu_{s}-\hat{\mu}_{s})ds\right\}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq T\left\{\mathrm{E}\int_{0}^{t}|G_{s}|^{p}ds\right\}^{\frac{2}{p}}\left\{\mathrm{E}\int_{0}^{t}|\mu_{s}-\hat{\mu}_{s}|^{r}ds\right\}^{\frac{2}{r}}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\mu_{s}-\hat{\mu}_{s}\|_{r}^{2},

where we have used the Hölder inequality and Asm.
4.1
.

Under Asm.
4.2
,
\|\mu_{s}-\hat{\mu}_{s}\|_{r}^{2}
converges to zero. Hence, by the Chebyshev’s inequality, we have
R_{1,t}\to 0
in probability for every
t
. Then, we can use Lem.
C.4
and the chaining lemma (
Pollard 2012
; Lem. VII 9) to conclude the proof.
∎

C.1.2
Convergence of
R_{2}

Proposition C.5
.

Under Asm.
4.2
, it holds that
\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|R_{2,t}|\to 0
in probability as
N\to\infty,\delta\to 0
.

Proof.

The idea is to first show the process
R_{2,t}
is a martingale, then use Doob’s submartingale inequality to bound the probability
P(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|R_{2,t}|\geq\epsilon)
to zero.

To be specific, use Thm. 6.5.8 (
Kuo 2006
; p. 88) with Prop.
1.4
and Asm.
4.1
, we have that:

t\mapsto\int_{0}^{t}G_{s}d\mathbf{M}_{s}\quad\text{and}\quad t\mapsto\int_{0}^{t}\hat{G}_{s}d\mathbf{M}_{s}

are both square integrable, mean zero
\mathcal{G}_{t}
-martingale conditionally on
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{1}^{c}
.

We then conclude that
R_{2,t}
, which is defined as

R_{2,t}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{j}\int_{0}^{t}(\hat{G}_{j,s}-G_{j,s})d\mathbf{M}_{j,s},

is a square integrable, mean zero
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{t}
-martingale conditionally on
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{1}^{c}
. Hence, the squared process
R_{2,t}^{2}
is a
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{t}
-submartingale.

Then, by Doob’s submartingale inequality,

\displaystyle P\left(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|R_{2,t}|\geq\epsilon\right)

\displaystyle=P\left(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|R_{2,t}|^{2}\geq\epsilon^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}\,P\left(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|R_{2,t}|^{2}\geq\epsilon^{2}\mid\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{1}^{c}\right)\leq\frac{\mathrm{E}\,\mathrm{Var}(R_{2,1}|\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{1}^{c})}{\epsilon^{2}}

for any
\epsilon>0
.

Remind that the collection of random variables

\left\{\int_{0}^{1}(G_{j,u}-\hat{G}_{j,u})d\mathbf{M}_{j,s}\mid j\in J_{N}\right\}

are i.i.d conditionally on
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{1}^{c}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(R_{2,1}|\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{1}^{c})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j}\mathrm{Var}\left\{\int_{0}^{1}(G_{j,u}-\hat{G}_{j,u})d\mathbf{M}_{j,s}\mid\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{1}^{c})\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}\left\{\int_{0}^{1}(G_{s}-\hat{G}_{s})^{2}d[\mathbf{M}]_{s}\mid\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{1}^{c}\right\}=\mathrm{E}\left\{\int_{0}^{1}(G_{s}-\hat{G}_{s})^{2}|\sigma_{\beta}|^{2}ds\mid\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{1}^{c}\right\}.

Thus,

\mathrm{E}\,\mathrm{Var}(R_{2,1}|\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{1}^{c})\leq|\sigma_{\beta}|^{2}\,\mathrm{E}\int_{0}^{1}(G_{s}-\hat{G}_{s})^{2}ds=|\sigma_{\beta}|^{2}\|G-\hat{G}\|_{2}^{2}.

We then conclude that

P\left(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|R_{2,t}|\geq\epsilon\right)\leq\frac{|\sigma_{\beta}|^{2}\|G-\hat{G}\|_{2}^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\to 0

as
N\to\infty,\delta\to 0
by Asm.
4.2
.
∎

Convergence of

Proposition C.6
.

Under Asm.
4.2
, it holds that
\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|R_{3,t}|\to 0
in probability as

.

We show the result by showing that
\mathrm{E}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|R_{3,t}|\right)\to 0
as

.

To be specific, we have that

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|R_{3,t}|\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}\,\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{j}\int_{0}^{t}(G_{j,s}-\hat{G}_{j,s})(\mu_{j,s}-\hat{\mu}_{j,s})ds\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{j}\mathrm{E}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\int_{0}^{t}|G_{j,s}-\hat{G}_{j,s}|\cdot|\mu_{j,s}-\hat{\mu}_{j,s}|ds\right)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{N}\mathrm{E}\int_{0}^{1}|G_{s}-\hat{G}_{s}|\cdot|\mu_{s}-\hat{\mu}_{s}|ds

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{N}\sqrt{\mathrm{E}\int_{0}^{1}|G_{s}-\hat{G}_{s}|^{2}ds}\sqrt{\mathrm{E}\int_{0}^{1}|\mu_{s}-\hat{\mu}_{s}|^{2}ds}

C.2
Proof of Prop.
4.5

In this section, we introduce the proof of Prop.
4.5
. The proof relies on the martingale Central Limit Theorem (CLT)
Rebolledo 1980
and its formulation in
Andersen et al. 2012
, for which we state below:

Theorem C.7

(Martingale CLT;
Andersen et al. 2012
, Thm. II.5.2)
.

Let
\left\{U^{(N)}\right\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of local square integrable martingales in
C[0,T]
, possibly defined on different samples spaces and with different filtrations for each
N
. Let
U_{0}
be a continuous Gaussian martingale with continuous variance function
\mathcal{V}:[0,T]\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{+}
, and assume that
U^{(N})(0)=U_{0}(0)=0
has initial values equal to zero. Suppose further that for every
t\in[0,T]
,

[U^{(N)}]_{t}\to_{\mathcal{P}}\mathcal{V}_{t}

as
N\to\infty
. Then it holds that
U^{(N)}\to_{\mathcal{D}}U_{0}
in
C[0,T]
as
N\to\infty
.

We now state the proof for Prop.
4.5
.

Proposition
4.5
.

Under Asm.
4.1
, it holds that

U\to_{\mathcal{D}}U_{0}

in
C[0,T]
as
N\to\infty
, where
U_{0}
is a mean-zero continuous Gaussian martingale on
[0,T]
with variance function
\mathcal{V}
.

Proof.

We prove by applying the martingale CLT. We first show that
U
is a mean zero, square integrable martingale, following by showing that
[U]_{t}\to_{\mathcal{P}}\mathcal{V}_{t}
for every
t
.

Define
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{t}
to be the smallest regular filtration generated by the filtrations
\{\mathcal{G}_{j,t}\mid j\in J_{N}\}
. Recall that

U_{t}=\sum_{j}\int_{0}^{t}H_{j,s}d\mathbf{M}_{s},\quad\text{where}\,\,H_{i,s}=\frac{G_{j,s}}{\sqrt{N}}.

Follow the proof of Prop.
1.5
, we can show that each component,
\int_{0}^{t}H_{j,s}d\mathbf{M}_{s}
, of
U_{t}
is a mean zero, square integrable martingale under Asm.
4.1
. By independence of the observations for each
j
, we can enlarge the filtration for each term, and conclude that they are also mean zero, square integrable
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{t}
-martingale. Thus,
U
is also a mean zero, square integrable
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{t}
-martingale.

Moreover, apply Thm. 6.5.9 of
Kuo 2006
, we can show that

[I]_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}G_{s}^{2}d[\mathbf{M}]_{s},\quad\text{where}\,\,I_{t}:=\int_{0}^{t}G_{s}d\mathbf{M}_{s}.

Follow the proof of Prop.
1.5
, it can be further shown that

[\mathbf{M}]_{t}=|\sigma_{\beta}|^{2}t.

Therefore, we have

[I]_{t}=|\sigma_{\beta}|^{2}\int_{0}^{t}G_{s}^{2}ds.

Since the components of
U
are independent, their co-quadratic variations are zero. Therefore, we have

[U]_{t}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j}[I_{j}]_{t}=\frac{1}{N}|\sigma_{\beta}|^{2}\sum_{j}\int_{0}^{t}G_{j,s}^{2}ds.

By directly apply the moment bound in Asm.
4.1
, we see that the
\int_{0}^{t}G_{s}^{2}ds
is integrable. Thus, for fixed
t\in[0,T]
, the Law of Large Numbers gives that

[U]_{t}=\frac{1}{N}|\sigma_{\beta}|^{2}\sum_{j}\int_{0}^{t}G_{j,s}^{2}ds\to_{\mathcal{P}}\mathcal{V}_{t}.

The proof now concludes by applying the martingale CLT.
∎

C.3
Proof of Prop.
4.6

In this section, we introduce the proof of Prop.
4.6
.

Proposition
4.6
.
Under Asm.
4.1
and Asm.
4.2
, it holds that

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\hat{\mathcal{V}}(t)-\mathcal{V}(t)|\to_{\mathcal{P}}0

as
N\to\infty,\delta\to 0
.

Proof.

Consider the decomposition of
\hat{\mathcal{V}}
given by:

\hat{V}(t)=|\hat{\sigma}_{\beta}|^{2}\left(A_{t}+B_{t}+C_{t}\right),

where

\displaystyle A_{t}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j}\int_{0}^{t}G_{j,s}^{2}ds,

\displaystyle B_{t}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j}\int_{0}^{t}\left(G_{j,s}-\hat{G}_{j,s}\right)^{2}ds,

\displaystyle C_{t}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j}\int_{0}^{t}G_{j,s}\left(G_{j,s}-\hat{G}_{j,s}\right)ds.

First
, we have that
A_{t}
is the empirical mean of
N
i.i.d. samples of the process
\int_{0}^{t}G_{s}^{2}ds
. Furthermore, Asm.
4.1
assures that
\int_{0}^{t}G_{s}^{2}ds
is integrable. Therefore, applying the Law of Large Numbers yields that:

A_{t}\to_{\mathcal{P}}\int_{0}^{t}G_{s}^{2}ds

for every
t\in[0,T]
.

By the continuous mapping theorem, we further obtain that:

|\hat{\sigma}_{\beta}|^{2}A_{t}\to_{\mathcal{P}}\mathcal{V}(t)

for every
t\in[0,T]
.

Note that
|\hat{\sigma}_{\beta}|^{2}A_{t}
and
\mathcal{V}(t)
are both nondecreasing, and the limiting process
\mathcal{V}(t)
is stochastic equicontinuous in that:

\mathcal{V}(t)-\mathcal{V}(s)=|\sigma_{\beta}|^{2}\int_{s}^{t}\mathrm{E}G_{u}^{2}du\leq C(t-s).

Therefore, applying Lem. B13 of
Christgau et al. 2024
allows us to conclude that:

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left||\hat{\sigma}_{\beta}|^{2}A_{t}-\mathcal{V}(t)\right|\to_{\mathcal{P}}0.

Second
, we have under the assumption
4.2
that:

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}B_{t}=\mathrm{E}B_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}\left\{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j}\int_{0}^{t}\left(G_{j,s}-\hat{G}_{j,s}\right)^{2}ds\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}\mathrm{E}\left\{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j}\int_{0}^{t}\left(G_{j,s}-\hat{G}_{j,s}\right)^{2}ds\mid\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{1}^{c}\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}\int_{0}^{t}\left(G_{s}-\hat{G}_{s}\right)^{2}ds=g^{2}\to 0.

Therefore, we have
\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}B_{t}\to 0
in probability. Applying the continuous mapping theorem, it follows that:

|\hat{\sigma}_{\beta}|^{2}\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}B_{t}\to_{\mathcal{P}}|\sigma_{\beta}|^{2}\cdot 0=0.

Finally
, we see that

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\left|\sup_{t\in[0,T]}C_{t}\right|\leq\mathrm{E}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|C_{t}|

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{E}\left\{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\int_{0}^{t}|G_{j,s}|\cdot|G_{j,s}-\hat{G}_{j,s}|ds\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}\left\{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j}\int_{0}^{1}|G_{j,s}|\cdot|G_{j,s}-\hat{G}_{j,s}|ds\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}\int_{0}^{1}|G_{s}|\cdot|G_{s}-\hat{G}_{s}|ds

\displaystyle\leq\left(\mathrm{E}\int_{0}^{1}|G_{s}-\hat{G}_{s}|^{2}ds\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\mathrm{E}\int_{0}^{1}|G_{s}|^{2}ds\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq Cg\to 0.

Therefore, we have
\sup_{t}C_{t}\to 0
in probability. Applying again the continuous mapping theorem, it follows that:

|\hat{\sigma}_{\beta}|^{2}\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}C_{t}\to_{\mathcal{P}}|\sigma_{\beta}|^{2}\cdot 0=0.

Combining the convergence results
established above, we get by the Slutsky’s theorem that:

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\hat{\mathcal{V}}(t)-\mathcal{V}(t)|\to_{\mathcal{P}}0.

∎

C.4
Proof of Prop.
4.7

In this section, we introduce the proof of Prop.
4.7
. To this end, we will need the following notion of tightness:

Definition C.8

(Tightness;
Billingsley 2013
pp. 8)
.

Let
(\mathbb{D},d)
denote a general metric space. A probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{D}
is
tight
if for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists a compact set
K\subseteq\mathbb{D}
such that
\mu(K^{c})<\epsilon
. A sequence of probability measures
\{\mu_{n}\}
is
tight
if for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists a compact set
K\subseteq\mathbb{D}
such that
\sup_{n}\mu(K^{c})<\epsilon
.

A random variable
X
is
tight
if its induced measure
P\circ X^{-1}
is tight. A sequence of random variables
\{X_{n}\}
is
tight
if the sequence of induced measures
\{P\circ X_{n}^{-1}\}
is tight.

The following result, which we will use in the proof of Prop.
4.7
, is an example pf Prokhorov’s method of “tightness + identification of limit”:

Lemma C.9

(Lem. B.14;
Christgau et al. 2024
)
.

Let
X
and
\{X_{n}\}
be random variables on
(C[0,T],\ell_{\infty})
. Suppose that

(i)

the finite dimensional marginals converge in distribution:
\forall\,0\leq t_{1}<\cdots<t_{k}\leq T
,

\left\{X_{n}(t_{1}),...,X_{n}(t_{k})\right\}\to_{\mathcal{D}}\left\{X(t_{1}),...,X(t_{k})\right\}

as random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
;

(ii)

X
and
\{X_{n}\}
are both tight.

Then, we have
X_{n}\to_{\mathcal{D}}X
on
(C[0,T],\ell_{\infty})
as
n\to\infty
.

For random processes with continuous sample paths, tightness is equivalent to stochastic equicontinuity:

Proposition C.10

(
Billingsley 2013
; Thm. 7.3)
.

Let
\{X_{n}\}
be a sequence of random variables on
C[0,T]
such that
X_{n}(0)=0
almost surely for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then,
\{X_{n}\}
is tight if and only if it is stochastic equicontinuous.

We now introduce the proof of Prop.
4.7
. For clarity. we separate the discussion for
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
into two lemmas, respectively, which together amount to Prop.
4.7
.

Lemma C.11
.

Assume Asm.
4.1
and Asm.
4.2

D_{1}-\sqrt{N}\gamma
converges in distribution in

Proof.

We introduce some notations
to ease the discussion. Denote

\{W_{j}\}
are i.i.d. copies of the random process:

W_{t}:=\int_{0}^{t}G_{s}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{s}-\mu_{s})ds-\gamma_{t}.

and
\mathrm{E}\int_{0}^{t}G_{s}d\mathbf{M}_{s}\equiv 0

Denote
\Gamma

(s,t)\mapsto\mathrm{Cov}(W_{s},W_{t}),

(43)

which is well-defined by the computations shown below.

In the following, we will show that

\tilde{D}_{1}\to_{\mathcal{D}}\Gamma

in

C.9

To this end, we first show

\forall\,0\leq t_{1}<\cdots<t_{k}\leq T

4.1

C[0,T]
as
N\to\infty,\delta\to 0
.

Proof.

We first show

D_{2}(t)\to_{\mathcal{P}}0
for every
t
. Recall that:

D_{2}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{j}\int_{0}^{t}(G_{j,s}-\hat{G}_{j,s})(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{s}-\mu_{s})ds.

Conditionally on
\mathcal{G}_{1}^{c}
, the terms in
D_{2}
are i.i.d. with the same distribution as the process
\zeta
given by:

\zeta_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}(G_{s}-\hat{G}_{s})(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{s}-\mu_{s})ds.

Furthermore, we observe that:

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\left\{(\hat{G}_{t}-G_{t})(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t}-\mu_{t})\mid\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{1}^{c}\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}\left[\mathrm{E}\left\{(\hat{G}_{t}-G_{t})(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t}-\mu_{t})\mid\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{1}^{c}\lor\mathcal{F}_{t}\right\}\mid\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{1}^{c}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}\left\{(\hat{G}_{t}-G_{t})\mathrm{E}\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t}-\mu_{t}\mid\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{1}^{c}\lor\mathcal{F}_{t}\right)\mid\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{1}^{c}\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}\left[(\hat{G}_{t}-G_{t})\left\{\mathrm{E}\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t}\mid\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{1}^{c}\lor\mathcal{F}_{t}\right)-\mu_{t}\right\}\mid\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{1}^{c}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}\left[(\hat{G}_{t}-G_{t})\left\{\mathrm{E}\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}\right)-\mu_{t}\right\}\mid\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{1}^{c}\right]=0.

Hence, we have
E(\zeta_{t}\mid\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{1}^{c})=0
. It now follows that:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(D_{2,t}\mid\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{1}^{c})=\mathrm{E}\left[\left\{\int_{0}^{t}(G_{s}-\hat{G}_{s})(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{s}-\mu_{s})ds\right\}^{2}\mid\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{1}^{c}\right].

Furthermore, since
\mathrm{E}(D_{2,t}\mid\tilde{G}_{1}^{c})=0
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(D_{2,t})=\mathrm{E}\left\{\mathrm{Var}(D_{2,t}\mid\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{1}^{c})\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}\left\{\int_{0}^{t}(G_{s}-\hat{G}_{s})(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{s}-\mu_{s})ds\right\}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq T\left(\|\boldsymbol{\mu}\|_{p}^{2}+\|\mu\|_{p}^{2}\right)\|G-\hat{G}\|_{r}^{2}\leq C\|G-\hat{G}\|_{r}^{2}\to 0

under Asm.
4.1
and Asm.
4.2
. Therefore, we have
D_{2}(t)\to_{\mathcal{P}}0
for every
t
.

In the following, we show that

D_{2}
is stochastic equicontinuous. In this regard, we have
\sup_{t}D_{2}(t)\to_{\mathcal{P}}0
and conclude the proof.

From the derivation above, we observe that:

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}|D_{2,t}-D_{2,s}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}\left\{\int_{s}^{t}(G_{u}-\hat{G}_{u})(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{u}-\mu_{u})du\right\}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(t-s)\int_{s}^{t}\mathrm{E}(G_{u}-\hat{G}_{u})^{2}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{u}-\mu_{u})^{2}du\leq C(t-s)^{2}

with the Hölder inequality, under Asm.
4.1
and Asm.
4.2
.

We then conclude from Coro.
C.3
that
\tilde{D}_{2}
is also stochastic equicontinuous, which concludes the proof.
∎

C.5
Proof of Thm.
4.8

In this section, we introduce the proof of Thm.
4.8
. To this end, we first show the Gaussian martingale
U_{0}
from Prop.
4.5
is tight (Def.
C.8
) in
C[0,T]
.

Lemma C.14
.

Let
U_{0}
be a mean-zero continuous Gaussian martingale on
[0,T]
with variance function
\mathcal{V}
. Then, under Asm.
4.1
,
U_{0}
is tight as a random variable on
C[0,T]
.

Proof.

According to Prop. C.2 of
Christgau et al. 2024
,
U_{0}
has a distributional representation as a time-transformed Wiener process such that:

\left\{U_{0}\right\}_{t\in[0,T]}=_{\mathcal{D}}\left\{W_{\mathcal{V}(t)}\right\}_{t\in[0,T]},

(45)

where
W=(W_{t})
is a standard Wiener process.

In the following, we will use this representation and Thm. 7.3 of
Billingsley 2013
, which characterizes tightness of measures on
C[0,T]
, to show the statement.

The first condition of Thm. 7.3
Billingsley 2013
,
P(U_{0}=0)=1
, is trivially satisfied. To show the second condition, which requires
U_{0}
to be stochastic equicontinuous, we note that for any
\epsilon>0
,

\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}P\left(\sup_{|t-s|<\delta}\left|U_{t}-U_{s}\right|>\epsilon\right)=\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}P\left\{\sup_{|t-s|<\delta}\left|W_{\mathcal{V}(t)}-W_{\mathcal{V}(s)}\right|>\epsilon\right\}

due to the distributional representation (
45
).

Since Wiener process is
\alpha
-Hölder continuous for
\alpha\in\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right)
:

|W_{t}-W_{s}|\leq C|t-s|^{\alpha}.

We further have that:

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}P\left(\sup_{|t-s|<\delta}\left|U_{t}-U_{s}\right|>\epsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}P\left\{C\sup_{|t-s|<\delta}\left|\mathcal{V}(t)-\mathcal{V}(s)\right|^{\alpha}>\epsilon\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}P\left\{CL^{\alpha}\sup_{|t-s|<\delta}\left|t-s\right|^{\alpha}>\epsilon\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}P\left\{CL^{\alpha}\delta^{\alpha}>\epsilon\right\}=0,

where we use the fact that
t\mapsto\mathcal{V}(t)
is
L
-Lipschitz continuous under Asm.
4.1
.

Therefore, the second condition of Thm. 7.3
Billingsley 2013
is satisfied. We conclude that
U_{0}
is tight.
∎

Theorem
4.8
.

Assume Asm.
4.1
and Asm.
4.2
. Then,

(i)

under
\mathbb{H}_{0}
, it holds that

\sqrt{N}\hat{\gamma}\to_{\mathcal{D}}U_{0}

in

as
N\to\infty,\delta\to 0

For the first part
, we first note that under
\mathbb{H}_{0}
,
\mu=\boldsymbol{\mu}
, which implies that
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
are both zero processes.

Combine Prop.
4.3
and Prop.

\sqrt{N}\gamma=\underbrace{U}_{\to_{\mathcal{D}}U_{0}}+\underbrace{R_{1}+R_{2}+R_{3}}_{\to_{\mathcal{P}}0}+\underbrace{D_{1}+D_{2}}_{=0\,\text{under}\,\mathbb{H}_{0}}\to_{\mathcal{D}}U_{0},

4.3

Theorem
5.2
.

Under Asms.

,
4.2

, we have:

\hat{T}\to_{\mathcal{D}}S

as

\alpha\in(0,1)

In other words, the LCT has an asymptotic level
\alpha

4.9
with the function
\mathcal{J}
given by:

\mathcal{J}(f_{1},f_{2}):=1(f_{2}\neq 0)\frac{\|f_{1}\|_{\infty}}{\sqrt{|f_{2}(T)|}},\quad f_{1},f_{2}\in C[0,T].

on the set
\mathcal{A}

\mathcal{A}:=C[0,T]\times\left\{f\in C[0,T]\mid|f(T)|\geq\nu_{0}\right\},

where
\nu_{0}
is the positive constant assumed in Asm.
5.1
.

To see that
\mathcal{J}
is continuous on
\mathcal{A}
with the uniform topology, note that it is the composition of two continuous maps, namely
(f_{1},f_{2})\mapsto(\|f_{1}\|_{\infty},|f_{2}(T)|)
that maps
\mathcal{A}
to
\mathbb{R}^{+}\times[\nu_{0},\infty)

(x_{1},x_{2})\mapsto\frac{x_{1}}{\sqrt{x_{2}}}
that maps
\mathbb{R}^{+}\times[\nu_{0},\infty)

Therefore, Prop.
4.9
implies that:

\hat{T}=\mathcal{J}(\sqrt{N}\hat{\gamma},\hat{\mathcal{V}})\to_{\mathcal{D}}\mathcal{J}(U_{0},\mathcal{V})=\frac{\|U_{0}\|_{\infty}}{\mathcal{V}(T)}.

Use Prop. C.2 of
Christgau et al. 2024
, we can show that:

\frac{\|U_{0}\|_{\infty}}{\mathcal{V}(T)}=_{\mathcal{D}}\frac{\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|W_{\mathcal{V}(t)}\right|}{\mathcal{V}(T)}=_{\mathcal{D}}\frac{\sup_{s\in[0,\mathcal{V}(T)]}\left|W_{s}\right|}{\mathcal{V}(T)}=_{\mathcal{D}}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|W_{t}\right|,

where we have used that
\mathcal{V}
is (Lipschitz) continuous and that the Brownian motion is scale invariant.

For the second part, we first note that the distribution of
S

\limsup_{N\to\infty,\delta\to 0}\left|P(\hat{T}\leq z_{1-\alpha}-(1-\alpha))\right|=0.

Proof of Thm.

Theorem
5.3
.

Assume Asm.
4.1
and Asm.
4.2
. Then, for any
0<\alpha<\beta<1
, there exists
c>0
such that:

\liminf_{N\to\infty,\delta\to 0}\inf_{\theta\in\mathcal{A}_{c,N}}P(\Phi=1)\geq\beta,

where
\mathcal{A}_{c,N}:=\left\{\theta:\|\gamma^{\theta}\|_{\infty}\geq cN^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\}
. In other words, the type II error rate of the test can be controlled to arbitrarily small.

Proof.

Let
0<\alpha<\beta<1
be given. The second part of Thm.
4.8
allows us to choose
K>0
sufficiently large such that:

\limsup_{N\to\infty,\delta\to 0}\sup_{\theta\in\mathcal{A}_{c,N}}P(\sqrt{N}\|\hat{\gamma}-\gamma\|_{\infty}>K)<1-\beta.

Recall that
t\mapsto\mathcal{V}(t)
is (Lipschitz) continuous, thus we have
\mathcal{V}(T)\leq C
is bounded. We then choose
c>K+z_{1-\alpha}\sqrt{1+C^{2}}
such that:

\sqrt{N}\|\gamma\|_{\infty}-z_{1-\alpha}\sqrt{1+\mathcal{V}(T)}\geq c-z_{1-\alpha}\sqrt{1+C^{2}}>K.

The reverse triangle inequality now yields that:

\displaystyle\left\{\Phi=0\right\}=\left\{\hat{T}\leq z_{1-\alpha}\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{\|\hat{\gamma}\|_{\infty}\leq\sqrt{\hat{\mathcal{V}}(T)}\cdot\frac{z_{1-\alpha}}{\sqrt{N}}\right\}

\displaystyle\subseteq\left\{\|\gamma\|_{\infty}-\|\hat{\gamma}-\gamma\|_{\infty}\leq\sqrt{\hat{\mathcal{V}}(T)}\cdot\frac{z_{1-\alpha}}{\sqrt{N}}\right\}.

Therefore, we obtain that:

\displaystyle\sqrt{N}\|\gamma\|_{\infty}\geq K+z_{1-\alpha}\sqrt{1+\mathcal{V}(T)}

\displaystyle\sqrt{N}\left(\|\gamma\|_{\infty}-\|\hat{\gamma}-\gamma\|_{\infty}\right)\leq z_{1-\alpha}\sqrt{\hat{\mathcal{V}}(T)}.

Combine these two inequalities, we obtain that:

\sqrt{N}\left(\|\gamma\|_{\infty}-\|\hat{\gamma}-\gamma\|_{\infty}\right)\geq K+z_{1-\alpha}\left(\sqrt{1+\mathcal{V}(T)}-\sqrt{\hat{\mathcal{V}}(T)}\right).

Then, denote two events:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{1}:=\left\{\sqrt{N}\left(\|\gamma\|_{\infty}-\|\hat{\gamma}-\gamma\|_{\infty}\right)>K\right\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{2}:=\left\{\hat{\mathcal{V}}(T)>1+\mathcal{V}(T)\right\}\subseteq\left\{\left|\hat{\mathcal{V}}(T)-\mathcal{V}(T)\right|>1\right\},

we then have:

\left\{\Phi=0\right\}\subseteq\left\{\|\gamma\|_{\infty}-\|\hat{\gamma}-\gamma\|_{\infty}\leq\sqrt{\hat{\mathcal{V}}(T)}\cdot\frac{z_{1-\alpha}}{\sqrt{N}}\right\}\subseteq\mathcal{E}_{1}\cup\mathcal{E}_{2}.

From Prop.
4.6
, we know that:

\lim_{N\to\infty,\delta\to 0}P(\mathcal{E}_{2})=0.

Hence, from the choice of
K
we conclude that:

\limsup_{N\to\infty,\delta\to 0}\sup_{\theta\in\mathcal{A}_{c,N}}P(\Phi=0)\leq\limsup_{N\to\infty,\delta\to 0}P\left(\mathcal{E}_{2}\right)<1-\beta.

The desired statement now follows from substituting
P(\Phi=0)=1-P(\Phi=1)
into the above equation.
∎
