SPINK4 affected M2 macrophage polarization to promote colorectal cancer malignant phenotype by PI3K/AKT pathway
Authors: Mingxiao Cao, Jiaqi Hao, Lixin Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to elucidate the oncogenic role of SPINK4 in colorectal cancer (CRC) and its underlying mechanism regulating macrophage polarization in the tumor microenvironment. In vitro, we constructed SPINK4-overexpressing and SPINK4-knockdown CRC cell lines (HT29 and HCT116) to evaluate their effects on cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. We then established a macrophage-CRC cell co-culture system to explore whether SPINK4 promotes macrophage recruitment and M2 polarization. Mechanistically, we not only regulated the PI3K/AKT pathway using inhibitor LY294002 and activator 740Y-P but also specifically knocked down PI3K via small interfering RNA (siRNA) to confirm if SPINK4’s function depends on this pathway. In vivo, we established HCT116 xenograft models in nude mice, monitored tumor volume and weight, evaluated tumor proliferation by Ki67 immunohistochemistry, quantified tumor-infiltrating macrophages by flow cytometry, and detected M1/M2 macrophage marker expression by RT-qPCR. Experimental results showed SPINK4 overexpression significantly enhanced CRC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, induced macrophage recruitment and M2 polarization, and upregulated IL-33, IL-4, IL-10, CSF1, CCL2, and VEGF-C secretion. Inhibiting PI3K/AKT reversed these effects. In vivo, SPINK4 overexpression activated PI3K/AKT, promoting tumor growth and M2 macrophage infiltration. Collectively, SPINK4 acts as an oncogene to promote macrophage recruitment and M2 polarization via PI3K/AKT, driving CRC malignant progression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-16126-8.

Introduction

Macrophages are the most abundant immune-related stromal cells in and around tumors [1]. Macrophages influenced tumor development by phagocytosis and producing cytokines and chemokines [2]. Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) finely regulated the biological processes of tumor cell growth, development, invasion, and metastasis through a variety of growth factors and receptors, as well as a complex network of signal transduction. TAM could produce a variety of chemokines, including chemokine (C–C motif) ligand 2 (CCL2), interleukin (IL)−6, and IL-10 et al., and influence the malignant phenotype of cancer through the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway [3], STAT3 pathway [4], and JAK1/STAT1/NF-κB/Notch1 pathway [5], et al.

Macrophages can polarize into M1 and M2 phenotypes in response to tumor microenvironment signals [6]. M1 macrophages showed the strong anti-tumor effects. On the contrary, M2 macrophages promoted angiogenesis, tissue remodeling, and tumor growth [7]. At present, M2 macrophages were confirmed to contribute to the malignant progression of colorectal cancer (CRC), including metastasis [8, 9], drug resistance [10–12], immune evasion [13], et al. A study revealed that tumor associated fibroblasts (CAFs) secreted C-X-C motif chemokine 12 (CXCL12), recruited macrophages and promoted M2 polarization of macrophages, thus accelerating the promotion, migration, invasion and cisplatin resistance of colorectal cancer [12].

Serine protease inhibitor kazal type 4 (SPINK4), a serine protease inhibitor, was initially isolated from pig intestine [14] and mainly expressed in the gastrointestinal tract and immune system [15]. SPINK4 was closely related to the malignant progression of colorectal cancer. Clinical studies showed that serum SPINK4 levels were significantly higher in colorectal cancer patients than in healthy volunteers, with preoperative levels surpassing postoperative levels [16]. Several reports also demonstrated the role and underlying mechanism of SPINK4 in CRC, SPINK4 overexpression promoted CRC tumor growth and metastasis, possibly by inhibiting ferroptosis [17]. ELF-1 is a transcriptional factor upstream of SPINK4, upregulating the expression of SPINK4. SPINK4 knockdown inhibited colon cancer cell proliferation, invasion, and migration [18]. However, there was no study on the regulation of SPINK4 on TAMs in cancers. This was our research focus.

In this study, we studied the relationship between the regulation of SPINK4 on the growth of colorectal cancer and PI3K/AKT, and whether SPINK4 can regulate PI3K/AKT pathway to promote the transformation of M2 macrophages, thereby promoting the malignant phenotype of colorectal cancer.

Materials and methods

Cell incubation and transfection

Human colon cancer cell lines HT29 (CL-0118) and HCT116 (CL-0096) were obtained from Pricella (Wuhan, China) and incubated in McCoy’s 5A medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S) in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

In order to specifically overexpress and downregulate SPINK4, HT29 and HCT116 cells were transfected with lentiviral vectors. SPINK4-overexpressing lentiviruses (SPINK4-OE), which were cloned with the full-length cDNA sequence (NC_000009.12:33240167–33241567) of SPINK4, and SPINK4 knockdown lentiviruses were obtained from GENMEDICN BIOPHARMA (Nanjing, China). The target sequence of SPINK4-KD was 5’-CCCATCTGTGAACACATGGTA-3’. Blank lentiviruses were used as the negative control (NC) of SPINK4-OE and SPINK4-KD. Prior to lentiviral infection, a puromycin killing curve test was completed to screen the optimal drug concentration for cell screening. Continuous puromycin screening was applied to obtain stable SPINK4-overexpressing and knockdown cell lines. 1 × 105 HT29 and HCT116 cells were inoculated into six-well plates for 18 h, and then incubated in the fresh medium containing 6 μg/mL polybrene and viruses (MOI values of SPINK4-OE and SPINK4-KD were 30 and 10) for 24 h. After that, the medium was replaced with the fresh medium, and the green fluorescence was observed, and then the cells were collected for identifying the transfection efficiency.

siRNAs targeting the target gene (si-PI3K #1, #2, #3) and a non-targeting negative control siRNA (si-NC) were synthesized by Genecreate (Wuhan, China). The sequences of these siRNAs are as follows: si-PI3K #1: 5′-GTGCAACCTACGTGAATGTAA-3′; si-PI3K #2: 5′-GGAGAACCCTTATGTGACAAT-3′; si-PI3K #3: 5′-GCAATTTCTACACGAGATCCT-3′; si-NC: 5′-GATCCTTATACCGCCAATGTA-3′. HCT116 cells were seeded into six-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well. When the cell confluence reached approximately 60%, the original culture medium was replaced with 2 mL of fresh complete medium. Subsequently, cells were transfected with siRNA using Lipo8000™ Transfection Reagent (C0533, Beyotime), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 100 pmol of siRNA was diluted into 125μL of Opti-MEM® Medium, and 4 μL of Lipo8000™ was added to the mixed solution. After gentle mixing and incubation at room temperature for 2 h, the transfection mixture was added to each well. At 48 h post-transfection, cells were collected for subsequent Western blot analysis.

Macrophage differentiation

Human monocytic leukemia cell line THP-1 (CL-0233) was purchased from Pricella (Wuhan, China) and cultured in RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% P/S and 0.05 mM β-mercaptoethanol. For M0 macrophage, THP-1 cells were incubated with 200 ng/mL phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) (HY-18739, MCE) for 48 h [19]. For M2 macrophage, M0 macrophages were incubated with 20 ng/mL IL-4 and 20 ng/mL IL-13 for 4 days [19]. The proportion of CD11b + M0 macrophages and CD206 + M2 macrophages was determined by flow cytometry and M0 and M2 macrophages were sorted for the following study.

Macrophage cell sorting

Macrophages were collected and centrifuged at 300 g for 5 min. 100 μL PBS buffer containing 2% FBS was added to the cell precipitation, and 5 μL CD11b (E-AB-F1081C, Elabscience) or CD206 (APC-18704, Proteintech) antibody was added to label macrophages, which were incubated at room temperature in dark for 30 min. After that, the cells were washed twice with PBS and sorted by flow cytometry. Prepare 15 mL centrifuge tube containing 50% FBS and 2% P/S medium for cell collection. After sorting, the cells were left to stand at 4 ℃ for 30 min. Then, the centrifuge tube was filled with serum, and centrifuged at 4℃ for 10 min at 600 g. The plasma cell precipitation was suspended in the culture medium and continued to be cultured for subsequent experiments. During the experiment, we need to set a negative control, without adding CD11b or CD206 antibody, for gating.

CCK8 assay

100 μL cell suspension (5,000 cells/mL) was inoculated into 96-well plates, and received different treatments according to experimental groups. Then, 10μL CCK8 solution was added into each well for additional 3 h. Then optical density (OD) value was detected at the wavelength of 450 nm using microplate reader. Trypan blue exclusion assay was performed in parallel with the CCK-8 assay.

The experimental dosage of 740Y-P was also selected according to CCK8 assay. 5000 HT29 and HCT116 cells were incubated with 740Y-P at different concentrations (0, 0.3125, 3.125, 6.25, 12.5, 25, 50, 75, 100, 200 μg/mL for HT29; 0, 0.0185, 0.039, 0.078, 0.15625, 0.3125, 3.125, 6.25, 12.5, 50, 75, 100, 125, 150, 200 μg/mL for HCT116) for 48 h, and then CCK8 was performed.

Transwell

Cell invasion

1 × 105 cells were inoculated into the upper chamber (8 μm) coated with Matrigel (1:9 dilution ratio), and 10% FBS-containing medium was added into the lower chamber. After 24 h, the cells in upper chamber were wiped off using cotton swab, then the inserts were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, and stained with 1% crystal violet for 15 min. The inserts were washed under distilled water until colorless and dried naturally. The invasive cells were observed under a light microscope and captured at 200X magnification, with five random fields selected per chamber.

Macrophage recruitment

M0 macrophages were seeded in upper chamber of transwell inserts (8 μm), and the conditioned medium was added to the 24-well plates, after 24 h, the migrated macrophages were stained with 1% crystal violet and visualized under optical microscope. The images were captured under the magnification of 200X, with five random fields selected per chamber.

Scratch assay

1 × 106 cells were inoculated into per hole of six-well plates, the scratch was made when the cell density reached 90%. The floating cells were washed away by PBS. The scratch was observed through light microscope and the images were taken at 0 h and 24 h under the magnification of 100X. Ethic statement

Twenty-five Balb/c nude mice (SPF grade, male, 4 weeks old) were purchased from Shandong aimaodakang Life Science Co., Ltd. Licence: SCXK (Lu) 2023 0010. After 7 days of adaptive feeding, the experiment began. During this period, the nude mice were free to eat and drink. The feeding environment: temperature 23 ± 2℃, humidity 50–60%, 12 h/12 h light and dark cycle.

The animal experimental protocol was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Yantai Yuhuangding Hospital (Approval No.: 2025–634). All animal experiments were performed in strict accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (NIH Publication No. 85–23, revised 1985) and relevant national/institutional animal welfare regulations, to minimize animal suffering and ensure humane treatment. All surgical procedures were performed under anesthesia, and appropriate postoperative care was provided to reduce pain.

Xenografts tumor nude mice and treatment

Twenty-five nude mice were randomly divided into 5 groups: control group, NC group, OE group, KD group, OE/LY294002 group, n = 5. 2 × 107 HCT116 cells were inoculated into the armpit of mouse right forelimb. In OE/LY294002 group, mice received the intraperitoneal injection of 7.5 mg/kg [22] LY294002 twice a week. The length and width of tumors were measured every 3 days. After 18 days, the mice were euthanized and sacrificed through cervical dislocation. Tumors were weighed and stored in −80℃ for western blot and RT-qPCR, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, and embedded into paraffin wax for pathological staining, and digested by collagenase to detect the phenotype of macrophages in tumor tissues.

Pathological staining

The tumor tissues were embedded in paraffin and made into 3 μm sections. After paraffin sections were dewaxed in xylene and hydrated in gradient ethanol, Ki67 immunohistochemistry was performed.

For immunochemical staining, the primary antibody Ki67 (1:100, AB2008, Beyotime) were applied. The slices were incubated with 3% H2O2 solution for 1 h. Antigen thermal repair was performed in sodium citrate. After cooling to room temperature, the slices were further incubated with primary antibodies at 4 ℃ overnight. The next day, after reheating the slices, they were incubated with horseradish peroxidase labeled secondary antibody (1:200, A0208, Beyotime) at 37 ℃ for 1 h. After DAB staining, the slices were counterstained with hematoxylin for 3 min, differentiated with hydrochloric acid for 1 min, dehydrated with gradient ethanol, transparent with xylene, and sealed with neutral resin. Images were captured at the magnification of 400X under an optical microscope. Blinded analysis was adopted in this study [23, 24]. Five random and non-overlapping fields were selected for each section, and the positive expression level of Ki-67 was quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software with unified scale calibration and consistent threshold parameters.

Flow cytometry

In vitro, macrophages were collected and centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 3 min. After fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, macrophages were treated with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 15 min, and blocked with normal goat serum for 30 min. Afterwards, the cells were incubated with primary antibodies FITC-labeled CD11b (1:200, E-AB-F1081C, Elabscience), PE-conjugated iNOS (1:200, 12–5920–82, Thermo Fisher), and APC-conjugated CD206 (1:200, E-AB-F1161E, Elabscience) at room temperature for 1 h. Then, cells were washed 3 times with PBS, and flow cytometer was used to measure the proportion of CD11b+, iNOS+, and CD206+ macrophages.

The tumor tissues were cut into 1 mm3 tumor blocks and put into the mixed solution of 1 mg/mL collagenase IV and DNaseI, digested at 37℃ for 45 min, and centrifuged to collect the single cell suspension. Next, we performed cell immobilization, permeability, blocking, and primary antibody labeling. Elab Fluor Violet 450 CD45(E-AB-F1136Q, Elabscience); PE-CD11b (E-AB-F1081D, Elabscience); FITC-F4/80 (E-AB-F0995C, Elabscience); APC-CD206 (E-AB-F1135E, Elabscience); Elab Fluor®700 CD86 (E-AB-F0994M1, Elabscience). After antibody staining, 5 μL of 7-AAD staining solution was added to each sample, then incubated with the samples in the dark for 10 min. Flow cytometer was used to detect the proportion of CD45+CD11b+F4/80+CD206+ and CD45+CD11b+F4/80+CD86+ macrophages in tumor tissues. Fluorescence compensation was calibrated for PE and FITC channels. In addition, fluorescence minus one (FMO) control was established, CD45+CD11b+F4/80+IgG control for CD86 and CD206, where matched IgG antibodies served as the corresponding isotype controls.

Western blot

The total protein was extracted from cells and tissues with RIPA buffer, and its concentration was detected by BCA protein quantitative kit (P0009, Beyotime). The protein sample (30 μg/Lane) was separated by 10% gel and transferred to PVDF membrane, and sealed in 5% skimmed milk at room temperature for 30 min, and incubated with primary antibodies (SPINK4, p-AKT, AKT, p-PI3K, PI3K, GAPDH) overnight at 4℃. Then, the membrane was incubated with goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody at 37 ℃ for 2 h. ECL reagent (P0018S, Beyotime) was used for development, GAPDH was used as internal reference, and Image J software was used for protein gray value analysis. Bioinformatics analysis

TCGA and GEO database analyses revealed that SPINK4 was significantly upregulated in colorectal cancer tissues, suggesting its oncogenic role in CRC progression (Fig. S4A–B). Tumor purity-corrected correlation analysis based on the TIMER database showed that SPINK4 expression was negatively correlated with tumor purity (COAD: cor = − 0.211, p = 1.70 × 10−5; READ: cor = − 0.267, p = 1.45 × 10−3), indicating that SPINK4-high tumors exhibited elevated immune and stromal infiltration (Fig. S4C).

For key genes of the PI3K/AKT pathway, after adjusting for tumor purity, SPINK4 was positively correlated with AKT1 (COAD: cor = 0.105, p = 3.39 × 10–2; READ: cor = 0.217, p = 1.45 × 10−3) but negatively correlated with PIK3CA at the transcriptional level (COAD: cor = −0.144, p = 1.03 × 10–2; READ: cor = − 0.144, p = 8.98 × 10–2). These bioinformatics results supported the intrinsic regulation of AKT1 by SPINK4 in CRC cells (Fig. S4C). Combined with our experimental data (Figs. 3 and 7E), SPINK4 elevated the phosphorylation of PI3K protein, indicating that PI3K/AKT pathway activation was driven by post-translational modification rather than transcriptional induction of PIK3CA.

Moreover, SPINK4 expression was closely associated with macrophage infiltration in CRC, further verifying its critical role in remodeling the tumor immune microenvironment, especially in regulating tumor-associated macrophage recruitment (Fig. S4D). Collectively, these bioinformatic findings demonstrate that SPINK4 is tightly linked to aberrant PI3K/AKT activation and macrophage-mediated immune microenvironment. Consistent with our in vitro and in vivo results, this evidence confirms that SPINK4 facilitates CRC malignant progression by activating the PI3K/AKT cascade and promoting macrophage infiltration as well as M2 polarization.

Discussion

Immune cell infiltration is one of the signs of tumor progression. Among all immune cells, TAMs serve as the most critical regulatory center in the interaction between the immune system and tumors. TAMs are macrophages that infiltrate tumor tissues. They are distinguished from peripheral monocytes and secrete a variety of cytokines influenced by the tumor microenvironment, which play a crucial role in the development, metastasis, and invasion of tumors [25–27]. TAMs can be classified into two categories: pro-inflammatory M1 type and anti-inflammatory M2 type [28]. M1 macrophages are typically activated by Toll-like receptor (TLR) ligands, such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS), or Th1 cytokines, including TNF-α, IFN-γ, and GM-CSF, et al. They exhibit high expression levels of TLR2, TLR4, CD80, and CD86, demonstrating robust antigen-presenting capabilities and anti-tumor activity [29–31]. M2 macrophages are activated by Th2 cytokines, such as IL-4, IL-10, and IL-13. They secrete anti-inflammatory cytokines, including TGF-β and IL-10, which contribute to the formation of an immunosuppressive microenvironment and promote tumor progression [32–34]. In all stages of tumor development, both M1 and M2 TAMs are present; however, M1 TAMs are predominant in the early stages, whereas M2 TAMs become more prevalent in the middle and late stages. As the tumor progresses, M1 macrophages gradually polarize to the M2 type [35]. In this study, we developed a co-culture system of macrophages and tumor cells. Our results confirmed that M2 macrophages promoted the proliferation and invasion of HT29 and HCT116 cells.

In the tumor microenvironment, tumor cells secrete cytokines like IL-33, IL-4, IL-10, and TGF-β, and regulate signaling pathways such as MAPK and NF-κB, promoting macrophage polarization to the M2 phenotype [36]. In addition, specific molecules on macrophage surface can influence macrophage polarization by interacting with their receptors. For instance, CD47 on tumor cells inhibited macrophage phagocytosis and promoted M2 polarization by interacting with SIRPα receptors on macrophages [37, 38]. Our experimental results aligned with prior studies. We collected supernatant from HT29 and HCT116 cells as conditioned medium for M0 macrophages. We found that tumor cell-secreted factors promoted M2 macrophage polarization.

In this study, we transfected colorectal cancer cells with lentiviruses to specifically overexpress or knockdown SPINK4, and examined how SPINK4 affected macrophage polarization and recruitment. Our results confirmed that SPINK4-overexpressing HT29 and HCT116 cells secreted more cytokines (IL-33, IL-4, IL-10), recruited additional macrophages, and enhanced M2 macrophage polarization.

SPINK4 is a serine protease inhibitor secreted by the gastrointestinal tract. Human SPINK4 is found at 9p13.3 on chromosome 9 and is primarily expressed in the colon, rectum, duodenum, and small intestine [39]. Besides the intestinal tract, SPINK4 mRNA expression was also found in uterine epithelial cells and endothelial cells of some blood vessels [40]. However, the mRNA and protein expression of SPINK4 was undetectable in the immune system, such as monocytes, macrophages, and the spleen [41]. Many studies have emphasized the role of SPINK4 in colorectal cancer. A study found high levels of SPINK4 in the serum of patients with colorectal cancer. The serum SPINK4 level of patients with colorectal cancer before operation was higher than that after operation, and the serum SPINK4 level of patients with rectal cancer was higher than that of patients with rectal cancer [16]. In 2023, SPINK4 promoted the proliferation, metastasis, and tumor growth of colorectal cancer LoVo and HCT116 cells, and was associated with ferroptosis [17]. Our study also determined that SPINK4 overexpression increased proliferation, migration, and invasion in colorectal cancer cell lines HT29 and HCT116.

To further explore the clinical relevance of SPINK4 in colorectal cancer, we supplemented bioinformatics analysis using data from TCGA database. The results showed that SPINK4 mRNA expression was significantly upregulated in CRC tissues compared with adjacent normal colorectal tissues (P < 0.05), which was consistent with the previous finding of elevated serum SPINK4 levels in CRC patients [16]. This study further confirmed the diagnostic value of SPINK4 in CRC, demonstrating that serum SPINK4 levels are elevated in CRC patients and correlated with tumor location and distant metastasis, though no association was found between serum SPINK4 levels and patient prognosis. These results further confirm that SPINK4 is closely related to the malignant progression of CRC and may serve as a potential clinical diagnostic marker. More importantly, we found that overexpression of SPINK4 increased IL-33, IL-4, and IL-10 cytokine levels secreted by tumor cells. These factors are critical for M2 macrophage polarization. We collected the supernatant from SPINK4-OE and SPINK4-KD cells and co-incubated it with M0 cells. SPINK4 overexpression promoted M2 macrophage polarization and inhibited M1 polarization.

Also, we found SPINK4 overexpression increased chemokine CSF1, CCL2, and VEGF-C levels secreted by HT29 and HCT116 cells, promoting macrophage recruitment. The expansion of macrophages and recruitment of monocytes in the tumor microenvironment are vital for solid tumor development [42]. Tumor cells secrete chemokines like CSF1, CCL2, CCL3, CCL5, and CX3CL1 that recruit monocytes from peripheral circulation to TME. In the TME, monocytes differentiate into macrophages, which promote tumor survival by generating TAMs [43–46]. Inhibiting monocyte and macrophage recruitment is a promising strategy to reduce TAMs involvement in tumorigenesis. In this study, we placed M0 macrophages in the Transwell upper chamber and added tumor cell supernatant to a 24-well plate. Crystal violet staining showed more migrating macrophages, likely due to increased CSF1 and CCL2 chemokines secreted by tumor cells caused by SPINK4 overexpression.

The PI3K/AKT signaling pathway is vital for various physiological processes and is particularly important in tumor development [47]. The interaction between TAMs and tumor cells can enhance the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, create a positive feedback loop, and promote tumor growth and metastasis [44, 48]. A study reported that NADPH oxidase 4 (NOX4) in non-small cell lung cancer cells recruited mouse peritoneal macrophages and induced M2 polarization. During this process, NOX4 stimulated cytokine production, including VEGF-C, IL-8, CCL7, and CSF-1, through the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway [49]. In this study, to verify the involvement of PI3K/AKT, we applied PI3K antagonist LY294002 and activator 740Y-P. Graphical abstract. High expression of SPINK4 activated the PI3K/AKT pathway, thereby promoting the proliferation, migration, and invasion of HCT116 and HT29 cells. It also stimulated the secretion of macrophage-recruiting factors such as CCL2, CSF1, and VEGF-C, as well as IL-33, IL-4, and IL-10, thereby facilitating the transformation to the M2 macrophage

Our study verified that SPINK4 activated the PI3K/AKT pathway to stimulate the secretion of macrophage-recruiting factors to facilitating the transformation to the M2 macrophage, the mechanism has an important impact on the tumor microenvironment, which can further carry out experiments to explore the impact of anti-tumor immunity on the tumor immune microenvironment of colorectal cancer, and provide more treatment options for “cold tumor” colorectal cancer. Consistent with this insightful perspective, our study not only confirmed that SPINK4 promoted the proliferation of colorectal cancer cells via the PI3K/AKT pathway, but also demonstrated that colorectal cancer cells secreted a panel of key factors including CCL2, VEGF-C, IL-10, IL-4, CSF1 and IL-33 to drive M2 macrophage polarization. Furthermore, we innovatively revealed the synergistic pro-tumor effect between SPINK4 and M2 macrophages, which jointly accelerated the malignant progression of colorectal cancer cells.

Notably, the synergistic pro-tumor effect between SPINK4 and M2 macrophages observed in our study can be further supported by the well-documented secretory phenotype of M2 macrophages in the tumor microenvironment. Extensive prior research has validated that M2 macrophages secrete a panel of critical tumor-promoting factors, including IL-8 [50], IL-10 [51], PDGF [52], and MMP9 [51, 53], which collectively drive malignant progression of colorectal cancer. Specifically, IL-8 derived from M2 macrophages acted as a key chemokine to recruit immune cells and boost cancer cell migration and invasion [50]; IL-10 mediated immunosuppression to impair anti-tumor immune responses [51]; PDGF served as a growth factor to promote tumor cell proliferation and stromal remodeling [52]; and MMP9 functioned as a major matrix metalloproteinase to degrade extracellular matrix and facilitate tumor invasion and metastasis [51, 53]. These secreted factors may act in concert with SPINK4 to amplify the malignant phenotype of colorectal cancer cells, forming a positive feedback loop that fuels tumor progression. This mechanistic inference, based on published evidence, complements our experimental findings and provides a reasonable explanation for the synergistic effect between SPINK4 and M2 macrophages in colorectal cancer.Despite these novel findings, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. (1) This work primarily focused on the PI3K/AKT pathway as the downstream mechanism of SPINK4. The potential crosstalk between SPINK4 and other oncogenic signaling axes (e.g., MAPK and NF-κB) associated with macrophage polarization remains unclarified. Relevant experiments on macrophage phenotypic transition were not performed due to the limited scope, and further investigation into macrophage functional heterogeneity will be conducted in our follow-up study. (2) All cellular experiments were completed in vitro using two CRC cell lines (HT29 and HCT116) and a simple co-culture system, which fails to fully recapitulate the complex structure and multicellular interactions of the in vivo tumor immune microenvironment. (3) Although the synergistic pro-tumor effect of SPINK4 and M2 macrophages was validated, the upstream regulatory molecules and post-transcriptional or epigenetic mechanisms governing SPINK4 expression remain elusive. Moreover, the HCT116 xenograft model adopted a small sample size (n = 5 per group), and a priori power analysis for evaluating 50% tumor growth inhibition was not performed. Limited by ethical quotas and experimental duration, the in vivo sample size was not optimized initially. Despite stable tumor growth and low intra-group variation that support our core conclusions, the statistical power was insufficient. Future studies will incorporate adequate sample sizes with rigorous power calculation to strengthen in vivo evidence. (4) Although we established stable SPINK4-overexpressing and knockdown cell lines through rigorous puromycin screening and included appropriate blank and empty vector controls, potential off-target effects could not be completely ruled out. (5) The clinical correlation analysis was only preliminarily explored based on public databases and serum samples. Large-scale clinical validation and long-term follow-up are required to confirm the prognostic value of SPINK4 and its translational potential for cold tumor immunotherapy. (6) This study only used PMA-induced THP-1-derived M0 macrophages without verification in primary human monocytes. Although THP-1 is a classic model for macrophage research, this immortalized cell line possesses inherent cellular plasticity, with less stable phenotypes compared with primary macrophages under physiological conditions.

Conclusion

In summary, our research has drawn the following conclusions: First, SPINK4 promoted the proliferation, migration, and invasion of colorectal cancer, as well as tumor growth in vivo. Second, SPINK4 activated the PI3K/AKT pathway in colorectal cancer cells. Third, the SPINK4 pathway activated the PI3K/AKT pathway, facilitating the malignant progression of colorectal cancer. Fourth, SPINK4 promoted macrophage recruitment and M2 polarization, which was also associated with the activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway. Finally, M2 macrophages and SPINK4 work synergistically to enhance the malignant phenotype of colorectal cancer, thereby accelerating the proliferation and invasive capabilities of colorectal cancer cells. Our research confirmed that SPINK4 was a potential therapeutic target for colorectal cancer, providing theoretical evidence that SPINK4 facilitated the colorectal cancer progression.
