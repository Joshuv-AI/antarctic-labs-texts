Metabolomics analysis reveals kernel quality variation among main yellowhorn (Xanthoceras sorbifolium Bunge) cultivars
Authors: Lu Lu, Ruoyao Zhao, Dan Wu, Qiang Liang, Yi Han, Zhigang Bao, Hongcheng Fang, Yongjun Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Yellowhorn (Xanthoceras sorbifolium Bunge) is a woody oil species endemic to China. Its oil is predominantly composed of unsaturated fatty acids (UFAs, 85–93%), including the functional component nervonic acid. The kernels were approved as a new food raw material in 2023, highlighting their nutritional and economic value. However, research on differences in kernel nutritional composition and metabolite components among its main cultivars remains insufficient, and no analysis of quality variation among widely cultivated high-yield cultivars from major production regions in China has been conducted. This study compared the main nutrient compositions in kernels from seven yellowhorn cultivars (lines) using gas chromatography (GC), gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC–MS), and high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), and conducted the first investigation of widely targeted metabolites in kernels from three representative types using ultra-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS). The results showed significant variation in nutrient content across the analyzed samples—LG08 exhibited the highest oil and total fatty acid content, and the highest comprehensive quality among the tested accessions, representing a promising genetic material for high-quality variety breeding. A total of 782 metabolites were identified, with 164–324 showing significant differences. The differential metabolites were significantly enriched in pathways, including ABC transporters and biosynthesis of amino acids, indicating that differences in kernel nutrient accumulation among cultivars may be related to genetic variations in transmembrane transport and primary metabolic regulation. These results provide data supporting the further breeding, processing, and industrial utilization of yellowhorn kernels.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09097-2.

Introduction

Growing attention has been devoted to identifying biologically active compounds and to characterizing specific proteins from diverse plant species, given the vast plant biodiversity worldwide [1, 2]. Special attention is being given to unique and underexplored plants to discover their potential health benefits [3]. Modern analytical methods and technologies are widely applied to enhance the accumulation of specific secondary metabolites for human use and applied research purposes [4, 5]. Yellowhorn (Xanthoceras sorbifolium Bunge), also known as “mù guā,” “yá mù guā,” and “wén guān huā,” is a deciduous shrub or small tree in the genus Xanthoceras of the Sapindaceae family [6]. It is a Chinese endemic woody oil species native to the Loess Plateau and distributed across northern regions, primarily in the Yellow River Basin [7]. The plant is resistant to cold, drought, barren conditions, salt-alkali stress, and wind-sand intrusion [8]. Its entire plant, including stems, flowers, fruit shells, and seeds, holds significant value across multiple domains: for food applications, it can be processed into edible oils, flower teas, and leaf teas; for medicinal uses, it contains various bioactive components, and its dried stems and twigs are a common raw material in Mongolian ethnomedicine; for ornamental purposes, it serves as an excellent species for urban landscaping; and for ecological applications, it has potential for the production of industrial biodiesel [9, 10]. Yellowhorn kernels are rich in oil, and the oil mainly consists of unsaturated fatty acids (UFAs, 85–93%), including oleic acid, linoleic acid, and nervonic acid [11]. These compounds exhibit cardioprotective, anti-diabetic, anti-inflammatory, and Alzheimer’s disease-preventive properties, giving them high medicinal and nutritional value [12, 13]. Yellowhorn seed oil contains 182 mg/100 g of phytosterols [14]. Phytosterols are important dietary micronutrients that play key roles in human metabolism, and plant oils are one of their main sources [15]. Additionally, the kernels have a significant protein content (18.00–32.08%) and contain all essential amino acids, making them an important plant-based protein source [16, 17]. In September 2020, the National Health Commission of the People’s Republic of China (NHC) approved yellowhorn seed oil as a common food [18]. In July 2023, NHC issued the “Announcement on Eight ‘Three New Foods’ Including Yellowhorn Kernel” and approved yellowhorn kernels and leaves as new food raw materials [19]. This move opened new opportunities for industrial use of yellowhorn.

To date, several studies have investigated the nutritional composition and metabolite components of yellowhorn kernels. For example, Tang et al. analyzed the variability in oil content and fatty acid composition of seeds and kernels from 21 yellowhorn germplasms of different provenances [20]. Venegas-Calerón et al. determined the contents of triacylglycerols, sterols, and tocopherols in two batches of yellowhorn seeds collected in Massachusetts, USA [21]. Liang et al. assessed the phenotypic traits, fatty acid, and amino acid compositions of kernels from 35 10-year-old individual plants in a natural yellowhorn population and identified superior individual plants for oil production [16]. Lu et al. used kernels from six yellowhorn provenances as experimental materials to analyze the composition and content of fatty acids, amino acids, and bioactive compounds [22]. Liang et al. evaluated the kernel oil content, fatty acids, phytosterols, vitamins, proteins, amino acids, and other nutritional compositions of six yellowhorn germplasm resources [14]. Zhang et al. investigated the dynamics of seed phenotype, oil content, and primary metabolite content during yellowhorn development from flowering to kernel maturation [23]. Guo et al. conducted metabolite determination at four developmental stages of yellowhorn kernels using a non-targeted metabolomics approach based on gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC–MS), identified 2269 metabolites and 335 differential metabolites, and screened key candidate genes for fatty acid biosynthesis by combining transcriptomic analysis [24].

In recent years, multiple domestic institutions have been conducting cultivar breeding and popularizing yellowhorn, leading to the selective breeding of a batch of high-yield cultivars [25–27]. However, research on the differences in kernel nutritional composition and metabolite components among its main cultivars remains insufficient. Therefore, this study selected 6 widely cultivated high-yield yellowhorn cultivars from China's major production regions and 1 independently bred high-yield line with stable excellent agronomic traits as experimental materials, analyzed the oil, fatty acid, phytosterol, protein, and amino acid contents in their kernels, evaluated the intrinsic quality of the kernels, and for the first time used widely targeted metabolomics technology to investigate the metabolite characteristics and metabolic pathways in kernels from three representative types. Therefore, the objectives of this study were to: quantify the variations in nutritional composition and metabolite components of kernels from main yellowhorn cultivars and line; screen genetic materials with excellent quality traits that can be used for further breeding; provide data support for the processing and industrial utilization of yellowhorn kernels.

Materials and methods

Experimental material

Seven yellowhorn accessions were used, including six high-yield cultivars ‘Luguan 4’ (released No.: Lu S-SV-XS-034–2023, designated as ‘LG04’), ‘Luguan 6’ (released No.: Lu S-SV-XS-035–2023, designated as ‘LG06’), ‘Luguan 7’ (released No.: Lu S-SV-XS-036–2023, designated as ‘LG07’), ‘Feidou’ (plant variety right No.: CNA20220455, designated as ‘FD’), ‘Zhongshi 4’ (released No.: Guo S-SV-XS-015–2020, designated as ‘ZS04’), ‘Shanyou 2’ (released No.: Lu S-SV-XS-022–2020, designated as ‘SY02’), and one high-yield line (LG08). All the aforementioned yellowhorn cultivars and the breeding line were bred by our research team. In April 2018, each accession was grafted onto one-year-old common yellowhorn rootstocks and planted in a completely randomized block design with three blocks and six trees per block at our experimental field, the Zibo Yellowhorn Provincial Forest Tree Germplasm Resource Bank, Zibo, Shandong, China (118.2612° E, 36.8605° N). The trees were grown under uniform conditions: plant-row spacing 3 m × 4 m, non-saline and non-alkaline soil (pH 8.59, exchangeable sodium percentage 4.25%, total water-soluble salt 0.81 g/kg, and electrical conductivity 10.49 mS/m), and simplified agronomic management. During the study period, the mean annual temperature was 14.4–14.7 °C, the frost-free period was 216–275 days, the annual sunshine duration was 2157.5–2274.7 h, and the mean annual precipitation was 559.3–811.1 mm. In July 2024, 50 fruits were harvested per accession from 5–6 uniform 6-year-old trees per block, with each block serving as one biological replicate. Following husk removal, seeds were dried in a controlled environment (15 °C, 15% relative humidity) until constant weight was reached. Seed coats were then removed, and kernels were sealed and stored at 4 °C until subsequent analysis.

Determination of the nutrient composition

Determination of oil content

Oil content was determined using a benchtop nuclear magnetic resonance spectrometer (MQ-One, Bruker, Germany), following the Chinese national standard GB/T 37517–2019 and a previously described method [28]. For each accession, approximately 30 g of kernel samples were dried at 110 °C to constant mass and cooled to room temperature. Prior to measurement, the kernels were placed in a dedicated test tube, with the filling stopped 2–3 mm below the graduation mark, and the exact mass of the kernels was recorded.

Analysis of fatty acid composition

Fatty acid composition analysis employed the internal standard method (GB 5009.168–2016). Firstly, 0.3 g of yellowhorn kernel powder was combined with 2 mL of the glyceryl triundecanoate internal standard solution (5 mg/mL). Subsequently, 100 mg of pyrogallic acid, a few boiling chips, 2 mL of 95% ethanol, and 4 mL of water were added, and the mixture was mixed thoroughly. The resulting solution was uniformly mixed with 10 mL of 8.3 mol/L hydrochloric acid solution, heated in a 75 °C water bath for 40 min, cooled to room temperature, and then supplemented with 10 mL of 95% ethanol. The mixture was extracted three times with 50 mL of diethyl ether-petroleum ether mixture (1:1, v/v). The combined extracts were concentrated to dryness via a rotary evaporator to obtain the lipid extract.

Lipid saponification and fatty acid methyl esterification were performed by mixing the lipid extract with 8 mL of 2% sodium hydroxide-methanol solution. The mixture was heated under reflux in an 80 °C water bath using a condenser until oil droplets disappeared. Next, 7 mL of 15% boron trifluoride-methanol solution was added through the upper end of the condenser, and reflux was continued for an additional 2 min before the mixture was rapidly cooled to room temperature. Then, 10–30 mL of n-heptane was accurately added, and the mixture was shaken for 2 min. Saturated sodium chloride solution was subsequently added, and the mixture was allowed to separate into layers under static conditions. Approximately 5 mL of the upper layer was pipetted out, mixed with 3–5 g of anhydrous sodium sulfate, shaken for 1 min, and static layering was allowed for 5 min. Finally, the upper layer solution was collected for subsequent analysis.

Analysis was performed using a Nexis 2030 gas chromatography (GC) system (Shimadzu Corp., Kyoto, Japan) with a flame-ionization detector (FID) and a capillary column (100 m × 0.25 mm, 0.2 μm, SH-2560); injection temperature, 270 °C; detector temperature, 280 °C. Temperature program: 100 °C, held for 13 min; 10 °C/min to 180 °C, held for 6 min; 1 °C/min to 200 °C, held for 20 min; 4 °C/min to 230 °C, held for 10.5 min. Carrier gas, nitrogen; split ratio, 100:1; injection volume, 1 μL. Fatty acid standard solutions and sample solutions were injected under the aforementioned conditions, and quantification was performed based on chromatographic peak areas.

Analysis of phytosterol composition

Phytosterols were determined according to the Chinese agricultural industry standard NY/T 3111–2017. Before analysis, kernel oil was obtained via Soxhlet extraction. Next, 5 g of yellowhorn kernel powder was extracted with petroleum ether solvent at 60 °C for 8–10 h using a Soxhlet extractor. After solvent evaporation, the extracted oil was dried at 105 °C for 15 min, then cooled and weighed. Subsequently, the oil sample was saponified. Accurately weighed 250 mg of oil was combined with 100 μL of cholestanol internal standard solution (1 mg/mL), followed by sequential addition of 3 mL dichloromethane, 10 mL of 0.5 mol/L potassium hydroxide-ethanol solution, and a few boiling chips. The mixture was thoroughly mixed, heated to gentle boiling for 15 min, then cooled. Subsequently, 7 mL of water and 10 mL of dichloromethane were added, followed by thorough mixing. The mixture was allowed to separate into layers statically, and the aqueous phase was discarded. The organic phase was washed three times with water, then dried under a stream of nitrogen at 45 °C. The residue was redissolved in 5 mL of n-hexane. The solution was loaded onto a solid-phase extraction column, and phytosterols were eluted with 10 mL of ethyl acetate-n-hexane solution (25:75, v/v). The eluate was gently concentrated to 1 mL under nitrogen at 45 °C, transferred to a reaction vial, and completely dried. Finally, 100 μL of silylating reagent was added, the vial was sealed, and placed in an oven at 105 °C for 15 min to form sterol trimethylsilyl ethers.

A GC-MS system (7010 B, Agilent, USA) with a capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm, 0.25 μm, DB-5MS) was employed. Chromatographic conditions: injection temperature, 320 °C; injection volume, 1 μL; carrier gas, helium; flow rate, 0.7 mL/min; split ratio, 1:40. Temperature program: 180 °C, held for 1 min; 4 °C/min to 300 °C, held for 25 min. Mass spectrometry conditions: full-scan mode; scan range, 50–650 m/z, electron impact ionization (EI); electron energy, 70 eV; source temperature, 250 °C; transfer line temperature, 300 °C. Both the phytosterol standard and sample solutions were injected under the aforementioned conditions, and quantification was performed using the internal standard method.

Determination of protein content

The Kjeldahl method was used to determine protein content in accordance with GB 5009.5–2016. Firstly, 0.2–2 g of yellowhorn kernel powder was digested with 0.4 g of copper sulfate, 6 g of potassium sulfate, and 20 mL of concentrated sulfuric acid in a digestion furnace. Once the furnace temperature reached 420 °C, digestion was continued for an additional 1 h. After removal from the digestion tube, the liquid was cooled, and 50 mL of water was added. Finally, the sample was analyzed using a Kjeltec Sampler 8420 analyzer (FOSS Analytical A/S, Hilleroed, Denmark).

Analysis of amino acid composition

The sample hydrolysis followed the method specified in GB 5009.124–2016. Firstly, 0.1 g of yellowhorn kernel powder was placed into a hydrolysis tube, mixed with 10 mL of 6 mol/L hydrochloric acid solution, and 3–4 drops of phenol were added. The hydrolysis tube was submerged in a freezing agent for 3–5 min. Before sealing the tube, it was evacuated to near 0 Pa, then filled with nitrogen, and the process was repeated three times. The mixture was hydrolyzed at 110 °C for 22 h in an oven, then cooled to room temperature. The hydrolyzate was filtered, and the filtrate was diluted to 50 mL. Subsequently, 1 mL of the hydrolysate was accurately added, dried using a concentrator at 40–50 °C, and the residue was redissolved in 1–2 mL of water; this drying step was repeated. The residue was dissolved in 1–2 mL of sodium citrate buffer (pH 2.2) and mixed thoroughly. Finally, the solution was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane filter, and the filtrate was collected for subsequent analysis.

High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analyses were conducted on an Agilent 1260 system (Agilent Technologies Inc., CA, USA) equipped with a variable-wavelength detector (VWD) and an Agilent Hypersil ODS column (250 mm × 4.0 mm, 5 μm). Column temperature, 40 °C; flow rate, 1 mL/min. The mobile phase comprised (A) 27.6 mmol/L sodium acetate-triethylamine-tetrahydrofuran solution (500:0.11:2.5, v/v/v) and (B) 80.9 mmol/L sodium acetate-methanol-acetonitrile (1:2:2, v/v/v). Gradient elution conditions: 0 min, 8% B; 17 min, 50% B; 20.1 min, 100% B; 24 min, 0% B. The VWD was set at 338 nm, with proline detected at 262 nm. Amino acid contents were quantified using the external standard method [29].

Extraction and analysis of metabolites

The samples were processed according to the method described previously [30]. Before analysis, 15 mg of yellowhorn kernel powder was mixed with 1 mL of methanol-water solution (4:1, v/v), sonicated in an ice bath for 20 min, and then stored at –20 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, the sample was centrifuged for 20 min (16,000 g, 4 °C), and the supernatant was collected and dried in a high-speed vacuum concentrator (Concentrator Plus, Eppendorf, Germany). Then, 100 μL of methanol-water solution (1:1, v/v) was added for reconstitution. Afterward, the sample was centrifuged for 15 min (20,000 g, 4 °C), and the supernatant was collected. To assess system stability and data reliability, all samples were pooled and mixed in equal amounts as quality control (QC) samples.

Following metabolite extraction, the samples were analyzed by ultra-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS) [31]. Chromatographic conditions: the samples were analyzed using a Nexera X2 LC-30AD UPLC system (Shimadzu Corp., Kyoto, Japan). The column was an Acquity UPLC HSS T3 (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm); column temperature, 40 °C; injection volume, 5 μL; flow rate, 200 μL/min; mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid aqueous solution (A) and acetonitrile (B). Gradient elution conditions: 0–2.5 min, 0% B; 2.5–9 min, 0–30% B; 9–10 min, 30–100% B; 10–15.4 min, 100% B; 15.4–15.5 min, 100–0% B; 15.5–18 min, 0% B. Samples were stored in an automated sampler at 4 °C during the analysis process. Mass spectrometry conditions: the samples were detected in both positive and negative ion modes via electrospray ionization (ESI) using a QTRAP 5500 MS system (AB SCIEX LLC., CA, USA). Ionization conditions: source temperature, 550 °C; ion spray voltage (IS), 5500 V and − 4500 V; ion source gas I (GSI), gas II (GSII), and curtain gas (CUR) were set to 40, 50, and 35 psi, respectively. Data processing: the raw data were analyzed using MultiQuant 3.0.2 (AB SCIEX LLC., CA, USA), including peak alignment, retention time correction, and peak area extraction. Metabolite structure identification was performed by accurate mass matching (mass deviation < 20 ppm) and MS/MS spectral matching (mass deviation < 0.02 Da). Metabolite annotation was performed by searching public databases, including the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB), as well as in-house custom databases. To ensure data quality, metabolites were retained only if the following criteria were met: (1) no missing values and a relative standard deviation (RSD) < 30% across all QC samples; and (2) either ≥ 50% non-missing values in each study group, or detection in all samples of at least one study group. Remaining missing values were imputed with one-half of the minimum peak area detected for that metabolite across all analyzed samples. Filtered metabolites acquired under both positive and negative ionization modes were merged to generate the final dataset for subsequent statistical analysis.

Data analysis

All experiments were performed with three independent biological replicates per accession, with data presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Significant differences among samples were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) in SPSS 29.0 (IBM Corp., NY, USA), followed by Tukey’s test. Prior to ANOVA, the normality of data distribution was verified using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was confirmed using Levene’s test. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05. Principal component analysis (PCA), orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA), and hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA) were performed using Origin 2021, stats v3.6.2, MetaboAnalyst, and the pheatmap R package, respectively.

Seventeen fatty acids were detected in the yellowhorn kernels, with total fatty acid content ranging from 64.766 ± 3.309 g/100 g in ZS04 to 76.769 ± 1.107 g/100 g in LG08 (CV of 6.06%), with a mean value of 70.844 ± 4.292 g/100 g. On average, the contents of UFAs and saturated fatty acids (SFAs) were 65.213 ± 3.953 g/100 g and 5.630 ± 0.397 g/100 g, respectively. UFAs ranged from 59.621 ± 3.014 g/100 g in ZS04 to 70.611 ± 1.003 g/100 g in LG08 (CV of 6.06%), while SFAs ranged from 5.145 ± 0.296 g/100 g in ZS04 to 6.206 ± 0.155 g/100 g in SY02 (CV of 7.06%). UFAs accounted for 91.71% to 92.59% of total fatty acid content, and this proportion is higher than that of olive (82.07–85.05%) [35], Camellia oleifera Abel (86.55–90.10%) [36], Acer truncatum Bunge (87.05–90.16%) [37], and pecan (≥ 85%) [40]; similar to that of Siberian apricot (93.39–95.91%) [38] and walnut (90.88%) [41].

Linoleic acid (C18:2) (29.687 ± 2.034 g/100 g) and oleic acid (C18:1) (21.303 ± 2.085 g/100 g) were the major fatty acid constituents, which accounted for 39.76% to 43.40% and 27.11% to 32.65% of total fatty acid content, respectively. These two fatty acids are also significant components of kernels from olive (5.86% and 74.07%, respectively) [35], Camellia oleifera Abel (8.31% and 80.76%, respectively) [36], Acer truncatum Bunge (32.28% and 25.36%, respectively) [37], Siberian apricot (29.17% and 65.08%, respectively) [38], pecan (total content of these two fatty acids ≥ 80%) [40], and walnut (57.54% and 26.32%, respectively) [41]. Linoleic acid is an essential fatty acid for humans and has been shown to inhibit various chemically induced cancers, reduce blood cholesterol levels, and lower the risk of cardiovascular disease, while oleic acid promotes vascular health and enhances metabolic function [11, 42, 43].

Notably, the average nervonic acid (C24:1) concentration was 2.315 ± 0.263 g/100 g, accounting for 2.80% to 3.97% of total fatty acid content. This proportion was higher than that of Tropaeolum majus L. (1.0–2.0%) [44] and Borago officinalis L. (1.57–1.60%) [45]; but lower than that of Acer truncatum Bunge (3.85–6.55%) [37], Acer triflorum Kom. (4.31–5.28%) [46], Macaranga indica Wight (32.17%) [47], and Malania oleifera Chun & S. K. Lee (55.7–67.0%) [48]. Nervonic acid is a key component of nerve cells and brain tissue, crucial for maintaining nervous system health, promoting nerve fiber growth and repair, and helping prevent and treat neurodegenerative diseases [49]. LG06 had a significantly higher nervonic acid concentration (2.734 ± 0.101 g/100 g) than the other analyzed samples (P < 0.05), suggesting it may be a high-yield cultivar rich in nervonic acid. The average erucic acid (C22:1) concentration was 6.250 ± 0.425 g/100 g, accounting for 8.26% to 9.55% of total fatty acid content. Animal studies suggest that erucic acid may exhibit cardiotoxicity in humans, and long-term excessive intake may be linked to myocardial lipid deposition [50]. However, cardiac adverse effects have not been confirmed in humans; furthermore, most relevant animal studies were conducted over 40 years ago and have several limitations [50]. The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) has set a tolerable daily intake (TDI) of 7 mg/kg body weight for erucic acid [51]. According to Chinese national standards GB/T 10464–2017 and GB/T 1536–2021, sunflower (Helianthus Annuus L.) seed oil should contain ≤ 0.3% erucic acid, while rapeseed oil with < 3.0% erucic acid is classified as low-erucic acid rapeseed oil. No official limit for erucic acid content has been established for yellowhorn seed oil.

Amino acids are indispensable for normal human development and physiological functions; besides serving as the basic units for protein biosynthesis, they also act as precursors for the biosynthesis of various nitrogen-containing compounds that play critical roles in detoxification metabolism and interorgan crosstalk [67]. Glutamic acid and arginine are the dominant amino acids in the analyzed samples. Studies demonstrate that they significantly regulate gene expression, cell signal transduction, antioxidant responses, and immune function, thus being classified as functional amino acids [68]. In the daily diet, glutamic acid serves as a key metabolic fuel and an umami flavor enhancer, modulating appetite for protein-rich foods [69, 70]. Arginine is a conditionally essential amino acid in humans, which ameliorates obesity, hyperglycemia, dyslipidemia, hypertension, cardiovascular dysfunction, and other metabolic syndrome-related conditions [68]. ZS04, FD, and LG06 exhibited superior protein and amino acid contents. Particularly, ZS04 had the highest levels of protein (30.250 ± 0.286 g/100 g), total amino acids (26.707 ± 1.156 g/100 g), and nonessential amino acids (18.625 ± 0.687 g/100 g), with an essential amino acid content of 8.082 ± 0.534 g/100 g, making it the most nutritionally valuable for protein content and amino acid composition.

Widely targeted metabolite analysis among three representative kernel types

Multivariate statistical analysis of the metabolites

Research on differences in metabolites among cultivars has attracted growing attention across diverse plant species [71–74]. To comprehensively characterize metabolite component differences in yellowhorn kernels, a widely targeted metabolomics analysis was applied for the first time. Based on the PCA of the seven accessions (Fig. 1), three types representing the major phenotypic clusters were selected for metabolomics analysis: LG08 (high oil and fatty acid contents), ZS04 (high levels of protein, amino acids, and phytosterols), and FD (moderate contents of all analyzed components). A total of 782 metabolites were identified, including 95 amino acids and derivatives, 80 organic acids, 72 lipids, 58 nucleotides and derivatives, 53 carbohydrates, 51 phenols, 46 glycosides, 40 alkaloids, 31 flavonoids, 18 plant hormones, 17 benzene and substituted derivatives, 16 vitamins and derivatives, 11 lignans and coumarins, 6 terpenoids, 52 other metabolites, and 136 unclassified metabolites (Fig. 2A and Table S1). Among these, 782, 781, and 781 metabolites were detected in LG08, ZS04, and FD, respectively. These kernels are not only rich in primary metabolites, such as amino acids, lipids, nucleotides, and carbohydrates, but also abundant in secondary metabolites, including phenols, flavonoids, and alkaloids, thereby conferring significant nutritional value and promising pharmaceutical potential [8, 75]. PCA revealed that three PCs accounted for 57.95% of the total variance, with tight clustering of biological replicates within each sample, confirming the dataset’s repeatability and reliability (Fig. 2B). Additionally, the three samples formed distinct clusters separate from the QC samples, indicating significant differences in their metabolite components. The coordinated changes in amino acids and derivatives, as well as in organic acids (downregulated in LG08 relative to ZS04 but upregulated relative to FD), primarily reflect differences in nitrogen assimilation and carbon-skeleton supply. The activity of key nitrogen assimilation enzymes (glutamine synthetase/glutamate synthase, GS/GOGAT) is a critical factor driving genotypic variation in amino acid pools in crops such as rice and wheat [76, 77]. Concurrently, anaplerotic reactions mediated by phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase (PEPC) regulate carbon flow into the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and organic acid levels. Variation in PEPC activity in species, including grapevine and tea plant, directly alters organic acid and amino acid metabolism [78, 79]. The downregulation of nucleotides and derivatives in LG08 and FD (compared to ZS04) may functionally link to the parallel decrease in amino acids and derivatives. De novo nucleotide synthesis depends highly on precursors such as glutamine and aspartate [80]. Consequently, a genotype with lower GS/GOGAT activity or reduced TCA cycle flux directly limits substrate availability for key steps in purine and pyrimidine synthesis. The significant downregulation of glycosides in LG08 and ZS04 relative to FD may arise from altered activity of uridine diphosphate glycosyltransferases (UGTs) and glycosidases. Glycosylation, catalyzed by UGTs, serves as a critical control point regulating the stabilization and diversion of phenylpropanoids. Studies in species such as poplar show that altered UGT activity drastically shifts metabolic profiles, either leading to glycoside accumulation or channeling precursors toward alternative branches such as lignin [81, 82]. Collectively, these metabolic differences may stem from the combinatorial regulation of key enzymes at multiple biochemical nodes, reflecting the distinct adaptation of each genotype to its specific physiological or ecological context [83–86]. It should be noted that this study focused on metabolomics analysis to explore kernel quality variation among the main yellowhorn cultivars. While the metabolomic profiling was restricted to three representative types, potentially limiting the detection of genotype-specific variations, correlation analysis between key metabolites and agronomic traits was also not performed. Addressing these limitations will be a priority for future research involving a broader range of cultivars.

Screening of differential metabolites and KEGG enrichment

To identify the major pathways of DAMs across the three samples, KEGG enrichment analysis was performed. The DAMs were enriched in 72, 73, 75, and 86 pathways in the LG08 vs ZS04, LG08 vs FD, ZS04 vs FD, and LG08 vs ZS04 vs FD, respectively (Table S3). Bubble plots were generated to visualize the pathways with P < 0.05, with a maximum of 30 pathways displayed, while the color of each bubble represents the BH adjusted P (Fig. 5). For the LG08 vs ZS04, ZS04 vs FD, and LG08 vs ZS04 vs FD, the ABC transporters pathway was identified as the most significantly enriched pathway for DAMs (Fig. 5A, C, and D). This pathway is categorized under the second class of membrane transport within the top class of environmental information processing. As a transmembrane transport carrier, the pathway participates in diverse physiological processes and is crucial for normal plant growth and development, effective interactions with the environment, and fundamental metabolic processes [87, 88]. The significant enrichment of DAMs in the ABC transporters pathway may be associated with the directed accumulation of nutrients, such as fatty acids, phytosterols, and amino acids, during kernel development. The 26 DAMs identified in this pathway were primarily classified into nucleotides and derivatives, amino acids and derivatives, organic acids, and carbohydrates, which are known substrates of ABC transporters. Notably, lipid-related DAMs may be associated with high oil and fatty acid content phenotypes, as they facilitate the transport of lipid biosynthesis precursors and phytosterols to lipid-synthesizing sites. Alterations in their accumulation patterns may directly affect the content of key nutritional components in kernels. For LG08 vs FD, the biosynthesis of amino acids pathway was found to be the most significantly enriched pathway for DAMs (Fig. 5B). This pathway falls under the second class of global and overview maps within the top class of metabolism. The enrichment of DAMs in this pathway indicates its role as a regulatory hub for kernel protein content and amino acid composition. The upregulation of this pathway may be associated with the phenotype of higher protein and amino acid content observed in FD compared to LG08. Conclusions

In this study, GC, GC-MS, HPLC, and UPLC-MS/MS were used to investigate the main nutrient compositions and widely targeted metabolites in kernels from six main yellowhorn high-yield cultivars and the high-yield line LG08. Significant variations were observed among these accessions. LG08 exhibited significantly higher levels of oil, total fatty acids, and total UFAs; LG06 had the highest nervonic acid content; and ZS04 showed the highest levels of total phytosterols, protein, total amino acids, and nonessential amino acids. PCA classified LG08, SY02, and LG07 as high-oil accessions, while ZS04 was classified as a high-protein, high-phytosterol accession. LG08 exhibited the best overall quality among the tested accessions and represents an excellent breeding line for developing high-quality, high-yield, and high-oil yellowhorn cultivars. LG06 is a high-yield cultivar with high nervonic acid content, while ZS04 is a high-yield cultivar rich in phytosterols and protein, making it a promising candidate for functional food development and pharmaceutical raw material production.

A total of 782 metabolites were identified from three representative kernel types across 15 categories, including amino acids and derivatives, organic acids, and lipids. The number of DAMs ranged from 164 to 324, with amino acids and derivatives as the most abundant category. These DAMs form a synergistic regulatory network across multiple pathways through shared intermediate metabolites. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report on widely targeted metabolomics analysis of yellowhorn kernels. Collectively, this study further clarifies variations in the nutritional composition and metabolite components of yellowhorn kernels, providing data to support the improvement, processing, and industrial utilization of yellowhorn cultivars.
