Disruption of cerebral cholesterol homeostasis by PS-NPs: astrocytic endoplasmic reticulum stress
Authors: Lei Tian, Yizhe Wei, Jianping Ma, Yiming Zhao, Yanan Mi, Leili Zhang, Bingyan Wang, Jiang Chen, Kang Li, Yue Shi, Wenqing Lai, Huanliang Liu, Bencheng Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cholesterol plays a crucial role in regulating synaptic membrane fluidity and ion channels. Due to the blood-brain barrier, cholesterol in the brain is primarily self-synthesized by astrocytes. However, limited research has been conducted on the effects of polystyrene nanoplastic (PS-NPs) on intracranial cholesterol metabolic pathways. In this study, we exposed whole-brain organoids (WBOs) to PS-NPs and identified significant changes in endoplasmic reticulum stress and cholesterol biosynthesis pathways through whole-transcriptome sequencing. To investigate potential mechanisms of altered cholesterol pathways, we constructed a Transwell neuronal-astrocyte co-culture model. Results demonstrated that PS-NPs induced significant endoplasmic reticulum stress in astrocytes, specifically manifested by elevated levels of ATF4 and CHOP, along with increased autophagy indicated by the elevated LC3-II/I ratio. PS-NPs significantly inhibited the AKT/ACLY pathway of cholesterol biosynthesis, leading to marked reductions in acetyl-CoA and cholesterol within astrocytes (P < 0.05). In addition, PS-NPs led to a significant reduction of apolipoprotein APOE, which hindered cholesterol transport and ultimately inhibited synaptin (SYN) formation. In summary, PS-NPs induce endoplasmic reticulum stress and autophagy in astrocytes, impair cholesterol de novo synthesis and apolipoprotein-mediated transport, ultimately inhibiting neuronal synaptogenesis. Furthermore, specific inhibition of ERs restored cholesterol synthesis in astrocytes and neuronal synapses. This study demonstrates that PS-NPs produce neurotoxic effects by affecting cholesterol homeostasis in the brain.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

Nanoplastics contaminate drinking water, ambient air, and food [1–3]. Nanoplastics have been detected in diverse human tissues, organs, and body fluids [4, 5]. In brain tissue samples collected in 2024, the concentration of nanoplastics was 30 times higher than that in liver and kidney samples [6]. This indicates that nanoplastics accumulate more in the brain compared to other organs. Numerous previous studies have provided evidence that nanoplastics can cross the placental barrier [7, 8] and blood-brain barrier [9], leading to neurodevelopmental disorders. Maternal rat exposure to PS-NPs results in changes in neurotransmitters and neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction pathways [7] in offspring, along with behavioral alterations. Exposure of embryonic zebrafish to PS-NPs significantly reduces the expression levels of neurodevelopment-related genes. Recently, researchers have exposed brain organoids to PS-NPs and found reduced differentiation of neural precursor cells [10] and neurons [7] in the organoids. Currently, investigations into the mechanisms by which PS-NPs affect neurodevelopment are lacking. In particular, direct human evidence for the impact of nanoplastics exposure on neurodevelopment is very limited. To address this knowledge gap, we conducted our research using a whole-brain organoid model.

The brain, characterized by an exceptionally high cholesterol demand, requires large quantities during neural development to support synaptogenesis and dendritic spine expansion [11]; the synthetic rate in early life exceeds that of mature animals. Owing to the blood–brain barrier, astrocytes resident within the central nervous system generate the majority of its cholesterol [12], which is subsequently supplied to neurons for synapse formation [13] and for maintaining the clustering and stability of neurotransmitter receptors [14, 15].Based on existing research evidence and subsequent omics data, this study focuses on investigating the disruption of cholesterol homeostasis in the brain by PS-NPs and its association with synaptic plasticity impairment.

The endoplasmic reticulum (ER) serves as the principal hub for lipid biosynthesis and maintains proteostasis and lipidostasis to meet cellular demands [16]; its core functions encompass protein folding, post-translational modification, and de novo lipid synthesis. Accumulating evidence demonstrates that nanoplastics disturb intracellular proteostasis, trigger the unfolded protein response (UPR) [17], and precipitate apoptosis and metabolic dysregulation [18]. ER stress typically suppresses mTOR-mediated anabolism, dampens AKT signalling [19], amplifies autophagic flux, and reprogrammes cellular metabolism [20]. Activation of the PERK [21] branch of the UPR has been shown to deprive astrocytes of their capacity to provide trophic support for neuronal synapses. Our novel findings establish that PS-NP-induced ER stress in astrocytes disrupts cholesterol synthesis and ApoE-dependent export, ultimately impairing synaptogenesis in neurons.

During early development, the offspring nervous system exhibits heightened vulnerability to environmental perturbations, representing a critical window for evaluating neurodevelopmental risk. This period encompasses sequential milestones—neural progenitor proliferation and differentiation, neuronal migration, synaptogenesis, and activity-dependent apoptosis. The anatomical complexity of the human brain, combined with the constraints of conventional rodent and cell-line models, has left the neurodevelopmental toxicity of micro- and nanoplastics largely unresolved. Cerebral organoids, in contrast, recapitulate human fetal cortical architecture [22], cellular diversity, transcriptomic signatures and proteomic landscapes [23–25], thereby providing an improved platform for modelling early-life neural differentiation and maturation. Here, we demonstrate that PS-NPs impair cholesterol synthesis and ApoE-mediated export in astrocytes, offering mechanistic insight into the developmental neurotoxicity of nanoplastics in the offspring.

Materials and methods

Establishment of whole-brain organoids (WBOs)

Human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs; GD00001, Guidon Pharmaceutics, China) were maintained at 37 ℃ in a humidified 5% CO₂ atmosphere (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Asheville, NC, USA). Differentiation induction of hiPSCs was performed according to the literature protocol [26]. Regular microscopic observations were conducted, and the culture medium was replaced every three days.

Characterization information and intervention methods of PS-NPs

Nanoplastic particles (50 Nm PS-NPs suspension, PS000050, density 1.05 g/mL) were obtained from Beijing Zhongke liming technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China); the particles exhibited a spherical morphology with a nominal diameter of 50 Nm. Dynamic light scattering in ultrapure water revealed a hydrodynamic diameter of 56.71 ± 25.54 Nm (peak 64.20 Nm) ([8]. Exposure concentrations (50 and 100 µg/mL) were selected on the basis of published environmental and brain-organoid studies ([7, 10, 27, 28]. On day 12 of culture, organoids were exposed to PS-NPs or maintained in control medium without PS-NPs

Establishment of transwell co-culture model of astrocytes and neurons

Human astrocytes(CL-0208,Procell, Wuhan, China) and human neurons(CP-H122,Procell, Wuhan, China) were seeded at a 1:3 ratio into the upper and lower compartments of Transwell inserts (3450, Corning), respectively. Astrocytes were maintained in DMEM (CM10013, Zhongke Maichen) and neurons in SH-SY5Y medium (CM-0208, Puno Sai); inserts were equilibrated overnight at 37 ℃ in a humidified 5% CO₂ atmosphere. Exposure concentrations (0, 25, 50, 75 and 100 µg/mL PS-NPs) were selected on the basis of prior environmental and astrocyte exposure studies [27].The medium was replaced every two days [29, 30].Exposure to PS-NPs for 7 days ensures that the effects of PS-NPs on the functions of neurons and astrocytes reach a stable phase, and this is a well-validated protocol used in previous studies to evaluate astrocyte toxicity [27].

For experimental investigation, certain groups received specific additional treatment alongside PS-NPs exposure: the ERS inhibitor Salubrinal. Salubrinal (HY-15486, MedChemExpress) was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to prepare a 10 mM stock solution, which was stored at −20 ℃ in the dark. Before use, the stock solution was diluted to the working concentration with cell culture medium. All operations were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Whole transcriptome sequencing

RNA library sequencing was carried out on the Illumina NovaSeq X Plus by Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China). Total RNA was extracted using the Trizol method. The upper aqueous phase was collected, mixed thoroughly with an equal volume of phenol: chloroform (25:24), and centrifuged at 4 ℃ at 12,000 rpm for 10 min. The upper aqueous phase was then collected, mixed with an equal volume of chloroform, and centrifuged again at 4 ℃ at 12,000 rpm for 10 min. The upper aqueous phase was mixed with an equal volume of isopropanol and left to sit at −20℃ for 1 h. The supernatant was discarded, and 1 mL of 75% ethanol was added to wash the precipitate, followed by centrifugation at 4℃ at 8000 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was again discarded. 20–50 µL of RNase-Free Water was added to dissolve the precipitate at room temperature for 10 min, mixed, and then briefly centrifuged; it was stored at −80℃. RNA quality assessment was conducted. Subsequently, the construction of the transcriptome library and library quality control were performed before proceeding to sequencing. The library was loaded onto the Illumina NovaSeq X Plus (Illumina, USA) for high-throughput sequencing.

After quality control of the transcriptome data using FastQC, differential expression analysis was performed with DESeq2 software, with the screening criteria of |log2 fold change| ≥ 1 and FDR < 0.1. GO/KEGG enrichment analyses were conducted using the clusterProfiler package: over-representation analysis was applied to verify the enriched pathways of significant DEGs, and GSEA was used to analyze the genome-wide expression trends (criteria: absolute value of NES ≥ 1, FDR < 0.5 or P value (NoM p-val) < 0.1); FDR multiple test correction was performed on the pathways enriched by differential genes, and the results were visualized and analyzed using the Omicsmart platform.

ELISA

Centrifuge the culture medium of the whole brain organoid at 10,000×g for 5 min and collect the supernatant. The protein concentration was determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beijing) (Solarbio Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China). ELISA kits obtained from Jiangsu Enzyme-Immuno Industrial Co., Ltd. (Jiangsu, China) were used to assess the concentrations of IL-6, MDA, SOD, and GSH-Px. Detection was performed using the Human IL-6 ELISA Kit (MM-0049H1), MDA ELISA Kit (MM-2037H1), SOD ELISA Kit (MM-0390H1), and Human GSH-Px ELISA Kit (MM-0457H1). Procedures were conducted according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Place the WBOs tissue into PBS (Solarbio, China) and process using a high-throughput tissue homogenizer (Tissuelyser-24, China).All detections in this section were performed using cell lysates. Homogenate was centrifuged at 10,000×g for 5 min at 4℃. The test sample was WBOs lysate. The cell lysate was collected for the detection of human Acetyl-CoA using ELISA kit CB12747-Hu (COIBO BIO) and human cholesterol (CH) using ELISA kit CB10477-Hu (COIBO BIO), following the instructions provided by the manufacturer.

Neurons and astrocytes were placed in PBS (Solarbio, China) and processed using a high-throughput tissue homogenizer (Tissuelyser-24, China). All detections in this paragraph were performed using cell lysates. The homogenate was centrifuged at 10,000×g for 5 min at 4℃. Total cholesterol(TC) content was measured using a Total Cholesterol (TC) Assay Kit (JONLNBIO, JL-T1371), acetyl-CoA content was assessed with an Acetyl-CoA Assay Kit (Solarbio, BC0985), and HMG-CoA reductase was measured using an HMG-CoAR ELISA Kit (CUSBIO, CSB-E15964h) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The levels of TNF-α and IL-1β were measured using the TNF-α(COIBO BIO, CB11762-Hu) and IL-1β(COIBO BIO, CB10347-Hu) detection kits.Determination of 5-HT in neurons by 5-HT kit(COIBO BIO, CB10030-Hu).

A cryostat (Leica CM1950, Leica Biosystems, Germany) is used to section the cerebral organoid samples at a thickness of 10–15 μm. The frozen sections are affixed to pre-cooled glass slides. Primary antibodies are incubated overnight at 4℃. Different antibodies are used as shown in Table 1, and observations are made using a scanner (3DHISTECH, PANNORAMIC SCAN II, Hungary). ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA) is employed for quantitative analysis of the fluorescent intensity of the images.

Quantify the protein concentration in the extracted samples using the BCA assay (Solarbio, P0011, China), and then separate these protein samples via SDS-PAGE gel electrophoresis (Solarbio, P1200, China). Statistical analysis

We employed independent samples t-test to analyze the statistical differences between the two groups of data; ANOVA and Tukey’s multiple comparison test were used to analyze the statistical differences among the three groups of data. Rosette formation data were analyzed using Nonparametric Tests. This analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 25 software (International Business Machines Corporation, USA) and GraphPad Prism 9.0 software (GraphPad Software, USA). The resulting data are presented as M ± SD.

Characterization for developmental trajectories of brain organoids

To evaluate the developmental impact of PS-NPs on WBOs, we performed daily bright-field microscopy (Fig. 1a). By the end of the differentiation window, control WBOs had consistently generated optically translucent ectodermal neuroepithelial buds surrounding a compact, radially unstructured core. In PS-NPs exposed organoids exhibited indistinct light/dark layering in neuroectodermal buds and defective neuroepithelial bud formation compared with controls(Fig. 1c). Upon maturation of the whole-brain organoids, various cell types were identified based on cell markers, including neural progenitor cells (SOX2), neuronal cells (TUJ1), oligodendrocytes (OLIG2), and astrocytes (S100β) (Fig. 1d). The growth curve of the WBOs revealed that after exposure to 50–100 µg/ml PS-NPs, the size of organoid was significantly reduced (Fig. 1e).

PS-NPs induce inflammatory response and oxidative stress in WBOs and astrocytes

Inflammatory profiling revealed a PS-NPs dose-dependent rise in IL-6,TNF-α, IL-1β.At an exposure concentration of 50 µg/ml, the IL-6 level was 1.1-fold that of the control group; at 100 µg/ml, the IL-6 level further increased to 1.3-fold that of the control group (P < 0.05, Fig. 3a).The detected samples for TNF-α and IL-1β were astrocyte lysates.At an exposure concentration of 50 µg/ml, the TNF-α level was 1.9-fold that of the control group; at 100 µg/ml, the TNF-α level further increased to 2.9-fold that of the control group(P < 0.05, Fig. 3b).At an exposure concentration of 50 µg/ml, the IL-6 level was 1.6-fold that of the control group; at 100 µg/ml, the IL-6 level further increased to 2.2-fold that of the control group(P < 0.05, Fig. 3c).

Oxidative stress was confirmed by core markers MDA, SOD and GSH-Px. PS-NPs dose dependently increased MDA while suppressing both SOD and GSH-Px (p < 0.05, Fig. 3d-f). Relative to controls, 50 µg/ml raised MDA 1.16-fold and lowered SOD and GSH Px by 19% and 15%, respectively; 100 µg/ml elevated MDA 1.39-fold and reduced SOD and GSH-Px by 40% and 17%.

PS-NPs trigger endoplasmic reticulum stress in astrocytes

Whole-transcriptome profiling revealed significant enrichment of ERS genes in cerebral organoids (Fig. 4a-d). Functionally, differential genes converged on protein–lipid complex and ER lumen annotations. Biologically, they were enriched for protein–lipid complex remodelling and protein-containing complex remodelling. KEGG mapping highlighted protein digestion and absorption (Fig. 4d), whereas GSEA showed up-regulation of ERS gene sets (Fig. 4e).

ERS marker analysis showed ATF4, CHOP and p-eIF2α up-regulated 1.23-fold, 1.25-fold and 1.98-fold versus controls (p < 0.05, Fig. 5a–f). Fluorescence co-localisation revealed a strong positive correlation between ATF4 and CHOP (PCC = 0.89), and S100β-positive astrocytes displayed coincident p-eIF2α signal (Fig. 5i).

To verify ER stress in astrocytes within WBOs, we co-cultured neurons with astrocytes in Transwell inserts and assessed ER stress specifically in the astrocytes. After PS-NPs exposure, CCK-8 assays showed a marked drop in mean survival rate (IC₅₀ = 50.09 µg/ml; Fig. 6b). Since ATF4 antagonises CREB-mediated transcription and thereby impairs synaptic plasticity, we quantified p-CREB and found it decreased by 21% versus controls (Fig. 5g, h).

To confirm the induction of ER stress and autophagy in astrocytes, we quantified canonical ER stress and autophagy markers after exposure to 50 µg/ml PS-NPs. Compared with controls, the ER-stress transcription factors ATF4 and CHOP rose 1.57-fold and 1.59-fold, respectively, whereas the p-eIF2α/eIF2α ratio increased 1.62-fold (Fig. 6c–e). The autophagosome-associated LC3-II/LC3-I ratio mirrored this trend, also climbing 1.62-fold (Fig. 6f).

ER stress in astrocytes disrupts brain cholesterol metabolism

Transcriptomic GO profiling of WBOs revealed a marked enrichment of lipid-associated genes (Fig. 4a–c). At the molecular-function level, the differentially expressed genes(DEGs) were over-represented in terms such as very-low-density lipoprotein particle, high-density lipoprotein particle and spherical high-density lipoprotein particle. Cellular-component analysis showed significant enrichment of DEGs linked to high-density lipoprotein particle receptor binding and cholesterol transfer activity. In the biological-process category, the same set was strongly enriched for regulation of CoA-transferase activity and reverse cholesterol transport.

KEGG analysis revealed significant enrichment of DEGs in the PPAR signalling pathway and cholesterol metabolism (Fig. 4d). GSEA further showed marked down-regulation of gene sets linked to reverse cholesterol transport (Fig. 5f). Lipid-related pathways, most prominently cholesterol metabolism, were over-represented among DEGs, highlighting their pivotal role in maintaining lipid homeostasis. As cholesterol governs membrane fluidity and synaptic plasticity required for neural development, we proceeded to a focused set of cholesterol-centred assays.

To verify the cholesterol-synthesis and transport defects predicted by whole-transcriptome analysis, we quantified acetyl-CoA in WBOs and cholesterol in the medium. acetyl-CoA content fell by 42% and extracellular cholesterol by 63% relative to controls (p < 0.05; Fig. 7b).

Inhibition of ER stress restore cholesterol homeostasis disruption induced by PS-NPs in astrocytes

PS-NPs treatment significantly increased the expression of ER stress-related proteins and remarkably inhibited cholesterol synthesis.In the co-culture system of astrocytes and neurons supplemented with the ER stress inhibitor Salubrinal, obvious alleviation of cell death was observed in the Salubrinal intervention group (Fig. 9b). Meanwhile, the expression of CHOP protein and the LC3Ⅱ/LC3Ⅰ ratio were significantly decreased in these groups (Fig. 9cd). Further detection of related proteins in the AKT pathway showed that the p-AKT/AKT ratio was significantly increased in the Salubrinal group (Fig. 9e). These results indicate that ER stress acts as an upstream regulator of p-AKT, and inhibition of ER stress mitigates PS-NPs-induced apoptosis. Notably, cholesterol levels in astrocytes and neurons were partially restored in the Salubrinal group (Fig. 9fg).Additional assays revealed no significant differences in the levels of synaptic markers (SYN1, PSD95) and 5-HT between the Salubrinal group and the control group. Collectively, these results demonstrate that PS-NPs affect the p-AKT/AKT ratio through ER stress, thereby leading to impaired cholesterol synthesis.

Discussion

Although PS-NPs neurotoxicity has been documented, the underlying mechanisms remain poorly defined. To close this gap, we exposed WBOs and transwell co-cultures to PS-NPs. WBOs sequentially passed through embryoid-body formation, differentiation, expansion, and maturation, yielding neurons, astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, and neural progenitor cells. Within these WBOs, neural stem cells arranged into radial neuro-rosettes [31–33]. PS-NPs treatment disrupted rosette architecture and impaired cellular migration, concomitant with a significant reduction in organoid diameter, indicative of suppressed proliferation and growth [34].

Next, we profiled the inflammatory response and oxidative stress occurring in WBOs. By measuring the inflammatory cytokine IL-6,TNF-α、IL-1β and oxidative stress indicators MDA, SOD, and GSH-Px, the results indicated that PS-NPs caused significant inflammatory responses and oxidative stress in WBOs. This is consistent with previous research, which demonstrated that exposure to PS-NPs leads to inflammation and oxidative stress in the nervous system of zebrafish [35].

Previous studies have established that ROS trigger ER stress [36]. Consistent with this paradigm, the present findings demonstrate that PS-NPs evoke robust ER stress in primary astrocytes, as evidenced by a pronounced up-regulation of the proximal sensor PERK. Activated PERK phosphorylates eIF2α, thereby selectively translating ATF4, which in turn antagonizes CREB-dependent transcription and suppresses memory consolidation [37, 38]. ATF4 further amplifies the stress response by driving CHOP expression [39]. CHOP facilitates the lipidation of LC3-I to LC3-II and accelerates autophagic flux [40]. The resultant accumulation of LC3-II serves as a quantitative proxy [41] for autophagosome biogenesis and is in full agreement with earlier reports that PS-NPs enhance autophagy. The study demonstrates that PS-NPs can enhance the autophagic process by inducing ER stress [42, 43](Fig. 10).

Previous studies have shown that increased expression of the CHOP protein due to ER stress significantly inhibits the phosphorylation of AKT [44–46]. The reduction of p-AKT inhibits the phosphorylation of ACLY. ACLY is a key lipogenic enzyme that converts cytosolic citrate derived from the tricarboxylic acid cycle into acetyl-CoA in the cytoplasm. p-ACLY converts citrate and acetate into acetyl-CoA [47]. Acetyl-CoA in the cytoplasm serves as the initial substrate for the cholesterol biosynthesis pathway [48], which generates cholesterol through enzymatic reactions. A decrease in p-ACLY leads to reduced synthesis of acetyl-CoA, thereby resulting in decreased cholesterol synthesis [49]. HMG-CoAR is the rate-limiting enzyme in the cholesterol biosynthesis pathway, reducing HMG-CoA to mevalonate. PS-NPs exposure reduced HMG-CoAR content and suppressed de-novo cholesterol synthesis. PS-NPs evoked ER stress in astrocytes, suppressed the AKT/ACLY pathway [50, 51] and impaired cholesterol homeostasis(Fig. 8). This findings explains the lipid-metabolic decline detected after PS-NPs exposure [27].

Whole-transcriptome profiling (Fig. 4) reveals a selective disruption of cholesterol trafficking. In the brain, astrocytes synthesize cholesterol and deliver it to neurons via ApoE3 [52], thereby fueling synaptogenesis [53]. PS-NPs exposure lowers ApoE3 abundance, diminishes neuronal cholesterol content, and compromises synaptic plasticity. Consistent with this phenotype, The levels of synaptic markers SYN1 [54] and PSD95 were significantly decreased.As a scaffolding protein in neuronal dendritic spines [55], PSD95 is essential for the maturation of neuronal dendrites and spines [56]. Previous studies have shown that reduced PSD95 expression leads to learning and memory impairments [57].Reduced BDNF levels impair synaptic transmission efficiency. Using the ER stress-specific inhibitor Salubrinal, our results demonstrated that compared with the 50 µg/mL PS-NPs exposure group, the apoptosis of astrocytes was significantly reduced, while the synaptic function and neurotransmitter levels in neurons were significantly restored (Fig. 9).

Conclusion

The brain is isolated from systemic cholesterol by the blood–brain barrier; consequently, astrocytes synthesise cholesterol in situ and deliver it to neurons. PS-NPs exposure induced WBOs inflammation and oxidative stress, triggered PERK-mediated ER stress in astrocytes, and suppressed cholesterol synthesis and transport. Consequently, neurons received less cholesterol, synapse formation declined, and neurotransmitter synthesis was impaired. To our knowledge, this is the first demonstration that PS-NPs disrupt brain cholesterol homeostasis.Meanwhile, we acknowledge the limitations of this study: Firstly, the WBOs lack the authentic blood-brain barrier and functional vascular architecture, resulting in exposure pathways and concentrations that differ from real-world conditions. Secondly, This study did not comprehensively investigate all lipid metabolic pathways in the brain and their regulatory mechanisms, but only focused on cholesterol metabolism in astrocytes. In subsequent studies, we will further examine the effects of other lipid metabolic pathways and different cell types on cholesterol metabolism.
