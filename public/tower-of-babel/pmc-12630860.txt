Detection of violence in football sport based on deep learning and optimization algorithm
Authors: Ya Dong Li, Yong Po Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Among the various sports activities that are carried out all over the world, football is undoubtedly the most popular, most participated in and most watched activity and sport. The increasing spread of sports has caused it to break down geographical, racial, ethnic, political and ideological boundaries. Social and cultural analysts have conducted research on various customs, rituals, values and social patterns prevalent in football in recent decades. Among the important issues that have attracted the attention of scholars, especially in the last two decades, is the study of violent events and incidents related to football. This paper introduces a real-time violence detection system that uses deep learning techniques, including convolutional neural networks and long short-term memory (LSTM) for extracting spatial features and understanding temporal relationships. The proposed model was then optimized by a modified version of shuffled shepherd optimizer (MSSO) to provide better efficiency by optimal selection of network hyperparameters. The model is then compared with earlier studies to show its superiority to be the most efficient in violence detection.

Introduction

Football is a team sport and the most popular game around the world, played by two teams of eleven players with a soccer ball on natural or artificial grass to score goals. The eleven players on each team include several forwards, midfielders, defenders, and a goalkeeper, who are arranged on the field by the coach and act as a system called the football system1. Today, there are more than 250 million footballers in more than 200 countries2.

Soccer, widely known as football in several countries, is the most popular and most played sport in the world3. It’s a worldwide sensation with 10s of millions of fans and participants among various demographics4. With its universality, the game is a uniting force, cutting across geographies, races, ethnicities, faiths, ideologies and politics5. Football matches, at the amateur, professional and international levels, attract large crowds and significant media coverage that illustrating the social and cultural importance of the sport6.

Sociologists, cultural analysts and sports researchers have spent much of the last several decades studying all manner of football. These have included the customs, rituals, values, and social patterns that surround the sport, and what it reflects about society. One of the major problems that have arisen over the past couple of decades is the violent acts arising during football matches. Sporting incidents may include a variety of difficulties, such as player altercations and fan disruptions, which are often the result of violence and hooliganism. Such activities not only damage the sport’s image, but also endanger the safety of players, officials, and spectators.

Employing the smart strategies is too important for spotting during aggregation of a football player. The usual methods like having people watch closely and relying on security teams aren’t always reliable or quick enough. Because of this, there’s a growing curiosity about how we can use new tech, like machine learning (ML), and artificial intelligence (AI), to build automated systems that can catch violent behavior. Sernani et al.7 presented 3 diverse models on the basis of deep learning for violence diagnosis, which were examined using AIRTLab. The outcomes of the suggested network were in agreement with existing scientific research regarding accuracy that transfer learning-based models demonstrated superior generalization abilities compared to those trained using scratch. Additionally, the evaluations revealed that the majority of categorization errors related to the recognition of non-violent clips, which confirmed the effectiveness of the suggested dataset’s design. Lastly, to represent the importance the suggested networks, the study included a comparison with pertinent studies and networks that utilized well-developed previously trained two-dimensional CNNs. This comparison illustrated that three-dimensional networks achieved superior accuracy performance in comparison with time-distributed two-dimensional CNNs when dealing with the spatio-temporal characteristics of video clips.

AlDahoul et al.8 suggested a framework of end-to-end LSTM-CNN that could implement low-cost IoT device like raspberry pi board. The study employed CNN for learning spatial characteristics from frames of videos that have been utilized in LSTM for classification of video into non-violence and violence categories. Two publicly available datasets, including RLVS-2000and RWF-2000, were utilized for both evaluation and training. The videos presented difficult contexts like chaotic scenes and crowds, distant small objects, brief actions, and low resolution. Furthermore, the footage was recorded in a range of settings including schools, prisons, and streets, featuring various human activities like playing basketball, eating, swimming, tennis, and football. The results of the experiments indicated that the proposed violence detection model performed well that could accomplish the accuracy value of 73.35%, a recall value of 76.90%, a precision value of 72.53%, an F1-score value of 74.01%, a false negative value of 23.10%, a false positive value of 30.20%, and an AUC value of 82.0%. The suggested CNN-LSTM effectively balanced solid efficiency with a low quantity of variables, making it suitable for implementation on cost-effective IoT nodes.

Accattoli et al.9 aimed solve the problems of detecting the violence existing in videos, since it was difficult for human beings to detect them and it was time-consuming and expensive. To this aim, a solution was presented that was in accordance with a three-dimensional CNN that could detect violence scenes, aggressive motions, and fights in live videos. The suggested model was compared to other advanced networks. Eventually, it was revealed by the results that the suggested model could perform superior to the other models.

Ullah et al.10 suggested end-to-end deep learning with triple stages for detecting the violence existing in videos. Initially, individuals were identified in the surveillance videos utilizing a lightweight CNN, which minimized the extensive processing of irrelevant frames. Next, 16 frames containing detected individuals was sent to a three-dimensional CNN that the spatiotemporal characteristics of each sequence was derived and input into the Softmax categorizer. Additionally, the three-dimensional CNN was refined using neural networks optimization toolkit and an open visual inference created by Intel, which transformed the trained network into an intermediate illustration and optimized it to efficiently implement on the target context for the eventual forecast of violent behavior. The suggested technique has been shown to exceed the performance of other advanced techniques on various benchmark datasets.

Fenil et al.11 developed a model for real-life violence diagnosis, which could process massive input data and identify the violence. In the Spark architecture, the frames were separated, and the characteristics of individual frames were extracted through utilizing HOG. After that, the frames were labeled on the basis characteristics as violence network, negative model, and human part model, which have been utilized for training the BiLSTM for recognizing scenes of violence. The BiLSTM had access to the data in reverse and forward direction. Consequently, the output was produced based on future and past information. The network was trained using the VID (Violent Interaction Dataset), which included 2314 videos, 1237 non-fight videos and comprising 1077 fight videos. Additionally, to enhance the model’s effectiveness in detecting violence, a dataset has been gathered consisting of 409 clips featuring violent scenes and 410 video clips with non-violent scenes, sourced from a football stadium. The model’s efficacy has been validated, demonstrating its robustness with an accuracy value of 94.5% in identifying violent actions.

In this paper, we propose an upcoming real-time violence detection system at football event. The method employs deep learning algorithms, including CNNs and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, to capture spatial characteristics and temporal dependencies from the captured video data. These features are then analyzed by the system to detect violence with high accuracy. This also proposes an improved model fine-tuning approach based on a modified Shuffled Shepherd Optimization (MSSO) model, which allows for optimal selection of network hyperparameters, hence maximizing the model to be more efficient12.

This study aims to design and implement a reliable, real-time, efficient violence detection system that allows notifying the authorities as soon as the violence in the stadium occurs, ultimately making it safer, satisfying the necessary entertainment demands. The other major objective of the study is to illustrate how the model outperformed the existing violence detection systems in terms of accuracy and computational efficiency.

In the following sections, this paper describes and thoroughly discusses the related work in violence detection, the methodology and techniques behind our proposed system, experimental results, discussion and conclusions of this work. This study endeavours to be a contribution to the overarching field of safety in sport, aiding the fight against the vitally important underlying issue of violence in football, ensuring the space can continue to be a special place for stakeholders alike.

Dataset description

RWF2000 (Real-World Fighting) is used in this study as the model is trained on it, while the suitability of the model is validated. RWF2000 is a complete video database for violence detection. It contains 2,000 video frames from real-world scenes where surveillance videos are collected and annotated with the labels of violent or non-violent activities to serve as a rich resource for the training and evaluation of violence detection models. Figure 1 shows some samples of different types of the model.

Key features include 2,000 video clips with a total of 300,000 frames, videos captured in various resolutions reflecting real-world surveillance conditions, labels for each video clip as either violent or non-violent, a typical frame rate of 30 frames per second (fps), and content that includes a variety of violent behaviors such as physical altercations, fights, and aggressive actions, as well as non-violent activities.

The challenges include the poor quality of images in surveillance action due to the nature of surveillance footage, crowded videos, and chaotic environments, which made it challenging to segregate action undertaken by a single person and partial clips where the body of the perpetrator is not visible.

It serves as a tool for creating and refining algorithms capable of differentiating violent conduct in video footage, and it allows researchers to build and deploy deep learning networks including CNNs and LSTMs, which can be trained to identify and classify violence. The dataset is available for download on Kaggle from the following website: https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/vulamnguyen/rwf2000.

This study recognizes the importance of statistical rigor in assessing the fitness of a dataset, and in this light, we defend the use of the RWF2000 dataset comprising 2,000 video clips (1,000 violent and 1,000 non-violent) in the realm of football violence detection.

While power analysis is not often conducted formally in deep learning-based video classification due to the challenges imposed by spatiotemporal data and model architectures, the sample size in RWF2000 has been previously and empirically verified in studies to be of adequate size to train models that are adequately robust on real-world tasks in violence detection.

The dataset takes into consideration an array of violent acts, from physical fights, through aggressive gestures, to rushes recorded under different illuminations, camera angles, or crowd densities, therefore better emulating real stadium conditions for football. Additionally, the remarkable accuracy achieved by our model (95.2%), combined with the well-balanced performance in precision, recall, and F1-score, is evidence of good learning and generalization from the present dataset, implying that the dataset volume is adequate for the given task.

In addition, a 5-fold cross-validation method was used during training, thus making the reliance of the model less prone to overfitting and adding more confidence in the generalizability of the model. Future work will explore ever larger, domain-specific football violence datasets, which will also help validate on a broader scale the scalability and robustness we have shown so far.

It should be note that nonetheless, this study has specifically selected the RWF2000 dataset as the benchmark because it is uniquely appropriate as a test for reality-based football violence detection, thus providing a good balance of 1000 violent and 1000 non-violent video shots made under various situational conditions-such as light, crowd density, camera angles, and resolution, which mimic the active conditions used by stadium surveillance systems13.

The RWF2000 dataset has characteristics that only relate to actual crowds with real-world altercations inside a football context, thus making it widely accepted in modern literature on video-based violence detection (like AlDahoul et al., 2021; Fenil et al., 2019). Furthermore, cross-task evaluations were conducted using the same dataset to minimize the likelihood of overfitting and test the model’s adaptability.

Moreover, it performs well in Football Violence Detection (95% accuracy, F1 = 0.87), General Violence Detection (89% accuracy, F1 = 0.85), and Abnormal Behavior Detection (88% accuracy, F1 = 0.84), as shown in Sect. 4.3. The findings indicate that the model learns transferable spatiotemporal features beyond narrow context-specific patterns.

Methodology

The major concept of the suggested approach is identifying the violence at the moment for controlling the violence before occurrence by notifying the in time to the security personnel. In order to this, a violence recognition system with higher efficiency is suggested. Also, a source of input for the suggested network has been found to be several videos captured by different sources. Spark Structured Streaming takes video streams and converts them to non-overlapping images from video streams. Built on top of the Apache Spark framework, it is a scalable and fault-tolerant stream processing engine. Spark Structured Streaming enables to process real-time data using the same APIs and programming constructs as in batch processing of data in Spark. It provides top-notch, easy-to-use APIs for handling data streams, simplifying the process for developers dealing with streaming data. When the incoming video stream block arrives, Spark Structured Streaming breaks it down into frames and sends them over to a CNN for extraction of feature.

The categories are divided into four segments based on the activities. The “Ground side-violence model” considers the struggle among the individuals within the ground. The “crowdside-violence model” mitigates the maldevelopment, deprivation, or physiological harm amid the crowd within the side of audience. The “Human part model” includes illustrations of independent human parts that are sidelined throughout the violence. The “non-violence model” contains visuals of no violence in a certain location. An optimized LSTM network based on the Modified Version of Shuffled Shepherd Optimizer (MSSO) is then used to train all the models.

After training the proposed LSTM/MSSO model, the proposed work aims to validate the frames of input one by one and recognizes actions of violence that are capable of managing both forward and backward dependencies. If the input frames match the violent models, a security has been alerted (Crowdside Violence, Groundside Violence). Also, each violent frame detected or anticipated within the videos have been stored in the violence models for future use. The following sections will explain the Spark Structured Streaming, the CNN, and the LSTM/MSSO model.

Long short-term memory (LSTM)

Time series data is prevalent, and the forecast of future events continues to be a significant focus for people. Gooijer and Hyndman review studies published by the International Institute of Forecasters (IIF) as well as notable predictions in other scholarly works. Their analysis traces the evolution from the conventional Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) linear models, including the SETAR model. The development of nonlinear prediction models has gained importance in recent years, driven by advancements in electronic storage capabilities and computational power.

The Neural Network (NN) model is a significant representation of nonlinear and nonparametric models. It operates independently of any assumptions regarding the parametric structure of the functional relationship among parameters. Numerous studies have investigated the application of NN. For instance, Chen and Caraka have applied Vector Autoregressive (VAR) methods to enhance NN for the prediction of space-time pollution data. Subsequently, the VAR-NN-PSO system has been employed to forecast PM2.5 concentrations in the atmosphere. Additionally, Suhartono and Prastyo have utilized Generalized Space-Time Autoregressive (GSTAVR) models within Feedforward Neural Networks (FFNN) to predict oil production. Recently, various forms of Recurrent Neural Networks (RNN) have been increasingly adopted for forecasting, yielding favorable outcomes. The primary reason for this is that RNNs have the ability to retain information from previous time stages and apply it to the current time stage. However, RNNs encounter difficulties in preserving long-term dependencies due to the issue of vanishing gradients.

An LSTM network represents a specific type of Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) designed to capture long-term dependencies. In a comprehensive financial market prediction project, it was observed that the LSTM outperformed other models, including random forests, basic logistic regression, and conventional deep neural networks. Furthermore, when comparing the LSTM to facebook network, the LSTM model exhibited superior performance in predicting the lowest air temperature.

ChihHung and Yu-Feng have introduced an innovative forecasting model that employs LSTM to predict daily bitcoin prices, showcasing its applicability in forecasting various cryptocurrencies. Additionally, Helmini and Jihan have implemented a modified LSTM model featuring connections to enhance sales predictions. Their findings indicate that both the standard LSTM and the enhanced version surpassed other machine leaning models, such as the random Forest Regressor (RFR) and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGB), in terms of overall performance.

Gers and Schmidhuber introduced an innovative adaptable “forget gate” designed to enhance the performance of the LSTM model, allowing the LSTM cell to determine the most suitable moments for self-resetting. Additionally, Majumdar and Karim incorporated an attention mechanism to identify portions of the class label by utilizing the context vector.

Li and saeed introduced a hybrid model known as BiLSTM, which effectively identified quality intervals based on the high-quality principle and demonstrated remarkable predictive capabilities. Treigy and venskus established two approaches, wild bootstrapping for forecasting region and LSTM for learning predictive areas, achieving favorable outcomes, particularly in the diagnosis of abnormal marine traffic. Cheng and Chen suggested a classification method for arrhymias that integrates 2-D CNN and LSTM, utilizing ECG images as input data to enhance the early detection of cardiovascular diseases. At present, LSTM and its alternatives are widely acknowledged as the leading techniques for predicting time-series data.

The suggested model integrates a gating mechanism within its network architecture, allowing it to modulate the impact of historical data in light of current data inputs. Consequently, this model features an enhanced memory capability aimed at tackling challenges associated with extended nonlinear sequence forecasting. The LSTM architecture consists of input gates, forget gates, unit states, and output gates.

LSTM is a specialized type of Recurrent Neural Network (RNN). Its unique gate architecture addresses the issues of vanishing and exploding gradient commonly witnessed in traditional RNNs. Additionally, it is proficient in capturing long-term dependencies. As a result, an LSTM network equipped with memory capabilities offers significant benefits in tackling problems associated with the classification and forecasting of time series data.

Convolutional neural network (CNN) model

The characteristics of the frames must be manually extracted to identify the violence, utilizing learning approaches for assistance14. A range of image processing is applied, in collaborate with field experts, to achieve this objective. The features extracted from each image have been utilized as an intelligent classification method through neural networks or as input to train a model.

A CNN is identified as a specific category of ANN (Artificial Neural Network) characterized by weights, neurons, and layers, akin to other neural networks. The primary distinction between ANNs and CNNs is within their different skill, which allows them to identify a range of pictures, sounds, and handwriting15. As a result, the input for these networks is structured as matrices with higher dimensions.

Convolutional Neural Networks represent a specific category of fully connected, multilayer neural networks, which include convolutional, pooling, and fully connected layers. A fundamental neural network processes an input by transmitting it through multiple hidden layers. The layers comprise a set of neurons organized within vector format, with each neuron being fully connected to every neuron within these layers’ function autonomously, lacking any shared weights or associations.

The output layer of the current network is tasked with determining the eventual classification of the data. Considering CNN, the input is represented as a multi-layer or a depth matrix structure, commonly referred to as a tensor. This input is generally in the form of a picture, and the layer of current network differs from that of common Artificial Neural Networks due to its three-dimensional arrangement of neurons. Within the networks, each neuron within a layer has been connected to a minor region of the preceding layer, rather than being linked to the neurons in that layer. In contrast, in a conventional neural network, each neuron is in interconnected with all others; however, within a CNN, each neuron has been organized within three dimensions.

The resulting output is presented as feature maps. As a result, each neuron within feature map is equipped with several similar weights and biases, enabling the recognition of pictures across different locations16,17. In this context, association are established within localized and compact regions. Typically, all neurons in the initial hidden layer are connected to a small segment of kernel or input neurons. FC layer

The final layer of a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) is known as the fully connected layer. Similar to a standard NN, it establishes extensive links with the preceding layer’s output. The present layer aims to take an input and produce a vector of output containing N elements. The number of elements corresponds to the categories that the model has been designed for classifying. When an input image is processed, the model generates a vector with multiple elements, where the values indicate the possibility of the input that belongs to a particular category. The best value in this vector signifies the predicted outcome of the trained network.

It is crucial to evaluate the accuracy of categorization or learning within learning algorithms. The association should be minimized and optimized through appropriate approaches25–27. Consequently, the fundamental concept of training a learning algorithm or network involves minimizing the loss function by following specific stages to achieve optimal results. Given the vast number of trained variables and the deep layers present in Convolutional Neural Networks, there is a tendency for the networks to overfit during training.This means that while the network demonstrates high efficiency in training data, its performance declines when test data is introduced. Several strategies are available to address this challenge.A prevalent strategy involves raising the volume of training data, while the other approach incorporates an appropriate dropout layer within the fully connected layer to address the existing challenge. In the dropout apprroach, a certain number of neurons are randomly removed from the training process before each training iteration, with the proportion predetermined within the specified application. Furthermore, merely the retained neurons take part within the phase of training.

Some numbers of solutions (hyperparameter sets) kicks off the optimization process by being randomly generated within the solution space. Each potential solution has been assessed based on the fitness function. The top candidates are then selected based on their fitness scores. The LSTM/CNN model’s optimum hyperparameters are the candidate solution with the best fitness score after optimization. Discussion of real-world implementation challenges

Though the proposed MSSO-optimized CNN-LSTM model is able to achieve an accuracy of 95.2% and computation efficiency for the detection of football-related violence, several real-world practical and ethical challenges were posed in its implementation. On the operational side, the technology should integrate with existing stadium surveillance systems built on heterogeneous camera networks and variable lighting and will experience real-time bandwidth constraints, especially because of legacy infrastructure. Performance may appear degraded when a scene is highly occluded or under low resolution demanding preprocessing improvement or further strategies involving multiple cameras.

In addition, there are chances of false positives -wrong flagging of celebratory jumps or gestures for violence, which could precipitate needless security measures, whereas a false negative could allow real incidents to remain undetected. Both kinds have high costs in dense environments. The automated surveillance opens up ethical concerns such as mass surveillance, data privacy, or misuse possibilities.

Violation of compliance with data protection regulations (e.g., GDPR) has to take place with on-premises processing to mitigate storage of those data in the cloud and generate alerts that require human verification before an action can be taken. People are required to trust the algorithmic decision-making transparency by stakeholder engagement with fan groups and auditing the performance of the system. Future work will extend to pilot testing in conjunction with stadium operators so as to gauge robustness under live conditions towards formulating governance frameworks that balance safety, privacy, and civil liberties within the context of smart sports venues.

Conclusions

The ascent of football as the most popular sport in the world has crossed geographical, racial, ethnic, political, and ideological divides. Given the large socio-cultural influence of football, the research regarding violence linked to the sport has found its way to the forefront in the last twenty years. Football culture has also been studied; scholars have examined different customs, checks, and balances on rituals, values, and patterns of social behavior in football and the way they manifest in the game. In this paper, we present a new model of a real-time violence detection system based on state-of-the-art deep learning methods. Our proposed model efficiently detects violent incidents in football games by employing Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) for spatial feature extraction and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks for extracting temporal dependencies. The hyperparameters of the model were then further tuned using a modified version of the Shuffled Shepherd Optimizer (MSSO). The experimental results show that such an MSSO optimized model achieves better accuracy than previous methods while significantly reducing computation times. This is a real contribution to neural networks because the model passed more trials than any other, due to the hyperparameters of the network were optimally selected. This study shows the power of deep learning coupled with state-of-the-art optimization algorithms for solving complex problems in real-time settings.

This new feature directly connects to lending from the benchmarking protocols (RTSP/ONVIF) expected to support CCTV-based applications. It processes feeds in real time through the Spark Structured Streaming framework, and issues automatic violence alerts (Groundside/Crowdside) to security operation centers (SOCs) integrated with VMS (Milestone, Genetec, Avigilon). The system supports the feature of having humans verify and reduce false alarms while ensuring accountability. It is designed with efficiency in mind (180-sec optimization time) such that it can run well on edge devices or local servers, not depending on the cloud for faster responses. This will be followed by actual live pilot testing in the future, which will help to further drill down the alert thresholds, enable zone-specific monitoring, and make it interoperable with multi-sensor security ecosystems (access control, PA systems), ultimately maturing into a real, comprehensive solution for safety in real-time. Although the technical development and refinement of a deep learning-based system for violence recognition in football stadiums is the main focus of this study, contextual integration and ethical implications from a real-world standpoint are equally important. The MSSO-optimized CNN-LSTM model proposed here is to provide real-time alerts to security personnel within the existing stadium security infrastructure with a fast response time that aids human judgment but does not replace it. Future implementations must also create feedback loops from automated alerts into on-site security teams for confirmation of incidents and minimization of impacts of false positives (e.g., celebratory gestures mistakenly labeled as violence) or false negatives (e.g., early signs of crowd unrest). Automated surveillance generates ethical concerns, from privacy to data handling, that need to be handled alongside safe adherence to local regulations, data anonymization, and clear operational standards. We are also aware that, although our model is trained on the RWF2000 dataset, which contains football-specific instances, football violence often displays unique patterns of coordinated fan confrontations, use of pyrotechnics, or tensions ahead of matches that are not fully covered by the so-called generic violence datasets. Accordingly, results provided ablation and adaptability analysis proving that the model was indeed effective in the detection of domain-aware violence in football, as it achieves an accuracy of 95% and a strong F1 score (0.87) with high sensitivity to domain-relevant features. However, in future work, we will work on setting up more domain-specific datasets annotated with football crowd behavior and collaborating with stadium security to develop this system for a context-aware and ethically responsible deployment.
