Bourgain’s Condition, Sticky Kakeya, and new Examples
Arian Nadjimzadah

Abstract
. We prove that in all dimensions at least 3 and for any Hörmander-type oscillatory integral operator satisfying Bourgain’s condition, the sticky case of the corresponding curved Kakeya conjecture reduces to the sticky case of the classical Kakeya conjecture. This supports a conjecture of Guo–Wang–Zhang, that an operator satisfies the same L p L^{p} bounds as in the restriction conjecture exactly when it satisfies Bourgain’s condition. Our result follows from a new geometric characterization of Bourgain’s condition based on the structure of curved δ \delta -tubes in a δ 1 / 2 \delta^{1/2} -tube. We find examples in all dimensions at least 3 which show this property does not persist in a larger tube, and in particular these are the first operators satisfying Bourgain’s condition for which there is no diffeomorphism taking the corresponding families of curves to lines. This suggests that a general to sticky reduction in the spirit of Wang–Zahl needs substantial new ideas. We expect these examples to provide a good starting point.



1.
Introduction

We recall the definition of a Hörmander-type phase function and the corresponding Hörmander-type oscillatory integral operator. Fix
n\geqslant 2
and let
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
and
M\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be simply connected open sets, with origins
0_{M}\in M
and
0_{\Sigma}\in\Sigma
. We reserve the right to restrict
M
and
\Sigma
to smaller simply connected open sets containing
0_{M}
and
0_{\Sigma}
respectively.
We say that a smooth function
\phi:M\times\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}
is a
Hörmander-type phase function
if it satisfies the following conditions:

(H1)

\mathrm{rank}(\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\nabla_{\xi}\phi(\mathbf{x},\xi))=n-1
on
M\times\Sigma
, and

(H2)

with the Gauss map
G:M\times\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
defined by

\displaystyle G(\mathbf{x},\xi)=\bigwedge_{j=1}^{n-1}\partial_{\xi_{j}}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\phi(\mathbf{x},\xi),

(1.1)

the matrix

\displaystyle(G(\mathbf{x},\xi)\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}})\nabla_{\xi}^{2}\phi(\mathbf{x},\xi)

(1.2)

is nondegenerate on
M\times\Sigma
.

We say that (H2+) holds if the matrix (
1.2
) is further positive-definite, and we call
\phi
a
positive-definite
Hörmander-type phase function in that case.
Let
a:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\to\mathbb{R}
be a smooth bump function supported on
M\times\Sigma
. We define the Hörmander-type oscillatory integral operator associated to
\phi
by

\displaystyle T_{\phi}^{\lambda}f(\mathbf{x})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}e^{i\lambda\phi(\mathbf{x}/\lambda,\xi)}f(\xi)a(\mathbf{x}/\lambda,\xi)d\xi.

(1.3)

A central open problem is to determine the range of
p
for which

\displaystyle\|T_{\phi}^{\lambda}f\|_{p}\leqslant C_{p,\varepsilon}\lambda^{\varepsilon}\|f\|_{\infty}.

(1.4)

This is often called Hörmander’s oscillatory integral problem. Writing
\mathbf{x}=(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}
and choosing

\displaystyle\phi_{n,\mathrm{rest}}(\mathbf{x},\xi)=x\cdot\xi+\tfrac{1}{2}t|\xi|^{2},\quad(0_{M},0_{\Sigma})=(0,0),

(1.5)

T_{\phi_{n,\mathrm{rest}}}^{\lambda}
becomes the Fourier extension operator for the paraboloid. In that case we obtain the Fourier restriction problem, and the conjectured range for (
1.4
) is
p>\frac{2n}{n-1}
. By choosing

\displaystyle\phi_{n,\mathrm{BR}}(\mathbf{x},\xi)=t^{-1}\sqrt{1+|x-t\xi|^{2}},\quad(0_{M},0_{\Sigma})=((0,1),0),

(1.6)

T_{\phi_{n,\mathrm{BR}}}^{\lambda}
becomes the
reduced
Bochner–Riesz operator. This language was used in
[
3
]
for Carleson–Sjölin operators on a manifold, where the Bochner–Riesz operator is the special case of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with the standard metric as the manifold. If (
1.4
) holds with
\phi=\phi_{n,\mathrm{BR}}
for all
p>\frac{2n}{n-1}
, then the Bochner–Riesz conjecture is true (see
[
3
]
for an exposition of this connection).
These examples begin to demonstrate the importance of the following more refined question.

Question 1.1
.

For which Hörmander-type phase functions
\phi
does
T_{\phi}^{\lambda}
satisfy (
1.4
) for all
p>\frac{2n}{n-1}
?

Going forward, we will often write
phase function
to mean Hörmander-type phase function.
As shown by Hörmander, the answer to Question
1.1
is all phase functions in the
n=2
case
[
12
]
. However Bourgain proved that (
1.4
) fails for a
generic
phase function in the
n=3
case
[
1
]
. This construction was later generalized to all
n\geqslant 3
by Guo–Wang–Zhang
[
6
]
: if
\phi
is a phase function such that for some
(\mathbf{x},\xi)\in M\times\Sigma
,

\displaystyle(G(\mathbf{x},\xi)\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}})^{2}\nabla_{\xi}^{2}\phi(\mathbf{x},\xi)\text{ is not a multiple of }(G(\mathbf{x},\xi)\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}})\nabla_{\xi}^{2}\phi(\mathbf{x},\xi),

(1.7)

then (
1.4
) fails for all

\displaystyle p<\frac{2n}{n-1}+\frac{2}{(n-1)(2n^{2}-n-2)}.

(1.8)

Motivated by these examples, Guo–Wang–Zhang introduced
Bourgain’s condition
.

Definition 1.2

(Bourgain’s condition)
.

Let
\phi
be a Hörmander-type phase function. Then
\phi
satisfies Bourgain’s condition if there exists a (necessarily smooth) function
\lambda:M\times\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}
such that on
M\times\Sigma
,

\displaystyle(G(\mathbf{x},\xi)\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}})^{2}\nabla_{\xi}^{2}\phi(\mathbf{x},\xi)=\lambda(\mathbf{x},\xi)(G(\mathbf{x},\xi)\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}})\nabla_{\xi}^{2}\phi(\mathbf{x},\xi).

(1.9)

The phase functions
\phi_{n,\mathrm{rest}}
and
\phi_{n,\mathrm{BR}}
notably satisfy Bourgain’s condition, and (
1.7
) holding at some point
(\mathbf{x},\xi)
is the statement that Bourgain’s condition fails. This leads to the following conjecture proposed by Guo–Wang–Zhang to hopefully answer Question
1.1
, and unify the Bochner–Riesz and Fourier restriction conjectures.

Conjecture 1.3

(Hörmander dichotomy conjecture)
.

The operators
T_{\phi}^{\lambda}
satisfy (
1.4
) for
p>\frac{2n}{n-1}
if and only if
\phi
satisfies Bourgain’s condition.

Guo–Wang–Zhang proved, via a surprising calculation, that Bourgain’s condition is invariant under diffeomorphism in
\mathbf{x}
and
\xi
separately—this is promising since the estimate (
1.4
) is also invariant under such diffeomorphisms. Finding a geometric explanation for this invariance is one of the goals of our work.
In support of Conjecture
1.3
, Guo–Wang–Zhang proved strong oscillatory integral estimates for phase functions satisfying Bourgain’s condition, using a polynomial partitioning argument. Precisely, if
\phi
satisfies Bourgain’s condition and (H2+), then (
1.4
) holds for

\displaystyle p>2+\frac{2.5921}{n}+O(n^{-2}).

(1.10)

At the time, this gave the best bounds for the restriction conjecture in high dimensions. Both this positive result when Bourgain’s condition is satisfied, and the negative result when Bourgain’s condition fails, rely on the geometry of the wavepackets of
T^{\lambda}_{\phi}
. In other words, they rely on the properties of certain Kakeya sets of curves.

The wavepacket geometry of
T^{\lambda}_{\phi}
and Kakeya sets of curves

Definition 1.4

(

Let
\phi

•

We define

\displaystyle\ell_{\xi,v}=\{\mathbf{x}\in M:\nabla_{\xi}\phi(\mathbf{x},\xi)=v\}.

-parameter family of

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\phi):=\{\ell_{\xi,v}:(\xi,v)\in\Sigma\times\mathcal{V}\}.

(1.12)

•

A

-tube

\phi

\displaystyle\mathrm{line}_{\xi,v}:=(v,0)-\mathbb{R}(\xi,1).

(1.13)

The counterexamples of Bourgain and Guo–Wang–Zhang arose from special configurations of

-tubes, parts of which can compress into a surface. On the other hand when
\phi
satisfies Bourgain’s condition, Guo–Wang–Zhang proved that
\phi
-tubes satisfy a strong form of the

[
6

[
6

More recently, Wang–Wu put forward a powerful technique to convert Kakeya-type incidence estimates to restriction type estimates, which has led to the state of the art for the restriction conjecture in all dimensions
[
14
]
. To reach the full restriction conjecture, one likely needs a more complete understanding of the incidence geometry of tubes, and a first step is to understand the Kakeya conjecture—a compact subset of

containing a line segment in every direction has Hausdorff dimension

. Wang–Zahl have recently made spectacular progress in this direction by solving the

[
15
,
16

17
]
. Our understanding of Hörmander-type oscillatory integrals in dimension

2

1

3

To avoid any artificial issues in studying a Kakeya set whose curves lie near (resp. the parameters of whose curves lie near) the boundary of

(resp.
\Sigma
or
\mathcal{V}
), we fix

and

Definition 1.5

(
\phi
-Kakeya set)
.

A compact set

The

Conjecture 1.6

(Curved Kakeya conjecture for Bourgain’s condition)
.

If
\phi

\phi
-Kakeya sets have Hausdorff dimension
n
.

Remark 1.7
.

One hopes that Conjecture

So far, the best partial progress toward Conjecture

1.2.
Geometric characterization of Bourgain’s condition

We find a new purely geometric characterization of Bourgain’s condition, which is transparently invariant with respect to diffeomorphisms in
\mathbf{x}
and
\xi
separately. It also quickly leads to a reduction from a sticky version of Conjecture
1.6
in dimension
n
to the classical sticky Kakeya conjecture in dimension
n
.

Stated informally,
\phi
satisfies Bourgain’s condition if and only if for each
(\delta^{1/2},\phi)
-tube
T_{0}
, the family of
(\delta,\phi)
-tubes contained in
T_{0}
is diffeomorphic to a family of straight
\delta
-tubes in a straight
\delta^{1/2}
-tube, while preserving the directions. Thus we found an explicit connection between standard Kakeya sets and
\phi
-Kakeya sets when
\phi
satisfies Bourgain’s condition. Given a curve
\gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we write
\gamma+O(r)
to denote the
O(r)
neighborhood of
\gamma
below.

Theorem 1.8

(Geometric characterization of Bourgain’s condition)
.

A Hörmander-type phase function
\phi
satisfies Bourgain’s condition if and only if there exist smooth maps
F_{\xi_{0},v_{0}}:M\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\Xi_{\xi_{0},v_{0}}:\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
, and
V_{\xi_{0},v_{0}}:\Sigma\times\mathcal{V}\to\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
depending smoothly on
(\xi_{0},v_{0})\in\Sigma\times\mathcal{V}
, satisfying the following:

•

The maps
F_{\xi_{0},v_{0}}
and
\Xi_{\xi_{0},v_{0}}
are local diffeomorphisms.

•

Fix
(\xi_{0},v_{0})\in\Sigma\times\mathcal{V}
and let
F=F_{\xi_{0},v_{0}}
,
\Xi=\Xi_{\xi_{0},v_{0}}
, and
V=V_{\xi_{0},v_{0}}
. Then

\displaystyle F(\ell_{\xi,v})\subset\mathrm{line}_{\Xi(\xi),V(\xi,v)}+O(|(\xi,v)-(\xi_{0},v_{0})|^{2})

(1.14)

on
\Sigma\times\mathcal{V}
.

Theorem
1.8
also has the following consequence. If
\phi

can be put into a normal form via

We can see the diffeomorphism invariance in

\displaystyle\tilde{\ell}_{\xi,v}

\displaystyle=G^{-1}(\ell_{H(\xi),\nabla H(\xi)v}).

(1.15)

Thus (

The diffeomorphisms

1.3.
Sticky
\phi
-Kakeya sets, and a reduction to classical sticky Kakeya sets under Bourgain’s condition

It was demonstrated in the series of landmark papers of Wang–Zahl
[
15
,
16
,
17
]
that the sticky Kakeya conjecture is true in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and that the Kakeya conjecture in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
can be reduced to this sticky case. It is expected that the sticky Kakeya conjecture is true in higher dimensions, and that a general to sticky reduction is plausible in higher dimensions (see the discussion in
[
8
, Section 9]
). Roughly speaking, a Kakeya set is sticky if it has a self-similar structure. This idea can be captured in different ways. In
[
15
]
, a Kakeya set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is said to be sticky if its set of (direction separated) lines has packing dimension
n-1
. However the general to sticky reduction in
[
17
]
required one to study sets of tubes which are not necessarily direction separated, but instead only satisfy the
convex Wolff axioms
. Correspondingly, a version of the sticky Kakeya conjecture in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
involving the convex Wolff axioms was needed. This required a mild adjustment to the arguments in
[
15
]
, which appeared in
[
16
]
.

It seems plausible that the sticky case is also the enemy case for Conjecture
1.6
.
We choose to formalize stickiness for
\phi
-Kakeya sets by generalizing the definition in
[
15
]
, since it is more straightforward to write in general dimension. At least in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, a suitable
polynomial Wolff axiom
version, analogous to the
convex Wolff axiom
version appearing in
[
17
]
can be adapted, along with a corresponding version of Theorem
1.19
below. We very briefly discuss this in Section
1.6
.
To measure how closely
\phi
-curves pack together, we equip
\mathcal{C}(\phi)
with the metric

\displaystyle d(\ell_{\xi,v},\ell_{\xi^{\prime},v^{\prime}})=|\xi-\xi^{\prime}|+|v-v^{\prime}|.

(1.26)

In the special case of
\phi=\phi_{n,\mathrm{rest}}
, this agrees with the metric on lines used in
[
15
]
up to bi-Lipschitz equivalence.
We are led to the following generalization of classical sticky Kakeya sets from
[
15
]
to sticky
\phi
-Kakeya sets.

Definition 1.12

(Sticky
\phi
-Kakeya set)
.

A compact set
K\subset M_{0}
is called a sticky
\phi
-Kakeya set if there is a set of
\phi
-curves
L\subset\mathcal{C}(\phi)
with packing dimension
n-1
that contains at least one curve for each direction
\xi\in\Sigma_{0}
, so that
\ell\cap M_{0}\subset K
for each
\ell\in L
.
More generally, a
\phi
-Kakeya set is
\eta
-close to being sticky if the packing dimension of

is at most

.

A classical sticky Kakeya set is then a sticky

-Kakeya set.
We are led to the sticky case of Conjecture

.

Conjecture 1.13

(Sticky curved Kakeya conjecture for Bourgain’s condition)
.

If
\phi
satisfies Bourgain’s condition, then sticky
\phi
-Kakeya sets in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
have Hausdorff dimension
n
.

By leveraging Theorem
1.8
and a simple induction on scales argument, we reduce Conjecture
1.13
in dimension

to the classical sticky Kakeya conjecture in dimension
n
.

Theorem 1.14

((Informal) Classical sticky Kakeya implies curved sticky Kakeya for Bourgain’s condition)
.

If classical sticky Kakeya sets in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
have dimension
n
, then sticky
\phi
-Kakeya sets in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
have dimension
n
when
\phi
satisfies Bourgain’s condition.
In particular, Conjecture
1.13
is true when

.

To make the implication in Theorem
1.14
formal, we need to introduce a discretization scheme analogous to
[
15
]
. We first introduce some notation. Given a
(\delta,\phi)
-tube
T=T_{\xi,v}^{\delta}
, define
T^{*}=(\xi,v)
which is the parameter of the central curve. Define
\mathrm{dir}(T)=\xi
.
A shading of
T
is a measurable subset
Y(T)\subset T
. Write
(\mathbb{T},Y)_{\delta}
for a collection of
(\delta,\phi)
-tubes and their associated shadings.
We say two
(\delta,\phi)
-tubes

and
T_{2}=T_{\xi_{2},v_{2}}
are

if
|(\xi_{1},v_{1})-(\xi_{2},v_{2})|\geqslant\delta
. We say
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
are

if
|\mathrm{dir}(T_{1})-\mathrm{dir}(T_{2})|\leqslant\delta
.
A collection of
(\rho,\phi)
-tubes
\mathbb{T}_{\rho}

covers
a collection of
(\delta,\phi)
-tubes
\mathbb{T}_{\delta}
if the balls
\{B_{\rho}(T^{*}):T\in\mathbb{T}_{\rho}\}
cover the balls
\{B_{\delta}(T^{*}):T\in\mathbb{T}_{\delta}\}
.

We may now define the discretized version of sticky
\phi
-Kakeya sets having dimension
n
.

Definition 1.15

(Assertion
\mathrm{SK}(\phi,\varepsilon,\eta,\delta_{0})
)
.

Fix a Hörmander-type phase function
\phi
. We say that
\mathrm{SK}(\phi,\varepsilon,\eta,\delta_{0})
holds if for all
\delta\in(0,\delta_{0})
and all pairs
(\mathbb{T},Y)_{\delta}
satisfying the properties

(a)

the
(\delta,\phi)
-tubes in
\mathbb{T}
are essentially distinct,

(b)

for each
\delta\leqslant\rho\leqslant 1
,
\mathbb{T}
can be covered by a set of
(\rho,\phi)
-tubes, at most
\delta^{-\eta}
of which are pairwise essentially parallel, and

(c)

\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}}|Y(T)|\geqslant\delta^{\eta}
,

one has

\displaystyle|\bigcup_{T\in\mathbb{T}}Y(T)|\geqslant\delta^{\varepsilon}.

(1.27)

Using the language of Definition
1.15
, the discretized version of the classical sticky Kakeya conjecture in dimension
n
can be stated as follows.

Conjecture 1.16

(Discretized classical sticky Kakeya conjecture in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
)
.

For every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\eta=\eta_{n,\mathrm{rest}}(\varepsilon)>0
and
\delta_{0}=\delta_{0,n,\mathrm{rest}}(\varepsilon)>0
such that
\mathrm{SK}(\phi_{n,\mathrm{rest}},\varepsilon,\eta,\delta_{0})
holds.

The main result of
[
15
]
is then the following discretized statement.

Theorem 1.17

(Discretized classical sticky Kakeya in
\mathbb{R}^{3}

[
15
]
)
.

Conjecture
1.16
is true in the case
n=3
.

Analogously to
[
15
]
, the discretized statement
\mathrm{SK}(\phi,\varepsilon,\eta,\delta_{0})
implies the following Hausdorff dimension statement. Below

denotes Hausdorff dimension.

Proposition 1.18

(Discretized to dimension statement)
.

Suppose that for every
\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
there exists
\eta^{\prime},\delta_{0}>0
such that
\mathrm{SK}(\phi,\varepsilon^{\prime},\eta^{\prime},\delta_{0})

Then for all
\varepsilon>0
, there is
\eta>0
so that the following holds. Let

be a
\phi
-Kakeya set that is

We now state the formal version of Theorem

Theorem 1.19

((Formal) Classical sticky Kakeya implies curved sticky Kakeya for Bourgain’s condition)

Let

Sharpness examples for Theorem

In each dimension

we give a phase function

satisfying Bourgain’s condition, for which there does not exist a diffeomorphism taking the family of

Example 1.20

(The

-example in

Proposition 1.21

(
\tan
-example is curved)
.

For any fixed
\varepsilon_{0}>0
, there does not exist a diffeomorphism

onto its image and smooth

for

near

.

\phi_{n,\tan}

1.6.
Discussion and future work

Having proved the sticky case of Conjecture
1.6
in dimension 3, a natural next step is to attempt a general to sticky reduction in dimension 3 in the spirit of Wang–Zahl. As mentioned above, in light of the
\tan
-example, this will require new ideas. The most promising route seems to be to replace the Katz-Tao and Frostman non-concentration conditions in convex sets
[
17
]
, with the analogous non-concentration conditions in semi-algebraic sets with bounded complexity. Following the outline in
[
9
]
, a collection of
(\delta,\phi)
-tubes is then sticky if it satisfies the Katz–Tao
polynomial
Wolff axioms along a sequence of nearby scales. The version of Conjecture
1.13
in dimension
3
with this notion of stickiness continues to be true by running an induction on scales argument similar to the proof of Theorem
1.19
. Though one must adjust the inductive hypothesis accordingly and replace the application of Theorem
1.17
from
[
15
]
with
[
17
, Theorem 10.2]
. The general to sticky reduction for Conjecture
1.6
will then require a better understanding of the grains that can arise from
(\delta,\phi)
-tubes. We are currently trying to understand these new phenomena for the
\tan
-example.

By following the proof of Theorem
1.19
, even when
\phi
is a phase function failing Bourgain’s condition, the study of sticky
\phi
-Kakeya
sets can be reduced to the study of sticky
(\delta,\phi)
-tubes in a
(\delta^{1/2},\phi)
-tube. By Taylor expanding, we need to understand sticky Kakeya sets of certain
semi-linearized
families of curves of the form

\displaystyle\ell_{\xi,v}=\{(v+A(t)\xi,t):|t|\leqslant 1\},

(1.32)

where
A(t)=tI_{n-1}+O(t^{2})
. Theorem
1.8
shows that the structure of the semi-linearized families can completely describe Bourgain’s condition. It would be interesting to explore whether the semi-linearized families for other phase functions might suggest a more refined classification for Hörmander’s oscillatory integral problem. Some work in this direction based instead on the polynomial method and
contact order
appears in
[
3
]
.

As part of an in-progress collaboration, we build off Proposition
1.10
to find more global information about Bourgain’s condition, and study the genericity of examples like the
\tan
-example.

Proof of Proposition
1.10

Recall the Gauss map
G(\mathbf{x},\xi)=\bigwedge_{j=1}^{n-1}\partial_{\xi_{j}}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\phi(\mathbf{x},\xi)
. We will make use of the fact that

\displaystyle(G(\mathbf{x},\xi)\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}})\nabla_{\xi}\phi(\mathbf{x},\xi)=0

(2.1)

below.

2.1.
Forward implication

Proof.

Assume that
\phi
satifies Bourgain’s condition and fix
\xi_{0}\in\Sigma
.
Since
G(\cdot,\xi_{0})
is a nonzero vector field on
M
, there are local coordinates, depending smoothly on the choice of
\xi_{0}
, for which
G(\mathbf{x},\xi_{0})=e_{n}
.
In the new coordinates (
2.1
) becomes
\partial_{x_{n}}\nabla_{\xi}\phi(\mathbf{x},\xi_{0})=0
.
Thus
\nabla_{\xi}\phi(\mathbf{x},\xi_{0})
depends only on
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1})
, and by (H1) is therefore diffeomorphic to
x
(depending smoothly on the choice of
\xi_{0}
).

In the new coordinates (
1.9
) becomes

\displaystyle\partial_{x_{n}}^{2}\nabla_{\xi}^{2}\phi(\mathbf{x},\xi_{0})=\lambda(\mathbf{x},\xi_{0})\partial_{x_{n}}\nabla_{\xi}^{2}\phi(\mathbf{x},\xi_{0}).

(2.2)

We may solve this differential equation in
x_{n}
to find smooth symmetric-matrix-valued functions
\tilde{A},\tilde{B}:\mathcal{V}\times\Sigma\to\mathrm{Sym}_{n-1}(\mathbb{R})
, and a smooth function
\tilde{c}:M\times\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle\nabla_{\xi}^{2}\phi(\mathbf{x},\xi_{0})=\tilde{A}(x,\xi_{0})+\tilde{c}(\mathbf{x},\xi_{0})B(x,\xi_{0}).

(2.3)

By using that
\nabla_{\xi}\phi(\mathbf{x},\xi)
is diffeomorphic to
x
and then undoing the change of variables in
\mathbf{x}
,
we obtain
A,B,c
satisfying

\displaystyle\nabla_{\xi}^{2}\phi(\mathbf{x},\xi_{0})=A(\nabla_{\xi}\phi(\mathbf{x},\xi_{0}),\xi_{0})+c(\mathbf{x},\xi_{0})B(\nabla_{\xi}\phi(\mathbf{x},\xi_{0}),\xi_{0}).

(2.4)

Since the change of variables in
\mathbf{x}
depends smoothly on the choice of
\xi_{0}
, the functions
A(v,\xi_{0}),B(v,\xi_{0}),c(\mathbf{x},\xi_{0})
are also smooth in
\xi_{0}
.

By applying the vector field
G(\mathbf{x},\xi_{0})\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}
to (
2.4
), we see from (H2) that
(G(\mathbf{x},\xi_{0})\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}})c(\mathbf{x},\xi_{0})\neq 0
and
B(\nabla_{\xi}\phi(\mathbf{x},\xi_{0}),\xi_{0})
is nondegenerate. When (H2+) is satisfied,
B(\nabla_{\xi}\phi(\mathbf{x},\xi_{0}),\xi_{0})
is furthermore positive-definite.
∎

2.2.
Reverse implication

Proof.

Suppose that we have data
A,B,c
such that (
1.19
) holds.
Applying the operator
(G(\mathbf{x},\xi)\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}})
to both sides of (
1.19
) and using (
2.1
),

\displaystyle(G(\mathbf{x},\xi)\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}})\nabla_{\xi}^{2}\phi(\mathbf{x},\xi)

\displaystyle=(G(\mathbf{x},\xi)\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}})c(\mathbf{x},\xi)\cdot B(\nabla_{\xi}\phi(\mathbf{x},\xi),\xi).

(2.5)

If
(G(\mathbf{x},\xi)\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}})c(\mathbf{x},\xi)\neq 0
and
B
is nondegenerate (resp. positive-definite), then (H2) (resp. (H2+)) holds.
Applying the operator
G\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}
once again,

\displaystyle(G\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}})^{2}\nabla_{\xi}^{2}\phi=(G\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}})^{2}c\cdot B(\nabla_{\xi}\phi,\xi).

(2.6)

By combining (
2.5
) and (
2.6
), we find

\displaystyle(G\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}})^{2}\nabla_{\xi}^{2}\phi=\frac{(G\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}})^{2}c}{(G\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}})c}\cdot(G\cdot\nabla_{x})\nabla_{\xi}^{2}\phi.

(2.7)

Therefore Bourgain’s condition in the sense of Definition
1.2
holds with
\lambda=\frac{(G\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}})^{2}c}{(G\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}})c}
.
∎

easier to compute, we will first simplify the curves
\tilde{\ell}_{\xi,v}
via a Taylor expansion and a diffeomorphism. Then we will write down the parameterization, and finally show that the tangent directions through
\mathbf{p}
are not contained in a
2
-plane. The details are as follows.

Proof.

Step 1: Simplifying the family of curves.

Each
\tilde{\ell}_{\xi,v}
(restricted to
B_{\varepsilon_{0}}(0_{M})
) takes the form

\displaystyle\tilde{\ell}_{\xi,v}

\displaystyle=\{(X(\xi,v,t)+O(|(\xi,v)|^{4}),t):|t-1|<\varepsilon_{0}\}

(6.21)

\displaystyle=\{(v^{\prime}-t^{2}\xi^{\prime}+O(|(\xi,v)|^{4}),\tan^{-1}(\frac{v_{n-1}}{t})-t\xi_{n-1}+O(|(\xi,v)|^{4}),t):|t-1|<\varepsilon_{0}\}.

(6.22)

By Taylor expanding in
v_{n-1}
to order
3
near 0,

\displaystyle\tilde{\ell}_{\xi,v}

\displaystyle=\{(v^{\prime}-t^{2}\xi^{\prime}+O(|(\xi,v)|^{4}),\frac{v_{n-1}}{t}-\frac{v_{n-1}^{3}}{3t^{3}}-t\xi_{n-1}+O(|(\xi,v)|^{4}),t):|t-1|<\varepsilon_{0}\}.

(6.23)

By composing
F
with the diffeomorphism
G(\mathbf{x})=(x^{\prime},t^{3}x_{n-1},t^{2})
, we may replace
\tilde{\ell}_{\xi,v}
with
\tilde{\ell}_{\xi,v}=\{\tilde{\ell}_{\xi,v}(t):|t-1|\lesssim\varepsilon_{0}\}
, where

\displaystyle\tilde{\ell}_{\xi,v}(t)=(v^{\prime}-t\xi^{\prime}+O(|(\xi,v)|^{4}),tv_{n-1}-\frac{v_{n-1}^{3}}{3}-t^{2}\xi_{n-1}+O(|(\xi,v)|^{4}),t).

(6.24)

Step 2: The parameters of a curve
\tilde{\ell}_{\xi,v}
through a point
p
and
\tilde{\ell}_{0,0}
.

Consider the point
\mathbf{p}=(p,t_{0})=(p^{\prime},p_{n-1},t_{0})\in B_{\varepsilon_{0}}(0_{M})
, where
p\neq 0
and
t_{0}>1
. We need to solve for the parameters
\xi_{\mathbf{p}}(s),v_{\mathbf{p}}(s)
such that

\displaystyle\tilde{\ell}_{\xi_{\mathbf{p}}(s),v_{\mathbf{p}}(s)}(s)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\ell}_{0,0}(s),

(6.25)

\displaystyle\tilde{\ell}_{\xi_{\mathbf{p}}(s),v_{\mathbf{p}}(s)}(t_{0})

\displaystyle=p.

(6.26)

We consider
s
near 1 and solve for
\xi_{\mathbf{p}}(s)
and
v_{\mathbf{p}}(s)
to third order in
p
near 0 (with
t_{0}>1
fixed). We have
\xi_{0}(s)=v_{0}(s)=0
, so
\xi_{\mathbf{p}}(s),v_{\mathbf{p}}(s)=O(|p|)
. Below we will suppress the “
\mathbf{p}
” in
\xi_{\mathbf{p}}(s)
and
v_{\mathbf{p}}(s)
for ease of notation.
In coordinates, (
6.25
) and (
6.26
) give the following system of equations

\displaystyle v^{\prime}(s)-s\xi^{\prime}(s)+O(|p|^{4})

\displaystyle=0,

(6.27)

\displaystyle v^{\prime}(s)-t_{0}\xi^{\prime}(s)+O(|p|^{4})

\displaystyle=p^{\prime},

(6.28)

\displaystyle sv_{n-1}(s)-\frac{v_{n-1}(s)^{3}}{3}-s^{2}\xi_{n-1}(s)+O(|p|^{4})

\displaystyle=0,

(6.29)

\displaystyle t_{0}v_{n-1}(s)-\frac{v_{n-1}(s)^{3}}{3}-t_{0}^{2}\xi_{n-1}(s)+O(|p|^{4})

\displaystyle=p_{n-1}.

(6.30)

Solving for
\xi^{\prime}(s)
using (
6.27
) and (
6.28
), we get

\displaystyle\xi^{\prime}(s)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{t_{0}-s}p^{\prime}+O(|p|^{4}),

(6.31)

\displaystyle v^{\prime}(s)

\displaystyle=-\frac{s}{t_{0}-s}p^{\prime}+O(|p|^{4}).

(6.32)

Using (
6.29
) to solve for
\xi_{n-1}(s)
in terms of
v_{n-1}(s)
,

\displaystyle\xi_{n-1}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{v_{n-1}(s)}{s}-\frac{v_{n-1}(s)^{3}}{3s^{2}}+O(|p|^{4}).

(6.33)

Substituting (
6.33
) into (
6.30
) and simplifying the result, we obtain the following cubic equation in
v_{n-1}(s)
:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3}(\frac{t_{0}^{2}}{s^{2}}-1)v_{n-1}(s)^{3}-t_{0}(\frac{t_{0}}{s}-1)v_{n-1}(s)-p_{n-1}+O(|p|^{4})=0.

(6.34)

Solving (
6.34
) iteratively in powers of
p_{n-1}
, we obtain

\displaystyle v_{n-1}(s)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{t_{0}(\frac{t_{0}}{s}-1)}p_{n-1}-\frac{1}{3}\frac{(\frac{t_{0}^{2}}{s^{2}}-1)}{t_{0}^{4}(\frac{t_{0}}{s}-1)^{4}}p_{n-1}^{3}+O(|p|^{4})

(6.35)

\displaystyle=-\frac{s}{t_{0}(t_{0}-s)}p_{n-1}-\frac{s^{2}(t_{0}+s)}{3t_{0}^{4}(t_{0}-s)^{3}}p_{n-1}^{3}+O(|p|^{4}).

(6.36)

Now we substitute (
6.36
) back into (
6.33
) to obtain

\displaystyle\xi_{n-1}(s)=-\frac{1}{t_{0}(t_{0}-s)}p_{n-1}-\frac{s^{2}}{3t_{0}^{4}(t_{0}-s)^{3}}p_{n-1}^{3}+O(|p|^{4}).

(6.37)

Step 3: The curves through
p
and
\tilde{\ell}_{0,0}
are not in a surface.

Define
\gamma(s)=\frac{d}{dt}\tilde{\ell}_{\xi(s),v(s)}(t_{0})
, the tangent vector at point
\mathbf{p}
of the unique curve passing through
\tilde{\ell}_{0,0}(s)
and
\mathbf{p}
.
We compute

\displaystyle\gamma(s)=

\displaystyle(-\xi^{\prime}(s)+O(|p|^{4}),v_{n-1}(s)-2t_{0}\xi_{n-1}(s)+O(|p|^{4}),1)

(6.38)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{1}{t_{0}-s}p^{\prime}+O(|p|^{4}),

(6.39)

\displaystyle\frac{2t_{0}-s}{t_{0}(t_{0}-s)}p_{n-1}+\frac{s^{2}}{3t_{0}^{4}(t_{0}-s)^{2}}p_{n-1}^{3}+O(|p|^{4}),1\Big).

(6.40)

We may now compute
\gamma(1),\dot{\gamma}(1),\ddot{\gamma}(1)
:

\displaystyle\gamma(1)=

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{1}{t_{0}-1}p^{\prime}+O(|p|^{4}),

(6.41)

\displaystyle\frac{2t_{0}-1}{t_{0}(t_{0}-1)}p_{n-1}+\frac{1}{3t_{0}^{4}(t_{0}-1)^{2}}p_{n-1}^{3}+O(|p|^{4}),1\Big),

(6.42)

\displaystyle\dot{\gamma}(1)=

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{1}{(t_{0}-1)^{2}}p^{\prime}+O(|p|^{4}),

(6.43)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(t_{0}-1)^{2}}p_{n-1}+\frac{2}{3t_{0}^{3}(t_{0}-1)^{3}}p_{n-1}^{3}+O(|p|^{4}),0\Big),

(6.44)

\displaystyle\ddot{\gamma}(1)=

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{2}{(t_{0}-1)^{3}}p^{\prime}+O(|p|^{4}),

(6.45)

\displaystyle\frac{2}{(t_{0}-1)^{3}}p_{n-1}+\frac{2(2+t_{0})}{3t_{0}^{3}(t_{0}-1)^{4}}p_{n-1}^{3}+O(|p|^{4}),0\Big).

(6.46)

Choose
p^{\prime}=(0,\ldots,0,p_{n-2})
so that
\gamma(1),\dot{\gamma}(1),\ddot{\gamma}(1)\in\{0\}^{n-3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and we may view these as vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. We may finally compute

\displaystyle|\gamma(1)

\displaystyle\wedge\dot{\gamma}(1)\wedge\ddot{\gamma}(1)|=|\det(\gamma(1),\dot{\gamma}(1),\ddot{\gamma}(1))|

(6.47)

\displaystyle=\left|\det\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{(t_{0}-1)^{2}}p^{\prime}+O(|p|^{4})&\frac{1}{(t_{0}-1)^{2}}p_{n-1}+\frac{2}{3t_{0}^{3}(t_{0}-1)^{3}}p_{n-1}^{3}+O(|p|^{4})\\
\frac{2}{(t_{0}-1)^{3}}p^{\prime}+O(|p|^{4})&\frac{2}{(t_{0}-1)^{3}}p_{n-1}+\frac{2(2+t_{0})}{3t_{0}^{3}(t_{0}-1)^{4}}p_{n-1}^{3}+O(|p|^{4})\end{pmatrix}\right|

(6.48)

\displaystyle=\frac{2|p_{n-1}|^{3}|p_{n-2}|}{3t_{0}^{2}(t_{0}-1)^{6}}+O(|p|^{5}).

(6.49)

By choosing
p_{n-1}
and
p_{n-2}
small enough,

\displaystyle|\gamma(1)\wedge\dot{\gamma}(1)\wedge\ddot{\gamma}(1)|>0.

(6.50)

Step 4: Reaching a contradiction with (
6.20
)

By (
6.20
),
F(\tilde{\ell}_{\xi,v})
is a family of lines. Thus for
s
near
0
,
F(\tilde{\ell}_{\xi(s),v(s)})
is a family of lines passing through
F(\tilde{\ell}_{0,0})
and
F(\mathbf{p})
, so it is contained in a fixed plane. In particular the tangent vectors of
F(\tilde{\ell}_{\xi(s),v(s)})
at
F(\mathbf{p})
lie in a fixed plane, so
\eta(s):=\frac{d}{dt}F(\tilde{\ell}_{\xi(s),v(s)}(t_{0}))
satisfies
|\eta(1)\wedge\dot{\eta}(1)\wedge\ddot{\eta}(1)|=0
. But by the chain rule

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=|\eta(1)\wedge\dot{\eta}(1)\wedge\ddot{\eta}(1)|=|DF(\mathbf{p})\gamma(1)\wedge DF(\mathbf{p})\dot{\gamma}(1)\wedge DF(\mathbf{p})\ddot{\gamma}(1)|,

(6.51)

which contradicts (
6.50
).
∎
