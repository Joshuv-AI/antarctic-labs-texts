Topological Recursion, explicit ABCD tensors and implementation
B. Eynard

Abstract




Abstract

We provide explicit expressions of ABCD tensors for the most classical classes of spectral curves.
And we discuss algorithmic implementation of Topological Recursion.

1
Introduction

Topological Recursion
was invented in order to compute recursively the large size asymptotic expansion of random matrices
[
6
]
, and then it was realized that Topological Recursion is ubiquitous in mathematical physics, beyond random matrices, it has applications to enumerative geometry, string theory, integrable systems, conformal field theory, combinatorics of maps, statistical physics…etc.

In
[
5
]
Topological Recursion was formulated as a process that to a spectral curve (a Riemann surface with extra structure) associates a sequence of differential forms, called the
invariants
of the spectral curve.
These invariants often coincide with classical geometric invariants.
For example:

spectral curve

\to
T.R. invariants

Airy

y=\sqrt{x}

Witten-Kontsevich intersection numbers

Sine

y=\sin{(2\pi z)}

Weil-Petersson volumes

Mirror of toric CY3-fold

P(e^{x},e^{y})=0

Gromov-Witten invariants

semi-circle (GUE)

y=\sqrt{4-x^{2}}

higher Catalan numbers

A-polynomial of knot

P(e^{x},e^{y})=0

Jones polynomial

classical stress–energy

y^{2}=T(x)

CFT conformal blocs

tensor

etc…

There exists many lectures, introductory lecture notes and review articles on topological recursion and its applications.

In
[
8
]
, Topological Recursion was reformulated as ”
Quantum Airy Structure
”, in terms of 4 tensors called
A,B,C,D
, which was further developed in
[
1
]
.

The ABCD tensor formulation amounts to decomposing the invariants on a basis of forms, and consider the recursion for the coefficients.
This is how Topological recursion is actually implemented algorithmically.

In this article, we give explicit expressions of the ABCD tensors for many classical spectral curves, in a way used for actual implementation in a python package.
We also discuss the algorithmic issues and implementation.

2
Topological Recursion and ABCD tensors

Consider a Riemann surface
\overset{\circ}{{\Sigma}}
called the base curve.
Most often
\overset{\circ}{{\Sigma}}={\mathbb{C}}P^{1}={\mathbb{C}}\cup\{\infty\}
the Riemann sphere, and this is what we shall consider in all examples below.

2.1
Spectral curve

A
spectral curve
is the following data

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}{\Sigma}=\text{Riemann surface}\cr x:{\Sigma}\to\overset{\circ}{{\Sigma}}\quad=\text{ramified covering map}\cr{\rm y}=ydx=\text{ meromorphic 1-form on }{\Sigma}\cr B(z_{1},z_{2})=\text{fundamental 2nd kind differential },B\in H^{0}({\Sigma}\times{\Sigma},K_{\Sigma}\overset{\text{Sym}}{\boxtimes}K_{\Sigma})_{\mathop{\,\rm Res\,}=1}\cr R=\{\text{ramification points}\}\cr a\in R\ :\ \ \sigma_{a},\ \text{local anayltic involution such that }x(\sigma_{a}(z))=x(z)\text{ and }\sigma_{a}(a)=a.\end{array}\right.

(2-1)

For regular spectral curves, ramification points are the zeros of
dx
, and we assume that they are simple zeros.
Therefore near
z=a
,
x
is
2:1
, and there exists a unique local involution
\sigma_{a}\neq\text{Id}
, such that
\sigma_{a}(a)=a
and
x(\sigma_{a}(z))=x(z)
in a neighborhood of
a
.

We shall consider non-regular spectral curves in section
7
below.

Then we define the
recursion kernel
:

K_{a}(z_{1},z)=\frac{dS(z_{1},z)}{(y(z)-y(\sigma_{a}(z)))dx(z)}\quad\text{where}\quad dS(z_{1},z)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\sigma_{a}(z)}^{z}B(z_{1},.)

(2-2)

which is meromorphic for
z
in a neighborhood of the ramification point
a
, with a simple pole at
z=a
.

2.2
TR invariants

Topological Recursion
is a recursive procedure that to a spectral curve, associates its
invariants

\omega_{g,n}
defined as follows:

\displaystyle\omega_{0,1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle ydx

(2-3)

\displaystyle\omega_{0,2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B

(2-4)

\displaystyle\omega_{0,3}(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in R}\mathop{\,\rm Res\,}_{z\to a}K_{a}(z_{1},z)\left[B(z,z_{2})B(\sigma_{a}(z),z_{3})+B(z,z_{3})B(\sigma_{a}(z),z_{2})\right]

(2-5)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\sum_{a\in R}\mathop{\,\rm Res\,}_{z\to a}K_{a}(z_{1},z)B(z,z_{2})B(z,z_{3})

(2-6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{a\in R}\mathop{\,\rm Res\,}_{z\to a}\frac{B(z,z_{1})B(z,z_{2})B(z,z_{3})}{dx(z)dy(z)}

(2-7)

\displaystyle\omega_{1,1}(z_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in R}\mathop{\,\rm Res\,}_{z\to a}K_{a}(z_{1},z)B(z,\sigma_{a}(z))

(2-8)

and for
n\geq 1
and
2g-2+n>1

\displaystyle\omega_{g,n}(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sum_{j=2}^{n}\sum_{a\in R}\mathop{\,\rm Res\,}_{z\to a}K_{a}(z_{1},z)

(2-12)

\displaystyle\qquad B(z,z_{j})\omega_{g,n}(\sigma_{a}(z),z_{2},\dots,z_{j-1},z_{j+1},\dots,z_{n})

\displaystyle+\sum_{a\in R}\mathop{\,\rm Res\,}_{z\to a}K_{a}(z_{1},z)\Big[\omega_{g-1,n+1}(z,\sigma_{a}(z),z_{2},\dots,z_{n})

\displaystyle+\sum_{g_{1}+g_{2}=g}\sum^{\text{stable}}_{I_{1}\sqcup I_{2}=\{z_{2},\dots,z_{n}\}}\omega_{g_{1},1+|I_{1}|}(z,I_{1})\omega_{g_{2},1+|I_{2}|}(\sigma_{a}(z),I_{2})\Big]

For
n=0
, the TR invariants are defined for
g\geq 2
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{g}=\omega_{g,0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2-2g}\sum_{a\in R}\mathop{\,\rm Res\,}_{z\to a}\omega_{g,1}(z)F_{0,1}(z)

(2-13)

where
dF_{0,1}=\omega_{0,1}
(any antiderivative of
\omega_{-0,1}={\rm y}=ydx
gives the same result).

Here we won’t consider
\mathcal{F}_{0}
and
\mathcal{F}_{1}
.

Homogeneity property
:

If we rescale
ydx\to\lambda ydx
(i.e. we rescale either
x
or
y
or both), then

\omega_{g,n}\mapsto\lambda^{2-2g-n}\omega_{g,n}

(2-14)

Moreover
\omega_{g,n}
is invariant under translations
x\mapsto x+c
.

2.3
Basis of 1-forms

Consider a vector space
V
, subspace of the space of meromorphic 1-forms on
{\Sigma}
, such that for all
2g-2+n>0
we have

\omega_{g,n}\in V\otimes\dots\otimes V.

V
can be chosen as a subspace of meromorphic 1-forms that have poles at the ramification points, generated by the coefficients of the Taylor expansion of
B(z,z^{\prime})
at
z^{\prime}\in R
, i.e. by derivatives of
B(z^{\prime},z)
with respect to
z^{\prime}
, at
z^{\prime}\in R
:

V\subset\mathop{\operatorname{Span}}_{a\in R,d\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}\Big\langle(d/dx(z^{\prime}))^{d}(B(z^{\prime},z)/d\zeta_{a}(z^{\prime}))_{z^{\prime}=a}\Big\rangle

(2-15)

(using the local coordinate
\zeta_{a}(z^{\prime})=\sqrt{x(z^{\prime})-x(a)}
near
a\in R
).

V
can be chosen smaller for example when the spectral curve has symmetries.

Consider a basis
\{d\xi_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in J}
of
V
.

\omega_{g,n}
can be decomposed on the basis:

\displaystyle\omega_{g,n}(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha_{1};\dots;\alpha_{n}}F_{g,n}[\alpha_{1};\dots;\alpha_{n}]\prod_{i=1}^{n}d\xi_{\alpha_{i}}(z_{i})

(2-16)

where we assume that the basis is chosen so that the number of terms is finite.
Despite our notation
d\xi_{\alpha}
needs not be an exact form.

The goal is to be able to compute the coefficients
F_{g,n}[\alpha_{1};\dots;\alpha_{n}]
.

We also consider the antiderivative (only defined locally):

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{g,n}(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha_{1};\dots;\alpha_{n}}F_{g,n}[\alpha_{1};\dots;\alpha_{n}]\prod_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{\alpha_{i}}(z_{i}).

(2-17)

Examples of basis:

•

any basis of 1-forms
d\xi_{a,d}(z)
, such that

d\xi_{a,d}(z)
has a pole of degree
2d+2
at
z=a
, and

d\xi_{a,d}(z)+d\xi_{a,d}(\sigma_{a}(z))
has no pole at
z=a
.

•

d\xi_{a,d}(z)=\mathop{\,\rm Res\,}_{z^{\prime}\to a}(x(z^{\prime})-x(a))^{-(d+1/2)}B(z,z^{\prime})

•

d\xi_{a,0}(z)=\mathop{\,\rm Res\,}_{z^{\prime}\to a}(x(z^{\prime})-x(a))^{-1/2}B(z,z^{\prime})
and
d\xi_{a,d+1}(z)=d(d\xi_{a,d}(z)/dx(z))
.

•

many other choices are possible…

2.4
TR and ABCD Tensors

Topological Recursion (
2-12
), written for the coefficients amounts to:

\displaystyle F_{0,3}[\alpha_{1};\alpha_{2};\alpha_{3}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A[\alpha_{1};\alpha_{2};\alpha_{3}]

(2-18)

\displaystyle F_{1,1}[\alpha_{1}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D[\alpha_{1}]

(2-19)

and for
2g-2+n>1
:

\displaystyle F_{g,n}[\alpha_{1};\dots;\alpha_{n}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sum_{i=2}^{n}\sum_{\alpha\in J}B[\alpha_{1};\alpha_{i}|\alpha]\ F_{g,n-1}[\alpha;\alpha_{2};\dots;\widehat{\alpha_{i}};\dots;\alpha_{n}]

(2-22)

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\in J}C[\alpha_{1}|\alpha;\alpha^{\prime}]\ \Big[F_{g-1,n+1}[\alpha;\alpha^{\prime};\alpha_{2};\dots;\alpha_{n}]

\displaystyle+\sum_{g_{1}+g_{2}=g}\sum^{\text{stable}}_{I_{1}\cup I_{2}=\{\alpha_{2};\dots;\alpha_{n}\}}F_{g_{1},1+|I_{1}|}(\alpha;I_{1})F_{g_{2},1+|I_{2}|}(\alpha^{\prime};I_{2})\Big]

where we have introduced the following tensors:

\bullet

A\in V^{*}\otimes V^{*}\otimes V^{*}
:

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}}A[\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}]\ d\xi_{\alpha_{1}}(z_{1})d\xi_{\alpha_{2}}(z_{2})d\xi_{\alpha_{3}}(z_{3})

(2-24)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in R}\mathop{\,\rm Res\,}_{z\to a}\frac{B(z,z_{1})B(z,z_{2})B(z,z_{3})}{dx(z)dy(z)}

(2-25)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\sum_{a\in R}\mathop{\,\rm Res\,}_{z\to a}K_{a}(z_{1},z)B(z,z_{1})B(z,z_{2})

(2-26)

\bullet

B\in V^{*}\otimes V^{*}\otimes V
:

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha_{1}}\sum_{\alpha_{2}}B[\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}|\alpha_{3}]d\xi_{\alpha_{1}}(z_{1})d\xi_{\alpha_{2}}(z_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in R}\mathop{\,\rm Res\,}_{z\to a}K_{a}(z_{1},z)B(z,z_{2})d\xi_{\alpha_{3}}(\sigma_{a}(z))

(2-28)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{a\in R}\mathop{\,\rm Res\,}_{z\to a}K_{a}(z_{1},z)B(z,z_{2})d\xi_{\alpha_{3}}(z)

(2-29)

\bullet

C\in V^{*}\otimes V\otimes V
:

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha_{1}}C[\alpha_{1}|\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}]d\xi_{\alpha_{1}}(z_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in R}\mathop{\,\rm Res\,}_{z\to a}K_{a_{1}}(z_{1},z)d\xi_{\alpha_{2}}(z)d\xi_{\alpha_{3}}(\sigma_{a}(z))

(2-31)

\bullet

D\in V^{*}
:

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha_{1}}D[\alpha_{1}]d\xi_{\alpha_{1}}(z_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in R}\mathop{\,\rm Res\,}_{z\to a}K_{a}(z_{1},z)B(z,\sigma_{a}(z)).

(2-33)

These tensors are the same as those of Quantum Airy Structures of Kontsevich Soibelman
[
8
]
and
[
1
]
.
Now we shall compute explicitely
A,B,C,D
for several examples of spectral curves.

3
Airy and KdV

3.1
Spectral curve

The
KdV
spectral curve with times
\{\tilde{t}_{k}\}_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}
is defined as

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}{\Sigma}={\mathbb{C}}\cr x=z^{2}+\tilde{t}_{1}\cr y=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k\geq 3}\tilde{t}_{k}z^{k-2}\cr B=\frac{dz_{1}dz_{2}}{(z_{1}-z_{2})^{2}}\cr R=\{0\},\ \ \sigma_{0}(z)=-z\end{array}\right.

(3-1)

Airy
is the case

\tilde{t}_{k}=-2\delta_{k,3}.

(3-2)

The
KP-times
are related to
KdV-times
as follows:

\displaystyle t_{k}=-\delta_{k,1}\tilde{t}_{1}+\sum_{j\geq 0}\begin{pmatrix}-k/2\cr j\end{pmatrix}\tilde{t}_{k+2j}\tilde{t}_{1}^{j}.

(3-3)

In particular if
\tilde{t}_{1}=0
, KdV and KP times are the same
t_{k}=\tilde{t}_{k}
.

The
Minimal model
of type
(p,2)
is such that
\tilde{t}_{k}=0
for
k>p+2
.

Airy
is the minimal model
(1,2)
.

The minimal model
(3,2)
is often called
pure-gravity
, or
Painlevé 1
.

The minimal model
(5,2)
is often called
Lee-Yang
.

The case
\tilde{t}_{2k+3}=t_{2k+3}=\frac{(-1)^{k}(2\pi)^{2k}}{(2k+1)!}
is called
Weil-Petersson
.

(4-4)

Algorithmic implementation

8.1
Issues with standard tensor libraries

The implementation of TR is mostly about tensor products and contractions.
There exists many tensor libraries, often implemented using GPU, in python and other languages, however, we found several issues to use them:

•

Tensor contractions are over indices that can appear in different permuted orderings. In other words we need tensors whose indices are not labeled by [0,1,2,3…,rank] but by some labels which are not always in the same order.
Moreover in the recursive procedure, the same tensor
F_{g,n}
is re-used many times, with different labellings.
The idea is to store tensors only once, don’t permute their columns, but only permute the labelling, which is less time consuming.

•

The rank
n
tensors
F_{g,n}
have finite dimension.
But the tensors
A,B,C,D
and
A^{(h)}_{n|m}
have typically infinite dimension.
For example when applying the tensor
B
, we need to perform the operation

\sum_{\beta}B[\alpha_{1},\alpha_{j}|\beta]\ F_{g,n-1}[\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{j-1},\beta,\alpha_{j+1},\dots,\alpha_{n}]

(8-1)

For
B[\alpha_{1},\alpha_{j}|\beta]
in principle there can be an infinite number of values of
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{j},\beta
.
However since the tensor
F_{g,n-1}
is of finite dimension, there are finitely many possible values of
\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{j-1},\beta,\alpha_{j+1},\dots,\alpha_{n}
, in particular of
\beta
.
Then, remark that for a given
\beta
, there are only finitely many values of
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{j}
for which
B[\alpha_{1},\alpha_{j}|\beta]\neq 0
, so that eventually, the sum is a finite sum, and the result is a finite dim tensor.

Therefore we need a tensor library that contains the information that for given out-indices, only finitely many possible in-indices are attainable.
And moreover since the tensors
A^{(h)}_{n|m}
have infinitely many possible indices, the value can’t be stored as a tensor, but as functions of indices.

•

A less critical issue, is the fact that the tensors
F_{g,n}
are symmetric. Storing all the values is very redundant (
n!
times too much), and could be optimized.
