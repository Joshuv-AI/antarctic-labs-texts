An Unusual Presentation of Epidermolysis Bullosa Simplex with Predominant Plantar Keratoderma Due to a Novel KRT14 Variant
Authors: Bassi Andrea, Brena Michela, Colavito Davide, Tadini Gianluca, Mazzatenta Carlo
Published: 2026

Introduction

Epidermolysis bullosa (EB) simplex is well known to present with vesiculobullous lesions, especially in sites of trauma. Keratoderma lesions on the soles may be a late manifestation during the year in the most advanced cases. We present a child with only keratoderma-like lesions on the soles, moreover associated with a new KRT14 mutation.

Case Presentation

An 11-year-old female was referred to our dermatology unit for evaluation of hard, thick bilateral plantar lesions that had been present for several years. Physical examination revealed symmetrical, yellow-brown, thickened plaques with fissures on both soles (Figure 1). No active bullae were observed. The skin elsewhere appeared normal, with no signs of atrophy, scarring, or milia. No lesion was present on the hands. The patient was the second of two siblings born to healthy, non-consanguineous parents. Her 13-year-old brother exhibited similar but milder findings, characterized by a more vesicular rather than hyperkeratotic presentation on the feet. His clinical course was self-limiting and non-disabling. The children’s 38-year-old mother presented with lesions that were clinically more pronounced but similar to those of her daughter and that had also been present for many years (Figure 2). No other family member, based on available clinical history, was affected. There was no significant involvement of mucosal sites, nails, or other ectodermal structures, except for the mother, who exhibited nail hyperkeratosis and dystrophy. Both children had previously been diagnosed with recurrent warts and later with familial psoriasis. Due to the unusual presentation and keratoderma-like lesions, molecular genetic testing was pursued with suspicion of EB. Targeted next-generation sequencing (NGS) of EB-associated genes identified a heterozygous missense variant in KRT14: c.1237G>C, resulting in the amino acid substitution p.Ala-413Pro. This variant lies within the highly conserved helix termination motif of the 2B domain of keratin 14, a region critical to filament assembly and stability. Segregation analysis confirmed that the brother carried the same heterozygous variant, while the mother declined genetic testing.

Discussion

The phenotype observed in our patient differs from the classic epidermolysis bullosa simplex (EBS) presentation (1,2). Instead of prominent blistering, the clinical course was dominated by plantar keratoderma (PK), a feature more commonly associated with other keratopathies such as palmoplantar keratoderma (e.g., KRT16-related disorders) (3). The minimal history of blistering in both children, combined with prominent keratoderma, raises the possibility that certain KRT14 variants may predispose to a keratinization-dominant phenotype (4).

The identified variant, p.Ala413Pro, resides in a structurally crucial region of keratin 14 and is likely to impair filament assembly, potentially triggering cellular stress, which promotes hyperproliferation and keratinization over blistering. This observation supports a broader clinical spectrum for KRT14-associated disease than previously recognized. Notably, this specific variant (c.1237G>C; p.Ala413Pro) has been reported only once in the literature, in a case with a similar keratoderma-predominant phenotype, suggesting a possible genotype–phenotype correlation (5).

Conclusion

While additional cases are needed to confirm a definitive genotype–phenotype relationship, the recurrence of similar clinical features supports the pathogenicity and distinct phenotypic expression of this rare variant. Clinicians should consider KRT14 analysis in patients with unexplained keratoderma, especially when subtle or atypical blistering is present, and remain aware of the expanding phenotypic spectrum associated with keratin gene mutations.
