Anterior segment assessment in atrial fibrillation and congestive heart failure: insights from a regional cohort
Authors: Mustafa Kayabaşı, Ezgi Karataş, Fahri Er, Seher Köksaldı, Yavuz Oruç
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to determine whether cardiovascular conditions, specifically atrial fibrillation (AF) and congestive heart failure (CHF), are associated with detectable alterations in anterior segment morphology via Scheimpflug-based corneal tomography (Pentacam®, Oculus, Germany).MethodsA total of 120 eyes from 120 participants were included in this cross-sectional study. The subjects were categorized into four groups: AF, CHF, AF + CHF, and age- and sex-matched healthy controls (n = 30 per group). All participants underwent a comprehensive ophthalmic evaluation, including Pentacam imaging. The quantitative analyses included keratometric parameters, corneal pachymetry profiles, anterior chamber metrics, pupil diameter, and indices of corneal surface irregularity.ResultsThe groups were comparable in terms of age and sex (p > 0.05). No significant intergroup differences emerged in keratometric values, corneal thickness, or anterior chamber metrics, though astigmatism trended lower in the AF + CHF group. Among the irregularity indices, only the keratoconus index (KI) demonstrated an overall group effect (p = 0.049), but post hoc comparisons failed to confirm significance.ConclusionsScheimpflug-based tomography revealed largely preserved anterior segment characteristics in individuals with AF and/or CHF. The minor variation observed in the KI may reflect subtle corneal surface irregularities potentially associated with systemic cardiovascular pathology, highlighting the need for larger, longitudinal studies to clarify these findings.Keywords: Anterior segment, Atrial fibrillation, Congestive heart failure, Corneal tomography, Cardiovascular disease, Scheimpflug imaging

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) and congestive heart failure (CHF) are among the most prevalent cardiovascular disorders, which frequently coexist and contribute significantly to global morbidity, mortality, and healthcare expenditure. These conditions share multiple modifiable and nonmodifiable risk factors, such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus, obesity, and smoking, and often arise through interrelated pathophysiologic processes involving structural and electrical remodeling, neurohormonal activation, and autonomic imbalance. Given the aging global population and the growing burden of cardiovascular disease, the concomitant occurrence of AF and CHF is projected to increase substantially in the coming decades, posing a major public health challenge [1, 2].

Growing evidence supports the eye as a noninvasive indicator of systemic health. Modern imaging technologies now permit detailed visualization of anterior segment structures that may reflect vascular or connective tissue changes elsewhere in the body. Corneal alterations have been described in systemic diseases such as diabetes and hypertension [1, 3, 4]; however, the impact of AF and CHF on anterior segment morphology has not been clearly established.

Both AF and CHF have been associated with systemic microvascular dysfunction, chronic low-grade inflammation, and altered extracellular matrix turnover, processes implicated in vascular remodeling and tissue fibrosis [5, 6]. These pathophysiological changes, in particular disturbances in collagen remodeling, endothelial dysfunction, and autonomic imbalance, might influence corneal hydration, stromal architecture, and anterior chamber biomechanics.

Determining whether chronic cardiac dysfunction leads to measurable anterior segment changes may deepen our understanding of systemic-ocular interactions. Scheimpflug tomography provides reproducible, high-resolution evaluation of corneal and anterior chamber parameters, facilitating detection of subtle morphologic differences. Compared with conventional Placido-based topography, Scheimpflug imaging offers highly reproducible three-dimensional measurements and improved characterization of both corneal and anterior chamber structures [7].

To our knowledge, no prior study has systematically compared these parameters among patients with AF, CHF, and their coexistence. This study aimed to fill that gap by characterizing and contrasting anterior segment findings across these groups and healthy controls.

Materials and methods

This study was designed as a retrospective, cross-sectional analysis conducted collaboratively by the Departments of Ophthalmology and Cardiology at a tertiary care training and research hospital in eastern Turkey. Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional review board, and all procedures adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. As the study involved retrospective data analysis without additional procedures, the requirement for informed consent was waived. All clinical data were fully anonymized before being accessed for analysis.

Study population

The study included 120 participants aged 29–88 years, who were equally distributed into four groups (n = 30 each): AF, CHF, combined AF + CHF, and healthy age- and sex-matched controls. Cardiovascular diagnoses were confirmed by a cardiologist following the latest European Society of Cardiology guidelines, via clinical evaluation, electrocardiography, and echocardiography [8]. Atrial fibrillation status was identified on the basis of documented history, relevant medication use, or electrocardiographic evidence at admission [9].

Among the 120 patients initially screened in the AF, CHF, and AF + CHF groups, 30 were excluded due to additional systemic comorbidities other than hypertension or diabetes mellitus, leaving 90 patients eligible for final analysis in the cardiac cohorts.

Cardiological data extracted from the most recent cardiology visit corresponding to the ophthalmological evaluation included AF subtype (paroxysmal, persistent, or permanent) and duration; CHF phenotype (heart failure with midrange ejection fraction [HFmrEF], heart failure with reduced ejection fraction [HFrEF], or heart failure with preserved ejection fraction [HFpEF]) and duration; New York Health Associaton (NYHA) class; left ventricular ejection fraction measurement; systolic and diastolic blood pressures; heart rate; glomerular filtration rate; presence of hypertension or diabetes mellitus; smoking status; and medication profile (use of beta-blockers, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor, diuretics, anticoagulants, anti-arrhythmics, sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitors and digoxin). The duration of AF and CHF was determined based on the interval between the first documented cardiology diagnosis and the time of the ocular examination that was used for analysis.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Eligible participants were required to have undergone both cardiological and ophthalmological assessments within a one-month period; to have a best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) of 20/40 or better; to have clear corneas permitting accurate Scheimpflug imaging; and to demonstrate sufficient cooperation during examination. Only participants without systemic illnesses other than hypertension, diabetes mellitus, AF and/or CHF were included, as hypertension and diabetes mellitus are well-recognized major confounders in cardiovascular diseases [10, 11].

The exclusion criteria included a history of ocular surgery or trauma, contact lens use, glaucoma, uveitis, optic neuropathy, corneal opacity, or pterygium. Individuals with high refractive error (spherical equivalent > ± 3.00 D), active inflammatory or autoimmune disorders, neurological disease, recent stroke, or current use of topical or systemic corticosteroids or amiodarone were also excluded, as were patients with missing or poor-quality Scheimpflug scans. Patients with a remote history of stroke (> 6 months) were included only if no persistent neurological deficits were present.

Ophthalmologic examination

All participants underwent a comprehensive ophthalmologic examination, including slit-lamp biomicroscopy, measurement of intraocular pressure via Goldmann applanation tonometry, and anterior segment imaging via the Pentacam (Oculus, Germany). To avoid pharmacologic effects on pupil diameter (PD), Scheimpflug imaging was always performed prior to pharmacologic dilation for fundus assessment. All procedures were conducted by a single experienced technician under consistent lighting conditions to ensure measurement reliability and reproducibility. Pupil diameter was recorded under the standardized mesopic illumination supplied by the Pentacam device.

The imaging protocol captured a wide array of anterior segment parameters, including keratometric readings of the anterior and posterior corneal surfaces as well as true net keratometry. Corneal astigmatism was assessed for the anterior, posterior, and total corneal surfaces, whereas corneal asphericity (Q values) was recorded for both surfaces to evaluate curvature profiles. The corneal thickness measurements included central, apical, and thinnest points, and the anterior segment biometrics included the corneal volume, anterior chamber depth and volume, and pupil diameter. Surface irregularity was quantified via multiple indices, including the index of surface variance (ISV), index of vertical asymmetry (IVA), keratoconus index (KI), central keratoconus index (CKI), index of height asymmetry (IHA), index of height decentration (IHD), and minimum sagittal curvature radius (Rmin).

For each participant, one eye was chosen using a simple randomization procedure based on a computer-generated 0–1 number sequence, whereby values < 0.5 indicated the right eye and ≥ 0.5 indicated the left eye. For each included eye, three consecutive high-quality Scheimpflug scans were obtained, and the mean value was used for subsequent statistical analysis. Only scans meeting the manufacturer’s quality criteria (QS: ‘OK’) were included in the analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed via IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data: categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages, whereas continuous variables are expressed as the means ± standard deviations. The normality of distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, given the sample size of fewer than 50 participants per group. Homogeneity of variances was evaluated with Levene’s test. Normally distributed variables were compared using one-way ANOVA, whereas non-normally distributed variables were analyzed with the Mann-Whitney U and Kruskal-Wallis tests. Categorical data were analyzed via the Pearson chi-square test. Post-hoc analyses were performed using the independent samples t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate, with Bonferroni correction applied for multiple comparisons. A p-value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Demographic and cardiovascular characteristics

A total of 120 eyes from 120 individuals were included in the study. The cohort comprised 62 males (51.7%) and 58 females (48.3%), with a mean age of 65.43 ± 9.63 years (range: 29–88 years). Thirty participants were allocated to each of the following groups: the control, AF, CHF, and combined AF + CHF groups. Baseline demographic characteristics, including age and sex distribution, were comparable among the four groups (p = 0.160 and p = 0.518, respectively), thereby minimizing the risk of demographic confounding (Table 1).

Corneal keratometric parameters

Comparative analysis of anterior, posterior, and true keratometric parameters revealed no statistically significant differences among the groups (p > 0.05 for all). No significant differences were found in terms of the mean anterior flat, steep, mean or maximum keratometry values among the groups (p > 0.05 for all). Similarly, posterior keratometric values (K1, K2, and Km) demonstrated no significant intergroup differences (p > 0.05 for all). Although the combined AF + CHF group presented numerically lower astigmatism values on anterior, posterior, and true keratometric surfaces, these differences did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.426, 0.209, and 0.276, respectively). Furthermore, the corneal asphericity was comparable among all groups for both the anterior and posterior corneal surfaces (p > 0.05 for both) (Table 3).

Anterior segment biometrics and corneal thickness parameters

There were no statistically significant differences in central corneal thickness, apical corneal thickness, or thinnest corneal thickness among the four groups (p = 0.957, 0.926, and 0.837, respectively). The groups also did not demonstrate significant differences in terms of the mean corneal volume, anterior chamber volume, or anterior chamber depth values (p > 0.05 for all). Additionally, PD, a potential indicator of autonomic function, showed no significant intergroup difference (p = 0.275) (Table 4).

Corneal surface irregularity indices

Among the evaluated surface irregularity indices, only the KI demonstrated a statistically significant difference among the groups (p = 0.049). However, subsequent pairwise comparisons did not yield any significant differences between individual groups, indicating a possible trend that did not achieve conclusive significance. The other indices, ISV, IVA, CKI, IHA, and IHD, did not show significantly differ among the groups (p > 0.05 for all). Similarly, the minimum sagittal curvature radius (Rmin) values showed no significant intergroup variation (p = 0.890) (Table 4).

Discussion

The relationships between anterior segment parameters and cardiovascular conditions such as AF and CHF remain insufficiently investigated. Our findings indicate that most biometric measures of the cornea and anterior segment including keratometric readings, corneal thickness, anterior chamber depth, anterior chamber volume, and PD were similar among all the study cohorts. Notably, among the corneal surface irregularity indices, only the KI reached statistical significance (p = 0.049); however, pairwise group comparisons did not confirm these differences. In line with this outcome, the KI result should be interpreted cautiously, as the borderline ANOVA finding without significant post-hoc support likely represents a non-robust statistical signal rather than a reproducible intergroup difference.

Clinically, the lack of significant differences in anterior segment measurements across AF, CHF, and control groups suggests that these cardiovascular conditions do not impair the accuracy of routine ocular biometry. This is reassuring for cataract and refractive surgery planning, where reliable keratometry and pachymetry values are crucial. Our findings indicate that corneal morphology remains stable in patients with chronic cardiovascular disease, and that Pentacam measurements can be interpreted with confidence in this population.

Most prior studies on cardiovascular disease and the eye have focused on posterior segment involvement, reporting retinal microangiopathy and optic nerve perfusion abnormalities [9, 12, 13]. However, evidence addressing anterior segment or corneal structural alterations in systemic cardiac conditions remains sparse. In the present cohort, no significant intergroup differences were detected, possibly reflecting the cornea’s avascularity and resistance to systemic circulatory stress [14]. The inherent mechanical strength and adaptive capacity of the ocular surface may further explain this stability. Together, these observations suggest that anterior segment architecture is largely preserved in the presence of cardiovascular disease.

From another perspective, detailed corneal tomographic analyses in diabetes mellitus have shown that chronic hyperglycemia can markedly alter the spatial distribution of corneal thickness, even when central corneal thickness remains within normal limits. Scheimpflug tomography studies have demonstrated that diabetic patients exhibit a significantly steeper peripheral corneal thickness gradient and higher pachymetry slope values compared with healthy controls, with these alterations being more pronounced in individuals with poor glycemic control [15]. These findings suggest that metabolic and microvascular derangements preferentially affect the corneal periphery. By contrast, in our AF and CHF cohorts, neither central, apical, nor thinnest corneal thickness, nor average corneal volume differed significantly from those of age-matched controls. Taken together, whereas certain systemic diseases such as diabetes mellitus induce characteristic and spatially heterogeneous patterns of corneal remodeling, chronic cardiovascular dysfunction associated with AF and/or CHF appears to exert a far more limited influence on corneal pachymetry profiles in the relatively stable outpatient population assessed in this study.

Pupil diameter, which is frequently regarded as a surrogate indicator of autonomic nervous system activity [16], was not significantly different among the study groups. This finding suggests that, despite the well-established impact of AF and CHF on systemic autonomic balance, such alterations may not be reflected in resting pupillary size under standard photopic conditions [17]. Several factors could underlie this observation, including compensatory mechanisms within ocular autonomic pathways, interindividual variability in autonomic responsiveness, and the use of systemic medications such as beta-blockers commonly prescribed in these patient populations. Furthermore, static PD measurements may lack sensitivity to subtle autonomic variations; dynamic pupillometric assessments under variable light or pharmacologic stimuli might be more effective in revealing these differences [18].

Wang et al. [19] reported that pediatric patients with congenital heart disease presented with thinner corneas, lower intraocular pressure, and deeper anterior chambers than healthy controls. In the present study, although the differences did not reach statistical significance, eyes in the combined AF + CHF group presented numerically lower values for central, apical, and thinnest corneal thicknesses, as well as reduced anterior and posterior corneal astigmatism. These subtle tendencies may reflect early or subclinical alterations in corneal biomechanics or curvature symmetry. Chronic cardiovascular disease could plausibly contribute to such modifications through mechanisms involving sustained vascular insufficiency, impaired collagen turnover, or autonomic dysregulation [20, 21].

This study was conducted in a rural area situated at a moderate altitude of approximately 1,600 m above sea level. High-altitude environments are known to impose physiological challenges, including mild hypobaric hypoxia, which may influence ocular and systemic parameters [22–24]. Such environmental factors could modulate both the clinical presentation of AF, and CHF, and corneal measurements obtained through Scheimpflug tomography, particularly those related to thickness and endothelial integrity [25]. In our cohort, however, the anterior segment parameters of the control group, especially central corneal thickness and keratometric values, were consistent with normative ranges reported for similar age groups in the literature, suggesting that the altitude of our region did not meaningfully shift baseline corneal metrics. Although mean corneal thickness values across all study groups were slightly lower than those reported in large normative datasets, the intergroup differences remained nonsignificant [26, 27]. These observations indicate that systemic cardiovascular pathology has a minimal interplay with anterior segment morphology under moderate-altitude conditions. Nonetheless, further multicenter studies across varied geographic and environmental settings would be valuable to confirm these observations.

Cardiovascular diseases such as AF and CHF are associated with systemic endothelial impairment, chronic inflammation, and altered hemodynamics, all of which might theoretically influence corneal biomechanics and ocular microcirculation [2, 13, 28, 29]. In this study, however, no significant alterations in corneal curvature, thickness, or anterior segment metrics were observed among affected patients. The minor differences in the KI merit further study but do not indicate a clinically relevant change at present.

Recently, heart failure pharmacotherapies including sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitors, have been shown to exert pleiotropic effects that extend beyond hemodynamic control, influencing inflammation, fibrosis, and autonomic regulation. The latest evidence indicates that such agents may modulate atrial remodeling and reduce the burden of atrial fibrillation through extracardiac pathways [30]. Although our study did not directly assess medication-related influences on ocular parameters, these therapeutic effects underscore the complex interplay between systemic cardiovascular treatments and tissue-level remodeling. Future studies incorporating detailed pharmacologic stratification may help clarify whether specific heart failure regimens differentially affect corneal morphology or autonomic-related ocular metrics such as PD.

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. The sample size was relatively modest, with 30 participants per group, which may have constrained the statistical power and limited generalizability. Although no a priori sample size calculation was performed in this study, we conducted a post-hoc detectable effect-size assessment, which suggested that the available sample size (30 eyes per group) was adequate to detect moderate, clinically meaningful differences in central corneal thickness and keratometry parameters. Therefore, while very small effects cannot be excluded, the study was sufficiently powered to identify differences of potential clinical relevance. Another limitation was that all participants were recruited from a single tertiary hospital, which may introduce regional bias. Not all cardiologic parameters (e.g. full medication profiles, atrial and B-type natriuretic peptide levels) were uniformly available due to the retrospective nature of data analysis, which may introduce variability in disease severity across groups. Intra-session reproducibility statistics, such as intraclass correlation coefficients, were not calculated, which may be considered a methodological limitation.

Despite these limitations, this study provides novel insights into the potential associations between systemic cardiac dysfunction and anterior segment ocular parameters, an area that remains underexplored. The strengths of the study include its methodical design with demographically matched, evenly sized groups, the inclusion of only one eye per participant to reduce selection bias, and the use of Scheimpflug tomography for sensitive, objective assessment of corneal structure. To our knowledge, this is the first study to comprehensively evaluate corneal morphological changes in patients with AF, CHF, or both.

In conclusion, anterior segment parameters appear largely preserved in patients with AF, CHF, or their combination, supporting the reliability of biometric measurements for clinical and surgical decision-making. These findings represent an initial step toward understanding the potential effects of cardiovascular disease on corneal morphology. Future studies with larger cohorts, longitudinal follow-up, and the incorporation of corneal biochemical or biomechanical markers will be important to further elucidate these relationships.
