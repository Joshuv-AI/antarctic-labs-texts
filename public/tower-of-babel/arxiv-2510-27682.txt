The zero capillarity limit for the Euler-Korteweg system with no-flux boundary conditions
Paolo Antonelli, Yuri Cacchiò

Abstract
In this article, we study the small dispersion limit of the Euler-Korteweg system in a bounded domain with no-flux boundary conditions. We exploit a relative entropy approach to study the convergence of finite energy weak solutions towards strong solutions to the compressible Euler system. Since we consider non-trivial boundary conditions, our approach needs a correction for the limiting particle density, due to the appearance of a (weak) boundary layer. We believe this approach can be adapted to study similar singular limits involving non-trivial boundary conditions.



1
Introduction

We study the following Euler-Korteweg (EK) system,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\partial_{t}\rho+\diver(\rho u)&=0;\\
\partial_{t}(\rho u)+\diver\left(\rho u\otimes u\right)+\nabla p(\rho)&=\varepsilon^{2}\rho\nabla\left(\diver(k(\rho)\nabla\rho)-\frac{1}{2}k^{\prime}(\rho)|\nabla\rho|^{2}\right),\end{array}\right.

(1.1)

posed on a domain
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
The model (
1.1
) describes the time evolution of a compressible, inviscid flow in terms of its particle density
\rho=\rho(t,x)
and velocity field
u=u(t,x)
.
For simplicity, we assume the pressure to satisfy a power-type state law, namely we impose
p(\rho)=\rho^{\gamma}
, with
\gamma>1
. The term on the right-hand side of the equation for the momentum density represents the Korteweg stress tensor and accounts for capillary effects in the model
1573105975105495040
;
MR775366
.
The capillarity coefficient
k=k(\rho)\geq 0
is given and we assume it satisfies the following equation of state

k(\rho)=c_{\alpha}\rho^{\alpha}\text{ with }c_{\alpha}>0,\,\alpha\geq-1.

(1.2)

The Korteweg term was originally introduced in
1573105975105495040
to describe capillary effects in liquid-vapor mixtures with diffuse interfaces, and it was derived later in
MR775366
. We also refer to
MR2760987
, where the derivation is obtained without introducing the concept of interstitial working.
Similar models also arise in different physical contexts. For instance, equations (
1.1
) with
k(\rho)=\frac{1}{4\rho}
play a prominent role in the description of quantum fluids such as Bose-Einstein condensates or superfluid Helium
pitaevskii2016bose
. With this choice of the capillarity coefficient, equations (
1.1
) are usually called the
quantum hydrodynamics
(QHD) system.
For a more detailed discussion on the physical motivations and the derivation of models, we refer to
MR775366
;
MR2760987
;
MR3916773
for general EK systems, and to
AM_K
for QHD.

Our main interest lies in the analysis of the vanishing capillarity limit
\varepsilon\to 0
for a class of initial-boundary value problems (IBVP) associated to (
1.1
). More precisely, we complement system (
1.1
) with initial data

\rho|_{t=0}=\rho_{0},\qquad(\rho u)|_{t=0}=J_{0},

(1.3)

and no-flux boundary conditions,

u\cdot n|_{\partial\Omega}=0,\qquad\partial_{n}\rho|_{\partial\Omega}=0.

(1.4)

We denote by
n
the unit outward normal.
Let us emphasize that
\Omega
is allowed to be unbounded, in which case we assume
\rho\to 0
,
\rho u\to 0
, as
|x|\to 0
.

In many physical contexts, the constant
\varepsilon>0
appearing in front of the Korteweg stress tensor (
2.1
) is relatively small
jamet
.

Formally, we expect the asymptotic dynamics to be governed by the compressible Euler system

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\partial_{t}\rho^{E}+\diver(\rho^{E}u^{E})&=0;\\
\partial_{t}(\rho^{E}u^{E})+\diver\left(\rho^{E}u^{E}\otimes u^{E}\right)+\nabla p(\rho^{E})&=0,\end{array}\right.

(1.5)

complemented with the following boundary conditions,

u^{E}\cdot n|_{\partial\Omega}=0.

(1.6)

Due to the mismatch of boundary conditions between (
1.4
) and (
1.6
), the formation of a boundary layer is expected in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
.

Our main results provide a rigorous justification of the above heuristics in several aspects. First of all, we show the convergence of weak solutions to (
1.1
), (
1.4
), towards solutions to (
1.5
), (
1.6
), in the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
. This is achieved by a relative entropy argument. More precisely, we study the time evolution of a functional that measures the distance between solutions to (
1.1
) and (
1.5
) in a suitable topology.
However, this functional is insensitive to the phenomenon of boundary layer formation mentioned above. For this purpose, we consider a second relative entropy functional that also involves first order derivatives of the particle density, see (
2.35
) for a precise definition.

In this second case, the gradient of (a nonlinear function of) the limiting particle density,

v^{E}=\sqrt{c_{\alpha}}\nabla(\rho^{E})^{\frac{\alpha+1}{2}},

(1.7)

needs to be taken as suitable test function in the weak formulation for the momentum density equation in (
1.1
). Since in general
\rho^{E}
does not satisfy the Neumann boundary condition in (
1.4
), and consequently
v^{E}\cdot n|_{{\partial}\Omega}\neq 0
, we introduce a boundary layer correction
v_{bl}
, so that
v^{E}_{bl}=v^{E}-v_{bl}
satisfies
v^{E}_{bl}\cdot n|_{{\partial}\Omega}=0
. This in turn implies also the convergence of first order derivatives of the particle density, see Theorem
2.8
for a more precise statement.

Let us further remark that, unlike
classical results in vanishing viscosity limits for the Navier-Stokes equation
MR765230
, see also
MR3171346
for the compressible case, our result does not require conditions on the lack of anomalous dissipation at the boundary. This is due to the fact that the vanishing capillarity limit is a weaker boundary layer compared to the one appearing for vanishing viscosity limits.

Before presenting and discussing our main Theorems
2.4
and
2.8
below, we first recall some results already existing in the literature that are relevant to our analysis.

1.1
Survey of related results

The Cauchy problem (
1.1
), (
1.3
) posed on
\Omega={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is known to be locally well-posed for
(\rho,u)\in(\bar{\rho},\bar{u})+H^{s+1}\times H^{s}
, with
s>\frac{d}{2}+1
,
(\bar{\rho},\bar{u})\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
being a reference homogeneous solution

. Global dispersive solutions may be obtained for irrotational flows under more restrictive assumptions on the initial data, see
AH
. For general solutions, the time of existence may be estimated in terms of the rotational part of the velocity field
MR4401388
.

The problem is also studied on periodic domains
BMM
;
FIM
, where it is also possible to prove non-uniqueness results by relying upon convex integration techniques
DFM_ill
.
In this context, the vanishing capillarity limit is studied in
MR3647257
by a relative entropy argument, under specific assumptions on the pressure and the capillarity term.
A similar relative-entropy approach is also exploited to analyze the viscous counterpart of system (
1.1
), given by the so-called Navier-Stokes-Korteweg (NSK) system. In
MR3961293
, for instance, the authors construct dissipative solutions to (
1.1
) as vanishing viscosity limit of global-in-time weak solutions to the NSK system.
Moreover, the vanishing capillarity limit of NSK towards the compressible Navier-Stokes equations may also be proved by a compactness argument
BDL
, owing to the extra dissipative estimates available in this case.
Similar arguments yield also results on different singular limits, see for instance
ACLS
;
BCMNX
;
CCL
;
carnevale2025extending
.

The QHD system, obtained by choosing
k(\rho)=\frac{1}{4\rho}
in (
1.1
), plays a special role in the theory, being related to the following nonlinear Schrödinger (NLS) equation

i\varepsilon{\partial}_{t}\psi=-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\Delta\psi+\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}|\psi|^{2(\gamma-1)}\psi,

(1.8)

via the so-called Madelung transformations

. More precisely, Madelung’s approach derives system (
1.1
) as the evolution equations for the momenta associated to
\psi
, by defining
\rho=|\psi|^{2}
,
\rho u=\varepsilon\IM(\bar{\psi}\nabla\psi)
.
For this reason, more results are available for the QHD, than for general EK systems. In particular, it is possible to show the existence of global-in-time, finite energy weak solutions, see
MR2481754
;
MR2885568
;
MR4244267
where the authors also establish a rigorous justification of the Madelung transformation for general, finite energy wave functions.
Notice that the hydrodynamic boundary conditions (
1.4
) correspond to homogeneous Neumann conditions for the wave function, i.e.

\partial_{n}\psi|_{\partial\Omega}=0.

(1.9)

Furthermore, equation (
1.8
) also highlights the relation between the vanishing capillarity limit for QHD and the semiclassical limit for the NLS equations, for which an extensive literature already exists. We refer to
MR2500991
;
MR1425123
, see also the monograph
MR4274579
for a comprehensive presentation of the mathematical results in this context.
On the other hand, most of the existing literature considers the vanishing capillarity/semiclassical limit problem in the whole space. Very few results are dedicated to domains with non-trivial boundaries. In
MR2208290
, by a modulated energy (relative entropy) approach, the semiclassical limit for (
1.8
), (
1.9
) with
\gamma=2
, is proven in a two-dimensional exterior domain with the Neumann boundary condition (
1.9
) and non-trivial conditions at space infinity.
The same problem posed on the half-space is considered in
MR2500991
, where the authors study the expansion of the solution
\psi^{\varepsilon}
to (
1.8
) in powers of
\varepsilon
by means of the WKB approach, by extending the result in
MR1425123
in various aspects. In particular, the result in
MR2500991
also computes the boundary layer expansion to prove the convergence at any order of
\varepsilon
.
Their analysis shows that a weak boundary layer of spatial size
O(\varepsilon)
is formed.
A similar result is also obtained with Dirichlet boundary conditions
MR4379626
, where an extra smallness condition on initial and boundary data is needed, in order to deal with the stronger boundary layer.
In the recent paper
audiard2025zeroA
, the authors study the vanishing dispersion limit for the Euler-Korteweg system. In particular, they also perform a WKB analysis for the QHD system (i.e., they fix
k(\rho)=1/4\rho)
on the half-plane with some hybrid boundary conditions (homogeneous Dirichlet for
\rho
, no-flux for
u
).

An alternative approach to study the vanishing capillarity limit for (
1.1
) is to derive suitable time-decay properties for the linearized operator and show uniform estimates in the parameter
\varepsilon>0
,
see the interesting analysis performed in
song2025global
. We also refer to
MR3196944
, where the same problem is studied in the viscous setting.

Our strategy of proof exploits a relative entropy argument. This is a well established tool for studying PDEs, where it is used to prove results about stability
Daf
;
dafermos1979stability
, weak-strong uniqueness
MR523630
;
MR2992037
, singular limits
MR1748352
;
MR3594360
;
MR1944031
;
carnevale2025extending
.
In our paper, we study a relative entropy functional as in
MR3961293
;
MR3647257
to analyze the singular limit from solutions to (
1.1
), (
1.3
), (
1.4
), towards solutions to (
1.5
), (
1.3
), (
1.6
). In what follows we highlight some of the main features of our result.

1.2
Main contributions of the paper

As already mentioned, this paper exploits a relative-entropy method to show the convergence of finite energy weak solutions to (
1.1
), (
1.4
), towards strong (classical) solutions to (
1.5
), (
1.6
). A similar approach was already considered in
MR3647257
for the problem posed in a periodic domain.
However, the result in
MR3647257
holds under some restrictive conditions on the capillarity coefficient and the pressure law, see assumptions (Set1) and (Set2) there.
In our paper we are able to consider non-trivial boundary conditions, and furthermore we allow for a more general class of Euler-Korteweg systems.
This is mainly due to the different structure of the relative entropy functional we consider. Indeed, in the higher order entropy
\mathcal{E}_{h}
, that will be introduced in (
2.35
) below, we interpret the capillary energy as the kinetic energy associated to an auxiliary velocity field, defined as in (
1.7
).
This is reminiscent of the augmented formulation of Euler-Korteweg and Navier-Stokes-Korteweg models, see for instance
MR3734208
. A similar relative entropy was already introduced in
MR3961293
to study the vanishing viscosity limit for the Navier-Stokes-Korteweg system, see also the recent preprint
carnevale2025extending
.

Moreover, as already commented, our result is also similar in spirit to the semiclassical limit for NLS equations. We remark that the result in
MR2208290
would yield, thanks to the Madelung transformations, a similar convergence in the case of the QHD system, i.e.
k(\rho)=1/4\rho
, with a quadratic pressure, i.e.
\gamma=2
. Moreover, in order to perform the relative entropy argument in
MR2208290
, a
H^{2}-
regularity for initial data to the NLS dynamics is required.
The present analysis extends to a more general class of Euler-Korteweg systems. Notice that in this case, the analogy given by the Madelung transformations would yield a
quasilinear
Schrödinger equation, whose analysis presents analogous difficulties as system (
1.1
).

2
Preliminaries and statement of the main results

In this Section, we recall some preliminary results, introduce the basic notions needed throughout our paper and state the main results.

First of all, we notice that
the Korteweg term may be written in divergence form as follows,

\varepsilon^{2}\rho\nabla\left(\diver(k(\rho)\nabla\rho)-\frac{1}{2}k^{\prime}(\rho)|\nabla\rho|^{2}\right)=\varepsilon^{2}\diver\mathbb{K},

(2.1)

where the Korteweg stress tensor
\mathbb{K}
is given by

\mathbb{K}=\left(\diver(\rho k(\rho)\nabla\rho)-\frac{1}{2}(\rho k^{\prime}(\rho)+k(\rho)|\nabla\rho|^{2})\right)\mathbb{I}-k(\rho)\nabla\rho\otimes\nabla\rho,

(2.2)

and
\mathbb{I}
is the identity matrix.

The total energy corresponding to the Euler-Korteweg system (
1.1
) is defined by

E_{EK}=\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}\rho|u|^{2}+f(\rho)+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}k(\rho)|\nabla\rho|^{2}\,dx,

(2.3)

where

f(\rho)=\rho\int^{\rho}_{0}\frac{p(s)}{s^{2}}ds.

(2.4)

Notice that in the particular case of

p(\rho)=\rho^{\gamma},\qquad\gamma>1

(2.5)

the internal energy density
becomes

f(\rho)=\frac{1}{\gamma-1}\rho^{\gamma}.

Moreover, the total energy
E_{EK}
is formally conserved along solutions to (
1.1
). Indeed, no dissipative effects are encoded in system (
1.1
) and the only natural a priori estimates on
(\rho,u)
are the ones given by (
2.3
).

In particular, the only available bound on
u
is in
L^{2}(\Omega;\rho\,dx)
, which implies that no control on the velocity field is available in the vacuum region
\{\rho=0\}
.

For this reason, it seems more convenient to write the system in terms of the mass and momentum densities
\rho
and
J=\rho u
, respectively. In this way, the total energy becomes

E_{EK}=\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}\frac{|J|^{2}}{\rho}+\frac{1}{\gamma-1}\rho^{\gamma}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}k(\rho)|\nabla\rho|^{2}\,dx.

(2.6)

For a finite energy state
(\rho,J)
we then have that
J=0
a.e. on
\{\rho=0\}
.
In fact, for the purpose of our analysis, it will be also convenient to consider the pseudovelocity field

\Lambda=\frac{J}{\sqrt{\rho}}=\sqrt{\rho}u,

for which the energy bound yields
\Lambda\in L^{\infty}_{t}L^{2}_{x}
.
The vector field
\Lambda
does not have a clear physical interpretation, however it allows to provide a good definition (in the distributional sense, at least) to all terms in (
1.1
)-(
2.3
) involving the velocity field.
For convenience, we also define the following auxiliary state functions,

\displaystyle K(\rho)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\rho}sk(s)ds,

(2.7)

\displaystyle\beta(\rho)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\rho}\sqrt{k(s)}ds,

(2.8)

so that the Korteweg term may be formally written as

\diver\mathbb{K}=\nabla\left(\Delta K(\rho)-\frac{1}{2}K^{\prime\prime}(\rho)|\nabla\rho|^{2}\right)-\diver\Big(\nabla\beta(\rho)\otimes\nabla\beta(\rho)\Big).

Our choice of the capillarity coefficient (
1.2
)
implies that

\beta(\rho)=\frac{2c_{\alpha}}{2+\alpha}\rho^{1+\frac{\alpha}{2}},\quad K(\rho)=\frac{c_{\alpha}}{2+\alpha}\rho^{2+\alpha},

(2.9)

and the capillary energy is then written as follows

\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}k(\rho)|\nabla\rho|^{2}\,dx=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\beta(\rho)|^{2}\,dx.

Thus, a finite energy state must satisfy

\nabla\beta(\rho)\in L^{2}
.
Moreover, our assumption (
1.2
) implies that there exists a constant
\omega>0
such that

|\rho k^{\prime}(\rho)|\leq\omega k(\rho),

(2.10)

that in turn gives

|K^{\prime\prime}(\rho)||\nabla\rho|^{2}\lesssim k(\rho)|\nabla\rho|^{2}=|\nabla\beta(\rho)|^{2}\in L^{1}.

Resuming, we may rewrite the Euler-Korteweg system (
1.1
) as follows,

\left\{\begin{aligned} &{\partial}_{t}\rho+\diver J=0\\
&{\partial}_{t}J+\diver\left(\Lambda\otimes\Lambda\right)+\nabla p(\rho)=&\varepsilon^{2}\nabla\left(\Delta K(\rho)-\frac{1}{2}K^{\prime\prime}(\rho)|\nabla\rho|^{2}\right)\\
&&-\varepsilon^{2}\diver(\nabla\beta(\rho)\otimes\nabla\beta(\rho)),\end{aligned}\right.

(2.11)

where
\Lambda
is such that
J=\sqrt{\rho}\Lambda
.

Moreover, in view of the weak formulation we are going to provide below for system (
2.11
), we see it is more suitable to adapt the boundary conditions (
1.4
) as follows

{\partial}_{n}\beta(\rho)|_{{\partial}\Omega}=0,\qquad\Lambda\cdot n|_{{\partial}\Omega}=0.

(2.12)

Again, this is dictated by the possible appearance of vacuum regions in the problem, that prevents to uniquely define the velocity field.

2.1
Relative entropy functionals

We now introduce the relative entropy functionals we are going to consider in this paper.

Given a pair of ”test functions”
(r,U)
, we define

\mathcal{E}([\rho,\Lambda]|[r,U])=\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}|\Lambda-\sqrt{\rho}U|^{2}+f(\rho|r)+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}|\nabla\beta(\rho)|^{2}dx

(2.20)

where the relative internal energy density is defined by

f(\rho|r)=f(\rho)-f^{\prime}(r)\cdot(\rho-r)-f(r).

(2.21)

Throughout the paper, we write
\mathcal{E}
instead of
\mathcal{E}([\rho,\Lambda]|[\rho^{E},u^{E}])
whenever it does not create any confusion.

Remark 2.3
.

Notice that, for the QHD system, i.e.
k(\rho)=\frac{1}{4\rho}
, the relative entropy functional reads

\mathcal{E}=\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}|\Lambda-\sqrt{\rho}u^{E}|^{2}+f(\rho|\rho^{E})+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}|\nabla\sqrt{\rho}|^{2}\,dx.

By considering the analogy with the NLS dynamics (
1.8

\mathcal{E}=\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}|\varepsilon\nabla\psi-iu^{E}\psi|^{2}+||\psi|^{2}-\rho^{E}|^{2}\,dx.

More precisely, if

satisfies

,
\varepsilon\imaginary(\bar{\psi}\nabla\psi)=J
, then the two functionals above coincide. This may be easily checked by using the polar factorization method developed in
MR2481754
;
MR2885568
;
MR4440459
. Notice that the entropy functional (

) is reminiscent of the one used in
MR2208290

(

By using the above definitions, we check that the following identity holds

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}([\rho,\Lambda]|[r,U])=

\displaystyle E_{EK}-\int_{\Omega}J\cdot U+\rho\left(-\frac{1}{2}|U|^{2}+f^{\prime}(r)\right)\,dx+\int_{\Omega}p(r)\,dx.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\rho:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
be a non-zero function.
Then, the following inequalities hold:

\norm{\rho^{\frac{2+\alpha}{2}}}_{L^{2}}\lesssim\|\rho\|_{L^{1}}^{a}\|\nabla\beta(\rho)\|_{L^{2}}^{b},

(3.3)

where

•

If

and
b=\frac{1+\alpha}{3+\alpha}

•

If
d=2

a=\frac{1}{2}
and
b=\frac{1+\alpha}{2+\alpha}

•

If
d\geq 3
, then
a=\frac{2+\alpha}{d(1+\alpha)+2}
and
b=\frac{d(1+\alpha)}{d(1+\alpha)+2}

Proof.

\bullet\ \textbf{d=1}
.

By exploiting the inequality

\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}+\frac{3}{2}}\leq C\|\rho\|_{L^{1}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\partial_{x}\beta(\rho)\|_{L^{2}},

we infer that

\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\|\rho\|_{L^{1}}^{\frac{1}{\alpha+3}}\|\partial_{x}\beta(\rho)\|^{\frac{2}{\alpha+3}}_{L^{2}}.

Consequently,

\left(\int\rho^{2+\alpha}\,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}}^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}\norm{\rho}_{L^{1}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq C\|\rho\|_{L^{1}}^{\frac{\alpha+2}{\alpha+3}}\|\partial_{x}\beta(\rho)\|^{\frac{\alpha+1}{\alpha+3}}_{L^{2}}.

\bullet\ \textbf{d=2}
.

Let
p\geq 2
and let us define
q=\frac{2+\alpha}{2}p>1
(recall that we are assuming
\alpha>-1
). Then, by interpolation, we have

\|\rho\|_{L^{2+\alpha}}\leq\|\rho\|_{L^{1}}^{1-\theta}\|\rho\|_{L^{q}}^{\theta},

where
\theta=\frac{(1+\alpha)p}{2(q-1)}
. Notice that
\alpha\geq-1
and
p\geq 2
imply that
\theta\in[0,1]
. Moreover, by Gagliardo-Nirenberg we also have that

\|\rho^{1+\frac{\alpha}{2}}\|_{L^{p}}\lesssim\|\rho^{1+\frac{\alpha}{2}}\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{2}{p}}\|\nabla\beta(\rho)\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{p-2}{p}},

that is

\|\rho\|_{L^{q}}^{1+\frac{\alpha}{2}}\lesssim\|\rho\|_{L^{2+\alpha}}^{\frac{2+\alpha}{p}}\|\nabla\beta(\rho)\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{p-2}{p}}.

Consequently,

\|\rho\|_{L^{2+\alpha}}^{\frac{2+\alpha}{2\theta}}\lesssim\|\rho\|_{L^{1}}^{\frac{(1-\theta)(2+\alpha)}{2\theta}}\|\rho\|_{L^{2+\alpha}}^{\frac{2+\alpha}{p}}\|\nabla\beta(\rho)\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{p-2}{p}},

which in turn implies that

\|\rho\|_{L^{2+\alpha}}^{\frac{2+\alpha}{2\theta}-\frac{2+\alpha}{p}}\lesssim\|\rho\|_{L^{1}}^{\frac{(1-\theta)(2+\alpha)}{2\theta}}\|\nabla\beta(\rho)\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{p-2}{p}}.

We have

\frac{2+\alpha}{2\theta}-\frac{2+\alpha}{p}=\frac{(p-2)(2+\alpha)^{2}}{2p(1+\alpha)}.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\|\rho\|_{L^{2+\alpha}}^{\frac{2+\alpha}{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\rho\|_{L^{1}}^{\frac{(1-\theta)}{2\theta}\frac{(1+\alpha)p}{p-2}}\|\nabla\beta(\rho)\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2+\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\|\rho\|_{L^{1}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\nabla\beta(\rho)\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2+\alpha}}.

\bullet\ \textbf{d}\geq\textbf{3}
.

Sobolev:

\norm{\rho}_{L^{\frac{d(2+\alpha)}{d-2}}}^{1+\frac{\alpha}{2}}=\norm{\rho}^{1+\frac{\alpha}{2}}_{L^{2^{*}\left(1+\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)}}\lesssim\norm{\nabla\beta(\rho)}_{L^{2}}

(3.4)

Interpolation: let
\vartheta=\frac{d(1+\alpha)}{d(1+\alpha)+2}

\norm{\rho}_{L^{2+\alpha}}\leq\norm{\rho}^{1-\vartheta}_{L^{1}}\norm{\rho}^{\vartheta}_{L^{\frac{d(2+\alpha)}{d-2}}}

(3.5)

Then,

\displaystyle\norm{\rho}_{L^{2+\alpha}}^{\frac{2+\alpha}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\norm{\rho}_{L^{1}}^{\frac{(2+\alpha)(1-\vartheta)}{2}}\norm{\rho}_{L^{\frac{d(2+\alpha)}{d-2}}}^{\vartheta\frac{2+\alpha}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\norm{\rho}_{L^{1}}^{\frac{(2+\alpha)(1-\vartheta)}{2}}\norm{\nabla\beta(\rho)}_{L^{2}}^{\vartheta}

\displaystyle=\norm{\rho}_{L^{1}}^{\frac{2+\alpha}{d(1+\alpha)+2}}\norm{\nabla\beta(\rho)}_{L^{2}}^{\frac{d(1+\alpha)}{d(1+\alpha)+2}}.

∎
