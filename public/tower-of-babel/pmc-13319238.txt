Synthesis and multi-technique structural characterization of trimetallic oxide nanocomposites for environmental and medicinal applications
Authors: Sadaf Ilyas Awan, Wajid Rehman, Rida Zameer, Mona A. Alamri, Thoraya A. Farghaly, Muhammed Shakeel, Abdullah Y. A. Alzahrani, Farrukh Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to synthesize a trimetallic oxide nanocomposite (NC) consisting of copper oxide (CuO), tin dioxide (SnO2) and hematite (Fe2O3) employing a sol-gel technique. Subsequently, the synthesized nanocomposite was calcined in an oxygen-rich environment at 100, 300, 600, and 900 °C. In order to investigate the morphological, optical, as well as structural characteristics of resulting nanocomposites, fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy, thermo-gravimetric analysis (TGA), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), X-ray diffraction (XRD), energy dispersive X-ray (EDX) spectroscopy, and ultraviolet–visible (UV-Vis) spectroscopy were employed. As demonstrated by variations in SEM micrographs, FT-IR bands, and XRD diffraction patterns, these investigations confirmed the successful synthesis of the nanocomposites and emphasized the significant impact of calcination temperature on their morphological and structural characteristics. For the degradation of crystal violet (CV) dye, synthesized nanocomposites were further analyzed as photocatalysts where the sample calcined at 100 °C exhibited the highest degradation efficiency i.e., 90.95%. In addition, significant biological activity was shown by these nanocomposites demonstrating promising antioxidant as well as antibacterial characteristics, thereby revealing their potential multifunctional applications in biomedical and environment fields.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-49518-8.

Introduction

Owing to its ability to take advantage of the unique characteristics of materials at nanoscale, nanotechnology rapidly became a prominent research area in the early 21st century1. Nanoparticles refer to solid or dispersed particles that are 10 to 100 nanometers in size2. Nanocomposites are materials in which at least one constituent phase possesses dimensions within the nanometer scale (1 nm = 10⁻⁹ m)3. As a result, nanocomposites can exhibit a combination of, or distinctly enhanced, mechanical, electrochemical, electrical, catalytic, thermal, and optical properties compared to those of their individual constituent materials4. Nanocomposites are generally made from two or more diverse nano-oxides and are determined by the relative amount of each oxide component to decide their overall properties. Metal oxide (MO) nanocomposites (NCs) have gained much attention over the past few years as a result of possessing exceptional optical, electrical, thermal, photocatalytic, mechanical, adsorptive, and structural properties5. MO nanocomposites have been of tremendous potential in various areas like solar energy harvesting, photovoltaic devices, battery electrodes, gas sensing sensors, and fuel cell applications6. Owing to the ability of generating synergistic effects upon the integration of three oxides, ternary metal oxide systems have gained notable interest resulting a features like enhanced electrical conductivity, better thermal stability and improved catalytic activity in comparison to unary as well as binary systems, thus facilitating the fabrication of materials with application-specific functionalities7. Due to superior physicochemical properties over their monometallic and bimetallic counterparts, trimetallic oxide nanocomposites have drawn a lot of interest8.

Today, water pollution has become a significant global issue with organic dyes representing a severe risk to aquatic ecosystems9. A major global public health challenge impacting millions annually is bacterial infections. Although antibacterial drugs are frequently employed for their treatment, their indiscriminate and excessive usage has caused the rise of antibiotic-resistant microorganisms, further complicating disease management10. Free radicals, which come from a variety of chemical sources, are extremely unstable and have tendency to denature target molecules by abstracting electrons from nearby molecules to achieve stability. Human body constantly produces these extremely reactive species, which can harm short-lived molecules and important cellular components. Eliminating such free radicals can help reduce their damaging impacts11.

MO NCs have been used in environmental remediation to remove heavy metals from wastewater, degrade organic pollutants, and purify water and air systems12. Additionally, trimetallic oxide NCs have broad spectrum efficacy against both gram negative and gram positive bacterial strains13. Release of metal ions, production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and disturbance in microbial membranes collectively cause cellular damage and bacterial growth inhibition which leads to enhanced antibacterial efficiency14. These NCs exhibit high antioxidant activity in addition to antibacterial activity. Because transition metals are available in different oxidation states (e.g. Cu+ 2/Cu+, Fe3+/Fe2+), they can use electron transfer and redox reactions to capture free radicals like ABTS and DPPH15. This makes them appropriate candidates for biomedical applications like anti-inflammatory formulations, wound healing, and protective agents in oxidative stress-related disorders16. Additionally, trimetallic oxide NCs are frequently used for degrading organic dyes and pollutants under UV or visible light in photocatalysis17. Three metal oxides improve photocatalytic efficacy by reducing electron-hole recombination, promoting charge separation, and widening light absorption ranges18.

Various synthesis strategies have been formulated to tailor the size, morphology, and composition of these materials. The choice of the synthesis route significantly influences the phase purity, crystallinity, particle size, surface area, and finally the functional performance of the trimetallic oxide nanocomposite19. Combustion synthesis, microwave-assisted synthesis, and green synthesis by plant extracts or biopolymers are gaining prominence as green and energy-sparing techniques20. Among different techniques, the sol–gel route is most commonly used because it is simple, homogeneously molecular at the nanoscale, and involves low processing temperatures21. In this process, metal precursors (most commonly metal nitrates or alkoxides) are hydrolyzed and condensed with the help of a stabilizing agent to create a gel that is dried and calcined to obtain the oxide nanocomposite22.

Copper oxide (CuO) is a p-type semiconductor characterized by a narrow band gap ranging from 1.2 to 2.0 eV23. The photoconductive and photochemical characteristics of CuO make it an excellent candidate for constructing heterostructures24. CuO nanoparticles have garnered interest for their applications in electronics, optoelectronics, gas sensors, solar cells, magnetic storage, and nanoscale catalysts25. Owing to its exceptional electrochemical, optical, and electrical characteristics, Tin dioxide (SnO2) has drawn prominent research interest in past few decades and band gap of SnO2 is 3.67 eV26. It is extensively employed in optoelectronics and electronics, as well as biomedicine, catalysts, gas sensors, and batteries27. Band gap of Fe2O3 lies between 2.1 and 2.2 eV28. Unique catalytic, optical, and electrical properties of Fe2O3 enable its applications in catalysts, data storage materials, pigments, fine ceramics, sensors, and photoelectrochemical cells29.

The goal of this study was to synthesize a CuO-SnO2-Fe2O3 NC using a simple sol-gel technique. The final product was then calcined at various temperatures i.e., 100, 300, 600, and 900 °C. XRD, SEM, TGA, FT-IR, and EDX analyses were employed to analyze the morphological as well as structural variations induced by calcination. Thereafter, impacts of calcination temperature, catalyst dosage, and initial dye concentration were thoroughly investigated, as the NCs were assessed as photocatalysts for the degradation of crystal violet dye. Moreover, NCs’ antibacterial activity was evaluated against selected pathogenic bacteria to ascertain their potential for inhibiting microbial growth. To evaluate their capability of scavenging free radicals, their antioxidant activity was also examined, demonstrating potential applications in promoting biomedical and environmental functionality as well as in mitigating oxidative stress.

Materials and methods

Materials

All the experimental procedures were carried out in Pyrex Type (A) glassware. The chemical reagents such as copper (II) chloride hexahydrate (CuCl2·6H2O), Iron (II) chloride tetrahydrate (FeCl2.4H2O), stannous chloride dihydrate (SnCl2.2H2O), potassium persulfate, ethanol, and sodium hydroxide (NaOH) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich and used as received without further purification. Distilled water (DW) was utilized for the whole experimental process.

Synthesis of CuO-SnO2 -Fe2O3 nanocomposite

CuO-SnO2-Fe2O3 nanocomposite was synthesized using a modified sol-gel technique30. Initially, 30 mM stock solutions of copper(II) chloride dihydrate, stannous chloride dihydrate, and iron(III) chloride tetrahydrate were separately prepared. From each stock solution, 80 mL was measured and individually mixed with 20 mL of ethanol in separate beakers. The solutions were then stirred and heated at 50 °C for 10 min. The pH of each solution was gradually adjusted to 10 by the slow addition of a 0.5 mM sodium hydroxide solution, promoting hydrolysis and gel formation. After continuous stirring and heating for approximately 2 h, gelation was observed in all three solutions. To eliminate soluble by-products, produced gels were washed comprehensively with DW. Afterwards, individual gels were mixed in a single container, to which 100 mL of DW was added. To accomplish complete homogenization, combined gel system was heated for 5 h while being continuously stirred. In order to increase gel integrity, reaction mixture was then allowed to age for 24 h at room temperature31. To remove any remaining contaminants, gel was washed thrice with DW after aging. To achieve a solid precursor, purified composite was dried at 150 °C, which, to maintain its stability, was kept in an airtight container. To achieve crystalline trimetallic oxide NC, dried material was calcined 100, 300, 600, and 900 °C.

Bactericidal analysis

The Agar-well diffusion process was followed to evaluate the antibacterial activity of all the samples against B. cereus and E.coli where vancomycin (VA30) and gentamycin served as positive controls, respectively. To prepare nutrient agar plates, agar was dissolved in DIW and allowed to solidify at ambient temperature. Using a polystyrene tip, wells were created in agar, after spreading bacterial cultures across the plates and incubating overnight. By ultrasonically dispersing various amounts (30, 50, and 100 mg) in 5 mL of DIW, samples stock suspensions were prepared. From each suspension, a volume of 100 µL from each suspension was added to the respective wells. The antibacterial activity was estimate by By assessing the zones of inhibition (ZOI) in millimeters after incubation for 24 h at 37 °C.

Photocatalytic analysis

The photocatalytic activity of CuO-SnO2-Fe2O3 NCs calcined at 100, 300, 600, and 900 °C was assessed during the degradation of crystal violet (CV) dye. Using DW, a standard CV dye solution (15 ppm) was prepared. Thereafter, in a reaction vessel, 20 mg of each sample was added to 100 mL of dye solution and a 30 min stirring in dark allows the system to attained equilibrium. The photocatalytic experiments were carried out under simulated solar irradiation using a solar simulator equipped with a xenon arc lamp. The light intensity was adjusted to 100 mW cm− 2 to match standard AM 1.5G solar conditions. The emission spectral range of the light source was 300–800 nm, covering the UV–visible region. The light intensity was measured using a calibrated radiometer prior to each experiment. The distance between the lamp and the reaction vessel was maintained at 15 cm to ensure uniform irradiation. All experiments were performed under continuous magnetic stirring to maintain homogeneous suspension and consistent light exposure. Afterwards, using a double-beam UV-Vis spectrophotometer, changes in dye absorbance were monitored. The mineralization of dye was judged from the decrease in the absorbance maxima at a regular interval of time. Results and discussion

SEM analysis

To examine the surface morphology of the CuO-SnO2-Fe2O3 NC calcined at various temperatures (100, 300, 600, and 900 °C), at both low and high magnifications, SEM was applied. A strong dependence on calcination temperature was revealed by morphological characteristics. At low magnification (Fig. 1(a-d)), all CuO-SnO2-Fe2O3 NC samples showed agglomerated morphologies, which is a characteristic property of oxide nanomaterials arising from strong interparticle interactions and high surface energy. Figure 1(a) displays the sample calcined at 100 °C exhibiting irregular and loosely packed agglomerates with a broad size distribution, where smaller aggregates were excessively attached to larger clusters. Multiple cavities of different sizes were noticed, which arose due to the evaporation of residual solvents and the incomplete decomposition of organic species at this low calcination temperature. Microstructure evolved toward a more compact arrangement as the calcination temperature rose to 300 °C, shown in Fig. 1(b). Enhanced particle packing, and partial consolidation were noted, along with a discernible decrease in the size and number of cavities, even though agglomeration was still evident. Increased particle rearrangement and elimination of organic residues driven by enhanced thermal energy caused this variation. At 600 °C, (Fig. 1(c)) a noticeable morphological transformation took place, where microstructure was dominated by enhanced densification. Significant grain growth and sintering enabled by increased atomic diffusion at high temperatures were shown by the development of a dense and continuous background matrix as well as a sharp decrease in cavity density. Against this dense background, aggregates of various morphologies and sizes were noted to be attached or partially dispersed. Figure 1(d) reveals a thermodynamically stabilized as well as highly compact structure formed by further calcination at 900 °C. It was characterized by a dense background with aggregates as well as agglomerates of different dimensions and sizes. Although certain agglomerated characteristics remained because of strong particle fusion at increased temperature, cavities were minimal at this stage indicating significant grain coalescence, advanced sintering, and recrystallization processes.

A deeper insight into the temperature-driven microstructural evolution of CuO-SnO2-Fe2O3 NC was provided by high-magnification SEM images, shown in Fig. 2 (a-d). Figure 2(a) represents sample calcined at 100 °C revealing significant agglomeration with a highly irregular as well as loosely packed surface with numerous cavities. Due to insufficient thermal energy, smaller aggregates were often adhered to larger agglomerates representing weak interparticle bonding and incomplete condensation. Microstructure evolved toward a comparatively denser and more organized arrangement with calcination at 300 °C, shown in Fig. 2(b). Agglomeration continued but cavity sizes significantly decreased and morphological characteristics grew more uniform, tending toward near-spherical geometries. Onset of partial crystallization and increased surface diffusion at moderate thermal treatment caused this transition. An interconnected, network-like morphology developed, when temperature was risen to 600 °C (Fig. 2(c)), where complex geometrical configurations formed due to the continuous interlinking of agglomerates. Strong interfacial interactions among CuO, SnO2, and Fe2O3 phases were suggested by the presence of elongated and near-spherical aggregates while the number of cavities reduced further. At 900 °C (Fig. 2(d)), the most significant morphological transformation took place where smooth, elongated capsule-like structures adhered to one another dominated microstructure. Capsule-like morphology is due to sintering-driven process. At high temperature, particles coalescence lead to formation of rounded, capsule shaped grains as the material minimizes surface energy through sintering, rather than a phase transformation dictating shape37. These elongated features dominated overall morphology despite the presence of a dense agglomerated background. Advanced densification, increased phase diffusion, thermal stabilization, and extensive grain growth attained at the highest calcination temperature were all reflected in the noticeable decrease in cavity density.

HRTEM analysis

The high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) analysis provided significant insight into the structural characteristics and interfacial morphology of the synthesized CuO–SnO₂–Fe₂O₃ nanocomposite, as illustrated in Fig. 3(a&b). The HRTEM micrographs of the composite sample clearly demonstrate that all three crystalline resources are well-defined and have been successfully identified for each phase. Notably, the observed lattice fringes were consistent with the crystallographic planes associated with the monoclinic CuO (JCPDS card No. 48-1548), tetragonal SnO₂ (JCPDS card No. 41-1445) and rhombohedral Fe₂O₃ (JCPDS card No. 33–0664) respectively. Therefore, it is confirmed that CuO, SnO₂ and Fe₂O₃ co-exist as separate phases within the dimensions of the composite material. Moreover, there are clearly shown as well-interfaced hetero-structural interfaces between CuO, SnO₂ and Fe₂O₃ nanomaterials, which promote a strong level of interfacing contact among the three materials. Such highly integrated interfaces are beneficial for facilitating effective charge transfer between the heterojunction interfaces. This structural arrangement promotes the rapid separation and migration of charge carriers through the interfaces and improves the overall composite material’s functional performance.

EDX analysis

To thoroughly examine the purity and elemental make up of CuO-SnO2-Fe2O3 NCs as a function of calcination temperature, EDX analysis was conducted which is shown in Fig. 4(a-d). EDX spectrum of CuO-SnO2-Fe2O3 NCs calcined at 100 °C (Fig. 3(a)) validated the existence of Cu, Sn, Fe, and O. A moderate oxygen peak indicated partial oxidation at this early sol-gel stage while characteristic peaks formed at approximately 1.5, 8.5, and 8.9 keV for Cu, 3.4 keV for Sn, and 6.4 keV for Fe suggesting successful incorporation of the metallic constituents38. Residual organic species, chloride containing precursor salts, and environmental contamination contributed too minor signals of C, Cl, and Si suggesting that higher calcination temperatures were needed for oxide formation and crystallization. Figure 3(b) shows EDX spectrum of sample calcined at 300 °C that also confirmed the presence of Cu, Sn, Fe, and O, with clear peaks at ~ 0.9 and ~ 8 keV for Cu, ~ 3.4 keV for Sn, and ~ 6.4 keV for Fe. Residual Cl signals from the precursors showed that full oxidation and crystallinity had not yet been attained but the rising intensity of oxygen peak indicated progressive oxidation and the formation of CuO, SnO2, and Fe2O3 phases. Oxygen content was also inferred from XRD and FTIR analysis. Presence of metal-oxygen peaks in FTIR and phase identification in XRD support expected oxygen content. EDX spectrum of NC calcined at 600 °C, shown in Fig. 4(c), exhibited well defined peaks for Cu (~ 0.9 and ~ 0.8 keV), Sn (~ 3.4 keV), and Fe (~ 6.4 keV), indicating increased elemental definition because of enhanced crystallinity. With only traces Si and weak Cl peaks left, lowered impurity signals indicated efficient degradation of organic residues, even at 900 °C suggests chlorine is incorporated into stable structure that require very high temperatures to fully decompose or volatilize. Chlorine residue acts as recombination centers for photo-generated charge-carriers, reducing efficiency of the photocatalyst. It also alters surface chemistry potentially lowering the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) that are responsible for antibacterial action. while the prominent oxygen peak validated near-complete oxidation to the respective metal oxides. Finally, Fig. 4(d) illustrated the EDX spectrum of the nanocomposite calcined at 900 °C, where sharp and intense peaks for Cu (~ 0.9, ~ 8.0, and ~ 8.9 keV), Sn (~ 3.4 keV), and Fe (~ 6.4 and ~ 7.1 keV) demonstrated complete incorporation into their oxide phases. The very strong oxygen signal confirmed full oxidation to CuO, SnO₂, and Fe₂O₃, while the absence of carbon and silicon peaks indicated the complete removal of organic residues and high material purity; only weak Cl signals persisted from precursor remnants. At this temperature, the nanocomposite exhibited full crystallization, phase stability, and grain growth, in agreement with the SEM observations. The quantitative EDX results presented in Tables 1, 2, 3 and 4 confirm the presence of Cu, Sn, Fe, and O as the major constituents of the nanocomposite. The measured atomic percentages of Cu, Sn, and Fe are consistent with the intended stoichiometric composition within experimental uncertainty. The relatively small standard deviation in elemental percentages (if multiple measurements were taken) indicates compositional consistency across the analyzed regions. The minor signals of C, Cl, and Si are present in trace amounts and are attributed to surface contamination, residual precursor species, or contributions from the substrate. The absence of significant variation in elemental ratios suggests that no observable compositional gradients are present within the probed regions, supporting the formation of a homogeneous trimetallic oxide nanocomposite.

FT-IR analysis

The FTIR spectra of CuO-SnO₂-Fe₂O₃ NCs calcined at 100 °C, 300 °C, 600 °C, and 900 °C revealed pronounced vibrational features corresponding to metal–oxygen bonds and functional groups (Fig. 5). A broad O-H stretching band observed at ~ 3311 cm⁻¹, which was prominent at lower temperatures (100 °C and 300 °C), decreased at higher temperatures due to the loss of hydroxyl groups and adsorbed water39. The band at 1628 cm⁻¹ was attributed to H–O–H bending, reflecting residual moisture40. Bands appearing at 1356 cm⁻¹ and 1365 cm⁻¹ were assigned to C–O or O–C = O stretching of the organic precursors41. Band at 1122 cm⁻¹ ascribed to the M-OH vibrations42. At 925 cm− 1, band arose due to the O-M-O vibrations43. M-O-M vibrations led to the band at 897 cm− 144. Band at 841 cm− 1 was attributed to the M-O-C vibrations45. M-O vibrations, where M = Cu, Sn, and Fe, caused the band at 832 cm− 146. In infrared (IR) spectroscopy, the exact wavenumber of the metal–oxygen (M–O) stretching vibration depends primarily on the mass of the metal atom and the strength of the M–O bond. Generally, heavier metals and weaker bonds result in lower vibrational frequencies, whereas stronger bonds shift the absorption to higher wavenumbers.

Typically, Cu–O stretching vibrations are observed in the region of 500–600 cm⁻¹, while Fe–O vibrations appear around 550–650 cm⁻¹. In comparison, Sn–O stretching modes are commonly detected at 600–700 cm⁻¹. However, these spectral ranges often overlap due to similarities in bond environments, coordination geometry, and ligand effects, which can obscure clear assignment of individual metal–oxygen vibrations in complex systems. With increasing calcination temperature, particularly at 600 and 900 °C, the intensity of these bands enhanced revealing oxide formation and increased crystallinity. In general, progressive phase development and better structural ordering were indicated by narrower and sharper bands forming at higher calcination temperatures.

TGA analysis

A multi-step thermal decomposition behavior was revealed by the TGA curves of CuO-SnO2-Fe2O3 NCs calcined at 100, 300, 600, and 900 °C, as illustrated in Fig. 6. An initial weight loss of approximately 13.8% around 50 °C in the sample calcined at 100 °C (Fig. 6(a)) was ascribed to evaporation of water and trapped solvents which was followed by a significant weight loss between 220 and 700 °C because of the breakdown of chlorides and organic precursors47. The completion of metal oxide (CuO, SnO2, and Fe2O3) formation caused the curve to become nearly flat beyond 800 °C. This low weight loss in this region indicated the potential onset of sintering or phase stabilization and high thermal stability. Figure 6(b) reveals the TGA curve of the sample calcined at 300 °C suggesting multi-stage decomposition, with an initial 0.3–1.3% weight loss at approximately 50 °C. This was attributed to the evaporation of moisture, followed by the largest loss (~ 13%) between 200 and 700 °C because of the decomposition of gel residues, chlorides, and organics. Development of stable oxide phases and evaporation of volatiles led to the stabilization above 700 °C. While entire phase development was achieved between 700 and 900 °C, partial degradation with the start of oxide formation took place at this temperature. In the sample calcined at 600 °C (Fig. 6(c)) an initial weight loss of ~ 2.7% around 50 °C was caused that was attributed to moisture and trace solvents. The decomposition of precursors and residual organics, which resulted in the formation of CuO, SnO2, and Fe2O3, caused a more pronounced loss of ~ 5.9% between 200 and 500 °C. The completion of oxide phase formation and material’s thermal stabilization were confirmed by the curve that became constant beyond 650 °C. In contrast to the lower calcination temperatures, this trace validated full transformation into stable metal oxides within the 600–900 °C range. Finally, the 900 °C sample (Fig. 6(d) showed minimal total weight loss, with an initial 11.5% loss near 50 °C arising from residual solvents, absorbed water, and trace organics or chlorides, and no significant decomposition, thereafter, indicating prior thermal treatment. The weight loss near 50 °C is 11.5% attributed to surface-adsorbed moisture and hydroxyl groups. Metal oxides are hygroscopic and can absorb atmospheric moisture during storage prior to TGA measurement. This does not indicate structural decomposition. The curve flattened between 580 °C and 900 °C, reflecting structural stabilization, crystallization of the Cu–Sn–Fe oxides, and the development of thermally stable crystalline phases48. The decomposition stages were identified from the slope variations observed in the TGA curves. The major weight-loss regions were clearly distinguishable from changes in mass-loss rate at lower and intermediate temperature ranges. The initial weight loss below 150 °C is attributed to removal of physically adsorbed moisture and residual solvents. The second stage corresponds to decomposition of organic precursors and intermediate hydroxides, while above 450 °C the mass remains nearly constant, indicating complete oxide formation and thermal stability of the trimetallic system. The plateau region confirms the formation of thermally stable CuO–SnO2-Fe2O3 oxide phases.

Biological applications

Antibacterial activity

The enhanced antibacterial activity of the CuO–SnO2–Fe2O3 trimetallic oxide nanocomposite can be attributed to multiple synergistic mechanisms rather than a single pathway. One of the primary mechanisms involves the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), including superoxide radicals (O2-.), hydroxyl radicals (•OH), and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), on the nanocomposite surface. The heterojunction formation between CuO, SnO2, and Fe2O3 facilitates charge separation, promoting electron–hole pair stabilization and enhancing ROS production. These reactive species induce oxidative stress in bacterial cells, leading to lipid peroxidation, protein denaturation, and DNA damage. In addition to ROS-mediated toxicity, the release of Cu2+ and Fe3+ ions contribute to antibacterial action. These metal ions interact with negatively charged bacterial cell membranes, increasing membrane permeability and disrupting cellular integrity. The strong electrostatic interaction between the positively charged metal ions and bacterial cell walls further enhances membrane damage. Moreover, the nanoscale particle size increases surface area, promoting direct surface contact with bacterial cells and facilitating mechanical disruption. Therefore, the antibacterial activity of the trimetallic nanocomposite is governed by a synergistic combination of ROS generation, metal ion release, and direct surface interaction.

The antibacterial efficacy of CuO–SnO2–Fe2O3 nanocomposites, calcined at 100 °C, 300 °C, 600 °C, and 900 °C, was evaluated against both Gram-positive (Bacillus cereus) and Gram-negative (Escherichia coli) bacteria, with vancomycin and gentamycin serving as the respective positive controls, as summarized in Fig. 8. All samples produced measurable inhibition zones, confirming their antibacterial activity. Against Bacillus cereus, the 100 °C-calcined sample exhibited a ZOI of 20 mm, attributed to its surface area and the availability of active sites, while the 300 °C sample showed a slightly reduced ZOI of 19 mm, likely due to partial sintering. The 600 °C sample demonstrated a ZOI of 24 mm, which may be linked to enhanced crystallinity and increased generation of ROS and the 900 °C sample exhibited a ZOI of 21 mm, indicating maintained structural integrity and effective ROS activity. Vancomycin, used as the control, displayed a ZOI of 25 mm, confirming its high antibacterial efficacy. Against Escherichia coli, the 100 °C and 300 °C samples showed ZOIs of 13 mm and 11 mm, respectively, reflecting lower activity due to incomplete oxide formation, while the 600 °C sample exhibited a ZOI of 19 mm, likely resulting from optimal crystallinity and enhanced ROS production. The 900 °C sample showed a slightly reduced ZOI of 14 mm, possibly due to nanoparticle agglomeration. Gentamycin, serving as the positive control, exhibited a ZOI of 20 mm. Overall, the results indicated temperature-dependent antibacterial activity of the CuO–SnO2–Fe2O3 nanocomposites, with the 600 °C sample demonstrating the highest observed effect. Antibacterial activity depends on crystallinity, ROS generation, ion release, and surface area. At 600 °C, optimal crystallinity and moderate defect density enhance ROS production and ion release. At 900 °C, severe sintering reduces surface area and active sites, lowering antibacterial efficiency.

Antioxidant activity

The antioxidant activity of the CuO–SnO2–Fe2O3 nanocomposite can be explained by its ability to donate electrons or hydrogen atoms to neutralize free radicals. The presence of multiple transition metal ions enables redox cycling between different oxidation states (Cu2+/Cu+ and Fe3+/Fe2+) facilitating electron transfer reactions. This redox behavior enhances radical scavenging efficiency in DPPH or similar assays. Furthermore, oxygen vacancies and surface defects present in the mixed oxide system act as active sites for radical adsorption and stabilization. The synergistic electronic interaction among CuO, SnO2, and Fe2O3 improves charge mobility, thereby increasing the radical quenching capability of composite material. To evaluate the antioxidant characteristics of all synthesized samples, ABTS•+ assay was employed where ascorbic acid served as a standard reference. To ascertain their radical scavenging capacities, different concentrations of ABTS•+ (5, 10, 15, 30, 60, and 100 µg/mL) were added into solutions containing prepared samples and ascorbic acid, summarized in Table 5 and Table S1-S4. Antioxidant activity of all samples increased with concentration suggesting that effective quenching of ABTS•+ radical required higher amounts of samples, as supported by observed findings49. Higher availability of NPs capable of effectively neutralizing the ABTS•+ free radicals led to an increased scavenging activity with an increase in the proportion of NPs50. At 100 °C, the activity ranged from 37.38 to 88.16%, which decreased to 31.63–74.60% at 300 °C, 25.88–61.03% at 600 °C, and 20.54–48.44% at 900 °C, indicating a gradual decrease as the calcination temperature increased. These findings could be assigned to the increase in particle size with rising calcination temperature51. Corresponding particle growth may account for this trend. Number of accessible active sites was maximized by the significant porosity, small particle size, and high specific surface area of NC at a low temperature of 100 °C. In comparison, larger particles were produced with dense, low porosity structures and lower specific area when calcination at 900 °C induced sintering. Consequently, the number of accessible active sites significantly decreased because majority became buried, which was reflected in the observed activity decline. The quantity of an antioxidant required to neutralize half of the ABTS•+ is referred to as IC50 value52. CuO-SnO2-Fe2O3 NC calcined at 100 °C depicted an IC50 value of 11.66 µg/mL. The corresponding IC50 values were 30.17, 56.94, and 95.22 µg/mL for the NCs calcined from 300 to 900 °C, respectively. The sample with smallest IC50 value is considered to be the most active one53. Accordingly, the highest antioxidant activity among all the examined samples was revealed by the NC calcined at 100 °C. A measure of the consistency of results, with lower values signifying higher precision, is termed standard deviation54. For CuO-SnO2-Fe2O3 NCs calcined at 100, 300, 600, and 900 °C, standard deviation values were 1.14, 0.96, 0.79, and 0.62, respectively, where the sample calcined at 900 °C showing the most consisted results. The square of the standard deviation, which provides a measure of dispersion data, is called variance52. CuO-SnO2-Fe2O3 NCs calcined at 100 °C exhibited a variance of 1.30 while the values for the samples calcined at 300, 600, and 900 °C were 0.93, 0.62, and 0.39, respectively indicating that the most consistent antioxidant activity was shown by the sample calcined at 900 °C.

Photocatalytic Activity

To evaluate the Photocatalytic Activity of CuO-SnO2-Fe2O3 NCs calcined at 100, 300, 600, and 900 °C, CV was used as the test dye. With the passage of time, decolorization of the reaction mixture enhanced slowly. A double beam spectrophotometer was used to perform UV-Vis analysis. A significant decrease in absorbance at 590 nm, corresponding to absorption maximum was observed suggest the modification of the chromophore responsible for light absorption at this wavelength as shown in Fig. 9. A sharp decrease in absorbance maxima was noted within the first 15 min, which was most prominent in the sample calcined at 100 °C.

For all samples, the rate of reduction declined gradually thereafter. Overall, the decline in the absorbance peak remained most prominent in the sample calcined at 100 °C. It was observed that the samples calcined at 300 and 600 °C exhibited nearly comparable reductions during the initial 30 min; however, their behavior diverged thereafter. The sample calcined at 300 °C showed a more pronounced reduction compared to the 600 °C sample, which can be attributed to its higher surface area.

The sample calcined at 100 °C exhibited high surface area and abundant defect sites, resulting in superior photocatalytic and antioxidant activity due to enhanced reactive oxygen species generation and surface reactivity. At 600 °C, optimal crystallinity with balanced defect concentration was achieved, leading to the highest antibacterial activity through improved charge transfer and effective ROS-mediated bacterial inhibition. In contrast, the 900 °C sample showed severe sintering and grain growth, causing reduced surface area and diminished overall performance. These results confirm that structural evolution with temperature directly governs the observed physicochemical and biological properties.

Effect of pH on percentage degradation

One of the important factors which affect the photocatalytic reactions is pH of solution as CV is a cationic dye (C25H30N3Cl) which belongs to triphenylmethanes family57. The pH of different CV solution was adjusted 5, 6, 7, 8 and 9 using HCl and NaOH solutions (0.1 M each) to investigate the impact of pH on the progress photocatalytic reaction (Fig. 12). The degradation efficiency of prepared sample was evaluated over a pH range of 5 to 9, yielding corresponding degradation percentages of 62.45%, 68.73%, 77.60%, 83.01%, and 90.95%. These findings showed that under acidic conditions, degradation efficiency was comparatively low which progressively increased with pH and peaked at 9, which corresponds to an alkaline medium. Due to the protonation of surface hydroxyl groups, CuO, SnO2, and Fe2O3 own positively charged surfaces in acidic conditions. Under similar conditions, CV also predominantly exists its cationic form (CV+). As a result, the adsorption of CV onto the catalyst was hindered due to the electrostatic repulsion between the positively charged NC surface and the cationic dye molecules, which ultimately slowed down the photocatalytic reaction58. Furthermore, the limited availability of hydroxide ions restricted the production of hydroxyl radicals which also contributed to the decreased degradation rates in acidic conditions. On the other hand, photocatalytic degradation increased significantly under alkaline conditions, and, owing to multiple factors, maximum efficacy was achieved around pH 9. Surfaces of CuO, SnO2, and Fe2O3 undergo deprotonation at alkaline pH and attain negative charges. At the same time, nucleophilic attack by hydroxyl ions causes CV to partially transform into a carbinol (leuco) form (CV-OH) while remaining in its cationic form (CV+). Increased adsorption is facilitated by a significant fraction of CV that keeps its positive charge despite partial transformation, enabling strong electrostatic attraction between negatively charged NC surface and dye molecules59. Moreover, the conjugated chromophore responsible for visible light absorption was weakened by the increased production of highly reactive hydroxyl radicals (•OH) in an alkaline medium, together with the formation of leuco form. Consequently, these combined effects facilitated the degradation of CV, making alkaline pH the most favorable condition for photocatalytic activity in the present system.

Photocatalytic degradation mechanism

Proposed photocatalytic mechanism for crystal violet degradation over the CuO–SnO₂–Fe₂O₃ ternary heterojunction under visible-light irradiation as been presented in scheme 2. An important factor in the CuO–SnO₂–Fe₂O₃ ternary composite’s photocatalytic ability to degrade crystal violet has been attributed to the generation of an effective heterojunction, promoting charge separation and the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS). When illuminated, electrons from CuO, Fe₂O₃, and SnO₂ will be excited and moved from the valence band to the conduction band, forming e–/h+ pairs in each of the respective materials. Due to the optimal alignment of the bands (i.e. favorable band alignment), the electrons will migrate towards the conduction band of SnO₂ while the h+ will remain in the valence bands of CuO and Fe₂O₃, thus minimizing charge recombination. The accumulated electrons will reduce dissolved oxygen (O₂) molecules producing superoxide (O₂•⁻), while the h+ oxidize water (H₂O) or surface OH (hydroxyl) groups, producing hydroxyl (•OH) radicals. These two highly reactive radicals then react with and degrade the crystal violet molecule by a process of oxidation, and through multiple oxidation reactions, the crystal violet molecules will finally be completely degraded into carbon dioxide (CO₂), water (H₂O) and other harmless products. The synergistic combination of CuO, SnO₂, and Fe₂O₃; therefore, results in increased light absorption, increased charge carrier separation, and increased ROS generation leading to enhanced photocatalytic activity.

Recycling of catalyst

The sustainability of a catalyst in a multiple step process are vital for enhanced performance during photochemical reaction. The reusability of CuO-SnO2-Fe2O3 catalyst calcined at 100 °C was assessed over the course of five successive reuse cycles, which revealed a slow decrease in degradation efficiency from 90.95% to 61.98% up to 5th cycle (Fig. 15). The initial high activity (90.95%) was might be due to cause by the optimal conditions that maximized the availability of active sites, whereas the progressive decrease in efficiency that followed could have been caused by early deactivation, surface contamination, or minor structural variations12. Photocatalysts degrading efficiency notably decreased due to the mass loading of catalyst and surface saturation during recycling process64. Surface contamination and reduction in accessible binding cites due to catalyst deactivation caused by structural variations, agglomeration across several cycles, or leaching of active species could be the cause of decline in catalytic activity upon reuse65. Conclusions

In conclusion, CuO-SnO₂-Fe₂O₃ NCs were successfully synthesized via a sol-gel method, which enabled precise control over particle size and morphology. The NCs were subsequently calcined at 100, 300, 600, and 900 °C to evaluate the effect of temperature on their structural and functional properties. Compared to conventional methods, the sol-gel approach proved to be simpler, faster, and more efficient for the preparation of these nanocomposites. Physicochemical analyses shown that elevating calcination temperature significantly influenced crystallinity, particle size, and morphology. This structural evolution was directly correlated with the photocatalytic performance, as higher calcination temperatures reduced surface area and consequently decreased the efficiency of CV dye degradation. Optimization studies further indicated that the pH, catalyst dosage, and initial dye concentration critically affected photocatalytic activity. The sample calcined at 100 °C exhibited the highest antioxidant activity, attributed to its smaller particle size, larger specific surface area, and enhanced porosity In contrast, antibacterial activity showed differences across the samples, with the nanocomposite calcined at 600 °C demonstrating the most pronounced effect, likely due to improved crystallinity and increased generation of reactive oxygen species. The results of this study highlight the strong dependence of the physicochemical and functional properties of CuO-SnO₂-Fe₂O₃ NCs on calcination temperature and underscore their potential for multifunctional applications in environmental remediation and biomedical fields.
