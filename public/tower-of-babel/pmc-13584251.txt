Beyond antibiotic duration in acute cholangitis: system-level determinants after biliary drainage
Authors: Linda Pasta, Fabrizio Bronte, Emma Aragona
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe COBRA trial addresses whether antibiotic exposure can be safely reduced after adequate biliary drainage in acute cholangitis. We argue that antibiotic duration should be interpreted within the broader care pathway in which treatment is delivered, because source control, device management, host susceptibility, local microbiological ecology, and reliability of care processes may modify clinical outcomes.Main bodyDrawing on the Medical Research Council framework for complex interventions, we propose a system-level interpretation that complements, rather than replaces, the randomized comparison of antibiotic duration. A 24-month single-center quasi-experimental study in a gastroenterology unit, recently accepted for publication, provides hypothesis-generating support for this perspective: implementation of a standardized and traceable work-support intervention was associated with reductions in healthcare-associated infections, ward microbial pressure, and clinically expressed microbial burden. These findings do not establish causality or determine optimal antibiotic duration in acute cholangitis. To make care-pathway variability more explicit in future studies, we propose a Bundle Deviation Score based on six domains: timely diagnosis, timing of biliary drainage, adequacy of drainage and source control, antimicrobial strategy, device management, and standardized high-contact care. This contextual process measure could complement patient-centered outcome frameworks such as Desirability of Outcome Ranking by distinguishing treatment effects from variability in how care is delivered.ConclusionsThe COBRA trial provides important evidence for antimicrobial stewardship after biliary drainage. Its interpretation and transferability may be strengthened by measuring the reliability of the broader care pathway alongside antibiotic duration. Prospective validation is required before the proposed Bundle Deviation Score can be used for clinical or research applications.Keywords: Acute cholangitis, Antimicrobial stewardship, Antibiotic duration, Biliary drainage, Complex interventions, Care-pathway reliability

Dear Editor,

The main challenge in acute cholangitis concerns not only the duration of antibiotic therapy but also the definition and control of the clinical pathway within which antibiotic decisions are made.

From this perspective, the COBRA trial by Overdevest and colleagues addresses an important and timely question for antimicrobial stewardship by evaluating whether ultra-short antibiotic therapy is non-inferior to a longer course after adequate biliary drainage [1]. Reducing unnecessary antibiotic exposure is clinically relevant, particularly in the context of antimicrobial resistance, drug-related adverse events, and the need for more sustainable infection management.

However, antibiotic duration should not be interpreted in isolation from the broader system of care. According to the UK Medical Research Council framework for complex interventions, outcomes in multifactorial conditions arise from the interaction of multiple components rather than from individual variables considered separately [2]. Acute cholangitis is a clear example of this complexity: diagnosis, timing of intervention, adequacy of biliary drainage, antimicrobial choice, source control, device management, host susceptibility, and local microbiological ecology may all influence clinical trajectories.

The COBRA trial appropriately focuses on a defined intervention after adequate endoscopic drainage. This design is valuable because it isolates a specific antimicrobial-stewardship question under controlled assumptions. In real-world clinical practice, however, “adequate drainage” and “short antibiotic duration” are embedded within broader care processes. Variability in these processes may contribute to differences in outcomes across patients and centers. For this reason, interpretation of antibiotic duration may benefit from a system-level framework that explicitly considers the conditions under which a short course is most likely to be safe and effective.

Our interest in this topic is supported by a 24-month single-center quasi-experimental study, recently accepted for publication, conducted in a Gastroenterology unit, in which a robotic system for standardized bedside hygiene, surveillance and prevention was used as a digital work-support intervention designed to support, standardize and trace repeated high-contact bedside care [3]. In that study, reductions in healthcare-associated infections were observed together with reductions in ward microbial pressure and clinically expressed microbial burden. These findings should not be overinterpreted as direct evidence for the management of acute cholangitis or as proof of causality in the context of the COBRA trial. Rather, they provide observational support for the broader hypothesis that microbial burden, consistency of care processes, and ward-level transmission dynamics may influence infectious outcomes and therefore the clinical context in which antimicrobial-duration strategies are implemented.

The relevance of the Gastroenterology study lies not only in the observed outcomes but also in the operational characteristics of the intervention. The robotic system was designed to improve the reliability and traceability of repeated high-contact bedside care and to structure routine bedside activities, including hygiene, oral care, mobilization, repositioning, elimination assistance, nutritional support and device-related assistance [3]. Monitoring, operational feedback, and reduction of care variability are conceptually aligned with the multimodal implementation approach described in WHO Core Component 5, which emphasizes the coordinated use of system change, education and training, monitoring and feedback, reminders and communication, and a supportive safety culture [4]. This alignment concerns implementation principles and should not be interpreted as validation of a specific technology by WHO. The scientific question is therefore broader than the performance of a particular device: it concerns whether making care processes more reliable, observable, and reproducible can modify the ecological conditions in which infections emerge and antimicrobial decisions are made.

This distinction is important. The study, accepted for publication, is single-center and quasi-experimental, and its findings should be considered hypothesis-generating. It does not establish that system-level interventions determine the optimal duration of antibiotic therapy after biliary drainage. However, it suggests that infectious outcomes may be shaped not only by pathogen identity or antibiotic duration, but also by the intensity of microbial circulation, the reliability of care processes, and the ability of the healthcare system to sustain consistent IPC practices over time.

In patients with biliary infections, this aspect may be particularly relevant. Obstruction, biliary drainage, devices, colonization, and persistence of the infectious reservoir may interfere with a simple relationship between antibiotic duration and clinical outcome. Therefore, the success of a short antibiotic course does not depend exclusively on the number of treatment days, but also on achieving effective source control, timely intervention, appropriate antimicrobial exposure, device management, and reliable infection-prevention practices.

The DOOR—Desirability of Outcome Ranking—framework may be particularly useful in this context [5]. DOOR/RADAR approaches were developed to evaluate antibiotic strategies by ranking overall patient outcomes while considering benefits and harms together with antibiotic exposure. This is conceptually aligned with the question raised by COBRA: the most desirable outcome is not simply clinical cure, but clinical cure achieved with the least unnecessary antibiotic exposure and without avoidable complications. In acute cholangitis, a DOOR-based approach could integrate competing outcomes such as clinical resolution, recurrence, adverse events, need for re-intervention, microbiological complications, length of stay, and antibiotic duration. Importantly, such a framework avoids treating fewer antibiotic days as intrinsically beneficial when a shorter course is accompanied by recurrence, additional procedures, or other clinically meaningful harms. Conversely, when clinical outcomes are equivalent, reduced antibiotic exposure becomes a genuine component of desirability. This patient-centered hierarchy may therefore reflect the clinical value of ultra-short therapy more completely than treatment duration considered as an isolated endpoint.

From this perspective, we propose that future studies of acute cholangitis consider process-based measures capable of describing deviations from the expected care pathway. One possible approach is a Bundle Deviation Score (BDS), defined as the cumulative number of deviations from predefined components of optimal care. These six domains could include the following: timely diagnosis; appropriate timing of biliary drainage; adequacy of biliary drainage and source control; antimicrobial strategy, including appropriate empirical therapy and microbiology-informed de-escalation when feasible; device management; and standardized high-contact care. Each domain could be coded as achieved or not achieved, generating a simple measure of pathway reliability. The value of such a measure would not lie in assigning equal biological importance to every component, but in making otherwise hidden process heterogeneity visible and analysable across patients and centers.

The proposed BDS should be regarded as a conceptual, hypothesis-generating metric rather than a validated score. Its purpose would not be to replace trial endpoints or diminish the value of randomized designs such as COBRA. Rather, it could describe the system context in which antibiotic-duration strategies are implemented and permit prespecified sensitivity or stratified analyses. A patient with complete and timely source control, low process deviation, and a stable clinical response may differ substantially from a patient with delayed drainage, persistent device-related contamination, incomplete microbiological reassessment, or delayed de-escalation, even when both nominally receive the same antibiotic duration. Prospective validation would be required to determine component definitions, weighting, reproducibility, and whether the score adds information beyond established clinical severity measures.

A similar framework could also integrate DOOR-based outcomes. DOOR describes the desirability of patient-level outcomes, whereas the BDS could describe the reliability of the care pathway that led to those outcomes. Used together, they could help future trials distinguish whether outcome differences are attributable mainly to antibiotic duration or to variability in source control, procedural timing, device management, microbiological reassessment, and infection prevention. This distinction is particularly relevant in pragmatic or multicenter studies, where real-world process variability is inevitable and may interact with treatment effects. In analytical terms, DOOR would remain an outcome framework, while BDS would function as a contextual process measure; conflating the two would weaken rather than strengthen inference. Their complementary use could therefore improve both interpretability and transferability without changing the randomized comparison itself.

This commentary has important limitations. First, the system-level interpretation proposed here is not derived from the results of the COBRA trial, which remain the appropriate evidence base for the specific intervention tested. Second, the quasi-experimental study cited in this commentary was conducted in a single Gastroenterology unit and was not designed to evaluate antibiotic duration in acute cholangitis. Third, the proposed BDS has not yet been prospectively validated and should not be used for clinical decision-making without further study. Fourth, the relationship between ward microbial pressure, biliary-infection ecology, and individual patient outcomes remains inferential and requires multicenter validation. Finally, the conceptual alignment between multimodal IPC principles and the operational features of the work-support intervention does not establish that any observed effect is attributable to a single technological component; the intervention is intrinsically multicomponent, and its mechanisms should be evaluated accordingly.

In conclusion, the COBRA trial represents an important contribution to antimicrobial stewardship in acute cholangitis by addressing whether antibiotic exposure can be safely reduced after adequate biliary drainage. We suggest that its implications can be further strengthened by considering the broader care system within which antibiotic decisions are made. The 24-month Gastroenterology study, now accepted for publication, evaluated a standardized and traceable robotic work-support intervention and supports the hypothesis that multimodal IPC systems, when implemented through coordinated monitoring, feedback, workflow standardization, and real-time analytics, may contribute to lower ward microbial pressure and more reliable care pathways. The relevance of that observation is conceptual rather than confirmatory for cholangitis. Future studies could therefore benefit from integrating patient-centered outcome frameworks such as DOOR with process-based measures such as BDS, while preserving source control and clinical response as central determinants of treatment success. Such an approach may help clarify not only whether shorter antibiotic therapy works, but under which care conditions it is safest, most effective, and most transferable to routine practice (Fig. 1).
