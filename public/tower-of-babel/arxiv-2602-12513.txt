An LP-Based Approach for Bilinear Saddle Point Problem with Instance-dependent Guarantee and Noisy Feedback
Jiashuo Jiang † \text{Jiashuo Jiang}^{{\dagger}}, Mengxiao Zhang ‡ \text{Mengxiao Zhang}^{{\ddagger}}

Abstract
In this work, we study the sample complexity of obtaining a Nash equilibrium (NE) estimate in two-player zero-sum matrix games with noisy feedback. Specifically, we propose a novel algorithm that repeatedly solves linear programs (LPs) to obtain an NE estimate with bias at most ε \varepsilon with a sample complexity of 𝒪 ⁡ ( m 1 ​ m 2 ε ​ min ⁡ { δ 2 , σ 0 2 , σ 3 } ​ log ⁡ m 1 ​ m 2 ε ) \mathcal{O}\left(\frac{m_{1}m_{2}}{\varepsilon\min\{\delta^{2},\sigma_{0}^{2},\sigma^{3}\}}\log\frac{m_{1}m_{2}}{\varepsilon}\right) for general m 1 × m 2 m_{1}\times m_{2} game matrices, where σ \sigma , σ 0 \sigma_{0} , δ \delta are some problem-dependent constants. To our knowledge, this is the first instance-dependent sample complexity bound for finding an NE estimate with ε \varepsilon bias in general-dimension matrix games with noisy feedback and potentially non-unique equilibria. Our algorithm builds on recent advances in online resource allocation and operates in two stages: (1) identifying the support set of an NE, and (2) computing the unique NE restricted to this support. Both stages rely on a careful analysis of LP solutions derived from noisy samples.

Introduction

Minimax optimization
(
54
)
is a fundamental problem in machine learning and game theory, with applications including adversarial training
(
25
)
and robust optimization
(
48
)
. A canonical example of a minimax problem is the two-player zero-sum matrix game, also known as the bilinear saddle-point problem, defined as
\min_{\bm{x}}\max_{\bm{y}}\bm{x}^{\top}A\bm{y}
, where
A
is the payoff matrix, and
(\bm{x},\bm{y})
are the strategies of the two players lying within the simplex. The goal is to compute a
Nash Equilibrium (NE)
, a strategy pair in which neither player can improve their expected payoff by deviating from their strategy.

When the payoff matrix
A
is fully known, computing an NE reduces to solving a pair of primal and dual linear programs or an equivalent saddle-point formulation. This full-information setting is polynomial-time solvable and well understood: one can compute the game value and an equilibrium strategy profile by solving an LP. When the game is of large scale, iterative approaches become essential to ensure an efficient computation of an NE. A typical approach is via regret minimization, where both players employ no-regret learning algorithms to sequentially update their strategies. Specifically,
22
shows that if two players adopts algorithms such as Hedge or Online Gradient Descent (OGD), the average iterate converges to an
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{T})
-approximate NE, where
T
is the total number of iterates. Later, there is a surge of works
(
16
,
49
,
53
)
showing that with an optimistic variant of these algorithms, the convergence rate can be improved to
\mathcal{O}(1/T)
.

Compared to the full information setting, a much harder learning environment is when each player picks an action and only receives a
noisy
observation of
A
on the
chosen action pair
. This indeed includes many real-world applications which we discuss as follows.

Application 1: Dueling bandit and preference learning

Dueling bandits
(
61
)
are a class of online learning problems that naturally form a two-player zero-sum game, in which the expected utility matrix can be formed as
A\in[0,1]^{m_{1}\times m_{2}}
with
m_{1}=m_{2}
. Each time, the learner selects two actions
i,j\in[m_{1}]
to duel and observes a binary outcome indicating which action is preferred in that comparison. Specifically, the probability of action
i
winning over action
j
is represented by an unknown payoff matrix entry
A_{ij}
, and binary feedback is stochastic under this underlying preference.

Dueling bandits in fact appear in many diverse real-world applications. For instance, in clinical trials, two treatments can be compared by presenting subsets of patients with either treatment and observing binary preference feedback, efficiently determining the superior option with minimal patient exposure
(
52
)
. In recommendation systems, users presented with pairs of products or movies provide binary preference feedback, enabling quick convergence to preferred items with fewer comparative tests
(
65
)
. Similarly, search engine rankings optimized via dueling comparisons can efficiently identify preferable result rankings from noisy user click data. Another important application is in online A/B testing
(
46
,
47
)
with limited feedback, commonly used in e-commerce platforms. Instead of measuring absolute performance of individual variants, the platform compares two website layouts, pricing schemes, or advertisement creatives and receive only a binary signal. This dueling setup allows for more sample-efficient learning and avoids assumptions on cardinal rewards, making it suitable for high-traffic, real-time systems with noisy preference data.
A more recent and significant example of dueling bandits is Reinforcement Learning with Human Feedback (RLHF), widely used in fine-tuning large language models, where human evaluators indicate preferences between two model-generated outputs. These pairwise comparisons form the noisy binary feedback and guide the model to learn a policy that aligns with human judgment
(
45
)
.

Application 2: Market making in financial markets

Financial market making provides another significant application for two-player zero-sum games under noisy and bandit feedback
(
56
,
55
)
. Market making involves two opposing players: the market maker, who provides liquidity by quoting bid and ask prices, and traders who seek to exploit profitable trades by selecting optimal trade execution strategies. Each player’s actions lead to uncertain outcomes influenced by stochastic price movements, liquidity variations, and unpredictable external events. The market maker aims to minimize losses and maintain profitability, while traders strive to maximize their gains through informed or strategic trading. Learning optimal strategies through limited, noisy observations is essential for achieving competitive advantages in these adversarial financial interactions.

Application 3: Pricing and bidding competition in revenue management

Zero-sum matrix games are useful tools in revenue management for modeling competitive or adversarial decision-making situations. For example, suppose there are two hotels, located in the same area, competing for the same group of customers. Each hotel wants to set a room price from a set of pricing strategies in order to compete with the other hotel’s pricing strategy. This is a classic case of strategic decision-making and can be modeled as a matrix game (see for example
(
8
,
18
)
), where each entry of the game matrix represents the possible outcome based on the pricing choices of both hotels. The outcome can be stochastic due to uncertain customer demand, which introduces noise when we make observations over matrix entries. Such a matrix game formulation helps hotels optimize strategically over pricing in a competitive market.

Application 4: Attacker-defender games in blockchain security

Beyond dueling bandits, blockchain security provides another compelling domain for learning Nash equilibria under noisy, bandit feedback. Blockchain networks can often be modeled as two-player zero-sum games between attackers, aiming to disrupt or compromise the system, and defenders, striving to maintain the integrity and security of the network. Actions taken by attackers, such as launching double-spending attacks, mining pool attacks, or manipulating consensus mechanisms, result in probabilistic outcomes influenced by factors like network latency, node honesty, and random block validation processes. Defenders, conversely, select protective strategies such as dynamic difficulty adjustments, node validation schemes, or network partitioning techniques, which similarly yield uncertain results due to inherent system stochasticity and evolving adversarial capabilities. Learning effective strategies under such noisy bandit feedback, where only outcomes of specific attacks or defenses are observable, is crucial for robust blockchain operation and resilience against sophisticated adversaries.

Despite its wide applications, less is known about learning NE under this more challenging bandit and noisy feedback environment.

Main Results and Contributions

Inspired by the recent advance of online resource allocation (e.g.

3

3

Specifically, in
Section

3

N/m

Concretely, at each step, we select an entry from the current support and compare the objective value of the empirical LP in two cases: one where the strategy remains unrestricted and one where the selected entry is removed. If the two objective values are sufficiently close, this indicates that the selected entry can be safely removed while still ensuring the existence of an NE. After obtaining the support of an NE, we show in

Other Related Works

The literature on learning in game is broad. There is a line of works considering the sample complexity of finding NE for matrix games. When the observations are deterministic,
40
proposed an inefficient algorithm that identifies all Nash Equilibria by querying

15

\mathcal{O}(n)
samples. This was subsequently improved by
14
, who introduced a randomized algorithm that further reduced the runtime complexity while maintaining the same sample complexity.

When the observations are noisy,
63
proposed a lower-upper-confidence-bound (LUCB)-based algorithm
(
34
)
for identifying PSNE with probability at least
1-\epsilon

\mathcal{O}(\log(1/\epsilon))
.
41

,
42

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\min\{\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}},\max\{\frac{1}{\Delta_{\min}^{2}},\frac{c}{\varepsilon^{2}D^{2}}\}\}\cdot\log(1/\epsilon))

\varepsilon

\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}=\Delta_{m_{1}}}\max_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}=\Delta_{m_{2}}}\bm{x}^{\top}A\bm{y},

Conclusion

In this paper, we develop new algorithms for computing Nash equilibrium (NE) estimates in matrix games with noisy bandit feedback. Our approach is inspired by the linear programming (LP) formulations for computing primal and dual optimal solutions and consists of two main components. First, we identify the support set and the associated constraints of an optimal solution, which is equivalent to recovering an optimal basis of the corresponding LP from finite samples. Then, we design a resolving algorithm to approximate the optimal solution given this basis. The resulting algorithm achieves the better of the instance-dependent and instance-independent guarantees. There are several natural extensions. In this work, we consider the fixed payoff matrix
A
and the observations of each entry follow a stationary stationary distribution. Developing policies for time-varying nonstationary games
(
13
,
62
)
remains open. Moreover, while our analysis assumes a stochastic setting, it would be interesting to investigate the robustness of our algorithms under adversarial corruptions. We leave these directions to future research.

C.3
Proof of
Theorem

6.1

Note that following standard Hoeffding inequality, for any
\epsilon>0
, we know that the gap between each element of the matrix
A
and
\widehat{A}
is upper bounded by
\mathrm{Rad}(n/m,\epsilon/(2n^{2}m))
with a probability at least
1-\epsilon/(2n^{2})
, where we denote this event as
\mathcal{E}_{n}
. We now denote by

\mathcal{E}=\cap_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{E}_{n}

and from the union bound, we have that

\Pr[\mathcal{E}]\geq 1-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\Pr[\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}]\geq 1-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\epsilon/(2n^{2})\geq 1-\epsilon.

We now condition on the event
\mathcal{E}
happens.
Then, from
2
and
3
, we know that the value between
V^{\mathrm{Prime}}_{\mathcal{I}}
and
\widehat{V}^{\mathrm{Prime}}
, as well as
V^{\mathrm{Dual}}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}}
and
\widehat{V}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}}
, for any
\mathcal{I}\subset[m_{1}]
and
\mathcal{J}\subset[m_{2}]
, is upper bounded by
\mathrm{Rad}(n/m,\epsilon/(2n^{2}m))
. This result further implies that the following inequalities

|\delta_{1}-\widehat{\delta}_{1}|\leq 2\cdot\mathrm{Rad}(n/m,\epsilon/(2n^{2}m))\text{\penalty\ \penalty\ and\penalty\ \penalty\ }|\delta_{2}-\widehat{\delta}_{2}|\leq 2\cdot\mathrm{Rad}(n/m,\epsilon/(2n^{2}m)).

Recall the definition that

\delta=\min\{\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\}\text{\penalty\ \penalty\ and\penalty\ \penalty\ }\widehat{\delta}=\min\{\widehat{\delta}_{1},\widehat{\delta}_{2}\}.

Without loss of generality, we let
\delta=\delta_{1}
, then, conditional on the event
\mathcal{E}
happens, we know that

\widehat{\delta}\leq\widehat{\delta}_{1}\leq\delta_{1}+2\cdot\mathrm{Rad}(n/m,\epsilon/(2n^{2}m))=\delta+2\cdot\mathrm{Rad}(n/m,\epsilon/(2n^{2}m)).

Moreover, note that

\widehat{\delta}_{1}\geq\delta_{1}-2\cdot\mathrm{Rad}(n/m,\epsilon/(2n^{2}m))\geq\delta-2\cdot\mathrm{Rad}(n/m,\epsilon/(2n^{2}m))

and

\widehat{\delta}_{2}\geq\delta_{2}-2\cdot\mathrm{Rad}(n/m,\epsilon/(2n^{2}m))\geq\delta-2\cdot\mathrm{Rad}(n/m,\epsilon/(2n^{2}m)).

We have that

\widehat{\delta}\geq\delta-2\cdot\mathrm{Rad}(n/m,\epsilon/(2n^{2}m))

conditional on the event
\mathcal{E}
happens.
Thus, it holds that

\Pr\left[|\delta-\widehat{\delta}|\geq 2\cdot\mathrm{Rad}(n/m,\epsilon/(2n^{2}m))\right]\leq\epsilon.

Therefore, as long as

\widehat{\delta}\geq 4\cdot\mathrm{Rad}(n/m,\epsilon/(2n^{2}m)),

we know that

\frac{\widehat{\delta}}{2}\leq\delta\leq 2\widehat{\delta}

with a probability at least
1-\epsilon
. On the other hand, in order to bound
N_{3}
, we know that with a probability at least
1-\epsilon
, it holds that

\delta\leq\widehat{\delta}+2\cdot\mathrm{Rad}(N_{3}/m,\epsilon/(2N_{3}^{2}m)).

The definition of
N_{3}
implies that
\widehat{\delta}=4\cdot\mathrm{Rad}(N_{3}/m,\epsilon/(2n_{3}^{2}m))
,
which implies that

\delta\leq 6\cdot\mathrm{Rad}(N_{3}/m,\epsilon/(2N_{3}^{2}m))

and thus

N_{3}\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{m}{\delta^{2}}\cdot\log(2m/\epsilon)\right).

Our proof is thus completed.

Proof of

Note that following standard Hoeffding inequality, for any
\epsilon>0
, we know that the absolute value of the matrix

\Pr[\mathcal{E}_{n}]=\Pr\left[\left|\sigma_{{\mathcal{I}},{\mathcal{J}}}-\widehat{\sigma}\right|\geq d^{\prime}\cdot\mathrm{Rad}(n/(d^{\prime})^{2},\epsilon/(2n^{2}(d^{\prime})^{2}))\right]\leq\epsilon/(2n^{2}).

\mathcal{E}=\cap_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{E}_{n}

and from the union bound, it holds that

\Pr[\mathcal{E}]\geq 1-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\Pr[\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}]\geq 1-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\epsilon/(2n^{2})\geq 1-\epsilon.

We now condition on the event
\mathcal{E}

C.5
Oracle Algorithm for Solving the Problem in

We consider the general LP of the form:

V=\min\penalty\ \bm{c}_{0}^{\top}\bm{x}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \mbox{s.t.}\penalty\ A_{0}\bm{x}\preceq\bm{b}_{0},\penalty\ \bm{0}\preceq\bm{x}\preceq\bm{1},

where the decision variable
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{0}}
. For any set
{\mathcal{I}}\subset[m_{0}]
, we also denote by

V_{{\mathcal{I}}}=\min\penalty\ \bm{c}_{0}^{\top}\bm{x}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \mbox{s.t.}\penalty\ A_{0}\bm{x}\preceq\bm{b}_{0},\penalty\ \bm{x}_{{\mathcal{I}}}=\bm{0},\penalty\ \bm{0}\preceq\bm{x}\preceq\bm{1}.

We now provide one potential oracle algorithm that can solve the following problem

V_{\delta}=\min_{\mathcal{I}\subset[m_{0}]}\{V_{\mathcal{I}}-V:V_{\mathcal{I}}-V>0\}

(122)

as long as we are given an
\epsilon>0
such that
\epsilon<V_{\delta}
. In practice, the value
\epsilon
can be set as the desired accuracy level for computing
V_{\delta}
or just be a sufficiently small value. To be specific, we can solve the following Mixed Integer Programming (MIP) problem to determine the value of
V_{\delta}
.

\displaystyle V_{\delta}=\min\penalty\penalty

\displaystyle\bm{c}_{0}^{\top}\bm{x}-V

(123)

\displaystyle\mbox{s.t.}\penalty\penalty

\displaystyle\bm{c}_{0}^{\top}\bm{x}-V\geq\epsilon

\displaystyle A_{0}\bm{x}\preceq\bm{b}_{0}

\displaystyle\bm{z}\succeq\bm{x}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m_{0}}z_{i}\;\leq\;m_{0}-1,

\displaystyle\bm{z}\in\{0,1\}^{m_{0}},\bm{x}\succeq\mathbf{0}.

The above MIP problem can be solved by calling the modern LP solver.
