Genetic structure and selection signatures of Beijing-You chicken populations provide insight into breed conservation
Authors: Hongchang Gu, Shaoqing Tang, Xiaoliang Wang, Yunlei Li, Zhongtao Yin, Xia Chen, Lingchao Zeng, Xiaoran Lin, Jilan Chen, Zhuocheng Hou, Huagui Liu, Rupeng Luan, Qin Chu, Yuze Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPreserving genetic diversity and maintaining population viability are critical yet challenging goals that demand rigorous evaluation of conservation strategies. Beijing-You chicken, as the sole indigenous chicken breed originating from Beijing, China, is currently maintained as four independent populations under distinct conservation programs. How different conservation regimes have shaped its genomic architecture remains largely unknown, limiting evidence-based evaluation. Here, we generated whole-genome resequencing data from 240 individuals representing four Beijing-You chicken populations to assess population structure, genetic diversity, and signatures of selection over decades of conservation. All four populations formed distinct clusters, reflecting measurable differentiation after decades of separate conservation. The differences in genetic diversity were broadly consistent with the variation in effective population size estimates. Runs of homozygosity and linkage disequilibrium decay patterns further characterized each population, with extended values indicating reduced effective population size and increased inbreeding under long-term conservation. We applied the fixation index (FST) and pairwise diversity ratio (θπ) methods to identify selection signatures. A total of 171 genes were identified as candidates. These genes were enriched in pathways related to reproduction, growth regulation, and environmental adaptation. These findings highlight patterns of reduced diversity and skewed relatedness, which could arise from management-related factors such as breeding preferences or mating strategies. Still, they are also compatible with neutral processes, including drift and founder effects. Regardless of the underlying cause, integrating scientifically informed conservation strategies with routine genomic monitoring across generations is essential for sustaining genetic diversity in Beijing-You chicken and other indigenous breeds.Keywords: Conservation, Genetic diversity, Selection signature, Beijing-You chicken

Introduction

Since domestication approximately 3,500 years ago, and particularly over the past century, chickens have been subjected to sustained and intensive artificial selection, driven by escalating human demand for animal-derived protein and fat (Peters et al., 2022; Rubin, et al., 2010; Wang, et al., 2020). This prolonged selection pressure has profoundly shaped the performance of domestic chickens, ultimately steering the directional breeding of commercial lines distinguished by high productivity and pronounced phenotypic uniformity (Liu, et al., 2019; Tallentire, et al., 2016). Indigenous chickens have also been subject to both artificial and natural selection; however, the absence of intensive selection pressure has allowed them to retain more divergent traits rather than production-oriented uniformity (Bed'hom, et al., 2012; Wang, et al., 2022; Wright, et al., 2009; Zhang, et al., 2020).

As the country with the most abundant chicken genetic resources globally, China has documented a total of 140 indigenous chicken breeds. Among these, the Beijing-You chicken (BYC) stands out as the sole local breed native to Beijing, renowned for its high-quality meat and eggs, as well as its distinctive morphological traits, including feathered shanks, beards, and crests. In addition to its unique feathering characteristics (Chu, et al., 2017), BYC exhibits notable diversity in traits such as toe number, eggshell color, and plumage color, while also demonstrating high adaptability to variable environments and strong disease resistance. Phenotypic differences between species or breeds can arise from genetic changes affecting the function of gene products as well as their sequence (Hoekstra and Coyne, 2007; McManus, et al., 2014; Wray, 2007). Accordingly, the extensive phenotypic and adaptive diversity of BYC reflects its rich genetic diversity, underscoring its pivotal role in the conservation and sustainable utilization of these genetic resources. It is precisely this recognition that has elevated the priority of conserving and utilizing chicken breed diversity in response to the ongoing homogenization of genetic diversity ((CGRFA), 2025; Zeng, et al., 2022). Conservation strategies for chickens generally fall into three main categories: in situ live conservation through specialized breeding farms, ex situ live conservation in non-native settings, and ex situ cryopreservation of genetic materials (e.g., DNA, cells, embryos) in germplasm resource banks (Li, et al., 2024b; Nakamura, 2016; Zhang, et al., 2023). Among these, live conservation remains the predominant approach, as it maintains complete and dynamic populations suitable for sustainable utilization. To date, 128 indigenous chicken breeds in China have been conserved as live populations at the National Conservation Farm (NCF) (Zeng, et al., 2022). Although the overarching goal of live conservation is uniform, the specific protocols adopted by individual farms often differ in effective population size, mating scheme, and daily management. These operational differences may translate into divergent genetic outcomes, even among populations of the same breed.

Since its recovery from the brink of extinction in the 1970s, BYC has been incorporated into scientific conservation programs for several decades. At present, multiple independent conservation bases across China are engaged in the live conservation of this breed. A robust conservation strategy is fundamentally contingent upon two interrelated factors: a sufficiently large effective population size (Ne) and the deployment of judicious selection and mating regimes (Meuwissen and Woolliams, 1994). Ne should be maintained at a minimum of 50 to balance genetic fitness with practical manageability, whereas maintaining adaptive potential over long-term conservation requires substantively larger Ne (Meuwissen, 2009). The core objective of selection and mating strategies is to minimize average kinship among breeding individuals. Common approaches include random mating with random selection, within-family selection with random mating, and family rotational mating. It is evident that each BYC conservation base operates under its own tailored breeding and management plan adapted to local conditions and resources. In addition, various extrinsic factors such as differences in housing environments, disease control protocols, and breeder management practices cannot be overlooked. These factors may collectively contribute to divergent conservation outcomes among populations, potentially leading to genetic differentiation even when fundamental conservation principles are consistently followed (Boettcher, et al., 2010).

Selection signature analysis provides a straightforward framework for characterizing such genetic differentiation at the genomic level (Bello, et al., 2023). When populations are maintained separately over many generations, region-specific selective pressures tend to drive divergence in allele frequencies at functionally relevant loci, leaving detectable footprints in the genome. FST and θπ are two statistics commonly used for genome-wide selection scans in chicken populations (Akey, et al., 2002; Li, et al., 2019). FST quantifies population differentiation by measuring allele frequency divergence between groups (Weir and Cockerham, 1984). θπ captures nucleotide diversity within a population. A local reduction in θπ often signals a recent selective sweep. Putative selection simultaneously increases between-group divergence and erodes within-group variation at the target locus and its surrounding haplotype (Qanbari and Simianer, 2014). Each statistic can pinpoint population-specific selection signals with considerable precision. Their joint application offers a more reliable strategy for distinguishing genuine selection targets from stochastic background noise.

The ability to maintain genetic diversity within a conserved population over successive generations serves as a direct reflection of conservation scheme effectiveness (Allendorf, et al., 2010). Genome-wide DNA markers provide an efficient tool for estimating genetic diversity both within and between conserved populations. Here, four independently conserved BYC populations originating from different regions were selected for whole‑genome sequencing to profile the genetic variants. The aim was to evaluate conservation effectiveness and to investigate the genetic differentiation and potential selective signatures occurring under different conservation programs.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and sequencing

A total of 240 blood samples were obtained from Beijing Livestock and Poultry Germplasm Resource Bank (Beijing, China), covering four distinct BYC conservation populations located in Beijing, Jiangsu, and Shanghai (designated B1 to B4), with 30 males and 30 females per population. The populations were conserved in accordance with the breed conservation requirements of the Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs of the People’s Republic of China, with more than 300 hens and 30 cocks from different families being retained for each generation. No active selection for specific traits had been applied during breeding and management. All sampled birds were from the same generation (the 2025 hatch year) to minimize temporal genetic variation. All sampled populations monitored inbreeding coefficients and avoided mating between close relatives during generation turnover. The 60 individuals per population were selected from different families to represent the genetic diversity of each conserved population, while avoiding close relatives and half-siblings. The blood sample collection was carried out following the guidelines approved by the institutional animal care and use committee (IACUC). Genomic DNA was extracted using a DNA isolation kit (catalog No. DP348-03, Tiangen, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For genome sequencing, a minimum of 200 ng of genomic DNA from each sample was used to construct a library. Paired-end sequencing libraries were constructed and then sequenced using DNBSEQ-T7 platform (BGI, Shenzhen, China).

Genomic kinship analysis

To evaluate sampling independence and rule out close familial relationships, genomic relatedness among all 240 individuals was estimated using the KING-robust estimator implemented in PLINK2 (--make-king). The distribution of pairwise kinship coefficients was examined.

Sequence filtering, reads mapping and variant calling

We assessed the quality of resequencing data and conducted filtering with fastp (v0.20.0) using default parameters (Chen, 2023, 2018). The remaining high-quality paired-end reads were mapped to the chicken reference genome GRCg7b (GCF_016699485.2) using the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (v0.7.17) (Li and Durbin, 2009). Mapping results were then converted into the BAM format and sorted using SAMtools (v1.20) (Li, et al., 2009). Duplicate reads were removed from individual sample alignments using Picard MarkDuplicates (v3.2.0). After alignment, we performed SNP calling using GENOME ANALYSIS TOOLKIT (GATK, v4.6.0.0) (McKenna, et al., 2010). Variants were filtered using GATK with the following stringent criteria: QUAL > 30.0, QD > 5.0, FS < 60.0, MQ > 40.0, MQRankSum > −12.5, and ReadPosRankSum > −8.0. Variants failing any of these thresholds were removed. After above filtering, we applied per‑genotype quality filters using VCFtools (v0.1.16) (Danecek, et al., 2011). Genotypes with GQ < 20 and minDP < 3 were set as missing. Further quality control was performed using PLINK (v1.9) (Purcell, et al., 2007). SNPs with a genotype call rate below 90% and individuals with more than 10% missing genotypes were removed. SNPs with minor allele frequency (MAF) below 0.02 were excluded. After variant calling and quality filtering, a total of 15,871,965 SNPs were retained across all 240 individuals.

Population structure

For population structure analyses, LD-pruning was performed on the MAF>0.02 filtered SNP set using PLINK (v1.9) with the “--indep-pairwise 50 5 0.2″ command (Purcell et al., 2007). After pruning, 1,172,915 SNPs were retained. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using GCTA (v1.94.3) based on the binary SNP data (Yang, et al., 2011). A neighbor-joining (NJ) tree was constructed based on the SNPhylo pipeline with 1000 bootstrap replicates (Lee, et al., 2014). Population structure was then inferred using ADMIXTURE (v1.3.0) to estimate individual ancestry and genome-wide admixture among four BYC populations (Alexander, et al., 2009), with the number of ancestral components K ranging from 1 to 6. Cross-validation error was calculated for each K to determine the most appropriate number of ancestral populations.

Genetic diversity analysis

The polymorphic information content (PIC), polymorphic marker ratio (PN), observed heterozygosity (Ho), and expected heterozygosity (He) were calculated using PLINK (v1.9). PN was calculated as the proportion of SNPs with MAF > 0.02 within each population relative to the total number of SNPs retained after quality control, expressed as a percentage. where p and q are the allele frequencies; population-level PIC values were then obtained by averaging across all SNPs. Contemporary Ne was estimated using NeESTIMATOR (v2.0.1) (Do, et al., 2014) with the LD method under the random mating model, based on the LD-pruned SNP set (1,172,915 SNPs). We used a critical allele frequency of 0.02 in NeESTIMATOR. Ne estimates are reported with 95% confidence intervals calculated by the improved Jackknife method. By sliding a 100-kb window with 10-kb steps across the genome, the nucleotide diversity (π) was calculated using VCFtools (v0.1.16) (Danecek et al., 2011). To estimate linkage disequilibrium (LD) decay for different groups, we employed PopLDdecay (v3.42) and calculated the correlation coefficient (r2) between pairwise SNPs (Zhang, et al., 2019). The runs of homozygosity (ROH) were determined using PLINK (v1.9), with the following command '-homozyg-window-snp 20 -homozyg-window-het 1 -homozyg-snp 10 -homozyg-kb 100 -homozyg-density 10 -homozyg-gap 100'. Pairwise comparisons for ROH number and total ROH length were assessed using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests with FDR correction using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. The genomic inbreeding coefficient (FROH) was calculated for each individual as the total length of ROH segments divided by the total length of the autosomal genome.

Selective signatures analysis

To identify putative selection signatures among BYC populations, we applied a rigorous dual-parameter method. By calculating the coefficient of nucleotide differentiation (FST) value and the pairwise diversity ratio (θπ) value, we filtered regions that simultaneously met the thresholds of both as candidate regions of differentiation.

Specifically, the FST between two populations was calculated by the unbiased estimator of Weir and Cockerham (1984) with default parameters (Weir and Cockerham, 1984). We employed VCFtools to assess FST (100-kb sliding window and 10-kb step). Based on the nucleotide diversity (π, 100-kb windows with 10-kb steps), we used VCFtools to calculate θπ between two populations, computing the average difference per locus for each pair of accessions. For each pairwise comparison, we calculated the θπ ratio as θπ (population A / population B) for each window and applied a log₂ transformation. Regions with FST in the top 5% and a log₂(θπ ratio) in the top 5% (indicating reduced diversity in population B relative to population A) were classified as putative selection signals for population B, whereas those with FST in the top 5% and log₂(θπ ratio) in the bottom 5% (indicating reduced diversity in population A) were classified as selection signals for population A.

The detected selective regions were functionally annotated to identify corresponding genes. A gene was considered under selection only if at least 50% of its length was covered by a selective scaffold. This threshold was chosen based on sensitivity analyses using alternative overlap cutoffs (0%, 50%, and 80%). Then we considered a gene as being prone to selection if it was identified as a "selected gene" in three or more of the six possible pairwise comparisons among the four populations. This criterion aimed to identify genes that are recurrently detected across multiple population contrasts. Functional enrichment analysis was performed using Metascape (Zhou, et al., 2019) with the following parameters: Gallus gallus (chicken) was selected as the background species, and the whole-genome gene list was used as the background gene set. Gene Ontology (GO) terms and KEGG pathways with enrichment FDR-adjusted P-values < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched. The enrichment results were visualized using custom R scripts (R version 4.3.3). The UpSet plot was generated using the UpSetR package (version 1.4.0) in R to visualize the intersections and unique components of gene sets across pairwise comparisons.

Results

Sample sequencing and variation detection

We individually sequenced 240 chickens from four BYC conservation populations from three distinct regions in China. A total of 2491.45 Gb clean data were uniquely mapped to chicken reference genome, resulting in an average of 97.42% coverage with 10.47× depth per individual (ranging from 8.45× to 14.06×). After variant calling and quality control, we identified 18.71 million variants, consisting of 15.87 million SNPs and 2.84 million InDels. Functional annotation of the SNPs indicated that 56.48% of SNPs were located in intergenic regions, followed by 36.05% in intronic regions and 7.47% in exonic regions (Fig. 1A). Of all detected InDels, 52.36% were insertions and 47.64% were deletions (Fig. 1B).

Kinship analysis

Pairwise genomic relatedness was estimated among all 240 individuals. Kinship coefficients ranged from -0.832 to 0.237 (Fig. S1). No pair exceeded the 0.25 threshold, and 12 pairs (0.042%) exceeded 0.125, all within the same subpopulation.

Population structure and phylogenetic analysis

Four groups were supported by principal component analysis (PCA), in which the first and second principal components explained 10.14% and 6.34% of the genetic variation, respectively (Fig. 2A). No sex-biased clustering was observed within populations. The first principal component segregated B4 from the other populations, and the second principal component separated B3 into a distinct cluster. The two conservation populations B1 and B2 exhibited similar clustering patterns, but showed clear separation on PC3, which explained 3.05% of the variance (Fig. S2). Phylogenetic relationships, based on a neighbor-joining of pairwise genetic distances of whole-genome SNPs, as illustrated in Fig. 2B, also showed strong clustering into four distinct genetic groups.

Population genetic structure of four Beijing-You chicken populations. (A) Principal component analysis (PCA) plot. (B) Neighbor-joining phylogenetic tree. (C) Population genetic structure. The length of each colored segment represents the proportion of the individual genome inferred from ancestral populations (K = 2-4).

We further performed population structure analysis using ADMIXTURE, which estimated individual ancestry and admixture proportions assuming K ancestral populations (Fig. 2C). Cross-validation (CV) error decreased from K = 1 (CV error = 0.403) to K = 4 (CV error = 0.209), then increased to 0.409 at K = 5 and 0.611 at K = 6, indicating K = 4 as the optimal model (Fig. S3). With K = 2 (CV error = 0.378), a clear division was found between B4 and the other three populations. With K = 3 (CV error = 0.323), there was a higher genetic similarity between B1 and B2. At K = 4, all four populations were fully separated with nearly complete within-population ancestry assignment, indicating detectable genetic differentiation among the sampled conservation groups, likely influenced by independent management, genetic drift, founder effects, and possibly selection.

Analysis of population genetic diversity

Based on genome-wide SNP markers, we assessed the genetic diversity of each population using multiple metrics to characterize the allelic richness and distribution patterns at the gene level (Table 1). Overall, B1 showed the highest PIC, PN, Ho, and π. Notably, these parameters were consistently correlated with each other across populations. Populations with higher PIC tended to display higher PN, Ho, and π values, reflecting a concordant pattern among complementary diversity indices. He remained relatively stable across populations and was consistently higher than Ho in all cases. Ne estimates ranged from 60 to 135 across populations (Table 1), with 95% confidence intervals of 35–112 (B4), 51–138 (B3), 72–185 (B2), and 89–218 (B1).

Analysis of ROH revealed considerable variation among populations (Fig. 3A and B). The average number of ROHs per population ranged from 788.15 in the B1 population to 1284.83 in the B4 population. Populations with higher ROH counts also tended to exhibit longer ROH segments, indicating cumulative inbreeding effects. Length-stratified analysis of ROH segments (<150 kb, 150–300 kb, >300 kb) further revealed population differences in inbreeding history. Short ROH segments may reflect older shared ancestry or linkage disequilibrium, whereas longer ROH segments are more informative for recent inbreeding. Specifically, the total number of ROH segments and the total length in each category increased progressively from B1 to B4, with all three categories showing the lowest values in B1 and the highest in B4 (Table S1). The genomic inbreeding coefficient (FROH), calculated as the proportion of the autosomal genome covered by ROH, increased accordingly from 14.17% ± 2.60% (B1) to 23.37% ± 2.76% (B4) (Table 1).

Genome-wide LD decay patterns were assessed by measuring the physical distance at which r² declined to half of its maximum value (Fig. 3C). Considerable variation in LD decay rate was observed across populations. The r² values declined to half of their maximum at approximately 66 kb for B1, 67 kb for B2, 92 kb for B3, and 162 kb for B4. Importantly, the rate of LD decay was negatively associated with both ROH length and number. Populations characterized by slower LD decay (i.e., longer-range LD) consistently showed extended ROH length and a higher number of ROH segments. These interrelationships among multiple independent diversity metrics, including PIC, Ho, π, ROH, Ne, and LD decay, provide a coherent and cross-validated picture of the genetic landscape across the four conserved populations.

Identification of genomic regions under selection

To elucidate the potential selection occurring during different conservation situations, we identified strong selective sweep signals among different populations. Sensitivity analyses using alternative gene-overlap thresholds (0%, 50%, and 80%) were performed to evaluate the robustness of the cutoff. The 50% threshold was chosen as it balanced the number of candidate genes against the risk of false positives (Table S2). We identified 195 genes, defined by concurrent localization in the top 5% of FST and in either the top or bottom 5% of θπ between B1 and B2 (Fig. 4A), putatively under positive selection. Using the same pairwise comparative approach applied between groups (B1 vs. B3, B1 vs. B4, B2 vs. B3, B2 vs. B4, and B3 vs. B4), 185, 209, 196, 212, and 211 genes were detected, respectively (Fig. S4, Table S3). Based on the six sets of candidate genes identified from the pairwise comparisons described above, an UpSet plot was constructed to visualize their intersections and unique components (Fig. 4B). The analysis revealed that only 3 genes were shared across all six comparisons, and the largest unique set was observed in the B1 vs. B2 comparison (95 genes). This pattern highlights both conserved and divergent selective signals among the different population pairs. The widespread gene overlap identified specifically among the comparisons involving B4 suggests a compelling pattern. This shared genomic signature likely reflects selection pressures uniquely associated with population B4.

To explore functional themes among the recurrently detected genes, we focused on the 171 genes that appeared in three or more pairwise comparisons. While these genes differ in their detection frequency across comparisons, they represent a relatively consistent set of differentiated loci across populations and may provide a broader view of the functional pathways potentially involved. Several of these genes have been previously associated with growth and fat deposition (BRAF, PLA2G4A, TNC), gonadal development and egg quality (ATP2B2, CACNA1D, BRINP1), and neurodevelopment and stress response (AGAP3, ERC2, SYN2). Functional enrichment analyses were performed using annotations from the GO and KEGG databases for 171 genes (Fig. 4C, 4D). The annotation results showed that many genes were enriched in purine nucleotide binding (FDR = 8.70×10−5), pyrophosphatase activity (FDR = 8.77×10−5), and nucleotide binding (FDR = 5.80×10−4). KEGG pathway analysis showed that genes were mainly enriched in the GnRH signaling pathway (FDR = 2.03×10−3), the MAPK signaling pathway (FDR = 5.42×10−3), and the vascular smooth muscle contraction pathway (FDR = 8.22×10−3).

Discussion

Here, we dissected the genomic architecture of 240 individuals using whole-genome sequencing and identified distinct population structure, compromised genetic diversity in specific lines, and differentiated genes enriched for fitness-related pathways. These findings provide a genomic basis for evaluating the effectiveness of current conservation strategies and highlight the need for integrating routine genetic monitoring into indigenous breed management programs.

Using whole-genome sequencing, we identified a total of 15.87 million SNPs. The predominance of SNPs in intergenic regions (56.48%), compared to the lower proportion within exonic regions (7.47%), may correspond to the expected profile under potential purifying selection (Al-Shuhaib, 2025; Fadason, et al., 2022), which can efficiently remove deleterious mutations from coding sequences. For InDels, the approximately equal proportion of insertions and deletions (52.36% vs. 47.64%) may suggest the overall genomic structural stability (He, et al., 2019; Kapusta, et al., 2017). Based on the genome-wide spectrum of genetic variants, we evaluated the population structure, revealing significant divergence in genetic background among the four populations. PCA and NJ tree analysis demonstrated that individuals from each population clustered separately and exhibited homogeneous genetic composition within each group. Interestingly, the close relationship between B1 and B2 was consistent in the NJ tree and PCA, which also mirrored the similar geographical distributions of the two populations. The isolation by geographical distance probably played a role in population differentiation (Malomane, et al., 2021; Nie, et al., 2019; Ren, et al., 2024). However, geographical proximity alone may not fully account for this pattern. The closer affinity between B1 and B2 may reflect shared ancestry from overlapping founder sources at the time these conservation populations were established, or residual historical gene flow prior to the onset of strict isolated breeding. Additionally, the comparable effective population sizes and conservation histories of B1 and B2 may have subjected them to similar drift dynamics, further contributing to their convergent genomic profiles. The results of admixture also implied a visible genetic differentiation among the four populations. Notably, the model with K = 2 clearly separated population B4 from the other three populations (B1, B2, and B3). At K = 3, B1 and B2 remained largely inseparable, forming a joint cluster distinct from B3 and B4. At K = 4, the cross-validation error reached its minimum, and the four populations were fully resolved into distinct clusters, reflecting the most plausible genetic subdivision. This pattern is consistent with the PCA results and the phylogenetic tree, confirming the genetic distinctness of B4 and the closer relationship between B1 and B2. While ADMIXTURE models ancestry components rather than discrete evolutionary units, the differentiation observed here likely reflects recent, fine-scale population-specific drift and subtle selection pressures, rather than deep ancestral subdivisions. The consistent signal of population-specific clustering across all independent analytical methods (PCA, NJ tree, ADMIXTURE) reflects the cumulative effects of multiple evolutionary forces operating over decades of independent conservation, including differential genetic drift, founder effects, population-specific unintentional selection, and local environmental pressures (Charlesworth, 2009; Nayfa, et al., 2020).

Compared with commercial lines, the indigenous populations tend to harbor higher levels of genetic diversity, which could be due to traditional breeding practices characterized by natural and random mating with far less artificial intervention (Tian, et al., 2023; Yang, et al., 2025b). BYC shows extraordinary phenotypic characteristics, such as diverse morphology and high environmental adaptability, and its abundant breed diversity provides plentiful phenotypic variance. The influence of different conservation strategies on population diversity may be directly and sensitively reflected in its manifestation within genetic diversity (Li, et al., 2024a; Song, et al., 2025; Zhang, et al., 2018). We assessed various indicators of genetic diversity under conservation of these four populations. All genetic diversity indices consistently pointed to similar conclusions, B1 exhibited the highest genetic diversity, whereas B4 exhibited the lowest. B2 showed slightly lower or comparable values for genetic diversity parameters (PIC, PN, He, Ho, FROH and π) relative to B1, along with marginally higher ROH and LD estimates. B3 fell between B2 and B4 and was significantly differentiated from both. During conservation of small or managed populations, stochastic genetic drift is an unavoidable consequence of finite population size, whereas founder effects commonly arise when populations are established from a limited number of individuals (Allendorf, et al., 2022; LACY, 1987; Witzenberger and Hochkirch, 2011). However, these forces alone may not fully explain the consistent and functionally relevant genomic differentiation observed among populations of the same breed. The Ne estimates should be interpreted with caution, as their 95% confidence intervals overlapped considerably across populations in our study. This overlap reflects the inherent uncertainty in LD-based Ne estimation. We therefore treat the ranking of Ne estimates as broadly indicative rather than statistically definitive. Notwithstanding this uncertainty, populations with smaller Ne estimates tended to have lower genetic diversity. This pattern is consistent with the expected effects of drift and inbreeding under reduced population size (Makino, et al., 2018; Mtileni, et al., 2016). The functionally relevant genomic differentiation we observed among populations could reflect these neutral processes, or directional forces such as unintentional selection.

To identify genomic regions with elevated differentiation among populations, we scanned the genome for windows with extreme FST divergence and large differences in π. Overlapping the candidate regions from pairwise comparisons identified 171 genes that were detected in three or more of the six comparisons. Functional enrichment of these genes revealed annotations related to nucleotide binding, pyrophosphatase activity, and hydrolase activity, which are involved in cellular energy metabolism and biosynthetic processes. Enrichment analyses also implicated the GnRH, MAPK, and calcium signaling pathways, which have known roles in reproduction, growth, and environmental adaptation (Cheng, et al., 2025; Gupta, et al., 2025; Shen, et al., 2023). Several genes identified in our study fall within these pathways. BRAF links reproductive hormone signaling to somatic growth and fat deposition via the MAPK cascade (Zhu, et al., 2024). PLA2G4A modulates arachidonic acid metabolism and was previously implicated in chicken abdominal fat deposition (Qing, et al., 2018; Xiao, et al., 2021), while TNC expression in adipose tissue is sensitive to age and diet, suggesting a role in fat partitioning (Bohannon-Stewart, et al., 2014). Beyond growth, AGAP3 has been associated with synaptic plasticity and spatial cognition (Branch, et al., 2022), and PHF2 plays a role in chicken neural progenitor proliferation and genome stability during neurogenesis (Pappa, et al., 2019). For reproductive traits, several calcium homeostasis genes are noteworthy: ATP2B2 is involved in eggshell calcification and quality (Cui, et al., 2025; Gloux, et al., 2019; Yang, et al., 2025a), CACNA1D has been linked to ovarian aging and follicular calcium homeostasis (Gao, et al., 2026), and BRINP1 is differentially expressed in ovarian follicles between lines with divergent egg production (Sun, et al., 2021). PHF2 was also identified as a QTL-linked candidate for egg number in an independent GWAS (Lei, et al., 2024). These 171 genes are heterogeneous in their detection frequency. Only three were detected in all six comparisons, whereas the rest appeared in three to five. The enrichment results should therefore be seen as reflecting general functional trends across these loci, rather than consistent strong functional signals across all populations. Moreover, the functional pathways identified here are relatively broad and do not directly demonstrate selection on specific traits. Without phenotypic data from the four populations, these enrichment results remain hypothesis-generating and require independent validation in future studies. Nonetheless, the repeated involvement of certain pathways across multiple comparisons suggests they may be relevant to the differentiation observed among these populations. The functional enrichment patterns we observed could be interpreted in multiple, non-mutually exclusive ways. On one hand, they are consistent with the expected consequences of genetic drift, founder effects, and reduced Ne in a closed conservation population. On the other hand, unintentional selection arising from subtle, long-term biases in breeding or management practices may have also contributed, but our data cannot distinguish among these forces. Should selection have played a role, its effects would likely be diffuse, potentially favoring traits such as reproductive performance, growth, or stress resilience, but this remains speculative and requires independent phenotypic or management data for validation.

Balancing the maintenance of genetic diversity with the preservation of genetic distinctiveness presents a fundamental challenge in conservation genetics. Leveraging population-based assessments of genetic diversity and selection signatures (Kong, et al., 2025; Restoux, et al., 2022), our study elucidates the underlying factors that influence conservation efficacy and provides molecular evidence to inform scientifically grounded conservation strategies for BYC. First, minimizing confounding effects arising from environmental variation and management practices is imperative. Second, maintaining an adequate Ne is critical for sustaining genetic diversity (Mergeay, et al., 2026). A small Ne accelerates both genetic drift and inbreeding, while simultaneously increasing the susceptibility of populations to selection. Routine genomic monitoring across generations is therefore essential for early detection of diversity loss. Third, practical measures such as optimizing the sex ratio, applying structured mating schemes, and supplementing with new bloodlines can help mitigate inbreeding and maintain genetic diversity.

In general, our results suggest that even conservation programs designed to be neutral can be accompanied by measurable genetic changes, including reduced diversity, elevated inbreeding, and population differentiation. These changes may reflect the combined effects of genetic drift, founder effects, reduced Ne, unequal family contribution, and management-related factors. To counter this, more scientifically informed strategies for mating schemes, breeder selection, and flock management are urgently needed. When combined with routine genomic monitoring of genetic diversity, such integrated approaches provide an evidence-based framework essential for the long‑term conservation of BYC and other indigenous breeds.

Conclusion

This study presents a genomic analysis of four independently conserved BYC populations based on whole-genome resequencing. These populations exhibited distinct genetic clusters after decades of separate conservation. Functional enrichment of candidate differentiation regions identified pathways potentially linked to reproduction, growth, and environmental adaptation, including the GnRH, MAPK, and calcium signaling pathways. The observed genomic differentiation could reflect the combined effects of both unintentional selection and several neutral processes, though the contribution of each has not been clearly distinguished. Regardless of the underlying causes, the observed reductions in diversity and elevations in inbreeding across several populations highlight the value of routine genomic monitoring. We suggest that genomic indicators such as Ne and FROH should be incorporated into ongoing conservation programs to detect diversity loss and inform management decisions. This work provides a genomic baseline for Beijing-You chicken and a reference for evidence-based management of indigenous poultry breeds.
