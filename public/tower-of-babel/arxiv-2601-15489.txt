Abstract
We construct four-dimensional de Sitter space as an excited state, rather than as a vacuum configuration, in type IIB, heterotic S ​ O ​ ( 32 ) SO(32) , and heterotic E 8 × E 8 \mathrm{E}_{8}\times\mathrm{E}_{8} string theories. This framework provides a mechanism to evade vacuum-based no-go theorems for de Sitter solutions in string theory. Starting from a generic M-theory configuration, we obtain de Sitter isometry in the dual string theories through appropriate dynamical duality sequences in the late-time limit. The excited state, identified as a Glauber–Sudarshan state, is constructed as the expectation value of the metric operator in M-theory using path-integral techniques. We further analyze the conditions required for the existence of a well-defined effective field theory description and show that these conditions are equivalent to the Null Energy Condition for a ( 3 + 1 ) (3+1) -dimensional FLRW cosmology. Finally, we investigate constraints arising from axionic cosmology and demonstrate how the time-dependent solutions are modified when experimental bounds on the axionic coupling constant are taken into account. This article serves as a computational companion to sections 3 and 4 of the paper 16 , where we present the detailed intermediate steps underlying the analysis in those sections.

Introduction

The construction of de Sitter vacua within a consistent quantum theory of gravity has long remained a challenging problem. For instance, the Gibbons, Maldacena and Nuñez no-go theorem
21

30

20

34

24

23

have continued to highlight persistent obstructions in constructing de Sitter compactifications within string/M-theory. It is therefore fair to say that the existence of a fully controlled classical de Sitter vacuum in these theories remains an open question
32
.
In this work, we take a different perspective, realising de Sitter not as a vacuum configuration but as an excited state over a time-independent supersymmetric Minkowski background. In particular, we construct de Sitter as a Glauber–Sudarshan state, which can be viewed as a statistical mixture of fundamental coherent states
16
. Such a state can be constructed in M-theory, while demanding de Sitter isometry in the dual string theory. As discussed in detail in
13
, realising de Sitter in this manner resolves several conceptual issues. To mention a few, it allows for the implementation of an exact renormalisation group procedure, since the underlying spacetime is time-independent Minkowski and hence the notion of frequency remains well defined, unlike in a de Sitter background where modes are continuously redshifted. As a result, one can write down a low-energy Wilsonian effective action whose corresponding equations of motion yield the appropriate de Sitter solution. Furthermore, since the background Minkowski space is supersymmetric, zero-point energies are automatically cancelled, thereby circumventing the issues associated with the zero-point energy problem.

In M-theory, we perform a path integral analysis to evaluate the expectation value of the metric operator with respect to the aforementioned Glauber-Sudarshan state using perturbative techniques. Since the ultimate goal is to describe a universe undergoing an accelerated expansion—closely resembling our own—it becomes necessary to incorporate contributions from all orders in the perturbative series.
It is well known from quantum field theory that, except in rare cases, exact results for observables are difficult to obtain. One generally relies on perturbative analysis, which requires identifying a suitable expansion parameter that can be tuned to be small, allowing the observable of interest to be expressed as a power series in that parameter. Although perturbative methods have had notable success—for instance, in the remarkable precision tests of the anomalous magnetic moment of the electron—they also come with shortcomings. In general, perturbative series are asymptotic with zero radius of convergence. There are two primary sources of such divergences:
(i)
the factorial growth of the number of Feynman diagrams at higher orders in the perturbative expansion due to combinatorics
19
, and
(ii)
UV and IR divergences of the so-called renormalon type, arising from the high- and low-momentum regions of phase-space integrals, respectively
3
.
In our analysis, we encounter divergences of the former kind. Importantly, the asymptotic nature of the perturbation series does not represent a fundamental obstacle to computing the observable of interest, but rather signals the necessity of incorporating non-perturbative contributions. These non-perturbative effects arise from non-trivial saddle points of the path integral, known as
instantons

27
. The formal series obtained by adding instanton corrections to the original asymptotic expansion is known as a
trans-series

31
. We then employ Borel-Écalle resummation techniques to resum the trans-series and obtain physically meaningful results for the observables.

5

We generalise equation (

\displaystyle~~~(\mathrm{N}!)^{\alpha}\sim\frac{(\alpha\mathrm{N})!}{\alpha^{\alpha\mathrm{N}}}.

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathrm{N}=0}^{\infty}g^{\mathrm{N}}a_{\mathrm{N}},~~~~\mathrm{where}~~a_{\mathrm{N}}\sim\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{N}}(\alpha\mathrm{N})!.

(144)

Here we have absorbed a constant
\alpha^{\alpha}
into the definition of the parameter
g
. To resum this series we introduce a factor of
(\alpha\mathrm{N})!
in its numerator and denominator as:

\displaystyle\mathscr{F}(g)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathrm{N}=0}^{\infty}g^{\mathrm{N}}a_{\mathrm{N}}\frac{\int_{0}^{\infty}d\mathrm{S}\exp\left(-\mathrm{S}\right)\mathrm{S}^{\alpha\mathrm{N}}}{(\alpha\mathrm{N})!}~.

(145)

Interchanging the order of summation and integral we get

\displaystyle\mathscr{F}(g)

\displaystyle\rightarrow\int_{0}^{\infty}d\mathrm{S}\exp(-\mathrm{S})\left[\sum_{\mathrm{N}=0}^{\infty}g^{\mathrm{N}}a_{\mathrm{N}}\frac{\mathrm{S}^{\alpha\mathrm{N}}}{(\alpha\mathrm{N})!}\right],

(146)

The quantity inside the square bracket is termed as
Borel transform
of
\mathscr{F}(g)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}[\mathscr{F}](g\mathrm{S})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathrm{N}=0}^{\infty}g^{\mathrm{N}}a_{\mathrm{N}}\frac{\mathrm{S}^{\alpha\mathrm{N}}}{(\alpha\mathrm{N})!},

(147)

which in fact has a finite radius of convergence. Thus, the
Borel resummation
of the formal power series
\mathscr{F}(g)
is basically the Laplace transform of the Borel transform

\displaystyle\mathfrak{s}(\mathscr{F})(g)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\mathrm{S}\exp\left(-\mathrm{S}\right)\mathcal{B}[\mathscr{F}](g\mathrm{S}).

(148)

Further simplification of this result yields

\displaystyle\mathfrak{s}(\mathscr{F})(g)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\mathrm{S}~\exp\left(-\mathrm{S}\right)\sum_{\mathrm{N}=0}^{\infty}g^{\mathrm{N}}\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{N}}\mathrm{S}^{\alpha\mathrm{N}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{g^{1/\alpha}}\right)\int_{0}^{\infty}d\mathrm{S}~\exp\left(-\frac{\mathrm{S}}{g^{1/\alpha}}\right)\sum_{\mathrm{N}=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{N}}\mathrm{S}^{\alpha\mathrm{N}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{g^{1/\alpha}}\right)\int_{0}^{\infty}d\mathrm{S}\exp\left(-\frac{\mathrm{S}}{g^{1/\alpha}}\right)\frac{1}{1-\mathcal{A}\mathrm{S}^{\alpha}},

(149)

where to go from 1st line to the 2nd line we have made a change of integration variable
\mathrm{S}\rightarrow\frac{\mathrm{S}}{g^{1/\alpha}}
and to arrive at the last line we have performed the sum over
\mathrm{N}
. We note that the Borel resummation (
149
) has led to non-perturbative terms of the form
\exp\left(-\frac{\mathrm{S}}{g^{1/\alpha}}\right)
. The zeros of the following polynomial

\displaystyle\mathrm{S}^{\alpha}-\frac{1}{\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle\equiv\prod_{i=1}^{\alpha}\left(\mathrm{S}-a_{i}\right)

(150)

are nothing but the poles in the Borel plane.
Substituting the Borel resummed series (
149

142

\displaystyle\mathrm{Num}[\langle\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{\mu\nu}\rangle_{\overline{\sigma}}]

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{1}{g^{1/\alpha}}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\mathrm{S}\exp\left(-\frac{\mathrm{S}}{g^{1/\alpha}}\right)\frac{1}{1-\mathcal{A}\mathrm{S}^{\alpha}}\right]_{\mathrm{P.V.}}\int d^{11}k\frac{\overline{\alpha}_{\mu\nu}(k)}{a(k)}\psi_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathrm{X})e^{-ik_{0}t}.

(151)

6
Null Energy Condition

There are various energy conditions that put restrictions on the stress energy tensor. For instance, according to the null energy condition (NEC), the stress energy tensor has to satisfy the following constraint

\displaystyle T_{\mu\nu}k^{\mu}k^{\nu}\geq 0,

(152)

for any null vector
k^{\mu}
. For a perfect fluid in 4 dimensional spacetime, the stress energy tensor is of the form

\displaystyle T_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=(\rho+p)u_{\mu}u_{\nu}+pg_{\mu\nu}

(153)

where
\rho
denotes the energy density,
p
denotes the momentum density and
u_{\mu}
is the velocity 4 vector. Substituting (
153
) in (
152
) we obtain

\displaystyle T_{\mu\nu}k^{\mu}k^{\nu}

\displaystyle=(\rho+p)u_{\mu}k^{\mu}u_{\nu}k^{\nu}+pg_{\mu\nu}k^{\mu}k^{\nu}\geq 0

\displaystyle\implies(\rho+p)(u_{\mu}k^{\mu})^{2}\geq 0.

(154)

Since the factor
(u_{\mu}k^{\mu})^{2}
is non-negative, the NEC for a perfect fluid boils down to

\displaystyle(\rho+p)\geq 0.

(155)

For a flat 3+1 dimensional FLRW spacetime with metric configuration

\displaystyle ds^{2}=-d\tau^{2}+a^{2}(\tau)((dx^{1})^{2}+(dx^{2})^{2}+(dx^{3})^{2})

(156)

the Friedman equations take the form

\displaystyle(H(\tau))^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{8\pi G_{N}}{3}\rho,~~~~\dot{H}(\tau)=-4\pi G_{N}(\rho+p),

(157)

where
H(\tau)=\dot{a}(\tau)/a(\tau)
is the Hubble parameter and
\tau\in(-\infty,\infty)
is the time coordinate. Thus, the NEC in equation (
155
) implies
\dot{H}(\tau)\leq 0
. If we assume
a(\tau)\propto\tau^{\delta}
then the null energy condition becomes
\delta\geq 0
. In terms of the conformal time coordinate
t
, the FLRW metric takes the form

\displaystyle ds^{2}

\displaystyle=a^{2}(t)\left(-dt^{2}+(dx^{1})^{2}+(dx^{2})^{2}+(dx^{3})^{2}\right)

(158)

The relation between
\tau
and
t
can be determined by noting that

\displaystyle\int\tau^{-\delta}d\tau

\displaystyle=\int dt

\displaystyle\implies\tau=((

\displaystyle 1-\delta)t)^{{1}/{(1-\delta)}}

(159)

Therefore,

\displaystyle a(t)\propto t^{\delta/(1-\delta)}:=t^{n}.

(160)

Consequently, the null energy condition
\delta\geq 0
becomes

\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle=\frac{1+n}{n}\geq 0\implies\frac{1}{n}\geq-1.

(161)

Now, a well defined effective field theory description of the excited state configuration requires us to put the following constraint on the type IIA coupling constant
5

\displaystyle\frac{\partial g_{s}}{\partial t}\propto g_{s}^{+\mathrm{ve}}.

(162)

Let us analyze this for the case of de Sitter in type IIB theory as studied in Section
3.1
. For convenience, below we write down the metric of IIB theory from equation (
12
)

\displaystyle ds^{2}_{\mathrm{IIB}}

\displaystyle=\frac{{a}^{2}(t)}{{\rm H}^{2}(y)}\left(-dt^{2}+\left(dx^{1}\right)^{2}+\left(dx^{2}\right)^{2}+\left(dx^{3}\right)^{2}\right)+{\rm H}^{2}(y)\mathrm{F}_{1}(t)g_{\alpha\beta}(y)dy^{\alpha}dy^{\beta}

\displaystyle~~~~~~+{\rm H}^{2}(y)\mathrm{F}_{2}(t)g_{mn}(y)dy^{m}dy^{n}.

(163)

We consider the scale factor to be of the form

\displaystyle a^{2}(t)

\displaystyle=(\Lambda t^{2})^{n},

(164)

where the parameter
n
is defined analogously to that appearing in equation (
160
). The dual type IIA coupling constant (
16
) becomes

\displaystyle\overline{g}_{s}\equiv\frac{g_{s}}{\mathrm{H}(y)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{a(t)}=\frac{1}{(\Lambda t^{2})^{n/2}}.

(165)

Correspondingly, the EFT condition (
162
) implies

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\overline{g}_{s}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-\frac{n}{t}\overline{g}_{s}=-n{\Lambda^{1/2}\overline{g}_{s}^{1/n}}\overline{g}_{s}\propto\overline{g}_{s}^{(1+1/n)\geq 0}

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~\implies\frac{1}{n}\geq-1.

(166)

This is precisely, the null energy condition for a flat 3+1 dimensional FLRW cosmology as appearing in equation (
161
). Thus, for the de Sitter configuration in type IIB theory
the existence of well defined effective field theory description boils down to satisfying the null energy condition
. In the following subsections we study the null energy condition in heterotic
\mathrm{SO}(32)
and
\mathrm{E}_{8}\times\mathrm{E}_{8}
theory.

Conclusion and Outlook

In this article, we have investigated the existence of four-dimensional de Sitter space as an excited state over a supersymmetric Minkowski background. In Section

A brief overview of coherent states

The importance of coherent states for the study of photon statistics of radiation fields is widely known
22
. The property that makes these quantum states of considerable interest is their well defined classical limit. In fact, they are typically known as the
minimum uncertainty states
- the expectation value of any observable computed with respect to such states follow the classical trajectory with minimum uncertainty. Even though the coherent states are not orthogonal to each other, they form a complete set, to be precise, an overcomplete set. We characterise them as eigenstate
|z\rangle
of annihilation operator
\hat{a}
, in the context of harmonic oscillator problem of quantum mechanics:

\displaystyle\hat{a}|z\rangle

\displaystyle=z|z\rangle,

(250)

where
z
is the corresponding eigenvalue. As
\hat{a}
is a not a hermitian operator,
z=|z|e^{i\varphi}\in\mathbb{C}
is generically a complex number. Let us express the state
|z\rangle
as a linear combination of the basis states
\{|n\rangle,~n=0,1,...\}
, viz. the eigenstates of the number operator of harmonic oscillator

\displaystyle|z\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}|n\rangle=c_{0}|0\rangle+c_{1}|1\rangle+c_{2}|2\rangle+c_{3}|3\rangle+\cdots

\displaystyle\implies\hat{a}|z\rangle

\displaystyle=c_{1}|0\rangle+\sqrt{2}c_{2}|1\rangle+\sqrt{3}c_{3}|2\rangle+\cdots=z(c_{0}|0\rangle+c_{1}|1\rangle+c_{2}|2\rangle+\cdots)

(251)

where we have used
\hat{a}|n\rangle=\sqrt{n}|n-1\rangle
. Comparing both sides we see that the coefficients satisfy the recurrence relation

\displaystyle c_{n+1}=\frac{z}{\sqrt{n+1}}c_{n}.

(252)

Hence the coherent state is as follows

\displaystyle|z\rangle

\displaystyle=c_{0}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{n}}{\sqrt{n!}}|n\rangle

(253)

We can fix the overall constant
c_{0}
by demanding the state
|z\rangle
to be normalised

\displaystyle\langle z|z\rangle

\displaystyle=|c_{0}|^{2}\sum_{m,n}\frac{(z^{\star})^{m}z^{n}}{\sqrt{m!n!}}\langle m|n\rangle

\displaystyle\implies|c_{0}|

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{|z|^{2}}{2}\right)

(254)

Upon expressing the
n
-th excited state in terms of the vacuum state, the coherent state

\displaystyle|z\rangle

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{|z|^{2}}{2}\right)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{n}}{\sqrt{n!}}\frac{(\hat{a}^{\dagger})^{n}}{\sqrt{n!}}|0\rangle\equiv\hat{\mathrm{D}}(z)|0\rangle,

(255)

can be interpreted as a state obtained by displacing the vacuum configuration
|0\rangle
where the displacement operator

\displaystyle\hat{\mathrm{D}}(z)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{|z|^{2}}{2}\right)\exp\left(z\hat{a}^{\dagger}\right)\exp\left(-z^{\star}\hat{a}\right)=\exp\left(z\hat{a}^{\dagger}-z^{\star}\hat{a}\right),

(256)

is a unitary operator satisfying

\displaystyle\hat{\mathrm{D}}(z)\hat{\mathrm{D}}^{\dagger}(z)=\hat{\mathrm{D}}^{\dagger}(z)\hat{\mathrm{D}}(z)=1.

(257)

In
3+1
dimensional quantum field theory where we have one harmonic oscillator for each momentum mode, we can express the coherent state (
255
) in the following way
39

\displaystyle|\{z_{k}\}\rangle

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\frac{1}{\mathrm{V}}\sum_{n}\left(z_{k_{n}}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{k_{n}}-z^{\star}_{k_{n}}\hat{a}_{k_{n}}\right)\right)|0\rangle

(258)

where we have considered the four dimensional space-time to be a square lattice of volume
\mathrm{V}=L^{4}
such that
k_{n}^{\mu}=\frac{2\pi n^{\mu}}{L}
,
n^{\mu}
are integers.
As mentioned in the introduction, the state that we construct differs from the usual coherent states. We spell out the differences in the following.

(a)

Rather than a free vacuum
|0\rangle
, the state of our consideration is obtained by displacing an
interacting vacuum

|\Omega\rangle
. Suppose we consider a double well potential of the form
V(x)=(x^{2}-a^{2})^{2}
.

Figure 13
:
Double well potential
V(x)=(x^{2}-2^{2})^{2}
with ground state and 1st excited state around the minima at
x=+2
.

It contains two minima at
x=\pm a
. The ground state corresponding to any of these two minima we refer to as the free vacuum. In figure
13
, the free vacuum corresponding to the minima at
x=+a
is shown by the orange curve.

Figure 14
:
Double well potential
V(x)=(x^{2}-2^{2})^{2}
with the true ground state and 1st excited state.

The true ground state of the double well potential is depicted by the orange curve in figure
14
. We refer to this particular state as the interacting vacuum. The interacting vacuum can be expressed using the total Hamiltonian
H=H_{0}+H_{\mathrm{int}}
of a system as

\displaystyle|\Omega\rangle

\displaystyle=\lim_{{T}\rightarrow\infty(1-i\epsilon)}\left(e^{-iE_{0}T}\langle\Omega|0\rangle\right)^{-1}e^{-iHT}|0\rangle

(259)

where
E_{0}=\langle\Omega|H|\Omega\rangle
is the true ground state energy.

(b)

For such an interacting vacuum configuration the form of the corresponding displacement operator must differ from (
256
). We introduce effective creation and annihilation operators
\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\mathrm{eff},k}
and
\hat{a}_{\mathrm{eff},k}
respectively. The interacting vacuum is annihilated by
\hat{a}_{\mathrm{eff},k}

10

\displaystyle\hat{a}_{\mathrm{eff},k}|\Omega\rangle

\displaystyle=0

(260)

The best one can do is express the displacement operator corresponding to the interacting vacuum of a quantum field theory in terms of effective creation and annihilation operators as

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbb{D}}(\sigma(k))

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\frac{1}{\mathrm{V}}\sum_{i}\left(\sigma(k_{i})\hat{a}_{\mathrm{eff},k_{i}}^{\dagger}+\sigma^{\star}(k_{i})\hat{a}_{\mathrm{eff},k_{i}}\right)\right)

(261)

where
\hat{\mathbb{D}}(\sigma(k))
is a non-unitary operator. In terms of the fourier modes of the fields, the operator (
261
) can be expressed as

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbb{D}}(\sigma(k))

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\frac{1}{\mathrm{V}}\sum_{i}\left(\sigma(k_{i})\tilde{\varphi}^{\star}(k_{i})+\sigma^{\star}(k_{i})\tilde{\varphi}(k_{i})\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\frac{2}{\mathrm{V}}\sum_{i}\left(\mathbf{Re}~\sigma(k_{i})~\mathbf{Re}~\tilde{\varphi}(k_{i})+\mathbf{Im}~\sigma(k_{i})~\mathbf{Im}~\tilde{\varphi}(k_{i})~\right)\right).

(262)

At this point one might be worried that because of the non unitarity of the displacement operator, the state
|\sigma\rangle
is unnormalised. However, note that

\displaystyle\langle\sigma|\sigma\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\langle\Omega|\hat{\mathbb{D}}^{\dagger}(\sigma)\hat{\mathbb{D}}(\sigma)|\Omega\rangle}{\langle\Omega|\Omega\rangle}\langle\Omega|\Omega\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\hat{\mathbb{D}}^{\dagger}(\sigma)\hat{\mathbb{D}}(\sigma)\rangle_{\Omega}~\langle\Omega|\Omega\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}\langle\Omega|\Omega\rangle.

(263)

Thus we can in principle define the normalised state as

\displaystyle|\sigma^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{N}}}|\sigma\rangle.

(264)

But we will stick to the former definition.

To make distinction from coherent states, we refer to these states as
Glauber-Sudarshan states
. Since the set-up of our consideration doesn’t contain any free vacuum we are bound to consider an interacting vacuum and correspondingly a Glauber Sudarshan state.

Appendix B
Functional integral approach for
\varphi^{4}
theory

In this appendix, we elaborate the two point correlator of
\varphi^{4}
theory in 3+1 dimensional quantum field theory for a Klein Gordon field using the method of functional integral. We follow closely the method given in Chap. 9 of Peskin & Schroeder
33
:

\displaystyle\langle\varphi(x_{1})\varphi(x_{2})\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\int[\mathcal{D}\varphi(x)]e^{i\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{tot}}}\varphi(x_{1})\varphi(x_{2})}{\int[\mathcal{D}\varphi(x)]e^{i\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{tot}}}}

(265)

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{tot}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{S}_{0}+\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{int}}=\int d^{4}x\left(\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\mu}\varphi(x)\partial^{\mu}\varphi(x)-\frac{1}{2}m^{2}\varphi^{2}(x)\right)+\frac{\lambda}{4!}\int d^{4}x\varphi^{4}(x).

(266)

The first step is to map the space-time into a square lattice having discrete points and thus the field
\varphi(x)
defined on a continuum of points has to be mapped to
\varphi(x_{i})
defined at the points of the aforementioned lattice. Consequently, the integral
\mathcal{D}\varphi
over the space of all field configurations can be replaced in terms of a finite but large number of integrals over the lattice points

\displaystyle\int[\mathcal{D}\varphi(x)]\rightarrow\prod_{i}\int d\varphi(x_{i})

(267)

The discrete Fourier transform of the field
\varphi(x_{i})
is given by

\displaystyle\varphi(x_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathrm{V}}\sum_{n}e^{-ik_{n}\cdot x_{i}}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{n}),

(268)

where the four dimensional space-time volume
\mathrm{V}=L^{4}
. The periodic boundary condition
\varphi(x_{i})=\varphi(x_{i}+L)
imposed on the fields implies
k_{n}^{\mu}=2\pi n^{\mu}/L
,
n^{\mu}
are the integers. Since we are considering a real Klein Gordon field

\displaystyle\varphi^{\star}(x_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathrm{V}}\sum_{n}e^{ik_{n}\cdot x_{i}}\widetilde{\varphi}^{\star}(k_{n})=\varphi(x_{i}),

(269)

the Fourier coefficients has to obey the constraint
\widetilde{\varphi}^{\star}(k_{n})=\widetilde{\varphi}(-k_{n})
. Therefore, we can consider
\mathbf{Re}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{n})
and
\mathbf{Im}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{n})
with
k_{n}^{0}>0
as independent variables. In terms of these fourier coefficients we can rewrite the integral in (
267
) as

\displaystyle\int[\mathcal{D}\varphi(x)]\rightarrow\prod_{k^{0}_{n}>0}\int d~\mathbf{Re}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{n})d~\mathbf{Im}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{n})

(270)

Likewise, the free part of the action (
266
) can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{0}

\displaystyle=\int d^{4}x\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_{\mu}\varphi(x)\partial^{\mu}\varphi(x)-m^{2}\varphi^{2}(x)\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\mathrm{V}^{2}}\sum_{m,n}\int d^{4}x\frac{1}{2}~e^{-i(k_{m}+k_{n})\cdot x}(k_{m\mu}k_{n}^{\mu}+m^{2})\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{m})\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{n})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\mathrm{V}}\sum_{n}\frac{1}{2}(m^{2}-k_{n}^{2})|\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{n})|^{2}=-\frac{1}{\mathrm{V}}\sum_{k_{n}^{0}>0}(m^{2}-k_{n}^{2})|\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{n})|^{2},

(271)

and the interaction part can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{int}}=\frac{\lambda}{4!}\int d^{4}x\varphi^{4}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{4!}\frac{1}{\mathrm{V}^{4}}\sum_{m,n,p,q}\int d^{4}xe^{-i(k_{m}+k_{n}+k_{p}+k_{q})\cdot x}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{m})\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{n})\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{p})\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{q})

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{4!}\frac{1}{\mathrm{V}^{3}}\sum_{m,n,p}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{m})\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{n})\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{p})\widetilde{\varphi}^{\star}(k_{m}+k_{n}+k_{p}).

(272)

With these ingredients we now analyse the numerator of (
265
)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Num}[\langle\varphi(x_{1})\varphi(x_{2})\rangle]=\int[\mathcal{D}\varphi(x)]e^{i\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{tot}}}\varphi(x_{1})\varphi(x_{2})

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{k_{n}^{0}>0}\int d~\mathbf{Re}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{n})d~\mathbf{Im}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{n})\right)\left(\frac{1}{\mathrm{V}^{2}}\sum_{q,r}e^{-i(k_{q}\cdot x_{1}+k_{r}\cdot x_{2})}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{q})\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{r})\right)

\displaystyle\times\exp[-\frac{i}{\mathrm{V}}\sum_{k_{n}^{0}>0}(m^{2}-k_{n}^{2})|\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{n})|^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{4!}\frac{i}{\mathrm{V}^{3}}\sum_{m,n,p}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{m})\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{n})\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{p})\widetilde{\varphi}^{\star}(k_{m}+k_{n}+k_{p})\bigg]

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{k_{n}^{0}>0}\int d~\mathbf{Re}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{n})d~\mathbf{Im}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{n})\right)\exp[-\frac{i}{\mathrm{V}}\sum_{k_{n}^{0}>0}(m^{2}-k_{n}^{2})\left[(\mathbf{Re}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{n}))^{2}+(\mathbf{Im}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{n}))^{2}\right]\bigg]

\displaystyle~~~~~\times\left(\frac{1}{\mathrm{V}^{2}}\sum_{q,r}e^{-i(k_{q}\cdot x_{1}+k_{r}\cdot x_{2})}(\mathbf{Re}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{q})+i\mathbf{Im}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{q}))(\mathbf{Re}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{r})+i\mathbf{Im}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{r}))\right)

\displaystyle~~~~~\times\bigg(1+\frac{\lambda}{4!}\frac{i}{\mathrm{V}^{3}}\sum_{m,n,p}\left(\mathbf{Re}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{m})+i\mathbf{Im}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{m})\right)\left(\mathbf{Re}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{n})+i\mathbf{Im}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{n})\right)

\displaystyle~~~~~\left(\mathbf{Re}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{p})+i\mathbf{Im}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{p})\right)\left(\mathbf{Re}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{m}+k_{n}+k_{p})-i\mathbf{Im}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{m}+k_{n}+k_{p})\right)+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{2})\bigg)

(273)

The tree level contribution to the above integral is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{Num}[\langle\varphi(x_{1})\varphi(x_{2})\rangle]\bigg|_{\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{0})}

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{k_{n}^{0}>0}\int d~\mathbf{Re}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{n})d~\mathbf{Im}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{n})\exp[-\frac{i}{\mathrm{V}}(m^{2}-k_{n}^{2})\left[(\mathbf{Re}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{n}))^{2}+(\mathbf{Im}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{n}))^{2}\right]\bigg]\right)

\displaystyle~~~~~\times\left(\frac{1}{\mathrm{V}^{2}}\sum_{q,r}e^{-i(k_{q}\cdot x_{1}+k_{r}\cdot x_{2})}(\mathbf{Re}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{q})+i\mathbf{Im}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{q}))(\mathbf{Re}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{r})+i\mathbf{Im}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{r}))\right)

(274)

From the structure of the integrand it is obvious that for the values of
k_{q}\neq k_{r}
the integral evaluates to zero. The result may become non vanishing for
k_{q}=\pm k_{r}
. Below we analyse these two cases separately.

Case 1:

\bm{k_{q}=+k_{r}}

\displaystyle\mathrm{Num}[\langle\varphi(x_{1})\varphi(x_{2})\rangle]\bigg|^{\mathrm{Case~1}}_{\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{0})}

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{k_{n}^{0}>0}\int d~\mathbf{Re}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{n})d~\mathbf{Im}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{n})\exp[-\frac{i}{\mathrm{V}}(m^{2}-k_{n}^{2})\left[(\mathbf{Re}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{n}))^{2}+(\mathbf{Im}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{n}))^{2}\right]\bigg]\right)

\displaystyle~~\times\left(\frac{1}{\mathrm{V}^{2}}\sum_{q}e^{-ik_{q}\cdot(x_{1}+x_{2})}((\mathbf{Re}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{q}))^{2}-(\mathbf{Im}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{q}))^{2}+2i\mathbf{Re}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{q})\mathbf{Im}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{q}))\right)

(275)

The third term
\mathbf{Re}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{q})\mathbf{Im}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{q})
makes the integrand odd and hence doesn’t contribute to the integral. The remaining two terms viz.
(\mathbf{Re}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{q}))^{2}
and
(\mathbf{Im}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{q}))^{2}
contribute equally to the integral and hence the net result vanishes.

Case 2:

\bm{k_{q}=-k_{r}}

\displaystyle\mathrm{Num}[\langle\varphi(x_{1})\varphi(x_{2})\rangle]\bigg|^{\mathrm{Case~2}}_{\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{0})}

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{k_{n}^{0}>0}\int d~\mathbf{Re}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{n})d~\mathbf{Im}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{n})\exp[-\frac{i}{\mathrm{V}}(m^{2}-k_{n}^{2})\left[(\mathbf{Re}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{n}))^{2}+(\mathbf{Im}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{n}))^{2}\right]\bigg]\right)

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~\times\left(\frac{1}{\mathrm{V}^{2}}\sum_{q}e^{-ik_{q}\cdot(x_{1}-x_{2})}((\mathbf{Re}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{q}))^{2}+(\mathbf{Im}\widetilde{\varphi}(k_{q}))^{2})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathrm{V}^{2}}\sum_{q}e^{-ik_{q}\cdot(x_{1}-x_{2})}\left(\prod_{k_{n}^{0}>0}\frac{-i\pi\mathrm{V}}{m^{2}-k_{n}^{2}}\right)\frac{-i\mathrm{V}}{m^{2}-k_{q}^{2}}

(276)

The factor similar to that in the parentheses of last line also appears from the denominator of the path integral (
265
) and hence gets cancelled out once we take into account the denominator as well. Now, if we take the continuum limit (of the remaining terms),
\mathrm{V}\rightarrow\infty
we get the tree level contribution to be

\displaystyle\mathrm{Num}[\langle\varphi(x_{1})\varphi(x_{2})\rangle]\bigg|_{\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{0})}

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{k_{n}^{0}>0}\frac{-i\pi\mathrm{V}}{m^{2}-k_{n}^{2}}\right)\int\frac{d^{4}k}{(2\pi)^{4}}\frac{ie^{-ik\cdot(x_{1}-x_{2})}}{k^{2}-m^{2}}.

(277)

which is nothing but the Feynman propagator upto an overall factor. In a similar vein one can analyse the quantum corrections. We follow this method extensively in Section
4
to evaluate one point function with respect to an excited state.

Appendix D
Volume of the internal manifold

Consider the six-dimensional internal space metric (

\displaystyle ds^{2}_{6}

\displaystyle=\delta_{\alpha\beta}\left(dy^{\alpha}+\mathcal{B}^{(1)\alpha}_{~~~m}dy^{m}\right)\left(dy^{\beta}+\mathcal{B}^{(1)\beta}_{~~~n}dy^{n}\right)+\mathrm{H}^{4}(y)\mathrm{F}_{1}(t)\mathrm{F}_{2}(t)g_{mn}dy^{m}dy^{n}

The vectors

\tilde{\mathbf{S}}^{1}_{4}\times\tilde{\mathbf{S}}^{1}_{5}

\mathcal{M}_{4}

The six dimensional metric can be written in block form as

g_{MN}=\begin{pmatrix}\delta_{\alpha\beta}&~~\mathcal{B}^{(1)\gamma}{}_{n}\delta_{\alpha\gamma}\\
\mathcal{B}^{(1)~\gamma}_{~~m}\delta_{\gamma\beta}&~~Ag_{mn}+\mathcal{B}^{(1)}_{~~m\gamma}\delta^{\gamma\tilde{\gamma}}\mathcal{B}^{(1)}_{~~\tilde{\gamma}n}\end{pmatrix},~~\mathrm{where}~M,N=4,5,6,\cdots,9.

Using the standard block determinant identity

\displaystyle\det\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{P}&\mathrm{Q}\\
\mathrm{Q}^{\mathrm{T}}&~~\mathrm{S}\end{pmatrix}=\det(\mathrm{P} )\,\det( \mathrm{S} - \mathrm{Q}^{\mathrm{T}} \mathrm{P}^{-1} \mathrm{Q}),

(312)

for invertible
\mathrm{P}
and identifying

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}_{2\times 2}=\delta_{\alpha\beta},~~\mathrm{Q}_{2\times 4}=\mathcal{B}^{(1)}_{~~\alpha n},~~\mathrm{S}_{4\times 4}=Ag_{mn}+\mathcal{B}^{(1)}_{~~m\gamma}\delta^{\gamma\tilde{\gamma}}\mathcal{B}^{(1)}_{~~\tilde{\gamma}n}

(313)

we find

\displaystyle\mathrm{det}(g_{MN})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{det}(\delta_{\alpha\beta})\mathrm{det}(Ag_{mn}+\mathcal{B}^{(1)}_{~~m\gamma}\delta^{\gamma\tilde{\gamma}}\mathcal{B}^{(1)}_{~~\tilde{\gamma}n}-\mathcal{B}^{(1)}_{~~m\alpha}\delta^{\alpha{\beta}}\mathcal{B}^{(1)}_{~~{\beta}n})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{det}(\delta_{\alpha\beta})\mathrm{det}(Ag_{mn})=A^{4}\mathrm{det}(g_{mn}).

(314)

It shows that the contribution due to the KK vector cancels out. Therefore, the internal six-dimensional volume is

\mathrm{Vol}_{6}(t)=\int d^{6}y\,\sqrt{g_{6}(y,t)}=\int d^{6}y\;\,\big(\mathrm{H}^{4}(y)\mathrm{F}_{1}(t)\mathrm{F}_{2}(t)\big)^{2}\,\sqrt{g_{4}(y)}.

Differential-form interpretation

With the introduction of the shifted one-forms

\eta^{\alpha}=dy^{\alpha}+\mathcal{B}^{(1)\alpha}_{~~~m}dy^{m},

the metric takes the form

ds_{6}^{2}=\delta_{\alpha\beta}\,\eta^{\alpha}\eta^{\beta}+A(y,t)g_{mn}(y)dy^{m}dy^{n}

The six-dimensional volume form is proportional to

\displaystyle\mathrm{Vol}_{6}

\displaystyle\sim\eta^{4}\wedge\eta^{5}\wedge dy^{6}\wedge dy^{7}\wedge dy^{8}\wedge dy^{9}

\displaystyle=(dy^{4}+\mathcal{B}^{(1)\alpha}_{~~~m}dy^{m})\wedge(dy^{5}+\mathcal{B}^{(1)5}_{~~~n}dy^{n})\wedge dy^{6}\wedge dy^{7}\wedge dy^{8}\wedge dy^{9}

\displaystyle=dy^{4}\wedge dy^{5}\wedge dy^{6}\wedge dy^{7}\wedge dy^{8}\wedge dy^{9},

(315)

since the wedge product is completely anti-symmetric, the terms involving the KK vectors do not contribute to the volume form.

Geometric interpretation

The KK vectors
B^{\alpha}{}_{m}
act as connection one-forms on the fiber bundle.
They describe how the internal two-torus is twisted over the four-dimensional
base manifold. A connection affects parallel transport and curvature, but not
local volumes. Consequently, the internal volume depends only on the base metric

Ag_{mn}
and the fiber metric
\delta_{\alpha\beta}
, and not on the KK
connection.
