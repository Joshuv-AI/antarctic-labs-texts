Methodological considerations regarding dorsal cortex drilling in distal radius fracture surgery: a critical appraisal on “Is dorsal cortex drilling necessary for distal radius fractures treated with a volar locking plate? A comparative study of near-cortex-only and far-cortex drilling”
Authors: Ahmet Acar
Published: 2026

We read with great interest the 2025 study by Kim et al. [1], which evaluated whether dorsal cortex drilling is necessary in distal radius fractures treated with a volar locking plate. The study offers valuable insights into surgical technique and the prevention of extensor tendon–related complications. Although its findings contribute meaningfully to the literature on plating strategies, several methodological aspects warrant further clarification, particularly with respect to study design, patient selection, and potential confounding variables. The following comments are intended to support constructive academic discussion and improve methodological transparency.

PATIENT SELECTION AND STUDY DESIGN

The authors state that patients were “randomly assigned” to the two groups. In retrospective analyses, however, group allocation is typically based on predefined clinical criteria rather than prospective randomization. Clarifying this terminology would therefore improve methodological clarity and reduce the risk of misinterpreting the study design. In addition, because the study was retrospective, control over potential confounding variables was inherently limited; this limitation should be considered when interpreting the reported findings.

UNCLEAR FRACTURE TYPE

Although the fracture classification was specified as AO type C, longer screws are often preferred in fractures with dorsal cortical fragmentation to achieve adequate fixation stability. AO type C fractures indicate intra-articular involvement but do not define dorsal cortical morphology, fragment size, or the presence and extent of dorsal comminution.

Faruque [2] reported a 2.5% rate of extensor tendon rupture in the nonsurgical group after distal radius fracture, whereas no rupture was observed in the surgical group. This finding is cited here to provide general context regarding extensor tendon complications after distal radius fractures, rather than as direct evidence concerning dorsal cortical fragmentation. Similarly, several studies have reported that extensor tendon injury is more common after dorsal fragmentation [3,4].

Therefore, relying solely on the AO type C classification when evaluating extensor tendon complications may not adequately capture fracture characteristics relevant to tendon injury risk. Because dorsal cortical fragmentation was not specifically reported in the original study, it should be considered a potential unmeasured confounder and may require further clarification in future studies.

EFFECT OF SCREW LENGTH ON FRACTURE STABILITY

Although all patients in the study had intra-articular fractures classified as AO type C, short screws were used in group A. Wall et al. [5] reported that screws with a length equivalent to 75% of the distal radius transverse length provided stability comparable to that of bicortical screws, whereas 75% fixation provided clinically superior stability compared with 50% fixation. They also noted that 50% screw fixation resulted in loss of stability under 200 N of force, which is well below the minimum 250 N required to safely perform light finger movements. Similarly, another study that applied axial compression and bending loads to distal radius fractures found that the clinical failure rate was significantly higher in the 50% fixation group than in the 75% and bicortical fixation groups [6]. In the current study, screw length in the short-screw group, group A, was reported to range from 38% to 60%.

These biomechanical findings are presented as theoretical considerations that may provide context for interpreting the possible implications of short-screw fixation. However, the original study did not show increased loss of reduction in the short-screw group, indicating that these experimental concerns were not reflected in measurable clinical instability in this cohort.

RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN SCREW LENGTH AND EXTENSOR TENDON INJURY

In the study, standard 12-mm screws were used in the short-screw group, whereas screws 2 mm shorter than the measured length were used in the long-screw group with dorsal cortex drilling. Several studies recommend using screws 2 mm shorter than the measured length to reduce the risk of extensor tendon irritation [7–10]. The two screw strategies differ technically: group A used fixed-length 12-mm screws with near-cortex drilling, whereas group B used measured-length screws shortened by 2 mm after dorsal cortex penetration. Therefore, their biological and mechanical implications may not be directly comparable. In addition, these technical differences, particularly the presence or absence of dorsal cortex drilling, may have influenced the observed complication rates, suggesting that the outcomes cannot be attributed solely to screw length. Because dorsal cortical morphology and drilling penetration were not specifically reported in the original study, definitive conclusions regarding the mechanism of extensor tendon complications cannot be drawn.

CONCLUSIONS

Although the study by Kim et al. [1] provides valuable clinical insights, consideration of these methodological issues may improve interpretation of the findings and clarify their generalizability. We believe that these points may contribute to ongoing discussion and future research in this field.
