Endoscopic accessibility and conduit passage after retrosternal vs. posterior mediastinal gastric conduit reconstruction following McKeown esophagectomy: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Yiming Yao, Dong Jiang, Yifan Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe optimal route for gastric-conduit reconstruction after McKeown esophagectomy remains uncertain. Previous comparisons have focused primarily on anastomotic leakage and pulmonary morbidity, whereas postoperative endoscopic accessibility and clinically evident conduit-passage disturbance have been less well characterized. We compared retrosternal (RS) and posterior mediastinal (PM) reconstruction, focusing on these functional outcomes.MethodsThis single-center retrospective cohort included 37 patients treated by the same surgical team between March 2025 and April 2026 (RS, n=14; PM, n=23). Upper gastrointestinal contrast assessment was generally performed at approximately 2 weeks and routine postoperative endoscopy at approximately 3 weeks, unless endoscopy was contraindicated or not feasible. Endoscopy time and route-related difficulty were evaluated at the examination level. Clinically evident passage disturbance, defined as dysphagia with retained food or impaired bolus transit requiring endoscopic confirmation or treatment, was evaluated at the patient level during 3 months of follow-up. Early and late delayed gastric conduit emptying (DGCE) and secondary perioperative outcomes were also assessed. All comparisons were unadjusted and exploratory.ResultsAmong examinations with recorded duration (13 RS and 24 PM), median endoscopy time was longer after RS reconstruction [14.00 (8.00, 17.00) vs. 7.50 (6.00, 11.00) minutes, P=0.01]. Route-related difficulty in scope advancement or incomplete anastomotic visualization was documented in four RS examinations and in none of the 24 completed PM examinations. Four RS patients developed clinically evident passage disturbance with dysphagia and retained food, compared with no PM patients (4/14 vs. 0/23, P=0.02). All four events were associated with anastomotic stricture; retained food was removed endoscopically, followed by dilation in three patients and stent placement in one, and all subsequently resumed oral intake. Anastomotic stricture occurred in 4/14 RS and 1/23 PM patients (P=0.057). No statistically detectable between-group differences were observed for early DGCE (1/14 vs. 1/23; P>0.99), late DGCE (2/14 vs. 0/23; P=0.14), or anastomotic leakage (1/14 vs. 2/23; P>0.99).ConclusionsRS reconstruction was associated with longer examination-level endoscopy time and four stricture-related passage-disturbance events in this small cohort, whereas no statistically detectable route-related difference in DGCE was observed. These unadjusted findings are hypothesis-generating and do not establish the superiority of either route. Larger prospective studies incorporating standardized endoscopic assessment, route-geometry measures, validated dysphagia instruments, and longitudinal functional outcomes are warranted.Keywords: McKeown esophagectomy, gastric conduit, retrosternal reconstruction (RS reconstruction), posterior mediastinal reconstruction (PM reconstruction), endoscopy

Highlight box

Key findings

• In 37 patients, retrosternal (RS) reconstruction was associated with longer episode-level endoscopy time and more difficult or abnormal endoscopy reports. Four RS patients, but no posterior mediastinal patients, required endoscopic extraction of retained food. These unadjusted findings are exploratory.

What is known and what is new?

• Prior route-comparison studies have emphasized leakage, pulmonary morbidity, and survival.

• This study adds a focused, real-world description of postoperative endoscopic accessibility and clinically evident conduit-passage disturbance, while explicitly separating patient-level from examination-level denominators.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The results do not justify a preferred route. They support documenting route-selection criteria, anticipating route-specific endoscopic difficulty, and using complementary radiographic and endoscopic evaluation when passage symptoms occur. Confirmation requires standardized prospective follow-up.

Introduction

McKeown esophagectomy with cervical esophagogastric anastomosis is widely used for resectable thoracic esophageal cancer. The gastric conduit may be elevated through the posterior mediastinal (PM) route, which is anatomically direct, or through a retrosternal (RS) tunnel, which separates the conduit from the PM dissection field. Comparative studies and meta-analyses have not established a universally superior route, and route selection remains influenced by anatomy, oncologic considerations, prior treatment, and institutional practice (1-9).

Postoperative endoscopy provides direct assessment of the anastomosis, conduit mucosa, ischemia, stricture, and retained food and can permit immediate therapy. Route geometry may affect the feasibility of this examination. An RS conduit is longer and may be angulated or externally compressed near the thoracic inlet or sternoclavicular joint, potentially limiting scope advancement and bolus transit (10-13). By contrast, a PM conduit generally follows the esophageal bed. Previous research has focused mainly on leakage, pulmonary complications, and survival; endoscopic accessibility and clinically evident passage disturbance have received less attention.

We therefore compared RS and PM reconstruction after McKeown esophagectomy, with postoperative endoscopic accessibility and clinically evident conduit-passage disturbance as the central outcomes. Secondary perioperative variables were retained to characterize the cohort but were interpreted as exploratory. We hypothesized that route-related geometry could influence endoscopy time, ease of anastomotic visualization, and postoperative bolus passage. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1615/rc) (14).

Methods

Study design and patients

This single-center retrospective cohort study included patients treated by the same thoracic surgical team at the Esophageal Cancer Center, Department of Thoracic Surgery, The First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University, between March 2025 and April 2026. Patients were grouped according to reconstruction route: RS reconstruction (observation group, n=14) and PM reconstruction through the esophageal bed (control group, n=23).

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) histologically confirmed esophageal malignancy by endoscopic biopsy; (II) McKeown esophagectomy with cervical esophagogastric anastomosis performed at our institution; (III) preoperative staging by contrast-enhanced neck/chest/abdominal computed tomography (CT), brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) when indicated, and positron emission tomography (PET)-CT when clinically necessary, without unresectable distant metastasis; (IV) preoperative evaluation suggesting tolerance of surgery, including blood tests, coagulation and biochemical tests, electrocardiography, echocardiography, pulmonary function testing, and coronary CT angiography when indicated; (V) no history of severe thoracic trauma or major thoracic surgery; (VI) complete clinical data and postoperative follow-up information.

The exclusion criteria were: (I) prior severe thoracic trauma or thoracic surgery precluding standard three-incision esophagectomy; (II) other active malignancy or distant metastasis not suitable for surgery; (III) severe coagulopathy, immunodeficiency, severe cardiac, cerebral, pulmonary, renal, or gastrointestinal dysfunction, or uncontrolled systemic infection; and (IV) inability to cooperate with postoperative assessment or follow-up because of cognitive or psychiatric impairment.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University [approval No. (2026) Lun Shen Pi No. 644]. The requirement for informed consent was waived because of the retrospective design.

Route selection and neoadjuvant treatment

Reconstruction route was not randomized. The reconstruction route was determined intraoperatively by the attending thoracic surgeon following comprehensive evaluation of both oncological and anatomical conditions. The decision was generally made during thoracic esophageal mobilization after assessment of the posterior mediastinum. Major considerations included tumor stage and extent of invasion, PM conditions (including adhesions, fibrosis, or residual disease), thoracic inlet anatomy, gastric conduit length and blood supply, anticipated need for postoperative radiotherapy, and the surgeon’s intraoperative judgment and experience. Tumor location was considered a reference factor but was not the primary determinant of route selection.

Patients with extensive PM tumor involvement, severe PM adhesions or fibrosis (particularly after neoadjuvant radiotherapy or previous thoracic surgery), or those anticipated to require postoperative radiotherapy to the esophageal bed were more likely to undergo RS reconstruction to avoid placing the gastric conduit within an irradiated or compromised posterior mediastinum. Because the RS route is generally longer than the PM route, adequate gastric conduit length and satisfactory blood supply were carefully confirmed before RS reconstruction was performed.

When PM conditions were favorable and conduit length and perfusion were considered adequate, PM reconstruction was generally preferred because it provides the shortest reconstruction route with less conduit tension. The final reconstruction route was determined according to the surgeon’s comprehensive intraoperative assessment and clinical judgment. The institutional strategy regarding reconstruction route selection remained unchanged throughout the study period. Accordingly, all between-route comparisons represent unadjusted associations rather than causal effects.

Neoadjuvant treatment was determined by the institutional multidisciplinary team (MDT) according to tumor stage and patient characteristics rather than reconstruction route. Detailed information regarding the neoadjuvant treatment regimen and treatment cycles for all patients receiving preoperative therapy is provided in Table S1.

Surgical procedure

All patients underwent minimally invasive three-incision McKeown esophagectomy under general anesthesia. The thoracic phase was performed thoracoscopically using single-lumen endotracheal intubation with right bronchial blockade when required. The esophagus was mobilized from the thoracic inlet to the diaphragmatic hiatus, the azygos arch was divided, and mediastinal lymphadenectomy was performed according to tumor location and clinical indications. The abdominal phase was completed laparoscopically. Gastric mobilization preserved the right gastroepiploic vascular arcade, and a greater-curvature-based tubular gastric conduit was fashioned.

The gastric conduit was routinely constructed with a target width of approximately 4 cm using serial linear staplers along the lesser curvature. The conduit length was individualized according to gastric morphology while ensuring adequate reach to the cervical incision. In patients with a relatively short stomach, a Z-shaped stapling technique was occasionally adopted to maximize conduit length, whereas conventional linear stapling was sufficient in patients with a relatively long stomach. The final conduit length was generally approximately 30 cm. Staple-line reinforcement with purse-string sutures was routinely performed after conduit construction. No pyloric intervention, including pyloroplasty or pyloric dilatation, was performed.

For conduit elevation, the gastric apex was connected to a previously positioned guiding drainage tube using traction sutures, and the conduit was gently advanced to the neck by senior thoracic surgeons experienced in esophageal reconstruction. Before elevation, the conduit was lubricated with sterile paraffin oil. Continuous orientation markings were placed along the staple line, and the conduit was secured with multiple fixation sutures to minimize conduit rotation during pull-up. Conduit orientation was sequentially confirmed at the diaphragmatic hiatus, mid-mediastinum, thoracic inlet, and immediately before cervical anastomosis. If excessive resistance was encountered, traction was discontinued until torsion or external compression had been excluded.

In the PM group, the esophageal hiatus was bluntly enlarged to approximately three fingerbreadths, and the conduit was delivered through the PM esophageal bed to the left cervical incision. In the RS group, an RS tunnel was established laparoscopically by opening the retroxiphoid substernal space through a small upper abdominal incision, followed by blunt dissection to connect with the cervical incision. The thoracic inlet was bluntly enlarged during tunnel creation to facilitate conduit passage while minimizing compression. No additional decompression procedures or other specific thoracic inlet enlargement techniques were performed.

A left cervical incision of approximately 6 cm was made along the anterior border of the sternocleidomastoid muscle. Cervical esophagogastric anastomosis was routinely performed using a 21-mm circular stapler (ECS21A, CDH21A, or ICS21 according to intraoperative availability). Because of normal anatomical variation among patients, the exact cervical anastomotic level differed slightly between individuals. Therefore, the anatomical position of the cervical anastomosis relative to the thoracic inlet was retrospectively collected for all 37 patients and analyzed as a standardized operative parameter. Gastric decompression and enteral-feeding tubes were routinely placed, and thoracic, mediastinal, and abdominal drainage tubes were inserted according to the reconstruction route and intraoperative findings. All procedures were performed by the same experienced thoracic surgical team using a standardized operative protocol throughout the study period.

Postoperative evaluation, endoscopy, and oral intake

Postoperative assessment included clinical examination, laboratory testing, chest CT, upper gastrointestinal contrast study, and endoscopy according to the institutional postoperative pathway and the patient’s clinical condition.

At our institution, postoperative endoscopy is a routine component of the standardized postoperative management protocol following McKeown esophagectomy and is generally scheduled at approximately 3 weeks after surgery to evaluate anastomotic healing. Endoscopy was performed unless contraindicated, including patient refusal, inability to safely tolerate the examination because of severe cardiopulmonary instability, suspected gastrointestinal perforation with hemodynamic instability, or other conditions judged unsuitable by the treating physicians. Patients who did not undergo endoscopy were evaluated using upper gastrointestinal contrast studies when clinically appropriate.

Endoscopy was performed transorally using a standard electronic gastroscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) by experienced attending gastroenterologists or senior endoscopists. CO2 insufflation was routinely used. The examination evaluated anastomotic healing, mucosal color and viability, anastomotic patency, anastomotic stricture, and retained food, while therapeutic interventions were performed when indicated. Throughout the procedure, oxygen saturation, heart rate, and blood pressure were continuously monitored. Gentle scope advancement with minimal insufflation was adopted to avoid excessive distension of the gastric conduit and cervical anastomosis.

The endoscopy analysis distinguished patient-level and examination-level denominators. Endoscopy rate was calculated per patient, whereas examination time and report characteristics were summarized per examination because several patients underwent two examinations. “Difficult or abnormal” reports comprised difficult intubation or scope advancement, incomplete visualization of the anastomosis, or an anastomotic stricture that was barely traversable. Examination times were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test without adjustment for within-patient correlation.

Of the seven patients who did not undergo postoperative endoscopy (RS, n=3; PM, n=4), one patient in the PM group was unable to cooperate with endoscopy and was classified as not having undergone postoperative endoscopy for analysis; one had an anastomotic leak and was evaluated only by upper gastrointestinal contrast study after healing; three patients (RS, n=1; PM, n=2) had already resumed oral intake uneventfully after postoperative contrast examination and therefore did not undergo routine endoscopy; and two RS patients were considered unsuitable for endoscopy because of poor general condition.

Prior studies have evaluated protocolized postoperative endoscopy for mucosal ischemia and anastomotic integrity and have compared endoscopy with CT or contrast assessment (15-17). Kobayashi et al. performed endoscopy on postoperative days 1 and 8 using a predefined mucosal ischemic index (15). Although the timing differed, postoperative endoscopy in the present study was likewise performed according to a standardized institutional protocol rather than solely at the discretion of the treating physicians.

Available reports documented one RS examination in which withdrawal of the endoscope displaced the gastric decompression and enteral-feeding tubes. No other major procedure-related complications were documented in the available medical records. Because a prospective endoscopy-safety registry was not maintained, the absence of additional report entries cannot be interpreted as proof that no other minor adverse events occurred.

At our institution, upper gastrointestinal contrast study is routinely performed at approximately 2 weeks after McKeown esophagectomy, followed by routine postoperative endoscopy at approximately 3 weeks unless contraindicated or declined by the patient. Patients with contraindications to endoscopy or who were unable to cooperate underwent upper gastrointestinal contrast study instead to evaluate the anastomosis before oral intake. Oral intake was resumed only after endoscopy (or contrast study when endoscopy was not feasible) confirmed the absence of an anastomotic leak. Patients were initially allowed small amounts of warm water, followed by a gradual progression to a liquid diet after 2–3 days if no fever, nausea, vomiting, or other clinical abnormalities developed. Approximately 1 week later, patients without evidence of intolerance progressed to a semi-liquid diet. Patients with newly diagnosed anastomotic leakage remained fasting until healing was confirmed, whereas those with suspected conduit obstruction or anastomotic narrowing underwent further diagnostic and, when indicated, therapeutic endoscopic intervention.

Outcomes

The principal outcome domain was postoperative endoscopic accessibility. Patient-level measures were the proportion undergoing endoscopy and clinically evident conduit-passage disturbance. Examination-level measures were examination time, documented scope-passage difficulty, and completeness of anastomotic visualization.

Clinically evident conduit-passage disturbance was defined as retained food or impaired bolus transit associated with swallowing difficulty and requiring endoscopic confirmation or therapeutic extraction. To avoid ambiguity between clinically evident conduit-passage disturbance (primarily postoperative food retention and dysphagia) and delayed gastric conduit emptying (DGCE), postoperative early and late DGCE were additionally assessed in all 37 patients according to internationally accepted diagnostic criteria for DGCE (18-20). The incidence of DGCE was subsequently compared between the RS and PM reconstruction groups.

Secondary outcomes included anastomotic leakage, anastomotic stricture (postoperative anastomotic stricture was diagnosed primarily on the basis of postoperative upper gastrointestinal contrast studies. Although postoperative anastomotic stricture may be associated with subsequent conduit-passage disturbance during long-term postoperative follow-up, it should not be confused with clinically evident conduit-passage disturbance (manifested as retained food during the 3-month postoperative follow-up) or postoperative DGCE, as these represent distinct clinical entities with different diagnostic criteria), recurrent laryngeal nerve injury confirmed by laryngoscopy, pulmonary infection, arrhythmia, reflux or heartburn, postoperative atelectasis, operative time, blood loss, drainage volume and duration, postoperative fasting duration, and length of hospital stay.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as median (first quartile, third quartile) and compared with the Mann-Whitney U test. Age, body weight, and body mass index (BMI) were retained as mean ± standard deviation in the descriptive baseline table because only aggregate source values were available. Categorical variables were summarized as number (percentage) and compared with the Chi-squared test or Fisher exact test, as appropriate. Two-sided P<0.05 was used in the source analysis.

No multiplicity adjustment was applied despite the number of outcomes. Therefore, all P values, including P=0.01 for examination time and P=0.02 for passage disturbance, were treated as exploratory. Repeated examinations from the same patient were not independent, so the episode-level P value for examination time should be interpreted cautiously.

Propensity-score matching or multivariable adjustment was considered during revision but was not performed. With only 37 patients, an unbalanced 14:23 allocation, few primary events, and incomplete documentation of route-selection covariates, an adjusted model would be unstable and potentially misleading. The analysis therefore remains unadjusted and does not support causal inference.

No imputation of missing data was performed. Analyses were conducted using available-case data, and variable-specific denominators are reported where appropriate.

Results

Cohort and baseline characteristics

The analyzed cohort comprised 14 RS patients (37.8%) and 23 PM patients (62.2%). The RS group included 13 men and 1 woman with a mean age of 71.36±7.39 years; the PM group included 18 men and 5 women with a mean age of 69.70±7.07 years. Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) predominated. Neoadjuvant treatment was recorded in five RS patients and nine PM patients.

Baseline variables are presented descriptively in Table 1. The distributions of pathological stage and documented comorbidity differed numerically between the small groups; for example, stage I disease was recorded in 1/14 RS patients and 11/23 PM patients. These imbalances, together with non-random route selection, preclude an assumption that the groups were exchangeable.

Because postoperative endoscopic accessibility and conduit configuration may be influenced by the location of the cervical anastomosis, the anatomical position of the anastomosis relative to the thoracic inlet was additionally reviewed in all patients. No significant between-group difference was identified (Table S2), indicating that the observed differences in postoperative endoscopic findings were unlikely to be attributable to variations in anastomotic level.

Endoscopic accessibility, conduit-passage disturbance, and postoperative DGCE

Postoperative endoscopy was performed in 11/14 (78.6%) RS patients and 19/23 (82.6%) PM patients. Thus, three RS patients and four PM patients did not undergo postoperative endoscopy. Patients who did not undergo endoscopy were evaluated with upper gastrointestinal contrast studies according to the institutional postoperative protocol. The reasons for not performing endoscopy have been described in the Methods section.

Most patients underwent a single routine postoperative endoscopic examination at approximately 3 weeks after surgery. A small number of patients underwent a repeat endoscopic examination at approximately 4 weeks because of postoperative clinical recovery or when further confirmation of the initial endoscopic findings was considered clinically appropriate. A total of 13 RS and 24 PM endoscopic examinations were analyzed; examination time was available for all 13 RS examinations and 24 PM examinations. The median examination time was 14.00 (8.00, 17.00) minutes in the RS group and 7.50 (6.00, 11.00) minutes in the PM group (Z=−2.45, P=0.01). Difficult or abnormal endoscopic findings were documented in 4/13 RS examinations and 0/24 PM examinations. Because several patients underwent two endoscopic examinations, examination-level results should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Documented difficulties in the RS group included difficult advancement of the endoscope and incomplete visualization of the anastomosis. During withdrawal of the endoscope in that examination, the gastric decompression tube and enteral-feeding tube were inadvertently dislodged. By contrast, PM reconstruction generally provided smoother scope advancement and clearer direct visualization of the anastomotic region. These findings suggest that reconstruction route may influence the feasibility of postoperative endoscopic surveillance. During the 3-month postoperative follow-up after esophagectomy, four patients in the RS group developed clinically evident conduit-passage disturbance with dysphagia and retained food. All four patients initially underwent endoscopic extraction of the retained food. Subsequently, one patient underwent esophageal stent placement in the Department of Interventional Radiology, whereas the remaining three patients underwent endoscopic esophageal dilation as secondary postoperative interventions. Following these interventions, all patients were kept nil per os for 24 hours, received supportive care including intravenous fluid therapy, and subsequently resumed oral intake successfully. No comparable event was observed in the PM group [4/14 (28.6%) vs. 0/23; Fisher’s exact P=0.02].

Early and late postoperative DGCE were additionally assessed in all 37 patients according to the internationally accepted diagnostic criteria, and the incidence of postoperative DGCE was compared between the two reconstruction routes. No statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups in the incidence of either early or late postoperative DGCE. These findings suggest that the choice of reconstruction route alone may not be a major determinant of postoperative gastric conduit emptying. Instead, the development of DGCE is likely multifactorial, involving factors such as gastric conduit motility, pyloric function, vagal denervation, conduit geometry, and perioperative management. However, given the limited sample size of this study, these findings should be interpreted with caution and warrant confirmation in larger prospective studies.

Patients diagnosed with postoperative DGCE initially received conservative treatment, including dietary modification, enteral nutritional support, and prokinetic therapy when clinically indicated. Patients with persistent symptoms underwent further assessment by upper gastrointestinal contrast studies and/or endoscopy. When postoperative conduit dysfunction was associated with anastomotic stricture or retained food, appropriate endoscopic interventions, including food extraction, balloon dilation, or stent placement when clinically indicated, were performed. Routine pyloric intervention was not performed during the primary operation.

Secondary complications and recovery outcomes

Anastomotic leakage occurred in 1/14 RS patients and 2/23 PM patients (P>0.99). Anastomotic stricture was recorded in 4/14 RS patients and 1/23 PM patients (P=0.057). The clinically evident conduit-passage disturbance was secondary to anastomotic stricture, resulting in retained food and dysphagia. Although postoperative anastomotic stricture was also observed in the PM group, the upper gastrointestinal contrast studies demonstrated only mild anastomotic narrowing (Figure 10). Accordingly, no patients in the PM group developed clinically evident conduit-passage disturbance, including retained food or dysphagia, during postoperative follow-up. Other recorded complications did not differ statistically (Table 3). Given the sample size and number of comparisons, these analyses are descriptive and underpowered.

Discussion

Key findings

This study examined postoperative endoscopic accessibility and clinically evident conduit passage after RS vs. PM gastric-conduit reconstruction following McKeown esophagectomy. Among examinations with recorded duration, endoscopy took approximately twice as long after RS reconstruction as after PM reconstruction, and route-related difficulty with scope advancement or anastomotic visualization was documented in four RS examinations but in none of the 24 completed PM examinations. During the 3-month postoperative follow-up, four RS patients developed dysphagia with retained food requiring endoscopic extraction, compared with no PM patients. In this cohort, all four passage-disturbance events were associated with anastomotic stricture and were successfully managed by food extraction followed by endoscopic dilation in three patients and stent placement in one. By contrast, no statistically detectable between-group differences were observed in early or late DGCE or anastomotic leakage. The differences in thoracic and mediastinal drainage profiles were secondary exploratory findings and were not accompanied by statistically detectable differences in fasting duration or hospital stay.

The unit of analysis is important when interpreting these findings. Passage disturbance and DGCE were patient-level outcomes, whereas examination time and technical difficulty were examination-level outcomes. Because several patients underwent repeat endoscopy and within-patient correlation was not modeled, the P value for examination time may overstate precision. In addition, all P values were unadjusted for multiple comparisons. The findings should therefore be viewed as signals that inform future study design rather than as definitive evidence favoring one reconstruction route.

Strengths and limitations

Several features strengthen the clinical interpretability of this study. All operations were performed by the same experienced team during a relatively short period using a standardized minimally invasive McKeown approach. Gastric-conduit construction, orientation checks, RS tunnel creation, and cervical anastomosis were described in detail (21); both groups received a 21-mm circular stapler, and the measured distance from the thoracic inlet to the anastomosis did not differ statistically. Postoperative contrast assessment and endoscopy were embedded in an institutional pathway, with endoscopy generally scheduled at approximately 3 weeks rather than performed exclusively in response to symptoms. The analysis also separated patient-level from examination-level denominators, applied consensus-based early and late DGCE categories, and documented the subsequent treatment and oral-intake outcome of each clinically evident passage-disturbance event.

The study nevertheless has substantial limitations. First, it was retrospective, single-center, and included only 37 patients. Route assignment was not randomized and remained dependent on intraoperative oncologic and anatomical judgment. The RS group had numerically more advanced disease and more recorded comorbidity, creating a strong possibility of confounding by indication; the available sample and event counts did not support reliable multivariable adjustment or propensity-score analysis. Second, some patients did not complete routine endoscopy, and repeat examinations were not statistically independent. Endoscopist identity, sedation, and detailed technical conditions may also have varied, while difficulty was classified retrospectively from clinical reports rather than by a prospectively validated score. Third, clinically evident passage disturbance was restricted to symptomatic food retention or impaired transit requiring endoscopic management. A validated dysphagia scale, standardized contrast-transit measure, scintigraphic emptying test, and serial patient-reported outcome measures were not available. Fourth, the 3-month window captured early functional events but not recurrent stricture, long-term reflux, nutritional recovery, quality of life, or survival. Finally, the multiple unadjusted comparisons increase the probability of chance findings, and the low event counts produce considerable statistical uncertainty. The absence of a statistically significant difference for DGCE or other complications must not be interpreted as equivalence.

Comparison with similar research

Most previous route-comparison studies have concentrated on anastomotic leakage, pulmonary complications, mortality, and length of stay rather than the feasibility of postoperative endoscopy. The published evidence remains heterogeneous. A large Japanese National Clinical Database analysis identified RS reconstruction as a risk factor for anastomotic leakage and surgical-site infection but PM reconstruction as a risk factor for pneumonia, whereas recent meta-analyses and cohort studies have reached differing conclusions regarding leakage and other short-term outcomes (6-9). A contemporary review similarly emphasized that outcomes reflect not only route designation but also conduit construction, perfusion, thoracic-inlet anatomy, anastomotic technique, and institutional practice (22). The low leakage counts in the present study cannot confirm or refute those larger studies.

The present work adds a different, clinically relevant perspective by examining access through the reconstructed conduit. Published postoperative endoscopy protocols vary in both timing and purpose. Kobayashi et al. performed protocolized endoscopy on postoperative days 1 and 8 and showed that a structured mucosal ischemia index could help predict anastomotic complications, while other studies have evaluated early ultrathin endoscopy or compared routine endoscopy with contrast examination (15-17). In the present pathway, contrast assessment at approximately 2 weeks was followed by endoscopy at approximately 3 weeks to evaluate healing, patency, retained food, and readiness for oral intake. Routine scheduling reduces, but does not eliminate, indication bias because contraindications, non-completion, and clinically prompted repeat examinations remained possible.

Evidence on longer-term function also cautions against equating a technically easier examination with better overall recovery. A small randomized study reported greater radionuclide retention in the middle and entire gastric conduit after RS reconstruction, providing functional plausibility for the present food-retention findings (23). Conversely, a propensity-matched study of standardized laparoscopic RS tunnel creation found broadly comparable postoperative outcomes and anastomotic stricture rates between routes (24), and a longitudinal study using validated EORTC instruments found no adjusted route-related difference across multiple quality-of-life domains (25). Multicenter data have suggested that longer-term weight loss may differ by route even when early morbidity does not (26). Thus, endoscopic accessibility, objective transit, symptoms, nutrition, and quality of life are related but non-interchangeable outcomes.

Explanations of findings

The observed difference in endoscopic access is anatomically plausible. The PM conduit follows the native esophageal bed and is generally the shorter, more direct route. The RS conduit must traverse a newly created substernal tunnel and the thoracic inlet, introducing additional opportunities for angulation, redundancy, traction, or external compression. Comparative anatomical studies have demonstrated route-length differences, and clinical reports have linked compression behind the sternoclavicular joint with anastomotic leakage and stenosis (10-13). CT-based work has further shown that thoracic-inlet dimensions can modify the risk of cervical anastomotic leakage after RS reconstruction (27). Standardized laparoscopic and cervical techniques for tunnel creation are therefore important when comparing results across centers (21,24).

The standardized 21-mm stapler and similar anastomotic level in the two groups make device diameter and vertical anastomotic position less likely to fully explain the present findings. They do not, however, exclude unmeasured differences in conduit curvature, tension, perfusion, tunnel width, or focal compression. The clustering of all four clinically evident passage-disturbance events among patients with anastomotic stricture supports a possible pathway from route geometry and local mechanical stress to narrowing, difficult endoscopic passage, and food retention. This interpretation remains mechanistic and cannot be established from the current observational data.

Clinically evident passage disturbance should also be distinguished from DGCE. The four symptomatic events in the RS group required food extraction and treatment of anastomotic narrowing, whereas DGCE is a broader syndrome defined by postoperative timing, symptoms, and objective evidence and may reflect vagal denervation, conduit dysmotility, pyloric resistance, redundancy, or unfavorable geometry (18-20). The present study did not identify statistically detectable route-related differences in early or late DGCE, but the very small number of events leaves substantial uncertainty. The data therefore support separation of these outcomes rather than the conclusion that reconstruction route has no effect on gastric emptying.

The drainage differences are more likely to reflect route-specific anatomy and management than a direct functional advantage. Mediastinal and thoracic drainage volumes may be influenced by the compartment traversed by the conduit, drain position, dead space, tissue dissection, and removal criteria. Because fasting duration and hospital stay were not statistically different, these drainage findings should remain secondary and should not determine route selection without prospective confirmation.

Implications and actions needed

The results do not justify routine preference for PM reconstruction or abandonment of RS reconstruction. Route selection should remain individualized according to tumor extent, PM conditions, anticipated radiotherapy, conduit length and perfusion, thoracic-inlet anatomy, and surgeon judgment. When RS reconstruction is selected, meticulous creation of an adequately wide tunnel, avoidance of conduit rotation or redundancy, and confirmation of a tension-free course through the thoracic inlet may be particularly important. During postoperative surveillance, clinicians and endoscopists should anticipate potentially greater technical difficulty after RS reconstruction. New dysphagia, regurgitation, or food retention should prompt early assessment for anastomotic stricture and focal conduit angulation or compression, using endoscopy together with contrast imaging when appropriate.

Future studies should be multicenter and prospective, with prespecified route-selection variables and standardized measurements of thoracic-inlet dimensions, conduit geometry, and perfusion. A protocolized endoscopic scoring system should be combined with validated dysphagia and quality-of-life instruments, objective emptying or transit tests, serial nutritional measures, and longer follow-up. Repeated examinations should be analyzed with patient-level longitudinal or correlated-data methods. Such a design would clarify whether the present findings reflect route itself, modifiable technical features of RS reconstruction, or the clinical factors that led surgeons to choose that route.

Conclusions

After McKeown esophagectomy, RS gastric-conduit reconstruction was associated with longer examination-level endoscopy time and four stricture-related passage-disturbance events, whereas no statistically detectable between-group difference in early or late DGCE was observed. These small, unadjusted, non-randomized data suggest that reconstruction geometry may influence postoperative endoscopic access and bolus passage but do not establish the superiority of either route. Route selection should remain individualized, with careful attention to RS tunnel and thoracic-inlet configuration and prompt evaluation of postoperative dysphagia. Larger prospective studies with standardized endoscopic, functional, and patient-reported outcomes are required.
