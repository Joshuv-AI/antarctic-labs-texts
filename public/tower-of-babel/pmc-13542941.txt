Quiescence as an Active and Dynamic Regulator of Neural Stem Cells in Adult Olfactory Neurogenesis
Authors: Jose Manuel Morante‐Redolat, Isabel Fariñas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAdult olfactory neurogenesis depends largely on the persistence of resident quiescent neural stem cells (NSCs) in the ventricular‐subventricular zone (V‐SVZ) and their capacity to reversibly switch to an activated state to generate new neurons. These cells exhibit reduced cell‐cycle and biosynthetic activity while maintaining key signaling and stress‐response programs. Rather than representing a uniform condition, quiescence encompasses a continuum of substates, ranging from deep to shallow, each characterized by distinct activation thresholds. The dynamics and reversibility of state transitions are regulated by interactions between NSCs and neighboring cells, the extracellular matrix, and systemic factors, among others. With aging, NSCs progressively shift toward deeper quiescent states that are less permissive to activation, thereby reducing overall neurogenic output. Consequently, quiescence should no longer be regarded as a passive pause in stem cell activity, but an organizing principle that underlies the long‐term maintenance and adaptability of adult NSCs. A deeper understanding of how quiescence is established, diversified, and dynamically regulated will be essential for elucidating the principles governing adult olfactory neurogenesis and for harnessing its regenerative potential in aging and disease.

Cellular Quiescence: Definitions, Functional Significance and Open Questions

Cellular quiescence is classically defined as a reversible exit from the cell cycle, in which cells reside in a G0 state distinct from both proliferative cycling and terminal differentiation (Coller et al. 2006; Coller 2007). Quiescent cells, however, retain the competence to re‐enter the cell cycle in response to appropriate physiological cues, while actively maintaining mechanisms that prevent unscheduled proliferation or lineage commitment. This reversibility distinguishes quiescence from senescence or terminal differentiation and highlights its importance in tissues that require lifelong regenerative capacity. In adult stem cell systems, quiescence is increasingly recognized as a protective and regulatory strategy rather than a default or passive condition. Far from representing a mere absence of proliferation, quiescence has emerged as a tightly regulated, functional cellular state that is central to stem cell biology across tissues (Cheung and Rando 2013; van Velthoven and Rando 2019). Studies in hematopoietic, muscle, and intestinal stem cells have demonstrated that quiescence preserves genomic integrity, limits replicative stress, and safeguards long‐term regenerative potential (Cheung and Rando 2013; de Morree and Rando 2023). At the same time, excessive or irreversible quiescence can compromise tissue repair and contribute to age‐related functional decline, highlighting the need for precise regulation of this state (Tumpel and Rudolph 2019).

The concept of reversible quiescence inherently implies a dynamic state of transition, whereby quiescent stem cells can be recruited to re‐enter the cell cycle, that is, to become activated, and can subsequently return to quiescence. Accumulating evidence indicates that this switch is far from a simple change in cell cycle phase, instead involving profound reconfiguration of cellular physiology. Moreover, although experimental approaches often require classifying cells as either quiescent or active, the bidirectional nature of these transitions points to a continuum of intermediate states, with cells progressively shifting toward one end or the other (Cheung and Rando 2013; de Morree and Rando 2023). Within this framework, several fundamental questions remain unresolved in stem cell biology. What is the full network of signals and pathways governing the induction, maintenance, and exit from quiescence? Is quiescence a state that can only be acquired following cell division, and is it intrinsically coupled to the molecular machinery of proliferation? To what extent are populations classified as quiescent or active truly homogeneous? How does an active cell that remains in a prolonged early G1 phase differ from one transitioning toward quiescence? In niches where most stem cells persist in a quiescent state throughout life, are cells that have undergone activation and subsequently returned to quiescence functionally or molecularly distinct from those that have never been activated? In other words, do stem cells retain a molecular memory of prior activation events that influences their future behavior or regenerative potential? When and how does protective quiescence become maladaptive, contributing to impaired regeneration or disease?

These, among other open questions, remain at the forefront of current research. We have only just begun to disentangle the complex nature of quiescent stem cells, yet it is undeniable that most stem cell niches cannot be fully understood unless viewed through the lens of the reversible crosstalk between quiescence and activation.

Quiescence in Adult Neural Stem Cells: General Principles

Adult neural stem cells (NSCs) are maintained throughout life in specialized neurogenic niches of the mammalian brain, where they sustain persistent neurogenesis by balancing long‐term self‐preservation with production of progeny. The best‐characterized neurogenic niches in the adult mouse brain include the ventricular‐subventricular zone (V‐SVZ) located in the walls lining the lateral ventricles, and the subgranular zone (SGZ) of the dentate gyrus (Bond et al. 2015). In the V‐SVZ, self‐renewing NSCs produce highly proliferative transit‐amplifying neural progenitor cells (NPCs), which divide a few times before they commit to become neuroblasts (NBs). The latter (early NBs), still undergo one or two cell cycle rounds before they exit cell cycle as late NBs. While doing so, they migrate collectively, forming a rostral migratory stream that ultimately reaches the olfactory bulbs (OBs). There, NBs switch to a radial migration to become incorporated in specific OB layers where they terminally differentiate as interneurons that participate in olfactory discrimination (Gheusi et al. 2000; Petreanu and Alvarez‐Buylla 2002). NSCs in this niche enter quiescence at mid‐gestation (Fuentealba et al. 2015; Furutachi et al. 2015), and, in the adult, only a minor proportion (5%) are active at any given time (Mirzadeh et al. 2008; Ponti et al. 2013) dividing sporadically to generate progeny. However, they can be recruited to regenerate the whole lineage if needed, that is, after ablation of proliferative populations with antimitotic drugs (Doetsch et al. 1999; Mich et al. 2014; Basak et al. 2018; Belenguer et al. 2021).

Although early proliferation studies already suggested that adult NSCs spend most of their lifespan outside the cell cycle (Ponti et al. 2013), it is only in the past decade that quiescence has been studied as a distinct biological state. Prospective purification strategies and sc‐RNASeq analyses have enabled the molecular characterization of these cells, revealing transcriptional signatures that clearly distinguish quiescent from activated NSCs and their progeny (Daynac et al. 2013; Codega et al. 2014; Giachino et al. 2014; Mich et al. 2014; Llorens‐Bobadilla et al. 2015; Dulken et al. 2017; Basak et al. 2018; Morizur et al. 2018; Zywitza et al. 2018; Kalamakis et al. 2019; Mizrak et al. 2019; Xie et al. 2020; Belenguer et al. 2021; Cebrian‐Silla et al. 2021).

Transcriptomic profiling has shown that quiescent NSCs exhibit a complex transcriptome. While actively repressing cell‐cycle and biosynthetic programs, they up‐regulate genes involved in signal reception, stress response, cell–cell communication, specific metabolic pathways, or proteostasis, among others (Llorens‐Bobadilla et al. 2015; Dulken et al. 2017). These features argue against a view of quiescence as a metabolically inert or transcriptionally silent state. Instead, quiescent NSCs appear to be transcriptionally poised, maintaining the machinery required to sense and integrate established environmental cues that determine whether they remain dormant or become activated. Although transcriptomic studies have identified distinct intrinsic molecular signatures associated with quiescent and activated NSC states, only a limited number of these predictions have been functionally validated. Consistent with RNA‐seq data, functional studies have demonstrated that quiescent NSCs rely primarily on fatty acid oxidation, whereas activation is accompanied by increased lipogenesis (Knobloch et al. 2013; Knobloch et al. 2017; Ruetz et al. 2024; Scandella et al. 2023). Transcriptomic analyses have also revealed that quiescent NSCs upregulate lysosome‐associated genes while downregulating proteasome‐related genes relative to activated NSCs (Leeman et al. 2018). In agreement with these findings, quiescent NSCs exhibit reduced global protein synthesis, yet maintain robust proteostatic capacity through elevated autophagic flux and lysosomal contact. As a result, protein aggregates accumulate during quiescence and are efficiently cleared upon activation (Leeman et al. 2018; Kobayashi et al. 2019). Collectively, these findings highlight that defining NSC states requires approaches that extend beyond transcriptional profiling, emphasizing the importance of functional analyses to uncover the mechanisms that establish, maintain, and regulate quiescence.

The predominance of quiescent NSCs in the adult V‐SVZ has important functional implications. By limiting the frequency of cell division, quiescence reduces replicative stress and the risk of accumulating DNA damage, thereby preserving the integrity of long‐lived stem cells (Audesse and Webb 2020). At the population level, this strategy likely supports life‐long neurogenesis despite the relatively low rate of NSC activation observed under homeostatic conditions. At the same time, quiescence confers a degree of functional plasticity. Quiescent NSCs retain the capacity to become activated in response to physiological demands, injury or systemic signals, allowing the niche to adapt neurogenic output to changing conditions (Paul et al. 2017; Belenguer et al. 2021). In this context, NSCs in specific domains of the V‐SVZ can activate in response to transient physiological states, such as pregnancy, suggesting the existence of subsets of quiescent NSCs that respond to specific stimuli (Chaker et al. 2024). This plasticity reinforces the importance of viewing quiescence not as a static endpoint, but rather as a dynamic and regulated state occupying a central position in the NSC life cycle.

Finally, dysregulation of quiescence has emerged as a central feature of aging and other pathological conditions. Rather than resulting solely from stem cell depletion, the age‐associated decline in neurogenesis appears to reflect altered regulation of NSC state transitions. In the aged V‐SVZ, NSCs progressively accumulate in deeper quiescent states characterized by reduced activation frequency and diminished responsiveness to mitogenic signals (Conover and Shook 2011; Leeman et al. 2018; Kalamakis et al. 2019). Transcriptomic studies further show that aging increases the proportion of NSCs expressing quiescence‐associated molecular signatures while reducing the fraction of activation‐primed cells (Apostolopoulou et al. 2017; Kalamakis et al. 2019). This shift toward deeper quiescence is accompanied by age‐related changes in the stem cell niche, including increased inflammatory signaling, altered systemic cues, and remodeling of the local microenvironment (Rojas‐Vazquez et al. 2021). Although aged NSCs are less likely to become activated, their proliferative and differentiation capacities remain largely intact once activation occurs (Kalamakis et al. 2019). These findings suggest that aging primarily increases the threshold for quiescence exit rather than diminishing the intrinsic potential of NSCs. Enhanced stabilization of quiescence may therefore represent an adaptive mechanism that preserves the long‐term stem cell pool at the expense of reduced neurogenic output. This perspective reframes age‐related neurogenic decline as a consequence of altered state regulation rather than irreversible stem cell loss. This conceptual framework has important therapeutic implications. Strategies to restore neurogenesis or promote brain repair will likely need to selectively enhance quiescence exit while preserving the long‐term maintenance of the NSC pool. Achieving this balance will require a deeper understanding of how quiescent states are established, stabilized, and reversibly regulated in vivo (Urban and Cheung 2021).

Heterogeneity and Depth of Quiescence in Adult NSCs

A major conceptual shift in the field of adult stem cell biology has been the recognition that quiescence is not a single, uniform cellular state. Instead, quiescence encompasses a spectrum of substates that differ in their molecular configuration, functional potential and responsiveness to activating cues (van Velthoven and Rando 2019). This notion, first articulated in non‐NSC systems, has profound implications for understanding how adult NSCs are maintained and deployed throughout life.

In early models, quiescent NSCs were often treated as a homogeneous population defined primarily by the absence of proliferation markers (Morshead et al. 1994; Doetsch et al. 1999; Maslov et al. 2004; Mirzadeh et al. 2008; Pastrana et al. 2009; Ponti et al. 2013; Codega et al. 2014). However, accumulating evidence from lineage tracing, cell‐cycle analyses, and, more recently, single‐cell transcriptomics has challenged this simplified view. Adult NSCs in the V‐SVZ appear to occupy multiple quiescent configurations, ranging from deeply dormant states to more “primed” or activation‐ready conditions, each with distinct biological properties (Cheung and Rando 2013; Llorens‐Bobadilla et al. 2015; Urban et al. 2019; Belenguer et al. 2021).

The idea that quiescence exists in different “depths” or layers originated largely from studies in hematopoietic and muscle stem cells. In skeletal muscle, satellite cells remain quiescent and do not become activated unless the tissue is directly injured. However, remote injury can induce a transient “primed” state in satellite cells residing in uninjured muscles, rendering them more poised for subsequent activation without triggering cell‐cycle entry (Rodgers et al. 2014). Similarly, hematopoietic stem cells comprise distinct quiescent subsets that differ in their propensity to re‐enter the cell cycle: While deeply quiescent cells require longer to activate, others are already closer to activation and respond more rapidly, a property associated with differential CDK6 expression levels (Laurenti et al. 2015). Together, these observations led to the proposal that quiescence is not a uniform state but instead encompasses multiple functional layers that differ in their readiness to respond to activating cues and suggest that quiescence should be understood as a dynamic continuum rather than a binary on/off state. Applied to the adult brain, this framework has provided a powerful lens through which to reinterpret earlier observations of NSC behavior. For instance, variability in proliferation rates, clone sizes, and lineage output observed in clonal analyses of NSCs can be reconciled with the existence of functionally distinct quiescent substates in the V‐SVZ (Bonaguidi et al. 2011; Obernier et al. 2018; Belenguer et al. 2021). Rather than reflecting stochastic noise, such variability may arise from differences in the depth of quiescence from which individual NSCs initiate activation.

Single‐cell transcriptomic studies have been instrumental in revealing the molecular heterogeneity that exists within the quiescent NSC compartment. Analyses of adult V‐SVZ cells have consistently identified transcriptionally distinct clusters corresponding to quiescent NSCs, primed NSCs and activated NSCs, with gradual transitions rather than sharp boundaries between these states (Llorens‐Bobadilla et al. 2015; Dulken et al. 2017; Basak et al. 2018; Kalamakis et al. 2019; Mizrak et al. 2019; Belenguer et al. 2021). Deeply quiescent NSCs are characterized by strong repression of cell‐cycle genes, low ribosomal biogenesis and a transcriptional emphasis on stress resistance, niche interaction and long‐term maintenance. In contrast, primed quiescent NSCs exhibit partial engagement of biosynthetic programs, increased ribosomal activity and low‐level expression of early cell‐cycle regulators, while still lacking overt proliferation markers (Llorens‐Bobadilla et al. 2015; Urban et al. 2019; Belenguer et al. 2021). These primed states therefore appear to be poised for activation, requiring fewer or weaker stimuli to re‐enter the cell cycle.

Importantly, these transcriptional differences are not merely correlative. Functional assays indicate that primed NSCs are more likely to activate in response to physiological or experimental stimuli, whereas deeply quiescent cells display higher resistance to activation and may remain dormant over extended periods (Basak et al. 2018; Belenguer et al. 2021). This functional stratification supports the idea that heterogeneity within the quiescent pool is biologically meaningful and contributes to the regulation of neurogenesis at the population level.

The existence of multiple quiescent substates raises fundamental questions about how adult neurogenesis is sustained over the lifespan. One attractive model posits a division of labor within the NSC pool, whereby primed quiescent NSCs contribute to ongoing neurogenesis under homeostatic conditions, while deeply quiescent NSCs serve as a long‐term reserve that preserves regenerative capacity (Cheung and Rando 2013; Urban 2022; de Morree and Rando 2023). Such a hierarchical organization would confer robustness to the system, allowing the niche to maintain a baseline level of neuron production while protecting a subset of NSCs from exhaustion. It may also help explain why neurogenesis declines with age despite the persistence of NSCs: Aging could preferentially stabilize deeper quiescent states or impair transitions from deep to primed quiescence, thereby reducing the pool of activation‐competent cells as suggested by transcriptomic data (Kalamakis et al. 2019). Alternatively, quiescent substates may represent transient phases along a continuous trajectory, with individual NSCs cycling between deeper and shallower quiescence in response to fluctuating niche signals. Distinguishing between these models, stable subpopulations versus dynamic interconversion, remains a central unresolved issue in the field.

Experimental Challenges in Defining Quiescent Substates

Despite significant progress, dissecting quiescence heterogeneity in vivo remains technically challenging. Definitive markers that uniquely label deeply quiescent versus primed NSCs are still lacking, complicating efforts to prospectively isolate and fate‐map these populations over long time scales. Most current approaches rely on combinations of negative proliferation markers, reporter lines or transcriptomic signatures, which may not fully capture functional differences. Moreover, many commonly used in vitro assays, such as neurosphere formation, selectively capture activated or activation‐prone NSCs and therefore underestimate the size and contribution of deeply quiescent populations (Pastrana et al. 2011; Belenguer et al. 2021). This methodological bias has important implications for how quiescence heterogeneity is interpreted and evidences the need for experimental platforms that preserve niche‐derived constraints and signaling. Recent advances combining flow cytometry‐based purification, in vivo labeling strategies, and longitudinal single‐cell analyses are beginning to address these limitations (Codega et al. 2014; Obernier and Alvarez‐Buylla 2019; Belenguer et al. 2021). However, linking molecular states to long‐term functional outcomes remains an outstanding challenge.

The recognition of heterogeneous quiescent states also intersects with broader debates about how cell states should be defined and distinguished from cell identity (Morris 2019). Traditional categorical definitions based on discrete markers or cell‐cycle status may be insufficient to capture the probabilistic and dynamic nature of NSC behavior (Urban 2022). Instead, identity can be viewed as a stable, ‘hard‐wired’ configuration, while quiescence may be better understood as a state that occupies a region within a multidimensional state space shaped by transcriptional, metabolic, and environmental parameters (Morris 2019; Urban 2022). Within this framework, depth of quiescence reflects the position of a cell within this state space and determines its likelihood of transitioning toward activation. Such a view aligns well with emerging probabilistic models of stem cell fate and provides a conceptual bridge between molecular heterogeneity and functional output.

Under this paradigm, a stem cell cannot be defined solely by the combination of markers or features that assign it to a given quiescent or activated state, but also by the directionality of that state, namely, its propensity to transition along the quiescence‐activation axis. An analogy may help illustrate this concept. Consider a group of passengers travelling on an airport shuttle between the terminal and the aircraft. At a given moment, they may appear indistinguishable, sharing the same space and status as “passengers.” However, a snapshot does not reveal whether they are moving toward departure or returning after arrival. Moreover, even among individuals traveling in the same direction, it does not capture whether they are embarking on a journey for the first time or returning after a prior trip. Likewise, cells in similar phenotypic states may not only follow opposite trajectories but may also differ in their prior history in the quiescence‐activation cycle.

The Dynamic Nature of Quiescence: Induction, Maintenance and Exit

Quiescence is not simply the absence of proliferation, but a dynamic and actively regulated cellular state that can be acquired, maintained for prolonged periods, and ultimately reversed in response to physiological demand. Throughout adult life, NSCs undergo regulated transitions between quiescence and activation while preserving their long‐term regenerative potential. This dynamic behavior requires the coordinated regulation of three interconnected processes: quiescence induction, maintenance, and exit. A major conceptual challenge is distinguishing the mechanisms that induce quiescence from those that maintain it. Although these processes are often experimentally conflated, increasing evidence suggests that they rely on overlapping but distinct regulatory programs. Importantly, the molecular mechanisms that initiate quiescence are not necessarily identical to those required to maintain it over long periods, highlighting the need to distinguish these two processes experimentally. Rather than being controlled by a single dominant pathway, quiescence emerges from the integration of multiple intrinsic and extrinsic signals that collectively establish a stable yet reversible state (Cheung and Rando 2013). This regulatory architecture allows NSCs to continuously adjust their probability of activation in response to changes in the local and systemic environment without necessarily undergoing irreversible transitions between proliferative and dormant states.

Quiescence induction refers to the process by which NSCs withdraw from the cell cycle while preserving their stem cell identity and long‐term competence. For the V‐SVZ, quiescence is first established during embryonic development, giving rise to the reservoir of adult NSCs that will remain largely dormant throughout life (Fuentealba et al. 2015; Furutachi et al. 2015). Subsequently, quiescence can be re‐established after episodes of activation and proliferation, allowing activated NSCs to replenish the quiescent stem cell pool (Basak et al. 2018; Belenguer et al. 2021). Although adult quiescence is often viewed as an emergent property of niche‐derived regulation, its initial establishment during development is likely to involve cell‐intrinsic programs that precede the maturation of the adult niche. Whether embryonic V‐SVZ NSCs rely on intrinsic metabolic programs, specialized developmental microenvironments, or a combination of both to initiate quiescence remains poorly understood and represents an important area for future investigation (Scandella et al. 2023).

Several molecular regulators have been implicated in promoting or re‐establishing quiescence. The proneural transcription factor Ascl1 must be rapidly degraded for activated NSCs to return to quiescence (Urban et al. 2016), whereas cyclin‐dependent kinase inhibitors, including p21 and p57, contribute to stable cell‐cycle withdrawal and long‐term stem cell preservation (Kippin et al. 2005; Furutachi et al. 2013; Porlan et al. 2013). Sustained expression of Hes1 further reinforces quiescence by suppressing proneural gene expression and preventing premature activation (Imayoshi et al. 2013). Together, these factors illustrate that quiescence induction is an actively regulated process rather than a passive consequence of cell‐cycle arrest.

Quiescence maintenance, on the other hand, is an active process required to prevent inappropriate activation while preserving stem cell identity and long‐term regenerative capacity. This maintenance depends on the continuous engagement of intracellular programs that stabilize the quiescent state and protect NSCs from metabolic and proteostatic stress. One hallmark of quiescent NSCs is the adoption of a distinct metabolic state characterized by low biosynthetic activity, reduced protein synthesis, and an increased reliance on autophagy and other proteostatic mechanisms (Knobloch et al. 2017; Leeman et al. 2018). These adaptations minimize the accumulation of damaged proteins and organelles while preserving cellular fitness during prolonged periods of dormancy. Perturbation of these metabolic programs destabilizes quiescence and frequently results in inappropriate activation, stem cell exhaustion, or loss of regenerative potential. Maintenance of quiescence also requires the continuous preservation of transcriptional and epigenetic programs that repress proliferation while maintaining lineage competence. Quiescent NSCs exhibit characteristic chromatin configurations that remain reversible but must be actively sustained to prevent spontaneous differentiation or irreversible senescence (Urban et al. 2019; Blasco‐Chamarro and Farinas 2023). Together, these intrinsic mechanisms establish a robust cellular framework that preserves quiescence over long periods while maintaining the capacity to respond rapidly when activation signals are received.

Finally, quiescence exit represents the transition by which dormant NSCs re‐enter an activation trajectory while preserving the capacity for long‐term self‐renewal. Quiescence exit should therefore be viewed as a probabilistic transition rather than an irreversible commitment to proliferation. Recent fate‐mapping studies have shown that, under physiological conditions, a fraction of activated V‐SVZ NSCs returns to a quiescent state and remains available for future rounds of activation (Basak et al. 2018; Obernier et al. 2018). Mathematical modelling of lineage‐tracing data based on Ki67 expression further suggests that the probability of returning to quiescence is constrained by niche occupancy, such that increased activation rates are predicted to be accompanied by higher frequencies of quiescence re‐entry (Basak et al. 2018). These findings indicate that quiescence exit and re‐entry are not independent events but components of a dynamic homeostatic cycle that balances neurogenic output with long‐term preservation of the stem cell pool.

Together, these findings redefine quiescence exit as a reversible transition rather than a one‐way commitment to proliferation. Instead, activation, lineage progression, and return to quiescence should be viewed as interconnected processes that collectively preserve long‐term neurogenic capacity while allowing the niche to adapt its output to physiological demand. This dynamic perspective provides a conceptual framework for interpreting how alterations in activation thresholds or quiescence re‐entry may contribute to aging and disease.

Integrative View of Quiescence Control by External Signals

Quiescence of adult NSCs cannot be understood as a purely cell‐autonomous state. Instead, it emerges from continuous and dynamic interactions between NSCs and the specialized microenvironment in which they reside. In the V‐SVZ, this microenvironment, or niche, is uniquely complex, integrating structural, cellular, molecular, and systemic cues that collectively regulate the induction, maintenance, and exit from quiescence. The V‐SVZ niche is organized as a highly structured interface between the ventricular cavity and the underlying brain parenchyma. NSCs extend specialized apical processes that contact the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and basal processes that interact with blood vessels and extracellular matrix (ECM) components (Mirzadeh et al. 2008; Tavazoie et al. 2008). This polarized architecture positions NSCs to integrate signals from multiple sources, reinforcing the view that quiescence is an emergent property of niche integration rather than a fixed intrinsic state.

The V‐SVZ architecture facilitates the integration of signals from multiple sources. Ependymal cells lining the ventricular surface provide critical factors such as Noggin, which acts as a localized antagonist to pro‐quiescence BMP signaling (Lim et al. 2000). Interestingly, BMPs are produced by the NSCs themselves or neighboring progenitors, establishing potentially complex autocrine or paracrine regulatory loops (Lim et al. 2000). The unique pinwheel organization of the niche ensures that NSCs are positioned to receive these balanced cues, which fine‐tune the transition between quiescence and activation (Mirzadeh et al. 2008). Rather than forming long‐range morphogen gradients, the spatial proximity between ependymal cells and the apical processes of NSCs allows for a tightly controlled signaling milieu that constrains inappropriate proliferation and preserves the quiescent pool (Lim et al. 2000; Mirzadeh et al. 2008). Consistent with RNA‐seq data, functional studies have established a central role for lipid metabolism in regulating NSC quiescence and activation, with quiescent NSCs relying primarily on fatty acid oxidation, whereas activation is accompanied by increased lipogenesis (Knobloch et al. 2013; Knobloch et al. 2017; Scandella et al. 2023; Ruetz et al. 2024). Transcriptomic analyses have also revealed that quiescent NSCs upregulate lysosome‐associated genes while downregulating proteasome‐related genes relative to activated NSCs (Leeman et al. 2018). In agreement with these findings, quiescent NSCs exhibit reduced global protein synthesis, yet maintain robust proteostatic capacity through elevated autophagic flux and lysosomal contact. As a result, protein aggregates accumulate during quiescence and are efficiently cleared upon activation (Leeman et al. 2018; Kobayashi et al. 2019). Collectively, these findings highlight that defining NSC states requires approaches that extend beyond transcriptional profiling, emphasizing the importance of functional analyses to uncover the mechanisms that establish, maintain, and regulate quiescence. Astrocytes and microglia further modulate quiescence. While astrocytes contribute structural and metabolic support, microglia act as context‐dependent regulators, capable of either promoting activation or reinforcing quiescence depending on their activation state and the inflammatory milieu (Ekdahl et al. 2009; Belenguer et al. 2021).

Direct cell–cell interactions play a central role in enforcing quiescence. Adhesion molecules such as N‐cadherin and VCAM1 contribute to niche architecture and mediate contact‐dependent signaling that restrains NSC activation (Kokovay et al. 2012; Porlan et al. 2014). These adhesive interactions not only anchor NSCs within the niche but also transmit mechanical and biochemical signals that influence cytoskeletal organization and intracellular signaling pathways linked to quiescence. Notch signaling represents a paradigmatic example of contact‐dependent regulation. Activation of Notch receptors through interaction with ligands expressed by neighboring cells reinforces NSC identity and suppresses differentiation and proliferation (Morrison et al. 2000; Ottone et al. 2014). Importantly, Notch activity is heterogeneous within the NSC population, suggesting that differential engagement of this pathway may contribute to the establishment of quiescence heterogeneity (Lampada and Taylor 2023).

Beyond cellular interactions, the ECM has emerged as an active regulatory element in the V‐SVZ niche. The ECM provides structural support but also modulates growth factor availability, mechanical properties and integrin‐mediated signaling (Mercier et al. 2002; Kjell et al. 2020). NSCs are embedded in a specialized ECM enriched in laminins, proteoglycans and glycoproteins that differ markedly from surrounding parenchyma. ECM components influence quiescence by sequestering or presenting signaling molecules, shaping local gradients and modulating mechano‐transduction pathways. Integrin signaling, in particular, has been implicated in maintaining NSC anchorage and regulating activation thresholds (Sato et al. 2019). Changes in ECM composition or stiffness can alter NSC behavior, suggesting that physical properties of the niche contribute to the stabilization of quiescent states. Importantly, ECM regulation is dynamic. Remodelling of ECM components occurs during aging, injury and disease, potentially altering the balance between quiescence and activation. While the precise mechanisms linking ECM dynamics to quiescence depth remain an area of active investigation, accumulating evidence supports the idea that ECM‐mediated cues are integral to the long‐term maintenance of NSCs in the V‐SVZ.

In addition to local niche‐derived cues, systemic factors exert a significant influence on NSC quiescence. Circulating cytokines, hormones, and metabolic signals can access the niche via the vasculature or CSF and modulate NSC behavior (Villeda et al. 2011; Kalamakis et al. 2019; Belenguer et al. 2021). These systemic signals provide a mechanism by which organismal physiological state is coupled to brain plasticity. Inflammatory signals are particularly potent modulators of quiescence. Interferons and tumour necrosis factor (TNF) signaling have been shown to promote deeper quiescent states in adult NSCs, particularly in aging or chronic inflammatory contexts (Kalamakis et al. 2019; Belenguer et al. 2021). While this response may be protective in the short‐term, prolonged exposure can impair neurogenic output and contribute to age‐associated decline. Conversely, systemic rejuvenation paradigms, such as heterochronic parabiosis, have demonstrated that circulating factors can partially restore NSC activity in aged animals, highlighting the reversibility of quiescence under appropriate conditions (Villeda et al. 2011).

The V‐SVZ niche acts as a multilayered regulatory system in which quiescence emerges from the integration of cellular contacts, soluble signals, ECM‐mediated cues, and systemic inputs. Rather than acting independently, these elements converge on shared intracellular pathways that regulate metabolism, cytoskeletal dynamics, transcriptional programs, and cell‐cycle control. This integrative perspective helps explain how quiescence can be both stable and flexible: stable enough to preserve NSCs over long periods, yet flexible enough to allow context‐dependent activation. It also provides a conceptual framework for understanding how perturbations at different levels of the niche, such as vascular dysfunction, inflammation, or ECM remodeling, can converge on quiescence dysregulation. These findings reinforce the concept that quiescence is dynamically regulated by both local and systemic environments. We need to understand how transitions out of quiescence are initiated and controlled, and how the balance between quiescence maintenance and activation is altered during aging and disease.

Conclusions

An important conceptual advance highlighted in this review is the distinction between induction, maintenance and exit from quiescence. These processes, though interconnected, appear to rely on partially distinct mechanisms and regulatory inputs. Failure to clearly separate them has contributed to ambiguity in interpreting experimental data and, in some cases, to apparently contradictory conclusions in the literature. Explicitly framing quiescence as a dynamic state defined by regulated transitions provides a more precise vocabulary for describing NSC behavior and for designing experiments aimed at manipulating neurogenesis. This dynamic perspective also reshapes our understanding of aging and disease. Accumulating evidence suggests that age‐associated declines in neurogenesis reflect not simply NSC depletion, but altered quiescence regulation—particularly excessive stabilization of deep quiescent states and impaired exit into activation. Similarly, inflammatory and pathological contexts often bias NSCs toward protective quiescence at the expense of regenerative output. These observations highlight quiescence as a critical node at which multiple adverse signals converge, making it both a vulnerability and a potential point of intervention.

From a translational standpoint, the emerging picture argues against simplistic strategies aimed at globally “activating” quiescent NSCs. Such approaches risk exhausting the stem cell pool or disrupting niche integrity. Instead, future therapeutic efforts will likely need to target specific aspects of quiescence regulation, such as modulating quiescence depth, enhancing responsiveness to physiological activation cues, or restoring balanced transitions in aged or diseased niches. Achieving this level of precision will require a deeper mechanistic understanding of how quiescent states are stabilized and how they can be safely and reversibly altered.

Evidence accumulated over the last two decades suggests that, unlike in rodents, the neurogenic potential of the human brain may be largely restricted to early life. Although the existence and extent of adult hippocampal neurogenesis in humans remain actively debated (Boldrini et al. 2018; Sorrells et al. 2018; Moreno‐Jimenez et al. 2019), even less is known about neurogenesis in the V‐SVZ. Studies addressing the fate of human V‐SVZ‐derived neuroblasts have reached divergent conclusions regarding both the persistence and destination of newly generated neurons. While some studies reported limited migration toward the OB, others proposed the striatum as an alternative destination or concluded that neuronal migration declines dramatically after infancy (Sanai et al. 2004; Wang et al. 2011; Bergmann et al. 2012; Ernst et al. 2014). Nevertheless, these studies consistently indicate that neurogenic activity in the human V‐SVZ declines sharply with age (Sanai et al. 2004; Ernst et al. 2014). An intriguing possibility is that this decline does not merely reflect stem cell depletion but rather a progressive transition of human NSCs into deep quiescent states. If so, understanding the molecular and cellular mechanisms governing quiescence depth could provide crucial insights into the limited neurogenic capacity of the adult human brain and reveal novel therapeutic avenues to reactivate dormant NSCs for brain repair.
