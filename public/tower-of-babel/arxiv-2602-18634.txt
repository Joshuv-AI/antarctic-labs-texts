From an Elementary Proof of Error Representation for Hermite Quadrature to a Rediscovery of Legendre Polynomials and Rodrigues Formula Thanks: Part of this work was carried out during Moncrief Summer Internship Program 2025 at the Oden Institute for Computational Engineering and Sciences.
Tan Bui-Thanh, Giancarlo Villatoro, C G Krishnanunni

Abstract
We generalize two-point interpolatory Hermite quadrature to functions with available values and the first n − 1 n-1 derivatives at both end points. Armed with integration by parts in the reverse form we provide an elementary derivation of an exact error represenation of Hermite quadrature rule. This approach possesses several advantages over the classical approaches: i) Only integration by parts is needed for the derivation; ii) the error representation requires much milder regularity, namely the existence of n n th-order derivative rather than a ( 2 ​ n ) (2n) th-order derivative of the function under consideration. As a result, our error formula is valid for less regular functions for which the classical ones are not valid; iii) our approach rediscovers Legendre polynomials and more interestingly it provides a surprisingly elegant relation between Legendre polynomial and Hermite interpolation. In particular, Legendre polynomials are precisely the error kernels for interpolatory Hermite quadrature rules; and iv) We also rediscover the Rodrigues formula for Legendre polynomials as part of our findings. For those who are interested in a different proof of the exact error representation for Hermite quadrature rule, we provide an alternative proof using the Peano kernel theorem. We also provide a composite interpolatory Hermite quadrature rule for practical applications.

Introduction

Numerical integration is a cornerstone of scientific computing, and the Newton–Cotes formulas are among the most widely used techniques.
These methods approximate the integral of a function using only its values at selected points in the interval.
The general Newton–Cotes formula for approximating the integral of
f(x)
on
[a,\ b]
takes the form
[
16
]
:

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dx=\sum_{i=0}^{n}w_{i}f(x_{i})+\mathrm{error},

(1)

where
w_{i}
are the weights,
\{x_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n}
are
(n+1)
equally spaced points in the interval
[a,\ b]
with
x_{0}=a
and
x_{n}=b
, and it is assumed that the values
f(x_{i})
are known at these points.
For instance, the case
n=1
is referred to as the
trapezoidal rule
, and
n=2
corresponds to
Simpson’s rule
.
A conventional derivation of the error estimates for the trapezoidal and Simpson’s rules is based on Newton’s divided differences formula and the integral mean value theorem
[
3
]
.
A much simpler technique, relying on reverse integration by parts, was developed by Cruz et al.
[
5
]
to derive error bounds for the trapezoidal rule.
Subsequent work extended this approach to obtain analogous estimates for Simpson’s rule
[
8
,
11
]
.
This method has attracted our attention due to its simplicity, requiring only an elementary background in calculus to derive an exact error representation.
Moreover, it requires less regularity, and thus valid for broader classes of functions, in contrast to the conventional (classical) approach.

In this work, we consider the setting in which, in addition to the function values
f(x_{i})
, one also has access to derivative information at these points.
The natural extension for quadrature in this case is to first use Hermite interpolation
[
4
,
17
,
15
,
10
,
7
,
19
]

In particular, we define and investigate the following Hermite quadrature rule:

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dx=\int_{a}^{b}H_{n}(f;\ x)\,dx+E_{n},

(2)

where
H_{n}(f;\ x)
is the two-point Hermite interpolating polynomial constructed using the following information on the function and its
n-1
derivative values at the two end points:

f(a),\ f^{(1)}(a),\dots,f^{(n-1)}(a)
and
f(b),\ f^{(1)}(b),\dots,f^{(n-1)}(b)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi}f(x)\,dx=\pi^{2}-4,\quad\mathrm{(true\ value)},

(3)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi}f(x)\,dx\approx\frac{\pi-0}{2}\left(f(0)+f(\pi)\right)=0,\quad\mathrm{(trapezoidal\ rule)},

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi}f(x)\,dx\approx\int_{0}^{\pi}H_{2}(f;\ x)\,dx=\int_{0}^{\pi}-(x^{3}-\pi x^{2})\,dx=\frac{\pi^{4}}{12},\quad\mathrm{(Hermite)},

Thus, in scenarios where derivative information is available or easy to compute, Hermite-based quadrature rules present a compelling alternative to classical Newton–Cotes methods.

Proposition 3.3
.

4
An elementary approach to derive Hermite quadrature error using milder conditions

We start by recalling the reverse integration by parts formula:

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dx=f(b)(b+c)+f(a)(-a-c)-\int_{a}^{b}f^{(1)}(x)(x+c)\,dx,

where
c\in\mathbb{R}
is arbitrary.
Performing reverse integration by parts process once more gives

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dx=f(b)(b+c)+f(a)(-a-c)+f^{(1)}(b)\left(-\frac{1}{2}(b+c)^{2}-\delta_{0}\right)\\
\quad+f^{(1)}(a)\left(\frac{1}{2}(a+c)^{2}+\delta_{0}\right)+\int_{a}^{b}f^{(2)}(x)\left(\frac{1}{2}(x+c)^{2}+\delta_{0}\right)\,dx,

(18)

where
\delta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
is arbitrary.
More generally, if we repeat the procedure
n
times, we arrive at the following
n
th-time reverse integration by parts formula:

Proposition 4.5
(
n
th-time reverse integration by parts).

Assume
f(x)\in C^{(n)}[a,\ b]
,
2
2

2

C^{(n)}[a,\ b]
denotes the set of all functions that are
n
times continuously differentiable on the closed interval
[a,\ b]
.
where
n\in\mathbb{N}
. The following
\left(n-1\right)
th-time reverse integration by parts hold:

\boxed{\begin{aligned} \int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dx&=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}f^{(j)}(b)(-1)^{j}\left[\frac{(b+c)^{j+1}}{(j+1)!}+\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}\delta_{\,i+n-1-j}\frac{b^{i}}{i!}\right]\\
&\quad+\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}f^{(j)}(a)(-1)^{j+1}\left[\frac{(a+c)^{j+1}}{(j+1)!}+\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}\delta_{\,i+n-1-j}\frac{a^{i}}{i!}\right]\\
&\quad+\int_{a}^{b}(-1)^{n}f^{(n)}(x)\left(\frac{(x+c)^{n}}{n!}+\sum_{i=0}^{n-2}\delta_{i}\frac{x^{i}}{i!}\right)\,dx,\end{aligned}}

(19)

where the set of free parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}:=\left\{c,\ \delta_{0},\dots,\delta_{n-2}\right\}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
are arbitrary.

Proof 4.6
.

Let us define the kernel
K_{n}
as

K_{n}(x,\boldsymbol{\theta}):=\frac{(x+c)^{n}}{n!}+\sum_{i=0}^{n-2}\delta_{i}\frac{x^{i}}{i!},

(20)

and it follows that
K_{n}^{(n)}\left(x,\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)=1
. The
n

\int_{a}^{b}f^{(n)}(x)K_{n}(x,\boldsymbol{\theta})\,dx=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{k}\left[f^{(n-1-k)}(x)K_{n}^{(k)}(x,\boldsymbol{\theta})\right]_{x=a}^{x=b}\\
+(-1)^{n}\int_{a}^{b}f(x)K_{n}^{(n)}(x,\boldsymbol{\theta})\,dx.

Since
K_{n}^{(n)}(x,\boldsymbol{\theta})=1
, multiplying by
(-1)^{n}
and renaming the indices for the derivatives we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}f^{(j)}(b)(-1)^{j}K_{n}^{(n-1-j)}(b,\boldsymbol{\theta})\;+\;\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}f^{(j)}(a)(-1)^{j+1}K_{n}^{(n-1-j)}(a,\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle+\int_{a}^{b}(-1)^{n}f^{(n)}(x)K_{n}(x,\boldsymbol{\theta})\,dx.

Straightforward differentiation gives

K_{n}^{(n-1-j)}(x,\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{(x+c)^{j+1}}{(j+1)!}+\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}\delta_{i+n-1-j}\,\frac{x^{i}}{i!},\qquad 0\leq j\leq n-1,

and this concludes the proof.

5.2
General bounds for
E_{2}
with uniform and
L_{2}
norms

Proposition 5.12
.

Define the midrange of the second derivative as

\beta_{2}:=\frac{\inf_{\left[a,b\right]}f^{(2)}(x)+\sup_{\left[a,b\right]}f^{(2)}(x)}{2}

and the mean as
\overline{f^{(2)}}=\frac{1}{b-a}\int_{a}^{b}f^{(2)}(x)\,dx
. Furthermore, define the deviations as

\delta(x):={f^{(2)}(x)-\beta_{2}},\quad\text{and}\quad\Delta(x):={f^{(2)}(x)-\overline{f^{(2)}}}.

Then

|E_{2}|\leq\left\|\delta\right\|_{\infty}\cdot\frac{(b-a)^{3}\sqrt{3}}{54},\text{ and }|E_{2}|\leq\left\|\Delta\right\|_{2}\frac{(b-a)^{5/2}}{\sqrt{720}}.

Proof 5.13
.

Since
\int_{a}^{b}K_{2}(x)\,dx=0
:

\displaystyle\left|E_{2}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{a}^{b}f^{(2)}(x)K_{2}(x)\,dx\right|=\left|\int_{a}^{b}\delta(x)K_{2}(x)\,dx\right|\leq\left\|\delta\right\|_{\infty}\int_{a}^{b}\left|K_{2}(x)\right|\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\delta\right\|_{\infty}\cdot\frac{(b-a)^{3}\sqrt{3}}{54}.

On the other hand, by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we have

\left|E_{2}\right|=\left|\int_{a}^{b}\Delta(x)K_{2}(x)\,dx\right|\leq\left\|\Delta\right\|_{2}\left\|K_{2}\right\|_{2}=\left\|\Delta\right\|_{2}\frac{(b-a)^{5/2}}{\sqrt{720}}.

Remark 5.14
.

If we do not exploit the fact that
\int_{a}^{b}K(x)\,dx=0
, then the obvious bounds would be

|E_{2}|\leq\left\|f^{(2)}\right\|_{\infty}\cdot\frac{(b-a)^{3}\sqrt{3}}{54},\text{ and }|E_{2}|\leq\left\|f^{(2)}\right\|_{2}\frac{(b-a)^{5/2}}{\sqrt{720}},

which would be more conservative owing the fundamental results for the deviation from the midrange in the infinity norm and the mean in the
L^{2}
-norm:

\left\|\delta\right\|_{\infty}\leq\left\|f^{(2)}\right\|_{\infty},\text{ and }\left\|\Delta\right\|_{2}\leq\left\|f^{(2)}\right\|_{2}.

Note the constants
\beta_{2}
and
\overline{f^{(2)}}
are the best in the sense that they are chosen to solve the following optimization problems

\beta_{2}=\arg\min_{\beta}\left\|f^{(2)}-\beta\right\|_{\infty},\quad\text{ and }\quad\overline{f^{(2)}}=\arg\min_{\beta}\left\|f^{(2)}-\beta\right\|_{2}.

6.1
Exact error representation via coefficient matching

We have shown in the previous section how to determine free parameters

.
In the proof of

, we notice that there are 4 conditions, but we have only 2 parameters
c
and
\delta_{0}
. It is, however, not an overdetermined system as two of them are redundant as we now show for general
n
. To begin, let us rewrite
eq.

19
in the following form

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\alpha_{j}^{b}f^{(j)}(b)+\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\alpha_{j}^{a}f^{(j)}(a)+\int_{a}^{b}(-1)^{n}f^{(n)}(x)\,K_{n}(x,\boldsymbol{\theta})\,dx,

where the kernel

\displaystyle\alpha_{j}^{b}

\displaystyle:=(-1)^{j}\left[\frac{(b+c)^{j+1}}{(j+1)!}+\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}\delta_{\,n-1-j+k}\,\frac{b^{k}}{k!}\right],

\displaystyle\alpha_{j}^{a}

\displaystyle:=(-1)^{j+1}\left[\frac{(a+c)^{j+1}}{(j+1)!}+\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}\delta_{\,n-1-j+k}\,\frac{a^{k}}{k!}\right].

Idea

Equation

23

7
when

\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\alpha_{j}^{b}f^{(j)}(b)+\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\alpha_{j}^{a}f^{(j)}(a)\\
=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}w_{j}^{a}f^{(j)}(a)+\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}w_{j}^{b}f^{(j)}(b),\quad\forall f\in C^{(n)}[a,\ b].

(24)

Lemma 6.20
(Matching conditions).

The condition in
eq.

24
holds iff

\alpha_{j}^{a}=w_{j}^{a},\text{ and }\alpha_{j}^{b}=w_{j}^{b},\quad\forall j=0,\ldots,n-1.

(25)

Proof 6.21
.

The sufficiency is clear, and we focus on the necessary. We start by defining the difference linear functional

D(f):=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}(\alpha_{j}^{b}-w_{j}^{b})f^{(j)}(b)+\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}(\alpha_{j}^{a}-w_{j}^{a})f^{(j)}(a).

By (
24
), we have
D(f)=0
for all
f\in C^{(n)}[a,b]
.
Let us define
d_{j}^{a}:=\alpha_{j}^{a}-w_{j}^{a}
and
d_{j}^{b}:=\alpha_{j}^{b}-w_{j}^{b}
. There exists a smooth cut-off function
\phi
such that

\phi=1
on a neighborhood of
a
,
and
\phi\equiv 0
on a neighborhood of
b

[
9
,
12
]
.
Next, let us define

f_{j_{0}}(x):=\frac{(x-a)^{j_{0}}}{j_{0}!}\,\phi(x).

(26)

Because
\phi=1
near
a
, all derivatives of
\phi
vanish at
a
, so

f_{j_{0}}^{(j)}(a)=\delta_{j,j_{0}}\qquad(j=0,\dots,n-1).

where
\delta_{j,j_{0}}
is the Kronecker delta. Because
\phi=0
near
b
,
f_{j_{0}}=0
near
b
, hence

f_{j_{0}}^{(j)}(b)=0\qquad(j=0,\dots,n-1).

Substituting into

gives

0=D(f_{j_{0}})=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}d_{j}^{a}f_{j_{0}}^{(j)}(a)+\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}d_{j}^{b}f_{j_{0}}^{(j)}(b)=d_{j_{0}}^{a},

so

. Since

was arbitrary,

for all

.
Similarly, we can show that

for all

Therefore
\alpha_{j}^{a}=w_{j}^{a}
and
\alpha_{j}^{b}=w_{j}^{b}
for all

6.2
Redundancy in matching conditions and properties of the error kernel

In the matching conditions
eq.

25
, there are
2n
equations for
n
unknowns
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\left\{c,\delta_{n-2},\ldots,\delta_{0}\right\}
. We first consider
j=0
. In this case,
eq.

25
becomes

\displaystyle b+c

\displaystyle=(b-a)n\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\binom{k}{j}\frac{(n+k-j-1)!}{(n+k+1)!}=\frac{b-a}{2},

\displaystyle-(a+c)

\displaystyle=(b-a)n\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\binom{k}{j}\frac{(n+k-j-1)!}{(n+k+1)!}=\frac{b-a}{2},

owing to the fact that

n\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{(n+k-1)!}{(n+k+1)!}=\frac{1}{2}.

Though there are 2 equations for
c
they are not independent. Solving either of them for
c
gives

c=-\frac{a+b}{2},

independent of
n
. That is, if
\alpha_{0}^{a}=w_{0}^{a}
, then
\alpha_{0}^{b}=w_{0}^{b}
, and vice versa. We are going to show that this also holds for all
1\leq j\leq n-2
. To that end, assume
\alpha_{0}^{a}=w_{0}^{a}
, we need somehow to tie the RIBP
eq.

19
and the Hermite quadrature
eq.

7
in order to relate
\alpha_{j}^{b}
and
w_{j}^{b}
.

The classical result
eq.

6
gives us the desired connection by taking
f\in\mathcal{P}^{2n-1}\left[a,b\right]
, where
\mathcal{P}^{2n-1}\left[a,b\right]
is the set of polynomials of order at most
2n-1
, as the Hermite interpolation error
\frac{f^{(2n)}(\varepsilon)}{(2n)!}\left((x-a)(x-b)\right)^{n}
vanishes. In particular, we have

\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}w_{j}^{a}f^{(j)}(a)+\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}w_{j}^{b}f^{(j)}(b)=\int_{a}^{b}H_{n}(f;\ x)dx=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)dx\\
=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\alpha_{j}^{a}f^{(j)}(a)+\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\alpha_{j}^{b}f^{(j)}(b)+\int_{a}^{b}(-1)^{n}f^{(n)}(x)K_{n}\left(x,\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)\,dx,

for any
f\in\mathcal{P}^{2n-1}\left[a,b\right]
.
After using the assumption
\alpha_{j}^{a}=w_{j}^{a}
, we obtain

\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\left(w_{j}^{b}-\alpha_{j}^{b}\right)f^{(j)}(b)=(-1)^{n}\int_{a}^{b}f^{(n)}(x)K_{n}\left(x,\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)\,dx,

(27)

for any
f\in\mathcal{P}^{2n-1}\left[a,b\right]
. What remains is to choose
f\in\mathcal{P}^{2n-1}\left[a,b\right]
judiciously so that the right hand side vanishes while isolating
\left(w_{j}^{b}-\alpha_{j}^{b}\right)
out on the left hand side. To annihilate the right hand side, the simplest choice is
f\in\mathcal{P}^{n-1}\left[a,b\right]
, and to single out
\left(w_{j}^{b}-\alpha_{j}^{b}\right)
we exploit what we already knew in
eq.

26
. By taking
f=\frac{(x-a)^{j_{0}}}{j_{0}!}
for
0\leq j_{0}\leq n-1
, we have

f^{(j)}(a)=\delta_{j,j_{0}},\quad\text{ and thus }\quad w_{j_{0}}^{b}-\alpha_{j_{0}}^{b}=0,\quad\forall 0\leq j_{0}\leq n-1,

which concludes the proof of the following redundancy theorem.

Theorem 6.22
(Redundancy).

\alpha_{j}^{a}=w_{j}^{a},0\leq j\leq n-1
if and only if
\alpha_{j}^{b}=w_{j}^{b},0\leq j\leq n-1
.

6.3
Polynomial kernels
K_{n}\left(x,\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}\right)
are shifted Legendre polynomials

Now, revisiting
eq.

27
and using
theorem

6.22
yield interesting properties of the polynomial kernel
K_{n}\left(x,\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)
.

Corollary 6.23
.

Suppose there exists a set of parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}
such that
\alpha_{j}^{a}=w_{j}^{a},0\leq j\leq n-1
. Then

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)K_{n}\left(x,\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}\right)\,dx=0,\quad\forall f\in\mathcal{P}^{n-1}\left[a,b\right],

(28)

which in turns implies that
K_{n}\left(x,\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}\right)
is, up to a sign, symmetric around
x=(a+b)/2
, i.e.,

K_{n}\left(a+b-x,\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}\right)=(-1)^{n}K_{n}\left(x,\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}\right).

(29)

Proof 6.24
.

We prove the second assertion, as the first one is clear. Let us define

P\left(x\right):=K_{n}\left(a+b-x,\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}\right)-(-1)^{n}K_{n}\left(x,\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}\right).

Then,
P\left(x\right)
is a polynomial of order at most
n-1
. Now from
eq.

27
we have, for any
f\in\mathcal{P}^{n-1}\left[a,b\right]
,

\int_{a}^{b}f(a+b-x)K_{n}\left(x,\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}\right)\,dx=0,

which implies

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)K_{n}\left(a+b-x,\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}\right)\,dx=0.

Combining the last two equations gives

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)P\left(x\right)\,dx=0,\quad\forall f\in\mathcal{P}^{n-1}\left[a,b\right],

which, after taking
f(x)=P\left(x\right)
, becomes

\int_{a}^{b}P^{2}\left(x\right)\,dx=0,

and this concludes the proof.

Remark 6.25
(
K_{n}
is the shifted unnormalized
n
th-order Legendre polynomial).

The orthogonality condition
eq.

27
of the kernel
K_{n}
shows that it is the (shifted and unnormalized)
n
th-order Legendre polynomial. In other words, we have rediscovered Legendre polynomials.

6.4
Rediscovery of Rodrigues formula when

is available

In this section, we explore the orthogonality conditions of the polynomial kernels

in

27

6

Lemma 6.26
.

There holds:

K_{n}^{j}\left(a\right)=K_{n}^{j}\left(b\right)=0,\quad\forall j=1,\cdots,n,

and thus there exist a constant
C
such that

Proof 6.27
.

We proceed by induction and by definition we only need to show

j=1

27
(by taking

Now suppose that the assertion holds up to

for

By taking

in

27

and by integrating by parts

f^{(n+k)}
is available

For this section we suppose that
f^{(n+k)}
,
0\leq k<n-1
, are available and continuous. We again capitalize on the orthogonality relation
eq.

28
to upper-bound
eq.

31
using triangle inequality as

\left|E_{n}\right|=\left|\int_{a}^{b}\left[f^{(n)}(x)-p_{k}\left(x\right)\right]\,K_{n}(x,\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*})\,dx,\right|\\
\leq\left\|f^{(n)}(x)-p_{k}\left(x\right)\right\|_{\infty}\int_{a}^{b}\left|K_{n}(x,\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*})\right|\,dx,

where
p_{k}\left(x\right)
is any polynomial of order at most
k
. The best polynomial
p_{k}^{*}\left(x\right)
solves the following minimax problem

p_{k}^{*}\left(x\right)=\arg\min_{p_{k}}\left\|f^{(n)}(x)-p_{k}\left(x\right)\right\|_{\infty},

which is challenging to solve analytically. We can resort to a computational suboptimal solution
p_{k}^{*}\left(x\right)
using Chebyshev interpolation at
k+1
Chebyshev’s nodes. If we take
a=-1
and
b=1
for clarity, the error bound is given as

\left|E_{n}\right|\leq\frac{1}{2^{k}\left(k+1\right)!}\left\|f^{(n+k+1)}\right\|_{\infty}\int_{-1}^{1}\left|K_{n}(x,\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*})\right|\,dx.

On the other hand, if we invoke the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, then the bound reads

\left|E_{n}\right|\leq\left\|f^{(n)}-p_{k}\right\|_{2}\left\|K_{n}\right\|_{2}.

The best polynomial
p_{k}^{*}\left(x\right)
in this case solves the following least-squares problem

p_{k}^{*}\left(x\right)=\arg\min_{p_{k}}\left\|f^{(n)}(x)-p_{k}\left(x\right)\right\|_{2},

which can be solved in the closed forms, but we omit the details.

Error derivation for Hermite quadrature using Peano kernel theorem

In this section, we briefly discuss an alternative error derivation using Peano kernel theorem when the integrand resides in
C^{(2n)}\left[a,b\right]
. Let us define the following linear operator

\mathcal{L}f:=\int_{a}^{b}\left[f\left(x\right)-H_{n}\left(f;\ x\right)\right]\,dx,

then
\mathcal{L}f=0
for all
f\in\mathcal{P}^{2n-1}\left[a,b\right]
due to the exactness of Hermite quadrature. Furthermore, owing to the linearity of the integrals and the Hermite interpolations we have:
\forall g\left(x,y\right)\in\mathcal{P}^{2n}\left[a,b\right]
,

\mathcal{L}\int_{a}^{b}g\left(x,y\right)\,dy=\int_{a}^{b}\int_{a}^{b}g\left(x,y\right)\,dydx-\int_{a}^{b}H_{n}\left(\int_{a}^{b}g\left(x,y\right)\,dy;\ x\right)\,dx\\
=\int_{a}^{b}\int_{a}^{b}\left[g\left(x,y\right)\,dx-H_{n}\left(g\left(x,y\right);\ x\right)\right]\,dx\,dy=\int_{a}^{b}\mathcal{L}g\left(x,y\right)\,dy.

An application of the Peano kernel theorem
[
18
,
13
]
then gives

\mathcal{L}f=\int_{a}^{b}f^{(2n)}\left(x\right)K\left(x\right)\,dx,\quad\forall f\in C^{(2n)}\left[a,b\right],

where the Peano kernel
K\left(x\right)
is given by

K\left(x\right):=\mathcal{L}\left[\frac{\left(y-x\right)^{2n-1}_{+}}{\left(2n-1\right)!}\right],\quad\text{ with }\quad\left(y-x\right)_{+}:=\begin{cases}y-x&\text{if }y>x,\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Proposition 8.32
.

The Peano kernel theorem yields the same result as RIPB when
f^{(2n)}
is available. In particular,
K\left(x\right)=\frac{1}{\left(2n\right)!}\left(x-a\right)^{n}\left(x-b\right)^{n}
.

Proof 8.33
.

We sketch a proof in a few steps here.

1.

Using the defintion of the linear operator
\mathcal{L}
and the Peano kernel, we have

K\left(x\right)=\frac{\left(b-x\right)^{2n}}{\left(2n\right)!}-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}w_{k}^{b}\frac{\left(b-x\right)^{2n-1-k}}{\left(2n-1-k\right)!}.

(32)

2.

Since the lowest power for
\left(b-x\right)
in
K\left(x\right)
is
n
,
\left(b-x\right)^{n}
divides
K\left(x\right)
.

3.

Using the exactness of Hermite quadrature for polynomial of order less than or equal
2n-1
, we can show that

K^{(j)}\left(a\right)=0,\quad\forall j=0,\ldots,n-1,

which then implies that
\left(x-a\right)^{n}
divides
K\left(x\right)
.

4.

At this point, we know that
K\left(x\right)
must have the form

K\left(x\right)=C\left(x-a\right)^{n}\left(x-b\right)^{n},

since the highest power for
x
in
eq.

32
is
2n
. Since the coefficient for the highest power for
x
in
eq.

32
is
\left(2n\right)!
, we conclude that

K\left(x\right)=\frac{1}{\left(2n\right)!}\left(x-a\right)^{n}\left(x-b\right)^{n},

and this concludes the proof.

Remark 8.34
.

Similar to
eq.

5
, the Peano kernel theorem approach also requires the same
C^{(2n)}
-regularity, which is much stringent than our approach that requires only
C^{(n)}
-regularity.

Composite Hermite quadrature rule

This section briefly discuss an extension of the Hermite quadrature rule on one interval to multiple ones. Let
[a,b]
be partitioned into
m
subintervals:

a=x_{0}<x_{1}<x_{2}<\cdots<x_{m-1}<x_{m}=b,

and define the subinterval widths as

h_{i}=x_{i+1}-x_{i},\quad i=0,1,\ldots,m-1.

On each subinterval
[x_{i},x_{i+1}]
, we use the two-point Hermite interpolation polynomial
H_{n}^{i}(f;\ x)
that matches: i)
f^{(k)}(x_{i})
for
k=0,1,\ldots,n-1
, and ii)
f^{(k)}(x_{i+1})
for
k=0,1,\ldots,n-1
. We define a composite Hermite quadrature as

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dx\approx\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\int_{x_{i}}^{x_{i+1}}H_{n}^{i}(f;\ x)\,dx\\
=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}h_{i}^{j+1}\omega_{j}\left[(-1)^{j}f^{(j)}(x_{i+1})+f^{(j)}(x_{i})\right],

where

\omega_{j}=n\sum_{k=j}^{n-1}\binom{k}{j}\frac{(n+k-j-1)!}{(n+k+1)!}.

The error for the composite Hermite quadrature is simply a summation of the errors on all intervals, each of which is of the form we discussed above. We omit the details.

10
Conclusion

In this paper, we generalize two-point interpolatory Hermite quadrature to functions with available values and the first
n-1
derivatives at both end points. Using elementary integration by parts, we derive an exact error expression for the Hermite quadrature rule. We rigorously study the approach and its results. As a by-product of our study, we discovered that (shifted and unnormalized) Legendre polynomials are the error kernels of the Hermite quadrature rule, thus providing an elegant relationship between the classical Hermite interpolation and Legendre polynomails. Part of our finding is a rediscovery of the Rodrigues formula for Legendre polynomials. Besides these advantages over the classical derivation and error representation, our approach is valid over much broader class of integrands in
C^{(n)}\left(a,b\right)
instead of
C^{(2n)}\left[a,b\right]
. For completeness, we also provide an alternative derivation using the Peano kernel theorem, and a composite Hermite quadrature rule.
