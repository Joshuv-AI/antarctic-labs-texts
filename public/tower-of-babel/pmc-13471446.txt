Umbilical cord–derived mitochondrial transplantation restores tendon structure and function in a preclinical model of achilles tendinopathy
Authors: Da-Yoon Kim, Mi Jin Kim, Min-Jeong Park, Mina Lim, Eun-Seo Back, Chang-Koo Yun, Seong Hun Kim, Hyuk Nam Kwon, Chaeyoung Lee, Kyunghoon Min, Yong-Soo Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAchilles tendinopathy is a degenerative musculoskeletal disorder for which disease-modifying therapies remain limited, largely due to the inability of current interventions to directly restore cellular bioenergetic function. Emerging evidence has identified mitochondrial dysfunction as a central contributor to tendon degeneration, highlighting mitochondria as a potential therapeutic target. Here, we evaluated umbilical cord–derived mitochondria (UC-MT) as a dose-defined, cell-free therapeutic strategy for tendinopathy.MethodsUC-MT were isolated from human umbilical cord–derived mesenchymal stem cells and characterized for mitochondrial integrity and bioenergetic activity. Therapeutic efficacy was evaluated in vitro using TNF-α–induced human tenocyte injury models and in vivo in a collagenase-induced rat model of Achilles tendinopathy. Dose–response effects were systematically assessed (5, 10, and 20 µg), and mitochondrial function, metabolic profiles, extracellular matrix remodeling, and functional recovery were analyzed using integrated molecular, histological, and functional assays, including transcriptomic and metabolomic profiling.ResultsUC-MT treatment significantly restored mitochondrial membrane potential, ATP production, and respiratory complex activity in injured tenocytes, accompanied by attenuation of inflammatory signalling. Among the tested doses, 10 µg UC-MT consistently produced the most robust therapeutic effects across mitochondrial, metabolic, and structural outcome measures. In vivo, UC-MT administration improved tendon histoarchitecture, collagen organization, and functional performance, while integrated multi-omics analyses revealed coordinated metabolic reprogramming, including restoration of mitochondrial complex I-linked bioenergetic pathways.ConclusionsTaken together, these findings position UC-MT as a dose-defined, cell-free therapeutic modality with translational potential for tendon regeneration. Introduction

Tendinopathy is a prevalent degenerative musculoskeletal disorder characterised by persistent pain, progressive tendon degeneration, and disruption of extracellular matrix (ECM) organisation leading to impaired mechanical function and long-term functional decline [1–4]. Its pathogenesis involves sustained low-grade inflammation, an imbalance between catabolic and anabolic activities, and mitochondrial dysfunction within tenocytes [5–7]. Recent reviews have further highlighted mitochondrial destabilisation as a central driver of tendon pathology, rather than simply a secondary consequence of tissue damage, underscoring the need for mitochondria-focused regenerative approaches that address the underlying cellular bioenergetic deficits contributing to chronic tendon degeneration [8, 9]. Despite the high prevalence of tendinopathy and its substantial socio-economic burden, no pharmacological therapy currently exists that can fundamentally halt or reverse disease progression by addressing the underlying cellular and metabolic dysfunction.

Conventional treatments for tendinopathy remain largely palliative and fail to address the underlying pathological processes driving disease progression. Current clinical options include physiotherapy, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), corticosteroid injections, and surgical interventions [5–8, 10], while biological approaches such as platelet-rich plasma (PRP) and mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) therapy have also been explored [11, 12]. However, PRP often induces an initial inflammatory response and exhibits inconsistent efficacy due to variability in preparation protocol, whereas MSC-based therapies are limited by prolonged in vitro expansion requirements and low post-transplant survival. Recent reviews have suggested that cell-free but bioenergetically active therapeutic modalities, including mitochondrial transplantation, may overcome these limitations by providing more consistent and mechanistically targeted outcomes than PRP- or exosome-based approaches [13, 14]. Collectively, these limitations underscore the need for therapeutic strategies capable of restoring tendon structure and function, rather than simply alleviating symptoms.

In response to these limitations, there is a growing interest in cell-free, bioenergetically active therapies. Recent studies and reviews suggest that mitochondrial transplantation may offer more consistent and mechanistically targeted outcomes than traditional PRP- or exosome-based approaches. Mitochondria (MT) are central regulators of cellular energy metabolism, redox homeostasis, and cell survival, and their dysfunction has been increasingly implicated as a key driver of tendon pathology [15]. Impaired oxidative phosphorylation, excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and heightened pro-inflammatory signalling exacerbate tendinopathy progression [16–18]. Importantly, exogenous delivery of healthy mitochondria has been shown to restore bioenergetic function through integration into the endogenous mitochondrial network [14, 19–21].

Previous studies have provided proof-of-concept that mitochondrial transplantation can restore mitochondrial function and attenuate inflammatory responses in human tenocytes. However, these early investigations were limited in scope, often focusing on a single cell type, lacking dose optimisation, and providing limited mechanistic understanding in vivo. Moreover, while stem cell–derived mitochondria have shown promise in tissue repair, key questions remain regarding the optimal mitochondrial source, dosing strategies, and the precise molecular and metabolic mechanisms underlying tendon regeneration.

To address these gaps, the present study systematically evaluates the therapeutic potential of umbilical cord–derived mitochondrial transplantation in both in vitro and in vivo models of Achilles tendinopathy. Human umbilical cord–derived mitochondria (UC-MT) were selected for their superior bioenergetic capacity and regenerative potential compared with MT from differentiated adult cells [22]. In this study, we implemented a systematic dose-optimisation strategy and integrated transcriptomic, metabolomic, and functional outcome analyses to elucidate the molecular, metabolic, and physiological mechanisms by which UC-MT supports tendon recovery. Collectively, our findings establish UC-MT as a biologically active, cell-free therapeutic strategy with translational relevance for restoring tendon structure and function.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and reagents

Umbilical cord-derived mesenchymal stem cells (UC-MSCs) were isolated from Wharton’s jelly and cryopreserved as previously described [22]. The use of human umbilical cord tissue was approved by the Institutional Review Board of CHA University (CHAMC 2022-06-039-001), and informed consent was obtained from all donors. For experiments, UC-MSCs were cultured in T-175 flasks using Minimum Essential Medium Alpha (α-MEM; Welgene, Gyeongsan-si, South Korea) supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS; Atlas Biologicals, Fort Collins, CO, USA), 100 IU/mL penicillin, 100 µg/mL streptomycin (P/S; HyClone, Logan, UT, USA), and 20 ng/mL basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF; CHAbio, Seongnam-si, South Korea). Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂ and sub-cultured upon reaching 80–90% confluence. UC-MSCs at passage 7 were used in all experiments.

Primary human tenocytes (catalogue no. TEN-F; ZenBio Inc., Durham, NC, USA) were cultured in tenocyte growth medium (ZenBio Inc.) under the same incubation conditions. Prior to seeding, culture plates were coated with type I collagen (C8919; Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 24 h and washed with Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS; Welgene). Tenocytes at passage 4 were used for all experiments.

Mitochondrial isolation and quality control

Mitochondria were isolated from UC-MSCs by mechanical homogenisation followed by differential centrifugation. Briefly, 2 × 10⁷ cells were suspended in SHE buffer (0.25 M sucrose, 20 mM HEPES, pH 7.4, 2 mM EGTA, 0.1% defatted BSA) and disrupted using a 1 mL syringe. The lysate was centrifuged at 1,500 × g for 5 min at 4°C to remove nuclei and intact cells, and the supernatant was further centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 10 min to obtain mitochondrial pellets. Pellets were washed once with SHE buffer and resuspended in 200 µL DPBS. Protein concentration was quantified using a BCA assay (Pierce, IL, USA).

Mitochondrial size distribution and particle concentration were analysed using a ZetaView PMX-130 system (Particle Metrix, Germany). Samples were diluted in DPBS to 2 mL and pre-tested to ensure 100–200 particles per frame. Each measurement was performed in triplicate by scanning 10 cell positions per cycle. Instrument settings included autofocus, camera sensitivity 80.0, shutter speed 100, and chamber temperature 24°C. Data were analysed with ZetaView software, with the maximum particle-size cut-off set at 1,000 nm.

The ultrastructure of isolated mitochondria was examined using transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Freshly isolated UC-MT were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde overnight at 4°C, post-fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide, dehydrated, and embedded in epoxy resin. Ultrathin sections (70 nm) were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate and visualised using a JEM-1400 TEM (JEOL, Japan). Images were obtained at 10,000× and 30,000× magnification to assess mitochondrial morphology, cristae structure, and membrane integrity.

The quantity and purity of isolated mitochondria were further evaluated by BCA assay and flow cytometry with dual staining (TOMM20-APC and MitoTracker Green-FITC). Functional activity of isolated mitochondria was assessed by enzymatic assays of respiratory-chain complexes and citrate synthase. For each assay, 5 µg of mitochondrial protein was used. Complex I + III activity was measured by monitoring NADH-dependent reduction of cytochrome c at 550 nm in the presence of potassium phosphate buffer, BSA, KCN, and oxidised cytochrome c. Complex IV activity was determined by following oxidation of reduced cytochrome c at 550 nm. Citrate synthase activity was evaluated by a DTNB-based colorimetric assay at 450 nm, using acetyl-CoA and oxaloacetate as substrates. Absorbance changes were recorded over 20 min using a microplate reader (BioTek, USA). Mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) was evaluated using the JC-1 assay (Invitrogen). Isolated mitochondria (10 µg) were incubated with 5 µM JC-1 dye for 20 min at 37°C in the dark. Fluorescence was analysed by flow cytometry (CytoFLEX, Beckman Coulter, USA). Experimental design included three groups: unstained mitochondria (negative control), CCCP-treated mitochondria (50 µM, 30 min; positive control for depolarisation), and JC-1–stained mitochondria (test group). The red/green fluorescence ratio was used as an index of mitochondrial membrane potential.

In vitro experimental design and efficacy assays

Establishment of injury model and mitochondrial transfer

Primary human tenocytes were stimulated with TNF-α (10 ng/mL, 24 h) to establish an in vitro injury model, followed by mitochondrial transfer using a centrifugation-based method [30]. Cells were analysed 24 h after transfer for functional assays including ATP synthesis, ROS production, oxygen consumption rate (OCR), transcriptomic profiling, and metabolic analyses.

For mitochondrial transfer, tenocytes (1 × 10⁵ cells) were mixed with 10 µg of freshly isolated mitochondria in 100 µL DPBS and centrifuged at 1,500 × g for 5 min. After washing, cells were re-plated and incubated for 24 h before analysis. Transfer efficiency was verified using MitoTracker Green-labelled mitochondria, analysed by flow cytometry and fluorescence microscopy.

Dose–response evaluation and working concentration

To determine the effective dose, TNF-α–injured tenocytes were treated with UC-MT at 5, 10, or 20 µg. Dose-dependent responses were evaluated by ATP production, mitochondrial ROS levels, mitochondrial membrane potential, apoptosis-related proteins (Bax/Bcl-2), inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6), and tendon-specific markers (TNMD, COL1). Based on these findings, 10 µg UC-MT was selected as the working concentration for confirmatory experiments, including OCR analysis and omics profiling.

ATP synthesis assay

ATP synthesis was evaluated using isolated mitochondria. Briefly, 5 µg of isolated mitochondria suspended in 95 µL of DPBS was added to each well of a white 96-well plate. Then, 5 µL of 0.1 M ADP was added, and the reaction mixture was incubated for 45 min at 37°C. After incubation, 100 µL of CellTiter-Glo® 2.0 reagent (Cat# G9243, Promega, Madison, WI, USA) was added to each well, followed by shaking for 2 min. Luminescence was measured using a Synergy H1 microplate reader (Agilent BioTek, Santa Clara, CA, USA). ATP concentrations were calculated from an ATP standard curve after subtraction of blank values.

Mitochondrial ROS measurement

Mitochondrial ROS levels were measured using MitoSOX™ Red (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA). Tenocytes were seeded in black 96-well plates (1 × 10⁴ cells/well), treated as indicated, and incubated with 5 µM MitoSOX Red in HBSS for 20 min at 37 °C in the dark. After two washes, fluorescence was measured using a Synergy H1 reader (excitation/emission: 510/580 nm) and a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, USA). Data were normalised to cell number and expressed relative to control.

Oxygen consumption rate

Tenocytes (2 × 10⁴ cells/well) were seeded in Seahorse XF96 microplates (Agilent Technologies, USA) and incubated overnight. On the day of assay, culture medium was replaced with Seahorse XF assay medium containing 25 mM glucose, 2 mM glutamine, and 1 mM sodium pyruvate. Cells were equilibrated at 37°C without CO₂ for 1 h. OCR was measured under basal conditions and after sequential injection of oligomycin (1 µM), FCCP (1 µM), and rotenone/antimycin A (0.5 µM). Basal respiration, ATP-linked respiration, maximal respiration, and spare respiratory capacity were calculated using Wave software (Agilent). Prior to mitochondrial transfer and Seahorse measurement, cells were detached and re-seeded to ensure uniform cell distribution across wells. To minimise variability, equal numbers of cells (2 × 10⁴ cells/well) were carefully plated in each well. Thus, metabolic measurements were obtained under standardized cell-number conditions. Although normalization to DNA or total protein content was not performed, strict control of initial cell seeding ensured comparability across experimental groups. All Seahorse experiments were performed with consistent seeding density across groups.

Gene expression analysis

Expression of tendon-specific markers (TNMD, COL1) and a matrix-remodelling enzyme (MMP-1) was analysed by qRT-PCR. Total RNA was isolated from tenocytes using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA), and cDNA was synthesised using a reverse transcription kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). qPCR was performed with SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, USA) on a QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher). GAPDH was used as the internal control. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2–ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are provided in Table 1.

MitoPlate S-1 metabolic activity assay

Tenocytes (1 × 10⁴ cells/well) were seeded into MitoPlate S-1 plates (Biolog, Hayward, CA, USA) containing 31 metabolic substrates. Cells were permeabilised with Mitochondrial Assay Solution supplemented with 50 µM digitonin, 1 mM NAD⁺, 0.5 mM ATP, and 0.1 mM ADP. Redox dye mix was added to monitor substrate oxidation, and absorbance at 590 nm was measured every 15 min for 4 h using a Synergy Multi-Mode Microplate Reader (BioTek, USA). Substrate-utilisation rates were calculated from the slopes of absorbance increase and normalised to controls.

In vivo mitochondrial transplantation model

Induction of Achilles tendinopathy

All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of CHA University (IACUC230145). Male Sprague–Dawley rats (6–8 weeks old, 100–150 g) were used. Achilles tendinopathy was induced by injecting collagenase type I (0.6 mg in 40 µL DPBS) into the mid-substance of the tendon using a 29-gauge syringe.

For the pilot dose-finding study, animals were randomly assigned to control, collagenase-only, or collagenase plus mitochondrial transplantation at 10, 20, or 50 µg (n = 7 per group). Based on functional and histological outcomes, 10 µg was selected as the effective dose. In the confirmatory study, three groups were established (control, collagenase, and collagenase + MT 10 µg) with seven animals per group. Subsets of animals were allocated to omics analyses (RNA-seq and metabolomics), protein-level assays (Western blotting and ELISA), and histological/functional assessments.

Mitochondrial transplantation procedure

For transplantation, mitochondria were freshly isolated from UC-MSCs. UC-MSCs were pre-labelled with MitoTracker CMXRos Red (Invitrogen, M7512) prior to mitochondrial isolation when tracking was required. Labelled or unlabelled mitochondria were suspended in DPBS and injected locally into the injured tendon at the predetermined dose (10 µg).

Verification of transplanted mitochondria in tendon tissues

Tendons injected with CMXRos Red-labelled mitochondria were harvested for cryo-sectioning. Tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C overnight, washed, and cryoprotected in a sucrose gradient (10%, 20%, 30%). Samples were embedded in OCT compound and cryosectioned at 5 μm. Sections were stained with MitoTracker Green, counterstained with DAPI, and imaged by confocal microscopy (LSM 880; ZEISS) to confirm mitochondrial localisation and integration.

Histological analysis

Tendon tissues were sectioned and stained with haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for morphology and Masson’s trichrome for collagen deposition. H&E staining visualised nuclei (blue) and cytoplasm (pink), whereas trichrome staining highlighted collagen fibres (blue), muscle fibres (red), and nuclei (dark). These complementary stains were used to evaluate tissue organisation, inflammation, and fibrosis.

Behavioural testing

Functional recovery was assessed through gait analysis, grip strength, and weight-bearing asymmetry. For gait analysis, coloured ink was applied to fore- and hind-paws, and animals walked along a paper-lined runway within a narrow tunnel to ensure straight-line locomotion. Stride length and footprint pattern were quantified. Hindlimb grip strength was measured using a grip-strength meter (Jeongdo B&P, Seoul, South Korea). Weight-bearing asymmetry was evaluated using an incapacitance meter (IITC, Woodland Hills, CA, USA), which recorded hindlimb loading distribution.

Hydroxyproline content measurement

Collagen content was quantified by hydroxyproline assay (Abcam, ab222941). Tendon tissues were snap-frozen, hydrolysed, and processed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Absorbance was measured at 560 nm, and hydroxyproline content was expressed as µg/mg tendon wet weight.

Cytokine assay

Inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and IL-6 were measured by ELISA. Tendon tissues were homogenised in RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors. Lysates were clarified by centrifugation, and total protein was quantified by BCA assay. Supernatants were analysed using rat-specific ELISA kits (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA), and cytokine levels were normalised to protein content (pg/mg).

Western blotting analysis

Protein expression was assessed in tendon lysates by Western blotting. Samples were resolved on 10–12% SDS-PAGE gels, transferred to PVDF membranes, and blocked with 4% BSA. Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against Bax, Bcl-2, TNMD, TNC, COL1, MMP-1, NF-κB (p65 and p-p65), OXPHOS complexes, PGC-1α, and β-actin (loading control). HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were applied, and bands were visualised using ECL reagents (Cytiva) and a LAS-4000 imaging system.

Metabolite extraction and LC/MS data processing

Metabolite extraction and LC/MS analysis were carried out as previously described [23]. Briefly, tissue samples were subjected to methanol–chloroform extraction, followed by biphasic separation to obtain the aqueous metabolite fraction. Dried extracts were stored at − 80 °C until use. Samples were reconstituted and analysed by LC/MS on a high-resolution Orbitrap mass spectrometer operated in both positive and negative electrospray ionisation (ESI) modes. Data processing, including conversion, alignment, and peak integration, was performed using established pipelines (MSConvert, EI-Maven). Signal intensities were log-transformed and scaled to reduce technical variability and stabilise variance across samples. Multivariate analyses were conducted using standard normalisation and cross-validation procedures to ensure model robustness and minimize overfitting.

RNA-seq data analysis

RNA was extracted from tendon tissues, sequenced, and analysed for differentially expressed genes using standard normalisation and differential expression pipelines, with quality control procedures applied to ensure consistency across samples. Gene ontology (GO) enrichment was performed to assess biological processes, molecular functions, and cellular components associated with UC-MT treatment. Data processing followed established pipelines [23, 24].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA). Data are presented as mean ± SD. Group comparisons were conducted using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Statistical significance was defined as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001.

Results

Characterization and quality control of UC-MT

Mitochondria were successfully isolated from UC-MSCs by mechanical homogenisation and differential centrifugation. Transmission electron microscopy confirmed well-preserved ultrastructure with intact double membranes and clearly defined cristae, indicating structural integrity of the preparation (Fig. 1a). Quantification of protein yield showed that 2 × 10⁷ UC-MSCs produced 297.3 ± 9.224 µg of total mitochondrial protein (Fig. 1b). Nanoparticle tracking analysis demonstrated a predominant particle-size distribution between 100 and 200 nm, consistent with intact mitochondria and supporting the uniformity of the preparation (Fig. 1c). Flow cytometry verified high purity, with > 98% of events double-positive for TOMM20 and MitoTracker Green (Fig. 1d). Functional integrity of UC-MT was subsequently validated using mitochondrial membrane potential and enzymatic activity assays. JC-1 staining revealed robust mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) in freshly isolated mitochondria (fMT), whereas carbonyl cyanide m-chlorophenyl hydrazone (CCCP)-treated mitochondria (dMT) exhibited a marked loss of ΔΨm, serving as a positive control for depolarisation (Fig. 1e). Enzymatic activity assays further showed that fMT retained intact respiratory-chain complex activities as well as citrate synthase activity. In contrast, dMT displayed substantially reduced activity across all measured parameters (Fig. 1f), including Complex I + III (Welch’s t(2.15) = 9.216, p = 0.0092, η2 = 0.975, N = 3 per group), Complex IV (t(2.654) = 4.128, p = 0.0326, η2 = 0.8653, N = 3 per group), Citrate synthase (t(2.095) = 64.66, p = 0.0002, η2 = 0.9995, N = 3 per group) and ATP synthesis (t(2.000) = 32.23, p = 0.0010, η2 = 0.9981, N = 3 per group). Together, these data confirm that UC-MT maintain structural integrity, high purity, and functional competence, supporting their suitability for subsequent transplantation and efficacy studies.

Establishment of tenocyte injury model and dose-dependent effects of UC-MT

To establish an in vitro tenocyte injury model, primary human tenocytes were stimulated with TNF-α (10 ng/mL, 24 h), which induced classic tendinopathic changes: elevated inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β and IL-6), increased mitochondrial ROS (mROS), reduced mitochondrial membrane potential, and dysregulation of tendon-specific gene expression. RT-qPCR analysis revealed marked down-regulation of TNMD and COL1, accompanied by up-regulation of the matrix-remodeling enzyme MMP-1, confirming the establishment of a pathological phenotype characterised by mitochondrial dysfunction, inflammation, and impaired tenogenic signalling (Supplementary Fig. 1a–d).

UC-MT exerted dose-dependent therapeutic effects when administered at 5, 10, and 20 µg. ATP synthesis was progressively enhanced, with maximal recovery observed at 10 µg (ordinary one-way ANOVA, F(4, 5) = 24.75, p = 0.0017, η2 = 0.952; Fig. 2a), while mROS production was significantly attenuated across all doses (ordinary one-way ANOVA, F(4, 10) = 22.32, p < 0.0001, η2 = 0.8993; Fig. 2b). JC-1 staining demonstrated substantial restoration of mitochondrial membrane potential following UC-MT treatment (ordinary one-way ANOVA, F(4, 10) = 3.986, p = 0.0346, η2 = 0.6146; Fig. 2c). Consistent with these mitochondrial improvements, UC-MT significantly suppressed inflammatory responses, as evidenced by reduced secretion of IL-1β and IL-6 (IL-1β: ordinary one-way ANOVA, F(4, 15) = 98.39, p < 0.0001, η2 = 0.9633; IL-6: F(4, 15) = 279.1, p < 0.0001, η2 = 0.9867; Fig. 2d). Western blotting analysis further revealed a shift in apoptotic signalling towards cell survival, indicated by decreased Bax expression, increased Bcl-2 levels, and a favourable Bax/Bcl-2 ratio (Bax: ordinary one-way ANOVA, F(4, 5) = 117.9, p < 0.0001, η2 = 0.9895; Bcl-2: F(4, 5) = 2.248, p = 0.1990, η2 = 0.6426; Fig. 2e). Importantly, TNF-α–suppressed tendon-specific markers (TNMD and COL1) were restored in a dose-dependent manner following UC-MT treatment at both the mRNA level (TNMD: ordinary one-way ANOVA, F(4, 5) = 13.09, p = 0.0074, η2 = 0.9128; COL1: F(4, 5) = 9.573, p = 0.0146, η2 = 0.8845; Fig. 2f) and protein level (TNMD: ordinary one-way ANOVA, F(4, 5) = 218.5, p < 0.0001, η2 = 0.9943; COL1: F(4, 5) = 34.43, p = 0.0008, η2 = 0.9650; Fig. 2g).

Collectively, these findings validate the TNF-α–induced tenocyte injury model and demonstrate that UC-MT treatment restores mitochondrial function, attenuates inflammation, inhibits apoptotic signalling, and promotes tenogenic gene expression, with 10 µg identified as the optimal dose for functional rescue.

Therapeutic efficacy of UC-MT transfer in vitro

Following the experimental timeline (Fig. 3a), TNF-α–injured tenocytes were treated with UC-MT (10 µg) and analysed after 24 h. Mitochondrial uptake was highly efficient, with more than 95% of cells exhibiting MitoTracker Green fluorescence, as confirmed by flow cytometry and fluorescence microscopy (Fig. 3b). Comparable mitochondrial uptake efficiency was also observed between healthy and TNF-α–injured tenocytes (Supplementary Fig. 2), indicating that TNF-α–induced injury did not measurably impair mitochondrial internalization. UC-MT transfer markedly improved cellular bioenergetics, restoring ATP synthesis to near-control levels (ordinary one-way ANOVA, F(2, 12) = 170.2, p < 0.0001, η2 = 0.9660; Fig. 3c) and significantly reducing mROS production compared with TNF-α–treated cells (ordinary one-way ANOVA, F(2, 6) = 595.1, p < 0.0001, η2 = 0.9950; Fig. 3d). To determine whether UC-MT directly alters tenocyte homeostasis under basal conditions, additional experiments were performed including healthy tenocytes treated with UC-MT (Control + UC-MT). UC-MT administration did not significantly affect intracellular ATP production, mitochondrial ROS levels, or cell viability/proliferation in non-injured tenocytes compared with untreated controls (Supplementary Fig. 3). In contrast, UC-MT markedly restored mitochondrial bioenergetics and cell viability under TNF-α–injured conditions, indicating that its biological effects primarily reflect correction of injury-associated mitochondrial dysfunction rather than nonspecific stimulation of healthy cells. To further determine whether preservation of mitochondrial function is required for the observed therapeutic effects, additional experiments were performed using functionally damaged mitochondria prepared from the same donor-derived UC-MT source. Unlike intact UC-MT, damaged mitochondria failed to restore intracellular ATP production, reduce mitochondrial ROS, or improve cell proliferation in TNF-α-treated tenocytes (Supplementary Fig. 4), indicating that preservation of mitochondrial functional integrity, rather than simple exposure to mitochondrial particles, is essential for the observed biological effects.

Parallel WST-1–based cell viability/proliferation analysis performed under the identical experimental conditions demonstrated no detectable differences in cell number among experimental groups during the Seahorse assay period (Supplementary Fig. 5), supporting the interpretation that the observed OCR changes primarily reflect altered mitochondrial bioenergetics rather than differences in cell number.

Metabolic profiling using the MitoPlate S-1 assay further revealed broad functional recovery across multiple metabolic pathways. TNF-α injury impaired oxidation of cytoplasmic substrates (one-way ANOVA; alpha-D-glucose: F(2, 6) = 209.9, p < 0.0001, η2 = 0.9859; Glycogen: F(2, 6) = 67.82, p < 0.0001, η2 = 0.9576; D-glucose-6-PO4: F(2, 6) = 364.4, p < 0.0001, η2 = 0.9918), TCA-cycle intermediates (Pyruvic acid: F(2, 6) = 257.3, p < 0.0001, η2 = 0.9885; Citric acid: F(2, 6) = 26.40, p = 0.0011, η2 = 0.8980; α-keto-glutaric acid: F(2, 6) = 914.7, p < 0.0001, η2 = 0.9967), and mitochondrial-related (α-keto-butyric acid: F(2, 6) = 125.2, p < 0.0001, η2 = 0.9766; Acetyl-L-Carnitine + L-Malic acid 100 µM: F(2, 6) = 174.6, p < 0.0001, η2 = 0.9831; Palmitoyl-D, L-L-Carnitine + L-Malic acid 100 µM: F(2, 6) = 272.1, p < 0.0001, η2 = 0.9891; Pyruvic Acid + L-Malic acid 100 µM: F(2, 6) = 180.3, p < 0.0001, η2 = 0.9836). UC-MT treatment restored substrate utilisation across all categories, highlighting comprehensive metabolic reprogramming towards a regenerative phenotype (Fig. 3f; Supplementary Fig. 6).

Together, these results demonstrate that UC-MT transfer corrects mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress while reinstating bioenergetic capacity and metabolic flexibility, thereby re-establishing a pro-regenerative state in injured tenocytes.

In vivo model establishment and pilot dose-finding study

To establish a reproducible animal model of tendinopathy, collagenase was injected into the rat Achilles tendon (Supplementary Fig. 7a). Gross examination revealed pronounced swelling and surface irregularities in collagenase-injured tendons compared with controls (Supplementary Fig. 7b). Tendon thickness was significantly increased at two weeks (control: 1.58 ± 0.04 mm vs. collagenase: 1.83 ± 0.02 mm, p < 0.05; Supplementary Fig. 7c), whereas body weight remained unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 7d). Pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and IL-6 were markedly elevated in injured tendons (IL-1β: ~3.5-fold; IL-6: ~2.6-fold, p < 0.05; Supplementary Fig. 7e), confirming successful establishment of an in vivo Achilles tendinopathy model.

Using this model, a pilot dose-finding study was performed with UC-MT at 10, 20, or 50 µg (n = 7 per group). Body weight remained comparable among groups, indicating no systemic effects (Fig. 4a). Tendon thickness was significantly reduced in the 10 µg group (collagenase: 2.21 ± 0.02 mm vs. UC-MT 10 µg: 1.71 ± 0.01 mm, p < 0.05), while 20 µg showed a similar trend and 50 µg provided no additional benefit (ordinary one-way ANOVA, F(4, 65) = 7.845, p < 0.0001, η2 = 0.3256; Fig. 4b). Grip strength was restored in the 10 µg group (collagenase: 0.22 ± 0.02 vs. UC-MT 10 µg: 0.26 ± 0.02, p < 0.01), approaching control levels (ordinary one-way ANOVA, F(4, 127) = 16.15, p < 0.0001, η2 = 0.3372; Fig. 4c). Fluorescence microscopy confirmed successful mitochondrial delivery, with MitoTracker Red-labelled UC-MT co-localising with endogenous mitochondria in injured tendons (Fig. 4d).

Biochemical analyses further supported these findings. Western blotting demonstrated partial recovery of OXPHOS complex expression in the 10 µg group, with significant increases in Complex I and Complex V, whereas Complex II exhibited a distinct recovery pattern compared with the other ETC complexes and the 50 µg group did not confer additional improvement (one-way ANOVA; Complex I: F(4, 10) = 68.11, p < 0.0001, η2 = 0.9646; Complex Ⅱ: F(4, 20) = 18.74, p < 0.0001, η2 = 0.7894; Complex Ⅳ: F(4, 20) = 103.3, p < 0.0001, η2 = 0.9538; Complex Ⅴ: F(4, 15) = 39.97, p < 0.0001, η2 = 0.9142; Fig. 4e). Apoptotic balance shifted towards cell survival, with reduced Bax and increased Bcl-2 expression, resulting in a higher Bcl-2/Bax ratio (one-way ANOVA; Bax: F(4, 10) = 24.45, p < 0.0001, η2 = 0.9072; Bcl-2: F(4, 10) = 2.361, p = 0.1234, η2 = 0.4857; Fig. 4g). Together, these data demonstrate dose-dependent molecular and functional improvements following UC-MT delivery in vivo, identifying 10 µg as the optimal effective dose for subsequent studies.

Structural and functional recovery following UC-MT transplantation in vivo

To validate efficacy at the optimised dose, UC-MT (10 µg) were administered to collagenase-injured tendons and evaluated for structural and functional outcomes. Gross observation showed reduced swelling and improved tissue appearance in UC-MT-treated tendons compared with collagenase-only controls (Fig. 5a). Tendon thickness, which was markedly increased after collagenase injection (control: 1.81 ± 0.02 mm vs. collagenase: 2.22 ± 0.09 mm, p < 0.05), was significantly reduced following UC-MT treatment (10 µg: 1.91 ± 0.06 mm, p < 0.05 vs. collagenase; one-way ANOVA; F(2, 55) = 41.87, p < 0.0001, η2 = 0.6036; Fig. 5b). Wet tissue weight was also restored (collagenase: 0.95 ± 0.09 g vs. UC-MT 10 µg: 1.16 ± 0.10 g, one-way ANOVA; F(2, 26) = 5.064, p = 0.0139, η2 = 0.2803; Fig. 5c). Functional assessments demonstrated robust recovery. Grip strength, which was impaired in collagenase-injured rats, improved following UC-MT treatment (one-way ANOVA; F(2, 171) = 13.41, p < 0.0001, η2 = 0.1356; Fig. 5d). Weight-bearing asymmetry was significantly reduced (one-way ANOVA; F(2, 33) = 12.02, p = 0.0001, η2 = 0.4215; Fig. 5e), and gait analysis revealed restoration of stride length (one-way ANOVA; F(2, 26) = 12.61, p = 0.0001, η2 = 0.4923; Fig. 5f). Representative gait footprints are provided in Supplementary Fig. 8 and Movie 1. Fluorescence microscopy confirmed persistence of transplanted mitochondria within tendon tissues, with MitoTracker Red-labelled UC-MT co-localising with host mitochondria and nuclei (Supplementary Fig. 9).

Histological and molecular analyses after mitochondrial transplantation in vivo

Histological analyses confirmed that UC-MT transplantation markedly restored tendon architecture in vivo. H&E staining (Fig. 6a) showed severe fiber disorganisation and inflammatory cell infiltration in collagenase-injured tendons, which were substantially alleviated following UC-MT treatment. Masson’s trichrome staining (one-way ANOVA; F(2, 15) = 42.48, p < 0.0001, η2 = 0.8499; Fig. 6b) demonstrated fragmented and loosely arranged collagen bundles in the collagenase group, while UC-MT treatment restored collagen density and fiber alignment. Consistent with these observations, quantitative hydroxyproline analysis showed that collagen content, markedly reduced by collagenase injury, was significantly recovered after UC-MT transplantation (one-way ANOVA; F(2, 15) = 30.37, p < 0.0001, η2 = 0.8020; Fig. 6c).

Inflammatory mediators were also ameliorated. Collectively, these results indicate that UC-MT transplantation restores tendon structural integrity, attenuates inflammatory and apoptotic signalling, normalises mitochondrial function, and promotes a regenerative molecular phenotype in vivo.

Transcriptomic profiling revealed distinct clustering among control, collagenase-injured, and UC-MT–treated tendon tissues (Fig. 7a). Collagenase injury suppressed key gene programmes associated with extracellular-matrix (ECM) organisation, tissue development, and cell adhesion (Fig. 7b), whereas UC-MT transplantation restored these transcriptional programmes, particularly those linked to ECM remodelling and tissue morphogenesis (Fig. 7c).

Untargeted metabolomic profiling demonstrated pronounced metabolic shifts across experimental groups. Clear separation between groups was evident in partial least squares–discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) plots (Fig. 7d, g), supported by heat-maps analyses revealing distinct metabolite-abundance patterns (Fig. 7e, h). Metabolite-set enrichment analysis identified glycolysis, amino-acid metabolism, glutathione metabolism, and the urea cycle as pathways most affected by collagenase injury (Fig. 7f), all of which were significantly restored following UC-MT treatment (Fig. 7i).

Targeted metabolite analysis further highlighted recovery of branched-chain amino acids (leucine and valine), metabolites within the arginine–proline–glutamate-axis (arginine, proline, citrulline, and N-acetyl-ornithine), and glutathione precursors (glutamate and glycine). Increased uric acid levels were also observed, consistent with enhanced antioxidant capacity (Fig. 7j). Pathway enrichment analysis confirmed that UC-MT broadly re-programmed amino-acid metabolism, redox regulation, and nitrogen balance (Fig. 7k).

Together, these findings demonstrate that UC-MT transplantation restores tendon homeostasis by rescuing transcriptional programmes and re-establishing metabolic networks that support structural repair and redox balance.

Discussion

This study provides preclinical evidence that UC-MT transplantation restores tendon integrity by directly addressing mitochondrial dysfunction, an increasingly recognised but under-targeted driver of tendinopathy [19, 25–27]. Whereas earlier regenerative approaches, including MSC therapy [28], PRP [29–33], and exosome-based strategies [34–37], predominantly rely on paracrine signalling, our findings demonstrate that UC-MT delivers metabolically active organelles capable of supporting bioenergetic recovery and reprogramming tendon homeostasis. By targeting mitochondrial dysfunction at the organelle level, UC-MT represents a distinct translational strategy not adequately addressed by existing biological therapies. These findings further support the concept that mitochondrial transplantation represents a unique form of cell-free yet functionally active therapy capable of restoring intracellular mitochondrial homeostasis.

A key mechanistic insight of this study is the identification of electron transport chain (ETC) dysfunction, with impairment of Complex I, as a major contributor to tendinopathy progression [19]. In vivo analyses revealed marked suppression of Complex I-associated expression following collagenase injury, together with reduced expression of downstream Complexes III and IV. This mitochondrial deficit likely contributes to inflammatory signaling and tissue degeneration, consistent with previous evidence linking mitochondrial dysfunction to tendinopathy-related inflammation [25]. While our earlier in vitro work demonstrated a similar pattern of mitochondrial dysfunction [38], the present findings extend these observations by demonstrating substantial restoration of ETC complex expression in an in vivo tendinopathy model. Critically, UC-MT transplantation was associated with robust recovery of Complex I protein expression in injured tendons, together with improvement in downstream ETC complex expression patterns, particularly Complexes III, IV, and V. By contrast, the response pattern of Complex II appeared less uniform, which may reflect its distinct dual role as both a TCA cycle enzyme and an ETC component (Fig. 6).

Beyond restoration of ETC-related profiles, UC-MT induced profound metabolic re-programming, as revealed by integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses (Fig. 7). Recovery of Complex I-related mitochondrial function appeared to act as a pivotal trigger for reactivation of downstream oxidative metabolism, enhancing flux through the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and supporting biosynthetic processes required for tissue repair. Notably, we observed that α-ketoglutarate levels were further decreased in the UC-MT transplantation group compared to the tendinopathy group. This finding likely reflects enhanced metabolic flux, as restored mitochondrial function may accelerate α-ketoglutarate utilisation for oxidative metabolism and as a precursor for proline biosynthesis. Increased consumption of α-ketoglutarate is consistent with a shift toward active tissue regeneration, where proline is required for type I collagen (COL1) synthesis [39, 40], the main structural component of healthy tendon ECM [41–43]. Our data support this interpretation, as UC-MT transplantation led to higher proline levels and significant restoration of COL1 gene and protein expression, ultimately supporting ECM regeneration and mechanical recovery. In parallel, recovery of the malate–aspartate shuttle and the urea cycle contributed to stabilisation of nitrogen balance, ammonia detoxification, and glutamine homeostasis, thereby reducing inflammatory burden [44–46]. Collectively, these systems-level changes indicate that UC-MT acts not merely as an energy donor, but as an active metabolic modulator that restores coordinated redox balance and biosynthetic fluxes required for effective tendon regeneration.

Our study also addresses several translational challenges. We optimized and validated an effective UC-MT dose (10 µg) that consistently restored mitochondrial and tendon function, a key step for clinical scalability. At this concentration, tendon thickness, collagen content, NF-κB activity, and functional recovery were significantly improved, whereas higher doses ranging from 20 to 50 µg did not confer additional benefit (Fig. 4). Importantly, no overt toxicity was observed even at the highest tested dose, indicating a favourable safety margin. Identification of a biologically effective dose has practical implications for clinical translation, as therapeutic efficacy can be achieved without excessive mitochondrial preparation. This reduces manufacturing burden and supports scalability for human application [47, 48]. Notably, the identification of an optimal intermediate dose (10 µg) rather than a linear dose–response relationship suggests the existence of a therapeutic window, beyond which additional mitochondrial delivery may not further enhance bioenergetic recovery. One possible explanation is that mitochondrial uptake by recipient tenocytes may be saturable, and that cells have a limited capacity to process or maintain exogenous mitochondria once internalized. Therefore, excessive mitochondrial loading may fail to provide additional bioenergetic benefit and instead trigger compensatory cellular stress responses, resulting in a plateau of functional recovery. Furthermore, definition of an effective dose provides a rational framework for the development of preservation and short term storage strategies for large scale production and clinical delivery. Together, these findings strengthen the translational feasibility of UC-MT as a regenerative intervention for tendinopathy. Because the present study evaluated only a single UC-MT administration, the potential additive or sustained effects of repeated dosing were not assessed. Future studies comparing single and repeated administration regimens will be important to determine the optimal dosing strategy, long-term therapeutic durability, and translational applicability of UC-MT.

Compared with existing regenerative approaches, UC-MT offers several distinct translational advantages. Unlike MSC transplantation, UC-MT circumvents limitations such as prolonged in vitro expansion, low post-transplant survival, and potential safety concerns associated with cell engraftment [49–53]. In contrast to platelet-rich plasma or exosome-based therapies, UC-MT delivers intact and metabolically active mitochondria that may provide immediate bioenergetic support and integrate into host mitochondrial networks [48]. These properties enable UC-MT to provide direct organelle supplementation rather than relying on secondary signalling cascades. As a result, UC-MT represents a next-generation, cell-free regenerative modality with the potential for more predictable bioenergetic and metabolic restoration in degenerative tendon disorders.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the collagenase-induced Achilles tendinopathy model primarily reflects acute inflammatory and degenerative changes and does not fully recapitulate the chronic, multifactorial, and overuse-driven pathophysiology of human tendinopathy [54, 55]. Future studies using mechanical overload or overuse models will therefore be important to further validate the therapeutic effects of UC-MT in more clinically relevant chronic injury settings.

Second, while mitochondrial uptake and persistence within tendon tissues were confirmed, the precise cellular targets of UC-MT, including tenocytes, resident fibroblasts, and immune cells, were not selectively resolved, reflecting existing technical limitations in mitochondrial transfer research [56]. In addition, preliminary co-localisation analyses indicated that transplanted mitochondria predominantly associated with TNMD-positive tenocytes, whereas only limited co-localization was observed with CD68-positive macrophages, suggesting preferential uptake by tendon-resident cells rather than infiltrating immune cell populations. However, the precise mechanisms governing mitochondrial uptake, including the relative contributions of endocytosis, membrane fusion, or tunneling nanotubes, remain to be fully elucidated. Although quantitative uptake analysis demonstrated efficient mitochondrial internalisation, the present study did not directly inhibit mitochondrial uptake. Our complementary experiments using functionally damaged mitochondria indicated that preservation of mitochondrial function is essential for therapeutic efficacy. Future pharmacological or genetic inhibition studies will be valuable to determine whether intracellular mitochondrial uptake is required for the therapeutic effects of UC-MT.

Third, although the present study demonstrates that UC-MT restores mitochondrial bioenergetics and promotes tendon repair, the intracellular fate of transplanted mitochondria after uptake by recipient tenocytes remains to be fully elucidated. Previous studies using the same UC-MT platform demonstrated restoration of mitochondrial quality-control pathways, including PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy and mitochondrial dynamics, suggesting that mitochondrial quality control may contribute to maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis after mitochondrial transfer. Future studies should directly investigate the intracellular processing of transferred mitochondria, including mitophagy, mitochondrial dynamics, and lysosomal processing, to define the long-term fate of transplanted mitochondria within recipient cells.

Fourth, although integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses revealed coordinated metabolic reprogramming following UC-MT treatment, causal relationships between individual metabolic pathways and functional tendon repair were not directly tested, as commonly observed in systems-level omics studies [57–59]. Future studies integrating isotope tracing or cell-specific metabolic flux analyses will be required to establish direct causal links between individual metabolic pathways and tendon regeneration.

Fifth, although UC-MT preparations were generated from four independent donor-derived UC-MSC batches, future studies including larger donor cohorts will be required to fully characterise donor-to-donor biological variability and further strengthen the generalisability of the findings.

Finally, while mitochondrial persistence was confirmed within the experimental timeframe, the long-term stability and functional integration of transplanted mitochondria require further investigation. Although multiple independent functional assays consistently supported the overall biological effects observed in this study, several exploratory experiments were conducted using relatively small biological sample sizes. Accordingly, these findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution and further validated in larger independent biological replicates in future studies. Nevertheless, the significant structural and functional improvements observed following a single UC-MT administration suggest that early restoration of mitochondrial homeostasis may be sufficient to initiate regenerative processes within injured tendon tissue.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that umbilical cord-derived mitochondrial transplantation represents a robust cell-free approach for tendon regeneration by directly addressing mitochondrial dysfunction, particularly Complex I deficiency, at the core of tendinopathy pathogenesis. UC-MT supplementation restored key aspects of ETC integrity, most notably Complex I expression, thereby supporting recovery of mitochondrial homeostasis and interrupting the cycle of inflammation and degeneration. Restoration of Complex I-related mitochondrial function was associated with reactivation of TCA cycle-linked metabolism, enhanced α-ketoglutarate utilization, and promotion of proline synthesis, culminating in increased type I collagen production and structural recovery of tendon tissue. These findings provide mechanistic insight into the relationship between mitochondrial bioenergetics and ECM remodeling, supporting a strong scientific rationale for UC-MT as a next-generation regenerative therapy. The identification of a reproducible, safe, and effective dosing regimen further supports the translational potential of this strategy. Future research should focus on long-term efficacy, cell-specific uptake mechanisms, and validation in large-animal and clinical settings to fully realize the therapeutic promise of mitochondrial transplantation for tendinopathy and other degenerative musculoskeletal diseases.
