Development and validation of pathomics signature for predicting prognosis of advanced high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma patients after platinum-based chemotherapy
Authors: Zhenhong Pu, Kaiting Long, Longyan Ran, Jianglong Feng, Hongwei Ding, Jie Xiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In patients with advanced high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma (HGSOC), the standard treatment typically involves platinum-based chemotherapy after debulking surgery. However, the prognosis of patients following platinum-based chemotherapy varies, and known prognostic factors do not fully explain this variability. Therefore, developing an accurate and validated prognostic tool is essential. This study retrospectively analyzed hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining whole-slide images (WSIs) from 392 advanced HGSOC patients. A pathomics signature (PS) was developed and validated using pathomics features derived from the best model in an integrated machine learning framework. Discrimination performance was quantified using time-dependent 5-year receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. Calibration curves were also generated to compare predicted survival probabilities with actual outcomes. Genomic analysis was performed to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the model’s predictions. PS demonstrated significant risk stratification for overall survival and successfully identified patients likely to benefit from platinum-based chemotherapy. The predictive power of PS was validated in an external validation cohort. Furthermore, genomic analysis suggested that PS is influenced by the cytoskeleton, protein digestion and absorption pathways, and is correlated with immune cell infiltration, which may help predict the prognosis of advanced HGSOC patients after platinum-based chemotherapy. PS based on pathomics features is a potential prognostic marker for advanced HGSOC patients after platinum-based chemotherapy. This marker demonstrates robust prognostic ability and broad applicability. Further prospective studies are needed to assess the practical application of this model in precision therapy. Introduction

Ovarian cancer is the most common gynecological malignancy1. Most cases of ovarian carcinoma are high-grade serous (HGSOC), characterized by advanced stages (III and IV) at diagnosis, rapid progression, and poor prognosis2,3. Poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors and antiangiogenic monotherapy have proven effective as maintenance treatments in patients with HGSOC4, but Platinum-based chemotherapy following debulking surgery remains the standard treatment5. Patients respond differently to platinum-based chemotherapy in clinical practice, leading to differences in prognosis, about 20% of patients with advanced HGSOC achieve long-term survival, while the rest experience recurrence within two years and have limited treatment options6.

Although various genomic, transcriptomic, and proteomic biomarkers have been proposed to predict the outcomes of platinum-based chemotherapy, none has yet been introduced into standard clinical practice. In addition, genomic assays for BRCA1/2 (BRCA) mutation status, and homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) status are expensive, entail a long turnaround time, and require tumor DNA/RNA samples for analysis, making them challenging to implement in every patient with HGSOC, especially in low-resource settings7,8. In addition to genomic factors, clinical indicators—such as patient age, pathological stage, and residual disease status after debulking surgery—also serve as prognostic factors9. However, these factors alone cannot fully account for the variability in patient prognosis after platinum-based chemotherapy. Therefore, an accurate and effective prognostic tool is needed, with significant implications for the treatment and prevention of HGSOC.

Recently, “pathomics” has garnered increasing attention10, pathomics involves acquiring high-throughput features from H&E staining whole-slide images (WSIs) and combining them with machine learning to aid in risk stratification, prognosis prediction, and the evaluation of adjuvant chemotherapy efficacy11–14. Pathomics features provide microstructural information about the tumor microenvironment, which can complement tumor heterogeneity and enhance the predictive power of existing models. Therefore, we hypothesize that a pathomics signature could effectively predict the prognosis of HGSOC patients following platinum-based chemotherapy.

Materials and methods

Patients and database

This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Guizhou Medical University (No. 2023447), Due to the retrospective nature of this study, the requirement for informed consent was waived, and all methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. For the training cohort, we selected patients with TP53 mutations, follow-up data, and WSIs from 585 ovarian cancer patients in the TCGA-OV dataset (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). After excluding cases with unusable slide images, 254 stage III-IV HGSOC patients who had undergone primary debulking surgery and platinum-based chemotherapy were included in further analysis. For the external validation cohort, data from 138 HGSOC patients treated between January 2020 and January 2023 were retrieved from the Affiliated Hospital of Guizhou Medical University. These patients were stage III-IV at presentation, underwent primary debulking surgery followed by at least six cycles of platinum-based chemotherapy, and did not receive PARP inhibitors during the first two years after diagnosis. Clinicopathological data for the external validation cohort were obtained from medical records and pathology reports. A flowchart of the patient collection process is shown in Fig. 1.

Images acquisition and processing

For the training cohort, H&E slide images were obtained from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) or fresh-frozen surgical resection specimens collected prior to systemic treatment. Slides lacking identifiable tumor tissue, low resolution, or containing artifacts were excluded. Pathologists confirmed that all remaining slides contained high-quality tumor tissue suitable for analysis. For the external validation cohort, all H&E-stained slides were prepared from surgically resected FFPE specimens. A pathologist (Jie Xiang) with 2 years of diagnostic pathology experience scanned all slides using a digital slide scanner (Leica APER10 AT2), obtained WSIs of HGSOC patients, and digitized the images into SVS format files.

Two expert pathologists(Zhenhong Pu and Bo Li) were responsible for annotating tumor cells and tumor stroma using QuPath (version 0.4.4)15. A third senior pathologist reviewed the annotated regions of interest (ROIs) to ensure accurate delineation. To manage the large size of the digitized images, the ROIs were divided into 1000 × 1000 pixel patches using non-overlapping sampling at 20× magnification. All patches underwent color normalization using the Macenko method16. A pathologist selected sample areas exhibiting nuclear pleomorphism, mitosis, cancer infiltration, cancer invasion, tumor cell differentiation, and pathological grading. Ten typical 1000 × 1000 pixel screenshots were then obtained and confirmed by a second pathologist. If there was disagreement between the two, a third pathologist was consulted for a final decision.

Pathomics feature extraction

To develop a model based on pathomics features, we extracted two types of quantitative data: pathomics nucleus features and pathomics deep microenvironment features. These categories include information on individual cell morphology, cellular spatial distribution, and the overall microenvironment, offering a comprehensive view of the tumor environment.

First, we used the open-source image analysis software CellProfiler (version 4.0.7), developed by the Broad Institute (Cambridge, Massachusetts), to extract pathomics tumor nucleus features from the selected screenshots17,18. We used the “Unmix Colors” module to separate H&E-stained images and convert them into grayscale images for hematoxylin and eosin, followed by the “ColorToGray” module to convert them into grayscale. We performed two types of measurements. In the first, we assessed overall image quality using the “MeasureImageQuality” module on grayscale H&E, hematoxylin, and eosin images. This module evaluates three features: blur, intensity, and threshold. Next, the “MeasureColocalization” module measured the co-localization and intensity correlation between hematoxylin and eosin images on a pixel-by-pixel basis. The “MeasureGranularity” module then generated texture size spectra for all three image types, and the “MeasureTexture” module quantified the degree and properties of textures, including roughness and smoothness. These processes yielded 296 original features. In the second measurement, we focused on hematoxylin images. First, we segmented the stained images around the nucleus using the “IdentifyPrimaryObjects” and “IdentifySecondaryObjects” modules. We then extracted and measured features such as shape, size, texture, and pixel intensity distribution using various modules. Given the large number of recognized objects, we calculated the mean, median, and standard deviation for each feature, resulting in 844 pathological features. Features from both overall and object measurements were summarized by averaging every 10 screenshots per WSI for each patient. Finally, deep microenvironment pathomics features were extracted using a pretrained ResNet34 model on ImageNet as an encoder. This model converted each patch into a 512-dimensional feature vector through spatial average pooling after the third residual block19. the extracted features were aggregated by mean of the values for every 10 tiles in each WSI.

Pathomics signature construction

Pathomics features provide insights into tumor characteristics from the microenvironment perspective. However, their high dimensionality can hinder accurate predictions. Thus, we aimed to identify the features most strongly associated with prognosis in the training cohort. First, all variables were normalized, and univariate Cox regression analysis was applied to each feature for preliminary selection, removing redundant features. A p-value threshold of 0.05 was set to effectively extract discriminative features. We then performed correlation analysis to address feature dependence; if the correlation coefficient between two features exceeded 0.7, one feature was excluded. An ensemble strategy was employed to predict the prognosis of HGSOC patients after platinum-based chemotherapy. This ensemble approach combined individual and paired algorithms, including Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO), Random Survival Forest (RSF), Partial Least Squares Regression for Cox Models (plsRcox), Ridge Regression, Stepwise Cox Regression, Supervised Principal Components (SuperPC), and Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM)20. We trained 35 different prediction models using a machine learning framework and calculated the C-index for each model across the entire cohort. The model with the highest average C-index was selected as the optimal prognostic model, and its predicted values were used as the pathomics signature (PS) for further research.

Pathomics signature validation

The cut-off values for PS were set as the median of the training cohort, and patients were stratified into high- and low-PS groups. Survival curve differences between the two groups were evaluated. To assess whether PS was independently associated with survival, Cox regression analyses were performed. Discrimination performance was quantified using 5-year time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. Calibration curves were generated to compare predicted survival probabilities with actual outcomes.

Genomics validation

We used the TCGA-OV data set, which comprises publicly accessible genomic/transcriptomic data along with corresponding WSIs, for conducting genomics analysis. The gene expression data and WSIs from 254 patients were used for analysis, and release the gene expression data (Supplementary file 2).

After processing the WSIs, We conducted Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes (KEGG) and Genomes analyses based on high and low-risk groups In order to elucidate the biological implications of pathomics signature21. The default values were applied to all parameters, and a statistically significant adjusted P value of < 0.05 was used. The association between the pathomics signature and immune-related features was additionally examined using gene expression data.The Estimation of STromal and Immune cells in MAlignant Tumor tissues using Expression data (ESTIMATE) score, immune score, stroma score, and tumor purity were calculated using the estimate package in R. The ESTIMATE score, immune score and stroma score were presented based on the distribution of samples.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R studio (version 4.2.3, http://www.R-project.org). The time-dependent ROC curves were plotted using the “timeROC” package. Survival analyses were performed with the “survminer” package. Survival curves were constructed using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. All tests were two-tailed and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Pathomics signature construction

Univariate Cox regression and Spearman correlation coefficient analysis were performed to exclude redundant features, resulting in 120 pathomics features per patient. These selected features were then incorporated into a machine learning-based ensemble framework. The C-index for each model was computed across all cohorts, and the highest mean C-index was obtained using the LASSO model, which had a training cohort C-index of 0.747 (95% CI 0.695–0.842) and an external validation cohort C-index of 0.753 (95% CI 0.714–0.851), making LASSO the optimal prognostic model for this study (Fig. 3A). LASSO uses an L1 penalty to shrink the coefficients of each feature to zero, and it is widely used for survival analysis with high-dimensional data. The penalty parameter, lambda, controls the strength of the penalty: reducing lambda relaxes the penalty and allows more predictors to enter the model. In this study, 10-fold cross-validation with minimum criteria was used to determine the optimal lambda value of 0.03799, based on partial likelihood deviance in the training cohort (Figs. 3B, C), resulting in 28 selected pathomics features. Figure 3D shows the coefficient arrangement of these features. Using these feature values and corresponding coefficients, the PS was constructed via a linear combination of the selected features, and the PS for the external validation cohort was calculated directly from the formula derived from the training cohort.

Association of pathomics signature with prognosis

In the univariate Cox regression analysis, the PS and BRCA mutation statuswere significantly associated with OS in the training cohort. The backwards stepwise multivariate Cox regression analysis showed that the PS was an independent predictor of OS in HGSOC patients. Significant hazard ratios (HRs) were observed in both univariate analysis (HR = 3.7 [2.7–5.0], P < 0.001) and multivariate analysis (HR = 2.62 [1.44–2.98], P = 0.004) (Table 2). In the training cohort, patients were divided into high- and low-PS groups using the median PS (0.543) as the optimal cutoff value; in the validation cohort, the median PS from the training cohort was also used to divide patients into high- and low-PS groups. Kaplan-Meier survival curves for both the training and validation cohorts, are shown in Fig. 4A and B. Patients in the high PS subgroup had significantly worse clinical outcomes compared to those in the low PS subgroup (training cohort: HR = 3.7 [2.7–5.0], P < 0.001; external validation cohort: HR = 5.0 [3.1–8.0], P < 0.001).

Validation of pathomics signature

We developed a prognostic PS using 28 features to predict the outcomes of HGSOC patients following platinum-based treatment. In the training cohort, the time-dependent ROC curve for PS yielded AUC values of 0.830, 0.831, and 0.939 for 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS predictions, respectively (Fig. 5A). In the validation cohort, the ROC curve for PS showed AUC values of 0.778, 0.881, and 0.923 for 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS predictions, respectively (Fig. 5B), demonstrating the excellent performance of the model. Additionally, calibration curves showed good agreement between the predicted and actual survival outcomes (Fig. 5C, D).

Molecular validation of pathomics signature

To explain the biological foundations of the PS, we conducted an in-depth analysis of genomics.To investigate the transcriptional differences between high-risk or low-risk group identified by the PS, the differentially expressed genes between them were identified (absolute log2 fold change > 1 and P < 0.01)(Fig. 6A), 42 up-regulated genes and 36 down-regulated genes for the high-risk group were identified compared to low-risk group (Table S1). According to the results of KEGG pathway enrichment analysis, these differentially expressed genes were mainly enriched in categories related to cytoskeleton, protein digestion and absorption pathway (Fig. 6B). Subsequently, we investigated the associations between the PS and immune-related characteristics. Significant differences were observed in stroma score between high-risk and low-risk group identified by the PS. The high-risk group exhibited higher stroma score (Fig. 6C). These findings suggest that the PS is driven by molecular-level immune cell infiltration, which holds promise for predicting the prognosis of HGSOC patients after platinum-based treatment.

Discussion

Accurate Accurate prognosis prediction is a key element in the clinical risk stratification and management of HGSOC patients. In this study, we developed a PS to predict the prognosis of HGSOC patients after platinum-based chemotherapy using a multi-machine learning combinatorial framework. We then interpreted the results through various perspectives, including classification, calibration, and biological function. Our findings demonstrate that PS not only provides insights into individual cell morphology, spatial distribution, and the overall tumor microenvironment but also effectively predicts the prognosis of HGSOC patients following platinum-based chemotherapy. This study presents a reliable and reproducible tool for prognostic prediction, which could facilitate the clinical application of computer-assisted, personalized management for HGSOC patients. PS can help clinicians proactively predict OS, aiding in the development of personalized treatment plans and optimizing patient management.

Prognostic biomarkers for advanced HGSOC have long been a key focus in ovarian cancer research; however, not all are suitable for clinical application. The accessibility of predictive biomarkers should be prioritized. Over the past decade, a range of tools, including single-gene and multigene combinations, have been developed to predict the prognosis of HGSOC patients after platinum-based chemotherapy22,23. These tools offer personalized risk assessments for disease recurrence and valuable prognostic information; however, the high cost of gene sequencing currently restricts their widespread use in clinical practice.

In pathology, experienced pathologists use microscopes with varying magnifications to examine tissue features, determine tumor malignancy, assess cell growth patterns, and evaluate differentiation. However, due to the limitations of microscope magnification and the vast amount of pathological data, pathologists are unable to routinely describe the sub-structural details of each tissue section, let alone the deeper characterization of the tumor microenvironment.

Recently, several studies have focused on extracting features from digital pathology slides for quantitative analysis, with increasing interest in combining pathohistology and machine learning to predict clinical outcomes10,24. Pathomics is an innovative approach to studying tumor heterogeneity from an in vivo perspective. The microscopic features of tumor cells vary across individuals, influenced by disease progression, clinical outcomes, treatment responses, and potential alterations in the tumor microenvironment. Conventional pathological assessments cannot fully capture the microscopic details of digital pathology slides. Therefore, pathomics can complement traditional methods, yielding more accurate results. Studies have shown that pathomics features can predict the prognosis of gastric cancer and clear cell renal carcinoma11,14, as well as the efficacy of neoadjuvant chemotherapy in rectal and breast cancer patients, with favorable outcomes25,26.

The core of pathomics is the extraction of features, but there is no consensus on the best method10,27. Currently, two primary methods are used to extract pathomics features: deep neural networks and bioimaging software. Despite the controversy surrounding the “black box” nature of deep learning, it demonstrates strong predictive power in pathomics and offers a comprehensive view of the tumor microenvironment28. In contrast, CellProfiler is a free, open-source software that performs automated batch measurements of images, offering ease of use, reproducibility, and features with greater interpretability and clinical utility29. This method has been widely adopted in digital pathology analysis.

In this study, we employed a combined approach, extracting CellProfiler-based pathological nuclear features and ResNet34-based deep microenvironmental features, which were then integrated within a multi-machine learning framework to predict the prognosis of HGSOC patients following platinum-based chemotherapy. Biologically, these two feature types provide complementary insights, detailing both individual cell morphology and the broader tumor microenvironment. Clinically, the PS is designed to offer personalized treatment recommendations for HGSOC patients. In the external validation cohort, PS demonstrated superior predictive performance for HGSOC prognosis after platinum-based chemotherapy, with higher C-index and AUC values compared to several molecular predictors22,23. PS showed superior predictive performance for HGSOC prognosis after platinum-based chemotherapy. Pathology examination has become a routine diagnostic procedure for HGSOC patients, enhancing accessibility. Predictive models based on pathology images are widely applicable. A strength of our study is the genomic analysis used to investigate and validate the genetic components of our model. This analysis identified several immune-related pathways and confirmed their significance in our model’s decision-making process. Understanding these molecular mechanisms offers clinicians valuable insights, aiding in model comprehension and guiding basic research into prognosis mechanisms in HGSOC patients post-chemotherapy.

Our study has several limitations. First, while PS was validated in an external cohort, further validation is needed in a larger prospective cohort with a homogeneous patient population, treatment regimens, and imaging protocols. Second, this study lacked the collection and analysis of other clinical factors, necessitating the integration and validation of these factors with PS in future models. Third, improving the interpretability of PS remains an important goal. While genomics analyses relevant to the model’s predictions were performed, the correlation between PS and underlying molecular mechanisms has not yet been explored. Future research aimed at understanding these pathomics features and their molecular basis will help establish causal patho-immunogenomic relationships. Additionally, while H&E-stained slides are fundamental for anatomic-pathologic diagnosis, the inherent tumor heterogeneity and the limitations of sampling small biopsy specimens must be considered, as they may not fully represent the tumor.

Despite these limitations, our findings suggest that PS could play a valuable role in precision oncology, offering tools for tailoring treatments to the specific characteristics of HGSOC.
