Novel biodegradable device for transcatheter closure of atrial septal defects
Authors: Hirotsugu Kurobe, Tomohisa Sakaue, Yoichiro Hirata, Tomozo Moritani, Mie Kurata, Takashi Higaki, Toshiharu Shinoka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The long-term safety of transcatheter atrial septal defect (ASD) closure devices remains a concern, despite favorable short- and mid-term outcomes. We developed a fully biodegradable atrial septal occluder (ASO) delivered via femoral vein catheterization. The device comprises a double-disc main body of poly(L-lactide-co-ɛ-caprolactone) (P(LA/CL)) and a locking component of polylactic acid with palladium alloy markers. In vitro, pH decreased from 7.56 ± 0.01 to 6.58 ± 0.03 over 16 weeks, mechanical strength was lost by 12 weeks, and mass decreased to 60% at 48 weeks. Device weight declined from 268,002 ± 3,226 (Da) to 2,405.8 ± 159.2 (Da) by 36 weeks. ASDs were closed in twelve pigs in vivo using the Brockenbrough technique. No perioperative or postoperative deaths occurred. Echocardiography and angiography performed up to 18 months confirmed complete closure without residual shunt. Macroscopic examination revealed persistence at 6 months, marked degradation at 12 months, and near-complete resorption with tissue replacement at 18 months. Histological examination revealed cellular infiltration and neotissue formation. These findings indicate that the biodegradable ASO is safe and effective for ASD closure and promotes host tissue regeneration, supporting its translation into clinical trials.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48868-7.

Introduction

An atrial septal defect (ASD) is one of the most common congenital heart diseases that requires surgical intervention1. Congenital heart disease has an incidence of approximately 9–13 per 1,000 live births, and ASD accounts for approximately 6–15% of all congenital heart disease (CHD) cases2–5. Surgical closure remains a standard option, alongside device-based methods. In Japan, out of the 10,000 patients undergoing surgical treatment for CHD, approximately 1,500 are diagnosed with ASD annually6.

Recent minimally invasive interventions for ASD that avoid cardiac arrest have been expanding widely due to improvements in patients’ quality of life (QOL) outcomes compared with open-heart surgery with CPB. The double-disk transcatheter occluder device was first reported to be successful in several patients in 19767. Although the research and development of transcatheter occluder devices for ASD have been evaluated over several decades8–12, the results of its initial clinical application were reported in 19905. Various improvements have been made for clinical use, and the Amplatzer septal occluder (Abbott, MN, USA) was approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) as the first transcatheter occluder device for ASD intervention in 2001, and transcatheter ASD closure procedures have become increasingly common in humans worldwide now.

Although these devices offer minimally invasive alternatives, clinical concerns remain, including device embolization13,14, cardiac tissue erosion15,16, residual shunts17, and the risk of infective endocarditis18,19. These complications underscore the importance of careful patient selection, device sizing, and meticulous procedural techniques, as well as the need for long-term follow-up to ensure safety and efficacy.

To help mitigate such risks or demerits after implantation, we designed a transcatheter atrial septal occluder system with biodegradable materials that would be bioresorbed within a couple of years after implantation. As previously reported, preliminary short-term animal experiments were conducted using a prototype semi-biodegradable atrial septal occluder with discs manufactured with P(LA/CL) incorporating a Nitinol spring20. Subsequently, a PLA-based locking system was developed to replace the Nitinol spring, thereby enabling the design of a completely biodegradable atrial septal occluder (Fig. 1). Accordingly, long-term animal experiments were performed, with follow-up extending up to 1.5 years after implantation.

The objective of this study was to assess the function and safety of biodegradable atrial septal occluders for the transcatheter closure of atrial septal defects in a large-animal model.

Results

ASD occluder devices possess a hydrolytic property

The pH of the PBS medium decreased from 7.56 ± 0.01 at baseline to 6.58 ± 0.03 within 16 weeks and subsequently remained at approximately 7.0 up to 48 weeks (Fig. 2A). The assessment of mechanical strength retention demonstrated that although the mechanical strength remained at approximately 40 N up to 4 weeks and deteriorated rapidly to approximately 0 N by 12 weeks (Fig. 2B). The mass of the occluder decreased to 60.68 ± 0.67% of its original weight at 48 weeks (Fig. 2C). The molecular weight (Da) of the device decreased by more than 90% within 36 weeks (268,002.4 ± 3,226.4 Da at baseline to 2,405.8 ± 159.2 Da at 36 weeks), indicating that the ASD occluder devices developed in this study possessed hydrolytic properties.

In vivo effectiveness of ASD occluder devices

The functional effectiveness of ASD occluder devices was evaluated in 12 pigs using biodegradable closure devices. An ASD model with a diameter of approximately 14–15 mm in the atrium was generated, and the devices were implanted at the defect closure site. Successful implantation was confirmed using radiographic examination (Fig. 3A and D) and transthoracic echocardiography (Fig. 3E and H). No acute deaths due to bleeding occurred.

Further histological analysis showed that 6 months after implantation, inflammatory cells invaded the implanted ASD closure device, and mild fibrosis occurred around the biodegradable fibers (Fig. 6A and B). After 12 months, P(LA/CL) and PGA were partially degraded and replaced with native fibrotic tissue (Fig. 6B). At 18 months post-implantation, augmented fibrous tissue formation was observed around the biodegradable fibers, although microcalcifications were detected via von Kossa staining inside the implanted ASD device. Calcified tissues were surrounded by fibrotic tissue, which separated the cardiomyocyte layer from the calcified area (Fig. 6B).

Finally, the absence of cerebral infarction was confirmed using MRI (Supplementary Fig. 3), and no pulmonary infarction was identified via histological analysis of the lung tissue (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Discussion

ASD is one of the most common CHDs in children, accounting for 6–15% of all cases of CHD2–5. Moreover, in recent years, the incidence of iatrogenic ASD has increased due to the growth of transcatheter treatments involving atrial septal puncture, such as transcatheter mitral valve repair and ablation procedures for the treatment of cardiac arrhythmias21,22.

Open-heart surgery with CPB for the treatment of ASD is often performed during childhood in patients with pulmonary hypertension due to the large volume of left-to-right atrial shunts. Even when ASD is undetected in childhood, it is often diagnosed in adulthood due to complications such as cerebral infarction, leading to open-heart surgery for curative treatment23. However, ASD closure using CPB requires median sternotomy and imposes psychological, cosmetic, and physical burdens on patients during the sensitive adolescent period, from infancy to young adulthood.

Therefore, the development of a transcatheter ASD closure device via the femoral vein began in 1974 by King and colleagues in the United States7,24, and clinical use commenced in the US in 2001 by Amplatz and colleagues9. Subsequently, it was covered by insurance in Japan in 2006 and rapidly gained widespread adoption for the treatment of ASD. The development and evolution of transcatheter ASD closure devices have, in certain cases, made it possible to avoid open-heart surgery with CPB that had previously been performed, thereby significantly contributing to improved postoperative QOL for patients. However, concerns have been raised regarding the lifelong presence of non-degradable ASD closure devices, composed of artificial materials, such as nickel-titanium alloy (nitinol) and polytetrafluoroethylene, within the body of many young children and young adults25,26. In response to these concerns, several partially and fully biodegradable atrial septal occluder devices have been developed and evaluated in preclinical studies and early human trials27,28. These devices demonstrate the feasibility of bioresorbable atrial septal closure and reflect a growing clinical demand for alternatives to permanent metallic implants. However, previous reports have also highlighted unresolved issues, including incomplete degradation, inflammatory responses, and device-related calcification, indicating that the long-term safety and durability of biodegradable occluders have not yet been fully established. Therefore, the development of a fully biodegradable ASD occluder with reliable closure performance and favorable long-term biocompatibility continues to represent an unmet clinical need.

Langer (Massachusetts Institute of Technology) and Vacanti (Harvard University) proposed tissue engineering for organ regeneration using biodegradable polymers as cellular scaffolds29,30. The biodegradable scaffold undergoes self-organization through cell infiltration from the surrounding tissues, but disappears with simultaneous hydrolysis. Therefore, we hypothesized that ASD could be closed by self-organizing tissue by simply providing a biodegradable scaffold covering the ASD hole.

In this study, we fabricated a biodegradable atrial septal defect (ASD) occluder composed of poly(L-lactide-co-ɛ-caprolactone) (P(LA/CL)), which can be delivered via a 12-Fr sheath through the femoral vein. This polymer was selected because of its high elasticity, allowing up to 70% elongation compared with other materials, and its in vitro degradation profile, with an approximate degradation period of one year. The device comprises a double-disc main body made of P(LA/CL) and a locking component made of polylactic acid with palladium alloy markers. The locking component consists of a plug-and-socket mechanism that enables bidirectional locking and unlocking, thereby allowing controlled deployment as well as retrieval and repositioning of the device during implantation. In the present animal experiments, no device embolization was observed, supporting the procedural safety of the occluder in this preclinical setting. The data were clarified up to 1.5 years after transcatheter ASD occluder placement via the femoral vein to an artificially created ASD using the Brockenbrough technique in pigs. Following a period of 1.5 years postoperatively, the site of ASD occluder placement in the atrial septum exhibited well-organized tissue regeneration, and cardiac echocardiography and angiography performed before sacrifice revealed no residual shunt, suggesting that the ASD occluder functioned satisfactorily (Figs. 3 and supplemental Fig. 2). The absence of cerebral infarction on MRI and pulmonary infarction on histological analysis of the lungs suggested that the ASD closure device functioned effectively without causing cerebral or pulmonary embolization.

Conversely, biodegradable fibers, which were expected to disappear within approximately 1 year in vitro, were still observed in the tissue even after 1.5 years, and a rapid progression of micro-calcification was observed between 1.0- and 1.5-years post-surgery based on tissue analysis (Fig. 6). Although this microcalcification was not severe and did not result in adverse events such as embolization or inflammatory reactions—as confirmed by histological evaluation and MRI findings—calcification should still be considered a potential concern following biodegradable polymer grafting. Therefore, long-term observation and further validation in larger animal cohorts are warranted. A previous study demonstrated that fast-degrading bioresorbable arterial grafts could avoid graft calcification31. However, while rapid biodegradation can result in residual shunts after implantation, prolonged bioabsorbability can lead to calcification after implantation. Therefore, achieving the appropriate balance between these opposing factors remains to resolve in the future.

In addition, in vitro experiments also revealed that the pH level reached its lowest value around week 16 (Fig. 2-A). Following implantation of the biodegradable occluder and exposure to systemic circulation, homeostasis is maintained by physiological buffering mechanisms; therefore, such pH alterations are unlikely to result in substantial systemic effects. However, previous reports have indicated that localised pH changes may influence the osteoblast activity32. Further investigation is required to clarify the relationship between the microcalcification observed in our animal experiments and local pH change at the device–tissue interface. Preliminary data from ongoing clinical trials have begun to emerge; however, further careful monitoring may be required regarding calcification after implantation and other such issues27,28.

This study had certain limitations. First, the sample size was small because of the use of large animal models. Additionally, the implanted anatomical structure of the atrial septum was not an actual ASD in humans but an artificially created defect in animals, which may differ from real-world conditions. Furthermore, all created defects were treated with device implantation; therefore, the natural history of untreated defects could not be evaluated. Defect size was assessed using transthoracic echocardiography, and transesophageal or intracardiac echocardiography was not used in this study, which may have limited the precision of intraprocedural assessment.

Further animal studies are warranted to better characterize the device’s usability and clinical efficacy, including its safety aspects such as postoperative reactions in vivo and the potential risk of complications such as cerebral infarction and pulmonary embolism.

In conclusion, there have been relatively few experimental studies on biodegradable ASD closure devices. The demand for transcatheter ASD closure procedures is expected to increase as a treatment for congenital heart disease and iatrogenic ASD following transcatheter interventions. For long-term patient benefits, restoration of native tissues after implantation may be preferable to leaving permanent metallic implants in the heart. Our preliminary findings are promising and support further investigation and potential clinical translation of biodegradable ASD occluder devices in the near future.

Methods

Design of a biodegradable occluder device for ASD

A biodegradable occluder comprises two components. The main component consists of two discs and a connecting duct between the two discs, which were knitted using a 5 − 0 monofilament made from poly(L-lactide-co-ɛ-caprolactone) (P(LA/CL) (GUNZE MEDICAL Ltd, Kyoto, Japan). The diameter of the waist is approximately 10–12 mm, and that of the atrial disk is approximately 25–27 mm (Fig. 1A-C).

The other component consisted of small parts made of polylactic acid (PLA) (GUNZE MEDICAL Ltd, Kyoto, Japan) attached to the top and caudal sides of the main component to crimp the two discs to close the ASD (Fig. 1D). The locking component consists of two parts (plug and socket) and is designed to function bidirectionally, enabling both locking and unlocking. When the delivery cable is connected to the occluder, it can be re-unlocked as required and retrieved into the sheath. After disconnection of the delivery cable, retrieval of the occluder into the sheath can be performed using a catcher or a similar device.

Palladium alloy markers were used to visualize the implanted device using X-ray fluoroscopy (Fig. 1A and C).

Chemical and physical properties

Given that the prototype device, which was made from these biodegradable polymers without a nitinol spring, underwent hydrolytic degradation with acidic byproducts, the pH of the immersion fluid was measured, and the weight of the device was monitored for 48 weeks. All samples were treated with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at 37 °C, and the medium was exchanged with fresh PBS every week. The mechanical strength retention of the P(LA/CL) frame fibers was monitored to clarify the effect of the hydrolytic degradation process in vitro. The molecular weight of each sample (5.0 mg) dissolved in chloroform was determined using an HLC-8320GPC system (Tosoh Bioscience, LLC) equipped with a gel filtration column (pore size, 0.5 μm). The pH of the culture medium was measured using a pH meter (model 9615 S-10D; HORIBA Advanced Techno Co., Ltd., Kyoto, Japan) (n = 5).

Animal experiments

The Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Fukushima Medical Device Industry Promotion Agency approved (approved no. 18ANM0011) the use of animals and all procedures described in this study. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with relevant institutional and national guidelines and regulations and are reported in compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org). Conventional Crown-type miniature pigs were obtained from the Institute for Miniature Pig Research, Non-Profit Organization (NPO), Isa City, Kagoshima, Japan.

An animal study including 12 animals was performed in which ASD occluder devices were implanted using skeleton sutures of sizes ranging from 3 − 0 to 5 − 0. The procedures were performed using the Brockenbrough technique with a transcatheter approach via the femoral vein under general anesthesia induced with ketamine/xylazine and maintained with inhalational gas anesthesia. No procedure-related mortalities were observed. Clinical imaging and histological data are presented for the specimen with the longest implantation period (18 months), in which an ASD closure device constructed using 5 − 0 skeleton sutures was implanted. The pigs were intubated via the trachea with a single-lumen tube following general anesthesia and were placed in the supine position for the experiment. A 7-Fr sheath (Terumo Corporation, Japan) was inserted into the right femoral vein and exchanged for a 12-Fr sheath with an angled tip (45°). After a 12-Fr sheath was inserted into the right atrium, the atrial septum was punctured using the Brockenbrough technique. Then, a 0.035-inch stiff Radifocus® guidewire (Terumo Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) was inserted from the right atrium to the left atrium, and the lumen of the atrium septum was dilated with a 15-mm XXL balloon (Boston Scientific Japan Corp., Japan). Subsequently, a 12-Fr sheath was inserted into the left atrium (Supplemental Fig. 1). After generating the ASD, biodegradable ASD occluder devices were implanted into the atrial septum via a 12-Fr sheath.

Postoperatively, the pigs were treated with fentanyl patches and flunixin meglumine (Banamine; Merck Animal Health, NJ, USA) for analgesia. Postoperative antiplatelet therapy (aspirin, 100 mg/day) was administered. All pigs received humane care in compliance with the ‘‘Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals’’ published by the National Institutes of Health, Animal Welfare Act, and Animal Welfare Regulations. No deaths occurred during the acute or chronic phases after surgery. Post-implantation, pigs were euthanized by administration of potassium chloride via the vein after undergoing echocardiography and angiography at 6, 12, and 18 months postoperatively. Hearts were harvested for tissue analysis. The lungs and brains were harvested 18 months postoperatively to evaluate the presence or absence of complications using histological analysis and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), respectively.

Histological evaluations

Hearts with implanted devices and lungs were surgically explanted at 6, 12, and 18 months postoperatively. The explanted tissues were first observed macroscopically, fixed in 10% paraformaldehyde, and embedded in paraffin for histological analysis. Next, 5-µm sections were stained using standard techniques, including hematoxylin and eosin, Masson’s trichrome (collagen), Alcian blue (glycosaminoglycans), von Kossa, and elastica van Gieson (elastin) as previously reported33.
