The relationship between social support, life satisfaction and problematic WeChat use among college students
Authors: Ting Li, Yanjie Shan, Yadong Sun, Ke Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractInvestigating the risk factors and underlying mechanisms of problematic WeChat use(PWU) holds significant promise for preemptively addressing and mitigating such behaviors among college students, thereby fostering improvements in their mental well-being. The present study aims to characterize PWU and to examine differences in social support and life satisfaction potentially across these profiles. In addition, this study also employs latent profile analysis to delve into the influence of social support and life satisfaction on PWU among college students. Using convenience sampling, a sample of 7000 Chinese college students from three universities completed a questionnaire assessing social support, PWU and life satisfaction. The latent profile analysis identifed six PWU profiles, which were labelled as lowest, very lower, low PWU and high, very higher, highest PWU. Moreover, life satisfaction played a mediating role in the relationship between social support and PWU, but this effect exhibited significant differences across PWU risk groups. Specifically, in the very low and very high PWU groups, the indirect effect value of life satisfaction was the highest, and it significantly mediated the impact of social support on PWU. Second, in the lowest, low, and high PWU groups, the indirect effect of life satisfaction was lower, yet it still significantly mediated the relationship between social support and PWU. However, for the highest group, the aforementioned mediating pathway was completely non-significant (indirect effect = -0.01, 95% CI = [-0.02, 0.02], P > 0.05), indicating that life satisfaction did not mediate the relationship between social support and PWU in this group. This study offers valuable insights for alleviating the adverse consequences associated with PWU, thereby fostering enhanced mental health outcomes among this demographic.Keywords: Social support, Life satisfaction, PWU, Latent profile analysis, Mediate analysisSubject terms: Psychology, Health care

Introduction

In the digital era, the internet has seamlessly woven into the fabric of people’s lives, becoming an indispensable aspect, especially for college students who are considered the “native inhabitants” of this internet age. It has become virtually impossible for them to navigate their studies and daily routines without the aid of the internet. Alongside this technological advancement, social media platforms have emerged as prominent communication channels, including the likes of TikTok, Weibo, among others. However, with this proliferation of social media comes the concern of addiction, particularly among college students, falling within the broader scope of problematic social media use. Problematic Social Media Use(PSWU) refers to a form of behavioral addiction marked by excessive preoccupation with social media, compulsive usage patterns, and the disproportionate allocation of time and attention to these platforms, often leading to adverse consequences in multiple life domains1. Although problematic social media use and its negative impacts have been focal points of research attention, existing studies have predominantly explored general problematic social media use, while studies on problematic WeChat use(PWU) were relatively limited. PWU is associated with a decline in attention control ability, particularly among college students and adolescents. It may also interfere with the development of their executive functions and consume cognitive resources. The costs of PWU can manifest as reduced efficiency in task completion, compressed reading time, interrupted interpersonal communication, and even frequent emotional fluctuations. This is analogous to the impairment of a host’s adaptability as described in biology—during the process of being parasitized, the host’s original behavioral freedom and physiological stability are both adversely affected2.This excessive usage can consume significant time and energy, inevitably impacting one’s academic or professional efficiency3. Therefore, it is essential to study the influencing factors and mechanisms of action of PWU.

Social support and PWU

Rooted in the use and gratification theory, the propensity for social media use stems from the desire to fulfill cognitive and emotional needs. When individuals perceive a deficit in meeting these needs within their real-life interactions, they often resort to online social media platforms as a means of fulfillment. Consequently, a cycle ensues where prolonged engagement with social media heightens expectations and dependencies, potentially leading to addiction and loss of control over usage patterns. Research indicates a correlation between low social support and diminished self-esteem levels among individuals4,5, thereby predisposing them to negative self-appraisals. Moreover, studies have consistently demonstrated a negative association between social media addiction and life satisfaction. In essence, while social media serves as a conduit for social interaction and gratification, its excessive use can engender adverse psychological outcomes, particularly among college students grappling with real-world social support deficits and self-esteem challenges6,7. The emphasis on group belonging and shared responsibility in collectivism leads individuals to tend toward seeking and providing holistic support based on relational networks (such as resource mutual assistance at the family and community levels) in the context of social support. Individuals are more reliant on strong-tie networks (e.g., circles of relatives and friends, colleague groups) maintained through social media to obtain emotional and instrumental support, and the satisfaction with their support experience is also more susceptible to the evaluations of others in the relational network and the frequency of interactions. Social support and self-esteem, as core variables of individual self-assessment, have always received the attention of researchers. Davis proposed a “Cognitive-Behavioral Model” for the issue of PWU8. This model divides the factors influencing PWU into distal necessary conditions and proximal sufficient conditions. Distal necessary conditions include predisposing qualities and life events, while the proximal sufficient conditions primarily consist of maladaptive cognition, social isolation, and the lack of social support, among other factors. Hence, it is clear that social support is an important factor affecting PWU.

Life satisfaction as a mediating variable

Life satisfaction is a vital measure of individual well-being and quality of life, epitomizing a person’s general and stable cognitive judgment of their existence or its significant aspects9,10. According to the stress-buffering model, strengthening social support can buffer the negative impact of stress (such as depressive symptoms) and improve the quality of life11. The higher the level of social support obtained, the better they can cope with various challenges in their environment. Individuals with higher life satisfaction typically gain more positive experiences and emotional support in real life, making it easier for them to fulfill their psychological needs through real-life social interactions12, social support can also improve strained interpersonal relationships and build harmonious social networks, further consolidating a positive psychological state.

PWU Compensation Theory underscores a correlation between the impediments to fulfilling various needs in the daily lives of college students and the proliferation of negative emotions and dissatisfaction13,14. As they confront these barriers, students may increasingly flock to the digital world in search of gratification and a sense of accomplishment—a process which could be characterized as a form of “pathological compensation.” Further studies bolster the notion that life satisfaction is a critical forecaster of PWU15,16. Notably, if individuals (especially college students) experience disturbances in self-perception or emotional well-being due to insufficient social support, their resistance to PWU will significantly decrease17,18. This also implies that life satisfaction may play a mediating role in the relationship between social support and PWU19.

Current study

As a highly personalized interactive domain, social media does not exhibit a “single pattern” or “linear regularity” in terms of individuals’ usage behaviors, psychological experiences, and response styles. Instead, there exist significant qualitative differences. Individuals are not a “homogeneous group” in social media use; rather, there are distinct “Usage Profiles” — each profile represents a cluster of individuals with similar “motivation-experience-behavior” characteristics. If research only focuses on the average associations between variables (e.g., “the correlation between usage duration and loneliness”), it will fail to capture such essential individual differences, leading to research conclusions that cannot accurately reflect the real situations of different groups.

Previous studies have mostly explored the relationship between social support, life satisfaction, and PWU using variables as the center, and differentiated the degree of PWU based on questionnaire scores. However, this approach fails to identify the heterogeneity of PWU among different groups of individuals.The variable-centred strategy “ignores the fact that participants may come from different sub-populations in which the observed relations between variables may differ, quantitatively and qualitatively”20. This implies that sample heterogeneity may contribute to the correlations of personal value with mental health disorders and life satisfaction in the overall sample, which may differ markedly from such correlations within some or all subgroups. One superior alternative approach is latent profile analysis (LPA), a person-centred strategy for identifying latent homogeneous subgroups based on probability distributions21, allowing the analysis of the relationship between sub-population membership and a variety of outcome variables22. Additionally, LPA possesses several advantages over the related techniques of k-means or hierarchical cluster analysis, including non-reliance on common assumptions such as normality and linearity that are easily violated23.

In sum, despite the long-standing interest in PWU, empirical studies on inter-individual differences in PWU and their relationship with social support and life satisfaction are far fewer than one might expect. To narrow these research gaps, this study aims to characterize PWU profiles and to examine differences in social support and life satisfaction potentially across these profiles, theoretically drawing on Cognitive-behavior theory and methodologically using LPA. There are some key theoretical and practical necessities. Based on Cognitive Behavioral Theory and Compensation Theory, the research hypothesis proposes that in the social media environment, individuals’ perception and expectations of social support will affect their life satisfaction and may manifest through cognitive and behavioral responses, particularly social media usage. Specifically, individuals lacking social support or life satisfaction may tend to excessively use social media to seek a sense of belonging or compensate for psychological deficiencies, potentially leading to problematic social media addiction. Theoretically, this study sheds light on how different PWU types are related to social support and life satisfaction in novel or more complex ways in Chinese youth groups. Practically, this knowledge may also prevent and intervene in the development of college PWU.

Based on previous research, the current study seeks to further investigate how social support directly and indirectly influence PWU, along with the underlying cognitive mechanisms and pathways. The specific research questions to be addressed are as follows (Fig. 1).

Research Hypothesized Model.

H1: Social support may be an important predictor of PWU among college students.

H2: Social support may have a positive impact on life satisfaction.

H3: Life satisfaction may have a negative impact on different groups of PWU.

H4: Social support and life satisfaction as predictors of PWU to further examine the differences in their predictive effects on different groups of PWU.

H5: Life satisfaction plays a mediating role between social support and different groups of PWU.

Methods

Study design

This study used data from cross-sectional online surveys carried out in China. Data was gathered beginning on September 2 and was finished on October 10, 2020. The surveys were carried out online using SOJUMP (http://www.sojump.c om), a popular online survey platform in China. After reading the objectives and design of the study, participants willingly clicked the link in the announcement to access the survey platform. By asking each participant to read the terms and conditions and click “agree,” we were able to gain their informed consent online. After that, they answered questions about sociodemographic characteristics, social support, life satisfaction and PWU.

Participants

Using convenience sampling, a total of 8,700 undergraduates were recruited from colleges in Southwest China. Participant inclusion criteria were (a) undergraduates who lived in China and (b) were between 18 and 22 years old. There were no exclusion standards, and all undergraduates were included. 1000 special college student samples were excluded, including undergraduate students aged 14–17 and postgraduate students (master’s and doctoral candidates) aged 24–30, and 7000 (Mage = 20, SDage = 1.96, 58.7% male) valid questionnaires were obtained, for a response rate of 98.17%. Participants originate from a wide variety of cities nationally. The participants were from diverse academic disciplines (67.9% natural sciences, 32.1% social sciences).

Participants completed questionnaires on social support, life satisfaction, and PWU. Their vision was not corrected or corrected to normal, and they did not have color blindness or color vision defects. All participants had no history of drug abuse, addiction, or mental illness. They voluntarily participated in the study and received a certificate of participation. All participants provided written informed consent before participating in the study.The Chongqing University approved this study and experimental procedure, which was in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki (1991).

Measures

Social support

The Social Support Rating Scale developed by Xiao Shuiyuan (1994) was used to measure the social support status of the subjects24. The questionnaire includes 10 items, which are divided into three dimensions: subjective support (SSU), objective support (OSU), and utilization of support (US). Using a 1–4 rating scale, the scores of each dimension were calculated separately. The higher the score, the higher the level of subjective support and objective support of the subject, and the better the utilization of social support. The reliability of this questionnaire is 0.92, and the consistency of each item is between 0.89 and 0.94.

Life satisfaction

The Satisfaction With Life Scale (SWLS) is a 5-item measurement designed to assess global life satisfaction25, and its Chinese version was revised by Mantak Yuen in 2002. Researchers in China have consistently used this scale to assess students’ life satisfaction26,27. Participants’ responses range from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Higher scores indicate higher levels of life satisfaction. A representative item is: “In most ways, my life is close to my ideal.” In our sample, the reliability is very good (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.81).

PWU

In this study, WeChat Excessive Use Scale (WEUS) developed by Hou et al.(2017), it consists of 10 items rated on a 5-point scale and was used to test subjects’ social media use28. It includes three dimensions: emotional regulation, salience, and conflict.Items are rated on a Likert scale from “1” (“ rare “) to “5” (“ always “). In this study, the Cronbach’s α was 0.96.

Statistical analyses

This study used SPSS 22.0 and Mplus 8.3 for data processing. First, descriptive statistics and correlation analysis were conducted on the raw data in the questionnaire to determine the relationship between variables. A latent profile analysis (LPA) was conducted on 8,540 data to explore whether there were different categories of PWU among college students. LPA was used to investigate the optimal number of latent classes (profiles) that describe the students’ scores in different types of PWU among a sample of Chinese students. We used maximum likelihood estimation with robust standard errors in Mplus 8.3, which is robust to non-normality and non-independence when estimating standard errors and chi-square statistics. One to six class solutions of LPA models were evaluated and compared based on fit indices, parsimony, and interpretability. We adopted the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC)29, Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC)30, sample-size-adjusted Bayesian Information Criterion (aBIC)31, entropy, Lo- Mendell-Rubin test (LMR), and Bootstrap Likelihood Ratio Test (BLRT)32. Lower AIC, BIC and ABIC values indicate optimal model fit to the data33. Significant p-values (p < 0.05) of LMRT indicate that the target (e.g., k-class) model fits the data better than the one with fewer classes (e.g., k-1 class)34. A higher value of the entropy was indicative that the class solution has better goodness of fit, with values equal to or greater than 0.80, demonstrating a more accurate classification35. We estimated the random sets of starting values with 2000 seeds and 500 iterations to avoid local maxima. On this basis, a multivariate analysis of variance was used to explore the differences in social support and life satisfaction among different types of PWU groups. In addition, a multivariate logistic regression was conducted to explore the predictive role of social support and life satisfaction on different types of PWU groups. Finally, a mediation analysis was conducted to explore whether life satisfaction played a mediating role in social support and different types of PWU groups. Model 4 in the SPSS 22.0 PROCESS macro developed by Hayes was adopted, which is suitable for mediation analysis. The number of Bootstrap samples was set to 5000 to ensure the stability of the confidence interval, and the 95% bias-corrected confidence interval was used. If the 95% CI does not include 0, the indirect effect is considered significant; if the 95% CI includes 0, the indirect effect is considered non-significant.

Differences in social support and life satisfaction between different PWU groups

To examine the differences in social support and life satisfaction between different groups, we conducted a multiple factor analysis of variance with group as the independent variable and social support and life satisfaction as the dependent variables. The results showed significant differences in social support(F(5, 6994) = 14.30, p < 0.001) and life satisfaction(F(5, 6994) = 19.01, p = 0.001) among the groups. The LSD post hoc multiple comparison results showed that the social support of the Lowest PWU Group and the Very Low PWU Group was significantly higher than that of the High PWU Group, the Very High PWU Group, and the Highest PWU Group (p < 0.001; p < 0.001; p < 0.001); the life satisfaction of the Highest PWU Group was significantly lower than that of the Very High PWU Group, High PWU Group, the Very Low PWU Group, the Low PWU Group, and the Lowest PWU Group (p < 0.001; p < 0.001; p < 0.001; p < 0.001; p < 0.001).

Discussion

This study explored different potential categories of PWU among college students, and examined the impact of social support and life satisfaction as independent variables on these potential categories of PWU. The study identified, through Latent Profile Analysis (LPA), that college students’ PWU can be categorized into six distinct groups—lowest, very low, low, high, very high, and highest PWU groups—based on three dimensions of PWU: emotional regulation, salience, and conflict. This finding indicates that there is a certain degree of heterogeneity in PWU among college students. Additionally, the study revealed that life satisfaction plays a partial mediating role in the relationship between social support and PWU. Specifically, life satisfaction mediates the association between social support and PWU only among students with minimum, very low, low, high, and very high PWU levels; whereas this mediating effect is not significant in the group with the maximum PWU level.

This phenomenon may be attributed to a variety of reasons. From the perspective of the threshold effect37, there may exist a threshold in the process of social support influencing PWU. When PWU is at a relatively low level, social support exerts an impact on PWU by influencing life satisfaction. As the level of PWU gradually increases, the relationship between social support and life satisfaction may undergo changes. Before PWU reaches the highest level, life satisfaction can play a mediating role between social support and PWU—this means that social support, by enhancing life satisfaction, inhibits the increase of PWU to a certain extent. For instance, when individuals perceive more social support, their life satisfaction improves, which in turn reduces the negative use of social media. However, when PWU reaches the highest level, it may have exceeded the range that life satisfaction can regulate, that is, the threshold has been crossed. At this point, the impact of social support on life satisfaction is insufficient to alter the extremely high state of PWU, resulting in the failure of the mediating role of life satisfaction.

In addition, the failure of protective mechanisms may also explain this discrepancy. Typically, social support can be regarded as a protective factor, which buffers the negative impacts of PWU on individuals by enhancing life satisfaction38,39. When PWU is at levels ranging from low to very high, this protective mechanism operates normally: social support strengthens individuals’ psychological resilience, enabling them to maintain a relatively high level of life satisfaction when engaging with social media, thereby reducing negative usage behaviors. However, when PWU reaches the highest level, the original protective mechanism may fail—possibly due to excessive stress experienced by individuals or their long-term exposure to an unhealthy social media usage environment. Even if individuals perceive social support and have high life satisfaction, these factors cannot prevent PWU from remaining in an extremely high state. Existing studies have shown that long-term excessive social media use may alter the neural circuits in individuals’ brains, leading to the development of social media dependence; such dependence may be difficult to reverse through the regulation of social support and life satisfaction40,41. Furthermore, other factors may influence this mediating relationship. For example, individuals’ personality traits may play a moderating role. For those with specific personality traits (e.g., high neuroticism), even when sufficient social support is available and life satisfaction is high, they may be more prone to engaging in negative social media usage behaviors once PWU reaches an extremely high level—without being affected by the mediating role of life satisfaction42,43.

Existing studies have revealed that social support can significantly and negatively predict adolescent problem behaviors. There is an interactive relationship between life satisfaction and internalizing problem behaviors, as well as a mutual predictive relationship between the two. Additionally, a one-way predictive effect exists between life satisfaction and externalizing problem behaviors. However, previous studies have all adopted a variable-centered approach to exploration, neglecting the differences among individuals. Therefore, this study adopts a person-centered approach to investigate the roles of social support and life satisfaction in different categories of PWU. This finding fills the gap in person-centered research regarding the “link between behavioral profiles and psychological experiences” and provides a basis for the transformation of intervention strategies from “behavioral intervention” to “psychological empowerment”.

This study, based on cross-sectional data, has verified the predictive effect of perceived social support on PWU. However, it is important to note that a reciprocal relationship may exist between the two—PWU is not only a consequence of the lack of perceived social support, but may also inversely reduce individuals’ perceived social support levels through mechanisms such as social substitution and cognitive bias. This bidirectional association requires further verification in subsequent studies. The mediating role of life satisfaction in the relationship between social support and PWU varies across different PWU levels. Future studies could further explore these underlying mechanisms to gain a better understanding of the complex relationships among negative social media usage behaviors, social support, and life satisfaction.

Practical implications and limitations

To ensure the precision and operability of intervention measures for high-risk/severely high-risk groups across different PWU levels (Lowest, Very Low, Low, High, Very High, and Highest), we have developed a hierarchical plan based on the behavioral characteristics, risk triggers, and psychological needs of each group:

For the Highest group, the core lies in “professional medical intervention and personalized supervision”. We will collaborate with on-campus and off-campus professional institutions to provide one-on-one psychological counseling and establish a tripartite supervision mechanism. For the Very High group, the focus is on “behavioral guidance and psychological counseling”. Group counseling sessions will be conducted, and student volunteers will be assigned to serve as behavioral supervisors.For the High group, “environmental intervention and skills training” will be adopted. Reminder signs will be placed, and courses on time management and stress adjustment will be offered. For the Low, Very Low, and Lowest groups, “preventive education and regular monitoring” will be implemented, including popular science promotion activities and regular questionnaire surveys.

Meanwhile, universities can provide targeted support through the following initiatives: Establishing peer support groups: Recruiting and training volunteers under the auspices of relevant departments to carry out supervision, communication, and experience-sharing activities, as well as setting up a support hotline and online groups. Launching digital literacy workshops: Conducting batch-based courses with themed modules covering a wide range of content, and establishing follow-up records for participants. Implementing stress management programs: Divided into three phases (assessment, intervention, and feedback), with the corresponding courses integrated into freshmen orientation education.

While the study provided valuable insights, there are notable limitations that warrant attention. Adopting cross-sectional studies cannot reveal the causal relationship between variables. Future research should conduct longitudinal studies to reveal the relationship between these variables in a more comprehensive way. Additionally, the study relied solely on questionnaire-based data collection, and incorporating other methods, such as experiments, could enhance the robustness and replicability of the results.

Despite these limitations, This study aims to expand the understanding of the relationship between social support, life satisfaction, and PWU through latent profile analysis and mediation models, clarifying their interconnections. From a practical perspective, these findings are of significant importance in preventing students’ PWU. Families, teachers, counselors, and universities should take measures to enhance students’ life satisfaction, strengthen cognitive development, and reduce the risk of addiction. These efforts collectively contribute to the development of mental health among college students.
