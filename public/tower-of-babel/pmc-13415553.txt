Quercetin accelerates full-thickness burn wound repair by modulation of oxidative stress and inflammation
Authors: Divya Yadav, Ananya Mahajan, Alpesh K Sharma, Ashok Priyadarshi, Asheesh Gupta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBurn injuries disrupt the skin barrier, incite dysregulated inflammation, oxidative stress, and sepsis, which often delay healing. Quercetin, a bioactive flavonoid, has strong antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects; however, its specific molecular mechanisms in burn wound healing remain largely unexplored. This study systematically investigated the effects of topical application of quercetin (0.1% and 0.3%, w/v) on full-thickness burn wounds in rats and compared with silver sulfadiazine (1% SSD; reference care). Biochemical and molecular markers related to proliferation, angiogenesis, inflammation, and redox homeostasis were analysed using ELISA, histopathology, immunohistochemistry, and immunoblotting. Topical 0.3% quercetin treatment significantly (p < 0.05) accelerated healing seven days post-wounding by enhancing proliferation (PCNA, TGF-β1), re-epithelialization (cytokeratin 14), angiogenesis (CD31), ECM remodeling (fibronectin), wound contraction (α-SMA), and antioxidant defense (Nrf2, SOD, GST), compared with the control and SSD groups. Quercetin also significantly reduced inflammatory, pain-associated markers (NF-κB, TNF-α, COX-2, substance-P receptor), enhanced calcium homeostasis (TRPV3), and cytoprotection (GRP78). Histopathological observations revealed a reduced epithelial gap, well-organized collagen deposition, and decreased mast cell infiltration after quercetin treatment. Introduction

Burn wound repair is an intricate process, hindered by several challenges such as delayed tissue regeneration, exaggerated inflammation, oxidative stress, microbial infection, sepsis, tissue hypoxia, and altered metabolism. Despite advances in burn wound care, effective treatments remain limited, particularly for deep and extensive burns, leading to poor functional and aesthetic outcomes1. Complications like scarring, contractures, keloids, and persistent pain diminish the quality of life for burn patients.

Effective restoration of skin integrity following burns requires precise coordination of hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling phases2. Recent innovative therapeutic approaches, including bioactive dressings, skin grafts, and bioengineered scaffolds, appear promising but are expensive, and limited access prevents widespread adoption. The dearth of broadly accessible, cost-efficient, and universally effective treatments emphasizes the urgent need for research to develop safe and efficient healing strategies for impaired wounds3.

Herbal formulations have demonstrated therapeutic potential for enhancing the healing process through various mechanisms targeting different phases of burn wound repair. These natural remedies offer effective alternatives or complementary treatments to conventional burn care3–7. For instance, we previously demonstrated that Sea buckthorn leaf aqueous lyophilized extract (SBTL-ALE) significantly accelerated both acute and full-thickness burn wound healing by attenuating oxidative stress and inflammation and enhancing cellular proliferation, angiogenesis, collagen maturation, dermal hydration, and bioenergetic activation6,7. Phytochemical characterization of SBTL-ALE identified quercetin and its glycoside derivatives as major bioactive flavonoid constituents, which might be responsible for the observed healing effects. However, herbal extract-based approaches can limit mechanistic specificity, dose standardization, and reproducibility in translational research due to their multicomponent phytoconstituents.

Quercetin, a bioactive polyhydroxy flavonoid found in medicinal plants like Calendula officinalis, Hippophae rhamnoides, Hypericum perforatum, Ginkgo biloba, and Centella asiatica, has potent anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties8. Among herbal extracts used for burn repair, quercetin emerges as a pivotal phytochemical linking diverse remedies through shared pathways9,10. Quercetin’s antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties extend to the prevention and treatment of cardiovascular and pulmonary diseases, cancer, biofilm formation, and anti-immunosuppression8,11. Its antioxidant mechanisms include direct free radical scavenging, metal ion chelation, lipid peroxidation prevention, and antioxidant defense system modulation12,13.

Preclinical studies demonstrated that quercetin increased cellular glutathione, superoxide dismutase (SOD), and catalase, while reducing oxidative damage in cutaneous wounds through its potent antioxidant activity. Additionally, quercetin inhibited nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling in fibroblasts and keratinocytes, decreasing proinflammatory cytokines and upregulating anti-inflammatory mediators, thereby fostering a wound milieu conducive to tissue repair. Besides inflammation regulation, quercetin enhanced fibroblast migration and collagen deposition. At the vascular level, quercetin upregulated vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and cluster of differentiation 31 (CD31) expression, promoting angiogenesis critical for tissue regeneration and oxygen delivery14–17. Although doses of quercetin have been evaluated for wound healing, the optimal dosage regimens and the molecular mechanisms involved in transdermal burn wound repair are still not well understood14,15,18,19.

Therefore, the present study was undertaken to systematically evaluate the effectiveness of two topical quercetin concentrations (0.1% and 0.3%, w/v) and compare their effects with reference care silver sulfadiazine (1% SSD) on full-thickness burn wound healing in rats by analyzing the rate of wound contraction and assessing molecular markers associated with cellular proliferation, oxidative stress, inflammation, pain, cytoprotection, and histopathology.

Results

Quercetin accelerated wound contraction

The results showed that the quercetin treatment groups exhibited progressive burn wound healing compared to the burn control and reference care SSD groups. Additionally, the wound beds in both quercetin-treated groups showed no evidence of erythema, oedema, exudate, pus, or wound bleeding and exhibited improved margins. Indeed, the best outcome was observed in the 0.3% quercetin-treated group, with significantly faster wound contraction on days 4 and 8 (p < 0.05) compared to the both control and SSD-treated groups (Fig. 1).

Quercetin enhanced cellular proliferation, re-epithelialization, and collagen deposition

Immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis showed significantly enhanced expression of proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA) and cytokeratin 14 in the quercetin-treated groups as compared to the control and SSD groups (p < 0.05), which indicated enhanced cellular proliferation and re-epithelialization, respectively. The SSD group also showed a significant increase in cytokeratin 14, but not PCNA, compared with the control group (Fig. 2A–D). ELISA quantification further revealed significantly (p < 0.05) elevated transforming growth factor-beta1 (TGF-β1) levels in the quercetin-treated groups seven days post-wounding (Fig. 2E). Quercetin topical treatment (0.3%) showed the maximal proliferation and re-epithelialization among all treatment groups. In addition, the 0.3% quercetin group showed significantly higher DNA levels (54%) than the control and SSD groups, along with higher total protein (40%) than the control group (p < 0.05). In contrast, the SSD group showed no significant change in DNA and protein levels compared with the control group (Fig. 2F,G).

Quercetin reduced inflammation and pain markers

The 0.3% quercetin treatment significantly reduced the inflammatory response and pain mediators. Protein expression of NF-κB, tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), substance-P receptor, and cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) was significantly lower (p < 0.05) in the quercetin-treated groups compared with the control and SSD-treated groups (Fig. 6A–E). These findings suggest that quercetin reduces inflammation and pain markers during burn wound healing. In the SSD-treated group, TNF-α expression was also significantly reduced compared to the burn control group (p < 0.05).

Discussion

Burn care demands therapeutic interventions that manage both drug-resistant infections and hypertrophic scarring instigated by prolonged inflammation and oxidative damage. Additionally, the hypermetabolic response and nutritional deficiencies exacerbate tissue breakdown, while limited access to specialized burn centers and inadequate post-burn care impede optimal recovery1. These insights emphasize the need for developing effective and affordable therapeutic approaches that regulate inflammation and redox imbalance while promoting organized burn tissue repair.

Quercetin, a naturally occurring flavonoid, has garnered significant interest as a therapeutic agent for tissue repair due to its multifaceted pharmacological properties18. In wound healing, quercetin exerts strong antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anti-microbial, and pro-angiogenic effects, all essential for an effective healing process. Its ability to downregulate pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) helps in reducing excessive inflammation that can otherwise delay progression to the proliferative phase of the healing process20. Additionally, quercetin increases the production of key growth factors like VEGF and TGF-β1, promoting angiogenesis, enhancing granulation tissue formation, and faster re-epithelialization in excision wounds21,22.

The present findings showed that quercetin enhanced full-thickness burn wound healing in rats. Topical administration of 0.3% quercetin markedly accelerated wound contraction by promoting myofibroblast activity, enhancing ECM remodeling, and reducing inflammation. The wound healing effects of quercetin were validated through molecular marker analyses and were supported by histopathological findings compared with both the burn control and SSD-treated groups. Quercetin-treated wounds showed a more rapid reduction in wound size. Histological examination further revealed reduced inflammatory cell infiltration and improved re-epithelialization in the quercetin groups, indicating an expedited shift from the inflammatory to proliferative phase. These histological findings were corroborated by IHC analyses showing increased expression of PCNA and cytokeratin 14. PCNA is a well-established marker of active cell-cycle progression and reflects increased proliferative activity during tissue repair, whereas cytokeratin 14 is a characteristic marker of basal keratinocytes and regenerating epidermis, indicating keratinocyte activation and re-epithelialization. The increased expression of PCNA and cytokeratin 14 in quercetin-treated wounds further supported the enhanced proliferative response and improved epidermal restoration observed histologically. These findings are consistent with previously reported healing effects of quercetin in other wound models14,18,19. Furthermore, SSD can lead to the accumulation of silver, which may have cytotoxic effects on fibroblasts and keratinocytes, causing delayed healing23. However, SSD cream is commonly used for burn wounds as standard care in both preclinical and clinical studies; therefore, the present study also used SSD as the reference care24.

Treatment with 0.3% quercetin significantly enhanced TGF-β1 expression, the central orchestrator of burn wound healing across all phases, from initial inflammation through tissue remodelling25,26. TGF-β1 is rapidly released following thermal injury, with serum levels increasing from days 3–8 post-burn27. Platelets supply abundant TGF-β1, which promotes the influx of inflammatory cells (neutrophils, macrophages, lymphocytes) through chemotactic activity, initiating an inflammatory cascade for wound debridement and infection control. During the proliferation phase, TGF-β1 affects multiple cell types crucial for regeneration. It stimulates fibroblast proliferation and differentiation into contractile myofibroblasts, characterized by α-SMA expression and responsible for wound contraction and collagen deposition.

Local TGF-β1 administration restored myofibroblast differentiation and promoted wound contraction in CD18-/- mice28. TGF-β1 stimulates the production of key ECM components, particularly collagen types I and III, and enhances the angiogenic properties of endothelial progenitor cells, facilitating blood vessel development to supply healing tissue nutrients. Vascular formation supports the metabolic demands of proliferating cells in the wound bed25. This is further confirmed by enhanced CD31 expression levels observed in IHC analysis. Thus, quercetin promotes angiogenesis, a key part of the proliferative phase, by enhancing endothelial cell proliferation and migration. Additionally, the increased expression of the endothelial marker CD31 in quercetin-treated wounds indicates greater microvessel density, which enhances oxygen and nutrient delivery to the regenerating tissue.

Furthermore, Masson’s trichrome staining revealed a denser, more uniformly organized collagen network and significantly higher collagen intensity in 0.3% quercetin-treated wounds, signifying robust collagen deposition and maturation essential for wound closure. The increased collagen fiber deposition corresponds with increased α-SMA expression in myofibroblasts, further supporting quercetin’s role in promoting matrix remodeling. By regulating fibroblast activity, quercetin may normalize fibronectin deposition, preventing excessive matrix accumulation that can lead to hypertrophic scarring. Simultaneously, it promotes myofibroblast differentiation, as evidenced by increased α-SMA expression, which aids in effective wound contraction, and the findings are in accordance with Kant et al. 202021. A study showed that quercetin modulated fibroblast integrins via increasing αV and lowering β1, which reduced scarring without delaying wound closure. These effects emphasize quercetin’s potential for promoting efficient, scarless skin repair29,30. Together, these histopathological findings confirm that quercetin not only accelerates wound contraction by modulating cellular dynamics but also ensures high-quality repair through controlled collagen architecture, thereby reducing scar formation.

In addition to modulating key ECM and cellular markers like fibronectin and α-SMA, quercetin increased TRPV3 and GRP78 protein expression, thereby strengthening the endoplasmic reticulum’s capacity to manage protein-folding demands in injured tissue. Through its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory actions, quercetin protects TRPV3 channel activity, facilitating efficient calcium-dependent cell migration and bacterial clearance essential for re-epithelialization31. In wounds treated with quercetin, higher GRP78 expression functions as a molecular chaperone, enhancing the processing and maturation of secretory proteins such as collagen and growth factors that are essential for granulation tissue formation and re-epithelialization. This increase in GRP78 promotes survival pathways in keratinocytes and fibroblasts by alleviating unresolved ER stress, preventing apoptosis, and maintaining calcium homeostasis32. By increasing TRPV3 and GRP78, quercetin ensures that the biosynthetic machinery of regenerating cells remains robust under oxidative and inflammatory conditions, thereby accelerating the proliferative phase and improving matrix deposition. These diverse functions collectively suggest that quercetin is a potent adjunctive agent that orchestrates ECM remodeling, cellular contractility, ion channel-mediated migration, and proteostasis to enhance burn wound repair.

IHC analysis showed increased Nrf2 expression in quercetin-treated wounds. This implies that quercetin improved burn wound healing by stimulating cellular defense signals through activation of Nrf2, the key antioxidant transcription factor. During burn injury, excessive production of ROS creates a harmful microenvironment that impairs cellular viability and delays healing. Quercetin activates the Nrf2/Keap1/HO-1 pathway, promoting Nrf2 nuclear translocation and transcriptional upregulation of critical downstream antioxidant enzymes33. Our findings revealed that 0.3% quercetin treatment significantly increased SOD and GST activities, establishing a robust antioxidant defense network.

Quercetin treatment increased SOD, which accelerated the dismutation of superoxide anions into hydrogen peroxide, thereby reducing superoxide-mediated lipid peroxidation and prevents proteolytic damage in the wound milieu16. Concurrently, quercetin enhanced GST activity, a phase II detoxification enzyme that conjugates reduced GSH with electrophilic ROS byproducts, facilitating their neutralization and elimination. Additionally, increased GSH turnover supports collagen synthesis and facilitates myofibroblast-mediated wound contraction, both essential for tissue repair34. Our study showed that increased activities of SOD and GST helps shift the wound environment toward healing. Reduced oxidative stress maintains growth factor signaling and matrix integrity during wound repair.

The current findings showed that quercetin treatment reduced inflammatory responses in burn wounds by decreasing the protein expression of key mediators, including NF-κB, TNF-α, COX-2, and substance-P receptor. Previous studies have reported that quercetin suppresses NF-κB activation at the transcriptional level, thereby reducing the transcription of pro-inflammatory genes. As a result, the levels of TNF-α and IL-1β are significantly downregulated, limiting the pro-inflammatory cytokine cascade that prolongs inflammation and impedes the healing process17,35. Additionally, quercetin downregulates COX-2 expression, which plausibly limits prostaglandin E2 synthesis by suppressing the PI3K/Akt and MAPK pathways, thus reducing prostanoid-mediated edema and pain-associated markers36. During the acute phase of burn injury, substance-P released from sensory nerves amplifies neurogenic inflammation and increases vascular permeability; quercetin treatment alleviates this by stabilizing mast cells and inhibiting neuropeptide-induced degranulation, thereby reducing plasma extravasation37. By targeting pain markers (substance-P receptor and COX-2), and NF-κB signaling pathways, which reduce the production of inflammatory mediators (TNF-α and IL-1β), quercetin orchestrates a broad-spectrum anti-inflammatory environment that expedites inflammation resolution, promotes granulation tissue formation, and enhances overall burn wound healing.

Similarly, a study showed that quercetin accelerated wound healing in diabetic rats by promoting macrophage polarization from pro-inflammatory M1 (iNOS+) to anti-inflammatory M2 (CD206+) phenotypes. Quercetin at a concentration of 40 mg/mL resulted in faster wound contraction and improved tissue repair, as evidenced by decreased inflammatory marker (TNF-α), increased levels of anti-inflammatory cytokines (interleukin-10) and angiogenic factors (VEGF and CD31)15. Overall, as depicted in Fig. 7, the anti-inflammatory, pro-angiogenic, and potent antioxidant properties of quercetin position it as a promising therapeutic agent that can synchronize the different phases of wound healing and fill the persistent gaps in burn care management. The current findings indicate that quercetin (0.3%) accelerates neovascularization, promotes collagen deposition, facilitates cytoprotection, decreases inflammatory responses, and maintains redox homeostasis to enhance the healing of full-thickness burn wounds.

Conclusions

Overall, our findings demonstrated that topical application of quercetin accelerated burn wound healing through the regulation of multiple mechanisms, including activating the Nrf2-driven antioxidant defense, suppressing NF-κB-mediated pro‐inflammatory signaling, and promoting TGF-β1-mediated tissue regeneration. Quercetin 0.3% promoted faster wound closure, enhanced angiogenesis, collagen deposition, and restored epithelial integrity more effectively than the control and SSD (reference care) groups. Further research should aim to improve quercetin bioavailability, develop sustained‐release systems, and rigorously evaluate long‐term safety. Nevertheless, the present results suggest that quercetin is a safe and effective therapeutic candidate for augmenting burn wound care and supporting the natural healing process.

Methods

All methods are reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org).

Experimental animals

Healthy male Sprague-Dawley rats, 8–10 weeks old and weighing 200 ± 20 g, were used in this study. The rats were housed in the institutional animal facility under a 12-hour light/dark cycle at an ambient temperature of 25 ± 1 °C, with access to rodent diet and water ad libitum. The experimental protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Ethical Committee (IAEC/ DIPAS/2025/07), which is accredited by the Committee for Control and Supervision of Experiments on Animals (CCSEA), Government of India. All the animal studies and protocols were in accordance with the standards outlined in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (National Academy of Sciences, Washington, DC). Efforts were made to minimize animal suffering during the experimental procedures.

Full-thickness burn wound model

Rats were anesthetized via administration of a ketamine and xylazine cocktail intraperitoneally (90:10 milligrams per kilogram of body weight). Using an electric fur cutter, the dorsal hair of the animals was shaved, and the skin surface was wiped with alcohol. Subsequently, a metallic probe with a diameter of 15 mm was preheated to 85 °C and placed on the dorsal side of the animal for twenty seconds to induce a full-thickness transdermal burn, followed by resuscitation, as reported earlier38. After burn induction, rats were individually caged under standard conditions. Twenty-four hours post-burn, the injured tissue was excised using surgical tools. Burn wounds remained open throughout the entire treatment period.

Experimental plan

Quercetin [2-(3,4-Dihydroxyphenyl)-3,5,7-trihydroxy-4H-1-benzopyran-4-one] was procured from Sigma-Aldrich (Q4951; source: synthetic; >95% purity) and prepared in propane-1,2-diol [propylene glycol (PG)]; S.D. Fine, India. PG acts as a humectant and penetration enhancer in topical wound healing formulations by facilitating hydration and transdermal permeation39–41. The solution was freshly prepared and stored under refrigeration.

Initially, a dose-response study was performed to evaluate the healing efficacy of quercetin for burn wound repair in rats by selecting different doses of 0.03%, 0.1%, 0.3%, and 3% (w/v) prepared in PG and applied topically. The preliminary dose-response study showed that quercetin was effective at two doses, i.e., 0.1% and 0.3%. Subsequently, detailed biophysical, biochemical, molecular, histopathological, and histomorphometric analyses of wound healing were performed using these two doses.

A total of 20 animals were randomly assigned into four groups: a burn control group treated with vehicle alone (propylene glycol), a 0.1% quercetin-treated group, a 0.3% quercetin-treated group, and a reference care group treated with 1% silver sulfadiazine cream. In the quercetin-treated groups, 200 µL of the respective quercetin was topically administered to the wound site twice daily for seven days. On day 8, all animals were euthanized via intraperitoneal injection of thiopental (90 mg/kg), and the regenerated wound tissues were collected for biochemical, molecular, histopathological, and immunohistochemical analyses.

Wound contraction, DNA, and total protein estimations

Digital photographs of the wounds were taken at 0 (day of injury), 4th, and 8th day using a digital camera (Sony, DSC-W800) alongside a ruler to calibrate measurements of the wound area. The wound surface area was estimated using ImageJ (NIH, USA) and expressed in mm2, as previously described38. The granulation tissue was excised seven days after wounding to estimate DNA42 and total protein contents43.

Histopathological and morphometric analyses

Biopsy samples collected seven days after wounding were preserved in 10% formalin, then embedded in paraffin and used to prepare thin sections (5 μm). These sections were stained with Hematoxylin-eosin and Masson’s trichrome. Mast cells in paraffin-embedded wound tissue sections were stained using the Eosinophil-Mast Cell Stain Kit (#ab150665, Abcam) as per the manufacturer’s instructions, counterstained using hematoxylin, and mounted with DPX. Histopathological changes were examined under the light microscope (Olympus Microscope BX61) at different magnifications (100X and 400X). Quantification of inflammatory cells and collagen intensity was performed and expressed as cells/mm2 and collagen intensity (%), respectively, using ImageJ44,45. Mast cells were quantified using ImageJ and expressed as mast cells/site46. Additionally, epithelial gap analysis was performed at 40X magnification using ImageJ and expressed in mm.

Immunohistochemical analysis

Immunohistochemistry was performed using primary antibodies against PCNA (AF0239, Affinity Biosciences), cytokeratin-14 (MA5-32214, Invitrogen), CD31 (EPR17259, Abcam), and Nrf2 (AB32280, Elabscience) at a 1:500 dilution in diluent buffer, incubated overnight. The next day, tissue sections were treated with a fluorescence-labelled secondary antibody (1:500 dilution), goat anti-rabbit IgG Alexa Fluor 488 and 594 (A-11008 and R37117, Invitrogen). Slides were counterstained with diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; P36962, Invitrogen) for mounting. Images were captured via fluorescence microscopy and analyzed using image analysis software, ImageJ. Changes in expression levels were indicated as integrated fluorescence intensity (% control).

TGF-β1 ELISA quantification

TGF-β1 was measured using a sandwich‐ELISA kit (E-EL-R0084; Elabscience) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, tissue lysate was prepared in ice-cold 1x radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (20–188; Sigma‐Aldrich), and the supernatant was added to a pre-coated micro-ELISA plate and kept at 37 °C for 90 min. After discarding the supernatant, biotinylated antibody was added and kept at 37 °C for 60 min. Following washing, HRP‐conjugate was added and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C, then washed again. Under dark conditions, substrate solution was added and left for 15 min. Lastly, the stop solution was added, and immediately, optical density was measured spectrophotometrically at 450 nm. The observations were represented as picograms per milligram of protein.

Western blot analysis

The granulation wound tissue was treated with chilled 1x RIPA buffer containing a cocktail of protease inhibitors (Sigma-Aldrich) to prepare a 20% (w/v) skin tissue homogenate. After sonicating the homogenate, it was further spun at 4000 x g for 30 min at 4 °C, and then the supernatant was stored and used for immunoblotting to analyse protein expression. Concentration of protein was measured via the Lowry method43. 40 micrograms of total protein were separated on a 10% sodium dodecyl sulphate-polyacrylamide gel and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes. Blocked overnight using a blocking agent and the next day incubated at room temperature with primary antibodies for 2.5 h, followed by secondary antibodies for 1.5 h. The primary antibodies included anti-TRPV3, anti-COX-2, and anti-GRP78 (#ab85022, ab15191, ab21685; Abcam), anti-TNF-α and anti-β-actin (#AF7014, T0022; Affinity Biosciences), anti-fibronectin, anti-substance P receptor, and anti-α-SMA (#F3648, S8305, A2547; Sigma-Aldrich), and anti-NF-κB (51-3500; Invitrogen). Immunoreactive bands were detected with a chemiluminescent substrate (CPS1120; Sigma-Aldrich) and captured on autoradiographic film. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ and are presented as mean intensity (% control), with β-actin serving as the internal loading control.

Analysis of endogenous antioxidants and oxidative stress

A 10% (w/v) tissue lysate prepared in KCl-EDTA buffer was utilized to measure SOD and GST activity. SOD activity in the tissue homogenate was represented as units per milligram of protein, where one unit corresponds to the enzyme concentration needed to block auto-oxidation by 50%47. GST activity was determined using the extinction coefficient (εCDNB = 9600 M−1cm− 1)48. Total protein concentrations were quantified by the Lowry method43.

Statistical analysis

Data are represented as mean ± standard error. Data analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism version 8.4.3 software. Statistical comparisons were performed using two-way ANOVA, followed by post-hoc Bonferroni’s test to compare wound contraction between the groups as one factor and post-wounding days as another factor. For other parameters, one-way ANOVA followed by post-hoc Bonferroni’s test was used. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
