Qualitative exploration of consumers’ experiences and perceptions of telehealth for allied health services in Australia
Authors: Deniz Senyel, Madison Frith, Katherine Burnard, Alison Coughlan, Feby Savira, Richard Norman, Suzanne Robinson, James Boyd
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe COVID-19 pandemic accelerated telehealth adoption, offering remote consultations via phone and video. Allied health services are one part of the healthcare system where telehealth, in specific teleconsultations, has been applied and has shown promising results in improving healthcare access by breaking down financial, logistical and geographic barriers. However, more insight is needed into the consumer’s perspective. A consumer is anyone who has used, currently uses or will use telehealth for allied health services. Therefore, this study explores consumer experiences and preferences, identifying barriers and facilitators to telehealth for allied health services.MethodsThis qualitative study used focus group discussions to evaluate consumers’ experiences with telehealth for allied health services. Allied health was defined as healthcare professionals distinct from medical, dental and nursing fields. Eight focus groups with 57 participants were conducted. The participants were recruited from the general public as well as seldom represented communities such as a support service focused on improving and maintaining members’ mental health, the deaf and hard-of-hearing community, young disabled people and the Pacific Islander community. An inductive thematic analysis was used to analyse the data.ResultsFive main themes were identified. First, the consumer with their individual characteristics and context played a major role in the suitability of telehealth. Second, the allied health practitioner and their skills influenced the quality and therefore the success of remote consultations. Further, the relationship between consumer and practitioner contributed to the success of telehealth. The appointment itself was equally often discussed. While telehealth improved access to care, remote appointments were seen as more suitable for questions and verbal exchanges. Lastly, the technology was an important factor with the availability of necessary technology and the accessibility of it playing a central role.ConclusionsThe findings reinforce existing research while highlighting new insights from often under-represented groups, emphasising the importance of telehealth choice, accessible technology and quality standards.

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated the adoption of telehealth technology across the globe.1 2 Many healthcare systems implemented telehealth applications to help reduce infection risks and contain the spread of the virus.3 Although the initial surge in telehealth use was primarily driven by the need to minimise in-person interactions,3 telehealth offers numerous other benefits that can enhance healthcare delivery well into the postpandemic era.4 While telehealth is often used interchangeably with telemedicine, Bashshur et al5 defined telehealth as a distinct subcategory that encompasses areas such as health behaviour and education, as well as health management and policy. Telehealth sits alongside E-health, which includes electronic health records, clinical decision support systems and physician order entry, as well as M-health, which refers to mobile technologies such as smartphone applications.

Bashshur et al5 further describe three key dimensions of telehealth. The first dimension is the technology, which can be synchronous or asynchronous. Examples include live teleconsultations, where information is exchanged in real time, or asynchronous communication such as email exchanges. The second dimension is functionality, which can involve diagnosis, consultation, monitoring or mentoring. The third dimension is application, which describes the context in which the intervention is used, including the treatment, specialty, disease and setting. In this study, we focus on the telehealth application of synchronous teleconsultations encompassing all functionalities of diagnosis, consultation, monitoring and mentoring across the full range of all allied health professions.

Virtual consultations allow general practitioners, specialists and other healthcare providers, such as allied health professionals, to connect with patients in real time, regardless of geographical barriers. The advantages of teleconsultations include reduced travel time, increased convenience and improved accessibility to healthcare services.6 This is especially critical for large countries with vast, sparsely populated areas, such as Australia, where teleconsultation can potentially serve as a vital tool for improving equitable access to healthcare.7

Telehealth is used across the healthcare system, with one important application being allied health consultations. The term ‘allied health’ is relatively new and lacks a universal definition. Various governments, health providers, insurers and educators classify professions differently under this category. However, there is consensus on key principles: allied health professionals are distinct from medical, dental and nursing fields. They specialise in preventing, diagnosing and treating various conditions. Often working in multidisciplinary teams, they provide targeted support for diverse patient needs.8 For example, in Australia, allied health professionals represent a substantial segment (38%) of the healthcare workforce and play an essential role in delivering comprehensive and multidisciplinary care.9 Allied Health Professions Australia, the national advocacy organisation for allied health professionals, lists over 20 different professions,10 with the biggest groups including psychologists, physiotherapists and occupational therapists.11 Prior research suggests that telehealth enables allied health professionals to deliver high-quality care remotely, overcoming the geographical and logistical barriers associated with traditional face-to-face appointments.12 Further advantages include reduction in time, travel and cost for both patients and providers.1317 Telehealth also provides convenience and comfort to both patients and allied health professionals.1821 However, telehealth also presents challenges. For some allied health providers, the patient–provider relationship may be negatively affected, as virtual care can impede rapport building and emotional connection.131522 23 Satisfaction with care provision varies across professions, particularly in areas such as physiotherapy, where the absence of hands-on treatment can limit effectiveness.2426 Conversely, for certain allied health professionals, such as psychologists, telehealth can actually improve care, as some patients find it easier to engage and disclose personal information from the comfort of their own home.27

For consumers, the geographical location can impact their preference for telehealth for allied health services, but it is not the sole determining factor. A variety of other factors also play a role in shaping whether an individual is willing or able to engage with telehealth services. These factors can include sociodemographic characteristics, such as age, socioeconomic status and education level, as well as previous experiences with telehealth and individual personal beliefs.6 28 29 Some individuals may have privacy concerns or prefer in-person care, while others may find telehealth more convenient and supportive compared with traditional face-to-face consultations. The suitability of telehealth services is impacted by the allied health discipline and the nature of care provided and required. Understanding consumer perceptions and experiences with telehealth is essential, as these insights can guide the development of more person-centred telehealth models, making healthcare more inclusive and responsive to diverse needs.

The aim of this study was to gain a deeper understanding of consumer experiences with allied health services delivered through telehealth in Australia. Specifically, the study sought to identify and explore the perceived barriers and facilitators associated with accessing allied health services via telehealth. The guiding research question was: What barriers and facilitators do consumers perceive when accessing allied health services via telehealth in Australia?

Methods

This study is reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research Guidelines (online supplemental file 1).30

This qualitative study used a participatory design approach to explore consumer preferences and experiences with allied health services delivered through telehealth.

Patient and public involvement

The project was conducted in partnership with the Health Consumers Centre (HCC), at the Institute for Health Transformation, Deakin University. The HCC is the leading health consumer agency in Victoria, Australia, which facilitates consumer engagement in health research and health system transformation. HCC facilitated the establishment of a consumer reference group (CRG) which then helped shape the project from design to execution. The CRG consists of seven community members with diverse backgrounds and experience of the healthcare system. Drafted questions for the focus groups were presented to the CRG, who then provided feedback to ensure that the questions were appropriate. The CRG also provided feedback on the focus group expression of interest process and promotional material to aid in making the recruitment process more accessible and inclusive.

Participants and recruitment

Recruitment was led by the HCC and followed a purposive sampling strategy. The aim was to hold eight focus groups, each comprising eight participants. First, all participants responding to the expression of interest call were filtered based on the eligibility criteria. Participants were eligible if they lived in Australia, had previously accessed allied health services and had adequate connectivity access to enable them to use telehealth. Not having previous experiences with telehealth was not an exclusion criterion. The remaining applicants were reviewed based on gender, age, location, education level, cultural and linguistic background and health experiences. Two team members of the HCC independently shortlisted the applicants based on the desire for diversity across as many dimensions as possible. The team members then met and reviewed the shortlist and arrived at the final group to be offered a place in the focus group. The focus groups were designed to include a diverse range of participants, incorporating both members of the general population (in four online sessions) and individuals from specific communities (in four in-person, online or hybrid sessions). The focus groups involving community-specific participants were organised and facilitated in collaboration with population-specific and condition-specific organisations based in Victoria, as this is where HCC primarily operates. The Deaf Victoria focus group was held as a hybrid session at the request of two participants who were unable to attend in person. Meaningful participation was ensured by including two Auslan interpreters, a notetaker, a captioner, two HCC staff as well as a Deaf Victoria staff member. The YDAS group was offered as a hybrid but shifted to an online group based on the preferences of the participants. The other two groups were both in-person sessions run in the local community of the participants.

To recruit participants from the general population, the study used the monthly newsletter of the HCC and the social media channels of both HCC and Deakin University. Recruitment for the online focus groups aimed to prioritise involvement of consumers outside of Victoria, given the participants recruited in collaboration with the population-specific and condition-specific organisations were all Victorian.

A total of 57 consumers participated in the focus groups. Although each group was initially assigned at least eight participants, last-minute cancellations resulted in some sessions having fewer attendees than planned. Five of the focus groups ended up with seven participants, two groups had eight participants and one group had six participants.

Focus group discussions

Focus group discussions lasted 2 hours and were held in-person (n=2), online (n=5), and hybrid (n=1). The in-person and hybrid focus group discussion was held at Deakin University, a participating organisation’s clubhouse and a community centre. The focus groups took place between 11 June 2024 and 25 July 2024. No prior relationship between participants and facilitators existed. However, participants were aware of the role of the HCC and the project aims. Discussions focused on previous experiences with allied health (via telehealth) as well as perceived barriers and facilitators for allied health services via telehealth. Participants were renumerated for their participation. All eight focus groups were facilitated by an HCC team member. Four of the focus groups were co-facilitated with a staff member of the condition-specific or population-specific organisation. Besides the participants, interpreters were at times present in the focus groups. The four general online focus groups were co-facilitated with a member of the study’s CRG. The focus group discussion guide was developed by the research team based on previous literature reviews and subsequently reviewed and refined by the CRG. It is attached as online supplemental file 2. The focus group discussions were audio recorded and transcribed verbatim.

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted with NVivo software using an inductive thematic analysis approach using the framework outlined by Braun and Clarke.31 This process involved several stages: first, familiarisation with the data to gain a deep understanding of the content; second, generating initial codes to capture key features; third, searching for themes by grouping related codes; fourth, reviewing the themes to ensure they accurately represented the data; fifth, defining and naming the themes to convey their essence; and finally, producing the report to summarise the findings.

Reflexive accounting

Since this qualitative study is part of a larger mixed-methods project, the research team brings together diverse skills to support different aspects of the research. For this phase, two female members of the HCC, KB and AC, organised and facilitated the focus groups. Both have extensive experience working with consumer groups and conducting qualitative research. DS, a female PhD student at La Trobe University, specialises in descriptive qualitative research and was responsible for the data analysis. Additionally, MF, a female research assistant with prior experience in qualitative and mixed-methods research, provided support for the project. SR, a female health economics professor, has extensive expertise in consumer health and qualitative research.

Lastly, FS, a female research fellow holding a PhD, along with JB and RN, both male professors, contributes expertise in quantitative methods, which will be applied in later stages of the project.

Themes

Participants access a wide variety of allied health services including: psychologists and psychotherapists, physiotherapists and exercise physiologists, occupational therapists, dietitians, art therapists, lactation consultants, social workers, chiropractors and pharmacists. Lastly, speech pathologists were mentioned in relation to participants’ children.

Five main themes emerged from the focus group discussions: consumer, allied health professionals, relationship between consumer and allied health professional, appointment and technology (online supplemental file 3).

Consumer

The theme of ‘consumer’ encompasses all person-specific factors that influence the telehealth experience. This included factors such as physical and mental health, personal beliefs and perceptions, and personal context and circumstances.

Many participants emphasised the importance of having a private space for telehealth, especially when living with others or when attending sensitive appointments. Having limited internet access at home can necessitate the use of public spaces for telehealth consultations, compromising privacy. Participants suggested that community and medical centres could provide private rooms to facilitate secure telehealth appointments.

I was even thinking about the poor people sitting in libraries in one big room having to do telehealth consultations with nosy people sitting next to them and having to speak about their intimate confidential stuff with health practitioners online next to strangers!(P28)

Participants often felt more comfortable and emotionally expressive at home, appreciating familiar surroundings during telehealth sessions. Neurodivergent participants reported that home-based telehealth appointments were less overwhelming due to reduced sensory stimuli and the ability to openly facilitate stimming behaviours.

I’m autistic and I think a lot better when I’m stimming, and I’ve found, even subconsciously, every single session I’m always, you know, just mindlessly drawing or just fidgeting and stuff. And it makes it a lot easier to discuss what we’re discussing, and I feel like that’s been a limitation in person, like counselling sessions, that I haven’t felt fully comfortable yet to be able to, you know, self-regulate like that. (P27)

Those with chronic illnesses or mobility issues also preferred telehealth, though a couple of participants valued in-person appointments as a reason to leave the house. Telehealth was seen as potentially more accessible for some people with mental health needs.

Oh yes and if I can’t get to my in-person appointment on time because of a migraine hitting or pain flaring or my mental health worsening I like to be able to call reception at the last minute and changing to telehealth so it’s much less pressure. (P28)

Travel reduction was seen as beneficial in terms of time and cost savings, especially if telehealth could prevent interstate travel. For people with mental health issues or physical disabilities, reducing travel was a major advantage. Telehealth was particularly valuable for rural residents, although regional internet connectivity issues posed challenges. Factors such as reduced travel and the comfort of one’s own home led to telehealth often being described as convenient.

It’s kind of niche but I struggle with driving a lot, likely due to being autistic which means I can’t really get to any allied health professionals in person without a lot of effort of organising lifts. (P27)

The ability to choose between telehealth and in-person appointments was widely appreciated by participants. Some participants expressed frustration when options were limited or unavailable post-COVID.

Digital literacy also played a significant role in telehealth experiences. While technological proficiency facilitated telehealth use, lower digital literacy was perceived as a barrier. Participants especially highlighted older age and culturally and racially marginalised/culturally and linguistically diverse status as potential contributing factors to lower digital literacy. For those with low-tech literacy, instructions or support from healthcare professionals were considered necessary.

Additionally, some perceived telehealth as lower quality and therefore less valuable than in-person appointments, based on past experiences or assumptions.

Allied health professionals

The second theme was the ‘allied health professional’, who played a vital role in the perception and experience of telehealth by consumers. The attitude of allied health professionals towards telehealth was noted as influential, with a negative attitude potentially hindering the effectiveness of appointments.

Maintaining professionalism during telehealth consultations was highlighted by participants, who expected providers to maintain a distraction-free, professional environment. Concerns were raised about practitioners possibly multitasking during sessions, which could undermine consumer confidence.

Participants highlighted the need for allied health professionals to have strong telehealth skills, including the ability to adapt sessions to a virtual format, use technology effectively and address accessibility needs, such as enabling captions. Training in telehealth was recommended to equip professionals with the necessary skills to facilitate high quality healthcare via telehealth.

There should be more education for allied health professionals and continuing professional development on how impactful and helpful telehealth services can be for certain people and also like more understanding on different access needs. (P40)

Additional training should also be provided to allied health professionals to enhance their communication skills when engaging with deaf and hard-of-hearing individuals, ensuring effective communication and respecting patient autonomy. Formal qualifications in both telehealth and deaf awareness could help members of the community identify skilled professionals.

Participants expressed the need for quality and practice standards for telehealth. These standards should ensure that telehealth sessions offer the same quality as in-person visits. Guidelines should outline appropriate technology and platforms, address the needs of people with disabilities, and include a risk assessment checklist to determine the suitability of telehealth for each consumer. Guidelines should also specify how people are informed about telehealth appointments and what information should be provided to them prior to the appointment. These standards would aim to improve consistency and reliability in telehealth services.

Relationship between consumer and allied health professional

The ‘relationship’ between consumers and allied health professionals was often seen as an important factor for telehealth. The Engage Pasefika community group specifically highlighted the importance of community and relationships:

I think stressing the importance of like relationships with our health professionals and coming back to community, I mean, yeah, so community is a really big thing and I feel like all of these phone appointments, they just take away from that and just force us to be independent when we really thrive when we build these connections with people who are here to look after us, you know, but also so we can look after each other and hopefully them as well. (P09)

Participants were generally more forgiving of minor issues during telehealth if they had a strong rapport with their provider. However, relationships could feel different in telehealth settings. While some participants valued a hybrid care model of both face-to-face and telehealth appointments, one expressed hesitation about meeting their provider in person after primarily engaging in online interactions. Most participants preferred an initial in-person appointment to help establish a relationship, with telehealth as an option for subsequent appointments if appropriate. Telehealth was also noted for helping to equalise the power dynamic between consumers and professionals.

So for example, if the condition is chronic and you’re going to see that person for a long time, say months or years, I would value especially for the first few appointments, being able to build rapport, understanding their personality and how they approach the issue with you and then I might switch to telehealth. (P17)

Communication was generally perceived as less effective in telehealth, particularly due to less visual cues, making it difficult to interpret body language and social signals. This was a particular concern for those who rely on lip-reading. Allied health professionals were encouraged to ensure consumer comprehension during telehealth, especially for individuals with low health literacy or those whose first language was not English. People with anxiety or communication difficulties faced challenges as well; one participant mentioned adopting a ‘fake’ voice due to anxiety on phone calls, making it difficult to be authentic. In group therapy via telehealth, participants found conversations hard to follow due to people speaking over each other.

Deaf and hard-of-hearing participants faced unique barriers in telehealth. Lip-reading was often compromised on a video call, and captions were unreliable. Phone calls were generally seen as inaccessible for this group.

Some neurodivergent participants found telehealth preferable, as it allowed them to avoid masking behaviours often needed in person. Telehealth enabled them to feel more comfortable and to communicate more effectively with their providers.

The mental toll it takes to ‘mask’ my autistic and ADHD traits in person, which I do much more in person than on telehealth (P28)

Appointment

The allied health ‘appointment’, which included who it was with, what it was for and how it was delivered, was another important theme during the focus group discussions. Overall, telehealth was found to increase access to healthcare services. In cases where individuals could not travel to the clinic due to distance, access needs, limited public transport or being cut-off by natural disasters, telehealth offered a viable alternative to in-person appointments. Further, participants appreciated being able to choose from a wider selection of allied health professionals as they were not bound by location. This made it possible to see specialist allied health providers or provide continuity of care if patients moved or if there was not a suitable local provider.

I wanted to add that telehealth has given me much more choice of allied healthcare professionals. I’ve been able to work with professionals who are experts in my specific needs who work in other states. (P25)

Providing consumers with enough information was seen as important. This starts with training people on how to use technology or providing resources and guidance on telehealth. This could be in the form of instructions given to the patient in person or sent via email. Further, the instructions should include information about confidentiality and privacy, if the sessions will be recorded, and costs and fees.

Telehealth was often seen as more suitable for appointments in which only verbal information was conveyed. Some examples of potentially more telehealth-appropriate professions were psychology, counselling, dietitians and pharmacists. However, participants expressed safety concerns regarding telehealth psychology appointments, particularly for people experiencing emotional distress. Appointments involving physical activities, such as assessments or exercises, were considered less suitable for telehealth. This included services like physiotherapy, occupational therapy, audiology, optometry, chiropractic care and podiatry. Participants also reported not having the right equipment, such as exercise equipment, at home to achieve the same results as when attending appointments in person. One participant had safety concerns about executing exercises by themselves.

I was thinking that for things like OT assessments, physio assessments and for manual therapy and even demonstrating exercises etc., they really wouldn’t be very suitable for telehealth, even on video. (P28)

The topic of cost received mixed reactions. Some participants stated that since they paid for the skills and knowledge of the practitioner, the out-of-pocket costs should be the same for in-person or telehealth appointments. However, some participants noted that practitioners could potentially reduce out-of-pocket costs for consumers by providing telehealth services from their homes, as this could lower their operational expenses. It was highlighted that cheaper out-of-pocket fees should not be used to pressure people experiencing financial stress into choosing telehealth over in-person appointments. Additionally, it was noted that billing for telehealth appointments should be fair, and that brief phone calls or appointments disrupted by internet issues should not be charged as full sessions.

Disregarding out-of-pocket costs, telehealth was often perceived as cost-saving for the individual due to reduced travel expenses.

Scheduling telehealth appointments had both benefits and drawbacks. On the positive side, participants appreciated being able to access appointments at short notice, as there was no need to travel to the clinic. Another advantage was the flexibility to fit telehealth into one’s schedule, with some participants even able to multitask while waiting for appointments to start. Telehealth was also seen to take up less time overall, making it easier to incorporate into the week, such as when needing to take time off work. Additionally, telehealth can reduce wait times and provide more timely support.

However, there were challenges when allied health professionals did not call at the agreed time or provided only a time window rather than a specific appointment time. This led to missed calls or appointments at inconvenient times. Participants felt that some allied health professionals expected them to always be available, without considering other commitments like work. Having a specific appointment time became especially important when third parties, such as translators or interpreters, were involved.

But then, at the same time, you don’t know as well when it comes to the phone appointment [whether] they [will] actually see you [at] the time of the appointment. I [was] like, ‘Oh, I already told my workplace I [would be] working at this time, but then I’m [waiting] for like another half an hour. (P45)

The absence of a waiting room caused discomfort and issues for participants. For example, participants reported a lack of information in the event of delays, leaving them unaware of when their practitioner would arrive. Additionally, people who used interpreters were unable to communicate with them beforehand, preventing proper introductions. As a result, introductions had to occur during the appointment itself, taking away valuable treatment time.

Technology

As expected, ‘technology’ was a central aspect of telehealth and became an important theme in the focus group discussions.

Internet availability and speed were recognised as crucial for effective healthcare provided via telehealth. Participants in regional or remote areas often struggled with unstable connections, which disrupted sessions and reduced treatment time. In some cases, participants had to upgrade to more expensive data plans, adding financial burden. Issues with internet connectivity were seen as a common and significant drawback of telehealth. Additionally, technological issues like malfunctioning microphones were cited as further detractors as they reduced the time available for treatment. While some of the participants reported no personal issues with accessing telehealth technology, both the lack of technology and challenges in using it were mentioned as potential barriers.

But then there’s still a lot of people who are disadvantaged because they’re not able to have the technology required to be able to attend these appointments. (P24)

Setting up the environment in which the appointment would take place was another barrier. For instance, when observing specific actions, such as walking upstairs, achieving the optimal camera angle for allied health professionals can be challenging, potentially impacting the quality of care. Screen sharing, however, was seen as a helpful tool, allowing allied health professionals to walk people through results and enhancing communication between consumer and provider.

Accessibility was a recurring topic, particularly for the deaf and hard-of-hearing community. Participants highlighted the need for telehealth platforms to be designed with accessibility in mind, such as incorporating interpreter-friendly features and improving caption quality through artificial intelligence. While captioning was noted as an essential feature, the quality and usability varied across platforms.

That basic things when people choose to use telehealth like not being able to use close captioning and stuff makes it really difficult and sometimes seems to be things that aren’t thought about, which I find interesting in Allied Health. (P44)

Usability was also a concern, with several participants mentioning that apps and websites could be challenging to navigate and should be designed with the user experience in mind. The inconsistency in platforms used by different providers led to frustration, with participants expressing a preference for a standardised platform. Although individual preferences varied, no single platform was widely preferred or disliked.

Participants noted a need for telehealth support services. In the absence of such support, allied health professionals often had to assist people in setting up or logging into appointments, which decreased the time for actual treatment.

There will be some people who don’t know how to navigate it. For whatever reason, and they deserve accessibility just as much as anyone else. […] who are the people that will be supporting them? (P36)

Modality preference varied among participants. Video calls were generally favoured over phone calls due to the added benefit of visual communication. However, phone calls were seen as more convenient for quick check-ins or when internet access was unreliable. Personal preferences differed, with one participant preferring phone calls for therapy sessions to avoid being seen crying, while another found phone calls anxiety-inducing and preferred video calls instead. Concerns around confidentiality and data security were raised by some participants, and many requested more information on these topics for future telehealth appointments.

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to explore consumer perspectives on telehealth for allied health services and to assess barriers and enablers as well as advantages and disadvantages. Of particular importance, four of the eight focus groups were conducted with seldom-represented community groups: Kindred Clubhouse (a hyperlocal peer support service focused on improving and maintaining members’ mental health), Deaf Victoria (focused on deaf and hard-of-hearing Victorians), YDAS (focused on young disabled people) and Engage Pasefika (focused on members of the Pacific Islander community in Victoria). Incorporating these often-underrepresented groups in research provides a broader and more nuanced understanding of telehealth experiences through the inclusion of often unheard voices. Five main themes emerged from the focus group discussions—the consumer, the allied health practitioner, the relationship between consumer and practitioner, the appointment and the technology. While the five themes are well-established in existing literature as key barriers and facilitators for telehealth, additional findings specific to these community groups offer new and valuable insights that can inform more equitable telehealth practices.

The individual consumer, with their unique context, characteristics and preferences, plays a significant role in the suitability and success of telehealth. Patient characteristics, such as technological literacy, age and socioeconomic background have been shown to influence telehealth uptake in primary care settings.6 Research indicates that while some people are open to telehealth, a lack of comfort with technology can hinder its adoption.6 28 29 In addition to the person’s characteristics, their personal context plays a major role. Especially having an appropriate space for attending telehealth sessions is crucial.32 33 Focus group participants highlighted the privacy concerns of having to attend telehealth sessions while either being in public spaces or being at home with other people. Similarly, concerns were raised about the privacy of the space occupied by allied health professionals during appointments, such as whether others were present in the background or listening in. Nonetheless, their own home was seen as bringing comfort and thus can be even an advantage for telehealth sessions. Especially neurodivergent individuals benefited from being in their own home as they felt freer to engage in stimming behaviours.

Other benefits such as reduced travel can influence a person’s preferences for telehealth.34 Our results show that a reduction in travel time and cost was seen as a key advantage of telehealth. For individuals with chronic illnesses or mobility issues, avoiding the physical strain of travelling to appointments can make healthcare more accessible and manageable. Additionally, participants stated that, for neurodivergent individuals or those with mental health conditions, telehealth can be less overwhelming than in-person visits, as it removes the stress of navigating unfamiliar environments, social interactions or sensory overload.

Previous literature also found allied health professionals’ competencies to be a key factor for successful telehealth appointments.35 Important competencies included: communication, effectively using technology, person-centred care, practice-based assessment and intervention knowledge/behaviour/skills, privacy, security, patient safety, professionalism and setting up the technical environment. Each of these competencies was also noted by participants in this study. It is noted that without these additional competencies—beyond those required for face-to-face consultations—allied health professionals may struggle to deliver effective and safe services due to feelings of uncertainty and apprehension.17

The theme of the patient–practitioner relationship has also been previously documented in the literature. For example, a prior existing relationship between a person and their practitioner has been reported to facilitate the successful delivery of telehealth in primary care settings.34 In our study, the Engage Pasefika focus group participants stressed the importance of relationships and community, which they felt were damaged by telehealth.

Another notable theme was the appointment itself, which included improved access to allied health services. During the COVID-19 pandemic in Australia, healthcare usage data indicated not only a rise in telehealth usage but also an increase in allied health consultations overall, suggesting an increased need for and improved access to these services.36 However, telehealth suitability varies across allied health services. Studies from primary care settings show that telehealth is generally more appropriate for appointments not requiring physical examination.34 Physiotherapy, for instance, is often regarded as challenging to deliver via telehealth, a view shared by many physiotherapists.37 38

Cost considerations also emerged as a significant factor. Some participants suggested that cost savings achieved by allied health professionals through telehealth should result in lower out-of-pocket costs for consumers. Nonetheless, the evidence does not substantiate this assumption of reduced professional costs.3941 The primary financial benefit to individuals appears to be reduced travel expenses. Thus, even if consultation fees remain unchanged, overall costs to the consumer may still be lower due to savings on travel. Evidence does suggest that people are often cost driven, which makes it an important consideration when designing or optimising telehealth services.42

In our study, technology was another critical factor, with well-documented barriers related to internet access, equipment availability and connection stability.6 32 34 Besides the availability and reliability of technology, its accessibility for people with disabilities is essential.4345 Our findings showed that, for example, the deaf and hard-of-hearing community faces significant barriers to telehealth access due to poor-quality closed captioning and the inability of telehealth platforms to effectively accommodate interpreters. The inaccessibility and lack of user-friendliness of technology thus reduces the likelihood of people using telehealth services. The WHO and International Telecommunication Union have published guidelines to improve telehealth accessibility for people with disabilities, covering visual and hearing impairments, speech difficulties, physical disability and developmental or intellectual disability.46 Such guidelines are crucial for promoting equity in healthcare. Unfortunately, accessibility standards are often overlooked when designing and implementing eHealth interventions.47

Recommendations for practice, policy and research

For participants, having the option to choose between in-person and telehealth appointments was crucial. Many noted a significant reduction in telehealth availability since the COVID-19 pandemic. Health services and policymakers should therefore mandate the ongoing provision of telehealth where clinically appropriate and ensure that it is not deprioritised as in-person services resume. Consumers must be provided with a genuine and supported choice between telehealth and in-person appointments. Clinical appropriateness should guide eligibility, but where both modes are suitable, services must avoid steering consumers towards a default option, whether telehealth or in-person. Given the wide range of personal, cultural and logistical factors influencing decisions about telehealth, many of which are not immediately visible to clinicians or administrative staff, services must routinely ask consumers about their preferences, needs and comfort level with telehealth.

Another key factor was the need for sufficient information about telehealth. Information can reduce the hesitance to use telehealth for people with lower digital literacy. For example, participants often reported a lack of guidance on how to use telehealth platforms, as well as information on confidentiality and data security. This lack of information can hinder the adoption of telehealth, highlighting the need for enhanced informational support to improve accessibility and uptake. Policymakers and services should develop and implement standardised, easy-to-understand informational resources that cover platform use, troubleshooting, confidentiality, privacy, data security and what to expect during a telehealth session.

Besides informational support, participants also asked for more quality standards and assessments for telehealth. The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated the development of telehealth at an unprecedented rate. National and state-level policymakers should prioritise the development of formal telehealth standards that address clinical appropriateness, technology requirements, administrative processes, clinician training, documentation, risk management, accessibility features and cost transparency. Evidence from the UK indicates that, although guidelines exist for allied health professionals, there is limited information regarding the assessment of telehealth’s use and effectiveness, as well as its barriers and limitations, logistical management, the roles of families and caregivers, and associated costs.48 49 Additionally, there is a lack of clarity around allied health professionals’ registration requirements, costs and coverage, and legal considerations. Besides guidelines by allied health professions bodies, research-based frameworks have emerged. A framework by Thomas et al highlights multiple issues that align with the findings of our study.50 Some of these include developing checklists to assess an individual’s suitability for telehealth, training clinicians, administrators and consumers, standardising conference platforms, and considering not just the appointment but the whole process including administrative procedures.

Additionally, based on our findings, telehealth appointments must be flexible and tailored to meet individual needs. This includes addressing accessibility requirements, such as providing accurate closed captions for people who are deaf or hard of hearing. Another major concern is the accommodation of interpreters and translators. This should be integrated throughout the appointment process, including options during booking, adherence to agreed times, provision of a virtual waiting room, or allowing the consumer and interpreter to join early to become familiar with each other.

Our study was conducted in a Western, high-income country with comparatively strong digital infrastructure and established allied health systems. As such, the transferability of these findings to low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) remains unclear. Future research should therefore examine how insights from Western high-income contexts can be adapted to settings where digital access, technological infrastructure and workforce capacity may be more limited. This includes understanding the barriers and enablers of telehealth in LMICs, identifying culturally and contextually appropriate models of care, and evaluating strategies to reduce digital inequities. Research that directly compares implementation approaches across diverse global contexts would significantly strengthen the evidence base needed to inform international telehealth policy and investment.

Strengths and limitations

Allied health services encompass a broad spectrum of professions, each varying in suitability for telehealth. This study did not cover every allied health profession, as the focus groups were based on participants’ specific experiences. Nevertheless, the insights gathered allow for broader telehealth recommendations that can be generalised to other allied health appointments. For instance, the convenience of telehealth for follow-up sessions, which often involve simply answering questions, could be applicable across many allied health fields.

This study was conducted in Australia, an advanced country with high levels of education, major metropolitan centres, and large areas of relatively uninhabited land, and a COVID-19 experience involving significant public health constraints, but relatively lower incidence than in many other countries. The generalisability of the findings will likely be greatest in countries with similar characteristics; however, we also believe that the identified themes are broad enough to apply across a wide range of jurisdictions.

A second limitation is the diversity of the participants. While we aimed to include a highly diverse study population, the recruitment aim was to include seldom-represented population groups. By recruiting from often underrepresented groups, such as the deaf and hard-of-hearing and Pacific Islander communities, we sought to amplify voices that might otherwise go unheard and uncover gaps in health equity. While this study does not capture the full range of needs and preferences across all Australians, it enriches existing literature and addresses some previously overlooked perspectives. The study population only included 19% of participants from regional and rural areas, which may underrepresent the challenges faced by Australians in regional and rural areas when accessing allied health services through telehealth. Lastly, the varying modes of delivery for the focus groups (in-person, hybrid and online) may have influenced group dynamics and, in turn, the outcomes. However, because we aligned the format with participants’ preferences wherever possible, the chosen settings are likely to have enhanced participants’ comfort rather than diminished it.

Conclusions

The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated the adoption of telehealth, significantly reshaping healthcare delivery, including allied health services. While initially driven by necessity, telehealth has proven to be a valuable option that continues to have a place in the postpandemic era. Its flexibility, accessibility and potential to meet diverse patient needs make it a viable complement to in-person care. However, as telehealth becomes a permanent feature of allied health, it is essential to base guidelines on a thorough understanding of consumer preferences and needs. Insights from this research offer a foundation for developing standards that ensure quality and consistency in telehealth services, while respecting individual autonomy and enhancing the overall healthcare experience. By building on these insights, policymakers and practitioners can create a telehealth framework to guide practice that is both person-centred and sustainable.
