The hydrogen-to-carbon mole perspective reveals cleanness and inequality of global and national fossil fuel transition
Authors: Guobao Song, Xinyue Zhao, Yu Zhang, Lin Lv, Dabo Guan, Gang Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTransition away from fossil fuels concerns both carbon and hydrogen as the main energy carriers in different hydrocarbons, but the current energy transition literature primarily emphasizes carbon, with limited attention to hydrogen embedded in fossil fuels. Here we introduce the index of Hydrogen-to-Carbon Mole Ratio (HCR) to measure the clean transition and inequality of global and national fossil fuel consumption, production, export, and import over nearly four decades (1980-2016). We show that the HCR reveals the cleanness of fossil fuel consumption and production falling behind those of import and export. Energy transition demonstrates inequality, as high and upper-middle income economies continuously consume cleaner fossil fuels with higher HCR than the lower-middle and low income economies. Despite higher per capita mole consumption, high-income economies require less hydrogen and carbon intensity than low income economies to sustain economic growth. Metrics based on the molecular HCR can be extended to long-term energy transition beyond fossil fuel, and help monitor the transition, inequality, and decarbonization strategies in human energy systems.Subject terms: Environmental social sciences, Energy science and technology

Introduction

Climate change mitigation necessitates energy transition, particularly replacing fossil fuels by renewable energy1,2. “Transitioning away from fossil fuels”3, as phrased in roadmap of the 28th Conference of the Parties (COP28) to the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC), takes time and requires careful policy design. Despite the continuously rising role of renewable energy4, fossil fuels (i.e., coal, petroleum, and natural gas) still contributes ~80%5,6 of the world’s energy supply with projections slightly declining to 75% by 20305. Consequently, carbon emissions from fossil fuel combustion would double to 43.2 Gt by 20307 accordingly, compared with the level in the 1990s4.

Fossil fuel is a variety of different hydrocarbons based on carbon and hydrogen as the main energy carriers, while carbon is meanwhile the main cause behind climate change. Therefore, from the mole perspective, clean energy transition reflects the dynamics (often increase) of hydrogen-to-carbon mole ratio (HCR; see Fig. 1), by the avenues of replacing traditional biomass fuels with coal, then coal with petroleum, petroleum with natural gas, and natural gas with pure hydrogen, to improve its cleanness (in terms of combustion induced carbon emissions) and eventually achieve net-zero emissions (Fig. 1).

According to Fig. 1, historically, traditional biomass (e.g., wood with HCR ~ 1:10) supplies 50–75% of global energy demand until 1902. Afterwards, coal (e.g., anthracite, bituminous, or lignite with HCR ~ 1:2) surpasses biomass and contribute 32–56% to the world’s energy sources due to the widespread uses of Watt’s steam engines. The invention of the internal combustion engine then increases the world’s demand for petroleum (e.g., a hydrocarbon with HCR ~ 2:1). Approximately in 1964, petroleum exceeds coal and meets 36% of global energy demand. Natural gas, mainly composes of methane (with HCR ~ 4:1), has become popular, especially driven by the U.S. breakthroughs in horizontal drilling and hydraulic fracturing technologies8. Natural gas is therefore projected to surpass petroleum and dominate global energy supply by 20424. Facing climate change, hydrogen as energy with zero-carbon (with HCR 2:0) has started its unprecedented momentum, with world’s demand growing over three times since 1975 and reaching ~75 million tons in 20189 and 94 million tons in 202110.

However, the global and regional energy transitions have hitherto been mostly measured by carbon mass-based metrics, such as total carbon emissions11, carbon intensity of energy and fuels12,13 or of economic output14,15, and consumption-based carbon footprint16. These metrics only emphasize carbon due to its essential role for global warming, but give a limited consideration to the parallel role and percentage of hydrogen embedded in fossil fuels. In contrast, discussion on the role of hydrogen in the energy and climate literature usually considers less of carbon as well17,18.

Here, we coin the HCR metrics and introduce its quantification methodology to measure the transition and cleanness of global and national fossil energy activities (i.e., consumption, production, export, and import) from 1980 to 2016 (see details in Methods). Further, we derive two trade-related metrics, beneficence and externality, to examine the effects of fossil fuel trade on the clean energy transition of its own and its trading patterns. Then, quantile regression, along with ordinary least squares (OLS) regression, are performed to examine the uneven or heterogenous marginal effect of wealth growth on hydrogen and carbon consumption. Our results, revealed by the HCR metrics, equally consider the role of carbon and hydrogen in energy transition, comparing to narrow focus on carbon only in the existing indicators, and thus offer insights on measuring the cleanness of human fossil energy activities and essential implications for energy and climate policy-making.

Results

General HCR features over years

From 1980 to 2016, global hydrogen and carbon consumption from fossil fuel parallelly increase by 136% and 135%, respectively (Fig. 2a). The mean HCR of fossil fuel consumption equals to that of production (1.50), but is lower than the HCR of import (1.72) or export (1.71). The HCR of production and consumption rose slightly by 3% and 0.8%, respectively, reflecting that the global fossil energy system became cleaner over years.

Based on the abrupt changes in the rising or falling trend of HCR of global fossil fuel consumption, we divide the study period into four stages by joint-point regressions (I-IV; see Fig. 2b, and details in Methods). The HCR of production and consumption increases by 10% and 8%, respectively, in stage II (1985–1999) and decreases by 7% each in stage III (1999–2013). This decrease is in line with the 3% growth rate of global carbon emission in the same period19. Notably, HCR of fossil export fluctuates more violently than that of import, which may attribute to regional military conflicts or economic shocks. For example, the HCR of global fossil exports drops by 13% in 2000–2001 following the 9/11 terrorist attacks (Fig. 2c). This attack reduced the petroleum and natural gas exports from the Middle East by 12% and 55%, respectively, relative to 200020. Another example illustrates an abrupt 24% decline in the HCR of fossil fuel exports from 2008 to 2010. This drop may be attributed to the 2008 financial crisis, which led to a decrease in crude oil and natural gas prices by 71%21 and 64%22, respectively, due to reduced demand.

In the stage IV (2013–2016), the mean per capita annual hydrogen and carbon consumption is 215 and 147 thousand moles, respectively; this consumption equals to 1979 kg (i.e., three times of annual per person food consumption23) or 40% higher than that in stage I. Coal and natural gas accounted for 67% and 24% of the growth in carbon consumption, respectively, and 27% and 62% of the growth in hydrogen consumption, respectively, from stage I to IV (Fig. 2d). The hydrogen and carbon consumption are considerably uneven across economies. The top five economies (China, the U.S., India, Russia, and Japan) consume 53% and 63% of the global hydrogen and carbon, respectively, in stage IV, while the bottom 100 economies consume only 1% and 1.3% collectively in the same period (Fig. 2e). The hydrogen shares of the U.S. fossil-fuel consumption decrease from 34% in stage I to 18% in stage IV, and carbon from 28% and 15%; in contrast, China’s hydrogen percentage rose from 7% to 19% and from 13% to 33% accordingly (Fig. 2e). These inequalities of consumption across top and bottom economies are reflected by the high Gini coefficients at the economy scale (~0.9) and individual scale (~0.7; Fig. 2f).

The HCR ranges from 0.5 to 4.0, where the minimum value (0.5) represents coal-related activities (consumption, production, import, or export) and the maximum value (4.0) corresponds to natural gas activities. Among the major fossil fuel producers and consumers, Iran, Russia, and Canada had the highest HCR values for both production (2.18–2.22) and consumption (2.20–2.47), exceeding those of the United States (1.59 and 1.70 for production and consumption, respectively). Russia also ranks as the cleanest fossil exporter, while Germany as the cleanest importer, as reflected by their respectively stable and high HCR values over years. Notably, fossil consumption in Japan is cleaner (HCR: 1.59–1.71) than its Asian neighbor Korea (HCR: 1.07–1.58) and comparable to the United States and Canada. Japan’s flat trend in HCR of consumption indicates its consistent energy ties with international trade partners, despite its high dependence on energy imports. China with rich coal resources had the lowest HCR of consumption. India, as another coal-rich giant, consumes cleaner fossil fuels (HCR: 0.88–1.00) than China, owing to its lower share of coal in total energy demand (55% in India versus 70% in China in 1990–201525) although the share of coal consumption in China’s total energy demand declines considerably to 55.3% in 2023. Indonesia, despite only sharing 1.4% of global fossil fuel consumption in 202026, plays a crucial role in the world’s energy transition. This is because that Indonesia surpasses Australia as the largest coal exporter since 2011, and HCR of fossil production has remarkably declined since 1999.

HCR inequality across income-economy groups

Figure 4 shows the trends of the cleanness of fossil fuel activities across income-grouped economies, which are defined referring to the income standard of the World Bank (see Income Groups of Economies in Methods section). For consumption, the high (H) and upper-middle (UM) income economies achieve cleaner transition of their energy systems than the low (L) and lower-middle (LM) income groups, especially in stages III and IV. Although the H and UM groups show the rising trend in the cleanness of fossil fuel consumption (Fig. 4a), the global HCR of consumption still declines from 1999 to 2013 (Fig. 2b). This inconsistency reveals the crucial role of the LM income group in the global decarbonization process. The cleanness of fossil fuel production follows a similar pattern to that of consumption across all the four income groups (Fig. 4b).

From 1992 to 2016, the HCR of fossil fuel imports for the H group steadily increases by 6.7%, reflecting a trend towards importing cleaner fossil fuels. However, the UM, LM, and L groups fail to exhibit the same rising trend. Instead, their HCR of fossil import decline by 3.5–33% by 2016. The standard deviation of fossil-import HCR of the H group is 0.05, implying a stability in the fossil imports. The corresponding standard deviation for the L and LM income groups, by contrast, is three and four times that of the H group, indicating instable energy linkage with their trading patterns. The L income group exhibit a 443% growth in HCR of fossil fuel export from stage II to IV (Fig. 4d), while their HCR of domestic consumption dropped by 11% from 1992 to 2016 (Fig. 4d). This observation show that fossil trade may exaggerate the inequality in raising the cleanness of fossil consumptions, thus denying the decarbonization opportunities of the LM and L income economies.

Beneficence and externality of fossil trade

We have further derived two indices to measure the accumulative effect of fossil trade on national decarbonization process over the period 1992–2016 (Fig. 5; consistent trade data for all economies start from 1992): beneficence (B) as the ratio of HCR of fossil import to that of consumption, and externality (E) as the ratio of HCR of export to that of import (see details in Methods). The world economies are grouped into four categories (i.e., E+B+, E+B-, E-B-, and E-B+) based on positive (+) or negative (-) values for E and B, with accumulative shares of various fossil fuel activities presented in Supplementary Table 1. The E-B+ category comprises 45 economies that benefit from importing 41% of the world’s fossil fuels with HCR of import higher than their domestic consumption. However, these economies compromise decarbonization opportunities of others by exporting 24% of fossil fuels with relative low HCR. Contrastively, the 73 economies of E+B- category assist their trade partners in promoting a low-carbon growth by exporting 70% of global fossil energy cleaner than their imports while consuming 34% of the world’s low-HCR fossil fuels. The E+B+ and E-B- category consist of 13 and 25 economies, respectively, which co-benefit (co-damage) the low-carbon transitions of themselves and their trade partners, reflected by the beneficence and externality indices higher (lower) than the reference value of 1.0 (Fig. 5a, b).

Besides the magnitude of beneficence reflected by HCR-HCR ratios, import volume also determines the benefit of decarbonization. For example, Montenegro has high beneficence values but still faces a low decarbonization process because cleaner imports only meet 6% of its energy demands. By contrast, Germany and India, despite with relative low beneficence value, still benefit from imports that meet 64% and 30% of their fossil energy demands, respectively. Some importers, such as South Korea, Chile, and the Netherlands, may increase their carbon intensity because 53–85% of their fossil fuel demand depends on imports with low beneficence values. For externality, Russia and Saudi Arabia, which account for 15% and 12% of the global fossil fuel export, respectively, have positive externality and facilitate low-carbon transition of their partners. Contrastively, Indonesia, Australia, Mexico, Kenya, Colombia, the U.S., China, and South Africa with low externality, with a share of 22% of global fossil fuel export accumulatively during 1992–2016, may hinder the decarbonization process of their trading partners. Figure 5c, d illustrates the temporally varied beneficence and externality of global top 10 carbon emitters.

Marginal effect of wealth growth on HCR

Figure 6 shows the differentiated marginal effect of economic growth on hydrogen and carbon consumptions. Wealth had a stronger positive effect on per capita hydrogen consumption (13.9–18.5 moles $-2010−1) than on carbon consumption (6.7–9.2 moles $-2010−1; “$-2010” means U.S. dollars in 2010 prices by inflation-adjusted; Fig. 6a, b). This marginal effect varies across the quantiles of carbon and hydrogen consumptions. For the example in 1987, 1 $ growth in per capita gross domestic product (GDP) is associated with 6.7 (3.6) moles increase in per capita hydrogen (carbon) consumption at the 10th quantile, which is only a fifth to a fourth of the effect at the 80th and 90th quantiles (Fig. 6c). These unevenly distributed marginal effects are consistently observed over years 1980–2016 (Supplementary Fig. 1a). The effect of wealth growth on hydrogen and carbon consumptions decline parallelly at the 10th–70th quantiles; contrastively, the 90th quantile presented a divergency, showing that the H group are more likely to decarbonize their fossil-energy system than the L group (Supplementary Fig. 1b). This finding explains the inequal HCR index of fossil fuel consumptions across varied income- economies groups (Fig. 4).

Wealth also had a heterogeneous effect on the cleanness of fossil fuel consumption over years (Fig. 6d). The marginal effect of wealth on the HCR, for instance, is positive but statistically insignificant at the 50th percentile for most years of 1980–2016. This effect—1 $ increase in per capita GDP raises the mean HCR by 2.9 × 10−5—increase with years, suggesting a more rapid clean transition of fossil fuel consumption as economy develops. From the 10th to 70th quantiles, the rising trends of wealth effect on HCR of fossil consumptions are observed similarly as at the 50th, contrary to the declining effect at the 80th and 90th quantiles (Supplementary Fig. 2). This contrast reflects a rising challenge for economies to continue improving their HCR of fossil fuel consumption through decarbonization. Therefore, alternative renewable and cleaner energy like wind and solar power and pure hydrogen needs to be further invested to continue decarbonization and eventually achieve net-zero emissions, as shown in Fig. 1.

Discussion

Addressing the current global climate urgency builds essentially on a fundamental transformation of human energy system13. This requires not only visionary policy and technological innovation for renewable energy beyond fossil fuels in the long run, but also careful and timely design of the fossil fuel energy transition in the near term. While both carbon and hydrogen are important energy carriers, most previous discussion on energy transition are based solely on carbon mass-based metrics like global or local carbon emissions27,28. The HCR metrics we have coined and quantified in this analysis could equally consider carbon and hydrogen in energy transition, and thus offer insights into fossil fuel replacement, help quantify the cleanness of long-term energy transition, and facilitate the discussion and tracking of global and national energy inequality and decarbonization process from an additional dimension.

Our results on the varying HCR patterns across different income-economy groups have policy implications. Specifically, the HCR reveals the cleanness of fossil fuel consumption is on the rise in the H and UM income economies, whereas it is on the decline in the LM and L groups. This disparity can be explained by the unevenly distributed marginal influence of economic growth on hydrogen and carbon consumption (Supplementary Fig. 3), and may be also contributed to the unbalanced externality and beneficence of fossil trade. When per capita GDP approaches the turning point of ~5000 $-2010 (i.e., 4790 and 5250 $-2010; Supplementary Fig. 3), the global average hydrogen and carbon intensity of fossil fuel consumption peaks at 23 and 13 moles per dollar with buying power for the year 2010, respectively. Beyond this threshold, both intensities decline, indicating a shift in fossil fuel composition to support continued economic growth. The H and UM income groups, such as the United States, Canada, Japan, and Germany, have low hydrogen and carbon intensities, implying that their wealth growth is dependent less on burning fossil fuels than other top-10 economies (such as Russia and India). Whilst having the lowest HCR of fossil fuel consumption, China shows the longest steadily decreasing trajectory, showing its fast wealth growth fueled by rapidly declining fossil consumptions (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 3). This decrease is a consequence of China’s rising HCR trend in fossil fuel consumption by 11% after 1989 (Fig. 3) owing to continually rising natural gas consumption29 and is consistent with China’s carbon intensity decrease from 2005 to 202015.

The disparity in HCR values between the H/UM and the LM/L income groups may exacerbate the obstacles that the LM/L income economies encounter during a policy-driven energy transition as well. The sustained use of fossil fuels is significantly influenced by their affordability and availability. Consequently, their dependence on fossil fuels with lower HCR may be a result of cost and accessibility constraints, which should be considered in corresponding global energy and climate governance. Additionally, the beneficence and the externality indices must be considered alongside domestic resource endowments and industrial development that largely shape an economy’s energy transition, regardless of small-scale imports of cleaner fossil fuels with higher HCR than domestic consumption.

As we demonstrated using examples of 9/11 attacks and 2008 financial crisis in this analysis, the HCR metrics could help capture the impact of fossil fuel trade pattern changes caused by both conventional and non-conventional risk factors on the global and national clean energy transition and decarbonization. This becomes particularly relevant in the background of increasing geopolitical tensions and regional conflicts, and can be analyzed using the HCR metrics in future studies. For example, the ongoing Russia-Ukraine war has changed global energy trade patterns so that Europe has shifted its natural gas sources from Russia to the United States. Germany, the largest beneficiary of cleanest fossil import (Figs. 3 and 5c), has restarted its coal facilities and reevaluated its policy to exit nuclear power. India may have benefited from importing a greater volume of natural gas from Russia at discounted price due to the conflict. The series of HCR metrics for fossil fuels proposed in this analysis could be used to understand the impact of such regional conflicts (e.g., in Ukraine and in the Middle East) on the cleanness of regional and global energy system.

It is important to point out that, although global energy consumption is shifting toward lower carbon content (Fig. 1b), the absolute consumption of fossil fuels continues to rise (Fig. 1a). The HCR index, which quantifies fossil fuel cleanness at the molecular level, should be used alongside established metrics such as total carbon emissions¹² and consumption-based carbon footprints¹⁷. Considering energy transition in the future, the HCR metrics can actually be extended to understanding and informing energy transition beyond the four fossil-fuel activities and incorporating the decarbonization effects of energy technology innovations in the long run. For example, carbon negative technologies, such as by Carbon Capture, Utilization, and Storage (CCUS), can be reflected by minus the carbon mole of HCR denominator. The contribution of innovative hydrogen production technologies, such as by concentrated solar irradiation, can also be quantified by increasing the hydrogen mole value of the HCR numerator. Therefore, these HCR metrics has the potential to be used to quantify and track long-term clean transition of human energy systems both retrospectively (e.g., including biomass) and prospectively (e.g., including hydrogen and carbon negative technologies). This may provide insights to inform relevant energy and climate policy and targets, such as Sustainable Development Goal 7 on Affordable and Cleaner Energy.

Methods

Hydrogen-to-carbon mole ratio

We define the concept of energy cleanness—quantified by the Hydrogen-To-Carbon Mole Ratio (HCR)—to evaluate the cleaning degree of certain fossil fuel activities for a specific unit (such as, an economy, province, continent, or the world) during the transition toward greener energy. The HCR, as the molar ratio of hydrogen to carbon, quantitatively reflects the change toward higher hydrogen content and lower carbon content in fossil fuels. HCR pertains to four fossil fuel activities, including production, consumption, import, and export, as calculated in Eq. (1). By including both carbon and hydrogen as the two most important energy carriers and carbon as the key element in greenhouse emissions in the metrics, HCR can quantitatively assess the transition and cleanness of energy systems at global, national, and regional levels over time. This study includes coal, petroleum, and natural gas as fossil fuels.

where mcoal,i,j,t, mpetroleum,i,j,t and mgas,i,j,t represent the tons of coal, petroleum and natural gas, respectively, reported by economy j in year t for activity i. Mc and Mh represent the molar masses of carbon (12) and hydrogen (1), respectively. The variables wcoal_C (wcoal_H), wpetroleum_C (wpetroleum_H), and wgas_C (wpetroleum_H) represent the carbon (hydrogen) mass content of coal, petroleum, and natural gas, respectively.

Data source and handling

Considering the availability and representativeness (e.g., to exclude the impact of recent pandemic and geopolitical issues) of reported data points (detailed in Supplementary Fig. 4), we select years 1980–2016 as the study period for production and consumption analysis and 1992–2016 for import and export analysis. The production and consumption data for fossil fuels (coal, petroleum, and natural gas) are obtained from the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA; www.eia.gov/international/data/world)30. The data for production and consumption include 146 and 187 economies, respectively, and represent more than 99% of the global total. Coal production and consumption, measured in million short tons, include five types (anthracite, metallurgical, bituminous, subbituminous, and lignite). Petroleum production, measured in thousands of barrels per day, includes crude oil, liquids from natural gas plants, and other liquids (such as ethanol, liquids derived from coal, and oil shale). All domestic uses and international bunkering of refinery products and fuel for shipment, as well as direct combustion of crude oil and refinery byproducts, are included in the consumption for petroleum. To assure the comparability of data across economies, the EIA only publishes the annual production of dry natural gas, measured in billion cubic feet, excluding all non-hydrocarbon gases for all economies (See EIA30 for more details).

The trade data of fossil fuels across economies are obtained and compiled from the UN Comtrade database (http://comtrade.un.org/)31. This study encompasses a total of 168 exporting economies and 185 importing economies. For coal, the trade data includes items of the Harmonized System (HS) codes 2701 and 2702, which include briquettes, ovoids, and solid fuels from coal. For petroleum, the HS code 2709 is used, which includes petroleum and oil derived from bituminous minerals. For natural gas, both liquefied natural gas (HS code 271111) and gaseous natural gas (HS code 271121) are included. The global re-exports and re-imports of fossil fuel energy are not considered in this analysis, due to their negligible shares (0.006–0.011‰ for re-import and 0.031–0.115‰ for re-export in 1992–2016) in the global total trade volume and lack of economy specific data. Referring to the standard conversion factors such as those in the World Energy Statistics Yearbook of British Petroleum32, we standardize the data unit of all production, consumption, import, and export as metric tons, on which the mole and related HCR are calculated for in-depth analysis.

Income groups of economies

The number of economies for energy production (146), consumption (187), import (168), and export (185) differs between the U.S. EIA and UN Comtrade databases, and some of the economy names in the two databases do not match exactly. Therefore, regarding economies from different data sources, we follow the three-digit alphabetic economy identifiers of the International Organization for Standardization (ISO)33 for economy name alignment.

Referring to the criterion of gross national income, the World Bank annually categorizes all economies into four income groups: High (H), upper middle (UM), lower middle (LM), and low (L)34. However, the rank of one economy may change from one group to another over the years. Therefore, to track the cleanness of fossil-fuel activities among groups, we summarize the rank frequency for each economy over the years 1980–2016, and then categorize all economies into one of the H, UM, LM, and L income groups by referring to the highest occurrence frequency. Notably, in 1991, the Soviet Union is dissolved into 15 member economies. More than 90% of them belong to the LM income group, which dominates over 95% and 92% of the Soviet Union’s cumulative fossil consumption and production, respectively. To ensure the temporal continuity of data, we classify all the 15 member economies into the LM group, even though, according to the historical classification of the World Bank, the income of the former Soviet Union belongs to the UM group before its dissolution. Supplementary Table 2 gives the details on the economy list for each income group.

Beneficence and externality of trade

Based on the cleanness measured by HCR of Eq. (1), we originally develop two indices of beneficence and externality. The beneficence index of Eq. (4) assesses the impact of an economy’s fossil fuel imports on its HCR of domestic consumption and cleanness of fossil fuel transition. For instance, if the target economy that relies heavily on coal imports large quantities of hydrogen-rich natural gas for domestic consumption, the import benefits its own decarbonization process.

where HCRimp,i,t and HCRcon,i,t represent the HCR of fossil-fuel import and consumption for economy i in year t, as defined by Eq. (1). If Bi,t > 1 (or Bi,t + ), the decarbonization of the economy’s fossil energy consumption is more likely to be aided by hydrogen-rich fossil imports. In contrast, Bi,t < 1 (or Bi,t-) indicates that carbon-rich fossil imports may impede the decarbonization of importers, as more polluting fossil fuels are imported compared to domestic consumption with a higher HCR. Bi,t = 1 indicates that imports had no impact on decarbonization.

To complement the beneficence index, the externality index of Eq. (5) as evaluates the effects of fossil fuel exports and imports of a target economy on the HCR and thus cleanness of its trade partners or the rest of the world. For instance, if the target economy exports a large proportion of hydrogen-rich fossils (i.e., with a higher HCR) and imports carbon-rich fossils (i.e., with a lower HCR), then the target economy has a positive externality benefiting others.

where HCRexp,i,t and HCRimp,i,t represent the HCR of fossil-fuel export and import of the target economy i in year t, respectively. If Ei,t > 1 (or Ei,t + ), the target economy contributes to the decarbonization of the remaining economies by exporting fossils rich in hydrogen and importing fossils rich in carbon. In contrast, Ei,t < 1 (or Ei,t-) indicates that the target nation hinders the decarbonization of other economies. Ei,t = 1 indicates that there is no external effect on others.

The carbon and hydrogen contents of coal, petroleum, and natural gas vary considerably across economies, mining fields, or even coal types. Given China’s dominant role in the global coal production, we use the carbon and hydrogen contents of China’s coal market as a reference35. The carbon and hydrogen contents of petroleum and natural gas are compiled from industry report36, official document37, leading knowledge website38, and professional books39,40, as detailed in Supplementary Table 3.

Joint point regressions of the consumption HCR

Identifying changes in HCR increase and decrease trends over years, particularly for consumption of fossil fuels, assists policymakers in comprehending the complex process of energy decarbonization. Therefore, we conduct join-point regression that is suitable for regression with one independent variable (i.e., years). This method is supported by the grid-search algorithm41 to determine whether multi-segmented regression lines for HCR of fossil consumption versus years are substantially superior to the fit of a straight line or lines with fewer segments. The number of identified joint points is dependent on the statistical significance level p-value < 0.05. Join-point trend analysis software (versions 4.5.0.1)42 is authorized to execute. To ensure the robustness, we test 1–5 joint points that divide the study period into 2–6 stages. After a comprehensive evaluation of all scenarios, we select a four-stage division for our analysis over nearly four decades (980–2016; Supplementary Table 4). Years 1985, 1999, and 2013 are eventually identified as join-point years (as depicted in Fig. 2b), which are further used for HCR analysis in Fig. 4.

OLS and quantile regressions

Quantile regression and ordinary least squares (OLS) regression are two methods used to investigate the marginal effects of economic growth on hydrogen and carbon consumptions, as well as on the corresponding HCR. Quantile regression is the generalization of OLS because it provides estimate across the entire distribution except for the mean. For the median (50th percentile), quantile egression and OLS generate the similarly estimates. Namely, OLS examines the mean effect, while quantile regression enables more precise estimates of the conditional median or any quantiles (e.g., 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles) of the dependent targeted variables43,44. Therefore, quantile regression is more appropriate than the OLS model for detecting the unequal marginal effects of economic growth, particularly when high degree of heterogeneity in the hydrogen and carbon consumption per capita are observed across income-group economies (Fig. 2e). Besides, quantile regression is less sensitive to the occurrence of outliers and non-normal distributions of all dependent or in-dependent variables44,45. The quantile regression and estimate are shown in Eqs. (6) and (7).

Uncertainties and limitation

The absolute HCR values in this analysis bear unavoidable uncertainties due to data gaps and inconsistencies regarding the large number of datasets we have compiled (three types of fossil fuels, four types of energy activities, and socioeconomic data across ~160 world economies). In particular, the hydrogen and carbon content of fossil fuels vary across economies, mining fields, oil fields, and even among coal types48,49, but are only estimated based on limited amount of data. Referring to the technical guidelines of Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change50, we conduct Monte Carlo simulations to quantify data uncertainties. The hydrogen and carbon contents of fossil fuels are defined as triangular distributions by using mean as the parameter of likeliest, along with minimum and maximum. Then, Monte Carlo simulations are implemented at national and international scales by assigning 5000 trials for each simulation. We show that the average 5% and 95% percentiles of HCR fluctuations are 1.33–1.55 for fossil energy production, 1.29–1.52 for consumption from 1980 to 2016, 1.61–1.79 for import, and 1.56–1.76 for export from 1992 to 2016 (Fig. 4).

For methodology, besides quantile regression used in this study, alternative methods, such as unbalanced panel data regression, could also quantitatively assess the effect of wealth growth on hydrogen-carbon consumption and its HCR. Moreover, our analysis focuses solely on wealth growth, without incorporating other factors like north-south economic differences or coastal-landlocked status, which warrant further investigations by using panel data analysis.
