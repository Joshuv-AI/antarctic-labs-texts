Diagnostic Performance of Ultrasonography Compared to Cross-Sectional Imaging in Thyroid-Related Nodal Metastasis: A Retrospective Cohort Study from Saudi Arabia
Authors: Alzahraa Mustafa Alawami, Abdulaziz Al-Sharydah, Mishael M AlObaid, Zahra AlShabeeb, Jomana Alnasser, Maryam Aldeen, Fatimah Abbas A Aldawood, Mohammed Ahmed Al Mulhim, Mohammed S AlAftan, Hussain Al-Arfaj, Ahmad M Al-Essa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAccurate detection of metastatic cervical lymph nodes in thyroid cancer is needed for treatment planning. The present study evaluated the diagnostic performance of ultrasound (US), computed tomography (CT), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) for detecting metastatic cervical lymph nodes in a Saudi cohort.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study reviewed patients with thyroid nodules and cervical lymphadenopathy treated at King Fahd Hospital of the University, Saudi Arabia, between 2015 and 2024. This retrospective cohort study reviewed 150 retrieved records, of which 111 patients underwent detailed assessment; 10 were excluded for missing pathology or incomplete diagnostic data, leaving 101 patients in the final analysis. Conventional imaging features were assessed using predefined modality-specific criteria. US-guided fine needle aspiration cytology was used as the reference standard. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, accuracy, and 95% confidence intervals were calculated. Agreement and discordance were assessed using Cohen’s kappa and McNemar’s exact test, respectively.ResultsA total of 101 patients were included. US was performed in 59 cases, CT in 34, and MRI in 8. FNA cytology was positive in 48/101 cases. Overall radiological classification showed sensitivity of 89.6%, specificity of 88.7%, and accuracy of 89.1%, with substantial agreement with FNA cytology (κ=0.782). In modality-stratified descriptive analyses, US showed the highest observed agreement with FNA cytology, CT showed moderate agreement, and MRI findings were limited by the limited sample size.ConclusionWithin this cohort, ultrasound demonstrated high diagnostic performance, with the highest estimates of agreement and accuracy; CT showed moderate performance; and MRI results were limited by the small sample size. Given the distinct modality‑specific subgroups, these findings should be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis‑generating rather than as definitive evidence of modality superiority.

Background

Thyroid cancer is the most common endocrine malignancy with a significantly higher incidence in females. The global incidence of subclinical malignancies has surged over the past few decades, largely due to advances in imaging technologies that enable earlier detection. Thyroid cancer is the third most common cancer in Saudi Arabia, highlighting its significant impact on the healthcare system and the need for effective diagnostic strategies.1–3 Recent national data indicate a rising incidence of thyroid cancer over time, particularly among women, highlighting its growing impact on the Saudi healthcare system.1–3

The predominant subtype of thyroid cancer is papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC), which has a strong propensity for cervical lymph node (LN) metastasis. LN metastasis is a critical predictor of disease progression and recurrence, with studies indicating that it occurs in 30–80% of thyroid cancer cases. The accurate identification of metastatic LNs is essential for guiding surgical planning and treatment strategies to optimize patient outcomes. Various imaging modalities, including ultrasonography (US), computed tomography (CT), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), have been used to evaluate LN involvement and disease spread. These tools are crucial for an early and accurate diagnosis, which can reduce mortality and minimize tumor recurrence.4–6

Although the US is the most widely adopted imaging technique for both preoperative and postoperative evaluations owing to its accessibility and cost-effectiveness, it is limited by its operator-dependent nature and difficulty in detecting deep nodal metastases. In contrast, CT and MRI offer advantages such as superior anatomical visualization and the ability to detect lesions in the deeper regions of the neck. Although MRI provides better soft-tissue contrast and avoids ionizing radiation, CT remains the preferred choice for many surgeons because of its high-resolution anatomical detail, which is crucial for surgical planning.6–8 The Thyroid Imaging Reporting and Data System (TIRADS)‑guided evaluation provides a standardized framework for sonographic risk stratification, and when combined with targeted fine-needle aspiration (FNA) of suspicious nodules or lymph nodes, it refines selection for cytological sampling and can enhance overall diagnostic accuracy.4–6,9 Despite this increasing burden, there is a paucity of modality‑specific diagnostic performance data comparing ultrasound with cross-sectional imaging for cervical lymph node metastasis in the Saudi population, underscoring the need for region‑specific evaluation.6–8

This study aimed to evaluate the modality-specific diagnostic performance of ultrasound and cross-sectional imaging, including CT and MRI, for detecting metastatic cervical lymph nodes in patients with thyroid nodules using the TIRADS classification and suspected cervical lymphadenopathy, using US-guided FNA cytology as the reference standard. In this study, imaging assessment is conventional and guideline-based, relying on qualitative and semi‑quantitative features on ultrasound, CT, and MRI, and does not incorporate a radiomics workflow involving high-throughput quantitative feature extraction or radiomics-based predictive modeling.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

The study design complied with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) checklist of the EQUATOR Network’s (https://www.equator-network.org/reporting-guidelines/strobe/) standard reporting guidelines for observational studies. This retrospective cohort study analyzed the medical archives, images, and FNA cytology reports of all patients who were diagnosed with thyroid nodules and concomitant lymphadenopathy at the Radiology Department of King Fahd Hospital of the University (KFHU), Al-Khobar, Saudi Arabia, between August 01, 2015, and August 30, 2024.

The keywords “lymphadenopathy, nodal disease, and node metastasis” of the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision coding system, under codes C77, C73, C81–C86, and R59, were used to retrieve medical file reports from the hospital’s electronic medical filing system. From 2015 to 2024, 150 patient records with thyroid nodules and cervical lymphadenopathy were initially retrieved from the electronic database. After excluding 39 records with ineligible indications or duplicated entries, 111 cases underwent detailed chart and imaging review. Of these, 10 were excluded because of missing FNA cytology or incomplete diagnostic data, resulting in 101 patients included in the final analysis.

Study Population

This retrospective cohort included patients with solid or partly solid thyroid nodules demonstrating malignant behavior on imaging and concomitant cervical lymphadenopathy, for whom cervical lymph node metastasis was clinically suspected, and US‑guided FNA was requested. Benign‑appearing thyroid nodules, ie, cystic nodules or nodules with completely fluid content, without concomitant lymphadenopathy, and cases with incomplete imaging or FNA data, were excluded to ensure completeness of the modality‑specific analyses. Accordingly, the study population represents a targeted, higher‑risk diagnostic cohort rather than an unselected thyroid nodule screening population, reflecting the clinical context in which nodal assessment and FNA are typically considered.

Search Strategy

A rapid literature review was conducted across four databases (MEDLINE, EMBASE, PubMed Central [PMC], and SCHOLAR) from August 20, 2024, through October 1, 2024, using a search strategy adapted to address our study purpose. The search was restricted to the English-language publications.

We searched for additional potentially pertinent articles after conducting a literature search among five medical physicians.

Questionnaire Formulation

The data collection sheet was structured and formulated by experienced interventional radiology and pathology consultants (AS and AE) who thoroughly reviewed recent literature and comparable studies. Our data collection sheet was transparent and crafted using the latest and best available resources for each modality.5,6,9 The sheet was specifically designed to guide the standardized collection of relevant information from patients’ medical records and was used to retrospectively review data related to imaging findings.

US-Guided FNA Procedure

Before the procedure, the patients were thoroughly informed of the purpose and process, and informed consent was obtained. Patients were positioned supine with their necks slightly extended. The skin overlying the lesion was cleaned with a 10% povidone-iodine solution, which also served as a coupling agent, eliminating the need for an ultrasound gel. The area was draped to maintain sterility.

A high-resolution linear array transducer (7.5–15 MHz) covered with a sterile sheath was used for ultrasound guidance. Local anesthesia, typically 1–2 mL of 1% lidocaine hydrochloride, was administered to minimize discomfort. FNA was performed using 23–27-gauge needles (EchoCoat US needle; STS Biopolymers Inc., Henrietta, NY, USA).

Color Doppler imaging was used to identify the blood vessels near the LNs to avoid vascular injury. The freehand FNA technique and needle tips were carefully monitored. Data Collection

Clinical and demographic variables were abstracted from the electronic medical records by sixth‑year medical students using a structured data collection sheet, without access to the imaging classifications. Imaging features were obtained by direct re-evaluation of ultrasound, CT, and MRI studies in PACS rather than from narrative reports. Two radiology residents (each with 2–3 years of thyroid and head‑and‑neck imaging experience) independently reviewed all examinations using the predefined modality‑specific criteria while blinded to both FNA cytology results and each other’s readings; discrepancies were resolved in a consensus session with an interventional radiology consultant (5 years’ experience), whose adjudicated opinion defined the final imaging classification. A cytopathology consultant (5 years’ experience in cytology) independently assessed the FNA slides and reports, and overall inter‑rater agreement among assessors was high (kappa >0.80), supporting the reliability of the collected data.

Statistical Analysis

Agreement between radiological classification (positive and negative) and reference standard FNA cytology was assessed using Cohen’s kappa (κ) with 95% confidence intervals. McNemar’s exact test was used to evaluate the asymmetry in discordant classifications. A Bland–Altman analysis was not performed because it is intended for paired continuous measurements, whereas the study outcome was dichotomous. The collected data were analyzed using SPSS (version 23.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Diagnostic performance (sensitivity, specificity, predictive values, accuracy, and kappa) was calculated separately for ultrasound, CT, and MRI using patients who underwent each modality; these modality‑specific estimates are descriptive and do not reflect a fully paired head‑to‑head design. Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages and were compared using the chi-square test. If the chi-square assumptions were violated, a Monte Carlo test was used. The confidence interval was 95%, and statistical significance was set at p <0.05.

Ethical Considerations

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Imam Abdulrahman Bin Faisal University (approval number: IRB-UGS-2024-01-707) on October 10, 2024, and conformed to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (2024). Informed consent was obtained from all patients and/or their legal guardian(s).

CT Findings

Nodal stations were mostly at Level II (32.26%), followed by Level IV (12.90%). Calcification was observed in 9.38% of the LNs. These distributions reflect radiological assignment of cervical lymph node levels; because FNA cytology was targeted to the most suspicious nodes rather than all nodes at each level, the level‑by‑level findings should be considered descriptive and exploratory rather than pathologically validated for every radiological station. Absence of the hilum was observed in 3.13%. The size of the primary thyroid tumor was >2 cm in 23.33%, and the maximum short diameter of LN was >0.5 cm in 64.52%. Avid cortical enhancement (focal or diffuse) was present in 3.13% LNs. Heterogeneous enhancement was observed in 9.38% LNs (Figure 3). Discussion

This study provided valuable insights into the assessment of metastatic cervical LNs in patients with suspected thyroid cancer lesions. A significant achievement of this study was the refinement of the diagnostic criteria for metastatic nodal disease based on imaging characteristics validated in previous studies, thereby validating risk stratification, assessing the reliability of identifying metastatic LN, and comparing its performance with existing stratification models.9,11,12 Our study adds region‑specific, modality‑stratified diagnostic performance data for cervical nodal metastasis in thyroid cancer, reflecting real‑world imaging pathways in a Saudi tertiary center, rather than a randomized or fully paired accuracy trial.

Thyroid cancer commonly metastasizes to the nearby neck LNs, and the detection of metastasis is essential and affects patient management.13 FNA of LNs in thyroid cancer divides them into those that are either negative or positive for malignancy. PTC accounts for >70% of thyroid malignancies, making it the most common type of malignant thyroid tumor.14 Diagnosing PTC depends on multiple features, including architectural and nuclear features. Nuclear changes are crucial in diagnosing PTC and include intranuclear pseudo-inclusions, pale nuclei, irregular nuclei with a ground-glass appearance, prominent micronucleoli, nuclear grooves, and psammoma bodies (Figure 2a–2c).15–17 One common nonspecific feature observed in the cervical LNs was the presence of foamy macrophages (Figure 2d).

Xing et al18 advocated combining multiple imaging modalities to improve the diagnostic sensitivity and specificity for metastatic LNs. However, systematic prioritization of imaging modalities provides an early, practical guide for clinicians assessing LNs in patients with suspicious thyroid lesions and concomitant nodal disease. Because feature‑level sensitivity, specificity, and odds ratios were not formally analyzed, comments on individual imaging features in this study should be viewed as descriptive and hypothesis‑generating rather than as definitive predictors of malignancy.

The size ratio (long/short-axis) <2 is often considered normal for LNs.19 A size ratio of <1.5 was specifically evaluated in our study cervical LNs, with the majority of LNs (71.15%) being ≥1.5. This suggests a size ratio of ≥1.5 might be frequently associated with potential abnormalities. This can vary depending on the context and specific guidelines.

It is difficult to determine which cystic changes are indicative of LN metastases.20 However, cystic changes in LNs can be associated with various conditions, including metastasis, infection, or benign processes. In the context of thyroid cancer, cystic changes in the LNs might suggest a higher likelihood of metastasis; however, this typically requires further evaluation to confirm malignancy.20

The presence of punctate echogenic foci (calcifications) in LNs on CT can be associated with various conditions, including microcalcifications or psammoma bodies, which are often seen in PTC metastases.21 These findings suggest a higher likelihood of malignancy; however, further evaluation, such as FNA cytology, is typically required to confirm the diagnosis. Calcifications, especially microcalcifications, are highly specific to PTC and are often observed in metastatic LNs. In some studies, the presence of calcification in LNs suggests a high likelihood of malignancy, and all LNs showing calcification were diagnosed with metastases.22

Abnormal vascularity within the LNs, as detected by color Doppler ultrasound, suggests increased blood flow, which is associated with malignancy or inflammation. This feature is often considered a suspicious ultrasound characteristic of metastatic LNs.23

Notably, a smooth margin in the LNs or thyroid nodules is not always indicative of benignity.24 Although malignant nodules may exhibit irregular or lobulated margins persistently, this finding is not absolute. Our data revealed that 90.38% of the nodes had smooth margins, suggesting a predominance of benign characteristics; however, this requires further evaluation.24

A parallel orientation of the LNs or thyroid nodules typically means that the long axis of the node or nodule aligns with the long axis of the surrounding tissue or organs, such as the thyroid gland. This orientation is more commonly associated with benign conditions,25 because malignant nodules often exhibit nonparallel growth that disrupts the normal tissue plane.

Although cystic changes, punctate echogenic foci, abnormal vascularity, irregular margins, and nonparallel growth were relatively infrequent in our cohort, these features are well described as suspicious imaging markers of metastatic or malignant cervical lymph nodes in patients with thyroid cancer and, when present, in our series they were usually seen in cytology‑positive nodes.20–25 Accordingly, we interpret them as high‑risk imaging features that warrant targeted FNA rather than as benign variants. CT is considered the best modality for level-by-level evaluation of thyroid-related nodal disease and is less operator-dependent than US. CT is particularly valuable for assessing deeper LNs in the retropharyngeal, retrosternal, and mediastinal regions, which are difficult to assess using US. A systematic review and meta-analysis reported that CT had a sensitivity of 62% and specificity of 87% in detecting cervical LNs metastasis among patients with PTC.26 On CT, metastatic LNs are typically enlarged with blurred margins, cystic degeneration, absence of a fatty hilum, intense enhancement, calcification, and necrosis.9,27

LN stations are anatomical locations of LN, and their involvement can indicate the spread of cancer. Common sites for thyroid cancer include levels II–VI in the neck. The presence of metastases at these stations can influence treatment planning and prognosis.28 Our data were reflective, with the most common nodal station being Level II (31.37%). In particular, our analysis of nodal levels is based on imaging‑defined cervical stations without systematic level‑by‑level pathological confirmation of all nodes, so the reported level‑specific patterns should be interpreted as descriptive rather than as formal level‑wise diagnostic accuracy estimates.

A primary thyroid lesion with size ≥1.86 cm may indicate a higher risk of LN involvement, as larger tumors are more likely to metastasize.29 In thyroid cancer staging, tumors >2 cm and ≤4 cm are classified as T2 and may require aggressive management, including LN dissection, depending on LN status and extra-thyroidal extension.10,29 In our cohort, 23.33% of suspected thyroid lesions had a mean size ≥1.86 cm, suggesting that a substantial portion met size-based risk criteria.

The short-axis diameter of LNs is an established risk factor for malignancy.19

In our cohort, more than half of the LNs exceeded the short-axis threshold of 0.59 cm, raising suspicion for metastasis. The presence of multiple suspicious LNs further increases the probability of metastasis and may influence treatment decisions, including LN dissection or truculent therapy. According to Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST), a transverse diameter >10 mm can indicate metastasis.28

Although larger LNs are more likely to be metastatic, mean size thresholds may differ depending on criteria and clinical context in thyroid cancer.

Strong focal or diffuse cortical enhancement, reflecting increased vascularity and metabolic activity, has been associated with malignancy;30,31 however, this feature was observed in only 3.12% of LNs in our study, indicating limited prevalence in this cohort. These proportions translate into high sensitivity and specificity for ultrasound and moderate performance for CT in this cohort, supporting ultrasound as an effective first‑line modality for identifying nodes that warrant FNA, with CT providing complementary anatomic detail for staging.

MRI is characterized by the apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) diminution or restricted diffusion, ie, (ADC value <1,100), which was observed in 20% of LNs. Microcalcifications, early or late gadolinium enhancement, perinodal spread, or cystic or necrotic changes were not observed. Invasion or displacement of adjacent tissues, airways, or blood vessels by the tumor was found in 33.33% of cases, with the absence of ionizing radiation, reduced renal toxicity of the contrast material, and superior soft-tissue contrast providing additional advantages over other modalities. Cho et al6 performed a systematic review and meta-analysis and reported sensitivity and specificity of MRI for detecting metastatic LNs at 80% and 85%, respectively, which were higher than those of CT, making it a valuable alternative for nodal evaluation.

Signal intensity in MRI can provide valuable information regarding LN characteristics. T2WI high signal intensity is often associated with cystic or necrotic components within LNs, which can indicate metastasis, particularly in papillary thyroid carcinomas. Intermediate signal intensity is typically observed in the solid portions of LNs, which may be benign or malignant. Low signal intensity is less commonly associated with metastasis, but can be seen in some cases.32,33

In our data, a T2WI high signal intensity was observed in 60% of the cases, intermediate signal intensity in 40%, and low signal intensity was observed in none of the cases.

To evaluate the diffusion of water molecules within tissues, the ADC values were used. Therefore, lower ADC values often indicate restricted diffusion, which can be associated with malignancy owing to increased cellularity and reduced extracellular space. A lower ADC value suggests a higher likelihood of metastasis in the context of thyroid cancer, as malignant tissues tend to have restricted diffusion compared to benign tissues.34 However, these MRI findings are derived from only eight patients, with very wide confidence intervals, and therefore represent low‑precision, exploratory estimates that cannot support firm conclusions about intrinsic MRI performance.

Despite the lack of significant findings regarding the absence of a hilum, cystic changes, calcifications, strong cortical enhancement, heterogeneous enhancement, and perinodal spread were observed in our study sample. We believe that these features are more inclined toward malignancy in individual case assessments. However, given the capability of cross-sectional imaging to assess cervical nodal levels, the most frequently involved nodal station in our results was Level II, which concurs with the existing literature.28

Our findings should also be considered alongside ongoing developments in cytology‑based and molecular risk stratification, which increasingly refine preoperative assessment of thyroid nodules and nodal disease. Recent work in thyroid cytopathology and interventional cytology has highlighted how standardized reporting systems and ancillary testing can improve diagnostic certainty and guide management decisions in indeterminate or high‑risk nodules.35–38 Other studies have shown that molecular profiling and multigene testing on FNA samples further enhance risk stratification and help avoid unnecessary surgery in cytologically indeterminate lesions.37–39 In parallel, advanced imaging‑based risk stratification and guideline‑based sonographic systems continue to evolve for both thyroid nodules and cervical lymph nodes, refining the definition of suspicious features and modality‑specific roles.37–39 Within this broader context, the present study contributes region‑specific, modality‑stratified diagnostic performance data that complement these cytologic, molecular, and imaging frameworks and may inform integrated risk‑adapted algorithms for nodal assessment in thyroid cancer.

Strengths and Limitations

To the best of the author’s knowledge, this study is among the first to provide modality‑stratified diagnostic performance data for ultrasound, CT, and MRI in cervical nodal assessment in a Saudi thyroid cancer population, using standardized imaging criteria and a consistent FNA reference standard, thereby adding region‑specific evidence to complement existing international reports. This study advances imaging-based stratification of lymph node assessment in thyroid cancer by applying standardized criteria that may reduce interobserver variability and by providing region-specific, modality-stratified performance data as a key strength. It also reflects a clinically realistic nodal distribution, with predominant Level II involvement, and incorporates multiple imaging modalities, supporting its relevance to everyday practice.

However, several limitations should be noted. The single-center design, modest sample size, and particularly small MRI subgroup may restrict generalizability and preclude a definitive ranking of modalities or firm claims of ultrasound superiority for comprehensive nodal staging. Additionally, by restricting inclusion to patients with thyroid nodules showing malignant behavior and concomitant lymphadenopathy, the cohort is enriched for higher‑risk disease and may introduce spectrum bias when compared with unselected thyroid nodule populations. Nevertheless, this design reflects the real‑world group in whom nodal imaging and FNA are actually undertaken, and our conclusions are intended to apply to this targeted diagnostic context.

Ultrasound, CT, and MRI were performed according to clinical indications rather than in a standardized paired fashion, so our estimates represent modality‑specific performance rather than direct head‑to‑head testing in every individual patient. Also, US‑guided FNA cytology, rather than systematic surgical histopathology of all cervical lymph nodes, served as the primary reference standard, which may underestimate occult metastasis and could influence sensitivity and specificity; our findings should therefore be interpreted as performance relative to clinically indicated FNA sampling rather than to exhaustive nodal dissection.

Because ultrasound, CT, and MRI were not uniformly performed across all patients, modality-specific performance estimates were derived from clinically selected, non-overlapping subgroups. This non-paired design introduces potential selection bias and precludes formal head-to-head comparison; accordingly, between-modality inferences should be interpreted as exploratory and descriptive rather than definitive. In particular, the MRI subgroup comprised only eight patients; therefore, the apparent 100% accuracy is a highly imprecise point estimate and should not be interpreted as evidence of consistently excellent MRI performance.

Ultrasound’s operator dependence introduces potential diagnostic variability, the reference standard was largely cytology from targeted FNA rather than systematic surgical histology (risking underrepresentation of deep or unsampled nodes), and ADC values did not demonstrate clear incremental value for neck nodal assessment and require validation in larger prospective cohorts.

Future studies should include multicenter, larger, and more diverse populations, integrate advanced techniques such as radiomics and artificial intelligence, and incorporate longitudinal follow-up to strengthen external validity and refine preoperative risk stratification.

Conclusions

This study provides region-specific insights into thyroid cancer diagnostics in Saudi Arabia, with demographics mirroring broader regional trends. Ultrasound, despite slightly lower diagnostic precision, remains pivotal for initial assessment because it depicts high-risk nodal morphology, including axis ratios and vascular patterns. CT offers higher sensitivity and accuracy for nodal staging, particularly in defining involved levels and structural anomalies, making it essential for preoperative planning. These findings reflect diagnostic performance relative to FNA cytology in a real-world, clinically indicated sampling context rather than exhaustive histologic confirmation of all nodal metastases. Given the non‑paired design, higher‑risk cohort, and use of US‑guided FNA as an imperfect reference standard, the results support ultrasound as a high‑performing first‑line modality for triaging cervical lymph nodes, while preserving a clear role for CT and MRI in deeper nodal mapping and complex anatomical evaluation. In practice, ultrasound, CT, and MRI should be regarded as complementary modalities, and the reported estimates as descriptive guidance to inform, not dictate, individualized imaging strategies. Although less commonly used, MRI adds tissue composition and diffusion information in complex cases, underscoring the need to leverage modality-specific strengths in Saudi practice, where early detection must balance diagnostic rigor with resource availability.
