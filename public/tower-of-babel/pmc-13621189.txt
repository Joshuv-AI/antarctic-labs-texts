Cardiometabolic and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease outcomes associated with tirzepatide: insights from a 1-year multicenter observational study
Authors: Rene de J Rivera Gutierrez, Regina Castaneda, Alfredo Verastegui, Wissam Ghusn, Dima Bechenati, Maria A Espinosa, Jose Villamarin, Elif Tama, Kirsten Frederiksen, Andres Acosta, Maria D Hurtado Andrade
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate the association of long-term, continuous tirzepatide use with cardiometabolic risk parameters and estimated 10-year atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) risk in real-world practice.MethodsThis multicenter retrospective observational study included 1028 adults with overweight or obesity who received weekly subcutaneous tirzepatide for at least 12 months within the Mayo Clinic Health System from June 2022 through May 2024. Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease risk analyses included 275 individuals with complete baseline and 12-month follow-up data. Outcomes were changes in cardiometabolic parameters, estimated 10-year ASCVD risk, and ASCVD risk category.ResultsAt 12 months, median total body weight loss (TBWL) was 12.6%. Fasting glucose, HbA1c, triglycerides, and alanine aminotransferase decreased by 19.5%, 16.0%, 8.5%, and 10.6%, respectively, whereas HDL-cholesterol increased by 11.3% (all P < .001). Mean 10-year ASCVD risk did not change significantly overall (mean relative change, −3.7%; P = .18), but changes differed by TBWL category (analysis of variance, P < .001). Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease risk decreased among individuals achieving 10% to 20% TBWL (−9.6%; P = .009) and >20% TBWL (−14.2%; P = .005), but not among those achieving <10% TBWL (+7.7%; P = .22). Low-risk prevalence increased from 38.3% to 46.4% (P < .001), with 11.7% transitioning from higher-risk categories to low risk.Main findingsContinuous tirzepatide use for at least 12 months was associated with clinically meaningful improvements in cardiometabolic parameters. Reductions in estimated 10-year ASCVD risk were observed among individuals achieving at least 10% TBWL, supporting greater weight loss as an important marker of cardiovascular risk improvement.Keywords: tirzepatide, obesity, weight loss, cardiometabolic risk, ASCVD risk score

Obesity is a chronic disease resulting from the interplay of genetic, environmental, behavioral, and metabolic factors (1). In 2021, 172 million adults aged 25 and older in the United States were living with overweight or obesity, a number that is projected to rise to 213 million by 2050 (2). Independent of other major risk factors, such as dyslipidemia, obesity is a well-established contributor to cardiovascular disease, mediated through complex metabolic and inflammatory pathways driven by dysfunctional adipose tissue (3-5).

As a key regulator of metabolism, vascular tone, and inflammation, adipose tissue becomes pathologic in obesity, a state known as adiposopathy and characterized by adipocyte hypertrophy, oxidative stress, and chronic systemic inflammation (6). This dysfunction diminishes the vasoprotective effects of perivascular adipose tissue, shifting it to a pro-inflammatory phenotype that further impairs vascular tone, promotes vascular remodeling and stiffness, and accelerates atherosclerosis (7, 8). Furthermore, adiposopathy drives ectopic fat deposition, insulin resistance, and metabolic dysfunction, fueling hypertension, dyslipidemia, and type 2 diabetes, all independent cardiovascular disease risk factors (9-11). In this context, weight-loss interventions that effectively target adiposopathy have been shown to improve cardiometabolic health and reduce cardiovascular disease risk (12).

Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs), effective for weight management, have well-established pleiotropic cardiometabolic benefits (13). In cardiovascular outcomes studies, liraglutide and semaglutide have shown to reduce the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs), a composite of cardiovascular death, nonfatal myocardial infarction, and nonfatal stroke, among individuals with overweight or obesity and type 2 diabetes (14, 15). Semaglutide has also demonstrated MACE risk reduction in individuals with overweight or obesity without type 2 diabetes (16).

Tirzepatide, a dual GLP-1 and glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide receptor agonist approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for chronic weight management in 2023, has shown superior weight reduction efficacy compared with older agents, achieving a mean placebo subtracted weight loss of 18% after 72 weeks of treatment (17, 18). Remarkably, a post hoc analysis from the SURMOUNT-1 trial demonstrated a 22% reduction in the 10-year atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) risk in individuals with overweight or obesity without type 2 diabetes (19, 20). Despite its proven efficacy, real-world evidence for tirzepatide's impact on cardiometabolic health and cardiovascular disease risk reduction remains scarce. This study evaluates the effects of long-term, continuous tirzepatide use on cardiometabolic outcomes and estimated ASCVD risk in a real-world setting and examines the extent to which these changes are associated with the magnitude of weight loss achieved.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This multicenter retrospective analysis of electronic health records (EHRs) from the Mayo Clinic Health System assessed cardiometabolic outcomes in adults receiving weekly subcutaneous (SQ) tirzepatide injections for weight management. The study was approved by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board (#17-001068). Patients who initiated treatment between 3 June 2022 and 19 May 2024 were identified from a comprehensive clinical database (Fig. 1). Eligible participants included individuals with overweight (body mass index [BMI] ≥27 kg/m2) and adiposity-related comorbidities or those with obesity (BMI ≥30 kg/m2) regardless of comorbidities. Per protocol design, only individuals with at least 12 months of continuous tirzepatide use were included. This is because this study was not designed to estimate the overall weight loss effectiveness of tirzepatide; rather, it aimed to accurately assess the clinical cardiometabolic effects of tirzepatide among patients maintained on long-term, continuous therapy. Exclusion criteria encompassed prior bariatric surgery (surgical or endoscopic), concurrent use of other obesity medications (eg, orlistat, phentermine/topiramate), compounded weight loss products, inconsistent medication adherence, pregnancy, active malignancy, or medical conditions likely to significantly affect weight loss (eg, uncontrolled hypothyroidism). Participants lacking documented baseline or follow-up cardiometabolic parameters were also excluded. Patients with a prior history of established cardiovascular disease were retained in the primary cardiometabolic outcomes cohort but were excluded from analyses involving ASCVD risk estimation. The study adhered to Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Data collection

Data were collected from the EHRs at baseline (±14 days from tirzepatide initiation) and at 12 months (±45 days). Medication start date and adherence were assessed through detailed chart review, including documentation of prescription refills, patient-reported use, and clinician notes during follow-up visits. Demographic characteristics (age, sex, and race), anthropometric measurements (weight, BMI), and vital signs (systolic and diastolic blood pressure) were collected. Laboratory data comprised glycated hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), fasting glucose, triglycerides, total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and alanine aminotransferase (ALT). Medical history data included adiposity-related cardiometabolic comorbidities (type 2 diabetes, dyslipidemia, hypertension, metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease, and obstructive sleep apnea), smoking history (current or former and duration of smoking), hypertension therapy, and use of statin and aspirin. The maximum tirzepatide dose achieved, defined as the dose achieved at last follow-up rather than a time-specific exposure, was recorded and categorized as low (2.5, 5, or 7.5 mg) or high (10, 12.5, or 15 mg). All EHR-derived data were rigorously validated through a comprehensive manual chart review to ensure the accuracy and completeness of weight measurements and medication records. Additionally, quality checks were performed, including cross-checking recorded values against clinician notes and visit summaries, as well as identifying and reconciling inconsistent entries.

Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease risk scores were obtained using the ASCVD Risk Estimator Plus tool from the American College of Cardiology (21). Baseline and follow-up scores were calculated for all patients with sufficient data and without a prior history of established cardiovascular disease, defined as acute myocardial infarction, angina or documented coronary artery disease (including prior coronary revascularization), ischemic stroke, or symptomatic peripheral artery disease. We restricted the analysis to participants aged 40-79 years, consistent with the derivation and validation of the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association Pooled Cohort Equations, which were developed to estimate 10-year ASCVD risk only within this age range (22). Patients were then categorized as low risk (<5%), borderline risk (5-7.4%), intermediate risk (7.5-19.9%), and high risk (≥20%), as defined by the American College of Cardiology (22).

Study endpoints

The primary endpoint was the change in cardiometabolic parameters after 12 months of tirzepatide therapy. Secondary endpoints included changes in ASCVD risk score and shifts in ASCVD risk category, assessed overall and stratified by total body weight loss (TBWL) and type 2 diabetes status. A 3-level TBWL categorization (<10%, 10-20%, and >20%) was used to analyze relative change in continuous 10-year ASCVD risk, whereas a 2-level categorization (<10% vs ≥10%) was used to analyze ASCVD risk category transitions, given the sample size requirements for Bowker and McNemar tests.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were compared between baseline and follow-up using Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Relative change was calculated as (follow-up − baseline)/baseline × 100 and reported as the mean percent change unless otherwise specified; mean relative percent changes were additionally evaluated using 2-sided paired t-tests against zero, with bootstrap-derived 95% confidence intervals for individual-level relative changes. Comparisons of relative changes across TBWL categories were performed using 1-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey post hoc test. Categorical transitions in ASCVD risk categories were evaluated using Bowker test of symmetry, and binary transitions in low ASCVD risk status (low vs not low) were assessed using McNemar test. All tests were 2-sided with statistical significance defined as P < .05. Analyses were conducted using BlueSky Statistics (version 10.3.7) and R (version 4.4.2), and figures were generated using R and GraphPad Prism (version 10.4.2).

Results

A total of 24 230 tirzepatide prescriptions were issued between June 2022 and May 2024 within the Mayo Clinic Health System. A total of 2325 patients met our inclusion criteria of at least 12 months of treatment and a BMI ≥27 kg/m2 with an adiposity-related comorbidity or BMI ≥30 kg/m2; of these, 1297 patients met at least one exclusion criterion and were therefore excluded. The most common reasons for exclusion were history of bariatric surgery (n = 405), concomitant use of other obesity medications (n = 371), active malignancy (n = 221), inconsistent use (n = 138), missing baseline weight (n = 87), no follow-up weight (n = 42), pregnancy (n = 8), medical conditions that interfere with outcomes (eg, Prader–Willi syndrome, uncontrolled hypothyroidism; n = 7), and use of compounded tirzepatide (n = 4). The remaining 1028 patients were included in the primary analysis (Fig. 1). For the secondary endpoints, we excluded participants with a prior cardiovascular disease event, incomplete data on ASCVD risk score parameters, or age younger than 40 or older than 79 years (n = 753), leaving 275 patients for calculation of the 10-year ASCVD risk score at baseline and at last follow-up.

Baseline characteristics and demographics

Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The primary analysis cohort (n = 1028) was predominantly middle-aged, with a median age of 57 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 48-63), nearly 60% women (n = 588), and most participants were White (90%, n = 926). At baseline, the median weight was 107.2 kg (IQR: 93.1-126.6) and BMI was 36.7 kg/m2 (IQR: 32.7-42.2), with one-third of the cohort (34%) classified as having class III obesity (≥40 kg/m2). Adiposity-related comorbidities were common, including dyslipidemia (78%), type 2 diabetes (75%), and hypertension (72%). Active smoking was reported in 7% of participants. History of previous obesity medication was noted in 35% of participants. Tirzepatide dosing varied, with 35% achieving the 15 mg weekly dose and 71% reaching a high dose (≥10 mg weekly).

In the ASCVD cohort (n = 275), the median age was slightly higher at 59 years (IQR: 52-65), two-thirds were women (66%, n = 183), and most were White (91%). Median weight and BMI were 105.5 kg (IQR: 91.0-119.6) and 36.1 kg/m2 (IQR: 32.4-41.4), respectively, with 30% classified as having class III obesity. The rate of comorbidities was similar to the full cohort, with dyslipidemia (83%), type 2 diabetes (77%), and hypertension (72%) being the most prevalent. Active smoking was reported in 8%. Nearly half (46%) reported prior to obesity pharmacotherapy. Use of cardioprotective medications was frequent, with 65% on antihypertensives, 61% on statins, and 30% on aspirin. Most patients reached a high dose of tirzepatide (68%), with over one-third (34%) achieving the maximum weekly dose of 15 mg.

Cardiometabolic outcomes following tirzepatide

Following 12 months of treatment, patients achieved a median TBWL of 12.6 (IQR: 6.7-19.4%) percent. After 12 months of treatment, patients achieved a median TBWL of 12.6% (IQR, 6.7-19.4%) and experienced significant improvements across most cardiometabolic parameters (Fig. 2). Glucose parameters demonstrated marked reductions, with fasting glucose decreasing by a mean of 19.5% (95% confidence interval [CI], 13.3-24.0%; P < .001) and HbA1c by 16.0% (95% CI, 15.0-16.9%; P < .001). Blood pressure parameters decreased modestly, with systolic blood pressure reducing by 2.8% (95% CI, 2.0-3.6%; P < .001) and diastolic blood pressure by 2.6% (95% CI, 1.7-3.5%; P < .001). Among lipid parameters, triglycerides decreased by 8.5% (95% CI, 4.7-12.3%; P < .001) and HDL-cholesterol increased by 11.3% (95% CI, 9.3-13.5%; P < .001), while changes in total cholesterol (−1.8%; 95% CI, −3.6% to 0.2%; P = .069) and LDL-cholesterol (−1.2%; 95% CI, −4.6% to 3.0%; P = .560) did not reach statistical significance. Liver enzymes showed a significant reduction in ALT (−10.6%; 95% CI, −14.7% to −6.3%; P < .001), whereas AST changes were not significant (−0.1%; 95% CI, −3.8% to 3.7%; P = .941) (Table 2).

Concomitant cardiovascular medication use

To assess whether changes in concomitant cardiovascular pharmacotherapy contributed to the observed cardiometabolic improvements, we examined statin, antihypertensive, and aspirin use at baseline and 12 months. In the full cohort (n = 1028), statin use was 64.8% at baseline and 66.2% at 12 months, antihypertensive use was 66.5% and 67.2%, and aspirin use was 39.7% and 38.2%, respectively. After correction for multiple comparisons, none of these changes was statistically significant (all Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted P > .05). Among patients on antihypertensives, 90.5% had no change in the number of agents during follow-up (Wilcoxon signed-rank P = .647). In a sensitivity analysis restricted to 903 patients (87.8%) whose statin, antihypertensive, and aspirin status remained unchanged throughout follow-up, the direction, magnitude, and statistical significance of all cardiometabolic parameter changes were consistent with the primary analysis (Tables S1 and S2) (23).

Ten-year ASCVD risk reduction by weight loss risk category

Among 275 individuals with 10-year ASCVD risk scores available at baseline and 12 months, mean 10-year ASCVD risk did not change significantly in the overall cohort, but changes differed by TBWL (Fig. 3A): participants with <10% TBWL had a nonsignificant increase in risk, whereas those achieving 10% to 20% TBWL showed a significant reduction and those with >20% TBWL demonstrated the largest significant improvement. In the ASCVD cohort, concomitant cardiovascular medication use was similarly stable during follow-up (statin 61.7% vs 65.0%; antihypertensive 64.6% vs 65.0%; aspirin 29.9% vs 28.8%; all Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted P > .05), and a sensitivity analysis excluding the 27 patients (9.9%) with any medication change yielded results consistent with the primary analysis (Table S3) (23). Consistently, mean relative ASCVD risk change was +7.7% (95% CI, 0.7-14.8) for <10% TBWL vs −9.6% (95% CI, −15.3 to −3.9) for 10% to 20% and −14.2% (−22.0 to −6.3) for >20% TBWL (Fig. 3B; Table 3). Between-group differences in relative 10-year ASCVD change among TBWL thresholds were statistically significant (1-way ANOVA, P < .001), with post hoc Tukey comparisons demonstrating that the relative change in ASCVD risk was significantly different between the <10% TBWL group and both the 10% to 20% and >20% TBWL groups (both P < .001), whereas the difference between the 10% and 20% and >20% TBWL groups was not significant (P = .66) (Fig. 3B).

Ten-year ASCVD risk category transitions

Analysis of ASCVD risk category transitions demonstrated a significant improvement in ASCVD risk distribution (Bowker χ2 = 13.20, P = .010) for the entire cohort (n = 275), with 11.7% (32/275) of patients transitioning to low-risk status from a higher-risk category after 12 months of tirzepatide treatment (Fig. 4A). The proportion of individuals at low risk increased from 38.3% to 46.4%, representing an absolute increase of 8.0 percentage points and a 21.0% relative improvement (McNemar χ2 = 11.52, P < .001).

When stratified by weight loss magnitude (Fig. 4B), patients with <10% TBWL (n = 112) demonstrated minimal change in risk distribution (Bowker χ2 = 2.89, P = .577), with only 7.1% (8/112) transitioning to low-risk status from a higher-risk category. The proportion in the low-risk category remained largely unchanged, from 31.2% at baseline to 33.0% at 12 months (absolute increase, 1.8 percentage points; 5.7% relative increase; McNemar χ2 = 0.29, P = .593). In contrast, patients achieving ≥10% TBWL (n = 162) experienced significant favorable risk transitions over 12 months (Bowker χ2 = 19.76, P < .001). Within this group, 75.0% (21/28) of borderline-risk patients and 8.3% (3/36) of intermediate-risk patients moved into the low-risk category. The proportion at low risk increased from 43.2% (70/162) at baseline to 55.6% (90/162) at 12 months, representing an absolute increase of 12.3 percentage points and a 28.6% relative improvement (McNemar χ2 = 14.29, P < .001). While the proportion of patients transitioning to a low-risk category was higher among those achieving ≥10% TBWL than among those achieving <10% TBWL, this between-group difference did not reach statistical significance: 14.8% (24/162) of patients in the ≥10% TBWL group transitioned to low-risk status from a higher-risk category, compared with 7.1% (8/112) in the <10% TBWL group (between-group difference, 7.7 percentage points; χ2 = 3.12, P = .077).

We further evaluated the relative contribution of weight loss to ASCVD risk reduction by comparing ASCVD risk category transitions among individuals achieving 10% to 20% TBWL (n = 92) vs >20% TBWL (n = 70). Both groups demonstrated meaningful increases in low-risk prevalence: patients with 10% to 20% TBWL increased from 34.8% to 47.8% (absolute increase, 13.0 percentage points; 37.5% relative increase; McNemar χ2 = 10.29, P = .001), while those with >20% TBWL increased from 54.3% to 65.7% (absolute increase, 11.4 percentage points; 21.1% relative increase; McNemar χ2 = 4.57, P = .033). Although both groups exhibited favorable shifts in ASCVD risk distribution, the shift reached statistical significance in the 10% to 20% TBWL group (Bowker χ2 = 13.62, P = .003) but not in the >20% TBWL group (Bowker χ2 = 7.27, P = .122). Notably, among patients with >20% TBWL, 21.4% (3/14) of intermediate-risk patients transitioned to low-risk status, compared with none (0/22) in the 10% to 20% TBWL group, though the >20% TBWL group began with a substantially higher baseline low-risk prevalence (54.3% vs 34.8%).

Because type 2 diabetes is an independent risk factor for cardiovascular disease and, depending on disease severity, may be considered a coronary heart disease equivalent, we evaluated tirzepatide-associated ASCVD risk category transitions among individuals with and without type 2 diabetes (Fig. 4C). Although both groups exhibited favorable shifts in ASCVD risk distribution, these overall distributional changes were not statistically significant for either diabetes (Bowker χ2 = 8.62, P = .071) or no diabetes (Bowker χ2 = 6.50, P = .090). However, among individuals with diabetes (n = 210), the proportion classified as low risk increased from 29.0% (61/210) to 36.7% (77/210), an absolute increase of 7.6 percentage points and a 26.2% relative increase (McNemar χ2 = 8.00, P = .005). In this group, 59% (20/34) of borderline-risk and 6% (4/65) of intermediate-risk patients transitioned to low-risk status. A similar directionality was observed among individuals without diabetes (n = 64), but the change did not reach statistical significance: the proportion classified as low risk increased from 68.8% (44/64) to 78.1% (50/64), an absolute increase of 9.4 percentage points and a 13.6% relative increase (McNemar χ2 = 3.60, P = .058), with 78% (7/9) of borderline-risk patients transitioning to low-risk status.

Discussion

In this real-world study of over 1000 adults with overweight or obesity treated with tirzepatide, we observed durable, clinically meaningful improvements in body weight and cardiometabolic risk markers over 12 months. Furthermore, among participants with complete ASCVD risk data, the 10-year ASCVD risk did not change significantly in the overall cohort; however, changes varied by weight loss magnitude, with significant reductions observed only among individuals losing ≥10% of baseline weight. These patients experienced favorable transitions into lower ASCVD risk categories, underscoring the real-world relevance of achieving and sustaining clinically meaningful weight loss for cardiovascular risk reduction.

Our observed 12-month median TBWL of 12.6% aligns with, and helps extend, pivotal randomized trial evidence into routine care settings. In SURMOUNT (tirzepatide for obesity treatment) and SURPASS (tirzepatide for type 2 diabetes treatment) programs, TBWL ranged from approximately 12% with 5 mg to 20% with 15 mg after 48 weeks in individuals without diabetes and from 8% to 13% after 40 weeks in those with type 2 diabetes (17, 24). By including patients with and without diabetes and following them for 1 year, our findings reinforce that double-digit weight loss is achievable outside clinical trials, while also emphasizing the accompanying improvements in glycemia, blood pressure, and lipids—pathways that plausibly mediate downstream cardiovascular risk reduction (25).

Previous studies from the SURPASS and SURMOUNT programs, which included patients with and without diabetes, respectively, have demonstrated the favorable glycemic and cardiometabolic effects of tirzepatide (26). The ASCVD risk estimator remains a widely used clinical tool to guide intensity of preventive therapies, making changes in predicted risk highly interpretable at the point of care. In that context, our findings are directionally consistent with post hoc analyses from SURPASS and SURMOUNT demonstrating favorable cardiometabolic effects of tirzepatide across diverse populations (19, 26, 27). For example, post hoc analyses from SURMOUNT reported significant reductions in 10-year ASCVD risk among individuals with obesity and prediabetes, with larger decreases at higher tirzepatide doses vs placebo (19). Recent SURMOUNT-5 post hoc analyses further support these findings, showing that tirzepatide achieved a 23.7% relative reduction in predicted 10-year cardiovascular disease risk compared with 13.6% for semaglutide (P < .001), in parallel with greater mean weight loss with tirzepatide at week 72 (−20.2% vs −13.7%) (28). Together with these trial data, our real-world results support the concept that achieving larger, sustained weight loss is associated with clinically meaningful improvements in global cardiovascular risk estimation.

Predicted risk reductions provide an important complement to cardiovascular outcomes evidence by capturing near-term, clinically interpretable changes in global risk profiles that align with—and may help contextualize—observed event reductions. Semaglutide, available on the market for a longer period and therefore supported by more cardiovascular outcomes data, has demonstrated to reduce the risk of a composite of death from cardiovascular causes, nonfatal myocardial infarction, or nonfatal stroke by 20% (hazard ratio [HR] 0.80; 95% CI, 0.72-0.90) when administered once weekly at 2.4 mg for a mean duration of 33 months (16). These results have been consistently supported by recent meta-analyses demonstrating significant reductions in cardiovascular mortality and MACE (29). For tirzepatide, preliminary data from the ongoing SURPASS-CVOT, which will provide definitive information on the cardiovascular safety and efficacy of tirzepatide in patients with type 2 diabetes and established ASCVD, suggest a 28% reduction in MACE (HR 0.72; 95% CI 0.55-0.94) and a 39% reduction in all-cause mortality (HR 0.61; 95% CI 0.45-0.82), positioning tirzepatide as a therapy with established cardiovascular benefits beyond its superior weight-loss efficacy (30). Against this backdrop, our real-world findings extend the evidence base by demonstrating that tirzepatide use in routine care is associated with meaningful reductions in predicted 10-year ASCVD risk, particularly among individuals achieving clinically important weight-loss thresholds, thereby reinforcing the plausibility of downstream cardiovascular benefit.

A key question is how much of tirzepatide's cardiometabolic benefit is explained by weight loss itself. Notably, improvements in glycemic and metabolic parameters have been reported even among individuals achieving <5% TBWL, suggesting mechanisms that may be partially weight-loss independent, potentially via incretin receptor–mediated effects on insulin sensitivity, β-cell function, and lipid metabolism (31). At the same time, our data show that greater weight reduction is associated with more pronounced improvements in cardiometabolic parameters and larger reductions in 10-year ASCVD risk, consistent with contemporary guidance emphasizing that clinically meaningful cardiometabolic and cardiovascular benefits generally emerge with ≥5% to 10% TBWL and increase beyond that range (32). Taken together, these observations support a dual framework: tirzepatide likely exerts direct metabolic effects, but the magnitude of global risk reduction appears to be amplified as weight loss increases.

The biological plausibility of larger cardiovascular benefit with greater weight loss is strong and likely multifactorial (33). Mechanistically, greater weight loss typically yields larger improvements in insulin resistance, glycemia, blood pressure, and atherogenic lipid profiles (34). In parallel, reductions in visceral and ectopic fat depots—though not directly measured here—may be particularly consequential. Decreases in visceral adipose tissue and perivascular adipose tissue have been linked to lower vascular inflammation, improved endothelial and microvascular function, enhanced arterial compliance, and reduced cardiometabolic dysfunction (35). In other words, larger reductions in body weight likely reflect preferential mobilization of visceral and ectopic fat depots, which disrupt the pathophysiological links between obesity and cardiovascular disease (36).

Beyond changes in continuous risk scores, the downward movement across ASCVD risk categories among participants achieving >10% TBWL is particularly actionable in clinical practice. Risk strata guide statin initiation and intensity, blood pressure treatment thresholds, and consideration of aspirin (37, 38). The transitions to lower ASCVD risk categories observed in our study highlight a potential reorientation of cardiometabolic risk management—from incrementally treating dyslipidemia, hypertension, and hyperglycemia as separate problems to targeting excess adiposity as a central upstream driver that simultaneously improves multiple interrelated risk factors. In this framework, sustained double-digit TBWL with tirzepatide becomes not only a weight goal but a practical therapeutic strategy to remodel overall cardiometabolic risk profiles in routine care.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths, including its large sample size and the use of real-world data from a heterogeneous patient population, which enhances generalizability beyond randomized controlled trials. In addition, the fact that all included patients used tirzepatide for at least 12 months allows for a meaningful assessment of long-term treatment effects. For the primary cardiometabolic outcomes analysis, all patients had complete baseline and 12-month follow-up data. For ASCVD risk score calculations, only patients with complete data for all required parameters were included, ensuring accuracy of risk estimates without reliance on imputation methods.

Despite these strengths, the study also has limitations inherent to its retrospective, observational design. First, despite a meticulous chart review, the documentation of medication start and stop dates may not be entirely precise. Although all patients received lifestyle intervention advice, incomplete documentation on adherence introduces residual confounding, as concomitant lifestyle modification with tirzepatide treatment cannot be fully accounted for. Similarly, cardiovascular medication use was largely stable during follow-up, and sensitivity analyses excluding patients with medication changes yielded consistent results; however, unmeasured differences in medication dosing, titration, or adherence may have independently influenced cardiometabolic outcomes.

A potential overestimation of the treatment effect may have been introduced by including only patients who completed 12 months of tirzepatide treatment, as individuals who discontinued therapy early due to side effects or lack of efficacy were excluded. This bias likely enriched the cohort for individuals who tolerated and responded favorably to treatment, potentially leading to larger observed improvements in weight, glycemic control, lipid profiles, blood pressure, and estimated ASCVD risk. Consequently, the findings should be interpreted as reflecting outcomes among persistent users. Future studies using an intention-to-treat design would help quantify the impact of treatment discontinuation on cardiometabolic outcomes and provide more conservative estimates of treatment effectiveness. However, we emphasize that the focus of this study was to assess the effect of continuous, long-term use of tirzepatide on cardiometabolic risk factors and 10-year ASCVD risk reduction.

The exclusion of patients with incomplete ASCVD risk data may also introduce selection bias, as those with missing laboratory values may differ systematically from the analyzed cohort. Although baseline characteristics between included and excluded patients were generally comparable, selection bias remains possible, as individuals excluded due to missing risk factor data, prior cardiovascular disease, or eligibility criteria may have had different baseline cardiovascular risk profiles than the analyzed cohort.

An important limitation is the absence of waist circumference, visceral adipose tissue, and body composition data. These parameters, particularly visceral adiposity, are intimately linked to cardiovascular disease risk and may offer more precise risk stratification than total body weight change. Future studies incorporating these measures would be valuable for understanding which components of weight loss most strongly predict cardiovascular benefit. Finally, given that the study population was predominantly White, these findings may not be fully generalizable to racially and ethnically diverse populations, particularly since both the effectiveness of GLP-1–based medications and the cardiovascular benefits of weight loss may vary across demographic groups.

In addition to potential differences in the effectiveness of GLP-1–based therapies and the cardiovascular benefits of weight loss across demographic groups, the applicability of our ASCVD risk estimates may be limited by the use of the American College of Cardiology (ACC)/American Heart Association (AHA) Pooled Cohort Equations, which were derived primarily from White and African American cohorts and have been less extensively validated in Hispanic/Latino and other populations. However, given that our cohort was predominantly White, the use of the Pooled Cohort Equations likely introduced less uncertainty within the studied population than it would in more diverse cohorts. Nevertheless, the observed changes in predicted ASCVD risk should be interpreted cautiously when extrapolated to more diverse patient populations.

It is important to emphasize that the observed findings should be interpreted as associations, as the retrospective design precludes causal inference. These findings warrant further investigation to better characterize the long-term cardiometabolic effects of tirzepatide and inform individualized treatment decisions. Nevertheless, this study provides important real-world evidence on the long-term cardiometabolic effects of tirzepatide across a large clinical population, complementing existing clinical trial data.

Conclusion

In this real-world study, tirzepatide therapy was associated with meaningful improvements across key cardiometabolic parameters, including blood pressure, glycemic control, and lipid profile. Remarkably, superior weight loss was associated with larger reductions in calculated 10-year ASCVD risk, with a substantial proportion of patients shifting to lower risk categories. These findings highlight tirzepatide not only as an effective treatment for weight management but also as a therapy with the potential to reduce cardiovascular risk, particularly when a meaningful weight loss threshold is achieved, further emphasizing the relevance of more substantial weight reduction for optimizing cardiometabolic outcomes.
