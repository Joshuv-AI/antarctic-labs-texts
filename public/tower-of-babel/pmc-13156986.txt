Nine familial exterminations: Cas13d eliminates uveal melanoma cells through collateral RNA degradation
Authors: Dejin Zheng, Bin Gu
Published: 2026

Main text

In ancient China, grave treason was punished by nine familial exterminations, leaving no chance of future rebellion. In this issue of Molecular Therapy Oncology, Stauber et al. leveraged the principle of “guilty by association” and exploited the collateral RNA degradation activity of Cas13d to eliminate uveal melanoma (UM) cells.1 This research, together with other recent studies exploring similar approaches in aggressive cancers such as metastatic prostate cancer,2,3 is opening new avenues for treating cancers driven by oncogenes that are not targetable by current approaches, such as small-molecule inhibitors and antibody therapies.

Targeting cancer-specific or cancer-overexpressed dependency genes with small-molecule inhibitors or antibody-based agents has transformed the landscape of cancer therapy. However, the majority of potential targets are not amenable to either modality. For these targets, RNA interference (RNAi)-based knockdown (KD) and CRISPR-Cas9-mediated DNA-level targeting are being explored, each with distinct limitations. RNAi often suffers from relatively low on-target KD efficiency and substantial off-target effects, whereas CRISPR-Cas9 faces challenges in delivering large ribonucleoprotein complexes or their coding vectors, as well as risks of genome instability arising from DNA damage and permanent sequence alterations.

Recently, RNA-targeting type VI CRISPR systems, including the Cas13 family, have emerged as promising tools for RNA KD.4 Cas13 effectors exhibit higher on-target efficiency and reduced off-target activity compared with RNAi and are smaller in size, enabling more efficient delivery to target cells using viral or non-viral vectors. The on-target KD activity of Cas13 has been leveraged to selectively target oncogenes, such as mutant KRAS, in cancer cells, as well as to manipulate immune cells and the tumor microenvironment to enhance immunotherapies.5,6,7

However, on-target KD alone does not address a key biological challenge in cancer therapy: the remarkable plasticity of cancer cell gene regulatory networks (GRNs). Even after initial inhibition, cancer cells can rewire their GRNs to escape dependence on the targeted oncogene, including following clean genetic knockouts.8 Cas13 proteins exhibit a “collateral effect,” characterized by promiscuous ribonuclease activity upon target recognition, which enables concomitant depletion of survival-supporting transcripts—such as the housekeeping gene GAPDH—alongside oncogene KD, potentially limiting such escape mechanisms.2 Leveraging this collateral activity, the Qi group and others have targeted aggressive cancers.

In their study titled “A CRISPR-Cas13d cancer therapeutic enables selective elimination of uveal melanoma,” the Qi group presented a complete “target-to-therapy” workflow for developing a Cas13d-based therapy for highly aggressive and intractable UM.1 They established a computational pipeline integrating large-scale gene expression datasets (The Cancer Genome Atlas [TCGA] and The Genotype-Tissue Expression Portal [GTEx]) and functional databases (DepMap and Open Targets), identifying RASGRP3 as a highly expressed, UM-specific dependency that is not tractable by small molecules or antibodies. An optimized guide RNA was subsequently designed and screened for Cas13d-mediated RASGRP3 KD, followed by development of an effective lipid nanoparticle (LNP) delivery system for in vitro application. The resulting Cas13d therapeutics demonstrated potent cytotoxicity toward UM cells (>90% reduction in viability) while exhibiting minimal toxicity in non-UM cells with low RASGRP3 expression. Importantly, collateral KD of housekeeping genes such as GAPDH contributed substantially to the enhanced killing efficacy relative to small interfering RNA (siRNA)- and CRISPR-Cas9-based approaches (∼50% difference).

Beyond mitigating target-dependency escape, Cas13d collateral-effect-based therapeutics offer a key advantage in reducing therapy-mediated toxicity (Figure 1). Few cancer dependency genes are truly cancer-specific. Broadly overexpressed oncogenes without genetic alterations—such as cMYC, SOX2, and YAP—are also required for the survival of certain normal cells.9 For conventional targeting modalities, given equivalent delivery efficiency, target inhibition is expected to be uniform (CRISPR-Cas9) or to scale semi-linearly (RNAi and small molecules), resulting in disproportionately greater toxicity to normal cells and imposing a dosage ceiling below that required for effective tumor killing. Consequently, these approaches demand highly cancer-specific delivery, a formidable challenge in aggressive solid tumors.

Collateral-effect-based Cas13d therapy fundamentally reverses this scaling constraint. Because the magnitude of collateral RNA degradation correlates with on-target activity—and thus with target gene expression level—Cas13d therapeutics may preferentially exert stronger cytotoxic effects in cancer cells than in normal cells. This unique property substantially relaxes the requirement for cancer-specific delivery and shifts the emphasis toward achieving high overall tumor cell penetrance, a more tractable goal in solid tumors. As a result, Cas13d-based strategies hold significant promise for targeting broadly overexpressed oncogenes such as cMYC, SOX2, and YAP.

Despite this promise, key challenges remain. The extent of collateral RNA degradation is currently gene- and guide RNA (gRNA)-specific and must be empirically determined, necessitating substantial screening efforts. Improved computational models for predicting collateral activity would accelerate therapeutic development, both for identifying appropriate target gene and designing efficient gRNAs. Additionally, although Cas13d may reduce delivery specificity requirements, effective targeting of the majority of tumor cells in solid cancers remains an unmet challenge. Finally, evaluating Cas13d therapeutics in immunocompetent models will be critical for understanding their capacity to elicit antitumor immune responses, including bystander tumor clearance. This investigation will also provide critical knowledge that can guide the strategies to combine gene therapies and immune therapies, which would likely be required to eliminate solid tumors in patients.

Overall, leveraging the principle of “guilty by association,” Cas13d-collateral-effect-based therapeutics recently presented by the Qi group and others offer unique opportunities to target cancer-overexpressed dependency genes.
