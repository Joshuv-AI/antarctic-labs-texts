Chinese version of the nursing information literacy scale: translation, cultural adaptation, and validation
Authors: Fang Zou, Caini Song, Libo Yao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWith the rapid development of information technologies such as artificial intelligence, big data, and 5G driving nursing excellence, there is a need for a validated tool to assess the information literacy of nurses. Information literacy refers to an individual’s ability to access, evaluate, utilize, and create information.ObjectiveTo translate, culturally adapt, and assess the reliability and validity of the Nursing Information Literacy Scale (NILS).MethodsWe followed the Brislin translation guidelines for scale translation, which include four standardized steps: forward translation, synthesis and back-translation, expert review, and pilot testing. A total of 493 clinical nurses from Changsha, China, were recruited. The full sample of 493 nurses was used for item analysis. Using simple random sampling, the participants were divided into Sample 1 (n = 246) and Sample 2 (n = 247) for exploratory factor analysis and confirmatory factor analysis, respectively, to assess structural validity. Content validity was evaluated by an expert panel, including the item-content validity index (I-CVI) and the scale-content validity index (S-CVI/Ave). Internal consistency reliability was examined using the entire sample of 493 nurses. For test-retest reliability, 30 nurses were randomly selected from the total sample and retested after a two-week interval, assessed using the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC). Convergent validity was assessed against the Information Literacy Questionnaire (ILQ).ResultsThe scale consisted of 7 dimensions with 27 items. Exploratory factor analysis revealed a 7-factor structure, which collectively accounted for 71.979% of the total variance. The Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin measure was 0.900, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (χ² = 3527.479, P < 0.001). Confirmatory factor analysis supported the model with good fit indices: χ²/df = 0.933; CFI = 1.000; TLI = 1.007; RMSEA = 0.000; SRMR = 0.033. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the scale was 0.927, and the range of Cronbach’s alpha values for each dimension was 0.781–0.898; the retest reliability (ICC) was 0.852. The item-level content validity index (I-CVI) ranged from 0.83 to 1.00, and the scale-level content validity index (S-CVI/Ave) was 0.994. The convergent validity coefficient, calculated against the ILQ, was r = 0.772.ConclusionsThe Chinese version of the Nursing Information Literacy Scale demonstrates strong psychometric properties, including good reliability, content validity, construct validity, and convergent validity. Its clear structure, simple language, and appropriate length make it a valid and reliable tool for assessing the nursing information literacy of clinical nurses in China. It is recommended for use in both clinical practice and future research to identify training needs and evaluate the effectiveness of information literacy education programs.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-025-04060-2.Keywords: Nursing information literacy, Information literacy, Nurses, Reliability, Validity

Introduction

Information Literacy is the ability of an individual to identify, retrieve, evaluate, effectively use, organize, produce, and communicate information to solve practical problems or to create information [1]. Information literacy is particularly important in the healthcare system. It is an important factor in promoting the quality of healthcare services and facilitating patient health management, and it also helps healthcare professionals to quickly and accurately grasp the latest medical research, clinical guidelines, and disease prevention knowledge to improve their diagnosis and treatment [2].

The National Nursing Career Development Plan (2021–2025) emphasizes that nursing informatization should be vigorously promoted with the help of information technology and in conjunction with requirements such as the construction of smart hospitals and ‘Internet + healthcare’ [3]. Nursing information literacy, i.e., the ability to identify information needs, to discover and evaluate information, to adopt information rationally and ethically, and to use information to achieve optimal nursing practice goals [4]. Research has demonstrated that nursing information literacy can integrate and use information through critical thinking, stimulate innovation, optimize the clinical care process, ensure the accuracy and completeness of nursing documentation, and thus enhance service efficiency and management quality, and has been considered an essential skill for modern clinical nurses in healthcare reform [5–7].

However, research into nursing information literacy measurement in China is still in its early stages. The literature contains few studies on nurses’ information literacy levels, with most relying on assessment tools such as the Nurses’ Nursing Information Competence Scale [8] and the Clinical Nurses’ Information Literacy Self-Assessment Scale [9]. The former was created by Luo., et al. [8] and published in 2016. Based on a ‘knowledge, skills, and attitudes’ framework, it comprises 32 items across five dimensions: nursing information awareness, computer operation ability, computer software management ability, nursing information operation ability, and nursing information management ability. The latter was created in 2020 by Huan., et al. [9], and contains 35 items across three dimensions: information awareness, information competency, information security, and ethics. A comprehensive nursing information literacy scale should encompass the abilities to identify information needs, retrieve information efficiently, critically evaluate the credibility and relevance of information, apply it effectively to clinical decision-making and patient care, and understand the ethical and security implications of its use. While the aforementioned scales contribute to the field, they possess significant limitations for contemporary clinical application. Critically, both scales are notably lengthy (32 and 35 items), which can lead to respondent fatigue and reduce practicality in busy healthcare settings. Furthermore, their theoretical frameworks and item content appear to emphasize general computer skills and information management theory rather than the applied, critical integration of information into specific nursing workflows and evidence-based practice. This focus on theory over practical application, as noted in previous criticism [10], limits their effectiveness in accurately measuring the unique competencies required for nursing informatics. Consequently, there is a lack of efficient and precise instruments for evaluating nursing information literacy in China, which restricts the assessment and enhancement of nurses’ proficiency in this critical area. Therefore, identifying and introducing a more targeted, concise, and practically-oriented assessment tool is necessary.

Developed by Korean scholar M Jo [11], the Nursing Information Literacy Scale for Nurses measures nurses’ capacity to use the best information retrieved and screened to address patients’ real nursing issues. The scale has undergone numerous revisions and enhancements, and it is widely utilized in nursing education and clinical practice [11]. Nevertheless, there is currently no Chinese translation of the scale. Therefore, to address the lack of suitable assessment tools, this study aims to translate, culturally adapt, and validate the Korean version of the Nurse Information Literacy Scale (NILS). The specific objectives are: (1) To assess the reliability of the Chinese version of NILS through internal consistency and test-retest reliability; (2) To evaluate its validity, encompassing content validity, construct validity, and convergent-related validity; and (3) To examine its feasibility and practicality for use among clinical nurses. This endeavour aims to develop an effective measurement tool for accurately assessing the nursing information literacy levels of nurses across various healthcare institutions, thereby enabling the design of targeted training and enhancement programs to improve the quality of nursing services further.

Methods

Study design and participants

A cross-sectional study was conducted to evaluate the information literacy competence of clinical nurses at a tertiary hospital in Changsha, Hunan Province, China, between December 2023 and February 2024. Participants were recruited using a consecutive convenience sampling approach. The eligibility criteria were as follows: Inclusion criteria: (1) registered nurses with a valid practicing certificate and at least one year of clinical experience; (2) those who provided informed consent and volunteered to participate. Exclusion criteria: (1) nurses who were on extended leave (e.g., for further training or personal leave) during the data collection period; (2) non-permanent staff, including nursing interns, visiting advanced training students, and specialist trainees.

The sample size for this psychometric validation study was determined based on widely accepted heuristic rules [12]. For Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA), a sample size of 5 to 10 participants per scale item is recommended. The scale comprises 27 items, thus requiring a sample between 135 and 270. For Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA), a sample size exceeding 200 is considered adequate, a criterion met by our sample of 247 participants. A total of 493 clinical nurses were recruited, which comfortably meets both requirements.

Tools

General information questionnaire

This questionnaire was developed by our research team through reviewing the literature, discussing in the group, and pre-survey, including age, gender, marital status, education, years of working experience, department, title, position, mode of employment, whether or not he has taken a literature search class, whether or not he has experience in publishing articles, etc. See Table 1 in supplementary file.

Source scale-----Nursing Information Literacy Scale (NILS)

It was developed by Jo M et al. [11] in 2019 to measure the information literacy skills of nurses. Translated into Chinese by our research team, and used the Chinese version for the survey. See Table 2 in supplementary file. The scale consists of 7 dimensions and 27 items, which are the ability to assess problems (items 1–4), the ability to identify potential sources of information (items 5–9), the ability to retrieve information (items 10–13), the ability to assess information (items 14–18), the ability to acquire and manage information (items 19–22), the ability to use information appropriately items 23–25), and the ability to integrate information (items 26–27). A Likert 5-point scale was used, with a score of 1 to 5 in order from never to always, and a total score of 27 to 135, with higher scores indicating better nursing information literacy skills. The total Cronbach alpha coefficient of the scale was 0.93, and the Cronbach alpha coefficients of the seven dimensions were 0.76, 0.76, 0.71, 0.84, 0.80, 0.75, and 0.75. The scale has been applied in South Korea [13], where it demonstrated good reliability and validity, including a criterion validity coefficient of 0.60 against a relevant information literacy assessment tool, and the Cronbach’s α is 0.96, which is considered acceptable.

Convergent measure----Information Literacy Questionnaire

In this study, the Information Literacy Questionnaire (ILQ) was employed as the convergent measure to assess convergent validity. It was developed by Shu Y [14], in 2009 to assess individuals’ information literacy levels. The scale consists of 20 items across four dimensions: information awareness, information competence, information knowledge, and information ethics. Responses are rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (‘completely disagree’) to 5 (‘completely agree’), yielding a total score between 20 and 100, with higher scores indicating a greater level of information literacy. In its original development [14], the ILQ demonstrated strong psychometric properties, exhibiting excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.904) and split-half reliability (0.837). A high KMO value (0.895) indicated the data’s suitability for factor analysis. Furthermore, the ILQ has been widely used and validated in subsequent research involving comparable populations, such as healthcare professionals. For example, studies among nurses in tertiary hospitals have reported consistently high Cronbach’s α coefficients of 0.911 [15] and 0.971 [16], demonstrating its robust reliability in similar contexts. These established properties support the selection of the ILQ as an appropriate convergent measure for the present study.

Cross-cultural adaptation of the scale

Prior to initiation of the translation process, formal permission was obtained from the original developer of the scale [Yeongmi Ha]. The cross-cultural adaptation of the scale was performed using the classic Brislin translation model, which is widely recognized as a gold standard for ensuring conceptual, semantic, and content equivalence between the original and translated versions [17]. The process consisted of the following steps:

Forward Translation and Reconciliation: Two independent translators (a professor of English and a chief nurse engaged in nursing education) translated the original English scale into two Chinese versions (A1 and A2). A research team of four members then compared, synthesized, and discussed A1 and A2 to resolve discrepancies and form a consensus Chinese translated version A.

Forward Translation and Reconciliation: Two independent translators (a professor of English and a chief nurse engaged in nursing education) translated the original English scale into two Chinese versions (A1 and A2). A research team of four members then compared, synthesized, and discussed A1 and A2 to resolve discrepancies and form a consensus Chinese translated version A.

Back-Translation: Two other translators (a nursing master’s student and an English master’s student), blinded to the original scale, independently back-translated version A into English, producing two back-translated versions (B1 and B2). The research team compared B1 and B2 with the original scale to identify and correct any semantic errors or misunderstandings introduced during the forward translation, resulting in a finalized back-translated version B.

The research team compared B1 and B2 with the original scale to identify and correct any semantic errors or misunderstandings introduced during the forward translation, resulting in a finalized back-translated version B.

Expert Committee Review: An expert committee was formed, consisting of six experts (nursing associate professors, a chief physician, a chief nurse, and associate chief nurses). All members reviewed the original scale, Chinese version A, and back-translated version B. Discussions and modifications were made focusing on conceptual equivalence, semantic meaning, and cultural appropriateness for the Chinese context, resulting in the pre-final Chinese version of the scale (version C). This process ensured the relevance and clarity of the items’ wording.

Data collection

Participant Recruitment: Informed consent to participate was obtained from all of the participants in the study. A convenience sampling method was employed to recruit clinical nurses from a tertiary hospital in Changsha, Hunan Province, China. Permission to conduct the study was secured from the hospital administration prior to recruitment.

Data Collection Procedure: A web-based survey was administered using the Questionnaire Star platform between December 2023 and February 2024. Trained research nurses acting as investigators distributed the survey link (in the form of a QR code) to head nurses of various departments. The head nurses subsequently forwarded the link to departmental WeChat groups. The first page of the online survey contained a detailed participant information sheet, which outlined the study’s purpose, significance, procedures, and confidentiality assurances. Informed consent was obtained electronically from all participants before they could proceed to the questionnaire items.

Quality Control: To ensure data quality, several measures were implemented: (1) Each internet protocol address was restricted to a single submission to prevent duplicate items. (2) All questionnaire items were set as mandatory to minimize missing data. (3) Submitted questionnaires were manually screened for patterns indicative of careless responding (e.g., identical answers to all items, obvious logical errors). Out of 500 distributed surveys, 493 valid questionnaires were retained for analysis, resulting in a valid response rate of 98.6%.

Incentive: To acknowledge their time and contribution, participants who completed the survey received a small gift (approx. value 10 RMB). This incentive was not a prerequisite for participation.

Data management

Following data collection, the dataset was downloaded from the Questionnaire Star platform. Two research assistants independently performed double data entry into a customized database using EpiData software (version 3.1). The two datasets were then cross-checked for any discrepancies, which were resolved by referring back to the original electronic records. The final cleaned dataset was exported for statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 25.0) and Mplus (version 8.7). A two-tailed p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Item analysis

Item analysis was performed to evaluate the discrimination and homogeneity of each item.

Critical Ratio Method: Participants (N = 493) were ranked by their total scores. Independent samples t-tests were used to compare item scores between the top 27% (high-scoring group) and the bottom 27% (low-scoring group). Items with a critical ratio (CR value) achieving statistical significance (P < 0.05) and an absolute t-value ≥ 3.0 were considered to have good discriminatory power.

Item-Total Correlation: The Pearson correlation coefficient between each item and the total scale score (with the item itself excluded) was computed. Items with a correlation coefficient (r) below 0.4 or a non-significant correlation (P ≥ 0.05) were flagged for potential removal due to poor homogeneity.

Reliability analysis

Reliability was assessed to determine the scale’s internal consistency and temporal stability.

Internal Consistency: Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was calculated for the entire scale. Values greater than 0.80, between 0.60 and 0.80, and below 0.60 were interpreted as indicating excellent, good, and poor internal consistency, respectively.

Test-Retest Reliability: A random subset of participants (n = 30) was invited to complete the scale again after a two-week interval. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) based on a two-way mixed-effects model was calculated to assess temporal stability. An ICC value greater than 0.75 was considered indicative of good reliability.

Validity analysis

Content validity

Content validity was evaluated by a panel of six experts. Using a 4-point Likert scale (1 = not relevant, 4 = highly relevant), experts rated the relevance of each item. The item-content validity index (I-CVI) and the scale-content validity index (S-CVI/Ave) were computed. I-CVI ≥ 0.78 and S-CVI/Ave ≥ 0.90 were deemed acceptable [18].

Structural validity

The sample was randomly split into two halves for exploratory (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA).

EFA: Conducted on the first subsample (n = 246) using principal component analysis with Varimax rotation, to explore potential component structures. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure and Bartlett’s test of sphericity were used to assess sampling adequacy. Factor retention was guided by eigenvalues > 1, the scree plot, and parallel analysis. Items were retained if they exhibited factor loadings ≥ 0.40, communalities ≥ 0.40, and no salient cross-loadings (difference < 0.20 between primary and secondary loadings).

CFA: Performed on the second subsample (n = 247) using maximum likelihood estimation in Mplus. To validate the structure identified through exploratory factor analysis. Model fit was evaluated using the following indices: χ²/df ≤ 3, comparative fit index (CFI) ≥ 0.90, Tucker-Lewis index (TLI) ≥ 0.90, root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) < 0.08, and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR) < 0.08. Modification indices were reviewed only for theoretically justified modifications if the initial model fit was inadequate.

Convergent validity

Convergent validity was examined by calculating the Pearson correlation coefficient between the total scores of the translated scale and the Information Literacy Questionnaire (ILQ). A moderate to strong positive correlation (r > 0.40) was anticipated.

Item analysis was performed on the data from all 493 participants. The critical ratio (CR) values for all items ranged from 10.714 to 17.099, and the differences between the high and low-score groups were all statistically significant (P < 0.001). The Pearson correlation coefficients between each item and the total scale score (with the item excluded) ranged from 0.451 to 0.588 (P < 0.001). As all items met the pre-defined criteria for retention (CR ≥ 3.0 and correlation coefficient r ≥ 0.4), all items were retained for subsequent analysis.

Test-Retest Reliability: As recommended by Koo and Li (2016), the ICC was calculated using a single-rater/measurement, absolute-agreement definition [17]. According to the benchmarks proposed by Koo and Li, these values indicate good (ICC >0.75) to excellent (ICC >0.9) reliability over time.

Validity analysis

Content validity

Six experts evaluated the content validity of the scale. The Item-Content Validity Index (I-CVI) ranged from 0.830 to 1.000, and the Scale-Content Validity Index/Average (S-CVI/Ave) was 0.994. These indices exceed the recommended thresholds (I-CVI ≥ 0.78, S-CVI/Ave ≥ 0.90), confirming that the scale has excellent content validity.

Construct validity

Exploratory factor analysis (EFA)

EFA was conducted on a random subsample of 246 participants. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure was 0.900, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (χ² = 3527.479, P < 0.001), confirming the data’s suitability for factor analysis. Principal component analysis with Varimax rotation extracted seven main factors with eigenvalues greater than 1, which collectively accounted for 71.979% of the total variance. All items had factor loadings greater than 0.40 on their primary factor without salient cross-loadings. The seven factors were sequentially named as follows: (1) Ability to Assess Problems, (2) Ability to Identify Potential Sources of Information, (3) Ability to Retrieve Information, (4) Ability to Assess Information, (5) Ability to Acquire and Manage Information, (6) Ability to Use Information Rationally, and (7) Ability to Integrate Information. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA)

CFA was performed on an independent random subsample of 247 participants using the maximum likelihood estimation method. The model fit indices for the seven-factor structure were as follows: χ²/df = 0.933, Comparative Fit Index (CFI) = 1.000, Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI) = 1.007, Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) = 0.000, and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR) = 0.033. These results indicate an excellent fit of the model to the data, with all indices meeting or exceeding the recommended standards for good model fit.

It is noteworthy that the CFA model demonstrated excellent fit indices. While this may reflect the well-defined internal structure of the data, the potential for sample-specificity is acknowledged. The model’s stability requires future cross-validation with independent samples.

Convergent validity

Convergent validity was assessed by examining the Pearson correlation between the total score of the new Chinese version of the Nursing Information Literacy Scale and the total score of the established Information Literacy Questionnaire. A strong positive correlation was found (r = 0.772, P < 0.01), providing evidence for good convergent validity.

Discussion

This study successfully translated and validated the Chinese version of the Nursing Information Literacy Scale (NILS) following a rigorous cross-cultural adaptation process. The findings demonstrate that the Chinese NILS possesses strong psychometric properties, indicating its suitability for assessing information literacy among clinical nurses in China.

Reliability

The excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.927) and test-retest reliability (ICC = 0.852) of the Chinese NILS are comparable to, and in some cases superior to, those reported in validation studies of other information literacy scales. For instance, the original Korean version of the NILS reported a Cronbach’s α of 0.96 [13], while other tools like the Information Literacy Questionnaire (ILQ) reported α coefficients ranging from 0.904 to 0.971 in different populations [17, 18]. The high reliability observed in our study may be attributed to the clear conceptual structure of the scale and the precise wording of items following cultural adaptation, which enhanced respondents’ understanding and consistent responding.

Validity and scale content

The strong content validity (S-CVI/Ave = 0.994) confirms the relevance and comprehensiveness of the scale items for the target construct within the Chinese context. The EFA and CFA robustly supported the original seven-factor structure, which aligns with the multi-dimensional nature of information literacy. This factor solution is conceptually similar to frameworks proposed by other scales, such as the ILQ’s four dimensions (awareness, competence, knowledge, ethics) [14] and the Nurses’ Nursing Information Competence Scale’s five dimensions (awareness, operation ability, etc.) [8]. However, the NILS offers a more nuanced structure specifically tailored to nursing practice, encompassing critical aspects like information evaluation in clinical decision-making and ethical application in patient care, which are less emphasized in generic tools. This specificity makes the NILS particularly valuable for capturing the unique information competencies required in nursing.

The convergent validity was substantiated by a strong correlation with the ILQ (r = 0.772). This indicates that the NILS effectively measures the broader information literacy construct while also capturing nursing-specific nuances. The explained variance (71.979%) in EFA is satisfactory and higher than that reported in some similar scale validation studies (e.g., often around 60–65% [19]), suggesting that the NILS structure effectively captures the core aspects of nursing information literacy in our sample.

Limitations

Despite the promising results, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. The primary constraint stems from the use of a convenience sample recruited from a single tertiary hospital, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to nurses working in primary or secondary care settings, rural areas, or other geographical regions across China. Another limitation involves the reliance on self-reported measures, which are susceptible to social desirability bias; future studies could therefore benefit from integrating objective assessments or behavioral observations to complement self-report data. Furthermore, although the Information Literacy Questionnaire (ILQ) served as a reasonable criterion, the lack of a gold standard for nursing-specific information literacy remains a methodological constraint. To address these issues, future research should employ multi-center, stratified random sampling strategies to include nurses from diverse healthcare institutions and educational backgrounds. Additionally, large-scale validation studies are needed to compare the NILS with clinical performance indicators or other objective outcomes, thereby comprehensively evaluating its reliability, validity, and applicability across varied healthcare contexts.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the Chinese version of the Nursing Information Literacy Scale (NILS) demonstrates robust psychometric properties, including good reliability, content validity, construct validity, and convergent validity, establishing it as a reliable and valid instrument for measuring information literacy among clinical nurses in China. Developed through a rigorous translation and cultural adaptation process, its clear structure, concise language, and moderate length render it a practical tool for both clinical and academic settings. This scale can be valuably applied to identify training needs, evaluate the effectiveness of educational interventions, and investigate the impact of information literacy on nursing practice and patient outcomes. However, it is crucial to note that the scale’s validity has been established solely within the context of tertiary hospitals in China. Consequently, its generalizability to primary or secondary healthcare institutions, rural areas, or other geographical regions remains to be verified.
