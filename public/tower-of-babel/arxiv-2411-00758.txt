Inverse methods for freeform optical design
J. H. M. ten Thije Boonkkamp, 1 K. Mitra, 1 M. J. H. Anthonissen, 1 L. Kusch, 1 P. A. Braam, 1 and W. L. IJzerman 1,2

Abstract
We present a systematic derivation of three mathematical models of increasing complexity for optical design, based on Hamilton’s characteristic functions and conservation of luminous flux, and briefly explain the connection with the mathematical theory of optimal transport. We outline several iterative least-squares solvers for our models and demonstrate their performance for a few challenging problems.

Introduction

Nowadays, traditional incandescent light bulbs have largely been replaced by LED lamps, mainly because LEDs are very energy efficient and have long lifetimes. However, to create a desired light output with LEDs, optical systems are required. Therefore, in the illumination optics industry a lot of research is carried out on the design of optical systems for a myriad of applications.

The industry standard in optical design is (quasi-)Monte Carlo ray tracing. These are forward methods, which compute the target light distribution for a given source, typically LED, and a
given
optical system, tracing a large collection of rays from source to target. Convergence of ray tracing is slow, and requires billions of rays to accurately compute the target distribution; see
[
9
, Chapter 3]
. Moreover, to realize the
desired
target distribution, these methods have to be embedded in an iterative procedure, adjusting system parameters, which makes them inefficient.

A promising alternative are inverse optical design methods. These methods compute the shape/location of the optical surface(s) in one go, given a source distribution and a
desired
target distribution. The surfaces are referred to as freeform since they have no imposed symmetries. Mathematical models for inverse methods are based on the principles of geometrical optics and conservation of luminous flux. These models contain the following: a geometrical equation describing the shape/location of the optical surface(s), equations for the optical map, which expresses target coordinates as a function of source coordinates, and a conservation law of luminous flux. The geometrical equation has multiple solutions. Combining one of the equations for the optical map with the balance equation for luminous flux, we can derive a Jacobian equation for the optical map. This equation has to be supplemented with the unconventional transport boundary condition.

In mathematics, the abstract theory of optimal transport is the correct framework to formulate optical design problems. The subject of optimal transport theory is to compute a transport plan (mapping) that transforms a given density function into another one, minimizing the transportation cost and subject to a(n) (integral) conservation law; see
[
27
]
. One of the governing equations is precisely the geometrical equation, containing a so-called cost function in the right hand side. The transportation cost is then a cost functional (an integral of the cost function). The analogy with optical design is obvious, the two density functions correspond to the source and target distributions, the transport plan to the optical map, the cost functional can be associated with the optical path length and the conservation law applies to the luminous flux.

Based on the analogy with optimal transport theory, we can distinguish three mathematical models of increasing complexity. First, in the simplest model the geometrical equation contains a quadratic cost function, and the Jacobian equation is the standard Monge-Ampère (MA) equation, which is a second-order, nonlinear, elliptic partial differential equation (PDE);
[
13
]
. In the next model, the cost function is no longer quadratic and the Jacobian equation is a generalized Monge-Ampère (GMA) equation. Finally, the most complicated model does not allow a description in terms of a cost function anymore, instead a so-called generating function is required. The Jacobian equation is now a generated Jacobian (GJ) equation.

Inverse methods require the numerical solution of a nonlinear elliptic PDE of Monge-Ampère type, for which a variety of methods exist. We just mention a few publications and refer to
[
24
, Chapter 5]
for a detailed overview. The publication that inspired us to develop our numerical solvers is by
[
6
]
. In this paper the authors present a least-squares method to solve the Dirichlet problem for the standard MA equation. Prins adjusted their method to include the transport boundary condition; see
[
20
]
. In a series of papers Yadav and Romijn extended the method for the GMA equation, corresponding to a non-quadratic cost function; see
[
30
]
,
[
22
]
,
[
23
]
. Romijn further extended our least-squares methods to deal with the GJ equation; see
[
25
]
and
[
26
]
.
On the other hand, based on the work of
[
5
]
, geometrical approaches of constructing optical surfaces with paraboloids have been investigated by
[
16
]
and
[
18
]
for the GMA equation, and by
[
11
]
for the GJ equation. Finally, adhering to a PDE perspective, numerical algorithms for solving the standard MA equation with transport boundary condition have been investigated by
[
3
]
using finite differences,
[
14
]
using finite elements, and
[
10
]
through an iterative scheme, to name some examples.

For the cost function models we have developed a two-stage algorithm, i.e., we first compute the optical map and subsequently the shape/location of the optical surface(s). For both stages we apply a least-squares method. For the generating function model, the optical map and the optical surface(s) have to be computed
simultaneously, also in a least-squares fashion.

In this paper we present a systematic derivation of all models and outline numerical simulation methods.
The content is then the following. First, in Section
2
we introduce Hamilton’s characteristic functions needed to derive the geometrical equations. In Section
3
we give an outline of optimal transport theory, needed to select a uniquely defined solution of the geometrical equation. A generic conservation law for luminous flux is presented in Section
4
. Next, in Section
5
we apply the results of the previous sections to present a hierarchy of models based on three example systems. A concise description of our numerical methods is presented in Section
6
. To demonstrate the performance of our methods, we present a few challenging examples in Section
7
. Concluding remarks are given in Section
8
and the paper is concluded with a nomenclature list.

2
Hamilton’s characteristic functions

In this section we introduce Hamilton’s characteristic functions which are needed for the derivation of the geometrical equations in
Section

5
; see, e.g.,
[
17
, pp. 94 - 107]
,
[
4
, Section 4.1]
for a more detailed account. Our starting point is the eikonal equation given by

|\nabla\varphi|=n,

(1)

where
\varphi=\varphi(\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}})
is the phase of an electromagnetic wave and
n=n(\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}})
is the refractive index field, as functions of the (three-dimensional) position vector (or coordinates)
\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
of a typical point. A surface
\varphi(\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}})=\mathrm{Const}
is a wavefront. We denote three-dimensional vectors in bold font with an underbar (
\underline{\;\;}
), to distinguish them from two-dimensional vectors, which are only written in bold font. Equation (
1
) describes free propagation of light waves and no longer holds when a light wave is reflected or refracted at an optical surface. The eikonal equation (
1
) is a first-order nonlinear PDE, which has the characteristic strip equations

\frac{\mathrm{d}{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}}}{\mathrm{d}{s}}=\frac{1}{n}\nabla\varphi,\quad\frac{\mathrm{d}{\varphi}}{\mathrm{d}{s}}=n,\quad\frac{\mathrm{d}{}}{\mathrm{d}{s}}(\nabla\varphi)=\nabla n,

(2)

see, e.g.,
[
15
]
. The curves
\mathcal{C}:\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}=\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}(s)
, parameterized by the arc length
s
, are the characteristics of (
1
). The system of ordinary differential equations in (
2
) determines the location of the characteristics and the solution along the characteristics, from which the solution of (
1
) can be reconstructed. From the first equation in (
2
) we conclude that the characteristics are orthogonal trajectories to the wavefronts, i.e., characteristics are light rays; see, e.g.,
[
4
, p. 114]
.

Hamilton’s characteristic functions are related to the optical path length of a ray, which we therefore introduce next. We assume that
n(\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}})
is piecewise continuous, with jumps at lens surfaces, consequently, rays are continuous and consist of piecewise differentiable curve-segments; cf. (
2
).
In this setting, we define the optical path length (OPL) between the points
\mathrm{P}_{1}
(position vector
\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}=\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}_{1}
) and
\mathrm{P}_{2}
(position vector
\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}=\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}_{2})
, connected by a light ray along the curve
\mathcal{C}
, as

\displaystyle[\mathrm{P}_{1},\mathrm{P}_{2}]=d_{\rm opt}(\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}_{1},\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}_{2}):=\int_{\mathcal{C}}n(\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}(s))\,\mathrm{d}s.

(3)

A consequence of Fermat’s principle is that the optical path length
[\mathrm{P}_{1},\mathrm{P}_{2}]
is stationary with respect to variations of the curve
\mathcal{C}
; see, e.g.,
[
4
, p. 740]
.

Consider an optical system consisting of a source, located in a plane
z=z_{\mathrm{s}}
(source plane), emitting light which via a sequence of reflectors and/or lenses arrives at a plane
z=z_{\mathrm{t}}
(target plane).
The
z
-axis is referred to as the optical axis. In this paper, we use the subscripts
\mathrm{s}
and
\mathrm{t}
for variables/vectors to denote their value at source and target, respectively. Let
\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$v$}}}}=(v_{1}\,v_{2}\,v_{3})^{\mathrm{T}}=\mathrm{d}\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}/\mathrm{d}s
denote the unit tangent vector of a ray, thus
\nabla\varphi=n\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$v$}}}}
; cf. Eq. (
2
). Unit vectors are denoted with a hat (
\,\hat{}\,
).
Let
\mathrm{Q}_{\mathrm{s}}
(position vector
\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}=\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}_{\mathrm{s}}
) and
\mathrm{Q}_{\mathrm{t}}
(position vector
\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}=\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}_{\mathrm{t}}
) be two points on the source and target plane, respectively, then from (
3
) we obtain

\displaystyle[\mathrm{Q}_{\mathrm{s}},\mathrm{Q}_{\mathrm{t}}]=d_{\rm opt}(\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}_{\mathrm{s}},\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}_{\mathrm{t}})=\int_{\mathcal{C}}n\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$v$}}}}\bm{\cdot}\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$v$}}}}\,\mathrm{d}s=\int_{\mathcal{C}}\nabla\varphi\bm{\cdot}\mathrm{d}\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}=\varphi(\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}_{\mathrm{t}})-\varphi(\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}_{\mathrm{s}}).

(4)

Note that reversing the direction of integration yields the symmetry property of
d_{\rm opt}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle d_{\rm opt}(\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}_{\mathrm{t}},\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}_{\mathrm{s}})=\int_{-\mathcal{C}}(-n\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$v$}}}})\bm{\cdot}(-\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$v$}}}})\,\mathrm{d}s=\int_{-\mathcal{C}}-\nabla\varphi\bm{\cdot}\mathrm{d}\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}=-\varphi(\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}_{\mathrm{s}})+\varphi(\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}_{\mathrm{t}})=d_{\rm opt}(\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}_{\mathrm{s}},\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}_{\mathrm{t}}),

(5)

as one would expect from the definition in (
3
).
Observe that
d_{\rm opt}
is a metric since
d_{\rm opt}(\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}_{\mathrm{s}},\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}_{\mathrm{t}})\geq 0
, with equality only if
\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}_{\mathrm{s}}=\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}_{\mathrm{t}}
, and it satisfies the triangle inequality due to Fermat’s principle. The symmetry of
d_{\rm opt}
is stated in (
5
).

For ease of presentation, we use the shorthand notation
\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$v$}}}}_{\mathrm{s}}=\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$s$}}}}
and
\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$v$}}}}_{\mathrm{t}}=\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$t$}}}}
.
Straightforward differentiation of the expression for
d_{\rm opt}(\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}_{\mathrm{s}},\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}_{\mathrm{t}})
in (
4
) gives

\nabla_{\mathrm{s}}d_{\rm opt}(\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}_{\mathrm{s}},\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}_{\mathrm{t}})=-\nabla\varphi(\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}_{\mathrm{s}})=-n_{\mathrm{s}}\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$s$}}}},\quad\nabla_{\mathrm{t}}d_{\rm opt}(\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}_{\mathrm{s}},\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}_{\mathrm{t}})=\nabla\varphi(\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}_{\mathrm{t}})=n_{\mathrm{t}}\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$t$}}}},

(6)

where
\nabla_{\mathrm{s}}
denotes the gradient with respect to the source coordinates
\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}_{\mathrm{s}}
and likewise for
\nabla_{\mathrm{t}}
. From these relations we readily conclude that

|\nabla_{\mathrm{s}}d_{\rm opt}|=n_{\mathrm{s}},\quad|\nabla_{\mathrm{t}}d_{\rm opt}|=n_{\mathrm{t}},

i.e.,
d_{\rm opt}
satisfies the eikonal equation in both source and target coordinates.

A ray is completely determined by its position and direction coordinates.
Position coordinates
\bm{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
are defined as the projection on a plane
z=\mathrm{Const}
of the position vector
\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}
of a typical point on the ray. Position coordinates in the source and target planes are denoted by
\bm{q}_{\mathrm{s}}
and
\bm{q}_{\mathrm{t}}
, respectively. Thus
\mathrm{Q}_{\mathrm{s}}
, with position vector
\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}_{\mathrm{s}}=(\bm{q}_{\mathrm{s}},z_{\mathrm{s}})
, will denote the point at which a light ray is emitted from the source plane, and
\mathrm{Q}_{\mathrm{t}}
, with position vector
\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}_{\mathrm{t}}=(\bm{q}_{\mathrm{t}},z_{\mathrm{t}})
, as the point at which it arrives at the target plane. Next, selecting the first two components from the equation
n\tfrac{\mathrm{d}{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}}}{\mathrm{d}{s}}=n\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$v$}}}}=\nabla\varphi
, we introduce the momentum vector
\bm{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
as

\bm{p}:=n\frac{\mathrm{d}{\bm{q}}}{\mathrm{d}{s}}=n\begin{pmatrix}v_{1}\\
v_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\tfrac{\partial{\varphi}}{\partial{q_{1}}}\\[5.69046pt]
\tfrac{\partial{\varphi}}{\partial{q_{2}}}\end{pmatrix}=:\frac{\partial{\varphi}}{\partial{\bm{q}}}.

(7)

Thus, the momentum vector is the projection of
\nabla\varphi(\bm{x})
on a plane
z=\mathrm{Const}
and determines the direction of a light ray. Momentum vectors at source and target are denoted with
\bm{p}_{\mathrm{s}}
and
\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}}
, respectively. The variables
\bm{q}=\bm{q}(z)
and
\bm{p}=\bm{p}(z)
are referred to as phase-space coordinates and together constitute phase space.

In the remainder of this paper, we assume that
n(\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}})
is piecewise constant, with possibly discontinuities at optical interfaces, and consequently a light ray consists of piecewise straight line segments. Below, we define four characteristic functions which relate the optical path length to phase-space coordinates at source and target.

Point characteristic.
As a function of the position coordinates
\bm{q}_{\mathrm{s}}
and
\bm{q}_{\mathrm{t}}
, the point characteristic
V
is the optical path length of the ray connecting the points
\mathrm{Q}_{\mathrm{s}}
and
\mathrm{Q}_{\mathrm{t}}
, i.e.

V(\bm{q}_{\mathrm{s}},\bm{q}_{\mathrm{t}}):=d_{\rm opt}((\bm{q}_{\mathrm{s}},z_{\mathrm{s}}),(\bm{q}_{\mathrm{t}},z_{\mathrm{t}}))=[\mathrm{Q}_{\mathrm{s}},\mathrm{Q}_{\mathrm{t}}].

(8)

From (
4
) we conclude that
V(\bm{q}_{\mathrm{s}},\bm{q}_{\mathrm{t}})=\varphi(\bm{q}_{\mathrm{t}},z_{\mathrm{t}})-\varphi(\bm{q}_{\mathrm{s}},z_{\mathrm{s}})
. Combining this relation with the equations in (
6
), selecting the first two components in both equations, we find for the directional derivatives
\tfrac{\partial{V}}{\partial{\bm{q}_{\mathrm{s}}}}
and
\tfrac{\partial{V}}{\partial{\bm{q}_{\mathrm{t}}}}
the following expressions

\frac{\partial{V}}{\partial{\bm{q}_{\mathrm{s}}}}=-n_{\mathrm{s}}\begin{pmatrix}s_{1}\\
s_{2}\end{pmatrix}=-\bm{p}_{\mathrm{s}},\quad\frac{\partial{V}}{\partial{\bm{q}_{\mathrm{t}}}}=n_{\mathrm{t}}\begin{pmatrix}t_{1}\\
t_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}};

(9)

cf. Eq. (
7
). Typically, Eqs. (
8
) and (
9
) are used to derive a geometrical equation for optical systems described in terms of the position coordinates
\bm{q}_{\mathrm{s}}
and
\bm{q}_{\mathrm{t}}
. An example is the parallel-to-near-field reflector which we discuss in Section
5.3
.

Mixed characteristic of the first kind.
Let a light ray connect the points

\mathrm{Q}_{\mathrm{s}}(\bm{q}_{\mathrm{s}},z_{\mathrm{s}})
and
\mathrm{Q}_{\mathrm{t}}(\bm{q}_{\mathrm{t}},z_{\mathrm{t}})
with momentum
\bm{p}_{\mathrm{s}}
and
\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}}
, respectively, then the mixed characteristic of the first kind
W
is defined by

W(\bm{q}_{\mathrm{s}},\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}}):=V(\bm{q}_{\mathrm{s}},\bm{q}_{\mathrm{t}})-\bm{q}_{\mathrm{t}}\bm{\cdot}\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}}.

(10)

Straightforward differentiation gives
\partial W/\partial\bm{p}_{\mathrm{s}}=\bm{0}
and
\partial W/\partial\bm{q}_{\mathrm{t}}=\bm{0}
, using Eq. (
9

W=W(\bm{q}_{\mathrm{s}},\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}})
indeed. Moreover, we readily verify that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial W}{\partial\bm{q}_{\mathrm{s}}}=-\bm{p}_{\mathrm{s}},\quad\frac{\partial W}{\partial\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}}}=-\bm{q}_{\mathrm{t}}.

(11)

W
can be interpreted as a modification of
[\mathrm{Q}_{\mathrm{s}},\mathrm{Q}_{\mathrm{t}}]
, which we show as follows.
Assume that
z_{\mathrm{t}}>z_{\mathrm{s}}
and define the point
\mathrm{P}_{\mathrm{t}}
as the intersection of the ray segment arriving at
\mathrm{Q}_{\mathrm{t}}
, parameterized by
\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}(\lambda)=\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}_{\mathrm{t}}+\lambda\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$t$}}}}
, and the plane passing through the origin
\mathrm{O}_{\mathrm{t}}(\bm{0},z_{\mathrm{t}})
of the target plane (position vector
\bm{\underline{\hbox{$o$}}}_{\mathrm{t}}
) perpendicular to this ray, given by the normal equation
(\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}-\bm{\underline{\hbox{$o$}}}_{\mathrm{t}})\bm{\cdot}\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$t$}}}}=0
; see Figure
1

The intersection point is given by
\lambda=\lambda(\mathrm{P}_{\mathrm{t}})=-(\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}_{\mathrm{t}}-\bm{\underline{\hbox{$o$}}}_{\mathrm{t}})\bm{\cdot}\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$t$}}}}
. Since the third component of
\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}_{\mathrm{t}}-\bm{\underline{\hbox{$o$}}}_{\mathrm{t}}
vanishes, this can be rewritten as
\lambda(\mathrm{P}_{\mathrm{t}})=-\bm{q}_{\mathrm{t}}\bm{\cdot}\begin{pmatrix}t_{1}\\
t_{2}\end{pmatrix}=-\bm{q}_{\mathrm{t}}\bm{\cdot}\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}}/n_{\mathrm{t}}
; cf. Eq. (
7
) or Eq. (
9

\mathrm{P}_{\mathrm{t}}
we have

with

). From this we conclude that
\lambda(\mathrm{P}_{\mathrm{t}})>0
if

, i.e.,
\mathrm{P}_{\mathrm{t}}
is located behind the target plane as seen from the source, and
\lambda(\mathrm{P}_{\mathrm{t}})<0
otherwise. Obviously, the distance

, implying that

Mixed characteristic of the second kind.
Under the same setting, the mixed characteristic of the second kind

is defined as

Angular characteristic.
The angular characteristic
T
is another modification of
[\mathrm{Q}_{\mathrm{s}},\mathrm{Q}_{\mathrm{t}}]
and is a function of the momenta
\bm{p}_{\mathrm{s}}
and
\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}}
. Its definition and interpretation (for
z_{\mathrm{t}}>z_{\mathrm{s}}
) are given by

\begin{split}T(\bm{p}_{\mathrm{s}},\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}})&:=V(\bm{q}_{\mathrm{s}},\bm{q}_{\mathrm{t}})+\bm{q}_{\mathrm{s}}\bm{\cdot}\bm{p}_{\mathrm{s}}-\bm{q}_{\mathrm{t}}\bm{\cdot}\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}}\\
&=[\mathrm{Q}_{\mathrm{s}},\mathrm{Q}_{\mathrm{t}}]+\sigma_{\mathrm{s}}[\mathrm{Q}_{\mathrm{s}},\mathrm{P}_{\mathrm{s}}]-\sigma_{\mathrm{t}}[\mathrm{Q}_{\mathrm{t}},\mathrm{P}_{\mathrm{t}}].\end{split}

(13)

From Eq. (
9
) and Eq. (
13
) we readily verify that
\partial T/\partial\bm{q}_{\mathrm{s}}=\bm{0}
,
\partial T/\bm{q}_{\mathrm{t}}=\bm{0}
, implying that
T=T(\bm{p}_{\mathrm{s}},\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}})
indeed. Moreover, it is evident that

\frac{\partial T}{\partial\bm{p}_{\mathrm{s}}}=\bm{q}_{\mathrm{s}},\quad\frac{\partial T}{\partial\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}}}=-\bm{q}_{\mathrm{t}}.

Note that
T(-\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}},-\bm{p}_{\mathrm{s}})=T(\bm{p}_{\mathrm{s}},\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}})
, i.e., the angular characteristic is invariant if the direction of the ray is reversed. This function is used to describe optical systems in terms of
\bm{p}_{\mathrm{s}}
and
\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}}
, such as the point-to-far-field lens; see Section
5.2
.

3
Geometrical description of optical systems: an optimal transport point of view

To derive a geometrical description of an optical system, i.e., an algebraic relation defining the location and shape of the optical surface(s), we have to evaluate one of the four Hamilton’s characteristic functions. However, this geometrical equation does not have a unique solution. In this section we discuss how to select a uniquely defined solution from this equation. Subsequently, we derive an equation for the so-called optical map, which relates source and target coordinates of a ray.

First we introduce some notation. We denote with
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{T}
the (physical) source and target domain, respectively. Source and target domain are parameterized by
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, respectively, i.e.,
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
are the parameter domains for source and target. We assume that
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
are compact, i.e., bounded and closed, and consequently any continuous function defined on one of these domains assumes a minimum and a maximum. Key in the following discussion is the optical map
\bm{m}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
, defining how a point on the source domain (specified by
\bm{x}
) is connected via a light ray with a point on the target domain (specified by
\bm{y}=\bm{m}(\bm{x})
). Given source and target domain, the optical map has to satisfy the condition
\mathcal{Y}=\bm{m}(\mathcal{X})
, i.e.,
\mathcal{Y}
is the image of
\mathcal{X}
under the mapping
\bm{m}
.

We consider optical systems having one or two freeform optical surfaces, i.e., surfaces without any symmetry.
Consider an arbitrary ray connecting source and target, for which
\bm{y}=\bm{m}(\bm{x})
.
Evaluation of the appropriate Hamilton’s characteristic function for such a ray leads to either one of the two algebraic equations

\displaystyle u_{1}(\bm{x})+u_{2}(\bm{y})

\displaystyle=c(\bm{x},\bm{y}),

(14a)

\displaystyle u_{2}(\bm{y})

\displaystyle=H(\bm{x},\bm{y},u_{1}(\bm{x})),

(14b)

which we refer to as geometrical equations,
where
u_{1}(\bm{x})
defines the location of (one of) the optical surface(s) and
u_{2}(\bm{y})
is either an auxiliary variable or defines the location of the other surface. We like to emphasize that in Eqs. (
14
) source and target coordinates are related via
\bm{y}=\bm{m}(\bm{x})
.
In Section
5
we will derive geometrical equations for several optical systems.
Note that in Eq. (
14a
) source and target coordinates are separated in the left-hand side. This equation occurs in the theory of optimal transport, where
u_{1}(\bm{x})
and
u_{2}(\bm{y})
are referred to as Kantorovich potentials and
c(\bm{x},\bm{y})
as the cost function; see, e.g.,
[
27
]
for a rigorous mathematical account. In Eq. (
14b
) source and target coordinates are no longer separated. It will turn out that for fixed
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
, the function
H(\bm{x},\bm{y},\cdot)
has a unique inverse
H^{-1}(\bm{x},\bm{y},\cdot)=G(\bm{x},\bm{y},\cdot)
, referred to as the generating function; see
[
12
]
. Thus the following holds

\forall\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}\,\forall\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}\quad\big(u_{2}(\bm{y})=H(\bm{x},\bm{y},u_{1}(\bm{x}))\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad u_{1}(\bm{x})=G(\bm{x},\bm{y},u_{2}(\bm{y}))\big).

(15)

Obviously, Eq. (
14a
) is a special case of Eq. (
14b
) if we choose
H(\bm{x},\bm{y},w)=c(\bm{x},\bm{y})-w
. Nevertheless, for the sake of completeness we discuss both cases separately.

c
-convex analysis.

Equation (
14a
) has multiple solution pairs
(u_{1}(\bm{x}),u_{2}(\bm{y}))
. From the theory of optimal transport we know that a possible solution of Eq. (
14a
) is given by

\displaystyle\forall\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}\quad u_{1}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\max_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\big(c(\bm{x},\bm{y})-u_{2}(\bm{y})\big),

(16a)

\displaystyle\forall\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}\quad u_{2}(\bm{y})

\displaystyle=\max_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\big(c(\bm{x},\bm{y})-u_{1}(\bm{x})\big),

(16b)

which implicitly defines the optical map as alternative to a geometrical optics derivation. To be more precise,
for any
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
its image
\bm{y}^{\ast}=\bm{m}(\bm{x})\in\mathcal{Y}
is the maximizer in (
16a
). Also, for any
\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}
we have that
\bm{x}^{\ast}\in\mathcal{X}
such that
\bm{y}=\bm{m}(\bm{x}^{\ast})
is the maximizer in (
16b
), which is possible since
\mathcal{Y}=\bm{m}(\mathcal{X})
. Under certain conditions, to be specified shortly, the maxima in (
16
) are unique, which we henceforth assume.
The solution in (
16
) is referred to as a
c
-convex pair; see
[
28
]
. The proof of the equivalence of (
16a
) and (
16b
) proceeds as follows.
Suppose, Eq. (
14a
) and the expression for
u_{1}(\bm{x})
in Eq. (
16a
) hold, then we have to derive the expression for
u_{2}(\bm{y})
in Eq. (
16b
). From (
16a
) we conclude

\forall\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}\,\forall\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}\quad u_{1}(\bm{x})\geq c(\bm{x},\bm{y})-u_{2}(\bm{y}),

or equivalently, swapping
u_{1}(\bm{x})
and
u_{2}(\bm{y})
,

\forall\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}\,\forall\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}\quad u_{2}(\bm{y})\geq c(\bm{x},\bm{y})-u_{1}(\bm{x}).

Since the latter inequality holds for all
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, we can take the maximum over all
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
to obtain

\forall\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}\quad u_{2}(\bm{y})\geq\max_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\big(c(\bm{x},\bm{y})-u_{1}(\bm{x})\big).

Recall that
\mathcal{Y}=\bm{m}(\mathcal{X})
. Let
\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}
, then
\bm{y}=\bm{m}(\tilde{\bm{x}})
for some
\tilde{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}
. From (
14a
) we conclude that

u_{2}(\bm{y})=c(\tilde{\bm{x}},\bm{y})-u_{1}(\tilde{\bm{x}})\leq\max_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\big(c(\bm{x},\bm{y})-u_{1}(\bm{x})\big).

Combining both inequalities we obtain the expression for
u_{2}(\bm{y})
in Eq. (
16b
) and conclude that
\tilde{\bm{x}}=\bm{x}^{\ast}
is the (unique) maximizer of
c(\bm{x},\bm{y})-u_{1}(\bm{x})
. Thus, the expression for
u_{1}(\bm{x})
in Eq. (
16a
) combined with Eq. (
14a
) implies the expression for
u_{2}(\bm{y})
in Eq. (
16b
). Conversely, given the expression for
u_{2}(\bm{y})
in Eq. (
16b
) and Eq. (
14a
) we can derive in a similar manner the expression for
u_{1}(\bm{x})
defined in Eq. (
16a
).

Alternatively, Eq. (
14a
) has a
c
-concave solution pair, for which the maxima in Eq. (
16
) are replaced by minima, defining another mapping
\bm{m}
. In either case, a necessary condition is that
(\bm{x},\bm{m}(\bm{x}))
is a stationary point of
c(\bm{x},\bm{y})-u_{1}(\bm{x})
for all
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, i.e.,

\nabla_{\bm{x}}c(\bm{x},\bm{m}(\bm{x}))-\nabla u_{1}(\bm{x})=\bm{0},

(17)

where
\nabla_{\bm{x}}c
is the gradient of
c
with respect to the source coordinates
\bm{x}
. A sufficient condition for the
c
-convex pair (max/max solution) in (
16
) is that
\mathrm{D}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}c(\bm{x},\bm{m}(\bm{x}))-\mathrm{D}^{2}u_{1}(\bm{x})
be symmetric negative definite (SND) for all
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, where
\mathrm{D}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}c
and
\mathrm{D}^{2}u_{1}
are the Hessian matrices of
c
(with respect to
\bm{x}
) and
u_{1}
, respectively. The function
c(\cdot,\bm{y})-u_{1}
is then concave, guaranteeing a unique maximum in Eq. (
16b
). Alternatively, for a
c
-concave pair (min/min solution) the sufficient condition is that
\mathrm{D}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}c(\bm{x},\bm{m}(\bm{x}))-\mathrm{D}^{2}u_{1}(\bm{x})
be symmetric positive definite (SPD) for all
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
. Then
c(\cdot,\bm{y})-u_{1}
is convex and has a unique minimum.

According to the implicit function theorem, the optical map
\bm{y}=\bm{m}(\bm{x})
is well defined by Eq. (
17
), provided the mixed derivative matrix
\mathrm{D}_{\bm{x}\bm{y}}c=\big(\tfrac{\partial^{2}c}{\partial x_{i}\partial y_{j}}\big)
is regular in
(\bm{x},\bm{m}(\bm{x}))
for all
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, the so-called twist condition.
However, for many optical systems the actual computation of
\bm{y}=\bm{m}(\bm{x})
from (
17
) is quite complicated, if not impossible. Therefore, to derive an equation for the optical map, we differentiate the zero-gradient condition in Eq. (
17
) to obtain the equation

\bm{C}(\bm{x},\bm{m}(\bm{x}))\mathrm{D}\bm{m}=\bm{P}(\bm{x},\bm{m}(\bm{x})),\quad\bm{C}(\bm{x},\bm{y})=\mathrm{D}_{\bm{x}\bm{y}}c(\bm{x},\bm{y}),\quad\bm{P}(\bm{x},\bm{y})=\mathrm{D}^{2}u_{1}(\bm{x})-\mathrm{D}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}c(\bm{x},\bm{y}),

(18)

where
\mathrm{D}\bm{m}=\big(\tfrac{\partial m_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}\big)
is the Jacobi matrix of the optical map.
We refer to Eq. (
18
) as the matrix-Jacobi equation. Note that the matrix
\bm{P}
is either SPD, for a
c
-convex, or SND for a
c
-concave solution pair.

Equation (
18
) has to be supplemented with the so-called transport boundary condition

\bm{m}(\partial\mathcal{X})=\partial\mathcal{Y},

(19)

stating that the boundary of the source domain
\partial\mathcal{X}
is mapped to the boundary of the target domain
\partial\mathcal{Y}
. This condition is a consequence of the edge-ray principle of geometrical optics by
[
21
]
, and guarantees that all light emitted from the source arrives at the target.

For some basic optical systems
c(\bm{x},\bm{y})=\bm{x}\bm{\cdot}\bm{y}
, implying that
\bm{C}=\bm{I}
and
\mathrm{D}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}c=\bm{O}
; see Section
5.1
. The
c
-convex solution pair in (
16a
) reduces to a conjugate pair of Legendre-Fenchel transforms, see e.g.,
[
24
]
, for which the necessary condition (
17
) reduces to
\bm{m}(\bm{x})-\nabla u_{1}(\bm{x})=\bm{0}
. The sufficient condition for the
c
-convex solution pair is that
\mathrm{D}^{2}u_{1}(\bm{x})
is SPD for all
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, i.e.,
u_{1}(\bm{x})
is a convex function. Likewise, for the
c
-concave solution pair
\mathrm{D}^{2}u_{1}(\bm{x})
has to be SND for all
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, and
u_{1}(\bm{x})
is then concave. In both cases
c(\cdot,\bm{y})-u_{1}
has a unique extremum.

In our numerical algorithm, the matrix-Jacobi equation (
18
), subject to the transport boundary condition (
19
), is employed to compute the optical map, and subsequently
u_{1}(\bm{x})
is reconstructed from Eq. (
17
). If needed,
u_{2}(\bm{y})
is computed from (
14a
). From
u_{1}(\bm{x})
and possibly
u_{2}(\bm{y})
the shape of the optical surface(s) is computed. A concise account of the numerical algorithm is given in Section
6
.

H
-convex analysis.

Next, we consider the geometrical equation (
14b
), which can always be formulated such that

H_{w}(\bm{x},\bm{y},\cdot)>0
for all
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
and
\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}
, implying that
H(\bm{x},\bm{y},\cdot)
has an inverse. Also Eq. (
14b
) has multiple solution pairs
(u_{1}(\bm{x}),u_{2}(\bm{y}))
. In analogy with (
16
) a possible solution reads

\displaystyle\forall\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}\quad u_{1}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\max_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}G(\bm{x},\bm{y},u_{2}(\bm{y})),

(20a)

\displaystyle\forall\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}\quad u_{2}(\bm{y})

\displaystyle=\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}H(\bm{x},\bm{y},u_{1}(\bm{x})),

(20b)

implicitly defining the optical map as follows:
\bm{y}^{\ast}=\bm{m}(\bm{x})\in\mathcal{Y}
is the maximizer in (
20a
) and
\bm{x}^{\ast}\in\mathcal{X}
such that
\bm{y}=\bm{m}(\bm{x}^{\ast})
is the minimizer in (
20b
). Provided a condition on
H
holds, which we specify shortly, the extrema in (
20
) are unique, which we henceforth assume.
The functions
u_{1}(\bm{x})
and
u_{2}(\bm{y})
are referred to as a
G
-convex
H
-concave solution pair; see
[
24
]
. We prove that (
20a
) together with (
14b
) implies (
20b
); the implication in the reverse order proceeds in a similar way. Assume that the expression for
u_{1}(\bm{x})
in (
20a
) holds, then

\forall\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}\,\forall\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}\quad u_{1}(\bm{x})\geq G(\bm{x},\bm{y},u_{2}(\bm{y})).

Applying the inverse
H(\bm{x},\bm{y},\cdot)=G^{-1}(\bm{x},\bm{y},\cdot)
and using that
H_{w}(\bm{x},\bm{y},\cdot)>0
, we obtain

\forall\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}\,\forall\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}\quad H(\bm{x},\bm{y},u_{1}(\bm{x}))\geq u_{2}(\bm{y}).

Since this inequality holds for all
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, we can take the minimum and find

\forall\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}\quad u_{2}(\bm{y})\leq\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}H(\bm{x},\bm{y},u_{1}(\bm{x})).

Let
\bm{y}=\bm{m}(\tilde{\bm{x}})\in\mathcal{Y}
for some
\tilde{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}
.
Then, by virtue of Eq. (
14b
)

\quad u_{2}(\bm{y})=H(\tilde{\bm{x}},\bm{y},u_{1}(\tilde{\bm{x}}))\geq\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}H(\bm{x},\bm{y},u_{1}(\bm{x})).

Combining both inequalities we arrive at the expression for
u_{2}(\bm{y})
in Eq. (
20b
) with
\tilde{\bm{x}}=\bm{x}^{\ast}
the unique minimizer. Conversely, given the expression for
u_{2}(\bm{y})
in (
20b
), we can derive in a similar manner the expression for
u_{1}(\bm{x})
in (
20a
).

Eq. (
14b
) also has a
G
-concave
H
-convex (min/max) solution pair. The necessary condition for both solutions is that
(\bm{x},\bm{m}(\bm{x}))
is a stationary point of
\widetilde{H}(\bm{x},\bm{y})=H(\bm{x},\bm{y},u_{1}(\bm{x}))
, i.e.,

\nabla_{\bm{x}}\widetilde{H}(\bm{x},\bm{m}(\bm{x}))=\nabla_{\bm{x}}H(\bm{x},\bm{m}(\bm{x}),u_{1}(\bm{x}))+H_{w}(\bm{x},\bm{m}(\bm{x}),u_{1}(\bm{x}))\nabla u_{1}(\bm{x})=\bm{0}.

(21)

A sufficient condition for the max/min pair is that
\mathrm{D}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}\widetilde{H}(\bm{x},\bm{m}(\bm{x}))
be SPD for all
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
. Alternatively, for the min/max pair, the Hessian matrix
\mathrm{D}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}\widetilde{H}(\bm{x},\bm{m}(\bm{x}))
should be SND for all
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
. In both cases the solution pair is unique.

Equation (
21
) implicitly defines the optical map
\bm{y}=\bm{m}(\bm{x})
provided the mixed derivative matrix
\mathrm{D}_{\bm{x}\bm{y}}\widetilde{H}(\bm{x},\bm{y})
is regular in
(\bm{x},\bm{m}(\bm{x}))
for all
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, but, the actual computation of the optical map from (
21
) is virtually impossible for most optical systems. So, we differentiate the zero-gradient condition (
21
) and recover the matrix-Jacobi equation in Eq. (
18
), however, with matrices
\bm{C}
and
\bm{P}
given by

\bm{C}(\bm{x},\bm{y},u_{1}(\bm{x}))=\mathrm{D}_{\bm{x}\bm{y}}\widetilde{H}(\bm{x},\bm{y}),\quad\bm{P}(\bm{x},\bm{y},u_{1}(\bm{x}))=-\mathrm{D}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}\widetilde{H}(\bm{x},\bm{y}).

(22)

Notice, there is one difficulty, i.e., the function
\widetilde{H}(\bm{x},\bm{y})
depends on
u_{1}(\bm{x})
, and consequently, also the matrices
\bm{C}
and
\bm{P}
do. Therefore, the computation of the optical map and the function
u_{1}(\bm{x})
can no longer be decoupled; see Section
6
for more details.
Finally, also in this case the transport boundary condition (
19
) applies.

4
Conservation of luminous flux

In the previous section we presented equations describing the geometry of an optical system, more specifically, Eqs. (
14
) for the shape/location of the optical surface(s), the zero-gradient conditions (
17
) and (
21
), and the matrix-Jacobi equation (
18
) for the optical map. To close the model, we have to formulate the conservation law of luminous flux.

We first introduce stereographic projections of a unit vector
\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$v$}}}}\in\mathrm{S}^{2}
, needed in some of the flux balances. A unit vector
\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$v$}}}}
can be represented by a point
\mathrm{P}
or
\mathrm{Q}
on the unit sphere; see Figure
2
. There are two stereographic projections of
\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$v$}}}}
, i.e., the projection from the north pole and from the south pole. To compute the stereographic projection from the north pole
\mathrm{N}
, we have to compute the intersection of the line through
\mathrm{N}
and
\mathrm{P}
, given by

\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}(\lambda)=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
1\end{pmatrix}+\lambda\begin{pmatrix}v_{1}\\
v_{2}\\
v_{3}-1\end{pmatrix},

with the equator plane
z=0
. This way we obtain the stereographic projection
\bm{z}=\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{N}}(\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$v$}}}})
given by

\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{N}}(\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$v$}}}})=\frac{1}{1-v_{3}}\begin{pmatrix}v_{1}\\
v_{2}\end{pmatrix},

(23a)

which is defined for
v_{3}\neq 1
. The latter condition means that
\mathrm{P}
and
\mathrm{N}
should not coincide.
Using the relations
|\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$v$}}}}|^{2}=1
and
v_{3}\neq 1
, we can compute the inverse

\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$v$}}}}=\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{N}}^{-1}(\bm{z})
, and find

\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{N}}^{-1}(\bm{z})=\frac{1}{1+|\bm{z}|^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}2z_{1}\\
2z_{2}\\
-1+|\bm{z}|^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

(23b)

In a similar manner we can determine the stereographic projection from the south pole and its inverse. These are given by

\bm{z}=\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{S}}(\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$v$}}}})=\frac{1}{1+v_{3}}\begin{pmatrix}v_{1}\\
v_{2}\end{pmatrix},\quad\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$v$}}}}=\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{S}}^{-1}(\bm{z})=\frac{1}{1+|\bm{z}|^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}2z_{1}\\
2z_{2}\\
1-|\bm{z}|^{2}\end{pmatrix},

(24)

and are defined for
v_{3}\neq-1
. Both projections are parameterizations of
\mathrm{S}^{2}
, and in either case an area element
\mathrm{d}S(\bm{z})
on
S^{2}
generated by
\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$v$}}}}
is given by

\mathrm{d}S(\bm{z})=\Big|\frac{\partial{\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$v$}}}}}}{\partial{z_{1}}}\bm{\times}\frac{\partial{\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$v$}}}}}}{\partial{z_{2}}}\Big|\,\mathrm{d}\bm{z}=\frac{4}{\big(1+|\bm{z}|^{2}\big)^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{z}=:J(\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$v$}}}}(\bm{z}))\,\mathrm{d}\bm{z}.

(25)

The choice for the stereographic projection depends on the direction of
\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$v$}}}}
. If
v_{3}>0
, a suitable choice is the projection from the south pole, since then the domain for
\bm{z}
is included in the interior of the disc
|\bm{z}|\leq 1
. The projection from the north pole gives a domain outside this disc; see Figure
2
. Likewise, if
v_{3}<0
, the projection from the north pole is the proper choice.

\mathrm{N}
.

\mathrm{S}

Assuming there is no energy lost, the most generic form of the luminous flux balance reads

\int_{\mathcal{A}}f(\bm{x})\,\mathrm{d}S(\bm{x})=\int_{\bm{m}(\mathcal{A})}g(\bm{y})\,\mathrm{d}S(\bm{y}),

(26)

for every subset
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{X}
and image set
\bm{m}(\mathcal{A})\subset\mathcal{Y}
, where
f(\bm{x})
and
g(\bm{y})
are the flux densities, to be specified shortly, at the source and target, respectively. Here,
\mathrm{d}S(\bm{x})
denotes an area element on the (curved) surface
\mathcal{S}
describing the source, and parameterized by
\bm{x}
. Analogously,
\mathrm{d}S(\bm{y})
is an area element on the target surface
\mathcal{T}
, parameterized by
\bm{y}
. Equation (
26
) should hold for any subsets
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{X}
and
\bm{m}(\mathcal{A})\subset\mathcal{Y}
, so also for
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{X}
and
\bm{m}(\mathcal{A})=\mathcal{Y}
, giving the global luminous flux balance. This implies that an inverse design problem can only have a solution if
f
and
g
satisfy this global flux balance.

The precise form of the flux balance depends on the source and target. For the source we consider two options, first, a planar source located in
z=0
and emitting a parallel beam of light with
\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$s$}}}}=\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$e$}}}}_{z}
, and second, a point source located in the origin and emitting a conical beam of light with
\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$s$}}}}=\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$e$}}}}_{r}
. Both are zero-étendue (English: zero-extent) sources, meaning that the emitted beam has either spatial or angular extent, but in the embedding four-dimensional phase space it has zero volume; see
[
7
]
. For the planar source
\mathcal{S}=\{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}(\bm{x})=(\bm{x},0)|\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}\}
,
\bm{x}=\bm{q}_{\mathrm{s}}
are spatial coordinates (Cartesian/polar),
\mathrm{d}S(\bm{x})=\mathrm{d}\bm{x}
, the area element in the source plane, and
f(\bm{x})=M(\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}(\bm{x}))
, the emittance, i.e., the luminous flux per unit area emitted by the source. The point source has no spatial extent, but emits light rays in an angular domain, specified by
\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$s$}}}}\in\mathcal{S}\subset\mathrm{S}^{2}
. As parameterization we choose a stereographic projection, either from the north or south pole, the area element
\mathrm{d}S(\bm{x})=J(\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$s$}}}}(\bm{x}))\mathrm{d}\bm{x}
, and
f(\bm{x})=I_{\mathrm{s}}(\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$s$}}}}(\bm{x}))
, the intensity of the source, i.e., the emitted luminous flux per unit solid angle. We can express both flux densities in the form
f=\tilde{f}\circ\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}
, where
\tilde{f}=M
and
\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{s}}:(\bm{x},0)\mapsto\bm{x}
, the trivial projection from
\mathcal{S}
to
\mathcal{X}
, for the planar source and
\tilde{f}=I_{\mathrm{s}}
and
\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{s}}=\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{N}}
or
\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{s}}=\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{S}}
for the point source.

For the target we consider the following cases, first a near-field target located on a curved surface

\mathcal{T}=\{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}(\bm{y})=(\bm{y},v(\bm{y}))|\,\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}\}
, and second, a far-field target.
For the near-field target
\bm{y}=\bm{q}_{\mathrm{t}}
are spatial coordinates in a reference plane
z=z_{\mathrm{t}}
, the area element

\mathrm{d}S(\bm{y})=\Big|\frac{\partial{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}}}{\partial{y_{1}}}\bm{\times}\frac{\partial{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}}}{\partial{y_{2}}}\Big|\mathrm{d}\bm{y}=\sqrt{|\nabla v(\bm{y})|^{2}+1}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{y},

and
g(\bm{y})=E(\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}(\bm{y}))
, the illuminance, i.e, the luminous flux per unit area incident on the target surface. The domain of a far-field target is an angular domain, determined by the transmitted rays with
\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$t$}}}}\in\mathcal{T}\subset\mathrm{S}^{2}
. Therefore we choose for
\bm{y}
one of the two stereographic projections for which

\mathrm{d}S(\bm{y})=J(\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$t$}}}}(\bm{y}))\mathrm{d}\bm{y}
. However,
g(\bm{y})=I_{\mathrm{t}}(\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}}(\bm{y}))
, the intensity in the target domain as a function of
\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}}
, the unit direction vector directed straight from source to target domain, skipping all optical surfaces, rather than a function of
\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$t$}}}}
. In the far-field approximation the distance from source to target is large compared to the size of the optical system, specifically
|\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}-d\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$t$}}}}|/|\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}|\ll 1
, where
d
is the distance from optical surface to target, measured along a reflected ray; see, e.g.,
[
24
, pp. 59 - 60]
. The optical system is considered a point contracted at the origin so we simply take
g(\bm{y})=I_{\mathrm{t}}(\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$t$}}}}(\bm{y}))
. Also for the target both flux densities can be unified in the expression
g=\tilde{g}\circ\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{t}}^{-1}
, where for the near field
\tilde{g}=E
and
\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{t}}:(\bm{y},v(\bm{y}))\mapsto\bm{y}
is the orthogonal projection from
\mathcal{T}
to
\mathcal{Y}
, and where for the far-field target
\tilde{g}=I_{\mathrm{t}}
and either
\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{t}}=\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{N}}
or
\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{t}}=\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{S}}
.

5
Reflector and lens equations: a hierarchy of mathematical models

We present a hierarchy of mathematical models for optical systems with zero-étendue source, based on three example systems, i.e., parallel-to-far-field reflector, point-to-far-field lens and parallel-to-near-field reflector. In this section, we combine the geometrical equations with the conservation law for luminous flux.

5.1
Parallel-to-far-field reflector

Figure 3
:
Sketch of a parallel-to-far-field reflector.

We consider a light source located in the plane
z=0
emitting a parallel beam of light for which
\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$s$}}}}=\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$e$}}}}_{z}
. Light rays strike the reflector
\mathcal{R}
defined by
z=u(\bm{x})
,
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and are reflected off in the direction
\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$t$}}}}\in\mathcal{T}\subset\mathrm{S}^{2}
.
The reflected rays intersect an auxiliary target plane
z=L
; see Figure
3
. To derive a geometrical equation for the reflector surface, we determine from Eq. (
10
) the mixed characteristic of the first kind
W(\bm{q}_{\mathrm{s}},\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}})
for an arbitrary ray with
\bm{q}_{\mathrm{s}}=\bm{x}
and
\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}}=(t_{1}\;t_{2})^{\rm T}
. The point characteristic
V(\bm{q}_{\mathrm{s}},\bm{q}_{\mathrm{t}})
involved is given by

V(\bm{q}_{\mathrm{s}},\bm{q}_{\mathrm{t}})=[\mathrm{Q}_{\mathrm{s}},\mathrm{Q}_{\mathrm{t}}]=u(\bm{x})+d,\quad d=\sqrt{|\bm{q}_{\mathrm{t}}-\bm{x}|^{2}+(L-u(\bm{x}))^{2}},

(27)

where
d=d(\mathrm{P},\mathrm{Q}_{\mathrm{t}})
is the distance between the points
\mathrm{P}(\bm{x},u(\bm{x}))
and
\mathrm{Q}_{\mathrm{t}}(\bm{q}_{\mathrm{t}},L)
, which are the points where the reflected ray hits reflector and target plane, respectively, loosely referred to as intersection points. For the direction vector
\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$t$}}}}
of the reflected ray we have

\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}}=\begin{pmatrix}t_{1}\\
t_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{d}(\bm{q}_{\mathrm{t}}-\bm{x}),\quad t_{3}=\frac{1}{d}(L-u(\bm{x})).

(28)

Moreover,
\partial W/\partial\bm{q}_{\mathrm{s}}=-\bm{p}_{\mathrm{s}}=\bm{0}
, since
\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$s$}}}}
is perpendicular to the source plane, implying that
W=W(\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}})
. Elaborating the expression for
W
, using the relations in Eqs. (
27
) and (
28
) we find

\begin{split}W(\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}})&=u(\bm{x})+d-\bm{q}_{\mathrm{t}}\bm{\cdot}\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}}\\
&=u(\bm{x})+\frac{1}{d}\big(|\bm{q}_{\mathrm{t}}-\bm{x}|^{2}+(L-u(\bm{x}))^{2}-\bm{q}_{\mathrm{t}}\bm{\cdot}(\bm{q}_{\mathrm{t}}-\bm{x})\big)\\
&=u(\bm{x})+\frac{1}{d}\big(-\bm{x}\bm{\cdot}(\bm{q}_{\mathrm{t}}-\bm{x})+dt_{3}(L-u(\bm{x}))\big)\\
&=u(\bm{x})(1-t_{3})-\bm{x}\bm{\cdot}\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}}+Lt_{3}.\end{split}

(29)

To separate the variables
\bm{x}
(source) and
\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}}
(target), we bring all terms that solely depend on
\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$t$}}}}
to the left-hand side, and we obtain

\frac{W(\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}})-Lt_{3}}{1-t_{3}}=u(\bm{x})-\frac{\bm{x}\bm{\cdot}\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}}}{1-t_{3}}.

(30)

Note that
t_{3}\neq 1
since rays cannot pass straight through the reflector.
Using the expressions for
d
and
t_{3}
we can show that

\frac{\partial{W}}{\partial{L}}=\frac{\partial{d}}{\partial{L}}-\frac{\partial{\bm{q}_{\mathrm{t}}}}{\partial{L}}\bm{\cdot}\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}},\quad\frac{\partial{d}}{\partial{L}}=\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}}\bm{\cdot}\frac{\partial{\bm{q}_{\mathrm{t}}}}{\partial{L}}+t_{3}

from which we readily conclude that the left-hand side in Eq. (
30
) is independent of
L
, which makes sense since a far-field target domain should not depend on the choice of a particular plane
z=L
.
As stated in Section
4
the proper choice to parameterize the target
\mathcal{T}
is the stereographic projection from the north pole, thus
\bm{y}=\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}}/(1-t_{3})
and
\mathcal{Y}=\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{N}}(\mathcal{T})
; cf. Eq. (
23
).
Substituting the expression for
\bm{y}
in Eq. (
30
), we arrive at the geometrical equation

u_{1}(\bm{x})+u_{2}(\bm{y})=c(\bm{x},\bm{y}),\quad c(\bm{x},\bm{y})=\bm{x}\bm{\cdot}\bm{y},

(31)

where
u_{1}(\bm{x})=u(\bm{x})
and
u_{2}(\bm{y})=(Lt_{3}-W(\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}}))/(1-t_{3})
.

Referring to Section
3
, a possible solution of Eq. (
31
) is the max/max solution in (
16
), which is a conjugate pair of Legendre-Fenchel tranforms since
c(\bm{x},\bm{y})=\bm{x}\bm{\cdot}\bm{y}
. The necessary condition in (
17
) and the matrix equation for the optical map in (
18
) reduce to

\displaystyle\bm{y}-\nabla u_{1}(\bm{x})=\bm{0},

(32a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}\bm{m}=\bm{P}(\bm{x}),\quad\bm{P}(\bm{x})=\mathrm{D}^{2}u_{1}(\bm{x}).

(32b)

Recall that equation (
32b
) has to be supplemented with the transport boundary condition (
19
).

To close the model, we elaborate the flux balance in Eq. (
26
). Substituting
f(\bm{x})=M(\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}(\bm{x}))
,
\mathrm{d}S(\bm{x})=\mathrm{d}\bm{x}
,
g(\bm{y})=I_{\mathrm{t}}(\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$t$}}}}(\bm{y}))
and
\mathrm{d}S(\bm{y})=J(\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$t$}}}}(\bm{y}))\mathrm{d}\bm{y}
, we obtain

\int_{\mathcal{A}}M(\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}(\bm{x}))\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=\int_{\bm{m}(\mathcal{A})}I_{\mathrm{t}}(\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$t$}}}}(\bm{y}))J(\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$t$}}}}(\bm{y}))\,\mathrm{d}\bm{y},

(33)

for arbitrary
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{X}
and
\bm{m}(\mathcal{A})\subset\mathcal{Y}
. Substituting
\bm{y}=\bm{m}(\bm{x})
and the expression for
J(\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$t$}}}}(\bm{y}))
, replacing
\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$v$}}}}(\bm{z})
by
\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$t$}}}}(\bm{y})
in Eq. (
25
), we can rewrite the integral in the right-hand side, and find

\int_{\mathcal{A}}M(\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}(\bm{x}))\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=\int_{\mathcal{A}}I_{\mathrm{t}}(\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$t$}}}}(\bm{m}(\bm{x})))\frac{4}{\big(1+|\bm{m}(\bm{x})|^{2}\big)^{2}}\det(\mathrm{D}\bm{m})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x},

(34)

where we used that
\det(\mathrm{D}\bm{m})>0
, which is correct because
\mathrm{D}\bm{m}
is either SPD or SND.
Since Eq. (
34
) should hold for arbitrary
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{X}
, we obtain the differential form

\det(\mathrm{D}\bm{m})=\tfrac{1}{4}\big(1+|\bm{m}(\bm{x})|^{2}\big)^{2}\frac{M(\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}(\bm{x}))}{I_{\mathrm{t}}(\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$t$}}}}(\bm{m}(\bm{x})))}=:F(\bm{x},\bm{m}(\bm{x})),

(35)

referred to as the Jacobian equation. Substituting
\bm{m}=\nabla u_{1}
, this equation reduces to the standard MA equation
\det(\mathrm{D}^{2}u_{1})=F(\bm{x},\nabla u_{1}(\bm{x}))
; see, e.g.,
[
13
]
.

5.2
Point-to-far-field lens

We consider a point source located in the origin
\mathrm{O}_{\mathrm{s}}
(position vector given by
\bm{q}_{\mathrm{s}}=\bm{0}
and
z_{\mathrm{s}}=0
), emitting upwards a conical beam of light with direction vector
\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$s$}}}}=\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$e$}}}}_{r}
. Light rays hit a lens of refractive index
n
; see Figure
4
. The first (entrance) surface is spherical with centre
\mathrm{O}_{\mathrm{s}}
and radius
R
, hence rays are not refracted there, however, the second (exit) surface is freeform and given by the parameterization
\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}(\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$s$}}}})=u(\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$s$}}}})\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$s$}}}}
. Rays are refracted at the exit surface and arrive at an auxiliary target screen
z=L>0
. To derive a geometrical equation for the freeform surface, we evaluate the angular characteristic

T(\bm{p}_{\mathrm{s}},\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}})
defined in (
13
) for an arbitrary ray connecting source with target plane. The point characteristic
V(\bm{q}_{\mathrm{s}},\bm{q}_{\mathrm{t}})
needed to evaluate
T(\bm{p}_{\mathrm{s}},\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}})
is given by

V(\bm{q}_{\mathrm{s}},\bm{q}_{\mathrm{t}})=[\mathrm{O}_{\mathrm{s}},\mathrm{Q}_{\mathrm{t}}]=R+n(u(\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$s$}}}})-R)+d,\quad d=\sqrt{|\bm{q}_{\mathrm{t}}-u(\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$s$}}}})\bm{p}_{\mathrm{s}}|^{2}+(L-u(\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$s$}}}})s_{3})^{2}},

(36)

where
d=d(\mathrm{P},\mathrm{Q}_{\mathrm{t}})
is the distance between the points
\mathrm{P}(u(\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$s$}}}})\bm{p}_{\mathrm{s}},u(\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$s$}}}})s_{3})
and
\mathrm{Q}_{\mathrm{t}}(\bm{q}_{\mathrm{t}},L)
, which are the intersection points of the refracted ray with exit surface and target screen, respectively. The direction vector
\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$t$}}}}
of the refracted ray is given by

\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}}=\begin{pmatrix}t_{1}\\
t_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{d}(\bm{q}_{\mathrm{t}}-u(\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$s$}}}})\bm{p}_{\mathrm{s}}),\quad t_{3}=\frac{1}{d}(L-u(\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$s$}}}})s_{3}).

(37)

Note that
\partial T/\partial\bm{p}_{\mathrm{s}}=\bm{q}_{\mathrm{s}}=\bm{0}
, implying that

T=T(\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}})
. Evaluating the expression for
T(\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}})
in Eq. (
13
) using the relations in Eqs. (
36
) and (
37
), we obtain

\begin{split}T(\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}})&=R+n(u(\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$s$}}}})-R)+d-\bm{q}_{\mathrm{t}}\bm{\cdot}\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}}\\
&=(1-n)R+nu(\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$s$}}}})+\frac{1}{d}\big(|\bm{q}_{\mathrm{t}}-u(\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$s$}}}})\bm{p}_{\mathrm{s}}|^{2}+(L-u(\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$s$}}}})s_{3})^{2}-\bm{q}_{\mathrm{t}}\bm{\cdot}(\bm{q}_{\mathrm{t}}-u(\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$s$}}}})\bm{p}_{\mathrm{s}})\big)\\
&=(1-n)R+nu(\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$s$}}}})+\frac{1}{d}\big(-u(\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$s$}}}})\bm{p}_{\mathrm{s}}\bm{\cdot}(\bm{q}_{\mathrm{t}}-u(\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$s$}}}})\bm{p}_{\mathrm{s}})+dt_{3}(L-u(\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$s$}}}})s_{3})\big)\\
&=(1-n)R+nu(\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$s$}}}})-u(\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$s$}}}})\bm{p}_{\mathrm{s}}\bm{\cdot}\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}}+t_{3}(L-u(\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$s$}}}})s_{3})\\
&=(1-n)R+(n-\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$s$}}}}\bm{\cdot}\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$t$}}}})u(\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$s$}}}})+Lt_{3}.\end{split}

(38)

To derive the last expression for
T
in (
38
) we substituted the relation
\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$s$}}}}\bm{\cdot}\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$t$}}}}=\bm{p}_{\mathrm{s}}\bm{\cdot}\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}}+s_{3}t_{3}
.
Next, we move all terms that solely depend on
\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$t$}}}}
and the constant
(1-n)R
to the left-hand side and find

T(\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}})-Lt_{3}+(n-1)R=(n-\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$s$}}}}\bm{\cdot}\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$t$}}}})u(\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$s$}}}}).

(39)

Differentiating the first expression in (
38
) with respect to
L
and combining the expression for
d
with (
37
) we obtain

\frac{\partial{T}}{\partial{L}}=\frac{\partial{d}}{\partial{L}}-\frac{\partial{\bm{q}_{\mathrm{t}}}}{\partial{L}}\bm{\cdot}\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}},\quad\frac{\partial{d}}{\partial{L}}=\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}}\bm{\cdot}\frac{\partial{\bm{q}_{\mathrm{t}}}}{\partial{L}}+t_{3},

from which we readily see that the left-hand side in (
39
) is independent of
L
, as anticipated.

To separate source and target coordinates, analogous to Eq. (
31
), we have to take the logarithm of the left- and right-hand sides of equation (
39
). Note that
n-\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$s$}}}}\bm{\cdot}\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$t$}}}}>0
and
u(\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$s$}}}})>0
, consequently, the left-hand side is positive as well, and we can take the logarithms. In addition, we substitute the stereographic projections (from the south pole)
\bm{x}
of
\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$s$}}}}
and
\bm{y}
of
\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$t$}}}}
, since both
\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$s$}}}}
and
\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$t$}}}}
are directed upwards; cf. Eq. (
24
). This way, we obtain

u_{1}(\bm{x})+u_{2}(\bm{y})=c(\bm{x},\bm{y}),

(40a)

where the variables
u_{1}(\bm{x})
and
u_{2}(\bm{y})
and the cost function
c(\bm{x},\bm{y})
are defined by

\displaystyle u_{1}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\log u(\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$s$}}}}(\bm{x})),

(40b)

\displaystyle u_{2}(\bm{y})

\displaystyle=-\log\big(T(\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}}(\bm{y}))-Lt_{3}(\bm{y})+(n-1)R\big),

(40c)

\displaystyle c(\bm{x},\bm{y})

\displaystyle=-\log\big(n-\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$s$}}}}(\bm{x})\bm{\cdot}\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$t$}}}}(\bm{y})\big).

(40d)

Formulated in terms of stereographic coordinates the cost function reads

c(\bm{x},\bm{y})=-\log\Big(n-1+\frac{2|\bm{x}-\bm{y}|^{2}}{(1+|\bm{x}|^{2})(1+|\bm{y}|^{2})}\Big),

which is no longer quadratic. A possible solution of Eq. (
40a
) is the
c
-convex pair in (

), for which the necessary condition (
17
) holds. For the optical map, the matrix equation (
18
) accompanied with the transport boundary condition (

) is given.

Referring to Section
4
, in the far-field approximation the luminous flux balance reads

\int_{\mathcal{A}}I_{\mathrm{s}}(\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$s$}}}}(\bm{x}))J(\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$s$}}}}(\bm{x}))\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=\int_{\bm{m}(\mathcal{A})}I_{\mathrm{t}}(\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$t$}}}}(\bm{y}))J(\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$t$}}}}(\bm{y}))\,\mathrm{d}\bm{y},

(41)

for arbitrary
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{X}
and image set
\bm{m}(\mathcal{A})\subset\mathcal{Y}
. To derive the differential form, we substitute the expressions for

and

according to (
25
) and subsequently substitute
\bm{y}=\bm{m}(\bm{x})
. Assuming

, we obtain

\det(\mathrm{D}\bm{m})=\left(\frac{1+|\bm{m}(\bm{x})|^{2}}{1+|\bm{x}|^{2}}\right)^{2}\frac{I_{\mathrm{s}}(\bm{x})}{I_{\mathrm{t}}(\bm{m}(\bm{x}))}=:F(\bm{x},\bm{m}(\bm{x})).

(42)

Combining this equation with the matrix equation in (

) we obtain the GMA equation
\det(\mathrm{D}^{2}u_{1}-\mathrm{D}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}c)=\det(C(\bm{x},\bm{m}(\bm{x}))F(\bm{x},\bm{m}(\bm{x}))
.

5.3
Parallel-to-near-field reflector

Figure 5
:
Sketch of a parallel-to-near-field reflector.

The last example concerns a planar source, located in
z=0
, emitting a parallel beam of light rays in the direction
\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$s$}}}}=\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$e$}}}}_{z}
, consequently
\bm{p}_{\mathrm{s}}=\bm{0}
, which hits a reflector
\mathcal{R}
given by
z=u(\bm{x})
, with
\bm{x}=\bm{q}_{\mathrm{s}}
spatial coordinates, and are reflected off in the direction
\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$t$}}}}
and lands at a target surface given by
z=v(\bm{y})
, with
\bm{y}=\bm{q}_{\mathrm{t}}
spatial coordinates in the reference plane
z=z_{\mathrm{t}}=0
. Thus, source and target planes coincide; see Figure
5
. We evaluate Hamilton’s point characteristic and find

\begin{split}V(\bm{q}_{\mathrm{s}},\bm{q}_{\mathrm{t}})&=[\mathrm{Q}_{\mathrm{s}},\mathrm{Q}_{\mathrm{t}}]\\
&=u(\bm{x})+d,\quad d=\sqrt{|\bm{y}-\bm{x}|^{2}+(v(\bm{y})-u(\bm{x}))^{2}},\end{split}

(43)

where
d=d(\mathrm{P},\mathrm{Q}_{\mathrm{t}})
is the distance between the intersection points
\mathrm{P}(\bm{x},u(\bm{x}))
and
\mathrm{Q}_{\mathrm{t}}(\bm{y},v(\bm{y}))
of the reflected ray with the reflector and the target surface, respectively. Since
\partial V/\partial\bm{q}_{\mathrm{s}}=-\bm{p}_{\mathrm{s}}=\bm{0}
, we find that
V=V(\bm{q}_{\mathrm{t}})
. In this case it is no longer possible to separate the source and target coordinates
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
like in Eqs. (
31
) or (
40a
). Instead, we write

u_{2}(\bm{y})=H(\bm{x},\bm{y},u_{1}(\bm{x})),\quad H(\bm{x},\bm{y},w)=w+\sqrt{|\bm{y}-\bm{x}|^{2}+(v(\bm{y})-w)^{2}},

(44)

where
u_{1}(\bm{x})=u(\bm{x})
and
u_{2}(\bm{y})=V(\bm{q}_{\mathrm{t}})
.
Straightforward differentiation gives
H_{w}(\bm{x},\bm{y},\cdot)>0
, implying that for fixed
\bm{x},\bm{y}
the inverse
G(\bm{x},\bm{y},\cdot)=H^{-1}(\bm{x},\bm{y},\cdot)
exists.
Therefore, we can explicitly determine
u_{1}(\bm{x})
from (
44
), and we obtain

u_{1}(\bm{x})=G(\bm{x},\bm{y},u_{2}(\bm{y})),\quad G(\bm{x},\bm{y},w)=\tfrac{1}{2}\Big(w+v(\bm{y})-\frac{|\bm{y}-\bm{x}|^{2}}{w-v(\bm{y})}\Big).

(45)

A possible solution of Eqs. (
44
) and (
45
) is the
G
-convex
H
-concave pair in (
20
) for which the necessary condition (
21
) holds. The matrix equation for
\bm{m}
is given in (
18
) with matrices
\bm{C}
and
\bm{P}
defined in (
22
). Recall that these matrices explicitly depend on
u_{1}(\bm{x})
. Obviously, also the transport boundary condition (
19
) holds.

Making the proper choices for the flux densities and area elements, the flux balance (
26
) reduces to

\int_{\mathcal{A}}M(\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}(\bm{x}))\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=\int_{\bm{m}(\mathcal{A})}E(\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}(\bm{y}))\sqrt{|\nabla v(\bm{y})|^{2}+1}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{y}

(46)

for an arbitrary set
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{X}
and image set
\bm{m}(\mathcal{A})\subset\mathcal{Y}
, where
M(\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}(\bm{x}))
and

denote the emittance and illuminance of source and target, respectively.
Substituting
\bm{y}=\bm{m}(\bm{x})
, assuming
\det(\mathrm{D}\bm{m})>0
, we obtain

\det(\mathrm{D}\bm{m})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\nabla v(\bm{m}(\bm{x}))|^{2}+1}}\frac{M(\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}(\bm{x}))}{E(\bm{\underline{\hbox{$r$}}}(\bm{m}(\bm{x})))}=:F(\bm{x},\bm{m}(\bm{x})).

(47)

Combining this equation with Eq. (
18
) and the matrices
\bm{C}
and
\bm{P}
defined in Eq. (
22
), we obtain the equation
\det(\mathrm{D}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}\widetilde{H})=\det(\bm{C}(\bm{x},\bm{m}(\bm{x}),u_{1}(\bm{x}))F(\bm{x},\bm{m}(\bm{x}))
, referred to as the GJ equation.

5.4
Summary of mathematical models

All mathematical models considered consist of a geometrical equation, a condition for a stationary point, a matrix equation for the optical map coupled to the transport boundary condition and a luminous flux balance

\det(\mathrm{D}\bm{m})=F(\bm{x},\bm{m}(\bm{x}))
. The conditions for the stationary point read

cost function

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{x}}c(\bm{x},\bm{y})-\nabla u_{1}(\bm{x})=\bm{0},

(48a)

generating function

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{x}}\widetilde{H}(\bm{x},\bm{y})=\bm{0}.

(48b)

Substituting the optical map
\bm{y}=\bm{m}(\bm{x})
and differentiating with respect to
\bm{x}
gives the matrix equation
\bm{C}\mathrm{D}\bm{m}=\bm{P}
where the matrices
\bm{C}
and
\bm{P}
are given by

quadratic cost function

\displaystyle\bm{C}=\bm{I},\quad

\displaystyle\bm{P}=\mathrm{D}^{2}u_{1},

(49a)

general cost function

\displaystyle\bm{C}=\mathrm{D}_{\bm{x}\bm{y}}c,\quad

\displaystyle\bm{P}=\mathrm{D}^{2}u_{1}-\mathrm{D}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}c,

(49b)

generating function

\displaystyle\bm{C}=\mathrm{D}_{\bm{x}\bm{y}}\widetilde{H},\quad

\displaystyle\bm{P}=-\mathrm{D}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}\widetilde{H}.

(49c)

Combining the matrix equation for
\bm{m}
with the flux balance gives

\det(\bm{C})F(\bm{x},\bm{m}(\bm{x}))=\det(\bm{P}),

which we refer to as the luminous flux constraint.
Note that for the cost-function model, the matrices
\bm{C}
and
\bm{P}
explicitly depend on
\bm{x}
and
\bm{m}(\bm{x})
, on the other hand, for the generating-function model, both matrices in addition depend on
u_{1}(\bm{x})
.
In
[
1
]
we have given a similar overview of mathematical models of sixteen base optical systems.

In the next section we outline numerical methods to compute the optical map
\bm{y}=\bm{m}(\bm{x})
and the auxiliary variable
u_{1}(\bm{x})
. The shape/location of the optical surface is then reconstructed from a simple algebraic equation, i.e.,
u(\bm{x})=u_{1}(\bm{x})
for the parallel-to-far-field and parallel-to-near-field reflectors and
u(\hat{\bm{\underline{\hbox{$s$}}}}(\bm{x}))=\mathrm{e}^{u_{1}(\bm{x})}
for the parallel-to-far-field lens.

Iterative least-squares methods

In this section we outline iterative least-squares methods for the models presented in the previous section, starting with the base scheme for the cost-function models and adding modifications for the generation-function model. A detailed account of these methods is presented in a series of theses, see
[
19
,

,

]
, and papers, see

,

,

,

,

,

,

.

Base scheme for cost function.

To compute the layout of the optical system we apply a two-stage method, i.e., we first compute the optical map
\bm{m}
and subsequently the auxiliary variable
u_{1}
and possibly
u_{2}
, from which we trivially can compute the shape/location of the optical surface(s). We employ a uniform rectangular grid covering the parameter space of the source domain.

We iteratively compute a symmetric matrix
\bm{P}
as approximation of the symmetric part of
\bm{C}\mathrm{D}\bm{m}
, satisfying
\det(\bm{P})=\det(\bm{C}(\cdot,\bm{m}))F(\cdot,\bm{m})
, and a vector field
\bm{b}:\partial\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\partial\mathcal{Y}
, from which we subsequently compute
\bm{m}
.
Our solution strategy is then to minimize the functionals

\displaystyle J_{\mathrm{I}}[\bm{m},\bm{P}]

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{X}}|\!|\bm{C}\mathrm{D}\bm{m}-\bm{P}|\!|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x},

\displaystyle\text{subject to}\quad\det(\bm{P})=\det(\bm{C}(\cdot,\bm{m}))F(\cdot,\bm{m}),

(50a)

\displaystyle J_{\mathrm{B}}[\bm{m},\bm{b}]

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\oint_{\partial\mathcal{X}}|\bm{m}-\bm{b}|_{2}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s,

(50b)

\displaystyle J[\bm{m},\bm{P},\bm{b}]

\displaystyle=\alpha J_{\mathrm{I}}[\bm{m},\bm{P}]+(1-\alpha)J_{\mathrm{B}}[\bm{m},\bm{b}],\quad(0<\alpha<1),

(50c)

where
|\!|\cdot|\!|_{\mathrm{F}}
denotes the Frobenius norm. The minimization of
J_{\mathrm{B}}[\bm{m},\bm{b}]
is to enforce the transport boundary condition (
19
). Given an initial guess
\bm{m}^{0}
, the iteration scheme then reads

\displaystyle\bm{P}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{argmin}_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}(\bm{m}^{k})}J_{\mathrm{I}}[\bm{m}^{k},\bm{P}],

(51a)

\displaystyle\bm{b}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{argmin}_{\bm{b}\in\mathcal{B}}J_{\mathrm{B}}[\bm{m}^{k},\bm{b}],

(51b)

\displaystyle\bm{m}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{argmin}_{\bm{m}\in\mathcal{M}}J[\bm{m},\bm{P}^{k+1},\bm{b}^{k+1}],

(51c)

where the corresponding function spaces are given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\bm{m})

\displaystyle=\{\bm{P}\in C^{1}(\mathcal{X})^{2\times 2}\big|\bm{P}^{\mathrm{T}}=\bm{P},\det(\bm{P})=\det(\bm{C}(\cdot,\bm{m}(\cdot)))F(\cdot,\bm{m}(\cdot))\},

(52a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}

\displaystyle=\{\bm{b}\in C(\partial\mathcal{X})^{2}\big|\bm{b}(\bm{x})\in\partial\mathcal{Y}\},

(52b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}

\displaystyle=C^{2}(\mathcal{X})^{2}.

(52c)

In common parlance,
\mathcal{P}(\bm{m})
is the space of
2\times 2
matrix functions, that are continuously differentiable on
\mathcal{X}
, are symmetric and satisfy the luminous flux constraint.
\mathcal{B}
is the space of continuous vector functions, that map the boundary
\partial\mathcal{X}
to the boundary
\partial\mathcal{Y}
.

The minimization of
J_{\mathrm{I}}[\bm{m},\bm{P}]
to compute
\bm{P}
can be performed point-wise and requires the solution of a constrained minimization problem.
\bm{P}
has to be either SPD or SND, which can be enforced by a constraint on
\mathrm{tr}(\bm{P})
. The minimization of
J_{\mathrm{B}}[\bm{m},\bm{b}]
to compute
\bm{b}
is a piecewise projection of
\bm{m}
on
\partial\mathcal{X}
. For the minimization of
J[\bm{m},\bm{P},\bm{b}]
we impose the first variation with respect to
\bm{m}
to vanish, i.e.,

\delta J[\bm{m},\bm{P},\bm{b}](\bm{\eta})=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\big(J[\bm{m}+\varepsilon\bm{\eta},\bm{P},\bm{b}]-J[\bm{m},\bm{P},\bm{b}]\big)=0,

(53)

for arbitrary
\bm{\eta}\in C^{2}(\mathcal{X})^{2}
. Evaluating this limit, applying Gauss’s theorem and the fundamental lemma of calculus of variations
[
8
, p. 185]
, we derive the following coupled boundary value problem (BVP) for
\bm{m}

\displaystyle\nabla\bm{\cdot}(\bm{C}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{C}\mathrm{D}\bm{m})

\displaystyle=\nabla\bm{\cdot}(\bm{C}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{P}),\quad\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X},

(54a)

\displaystyle(1-\alpha)\bm{m}+\alpha(\bm{C}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{C}\mathrm{D}\bm{m})\hat{\bm{n}}

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)\bm{b}+\alpha\bm{C}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{P}\hat{\bm{n}},\quad\bm{x}\in\partial\mathcal{X},

(54b)

where
\hat{\bm{n}}
is the unit outward normal on
\partial\mathcal{X}
. The divergence of a
2\times 2
matrix function
\bm{A}=(\bm{a}_{1}\;\bm{a}_{2})
is defined as
\nabla\bm{\cdot}\bm{A}=\begin{pmatrix}\nabla\bm{\cdot}\bm{a}_{1}\\
\nabla\bm{\cdot}\bm{a}_{2}\end{pmatrix}
. For discretization of (
54
) we employ the standard finite volume method
[
24
, pp. Appendix B]
.

Next, upon convergence of the iteration (
51
), we compute
u_{1}
from (
48a
) by minimizing the functional

I[u_{1}]=\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{X}}|\nabla_{\bm{x}}c(\cdot,\bm{m})-\nabla u_{1}|_{2}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}.

(55)

Analogous to the derivation of (
54
), we set the first variation
\delta I[u_{1}](v)=0
for arbitrary
v\in C^{2}(\mathcal{X})
, to obtain the Neumann problem

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}u_{1}

\displaystyle=\nabla\bm{\cdot}\nabla_{\bm{x}}c(\cdot,\bm{m}),\quad\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X},

(56a)

\displaystyle\nabla u_{1}\bm{\cdot}\hat{\bm{n}}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\bm{x}}c(\cdot,\bm{m})\bm{\cdot}\hat{\bm{n}},\quad\bm{x}\in\partial\mathcal{X}.

(56b)

We employ standard finite differences to (
56
).
u_{1}
is determined up to an additive constant, which translates in an additive constant in the location of the parallel-to-far-field reflector, and a multiplicative constant for the location of the point-to-far-field lens. To compute a unique solution from (
56
) we either fix the distance from source to optical surface along a specific ray, or prescribe an average for
u_{1}
.

Extension to generating function.

The optical map
\bm{m}
and the variable
u_{1}
have to be computed simultaneously since the matrices
\bm{C}
and
\bm{P}
depend on both variables. Like in the previous case, the optical map satisfies the equation
\bm{C}\mathrm{D}\bm{m}=\bm{P}
, with the matrices
\bm{C}
and
\bm{P}
defined in (
49c
), and the luminous flux balance
\det(\mathrm{D}\bm{m})=F
. Consequently, the functional
J_{\mathrm{I}}[\bm{m},\bm{P}]
remains the same, albeit with different matrices
\bm{C}
and
\bm{P}
. The variable
u_{1}
has to be computed from equation (
48b
), for which we introduce the functional

\begin{split}I[u_{1},\bm{m}]&=\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\big|\nabla_{\bm{x}}\widetilde{H}(\cdot,\bm{m})\big|_{2}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\\
&=\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\big|\nabla_{\bm{x}}H(\cdot,\bm{m},u_{1})+H_{w}(\cdot,\bm{m},u_{1})\nabla u_{1}\big|_{2}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}.\end{split}

(57)

To minimize the functional in (
57
) we require
\delta I[u_{1},\bm{m}](v)=0
for arbitrary
v\in C^{2}(\mathcal{X})
, with
\delta I[u_{1},\bm{m}](v)
the first variation of
I
with respect to
u_{1}
, defined analogously to (
53
). This way we can derive the BVP

\displaystyle\nabla\bm{\cdot}\big(H_{w}\nabla_{\bm{x}}H+H_{w}^{2}\nabla u_{1}\big)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}{}}{\mathrm{d}{w}}\big|\nabla_{\bm{x}}H+H_{w}\nabla u_{1}\big|_{2}^{2},\quad\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X},

(58a)

\displaystyle H_{w}\big(\nabla_{\bm{x}}H+H_{w}\nabla u_{1}\big)\bm{\cdot}\hat{\bm{n}}

\displaystyle=0,\quad\bm{x}\in\partial\mathcal{X}.

(58b)

We employ the standard finite volume method for discretization. We can show that also the solution of (
58
)
is determined up to an additive constant, and compute a unique solution prescribing the average value of
u_{1}
; see
[
24
, pp. 166 - 167]
for more details.
Finally, given an initial guess
\bm{m}^{0}
and
u_{1}^{0}
, the iteration scheme then reads

\displaystyle\bm{P}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{argmin}_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}(\bm{m}^{k})}J_{\mathrm{I}}[\bm{m}^{k},u_{1}^{k},\bm{P}],

(59a)

\displaystyle\bm{b}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{argmin}_{\bm{b}\in\mathcal{B}}J_{\mathrm{B}}[\bm{m}^{k},\bm{b}],

(59b)

\displaystyle\bm{m}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{argmin}_{\bm{m}\in\mathcal{M}}J[\bm{m},u_{1}^{k},\bm{P}^{k+1},\bm{b}^{k+1}],

(59c)

\displaystyle u_{1}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{argmin}_{v\in\mathcal{U}}I[v,\bm{m}^{k+1}],

(59d)

with
\mathcal{U}=C^{2}(\mathcal{X})
, where we have included
u_{1}^{k}
in the argument list of
J_{\mathrm{I}}
and
J
to denote their implicit dependence on
u_{1}
via the matrix
\bm{C}
.

8
Concluding remarks

We have presented a systematic and generic derivation of the governing equations for inverse optical design. Using Hamilton’s characteristic functions, we could derive a geometrical equation defining the shape/location of the optical surface(s). From this equation, applying concepts from optimal transport theory, we derived equations for the optical map. To close the model, we presented a generic conservation law of luminous flux. Combining all equations, we derived a Jacobian equation for the optical map. Subsequently, we elaborated the generic model in three different models of increasing complexity, on the basis of three example optical systems. We briefly outlined numerical least-squares methods for all models, and demonstrated their performance for a few examples.

We intend to expand our research on inverse methods along the following lines. First, we will adjust our numerical methods to incorporate two target distributions, second, we will develop mathematical models and numerical methods for catadioptic systems where light rays propagate via different paths from source to target, and finally, and probably the most challenging, we intent to generalize our models and numerics to optical systems with finite sources as opposed to zero-étendue sources.
