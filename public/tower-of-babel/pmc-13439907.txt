Modeling suicidal thoughts and behaviors in university students: integrating suicide theory with interpretable machine learning
Authors: Li-Chung Lin, Chih-Han Leng, Grace Yao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUniversity students are at risk of suicidal thoughts and behaviors (STB), with prevalence rates higher than those in the general population. Machine learning (ML) enhances the accuracy of STB prediction and provides new possibilities and explanatory frameworks for this issue. This study extends prior research in two ways: (1) by employing, for the first time, a sample of Taiwanese university students and (2) by synthesizing suicide-related theoretical knowledge and empirical findings to identify individual, risk, and protective factors. Based on these elements, a comprehensive ML-based prediction model of STB among university students was developed with four analytic modes to evaluate the relative importance and contributions of three predictor categories. Using data from the NTU Students’ Physical and Mental Health Assessment System, a total of 3,901 students were retained in the current study, with 13 self-report assessments administered. Classic logistic regression and five ML classifiers (e.g., bagged LR, RF, LightGBM, linear SVM, and RBF SVM) were utilized. Compared with traditional statistical methods, ML algorithms improved prediction of STB among high‑risk students. RF and LightGBM performed best, with similar results in Mode 2 (risk factor only) and Mode 4 (integrated individual, risk, and protective factors), both outperforming Mode 3 (protective factor only) and Mode 1 (individual factor only). Ultimately, this study enhances the interpretability of ML-based models for predicting STB. Moreover, it provides researchers with deeper insights for developing systematic prediction models and offers a screening tool for identifying university students at high risk of STB.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-026-08171-1.Keywords: Suicidal thoughts and behaviors (STB), Machine learning, Protective factor, Risk factor

Introduction

University students worldwide are widely exposed to the risk of suicidal thoughts and behaviors (STB), including suicide death, suicidal ideation, and suicide attempts [1, 2]. The prevalence rates among university students are higher than those observed in the general adult population [3, 4]. Macalli et al., [5], who analyzed data from 19 universities across eight countries, reported a 17.2% prevalence of suicidal ideation among university students. Furthermore, compared with North American students, Asian university students exhibit higher prevalence rates of STB [6]. Given that the suicide risk among college students reflects the interplay of multidimensional factors—psychological, interpersonal, and behavioral [7]—the development of suicide-related theories to explain individual causes of suicide, the construction of measurement tools and models to predict or diagnose individuals at high risk, and the identification of critical predictors have long been central concerns for psychologists and researchers.

Suicide-related theories and empirical research findings

Recent integrative suicide-related theories have been proposed to systematically explain the stages and causes by which individuals develop STB. Three important multistage suicide theoretical models include the interpersonal–psychological theory of suicide (IPT; [8, 9]), the integrated motivational–volitional theory of suicide (IMV; [10]), and the three-step theory of suicide (3ST; [11, 12]). IPT posits that the interaction of thwarted belongingness, perceived burdensomeness, and acquired capability for suicide results in maximal suicide risk [9, 13]. IMV posits that the progression from suicidal ideation to suicidal behavior unfolds across three stages: (1) the premotivational phase, which involves any negative life event experienced by the individual; (2) the motivational phase, emphasizing the role of threat-to-self moderators (e.g., autobiographical memory biases and rumination) and motivational moderators (e.g., thwarted belongingness and social support); and (3) the volitional phase, where volitional moderators determine the risk conditions for transitioning from suicidal intent to suicidal behavior [14, 15]. The 3ST explains the development of high suicide risk in stages on the basis of the individual’s experience of pain and hopelessness, connectedness to others, and ability to attempt suicide.

Suicide-related theories focus on explanatory models for individuals’ STB, providing a foundation for and promoting the development of empirical research [16]. Conducted a meta-analysis of 365 suicide-related studies published between 1965 and 2015 and revealed that previous research focused primarily on risk factors for STB, such as externalizing psychopathology, normative personality traits, physical illness, treatment history, prior self-injurious thoughts or behaviors, and exposure to self-injurious thoughts and behaviors. The meta-analysis also revealed that earlier studies were limited by traditional statistical methods, which failed to adequately characterize the complex relationships between risk factors and STB, leading to poor predictive accuracy. Franklin et al. (2016) further emphasized the advantages of machine learning (ML) in (1) identifying optimal algorithms, (2) managing complex relationships among predictive variables, and (3) improving model generalizability, recommending that future research utilize ML to develop algorithms for STB.

Inspired by Franklin et al. (2016), ML has been widely applied in recent years to research, predict or identify suicide risk. Several systematic reviews and meta-analyses on this topic have indicated that ML not only enhances the accuracy of suicide risk prediction [17–19] but also identifies important suicide risk predictors [20]. Moreover, ML offers new possibilities and frameworks for suicide risk prediction and prevention [21, 22]. Systematic reviews by Burke et al. [20] and Nordin et al., [23] have summarized several crucial categories of predictors, including social support, psychological well-being and resilience, family history of suicide attempts, previous self-injurious behaviors, negative life events, anxiety and depression, hopelessness, and despair.

This study synthesizes suicide-related theories (IPT, IMV, and 3ST) and empirical research findings and identifies three key dimensions that predict individuals’ STB: individual factors, risk factors, and protective factors (see Table 1).

For example, the risk of STB increases as individuals exhibit more traits of borderline personality disorder and antisocial personality disorder. Conversely, when individuals facing suicide risk maintain negative thoughts about suicidal ideation and behaviors and hold positive attitudes toward seeking help, they are less likely to experience STB [24].

Risk factors include individuals’ negative life events, pain, despair, frustration, or feelings of humiliation and other negative emotional experiences. Previous studies have shown that when individuals experience affective dysregulation [25], anxiety and depression [26–29], negative life events, hopelessness, despair [30, 31], frustration and perceived burdensomeness [32], and a personal or significant other’s history of STB (Fergusson et al. 2003; [33]), their risk of suicide increases.

Protective factors include connections and attachments with significant others, psychological resilience, and well-being, which enhance individuals’ tolerance to negative life experiences and emotional distress, thereby protecting them from maintaining low or no suicide risk. For example, individuals with higher levels of social support [34], psychological well-being and resilience [35], belongingness [36], self-esteem [37], and meaning in life [38] are less likely to experience suicide risk.

Limitations of previous research and the purposes of the current study

Although substantial advancements have been reported in the literature—including the development of systematic, integrative suicide theories explaining how individuals develop STB, and the application of ML algorithms to more accurately capture complex relationships among predictive variables and STB, thereby enhancing predictive power—two significant research issues warrant further exploration.

Our literature review revealed that no studies have simultaneously considered these three major predictive factors and examined their relative contributions to the development of suicide risk algorithms. Individual factors, risk factors, and protective factors are important dimensions influencing the formation of STB. Individual factors focus on the internal trait structures and cognitive systems of individuals and how these influence the interpretation of life events and the perception of emotional experiences, thereby increasing or decreasing suicide risk. Risk factors emphasize negative life events and emotional experiences that directly trigger STB. Protective factors reduce the likelihood of STB by emphasizing positive life events and emotional experiences such as social support, psychological resilience, and meaning in life. Because these three factors influence the pathways to suicide risk differently, each has unique predictive power and importance in the development of suicide risk algorithms. Therefore, it is necessary to consider all these important factors simultaneously.

In the past decade, the use of ML to develop suicidal ideation and behavior risk algorithms among university students has gradually expanded globally, including in countries such as the United States [39], France [5], China [40], and South Korea [41]. However, according to our literature review, no studies have developed suicidal ideation and behavior prediction algorithms using data from Taiwanese university students. Since STB are embedded within cultural contexts [42], suicide risk algorithms vary according to local social, cultural, and geographical environments [27, 33]. Moreover, in the past decade, the number of suicide deaths among young people (aged 15–24) in Taiwan has increased annually, with the crude mortality rate nearly doubling. Furthermore, the number of reported suicide risk cases related to campus issues has increased from 220 to 2,849. These findings indicate that suicide-related incidents are becoming increasingly prevalent on campuses and must be addressed. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop algorithms to predict STB among Taiwanese university students.

To address the aforementioned research gaps, the current study is the first to simultaneously consider individual, risk, and protective factors when developing a ML algorithm for detecting suicidal ideation and behavior among Taiwanese university students. Four analytic modes were constructed to evaluate the relative contributions of these three major predictors in the development of the risk algorithm and to identify the most salient predictive indicators.

Methods

Procedures

The dataset analyzed in this study was provided by the Office of Student Affairs (OSA) at National Taiwan University (NTU) as part of the project “The Development of the NTU Students’ Physical and Mental Health Assessment System.” The project was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of NTU (Approval No. 202106HS019).

Data collection was conducted during the first three months of the fall semester beginning in September 2024. All undergraduate and graduate students at NTU were invited to voluntarily complete an online questionnaire assessing demographic characteristics, psychological states, environmental factors, and experiences related to STB. Participation was anonymous, and informed consent was obtained prior to survey completion. The questionnaire was administered through the NTU business process management system, with recruitment announcements posted on the OSA website and distributed via university-wide email.

Importantly, the assessment was designed for early identification and prevention purposes rather than clinical diagnosis, and no automated feedback or intervention was generated on the basis of individual responses.

Participants

A total of 3,908 students completed the questionnaire. After respondents with missing data were excluded, the final analytic sample consisted of 3,842 students, including 2,038 females. The participants ranged in age from 17 to 26 years (). The sample included 1,183 first-year students, 831 s-year students, 775 third-year students, 934 fourth-year students, 64 fifth-year students, and 55 sixth-year students.

Suicidal thoughts and behaviors

Measures of STB were derived by integrating key components from three multistage suicide theories—the IPT, the IMV model, and the 3ST—along with items adapted from the Self-Injurious Thoughts and Behaviors Interview (SITBI; [43]).

Four items were used to assess STB: suicidal thoughts, suicide planning, self-harm behaviors, and suicide attempts. The first three items were measured on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 to 5 (1 = never, 2 = rarely, 3 = sometimes, 4 = often, and 5 = always), whereas suicide attempts were assessed using a binary response format. Notably, in this study, college students at high risk for STB were operationally defined as those who experience STB with a relatively high frequency or on a recurrent basis in daily life. Accordingly, students were classified as being at high risk for STB if they reported a score greater than 3 on any of the first three items. This threshold was chosen to balance sensitivity and specificity for identifying individuals at elevated risk rather than to establish clinical diagnoses.

Features

This study measured college students’ demographic characteristics, including gender, grade level, and age, as well as a range of individual, risk, and protective factors. The basic characteristics of these measured variables are summarized in Table 2. As emphasized by the IMV and 3ST, individual factors were measured using the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale (RSES; [44]), the Brief Fear of Negative Evaluation Scale (Brief-FNE; [45]), the frost multidimensional perfectionism scale–brief (F-MPS-Brief; [46, 47]), and the Self-Compassion Scale (SCS; [48]). The RSES, Brief-FNE, and SCS measure the relationship between the self-threat moderator and the self-related evaluation. The F-MPS-Brief assesses personality traits. According to the IPT, IMV, and 3ST, risk factors included the short-form UCLA Loneliness Scale score (ULS-8; [49]), the Depression Anxiety Stress Scale score (DASS9; [50]), the Perceived Stress Scale score (PSS; [51]), mental illness, and a history of suicide-related events. The ULS-8 and DASS9 measure psychological pain. The relationships between mental illness and negative life events and between a history of suicide-related events and prior suicide experience were investigated. Based on the IMV and 3ST, protective factors were assessed using the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS; [52]), the short-form version of the Connor–Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC-10; [53]), the Peace of Mind Scale (POMS; [54]), the Purpose in Life Test-Short Form (PIL-SF; [55]), and the Subjective Academic Achievement Scale (SAAS; [56]). The MSPSS measures social support, the CD-RISC-10 investigates psychological resilience, the POMS assesses psychological well-being, and the SAAS evaluates self-achievement and self-satisfaction.

Data preparation

All of the analyses were conducted in Python with a fixed random seed of 6,666 to ensure reproducibility. Among the 3,842 participants, 213 were classified as exhibiting STB (coded as 1), while the remaining participants were coded as 0.

The dataset was randomly split into training and testing sets at a 70:30 ratio. All preprocessing procedures (e.g., standardization) were conducted using parameters derived exclusively from the training set and then applied to the testing set to prevent data leakage.

Given the substantial class imbalance inherent in suicide-related outcomes, when cases of STB were relatively rare, specific strategies were implemented to mitigate potential bias toward the majority class. The synthetic minority oversampling technique (SMOTE; [57]) was applied exclusively to the training data to generate synthetic samples of the minority class, thereby improving class balance during model training. In addition, SMOTE was implemented within the cross-validation process to avoid information leakage across folds. This approach ensures that synthetic samples were generated only from the training portion of each fold, preserving the integrity of model evaluation under imbalanced conditions.

Statistical analysis

To examine the relative contributions of different behavioral domains, four modeling modes were constructed: (M1) demographic and individual factors; (M2) demographic, individual, and risk factors; (M3) demographic, individual, and protective factors; and (M4) an integrated model incorporating all three predictor domains.

These four modeling modes were also designed to function as an ablation framework, enabling systematic evaluation of the incremental contribution of individual, risk, and protective factors. By comparing model performance across modes, we assessed how the inclusion or exclusion of each predictor domain influenced predictive performance. The overall analytical framework is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Analytical framework integrating individual, risk, and protective factors for suicidal ideation prediction

Within each analytic mode, one traditional statistical model—logistic regression (classic LR)—and five machine learning classifiers—bagged LR, random forest (RF), light gradient boosting machine (LightGBM), linear support vector machine (SVM), and radial basis function (RBF) SVM—were trained. Model training and hyperparameter optimization were conducted using 5-fold cross-validation. To enhance transparency and reproducibility, the analytical procedures for the main classifiers are summarized in pseudocode provided in the Appendix. In addition, the full implementation code is publicly available in the second author’s GitHub repository (“Suicidal_Ideation”), allowing independent verification and replication of the results.

Given the substantial class imbalance in STB outcomes, model selection prioritized the F1 score, which balances precision and recall and provides a more informative evaluation of performance for the minority class than accuracy alone. In imbalanced settings, accuracy may be inflated by the majority class, whereas F1 emphasizes the model’s ability to correctly identify high-risk individuals while controlling for false positives.

In addition to the F1 score, we reported multiple evaluation metrics, including accuracy, precision, recall, specificity, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), to provide a comprehensive assessment of model performance under class imbalance.

For the best-performing classifier within each mode, feature importance was examined using classifier-specific metrics, and SHAP values were computed to enhance interpretability and facilitate the behavioral interpretation of model outputs.

Relative importance and contribution of features in STB prediction

To enhance the interpretability of RF and LightGBM, we employed both model-specific and model-agnostic post hoc interpretability approaches. For RF, feature importance was estimated using the mean decrease in impurity (MDI), which quantifies the total reduction in node impurity (e.g., Gini impurity) attributed to each feature across all trees. For the LightGBM model, feature importance was computed based on split frequency, which was defined as the number of times a feature was selected for node splitting across the ensemble.

Because these feature importance measures are defined differently, they provide complementary perspectives on the contribution of predictors. To further improve interpretability and ensure consistency across models, we additionally computed SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values as a model-agnostic framework. SHAP values quantify the contribution of each feature to individual predictions while also indicating the direction of the effect (i.e., whether higher or lower values increase the predicted risk of STB).

The top 10 predictive features identified by RF and LightGBM in M2 and M4 are presented in Table 4, and their corresponding SHAP value distributions are illustrated in Fig. 3. Together, these approaches enable both global and local interpretation of model behavior, thereby improving the clinical interpretability of the results.

Although RF and LightGBM are both tree-based models, they rely on different learning mechanisms—bagging versus boosting—which may lead to differences in how features are prioritized. Despite these differences, a substantial degree of consistency was observed across models. As shown in Table 4, in M2, both RF and LightGBM consistently identified six key features: close to panic, lack of future hope, feelings of isolation, loss of control, lack of initiative, and feeling disconnected. In M4, three features—close to panic, lack of future hope, and self-coldness—were consistently identified across both models.

The SHAP value distributions (Fig. 3) further provide directional and magnitude-based interpretations of these predictors. In M2, higher levels of close to panic, lack of future hope, feelings of isolation, loss of control, lack of initiative, and feeling disconnected were associated with an increased predicted risk of STB. These patterns were consistent across both RF and LightGBM, indicating that the models converge on similar high-risk psychological profiles despite differences in feature importance rankings.

Importantly, these consistently identified features align closely with established suicide theories, particularly the IMV model and the three-step theory, which emphasize psychological distress, hopelessness, and social disconnection as central mechanisms underlying suicidal ideation. This convergence enhances the clinical interpretability and credibility of the model outputs.

Discussion

This study systematically and comprehensively synthesizes theoretical knowledge and empirical findings on suicide to establish a ML-based prediction model for STB among university students. The research extends prior findings in two ways: first, by employing data from Taiwanese university students—the first study of its kind—thereby enhancing external validity; and second, by constructing four analytic modes to examine the relative contributions of individual, risk, and protective factors in developing STB prediction models and identifying key predictors. Consistent with findings of studies using university samples from the United States [39], France [5], China [40], South Korea [41], and Bangladesh [28], ML algorithms demonstrated greater predictive accuracy for STB among Taiwanese university students than traditional statistical methods did. Among the algorithms applied, RF and LightGBM performed best in M2 (risk factors) and M4 (integrated individual, risk, and protective factors), with AUC values exceeding 0.80 and an accuracy above 0.95. In ML applications, such as suicide prediction [58] and pharmacological treatment [59], an accuracy above 0.80 could be considered an acceptable indicator of good performance.

From an ablation perspective, these findings suggest that risk factors provide the primary predictive signal, whereas individual and protective factors contribute complementary but less dominant information. Within the four predictive models constructed for STB among Taiwanese university students, considering individual factors alone (M1) or protective factors alone (M3) did not accurately identify students at high risk. According to suicide-related theories, the integrated motivational–volitional model of suicidal behavior [10, 14, 60] posits that individuals progress through three stages from the emergence of suicidal ideation to the enactment of suicidal behavior. The pre-motivational stage explains broad contextual influences, including personality traits (e.g., socially prescribed perfectionism) and environmental factors. The motivational stage highlights the role of self-threat variables (e.g., autobiographical memory bias and rumination) and motivational moderators (e.g., thwarted belongingness and social support) in the development of suicidal ideation and intent. The volitional stage focuses on volitional moderators that facilitate the transition from suicidal intent to behavior, encompassing environmental, psychological, social, and physiological factors such as the ability to formulate a suicide plan, exposure to suicide or related behaviors, chronic physical pain, imagining post-suicide scenarios, and prior suicide attempts. The three-step theory proposes a sequential process for the development of suicide attempts. Step one suggests that suicidal ideation arises from pain and hopelessness; when individuals experience psychological or physical discomfort, such as social isolation, failure, adversity, or negative self-perception [61], their desire for life may diminish, leading to suicidal thoughts [62]. Step two emphasizes that among those experiencing both pain and hopelessness, social connectedness serves as a crucial protective factor against the escalation of suicidal ideation, including attachments to close friends, family, work, or pets. Step three explains that the transition from suicidal ideation to suicide attempts occurs when individuals possess high suicide ability, such as habituation to or frequent exposure to pain, injury, fear, and death-related experiences, as well as when they have knowledge of specific methods of suicide. On the basis of these theories, both the IMV model and the 3ST highlight individual and protective factors as important in the formation of STB. However, these factors function primarily as antecedent or moderating variables rather than direct influences, which may explain the poor performance of M1 (individual factors) and M3 (protective factors) in predicting STB.

On the other hand, the risk-factor mode (M2) performed well, which is consistent with earlier studies emphasizing the role of risk factors in predicting STB [2, 63, 19]. Individuals with high-risk characteristics—such as affective dysregulation [25], anxiety and depression [27–29], negative life events [30, 31], perceived burdensomeness [32], history of STB in themselves or their significant others (Fergusson et al. 2003; [33]), and engagement in high-risk behaviors [64]—are more likely to exhibit elevated suicide risk. The interpersonal theory of suicide posits that when individuals experience high levels of thwarted belongingness and perceived burdensomeness, combined with hopelessness in these states, and simultaneously develop fearlessness about death and increased pain tolerance (i.e., suicide capability), they not only possess strong suicidal desire but also a sufficient capacity to engage in suicide [13]. In such circumstances, individuals face a very high risk of lethal suicidal behavior. Similarly, both the IMV model and the 3ST emphasize that stressful life events, negative emotions, psychiatric history, and prior self-harm or suicidal behaviors function as risk factors across multiple stages of STB development, particularly as proximal predictors with significant influence.

The performance of the integrated model (M4) was comparable to that of the risk-factor model (M2) and superior to that of the individual-factor (M1) and protective-factor (M3) models in detecting university students at high risk of STB. Notably, the most important predictive features in M4 included individual, risk, and protective factors. In the RF model, the top ten predictors included one item from the Self-Compassion Scale (individual factor), four items from the Depression–Anxiety–Stress Scale (risk factor), and five items from the Well-Being Scale (protective factor). In LightGBM, the top ten predictors included one item from Fear of Negative Evaluation, one item from Perfectionism, and two items from Self-Compassion (individual factors); two items from the Depression–Anxiety–Stress Scale, one item from Suicide Capability, and one item from history of suicide-related events (risk factors); and one item each from Multidimensional Perceived Social Support and Resilience (protective factors). These findings align with prior research indicating that protective factors significantly influence the prediction of STB ([65]; Fergusson et al. 2003), whereas M4 further highlights the substantial contribution of individual factors.

The present study found that both RF and LightGBM demonstrated good model performance; however, differences were observed in their identified feature importance. One possible explanation is that although RF and LightGBM are both tree-based models, they rely on different learning mechanisms—namely, bagging versus boosting—which may lead to differences in how features are prioritized. RF constructs multiple trees using bootstrap sampling and random feature selection, thereby tending to emphasize features that are consistently informative across the dataset. In contrast, LightGBM builds trees sequentially to minimize prediction error and may therefore assign greater importance to features that provide strong incremental improvements.

Furthermore, although the top 10 important features identified by RF and LightGBM differ, both models consistently capture personal, risk, and protective factors; the primary difference lies in the proportion of items selected from each factor. Among the top 10 important features, RF placed the greatest emphasis on protective factors (5 items), followed by risk factors (4 items) and personal factors (1 item). In contrast, LightGBM showed a more balanced distribution, considering protective (2 items), risk (4 items), and personal (4 items) factors. Because clinicians conducting suicide risk assessments are required to comprehensively evaluate multiple contributing factors [42, 66], the LightGBM model is more aligned with clinical assessment procedures.

Implications of clinical applicability

Contemporary university students are widely exposed to the risk of suicidal thoughts and behaviors (STB) [3–5], making the detection of STB risk on campus and suicide prevention critically important. Because STB result from the interaction of multiple influences—including individual, social, psychological, cultural, biological, and environmental factors [7, 10, 67, 68]—this study, drawing on major suicide theories (IPT, IMV, and 3ST) and M4 findings, recommends that ML algorithms for STB prediction incorporate individual, risk, and protective factors. According to the findings of this study, when detecting STB risk among university students, campus clinicians may primarily rely on clinical diagnostic information, while incorporating the outcomes of the ML-based suicide risk screening model developed in this study to improve the accuracy of decision-making in identifying students at high risk of STB.

In the context of developing suicide prevention programs, the top ten features identified in this study may serve as potential reference points for campus clinicians when considering directions for intervention planning. The predictors were primarily derived from scales such as the SCS, DASS, ULS-8, and POMS, which assess self-threat moderators, psychological pain, and psychological well-being. These constructs have been highlighted as critical predictors of STB in theoretical frameworks such as the IPT, IMV, and 3ST. Based on these findings, future university-based suicide prevention programs should not only aim to reduce individuals’ psychological pain but also enhance self-evaluation and psychological well-being.

Limitations and future directions

Although this study is the first to develop a ML model for predicting STB among Taiwanese university students by comprehensively considering the contributions of individual, risk, and protective factors, the measurement was constrained by the overall questionnaire design. As a result, not all the elements of these three critical predictors were included, such as personality traits (individual factors), hopelessness (risk factors), and attachment relationships (protective factors). Future research should build on the present findings by selecting the most important predictors and further incorporating these unmeasured variables into predictive models.

Second, this study employed cross‑sectional data to develop a risk screening algorithm for STB among university students, which limits the applicability of the findings, as the model cannot be used to detect students who may exhibit suicide-related behaviors in the future. Pigoni et al. [18] emphasized that time is a critical factor in predicting STB, requiring consideration of short-, medium-, and long-term suicide risk. Furthermore, stage-based theories of suicide (e.g., the IMV and the 3ST) delineate the developmental progression from suicidal ideation to behavior. Such frameworks underscore the necessity of longitudinal data to develop stage-specific, machine learning–based suicide risk screening models. However, previous studies have rarely collected longitudinal data to construct algorithms for predicting persistent STB risk [21] or to examine long-term stable predictors of persistent STB. Future research is therefore encouraged to collect longitudinal follow-up data to develop algorithms capable of predicting sustained suicide risk and to identify predictors with cross‑temporal stability.

Third, the data source of this study was limited to university students in Taiwan. Future research should collect samples from multiple universities across Taiwan to expand the external validity of the findings. Finally, owing to practical constraints, this study relied solely on self‑report measures to assess students’ academic performance. Subsequent research, when feasible, should incorporate objective indicators of academic achievement to more accurately evaluate the contribution of academic performance to the prediction of STB among university students.

Several methodological limitations should be acknowledged. First, the definition of suicidal thoughts and behaviors (STB) in this study was based on a screening-oriented threshold (responses > 3) rather than a clinically validated diagnostic cutoff. This criterion was selected to identify individuals who endorsed suicidal thoughts or behaviors above the midpoint of the response scale, consistent with prior research that operationalizes psychological risk based on elevated symptom endorsement rather than strict diagnostic criteria [16, 43, 69]. However, this approach may capture individuals with elevated psychological distress in addition to those at clinically meaningful risk, potentially limiting the specificity of the outcome definition. Additionally, to assess the sensitivity of the outcome definition, we conducted additional analyses using a more stringent threshold (responses > 4) to define high-risk cases. The results remained consistent, with RF and LightGBM in M2 and M4 demonstrating the best performance and largely concordant feature importance rankings, suggesting that the main conclusions are robust to variations in cutoff selection.

Second, to address class imbalance, the synthetic minority oversampling technique (SMOTE) was applied to the training data. While this approach improves model sensitivity to minority cases, synthetic samples may not fully reflect real-world data distributions and may introduce a risk of overfitting or optimistic performance estimates. In addition, although multiple evaluation metrics were reported, the precision of some models remained modest, indicating a relatively high false-positive rate. This suggests that model predictions should be interpreted with caution, particularly when identifying at-risk individuals. Future research may explore alternative strategies for handling class imbalance, such as cost-sensitive learning, focal loss, or more refined data partitioning approaches, which have been shown to improve stability and performance in related psychological prediction tasks.

Third, the models were developed and evaluated using a single dataset with internal validation only. External validation using independent samples is needed to further assess generalizability and robustness across populations and settings.

Conclusion

This study synthesized three well-known multi-stage suicide theories—the IPT, the IMV, and the 3ST—along with empirical findings to identify individual, risk, and protective predictors for developing a ML)-based model of STB among Taiwanese university students. By evaluating the performance of the four analytic modes, this study examined the relative contributions of these three factors and identified key variables for detecting students at high risk of STB. Overall, the findings suggest that STB prediction models should not only emphasize risk factors but also incorporate individual and protective factors. Furthermore, compared with traditional statistical methods, ML algorithms improved the prediction accuracy for STB, with RF and LightGBM performing best. In summary, the design and findings of this study enhance the interpretability of ML-based STB prediction models. Theoretically, they provide researchers with deeper insights to support the development of more comprehensive and systematic models. Practically, this study provides a useful screening tool for identifying college students at high risk of STB. Researchers may primarily rely on clinical diagnostic information, supplemented by the machine learning–based model developed in this study, to enhance and improve the accuracy of decision-making in identifying college students at elevated risk of STB.
