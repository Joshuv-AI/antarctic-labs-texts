Investigation of Antimicrobial Resistance Genes and Clonal Relatedness of Outbreak-Associated Salmonella Enteritidis Isolates Using PFGE
Authors: Farhad Nikkhahi, Fatemeh Zeynali Kelishomi, Mohammad Mehdi Soltan Dallal, Anita Fardsanei, Fatemeh Fardsanei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeSalmonella Enteritidis is one of the most common causes of gastroenteritis outbreaks. Detailed characterization of outbreak-associated isolates is essential for understanding local epidemiology, antimicrobial resistance patterns, and transmission dynamics. Epidemiological investigations of these strains can help to control the spread of this bacterium.MethodsIn the current study, 27 strains of S. Enteritidis were isolated from patients with gastroenteritis. Antibiotic resistance of these strains was determined using phenotypic and genotypic methods. Biofilm formation ability was evaluated using the microtiter method. The strains were clustered using pulsed-field gel electrophoresis (PFGE) with AvrII.ResultsThe highest rate of antibiotic resistance was associated with cefuroxime (74.1%). Five antibiotic-resistance genes were detected, and all of them were blaTEM. Of 27 strains, 96.3% were biofilm producers. According to PFGE results, the studied strains were all included in one clone.ConclusionThis study provides data on the antimicrobial susceptibility and high biofilm-forming capacity of outbreak-associated S. Enteritidis isolates in Iran. The limited genetic diversity observed by PFGE highlights the clonal nature of these strains and suggests that combining PFGE with additional DNA-based methods is beneficial for an accurate diagnosis.Keywords: Salmonella enterica serotype Enteritidis, PFGE, biofilm formation, antibiotic resistance, outbreak

Introduction

Salmonella is a genus of the Enterobacteriaceae family that includes motile and facultative anaerobic bacteria. Salmonella is one of the most important foodborne pathogens that causes food poisoning and gastroenteritis.1 The prevalence of non-typhoidal Salmonella that causes diarrhea is 93.8 million worldwide, and the annual death rate is 155,000.2 Symptoms of Salmonella gastroenteritis include diarrhea (more than three times in 24 h), nausea, fever, vomiting, and abdominal cramps.3 Gastroenteritis caused by Salmonella is mostly self-limiting and does not require antibiotic treatment. However, in cases such as children, the elderly, and immunocompromised patients, it can be life-threatening.4 S. Enteritidis is the most common serovar in Europe and Asia and is associated with most outbreaks.5,6

Patients typically respond well to antibiotic treatment. However, the increasing spread of antibiotic resistance is a severe challenge. Antibiotics and growth factors are used as therapeutic agents in chicken farms, which can be one of the reasons for the spread of antibiotic resistance.7 Antibiotic resistance in Salmonella occurs primarily because of the transfer of resistance genes by plasmids.8 Other resistance mechanisms include chromosomal mutations, antibiotic enzyme inactivation, efflux pumps, and changes in target proteins.9,10

On the other hand, bacteria produce biofilm structures to protect their populations. Biofilm production adversely affects public health, industry, and the environment.11 Biofilm not only makes it difficult to remove bacteria, but the bacteria inside the biofilm have higher resistance to chemical agents like antibiotics and even the human immune system. Higher resistance of bacteria inside the biofilm causes chronic infections in the human body.12,13

Various methods have been proposed for the accurate detection of Salmonella strains. Routine procedures, such as biotyping, serotyping, phage typing, and plasmid analysis, do not have high discrimination power.14 Molecular methods including ribotyping, plasmid profiling,15,16 and PFGE,17 have been widely used and provide valuable epidemiological information. In recent years, high-resolution genomic approaches, particularly whole-genome sequencing (WGS) and core-genome multilocus sequence typing (cgMLST), have largely replaced PFGE as the gold standard for outbreak investigation and surveillance because of their superior discriminatory power. Nevertheless, PFGE remains a practical and informative tool in many laboratories, especially in resource-limited settings where WGS is not routinely available. The purpose of this study was to evaluate the antibiotic resistance of Salmonella Enteritidis, its ability to produce biofilm, and the epidemiological relationship of isolates from patients with gastroenteritis in four cities of Iran.

Materials and Methods

Bacterial Strains

The current study was performed by approval of the Ethics Committee of Qazvin University of Medical Sciences with approval number IR.QUMS.REC.1404.421. In this study, we used 27 strains of Salmonella Enteritidis from our previous study.18 These strains were isolated from gastroenteritis patients during outbreaks that occurred in 2017 in four cities of Iran (Karaj, Hamedan, Semnan, and Yazd). Based on available epidemiological data, the outbreaks were considered independent events with no identified common source. These samples were identified using serological methods.19 Multiplex PCR was performed to confirm the results.20

Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing

Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines and the Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion method were used to determine the antibiotic susceptibility of the strains.21 The antibiotics used were chloramphenicol (30 μg), imipenem (10 μg), meropenem (10 μg), tetracycline (30 μg), streptomycin (10 μg), cefuroxime (30 μg), ceftazidime (30 μg), cefotaxime (30 μg), cefepime (30 μg), amoxicillin (20 μg), trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (25 μg), and ciprofloxacin (5 μg) (Mast Group Ltd., UK). Phenotypic detection of extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL) and AmpC beta-lactamase producers was also performed according to the literature. Escherichia coli ATCC 25922 was used as quality control.

Molecular Detection of Antimicrobial Resistance Genes

The QIAamp DNA extraction kit (Qiagen, Germany) was used to extract the genomic DNA, and PCR was performed to detect antimicrobial resistance genes. Detailed information about the primer sequences and annealing temperatures is shown in Table 1. PCR products were visualized using 1% agarose gel, green viewer stain, and a UV-transilluminator, and the results were confirmed by Sanger sequencing of each detected gene.

Biofilm Formation

A quantitative assessment of the biofilm production capacity of the strains was performed according to previous studies.31 Briefly, after overnight culture of the samples in 96-well polystyrene plates, the formed biofilms were fixed with methanol, stained with crystal violet 0.1% solution (w/v), and the bound dye was dissolved in 33% glacial acetic acid. The optical density (OD) was measured at 570 nm using an ELISA reader, and the results were interpreted according to a study by Zeynali Kelishomi et al.31

Pulsed-Field Gel Electrophoresis (PFGE)

Fingerprinting of the strains was performed using the PFGE method according to the PulseNet protocol.32 In a previous study, we used the restriction enzyme XbaI (5´-TCTAGA-3´) to digest the genomic DNA and observed that a large proportion of the isolates shared an identical pulsotype. In the present study, PFGE was performed using 30 U of AvrII (5´-CCTAGG-3´) for strains with similar XbaI profiles in an attempt to achieve further discrimination. Electrophoresis was performed using a CHEF DRIII CHILLER (Bio-Rad, CA, USA) for 18 h on a 1% agarose gel at a voltage of 6 V/cm and an angle of 120°. The parameters were set as 2.2–63.8. Gels were stained with ethidium bromide for 30 min, washed with distilled water, and analyzed using a UV transilluminator. The patterns were obtained using standard molecular markers and measuring them through GelCompar II software. Then, a dendrogram was constructed using the unweighted-pair group method with average linkage (UPGMA) and Dice coefficients.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were performed using SPSS Version 16. The data are presented as percentages and frequencies. PFGE patterns were analysed with GelCompar II software.

Results

Characteristics of Isolates

Of the 27 isolates, 13 were isolated from females and 14 from males. All 27S. Enteritidis strains were susceptible to cefotaxime, imipenem, meropenem, chloramphenicol, and tetracycline. Resistance rates were as follows: cefuroxime (74.1%), ciprofloxacin (48.2%), streptomycin (22.2%), amoxicillin (14.8%), ceftazidime (3.7%); ceftriaxone (3.7%); cefepime (3.7%); and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (3.7%). No ESBL-or AmpC beta-lactamase-producing strains were found. Based on the sensitivity to the studied antibiotics, the strains were classified into nine different antibiotypes (Table 2).

Discussion

Over the past few years, S. Enteritidis has become the most common serovar causing outbreaks. Understanding the characteristics of this serovar can help us quickly diagnose food poisoning, control the outbreak, and prevent its spread. This study was conducted on 27 strains isolated from hospitals in four cities of Iran in 2017.

Excessive use of antibiotics increases antibiotic resistance and medical costs and causes treatment failure.33 This study investigated the antibiotic resistance of S. Enteritidis strains using the disk diffusion method. The highest antibiotic resistance rate was reported for ciprofloxacin and cefuroxime, which is concerning because cephalosporins and fluoroquinolones are the drugs of choice for treating Salmonella-related infections. Ciprofloxacin is widely used in poultry farms. High resistance to ciprofloxacin has been reported in Iran,34 the United States,35 Saudi Arabia,36 and Greece,37 and high resistance to cefuroxime has been reported in Saudi Arabia,36 Greece,37 and Iran.34 Resistance to third-generation cephalosporins is approximately 3.5% in the United States and 1.8% in Europe.38,39 Fortunately, similar to previous studies from Iran34 and Saudi Arabia,36 a high sensitivity rate to other cephalosporins (third and fourth generation) was observed, indicating that these antibiotics can still be used in the treatment of salmonellosis. Aminoglycosides are used as growth promoters in chickens, so the resistance to this antibiotic family has increased.40 In the present study, 22% of the strains showed resistance to streptomycin. Other studies have also reported higher resistance rates;34,36 however, Han et al reported low resistance to streptomycin in the United States.35

In the present study, no strains were resistant to imipenem and meropenem, which indicates that resistance to these antibiotics is still very rare in S. Enteritidis owing to the limitation in prescribing carbapenems.41 The most effective antibiotics against the studied strains were cefotaxime, imipenem, meropenem, chloramphenicol, and tetracycline. Fortunately, the prevalence of antibiotic resistance genes in the strains was very low, with only one gene (blaTEM) detected in five isolates. In another study from Iran, the most prevalent genes were blaCTX-M (20.8%), sul1 (10.7%), and blaTEM (10.4%).42 In studies from China43 and Egypt,44 a high rate of resistance genes has been reported. These results show that, despite the high prevalence of this serovar, its antibiotic resistance is still not widespread in Iran. Nevertheless, a regulator is necessary to monitor the use of antibiotics in both industry and medicine to ensure their appropriate use.

Despite the high phenotypic resistance to cefuroxime (74.1%), only five isolates carried the blaTEM gene, and none of the other tested β-lactamase genes were detected. This phenotype–genotype discrepancy indicates that resistance in most isolates is likely mediated by mechanisms not investigated in the present study, including other β-lactamase variants, chromosomal mutations, overexpression of efflux pumps, or alterations in outer-membrane porins. Further investigation using broader gene panels or whole-genome sequencing would be required to elucidate these additional resistance mechanisms.45,46

The treatment of bacteria capable of biofilm production has become a severe challenge in industry, the environment, and medicine. In the present study, 96.3% of the strains were biofilm producers. Zhou et al reported that 100% of the strains were able to produce biofilm.43 In a study from Greece, the ability of S. Enteritidis to produce biofilms was reported to be 62%.47 Another study from Iran reported that 39.28% of the strains were biofilm producers.42 This difference may be due to the source of isolation of samples and the way antibiotics are used in different geographical areas.

The high prevalence of biofilm formation observed in these outbreak-associated isolates has important biological and clinical implications. Biofilm production enhances the ability of S. Enteritidis to survive on abiotic surfaces, in food-processing environments, and under stressful conditions, thereby facilitating environmental persistence and potential transmission during outbreaks.11,12 Bacteria embedded in biofilms exhibit increased tolerance to disinfectants and host immune defenses, which may contribute to prolonged survival and recurrent infections.12,13 In this study, the high overall rate of biofilm formation among both resistant and susceptible isolates is noteworthy. Biofilm-mediated tolerance can reduce the effectiveness of antibiotics even in the absence of classical resistance genes, potentially complicating treatment and outbreak control.48 These findings highlight the need for continued attention to biofilm-forming capacity in the surveillance and management of S. Enteritidis outbreaks.

Epidemiological studies are very important for determining the origin of infectious diseases and facilitating the control of the their spread. PFGE has long been regarded as a gold-standard method for typing Salmonella strains because of its relatively high discriminatory power for many serovars. However, S. Enteritidis is known to exhibit low genetic diversity and high clonality, with isolates often showing up to 94% genetic similarity.18 The results of the present study are consistent with these observations: the majority of isolates that shared an identical XbaI profile remained indistinguishable after digestion with AvrII, resulting in a single large cluster. Despite the use of two enzymes, the power of differentiation did not increase. These findings reinforce the well-recognized limitations of PFGE for S. Enteritidis and support the recommendation that PFGE be combined with higher-resolution genome-based methods, such as whole-genome sequencing or cgMLST, whenever more precise strain differentiation is required.

Although the present study provides useful phenotypic and molecular data on outbreak-associated S. Enteritidis isolates, several limitations should be acknowledged. The relatively small number of isolates limits the generalizability of the findings. The analyses relied on conventional methods, including disk diffusion, PCR detection of a selected panel of resistance genes, and PFGE. These approaches are less discriminatory than high-resolution genomic techniques such as WGS and cgMLST, which have become the gold standard for outbreak investigation and surveillance in many well-resourced countries.

Despite these constraints, the results offer valuable region-specific information on antimicrobial susceptibility, the high prevalence of biofilm formation, and the marked clonal relatedness of S. Enteritidis strains circulating in Iran. In resource-limited settings where WGS is not routinely accessible, the combination of phenotypic testing, targeted PCR, and PFGE continues to provide practical data for local outbreak investigation and surveillance. Future studies incorporating whole-genome sequencing would substantially enhance discriminatory power and biological interpretation.

Conclusions

This study provides valuable data on the antimicrobial susceptibility profiles and biofilm-forming ability of outbreak-associated S. Enteritidis isolates. Although PFGE with XbaI and AvrII demonstrated limited discriminatory power due to the high clonality of the isolates, the combined phenotypic and molecular approach used here remains a practical and informative tool for characterizing S. Enteritidis in outbreak settings. Advanced genome-based methods offer higher resolution and should be encouraged for future surveillance and outbreak investigations whenever resources allow; however, their routine application may still be constrained by cost and technical capacity in some laboratories. Therefore, integrating conventional phenotypic testing with accessible molecular methods continues to provide important epidemiological insights while higher-resolution genomic approaches are progressively adopted.
