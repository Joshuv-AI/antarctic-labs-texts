Sociodemographic characteristics, clinical manifestations, and concurrent outcomes of Oropouche fever and dengue in Espírito Santo, Brazil
Authors: João Paulo Cola, Thiago Nascimento do Prado, Otavio T. Ranzani, Ana Paula Brioschi dos Santos, Brenda Silva Freire, Raphael Lubiana Zanotti, Adriana Endlich da Silva Dela Costa, Karina Bertazo Del Carro, Lesliane de Amorim Lacerda Coelho, Cintia Lepaus Thomas, Dijoce Prates Bezerra, Orlei Amaral Cardoso, Angelica Espinosa Miranda, Ethel Leonor Noia Maciel, Creuza Rachel Vicente
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjective.To compare sociodemographic and clinical characteristics and the outcome severity between confirmed cases of Oropouche fever and dengue in the state of Espírito Santo, Brazil.Methods.A cross-sectional study used secondary data from the state health surveillance system of Espírito Santo, 2024–2025. The study includes laboratory-confirmed cases of Oropouche fever and dengue. The authors calculated absolute and relative frequencies of variables. Pearson’s chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests assessed differences. Firth’s penalized logistic regression estimated odds ratios (OR) for severe manifestations and death, with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI).Results.A total of 12 135 OF cases and 18 018 dengue cases were confirmed. Compared with dengue cases, Oropouche fever was more prevalent among males (53.5%), white individuals (56.3%), and residents of rural and peri-urban areas (52.1%). Fever (84.5% vs. 78.7%), headache (83.9% vs. 72.1%), and myalgia (76.5% vs. 67.5%) were the most common symptoms in both groups, with significantly higher proportions among Oropouche fever cases. Dengue cases showed higher odds of progressing to severe disease (OR 15.35; 95% CI 10.60–22.23) and death (OR 2.09; 95% CI 1.10–3.98).Conclusion.Oropouche fever and dengue presented overlapping clinical profiles; however, Oropouche fever was more frequent in rural and peri-urban areas. Dengue was associated with greater severity and lethality. These findings underscore the importance of surveillance to support strategies for monitoring, clinical management, and control, while accounting for the distinct epidemiological and clinical profiles of Oropouche fever and dengue.

INTRODUCTION

Since its discovery in Trinidad and Tobago in the 1950s, the Oropouche virus (OROV) has remained localized and caused outbreaks in the Amazon region of Latin America (1, 2). OROV produces an acute febrile illness that resembles other arboviral infections, such as Zika, dengue, and chikungunya (1, 2). However, it is transmitted by a different vector; Culicoides paraensis is the main vector of the urban/epidemic cycle (1). The most frequently reported symptoms following OROV infection include fever, headache, myalgia, orbital pain, nausea, and vomiting (3). Reports have also documented vertical transmission (4, 5), congenital malformations (4, 5), and neurological (6) and hemorrhagic (7) manifestations.

In Brazil, OROV initially spread within the Amazon region. However, in 2024, outbreaks were recorded outside this area (8). The expansion may be related to viral genetic recombination of the M1L2S2 lineage (9). However, the available evidence does not establish a causal link demonstrating that this lineage was solely responsible for the virus’s expansion (9). In Espírito Santo (ES) state in southeastern Brazil, the virus spread rapidly, particularly in rural and peri-urban areas, as well as in regions with banana, coffee, and cacao plantations, which reported the highest number of Oropouche fever (OF) cases in the country (10).

Between 2024 and 2025, ES state experienced an unprecedented and concerning epidemiological scenario, characterized by the largest OF epidemic ever documented outside the Amazon in Brazil, which occurred concomitantly with the state’s largest dengue epidemic on record (11). Cases of arboviral coinfection were also documented in ES, most notably dengue and chikungunya and dengue and OROV coinfections (12). This context led to a substantial number of cases and directly strained the health care system. Notably, this period registered a high number of dengue-related deaths (11), as well as the first reported deaths from OF in both Brazil (7) and ES (13).

In several recent outbreaks outside the Amazon region, including ES, OF and dengue have occurred concurrently, creating major challenges for epidemiological surveillance, timely diagnosis, and appropriate clinical management (8). The co-circulation of these arboviruses may compromise control efforts, as they are transmitted by different vectors and require distinct strategies. At the clinical level, overlapping symptoms complicate differential diagnosis, often delaying appropriate treatment and increasing the risk of severe disease (3). This scenario not only increases the risk of disease progression but also strains health services, which must follow parallel protocols and care pathways. Comparing dengue and OF is therefore particularly relevant, given their shared geography, overlapping clinical features, and combined burden on surveillance, patient care, and health system capacity.

A joint analysis of these diseases can support more effective prevention and control strategies, optimize laboratory and health care resources, and guide the development of appropriate clinical protocols, especially in regions experiencing simultaneous outbreaks, as occurred in ES. The objective of this study is to compare sociodemographic and clinical characteristics, as well as severity and mortality outcomes, between confirmed cases of OF and dengue in ES, Brazil.

METHODS

Study Design and Setting

We conducted a cross-sectional study using secondary data from the ES State Health Surveillance System (eSUS-VS). The State Department of Health (SESA-ES) provided the data. The e-SUS VS system was implemented in the state of ES in January 2020, replacing the Brazilian Ministry of Health’s Information System for Notifiable Diseases (Sistema de Informação de Agravos de Notificação—SINAN). eSUS-VS is the only official system for the compulsory notification of diseases in the state and was developed in partnership with the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO). It is important to note that in March 2024, ES implemented reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) testing for the detection of OROV. Since then, all suspected arboviral cases in the state have been prospectively tested to OROV.

ES is a coastal state in southeastern Brazil, with approximately 4.1 million inhabitants distributed across 78 municipalities. The state covers an area of 46 000 km², encompassing both urban centers and rural areas dedicated to agriculture, particularly coffee, cacao, and banana production. ES has a tropical climate, with marked seasonal rainfall patterns.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

This study included all symptomatic cases with suspected arboviral infection that were laboratory-confirmed for OF or dengue and reported to eSUS-VS between March 20, 2024, and May 30, 2025. Non-autochthonous cases of dengue and OF were excluded. This study classified cases as non-autochthonous when, after investigation, the probable place of infection was outside ES or the individual’s official residence was outside ES.

In public health surveillance, a suspected arboviral case is defined as an individual with fever (typically lasting 2 to 7 days) and at least two of the following signs or symptoms: nausea or vomiting, rash, myalgia or arthralgia, headache or retro-orbital pain, petechiae, or a positive tourniquet test (14). However, in practice, clinical suspicion does not always fully meet these criteria and may include presentations without fever or with fewer accompanying symptoms.

The authors classified as laboratory-confirmed OF cases those with OROV RNA detected by RT-PCR in serum samples collected up to 5 days after symptom onset, or in urine or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) samples collected up to 15 days after onset. For dengue cases, this study considered laboratory confirmation by viral RNA detection via RT-PCR, NS1 antigen positivity in serum samples collected within 5 days of symptom onset, or reactive IgM, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, antibodies after this period.

This study classified severe manifestations of dengue cases as those with warning signs or severe dengue, according to Brazilian Ministry of Health guidelines (14). The authors classified severe manifestations of OF cases as those requiring hospitalization or presenting with signs or symptoms compatible with encephalitis, hemorrhagic manifestations, hepatic dysfunction, renal dysfunction, or decompensation of pre-existing comorbidities. The Special Epidemiological Surveillance Unit of Espírito Santo’s State Health Department investigated all deaths from OF and dengue and classified them as disease-related or due to other causes.

The authors classify residential areas as urban areas, which are densely built with concentrated population and services. Rural areas are sparsely populated and dominated by agriculture or natural lands. Peri-urban areas are transition zones on the urban periphery, blending urban and rural characteristics.

Statistical Analysis

This study constructed an epidemic curve showing the temporal distribution of dengue and OF cases by epidemiological week of symptom onset. Absolute (N) and relative (%) frequencies were conducted for categorical variables. Pearson’s chi-square test and Fisher’s the mean and standard deviation were calculated.

The authors applied Firth’s penalized logistic regression models to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) for severe manifestations and death, comparing dengue to OF cases. The models were adjusted for age and the presence of ≥1 comorbidity.

Incidence maps were constructed from georeferenced data at the municipal level on reported OF and dengue cases in ES. The maps highlight the Legal Amazon territory and ES with its metropolitan region. The study then represented the spatial distribution of absolute case counts and incidence rates (per 10 000 inhabitants) in municipal-level maps, using graded color scales to indicate intensity of case occurrence. The maps were generated using R software (version 4.5.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

All analyses were performed using Stata v.14.0 (Stata Corp, College Station, TX, USA), with p-values <0.05 considered as statistically significant.

Ethical Considerations

This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Centro de Ciências da Saúde da Universidade Federal do Espírito Santo (UFES) with Certificate of Presentation of Ethical Assessment (CAAE) number 70443223.5.0000.5060, and the requirement for informed consent was waived. All data were anonymized to ensure patient confidentiality.

RESULTS

Epidemic Dynamics

Between March 20, 2024, and May 30, 2025, a total of 12 135 laboratory-confirmed cases of OF and 18 018 laboratory-confirmed cases of dengue were reported in ES. Five non-autochthonous OF cases and 230 non-autochthonous dengue cases were excluded. All OF cases were confirmed by RT-PCR. Among dengue cases, 4 125 (22.9%) were confirmed by RT-PCR, 6 944 (38.5%) by NS1 testing, and 6 949 (38.6%) by IgM testing.

The authors observed a sharp increase in confirmed OF cases starting in epidemiological week 43 of 2024, with continuous growth and evident acceleration between weeks 47 and 52. Transmission peaked in week 2 of 2025, with 938 confirmed cases. Between weeks 47 of 2024 and 5 of 2025, more than 6 000 OF cases were reported, representing the main epidemic period. From week 6 of 2025 onward, a sustained downward trend was observed, although viral circulation persisted until week 22 of 2025. In contrast, dengue cases were concentrated between epidemiological weeks 13 and 41 of 2024, peaking in week 13 with 2,134 cases, followed by a gradual decline and stabilization at low levels from week 31 onward (Figure 1).

Distribution of cases by epidemiological week

The spatial distribution of OF and dengue cases in ES revealed distinct patterns. OF had the highest incidence in inland municipalities, particularly rural and peri-urban transition areas, forming localized high-rate clusters outside the metropolitan region. By contrast, dengue displayed a more diffuse pattern, with higher incidence concentrated in the metropolitan corridor and urban centers. Municipalities with the greatest OF incidence generally coincided with areas of lower dengue incidence, indicating distinct and geographic risk profiles (Figure 2).

Table 1 presents the sociodemographic characteristics of confirmed OF and dengue cases. The mean age was similar between OF (42 years; SD ±18.1) and dengue (40 years; SD ±20.2). The age distribution differed significantly between the two arboviruses: OF cases were more frequent among adults aged 20–39 years (34.5%) and 40–59 years (34.8%), whereas dengue cases were more heterogeneous, with the highest proportion among individuals aged 20–39 years (31.1%).

With respect to sex, OF was more frequent among men (53.5%) and pregnant individuals (1.9%), while dengue predominated in women. Regarding race/skin color, most OF cases occurred in white individuals (56.3%), whereas dengue predominated among non-white individuals (59.6%). Residential areas also showed a marked difference: more than half the OF cases occurred in rural and peri-urban areas (52.1%), whereas dengue cases were predominantly urban (84.2%).

For clinical presentation, fever, headache, and myalgia were the most prevalent symptoms in both groups, but their proportions were significantly higher among OF cases: fever (84.5% vs. 78.7%), headache (83.9% vs. 72.1%), and myalgia (76.5% vs. 67.5%). Other symptoms were also more frequent in OF, such as nausea (37.1% vs. 34.8%), retro-orbital pain (35.7% vs. 31.2%), and back pain (31.5% vs. 27.2%). Conversely, some symptoms were more common in dengue patients, including skin rash (5.8% vs. 2.8%) and petechiae (6.4% vs. 2.5%) (Table 1).

The presence of at least one comorbidity was similar between OF (11.6%) and dengue (10.2%). Hypertension was the most prevalent comorbidity in both groups, and it was more frequent in OF cases (10.2% vs. 8.8%) (Table 1).

Incidence of Severe Clinical Manifestations and Death

Dengue patients presented a higher frequency of severe manifestations (632/18 018; 3.5%) compared with OF (29/12 135; 0.2%). Although both diseases showed low case fatality rates, dengue-related deaths were more frequent (20/18 018; 0.1%) than OF-related deaths (2/12 135; 0.02%) (Table 1).

In the unadjusted Firth’s penalized logistic regression analysis, dengue cases showed higher odds of evolving with severe manifestations (OR 2.70; 95% CI 2.33–3.07) and disease-related death (OR 1.64; 95% CI 1.03–2.29) compared with OF. After adjusting for age and the presence of ≥1 comorbidity, dengue cases maintained significantly higher odds of severe manifestations (OR 15.35; 95% CI 10.60–22.23) and disease-related death (OR 2.09; 95% CI 1.10–3.98) (Table 2).

DISCUSSION

The ES experienced, for the first time and concurrently, the largest epidemic of OF recorded outside the Amazon region in Brazil, and the most intense dengue epidemic ever documented in the state between 2024 and 2025. We observed broad dissemination of OF in ES. For both OF and dengue, fever, headache, and myalgia were the most common manifestations. However, dengue cases exhibited a higher likelihood of progressing to severe clinical manifestations and of death from the illness. OF cases predominated among young adults, males, and residents of rural and peri-urban areas, findings that align with studies from the Legal Amazon, an administrative region of the Brazilian Amazon encompassing nine states, and other parts of Latin America (15, 16).

The epidemic pattern documented suggests seasonal transmission, similar to other arboviruses, characterized by the rapid spread and concentration of cases in the early months of the year, i.e., corresponding to the rainy/warm season in tropical and subtropical regions. These findings underscore the need for active surveillance and targeted preventive measures during high-risk periods.

OF predominated in small municipalities, particularly those with extensive banana and coffee plantations, environments that favor vector proliferation (8). This pattern suggests that agricultural practices, together with climatic changes that may increase vector density outside the Amazon, have contributed to local establishment of the virus in ES (17). This hypothesis is consistent with our observation of higher OF frequency in rural and peri-urban zones, particularly in the municipalities of Alfredo Chaves, Laranja da Terra, Iconha, Itarana, and Itaguaçu, which recorded the highest OF incidence (18).

The authors found distinct sociodemographic profiles for dengue and OF. Dengue occurred more frequently in females and was concentrated in urban areas, reflecting the urban ecology of Aedes aegypti (19). Nonetheless, an ongoing inward spread of dengue into peri-urban and rural settings was documented, indicating a possible expansion of transmission beyond traditional urban centers. This phenomenon may partly reflect the involvement of Aedes albopictus in peri-urban and rural transmission and highlights the need to expand surveillance and control measures into areas that have been historically underserved (20).

The sex distribution differences between the two arboviruses may relate to occupational exposures. Prior studies have linked OF occurrence to banana, coffee, and cacao plantations, rural environments in which men are more frequently engaged in harvesting and crop management and therefore more exposed to the Culicoides paraensis vector (8). Occupational exposure may thus explain the male predominance in OF (21). In contrast, dengue’s predominantly urban transmission and greater impact on women may reflect differences in housing, mobility, and domestic exposure to Aedes aegypti.

Clinically, most cases presented symptoms common to other arboviral infections, fever, headache, and myalgia. Although statistically significant differences in symptom frequencies were found between OF and dengue, their clinical similarity makes the differential diagnosis challenging in areas with co-circulation of other arboviruses such as chikungunya, dengue, and Zika. Systematic reviews (3, 22) comparing OF to chikungunya, dengue, and Zika report no clear distinguishing symptom profile, which may lead to underreporting of OF in regions endemic for other arboviruses, especially where specific laboratory testing is unavailable.

Laboratory diagnosis of arboviral infections still faces important access barriers, complicating differentiation between clinically similar diseases such as dengue and OF. Serological and immunochromatographic tests for dengue are widely available in Brazil and facilitate early detection; however, these methods have limitations in sensitivity and specificity depending on the timing of sample collection relative to symptom onset (23). Molecular assays (RT-PCR) are more effective for acute-phase differential diagnosis and viral detection but require specific conditions, timely sample transport, and specialized laboratory infrastructure, limitations that restrict access, particularly outside large urban centers. Despite these constraints, clinical guidelines advise that the absence of laboratory confirmation should not preclude consideration of alternate arboviral infections; clinical management should therefore rely on syndromic assessment and the presence of warning signs.

The systematic adoption of RT-PCR for OROV diagnosis in ES enabled laboratory confirmation of cases and helped delineate the OF outbreak. Nevertheless, reliance on early testing and the lack of widely available serological assays remain obstacles to large-scale OROV surveillance. These findings illustrate the importance of laboratory capacity and coordination between surveillance and clinical services when confronting emerging arboviruses.

The authors observed a higher frequency of severe manifestations and greater case fatality associated with dengue compared with OF. These associations remained elevated after adjustment for age and the presence of comorbidities, reinforcing dengue’s higher clinical severity potential. At the same time, the results highlight that OF can also lead to severe disease, particularly among older adults and individuals with comorbidities. Another plausible explanation is that a substantial proportion of dengue cases may have been secondary infections, which are known to increase the risk of severe disease, given that ES is dengue-endemic and has experienced sustained increases in dengue cases since 2023. By contrast, OF cases likely reflected first-time exposures to OROV, and the occurrence and clinical impact of possible secondary OROV infections remain uncertain (24).

Although OF has traditionally been described as a self-limited disease, this study identified severe manifestations and deaths due to OROV infection; two documented deaths were attributed to OF. In 2024, ES faced its largest dengue epidemic (11), with elevated lethality, and the broader implementation of OROV testing in Brazil that year improved laboratory surveillance of suspected arboviral deaths, enabling the confirmation of OF-attributed fatalities. Case reports in the literature describe fatal OF courses marked by acute hepatic failure and hemorrhagic manifestations, suggesting a possible hepatic tropism of OROV or an exaggerated inflammatory response in susceptible individuals (7, 13).

This OF clinical trajectory resembles severe dengue, which can also present with hepatic dysfunction, coagulopathy, and hemorrhage (25). Given the clinical overlap between these conditions, clinicians should consider OROV in management algorithms for arboviral infections with potential severity, particularly in settings of viral co-circulation and in patients with risk factors for complications.

In endemic or co-circulating contexts, maintaining high clinical sensitivity for severe cases is essential to prevent deaths. Incorporating suspected or confirmed OF cases into dengue clinical management pathways may improve patient outcomes (26). Further investigations into the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying severity in OROV infection are necessary to inform more targeted clinical interventions.

This study makes a meaningful public health contribution by documenting the emergence of OF as a relevant threat outside the Amazon, with a rapid spread in rural and peri-urban areas. By comparing the clinical-epidemiological profiles and outcomes of OF and dengue, the findings emphasize the importance of differential diagnosis in co-circulation settings and highlight the risks of underreporting and inappropriate clinical management. The identification of severe manifestations and deaths due to OF supports the need to revise clinical and surveillance protocols to include this arbovirus in clinical management flows.

This study has some limitations. The use of secondary surveillance data may have led to incomplete reporting, particularly of mild cases, and the confirmation strategy could have introduced selection bias by excluding cases without laboratory confirmation. In addition, the reliance on IgM serology may have caused misclassification, as IgM can persist for several months after acute infection and may exhibit cross-reactivity, especially among flaviviruses, potentially yielding false-positive results and biasing estimates.

The findings reflect the epidemiological context of ES and may not be generalizable to regions with different surveillance or diagnostic capacities. It is important to acknowledge that surveillance coverage and notification capacity likely differ across municipalities, particularly between more developed urban centers and less-resourced rural or peri-urban areas. In regions less equipped for diagnosis and reporting, OF cases may go undetected or misclassified.

In conclusion, OF disseminated widely in ES, primarily affecting young adult males living in rural and peri-urban areas. Most cases were mild and clinically similar to other arboviruses, although severe cases and deaths occurred. OF and dengue presented overlapping clinical profiles, but dengue was associated with greater severity and lethality.

These results reinforce the need for active surveillance to appropriately guide surveillance, clinical care, and control measures. In this context, active surveillance entails proactive case finding, strengthening syndromic surveillance for acute febrile illness in primary care, conducting field investigations in areas with suspected transmission, and expanding and expediting laboratory diagnosis for arboviral diseases.
