Multilevel randomized quasi-Monte Carlo estimator for nested integration
Arved Bartuska #,1,2 , André Gustavo Carlon 1 , Luis Espath 3 , Sebastian Krumscheid 5 , & Raúl Tempone 1,2,4

Abstract
. Nested integration problems arise in various scientific and engineering applications, including Bayesian experimental design, financial risk assessment, and uncertainty quantification. These nested integrals take the form ∫ f ⁡ ( ∫ g ⁡ ( 𝒚 , 𝒙 ) ​ 𝑑 𝒙 ) ​ 𝑑 𝒚 \int f\left(\int g(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}{}\boldsymbol{x}\right)\,\mathrm{d}{}\boldsymbol{y} , for nonlinear f f , making them computationally challenging, particularly in high-dimensional settings. Although widely used for single integrals, traditional Monte Carlo (MC) methods can be inefficient when encountering complexities of nested integration. This work introduces a novel multilevel estimator, combining deterministic and randomized quasi-MC (rQMC) methods to handle nested integration problems efficiently. In this context, the inner number of samples and the discretization accuracy of the inner integrand evaluation constitute the level. We provide a comprehensive theoretical analysis of the estimator, deriving error bounds demonstrating significant reductions in bias and variance compared with standard methods. The proposed estimator is particularly effective in scenarios where the integrand is evaluated approximately, as it adapts to different levels of resolution without compromising precision. We verify the performance of our method via numerical experiments, focusing on estimating the expected information gain of experiments. When applied to Gaussian noise in the experiment, a truncation scheme ensures finite error bounds. The results reveal that the proposed multilevel rQMC estimator outperforms existing MC and rQMC approaches, offering a substantial reduction in computational costs and offering a powerful tool for practitioners dealing with complex, nested integration problems across various domains. 1.
Introduction

In this work, we address the computational challenges of nested integration problems of the form

\int f\left(\int g(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}{}\boldsymbol{x}\right)\,\mathrm{d}{}\boldsymbol{y}
where
f
is a nonlinear function. Such problems frequently arise in engineering, mathematical finance, and medical decision-making. Traditional quadrature methods are constrained by the curse of dimensionality, making them impractical as the dimension increases. In contrast, Monte Carlo (MC) methods offer a more scalable solution but may become less efficient when managing complex nested structures. Our goal is to improve the efficiency of these estimators by developing particularly tailored, hierarchical quasi-MC (QMC) techniques.

The MC estimators are widely used for standard (i.e., nonnested) integration problems due to their scalability. However, achieving a desired error tolerance
TOL>0
with standard MC comes with a computational cost of
\mathcal{O}(TOL^{-2})
. The QMC methods, utilizing low-discrepancy sequences, can provide improved convergence for smooth integrands, reducing computational effort for instance to almost
\mathcal{O}(TOL^{-1})
, where the multiplicative constant usually depends exponentially on the dimension of the integration domain. Dimension-independent constant terms are possible for certain integrands when weighted function spaces are employed
[
21
]
. Moreover, higher-order QMC methods permit even faster convergence for certain integrands
[
21
]
.
Despite these improvements, challenges remain, especially when dealing with singularities. Indeed, standard error bounds for QMC methods via the Koksma–Hlawka inequality
[
24
]
become obsolete for singular integrands that exhibit infinite Hardy–Krause variation
[
15
]
. A useful error bound for such integrands was introduced by Owen
[
26
]
and later generalized by He et al.
[
27
]
.

However, randomized QMC (rQMC) methods combine the efficiency of QMC with important properties of MC methods, including its unbiasedness and practical, variance-based error estimates. Moreover, the computational cost of such methods can be as low as almost
\mathcal{O}(TOL^{-2/3})
for highly regular integrands. Nevertheless, for nested integration, the nonlinear function
f
that evaluates the inner integrand introduces a bias in the outer integral estimation, even if the inner integral is estimated using an unbiased method. Thus, the number of inner and outer samples of numerical estimators must be carefully controlled to reduce the overall error.

Ryan
[
1
]
introduced the double-loop MC (DLMC) estimator for the expected information gain (EIG) of an experiment, which is a comprehensive tool to evaluate the quality of an experimental setup, or design
[
5
,
6
]
. Estimating the EIG efficiently and accurately enables design optimization in a Bayesian setting, and hence efficient data collection in real-world applications. We consider integrands whose approximate evaluation requires a numerical solution to a partial differential equation (PDE), for instance, via the finite element method (FEM). The DLMC estimator was further developed in
[
3
]
with regard to additional bias error stemming from the FEM approximation of the experiment model. The computational cost of this estimator to achieve an error tolerance
TOL
is
\mathcal{O}(TOL^{-3})
with no finite element approximation, and is
\mathcal{O}(TOL^{-3-\gamma/\eta_{\rm{w}}})
with such an approximation, where the term
\gamma/\eta_{\rm{w}}
relates to computational work and accuracy of the FEM and will be specified later.

A multilevel MC (MLMC) estimator for the EIG, based on the error analysis by Giles
[
10
]
, was introduced in
[
4
]
. This estimator addressed the inner sampling and FEM discretization error. An MLMC estimator combined with antithetic sampling for EIG estimation was also proposed in
[
30
]
, where the inexact evaluation of the inner integrand was not considered. Attempts to combine multilevel estimators with rQMC were made in
[
32
]
where dimension truncation via the Karhunen–Loéve expansion was also considered; however, there was no nested integration structure. The works
[
12
]
and
[
13
]
introduced estimators for nested integrals based on the MLMC method, where the number of samples in the inner QMC estimator defines the levels. On the other hand,
[
14
]
proposed a multilevel rQMC estimator where the levels are defined by the number of samples in an inner MC estimator. Neither of these studies addressed FEM discretizations. Estimators for nested integrals with more than one nesting were analyzed in
[
40
]
and
[
41
]
for general applications. The latter of these studies developed a randomized (i.e., unbiased) MLMC method, achieving a complexity independent of the number of nestings. Randomized MLMC estimators for financial applications were proposed in
[
42
,
43
]
and for Bayesian experimental design applications in
[
31
]
. The recent work
[
37
]
proposed a multi-index MC (MIMC) estimator for EIG with discretized inner integrand that can achieve a computational cost of
\mathcal{O}(TOL^{-2})
. MIMC methods are an extension to MLMC methods, where an optimization among multi-indices of accuracies is performed
[
25
]
. This scheme allows for exploitation of anisotropies in the error convergence among different kinds of discretizations present in the integrand. The randomized double-loop QMC (rDLQMC) estimator was introduced in
[
2
]
, where rQMC methods were applied to estimate both integrals. This estimator achieves an optimal computational cost of almost
\mathcal{O}(TOL^{-10/9})
for a high-dimensional example where both the inner and outer integrands are polynomials and a computational cost of almost
\mathcal{O}(TOL^{-4/3-\gamma/\eta_{\rm{w}}})
for EIG applications.

The two estimators based on rQMC methods with either a full tensor product structure or a sparse tensor product structure proposed in
[
35
]
were geared toward inner integrands that could be evaluated exactly, and achieved computational work of almost
\mathcal{O}(TOL^{-2})
and
\mathcal{O}(TOL^{-1})
, respectively. Moreover, the FEM-based approximation was treated as the exact integrand in
[
35
]
whenever needed. These estimators use a truncation of the Gaussian observation noise to address singularities arising in the EIG application. As the error tolerance
TOL
approaches zero, this truncation extends towards the boundaries of the integration domain, and as a result, a factor is introduced in their root-mean-square-error bound that grows exponentially with the noise dimension (see
[
35
, Theorem 6.2]
). Such behavior is not observed for the truncation used in the present work for error bounds based on the Hardy–Krause variation or Owen’s boundary growth condition, see Corollary
4
and the discussion in
[
2
]
.

As our main contributions, we propose a novel estimator including a multilevel structure, combined with QMC and rQMC methods, analyze the resulting bias and statistical errors, and derive the optimal number of inner and outer samples and the discretization parameter. Moreover, we derive conditions for applying this estimator to the EIG of an experiment based on a truncation scheme introduced in a previous work
[
2
]
. We then demonstrate that our estimator achieves computational cost of nearly
\mathcal{O}(TOL^{-1-\gamma/\eta_{\rm{w}}})
for EIG estimation in the presence of FEM discretization, and almost
\mathcal{O}(TOL^{-1})
without such discretization. Moreover, a computational cost of nearly
\mathcal{O}(TOL^{-8/9})
is achieved for an example where the inner and outer integrands are polynomials. Whenever the evaluation of the integrand that is approximated via MC (or rQMC) methods requires additional discretization methods, such as the inner rQMC method in this setting, multilevel techniques employ a telescoping structure of increasing accuracy of the integrand approximation to reduce the variance of the combined estimator. The integral is approximated for a very coarse inner integrand, and the result is then corrected via increasing levels of difference estimators.
The primary application of the estimator presented in this study is to approximate the EIG, where the nonlinear function separating the inner and outer integrals is the logarithm. The regularity assumptions stated in this work should be understood in this context; however, the analysis is conducted in general terms and can be adapted to more specific settings.

This work is structured as follows. In Section
2
, we provide a brief overview of numerical approximation methods of integrals and introduce useful notation. As the primary contribution, we present the multilevel double-loop QMC (MLDLQMC) estimator with inexact sampling in Section
3
, along with asymptotic error bounds of the bias and statistical error. This section concludes with a bound on the total work of this estimator for a given error tolerance based on a nearly optimal allocation of the inner and outer samples. In Section
4
, we demonstrate how to apply the MLDLQMC estimator to estimate the EIG of an experiment. This analysis involves a truncation of the Gaussian observation noise present in the experiment model. Section
5
provides numerical results to highlight the practical applicability and competitiveness of the proposed estimators for a Bayesian experimental design setting.

2.
Overview of numerical integration methods

For the reader’s convenience, we provide an overview of existing numerical methods to estimate integrals and nested integrals that are relevant to this work. Moreover, we provide error bounds and analyze the computational cost associated with these methods.

2.1.
Monte Carlo method

Given an integral

(1)

I=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\varphi(\boldsymbol{z})\,\mathrm{d}{}\boldsymbol{z},

where
\varphi:[0,1]^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is square-integrable and
d
is a positive integer, the MC estimator

(2)

I_{\rm{MC}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\varphi(\boldsymbol{z}^{(m)})

uses independent and identically distributed (iid) samples
\boldsymbol{z}^{(m)}
, where
1\leq m\leq M
, from the uniform distribution
\mathcal{U}\left([0,1]^{d}\right)
, to approximate
I
in (
1
). The central limit theorem (CLT)
[
19
]
reveals that the error of the MC estimator is proportional to the estimator variance
\mathbb{V}[I_{\rm{MC}}]
in a probabilistic sense:

(3)

\mathbb{P}\left(|I_{\rm{MC}}-I|\leq C_{\alpha}\sqrt{\mathbb{V}[I_{\rm{MC}}]}\right)\geq 1-\alpha,

as
M\to\infty
, where
\alpha\in(0,1)
is a parameter,
C_{\alpha}=\Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha/2)
,
\Phi^{-1}
denotes the inverse cumulative distribution function (CDF) of the standard normal distribution, and
C_{\alpha}\to\infty
as
\alpha\to 0
. The bound (
3
) motivates the use of variance reduction techniques to control the error. Independence of the samples in the MC estimator ensures that

(4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}[I_{\rm{MC}}]=\frac{\mathbb{V}[\varphi]}{M}.

Increasing the number of samples
M
is a straightforward way to reduce the variance (
4
). The cost of the MC estimator is proportional to the number of times the function
\varphi
is evaluated, i.e.,
M
. Thus, for a linear increase in cost, we obtain a linear decrease in variance, and a sublinear decrease in the error (
3
), namely, at rate
1/2
. A total number of samples proportional to
TOL^{-2}
is required to achieve an error less than a prescribed tolerance
TOL>0
, i.e.,

(5)

C_{\alpha}\sqrt{\frac{\mathbb{V}[\varphi]}{M}}<TOL.

Thus, the total computational work of the MC estimator is

(6)

W_{MC}^{\ast}=\mathcal{O}\left(TOL^{-2}\right),

as
TOL\to 0
.

2.2.
Quasi-Monte Carlo method

The convergence rate of the MC error is independent of the dimension
d
. However, the conventional rate of
1/2
is not large enough for many practical applications. One primary concern with iid samples is that they do not fill the integration domain well. The QMC method,

(7)

I_{\rm{Q}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\varphi(\boldsymbol{z}^{(m)}),

where

(8)

\boldsymbol{z}^{(m)}\coloneqq\boldsymbol{t}^{(m)},\hskip 10.00002pt1\leq m\leq M,

instead uses deterministic points
\boldsymbol{t}^{(m)}
, where
1\leq m\leq M
. These are known as low-discrepancy points with the following property:

\displaystyle D^{\ast}(\boldsymbol{t}^{(1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{t}^{(M)}){}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sup_{(s_{1},\ldots,s_{d})\in[0,1]^{d}}\left|\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathds{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{t}^{(m)}\in\prod_{i=1}^{d}[0,s_{i})\}}-\prod_{i=1}^{d}s_{i}\right|\;,

(9)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{\epsilon,d}}{M^{1-\epsilon}},

for any
\epsilon>0
, where
C_{\epsilon,d}>0
is independent of
M
, and
C_{\epsilon,d}\to\infty
as
\epsilon\to 0
, where
D^{\ast}
is the star-discrepancy
[
15
, Def. (5.2)]
. An important class of low-discrepancy sequences are digital sequences
[
16
,
29
,
17
,
20
]
. We apply a digital sequence called the Sobol’ sequence
[
22
]
throughout this work. However, other low-discrepancy sequences could also be used as long as the bound in (
9
) is satisfied. The approximation error of the QMC estimator is bounded by the Koksma–Hlawka inequality
[
24
,
16
,
15
]
,

(10)

|I_{\rm{Q}}-I|\leq D^{\ast}(\boldsymbol{t}^{(1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{t}^{(M)})V_{\rm{HK}}(\varphi),

where
V_{\rm{HK}}(\varphi)

is the recursively defined Hardy–Krause variation
[
15
, Def. (5.8)]
for continuously differentiable
\varphi
:

(11)

V_{\rm{HK}}(\varphi)\coloneqq\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\left|\left(\prod_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{i}}\right)\varphi(\boldsymbol{z})\right|\,\mathrm{d}{}\boldsymbol{z}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}V_{\rm{HK}}(\varphi(\boldsymbol{z})|_{z_{i}=1}),

with
\varphi(\boldsymbol{z})|_{z_{i}=1}:[0,1]^{d-1}\to\mathbb{R}
representing the restriction of
\varphi(\boldsymbol{z})
to
\varphi(z_{1},\ldots,z_{i}=1,\ldots,z_{d})
. For
\varphi:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
, the Hardy–Krause variation is defined as follows:

(12)

V_{\rm{HK}}(\varphi)\coloneqq\int_{[0,1]}\left|\frac{\,\mathrm{d}{}}{\,\mathrm{d}{}z}\varphi(z)\right|\,\mathrm{d}{}z.

To achieve an error less than
TOL
, the QMC estimator consequently has computational work

(13)

W_{Q}^{\ast}=\mathcal{O}\left(TOL^{-\frac{1}{1-\epsilon}}\right)

as
TOL\to 0
for integrands with bounded Hardy–Krause variation (
11
), based on the bounds in (
9
) and (
10
), where the multiplicative constant may depend on the integrand dimension
d
.
The main concerns with the QMC method are, in no particular order, that:

•

There may be a multiplicative constant in the error depending on the dimension
d
;

•

The first point of many popular low-discrepancy sequences is the origin, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{t}^{(1)}=\boldsymbol{0}
, including the Sobol’ sequence;

•

The Hardy–Krause variation in the error bound in (
10
) is usually more restrictive and more challenging to compute or estimate than the estimator variance arising in (
3
), which plays a crucial role in the error of the rQMC method;

•

Many popular integrands exhibit infinite Hardy–Krause variation.

The first concern can be remedied for certain integrands and specialized QMC methods
[
21
]
. The second concern can be elegantly addressed by introducing randomization to the deterministic low-discrepancy points, as will be discussed in the following section. The third concern can also be partially addressed by randomization. The sample variance taken over multiple independent randomizations, as will be elaborated in the next section, serves as a practical error estimate through Chebyshev’s inequality. Regarding the fourth concern, there are certain types of popular integrands, e.g., integrands weighted by a normal density rather than the uniform one, which must be mapped back to the interval
[0,1]^{d}
. This mapping results in infinite Hardy–Krause variation of the resulting integrand, even though the corresponding QMC error is finite.

2.3.
Randomized Quasi-Monte Carlo method

The rQMC estimator for the integral (
1
) with
R
randomizations is defined as follows:

(14)

I_{\rm{rQ}}^{(R)}\coloneqq\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\varphi(\boldsymbol{z}^{(r,m)}),

where

(15)

\boldsymbol{z}^{(r,m)}\coloneqq\boldsymbol{\rho}^{(r)}(\boldsymbol{t}^{(m)}),\hskip 10.00002pt1\leq r\leq R,\,1\leq m\leq M.

This estimator uses independent randomizations
\boldsymbol{\rho}^{(r)}\in\Pi
, where
1\leq r\leq R
, of the deterministic low-discrepancy sequence
\boldsymbol{t}^{(m)}
, where
1\leq m\leq M
, and
\Pi
denotes the space of randomizations. Owen’s scrambling for digital sequences
[
16
,
29
,
17
]
is employed throughout this work, where a random permutation is applied to the base-two digits of
\boldsymbol{t}^{(m)}
, where
1\leq m\leq M
. This randomization scheme preserves the low-discrepancy property with probability one (see
[
29
,
20
]
for details on this method). It holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}[I_{\rm{rQ}}^{(R)}]{}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{V}\left[\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\varphi(\boldsymbol{z}^{(r,m)})\right],

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{R}\mathbb{V}\left[\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\varphi(\boldsymbol{\rho}(\boldsymbol{t}^{(m)}))\right],

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{R}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\varphi(\boldsymbol{\rho}(\boldsymbol{t}^{(m)}))-\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\varphi(\boldsymbol{\rho}(\boldsymbol{t}^{(m)}))\right]\right|^{2}\right],

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{R}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\varphi(\boldsymbol{\rho}(\boldsymbol{t}^{(m)}))-I\right|^{2}\right],

(16)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{R}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|D^{\ast}\left(\boldsymbol{\rho}(\boldsymbol{t}^{(1)}),\ldots,\boldsymbol{\rho}(\boldsymbol{t}^{(M)})\right)V_{\rm{HK}}(\varphi)\right|^{2}\right],

where the last line follows from the Koksma–Hlawka inequality (
10
) and the variance and expectation are taken over the randomization
\boldsymbol{\rho}
. The variance of the rQMC estimator can thus be bounded as

(17)

\mathbb{V}[I_{\rm{rQ}}^{(R)}]\leq\frac{C_{\epsilon,d}^{2}(V_{\rm{HK}}(\varphi))^{2}}{RM^{2-2\epsilon}},

for any
\epsilon>0
and
C_{\epsilon,d}\to\infty
as
\epsilon\to 0
via (
9
) for all sequences that maintain the low-discrepancy property in
L^{2}(\Pi,\mathbb{R})
under randomization, such as the Sobol’ sequence with Owen’s scrambling. It follows that increasing the number of QMC points is often more efficient than increasing the number of randomizations. There are two important concerns with the Hardy–Krause variation. First, several important classes of functions have infinite Hardy–Krause variation and finite variance. Thus, for such cases, the Koksma–Hlawka inequality does not provide a useful error bound. Second, it is challenging to obtain practical estimates of the Hardy–Krause variation, whereas the variance can be estimated using the sample variance. Regarding the latter point, the rQMC variance
\mathbb{V}[I_{\rm{rQ}}^{(R)}]
can be estimated using
R
independent randomizations whereas the bound in (
16
) is only for theoretical purposes. This situation presents a trade-off: increasing the number of randomizations improves the reliability of the variance estimate but only improves the estimate of the integral, i.e., expectation, at the slower MC rate. A naive approximation based on the CLT for finite
R
can be problematic for certain integrands
[
18
,
23
]
. Chebyshev’s inequality serves as an alternative, at the expense of a larger confidence constant
C_{\alpha}=1/\sqrt{\alpha}
. The number of randomizations
R
plays a lesser role in theoretical error bounds, and we thus introduce the rQMC estimator with one randomization as

(18)

I_{\rm{rQ}}\coloneqq I_{\rm{rQ}}^{(1)}.

As for the first point mentioned above, the following result in (
21
) provides an error bound for a specific, yet common, type of integrands that have infinite Hardy–Krause variation. We begin by introducing Assumption
1
(from
[
26
]
):

Assumption 1

(Boundary growth condition)
.

Let
\varphi:[0,1]^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
and assume that there exists
0<b<\infty
and
A_{i}>0
for
1\leq i\leq d
, where

(19)

A_{\rm{max}}\coloneqq\max_{1\leq i\leq d}A_{i}<\frac{1}{2},

such that

(20)

\left|\left(\prod_{j\in u}\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{j}}\right)\varphi(\boldsymbol{z})\right|\leq b\prod_{i=1}^{d}\min(z_{i},1-z_{i})^{-A_{i}-\mathds{1}_{\{i\in u\}}}

for all
\boldsymbol{z}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})\in(0,1)^{d}
and all
u\subseteq\{1,\ldots,d\}
with the convention that
(\prod_{j\in u}\partial/\partial z_{j})\varphi\equiv\varphi
for
u=\emptyset
.

Assumption
1
implies, among other things, that
\varphi\in L^{2}([0,1]^{d})
, i.e., that
\varphi(\boldsymbol{Z})
, where
\boldsymbol{Z}
is a uniform random variable, has finite variance. This allows for the application of standard error bounds for the MC method. Next, the following proposition is stated (from
[
27
]
):

Proposition 1

(Thm. 3.1 in
[
27
]
)
.

Given Assumption
1
, the rQMC estimator (
18
) satisfies the following
L^{2}([0,1]^{d})
error bound:

(21)

\mathbb{E}[|I_{\rm{rQ}}-I|^{2}]^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq bB_{A}C_{\epsilon,d}M^{-1+\epsilon+A_{\rm{max}}},

for all
\epsilon>0
, where
B_{A}\to\infty
as
\min_{1\leq i\leq d}A_{i}\to 0
and
C_{\epsilon,d}\to\infty
as
\epsilon\to 0
.

This proposition is an extension of an earlier result from
[
26
]
, where a similar bound was provided for the
L^{1}([0,1]^{d})
error.
The rQMC method offers another important benefit over the deterministic QMC method. If the integrand satisfies an even stricter regularity requirement than the bounded Hardy–Krause variation stated above, convergence rates of the estimator variance of almost three are possible. In particular, for continuously differentiable integrands
\varphi
, let the generalized Vitali variation of order one in dimension
d
(see
[
20
, Chapter 13]
and
[
2
, Appendix F]
) be defined as follows:

(22)

V^{(d)}(\varphi)\coloneqq\left(\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\left|\frac{\partial^{d}\varphi}{\partial z_{1}\ldots\partial z_{d}}(\boldsymbol{z})\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{}\boldsymbol{z}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Next, let
\mathcal{I}_{d}\coloneqq\{1,\ldots,d\}
, and
\mathfrak{u}
be the set of all subsets of
\mathcal{I}_{d}
and define

(23)

\varphi_{\mathfrak{u}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{\mathfrak{u}})\coloneqq\int_{[0,1]^{d-|\mathfrak{u}|}}\varphi(\boldsymbol{z})\,\mathrm{d}{}\boldsymbol{z}_{\mathcal{I}_{d}\setminus\mathfrak{u}},

where
|\mathfrak{u}|
denotes the cardinality of
\mathfrak{u}
and
\boldsymbol{z}_{\mathfrak{u}}
contains those entries of
\boldsymbol{z}
that are in
\mathfrak{u}
whereas
\boldsymbol{z}_{\mathcal{I}_{d}\setminus\mathfrak{u}}
contains those entries of
\boldsymbol{z}
that are not in
\mathfrak{u}
. The generalized Hardy–Krause variation of order one of
\varphi
on
[0,1]^{d}
is subsequently defined as follows:

(24)

V_{\rm{GHK}}(\varphi)\coloneqq\left(\left|\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\varphi(\boldsymbol{z})\,\mathrm{d}{}\boldsymbol{z}\right|^{2}+\sum_{\emptyset\neq\mathfrak{u}\subseteq\mathcal{I}_{d}}\left(V^{(|\mathfrak{u}|)}(\varphi_{\mathfrak{u}})\right)^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

The above definition essentially differs from the one in
11
in that the mixed derivatives must be square-integrable.

Proposition 2

(Theorem 6.25 in
[
21
]
)
.

For integrands
\varphi
with bounded generalized Hardy–Krause variation of order one, the variance of an rQMC estimator with
R=1
randomizations and
M
Sobol’ points randomized via Owen’s scrambling is bounded as follows:

(25)

\mathbb{V}[I_{\rm{rQ}}]\leq\left(V_{\rm{GHK}}(\varphi)\right)^{2}C_{\epsilon,d}^{2}M^{-3+2\epsilon},

where
C_{\epsilon,d}\to\infty
as
\epsilon\to 0
.

From the above results, it follows that the computational work of the rQMC estimator to achieve a root mean square error less than
TOL
is

(26)

W_{rQ}^{\ast}=\mathcal{O}\left(TOL^{-\frac{1}{1-\epsilon}}\right)

as
TOL\to 0
for integrands with bounded Hardy–Krause variation, and

(27)

W_{rQ}^{\ast}=\mathcal{O}\left(TOL^{-\frac{1}{1-\epsilon-A_{\rm{max}}}}\right)

as
TOL\to 0
for integrands with singularities at the boundary satisfying Assumption

, where
A_{\rm{max}}>0
as in (

also implies that the rQMC error converges faster than the MC error. Finally, the computational work of the rQMC estimator to achieve a root mean square error less than
TOL
is

(28)

W_{rQ}^{\ast}=\mathcal{O}\left(TOL^{-\frac{2}{3-2\epsilon}}\right)

as
TOL\to 0
for integrands with bounded generalized Hardy–Krause variation of order one.

2.4.
Double-loop Monte Carlo method for nested integrals

Following
[
2
]
, a nested integral is defined as follows:

Definition 1

(Nested integral)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a nonlinear function and let
g:[0,1]^{d_{1}}\times[0,1]^{d_{2}}\to\mathbb{R}
define a nonlinear relation between
\boldsymbol{x}\in[0,1]^{d_{2}}
and
\boldsymbol{y}\in[0,1]^{d_{1}}
, where
d_{1},d_{2}
are positive integers. Then, a nested integral is defined as follows:

(29)

\displaystyle I{}

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,1]^{d_{1}}}f\left(\int_{[0,1]^{d_{2}}}g(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}{}\boldsymbol{x}\right)\,\mathrm{d}{}\boldsymbol{y}.

Depending on the context, it may be beneficial to write the nested integral as a nested expectation instead, that is,

(30)

I=\mathbb{E}[f(\mathbb{E}[g(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{x})|\boldsymbol{y}])],

where
\boldsymbol{y}\sim\mathcal{U}\left([0,1]^{d_{1}}\right)
and
\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathcal{U}\left([0,1]^{d_{2}}\right)
. Moreover, the notation
\bar{g}:[0,1]^{d_{1}}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\bar{g}(\boldsymbol{y}){}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[g(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{x})|\boldsymbol{y}],

(31)

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,1]^{d_{2}}}g(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}{}\boldsymbol{x}.

Ryan
[
1
]
introduced the DLMC estimator for nested integrals:

(32)

I_{\rm{DLMC}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}f\left(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}g(\boldsymbol{y}^{(n)},\boldsymbol{x}^{(n,m)})\right),

where
\boldsymbol{y}^{(n)}
, for
1\leq n\leq N
, are iid samples from
\mathcal{U}\left([0,1]^{d_{1}}\right)
. Moreover,
\boldsymbol{x}^{(n,m)}
, for
1\leq n\leq N
and
1\leq m\leq M
, are iid samples from
\mathcal{U}\left([0,1]^{d_{2}}\right)
for the specific application of EIG estimation where
f
is the logarithm and
g
is the likelihood of the data observation. For large values of
N
and
M
, this estimator has a total error based on the CLT
[
1
,
3
]
of

(33)

|I_{\rm{DLMC}}-I|\leq\frac{C_{\rm{DL,B}}}{M}+C_{\alpha}\sqrt{\frac{C_{\rm{DL,V}}^{(1)}}{N}+\frac{C_{\rm{DL,V}}^{(2)}}{NM}},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{with probability at least $1-\alpha$},

for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
, where the first term is a bias stemming from the variance of the inner MC method, and the second term is derived from the total variance of the DLMC estimator, and
C_{\alpha}\to\infty
as
\alpha\to 0
. Moreover,
C_{\rm{DL,B}}>0
and
C_{\rm{DL,V}}^{(1)},C_{\rm{DL,V}}^{(2)}>0
are constants independent of
N
and
M
. A total of
N\times M
samples is required for this estimator, and thus the total work to achieve an error less than
TOL
is:

(34)

W_{DL}^{\ast}=\mathcal{O}\left(TOL^{-3}\right)

as
TOL\to 0
.

2.5.
Double-loop randomized quasi-Monte Carlo method for nested integrals

The rDLQMC estimator as introduced in
[
2
]
is:

(35)

I_{\rm{rDLQ}}^{(S,R)}\coloneqq\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}f\left(\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}g(\boldsymbol{y}^{(s,n)},\boldsymbol{x}^{(s,n,r,m)})\right),

where the samples take the following form

(36)

\boldsymbol{y}^{(s,n)}\coloneqq\boldsymbol{\tau}^{(s)}(\boldsymbol{u}^{(n)}),\hskip 10.00002pt1\leq s\leq S,\,1\leq n\leq N.

Here,
\boldsymbol{\tau}^{(s)}
, with
1\leq s\leq S
, are independent randomizations of the Sobol’ sequence
\boldsymbol{u}^{(n)}
in
d_{1}
, where
1\leq n\leq N
, using Owen’s scrambling, and

(37)

\boldsymbol{x}^{(s,n,r,m)}\coloneqq\boldsymbol{\rho}^{(s,n,r)}(\boldsymbol{t}^{(m)}),\hskip 10.00002pt1\leq s\leq S,\,1\leq n\leq N,\,1\leq r\leq R,\,1\leq m\leq M,

where
\boldsymbol{\rho}^{(s,n,r)}
, with
1\leq s\leq S
,
1\leq n\leq N
, and
1\leq r\leq R
are independent randomizations of the Sobol’ sequence
\boldsymbol{t}^{(m)}
in
d_{2}
, where
1\leq m\leq M
, using Owen’s scrambling. Assuming that the nested integrand
f(\bar{g})
satisfies Assumption
1
with
\bar{g}
as in (
31
), for
0<A_{\rm{max}}<1/2
, whereas the inner integrand
g
has bounded generalized Hardy–Krause variation of order one, this estimator has a total error based on Chebyshev’s inequality
[
2
]
of

(38)

|I_{\rm{rDLQ}}^{(1,1)}-I|\leq\frac{C_{\rm{DLQ,B}}}{M^{3-2\epsilon}}+C_{\alpha}\sqrt{\frac{C_{\rm{DLQ,V}}^{(1)}}{N^{2-2\epsilon-2A_{\rm{max}}}}+\frac{C_{\rm{DLQ,V}}^{(2)}}{NM^{3-2\epsilon}}},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{with probability at least $1-\alpha$},

for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
C_{\alpha}\to\infty
as
\alpha\to 0
, for
S=R=1
randomizations, where
C_{\rm{DLQ,B}}>0
and
C_{\rm{DLQ,V}}^{(1)},C_{\rm{DLQ,V}}^{(2)}>0
are constants independent of
N
and
M
. The total work to achieve an error less than
TOL
based on this error bound is:

(39)

W_{rDLQ}^{\ast}=\mathcal{O}\left(TOL^{-\frac{1}{1-\epsilon-A_{\rm{max}}}-\frac{1}{3-2\epsilon}}\right)

as
TOL\to 0
. This setting is also analyzed in particular for the EIG estimation in
[
2
]
.

2.6.
Multilevel double-loop Monte Carlo method for nested integrals

The multilevel method for nested integrals
[
30
]
constructs a sequence of increasingly accurate MC estimators for the inner integral to harness variance reduction achieved by taking differences of adjacent levels evaluated for the same random variables. The general form of the MLMC estimator for a nonnested integral is given as follows:

(40)

I_{\rm{ML}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{N_{0}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{0}}\varphi_{0}\left(\boldsymbol{z}^{(0,n)}\right)+\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\frac{1}{N_{\ell}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\ell}}\left[\varphi_{\ell}\left(\boldsymbol{z}^{(\ell,n)}\right)-\varphi_{\ell-1}\left(\boldsymbol{z}^{(\ell,n)}\right)\right],

where
\boldsymbol{z}^{\ell,n}
are iid samples from
\mathcal{U}([0,1]^{d})
for
1\leq n\leq N_{\ell}
, and
1\leq\ell\leq L
,
L\in\mathbb{N}
. The multilevel double-loop MC (MLDLMC) estimator introduced in
[
30
]
for nested integrals defines the level
\varphi_{\ell}
as follows:

(41)

\varphi_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{z}^{(\ell,n)})\coloneqq f\left(\frac{1}{M_{\ell}}\sum_{m=1}^{M_{\ell}}g\left(\boldsymbol{y}^{(\ell,n)},\boldsymbol{x}^{(\ell,n,m)}\right)\right),

where
\boldsymbol{z}^{(\ell,n)}\coloneqq(\boldsymbol{y}^{(\ell,n)},\boldsymbol{x}^{(\ell,n,m)})
and
M_{\ell}=M_{0}2^{\ell}
, for
M_{0}>0
. An antithetic construction first introduced in
[
38
]
to increase the coupling, and thus reduce the variance further, was used to obtain computational work:

(42)

W_{\rm{ML}}^{\ast}=\mathcal{O}\left(TOL^{-2}\right)

to achieve an error tolerance
TOL>0
as
TOL\to 0
for estimating the EIG. Note that the asymptotic work presented in
[
30
]
was derived under the assumption that exact evaluations of
g
were available. Depending on the considered application, this assumption may be inadequate, because the considered experiment generating the data may involve numerical approximations.

2.7.
Inexact sampling

Inner integrands
g
encountered in many practical applications cannot be evaluated exactly at a given point
\boldsymbol{z}
. Instead, an approximation
g_{h}
is introduced, where
g_{h}\to g
as
h\to 0
in a suitable sense, for a discretization parameter
h>0
. This setting is referred to as inexact sampling and examples include finite difference methods, FEM, and time stepping methods. This discretization introduces bias to the estimators discussed in the previous sections, and thus affects the computational work required to achieve an error tolerance
TOL
. To account for this additional bias, the following assumptions are considered in this work:

Assumption 2

(Weak convergence rate)
.

Let
g_{h}
be an approximation of
g:[0,1]^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
, and
h>0
such that the weak error of
g_{h}
is given by

(43)

\left|\int_{[0,1]^{d_{1}}}f\left(\int_{[0,1]^{d_{2}}}g_{h}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}{}\boldsymbol{x}\right)\,\mathrm{d}{}\boldsymbol{y}-\int_{[0,1]^{d_{1}}}f\left(\int_{[0,1]^{d_{2}}}g(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}{}\boldsymbol{x}\right)\,\mathrm{d}{}\boldsymbol{y}\right|\leq C_{\rm{w}}h^{\eta_{\rm{w}}},

where
\eta_{\rm{w}}>0
is the weak
h
-convergence rate, and
C_{\rm{w}}>0
is a constant independent of
h
. The work of evaluating
g_{h}
is assumed to be of order
\mathcal{O}(h^{-\gamma})
, for
\gamma>0
uniformly in
[0,1]^{d}
.

In the inexact sampling case, an additional term
{-\gamma/\eta_{\rm{w}}}
is added to the exponent of
TOL
in the total computational work of the MC, QMC, rQMC, DLMC, and rDLQMC estimators
[
3
,
2
]
, which is motivated by the EIG application. Note that only the inner integrand in the nested setting is assumed to require discretization throughout this work. The work
[
4
]
proposed an MLMC estimator for nested integration applied to the EIG, where the data generation involves approximating a PDE via the FEM. The accuracy level in the error bound proposed in this work was determined by the inner number of samples and the discretization parameter of the FEM. However, the total computational work is then bounded for a fixed inner number of samples as
\mathcal{O}(TOL^{-2})
for large and intermediate error tolerances
TOL>0
, and
\mathcal{O}(TOL^{-3})
asymptotically as
TOL\to 0
. This change in complexity regime is due to the effects of Laplace-based importance sampling used in this estimator, which renders just one inner sample sufficient for
TOL
large enough.

Remark 1

(Laplace-based importance sampling)
.

Laplace-based importance sampling is an efficient variance reduction technique for EIG estimation
[
9
,
3
,
4
]
. However, combining this method with multilevel strategies and especially with rQMC methods is nontrivial
[
28
]
. Thus, we leave the study of this method for future work.

3.
Multilevel double-loop randomized quasi-Monte Carlo method for nested integrals with inexact sampling

As the primary focus of this work we analyzed the multilevel double-loop randomized quasi-Monte Carlo (MLDLQMC) estimator with inexact sampling.
This estimator combines the multilevel technique with both deterministic QMC and rQMC methods.
At all levels
\ell
, where
0\leq\ell\leq L
, the outer integral is estimated using the rQMC method, allowing for a practical error estimate via the sample variance. This estimate is theoretically justified by Proposition
1
. The inner estimation at levels
\ell
, where
0\leq\ell\leq L-1
, is based on QMC methods. Due to the deterministic nature of these methods, the variances of the estimators for those levels depend only on the outer samples. The multilevel structure ensures that only the final level
L
contributes to the bias of the overall estimator.
The rQMC method is applied for the inner integral approximation only at level
L
, allowing for practical error estimates of the bias and variance of the estimator.

3.1.
Estimator introduction

Definition 2

(Multilevel double-loop randomized quasi-Monte Carlo estimator with inexact sampling)
.

The MLDLQMC estimator with inexact sampling is defined as follows:

\displaystyle I_{\rm{MLDLQ}}^{(S,R)}\coloneqq{}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\frac{1}{N_{0}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{0}}f\left(\frac{1}{M_{0}}\sum_{m=1}^{M_{0}}g_{h_{0}}\left(\boldsymbol{y}^{(0,s,n)},\boldsymbol{x}^{(m)}\right)\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\ell=1}^{L-1}\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\frac{1}{N_{\ell}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\ell}}\left[f\left(\frac{1}{M_{\ell}}\sum_{m=1}^{M_{\ell}}g_{h_{\ell}}\left(\boldsymbol{y}^{(\ell,s,n)},\boldsymbol{x}^{(m)}\right)\right)-f\left(\frac{1}{M_{\ell-1}}\sum_{m=1}^{M_{\ell-1}}g_{h_{\ell-1}}\left(\boldsymbol{y}^{(\ell,s,n)},\boldsymbol{x}^{(m)}\right)\right)\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\frac{1}{N_{L}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{L}}\left[f\left(\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\frac{1}{M_{L}}\sum_{m=1}^{M_{L}}g_{h_{L}}\left(\boldsymbol{y}^{(L,s,n)},\boldsymbol{x}^{(s,r,m)}\right)\right)\right.

(44)

\displaystyle\left.\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt-f\left(\frac{1}{M_{L-1}}\sum_{m=1}^{M_{L-1}}g_{h_{L-1}}\left(\boldsymbol{y}^{(L,s,n)},\boldsymbol{x}^{(m)}\right)\right)\right].

Here,
g_{h_{\ell}}
is an approximation of
g
such that Assumption
2

holds for
0\leq\ell\leq L
, and

(45)

\boldsymbol{y}^{(\ell,s,n)}\coloneqq\boldsymbol{\tau}^{(\ell,s)}(\boldsymbol{u}^{(n)}),\hskip 10.00002pt0\leq\ell\leq L,\,1\leq s\leq S,\,1\leq n\leq N_{\ell},

where
\boldsymbol{\tau}^{(\ell,s)}
are iid Owen’s scramblings of points in the Sobol’ sequence
\boldsymbol{u}^{(n)}
in dimension
d_{1}
, for
1\leq s\leq S
,
1\leq n\leq N_{\ell}
, and
0\leq\ell\leq L
, such that

(46)

\mathbb{E}[D^{\ast}(\boldsymbol{y}^{(\ell,s,1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{y}^{(\ell,s,N_{\ell})})]\leq\frac{C_{\epsilon,d_{1}}}{N_{\ell}^{1-\epsilon}},\hskip 10.00002pt0\leq\ell\leq L,\,1\leq s\leq S,

for all
\epsilon>0
, where
C_{\epsilon,d_{1}}>0
is independent of
N_{\ell}
, where
0\leq\ell\leq L
, and
C_{\epsilon,d_{1}}\to\infty
as
\epsilon\to 0
. Moreover,

(47)

\boldsymbol{x}^{(m)}\coloneqq\boldsymbol{t}^{(m)},\hskip 10.00002pt1\leq m\leq M_{\ell},

are points in the Sobol’ sequence
\boldsymbol{t}^{(m)}
in dimension
d_{2}
, for
1\leq m\leq M_{\ell}
,
0\leq\ell\leq L-1
, such that

(48)

D^{\ast}(\boldsymbol{x}^{(1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}^{(M_{\ell})})\leq\frac{C_{\epsilon,d_{2}}}{M_{\ell}^{1-\epsilon}},\hskip 10.00002pt0\leq\ell\leq L,

for all
\epsilon>0
, where
C_{\epsilon,d_{2}}>0
is independent of
M_{\ell}
, where
0\leq\ell\leq L
, and
C_{\epsilon,d_{2}}\to\infty
as
\epsilon\to 0
. Finally, the following construction is applied for the randomized inner points at level
L
:

(49)

\boldsymbol{x}^{(s,r,m)}\coloneqq\boldsymbol{\rho}^{(s,r)}(\boldsymbol{t}^{(m)}),\hskip 10.00002pt1\leq s\leq S,\,1\leq r\leq R,\,1\leq m\leq M_{L},

where
\boldsymbol{\rho}^{(s,r)}
are iid Owen’s scramblings of points in the Sobol’ sequence
\boldsymbol{t}^{(m)}
in dimension
d_{2}
, for
1\leq s\leq S
,
1\leq r\leq R
, and
1\leq m\leq M_{L}
, such that

(50)

\mathbb{E}[D^{\ast}(\boldsymbol{x}^{(s,r,1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}^{(s,r,M_{L})})]\leq\frac{C_{\epsilon,d_{2}}}{M_{L}^{1-\epsilon}},\hskip 10.00002pt1\leq s\leq S,\,1\leq r\leq R,

for all
\epsilon>0
, where
C_{\epsilon,d_{2}}>0
is independent of
M_{L}
and
C_{\epsilon,d_{2}}\to\infty
as
\epsilon\to 0
.

For now, we set:

(51)

M_{\ell}\coloneqq M_{0}2^{\ell},\hskip 10.00002pt1\leq\ell\leq L,

where
M_{0}\geq 1
, and

(52)

h_{\ell}\coloneqq h_{0}2^{-\ell},\hskip 10.00002pt1\leq\ell\leq L,

where
h_{0}>0
.
The choices (
51
) and (
52
) are not specific to the MLDLQMC estimator and are revisited later (see Remark
3
). It is crucial for the quality of the estimator that all point sets used have the low-discrepancy property (
9
); however, it would be possible to use different points
\boldsymbol{x}^{(m)}
, where
1\leq m\leq M_{\ell}
, at different levels
1\leq\ell\leq L-1
, as long as the telescoping property is preserved. The following notations are defined for convenience:

(53)

I_{\rm{MLDLQ}}^{(S,R)}=I_{\rm{MLDLQ}_{0}^{0}}^{(S,R)}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\left[I_{\rm{MLDLQ}_{\ell}^{\ell}}^{(S,R)}-I_{\rm{MLDLQ}_{\ell}^{\ell-1}}^{(S,R)}\right],

where

(54)

I_{{\rm{MLDLQ}}_{\ell}^{k}}^{(S,R)}\coloneqq\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\frac{1}{N_{\ell}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\ell}}f\left(\frac{1}{M_{k}}\sum_{m=1}^{M_{k}}g_{h_{k}}\left(\boldsymbol{y}^{(\ell,s,n)},\boldsymbol{x}^{(m)}\right)\right),\hskip 10.00002pt1\leq\ell\leq L,\,1\leq k\leq L-1,

and

(55)

I_{{\rm{MLDLQ}}_{L}^{L}}^{(S,R)}\coloneqq\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\frac{1}{N_{L}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{L}}f\left(\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\frac{1}{M_{L}}\sum_{m=1}^{M_{L}}g_{h_{L}}\left(\boldsymbol{y}^{(L,s,n)},\boldsymbol{x}^{(s,r,m)}\right)\right).

Here, two additional indices were introduced to distinguish between levels of the estimator and the two terms within each level difference. The MLDLQMC estimator with
S=R=1
randomizations is defined as follows:

(56)

I_{\rm{MLDLQ}}\coloneqq I_{\rm{MLDLQ}}^{(1,1)},

with
I_{{\rm{MLDLQ}}_{\ell}^{k}}
defined analogously for
1\leq\ell,k\leq L
,
and
\bar{g}_{h}
is defined analogously to (
31
) for
\boldsymbol{y}\in[0,1]^{d_{1}}
and
\boldsymbol{x}\in[0,1]^{d_{2}}
. We proceed by bounding the total error of the MLDLQMC estimator based on certain assumptions that are verified for the EIG application, where
f
is the logarithm and
g
is the likelihood combined with appropriate inverse CDFs. The total error is split into bias and statistical errors as follows:

(57)

|I_{\rm{MLDLQ}}-I|\leq\underbrace{|\mathbb{E}[I_{\rm{MLDLQ}}]-I|}_{\text{bias error}}+\underbrace{|I_{\rm{MLDLQ}}-\mathbb{E}[I_{\rm{MLDLQ}}]|}_{\text{statistical error}}.

3.2.
Bias error bounds

This study follows the work by
[
2
]
and restates the necessary assumptions for completeness to bound the bias of the MLDLQMC estimator.

Assumption 3

(Integrability assumption for the composition of the outer and inner integrand)
.

There exists
0<\delta_{K}<1
and
1\leq p\leq\infty
such that
\tilde{f}(\cdot)\equiv f(K\bar{g}_{h}(\cdot)):[0,1]^{d_{1}}\to\mathbb{R}
is an element of the Sobolev space
W^{3,p}([0,1]^{d_{1}})
for all
K\in(1-\delta_{K},1+\delta_{K})
, where
\bar{g}_{h}
is as in (
31
).

Assumption 4

(Inverse inequality for the inner integrand)
.

Let
g_{h}(\cdot,\cdot):[0,1]^{d_{1}}\times[0,1]^{d_{2}}\to\mathbb{R}
and assume that there exists
0\leq k<\infty
such that

(58)

\sup_{\boldsymbol{y}\in[0,1]^{d_{1}}}\left\lvert\frac{V_{\rm{HK}}(g_{h}(\boldsymbol{y},\cdot))}{\bar{g}_{h}(\boldsymbol{y})}\right\rvert\leq k\;,

with
V_{\rm{HK}}
as in (
11
),
g_{h}
and
\bar{g}_{h}
as in Assumption
2
and (
31
) as
h\to 0
. Furthermore, if Assumption
3
holds, assume that there exists
1\leq q\leq\infty
with
1/p+1/q=1
for
p
as in Assumption
3
such that
\bar{g}_{h}\in L^{3q}([0,1]^{d_{1}})
.

Together, these assumptions ensure that the bias from the inner sampling in the MLDLQMC estimator remains bounded. The assumptions depend on the particular integrands
f
and
g
and must be verified for each application. For the EIG, it is demonstrated that these assumptions hold in
[
2
]
and also in Section
4
and Appendix
A
of this work, contingent on certain auxiliary assumptions on the experiment under consideration.
The expectation of the MLDLQMC estimator is equal to the expectation of the corresponding single-level estimator at the finest level
L
, that is,

(59)

\mathbb{E}[I_{\rm{MLDLQ}}]=\mathbb{E}[I_{{\rm{MLDLQ}}_{L}^{L}}].

The bound on the bias of the MLDLQMC estimator follows immediately from
[
2
, Proposition 3]
with
M=M_{L}
and
h=h_{L}
as stated below.

Proposition 3

(Bias of the MLDLQMC estimator with inexact sampling)
.

Given Assumption
2
for
g_{h_{L}}(\cdot,\cdot):[0,1]^{d_{1}}\times[0,1]^{d_{2}}\to\mathbb{R}
and Assumptions
3
and
4
, if
|f^{\prime\prime\prime}|
is monotonic, the bias of the MLDLQMC estimator (
56
) with inexact sampling has the following upper bound:

(60)

|\mathbb{E}[I_{\rm{MLDLQ}}]-I|\leq C_{\mathrm{w}}h_{L}^{\eta_{\rm{w}}}+\frac{\mathbb{E}[|\bar{g}_{h}|^{2}\left|f^{\prime\prime}(\bar{g}_{h})\right|]k^{2}C_{\epsilon,d_{2}}^{2}}{2M_{L}^{2-2\epsilon}}+\frac{\mathbb{E}[\left|\bar{g}_{h}\right|^{3}\left|f^{\prime\prime\prime}(K\bar{g}_{h})\right|]k^{3}C_{\epsilon,d_{2}}^{3}}{6M_{L}^{3-3\epsilon}},

for any
\epsilon>0
, where
C_{\epsilon,d_{2}}\to\infty
as
\epsilon\to 0
.

3.3.
Statistical error bounds

The statistical error of the MLDLQMC estimator is bounded in probability using Chebyshev’s inequality as follows:

(61)

\mathbb{P}\left(|I_{\rm{MLDLQ}}-\mathbb{E}[I_{\rm{MLDLQ}}]|\leq C_{\alpha}\sqrt{\mathbb{V}[I_{\rm{MLDLQ}}]}\right)\geq 1-\alpha

for
\alpha\in(0,1)
, where
C_{\alpha}=1/\sqrt{\alpha}
and
\mathbb{V}[I_{\rm{MLDLQ}}]
is the estimator variance. The randomizations of the outer samples are independent for each level; thus, the total variance of the MLDLQMC estimator is decomposed into the variances of the individual levels, that is,

(62)

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}\left[I_{\rm{MLDLQ}}\right]={}

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}\left[I_{\rm{MLDLQ}_{0}^{0}}\right]+\sum_{\ell=1}^{L-1}\mathbb{V}\left[I_{\rm{MLDLQ}_{\ell}^{\ell}}-I_{\rm{MLDLQ}_{\ell}^{\ell-1}}\right]+\mathbb{V}\left[I_{{\rm{MLDLQ}}_{L}^{L}}-I_{{\rm{MLDLQ}}_{L}^{L-1}}\right].

The terms appearing in (
62
) are bounded individually in the following Propositions
4
-
6
, leading to an upper bound of the total variance of the MLDLQMC estimator.

Assumption 5
.

Let
\varphi_{h_{0},M_{0}}^{(0)}
be the auxiliary function

(63)

\varphi_{h_{0},M_{0}}^{(0)}(\cdot)\coloneqq f\left(\frac{1}{M_{0}}\sum_{m=1}^{M_{0}}g_{h_{0}}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{x}^{(m)})\right).

Moreover, let
h_{0}>0
,
M_{0}\geq 1
and
\boldsymbol{x}^{(m)}
be as in (
47
), where
1\leq m\leq M_{0}
. Then, assume that
\varphi_{h_{0},M_{0}}^{(0)}
satisfies Assumption
1
, that is, there exists
0<b^{(0)}<\infty
and
A_{i}^{(0)}>0
for
1\leq i\leq d_{1}
, where

(64)

A_{\rm{max}}^{(0)}\coloneqq\max_{1\leq i\leq d_{1}}A_{i}^{(0)}<\frac{1}{2},

such that

(65)

\left|\left(\prod_{j\in u}\frac{\partial^{u}}{\partial y_{j}}\right)\varphi_{h_{0},M_{0}}^{(0)}(\boldsymbol{y})\right|\leq b^{(0)}\prod_{i=1}^{d_{1}}\min(y_{i},1-y_{i})^{-A^{(0)}_{i}-\mathds{1}_{\{i\in u\}}}

for all
\boldsymbol{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{d_{1}})\in(0,1)^{d_{1}}
and all
u\subseteq\{1,\ldots,{d_{1}}\}
.

The zeroth level of the MLDLQMC estimator may be regarded as an rQMC estimator (
18
), with
N=N_{0}
, for a particular choice of integrand. In view of Assumption
5
, the bound on the variance contribution of the zeroth level of the MLDLQMC estimator follows immediately from Proposition
1
as stated next.

Proposition 4

(Variance of level 0 of the MLDLQMC estimator with inexact sampling)
.

Let
\varphi_{h_{0},M_{0}}^{(0)}
as in (
63
) satisfy Assumption
5
. The variance contribution of the zeroth level of the MLDLQMC estimator (
56

Remark 4

(Extension of the rDLQMC estimator to the multilevel setting)
.

It becomes apparent from Propositions
4
–
6
that the particular structure of the MLDLQMC estimator (
56
) (deterministic inner samples at levels
0\leq\ell\leq L-1
, and randomized inner samples at level
L
) is crucial. Adapting the rDLQMC estimator
[
2
]
to the multilevel setting would deteriorate the convergence of the variances at levels
0\leq\ell\leq L-1
to rate
N
rather than rate
N^{2-2\epsilon-2A_{\rm{max}}}
. Instead, randomizing the inner samples in the last level does not affect the overall estimator complexity and provides a practical error estimate for the bias.

Remark 5

(Antithetic sampling)
.

The coupling in multilevel estimators can be increased by an antithetic construction as in the works
[
38
,
30
]
; however, such an antithetic construction is not possible for the MLDLQMC estimator because of the combination of deterministic and randomized inner samples.

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(TOL^{-\frac{1}{1-\epsilon-A_{\rm{max}}}}\log_{2}\left(TOL^{-1}\right)\right).

If the exponent in (

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{L^{\ast}}N_{\ell}^{\ast}W_{\ell}{}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(TOL^{-\frac{2}{a}\left(1-\min\left\{\frac{\eta_{{\rm{s}},d_{1}}}{\eta_{\rm{w}}},\frac{b}{c}\right\}\right)-\left(\frac{2}{c}+\frac{\gamma}{\eta_{\rm{w}}}\right)}\right).

It follows from

and
a=b
that

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{L^{\ast}}N_{\ell}^{\ast}W_{\ell}{}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(TOL^{-\frac{2}{a}\left(1-\frac{\eta_{{\rm{s}},d_{1}}}{\eta_{\rm{w}}}\right)-\left(\frac{2}{c}+\frac{\gamma}{\eta_{\rm{w}}}\right)}\right),

(145)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(TOL^{-\frac{1}{1-\epsilon-A_{\rm{max}}}+\frac{\eta_{{\rm{s}},d_{1}}}{\eta_{\rm{w}}(1-\epsilon-A_{\rm{max}})}-\left(\frac{1}{1-\epsilon}+\frac{\gamma}{\eta_{\rm{w}}}\right)}\right).

Finally, the second term in the total work is bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{L^{\ast}}W_{\ell}{}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(TOL^{-\left(\frac{2}{c}+\frac{\gamma}{\eta_{\rm{w}}}\right)}\right),

(146)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(TOL^{-\left(\frac{1}{1-\epsilon}+\frac{\gamma}{\eta_{\rm{w}}}\right)}\right).

∎

Instead of choosing

4.
Expected information gain estimation

We now demonstrate how to apply the MLDLQMC estimator to estimate of the EIG of an experiment. This is an application from Bayesian experimental design, where uncertain parameters of interest
\boldsymbol{\theta}
are modeled as random variables with a prior distribution
\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta})
. The selection of the prior reflects the a priori knowledge of the experimenter. The observation data is modeled as follows:

(167)

\boldsymbol{Y}(\boldsymbol{\xi})=\boldsymbol{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t},\boldsymbol{\xi})+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},

where
\boldsymbol{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
. The design
\boldsymbol{\xi}
is omitted for conciseness, as it is not essential for the integral approximation that is the focus of the current work, and finding optimal designs is outside the scope of this study. Furthermore,
\boldsymbol{G}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{\theta}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{\xi}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
is an appropriate model for the experiment evaluated at the unknown true parameter vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}
and
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\mathrel{{\mathop{\sim}\limits}}\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}})
is additive Gaussian noise with known covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}\times d_{y}}
(i.e., symmetric and strictly positive definite). The noise
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
should not be confused with the factor
\epsilon
in the convergence rate of rQMC methods used throughout the remainder of this study.
The EIG quantifies the amount of information that is expected to be gained from the data observation regarding the parameters of interest. It is defined as

(168)

EIG\coloneqq\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\int_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}\log\left(\frac{\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{Y})}{\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta})}\right)\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{Y})\,\mathrm{d}{}\boldsymbol{\theta}p(\boldsymbol{Y})\,\mathrm{d}{}\boldsymbol{Y},

where
\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{Y})
is the posterior distribution of the parameters of interest given the data, and
p(\boldsymbol{Y})
is the evidence, or marginal likelihood. The data model (
167
) implies the following likelihood:

(169)

p(\boldsymbol{Y}|\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{1}{\det(2\pi\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}})^{\frac{1}{2}}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{Y}-\boldsymbol{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{-1}}^{2}},

where
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{-1}}^{2}\coloneqq\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{-1}}
, and
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{-1}}
is the
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{-1}}
-inner product, that is,
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{-1}}\coloneqq\left<\cdot,{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{-1}}\cdot\right>
, and
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>
is the standard
L^{2}
inner product. This likelihood is used together with Bayes’ theorem and marginalization to rewrite (
168
) as follows:

(170)

\displaystyle EIG{}

\displaystyle=\int_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\left[\log\left(p(\boldsymbol{Y}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\right)-\log\left(\int_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}p(\boldsymbol{Y}|\boldsymbol{\vartheta})\pi(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})\,\mathrm{d}{}\boldsymbol{\vartheta}\right)\right]p(\boldsymbol{Y}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\mathrm{d}{}\boldsymbol{Y}\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\mathrm{d}{}\boldsymbol{\theta},

where
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
indicates an auxiliary variable for integration. Substituting (
167
) into (
169
) yields

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{Y}|\boldsymbol{\theta}){}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\det(2\pi\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}})^{\frac{1}{2}}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{Y}-\boldsymbol{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{-1}}^{2}},

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\det(2\pi\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}})^{\frac{1}{2}}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}-\boldsymbol{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{-1}}^{2}},

(171)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\det(2\pi\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}})^{\frac{1}{2}}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{-1}}^{2}},

and

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{Y}|\boldsymbol{\vartheta}){}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\det(2\pi\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}})^{\frac{1}{2}}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{Y}-\boldsymbol{G}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{-1}}^{2}},

(172)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\det(2\pi\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}})^{\frac{1}{2}}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(\left\lVert\boldsymbol{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{-1}}^{2}+\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{-1}}^{2}+\left\lVert\boldsymbol{G}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{-1}}^{2}+2\left<\boldsymbol{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\right>_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{-1}}-2\left<\boldsymbol{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\boldsymbol{G}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})\right>_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{-1}}-2\left<\boldsymbol{G}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\right>_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{-1}}\right)}.

From this, it follows that

\displaystyle EIG{}

\displaystyle=\int_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}}\left[\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{-1}}^{2}+\left<\boldsymbol{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\right>_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{-1}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\hskip 10.00002pt-\log\left(\int_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(\left\lVert\boldsymbol{G}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{-1}}^{2}-2\left<\boldsymbol{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\boldsymbol{G}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})\right>_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{-1}}-2\left<\boldsymbol{G}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\right>_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{-1}}\right)}\pi(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})\,\mathrm{d}{}\boldsymbol{\vartheta}\right)\right]

(173)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\times\frac{1}{\det(2\pi\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}})^{\frac{1}{2}}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{-1}}^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}{}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\mathrm{d}{}\boldsymbol{\theta}.

The first term in (
173
) is not nested and can thus be estimated using the standard rQMC or similar methods. The second term is symmetric around the origin and thus vanishes as it is integrated against the Gaussian measure for
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
. The remaining term constitutes a nested integral and is the main subject of this investigation. We introduce the notation
f\equiv\log
, and

(174)

g(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{x})\coloneqq\exp\left({-\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{G}(F_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{x}))\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{-1}}^{2}+\left<\boldsymbol{G}(F_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{y}_{1})),\boldsymbol{G}(F_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{x}))\right>_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{-1}}+\left<\boldsymbol{G}(F_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{x})),\Phi^{-1}(\boldsymbol{y}_{2})\right>_{\boldsymbol{\tilde{\Sigma}}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{-\scriptscriptstyle\mskip-1.0mu\top\mskip-2.0mu}}}\right),

where
F_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{-1}
is the inverse CDF of the parameters of interest,
\boldsymbol{y}=(\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{2})
,
F_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{y}_{1})\coloneqq\boldsymbol{\theta}
,
F_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{x})\coloneqq\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
,
\boldsymbol{\tilde{\Sigma}}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\Phi^{-1}(\boldsymbol{y}_{2})\coloneqq\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
, and

(175)

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}=\boldsymbol{\tilde{\Sigma}}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\boldsymbol{\tilde{\Sigma}}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mskip-1.0mu\top\mskip-2.0mu}.

6.
Conclusion

This work proposes the multilevel double-loop randomized quasi-Monte Carlo estimator for nested integrals with inexact sampling. This estimator combines the multilevel strategy with deterministic and randomized quasi-Monte Carlo (QMC) methods to achieve high approximation accuracy when the inner integrand of a nested integration problem must itself be approximated. The bias and statistical errors are bounded asymptotically, yielding an estimate of the total number of inner and outer samples and the discretization parameter required to achieve a specified error tolerance. Depending on the regularity of the integrands, several different settings are analyzed. In the optimal setting, where both the outer and inner integrands have bounded generalized Hardy–Krause variation of order one, the MLDLQMC estimator achieves a total complexity of almost
\mathcal{O}(TOL^{-8/9})
if no additional discretization is necessary, that is, the exact sampling case. A simple example with polynomial integrands demonstrates this optimal complexity numerically. The estimator is then applied to expected information gain (EIG) estimation, where singularities render standard QMC error bounds via the Koksma–Hlawka inequality pointless. A truncation scheme of the Gaussian observation noise in the experiment model with error bounds introduced by Owen
[
26
]
and extended by He et al.
[
27
]
provide error bounds for this setting, yielding a total complexity of almost
\mathcal{O}(TOL^{-1-\gamma/\eta_{\rm{w}}})
, where
\gamma
and
\eta_{\rm{w}}
relate to the cost and weak convergence rate of the discretization scheme of the inner integrand, respectively. The efficiency of the method for EIG applications is demonstrated on three numerical examples.
