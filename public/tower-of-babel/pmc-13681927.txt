Psychometric evaluation of the persian version of the Social Self-Compassion Scale (SSCS): associations with Perceived Social Self-Efficacy, fear of negative evaluation, and loneliness among female undergraduate students
Authors: Azadeh Taheri, Roghieh Nooripour, Mohammadreza Shabanzadeh, Hamid Barani, Alison L Rose
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe Social Self-Compassion Scale (SSCS) was developed to assess self-compassion in social and interpersonal contexts. However, the psychometric properties of the SSCS have not yet been evaluated among Iranian female undergraduate students. This study aimed to translate the SSCS into Persian and examine its psychometric properties among Iranian female undergraduate students.MethodsA cross-sectional, correlational design was employed in 2023 using convenience sampling with a sample of 303 female undergraduate students from Alzahra University. Participants completed the Persian version of the SSCS and the Perceived Social Self-Efficacy (PSSE), Adaptive Disengagement Scale (ADS), Brief Fear of Negative Evaluation-II (BFNE-II), and Social and Emotional Loneliness Scale for Adults (SELSA-S). These additional measures were included to examine the convergent and divergent validity of the Persian SSCS by assessing its associations with theoretically related constructs. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 22 and AMOS version 24. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted to evaluate the factor structure and model fit, while Cronbach’s alpha and construct reliability (CR) were calculated to assess the internal consistency and reliability of the SSCS.ResultsThe Persian version of the SSCS demonstrated adequate face and content validity. Regarding reliability, the SSCS exhibited acceptable internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha values exceeding 0.70 for both the positive and negative factors. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) demonstrated acceptable model fit (CMIN/df = 1.42, CFI = 0.96, GFI = 0.93, NFI = 0.93, RMSEA = 0.03). The SSCS was positively correlated with social self-efficacy (r = 0.45, p < 0.01) and adaptive disengagement (r = 0.71, p < 0.01), and negatively correlated with fear of negative evaluation (r = − 0.67, p < 0.01) and social and emotional loneliness (r = − 0.45, p < 0.01).ConclusionThe Persian version of the SSCS demonstrated acceptable reliability and construct validity for assessing social self-compassion among Iranian female undergraduate students.Keywords: Social Self-Compassion Scale, Compassion, Validity, Reliability, Undergraduate Students

Introduction

Undergraduate students experience a range of biological, psychological, and social changes that may be accompanied by various psychological difficulties, including heightened emotional reactivity, aggressive behaviors, depression, and eating disorders [1]. Empirical evidence indicates that psychological and emotional problems are prevalent among individuals under the age of 24 [2]. During this period, individuals may also encounter distressing interpersonal experiences, such as bullying, criticism, and negative evaluation, which can elicit unpleasant emotions, including shame, guilt, anger, fear, and anxiety. Such experiences may be salient for undergraduate students, whose social environments involve ongoing interactions with peers and other members of their social networks. Recent studies suggest that self-compassion may help individuals manage distress and reduce negative emotional responses when facing challenging interpersonal experiences, including bullying and criticism [3–6]. Higher levels of self-compassion have also been associated with a lower risk of psychological difficulties, including depression and anxiety-related conditions such as social anxiety disorder [7–9]. Heightened self-criticism in interpersonal contexts has been associated with an increased propensity for suicidal ideation and behavior [10, 11]. Therefore, examining self-compassion in the context of adverse social and interpersonal experiences may provide a more specific understanding of how individuals respond to distress arising from their interactions with others.

Self-compassion refers to a set of cognitive, emotional, intentional, and motivational processes through which individuals respond to their own suffering, difficulties, and perceived shortcomings with understanding and care. In Neff’s original conceptualization, self-compassion comprises three core elements: self-kindness, common humanity, and mindfulness [12]. Self-kindness involves adopting a compassionate and understanding attitude toward oneself. Common humanity entails recognizing that imperfection and mistakes are shared aspects of the human experience. Mindfulness refers to maintaining balanced awareness of one’s thoughts, emotions, and experiences without becoming overly identified with them [13]. Subsequent conceptualizations have described these three components alongside their contrasting uncompassionate responses: self-kindness versus self-judgment, common humanity versus isolation, and mindfulness versus over-identification [13–15]. The uncompassionate components reflect tendencies to respond to personal difficulties with harsh self-evaluation, feelings of disconnection from others, and excessive identification with distress. Such uncompassionate self-responding has been associated with psychological difficulties, including eating disorders, depression, and suicidal behavior [10, 11].

Self-compassion may function as a mediating variable in the relationship between childhood experiences and maladaptive behaviors, such as self-harm, suicidality, and aggression [16]. Empirical evidence further suggests that self-compassion may play a protective role in the context of interpersonal trauma. For example, one study found that self-compassion mediated the association between cumulative interpersonal trauma exposure and the severity of complex post-traumatic stress disorder (CPTSD) among adults experiencing homelessness [17]. Increased self-compassion has been associated to greater social safety and lower levels of social anxiety, shame proneness, and self-criticism [18]. There is accumulating evidence suggesting that self-compassion plays a crucial role in promoting psychological well-being, physical health, secure attachment, prosocial behavior, and high-quality interpersonal relationships [19–22]. Despite the extensive benefits of self-compassion, it is important to note that, according to positive psychology, self-compassion does not entail the suppression of negative emotions but rather involves acknowledging and accepting distress while striving to experience it with benevolence and care [6].

The positive psychology approach emphasizes the enhancement of factors that contribute to individuals’ well-being, taking into account their psychological capacities [23, 24]. Aligned with this approach, it highlights various processes through which positive psychological factors may buffer against psychological difficulties, bolster mental health during stressful circumstances, and build psychological resources through adversity. Buffering factors, such as resilience and social support, help prevent or mitigate psychological distress and mental illness. In contrast, the bolstering effect refers to processes through which positive psychological characteristics and practices help individuals maintain mental health despite stressful or emotionally challenging circumstances. The building effect, on the other hand, refers to the development of personal qualities by transforming distress into an opportunity for growth, leading to outcomes such as enhanced intimacy and compassion following adversity [6]. Self-compassion is psychological phenomenon that can exert buffering and bolstering effects [14, 25, 26], and its central characteristics may be beneficial in stressful conditions [6]. Although research on self-compassion among younger populations has grown in recent years, the literature on this topic remains limited [13]. Self-compassion may be particularly relevant in social contexts among younger individuals, as social acceptance, interpersonal relationships, and concerns about how one is perceived by others can be especially salient during this developmental period. There is a notable need for further exploration of self-compassion in interpersonal contexts to better understand its potential contribution to the quality of social relationships and overall well-being. Examining self-compassion within the framework of social and interpersonal relationships may provide greater insight into how individuals respond to suffering and vulnerability in social and intimate situations and may inform future assessment and intervention efforts.

The Self-Compassion Scale (SCS) is widely used to assess general self-compassion [14]. The SCS assesses self-compassion in terms of self-kindness, common humanity, and mindfulness, along with their contrasting dimensions of self-judgment, isolation, and over-identification. Several related measures have also been developed to assess self-compassion and related constructs, including the Short-Form Self-Compassion Scale [27], and the Fear of Compassion Measure [18]. Recent systematic evidence suggests that the potential mental health benefits of self-compassion may involve adaptive coping and resilience, emotion regulation, self-related processes, and cognitive styles, providing potential pathways through which self-compassion may attenuate the effects of stress [28]. Although these measures have contributed substantially to the assessment of self-compassion, they assess self-compassion as a general psychological characteristic and do not specifically capture how individuals respond to distress arising within social and interpersonal contexts.

To provide a more focused analysis of social and interpersonal contexts, some scholars have recommended the development of domain-specific measures as supplementary tools [29–31]. Such measures can provide information about how psychological characteristics are expressed within specific domains of experience that may not be fully captured by general measures. In the context of self-compassion, assessing responses to social and interpersonal adversity may complement the assessment of general self-compassion by focusing on how individuals respond to experiences such as criticism, negative evaluation, stigma, and interpersonal difficulties.

In 2021, Rose and Kocovski developed the Social Self-Compassion Scale (SSCS) as a domain-specific measure designed to assess self-compassion in response to adverse experiences arising within interpersonal relationships and social contexts [32]. This 12-item scale consists of two factors reflecting a positive self-compassionate orientation and a negative self-critical orientation. The SSCS was validated among post-secondary students, with a mean age of approximately 19 years, from a single institution in Ontario, Canada. Exploratory Factor Analysis (CFA) supported the expected factor structure, and the two factors were significantly correlated. Internal consistency was acceptable, with Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.74 for the positive factor and 0.87 for the negative factor. The SSCS captures both self-compassionate and self-critical responses to interpersonal distress, allowing researchers to examine how individuals respond to social and interpersonal adversity along these related dimensions [32]. Whereas the SCS assesses self-compassion as general construct, the SSCS focuses specifically on self-compassionate and self-critical responses to social and interpersonal adversity.

Although several standardized scales in Iran assess self-compassion or related compassion constructs, including general self-compassion and compassionate love [4, 33, 34], no existing scale specifically measures self-compassionate and self-critical responses within the context of social and interpersonal relationships among Iranian female undergraduate students. The concept of social self-compassion is also embedded in Iranian religious and cultural traditions, including Zoroastrianism, Mithraism, Islam, and Sufism [35, 36]. Within this cultural framework, the development of scale measuring self-compassion in social interactions among Iranian female undergraduate students may offer greater cultural relevance. This study aimed to translate the SSCS into Persian and examine its psychometric properties in a sample of Iranian female undergraduate students.

Methods

A cross-sectional, correlational design was employed in 2023.

Participants

A total of 310 female undergraduate students from Alzahra University in Tehran were invited to participate. Following data screening, seven cases were excluded based on the prespecified data-quality criteria resulting in a final sample of 303 participants. The final sample ranged in age from 18 to 41 years (M = 20.48, SD = 2.93). No participants were excluded solely based on being older than the conventional age range of emerging adulthood. Although the mean age indicates that the sample was predominantly composed of individuals within the emerging adulthood period, the sample is described more precisely throughout the revised manuscript as female undergraduate students. Data collection was conducted via online survey distributed through Google Forms. Eligibility for participation required students to be actively enrolled in undergraduate programs at the university, self-identify as female, be at least 18 years of age, and demonstrate adequate proficiency in Persian to complete the questionnaire independently. Only those who voluntarily provided written informed consent and submitted complete responses that met the prespecified eligibility and data-screening criteria were retained for analysis. Individuals who were not officially enrolled at the time of data collection, submitted duplicate responses or responses that did not meet the prespecified eligibility criteria, reported a history of severe psychiatric conditions (e.g., psychotic disorders), or submitted duplicate responses were excluded from the final dataset.

Following data screening based on these criteria, seven participants were removed, resulting in a final sample of 303 students. These individuals represented a range of academic disciplines: 36.3% (n = 110) in liberal arts, 26.1% (n = 79) in mathematical and technical sciences, 20.1% (n = 61) in natural sciences and 17.5% (n = 53) in basic sciences.

Procedure

Google Forms was used to reduce manual data-entry and transcription errors because participants entered their responses directly into the electronic form and the responses were exported electronically for analysis. All questionnaire items were designated as required that prevented accidental item omission, and participants could review and revise their responses before submitting the form. These features reduced, but did not eliminate, the possibility of response or data entry errors. A convenience sampling method was employed. Undergraduate female students at Alzahra University were invited to participate through a variety of accessible platforms, including academic email lists, social media (e.g., Instagram, LinkedIn, Telegram, WhatsApp), and in-class announcements. This strategy aimed to enhance participant reach while maintaining the study’s focus on female undergraduate students through a homogeneous university-based sample. To ensure linguistic and conceptual equivalence, the Social Self-Compassion Scale (SSCS) was translated into Persian using forward-backward translation procedure [37]. Three independent translators conducted the forward and backward translations. A panel of experts reviewed all versions to resolve discrepancies and ensure conceptual clarity. Finally, an English linguistics specialist revised the final version for accuracy and fluency. All steps adhered to the International Test Commission (ITC) Guidelines for Translating and Adapting Tests to ensure cultural and psychometric validity.

Measures

The Social Self-Compassion Scale (SSCS)

The SSCS is a two-factor scale consisting of 12 items that was used in the present study to assess social self-compassion. Each item on the SSCS is rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly agree) to 5 (strongly disagree). The SSCS contains six positively worded items (Items 2, 3, 5, 6, 7, and 10) and six negatively worded items (Items 1, 4, 8, 9, 11, and 12). Before calculating the total score, the negatively worded items are reverse-coded so that higher scores consistently represent greater social self-compassion. The recoded item scores are then summed to produce the total SSCS score. Higher scores indicate greater levels of social self-compassion, reflecting the extent to which individuals adopt a kind, nonjudgmental, and accepting attitude toward themselves in social contexts [32]. The developers reported acceptable reliability for the SSCS, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of 0.74 and 0.87 for the positive and negative factors. They also reported a negative association between the SSCS and fear of negative evaluation, as measured by the Brief Fear of Negative Evaluation Scale-Second Version (BFNE-II), providing support for the convergent validity of the measure. After obtaining prior permission from the developers of the SSCS, Brislin’s method was used to translate the English version into Persian to ensure a more accurate and culturally appropriate translation [37]. Two bilingual experts who were independent of the research team and were not involved in the study design, data collection, or data analysis independently reviewed the translated versions. First, one expert translated the original English version of the SSCS into Persian. The second expert, who was blinded to the initial translation, independently back-translated the Persian version into English. Three additional individuals who were multilingual and proficient in Persian then reviewed the two translated versions of the SSCS. Their review confirmed that there were no substantial discrepancies in content or conceptual meaning between the Persian version and the original SSCS.

The Brief Fear of Negative Evaluation Scale – version II (BFNE-II)

It is a revised version of the Brief Fear of Negative Evaluation Scale (BFNE) consisting of 12 items that assess fear of negative evaluation that is a characteristic of social anxiety disorder [38, 39]. Participants rate each item on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (not at all characteristic of me) to 4 (extremely characteristic of me), and the item scores are summed to obtain a total score. Higher scores indicate greater fear of negative evaluation. A study examining the psychometric properties of the scale reported excellent internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.97. The BFNE-II was also found to be positively correlated with the Social Interaction Anxiety Scale [40]. A study examining the validity and reliability of the BFNE-II among Iranian students reported excellent internal consistency for the Persian version with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.92 [41].

The Adaptive Disengagement Scale (ADS)

This is a four-item measure that assesses individuals’ tendency to adaptively disengage from negative feedback through self-protection and self-safety strategies [42]. Participants rate each item on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). The item scores are summed to obtain a total score, with higher scores indicating a greater tendency toward adaptive disengagement. A study examined the psychometric properties of the ADS [42]. The scale demonstrated good internal consistency with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.85. It was also positively associated with the Psychological Well-Being Scale [42, 43]. In the present study, the ADS also demonstrated good internal consistency with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.80.

The Perceived Social Self-Efficacy Scale (PSSE)

The PSSE is a one-factor questionnaire consisting of 25 items. It assesses perceived social self-efficacy across six areas of social interaction: making friends, social assertiveness, initiating romantic relationships, performing in public situations, participating in groups and parties, and seeking or accepting help [31]. Participants rate their confidence in their social skills and abilities on a 5-point Likert scale. Responses range from 1 (not at all confident) to 5 (completely confident). The item scores are summed to obtain a total score, with higher scores indicating greater perceived social self-efficacy. A study examined the psychometric properties of the PSSE. The scale demonstrated strong internal consistency with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.94. The PSSE was positively correlated with personal self-esteem. It was negatively correlated with shyness, depression, and social anxiety [31]. In the present study, the PSSE demonstrated satisfactory internal consistency with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.87.

The Short Version of Social and Emotional Loneliness Scale (SELSA-S)

The SELSA-S is a multidimensional scale that assesses loneliness across three domains: social, family, and romantic loneliness [44]. Social loneliness refers to a lack of friends and a satisfying social network. Family loneliness reflects a lack of family support and attachment difficulties in caregiver-child relationships. Romantic loneliness refers to a lack of attachment in affective or loving relationships. Participants rate each of the 15 items on a 7-point Likert scale. Responses range from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). The item scores are summed within each domain to obtain separate scores for social, family, and romantic loneliness, with higher scores indicating greater loneliness in the corresponding domain. The developers reported adequate internal consistency for the SELSA-S with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients ranging from 0.87 to 0.90 [44]. A study examined the validity and reliability of the SELSA-S among Iranian participants. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were 0.92 for romantic loneliness, 0.84 for social loneliness, and 0.78 for family loneliness [45]. Another study found a strong positive correlation between the SELSA-S and the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support [46].

Face and content validity

Face validity of the Persian version of the 12-item SSCS was assessed using qualitative and quantitative methods. In the qualitative phase, five experts, including two psychologists and three university faculty members, evaluated the items for clarity, relevance, difficulty, and the presence of semantically ambiguous or culturally inappropriate terms. Ten undergraduate students who were not part of the main sample also reviewed the items for potential ambiguities, complexities, or irrelevancies. In the quantitative phase, the revised 12-item scale was administered to eight individuals from the target population. Item impact scores were calculated using the formula: Impact Score = Frequency × Importance. Items with an impact score of 1.5 or above were considered acceptable. Content validity was assessed using qualitative and quantitative methods. In the qualitative stage, 12 psychology experts evaluated the appropriateness of the expressions and grammar used in the scale, and their feedback was used to revise the items. In the quantitative stage, 10 experts evaluated the simplicity, relevance, and clarity of the 12 items using a four-point Likert scale. The necessity of each item was also evaluated using a three-point scale to calculate the Content Validity Ratio (CVR). The Content Validity Index (CVI) and CVR were calculated based on these evaluations. A CVR value of 0.62 or higher and a CVI value of 0.70 or higher were considered acceptable criteria for item retention.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 22 and AMOS version 24. The normality of data was assessed by examining skewness and kurtosis values. Values between ± 2 and ± 5 were considered indicative of normal distribution [47]. The results showed that the data fell within the acceptable range, confirming the normality of the data. Potential outliers were identified using Mahalanobis D². The largest Mahalanobis D² value was divided by the 12 items of the SSCS. A value below 4 indicated the absence of outliers. Values above 4 indicated potential outliers and led to the removal of problematic cases [47]. Following this procedure, the results indicated that there were no outliers in the data. Descriptive statistics, including means and standard deviations, were calculated for all study variables. Pearson’s correlation coefficients were calculated to examine the relationships among the study variables. To examine the validity of the Social Self-Compassion Scale (SSCS), Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was conducted using AMOS version 24. Several fit indices were examined to evaluate the model fit. These included CMIN/df, CFI, TLI, GFI, NFI, RMSEA, and IFI [48]. Cronbach’s alpha and Composite Reliability (CR) coefficients were calculated to assess the internal consistency and reliability of the SSCS. Face validity and content validity of the Persian version of the SSCS were also evaluated. Face validity was assessed using item impact scores, and content validity was assessed using the Content Validity Index (CVI) and Content Validity Ratio (CVR).

Face validity

All 12 items exceeded the prespecified impact score threshold of 1.5, indicating acceptable face validity. The findings showed that the target population perceived the items as clear, relevant, and important.

Content validity

The CVR values for all 12 items exceeded the critical value of 0.62 for a panel of ten experts [49], indicating that all items met the criterion for retention (Table 1). The CVI values for simplicity, relevance, and clarity also met the prespecified criterion of 0.70 or higher [50]. These findings provided further support for the content validity of all 12 items (Table 1).

The CVI was calculated as CVI = ne/N, where ne represents the number of experts who rated the item 3 or higher and N represents the total number of experts [51].

Construct validity

To assess the construct validity of the Social Self-Compassion Scale (SSCS), the CFA was conducted. The CFA included two dimensions of the scale, with the respective items. The factor loadings for each item were evaluated. All item loadings exceeded the acceptable criterion of 0.40 (Fig. 1). This indicates that the items were adequately associated with their respective factors [52]. Following this, model fit indices were examined to assess the overall adequacy of the model. After applying the necessary corrections (including establishing covariance between error terms), the following fit indices were obtained: CMIN/df = 1.42, CFI = 0.97, GFI = 0.96, TLI = 0.97, NFI = 0.93, RMSEA = 0.03, and IFI = 0.97. These values indicate excellent fit of the model, confirming that the two dimensions of the SSCS and their associated items are well-validated. The construct validity of the SSCS was confirmed based on these two factors (Table 2).

Convergent and divergent validity

To assess the convergent validity of the SSCS, correlations were examined between social self-compassion and theoretically related constructs, including social self-efficacy and adaptive disengagement. Social self-compassion was positively correlated with social self-efficacy (r = 0.45, p < 0.01) and adaptive disengagement (r = 0.71, p < 0.01). These findings were consistent with theoretically expected direction of associations and provided evidence supporting the convergent validity of the Persian version of the SSCS (Table 4).

To examine divergent validity, correlations were assessed between social self-compassion and theoretically distinct constructs, including fear of negative evaluation and social and emotional loneliness. Social self-compassion was negatively correlated with fear of negative evaluation (r = − 0.67, p < 0.01) and social and emotional loneliness (r = − 0.45, p < 0.01). The observed pattern of associations was consistent with the theoretically expected inverse relationships between social self-compassion and these indicators of interpersonal and social difficulties. The correlation pattern provided additional evidence for the construct validity of the Persian version of the SSCS.

Discussion

This research aimed to further establish the psychometric properties of the Social Self-Compassion Scale (SSCS) and evaluate its use among Iranian female undergraduate students. The findings provide preliminary evidence that the Persian version of the SSCS is a suitable measure of social self-compassion in this population. Given the cultural and religious context of Iran, the scale may help address an existing gap by providing a measure of self-compassion in social and interpersonal relationships among Iranian female undergraduate students. Additional measures were included to examine convergent and divergent validity. The SSCS total score was significantly correlated with the external measures in theoretically expected directions, although subscale-specific correlations were not examined. Findings are in keeping with the literature that self-compassion is associated with various indicators of psychological wellbeing [53–56]. The total social self-compassion score was positively correlated with perceived social self-efficacy that was in line with findings of the scale developers [32, 56]. Based on a body of literature, cultivating mindfulness and non-judgmental acceptance of oneself can enhance self-regulation and improve self-care skills during stressful conditions [57–59]. It is conceivable that those who are more self-compassionate and less self-critical can adopt a warm, kind, accepting and understanding manner toward them through using a balanced and mindful approach involving staying present in the here-and-now. These processes are important to engage in when facing distressing and difficult interpersonal situations, and may lead to a strengthening of confidence and competence with regards to handling the social tasks of founding or maintaining a relationship. Indeed, self-compassion (and social self-compassion), may foster self-efficacy through increasing positive feelings towards the self [60, 61].

An ongoing debate concerns whether positive and negative components of self-compassion should be integrated into a single overall score or examined as distinct dimensions. A recent study noted that the available evidence does not definitively support either single-dimensional or two-dimensional interpretation of self-compassion. It also emphasized that the relationship between compassionate and uncompassionate self-responding may vary across contexts and individuals [62]. A recent meta-analytic evidence suggests that a general bipolar factor can coexist with specific component factors that may provide additional information when assessing self-compassion [63]. In the present study, the two-factor structure of the SSCS, comprising positive and negative components, supports distinguishing these components at the measurement level. At the same time, the use of total score reflects an integrated scoring approach. However, because associations with external variables were examined only for the total SSCS score, the present findings cannot determine whether the positive and negative components exhibit distinct patterns of association with theoretically related constructs.

It is noteworthy that the ADS was positively correlated with the SSCS total score, a finding that is consistent with findings reported in previous studies [32, 56]. Some research has shown that individuals who engage in positive distraction when faced with unpleasant situations tend to be better able to cope adaptively with aversive experiences and maintain their self-esteem [42, 64]. They are kinder and less judgmental towards themselves as they tend not to fixate on flaws or inadequacies when facing negative feedback. Distractive strategies may be considered adaptive when they are accompanied by acceptance rather than avoidance. From the perspective of acceptance and commitment therapy, disengagement from undesirable situations, when accompanied by acceptance and mindfulness, may promote psychological flexibility, reduce internal struggles, and contribute to greater self-compassion [65–67].

Another notable finding from the present research was the strong negative correlation between social self-compassion and fear of negative evaluation that was also consistent with prior literature [32, 56]. Previous studies have indicated that fear of negative evaluation is significantly negatively correlated with self-compassion and significantly positively correlated with self-criticism [68, 69]. According to a substantial body of literature [69–72], the distressing emotions elicited by negative evaluations from others may be so overwhelming that individuals experience intolerance, impatience, and self-blame in response to perceived faults and inadequacies within social contexts. This emotional burden may result in the avoidance of subsequent social interactions due to an intensified fear of further negative evaluation and disapproval. Individuals may develop excessive perfectionism and a tendency toward over-identification. This may involve obsessive rumination and fixation on perceived shortcomings as a way of avoiding negative judgment from others [73–75]. When individuals are subjected to negative evaluation, they may experience heightened feelings of rejection and diminished self-esteem that can subsequently trigger elevated levels of self-doubt and self-criticism. These outcomes are consistent with findings from a recent study conducted among nursing undergraduates that revealed strong associations between fear of negative evaluation, experiences of rejection or abandonment, poor interpersonal skills, and perfectionism in romantic relationships [75]. Social self-compassion may function as a protective psychological resource by reducing the excessive need for external validation and approval, thereby supporting the maintenance and potentially enhancing self-esteem, self-confidence, and a sense of competence.

The overall SSCS score demonstrated a strong negative association with SELSA-S scores, aligning with findings from recent studies suggesting that self-compassion and dispositional mindfulness are inversely related to loneliness, with rejection sensitivity serving as a mediating factor [76, 77]. A study reported that the association between self-compassion and loneliness was mediated by social anxiety and fear of negative evaluation [78].

Biased cognitive processing, defined as the tendency to interpret stimuli and experiences in ways that reinforce negative self-perceptions, can significantly exacerbate feelings of loneliness in social contexts by reinforcing self-critical and judgmental cognitions. This perspective is consistent with the theoretical framework that posits that experiences of loneliness may activate the emotional threat system, leading individuals to perceive their social environment as unsafe or hostile and subsequently engage in unkind, avoidant, or even aggressive behaviors toward themselves [79]. A self-compassionate orientation within interpersonal settings may be associated with more adaptive responses to loneliness and interpersonal challenges helping individuals manage negative emotional consequences associated with loneliness through a kinder and more understanding self-concept and more mindful responses to interpersonal difficulties [80, 81].

It is important to acknowledge certain limitations when interpreting and generalizing the findings of this study. The sample consisted of female undergraduate students from Alzahra University in Tehran, a single-gender university for women. Although this university-based sample provides valuable insights into social self-compassion and related constructs among female undergraduate students, the single-institution and female-only composition of the sample may limit the generalizability of the findings to more diverse student populations. The sample represents an important university-based population for examining social self-compassion, perceived social self-efficacy, fear of negative evaluation, and loneliness given the relevance of these constructs to students’ interpersonal and psychological experiences. Notwithstanding these limitations, the present study provides preliminary evidence that the Persian version of the SSCS is a reliable and valid measure of social self-compassion among female undergraduate students.

Future research should include more heterogeneous samples, including male and non-student participants from different educational and occupational backgrounds, geographical regions, and age groups. Cross-cultural replications are encouraged to further validate the psychometric properties of the SSCS and to examine its applicability across different sociocultural contexts. Future studies could further strengthen the validity evidence for the SSCS by examining its associations with alternative measures of theoretically related and distinct constructs.

In conclusion, the Persian SSCS showed promise as a research instrument for assessing social self-compassion among female undergraduate students. Further evaluation in clinical, male, and more diverse community samples is needed before broader clinical application can be recommended. By offering a multidimensional measure of social self-compassion, the SSCS enables psychologists and counselors to gain a deeper understanding of individuals’ self-compassion in the interpersonal relationships. This enhanced insight allows for the implementation of more targeted and effective interventions, including compassion-focused therapy and dialectical behavior therapy. The SSCS may aid in identifying and reducing self-critical tendencies, fostering increased self-compassion in socially challenging situations, promoting interpersonal resilience and flexibility, and enhancing the quality of interpersonal communication. The instrument holds potential for advancing understanding of the psychological mechanisms underlying reduced activation of the emotional threat system, encouraging further clinical inquiry across a spectrum of psychiatric conditions.
