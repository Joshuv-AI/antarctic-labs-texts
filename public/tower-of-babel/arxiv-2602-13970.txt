Introduction

All graphs considered in this paper are simple and loopless. Throughout the paper, we use
\mathbb{N}
to denote the set of nonnegative integers, and
\mathbb{N}^{G}
denote the set of mappings from
V(G)
to
\mathbb{N}
. For a positive integer
k
, let
[k]=\{1,\ldots,k\}
.

Suppose
G
is a graph,
f,g\in\mathbb{N}^{G}
are two functions, with
f(v)\geq g(v)
for each
v\in V(G)
. An
f
-list assignment
of
G
is a mapping
L
which assigns to each vertex
v
of
G
a set
L(v)
of
f(v)
integers as
permissible colors
. A
g
-fold coloring
of
G
is a mapping
C
which assigns to each vertex
v
of
G
a set
C(v)
of
g(v)
colors such that
C(v)\cap C(u)=\emptyset
for any two adjacent vertices
u
and
v
. If
g\equiv b
(i.e.,
g(v)=b
for any
v\in V(G)
), then
(L,g)
-colorable is denoted by
(L,b)
-colorable.
If
L(v)=[a]
for each
v\in V(G)
, then
(L,b)
-colorable is called
(a,b)
-colorable. The
b
-fold chromatic number

\chi_{b}(G)
of
G
is the least
k
such that
G
is
(k,b)
-colorable. When
b=1
,
\chi_{1}(G)
is the usual chromatic number, denoted by
\chi(G)
. The
fractional chromatic number
of
G
is defined as
\chi_{f}(G)=\inf\{\frac{a}{b}:\text{$G$ is $(a,b)$-colorable}\}
.

Similarly, we say
G
is
(f,g)
-choosable
if for every
f
-list assignment
L
,
G
is
(L,g)
-colorable. Some special cases of
(f,g)
-choosability have alternate names:

•

If
g\equiv b
, then
(f,g)
-choosable is called
(f,b)
-choosable.

•

If
f\equiv k
, then
(f,b)
-choosable is called
(k,b)
-choosable.

•

If
b=1
, then
(f,1)
-choosable is called
f
-choosable.

•

If
f\equiv k
, then
f
-choosable is called
k
-choosable.

The
choice number

ch(G)
of
G
is the minimum
k
such that

G
is
k
-choosable. The
b
-fold choice number

ch_{b}(G)
of
G

is the minimum
k
such that
G
is
(k,b)
-choosable. The
fractional choice number
of
G
is defined as
ch_{f}(G)=\inf\{\frac{a}{b}:\text{$G$ is $(a,b)$-choosable}\}
.
The concept of list coloring of graphs was introduced in the 1970s by Vizing
[
13
]
and independently by Erdős, Rubin and Taylor
[
7
]
, and has been studied extensively in the literature. The area offers numerous challenging
problems and has attracted increasing attention since the 1990s.

From the definitions we have, for any graph
G
,

\chi_{f}(G)\leq\chi(G)\leq ch(G)~\text{and}~\chi_{f}(G)\leq ch_{f}(G).

It is known that there exist bipartite graphs with arbitrary large choice number. However, Alon, Tuza and Voigt
[
1
]
proved that
ch_{f}(G)=\chi_{f}(G)
for every infinite graph
G
. Hence
ch_{f}(G)
is not a genuinely new parameter. This motivates the concept of
strong fractional choice number
of a graph, which was introduced in
[
19
]
and defined as

ch_{f}^{s}(G)=\inf\{r:G\text{ is $(a,b)$-choosable for all integers $a$ and $b$ with }a/b\geq r\}.

The strong fractional choice number of a family
\mathcal{G}
is the supremum of
ch_{f}^{s}(G)
over all
G\in\mathcal{G}
.
It follows from the definition that
ch_{f}^{s}(G)\geq ch(G)-1
. This lower bound can be attained: for example, Xu and Zhu
[
18
]
showed that every bipartite
3
-choice critical graph
G
satisfies
ch_{f}^{s}(G)=2
, while its choice number is 3. For the upper bound, it remains open and asked by Xu and Zhu
[
17
]
that whether
ch^{s}_{f}(G)\leq ch(G)
for any graph
G
.

Let
a\geq b
be two positive integers. It is straightforward that every
(a,b)
-colorable graph is also
(am,bm)
-colorable for every positive integer
m
. Erdős, Rubin and Taylor
[
7
]
asked whether every graph that is
(a,b)
-choosable must also be
(am,bm)
-choosable. If the answer is positive, then it would imply that
ch^{s}_{f}(G)\leq ch(G)
for any graph
G
. Tuza and Voigt
[
12
]
proved this holds for
a=2
and
b=1
. However, for more general settings, Dvořák, Hu and Sereni
[
5
]
proved that for any integer
k\geq 4
, there is a
k
-choosable graph which is not
(2k,2)
-choosable. Nevertheless, it is still possible that
ch^{s}_{f}(G)\leq ch(G)
for any graph
G
.

It is well known that every planar graph is
4
-colorable by the Four-Color Theorem, yet there exists planar graphs that are not
4
-choosable
[
14
]
. Zhu
[
19
]
strengthened this by showing that for every positive integer
m
, there exists a planar graph which is not
(4m+\lfloor\frac{2m-1}{9}\rfloor,m)
-choosable. The value
4m+\lfloor\frac{2m-1}{9}\rfloor
was later improved to
4m+\lfloor\frac{m-1}{3}\rfloor
by Xu and Zhu
[
17
]
, and then to
4m+\lfloor\frac{2m-1}{5}\rfloor
by Chappell
[
2
]
. On the other hand, Thomassen
[
11
]
proved that every planar graph is
5
-choosable. Voigt
[
16
]
extended this by showing that every planar graph is
(5m,m)
-choosable for every positive integer
m
. Consequently, for the family of planar graphs
\mathcal{P}
, we have

4+\frac{2}{5}\leq ch^{s}_{f}(\mathcal{P})\leq 5.

It remains unknown if
ch^{s}_{f}(\mathcal{P})<5
, this would follow if the following conjecture holds:

Conjecture 1
(
[
19
]
)

There exists a constant integer
m
such that every planar graph is
(5m-1,m)
-choosable.

By the Four-Color Theorem, every planar graph is
(4m,m)
-colorable for any positive integer
m
. Without using the Four-Color Theorem, Hilton, Rado and Scott
[
9
]
proved that every planar graph is
(5m-1,m)
-colorable with
m=|V(G)|+1
. Cranston and Rabern
[
3
]
proved that every planar graph is
(9,2)
-colorable. However, the technique of contractions of nonadjacent vertices used in the proof
[
3
]
can not be applied to the study of list coloring, and Conjecture
1
remains largely open.

In this paper, we focus on the strong fractional choice number of the family of triangle-free planar graphs. The famous Gröstzch Theorem
[
8
]
asserts that every triangle-free planar graph is
3
-colorable. Dvořák, Sereni and Volec
[
6
]
proved that for every
n
-vertex triangle-free planar graph
G
,
\chi_{f}(G)\leq 3-\frac{1}{3n+1}
. Later, Dvořák and Hu
[
4
]
improved this bound to
3-\frac{1}{2n+1}
. However, Voigt
[
15
]
showed that there exists a triangle-free planar graph that is not
3
-choosable. Jiang and Zhu
[
10
]
further strengthened this result by showing that for every positive integer
m
, there exists a triangle-free planar graph that is not
(3m+\lceil\frac{m}{17}\rceil,m)
-choosable. On the other hand, every triangle-free planar graph is
3
-degenerate, hence it is
(4m,m)
-choosable for any positive integer
m
. Consequently, for the family of triangle-free planar graphs
\mathcal{P}_{3}
,

3+\frac{1}{17}\leq ch^{s}_{f}(\mathcal{P}_{3})\leq 4.

It is unknown whether the upper bound is tight. Jiang and Zhu
[
10
]
posed the following question, whose positive answer would imply
ch^{s}_{f}(\mathcal{P}_{3})<4
.

Question 2
(
[
10
]
)

Does there exist a positive integer
k
such that every triangle-free planar graph is
(4k-1,k)
-choosable ?

Note that the smallest possible value
k
in the question above is
k=2
. The special case
m=1
of our main result below answers this question positively with
k=4
.

Theorem 3

Every triangle-free planar graph is
(15m,4m)
-choosable for any positive integer
m
. As a corollary,
3+\frac{1}{17}\leq ch^{s}_{f}(\mathcal{P}_{3})\leq 3+\frac{3}{4}
.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce a new language to describe how to extend a partial coloring of a graph to a complete coloring, and provide several useful lemmas. In Section
3
, we show that certain subgraphs are reducible in a minimum counterexample. In Section
4
, we apply the discharging method to obtain a contradiction, thereby proving Theorem

.

2
Preliminaries

For a graph
G
and a vertex
u\in V(G)
,
N_{G}(u)
is the set of neighbors of
u
, and
d_{G}(u)=|N_{G}(u)|
is the degree of
u
. For brevity, we simply write
d(u)
when the underlying graph is clear from the context. For a subset
S\subseteq V(G)
,
G-S
is the graph obtained from
G
by deleting the vertices in
S
together with all edges incident to them, and
G[S]
is the subgraph of
G
induced by
S
. For a subgraph
H
of
G
and a vertex
u\in V(G)
(not necessarily in
V(H)
),
N_{H}(u)=N_{G}(u)\cap V(H)
and
d_{H}(u)=|N_{H}(u)|
.

For
f\in\mathbb{N}^{G}
and a subset
U\subseteq V(G)
,
let
f|_{U}
denote the restriction of
f
to
U
.
When no confusion arises, we simply write
f
instead of
f|_{U}
.
The same convention applies to the restriction
L|_{U}
of a list assignment
L
.
Let
\mathcal{T}
denote the collection of triples
(G,L,g)
, where
G
is a graph,

L
is a list assignment of
G
, and
g\in\mathbb{N}^{G}
specifies the number of
colors required at each vertex.

Unlike ordinary
1
-fold coloring, where a vertex can be discarded once it is
assigned a color, in multiple list coloring a vertex
v
with
g(v)>0
must
remain under consideration until all its color demands are satisfied.
Accordingly, colors may be assigned incrementally, while both the list
assignment and the demand function are updated to reflect these partial
assignments. To formalize this process, we describe such local updates as operations acting on
triples
(G,L,g)\in\mathcal{T}
, which allows us to track changes in a
systematic way and avoids repeating routine update arguments in later proofs.

2.1
Local reduction operations

We begin by formalizing the operation of assigning colors to a subset of vertices,
while leaving the remaining demands to be satisfied later.

Definition 4

Let
(G,L,g)\in\mathcal{T}
and let
\phi
be a partial
(L,g)
-coloring of
G

on
U\subseteq V(G)
. (We interpret
\phi(v)=\emptyset
for
v\notin U
.)
The
partial coloring operation

\mathsf{ParCol}_{\phi,U}:(G,L,g)\mapsto(G,L^{\prime},g^{\prime})

is defined by, for each
v\in V(G)
,

g^{\prime}(v)=g(v)-|\phi(v)|,\quad\text{and}\quad L^{\prime}(v)=L(v)\setminus\Bigl(\phi(v)\cup\bigcup_{u\in N_{G}(v)}\phi(u)\Bigr).

We say
\mathsf{ParCol}_{\phi,U}
is
legal
if
|L^{\prime}(v)|\geq g^{\prime}(v)
for all
v\in V(G)
.

Remark 1

We mainly use the following special cases of the partial coloring operation

\mathsf{ParCol}_{\phi,U}
.

(1)

If
U=\{u\}
and
\phi(u)=A
, we write it as

\mathsf{ParCol}(u,A)
.

(2)

If
U=\{u\}
,
uv\in E(G)
, and
\phi
satisfies

\phi(u)\subseteq L(u)\setminus L(v)
and
|\phi(u)|=k
,
we write it as
\mathsf{ParCol}(u\mid v,k)
.

(3)

If
U=\{u_{1},u_{2}\}
,
v
is a common neighbor of
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
, and
\phi
satisfies

\phi(u_{1})=S\cup R
and
\phi(u_{2})=T\cup R
for some

S\subseteq L(u_{1})\setminus L(v)
,

T\subseteq L(u_{2})\setminus L(v)
,

R\subseteq L(u_{1})\cap L(u_{2})\cap L(v)
,
and
|S|+|T|+|R|=k
,
we write it as
\mathsf{ParCol}(\{u_{1},u_{2}\}\mid v,k^{*})
.

Note that in Remark
1
(2)
, the partial coloring guarantees that the vertex
u

is assigned
k
colors without reducing the available colors of its neighbor
v
,
and hence saves
k
colors for
v
.
In Remark
1
(3)
, the partial coloring assigns a total of
k+|R|
colors to
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
, while using only
|R|
colors from
L(v)
, and therefore saves
k
colors
with respect to
v
.

In many situations, a vertex can be safely removed once its list is large
enough compared with the remaining color demands of its neighbors.
This allows us to reduce the graph while preserving the possibility of
extending a coloring.

Definition 5

The
degenerate deletion operation

\mathsf{DegDel}(u):\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{T}

is defined by

\mathsf{DegDel}(u)(G,L,g)=(G^{\prime},L^{\prime},g^{\prime}),

where
G^{\prime}=G-u
,
L^{\prime}=L|_{V(G^{\prime})}
, and
g^{\prime}=g|_{V(G^{\prime})}
.
We say that
\mathsf{DegDel}(u)
is
legal
if

|L(u)|\geq g(u)+\sum_{v\in N_{G}(u)}g(v)
.

Definition 6

A
reduction scheme
is an operator

\Omega=\mathsf{Red}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\ldots,\theta_{k}):\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{T}
,
where for each
i
, one of the following holds:

\theta_{i}=\langle u\rangle
, representing the degenerate deletion operation

\mathsf{DegDel}(u)
;

\theta_{i}=\langle U,\phi\rangle
, representing the partial coloring operation

\mathsf{ParCol}_{\phi,U}
.
The reduction scheme
\Omega
is defined recursively by

\mathsf{Red}(\langle u\rangle)(G,L,g)=\mathsf{DegDel}(u)(G,L,g),\quad\mathsf{Red}(\langle U,\phi\rangle)(G,L,g)=\mathsf{ParCol}_{\phi,U}(G,L,g),

and for
k\geq 2
,

\mathsf{Red}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\ldots,\theta_{k})(G,L,g)=\mathsf{Red}(\theta_{k})\bigl(\mathsf{Red}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\ldots,\theta_{k-1})(G,L,g)\bigr).

We say that the reduction scheme

\mathsf{Red}(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k})
is
legal

for
(G,L,g)\in\mathcal{T}
if

\mathsf{Red}(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k-1})
is legal for
(G,L,g)
and

\theta_{k}
is legal for

\mathsf{Red}(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k-1})(G,L,g)
.

Remark 2

In the construction of reduction schemes, we will use the following
special forms of
\langle U,\phi\rangle
corresponding to the partial coloring
operations introduced in Remark
1
:

\theta=\langle u,A\rangle,\quad\theta=\langle u\mid v,k\rangle\quad\text{and}\quad\theta=\langle\{u_{1},u_{2}\}\mid v,m^{*}\rangle,

which represent
\mathsf{ParCol}(u,A)
,
\mathsf{ParCol}(u\mid v,k)
, and

\mathsf{ParCol}(\{u_{1},u_{2}\}\mid v,m^{*})
, respectively.

Observation 7

Let
(G,L,g)\in\mathcal{T}
and let

\Omega=\mathsf{Red}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\ldots,\theta_{k})

be a reduction scheme that is legal for
(G,L,g)
.
If
\Omega
removes all vertices of
G
, then
G
admits an
(L,g)
-coloring.

2.2
Nice subgraphs and associated functions

In this section, we develop a framework for proving reducibility of configurations.
We introduce a notion of
nice
subgraphs and associate with each such subgraph
a pair of functions
(f_{H},g_{H})
. We then prove a key lemma that will be used repeatedly
in reducibility arguments.

Definition 8

For a subgraph
H
of
G
, let

U_{H}=\{u\in V(H)\mid d_{H}(u)\geq d_{G}(u)-1\},

and let
F_{H}
be the graph induced by
V(H)\setminus U_{H}
.
We say that
H
is
nice
if
U_{H}\neq\emptyset
,
d_{H}(u)\geq d_{G}(u)-2
for each
v\in V(H)
, and each component of
F_{H}
is isomorphic to a complete bipartite graph
K_{1,3}
or an
\ell
-vertex path
P_{\ell}
for some
\ell\in[4]
.

Note that if
H
is nice, then
V(F_{H})=\{u\in V(H)\mid d_{G}(u)-d_{H}(u)=2\}
.

Definition 9

For a nice subgraph
H
of
G
, let
f_{H},g_{H}\in\mathbb{N}^{H}
be two functions
defined as follows.

(1)

If
u
lies in a component of
F_{H}
isomorphic to
P_{1}
, then

f_{H}(u)=7m
and
g_{H}(u)=4m
.

(2)

If
u
lies in a component of
F_{H}
isomorphic to
P_{2}
, then

f_{H}(u)=6m
and
g_{H}(u)=3m
.

(3)

If
u
lies in a component of
F_{H}
isomorphic to
P_{3}
, then

f_{H}(u)=5m
, and

g_{H}(u)=2m
if
d_{F_{H}}(u)=2
, while

g_{H}(u)=3m
if
d_{F_{H}}(u)=1
.

(4)

If
u
lies in a component of
F_{H}
isomorphic to
P_{4}
, then

(f_{H},g_{H})(u)=(5m,3m)
if
d_{F_{H}}(u)=1
, and
(f_{H},g_{H})(u)=(4m,2m)
if
d_{F_{H}}(u)=2
.

(5)

If
u
lies in a component of
F_{H}
isomorphic to
K_{1,3}
, then

f_{H}(u)=4m
, and

g_{H}(u)=m
if
d_{F_{H}}(u)=3
, while

g_{H}(u)=3m
if
d_{F_{H}}(u)=1
.

(6)

If
u\in V(H)\setminus V(F_{H})
, then
g_{H}(u)=4m
, and

f_{H}(u)=15m-4m(d_{G}(u)-d_{H}(u))-\sum_{v\in N_{F_{H}}(u)}(4m-g_{H}(v)).

Lemma 10

Let
G
be a minimal triangle-free planar graph that is not
(15m,4m)
-choosable for some integer
m
. If
H
is a nice subgraph of
G
, then
H
is not
(f_{H},g_{H})
-choosable. Consequently, by Observation
7
,
there is no legal reduction scheme that deletes all vertices of
H
.

Proof.

If
f_{H}(v)<g_{H}(v)
for some
v\in V(H)
, then
H
is not
(f_{H},g_{H})
-choosable obviously. Thus we assume that
f_{H}(v)\geq g_{H}(v)
for every vertex
v\in V(H)
.

Let
L
be a
15m
-list assignment of
G
. By the minimality of
G
, the graph
G-U_{H}
has an
(L,4m)
-coloring
\phi
since
U_{H}\neq\emptyset
. Define a list assignment
L_{0}
of
H
by

L_{0}(u)=L(u)\setminus\{\phi(v)\mid v\in N_{G-V(H)}(u)\},~~u\in V(H).

Thus by deleting extra colors not in
\phi(u)
, we assume that

•

|L_{0}(u)|=7m
for each
u\in V(F_{H})
, and

•

|L_{0}(v)|=15m-4m|N_{G-U_{H}}(v)|=15m-4m(d_{G}(v)-d_{H}(v))
for each
v\in V(H)\setminus V(F_{H})
.

Moreover, since
F_{H}
is a subgraph of
G-U_{H}
and
\phi
is an
(L,4m)
-coloring of
G-U_{H}
, it follows that
F_{H}
has an
(L_{0},4m)
-coloring. If
H
had an
(L_{0},4m)
-coloring
\phi^{\prime}
, then
\phi\cup\phi^{\prime}
would be an
(L,4m)
-coloring of
G
, a contradiction. Hence
H
is not
(L_{0},4m)
-colorable.

If
F_{H}
is an independent set, then by Definition
9
(1)
and
(6)
,
L_{0}
is an
f_{H}
-list assignment and
g_{H}(u)=4m
for each
u\in V(H)
. Consequently,
H
is not
(f_{H},g_{H})
-colorable, as desired.

Now assume that some component of
F_{H}
is isomorphic to
K_{1,3}
or to
P_{\ell}
for some
\ell\in\{2,3,4\}
, and let
F_{H}^{*}
be the union of all such components. In the remainder of the proof, we construct a partial coloring
\psi
of
F_{H}^{*}
such that the operation
\mathsf{ParCol}_{\psi,V(F_{H}^{*})}
is legal and
|L_{1}(v)|\geq f_{H}(v)
for each
v\in V(H)
, with
g_{1}=g_{H}
, where

(H,L_{1},g_{1})=\mathsf{ParCol}_{\psi,V(F_{H}^{*})}(H,L_{0},4m).

This implies that
H
is not
(L_{1},g_{1})
-colorable, and hence is not
(f_{H},g_{H})
-choosable.
Therefore, it suffices to define
\psi
on each component of
F_{H}^{*}
.

Let
F
be such a component. We define
\psi
on
F
by considering four cases.

Case 1.

F\cong P_{2}
, say
F=v_{1}v_{2}
.

Since
F
admits an
(L,4m)
-coloring, we have
|L(v_{1})\cup L(v_{2})|\geq 8m
. As
|L(v_{1})|=|L(v_{2})|=7m
, there exists an
m
-set
A\subseteq L(v_{1})\setminus L(v_{2})
and an
m
-set
B\subseteq L(v_{2})\setminus L(v_{1})
. Let
\psi(v_{1})=A
and
\psi(v_{2})=B
. It is easy to see that
|L_{1}(v_{i})|\geq 6m=f_{H}(v_{i})
and
g_{1}(v_{i})=3m

2.3
Two key techniques for constructing reduction schemes

Lemma 11

Suppose
m
is a positive integer,
A,B,C
are three sets. If
|A|+|B|\geq|C|+m
, then there exist sets
S\subseteq A\setminus C
,
T\subseteq B\setminus C
and
R\subseteq A\cap B\cap C
such that
|S|+|T|+|R|=m
.

Proof.

By the assumption
|A|+|B|\geq|C|+m
and the inclusion-exclusion principle,
we have

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle\leq|A|+|B|-|C|=|A\cup B|+|A\cap B|-|C|

\displaystyle=|(A\cup B)\cap C|+|(A\cup B)\setminus C|+|A\cap B|-|C|

\displaystyle\leq|(A\cup B)\setminus C|+|A\cap B|

\displaystyle=|(A\setminus C)\cup(B\setminus C)|+|A\cap B|

\displaystyle=|A\setminus C|+|B\setminus C|-|(A\cap B)\setminus C|+|A\cap B|

\displaystyle=|A\setminus C|+|B\setminus C|+|A\cap B\cap C|.

The above inequality guarantees the existence of subsets

S\subseteq A\setminus C
,
T\subseteq B\setminus C
and

R\subseteq A\cap B\cap C
satisfying
|S|+|T|+|R|=m
.

Suppose that we aim to prove that a graph
H
is
(L_{H},g_{H})
-colorable, and that
there exists a vertex
v\in V(H)
satisfying

|L_{H}(v)|+m\geq g_{H}(v)+\sum_{u\in N_{H}(v)}g_{H}(u).

(2.1)

for some integer
m\geq 1
. Note that
\langle v\rangle
maybe not legal for
(H,L_{H},g_{H})
.

If there exists a neighbor
u
of
v
such that

|L_{H}(u)\setminus L_{H}(v)|\geq m,

(2.2)

then we may first apply
\langle u\mid v,m\rangle
, and then apply
\langle v\rangle
. It is easy to see that if
\langle u\mid v,m\rangle
is legal for
(H,L_{H},g_{H})
, then
\langle v\rangle
is legal for
\mathsf{Red}(\langle u\mid v,m\rangle)(H,L_{H},g_{H})
.

Similarly, if there exist two neighbors
u_{1},u_{2}
of
v
such that

|L_{H}(u_{1})|+|L_{H}(u_{2})|\geq|L_{H}(v)|+m,

(2.3)

then by Lemma
11
, we may first apply

\langle\{u_{1},u_{2}\}\mid v,m^{*}\rangle
, and then apply

\langle v\rangle
.
We now show that if
\langle\{u_{1},u_{2}\}\mid v,m^{*}\rangle
is legal for
(H,L_{H},g_{H})
, then
\langle v\rangle
is legal for
\mathsf{Red}(\langle\{u_{1},u_{2}\}\mid v,m^{*}\rangle)(H,L_{H},g_{H})
.
Indeed, assume that
(H^{\prime},L^{\prime},g^{\prime})=\mathsf{ParCol}(\{u_{1},u_{2}\}\mid v,m^{*})(H,L_{H},g_{H})
, and the corresponding coloring is
\phi
, with
\phi(u_{1})=S\cup R
and
\phi(u_{2})=T\cup R
, where
S\subseteq L(u_{1})\setminus L(v)
,
T\subseteq L(u_{2})\setminus L(v)
, and
R\subseteq L(u_{1})\cap L(u_{2})\cap L(v)
, and
|S|+|R|+|T|=m
. Since
S\cap R=\emptyset
and
T\cap R=\emptyset
, we have
g^{\prime}(u_{1})=g_{H}(u_{1})-|S|-|R|
,
g^{\prime}(u_{2})=g_{H}(u_{2})-|T|-|R|
and that
\langle v\rangle
is legal by the following:

\displaystyle|L^{\prime}(v)|

\displaystyle=|L_{H}(v)|-|R|\geq g_{H}(v)+g_{H}(u_{1})+g_{H}(u_{2})-m-|R|+\sum_{w\in N_{H}(v)\setminus\{u_{1},v_{2}\}}g_{H}(w)

\displaystyle=g_{H}(v)+g_{H}(u_{1})+g_{H}(u_{2})-|S|-|T|-2|R|+\sum_{w\in N_{H}(v)\setminus\{u_{1},v_{2}\}}g_{H}(w)

\displaystyle=\big(g_{H}(u_{1})-|S|-|R|\big)+\big(g_{H}(u_{2})-|T|-|R|\big)+g_{H}(v)+\sum_{w\in N_{H}(v)\setminus\{u_{1},v_{2}\}}g_{H}(w)

\displaystyle=g^{\prime}(v)+g^{\prime}(u_{1})+g^{\prime}(u_{2})+\sum_{w\in N_{H}(v)\setminus\{u_{1},v_{2}\}}g^{\prime}(w).

The arguments above illustrate two standard ways of applying the saving operations

\langle u\mid v,m\rangle
and
\langle\{u_{1},u_{2}\}\mid v,m^{*}\rangle

which are often used in combination with
\langle v\rangle
.
In the remainder of the paper, whenever one of these reduction patterns is used,
we will not repeat the verification, even though

does not always immediately follow.
Instead, it will be clear from the context that the corresponding inequalities
(
2.1
), (
2.2
), and (
2.3
) are satisfied.

4.3
Proof of Theorem
3

Since
G
is a counterexample to Theorem
3
,
every face
f
satisfies
d(f)\geq 4
.
If
d(f)=4
, then
c^{*}(f)\geq 0
by Lemma
13
;
if
d(f)\geq 5
, then
c^{*}(f)=c(f)=2d(f)-10\geq 0
.

By Claim
1
,
d(v)\geq 3
for all
v\in V(G)
.
If
d(v)=3
, then
c^{*}(v)\geq 0
by Lemma
14
.
If
d(v)=4
, then
c^{*}(v)\geq 0
by Lemma
15
.
If
d(v)=5
, then
c^{*}(v)\geq 0
by Lemma
27
.
If
d(v)=6
, then
c^{*}(v)\geq 0
by Lemma
20
.
If
d(v)\geq 7
, then
c^{*}(v)\geq 0
by Lemma
19
.

Thus the final charge
c^{*}(z)
is nonnegative for every

z\in V(G)\cup F(G)
.
However, the total charge is preserved:

-20=\sum_{z\in V(G)\cup F(G)}c(z)=\sum_{z\in V(G)\cup F(G)}c^{*}(z)>0,

a contradiction.
This completes the proof of Theorem

.
