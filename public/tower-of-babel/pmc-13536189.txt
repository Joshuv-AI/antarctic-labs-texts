Reward Processing in Binge Eating and Harmful Drinking: A Systematic Review
Authors: Taylor N Breddy, Kendall M Schmidt, Lindsay P Bodell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveBinge eating and harmful drinking commonly co‐occur, which worsens the physical and psychological consequences associated with either behavior alone. Reward processing alterations have been demonstrated among individuals who engage in binge eating or harmful drinking alone; however, there is limited understanding of how different components of reward processing might relate to both behaviors together and in isolation. The current systematic review aimed to consolidate knowledge on the shared and unique reward processes associated with binge eating and harmful drinking, with findings organized by reward construct and assessment method.MethodThe review was pre‐registered (osf.io/njpyh). Databases were searched for empirical articles that included assessment of binge eating, harmful drinking, and reward processing. All search strategy, screening, quality assessment, and data extraction files are available (osf.io/4h6c5).ResultsRelevant findings from 25 included articles were summarized and compared. Higher self‐reported general and food‐ and alcohol‐specific reward anticipation were associated with co‐occurring and separate engagement in binge eating and harmful drinking. Prolonged engagement in either behavior was associated with increased habit‐based reward learning.DiscussionThis review highlights the potential clinical utility of incorporating assessment, monitoring, and management of elevated anticipatory reward responsiveness and altered reward learning into intervention strategies for binge eating and harmful drinking. Future studies investigating various components of reward processing with different reward types (e.g., food, alcohol, money) and more diverse samples are necessary to clarify the nature and specificity of reward processing alterations related to binge eating and harmful drinking.Keywords: addiction, alcohol, alcohol use disorder, binge drinking, binge eating, eating disorder, harmful drinking, incentive sensitization, reward, systematic review

Introduction

Binge eating and harmful drinking are behaviors that involve the repetitive, excessive, or compulsive consumption of food or alcohol (Ágh et al. 2015; APA 2022; Courtney and Polich 2009). Binge eating describes discrete episodes during which one eats a large amount of food and feels a loss of control over their eating (APA 2022). Binge eating is a symptom present in many eating disorders and a defining feature of Binge Eating Disorder (BED) and Bulimia Nervosa (BN; APA 2022). It is estimated that approximately 13% of women and 4% of men engage in regular binge eating (Smith et al. 2021). Frequent binge eating is associated with health issues, increased health care utilization and costs, and significant impairments in health‐related quality of life (Ágh et al. 2015; Mitchell 2016; Sandri et al. 2025). Harmful drinking involves consuming large amounts of alcohol within a short (e.g., binge drinking) or prolonged (i.e., heavy drinking) period and is accompanied by strong craving or desire to consume alcohol, drinking in hazardous situations, or feeling a lack of control over consumption (APA 2022). Frequent engagement in harmful drinking increases the likelihood of developing an Alcohol Use Disorder (AUD; APA 2022). Approximately 26% percent of men and 12% percent of women engage in regular binge drinking (OECD 2021). Harmful drinking is associated with significant social and health‐related consequences including vehicle collisions and neurocognitive deficits (Courtney and Polich 2009; Miller et al. 2007).

Co‐Occurrence of Binge Eating and Harmful Drinking Behaviors

Binge eating and harmful drinking commonly co‐occur, and binge‐type eating disorders (e.g., BED and BN) are highly co‐morbid with AUD (Azevedo et al. 2021; Bogusz et al. 2021; Souza Da Silva et al. 2016). National surveys conducted in the United States have reported that up to 80% of individuals with a binge‐type eating disorder have a lifetime alcohol use disorder (range 21.4%–79.9%; Hudson et al. 2007; Udo et al. 2019). Notably, the co‐morbidity of binge‐type eating disorders and substance use disorders (e.g., AUD) is associated with greater illness severity and adverse consequences (e.g., more frequent diuretic use, greater financial problems) and increased likelihood of experiencing other co‐occurring psychiatric disorders (e.g., anxiety, depression, and personality disorders) compared to experiencing either disorder alone (Bulik et al. 1997; Duncan et al. 2006; Peterson et al. 2005; Strasser et al. 1992). Co‐morbidity can be a challenge for treatment, because individuals with co‐morbid binge‐type eating disorders and AUD might represent a distinct patient population with different clinical presentations, psychological risk factors, illness trajectories, and treatment responses compared to those with either disorder alone (Becker and Grilo 2015; Burr et al. 2025; Duncan et al. 2006). Identification of both shared and distinct factors associated with binge eating and harmful drinking would improve understanding of processes that contribute to the development and maintenance of eating pathology versus drinking pathology, or their co‐occurrence (Baskin‐Sommers and Foti 2015; Benjamin and Wulfert 2005). Ultimately, this knowledge could inform more personalized and evidence‐based prevention and intervention strategies for those who experience these behaviors together and in isolation (Courbasson et al. 2005; Gordon et al. 2008).

Reward Processing in Binge Eating and Harmful Drinking

Reward processing is one factor hypothesized to be particularly relevant to pathologies characterized by excessive consumption of pleasurable stimuli (Baskin‐Sommers and Foti 2015; Burr et al. 2025). Reward processing generally represents the ways by which individuals perceive, evaluate, and respond to rewarding stimuli. One of the most widely used and empirically supported conceptual frameworks of reward processing is the incentive‐sensitization theory of addiction (Berridge and Robinson 2016). This model outlines three key components of reward processing that underlie the propensity for and maintenance of appetitive behaviors, namely motivational reward (i.e., “wanting”), consummatory reward (“liking”), and reward learning (Berridge et al. 2009; Vujanovic et al. 2017). Motivational reward refers to the level of incentive salience attributed to reinforcing stimuli, which is associated with increased levels of craving, effort, and a sense of wanting during the anticipation of and approach towards reward (Berridge et al. 2009). Consummatory reward involves the positive affective and hedonic experiences during the initial and/or sustained receipt of reward or during exposure to reward‐related stimuli (Berridge et al. 2009; Schmidt and Bodell 2024). Reward learning involves the processes by which certain stimuli become associated with reward and with changes in behaviors, outcomes, and expectations based on prior reward‐related experiences (Berridge et al. 2009; NIMH 2024). In short, the incentive‐sensitization theory, posits that “wanting” of the reward stimulus increases over time due to cue‐reward learning, whereas “liking” stays the same or decreases, and these reward processing changes impact the maintenance of substance use (Berridge and Robinson 2016; King et al. 2021; Koob and Volkow 2010). This reward processing framework has been used to guide several recent reviews examining reward processing in various mental illnesses (e.g., substance misuse, major depressive disorder, posttraumatic stress; Baskin‐Sommers and Foti 2015; Skumlien et al. 2021; Vujanovic et al. 2017).

Knowledge on associations between reward processing constructs and binge eating or harmful drinking have been summarized in prior reviews (Galandra et al. 2018; Leenaerts et al. 2022; Minari et al. 2026; Moreno Padilla et al. 2017; Pasquale et al. 2024; Sutton et al. 2022; Vrieze and Leenaerts 2023; Zeng et al. 2023). Narrative and systematic reviews of the specific reward processes involved in binge eating indicate that relative to healthy volunteers, those who engage in binge eating have increased levels of general motivational reward, elevated anticipatory responses to food reward, and greater habitual reward learning (Leenaerts et al. 2022; Pasquale et al. 2024; Sutton et al. 2022; Vrieze and Leenaerts 2023). Consistent with the incentive‐sensitization theory, reward processing alterations were suggested to differ according to stage of illness, such that increased anticipatory responses to food reward and decreased food‐specific consummatory reward responses were associated with increasing duration of BED (Bodell and Racine 2023; Pasquale et al. 2024; Vrieze and Leenaerts 2023). Reviews and meta‐analyses of reward processing in those with AUD show that prolonged engagement in harmful drinking is associated with increased general reward motivation, decreased anticipatory responses to monetary reward, and increased habit‐driven reward learning (Galandra et al. 2018; Moreno Padilla et al. 2017; Zeng et al. 2023). Mixed findings have been reported regarding consummatory responses to monetary reward relative to healthy controls (Galandra et al. 2018; Luijten et al. 2017; Zeng et al. 2023). Notably, several of these reviews in both the binge eating and alcohol literatures focused on one assessment modality (e.g., self‐report) or reward type (e.g., money), which limits understanding of reward‐related dysfunction (Galandra et al. 2018; Sutton et al. 2022; Vrieze and Leenaerts 2023; Zeng et al. 2023). Indeed, it has been emphasized that inconsistent findings (e.g., different reward‐related neural activation patterns) across studies are likely due to between‐study heterogeneity in sample characteristics (e.g., treatment stage, psychiatric co‐morbidities), recruitment methods (e.g., community versus inpatient clinics), and reward constructs being measured (Leenaerts et al. 2022; Luijten et al. 2017; Pasquale et al. 2024; Vrieze and Leenaerts 2023; Zeng et al. 2023). Consequently, these variables must be carefully considered when organizing and interpreting study findings to draw more accurate conclusions about the nature and specificity of reward processing alterations in relation to binge eating and harmful drinking.

Moreover, although prior reviews have summarized studies that examined reward processes in samples characterized by either binge eating or harmful drinking in relation to control participants, there have been no systematic reviews of studies comparing reward processing in individuals with binge eating, harmful drinking, or both. Addressing this gap is important to facilitate knowledge on the specific reward‐related factors that may relate to co‐morbidity versus the presence of either behavior alone (Benjamin and Wulfert 2005). The synthesis of studies that include samples of those who engage in binge eating and those who engage in harmful drinking will promote a clearer depiction of how reward processing might differentially relate to these behaviors in isolation as well as their co‐occurrence (Baskin‐Sommers and Foti 2015; Burr et al. 2025).

Summary and Aims of the Current Review

Binge eating and harmful drinking often co‐occur and are each independently associated with alterations in reward processing. However, possible differences in reward processing between those who engage in both binge eating and harmful drinking compared to those who engage in only binge eating, only harmful drinking, or neither behavior are largely unclear (Becker and Grilo 2015; Dawe and Loxton 2004). The purpose of the present review was to synthesize and evaluate findings from studies that examined reward processing in individuals who engage in both binge eating and harmful drinking and those engaging in either behavior alone. We integrated study findings within the reward processing framework outlined in the incentive‐sensitization theory, which has been used in prior reviews (Berridge et al. 2009; Vujanovic et al. 2017). Findings are arranged and interpreted according to reward processing component (e.g., motivational reward, consummatory reward, reward learning), assessment type (e.g., self‐report, behavioral, physiological), reward stimuli (e.g., food, alcohol), and sample characteristics (e.g., university students, treatment‐seeking) to provide a nuanced understanding of the overlapping and independent contributions of reward processing to binge eating and harmful drinking.

Method

For the current review, the search strategy, study selection procedures, data extraction, and interpretation of results were conducted according to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Moher et al. 2015). The methods, including study objectives, search strategy, screening and data extraction instructions, and synthesis plan were pre‐registered (osf.io/njpyh). The authors deviated from the pre‐registered search criteria to include gray literature (see Data Availability Statement). Covidence web‐based software platform was used to manage references, extract data, and synthesize results. All relevant searches with citations, screening, quality information, and data extraction templates and data files are available (osf.io/4h6c5).

Eligibility Criteria

Study eligibility criteria were established using the Population Intervention/Exposure, Comparison, Outcome, Study Characteristics (PICOS) framework (Higgins et al. 2024). Inclusion criteria were empirical articles with (1) a single sample of individuals with assessments of both binge eating and harmful drinking and/or (2) a sample of individuals engaging in binge eating and a sample of individuals engaging in harmful drinking. Although most studies included healthy controls, this was not stated as an inclusion criterion. Studies examining binge eating and harmful drinking in one large community sample without making direct group comparisons were included. To capture a broad range of studies, any level of alcohol misuse reported in the sample was deemed eligible (e.g., heavy drinking, binge drinking, AUD). We chose to focus on articles that measured alcohol use rather than general substance use due to similarities in risk factors and age of initiation (i.e., adolescence and youth) for binge eating and harmful drinking (Ferriter and Ray 2011; Sampedro‐Piquero et al. 2022). Moreover, overeating and alcohol consumption are both legal and generally socially acceptable behaviors in Western cultures, and legality and social perceptions are known to influence risk and patterns of engagement (Filstead et al. 1988; Gilson et al. 2023).

Studies also had to include a measure of reward processing. Table 1 shows the reward processing measures that were eligible for inclusion. The assignment of different self‐report, behavioral, and physiological measures to relevant components of reward processing was informed by (1) the incentive‐sensitization theory, (2) prior reviews that used a similar framework to define reward processing, and (3) factor analysis studies of different self‐report measures (Berridge et al. 2009; Carlson et al. 2013; Christodoulou and Rosen 1995; Mardaga and Hansenne 2007; Skumlien et al. 2021). Measures of delay discounting (i.e., the preference for smaller immediate rewards relative to larger delayed rewards) were included as a separate reward processing category. This decision was made because the incentive‐sensitization theory does not include information about delay discounting, but this construct has been conceptualized across studies as an aspect of reward valuation (i.e., the process of ascribing value to a reward using information about its size and immediacy), reward anticipation (i.e., the reward is evaluated but is not received), or a behavioral economic index of impulsivity (MacKillop et al. 2011; NIMH 2024; Schmidt and Bodell 2024; Weinsztok et al. 2021).

Exclusion criteria were books, reviews, case studies, commentaries or correspondences, editorials, poster presentations, symposium talks, obituaries, and scale validation studies. Intervention studies (e.g., randomized controlled trials for pharmacological and psychological treatments) were also excluded. Empirical articles with study samples of non‐human animals were excluded. Empirical studies were excluded if their study sample and primary aims were irrelevant for the purposes of the current study (e.g., differentiating sub‐types of bipolar disorder). Additionally, empirical articles with a study sample consisting of only a group of individuals engaging in binge eating or harmful drinking were excluded, given prior reviews and differences in sample characteristics (e.g., age, inclusion criteria) that limit comparability between individuals with binge eating and harmful drinking from different samples. Empirical articles that did not include a measure of reward processing were excluded. No exclusion criteria were made regarding age, gender, sex, race, ethnicity, socioeconomic status, or body mass index of the study sample. Studies could be cross‐sectional or longitudinal.

Information Sources and Search Strategy

The following electronic databases were searched: Embase, Scopus, APA PsycINFO, MEDLINE, and CINAHL. Embase, APA PsycINFO, and MEDLINE were searched using the Ovid SP interface. Scopus and CINAHL were searched using the Elsevier and EBSCOhost interfaces, respectively. Searches were repeated every 60 days from April 20, 2024, until January 9, 2026, once data extraction was completed. On January 9, 2026, the following sources were also used to search for unpublished literature (e.g., theses, dissertations, pre‐prints): ProQuest, Open Access Theses and Dissertations (OATD), Scopus preprints, PsyArXiV, MedRxiv, BioRxiv, and E‐Theses Online Service (EthOS). PICOS criteria were used to develop the search strategy. Search queries included the following: (binge eat* OR bulimi*) AND (alcohol OR binge drink*) AND (reward* OR craving OR anhedonia OR incentive OR motivation OR hedonic). All searches were conducted without the use of language filtering so non‐English articles could be included. However, no non‐English articles were retrieved. Citation searching also was used.

Study Selection

Two reviewers (TB & KS) who were informed of the study inclusion and exclusion criteria completed the title and abstract screening and full‐text review stages independently. After each stage, reviewers met to resolve any conflicts. Any remaining conflicts were resolved after discussion with the reviewers' supervisor (LB). Notes documenting study selection and conflict decisions were made in Covidence and Microsoft Excel files. Inter‐rater agreements between the two reviewers at each stage were calculated using Cohen's kappa (McHugh 2012).

Quality Assessment

Prior to quality assessment, a quality assessment template was created in Covidence and exported as a Microsoft Excel file. This template was developed using the STROBE Evidence Appraisal Form for cohort, case–control, or cross‐sectional studies (Von Elm et al. 2007) and Cochrane GRADE approach for systematic reviews (Higgins et al. 2024). The validity, reliability, and applicability of all included articles were assessed by obtaining relevant information on entities including study aims, inclusion and exclusion criteria, methods, measures, outcomes, drop‐outs, missing data, and conflicts of interest. An overall risk of bias judgment (high, low, or unsure) was made for each included study by the first two authors (TB & KS). Following the completion of quality assessment, decisions were discussed and conflicts in judgments were resolved. Inter‐rater agreement for the overall risk of bias judgment between the two reviewers was calculated using Cohen's kappa (McHugh 2012).

Data Extraction

Before data were extracted, the data extraction template in Covidence was exported as a Microsoft Excel file and modified based on the PICOS system (Higgins et al. 2024) and data reported in related systematic reviews. Information on the following entities was extracted from all included articles: study identification, methods, population, and outcomes. Data were extracted by two independent authors, and any conflicts were addressed and resolved following data extraction. Relevant information on all included articles was compared and summarized in a narrative synthesis, following the Guidance on the Conduct of Narrative Synthesis in Systematic Reviews (Popay et al. 2006).

Additional Statistical Analyses

Two articles included data relevant to the primary aims of this review study but did not present results from such analyses. In these cases, we conducted additional statistical analyses using data available from those prior studies (Mackinnon et al. 2021; Voon et al. 2014). Analyses were conducted by the first author using RStudio (version 2024.12.0 + 467) and annotated RStudio files for all analyses are available (osf.io/4h6c5). Following the analyses used by Voon et al. (2014), differences in motivation indices between adults with BED and adults with alcohol dependence were analyzed using analyses of covariance with intelligence quotient and depression scores as covariates. Data from the 21‐day daily diary study conducted by Mackinnon et al. (2021) are available (osf.io/gduy4). Using these raw data, Spearman correlations were conducted using the daily and/or baseline mean scores for each participant on relevant binge eating, harmful drinking, and reward measures. Similar statistical analyses were used by other included articles (Albertella et al. 2019; Simons et al. 2021).

Results

Study Selection

Twenty‐five articles were included in the present review, which were identified using the study selection process depicted in the PRISMA Flow Diagram (Haddaway et al. 2022) (Figure 1). The inter‐rater agreement for the title and abstract screening stage (κ = 0.670) and full‐text review stage (κ = 0.693) was “moderate” (McHugh 2012). For the title and abstract screening stage and full‐text review stage, reviewers agreed on 89.18% of the articles.

PRISMA Flow Diagram summarizing the study selection process. The following electronic databases were searched: Embase, Scopus, APA PsycINFO, MEDLINE, and CINAHL. The following sources were used to locate unpublished (i.e., gray) literature: ProQuest, Open Access Theses and Dissertations (OATD), Scopus preprints, and PsyArXiV. Searches for unpublished literature were also conducted in MedRxiv, BioRxiv, and E‐Theses Online Service (EthOS); however, no articles were retrieved. One article was identified using citation searching.

Study Characteristics

The inter‐rater agreement for the overall risk of bias judgment was 100% (i.e., both reviewers rated the overall risk of bias for each included article as “low”). Twenty‐three (92%) articles used a cross‐sectional study design, and two (8%) articles used intensive longitudinal study designs. All articles except one (a master's thesis) were published in peer‐reviewed journals. Thirteen articles (52%) included community samples, six (24%) included clinical samples (i.e., participants currently receiving or seeking treatment for binge eating and/or harmful drinking), and six (24%) included university samples. Total sample sizes for individual studies ranged from 32 to 1018, with a median sample size of 150. Ten studies (43%) reported on sex only, six (24%) reported on gender only, and nine (39%) used the terms “sex” and “gender” interchangeably in their reporting of sample characteristics. Studies included individuals with a diagnosis of BED (n = 7), a diagnosis of BN (n = 5), or any self‐reported binge eating (n = 14). Harmful drinking was captured by a diagnosis of AUD with (n = 2) or without binge drinking (n = 4), alcohol dependence (n = 5), alcohol abuse or dependence (n = 2), or any self‐reported alcohol misuse (n = 11). Fourteen studies (56%) reported characteristics of the whole sample, and sixteen studies (64%) reported characteristics for the any of the following groups: binge eating (n = 15), harmful drinking (n = 12), binge eating and harmful drinking (n = 4), matched healthy controls (n = 7), or unmatched healthy controls (n = 5).

Motivational Reward

Sixteen studies measured motivational reward (Table 2), with five assessing general reward and 11 assessing food‐ or alcohol‐specific reward. Of these 16 studies, fourteen (n = 14; 56%) evaluated motivational reward using only self‐report measures, one (n = 1; 4%) used only a behavioral measure, and one (n = 1; 4%) used both a self‐report and a behavioral measure.

Self‐Report Measures of General Motivational Reward

Five studies included a self‐report measure of general anticipatory reward processing, which was not specific to food and/or alcohol. Overall, most of these studies (n = 3; 60%) demonstrated that higher levels of self‐reported reward anticipation related to both binge eating and harmful drinking in community and university adult samples. One study found significant positive correlations between self‐reported reward anticipation and binge eating, but not harmful drinking (Van Malderen et al. 2024); however, the study sample was high school students (mean age = 14.77 years) and self‐reported levels of harmful drinking were lower than samples in other reviewed studies (Van Malderen et al. 2024). The use of younger‐aged samples might contribute to nonsignificant findings, because adolescents have lower engagement and/or disclosure of harmful drinking relative to adults (SAMHSA 2023; Van Malderen et al. 2024). Moreover, secondary analyses of Mackinnon et al. (2021) 21‐day daily diary study revealed nonsignificant or small negative correlations between anticipatory reward processing with binge eating and harmful drinking, which contrasts with the other studies. Contrasting findings may be due to differences in the measures used and their time of administration. Specifically, daily and past 12‐month alcohol problems were measured using the Alcohol Problems Checklist and Rutger's Alcohol Problem Index, respectively, whereas the other reviewed studies in this section evaluated the severity of harmful drinking using the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT; Saunders et al. 1993). Moreover, the reward processing measure was administered once at baseline while binge eating scores were averaged over 21 days (Mackinnon et al. 2021).

Self‐Report Measures of Motivational Reward Specific to Food and/or Alcohol

Eleven studies included a self‐report measure of anticipatory reward processing that was specific to food and/or alcohol. Of these studies, the majority (n = 8; 73%) showed that increased levels of anticipatory reward processing of food or alcohol were related to binge eating, harmful drinking, or both. Findings from the other three studies were more nuanced, which might be related to the severity of binge eating or harmful drinking and/or the methods used to evaluate these behaviors in the study samples. Specifically, one study found that compared to binge eating, harmful drinking was more likely to occur in emotional enhancement situations; however, findings might reflect the higher clinical severity of harmful drinking than binge eating in this sample (Birch et al. 2007). Another study found that female college students with BED and BN did not significantly differ from healthy volunteers in drinking enhancement motivation (i.e., drinking to increase positive affect); however, the sample had lower levels of harmful drinking relative to other included studies (as evidenced by the average number of drinking episodes per month; Luce et al. 2007). Finally, nonsignificant associations between eating enhancement motives and bulimia symptoms found by Simons et al. (2021) might be related to the use of a measure simultaneously assessing binge eating and compensatory behaviors (e.g., fasting, purging) rather than binge eating alone.

Notably, even when including assessments of both binge eating and drinking, most studies only investigated associations between eating or drinking motives with their respective behavior. However, two studies investigated how drinking motives relate to both behaviors and indicated that reward‐motivated alcohol consumption might represent a transdiagnostic risk factor for binge eating (Mackinnon et al. 2021; Stewart et al. 2006). Findings from another study indicated that craving for palatable food might relate to harmful drinking (Gendall et al. 1997). Together, these findings support that indicators of specific motivational reward might generalize to other reward types and confer risk for co‐morbidity.

Only three of the 11 self‐report studies of food‐ or alcohol‐specific anticipatory reward (27%) directly compared groups of adults who were engaging in co‐occurring binge eating and harmful drinking relative to a group engaging in only one of these behaviors. One study found that college students who engaged in binge eating only had higher eating enhancement motivation compared to those who engaged in binge drinking only, those who engaged in both binge eating and binge drinking, and healthy volunteers (Trojanowski et al. 2019). However, those who were engaging in both behaviors had significantly higher drinking enhancement motivation relative to all other groups (Trojanowski et al. 2019). The two other studies only compared groups on drinking enhancement motivation and found no significant differences between those with co‐occurring harmful drinking and binge eating and those with harmful drinking only (Duffy 2020; Stewart et al. 2006). These latter studies included patients receiving treatment for AUD (Stewart et al. 2006) or college students who reported at least one binge drinking episode (Duffy 2020). In contrast, Trojanowski et al. (2019) included those with current repeated engagement in binge drinking. Together, findings indicate that individuals who engage in harmful drinking with or without binge eating have heightened enhancement motivation that is specific to alcohol reward, and this effect might be more pronounced in those currently engaging in binge drinking.

Behavioral Measures of Motivational Reward

Two studies included a behavioral measure of motivational reward (Albertella et al. 2019; Kane et al. 2004). Kane et al. (2004) demonstrated that female adults with BN with and without alcohol dependence had significantly higher anticipatory reward responses relative to healthy controls using the Card Arranging Reward Responsiveness Test (Kane et al. 2004). In contrast, Albertella et al. (2019) did not find significant correlations between scores on a reward‐only variant of an attentional capture procedure with self‐reported binge eating or harmful drinking in a community sample (Albertella et al. 2019). However, this latter study did not analyze group differences, and the severity of binge eating and harmful drinking were not reported (Albertella et al. 2019). Nonsignificant findings may relate to a lower severity of binge eating and/or harmful drinking relative to the aforementioned study sample (i.e., Kane et al. 2004).

Consummatory Reward

Only two studies assessed consummatory reward (Table 3). One study measured self‐report (i.e., pleasantness ratings) and physiological (i.e., eye‐tracking) responses to the presentation of neutral and disorder‐specific visual stimuli, without anticipation of subsequent reward receipt, in adults with AUD, BED, and healthy controls (Schag et al. 2019). In this study, adults with AUD rated disorder‐specific stimuli as less pleasant and spent significantly less time fixating on these stimuli compared to adults with BED and matched control groups (Schag et al. 2019). There were no significant differences in fixation time following the presentation of food stimuli between adults with BED and healthy controls (Schag et al. 2019). These findings suggest that adults with AUD might differ in their processing of reward‐related and neutral stimuli, such that those with AUD might avoid disease‐specific reinforcing stimuli relative to those with BED and healthy controls. However, findings might be due to treatment effects, given that 95% of the AUD sample was receiving treatment, and participants may have been learning to avoid alcohol and alcohol‐related cues (Schag et al. 2019). Finally, secondary analyses using data from a 21‐day daily diary study did not demonstrate significant correlations between self‐reported reward reactivity with binge eating and harmful drinking (Mackinnon et al. 2021). Nonsignificant between‐person effects might be related to the way in which data were collected as binge eating was measured at baseline and reward measures were administered daily (Mackinnon et al. 2021).

Self‐Report Measures of Reward Learning

One study used the persistence subscale on the Tridimensional Personality Questionnaire (TPQ) as a self‐report measure of reward learning (Bulik et al. 1994). There were no significant differences in persistence subscale scores between women with both BN and alcohol dependence relative to women with BN only. This finding suggests that reward learning alterations may not confer additional risk for alcohol and binge eating comorbidity above and beyond associations with binge eating (Bulik et al. 1994).

Behavioral Measures of Reward Learning

Four studies included a behavioral paradigm that assessed reward learning. Two of these studies used computerized tasks with several trials during which participants were instructed to press a button to stop a rotating clock (Morris et al. 2016) or react to the emergence of a visual target (Voon et al. 2014). These studies used explore‐exploit or motivational indices to assess differences in reaction time following monetary reward feedback (i.e., learning about differences between actual and expected reward values or reward prediction error; Morris et al. 2016; Voon et al. 2014). Morris et al. (2016) found that relative to healthy controls, adults with BED were more likely to make exploratory (i.e., less certain) choices in situations involving monetary reward, whereas adults with alcohol dependence were less likely to make exploratory choices (Morris et al. 2016). Similarly, Voon et al. (2014) showed that compared to adults with BED and healthy controls, adults with alcohol dependence had lower motivation to receive monetary reward following learning. Moreover, there was a negative correlation between motivation indices and self‐reported harmful drinking (Voon et al. 2014). Although motivation indices for adults with BED did not significantly differ from age‐and gender‐matched controls, significant negative correlations between motivation indices and self‐reported binge eating were observed (Voon et al. 2014). Overall, these findings suggest that those with binge eating and those with harmful drinking have dysregulated learning following monetary reward conditioning. However, the types of learning alterations that are present with either behavior might be distinct, such that relative to those who engage in binge eating, individuals who engage in harmful drinking exhibit decreased responsiveness to monetary reward following learning.

Two studies used computerized behavioral paradigms to evaluate persistent responding following reward learning in clinical samples (Banca et al. 2016; Voon et al. 2015). Banca and colleagues (2019) used a task with acquisition and reversal phases, during which participants chose one of three stimulus‐pairs that were each associated with different probabilistic outcomes. Researchers found that adults with BED or alcohol dependence did not significantly differ from their age‐and gender‐matched controls in their rate of learning initial reward stimulus‐pair contingencies; although, adults with BED or alcohol dependence were significantly slower than controls at learning reward stimulus‐pair contingencies following their reversal (Banca et al. 2016). These results suggest that those with binge eating and harmful drinking might have impaired ability to discontinue previously learned responses relative to those who do not engage in these behaviors. Voon et al. (2015) used a task during which participants chose between two stimuli‐pairs that were presented with fixed reward probabilities in sequential learning stages. Temporal difference and reinforcement learning algorithms were used to distinguish the contribution of habit‐based and goal‐directed learning during the task, respectively (Voon et al. 2015). Compared to weight‐matched controls, adults with BED were significantly more likely to use habitual rather than goal‐directed learning; however, there were no significant differences in reward learning between adults with alcohol dependence and their matched controls (Voon et al. 2015). Notably, this alcohol group was abstinent from alcohol for 2 weeks to 1 year prior to participation and increased duration of abstinence was significantly correlated with decreased habitual responding. These findings suggest that alcohol use itself might be related to increased habitual learning (Voon et al. 2015).

Physiological Measures of Reward Learning

In addition to probabilistic responding, the study by Voon et al. (2015) analyzed how structural brain differences might relate to reward learning, using a smaller sub‐sample of adults with BED (n = 20) and healthy controls (n = 20; Voon et al. 2015). Relative to healthy controls, adults with BED had significantly less gray matter volume in the ventral striatum, medial orbitofrontal cortex, and caudate. However, these differences were no longer significant after controlling for reward learning biases, suggesting that these brain areas might be involved in altered habit formation in BED (Voon et al. 2015).

Delay Discounting

Three studies measured delay discounting (Table 5). Two used a self‐report monetary choice questionnaire and one used a behavioral task and neuroimaging to evaluate delay discounting for money and relevant reinforcing stimuli. One self‐report study demonstrated that adults with BED or alcohol dependence had significantly higher delay discounting (i.e., increased preference for small and immediate rewards rather than larger and delayed rewards) than age‐and gender‐matched healthy controls (Mole et al. 2015). In contrast, the other self‐report study did not find significant associations between binge eating or harmful drinking measures and delay discounting (Stojek et al. 2014). However, group differences were not analyzed and the sample was characterized by infrequent binge eating (i.e., only 30% of the sample had engaged in at least one binge eating episode within the past month), which may contribute to nonsignificant findings if severity is associated with delay discounting alterations (Stojek et al. 2014).

Discussion

The overall aim of the current review was to consolidate findings from studies that examined the potential shared and unique contributions of different components of reward processing to binge eating and harmful drinking. We presented findings from 25 articles, which were organized by reward construct and measure.

Summary of Findings

Most included studies demonstrated that binge eating and harmful drinking are similarly associated with differences in reward processing; in particular, heightened reward‐related motivation, dysregulated learning following reward feedback, and increased habitual responding, relative to engagement in neither behavior. At this time, conclusions cannot be drawn regarding differences in consummatory reward processes (i.e., reward‐related liking) or delay discounting, given the small number of studies (n = 2–3) and their methodological limitations (Leenaerts et al. 2025; Mackinnon et al. 2021; Mole et al. 2015; Schag et al. 2019; Stojek et al. 2014).

Most included studies (n = 16; 64%) evaluated reward‐related wanting rather than liking or learning. Overall, findings suggest that binge eating and harmful drinking are similarly associated with heightened reward‐related motivation. Specifically, higher general reward‐related wanting, as well as food‐ or alcohol‐specific wanting, might represent transdiagnostic risk factors for several maladaptive appetitive behaviors, including binge eating and alcohol misuse (Gendall et al. 1997; Kane et al. 2004; Leenaerts et al. 2024). Furthermore, our synthesis of findings indicates that relative to other rewarding behaviors, current repetitive binge drinking might be associated with greater enhancement of alcohol‐specific wanting (Trojanowski et al. 2019). The only included studies that do not find evidence of enhanced reward‐related wanting in binge eating or harmful drinking use samples of individuals without clinical diagnoses (Luce et al. 2007; Mackinnon et al. 2021; Van Malderen et al. 2024). Taken together, these findings support the incentive‐sensitization theory and the mechanistic staging model of reward processing in binge‐type eating disorders. Specifically, these theories posit that as one repeatedly consumes a substance or engages in an appetitive behavior, reward‐related neural circuitry becomes progressively more sensitized, which increases the incentive salience or rewarding value attributed to that substance or behavior (Berridge and Robinson 2016; Bodell and Racine 2023). Findings from studies that examined reward learning also support theories of addiction, which hold that with prolonged and repetitive engagement, appetitive behaviors become more compulsive and habitual over time (Berridge and Robinson 2016; Koob 2001).

Research Implications of Findings

In addition to summarizing the extant literature, the present review emphasizes the importance of evaluating the clinical characteristics of samples when understanding and comparing study findings. First, studies that did not find significant differences in reward processing generally used self‐report measures to evaluate binge eating and harmful drinking in community samples of participants with low severity of one or both behaviors (Albertella et al. 2019; Luce et al. 2007; Van Malderen et al. 2024). Notably, most included studies (n = 15; 60%) did not report on the average duration of engagement in binge eating and/or harmful drinking and most (n = 20; 80%) did not report on the number of participants receiving treatment for binge eating and/or harmful drinking. As described in the incentive‐sensitization theory and related theories of reward processing in binge eating and harmful drinking, the nature of reward processing alterations may differ based on illness or treatment stage (Bodell and Racine 2023; King et al. 2021; Schaefer et al. 2023). Thus, the lack of reporting or failure to consider these clinical variables might hinder the interpretation of findings across studies.

Second, this review highlights the value of accounting for potential inconsistencies in the assessment of binge eating and harmful drinking across studies when comparing outcomes. There were contrasting findings from studies that only assessed binge eating or BED compared to studies that evaluated bulimia symptoms or BN, which coincides with other studies that have found differences in reward processing, encoding of food stimuli, and temperament between patients with BED and BN (Atiye et al. 2015; Farmer et al. 2001; Weygandt et al. 2012). In particular, studies have found positive correlations between reward responsiveness following monetary feedback and purging frequency but not binge eating frequency, as well as heightened brain activity in response to food cues in patients with BN relative to BED (Farmer et al. 2001; Weygandt et al. 2012). Relative to those who engage in binge eating only, individuals with purging behaviors might be more sensitive to the negative reinforcing properties of these behaviors (e.g., less distress about caloric intake from binge eating; Farmer et al. 2001). In the present review, nine studies (36%) did not report on engagement in compensatory behaviors including purging. Given that engagement in purging might relate to different reward processing alterations, it is important that behaviors are separately assessed and reported.

Additionally, this review's synthesis of findings underscores the value in considering how the clinical severity of study samples, measures used to assess drinking, and stage of alcohol use (e.g., active use, abstinence, withdrawal) might impact results. Studies that did not find evidence of altered reward processing in those who engage in harmful drinking (n = 5) included samples with lower levels of harmful alcohol consumption (n = 3), did not specify illness duration (n = 1), or used self‐report measures to evaluate alcohol‐related problems (n = 1), relative to most other studies that used clinical interviews and included samples with clinically severe alcohol dependence (n = 13). Findings across studies suggest that individuals with more clinically severe drinking behaviors such as binge drinking have significantly higher levels of alcohol reward motivation relative to those who drink socially and that levels of motivational reward might diminish during prolonged abstinence (Balodis and Potenza 2015; Blaine et al. 2023; Leenaerts et al. 2024). Indeed, two reviewed studies with findings of decreased or similar reward processing in those who engage in harmful drinking relative to healthy controls provide evidence of potential treatment impacts on reward processing. Both of these studies had samples of individuals receiving treatment or who were currently abstinent from alcohol, respectively (Schag et al. 2019; Voon et al. 2015). Thus, the operationalization of harmful drinking must be carefully considered because reward processing findings will likely differ depending on the assessment of harmful drinking and their clinically relevant features.

Clinical Implications of Findings

There is a lack of evidence‐based intervention strategies designed for those with binge eating and harmful drinking and there are limited simultaneous treatment services available for those experiencing co‐morbid binge‐type eating disorders and AUD (Burr et al. 2025; Courbasson et al. 2005; Gordon et al. 2008). The current findings highlight the potential importance of incorporating reward‐related targets into assessment and treatment of these disorders. In particular, heightened food‐or alcohol‐related enhancement motivations (i.e., eating and drinking to enhance positive affect) and general levels of anticipatory reward responsiveness might contribute to binge eating and harmful drinking together and in isolation. Thus, it might be beneficial for risk assessment and prevention approaches to incorporate screening and ongoing monitoring of elevated anticipatory reward responses including food and alcohol craving and heightened reward responses that generalize across situations.

Tailored interventions that focus on the management of food and alcohol cravings in those with binge eating and harmful drinking also may be recommended. Such interventions could include urge surfing mindfulness techniques (Palfai and Ostafin 2003), cue exposure and response prevention (Norberg et al. 2021), approach bias modification (Brockmeyer et al. 2016), and smartphone‐delivered momentary coping strategies (Boutelle et al. 2025; Dulin and Gonzalez 2017). Moreover, techniques that consider elevated anticipatory reward processing and habitual responding can be incorporated into cognitive‐behavioral and relapse prevention treatment strategies for binge eating and harmful drinking (Gordon et al. 2008; Iacovino et al. 2012; Witkiewitz et al. 2019). For instance, individuals can be encouraged to reflect on emotional reward situations as potential high‐risk situations when completing relapse prevention plans and behavioral management approaches can focus on participation in alternative activities that lead to enhancement of positive emotions (Iacovino et al. 2012; Witkiewitz et al. 2019). Relapse prevention planning can encourage the identification and monitoring of maladaptive habits and include strategies (e.g., mindfulness skills) that promote shifts from habitual to intentional behaviors (Iacovino et al. 2012; Witkiewitz et al. 2019). Longitudinal studies that examine whether heightened reward‐related wanting and habit‐based learning might serve as risk factors for the development of binge eating and/or harmful drinking or an adaptation that results from prolonged engagement in one or both behaviors can provide information to aid in treatment‐sequencing decisions and the development of efficacious intervention strategies.

Limitations of Included Studies and Future Research Directions

Included studies are limited by inconsistent and underreporting of demographic variables including gender, race and ethnicity, socioeconomic status, and body mass index as well as clinical variables such as illness duration. Indeed, twelve studies (48%) only included women and only two studies (8%) reported on the number of individuals who were transgender, genderqueer, or non‐binary. Only fourteen (57%) studies reported the race or ethnicity of the sample. Of these studies that reported race or ethnicity, the vast majority of participants (78%) identified as white or Caucasian. Most studies (n = 18; 72%) did not provide any information regarding the socioeconomic status of the sample. Thirteen studies (52%) did not report on the body mass index of the study sample, and another three (12%) did not report on the body mass index of the harmful drinking groups. Additionally, five studies (20%) only included those with a body mass index of 30 or greater in the binge eating group. Importantly, the prevalence, risk factors, co‐morbidities, and/or consequences associated with binge eating and harmful drinking have been shown to differ depending on gender, race, ethnicity, socioeconomic status, and body mass index (Ágh et al. 2015; Albani et al. 2018; Allen et al. 2018; Caetano et al. 2014; Calling et al. 2019; Hughto et al. 2021; Lydecker and Grilo 2016; Parker and Harriger 2020; Reagan and Hersch 2005), and limited data on these sociodemographic factors precludes the comparison of findings between studies. Future studies are encouraged to use targeted recruitment methods to obtain more diverse samples, which is necessary to evaluate the generalizability of findings.

Another limitation of the included studies is that most were cross‐sectional, which hinders understanding of the temporal associations between reward processing alterations and engagement in binge eating or harmful drinking. Future studies are encouraged to use longitudinal designs to examine whether aspects of reward processing differ depending on which behavior is initiated first (e.g., binge eating followed by harmful drinking) and whether these differences are influenced by other factors that have been shown to influence appetitive behaviors, including time of behavior onset, social context, and environmental cues (Ramirez and Miranda 2014; Shiffman 2009). High resolution experience sampling methods are also encouraged, which can provide information about how reward processes (e.g., self‐reported craving) might change immediately prior to and directly following binge eating or harmful drinking episodes (Piasecki 2019).

The organization of study findings used in the present review was informed by empirically supported theories and frameworks used in prior research; however, there is still some degree of subjectivity in this classification, and categories might differ depending on the conceptualization of reward processing. Most studies (n = 22; 88%) in the present review only included one reward type (e.g., food, alcohol, or money) or assessment method (e.g., self‐report), which precludes a more nuanced understanding of reward processing alterations and psychopathology. Indeed, altered reward‐related behavioral responses and neural activity and has been shown in those with binge eating or harmful drinking alone; however, it is unclear whether these findings vary depending on reward construct and reward type. Thus, to clarify the nature and specificity of the reward processing alterations that are associated with binge eating and harmful drinking, studies examining differences in responses to food and alcohol using different behavioral measures (e.g., modified incentive delay tasks) and neuroimaging methods in larger and diverse samples are necessary (Moreno Padilla et al. 2017; Vrieze and Leenaerts 2023).

Most importantly, only two studies (8%) made direct comparisons between all four groups of interest: binge eating and harmful drinking, binge eating only, harmful drinking only, and healthy volunteers. To accurately examine whether those who engage in co‐occurring binge eating and harmful drinking differ in reward processing compared to those who engage in binge eating and harmful drinking alone or neither behavior, future studies should include and directly compare all four groups.

Conclusions

Study findings were considered within the context of the incentive‐sensitization theory and generally show that elevated anticipatory reward responses are particularly relevant for both binge eating and harmful drinking and that increased levels of craving across reward types might represent a transdiagnostic factor related to both behaviors. Repeated and prolonged engagement in binge eating or harmful drinking might result in more habitual and compulsive responses to reward, characterized by a decreased ability to inhibit reward‐related responses and manage cravings. This review underscores the importance of evaluating how study findings might differ by reward construct as well as the severity of binge eating or harmful drinking in the study sample. Given the lack of evidence‐based intervention strategies that simultaneously consider both hazardous behaviors, it is important to identify specific transdiagnostic factors that relate to these behaviors.
