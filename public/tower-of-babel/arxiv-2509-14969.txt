Stochastic Adaptive Gradient Descent Without Descent
Jean-Francois Aujol, Jérémie Bigot & Camille Castera

Abstract
We introduce a new adaptive step-size strategy for convex optimization with stochastic gradient that exploits the local geometry of the objective function only by means of a first-order stochastic oracle and without any hyper-parameter tuning. The method comes from a theoretically-grounded adaptation of the Adaptive Gradient Descent Without Descent method to the stochastic setting. We prove the convergence of stochastic gradient descent with our step-size under various assumptions, and we show that it empirically competes against tuned baselines.



1
Introduction

We consider the stochastic convex optimization problem

\min_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(x)=\min_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\mathbb{E}\left[f_{\xi}(x)\right],

(1)

where the expectation is taken with respect to some random variable
\xi

such that,
f
and
f_{\xi}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
are convex, differentiable and lower-bounded functions for all
\xi
. We furthermore assume that
f
has at least one minimizer
x^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and we denote its smallest value by
f^{\star}=f(x^{\star})
.
When
\xi
follows a uniform distribution on subsets of
\{1,\ldots,N\}
(for some integer
N>0
), (
1
) reads

\min_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(x)=\min_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}f_{\ell}(x),

(2)

where
f_{1},\ldots,f_{N}
is a set of convex, differentiable and lower-bounded functions.

1.1
Main contributions

We introduce a new adaptive step-size strategy for stochastic gradient descent (SGD) to tackle (
1
). We build upon the adaptive strategy introduced by
malitsky2019adaptive
for deterministic (full batch) convex optimization.
They propose a step-size that automatically adapts to the local geometry of
f
only by means of first-order oracle calls (
i.e.,
gradient evaluations). Their method has the notable advantages of not requiring any hyper-parameter tuning nor the global Lipschitz smoothness of the gradient of the objective function (Definition
1
).
While a stochastic version of the algorithm has been suggested in
(
malitsky2019adaptive
, Section 3)
, it does not inherit the main advantages from the deterministic version: its implementation requires the careful tuning of a hyper-parameter, crucial to guarantee convergence. In contrast, we propose a different stochastic adaptation of their method that preserves the aforementioned advantages in the deterministic setting. As illustrated on Figure
1
, our algorithm converges without requiring the tuning of any hyper-parameter, under various standard assumptions and models for stochastic optimization.

\forall x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

\mathbb{E}_{{k-1}}\left[\nabla f_{{\xi_{k}}}(x)\right]=\nabla f(x)

(3)

\xi_{k}

(4)

arbitrarily small
.

1.3
Related work

Tuning the step-size in gradient descent (GD) has been the topic of a large body of literature.
Without being exhaustive, we review the related work on adaptive step-sizes, with a focus on SGD.

Further adaptation of
malitsky2019adaptive

In the deterministic setting,
malitsky2019adaptive
introduced an adaptive version of GD (here-after called AdaGD) that they later refined in
(
malitsky2024adaptive
)
to improve constant factors in the choice of the step-size. A proximal version has been developed
(
latafat2024adaptive
)
.
As far as we know, there has been little work on adapting AdaGD to the stochastic setting (beyond the heuristics in
malitsky2019adaptive
). One exception is the work of
defazio2022grad
, which takes inspiration from AdaGD, but which derives a rather different algorithm, that only allows for increasing step-sizes. In contrast, our method adapts to the local geometry at every iteration which generates non-monotone step-sizes.

Adaptive methods.

Adaptive methods are a very active topic due to their extensive use for training neural networks
(
duchi2011adaptive
;
mcmahan2010adaptive
;
kingma2014adam
;
tieleman2012lecture
)
. However, they still feature a step-size parameter (and possibly others), whose choice significantly affects the performances
(
sivaprasad2020optimizer
)
.
Beyond AdaGD, many other strategies exist in the deterministic setting
(
li2023simple
;
lan2023optimal
;
khaled2023dowg
)
.
In particular, AdaGD can be seen as a more stable version of the Barzilai-Borwein step-sizes
(
barzilai1988two
;
raydan1997barzilai
)
. Stochastic adaptations of these step-sizes have been proposed for convex
(
tan2016barzilai
)
and non-convex optimization
(
8945980
;
castera2022second
)
, but they always mitigate the instability of the methods through additional hyper-parameters.
Overall, we stress that the stochastic setting possesses additional difficulties that make it significantly more challenging
(
orabona2020icml
)
.

Parameter free algorithms and normalized gradients

Regarding the stochastic setting, the term
parameter-free
may have several meanings.
It is often connected to online learning where one seeks adaptive strategies of methods that
provably
minimize
f
as well as the optimally-tuned instance of the method (up to a factor), see
e.g.
,
(
orabona2014simultaneous
;
Tuningfree24
)
. The definition of parameter free may vary depending on the assumptions on the function, the geometric constants of the problems that are assumed to be known, and the oracles used (e.g., accessing
f_{\xi}
or not).
Despite the name, parameter-free algorithms may still assume additional knowledge of the problem, such as a bound on gradient norms, see
e.g.,

(
orabona2023normalized
, Remark 1)
.
A significant part of the work on parameter-free method uses normalized gradient strategies,
à la
Adagrad
(
mcmahan2010adaptive
;
duchi2011adaptive
)
. They normalize step-sizes using past (stochastic) evaluations of gradients, which has a provable benefit
(
pmlr-v89-li19c
)
. Without being exhaustive, existing approaches beyond the ones above include
(
levy2017online
;
orabona2021parameter
;
pmlr-v178-faw22a
;
ivgi2023dog
)
. We refer to
orabona2023normalized
for a detailed discussion on gradient normalization.
When an upper-bound on gradient norms is known, the coin-betting strategy
(
orabona2016coin
;
orabona2017training
)
provides acceleration by estimating the initial optimality gap
\|x_{0}-x^{\star}\|
, see
(
orabona2023normalized
)
and references therein for more details on this line of work. In this setting, a close-to-optimal bisection method has been developed
(
carmon2022making
)
.
When designing algorithms that can additionally access function values, the family of Polyak step-sizes
(
polyak1987introduction
)
can be considered, even though it was originally designed for deterministic optimization. This kind of methods has recently been adapted to the stochastic setting
(
hazan2019revisiting
;
loizou2021stochastic
)
, but it requires knowledge of the optimal values of the functions
(f_{\xi})_{\xi}
. A different approach with access to function values has been proposed in
(
chen2019fast
)
.
If the functions
f
and the
f_{\xi}
are themselves Lipschitz continuous, recent works show that this can be leveraged to design adaptive strategies in deterministic and stochastic settings
(
cutkosky2019artificial
;
defazio2023learning
;
mishchenko2023prodigy
)
.

Difference with parameter-free approaches

Although our work is connected to parameter-free considerations, we do not focus on theoretical comparisons with optimally tuned SGD. As a matter of fact, even in the deterministic setting, no theoretical acceleration was proved by
malitsky2019adaptive
compared to GD. Instead, our
work focuses on adapting AdaGD to the stochastic setting while preserving the benefits of the method (see Section
1.1
).

Other related approaches

Line-search is a usual way to alleviate step-size selection.
It is however difficult to adapt to the stochastic setting. Existing approaches
(
byrd2012sample
;
franchini2023learning
)
often require auxiliary hyper-parameters for the sake of stability (similarly to the aforementioned Barzilai-Borwein methods).
Beyond GD, inertial methods are ubiquitous in optimization as they allow achieving optimal rates for convex
(
nesterov1983method
)
and strongly convex optimization
(
nesterov2013introductory
, Algorithm 2.2.11)
. Adaptivity can also help to select the additional parameters these methods feature
(
barre2020complexity
;
maier2023near
)
or be used for adaptive restart strategies
(
aujol2024parameter
;
aujol2025fista
)
.
Finally,
universal methods

(
nesterov2015universal
;
li2023simple
;
grimmer2024optimal
)
are worth mentioning.
They relax the Lipschitz continuity of the gradient by assuming it has an (unknown) level of Hölder continuity. They try to automatically adapt to this level of Hölder continuity, without knowing it. The focus is thus different than that of the parameter-free setting previously discussed.

Convergence of SGD.

Despite the different theoretical focus, we do provide theoretical guarantees via the Robbins-Siegmund theorem
(
RS71
)
. The latter is among the main tools to prove convergence in the stochastic setting. Many other proof strategies are possible depending on the variance assumption of the stochastic algorithm considered, see the discussion in
cortild2025new
for further details, and
garrigos2023handbook
for a recollection of proofs.

1.4
Organisation of the paper

In Section
2
, we propose the derivation of a Lyapunov function that is inspired by the one proposed in
malitsky2019adaptive
in a deterministic setting and that we adapt to a stochastic setting. Using such a Lyapunov function, we discuss in Section
3
various assumptions of the functions
f_{\xi}
that allow to derive the convergence of the stochastic sequence
(x_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
using the Lipschitz-adaptive choice (
4
) for the step-size. Numerical experiments are reported in Section
4
to illustrate the performances of our approach over a range of stochastic optimization problems. Finally, some auxiliary results are postponed to a technical Appendix at the end of the paper.

2
Design of the Method

The idea from
malitsky2019adaptive
comes from the derivation of an original Lyapunov function. We present the main idea of this derivation and where our approach departs from theirs. Although Lipschitz continuity is not directly used below, we recall it in Definition
1
in the Appendix.

2.1
Previous Results

In the deterministic setting,
malitsky2019adaptive
propose to use GD (
i.e.,
(
3
) with full batch) algorithm to solve (
2
) with step-size

\lambda_{k}=\min\left\{\frac{\left\|x_{k}-x_{k-1}\right\|}{2\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{k-1})\right\|},\lambda_{k-1}\sqrt{1+\theta_{k-1}}\right\},\mbox{ with }\theta_{k-1}=\frac{\lambda_{k-1}}{\lambda_{k-2}}.

(5)

To ease the reading, we use the same notation
(x_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
to denote the iterates both in stochastic and deterministic settings. The main idea is that for the step-size (
5
), the sequence defined for
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
by

T_{k}=\left\|x_{k+1}-x^{\star}\right\|^{2}+2\left(\lambda_{k}\left(1+\theta_{k}\right)\right)\left(f(x_{k})-f^{\star}\right)+\frac{\left\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\right\|^{2}}{2}

(6)

is non-increasing along the iterates of GD. We thus call
(T_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
a Lyapunov sequence (or function). This is key as the choice (
5
), does not guarantee the decay of
(f(x_{k}))_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, which is the usual Lyapunov sequence used for GD. This motivated the name “adaptive gradient descent without descent” (AdaGD).
Not ensuring descent at each iteration allows for a choice
\lambda_{k}
that adapts to the local Lipschitz constant of
\nabla f
as argued in
malitsky2019adaptive
.

In the case of (
3
), the authors consider the choice

\lambda_{k}=\min\left\{\alpha\Lambda_{k},\lambda_{k-1}\sqrt{1+\theta_{k-1}}\right\}

with either

\Lambda_{k}=\frac{\left\|x_{k}-x_{k-1}\right\|}{\left\|\nabla f_{{\xi_{k}}}(x_{k})-\nabla f_{{\xi_{k}}}(x_{k-1})\right\|}\quad\text{ or }\quad\Lambda_{k}=\frac{\left\|x_{k}-x_{k-1}\right\|}{\left\|\nabla f_{{\zeta_{k}}}(x_{k})-\nabla f_{{\zeta_{k}}}(x_{k-1})\right\|},

with
\zeta_{k}
an independent copy of
\xi_{k}
,
and where
\alpha>0
is a hyper-parameter to be tuned.
They call these choices “biased” and “unbiased” for a reason we explain later.
In contrast, in (
4
), we evaluate the difference of gradients on the
previous
random variable
\xi_{k-1}
:
\left\|\nabla f_{{\xi_{k-1}}}(x_{k})-\nabla f_{{\xi_{k-1}}}(x_{k-1})\right\|
. This is a small but crucial difference that allows getting rid of the hyper-parameter
\alpha
their approach features, and whose choice depends on the (a priori unknown) smoothness constants of the functions
\nabla f_{{\xi_{k}}}
.

2.2
Adaptive stochastic gradient descent

We derive a Lyapunov analysis similar to that in
malitsky2019adaptive
, and highlight the main differences between our approach and the original one.
In what follows, we assume
and ensure
that the step-size satisfies the following.

Assumption 1
.

The step-size
\lambda_{k}>0
is independent of
\xi_{k}
conditionally on
\mathcal{F}_{k-1}
.

Let
k\geq 2
, as in the deterministic case we begin with the decomposition

\displaystyle\left\|x_{k+1}-x^{\star}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\right\|^{2}+2\left\langle x_{k+1}-x_{k},x_{k}-x^{\star}\right\rangle

\displaystyle+\left\|x_{k}-x^{\star}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\right\|^{2}-2\left\langle\lambda_{k}\nabla f_{{\xi_{k}}}(x_{k}),x_{k}-x^{\star}\right\rangle

\displaystyle+\left\|x_{k}-x^{\star}\right\|^{2}.

We introduce the notation
\Delta_{k}=x_{k+1}-x_{k}
.
Taking conditional expectations on both sides of the above equality, we obtain that

\mathbb{E}_{{k-1}}\left[\left\|x_{k+1}-x^{\star}\right\|^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{{k-1}}\left[\left\|\Delta_{k}\right\|^{2}\right]-2\left\langle\mathbb{E}_{{k-1}}\left[\lambda_{k}\nabla f_{{\xi_{k}}}(x_{k})\right],x_{k}-x^{\star}\right\rangle+\left\|x_{k}-x^{\star}\right\|^{2}.

(7)

Focusing on the second term in the right-hand side: due to Assumption
1
the conditional independence between
\xi_{k}
and
\lambda_{k}
allows splitting the conditional expectation into a product. This is the reason for the terminology “unbiased” as it then allows to use
the property that
\mathbb{E}_{{k-1}}\left[\nabla f_{{\xi_{k}}}(x_{k})\right]=\nabla f(x_{k})
, meaning that
\nabla f_{{\xi_{k}}}(x_{k})
is an unbiased estimator of
\nabla f(x_{k})
. We obtain

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathbb{E}_{{k-1}}\left[\lambda_{k}\nabla f_{{\xi_{k}}}(x_{k})\right],x_{k}-x^{\star}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{{k-1}}\left[\lambda_{k}\right]\left\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),x_{k}-x^{\star}\right\rangle\geq\mathbb{E}_{{k-1}}\left[\lambda_{k}\right]\left(f(x_{k})-f^{\star}\right),

(8)

where we used the convexity of
f
in the last inequality. The function values
f(x_{k})
appears above, so we expect the quantity
f(x_{k-1})
to appear as well later in our Lyapunov analysis.

Now focusing on
\Delta_{k}
, it is trivial but convenient to write

\left\|\Delta_{k}\right\|^{2}=2\left\|\Delta_{k}\right\|^{2}-\left\|\Delta_{k}\right\|^{2}
and use the identity

2\left\|\Delta_{k}\right\|^{2}=-2\left\langle\lambda_{k}\nabla f_{{\xi_{k}}}(x_{k}),\Delta_{k}\right\rangle
.
We then make an extra stochastic gradient appear,

\displaystyle\begin{split}-2\left\langle\lambda_{k}\nabla f_{{\xi_{k}}}(x_{k}),\Delta_{k}\right\rangle=\underbrace{-2\lambda_{k}\left\langle\nabla f_{{\xi_{k}}}(x_{k})-\nabla f_{{\xi_{k-1}}}(x_{k-1}),\Delta_{k}\right\rangle}_{:=A_{k}}\underbrace{-2\lambda_{k}\left\langle\nabla f_{{\xi_{k-1}}}(x_{k-1}),\Delta_{k}\right\rangle}_{:=B_{k}}\end{split}

(9)

This is a first pivoting choice in our analysis, it yields two terms that we analyze separately.

2.2.1
Bounding
B_{k}

We first use (
3
) to substitute
\nabla f_{{\xi_{k-1}}}(x_{k-1})
in
B_{k}
:

B_{k}=-2\lambda_{k}\left\langle-\frac{1}{\lambda_{k-1}}(x_{k}-x_{k-1}),-\lambda_{k}\nabla f_{{\xi_{k}}}(x_{k})\right\rangle=-2\frac{\lambda_{k}^{2}}{\lambda_{k-1}}\left\langle x_{k}-x_{k-1},\nabla f_{{\xi_{k}}}(x_{k})\right\rangle

We take conditional expectation and use again Assumption
1
and the convexity of
f
to get

\mathbb{E}_{{k-1}}\left[B_{k}\right]=-2\frac{\mathbb{E}_{{k-1}}\left[\lambda_{k}^{2}\right]}{\lambda_{k-1}}\left\langle x_{k}-x_{k-1},\nabla f(x_{k})\right\rangle\leq 2\frac{\mathbb{E}_{{k-1}}\left[\lambda_{k}^{2}\right]}{\lambda_{k-1}}(f(x_{k-1})-f(x_{k})),\\
=2\frac{\mathbb{E}_{{k-1}}\left[\lambda_{k}^{2}\right]}{\lambda_{k-1}}(f(x_{k-1})-f^{\star})-2\frac{\mathbb{E}_{{k-1}}\left[\lambda_{k}^{2}\right]}{\lambda_{k-1}}(f(x_{k})-f^{\star})

(10)

We obtain a term with
f(x_{k-1})
that will be balanced by (
8
) and will result in a condition between
\lambda_{k}
and
\lambda_{k-1}
, similar to that in (
5
). Plugging
\nabla f_{{\xi_{k-1}}}(x_{k-1})
into (
9
) allows our analysis to remain faithful to the approach followed in the deterministic case.

2.2.2
Dealing with the term
A_{k}

To handle
A_{k}
in (
9
) we start by using
|\left\langle x,y\right\rangle|\leq\left\|x\right\|^{2}+\frac{\left\|y\right\|^{2}}{4}
,
\forall x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
:

-2\lambda_{k}\left\langle\nabla f_{{\xi_{k}}}(x_{k})-\nabla f_{{\xi_{k-1}}}(x_{k-1}),\Delta_{k}\right\rangle\leq 2\lambda_{k}^{2}\left\|\nabla f_{{\xi_{k}}}(x_{k})-\nabla f_{{\xi_{k-1}}}(x_{k-1})\right\|^{2}+\frac{\left\|\Delta_{k}\right\|^{2}}{2}.

The second term in the right-hand side above will be compensated by the
-\left\|\Delta_{k}\right\|^{2}
obtained above (
9
). Handling the first term is more involved. We plug again an extra stochastic gradient:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\left\|\nabla f_{{\xi_{k}}}(x_{k})-\nabla f_{{\xi_{k-1}}}(x_{k-1})\right\|^{2}\\
=&\left\|\nabla f_{{\xi_{k}}}(x_{k})-\nabla f_{{\xi_{k-1}}}(x_{k})+\nabla f_{{\xi_{k-1}}}(x_{k})-\nabla f_{{\xi_{k-1}}}(x_{k-1})\right\|^{2}\\
\leq&2\underbrace{\left\|\nabla f_{{\xi_{k}}}(x_{k})-\nabla f_{{\xi_{k-1}}}(x_{k})\right\|^{2}}_{\text{induced by sampling}}+2\underbrace{\left\|\nabla f_{{\xi_{k-1}}}(x_{k})-\nabla f_{{\xi_{k-1}}}(x_{k-1})\right\|^{2}}_{\text{induced by curvature}},\end{split}

(11)

where we used
\left\|x+y\right\|^{2}\leq 2\left\|x\right\|^{2}+2\left\|y\right\|^{2}
. The decomposition (
11
) is a subtle but crucial difference with
malitsky2019adaptive
in the stochastic setting. As highlighted in (
11
) it allows bounding the difference of stochastic gradients by a sampling error and a “curvature-induced” term. In the deterministic setting (
\xi_{k}=\xi_{k-1}
) the first term vanishes and the curvature term boils down to the difference of gradients appearing in (
5
). This leads to our first condition on
\lambda_{k}
in (
4
):

\lambda_{k}\leq\frac{\left\|x_{k}-x_{k-1}\right\|}{2\sqrt{2}\left\|\nabla f_{{\xi_{k-1}}}(x_{k})-\nabla f_{{\xi_{k-1}}}(x_{k-1})\right\|}

(12)

that allows bounding the curvature term:

4\lambda_{k}^{2}{\left\|\nabla f_{{\xi_{k-1}}}(x_{k})-\nabla f_{{\xi_{k-1}}}(x_{k-1})\right\|^{2}}\leq\frac{\left\|\Delta_{k-1}\right\|^{2}}{2}.

Therefore, using again Assumption
1
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}_{{k-1}}\left[A_{k}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}_{{k-1}}\left[\lambda_{k}^{2}\right]\mathbb{E}_{{k-1}}\left[\left\|\nabla f_{{\xi_{k}}}(x_{k})-\nabla f_{{\xi_{k-1}}}(x_{k})\right\|^{2}\right]+\frac{\left\|\Delta_{k-1}\right\|^{2}}{2}+\frac{\left\|\Delta_{k}\right\|^{2}}{2}.

(13)

2.3
Decay of the Lyapunov function in Expectation

We use (
10
) and (
13
) in (
9
) to bound the first term in (
7
):

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{k-1}}\left[\left\|\Delta_{k}\right\|^{2}\right]\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{k-1}}\left[\lambda_{k}^{2}\right]\mathbb{E}_{{k-1}}\left[\left\|\nabla f_{{\xi_{k}}}(x_{k})-\nabla f_{{\xi_{k-1}}}(x_{k})\right\|^{2}\right]+\frac{\left\|\Delta_{k-1}\right\|^{2}}{2}

(

5
Conclusion

We introduced AdaSGD, an adaptation of AdaGD to the stochastic setting that coincides with AdaGD in the full-batch case.
AdaSGD transfers the advantages of AdaGD to the stochastic setting, some of which were lost in the original stochastic adaptation of AdaGD. Our method does not rely on any heuristics and is rather derived via a careful Lyapunov analysis, resulting in three variants step-size strategies.
We conducted a thorough analysis, showing convergence and rates for our methods. These guarantees however rely on stronger assumptions than in the deterministic setting, which is due to the noise induced by stochastic gradients.
We show promising experiments that evidence that our primary goal is achieved: AdaSGD is very robust to the choice of the initial step-size without sacrificing minimization performance.

The main directions for further research revolve around relaxing the requirements related to convexity.
In Appendix
C
, we provide partial theoretical results that suggest that the convergence results of Section
3
could possibly be extended to all convex functions. On the other-hand, adapting AdaSGD to non-convex functions would allow training neural networks but represents a significant challenge since the derivation of Section
2
relies heavily on the convexity inequality.

Appendix A
Ensuring Square-summability of the Step-sizes

Theorem
3.2
holds true when the sequence of step-sizes
(\lambda_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is square-summable.
We can guarantee this in
Case-1
,
Case-2
and
Case-3
as discussed in the main text. We now prove this and provide further discussion on these three settings.

A.1
Case-1
: Strong Convexity

In
Case-1
, we assume that all the functions
f_{\xi}
are strongly convex, which is a sub-class of convex functions. We recall that this means that there exists
\mu>0
, such that for all
\xi
, and
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

f_{\xi}(y)\geq f_{\xi}(x)+\langle\nabla f_{{\xi}}(x),y-x\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}\|x-y\|^{2},

or equivalently

\langle\nabla f_{{\xi}}(x)-\nabla f_{{\xi}}(y),x-y\rangle\geq\mu\|x-y\|^{2}.

(17)

Note that we assume that all the functions
f_{\xi}
have the same strong convexity parameter
\mu
. We could have equivalently assumed that there exists
\mu>0
such that each
f_{\xi}
is
\mu_{\xi}
strongly convex with
\mu_{\xi}\geq\mu>0
. In the case where
f
is a finite-sum of functions this is always possible (by taking
\mu=\min_{\xi}\mu_{\xi}
).

Strong convexity allows upper-bounding the step-sizes
(\lambda_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
. Indeed, by using Cauchy-Schwarz inequality on (
17
), we get,

\left\|\nabla f_{{\xi}}(x)-\nabla f_{{\xi}}(y)\right\|\left\|x-y\right\|\geq\mu\|x-y\|^{2}.

So in particular, using this on the iterates
(x_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
and mini-batches
(\xi_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
in Algorithm
2
, for all
k\geq 1

\frac{\left\|x_{k}-x_{k-1}\right\|}{\left\|\nabla f_{{\xi_{k-1}}}(x_{k})-\nabla f_{{\xi_{k-1}}}(x_{k-1})\right\|}\leq\frac{1}{\mu}.

(18)

Hence, from the definition of the step-size
\lambda_{k}
in (
4
), we deduce that,

\lambda_{k}\leq\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}\mu},\quad&\text{(Variant {I})}\\
\frac{1}{k^{1/2+\delta}}\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}\mu}\quad&\text{(Variant {II} and {III})}\end{cases}

Therefore, for Variants
II
and
III
of the step-size, we can ensure that,

\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\lambda_{k}^{2}\leq\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{k^{1+2\delta}}<+\infty.

Therefore, Theorem
3.2
holds true in
Case-1
. Why strong convexity may seem to be the natural assumption for ensuring square-summability, a similar result can be derived in non-strongly convex settings.

A.2
Case-2
: Linear Regression.

We now consider the classical machine learning problem of linear regression, where
f
is a finite sum (
2
) and the
f_{\ell}
’s need not be strongly convex. We assume that for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, the functions
(f_{\ell})_{\ell\in\{1,\ldots,N\}}
take the following form:

f_{\ell}(x)=\frac{1}{2}(y_{\ell}-\left\langle w_{\ell},x\right\rangle)^{2},

where
w_{\ell}
is a
d
-dimensional vector of predictors (with
w_{\ell}\neq 0
) and
y_{\ell}
is a scalar response variable. The discussion can easily be extended to the case where
w_{\ell}
are matrices and
y_{\ell}
are vectors, which we do not consider for the sake of simplicity.

For all
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
, and
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we have

\nabla f_{{\ell}}(x)=(\left\langle w_{\ell},x\right\rangle-y_{\ell})w_{\ell},

(19)

and the Hessian of
f_{\ell}
is given by

\nabla^{2}f_{\ell}(x)=w_{\ell}w_{\ell}^{T},\quad\mbox{ for all }x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

Since
w_{\ell}w_{\ell}^{T}
is a rank-1 matrix, the Hessian admits a zero eigenvalue (actually it even has
d-1
) so the function
f_{\ell}
is not strongly convex.

We now use this on the iterates of Algorithm
1
. For the sake of simplicity, we assume, without loss of generality, that the mini-batches are single valued,
i.e.
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\xi_{k}\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
.
Then remark that for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
of Algorithm
1
, (
19
) implies that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left\|\nabla f_{{\xi_{k-1}}}(x_{k})-\nabla f_{{\xi_{k-1}}}(x_{k-1})\right\|^{2}&=\left\|(y_{\xi_{k-1}}-\left\langle w_{\xi_{k-1}},x_{k}\right\rangle)w_{\xi_{k-1}}-(y_{\xi_{k-1}}-\left\langle w_{\xi_{k-1}},x_{k-1}\right\rangle)w_{\xi_{k-1}}\right\|^{2}\\
&=\left\|\left\langle w_{\xi_{k-1}},x_{k}-x_{k-1}\right\rangle w_{\xi_{k-1}}\right\|^{2}=\left\langle w_{\xi_{k-1}},x_{k}-x_{k-1}\right\rangle^{2}\left\|w_{\xi_{k-1}}\right\|^{2}.\end{split}

But remark that from the definition of Algorithm
1
,

x_{k}-x_{k-1}=-\lambda_{k-1}w_{\xi_{k-1}}=-\lambda_{k-1}\alpha_{k-1}w_{\xi_{k-1}},

where
\alpha_{k-1}=(\left\langle w_{\xi_{k-1}},x\right\rangle-y_{\xi_{k-1}})
.
So inserting the above in (
20
),

\left\|\nabla f_{{\xi_{k-1}}}(x_{k})-\nabla f_{{\xi_{k-1}}}(x_{k-1})\right\|^{2}=\lambda_{k-1}^{2}\alpha_{k-1}^{2}\left\|w_{\xi_{k-1}}\right\|^{6}.

(20)

Then similarly,

\left\|x_{k}-x_{k-1}\right\|^{2}=\lambda_{k-1}^{2}\alpha_{k-1}^{2}\|w_{\xi_{k-1}}\|^{2},

So we obtain that

\frac{\left\|x_{k}-x_{k-1}\right\|}{\left\|\nabla f_{{\xi_{k-1}}}(x_{k})-\nabla f_{{\xi_{k-1}}}(x_{k-1})\right\|}=\frac{1}{\|w_{\xi_{k-1}}\|^{2}}.

Finally, by using the finite-sum structure, we denote by
\hat{\mu}=\max_{\ell\in\{1,\ldots,N\}}{\|w_{\ell}\|^{2}}
and we arrive to the same inequality as in (
18
),
without
relying on strong convexity. Therefore the same reasoning as in
Case-1
applies here and we deduce that for
Variants
II
and
III
of the step-size,

\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\lambda_{k}^{2}<+\infty.

A.3
Case-3
: Sum of Ridge Functions

As a third setting, we consider a generalization of linear regression where the function
f
is a finite sum of convex ridge functions.
More precisely, we assume that, for each
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
, the function
f_{\ell}
takes the form

f_{\ell}(x)=g_{\ell}\left(\left\langle w_{\ell},x\right\rangle\right),

where
w_{\ell}
is defined as in
Case-2
, and
g_{\ell}\colon{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a known function that is differentiable and

-strongly convex on

. This setting includes the previous example of linear regression with
g_{\ell}(u)=\frac{1}{2}(u-y_{\ell})^{2}
, for all
u\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

Linear combinations of ridge functions constitute a functional class that is a central tool in the approximation theory of multivariate functions, and for the study of neural networks with a single or two hidden layers. For a survey on these topics we refer to
[
23
]
. Using sums of ridge functions has also proved to be relevant for the regularization of inverse problems
[
20
]
, the study of convex optimization in the space of measures
[
10
]
, and for adversarial bandit problems
[
50
]
to name a few.

Note that assuming that as for linear regression, the
f_{\ell}
are not strongly convex (they are only in one direction given by
w_{\ell}
), and that
f
needs not to be strongly convex as well. Hence, this setting remains different from
Case-1
.

Similarly to
Case-2
, for all
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
and
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

\nabla f_{{\ell}}(x)=g_{\ell}^{\prime}(\left\langle w_{\ell},x\right\rangle)w_{\ell}.

(21)

Again, assuming without loss of generality, that
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\xi_{k}\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
, using the strong convexity of
g_{\xi}
, the above implies for Algorithm
1
that, for all
k\geq 1
,

\left\|\nabla f_{{\xi_{k-1}}}({x_{k}})-\nabla f_{{\xi_{k-1}}}({x_{k-1}})\right\|^{2}=\left(g^{\prime}_{\xi_{k-1}}\left(\left\langle w_{\xi_{k-1}},{x_{k}}\right\rangle\right)-g^{\prime}_{\xi_{k-1}}\left(\left\langle w_{\xi_{k-1}},{x_{k-1}}\right\rangle\right)\right)^{2}\left\|w_{\xi_{k-1}}\right\|^{2}\\
\geq\mu_{\xi_{k-1}}^{2}\left(\left\langle w_{\xi_{k-1}},{x_{k}}-{x_{k-1}}\right\rangle\right)^{2}\left\|w_{\xi_{k-1}}\right\|^{2}=\mu_{\xi_{k-1}}^{2}\left(\left\langle w_{\xi},\lambda_{k-1}\nabla f_{{{\xi_{k-1}}}}({x_{k-1}})\right\rangle\right)^{2}\left\|w_{\xi}\right\|^{2}.

Moreover from (
21
), one has that

\left\langle w_{\xi},\nabla f_{{{\xi_{k-1}}}}({x_{k-1}})\right\rangle=g^{\prime}_{\xi}\left(w_{\xi}^{T}x\right)\left\|w_{\xi}\right\|^{2},

consequently

\left\|\nabla f_{{{\xi_{k-1}}}}({x_{k}})-\nabla f_{{{\xi_{k-1}}}}({x_{k-1}})\right\|^{2}\geq\mu_{\xi_{k-1}}^{2}\lambda_{k-1}^{2}g^{\prime}_{{\xi_{k-1}}}\left(w_{{\xi_{k-1}}}^{T}x\right)\left\|w_{{\xi_{k-1}}}\right\|^{6},

and similarly to
Case-1
, we obtain the following upper bound

U_{k}=-2\frac{{\lambda_{k}^{2}}}{\lambda_{k-1}}\left(f(x_{k-1})-f^{\star}\right)+2\left({\lambda_{k}}+\frac{{\lambda_{k}^{2}}}{\lambda_{k-1}}\right)\left(f(x_{k})-f^{\star}\right),

that is

U_{k}=2\frac{{\lambda_{k}^{2}}}{\lambda_{k-1}}\left(f(x_{k})-f(x_{k-1})\right)+2{\lambda_{k}}\left(f(x_{k})-f^{\star}\right)

Notice that we always have

f(x_{k})-f^{\star}\geq 0
. Two cases may occur:

(a)

If
f(x_{k})\geq f(x_{k-1})
, then we always have
U_{k}\geq 0
.

(b)

If
f(x_{k-1})>f(x_{k})
, then we see that
U_{k}\geq 0
if and only if

\lambda_{k}\leq\frac{f(x_{k})-f^{\star}}{f(x_{k-1})-f(x_{k})}\lambda_{k-1},

which concludes the proof thanks to the choice (
29
) for
\lambda_{k}
.

∎

Proposition C.2
.

Suppose that Assumptions
2
holds, and that Assumption
3
is satisfied for the choice of step-size (
29
).
Then, we have that

\sup_{k\geq 0}\mathbb{E}\left[\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|\right]<+\infty

Proof.

Thanks to Lemma
C.1
, we can apply the Robbins-Siegmund theorem (Theorem
B.2
) with
V_{k}=\left\|x_{k}-x^{\star}\right\|^{2}+\frac{\left\|x_{k}-x_{k-1}\right\|^{2}}{2}
which concludes the proof.
∎

We can now give convergence results.

Proposition C.3
.

Let us define

\hat{x}_{K}=\frac{\left({\lambda_{K}}+\frac{{\lambda_{K}^{2}}}{\lambda_{K-1}}\right)x_{K}+\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}w_{k}x_{k}}{S_{K}}

with

w_{k}={\lambda_{k}}+\frac{{\lambda_{k}^{2}}}{\lambda_{k-1}}-\frac{{\lambda_{k+1}^{2}}}{\lambda_{k}}

and

S_{K}={\lambda_{K}}+\frac{{\lambda_{K}^{2}}}{\lambda_{K-1}}+\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}w_{k}=\frac{{\lambda_{1}^{2}}}{\lambda_{0}}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\lambda_{k}}

Suppose that Assumption
2
holds, and that Assumption
3
is satisfied for the choice of step-size (
29
).
Then, there exists
C>0
such that
we have

f(\hat{x}_{K})-f^{*}\leq\frac{C}{S_{K}}\mbox{ a.s.}

Proof.

We have:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}U_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\left({\lambda_{K}}+\frac{{\lambda_{K}^{2}}}{\lambda_{K-1}}\right)\left(f(x_{K})-f^{\star}\right)-2\frac{\mathbb{E}_{{0}}\left[\lambda_{1}^{2}\right]}{\lambda_{0}}\left(f(x_{0})-f^{\star}\right)

\displaystyle+2\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}\left(\underbrace{\left({\lambda_{k}}+\frac{{\lambda_{k}^{2}}}{\lambda_{k-1}}-\frac{{\lambda_{k+1}^{2}}}{\lambda_{k}}\right)}_{=w_{k}}\left(f(x_{k})-f^{\star}\right)\right)

Thanks to Lemma
C.1
, we can apply Robbins-Siegmund theorem (Theorem
B.2
) to deduce that
\sum_{k\geq 0}U_{k}<+\infty
a.s. Then, notice that from the update rule (
29
) of
\lambda_{k}
, we have that
w_{k}\geq 0
.
We thus deduce that there exists a constant
C>0
such that:

\left({\lambda_{K}}+\frac{{\lambda_{K}^{2}}}{\lambda_{K-1}}\right)\left(f(x_{K})-f^{\star}\right)+\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}w_{k}\left(f(x_{k})-f^{\star}\right)\leq C\mbox{ a.s.}

Since
f
is convex, we now want to use Jensen inequality, that states that if
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\rho_{k}=1
:

f\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\rho_{k}x_{k}\right)\leq\sum_{k=1}^{K}\rho_{k}f(x_{k})

So that we get

f(\hat{x}_{K})-f^{*}\leq\frac{C}{S_{K}}\mbox{ a.s.}

(30)

which concludes the proof.
∎

We finally add a last assumption.

Assumption 4

(Divergence of the steps)
.

The random sequence
(\lambda_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of step-sizes satisfies

\sum_{k\geq 0}{\lambda_{k}}=+\infty

Notice that Assumption
4
is satisfied in the smooth and strongly convex case.
Notice also that this assumption would be directly satisfied if we only had the two first conditions in the update rule of
\lambda_{k}
.

Corollary C.4
.

Suppose that Assumptions
2
holds, and that Assumption
3
and
4
are satisfied for the choice of step-size (
29
).
Then, we have that

f(\hat{x}_{K})-f^{*}\to 0\mbox{ a.s.}

Proof.

Notice that

S_{K}=\frac{{\lambda_{1}^{2}}}{\lambda_{0}}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\lambda_{k}}\geq\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\lambda_{k}}

Hence, by using Assumption
4
in (
30
), we get the result of the corollary.

∎

•

,

All the methods feature a single tunable parameter
