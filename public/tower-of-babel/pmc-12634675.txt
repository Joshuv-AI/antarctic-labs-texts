Study on the failure law of overlying rock under coal seam in close distance coal seam and feasibility discrimination of upward mining
Authors: Yanping Miao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractUpward mining of close-distance coal seams is technically challenging due to the complex nature of overburden failure and the potential hazards associated with water and gas. This study assesses the feasibility of mining the 4–3 seam above the 4–4 seam at the Hongliulin Coal Mine using a combined analytical, numerical, and field-based approach. An elastic mechanics model was developed to estimate the maximum roof instability depth, and a correction was introduced to account for mining height and caving features. A discrete element model (3DEC) was then used to simulate the deformation and fracture evolution of the overburden. Finally, two boreholes were drilled to observe the caving and fractured zones for field verification. Results show that the maximum instability depth calculated ranges from 12.46 to 16.00 m, while field measurements give a caving height of 14.43 m, indicating good agreement among theory, simulation, and observation. The overlying coal seam remains stable with limited inclination and deformation, confirming the feasibility of upward mining under the studied geological setting. This integrated method provides a practical framework for upward mining assessment. The study also notes limitations in parameter variability and limited sampling, highlighting the need for further data collection and risk control. The findings offer valuable guidance for safe and efficient mining in close-distance seams.Keywords: Close distance coal seam group, Upward mining, Overburden rock destruction, Numerical simulation

Introduction

In the appendix of the coal mine safety regulations, the close distance coal seam is defined as “the coal seam with a small interlayer distance and great influence on each other during mining”. It has been proven that the number of mineable coal seams in most mining areas in China is less than 1/3 of the total number, and the number of mineable coal seams is less than 5. It can be observed that coal seams with varying geological structures are present in most mining areas in China. In recent years, to ensure the steady development of China’s scientific and technological level and the quality of people’s daily life, coal, the most crucial fossil energy source in China, has been subject to high-intensity mining. At the same time, the mining of coal seams with high loads in the mine has enabled the completion of mining coal seams with shallow burial depths and simple occurrence conditions1. Gradually, the mining of multiple coal seams has begun, and the associated mining problems have steadily been revealed2. The mining sequence of coal seam groups in China is primarily divided into three types: ascending mining, descending mining, and simultaneous mining of upper and lower coal seams3, with the latter being the primary method4. Downward mining is to mine the upper coal seam first and then the lower coal seam; Ascending mining is to mine the lower coal seam first and then the upper coal seam5. Downward mining is the preferred mining method when mining coal seams. However, due to the lack of fineness of geological exploration carried out in the past, there are still minable coal seams above the mined coal seams in some mining areas6, or due to the influence of mining technology constraints, rock burst risk, economic benefits and other factors, the lower coal seam is preferentially mined, and then the upward mining method is used to organize the production of the upper coal seam7.

The ascending mining of close-distance coal seams can effectively improve the surrounding rock stress and reduce the risk of dynamic disasters in scenarios involving deep high ground pressure, complex hydrogeology, or residual resource recovery8. Therefore, it is essential to study the development law of overlying rock fractures, the equilibrium condition of rock structure, the discrimination of mining feasibility and the law of rock pressure behavior of working face in the upward mining of close distance coal seams, which is also of great significance for safe and efficient production, reducing resource waste, prolonging mine life and ensuring social stability. The core of its safety implementation lies in feasibility discrimination. Relevant research has evolved from empirical formulas and comprehensive indicators to intelligent quantitative evaluation, gradually forming a systematic theoretical system9.

To address the series of technical challenges associated with close-distance coal seam mining10, extensive research has been conducted by scholars worldwide from multiple perspectives11. In the early stages, feasibility evaluations of upward mining primarily relied on the “three-zone” theory of overburden movement (caving zone, fracture zone, and bending subsidence zone)12. Among these, the height calculation method of the three zones became a fundamental criterion. By calculating the heights of the caving and fracture zones induced by lower seam extraction, the relative position of the upper seam can be determined. If it lies above the fracture zone, upward mining is considered feasible13–15. Subsequently, the overburden equilibrium method was developed as a supplementary approach. This method compares the actual inter-seam spacing with the critical spacing to evaluate whether the overburden is prone to step-like dislocation, thereby assessing its stability. In recent years, feasibility evaluation methods have evolved toward more dynamic and integrated approaches16. Numerical simulation techniques have been widely applied17,18, with FLAC3D used to analyze plastic zone expansion and stress redistribution after lower seam extraction19. Meanwhile, discrete element and PFC methods have revealed overburden failure and fracture evolution under complex conditions20,21,22. On this basis, comprehensive evaluation frameworks have gradually emerged, integrating the ratio method, three-zone method, overburden equilibrium method, and time-interval method (e.g., lag-time assessment), thereby forming a multi-indicator cross-validation system23.

Existing studies on upward mining in close-distance coal seams have primarily focused on a single approach, such as theoretical criteria or numerical simulations. Although some scholars have employed “two-zone” field measurements for validation, a systematic framework that integrates theoretical modeling, numerical simulation, and field verification is still lacking. Moreover, many previous works remain at a qualitative level, without sufficient quantitative error evaluation or in-depth discussion of risk control. Taking the upward mining of the 4–3 and 4–4 seams at Hongliulin Mine as the engineering background, this study establishes a theoretical mechanical model to calculate the maximum overburden failure depth induced by lower seam extraction, compares the results with the traditional “two-zone” criterion, and further employs 3DEC numerical simulations to reveal fracture evolution and validate the findings. In this way, the feasibility of upward mining for the 4–3 and 4–4 seams is comprehensively assessed. The research not only advances the fundamental theory of upward mining but also provides scientific guidance for the sustainable and efficient utilization of coal resources. Compared with existing studies, the main innovations of this work are as follows: (1) an analytical model of maximum overburden failure depth in upward mining is derived based on elastic mechanics, with a correction method that considers mining height and caving characteristics; (2) 3DEC numerical simulations are employed to quantitatively reveal fracture evolution and instability mechanisms, which are cross-validated with the theoretical model; (3) “two-zone” borehole measurements are conducted for field verification, establishing a theoretical–numerical–field triple validation framework that offers a more rigorous technical pathway for upward mining feasibility evaluation in close-distance coal seams.

Theoretical research on two-band analysis

Height analysis of the caving zone in the upward mining face of the coal seam group

After a large area of coal seam is mined out, the surrounding rock strata lose balance and deform, moving under the action of gravity. The deformation and movement of the surrounding rock are different under different geological conditions and mining technology conditions. When the long wall complete caving mining method is used to mine the near-horizontally or gently inclined coal seam, the movement of the overlying strata in the goaf is stable. Generally, the caving zone, fracture zone, and bending subsidence zone are formed from bottom to top. After the advancement of the working face, the roof caving of the goaf forms three zones in the vertical direction, namely, the caving zone, the fracture zone, and the bending subsidence zone. The rocks in the collapse zone are broken into blocks with a disorderly arrangement and significant gaps between them. Gas is mixed with a large amount of air. The fracture zone is located above the collapse zone, and the rock blocks in the collapse zone support the rock strata in this zone. After a fracture, the rock blocks are still orderly arranged, but more bedding and bedding fractures will be formed.

In the area of collapse zone, the rock fracture breaks away from the rock matrix and collapses, and the collapsed rock block is no longer in the original rock stratum; In the water flowing fracture zone, the rock mass is longitudinally fractured or separated, but the rock layer is not separated from the parent, and the rock block still maintains the overall layering. These characteristics are the primary indicators of the distinction between the fracture zone and the collapse zone.

The hydraulic fracture zone is a concept of rock movement that is used to comprehensively reflect the hydraulic conductivity of the overlying strata’s fracture range and fracture process. Under the condition that the rock stratum is relatively complex, because the cracks are relatively easy to expand, the scope of the crack zone can not fully reflect the stability of the rock mass in this area. Therefore, using the two-zone height method as the basis for discrimination in upward mining sometimes cannot entirely reflect the actual site situation. Under the condition of a small interval or thick coal seam, upward mining can still be successfully carried out under the condition that the development range of the fracture zone reaches the upper coal seam. Therefore, when using the two-zone method to evaluate the feasibility of upward mining, it must be ensured that the scope of the caving zone cannot reach the position of the upper coal seam, and there is a certain safe distance from the upper coal seam.

Simulation of overburden failure in close-distance coal seam mining

To further study the influence of 4–2 and 5–2 coal seam mining on the movement and deformation of 4–3 and 4–4 coal seams, the discrete element numerical simulation software 3DEC was used to analyze the stress field, displacement field and overburden fracture characteristics of 4–4 and 4–3 coal seams under the mining conditions of 4–2 and 5–2 coal seams in Hongliulin coal mine.

Establishment of numerical model

A 3DEC numerical calculation model of the Hongliulin mine has been established. The model size is 2000 m × 180 m (length × height)/2000 m × 240 m (length × height). The influence of five working faces on 4–3 and 4–4 coal seams was simulated. To calculate the boundary conditions of the model, firstly, the horizontal constraints are imposed on each boundary around the model, that is, the horizontal displacement of the surrounding boundary is 0; Then fix the bottom boundary of the model, that is, the horizontal and vertical displacement of the bottom boundary are 0; Finally, the top of the model is set as a free boundary. The elastic–plastic constitutive model is employed for numerical calculations, and the yield criterion used is the Mohr–Coulomb yield criterion. The Mohr–Coulomb strength criterion, based on elastic–plastic theory, is used for the model. The established numerical calculation model is shown in Fig. 10. Analysis of simulation results of stress distribution law

By monitoring the floor stress of 4–3 and 4–4 coal seams after mining 4–2 and 5–2 coal seams at the monitoring points arranged on the floor of 4–3 and 4–4 coal seams, according to the monitoring results, the floor stress of 4–3 and 4–4 coal seams is drawn as shown in Fig. 13.

Distribution characteristics of floor stress in 4–3 and 4–4 coal seams. (a) 4–4 Coal seam stress, (b) 4−3 Coal seam stress.

It can be seen from the figure that after the mining of coal face 5–2, the stress of coal floor 4–3 and 4–4 above the working face presents a single peak distribution, and the vertical stress from both sides of the working face to the middle increases first and then decreases. The reason is that on both sides of the working face, the overburden is hinged together after collapse and fracture, causing the overburden stress to deflect, and the vertical stress to decrease. As the distance to the middle of the working face decreases, the overburden collapses into the goaf, gradually compacts, and the stress increases, eventually returning to the original rock stress state in the middle of the working face. On the coal bodies on both sides of the working face, because the overlying strata of the working face collapsed along a certain caving angle, a cantilever structure was formed at both ends. The load of the cantilever rock mass is transferred to the coal bodies on both sides, increasing the vertical stress of the coal bodies. In contrast, at the principal working face, the vertical stress gradually decreased and recovered to the original rock stress.

It can be seen from the above figure that under the mining of the 4–2 and 5–2 coal seams, the floor stress of the 4–4 coal seam ranges from 0 to 16.5 MPa, while the floor stress of the 4–3 coal seam ranges from 0 to 12.3 MPa. Discussion

Theoretical contributions and engineering significance

This study built a mechanical model of overburden failure for upward mining in close-distance seams. Numerical simulation and borehole observation were combined for validation. Results show that mining of the 5–2 seam caused a maximum roof failure of 12.46–16 m, which did not reach the 4–4 and 4–3 seams. Simulation and field data matched well, confirming feasibility and safety under these geological conditions.

From the theoretical perspective, the study proposed a failure height method that integrates analytical modeling, numerical simulation, and field verification. This provides a systematic framework for feasibility evaluation, overcoming the limitations of single-method judgment. The method not only quantitatively estimates failure height but also reveals the control role of key strata, demonstrating academic innovation.

From an engineering perspective, the results provide a scientific basis for recovering the 4–4 and 4–3 seams, thereby reducing risks through empirical judgment. The workflow also offers a reference for other upward mining cases. It can improve resource recovery, extend mine life, and strike a balance between safety and economic benefits.

Engineering control and preventive measures

Although upward mining of the 4–3 and 4–4 seams is feasible, risks remain. Several measures are recommended. First, conduct systematic gas and hydrogeological surveys in the goaf, using geophysical and drilling methods. Implement pre-drainage and gas extraction to ensure safe conditions. Second, leave or widen protective pillars in key areas, and use grouting or backfilling when necessary to enhance stability. Third, adopt staged and sectional mining, control panel length and advance rate, and reduce large-scale failures. Fourth, establish real-time monitoring and emergency systems. Install gas, water pressure, and microseismic sensors, set alarm thresholds, and prepare emergency plans.

After lower seam mining, fractures may allow for early gas release, thereby reducing the accumulation risk. However, since the seams are shallow, the water-conducting fracture zone may extend to the surface, thereby increasing the risk of water hazards. Therefore, thorough detection and treatment of goaf water is essential to ensure safe and efficient upward mining.

Limitations

This study combines theory, simulation, and borehole verification; however, some limitations remain. First, field data came from only two boreholes at panel 25,214. The sample size is small and cannot fully reflect spatial variations in seam spacing, roof lithology, or structure. Second, numerical parameters were mainly lab averages. Parameter uncertainty and randomness were not fully considered, leading to idealization. Third, the study area has a relatively simple geology. Applicability to other mines with different structures and hydrogeology is limited. Finally, the study focused on the stability of overburden. Secondary hazards, such as abnormal gas emission or sudden water inrush from the goaf, were not thoroughly analyzed, although they are critical in practice.

Conclusion

This study developed a comprehensive methodological system and theoretical innovation for the feasibility assessment of upward mining in close-distance coal seams. First, the theoretical model was revised and validated. A roof failure depth prediction model considering the mining face scale effect was built based on elastic mechanics and the Mohr–Coulomb criterion. Its reliability was verified with field data.

Second, a multi-source information fusion system was established. This overcame the limitation of using only the “two-zone” height method. The approach integrated theoretical calculations and numerical simulation (3DEC) to back-analyze overburden fracture and stress evolution, and field observations of the “two zones.” A feasibility assessment framework was built with coordinated “stress–displacement–failure” indicators, improving accuracy and reliability. This methodological system has crucial theoretical value for upward mining studies in close-distance seams under similar geological conditions. The main conclusions are:

A plane strain mechanical model was established to calculate the roof failure depth under mining stress. For the 5–2 seam at Hongliulin Mine, the maximum instability height averaged 12.46 m, with a maximum of 16 m, insufficient to affect the 4–4 and 4–3 seams.

Considering the long remaining service life of Hongliulin Mine, the study suggests that in the later stage of mining, after stability of the 4–3 and 4–4 seams is confirmed in the first panel, and after detailed knowledge of their conditions affected by lower seam mining is obtained, special safety measures should be formulated to recover them. Later, recovery can proceed in the second and third panels after stabilization.

The research has significant engineering application and promotion value. It not only provides guidance for safe recovery of the 4–3 and 4–4 seams in Hongliulin Mine, but also offers a technical route and feasibility standard applicable to other Chinese mines with downward mining sequences and remaining upper seams. The results can help unlock large amounts of close-distance seam resources, reduce resource waste, and ensure safe and efficient production in coal mines.

Methods

This study focuses on the 4–3 and 4–4 coal seams in the Hongliulin coal mine, adopting a method that combines theoretical analysis, numerical simulation, and field measurement.

In theory, the roof stress field model is established based on elastic mechanics, and the formula for the maximum failure depth of the overlying rock is derived, combined with the Mohr–Coulomb criterion to calculate the roof instability height of the 5–2 coal seam.

The numerical simulation utilizes 3DEC software to construct a model that simulates the impact of 4–2 and 5–2 coal seam mining on the stress, displacement, and inclination of the 4–3 and 4–2 coal seams.

The height of the caving zone and the caving mining ratio of the 5–2 coal seam were determined through the “two zones” exploratory hole, combined with logging and hydrological observation, to verify the theory and simulation results, and comprehensively identify the feasibility of upward mining.
