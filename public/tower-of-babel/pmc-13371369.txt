Influence of clinical benefit and cost-effectiveness on reimbursement decisions for lung cancer drugs in Spain
Authors: Pelayo Nieto-Gómez, Celia Castaño-Amores, Antonio José García-Ruiz, Juan Oliva-Moreno
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn principle, reimbursement decisions for oncological drugs in Spain hinge on clinical benefit and cost-effectiveness, yet factors guiding these decisions are not fully explained in reimbursement reports. This study examines these criteria for a cohort of lung cancer drugs in Spain and estimates and describes the cost-effectiveness, clinical benefit, and reimbursement decisions for lung cancer and mesothelioma drugs authorized in Spain between 2010 and 2023.Materials and methodsClinical benefit was assessed using four scales from medical societies and Health Technology Assessment Agencies (HTAA). Cost-effectiveness was estimated using notified and reimbursed prices, and effects were obtained from HTAA reports. Reimbursement decisions were reviewed from official sources (BIFIMED). Statistical analyses and models were carried out to determine influencing factors.Results28 drugs and 55 indications were analysed. A total of 50 drugs and 165 indications were excluded from the analysis as they were not related to lung cancer. A positive association was found between clinical benefit and reimbursement decision (OR: 3.20; CI95%: 1.46–7.44; p < 0.01). The median of the estimated incremental cost-utility ratio (ICUR) was €91,568 (IQR 43,944 − 133,384) per Quality-Adjusted Life Year (QALY) gained with notified prices and €41,030/QALY (IQR 22,213 − 76,390) with reimbursed prices (p < 0.0001). Statistical models showed that substantial clinical benefit increased the probability of positive reimbursement decision by 35–40% points. Cost-effectiveness, presence of therapeutic alternatives, disease incidence or presence of uncertainty had no significant influence.ConclusionsOur study reveals a positive association between clinical benefit and reimbursement decisions for lung cancer drugs in Spain. No evidence was found that cost-effectiveness is associated with reimbursement decisions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12962-026-00771-8.

Introduction

Despite recent advances in lung cancer survival, this type of cancer still accounted for 18.7% of worldwide cancer deaths in 2022, according to GLOBOCAN figures [1]. It ranks among the cancers with the highest incidence in Spain, with a total of 31,282 estimated new cases in 2023. Additionally it is associated with one of the highest mortality rates, accounting for 22,712 deaths in 2022 (16,747 men and 5,965 women) [2], representing one of the diseases that causes the highest number of years of potential life lost and labour losses due to premature mortality in Spain [3].

The introduction of immunotherapy and targeted therapies has marked a significant milestone in the management of lung cancer, leading to rapid changes in clinical practice guidelines and medical procedures as new drugs continue to be developed. The impact of these new therapies on clinical benefit has spurred the development of assessment scales both by medical societies and by Health Technology Assessment Agencies (HTAA). The European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) and the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) have developed the ESMO-Magnitude of Clinical Benefit Scale (ESMO-MCBS) and the ASCO-Value Framework (ASCO-VF) respectively [4, 5], Similarly, the Haute Autorité Santé in France and the German Institute for Quality and Efficiency in Health Care (IQWIG) have devised their own assessment scales: Amélioration de Service Médical Rendú (ASMR) and the added benefit of IQWIG respectively [6, 7]. These scales have been instrumental in conducting research on the clinical benefit of drugs for lung cancer indications and oncology overall [8–10].

A general trend observed in Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) countries in recent years has been to strengthen their drug evaluation processes by incorporating the dimensions of cost-effectiveness and budgetary impact [11, 12]. Pricing and reimbursement decision are guided in Spain by Articles 92 and 94 of Royal Legislative Decree 1/2015 of 24 July [13]. This legislation establishes that the social value of the medicine and its incremental clinical benefit, taking into account its cost-effectiveness, is one of the criteria to be considered for its reimbursement by the Directorate General of Pharmacy (Ministry of Health) and that the Interministerial Commission on Prices of Medicines should take into consideration cost-effectiveness and budget impact analyses. However, it was only in 2020 that a proposed change in the evaluation process of medicines incorporating these economic dimensions was developed [14].

As a result, between 2021 and 2023, economic evaluation (EE) was introduced in Spain in some Therapeutic Positioning Reports (IPTs; Spanish acronym). This was considered a positive step towards clarifying how reimbursement decisions are made. However, a recent analysis of the EEs included in these IPTs concluded otherwise [15]. Significantly, only 19 (10.5%) of the IPTs published between June 2021 and July 2023 incorporated these economic assessments, and only 13 (7.2%) incorporated “de novo” EE with remarkable heterogeneity in their methodological quality. Moreover, as the authors point out, no clear relationship could be established between the results of these EE and the reimbursement decision made, mainly because the latter is influenced by several factors.

In the summer of 2023, this initiative for incorporating EE into the drug assessment suffered a setback when the Spanish courts override the 2020 Plan on formal grounds [16]. Since then, the evaluation of the medicinal product has been carried out by the Spanish Agency for Medicines and Medical Devices (AEMPS), following exclusively clinical criteria. Cost-effectiveness and budget impact analyses can be submitted by companies applying for public reimbursement, but it has not been specified how this information is evaluated.

In March 2023 an article was published aiming to examine whether there was an association between the reimbursement decisions made in Spain and the clinical benefit measured by ESMO-MCBS, among other outcomes [17]. A positive association was found, showing that indications with substantial clinical benefit were more likely to be reimbursed.

On the other hand, several studies have explored the cost-effectiveness of cancer drugs in Spain [18], as well as the criteria related to their pricing [19]. However, to our knowledge, no study has as yet related the reimbursement decision to the cost-effectiveness of lung cancer drugs in Spain. In this study, we reviewed the indications for lung cancer drugs authorised between 2010 and 2023. In the first part of the study, we describe the clinical benefit of these indications and estimate their cost-effectiveness using the incremental cost-effectiveness (ICER) and incremental cost-utility ratio (ICUR). In the following section, we explore whether there is any correlation between these ratios and the reimbursement decisions in Spain, as well as between cost-effectiveness and clinical benefit, measured by various clinical benefit scales. Additionally, we investigated other possible factors influencing the reimbursement decision such as the presence of therapeutic alternatives, uncertainty, or the incidence of the disease.

Materials and methods

As described in our previous research [17], we collected data on all indications for drugs targeting solid tumours authorised between 2010 and October 2022. Initially, we compiled a list of all the drugs classified under groups L01 (antineoplastic agents) and L02 (endocrine therapy) according to the Anatomical-Therapeutic-Chemical classification from the AEMPS Online Drug Information Center (AEMPS-CIMA) website [20]. The information collected was stored in a database and processed with Microsoft Excel®. For this study, we expanded the database to include all authorised indications up to December 2023. Following the initial data extraction, as described in the original study, we specifically selected the indications for lung cancer drugs, including those for mesothelioma. Exclusion criteria for this study were as follows: paediatric indications, indications for benign tumours, biosimilar drugs, generic drugs, and different doses of the same drug. For each indication, we obtained the reimbursement decision (dichotomised) using the Search Engine for Information on the Reimbursement Status of Medicines (BIFIMED) on the Spanish Ministry of Health website [21].

Clinical benefit assessment

Four scales were selected for the assessment of clinical benefit. Firstly, ESMO-MCBS version 1.1, along with the same scale adapted by the Austrian Institute of Health Technology Assessment (AIHTA) [4, 8]. The other two scales chosen were ASMR and the added benefit scale of IQWIG [6, 7]. Below, we describe these scales and justify their selection.

ESMO-MCBS version 1.1

This scale was chosen to assess the clinical benefit for each indication from the perspective of medical oncologists in our setting. It is a validated tool widely used in evaluations, guiding decisions in clinical practice and in positioning reports by HTAA. Importantly, ESMO-MCBS is included in IPTs as a measure of clinical benefit. The scale evaluates each indication for each drug against a comparator, usually the one from the pivotal trial. Scores can range between A, B and C for adjuvant setting, and 1 to 5 for advanced or metastatic cancers. Scores A, B, 4 and 5 indicate substantial clinical benefit, while a score of 3 is considered a moderate benefit. The score for each indication was adapted from the scorecards available on the ESMO website [22] and when not available, indications were assessed using the corresponding form.

ESMO-MCBS adapted by AIHTA

This scale assesses clinical benefit using ESMO-MCBS but addresses some of the limitations of the original scale. For instance, it employs the point estimate of the hazard ratio in overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) to establish the cut-off point, unlike ESMO-MCBS, which uses the lower limit. Moreover, it applies a stricter adjustment for toxicity and downgrades points if there are negative OS differences or no improvement in quality of life in the absence of OS improvement. The scale retains the same scoring system and interpretation as the original scale.

We chose this scale to incorporate a more demanding perception of clinical benefit, as evidenced by its use by AIHTA. The scores for this scale were adapted from the work published by Grössmann et al. [8]. In cases where scores were not available, they were obtained by applying the criteria published by these authors.

ASMR and IQWIG

The reason for including these scales is to incorporate tools currently used by the French and German HTAA, respectively, to evaluate clinical benefit, which are also employed in pricing and reimbursement decision-making.

ASMR classifies each indication into 5 categories: ASMR I (major benefit), ASMR II (important benefit), ASMR III (moderate benefit), ASMR IV (minor benefit), and ASMR V (no benefit). Meanwhile, IQWIG classifies each indication into six categories: non-quantifiable additional benefit, no additional benefit proven, less additional benefit, minor added benefit, substantial added benefit, and major added benefit. Data for these classifications were collected from reports on each indication from the respective HTAA websites [7, 23]. In cases where multiple updates were available, the latest updates were selected.

Score reclassification

To facilitate a combined analysis of added benefit, we opted to reclassify the scores using a similar approach to that of Brinkhuis et al. [24]. Table A in S1 text illustrates how the scores for each scale were reclassified.

As a result, we derived four clinical benefit scores: negative or non-quantifiable added benefit, minor added benefit, substantial added benefit, and major added benefit. This enabled us to perform a pooled analysis of the data, increase the number of observations and capture both the clinical benefit perspective of HTAA and that of medical societies.

Data on clinical benefit were obtained in October 2023 from the aforementioned sources (ESMO website, AIHTA, HAS, and IQWiG). Both the ESMO-MCBS assessments and the ESMO-MCBS adaptations by AIHTA are included in the supplementary material. Missing data from the ASMR and IQWIG scales could not be imputed. These data were treated as missing.

Considering that there might be a lag between the clinical benefit observed and the one considered in the reimbursement decision, since the data available at that time may not have been up to date, we retrieved the IPTs and assessed the clinical benefit according to the ESMO-MCBS based on those dates. Additionally, we conducted a new search to identify the ASMR and IQWiG versions closest to the reimbursement date. These data are available in S1 text.

Costs

To simplify cost estimation, we attempted to adapt the information available in the HTAA reports, except for the acquisition costs of medicines. However, in most reports, cost information was not disaggregated, so that health or social costs could not be adapted. Therefore, only acquisition costs were considered. As of December 2023, the notified prices (laboratory selling price) of the medicines were obtained from the General Council of Official Associations of Pharmacists platform BOT PLUS [25]. The discount specified by Royal Decree 8/2010 was applied [26].

The cost of treatment acquisition was calculated for both the experimental and the control arms. When multiple regimens were possible in the control arm, an average cost was considered. For generic and biosimilar drugs, the lowest cost was selected.

The treatment duration of each drug in the selected clinical trial (CT) was considered, obtained directly from the most current publication available. Following the methodological guide devised by the Group for Evaluation of Innovations, Standardization, and Research in Drug Selection – Spanish Society of Hospital Pharmacy (GENESIS-SEFH), the following criteria prioritized and considered based on availability were: number of cycles, and mean and median duration of treatment [27].

Years were taken to contain 365.25 days and months, 30.44 days. Vial optimization was considered. Anthropometric parameters obtained from the Health Atlas in Spain were used to calculate dosing schedules by weight or body surface area: weight 70.50 kg and body surface area 1.78 m2 [28]. To simplify calculations, the costs of drugs present in both arms of each CT were omitted, but only in cases where the differences in duration and total cost were insignificant.

Effects

The effects were obtained from reports published by Canada’s Drug and Health Technology Assessment (CDA-AMC) [29]. CDA-AMC was selected because it had the largest number of reports with available data. Incremental life years gained (LYG) and incremental quality-adjusted life years (QALY) were used for the calculation. Priority was given to Economic Guidance Panel data over submitter data: where these data were not available, data from the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) reports were obtained [30], and if neither were available, we searched the Tufts University Cost-effectiveness Analysis Registry database [31].

Time horizon

As the outcomes were adapted and treatment durations were extracted from the pivotal trials, no fixed time horizon was applied. For costs, the time horizon corresponded to the mean treatment duration, while for outcomes, it depended on the time horizon considered by each HTAA or author. No adjustment was made for time horizons when comparing the ICURs.

Discount

Costs were discounted from the first year of treatment. The discount rate applied in each case depended on the discount rate used in the effects. Generally, a discount rate of 1.5% was used, as this was the rate used in the CDA-AMC reports. When the NICE effects were adapted, a discount rate of 3.5% was applied. If data were obtained from another publication the corresponding discount rate was applied.

Sensitivity analysis

A univariate sensitivity analysis was conducted to test the variability of the ICERs/ICURs for the assumptions that posed the greatest uncertainty, such as the price of medicines. An analysis was performed using the reimbursed price obtained from Nomenclátor Alcántara (access only available to public employees due to price confidentiality) [32]. Sensitivity analysis was also performed without discounts.

To address the limitation of considering only acquisition costs in the ICER/ICUR calculation, we conducted an analysis with ICURs obtained from CDA-AMC or NICE reports. ICURs were converted to Purchasing Power Parity (PPP) using the OECD resources [33]. An adjustment was applied, both with and without inflation, to check for significant differences. Inflation adjustment was applied using the Consumer Price Index [34]. All prices were adjusted to January 2024.

Regarding the uncertainty in the effects, LYGs were calculated using the difference in median survival rates of each CT when available. An additional analysis was conducted considering effectiveness data only from the HTAA reports, in other words, without considering Tufts University Cost Effectiveness Analysis (CEA) registry data.

Statistical analysis

The Fisher’s test was used to assess the association between the reimbursement decision and clinical benefit. To establish the correlation between the reimbursement decision and the ICUR, as well as between the ICUR and clinical benefit, the Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (R) was calculated. All statistical tests were performed using R version 4.3.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

In addition, a statistical analysis was conducted to estimate the probability that the treatments analysed would receive public reimbursement. For this purpose, three probit models were used initially, in which the explanatory variables were substantial clinical benefit (model 1) and the ICUR (model 2: using the official or notified price; model 2bis: using the reimbursed price). These initial models were converted into multivariate models by adding other variables that are recognised in the Spanish regulations [13] and identified in the literature as potentially relevant for public reimbursement [35–41]. The variables used were the existence of a therapeutic alternative, the presence of uncertainty in the pivotal trials and the number of cases to be treated (incidence) with the new treatment. The definitions of the variables can be found in Table 1. It should be noted that the nature of the data precludes the establishment of causal links; instead, the analysis is limited to identifying statistically significant associations between the dependent and independent variables incorporated into the models.

Association between clinical benefit and reimbursement decision

S1 Figure presents descriptive data on reimbursement decision and clinical benefit. A total of 195 clinical benefit scores were obtained across the four scales. Excluding missing data (n = 25), 51.28% of the scores showed substantial or greater clinical benefit (100/195) on the reclassified scale and approximately 80% of the indications were reimbursed (41/52). The missing data corresponded to scores from the ASMR (n = 21) or IQWIG (n = 4) scales that were not available. In contrast, the unavailable scores for ESMO-MCBS and the ESMO-MCBS adapted for AIHTA were calculated as described in the Materials and Methods section. S1 Text Table B shows the scores for each scale. It should be noted that there were important differences between the 4 scales where, for example, there were no drugs classified as major clinical benefit for ASMR and only 7 with substantial added benefit.

As shown in Table 2, indications with a score of substantial or greater clinical benefit were associated with a higher probability of being reimbursed; Odds Ratio (OR) = 3.20 (CI95% 1.46–7.44; p < 0.01).

Sensitivity analysis

Figure 2 presents two charts of the univariate sensitivity analyses. The results should be interpreted in relation to those obtained in Table 3. A key factor influencing the ICUR results is the price used. The ICUR calculated with reimbursed prices varied significantly compared to the notified prices, with a median of €41,030 /QALY (IQR 22,213 − 76,390) (Wilcoxon signed rank test; p < 0.0001).

Sensitivity charts for ICUR and ICER (€/QALY)

The differences with the median ICUR obtained from HTAA reports were not substantially large when adjusted for PPP and inflation. The median HTAA ICUR adjusted for PPP was €81,256/QALY (IQR 57,129 − 117,809). Nonetheless, there was significant individual variability. When comparing each base case ICUR with the one obtained from the HTAA, we found that out of 36 cases with available data, the difference between the HTAA ICUR and the base case ICUR was less than 25% in only 8 cases (22.22%). In the remaining 28 cases, the differences were sometimes as much as 4 times (first line treatment with ceritinib) or even 6.5 greater (first line treatment with afatinib). Calculating the ICUR considering only HTAA report data did not have a large impact on the results because only 4 ICURs were obtained from sources other than the HTAA reports. Applying the discount rate did not change the ICUR results because it was applied to very few indications due to treatment durations often being less than one year.

The ICER was also affected by the calculation of reimbursed prices, showing a similar direction as the ICUR. There were significant differences in the ICER with the calculation of LYGs using median OS differences; however, these results were inconclusive given the low number of indications with available data (n = 25). The same minimal effect was seen in the ICER analysis considering only HTAA reports and when no discount was applied.

Correlation between ICUR, reimbursement decision and clinical benefit

Figure 3 shows the ICUR boxplots for the base case with respect to the reimbursement decision. We found no correlation between ICUR and the reimbursement decision (r = 0.07; p = 0.64). Additionally, there was no correlation between clinical benefit and base case ICUR (r = 0.06; p = 0.47). Since the sensitivity analysis showed significant differences in the ICUR calculated with reimbursed prices, these were also included in. The corresponding boxplots can be seen in Fig. 4. However, there was still no statistically significant correlation between ICUR and reimbursement decision when reimbursed prices were used to estimate incremental ratios (r = 0.11; p = 0.46) or clinical benefit (r = 0.06; p = 0.41).

Discussion

In this study, we have analysed the relationship between cost-effectiveness and clinical benefit and the reimbursement decision of drugs indicated for lung cancer in Spain in the last decade. As in the previous study [17], we found a positive association between clinical benefit and reimbursement decision for overall cancer drugs (OR = 3.20; CI95%:1.46–7.44; p < 0.01). However, almost half of the indications had no substantial clinical benefit. Garcia-Fumero et al. [9] found similar results, with 54.8% of indications having substantial clinical benefit in their review of non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) schemes. In their analysis, the authors considered only the ESMO-MCBS scale. In our case, we collected data from four scales to better represent the added clinical benefit as measured by both ESMO and HTAA. This approach aimed to overcome the limitations of the ESMO-MCBS scale (see introduction, ESMO-MCBS adapted by AIHTA) and to provide better information on the added clinical benefit against comparators used by HTAA rather than just the CT comparator. Although we had some missing data, we do not believe they pose a risk of bias, as they represent only a small portion of the observations. In fact, in the IPTs, the clinical benefit most commonly considered is the ESMO-MCBS, for which we had complete data available.

This information is valuable as it gives us an insight into the clinical benefit of reimbursed and non-reimbursed lung cancer indications. Necessarily this association does not imply causality; it does provide an insight to the clinical benefit of reimbursed and non-reimbursed indications. Our results can show to clinicians, patients, and healthcare providers that the cancer drugs reimbursed for lung cancer treatments in Spain are those with the best clinical outcomes in pivotal trials. Moreover, clinical benefit is a criterion to be considered by articles 92 & 94 of RD 1/2015 when deciding on the reimbursement of a medicine [13]. These articles govern the selective reimbursement of medicines based on a series of criteria: (a) severity of the condition; (b) the specific needs of certain subgroups; (c) the social and therapeutic value of the medicines and their incremental clinical benefit, also considering cost-effectiveness; (d) the rationalization of public spending on medicines and the budget impact from the perspective of the Spanish National Health Service (SNHS); (e) the availability of alternative therapeutic options for the condition at the same or lower price than the medicine under consideration; and (f) innovation.

It is important, to note that although clinical benefit appears to be the main factor guiding reimbursement decisions, 69.5% of the indications without relevant clinical benefit are still reimbursed. The prognosis for lung cancer is very poor. According to data from the GLOBOCAN in 2022, it was the cancer with the highest mortality rate, accounting for 18% of all cancer-related deaths, and it is usually diagnosed at an advanced or metastatic stage [1]. All of this may explain why the authorities may be more inclined to reimburse drugs with limited clinical benefit, even if the benefit is small.

On the other hand, contrary to the case of other researchers into cancer drugs [48–51], we did not detect a statistically significant association between reimbursement decision and cost-effectiveness results. Considering the reimbursed prices, almost two thirds of the indications are cost-effective at a WTP of €60,000/QALY. This threshold, although unofficial, has been proposed as an upper threshold for an efficient intervention in Spain, applicable only under special circumstances according to Sacristán et al. [47]. It is striking that of the indications considered not cost-effective at this threshold (one third), 14 out of 16 were reimbursed. Obviously, the cost-effectiveness criterion cannot be the single factor determining price and reimbursement decisions. A priori, a statistically significant association between cost-effectiveness and reimbursement decisions was expected, given that Spanish drug legislation identifies this as a key criterion for decision-making. Nontheless, caution is needed when interpreting these results. Further research, including full-cost models and larger samples is required for obtaining a more robust conclusion.

Although Spain is the fifth largest European market in terms of drug sales volume (and among the top 10 in the world), it still lacks an adequate regulatory and organisational structure that integrates the efficiency dimension into drug evaluation processes, providing useful information at the reimbursement and pricing decision stage. It is therefore essential for the SNHS to ensure that access to new drugs is compatible with the optimal allocation of available resources. Moreover, considering the societal significance of access to these drugs, accountability and transparency in the decision-making process should be mandatory, particularly for diseases associated with high mortality and disability.

At the time of writing, a major regulatory reform is being planned to adapt the evaluation structures to the needs required by the European reform on health technology assessment [52]. In this regard, the Ministry of Health has recently published an EE guideline commissioned by the Advisory Committee on Pharmaceutical Provision of the SNHS [53] and has pointed out in a press release that EEs of medicines submitted to the reimbursement and pricing process should adhere to the recommendations of these guidelines [54]. Coupled with the regulatory change, the creation of new evaluation structures, their coordination with the Spanish HTAA and the integration of the evaluation process into the decision-making process are important challenges that Spain must tackle without delay [55].

The relationship between the cost of cancer treatments and clinical benefit has been studied by several research teams across multiple countries, consistently finding no statistically significant correlation between them [10, 56, 57]. We aimed to investigate whether there was a correlation between the cost-effectiveness of the drugs indicated for lung cancer and the clinical benefit they provide. It might be assumed that drugs with greater clinical benefit would result in higher ICUR due to their higher prices. However, in our study, we found no statistically significant correlation, either for notified or for those reimbursed prices, although there was noted a trend towards higher ICUR for indications with substantial and major clinical for reimbursed prices.

Recent research has found a similar trend in laboratory profits [24]. In this research, drugs with substantial or major clinical benefit produced around $500 million greater profits than those with no added clinical benefit. It is possible that, for indications with high added clinical benefit, there is a tendency to accept higher prices and ICURs when negotiating reimbursement, since, as we have seen, added clinical benefit seems to be a relevant factor in the reimbursement decision.

In fact, ASMR is a scoring system of added clinical benefit used to establish the price of medicines and facilitate faster access, among other purposes. However, we did not find statistically significant results in our analysis, so we cannot draw such conclusions from this work. A future line of analysis would be to extend the present study, by considering all the new indications and new cancer drugs approved in the last decade.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several limitations. The primary limitation is the calculation of the ICUR and ICER, which were based solely on the acquisition cost of the drug, without including a substantial number of other costly resources. We acknowledge that this is a significant limitation which is difficult to address and that it may lead to imperfect estimates. However, these estimates, although imperfect, do not invalidate the results in the specific case of lung cancer treatments.

The underlying rationale is that, after performing a review of HTAA reports and the references from the Tufts CEA Registry, we found that the mean acquisition cost as a percentage of the total cost for experimental therapy was 74.82% (SD 22.55%), illustrating the significant impact acquisition costs have on total costs in lung cancer indications (see S1 Text Table G). In a more extensive analysis (see S1 Text Table H), in which we reviewed 220 indications authorized by the European Commission between 2010 and 2023 for oncology drugs, the proportion of drug-related costs relative to total costs, available for 47 indications, was around 66% (SD 19.5%). Moreover, when excluding adjuvant indications (where other types of costs such as surgery, radiotherapy, etc., may have a greater influence), this percentage rose to 69% (SD 18.3%). In the sample of lung cancer indications, 10 out of 11 had a percentage above 68%, with the only exception being neoadjuvant nivolumab for NSCLC with PD-L1 ≥ 1%, which had a percentage of 12%. This highlights the significant role of drug-related costs in cancer treatment and suggests that we can rely on our estimates, even if they are not ideal. Additionally, in the univariate sensitivity analysis, we found no major differences with the median ratio obtained from HTAA once adjusted for PPP.

Another aspect we would like to emphasize is that Spain has lacked a solid framework for EE. The Ministry of Health did not have its own guide for the EE of medicines until the year 2023. Until the development of this guideline, one of the reference guidelines mentioned in previous regulations was the guide from the GENESIS-SEFH group. This guide states that when a precise EE cannot be carried out, as is our case, the proportion of the overall cost attributable to the drug compared to other components must be considered. In the same context, it should be noted that a review of the IPT carried out in Spain that included an EE concluded that in many of these evaluations, the only type of cost included was that of the medicine being evaluated [15]. Although this does not imply the methodological correctness of our simulation of EEs, it does show that this practice is common and accepted in the evaluations of medicines presented to the Spanish decision makers on pricing and reimbursement.

Another limitation is the inconsistent application of the discount rate, which varied depending on the discount rate applied to the QALYs. However, we found that the discount rate did not significantly affect the results. In addition, the discount rate was applied to very few indications due to the short treatment durations caused, unfortunately, by the rapid progression of the disease and the limited life expectancy.

It is also important to note that the calculations were based on several assumptions. For the calculation of the ratios, mean or median values were used for factors such as weight, height, body surface area, and treatment duration. Possible dose reductions were not considered, and costs were calculated using standard doses. These factors could potentially influence the ratios. Moreover, for the sensitivity analysis, the difference in OS medians was calculated as defined by the clinical benefit in the ESMO-MCBS. However, this method may not fully capture the benefits of the drug, especially in long-term survivors, such as those treated with immunotherapy. Our aim in this study was to provide an estimate of clinical benefit and cost-effectiveness, and explore their potential association with reimbursement decisions and clinical outcomes, which is why we chose this approach.

Regarding the use of effect estimates from different sources, this could also introduce potential bias. However, in our analysis, most of the effects were obtained from CDA-AMC, and we only used data for from the Tufts CEA Registry or NICE reports for 6 indications. In the sensitivity analysis, there were no significant differences in the ICUR when excluding the results from the Tufts CEA Registry.

Given the cross-sectional nature of the study, the analysis was based on a single consultation of the reimbursed prices. Although we consulted prices as of December 2023, it is important to note that the reimbursed price may have changed as new indications were reimbursed. However, this only applies to reimbursed prices, as the notified price remained constant. In our analysis, neither the model using notified prices nor the one using reimbursed prices was statistically significant. Therefore, potential price reductions in the reimbursed price are not expected to have a significant impact on the results. Additionally, for single-indication drugs that were not reimbursed, we lacked price data, which could potentially introduce bias in interpreting the results. It is worth noting that, due to the use of confidential reimbursed price data, Models 2 bis and 4 bis can only be replicated by individuals with authorized access to such information, typically public sector researchers with credentials to consult the Nomenclator Alcántara.

Budget impact is another important factor in reimbursement decisions. However, in our study, we were unable to estimate it given that we did not have reliable and publicly available data on the number of patients accessing the different drugs and indications. In Spain, this information is neither available nor easy to estimate. We included the variable incidence in the models as an estimator of budget impact, although we acknowledge it is an imperfect proxy.

Finally, another limitation of this analysis is the small sample size and the lack of certain data. Since probit models require enough observations to accurately estimate marginal effects, using only a limited number of cases may increase the variance of the estimates, reduce the statistical power of the model, and limit the generalizability of the results. Furthermore, there may be factors not considered in the analysis that could influence the results (such as additional confidential discounts). Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution and considered indicative rather than conclusive, both for positive results and for null findings. Future research with larger samples would help validate and strengthen the results presented here.

On the other hand, this article provides evidence on how reimbursement decisions are being made in our country. In a previous article [17], we explored the influence of clinical benefit on these decisions, but in this one, we have included variables such as cost-utility, the presence of therapeutic alternatives, and uncertainty in clinical benefit. Although we obtained non-significant results, except for clinical benefit, we believe the findings are relevant for increasing transparency in the process of making reimbursement decisions in Spain. A recent article published by Pinilla-Dominguez et al. in the European Journal of Health Economics concludes that, in Spain, “the criteria used to justify the reimbursement decisions of medicines do not align with the information included in the HTAA reports, and that this discrepancy highlights the need for the ongoing HTAA reports reform to develop an appraisal framework that aligns with the HTAA assessment in a transparent, rigorous, and inclusive manner” [58]. The authors found an association between positive reimbursement decisions and the Criterion C social and therapeutic value of the medicines and incremental clinical benefit, also considering their cost-effectiveness. However, it is not possible to determine from this association whether the determining factor is clinical benefit, cost-effectiveness, or both. The authors state that cost-effectiveness analyses have rarely been included in the HTAA reports and that EE guidelines have not yet been implemented in Spain. Our results are consistent with theirs and evidence the lack of methodological reference guidelines for EE during the period 2010–2023.

Conclusion

In this study, we observed a statistically significant positive association between substantial or major clinical benefit and reimbursement decisions for lung cancer indications. No evidence was found that cost-effectiveness is associated with reimbursement decisions. This lack of correlation was consistent across both reimbursed and notified prices. Other factor as incidence, the presence of therapeutic alternatives or the presence of uncertainty neither did they show significant effects on reimbursement decisions.

Given the aforementioned limitations inherent to the nature of the data analyzed, the results must be interpreted with caution and should not be extrapolated to other therapeutic areas or contexts differing from those of the present analysis.
