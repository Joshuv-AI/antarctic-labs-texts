Pre-use acceptability of telemedicine among nursing students: a quantitative cross-sectional study
Authors: Simona Hvalič-Touzery, Angela Kydd, Nejc Berzelak, Jerneja Laznik, Mojca Šetinc, Vesna Dolničar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe healthcare sector has undergone digital transformation through telemedicine which serves as a strategic and efficient solution to address key health challenges. Nevertheless, little is known about how nursing students perceive and accept telemedicine, which is essential for its future implementation. This study examined how perceived ease of use, extended constructs of perceived usefulness, self-rated knowledge, and technology anxiety influence Slovenian nursing students’ willingness to use telemedicine in the pre-use acceptability stage.MethodsA cross-sectional, non-experimental, quantitative research design was used. Data were collected in 2023 in Slovenia using a self-administered web questionnaire. All eight Slovenian higher education institutions offering full-time and/or part-time undergraduate nursing programs were included. A total of 234 nursing students participated in the survey, with 178 providing complete responses relevant to the study’s key variables. Bivariate correlations and nested proportional odds models (ordered logit) were performed.ResultsSelf-rated knowledge (Coef = 0.49, p = 0.03), perceived usefulness in nursing practice (Coef = 1.33, p < 0.01) and technology anxiety (Coef = − 0.70, p < 0.01) were significant predictors of willingness to use telemedicine. Perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness for patient empowerment and interaction were not significant. The extended model significantly improved fit and explanatory power compared to the simpler model containing only self-rated knowledge and background variables (ΔLR = 55.62, p < 0.01).ConclusionsThis study integrates constructs from different theoretical models of technology acceptance to explain nursing students’ pre-use acceptability of telemedicine. Knowledge, perceived usefulness in nursing practice, and technology anxiety significantly predict willingness to use telemedicine. These findings highlight the importance of addressing both competence and emotional readiness when preparing students for digitally supported nursing care. It further underscores that pre-use acceptance is shaped by an interplay of cognitive evaluation and affective response.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09148-0.Keywords: Technology acceptability, Telemedicine, Technology acceptance model, Nursing education, Digital health, Healthcare professionals, Social care professionals, Technology anxiety, Slovenia

Background

In the context of rapid digital transformation and increasing demands on healthcare systems, the integration of digital technologies into healthcare practice and education has become both a necessity and therefore a strategic priority worldwide. These technologies are being employed to address major demographic and systemic challenges, including the rising prevalence of non-communicable diseases and the persistent shortage of healthcare professionals [1]. Within the spectrum of digital technologies, telemedicine – characterized by the remote transmission of physiological data between a patient at home and healthcare professionals [2], stands out as having the most significant long-term potential for positively transforming healthcare [3]. To ensure that healthcare graduates are adequately prepared to keep pace with innovation and digital transformation, educational programs must equip students with the necessary skills and competencies to use digital technologies safely and effectively [4–6]. Numerous studies confirm the crucial role of education and educational institutions in the development of key competences required for professional use and adoption of digital technologies [7]. Today’s nursing students are the future healthcare professionals who will shape the healthcare landscape, and their education in digital technologies is a forward-looking investment in the sustainability and quality of healthcare [3, 5].

At a political level, there is broad consensus in the European Union (EU) and internationally on the urgent need to strengthen digital skills in the healthcare sector. At the policy level, digital competence development in healthcare has become a strategic priority across Europe and globally. The Digital Decade Policy Programme [8] frames the strengthening of digital skills among current and future healthcare professionals as essential for the sustainable transformation of health systems. In parallel, international professional bodies emphasize the integration of digital technologies, artificial intelligence, and evidence-based innovations into nursing education to ensure that training remains aligned with evolving models of care.

While strong political support exists for digital integration in nursing, less is known about how nursing students themselves perceive and respond to these technological advances. Successful adoption of new technologies depends not only on knowledge and education, but also on perceptions and willingness to use these innovations [9]. In this context, assessing the attitudes and perceptions of nursing students is crucial for the effective and sustainable integration of digital technologies into clinical practice [10]. Nevertheless, existing research on nursing students’ acceptance of digital health technologies – covering both pre-use expectations and post-use experiences – remains limited [3, 10, 11]. In this already under-researched area, studies looking specifically at the pre-use acceptability and post-use acceptance of telemedicine are extremely limited [12]. Our search identified only three relevant studies, conducted in Korea [10], India [9] and Saudi Arabia [3]. These studies have generally shown that nursing students hold favorable views towards telemedicine but demonstrate low levels of knowledge and awareness about it. Furthermore, Mun et al. [10] found that nursing students’ attitudes towards telenursing – a specific subset of telemedicine – are influenced by factors such as perceived usefulness, social influence, self-efficacy, innovativeness, past experiences with telenursing, observations during clinical placements and exposure to telenursing education.

In the absence of studies focusing specifically on nursing students, the results of studies involving nurses can provide valuable insights. A meta-analysis by King and He [13] found significant overlap between students’ and professionals’ perceptions of technology, suggesting that research examining nurses’ attitudes and perceptions may contribute to our understanding of how future nurses engage with new technologies. Focusing more narrowly on telemedicine as a specific subset of digital technologies within nursing practice, a recent scoping review [12] showed that its pre-use acceptability among nurses – defined as users’ willingness to use a technology before any direct experience [14] – is shaped primarily by personal attitudes, individual needs, outcome expectations and ethical considerations. In contrast, acceptance, which refers to perceptions based on actual experience [14], is influenced by a broader and more complex set of factors, including perceived usefulness and ease of use, organizational factors, experience, patient-related issues and the availability of training and technical support. Similarly, Bagot et al. [15] found that the predictors of telemedicine adoption change over time. In the pre-use phase, perceived usefulness was found to be the only significant predictor of nurses’ willingness to use telemedicine, while in the post-use phase, both perceived usefulness and facilitating conditions (such as education or training to build confidence) were identified as significant predictors [12, 15].

To address gaps in the literature, this study builds on the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) [16], which emphasize perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness as key determinants of technology acceptance, and extends it to better capture nursing students’ attitudes toward telemedicine. To address both cognitive and affective dimensions of attitudes [17, 18], we incorporated technology anxiety, an increasingly recognized factor in healthcare contexts [19–21], alongside domain-specific constructs of perceived usefulness for nursing care delivery and patient involvement [22]. Finally, the model incorporates knowledge, a factor consistently shown to positively influence willingness to use digital health technology [3, 23, 24]. Guided by the Technology Acceptance Lifecycle [14], our study focused on the pre-use acceptability stage, examining nursing students’ willingness to use telemedicine before direct experience with it.

Given these research gaps and theoretical considerations, this study examines how perceived ease of use, extended perceived usefulness, self-rated knowledge, and technology anxiety predict Slovenian nursing students’ pre-use willingness to use telemedicine.

Methods

Study design and data collection

In this study, we employed a cross-sectional, non-experimental, quantitative research design. Data was collected using a survey questionnaire distributed online via the web survey software tool 1KA between September and December 2023 [see Supplementary Material 1]. The questionnaire (see Instrument and measures section) was developed by the research team based on established theoretical and empirical models [16, 22, 25, 26]. It was pre-tested following the TRAPD approach (Translation, Review, Adjudication, Pretesting, Documentation) [27], ensuring accurate translation, cultural relevance and clarity. Expert reviews and cognitive testing with students were conducted to address any potential issues before the final version was distributed.

Students eligible for this study had (1) completed at least the first year of an undergraduate nursing study programme, or (2) completed the undergraduate programme and were currently enrolled in a relevant nursing postgraduate programme. Representatives from all eight Slovenian Higher Education Institutions offering full-time and/or part-time undergraduate nursing programmes were requested to invite all eligible students to participate in the survey. The included institutions are aligned with the Bologna Process and comply with the requirements of the Directive 2013/55/EU [28] and the European Qualifications Framework [29]. Undergraduate nursing education in Slovenia corresponds to EQF level 6, while master’s level corresponds to EQF level 7.

Participants

A total of 2,102 nursing students were invited to participate in the survey and 234 (11%) agreed to participate. In the analysis for this study, we included 178 students who completed all questions relevant to this study. The complete dataset and codebook were included in the repository PsychArchives [30].

Instrument and measures

The survey was based on a self-administered questionnaire with up to 78 questions, depending on the participants’ answers, as questions that did not apply were automatically skipped. It included a 12-item screening section and five thematic sections: general technology use, use in nursing and social care, experience with wellbeing technologies, telemedicine and demographic data. For the purposes of the present analysis, we focused on two thematic sections. Telemedicine represented the central topic of this study, and all latent constructs described below were developed and analyzed within this framework. Selected variables from the socio-demographic theme were included to complement the analysis.

To provide a comprehensive assessment of attitudes, we analyzed the cognitive and affective components separately. In TAM-based research and its extensions, perceived usefulness (PU) and perceived ease of use (PEOU) [16] are among the most frequently studied cognitive constructs. However, in the present study we replaced the traditional PU construct with two domain-specific extensions based on the conceptual framework proposed by Holden et al. [22]: perceived usefulness for nursing care delivery and perceived usefulness for patient involvement. This adaptation was guided by a theoretical review of Holden et al.’s model and a contextual assessment of telemedicine within nursing education. The items were refined to capture both clinical and organizational dimensions relevant to students’ anticipated future professional roles. These adaptations are also supported by recent empirical findings suggesting that domain-specific dimensions of perceived usefulness may offer greater explanatory power than the original, singular PU construct [31].

We also considered established extensions of TAM, such as TAM2 [32]. TAM2 introduces additional constructs (e.g., job relevance, output quality, and result demonstrability) that require users to evaluate how a specific implemented system supports defined work tasks. In the present study, participants were asked to anticipate their future use of telemedicine without direct experience of an implemented system. Given this anticipatory and educational context, a domain-specific operationalization of perceived usefulness was considered more appropriate to better align the model with the pre-use focus of this study.

Building on perceived usefulness for nursing care delivery, we developed a broader construct entitled perceived usefulness in nursing practice (PU-NP) to better reflect the content of our items, which address clinical utilization, organizational relevance, professional responsibility and systemic integration of telemedicine in nursing care. Similarly, perceived usefulness for patient involvement was expanded into perceived usefulness for patient empowerment and interaction (PU-PEI), emphasizing patient–healthcare professional interaction and the patient’s active role in self-management, perceived safety and health awareness in the telemedicine context. Both constructs were measured on a 5-point agreement scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). Internal consistency was good for both scales (α = 0.83 for PU-NP and α = 0.82 for PU-PEI).

Perceived ease of use (PEOU) was measured using two items assessing perceived ease of learning and using telemedicine equipment (5-point agreement scale; ρ* = 0.82).

The affective component, technology anxiety (TA), was measured using two items reflecting fear and apprehension related to the use of telemedicine, following the framework proposed by Venkatesh et al. [26] (5-point agreement scale; ρ* = 0.84). Students’ self-rated knowledge (KNOW) of telemedicine was was assessed with two items adapted from Dolničar et al. [25] (5-point agreement scale; ρ* = 0.81).

Pre-use acceptability was operationalized using a single-item measure of willingness to use (WU) telemedicine: “How likely is it that you personally would like to use telemedicine in your future work?”, rated on a five-point scale ( 1 = definitely not to 5 = definitely yes). This question was adapted from the TAM behavioral intention item [16]. Although TAM typically uses agreement-based scales, response options were adapted to capture more nuanced expressions of students’ willingness to use telemedicine in their future practice.

In addition to the latent constructs, three background variables were included: type of degree programme (full-time or part-time), regular employment, and gender. Age was excluded due to low variability and lack of any relevant relation to the outcome variable in preliminary analysis.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the National Medical Ethics Committee of the Republic of Slovenia (0120–307/2023/3) and conducted in accordance with national and international ethical standards, including relevant declarations, conventions, and the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). Data were collected with informed consent and with full respect for participant’s anonymity.

Data analysis

To study relationships between the specified factors and the willingness to use telemedicine among nursing students, we examined bivariate correlations and performed regression analysis using proportional odds modelling (ordered logit). We employed nested modelling approach with initial inclusion of background variables and then the subsequent addition of other factors to evaluate their contribution. Comparisons between the models were done using the likelihood ratio tests, Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). All analyses were performed using Stata 16.1 statistical software.

The first model contains only background variables and self-rated knowledge, while the second model adds measures of technology anxiety, perceived usefulness in nursing practice and perceived usefulness for patient empowerment and interaction.

Self-rated knowledge is the only significant predictor of willingness to use in the first model. The association is positive, supporting the importance of knowledge in technology acceptance. None of the other background variables – full-time study, regular employment or gender – are significantly associated with the outcome variables.

All predictors from the initial model remain consistent, including the self-rated knowledge that maintains significant and positive relationship with willingness to use telemedicine. Discussion

The study examined nursing students’ pre-use acceptability of telemedicine. The findings indicate that perceived usefulness in nursing practice, technology anxiety, and self-rated knowledge significantly predict willingness to use telemedicine, underscoring the role of both cognitive and affective factors. In contrast, demographic variables and perceived ease of use demonstrated limited explanatory power.

Knowledge remained a significant factor in the extended model, further confirming its consistent influence on nursing students’ willingness to use telemedicine. These findings align with previous research demonstrating a positive association between higher levels of knowledge and the adoption of digital health technologies, including telemedicine, with limited knowledge or exposure associated with greater hesitancy toward use [3, 23, 24]. The present study did not assess the potential impact of clinical exposure to telemedicine, as national reports indicate low uptake and limited integration of telemedicine into the Slovenian healthcare system [33], likely resulting in minimal exposure of nursing students to telemedicine during clinical placements.

Another significant positive predictor of pre-use acceptability among nursing students was the perceived usefulness of telemedicine in nursing practice. The findings of the present study are consistent with previous studies conducted among registered nurses [22, 31]. A recent Slovenian study [31] found that both perceived usefulness for nursing care delivery and perceived usefulness for patient empowerment and interaction were significant positive predictors of nurses’ willingness to use telemedicine. In Laznik et al.’s study [31], perceived usefulness for patient empowerment and interaction demonstrated stronger and more consistent effects, suggesting that nurses’ perceptions of patients’ active participation in care may be more influential than perceptions related to care delivery alone.

In contrast to perceived usefulness in nursing practice, perceived usefulness for patient empowerment and interaction was not a significant predictor of pre-use acceptability in our model. Evidence from studies among nurses suggests that this association may depend on prior telemedicine experience. The same Slovenian study found that perceived usefulness for patient empowerment and interaction significantly predicted willingness to use telemedicine only among nurses without prior experience, whereas this association was absent among current and former users [31]. Similarly, Holden et al. [22], whose construct centered on patient-family involvement, reported no association between perceived usefulness for patient empowerment and interaction, and intention to use digital technologies among nurses with prior experience of digital technologies. Although our measures focused on patient–provider interaction and patient self-management, these findings, taken together, suggest that perceived usefulness for patients may be more influential in early stages of technology adoption-particularly among nurses unfamiliar with digital tools. While nursing students and registered nurses may share some general attitudes toward technology [13], students’ limited clinical exposure likely hinders their ability to fully conceptualize the patient-related benefits of telemedicine. This distinction highlights the importance of real-world experience in shaping not only confidence in using technology but also perceptions of technology. The lack of experience and limited knowledge of telemedicine may also negatively influence nursing students’ perceived ease of use. As emphasized by Felber et al. [35], direct experience is crucial for the accurate assessment of ease of use.

In addition to cognitive factors, the affective component – technology anxiety – also contributed to willingness to use telemedicine. Our findings indicate that technology anxiety negatively influences nursing students’ willingness to use telemedicine. We also found that self-rated knowledge is negatively correlated with technology anxiety. Previous research supports this relationship, showing that higher levels of knowledge and perceived competence in using digital technologies can reduce technology anxiety [5, 34] and increase users’ confidence in engaging with digital technologies, thereby enhancing their willingness to use such tools [34]. A study among Swedish nursing students further indicates that technology anxiety tends to decrease as students’ progress through their education. At the same time, the study found that mature students (aged 26–30) reported higher levels of technology anxiety and lower enthusiasm toward technology use, suggesting that chronological age and progression through the programme may influence technology perceptions differently. Gender differences were also observed, with women reporting higher levels of technology anxiety than men [5].

Implications

Understanding the factors that influence nursing students’ pre-use acceptability of telemedicine has important implications for nursing education, curriculum development, and digital health. Although telemedicine is included in the national strategy eHealth for a Healthier Society 2022–2027 to ensure broad accessibility by 2027, its integration into the Slovenian healthcare system remains limited [36]. Consequently, nursing students have little contact with telemedicine in clinical practice. Our findings show that students’ willingness to use telemedicine is influenced by their attitudes and knowledge. Previous research also confirms that attitudes are closely linked to telemedicine knowledge and exposure during clinical practice [9, 10]. The knowledge base of telemedicine is not consistently integrated into nursing curricula, which may warrant greater attention. These insights are particularly relevant in the context of broader efforts to modernize nursing education and practice, where political ambitions are high, but implementation often fails to keep up because of a lack of infrastructure. Beyond nursing, similar challenges exist throughout healthcare professional education, suggesting that our recommendations may also inform the integration of telemedicine into broader health curricula. In response, we propose several measures.

First, nursing curricula could be systematically updated to include digital technologies such as telemedicine, not only in terms of technical skills and knowledge, but also through pedagogical approaches that reduce technology anxiety and challenge students’ negative perceptions. The 52 competencies identified in a four-round Delphi study on telehealth [37] include communication skills, coaching skills, the ability to combine clinical experience with telehealth, ethical awareness, and the ability to support patients who are uncomfortable with technology. These competencies would provide a valuable foundation for such education. Although developed for telehealth, these competencies are also relevant to telemedicine and can inform the design of effective telemedicine education and training. This content should not only be embedded in elective modules, but as an integral part of core nursing education, woven into the curriculum. However, as found in international studies [38, 39] and the only Slovenian study of this kind [40], the content on digital technologies in nursing education is still fragmented, with telemedicine often treated as optional or supplementary rather than essential. Its meaningful inclusion may help prepare nursing students and healthcare professionals for evolving professional demands and it supports the wider integration of telemedicine into healthcare systems [3]. For this to happen, lecturers with expertise in digital health are needed within nurse, social work, professions allied to medicine and medical education.

Second, educational institutions and clinical environments should collaborate more closely to address the challenge of integrating telemedicine into clinical placements in a meaningful way. This means not only providing exposure to telemedicine but ensuring that students understand their practical relevance in real-world healthcare. Such exposure has been shown to significantly improve understanding of the benefits and role of digital technologies, increase digital self-efficacy and reduce technology anxiety [5]. Where telemedicine is not yet used in clinical settings, experiential learning methods, such as simulation-based education, can serve as an effective alternative [10] and can also be used to train educators. Such education should also be made available, not only as part of Continuous Professional Development (CPD) for all trained staff but also as part of educational updates for nurse lecturers and other health and social care educators.

Third, educators and clinical mentors need support to strengthen their own digital competences and develop an educational sensitivity to students’ emotional and cognitive responses to technology. As the International Council of Nurses [41] and European Federation of Educators in Nursing Science [42] emphasize, nurse educators should actively engage with digital innovations and act as confident, reflective role models. Their confidence, empathy and engagement can have a strong influence on students’ perceptions and willingness to adopt digital tools. Similar principles apply to other healthcare educators and clinical mentors, whose role modeling is equally important for shaping professional attitudes toward digital health.

Finally, these implications are in line with European and global policies calling for a transformation of nursing education and other health and social care professionals’ education in response to digitalization. The World Health Organization [43] highlights the importance of digital health competencies as part of resilient health systems. In line with earlier recommendations by Van Houwelingen et al. [37], strengthening digital competencies across undergraduate, postgraduate, and continuing professional learning warrants continued attention in nursing education.

Beyond telemedicine, the broader digital transformation of healthcare includes the increasing integration of artificial intelligence (AI) into clinical decision-making and care delivery. The cognitive and affective mechanisms identified in this study are likely to be relevant beyond telemedicine. As our findings show, knowledge and technology-related anxiety significantly influence students’ willingness to engage with digital tools. Educational strategies that enhance digital competence while addressing emotional readiness may therefore also support future acceptance of AI-driven healthcare technologies.

Strengths, limitations and future research directions

To our knowledge, this is the first study examining factors influencing nursing students’ pre-use acceptability of telemedicine in Slovenia and worldwide. Previous research has predominantly focused on trained healthcare professionals and other digital health solutions, particularly eHealth. By including new components of perceived usefulness, this study offers a context-specific contribution to understanding nursing students’ cognitive attitudes. However, our study is not without limitations. First, the focus was on the influence of cognitive and affective attitudes on nursing students’ pre-use acceptability of telemedicine, so other potentially relevant constructs were not considered. Given the cross-sectional design of the study, causal relationships between variables cannot be inferred. Future research incorporating a broader range of variables would therefore be necessary to provide an even more comprehensive explanation of students’ pre-use acceptability of telemedicine. Second, the response rate was relatively low (11%), which raises the possibility of non-response bias. Students who chose to participate may differ from non-respondents in their interest in digital technologies or attitudes toward telemedicine. As the direction of this potential bias cannot be determined, the findings may not fully represent the broader population of nursing students. This limits the generalizability of the results and warrants cautious interpretation. Providing on-line and paper surveys in future studies might improve the reach of the target population and improve the representativeness of the sample. Third, willingness to use telemedicine was assessed with a single item. While this provided a useful initial indication, it may not fully capture the complexity of the construct and does not allow for the assessment of internal consistency. Future studies should consider the use of multi-item measures to enhance reliability and provide a more comprehensive assessment of willingness.

Conclusions

In this study, constructs from different theoretical models were integrated to offer a more nuanced explanation of nursing students’ pre-use acceptability of telemedicine. Specifically, cognitive factors from the TAM (perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness as an extended domain-specific construct), technology anxiety (an affective attitude, drawn from TAM3 and conceptually supported by the TPB), and the additional factor of existing knowledge were combined. Our framework brings together elements from multiple theoretical perspectives and captures the interplay between cognitive evaluations and affective responses.

Our findings provide evidence that knowledge, perceived usefulness for nursing care delivery, and technology anxiety significantly predict nursing students’ willingness to use telemedicine in future practice. These results indicate that pre-use acceptability is shaped not only by cognitive assessments of usefulness but also by emotional preparedness and confidence based on knowledge. While policy frameworks strongly support digital integration in healthcare, students' limited exposure in both nursing education and clinical practice may be associated with higher levels of technology anxiety that influence willingness. Willingness to use telemedicine is a construct shaped by perceptions, confidence, and educational and practical experience. To foster willingness to use telemedicine, nursing education could benefit from moving beyond technical instruction to include targeted pedagogical strategies, such as expert patient sessions where patients share their experiences with telemedicine, supportive and knowledgeable staff, experiential learning environments, and meaningful digital engagement. Future research should further explore how educational and clinical exposure influence attitudes toward telemedicine and willingness to use it.
