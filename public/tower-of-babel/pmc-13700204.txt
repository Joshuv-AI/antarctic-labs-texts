Novel surgical approach to left ventricular diastolic dysfunction: an acute volume overload rat model
Authors: YangBo Yan, Jia Hu, Changping Gan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLeft ventricular diastolic dysfunction frequently complicates cardiac surgery, primarily driven by perioperative acute volume overload (AVO). This study aimed to establish a novel surgical rat model that recapitulates AVO-mediated left ventricular diastolic dysfunction for translational research.MethodsTwenty male Sprague Dawley rats (6–8 weeks old) were equally and randomly divided into AVO group (intraventricular saline infusion at 3 ml/h) and Sham group (no fluid infusion). A 22-gauge cannula was inserted into left ventricle chamber to record pressure and inject fluid after left thoracotomy. Hemodynamic and echocardiographic statistics were obtained at baseline, 10 min, 20 min, 30 min and 40 min. Two-way repeated-measures analysis of variance (RM-ANOVA) was applied to compare longitudinal cardiac parameters, with Mauchly’s sphericity test and Greenhouse-Geisser correction for sphericity violation; Bonferroni post-hoc tests corrected for multiple pairwise comparisons.ResultsRM-ANOVA detected statistically significant main effects of group, time, and group×time interaction for LV systolic pressure (LVSP), LV diastolic pressure (LVDP), and LV end-diastolic pressure (LVEDP) (all P < 0.001). In the AVO group, LVEDP increased progressively over time, rising by 102.7% relative to baseline at the 40 min time point. Post-hoc testing confirmed substantially higher LVEDP in AVO rats versus sham animals at 40 min (15.2 ± 0.79 mmHg vs. 7.5 ± 0.71 mmHg, adjusted P < 0.001). A prominent group×time interaction was also observed for the mitral E/A ratio (P < 0.001): this ratio declined to 0.54 in the AVO group at 40 min, which differed significantly from sham controls (0.54 ± 0.01 vs. 1.73 ± 0.01, adjusted P < 0.001). Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) exhibited a significant group×time interaction as well, falling to 62.82% at 40 min in AVO rats compared with 76.34% ± 0.36 in sham animals (adjusted P < 0.001). All hemodynamic and echocardiographic readouts remained stable across all time points in the sham group, with no statistically significant temporal changes observed.ConclusionsAcute volume overload induced heart diastolic dysfunction in rats is a cost-effective and time-efficient experimental animal model.Graphical AbstractAVO: acute volume overload. Introduction

Low cardiac output syndrome (LCOS) occurs frequently after cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass, and is a well-described risk factor for short-term morbidity and mortality. Overall in-hospital mortality is reported to be 20% to 40% [1–3]. LCOS can be categorized into three subtypes: left ventricular systolic dysfunction, right ventricular (RV) systolic dysfunction, and left ventricular (LV) diastolic dysfunction [1].

LV diastolic dysfunction occurs in up to 70% after cardiac surgery [4, 5]. The cause of LV diastolic dysfunction is acute volume overload (AVO). Some diseases could cause a small-sized ventricle (such as atrial septal defect and severe mitral stenosis) or left ventricular hypertrophy (hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy, and severe aortic stenosis). The underloaded left flow would suddenly become normal after intracardiac malformations are corrected by surgery, hence left ventricle would receive more blood after surgery. The left ventricular chamber is unable to accept an adequate volume of blood, thus causing the left diastolic function and circulatory failure [6, 7].

Studies focused on this important clinical question would be facilitated by the availability of a relevant and suitable animal model. Some experimental models of volume overload-induced heart diastolic failure have been reported. The arteriovenous shunt(AVS)model established by Flaim et al. is a chronic experimental model that involves the surgical creation of a direct connection between the abdominal aorta and the inferior vena cava [8]. Aortic regurgitation (AR) model is another volume overload model of HF that is induced by surgical interventions such as aortic valve leaflet perforation or cusp removal [9]. However, AVO-induced heart diastolic dysfunction model with normal intracardiac structure is lacking. Therefore, we sought to establish a novel surgical model of AVO-induced LV diastolic dysfunction model in this study.

Methods

Study design

6–8 weeks old male Sprague Dawley rats (GemPharmatech Co., Ltd, Nanjing, China) weighing 250 to 350 g were used. The experiment was performed in accordance with the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the National Institutes of Health (NIH Publication No.85 − 23). The protocol of animal study was approved by the Ethics committee of the animal welfare of West China hospital, Sichuan University (No.20240815004). All authors complied with the ARRIVE guidelines. Rats were divided into two groups randomly: (1) AVO group(n = 10), injecting 0.9% sodium chloride (3 ml/h) into the LV, and the total infused volume over 40 min was 2.0 mL; (2) Sham-operated group(n = 10), no fluid was given over the study.

Surgical procedures

All rats were anesthetized with isoflurane (induction: 3%, maintenance: 2%). Carprofen (5 mg/kg) was used to relieve pain. Hair was removed from the anterior chest and neck using chemical hair remover. Rats were restrained supine, intubated into air tube through a middle neck incision, and ventilated using a rodent ventilator (RWD Life Science, Shenzhen China). Three wires were inserted into the subcutaneous tissue of limbs of rats and connected to the MP160 system (BIOPAC Systems Inc, USA) to record electrocardiogram (ECG). A left thoracotomy was performed. A 22-gauge needle with cannula was inserted into left ventricle chamber, and then the needle was pull out. The cannula was connected to the pressure transducer of MP160 system using a 30-cm pressure monitoring line to record left ventricular pressure. A infusion pump with a 20 ml syringe was also connected to the MP160 through a 75-cm pressure monitoring line and a three-way stopcock. The infusion pump line was closed by the three-way stopcock when recording left ventricular pressure and opened when injecting fluid into left ventricle chamber (Fig. 1). Isoflurane concentration was adjusted to 1.5% after completing the surgical procedure above. AVO model was induced by injecting 0.9% sodium chloride (3 ml/h) into the left ventricle. No fluid was given in Sham group rats. Rats were euthanized by inhaling excessive carbon dioxide at the end of the experiment.

Hemodynamics

Hemodynamic parameters were obtained at baseline (the time point that surgical procedure was completed), 10 min, 20 min, 30 min and 40 min by MP 160 system. Left ventricular systolic pressure (LVSP), left ventricular diastolic pressure (LVDP), and left ventricular end diastolic pressure (LVEDP) were included.

Echocardiographic study

Echocardiography was performed at baseline, 10 min, 20 min, 30 min and 40 min by using Mindray M9Vet Ultrasound System (Mindray Inc, Shenzhen China). M-mode images at longitudinal axis between papillary muscles were obtained from a parasternal long axis view of left ventricle. Left ventricular end-systolic diameter (LVESD), left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVEDD) and interventricular septum (IVS) were measured. Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was calculated by automatic ejection fraction measurement of Mindray M9Vet. Early (E) and late (A) diastolic transmitral Doppler flow velocities were measured to assess LV diastolic function.

Standards for AVO-induced heart diastolic dysfunction model

The following indicators according to literatures [10–13] were confirmed to establish AVO-induced heart diastolic dysfunction model successfully: (1) left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) ≥ 50%; (2) left ventricular end diastolic pressure (LVEDP) rises (>30%){ the human thresholds in the guidelines is LVEDP≥16mmHg [rise ≥ 30% compared to normal LVEDP in human (8-12mmHg)]}; (3) E/A ratio<1.

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed by an investigator blinded to group assignment. All hemodynamic and echocardiographic parameters (LVSP, LVDP, LVEDP, E velocity, A velocity, E/A ratio, LVEF) were expressed as mean ± standard error (SE).

For data repeatedly measured at baseline, 10 min, 20 min, 30 min and 40 min in the same rat, two-way repeated-measures analysis of variance (RM-ANOVA) was performed. The between-subject factor was group (AVO vs. Sham), and the within-subject factor was time (five sequential time points). The interaction term of group × time was also tested. Mauchly’s sphericity test was applied to verify the sphericity assumption; if the sphericity assumption was violated, the Greenhouse-Geisser correction was adopted to adjust the degrees of freedom. When the main effect of group, main effect of time, or group×time interaction reached statistical significance (P < 0.05), post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted:1)Inter-group comparison: AVO vs. Sham at each single time point;2༉Intra-group comparison: each time point (10/20/30/40 min) vs. baseline within the same group. All post-hoc P values were corrected by Bonferroni method to avoid type I error caused by multiple comparisons. Two-tailed P < 0.05 was defined as statistically significant.

Results

Procedural mortality

The major aim of this study was to establish a new AVO-induced heart diastolic dysfunction model with low mortality. No obvious bleeding was observed after left ventricular catheterization. All rats were alive during the study and were euthanized after the study.

Hemodynamics

Two-way RM-ANOVA showed a significant main effect of group, main effect of time, and significant group×time interaction for LVEDP (all P < 0.001). Post-hoc Bonferroni-corrected comparisons revealed that LVEDP was significantly higher in the AVO group than the Sham group at 40 min (15.2 ± 0.79 vs. 7.5 ± 0.71 mmHg, adjusted P < 0.001). Within the AVO group, LVEDP increased progressively over time, with a 102.7% elevation at 40 min relative to baseline (adjusted P < 0.001). No significant temporal changes in LVEDP were observed across all time points in the Sham group (all adjusted P > 0.050) (Table 1).

Left ventricular function

RM-ANOVA detected a significant group×time interaction for mitral E/A ratio (P < 0.001). Post-hoc testing confirmed that the E/A ratio was markedly reduced in the AVO group at 40 min compared with Sham rats (0.54 ± 0.01 vs. 1.73 ± 0.01, adjusted P < 0.001). The AVO group exhibited a continuous time-dependent decline in E/A ratio from baseline to 40 min (all adjusted P < 0.05), while the E/A ratio remained stable throughout the experiment in the Sham group (all adjusted P > 0.05) (Table 2, Fig. 2).

Discussion

LV diastolic dysfunction often occurs after cardiac surgery [4, 5]. Left ventricular chamber is too small or hypertrophic to afford increased blood after cardiac surgery, then causing the left ventricular diastolic function [6, 7]. This experiment was designed to establish a simple, reproducible AVO model to simulate the process of acute volume overload and corrected intracardiac malformation.

As the small-sized ventricle is too difficult to simulate, we choose to establish the animal heart diastolic failure model with left ventricular volume overload comparatively in this study. AVS model [8, 14, 15] is a chronic experimental model which needs at least 4 weeks to induce heart failure. To our knowledge, LV diastolic dysfunction is a rapid course after cardiac surgery. Aortic regurgitation(AR)model [9] is an acute volume overload model of heart failure, however this model cannot simulate the process that intracardiac malformations have been corrected after surgery and the process of left ventricular acute volume overload. It was also the same reason why we did not place the cannula from carotid artery or left atrium into LV chamber to record LV pressure and inject fluid. The way that placing the cannula into left atrium can simulate the normal blood pathway (left atrium → mitral valve → LV). However, the left atrium of the rat is too small to make this operation feasible, and LVEDP cannot be measured as well by this way. Thus, we opted to place the cannula into LV chamber and inject fluid directly to establish the AVO model. And left thoracotomy was performed to avoid damaging coronary arteries when inserting the needle and cannula into LV chamber. Roncon-Albuquerque R Jr et al. had investigated the effects of acute volume on biventricular function and gene expression. They used eight weeks old adult male Wistar rats to establish the AVO model by injecting dextran40 to vein (5 ml/h), with a high mortality rate of 23.5%. In our study, the 0.9% saline was injected into the LV directly. Thus the infusion speed was decreased from 5 ml/h to 3 ml/h based on the concern that the SD rats might not afford the higher infusion (5 ml/h) [16].

In ESC Guideline for the diagnosis and treatment of acute and chronic heart failure (2021) and its update edition (2023) [12, 13], the diagnosis of LV diastolic dysfunction should include the following: (1)symptoms and signs of heart failure; (2)LVEF ≥ 50%;(3) objective evidence of cardiac functional abnormalities with the presence of LV diastolic dysfunction, and the gold standard for the diagnosis of HFpEF is LVEDP≥16mmHg(30% in human, at rest).In our study, LVEDP in AVO group increased by 102.7% at 40 min compared to baseline, E/A ratio decreased to 0.54, and LVEF decreased to 62.82%. Collectively, these findings indicated successful establishment of a diastolic dysfunction model with preserved LVEF following direct intraventricular saline infusion.

To address the limitation of inappropriate independent-group statistical tests in the initial draft, we adopted two-way repeated-measures ANOVA for longitudinal data analysis. The original Mann-Whitney U test only performed isolated cross-sectional comparisons at separate time points and ignored the correlation of repeated measurements obtained from the same rat, which may inflate type I error. Repeated-measures ANOVA simultaneously evaluates group differences, temporal changes, and divergent dynamic trends between the two groups via the group×time interaction term. Combined with Bonferroni correction for multiple post-hoc comparisons, this statistical strategy provides more rigorous and reliable statistical inference for the time-dependent progression of acute diastolic dysfunction induced by volume overload.

In our study, the LVEF of the AVO group decreased to 62.82% at 40 min, showing a significant difference compared with baseline (62.82%±0.50 vs. 76.38%±0.36, p<0.001). Two mechanistic explanations may underlie this decline in LVEF. First, short-term left ventricular volume overload may directly impair LV systolic function and lower LVEF. Second, left ventricular catheterization might cause subtle myocardial injury, even though we took great care intraoperatively to avoid coronary artery damage.

We did not measure other commonly used indices of diastolic dysfunction, such as deceleration time, isovolumic relaxation time, left atrial pressure and pulmonary vein flow, the reasons are as follows. Firstly, our open-chest rat model requires sustained ventricular infusion and real-time hemodynamic recording. Too many parameters might raise animal mortality and destabilize hemodynamic data. In addition, limited by experimental conditions the MP160 system we used could not measure the -dp/dtmax, which could be used to evaluate. Finally, our study aims to build a simple, repeatable AVO model mimicking post-cardiac surgery acute diastolic dysfunction. LVEDP, E/A and LVEF are readily available intraoperative markers consistent with clinical monitoring protocols. Conclusion

In summary, our novel surgical rat model reliably recapitulates acute left ventricular diastolic dysfunction triggered by intraventricular volume overload. This acute experimental paradigm is economical and time-efficient, with zero procedural mortality. It provides a readily reproducible preclinical tool to dissect underlying molecular mechanisms and test innovative therapeutic interventions for diastolic dysfunction complicating cardiac surgery.
