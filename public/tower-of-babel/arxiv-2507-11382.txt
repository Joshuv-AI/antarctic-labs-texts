Morse decomposition for semi-dynamical systems with an application to systems of state-dependent delay differential equations
István Balázs, Ábel Garab, Teresa Rauscher

Abstract
Understanding the structure of the global attractor is crucial in the field of dynamical systems, where Morse decompositions provide a powerful tool by partitioning the attractor into finitely many invariant Morse sets and gradient-like connecting orbits. Building on Mallet-Paret’s pioneering use of discrete Lyapunov functions for constructing Morse decompositions in delay differential equations, similar approaches have been extended to various delay systems, also including state-dependent delays. In this paper, we develop a unified framework assuming the existence and some properties of a discrete Lyapunov function for a semi-dynamical system on an arbitrary metric space, and construct a Morse decomposition of the global attractor in this general setting. We demonstrate that our findings generalize previous results; moreover, we apply our theorem to a cyclic system of differential equations with threshold-type state-dependent delay.



1
Introduction

Under quite natural and frequently met assumptions, semi-dynamical systems – whether induced by ordinary, delay, or parabolic differential equations, or difference equations – are dissipative. This yields that their forward dynamics eventually enters a bounded subset of the state space, hence a global attractor
\mathcal{A}
exists that contains all bounded entire solutions. Consequently,
\mathcal{A}
encompasses all dynamically relevant objects, such as equilibria, periodic solutions,
as well as homo- or heteroclinic orbits.

Beyond the mere existence of a global attractor, one is more interested in its internal structure. A Morse decomposition serves as an effective tool for this task for the following reasons:
it allows disassembling the global attractor into finitely many invariant compact subsets (the
Morse sets) and their connecting orbits; the recurrent dynamics in
\mathcal{A}
occurs entirely within the Morse sets; outside of them, the dynamics on
\mathcal{A}
is gradient-like.

Morse decompositions were introduced by Conley
[
7
]
and have been investigated by many scholars since then – see, e.g.
[
6
,
13
]
and references therein. To the best of our knowledge, Mallet-Paret
[
14
]
was the first to employ a discrete Lyapunov function (i.e. an integer-valued function on the state-space that is nonincreasing along solutions) to construct a Morse decomposition, which he developed for a class of delay differential equations (DDEs) with negative feedback and constant delay 1. This discrete Lyapunov function
V
is based on counting the number of sign changes on segments of solutions (these segments being elements of the infinite-dimensional state space
C([-1,0],\mathbb{R})
). The Morse decomposition was constructed by showing and utilizing several properties of
V
.

Mallet-Paret’s ideas have been successfully applied to obtain similar results based on analogously defined discrete Lyapunov functions for delay difference equations
[
11
]
and for (systems of) DDEs
[
18
,
10
]
, and, very recently, for scalar state-dependent delay differential equations
(SDDEs)

[
3
]
. All the proofs consist of the following main steps:

(1)

defining an appropriate discrete Lyapunov function
V
, and verifying several properties of it (which are rather similar to the ones seen in
[
14
]
);

(2)

defining the Morse sets (that are, roughly speaking, the sets of bounded entire solutions living within level sets of
V
);

(3)

proving the existence of the Morse decomposition in a series of lemmas, similarly as done in
[
14
]
.

The aim of this paper is twofold: On the one hand, we define a framework that is sufficiently flexible to unify all the results mentioned above. More precisely, we study a semi-dynamical system on an arbitrary metric space and assume that part (1) above is already done, that is, a discrete Lyapunov function with suitable properties exists. Then we carry out parts (2) and (3) in this general setting (see
Section

3
), and demonstrate that the results reproduce or refine those in
[
14
,
10
,
11
,
3
,
18
]
(see
Section

4.2
).

On the other hand, in
Section

4.1
we study a cyclic system of SDDEs with a threshold-type delay and show that, based on the abstract results in
Section

3
and our recent findings on discrete Lyapunov functions for DDEs with variable delay
[
2
]
, a Morse decomposition of the global attractor can be constructed.

There are other fields, such as tridiagonal ordinary differential equations
[
1
]
and their discretizations
[
16
]
, scalar reaction–diffusion equations
[
5
,
8
]
, or nonlinear Cauchy–Riemann equations
[
4
]
, where analogously defined discrete Lyapunov functions are available.
In addition, general dynamical systems and skew-product semi-flows that admit a discrete Lyapunov function have been studied (see, for example,
[
19
]
and
[
20
]
, respectively). We hope that the findings obtained in
Section

3
complement these abstract results and further facilitate the construction of new Morse decompositions in these and related settings.

In the next section, we introduce the necessary notation and recall some results from topology.

2
Preliminaries

Basic notation and definitions

Let
\mathbb{C},\mathbb{R},\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{N}_{0}
, and
\mathbb{N}
denote the set of complex, real, integer, nonnegative integer, and positive integer numbers, respectively,
(\mathcal{X},d)
be a metric space,

\mathbb{T}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{Z}\},\qquad\mathbb{T}_{+}=\mathbb{T}\cap[0,\infty),\qquad\mathbb{T}_{-}=\mathbb{T}\cap(-\infty,0],

and let
S\colon\mathbb{T}_{+}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
be a semi-dynamical system having a global attractor
\mathcal{A}
, defined as follows.

Definition 2.1

(
[
12
, p. 1]
)
.

The global attractor
\mathcal{A}
is the maximal compact invariant set containing the limit set

\omega(U)=\bigcap_{t\in\mathbb{T}}\overline{\bigcup_{s\geq t}S(s,U)}

of all bounded sets
U\subseteq\mathcal{X}
.

Definition 2.2
.

A function
\varphi\colon\mathbb{T}\to\mathcal{X}
is an entire solution of
S
if

\varphi(s+t)=S(t,\varphi(s))\quad\text{for all }s\in\mathbb{T}\text{ and }t\in\mathbb{T}_{+}.

Remark 2.3
.

The global attractor
\mathcal{A}
of
S
is the union of the trajectories of the bounded entire solutions of
S
.

For convenience, we will use the following equivalent definition for
\omega
-limit sets of singletons. Besides that, let us define
\alpha
-limit sets of entire solutions in a similar fashion.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
\xi\in\mathcal{X}
, then

\displaystyle\omega(\xi)

\displaystyle=\{\eta\in\mathcal{X}\mid\exists(t_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}:S(t_{n},\xi)\to\eta,\ t_{n}\to\infty,\ n\to\infty\}.

If
\varphi
is an entire solution, then

\displaystyle\alpha(\varphi)

\displaystyle=\{\eta\in\mathcal{X}\mid\exists(t_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}:\varphi(t_{n})\to\eta,\ t_{n}\to-\infty,\ n\to\infty\}.

We proceed to define the Morse decomposition, which is the main object of the paper.

Definition 2.5
.

A Morse decomposition of the global attractor
\mathcal{A}
of the semi-dynamical system
S
is a finite ordered collection
\mathcal{M}_{0}\prec\mathcal{M}_{1}\prec\dots\prec\mathcal{M}_{m}
of nonempty, compact, invariant, pairwise disjoint subsets of
\mathcal{A}
such that for any
\xi\in\mathcal{A}
and any bounded solution
\varphi\colon\mathbb{T}\to\mathcal{X}
of the semi-dynamical system
S
through
\xi
, there exist
N,K
in
\left\{0,\ldots,m\right\}
with
N\leq K
and

(M
1
)

\alpha(\varphi)\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{K}
,
\omega(\xi)\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{N}
,

(M
2
)

N=K
implies that
\varphi(t)\in\mathcal{M}_{N}
for all
t\in\mathbb{T}
.

We will refer to these properties as Morse properties.

Although the Morse sets are – by definition – nonempty, for brevity, we take the liberty to occasionally list some sets
\mathcal{M}_{0},\dots,\mathcal{M}_{m}
as a Morse decomposition with some (but not all) of them potentially empty.

Ascoli’s theorem

The following theorem is a form of Ascoli’s theorem for metric spaces that we will use several times in the following proofs.

Theorem A

(
[
17
, Theorem 47.1]
Ascoli’s theorem)
.

Let
\mathcal{U}
be a space, and let
(\mathcal{V},d_{\mathcal{V}})
be a metric space. Give
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{V})
the topology of compact convergence; let
\mathcal{F}
be a subset of
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{V})
.

(a)

If
\mathcal{F}
is equicontinuous under
d_{\mathcal{V}}
and the set

\mathcal{F}_{a}=\{f(a)\ |\ f\in\mathcal{F}\}

has compact closure for each
a\in\mathcal{U}
, then
\mathcal{F}
is contained in a compact subspace of
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{V})
.

(b)

The converse holds if
\mathcal{U}
is locally compact Hausdorff.

Since we will work in metric spaces, the conclusion of Ascoli’s theorem (part (a)) has the consequence that every infinite subset of
\mathcal{F}
has a limit point (cf.
[
17
, Theorem 28.2]
).

3
Abstract results

Using the notations introduced above, we assume that the semi-dynamical system
S
has global attractor
\mathcal{A}
, and the following hypotheses hold throughout the paper.

(H
1
)

The set
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
is compact, and invariant in the sense that
S(t,\mathcal{O})=\mathcal{O}
for all
t\in\mathbb{T}_{+}
.

(H
2
)

The set
\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
is positively invariant (i.e.
S(\widetilde{\mathcal{X}},t)\subseteq\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}
), moreover,
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}
.

(H
3
)

A metric
\widetilde{d}
is defined on
\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}
such that
for any
\xi\in\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}
and
(\xi_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\subseteq\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\widetilde{d}(\xi_{n},\xi)=0\quad\text{implies}\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}d(\xi_{n},\xi)=0.

(H
4
)

Let
(\xi_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence in
\mathcal{A}
and
\xi\in\mathcal{A}
with
\lim_{n\to\infty}d(\xi_{n},\xi)=0
. Then there exists a subsequence
(\xi_{n_{k}})_{k=1}^{\infty}
such that
\lim_{k\to\infty}\widetilde{d}(\xi_{n_{k}},\xi)=0
.

Let us define our most important tool, the discrete Lyapunov function

V\colon\dom(V)\to\mathbb{N}_{0}\cup\{\infty\},

where
\mathcal{X}\setminus\mathcal{O}\subseteq\dom(V)\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, and suppose that it has the following properties.

(V
1
)

For every sequence
(\xi_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\subseteq\mathcal{X}\setminus\mathcal{O}
and
\xi\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\mathcal{O}
with
\lim_{n\to\infty}d(\xi,\xi_{n})=0
it holds that

V(\xi)\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}V(\xi_{n}).

(V
2
)

There exists a set
\mathcal{R}\subseteq\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}\setminus\mathcal{O}
such that for every sequence
(\xi_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\subseteq\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}\setminus\mathcal{O}
and
\xi\in\mathcal{R}
with
\lim_{n\to\infty}\widetilde{d}(\xi,\xi_{n})=0
it holds that

V(\xi)=\lim_{n\to\infty}V(\xi_{n}).

(V
3
)

For all
t\in\mathbb{T}_{+}
and
\xi\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\mathcal{O}
it holds that
V(S(t,\xi))\leq V(\xi)
.

(V
4
)

There exists
T\in\mathbb{T}_{+}
such that for any
t\in\mathbb{T}_{+}
with
t\geq T
, if
V(S(t,\xi))=V(\xi)
, then
S(t,\xi)\in\mathcal{R}
.

(V
5
)

If
\mathcal{O}
is nonempty, then there exist
N^{\ast}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and an open neighborhood
U\subseteq\mathcal{X}
of
\mathcal{O}
such that for any entire solution
\varphi\colon\mathbb{T}\to\mathcal{X}\setminus\mathcal{O}
of the semi-dynamical system
S
the following statements hold:

(a)

If there exists
t_{0}
such that
\varphi(t)\in\overline{U}\cap\mathcal{A}
for all
t\leq t_{0}
, then
V(\varphi(t))\leq N^{*}
for all
t\in\mathbb{T}
.

(b)

If there exists
t_{1}
such that
\varphi(t)\in\overline{U}\cap\mathcal{A}
for all
t\geq t_{1}
, then
V(\varphi(t))\geq N^{*}
for all
t\in\mathbb{T}
.

Property
(V3)
explains the name “discrete Lyapunov function”.

We note that in the definition of
V
, the set
\mathbb{N}_{0}
could be replaced by any countable subset of isolated real numbers that is bounded below. For simplicity of notation, we work with the definition given above.

Now, let us define the sets that will form a Morse decomposition.
If
\mathcal{O}\neq\emptyset
(
\mathcal{O}=\emptyset
), for
N\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\setminus\left\{N^{*}\right\}
(for
N\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
) define the set

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{N}\coloneq\Big\{\xi\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{O}

\displaystyle:\text{there exists a bounded entire solution }\varphi\colon\mathbb{T}\to\mathcal{X}\text{ through }\xi

\displaystyle\mathrel{\hphantom{:}}\text{such that }V(\varphi(t))=N\text{ for all }t\in\mathbb{T},\text{ and }(\alpha(\varphi)\cup\omega(\xi))\cap\mathcal{O}=\emptyset\Big\},

and for a fixed
N_{0}>N^{*}
(
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
), let us also define the set

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{N_{0}}^{+}\coloneq\Big\{\xi\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{O}

\displaystyle:\text{there exists a bounded entire solution }\varphi\colon\mathbb{T}\to\mathcal{X}\text{ through }\xi

\displaystyle\mathrel{\hphantom{:}}\text{such that }V(\varphi(t))\geq N_{0}\text{ for all }t\in\mathbb{T},\text{ and }(\alpha(\varphi)\cup\omega(\xi))\cap\mathcal{O}=\emptyset\Big\}.

Furthermore, if
\mathcal{O}\neq\emptyset
, then let

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{N^{*}}\coloneq\Big\{\xi\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{O}

\displaystyle:\text{there exists a bounded entire solution }\varphi\colon\mathbb{T}\to\mathcal{X}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\hphantom{:}}\text{though $\xi$ such that }V(\varphi(t))=N^{*}\text{ for all }t\in\mathbb{T}\Big\}\cup\mathcal{O}.

Finally, for
n\leq N_{0}
we define the Morse sets as

\mathcal{M}_{n}\coloneq\begin{cases}\mathcal{S}_{n},&\text{if }n<N_{0},\\
\mathcal{S}_{N_{0}}^{+}&\text{if }n=N_{0}.\\
\end{cases}

Our first main theorem is the following.

Theorem 3.1
.

If the semi-dynamical system
S
has a global attractor
\mathcal{A}
and hypotheses
(H1)
–
(H4)
and
(V1)
–

are fulfilled, then the sets
\mathcal{M}_{0},\dots,\mathcal{M}_{N_{0}}
form a Morse decomposition of the global attractor
\mathcal{A}
for any
N_{0}>N^{\ast}

(N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}_{0})
in case of
\mathcal{O}\neq\emptyset

(\mathcal{O}=\emptyset)
.

Remark 3.2
.

Some of the assumptions might look artificial at first. However, they are constructed in a way that
Theorem

3.1
can cover all the corresponding results of
[
14
,
10
,
18
,
11
,
3
]
, as we demonstrate in
Section

4
.

Remark 3.3
.

Note that our conditions permit the case
\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}=\mathcal{X}
or
\widetilde{d}=d
. This is indeed the case when adapting the result to the discrete-time problem in
[
11
]
– see
Section

4.2
.

Remark 3.4
.

In all of the examples mentioned, the discrete Lyapunov function is based on counting the number of sign changes on suitable sets. In these examples,
\mathcal{O}
is the singleton of the origin (in the appropriate space), and
N^{\ast}
is related to the number of unstable eigenvalues of the linearization. These – together with other properties of the distribution of eigenvalues – make it possible to show condition
(V5)
. It is somewhat difficult to imagine that this condition holds for a different type of discrete Lyapunov function when
\mathcal{O}\neq\emptyset
; however,
\mathcal{O}=\emptyset
is admissible, in which case condition
(V5)
imposes no restriction.

We need several lemmas to prove
Theorem

3.1
. The next three lemmas are crucial for establishing the Morse properties.

Lemma 3.5
.

Suppose that
\varphi\colon\mathbb{T}\to\mathcal{X}
is a bounded entire solution through
\xi\in\mathcal{A}
.

(a)

If
N\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\lim_{t\to\infty}V(\varphi(t))=N
, then
V(\eta)=N
for any
\eta\in\omega(\xi)\setminus\mathcal{O}
.

(b)

If
N\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\cup\{\infty\}
and
\lim_{t\to\infty}V(\varphi(t))\geq N
, then
V(\eta)\geq N
for any
\eta\in\omega(\xi)\setminus\mathcal{O}
.

(c)

If
N\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\lim_{t\to-\infty}V(\varphi(t))=N
, then
V(\eta)=N
for any
\eta\in\alpha(\varphi)\setminus\mathcal{O}
.

(d)

If
N\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\cup\{\infty\}
and
\lim_{t\to-\infty}V(\varphi(t))\geq N
, then
V(\eta)\geq N
for any
\eta\in\alpha(\varphi)\setminus\mathcal{O}
.

Proof.

We only prove statements (a) and (b) (simultaneously), since the others can be shown analogously.

For an arbitrary
\eta\in\omega(\xi)\setminus\mathcal{O}
, by hypothesis
(H4)
, there exists a sequence
(t_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
in
\mathbb{T}_{+}
with
t_{n}\to\infty
and

\lim_{n\to\infty}\widetilde{d}(\varphi(t_{n}),\eta)=0.

(3.1)

Then, by the invariance of
\omega(\xi)\setminus\mathcal{O}
there exists a bounded entire solution
\psi\colon\mathbb{T}\to\mathcal{X}

with
\psi(t)\in\omega(\xi)\setminus\mathcal{O}
for all
t\in\mathbb{T}
and
\psi(0)=\eta
. Hence, for any
t\in\mathbb{T}
, there exists
t_{n}^{\prime}\to\infty
with
\lim_{n\to\infty}\varphi(t_{n}^{\prime})=\psi(t)
.

Note that this, together with
(V1)
, implies
V(\psi(t))\leq N
for all
t\in\mathbb{T}
, assuming the conditions of statement (a) are satisfied.

Furthermore, by
(V3)
, there exists
K\coloneq\lim_{t\to\infty}V(\psi(t))
. We claim that
K\geq N
.

Assume indirectly that
K<N
, and hence,
K<\infty
. Then,
(V4)
implies the existence of
T>0
such that
V(\psi(T))=K
and
\psi(T)\in\mathcal{R}
. Note that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\widetilde{d}(\varphi(t_{n}+T),\psi(T))=0

follows from (
3.1
) and the continuity of
S
. Applying
(V4)
again along with our assumption, we obtain

N\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}V(\varphi(t_{n}+T))=V(\psi(T))=K,

which is a contradiction. Thus,
V(\eta)\geq K\geq N
, implying statement (a).

It follows from
K\geq N
and the monotonicity of
V
that
V(\eta)\geq N
, as stated in part (b).
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{O}\neq\emptyset
and
\varphi\colon\mathbb{T}\to\mathcal{X}
is a bounded entire solution through
\xi\in\mathcal{A}
.

(a)

If
\lim_{t\to\infty}V(\varphi(t))\neq N^{*}
, then either
\omega(\xi)\subseteq\mathcal{O}
or
\omega(\xi)\subseteq\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{O}
.

(b)

If
\lim_{t\to-\infty}V(\varphi(t))\neq N^{*}
, then either
\alpha(\varphi)\subseteq\mathcal{O}
or
\alpha(\varphi)\subseteq\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{O}
.

Proof.

Again, we only prove part (a). Let us assume that
\omega(\xi)\cap\mathcal{O}\neq\emptyset
, but
\omega(\xi)\not\subseteq\mathcal{O}
, furthermore, let
U\subseteq\mathcal{X}
be as in property
(V5)
. Then there exists an open neighborhood
U_{1}
of
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
such that
U_{1}\subset U
and
\omega(\xi)\nsubseteq\overline{U_{1}}
. This, together with
\omega(\xi)\cap\mathcal{O}\neq\emptyset
, implies that
\varphi(t)
enters and leaves
\overline{U_{1}}
infinitely many times as
t\to\infty
.

Accordingly, there exist positive sequences
t_{n}
, and
\tau_{n}
in
\mathbb{T}_{+}
such that
t_{n}
satisfies

\varphi(t_{n})\in U_{1},\quad d(\varphi(t_{n}),\mathcal{O})\to 0,\quad t_{n}\to\infty,\quad\text{as }n\to\infty,

and
\tau_{n}
is defined by

\tau_{n}\coloneq\sup\left\{t\in\mathbb{T}_{+}:\varphi(u)\in U_{1},\ \forall u\in[t_{n},t_{n}+t)\cap\mathbb{T}\right\}.

Note that since
S(t,\mathcal{O})=\mathcal{O}
for all
t\in\mathbb{T}_{+}
, and
S
is continuous,
\tau_{n}
is well-defined for sufficiently large
n
, and we have
\tau_{n}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
. Moreover,
\varphi(t_{n}+\tau_{n})\notin U_{1}
. Then for each
n
, we can choose
s_{n}\in\mathbb{T}_{+}
such that
\varphi(t_{n}-s_{n})\notin U_{1}
,
\varphi(u)\in U_{1}
for all
u\in(t_{n}-s_{n},t_{n}+\tau_{n})\cap\mathbb{T}
, and
t_{n}+\tau_{n}\leq t_{n+1}-s_{n+1}
.

Set

\psi_{n}(t)=\varphi(t_{n}-s_{n}+t)\quad\text{ and }\quad\vartheta_{n}(t)=\varphi(t_{n}+\tau_{n}+t)\quad\text{ for }t\in\mathbb{T}.

Now let us consider the continuous-time case (
\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}
). We want to apply
A
with

\mathcal{U}=\mathbb{R},\quad\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{X},\quad d_{\mathcal{V}}=d,\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{F}=\{\psi_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}\subseteq\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{X}),

Obviously, the set

\left\{\psi_{n}(t):n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}=\left\{\varphi(t_{n}-s_{n}+t):n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}\subseteq\mathcal{A}

is precompact for each
t\in\mathbb{R}
as it is a subset of the compact global attractor
\mathcal{A}
.
To apply
A
, equicontinuity is needed. Therefore, we have to show that for all
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a
\delta>0
such that
d(\psi_{n}(t),\psi_{n}(\overline{t}))<\varepsilon
for all
t
,
n
with
|t-\overline{t}|<\delta.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, the set
[0,\varepsilon]\times\mathcal{A}
is compact. Since the semi-dynamical system
S
is continuous, the map
(s,\eta)\mapsto S(s,\eta)
is uniformly continuous on
[0,\varepsilon]\times\mathcal{A}
. Consequently, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a
\delta\in[0,\varepsilon]
such that
d(S(t,\eta),S(\overline{t},\overline{\eta}))<\varepsilon
for all
(t,\eta),\,(\overline{t},\overline{\eta})\in[0,\varepsilon]\times\mathcal{A}
satisfying
|t-\overline{t}|<\delta
and
d(\eta,\overline{\eta})<\delta
.

In particular, we obtain

\displaystyle d(\psi_{n}(t),\psi_{n}(\overline{t}))

\displaystyle=d(\varphi(t_{n}-s_{n}+t),\varphi(t_{n}-s_{n}+\overline{t}))

\displaystyle=d(S(t_{n}-s_{n}+t,\xi),S(t_{n}-s_{n}+\overline{t},\xi))

\displaystyle=d(S(t,S(t_{n}-s_{n},\xi)),S(\overline{t},S(t_{n}-s_{n},\xi)))<\varepsilon

for all
|t-\overline{t}|<\delta
. Therefore,
\psi_{n}
is equicontinuous and similarly, this also holds for
\vartheta_{n}
.

Now,
A
yields the existence of a sequence of indices
(n_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
and maps
\psi,\vartheta\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{X})
such that

\psi_{n_{k}}(t)\to\psi(t)\quad\text{and}\quad\vartheta_{n_{k}}(t)\to\vartheta(t)\quad\text{for all }t\in\mathbb{R},\text{ as }k\to\infty.

In the discrete-time case, the same follows directly from (sequential) compactness of
\mathcal{A}
.

Since
\varphi\colon\mathbb{T}\to\mathcal{X}
is a bounded entire solution and the semi-dynamical system is continuous, for all
s\in\mathbb{T}
,
t\in\mathbb{T}_{+}
, we obtain

\displaystyle S(t,\psi(s))

\displaystyle=S(t,\lim_{k\to\infty}\psi_{n_{k}}(s))

\displaystyle=S(t,\lim_{k\to\infty}\varphi(t_{n_{k}}-s_{n_{k}}+s))

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\to\infty}S(t,\varphi(t_{n_{k}}-s_{n_{k}}+s))

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\to\infty}\varphi(t_{n_{k}}-s_{n_{k}}+s+t)

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\to\infty}\psi_{n_{k}}(s+t)=\psi(s+t),

that is,
\psi\colon\mathbb{T}\to\mathcal{X}
is a bounded entire solution, and by an analogous argument, so is
\vartheta\colon\mathbb{T}\to\mathcal{X}
.

Furthermore, since for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\psi_{n}(t)\in U_{1}
for all
t\in(0,s_{n}+\tau_{n})
, and
\vartheta_{n}(t)\in U_{1}
for all
t\in(-s_{n}-\tau_{n},0)
, we also have

\displaystyle\psi(t)\in\overline{U_{1}}\subset\overline{U}\quad\text{for all }t\in\mathbb{T}_{+},\quad\vartheta(t)\in\overline{U_{1}}\subset\overline{U}\quad\text{for all }t\in\mathbb{T}_{-},

and

\displaystyle\psi(0)\in\mathcal{A}\setminus U_{1},\qquad\vartheta(0)\in\mathcal{A}\setminus U_{1}.

Thus
\psi
and
\vartheta
are nontrivial entire solutions.
By the monotonicity of
V
and
(V5)
, it follows that

V(\vartheta(t))\leq N^{*}\leq V(\psi(t))\quad\text{ for all }t\in\mathbb{T}.

In particular,

V(\vartheta(0))\leq N^{*}\leq V(\psi(0)).

But
\psi(0)
,
\vartheta(0)\in\omega(\xi)\setminus\mathcal{O}
, and by
Lemma

3.5
, we conclude

V(\psi(0))=V(\vartheta(0))=\lim_{t\to\infty}V(\varphi(t)).

This altogether yields

\lim_{t\to\infty}V(\varphi(t))=N^{*},

which is a contradiction.
∎

Lemma 3.7
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{O}\neq\emptyset
,
N
is a nonnegative integer, and
\varphi\colon\mathbb{T}\to\mathcal{X}
is a bounded entire solution through
\xi\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{O}
.

(a)

If
\lim_{t\to\infty}V(\varphi(t))=N\neq N^{*}
, then either
\omega(\xi)\subseteq\mathcal{O}
or
\omega(\xi)\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{N}
.

(b)

If
\lim_{t\to\infty}V(\varphi(t))\geq N>N^{*}
, then either
\omega(\xi)\subseteq\mathcal{O}
or
\omega(\xi)\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{N}^{+}
.

(c)

If
\lim_{t\to-\infty}V(\varphi(t))=N\neq N^{*}
, then either
\alpha(\varphi)\subseteq\mathcal{O}
or
\alpha(\varphi)\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{N}
.

(d)

If
\lim_{t\to-\infty}V(\varphi(t))\geq N>N^{*}
, then either
\alpha(\varphi)\subseteq\mathcal{O}
or
\alpha(\varphi)\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{N}^{+}
.

Proof.

In order to prove assertion (a) (and (b)), let us assume that
\omega(\xi)\not\subseteq\mathcal{O}
and that
\lim_{t\to\infty}V(\varphi(t))=N\neq N^{*}
(
\lim_{t\to\infty}V(\varphi(t))\geq N>N^{*}
). According to
Lemma

3.6
,
\omega(\xi)\cap\mathcal{O}=\emptyset
. Let
\eta\in\omega(\xi)
be arbitrary. Since
\omega(\xi)
is invariant, there exists a bounded entire solution
\psi\colon\mathbb{T}\to\mathcal{X}
through
\eta
such that
\psi(t)\in\omega(\xi)
for all
t\in\mathbb{T}
. By compactness of
\omega(\xi)
, it follows that
\alpha(\psi)\cup\omega(\eta)\subseteq\omega(\xi)
. Therefore
(\alpha(\psi)\cup\omega(\eta))\cap\mathcal{O}=\emptyset
.

On the other hand, since
\psi(t)\in\omega(\xi)
and by
Lemma

3.5
, we have
V(\psi(t))=N
(
V(\psi(t))\geq N
) for all
t\in\mathbb{T}
, so
\eta\in\mathcal{S}_{N}
(
\eta\in\mathcal{S}_{N}^{+}
).

The proof of statements (c) and (d) is analogous.
∎

The next lemma shows that the Morse sets different from
\mathcal{M}_{N^{\ast}}
are bounded away from
\mathcal{O}
, which is key in proving the compactness of the Morse sets.

Lemma 3.8
.

If
\mathcal{O}\neq\emptyset
, then for every
N\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\setminus\{N^{*}
}
, there exists an open neighborhood
\widetilde{U}
of
\mathcal{O}
in
\mathcal{A}
such that
\mathcal{S}_{N}\cap\widetilde{U}=\emptyset
. If
N>N^{*}
, then
\mathcal{S}_{N}^{+}\cap\widetilde{U}=\emptyset
.

Proof.

Assume for contradiction that there exists an
N\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\setminus\{N^{*}\}
and a sequence
(\xi_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
in
\mathcal{S}_{N}\setminus\mathcal{O}
(in
\mathcal{S}_{N}^{+}\setminus\mathcal{O}
) such that
d(\xi_{n},\mathcal{O})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\varphi_{n}\colon\mathbb{T}\to\mathcal{X}
be a bounded entire solution through
\xi_{n}
, such that
(\alpha(\varphi_{n})\cup\omega(\xi_{n}))\cap\mathcal{O}=\emptyset
and
V(\varphi_{n}(t))=N
for all
t\in\mathbb{T}
.

Case
N>N^{*}
. We claim that
\omega(\xi_{n})
cannot be a subset of
\overline{U}
, where
U\subseteq\mathcal{X}
is as in property
(V5)
.

To see this, suppose instead that
\omega(\xi_{n})\subseteq\overline{U}
. Let
\eta\in\omega(\xi_{n})
. By invariance of
\omega(\xi_{n})
, there exists a bounded entire solution
\psi_{n}\colon\mathbb{T}\to\mathcal{X}
through
\xi_{n}
, such that
\psi_{n}(t)\in\omega(\xi_{n})\subseteq\overline{U}
for all
t\in\mathbb{T}
. Then
(V5)
implies
V(\psi_{n}(t))=N^{*}
for all
t\in\mathbb{T}
, but this contradicts
V(\eta)=N
, which should also hold by
Lemma

3.7
. Hence,
\omega(\xi_{n})\nsubseteq\overline{U}
for all
n\geq 0
, as claimed.

Since
d(\xi_{n},\mathcal{O})\to 0
, we can define the sequence

s_{n}=\sup\{s\in\mathbb{T}:\varphi_{n}(t)\in U,\ t\in[0,s)\cap\mathbb{T}\}.

As the semi-dynamical system
S
is continuous and satisfies
S(t,\mathcal{O})=\mathcal{O}
for all
t\in\mathbb{T}_{+}
, it follows that
s_{n}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
. Let
\psi_{n}(t)\coloneq\varphi_{n}(t+s_{n})
for all
t\in\mathbb{T}
. By
A
, there exists a subsequence
\psi_{n_{k}}
and an entire solution
\psi
such that
\psi_{n_{k}}(t)\to\psi(t)
for all
t\in\mathbb{T}
as
k\to\infty
.

The solution
\psi
is nontrivial because
\psi(0)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\varphi_{n}(s_{n})\not\in U
. On the other hand,
\psi(t)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\varphi_{n}(t+s_{n})\in\overline{U}
for all
t\leq 0
.

Hence, property
(V5)
yields
V(\psi(t))\leq N^{*}
for all
t\in\mathbb{T}
.

Finally, by
(V3)
there exists some
T>0
, such that
\psi(T)\in\mathcal{R}
. Since

\varphi_{n_{k}}(s_{n_{k}}+T)=\psi_{n_{k}}(T)\to\psi(T)\quad\text{ for }k\to\infty,

we obtain by virtue of property
(V2)
– and by passing to a subsequence if necessary – that

N=\lim_{k\to\infty}V(\varphi_{n_{k}}(s_{n_{k}}+T))=V(\psi(T))\leq N^{*},

which contradicts
N>N^{\ast}
and proves our statement.

The proof for the case
N<N^{*}
is analogous.
∎

Lemma 3.9
.

The sets
\mathcal{S}_{N^{*}}
,
\mathcal{S}_{N}
, and
S_{N_{0}}^{+}
are closed for all nonnegative integers
N
and
N_{0}
with
N_{0}>N^{*}
.

Proof.

Let
(\xi_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence in
\mathcal{S}_{N}
for some
N\in\mathbb{N}
, such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\xi_{n}=\xi
for some
\xi\in\mathcal{A}
. We claim that
\xi\in\mathcal{S}_{N}
.

If
\xi\in\mathcal{O}
, then
Lemma

3.8
yields
N=N^{*}
. By definition of
\mathcal{S}_{N^{*}}
,
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{N^{*}}
also holds, so the statement is proved in this particular case.

Assume now that
\xi\not\in\mathcal{O}
. By the definition of
\mathcal{S}_{N}
and using
\xi_{n}\in\mathcal{S}_{N}
, there exist bounded entire solutions
\varphi_{n}\colon\mathbb{T}\to\mathcal{X}
through
\xi_{n}
, such that
\varphi_{n}(t)\in\mathcal{S}_{N}
and
V(\varphi_{n}(t))=N
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
t\in\mathbb{T}
. The compactness of
\mathcal{A}
and
A
yield a subsequence
\varphi_{n_{k}}
and a solution
\varphi\colon\mathbb{T}\to\mathcal{X}
such that
\varphi_{n_{k}}(t)\to\varphi(t)
for every
t\in\mathbb{T}
as
k\to\infty
.

Necessarily,
\varphi(0)=\xi
, and by the invariance of
\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{O}
, this yields
\varphi(t)\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{O}
for all
t\in\mathbb{T}
. Applying
(V4)
, we obtain
s_{1},s_{2}\in\mathbb{T}
with
s_{1}<0<s_{2}
and
\varphi(s_{1}),\varphi(s_{2})\in\mathcal{R}
, such that

V(\varphi(s_{1}))=\lim_{t\to-\infty}V(\varphi(t)),\qquad V(\varphi(s_{2}))=\lim_{t\to\infty}V(\varphi(t)).

On the other hand, using
(H4)
, we may assume – after passing to a subsequence if necessary – that

\widetilde{d}(\varphi_{n_{k}}(s_{1}),\varphi(s_{1}))\to 0\quad\text{and}\quad\widetilde{d}(\varphi_{n_{k}}(s_{2}),\varphi(s_{2}))\to 0\quad\text{as }k\to\infty,

so
(V2)
implies
V(\varphi(s_{1}))=V(\varphi(s_{2}))=N
. Therefore, we obtain
V(\varphi(t))=N
for all
t\in\mathbb{T}
.

If
N=N^{*}
or
\mathcal{O}=\emptyset
, then
\xi\in\mathcal{S}_{N}
is already established.

Otherwise, it remains to prove that
(\alpha(\varphi)\cup\omega(\xi))\cap\mathcal{O}=\emptyset
. In this case,
Lemma

3.8
gives an open neighborhood
\widetilde{U}
of
\mathcal{O}
in
\mathcal{A}
such that
\varphi_{n}(t)\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\widetilde{U}
for all
t\in\mathbb{T}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Hence,

\varphi(t)\in\overline{\mathcal{A}\setminus\widetilde{U}}=\mathcal{A}\setminus\widetilde{U}

for all
t\in\mathbb{T}
, which leads to
\alpha(\varphi)\cup\omega(\xi)\subseteq\mathcal{A}\setminus\widetilde{U}
. This shows that
\alpha(\varphi)\cup\omega(\xi)\cap\mathcal{O}=\emptyset
, completing the proof of the claim.

Let us now assume that
N_{0}>N^{\ast}
,
\xi_{n}\in\mathcal{S}_{N_{0}}^{+}
with
\lim_{n\to\infty}\xi_{n}=\xi\in\mathcal{A}
, and
\varphi_{n}
is a solution through
\xi_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then
V(\xi_{n})\geq N_{0}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

As shown earlier, using
A
, we may suppose that there exists a bounded entire solution
\varphi
with
\varphi(0)=\xi
such that
\varphi_{n}\to\varphi
. We have to show that
V(\varphi(t))\geq N_{0}
for all
t\in\mathbb{T}
.

Assume indirectly that there exists
t_{0}\in\mathbb{T}
with
V(\varphi(t_{0}))<N_{0}
. Then
(V4)
implies the existence of
T>0
such that

\lim_{t\to\infty}V(\varphi(t))=V(\varphi(T))\quad\text{and}\quad\varphi(T)\in\mathcal{R}.

By
(H4)
, we may suppose that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\widetilde{d}(\varphi_{n}(t),\varphi(t))=0
and hence, we conclude

N_{0}>V(\varphi(T))=V(\varphi_{n}(T))\geq N_{0},

contradicting the statement.
∎

Now, we are ready to prove our main theorem.

Proof of
Theorem

3.1
.

By definition, the sets
\mathcal{M}_{n}
are pairwise disjoint and invariant. Since
\mathcal{A}
is compact,
Lemma

3.9
yields that
\mathcal{M}_{n}
is compact for all possible
n
. It remains to prove that these sets satisfy the Morse properties
(M1)
,
(M2)
, that is, for every
\xi\in\mathcal{A}
and any bounded entire solution
\varphi\colon\mathbb{T}\to\mathcal{X}
through
\xi
, there exist
N
and
K
with
N\leq K
such that
\alpha(\varphi)\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{K}
,
\omega(\xi)\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{N}
, and in case
N=K
,
\xi\in\mathcal{M}_{N}
, thus
\varphi(t)\in\mathcal{M}_{N}
for all
t\in\mathbb{T}
. If
\mathcal{O}=\emptyset
, this follows directly from
Lemma

3.5
, so we may assume
\mathcal{O}\neq\emptyset
for the remainder of the proof.

If
\xi\in\mathcal{O}
, then invariance and compactness of
\mathcal{O}
, together with the definition of
\mathcal{M}_{N^{\ast}}
, imply that both Morse properties hold.
In order to show this for
\xi\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{O}
as well, let
\varphi\colon\mathbb{T}\to\mathcal{X}
be a bounded entire solution through
\xi
. Furthermore, define

K:=\lim_{t\to-\infty}V(\varphi(t))\quad\text{ and }\quad N:=\lim_{t\to\infty}V(\varphi(t)).

Note that from the monotonicity of
V
, we obtain
N\leq K
.

First, observe that if
N=N^{*}
, then
\omega(\xi)\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{N^{*}}
. In order to prove this, choose
\eta\in\omega(\xi)
arbitrarily. If
\eta\in\mathcal{O}
, then
\eta\in\mathcal{M}_{N^{*}}
by definition, thus we may assume
\eta\notin\mathcal{O}
. By
Lemma

3.5
,
V(\eta)=N^{*}
. Moreover, by the invariance of
\omega(\xi)\setminus\mathcal{O}
, there exists an entire solution
\psi\colon\mathbb{T}\to\omega(\xi)\setminus\mathcal{O}
through
\eta
, for which
Lemma

3.5
yields
V(\psi(t))=N^{*}
for all
t\in\mathbb{T}
, so
\eta\in\mathcal{M}_{N^{*}}
.

A similar argument shows that
K=N^{*}
implies
\alpha(\varphi)\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{N^{*}}
.

Next, we distinguish four cases based on the values of
N
and
K
.

Case 1.
If
N=K=N^{*}
, then
\alpha(\varphi)\cup\omega(\xi)\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{N^{*}}
by our previous observation. Moreover, from the monotonicity of
V
, it follows that
V(\varphi(t))=N^{*}
on
\mathbb{T}
. This implies
\xi\in\mathcal{M}_{N^{*}}
, thus both Morse properties hold.

Case 2.
Assume
N=N^{*}<K
. As shown above,
\omega(\xi)\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{N^{*}}
. Moreover, observe that
\alpha(\varphi)\not\subseteq\mathcal{O}
; otherwise property
(V5)
would imply
V(\varphi(t))\leq N^{*}
for all
t\in\mathbb{T}
, and thus
K\leq N^{*}=N
, which contradicts
K>N
. Therefore,
Lemma

3.7
can be applied to obtain
\alpha(\xi)\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{K}
, so property
(M1)
is fulfilled. Property
(M2)
follows automatically since the two Morse sets in question, i.e.
\mathcal{M}_{N^{*}}
and
\mathcal{M}_{K}
, are distinct.

Case 3.
A similar argument applies in the case when
N<N^{*}=K
.

Case 4.
In the case
N\neq N^{*}\neq K
, let

K_{1}\coloneq\begin{cases}K,&\text{if }K<N_{0},\\
N_{0},&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}\qquad N_{1}\coloneq\begin{cases}N,&\text{if }N<N_{0},\\
N_{0},&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Then
Lemma

3.7
yields that either
\omega(\xi)\subseteq\mathcal{O}
or
\omega(\xi)\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{N_{1}}
; similarly, either
\alpha(\varphi)\subseteq\mathcal{O}
or
\alpha(\varphi)\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{K_{1}}
. Note that
\omega(\xi)
and
\alpha(\varphi)
cannot both be in
\mathcal{O}
, since property
(V5)
would then imply
V(\varphi(t))\equiv N^{*}
on
\mathbb{T}
, contradicting
N_{1}\neq N^{*}\neq K_{1}
.

If
\omega(\xi)\not\subseteq\mathcal{O}\not\supseteq\alpha(\varphi)
, then
Lemma

3.7
yields
\omega(\xi)\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{N_{1}}
and
\alpha(\varphi)\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{K_{1}}
, so
(M1)
is fulfilled. If
N_{1}=K_{1}
, then
V(\varphi(t))=N=K
(
V(\varphi(t))\geq N_{0}
) for all
t\in\mathbb{T}
in case of
K_{1}=N_{1}<N_{0}
(
K_{1}=N_{1}=N_{0}
). Now, our assumption on the limit sets with
Lemma

3.6
ensure that
(\alpha(\varphi)\cup\omega(\xi))\cap\mathcal{O}=\emptyset
, thus
\varphi(t)\in\mathcal{M}_{N_{1}}
also holds for all
t\in\mathbb{T}
. This establishes property
(M2)
.

If
\omega(\xi)\subseteq\mathcal{O}\not\supseteq\alpha(\varphi)
, then
\omega(\xi)\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{N^{*}}
by definition. Furthermore, property
(V5)
implies
V(\varphi(t))\geq N^{*}
for all
t\in\mathbb{T}
, and consequently
N^{*}<N_{1}\leq K_{1}
. On the other hand,
Lemma

3.7
yields
\alpha(\varphi)\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{K_{1}}
, so
(M1)
holds again, and the
(M2)
is automatically fulfilled.

An analogous argument applies to the case
\omega(\xi)\not\subseteq\mathcal{O}\supseteq\alpha(\varphi)
.

We have now taken all possible cases into consideration, so the proof is complete.
∎

4
Applications

In the first part of this section, we present the second main result of the paper: in
Section

4.1
, we study a cyclic system of differential equations with a certain class of state-dependent delays, to which
Theorem

3.1
can be applied to obtain a Morse decomposition of the global attractor, thanks to recent findings from our paper
[
2
]
on a discrete Lyapunov function for DDEs with time-variable delays.

4.1
A cyclic system of differential equations with state-dependent delay

Let
N
be a nonnegative integer, and consider the system

\displaystyle\dot{x}^{i}(t)

\displaystyle=f^{i}(x^{i}(t),x^{i+1}(t)),

\displaystyle i\in\{0,\dots,N-1\},

(4.1)

\displaystyle\dot{x}^{N}(t)

\displaystyle=f^{N}(x^{N}(t),x^{0}(t-\tau(x_{t})))

under the following hypotheses:

(S
1
)

Each
f^{i}
is
C^{1}
-smooth on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and fulfills the feedback assumptions

\displaystyle vf^{i}(0,v)

\displaystyle>0,

\displaystyle D_{2}f^{i}(0,0)

\displaystyle>0,

\displaystyle i\in\{0,\dots,N-1\},

\displaystyle\delta vf^{N}(0,v)

\displaystyle>0,

\displaystyle\delta D_{2}f^{N}(0,0)

\displaystyle>0,

for all
v\neq 0
, where
\delta\in\{\pm 1\}
and
D_{2}
stands for differentiation w.r.t. the second argument.

(S
2
)

There exists
M>0
such that

\displaystyle f^{i}(u,v)

\displaystyle<0

\displaystyle\quad\text{if }u\geq M\text{ and }|v|\leq u

(4.2)

and

\displaystyle f^{i}(u,v)

\displaystyle>0

\displaystyle\quad\text{if }u\leq-M\text{ and }|v|\leq|u|

(4.3)

for all
0\leq i\leq N
.

Consider the Banach space
C(\mathbb{K},\mathbb{R})
equipped with the maximum norm:

\|\varphi\|=\max_{s\in\mathbb{K}}|\varphi(s)|,\quad\varphi\in C(\mathbb{K},\mathbb{R}),

where
\mathbb{K}\coloneq[-r,0]\cup\{0,\ldots,N\}
for some positive constant
r
, and let
x_{t}\in C(\mathbb{K},\mathbb{R})
be defined by

x_{t}(s)=\begin{cases}x^{0}(t+s),&\text{if }s\in[-r,0],\\
x^{s}(t),&\text{if }s\in\{1,\dots,N\}.\end{cases}

Let

L_{0}\coloneq\max\{|f^{i}(u,v)|:(u,v)\in[-M,M]^{2},\ i\in\{0,\dots,N\}\},

and set the phase space

\mathcal{X}\coloneq\{\varphi\in C(\mathbb{K},\mathbb{R})\colon\|\varphi\|<M\text{ and $\varphi$ is $L_{0}$-Lipschitz}\}.

Let
d
be the metric on
\mathcal{X}
induced by the maximum norm. Moreover, let

\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}\coloneq\{\varphi\in\mathcal{X}:\mbox{the restriction }\varphi|_{[-r,0]}\mbox{ is continuously differentiable}\}

with the norm

\|\varphi\|_{1}\coloneq\|\varphi\|+\sup_{s\in[-r,0]}|\dot{\varphi}(s)|,

and let
\widetilde{d}
be the metric induced by this norm.

Assume further that
\tau\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined by

\int_{-\tau(\varphi)}^{0}\alpha(\varphi(s))\,\mathrm{d}s=1,

(4.4)

where
\alpha\colon\mathbb{R}\to[\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}]
is continuous and
\alpha_{1}\coloneq 1/r\leq\alpha_{2}
. Then
\tau(\varphi)
is well-defined and bounded by

\frac{1}{\alpha_{2}}\leq\tau(\varphi)\leq r

for all
\varphi\in\mathcal{X}
(cf.
[
3
, Proposition 6.1]
).

For the sake of simplicity, we also make the following technical assumption.

(D
1
)

There exist
\alpha_{0}
and
\varepsilon>0
such that
\alpha(x)=\alpha_{0}
for all
|x|<\varepsilon
.

It is known that
\varphi\mapsto\tau(\varphi)
is globally Lipschitz
[
2
, Section 4.1]
. One can easily prove, analogously to the scalar case (cf. Propositions 2.1 and 2.4 in
[
3
]
), that for any
\varphi\in\mathcal{X}
, there exists a unique forward solution
x^{\varphi}\colon[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
such that
x^{\varphi}_{0}=\varphi
, and
(t,\varphi)\mapsto S(t,\varphi)\coloneq x^{\varphi}_{t}
defines a continuous semi-dynamical system that has a connected global attractor
\mathcal{A}\subset\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}
. Moreover, for any fixed solution
x\colon[t_{0},\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
, the mapping
t\mapsto\eta(t)\coloneq t-\tau(x_{t})
is strictly increasing.

Let
\mathcal{O}\coloneq\{\bf 0\}
, where
{\bf 0}\in\mathcal{X}
denotes the constant zero function. For
\varphi\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\mathcal{O}
and
a\in[-r,0)
, introduce the notation

\displaystyle V^{+}(\varphi,a)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\signc(\varphi,a),&\text{if }\signc(\varphi,a)\text{ is even or infinite,}\\
\signc(\varphi,a)+1,&\text{if }\signc(\varphi,a)\text{ is odd,}\end{cases}

\displaystyle V^{-}(\varphi,a)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\signc(\varphi,a),&\text{if }\signc(\varphi,a)\text{ is odd or infinite,}\\

4.2
Relation to other results and possible generalizations

Let us briefly demonstrate that several similar results on delay differential and difference equations (i.e.
[
18
,
14
,
11
,
10
,
3
]
) on the existence of Morse decompositions can be obtained as special cases of
Theorem

3.1
.

Application to delay difference equations

Let us consider the difference equation

x_{k+1}=f(x_{k},x_{k-n}),

where
f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{R})
fulfills some feedback conditions as in
[
11
, (
{\bf H}_{\bf 1})
–(
{\bf H}_{\bf 3})
]
, and assume that it has a global attractor – conditions guaranteeing this can be found in
[
11
, Section 5]
or in
[
9
]
.

Let
\mathbb{T}\coloneq\mathbb{Z}
,
\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}\coloneq\mathcal{X}\coloneq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
,
\mathcal{O}\coloneq\{(0,\dots,0)\}
, and
d=\widetilde{d}
be the Euclidean distance. Moreover, let the discrete Lyapunov function
V
(counting the number of sign changes in (
n+1
)-dimensional vectors) and
\mathcal{R}
be defined as in
[
11
, pp. 909–910]
. Note that
(H1)
–
(H4)
are trivially satisfied.

Furthermore,
[
11
, Proposition 3.2]
yields conditions
(V1)
and
(V2)
,
[
11
, Theorem 3.3]
guarantees conditions
(V3)
and
(V4)
(with
T=4n+2
), while Proposition 4.3 of the same paper ensures that
(V5)
holds (where
N^{\ast}
is defined on p. 914, and is closely related to the number of unstable eigenvalues of the linearized equation).

Thus,
Theorem

3.1
applies and yields a Morse decomposition of the global attractor. Moreover, choosing
N_{0}
large enough (namely,
N_{0}>n+1
) trivially yields that
\mathcal{S}_{N_{0}}^{+}
is the empty set, and in that case the Morse decomposition given by
Theorem

3.1
and by
[
11
, Theorem 4.1]
coincide.

Application to cyclic DDEs with constant delay

System (
4.1
) with a constant delay was studied in
[
10
, Section 3]
, where a Morse decomposition was also constructed (see Theorem 3.2 therein), generalizing the corresponding results of Mallet-Paret
[
14
]
and Polner
[
18
]
for scalar equations. Although condition
(D1)
is restrictive, it certainly allows constant delays, so
Theorem

4.1
can be applied.

It is worth noting that in
[
10
]
, boundedness of the corresponding Lyapunov function was also proved, and a Morse set like
\mathcal{S}_{N_{0}}^{+}
was unnecessary in the construction. Now, if we choose
N_{0}
larger than this upper bound, then
Theorem

4.1
gives exactly the same Morse decomposition as in
[
10
]
.

Scalar SDDEs with more general delays

The proof of
Theorem

4.1
uses condition
(D1)
only to verify
(V5)
. The latter condition is shown in a recent paper
[
3
, Lemma 4.4]
for the scalar case with negative feedback, assuming only boundedness and Lipschitz continuity of
\tau
, and strict monotonicity of the delayed argument. This means that – using the same notation as in
Section

4.1
– its main theorem (Theorem 4.2) is also a special case of
Theorem

3.1
.

Possible generalizations

We conjecture that
Theorem

4.1
can be extended to cyclic SDDE systems under more general assumptions on the delay, similarly to the scalar case
[
3
, Theorem 4.2]
. As demonstrated above, the only missing link is to prove condition
(V5)
. This boils down to showing that for any solution
x
with
V(x_{t})
less than a fixed value, there exists a uniform exponential growth bound backward in time (cf. inequality (
4.19
)). This would also imply that nontrivial solutions with finitely many sign changes on any bounded interval are not superexponential (i.e. they do not tend to zero faster than exponentially). Combining this with the results of
[
2
]
(Theorem 3.9 and Section 4.1), one obtains that there are no superexponential solutions on the global attractor. Since the mentioned results of
[
2
]
apply for a wider class of state-dependent delays, it would be desirable to show such growth bounds in this general setting. This seems nontrivial; however, one promising way forward may be to combine the proofs of
[
10
, Proposition 2.8]
, carried out for cyclic DDEs with constant delay, and
[
3
, Lemmas 3.3–3.4]
, which deals with scalar SDDEs. This can serve as a topic of future work.
