Risk factors for surgical site infection after patellar fracture surgery in the elderly
Authors: Hui Qi, Wei Duan, Ruili Jia, Shuai Wang, Ming An, Yubin Long
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatellar fractures are a common knee injury among elderly patients, with a high risk of developing surgical site infections (SSI) postoperatively, which severely affects patient prognosis and quality of life. Elderly patients are more susceptible to SSI due to various factors such as decreased immune function and chronic diseases. Therefore, identifying the risk factors for SSI is of great clinical significance for prevention.ObjectiveThis study aims to analyze the risk factors for postoperative SSI in elderly patients with patellar fractures, providing a basis for developing more effective clinical prevention and treatment strategies.MethodsThis retrospective study collected data from 856 elderly patients who underwent patellar fracture surgery at Baoding First Central Hospital between January 2017 and December 2023. Patients were divided into SSI and non-SSI groups based on the occurrence of SSI, and their demographic data, comorbidities, and laboratory results were analyzed. Logistic regression was used to identify independent risk factors for SSI, and ROC curve analysis was conducted to determine the optimal cutoff point for predictive indicators.ResultsThe incidence of SSI was found to be 2.1%. Univariate analysis showed that BMI, surgical delay, diabetes, hematocrit (HCT), and albumin (ALB) were significantly associated with SSI. Logistic regression analysis further confirmed that BMI (p = 0.043), surgical delay (p = 0.000), HCT (p = 0.038), ALB (p = 0.015), and diabetes (p = 0.022) were independent risk factors for SSI. ROC curve analysis indicated that the optimal cutoff points for BMI, HCT, and ALB were 25.39 kg/m2, 35.62%, and 39.3 g/L, respectively, with an AUC of 0.794 for the combined predictive indicators.ConclusionHigh BMI, surgical delay, diabetes, low HCT, and low ALB are independent risk factors for postoperative SSI in elderly patients with patellar fractures. Preoperative management targeting these high-risk factors, such as optimizing patient weight, controlling diabetes, and improving nutritional status, can effectively reduce the incidence of SSI and improve postoperative outcomes. Future multicenter studies may further validate these findings and provide additional prevention strategies.Keywords: Surgical site infection (SSI), Patellar fracture, Elderly patients, Surgery delay, Albumin, Hematocrit

Introduction

The patella plays an important role in knee joint movement by enhancing the leverage needed for knee extension and also helps protect the joint from external impacts [1]. Patellar fractures are a common knee injury, typically caused by direct impact or powerful muscle contraction, often occurring in falls, car accidents, or sports injuries [2, 3]. Patients usually present with severe knee pain, swelling, and restricted movement, and in severe cases, they may be unable to stand or walk [4]. Treatment of patellar fractures depends on the type and severity of the fracture. For minor fractures where the bone has not been displaced, conservative treatment, such as casting and physical rehabilitation, is usually adopted [5]. For severe or comminuted fractures with significant displacement, surgery may be required to realign and stabilize the bones to ensure proper healing and restoration of knee function [6]. Physical therapy during rehabilitation is also crucial in helping patients regain knee mobility and muscle strength.

However, despite advancements in surgical techniques for patellar fractures, postoperative complications, particularly infections, remain a critical challenge that significantly affects clinical outcomes. Postoperative infections, particularly SSI, are major factors influencing patient prognosis and quality of life. Studies have shown that prolonged surgery time, an unclean surgical environment, and improper wound management are important factors contributing to postoperative infections [7]. SSI not only prolongs hospital stays and increases medical costs but can also lead to reoperation or even permanent functional impairment in severe cases [8]. According to the literature, the incidence of postoperative infections following patellar fracture surgery varies across studies, generally ranging from 1 to 10% [9].

Elderly patients are at high risk of postoperative infection following patellar fractures.In this population, physiological functions gradually deteriorate with age, immunity declines, and the prevalence of underlying diseases such as diabetes and cardiovascular conditions is high, often accompanied by osteoporosis, which significantly increases the risk of postoperative SSI [10]. In elderly patients, SSI not only affects wound healing and functional recovery but also significantly raises the incidence of systemic complications, such as sepsis, pneumonia, and deep vein thrombosis [11]. Therefore, it is of great clinical significance to study the risk factors for postoperative infections in elderly patients with patellar fractures and to understand their specific physiological and pathological characteristics.This will help clinicians implement more targeted preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative preventive measures, thereby reducing infection rates and improving treatment outcomes.

Traditional metallic fixation techniques, such as tension band wiring, have been widely employed in the treatment of patellar fractures. However, these methods are often associated with complications, including hardware failure and infection [12]. In recent years, non-metallic implants, including suture-based fixation techniques, have emerged as an alternative, showing promise in enhancing fracture healing and reducing infection risks [13]. Furthermore, the combination of biological agents such as platelet-rich plasma (PRP) and mesenchymal stem cells (MSC) with non-metallic fixation has been explored as a strategy to promote bone healing, particularly in cases of nonunion [13]. These innovative treatment methods are particularly relevant for elderly patients, who are at a higher risk of postoperative infections following surgical intervention.

Recent biomechanical studies on fixation methods for transverse patellar fractures have highlighted several promising alternatives to traditional metallic hardware. These include non-metallic implants and biologic agents, which have shown potential in improving healing and reducing infection risks [14]. A systematic review of biomechanical studies on transverse olecranon and patellar fractures revealed that fixation hardware failure remains a major challenge, and new fixation techniques, such as non-metallic constructs, may offer a potential solution [14]. These developments are especially relevant for elderly patients, who are at higher risk of postoperative infection, and they highlight the importance of incorporating innovative materials and biological therapies into the treatment of patellar fractures.

The aim of this study is to analyze the risk factors for postoperative infection in elderly patients with patellar fractures, identifying both specific high-risk factors and common factors. This analysis will provide a theoretical basis for clinical practice to help develop more effective prevention and treatment strategies, thereby improving treatment outcomes, reducing postoperative infection rates, and ultimately enhancing the quality of life for elderly patients.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

In this study, we reviewed the electronic medical records of all patients who underwent patellar fracture surgery at our hospital between January 2017 and December 2023 (record number 2024181). The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki. Since this is a retrospective study, informed consent from patients was not required.

This retrospective study was conducted at Baoding First Central Hospital, a tertiary hospital with a Level 1 trauma center. Data were extracted from the electronic medical records of 1,492 patients based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria of this study. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) elderly patients aged ≥ 60 years; (2) patients with a confirmed diagnosis of patellar fracture who underwent surgical treatment; and (3) postoperative follow-up for at least six months with complete clinical data. The exclusion criteria were: (1) patients aged < 60 years; (2) patients with pre-existing infection at the patella or other locations; (3) incomplete postoperative follow-up or patients unable to complete follow-up for other reasons; (4) patients with a confirmed diagnosis of patellar fracture who did not undergo surgical treatment (Fig. 1).

Patients who meet and do not meet the inclusion/exclusion criteria

A total of 856 patients (398 females and 458 males) were included in this study. Based on the presence of SSI, they were divided into an SSI group and a non-SSI group. SSI were classified into three types according to the depth and location of the infection: superficial incisional, deep incisional, and organ/space infections. Superficial incisional infections involve only the skin and subcutaneous tissue and typically present with redness, pain, or purulent discharge. Deep incisional infections extend into the fascia and muscle layers and may be associated with wound dehiscence or abscess formation. Organ/space infections occur in internal organs or body cavities involved in the surgery, often accompanied by abscesses or fluid accumulation. During hospitalization, all patients with surgical site infections were initially treated with antibiotics, either oral or intravenous, depending on the severity of the infection and the type of bacteria. In severe cases, surgical debridement was necessary to remove necrotic tissue or pus to accelerate healing. Additionally, local care of the infection site was crucial, including regular dressing changes, cleaning with sterile solutions, and, if needed, abscess drainage or negative pressure wound therapy to facilitate wound healing (Figs. 2, 3, and 4).

This study reviewed patient demographic data, comorbidities, and laboratory tests conducted at admission. Demographic data included age, sex, BMI, ethnicity, ASA (American Society of Anesthesiologists) score, smoking history, alcohol consumption history, and time from injury to surgery. ASA scores were grouped into two categories: I-II and III-IV. Comorbidities included hypertension, diabetes, arrhythmia, coronary artery disease, stroke, and cerebral infarction.

Immunosuppressive therapy was not included due to limitations in data availability. During univariate analysis, variables such as smoking history, alcohol consumption, coronary artery disease, and cerebrovascular disease were assessed but excluded from the final multivariate model as they did not show statistically significant associations with SSI (p > 0.05). These exclusions ensured that the final model focused on variables with significant and independent effects on SSI risk, minimizing potential confounding and overfitting.

We also analyzed several laboratory parameters: basophils (BAS), eosinophils (EOS), hematocrit (HCT), hemoglobin (HGB), immature erythrocytes (IMM), lymphocytes (LYM), mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC), monocytes (MON), mean platelet volume (MPV), neutrophils (NEU), platelets (PLT), red blood cells (RBC), white blood cells (WBC), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), antithrombin III (AT), D-dimer, fibrin degradation products (FDP), fibrinogen (FIB), thrombin time (TT), albumin (ALB), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), calcium (Ca), potassium (K), sodium (Na), magnesium (Mg), phosphorus (P), chloride (Cl), globulin (GLOB), cholinesterase (CHE), creatine kinase (CK), creatinine (CREA), direct bilirubin (DBIL), glucose (GLU), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), total carbon dioxide (TCO2), urea (UREA), and uric acid (UA). All these laboratory markers were collected at admission.

Diagnosis of SSI

The diagnosis of surgical site infection (SSI) followed the criteria established by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) guidelines for SSI [15]. The criteria included at least one of the following:

For deep incisional or organ/space infections, additional confirmation using imaging modalities, such as ultrasound or CT scans, was employed to detect abscess formation or fluid accumulation [16].

SSI diagnoses were performed by a multidisciplinary team that included six orthopedic surgeons with an average of 15 years of clinical experience (range: 10–20 years) and two infection control specialists trained in hospital-acquired infection management. Diagnoses were reviewed during weekly team meetings to ensure consistency and accuracy. For cases with ambiguous clinical presentations, decisions were made by consensus, incorporating additional opinions from infection control specialists.

SSI cases were diagnosed within 30 days postoperatively for non-implant surgeries. For implant-related surgeries, cases diagnosed within one year of surgery were included, in line with the extended diagnostic period recommended by CDC guidelines [15]. This standardized approach, coupled with the involvement of a highly experienced team, ensures consistency, reliability, and reproducibility in identifying SSI cases.

Statistical analysis

This study used SPSS software (version 25.0, SPSS Inc., New York, USA), with a p-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess normality for continuous variables. When normality was confirmed, these variables were analyzed using the t-test and expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). However, if the data did not follow a normal distribution, the Mann–Whitney U test was used for statistical analysis between the two groups. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test and Fisher's exact test, and were expressed as numbers and percentages. Additionally, to identify the best predictors of SSI, binary logistic regression analysis was employed to determine the independent predictors of postoperative SSI in elderly patients with patellar fractures. Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis was routinely used to determine the optimal cutoff values for continuous variables (such as BMI), categorized into low-risk and high-risk based on the maximum Youden index (sensitivity + specificity−1). Diagnostic ability was assessed by the area under the ROC curve (AUC), ranging from 0 to 100%, with larger areas indicating stronger diagnostic capability.

Results

In this retrospective study of 856 elderly patients who underwent patellar fracture surgery, the incidence of SSI was 2.1%. Univariate analysis identified several predictors significantly associated with SSI, including BMI, time from injury to surgery, diabetes, HCT, and ALB. Logistic regression analysis further revealed that these factors were independently associated with the occurrence of SSI. ROC curve analysis determined the optimal cutoff points for predicting SSI: BMI of 25.39 kg/m2, time from injury to surgery of 6 days, HCT of 35.62%, and ALB of 39.3 g/L. The combination of these parameters significantly increased the AUC to 0.794, indicating that the combined predictive model had better diagnostic accuracy than any single parameter.

As shown in Table 1, there were significant differences between the SSI group and the non-SSI group in terms of BMI (P = 0.016), time from injury to surgery (P = 0.002), and diabetes (P = 0.017). The BMI level in the SSI group was higher compared to the non-SSI group. The time from injury to surgery was longer in the SSI group. Additionally, the proportion of patients with diabetes was higher in the SSI group. There were no significant differences between the two groups regarding age, sex, ethnicity, ASA score, history of drug allergies, open fractures, multiple fractures, smoking, alcohol consumption, history of hypertension, heart disease, or cerebral infarction (all P > 0.05).

Logistic regression analysis showed that BMI (P = 0.043, OR = 1.16, 95% CI 1.005–1.340), time from injury to surgery (P = 0.000, OR = 1.134, 95% CI 1.059–1.216), HCT (P = 0.038, OR = 0.929, 95% CI 0.866–0.996), ALB (P = 0.015, OR = 0.919, 95% CI 0.858–0.984), and diabetes (P = 0.022, OR = 3.009, 95% CI 1.170–7.737) were closely associated with the occurrence of SSI after surgery for patellar fractures in elderly patients (see Table 4).

ROC curve analysis determined the optimal cutoff points for BMI, time from injury to surgery, HCT, and ALB as 25.39 kg/m2, 6 days, 35.62%, and 39.3 g/L, respectively. For example, a BMI above 25.39 kg/m2 was categorized as high risk for SSI, determined by balancing sensitivity and specificity through ROC curve analysis.

The AUC values for these biomarkers were as follows: BMI alone (P = 0.020, AUC: 0.665, 95% CI 0.633–0.697), time from injury to surgery alone (P = 0.001, AUC: 0.716, 95% CI 0.684–0.746), HCT alone (P = 0.003, AUC: 0.661, 95% CI 0.628–0.692), and ALB alone (P = 0.003, AUC: 0.688, 95% CI 0.655–0.719). These individual AUC values indicate moderate diagnostic accuracy, with time from injury to surgery having the highest individual AUC.

When these parameters were combined, the AUC increased significantly, achieving a value of 0.794 (95% CI 0.765–0.821), demonstrating strong predictive utility for SSI risk. This combined model suggests that integrating multiple independent predictors enhances diagnostic accuracy and provides a more reliable tool for clinical risk stratification.

Discussion

Elderly individuals are susceptible to osteoporosis, reduced muscle strength, and degenerative joint disease, which are physiological changes associated with aging [17]. In addition, slower reaction times, chronic diseases, and the effects of medications increase the risk of falls, while vitamin D deficiency leads to inadequate bone calcification, further weakening the bones [18, 19]. These factors collectively make elderly individuals more prone to lower limb fractures from falls or minor trauma. One effective treatment for lower limb fractures, such as patellar fractures, is surgery. However, factors such as the surgical environment, duration, type of surgery, and the quality of postoperative care may increase the risk of SSI [20–22]. SSI is one of the common complications after patellar fracture surgery and can lead to prolonged recovery, poor wound healing, infection spread, increased risk of reoperation, and in severe cases, it can be life-threatening. Studies investigating SSI prevalence in patients undergoing knee surgery have reported rates ranging from 0.4 to 5%, consistent with our findings [23]. Given the serious consequences and poor prognosis associated with SSI, we believe it is necessary to analyze its predictive factors and take measures to prevent its occurrence. While most existing research focuses on the treatment and complications of patellar fractures, there is relatively little research specifically on postoperative SSI. Therefore, this study aims to analyze the risk factors associated with SSI after patellar fracture surgery in elderly patients, with the goal of providing a basis for preventing and reducing SSI incidence in this population.

This study conducted a retrospective analysis of elderly patients with patellar fractures, finding that the incidence of postoperative SSI was 2.1%. Univariate analysis identified several predictive factors associated with the SSI group, including BMI, time from injury to surgery, HCT, diabetes, and ALB. Logistic regression analysis showed that increased BMI, longer time from injury to surgery, a history of diabetes, and reduced levels of ALB and HCT were all closely associated with the occurrence of SSI after patellar fracture surgery in elderly patients. ROC curve analysis determined the optimal cutoff points for predicting SSI: BMI of 25.39, time from injury to surgery of 6 days, HCT of 35.62%, and ALB of 39.3 g/L. When used alone, the AUC values for these indicators were as follows: BMI (0.665), time from injury to surgery (0.716), HCT (0.661), and ALB (0.688). When these parameters were combined, the AUC increased significantly, indicating higher diagnostic accuracy. The combined model, including BMI, time from injury to surgery, HCT, and ALB, achieved an AUC of 0.794, highlighting its superior utility in predicting SSI. These findings emphasize the critical importance of identifying SSI risk factors to prevent its occurrence and manage associated complications.

In this study, we found a correlation between increased BMI and the formation of postoperative SSI. The patellar region has limited soft tissue, and although patients with high BMI have more adipose tissue, it provides limited protection at the patellar site, increasing the risk of localized infection [24]. Additionally, during surgery, patients with high BMI have restricted surgical fields, increasing the difficulty of the procedure and often leading to longer surgery times, which heightens the risk of bacterial contamination [25]. Internal fixation devices are commonly used after patellar fracture surgery, and poor healing of adipose tissue in patients with high BMI makes them more prone to periprosthetic infections. A meta-analysis of total knee arthroplasty showed that obese patients have a higher rate of revision for any reason, with an odds ratio of 1.30 (95% CI, 1.02–1.67) [26], which is consistent with our findings. Furthermore, drug metabolism and distribution in patients with high BMI differ from those of normal-weight individuals, particularly affecting the distribution of antibiotics in tissues, influenced by the fat-to-receptor ratio. The prophylactic antibiotics administered preoperatively may not achieve sufficient concentrations in the patella and surrounding tissues, reducing the effectiveness of infection prevention [27]. Therefore, we recommend that elderly patients with patellar fractures, particularly those with high BMI, adopt more proactive infection prevention measures, such as preoperative weight management optimization, appropriate adjustment of antibiotic dosages, and enhanced postoperative wound care, to reduce SSI incidence and improve postoperative outcomes. The application of these preventive strategies is crucial for the recovery of elderly patients.

Following the discussion of BMI's impact on postoperative SSI, another critical factor is the potential impact of time from injury to surgery on infection risk. The time from injury to surgery significantly influences the occurrence of postoperative SSI. Delayed surgery may result in prolonged exposure of the fracture site to an unstable condition, increasing local tissue edema and poor blood circulation, thus creating favorable conditions for bacterial growth [28]. A study found that the delay in debridement for patients with severe open fractures was closely associated with the incidence of deep infection, with a 3% increase in infection risk for each hour of delay [29]. Furthermore, delayed surgery may increase the length of hospital stay and overall infection risk, especially in elderly patients, whose weakened immune function and diminished tissue repair capacity make them more susceptible to external infection factors [30]. Therefore, early surgery helps reduce infection risk by shortening exposure time and lowering SSI incidence. Timely surgery is particularly crucial for elderly patients with patellar fractures to minimize postoperative complications.

Diabetes is also closely related to the occurrence of postoperative SSI. First, hyperglycemia affects the phagocytic and bactericidal functions of white blood cells, reducing the body's resistance to bacteria and increasing the risk of postoperative infection [31]. Additionally, microvascular complications caused by diabetes severely impair blood supply around the knee joint, particularly in the patellar region. Poor local blood flow results in wound hypoxia, affecting tissue repair and delaying healing, which increases opportunities for bacterial growth [32]. Studies have also shown that under hyperglycemic conditions, the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) signaling pathway is inhibited, reducing collagen expression, which impairs the migration of skin epithelial cells and further delays wound healing [33]. Diabetic neuropathy patients often experience sensory loss due to nerve compression, leading to diminished postoperative pain perception, which may delay the identification and treatment of infection symptoms, thereby worsening the severity of postoperative infection [34]. These factors make diabetic patients more susceptible to SSI following patellar fracture surgery, highlighting the importance of perioperative management and infection prevention for diabetic patients.

Our study found that low HCT is an independent risk factor for SSI. First, low HCT levels are often associated with anemia, which can impair immune function and reduce the body's resistance to infection [35]. Adequate hemoglobin for oxygen delivery is crucial for tissue healing during and after surgery. When HCT levels are too low, insufficient tissue oxygenation may lead to poor wound healing, thereby increasing the risk of SSI. A study found that low hematocrit was significantly associated with postoperative complications and mortality in cardiac surgery [36]. Elderly patients are particularly prone to anemia, and reduced HCT can lead to delayed wound healing and increased infection risk [37]. Secondly, fluctuations in HCT levels may be related to the patient's overall health and nutritional status. Elderly patients often face malnutrition and systemic inflammatory responses (e.g., elevated IL-6 levels) during postoperative recovery, which may further decrease HCT, creating a vicious cycle and increasing infection risk [38]. Lastly, studies have indicated that abnormal HCT levels may also suggest underlying cardiovascular disease or other comorbidities, which themselves are independent risk factors for SSI [39]. Therefore, in analyzing the risk factors for postoperative SSI in elderly patients with patellar fractures, HCT, as an easily accessible and clinically valuable indicator, should be given sufficient attention in both preoperative screening and postoperative management.

ALB, as a common indicator of nutritional status and systemic inflammatory response, plays a significant role in assessing the risk of surgical site infection (SSI) after patellar fracture surgery. This study found that low ALB levels are significantly associated with the occurrence of SSI in elderly patients postoperatively. A study of 524 patients undergoing gastrointestinal surgery showed that patients with preoperative hypoalbuminemia are more likely to develop deep wound infections postoperatively. Patients with albumin levels below 30 mg/dL are particularly prone to deep SSIs, consistent with previous studies [40]. Low albumin levels reflect preoperative malnutrition and underlying chronic inflammatory states in patients, leading to impaired immune function and an increased risk of infection [41]. Malnutrition is particularly common in elderly patients, who often have multiple comorbidities, further exacerbating the likelihood of poor postoperative healing [42]. Therefore, preoperative assessment of nutritional status and providing necessary nutritional support may help reduce the incidence of SSI. Additionally, ALB levels can serve as a relatively simple biomarker, providing clinical reference for the early identification and intervention of high-risk patients.

Although this study presents some new findings, its limitations should be acknowledged. First, this is a single-center study, and the results may be influenced by regional factors. Therefore, large-sample, multi-center prospective clinical studies are needed in the future to validate the results of this study and improve the external validity of the conclusions. Additionally, since this is a retrospective study, although many variables potentially related to SSI were included, data on certain factors, such as dementia, incontinence, surgical incision length, and the type of internal fixation materials or prostheses, were not comprehensively reported, which may have led to residual confounding factors, potentially impacting the results. Lastly, the study involved over a dozen trauma orthopedic surgeons, each with different clinical judgments regarding surgical methods and implant selection, possibly leading to inconsistencies in the surgical process. Therefore, future studies should adopt more rigorous control strategies, potentially incorporating machine learning methods to minimize the impact of confounding bias and more accurately assess the relevant risk factors. Additionally, the follow-up period in this study was relatively short, and long-term efficacy evaluation and continuous monitoring of complications are also necessary directions for future research.

In conclusion, we observed an SSI rate of 2.1% among the studied patients. High BMI, delayed surgery time, diabetes, and low HCT were identified as independent risk factors for SSI after patellar fracture surgery in elderly patients, and lower ALB levels were also significantly associated with SSI occurrence. Targeted preoperative management, such as assessing patients' BMI, surgical timing, diabetes control, and nutritional status, along with implementing appropriate preventive measures, is necessary and should be given high priority in elderly patients with patellar fractures. Future multi-center studies will further evaluate more patients and are expected to better identify and address the SSI risk factors in this patient population.
