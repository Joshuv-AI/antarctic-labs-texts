Multicenter evaluation of four large language models for automated spine imaging diagnosis
Authors: Haolai Liu, Hao Zhang, Haixin Wei, Yao Wei, Xiaowen Liu, Yuanrun Wei, Tao Liu, Xiaoyu Tan, Xuexiao Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate interpretation of spine imaging is essential for clinical decision-making, yet the diagnostic potential of large language models (LLMs) for radiological report analysis remains inadequately evaluated in terms of sample size, multi-model comparison, reproducibility, and cross-institutional generalisability. Here, we conducted a multicentre cohort study using 20,277 authentic clinical spine radiological reports from three teaching hospitals in China, systematically comparing the diagnostic performance, output consistency, and generalisability of four LLMs—GPT-4o, Claude-4, Qwen-3 Max, and DeepSeek-V3.1—across nine modality–region combinations under two input modes (with-option and without-option). All models showed high overall diagnostic performance, with specificity exceeding 90% and negative predictive value exceeding 96%. Cross-institutional validation demonstrated stable recall generalisability, with recall coefficients of variation ranging from 0.7% to 5.0%. However, performance was uneven across the disease spectrum: for low-prevalence conditions, precision declined by 19–42 percentage points, indicating a persistent long-tail diagnostic deficit. Input mode and prompt formulation also produced model-specific shifts in diagnostic behaviour. These findings suggest that LLMs may support report-based spine imaging diagnosis as clinical assistive tools, but deployment should account for disease prevalence, prompt sensitivity, and the need for domain-specific optimisation.

Introduction

Spinal disorders constitute a major global health burden, affecting ~619 million individuals worldwide and representing one of the leading causes of disability1. Accurate interpretation of spine imaging is essential for diagnosis, treatment planning, and surgical decision-making, yet this process is exceedingly complex. Spine image interpretation spans multiple anatomical regions, imaging modalities (radiography, CT, and MRI), and a broad disease spectrum, imposing a substantial cognitive workload on radiologists. With population ageing and increasing clinical demands, the risk of missed diagnosis and misdiagnosis has risen considerably2,3.

In the current landscape of artificial intelligence-assisted radiological diagnosis, mainstream research has focused on employing deep learning (DL) and machine learning (ML) models to process raw imaging data directly for tasks such as fracture detection4, disc segmentation5,6, and tumour identification7,8. However, with the rapid advancement of large language models (LLMs, e.g. GPT-4o, Claude) and their impressive performance on medical licensing examinations such as the United States Medical Licensing Examination (USMLE)9, LLMs have demonstrated considerable potential in assisting the generation of diagnostic impressions from imaging findings10,11. This “text-to-diagnosis” paradigm offers distinct advantages: it entails a low technical barrier, facilitating deployment across hospitals at all levels; and text-based inputs are readily amenable to data de-identification, thereby satisfying medical data privacy requirements.

However, several critical gaps persist in the existing literature on LLM-assisted radiology report diagnosis. Most evaluations have relied on small sample sizes with artificially constructed questions rather than authentic clinical reports12,13; moreover, systematic head-to-head comparisons of multiple LLMs on the same dataset are lacking14. Reproducibility has been largely overlooked, with most studies reporting single-run results from single-centre designs15,16, and the impact of disease prevalence on model performance remains inadequately evaluated17.

To address these gaps, we conducted a multicentre cohort study encompassing more than 20,000 authentic clinical spine radiological reports from three independent medical centres, covering three anatomical regions (C-spine, T-spine, and L-spine) and three imaging modalities (radiography, CT, and MRI). We systematically compared the performance of four LLMs—GPT-4o, Claude-4, Qwen-3 Max, and DeepSeek-V3.1—across the dimensions of diagnostic accuracy, reproducibility, and cross-institutional generalisability.

Results

Cohort characteristics

A total of 20,277 spine radiological reports from three independent medical centres were included in this study. Centre 1 (Affiliated Hospital of Qingdao University) contributed 19,307 cases, covering all nine modality–region combinations; Centre 2 (Qilu Hospital of Shandong University) contributed 667 cases; and Centre 3 (Shanghai Changzheng Hospital) contributed 303 cases, both comprising MRI data exclusively. Within the Centre 1 sample, lumbar spine examinations accounted for the largest proportion (11,723 cases, 60.7%), followed by the cervical spine (4984 cases, 25.8%) and the thoracic spine (2600 cases, 13.5%). Overall diagnostic performance across models

Under the with-option mode, diagnostic performance differed significantly among the four models (Fig. 1; Table 2). In terms of precision, GPT-4o (82.52%) and Claude (82.29%) performed comparably, with no statistically significant difference between them (P = 1.000), yet both were significantly higher than Qwen (75.37%) and DeepSeek (70.47%) (P < 0.001; Supplementary Table S3). In terms of recall, Qwen ranked highest at 90.45%, significantly exceeding Claude (88.29%), GPT-4o (87.34%), and DeepSeek (85.14%); all pairwise differences reached statistical significance (P < 0.001). Performance across anatomical regions and imaging modalities

Predictive performance varied across anatomical regions and imaging modalities (Fig. 3). Under the with-option mode, a consistent pattern emerged across anatomical regions: GPT-4o and Claude alternated in leading precision, while Qwen achieved the highest recall across all three regions. For the lumbar spine—the most represented region in this study—Claude (87.07%) and GPT-4o (85.43%) exhibited comparable precision, both exceeding 85%, whereas Qwen had a lower precision (78.47%) but the highest recall (88.63%). In the cervical spine, GPT-4o achieved the highest precision (78.25%), above Claude (71.62%), Qwen (68.26%), and DeepSeek (67.54%). In the thoracic spine, Claude attained the highest precision (80.93%), surpassing the second-ranked GPT-4o (75.65%) by 5.3 percentage points. Regarding imaging modality, GPT-4o and Claude demonstrated comparable precision across all three modalities (radiography: 79.66% vs 79.12%; CT: 83.96% vs 84.39%; MRI: 83.51% vs 83.23%), while Qwen maintained the highest recall across modalities (radiography 93.73%, CT 92.17%, MRI 88.50%). Stratified analysis by disease prevalence

Stratified analysis was performed according to disease prevalence (Fig. 5; Supplementary Table S2; Supplementary Fig. S1). Under the with-option mode, all models performed robustly for high-prevalence conditions, with precision ranging from 70.47% to 84.76% and recall from 86.95% to 92.53%. In contrast, performance declined substantially for low-prevalence conditions (prevalence <5%): precision dropped by 19–42 percentage points and recall by 23–29 percentage points relative to high-prevalence conditions, underscoring a persistent performance bottleneck of general-purpose LLMs for conditions situated in the tail of the long-tail distribution. Cross-institutional validation

Under the with-option mode, performance between Centre 1 and Centre 2 was highly consistent: precision for each model showed close agreement (Claude 83.23% vs 83.54%; GPT-4o 83.51% vs 83.37%), and recall differences were less than 2 percentage points. Centre 3 exhibited a decline in precision of ~10–14 percentage points across models (Claude 73.18%, GPT-4o 72.11%, Qwen 63.62%, DeepSeek 58.96%), while recall remained at relatively high levels (76.72–90.95%). Prompt design sensitivity and ablation analyses

To evaluate the impact of prompt design on LLM diagnostic behaviour, we conducted ablation and sensitivity experiments using a randomly sampled subset of 200 lumbar MRI reports. When the system role was modified from a neutral “radiologist” to an expert “spine surgeon with 20 years of experience,” precision decreased across all models, whereas recall increased in most models (Supplementary Table S4).

Furthermore, we observed a measurable susceptibility to option positional bias (Supplementary Table S5). Repositioning highly prevalent diseases from the front to the middle of the option list resulted in a widespread decline in precision, with absolute reductions ranging from 6.0 to 19.9 percentage points. This perturbation effect was heterogeneous across pathologies: conditions requiring multi-sentence contextual reasoning—such as spinal stenosis—demonstrated significant sensitivity to positional shifts, whereas keyword-distinct entities, such as disc herniation, remained unaffected.

Our findings also suggest that mitigating long-tail performance deficits solely through explicit prompt engineering can introduce unintended consequences. Augmenting the prompt with direct instructions to focus on rare conditions (such as infection or space-occupying lesions) successfully elevated target sensitivity, but simultaneously triggered what we term “prompt-induced confirmation bias” (Supplementary Table S6). This resulted in a disproportionate increase in false positives—for instance, GPT-4o generated 77 infection diagnoses for 5 true cases—substantially deteriorating overall precision. Similarly, incorporating specific disease names as exemplars in the without-option mode degraded global precision without yielding statistically significant gains in target sensitivity (Supplementary Table S7).

Discussion

This study systematically evaluated the performance of four mainstream LLMs in spine radiological report diagnosis. Overall, all models demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy, yet notable differences and limitations were also identified under specific scenarios.

The four models exhibited two distinct diagnostic strategy orientations. Under both with-option and without-option modes, GPT-4o and Claude consistently favoured a precision-oriented strategy, demonstrating stronger confirmatory capability in positive predictions, with leading positive predictive accuracy among all models. Conversely, Qwen and DeepSeek consistently displayed a recall-oriented strategy, exhibiting higher sensitivity in abnormality screening. Qwen, in particular, sustained the highest abnormality detection rate across anatomical regions and input modes, thereby minimising the risk of missed diagnosis. This finding carries direct clinical implications: based on the observed performance profiles, we propose that in scenarios demanding high sensitivity, such as primary screening alerts and emergency triage, recall-oriented models can serve as an effective primary diagnostic safeguard18, with the additional verification cost incurred by their higher false positive rate being absorbed during subsequent specialist review; whereas in scenarios requiring high specificity, such as preoperative planning, MDT preparation, and structured discharge summary generation, precision-oriented models represented by Claude or GPT-4o may be more appropriate, offering high output stability that effectively prevents the injection of hallucinations or erroneous coding into EHR systems19. Accordingly, clinical deployment may benefit from the selective implementation of models with differing characteristics according to specific workflow nodes and diagnostic requirements, rather than pursuing a single universal solution.

Regarding output consistency, most models demonstrated high test–retest reliability. Claude achieved a κ of 0.996, corresponding to almost perfect agreement; GPT-4o (κ = 0.929) and Qwen (κ = 0.946) exhibited equally strong stability. Notably, DeepSeek displayed an atypical performance pattern: its κ under with-option mode (0.676) was comparatively low, yet paradoxically increased to 0.731 upon switching to without-option mode. Under the conventional prompt learning paradigm, structurally constrained outputs are generally expected to exhibit greater stability than open-domain generation; however, the performance of DeepSeek deviated from this expectation. The precise mechanism underlying this observation remains unclear and warrants further investigation. We hypothesise that this phenomenon may be related to the model’s reinforcement learning alignment strategy: recent studies have suggested that reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) can alter model behaviour in constrained tasks, potentially affecting calibration and output diversity in ways that diverge from pre-alignment characteristics20–23. If this hypothesis holds, it is conceivable that DeepSeek’s intrinsic alignment may favour free-text representation, and that imposing rigid option constraints could potentially interfere with its implicit reasoning pathways—though this interpretation remains speculative and requires dedicated mechanistic investigation to confirm. This preliminary observation highlights a broader consideration for deploying general-purpose LLMs in specialised clinical domains: intrinsic alignment mechanisms may introduce unanticipated perturbations to output consistency in highly constrained task formats, an issue that merits systematic exploration in future LLM interpretability research.

Our ablation analyses demonstrate that meticulously refining prompt architecture plays a crucial and often determinative role in optimizing the diagnostic performance of LLMs. As demonstrated in our results, even minor modifications to the prompt architecture—such as elevating the systemic role from a neutral “radiologist” to a highly specialized “spine surgeon”—induced statistically significant shifts in the precision–recall balance across all models. This suggests that injecting an expert persona may inadvertently lower the model’s internal diagnostic threshold, generating a more liberal but less specific diagnostic profile. While such a profile might occasionally benefit high-sensitivity screening, it compromises the strict precision required for confirmatory diagnosis.

Furthermore, the perturbation of option sequences revealed a measurable susceptibility to positional bias. Repositioning highly prevalent diseases away from the top of the option list led to a consistent decline in precision. The heterogeneous responses observed across different models—ranging from precision–recall divergence to concurrent decline—potentially reflect intrinsic differences in their underlying alignment strategies. Crucially, this positional bias selectively targeted complex pathologies requiring the integration of diffuse, multi-sentence contextual reasoning (such as spinal stenosis), whereas keyword-distinct entities (such as disc herniation) remained largely unaffected. This differential susceptibility indicates that LLMs rely heavily on option sequencing as a cognitive anchor when reasoning demands are high, while straightforward keyword matching is more robust against structural perturbations.

This pattern suggests that, in the absence of structured option anchoring, models tend to adopt a more conservative output strategy for low-prevalence diagnostic entities with higher uncertainty, thereby reducing over-diagnosis tendency whilst substantially increasing the risk of missed diagnosis24. An alternative explanation is that the expanded lexical search space in without-option mode may dilute the model’s attention to low-prevalence entities, further compounding the detection deficit. This inverse precision–recall trade-off carries important implications for clinical deployment: in screening scenarios targeting low-prevalence, high-risk conditions, without-option mode may not represent the optimal choice; structured constraints may instead help preserve model sensitivity to rare conditions.

Our ablation study demonstrated that forcing the model to focus on low prevalence diseases using explicit instructions (e.g., “pay attention to signs of infection and space-occupying lesions”) is not a viable solution. Although this approach successfully increased the detection rate for these targeted conditions, it simultaneously caused the model to over-diagnose them drastically, generating up to 15 times more false positives than true cases. If deployed clinically, this tendency to over-report rare diseases would lead to severe alert fatigue for physicians, while causing unnecessary psychological stress and follow-up testing for patients25.

Consequently, if LLMs are to be applied in clinical practice for screening low-prevalence, high-risk conditions, domain-specific fine-tuning or RAG remains necessary for targeted optimisation, or, at the current stage, appropriate human oversight should be incorporated to compensate for the performance deficit of models in the tail of the disease distribution.

Furthermore, the present study revealed that switching between task modes resulted in substantial differences in discriminative ability (AUC) for individual models. Upon transitioning from with-option to without-option mode, the AUC for GPT-4o increased significantly (+5.4%), rising to the highest rank. This observation carries important implications for clinical application: for certain leading proprietary LLMs such as GPT-4o, permitting unconstrained generation may activate higher-order feature synthesis capability, rendering them suitable for complex clinical case summarisation; whereas for comparatively smaller open-source models, reliable deployment in primary healthcare settings necessitates the imposition of rigorous structured templates to constrain their outputs.

We assessed the impact of including disease name exemplars in the prompt. We found that providing specific examples failed to meaningfully improve diagnosis. Although exemplars slightly increased sensitivity for certain conditions, this small benefit was entirely negated by a significant decline in overall precision. The inclusion of explicit disease names acts as a form of diagnostic priming, encouraging the model to report these conditions even when the clinical evidence is weak. These findings demonstrate that even seemingly benign prompt modifications can inadvertently prime the model toward false positives, highlighting the critical importance of neutral and meticulously controlled prompt design in clinical LLM applications.

The clinical deployment of LLM-assisted diagnostic systems necessitates a careful consideration of cloud-based versus on-premises architectures, each presenting distinct advantages and trade-offs. Cloud-based API deployment offers access to full-scale models with hundreds of billions to trillions of parameters, superior performance in complex reasoning tasks, continuous vendor-driven updates, and minimal institutional infrastructure requirements. However, transmitting radiological reports containing sensitive patient information to external servers poses significant regulatory challenges under healthcare data privacy laws, requiring rigorous de-identification preprocessing. Network dependency also introduces potential single points of failure that may compromise clinical continuity of care.

Conversely, on-premises deployment provides physical data isolation that fundamentally resolves data leakage and compliance concerns, with stable and predictable response latencies suitable for high-throughput clinical scenarios. For high-volume tertiary hospitals, the marginal cost approaches zero after the initial hardware investment. However, current hardware constraints typically limit locally deployed models to 7–70 billion parameters, potentially underperforming cloud-based flagship models on highly complex diagnostic reasoning tasks26. Initial hardware procurement costs may reach hundreds of thousands of US dollars, and hospitals require dedicated technical personnel for model deployment, quantisation, acceleration, and version maintenance. As of May 2026, the break-even point—where cumulative on-premises hardware costs equal cumulative cloud API expenses—for a typical tertiary hospital is estimated at ~240 years. However, this threshold varies substantially not only with institutional imaging volume but also with the scale of the initial hardware procurement. The optimal deployment strategy likely depends on institutional factors including data volume, regulatory environment, available technical expertise, and budget constraints, and hybrid approaches combining cloud-based processing with local de-identification may offer a pragmatic intermediate solution.

Cross-institutional generalisability assessment revealed highly consistent diagnostic performance across models between Centre 1 and Centre 2. However, upon transfer to Centre 3, precision for all models declined by 10–14 percentage points, whilst recall remained stable at high levels (cross-centre recall CV as low as 0.7–5.0%). We propose that the core mechanism underlying cross-institutional precision variability is closely related to differences in disease spectrum composition across healthcare institutions. Analysis of actual disease frequency distributions from MRI data at the three centres showed that the proportion of low-prevalence complex conditions was highly homogeneous between Centre 1 (15.55%) and Centre 2 (14.54%); in contrast, Centre 3—a nationally renowned tertiary referral centre that receives complex and rare cases from across the country—had a significantly higher proportion of low-prevalence, long-tail conditions (25.29%), exhibiting a pronounced enrichment trend compared with conventional tertiary teaching hospitals. In line with the aforementioned long-tail effect, when the proportion of long-tail conditions increases sharply, the model’s pre-existing parameter distribution is challenged, resulting in an increase in false positives due to insufficient confidence. These findings indicate that, when general-purpose LLMs are deployed across hospitals of varying levels, their fundamental recall function as a high-sensitivity primary screening barrier demonstrates high robustness; however, perturbations arising from institution-specific rare disease spectrum frequencies readily induce an over-diagnosis tendency, resulting in appreciable loss of precision.

Notably, the LLMs evaluated in the present study demonstrated strong diagnostic capability on authentic clinical reports from three high-level hospitals, with overall precision for GPT-4o and Claude exceeding 82%, specificity exceeding 95%, and NPV exceeding 96%. This finding carries substantial public health significance. In China, quality healthcare resources are unevenly distributed, and the diagnostic experience and proficiency of radiologists at primary hospitals vary considerably. Deploying adequately validated LLM-assisted systems to primary healthcare institutions could substantially improve the accuracy of imaging diagnosis at the primary care level, reduce miss rates and misdiagnosis rates, and alleviate workforce shortages. Furthermore, open-source models such as Qwen demonstrate irreplaceable advantages in safeguarding local data sovereignty and reducing deployment costs, with particularly strong cross-centre recall consistency (CV 0.7%), confirming their potential for application in underserved healthcare settings. Future studies should explore the deployment of LLM-assisted diagnosis in primary healthcare settings to validate these findings in real-world clinical workflows.

This study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective study, although it encompassed more than 20,000 authentic reports from three centres, it lacked intervention testing within a prospective clinical workflow and therefore could not quantify the dynamic impact of models on physicians’ actual decision-making time and efficiency. Second, the analysis was limited to Chinese-language radiological report texts; extrapolation of these findings to other linguistic systems should be approached with caution. Third, this study evaluated the capacity of LLMs to re-interpret text reports already generated by radiologists, rather than the end-to-end diagnostic capability of multimodal large language models (MLLMs) reading DICOM images directly; accordingly, the performance ceiling is constrained by the descriptive accuracy of the original reporting radiologist. Fourth, the reference standard was derived from the original clinical reports rather than from an independent re-reading of the source images by a blinded expert panel. Although this approach is a well-established convention in large-scale retrospective radiology studies and was supplemented by systematic quality control measures, it remains inherently constrained by the diagnostic accuracy of the original reporting radiologists. Fifth, this study did not include conventional NLP classifiers (e.g., fine-tuned BERT or BioBERT) or radiology-tailored machine learning models as baselines. This was a deliberate design choice, as our primary objective was to evaluate the zero-shot diagnostic capability of general-purpose LLMs—a fundamentally different paradigm from supervised classification that requires domain-specific training data. A direct comparison between these paradigms would be methodologically complex, as differences in performance could reflect either intrinsic model capability or the availability and quality of training data. Moreover, the landscape of high-performing conventional NLP models is itself diverse and fragmented, making the selection of a single representative baseline a non-trivial decision. Sixth, the diagnostic outputs of general-purpose LLMs are inherently sensitive to prompt design, as demonstrated by our ablation analyses. Minor perturbations in prompt architecture or option sequencing can induce significant fluctuations in the precision–recall balance. Furthermore, the closed-source nature of proprietary models obscures their underlying training corpora and alignment strategies, limiting our ability to definitively explain the mechanistic drivers behind these vulnerabilities or guarantee consistent performance across future, unannounced model updates.

Future research should focus on the following directions: conducting a randomised controlled crossover study comparing the best-performing models with radiologists to quantify the benefits of human–AI collaboration on diagnostic time and agreement; exploring technical approaches such as RAG to mitigate performance degradation for low-prevalence conditions; and advancing the transition from text-only parsing to end-to-end multimodal medical models that integrate raw imaging data.

In summary, this multicentre evaluation showed that general-purpose LLMs achieved promising diagnostic performance on real-world spine radiology reports, while displaying distinct model-specific profiles. Proprietary models such as GPT-4o and Claude tended to show higher precision and output stability, whereas open-source models such as Qwen showed higher recall, suggesting potential utility in screening-oriented workflows. However, diagnostic reliability declined for low-prevalence long-tail conditions, and additional prompt-sensitivity analyses showed that simple prompt engineering may improve target recall at the cost of increased false positives. Cross-institutional validation further indicated that recall was relatively reproducible, whereas precision varied with institutional case mix and disease spectrum. These findings suggest that LLMs may serve as assistive tools for report-based spine imaging diagnosis, but clinical deployment should be preceded by workflow-specific validation, domain-specific optimisation, and human oversight.

Methods

Study design and data sources

This study was reported in accordance with the STARD-2015 reporting guideline for AI-centred diagnostic accuracy studies. Data were sourced from three tertiary teaching hospitals in China: Centre 1, the Affiliated Hospital of Qingdao University; Centre 2, Qilu Hospital of Shandong University (Qingdao); and Centre 3, Shanghai Changzheng Hospital. Spine radiological reports spanning a 10-year period (1 October 2015 to 1 October 2025) were randomly sampled at each centre, with no overlap between datasets. The Centre 1 cohort served for comprehensive evaluation of LLM performance across anatomical regions and imaging modalities; the Centre 2 and Centre 3 cohorts were dedicated to cross-institutional validation using MRI data, to examine model generalisability across different healthcare institutions. Data from the three centres were analysed entirely independently and were not pooled.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: routine spine imaging examinations (radiography, CT, and MRI for Centre 1; MRI only for Centres 2 and 3); availability of a complete diagnostic report; coverage of the cervical, thoracic, or lumbar spine; and adult patients (aged ≥18 years). The exclusion criteria were incomplete report information and paediatric cases (aged <18 years). To avoid sample-level bias from repeated imaging, we implemented a de-duplication protocol ensuring one report per patient: (1) matching unique patient IDs within each centre, and (2) matching names and dates of birth across centres. For cross-centre duplicates, one randomly selected report was retained.

Model selection and input modes

Four LLMs were selected for this study: GPT-4o, Claude-4, Qwen-3 Max, and DeepSeek-V3.1. All four models were accessed exclusively via their official cloud-based APIs. Evaluation was performed under two input modes—with-option mode and without-option mode—using standardised prompt templates to ensure consistency of inputs across all models. Under the with-option mode, the prompt was structured as follows: “You are a radiologist. Read the following imaging findings. Select one or more diseases from the options below that best match the description. Output only the corresponding letter codes; if there are multiple results, use ‘/‘ as the separator. OptionlistOptionlist. Imaging findings: [report text\].” Under the without-option mode, the prompt was structured as: “You are a radiologist. Read the following imaging findings and output one or more standardised diagnoses. Do not specify the responsible spinal level. Output only the diagnoses; if there are multiple results, use ‘/‘ as the separator. Imaging findings: reporttextreporttext.” All prompts were administered in Chinese (the original language of the clinical reports); The diagnostic option list varied by modality--region combination (Supplementary Table S1). To assess reproducibility, each model processed all reports three times under each mode. To evaluate the accuracy of model outputs against the reference standard, a two-stage automated-plus-expert pipeline was employed. First, outputs and reference diagnoses were standardized to diagnostic codes using a synonym mapping table; outputs lacking reliable matches were flagged. Second, two senior orthopedic surgeons independently reviewed all flagged unmatched cases and conducted random audits of the automatically matched cases (2% of cases). Reviews evaluated aggregated diagnostic results per report, with discrepancies resolved via consensus.

Reference standards and diagnostic classification framework

The reference standard for this study was derived from the original clinical radiological reports issued by the attending radiologists at each centre. For each report, the imaging findings section served as the input to the LLMs, while the diagnostic conclusion section served as the reference standard against which model outputs were evaluated. Based on the clinical characteristics of spine imaging, diagnoses were categorised by anatomical region and imaging modality into nine modality–region combinations, encompassing a total of 95 diagnostic categories (Supplementary Table S1). The Centre 1 evaluation covered all 95 diagnostic categories (22 for radiography, 33 for CT, and 40 for MRI), whereas Centres 2 and 3 evaluated only the 40 MRI-related diagnostic categories (14 cervical, 11 thoracic, and 15 lumbar).

Statistics and reproducibility

Evaluation metrics comprised two dimensions: diagnostic performance and stability. Diagnostic performance metrics included precision, recall, specificity, negative predictive value (NPV), F1 score, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Stability was assessed using Fleiss’ kappa (κ) to evaluate agreement across three repeated runs27. As the diagnostic accuracy metrics did not satisfy the assumption of normality (assessed by the Shapiro–Wilk test), non-parametric methods were employed for all pairwise comparisons. Paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Bonferroni correction were used to compare diagnostic accuracy between models, the DeLong test was used to compare AUC differences28, and Fisher’s exact test was used to compare disease-level error rates. In addition, the effects of imaging modality and anatomical region on model predictions were analysed, stratified performance comparisons were conducted according to disease prevalence, and cross-institutional generalisability was evaluated using the coefficient of variation (CV) across the three centres. All statistical tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (s.d.) unless otherwise stated.
