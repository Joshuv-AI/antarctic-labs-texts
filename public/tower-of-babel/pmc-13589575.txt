Admission Inflammatory-Metabolic Biomarker Profiles and Outcome-Specific Prognostic Patterns After Endovascular Therapy for Acute Ischemic Stroke: A Multicenter Cohort Study
Authors: Dayong Shen, Siyan Chen, Yuting Zhang, Aparna Sharma Chivukula, Shasha Zhu, Chao Zhao, Zeheng Li, Jie Sun, Wei Zhang, Lu Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClinical outcomes after endovascular therapy (EVT) for acute ischemic stroke (AIS) remain heterogeneous despite advances in reperfusion care. Whether admission inflammatory-metabolic biomarker profiles provide complementary and outcome-specific prognostic information beyond established clinical and imaging variables remains uncertain.MethodsThis multicenter retrospective cohort included consecutive adults with anterior-circulation large-vessel occlusion AIS treated with EVT at three centers between 2020 and 2025. The primary outcome was unfavorable 90-day functional outcome (modified Rankin Scale score 3–6); secondary outcomes were early neurological deterioration (END) and hemorrhagic transformation (HT). Multivariable logistic regression, restricted cubic splines, interaction analyses, and internally validated prediction models were used.ResultsAmong 740 patients, 424 (57.3%) had an unfavorable 90-day functional outcome. Per 1-SD increase, the C-reactive protein–triglyceride glucose index (CTI) was independently associated with unfavorable outcome (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 2.07, 95% CI 1.65–2.61) and END (aOR 1.74, 95% CI 1.46–2.08), but not with HT. Several cell-derived inflammatory indices were independently associated with HT, including the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR; aOR 1.47, 95% CI 1.25–1.74) and systemic immune-inflammation index (SII; aOR 1.35, 95% CI 1.15–1.58). The association between CTI and unfavorable outcome was stronger in patients with poor collateral circulation (P for interaction = 0.011). Adding CTI and NLR increased the baseline model AUC from 0.792 to 0.836 (ΔAUC 0.044, 95% CI 0.025–0.065; cross-validated AUC 0.818).ConclusionAdmission inflammatory–metabolic biomarkers showed complementary, outcome-specific associations after EVT. CTI was most consistently associated with END and unfavorable 90-day functional outcome, whereas cell-derived inflammatory indices were more strongly associated with HT. Combining CTI and NLR may refine early risk stratification after EVT, although prospective external validation is required before clinical application.Keywords: acute ischemic stroke, endovascular therapy, inflammatory–metabolic biomarkers, C-reactive protein–triglyceride glucose index, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, hemorrhagic transformation

Introduction

Endovascular therapy (EVT) has transformed the treatment of acute ischemic stroke caused by anterior-circulation large-vessel occlusion and is now a central component of reperfusion care in eligible patients.1,2 However, angiographic recanalization does not necessarily result in neurological recovery. Even after EVT, a substantial proportion of patients experience early neurological deterioration, hemorrhagic transformation, or persistent functional disability at 90 days.3,4 This mismatch between macrovascular reperfusion and clinical outcome highlights the complexity of prognosis after EVT. Infarct progression, collateral status, microvascular dysfunction, blood-brain barrier (BBB) integrity, and systemic inflammatory and metabolic disturbances may all influence clinical outcomes after EVT.5,6 Established clinical and imaging variables, including baseline stroke severity, the Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Score (ASPECTS), collateral status, occlusion site, treatment delay, and recanalization grade, remain essential for prognostic assessment. However, they may not fully reflect the systemic inflammatory and metabolic state present at admission. Routinely available admission biomarkers may therefore provide complementary information for early risk stratification after EVT.

Inflammatory and metabolic biomarkers are biologically plausible candidates for this purpose. Acute cerebral ischemia and subsequent reperfusion induce interconnected immune, vascular, and metabolic responses. Neutrophil and monocyte activation, platelet-leukocyte interactions, endothelial dysfunction, and BBB disruption may contribute to impaired microvascular perfusion and secondary tissue injury after EVT.7,8 Meanwhile, stress hyperglycemia, insulin resistance, and triglyceride-related metabolic disturbance may worsen oxidative stress, thrombogenicity, endothelial injury, and tissue susceptibility to ischemic damage.9,10 These processes are closely coupled. Metabolic stress can intensify inflammatory signaling, while systemic inflammation may aggravate metabolic dysregulation and vascular instability. Accordingly, routine admission laboratory variables, including C-reactive protein (CRP), glucose, triglycerides, circulating blood cell counts, and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), may serve as clinically accessible markers of systemic inflammatory and metabolic status. Indices derived from these variables may reflect distinct but partly overlapping aspects of systemic inflammatory and metabolic dysregulation relevant to outcomes after EVT.11

Previous studies have examined several admission inflammatory and metabolic biomarkers in relation to outcomes after acute ischemic stroke. C-reactive protein has been studied as a marker of systemic inflammatory activation, whereas the triglyceride-glucose index has been widely used as a surrogate of insulin resistance and metabolic stress in acute ischemic stroke and EVT-treated populations.12,13 Cell-count-derived indices, including the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio, monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio, systemic immune-inflammation index, and systemic inflammation response index, integrate different components of the circulating cellular inflammatory response.14,15 Lipid-related inflammatory ratios, including the neutrophil-to-HDL-C ratio (NHR) and monocyte-to-HDL-C ratio (MHR), may provide additional information on the balance between systemic inflammation and HDL-C-related vascular protection. More recently, the C-reactive protein-triglyceride glucose index (CTI) has been proposed as a composite marker integrating inflammatory activation with triglyceride-glucose-related metabolic disturbance. Although supporting evidence has emerged from population-based cohorts, EVT-specific data remain limited.16 Most previous studies, however, have evaluated individual biomarkers separately and have focused on a single clinical endpoint. It therefore remains uncertain whether these biomarkers provide redundant or complementary prognostic information across different outcomes after EVT.

This question is clinically relevant because adverse outcomes after EVT are related but biologically heterogeneous. Early neurological deterioration within the first 24 hours may reflect infarct progression, inadequate tissue-level reperfusion, cerebral edema, or limited neurological reserve. Hemorrhagic transformation represents a distinct clinical phenotype that is more closely associated with reperfusion-related endothelial injury, BBB disruption, and microvascular fragility. In contrast, unfavorable 90-day functional outcome is a downstream endpoint influenced by baseline injury, reperfusion quality, early complications, systemic vulnerability, rehabilitation, and subsequent recovery. Consequently, a biomarker associated with long-term functional outcome may not show the same relationship with early neurological deterioration or hemorrhagic transformation. Similarly, cell-derived inflammatory indices and composite inflammatory-metabolic indices may be associated with different clinical manifestations of post-EVT injury. Direct comparison across multiple biomarkers and outcomes is therefore needed to determine whether distinct admission biomarker domains show outcome-specific prognostic patterns.17,18

To address this gap, we conducted a multicenter retrospective cohort study of patients with acute ischemic stroke who underwent EVT for anterior-circulation large-vessel occlusion. We evaluated a prespecified panel of admission biomarkers spanning inflammatory, metabolic, cellular inflammatory, platelet-related, and lipid-related domains. We first examined their distributions and correlations and then assessed their adjusted associations with unfavorable 90-day functional outcome, early neurological deterioration, and hemorrhagic transformation. We further evaluated dose–response relationships, explored whether collateral status or successful recanalization modified the association between CTI and unfavorable 90-day functional outcome, and assessed whether selected complementary biomarkers improved prognostic performance beyond established clinical and imaging predictors. We hypothesized that admission inflammatory–metabolic biomarkers would show distinct, outcome-specific associations after EVT and that combining biomarkers from complementary biological domains would provide incremental prognostic information.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This multicenter retrospective cohort study included consecutive patients aged ≥18 years with neuroimaging-confirmed acute ischemic stroke who underwent EVT for angiographically confirmed anterior-circulation large-vessel occlusion at the Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University, Xuzhou First People’s Hospital, and Xuzhou Central Hospital between January 2020 and January 2025. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) and REporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely-collected health Data (RECORD) statements.19,20

Patients were eligible if they had baseline clinical data and admission laboratory measurements required for derivation of the prespecified inflammatory–metabolic biomarkers, including CRP, triglyceride, and glucose required for CTI calculation. Patients were excluded at baseline if they had intracranial hemorrhage or non-ischemic stroke, posterior circulation stroke, pre-stroke functional dependence defined as a modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score >2, or missing CTI components or other key baseline data. Among patients otherwise eligible for follow-up, further exclusions were applied for missing 90-day functional outcome assessment, non-stroke-related death, or other conditions precluding reliable outcome evaluation. Admission Inflammatory-Metabolic Biomarkers

Admission laboratory values were obtained from blood samples collected before EVT whenever feasible. Candidate biomarkers were selected to represent complementary inflammatory, metabolic, cellular inflammatory, platelet-related, and lipid-related domains. CTI was treated as a prespecified composite inflammatory-metabolic biomarker derived from C-reactive protein, triglyceride, and glucose using the published CTI equation.16 The triglyceride-glucose index (TyG) was calculated as ln[triglyceride (mg/dL) × glucose (mg/dL)/2] after conversion of triglyceride and glucose values to mg/dL.21,22 CRP was natural log-transformed and analyzed as ln(CRP). Additional derived biomarkers included the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), systemic inflammation response index (SIRI), neutrophil-to-HDL-C ratio (NHR), and monocyte-to-HDL-C ratio (MHR). Detailed formulas for derived biomarkers are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Outcome Definitions

The primary outcome was unfavorable 90-day functional outcome, defined as an mRS score of 3–6. Functional outcome was assessed during follow-up visits or by structured telephone interviews conducted by trained clinicians. Early neurological deterioration (END) was defined as an increase of ≥4 points in the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score within 24 hours after admission, excluding deterioration attributable to hemorrhagic transformation or procedural complications.23 Hemorrhagic transformation was ascertained from follow-up neuroimaging obtained during hospitalization and the corresponding clinical records.

Covariates

Baseline and procedural covariates were obtained from institutional stroke registries and electronic medical records using a standardized data collection protocol. Prespecified covariates included age, sex, vascular risk factors, baseline NIHSS score, ASPECTS, collateral circulation, occlusion site, intravenous thrombolysis, and onset-to-puncture time. Collateral status was graded on baseline angiography using the American Society of Interventional and Therapeutic Neuroradiology/Society of Interventional Radiology collateral grading system and dichotomized as good collateral (grade ≥2) or poor collateral (grade <2). Successful recanalization was defined as modified Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction grade 2b–3.24 Additional procedural characteristics and early clinical outcomes, including EVT technique, number of passes, puncture-to-reperfusion time, rescue angioplasty or stenting, hemorrhagic transformation, and END, were summarized descriptively in Table 1.

Missing Data

Patients with missing CTI components, key baseline variables, or unavailable 90-day functional outcome were excluded before construction of the final analytic cohort. The primary analyses used complete-case data for the variables included in each model. Derived biomarkers requiring triglyceride and glucose values were calculated from the curated admission laboratory dataset after verifying alignment with the final analysis dataset. k-nearest neighbors (KNN)-imputed dataset was used only for sensitivity analyses and was not used as the primary analytic dataset. Sensitivity analyses were performed to evaluate the robustness of the CTI-outcome association using additional covariate adjustment, the KNN-imputed dataset, and dichotomized CTI modeling (Supplementary Table S2).

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as medians with interquartile ranges, and categorical variables as counts with percentages. Between-group comparisons according to 90-day functional outcome were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables and the Pearson chi-squared test or Fisher exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. All tests were two-sided, and P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

The distributions of admission candidate inflammatory-metabolic biomarkers were compared between patients with favorable and unfavorable 90-day functional outcomes. Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated to describe the correlation structure among CTI, ln(CRP), TyG, and selected inflammatory and lipid-related ratio indices. NLR, PLR, MLR, SII, SIRI, NHR, and MHR were natural log-transformed before correlation analysis because of right-skewed distributions.

Outcome-specific associations between candidate biomarkers and clinical outcomes were evaluated using multivariable logistic regression. The outcomes included unfavorable 90-day functional outcome, END, and hemorrhagic transformation. Each biomarker was entered into a separate model. Odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals were reported per 1-SD increase. CRP was natural log-transformed to generate ln(CRP). CTI, ln(CRP), and TyG were standardized directly without further transformation. NLR, PLR, MLR, SII, SIRI, NHR, and MHR were natural log-transformed before standardization because of right-skewed distributions. Models were adjusted for age, sex, baseline NIHSS, ASPECTS, collateral status, occlusion site, hypertension, diabetes, atrial fibrillation, previous stroke or transient ischemic attack, current smoking, intravenous thrombolysis, and onset-to-puncture time.

Restricted cubic spline models were used to evaluate dose-response relationships for selected biomarker-outcome pairs identified from the multivariable association analyses. Splines were fitted with four knots placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of the biomarker distribution, with the median value as the reference. Overall association and nonlinearity were tested using likelihood-ratio tests. The main spline analyses examined CTI in relation to unfavorable 90-day functional outcome and END, and NLR in relation to hemorrhagic transformation.

Exploratory subgroup and interaction analyses were performed to assess whether the association between CTI and unfavorable 90-day functional outcome differed across clinically relevant strata. Prespecified modifiers included collateral status, successful recanalization, diabetes, age (<60 vs ≥60 years), and sex. Subgroup-specific odds ratios were estimated using the same adjustment set as the primary multivariable model, with the stratification variable omitted from the covariate set when applicable. P values for interaction were calculated by adding a multiplicative interaction term between CTI and the modifier variable. A broader exploratory interaction screening across CTI, NLR, SII, and selected clinical modifiers was also performed, with results provided in Supplementary Table S3.

Incremental prognostic performance was assessed for biomarker-augmented models predicting unfavorable 90-day functional outcome. The baseline clinical and imaging model included age, sex, baseline NIHSS, ASPECTS, collateral circulation, occlusion site, hypertension, diabetes, atrial fibrillation, previous stroke or transient ischemic attack, current smoking, intravenous thrombolysis, and onset-to-puncture time. Biomarker-augmented models incorporated CTI, NLR, or both. Extended model-performance analyses, including SII-based biomarker-augmented models, were performed as supplementary analyses (Supplementary Table S4). Model discrimination was assessed using area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Changes in AUC relative to the baseline clinical model were estimated with bootstrap confidence intervals. Likelihood-ratio tests were used to compare biomarker-augmented models with the baseline model, and Brier scores were used to evaluate overall prediction error. Internal validation was performed using 10-fold cross-validation. Cross-validated predicted probabilities were used to calculate cross-validated AUC (CV-AUC), cross-validated Brier score (CV-Brier score), calibration intercept, and calibration slope. Calibration curves were constructed by grouping patients into deciles of predicted risk. Clinical utility was assessed using decision curve analysis across a range of threshold probabilities.25 Calibration and decision curve analyses focused on the CTI-augmented and combined CTI + NLR models for visual clarity.

All analyses and graphics were performed using R version 4.5.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). No formal adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied; analyses beyond the prespecified primary association were interpreted as exploratory.

Results

Study Population and Baseline Characteristics

Between January 2020 and January 2025, 1365 patients with acute ischemic stroke who underwent EVT across three centers were screened, and 740 patients were included in the final analysis (Figure 1). Of these, 316 patients (42.7%) had a favorable 90-day functional outcome (mRS 0–2), whereas 424 patients (57.3%) had an unfavorable outcome (mRS 3–6). Baseline, procedural, and early clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Patients with unfavorable outcomes were older (median age, 71 vs 66 years; P < 0.001), more often female (40.3% vs 32.0%; P = 0.020), and more likely to have diabetes (38.9% vs 17.1%; P < 0.001). They also had greater baseline stroke severity, reflected by higher NIHSS scores (median, 19 vs 15; P < 0.001), lower ASPECTS (median, 7 vs 8; P < 0.001), and a higher proportion with poor collateral status (ASITN/SIR grade <2; 58.7% vs 28.5%; P < 0.001). Regarding procedural characteristics, patients with unfavorable outcomes had more thrombectomy passes, longer puncture-to-reperfusion times, and lower rates of successful recanalization (83.0% vs 92.4%; P < 0.001). Hemorrhagic transformation and END were also more frequent in the unfavorable outcome group (61.3% vs 31.6% and 52.4% vs 27.8%, respectively; both P < 0.001). Admission CTI was higher among patients with unfavorable outcomes (median, 5.88 vs 5.46; P < 0.001), together with higher neutrophil counts and lower lymphocyte counts.

Distribution and Correlation of Admission Inflammatory-Metabolic Biomarkers

The distributions of candidate inflammatory-metabolic biomarkers stratified by 90-day functional outcome are shown in Table 2. Compared with patients with favorable outcomes, those with unfavorable outcomes had higher admission CTI (5.88 vs 5.46; P < 0.001), ln(CRP) (2.25 vs 2.09; P = 0.018), NLR (9.95 vs 6.99; P < 0.001), PLR (218 vs 179; P < 0.001), MLR (0.54 vs 0.43; P < 0.001), SII (1805 vs 1285; P < 0.001), SIRI (4.45 vs 3.14; P < 0.001), and NHR (8.16 vs 7.03; P < 0.001). By contrast, TyG and MHR did not differ significantly between outcome groups (P = 0.777 and P = 0.855, respectively). The Spearman correlation matrix demonstrated distinct correlation patterns across biomarkers (Figure 2). CTI showed only weak correlations with TyG (ρ = 0.09), NLR (ρ = 0.14), and SII (ρ = 0.12), while several cellular inflammatory indices were strongly intercorrelated, including NLR with SII (ρ = 0.89), PLR with SII (ρ = 0.84), and MLR with SIRI (ρ = 0.91). These findings demonstrated strong intercorrelations among several cellular inflammatory indices, whereas CTI was only weakly correlated with these markers.

Outcome-Specific Adjusted Associations of Candidate Biomarkers

Outcome-specific adjusted associations are shown in Table 3 and Figure 3. After adjustment for demographic factors, vascular risk factors, baseline stroke severity, imaging features, intravenous thrombolysis, and onset-to-puncture time, CTI was independently associated with unfavorable 90-day functional outcome (aOR per 1-SD increase, 2.07; 95% CI, 1.65–2.61; P < 0.001) and END (aOR, 1.74; 95% CI, 1.46–2.08; P < 0.001). In contrast, CTI was not significantly associated with hemorrhagic transformation after adjustment (aOR, 1.16; 95% CI, 0.99–1.35; P = 0.062). ln(CRP) and TyG were not independently associated with any of the three outcomes after multivariable adjustment. For unfavorable 90-day functional outcome, several cellular or lipid-related inflammatory indices were also significant, including NLR (aOR, 1.73; 95% CI, 1.43–2.10), PLR (aOR, 1.38; 95% CI, 1.16–1.65), MLR (aOR, 1.36; 95% CI, 1.13–1.64), SII (aOR, 1.54; 95% CI, 1.28–1.85), SIRI (aOR, 1.49; 95% CI, 1.23–1.81), and NHR (aOR, 1.44; 95% CI, 1.20–1.73; all P ≤ 0.001). For END, however, CTI was the only biomarker with a statistically significant adjusted association. For hemorrhagic transformation, significant adjusted associations were observed for NLR (aOR, 1.47; 95% CI, 1.25–1.74; P < 0.001), SII (aOR, 1.35; 95% CI, 1.15–1.58; P < 0.001), SIRI (aOR, 1.32; 95% CI, 1.12–1.56; P = 0.001), and NHR (aOR, 1.31; 95% CI, 1.12–1.54; P < 0.001). Sensitivity analyses showed consistent associations between CTI and unfavorable 90-day functional outcome after additional adjustment for stroke etiology, additional adjustment for procedural variables, analysis of the KNN-imputed dataset, and dichotomization of CTI at the cohort median (Supplementary Table S2).

Exploratory Subgroup and Interaction Analyses of CTI

Exploratory subgroup and interaction analyses were performed to assess whether the association between CTI and unfavorable 90-day functional outcome differed across clinically relevant strata (Table 4). The association was significantly modified by collateral status. Among patients with good collateral status, the aOR per 1-SD increase in CTI was 1.69 (95% CI, 1.31–2.20; P < 0.001), whereas among those with poor collateral status, the corresponding aOR was 3.78 (95% CI, 2.14–6.68; P < 0.001; P for interaction = 0.011). By contrast, successful recanalization did not significantly modify the CTI-outcome association (P for interaction = 0.701). No significant interaction was observed for diabetes, age, or sex (all P for interaction > 0.05). A broader interaction screening further showed no significant interactions for NLR or SII across the examined clinical modifiers (Supplementary Table S3).

Incremental Prognostic Performance of Biomarker-Augmented Models

The incremental prognostic performance of biomarker-augmented models is summarized in Table 5. The baseline clinical and imaging model yielded an AUC of 0.792 and a 10-fold cross-validated AUC of 0.775. Adding CTI increased the AUC to 0.819 (ΔAUC, 0.027; 95% CI, 0.010–0.042; likelihood-ratio chi-square = 49.38; P < 0.001), improved the Brier score from 0.184 to 0.172, and increased the cross-validated AUC to 0.801. Adding NLR also improved model performance, although to a lesser extent (AUC, 0.812; ΔAUC, 0.020; 95% CI, 0.007–0.034; CV-AUC, 0.794). The combined CTI + NLR had the highest discrimination and lowest prediction error among the evaluated models, with an AUC of 0.836, the largest increase in AUC relative to the baseline model (ΔAUC, 0.044; 95% CI, 0.025–0.065), the lowest Brier score (0.163), and the highest CV-AUC (0.818) with the lowest CV-Brier score (0.172). Calibration plots showed acceptable agreement between predicted and observed risks, and decision curve analysis suggested greater net benefit for the CTI-augmented and combined CTI + NLR models than for the baseline model across a broad range of threshold probabilities (Figure 5). Extended model-performance analyses including SII-based models showed that the CTI + NLR model had the highest cross-validated discrimination and the lowest cross-validated Brier score among the biomarker-augmented models examined (Supplementary Table S4).

Discussion

Despite advances in EVT, functional recovery after acute ischemic stroke remains highly heterogeneous and is unlikely to be explained by a single postischemic process. In this multicenter cohort of EVT-treated patients, admission inflammatory-metabolic biomarkers showed outcome-specific prognostic patterns rather than functioning as interchangeable markers of overall risk. CTI demonstrated the most consistent associations with END and unfavorable 90-day functional outcome, whereas cell-derived inflammatory indices, particularly NLR and SII, were more strongly associated with hemorrhagic transformation. These associations were further supported by spline analyses, which suggested graded positive relationships for CTI with END and functional outcome, and for NLR with hemorrhagic transformation. In exploratory analyses, the association between CTI and unfavorable 90-day functional outcome was stronger in patients with poor collateral circulation but was not significantly modified by successful recanalization. Moreover, adding CTI and NLR to the baseline clinical and imaging model improved prognostic performance, with the combined model showing the highest discrimination and lowest prediction error among the evaluated models. Collectively, these findings suggest that admission inflammatory-metabolic biomarkers provide complementary prognostic information across different post-EVT outcomes. CTI was more closely associated with END and unfavorable 90-day functional outcome, whereas NLR and related cell-derived indices were more closely associated with hemorrhagic transformation.

The distribution and correlation findings help clarify how the biomarker profile should be interpreted. The candidate markers in this study were selected to represent complementary inflammatory, metabolic, cellular inflammatory, platelet-related, and lipid-related inflammatory domains, and their distribution and correlation structure support interpretation of these biomarkers as a profile rather than as isolated markers. Patients with unfavorable 90-day outcomes had higher CTI, ln(CRP), NLR, PLR, MLR, SII, SIRI, and NHR, whereas standard TyG and MHR did not differ significantly between outcome groups. Importantly, CTI showed only weak correlations with TyG and cell-derived inflammatory indices, while several cellular inflammatory markers were strongly intercorrelated. This pattern suggests that admission biomarkers after EVT should not be treated as interchangeable measures of the same systemic response. The limited signal from TyG alone suggests that the prognostic information provided by CTI cannot be attributed to metabolic stress in isolation; CTI may instead reflect an integrated inflammatory-metabolic state that differs from both standard TyG and cell-count-derived inflammatory ratios.26,27 Conversely, the high intercorrelation among several cellular inflammatory indices suggests that they partly reflect a shared cellular inflammatory component. Taken together, this correlation pattern helps explain why different biomarkers may show preferential associations with different post-EVT outcomes.

The outcome-specific analyses linked CTI primarily to END and unfavorable 90-day functional outcome rather than to hemorrhagic transformation. After multivariable adjustment, CTI remained independently associated with both END and unfavorable 90-day functional outcome, while ln(CRP) and standard TyG did not show independent associations with any of the three outcomes. CTI was also not clearly associated with hemorrhagic transformation after adjustment. These findings suggest that CTI may provide information related more closely to early neurological and functional outcomes than to hemorrhagic complications. END, particularly when deterioration attributable to hemorrhagic transformation or procedural complications is excluded, may represent a proximal clinical manifestation of early infarct evolution, impaired tissue-level reperfusion, and limited neurological reserve. A higher CTI may therefore reflect combined inflammatory and metabolic dysregulation associated with reduced ischemic tolerance and poorer neurological recovery after EVT.28–30 These findings are consistent with the possibility that recovery depends not only on macrovascular recanalization but also on downstream microcirculatory function, BBB integrity, and the tolerance of ischemic tissue to reperfusion.31–33 Thus, CTI should not be interpreted as a causal mediator established by this study, but as a composite admission signal that may provide prognostic information not fully represented by conventional clinical and imaging variables.

By contrast, hemorrhagic transformation was more strongly associated with cell-derived inflammatory indices. The most prominent adjusted associations with hemorrhagic transformation were observed for NLR, SII, SIRI, and NHR, with additional signals from PLR and MLR, whereas ln(CRP), TyG, and MHR were not independently associated with this outcome. This divergent pattern suggests that hemorrhagic transformation after EVT may be more closely related to vascular inflammatory processes involving reperfusion-related endothelial stress, BBB disruption, and microvascular fragility than to the combined inflammatory–metabolic signal represented by CTI.34–36 NLR may reflect neutrophil predominance and relative lymphocyte suppression, while SII and SIRI integrate neutrophil, platelet, lymphocyte, and monocyte components, thereby reflecting a broader cellular inflammatory and thromboinflammatory milieu. Such a milieu may be particularly relevant to endothelial injury, platelet-leukocyte activation, and barrier dysfunction during reperfusion.37–39 Accordingly, the associations of NLR, SII, SIRI, and NHR with hemorrhagic transformation suggest that cellular inflammatory indices and CTI may provide different prognostic information across post-EVT outcomes. However, because hemorrhagic transformation in this study was defined by follow-up neuroimaging during hospitalization rather than being restricted to symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage or specific radiological subtypes, such as hemorrhagic infarction versus parenchymal hematoma, these findings should be interpreted as applying to overall imaging-defined hemorrhagic transformation. Future studies should determine whether the observed biomarker associations differ according to radiological subtype and symptomatic status.

The spline and interaction analyses further refine the interpretation of these biomarker signals. Restricted cubic spline models showed graded positive associations of CTI with unfavorable 90-day functional outcome and END, and of NLR with hemorrhagic transformation, without statistically significant evidence of nonlinearity. Although uncertainty increased at the upper tails of the biomarker distributions, these findings support modeling CTI and NLR as continuous variables and provide no evidence for a specific threshold within the observed ranges. In exploratory interaction analyses, the association between CTI and unfavorable 90-day outcome appeared stronger among patients with poor collateral circulation, whereas successful recanalization did not significantly modify this association. This pattern is clinically meaningful because collateral circulation reflects the capacity to sustain ischemic tissue before reperfusion, while angiographic recanalization reflects restoration of macrovascular patency. Therefore, a high CTI in the setting of poor collateral compensation may indicate a subgroup in whom systemic inflammatory-metabolic disturbance coexists with limited tissue-level ischemic tolerance. These patients may be particularly susceptible to microvascular dysfunction, no-reflow phenomena, BBB stress, and impaired neurological recovery despite EVT.40–43 These interaction findings should be regarded as hypothesis-generating. Importantly, the absence of a significant interaction with successful recanalization should not be interpreted as evidence that recanalization is irrelevant; it indicates only that no clear effect modification was detected in this cohort. Prospective studies are needed to determine whether collateral status modifies the prognostic value of CTI.

The prediction analyses translate these association patterns into a clinically relevant prognostic framework. The baseline clinical and imaging model already incorporated established admission and treatment-related predictors, including age, sex, baseline NIHSS, ASPECTS, collateral status, occlusion site, vascular risk factors, intravenous thrombolysis, and onset-to-puncture time. Therefore, the incremental value of CTI and NLR should be interpreted as additional information beyond a relatively comprehensive routine clinical and imaging assessment, rather than as isolated biomarker discrimination. Adding CTI improved discrimination, reduced prediction error, and increased cross-validated discrimination; adding NLR also improved model performance, although to a lesser extent. The combined CTI + NLR model had the highest AUC and cross-validated AUC and the lowest Brier and cross-validated Brier scores among the evaluated models, with acceptable calibration and greater decision-analytic net benefit than the baseline model.44,45 This pattern is consistent with the statistical complementarity observed in the outcome-specific analyses: CTI may contribute information on inflammatory-metabolic susceptibility related to early deterioration and functional recovery, whereas NLR may add information on cellular inflammatory activation and vascular injury risk. Clinically, these findings suggest that readily available admission biomarkers may help refine early risk stratification after EVT when used as adjuncts to established clinical and imaging variables. However, the observed gains should be viewed as incremental rather than definitive, and external validation is required before biomarker-augmented models can be considered for routine clinical implementation.46,47

Several strengths and limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. The major strengths of this study include its multicenter EVT cohort, the use of a prespecified panel of admission biomarkers covering inflammatory, metabolic, cellular inflammatory, platelet-related, and lipid-related domains, and the evaluation of multiple clinically relevant outcomes rather than a single functional endpoint. The analytic framework also included biomarker distribution and correlation analyses, outcome-specific multivariable models, dose–response assessment, exploratory interaction testing, and incremental prediction analyses with internal cross-validation, calibration, and decision curve evaluation. Nevertheless, this study remains retrospective and observational; therefore, causal inference is not possible, and residual confounding or selection bias may persist despite multivariable adjustment. Complete-case analysis and exclusion of patients with missing biomarker components or unavailable 90-day outcomes may have introduced additional selection bias. In addition, all three participating centers were located within a single regional healthcare setting in China, and only patients with anterior-circulation large-vessel occlusion treated with EVT were included. Differences in patient demographics, laboratory practices, EVT workflows, and healthcare systems may limit the generalizability of these findings to geographically and ethnically diverse populations, posterior-circulation stroke, or other treatment settings. External validation in independent multicenter cohorts from diverse populations is therefore required. Biomarkers were assessed only at admission, precluding evaluation of dynamic inflammatory-metabolic trajectories. Hemorrhagic transformation was analyzed as an overall imaging-defined event rather than according to symptomatic status or radiological subtype. Finally, interaction analyses and secondary biomarker comparisons were exploratory, and no formal adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied, which may increase the risk of type I error. Accordingly, these secondary and interaction findings should be interpreted cautiously and as hypothesis-generating. The main conclusions of this study are therefore based on the overall consistency and biological coherence of the observed outcome-specific patterns across prespecified and exploratory analyses, rather than on isolated statistically significant findings. The prediction models also require external validation before clinical implementation. Future studies should validate these findings prospectively, incorporate serial biomarker measurements, define more granular hemorrhagic phenotypes, and test whether CTI- and NLR-informed risk stratification can improve post-EVT monitoring, trial enrichment, or individualized management.

Conclusion

In this multicenter cohort of EVT-treated acute ischemic stroke patients, admission inflammatory-metabolic biomarkers showed outcome-specific associations. CTI was most consistently associated with END and unfavorable 90-day functional outcome, whereas cellular inflammatory indices, particularly NLR and SII, were more closely aligned with hemorrhagic transformation. CTI and NLR provided complementary prognostic information and improved model performance beyond established clinical and imaging variables, while the association between CTI and unfavorable 90-day functional outcome was stronger among patients with poor collateral circulation. These findings suggest the potential value of admission biomarker profiling for early post-EVT risk stratification. Following prospective external validation, CTI- and NLR-based profiling could be evaluated as an adjunct to conventional clinical and imaging assessment to identify patients who may warrant intensified early monitoring and to enrich enrollment in future trials of adjunctive neuroprotective or anti-inflammatory strategies. Before clinical implementation, these potential applications require confirmation in independent prospective cohorts.
