TSPO deficiency promotes the progression of malignant peripheral sheath tumors by regulating the G2/M phase of the cell cycle via CDK1
Authors: Xingnan Zhang, Chenhao Hu, Shengqiao Sun, Chao Guo, Yakun Bu, Zicong Wang, Zewei Liu, Xiaoli Zhang, Dezhi Li, Song Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumors (MPNSTs) are highly aggressive Schwann cell-derived sarcomas that are sporadic or associated with Neurofibromatosis 1 (NF1) gene mutations. Traditional therapies are usually ineffective for treating MPNSTs, so new targets need to be identified for the treatment of MPNSTs. In the present study, the role of the mitochondrial translocator protein (TSPO) in the regulation of cell proliferation and the cell cycle in MPNSTs was investigated. TSPO expression was lower in MPNSTs than in NFs. Loss-of-function experiments revealed that TSPO deficiency promoted MPNST cell growth, migration, and invasion and influenced the cell cycle in vitro and in vivo. In addition, TSPO depletion suppressed cell apoptosis by downregulating the expression of caspase-3, caspase-8, HSP60, p27, p53, and BCL-2 and suppressed the cell cycle by upregulating CDK1, CDK2, CCNB1 and CCNA2. Furthermore, CDK1 was determined to be an upstream target of TSPO-mediated regulation via RNA-seq, qPCR, and Western blotting. Specifically, depletion of CDK1 weakened the effect of TSPO deficiency on cell proliferation and migration. More importantly, CDK1 knockdown induced significant cell cycle arrest in the G2/M phase. In summary, TSPO deficiency regulates the cell cycle in MPNSTs by targeting CDK1, which may be an effective molecular target for prognosis evaluation and treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-77933-2.

Introduction

Type 1 neurofibromatosis (NF1) is an autosomal dominant genetic disorder that affects multiple organ systems and has diverse clinical manifestations1. The NF1 gene is located on chromosome 17q11.2 and encodes the neurofibromin protein, which acts as a tumor suppressor2. The most representative tumors are peripheral nerve sheath tumors, including dermal or plexiform neurofibromas (pNFs)3. Unlike dermal neurofibromas, pNFs can develop into malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumors (MPNSTs), which are the main cause of death in NF1 patients4. The global average prevalence of NF1 is approximately 1 case per 3000 people5. MPNST is the most serious complication and leading cause of death in NF1 patients6. However, NF1 mutations alone do not lead to malignant tumors7. Additional somatic changes, such as the loss of cell cycle regulatory factors (especially p16/CDKN2A) and polycomb repressor complex components EED and SUZ12, are typically associated with MPNSTs1,8–10. The 5-year survival of MPNST patients is still relatively low, at only 20–50%3.

Translocator protein (TSPO) is located on the outer mitochondrial membrane and participates in various biological activities, such as metabolism, steroid formation, and inflammation/immune regulation11. In previous studies, TSPO was considered a marker of microglial cell activation12,13. In addition to participating in the biological functions of glial cells and macrophages, TSPO also participates in the proliferation, invasion, migration and other functions of tumor cells. In breast cancer14,15, hepatocellular carcinoma16, colorectal cancer17 and glioblastoma18,19, high TSPO expression promotes cell proliferation, migration and invasion. TSPO promotes hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) progression by inhibiting ferroptosis and antitumor immunity by regulating P62 accumulation and competes with KEAP1 to prevent Nrf2 from undergoing proteasomal degradation16. However, few studies have explored the role of TSPO in MPNSTs.

In our study, TSPO was expressed at lower levels in MPNSTs than in NFs. We also observed that TSPO depletion increased cell viability, promotes cell invasion and migration in vitro. RNA-seq revealed that CDK1 was upregulated in Lv-shTSPO-expressing cells. We transfected cell lines with siCDK1 and assessed cell viability, invasion and migration capacities and cell cycle progression. The results of this study will help to broaden the understanding of MPNSTs, comprehensively analyze the correlations among TSPO, CDK1 and the pathogenesis and progression of MPNSTs, and provide insight into the initiation and development of MPNSTs.

All experiments were performed according to the relevant guidelines, regulations, and ARRIVE guidelines.

Bulk RNA-seq and differential expression analysis

Genomic DNA from 3 MPNST tumor samples and 3 NF tumor samples was subjected to RNA-seq. Following library construction, genome sequencing was performed using the Illumina HiSeq PE150 platform. The raw sequencing data were subjected to a rigorous filtration and trimming process using Trimgalore (version 0.6.10) for the identification and subsequent exclusion of sequencing adapters, as well as for the removal of sequences characterized by low base quality. The clean sequencing data were aligned to the GRCh38 human reference genome with HISAT2 (version 2.2.1). This was followed by the organization and sorting of the aligned reads, a process executed using SAMtools (version 1.17). The final step in the data processing workflow involved the quantification of reads, for which featureCounts (version 2.0.3) was employed, thereby facilitating the subsequent analysis of gene expression.

Histological analysis

Hematoxylin–eosin (HE) and immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining of the FFPE samples was conducted following the manufacturer’s instructions. For HE staining, tissue sections were stained with a hematoxylin–eosin staining kit for morphological observation. For IHC, all slices were dewaxed with xylene and rehydrated with graded ethanol. Hydrogen peroxide (3%) was used to block endogenous peroxidase activity in methanol for 10 min. The sections were incubated with the target gene primary antibody (Supplementary Table 1) for 1 h at 37 °C and with the HRP-labeled secondary antibody at 37 °C for 0.5 h. Hematoxylin staining was performed at 37 °C for 30 s. Finally, target gene-positive cells were tallied in three random high-power fields (400×), and the average proportion of positive cells was calculated.

Cell culture and chemicals

The human MPNST cell line sNF96.2 and the plexiform neurofibroma cell line pNF05.5 were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), and the ST88-14 was a gift from Professor Yang Jilong of Tianjin Medical University. pNF05.5 and ST88-14 cells were cultured in DMEM (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Invitrogen Gibco) at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2. sNF96.2 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Invitrogen Gibco) at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from tissue samples by using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). Total RNA was extracted from the cells with the SteadyPure Quick RNA Extraction Kit (AG, China). cDNA was synthesized with the Evo M-MLV RT Mix Kit with gDNA Clean for qPCR (AG, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. We measured TSPO, CDK1, CDK2, CCNB1, CCNA2, MDM2, VDAC1, CDC25c, BCL-2, p27, caspase-3 and caspase-8 expression by quantitative real-time PCR (Supplementary Table 1), and GAPDH was used as a house keeping gene. Target gene expression levels were detected using the SYBR Green method and the StepOnePlus Real-time PCR System (Applied Biosystems).

siRNA and shRNA construction

Three pairs of siRNAs were designed, and knockdown efficiency was assessed via quantitative real-time PCR. The sequences used were 5’-CCACACUCAACUACUGCGUAUTT-3’, 5’-CUUCUUUGGUGCCCGACAAAUTT-3’ and 5’-CGGCUCCUACCUGGUCUGGAATT-3’. TSPO was silenced in pNF05.5, ST88-14 and sNF96.2 cells with RNAiMAX (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The most efficient TSPO short hairpin RNA (shRNA) (5’-CGGCUCCUACCUGGUCUGGAATT-3’) was chosen to create TSPO knockdown cell lines. The sequence of the negative control was 5’- UUCCAGACCAGGUAGGAGCCGTT-3’. These specific shRNA sequences were packaged into the PGMLV-ZsGreen-Puro lentiviral constructed by Genomeditech, which was subsequently transduced into pNF05.5, ST88-14 and sNF96.2 cells according to the instructions provided by Genomeditech. Screening stable transfected cell lines using puromycin at a concentration of 8ug/ml. The cell transduction efficiency was validated by qRT‒PCR.

Similarly, 3 siRNAs targeting CDK1 and control siRNA were used to transfect pNF05.5, ST88-14 and sNF96.2 cells according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The sequences are shown in Supplementary Table 1.

Cell viability assay

A CCK-8 (NCM Biotech, Suzhou) assay was used to measure cell proliferation potential. NF and MPNST cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 2000 cells per well in 100 µL of DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. At 0, 24, 48, 72, 96 and 120 h, the complete medium was discarded, and the wells were incubated with 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent for 1 h at 37 °C in the dark. Afterward, the absorbance value (OD) was measured at 450 nm with a SpectraMax 190 microplate reader.

Cell invasion and migration assays

Cell migration and invasion were studied by wound healing and Transwell assays. In the wound-healing assay, NF and MPNST cells were seeded into 6-well plates and grown to 100% confluence. A straight scratch was made across the cell monolayer with a pipette tip. At 0 h and 48 or 72 h, the distance that cells had migrated was measured. 24-well Transwell chamber inserts (Corning, Corning, NY, USA) and Corning BioCoat Growth Factor Reduced Matrigel Invasion Chambers (Corning) were used for migration and invasion assays, respectively. A total of 5 × 104 cells were resuspended in 500 µL of DMEM or RPMI 1640 medium and placed in the upper chamber, and 500 µL of DMEM or RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% FBS was added to the lower chamber. The plate was incubated for 24 h at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2. Afterward, the medium was discarded, and the cells were fixed with paraformaldehyde for 15 min and then stained with crystal violet for 15 min. Images of random fields were acquired under a microscope at 100×.

Endothelial tube formation assay

HUVECs were cultured in DMEM medium with or without shTSPO. After 24-h incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO2, cells were plated in 96-well plates coated with 50 µl of Matrigel (BD company, USA) at a concentration of 4 × 104 cells per well. Tubules were photographed through microscopy and computed by ImageJ software after incubating for 4 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

Staining. Add an appropriate volume of Calcein AM/PI detection solution. Usually, 100 µl is added to each well of a 96 well plate, Incubate at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min. After incubation, observe the staining effect under a fluorescence microscope and take photos under the microscope for recording.

Apoptosis and cell cycle assay

We used the Apoptosis and Necrosis Assay Kit (C1056, Beyotime, China) to assess apoptosis. In the apoptosis assay, pNF05.5, ST88-14 and sNF96.2 cells transduced with Lv-shTSPO or Lv-shcon were seeded into 12-well plates and grown to 100% confluence. After the 12-well plates were washed once with PBS, 5 µl of Hoechst and 5 µl of PI were added per 1 ml of cell staining buffer, followed by staining at 4 °C for 30 min, washing once with PBS and observation under a fluorescence microscope. Apoptotic cells exhibited weak red fluorescence with strong blue fluorescence.

In the cell cycle assay, we used the Cell Cycle and Apoptosis Analysis Kit (BL114A, Biosharp) to analyze the cell cycle distribution. Briefly, NF and MPNST cells were harvested, washed twice with cold PBS, and then fixed with 70% ethanol at 4 °C overnight. Afterward, the samples were stained with PI 25 µl and RNase A 10 µl (per sample) solution for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. Finally, the cell cycle distributions of these samples were detected by flow cytometry (BD C6 Plus, Beckman Coulter). FACS was analyzed with FlowJo software.

Afterward, the samples were stained with PI and RNase A solution for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. Finally, the cell cycle distributions of these samples were detected by flow cytometry (BD C6 Plus, Beckman Coulter).

Western blot analysis

Protein was isolated with RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (NCM Biotech), and quantification was performed using BCA assays (Biosharp). The proteins were separated by New Flash Protein anyKD PAGE (DAKEWE, China) and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). Primary antibodies were obtained from ABclonal, Cell Signaling, Abcam and Invitrogen, and all primary antibodies were anti-rabbit or anti-mouse. After the membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in TBS-T at room temperature for 1 h and washed 3 times for 5 min each, they were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The next day, the primary antibody was discarded, and the membranes were washed 3 times with TBS-T for 10 min and then incubated with goat anti-rabbit and goat anti-mouse HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature.

mRNA library construction, RNA-seq and data analysis

Total RNA was extracted from pNF05.5 and sNF96.2 cells in the shTSPO and shCtrl groups using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) and then quantified on a NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). Oligo(dT)-attached magnetic beads were used to purify the mRNA, and cDNA was generated via random hexamer-primed reverse transcription and amplified via PCR. The products were purified by Ampure XP beads and validated on an Agilent Technologies 2100 bioanalyzer for quality control. The double-stranded PCR products from the previous step were heated, denatured and circularized by the splint oligo sequence into single strand circular DNA (ssCir DNA) formatted as the final library. The RNA-seq library was constructed and RNA-seq was performed by Oebiotech (Shanghai, China). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with absolute log2 (FC) values > 0.5 and p < 0.05 were considered significant. We also performed Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG)20,21 pathway enrichment analysis, Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of the DEGs. The DEGs were uploaded to the Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins (STRING) (https://string-db.org/) to construct the PPI network. According to the network’s maximal clique centrality (MCC, associated median value), CytoHubba in Cytoscape was used to identify the top 10 core genes in the network and annotate the functions of the core genes.

Subcutaneous xenograft model in nude mice

We assessed the tumorigenesis of the Lv-shTSPO and Lv-shCtrl cell lines using mice in compliance with the guidelines of the Institute for Laboratory Animal Research, Beijing Neurosurgical Institute, as previously described. For this study, female BALB/c nude mice were purchased from Charles River Laboratories (Beijing, China). Two groups, each containing 6 mice, were injected with 1 × 108/ml Lv-shTSPO pNF05.5 cells or Lv-shCtrl pNF05.5 cells. Three weeks after injection, the mice were killed, and the subcutaneous tumors were dissected for size measurement, weighing and IHC assays. All animals were anesthetized with a concentration of 0.1% pentobarbital and euthanized by neck dissection.

Statistical analysis

All the experiments in this study were performed in triplicate, and the data were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. Student’s t Test was used to analyze the statistical significance between two groups and one-way ANOVA test was used for multi-groups. All statistical analysis was carried out by GraphPad Prism software. *P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistically significance, and **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001 and ****P < 0.0001 were considered to indicate high significance.

Results

TSPO is expressed at low levels in MPNST specimens and cell lines

First, we performed full transcriptome sequencing on three pairs of benign and malignant tissues; each pair was from a single patient; we detected low TSPO expression in the malignant tissues, and the results were visualized through heatmaps (Fig. 1. A, B) and supplement Table RNA_different. Subsequently, we extracted RNA from 4 benign patient tissues and 8 malignant patient tissues and detected low TSPO expression in MPNST tissues through qRT‒PCR (Fig. 1C). Due to our knowledge of the low expression of TSPO in clinical specimens, we detected TSPO in two MPNST cell lines and one pNF cell line using qRT‒PCR and Western blotting. Compared with that in pNF05.5, TSPO was downregulated to varying degrees in ST88-14 and sNF96.2 (Fig. 1. D, F). HE staining was performed on the patient tumor samples to determine whether the tumors were benign or malignant, and IHC staining also confirmed that TSPO was expressed at low levels in malignant tumor samples (Fig. 1E, G).

Construction of a TSPO knockdown model in pNF and MPNST cells

As expected, qPCR detection revealed low levels of TSPO in pNF and MPNST cell lines, including pNF05.5, ST88-14 and sNF96.2 cells (Fig. S1A), so these 3 cell lines were chosen for subsequent investigations. Moreover, the TSPO knockdown efficiency of three shRNAs was determined by qPCR, and shTSPO-3 was used for subsequent experiments (Fig. S1B). Furthermore, a green fluorescent signal was observed in 80% of the cells, indicating successful transduction (Fig. S1C, D, E). Moreover, the downregulation of TSPO at the mRNA and protein levels was proven by qPCR (Fig. 2A) and Western blotting (Fig. 2B), respectively, confirming the successful knockdown of TSPO in pNF05.5, ST88-14 and sNF96.2 cells. Subsequently, the cell lines were subjected to loss-of-function assays to investigate the effects of altered TSPO expression.

Depletion of TSPO promotes the malignant biological behavior of MPNST cells in vitro

To verify the function of TSPO in MPNST cells, we explored a range of biological processes, including proliferation, apoptosis, cell cycle progression, and migration. First, pNF05.5, ST88-14 and sNF96.2 cells with TSPO deletion (shTSPO) grew much more quickly than those without TSPO deletion (shCtrl) according to the CCK-8 assay (P < 0.05, Fig. 2C).

Similarly, TSPO deletion (shTSPO) promoted wound healing (Fig. 2. D, E) and transwell invasion (Fig. 2.F and Fig. S3A) in these 3 cell lines.

In addition, we assessed whether the depletion of TSPO influenced apoptosis and the cell cycle.

The effect of TSPO knockdown on apoptosis was assessed by Hoechst/PI staining in these 3 cell lines. As shown in part of Fig. 3A, brighter fluorescence was observed after TSPO knockdown in all 3 cell lines, indicating a low level of intracellular apoptosis. TSPO significantly increased the percentage of apoptotic cells (Fig. S3B). Flow cytometry assays revealed that TSPO interference arrested the cell cycle at the G2/M-phase transition in human pNF05.5 and sNF96.2 cells (Fig. 3. A, B and C).

Depletion of TSPO from MPNST cell lines promotes angiogenesis in HUVECs in vitro

Three NF1 cell lines were selected to establish a TSPO knockdown cell line (shTSPO) in cells with constitutive TSPO expression, as described in our previous study. After culture for 48 h, the tumor cell-conditioned medium (TCM) from shTSPO cells and the corresponding control (Ctrl) cells were collected and cultured with HUVECs for 48 h. A tube formation assay showed that shTSPO TCM promoted HUVEC tube formation (Fig. 3D). The statistical results showed that the absence of TSPO increased the total segment length, number of nodes and total branch length of the tubes (Fig. 3. E, F and G).

These results indicate that TSPO depletion promotes MPNST angiogenesis in vitro.

TSPO depletion promotes MPNST development by regulating CDK1

To elucidate how TSPO affects MPNST development, we analyzed gene expression profiles via RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) to evaluate the effect of TSPO on the transcriptome. We used pNF05.5 NC, pNF05.5 shTSPO, ST88-14 NC, ST88-14 shTSPO, sNF96.2 NC and sNF96.2 shTSPO cells, with three replicates of each group. As shown in Figs. 4A and 905, 237, and 593 genes were upregulated and 154, 483, and 443 genes were downregulated in shTSPO vs. shCtrl pNF05.5, ST88-14 and sNF96.2, respectively. We used FPKM ≥ 1, log2|shTSPO/NC| fold change ≥ 0.5 and Q ≤ 0.05 as inclusion criteria for identifying genes possibly regulated by the transcription factor TSPO. Heatmaps are presented in Fig. 4B/C (pNF05.5 and sNF96.2) and Supplementary Fig. 2A (ST88-14). Furthermore, we identified the top 20 differentially enriched KEGG pathways (Fig. 4D, E). The cell cycle pathway and P53 signaling pathway were enriched in pNF05.5 and sNF96.2 cells but not in ST88-14 cells (Supplementary Fig. 2B). Similarly, GSEA revealed enrichment of genes related to the cell cycle pathway (Fig. 4F, G). Protein–protein interaction (PPI) network interaction analysis revealed that CDK1 was also a hub gene (Fig. 4H, I).

Based on the RNA-seq results, we believe that CDK1 is a key gene involved in TSPO deficiency affecting the cell cycle. We assessed CDK1 protein expression in the Lv-shTSPO and Lv-shCtrl groups and found that CDK1 was highly expressed in the pNF05.5 and sNF96.2 groups but not in the ST88-14 group (Fig. 5A). Due to the significant differences between ST88-14 and the other two cell lines, pNF05.5 and sNF96.2 were selected for subsequent experimental verification. RT‒PCR confirmed that cell cycle-associated genes (CDK1, CDK2, CCNB1, CCNA2, and CDC25c) were obviously upregulated and that P53 signaling pathway or apoptosis-related genes (MDM2, P27, BCL2, TP53, caspase-3, and caspase-8) were downregulated (Fig. 5B). In addition, CDK1, cyclin B1 and cyclin A2 protein levels were increased in shTSPO pNF05.5 and sNF96.2 cells vs. shCtrl cells (Fig. 5H).

Depletion of CDK1 suppresses tumor growth in vitro

To verify the effect of CDK1 on tumor cells, we used small interfering RNA (siRNA) transfection to detect the proliferation and migration ability of MPNST cells. pNF05.5 and sNF96.2 cells were transfected with siCDK1 and ncCDK1 for 72 h. siCDK1 cells grew much more slowly than cells transfected with the negative control (ncCDK1) according to the CCK-8 assay (P < 0.05, Fig. 5C, D).

Similarly, CDK1 transfection (siCDK1) inhibited wound healing at 72 h pNF05.5 and 48 h in sNF96.2 cells (Fig. 5E). The statistical data of the wound healing experiments clearly demonstrated this result (Fig. 5F, G).

TSPO downregulation suppressed the cell cycle G2/M transition in MPNST cells via the CDK1 and CyclinA2/CyclinB1 pathways

Flow cytometry analysis was performed to explore the underlying mechanism involved in MPNST progression. As confirmed earlier, the results showed that the cell cycle was suppressed in Lv-shTSPO cells but strikingly arrested at the G2/M phase in siCDK1-transfected cells (Fig. 6A, B and C). Thus, we focused on detecting G2/M checkpoint-related protein markers, including CyclinA2, Cyclin B1 and CDK1, via Western blotting. As shown in Fig. 6D and E, all the abovementioned proteins were downregulated in the CDK1-silenced MPNST cells compared with the Lv-shTSPO and Lv-shCtrl cell lines. In summary, TSPO has a strong effect on MPNST cell cycle regulation, and TSPO silencing in GC cells promoted G2/M-phase progression by downregulating CyclinA2, Cyclin B1 and CDK1 (Fig. 6D and E).

TSPO silencing did not promote NF1-associated cell line growth but suppressed the G2/M cell cycle transition in vivo

pNF05.5 cells with or without TSPO knockdown were injected subcutaneously into mice, and an animal model was successfully constructed for TSPO functional validation in vivo. Twenty-one days after infection, the skin tissue was cut, revealing obvious tumor tissue near the nerves beneath the skin (Fig. 8A). After removing the tumor specimens, they were grouped and placed on a tabletop, and their sizes were assessed with a ruler and recorded (Fig. 8B). The tumors were measured, and the results revealed that TSPO deficiency did not significantly affect the size of the subcutaneous tumors at 21 days (Fig. 8C). However, more CDK1-, CyclinA2- and CyclinB1-positive cells were detected in the tumor sections of the shTSPO group than in those of the control group, which further verified the rationality of the above observation (Fig. 8D). Overall, TSPO downregulation arrested the cell cycle in vivo, which was consistent with the in vitro results.

Discussion

MPNSTs are invasiveness and high risks of local recurrence and metastasis22,23. The lack of effectiveness of traditional therapies indicates the need to explore new therapies for MPNSTs. We noted TSPO deficiency in MPNSTs through bioinformatic analysis and determined its correlation with prognosis via tissue RNA-seq. We revealed that TSPO deficiency promoted cell proliferation, migration, and invasion in vitro and influenced apoptosis and the cell cycle. We also showed that TSPO depletion regulated the cell cycle in vivo. Furthermore, transcriptomic analysis revealed that CDK1/cyclin B1 were promoting factors in the underlying mechanism of NF1-associated MPNSTs.

In previous studies, the expression and function of TSPO were inconsistent across different tumors. There was a significant increase in the transcript level in liver16,24, breast cancer14,25, pancreatic cancer26, and brain cancers18,27, while a significant decrease was observed in cancers of the colon and lung25. In fact, TSPO is highly expressed in most cancers but expressed at low levels in a few cancers. However, TSPO has both procancer28 and anticancer effects on gliomas29. TSPO deficiency induces mitochondrial dysfunction, leading to hypoxia, angiogenesis, and a growth-promoting metabolic shift toward glycolysis in glioblastoma29that Fu Y et al. showed. There has been no research on TSPO in NF1. We used 3 pairs of patient tumor tissue samples from patients who had benign neurofibroma and MPNST for transcriptomic analysis. IHC and RT‒PCR were performed on tumor specimens from both benign and malignant patients. We noted that TSPO was expressed at low levels in MPNSTs (Fig. 1). As in previous studies, TSPO knockdown in cell lines promoted cell proliferation and migration and suppressed apoptosis. Moreover, in addition to tumor cell-intrinsic changes, TSPO deficiency also increased angiogenesis, a typical feature of malignant tumors (Fig. 3E). Proangiogenic factors, including various forms of VEGF-A, MMP, and IL-8, participate in triggering the development of the vascular network29–31. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways indicated that low TSPO expression was mainly involved in the cell cycle, the p53 signaling pathway, and DNA replication. Moreover, GSEA revealed that TSPO regulated the G2/M-phase of the cell cycle by interacting with CDK1, CCNA2, and CCNB1 in human MPNSTs.

Cell cycle progression is the core event of all proliferating cells and is mainly driven by cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs)32. CDKs are a group of serine/threonine kinases with crucial roles in the regulation of cell cycle progression33. In addition, CDKs lead to uncontrolled proliferation and cell cycle dysregulation in tumor cells34. Cyclin-dependent kinase 1 (CDK1) is the only necessary CDK that facilitates the G2/M transition and regulates G1 progression and the G1/S transition35,36. Recent studies have shown that CDK1 is frequently overexpressed in hepatocellular carcinoma and is associated with tumor progression37. Similarly, this study revealed that CDK1 was overexpressed in Lv-shTSPO cell lines. Moreover, downregulation of CDK1 inhibited cell proliferation and migration and arrested the cell cycle. Interestingly, downregulation of CDK1 may offset the negative effects of TSPO deficiency, especially in the cell cycle.

In summary, TSPO regulates the progression and development of MPNSTs by targeting CDK1, which is a potential prognostic and therapeutic target for MPNSTs. However, this article also has certain limitations. ST88-14 with lower TSPO expression levels not giving similar results in all experiments when compared to the other lines, and pNF05.5 to represent MPNST cell lines had low persuasiveness in vivo. Hence, we need more experimental data to demonstrate and prove that TSPO is a meaningful target.
