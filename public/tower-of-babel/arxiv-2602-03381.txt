Dynamic Programming for Epistemic Uncertainty in Markov Decision Processes
Axel Benyamine, Julien Grand-Clément, Marek Petrik, Michael I. Jordan, Alain Durmus

Abstract
In this paper, we propose a general theory of ambiguity-averse MDPs, which treats the uncertain transition probabilities as random variables and evaluates a policy via a risk measure applied to its random return. This ambiguity-averse MDP framework unifies several models of MDPs with epistemic uncertainty for specific choices of risk measures. We extend the concepts of value functions and Bellman operators to our setting. Based on these objects, we establish the consequences of dynamic programming principles in this framework (existence of stationary policies, value and policy iteration algorithms), and we completely characterize law-invariant risk measures compatible with dynamic programming. Our work draws connections among several variants of MDP models and fully delineates what is possible under the dynamic programming paradigm and which risk measures require leaving it.



1
Introduction

Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) are a model of sequential decision-making in which an agent repeatedly interacts with a system to optimize rewards over a (possibly infinite) horizon
(
45
)
. It is well-documented that the performance of a policy, which dictates the choice of actions given the current state of the system, may severely deteriorate when the model parameters (such as the transition probabilities across states) are incorrect
(
11
)
. This
epistemic
uncertainty may occur in applications with high stakes, e.g., healthcare
(
17
;
55
)
and vehicle routing
(
40
)
.

The issue of model errors has been addressed in several ways in the literature. Robust MDPs (RMDPs) posit that the true parameters belong to an uncertainty set and optimize for the worst-case parameter realization
(
29
;
42
;
59
)
; the uncertainty set represents all plausible parameter realizations and can be estimated from a dataset.
RMDPs are among the most well-studied MDP models with uncertain kernels, and under appropriate rectangularity assumptions, they can be solved efficiently via dynamic programming
(
59
;
21
)
. Several other frameworks for MDPs with uncertain parameters exist, e.g., multi-model MDPs
(
55
;
56
)
that optimize over the average of the return across plausible parameters, models based on value at risk (percentile optimization)
(
11
;
43
;
3
)
, conditional value at risk (
CVaR
)
(
36
;
35
)
and the entropic risk measure (
ERM
)
(
50
)
.

Overall, several “uncertain MDP” models have been introduced independently, each with its own ad hoc analysis. For some models, computing an optimal policy is tractable via dynamic programming (RMDPs), for others, computing an optimal policy is NP-hard (e.g., for multi-model MDPs and percentile optimization), and for others, the answers to these questions are unknown (e.g., models based on
CVaR
and
ERM
).
One may wonder whether there is a common perspective unifying these models, and, crucially from an application standpoint, whether one could design new models with favorable tractable properties (beyond RMDPs).

In this work, we propose a unifying model for MDPs with uncertain parameters.
Our
contributions
are as follows.

•

We propose
ambiguity-averse MDPs
, a principled approach to epistemic uncertainty in MDPs, where the uncertain transition probabilities are modeled as random variables, and the return is estimated using a risk measure capturing the agent’s risk preferences.
This model unifies most of the literature on MDPs with parameter uncertainty. We then introduce the generalization of the dynamic programming principle for ambiguity-averse MDPs, which states that ambiguity-averse value functions are fixed points of some Bellman operator.

•

For a broad class of risk measures satisfying a set of axioms, we prove that the fundamental properties of the Bellman operators (monotonicity and contraction) are preserved.
We then establish that dynamic programming implies key structural and computational guarantees, most notably, the existence of stationary optimal policies and the validity of value and policy iteration algorithms.

•

Finally, we fully characterize the risk measures for which ambiguity-averse MDPs satisfy dynamic programming. Surprisingly, we show that, under a law-invariance and monotonicity assumption, or under a continuity assumption, dynamic programming forces the objective to collapse to only robust, optimistic, or risk-neutral MDPs (we refer to Theorem
4.4
and Theorem
4.5
for formal statements).

Overall, our work provides a unified perspective on parameter uncertainty in MDPs within the dynamic programming framework. On the one hand, dynamic programming enables several desirable tractability properties, however it also severely limits the choice of risk models (among law-invariant risk measures).
Our work emphasizes that designing further tractable ambiguity-averse MDP instances requires either going beyond dynamic programming and allowing, for instance, state-augmentation
(
8
)
, or solutions based on direct gradient descent of the returns
(
58
)
, or sticking to dynamic programming but going beyond law-invariance (e.g., focusing on nested risk measures
(
51
)
).

In the rest of the paper, we first provide background on MDPs and risk measures, then introduce the framework of ambiguity-averse MDPs, and finally state our main results on the algorithmic implications of dynamic programming and characterize risk measures compatible with dynamic programming. All the proofs are detailed in the appendices and we provide a literature review in Appendix
C
.

Notations

Random variables are denoted by a capital letter and a tilde, e.g.,
\tilde{X}
or
\tilde{S}
. We denote by

L_{c}^{\infty}(B)
the set of bounded random variables with convex support (see for instance Definition
A.1
) that are almost surely in
B
. The simplex over a set
\mathcal{S}
is denoted by
\Delta(\mathcal{S})
. For two vectors
v,w\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{S}}
, the inequality
v\leqslant w
is component-wise:
v(s)\leqslant w(s),\forall\;s\in\mathcal{S}
.

2
Preliminaries

We first introduce (nominal) MDPs and risk measures.

2.1
Nominal MDPs

A (discounted) MDP is a tuple
{\sf M}_{P}=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},r,P,\gamma,\mu)
, where
\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A}
are finite state and action spaces,
r\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S}}
represents the instantaneous rewards; i.e.,
r(s,a,s^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}
is the reward when taking action
a
in state
s
and moving to state
s^{\prime}
, and
P
is a (fixed) transition probability kernel in
\Delta(\mathcal{S})^{\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}
. The vector
\mu\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})
is the initial probability distribution, and the scalar
\gamma\in[0,1)
is a discount factor.
A history-dependent policy maps any finite history
(s_{0},a_{0},s_{1},...,s_{t})
up to period
t\in\mathbb{N}
to a distribution over actions in
\Delta(\mathcal{A})
. A stationary policy can be identified with a map
\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\Delta(\mathcal{A})
.
The set of history-dependent (resp. stationary) policies is denoted by
\Pi_{\sf H}
(resp.
\Pi_{\sf S}
). The goal of the decision-maker is to find an optimal policy that maximizes the expected discounted return; i.e., to solve

\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\sf H}}\mathbb{E}^{\pi,P}_{\mu}\left(\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}r(\tilde{S}_{t},\tilde{A}_{t},\tilde{S}_{t+1})\right)\;,

(1)

where
(\tilde{S}_{t},\tilde{A}_{t})
is the (random) state-action pair visited at time
t
and
\mathbb{E}^{\pi,P}_{\mu}
is the expectation with the measure induced by
\pi,P,\mu
over the trajectories
(\tilde{S}_{t},\tilde{A}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
.
Note that the discounted return is equal to
\mu^{\top}V^{\pi,P}
with
V^{\pi,P}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{S}}
the
value function
which characterizes the return obtained starting from each state and is defined as follows:

V^{\pi,P}(s)=\mathbb{E}^{\pi,P}_{s}\left(\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}r(\tilde{S}_{t},\tilde{A}_{t},\tilde{S}_{t+1})\right)\;,

(2)

for
\pi\in\Pi_{\sf H}
and
s\in\mathcal{S}
.
Given some transitions
P
and a stationary policy
\pi\in\Pi_{\sf S}
, the associated Bellman operator is denoted as
T^{\pi,P}:\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{S}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{S}}
with, for
v\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{S}},s\in\mathcal{S}
,

\displaystyle T^{\pi,P}v(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi(s,a)\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}P(s,a,s^{\prime})\left(r(s,a,s^{\prime})+\gamma v(s^{\prime})\right)\;.

(3)

T^{\pi,P}
is a contraction for the
\ell_{\infty}
-norm, and its unique fixed-point is the value function
V^{\pi,P}

(
45
)
:

V^{\pi,P}=T^{\pi,P}V^{\pi,P}\;.

(4)

Moreover, the optimal value function
V^{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{S}}
defined as
V^{P}(s)=\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\sf H}}V^{\pi,P}(s)
for
s\in\mathcal{S}
, satisfies the following Bellman optimality equations:

V^{P}(s)=\max_{\pi\in\Pi_{\sf S}}T^{\pi,P}V^{P}(s),\forall\;s\in\mathcal{S}\;.

(5)

A stationary optimal policy can be computed by choosing a policy that attains the
\max
in the right-hand side of (
5
).
The fact that value functions and optimal value functions satisfy fixed-point equations as in (
4
) and (
5
) is usually referred to as the
dynamic programming principle

(
57
;
21
)
and plays a central role in the favorable tractability properties of nominal MDPs. In particular, computing an optimal policy can be done efficiently by iteration, policy iteration, linear programming, and gradient descent
(
45
, see, e.g.,)
.
One of the main objectives of our work is to understand, in the case where the transition probabilities are uncertain, when a similar dynamic programming principle holds. To this end, we rely on risk measures that we now introduce.

2.2
Risk measures

A risk measure is a map from random variables to
\mathbb{R}
.
Examples include the expectation:
\rho(\tilde{X})=\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X})
and the Value-at-Risk (
{\sf VaR}_{\alpha}
) defined as the upper
(1-\alpha)
-quantile of
\tilde{X}
:
{\sf VaR}_{\alpha}(\tilde{X})=\inf\{x\in\mathbb{R}\;|\;\mathbb{P}(\tilde{X}\leqslant x)>1-\alpha\}
for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
, as well as the
essential infimum
(
\operatorname{ess\,inf}
) and the
essential supremum
(
\operatorname{ess\,sup}
), defined as

\displaystyle\operatorname{ess\,inf}(\tilde{X})

\displaystyle=\sup\{b\in\mathbb{R}\;|\;\mathbb{P}(\tilde{X}<b)=0\}

\displaystyle\operatorname{ess\,sup}(\tilde{X})

\displaystyle=\inf\{b\in\mathbb{R}\;|\;\mathbb{P}(b<\tilde{X})=0\}

which coincide with the infimum and supremum of the support of
\tilde{X}
(when
\tilde{X}
is bounded, see Lemma
A.2
).
Recent work on risk-averse MDPs has also focused on the Conditional Value-at-Risk (
CVaR
)
(
8
;
16
)
,
the Entropic Risk Measure (
ERM
) and the Entropic Value-at-Risk (
EVaR
)
(
25
)
.
Classical analysis of risk measures focuses on a small number of important properties. We introduce here the ones that play a role in our framework:

•

A risk measure
\rho
is
monotone
if
\rho(\tilde{X})\geqslant\rho(\tilde{Y})
for any random variables
\tilde{X},\tilde{Y}
such that
\tilde{X}\geqslant\tilde{Y}
a.s.

•

A risk measure
\rho
is
translation-invariant
if
\rho(\tilde{X}+c)=\rho(\tilde{X})+c
, for any
c\in\mathbb{R}
and random variable
\tilde{X}
.

•

A risk measure
\rho
is
law-invariant
if it only depends on the law of the random variables, i.e. if
\rho(\tilde{X})=\rho(\tilde{Y})
for any random variables
\tilde{X},\tilde{Y}
that have same law.
We provide an example and a discussion on risk measures that are not law-invariant in Example
A.3
.

Most standard risk measures, including
\mathbb{E}
,
\operatorname{ess\,inf}
,
\operatorname{ess\,sup}
,
{\sf VaR}
,
{\sf CVaR}
,
{\sf ERM}{}
and
{\sf EVaR}{}
, satisfy these properties. Non-law-invariant risk measures exhibit less interpretable behaviours, see Example
A.3
for an example. We refer to Chapter 4 in
14
for an introduction to risk measures and to Appendix
A
for further details.

3
Ambiguity-averse MDPs

3.1
Definition, objectives and examples

In this section, we introduce the framework of
ambiguity-averse MDPs
to model parameter uncertainty in MDPs.
The main idea is to consider the transition probabilities as a random variable
\tilde{P}
with distribution
\nu
, so that the return
\mu^{\top}V^{\pi,\tilde{P}}
is itself a random variable and the decision-maker optimizes for
\pi\mapsto\rho(\mu^{\top}V^{\pi,\tilde{P}})
, where
\rho
is a risk measure.
We focus on uncertain transition probabilities and known rewards (this is the most difficult case, already for RMDPs
(
21
)
).

Definition 3.1
.

A (discounted)
ambiguity-averse MDP

is a tuple
{\sf M}_{\nu}=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},r,\nu,\gamma,\mu)
where
\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},r,\gamma,\mu
are defined exactly as in nominal MDPs (see Section
2
) and
\nu
is a probability distribution over the set
\Delta(\mathcal{S})^{\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}
of feasible transition probabilities.

The distribution
\nu
models the likelihood of each realization of the transition kernels in
\Delta(\mathcal{S})^{\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}
. Uncertainty in transition probabilities is frequent in high-stakes applications where model parametrization is difficult, e.g., in healthcare
(
17
;
55
)
. Since
\Delta(\mathcal{S})^{\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}
is bounded for fixed
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A})
, any measure
\nu
in an ambiguity-averse MDP necessarily has compact support.
The distribution
\nu
naturally induces a distribution for the sequence
(\tilde{P}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
of random kernels for each decision period. To fully specify the dynamics induced by an ambiguity-averse MDP instance, we consider two types of uncertainty:

Definition 3.2
.

Let
{\sf M}_{\nu}
be an ambiguity-averse MDP. The (random) transition kernels
(\tilde{P}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
are
static
if
\tilde{P}_{t}=\tilde{P}_{0}
for any
t\in\mathbb{N}
, with
\tilde{P}_{0}\sim\nu
.
The transition kernels are
resampled
if
(\tilde{P}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
is i.i.d. with distribution
\nu
.

We refer to Appendix
B
for a rigorous formalization of these notions.
In principle, one can extend the model of resampled kernels to independent variables, not necessarily identically distributed, i.e., to the case where
\tilde{P}_{t}\sim\nu_{t}
for each
t\in\mathbb{N}
and some distributions
(\nu_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
.
We consider only the i.i.d. setting here to simplify the exposition of our main results, and because this model reduces to i.i.d. kernels for distributionally robust MDPs; see Appendix
B.1
.
We note that value functions
V^{\pi,\tilde{P}}
naturally extend to the case where
\tilde{P}
represents a sequence of kernels (we slightly overload notation here; see Appendix
B
).
We note that considering static or resampled kernels leads to very different computational implications. For instance, for multi-model MDPs, it is known that static kernels lead to NP-hard problems
(
55
)
while with resampled kernels, multi-model MDPs can be solved efficiently by dynamic programming.

We use the following notion of product structure.

Definition 3.3
.

A distribution
\nu
on
\Delta(\mathcal{S})^{\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}
is said to have a
product structure
if for any
\tilde{P}\sim\nu
, the family of random variables
\{\tilde{P}(s,\cdot,\cdot)\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})^{\mathcal{A}}\,:\,s\in\mathcal{S}\}
is independent.

We say that
{\sf M}_{\nu}
has a product structure when
\nu
does. In this case, the support of
\nu
, which is a subset of the set of kernels
\Delta(\mathcal{S})^{\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}
, has the classical
s-rectangularity property
widely studied for robust MDPs
(
59
)
and necessary for the existence of Bellman equations in the context of robust MDPs
(
21
)
, see Appendix
C
for details.

We will use the following simple example to illustrate the model of ambiguity-averse MDPs.

Example 3.4
.

Figure

provides a simple example of an ambiguity-averse MDP.

There is only a single action and the state
End
is absorbing. The transitions from state
Start
are modeled with a random variable
\tilde{X}
with a uniform distribution on
[0,1]
. Here,
\nu
is the distribution of the random matrix
\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{X}&1-\tilde{X}\\

. In the case of resampled kernels,
\tilde{X}
is resampled i.i.d. at each period
t\in\mathbb{N}
. In the case of static kernels, there is a single sample of
\tilde{X}
at time

, so that the kernel
\tilde{P}_{t}

Given an ambiguity-averse MDP
{\sf M}_{\nu}
, and a risk measure
\rho
, the objective of the decision maker is to solve:

\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\sf H}}\rho\left(\mathbb{E}^{\pi,\tilde{P}}_{\mu}\Big(\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}r(\tilde{S}_{t},\tilde{A}_{t},\tilde{S}_{t+1})\Big)\right)\;,

(6)

where the inner expectation
\mathbb{E}^{\pi,\tilde{P}}_{\mu}
is over random trajectories
(\tilde{S}_{t},\tilde{A}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
and the risk measure
\rho
is over the random kernel sequence
\tilde{P}=(\tilde{P}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
. Note that the objective function in (
6
) can be formulated as
\pi\mapsto\rho\left(\mu^{\top}V^{\pi,\tilde{P}}\right)
, with nominal value functions defined in (
2
).
Similarly to the case of nominal MDPs, we define the
ambiguity-averse value function

V^{\pi,\nu,\rho}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{S}}
under policy
\pi\in\Pi_{\sf H}
as:

V^{\pi,\nu,\rho}(s)=\rho\left(V^{\pi,\tilde{P}}(s)\right),\quad\forall s\in\mathcal{S}\;,

where we recall that the nominal value function is defined in Equation (
2
).
We recall that the randomness (for the risk measure
\rho
) is only in
\tilde{P}
.
The
optimal ambiguity-averse value function

V^{\nu,\rho}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{S}}
is

V^{\nu,\rho}(s)=\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\sf H}}V^{\pi,\nu,\rho}(s),\quad\forall s\in\mathcal{S}\;.

Examples and related work

We briefly overview some important examples of ambiguity-averse MDPs here; see Appendix
C

•

Nominal MDPs
with (fixed) kernel
P
correspond to static kernels with
\nu
a Dirac distribution supported on
P
, and
\rho(\tilde{X})=\mathbb{E}^{\nu}(\tilde{X})

\rho(\mu^{\top}V^{\pi,\tilde{P}})=\mu^{\top}V^{\pi,P}

•

Robust MDPs
with compact convex uncertainty set
\mathcal{P}

\nu
supported on
\mathcal{P}
, and
\rho(\tilde{X})=\operatorname{ess\,inf}(\tilde{X})

(

)

\rho(\mu^{\top}V^{\pi,\tilde{P}})=\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mu^{\top}V^{\pi,P}

The case of robust MDPs with Markovian kernels can be modeled with resampled kernels, see Appendix

We also cover

for

\rho(\tilde{X})=\mathbb{E}^{\nu}\left(\tilde{X}\right)

(

)
:
\rho(\mu^{\top}V^{\pi,\tilde{P}})=\mathbb{E}^{\nu}\left(\mu^{\top}V^{\pi,\tilde{P}}\right)

•

Percentile Optimization

\nu

. Similarly, we can recover related frameworks that use risk measures other than

, referred to as soft-robustness
(
36
)
or Bayes risk-aversion
(
35
)
, by choosing the appropriate risk measures.

Remark 3.5
.

Distributionally robust MDPs
(DRMDPs) require an
ambiguity set
of plausible distributions and not just a single distribution
\nu
as in Definition
3.1
. To model DRMDPs as ambiguity-averse MDPs, one would need the formalism of
ambiguous probability space

(
10
)
, requiring considerably more notation and formalization. This extension is left as an interesting next step.

Remark 3.6
.

We compare here the ambiguity-averse MDP framework to
risk-averse
MDPs
(
51
;
24
, see, e.g.,)
, which considers a single, known transition kernel
P
and hedges against the variability of the return over the random trajectories (sometimes called
aleatoric risk
) with a risk measure by solving

\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\sf H}}\rho\left(\sum_{t=0}^{+\infty}\gamma^{t}r(\tilde{S}_{t},\tilde{A}_{t},\tilde{S}_{t+1})\right)\;.

In contrast, our model (
6
) considers the expected return over the trajectories, and hedges against the uncertainty in parameters (sometimes called
epistemic risk
) with a risk measure; see Appendix
C.2
for more comparisons.

3.2
Dynamic programming

For a risk measure
\rho
, we define the
ambiguity-averse Bellman operator

T^{\pi,\nu,\rho}:\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{S}}\to\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{S}}
associated with a stationary policy
\pi\in\Pi_{\sf S}
and a random transition kernel following law
\nu
with identical marginals over time (static or resampled) as, for
v\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{S}}
,

T^{\pi,\nu,\rho}v(s)=\rho\left(T^{\pi,\tilde{P}}v(s)\right),\quad\forall s\in\mathcal{S},

(7)

where
\tilde{P}\sim\nu
on the right-hand side of (
7
) and
T^{\pi,\tilde{P}}
is the standard Bellman operator for nominal MDPs as defined in (
3
).
We then define the
optimal ambiguity-averse Bellman operator

T^{\nu,\rho}:\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{S}}\to\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{S}}
as, for
v\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{S}}
,

T^{\nu,\rho}v(s)=\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\sf S}}T^{\pi,\nu,\rho}v(s),\quad\forall s\in\mathcal{S}\;.

(8)

The operators
T^{\pi,\nu,\rho},T^{\nu,\rho}
play the same role as the Bellman operators
T^{\pi,P}
and
T^{P}
(introduced in Section
2
) in the theory of nominal MDPs and inherit their properties under some conditions on the risk measure
\rho
, as we show next.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
\rho
be a monotone, translation-invariant risk measure. Then we have the following properties for the operator
T\in\{T^{\pi,\nu,\rho},T^{\nu,\rho}\}
:

1.

Monotonicity: for any

Main Results

This section focuses on ambiguity-averse MDPs under the lens of dynamic programming. We first

4.1
Computing optimal policies

Dynamic programming has several useful practical and computational consequences, as it reduces policy evaluation and policy optimization to fixed-point computations. In fact, when
1
and
2
both hold, an optimal policy can be chosen to be stationary and can be recovered from the optimal value function, as we show next.

Theorem 4.1
.

Assume that
\rho
is monotone and translation-invariant, and that
1
and
2
hold for static or resampled kernels. Let
{\sf M}_{\nu}\in\mathcal{M}_{c}

\pi^{\star}\in\Pi_{\sf S}
that is optimal for starting from any state:

\exists\;\pi^{\star}\in\Pi_{\sf S},\forall\;s\in\mathcal{S},\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\sf H}}\rho\left(V^{\pi,\tilde{P}}(s)\right)=\rho\left(V^{\pi^{\star},\tilde{P}}(s)\right).

Moreover,
T^{\nu,\rho}
is a contraction and any policy
\pi\in\Pi_{\sf S}
such that
T^{\nu,\rho}V^{\nu,\rho}=T^{\pi,\nu,\rho}V^{\nu,\rho}
is optimal.

Theorem

Note that when dynamic programming holds for

4.2
Dynamic programming compatibility

We now characterize the risk measures for which
1
and
2
hold. We focus solely on

We first establish some important implications of dynamic programming, namely, a law-invariant risk measure that satisfies either

Let

\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R},\quad\rho(x)=x\;.

\rho
is positive homogeneous on

\quad\forall\tilde{X}\in L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}),\forall\alpha\geqslant 0,\quad\rho(\alpha\tilde{X})=\alpha\rho(\tilde{X})\;.

3.

\rho
is additive independent on
L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
:

for any independent
1

1

\tilde{X},\tilde{Y}\in L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})

\rho(\tilde{X}+\tilde{Y})=\rho(\tilde{X})+\rho(\tilde{Y})\;

Our first characterization requires the assumption that

\rho(\tilde{X})=\int_{\overline{\mathbb{R}}}{\sf ERM}_{a}(\tilde{X})d\mu(a),\quad\forall\;\tilde{X}\in L_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})\;,

.

Let

2.

3.

\rho

1.

\rho
satisfies

2.

\rho

Additivity and positive homogeneity give that

4.3
Discussion

Main takeaways

We have established that dynamic programming implies several useful properties, including the existence of stationary optimal policies and the extension of classical algorithms to the ambiguity-averse framework.
We have also shown that, in the class of monotone law-invariant risk measures, only
\operatorname{ess\,inf},\operatorname{ess\,sup}
and
\mathbb{E}
can satisfy dynamic programming equations. The risk measures
\operatorname{ess\,inf}
and
\operatorname{ess\,sup}
correspond to robust MDPs
(
42
;
29
)
. Our impossibility results explain why no new model compatible with dynamic programming has emerged in the wake of those seminal papers, despite substantial interest in other alternatives involving
VaR
or
CVaR
. Assuming stronger continuity properties (e.g.,
W^{1}
-continuity) only further restricts the class of risk measures compatible with dynamic programming.

Our work also clarifies the computational role of how uncertain kernels change over time. Static kernels induce significantly harder optimization problems than resampled kernels, mirroring the corresponding distinction for multi-model MDPs.
In fact, we note that our results closely align with the separations between NP-hard problems that require history-dependent policies (multi-model MDPs and percentile optimization with static kernels, e.g.,
11
;
55
) and models that can be solved efficiently and for which optimal stationary policies exist (e.g., robust and optimistic MDPs for both static and resampled kernels, see
59
;
19
). It would be interesting to understand if
1
and
2
are “equivalent” (in some sense) to the existence of optimal stationary policies.
We also note that, under the assumptions of Theorem
4.4
or of Theorem
4.5
, we establish that dynamic programming is valid for policy evaluation (
1
) if and only if it is valid for policy optimization (
2
). This is surprising since
1
imposes a fixed-point equation for the value functions of all stationary policies, whereas
2
imposes a fixed-point equation only for the optimal value function.

Limitations and assumptions

The translation-invariance and monotonicity assumptions are quite natural when the objective is to maximize returns, and are widely used in the risk measure literature.
As discussed in Section
3.2
, we could have written
1
and
2
without the restriction to instances with convex support and product structure, but (a) this would actually lead to more restrictive conditions, since the fixed point equations would need to hold for a larger set of instances, and (b) robust MDPs and optimistic MDPs would not satisfy these more restrictive conditions
(
57
;
21
)
. So in a sense, our formulations of dynamic programming as in
1
and
2
are the “most general formulations” for which we can recover robust and optimistic MDPs.
Finally, we show in Appendix
I
that relaxed versions of
1
and
2
, where the risk measure is allowed to be different in the objective function and the Bellman operators, are equivalent to our formulation of
1
and
2
.

Beyond dynamic programming

Our results do not preclude that one can solve the ambiguity-averse MDP problem (
6
) via other types of algorithms than dynamic programming over the set of states. In particular, while our results imply that attractive
law-invariant
risk criteria (such as
VaR
or
CVaR
) do not satisfy dynamic programming for value functions indexed solely by states, it is possible that one could obtain DP equations on
augmented
state sets as in risk-averse MDPs
(
8
;
24
)
. Another option would be to drop the law-invariance assumption and take inspiration from nested risk measures
(
51
)
to recover DP equations over the set of states (at the price of less interpretable risk criteria). Generalizing the dynamic principles to
collections of risk measures
in the same spirit as Bellman closedness in distributional reinforcement learning
(
49
)
is also an interesting next step.
Finally, it is worth noting that direct optimization of the objective function (
6
) (e.g., via gradient descent) is possible as soon as one can compute gradient estimates, although the potential absence of optimal
stationary
policies may be an issue for practical implementation.

5
Conclusion

We have introduced a unified theory of epistemic uncertainty in MDPs, in which the transition probabilities are treated as random variables. This ambiguity-averse MDP framework subsumes several existing models depending on the choice of risk measures in the objective functions. We define the ambiguity-averse equivalent of value functions and derive the fundamental properties of Bellman operators. Our main results show both the “advantages” of dynamic programming (stationary optimality and classical algorithmic machinery), and its modeling implications, as we provide a comprehensive characterization of law-invariant risk measures compatible with dynamic programming.
This provides useful takeaways for applications, where we can choose to stick with robust/optimistic formulations for dynamic programming tractability, or where we must anticipate the need for more advanced optimization methods (e.g., augmented state sets or nested risk measures).

It is worth investigating in more detail the connections with other variants, such as risk-averse MDPs, regularized MDPs, and distributionally robust MDPs.
Studying ambiguity-averse MDPs with other objectives (e.g., total or average return, Blackwell optimality) or with non-law-invariant risk measures is also a promising research direction. Deriving policy-gradient methods or value function approximation methods for ambiguity-averse MDPs with general risk measures is also an interesting next step.

Appendix A
Risk measures

In this section, we define general risk measures rigorously, present some common examples, and outline some of the most studied properties of risk measures. We follow the lines of Chapter 4 in
14
and Appendix A in
25
here.

Definitions.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space. The set
L(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P};\mathbb{R})
is the set of real random variables, i.e. of
\mathcal{F}
-measurable functions from
\Omega
to
\mathbb{R}
.
A risk measure
\rho
is a map from the set
L(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P};\mathbb{R})
to
\mathbb{R}
.

Definition A.1

(Support of a Random Variable and Support of a Measure)
.

The
support
of a random variable
\tilde{X}
is denoted
{\sf supp}(\tilde{X})
, and it is defined as the smallest closed set
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathbb{P}(\tilde{X}\in C)=1
.

More generally, for a probability measure
\nu
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the
support

{\sf supp}(\nu)
of
\nu
is the smallest closed set
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\nu(C)=1
, or equivalently, the complement of the largest open set having
\nu
-measure zero.

We say that a measure
\nu
has convex support if its support
{\sf supp}(\nu)
is convex, i.e., for any
\lambda\in[0,1]
and
P,P^{\prime}\in{\sf supp}(\nu)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have
\lambda P+(1-\lambda)P^{\prime}\in{\sf supp}(\nu)
. Accordingly, we say that a random variable
\tilde{X}
has convex support if its support
{\sf supp}(\tilde{X})
is convex.

Since by definition
{\sf supp}(\tilde{X})
is closed, we have that
{\sf supp}(\tilde{X})
is compact if and only if the random variable
\tilde{X}
is a.s. bounded. Similarly, the support of a measure
\nu
is compact if and only if there exists a bounded set with full
\nu
-measure.

Properties.

Risk measures satisfying the monotonicity and translation-invariance properties given in Section
2.2
are sometimes called
monetary
. A risk measure is said to be
concave
if it is monetary and it satisfies, for any random variables
\tilde{X},\tilde{Y}
and scalar
\lambda\in[0,1]
, the following inequality:

\rho(\lambda\tilde{X}+(1-\lambda)\tilde{Y})\geqslant\lambda\rho(\tilde{X})+(1-\lambda)\rho(\tilde{Y})\;.

A
coherent
risk measure is a concave risk measure that is positively homogeneous, i.e., that satisfies the following condition:

\forall\;\alpha\geqslant 0,\;\forall\tilde{X},\quad\rho(\alpha\tilde{X})=\alpha\rho(\tilde{X})\;.

Finally, a risk measure
\rho
is
law-invariant
if it only depends on the law of its argument, i.e. if
\rho(\tilde{X})=\rho(\tilde{Y})
when
\mathcal{L}(\tilde{X})=\mathcal{L}(\tilde{Y})
, where
\mathcal{L}(\tilde{X})
is the law of the random variable
\tilde{X}
. Note that we will generalize this notion for the ambiguity-averse MDP framework in Appendix
B
and Section
3.1
.

The essential supremum

\operatorname{ess\,inf}
and

Proof of Lemma

.

Recall that by definition (Definition

), the support of

\mathbb{P}(\tilde{X}\in C)=1

Let

\operatorname{ess\,sup}(\tilde{X})\leqslant M

{\sf VaR}_{\alpha}
) is defined as the upper
(1-\alpha)
-quantile:

{\sf VaR}_{\alpha}(\tilde{X})=\inf\{x\in\mathbb{R}\;|\;\mathbb{P}(\tilde{X}\leqslant x)>1-\alpha\}

for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
, and the Conditional Value-at-Risk (
{\sf CVaR}_{\alpha}
) is the expectation of the worst
(1-\alpha)
-fraction of
\tilde{X}
.
CVaR
can be computed via the following formula:

{\sf CVaR}_{\alpha}(\tilde{X})=\sup_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}}\xi-\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\mathbb{E}(\max\{\xi-\tilde{X},0\})\;.

(10)

CVaR
is a concave risk measure, and positively homogeneous, thus
CVaR
is coherent. In contrast,
VaR
is only monetary. Note we can extend
CVaR
by continuity, as
{\sf CVaR}_{0}(\tilde{X})=\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}),{\sf CVaR}_{1}(\tilde{X})=\operatorname{ess\,inf}(\tilde{X})
.

•

The Entropic Risk Measure (
ERM
) at risk level
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
is defined as

{\sf ERM}_{\beta}(\tilde{X})=\frac{1}{\beta}\log(\mathbb{E}(\exp(\beta\cdot\tilde{X})))

extended by continuity as
{\sf ERM}_{0}(\tilde{X})=\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}){\sf ERM}_{+\infty}(\tilde{X})=\operatorname{ess\,sup}(\tilde{X}),{\sf ERM}_{-\infty}(\tilde{X})=\operatorname{ess\,inf}(\tilde{X})
.
ERM
is convex for
\beta>0
and concave for
\beta\leqslant 0
.
The Entropic Value-at-Risk (
EVaR
) is defined as

{\sf EVaR}_{\alpha}(\tilde{X})=\sup_{\beta>0}{\sf ERM}_{\beta}(\tilde{X})+\frac{\log(1-\alpha)}{\beta}\;.

We note that
ERM
is concave but not coherent, while
EVaR
is a coherent risk measure. We refer to
25
for recent advances in using
ERM
and
EVaR
in risk-averse MDPs.

Note that most of this work focuses on risk measures that are law-invariant. For the sake of completeness, we provide a simple example of a non-law-invariant risk measure below.

Example A.3

(A Non-Law-Invariant Risk Measure)
.

Consider a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
where
\Omega=\{\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\}
with
\mathbb{P}(\{\omega_{i}\})=1/2
for
i\in\{1,2\}
. Define a risk measure
\rho
as follows:
for a random variable
\tilde{X}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
, let
\rho(\tilde{X})=\tilde{X}(\omega_{1})+\mathbb{E}[\tilde{X}]
, i.e., the risk measure depends explicitly on the value at a specific outcome
\omega=\omega_{1}
in addition to the expectation.

This risk measure is
not law-invariant
. To see this, consider two random variables:

\displaystyle\tilde{X}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}10&\text{if }\omega=\omega_{1}\\
0&\text{if }\omega=\omega_{2}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\tilde{Y}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }\omega=\omega_{1}\\
10&\text{if }\omega=\omega_{2}\end{cases}

Both
\tilde{X}
and
\tilde{Y}
have the same law: each takes the value
10
with probability
1/2
and the value
0
with probability
1/2
.

However, we have:

\displaystyle\rho(\tilde{X})

\displaystyle=\tilde{X}(\omega_{1})+\mathbb{E}[\tilde{X}]=10+10/2=15

\displaystyle\rho(\tilde{Y})

\displaystyle=\tilde{Y}(\omega_{1})+\mathbb{E}[\tilde{Y}]=0+10/2=5

Since
\rho(\tilde{X})\neq\rho(\tilde{Y})
despite the fact that
\tilde{X},\tilde{Y}
have the same law, the risk measure
\rho
is not law-invariant. The dependence on the specific outcome
\tilde{X}(\omega_{1})
violates law-invariance because the risk measure distinguishes between different realizations of the same distribution.

In the context of ambiguity-averse MDPs, non-law-invariant risk measures would depend on the specific sample path or realization of the uncertain transition kernel, rather than only on its distribution. This makes them less interpretable and difficult to work with, as they require tracking which particular realization of the uncertainty occurred, rather than just its statistical properties.

Remark A.4
.

We conclude by noting that risk measures are sometimes introduced for
minimizing costs
(e.g. this is the point of view adopted in
52
),
instead of
maximizing rewards
as in the present paper. These two settings are equivalent, in the sense that if we have a risk measure
\phi
for minimizing costs, then we can consider the risk measure for maximizing reward
\rho(\tilde{X}):=-\phi(-\tilde{X})
, which induces the same preferences over the random variables
\tilde{X}
modeling rewards than the risk measure
\phi
over random variables
-\tilde{X}
modeling costs. It is worth noting that, in the cost minimization framework, the role of concavity is replaced by that of convexity and translation invariance becomes
\phi(\tilde{X}+c)=\phi(\tilde{X})-c
for
\tilde{X}
a random variable and
c\in\mathbb{R}
.

Appendix B
Randomization and Risk Measure for Ambiguity-Averse MDPs

In this section, we provide a rigorous foundation for the ambiguity-averse MDPs introduced in Section
3
. We decide to keep this construction in the appendices to simplify the exposition of our results in the main body.
Overall, our goal is to provide a rigorous framework for the objective functions of the form
\pi\mapsto\rho(\mu^{\top}V^{\pi,\tilde{P}})
that appear in the ambiguity-averse MDP optimization problem (
6
). Recall that by definition, a risk measure is a map from the set of real random variables
L(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
. However, to rigorously model ambiguity-averse MDPs, we need one probability space
(\Omega_{{\sf M}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}})
per instance
{\sf M}
of ambiguity-averse MDP, and in principle, there is a distinct risk measure for each ambiguity-averse MDP instance (since the set of arguments
(\Omega_{{\sf M}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}})
depends on the instance). We therefore extend the notion of risk measure (defined in Appendix
A
for a single probability space) to risk measures for ambiguity-averse MDPs (Definition
B.2
), which can take as arguments random variables induced by different ambiguity-averse MDP instances (i.e., induced by different probability spaces).

Notation for sets of ambiguity-averse MDPs

Recall that we denote by
\mathcal{M}_{c}
the class of all “convex, product” ambiguity-averse MDPs, i.e. the set of all
{\sf M}_{\nu}=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},r,\nu,\gamma,\mu)
with any
\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},r,\gamma,\mu
, and any
\nu
with convex support and product structure.
We also denote by
\mathcal{M}
the set of all ambiguity-averse MDPs, i.e. the set of all
{\sf M}_{\nu}=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},r,\nu,\gamma,\mu)
with any
\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},r,\gamma,\mu
and any
\nu
(not necessarily with convex support or product structure).

Probability space for an instance.

We first define a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
for each ambiguity-averse MDP.

We first provide a slightly more rigorous definition of an ambiguity-averse MDP, which allows us to define a distribution over
sequences
of transition probabilities directly.

Definition B.1
.

A (discounted)
ambiguity-averse MDP

is a tuple
{\sf M}_{\hat{\nu}}=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},r,\hat{\nu},\gamma,\mu)
where
\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},r,\gamma,\mu
are defined exactly as in nominal MDPs (see Section
2
) and
\hat{\nu}
is a probability distribution over the set
\left(\Delta(\mathcal{S})^{\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}\right)^{\mathbb{N}}
of feasible sequences of transition probabilities.

When
\hat{\nu}
is equal to
\nu^{\otimes\mathbb{N}}
for a certain measure
\nu\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})^{\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}
,
{\sf M}_{\hat{\nu}}
corresponds to an ambiguity-averse MDP with resampled kernels according to
\nu
. Here
\nu^{\otimes\mathbb{N}}
is the
i.i.d.
distribution over the path space
(\Delta(\mathcal{S})^{\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}})^{\mathbb{N}}
. When
\hat{\nu}
is equal to the distribution of
(\tilde{P}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\tilde{P}_{0}\sim\nu
and
\tilde{P}_{t}=\tilde{P}_{0}
for any
t
,
{\sf M}_{\hat{\nu}}
corresponds to an ambiguity-averse MDP with static kernel.

For any ambiguity-averse MDP
{\sf M}_{\hat{\nu}}=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},r,\hat{\nu},\gamma,\mu)\in\mathcal{M}
, we define the uncertainty in the model transition kernel sequences via a probability space
(\Omega_{{\sf M}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}})
, where:

•

(\Omega_{{\sf M}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}})
is a measurable space with
\Omega_{{\sf M}}=\left(\Delta(\mathcal{S})^{\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}\right)^{\mathbb{N}}
the set of transition kernel sequences and
\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}}
the canonical
\sigma

\mathcal{B}(\Omega_{{\sf M}})=\mathcal{B}(\Delta(\mathcal{S})^{\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}})^{\otimes\mathbb{N}}
,

•

\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}}=\hat{\nu}
is a probability measure on
(\Omega_{{\sf M}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}})
.

Without loss of generality, we consider
(\tilde{P}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
to be the canonical process
\tilde{P}_{t}:(p_{s})_{s\in\mathbb{N}}\mapsto p_{t}
. In particular, it follows law
\hat{\nu}
in the sense of Definition
B.1
.

When
\hat{\nu}
is non-atomic (i.e., it has no set of positive measure that contains no subsets of smaller positive measure), the probability space
(\Omega_{{\sf M}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}})
is
nonatomic

(i.e., it supports a random variable uniformly distributed on

is rich enough to contain real bounded random variables with arbitrary distributions, see for instance
10
.

Note that by Definition
3.1
, the set
\mathcal{M}_{c}
of all ambiguity-averse MDPs such that the measure has convex support and product structure contains ambiguity-averse MDPs with non-atomic measure in both static kernels or resampled kernels cases (say for example the ambiguity-averse MDP of Example

in both static and resampled cases).

Formally, denoting by
\mathfrak{D}
the set of all distributions with bounded support (i.e.,
\mathfrak{D}
is the set of non-decreasing right-continuous functions
F:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
that attain both

and

), for any

there exists
{\sf M}_{\nu}\in\mathcal{M}_{c}
and
\tilde{X}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega_{{\sf M}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}};\mathbb{R})
such that
F_{\tilde{X}}=F
, where

is the distribution function of
\tilde{X}
under
\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}_{\nu}}
defined through
F_{\tilde{X}}(x)=\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}}(\tilde{X}\leqslant x)
.

Notation for sets of random variables

Throughout this work, we denote by
L^{\infty}(B)=\cup_{{\sf M}\in\mathcal{M}}L^{\infty}(\Omega_{{\sf M}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}};B)
the set of all bounded real random variables with support in

L^{\infty}_{c}(B)=\cup_{{\sf M}\in\mathcal{M}}L^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega_{{\sf M}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}};B)

Note that, by nonatomicity of some probability spaces

whether we consider static or resampled kernels), the set

(resp.

Risk measures

We now define the notion of risk measure for ambiguity-averse MDPs:

Definition B.2

(Risk Measure for Ambiguity-Averse MDPs)
.

A
risk measure for ambiguity-averse MDPs
is a family
\rho=(\rho_{{\sf M}})_{{\sf M}\in\mathcal{M}}
where each
\rho_{{\sf M}}
is a risk measure on the corresponding probability space:

\forall{\sf M}\in\mathcal{M},\quad\rho_{{\sf M}}:L^{\infty}(\Omega_{{\sf M}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}};\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R}\;.

We also define the following properties of a risk measure
\rho
. We say that a risk measure
\rho
is:

•

law-invariant
on
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
(resp.
L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
) if for any
{\sf M},{\sf M}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}
and
\tilde{X},\tilde{X}^{\prime}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega_{{\sf M}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}};\mathbb{R})\times L^{\infty}(\Omega_{{\sf M}^{\prime}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}^{\prime}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}^{\prime}};\mathbb{R})
(resp.
L^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega_{{\sf M}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}};\mathbb{R})\times L^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega_{{\sf M}^{\prime}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}^{\prime}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}^{\prime}};\mathbb{R})
),

F_{\tilde{X}}=F_{\tilde{X}^{\prime}}\implies\rho_{{\sf M}}(\tilde{X})=\rho_{{\sf M}^{\prime}}(\tilde{X}^{\prime})\;.

•

non-constant
on
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
(resp.
L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
) if there exists
{\sf M},{\sf M}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}
and
\tilde{X},\tilde{X}^{\prime}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega_{{\sf M}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}};\mathbb{R})\times L^{\infty}(\Omega_{{\sf M}^{\prime}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}^{\prime}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}^{\prime}};\mathbb{R})
(resp.
L^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega_{{\sf M}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}};\mathbb{R})\times L^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega_{{\sf M}^{\prime}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}^{\prime}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}^{\prime}};\mathbb{R})
) such that

\rho_{{\sf M}}(\tilde{X})\neq\rho_{{\sf M}^{\prime}}(\tilde{X}^{\prime})\;.

•

monotone
on
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
(resp.
L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
) if for any
{\sf M}\in\mathcal{M}
and
\tilde{X},\tilde{Y}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega_{{\sf M}^{\prime}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}^{\prime}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}^{\prime}};\mathbb{R})
(resp.
L^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega_{{\sf M}^{\prime}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}^{\prime}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}^{\prime}};\mathbb{R})
) such that
\tilde{X}\geqslant\tilde{Y}
a.s.,

\rho_{{\sf M}}(\tilde{X})\geqslant\rho_{{\sf M}}(\tilde{Y})\;.

For ease of notation, we drop the subscript
{\sf M}
when considering risk measures for ambiguity-averse MDPs that are law-invariant on
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
(resp.
L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
) and note
\rho_{{\sf M}}(\tilde{X})
as
\rho(\tilde{X})
for
\tilde{X}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega_{{\sf M}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}};\mathbb{R})
(resp.
\tilde{X}\in L^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega_{{\sf M}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}};\mathbb{R})
).

As expected, we also have the following characterization of law-invariant risk measures for ambiguity-averse MDPs:

Proposition B.3

(Existence and Uniqueness of
\varrho
)
.

If
\rho
is a risk measure for ambiguity-averse MDPs that is law invariant on
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, then there exists a unique functional
\varrho:\mathfrak{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that:

\rho_{{\sf M}}(\tilde{X})=\varrho(F_{\tilde{X}})\,,\quad\forall{\sf M}\in\mathcal{M},\forall\tilde{X}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega_{{\sf M}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}};\mathbb{R})\;.

The proof directly follows from Proposition 1 in
10
.

Proof.

Denoting by
{\sf M}_{0}
the MDP of Example
E.1
(whether we consider static or resampled kernels), we have that
(\Omega_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}_{0}})
is nonatomic, so by Proposition 1 in
10
there exists a unique functional
\varrho:\mathfrak{D}\to\mathbb{R}
such that for any
\tilde{X}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}_{0}};\mathbb{R})
:

\rho_{{\sf M}_{0}}(\tilde{X})=\varrho(F_{\tilde{X}}).

Now, for any
{\sf M}\in\mathcal{M}
and
\tilde{X}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega_{{\sf M}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}};\mathbb{R})
, because
(\Omega_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}_{0}})
is nonatomic, there exists
\tilde{X}_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}_{0}};\mathbb{R})
such that
F_{\tilde{X}}=F_{\tilde{X}_{0}}
. Therefore, by law invariance of
\rho
:

\rho_{{\sf M}}(\tilde{X})=\rho_{{\sf M}_{0}}(\tilde{X}_{0})=\varrho(F_{\tilde{X}_{0}})=\varrho(F_{\tilde{X}})\;.

Finally, if
\varrho,\varrho^{\prime}
are such that
\forall{\sf M}\in\mathcal{M},\forall\tilde{X}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega_{{\sf M}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}};\mathbb{R}),\quad\varrho^{\prime}(F_{\tilde{X}})=\rho_{{\sf M}}(\tilde{X})=\varrho(F_{\tilde{X}})
, then, because
(\Omega_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}_{0}})
is nonatomic, for any
F\in\mathfrak{D}
there exists
\tilde{X}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}_{0}};\mathbb{R})
such that
F_{\tilde{X}}=F
, and therefore
\varrho^{\prime}(F)=\varrho(F)
. This shows uniqueness of
\varrho
.
∎

Remark B.4
.

When adding or multiplying random variables
\tilde{X}
and
\tilde{Y}
, it is important to note that the risk measure
\rho
is only defined on bounded random variables living in one of the probability spaces
\left((\Omega_{{\sf M}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}})\right)_{{\sf M}\in\mathcal{M}}
. Consequently, to make sense of expressions like
\rho(\tilde{X}+\tilde{Y})
or
\rho(\tilde{X}\cdot\tilde{Y})
, the random variables
\tilde{X}
and
\tilde{Y}
must be defined on the same probability space
(\Omega_{{\sf M}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}})
for some ambiguity-averse MDP
{\sf M}\in\mathcal{M}
. When we state properties like additivity or multiplicativity for independent random variables, we implicitly assume they are defined on the same probability space and are independent with respect to the probability measure on that space. For instance, a rigorous formulation of Statement
3
of Proposition
4.3
would be:

Additive independence on
L^{\infty}_{c}
:

\text{For any }{\sf M}\in\mathcal{M}\text{ and independent }\tilde{X},\tilde{Y}\in L^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega_{{\sf M}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}};\mathbb{R}),\text{ we have }\rho(\tilde{X}+\tilde{Y})=\rho(\tilde{X})+\rho(\tilde{Y}).

B.1
More details on resampled kernels

In this section, we provide more details on the resampled kernels setup.

Value functions for sequences of kernels

For
\pi\in\Pi_{\sf H}
and
s\in\mathcal{S}
, we naturally extend the definition of the value function for nominal MDPs (see Equation (
2
)) to the case of a non-random transition kernel sequence
(P_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
that changes over time. In this setup, the value function
V^{\pi,(P_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{S}}
depends on
\pi
and
(P_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
and can be written as, for
s\in\mathcal{S}
,

V^{\pi,(P_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}}(s)=\mathbb{E}^{\pi,(P_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}}_{s}\Big(\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}r(\tilde{S}_{t},\tilde{A}_{t},\tilde{S}_{t+1})\Big)\;.

where
\mathbb{E}^{\pi,(P_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}}_{s}
is the expectation over the random trajectories
(\tilde{S}_{t},\tilde{A}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
starting in state
s
, with the distribution over trajectories induced by
\pi
and
(P_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
. In the paper, we choose to overload the notation
V^{\pi,\tilde{P}}
when
\tilde{P}=(\tilde{P}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
for the sake of simplicity (instead of writing
V^{\pi,(\tilde{P}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}}
).

Dynamic programming for non-i.i.d. kernels

We provide here a brief discussion of the case of resampled kernels with non-i.i.d. kernels. In particular, assume that
\tilde{P}_{t}\sim\nu_{t}
for each period
t\in\mathbb{N}
with each
\nu_{t}
having a product structure,
\tilde{P}_{t}\perp\!\!\!\perp\tilde{P}_{t}^{\prime}
for each
t\neq t^{\prime}
, but where the law
\nu_{t}
may be different at each period. To recover a dynamic programming equation, one needs to index value functions with a time index
t\in\mathbb{N}
, a situation similar to the case of finite horizon MDPs (see Chapter 4 in
45
).
For distributionally robust MDPs,
60
, the case of non-i.i.d. resampled kernels is called
nonstationary model
and the case of i.i.d. resampled kernels is called
stationary model
, see beginning of Section 4 in
60
; the term
stationary
/
nonstationary
refers to the to the stationarity of the sequence
(\nu_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
which is a stationary sequence of
\nu_{t}=\nu_{t^{\prime}},\forall\;t,t^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
. A central result shown by
60
is the case of
i.i.d. resampled kernels and non-i.i.d. resampled kernels are equivalent
in the sense that they yield the same optimal value functions and optimal policies (see Theorem 4.1 and Theorem 4.2 in
60
).
This diminishes the practical interest of the non-i.i.d. case, and this is one of the main reasons why we left a more formal analysis of this non-i.i.d. framework for future work.

Appendix C
Connection with previous models

In this section, we provide rigorous reformulations of several of the common variants of MDPs as ambiguity-averse MDPs. We also provide a literature review, discussing the connection of ambiguity-averse MDPs with risk-averse MDPs.

C.1
Reformulations and related work on MDPs with uncertain parameters

In this appendix we provide a rigorous reformulation of some of the classical variants of MDP models as ambiguity-averse MDPs. We summarize these reformulations in Table
1
.

Nominal MDPs

Consider a nominal MDP with transition probability
P\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})^{\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}
. We note that in the standard MDP model, the transition probabilities are fixed over time. Therefore, the transition kernel sequence is static. Consider a Dirac measure
\nu
that puts all its mass at
P:\nu=\delta_{P}
(we denote by
\delta_{x}
the distribution that puts a mass of
1
on an element
x
). Then
\rho(\mu^{\top}V^{\pi,\tilde{P}})=\rho(\mu^{\top}V^{\pi,P})
. We additionally choose
\rho=\mathbb{E}^{\nu}
. Because
\mu^{\top}V^{\pi,P}
is a constant, we get that
\rho(\mu^{\top}V^{\pi,P})=\mu^{\top}V^{\pi,P}
and we recover the standard objective for nominal MDPs. This shows the following proposition.

Proposition C.1
.

Any nominal MDP with transition kernel
P
can be reformulated as an ambiguity averse MDP with
\nu=\delta_{P}
and
\rho(\tilde{X})=\mathbb{E}^{\nu}(\tilde{X})
.

Robust MDPs and Optimistic MDPs

There are two main frameworks for robust MDPs
[
29
,
42
,
59
]
. An uncertainty set
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\Delta(\mathcal{S})^{\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}
is
s-rectangular
if it satisfies the following Cartesian product property:
\mathcal{P}=\times_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\mathcal{P}_{s}
for some
\mathcal{P}_{s}\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})^{\mathcal{A}}
for each state
s\in\mathcal{S}
. Note that if
\nu
is a distribution over
\Delta(\mathcal{S})^{\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}
with product structure, then its support
{\sf supp}(\nu)\subseteq\Delta(\mathcal{S})^{\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}
is a compact, s-rectangular uncertainty set.

We first focus on the model introduced in the seminal paper on s-rectangular robust MDPs
[
59
]
, which analyzes

\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\sf H}}\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mu^{\top}V^{\pi,P}

(11)

for some convex compact s-rectangular uncertainty set
\mathcal{P}
.
Note that here the transition probabilities
P
are fixed over time, so that we are in the static kernel case as described in Definition
3.2
. This model can be recovered by choosing a distribution
\nu
supported on
\mathcal{P}
for the distribution of the random variable
\tilde{P}
, and
\mathcal{P}
such that
{\sf supp}(\nu)=\mathcal{P}
, and
\rho(\tilde{X})=\operatorname{ess\,inf}(\tilde{X})
. In this case, we indeed have, following Lemma
A.2
, that

\operatorname{ess\,inf}\mu^{\top}V^{\pi,\tilde{P}}=\inf_{P\in{\sf supp}(\nu)}\mu^{\top}V^{\pi,P}=\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mu^{\top}V^{\pi,P}

so that we have shown the following proposition.

Proposition C.2
.

Consider a robust MDP as in (
11
) with convex compact s-rectangular uncertainty set
\mathcal{P}
. We can reformulate any robust MDPs as in (
11
) with an ambiguity-averse MDP with product structure and static kernels with distribution
\nu
supported on
\mathcal{P}
and
\rho(\tilde{X})=\operatorname{ess\,inf}(\tilde{X})
.

A second popular model of RMDPs allows the transition kernel to change over time and focuses on solving

\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\sf H}}\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathbb{N}}}\mu^{\top}V^{\pi,P}

(12)

where
\mathcal{P}^{\mathbb{N}}
is the set of sequences of kernels:
\mathcal{P}^{\mathbb{N}}=\{\left(P_{t}\right)_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\;|\;P_{t}\in\mathcal{P},\forall\;t\in\mathbb{N}\}
for some uncertainty set
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\Delta(\mathcal{S})^{\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}
. Note that Proposition 2.2 in
19
shows that (
11
) and (
12
) coincide (Proposition 2.2 in
19
in fact shows a stronger statement for history-dependent transition probabilities, but these include as a special case Markovian transition probabilities, so we indeed have that the two optimization problems (
11
) and (
12
) coincide).
We can reformulate (
12
) as an ambiguity-averse MDP with the same setup as for (
11
) but where we allow for resampled kernels. Indeed, writing
\nu^{\otimes\mathbb{N}}
for the law of the i.i.d. sequence of kernels
(\tilde{P}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
where
\tilde{P}_{t}\sim\nu
for each period
t\in\mathbb{N}
, we get that
{\sf supp}(\nu^{\otimes\mathbb{N}})={\sf supp}(\nu)^{\mathbb{N}}
, so that we obtain that for resampled kernels

\operatorname{ess\,inf}\left(\mu^{\top}V^{\pi,\tilde{P}}\right)=\inf_{(P_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\in{\sf supp}(\nu^{\otimes\mathbb{N}})}\mu^{\top}V^{\pi,P}=\inf_{(P_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\in{\sf supp}(\nu)^{\mathbb{N}}}\mu^{\top}V^{\pi,P}=\inf_{(P_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathbb{N}}}\mu^{\top}V^{\pi,P}

for
\mathcal{P}={\sf supp}(\nu)
.
We have shown the following proposition.

Proposition C.3
.

Consider a robust MDP as in (
12
) with convex compact s-rectangular uncertainty set
\mathcal{P}
. We can reformulate any robust MDPs as in (
12
) with an ambiguity-averse MDP with product structure and resampled kernels with distribution
\nu
supported on
\mathcal{P}
for i.i.d. kernels and
\rho(\tilde{X})=\operatorname{ess\,inf}(\tilde{X})
.

We also note that robust MDPs with non-rectangular uncertainty sets have been studied in the literature, e.g. the r-rectangular model
[
18
]
or model based on
\ell_{1}
-norm between the kernels
[
30
]
, but dynamic programming does not hold for these models
in all generality
, i.e. for all possible choices of instantaneous rewards. In particular, it is shown in
21
that s-rectangularity (implied by the product structure for the distribution
\nu
over kernels) is a necessary condition for dynamic programming to hold, and r-rectangular models can only satisfy weaker forms of dynamic programming (i.e. they require strong assumptions, e.g. that the rewards do not depend on the next state, see
21
).

Finally, we note that the case of optimistic MDPs, which focus on
\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\sf H}}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mu^{\top}V^{\pi,P}
can be reformulated analogously as for RMDPs by choosing a distribution
\nu
over the transition probabilities in
\Delta(\mathcal{S})^{\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}
with
{\sf supp}(\nu)=\mathcal{P}
and
\rho=\operatorname{ess\,sup}
. As noted in
29
,
17
and proved in
15
, the results for robust MDPs extend to optimistic MDPs and in particular
1
and
2
hold for
\rho=\operatorname{ess\,sup}
in both the static and resampled kernel cases.

Multi-model MDPs

Multi-model MDPs (MMDPs) consider an optimization problem of the form

\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\sf H}}\mathbb{E}^{\nu}(\mu^{\top}V^{\pi,\tilde{P}}).

(13)

This model has received many different names in the literature. For instance,
32
,
55
focus on this model in the case of finite horizon MDPs, and
32
also treats the case of discounted return (infinite-horizon MDPs). The multi-model MDP framework has been reintroduced and studied independently over the years, and is also called
soft-robust MDPs

[
12
,
36
]
,
concurrent MDPs

[
6
,
7
]
, and
multiple-environment MDPs

[
46
]
.
The optimization problem (
13
) is known to be NP-hard for static kernels, even in the case of supports of cardinality two, e.g.
32

proved that the following problem is NP-hard

\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\sf H}}\frac{1}{2}\mu^{\top}V^{\pi,P_{1}}+\frac{1}{2}\mu^{\top}V^{\pi,P_{2}}.

The same result for discounted return (infinite-horizon MDPs) is present in
[
6
]
.
The following reformulation of MMDPs as ambiguity-averse MDPs is straightforward.

Proposition C.4
.

Consider a multi-model MDP as in (
13
), with static or resampled kernels. Then we can model it as an ambiguity-averse MDP
{\sf M}_{\nu}
with the distribution
\nu
using
\rho(\tilde{X})=\mathbb{E}^{\nu}\left(\tilde{X}\right)
.

We note that multi-model MDPs also arise as a special case of distributionally robust MDPs (see Appendix
B.1
and Equation
15
below) where there is only a single possible distribution
\nu
(and not a full set of possible probability distributions
\mathcal{V}
). Given the seminal results for distributionally robust MDPs as in Section 4 in
60
, we know that multi-model MDPs with
resampled
kernels satisfy
1
and
2
. It is known that multi-model MDPs with
static
kernels do not satisfy
1
and
2
, and for the sake of completeness, we reprove this in Example
3.8
.

Percentile optimization

Percentile optimization
[
38
,
11
,
43
,
61
]
optimizes for the Value-at-Risk (
VaR
) at a certain risk level
\alpha\in(0,1)
, i.e. it optimizes

\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\sf H}}{\sf VaR}_{\alpha}\left(\mu^{\top}V^{\pi,\tilde{P}})\right).

(14)

where
\tilde{P}
is random and follows a distribution
\nu
(typically assumed uniform).
Note that it is NP-hard to compute an optimal policy for general distributions (corollary 1 in
11
).
The following reformulation is straightforward.

Proposition C.5
.

Consider a percentile optimization problem as in (
14
). Then it can be formulated as an ambiguity-averse MDP with
\rho(\tilde{X})={\sf VaR}_{\alpha}(\tilde{X})
and a distribution
\nu
for static kernels.

Other recent work

The authors in
36
consider optimizing for a convex combination of expectation and the Conditional Value-at-Risk (
CVaR
), i.e. optimizing
\pi\mapsto(1-\lambda)\mathbb{E}(\mu^{\top}V^{\pi,\tilde{P}})+\lambda{\sf CVaR}(\mu^{\top}V^{\pi,\tilde{P}})
for some
\lambda\in[0,1]
. This optimization problem can be cast as an ambiguity-averse MDP using the risk measure
\rho(\tilde{X})=(1-\lambda)\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X})+\lambda{\sf CVaR}(\tilde{X})
.

C.2
Other related work

Robust MDPs with uncertain rewards

An alternative robust MDP model considers that the rewards
r\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S}}
are uncertain while the transitions are known and fixed, or that both rewards and transitions are uncertain, e.g.
15
,
13
,
5
,
1
; it is also possible to analyze distributionally robust MDPs with both uncertain rewards and transitions, as in the seminal papers
[
60
]
. RMDPs with uncertain rewards represent a simpler optimization problem compared to RMDPs with uncertain transitions, because value functions are linear in the rewards (this follows from the linearity of the expectation operator) but depend in a non-linear fashion on the transition probabilities. In our paper, we decide to focus solely on the case of uncertain kernels for the sake of conciseness, noting that our framework could be extended to also model uncertain rewards, at the cost of heavier notations.

Distributionally robust MDPs

DRMDPs are introduced in
60
as the following optimization problem:

\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\sf H}}\inf_{\nu\in\mathcal{V}}\mathbb{E}^{\nu}(\mu^{\top}V^{\pi,\tilde{P}})

(15)

where
\mathcal{V}
is a set of probability distribution over the sequences of transition probabilities
(P_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
. For ambiguity-averse MDPs to cover this setting, we would need to define them with sets of distributions
\mathcal{V}
instead of a single distribution
\nu
. Note that DRMDPs also consider the case of uncertain transition kernels that can change over time in a non-i.i.d. fashion, so that we would need a different set
\mathcal{V}_{t}
of possible distributions
\nu_{t}
for the transition probabilities at each period
t
. This complicates the formulation of the optimization problem (
6
), which requires to define risk measures with arguments to have multiple distributions (one per choice of
\nu\in\mathcal{V}
). In principle, this can be done with the notion of an ambiguous probability space (see
10
), but it appears beyond the scope of this paper.

Necessary and sufficient conditions for dynamic programming

There are numerous papers obtaining necessary and sufficient conditions for the existence of dynamic programming in the context of MDPs. The authors in
21
focus on robust MDPs with an uncertainty set
\mathcal{P}
, and show that a dynamic programming equation holds for policy evaluation if and only if
\mathcal{P}
is an s-rectangular uncertainty set. The authors in
39
focus on risk-averse MDPs with a finite horizon (in the framework of distributional reinforcement learning) and show that for a Bellman optimality equation to hold it is necessary that the risk measure is
{\sf ERM}_{\beta}
for some
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
; the sufficiency is proved in
27
. The authors in
49
focus on distributional reinforcement learning and define
Bellman closedness
for a set of statistics, as the property that these statistics can be jointly optimized by dynamic programming (e.g., this is the case for the set consisting of the expectation and the variance
[
54
]
). The authors in
49
show that the only sets of Bellman closed statistics are sets of moments (see Theorem 4.3 in
49
).

Risk-averse MDPs

In risk-averse MDPs, the transition probabilities
P
and the rewards
r
are known, and the risk measures are with respect to the distribution over the trajectories induced by pairs of policies and transition kernels, i.e., risk-averse MDPs aim at solving

\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\sf H}}\rho\left(\sum_{t=0}^{+\infty}\gamma^{t}r(\tilde{S}_{t},\tilde{A}_{t},\tilde{S}_{t+1})\right)\;.

(16)

It is also common to focus on the case of finite-horizon MDPs, i.e., to optimize, for a given horizon
T\in\mathbb{N}
, the following objective:

\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\sf H}}\rho\left(\sum_{t=0}^{H}r(\tilde{S}_{t},\tilde{A}_{t},\tilde{S}_{t+1})\right)\;.

(17)

The existence of dynamic programming equations in risk-averse MDPs has been studied extensively, often using the terms “dynamic consistency” or “time consistency” for risk measures. Informally, a risk measure is time consistent when the risk preferences do not change throughout the execution
[
48
,
28
]
. Time consistency is typically a weaker requirement than our
1
and
2
because it is sufficient that it holds only for
\gamma=1
.

Several works from the risk-averse MDP literature study necessary and sufficient conditions for the time-consistency of risk measures.
31
shows that only law-invariant, time-consistent, and relevant risk measures are the entropic risk measure, which subsumes the expectation operator. Relevance is a technical condition that excludes risk measures that are insensitive to the
\operatorname{ess\,inf}
of the random variable. Similar results have been derived in later work, including the restriction for law-invariant coherent risk measures
[
53
]
, and for law-invariant and
W^{1}
-continuous (see Appendix
H.3
) risk measures
[
39
]
.

Despite some superficial similarity to our results, the literature on the time consistency of risk-averse MDPs does not apply to ambiguity-averse MDPs. There is a fundamental difference in the setup of the two problems. In risk-averse MDPs, the risk aversion with respect to randomness is in the returns of the uncertain histories (or trajectories). As a result, we derive that the properties of ambiguity-averse MDPs stem from the interaction between the risk measure, the expectation operator, and the discount factor.

Interestingly, one can use dynamic programming to compute policies for risk-averse MDPs using one of the following two approaches:

•

Dropping the law-invariance condition: nested risk measures.

If one drops the law-invariance requirement for the risk measure, it is possible to recover a dynamic programming equation over the set of states using nested risk measures
[
51
]
(also called Markov or iterated risk measures). Intuitively, nested risk measure replacing the objective
\rho\left(\sum_{t=0}^{+\infty}\gamma^{t}r(\tilde{S}_{t},\tilde{A}_{t},\tilde{S}_{t+1})\right)
by applying the risk measure
\rho
repeatedly, i.e., informally it corresponds to optimizing

~\rho\left(r(\tilde{S}_{0},\tilde{A}_{0},\tilde{S}_{1})+\rho\left(\gamma r(\tilde{S}_{1},\tilde{A}_{1},\tilde{S}_{1})+\rho\left(\gamma r(\tilde{S}_{2},\tilde{A}_{2},\tilde{S}_{2})+....\right)\right)\right).

(18)

From the above equation, one can construct a dynamic programming equation and derive value iteration algorithms
[
51
]
to compute optimal Markov policies. However, nested risk measures are not law-invariant, and their values can depend on the order in which rewards are accrued, complicating their interpretation. That is, the quantity in (
18
) differs from the original objective
\rho\left(\sum_{t=0}^{+\infty}\gamma^{t}r(\tilde{S}_{t},\tilde{A}_{t},\tilde{S}_{t+1})\right)

[
28
]
.

•

Extended state space.

For most common risk measures, including
VaR
,
CVaR
,
EVaR
,
ERM
, one can formulate dynamic programming equations after augmenting the state space with an additional variable. The particular augmentation depends on the risk measure. For example, to optimize discounted risk-averse objectives with
ERM
and
EVaR
, one can augment the state space with the current time step, yielding Markov-optimal policies
[
25
]
. When the objective is
VaR
, one can construct a dynamic program that augments the state space with the risk level
\alpha\in[0,1]

[
33
,
24
,
23
]
, resulting in a history-dependent policy. When the objective is a
CVaR
, one can construct a dynamic program by augmenting the state space with a real variable that keeps track of the accumulated rewards
[
2
]
and compute an optimal history-dependent policy. When
evaluating
a fixed policy with the
CVaR
objective, one can construct a dynamic programming that augments the state with the risk level
\alpha\in[0,1]
, similarly to
VaR
. However, the risk-level augmentation fails for
CVaR
when
optimizing
a policy
[
24
,
16
]
.

Bayes-adaptive MDPs

Epistemic uncertainty over MDPs parameters has been studied extensively in the context of Bayes-adaptive MDPs
[
22
]
. Most research on Bayes-adaptive MDPs has focused on reinforcement learning algorithms that compute history-dependent policies for the risk-neutral objective. For the discussion of the differences with the risk-averse setting, we refer the interested reader to
35
.

Appendix D
Proof of Proposition
3.7

Proof of Proposition
3.7
.

1.

Let
v,w\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{S}}
with
v\leqslant w
. Since
T^{\pi,P}
is a monotone operator for any realization of the transition probabilities
P
, that
T^{\pi,\tilde{P}}v\leqslant T^{\pi,\tilde{P}}w
almost surely. Since
\rho
is a monotone risk measure, we obtain that

\rho\left(T^{\pi,\tilde{P}}v\right)\leqslant\rho\left(T^{\pi,\tilde{P}}w\right),

(19)

which proves that
T^{\pi,\nu,\rho}
is a monotone operator for each
\pi\in\Pi_{\sf S}
. The fact that
T^{\nu,\rho}
is a monotone operator follows directly from taking the supremum over
\pi\in\Pi_{\sf S}
on both sides of (
19
).

2.

Let
v\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{S}},c\in\mathbb{R}
. We have

T^{\pi,\nu,\rho}(v+c\cdot 1_{\mathcal{S}})=\rho(T^{\pi,\tilde{P}}(v+c\cdot 1_{\mathcal{S}}))=\rho(T^{\pi,\tilde{P}}v+c\cdot 1_{\mathcal{S}})=\rho(T^{\pi,\tilde{P}}v)+c\cdot 1_{\mathcal{S}}=T^{\pi,\nu,\rho}v+c\cdot 1_{\mathcal{S}},

where the first equality is by definition of the operator
T^{\pi,\nu,\rho}
, the second equality is because
T^{\pi,\tilde{P}}
is translation-invariant for any realization of
\tilde{P}
, the third equality is from the translation-invariance of
\rho
, and the last equality is again by definition of ours operators. This proves that
T^{\pi,\nu,\rho}
is translation-invariant. The proof for
T^{\nu,\rho}
follows the same line, using additionally that
\max_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\{w(s)+c\}=\max_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\{w(s)\}+c
for any
w\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{S}}
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
.

3.

A direct calculation shows that the composition of a non-expansive risk measure and a contraction remains a contraction. Lemma 4.3 in
14
shows that a monotone, cash-invariant risk measure is non-expansive (this is also a consequence of Crandall-Tartar theorem
[
9
]
), in the sense that

|\rho(\tilde{X})-\rho(\tilde{Y})|\leqslant\operatorname{ess\,sup}|\tilde{X}-\tilde{Y}|,\quad\forall\;\tilde{X},\tilde{Y}\;.

(20)

Note that in
[
14
]
this result is written an
\ell_{\infty}
-norm for random variables, but for the sake of clarity we choose to use the notation
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
only for functions in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{S}}
in this paper.
For the sake of completeness, we provide a concise proof of (
20
) here. Let
\delta=\operatorname{ess\,sup}|\tilde{X}-\tilde{Y}|.
By definition,
|\tilde{X}-\tilde{Y}|\leqslant\delta
a.s. so that
\tilde{Y}-\delta\leqslant\tilde{X}\leqslant\tilde{Y}+\delta
a.s.. Using the monotonicity of
\rho
we get that
\rho(\tilde{Y}-\delta)\leqslant\rho(\tilde{X})\leqslant\rho(\tilde{Y}+\delta)
. Using translation invariance we get that
\rho(\tilde{Y})-\delta\leqslant\rho(\tilde{X})\leqslant\rho(\tilde{Y})+\delta
so that
|\rho(\tilde{X})-\rho(\tilde{Y})|\leqslant\delta
, which concludes the proof of (
20
).

Now we have

\displaystyle\|T^{\pi,\nu,\rho}v-T^{\pi,\nu,\rho}w\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\max_{s\in\mathcal{S}}|\rho(T^{\pi,\tilde{P}}v(s))-\rho(T^{\pi,\tilde{P}}w(s))|

\displaystyle\leqslant\max_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\operatorname{ess\,sup}|T^{\pi,\tilde{P}}v(s)-T^{\pi,\tilde{P}}w(s)|

\displaystyle\leqslant\max_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\gamma\|v-w\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leqslant\gamma\|v-w\|_{\infty}

where the first line is from the definition of
T^{\pi,\nu,\rho}
and of the
\ell_{\infty}
-norm for vectors, the second line is from the non-expansiveness of
\rho
as in (
20
), and the third line is from the fact for each kernel
P
the operator
T^{\pi,P}
(for nominal MDPs) is a contraction. Therefore, we have shown

\forall\;v,w\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{S}},\|T^{\pi,\nu,\rho}v-T^{\pi,\nu,\rho}w\|_{\infty}\leqslant\gamma\|v-w\|_{\infty}.

(21)

The contraction property of
T^{\nu,\rho}
follows directly from taking the supremum over
\pi\in\Pi_{\sf S}
in the right-hand side of (
21
).

We next prove the attainability property.

We show that for any
v\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{S}}
, we can find
\pi\in\Pi_{\sf S}
such that
T^{\nu,\rho}v=T^{\pi,\nu,\rho}v
, i.e. such that
\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\sf S}}T^{\pi,\nu,\rho}v(s)=\max_{\pi\in\Pi_{\sf S}}T^{\pi,\nu,\rho}v(s)
for each
s\in\mathcal{S}
. To prove this, it suffices to show that
\pi\rightarrow T^{\pi,\nu,\rho}v(s)
is continuous for
\pi\in\Pi_{\sf S}
(note that
\Pi_{\sf S}=\Delta(\mathcal{A})^{\mathcal{S}}
is a compact set and that
T^{\pi,\nu,\rho}v(s)
only depends on the s-th component of
\pi
). To prove that
\pi\rightarrow T^{\pi,\nu,\rho}v(s)
is a continuous function, we use the definition of the Bellman operator:
T^{\pi,\nu,\rho}v(s)=\rho(T^{\pi,\tilde{P}}v(s))
.
Let
\pi\in\Pi_{\sf S}
and consider a sequence
(\pi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of stationary policies such that
\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\pi_{n}=\pi
. Then

\displaystyle|T^{\pi_{n},\nu,\rho}v(s)-T^{\pi,\nu,\rho}v(s)|

\displaystyle=|\rho(T^{\pi_{n},\tilde{P}}v(s))-\rho(T^{\pi,\tilde{P}}v(s))|

\displaystyle\leqslant\operatorname{ess\,sup}|T^{\pi_{n},\tilde{P}}v(s)-T^{\pi,\tilde{P}}v(s)|

where the first equality uses the definition of the Bellman operator and the inequality uses the non-expansivity of
\rho
(Equation (
20
), similarly as in the proof for the contraction property of the operators in the third point above). Now from the definition of the operator
T^{\pi,\tilde{P}}
we get that for any
P\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})^{\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}
we have

|T^{\pi_{n},\tilde{P}}v(s)-T^{\pi,\tilde{P}}v(s)|\leqslant C\cdot\|\pi_{n}-\pi\|_{1}

where
C:=\max_{s,a,s^{\prime}}|r(s,a,s^{\prime}|+\gamma\max_{s\in\mathcal{S}}|v(s)|
.
Overall we obtained that

|T^{\pi_{n},\nu,\rho}v(s)-T^{\pi,\nu,\rho}v(s)|\leqslant C\cdot\|\pi_{n}-\pi\|_{1}

and it is therefore straightforward to conclude that
\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}|T^{\pi_{n},\nu,\rho}v(s)-T^{\pi,\nu,\rho}v(s)|=0
when
\pi_{n}\rightarrow\pi
, i.e. we have shown that
\pi\mapsto T^{\pi,\nu,\rho}v(s)
is a continuous function on the compact set
\Pi_{\sf S}
. By Weierstrass theorem, we conclude that
\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\sf S}}T^{\pi,\nu,\rho}v(s)=\max_{\pi\in\Pi_{\sf S}}T^{\pi,\nu,\rho}v(s)
.

Example E.1

We consider, for

\tilde{X}\sim\text{Unif([0,1])}

Ambiguity-averse MDP with
\mathcal{S}=\{{\sf Start},{\sf End}\}

\mathcal{S}=\{{\sf Start},{\sf End}\}

\mathcal{A}=\{1\}

\gamma\in[0,1)
and reward function

r({\sf End},1,s^{\prime})=0

Here,

\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{X}&1-\tilde{X}\\

We consider here the case of static kernels: there is a single sample of

\tilde{P}_{t}

\displaystyle\tilde{P}_{t}=\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{X}&1-\tilde{X}\\

Because the transitions are only random at
{\sf Start}

\nu

We consider the unique stationary policy
\pi

\pi

V^{\pi,\tilde{P}}=\begin{pmatrix}\sum_{t\geqslant 0}\gamma^{t}\tilde{X}^{t}\\
0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{1-\gamma\tilde{X}}\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

Therefore, the ambiguity-averse value function under policy
\pi

V^{\pi,\nu,\rho}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{1-\gamma\tilde{X}}\right)\\

v({\sf End})\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{E}\left(1+\tilde{X}\gamma v({\sf Start})+(1-\tilde{X})\gamma v({\sf End})\right)\\

\gamma v({\sf End})\end{pmatrix}\;,

and in particular, we have

Finally, we have

Because there is a single policy,

doesn’t satisfy

.

1.

We start by proving the results for value iteration.

Let

for

and

for any

is a contraction, we have that

converges to the fixed point of the operator

as

, i.e. we get that
\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\left(T^{\nu,\rho}\right)^{n}V^{0}=V^{\nu,\rho}

Moreover,

where the equality follows by definition of the sequence
(V^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and because
V^{\nu,\rho}
is a fixed point of
T^{\nu,\rho}
and the inequality is because
T^{\nu,\rho}
is a contraction under the assumption of Proposition
4.2
. We conclude by induction that
\|V^{\nu,\rho}-V^{n}\|_{\infty}\leqslant\gamma^{n}\|V^{\nu,\rho}-V^{0}\|_{\infty}
for any

.

There remains to bound

. Note that

\displaystyle\|V^{\nu,\rho}-V^{\pi^{n},\nu,\rho}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\|V^{\nu,\rho}-T^{\pi_{n},\nu,\rho}V^{n-1}+T^{\pi_{n},\nu,\rho}V^{n-1}-V^{\pi^{n},\nu,\rho}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\|V^{\nu,\rho}-T^{\nu,\rho}V^{n-1}+T^{\pi_{n},\nu,\rho}V^{n-1}-V^{\pi^{n},\nu,\rho}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leqslant\|V^{\nu,\rho}-T^{\nu,\rho}V^{n-1}\|_{\infty}+\|T^{\pi_{n},\nu,\rho}V^{n-1}-V^{\pi^{n},\nu,\rho}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\|T^{\nu,\rho}V^{\nu,\rho}-T^{\nu,\rho}V^{n-1}\|_{\infty}+\|T^{\pi_{n},\nu,\rho}V^{n-1}-T^{\pi_{n},\nu,\rho}V^{\pi^{n},\nu,\rho}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leqslant\gamma\|V^{\nu,\rho}-V^{n-1}\|_{\infty}+\gamma\|V^{n-1}-V^{\pi^{n},\nu,\rho}\|_{\infty}

where the first equality is by adding and substracting the vector
T^{\pi_{n},\nu,\rho}V^{n-1}
, the second equality is by definition of
\pi^{n}
for value iteration, the first inequality is by triangle inequality, the third equality is by
V^{\nu,\rho}
and
V^{\pi^{n},\nu,\rho}
being the fixed points of
T^{\nu,\rho}
and
T^{\pi^{n},\nu,\rho}
following
1
and
2
, and the last inequality is from these operators being contractions.
Now we also have

\displaystyle\|V^{n-1}-V^{\pi^{n},\nu,\rho}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\|V^{n-1}-V^{\nu,\rho}+V^{\nu,\rho}-V^{\pi^{n},\nu,\rho}\|_{\infty}\leqslant\|V^{n-1}-V^{\nu,\rho}\|_{\infty}+\|V^{\nu,\rho}-V^{\pi^{n},\nu,\rho}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\|V^{\nu,\rho}-V^{\pi^{n},\nu,\rho}\|_{\infty}\leqslant\gamma\|V^{\nu,\rho}-V^{n-1}\|_{\infty}+\gamma(\|V^{n-1}-V^{\nu,\rho}\|_{\infty}+\|V^{\nu,\rho}-V^{\pi^{n},\nu,\rho}\|_{\infty})

Evaluating the Bellman operators

The efficient computations of the Bellman operators have been studied in some cases.
For robust MDPs, we have, for

and

,

\displaystyle T^{\pi,\nu,\rho}v(s)

\displaystyle=\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}}T^{\pi,P}v(s)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\sf S}}\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}}T^{\pi,P}v(s)

[

]

[

]
.
The case of optimistic MDPs can be treated similarly by noting that

We will use the next technical lemma.

Lemma H.2
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
a non-constant function such that

\displaystyle\forall(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\times[0,1],\forall\gamma\in[0,1),\quad f\Big(\gamma\big(zx+(1-z)y\big)\Big)=f\Big(\gamma\big(zf(x)+(1-z)f(y)\big)\Big)

(22)

\displaystyle\forall C\in\mathbb{R},\forall\gamma\in[0,1)\quad f(C)=f(C+\gamma f(0))

(23)

Then
f
is the identity:
f(x)=x,\forall\;x\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Let
f
be a non-constant function that satisfies Equations (
22
)-(
23
).
We first have that
f(0)=0
. Indeed, if
f(0)\neq 0
, then for any
C\in\mathbb{R}
, Equation (
23
) directly implies that
f
is constant on any closed interval of length
\lvert f(0)/2\rvert>0
and then, splitting the real line with points
(n\frac{f(0)}{2})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
, we get that
f
is constant on
\mathbb{R}
, which contradicts the assumption.

We show the following auxiliary result:
\forall a<b,f(a)=f(b)\implies f\text{ is constant on }(a,b)
.
Let
a<b
such that
f(a)=f(b)
, and let
x,y\in(a,b)
. There exists
\gamma\in[0,1)
such that
x,y\in[\gamma a,\gamma b]
.
By (
22
),

\displaystyle f(x)=f\Big(\gamma\big(zb+(1-z)a\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=f\Big(\gamma\big(zf(b)+(1-z)f(a)\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=f\Big(\gamma f(a)\Big)

\displaystyle=f\Big(\gamma\big(z^{\prime}f(b)+(1-z^{\prime})f(a)\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=f\Big(\gamma\big(z^{\prime}b+(1-z^{\prime})a\big)\Big)=f(y)\;,

where
z=\frac{x/\gamma-a}{b-a}\in[0,1]
and
z^{\prime}=\frac{y/\gamma-a}{b-a}\in[0,1]
.
So
f
is constant on
(a,b)
.

We now prove by contradiction that
f
is injective.
Let
a<b
such that
f(a)=f(b)
. We show that
f
is constant on
\mathbb{R}
.
Let
x<y\in\mathbb{R}
such that
y-x=(b-a)/4
. We define
u=4x-a=4y-b\in\mathbb{R}
.
Then, by (
22
) with
\gamma=z=1/2
,

\displaystyle f(x)=f\left(\frac{1}{4}a+\frac{1}{4}u\right)

\displaystyle=f\left(\frac{1}{4}f(a)+\frac{1}{4}f(u)\right)

\displaystyle=f\left(\frac{1}{4}f(b)+\frac{1}{4}f(u)\right)

\displaystyle=f\left(\frac{1}{4}b+\frac{1}{4}u\right)=f(y)\;.

Therefore, using our auxiliary result,
f
is constant on any open interval of length
\left|b-a\right|/4>0
, so
f
is constant on
\mathbb{R}
, which contradicts the assumption.

Finally, by (
22
) with
\gamma=z=1/2
and
y=0
, we have

\forall x\in\mathbb{R},f(x/4)=f\left(\frac{1}{4}x+\frac{1}{4}0\right)=f\left(f(x)/4+f(0)/4\right)=f(f(x)/4)\;,

so by injectivity of
f
, we have
\forall x\in\mathbb{R},x/4=f(x)/4
and
f
is the identity.

only applies to risk measures on
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
). This is done in the next lemma, which states that an additive independent law-invariant risk measure defined on
L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
can be extended to a law-invariant risk measure
\mathring{\rho}
defined on
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, which preserves its main properties (e.g., additive independence and monotonicity).

The key insight is that any random variable with nonconvex support can be represented as the difference of two random variables with convex support by adding an independent uniform noise. This property allows us to define the extension
\mathring{\rho}
of any random variable with not-necessarily convex support as the difference of the images by
\rho
of two random variables with convex support and this way preserve the algebraic properties (monotonicity, additive independence, positive homogeneity) required by the representation theorem. We recall that
(\Omega_{{\sf M}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}})
is the probability space associated with a specific ambiguity-averse MDP
{\sf M}\in\mathcal{M}
, as defined right after Definition
B.2
.

Lemma H.6
.

Let
\rho
be a risk measure that is law-invariant on
L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
. Assume additionally that
\rho
is additive independent on
L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
:

\text{for any }{\sf M}\in\mathcal{M}\text{ and independent }\tilde{X},\tilde{Y}\in L^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega_{{\sf M}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}};\mathbb{R}),\quad\rho(\tilde{X}+\tilde{Y})=\rho(\tilde{X})+\rho(\tilde{Y})\;.

Then, there exists risk measure
\mathring{\rho}
that is law-invariant on
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, that coincides with
\rho
on
L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
(i.e.,
\forall X\in L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}),\,\mathring{\rho}(X)=\rho(X)
) and such that the following statements hold:

1.

\mathring{\rho}
is additive independent on
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
:

\text{for any }{\sf M}\in\mathcal{M}\text{ and independent }\tilde{X},\tilde{Y}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega_{{\sf M}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}};\mathbb{R}),\quad\mathring{\rho}(\tilde{X}+\tilde{Y})=\mathring{\rho}(\tilde{X})+\mathring{\rho}(\tilde{Y})\;.

2.

if
\rho
is positive homogeneous on
L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
, then
\mathring{\rho}
is positive homogeneous on
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
:

\Big[\forall\tilde{X}\in L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}),\forall\alpha\geqslant 0,\quad\rho(\alpha\tilde{X})=\alpha\rho(\tilde{X})\Big]\implies\Big[\forall\tilde{X}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}),\forall\alpha\geqslant 0,\quad\mathring{\rho}(\alpha\tilde{X})=\alpha\mathring{\rho}(\tilde{X})\Big]\;.

3.

if
\rho
is monotone on
L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
, then
\mathring{\rho}
is monotone on
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
:

\displaystyle\Big[\text{for any }{\sf M}\in\mathcal{M}\text{ and }\tilde{X},\tilde{Y}\in L^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega_{{\sf M}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}};\mathbb{R})\text{ s.t. }\tilde{X}\geqslant\tilde{Y}\text{ a.s.},\quad\rho(\tilde{X})\geqslant\rho(\tilde{Y})\Big]

\displaystyle\implies\Big[\text{for any }{\sf M}\in\mathcal{M}\text{ and }\tilde{X},\tilde{Y}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega_{{\sf M}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}};\mathbb{R})\text{ s.t. }\tilde{X}\geqslant\tilde{Y}\text{ a.s.},\quad\mathring{\rho}(\tilde{X})\geqslant\mathring{\rho}(\tilde{Y})\Big]\;.

Proof of Lemma
H.6
.

Let
\rho
be a risk measure that is law-invariant and additive independent on
L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
. Let
{\sf M}_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
an ambiguity-averse MDP such that
(\Omega_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}_{0}})
is non-atomic (for example the one of Example
E.1
).

For any
\tilde{X}\in L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
, we define
l_{X}=\operatorname{ess\,sup}(\tilde{X})-\operatorname{ess\,inf}(\tilde{X})
. By non-atomicity
(\Omega_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}_{0}})
, there exists
\tilde{X}^{\prime},\tilde{U}_{\tilde{X}}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}_{0}};\mathbb{R})
such that
\mathcal{L}(\tilde{X}^{\prime})=\mathcal{L}(\tilde{X})
,
\tilde{U}_{\tilde{X}}\sim\text{Unif}([0,l_{X}])
and
\tilde{X}^{\prime},\tilde{U}_{\tilde{X}}
are independent. We call
\tilde{U}_{\tilde{X}}
a noise for
\tilde{X}^{\prime}
.

We now observe that the support of
\tilde{X}^{\prime}+\tilde{U}_{\tilde{X}}
is the Minkowski sum of
{\sf supp}(\tilde{X}^{\prime})\subseteq[\operatorname{ess\,inf}(\tilde{X}),\operatorname{ess\,sup}(\tilde{X})]
and of
[0,l_{X}]
, so it is a closed interval of
\mathbb{R}
.
Therefore, we can now define

\mathring{\rho}(\tilde{X}):=\rho(\tilde{X}^{\prime}+\tilde{U}_{\tilde{X}})-\rho(\tilde{U}_{\tilde{X}})\;.

(26)

Note that by definition,
\mathring{\rho}
is well defined by law-invariance of
\rho
and it is itself a law-invariant risk measure on
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
(as a difference of two law-invariant risk measures) that coincides with
\rho
on
L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
by additive independence of
\rho
on
L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
.

We also have, for any noise
\tilde{V}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}_{0}};\mathbb{R})
independent of
\tilde{X}^{\prime}
and
\tilde{U}_{\tilde{X}}
with convex support that includes
[0,l_{X}]
,

\mathring{\rho}(\tilde{X})=\rho(\tilde{X}^{\prime}+\tilde{V})-\rho(\tilde{V})\;.

(27)

Indeed,
\tilde{U}_{\tilde{X}}
,
\tilde{V}
,
\tilde{X}^{\prime}+\tilde{U}_{\tilde{X}}
,
\tilde{X}^{\prime}+\tilde{V}
and
\tilde{X}^{\prime}+\tilde{U}_{\tilde{X}}+\tilde{V}
all have convex support, so,

\displaystyle\rho(\tilde{X}^{\prime}+\tilde{V})-\rho(\tilde{V})

\displaystyle=\rho((\tilde{X}^{\prime}+V)+\tilde{U}_{\tilde{X}})-\rho(\tilde{U}_{\tilde{X}})-\rho(\tilde{V})

\displaystyle=\rho((\tilde{X}^{\prime}+\tilde{U}_{\tilde{X}})+\tilde{V})-\rho(\tilde{V})-\rho(\tilde{U}_{\tilde{X}})

\displaystyle=\rho(\tilde{X}^{\prime}+\tilde{U}_{\tilde{X}})-\rho(\tilde{U}_{\tilde{X}})

\displaystyle=\mathring{\rho}(\tilde{X})\;.

1.

Let
{\sf M}\in\mathcal{M}
and two independent random variables
\tilde{X},\tilde{Y}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega_{{\sf M}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}};\mathbb{R})
. There exists independent random variables
\tilde{X}^{\prime},\tilde{Y}^{\prime},\tilde{U}_{\tilde{X}},\tilde{U}_{\tilde{Y}}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}_{0}};\mathbb{R})
such that
\mathcal{L}(\tilde{X}^{\prime})=\mathcal{L}(\tilde{X})
,
\mathcal{L}(\tilde{Y}^{\prime})=\mathcal{L}(\tilde{Y})
and
\tilde{U}_{\tilde{X}},\tilde{U}_{\tilde{Y}}
are noises for
\tilde{X}^{\prime}
and
\tilde{Y}^{\prime}
. We now have,

\displaystyle\mathring{\rho}(\tilde{X})+\mathring{\rho}(\tilde{Y})

\displaystyle=\rho(\tilde{X}^{\prime}+\tilde{U}_{\tilde{X}})+\rho(\tilde{Y}^{\prime}+\tilde{U}_{\tilde{Y}})-\rho(\tilde{U}_{\tilde{X}})-\rho(\tilde{U}_{\tilde{Y}})

\displaystyle=\rho(\tilde{X}^{\prime}+\tilde{Y}^{\prime}+\tilde{U}_{\tilde{X}}+\tilde{U}_{\tilde{Y}})-\rho(\tilde{U}_{\tilde{X}}+\tilde{U}_{\tilde{Y}})

\displaystyle=\mathring{\rho}(\tilde{X}+\tilde{Y})\;,

where the last equality comes from using
Equation

27
because
\tilde{U}_{\tilde{X}}+\tilde{U}_{\tilde{Y}}
has support exactly
[0,l_{X}+l_{Y}]
and is independent of
\tilde{X}+\tilde{Y}
.

Therefore,
\mathring{\rho}
is additive independent on
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
.

2.

Assume that
\rho
is positive homogeneous on
L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
.

Then, for any
\tilde{X}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
and
\alpha\geqslant 0
, there exists independent
\tilde{X}^{\prime},\tilde{U}_{\tilde{X}}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}_{0}};\mathbb{R})
such that
\mathcal{L}(\tilde{X}^{\prime})=\mathcal{L}(\tilde{X})
and
\tilde{U}_{\tilde{X}}
is a noise for
\tilde{X}^{\prime}
. We now have

\displaystyle\alpha\mathring{\rho}(\tilde{X})

\displaystyle=\alpha\rho(\tilde{X}^{\prime}+\tilde{U}_{\tilde{X}})-\alpha\rho(\tilde{U}_{\tilde{X}})

\displaystyle=\rho(\alpha\tilde{X}^{\prime}+\alpha\tilde{U}_{\tilde{X}})-\rho(\alpha\tilde{U}_{\tilde{X}})

\displaystyle=\mathring{\rho}(\alpha\tilde{X})\;,

where the last equality comes from using
Equation

27
because
\alpha\tilde{U}_{\tilde{X}}
has support exactly
[0,\alpha l_{X}]
and is independent of
\alpha\tilde{X}^{\prime}
.

Therefore,
\mathring{\rho}
is positive homogeneous on
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
.

3.

Assume that
\rho
is monotone on
L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
.

Let
{\sf M}\in\mathcal{M}
and two random variables
\tilde{X},\tilde{Y}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega_{{\sf M}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}};\mathbb{R})
such that
\tilde{X}\geqslant\tilde{Y}
almost surely. There exists random variables
\tilde{X}^{\prime},\tilde{Y}^{\prime},\tilde{U}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}_{0}};\mathbb{R})
such that
\mathcal{L}\left((\tilde{X}^{\prime},\tilde{Y}^{\prime})\right)=\mathcal{L}\left((\tilde{X},\tilde{Y})\right)
,
\tilde{U}\sim\text{Unif}([0,\max(l_{X},l_{Y})])
and
\tilde{U}
is independent to both
\tilde{X}^{\prime}
and
\tilde{Y}^{\prime}
. In particular, because their joint law is identical, we have
\tilde{X}^{\prime}\geqslant\tilde{Y}^{\prime}
and adding
\tilde{U}
we get
\tilde{X}^{\prime}+\tilde{U}\geqslant\tilde{Y}^{\prime}+\tilde{U}
almost surely.
As,
\tilde{X}^{\prime}+\tilde{U}
and
\tilde{Y}^{\prime}+\tilde{U}
have convex supports in
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathring{\rho}
coincides with
\rho
on
L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
, by monotonicity of
\rho
on
L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
, we have

\mathring{\rho}(\tilde{X}^{\prime}+\tilde{U})=\rho(\tilde{X}^{\prime}+\tilde{U})\geqslant\rho(\tilde{Y}^{\prime}+\tilde{U})=\mathring{\rho}(\tilde{Y}^{\prime}+\tilde{U})\;.

Now, subtracting by
\rho(\tilde{U})
on both side and applying
Equation

27
, we get
\mathring{\rho}(\tilde{X})\geqslant\mathring{\rho}(\tilde{Y})
.

Therefore,
\mathring{\rho}
is monotone on
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
.

∎

By Lemma
H.6
, we know that a version of Theorem
H.4
holds for risk measures on
L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
, that is, if
\rho
is a risk measure defined on
L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
that is monotone, additive independent, coincides with the identity on constant random variable, and law-invariant, then there exists a unique Borel probability measure
\mu
on
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
such that

\forall\tilde{X}\in L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}),\quad\rho(\tilde{X})=\int_{\overline{\mathbb{R}}}{\sf ERM}_{a}(\tilde{X})d\mu(a)\;.

We are now ready to prove
Theorem

4.4
.

Proof of
Theorem

4.4
.

Let
\rho
be a risk measure that is law-invariant, non-constant and monotone on
L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
and that satisfies
1
or
2
in either the case of static kernels or resampled kernels.

By the conclusion of Proposition
4.3
,
\rho
coincides with the identity on constants, is positive homogeneous and additive independent on
L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
. Also, by monotonicity of
\rho
on
L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
, for any
{\sf M}\in\mathcal{M}
and independent
\tilde{X},\tilde{Y}\in L^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega_{{\sf M}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}};\mathbb{R}_{+})
, we have
\rho(\tilde{X}\tilde{Y})=\rho(\tilde{X})\rho(\tilde{Y})
.

Then, using Lemma
H.6
, there exists
\mathring{\rho}
a risk measure that is law-invariant on
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, coincides with
\rho
on
L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
and is positive homogeneous, additive independent and monotone on
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
.
In particular,
\mathring{\rho}
also coincides with the identity on constants.

Let now
{\sf M}_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
an ambiguity-averse MDP such that
(\Omega_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}_{0}})
is non-atomic (for example the one of Example
E.1
). Because
\mathring{\rho}
is monotone, additive independent and law invariant on
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
and it coincides with the identity on constants, the specific risk measure

\displaystyle\mathring{\rho}_{{\sf M}_{0}}\colon L^{\infty}(\Omega_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}_{0}};\mathbb{R})

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\tilde{X}

\displaystyle\mapsto\mathring{\rho}(\tilde{X})

is a risk-measure on a non-atomic probability space that is law-invariant, monotone, additive independent, and that coincides with the identity on constants. Therefore, by Theorem 1 in
41
, there exists a unique Borel probability measure
\mu
on
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
such that

\forall\tilde{X}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}_{0}};\mathbb{R}),\quad\mathring{\rho}_{{\sf M}_{0}}(\tilde{X})=\int_{\overline{\mathbb{R}}}{\sf ERM}_{a}(\tilde{X})d\mu(a)\;,

where
{\sf ERM}_{a}(\tilde{X})=\frac{1}{a}\log\mathbb{E}(\exp(a\tilde{X}))
if
a\in\mathbb{R}^{\star}
,
{\sf ERM}_{0}(\tilde{X})=\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X})
,
{\sf ERM}_{+\infty}(\tilde{X})=\operatorname{ess\,sup}(\tilde{X})
and
{\sf ERM}_{-\infty}(\tilde{X})=\operatorname{ess\,inf}(\tilde{X})
.

Then, we fix
\lambda>0
and define the dilatation map
T_{\lambda}:\overline{\mathbb{R}}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
such that
T_{\lambda}(a)=\lambda a
if
a\in\mathbb{R}
and
T_{\lambda}(+\infty)=+\infty
,
T_{\lambda}(-\infty)=-\infty
.
Noting that for any
a\in\overline{\mathbb{R}}
and
\tilde{X}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}_{0}};\mathbb{R})
, we have
{\sf ERM}_{a}(\lambda\tilde{X})=\lambda{\sf ERM}_{T_{\lambda}(a)}(\tilde{X})
, we obtain by positive homogeneity of
\mathring{\rho}
,

\displaystyle\forall\tilde{X}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}_{0}};\mathbb{R}),\quad\mathring{\rho}_{{\sf M}_{0}}(\tilde{X})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathring{\rho}_{{\sf M}_{0}}(\lambda\tilde{X})=\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{\overline{\mathbb{R}}}{\sf ERM}_{a}(\lambda\tilde{X})d\mu(a)

\displaystyle=\int_{\overline{\mathbb{R}}}{\sf ERM}_{T_{\lambda}(a)}(\tilde{X})d\mu(a)

\displaystyle=\int_{\overline{\mathbb{R}}}{\sf ERM}_{a}(\tilde{X})d(T_{\lambda}\#\mu)(a)\;,

where
T_{\lambda}\#\mu
is the pushforward measure of
\mu
by
T_{\lambda}
.
By uniqueness of the representation of
\mathring{\rho}_{{\sf M}_{0}}
, we have
\mu=T_{\lambda}\#\mu
for any
\lambda>0
.

We now assume for contradiction that
\mu((0,+\infty))>0
.
Then, there exists
0<a_{1}<a_{2}<+\infty
such that
\mu((a_{1},a_{2}))>0
.
Taking
\lambda>a_{2}/a_{1}
, we have that the intervals
(\lambda^{n}a_{1},\lambda^{n}a_{2})
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
are pairwise disjoint and each has the same positive measure under
\mu
, contradicting the finiteness of
\mu
.

The same argument leads to
\mu((-\infty,0))=0
, finally showing that
\mu
is supported on
\{0,-\infty,+\infty\}
.

Consequently, noting
a=\mu(\{0\})
,
b=\mu(\{-\infty\})
and
c=\mu(\{+\infty\})
, we have
a+b+c=1
and

\forall\tilde{X}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}_{0}};\mathbb{R}),\quad\mathring{\rho}_{{\sf M}_{0}}(\tilde{X})=a\cdot\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X})+b\cdot\operatorname{ess\,inf}(\tilde{X})+c\cdot\operatorname{ess\,sup}(\tilde{X})\;.

We now show that
a=1,b=1
or
c=1
using that for any independent random variables
\tilde{X}^{\prime},\tilde{Y}^{\prime}\in L^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}_{0}};\mathbb{R}_{+})
,

\mathring{\rho}_{{\sf M}_{0}}(\tilde{Z}\tilde{X})=\rho(\tilde{Z}\tilde{X})=\rho(\tilde{Z})\rho(\tilde{X})=\mathring{\rho}_{{\sf M}_{0}}(\tilde{Z})\mathring{\rho}_{{\sf M}_{0}}(\tilde{X})\;.

By non-atomicity, for any
0\leqslant x_{1}\leqslant x_{2}
and
0\leqslant z_{1}\leqslant z_{2}
, there exists independent random variables
\tilde{X}^{\prime},\tilde{Z}^{\prime}\in L^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}_{0}};\mathbb{R})
such that
\tilde{X}\sim\text{Unif}([x_{1},x_{2}])
and
\tilde{Z}\sim\text{Unif}([z_{1},z_{2}])
.
In particular, because
L^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}_{0}};\mathbb{R}_{+})
, we have
\mathring{\rho}(\tilde{X}\tilde{Z})=\mathring{\rho}(\tilde{X})\mathring{\rho}(\tilde{Z})
.

First, noting
u=a/2+b
and
v=a/2+c
, we have

\displaystyle\mathring{\rho}_{{\sf M}_{0}}(\tilde{X})

\displaystyle=a\frac{x_{1}+x_{2}}{2}+bx_{1}+cx_{2}=ux_{1}+vx_{2}\;,

\displaystyle\mathring{\rho}_{{\sf M}_{0}}(\tilde{Z})

\displaystyle=a\frac{z_{1}+z_{2}}{2}+bz_{1}+cz_{2}=uz_{1}+vz_{2}\;,

\displaystyle\mathring{\rho}_{{\sf M}_{0}}(\tilde{X}\tilde{Z})

\displaystyle=a\frac{x_{1}z_{1}+x_{1}z_{2}+x_{2}z_{1}+x_{2}z_{2}}{4}+bx_{1}z_{1}+cx_{2}z_{2}\;.

so the equality
\mathring{\rho}_{{\sf M}_{0}}(\tilde{X}\tilde{Z})=\mathring{\rho}_{{\sf M}_{0}}(\tilde{X})\mathring{\rho}_{{\sf M}_{0}}(\tilde{Z})
gives

\displaystyle(ux_{1}+vx_{2})(uz_{1}+vz_{2})

\displaystyle=a\frac{x_{1}z_{1}+x_{1}z_{2}+x_{2}z_{1}+x_{2}z_{2}}{4}+bx_{1}z_{1}+cx_{2}z_{2}

\displaystyle u^{2}x_{1}z_{1}+uv(x_{1}z_{2}+x_{2}z_{1})+v^{2}x_{2}z_{2}

\displaystyle=\left(u-\frac{a}{4}\right)x_{1}z_{1}+\frac{a}{4}(x_{1}z_{2}+x_{2}z_{1})+\left(v-\frac{a}{4}\right)x_{2}z_{2}\;.

Therefore, because this equality holds for any
0\leqslant x_{1}\leqslant x_{2}
and
0\leqslant z_{1}\leqslant z_{2}
, we have the system of equations:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &u^{2}=u-\frac{a}{4}\\
&uv=\frac{a}{4}\\
&v^{2}=v-\frac{a}{4}\end{aligned}\;.\right.

From the first and third equations, we have that
u
and
v
are roots of the equation
X^{2}-X+a/4=0
.

If they are equal, then
u=v=1/2
since
u+v=a+b+c=1
. Then, the second equation gives
a=1
.

If
u\neq v
, we immediately have
a<1
as
a=1
implies
u=v
. We now prove by contradiction that
a=0
. In fact, if
a\neq 0
, we have
1-a\in(0,1)
and noting
w=\min(u,v)
, we have that
w=\frac{1-\sqrt{1-a}}{2}<\frac{a}{2}
which contradicts the non-negativity of
b
or
c
.
Finally
a=0
so
u=b
and
v=c
are the two roots of
X^{2}-X=0
and
\{b,c\}=\{0,1\}
.

Because
a=1,b=1
or
c=1
, we now have

\left\{\begin{aligned} \forall\tilde{X}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}_{0}};\mathbb{R}),&\quad\mathring{\rho}_{{\sf M}_{0}}(\tilde{X})=\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X})\\
&\text{or }\\
\forall\tilde{X}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}_{0}};\mathbb{R}),&\quad\mathring{\rho}_{{\sf M}_{0}}(\tilde{X})=\operatorname{ess\,inf}(\tilde{X})\\
&\text{or }\\
\forall\tilde{X}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}_{0}};\mathbb{R}),&\quad\mathring{\rho}_{{\sf M}_{0}}(\tilde{X})=\operatorname{ess\,sup}(\tilde{X})\end{aligned},\right.

By law-invariance of
\mathring{\rho}
on
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
and non-atomicity of
(\Omega_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}_{0}})
, it directly implies:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \forall\tilde{X}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}),&\quad\mathring{\rho}(\tilde{X})=\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X})\\
&\text{or }\\
\forall\tilde{X}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}),&\quad\mathring{\rho}(\tilde{X})=\operatorname{ess\,inf}(\tilde{X})\\
&\text{or }\\
\forall\tilde{X}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}),&\quad\mathring{\rho}(\tilde{X})=\operatorname{ess\,sup}(\tilde{X})\end{aligned},\right.

so the desired result holds because
\rho
and
\mathring{\rho}
coincide on
L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \forall\tilde{X}\in L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}),&\quad\rho(\tilde{X})=\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X})\\
&\text{or }\\
\forall\tilde{X}\in L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}),&\quad\rho(\tilde{X})=\operatorname{ess\,inf}(\tilde{X})\\
&\text{or }\\
\forall\tilde{X}\in L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}),&\quad\rho(\tilde{X})=\operatorname{ess\,sup}(\tilde{X})\end{aligned}\;.\right.

∎

H.3

-continuity and Proof of

In this section we derive results for the case of

-continuous functionals. We first define

-continuity.

Definition H.7
.

The Wasserstein-1 metric
W^{1}(\nu_{1},\nu_{2})
between two laws
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}
is defined as

W_{1}(\nu_{1},\nu_{2})=\int_{0}^{1}|F_{\nu_{1}}^{-1}(u)-F_{\nu_{2}}^{-1}(u)|du\;.

A law-invariant risk measure
\rho(\tilde{X})
can be written
\rho(\tilde{X})=\varrho(\mathcal{L}(X))
for some
\varrho
that maps laws to scalars, see Proposition
Proposition

B.3
. With this notation, the risk measure
\rho
is
W^{1}
-continuous if for any law
\nu
with compact supports and any sequence of laws with compact supports
(\nu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
we have

\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}W_{1}(\nu_{n},\nu)=0\Rightarrow\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\varrho(\nu_{n})=\varrho(\nu)\;.

Intuitively,
W^{1}
-continuity corresponds to a continuity in the quantiles of the law.
Table
2
summarizes the
W^{1}
-continuity properties of common risk measures.

Theorem

4.5
provides a complete characterization of
W^{1}
-continuous, law-invariant risk measures that satisfy
1
or
2
.

We first prove that if
\rho
is a
W^{1}
-continuous law-invariant risk measure that satisfies
1
or
2
in either the case of static kernels or resampled kernels, then
\rho=\mathbb{E}
.

Proof of Theorem
4.5
.

Let
\rho
a risk measure that is non-constant,
W^{1}
-continuous and law-invariant on
L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
and that satisfies
1
or
2
in either the case of static kernels or resampled kernels.

By the conclusion of Proposition
4.3
,
\rho
coincides with the identity on constants and is positive homogeneous and additive independent on
L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
.

Let
{\sf M}_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
an ambiguity-averse MDP such that
(\Omega_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}_{0}})
is non-atomic (for example the one of Example
E.1
).
Now, for any
\tilde{X}\in L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
, consider a sequence of i.i.d. random variables
(\tilde{X}_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathcal{F}_{{\sf M}_{0}},\mathbb{P}_{{\sf M}_{0}};\mathbb{R})^{\mathbb{N}}
such that all the
\tilde{X}_{i}
have the same law as
\tilde{X}
.
By additive independence and positive homogeneity on
L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
, we have

\forall n\in\mathbb{N},\quad\rho\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{X}_{i}\right)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\leqslant n}\rho(\tilde{X}_{i})=\rho(\tilde{X})\;.

Denoting by
\nu_{n}
the law of the sample mean
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{X}_{i}
and by
\nu=\delta_{\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X})}
the Dirac measure concentrated in
\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X})
, we now show that
W_{1}(\nu_{n},\nu)\to_{n}0
. Indeed,

\displaystyle W_{1}(\nu_{n},\nu)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\left|F_{\nu_{n}}^{-1}(u)-F_{\nu}^{-1}(u)\right|\,du

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\left|F_{\nu_{n}}^{-1}(u)-\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X})\right|\,du

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{X}_{i}-\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X})\right|\right)\;,

where we have used in first equality that
\nu
is a Dirac measure and in the second equality that if
\tilde{U}
is a random variable uniformly distributed on
(0,1)
, then
F_{\nu_{n}}^{-1}(\tilde{U})
has the same distribution as
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{X}_{i}
.
Then, by boundedness of
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{X}_{i}
, the Dominated Convergence Theorem gives

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left(\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{X}_{i}-\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X})\right|\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\lim_{n\to\infty}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{X}_{i}-\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X})\right|\right)=\mathbb{E}[0]=0\;,

where the second equality comes from the fact that
\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{X}_{i}-\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X})\right|\to 0
almost surely by the Strong Law of Large Numbers.

Therefore, using
W^{1}
-continuity,

\displaystyle\rho(\tilde{X})

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\rho\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{X}_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\rho\left(\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{X}_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\rho(\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}))=\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X})

where the last equality uses that
\rho(c)=c
for
c\in\mathbb{R}
, as shown in Proposition
4.3
.
∎

We can now conclude by noting that it is known that
\rho=\mathbb{E}
satisfies both

and
2
for resampled kernels (this corresponds to distributionally robust MDPs with a set of distribution equal to the singleton

]
) but not for static kernels (as shown in Example
3.4
-
