NeuroMusic: protocol for a randomised-controlled trial of keyboard and singing music training programmes for older adults with mild cognitive impairment
Authors: Zoe Menczel Schrire, Helen Frances Mitchell, Lee-Fay Low, Nicole Espinosa, Philip Eames, Joseph Toltz, Peter Walsh, Loren Mowszowski, David Espinoza, Cindy Shin-Yi Lin, Neal Peres Da Costa, Sharon L Naismith
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMusic-based training programmes, such as learning how to play an instrument or sing in a choir, have been suggested as potential interventions for promoting healthy brain ageing in older adults at risk of cognitive decline because of their ability to enhance cognitive functions and potentially promote neuroplasticity. However, there is limited empirical evidence in older adults at risk of dementia, especially that evaluates both piano and singing interventions and their effects on cognition and neuroplasticity. In this protocol, we outline a study to assess the efficacy of keyboard and singing music training programmes on reducing cognitive decline and other outcomes in older adults with Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI).Methods and analysisThis randomised, single-blind, controlled, parallel-group trial aims to enrol 432 individuals with MCI from the community in Sydney, Australia. Participants are randomly allocated to participate in either keyboard lessons, singing lessons or a film discussion control group once a week for 3 months. The primary objective is to assess the effectiveness of two music training programmes (keyboard and choral singing) for enhancing verbal memory after 3 months compared with control. Additionally, we will examine how these music-based interventions affect other aspects of cognition, mood, sleep, overall well-being, markers of brain plasticity and blood biomarkers of Alzheimer’s disease and neurodegeneration. Tertiary objectives are to identify factors that impact the success of the interventions, such as participation rates, engagement levels and key demographic and clinical features. Outcomes are collected at baseline and at 3 and 9 months. The primary endpoint analysis will include all randomised participants to estimate the treatment effect using intention-to-treat principles. Primary and secondary outcomes will be analysed using linear mixed models and effect size measures will be calculated.This study will be the first robust, randomised controlled trial to assess the potential and relative value of music engagement for cognitive decline in high-risk MCI individuals, as well as broader effects on other markers of mental health, well-being and neurodegeneration. Co-designed with implementation in mind, the music interventions can potentially be delivered within memory clinic or community settings.Ethics and disseminationThe Sydney University Human Research Ethics Committee (2023-026) has approved this protocol. Introduction

Background and rationale

Dementia is a neurodegenerative condition that affects over 50 million people globally, and Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the most prevalent form, whereby memory loss is a key early symptom.1 Research has not yet led to a definitive cure for AD and related dementias,2 warranting efforts to focus on primary and secondary dementia prevention. Indeed, in AD, amyloid-beta accumulates in the brain 10 to 20 years before cognitive changes are evident.3 4 Once cognitive decline is apparent, up to 45% of individuals will convert to dementia within 5 years (see review by 5), presenting a critical window of opportunity for targeted secondary prevention strategies. Various terms have been used to describe this ‘at-risk period’, such as mild cognitive impairment (MCI) (see review 5) or age-associated memory impairment.6 Each term may use different thresholds, but all require evidence of cognitive decline, with the relative preservation of functional abilities.

During these critical risk periods, non-pharmacological interventions have been touted as safe, efficacious and economical options to improve cognition, particularly in older adults.7 Both physical exercise and cognitive interventions (eg, memory strategy or computerised training) are recommended by the American Academy of Neurology and by Australian specialists.8 Cognitive interventions have been shown to be efficacious with associated medium effect size improvements in memory and other cognitive domains.9 10 As little as 10 cognitive training sessions have enhanced specific cognitive abilities, with effects persisting for 5 years after training.11 Cognitive interventions are cost-effective, are safe,7 have good adherence9 12 13 and promote independent living.14 They also foster neuroplasticity (the ability of the brain to reorganise or grow neural networks in response to an experience)15—enhancing metabolism, blood flow,16 brain structure and function17 18 after 12 weeks. Importantly, the effect estimate of cognitive training is modestly higher in MCI compared with healthy older adults and those with dementia, showing that this ‘at-risk’ period is the optimal time for intervention.19 However, such interventions may not appeal to all and often require specialist input, software or technology, which restricts scalability and accessibility.

Although there is no definitive evidence that cognitive training can prevent dementia, further studies incorporating neuroimaging and dementia biomarkers may provide further mechanistic insights. A 3 month cognitive training programme improved neural activity, connectivity, blood flow and cognition in healthy older adults.16 Another study showed that multimodal cognitive training reduced resting-state functional connectivity between the posterior cingulate and superior frontal gyrus, correlating with improved cognition.20 A systematic review of older adults suggested that multi-domain cognitive training could enhance hippocampal functional connectivity.21 Additionally, a 6-month multicomponent cognitive training programme improved cognition and reduced glucose metabolism decline in cortical regions affected by AD in individuals with amnestic MCI.22 These findings suggest that MRI markers are likely to be important markers in assessing the efficacy of potential cognitive interventions.

Building on the cognitive training literature and the general notion that brain engagement is beneficial for cognition in later life, there has also been interest in music engagement as a possible intervention. Music making is a cognitive intervention that may offer the requisite cognitive challenges23 24 while being more appealing and accessible, as some, such as choir, do not require specialist equipment, technologies or highly trained staff. Music interventions are scalable across communities and adaptable for diverse ethnic and socioeconomic groups. Music making can also be intrinsically motivating, enjoyable and potentially more rewarding than engaging in cognitive training, due to a heightened sense of achievement, motivation, socialisation, variation and activation of pleasure centres.2527 Additionally, research has shown that older adults experience positive health outcomes when they feel a sense of control or mastery28 or are in situations that provide meaningful social engagement with others.29 30 Animal studies have demonstrated that the activation of aminergic reward neurotransmitters can influence learning and plasticity.31 Music can stimulate similar pathways because of the emotions it evokes.32

Choir singing can be considered a viable intervention that combines music, language and breathing management. Singing involves left-brain hemisphere lateralisation for language perception and production, and right-hemisphere activity for the perception and production of singing tasks.3335 Studies have indicated that singing can improve response times to musical nuances36 and improve vocal function.37 Importantly, singing has been shown to enhance overall quality of life38 and psychological well-being.3942 In a self-selected sample, singing has been demonstrated to improve self-reported physical health, decrease doctor visits, lower medication use, decrease instances of falls and reduce the occurrence of other health problems.43 Community choirs have also been shown to reduce loneliness and increase interest in life among diverse older adults.44 Furthermore, singing has been shown to improve speech perception in noise,45 46 which is particularly beneficial as hearing loss is a risk factor for dementia.47 Qualitative research indicates that singing may enhance cognitive function by providing increased stimulation, opportunities for reminiscing and learning experiences.42 48 One pre-post quasi-experiment showed improvements in memory, language, speech information processing and executive function after 12 weeks of group singing.49 Another cross-sectional study showed that older adults engaged in choir singing performed better in verbal flexibility, the domain of cognitive function that focuses on one’s ability to adapt their communication style.50 In summary, choir singing emerges as a multifaceted intervention that may confer significant benefits for cognitive, mental and physical health, particularly among older adults.

Playing a keyboard has also been suggested to enhance cognitive skills. For example, the interplay between motor movements and cognitive tasks can improve cognitive flexibility.51 Additionally, learning to read sheet music requires repetitive use of cognitive resources, which can also improve cognition.52 53 More recently, there has been some research investigating the effects of piano training in individuals with MCI; however, the range of neuropsychological outcomes explored was limited.54 Based on a cross-sectional study, it has been suggested that playing a musical instrument may have a greater impact on learning, working memory and processing speed than singing.55 Older adults with prior music experience have been shown to have better executive functioning compared with those without.56 Interestingly, preliminary evidence suggests that learning how to play the piano later in life may promote structural and functional changes in the brain.57 Previous research on non-musicians aged 24–38 years old has shown that learning a musical sequence on the piano through sensorimotor-auditory training can induce neuroplastic reorganisation in the auditory cortex.58 In healthy older adults, four studies have been conducted to investigate the effects of music-making interventions on neurobiological measures, but none have focused on individuals with MCI.59 Among these, only two studies showed a positive effect of piano training intervention on brain markers, showing increased frontal theta power60 and grey matter volume in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and cerebellum61 in the piano group compared with the control group after the intervention. However, since the study by West et al61 had a small sample size (n=20) and high attrition rates, the findings should be interpreted with caution. Finally, two studies of 155 older adults from the same participant pool found a stabilisation effect in response to piano training. The control group showed a decline in fibre density in the fornix62 and grey matter volume in a section of the primary auditory cortex.63 Overall, while these findings suggest that while there is promising evidence for the cognitive and neurobiological benefits of piano training, more research is needed to fully understand its impact on the ageing brain, specifically within people with MCI.

Convergent, yet limited quality data are supportive of music interventions for the prevention of cognitive decline. While many studies have examined music listening for behavioural symptoms in dementia, few have researched music making or learning in MCI. When examining the efficacy of active music interventions combined with physical activity, one randomised controlled trial in MCI found that both playing musical instruments and dance improved general cognitive function but only dance significantly improved memory recall compared with a health education control group.64 Another ongoing trial in MCI is comparing music practice and psychomotor training, but results are not yet available (NCT04546451).65 A recent systematic review of 19 studies involving 1024 older adults with MCI or dementia was conducted to investigate the effects of music-based interventions including singing, percussion and music listening on cognitive subdomains.66 The study found significant improvements in global cognition (as assessed by the Mini Mental State Examination), executive function (as assessed by the Frontal Assessment Battery) and episodic memory (assessed by the Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test) for those who participated in music-based interventions. Notably, however, of the three studies that included participants with MCI, two involved music listening or music with movement, while only one small study (n=26) investigated karaoke training.67 These existing studies identified beneficial effects on memory, an important outcome that is intricately linked to early hippocampal compromise68 and changes in functional brain connectivity.69 Additionally, in older adults at risk of dementia, currently playing a musical instrument is associated with improved markers of brain health, in comparison to those who have ceased or never played an instrument.70

In summary, while music-making and choral interventions may benefit mood, quality of life, socialisation and physical health in healthy older adults,64 71 72 only a few studies with limited sample sizes and less common music interventions have focused on participants with MCI. Targeting memory decline in people with MCI addresses a key early, significant feature of MCI that prompts health seeking within primary care, as well as more detailed assessment and intervention within the memory clinic or specialist setting. Moreover, the effects of music interventions on biomarkers of AD pathology or neurodegeneration are unknown. This trial was designed to address these extant gaps.

Objectives

Here, we report the protocol of the NeuroMusic trial, which will boost the evidence base for the efficacy of music engagement interventions for people at-risk for dementia. The NeuroMusic trial will specifically examine the efficacy of keyboard and choral singing interventions developed via co-design and informed by an implementation science framework. This led us to examine two music interventions that feature frequently used, practical media.73

Specifically, in older people with MCI, we aim to evaluate whether, compared with an active control condition, music interventions (keyboard and choral singing) are associated with:

Less decline in memory (primary outcome) at 3 months and 9 months.

Less decline in memory (primary outcome) at 3 months and 9 months.

Improved aspects of broader health and well-being including mood, sleep and global cognition at 3 months and 9 months.

Improved aspects of broader health and well-being including mood, sleep and global cognition at 3 months and 9 months.

Less accumulation of neurodegenerative pathology in blood and imaging biomarkers at 9 months.

Less accumulation of neurodegenerative pathology in blood and imaging biomarkers at 9 months.

We hypothesise that in comparison to the control condition, both music-based training programmes will be associated with less decline in verbal episodic memory in older adults with MCI. Further, we hypothesise that in comparison to the control condition, both music-based training programmes will be associated with less accumulation of neurodegenerative pathologyin blood and imaging biomarkers and associated with improved mood, sleep, quality of life, social connectedness and global cognition.

Trial design

The current Randomised Controlled Trial (RCT) began recruitment in May 2023 and will continue until the end of 2027.

This study is a parallel, 3 month, single-blind, three-armed RCT in which 432 community-dwelling, musically naive older Australians (60–90 years), including permanent residents and those living temporarily in Australia, with objective memory difficulties (>1 SD below normative scores), are randomised (1:1:1) to either the keyboard intervention, choral singing intervention, or an active control condition (ie, film-viewing and discussion sessions).

The trial design is summarised in table 1. Primary and secondary outcomes are measured at 3 months. There is also a 9 month long-term follow-up, captured 6 months after intervention completion. Individuals are encouraged not to participate in music-based learning outside their assigned medium before the final follow-up. For example, participants in the choral singing intervention may join a choir after completing the study intervention but should not start taking keyboard lessons. This protocol is written following the SPIRIT Recommendations for Interventional Trials statement74 (online supplemental file 2) and the WHO Trial Registration Dataset (online supplemental file 3).

Methods: participants, interventions and outcomes

Patient and public involvement

Consumer focus groups informed the design of the music interventions. The focus groups canvassed ideas regarding preferred types of music and music making, desirability and feasibility of a music making intervention, preferred session type and length and probability of long-term engagement with our target population. The final ideas were tested with older people with cognitive impairment in a further focus group and refined as necessary, resulting in finalised interventions for the RCT. The RCT was then presented to the Sydney Dementia Network Lived Experience Expert Advisory Panel, consisting of people with MCI or dementia or caregivers, which guided recruitment strategies and evaluated impact from a real-world perspective.

Study setting

Participants are being recruited in ‘waves’ comprising a maximum of up to 36 participants. Each wave consists of a maximum of 12 participants per group (depending on the randomisation sequence and speed of recruitment), and all participants in each wave begin and end the intervention on the same dates.

Recruitment

Participants with memory complaints are recruited from the community using Facebook advertising, media (ie, news, podcasts, websites and radio), community events, flyers posted in community centres, letterbox drops and word of mouth. The trial is also being promoted to Memory and Cognition services in Sydney, Australia, and via the StepUp for Dementia Research online recruitment tool.82 Participants are directed to the study website where they can download the Participant Information Statement and provide self-consent for the online pre-screen. This determines if they meet the eligibility criteria and if so, prompts them to enter their contact information so that a member of the study team can call them. The research staff then contact provisionally eligible participants to explain the study’s details, including its objectives, benefits, risks and requirements, and verbally confirm that they do not meet any exclusion criteria. Participants are also administered the TICS-M as part of the pre-screening process. If a participant’s score falls below 22, they do not proceed to the screening stage. In this circumstance, the researcher provides additional resources to participants who express concern about their memory, including links and locations of memory clinics for further diagnostic investigations and management, outside the trial. Before enrolment, participants are encouraged to contact the study team if they have any questions or concerns. Study procedures are summarised in figure 1. Participants who have satisfied the aforementioned requirements are booked for a 90 minute online screening assessment. At this stage, participants are sequentially assigned a unique, three-digit participant screening number in ascending order. The results of screening tests are provided to ineligible participants on request.

Data collection and management

Plans for assessment and collection of outcomes

After obtaining informed consent, the neuropsychologist administers standardised neuropsychological tests, which are then scored and used to inform eligibility. If participants are eligible, their screening assessment is used as their baseline assessment. Participants are excluded if they score too highly on memory testing (ie, they do not have memory impairment) or, in rare circumstances, are found to score in a range that might signify dementia, as determined by the clinician. If this occurs, the participants are provided with feedback and with recommendations for further investigation and are provided with options as appropriate. Note that if participants have been referred from a memory and cognition clinic and if neuropsychological assessment was performed within the prior 3 months, consent is sought to access their scores, and these tests are not repeated. Eligible participants proceed to complete baseline measures. The same measures are completed for a 3 month follow-up. Questionnaires, cognitive tests and actigraphy are administered at the 9 month follow-up. Blood collection occurs at baseline and at 9 month follow-up (figure 1).

Magnetic Resonance Imaging

A subset of participants (the first four waves enrolled) undergoes a 60 min 3T MRI brain scan at the Brain and Mind Centre (GE scanner, 32 channel coil). The MRI protocol includes the following sequences: (i) Sag 3D T1 BRAVO, (ii) Sag 3D T2 FLAIR, (iii) two T2 high-resolution hippocampus, (iv) Axe SWI, (v) Diffusion weighted imaging (DWI) and (vi) Resting state functional MRI (rsfMRI). The follow-up MRI is conducted at a 3 month follow-up without the Sag 3D T2 FLAIR and Axe SWI to last 45 min to reduce participant burden.

These MRI sequences are collected to allow for the assessment of key brain measures such as hippocampal volume, prefrontal cortical thickness and white matter lesion volume, as well as the functional integrity of resting-state brain networks (rsfMRI) and the structural integrity of white matter tracts (DWI).

Blood collection

A fasting 30 mL blood sample is collected by a trained phlebotomist. Blood samples are processed within 30 min after collection, centrifuged and aliquoted into 500 µL (plasma and serum) and 1 mL (buffy coat) screw cap tubes and stored at −80°C for future batch analysis.

Neuropsychological Tests

Neuropsychological assessments are conducted by a trained neuropsychologist. To strengthen our cognitive conclusions, we will consolidate these test results into composite scores that reflect performance in five cognitive domain composites: executive functioning, verbal memory, verbal learning, processing speed and language (see table 1 for details). To create these cognitive composites, age-corrected standard z-scores will be calculated for each test. Then, for each participant, the mean z-score for all tests within a composite will be calculated, giving equal weight to each individual test used in a composite (eg, Executive functioning: ((Delis-Kaplan Executive Function System Colour Word Interference Test) (DKEFS CWIT) condition 3 z-scores+DKEFS CWIT condition 4 z-scores/2) + OTMT part B z-scores+COWAT z-scores+Digit span z-scores)/4).

Actigraphy monitoring and sleep diary

To objectively measure at-home sleep-wake patterns (such as sleep efficiency, nocturnal awakenings, sleep timing and total sleep time), all participants, except those who join within 1 week of the intervention starting, are offered an actigraphy watch (GeneActiv, Activinsights Ltd., Cambridgeshire, UK) to wear for 1 week before the intervention, 1 week after the intervention and during the 9 month follow-up. This is a non-invasive method of monitoring human rest/activity cycles and uses an accelerometer to record movement over a given threshold. The device is set to a measurement frequency of 30 Hz. During the screening process, the researcher explains the actigraphy watch and offers it to participants as an optional part of the study. Participants are given the watch when they attend in person for their blood collection and are given a paid return parcel to mail back. A 7 day sleep diary to record sleep quality and habits is given to participants with the actigraphy device.

Assignment of interventions: allocation and implementation

Sequence generation and randomisation

Randomisation occurs after completing all baseline assessments and is on a 1:1:1 basis to keyboard, choir or control group, stratified by age (60–65 or older than 65 years). The blinded study statistician created a computer-generated randomisation sequence and uploaded it to REDCap for the research team to follow. For each participant, the study coordinator opens their online RedCap record and clicks ‘randomise’ which allocates them the next group assignment in the pre-uploaded stratified sequence.

Concealment mechanism

Following baseline assessments, the unblinded study coordinator randomises participants. Each participant is assigned a unique randomisation number sequentially assigned in ascending order. To ensure the participants’ anonymity, all documents use the participants’ unique screening and randomisation numbers.

Assignment of interventions: blinding

Who will be blinded

To maintain allocation concealment, only the necessary study team members are aware of group allocation (eg, the study coordinator conducting randomisation and informing participants of their group, relevant research assistants, and the music intervention facilitators). All study members that conduct assessments (eg, phlebotomy or neuropsychology) and analyses are blinded.

Procedure for unblinding if needed

As participants are not blinded to the intervention, there is no procedure for unblinding.

Interventions

Explanation for the choice of comparators

Group description

Duration

All groups aim to complete 24 hours of group training (or active control) over 12 weeks. All participants are requested to attend a 2 hour session once a week (minimum request of 80% participation). However, once randomised, if participants miss more than 80% of sessions, they are still invited to attend, pending approval from the group facilitator as missing too many sessions in a row may disrupt the rest of the class.

Location

All three interventions occur at the Sydney Conservatorium of Music and are facilitated by professional musicians.

Music interventions

Approach

Each session is structured with an initial warm-up followed by a brief review of material, and the remaining portion of the class focuses on learning and practising new skills and concepts and practising repertoire. Straight after class, there is a 25 minute socialisation session with light refreshments in a dedicated room. The control group also attend a socialisation session.

Participants cover new and previously covered repertoire each week. There is no pressure on perfection but encouragement to attempt activities and participate. Facilitators focus on both gradual levels of short-term achievement and long-term goals. Their inclusion increases the size of the ensemble to reduce vocal exposure and provide more blending of voices. At 3 months, participants are invited to be part of a group performance in front of the unblinded study team and the other music intervention group and selected family or friends.

Progress and practice

Progress is recorded for each participant during each class session. Participants are recommended to practise for 30 minutes outside of the formal session, six times a week. If participants decide to practise at home, they receive weekly guidance and access to support resources. A series of weekly practice guides are provided to review course materials and recordings of each weekly class. This will also allow participants who were absent to catch up. Participants are asked to record their practice (eg, time spent, type of activity, level of engagement) in a practice diary provided by the instructor.

Keyboard

Keyboard intervention: Participants allocated to this arm receive keyboard lessons from a professional piano instructor. These lessons consist of basic piano technique and literature, music theory, dexterity exercises, conventional music notation reading and rhythmic training. At each training session, participants are expected to practise, and at times, perform technical exercises, sight reading examples and piano pieces. Lessons include solo and ensemble playing out loud as well as individual practice. To encourage practice, participants in the keyboard intervention are provided with small keyboards to take home until the end of the 9 month follow-up. They are also provided with headphones to wear (to avoid causing disturbance to co-inhabitants).

Documentation

Keyboard and choral singing intervention facilitators are asked to maintain a weekly journal on their experiences leading the musical interventions. Facilitators are also interviewed regarding their experience after the intervention as outlined below.

Active control group

The active control group in this trial involves attending weekly film-viewing events and taking part in a semi-structured discussion. The amount of time exposed to the control group is matched to the two active interventions. This discussion focuses on personal questions of taste and reaction to the film (from the participants). Importantly, the facilitator does not bring musical expertise or instruction into the discussion—the emphasis is on the participants’ perspectives. The facilitator might introduce discussion questions that address the film’s sociological, historical, political and cultural aspects to prompt discussions within the group. Thus, control participants experience social elements similar to those of the intervention groups and hence, the duration and the potential positive impacts of socialisation in the intervention groups are closely aligned. Participants watch the film for 45–60 minutes. Then, depending on the length of the film screening, the facilitator initiates a 20–30 minute semi-structured discussion. Copyright permission to use the films for educational purposes has been obtained. After completion of the trial at 9 months, participants in the active control group are offered the opportunity to engage with keyboard or choral singing interventions, depending on availability in those classes.

Strategies to improve adherence to interventions and plans to promote participant retention and complete follow-up

If a participant does not attend a weekly session of any of the groups, a member of the research team attempts to contact them over 2 weeks via telephone or email to establish the reason for the non-contact, checks the participant’s condition and encourages attendance. If these phone calls fail to reach the participant, the researcher emails them. However, the study team still attempts to contact the participant near the 3 and 9 month time points to collect outcome intention to treat data if the participant is willing. Participants are offered a $50 AUD gift voucher at the conclusion of their participation in the trial. Participants who withdraw but still are willing to complete follow-up are also offered this gift card.

Criteria for discontinuing or modifying allocated interventions

If all reasonable attempts to contact over a reasonable period fail, the investigator will declare the participant ‘lost to follow-up’.

Relevant concomitant care permitted or prohibited during the trial

During the trial, participants have no restrictions apart from engaging in any of the exclusion criteria. They are asked not to engage in any music learning, apart from that which pertains to the group in which they are allocated.

Provisions for post-trial care

The Trial Steering Committee (TSC) reviews any significant on-study incidents such as injury or death, dropout rates and participants lost to follow-up. The TSC regularly considers participant safety, confidentiality and data integrity issues and recommends study continuation. We do not anticipate the need for post-trial care due to the nature of the trial.

Outcomes

Primary outcome

The primary outcome is change in verbal memory performance measured by the Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test (RAVLT)75 delayed recall (ie, trial 7) score (number of correct answers/15 after 20 min delay) between baseline and 3 months. This outcome was chosen as it (a) is meaningful to patients who present to health services with memory concerns (b) is an early feature of dementia (c) has been shown to be sensitive to changes after 10–12 week cognitive training,13 93 94 sleep94 and other139396 interventions. While verbal memory tests such as the RAVLT have been shown to have practice effects when repeated, these would be present in all randomised conditions. Hence, only the standard version of the RAVLT was utilised.

Sample size

Statistical power analysis for the primary aim has been completed by our trial statistician (DE) using SAS (V.9.4) GLMPOWER. Our existing wave 1 and 2 data show an attrition rate of<10% for our target sample. Controlling for a type I error of 0.05 (two-tailed) with 80% statistical power and assuming a correlation of 0.5 between baseline and 3 month primary outcome RAVLT-7 scores (estimated from blinded analysis of wave 1 and 2 data), a sample size of 232 would be required to detect an effect size of 0.34 between the experimental groups (keyboard+choral) and control group. To accommodate comparisons of each intervention group with control, a final sample size of 308 would provide 0.80 power at alpha 0.05 to detect an effect size of 0.34 (as per meta-analysis).66 Accounting for attrition (estimated at 10%), and the clustering effect of the waves introduced by the delivery method of the intervention (intraclass correlation, ICC, of 0.02), the target sample size is 432 participants (n=144 per randomised condition). All statistical analyses will be described in a predefined analysis plan (see ‘Statistical Methods’ section).

Statistical methods

Statistical methods for primary and secondary outcomes

All randomised participants will be included in the primary endpoint analysis to estimate the treatment effect using an intention-to-treat principle.

Descriptive analyses will estimate means and proportions, CIs and measures of variability of all measures. Linear mixed models (LMMs) will be implemented to investigate changes in verbal memory performance measured by the RAVLT trial 7 between and within each group (keyboard, choral singing and control) across different time points (baseline and 3 months).

The primary and secondary outcomes will be analysed via LMMs using maximum likelihood estimation. The model will include random intercepts, fixed effects of time (baseline and 3 months), intervention (keyboard, choral singing and control), and their interactions. Assessment of the treatment effect will be via the treatment-by-time interaction.

Discussion

The results of the current study have the potential to provide valuable evidence regarding the effectiveness of music interventions in preventing cognitive decline and fostering neuroplasticity in older adults with MCI. Despite the promising theoretical foundation for music interventions, there remains a lack of robust evidence from randomised controlled trials specifically targeting this population. If the findings from this Phase 3 RCT support our hypothesis, the next step will be to focus on the practical translation of these interventions into sustainable, community-based health practices. If the interventions were scaled, other allied health facilitators could potentially deliver the interventions; however, this would require sufficient training, further testing and validation. Translation will involve co-designing programmes with the community to ensure they are culturally relevant, accessible and scalable, ultimately aiming to integrate them into public health services tailored to older adults with memory difficulties, such as memory and cognition clinics.

The existing body of literature examining music interventions for individuals with MCI is diverse, with an integrative review identifying eight studies that utilised 25 cognitive outcome measures. These studies also employed a variety of music interventions, including passive music listening, musical stimuli delivered via digital devices, multitask movement music therapy, recorder lessons and percussion instruction.108 While the review highlights some positive results, the studies showed significant variability in scope, cognitive profiles and quality, which made it difficult to draw consistent conclusions. Importantly, the review did not include studies focused on keyboard learning or singing—an area our protocol uniquely addresses. This gap underscores the contribution of our study, which will examine in a definitive trial, a previously under-researched aspect of music intervention that could provide new insights into cognitive enhancement for older adults with MCI.

A critical challenge for scaling music-based interventions is ensuring equitable access, particularly among older adults, socioeconomically or cognitively disadvantaged populations, diverse ethnic groups, or those living in rural or regional communities. It is essential that these interventions are not only effective but also feasible, cost-efficient and appealing to the target populations. Co-designing the programme with input from the community and key stakeholders has been a vital step to help ensure that the interventions are tailored to the specific needs and preferences of older adults with cognitive impairment. By actively involving the community in the design and implementation phases, we can ensure that the intervention is culturally relevant and resonates with participants, thereby enhancing engagement and adherence. Additionally, for such programmes to be adopted widely in public health practice, they must demonstrate broader benefits beyond cognitive outcomes, including improvements in quality of life, emotional well-being and daily functioning.109

This study has several notable strengths. The randomised controlled design enhances the internal validity by minimising selection bias, while the comprehensive assessment of outcomes, including cognition, neurobiological and blood biomarkers of dementia, mood and sleep, offers a holistic understanding of the interventions’ effects. Nonetheless, some limitations should be acknowledged. First, as a single-blind trial, participants were aware of their group allocation, which may have influenced their behaviour or self-reported outcomes. This limitation is largely unavoidable in behavioural intervention studies. Second, the study’s ability to generalise to diverse cultural, Indigenous and socioeconomic groups may be constrained by its inner-city setting, which required participants to travel weekly for 3 months. Third, the 3 month intervention duration, although chosen for feasibility, may not be sufficient to detect meaningful changes in some neurodegenerative markers. Finally, during the 9 month follow-up period, it was not possible to collect accurate adherence or engagement data; however, focus groups qualitatively explored participants’ ongoing music engagement, with findings to be reported separately. Despite these limitations, the rigorous design and breadth of outcome measures enhance the study’s contribution to understanding how structured music interventions may influence cognitive and emotional health in older adults with MCI.

In conclusion, this study seeks to build a robust evidence base for the use of keyboard or choral singing interventions for people with MCI. If the Phase 3 RCT results are promising, the next phase will focus on translating the intervention into a scalable model that is accessible, cost-effective and beneficial to older adults at risk for dementia. The involvement of the community in the co-design process will be crucial to ensuring that the intervention meets the needs of participants and can be effectively implemented in real-world settings. The goal is to incorporate these music-based interventions into public health practices, providing older adults with a sustainable, engaging and effective tool for cognitive health and overall well-being.

Trial status

Recruitment began in May 2023 and is estimated to last for 4 years.
