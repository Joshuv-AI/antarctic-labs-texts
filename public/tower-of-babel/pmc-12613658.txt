The effectiveness of unfractionated heparin vs. low molecular weight (Enoxaparin) in Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrom (ARDS) and metabolic dysfunction: an old drug for new indication!
Authors: Shabnam Delasoud, Mojtaba Mojtahedzadeh, Mohammad Sharifzadeh, Mohammad Hossein Ghahremani, Abbass Kebriaeezadeh, Maryam Gholami, Samin Sabzevari, Omid Sabzevari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute Respiratory Distress Syndrome is a lung disorder defined by the acute onset of hypoxemia, the commonest being abdominal sepsis.Many biomarkers have been studied for diagnostic prognostication and ARDS pharmacotherapy. The current study aim to assess the protective effects of UFH versus Enoxaparin in sepsis-induced ARDS and related metabolic sequelae.MethodsSepsis was initiated through cecal ligation and puncture (CLP). Wistar rats were divided to: sham, CLP, CLP + unfractionated Heparin, and CLP + Enoxaparin and CLP + distilled water groups. Levels of serum Lipoxin A4 (LXA4), lipoprotein lipase (LPL), leukotriene E4 (LTE4), leukotriene B4 (LTB4), interleukin-8 (IL-8), were quantified. Furthermore, mRNA expression of receptors for advanced glycation end products (RAGE) and angiopoietin-2 (ANG-2) were assessed. Histopathological study was conducted to assess any lung injuries.ResultsSeptic rats demonstrated higher levels of leukotriene E4, leukotriene B4, and interleukin-8, while treatment with unfractionated Heparin attenuated these levels but enoxaparin effectiveness on LTB-4 and IL-8 was not as significant as heparin while its was equally effective on LTE-4. Moreover, mRNA levels of RAGE and ANG-2 were enhanced in CLP rats. These elevations were mitigated by treatment with unfractionated Heparin and reduced by enoxaparin to a lesser extent. Treatment with unfractionated Heparin increased the lipoxin A4 and lipoprotein lipase levels but enoxaparin had no effect on the LPL level. Lung protective effect of unfractionated Heparin was further confirmed by histopathological observations of lung tissue samples.ConclusionOur study demonstrates that UFH can modulate ARDS and metabolic dysfunction in hyperinflammatory conditions like sepsis.Keywords: Unfractionated heparin, Enoxaparin, Sepsis, Metabolic dysfunction, ARDS

Introduction

Sepsis is a potentially life-threatening condition that occurs when the body’s response to an infection causes widespread inflammation [1]. This condition, along with septic shock, presents a significant global health concern, impacting millions of people annually and leading to high mortality rates, ranging from one-third to one-sixth of affected individuals [2]. Despite ongoing clinical trials, definitive treatment for sepsis remains elusive, and managing this disease remains a significant challenge in the Intensive Care Unit. Failure to properly treat sepsis can lead to Multi Organ Dysfunction Syndrome (MODS) in various organs, including the lungs, brain, kidney, and liver, further complicating treatment [3].

Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is a standalone risk factor for organ dysfunction and mortality in sepsis patients, often triggered by sepsis [4]. The mortality rate for patients with ARDS remains high, ranging from 24 to 48%, despite the use of lung recruitment and protective ventilation strategies, such as prone ventilation and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) [5]. Metabolism, which encompasses all chemical reactions in an organism, consists of anabolic and catabolic processes that are usually in equilibrium. However, this equilibrium can be disrupted in pathological conditions like sepsis. Understanding the role of metabolic disorders in the pathophysiology of sepsis can help improve treatment management and identify potential therapeutic targets [6]. Metabolic dysfunction in sepsis includes increased lactate and hydrolysis of ATP, hyperglycemia, decreased acetyl L-carnitine, dysfunction of the Tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, decreased PDH and LPL, reduction of specialized pro-resolving lipid mediators (SPM) and enhanced proinflammatory lipids [7].

It has been well-established that Unfractionated heparin (UFH) contains anticoagulant properties and is widely used in clinical practice due to its cost-effectiveness [8]. In recent years, there has been increasing attention on the anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects of UFH, suggesting its potential use as a therapeutic drug for a specific subset of septic patients. However, the exact mechanism of action of UFH has yet to be fully understood [9]. It can protect endothelial cells, neutrophils, and platelets and also benefits the lung, liver, kidney, coagulation system and immune system. Moreover, UFH, which is similar to glycosaminoglycans, has a strong binding capacity to heparin binding protein (HBP) and leads to the inactivation of it [10]. It should be noted that HBP is a biomarker of sepsis and is related to the severity of septic shock and organ dysfunction [11]. Also, it is reported that early usage of UFH in patients with severe sepsis improves coagulation function and shortens the length of intensive care unit (ICU) stay. Therefore, UFH, which has multiple biological effects, can be a suitable choice [12]. Low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH), such as Enoxaparin sodium, is commonly used as an anticoagulant medication [13]. Enoxaparin, which has a high affinity for heparin sulfate, inhibits factor Xa by catalyzing its binding to antithrombin. It has been shown to prevent microvascular thrombosis and attenuate endothelial damage in endotoxemic rats [14].

This research aims to assess the comparative effectiveness of unfractionated Heparin versus Low Molecular Weight Heparin (e.g., Enoxaparin) in ARDS and metabolic dysfunction following animal CLP-induced sepsis. For this purpose, ARDS biomarkers levels, including receptors for advanced glycation end products (RAGE), angiopoietin-2 (ANG-2), and interleukin-8(IL-8), were assessed. The effect of UFH and Enoxaparin treatments on the levels of metabolic dysfunction biomarkers such as LipoxinA4 (LXA4), lipoprotein lipase (LPL), leukotriene E4 (LTE4), and leukotriene B4(LTB4), was also evaluated.

Materials and methods

Reagents or chemicals

Unfractionated heparin (UFH) ampule and Enoxaparin pre-filled syringes were purchased from Iran Hormone Pharmaceutical Company (Tehran, Iran). Ketamine and xylazine were purchased from Sigma Chemical Co. (St. Louis, MO, USA). LPL and LTB4, ELISA kits were procured from CUSABIO, Wuhan, China. LPX4 kit used was produced from BioVision, USA. LTE4 and IL-8 ELISA kits were manufactured by abbexa, Cambridge, UK. RNAXPLUS reagent (Trizole), cDNA synthesis kit and Real-Time PCR Master mix 2X were procured from AnaCell, Iran.

Animals

For this study, adult male Wistar rats weighing 200–250 g in good health conditions were supplied by the Tehran University of Medical Sciences (TUMS), Tehran, Iran. They were kept in the standard polycarbonate cages for rodents. The environment temperature was kept at 23 ± 2 °C with normal light/dark cycles. Moreover, the animals had access to healthy, sufficient water and food. All rats used in this study were adapted to the above-mentioned experimental conditions for seven days before experiments were started. All animal-related operations were conducted according to NIH guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (National Institutes of Health Publication No. 85 − 23, revised 1996). Also, the operations were authorized by the TUMS Ethical Committee for Animal Experimentation (code: IR.TUMS.MEDICINE.REC.1400.040).

Sepsis induction

Poly-microbial sepsis was induced in the animals by the cecal ligation and puncture method. In summary, intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of a mixture of ketamine (80 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg) was used to anesthetize the animals. Under aseptic conditions, a midline abdominal incision was made to expose the cecum. During the procedure, the cecum was tied off below the ileocecal valve, punctured twice using a 21-gauge needle and compressed to expel fecal matter from the puncture site. Following the operation, the cecum was returned to the abdominal cavity, and the abdominal incision was sutured. In the sham-operated group, the same abdominal incision and intestinal manipulation were performed, but the cecum was not tied or punctured. All animals received 5 ml of normal saline subcutaneously for fluid resuscitation [15].

Grouping and drug administration

The animals were arbitrarily distributed into four groups, each consisting of five rats. The initial group, designated as the sham group, did not undergo any surgical procedure, while the CLP surgery was performed on all other groups. In the second group (CLP group), no drug treatment was administrated after induction of sepsis. The third group (UFH group) received unfractionated heparin (200 U/kg i.p.) at 6 h and 12 h after surgery. Six hours after surgery, the fourth (Enoxaparin) group received enoxaparin (1.5 mg/kg i.p.)

After the surgeries, the animals were euthanized within twenty-four hours to obtain their blood through cardiac puncture. The blood that was drawn was then spun at 3000 revolutions per minute for 15 min at a temperature of 4 °C to acquire serum samples. After that, the lung tissues were obtained and instantly kept at −80 °C.

Assessment of metabolic dysfunction

The serum lipoprotein lipase (LPL) and Leukotriene B4 (LTB4) levels were assessed by commercially available kits from CUSABIO, Wuhan, China. Serum Lipoxin A4 (LPX4) levels were measured using commercial ELISA kits from BioVision, USA. The serum levels of leukotriene E4 (LTE4) and IL-8 were quantified using rat-specific ELISA kits from Abbexa, Cambridge, UK. The results are presented in units per liter.

Quantitative reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

One Step RNAXPLUS reagent (Trizol) was bought from AnaCell, Iran, and used to extract total RNA from lung tissues. A cDNA synthesis kit (AnaCell, Iran, Catalog number: AC140345) was then used to reverse-transcribe the total RNA into complementary DNA (cDNA). All the test steps were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The study used primers provided by GenFanavaran (Tehran, Iran). Real-Time PCR Master mix 2X was bought from AnaCell, Iran, and was used for the Real-time PCR analysis.

The mRNA levels of RAGE and ANG-2 were adjusted based on the mRNA levels of glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH). The findings were expressed as fold changes in the experimental groups relative to the mRNA levels of the sham group. The 2 − ΔΔCt method was employed to determine the mRNA abundance. Histopathological examination

Lung tissues fixed in a 10% formalin solution were used for histopathological studies. This was followed by washing the lung tissues with PBS buffer overnight and drying out by increasing the alcohol concentrations via crossing through the solutions. Subsequently, the tissue samples were embedded in paraffin and arranged in blocks. The tissue sections, 5–6 μm thick, were stained with hematoxylin-eosin and examined under a light microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) at ×100 and ×400 magnification.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was carried out using GraphPad Prism software version 9. All data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). To analyze significant differences between different experimental groups, the one-way analysis of variance method (ANOVA) was employed. Post hoc comparisons were conducted using Tukey’s post hoc test, with a significance level set at p < 0.05.

Results

Serum Levels of LTE4, LTB4, LXA4 and LPL

The leukotrienes LTB4 and LTE4 are known to amplify inflammation. The levels of these leukotrienes were measured in various groups, and the results are shown in Figs. 1(a) and 1(b). The levels of LTB4 and LTE4 in the group that underwent a surgical procedure (CLP) were much higher than those in the control group (sham). However, the levels of LTE4 dropped significantly (p < 0.01) in groups treated with either UFH or Enoxaparin (CLP + UFH and CLP + E). These groups also had LTE4 levels similar to those of the sham group. Interestingly, the LTB4 levels were significantly lower in the CLP + UFH group compared to the CLP + E group.

LXA4 is a lipid that helps decrease inflammation. The levels of LXA4 in different groups were analyzed and presented in Fig. 2. As anticipated, the amount of LXA4 in the group that underwent a surgical procedure (CLP) was much lower than in the control group (sham). However, treatment with either UFH or enoxaparin led to an increase in LXA4 levels. The increase in LXA4 levels due to UFH was much more significant compared to enoxaparin. The LXA4 level in the group treated with UFH (CLP + UFH) was almost the same as the sham group.

Lipoprotein lipase (LPL) is an enzyme involved in breaking down triglycerides into fatty acids and glycerol [16]. The levels of LPL in different groups were analyzed and are presented in Fig. 3. The LPL levels in the group that underwent a surgical procedure (CLP) were much lower than the control group (sham). Treatment with UFH led to a significant increase in LPL levels, while enoxaparin did not have a significant effect. The group treated with UFH (CLP + UFH) also showed an even more significant increase in LPL levels than the sham group.

mRNA expressions of RAGE and ANG-2

The levels of RAGE and ANG-2 mRNAs in the lungs were gauged using RT-qPCR. Increased concentrations of RAGE and ANG-2 mRNAs were detected in the CLP group in contrast to the sham group (Fig. 5). Application of UFH (p < 0.001) and enoxaparin (p < 0.05) led to notably reduced RAGE expression levels in comparison to the CLP group (Fig. 5(a)). Furthermore, UFH (p < 0.001) and enoxaparin (p < 0.01) treatments resulted in significantly lower levels of ANG-2 mRNA in comparison to the CLP group (Fig. 5(b)).

Histopathological evaluation

Histopathological examination of the lung 24 h after CLP induction shows that the structure of the pulmonary lobule and alveoli was clear, the cavity was clean, and the alveolar wall was not thick. Inflammatory cell infiltration was not observed in the alveolar (Fig. 6A). Rats in the CLP group showed a completely indistinct structure of the pulmonary lobule (Fig. 6B). In addition, the alveolar cavities were hardly discernible with bleeding and discharge, and the alveolar wall was thickened and inflamed.

The scale bars for x100 and x400 are 50 µm and 259µm, respectively

In CLP-operated rats treated with unfractionated heparin (Fig. 6C), part of the structure of the pulmonary lobule was clear, and the alveolar cavity was distinct. The alveolar wall was clean and slightly thickened, and inflammatory cell infiltration in the alveoli was observed. Moreover, CLP-operated enoxaparin-treated rats (Fig. 6D) showed interstitial hemorrhage and moderate vascular congestion, partial degeneration of alveolar structures, cell debris in the lumen of some alveoli, and some areas, alveoli with regular morphology were observed.

Discussion

In the current study, we explored the effect of UFH and enoxaparin on metabolic dysfunction and ARDS in a CLP-induced sepsis model, and we found that UFH modulated metabolic dysfunction induced by CLP, alleviated ARDS thus attenuating the inflammation in the CLP model of sepsis. In our study, we found that inducing sepsis increased the levels of LTB-4, LTE-4, and IL-8 in the serum of rats. However, when septic rats were treated with UFH, the levels of these markers decreased but enoxaparin effectiveness on LTB-4 and IL-8 was not as significant as heparin and its was also equally effective on LTE-4. Furthermore, the mRNA levels of ANG-2 and RAGE in the lung were higher in septic rats, but treating them with UFH significantly reduced the expression of these genes and reduced by enoxaparin to a lesser extent. Additionally, sepsis induction resulted in decreased LPL and LPXA4 levels. UFH treatment not only exhibited anti-inflammatory effects but also effectively reduced metabolic dysfunction in septic rats. Moreover, UFH therapy increased the reduced levels of LPL and LPXA4 in septic rats but enoxaparin had no effect on the LPL level.

Moreover, the histopathological examinations of the lungs showed that the harmful effect of sepsis on the lungs was mitigated by UFH or enoxaparin, with UFH showing a more pronounced alleviation.

In 2017, Hammond et al. demonstrated that Heparin binds to LPL, causing it to be released into the bloodstream, where it can break down circulating triglycerides. The study showed that increasing LPL production and release could help insulin and heparin in lowering serum triglyceride levels [17]. Our findings revealed that UFH significantly increased the LPL rate in septic rats, while enoxaparin did not affect the LPL level.

Recently, Braz-de-Melo et al. showed that heparin boosts the anticoagulant effect of antithrombin (AT) and could be beneficial when used with fibrinolytic drugs for severe COVID-19 patients. Moreover, heparin can also regulate immune responses, reducing TNF-α-mediated inflammation, inhibiting IL-6 production and release, and binding to complement proteins and leukotriene B4 (LTB4) [18]. Also Madhur D. Shastri demonstrated a non-anticoagulant fraction of enoxaparin was reported as a partial inhibitor of IL-6 release, indicating that the signalization range assigned to this cytokine may be mitigated in the presence of LMWH [19]. Our study revealed that UFH significantly reduced the LTB4 level in septic rats. Additionally, we observed that enoxaparin was able to somewhat decrease the LTB4 levels, but its effectiveness was not as significant as heparin.

Suzuki and colleagues (2002) found that pretreatment with inhaled heparin reduced hyperventilation-induced eicosanoid mediator release. Our study demonstrated that heparin significantly decreased the LTE4 level in septic rats, and Enoxaparin was equally effective [20].

In this study, the researchers investigated the impact of UFH and enoxaparin on lipoxin A4 in an animal model of sepsis for the first time. The results indicated that UFH can increase the levels of lipoxin A4. Walker et al. showed that lipoxin A4 reduces damage in various inflammation models, such as renal ischemia-reperfusion injury, colitis, and LPS-induced acute lung injury, by preventing excessive neutrophil infiltration to the injury site [21].

In research conducted by Yupei Li et al. in it was demonstrated that heparin possesses anticoagulant properties as well as the ability to hinder the function of specific adhesion molecules. Additionally, it was found that heparin inhibits the production of inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IFN-ɣ, IL-6, and IL-8 by blocking NFκB signaling, and also blocks the cleavage of complement proteins. When unfractionated heparin (UFH) was introduced to histone-induced whole blood, concentrations of IL-6, IL-8, C3a, and C5a were notably decreased in either a dose-dependent or dose-independent manner [22].Our study also demonstrated that UFH reduced the IL-8 rate in septic rats, with enoxaparin showing some effectiveness but not as much as heparin.

In a study by Li et al. it was found that UFH (unfractionated heparin) increases Ang-1 and decreases Ang-2 mRNA expressions after LPS (lipopolysaccharide) stimulation. Their findings suggest a new potential way in which UFH could help reduce septic injury by positively affecting the Ang-1/Ang-2 ratio to prevent vascular leakage and support the integrity of the endothelial barrier. This action of UFH is likely to play a role in its protective effect in severe sepsis [23]. Our findings showed that UFH significantly decreased ANG-2 rate in septic rats, but Enoxaparin also reduced ANG-2 levels to some extent but was not as effective as heparin.

Recently, Ebeyer-Masotta et al. demonstrated that when HMGB1 interacts with heparin, it undergoes a conformational change and reduces its affinity for its main receptor, RAGE [24]. Khin-Mar Myint et al. reported for the first time that low–molecular weight heparin (LMWH) had an antagonistic action on RAGE and therapeutic effects in murine diabetic nephropathy [25]. Our results showed that UFH significantly decreased the RAGE rate in septic rats, while enoxaparin reduced the level of RAGE to some extent.

Also in 2017, Kasirzadeh et al. demonstrated that β-Sitosterol alters the inflammatory response in CLP rat model of sepsis by modulation of NFκB signaling. In their research, Serum levels of IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, AST, ALT, and liver glutathione (GSH) and Liver expression levels of TNF-α and NF-κBi mRNAs were assessed [26].

Moreover in a research conducted by Aliomrani et al. CLP model was employed in rats to investigate anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects of phloretin and its protective effect on liver tissue damage caused by sepsis [27].

Liu et al. explored the role and value of heparin-binding protein (HBP) in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF). Their study indicated that both BALF and plasma HBP levels were significantly elevated in lung injury of both animal models and human ARDS patients. Moreover, they showed that there is strong correlation between BALF HBP levels and the severity of lung injury. Hence, they suggested that BALF HBP could serve as a valuable biomarker for diagnosis and treatment of ARDS [28].

According to Cosgrave et al. nebulised unfractionated heparin reduces pulmonary microvascular thrombosis, blocks SARS-CoV-2 entry into cells, and decreases lung inflammation and therefore may weaken COVID-19 ARDS [29]. This shows the importance of UFH.

For the past few years, there have been many controversies regarding the best choices of pharmacological anticoagulation in patients with sepsis and cardiovascular diseases subject to thrombotic events. Although Low Molecular Weight Heparin (LMWH) is fourty times more expensive, the antidote for LMWH reversal is not available in Iran, and drug assay to plasma concentration and activated form of factor ten for optimization and individualization is unavailable in most local and national institutions. Unfractionated heparin is less expensive, and easy to monitor. The safety profile is excellent while reversal is quickly and easily available in every hospital around the country. While heparin exert comparable therapeutic benefits, easier means of monitoring and reversals. Recent findings suggest that heparins possess endothelial-stabilizing and anti-inflammatory properties, showing considerable promise in managing severe pathological conditions characterized by systemic inflammation, impaired fibrinolysis, and endothelial dysfunction. These factors are central contributors to thrombotic microangiopathy and coagulation disorders, such as those observed in sepsis.

The main purpose of this study is to evaluate heparin and enoxaparin effectiveness in a rat model of sepsis and to find the best anticoagulant for the treatment of sepsis and evaluated metabolic dysfunction in sepsis. This study can provide information regarding effectiveness of UFH and LMWH. Both are FDA approved.

Metabolomic is a new field that simultaneously analyzes a large number of biological compounds to identify clinically relevant biomarkers and their therapeutic targets. Given the advances in pharmacology and toxicology of lipid mediators, identifying specific targets could lead to new treatment strategies for sepsis and ARDS. We need further studies to identify SPM and pro inflammatory lipids and their association with protein biomarkers.

Conclusion

The findings from our study in comparing the pharmacological benefits of UFH with enoxaparin shows the effect of UFH had descent superiority complemented by its anti-inflammatory and positive protective metabolic profiles beyond anticoagulation, representing improved prognostication in ARDS. It also shows that unfractionated heparin (UFH) has the potential as an anti-inflammatory support during persistent systemic inflammation in sepsis. ARDS is usual in sepsis patients and relared with poor prognosis. Our study shows that UFH can modulate ARDS by suppressing archidonic metabolites following acute systemic inflammatory response in sepsis. It facilitates the identification of potential biological theragnostic and lipidomic for more precise treatment which could lead to improved therapeutic strategies for acute systemic inflammation and ARDS. Additionally, this study considers the cost effectiveness of both drugs and suggests using UFH as a inexpensive but effective drug.
