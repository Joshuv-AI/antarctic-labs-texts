Comparative evaluation of compressive strength of CAD-CAM polyetheretherketone and indirect composite Class II inlays: an in vitro study
Authors: Nidhi Sharma, Siddhant Mahajan, Ish Kumar Sharma, Neha Verma, Diksha Yadav, Nikita Poonia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Polyetheretherketone (PEEK) is a high-performance thermoplastic polymer that is currently being utilized in the dental field due to its desirable mechanical characteristics, biocompatibility, and ease of integration into digital CAD-CAM processes. Despite the esthetic and esthetic benefits of composite resins in Class II inlays, mechanical performance in high-stress posterior restorations continues to be a clinical issue with the material. Although other research has been conducted to utilize PEEK in different dental treatment procedures, there are no direct comparative studies dealing specifically with the compressive strength of Class II dental restorations using CAD-CAM developed PEEK inlays as compared to indirect composite inlays. This study aimed to compare the compressive strength of PEEK and composite resin when used as Class II inlays and evaluate PEEK’s suitability as an alternative restorative material. Thirty-four human premolars extracted for orthodontic reasons and randomly allocated to two groups (n = 17). Group A was provided with CAD-CAM-produced PEEK inlays, and Group B was restored with inlays made of an indirect composite. Fracture load testing of all specimens was done through a universal testing machine. An independent t -test was used to statistically analyze the compressive strength values. The PEEK group showed a mean compressive strength value of 381.88 N compared to 266.67 N in the composite group, and the p-value was below 0.001, which was a statistically significant difference. PEEK inlays proved to have better compressive strength than composite resin inlays, which implies that PEEK is a prospective Class II restorative material in stress-bearing posterior areas. Its clinical performance over the long term and in more applications should be the subject of future research.

Introduction

Polyetheretherketone (PEEK) is a high-performance thermoplastic polymer that is semi-crystalline and is made out of linear chains of polycyclic aromatic rings (Cogswell 2013). PEEK belongs to the polyaryletherketone (PAEK) family of polymers, and its commercial development as an industrial product began in the 1980 s and has since caught the interest of various factors, such as chemical stability, mechanical strength, and thermodynamic properties. It is normally made through pressure and heat casting, along with the CAD-CAM technology and eco-friendly activities like recycling of the wax waste (Lawson and Burgess 2016). It is biocompatible and physiologically inert, and also quite resistant to hydrolysis, ionizing radiation, chemical degradation, and high temperatures. Other desirable properties of PEEK in medical use include elastic modulus (3–4 GPa) that is comparable to the cortical bone of the human body, low density (1.23 g/cm3), and radiolucelency (Verma et al. 2022).

Recent advancements have accelerated the adoption of PEEK in dental applications, with studies highlighting its favorable long-term performance and “adaptability for digital workflows (Alqutaibi et al. 2025; Taymour et al. 2024). In dentistry, PEEK is becoming more common in the fabrication of crowns, fixed dental prostheses (FDPs), removable partial dentures (RPDs), posts, orthodontic wires, dental implants, and abutments (Akay and Ersöz 2020). Clinicians prioritize both the biological compatibility and the mechanical reliability of materials when fabricating dental appliances, whereas patients increasingly consider esthetics (tooth color) when selecting restorative materials—especially for posterior restorations (Bezgin et al. 2015). Composite resins have a favorable esthetic appeal and adaptability and are frequently combined with PEEK for the manufacture of dimensional restoration (i.e., inlays/onlays) that will meet functional and aesthetic requirements. The stability of PEEK is attributed to its molecular structure, composed of ester groups, a keto group, and repeating aromatic rings, which together provide superior thermal and dimensional resistance (Shukla et al. 2012).

Furthermore, PEEK also retains its stability during conventional sterilization processes owing to its PAEK backbone and high thermal resistance (Maloo et al. 2022). PEEK also shows promising results when reinforced with carbon or glass fibers, broadening its use in both orthopedic and dental areas. An in-vitro study reported promising mechanical performance of PEEK inlays under simulated chewing, comparable to or exceeding direct composite restorations (Prechtel et al. 2020), which supports the notion that PEEK has potential in stress-bearing applications. Likewise, a randomized clinical trial reported that PEEK intracoronal restorations were a suitable alternative to indirect composite in posterior teeth, showing encouraging short-term clinical performance (Josic et al. 2023).

Despite the growing body of literature on the application of PEEK in dentistry, most available studies have focused on its use in crowns, frameworks, implant components, or reinforced restorations, and comparatively little attention has been given to its immediate mechanical comparability with standardized Class II cavity designs with conventional indirect composite inlays. Moreover, the current studies tend to evaluate the fracture resistance under heterogeneous experimental settings, and it is therefore difficult to directly translate them to clinical settings. No controlled in vitro studies comparing the compressive properties of CAD-CAM fabricated PEEK inlays and indirect composite resin inlays under the same loading conditions, particularly under stress-bearing posterior restorations, have been done. Thus, the current research was supposed to fill this research gap by performing a systematic comparative analysis on the compressive strength of the PEEK and composite materials as Class II inlays. The proposed study will contribute to evidence-based material choice in the case of posterior restorations and will help comprehend the possible role of PEEK as a long-lasting substitute for traditional composite inlays.

Materials and methods

Study design

A comparative experimental study was an in vitro study aimed at assessing and comparing the compressive strength of Polyetheretherketone (PEEK) and Composite Resin as a restorative material (Class II inlay) on extracted human premolar teeth. Extracted teeth were collected following informed patient consent for research use, in accordance with institutional ethical guidelines.

Study area

The experimental procedures were carried out at an Analytical Laboratory in Noida, Uttar Pradesh, India. Extracted human premolar teeth used in this study were obtained from the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery, Inderprastha Dental College and Hospital, Ghaziabad, Uttar Pradesh, India.

Sample size

The G*Power software (version 3.1.9.6; Franz Faul, University of Kiel, Germany) was used to compute the sample size assuming the study power of 95 percent and confidence interval of 95 percent. According to this estimate, 34 intact human premolar teeth were sampled and randomly divided into two equal groups (n = 17 each).

Method of data collection

The number of extracted maxillary premolars that were collected based on orthodontic reasons was 34. The teeth were immediately extracted, cleaned with pumice to loosen the debris on the surface, disinfected, and placed in distilled water in an incubator at 37 °C to replicate intraoral conditions. To maintain the integrity of teeth during the entire study, the storage medium was periodically changed. Extracted teeth were collected after obtaining informed patient consent, in accordance with institutional ethical guidelines.

Inclusion & exclusion objectives

Inclusion Criteria: Extracted maxillary premolars only, with intact crowns and sound buccal and proximal enamel surfaces, and without any prior chemical or restorative treatment, were included to ensure anatomical and structural uniformity.

Exclusion Criteria: Teeth exhibiting caries, cracks, fractures, attrition, abrasion, erosion, developmental anomalies, hypoplasia, or any history of endodontic or restorative procedures were excluded from the study.

Detailed methodology

Sample preparation and cavity standardization

Preparation of 34 premolars to Class II inlay cavities was done by only one experienced operator in order to reduce operator variability. A high-speed handpiece with a new flat-end tapered diamond bur (ISO standard) was used to make standardized cavity preparations under constant water coolant. A positioning jig made of acrylic was custom-designed so that all samples had similar-sized cavity dimensions. The Class II cavities were made in the following meticulous measurements: an occlusal isthmus width of one-third of intercuspal distance, pulpal floor 2 mm, axial wall 1.5 mm, and a rounded internal line angle. The bur was changed after a preparation of five to ensure that cutting was efficient and consistent.

Fabrication of inlays

PEEK Inlays (Group A):

Digital scanning of all cavity preparations was accomplished, and PEEK inlays were designed and produced in accordance with a CAD-CAM workflow. Core I Tec 350 I milling machine (Imes-Icore, Germany) was used to mill by applying PEEK discs (Tanna Engineering Enterprises, Mumbai) (Fig. 1). Indirect Composite Inlays (Group B):

In the case of the composite group, inlays were made by a traditional indirect method, since indirect composite restorations are commonly made by impression and curing methods in contesting practice using a laboratory. Impressions of the putty were made, and hollows were filled with Z350 composite resin (3 M) after which they were allowed to polymerize with the help of an indirect light-curing unit (Sibari SR620) (Figs. 2 and 3). The clinically accepted workflow of this method to compare the materials under common fabrication protocols provides a realistic comparison of the materials used in indirect composite inlays. The application of the various fabrication workflows was deliberate and clinically supported since all the materials were subjected to their most prevalent and clinically applicable technique. Inlay surface treatment:

PEEK inlays (Group A): The internal surfaces were airborne-particle abraded using 50-µm aluminum oxide particles at 2 bar pressure, cleaned with oil-free air, and conditioned as recommended for polymer-based restorations.

Indirect composite inlays (Group B): The internal surfaces were sandblasted using 50-µm aluminum oxide, cleaned, and treated with the same adhesive system to ensure standardized bonding conditions.

Tooth surface treatment and Cementation:

The prepared enamel and dentin surfaces were cleaned with pumice, thoroughly rinsed, and gently air-dried. A 37% phosphoric acid etchant was applied selectively to the enamel surfaces for 15 s (selective etch technique). The etched surfaces were then rinsed with water and gently air-dried, leaving the dentin surface visibly moist. Syntac Primer (Ivoclar Vivadent) was applied to the dentin using a microbrush and allowed to act for 15 s, after which excess material was gently dispersed with air and dried thoroughly. This was followed by application of Syntac Adhesive (Ivoclar Vivadent), which was left in place for 10 s and then air-dried thoroughly using an air syringe.

Subsequently, a thin layer of Heliobond Adhesive (Ivoclar Vivadent) was applied over the adhesive layer and polymerized in conjunction with the dual-cure resin luting cement (Variolink Aesthetic, Ivoclar Vivadent) for 10 s, in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

Fracture load testing

These specimens were placed in a specially designed holder and tested under compressive load through a universal testing machine (UTM). To provide a uniform distribution of stress, a 0.5-mm sheet of tin foil (DENTAURUM, Germany) was put between the occlusal surface and a 6-mm hemispherical metal indenter. The loading was done vertically at a crosshead rate of 1 mm/min until fracture. Each specimen was measured in terms of fracture load in Newtons.

Statistical analysis

IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used to perform statistical analysis. The mean compressive strength and standard deviation of the two groups were obtained. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to determine data normality, and since the data were normally distributed, an independent samples t-test was used to test the groups. A p-value that was less than 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

The Institutional Review Board (IRB) provided ethical permission. IRB No.-IPDC/SS/2023/10189(3).

Results

The current study compared the compressive strength of CAD-CAM-manufactured PEEK inlays and indirect composite resin inlays in the case of Class II restorations. Each specimen was measured in terms of fracture load in a universal testing machine. Compressive strength values were analyzed only; no fracture mode analysis was taken because the study was aimed at quantitatively comparing the load-bearing capacity.

Data normality assessment

The Shapiro–Wilk test was conducted to test the normality of the data before conducting an inferential statistical analysis. The normal distribution was shown in the data of compressive strength of both samples (PEEK: p = 0.412; Composite: p = 0.368; p > 0.05), and this was the reason to use parametric tests.

The compressive strength of PEEK inlays was between 347.36 N and 405.61 N, with an average of 381.88 N, meaning that this material has solid and consistent compressive strength when under compressive loading. Composite inlays, on the other hand, showed a compressive strength level of between 242.03 N and 286.22 N, with the mean value being 266.67 N. Discussion

The current in vitro study compared and evaluated the compressive strength of Polyetheretherketone (PEEK) and Indirect composite resin as Class II inlay restorations. The findings proved that PEEK inlays were much stronger than composite inlays in compressive strength (381.88 N vs. 266.67 N; p < 0.001). This observation suggests that in controlled laboratory conditions, PEEK has a higher ability to sustain compressive loading compared to conventional indirect composite resin when made into an inlay restoration.

The high compressive strength that was demonstrated by PEEK can be explained by the semi-crystalline polymer structure and the desirable intrinsic mechanical properties. PEEK has a good strength-to-weight ratio, dimensional stability, and can withstand deformation in the presence of any loads. The results of the current research are supported by the previous ones on the topic, as Liebermann et al. (2016) and Kurtz and Devine (2007) have already reported that PEEK displays improved mechanical resistance than a range of traditional dental polymers. These properties are especially applicable when the materials are exposed to compressive forces in the axial direction, as in the way the present experiment was modeled.

The other reason that might be given to explain the high compressive strength of PEEK can be the elastic modulus of this material, which is more similar to that of the human cortical bone compared with most of the traditional restorative materials. Bathala et al. (2019) proposed that this modulus similarity can permit more advantageous load distribution of the stress, and thus the stress concentration on local points when compressing. It should be noted, though, that the current research tested the compressive strength in the static mode only and thus no conclusions about the distribution of stress in the dynamic or cyclic loading conditions could be made.

Conversely, the values of compressive strength were much lower in indirect composite resin inlays. Despite the popularity of composite resins in restorative dentistry and the fact that they do have certain benefits in terms of handling and composition, the polymeric matrix and filler composition might prevent their resistance to high compressive forces. The comparatively smaller range and smaller standard deviation encountered in the composite group indicate steady behavior of materials, although the absolute values of the material strengths were significantly lower than those achieved on PEEK. Such results are consistent with previous reports that show that composite restorations could be more prone to mechanical constraints when subjected to elevated load conditions (Coelho-de-Souza et al. 2008).

It is necessary to explain that the current research did not compare other clinically relevant parameters like fracture mode, fatigue resistance, wear behavior, marginal integrity, esthetics, or surface degradation. Also, no aging, thermal cycling, or cyclic fatigue loading procedure was introduced in the experiment design. Consequently, this has made the discussion of these properties be deliberately omitted in order to prevent overinterpretation of findings. The conclusions to be made out of this study are thus restricted to the compressive strength results obtained in the conditions of the static loading.

An in-vitro design of the research is a limitation of its own in trying to generalize the findings to clinical performance. One limitation of the present in vitro study is the absence of a separate untreated control group, such as intact teeth without restorations. The inclusion of a control group could have provided additional baseline data for comparing the absolute fracture resistance of restored versus non-restored teeth. However, the primary objective of this study was to perform a direct material-to-material comparison under standardized cavity preparation and loading conditions. Although the absence of a control group does not affect the comparative analysis between the tested materials, it limits interpretation of the absolute fracture resistance relative to intact teeth. Restorative materials are subjected to complex and changing conditions in the oral environment, such as temperature changes, moisture, enzymatic activity, and multidirectional occlusiveness. These can have a considerable impact on the behavior of the material with time and cannot be completely imitated in a laboratory. In turn, although the current findings offer controlled mechanical data in favor of the superior compressive strength of PEEK, they do not provide direct prognoses of long-term clinical efficacy. The different fabrication workflows used for each material may have influenced adaptation accuracy and polymerization characteristics, which should be considered when interpreting the mechanical outcomes.

Notwithstanding those drawbacks, the research provides useful comparative mechanical information as it analyzes inlays of PEEK and composite inlays in the case of standardized cavity preparation and evaluating tests. The results indicate the possibility of using PEEK as a material with high compressive loading resistance as compared to the indirect composite resin. The study, however, requires additional studies that will incorporate fatigue tests, thermal aging, and long-term clinical analysis to fully examine the performance of PEEK in restorative dentistry and to highlight its applicability beyond the mechanical strength factor.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this in vitro study, polyetheretherketone (PEEK) inlays demonstrated significantly higher compressive strength than indirect composite resin inlays when tested under static loading conditions. These findings indicate that PEEK exhibits superior resistance to compressive forces in a controlled laboratory setting. Only compressive strength values were analyzed; fracture mode evaluation was not performed as the study focused exclusively on quantitative load-bearing capacity. Further in vitro studies incorporating aging protocols and fatigue testing, as well as well-designed clinical trials, are required to comprehensively assess the clinical applicability of PEEK as a Class II inlay material.
