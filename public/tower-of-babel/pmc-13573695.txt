Beyond surface scraping to understand fluvial biofilms: an electron microscopy analysis of the rock–biofilm interface in Antarctic meltwater streams
Authors: Rebeca Arias-Real, Asunción de los Ríos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFluvial biofilms develop through the establishment of microbial communities on submerged mineral surfaces. However, they are routinely studied after detachment from their colonized substrate by scraping or brushing, a standard procedure that homogenizes biofilm architecture and disrupts the biofilm–rock interface. Using an in situ microscopy approach that examines biofilms without separating them from their colonized substrate, we studied undisturbed biofilm-covered rocks collected from Antarctic meltwater streams. Our observations revealed: (i) a highly organized three-dimensional architecture characterized by spatially structured microbial assemblages embedded in a matrix of extracellular polymeric substances; (ii) a close association among microbial cells, the colonized rock surface and fine-grained sedimentary components; (iii) the development of an endolithic biofilm fraction through the colonization of internal rock fissures and cavities; and (iv) a frequent laminated structure with alternating layers rich in microbial cells and layers composed mainly of sediment particles, which may preserve a physical signatures of hydrological variability and sediment deposition. Together, these observations challenge the widespread view of fluvial biofilms as uniform microbial layers developing on inert mineral surfaces. Integrating in situ microscopy approaches with conventional destructive approaches offers new opportunities to link microbial diversity with its spatial organization, providing a more complete understanding of fluvial biofilm ecology and microbial–mineral interactions.Keywords: Antarctic meltwater streams, Biofilm structure, Endolithic colonization, Hydrological archives, Microbial–mineral interactions, Rock–biofilm interface, Spatial organization

Introduction

Fluvial biofilms are one of the dominant forms of microbial life in river ecosystems [1]. These complex communities, composed mainly of bacteria, algae, fungi and protozoa embedded within a self-produced matrix of extracellular polymeric substances (EPS), colonize submerged surfaces ranging from rocks and sediments to wood, leaf litter, macrophytes and even microplastics [[2], [3], [4]]. Through primary production, respiration, organic matter decomposition, nutrient cycling and contaminant transformation, biofilms play a central role in ecosystem functioning and serve as sensitive indicators of environmental change (Besemer et al., 2011; [[5], [6], [7]]). Consequently, fluvial biofilms have become model systems for studying microbial ecology, ecosystem functioning and the impacts of global change in river ecosystems.

Over the last two decades, advances in molecular biology have transformed our understanding of these communities [[8], [9], [10]]. High-throughput sequencing approaches, including metabarcoding and metagenomics, now enable the characterization of microbial diversity, community composition and functional potential at unprecedented resolution across environmental gradients worldwide [[11], [12], [13], [14]]. At the same time, microscopy-based techniques have been extensively applied to quantify microbial cells, detect EPS production and estimate biofilm thickness and surface coverage [[15], [16], [17]]. Together, these approaches have considerably improved our understanding of which microorganisms are present and the ecological processes they mediate. Despite these methodological advances, one fundamental aspect of fluvial biofilm ecology has received comparatively little attention: the preservation of the original rock–biofilm interface, in which microorganisms, EPS and the underlying mineral substrate remain physically connected as a single ecological unit.

This preserved biofilm–rock interface represents the physical and ecological context in which biofilm communities develop. Rather than simple microbial assemblages attached to inert surfaces, lithic biofilms are highly organized three-dimensional systems whose structure emerges from the interrelationships among microorganisms, EPS and both biogenic and substrate-derived minerals [18]. However, regardless of the downstream analytical technique employed (i.e., molecular biology or microscopy-based techniques), most studies begin by removing the biofilm from the colonized substrate, typically by scraping or brushing submerged rocks [[19], [20], [21], [22]]. The collected material is subsequently homogenized prior to analyses [[23], [24], [25]]. This workflow has become the standard because it is logistically practical, analytically powerful and facilitates comparisons across sites and treatments. However, this destructive sampling inevitably disrupts the original spatial organization of microbial cells and EPS, causing the biofilm architecture to collapse into a homogenized suspension while simultaneously disrupting the rock-biofilm interface and the physical interactions between microorganisms and the mineral substrate [18,26]. Consequently, analyses performed on homogenized material provide valuable information on microbial diversity and function but cannot resolve how microorganisms are spatially organized, interact between them and with mineral surfaces or occupy distinct ecological niches within natural biofilms. Likewise, microorganisms tightly attached to the substrate or inhabiting endolithic microhabitats may be incompletely recovered during scraping, potentially biasing the representation of some structural components of natural biofilm communities. The application of in situ microscopy approach [27], which integrates several microscopy techniques without separating the biological component from its natural substrate, provides an opportunity to investigate biofilms while preserving this original spatial organization.

Here, we applied the in situ microscopy approach to examine fluvial biofilms colonizing rocks from Antarctic meltwater streams using complementary field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) and low-temperature field emission scanning electron microscopy (LT-FESEM) approaches. By analyzing the rock-biofilm interface, we identify structural features and potential geomicrobiological interactions that may be overlooked when destructive sampling approaches are used. Finally, we discuss how integrating this approach with complementary techniques, such as molecular biology, can provide a more comprehensive understanding of fluvial biofilm organization, microbial–mineral interactions and community assembly.

Methodology

Study area and sampling

Rock samples showing fluvial biofilms were collected during February 2024 from four Antarctic meltwater streams located near the Spanish Antarctic Station Juan Carlos I on Livingston Island (South Shetland Islands, Antarctica; Fig. 1). At each stream, ten rocks with presence of visible biofilms were collected (40 rocks in total). Immediately after collection, the rocks were transported to the laboratory at the Spanish Antarctic Station Juan Carlos I, where they were examined under a stereomicroscope to assess biofilm presence and preservation. Based on these observations, five rocks per stream (20 rocks in total) showing well-developed, undisturbed biofilms, were selected for electron microscopy analyses. Selected rocks were individually placed in rigid plastic containers to minimize mechanical disturbance during transport and subsequently stored at −20 °C until laboratory processing.

In situ microscopy study

Prior to electron microscopy preparation, selected biofilm-covered rock fragments were examined under a light microscope to document their general morphology and to support the interpretation of the electron microscopy observations.

Field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM)

Frozen samples were slowly thawed and rehydrated with Milli-Q water. Following the in situ microscopy approach (de los Rios & Ascaso, 2005), small rock fragments of approximately 2 × 2 cm in size were carefully obtained using a chisel, ensuring preservation of well-developed biofilms and the underlying rock–biofilm interface. These fragments were processed without separating the biofilm from the rock surface for scanning electron microscopy in back-scattered electron mode, according to the procedures described by Wierzchos and Ascaso [28]. Briefly, these rock fragments showing fluvial biofilms were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde followed by 1% osmium tetroxide, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series and embedded in LR White resin. Resin blocks were finely polished using successively grinding papers and silicon carbide abrasive sheets of decreasing grain size, followed by a liquid diamond polishing compound applied to napped cloths with an oil-basic lubricating fluid, to expose the undisturbed biofilm–rock interface in cross-section [29,30]. The polished blocks were then carbon-coated and examined in backscattered electron (BSE) mode using an Apreo 2S field emission scanning electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Germany).

Low-temperature field emission scanning electron microscopy (LT-FESEM)

Both intact rock fragments bearing fluvial biofilms and rock fragments subjected to experimental biofilm removal by gentle scraping with a sterile scalpel were prepared following the same cryofixation protocol for LT-FESEM. Briefly, both types of small rock fragments were rehydrated with Milli-Q water, excess surface moisture was carefully removed, and the samples were mounted on the specimen holder of a cryotransfer system (Quorum PP3010). Then, samples were immediately cryofixed by plunging into subcooled liquid nitrogen and transferred under vacuum to the preparation chamber. Frozen samples were fractured by impact using a dedicated fracturing device within the preparation chamber, etched for 2 min at −90 °C, sputter-coated with platinum and examined using the same Apreo 2S field emission scanning electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Germany).

Results

Spatial structure and organization

Biofilm ranged from thin, green, laminar biofilms to thicker, brownish or greenish biofilms with a more spongy appearance (Fig. 2). This macroscopic variability occurred both within and among streams, with no consistent site-specific pattern. Despite this morphological variability, all biofilms shared common features of spatial organization. Using the in situ microscopy approach combining FESEM and LT-FESEM, fluvial biofilms were observed as highly organized three-dimensional structures in which morphotypes consistent with cyanobacteria, diatoms and green algae were embedded within a common EPS matrix while exhibiting different spatial distributions (Fig. 3A–D, F–G). The EPS matrix was particularly well visualized by LT-FESEM (Fig. 3A–D), which enables us to analyze hydrated biofilms and thereby visualize their three-dimensional organization. Lichen thallus were also observed on the rock surface but remained structurally independent from the fluvial biofilm itself (Fig. 3E). Across the analyzed samples, a basal layer dominated by coccoid and filamentous cyanobacteria was consistently observed in direct contact with the rock surface (between black arrows in Fig. 3F and G), later overlain by filamentous morphotypes (Fig. 3A and F) and, in some cases, by diatoms (Fig. 3B and G). Bacterial-like colonies were commonly observed within the biofilm matrix, particularly associated with cyanobacterial and algal filamentous morphotypes (noted by small white arrows in Fig. 3F and G). Filamentous morphotypes formed interconnected networks throughout the biofilm (Fig. 3A–C), while small mineral fragments were frequently entrapped within the biofilm matrix (Fig. 3F).

Biofilm–rock interface and endolithic colonization

Microscopy observations revealed an intimate association between the biofilm and the underlying lithic substrate (Fig. 3H and I; Fig. 4A–D) with the EPS forming a continuous matrix that connected microbial cells with the mineral surface. Additionally, microbial cells morphologically resembling those of the epilithic fraction of the biofilm were frequently observed within internal fissures, pores and cavities of the rock substrate (Fig. 4C; Fig. 4E–H), indicating the presence of an endolithic biofilm fraction. Bacteria colonies, likely including cyanobacteria and heterotrophic bacteria, dominated this endolithic compartment (Fig. 4E–H). In several analyzed biofilms, microbial cells were closely associated with mineral fragments compositionally similar to the surrounding substrate (Fig. 4E–H; Fig. 5A).

LT-FESEM observations of experimentally scraped rocks showed that, although scraping removed most of the overlying biofilm, microbial cells and abundant EPS remained tightly attached to the rock surface (Fig. 3H and I).

Multilayered biofilm architecture

In some areas of the biofilm, microbial cells, particularly filamentous forms, were surrounded by fine sediment particles (Fig. 5B and C), which may result from biomineralization processes [31,32]. In several rocks, the biofilms exhibited a multilayered architecture composed of alternating microbial-rich and sediment-rich layers (Fig. 5D–F). Sediment-rich layers contained abundant mineral particles, rock fragments and empty diatom frustules (Fig. 5C), whereas adjacent microbial-rich layers were dominated by filamentous microorganisms embedded within the EPS matrix (Fig. 5D–F). Filamentous microbial cells were frequently observed extending across sediment-rich layers, connecting adjacent microbial layers (Fig. 5G).

Discussion

Although the biofilms found in different Antarctic meltwater streams differed markedly in their visual appearance, analysis of the rock–biofilm interface using the in situ microscopy approach revealed that they consistently exhibited a similar three-dimensional organization, with biological and mineral components intricately associated within a common EPS matrix and closely attached to the rock surface. In addition, an endolithic fraction was observed in some of the analyzed biofilms consisting on an extension of the biofilm into internal cavities and fissures of the underlying rock. These findings highlight the importance of avoiding scraping or brushing before the analysis of fluvial biofilms, as these destructive procedures may overlook key structural features of the biofilm and its interactions with the underlying rock substrate.

Beyond community composition

Destructive sampling does not merely eliminate the three-dimensional organization of biofilms; it may also bias our understanding of community composition itself [33,34]. Our observations of experimentally scraped rocks indicate that conventional scraping does not completely remove the biofilm–rock association. Instead, much of the overlying biofilm is detached, while microbial cells and EPS remain tightly attached to the rock surface. Consequently, analyses based exclusively on scraped material are likely to recover only a subset of the original biofilm community, while also disrupting its three-dimensional organization. Microorganisms occupying endolithic habitats, together with epilithic microorganisms tightly attached through dense EPS networks, are likely to be incompletely recovered during scraping and therefore underrepresented in downstream molecular analysis [18,30]. Conversely, detached biofilm material may overrepresent microorganisms associated with superficial layers or transient taxa. Without direct visualization of the spatial structure of the biofilms, transiently associated microorganisms may even be interpreted as stable structural members of the biofilm community [35]. Consequently, diversity analysis based on scraped samples may not provide representative snapshots of natural biofilm communities.

Our observations highlight the potential value of integrating structural and community composition analyses in future ecological studies [36,37]. Spatial organization provides the physical framework in which microorganisms interact and establish physicochemical gradients and specific microenvironments that are known to shape microbial establishment, persistence and ecological interactions [18,[38], [39], [40]]. Moreover, the repeated vertical organization observed across multiple samples, with a basal layer of coccoid and filamentous cyanobacteria directly attached to the rock surface and overlying layers dominated by other morphotypes, is compatible with a sequential colonization process [35,41,42]. However, static microscopy observations alone cannot unequivocally demonstrate succession, as similar spatial arrangements could also arise through post-colonization reorganization, differential growth among taxa, detachment and reattachment dynamics, or physiological stratification within mature biofilms [36,37]. Nevertheless, the recurrent organization observed across independent samples suggests that community assembly is not random [18,43]. In this sense, biofilm architecture may represent both an ecological product and an ecological driver, and the observed coupling between basal colonizers and overlying microbial layers suggests that early colonization may establish the structural template upon which subsequent biofilm development occurs.

Endolithic colonization: an overlooked component of fluvial biofilms

Among the pivotal components most likely to be overlooked by conventional sampling are microorganisms occupying protected niches within the rock substrate. Our observations revealed microbial cells located in internal pores, fissures and cavities of the underlaying rock, suggesting that some components of fluvial biofilms exploit an endolithic lithobiontic niche [44,45]. Although endolithic microorganisms have been extensively described in terrestrial lithobiontic communities [18,32,43], endolithic components have rarely been reported in fluvial biofilms. The endolithic compartment observed here was likely dominated by cyanobacteria together with likely heterotrophic bacteria and appearing to represent a direct extension of the surface biofilm into pores and fissures of the underlying rock [46,47]. Together with the microorganisms closely attached to the rock surface, these endolithic communities formed intimate associations with mineral particles, resembling those described in terrestrial lithobiontic communities and suggesting potential geomicrobiological interactions [26,48]. Beyond expanding our understanding of the structural organization of fluvial biofilms, the existence of this hidden compartment also has important ecological and methodological implications. In Antarctic meltwater streams, where microbial communities experience repeated freezing, desiccation, intense radiation and prolonged darkness, the endolithic niche may additionally provide a protected refuge contributing to long-term community persistence [49]. Future in situ microscopy studies integrating spatially resolved molecular analyses will help determine the taxonomic identity, metabolic activity and ecological role of these endolithic microorganisms [26,29].

Organo–mineral structures as signatures of geomicrobiological and hydrological processes

Beyond revealing spatial structure, preserving the rock–biofilm interface also highlight the interactions between microorganisms and mineral components in both spatial and temporal contexts. Fine mineral particles were observed closely associated with microbial cells and, in some cases, forming distinct sediment-rich layers interspersed with microbial-rich layers. Similar organo–sedimentary organizations have previously been described in microbial mats [32], suggesting that comparable sedimentary processes may also operate during the development of fluvial biofilms. One possible interpretation is that these multilayered structures reflect successive phases of biofilm development associated with hydrological fluctuations. During periods of flow, biofilms may actively develop before becoming buried by fine sediments during episodes of freezing, drying or sediment deposition [4,49,50]. Once hydrological connectivity resumes, persistent filamentous microorganisms may facilitate recolonization of these buried layers, producing the alternating microbial-rich and sediment-rich organization observed here. Although this hypothesis requires temporal validation, it raises the possibility that intact rock–biofilm associations preserve structural signatures consistent with repeated hydrological disturbance.

The close relationships established between microorganisms and minerals also suggests that the biofilm–rock interface may represent a geomicrobially active zone. Microbial processes, including EPS production, organic acid release, and other metabolic activities, may contribute to rock weathering and nutrient mobilization directly at the rock surface [22,29,51]. In the nutrient-poor waters of Antarctic meltwater streams, such localized bioweathering may constitute an important nutrient source contributing to the formation of favorable microhabitats [46,47,52]. Additionally, some of the mineral particles observed within the biofilm may also result from passive sediment trapping rather than active biomineralization or biologically mediated weathering. Distinguishing between these processes will require complementary analyses such as EDX elemental mapping, isotope analyses or time-series experiments.

Towards an integrated structural–diversity approach

Our observations suggest that preserving a subset of intact colonized rocks alongside conventionally scraped samples could substantially improve the ecological interpretation of fluvial biofilms without altering current analytical workflows. By preserving the rock–biofilm continuum, in situ microscopy approach provides the structural context needed to interpret molecular-based analyses of microbial diversity, particularly functional analyses, while guiding targeted sampling of strongly rock-attached, endolithic and sediment-associated microbial components that are likely to be underrepresented following conventional scraping.

Future studies should focus on integrating structural and molecular information on the same or paired samples collected from the same biofilms. Advances in spatial omics, automated image analysis and emerging non-destructive imaging techniques will further strengthen this integrative approach by enabling a more comprehensive and quantitative characterization of biofilm architecture, microbial interactions, and function while minimizing the need for sample preparation procedures that may alter delicate structural features.

Concluding remarks

The fluvial biofilms from Antarctic meltwater streams analyzed in this study are not simply microbial communities developing on inert lithic surfaces; rather, our observations suggest that they should be regarded as spatially structured lithobiontic biofilms. By preserving the original rock–biofilm interface, the in situ microscopy approach used here reveals a previously overlooked dimension of their structural organization, characterized by intricate spatial relationships among biofilm components, an intimate association with the underlying rock and, in some cases, the coexistence of epilithic and endolithic compartments. Although demonstrated here in Antarctic meltwater streams, similar structural complexity is likely to occur in fluvial biofilms worldwide.

The in situ microscopy approach complements existing analytical workflows by providing the structural context required to interpret microbial diversity within its natural spatial framework. While we have become highly efficient at cataloguing which microorganisms are present and inferring their potential functions, comparatively little attention has been paid to how they are spatially organized and interact with one another and with their mineral substrate. As a result, an important dimension of fluvial biofilm ecology may have remained largely overlooked. Integrating structural and molecular approaches therefore offers new opportunities to bridge the long-standing gap between microbial community composition and the spatial organization of fluvial biofilms. The methodological tools already exist; what is now required is their deliberate integration to study fluvial biofilms without first destroying the very structure we aim to understand.
