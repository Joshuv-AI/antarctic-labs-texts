One pot synthesis of N-7 alkylated xanthine derivatives and their exploration as antioxidant, antimicrobial and nematicidal agents
Authors: Amita Saini, Divya Utreja, N. K. Dhillon, Anu Kalia, Rajesh Kumar Pathak, Yadhu Suneja, Harwinder Singh Buttar, Prabhjot Kaur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The widespread presence of economically dominant pathogens has significantly hindered agricultural growth and productivity by affecting the quality and yield of crops. The recent deregistration of essential bactericides and nematicides have underscored the urgency for the development of novel agrochemical candidates. In response, N-7 alkylated xanthine derivatives were synthesized using various alkylbromides and characterized with the help of various spectroscopic techniques. The synthesized derivatives were explored for antioxidant, antibacterial, antifungal and antinemic properties. A comprehensive evaluation of antioxidant potential of synthesized compounds utilizing various models revealed that compound 7-decyl-1H-purine-2,6-(3H,7H)-dione (2j) exhibited strong antioxidant potential due to enhancement of electron-donating characterin reference to ascorbic acid. Furthermore, evaluation of antimicrobial activity against Dickeya sp., Xanthomonas campestris and Fusarium oxysporum, and antinemicactivity against Meloidogyne incognita, demonstrated that compound (2j), among other tested compounds, displayed noteworthy inhibitory activity against these pathogens as compared to reference drugs i.e. ciprofloxacin, gentamycin (antimicrobial activity) and carbofuran (antinemic activity). Also, in silico investigations were performed aiming for acetylcholine esterase enzyme inhibition activities to recognize novel interactions of the tested compounds with target binding sites of Meloidogyne incognita. The promising in vitro results warrant further validation under in vivo and field conditions. This research offers valuable insights that these compounds can serve as key precursors for future development of agrochemical agents for managing biotic stresses and improving crop productivity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48993-3.

Introduction

Agriculture is already a science of growing crops and supplying raw materials for various industries in India1. Nevertheless, a significant challenge in modern agriculture is addressing crop losses caused by adverse biotic stresses such as diseases caused by nematodes, fungi, bacteria and abiotic stresses caused by climate change, weather conditions and reactive oxygen species, which lower crop quality and quantity2,3. These stresses contribute to a rapid decline in agricultural productivity and growth.

Polyphagous root knot nematodes (Meloidogyne spp.) are destructive soil-borne pathogens causing major yield losses in vegetable crops4–6. They induce gall formation in roots via stylet-mediated feeding (Figures S1 (A-B); Supplementary data), disrupting water and nutrient uptake and weakening plant defences, which severely impairs plant physiological functions and increases susceptibility to secondary soil-borne pathogens7.

Plant pathogenic fungi and bacteria cause severe crop losses (approximately 10–35% annually) across globe though the disease severity varies considerably on basis of the crop variety as well as environmental conditions8. During the host-parasite interaction, a host physiological hijacking mechanism is followed to secrete toxins or protein-degrading enzymes into the host plant, the breakdown of structural components like pectin in plant cells or walls and genetic alterations resulting in host death. These are responsible for vascular wilt, galls, leaf spots, specks, scabs, necrosis, soft rot, tumours and cankers in plants9. Pseudomonas, Burkholderia, Erwinia, Pectobacterium, Ralstonia, Pantoea, Agrobacterium, Streptomyces, Acidovorax, Clavibacter, Spiroplasma, Phytoplasma and Xylella are the most prominent disease-causing pathogenic bacteria10. The average crop loss caused bythe plant bacterial diseases accounts for approximately 23.0 million dollars globallysignificantly affecting yield loss. Plant pathogenic fungi are the other group of microorganisms which are responsible for severe crop losses (10–35%) annually.Plants may get infected with these fungal pathogens from a variety of sources such as infected seed, soil, crop debris, nearby crops and weeds. Fusarium spp., Puccinia spp., Phytophtorainfestrans, Rhizoctonia solani, Sporisoriumscitamineum, Ustilago maydis and Colletotrichum spp.are the most prominent disease-causing pathogenic fungi. Considering tomato as the model plant, the two most common and widely studies bacterial diseases are bacterial spot and necrosis of tomato caused by Xanthomonas perforans11Pseudomonas cichorii, respectively(Figures S1 C-D)12, respectively. Likewise, the fungal pathogens i.e. Fusarium oxysporumand Rhizoctonia solanicause Fusarium wilt and damping off diseases in tomato plants (Figure S1 E-F).These phytopathogens secrete a variety of biomolecules phytohormones, toxins, effector proteins and cell wall degrading enzymes responsible for their pathogenicity. These organisms during host-parasite hijack mechanism secrete toxins or protein degrading enzymes from their body to the host body which result in the host death, breakdown of structural components such as pectin of plant cells or walls and genetic modifications8,9.

The production of Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) or electron deficient species such as nitric oxide, hydroxyl, peroxyl, superoxide, alkoxyl, singlet oxygen (1O2), hydroxy peroxide, organic peroxide, hypochlorous acid, ozone, aldehydes and peroxynitrite during numerous physiological and metabolic processes in living cells is an inevitable process13. Though homeostatic balance controls the level of these toxic free radical ions butthe severe rise of radicals causes oxidative stress14 and damage to electron rich centres such as proteins, lipids, DNA and cause mutations at cellular level15,16.

Although plants possess both structural and biochemical defence mechanisms-to counter these stresses, but these are insufficient and lead to appearance of diseases in plants17. To reduce the yield loss, various mechanical and cultural techniques/strategies such as intercropping, crop rotation, fumigation and agrochemicals have been implemented for the elimination of these pathogens18. However, these methods are often deemed less effective and come with a host of additional side effects such as damaging beneficial species, environment pollution and harmful to non-targeted pathogens.Moreover, major nematicides, bactericides and antimicrobial agents developed to overcome the loss caused by bacteria/fungi and nematodeshave been banned due to their adverse effects on human health and soil ecosystems. Also, the rapid evolution and resistance to these techniques and chemicals by these pathogens is a major destructive problem19–21. This underscores the urgent need for the development of safer molecules with low toxicity values to manage these pathogens.

Nitrogen-containing heterocyclic compounds are gaining prominence due to their broad spectrum of biological activities exhibited by them22–26. Among these, xanthine (3,7-dihydropurine-2,6-dione) particularly stands out in traditional medicine. Natural xanthine derivatives like caffeine, theophylline and theobromine are purine-based nitrogenous compounds with proven medicinal properties, utilized in various applications27. These compounds share a structural similarity to purines, the building blocks of ribonucleotides (RNA) and deoxyribonucleotides (DNA). Xanthine and its synthesized derivatives, such as Theo 24, Dilor, Tonapofylline, Dimenhydrinate and Apadenoson, exhibit diverse activities including A2 and A3 receptor agonism, phosphodiesterase inhibition, renal impact, anti-tumor effects and antibacterial properties28. The extensive range of biological activities, coupled with successful clinical applications and structural adaptability, has spurred researchers to delve into xanthine exploration.

Although, diverse acivities of xanthine and its derivatives have been reported but the information regarding in vitro antimicrobial, antinemic, antioxidant activity has not been explored. With abundant possibilities for the design of the xanthine scaffold29, efforts have been directed toward N−7 alkylated xanthine derivatives, investigating their antioxidant, antinemic, antibacterial, and antifungal potential. In-depth in silico studies have been conducted to gain insights into binding sites with pathogenic proteins or specific receptors. To ensure the viability of these molecules for drug design, predictive toxicology assessments like Lazy Structure-Activity Relationship (LAZAR) and Prediction of Rodent Oral Toxicity (ProTox) have been undertaken. Furthermore, detailed biochemical analyses, including acetylcholinesterase inhibitor activity, provide crucial insights into the interaction between these synthesized N−7 alkylated xanthine derivatives and targeted enzymatic proteins present in the pathogen M. incognita.

Materials and methods

This research study was divided into six parts: i) Synthesis of N−7 alkylated derivatives of xanthine ii) Evaluation of synthesized derivatives for antioxidant activity; iii) Evaluation of synthesized derivatives against pathogenic bacteria and fungi as well as its nematicidal efficacy against M. incognita; iv) In silico studies to understand the mode of action; v) Toxicological studies for its fitness to drug likeliness properties in agriculture industry; vi) Acetyl cholinesterase inhibitor activity.

Chemicals and equipment

Xanthine (3,7-dihydropurine-2,6-dione), alkyl halides (2a-j) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Chemical Company with > 99% purity. Other chemicals and solvents such as potassium carbonate (K2CO3), N, N’-dimethyl formamide (DMF), hexane and ethylacetate (EtOAc), were purchased locally and were purified-dried with literature reported methods30. Melting points of synthesized xanthine derivatives were examined using Nutronics digital melting point apparatus, Central Instrumentation Laboratory, Punjab Agricultural University (PAU), Ludhiana in open capillaries and are uncorrected. 1HNMR and 13CNMR spectra were recorded in CDCl3 on a Bruker Avance 500 MHz and 125 MHz spectrometer, respectively at 25 °C, using tetramethylsilane (TMS) as internal standard. FT-IR spectra of the compounds were recorded on a Perkin Elmer Spectrum Two-IR Fourier-Transform spectrophotometer in the range 400–4000 cm− 1 using potassium bromide (KBr) pellets. Elemental analysis was recorded on FLASH EA112 (Thermo Electron Corporation) analyser, Thermo Scientific, US and the results are quoted in % and Mass spectra were recorded on Waters LC-MS Quattro micro™. All these spectral studies were recorded from Sophisticated Analytical Instrumentation Facility (SAIF), Panjab University, Chandigarh. X-Ray crystallography of synthesized derivatives was recorded on Bruker SMART APEX2 detector diffractometer from Indian Institute of Technology (IIT), Rupnagar, Punjab.

General scheme for synthesis of N−7 alkylated xanthine derivatives

Xanthine (1) (5.26 mmol) and potassium carbonate (K2CO3) (5.21 mmol) in the presence of N, N’-dimethylformamide (DMF) (20 mL) were taken in a round bottomed flask (RBF) under the blanket of nitrogen atmosphere and were made to stir at 0 °C. Constant stirring led to the formation of a light-yellow colored anion of xanthine. After the generation of light yellow colored anion, a slow drop wise addition of varied electrophile of alkyl bromides i.e. n-bromomethane to n-bromodecane (0.16 mmol) was made in the reaction flask with the help of a syringe as shown in scheme 1 at the same temperature (0 °C). The reaction was allowed to stir for an hour at the same temperature i.e. 0 °C. After stirring for an hour, the reaction mixture was shifted to room temperature and allowed to stir until the completion of the reaction (TLC, approx. 10 h). After the completion of reaction, it was quenched with saturated brine solution (NaCl) and neutralization of the reaction mixture with 1.0 N hydrochloric acid (HCl) was carried out followed by extractive workup with ethyl acetate (3 × 30 mL). The combined organic extract was dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate (Na2SO4) and removal of solvent under reduced pressure furnished the crude product. Further the recrystallization from ethyl acetate and hexane, offered the pure product in good synthetic yield (70–80%).

Antimicrobial assay

Procurement of plant pathogenic bacterial and fungal cultures

The pure cultures of Xanthomonas campestris and Fusarium oxysporum were procured from Indian Type Culture Collection (ITCC), Department of Plant Pathology ICAR-IARI, New Delhi. While Dickeya sp. was procured from ICAR- Indian Institute of Maize Research, PAU campus, Ludhiana, India.

Preparation of nutrient agar (NA) media and potato dextrose agar (PDA) Media

For the preparation of 500 mL of NA media, protease peptone (2.5 g), NaCl (2.5 g) and beef extract (1.5 g) were dissolved in 100 mL of distilled water in a 500 mL conical flask. The solution was heated to get clear broth solution and final volume of broth was made 500 mL with the help distilled water in a measuring flask. 250 mL of NA broth was poured into two conical flasks followed by addition of agar (5.0 g) in each flask with pH 7.2 23. The cotton plugged, wrapped conical flasks of NA media were sterilized in autoclave at 15 psi (121.6 °C) for 1 h. Similarly, for the preparation of 500 mL of PDA media, the PDA agar (HI Media Laboratories Pvt. Ltd., Mumbai, India; 4.0 g) was dissolved in 100 mL of distilled water in a 500 mL conical flask. The final volume of media was made 500 mL with distilled water with pH 5.6 ± 0.2 in a conical flask. The cotton plugged, wrapped conical flasks of PDA media were sterilized in autoclave at 15 psi (121.6 °C) for 1 h.

Kirby-bauer antibiotic sensitivity method

For antibacterial and antifungal assay, molten NA media and PDA media respectively (15–20 mL) were poured into sterilized petri plates of 90 mm size and was allowed to solidify at room temperature with right side up for 20 to 30 min. The solidified NA media plates were spread with 0.1 mL of tested microbial inoculum type (Xanthomonas campestris and Dickeya sp.) by help of sterilized spreader. The tested compounds were poured in the wells of 8 mm and zone of inhibition was measured (mm) and was compared with standard Gentamycin. Further these compounds were evaluated for their minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) for bacterial strains under study by brothmacrodilution method23,32. Similarly, antifungal activity for the xanthine (1) and its derivative (2a-j) against Fusarium oxysporum was determined by poisoned food technique32. 0.05 mL of each synthesized xanthine (1) and its derivatives (2a-j) were loaded into the well of 7 mm which were prepared by sterilized cork borer. Afterwards, an agar bit of the pure fungal culture from the master culture plate was placed in the middle of the media plate. Further, the prepared plates were kept in an incubator at 28 ± 2 °C and zones of inhibition (mm) were recorded after 72–120 h as compared to Carbendazim which was used as standard.

Nematicidal activity bioassay

Inoculum preparation of Meloidogyne incognita

To evaluate nematicidal efficacy, M. incognita was selected as the test organism owing to its widespread occurrence in vegetable crops. Pure cultures of M. incognita were maintained on tomato plants (cv. Punjab Ratta) using the single-egg-mass technique33 in the pot house of the Department of Plant Pathology, PAU, Ludhiana. After 60 days, infected tomato roots were carefully uprooted and gently washed to remove adhering soil.Egg masses were hand-picked from infected roots using fine forceps and rinsed thoroughly with sterile distilled water and incubated using a modified tray method at 28 ± 2 °C. Freshly hatched second-stage juveniles (J2) collected within 24 h were used for nematicidal assays. Antinemic activity was carried against M. incognita using egg hatch inhibition (ovicidal) and second stage juveniles (J2) mortality (larvicidal) bioassay34.

Egg hatching and mortality test of M. incognita

Stock solutions for xanthine (1) and its derivatives (2a-j) of varying concentrations, such as 50, 100, 250, 500, 750, 1000, 1500 and 2000 ppm, were prepared using the normality equation and distilled water. Sterilized Petri plates of 60 mm diameter were filled with 30 ml of distilled water and each treatment was replicated three times and contained 0.1% Tween 20 as a wetting agent. The concentration of DMSO in each treatment did not exceed 1% v/v. Carbofuran was used as a positive control, while distilled water served as a negativecontrol. For the egg-hatching assay, 10 sterilized egg masses were transferred into each Petri plates containing 30 mL of test solution, sealed with parafilm and incubated at 27 ± 2 °C. The number of hatched juveniles was recorded every 24 h up to 7 days. The activities of the tested compounds were monitored using a binocular microscope at 40X magnification at regular intervals and the number of hatched eggs was recorded. Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted in a completely randomized design with three independent replications. Data obtained from antioxidant, antimicrobial, egg hatch inhibition and second-stage juvenile (J2) mortality bioassays were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Percentage data were subjected to arcsine square-root transformation prior to analysis to ensure normality. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to determine the significance of treatment effects using R-studio software. For concentration-dependent and time-dependent bioassays, one-way ANOVA was applied and treatment means were separated using Least Significant Difference (LSD) test at a 5% level of significance (p ≤ 0.05). ED₅₀ and ED90 values, along with their 95% confidence intervals, were estimated by probit analysis using SPSS software based on pooled mortality or egg hatch inhibition data across replications.

In silico studies

In silico studies were performed for xanthine (1) and its synthesized derivatives (2a-j) against selected target proteins from Meloidogyne incognita. The three-dimensional structures of the ligands (1 and 2a-j) were drawn using MarvinSketch software (https://chemaxon.com/marvin) and saved in PDB format. The 3D structures of the target proteins Cellulase (AF-Q5QJ65-F1-model_v3), β−1,4-endoglucanase (AF-Q9BJZ0-F1-model_v3), and Carboxylic ester hydrolase (AF-Q71JB7-F1-model_v3) were retrieved from the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database (https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/)35. The quality of these downloaded protein structures was assessed and validated via Ramachandran plot analysis using the Structure Analysis and Verification Server (SAVES) (https://saves.mbi.ucla.edu/). Binding site residues were predicted using the CASTp server (http://sts.bioe.uic.edu/castp/calculation.html)36. All ligand and protein structures were prepared using AutoDock Tools and converted into the PDBQT format. The predicted binding site residues were used to define the grid box coordinates for the docking. Molecular docking was then carried out using AutoDock Vina to obtain binding free energy values (https://vina.scripps.edu/)37. The resulting docked poses were visualized and analyzed using UCSF Chimera (https://www.cgl.ucsf.edu/chimera/) and BIOVIA Discovery Studio Visualizer (https://discover.3ds.com/discovery-studio-visualizer-download).

Acetylcholinesterase enzyme bioassay of M. incognita

Acetylcholinesterase (AChE) activity was measured by using a spectrophotometer based on modified Ellman’s method38. The enzyme hydrolysed the substrate acetylthiocholine iodide (ASChI) into thiocholine which further reacted with Ellman’s reagent (DTNB) to produce yellow coloured product 2-nitrobenzoate-5-mercaptothiocholine and 5-thio-2-nitrobenzoate. The formation of yellow colored compound was detected at 412 nm on spectrophotometer. In the test tube, 3.49 mL of 50 mM tris-HCl buffer (pH 8.0), 0.25 mL of xanthine (250 ppm), 1.0 mL of AChE (1.0 U/mL) along with 0.25 mL of 10.0 mM of 5,5’-dithio-bis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB) was added. In subsequent reactions, the parent compound xanthine was replaced with xanthine derivatives (2a-j) and the formation of end product was monitored. Galanthamine acted as a positive control and its serial dilutions were prepared which varied in concentration from 125 to 500 ppm. The mixture was incubated for 15 min at 37 °C. After incubation, 0.1 mL of acetylthiocholine iodide (200 mM) was added to the reaction mixture and cuvette was placed in spectrophotometer. The change in absorbance at 412 nm was recorded for 6 min, maintaining 30 s interval. For blank, tris-HCl buffer and DTNB solution was used. Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization

The synthesized products (2a-j) are cost effective and one pot method plays an important role in development of new agrochemical. In our present protocol, alkylbromides were preferred over alkyl iodides as the iodides are responsible for reaction poisoning to some extent. Anion was generated using aqueous K2CO3 base and reaction proceededsmoothly without any activation and no formation of side product was reported.

The optimization was done by treatment of xanthine (1) with DMF and anhydrous K2CO3 at 0 °C for 2 h. Light yellow colored anion formed and reaction preceded smoothly without any activation and no formation of side product was reported. It was very crucial step to add alkyl halide (n-methyl bromide) slowly and drop wise at 0 °C to avoid reaction poisoning and allow reaction at 25 °C until the completion of reaction. Extractive workup and column chromatography furnished product 2a in 80% yields (Scheme 1). Likewise, reactions of xanthine with long alkyl chains viz. n-ethyl bromide to n-decylbromide completed in 10–12 h to furnished the N−7 alkylated xanthine derivatives 2b-j in 70–80% yields, respectively. The 1H-NMR signals corresponding to NH peak was missing at position N−7 and hence confirmed the N-alkylation. The structures of all the compounds (2a-j) were established using spectroscopic techniques and known compounds were confirmed by comparison with reported literature39–42.

Antioxidant bioassay

Xanthine (1) and its synthetic derivatives (2a-j) have shown very good antioxidant activity at different tested concentrations (P < 0.05). With increase in concentration from 10 to 250 ppm, its antioxidant potential of tested compounds also increased. It was observed that during the study from the standard used, compound (2j) was identified as potent antioxidant agent or free radical scavenger. The compound (2j) has decyl alkyl chain at the N-7 position to the xanthine as a result of which, the electron donating character of the ring increased and scavenging activity increased as shown in Figure S2. Similar trend was found for rest of the model that compound i.e. compound (2j) act as very good antioxidant activity.

Antinemic activity

Hatching test

The average egg hatch inhibition (%) of M. incognita by xanthine (1) and its N-7-alkylated derivatives (2a-j) was evaluated across concentrations ranging from 50 to 2000 ppm as shown in Tables 4 and 5. Overall, N−7 alkylated xanthine derivatives demonstrated significantly higher (p < 0.05) egg hatch inhibition compared to the xanthine control across all concentrations (Figure S6). Among the derivatives, compound (2j) exhibited the highest inhibitory activity, with an average of 93.98% at 250 ppm and reaching 100% at concentrations of 500 ppm and above. In addition to compound (2j) being the most effective, compound (2i) emerged as the second most effective compound against M. incognita infestations. Compound (2i) demonstrated significant inhibitory activity, with average percent egg hatch inhibition ranging from 21.98% at 50 ppm to 100% at 2000 ppm. Notably, compound (2i) exhibited consistent and substantial inhibition across all concentrations tested, with particularly high efficacy observed at higher concentrations. Conversely, compound (2a) showed the lowest inhibition among the derivatives, although still higher than the xanthine control, with an average of 6.26% at 50 ppm and gradually increasing to 99.31% at 1500 ppm before reaching 100% at 2000 ppm. The mean egg hatch inhibition values across all concentrations indicated that the derivatives consistently outperformed the xanthine control, with the mean ranging from 15.31% at 50 ppm to 100% at 2000 ppm, while the xanthine control ranged from 4.36% to 100% over the same concentration range. These findings demonstrate significant in vitro nematicidal activity and indicate the potential of these compounds for further evaluation under greenhouse and field conditions33,34.

Mortality test

Tables 6 and 7 show the meanpercent mortality of second stage juveniles (J2s) of the root knot nematode M. incognita when subjected to various concentrations (ppm) of xanthine (1) and N−7 alkylated xanthine derivatives (2a-j). The data unveils a concentration-dependent escalation in J2s mortality across all compounds, with higher concentrations generally yielding greater mortality rates. Particularly noteworthy are compounds (2 g), (2 h), (2i), and (2j), which consistently exhibited remarkable J2s mortality rates across all concentration levels, achieving 100% mortality at the highest concentration (2000 ppm). Compound (2e) also displayed significant effectiveness, attaining nearly complete mortality at concentrations exceeding 750 ppm. In contrast, compound (2a) demonstrated comparatively lower mortality rates at all concentrations, indicating reduced efficacy against M. incognita J2s. The mean mortality rates underscore the overall potency of the tested compounds, with concentrations above 500 ppm resulting in mortality rates surpassing 50%. These findings highlight the potential of N−7 alkylated xanthine derivatives, particularly compounds (2 g), (2 h), (2i), and (2j) as highly effective agents for controlling M. incognita J2s, emphasizing the need for further exploration of their mechanisms and practical applications in nematode management strategies. A significant positive correlation (p < 0.05) was found between the tested concentrations, compounds and durations at p < 0.05 (Figure S7)34.

Analysis of ligand-protein interactions

The molecular docking of xanthine (1) and its derivatives (2a-j) against three key M. incognita proteins revealed that synthetic modification consistently enhanced binding affinity compared to the parent scaffold. Against cellulase, xanthine exhibited a binding energy of −4.8 kcal/mol. In contrast, its derivatives demonstrated significantly stronger predicted affinities, ranging from − 5.1 to −6.4 kcal/mol. The detailed interactions, including the number and type of bonding residues for each compound are summarized in Supplementary Table S1, with representative interaction diagrams provided in Figure S8. A similar trend was observed for β−1,4-endoglucanase. Here, the binding energy of xanthine was − 3.7 kcal/mol, while the derivatives showed improved energies between − 5.1 and − 6.5 kcal/mol. The specific ligand-protein interactions are catalogued in Table S2 and visualized in Figure S9. The most pronounced enhancement was noted for carboxylic ester hydrolase. Xanthine bound with an energy of −4.7 kcal/mol, whereas the derivatives achieved markedly lower energies, spanning from − 5.2 to −7.2 kcal/mol. The interaction details for this target are presented in Table S3 and Figure S10. Overall, the derivatives showed improved binding affinity across all targets with the most substantial gains observed for carboxylic ester hydrolase, where the top compound exhibited stronger binding than xanthine itself.

Xanthine (1) and its N−7 alkylated derivatives (2a-j) have shown suppressive and substantial effect against AChE of pathogenic root knot nematodes. The experiment was carried out at 250 ppm concentration because when we used Galanthamine which is a positive inhibitor of AChE enzyme we found that 95% of the inhibition of AChE took place and the concentration was finalized. The maximum and minimum inhibitions were observed at the compound of (1) and (2j). The % inhibition was observed in the range of 2.78–56.90% at 250 ppm concentration. The significant and positive correlation was found between the tested concentration, compounds and enzyme as p < 0.05 (Table 9). The coefficient of variation for inhibition of AChE assay was found to be 0.10 at CD (5%).

For toxicological studies, the Lazar, Protox, ADMET analysis of xanthine and its N-alkylated derivativeswas carried out before wet lab experimentation for drug development programme. The ADMET analysis of candidate was calculated from MarwinSketch 5.10.0 software. All the tested candidates established drug like behaviour and qualified Lipinski’s rule of five as their logP value was less than 5, hydrogen donor less than 5, hydrogen bond acceptor less than 10 and molecular weight is less than 500. Moreover, Protox analysis showed that all compounds belong to mild toxic category with Toxicity class IV and can cross blood brain barrier in humans. The tested compounds have shown negative toxicity for rat, mouse and rodent carcinogenicity, hepatotoxicity, immunotoxicity and mutagenicity. All the tested compounds were found to have good absorptivity for oral medication and have high bioavailability.

Discussion

Although varied number of innovative chemical moieties have been explored and introduced in the market from the past few decades, but there is declined trend in introduction of lead molecules for serving mankind27,47. In present scenario, focus is mainly on novel search of novel antioxidant, antibacterial as well as nematicidal agents to counter the already deregistered drugs. About 70% of the agrochemicals synthesized and introduced in the market are heterocyclic compounds with consideration electronic, solubility and steric effects. Substitution of one nitrogen at the N−7 position on five-membered ring led to induce its antioxidant, insecticidal and nematicidal activities. The observations during the present study revealed that the biological activity of xanthine and its analogues varied with structural modifications especially with alkyl chain length. The electron donating nature of the alkyl groups that attached at N−7 position is responsible to increase the inductive effect and these groups are also involved in the formation of covalent bonds as well as hydrogen bonds which generally increase their lipophilicity, bioavailability, increment to logP value and affinity for the receptor48. The four NHs and two carbonyl groups attached to the xanthine moiety contributed to the high lipophilicity of the compound as a result effective binding to the receptor proteins. The electron donating groups i.e. different alkyl groups that attached to xanthine subsequently increased its lipid soluble potency as compare to the parent moiety. The parent moiety i.e. xanthine is already reported to exhibit biological activities47. The alkyl groups attached may dissociate in the form of alkyl carbocation, carbanion, free radical or carbene and directly affects the DNA of the host proteins. These alkyl derivatives reacted with binding proteins of the DNA resulting in the disruption of the DNA linkage, collapse of DNA strands and death of the host cell. Thus, the compound (2j) with N-decyl derivative of xanthine was found to be most prominent moieties with respect to biological potential. The present assessment of toxic properties of drug candidates is important in the field of drug discovery to identify top molecules with less side and toxic effects. But to validate the drug candidate the toxic potential is needed to explore prior for commercialization. Due to several challenges such as time, cost, ethical issues in silico toxicology serve as integral platform for toxicity prediction49. These observations indicated that compound (2i) and (2j) of N-alkyl derivatives progressively impair M. incognita egg hatching metabolic capability and increased juvenile mortality. In silico molecular docking studies were performed to explore the affinity and binding interactions of the selected compounds in the binding site of the target.

The present studies revealed novel interaction of compound (2j) with pathogenic proteins which to the best of our knowledge has not been earlier studied and this mechanism of action is unique amongst nematicides. The organophosphates and carbamates are both known to act via disruption of neural signaling pathways; these compounds exhibit an effect on the motility by impairment of neural pathways which are responsible for movement. Fumigant nematicides have been predicted to act via the rapid block of integral metabolic activity, resulting in nematode death50. Fluopyram51 acts through a similar mechanism by inhibition of succinate dehydrogenase, which is an important component of the tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA) and oxidative phosphorylation metabolic pathways. Docking studies were conducted to predict the possible binding sites of the compound to a given receptor or protein involved in pathogenic infestations. The maximum numbers of interactions were shown by His 44 and were found to be major amino acid residue involved in the hydrogen bonds followed by Tyr 79, Gly 326, Arg 72 and Asn 53. Hydrogen bonding is very necessary for compound to exhibit its pesticide character and the increase in alkyl chain length hinders the various intermolecular interactions. The carbonyl groups attached at the C-2 and C-6 position of the xanthine played a major role during the interaction studies and can be possibly key features for increment of nematicidal character along with alkyl chain moiety. The in silico studies suggested that the binding of xanthine derivative (2a-j) with aspartyl protease gains importance as it plays important role in parasitism of nematode. The acetyl cholinesterase (AChE) hydrolyzes the neurotransmitter: Acetylcholine (ACh) which acts as excitatory neurotransmitter in somatic nervous system for voluntary muscles. In nematode, AChE demonstrated vital function in locomotion of these pathogenic organisms. All the tested compounds (2a-j) had shown their subsequent inhibition at all the tested concentration. Compounds (1) and (2a) had shown less inhibitory activity as compared to other tested compounds. On other hand compounds (2i) and (2j) had shown maximum inhibition ranging from 53.64 to 56.90% inhibition at 250 ppm concentration. The r2 value for the compound (2j) was calculated as 0.94 which indicated that 94% of the data was line in with regression model and apart from (2j) all the other tested compounds were found to fit in regression model. We recognize an important limitation of this study. Our docking results provide computational evidence for binding interactions, but they do not replace direct experimental validation. We have not yet tested whether these compounds bind to proposed targets in living nematodes. Therefore, while our proposed mechanism is consistent with the in silico data, it remains a model that requires confirmation through enzyme inhibition assays or other biochemical methods. We present our findings as a strong computational basis for further experimental investigation.

Conclusion

The convenient highly efficient, cost-effective one pot approach has been employed for the regioselective synthesis of N−7 alkylated xanthine derivatives. The method is simpler, cost effective, less time consuming and offers operational advantages over other methods reported. Anion was generated using potassium carbonate (K2CO3) base and N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF) as solvent, reaction proceeded smoothly without any activation and with no side product formation. From antioxidant screening of xanthine (1) and its N−7 alkylated derivatives (2a-j) it was found that the increase in electron donating character due to increase in alkyl chain length at the N−7 position increased the antioxidant potential of the tested compounds. The compound (2j) 7-decyl-1H-purine-2,6-(3H,7H)-dione with decyl chain attached at the N−7 position of parent moiety had shown potent DPPH free radical scavenging, hydrogen peroxide scavenging, nitric oxide scavenging and superoxide scavenging activity. From antimicrobial screening of xanthine (1) and its N−7 alkylated derivatives (2a-j) against D. solanum, X. campestris and F. oxysporum, it was found that the increase in electron donating character due to the long alkyl chain on the tested compounds, it enhanced its bactericidal potential of the tested compounds (2a-j). N−7 alkylated derivative (2j) i.e.7-decyl-1H-purine-2,6-(3H,7H)-dione with long alkyl substitution at N−7 position has shown potent activity against D. solanum and X. campestris. Similarly, compound (2j) had shown significant inhibition of F. oxysporum due to the presence of electron rich moieties present on it. Two carbonyl groups and nitrogen atoms along with alkylated chains present in the compounds (2a-j) were found responsible to exhibit its antimicrobial activity. The tested compounds were found to be very effective against this pathogenic gram-negative bacterial strain of X. campestris and least effective against the bacterial strain of the F. oxysporum. From in vitroantinemic screening of xanthine (1) and its N−7 alkylated derivatives (2a-j) against M. incognita it was found that the increase in electron donating character due to increase in alkyl chain length increased its antinemic potential of the tested compounds. The compound xanthine (1) and 7-decyl-1H-purine-2,6-(3H,7H)-dione (2j) with decyl chain at N−7 position, exhibited potent antinemic character. All the tested compounds (2a-j) had shown potent larvicidal activity but less ovicidal activity. The validation of biological activity was done through in silico studies carried against cellulase, β−1,4-endoglucanase and carboxylic ester hydrolase of M. incognita. Xanthine (1) and its N−7 alkylated derivatives (2a-j) had shown significant interaction with the protein complex of root knot nematode M. incognita. The maximum binding free energy had been observed between the compound (2j) and the carboxylic ester hydrolase of pathogenic bacterial strain of M. incognita. Further in vitro biochemical screening of xanthine (1) and its N−7 alkylated derivatives (2a-j) against acetylcholinesterase enzyme present in M. incognita. Results revealed that with increase in number of electron donating groups on the xanthine molecule caused significant inhibition of acetylcholinesterase enzyme. Due to inhibition of this enzyme, physiological impairment leads to paralysis and death of targeted organism. Xanthine derivatives (2j) with decyl chain caused maximum inhibition of this enzyme. More is the binding of derivatives (2a-j) with enzyme more is the inhibition. 7-Decyl-1H-purine-2,6-(3H,7H)-dione(2j) with decyl chain at N−7 position, exhibited potent antinemic character due to the inhibition of acetylcholinesterase enzyme. Overall, the synthesized N-7 alkylated xanthine derivatives, particularly compound (2j), demonstrated significant in vitro biological activity. These findings highlight their potential as promising lead candidates for further investigation toward agrochemical development, subject to validation through greenhouse and field studies.
