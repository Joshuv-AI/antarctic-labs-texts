Arthroscopic Latissimus Dorsi Transfer in the Lateral Decubitus Position for Irreparable Posterosuperior Massive Rotator Cuff Tears
Authors: Yang Yang, JianMin Zhang, Peng Wang, QingGuo Zhang, YongZhi Ye, XiaoBo Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe management of irreparable posterosuperior massive rotator cuff tears remains challenging. With advances in arthroscopic techniques, minimally invasive arthroscopic latissimus dorsi tendon (LDT) transfer has been increasingly used for irreparable posterosuperior massive rotator cuff tears. The present article describes arthroscopic LDT transfer in the lateral decubitus position for irreparable posterosuperior massive rotator cuff tears. The dissection and release of the LDT were performed through the interval between long head of the biceps tendon and humeral shaft. When releasing the LDT, meticulous attention is required to avoid injury to the radial nerve located anterior to the tendon, as well as the anterior circumflex humeral artery and the axillary nerve superiorly. Limited release (<5 mm) of the superior margin of the pectoralis major tendon may be necessary to facilitate release of the inferior border of the LDT. After exteriorization of the tendon through the posterior axillary incision, sutures of different colors are used for tendon weaving. During establishment of the posterior transfer pathway, the axillary nerve should be clearly identified arthroscopically and carefully protected. The transferred LDT is fixed with 2 knotless anchors, followed by additional knot tying to reduce the risk of loosening.

Irreparable posterosuperior massive rotator cuff tears are defined as tears in which the supraspinatus and infraspinatus tendons cannot be sufficiently mobilized and repaired to their anatomic footprint despite adequate release, even when the arm is positioned at 60° abduction. 1 The management of irreparable posterosuperior massive rotator cuff tears remains challenging, despite the availability of multiple treatment options. 2

In 1988, Gerber et al. first described latissimus dorsi tendon (LDT) transfer for the treatment of irreparable posterosuperior massive rotator cuff tears. 1 Subsequently, numerous modifications to the original technique have been reported. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 With advances in arthroscopic techniques, minimally invasive arthroscopic LDT transfer has been increasingly adopted. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 Based on our clinical experience in treating irreparable posterosuperior massive rotator cuff tears, we describe a modified arthroscopic LDT transfer technique to address this challenging condition. SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Patient Preparation and Positioning

Cefazolin or clindamycin is routinely administered as preoperative antibiotic prophylaxis. After the induction of general anesthesia, patients are placed in the lateral decubitus position with the operative arm abducted to 40° and flexed forward to 15°. A longitudinal traction of 4 kg is applied to the arm. Patient positioning is illustrated in Figure 1A, and the key operative steps are shown in Video 1.

The procedure begins with evaluation of the rotator cuff tear, followed by partial repair of the residual cuff and treatment of concomitant pathologies. The lateral portal serves as the viewing portal, and the anterolateral portal serves as the working portal. The long head of the biceps tendon is identified and released along the intertubercular groove proximal to the superior border of the pectoralis major tendon. The anterolateral portal is then converted to a viewing portal, and an anteroinferior portal is established as the working portal. The anterior circumflex humeral artery and the radial nerve are identified and carefully protected, followed by release of the LDT. The tendon is then woven using a locking‐edge stitch with 2 different‐colored high‐strength sutures. The arthroscope is reintroduced through the lateral portal, and the anterolateral portal is used as the working portal. Prior to establishing the posterior transfer pathway, the axillary nerve is identified arthroscopically and protected. The LDT is then passed through the posterior pathway into the joint and fixed at the junction of the supraspinatus and infraspinatus footprints using two 4.75‐mm knotless anchors (SwiveLock; Arthrex, Naples, FL). Step 1: Evaluation of Rotator Cuff Tears and Management of Concomitant Pathologies

Intra‐articular assessment is performed through the posterior and anterior portals to evaluate the labrum, long head of the biceps tendon (LHBT), and articular cartilage. Concomitant pathologies are addressed as indicated. To assess the rotator cuff tear pattern and acromial morphology, the lateral portal is then used as the viewing portal and the anterolateral portal is used as the working portal. Acromioplasty is routinely performed in patients with Hamada grade I or II and is omitted in those with more advanced degeneration. Arthroscopic LDT transfer is performed on confirmation of an irreparable posterosuperior massive rotator cuff tear.

Step 2: Identification and Release of the LDT Insertion

The lateral portal serves as the viewing portal, and the anterolateral portal serves as the working portal. The anterosuperior portal is established as an auxiliary portal under needle guidance. A switching stick is introduced into the joint through the anterosuperior portal to lever the anterior deltoid and surrounding soft tissues, thereby providing an adequate working space. The LHBT is released along the intertubercular groove proximal to the superior border of the pectoralis major tendon. The anterolateral portal is then converted into a viewing portal using a switching stick. An anteroinferior portal is created under needle guidance at the anterior margin of the deltoid muscle, 5 cm distal to the anterior portal (Figure 1). A switching stick introduced through the anterosuperior portal is used to elevate the LHBT. Radiofrequency ablation through the anteroinferior portal is used to dissect the soft tissues between the humeral shaft and LHBT, thereby exposing the LDT (Figure 2A).

Arthroscopic exposure of the latissimus dorsi tendon and key anatomic landmarks. After adequate releasing of the LHBT, the arthroscope is placed in the anterolateral portal and advanced through the interval inferior to the LHBT for visualization (A). The LDT and RN are identified (B). During release of the superior border of the LDT, care is taken to avoid injury to the ACHA (C). (ACHA, anterior circumflex humeral artery; LDT, latissimus dorsi tendon; LHBT, long head of the biceps tendon; RN, radial nerve.)

The anterior circumflex humeral artery is identified and preserved on exposure of the superior border of the LDT (Figure 2A). The radial nerve, located anterior to the latissimus dorsi muscle, is carefully identified and protected (Figure 2B). Complete exposure of the inferior border of the LDT occasionally requires the partial release of the pectoralis major tendon.

After complete exposure of the superior and inferior borders, surrounding soft tissues are dissected along the tendon surface using a suture grasper. A PDS II (Ethicon, Somerville, NJ) suture is preloaded into the suture hook, leaving approximately half the suture length outside the needle tip. Through the anteroinferior portal, the suture hook is passed from the inferior border to the superior border of the LDT to deploy a PDS loop (Figure 3a,b). Both sutured limbs are retrieved to ligate the tendons (Figure 3c).

Arthroscopic ligation, release, and delivery of the latissimus dorsi tendon. After the posterior delivery of the PDS II (Ethicon, Somerville, NJ, USA) suture, gentle traction is applied by an assistant to maintain the appropriate tension (d). Tendon insertion is released from superior to inferior (e), allowing smooth extraction of the LDT through the posterior axillary incision (f). Yellow arrows, LDT; green arrow, posterior axillary incision. (LDT, latissimus dorsi tendon; PDS, polydioxanone.)

A small longitudinal incision is made along the posterior axillary line (Figure 1A). A switching stick is advanced from the anteroinferior portal along the LDT surface, passing posteriorly, medially, and inferiorly to the exit through a posterior axillary incision, thereby establishing a tendon delivery pathway. Using an index finger through this pathway, additional blunt dissection is performed to further release the surrounding soft tissues and facilitate smooth tendon mobilization. The switching stick is removed, and the PDS II (Ethicon, Somerville, NJ) suture ligating the LDT is delivered from the anteroinferior portal through the posterior axillary incision using a suture grasper. Gentle traction is applied to the suture ends to maintain appropriate tension (Figure 3d). Finally, radiofrequency ablation is used to release the LDT insertion from the superior to the inferior positions (Figure 3e). After complete release, the LDT is delivered smoothly through the posterior axillary incision using PDS II (Ethicon, Somerville, NJ) sutures (Figure 3f).

Step 3: Latissimus Dorsi Muscle Release and Tendon Weaving

After delivery through the posterior axillary incision, excursion of the latissimus dorsi muscle is limited by the surrounding muscular branches, resulting in an insufficient length for direct transfer to the junction of the supraspinatus and infraspinatus footprints. Therefore, further muscle release is required.

Circumferential blunt release around the muscle belly is performed using the surgeon's index finger, and the myofascia is sharply divided with scissors. Adequate release is confirmed when the LDT can be easily pulled to the posterior incision with anterosuperior traction (Figure 3f).

As intrinsic torsion exists at the humeral insertion of the LDT, 2 differently colored high‐strength sutures are used for tendon weaving. On 1 side of the tendon, a locking‐edge stitch is placed from distal to proximal for 6 stitches, followed by reverse‐direction stitching from proximal to distal for an additional 6 stitches at approximately 5‐mm intervals (Figure 4A). The same technique is applied to the opposite side. After weaving, the tendon length and width are assessed to ensure sufficient dimensions for transfer (Figure 4A,B).

Weaving and evaluation of the latissimus dorsi tendon. Locking‐edge stitches are placed on both sides of the tendon using 2 different‐colored sutures, with 6 stitches in each direction at approximately 5‐mm intervals (A). The tendon length and width are assessed after weaving (B,C).

Step 4: Establishment of the Posterior Transfer Pathway and Tendon Introduction

The arthroscope is placed in the lateral portal, and the anterolateral portal is used as the working portal. Radiofrequency ablation is used to release the surrounding soft tissues. Meticulous dissection is performed to avoid axillary nerve injury. The axillary nerve exiting the quadrilateral space is identified and protected (Figure 5A). The surgeon inserts the index finger through the posterior axillary incision to palpate the teres major muscle and performs a blunt dissection posterior to it, progressing superiorly and anteriorly to establish the transfer pathway (Figure 5B). Further release is performed posterior to the quadrilateral space along the posterior surfaces of the teres major and minor and on the deep surface of the posterior deltoid to complete the pathway.

Establishment of the posterior transfer pathway and fixation of the transferred tendon. The arthroscope is introduced through the lateral portal to identify the axillary nerve at its exit from the quadrilateral space (A). Blunt finger dissection through the posterior axillary incision is performed posterior to the quadrilateral space, along the posterior surfaces of the teres major and teres minor, and on the deep surface of the posterior deltoid to complete the posterior transfer pathway (B). A nucleus pulposus forceps is introduced through the anterolateral portal to grasp the surgeon's glove, which is inserted into the glenohumeral joint via a posterior axillary incision. The forceps are then guided back through the established pathway and retrieved at the posterior axillary incision (C,D). The transferred latissimus dorsi tendon is confirmed to be free of torsion (E) and fixed using two 4.75‐mm knotless anchors (F). (LDT, latissimus dorsi tendon; TM, teres minor.)

After pathway establishment, the surgeon reinserts the index finger through the posterior axillary incision until the humeral head is palpated. Under arthroscopic visualization, the nucleus pulposus forceps are used to aid the entry of the surgeon's glove through the anterolateral portal grasp and advanced through the pathway to the posterior axillary incision (Figure 5C,D).

The forceps are then used to grasp sutures attached to the woven LDT. The medial and lateral borders of the tendon are reconfirmed (Figure 5E), designating the proximal portion as the lateral fixation point and the distal portion as the medial fixation point. The sutures are pulled through the pathway and retrieved from the anterolateral portal, allowing the introduction of the tendon into the glenohumeral joint.

Step 5: Post‐Transfer Tendon Tension Assessment and Fixation

After the transfer, the sutures are tensioned through the anterolateral portal. Fixation is performed only if the LDT can easily reach the posterior margin of the supraspinatus footprint.

Partial repair of the rotator cuff is completed prior to LDT fixation. In cases of substantial anterior cuff deficiency, proximal tenodesis of the LHBT is performed using a 5.5‐mm Corkscrew anchor (Arthrex, Naples, FL). The fixation site for the transferred tendon is the junction between the supraspinatus and infraspinatus footprints.

Two 4.75‐mm knotless anchors (SwiveLock, Arthrex, Naples, FL) are used for fixation. Correct tendon orientation without torsion is confirmed when the proximal tendon is fixed laterally and the distal tendon is fixed medially. The remaining sutures are secured with 3 to 4 sliding knots to prevent loosening caused by perianchor bone resorption (Figure 5F).

Postoperative Rehabilitation

All patients are immobilized in an abduction brace at 30° abduction and 15° forward flexion for 6 weeks. Passive range‐of‐motion exercises are initiated after the brace removal. Active shoulder motion begins at 3 months postoperatively, followed by gradual strengthening.

DISCUSSION

LDT transfer provides distinct advantages in the treatment of irreparable posterosuperior massive rotator cuff tears. 15 , 16 , 17 Although LDT does not re‐establish direct superior coverage of the humeral head, biomechanically, the transferred latissimus dorsi restores the shoulder force couple, exerts a depressor effect on the humeral head, and maintains active muscle function, rather than acting as a passive tendon graft. 18 , 19

The surgical technique of LDT has evolved from open and miniopen techniques to fully arthroscopic procedures. 1 , 4 , 7 , 15 , 20 Compared with previously reported arthroscopic LDT transfer techniques, 21 , 22 the present technique offers several technical advantages. First, during exposure of the LDT insertion, the LHBT is released distal to the intertubercular groove and elevated to enhance visualization. This approach avoids dissection along the superior border of the subscapularis and conjoined tendon, thereby reducing the risk of injury to the axillary nerve and the medial circumflex humeral artery. Furthermore, the release of the LHBT is limited to the level of pectoralis major insertion, obviating the need for excessive detachment of the pectoralis major tendon.

Second, a posterior transfer pathway is created by advancing the switching stick along the surface of the LDT to a small posterior axillary incision. With minimal additional blunt finger dissection, the tendon can be smoothly mobilized and delivered posteriorly, thereby minimizing soft‐tissue trauma.

Third, the transferred tendon fixation is performed at a tension‐balanced footprint located at the junction of the supraspinatus and infraspinatus insertion points. 23 After fixation with knotless anchors, the remaining suture limbs are secured with supplementary knots to mitigate the risk of anchor loosening and fixation failure.

The primary limitation of this technique is the requirement for advanced arthroscopic expertise and a comprehensive understanding of the periscapular and axillary anatomy, which is typically characterized by a substantial learning curve. Although critical neurovascular structures are routinely identified and protected intraoperatively, the risk of iatrogenic injury cannot be completely eliminated, particularly in the anteroinferior portal region where the cephalic vein may be at risk. In elderly patients with osteoporosis, fixation of the transferred LDT using 2 knotless anchors may be associated with a risk of loosening. Additionally, exposure of the inferior border of the LDT may require limited release (≤5 mm) of the pectoralis major tendon insertion (Table 2).
