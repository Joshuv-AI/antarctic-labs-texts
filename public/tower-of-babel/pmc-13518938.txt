Multidimensional analysis of nutritional status among children under 5 years in Africa, 1990–2024
Authors: Yonghao Li, Wenping Li, Mengdi Chen, Lulu Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The first five years of life are critical for growth and development, with nutritional status directly impacting lifelong health and population quality. Africa, characterized by a fragile nutritional foundation, remains a priority region for global health surveillance by the World Health Organization (WHO). This study aimed to analyze five key nutritional indicators among children under five in Africa from 1990 to 2024, utilizing data from the WHO Global Health Observatory (GHO) 2024 dataset. This study employed the 2024 dataset from GHO, which contains data on the nutritional status of children under 5 years of age across 46 African countries spanning 1990 to 2024. In accordance with the 2025 income classification criteria of the World Bank (WB), three representative countries were selected: Algeria (high-income), Senegal (middle-income), and Niger (low-income). Stratified analyses were conducted across the three countries according to gender, educational attainment, and residential residence. Statistical approaches including paired design and randomized block design were applied to examine inter-group differences in childhood nutritional status within each country. From 1990 to 2024, the rates of stunting, underweight, wasting and severe wasting declined to varying degrees among children under 5 years in Africa, while the prevalence of overweight increased slightly. In Algeria, the prevalence of wasting and overweight was higher among children aged 0–1 years. Higher stunting rates were observed in rural areas and among boys. The underweight rate was higher in the low-income group, whereas the overweight rate was higher in the high-income group. Children of mothers with no formal education exhibited higher rates of stunting and underweight. In Senegal, stunting and underweight were more prevalent among children aged 2–5 years, while wasting and severe wasting were higher in the 0–1 years age group. Higher malnutrition prevalence was found in rural residents, boys, the low-income population, and children of mothers with primary education. By contrast, overweight was more common among children aged 2–5 years, urban residents, the high-income population, and children of mothers with secondary or higher education. In Niger, the stunting rate was higher in the 2–5 years age group, and the rates of wasting, severe wasting and overweight were elevated among children aged 0–1 years. Rural residents and boys had a higher burden of malnutrition. Children of mothers with primary education showed higher prevalence of stunting and wasting. The nutritional status of children under 5 years of age in Africa has improved overall, though the progress varies across countries at different income levels. Socioeconomic factors exert heterogeneous effects on different nutritional indicators within the same country. Targeted intervention measures should therefore be formulated tailored to the national context of each country.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55642-2.

Introduction

Malnutrition among children under five is highly prevalent in Africa. It adversely affects child survival and development as well as socioeconomic advancement, with the associated health burden considerably higher than the global average1,2. This issue is closely correlated with childhood infectious diseases and irreversible long-term developmental impairment3,4. For instance, it leads to lifelong limitations in cognitive function and persistently weakened immunity in children. These outcomes substantially elevate the risk of non-communicable diseases in adulthood, thereby compromising individual health across the life course and hindering the socioeconomic sustainable development of Africa4,5. Notably, several African countries are confronted with the double burden of malnutrition, characterized by the coexistence of undernutrition and overnutrition6. This phenomenon coexists within the same country, community and even household, leading to an unbalanced and divergent trend in nutritional status7. According to WHO 2022 data, the prevalence of malnutrition among children under 5 years of age remains relatively high in Africa, with the continent accounting for a substantial proportion of the global burden of childhood stunting and wasting8. Meanwhile, the issue of childhood overweight in Africa also warrants close attention9. If the current nutritional health trends are not effectively curbed, Africa is projected to face a dual challenge of undernutrition and overnutrition by 2030, which will further exacerbate the challenges in the field of nutritional health across the region.

Based on the 2025 income classification criteria of WB, this study selected three African countries: Algeria (high-income), Senegal (middle-income), and Niger (low-income). Across each country, five nutritional indicators among children under 5 years of age were compared and analyzed from multiple dimensions, including age, gender, residential area, economic status, and maternal educational attainment. The findings provide references and implications for the formulation and implementation of targeted nutritional intervention strategies in different regions of Africa.

Materials and methods

Data sources

The dataset used in this study was derived from the WHO Health Inequality Monitor Database on Child Nutrition, jointly provided by the United Nations Children’s Fund (UNICEF), WHO and WB. The dataset is available at: https://www.who.int/data/sets/health-inequality-monitor-dataset#nut. Data pertaining to the African region were screened and extracted, comprising 36,110 valid records from 46 countries and territories. The core variables included three categories: basic information (survey year and country); stratified dimensions (age, sex, residential area, economic status, and maternal educational attainment); and nutritional status indicators (prevalence of stunting, underweight, wasting, severe wasting, and overweight).

Key nutritional assessment indicators for under 5 years children

It mainly includes five indicators: (1) Stunting is a core indicator of chronic malnutrition. It refers to height-for-age growth retardation, which is caused by long-term nutritional deficiency, recurrent infections, and poor sanitary conditions. Stunting impairs children’s cognitive development and lifelong health, and serves as an important indicator reflecting the long-term living environment of the population10. (2) Underweight is a comprehensive indicator of undernutrition. It reflects low weight relative to age and encompasses the overlapping conditions of acute and chronic malnutrition11. (3) Wasting is the core indicator of acute malnutrition. It refers to insufficient weight relative to height and is used to assess short-term deterioration in nutritional status12. (4) Severe wasting represents an extreme form of wasting. It is a life-threatening state of acute malnutrition that indicates severe deficiency of energy and protein in the body, and serves as a major risk factor for elevated childhood mortality3. (5) Overweight reflects the degree of body fat accumulation and is directly associated with energy intake exceeding energy expenditure13.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSSPRO software. GraphPad Prism 10.4.2 was used for figure plotting.

Descriptive statistics

Descriptive statistical analysis was performed on five nutritional indicators among children under 5 years of age across the African region during 1999–2024. Algeria (high-income), Senegal (middle-income), and Niger (low-income) were selected as typical research samples. Owing to varying degrees of missing annual data in the three countries, complete datasets were simultaneously available for 2019, which was also the closest year to the present. Accordingly, data from 2019 were adopted to compare the five nutritional indicators among the three countries.

Stratified analysis

All available data from Algeria, Senegal, and Niger were aggregated and analyzed across five dimensions: age, sex, residential area, economic status, and maternal educational attainment. For the five nutritional indicators, paired-sample Wilcoxon signed-rank tests matched by identical survey year were used to examine statistically significant differences across age groups, genders, and residential areas. Friedman tests for multiple matched samples (also matched by identical survey year) were applied to assess differences by economic status and maternal educational attainment, with Bonferroni correction used for post-hoc pairwise comparisons between groups.

Status of five nutritional indicators among children under 5 years in Algeria

The stunting rate decreased by 13.1%, with a relative reduction of 57.2%; the underweight rate fell by 6.5%, representing a relative decline of 70.7%. The wasting rate dropped by 4.4%, with a relative decrease of 62.0%; the severe wasting rate declined by 1.7%, corresponding to a relative reduction of 56.7%. The only indicator that increased was the overweight rate, which rose by 4.1%, with a relative increase of 47.1%.

Comparison across dimensions (Table 2): The wasting rate (7.38 ± 3.15, median ± standard deviation) and overweight rate (16.17 ± 2.42) in children aged 0–1 years were higher than those in the 2–5 years age group (4.64 ± 3.03 and 10.65 ± 2.06, respectively) (Fig. 3). The stunting rate among rural children (19.53 ± 6.96) was higher than that among urban children (15.79 ± 5.68). Male children had a higher stunting rate (19.68 ± 5.95) than female children (17.45 ± 6.36). Regarding economic stratification, the low-income group had higher rates of stunting (13.47 ± 4.31) and underweight (3.89 ± 0.82), but a lower overweight rate (11.49 ± 0.67). Children of mothers with no formal education showed higher stunting (15.31 ± 2.91) and underweight rates (3.71 ± 0.61) compared with those of mothers with secondary or advanced education (10.40 ± 1.65 and 2.51 ± 0.05, respectively). The stunting rate decreased by 7.3% with a relative reduction of 24.5%; the underweight rate decreased by 6.2% with a relative reduction of 32.6%. The wasting rate dropped by 2.2% with a relative reduction of 23.4%; the severe wasting rate decreased by 0.9% with a relative reduction of 30.0%. Discussion

Overall nutritional transition in Africa

The observed declines in stunting, underweight, wasting and severe wasting rates across Africa signify tangible progress in mitigating chronic and acute malnutrition burdens. This improvement likely reflects the impact of global nutritional aid, expanded maternal and child health programs, and enhanced control of childhood infectious diseases11,14,15. Conversely, the rise in overweight prevalence signals an emerging nutrition transition, potentially driven by urbanization, dietary shifts toward high-energy foods, and reduced physical activity16,17. If this trend continues, Africa may simultaneously face the dual pressure of developmental impairment related to childhood malnutrition and chronic diseases associated with overweight, which will further increase the long-term challenges in the field of nutritional health7.

Income-specific heterogeneity and contextual analysis

High-income country (Algeria)

The problem of malnutrition has decreased significantly, while the problem of overweight has become prominent. Socioeconomic factors and age may be the main influencing factors. The stunting rate is higher in rural areas and low-income groups, suggesting that children in rural and low-income families may suffer from chronic undernutrition18. The wasting rate and overweight rate of the 0–1 years old group were higher than those of the 2–5 years old group, suggesting that infants are more prone to acute nutritional fluctuations and overweight risks19. The underweight rate was higher in the group where mothers had no education, indicating that the mother’s educational level still has a certain impact on the children’s basic nutrition20. The overweight rate is characterized by being lower in the low-income group than in the high-income group, suggesting that children from high-income families are more prone to excessive energy intake21. Synthesizing the above characteristics, it suggests that Algeria may have entered the stage of dual nutritional burden. It is recommended to formulate dual intervention measures that address both undernutrition among children from poor families and overnutrition among children from wealthy families.

Middle-income country (Senegal)

The undernutrition rate showed a moderate decrease, and the impacts of various dimensions on nutritional indicators were widespread. It is mainly reflected in the following aspects: the malnutrition rate is higher in rural areas, among boys, and in low-income families than in urban areas, among girls, and in high-income families; the risk of overweight is higher in urban areas and high-income families; the higher the educational level of the mother, the lower the malnutrition rate of the child, but the higher the risk of overweight. The above results indicate that Senegal may be jointly affected by urban-rural differences, economic inequality, and maternal education level, and the nutritional problems show a socioeconomic gradient distribution. It is recommended to carry out comprehensive nutritional interventions covering multiple dimensions22,23.

Low-income country (Niger)

The problem of child malnutrition is relatively serious, with the smallest improvement range; the overweight rate shows no obvious change, and the overall nutritional deficiency indicators are higher than those of the other two countries (Fig. 2). The malnutrition indicators are higher in rural areas, among male children, and in the group of mothers with no education, which may be related to the lack of medical resources, backward economic conditions in rural areas, and low health literacy of mothers24,25. There is no significant difference in the overweight rate among different economic groups, which suggests that it may be related to the overall low economic level26. Based on the above results, it is recommended to prioritize ensuring the supply of basic nutrition and health equity in rural areas27.

Key determinants across multiple dimensions

Based on a comprehensive multi-dimensional analysis of the nutritional status of the three countries, the following findings were obtained: 1. Age: Infants aged 0–1 years are more prone to acute changes such as wasting and overweight; children aged 2–5 years are more likely to suffer from chronic malnutrition such as stunting28. 2. Gender: Male children generally have higher rates of stunting, underweight, and wasting than female children, which is consistent across regions29. 3. Place of Residence: The risk of malnutrition among rural children is higher than that among urban children, and the urban-rural gap may be an important source of nutritional inequality among African children30,31. 4. Economic Status: The malnutrition rate decreases with the increase of economic level, while the overweight rate increases with the increase of economic level32. 5. Maternal Education Level: The higher the mother’s educational level, the lower the risk of child nutritional inadequacy, which may be a stable protective factor33.

Research limitations

Although this study selected three countries with different income levels, namely Algeria, Senegal, and Niger, and conducted a comparative analysis of multiple nutritional indicators from multiple dimensions, there are still the following limitations: (1) Data for some countries and years are missing, which may affect the accuracy of inter-group comparisons; (2) The cross-country comparison did not consider the impact of national health policies and intervention measures; 3. Influencing factors such as dietary structure, feeding behaviors, hygiene conditions, and accessibility of medical services were not included. In the next step, methods such as pre- and post-intervention comparison, household surveys, and dietary assessment can be adopted to further analyze the mechanism of nutritional changes, and provide targeted intervention measures and public health suggestions for different countries.
