Three-dimensional morphometry and clinical implications of the atlantoaxial lateral mass joint: a study of sexual dimorphism
Authors: Tairui Zhang, Junwei Zhang, Maji Sun, Xiangyang Li, Ruxin Liu, Feng Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe atlantoaxial lateral mass joint is a pivotal structure facilitating cervical rotation and flexion-extension. Its morphological characteristics are intrinsically linked to degenerative pathologies and segmental instability. However, comprehensive three-dimensional anatomical data, particularly regarding sex-specific variations, remain insufficient. This study aimed to perform precise three-dimensional measurements of the atlantoaxial lateral mass joint in normal adults to elucidate its normative morphological features and sexual dimorphism, which may serve as a morphological reference for future studies exploring surgical planning, personalized implant design, and biomechanical modeling.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 200 patients (100 males, 100 females; aged 18–50 years) without cervical abnormalities, using cervical three-dimensional spiral CT images obtained between December 2024 and December 2025. Parameters measured on reconstructed images included the anteroposterior diameter (sagittal plane), left-right diameter (coronal plane), medial inclination angle, joint space width, and distance from the transverse foramen to the lateral mass joint midpoint. Statistical analysis employed independent samples t-tests to evaluate sex differences.ResultsAll parameters exhibited significant sex differences (P < 0.05). Males consistently demonstrated significantly larger anatomical dimensions than females across all linear and angular metrics. Notably, these sex-related differences remained significant after adjusting for Body Mass Index (BMI) via ANCOVA (P < 0.05), indicating that the observed dimorphism reflects intrinsic biological traits rather than overall body habitus.ConclusionThis study systematically quantifies the three-dimensional anatomy of the atlantoaxial lateral mass joint and confirms significant sexual dimorphism.These findings offer morphological context that may be relevant when investigating atlantoaxial pathologies and provide anatomical baseline data for future research concerning personalized fusion device design, surgical navigation, and finite element simulations.Keywords: Atlantoaxial joint, Lateral mass joint, Three-dimensional spiral CT, Sexual dimorphism, Personalized implant design, Finite element analysis

Introduction

The first cervical vertebra (atlas, C1) and the second cervical vertebra (axis, C2) are collectively referred to as the upper cervical spine. Their connection forms the atlantoaxial joint. The atlas, located at the craniocervical junction, lacks a spinous process and vertebral body, and consists of an anterior arch, a posterior arch, and left and right lateral masses forming an elliptical vertebral foramen. The superior articular fossa articulates with the occipital condyles to form the atlanto-occipital joint, while the inferior articular surfaces articulate with the axis to form the atlantoaxial joint [1]. The atlantoaxial joint comprises three small joints: two lateral joints and one median joint. The upper cervical lateral joint is formed by the inferior articular surfaces of the lateral masses of the atlas and the superior articular processes of the axis. The atlantoaxial lateral mass joint is the primary contributor to cervical axial rotation (AR), with a range of motion far exceeding that of other cervical segments. Simultaneously, this joint, together with the atlantoaxial joint, participates in flexion-extension (FE) movements. This unique distribution of motion makes it a key structure for cervical flexibility [2].

However, the complex anatomy and high mobility of this region also make it a common site for injury and degeneration. Clinical studies have shown that the morphological characteristics of the atlantoaxial joint are closely related to its pathological states. For example, morphological changes such as reduced contact area of the lateral mass joints, narrowing of the joint space, and abnormal inclination of the articular surfaces have been identified as important risk factors for atlantoaxial osteoarthritis (AAOA), instability, and secondary vertebrobasilar insufficiency [3, 4]. Trauma, inflammation, degenerative changes, or developmental anomalies can lead to atlantoaxial joint instability or dislocation, often requiring fusion surgery to restore stability [5]. Traditional surgical methods rely on standardized implants, but clinical practice shows that significant anatomical variations among individuals often result in poor matching between implants and bony structures, thereby affecting surgical outcomes. This mismatch can lead to interface micromotion, stress shielding, or stress concentration, increasing the risk of fusion failure, implant loosening, and even intraoperative complications (such as vertebral artery injury) [6, 7]. Studies indicate that even clinically acceptable screw path deviations (≤ 4 mm) can significantly reduce pull-out strength and affect long-term stability [8]. Therefore, obtaining precise three-dimensional anatomical data of the atlantoaxial region is crucial not only for improving surgical accuracy and safety but also for understanding the pathogenesis of diseases in this region, enabling early diagnosis and prevention, providing an important morphological basis.

Three-dimensional spiral CT technology, with its high resolution, isotropic scanning, and powerful three-dimensional reconstruction capabilities, provides an ideal platform for accurately quantifying complex bony structures [9]. Through multi-planar reconstruction (MPR) and three-dimensional modeling, precise measurements of parameters such as linear dimensions, angles, and spatial relationships of the atlantoaxial lateral mass joint can be achieved, providing reliable evidence for investigating the correlation between anatomical variations and diseases, implant design, surgical planning, and biomechanical analysis.

Existing research has mostly focused on the lower cervical spine, with a lack of three-dimensional morphological data for the upper cervical spine. While previous studies have explored the general morphology of the upper cervical spine, this study systematically quantifies the three-dimensional parameters of the atlantoaxial lateral mass joint with a specific focus on sex-related anatomical variations and their implications for personalized facet-interface fusion devices. Utilizing advanced 64-slice three-dimensional spiral CT reconstruction technology, images of the cervical spine in sagittal, axial, and coronal planes collected by our hospital’s imaging department were stored in the hospital’s PACS. Based on these, detailed measurements of the atlantoaxial joint parameters were conducted, focusing on analyzing their basic characteristics and sex differences, and exploring the clinical significance of these parameters in the following aspects: (1) establishing a comprehensive normative anatomical database for the atlantoaxial lateral mass joint, which can serve as a morphological reference for understanding anatomical variations; (2) serving as a morphological reference for the future design of personalized atlantoaxial facet joint interface fusion devices; (3) offering baseline anatomical data for screw implantation path planning in surgical navigation, reducing the risk of neurovascular injury; (4) providing normative geometric data that may aid biomechanical research such as finite element analysis, optimizing implant performance and surgical protocols.

Materials and methods

Study population

This study included 200 patients who underwent cervical three-dimensional CT examinations in the Spiral CT Room of the Imaging Department of the Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University between December 2024 and December 2025. These patients had no history of cervical surgery, and their spiral CT images showed no significant signs of osteophyte formation, spinal stenosis, disc herniation, infection, or tumors. The patients were divided into two groups by sex, 100 each (male average age 41.25 years, female average age 40.78 years), with the same age composition, ranging from 18 to 50 years. Additionally, the height and weight of each subject were recorded at the time of the examination, and the Body Mass Index (BMI) was calculated for all participants. A total of 10 three-dimensional parameters were measured for both lateral mass joints of the atlantoaxial joint in each sample. Studies suggest that the incidence of degeneration in cervical facet joints may be higher in people aged 50 and above, and the severity tends to increase with age [10]. Therefore, this study selected patients under 50 years old to exclude the influence of age-related degenerative changes on the measurement of various parameters of the lateral mass joint.

Methods for observing and measuring the atlantoaxial lateral mass joint

Based on the cervical three-dimensional CT data collected by our hospital’s imaging department for all eligible subjects, this study conducted subsequent work. Through advanced imaging reconstruction processing of these data using three-dimensional spiral CT, we generated thin-slice images suitable for precise measurement, which were uniformly imported into the hospital’s PACS system, ensuring all parameter measurements were performed under standardized conditions. The specific measurement methods were as follows: after logging into the PACS system, entering the subject’s name and image number to retrieve the cervical three-dimensional spiral CT measurement data, the following indicators were measured on the sagittal, axial, and coronal planes of the cervical three-dimensional spiral CT images:

Anteroposterior diameter of the atlantoaxial lateral mass joint: Measured on the sagittal plane of the three-dimensional CT image. The Anteroposterior diameter diameter is defined as the maximum distance between the most anterior and posterior margins of the contact surface between the C1 inferior articular surface and the C2 superior articular surface (Fig. 1).

On the sagittal reconstruction at the maximum cross-sectional area, JSW is defined as the minimum vertical distance between the inferior articular cortex of C1 and the superior articular cortex of C2. The green crosshairs pinpoint the narrowest location within the central weight-bearing region

Measurement of the distance from the transverse foramen to the lateral mass joint midpoint. On the axial CT slice at the level of the C2 superior articular process, the linear distance is measured from the geometric center of the transverse foramen (red crosshair) to the midpoint of the lateral mass joint (blue circle). This parameter defines the safe corridor for screw placement to avoid injury to the vertebral artery

Due to significant individual variations in the atlantoaxial lateral mass joint, this study measured the widest distance for each anatomical site. The measurement function of the PACS system was used to measure sequentially. All bilateral diameters, bilateral joint spaces, bilateral joint inclination angles, and bilateral distances from the transverse foramen to the lateral mass joint were measured twice and recorded, and the average value was taken. Measured lengths were accurate to 0.01 mm, and angles were accurate to 0.1°.

Statistical analysis

To ensure the measurement reliability and reproducibility of the morphometric data, the first author and a senior radiologist with 10 years of experience independently and blindly measured a random subset of 30 patients. To assess intra-observer reliability, the first author repeated these measurements after a two-week interval. Reliability was quantified using the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC), with ICC values categorized as follows: < 0.40, poor; 0.40–0.59, fair; 0.60–0.74, good; and 0.75–1.00, excellent. All measurement data were statistically processed using SPSS 26.0 software. For each subject, bilateral measurements (left and right sides) were averaged to yield a single representative value for each anatomical parameter. Thus, each patient contributed only one data point per parameter to the statistical analysis to avoid unit-of-analysis errors. Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x̄ ± s). Independent samples t-test was used to compare measurement data between groups, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Furthermore, to determine whether sex remained an independent factor after accounting for overall body size, an Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA) was performed for all anatomical parameters, with BMI included as a covariate.

The reliability analysis demonstrated excellent consistency for all measured parameters. The intra-observer ICC values ranged from 0.89 to 0.96, and the inter-observer ICC values ranged from 0.85 to 0.94. These results demonstrate excellent measurement reliability and confirm that the reported measurements are highly reproducible.

Comparison of the morphometric data between male and female participants revealed significant differences across all evaluated parameters (all P < 0.05, Table 1). Male subjects demonstrated consistently larger anatomical dimensions than female subjects in both linear and angular measurements. Specifically, males exhibited significantly greater values for anteroposterior and left-right diameters, joint space width, and the distance from the transverse foramen to the lateral mass joint midpoint. The sagittal inclination angle was also significantly larger in the male cohort compared to females. Notably, ANCOVA confirmed that these sex-related differences remained significant after adjusting for BMI (P < 0.05), indicating that the observed sexual dimorphism is independent of overall body habitus. Reliability analysis showed excellent consistency, with intra- and inter-observer ICC values ranging from 0.85 to 0.96.

Discussion

This study utilized the 64-slice three-dimensional spiral CT reconstruction technology employed by our hospital, combined with the PACS for standardized measurements, ensuring data accuracy and repeatability while obtaining images and data of the atlantoaxial lateral mass joint in healthy adults across different anatomical planes (coronal, sagittal, axial). Previous observations and measurements of the atlantoaxial lateral mass joint were mostly limited to anatomical studies on cadaveric specimens, with fewer imaging studies, primarily focusing on X-ray and conventional CT images [11]. Compared to traditional CT, three-dimensional spiral CT, through technical optimizations (such as multi-circle reverse helical scanning), can meet the imaging needs of long anatomical regions, completing large-scale three-dimensional imaging in a single scan and offering better adaptability to interference from metal implants, etc [12]. Previous research has largely focused on the lower cervical spine (e.g., C2-3 to C6-7 segments), while three-dimensional morphological data for the upper cervical spine are relatively scarce [13]. While previous CT-based studies have investigated C1–C2 morphology and vertebral artery anatomy, this research provides a more focused three-dimensional quantification of the lateral mass joint parameters. By establishing a normative database that accounts for sexual dimorphism, this study fills a specific gap in the anatomical data required for the development of personalized facet-specific implants and precise biomechanical modeling.

The lateral mass joint is the direct bony connection between the atlas (C1) and the axis (C2), participating in the rotational movement of the head and neck [14]. This region supports the weight of the head and enables a wide range of motion through the synergistic action of ligaments, bones, and muscles [15]. Furthermore, the stability of the C1-C2 joint is crucial for maintaining the path of the vertebral artery (VA). Medial encroachment (occurring in approximately 1% of cases) of the VA coursing over the posterior surface of the C1/C2 joint may compress neurovascular structures [16]. In summary, the C1-C2 lateral mass joint plays a central role in cervical stability and motor function through its unique anatomical structure and biomechanical properties. Statistical analysis of all measured parameters in this study confirmed sex-related differences in the sagittal plane anteroposterior diameter (male 16.09 ± 1.39 mm, female 14.58 ± 1.22 mm), coronal plane left-right diameter (male 14.51 ± 1.52 mm, female 13.43 ± 1.20 mm), joint space width (male 2.07 ± 0.43 mm, female 1.71 ± 0.25 mm), distance from the transverse foramen to the lateral mass joint (male 10.51 ± 1.27 mm, female 9.29 ± 1.02 mm), and sagittal inclination angle of the lateral mass joint (male 12.49 ± 1.81°, female 11.52 ± 1.64°), with male values being significantly larger than female values (P < 0.05). This trend is consistent with the measurements of the lower cervical facet joints by Wang QA [17]: in the sagittal plane, the superior-inferior diameter of the CFJ from C2-3 to C6-7 showed significant sex differences, with all parameters larger in males; in the coronal plane, the left-right diameter of the facet joints was very similar, increasing from C2-3 to C6-7 down the cervical spine, and the left-right diameter at each segment was also significantly larger in males than in females. While our study focuses on normal anatomy, the observed sexual dimorphism—specifically smaller joint dimensions in females—provides a morphological context that may be considered in future research investigating the prevalence of atlantoaxial disorders. The analysis in this study further demonstrates that these differences persist even after adjusting for BMI. This suggests that the observed sexual dimorphism in the atlantoaxial lateral mass joint reflects intrinsic biological traits rather than mere differences in overall body habitus.

The differences in anteroposterior and left-right diameters found in this study suggest that the male upper cervical spine has a larger joint contact area, potentially conferring greater mechanical stability. In contrast, the smaller joint space in females may increase the risk of stress concentration within the joint. Studies have shown that the contact area of the lateral mass joint is strongly associated with osteoarthritis; morphological abnormalities such as C2 lateral mass settlement and abnormal joint inclination can alter the joint contact area, leading to local peak pressures, thereby triggering cartilage degeneration and osteophyte formation [3, 4].This morphological difference theoretically provides a structural context that might relate to the higher prevalence of atlantoaxial joint diseases in females, though further clinical and biomechanical studies are required to confirm if female sex is an independent risk factor for these disorders. This aligns with the findings of Suga Y [18]: multivariate regression analysis showed that female sex was an independent risk factor for AAOA (OR = 2.1, p = 0.03), alongside transverse ligament calcification and advanced age. Furthermore, measurement of the articular surface inclination angle is crucial for understanding screw implantation angles, as even minor misplacement (≤ 4 mm) can reduce pull-out strength and increase the risk of postoperative loosening [8]. This study provides precise normative parameters using imaging, which are necessary for defining the baseline against which pathological changes can be measured in future clinical studies. These measurements are the necessary foundation for any subsequent interventional decisions, such as the necessity for personalized surgical design.

In patients with chronic whiplash-associated disorder (WAD), the length of the meniscoids in the atlantoaxial lateral mass joint was significantly shorter than in the healthy control group (control group ventral 6.07 mm, dorsal 7.24 mm; WAD group ventral 5.01 mm, dorsal 6.48 mm) [19], suggesting that shorter meniscoid structures are more prone to WAD, and atlantoaxial joint diseases are related to the length of the meniscoid structures. A larger atlantoaxial lateral mass joint might provide more physical space, theoretically allowing for larger development or existence space for the meniscoid structures, but whether the size of the atlantoaxial lateral mass joint is directly related to atlantoaxial joint diseases requires further research.

Upper cervical fusion is a primary treatment for atlantoaxial joint injuries (such as fractures, dislocations, or degeneration) [20], but traditional methods are limited by individual anatomical variations. Three-dimensional parametric design, by integrating imaging, computational mechanics, and materials science, significantly improves the individual compatibility and long-term efficacy of cervical fusion devices. Future directions include multi-objective optimization under dynamic loads, application of smart materials, and validating the universality of design algorithms through large-scale clinical data for designing fusion devices [21–23]. The design of fusion devices needs to comprehensively consider morphology, width, length, height, material, stability, and bone graft window factors [24]. The three-dimensional parameters measured in this study have the potential to inform the design and implantation of atlantoaxial facet fusion devices: for example, the anteroposterior and left-right diameters determine the length and width of the fusion device, the joint space influences the height design, and the distance from the transverse foramen is used to avoid the risk of vertebral artery injury. Personalized fusion devices based on sex and individual differences can improve interface matching and reduce intraoperative complications [25]. Furthermore, morphological measurement of the atlantoaxial vertebra can obtain key parameters that directly guide the selection of screw size and angle. For example, by analyzing the geometric characteristics of the valid pedicle region, screw path planning can be optimized to reduce the risk of cortical breach [26], lowering the probability of neurovascular injury, aligning with the trend of minimally invasive surgery.

Three-dimensional finite element models play an important role in studying the biomechanical properties of the atlantoaxial joint. Finite element models of the cervical spine (including the atlantoaxial joint) can be used to predict biomechanical responses under different loading conditions, such as ligament strain. By inputting precise three-dimensional geometric data (e.g., arbitrary cervical anatomy, spatial location of ligaments), the model can quantify injury thresholds (e.g., ligament elongation rate) or stress concentration areas, thereby revealing injury mechanisms [27, 28]. In the study by Li J [29], finite element models were established to analyze and compare the biomechanical differences among six fixation techniques, including Harms plating, transoral atlantoaxial reduction plate (TARP), and anterior transarticular screws (ATS), providing a basis for clinical selection of the optimal solution. The data from this study may serve as useful anatomical baseline data for finite element analysis, which could aid in improving the accuracy of finite element models in injury analysis, implant optimization, and biomechanical evaluation by providing anatomical details and mechanical boundary conditions [30].

This study has some limitations: First, the samples were all from a single region (Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University). Although the sample size (200 cases) is relatively large, regional representativeness may be insufficient. Second, all patients were limited to under 50 years old, which avoided the interference of degeneration but did not reflect changes in the elderly population. Future studies are recommended to be multi-center with larger samples, including different age groups and pathological states (e.g., patients with degeneration or trauma), and cross-validated with cadaveric anatomical data to enhance data universality and clinical applicability. Simultaneously, combining biomechanical testing and long-term follow-up to evaluate the effectiveness of fusion device designs is suggested.

Conclusion

This study precisely quantified the three-dimensional morphological parameters of the atlantoaxial lateral mass joint in adults using three-dimensional spiral CT, revealing significant sex differences. The results establish a detailed normative morphological database for the atlantoaxial lateral mass joint.These data offer a foundational anatomical reference that may aid future research and development in personalized implant design, surgical navigation, and biomechanical modeling of the upper cervical spine.
