Healthcare-associated infections and medical malpractice claims in the Veneto Region (2010–2024): a retrospective analysis of regional claims data
Authors: Mario Saia, Laura Salmaso, Alessandro Manzella, Silvia Cocchio, Andrea Cozza, Claudio Terranova, Vincenzo Baldo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHealthcare-associated infections (HAIs) are a major clinical and medico-legal concern, as they are associated with increased morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs. Severe HAIs may lead to malpractice claims related to failures in prevention or management. Although compensation claims capture only a fraction of HAI episodes, they mainly reflect the most serious cases and provide relevant clinical and medico-legal information. This study analyses trends in malpractice compensation claims related to HAIs in the Veneto Region (Italy).MethodsA retrospective analysis of medical malpractice claims was conducted using data from the Healthcare Error Monitoring Information System (HEMIS) in the Veneto Region (Italy) from 2010 to 2024. Claims related to healthcare-associated infections were classified by patient outcome, site of infection, and healthcare setting. Trend analyses were performed using three-year aggregated periods, and descriptive statistics were applied.ResultsAmong 19,380 medical malpractice claims filed over 15 years in the Veneto Region, 1,106 (5.7%) concerned HAIs. While overall claims declined over time, HAI-related claims increased both in absolute terms and proportionally. Non-fatal injuries accounted for 73.9% of HAI-related claims, whereas death-related claims rose from < 10% to > 40% over the study period. Surgical site infections were the most frequent, while bloodstream and respiratory tract infections predominated among death-related claims, the latter largely driven by SARS-CoV-2. Of 561 closed claims, 57.0% resulted in compensation, and total HAI-related compensation amounted to EUR 36.7 million, evenly distributed between death and non-fatal injury cases.ConclusionsHAI-related medical malpractice claims showed a distinct increasing trend over time, contrasting with the decline observed for overall claims, and accounted for a growing proportion of death-related cases. These findings highlight the medico-legal relevance of healthcare-associated infections and support the need for strengthened infection prevention strategies and risk management policies within healthcare systems.

Introduction

Professional liability is the legal responsibility of healthcare professionals or hospitals to provide assistance that meets certain standards of care [1]. When a healthcare professional commits an error that causes harm to a patient – resulting in non-fatal injury or death - it can result in a medical malpractice claim (MMC). In Italy, such liability may be disciplinary, civil, or criminal in nature [2]. Errors made by medical professionals can be due to negligence, imprudence, or incompetence. Negligence refers to a lack of care in the work, imprudence is acting without caution, and incompetence is a lack of technical ability. Professional responsibility may involve a single healthcare professional but often extends to multiple professionals working as a team, or indirectly to the hospital itself [2].

One area of particular concern in healthcare and the medico-legal fields is healthcare-associated infections (HAIs) [3]. HAIs are defined as infections caused by bacteria, fungi, viruses, or other less common pathogens, that are acquired during the provision of healthcare. They can occur in any healthcare setting and are not clinically manifest, nor in the incubation phase, at the time of admission or before the provision of care [4]. More simply HAIs are infections that patients acquire while receiving treatment for other conditions in a healthcare setting [5]. These infections may lead to both clinical and medico-legal consequences. They are associated with higher mortality rates and prolonged hospital stays. From a medico-legal perspective, they may result in increased healthcare costs and a higher number of malpractice claims related to failures in preventing the onset of the infection or to inappropriate management of the infectious episode [6].

The transmission of infection during healthcare requires three elements: the source of infectious microorganisms, a susceptible host, and a means of transmission from the microorganism to the host. The infection can be endogenous when the source is represented by pathogens present within the body, but more frequently exogenous. In this case, the infection is transmitted from the outside through medical equipment or devices, the environment, healthcare personnel, or contaminated drugs [4]. HAIs can occur in a variety of sites, including the urinary and respiratory tracts, bloodstream, and surgical sites [5]. Urinary tract infections may range from asymptomatic bacteriuria to more severe conditions such as pyelonephritis and renal abscesses. Respiratory infections include hospital-acquired and ventilator-associated pneumonia. Bloodstream infections are often associated with invasive devices such as vascular catheters and represent serious and potentially life-threatening conditions [7–9]. In hospital setting, respiratory infections may be device-associated (e.g. ventilator-associated pneumonia) or related to exposure to infected patients. Respiratory infections were, in particular, relevant during the period of the COVID-19 pandemic [10]. Nosocomial bloodstream infections (bacteraemia or sepsis) are a serious and potentially life-threatening conditions that can occur in patients who are hospitalized. These infections are caused by bacteria, viruses, or other microorganisms that enter the bloodstream usually through a catheter or other medical device.

Surgical site infections, a leading cause of malpractice claims in the United States and Italy [11], are a common type of HAI that can occur after surgery [12]. According to the Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), surgical site infections may be classified as an infection stemming from a surgical operation that appears around or near the surgical incision within 30 days of the operation or a year if a prosthetic material is placed during the surgery [13].

Risk factors for healthcare-associated infections include both patient-related characteristics (such as age, comorbidities, and immunosuppression) and healthcare-related exposures, including prolonged hospitalization, intensive care stay, invasive procedures, and the use of medical devices such as urinary or vascular catheters [4, 14].

Regardless of the site of infection or the type of pathogen involved, it is important to approach the study of HAIs with an objective, methodological approach to accurately analyse the phenomenon and identify potential areas for improvement. This includes using standardized definitions and methods for measuring and reporting HAIs, as well as controlling for potential confounders such as patient characteristics and healthcare facility characteristics. By using a rigorous, systematic approach, it is possible to identify the factors that contribute to HAIs and to develop evidence-based strategies for preventing and controlling these infections.

A contribution to the analysis of the phenomenon can be provided by the trend of compensation claims in relation to alleged nosocomial infection episodes. Malpractice claims represent a limited and partial source of information on the overall burden of healthcare-associated infections (HAIs), as only a proportion of HAI episodes result in a formal compensation claim. On the one hand, many infections are mild, successfully treated, or not perceived by patients as attributable to healthcare responsibility; on the other hand, underreporting may occur due to medico-legal factors, such as difficulties in proving a causal link between the infection and healthcare practices, as well as variability in patients’ access to legal remedies.

Despite these limitations, malpractice claims offer several relevant advantages as a data source. They tend to capture the most severe cases, often associated with significant clinical consequences, including prolonged hospitalization, permanent impairment, or death. Moreover, claims files usually contain detailed clinical documentation, expert opinions, and medico-legal assessments, allowing for a structured analysis of both the medical and legal aspects of the infectious event. The standardized nature of claims management also enables comparative evaluations over time and across institutions. For these reasons, the analysis of malpractice claims related to HAIs may provide meaningful insights into high-impact events and recurrent critical issues, complementing epidemiological surveillance systems and contributing to a better understanding of the medico-legal relevance of healthcare-associated infections.

On this basis, the aim of this study was to analyse temporal trends in medical malpractice compensation claims related to healthcare-associated infections in the Veneto Region (Italy) between 2010 and 2024, with a focus on patient outcomes, infection sites, and healthcare settings.

Materials and methods

Setting

The Veneto Region (Italy) has an average population of approximately 4.85 million inhabitants during the study period. Over time, the ageing index increased markedly, from 170 to 203. Acute care and rehabilitation hospital bed capacity averaged 3.17 beds per 1,000 inhabitants, decreasing from 3.25 to 3.06 per 1,000 over the study period. Annual hospital admissions were approximately 626,000, corresponding to a hospitalization rate of around 122 per 1,000 residents and an average length of stay of about 7.5 days. The average bed occupancy rate exceeded 80% between 2016 and 2023 [15].

Study design and data source

This was a retrospective observational study based on medical malpractice claims recorded in the Healthcare Error Monitoring Information System (HEMIS) of the Veneto Region (Italy) between 2010 and 2024. HEMIS is a regional database containing MMCs from public healthcare services, including hospitals as well as out-of-hospital services.

Civil malpractice claims were classified according to the type of harm caused to the patient, including non-fatal injury and death. Compensation claims were further categorized by site of infection—surgical site (SSI), bloodstream (BSI), respiratory tract (RTI), urinary tract (UTI), and other sites—as well as by the type of healthcare setting involved, including surgical wards, medical wards, emergency departments, and maternal and child wards. Claims were classified according to the healthcare setting in which the adverse event was reported to have occurred, as recorded in the HEMIS database. The categories (surgical area, medical area, maternity and paediatric services, emergency services, hospital services, rehabilitation, and out-of-hospital settings) reflect the clinical area involved in the event and include all claims attributed to that setting, regardless of the type of hospitalization. Out-of-hospital categories refer to events occurring outside hospital settings. Sex and age of the patients were also collected.

Additional variables included the time elapsed between the adverse event and the opening of the claim, and, for closed cases, whether compensation was awarded and the amount paid. To reduce year-to-year variability and improve the stability of estimates, trend analyses were conducted by aggregating data into three-year periods.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were generated for all the assessed variables. Continuous variables were presented as mean and standard deviation, and categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages.

Since denominator data such as the number of hospital admissions, surgical procedures, hospital beds, or overall healthcare activity were not available in the HEMIS database, this study was not designed to estimate the incidence of healthcare-associated infections or malpractice claims. Consequently, rates could not be calculated, and analyses were limited to absolute numbers and relative proportions over time. Significant trends over the years considered were assessed as average annual percent changes (AAPC) using the joint-point regression [16]. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 28.0 (IBM Corp Released. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 28.0, IBM Corp, Armonk, NY. 2021).

Data were analysed in anonymized and aggregated form in accordance with Italian regulations on secondary use of administrative healthcare data.

Results

In the 15-year study, 19,380 MMCs were filed in the Veneto Region. Overall, 13,442 (69.3%) claims were related to non-fatal injuries, 2,492 (12.9%) cases concerned death, and the remaining 3,446 (17.8%) referred to other types of damage (i.e., property damages or infringment of other legal rights). Table 1 shows the distribution of MMCs by three-year period. A similar temporal pattern was observed both overall and by type of damage, with an initial increase from the first to the second three-year period, followed by a decrease in subsequent periods. However, the magnitude of the reduction differed between claim types: non-fatal injury claims nearly halved over the study period (− 47.4%), whereas death-related claims decreased by only 5.2%.

Non-fatal injuries concerned 817 out of 1,106 (73.9%) HAI-related MMCs, whereas deaths referred to the remaining 289 (26.1%) cases. Over time, the latter showed a 6-fold increase (from 17 cases in 2010–2012, up to 102 cases in 2022–2024). By contrast, the number of HAI-related non-fatal injury claims fluctuated in the investigation period without a clear trend. Consequently, the proportion of deaths represented less than 10% of HAI-related claims at the beginning of the time series, but it increased to more than 40% in the latest three-year period.

The mean delay time from the HAI to the claim was between 3.0 and 3.7 years in each period, except in 2013–2015 when it increased to 5.0 years (data not shown).

Figure 2 shows the relative frequency of the infection site among HAI-related claims, separately in death and non-fatal injuries. SSIs were the most common infection site among non-fatal injury (59.9%). By contrast, the burden was more uniformly distributed among deaths where BSIs were the most common infection site (30.4%), closely followed by SSIs (24.9%) and RTIs (22.1%).

Discussion

This study contributes to the analysis of healthcare-associated infections (HAIs) in a large Italian region and provides a long-term assessment of medical malpractice claims related to HAIs.

Across the study period, the overall number of compensation claims steadily declined. In contrast, claims specifically attributable to HAI increased over time, both in absolute terms and as a proportion of all claims, rising from 3.9% in 2010–2012 to 9.5% in 2022–2024. This indicates a growing relative contribution of HAI-related claims despite an overall reduction in total compensation requests.

Moreover, even if the relative frequency of death events increased over time among overall MMCs (from 11% to 17%), their increase was much more marked among HAI-related claims (from 10% to 40%). In Italy, during the study period, healthcare liability was influenced by important legislative changes, which may help to interpret the observed trends in malpractice claims. In 2012, Law No. 189/2012 (the Balduzzi Law) introduced the concept of adherence to clinical guidelines in order to limit the widespread practice of defensive medicine. However, the law did not define clear criteria for the identification and selection of such guidelines, resulting in limited effectiveness [1].

A few years later, Law No. 24/2017 (the Gelli-Bianco Law) was enacted to address the further increase in both medical malpractice claims and defensive medicine practices. Our findings seem to support the need for this additional legislative intervention, as the three-year period following the introduction of the Balduzzi law (2013–2015) was characterized by a higher number of overall MMCs in the Veneto Region. By contrast, claims decreased after the introduction of the Gelli-Bianco law. However, this trend was not observed for HAI-related claims, which continued to increase, confirming that HAIs represent both an emerging public health issue [17, 18] and an area of growing medico-legal interest [3, 6, 19, 20]. Indeed, given the growing body of evidence supporting the effectiveness of preventive measures in reducing the risk of healthcare-associated infections (HAIs), the failure to adopt adequate protocols—or, more frequently, the inability to document their proper implementation (since, under Italian law, the burden of proof of correct conduct lies with the hospital)—exposes clinicians to findings of liability for nosocomial infections.

Fault-based liability may arise in case of non-fulfilment of specific hygiene and aseptic procedures during health service provision [3]. A systematic review and meta-analysis have shown that up to 55% of HAIs are preventable [21], even more according to certain estimates [22]. The lack or non-adherence to protocols of prophylaxis and/or prevention of nosocomial infection was the predominant cause of acceptance of claims in a recent study revising a series of sentences by various Italian Courts. Indeed, health facilities were often condemned as they were not able to provide documentary evidence of the use of their protocols [3, 6].

Although the HEMIS database misses epidemiological usefulness, the well-known patient risk factors for HAI are substantially in line with the demographic characteristics of patients who sued the health facilities for alleged HAI-related damages in the Veneto Region. Indeed, according to two study prevalence performed by the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC) among European acute care hospitals, respectively in 2016–2017 [23] and in 2022–2023 [17], the risk for HAI was higher among male patients (adjusted OR 1.1) and HAIs increased over time, both overall (from 6.0% to 7.0%), and, even more, among patients aged 85 or older (from 6.7% to 9.4%). These data, combined with an ageing population and the importance increasingly attributed to well-being in the elderly, might have contributed to the marked mean age increase among HAI-related plaintiffs (from 48.2 ± 19.8 in 2010–2012 up to 60.8 ± 17.8 years in 2022–2024) reported in the current study. Also in terms of infection sites and health care services involved, our findings are substantially in line with what was recently reported in other studies [3, 24], which consistently identify surgical site infections as the most frequent type of healthcare-associated infection. Recent evidence from Italian settings further confirms the high burden of SSIs and highlights their relevance for clinical practice and risk management [25, 26]. Moreover, the latest ECDC surveys addressing HAIs in acute care hospitals [17] and long-term care facilities [27] showed a largely higher prevalence in the hospital environment (7.0% and 3.1%, respectively). Regarding HAI-related MMCs, our study reinforces the view that this is essentially a hospital issue, as only a minority of cases (1.4%) occurred in out-of-hospital settings, and moreover, these were mostly due to SARS-CoV-2 infections.

The COVID-19 pandemic represented another landmark event in our analysis as several factors, such as emergency department congestion, lack of available personal protection equipment, and the recruitment of inexperienced healthcare professionals on an emergency basis [10, 28, 29], might have influenced the findings in that period. Furthermore, the psychological and economic impact of the pandemic may have persuaded patients to seek compensation more frequently [30].

Finally, the above-mentioned legislative changes introduced in Italy in the field of healthcare liability may also help to explain the observed trends in both the time elapsed between the index event and the filing of the claim, and the proportion of closed cases resulting in compensation. Indeed, the three-year period immediately following the introduction of the Balduzzi law was characterized by the longest mean delay between the event and the claim, suggesting that increased media coverage may have encouraged patients to pursue legal action for past events that they would not otherwise have reported. This interpretation is further supported by the finding that the same three-year period showed the lowest proportion of HAI-related closed cases resulting in compensation.

Limits of the study

The main limitation of this study is the lack of certain information within the HEMIS database. For example, the absence of data on length of hospital stay precluded more in-depth analyses. Additionally, information regarding closed claims was incomplete, particularly with respect to the phase of litigation at which the hospital agreed to settle and provide compensation, as well as the number of cases in which liability was ultimately attributed to the hospital or to individual clinicians. Despite these limitations, the study provides robust insights into long-term trends and medico-legal patterns of healthcare-associated infections in a large regional healthcare system.

Conclusions

In the Veneto region, claims related to healthcare-associated infections (HAIs) in medical malpractice cases have shown a steady increase, in contrast to the overall declining trend in malpractice claims. These HAI-related claims are increasingly associated with fatal outcomes. This pattern is consistent with the largely preventable nature of HAIs and with established risk factors, including advanced age, male sex, and exposure to hospital-based care. The observed trends are likely influenced by changes in healthcare liability legislation, as well as by the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on healthcare systems, which may have contributed to the increase in respiratory tract infection-related claims observed in the most recent period. The growing proportion of HAI-related claims highlights their relevance not only as a legal issue in medical malpractice but also as a significant public health concern, reinforcing the need for continued improvement in infection prevention strategies and more effective risk management approaches.
