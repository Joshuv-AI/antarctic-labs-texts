Rational points on K3 surfaces of degree 2
Júlia Martínez-Marín

Abstract
. A K3 surface over a number field has infinitely many rational points over a finite field extension. For K3 surfaces of degree 2, arising as double covers of ℙ 2 \mathbb{P}^{2} branched along a smooth sextic curve, we give a bound for the degree of such an extension. Moreover, using ideas of van Luijk and a surface constructed by Elsenhans and Jahnel, we give an explicit family of K3 surfaces of degree 2 defined over ℚ \mathbb{Q} with geometric Picard number 1 and infinitely many ℚ \mathbb{Q} -rational points that is Zariski dense in the moduli space of K3 surfaces of degree 2.

Primary 14G05; Secondary 14J28, 11G35

1.
Introduction

A K3 surface over an algebraically closed field contains a rational (possibly singular) curve
[
16
, Appendix]
. Therefore a K3 surface over a number field
K
has infinitely many rational points over a finite field extension of
K
over which the rational curve is defined and has a rational point. For a K3 surface
X
of degree 2, i.e. a double cover of
\mathbb{P}^{2}
branched along a smooth sextic curve, we find a bound (independent of
X
and
K
) for the degree of an extension
L
of
K
such that
X
has infinitely many
L
-rational points. It remains open whether the bound is sharp or not.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
X
be a K3 surface of degree 2 over a number field
K
. Then there is a field extension
L/K
with
[L:K]\leq 12
such that
X(L)
is infinite.

We may then wonder whether the set of rational points over a finite field extension of
K
is Zariski dense in
X
. If so, we say that the rational points on
X
are
potentially dense
. Apart from K3 surfaces, all other surfaces of Kodaira dimension
\kappa=0
have potentially dense rational points
[
9
, Proposition 4.2]
,
[
3
, Theorem 3.1]
. For K3 surfaces, it is conjectured that the rational points are also potentially dense. In
[
4
]
, Bogomolov and Tschinkel show that any double cover of
\mathbb{P}^{2}
branched along a sextic curve with a singular point has potentially dense rational points. The blow-up in the preimage of the singular point under the double cover is an elliptic K3 surface. More generally, Bogomolov and Tschinkel proved potential density of rational points for all elliptic K3 surfaces
[
2
]
. Such surfaces have geometric Picard number
\rho\geq 2
.

In
[
13
]
, van Luijk gave the first explicit example of a K3 surface over
\mathbb{Q}
with geometric Picard number
\rho=1
. With this K3 surface, which is of degree 4, he also gave a positive answer to the question of whether there exists a K3 surface over a number field with infinitely many rational points and geometric Picard number 1, asked by Swinnerton-Dyer and Poonen at a workshop in 2002. Using this, he proved the following theorem.

Theorem 1.2

(van Luijk, 2005
[
13
]
)
.

In the moduli space of K3 surfaces of degree 4, the set of surfaces defined over
\mathbb{Q}
with geometric Picard number 1 and infinitely many
\mathbb{Q}
-rational points is Zariski dense.

Using van Luijk’s method, Elsenhans and Jahnel constructed many examples of K3 surfaces of degree 2 with geometric Picard number 1 in
[
7
]
. Modifying slightly one of their examples and using the method developed in the proof of Theorem
1.1
to find infinitely many rational points, we prove the analogue of van Luijk’s theorem for K3 surfaces of degree 2.

Theorem 1.3
.

In the (coarse) moduli space of K3 surfaces of degree 2, the set of surfaces defined over
\mathbb{Q}
with geometric Picard number 1 and infinitely many
\mathbb{Q}
-rational points is Zariski dense.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
is divided into two steps. The first consists of finding a curve of geometric genus 1 lying on the K3 surface. This can always be done up to enlarging the base field by an extension of degree at most 6, as we prove in Lemma
4.1
. The proof of this lemma is constructive, so given an explicit K3 surface
X
of degree 2 over a number field, one can find an explicit extension over which
X
contains a curve of geometric genus 1. In fact, in most cases it will suffice to take the smallest extension
L
of
K
over which the branch curve
B
of
X\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{2}
has
B(L)\neq\emptyset
. This gives a way of finding examples of K3 surfaces (of degree 2) with infinitely many rational points over their base field, and this is the key to proving Theorem
1.3
. See Remark
4.4
for more details on this.

The second ingredient to prove Theorem
1.1
involves showing that there is a degree 2 extension
L
of a number field
K
over which a smooth curve
C
of genus 1 given by
y^{2}=h(x)
for a polynomial
h\in K[x]
of degree 4 has infinitely many
L
-rational points. It is not hard to see that this happens if we allow an extension of degree 4 since we can find a quadratic extension
K^{\prime}
of
K
such that
C(K^{\prime})\neq\emptyset
, so
C
becomes an elliptic curve over
K^{\prime}
. Then we can use Merel’s theorem
[
17
, p. 242]
to see that
C_{K^{\prime}}
must have a non-torsion point over some quadratic extension
K^{\prime\prime}
of
K^{\prime}
, i.e. an extension of
K
of degree 4. In Proposition
4.2
we do both steps at once and show that we only require a degree 2 extension.

1.1.
Outline

We begin in Section
2
with a brief review on K3 surfaces and relevant invariants of algebraic surfaces. In Section
3
, we discuss an example of a K3 surface of degree 2 with no rational points over
\mathbb{Q}
but Zariski dense rational points over a quadratic field extension.
In Section
4
, we prove Lemma
4.1
, which says that a K3 surface of degree 2 over a number field
K
contains a singular curve of geometric genus 1 over an extension
K^{\prime}
of
K
of degree at most 6. We show that the normalisation
C
of this curve is given by
y^{2}=h(x)
, for a separable polynomial
h\in K^{\prime}[x]
of degree 4. Proposition
4.2
now says that
C
becomes an elliptic curve with a non-torsion point over infinitely many quadratic extensions. These two results are combined to prove Theorem
1.1
. In Section
5
, we give an explicit family of K3 surfaces of degree 2 defined over
\mathbb{Q}
with geometric Picard number 1 and infinitely many rational points. Theorem
1.3
follows once we show that this family is Zariski dense in the moduli space of K3 surfaces of degree 2.

2.
Preliminaries

Let
X
be a smooth, projective surface over a field
k
.
The
Néron–Severi group
of
X
is the quotient
\operatorname{NS}X\coloneq\operatorname{Pic}X/\operatorname{Pic}^{0}X,

where
\operatorname{Pic}^{0}X
denotes the connected component of the Picard variety
\operatorname{Pic}X
containing the identity element.
The Néron–Severi group
\operatorname{NS}X
is a finitely generated abelian group
[
10
, p. 7]
.
The
Picard number
of
X
is
\operatorname{rk}\left(\operatorname{NS}X\right)
and its
geometric Picard number
is
\operatorname{rk}\left(\operatorname{NS}X_{\overline{k}}\right)
, where
X_{\overline{k}}=X\times_{k}\overline{k}
.
A
genus 1 fibration
is a morphism
\varphi:X\rightarrow C
onto a smooth, projective, irreducible curve
C
with generic fibre a smooth curve of genus 1. When
\varphi
has a section, we call it an
elliptic fibration
.

A
K3 surface
is a smooth, projective, geometrically integral surface
X
with trivial canonical bundle and
H^{1}(X,\mathcal{O}_{X})=0
. If
X
is a K3 surface, then
\operatorname{Pic}^{0}X
is trivial,
\operatorname{NS}X\cong\operatorname{Pic}X
is torsion free
[
1
, Proposition VIII.3.2]
, and its geometric Picard number is at most 22 (in characteristic 0, it is at most 20)
[
10
, p. 13]
. A
polarised K3 surface of degree

d>0
consists of a K3 surface
X
together with an ample line bundle
\mathcal{L}
such that
\mathcal{L}
is primitive, i.e. indivisible in
\operatorname{Pic}X
, with
\mathcal{L}^{2}=d

[
10
, p. 31]
.
By the Riemann–Roch formula the degree of a K3 surface is always even. For any even number
d>0
there exists a K3 surface of degree
d

[
10
, p. 32]
. K3 surfaces of degree 2 arise as double covers of
\mathbb{P}^{2}
branched along a smooth sextic curve, so they are given in the weighted projective space
\mathbb{P}(1,1,1,3)=\operatorname{Proj}\mathbb{Q}[x,y,z,w]
by equations of the form
w^{2}=f(x,y,z)
, where
f
is a homogeneous polynomial of degree 6 such that the curve
V(f)\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}
given by the vanishing of
f
is smooth.

3.
An example

Consider the hypersurface
X
in
\mathbb{P}(1,1,1,3)
given by

w^{2}=-x^{6}-y^{6}-z^{6}.

This is a double cover of
\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{Q}}
branched along the sextic curve
B=V(-x^{6}-y^{6}-z^{6})
. Using the Jacobian criterion, one can check that
B
is smooth, so
X
is a K3 surface of degree 2. Notice that
X
has no rational points over
\mathbb{Q}
. However, when base-changing to the quadratic extension
\mathbb{Q}(i)
,
X
already attains at least 3 points:
[1:0:0:i]
,
[0:1:0:i]
and
[0:0:1:i]
. In fact,
X(\mathbb{Q}(i))
is infinite, since
X_{\mathbb{Q}(i)}
contains, for example, the line
V(w-ix^{3},y-iz)
. It turns out that the set of
\mathbb{Q}(i)
-rational points
X(\mathbb{Q}(i))
is Zariski dense in
X
. This follows from Proposition
3.1
, since
X
is isomorphic over
\mathbb{Q}(i)
to the surface
Y
in the proposition.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
Y
be the hypersurface in
\mathbb{P}(1,1,1,3)
given by

w^{2}=x^{6}+y^{6}-z^{6}.

Then the set of rational points
Y(\mathbb{Q})
is Zariski dense in
Y
.

Proof.

To show this we use
[
12
, Proposition 3.8]
, which states that if a smooth projective surface
Y
admits two different genus 1 fibrations
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
, then there exists an explicit Zariski closed subset
Z\subset Y
such that if
Y(\mathbb{Q})\backslash Z(\mathbb{Q})\neq\emptyset
then
Y(\mathbb{Q})
is Zariski dense in
Y
.
In our case, it is enough to take
Z
to be the union of the singular fibres of
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
and the sets
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
which we now define. First, for
i=1,2
suppose we have a rational map
\alpha_{i}:Y\dashrightarrow Y
respecting
f_{i}
such that its restriction to the generic fibre
\mathcal{Y}_{i}
is not merely a translation by an element of the Jacobian
J(\mathcal{Y}_{i})
. We define
T_{i}
to be the union of all points
P
lying on smooth fibres of
f_{i}
such that
\alpha_{i}(P)
is a torsion point on the smooth fibre
f_{i}^{-1}(f_{i}(P))
with distinguished point
P
. For more details, see
[
12
, Section 2]
.

Let us therefore consider the following maps:

\displaystyle f_{1}:\ \ Y

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{}\ \mathbb{P}^{1}

\displaystyle[x:y:z:w]\

\displaystyle\mapsto\ [w+x^{3}:y^{3}-z^{3}],

\displaystyle f_{2}:\ \ Y

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{}\ \mathbb{P}^{1}

\displaystyle[x:y:z:w]\

\displaystyle\mapsto\ [w+x^{3}:y^{3}+z^{3}].

Both maps are morphisms outside pure codimension 1 subschemes of
Y
, and since rational maps extend along codimension 1
[
11
, Proposition 4.1.16]
, both
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
extend to morphisms from
Y
to
\mathbb{P}^{1}
. One can check that
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
are genus 1 fibrations by using a similar argument to that in
[
6
, Proof of Theorem 2.1]
.

Consider the lines
M_{1}=V(w-x^{3},y+z)
and
M_{2}=V(w-x^{3},y-z)
contained in
Y
. For each
i=1,2
, note that
M_{i}
is a multisection of
f_{i}
of degree 3, i.e.,
M_{i}.f_{i}^{-1}(P)=3
for general
P\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Following
[
12
, Remark 2.4]
, we consider the rational map
\alpha_{i}:Y\dashrightarrow Y
defined on smooth fibres of
f_{i}
by
\alpha_{i}(P)=R
, where
R
is the unique point on the fibre
F=f_{i}^{-1}(f_{i}(P))
for which
\mathcal{O}_{F}(R)
is isomorphic to the degree-1 line bundle
\mathcal{O}_{F}(M_{i})\otimes\mathcal{O}_{F}(-2P)
.

Consider the point
P=[2:3/2:1:69/8]\in Y(\mathbb{Q})
. Then,
P
is contained in the fibre
F_{1}=f_{1}^{-1}([7:1])
, given by

7(w-x^{3})=y^{3}+z^{3},

and in the fibre
F_{2}:=f_{2}^{-1}([19/5:1])
, given by

19(w-x^{3})=5(y^{3}-z^{3}),

as subschemes of
Y
. Both
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
are smooth curves of genus 1 with a rational point
P
, so they are elliptic curves over
\mathbb{Q}
. One can check computationally that both
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
have trivial torsion subgroups over
\mathbb{Q}
. Since
P
and the divisor corresponding to
\mathcal{O}_{F_{i}}(M_{i})
are defined over
\mathbb{Q}
, the point
\alpha_{i}(P)
is also rational and, by construction, it is contained in
F_{i}
. Therefore,
\alpha_{i}(P)
is a non-torsion point on
F_{i}
for both
i=1,2
, so
P\in Y(\mathbb{Q})\backslash Z(\mathbb{Q})
, where
Z
is the closed subset described above. It follows from
[
12
, Proposition 3.8]
that
Y(\mathbb{Q})
is Zariski dense in
Y
.
∎

4.
Proof of Theorem 1.1

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
X
be a K3 surface of degree 2 over a number field
K
. Then there is an extension
K^{\prime}/K
with
[K^{\prime}:K]\leq 6
such that
X_{K^{\prime}}
contains a singular curve
C
with geometric genus
p_{g}(C)=1
.

Proof.

Since
X
is a K3 surface of degree 2, it is given by
w^{2}=f(x,y,z)
for some homogeneous polynomial
f\in K[x,y,z]
of degree 6. Consider the double covering

\displaystyle X\

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\pi}\ \mathbb{P}^{2}_{K}

\displaystyle[x:y:z:w]\

\displaystyle\mapsto\ [x:y:z],

and denote the branch curve by
B=V(f)\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}_{K}
. Recall that
B
is smooth since
X
is smooth by assumption.

Let
y_{0}
be an arbitrary integer. After potentially changing
y_{0}
at most 5 times, we can assume that
f(x,y_{0},1)
is not constant. Thus
f(x,y_{0},1)
is a polynomial in one variable of degree at most 6. If
x_{0}
is a root of
f(x,y_{0},1)
, we have that
K^{\prime}=K(x_{0})
has degree
[K^{\prime}:K]\leq 6
.
By Bézout’s theorem, the tangent line
\ell
to
B
at
P=[x_{0}:y_{0}:1]
intersects
B
at 6 points (counting multiplicity). Since
\ell
is tangent to
B
at
P
, the intersection of
\ell
and
B
at
P
has multiplicity at least 2. Therefore
\#(B\cap\ell)(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})\leq 5
. Generically, the tangent line to a plane curve at a point is not tangent at any other points of intersection and has multiplicity 2 at the point of tangency. This guarantees that we can choose
y_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that the corresponding point
P=[x_{0}:y_{0}:1]\in B(K^{\prime})
has tangent line
\ell
satisfying
\#(B\cap\ell)(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})=5
, where
x_{0}
,
P
and
K^{\prime}
are defined as above for our fixed
y_{0}
. This means that
\ell
and
B
intersect at
P
with multiplicity 2 and at four other distinct points with multiplicity 1. Note that
\ell=V(ax+by+cz)\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}_{K^{\prime}}
for some
a,b,c\in K^{\prime}
by construction of
K^{\prime}
.

We next prove that the (scheme-theoretic) preimage
C\coloneqq\pi^{-1}(\ell)\subset X_{K^{\prime}}
is a singular curve with geometric genus 1 defined over
K^{\prime}
. First note that we can assume after potentially re-choosing
y_{0}
that the intersection
(B\cap\ell)(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})
does not contain any points with
z
-coordinate zero. Indeed,
B
only contains finitely many such points, since
\#(B\cap V(z))(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})\leq 6
by Bézout’s theorem. Then, on the set of lines in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
, imposing the conditions that the lines must contain one of the points in
(B\cap V(z))(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})
and that they intersect
B
at a point with multiplicity greater than one gives only finitely many lines, but we had infinitely many choices for
y_{0}
and therefore for
\ell
.
Therefore we can now restrict to the affine chart where
z\neq 0
.

Suppose
b\neq 0
. We have that
C=\pi^{-1}(\ell)\subset X_{K^{\prime}}
is given by

w^{2}=f(x,(-ax-c)/b,1)=\alpha(x-x_{0})^{2}(x-x_{1})(x-x_{2})(x-x_{3})(x-x_{4}),

for some
\alpha\in K^{\prime}
, and where
x_{0}\in K^{\prime}
is as above and
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}
are the (pairwise different)
x
-coordinates of the four points in
(B\cap\ell)(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})\backslash\{P\}
. Since
f(x,(-ax-c)/b,1)\in K^{\prime}[x]
, we have that
C
is defined over
K^{\prime}
. One can check that
C
has a singularity at
[x_{0}:(-ax_{0}-c)/b:1:0]
.

If
b=0
, then
a\neq 0
since otherwise,
\ell=V(ax+cz)\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}_{K^{\prime}}
would not contain the point
P=[x_{0}\colon y_{0}\colon 1]
. Then, similarly as above, the preimage
C=\pi^{-1}(\ell)\subset X_{K^{\prime}}
is given by

w^{2}=f(-c/a,y,1)=(y-y_{0})^{2}h(y),

where
y_{0}\in K^{\prime}
is as above and
h\in K^{\prime}[y]
is a separable polynomial of degree 4. In this case,
C
is singular at
[-c/a:y_{0}:1:0]
.

In either case, the curve
C
is of the form
w^{2}=(x-x_{0})^{2}h(x)
for a separable polynomial
h\in K^{\prime}[x]
of degree 4. Setting
w^{\prime}=w/(x-x_{0})
, we get that
C
is birationally equivalent to the curve
\widetilde{C}
given by
(w^{\prime})^{2}=h(x)
, which has genus 1. Thus
p_{g}(C)=p_{g}(\widetilde{C})=1
, which completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
C
be a genus 1 curve over a number field
K
given by
y^{2}=h(x)
, where
h\in K[x]
is a separable polynomial of degree 4. Then there are infinitely many quadratic extensions
L/K
for which
C_{L}
is an elliptic curve with an
L
-rational non-torsion point.

Proof.

Let
\alpha\in K
and consider the field extension
K_{\alpha}=K(\sqrt{h(\alpha)})
. Unless
h(\alpha)=0
,
the curve
C
has at least two rational points over
K_{\alpha}
corresponding to
w=\pm\sqrt{h(\alpha)}
.
Let
J
denote the Jacobian of
C
over
K
. Since
C
is smooth and has genus 1,
C(K_{\alpha})\neq\emptyset
implies that
C_{K_{\alpha}}\cong J_{K_{\alpha}}
and therefore
C(K_{\alpha})\cong J(K_{\alpha})
.
Letting
\alpha
vary in
K
, we get infinitely many quadratic extensions
K_{\alpha}
of
K
over which
J
attains a non-identity point. Assume these are all torsion points. Then Merel’s theorem (see
[
17
, p. 242]
for the statement,
[
15
]
for the proof) tells us they all have orders bounded above by a fixed integer
N
. But since
J
is an elliptic curve, we know that its
m
-torsion points
J[m]\coloneqq\{P\in J(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})\colon[m]P=O\}
satisfy
J[m]\cong\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z},
for any
m\geq 1
. Therefore
J
cannot have infinitely many distinct points of order at most
N
.
This implies that, for infinitely many
\alpha\in K
, the curve
C_{K_{\alpha}}
is an elliptic curve with a non-torsion point.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

By Lemma
4.1
there is an extension
K^{\prime}/K
with
[K^{\prime}:K]\leq 6
such that
X_{K^{\prime}}
contains a singular curve
C/K^{\prime}
of geometric genus 1. By construction of
C
in the proof of the lemma, we may assume it is given by

w^{2}=\alpha(x-x_{0})^{2}(x-x_{1})(x-x_{2})(x-x_{3})(x-x_{4}),

where
\alpha,x_{0}\in K^{\prime}
and
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}\in\bar{\mathbb{Q}}
are pairwise distinct and also different from
x_{0}
. Then, the normalisation
\widetilde{C}
of
C
is given by

w^{2}=\alpha(x-x_{1})(x-x_{2})(x-x_{3})(x-x_{4})\eqcolon h(x),

where
h(x)\in K^{\prime}[x]
.

By Proposition
4.2
there is a quadratic extension
L
of
K^{\prime}
over which
\widetilde{C}_{L}
is an elliptic curve with a non-torsion point
Q
. Taking the subgroup generated by
Q
yields infinitely many points in
\widetilde{C}
over

L
,
and since we have the morphisms

\widetilde{C}\xrightarrow{\nu}C\hookrightarrow X_{K^{\prime}}\rightarrow X,

we also get infinitely many
L
-rational points in
X
. By construction,
L
is an extension of
K
of degree
[L:K]=[L:K^{\prime}][K^{\prime}:K]\leq 2\cdot 6=12
.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

In the proof of Lemma
4.1
, we take the preimage under the double cover
X\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{2}
of a line tangent to the branch curve and this allows us to get infinitely many rational points on
X
over a finite field extension. If instead we had taken a transversal line, intersecting the branch curve at 6 distinct points, this method would not have worked: the preimage of such a line would be a curve
C
of genus 2 by the Riemann–Hurwitz formula, and by Faltings’s theorem
[
8
]
,
C(K)
is finite for any number field
K
.

Remark 4.4
.

The proof of Lemma
4.1
is constructive and gives a way to find examples of K3 surfaces with infinitely many rational points over their base field, as we now explain. Start with a K3 surface
X
of degree 2 over a number field
K
given by
w^{2}=f(x,y,z)
such that the branch curve
B=V(f)
has a rational point. Let
C
be the smooth curve of genus 1 obtained by normalising the singular curve arising from Lemma
4.1
. By construction,
C
is given by
w^{2}=h(x)
, where
h\in K[x]
. If
s\coloneqq h(0)\neq 0
, consider the surface
X^{\prime}
given by
w^{2}=f(x,y,z)/s
. Then, repeating the process for
X^{\prime}
gives a smooth genus 1 curve
C^{\prime}
of the form
w^{2}=h(x)/s\eqqcolon h^{\prime}(x)
, so
h^{\prime}\in K[x]
satisfies
h^{\prime}(0)=1
. This means that
C^{\prime}
has at least two rational points corresponding to
x=0,w=\pm 1
. Then,
C^{\prime}
is an elliptic curve and one can easily determine if one of these points in
C^{\prime}
is non-torsion, in which case
X^{\prime}
has infinitely many
K
-rational points. This is precisely how the surface
X_{0}
, used in the proof of Theorem
5.3
, was found.

5.
K3 surfaces of degree 2 with Picard number 1 and infinitely many rational points

In this section we show that there exists a family of K3 surfaces of degree 2 with geometric Picard number 1 and infinitely many rational points over
\mathbb{Q}
and we use this to prove Theorem
1.3
. We follow closely the ideas of van Luijk in
[
13
]
, where he solved the same problem for quartic K3 surfaces, and we adapt them to K3 surfaces of degree 2.

Let
h\in\mathbb{Z}[x,y,z]
be a (possibly zero) homogeneous polynomial of degree 6, and let
\mathfrak{X}_{h}
be the projective scheme over
\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb{Z}
given by

(1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}73w^{2}=&\ 7(11x^{5}y+7x^{5}z+x^{4}y^{2}+5x^{4}yz+7x^{4}z^{2}+7x^{3}y^{3}+10x^{3}y^{2}z+5x^{3}yz^{2}+4x^{3}z^{3}\\
&+6x^{2}y^{4}+5x^{2}y^{3}z+10x^{2}y^{2}z^{2}+5x^{2}yz^{3}+5x^{2}z^{4}+11xy^{5}+5xy^{3}z^{2}+12xz^{5}\\

We denote its base change to
\mathbb{Q}
and

by

and
\overline{X}_{h}
, respectively. Note that when

, this surface is isomorphic over

to the one in
[

, Corollary 30]

We will first show that the surfaces

are smooth, so they are indeed K3 surfaces. We show it in the same way as van Luijk did in
[

, Theorem 3.1]
for a family of quartic K3 surfaces.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
h\in\mathbb{Z}[x,y,z]
be a homogeneous polynomial of degree 6. Then the surface
X_{h}
is a K3 surface over
\mathbb{Q}
of degree 2.

Proof.

Let
X_{\mathbb{F}_{3}}/\mathbb{F}_{3}
denote the fibre of
\mathfrak{X}_{h}\rightarrow\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb{Z}
over
(3)\in\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb{Z}
, i.e.
X_{\mathbb{F}_{3}}
arises by reduction modulo 3 of
\mathfrak{X}_{h}
.
Since the factor
15h(x,y,z)
vanishes when reducing modulo 3, we can directly check that
X_{\mathbb{F}_{3}}
is smooth over
\mathbb{F}_{3}
. Consider the base change of
\mathfrak{X}_{h}
to the
3
-adic integers
\mathbb{Z}_{3}
, which is projective and flat over
\mathbb{Z}_{3}
.
Since
\mathbb{Z}_{3}
is a discrete valuation ring and the special fibre
X_{\mathbb{F}_{3}}\rightarrow\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb{F}_{3}
is smooth, the generic fibre

X_{h}\rightarrow\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb{Q}_{3}
is also smooth. By the Jacobian criterion, this implies that
X_{h}\rightarrow\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb{Q}
is also smooth.
Since
X_{h}
is a double cover of
\mathbb{P}^{2}
branched along a sextic and we have shown that it is smooth, it is indeed a K3 surface of degree 2.
∎

5.2.
Finding infinitely many rational points

We now find infinitely many
\mathbb{Q}
-rational points on a large set of surfaces (this is made precise in Theorem
5.3
) containing the surface
X_{0}
, defined as in (
1
) setting
h(x,y,z)=0
. To find infinitely many rational points, we follow the techniques described in the proofs of Lemma
4.1
and Theorem
1.1
. Some restrictions on the coefficients of the defining equations for the K3 surfaces of degree 2 will guarantee that everything takes place over
\mathbb{Q}
, so we will not need to enlarge the base field.

There are
{{6+3-1}\choose{3-1}}=28
monomials of degree 6 in 3 variables and, over
\mathbb{Q}
, a choice of a degree 6 polynomial
f\in\mathbb{Q}[x,y,z]
gives a double cover of
\mathbb{P}^{2}
branched along the curve
V(f)
. Therefore, the double covers of
\mathbb{P}^{2}
branched along a sextic curve given by
w^{2}=f(x,y,z)
are parametrised by
M\coloneqq\mathbb{A}^{28}_{\mathbb{Q}}
. Let
X\in M
and let
f
denote the polynomial defining the branch locus; we define the following properties for
X
:

•

(sm) X is smooth;

•

(coeff)
f
has the coefficients of
x^{6},x^{5}y,x^{5}z,x^{4}y^{2},x^{4}yz,x^{4}z^{2}
equal to
0,\frac{77}{73},\frac{49}{73},\frac{7}{73},\frac{35}{73},\frac{49}{73}
, respectively;

•

(inf)
X
has infinitely many rational points;

•

(eqn)
X
is defined by an equation as in (
1
);

•

(pic1)
X
is smooth and has geometric Picard number 1.

Let
M^{\prime}\cong\mathbb{A}^{22}_{\mathbb{Q}}\subset M
be the subset of double covers of
\mathbb{P}^{2}
satisfying (coeff). Let
V\subset M
denote the subset satisfying (sm), i.e.
V
parametrises the K3 surfaces of degree 2 defined over
\mathbb{Q}
. Let
W\subset M
be the subset satisfying (sm) and (coeff), so that
W=V\cap M^{\prime}
.

Theorem 5.3
.

There is a nonempty Zariski open subset
U\subset M^{\prime}
such that every surface
X\in U
defined over
\mathbb{Q}
is a K3 surface of degree 2 with infinitely many
\mathbb{Q}
-rational points.

Proof.

The singular surfaces in
M^{\prime}
form a Zariski closed subset of
M^{\prime}
, so taking its complement we get an open subset
W\subset M^{\prime}
. By the description of
M^{\prime}
above, the surfaces parametrised by
W
are K3 surfaces of degree 2 and, since the coefficient of the monomial
x^{6}
vanishes in their defining polynomial equation, we have
[1:0:0:0]\in X(\mathbb{Q})
for every
X\in W
. Let
X\in W
, with a double cover of
\mathbb{P}^{2}
given by
\pi:X\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{2}

branched along a smooth curve
B_{X}=V(f)\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}
, where
f\in\mathbb{Q}[x,y,z]
is a homogeneous polynomial of degree 6. Then,
X
is given by
w^{2}=f(x,y,z)
. Since
X\in W\subset M^{\prime}
, the coefficients of
x^{5}y
and
x^{5}z
in
f
are fixed to be
\frac{77}{73}
and
\frac{49}{73}
, respectively. Then, the tangent line to the branch curve at
[1:0:0]
is given by

\ell:11y+7z=0.

The equation of this tangent line is the same for every surface in
W
. The curve
C_{X}\coloneqq\pi^{-1}(\ell)=V(w^{2}-f,11y+7z)\subset\mathbb{P}(1,1,1,3)
is contained in
X
and given in the affine chart where
x\neq 0
by

w^{2}=f(1,y,-11/7y).

In this equation, the constant term and the coefficient of
y
are zero (by our previous choice of the coefficients of
x^{6},x^{5}y
and
x^{5}z
), and since the coefficients of
x^{4}y^{2},x^{4}z^{2}
and
x^{4}yz
in
f
are also fixed to be
\frac{7}{73},\frac{49}{73},\frac{35}{73}
, respectively,
y^{2}
has coefficient 1. This means that the point
(y,z,w)=(0,0,0)
or, in projective coordinates, the point
P\coloneqq[1:0:0:0]
, is a double point in
C_{X}
.
Generically,
B_{X}
and
\ell
intersect at exactly five points over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
.
To see this, recall that a polynomial has repeated roots if and only if its discriminant is zero, and this is a closed condition. Since the intersection of
\ell
with the
B_{X}
yields a degree 6 polynomial with a double root at
P
, dividing by the factor corresponding to the double root yields a degree 4 polynomial
g
. The open condition of the polynomial
g
having no repeated roots is equivalent to
\ell
intersecting the
B_{X}
at
P
and four other distinct points.

Let
W^{\prime}
be the open subset of
W
corresponding to the surfaces
X
with branch curve
B_{X}
satisfying
\#(B_{X}\cap\ell)(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})=5
. Then the normalisation
\widetilde{C}_{X}
of
C_{X}
is a smooth curve of genus one, as shown in the proof of Theorem
1.1
, and it is given by

w^{2}=f(1,y,-11/7y)/y^{2},

where the right-hand side is a degree 4 polynomial in the variable
y
with constant term 1. Therefore the points
P_{1}\coloneqq[1:0:0:1]
and
P_{2}\coloneqq[1:0:0:-1]
are rational points in
\widetilde{C}_{X}
. Specifying a
\mathbb{Q}
-rational point, say
P_{1}
, endows
\widetilde{C}_{X}
with the structure of an elliptic curve over
\mathbb{Q}
with origin
O=P_{1}
.

By Mazur’s Theorem
[
14
, Theorem III.5.1]
, the point
P_{2}
has finite order if and only if
mP_{2}=O
for some
m\in\{1,2,\dots,9,10,12\}
. Since
\operatorname{lcm}(1,2,\dots,9,10,12)=2520
, consider the subset of
W^{\prime}
of the surfaces
X
such that
\widetilde{C}_{X}
is a genus 1 curve (and therefore an elliptic curve as seen above) satisfying
2520P_{2}=O
. This is a closed subset, since the equality
2520P_{2}=O
is given in coordinates by polynomial equations depending on the coefficients of the Weierstrass equation for
\widetilde{C}_{X}
. Let
U
be the complement in
W^{\prime}
of this closed subset. Then, for each
X\in U
, the curve
\widetilde{C}_{X}
is an elliptic curve with a non-torsion point
P_{2}
, so
\widetilde{C}_{X}
and hence
X
have infinitely many rational points over
\mathbb{Q}
.

Finally, we show that
U
is nonempty. The surface
S=X_{0}
defined as in (
1
) with
h(x,y,z)=0
is contained in
M^{\prime}
as previously observed. It can be checked that it is smooth and that
\widetilde{C}_{S}
is a genus one curve given by

\widetilde{C}_{S}:w^{2}=\frac{16771780}{1226911}y^{4}-\frac{1540220}{175273}y^{3}+\frac{81451}{25039}y^{2}-\frac{4078}{3577}y+1.

This is an elliptic curve with a non-identity point over
\mathbb{Q}
, since it has at least the two rational points
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
described above. It can be checked computationally (e.g. using
Magma

[
5
]
) that the torsion subgroup of
\widetilde{C}_{S}

is trivial, so the non-identity point we found is a non-torsion point and hence
S\in U
.
∎
