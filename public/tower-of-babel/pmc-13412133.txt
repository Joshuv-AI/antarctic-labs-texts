Introduction

Small bowel obstruction is an acute abdominal condition often requiring emergency surgical intervention and can result from various etiologies [1]. Small bowel obstruction can result from a variety of factors, which are generally classified as either functional or mechanical. Functional causes include ileus, while mechanical causes encompass entities, such as tumors, adhesions, volvulus, foreign bodies, and fecalomas [2]. A fecaloma is defined as a hardened mass of accumulated feces within the intestinal lumen; While fecalomas typically occur in the left colon (sigmoid colon and rectum), right-sided colonic or small bowel fecalomas are exceedingly rare causes of small bowel obstruction [3]. Fecalomas typically develop in individuals with predisposing factors for constipation, including systemic illness, chronic medical conditions, and mental health disorders [4]. Their occurrence in young patients without these risk factors is uncommon [1, 4]. This case report highlights an unusual presentation of small bowel obstruction in a 30-year-old male from Somali, Ethiopia, caused by an ileal fecaloma. The absence of any identifiable predisposing factors for fecaloma formation further underscores the rarity of this case.

Case presentation

A 30-year-old male of Somali–Ethiopian descent, presented to the emergency department with a 2-day history of repeated vomiting, diffuse crampy abdominal pain, and inability to pass stool or flatus. The patient reports a history of intermittent constipation and chews khat regularly. He denies any other chronic illnesses or medication use. His typical diet consists of injera, camel milk, and pasta. The patient lives in a hot climate and states that his fluid intake is often inadequate. He reports no previous surgeries and denies any family history of bowel-related illnesses. The physical exam showed stable vital signs; however, the patient appeared acutely ill and in pain. Key physical exam findings included abdominal distension with hypertympany and mild periumbilical tenderness. Digital rectal examination revealed an empty rectum and normal sphincter tone. Laboratory investigations revealed normal complete blood count (CBC), organ function tests, and serum electrolyte levels. Random blood glucose (RBS) was 145 mg/dL. HIV serology was negative. Abdominal radiographs (as shown in Fig. 1) demonstrate multiple dilated loops of small bowel, located both centrally and peripherally, with multiple air–fluid levels. There is a paucity of gas in the rectum. No free intraperitoneal air is identified. These findings are suggestive of small bowel obstruction (SBO). Fecal mottling is noted within the small bowel, most likely in the ileum.

Abdominal X-ray showing multiple air–fluid levels, dilated bowel loops, and right-sided fecal mottling

Following the diagnosis of SBO, small bowel volvulus was the primary clinical suspicion. Upon presentation, the patient exhibited signs of an acute abdomen, including central abdominal distension and periumbilical tenderness on physical examination. Given these findings, and considering that small bowel volvulus is the predominant cause of small bowel obstruction across all age groups in our local epidemiology, it was our primary clinical suspicion. Plain abdominal radiographs further supported this, revealing multiple air–fluid levels consistent with small bowel obstruction. Conservative management was bypassed to avoid surgical delay and mitigate the risk of strangulation. After obtaining informed written consent, the patient underwent an urgent exploratory laparotomy. Intraoperative examination (Fig. 2) revealed a hard, impacted fecaloma palpable in the distal ileum. The consistency of a fecaloma was stony hard, desiccated, and calcified mass which was identified occupying and completely obstructing the distal ileum. The small bowel was dilated proximal to the impaction, and the colon was collapsed distal to the ileocecal valve. A small enterotomy was created on the antimesenteric border of the distal ileum, proximal to the identified fecaloma. The fecaloma was carefully extracted. The enterotomy was then closed primarily in a transverse fashion in two layers using absorbable suture. The patient tolerated the procedure well, demonstrated clinical improvement, and was discharged home after 1 week of hospital stay. The patient was seen for follow-up one week after discharge. The surgical wound was clean and healing well; he was passing flatus and feces, tolerating oral intake, and reported no new complaints. He was counseled on smoking and nutritional and lifestyle counseling for the prevention of fecaloma recurrence, and for primary prevention in other patients, included recommendations for a high-fiber diet incorporating locally available foods, such as teff, vegetables, and legumes. This was complemented by advice on adequate water intake, regular physical exercise, and prompt healthcare consultation for any symptoms suggestive of constipation, ensuring appropriate evaluation. At the one-month follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic and had resumed his daily activities. He reported passing normal, formed stools three times per day and was subsequently discharged from the clinic with nutritional advice. The clinical timeline is summarized in Table 1.

Discussion

Fecalomas, first described in 1967 [5], are characterized by the accumulation and hardening of fecal material within the intestinal lumen, forming a mass that can lead to obstruction [6]. While fecalomas typically cause obstruction in the large intestine, their formation in the small intestine and subsequent small bowel obstruction, particularly in young patients, is a rare occurrence, as seen in our case [1]. Fecalomas are extremely rare in the small bowel and far less common in the right colon than the left [7]. This difference stems from the small bowel's softer stool consistency and larger diameter, factors that minimize impaction compared to the narrower left colon [8]. Literature documenting fecalomas in the small intestine remains exceedingly scarce, potentially contributing to diagnostic challenges. Due to its atypical location, small intestinal fecaloma may be easily overlooked in favor of more common causes of small bowel obstruction, resulting in delayed or missed diagnosis [9]. Small bowel fecalomas predominantly occur in older adults [1]. An exception to this is a case reported by Mushtaq et al. in 2015, describing a 30-year-old patient with no identifiable risk factors, a demographic profile closely matching that of our present 30-year-old patient [9].

Fecaloma formation is associated with numerous systemic and local factors, including chronic medical conditions, such as hypertension and diabetes, mental disorders, congenital disorders like Hirschsprung’s disease, and neoplastic or inflammatory conditions [10, 11]. However, in contrast to these established risk factors, our patient presented with no identifiable predisposing conditions.

Our patient presented with an acute abdomen secondary to small bowel fecaloma. While this is one possible presentation, patients with fecalomas can exhibit a variety of symptoms [3]. Although not present in our case, other reported manifestations include urinary retention [12], stercoral colitis, and abdominal mass [13]. Fecalomas may also present with nonspecific abdominal pain and overflow-type diarrhea [14].

The varied and nonspecific presentations of fecaloma often necessitate extensive diagnostic workups to exclude other etiologies of small bowel obstruction. While abdominal CT scans can be helpful in identifying a characteristic intraluminal mass suggestive of fecaloma, resource limitations precluded its use in our setting [15]. Instead, our evaluation was limited to standard investigations and risk stratification, which did not reveal any abnormalities.

When a fecaloma is confirmed as the cause of small bowel obstruction, management strategies are debated. Conservative management, including hydration, nasogastric tube decompression, enemas, laxatives, and digital evacuation, is sometimes suggested if the diagnosis is made preoperatively [3, 5]. However, these measures are often ineffective once complete intestinal obstruction develops. In our case, as we did not achieve a preoperative diagnosis and the patient presented with a complete intestinal obstruction, surgical intervention involving removal of the fecaloma via enterotomy was necessary [3, 9]. Endoscopic removal can also be considered as an alternative treatment option, depending on the location of the fecaloma [16], although this approach was not feasible in our setting. Following surgical treatment, most patients achieve excellent outcomes [9]. Our patient demonstrated similar improvement, experiencing no complications during acute treatment or subsequent follow-up.

Conclusion

Ileal fecalomas, though rare, are a potential cause of small bowel obstruction in young patients, often presenting without any predisposing or readily identifiable risk factors. Given this unexpected occurrence, a high index of suspicion is crucial for timely diagnosis. Prompt and appropriate surgical intervention to relieve the obstruction and remove the fecaloma is paramount.
