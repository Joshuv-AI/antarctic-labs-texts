Bilinearization and solutions of the fourth-order lattice Gel’fand-Dikii type equations
Song-lin Zhao, Han Wang, Da-jun Zhang

Abstract
In this paper we derive bilinear forms and solutions in Casoratians for some fourth-order lattice Gel’fand-Dikii (lattice GD-4) type equations. These equations were recently formulated from the direct linearization approach and exhibit multidimensionally consistent property in multi-component form. The obtained solitons and Casoratian forms enable us to extend these equations by introducing a parameter δ \delta . These δ \delta -extended lattice GD-4 type equations are still consistent around the cube, and their bilinear forms together with Casoration solutions are presented. Keywords: fourth-order lattice Gel’fand-Dikii type equation; bilinearization, Casoratian solution



1
Introduction

Compared with the continuous and differential-difference case,
the discrete Gel’fand-Dikii (GD) type equations play more important roles
in the research of discrete integrable systems.
This is because it is too hard to derive them as reductions from the lattice Kadomtsev-Petviashvili (KP) equations.
This makes the difficulty in particular in understanding the high order lattice GD-type equations
(e.g. the lattice Boussinesq (BSQ)-type)
from the lattice KP equations.
The discretization (in both space and time) of the GD hierarchy was firstly proposed in
[
15
]

from the perspectives of direct linearization approach,
where the resulting lattice hierarchy takes
the (regular) quadrilateral lattice potential KdV (lpKdV) equation and
the
3\times 3
stencil lattice potential BSQ (lpBSQ) equation as two simplest members
in the second order and third order, respectively.
They have Miura-related counterparts,
namely, the lattice potential modified KdV (lpmKdV) equation
[
14
]

and lattice potential modified BSQ (lpmBSQ) equation
[
15
]
.
In addition, there are also lattice Schwarzian KdV (lSKdV) equation
[
14
]

and lattice Schwarzian BSQ (lSBSQ) equation
[
13
]
.
For these KdV type and BSQ type lattice equations,
it has been presented many interesting results in the last couple of decades.
The lattices KdV type equations are included in the
Adler-Bobenko-Suris (ABS) list
[
1
]
which are consistent-around-cube (CAC).
The lattice BSQ type equations are also CAC in three-component form
[
4
,
19
,
22
,
25
]
.
For the latter equations one can refer to the review paper
[
9
]
and references therein.
In addition, Lagrangian multiform structure for the generic member of lattice
GD hierarchy was investigated in
[
12
]
.
This structure was considered as a Lagrangian 2-form
when embedded in a higher dimensional lattice, obeying a closure relation.
In
[
2
]
, Atkinson et al. introduced an integrable multicomponent
lattice modified GD hierarchy and studied its Lagrangian formulation.
In addition, continuum limits for the Lagrangians of the ABS equations and lattice BSQ type equations
were also discussed in
[
21
]
.
Recently, in
[
18
]
, Tela et al. utilized the direct linearization approach to present
some lattice fourth-order GD (GD-4) type equations in quadrilateral form,
which are related to a quartic discrete dispersion relation (see Eq.(
2.1
)).
These lattice equations can be viewed as higher-order members in the lattice GD hierarchy,
while represent the regular, modified and Schwarzian type equations,
but appear as much more complicated forms of five-component difference equations.
Compared with the lattice BSQ case (see
[
4
,
9
,
25
]
) that are all multidimensionally consistent,
the lattice GD-4 type equations have something special
[
18
]
.
More concretely,
the number of equations in a quadrilateral lattice GD-4 system
has to be bigger than the number of dependent variables,
so that the evolution and the multidimensional consistency property of the system
are guaranteed simultaneously.
These lattice GD-4 type equations are expected to play more roles in understanding discrete integrable systems.
More recently, in
[
20
]
,
periodic reductions and Laurent property for a lattice GD-4 equation were investigated, where
a Somos-like integer sequence for the associated quadrilinear equations was established.

In fact, not only the nonlinear discrete equations are difficult to study,
even it is not clear enough how their bilinear forms are related to
the bilinear lattice KP equation, namely, the Hirota equation or the Hirota-Miwa equation.
A recent progress is due to
[
23
]

which builds the connections between the bilinear forms of some ABS equations
and the Hirota-Miwa equation.
However, for the lattice BSQ-type equations, how their bilinear forms
(see
[
9
]
) are obtained as reductions from the Hirota-Miwa equation is not known yet.

The purpose of this paper is to explore the lattice GD-4 type equations obtained in
[
18
]

from a bilinear perspective.
Hirota’s bilinear method
[
10
,
11
]

has been proved to be a powerful tool in the research of integrable systems.
The idea of this method is to introduce new variables to transform
the nonlinear differential/difference equations into bilinear forms,
for which the solutions would be obtained in explicit forms.
For the lattice equations in the GD hierarchy,
some ABS equations have been bilinearized and solutions are presented in Casoratians
[
6
]
.
The study was extended to the lattice BSQ type equations
in a series of papers
[
7
,
8
,
16
]

and a comprehensive review can be found in
[
9
]
.
So far the bilinearization of the lattice GD-4 type equations is still untouched.
In this paper we will study the lattice GD-4 (A-2), (B-2) and (C-3) equations obtained in
[
18
]

and present their bilinear forms and Casoratian solutions.
Note that these names of equations follow those given by Hietarinta
for the lattice BSQ type equations
[
4
]
.

The paper is organized as follows.
Section
2
concerns some preliminaries, involving the lattice GD-4 type equations,
discrete bilinear forms, and Casoratians.
Section
3
is contributed to the bilinearization and
Casoratian solutions of the GD-4 (B-2) equation.
Moreover, a parameter-extension of the GD-4 (B-2) equation
is obtained and several types of exact solutions are constructed, including solitons, Jordan-block solutions,
and quasi-rational solutions.
Sections
4
and
5
are devoted to the bilinearization and Casoratian solutions to
the GD-4 (A-2) and GD-4 (C-3) equations, respectively.
Conclusions are given in Section
6
.
There are four appendices where some formulae for the shifts of Casoratians are listed.

2.2
Discrete Hirota’s bilinear form and Casoratians

Let us first recall discrete Hirota’s bilinear form and its gauge property.
One can also refer to
[
6
]
for more details.

We say that an equation is in discrete Hirota’s bilinear form if it can be expressed as

\sum_{j}\,c_{j}\,f_{j}(n+\nu_{j}^{+},m+\mu_{j}^{+})\,g_{j}(n+\nu_{j}^{-},m+\mu_{j}^{-})=0,

(2.8)

where the index sums
\nu_{j}^{+}+\nu_{j}^{-}=\nu^{s},\mu_{j}^{+}+\mu_{j}^{-}=\mu^{s}
do not depend on
j
.
Such an equation is gauge invariant, i.e.,
if a set of functions
\{f_{j},g_{j}\}
solve (
2.8
),
then, so do the gauge transformed functions

f^{\prime}_{j}(n,m)=A^{n}B^{m}\,f_{j}(n,m),\quad g^{\prime}_{j}(n,m)=A^{n}B^{m}\,g_{j}(n,m),

(2.9)

where
A
and
B
are independent of
(n,m)
.

A Casoratian (or Casorati determinant) is a discrete version of a Wronskian, defined as

\displaystyle|\boldsymbol{\psi}(n,m,l_{1}),\boldsymbol{\psi}(n,m,l_{2}),...,\boldsymbol{\psi}(n,m,l_{N})|=|l_{1},l_{2},...,l_{N}|,

(2.10)

with the basic column vector
\boldsymbol{\psi}(n,m,l)=(\psi_{1}(n,m,l),\psi_{2}(n,m,l),\ldots,\psi_{N}(n,m,l))^{T}
.
Following the shorthand notation given in
[
3
]
, some
N
-th order Casoratians that we
often use are denoted by

\displaystyle|0,1,...,N-1|=|\widehat{N-1}|,\quad|0,1,...,N-2,N|=|\widehat{N-2},N|,

\displaystyle|0,1,...,N-3,N-1,N|=|\widehat{N-3},N-1,N|,

etc.

In the procedure of the verification of a solution in Casoratian form for a bilinear equation,
one needs some determinantal identities to simplify high order shifts.
Finally, the bilinear equation to be verified is reduced to the Plücker relation
(Laplace expansion of a zero-valued determinant).
Let us go through these determinantal identities.

Proposition 1
.

[
24
]

Let
\boldsymbol{\Xi}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
and denote its column vectors as
\{\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{j}\}
;
let
\boldsymbol{\Omega}=(\Omega_{i,j})_{N\times N}
be an operator matrix (i.e.
\Omega_{i,j}
are operators),
and denote its column vectors as
\{\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{j}\}
. The following relation holds

\displaystyle\sum^{N}_{j=1}|\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{j}*\boldsymbol{\Xi}|=\sum^{N}_{j=1}|(\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\hbox{\tiny\it{T}}})_{j}*\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\hbox{\tiny\it{T}}}|,

(2.11)

where

\displaystyle|\boldsymbol{A}_{j}*\boldsymbol{\Xi}|=|\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{j-1},~\boldsymbol{A}_{j}\circ\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{j},~\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{j+1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{N}|,

and
\boldsymbol{A}_{j}\circ\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{j}
stands for

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}_{j}\circ\boldsymbol{B}_{j}=(A_{1,j}B_{1,j},~A_{2,j}B_{2,j},\cdots,A_{N,j}B_{N,j})^{\hbox{\tiny\it{T}}},

in which
\boldsymbol{A}_{j}=(A_{1,j},~A_{2,j},\cdots,A_{N,j})^{\hbox{\tiny\it{T}}},~\boldsymbol{B}_{j}=(B_{1,j},~B_{2,j},\cdots,B_{N,j})^{\hbox{\tiny\it{T}}}

are
N
-th order vectors.

Proposition 2
.

[
5
]

Let
\boldsymbol{P}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times(N-1)}
,
\boldsymbol{Q}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times(N-k+1)}
be the remained of
\boldsymbol{P}

after removing its arbitrary
k-2
columns where

3\leq k<N
, and
\boldsymbol{a}_{i},~i=1,2,\cdots,k
, be
N
-th order column vectors. Then one has

\displaystyle\sum^{k}_{i=1}(-1)^{i-1}|\boldsymbol{P},\boldsymbol{a}_{i}|\cdot|\boldsymbol{Q},\boldsymbol{a}_{1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{a}_{i-1},\boldsymbol{a}_{i+1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{a}_{k}|=0,\quad k\geq 3.

(2.12)

When
k=3
, (
2.12
) yields the usual identity used in Wronskian/Casoratian verification
[
3
]
:

\displaystyle|\boldsymbol{M},\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}||\boldsymbol{M},\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{d}|-|\boldsymbol{M},\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{c}||\boldsymbol{M},\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{d}|+|\boldsymbol{M},\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{d}||\boldsymbol{M},\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{c}|=0,

(2.13)

where we have taken
\boldsymbol{P}=(\boldsymbol{M},\boldsymbol{a})
,
\boldsymbol{Q}=\boldsymbol{M}
,

\boldsymbol{a}_{1}=\boldsymbol{b}
,
\boldsymbol{a}_{2}=\boldsymbol{c}
and
\boldsymbol{a}_{3}=\boldsymbol{d}
.

In order to construct solutions in Casoratian form for the lattice GD-4 type equations,
we extend the basic column vector
\boldsymbol{\psi}(n,m,l)
with two more lattice variables

\alpha,\beta
corresponding to the spacing parameters

and thus we introduce an extension of the basic ingredients

\psi_{s}{(n,m,\alpha,\beta,l)}=\sum_{j=1}^{4}\rho_{j,s}^{(0)}(-\omega_{j}(k_{s}))^{l}(p-\omega_{j}(k_{s}))^{n}(q-\omega_{j}(k_{s}))^{m}(a-\omega_{j}(k_{s}))^{\alpha}(b-\omega_{j}(k_{s}))^{\beta},

(2.14)

where

represent the four roots of the quartic dispersion relation curve (
2.1

with

In addition, following the operations given in

(2.15)

Since

\displaystyle f(n,m,l+1,\alpha,\beta)=\bar{f},\quad f(n,m,l,\alpha+1,\beta)=\accentset{\circ}{f},\quad f(n,m,l,\alpha,\beta+1)=\accentset{\bullet}{f}.

we will make use of the

\mathcal{A}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccccc}a_{0}&0&0&\cdots&0&0\\

This is called a

All such matrices (of same order) compose a commutative set
\mathcal{H}

and the subset

\mathcal{F}=\big\{\mathcal{A}\big|~\big.\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{H},~|\mathcal{A}|\neq 0\big\}

Such kind of matrices play useful roles in the expression of exact solution for multiple-pole equations.
For more properties of such matrices one can refer to Refs.

3.3
Examples of exact solutions

Since the transformation (
3.12
) to connect
the GD-4 (B-2) equation (
3.1
) and its
\delta
-extension (
3.13
) is invertible,
in the following we will briefly consider some typical examples of
the exact solutions of the
\delta
-extended GD-4 (B-2) equation (
3.13
).
The first step to get them is to solve the CES (
3.18
).
From (
3.18
) we know that

\boldsymbol{\phi}_{[\boldsymbol{K}]}=\sum_{j=1}^{4}\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}^{(0)}(\delta\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j}(\boldsymbol{K}))^{l}(p\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j}(\boldsymbol{K}))^{n}(q\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j}(\boldsymbol{K}))^{m}(a\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j}(\boldsymbol{K}))^{\alpha}(b\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j}(\boldsymbol{K}))^{\beta},

(3.23)

in which
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j}(\boldsymbol{K}),j=1,2,3,4
are solutions to the matrix equation

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{G}(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{K}):=\boldsymbol{\omega}^{4}-\boldsymbol{K}^{4}+\alpha_{3}(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{3}-\boldsymbol{K}^{3})+\alpha_{2}(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{2}-\boldsymbol{K}^{2})+\alpha_{1}(\boldsymbol{\omega}-\boldsymbol{K})=0,

(3.24)

where
\boldsymbol{I}
is the
N
-th order identity matrix and
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}^{(0)},j=1,2,3,4
are constant column vectors.
Note that
\{\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j}(\boldsymbol{K})\}_{j=1}^{4}
commute with respect to matrix multiplication.

\boldsymbol{K}
is an arbitrary constant matrix in (
3.23
).
We claim that
\boldsymbol{K}
and its similar matrix
\boldsymbol{L}
yield same solutions to
the
\delta
-extended GD-4 (B-2) equation (
3.13
).
In fact, we suppose
\boldsymbol{L}
is any matrix that is similar to
\boldsymbol{K}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{L}=\boldsymbol{T}\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{T}^{-1},

(3.25)

where
\boldsymbol{T}
is the transform matrix. Taking (
3.25
) into (
3.23
) and noting that

\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j}(\boldsymbol{L})=\boldsymbol{T}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j}(\boldsymbol{K})\boldsymbol{T}^{-1}
, we denote
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}^{(0)}\boldsymbol{T}^{-1}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}^{(0)}
and then have

\boldsymbol{\phi}_{[\boldsymbol{L}]}=\boldsymbol{\phi}_{[\boldsymbol{K}]}\boldsymbol{T}^{-1}
.
It is apparent that
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{[\boldsymbol{L}]}
still satisfies the CES (
3.18
)
with
\boldsymbol{K}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{L}
.
In light of the following relations

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{[\boldsymbol{L}]})=|\boldsymbol{T}|f^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{[\boldsymbol{K}]}),~~g^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{[\boldsymbol{L}]})=|\boldsymbol{T}|g^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{[\boldsymbol{K}]}),~~h^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{[\boldsymbol{L}]})=|\boldsymbol{T}|h^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{[\boldsymbol{K}]}),~~s^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{[\boldsymbol{L}]})=|\boldsymbol{T}|s^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{[\boldsymbol{K}]})

\displaystyle\mu^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{[\boldsymbol{L}]})=|\boldsymbol{T}|\mu^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{[\boldsymbol{K}]}),~~\nu^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{[\boldsymbol{L}]})=|\boldsymbol{T}|\nu^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{[\boldsymbol{K}]}),~~\theta^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{[\boldsymbol{L}]})=|T|\theta^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{[\boldsymbol{K}]}),

as well as the transformation (
3.11
),
one can easily find that
the above Casoratians composed by
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{[\boldsymbol{K}]}
and by
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{[\boldsymbol{L}]}

lead to same solutions for the
\delta
-extended GD-4 (B-2) equation (
3.13
).
In what follows we can suppose
\boldsymbol{K}
in (
3.23
) is its canonical form.

Now let us start from the following column vector:

\boldsymbol{\phi}=\sum_{j=1}^{4}\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}^{(0)}(\delta\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j}(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}))^{l}(p\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j}(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}))^{n}(q\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j}(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}))^{m}(a\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j}(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}))^{\alpha}(b\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j}(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}))^{\beta},

(3.26)

where
\boldsymbol{K}
in (
3.23
) is already replaced by its canonical form
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
.
Then various exact solutions, including soliton solutions, Jordan-block solutions and quasi-rational solutions,
for the
\delta
-extended GD-4 (B-2) equation (
3.13
)
can be derived from different eigenvalue structures of matrix
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
.
Let us list them case by case.

Soliton solutions:
If
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
is a diagonal matrix

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Gamma}=\text{diag}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\dots,\gamma_{N})

(3.27)

with

\displaystyle\gamma_{s}=-(\delta^{4}-k_{s}^{4})+\alpha_{3}(\delta^{3}-k_{s}^{3})-\alpha_{2}(\delta^{2}-k_{s}^{2})+\alpha_{1}(\delta-k_{s}),\quad s=1,2,\dots,N,

(3.28)

then the basic elements
\{\phi_{s}{(n,m,\alpha,\beta,l)}\}

are given by (
2.15
). The resulting Casoratians (
2
) yield
the
N
-soliton solutions. In particular, the one-soliton solution reads

\displaystyle u^{\prime}_{0}=\frac{\tau^{[1]}_{1}}{\tau_{1}},\quad u^{\prime}_{1,0}=\frac{\tau^{[2]}_{1}}{\tau_{1}},\quad u^{\prime}_{2,0}=\frac{\tau^{[3]}_{1}}{\tau_{1}},\quad u^{\prime}_{0,1}=0,\quad u^{\prime}_{0,2}=0,

(3.29)

where

\tau_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{4}\rho_{i,j},~~~\tau^{[\ell]}_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{4}\zeta^{\ell}_{i,j}\rho_{i,j},~~~\zeta_{i,j}=\delta-\omega_{j}(k_{i})

(3.30a)

and
\rho_{i,j}
are the plane-wave factors defined as

\displaystyle\rho_{i,j}=\rho_{i,j}^{(0)}(p-\omega_{j}(k_{i}))^{n}(q-\omega_{j}(k_{i}))^{m}(a-\omega_{j}(k_{i}))^{\alpha}(b-\omega_{j}(k_{i}))^{\beta},~~\rho_{i,j}^{(0)}\in\mathbb{C}.

(3.30b)

Besides, the two-soliton solutions can be given by (
2
) together with

\displaystyle f^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{2}(-1)^{i+1}\tau_{i}\tau^{[1]}_{i+1},\quad g^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{2}(-1)^{i+1}\tau_{i}\tau^{[2]}_{i+1},\quad h^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{2}(-1)^{i+1}\tau_{i}\tau^{[3]}_{i+1},

(3.31a)

\displaystyle\mu^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{2}(-1)^{i+1}\tau_{i}\tau^{[4]}_{i+1},\quad s^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{2}(-1)^{i+1}\tau^{[1]}_{i}\tau^{[2]}_{i+1},\quad\theta^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{2}(-1)^{i+1}\tau^{[1]}_{i}\tau^{[3]}_{i+1},

(3.31b)

and
\nu^{\prime}=0
, where
\zeta_{3,j}=\zeta_{1,j}
and
\rho_{3,j}=\rho_{1,j}
.
It is worthy to note that all functions are non-zero for

.

Jordan-block solutions:
If
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
is a lower triangular Toeplitz matrix of the form

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Gamma}=(\gamma_{s,l}(k_{1}))_{N\times N},\quad\gamma_{s,l}(k_{1})=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{\partial_{k_{1}}^{s-l}\gamma_{1}}{(s-l)!},&&s\geq l,\\
&0,&&s<l,\end{aligned}\right.

(3.32)

then the basic column vector
\boldsymbol{\phi}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}=\sum_{j=1}^{4}\mathcal{A}_{j}\mathcal{P}_{j},

(3.33)

where
\mathcal{P}_{j}=(\mathcal{P}_{j,0},\mathcal{P}_{j,1},\dots,\mathcal{P}_{j,N-1})^{\hbox{\tiny\it{T}}}
with

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{j,s}=\frac{1}{s!}\partial_{k_{1}}^{s}[\rho^{(0)}_{j,1}(\delta-\omega_{j}(k_{1}))^{l}(p-\omega_{j}(k_{1}))^{n}(q-\omega_{j}(k_{1}))^{m}(a-\omega_{j}(k_{1}))^{\alpha}(b-\omega_{j}(k_{1}))^{\beta}],

and
\{\mathcal{A}_{j}\}
are lower triangular Toeplitz matrices of the form (
2.17
).
In this case, Jordan-block solutions (i.e. multiple-pole solutions)
can be obtained.

Quasi-rational solutions:
In principle, this type of solutions can be obtained
from the Jordan-block solutions by taking limit
k_{1}\rightarrow 0
(cf.
[
17
]
). Thus we
take
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Gamma}=(\gamma_{s,l}(0))_{N\times N},\quad\gamma_{s,l}(0)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{\partial_{k_{1}}^{s-l}\gamma_{1}}{(s-l)!}\bigg|_{k_{1}=0},&&s\geq l,\\
&0,&&s<l.\end{aligned}\right.

(3.34)

In this case, quasi-rational solutions can be generated via

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}=\sum_{j=1}^{4}\mathcal{A}_{j}\mathcal{Q}_{j},

(3.35)

in which
\mathcal{Q}_{j}=(\mathcal{Q}_{j,0},\mathcal{Q}_{j,1},\dots,\mathcal{Q}_{j,N-1})^{\hbox{\tiny\it{T}}}
with

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{j,s}=\frac{1}{s!}\partial_{k_{1}}^{s}[\rho^{(0)}_{j,1}(\delta-\omega_{j}(k_{1}))^{l+l_{0}}(p-\omega_{j}(k_{1}))^{n}(q-\omega_{j}(k_{1}))^{m}(a-\omega_{j}(k_{1}))^{\alpha}(b-\omega_{j}(k_{1}))^{\beta}]|_{k_{1}=0},

where

is either an integer large enough or a non-integer so that the derivative

\partial_{k_{1}}^{s}(\delta-\omega_{j}(k_{1}))^{l+l_{0}}\neq 0
(cf.
[
17
]
).
Here
\{\mathcal{A}_{j}\}
are lower triangular Toeplitz matrices.

Conclusions

In this paper we investigated bilinearization and derivation of Casoratian solutions
for all the lattice GD-4 type equations that arose from the direct linearization scheme
[
18
]
,
including GD-4 type (A-2), (B-2) and (C-3) equations.
These equations can be presented in different forms,
for example, both (
2.4
) and (
3.1
) are for the GD-4 (B-2) equation,
up to a set of point transformations (
3.2
).
Taking the GD-4 (B-2) equation as an example,
we started from equation (
3.1
),
introduced the transformation (
3.4
) and got its bilinear form (
3.5
).
Soliton solutions were given in terms of Casoratian in Theorem
1
.
By replacing the plane wave factor
\psi_{s}
with
\phi_{s}

which contains a parameter
\delta
,
we introduced new Casoratians
f^{\prime},g^{\prime},h^{\prime},\cdots
as in (
3.2
),
which are composed by
\phi_{s}
.
It is shown that the new and old Casoratians are connected as shown in
the relations presented in (
3.9
).
This then enabled us to get the new bilinear forms (
3.10
)
and new nonlinear forms (
3.13
).
The later is the
\delta
-extended GD-4 (B-2) equation (see also (
3.14
)).
Then we proved in Theorem
2
that the
\delta
-extended bilinear equation
set (
3.10
) allows more general Casoratian solutions,
of which the composing column vector
\boldsymbol{\phi}
is determined by (
3.18
).
The obtained solutions include not only solitons but also Jordan block (or multiple-pole solutions)
and quasi-rational solutions, as shown in Sec.
3.3
.
Then, along this line for the GD-4 (B-2) equation,
we also investigated the GD-4 (A-2) equation and its alternative from and
the GD-4 (C-3) equation.
We have derived their bilinear forms and
\delta
-extensions and Casoratian solutions.
It is also notable that the
\delta
-extended equations are all consistent around cube.

As we have mentioned before,
compared with the continuous and differential-difference case,
the discrete GD type equations play more important roles
in the research of (1+1)-dimensional discrete integrable systems,
because not only their bilinear forms but also their nonlinear forms
are difficult to obtain as reductions from those of the lattice KP equation.
We hope the results we obtained in this paper
can bring more insights on the lattice GD-4 type equations
as well as (1+1)-dimensional discrete integrable systems.
