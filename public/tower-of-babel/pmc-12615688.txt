Impact of COVID-19 restrictions on childhood allergic sensitization: a three-phase comparative study
Authors: Wenxia Shao, Qingyi Shao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to investigate the influence of COVID-19 restrictions on the prevalence of allergen sensitivity in children. This study included patients who underwent allergen testing at our hospital before deregulation, after deregulation, and during the corresponding period in 2021. Changes in allergen-specific IgE (sIgE) positivity, IgE levels, and polysensitization were analyzed to to evaluate the impact of lifting COVID-19 restrictions on allergic diseases. Compared with those in the prederegulation period and the same period in 2021, the number of children with allergies visiting the hospital decreased following deregulation. The rate of allergen positivity was lower after deregulation than before but higher than that during the same period in 2021. After deregulation, allergen sIgE levels were lower than those before deregulation but higher than those in the same period in 2021. The correlation between milk and beef sensitivity was weaker after deregulation than before but stronger than that in 2021 (R values: 0.814, 0.854, and 0.104, respectively). During the period of COVID-19 restrictions, lower levels of allergic disease activity were observed compared to the pre-deregulation period. However, following the deregulation and the subsequent surge in COVID-19 infections, levels of allergic disease activity appeared to increase relative to the same observation period in 2021.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23547-1.Keywords: Allergens, COVID-19, Nonpharmaceutical interactions, sIgESubject terms: Immunology, Medical research

Introduction

Allergic diseases represent a substantial public health burden, and their prevalence and severity have increased globally in recent years1–3. In the United States, more than one-third of children are affected by at least one allergic disease, and more than 8% of the population suffers from asthma; the economic burden associated with allergic diseases is estimated at 81 billion dollars annually4. In developed countries, allergic diseases affect approximately 20% of the population, with the direct medical cost in Scotland reaching 130 million pounds per year5,6. The increase in environmental exposure has significantly contributed to the increased prevalence of allergic diseases in recent decades7,8. Environmental factors have become increasingly important in the pathogenesis of these conditions9,10.

On December 7, 2022, China lifted COVID-19 control measures, leading to a rapid increase in COVID-19 positivity rates. Data from the China Center for Disease Control and Prevention, released on February 8, 2023, indicated that the percentage of COVID-19-positive influenza-like patients in sentinel hospitals reached 60% after deregulation. During this period, personal prevention and control measures, such as maintaining social distance, wearing masks, and practicing hand hygiene—collectively known as nonpharmaceutical interventions (NPIs)—were widely implemented. These measures have been shown to effectively control the prevalence of various diseases, including influenza11 and bronchiolitis12. However, the role of nonpharmaceutical interventions (NPIs) in mitigating allergic reactions remains a subject of debate and is not sufficiently supported by laboratory evidence. In the present study, we examined allergen data from children following COVID-19 deregulation, with the objective of evaluating the influence of COVID-19 restrictions on the prevalence of allergic diseases, the mode of allergen sensitization, and the mode of polysensitization. These findings offer valuable epidemiological data for the prevention and control of allergic diseases.

Methods

Study population

This was a retrospective cohort study conducted at the Children’s Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine. The investigation enrolled pediatric patients who had undergone allergen testing. All participants were under 18 years old and exhibited respiratory and gastrointestinal symptoms, such as coughing, sneezing, nasal discharge, nasal congestion, abdominal pain, vomiting, or diarrhea. Children with a history of nasal or adenoid surgery or known conditions, including pulmonary and bronchial dysplasia and gallstones, were excluded from the study.

Data Collection: Data were obtained by reviewing existing electronic medical records and patient charts. The recruitment process spanned three distinct periods: from December 2021 to January 2022 (designated Period A), from October 2022 to November 2022 (Period B), and from December 2022 to January 2023 (Period C). Data collection for Period A coincided with the relaxation of COVID-19 restrictions in 2021, while that for Period B occurred prior to the lifting of these restrictions, and that for Period C followed their removal.

Detection of IgE antibody concentrations for specific allergens

A specific allergen detection kit manufactured by Dixun Biological Genetic Engineering Co., Ltd., was used to measure the concentrations of 24 inhaled and food allergen-specific IgE antibodies in the children’s samples13. The detection procedure entailed immobilizing the allergen protein onto a nitrocellulose membrane and subsequently adding the child’s serum. If the serum contains allergen-specific IgE antibodies, these antibodies bind with the allergen protein on the membrane, forming an immune complex. Unbound substances were then eliminated through a washing step. Next, a biotin-conjugated anti-human IgE antibody complex was introduced to the plate, resulting in the formation of an allergen protein-IgE-conjugated anti-human IgE antibody-biotin immune complex. After another wash to remove any unbound materials, an alkaline phosphatase‒streptavidin conjugate was added to the detection plate, leading to the formation of an allergen protein‒IgE antibody‒human IgE antibody‒biotin‒streptavidin‒alkaline phosphatase complex. The final absorbance value was measured to determine the specific IgE (sIgE) content of the sample. Allergen-specific IgE antibodies with concentrations exceeding 0.35 IU/ml were considered positive, whereas those above 17.5 IU/ml were classified as strongly positive. The IgE antibody detection process was standardized in accordance with the fourth edition of the Chinese clinical laboratory procedures.

Statistics

The data analysis was conducted via SPSS 26.0 software. The chi-square test was used to compare the percentage of positive cells among the different groups. One-way analysis of variance was utilized for comparisons involving independent variables, normally distributed variables, and equal variance. The Kruskal‒Wallis test served as a nonparametric test for continuous variables that did not follow a normal distribution and lacked equal variance. Cluster analysis was applied to assess the similarity of allergen sIgE levels and categorize them into distinct clusters. The Spearman correlation coefficient was used to examine the correlation between groups. A radar chart was constructed to illustrate the positivity rates of other allergens in patients who were allergic to a particular allergen. To control for potential confounders, a multivariable logistic regression model was employed to examine the association between study periods (Period A, Period B, Period C) and polysensitization (defined as high sensitization levels to two or more allergens). The model was adjusted for age and gender. Results are presented as adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Analyses were conducted using R (version 4.2.2), with significance set at p < 0.05.

Ethics declarations

This study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Children’s Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine, 2021-IRB-228. All research was performed in accordance with relevant guidelines/regulations, for studies using human subjects, written informed consent was obtained from the subjects (or their parents/legal guardians/next of kin) to participate in the study. This study was conducted in strict accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki (as revised in 2013) and relevant national/international regulatory guidelines.

Results

Basic information

During Period A, a total of 4484 samples of allergen-specific IgE antibodies were detected, while 6540 samples were detected in Period B, and 3759 samples were detected in Period C. The majority of the children were in the age group of 3–6 years, accounting for 48.17% in Period A, 53.09% in Period B, and 43.92% in Period C. A chi-square test revealed significant differences in age distribution among the three periods (χ²=87.347, P < 0.001). Among the inhalation allergens, D. farinae/D. Pteronyssinus presented the highest positivity rates for sIgE, with percentages of 16.44%, 26.93%, and 22.91% in Periods A, B, and C, respectively. Dog hair dander followed, with positivity rates of 3.55%, 5.98%, and 5.13% in the respective periods. In terms of food allergens, milk had the highest detection rates, at 9.37%, 14.65%, and 13.78% for Periods A, B, and C, respectively. Egg white was the second most common food allergen, with positivity rates of 2.72%, 4.79%, and 3.41% in the respective periods (Table 1). We also described the baseline data for allergic comorbidities. Asthma: 7.4% of participants had a history of asthma diagnosed by a doctor at baseline in period A, 7.29% in period B, and 5.19% in period C. The asthma diagnosis was confirmed based on physician diagnosis and symptom history. In period A, 9.43% of participants reported a history of atopic dermatitis (AD) diagnosed by doctors, 9.77% in period B, and 12.26% in period C. Other allergic comorbidities are listed in the table, as shown in Table S1.

Impact of deregulation on the allergen positivity and the positivity rate

To assess the changes in the prevalence of allergic diseases following the lifting of control measures, we compared the differences in the number of positive allergens and the positivity rate across the three periods. In Periods A, B, and C, the percentages of samples positive for at least one inhalation allergen were 25.83% (1158 samples), 38.3% (2505 samples), and 34.37% (1292 samples), respectively. For food allergens, the positivity rates were 16.37% (734 samples), 22.78% (1490 samples), and 20.54% (772 samples) in the respective periods. Compared with those in Period B, the percentages of patients positive for at least one allergen were significantly lower in Period C for both inhalation and food allergens (P < 0.001 and P < 0.001, respectively). However, these percentages were significantly greater than those in Period A (P < 0.001 for inhalation allergens and P = 0.008 for food allergens). Significant differences were observed in the number and percentage of positive allergens among the three periods (Table 1). Compared with Period A, Period C presented significant increases in the percentages of positive inhalation allergens such as dog hair dander, short ragweed, and D. farinae/D. pteronyssinus, and house dust. Additionally, the percentages of positive food allergens, including beef, salmon, milk, and lobster/scallop allergens, also significantly increased. In contrast, compared with those in Period B, the percentages of patients positive for inhaled allergens such as Humulus, amaranthus, and D. farinae/D were significantly lower in Period C. pteronyssinus, and mixed grass. The percentages of positive food allergens, such as cashew nuts, egg whites, and shrimp, also decreased significantly (Figs. 1 and 2).

Impact of deregulation on polysensitization patterns

Cosensitization rates of different allergens

Figure 3 shows the percentages of patients allergic to one of the other eleven allergens. The radar chart shows the cosensitization rate of D. farinae/D. pteronyssinus was the highest among patients allergic to cat hair dander, short ragweed, soybean, milk, tree pollen, mixed grass, and mold (59%, 64%, 79%, 33%, 56%, 67%, and 69% in Period C, respectively). In the three periods, the positivity rates for other allergens varied among patients with soybean, amaranth, and tree pollen allergies. In Period C, the cosensitization rates of other allergens in patients with soybean and amaranth allergies were greater than those in Period A, whereas the cosensitization rates of other allergens in patients with tree pollen allergies were lower than those in Period B (Fig. 3).

Radar plots of positivity rates for 11 significant allergens. A closer position to the perimeter of the polygon signifies a higher positive rate for other allergens within the allergen-positive population, while a closer position to the center indicates a lower positive rate for other allergens in the allergen-positive population.

In the multivariate logistic regression analysis, after adjusting for age and gender, Period B was significantly associated with an increased risk of multisensitization compared with Period A (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 1.23, 95% CI: 1.11–1.36, p < 0.001). However, the multisensitization risk of Period C was not significantly different from that of Period 1 (aOR = 1, 95% CI: 0.89–1.12, p = 0.95) (Table S2).

Clustering and correlation of differential allergens

The polysensitization patterns of twelve allergens were analyzed, and the allergens were clustered on the basis of the similarity of sIgE levels (> 0.35 IU/ml) via cluster analysis. In Period A, the twelve allergens were divided into five polysensitization clusters. In Period B, they were divided into six clusters. In Period C, the allergens were again divided into five clusters (Fig. 4). Additionally, correlation analysis was conducted. In Period A, amaranth was strongly correlated with mixed grass and tree pollen (r = 0.724/0.869, P < 0.01), and the correlation between mixed grass and tree pollen was also strong (r = 0.720, P < 0.01). In Period B, milk and beef (r = 0.854, P < 0.01) and mixed grass and amaranth (r = 0.761, P < 0.01) were correlated. In Period C, milk and beef (r = 0.814, P < 0.01) and mixed grass and amaranth (r = 0.623, P < 0.01) were correlated, and mold and amaranth were strongly correlated with mixed grass (r = 0.693/0.600, P < 0.01). The correlation between mixed grass and amaranth was consistent across all stages, and the correlation between mold allergens and other allergens increased in Period C (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Owing to the high infection rate of COVID-19, individuals actively strengthened preventive and control measures, leading to the closure of many schools. As a result, NPIs such as mask wearing, frequent hand washing, enhanced indoor ventilation, and social distancing were strictly enforced14, similar to the early stages of the outbreak in late 2019. Our research results show that since the implementation of COVID-19 restrictions, the number of children visiting hospitals due to allergies has decreased by 42.52%. This observation is consistent with the experience in Philadelphia, where the adoption of public health interventions and the intensified use of NPIs led to a remarkable reduction in asthma-related healthcare utilization. Specifically, the number of outpatient asthma patients has plummeted by 87%, whereas the number of emergency and inpatient cases has decreased by 84%15. Moreover, video telemedicine has emerged as the predominant method for asthma treatment.

The potential effectiveness of COVID-19 restrictions in influencing allergic diseases underscores their ability to alleviate respiratory allergies. This is accomplished by reducing air pollution and minimizing outdoor allergen exposure, both of which are critical factors in lowering the prevalence of respiratory allergens after the lifting of control measures16,17. Notably, indoor allergens such as D. farinae/D. Pteronyssinus had a significant decrease in positivity rates (P < 0.001), highlighting the pivotal role of COVID-19 restrictions in curbing allergic reactions even after the relaxation of broader restrictions.

Detecting the concentration of allergen-specific IgE is crucial for assessing the severity of allergic reactions18–20, as it mediates the degranulation of immune effector cells such as mast cells and basophils21. Our findings indicate that the sIgE concentrations for all allergens with significant differences remained below pre-deregulation levels, further emphasizing the sustained correlation between COVID-19 restrictions and decreased allergic sensitization, even after these measures were lifted. Consequently, our study underscores the potential role of public health interventions introduced during the pandemic in shaping allergic disease outcomes and suggests that such strategies could guide future approaches to managing allergic conditions and responding to outbreaks. To delve deeper into the variations in allergen prevalence patterns across the three distinct time periods, we advocate for a comprehensive polysensitization model analysis targeting allergens that exhibit significant differences in sIgE levels. This analysis will encompass cosensitization rates, clustering, and correlation analyses. The increasing prevalence of polysensitization in allergic diseases offers valuable insights into the characteristics of patients’ allergens22–25. For example, Hu et al.26 tested 122 sIgE allergens and reported that 93.0% of patients were sensitive to more than one allergen, with over half (57.9%) being positive for five or more allergens. Kang et al.27 reported that polysensitization to dog and cat allergens is associated with allergic symptoms following exposure to these animals.

Polysensitization arises from IgE cross-reactions, where a single antibody can recognize multiple allergens. Specifically, the existing allergen-specific IgE antibody binds to the epitope of a secondary allergen that shares similarities with the primary allergen. This binding induces cross-linking of mast cells and basophils, subsequently triggering the release of mediators and the manifestation of clinical symptoms28,29. Therefore, examining polysensitization by analyzing allergen prevalence patterns in allergic diseases is essential for comprehending disease severity and persistence. Polysensitization is a frequent occurrence among individuals with allergies, primarily due to the presence of certain allergenic proteins in multiple allergens. A prime example is Bos d 6 and Bos d 7, two allergenic proteins found in both milk and beef, which often lead to common sensitization30. Research indicates that 73% to 93% of patients who are allergic to beef, particularly those sensitive to Bos d 6, also exhibit milk allergies31. Additionally, several studies have shown that monoclonal antibodies against casein and IgE from patients with milk allergies can recognize the soy allergen Gly m 6.040132. Conversely, casein demonstrates immune cross-reactivity with soybean globulin Gly m 538, Gly m Bd 28k, and oil-related Gly m Bd 30k33,34. Our study also revealed that beef and milk allergens clustered together in allergic patients across all three periods and that soybeans were added to this cluster after deregulation. The correlation between milk and beef was strong during both periods B and C, with an even stronger correlation in period B. However, the P-values for the correlation between soybeans and milk or beef did not indicate significant differences in any period. Nevertheless, cluster analysis suggests whether the grouping of soybeans with milk or beef after the lifting of COVID-19 restrictions reflects changes in dietary habits, environmental exposure, or pathogen prevalence during a specific period. Furthermore, we discovered that the degree of polysensitization decreased after deregulation, suggesting a reduction in the severity of allergic diseases following the lifting of control measures.

However, the removal of COVID-19 control measures led to a rapid increase in virus infections in the short term and impacted the effectiveness of nonpharmaceutical interventions (NPIs). From both clinical and physiological perspectives, respiratory viruses significantly influence allergic diseases. Multiple clinical studies have shown that COVID-19 can exacerbate the severity of allergic diseases, including allergic rhinitis and asthma. For example, an assessment of COVID-19-positive patients in South Korea revealed that patients with allergic rhinitis and asthma experienced more severe COVID-19 symptoms, likely due to underlying physiological mechanisms35. When respiratory viruses infect bronchial epithelial cells, they trigger a local inflammatory cascade. This process leads to neutrophil recruitment, T lymphocyte transport, and monocyte activation, ultimately destroying the bronchial defense system36. Additionally, viruses can induce cytokines such as IL-25 and IL-33, which activate the Th2 pathway. This activation results in increased eosinophil counts, proinflammatory cytokines, and mucin production, all of which contribute to the worsening of allergic diseases such as asthma37.

On the basis of the hypothesis that deregulation may exacerbate allergic diseases by increasing the range of viral infections in the population, we compared the incidence of allergic diseases two months after deregulation with that in the same period in 2021. Our findings indicate that the number of individuals seeking examinations for allergic diseases decreased by 16.17% after the control measures were lifted. However, the proportion of people who tested positive for allergens increased by 12.71%, suggesting a higher incidence of allergic diseases after deregulation than during the same period in 2021.

Supplementary Figure S1 shows that the proportion of pneumonia among children undergoing allergen testing rose from 13.11% during deregulation in 2021 to 28.54% after deregulation, likely due to viral infections. The positive rates and specific IgE levels for significant allergens remained higher after deregulation than in 2021, suggesting that the incidence and severity of allergic diseases have not been effectively controlled. Additionally, polysensitization analysis revealed higher cosensitization rates of other allergens in soybean and amaranth allergy patients after deregulation than in the same period in 2021. Cluster analysis of different sIgE allergens identified five clusters postderegulation. Therefore, in the period after deregulation, patients with specific allergen sensitivities should actively evaluate the allergenicity of other allergens via the tree diagram from the cluster analysis.

Several limitations should be considered. First, the retrospective design and hospital-based sampling (at a tertiary center) risk introducing selection bias and unmeasured confounders, which limits the generalizability of the findings to the broader population. Second, significant age differences between cohorts and distinct seasonal periods (Winter, Summer, Autumn) may confound comparisons of sensitization prevalence. Third, fluctuations in healthcare related to the pandemic (such as reduced visits during lockdowns) could impact testing rates. Finally, unadjusted environmental factors (for example, pollen) and socioeconomic factors (such as income) may influence observed trends. As this is an observational study, the findings suggest temporal associations but cannot establish causation.

Conclusion

In summary, our investigation revealed that, with the implementation of enhanced NPIs, the incidence and severity of allergic diseases following deregulation were lower than those in the period prior to deregulation. However, these metrics remained higher than those observed during the corresponding period in the preceding year. Changes in allergen sensitization rates were observed during NPI implementation periods. These changes may be influenced by NPIs, altered health-seeking behaviors, environmental factors, or other confounders. Nonetheless, our study has several limitations, including its retrospective nature, the potential for sampling bias, and the absence of experimental design validation.
