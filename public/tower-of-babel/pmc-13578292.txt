Genome-wide pQTL mapping in human skin identifies specific genetic regulators and mechanistic links to skin disorders
Authors: Yuanqiang Sun, Wenchao Li, Zhenzhen Wang, Yongliang Yang, Ziyang Zhang, Gongqi Yu, Chuan Wang, Xu Sang, Yingjie Lin, Guangliang Zhang, Haixia Wang, Yilei Wu, Jianwen Wang, Fuxiang Bai, Jianju Liu, Hong Liu, Furen Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Understanding how genetic variation shapes tissue-specific protein expression is fundamental for uncovering disease mechanisms and therapeutic targets. However, the proteomic landscape and its underlying genetic regulation in skin, the largest human organ, remain largely uncharted. Here, we present a population-level skin proteomic dataset, quantifying 5,774 proteins across 248 individuals and revealing substantial inter-individual variation shaped by genetic and non-genetic factors. Genome-wide pQTL analysis identifies 1,765 significant variant–protein associations involving 36 proteins, with 62% unsupported by skin eQTL and 33% not replicated in non-skin pQTL. Integrating skin pQTL with ancestry-matched leprosy GWAS identifies 11 risk proteins through proteome-wide association study (PWAS), with four showing causal evidence from colocalization or Mendelian randomization analyses. Further integration with European GWASs for atopic dermatitis and psoriasis identifies known and novel disease-related proteins, demonstrating the cross-ancestry utility of skin pQTL. Together, this work establishes a valuable resource for skin proteogenomics and disease-gene discovery in dermatology.

Introduction

Understanding disease mechanisms requires a comprehensive view across multiple molecular layers, including DNA, RNA, and protein. Advances in multi-omics technologies have enabled systematic profiling of these layers, connecting genetic variation to molecular phenotypes and, ultimately, to disease. At the DNA level, genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified thousands of genetic variants associated with complex traits and diseases. At the RNA level, transcriptomic analyses—particularly expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) mapping—have linked genetic variants to gene expression regulation1. Integrating eQTL data with GWAS has proven powerful for prioritizing candidate genes and elucidating potential mechanisms of diseases2. However, eQTL alone cannot fully resolve the functional variants and genes driving GWAS signals, as many genetic variants influence protein abundance without altering RNA levels3.

As the primary effectors of cellular functions, proteins constitute a critical layer of biological regulation. Advances in proteomics now allow systematic characterization of protein abundance, dynamics, and interactions, offering direct insights into cellular states and disease mechanisms4. Compared with transcriptomic data, proteomic profiles often provide complementary and sometimes divergent information, as protein abundance only moderately correlates with mRNA levels (correlation 0.3–0.6)5. Likewise, the genetic regulation of proteins is only partially shared with that of transcripts, with limited replication (30–60%) between eQTL and protein quantitative trait loci (pQTL)6,7. These observations underscore the importance of proteomic studies as an essential counterpart to genomic and transcriptomic analyses. Accordingly, pQTL mapping has emerged as a powerful approach to directly link genetic variation to protein abundance and function3, thereby revealing disease-relevant or biologically informative mechanisms not captured by GWAS or eQTL studies.

Recent efforts have focused on generating and utilizing large-scale pQTL datasets across multiple human tissues, including blood8–12, brain13–15, liver6,16 and cell lines17. For example, integrating cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) pQTL with Alzheimer’s disease GWAS has identified multiple causal proteins and improved disease prediction models15. Likewise, plasma pQTL data from over 50,000 UK Biobank participants have uncovered novel genomic variants linked to disease pathways and biomarkers10–12. Brain pQTL datasets have further elucidated the genetic regulation of brain proteins, revealing molecular mechanisms underlying psychiatric disorders and Alzheimer’s disease14. These studies underscore the importance of tissue-specific pQTL resources for understanding protein regulation and its implications in human disease. However, systematic proteomic and pQTL mapping of the skin—the body’s largest and immunologically active organ—remains lacking. Although previous studies have consistently revealed the complex and distinctive protein landscape of human skin18–21, their limited sample sizes and absence of matched genotypic data preclude pQTL mapping and integrative analyses needed to uncover novel disease mechanisms. This gap is particularly critical given the tissue specificity of the proteome and pQTL22–24. The absence of large-scale proteome and genome-wide pQTL data in skin has limited our understanding of how genetic variation influences skin protein expression and contributes to disease pathogenesis.

To address this gap, we profiled skin proteomes in a cohort of 248 individuals, representing the largest sample size to date, and integrated matched genomic data to systematically perform genome-wide pQTL mapping (Fig. 1a). We uncover both shared and protein-specific regulatory variants, providing novel insights beyond transcript-level analyses. Furthermore, by integrating skin pQTL with disease GWASs from both Chinese and European populations, we demonstrate the cross-ancestry utility of this resource in prioritizing candidate proteins for skin-related diseases, exemplified by experimental validation of ALDH2 in leprosy. Results

Landscape of population-level human skin proteomes

To comprehensively profile the skin proteome, we collected healthy skin tissue samples from each of 248 donors undergoing fracture surgery (median age: 52 years; 39% female; Supplementary Fig. 1a, b and Supplementary Data 1). Proteomic profiling was performed using four-dimensional data-independent acquisition (4D-DIA) technology25. For quality control, thirteen HeLa QC replicates were interspersed among samples to assess instrument stability (Supplementary Fig. 1c). Consistent identification of > 6000 proteins and high correlations (Pearson’s R > 0.95) among replicates confirmed stable instrument performance throughout the runs (Supplementary Fig. 1d, e). Furthermore, 4D-DIA quantification was validated against parallel reaction monitoring (PRM), a targeted proteomics method commonly used for validation of large-scale proteomic measurements26,27. Analyses of 10 samples showed strong concordance between the two methods (Spearman’s rho ≥ 0.8; Supplementary Fig. 2a, b), confirming the robustness and reliability of 4D-DIA for skin proteome quantification. After quality control (Supplementary Fig. 2c; Methods), the final dataset included 248 individuals, 33,806 peptides, and 5774 proteins (Fig. 1b). The quantified proteins exhibited a broad dynamic range in abundance, spanning over seven orders of magnitude (Fig. 1b). 83% of proteins were detected in >50% of samples (Supplementary Fig. 2d). Each sample quantified 3,858–5359 proteins (median 4788; Supplementary Fig. 2e). When compared with a published DIA-based proteome across 12 tissues18, our dataset showed the highest similarity to their skin proteome, with a strong overall correlation (Spearman’s rho = 0.73; Fig. 1c). The two skin datasets quantified comparable numbers of proteins (5774 vs. 6198) and shared 5008 proteins, demonstrating the comprehensive coverage and robustness of our dataset.

To characterize the basic features of skin proteome composition, we performed the Gene Ontology (GO) analysis of the top 10% most abundant proteins and found significant enrichment in pathways related to supramolecular fiber organization, wound response, and immune response, reflecting the hallmarks of skin structure and function (Supplementary Fig. 3a). Structural proteins such as keratins and collagens showed particularly high abundance (Fig. 1d), and the strong correlations observed between KRT5–KRT14 and KRT1–KRT10 (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. 3b) were consistent with previous observations28, confirming that our dataset captures representative characteristics of skin protein composition.

Compared with previous skin proteomic studies18–21, our population-scale dataset includes a larger and more diverse cohort, allowing the exploration of protein co-expression based on inter-individual variation. We applied treeClust, a previously established machine learning algorithm for inferring co-expressed proteins29, and visualized protein relationships using t-SNE, where proximity reflects expression similarity across individuals. The resulting map revealed clear protein clusters largely corresponding to subcellular components such as ribosome, nucleus, ER, and mitochondria (Fig. 1f). This analysis validated dataset quality and provided a resource for inferring the functions of uncharacterized proteins, based on the principle that co-expressed genes often participate in related biological pathways29. For example, plakophilin-1 (PKP1) and desmoplakin (DESP) showed strong co-expression (treeClust distance = 1.97, R = 0.99; Supplementary Fig. 3c), consistent with their known functional interaction in the formation of epidermal desmosomes30.

Leveraging population-level data also enabled assessment of inter-individual variability in protein abundance, which may be influenced by both genetic and non-genetic factors such as age and sex. Principal component analysis (PCA) showed no clear clustering by either factor (Supplementary Fig. 3d). Consistently, variance partitioning showed that age and sex explained only a minor fraction of the overall proteomic variation (median 0.43% and 0.33%, respectively; Fig. 1g), suggesting that factors beyond age and sex may also contribute to inter-individual differences in protein abundance. Given that samples were collected from multiple anatomical sites and may differ in cellular composition, we next evaluated the contribution of these factors to proteomic heterogeneity. Analysis by anatomical location (arm, leg and trunk; Supplementary Data 1) revealed no clear segregation by sampling site in PCA (Supplementary Fig. 3e), consistent with prior work reporting broadly similar transcriptomic profiles across trunk and extremities compared to more specialized regions such as palms/soles, perineum, or head/neck31. Accordingly, the sampling site accounted for only 1.02% of the overall proteomic variation (Fig. 1g).

To assess the contribution of cellular composition, we estimated cell-type proportions by deconvolving bulk RNA-seq data using a scRNA-seq atlas of healthy human skin from anatomically matched sites as a reference32. Twelve major skin cell types were resolved, including fibroblasts, keratinocytes, vascular endothelial cells (VEC), pericytes, myeloid cells, NK cells, lymphatic endothelial cells (LEC), T cells, smooth muscle cells (SMC), melanocytes, Schwann cells, and B cells (Supplementary Fig. 3f). The overall composition was dominated by keratinocytes and fibroblasts, together comprising approximately 50–70% of total cells, while less abundant immune and stromal populations showed moderate variability across samples (Supplementary Fig. 3f). These inferred composition patterns were consistent with previous scRNA-seq analyses of healthy human skin32. Notably, no distinct compositional differences were observed among anatomical sites (Supplementary Fig. 3f), in agreement with previous reports showing broadly similar cellular compositions across skin from the arm, leg and back33. Variance partitioning indicated that cell composition explained a modestly larger, yet still limited, proportion of proteomic variation than the other measured factors (median 3.38%; Fig. 1g). Although these measured factors did not dominate global proteomic variation, individual proteins exhibited pronounced factor-associated variation. For example, prolyl 4-hydroxylase subunit alpha-1 (P4HA1) was higher in fibroblast-rich samples (rho = 0.29, P = 3.81 × 10−5; Supplementary Fig. 3g), in line with its role in collagen biosynthesis and extracellular matrix organization34. Similarly, tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinases 3 (TIMP3) increased with age (rho = 0.66, P = 3.83 × 10−32; Supplementary Fig. 3h), consistent with age-related extracellular matrix remodeling and tissue aging35.

Together, these results establish a high-quality and well-characterized population-scale skin proteome resource. The dataset captures key structural and functional features of skin biology while revealing a multifactorial landscape of inter-individual proteomic variation, providing a robust framework for subsequent analyses of protein regulation.

Proteomic remodeling during human skin aging

Although age contributed minimally to total protein variability, the well-established impact of aging on skin physiology and the wide age range of our cohort (13–95 years) prompted us to further explore age-associated protein changes. Spearman correlation analysis between protein abundance and chronological age identified 156 age-correlated proteins (ACPs, FDR < 0.05), including 89 positively and 67 negatively correlated proteins (Fig. 2a, b and Supplementary Data 2). For example, serum amyloid P-component (SAP), a canonical amyloid-related factor, showed the strongest positive correlation with age (rho = 0.84, P < 2.2 × 10−16; Fig. 2c). SAP elevation has been observed in multiple aged tissues and may contribute to endothelial cell senescence, injury, and inflammation18. In contrast, collagen alpha-1(I) chain (CO1A1) showed a strong negative correlation with age (rho = –0.66, P < 2.2 × 10−16; Fig. 2c), consistent with collagen loss driving wrinkle formation in aged skin. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed that pathways enriched during aging (NES > 0) were predominantly associated with immune and inflammatory processes, including antigen processing and presentation, complement activation, and adaptive and humoral immune responses (Fig. 2d), suggesting enhanced immune and inflammatory activity during aging in skin. In contrast, pathways depleted during aging (NES < 0) were mainly related to skin structure, collagen biosynthesis, and post-transcriptional regulation, including translation and RNA splicing (Fig. 2d). The age-associated decline in proteins involved in these processes may be accompanied by altered post-transcriptional regulatory capacity during aging, potentially reflected in the correlation between transcription and protein output.

To further characterize this, we compared protein and RNA abundance in 199 individuals with matched transcriptomic–proteomic data, covering 5760 genes quantified at both levels. Overall, mRNA and protein abundance showed a moderate positive correlation (rho = 0.32; Supplementary Fig. 4b), consistent with prior studies5. To assess age-related changes, individuals were stratified into young (13–38 years, lowest quartile) and old (62–95 years, highest quartile) groups. Compared with the young group, the old group showed a markedly reduced number of genes with positive RNA–protein correlations (846 vs. 540 genes, χ2 test, P = 1.50 × 10−14; Fig. 2e) and significantly lower overall RNA-protein correlations (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, P = 2.11 × 10−15; Fig. 2f), indicating an age-associated decoupling between transcription and proteomic output. Genes showing loss of RNA–protein coupling in the old group were enriched for pathways related to extracellular matrix (ECM) structure, tissue organization, angiogenesis, and protein processing/signaling (Supplementary Fig. 4c, d), suggesting that these biological processes are particularly susceptible to post-transcriptional dysregulation with age. Furthermore, protein abundance variability increased in older individuals, reflected by higher standard deviation (SD) in aged samples (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, P = 2.76 × 10−184; Supplementary Fig. 4e), supporting the notion of proteomic dyshomeostasis in aging skin.

To determine whether proteins exhibiting pronounced age-associated RNA–protein decoupling also track chronological age across individuals, we constructed a multivariable regression model using the top-ranked decoupled proteins (Supplementary Fig. 5a). These proteins collectively captured a modest fraction of age-associated variation across individuals (adjusted R2 = 0.22, P = 2.89 × 10−10), suggesting that loss of RNA–protein coordination may represent one component of molecular aging. Among them, proteins encoded by CA2, PRDX4, IDI1, and NUCB2 showed the strongest independent associations (|standardized β | ≈ 0.19–0.23; Supplementary Fig. 5b), each accounting for approximately 3–5% of the model variance based on partial R2 and leave-one-out analyses (ΔR2) (Supplementary Fig. 5c). Similar results were obtained using RNA-adjusted protein residuals, suggesting that these effects are not solely attributable to transcriptional variation but also reflect RNA-independent regulation at the protein level (Supplementary Fig. 5d). Among the four key genes, PRDX4 encodes an antioxidant enzyme involved in redox homeostasis and is subject to extensive post-transcriptional regulation37. Its RNA–protein decoupling may reflect age-related disruption of protein turnover under oxidative conditions. The decoupling of IDI1, CA2, and NUCB2 likely reflects age-associated metabolic remodeling, pH shifts, and altered calcium/energy homeostasis, respectively.

Given the close link between age and dynamic protein expression changes, we sought to construct a “proteomic clock” for skin aging by developing a predictive model of biological age based on ACPs in skin (Supplementary Fig. 5e). Using an elastic net regression approach18, we built a high-accuracy model comprising eight proteins encoded by APCS, EMILIN2, NT5E, COL1A1, TIMP3, DDX46, ANGPTL2, and HTRA1. The predicted biological age showed strong concordance with chronological age (mean absolute error [MAE] = 6.79; R = 0.91, P = 1.50 × 10−27; Fig. 2g). Each of these proteins showed a permutation importance score greater than 1 (Fig. 2h and Supplementary Data 3), indicating their strong positive contributions to the model. To externally validate the model, we applied it to an independent skin proteomic dataset generated from our PRM–DIA comparison experiment (n = 10; ages 34–79). In this independent cohort, the predicted age showed a strong correlation with chronological age (R = 0.97, P = 2.35 × 10−5, MAE = 3.32; Supplementary Fig. 5f), supporting the robustness and generalizability of the model.

Overall, skin proteome abundance and its coupling with RNA undergo remodeling during aging, and a protein-based model enables accurate prediction of biological age.

Mapping genetic effects on protein abundance

Beyond non-genetic factors, genetic regulation represents another crucial contributor to inter-individual variation in the skin proteome. The availability of genotypic data from proteome-matched samples enables the identification of genetic variants that regulate skin protein levels through pQTL mapping. After quality control, 186 individuals with both proteomic and genome-wide genotypic data were included in the analysis, covering 5192 autosomal proteins and 3,461,609 cis-variants (minor allele frequency [MAF] > 0.05) within ± 1 Mb of each gene’s transcription start site (TSS). Using a linear model-based association approach implemented in FastQTL38, we identified 1765 significant variant–protein associations involving 36 proteins at a genome-wide FDR < 5% (Fig. 3a; Supplementary Data 4). Hereafter, we refer to proteins with significant cis-pQTL associations as pGenes. For each pGene, the pQTL variant with the lowest P-value was designated as the lead pQTL. To further refine putative causal variants underlying each association, we performed fine-mapping using the SuSiE framework40. Among the 36 pGenes, 32 yielded a single 95% credible set, while 4 had none (Supplementary Fig. 6a and Supplementary Data 4). Only 3 pGenes contained a variant with posterior inclusion probability (PIP) > 0.90, whereas for most loci the PIP was distributed across multiple highly correlated variants (Supplementary Fig. 6a), reflecting the limited resolution of fine-mapping and the difficulty of pinpointing causal variants, likely due to the modest sample size and linkage disequilibrium (LD) structure. Notably, the SNP with the highest PIP was in full agreement with the lead pQTL in all 36 pGenes (Supplementary Fig. 6a). Therefore, the lead pQTL was used as a proxy for the variant driving the association.

The absolute effect sizes of the 36 lead pQTL were inversely correlated with MAF (Spearman’s rho = −0.66, P = 1.21 × 10−5; Fig. 3b). These lead variants explained a median of 15.8% of inter-individual variance in protein abundance, ranging from 12.1% to 32.6% (Fig. 3c). For example, the lead pQTL rs77768175 of ALDH2 protein (PIP = 0.25), previously associated with alcohol consumption and metabolic traits41, accounted for 32.6% of the inter-individual variation in ALDH2 protein levels (Fig. 3c). Despite testing a ± 1 Mb region, most lead pQTL (28 of 36, 77.8%) located within 100 kb of the TSS (Fig. 3d). VEP-based functional annotation revealed that lead pQTL of pGenes were significantly enriched in intronic, missense, and 3′UTR regions compared with lead variants of non-pGenes (Fig. 3e), indicating stronger regulatory relevance. The functional relevance of pQTL variants was further assessed using the RegulomeDB42. 1,386 of the 1764 significant variants (78.6%) were classified as high-confidence functional variants (rank ≤ 2) compared to 43.9% of background variants (χ2 test, P = 1.81 × 10−189; Fig. 3f), indicating a significant enrichment of pQTL predicted to have a high likelihood of functional impact compared to background variants. Restricting the analyses to lead pQTL with moderate-to-high fine-mapping support (PIP ≥ 0.25) yielded similar patterns for TSS proximity, functional annotation, and predicted functional impact (Supplementary Fig. 6b–d), supporting the robustness of these findings.

Genome-wide trans-pQTL mapping (> 1 Mb from the TSS) identified only one protein, nuclear autoantigen Sp-100 on chr2, significantly associated with two linked SNPs (rs6085261 and rs6085262) on chr20. The scarcity of trans-pQTL likely reflects both the modest sample size and the cellular heterogeneity of skin tissue.

Comparison of skin cis-pQTL and cis-eQTL

Although skin eQTL mapping has been performed in GTEx43, the availability of matched genomic, transcriptomic, and proteomic data in our cohort provided a unique opportunity to directly compare genetic regulation at the RNA and protein levels. Using the same analytical pipeline as for pQTL, eQTL mapping in 150 individuals identified 20,999 cis-acting variants associated with 282 genes (eGenes) (Supplementary Figs. 7 and 8a and Supplementary Data 5). We cross-referenced our skin eQTL with those from GTEx (V8)43, which used a similar analysis framework to ours. Among the 35,725 overlapping gene–SNP pairs with nominal P-value < 1 × 10−4 in both datasets, effect sizes were highly correlated (Pearson’s R = 0.902, P < 2.2 × 10−16), and 98.8% showed concordant directions (Supplementary Fig. 8b). This concordance increased under stricter P-value thresholds (Supplementary Fig. 8c), confirming strong agreement between the two eQTL datasets. However, GTEx is predominantly composed of individuals of European ancestry, whereas our cohort is of Chinese ancestry. Differences in allele frequencies and linkage disequilibrium patterns across ancestries may contribute to residual discordance and should be considered when interpreting cross-dataset comparisons.

To investigate the relationship between genetic regulation of mRNA and protein abundance, we next compared skin cis-pQTL and cis-eQTL. Across 5052 genes and 3,444,802 variants tested in both analyses (15,443,226 gene–SNP pairs in total; Supplementary Fig. 7), 7487 pairs were nominally significant (P < 0.01) in both datasets—a 3-fold enrichment over random expectation (permuted mean = 2465, empirical P = 0.001; Supplementary Fig. 8d), suggesting that shared genetic effects at the RNA and protein levels occur more frequently than expected by chance. Effect sizes across these shared associations were positively correlated (Pearson’s R = 0.74, P < 2.2 × 10−16), with 88.3% displaying consistent effect directions (Fig. 4a). The concordance was further strengthened with more stringent P-value cutoffs (Fig. 4b), indicating that a subset of genetic effects is shared between RNA and protein levels. The small fraction (11.7%) of opposite-direction associations did not reach significance in both datasets simultaneously (Supplementary Fig. 8e), suggesting that pQTL–eQTL discordance may partly reflect differences in effect magnitude rather than true opposite effects. Beyond comparing variant-level effect sizes, we further evaluated gene-level replication between pQTL and eQTL. A pGene was considered replicated as an eGene if its lead pQTL showed a nominal P ≤ 0.01 with a concordant direction in the eQTL dataset, and vice versa17. Based on this criterion, 13 out of 35 pGenes (37.14%) tested in the eQTL analysis were also reproduced as eGenes. Likewise, 11 of 42 eGenes (26.19%) tested in the pQTL analysis were also replicated as pGenes (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 7). The asymmetric replication rate, with fewer eGenes validated at the protein level, is consistent with previous observations7,17 and likely reflects post-transcriptional buffering mechanisms that stabilize protein abundance relative to RNA expression44,45.

To further statistically dissect shared and distinct genetic regulation between proteins and transcripts, we performed Bayesian colocalization analyses across all shared SNPs tested in both cis-eQTL and cis-pQTL datasets for each gene46. The analysis estimated five posterior probabilities (PP.H0–PP.H4), where PP.H4 represents the probability of shared causal variants. We identified ten genes with strong evidence of colocalization (PP.H4 ≥ 0.7) between pQTL and eQTL signals (Fig. 4d; Supplementary Data 6). For example, COPG2, encoding a Golgi coatomer subunit gamma-2 (COPG2), showed linked eQTL that also acted as its pQTL (Supplementary Fig. 9a). The high posterior probability (PP.H4 = 1.00) indicates that genetic effects on COPG2 protein level are likely mediated through transcript-level regulation. In contrast, six genes exhibited strong pQTL without corresponding eQTL (PP.H1 ≥ 0.7; Fig. 4d). A representative example is F12, encoding coagulation factor XII, where a significant pQTL variant (rs1801020, PIP = 0.23) located in the 5′UTR disrupts the start codon of an upstream open reading frame (uORF) (Fig. 4e), which typically acts as a translational repressor47. This disruption likely enhances translation, leading to increased protein levels (β = 0.48, P = 3.76 × 10−9) without a corresponding RNA-level effect (β = 0.035, P = 0.67) (Fig. 4e). Another example is STING1, encoding the stimulator of interferon genes protein (STING), where several missense pQTL variants in strong LD were associated with reduced STING protein levels but showed no eQTL signals (Supplementary Fig. 9b). These variants likely act through post-transcriptional mechanisms such as altered translation or protein stability. Conversely, seven genes showed eQTL signals without corresponding pQTL (PP.H2 ≥ 0.7; Fig. 4d). For instance, ATP6V1D, encoding a subunit of the ATPase complex, had multiple eQTL variants whose effects were not observed at the protein level (Supplementary Fig. 9c), consistent with dosage compensation mechanisms acting on subunits of multiprotein complexes via translational and/or post-translational mechanisms48.

Together, these results show that while a subset of pQTL and eQTL share the same regulatory variants, many effects are layer-specific, underscoring the need for integrated multi-omic analyses to fully resolve the architecture of gene regulation in human skin.

At the pQTL variant level, effect sizes of overlapping associations were largely concordant in direction but only moderately correlated in magnitude between skin and non-skin tissues (R = 0.37–0.70 across tissues; Supplementary Fig. 10b), with plasma showing the lowest correlation with skin among all tissues. This discordance decreased under more stringent significance thresholds, approaching complete consistency at P < 1 × 10−5 (Supplementary Fig. 10c). Gene-level comparison revealed that 30 of 36 skin pGenes were also reported in other tissues, whereas six (CZIB, RANGAP1, IBA57, MRPL50, PLGRKT, and IVL) were only reported in our skin pQTL (Fig. 5a). Among these, only one pGene, IVL, which encodes the skin structural protein involucrin, showed protein expression exclusively in the skin proteome and was not detected in other tissues, suggesting its skin-restricted pQTL effect is likely due to its skin-specific expression (Supplementary Fig. 10d, e). The remaining five pGenes showed detectable protein expression in both skin and non-skin tissues (Supplementary Fig. 10d), but their pQTL signals were only reported in skin, indicating that their genetic regulation may be preferentially detectable in skin.

Although 30 pGenes were identified in both skin and other tissues, this overlap does not necessarily imply consistent underlying genetic regulation. As full summary statistics were unavailable for non-skin pQTL, we used LD-based alignment (R2 > 0.8) followed by conditional analyses to test whether non-skin pQTL variants could explain the observed skin associations (Supplementary Fig. 11a, b; Methods). If the skin pQTL was abolished after conditioning, the signal was considered replicated in other tissues, indicating shared genetic regulation. Using this criterion, 24 of 30 skin pGenes were replicated in at least one other tissue—most frequently in colon (15), followed by liver, lung, and thyroid (14 each), and heart and plasma (13 each) (Fig. 5a, b and Supplementary Data 8). Five pGenes (MMAB, CPNE1, AAMDC, PON2 and ERAP1) were replicated across all six tissues (Fig. 5b). These pGenes have also been previously reported in non-skin contexts, such as MMAB in liver16 and iPSCs17, CPNE1 in brain50, and AAMDC in brain13. Notably, CPNE1, encoding a calcium-dependent membrane-binding protein (CPNE1), exhibited strong cross-tissue replication. Its lead skin pQTL, rs12481228 (PIP = 0.01), is a missense variant predicted to disrupt CPNE1 protein structure51 and has been previously associated with diverse traits, including myogenesis51 and hippocampal volume50. Our findings suggest that the CPNE1 locus harbors a conserved genetic effect on protein abundance across tissues, potentially contributing to its pleiotropic influence on human phenotypes, with rs12481228 representing one candidate variant within this regulatory signal.

In contrast, six pGenes (ACADSB, ALDH2, FCGR3A, GAA, GSTM1, and IGHG3) were reported in both skin and other tissues, yet their skin pQTL were not replicated in non-skin tissues under our analysis framework (Fig. 5a, b; Supplementary Data 8). For example, rs77768175 was the lead pQTL for ALDH2 in skin (PIP = 0.25, β = − 1.18, P = 2.76 × 10−17) but was not reported in any non-skin tissue. Instead, distinct ALDH2 pQTL were reported in heart (rs10849941, β = −0.437, P = 3.66 × 10−5)24 and plasma (rs79430240, β = −0.2239, P = 1.83 × 10−38)49. Neither of these variants was significant in our skin pQTL, and their high-LD proxies also did not attenuate the skin ALDH2 pQTL signal in conditional analyses (Fig. 5b). Altogether, 12 of 36 (33.3%) skin pGenes were either not reported (n = 6) or not replicated (n = 6) in current non-skin datasets, suggesting potential differences in genetic regulation across tissues. Nevertheless, cross-tissue replication may be influenced by differences in sample size, statistical power and study design, and the absence of detection in other tissues does not necessarily imply tissue specificity.

Integrative skin pQTL analysis prioritizes leprosy-associated proteins

As proteins are functionally closer to phenotypes and pQTL often exhibit strong tissue specificity, integrating skin pQTL is expected to help the mechanistic interpretation of skin disease GWAS loci. As a proof of concept, we focused on leprosy, an infectious skin disease with high heritability (38.5%) and available Chinese GWAS summary statistics matching the ancestry of our pQTL cohort52. Using the FUSION pipeline53, we performed a proteome-wide association study (PWAS) integrating leprosy GWAS and skin pQTL data, identifying 11 proteins whose genetically regulated protein levels are significantly associated with leprosy (FDR < 0.05; Fig. 6a; proteins are hereafter referred to by the symbols of their encoding genes for convenience). Compared with previously reported GWAS risk loci, the PWAS results not only reinforced the previously reported risk genes (ALDH2), but also prioritized functional proteins within multiple-candidate loci (TSFM, APOBR, ANKRD13A and DECR1) and identified six previously unreported candidates (NAA25, MPST, OARD1, DDB2, HGFAC and GBA1) for leprosy risk (Supplementary Data 9; Discussion). ALDH2 encodes mitochondrial aldehyde dehydrogenase 2 (ALDH2), which catalyzes acetaldehyde metabolism. Colocalization identified its missense variant rs671 (Glu457Lys; 12:g.111803962 G > A) as a shared causal signal between leprosy and cis-pQTL for ALDH2 (PP.H4 = 0.99; Fig. 6d). Consistently, it was associated with markedly decreased protein abundance in skin (β = −1.15, P = 1.16 × 10−16; Fig. 6e), but had no significant effect on mRNA levels (β = −0.12, P = 0.22; Supplementary Fig. 12a, b), indicating its protein-specific genetic regulation independent of mRNA-level effects. These results extend previous GWAS findings by providing protein-level evidence that refines causal inference beyond variant-level associations and links ALDH2 genetic signals to functional interpretation.

To validate these findings, we examined ALDH2 function in cellular and mouse models (Fig. 7a). Leprosy is caused by Mycobacterium leprae (M. leprae) infection of host macrophages56,57. Notably, ALDH2 showed robust mRNA expression in macrophages in public skin scRNA-seq data57 (Supplementary Fig. 12d). To further evaluate the ability of the pQTL-based integrative analysis in prioritizing previously unreported functional proteins, we next experimentally assessed the in vivo function of NAA25, another candidate supported by multiple lines of evidence (PWAS.Z = −4.7024, PWAS.q = 9.32 × 10−4; MR.Z = −2.05, MR.P = 0.039; PP.H4 = 0.53; Fig. 6c), yet not previously implicated by leprosy GWAS. Cross-ancestry application of skin pQTLs to skin diseases

To evaluate the cross-ancestry applicability of our Chinese skin pQTL dataset, we integrated it with GWAS data for atopic dermatitis (AD) and psoriasis from European populations58, using the same analytic framework as in the leprosy analysis.

For AD, PWAS identified 29 proteins significantly associated with disease risk (FDR < 0.05; Fig. 8a). No pathway reached statistical significance in enrichment analyses (Supplementary Fig. 15), likely due to the limited number and functional heterogeneity of the identified proteins. Alternatively, functional annotation of these proteins could group them into several biologically interpretable categories relevant to AD pathogenesis (Supplementary Data 10), including epidermal barrier function and keratinocyte differentiation (e.g., CST6, KRT31, GBA1), lipid metabolism (e.g., DGAT2, ACSF2, PGS1, AAMDC), and immune/inflammatory signaling (e.g., C7, PSMB9, ILRUN, MAP4K4). Discussion

Despite the central role of proteins in mediating biological functions, their genetic regulation in human skin has remained largely unexplored. Leveraging a large-scale proteomic dataset quantifying 5774 proteins in 248 individuals, we systematically mapped the genetic architecture of the human skin proteome. This resource reveals extensive inter-individual variation and numerous skin pQTL, uncovering a class of protein- and tissue-specific genetic regulation distinct from that observed at the RNA level or in other tissues. By integrating skin pQTL with GWAS of leprosy, psoriasis, and AD, we prioritize putative causal proteins, with functional validation of ALDH2 demonstrating the utility of this resource. Together, our findings establish a foundation for understanding skin proteogenomic regulation and its contribution to dermatological diseases.

The diverse functions of skin rely on its complex and tightly regulated proteome. Previous skin proteomic studies have revealed the architectural and functional complexity of the skin18–21. Our work, taking advantage of the 4D-DIA proteomic approach, has advanced beyond the prior studies with limited sample size by extending skin proteome coverage to a large population scale, enabling quantification of inter-individual variation and identification of both genetic and non-genetic factors influencing protein abundance. We observed widespread age-associated changes in protein abundance and in protein-RNA correlation. Furthermore, we developed a parsimonious “proteomic clock” based on only eight proteins that accurately predicts chronological age, achieving comparable performance to the previous model that incorporated 56 skin proteins18. Further linking this proteomic clock with clinical features of aging-related skin conditions will be valuable for elucidating its functional and pathological relevance.

Beyond age-related effects, genetic regulation plays a key role in shaping the proteome. While previous pQTL studies have focused mainly on plasma due to sample accessibility, this approach largely overlooks intracellular or locally expressed proteins. Our parallel genomic and proteomic profiling of population-level human skin enables systematic identification of tissue-resident pQTL. We identified 36 pGenes, one-third of which (12 out of 36) exhibited distinct pQTL patterns in skin compared to other tissues. These included pGenes reported only in skin, as well as those shared across tissues but regulated by distinct genetic variants. Comparable proportions of tissue-specific pQTL have been reported in recent cross-tissue pQTL studies23,24. Such specificity may reflect tissue-dependent cis-regulatory elements or trans-acting factors, thereby contributing to tissue-specific gene regulation and complex-trait associations63. Notably, our cross-tissue comparisons were constrained by differences in sample size, ancestry, and proteomic platforms, and addressing these factors will enable more refined insights into tissue-dependent pQTL effects. Additionally, the overall number of QTL detected in our skin dataset is lower than that reported in large consortia datasets in other tissues, such as plasma9,11,49. This likely reflects both the smaller sample size, which directly limits statistical power for QTL discovery, and the intrinsic heterogeneity of skin tissue18–21, which can dilute genetic effects in bulk measurements due to cellular diversity and proteomic complexity. Although we inferred cell-type composition through deconvolution of bulk RNA-seq data, these estimates may not fully capture the true cellular composition of each proteomic sample. In addition, while adjustment for estimated cell-type composition in the pQTL analysis can partially control for heterogeneity arising from cellular differences, it may also obscure cell-type–specific genetic effects in bulk proteomic measurements. Future studies with larger sample sizes will improve statistical power for QTL discovery, and a more definitive resolution of skin heterogeneity will require layer-specific or even cell-type–resolved pQTL mapping. Unlike the bulk full-thickness skin samples used here to enable population-scale multi-omics analyses, spatially resolved and cell-type–resolved skin proteomic approaches have enabled characterization of fine-scale molecular heterogeneity across skin compartments and cellular populations20,21. Integrating such approaches with genetic mapping would enable direct attribution of genetic effects to specific cellular contexts and further refine the biological interpretation of pQTL signals.

We further evaluated the relevance of skin pQTL to disease. Because pQTL serve as intermediate molecular traits linking genetic variation to pathophysiology, integrating them with GWAS can clarify causal chains from genotype to phenotype. Our skin pQTL resource offers a unique opportunity to investigate skin diseases. We first applied our skin pQTL to leprosy due to its strong skin tropism and the availability of ancestry-matched full summary statistics GWAS data from Chinese populations. Through integrative analysis, we identified 11 proteins associated with leprosy. Compared with GWAS-reported risk genes, these proteins could be classified into three categories: (i) ALDH2, previously implicated by a GWAS risk locus in its exon64, was linked by PWAS to protein abundance and disease risk, providing a potential molecular mechanism that reinforces the prior findings; (ii) PWAS also prioritized the most likely functional gene at the loci where previous fine-mapping analysis could only narrow down to multiple candidates52, including TSFM, APOBR, ANKRD13A, and DECR1 (Supplementary Fig. 16); (iii) Six genes had not been reported as leprosy risk genes, because variants within these genes and nearby LD blocks did not show association evidence at genome-wide significance in GWAS (P-values = 10−5 to 10−7; Supplementary Fig. 17). These genes were identified by PWAS, which is designed to test the joint contributions of multiple genetic variants within a region, including those with minor effects that are likely missed by single-variant-based GWAS. The prioritization results from our integrative analyses were further supported by in vivo functional validation. ALDH2 was highlighted as the strongest causal candidate for leprosy, and functional assays demonstrated its protective role in host defense during mycobacterial infection. Mechanistically, these findings are consistent with ALDH2’s role in macrophage efferocytosis65, lipid degradation and foam cell formation66, processes critical for macrophage homeostasis during infection. While macrophages provide a relevant cellular model to investigate ALDH2 function in leprosy, its expression is not restricted to macrophages (Supplementary Fig. 12 d), suggesting that additional cell types may also contribute to its effects in disease, which warrants further investigation. Beyond ALDH2, NAA25 provides another example of a previously unreported leprosy risk gene prioritized by our integrative analyses and supported by functional validation in our study. NAA25 encodes a component of the NatB N-terminal acetyltransferase complex, suggesting a potential link between protein N-terminal acetylation and host defense against mycobacterial infection, consistent with emerging evidence implicating protein acetylation in host antimicrobial immunity and inflammation67,68. Collectively, integrating skin pQTL with GWAS data not only strengthens the mechanistic interpretation of established risk genes but also prioritizes likely functional genes and highlights previously unrecognized candidates for leprosy.

The integration of European GWASs for AD and psoriasis with our skin pQTL data identified both known and previously unreported disease-associated proteins, demonstrating the cross-ancestry applicability of skin pQTL. PWAS identified multiple significant proteins for each disease, encompassing both known and previously unreported candidates. For AD, the findings implicated pathways such as PI3K–AKT–mTOR signaling via AAMDC, suggesting new molecular links between metabolic regulation and skin inflammation. For psoriasis, hnRNP A/B encoded by HNRNPAB emerged as a putative causal protein, pointing to the potential involvement of nuclear ribonucleoprotein complexes in autoimmune pathogenesis. However, relative to the ancestry-matched leprosy analysis, differences in genetic architecture across ancestry may attenuate the power of causal inference in AD and psoriasis. For example, although loss-of-function (LoF) variants in the filaggrin gene (FLG) are well established as major genetic risk factors for AD, these variants are largely restricted to European populations and are rare in Asian populations69. In our dataset, known AD-associated FLG LoF variants (e.g., 2282del4, R501X) were either absent or present at very low frequency and therefore did not meet the inclusion criteria for pQTL analysis, resulting in no significant FLG signals being detected in our pQTL/PWAS/MR/colocalization analyses. In contrast, rs671 in ALDH2, a shared causal variant for both ALDH2 pQTL and leprosy, has a MAF of 0.24 in East Asians but < 0.0001 in European populations70. These examples illustrated how population-specific signals can be missed or misinterpreted in trans-ethnic analyses. Previous studies have suggested heterogeneity in the genetic susceptibility to AD and psoriasis across ancestries71,72, highlighting the importance of using ancestry-matched pQTL reference panels in combination with GWAS data for accurate interpretation of disease-associated variants. Therefore, cross-ancestry candidate prioritization based on PWAS, MR, and colocalization analyses should be interpreted as suggestive and evaluated in conjunction with other lines of evidence, rather than as definitive proof. Further validation, particularly in independent cohorts and functional studies, will further substantiate these findings.

Together, our study establishes the first skin pQTL resource and demonstrates its utility in elucidating the genetic regulation of protein abundance and its relevance to dermal disorders. Our findings reveal that the skin proteome is shaped by both age- and tissue-specific genetic mechanisms, and that integrating skin pQTL with GWAS enables the identification of causal proteins underlying disease susceptibility. Beyond its immediate insights, this resource lays the foundation for future studies in skin biology, disease gene discovery, and precision dermatology.

Methods

Ethics statement

This study complied with all relevant ethical regulations and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. This study protocol was approved by the institutional review board (IRB) committee at the Shandong Provincial Institute of Dermatology and Venereology, Shandong Academy of Medical Sciences (Approval No. 20221223KYKTKS001). Written informed consent was obtained from donors for their participation in the study, including on the publication of indirectly identifying information.

Study participants and sample collection

We obtained 249 samples of normal human skin tissues from the patients who sought medical treatment for fractures in Shandong Provincial Hospital Affiliated to Shandong First Medical University. In detail, skin samples were obtained as full-thickness biopsies (containing both epidermis and dermis) from otherwise healthy tissue excised during orthopedic surgery in fracture patients. After excision, visible blood was removed, excess subcutaneous fat was trimmed, and the tissue was snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C until further processing. Although the exact size of the excised tissue varied across patients due to surgical differences, each piece was at least ~ 1 × 1 cm to ensure sufficient material for both RNA-seq and proteomic measurement. From the same specimen, at least 0.5 × 1 cm was allocated to proteomics and the remainder to RNA-seq. Tissue subdivision was performed on the same full-thickness specimen without preferential separation of epidermal- or dermal-dominant regions to minimize potential discrepancies between omics layers. Genome-wide genotyping was performed using peripheral blood from the same individuals.

The number of samples used for generating transcriptome, genotype, and proteome, as well as the number of overlapping samples among them, are provided in Supplementary Data 1 and Supplementary Fig. 7. Both sample-level and summary-level demographic information, including sex, age, sampling site, and ethnicity, are provided in Supplementary Data 1.

Animals

ALDH2 knockout (ALDH2-/-) mice were generated on a C57BL/6 J (wild-type, WT) background and backcrossed with in-house C57BL/6 J mice for at least ten generations to establish a congenic strain. The ALDH2-/- mice were kindly provided by Professor Yuguo Chen (Qilu Hospital of Shandong University, Jinan, China). Age-matched WT C57BL/6 J mice (8–10 weeks old) were purchased from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China). To avoid the potential effects of estrogen, only male mice were used in this study. All mice were maintained under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle, ambient temperature of 22–26 °C, and relative humidity of 50–60%. All experimental procedures were approved by the Scientific Investigation Board of the Shandong Provincial Hospital for Skin Diseases and the Shandong Provincial Institute of Dermatology and Venereology (Approval No. 20221227DWLL001).

Sample preparation for mass spectrometry

Technical support for this portion of the work was provided by the Genechem Co., Ltd. Briefly, the skin samples were frozen in liquid nitrogen and ground with a pestle and mortar. 5 times volume of TCA/acetone (1:9) was added to the powder and mixed by vortex. The mixture was placed at − 20 °C for 4 h, and centrifuged at 6000 × g for 40 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was discarded. The pre-cooling acetone was added and washed three times. The precipitation was air dried. 30 times volume of SDT buffer (4% SDS, 100 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.6) was added to 20–30 mg powder, mixed and boiled for 5 min. The lysate was sonicated and then boiled for 15 min. After centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 15 min, the supernatant was filtered with 0.22 μm filters. The filtrate was quantified with the BCA Protein Assay Kit (P0012, Beyotime). 20 μg of proteins for each sample were mixed with a 6× loading buffer respectively and boiled for 5 min. The proteins were separated on 12% SDS-PAGE gel. Protein bands were visualized by Coomassie Blue R-250 staining.

200 μg of proteins for each sample were reduced with 100 mM DTT for 5 min at 100 °C. Then the detergent, DTT and other low-molecular-weight components were removed using UA buffer (8 M urea, 150 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.5) by repeated ultrafiltration (Sartorius, 30kDa). Then 100 μl iodoacetamide (100 mM IAA in UA buffer) was added to block reduced cysteine residues, and the samples were incubated for 30 min in darkness. The filters were washed with 100 μl UA buffer three times and then 100 μl 50 mM NH4HCO3 buffer twice. Finally, the protein suspensions were digested with 4 μg trypsin (Promega) in 40 μl 50 mM NH4HCO3 buffer overnight at 37 °C, and the resulting peptides were collected as a filtrate. The peptide segment was desalted by the C18 column. The peptide content was estimated by UV light spectral density at 280 nm using an extinction coefficient of 1.1 of 0.1% (g/l) solution that was calculated on the basis of the frequency of tryptophan and tyrosine in vertebrate proteins.

LC-MS/MS analysis

For spectral library generation, pooled peptides from all samples were fractionated by reversed-phase chromatography using the Agilent 1260 infinity II HPLC. The peptide mixture was diluted with buffer A (10 mM HCOONH4, 5% ACN, pH 10.0) and loaded onto a XBridge Peptide BEH C18 Column (5 μm, 4.6 mm × 100 mm column). The peptides were eluted at a flow rate of 1 mL/min with a gradient of 0–7% buffer B (10 mM HCOONH4, 85% ACN, pH 10.0) for 30 min, 7–40% buffer B during 30–58 min, 40–100% buffer B during 58–70 min, 100% buffer B during 70–85 min. The elution was monitored at 214 nm based on the UV light trace, and fractions were collected every 1 min during 5–50 min. The collected fractions were dried down via vacuum centrifugation at 45 °C. The peptides were reconstituted and diluted to a final concentration of 10 ng/μl in 0.1% formic acid (FA) into 12 fractions and analyzed in data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode. Pooled peptides generated from all samples were used for spectral library construction. HeLa cell samples were included as QC samples for routine instrument monitoring.

Under DDA mode, a total of 200 ng of each fraction was loaded onto individual Evosep platform for desalting and then washed with 20 μl 0.1% FA followed by the addition of 150 μl 0.1% FA to keep the Evotips wet until analysis. Then the samples were analyzed on an Evosep One system (Evosep, Denmark) coupled to a timsTOF Pro (Bruker, Bremen, Germany) equipped with a CaptiveSpray source. Peptides were separated on a 15 cm × 150 μm analytical column, 1.9 μm C18 beads with a packed emitter tip (Evosep, Denmark). The column temperature was maintained at 50 °C using an integrated column oven (Bruker, Germany). The timsTOF Pro was operated in PASEF mode. Mass Range 100–1700 m/z, 1/K0 Start 0.6 V s/cm2 End 1.6 V s/cm2, Ramp time 100 ms, Lock Duty Cycle to 100%, Capillary Voltage 1500 V, Dry Gas 3 l/min, Dry Temp 180 °C, PASEF settings: 10 MS/MS scans (total cycle time 1.16 sec), charge range 0-5, active exclusion for 0.4 min, Scheduling Target intensity 10000, Intensity threshold 2500, CID collision energy 20–59 eV.

Under data independent acquisition (DIA) mode, 200 ng peptide sample mixed with iRT was analyzed on an Evosep One system coupled to a timsTOF Pro equipped with a CaptiveSpray source. Peptides were separated on a 15 cm × 150 μm analytical column, 1.9 μm C18 beads with a packed emitter tip. The column temperature was maintained at 50°C using an integrated column oven. The mass spectrometer was performed in a data-independent acquisition (DIA) parallel accumulation-serial fragmentation (PASEF) mode. Fragment analysis was subdivided into 56 × 25 Th precursor isolation windows from m/z 100 to 1700 with 1.9 s cycle time. The collision energy was ramped linearly as a function of the mobility from 59 eV at 1/K0 = 1.6 Vs cm−2 to 20 eV at 1/K0 = 0.6 Vs cm−2. We adapted the instrument firmware to perform data-independent isolation of multiple precursor windows within a single TIMS separation (100 ms). We used a method with two windows in each 100 ms diaPASEF scan. 100 of these scans covered the diagonal scan line for doubly and triply charged peptides in the m/z – ion mobility plane with narrow 25 m/z precursor windows.

MS data analysis

DDA data were processed and analyzed by Spectronaut (version 16.2.220903.53000, Biognosys AG, Switzerland) with default settings to generate an initial target list. Spectronaut was set up to search against the FASTA sequence database consisting of Uniprot_HomoSapiens_20377_20220308_swissprot_9606 and iRT peptides sequence (Biognosys|iRT-Kits | ) with the parameters of trypsin, max missed cleavages at 2, fixed modification of carbamidomethyl (C), dynamic modification of oxidation (M) and acetyl (Protein N-term). All reported peptides were determined by a false discovery rate (FDR = N (decoy) * 2 / (N (decoy) + N (target))) ≤ 1% with 99% confidence for protein identification. The original raw files and DDA searching results were exported into Spectronaut to construct a spectral library.

The DIA data in the spectral library were analyzed using the Spectronaut with the parameters of dynamic iRT for retention time prediction, enable interference on the MS2 level correction, and enable cross-run normalization. Spectronaut will determine the ideal extraction window dynamically depending on iRT calibration and gradient stability. Q-value cutoffs on the precursor and protein levels were applied 1%. Proteins identified without unique peptides were excluded from downstream analyses.

Targeted parallel reaction monitoring (PRM) proteomics

Parallel Reaction Monitoring (PRM), a high-resolution and high-precision targeted proteomics approach, was employed to validate the accuracy of 4D-DIA in skin proteome measurements. We applied 4D-PRM to an additional set of ten skin tissue samples in parallel with 4D-DIA analysis of the same specimens. In total, 100 target proteins spanning five orders of magnitude in abundance (Supplementary Data 11), from low to high levels, were selected to systematically evaluate quantification performance across different abundance ranges. Protein extraction, SDS–PAGE and FASP digestion were performed using the same procedures as for the 4D-DIA experiments. Peptide samples were analyzed on a nanoElute (Bruker, Bremen, Germany) coupled to a timsTOF Pro (Bruker, Bremen, Germany) fitted with a CaptiveSpray source. The timsTOF Pro was operated in PRM-PASEF mode in positive ion detection. Acquisition parameters were: precursor m/z range 100–1700; ion-mobility (1/K0) range 0.6–1.6 V·s/cm²; accumulation time (Accu time) 50 ms and ramp time 50 ms; Lock Duty Cycle 100%; capillary voltage 1500 V; dry gas flow 3 L/min; dry temperature 180 °C; charge range 0–5; CID collision energy 10 eV. PRM data were analyzed with SpectroDive (Biognosys) using the UniProt human Swiss-Prot database.

Quality control of population-level skin proteomes

The quality of proteomic data was controlled by multiple filtering steps separately, as previously reported8. The proteomic matrix contained missing values, which may be due to the low abundance in certain samples or technical issues. First, we excluded samples in which the number of quantified proteins was less than 80% of the median value across all samples. Following this, only proteins quantified in at least 10 individuals were retained to eliminate potential false-positive signals caused by technical issues. To ensure unambiguous protein identification, we further excluded protein groups lacking discriminating (unique) peptides. Subsequently, we imputed the missing values with 1/2 of the minimum measured value for that protein across samples in the matrix, a method also employed in previous studies8,73. In total, 18.96% of all protein–sample measurements were imputed, consistent with ranges as previously reported73,74. At the protein level, 35.9% of proteins had no missing values, and the majority showed low levels of missingness (median = 7 missing values per protein). These steps resulted in a final protein abundance matrix containing 248 samples and 5774 proteins for the following analysis, of which 199 samples had matched transcriptome data.

Co-expression analysis of skin proteomes

The co-expression map of skin proteomes was conducted as previously described29. Briefly, we excluded proteins that were detected in less than 50 of the 249 samples. For the remaining 5596 proteins, we used the treeClust R package to calculate all 15,654,810 pairwise dissimilarities via the treeClust.dist function. The treeClust dissimilarities were transformed to similarities using the WGCNA R package, and then performed a sigmoid transformation to an adjacency matrix. Next, the adjacency matrix was transformed into a topological overlap matrix using WGCNA’s TOMsimilarity function. These TOM similarities are the co-regulation scores visualized by t-SNE. Organelles were labeled based on subcellular locations annotated by a previous study29.

Cell type deconvolution using bulk RNA-seq

Given that proteomics-based deconvolution methods are not yet well established, we inferred cellular composition based on our bulk RNA-seq data using a scRNA-seq atlas of healthy human skin as a reference, a more mature and robust approach. Because the bulk RNA-seq and proteomic data were generated from matched aliquots of the same skin samples, the RNA-based cell-type proportions, although not identical, are expected to closely approximate the cellular composition underlying the proteomic measurements.

Cell type proportions in bulk skin transcriptomic samples were estimated using the reference-based deconvolution framework implemented in the BisqueRNA package75. Briefly, a publicly available single-cell RNA-seq dataset of healthy human skin derived from the arm and trunk, anatomically matched to our sampling sites, was used as the reference32. Bulk RNA-seq data were matched to the single-cell reference based on shared gene symbols, and only overlapping genes were retained for deconvolution. Cell type proportions for each bulk sample were inferred using the ReferenceBasedDecomposition function with parameters “markers = NULL, use.overlap = FALSE”. The resulting estimates represent the relative abundance of each cell type within each sample and were subsequently included as covariates in downstream pQTL analyses to account for cell composition heterogeneity.

Assessment of inter-individual variability of skin proteomes

PCA was performed on the centered and scaled proteome matrix using the ‘prcomp’ function in R to further investigate the skin proteomic distances among different individuals. Variance partitioning analysis was used to estimate the contribution of age, sex, sampling site, and cell composition to inter-individual variation in protein abundance. For cell composition, the 12 inferred cell-type proportions were first transformed using an isometric log-ratio (ILR) transformation to account for their compositional nature, as the proportions are constrained to sum to one. PCA was then applied to the ILR-transformed coordinates, and the first five PCs were retained to summarize the major axes of cell-composition variation while reducing dimensionality and limiting over-parameterization in the variance-partitioning model. For each protein, we fitted a linear regression model as follows: lm(log2(protein abundance) ~ age + sex + sampling_site + Cell_PC1 + Cell_PC2 + Cell_PC3 + Cell_PC4 + Cell_PC5). The percentage of variance associated with each factor was quantified using the partial-Eta squared (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\eta }_{p}^{2}$$\end{document}ηp2), which represents the proportion of variance uniquely attributable to a given factor after accounting for the other factors in the model.

Gene ontology and gene set enrichment analyses

Gene ontology (GO) analysis was performed using the “enrichGO” function of R clusterProfiler package with a cutoff of adjusted.P < 0.05. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using the “clusterProfiler” package in R. Proteins were ranked by their Spearman correlation coefficient (rho) with chronological age. The ranked list was used as input for GSEA against Gene Ontology Biological Process (GO:BP) gene sets obtained from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB C5 collection) via the “msigdbr” package. Only gene sets with 10–500 members were considered. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using the “fgsea” algorithm with default weighting exponent, and P-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure (FDR < 0.05).

Identification of age-correlated proteins (ACPs)

Protein abundances were first normalized using variance stabilization normalization (VSN)76, and the effects of sex and sampling site were subsequently corrected using the “lmFit” function. Age-correlated proteins (ACPs) were then identified by performing Spearman correlation analysis between chronological age and the corrected protein abundances. Proteins with Spearman’s correlation coefficient (rho) > 0 and FDR < 0.05 (Benjamini–Hochberg) were defined as positive ACPs, while those with rho < 0 and FDR < 0.05 were defined as negative ACPs. To minimize bias from tied age values in Spearman correlation analysis, protein abundances were averaged across samples with the same age for each protein, resulting in 71 distinct age points ranging from 13 to 95 years. Detailed information on the identified ACPs is provided in Supplementary Data 2.

Skin transcriptome profiling

Total RNA was isolated from each sample using the RNA mini kit (Qiagen, Germany). RNA quality was examined by gel electrophoresis and with Qubit (Thermo, Waltham, MA, USA). For RNA-sequencing, 1 μg of total RNA was used for library construction. After removal of rRNA using the specific probes, sequencing libraries were generated using VAHTSTM Total RNA-seq (H/M/R) Library Prep Kit for Illumina®. The libraries were sequenced as 151-bp paired-end reads using Illumina Novaseq6000 according to the manufacturer’s instructions by the commercial service of Genergy Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

For raw pair-end reads, adapters and poor-quality bases were trimmed off using Skewer version 0.2.2. The clean reads were aligned to the genome reference genome and comprehensive gene annotation GTF file downloaded from the Ensembl website (http://ensemblgenomes.org/) using STAR version 2.5.3a under the settings “--twopassMode Basic --outSAMstrandField intronMotif --alignSJstitchMismatchNmax 5 −1 5 5”77. The expression levels of all annotated genes were quantified using StringTie version 1.3.1 with the settings “--rf -A -B -e -G”78. The gene expression is measured by the FPKM (Fragments Per Kilobase of gene per Million fragments mapped). The calculation formula of FPKM is: FPKM = total fragments / [mapped reads (millions) × exon length (KB)]. StringTie was used to calculate the FPKM expression of each sample by adding 1 and taking a log2 transformation.

We excluded two samples with a low uniquely mapping rate. Only annotated genes with ten or more samples with expression estimates > 0.1 FPKM and an aligned read count of six or more among all samples were considered significantly expressed and used in the following analysis. The final RNA expression matrix contained 206 samples and 30,775 genes, of which 199 samples had matched proteome data.

Analysis of age-associated RNA–protein uncoupling

Sampled individuals were stratified into a young group (ages 13–38, lowest quartile) and an old group (ages 62–95, highest quartile). For each gene, normalized protein abundance was matched to corresponding mRNA expression levels (FPKM) using gene IDs. Spearman correlation was then calculated between protein and mRNA levels for each gene within each age group. Proteins with correlation P < 0.05 and rho > 0 were considered positively correlated, while those with P < 0.05 and rho < 0 were considered negatively correlated. We also compared the overall distribution of RNA-protein rho in young and old groups using the cumulative distribution fraction. Moreover, we defined genes with positive RNA-protein correlation in young groups but lost correlation (P ≥ 0.05 or rho ≤ 0) in old groups as decoupling genes. Age-related changes in protein abundance variability were assessed by calculating the standard deviation (SD) of normalized intensity values for each protein in the young and old age groups.

Regression analysis of age-associated RNA–protein uncoupling

Age-associated changes in RNA–protein correlation were quantified for each protein as the absolute difference in correlation coefficients between the youngest and oldest groups (|Δr | ). Proteins were ranked based on |Δr | , and the top-ranked proteins were used to construct multi-protein regression models. To determine the optimal number of proteins to include, models were fitted using increasing numbers of top-ranked proteins (k = 5, 8, 10, 13, 15, and 20). Model performance was evaluated using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and adjusted R2. The optimal model size was selected at k = 10, where model fit was maximized, and further increases in model complexity yielded minimal improvement.

Linear regression models were then fitted with age as the dependent variable and protein abundance as the independent variable, adjusting for sex and sampling site. Standardized regression coefficients (β) were calculated to assess the relative contribution of each protein. In addition, partial R2 and leave-one-out analyses (ΔR²) were performed to quantify the independent contribution of each protein to variation in age. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF). RNA-adjusted protein residuals were computed by regressing protein abundance on the corresponding RNA expression levels and covariates (sex and sampling site) for each protein. These residuals were then used as predictors in the regression model in place of protein abundance, and the analysis was repeated under the same framework.

Construction of proteomic clocks for skin age prediction

The approach for building the proteomic clock was adapted from previous methods18. Briefly, age-correlated proteins (ACPs) in skin were used as input features, and the chronological age of each individual served as the training label. A multivariate linear regression model with elastic net regularization was implemented using the glmnet package in R. Hyperparameter tuning and model training were conducted via random search using the mlr3verse framework. To evaluate model performance, we applied leave-one-out cross-validation, in which each sample was iteratively excluded from training and used for prediction. To identify the optimal set of ACPs, we tested different ACPs subsets based on the P-values thresholds of Spearman’s protein-age correlation (Supplementary Fig. 5e). The final model was selected based on the lowest mean absolute error (MAE). To assess feature contributions, we estimated the importance of each protein in the final model using the permutation-based FeatureImp function from the iml package. Proteins with higher importance scores were considered key contributors to the model. Detailed information on eight proteomic clocks used in the predictive model and their importance is provided in Supplementary Data 3.

An independent skin proteomic dataset generated from the PRM–DIA comparison experiment was used for external validation. This dataset comprised 10 individuals with ages ranging from 34 to 79 years and was independent of the discovery cohort. Protein abundances were processed using the same normalization procedure as in the discovery dataset. The final proteomic clock model, consisting of eight proteins and their corresponding coefficients derived from the discovery cohort, was directly applied to the independent dataset to estimate biological age. Predicted age for each sample was calculated as a linear combination of standardized protein abundances and the corresponding model coefficients, plus the intercept. No model retraining or feature re-selection was performed. Model performance in the independent cohort was evaluated by calculating the Pearson correlation between predicted age and chronological age, as well as the MAE.

Genotyping data

All DNA genotyping was performed on blood-derived DNA samples. DNA from 198 donors was genotyped using the Illumina Asian Screening Array BeadChip. For quality control, variants were excluded from analysis if they are: (1) located on the X, Y, and mitochondrial chromosomes; (2) intensity-only SNPs and copy number variations; (3) with an MAF < 1% or call rate <95%; (4) with a P-value of the Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) < 1.0 × 10−8. Next, samples were removed with a call rate of < 96%, one of the duplicated or related individuals (first-, second-, or third-degree familial relationships) with a lower call rate, and population outliers based on the PC analysis method.

PLINK v2.00a2LM (https://www.cog-genomics.org/plink/2.0/) was used to convert the genotype files from plink format to vcf-4.2 format based on the human reference genome (UCSC Genome Browser hg38). The ChinaMAP reference panel that includes 10,155 unrelated Chinese individuals can achieve high-quality imputation for genetic studies of Chinese populations, and it was used as a reference panel79. The imputation was performed on the ChinaMAP imputation server by uploading all genotype vcf files to the ChinaMAP browser (www.mBiobank.com). The imputed SNPs were included in the subsequent association analysis to confirm whether they were well imputed (r2 > 0.8) and had an MAF ≥ 5% in all samples and SNPs without significant deviation from the HWE in the controls (p > 1.0 × 10−5). Finally, 4,524,652 qualified variants were retained in this study.

Cis-pQTL mapping

We analyzed a dataset comprising 186 samples for which both proteome and genotyping data were available. To ensure compatibility of protein IDs with other datasets in downstream analyses, we split each protein group into individual proteins, assigning each the same abundance as the original group. For quality control at the protein level, only proteins encoded by autosomal genes and those with missing values in less than half of the samples were retained for pQTL analysis. At the sample level, samples with protein identifications below 80% of the median across all samples were filtered out. We imputed the missing values with 1/2 of the minimum measured value for that protein across samples in the matrix, as previously reported8. The quality-controlled protein matrix containing 5192 proteins and 186 samples was normalized and standardized according to a previously developed approach7. Briefly, we centered and scaled each protein by subtracting out the mean value for the gene and dividing by the standard deviation. We then quantile-normalized the protein abundance in each individual to fit a standard normal distribution.

A pQTL was defined as cis when it was located within 1 Mb distance of the gene’s TSS. TSSs annotations of genes were accessed from Ensembl GRCh38 Version 110. cis-pQTL were identified by linear regression implemented in FastQTL38, adjusting for sex, age, anatomical sites, inferred cell-type composition, the top five genotype-based principal components (PCs), and the top ten PCs from the protein abundance matrix to account for hidden confounding effects. PCs were estimated by QTLtools mode pca80, and the optimal number of PCs was determined by findPC81. We tested variants within 1 Mb of each gene’s TSS with minor allele frequency (MAF) ≥ 0.05 and at least four samples carrying the minor allele. Nominal P-values for each variant-gene pair were estimated using the nominal pass mode of FastQTL. The nominal P-values of the most highly associated variant per gene (lead pQTL variant) was adjusted by empirical P-values, extrapolated from a beta distribution fitted to adaptive permutations with the setting “--permute 1000 10000” using the permutation pass mode. These gene-level empirical P-values were adjusted for multiple testing across genes using Storey’s q-value method, and genes with FDR ≤ 0.05 were considered significant (pGene). To identify the list of all significant variant-gene pairs, variants with a nominal P-value below the gene-level threshold were considered significant pQTL variants. For each pGene, the variant with the lowest nominal P-value from the cis-pQTL analysis was designated as the lead pQTL.

Conditional analysis

To identify secondary signals beyond the lead pQTL for each pGene, we performed conditional analysis using GCTA-COJO39. For each pQTL locus, a stepwise model selection procedure was applied, starting from the lead pQTL identified in the primary scan. At each step, the most significant variant was added as a covariate, and association testing was repeated on the residuals to detect additional independent signals. This iterative process continued until no further variants reached the significance threshold. To maintain consistency with the primary analysis, the same permutation-derived genome-wide significance threshold was used. All analyses were conducted using individual-level genotype data from 198 individuals to accurately account for linkage disequilibrium.

Fine-mapping of cis-pQTL loci using SuSiE

Fine-mapping was performed for each pGene to refine putative causal variants using the susieR R package40. Summary statistics from cis-pQTL mapping were used to compute Z-scores. Linkage disequilibrium (LD) matrices were calculated from genotype data of the same individuals using PLINK82. Given prior evidence from conditional analysis indicating a single independent signal per locus, the number of effect components was set to L = 1. Default parameters were used for credible set construction (coverage = 0.95; minimum absolute correlation = 0.5). For each locus, posterior inclusion probabilities (PIP) were computed for all variants, and the variant with the highest PIP was recorded. Credible sets were defined as the minimal set of variants whose cumulative PIP reached the specified coverage (0.95).

The annotation of functional region for pQTL

For all identified significant pQTL variants, the predicted effects of the variants were annotated with Ensembl VEP release 10584. Specifically, for each pQTL variant, we only considered its effects associated with its associated gene, discarding annotations relative to other genes. In cases where a variant had multiple effects annotated, we only retained the severest consequence according to the severity order provided by the Ensembl VEP website.

We also utilized RegulomeDB, a widely used database for evaluating the functional relevance of genetic variants42, which assigns functional confidence scores to variants by assessing their relationships with regulatory elements. For simplicity, we collapsed RegulomeDB ranking scores across subcategories into a single score (i.e., 1a-1f were all scored as 1), resulting in a simplified scale ranging from 7 (lowest functional likelihood) to 1 (highest functional confidence). Variants with collapsed scores ≤ 2 were considered high-confidence for functional impact, consistent with previous reports 85.

Trans-pQTL mapping

To identify trans-pQTL, we used QTLtools80 with the trans module in approximate permutation mode, specifying the parameters “--sample 1000 --window 1e6 --threshold 1e-5”. This approach tests each variant–protein pair where the variant is located at least 1 Mb away from the TSS of the gene encoding the protein or located on a different chromosome. For each protein, QTLtools performs 1,000 permutations and fits a beta distribution to the minimum p-values obtained under the null, allowing the calculation of adjusted p-values for each tested variant–protein pair. Significant trans-pQTL were defined using a 5% FDR threshold at the protein level, based on these adjusted P-values.

Cis-eQTL mapping

There were 150 samples for which both transcriptome and genotyping data were available. We only retained autosomal genes with FPKM ≥ 0.1 in ten or more samples and an aligned read count of six or more among all samples. The missing values were imputed by 1/2 of the minimum measured value for that gene across samples. The quality-controlled RNA matrix containing 30,775 genes and 150 samples was used for the following cis-eQTL analysis. cis-eQTL analysis was conducted with FastQTL following the same procedures as for cis-pQTL.

We cross-referenced our cis-eQTL with those identified in sun-exposed skin tissue from the GTEx (v8) database43, which employed a similar eQTL analysis pipeline. Effect sizes of shared eQTL were extracted from both datasets and compared across different P-value thresholds.

Permutation analysis of pQTL–eQTL overlap

To assess whether the observed overlap between pQTL and eQTL signals exceeded random expectation, we performed a permutation analysis using the full set of SNP–gene pairs tested in both analyses (n = 15,443,226) as the background. In each of 1,000 iterations, we randomly sampled SNP–gene pairs to match the number of SNP–gene pairs with nominal P < 0.01 observed in each of the pQTL or eQTL datasets, respectively. For each iteration, we computed the number of overlapping SNP–gene pairs. The empirical P-value was calculated using the plus-one method: (r + 1)/(n + 1), where r is the number of permutations with overlap greater than or equal to the observed, and n is the total number of permutations. Enrichment was defined as the ratio of the observed overlap to the mean overlap under the null distribution.

Cis eQTL and pQTL replication

We first compared the effect size of each SNP-gene pair overlapped by cis-eQTL and cis-pQTL under different nominal P-value thresholds. To assess the replication of QTL across RNA and protein layers, we then considered the pairwise replication of pQTL at the RNA level. Replication was defined as per the methodology described previously17: the lead pQTL variant of a pGene showed a nominal P-value ≤ 0.01 in the eQTL dataset, along with consistent effect directions. Vice versa, replication of eQTL at the protein level also followed the same criteria outlined above.

Cis eQTL and pQTL colocalization

Colocalization analysis was conducted to evaluate the likelihood that shared causal variants influence both gene expression and protein abundance. For each gene with both eQTL and pQTL signals, we used the coloc package in R with default settings to test whether the associations for the same gene were colocalized46. Evidence for colocalization was assessed by the posterior probability of hypothesis 4 (PP.H4), which represents the probability that both traits are associated and share a common causal variant. Genes with PP.H4 ≥ 0.7 were considered to have strong evidence for a shared causal variant underlying the cis-eQTL and cis-pQTL associations. Regional association plots with P-values shown were generated using the online LocusZoom tool (http://locuszoom.sph.umich.edu/).

Cross-tissue replication of skin pGenes

Cross-tissue replication of skin pGenes was assessed using LD analysis combined with conditional analysis (Supplementary Fig. 11). Specifically, we first identified 30 overlaps between our skin pGenes (n = 36) and pGenes reported in six non-skin tissues, including plasma8,9,11,49 and 5 solid tissues (heart, liver, lung, thyroid, and colon)24. For each of these overlapping pGenes, we extracted all associated SNPs in skin and in another non-skin tissue, respectively. For each non-skin pQTL, if the same variant was also present in skin pQTL for that pGene, we directly designated this variant as a conditional variant and conducted conditional analysis on the skin pQTL summary statistics for that pGene using GCTA-COJO with the parameters “gcta64 --diff-freq 0.2 --cojo-wind 100000 --cojo-collinear 0.8”. If the non-skin pQTL variant (SNP_non-skin) was absent among the associated variants for a given pGene in skin, we performed pairwise LD analysis between that SNP_non-skin and all skin associated variants (SNP_skin) for that pGene using PLINK2 with the parameters “plink2 --ld SNP_skin SNP_non-skin”82 (Supplementary Fig. 11a). For each SNP_skin showing strong LD (R² > 0.8) with the SNP_non-skin, the SNP_skin was considered a proxy for that SNP_non-skin. Each such proxy SNP was then, in turn, used as the conditional variant on the skin pQTL summary statistics of that pGene (Supplementary Fig. 11b).

After performing conditional analysis for every non-skin pQTL SNP against the corresponding skin pGene summary statistics, we recorded the minimum conditional P-value (min P’) per SNP. For each pGene–tissue pair, we then selected the maximum of these P_min values (P’MaxofMin), representing the greatest attenuation of the skin pGene association attributable to conditioning on that non-skin tissue (Supplementary Fig. 11b). If the P’MaxofMin did not pass the original permutation-derived genome-wide significance threshold for that skin pGene, we considered the skin pGene to be replicated in that non-skin tissue (i.e., at least one non-skin SNP recapitulated the skin association). Conversely, the pGene was considered not replicated in that tissue (Supplementary Fig. 11b). Detailed information on skin-specific or replicated pGenes is provided in Supplementary Data 8.

PWAS for leprosy, psoriasis and AD

The PWAS of leprosy was conducted using FUSION software53 with custom SNP-expression weights generated from our skin proteome dataset. By integrating pre-computed reference weights of skin proteomes with leprosy GWAS summary statistics (7738 cases and 7284 controls)52, FUSION estimated the associations of each protein abundance with leprosy. Specifically, reference weights were modeled using SNPs within a 1 Mb window around the TSS, applying the top1, BLUP, LASSO, BSLMM or elastic net models in FUSION, depending on which achieved the highest five-fold cross-validation R2. The calculated expression weights of those proteins with a statistically significant SNP-based heritability estimate (hsq P-value < 0.05) were then combined with GWAS summary data to impute association statistics between protein abundance and leprosy. LD was estimated using East Asian populations in the 1000 Genomes Project86. Multiple testing correction was applied using the FDR method via Storey’s q-value method, and genes that had a q-value < 0.05 were considered PWAS-significant genes. For the PWAS of psoriasis and AD, previously published GWAS summary statistics were used58, with similar procedures applied except that LD was estimated based on European ancestry data from the 1000 Genomes Project.

Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis

To further infer causal relationships between PWAS-significant skin proteins and related skin diseases, we performed a two-sample MR analysis following previous procedures87,88. Specifically, we selected conditionally independent skin cis-pQTL from full summary statistics as candidate instruments. Linkage disequilibrium (LD) clumping (LD r2 < 0.001) was applied to remove pQTL in LD with the lead SNP. We then harmonized effect alleles of instrumental variables (IVs) between pQTL and disease GWAS data. To reduce weak instrument bias, we calculated F-statistics for each pQTL and retained instruments with F > 10. In cases where only one SNP remained post-selection, we employed the Wald ratio to estimate causality between exposure and outcome89. In addition, to include more instruments and apply additional MR sensitivity methods, we re-selected instruments using a more relaxed P-value cut-off (P < 0.05) and F-statistic threshold (≥ 5), and removed highly correlated SNPs through clumping (LD r2 < 0.001). For scenarios with multiple IVs, we employed the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method90. These analyses were conducted using ‘TwoSampleMR’54, ‘MendelianRandomization’91 R packages. MR-Egger was not applied in this study since all the instruments we used were within a specific genomic region rather than across the whole genome. A PWAS-significant gene was considered supported by MR if the P-value < 0.05 in either the Wald ratio or IVW analysis.

Cis pQTL and disease GWAS colocalization

In order to provide evidence of shared causal variants of skin pQTL and leprosy GWAS, we performed a colocalization analysis using the coloc package46. Coloc was run on a 1 Mb region of the gene’s TSS (± 500 kb from the TSS) with the default prior probabilities. For each test, five posterior probabilities were computed: PP.H0: no association with either the cis-pQTL or leprosy GWAS trait, PP.H1: significant leprosy GWAS signal, but no association with the protein, PP.H2: significant cis-pQTL association but no association in leprosy GWAS, PP.H3: cis-pQTL and leprosy GWAS signals arise from two independent causal variants, and PP.H4: cis-pQTL and leprosy GWAS signals arise from a shared causal variant. A PWAS-significant gene was considered supported by colocalization if PP.H4 ≥ 0.5. A similar colocalization analysis was performed for AD and psoriasis.

Bacterial strain preparation

The Mycobacterium marinum m20 (Mma20) strain, which expresses mCherry fluorescent protein, was obtained from the collection of the National Institute of Public Health and the Environment (Bilthoven, The Netherlands) (Accession number, 2000-01053) and generously provided by Professor Annemarie H. Meijer (Leiden University, the Netherlands). The strain was cultured at 32 °C, 100 rpm in Mycobacterium Complete Medium (Gene-optimal) containing hygromycin B (50 μg/mL) (Solarbio).

ALDH2 knockdown and qPCR validation in macrophages

The human monocytic cell line THP-1 was maintained in culture medium composed of RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 μ/mL of penicillin and 100 μg/mL of streptomycin. The cells were passaged every 3 days. THP-1 cells suspended in culture medium were distributed to 12-well plates at the density of 1 × 106 cells/well and differentiated into macrophages during 24 h of culture with 100 ng/ml of phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) at 37 °C with 5% CO2. After a 1-day resting period in PMA-free complete medium, macrophages were transfected with either ALDH2-targeting siRNA (5′-GCAGAUCAUUCCGUGGAAUTT-3′) or a nonspecific control siRNA synthesized by GenePharma (Shanghai, China) using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 13778150) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Total RNA was extracted from cultured cells 32 hours after transfection using Rnafast200 (Fastagen), followed by cDNA synthesis with the HiScript II Q RT SuperMix for qPCR (Vazyme). RT–qPCR was performed using the ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. ALDH2 mRNA expression levels were quantified using specific primers (Forward: 5′-ATGGCAAGCCCTATGTCATCT-3′; Reverse: 5′-CCGTGGTACTTATCAGCCCA-3′) and normalized to the β-actin reference gene (Forward: 5′-CACCATTGGCAATGAGCGGTTC-3′; Reverse: 5′-AGGTCTTTGCGGATGTCCACGT-3′).

Phagocytosis and intracellular growth of bacteria

Macrophages were infected with M. marinum Mma20 at a multiplicity of infection of 10. 3 h post-infection, un-phagocytosed Mma20 was removed by discarding the supernatant and washing cells with PBS three times. Cells were detached by 0.25% trypsin-EDTA treatment and resuspended in 100 μL of PBS and analyzed in an LSRFortessa II cytometer, using FACSDiva software (BD Biosciences). Data was analyzed using FlowJo (Treestar). The percentage of infected cells was calculated as the proportion of mCherry-positive cells, defined by fluorescence intensity above the threshold set using uninfected control cells, among total cells in the same sample. To assess macrophage bactericidal activity, intracellular bacterial growth was measured. At 24 h post-infection, macrophages were washed and lysed with 1 ml of 0.2% Triton X-100 on ice for 30 min. Serial dilutions of the cell lysates were prepared and plated onto Middlebrook 7H10 agar supplemented with 10% of OADC. After 14 days of incubation at 32 °C, the CFU was counted.

Validation of ALDH2 knockout in mouse by western blot

Mouse liver tissues were lysed using RIPA buffer (R0020; SolarBio) supplemented with protease/phosphatase inhibitors (P1049, Solarbio). Equal amounts (20 μg) of protein were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE gel and transferred onto a methanol-activated polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (PVDF, 0.45 μm; Amersham Biosciences). The membrane was blocked with 5% BSA at room temperature for 1.5 h, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with the primary antibody against ALDH2 (ABclonal, A1226; 1:5000 dilution). The next day, the membrane was incubated with the corresponding secondary antibody for 1 hour at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) and imaged with an Amersham Imager 600.

Mouse infection and phenotype observation

Age-matched mice (8–10 weeks old) of both genotypes were used within each group. Five ALDH2-/- mutants and five WT control mice were infected with 5 × 107 M. marinum in 100 μL PBS via the tail vein, and were euthanized 12 days post infection. Visible tail lesions were examined, and a portion of the tails containing lesions was excised, fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin and decalcified in a decalcification solution (ZSGB-BIO, ZLI-9307) for 12 h. The tails were then embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 5 μm thickness, and stained with a hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining kit (Solarbio, G1120) and Acid-Fast Bacillus (AFB) staining kit (Solarbio, G1170) for histologic evaluation and mycobacteria examination. To determine CFU, the remaining tails were cut into small pieces, homogenized in 2 ml PBS with a gentleMACS™ Dissociator (Miltenyi Biotec), serially diluted and plated onto 7H9 agar plates (hopebio) supplemented with 10% (v/v) ADC additive (hopebio). CFU were counted after 7 days of incubation at 32 °C.

AAV-mediated knockdown of NAA25 in mouse

To assess the in vivo function of NAA25, an adeno-associated virus (AAV)-mediated knockdown approach was employed. Recombinant AAV vectors expressing shRNA targeting mouse Naa25 were constructed using the pAAV9-U6-MCS-shRNA-ZsGreen1 backbone (Lishan Biotechnology). A scrambled shRNA sequence (pAAV9-U6-shRNA [Scramble]-ZsGreen1) was used as a negative control (Scramble-shRNA-F: GATCCCTAAGGTTAAGTCGCCCTCGCTCGAGCGAGGGCGACTTAACCTTAGGTTTTTG; Scramble-shRNA-R: TCGACAAAAACCTAAGGTTAAGTCGCCCTCGCTCGAGCGAGGGCGACTTAACCTTAGG). Target sequences were designed using an online tool based on the mouse Naa25 reference transcript (NM_172722.4), with the selected targeting sequence 5′-CAGCATGGATAAGAGTCAGAA-3′. AAV packaging and production were performed by Lishan Biotechnology. Briefly, recombinant AAV particles were generated by co-transfecting HEK293 cells with the shRNA expression plasmid and helper plasmids using polyethyleneimine (PEI). Viral particles were harvested 72 h post-transfection, purified by iodixanol density gradient ultracentrifugation, and concentrated by ultrafiltration. Viral titers were determined prior to in vivo use.

For in vivo delivery, 6–8 weeks old mice (C57BL/6 J strain) were administered AAV via tail vein injection at a dose of 5 × 1011 viral genomes in 100 μL per mouse. Three weeks post-injection, a subset of mice (n = 3) was sacrificed to evaluate knockdown efficiency by WB using an anti-NAA25 antibody (Proteintech, Cat# 31748-1-AP; 1:5000 dilution). Upon confirmation of effective knockdown, the remaining mice were subjected to infection experiments and phenotype measurements following a protocol consistent with that used in the ALDH2 functional assays.
