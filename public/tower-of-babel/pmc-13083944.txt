Investigating antibody cross-reactivity and transmission dynamics of alphaviruses and flaviviruses using a multiplex serological assay
Authors: Victor Yman, Jason Rosado, Noé Ochida, Laura Garcia, Marie-Fabrice Gasasira, Gaëlle Baudemont, Estee Cramer, Myrielle Dupont-Rouzeyrol, Karl Huet, Maylis Douine, Alice Sanna, Yann Lambert, Gabriel Carrasco-Escobar, Oscar Nolasco, Dionicia Gamboa, Gamou Fall, Oumar Ndiaye, Oumar Faye, Cheikh Loucoubar, Aissatou Toure-Balde, Makhtar Niang, Ines Vigan-Womas, Simon Cauchemez, Michael T. White
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate serological tools are essential for monitoring the transmission of arboviruses with pandemic potential, yet cross-reactivity between closely related viruses hampers diagnostics and surveillance. Here, we develop a high-throughput multiplex serological assay to quantify antibody responses to 28 antigens from nine arboviruses (dengue, Zika, yellow fever, West Nile, Usutu, Japanese encephalitis, chikungunya (CHIKV), Mayaro (MAYV), and O’nyong-nyong virus) and apply it to over 4000 samples from epidemiologically distinct sites on four continents. We implement a flexible analytical method based on Bayesian finite mixture models and Receiver Operating Characteristic analysis to evaluate assay performance and define seropositivity thresholds. As a case study, we resolve cross-reactive and virus-specific responses for CHIKV and the emerging MAYV by combining competitive immunoassays with mathematical modelling of multiplex serological and epidemiological data. This approach yields cross-reactivity-adjusted estimates of local transmission dynamics, in agreement with existing epidemiological evidence, and reveals that CHIKV is more prone to induce cross-reactive antibody responses than MAYV. Our results demonstrate the power of combining multiplex serology with experimental validation and modelling to disentangle exposure histories in the face of serological cross-reactivity. This integrative approach holds promise for improving arbovirus surveillance, particularly in settings with overlapping transmission of multiple viruses and limited diagnostic capacity.

Introduction

Arboviruses, such as dengue virus (DENV), Zika virus (ZIKV), and chikungunya virus (CHIKV), pose a significant threat to global health, with half the world’s population living at risk of infection1–3. DENV alone has been estimated to cause approximately 400 million infections annually, leading to significant morbidity, mortality, and societal costs4. Despite numerous reports of increasing transmission over the past decades, the epidemiology of many arboviruses, particularly emerging viruses such as Mayaro virus (MAYV) and Usutu virus (USUV), remains poorly characterised5. Climate change is predicted to further increase the global disease burden by expanding suitable mosquito habitats to new geographical regions, as highlighted by the introduction of USUV and recurring local outbreaks of both DENV and CHIKV in Europe2,6.

For certain arboviruses, e.g. DENV and ZIKV, surveillance systems exist in some countries but are mainly based on reporting of confirmed cases through health care systems and will not detect the large proportion of subclinical cases occurring in communities7. Cases that do present to healthcare systems may also go undetected due to the unavailability of molecular testing in low-resource settings, the low proportion of patients presenting with viremia, and the inaccuracy of current serological tests used for diagnosis1,8. In addition, health systems with limited capacity may fail to detect and/or report cases, as indicated by the lack of data on arbovirus transmission from the African continent, where many arboviruses are known to be endemic4.

Serology (i.e. measuring pathogen-specific antibodies as a marker of prior or concurrent exposure) is therefore a vital tool for both diagnostics and surveillance9. In addition, the implementation of multiplex serological assays provides new opportunities for integrated surveillance of multiple pathogens. However, serological cross-reactivity (where infection with one virus induces antibodies that also bind related non-infecting viruses) between closely related flaviviruses (e.g. DENV and ZIKV) and alphaviruses (e.g. CHIKV and MAYV) remains a major barrier to accurate serodiagnosis and effective serological surveillance10. Significant research efforts are currently focused on tackling this challenge through the development of new immunoassays for distinguishing between cross-reactive antibodies11, or through machine learning techniques or mathematical models that simultaneously analyse multiple biomarkers from cross-reactive pathogens12,13.

Serological diagnosis and epidemiological estimates of disease transmission usually rely on applying a threshold to continuous serological assay results. The choice of threshold directly impacts the sensitivity and specificity of the serological test and any resulting inferences. Ideally, the threshold should be chosen based on the intended use-case of the test, a thorough understanding of its performance in discriminating negative from positive, and the epidemiology of the pathogen under study14,15. However, in practice, the choice is often based on convention or convenience (for example, using the reactivity of unexposed controls). Assessing assay performance can be challenging if well-characterised control samples with known exposure status are not available16. The challenge is further amplified in the context of multiplex immunoassays, where numerous antibodies towards multiple pathogens are measured simultaneously. This underscores the need for systematic frameworks for evaluating assay performance and establishing seropositivity thresholds across diverse targets. In the absence of well-characterised controls, mixture models can support the process by identifying latent seronegative and seropositive subpopulations in the data. However, mixture models may perform poorly when subpopulations are not well separated, and estimates may still require validation using control samples with known exposure status17.

Here, we establish a multiplex serological assay including 28 distinct antigens to quantify antibody responses towards nine arboviruses of global concern and apply it to samples collected in four geographically distinct areas in Peru, French Guiana, Senegal, and New Caledonia. We describe a structured, yet flexible, method to assess assay performance based on a combination of Bayesian mixture models and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. Finally, as a case study on alphaviruses, we demonstrate how competitive immunoassays in combination with joint mathematical modelling of multi-pathogen serological data and epidemiological data on age and location, can help disentangle serological cross-reactivity from true exposure and allow us to reconstruct the local transmission dynamics of MAYV and CHIKV.

Results

In total, we quantified antibody responses to 28 serological exposure markers from three alphaviruses (CHIKV, O’nyong-nyong virus (ONNV), and MAYV), and six flaviviruses (DENV, ZIKV, yellow fever virus (YFV), Japanese encephalitis virus (JEV), West Nile virus (WNV), and USUV) in 4046 plasma samples consisting of 560 validation samples and 3486 survey samples collected in eight cross-sectional surveys conducted in four distinct geographical areas (see “Methods” section). The validation samples consisted of 338 negative control samples from arbovirus-unexposed adult donors and 222 confirmed or suspected positive control samples from adult donors with serological or molecular evidence of prior arbovirus exposure (see “Methods” section). Mixture models identify seronegative and seropositive sub-populations

For each antibody response, we fitted two- and three-component Gaussian mixture models (GMMs) jointly to data from negative validation samples (known serostatus) and cross-sectional survey samples (unknown serostatus) from all surveys and study sites (see “Methods” section). The three-component model provided a superior fit to the data, as determined by the leave-one-out information criterion (LOOIC), for all antibody responses, except for WNV envelope domain III (EDIII), where the three-component GMM did not converge. In the three-component model, the lower and upper component distributions were assumed to represent the seronegative and seropositive populations, respectively. We determined the class membership (i.e. seronegative or seropositive) of the middle component by comparison to the observed reactivity of the suspected positive control samples (to which the GMM was not fitted). Two examples of the three-component GMM fitted to data for CHIKV VLP and DENV NS1 are presented in Fig. 1. Characterising the trade-off between sensitivity and specificity based on GMM estimates

We used ROC curves to assess the trade-off between sensitivity and specificity at a range of seropositivity thresholds for each antibody assay. We calculated empirical ROC curves based on the observed antibody data from negative and suspected positive control samples and analytical ROC curves based on the cumulative distribution functions (CDFs) of the component normal distributions identified using the GMMs (see “Methods” section).

For all antibody assays the area under the ROC curve (AUROC) were highly similar for empirical and analytical ROC curves, exemplified here by the ROC curves for CHIKV E2 (Fig. 2A and Supplementary Fig. 5). The analytical AUROC varied across the different antibody assays but for 19 out of the total 28 evaluated antigens the AUROC exceeded 0.9, indicating acceptable performance in discriminating seronegative from seropositive (Fig. 2B). For the alphaviruses, the assays for CHIKV VLP, ONNV VLP, MAYV E2, and CHIKV E2 antigens all exhibited good discriminatory performance with AUROCs ranging from 0.999 (95% credible interval (CrI): 0.998–0.999) to 0.977 (95% CrI: 0.930–0.993) (Fig. 2B). For the flaviviruses, we observed good performance for assays including DENV VLP and NS1 antigens (all serotypes), for ZIKV VLP and NS1 (both antigens), for YFV and JEV envelope proteins, as well as for WNV and USUV NS1 antigens (all AUROC > 0.9). However, for the YFV envelope protein, the uncertainty of the estimate was large (AUROC: 0.974; 95% CrI: 0.721–0.992) (Fig. 2B). We observed poorer performance in discriminating seronegative from seropositive for antigens representing the EDIII of the included flaviviruses, with AUROCs ranging from 0.877 (95% CrI: 0.856–0.899) for WNV EDIII to 0.699 (95% CrI: 0.650–0.737) for DENV3 EDIII (Fig. 2B).Fig. 2ROC analysis of assay performance.A Empirical and analytical ROC curves for antibody responses to CHIKV E2. Using a single common method to select a threshold of seropositivity will affect the trade-off between sensitivity and specificity differently depending on the underlying performance of the assay. To illustrate this, we selected a seropositivity threshold for all antigens at the point of the analytical ROC curve where specificity is 97.7% (Fig. 2C). At this threshold, sensitivity varied across the assays but was generally high for both alphavirus and flavivirus assays with good performance (i.e. AUROC > 0.9) (Fig. 2C). For assays with a poorer performance, particularly those including the DENV and ZIKV EDIII antigens, the sensitivity was low and components not well separated (Fig. 2C). The relationship between the thresholds and the distribution of the antibody data from each study site are presented in Supplementary Fig. 6.

We also evaluated mixture models based on the more flexible Weibull distribution with comparable results (Supplementary Fig. 7).

A majority of the evaluated antibody responses were highly positively correlated within both the alpha- and flavivirus families (Fig. 4A, B), consistent with varying degrees of both co-exposure and serological cross-reactivity. For the alphavirus responses, we observed the highest correlation between the responses to CHIKV VLP and either MAYV E2 or ONNV VLP (Spearman’s rho (ρ) = 0.92 and ρ = 0.91) (Fig. 4A). Responses to CHIKV E2 and MAYV E2 were also highly correlated (ρ = 0.80). However, we could identify distinct response patterns, likely reflecting both site-specific differences in exposure history, as well as virus-specific variation in the induction of cross-reactive antibodies (Fig. 4C).

Among the responses to flavivirus antigens, we observed a near-perfect correlation between different serotypes of DENV VLP antigens (all ρ > 0.97) (Fig. 4B). Antibody responses to DENV VLP (all serotypes) and ZIKV VLP were also highly correlated (all ρ > 0.78), as were responses to DENV VLP, YFV E, and JEV E antigens (all ρ > 0.85) (Fig. 4B). However, for DENV NS1 and ZIKV NS1 responses, the correlation was lower (all ρ ~ 0.6). Some individuals mounted high-level antibody responses to both viruses, while others responded only to one. We observed similar patterns for DENV NS1 and YFV NS1 antigens (Fig. 4D), as well as across several other NS1 antigen pairs. Although these asymmetric antibody response profiles between individuals within a given antigen pair could reflect distinct virus-specific exposures, those same profiles may share broader patterns of variation across multiple other NS1 antigens that are only evident when antibody responses are explored jointly. Correlation networks of multivariate antibody response associations

We applied regularised network analysis to characterise the between-antigen association structure within the full multiplex serological profiles. Networks were estimated using extended Bayesian information criterion (EBIC) graphical least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) with Spearman rank correlations, yielding a regularised partial-correlation network in which edge widths represent the strength of conditional pairwise associations after accounting for all other responses (see “Methods” section). To account for between-site differences in background antibody reactivity and exposure histories that could artificially inflate pairwise associations even in the absence of co-exposure and/or serological cross-reactivity, antibody responses were adjusted with respect to site via linear residualisation prior to network estimation. This focuses the analysis on the within-site conditional dependence structure more likely to be attributable to correlated infection histories and serological cross-reactivity. The network included all antibody responses except for flavivirus EDIII antigens and the MAYV NSP1 antigen, which were excluded due to their overall lower signal intensity and lower ability to discriminate seronegative from seropositive.

The resulting network revealed a structured clustering of antibody responses with clear separation between the alphavirus and flavivirus families (Fig. 5A). Alphavirus antigens formed a tight internally connected cluster with minimal connectivity to flavivirus antigens. Among the flaviviruses, antibody responses exhibited slightly more heterogeneous clustering, with strong conditional associations among E and VLP antigens, most notably among DENV VLP antigens, which formed a strongly interconnected subnetwork. In contrast, conditional associations involving flavivirus NS1 antigens were fewer and generally weaker. Virus-specific structure in flavivirus NS1 antibody response profiles

The less dense network structure of the flavivirus NS1 antigens suggests a more discriminative virus-level response structure compared to VLP and E antigens. To explore this further, we defined distinct serological response phenotypes a priori based on the univariate GMM results for flavivirus NS1 antigens (see “Methods” section). We then fitted a principal component analysis (PCA) to the NS1 antibody data to examine how these phenotypes were distributed in multivariate space. The phenotypes exhibited clear separation in low-dimensional space, occupying distinct but partially overlapping regions along the first three principal components (PCs), indicating exposure-structured variation in NS1 response profiles (Fig. 5B).

The component loadings revealed that PC 1 captured a pan-flavivirus axis, with contributions from all NS1 antigens. PC2 was driven by the contrast between DENV NS1 antigens and other flavivirus NS1 responses, while PC3 was driven primarily by the contrast between ZIKV NS1 antigens and other flavivirus NS1 responses (Fig. 5C). Collectively, these axes indicate that NS1 responses are organised along a dominant shared flavivirus gradient, with additional orthogonal dimensions capturing virus-specific variation for DENV and ZIKV. For additional epidemiological context, Fig. 5D summarises the age- and site-specific prevalence of the serological response phenotypes.

Competitive immunoassays quantify virus-specific and cross-reactive antibodies to alphavirus antigens

As highlighted by the correlation network analysis, antibody responses to alphavirus E2 and VLP antigens remained strongly correlated even after conditioning on all other antibody responses, consistent with substantial serological cross-reactivity among alphaviruses. Despite this overall pattern, responses to CHIKV E2 and MAYV E2 exhibited three distinct seroreactivity patterns (Fig. 6A). We hypothesised that these patterns represent individuals with prior exposure only to CHIKV, only to MAYV, or both viruses. To test this hypothesis, we applied competitive immunoassays to experimentally quantify both virus-specific and cross-reactive antibodies in a randomly selected subset of 30 samples from the three groups (ten per group) (Fig. 6A). We pre-incubated samples with increasing concentrations of soluble CHIKV E2, MAYV E2, or BSA before independently retesting antibody responses to MAYV E2 and CHIKV E2. In samples from individuals with suspected CHIKV mono-exposure (Fig. 6A), we found that antibody responses to CHIKV E2 could only be depleted by pre-incubating with the homologous competitor (CHIKV E2). In contrast, the response to MAYV E2 could be depleted using both the heterologous competitor (CHIKV E2) and the homologous competitor (MAYV E2). This indicated that the CHIKV response in this group is a true specific response, whereas the MAYV response is a cross-reactive response induced by CHIKV exposure (Fig. 6B).

Conversely, we found that in individuals with suspected MAYV mono-exposure (Fig. 6A), the observed low-level response to CHIKV could be depleted using both the homologous competitor (CHIKV E2) and the heterologous competitor (MAYV). In contrast, the response to MAYV could only be depleted using the homologous competitor. This is consistent with MAYV mono-exposure and suggests that MAYV exposure induces a more limited cross-reactive response towards CHIKV than vice versa (Fig. 6B).

Finally, in individuals with suspected prior exposure to both MAYV and CHIKV (Fig. 6A), the competitive assay result mimicked that in the suspected MAYV mono-exposed individuals. The CHIKV response could be depleted using both the homologous competitor (CHIKV E2) and the heterologous competitor (MAYV E2), while the MAYV response could only be depleted using the homologous competitor (MAYV E2) (Fig. 6B).

This suggested, contrary to our initial hypothesis, that these individuals may not have been exposed to both MAYV and CHIKV. Rather, they might represent a separate group of MAYV mono-exposed individuals with a more prominent cross-reactive response towards CHIKV E2.

The competitive immunoassays indicated that the true number of MAYV and CHIKV co-infected individuals may be lower than expected. Based on the results, we assigned a most likely exposure status of the three groups as follows: the first group as CHIKV mono-exposed, the second group as MAYV mono-exposed, and the third group, contrary to our initial hypothesis, as MAYV mono-exposed.

Modelling cross-reactivity to reconstruct MAYV and CHIKV transmission dynamics

To further quantify the degree of cross-reactivity between MAYV and CHIKV and estimate the underlying local transmission dynamics of each virus, we applied a previously validated mathematical model (see “Methods” section)12. The model allows us to quantify cross-reactivity by estimating how antibody levels to both the infecting and the non-infecting virus change upon infection. Simultaneously, it allows us to reconstruct the historical transmission of MAYV and CHIKV at each survey site by estimating the site-specific annual force of infection (FOI), and to predict the most likely individual exposure status.

As described above, the competitive immunoassay results indicated there might be fewer individuals than expected with co-exposure to both viruses. This observation could indicate that infection with one virus induces cross-protective immune responses that prevent subsequent infection with the other virus. To explore this, we evaluated two sets of models: a null model without cross-protection and an alternative model which incorporated the assumption of cross-protection (see “Methods” section). In both sets of models, we allowed the competitive immunoassay results to inform the model estimates by fixing the most likely exposure status for the 30 samples tested using the competitive assay (see above).

Based on model comparison using the Watanabe-Akaike Information Criterion (WAIC), we did not find evidence to support the presence of cross-protection. The null model provided a superior fit to the data compared to the alternative model (with cross-protection) and was able to accurately reproduce the observed age-specific distribution in antibody reactivity to both MAYV and CHIKV across all sites (Supplementary Fig. 11).

With the null model, we estimate that an infection with MAYV on average leads to a 19-fold (95% CrI: 17.5–20.5) increase in antibody reactivity to MAYV E2 with a concomitant 1.17-fold (95% CrI: 1.16–1.19) cross-reactive increase in antibody reactivity to CHIKV E2. Conversely, a CHIKV infection on average leads to a 5-fold (95% CrI: 4.84–5.25) increase in antibody reactivity to CHIKV E2, accompanied by a 1.7-fold (95% CrI: 1.65–1.72) cross-reactive increase in MAYV E2 antibody reactivity.

The cross-reactivity adjusted estimates of the age-specific seroprevalence of each virus at each site are presented in Fig. 7A. For sites in French Guiana, Senegal and New Caledonia, the model makes location-specific assumptions of the underlying transmission dynamics of both viruses based on external epidemiological evidence (see “Methods” section). However, for the Peruvian site, the transmission of MAYV and CHIKV is less well characterised. We therefore compared stable-transmission and single-outbreak models for both MAYV and CHIKV. The model assumes that MAYV is absent in Senegal and New Caledonia and that MAYV transmission in French Guiana has been stable over time. Model selection based on WAIC supported stable transmission of MAYV in Peru. Our best fit model estimated an annual probability of infection of MAYV in naïve individuals of 0.007 (95% CrI: 0.006–0.008) in French Guiana and 0.011 (95% CrI: 0.009–0.012) in Peru (Fig. 7B).

For CHIKV, the model assumes an outbreak in 2014 in French Guiana and in New Caledonia, resulting in an estimated attack rate of 0.145 (95% CrI: 0.118–0.173) and 0.021 (95% CrI: 0.011–0.035), respectively. For Peru, the WAIC-based model selection supported a CHIKV epidemic in 2014 with an estimated attack rate of 0.013 (95% CrI: 0.005–0.024). In Senegal, where CHIKV transmission was assumed to be stable over time, the estimated annual probability of infection was 0.017 (95% CrI: 0.016–0.020) in Dielmo and 0.020 (95% CrI: 0.017–0.022) in Ndiop (Fig. 7B).

Discussion

There is an urgent need for robust serological surveillance tools to monitor the transmission of arboviruses of public health concern. In this study, we used a recently developed multiplex assay for simultaneous detection of antibody responses to 28 serological markers from nine arboviruses across four geographically and epidemiologically distinct populations in which transmission of DENV, ZIKV, and CHIKV is well-documented18–25. We propose an analytical framework for assessing the performance of the individual serological markers in discriminating seronegative from seropositive samples and use the multidimensional serological data to explore cross-reactivity between closely related viruses. As a case study, we show how combining competitive immunoassays with joint mathematical modelling of serological data from related alphaviruses can enable us to quantify cross-reactivity, disentangle it from true exposure, and provide reliable estimates of the local transmission dynamics.

While one would typically expect the overall distribution of serological data to consist of two subpopulations (representing either the seronegative or the seropositive state), we found that for all antibody responses but one, a three-component GMM better captured the overall distribution of the data26. The third (middle) subpopulation likely reflects a biological heterogeneous mixture of individuals, e.g. individuals with lower-level responses due to young age or weak immune systems27, with either recent infection or waning antibody levels, or with higher levels of non-specific reactivity28. Interestingly, the class membership (seronegative or seropositive) of the middle subpopulation appeared to vary depending on the marker, suggesting that the underlying mechanism driving multimodality may differ by response. While positive control data are not needed to fit the GMMs, this highlights their value for validating model estimates and for assigning class membership to subpopulations which may otherwise remain ambiguous26. Rather than relying on a one-size-fits-all approach, combining GMMs with ROC analysis makes it possible to tailor seropositivity thresholds based on a thorough understanding of the assay performance and the trade-off between assay sensitivity and specificity, even when well-characterised control samples are not available. Importantly, we demonstrate a strong concordance between our approach of calculating analytical ROC curves from the GMM estimates and the classical data-driven approach based on samples from known negative and positive controls. We propose that this framework can be broadly applied to any serological assay.

Serological cross-reactivity between related arboviruses is largely due to the presence of highly conserved epitopes within the viral envelope proteins29. Although the approach discussed above allows for robust identification of seronegative and seropositive populations, it is agnostic about cross-reactivity, as data for each antibody response is analysed individually. However, our multiplex serological data from diverse populations with distinct transmission backgrounds18–24,30, provide an opportunity to explore the link between exposure and serological cross-reactivity more closely.

As expected, we found evidence of substantial cross-reactivity, particularly between flaviviruses. This can be illustrated by the robust response observed across sites to JEV, a virus not transmitted outside of Asia5. Our study provides insight into the extent of this cross-reactivity, as well as the intensity and geographic variation of overall flavivirus exposure. In light of cross-reactivity, these findings should be interpreted with caution with regard to exposure to individual flaviviruses. For example, a gradual increase in antibody responses towards DENV1 VLP with age may indicate both recent and frequently recurring flavivirus transmission, but, because of cross-reactivity, should not be taken as definitive evidence of DENV transmission. However, our network and PCA analyses did demonstrate that, among flaviviruses, antibody responses to NS1 antigens were less strongly correlated than responses to the structural antigens representing the viral envelope (E and VLP antigens).

We did not have access to individual-level data on yellow fever vaccination, which may also induce antibody responses that cross-react with other flavivirus antigens31. YFV vaccination has been part of the national immunisation program in Peru since 2002, and in Senegal since 199632. In French Guiana, vaccination coverage in the surveyed population is expected to exceed 97%33. However, the observed antibody responses to non-YFV antigens are unlikely to be explained by YFV vaccination alone. This is supported by both national-level coverage estimates, the observed age-seroprevalence patterns, the PCA results, and the presence of robust DENV NS1 and ZIKV NS1 responses in individuals without detectable antibody response to YFV NS1.

Following its global expansion, CHIKV has become a widely circulating alphavirus, whereas MAYV remains an emerging arboviral threat primarily confined to the Amazon basin34. Both viruses are maintained in an enzootic cycle with recurring spillover into human populations. For CHIKV, this has led to major epidemics involving sustained human-amplified transmission35. As a case study based on competitive immunoassays and mathematical modelling, we provide an in-depth characterisation of the relationship between MAYV and CHIKV exposure and cross-reactivity and estimate the local transmission dynamics of both viruses. We propose that this approach can be extended to other arboviruses, including flaviviruses, to improve the interpretation of multiplex serological data in the context of cross-reactive immune responses, however, this is beyond the scope of the present study.

In terms of understanding cross-reactivity, the two methods are complementary. The competitive immunoassays provide experimental evidence of the magnitude of both virus-specific and cross-reactive antibody responses on the individual level, while the model provides population-level estimates. Both the competitive immunoassays and mathematical model estimates pointed strongly towards an asymmetric induction of cross-reactivity, where infection with CHIKV is more likely to induce a cross-reactive antibody response to MAYV than vice versa. This has been observed previously for CHIKV and ONNV in Mali, where CHIKV was more prone to induce cross-neutralising antibodies towards ONNV36. This type of asymmetry has important implications for the interpretation of serological data and should be explored carefully for the other viruses.

By accounting for cross-reactivity, our model can probabilistically estimate the most likely individual-level exposure history and provide population-level estimates of the virus-specific FOI. The best-fit model assumes constant transmission of MAYV in Peru and French Guiana and of CHIKV in Senegal. While this assumption may oversimplify the true temporal variation in transmission, it provided a good fit to data and a reasonable approximation of the underlying transmission dynamics, consistent with frequent zoonotic spillover events of MAYV in Peru and French Guiana and of CHIKV in Senegal. This aligns with previously published estimates for MAYV in the Maroni area in French Guiana12 and with epidemiological evidence of repeated CHIKV circulation in Senegal37,38. Furthermore, the model is able to quantify the attack rate of the large CHIKV epidemic that occurred in French Guiana around 201439. The model estimates a small CHIKV epidemic in Peru and in New Caledonia, consistent with strong epidemiological evidence that, despite repeated introductions, CHIKV has not caused major local outbreaks40,41. There are reports suggesting that natural exposure, or vaccination, with one alphavirus may provide cross-protection against subsequent exposures with other alphaviruses42–45. Although the results from the competitive immunoassay provided an indication that this might occur, our model estimates did not corroborate it.

There are additional limitations of this work that should be acknowledged. While we provide a detailed characterisation of exposure and serological cross-reactivity between MAYV and CHIKV, we did not address this issue for flaviviruses. Future work should expand these efforts to develop similar integrative frameworks for flaviviruses, where overlapping transmission of multiple viruses and higher degrees of antigenic similarity present additional challenges31–33. Competitive immunoassays are labour-intensive and low-throughput, due to high sample and reagent requirements. Because of these constraints, we were only able to test 30 samples. Testing a larger number of samples and extending the approach to a broader set of antigens would have been valuable for exploring cross-reactivity of related pathogens more broadly. Finally, although the competitive assays and mathematical models account for cross-reactivity between MAYV and CHIKV, we cannot exclude the possibility of exposure to other alphaviruses in the studied populations, particularly to ONNV in Senegal37.

In summary, we demonstrate the utility of combining multiplex serological methods and competitive immunoassays with structured analytical and modelling approaches, to evaluate assay performance, quantify antibody cross-reactivity, and reconstruct arbovirus transmission dynamics across diverse epidemiological settings. Our findings underscore the importance of accounting for cross-reactivity and emphasise the need for immunoassays with greater ability to distinguish between cross-reactive antibodies, as well as further development of analytical methods for the simultaneous analysis of data from multiple biomarkers from cross-reactive pathogens. Continued investment in the development of such tools will be essential to meet the growing global need for accurate arbovirus surveillance.

Methods

Study design

Validation cohorts

For assay validation, 338 negative control samples were obtained from arbovirus-unexposed adult French (n = 249)46 and Peruvian donors (n = 89). Suspected alphavirus positive control samples from adult Senegalese donors (n = 38) with serological evidence of prior CHIKV infection were provided by the Centre for Arboviruses and Haemorrhagic Fever Viruses (CRORA) in Institut Pasteur de Dakar. Suspected flavivirus positive control samples (n = 222) were obtained from adult Peruvian donors (n = 102) with either suspected or confirmed DENV exposure (i.e. based on clinical history, serological testing, or PCR verification) and from adult Senegalese donors (n = 120, provided by CRORA) with serological evidence of suspected prior DENV (n = 60), WNV (n = 30), or YFV exposure (n = 30).

Cross-sectional surveys

Samples were obtained from eight cross-sectional surveys conducted at sites in Senegal, Peru, French Guiana, and New Caledonia. In Peru, 880 samples were obtained from an all-age cross-sectional population survey of arboviruses and malaria conducted in 2018 in three peri-urban communities in Iquitos district, Department of Loreto, in the Peruvian Amazon47. In Senegal, 1219 samples were obtained from four population-level cross-sectional malaria prevalence surveys conducted in 2016 and 2018 in villages Dielmo and Ndiop, which have been monitored longitudinally since the 1990’s with regard to the transmission of malaria48. In French Guiana, 787 samples were obtained from two multicentric cross-sectional surveys conducted in the Maroni area along the French Guiana-Suriname border in 2015 (n = 409) and in 2019 (n = 378) to study the prevalence of malaria in adult goldminers49. In New Caledonia, 600 samples were obtained from adults 18–64 years of age participating in the 2021–2022 Adult Health Barometer, a cross-sectional descriptive survey50.

No new samples were collected as part of this study. This study involved the secondary use of samples previously collected in other studies. For all studies, the ethical approval and the informed consent covered this additional analysis.

The surveys in French Guiana were approved by the Commissie voor Mensgebonden Wetenschappelijk Onderzoek (CMWO) in Suriname (Opinion Number VG10-14 & DVG-738) and by the Institut national de la santé et de la recherche médicale (INSERM) Ethics Evaluation Committee in France (No. 14–187 of 09.12.2014).

The Peruvian samples were collected from participants in the study Circles of Research on Arboviruses and Malaria (SIDISI 101645/2017), following approval by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Universidad Peruana Cayetano Heredia (UPCH).

The Senegalese samples were collected as part of the ongoing Dielmo and Ndiop project, which was examined and approved by the Senegalese National Health Research Ethics Committee (CNERS Sénégal). Approval to measure antibodies to arboviruses in these samples was granted by CNERS Sénégal (N 00000007 MSAS/CNERS/Sec). The study in New Caledonia was approved by the “Comité de Protection des Personnes Ouest IV” (21.05.16.92458-avis_50_21_2 and avis_50-21_2MS1) and by the Consultative Ethics Committee of New Caledonia (Avis 2021-03 002). Bead-based immunoassay procedures

A bead-based immunoassay was developed and optimised for simultaneous detection of IgG antibody responses to 28 recombinant antigens, including both structural and non-structural proteins, from commercially available sources. For CHIKV, antigens included a virus-like particle (VLP) (containing envelope, pre-membrane and membrane proteins) and the envelope protein 2 (E2). For MAYV, antigens included the E2 and the non-structural protein 1 (NSP1), whereas for ONNV, only a VLP was included.

DENV antigens included the envelope protein domain III (EDIII), a VLP, and the non-structural protein 1 (NS1) from each of the four serotypes (DENV1–4). ZIKV antigens included the EDIII and a VLP, as well as two distinct versions of NS1, representing the Suriname (SU) and Ugandan (U) viral strains, to account for geographical differences in strain-specific exposure. For YFV and JEV, antigens included NS1 and the envelope protein (E), whereas for USUV, only NS1 was included. For WNV, antigens included NS1 and EDIII. All antigens were commercially available and were produced by The Native Antigen Company, except for WNV EDIII, and MAYV NSP1, which were produced by Sino Biological and Alpha Diagnostic, respectively. Each recombinant antigen was covalently coupled to a spectrally unique set of carboxylated paramagnetic nanobeads (MagPlex; Luminex Corp) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Optimal protein concentrations were determined experimentally to generate a log-linear standard curve with a positive control plasma pool51. All antigens, their catalogue numbers, and the protein coupling concentrations are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

The multiplex assay was performed according to a previously described protocol51. Briefly, 50 μl of each plasma sample, diluted 1:100 in phosphate-buffered saline with 1% (w/v) bovine serum albumin and 0.05% (v/v) Tween 20 (PBT buffer), was added to each well of a 96-well plate and incubated with 50 μl of bead mix (containing 250 beads per analyte per well) for 30 min. Following three washes with PBT buffer, 100 μl of the secondary antibody (R-Phycoerytrin (PE) conjugated F(ab’)2 fragment Donkey anti-Human IgG, Jackson ImmunoResearch: JIR709116098), diluted 1:120 in PBT-buffer, was added to each well and incubated for 15 min. After three final washes with PBT buffer, beads were re-suspended in 100 μl of PBT buffer and plates were read using a Luminex MagPix instrument (Luminex Corp.). The same 11 technical controls, including pools of sera from both arbovirus-unexposed donors and exposed donors, were assayed on each plate and used to control for plate-to-plate-related assay variation.

Competitive immunoassays

Samples were pre-incubated with soluble antigens (MAYV E2, CHIKV E2, or BSA) at increasing concentrations to competitively block or deplete antibodies prior to detection on bead-bound antigen. Each sample was assayed under 30 individual conditions (3 competitor antigens × 5 concentrations × 2 bead-bound antigens). Fifty microliters of the sample at a 1:100 dilution in PBS-T was pre-incubated with each soluble competitor antigen (MAYV E2, CHIKV E2, or BSA) at 0, 0.01, 0.1, 1, and 10 µg/ml with shaking for 1 h at room temperature. After pre-incubation, each 50 µl aliquot was mixed with 50 µl of antigen-coupled beads (either MAYV E2 or CHIKV E2). Antibody responses towards MAYV and CHIKV were quantified independently in separate wells (mono-plex) but otherwise as described above (see Bead-based immunoassay procedures). MAYV E2 antibody binding was measured in the presence of soluble MAYV E2, CHIKV E2, or BSA, and CHIKV E2 binding was measured separately under the same competition conditions. The obtained MFI of each sample and experimental condition was expressed as a proportion of the maximum sample MFI, defined as the MFI measured without soluble competitor antigen.

Data management, normalisation and analysis

Data management and analysis were performed in R (R version 4.3.3). Bayesian finite mixture models were executed from R on the Stan platform (Stan Development Team, 2024, Stan Modelling Language version 2.35, https://mc-stan.org) using the cmdstanr package (cmdstanr: R Interface to ‘CmdStan’, version 0.7.1) as the R interface to Stan. Mathematical models of antibody cross-reactivity and virus transmission were implemented in Python (v. 3.13.1) using the PyMC module (v. 5.20.0).

ROC analysis

For each antibody response, ROC analysis was applied to assess the trade-off between sensitivity and specificity at all possible seropositivity thresholds.

An “empirical” ROC curve was calculated from the observed data from the negative and positive control samples. For the empirical ROC curve, the assay specificity is estimated as the proportion of negative control samples that are below a specified threshold, and the assay sensitivity is estimated as the proportion of positive control samples that are greater than that threshold.

In a situation where known seropositive and seronegative samples are not available, the ROC curve cannot be calculated directly from the data. It is, however, possible to generate an “analytical” ROC curve based on the parameter estimates from a finite mixture model and the CDF of the component normal distributions. For the analytical ROC curve, the specificity is estimated as the proportion of the CDF(s) of the mixture component(s) representing the seronegative population that is below a specified threshold, and assay sensitivity is estimated as the proportion of the CDF(s) of the mixture component(s) representing the seropositive population that is greater than that threshold. The performance of each antibody assay in discriminating seronegative from seropositive individuals was assessed using the AUROC, and an AUROC > 0.9 was considered acceptable.

Graphical LASSO partial correlation network analysis

Correlation network analysis was performed on log-transformed antibody responses. For each antigen, responses were first residualised using linear models with site as a predictor. Conditional-dependence networks were estimated from the residualised data using EBIC graphical LASSO based on Spearman's rank correlation. Network layouts were computed from the resulting partial correlation matrix using the Fruchterman–Reingold force-directed algorithm. Network stability was assessed by non-parametric bootstrapping (1000 iterations), and stable edges were defined by a bootstrap inclusion probability ≥0.5 with confidence intervals excluding zero. For visual clarity, non-stable edges and edges with partial correlations <0.1 were omitted from figures. Data for the flavivirus EDIII antigens and for the MAYV NSP1 antigen were excluded from the network analysis due to their lower signal intensity and lower ability to discriminate seronegative from seropositive.

PCA

PCA was applied to log-transformed, mean-centred antibody data for all flavivirus NS1 antigens. PC scores were extracted and visualised for the first three PCs to assess clustering of samples in reduced-dimensional space. Antibody response loadings were extracted for the same components to examine the relative contribution from each antigen. For visualisation purposes, PCA plots were colour-coded according to serological response phenotypes derived from the univariate GMM. Samples not exceeding the GMM-derived threshold of seropositivity for any of the NS1 antigens were designated “Unexposed-like”. For each NS1 antigen, samples with a posterior mean probability > 0.5 of belonging to the highest mixture component were classified as high responders and serological response phenotypes were defined as follows: High non-DENV/non-ZIKV-flavivirus, high for at least one NS1 antigen from WNV, USUV, JEV, or YFV and not high for DENV and ZIKV; High DENV-only, high for at least one DENV NS1 antigen and not high for any other; High ZIKV, high for at least one ZIKV NS1 antigen and not high for any other; High DENV + ZIKV only, High for at least one DENV and at least one ZIKV NS1 antigen and not high for any other; Pan-Flavivirus-high, high for at least one DENV and at least one ZIKV NS1 antigen and high for all other tested NS1 antigens (WNV, USUV, JEV, and YFV).

Mathematical model of antibody dynamics, cross-reactivity and virus transmission

We adapted a previously validated model developed by Hozé et al.12 to jointly characterise antibody cross-reactivity and reconstruct individual infection histories for MAYV and CHIKV within a unified inference framework. Specifically, the model quantifies how infection with one virus shapes the antibody response to that virus and how this response modulates the measured reactivity to a related, non-infecting virus. Simultaneously, it estimates the probability that individuals fall into one of four prior infection states: uninfected, infected with MAYV, infected with CHIKV, or infected with both. We fit the model jointly to log10-MFI data for MAYV E2 and CHIKV E2 from cross-sectional survey participants and known unexposed controls, where the latter contribute information on the background reactivity in unexposed individuals. We incorporate a hierarchical structure to account for site-specific variation in this background reactivity. For the subset of samples evaluated using the competitive immunoassay (n = 30), we assign a fixed infection status based on the assay results (see above).

Epidemiological situation:

Based on the available epidemiological evidence, we assumed a single CHIKV outbreak to have occurred in 2014 in Maroni (French Guiana)39 and in New Caledonia41. We assumed MAYV transmission to be constant over time in Maroni12 and CHIKV transmission to be constant over time in Dielmo and Ndiop (Senegal)38. We assumed MAYV to be absent from Senegal (Dielmo and Ndiop) and New Caledonia. For Peru (Iquitos), we fitted alternative transmission models for MAYV and CHIKV, assuming either a constant FOI or a single outbreak. The most likely scenario was identified based on model fit using the WAIC, alongside inspection of MCMC chain convergence.

Inference

Parameters were estimated in a Bayesian framework using MCMC using the NUTS algorithm implemented in the PyMC module55. Convergence was assessed through visual inspection of trace plots.
