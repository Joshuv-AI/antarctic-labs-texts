Lipoprotein(a) Distribution and Cardiovascular Risk in Japan: Insights from a Multi-center LEAP Study
Authors: Tomoaki Yamada, Hayato Tada, Masatsune Ogura, Sayaka Funabashi, Hiroshi Yoshida, Chika Hiraishi, Takae Ebihara, Takahiro Hadano, Takahiro Masaki, Yasuaki Takeji, Kazumi Fukatsu, Makiko Egawa, Yu Kataoka, Masahito Michikura, Daisuke Shishikura, Shimpei Fujioka, Tetsuo Sasano, Taketoshi Maehara, Masumi Ai, Mariko Shiba-Harada, Masayuki Yoshida
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAim: Although lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] is recognized as an independent risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD), its distribution and risk thresholds in the Japanese population remain unclear. This study aimed to characterize the distribution of Lp(a) in Japanese clinical settings and evaluate its association with CVD risk, including coronary artery disease (CAD), atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), and familial hypercholesterolemia (FH). Methods: The LEAP study is a multicenter retrospective cohort analysis of 6,173 patients from six Japanese institutions. The Lp(a) values were harmonized to nmol/L using kit-specific conversion equations, enabling a consistent analysis across different immunoassays. Associations with CAD, ASCVD, chronic kidney disease (CKD), diabetes mellitus (DM), and FH were assessed. The risk thresholds were defined using a receiver operating characteristic analysis. Results: The median Lp(a) concentration was 20.88 nmol/L, with a right-skewed distribution. Elevated Lp(a) levels were significantly associated with CAD, ASCVD, CKD, and FH. In contrast, DM was related to lower Lp(a). An ROC analysis identified 25 nmol/L as a screening threshold and 125 nmol/L as a high-risk boundary. The CAD prevalence increased stepwise across these categories: 20.2% (≤ 25 nmol/L), 30.3% (25–125 nmol/L), and 39.9% (＞125 nmol/L). Conclusion: This study provides the first nmol/L-standardized Lp(a) distribution in Japanese patients and proposes provisional thresholds for clinical risk stratification. Keywords: Cardiovascular disease, Lipoprotein(a), Cardiovascular risk assessment

Introduction

Lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] is a low-density lipoprotein (LDL)-like particle in which apolipoprotein B-100 is covalently linked to apolipoprotein(a). Lp(a) has been shown to contribute to atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) independently of and additively with LDL-cholesterol 1 - 5) . In many countries, including Japan, Lp(a) measurement is not routinely performed outside of lipid specialists. Moreover, another barrier to routine clinical adoption has been the lack of effective, selective Lp(a)-lowering therapies; statins may modestly increase the Lp(a) concentrations, while ezetimibe appears to have little or no clinically meaningful effect, and proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) inhibitors reduce Lp(a) only modestly 6 - 10) . In parallel, isoform-sensitive immunoassays and mixed unit reporting (mg/dL vs. nmol/L) complicate inter-laboratory comparisons and the setting of actionable thresholds. International recommendations now favor nmol/L reporting and harmonization against reference procedures 11 , 12) .

Evidence from East Asia has expanded, but it remains methodologically heterogeneous. Several Japanese and Korean reports were single-center and mass-unit based; more recent Chinese data confirm graded associations with coronary events, but still rely largely on isoform-sensitive assays 13 - 17) . As Lp(a)-targeted agents advance, health systems need pragmatic, data-driven thresholds that can guide risk management or prioritization for novel therapies.

Recently, Miida et al. compared five different immunoassay reagents using both the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) serum panel and a Japanese elderly cohort based on the mass spectrometry–based reference measurement procedure (IFCC-MS-RMP) provided by the U.S. CDC and the International Federation of Clinical Chemistry and Laboratory Medicine (IFCC) 18) . Although the correlations were generally good, there were substantial differences in the regression slopes among the reagents. However, after recalibration using the reference panel, the measurements became consistent, and the inter-assay variability was significantly reduced. In other words, Miida et al. demonstrated that recalibration according to IFCC-MS-RMP markedly minimized measurement discrepancies, thereby enabling clinically useful harmonization.

Therefore, we conducted a multicenter, retrospective study of serum Lp(a) measurements and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease in Japan (LEAP study) using an electronic chart review across participating hospitals. All Lp(a) results were converted from mg/dL to nmol/L using Miida’s kit-specific equations 18) , thereby removing any inter-assay drift and enabling a unified distribution. The present analysis (i) characterizes the nmol/L distribution of Lp(a) in Japanese clinical practice, (ii) quantifies its associations with familial hypercholesterolemia (FH), coronary artery disease (CAD), and composite ASCVD, and (iii) defines risk stratification anchored in receiver operating characteristic (ROC) performance (sensitivity/specificity balance), distributional features, and international benchmarks.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

The LEAP study is a multicenter retrospective analysis that integrated clinical data from six participating institutions: Institute of Science Tokyo Hospital, National Cerebral and Cardiovascular Center, Osaka Medical and Pharmaceutical University, Kanazawa University, Jikei University Kashiwa Hospital, and Kyorin University. Participants with at least one Lp(a) measurement were eligible. Because this was a hospital-based cohort, a large proportion of the participants were receiving guideline-directed lipid-lowering therapy at the time of Lp(a) testing (predominantly statins). Therefore, the LDL-C values reflected on-treatment levels rather than untreated baselines. The final analytic cohort comprised 6,173 unique individuals.

Data Collection

We extracted demographic characteristics and laboratory parameters (LDL-C and Lp(a)) from medical records. Two immunoassays (Nittobo and Sekisui) were employed across the study sites to measure Lp (a) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. As these two immunoassays report mass units (mg/dL), all raw Lp(a) values were converted to molar concentration (nmol/L) using kit-specific linear equations derived from an IFCC-endorsed reference measurement procedure 18) . Comorbidities were defined a priori: FH, meeting Japan Atherosclerosis Society clinical diagnostic criteria or having pathogenic/likely pathogenic variant in LDLR/APOB/PCSK9 genes 19) ; CAD, prior myocardial infarction, coronary revascularization, or angiographic stenosis ≥ 50%; ASCVD, CAD, ischemic stroke, carotid revascularization, or peripheral artery disease; diabetes mellitus (DM), HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, prescription of glucose-lowering medication; chronic kidney disease (CKD), eGFR ＜60 mL·min−1·1.73 m−2. Cardiovascular events and comorbidities, including myocardial infarction and ischemic stroke, were identified through a review of electronic medical records at each participating institution. Myocardial infarction and ischemic stroke were defined based on the clinical diagnoses made by attending specialists in accordance with the standard diagnostic criteria.

The Japan Atherosclerosis Society IRB served as the central review board for four participating sites, with local IRB approvals obtained at The Jikei University Kashiwa Hospital and Kyorin University.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the baseline characteristics. Continuous variables were reported as the mean±standard deviation or median with interquartile range (IQR), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages.

We constructed ROC curves using CAD as the outcome and performed risk stratification based on sensitivity and specificity. We examined whether the presence or absence of a history of disease differed across the stratified Lp(a) levels using the Wilcoxon rank sum test. For multiple comparisons of medians, we applied the Steel–Dwass test. For categorical variables analyzed using the Chi-square test, we applied the Bonferroni correction to adjust for multiple comparisons. Adjusted p-values ＜0.05 were considered statistically significant. We performed statistical analyses with R version 4.3.2

Distribution of Lp(a)

In the unconverted mg/dL data ( Supplemental Fig.1 ) , the Nittobo distribution had a median of 7.6 mg/dL, whereas the Sekisui distribution had a median of 13.1 mg/dL, accompanied by a more pronounced upper tail. The inter-kit median difference was 5.5 mg/dL, and the 95th-percentile gap exceeded 55 mg/dL, underscoring the risk of systematic misclassification. Therefore, all measurements were converted to nmol/L using the kit-specific linear equations derived from an IFCC value-assigned serum panel. Subsequent analyses were based exclusively on these harmonized nmol/L values.

Line plot of Lp(a) concentrations(mg/dL) by immunoassays. The orange line represents Nittobo, the blue line represents Sekisui. The median Lp(a) concentration was 7.6 mg/dL in Nittoboand 13.1 mg/dL in Sekisui. Abbreviations: Lp(a), lipoprotein(a).

The aggregated nmol-standardized dataset remained markedly right-skewed ( Fig.1 ) . Small negative values near 0 nmol/L may appear after standardization due to linear back-conversion and analytical noise; these values reflect the standardization process. The median Lp(a) concentration was 20.88 nmol/L (IQR, 8.92–61.7 nmol/L), with the 75th and 90th percentiles at 61.7 nmol/L and 137.1 nmol/L, respectively. These observations confirm that rigorous assay harmonization is essential for accurate population profiling and also reveal the national Lp(a) distribution in Japan.

Lp(a) Levels and CVD Risks

As shown in Fig.2 , we compared the Lp(a) levels between the groups stratified by the presence or absence of major comorbidities. Patients with established CAD displayed markedly higher Lp(a) levels than those without CAD (31.5 vs. 19.4 nmol/L, p＜0.001). A similar elevation was observed for ASCVD (33.8 vs. 18.7 nmol/L, p＜0.001) and CKD (31.4 vs. 19.2 nmol/L, p＜0.001). In contrast, the median Lp(a) value was lower in patients with DM than in those without DM (16.5 vs. 23.5 nmol/L, p＜0.001). Lp(a) Stratification and CVD Risks

An ROC analysis for prevalent CAD ( Supplemental Fig.4 ) identified 25.2 nmol/L as the Youden-optimized cut-off (sensitivity, 0.553; specificity, 0.611). Although the sensitivity is modest, this cut-point maximizes the joint diagnostic yield. Therefore, we adopted ≤ 25 nmol/L as the reference (“low-risk”) category. Regarding the high-risk boundary, we adopted 125 nmol/L as the “high-risk” threshold, where the specificity exceeded 0.9 ( Supplemental Table 1 ) . This threshold is consistent with prior reports and guideline practice 1 , 20 , 21) . Furthermore, when the participants were grouped at 25 nmol/L intervals, no significant increase in the prevalence of CAD was observed across the range from 25 nmol/L to 125 nmol/L, which also supported this threshold ( Supplemental Fig.5 ) . As shown in Fig.4 , CAD prevalence rose step-wise from 20.2 % in the ≤ 25 nmol/L group to 30.3 % in the 25–125 nmol/L group and 39.9 % in the ＞125 nmol/L group (χ2 P＜0.001). A similar step-wise prevalence rise was observed in ASCVD. The prevalence of CKD increased 27.2% in the ≤ 25 nmol/L group to 39.6% in the 25–125 nmol/L group (P＜0.001), but no significant difference was observed between the 25–125 nmol/L group and the ＞125 nmol/L group.

Discussion

In this multicenter, nmol/L-standardized retrospective cohort study, Lp(a) exhibited a right-skewed distribution centered near 20.9 nmol/L, with ~12% above 125 nmol/L. Lp(a) concentrations were significantly higher in patients with CAD and ASCVD than in those without these conditions. The cardiovascular risk increased across the continuum, and we observed data-driven inflection points at ~25 nmol/L (Youden-optimized screening threshold in this dataset) and a high-risk tier above 125 nmol/L (higher-specificity boundary). The Lp(a) levels increased stepwise from non-FH to HeFH and to HoFH, and the Lp(a)–CAD association was stronger among individuals with FH. The novelty lies in presenting nmol/L-harmonized distributions for Japanese patients and linking them both to the disease status (CAD/ASCVD) and to the operating points derived from explicit sensitivity–specificity trade-offs.

Although the cohort median Lp(a) was lower than reported in other studies, the overall distribution and the graded association between higher Lp(a) and cardiovascular risk were similar across studies 22 , 23) . In our hospital-based cohort, an ROC-derived cut-off of 25 nmol/L performed best for clinical triage, whereas ＞125 nmol/L identified a high-risk tier with markedly elevated absolute risk and potential eligibility for therapeutic intervention. After adjusting for Age, Sex, LDL-C, DM, and CKD, the Lp(a) levels remained significantly associated with CAD and ASCVD, supporting the notion that Lp(a) contributes to atherosclerotic risk through mechanisms distinct from the LDL metabolism ( Supplemental Table 2 ) . These findings confirm that Lp(a) confers additional cardiovascular risk beyond the conventional lipid parameters. Because these operating points were derived in patients already under care, we regard the 25 and 125 nmol/L schemas as provisional, and for public health use, a 125 nmol/L framework may remain a reasonable interim default until the Japanese population-based reference distributions can be determined.

From a clinical standpoint, our results justify integrating Lp(a), reported in nmol/L, into risk assessment in Japan, while recognizing the context. In tertiary-care practice, values at or above ~25 nmol/L can reasonably prompt confirmation (if management would change) and incorporation into the global risk discussion; and ＞125 nmol/L denotes a very-high-risk subgroup appropriate for individual risk management. Therefore, the immediate next step is to generate nationwide, population-representative data to define reference percentiles for both primary and secondary prevention in Japan.

The gene-dose pattern across FH genotypes is consistent with additive hepatic overproduction and/or impaired clearance that magnifies Lp(a)-related atherothrombosis 24) . Higher Lp(a) in CKD may reflect an altered catabolism, whereas lower values in diabetes have been observed in multiple cohorts. However, the mechanisms underlying these differences remain uncertain and potential contributors include insulin signaling and treatment/selection effects 25) . Therefore, targeted experimental work is warranted.

The key strengths of the study are as follows: (i) exclusive reliance on chart-abstracted real-world data across multiple tertiary centers, (ii) rigorous nmol/L harmonization using kit-specific conversion to eliminate inter-assay bias, and (iii) prespecified, clinically interpretable cut-points explicitly tied to sensitivity/specificity trade-offs, with reproducible signals across the major subgroups, including FH.

This investigation has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, this was a retrospective, chart-based analysis. Therefore, causal inference was not possible. Second, because the cohort comprised patients who attended the hospital, some selection bias toward higher-risk individuals was likely. The high prevalence of CAD and FH differs from that in the general population. Therefore, our results may not be generalizable to a broader, lower-risk population. Third, although PCSK9 inhibitor use may have influenced the Lp(a) levels, data on these factors were not available from some participating institutions and, thus, could not be included in the analysis 9 , 26 , 27) . Fourth, the accuracy of the diagnoses used in this study has some limitations. DM was defined using an HbA1c cutoff of ≥ 6.5%, although the HbA1c levels may overlap between non-diabetic and diabetic individuals. This inherent variability may have led to some degree of misclassification.

In summary, the present study provides a robust foundation for Lp(a) risk stratification in clinical practice and will help to establish Japan-tailored thresholds and pathways for Lp(a) management to prevent ASCVD.
