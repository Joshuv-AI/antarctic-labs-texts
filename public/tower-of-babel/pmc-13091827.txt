Photothermal preconditioning of platelet-rich plasma: An emerging regenerative strategy
Authors: Elena Sánchez-Vizcaíno Mengual, Laura Cordero, Hernán Pinto
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) is one of the most widely used autologous regenerative therapies across medical disciplines, including dermatology, orthopedics, wound care, sports medicine, and reconstructive procedures. Despite its broad adoption, clinical outcomes remain variable, partly due to heterogeneity in preparation methods, platelet activation protocols, and biological responsiveness.

PRP modulation has traditionally relied on platelet activation methods, such as calcium salts, thrombin, collagen/contact activation, and mechanical procedures, which affect bioactive factor release [1]. More recently, technology-driven ex vivo approaches, including photobiomodulation/photothermal preconditioning and pulsed electric field activation, have emerged to improve biological control and consistency [2]. This correspondence highlights an emerging regenerative modulation strategy of translational interest, rather than reporting definitive clinical efficacy. As regenerative medicine advances, there is increasing interest in approaches that enhance the biological consistency and functional potential of autologous products. One emerging strategy is the preconditioning of PRP using controlled photothermal biomodulation (PTBM) [3]. PTBM combines exposure to specific light wavelengths with controlled temperature modulation to influence platelet behavior and downstream signaling pathways in a standardized manner. Established principles of photobiology and cellular stress signaling support the rationale for this approach. The red and blue light spectra have been shown to influence mitochondrial activity, reactive oxygen species signaling, and transcriptional pathways involved in cytokine release and cellular metabolism. Red light can modulate platelet activation dynamics and growth factor (GF) release, while blue light can modulate extracellular vesicle (EV) secretion. Temperature modulation within controlled physiological ranges may alter membrane kinetics, enzymatic activity, and vesicular trafficking, potentially affecting platelet activation dynamics and the release of regenerative mediators. Importantly, this concept aligns with broader regenerative strategies that seek not merely to concentrate biological components but to modulate cellular responses in a controlled and reproducible fashion.

Photothermal preconditioning may be incorporated as an intermediate step between centrifugation and administration, using controlled light and temperature to prime platelets before their subsequent activation in the tissue microenvironment. Early experimental observations suggested that thermal conditioning may enhance the availability of growth factors in PRP preparations, supporting the concept of biological optimization beyond conventional activation [4,5]. More recently, attention has shifted toward EVs, particularly exosomes, as central mediators of regenerative signaling. Exosomes are nanoscale vesicles involved in intercellular communication, transferring proteins, lipids, GF, mRNA, and microRNA that influence angiogenesis, immune modulation, and tissue remodeling.

In vitro evidence indicates that photothermal preconditioning may increase exosome release from platelet-rich preparations, with different fluence parameters yielding distinct quantitative responses [2]. This suggests dose-dependent modulation rather than uniform activation. While quantitative increases in exosome concentration are encouraging, further research is required to determine whether qualitative changes in vesicle cargo composition and functional signaling capacity accompany these increases.

From a translational perspective, early clinical reports across heterogeneous indications have described favorable tolerability and regenerative responses. Published experiences include applications in complex wound management in metabolically compromised patients [6], musculoskeletal repair such as partial tendon injury [7], and other tissue-regenerative contexts [8]. Although these reports remain largely limited to pilot studies and case-based evidence, the convergence of observations across specialties suggests that photothermal preconditioning may influence fundamental repair pathways rather than organ-specific mechanisms. However, these findings remain preliminary and hypothesis-generating rather than established clinical evidence.

Traditional PRP preparation has mainly emphasized platelet counts and baseline GF concentrations. However, growing recognition of EVs and dynamic signaling mechanisms suggests that biological quality and release kinetics may be equally relevant. PTBM represents an attempt to move toward controlled modulation of these parameters. PRP heterogeneity is relevant when interpreting the effects of PTBM. Differences in platelet concentration, leukocyte content, residual erythrocytes, and baseline activation status may all influence GF release and EV dynamics. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted within the context of the specific PRP formulation used and not assumed to be directly transferable across all preparations.

To address procedural variability, a few medical device platforms have been proposed for preconditioning autologous products with predefined photothermal settings. Such approaches aim to standardize energy delivery and optical parameters, including fluence, temperature range, and exposure duration, to reduce operator-dependent variability and improve protocol reproducibility. While these strategies may facilitate harmonization, independent validation through rigorous clinical investigation remains essential.

At the same time, significant limitations must be acknowledged. The current literature reflects variability in applied parameters, and standardized consensus protocols are lacking. We have focused on exosomes; however, platelet-derived EVs represent a heterogeneous population that includes microvesicles and platelet microparticles, and distinguishing these vesicle populations remains technically challenging. Furthermore, the functional quality of EV cargo requires further characterization, and most clinical evidence remains preliminary. Broader analyses of exosome isolation and quantification highlight substantial methodological variability that can influence reported concentrations and biological interpretation [9]. Without harmonized analytical standards, comparisons across studies remain challenging.

Well-designed randomized controlled trials, dose–response investigations, and mechanistic studies are necessary to determine clinical relevance, optimal protocols, and long-term safety. Collaborative research across regenerative disciplines will be critical to determine whether photothermal preconditioning can meaningfully contribute to standardized autologous regenerative care.
