Quantum Error Correction-like Noise Mitigation for Wave-like Dark Matter Searches with Quantum Sensors Preprint: XXXX
Hajime Fukuda, Takeo Moroi, Thanaporn Sichanugrist

Abstract
We propose a quantum error correction-like noise mitigation protocol for enhancing the sensitivity of wave-like dark matter searches with quantum sensors. Our protocol uses multiple sensors to mitigate the noise affecting each sensor individually, allowing for the suppression of excitation noise that is parallel to the dark matter signal. We demonstrate that our protocol can improve the sensitivity to dark matter signals by a factor of N \sqrt{N} , where N N is the number of sensors used. Furthermore, we find that our protocol achieves the same performance as the standard quantum limit by the ideal measurement, which is impossible to achieve due to the unknown phase of the dark matter field. Our work can be widely applied to various types of signals with unknown phases, and has the potential to enhance the sensitivity of quantum sensors such as arrays of resonant cavities.



Introduction —

The dark matter (DM) in the universe is one of the most important mysteries in modern physics.
There are various candidates for DM, and one of the well-motivated candidates is light bosonic particles such as axions
36
;
2
, which is called “wave-like DM” due to its wave nature.
Recently, with the development of quantum technologies, growing attention has been paid to the use of quantum sensors for searching for wave-like DM
17
;
9
;
10
;
27
;
13
;
11
;
5
;
12
;
14
;
18
;
8
;
37
;
30
;
4
;
3
.
One of the important issues in using quantum sensors is the effect of noise;
although noises perpendicular to the DM signal can be reduced by entangled sensors,
noises parallel to the DM signal, which we call “excitation noise”, cannot be reduced by such methods and especially problematic
7
;
28
;
20
;
1
;
6
.

Classically, excitation noise can be suppressed by employing multiple sensors and analyzing their correlations, since the excitation noise at each sensor is independent while the DM signal is correlated.
This naturally raises the question of whether we can do the similar analysis for quantum sensors, assuming that the excitation noise at each sensor is independent.
Refs.
33
;
8
;
22
have proposed to measure sensors with
entangled states such as
W
states
19
, the equal superposition of single-excitation states across multiple sensors, to achieve this goal. Nevertheless, as discussed in Ref.
8
, this procedure needs the presence of other noises, such as de-excitation noises.
It seems counterintuitive that the protocol in Ref.
8

cannot reduce the excitation noise at all if there is no other noise.
The underlying reason is that, when excitation noise is dominant, it can lead to multiple sensors being excited simultaneously — events that the
W
-state measurement cannot distinguish.

In this letter, we explore the possibility of processing sensor states during measurements to avoid this restriction. The crucial insight is that sensor states after noise effects occupy a subspace nearly orthogonal to the “code space”, the space spanned by the
W
state and the ground state. By designing a sensing protocol inspired by quantum error correction (QEC) techniques
28
;
20
;
1
, which pulls erroneous states back into the original code space repeatedly, we demonstrate that excitation noise can be mitigated even in the absence of other noise. As a result, the sensitivity to the DM signal is enhanced by a factor of
\sqrt{N}
with
N
sensors, and the uncertainty scales as
1/N
.

Although we find that the QEC-like protocol can mitigate the excitation noise and the uncertainty scales with
1/N
, we do not claim that our protocol is beyond the standard quantum limit (SQL)
24
. Rather, we find that by our protocol, the sensitivity to the DM signal may reach the SQL by the ideal measurement if the number of sensors is sufficiently large.
This is consistent with the generic theorem that the QEC-assisted metrology cannot beat the SQL when the noise and the signal Hamiltonian are parallel
32
;
16
;
38
. Nonetheless, we emphasize that our protocol is useful in the context of DM searches with quantum sensors; DM signals are not coherent over long time scales, and thus, in practical situations, one cannot repeat the ideal measurements over long times.

DM Search with Quantum Sensors —

Let us first summarize our setup for DM searches with quantum sensors.
We consider
N
quantum sensors, each of which is modeled as a two-level system with ground state
\ket{0}_{i}
and excited state
\ket{1}_{i}
for the
i
-th sensor; see App.
A
for definitions of states used in this letter. Note that multi-level systems such as resonant cavities can be treated in the same framework by focusing on two relevant energy levels
23
.
The energy difference between the two states is denoted as
\omega_{0}
, so that the Hamiltonian of the
i
-th sensor is given by
H_{i}=-(\omega_{0}/2)\sigma_{i}^{z}
, where
\sigma_{i}^{a}
(
a=x,y,z
) are the Pauli operators acting on the
i
-th sensor.
On the other hand, the DM field is modeled as a classical oscillating field with frequency
m
. The interaction between the DM field and the sensors is described by the Hamiltonian
H_{\rm int}=\epsilon\cos(m t + \varphi)\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}^{x}
, where
\epsilon
is a small coupling constant and
\varphi
is an unknown phase. DM field is assumed to be coherent over a time scale
\tau_{\text{DM}}\sim 2\pi/(mv^{2})
, where
v\sim 10^{-3}
is the typical velocity of DM particles in our galaxy, so that
\varphi
can be treated as a constant during the time scale
\tau_{\text{DM}}
but changes randomly for longer time scales. The same conclusion holds if we treat the DM field as a classical stochastic field
21
;
23
.

To detect the DM signal, we start from the initial state
\ket{\psi_{\rm init}}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}\ket{0}_{i}
and let the system evolve under the total Hamiltonian
H=\sum_{i=1}^{N}H_{i}+H_{\rm int}
for a time duration
\tau\lesssim 2\pi/(mv^{2})
.
We move to the interaction picture with respect to
\sum_{i=1}^{N}H_{i}
assuming that
|\omega_{0}-m|\tau\ll 1
so that we can apply the rotating wave approximation (RWA) and neglect the rapidly oscillating terms.
Then, the effective Hamiltonian in the interaction picture is given by

\displaystyle H_{I}=\epsilon\sum_{i=1}^{N}\quantity( \sigma_i^x \cos\varphi- \sigma_i^y \sin\varphi).

(1)

In addition to the DM signal, we take into account the effect of environmental noise. For simplicity, we focus on the Markovian noises described by the Lindblad master equation
29
;
25
. In this letter, we consider the bit-flip noise for individual sensors in the interaction picture, which is described by the Lindblad operators
L_{i}=\sigma_{i}^{x}
for
i=1,2,\ldots,N
. The time evolution of the density matrix
\rho
in the interaction picture is then given by

\displaystyle\frac{d\rho}{dt}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}[\rho]\equiv-i[H_{I},\rho]+\gamma\sum_{i=1}^{N}D_{i}[\rho],

(2)

\displaystyle D_{i}[\rho]

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{2}\quantity( \sigma_i^x \rho\sigma_i^x - \rho),

(3)

where
\gamma
is the bit-flip rate and assumed to be the same for all sensors. Since we are interested in the situation where the noise is stronger than the signal, we assume
\gamma\gg|\epsilon|
and
\gamma\tau\lesssim 1
in this letter.
Even if we restrict ourselves to individual noises, i.e., noises acting on each sensor independently, there are other types of noise such as dephasing noise, de-excitation noise, and excitation noise. However, as explained in App.
B
,
these noises can be reduced to the bit-flip noise in, for example, the following way. First, we construct a logical qubit by constructing the GHZ state
26
from 3 physical qubits. Then, we apply QEC-like operations
28
;
20
;
1
to project noises to the bit-flip noise. Therefore, we may focus on the bit-flip noise.

A schematic diagram of our protocol is shown in Fig.
1
.
We divide the sensing time
\tau
into
N_{\tau}
intervals of duration
\Delta t=\tau/N_{\tau}
.

\Delta t
is chosen such that
N\gamma\Delta t\ll 1
and
m\Delta t\gg 1
,
so that the probability of having two or more bit-flip errors during each interval is negligible and the RWA is valid, respectively. Note that our protocol can be applied even if the RWA is not valid by performing a similar analysis as Ref.
34
.
Each unit interval, shown as the dashed box in Fig.
1
, consists of two steps; first, we let the system evolve under the master equation for a time duration
\Delta t
, which is denoted as the quantum channel
\mathcal{E}_{\Delta t}
in Fig.
1
.
Since the time evolution is governed by the Lindblad equation, the quantum channel
\mathcal{E}_{\Delta t}^{\otimes N}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\Delta t}^{\otimes N}[\rho(t)]=\rho(t+\Delta t)\simeq\rho(t)+\mathcal{L}[\rho(t)]\Delta t.

(4)

Here, we have used the fact that
N\gamma\Delta t\ll 1
to expand the time evolution up to the first order in
\Delta t
.
Next, we apply a control operation
\mathcal{E}_{\rm c}
to mitigate the bit-flip errors that may have occurred during the time interval
\Delta t
. As we have claimed, we assume that this control operation takes negligible time and does not introduce additional noise.

We now describe the construction of the control operation
\mathcal{E}_{\rm c}
for mitigating bit-flip errors. The DM signal Hamiltonian
H_{I}
excites
\ket{0}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}\ket{0}_{i}
to
\ket{W}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}^{x}\ket{0}
, motivating us to define the logical code space as
\mathcal{H}_{L}=\{\ket{0}_{L}\equiv\ket{0},\,\ket{1}_{L}\equiv\ket{W}\}
. We design
\mathcal{E}_{\rm c}
to (approximately) detect and correct single bit-flip errors such that the state after
\mathcal{E}_{\rm c}
lies within the code space.
Then, what can be the syndrome measurement and the recovery operation?
A bit-flip error on the
i
-th sensor transforms a code state
a\ket{0}+b\ket{W}
into
a\ket{i}+b\ket{2,i}+\mathcal{O}(N^{-1/2})
, where
\ket{i}
and
\ket{2,i}
span the error subspace
\mathcal{H}_{E_{i}}
. However, these error subspaces are not orthogonal to the code space, reflecting the fact that bit-flip errors cannot be perfectly distinguished from the DM signal
8
. We also note that error subspaces are mutually non-orthogonal, i.e.,
\innerproduct{2, i}{2, j}\neq 0
for
i\neq j
.

Thus, instead of projective syndrome measurements, we employ a POVM with the following measurement operators:

\displaystyle M_{0}

\displaystyle\equiv\outerproduct{0}{0}+\outerproduct{W}{W},

(5)

\displaystyle M_{i}

\displaystyle\equiv\sqrt{\frac{N}{N-1}}\ket{i}_{\perp}\!\!\bra{i}_{\perp}+\frac{\sqrt{N(N-1)}}{N-2}\ket{2, i}_{\perp}\!\!\bra{2, i}_{\perp},

(6)

\displaystyle M_{S}

\displaystyle\equiv\outerproduct{S}{S},

(7)

for
i=1,2,\ldots,N
, where

\displaystyle\ket{i}_{\perp}

\displaystyle\equiv\ket{i}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\ket{W},

(8)

\displaystyle\ket{2, i}_{\perp}

\displaystyle\equiv\ket{2, i}-\sqrt{\frac{2}{N}}\ket{S}.

(9)

The corresponding POVM elements are
E_{a}=M_{a}^{\dagger}M_{a}
for
a=0,1,\ldots,N,S
, and
\tilde{E}=I-\sum_{a}E_{a}
. As shown in App.
C
, these operators form a POVM, and
\text{Tr}[\tilde{E}\rho(t+\Delta t)]=0
up to
\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{2})
. Thus, the measurement outcomes are restricted to
\{0,1,\ldots,N,S\}
.
The measurement outcome
i=1,2,\ldots,N
corresponds to detecting a bit-flip error on the
i
-th sensor. The additional outcome
S
is required for the completeness.

We next define the recovery operation based on the measurement outcomes.
Errors are detected when the measurement outcome is
a=1,2,\ldots,N,S
.
When the outcome is
a=1,2,\ldots,N
, we apply such a unitary operation that maps
\frac{\ket{i}_{\perp}}{|\ket{i}_{\perp}|}
to
\ket{0}
and
\frac{\ket{2, i}_{\perp}}{|\ket{2, i}_{\perp}|}
to
\ket{W}
, which approximately corrects the bit-flip error on the
i
-th sensor.
When the outcome is
S
, this is originated from a bit-flip error on
\ket{W}
, and we apply a unitary operation that maps
\ket{S}
to
\ket{W}
.

Combining the syndrome measurement and the recovery operation, we define new measurement operators as
R_{0}\equiv M_{0}
and

\displaystyle R_{i}

\displaystyle\equiv\outerproduct{0}{i}_{\perp}+\sqrt{\frac{N-1}{N-2}}\outerproduct{W}{2, i}_{\perp}\!,

(10)

\displaystyle R_{S}

\displaystyle\equiv\outerproduct{W}{S}.

(11)

Note that
R_{a}^{\dagger}R_{a}=E_{a}
for all
a
. The control operation
\mathcal{E}_{\rm c}
is then defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\rm c}[\rho]\equiv\sum_{a}R_{a}\rho R_{a}^{\dagger}.

(12)

Obviously, states after the control operation lie in
\mathcal{H}_{L}
.
Throughout this control operation, we need to use
\mathcal{O}(\log N)
ancilla qubits for implementing the POVM
35
as projection measurements. This is small enough to be negligible when we discuss the scaling.

Let us now see the density matrix after a single unit interval corresponding to time
t
to
t+\Delta t
. Using Eqs. (
4
) and (
12
), we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\rm c}\circ\mathcal{E}_{\Delta t}^{\otimes N}[\rho(t)]

\displaystyle\simeq\rho(t)+\mathcal{E}_{\rm c}\quantity[\mathcal{L}[\rho(t)]]\Delta t,

(13)

up to
\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{2})
. Consequently,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\rm c}\quantity[\mathcal{L}[\rho(t)]]

\displaystyle=-i[H_{IL},\rho(t)]

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{N\gamma}{2}\rho(t)+\frac{\gamma}{2}\sum_{a}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathscr{S}_{a,i}^{x}\rho(t)\mathscr{S}_{a,i}^{x\dagger},

(14)

where

\displaystyle H_{IL}

\displaystyle\equiv R_{0}H_{I}R_{0}^{\dagger}=\epsilon\sqrt{N}\quantity( \sigma_L^x \cos\varphi- \sigma_L^y \sin\varphi),

(15)

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}_{a,i}^{x}

\displaystyle\equiv R_{a}\sigma_{i}^{x}R_{0}.

(16)

Here,
\sigma_{L}^{a}
(
a=x,y,z
) are the Pauli operators acting on the logical code space.
A straightforward calculation yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\rm c}\quantity[\mathcal{L}[\rho(t)]]

\displaystyle=-i[H_{IL},\rho(t)]+\frac{\gamma}{2}\quantity[-\rho(t) + \sigma_L^x \rho(t) \sigma_L^x]+\frac{c\gamma}{4}\quantity[ -\rho(t) + \sigma_L^z \rho(t) \sigma_L^z]\equiv\mathcal{L}_{L}[\rho(t)],

(17)

where we have defined
c\equiv\frac{2(N-1)}{N+\sqrt{N(N-2)}}=\mathcal{O}(N^{0})
, and

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\rm c}\circ\mathcal{E}_{\Delta t}^{\otimes N}[\rho(t)]

\displaystyle\simeq\rho(t)+\mathcal{L}_{L}[\rho(t)]\Delta t,

(18)

up to
\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{2})
.

\mathcal{L}_{L}
describes the effective time evolution of the density matrix within the code space and can be interpreted as an effective Lindblad superoperator for the logical qubit.

Uncertainty Estimation —

We now estimate the uncertainty of the DM signal with our QEC-like protocol.
Let the total time we use be
T
, during which we repeat multiple measurements with time
\tau
.
For the moment, we assume
that the phase
\varphi
can be treated as a constant during
T
.
As we have seen, after a single unit interval, the density matrix evolves in the code space according to the effective Lindblad equation with
\mathcal{L}_{L}
up to
\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{2})
.
As we take the limit
\Delta t\to 0
, the density matrix at time
T
is arbitrarily close to the solution of the effective Lindblad equation

\displaystyle\frac{d\rho_{L}}{dt}=\mathcal{L}_{L}[\rho_{L}],

(19)

with the initial condition
\rho_{L}(0)=\ket{0}_{L}\!\!\bra{0}_{L}
. To estimate the DM signal
\epsilon
, we perform a projective measurement
P_{W}\equiv\ket{1}_{L}\!\!\bra{1}_{L}
(or, for the states in the logical code space, equivalently,
\sum_{i}\ket{1}_{i}\!\!\bra{1}_{i}
on the physical qubits)
on the final state
\rho_{L}(T)
.

To discuss the performance of measurement protocols, we use the standard deviation of the estimator of
\epsilon
as a measure of uncertainty.
Using the linear approximation
23
, the standard deviation,
\delta\epsilon
, is given by

\displaystyle\delta\epsilon\simeq\frac{\sqrt{\expectationvalue{O^2}-\expectationvalue{O}^{2}}}{\absolutevalue{\pdv{\ev{O}}{\epsilon}}\sqrt{N_{\text{rep}}}},

(20)

where
O
is the observable to estimate
\epsilon
and
N_{\text{rep}}
is the number of repetitions of measurements, given as
T/\tau
.
The expectation values are calculated with respect to the final state after each sensing time
\tau
, which is determined so that
\delta\epsilon
is minimized for each protocol.

We first estimate the uncertainty without error mitigation or any entanglement between sensors, denoted as
\delta\epsilon_{0}
.
\delta\epsilon_{0}
is calculated using the density matrix
\rho_{0}(t)
, which is the solution of the original Lindblad equation, Eq. (
2
), with the initial condition
\rho_{0}(0)=\ket{0}\!\!\bra{0}
.
As the observable, we count the total number of excited sensors, i.e.,
O=P_{Z}\equiv\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{2}(1-\sigma_{i}^{z})
, which is typically used in DM searches with quantum sensors
9
.
The bit-flip noise causes exponential decay of the coherence, limiting the maximum sensing time to
\tau\lesssim 1/\gamma
. As a result, the expectation value is given by

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{P_Z}_{\rho_{0}(\tau)}

\displaystyle\simeq N\quantity(c_0^{(S)} \epsilon^2 \tau^2 + c_0^{(N)} \gamma\tau),

(21)

up to the leading order in
\epsilon^{2}\tau^{2}
and
\gamma\tau
, where
c_{0}^{(S)}
and
c_{0}^{(N)}
are constants of order unity.
Using Eq. (
20
), we obtain

\displaystyle\delta\epsilon_{0}\sim\frac{\sqrt{\gamma}}{\epsilon\tau\sqrt{NT}}\sim\frac{\gamma^{3/2}}{\epsilon\sqrt{NT}},

(22)

where we have chosen
\tau\sim 1/\gamma
to minimize
\delta\epsilon_{0}
.

We next estimate the uncertainty with our QEC-like protocol, denoted as
\delta\epsilon_{L}
with
O=P_{W}
. The effective Lindblad equation contains both bit-flip and dephasing noises, limiting the sensing time to
\tau\lesssim 1/\gamma
as in the previous case.
To estimate
\delta\epsilon_{L}
, we need to compare the signal rate,
\epsilon\sqrt{N}
, with the noise rate,
\mathcal{O}(N^{0})\times\gamma
.
With fixed
\epsilon
and
\gamma
with
\epsilon\ll\gamma
, the signal rate is much smaller than the noise rate for small
N
,
N\lesssim N_{\text{th}}\equiv(\gamma/\epsilon)^{2}
.
Then, the expectation value is given by

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{P_W}_{\rho_{L}(\tau)}^{\text{(small $N$)}}

\displaystyle\simeq c_{L}^{(S)}\epsilon^{2}N\tau^{2}+c_{L}^{(N)}\gamma\tau,

(23)

up to the leading order in
\epsilon^{2}N\tau^{2}
and
\gamma\tau
, where
c_{L}^{(S)}
and
c_{L}^{(N)}
are constants of order unity.
Using Eq. (
20
), we obtain

\displaystyle\delta\epsilon_{L}^{\text{(small $N$)}}\sim\frac{\gamma^{3/2}}{\epsilon N\sqrt{T}},

(24)

for small
N
, where we have chosen
\tau\sim 1/\gamma
to minimize the uncertainty.
Comparing this with Eq. (
22
), we find that our protocol improves the uncertainty by a factor of
\sqrt{N}
for small
N
. This is the main advantage of our protocol.

For
N\gtrsim N_{\text{th}}
, the signal rate becomes larger than the noise rate. In this case, we may ignore the noise contribution, yielding

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{P_W}_{\rho_{L}(\tau)}^{\text{(large $N$)}}

\displaystyle\simeq\sin^{2}\epsilon\sqrt{N}\tau\sim\epsilon^{2}N\tau^{2},

(25)

The uncertainty is then given by

\displaystyle\delta\epsilon_{L}^{\prime\text{(large $N$)}}\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{N\tau T}}\sim\frac{\sqrt{\epsilon}}{N^{1/4}\sqrt{T}},

(26)

where we have chosen
\tau\sim 1/\epsilon\sqrt{N}
. However, this is not optimal; rather, if we divide
N
sensors into
N/N_{\text{th}}
groups of size
N_{\text{th}}
and apply our protocol to each group independently, the number of repetitions increases by
N/N_{\text{th}}
and we obtain

\displaystyle\delta\epsilon_{L}^{\text{(large $N$)}}\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{N/N_{\text{th}}}\cdot\sqrt{N_{\text{th}}\tau T}}\sim\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{NT}},

(27)

where we have chosen
\tau\sim 1/\gamma
. One can check
\delta\epsilon_{L}^{\text{(large $N$)}}<\delta\epsilon_{L}^{\prime\text{(large $N$)}}
for
N>N_{\text{th}}
.
Therefore, we conclude that the scaling of the uncertainty with our protocol “saturates” for large
N
and scales as
1/\sqrt{N}
. This uncertainty is improved by a factor
\epsilon/\gamma
compared with the uncertainty without error mitigation.

Eq. (
27
) satisfies the quantum Cramér-Rao bound
31
for the
original Lindblad equation with the initial state
\ket{0}
, as shown in App.
D
. This means that there is an ideal measurement that achieves the same uncertainty as our protocol without error mitigation.
This is the Pauli-
Y
measurements on each sensor,
O=P_{Y}\equiv\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{2}(1+\sigma_{i}^{y})
.
\expectationvalue{P_Y}_{\rho_{0}(\tau)}\simeq\frac{N}{2}\quantity(1 - \epsilon\tau\cos\varphi)
and
the uncertainty without error mitigation becomes

\displaystyle\delta\epsilon_{0}^{(Y)}\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{N\tau T}}\sim\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{NT}},

(28)

where
\tau\sim 1/\gamma
. Indeed, this coincides with Eq. (
27
).
Generally, Pauli-
Y
measurements give larger signal effects,
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon\tau)
, compared to Pauli-
Z
measurements,
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2}\tau^{2})
, but without noise, this is compensated by the larger variance, resulting in the same uncertainty scaling. However, with the bit-flip noise, both measurements result in the larger variance due to the noise, but since the signal effect is larger for Pauli-
Y
measurements, the uncertainty scaling is better. This is why Pauli-
Y
measurements are the ideal measurements in this case.

This is consistent with Refs.
32
;
16
;
38
; when the signal and noise operators are parallel, we cannot surpass the SQL even with entanglement between sensors. Nonetheless, we claim that our protocol is useful, because
for the DM search, we may not perform Pauli-
Y
measurements due to the unknown phase
\varphi
.
The DM phase is unknown and can be treated as a random variable uniformly distributed in
[0,2\pi)
, which changes over time scales longer than the DM coherence time.
In fact, if we average over
\varphi
, the expectation value of
P_{Y}
becomes independent of
\epsilon
, making it impossible to estimate the DM signal.
On the other hand, our protocol works even with the unknown phase
\varphi
since the
\epsilon^{2}
term appears in the expectation value, which does not vanish after averaging over
\varphi
.

Lastly, we comment on the case where we have other types of noise than the bit-flip noise. Suppose, for simplicity, we have both excitation and de-excitation noises with the rate
\gamma_{\text{EX}}
and
\gamma_{\text{DE}}
, respectively. As explained in App.
B
, we can reduce these noises to the bit-flip noise with rate
\gamma_{\text{eff}}\sim\gamma_{\text{EX}}+\gamma_{\text{DE}}
by constructing logical qubits from 3 physical qubits and applying control operations. Assuming
\epsilon\ll\gamma_{\text{EX}}
, the uncertainty without error mitigation is then given as
8

\displaystyle\delta\epsilon_{0}^{(\text{EX$+$DE})}\sim\frac{\sqrt{\gamma_{\text{EX}}}\gamma_{\text{eff}}}{\epsilon\sqrt{NT}}.

(29)

On the other hand, with our protocol, the uncertainty is given by Eqs. (
24
) and (
27
) with
\gamma\to\gamma_{\text{eff}}
. Therefore, our protocol improves the uncertainty for this case as well.

Conclusions and Discussion —

In this letter, we have proposed a QEC-like error mitigation protocol to improve the sensitivity of quantum sensors for DM searches under general Markovian noise acting on each sensor independently. By constructing a logical qubit from
N
sensors and applying adaptive control operations, we have shown that our protocol improves the uncertainty by a factor of
\sqrt{N}
compared to the protocol without error mitigation for small
N
. For large
N
, the uncertainty scaling saturates to the SQL by the ideal measurement, which is impossible to achieve for the DM search due to the unknown DM phase.
We, for the first time, have shown that the ideal sensitivity can be achieved even with the unknown phase by using our protocol.
Although we mainly focus on the DM search, our protocol can be applied to general quantum sensing tasks where the signal Hamiltonian contains unknown random phases and the noise acts on each sensor independently.

The main advantage of our protocol stems from the fact that the DM signal includes a “nuisance” parameter that makes the repetition of the ideal measurement impossible. In the conventional quantum metrology setting, such parameters are absent, but in realistic applications, in particular in high-energy physics like the search for DM or high-frequency gravitational waves, it often appears. Our work opens up a new direction of quantum metrology for such situations.

In practice, we admit that our protocol is complicated and challenging to implement with current technology. It is an important future work to simplify the protocol, in particular for a smaller number of sensors, and to analyze the performance under imperfect quantum operations and finite operation time for practical applications.

Appendix B
Reduction of Various Noises to Bit-Flip Noise

In this appendix, we explain how various types of noise can be reduced to the bit-flip noise considered in the main text.
As we have mentioned, we first construct a logical qubit by using 3 physical qubits to form the GHZ state and then apply QEC operations.
We divide the total sensing time into small intervals,
\Delta t_{3}
, let the system evolve under the noise for a short time, and then apply a control operation.

The logical code space is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{3L}=\{\ket{+}_{3L}\equiv\ket{+++},\,\ket{-}_{3L}\equiv\ket{---}\},

(36)

where
\ket{\pm}=(\ket{0}\pm\ket{1})/\sqrt{2}
. The initial state is
\ket{\psi_{3, \rm init}}=\ket{0}_{3L}
, where

\displaystyle\ket{0}_{3L}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\quantity(\ket{+}_{3L} + \ket{-}_{3L})

(37)

\displaystyle\ket{1}_{3L}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\quantity(\ket{+}_{3L} - \ket{-}_{3L}).

(38)

For short enough
\Delta t_{3}
, we may assume that at most one noise event occurs during each interval. Therefore, depending on which sensor the noise acts on, three types of error subspaces appear:

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{3E,i}=\{\sigma_{i}^{z}\ket{+}_{3L},\,\sigma_{i}^{z}\ket{-}_{3L}\},

(39)

for
i=1,2,3
. Let
P_{i}
be the projection operator onto
\mathcal{H}_{3E,i}
and
P_{L}
be the projection operator onto the code space
\mathcal{H}_{3L}
. To detect errors, we perform the projective measurement with the measurement operators
\{P_{L},P_{1},P_{2},P_{3}\}
. If the measurement outcome is
P_{i}
, we apply the recovery operation
\sigma_{i}^{z}
to correct the error. Let us denote this control operation as
\mathcal{E}_{3\rm c}
.

Now, we consider the effect of various types of noise, as well as the DM signal, during the time interval
\Delta t_{3}
.
For simplicity, we focus on de-excitation noise, excitation noise, and the dephasing noise on individual sensors, but other types of noise can be treated similarly.
Similar to the main text, we start from the density matrix
\rho_{3}(t)
with the three qubits at time
t
within the code space and let the system evolve under the total Lindblad equation for a time duration
\Delta t_{3}
. Then, the density matrix at time
t+\Delta t_{3}
is given by

\displaystyle\rho_{3}(t+\Delta t_{3})

\displaystyle\simeq\rho_{3}(t)+\mathcal{L}_{3}[\rho_{3}(t)]\Delta t_{3},

(40)

up to first order in
\Delta t_{3}
. The Lindblad superoperator
\mathcal{L}_{3}
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{3}[\rho_{3}]

\displaystyle\equiv-i[H_{3I},\rho_{3}]+\sum_{i=1}^{3}\sum_{\alpha}\gamma_{\alpha}D_{i}^{(\alpha)}[\rho_{3}],

(41)

where
H_{3I}
is the sum of the interaction Hamiltonian in the interaction picture for the three qubits and
the sum over
\alpha
runs over DE (de-excitation), EX (excitation), and DP (dephasing)
with
\gamma_{\rm DE}
,
\gamma_{\rm EX}
, and
\gamma_{\rm DP}
being these noise rates, respectively.
The superoperators
D_{i}^{(\text{DE})}
,
D_{i}^{(\text{EX})}
, and
D_{i}^{(\text{DP})}
are defined as

\displaystyle D_{i}^{(\text{DE})}[\rho_{3}]

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{2}\quantity( a_i \rho_3 a_i^\dagger- \frac{1}{2}\{a_i^\dagger a_i,\rho_3 \}),

(42)

\displaystyle D_{i}^{(\text{EX})}[\rho_{3}]

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{2}\quantity( a_i^\dagger\rho_3 a_i - \frac{1}{2}\{a_i a_i^\dagger,\rho_3 \}),

(43)

\displaystyle D_{i}^{(\text{DP})}[\rho_{3}]

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{2}\quantity( \sigma_i^z \rho_3 \sigma_i^z - \rho_3),

(44)

where
a_{i}\equiv\ket{0}_{i}\!\!\bra{1}_{i}
is the annihilation operator for the
i
-th sensor.

After applying the control operation
\mathcal{E}_{3\rm c}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{3\rm c}\quantity[\mathcal{L}_3[\rho_{3}(t)]]

\displaystyle=-i[H_{3LI},\rho_{3}(t)]

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i}^{3}\sum_{\alpha}\gamma_{\alpha}\mathcal{E}_{3\rm c}\quantity[D_i^{(\alpha)}[\rho_{3}(t)]],

(45)

where

\displaystyle H_{3LI}

\displaystyle\equiv P_{L}H_{I}P_{L}=3\epsilon\sigma_{3L}^{x}\cos\varphi,

(46)

with
\sigma_{3L}^{a}
(
a=x,y,z
) being the Pauli operators acting on the logical code space,
\{\ket{0}_{3L},\ket{1}_{3L}\}
.
Although the oscillation term proportional to
\sigma_{3L}^{y}
vanishes, the effective coupling strength is of the same order as the original one.
By a straightforward calculation, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{3,\rm c}\quantity[D_i^{(\text{DE})}[\rho_{3}(t)]]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\quantity[-\rho_{3}(t) + \sigma_{3L}^x \rho_{3}(t) \sigma_{3L}^x],

(47)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{3,\rm c}\quantity[D_i^{(\text{EX})}[\rho_{3}(t)]]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\quantity[-\rho_{3}(t) + \sigma_{3L}^x \rho_{3}(t) \sigma_{3L}^x],

(48)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{3,\rm c}\quantity[D_i^{(\text{DP})}[\rho_{3}(t)]]

\displaystyle=0.

(49)

Therefore, the effective noise after the control operation is described by the bit-flip noise with the rate
3(\gamma_{\rm DE}+\gamma_{\rm EX})/2
. These procedures may change the signal strength, the noise rate and the number of sensors by factors of order unity, but the scaling of the uncertainty remains unchanged.

Appendix C
Positivity of Our POVM Elements

In this appendix, we prove that our POVM elements,
E_{a}
(
a=0,1,\ldots,N,S
), and
\tilde{E}=I-\sum_{a}E_{a}
are positive operators. It is obvious that
E_{a}
are positive operators since they are defined as
E_{a}=M_{a}^{\dagger}M_{a}
. Therefore, we only need to show that
\tilde{E}
is a positive operator.

To show this, we first separate the Hilbert space by the total excitation numbers.
Let
P_{k}
be the projection operator onto the subspace with
k
excitations.
By the definition, we have
E_{a}P_{k}=P_{k}E_{a}=0
for
k>2
and any
a
. Therefore, we need to prove that
P_{k}\tilde{E}P_{k}
are positive semidefinite for
k=0,1,2
.
For
k=0
, we have
P_{0}\tilde{E}P_{0}=P_{0}-\outerproduct{0}{0}=0
.
k=1
case is also straightforward; we have

\displaystyle P_{1}\tilde{E}P_{1}=P_{1}-\outerproduct{W}{W}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ket{i}_{\perp}\!\!\bra{i}_{\perp}=0,

(50)

where we have used
P_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\outerproduct{i}{i}
.

Finally, we consider the
k=2
case. Using the definition of
E_{a}
, we obtain

\displaystyle P_{2}\tilde{E}P_{2}

\displaystyle=P_{2}-\frac{N-1}{N-2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\outerproduct{2, i}{2, i}+\frac{N}{N-2}\outerproduct{S}{S}.

(51)

Let a matrix
G
be defined as
G\equiv\sum_{i=1}^{N}\outerproduct{2, i}{2, i}
. The kernel of
G
in the double-excitation subspace is an orthogonal complement of the space
\mathcal{H}_{G}\equiv\{\ket{2, i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
. Since
\ket{S}
is in
\mathcal{H}_{G}
, we need to focus on
\mathcal{H}_{G}
to check the positivity of
P_{2}\tilde{E}P_{2}
. Also, it is easy to see that
\ket{S}
is an eigenvector of
G
with the eigenvalue
2
.

To see the eigenvalues of
G
, we assume
\ket{2, i}
as a matrix with the row index being the index of the Hilbert space and the column index being
i
. Let us denote this matrix as
\mathcal{A}
. Then,
G
can be written as
G=\mathcal{A}\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}
. By the singular value decomposition, we can write
\mathcal{A}=U\Sigma V^{\dagger}
, where
U
and
V
are unitary matrices and
\Sigma
is a diagonal matrix with non-negative real numbers on the diagonal. Therefore, the non-zero eigenvalues of
G
are given by the square of the non-zero singular values of
\mathcal{A}
, which are equal to the non-zero eigenvalues of
\tilde{G}\equiv\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}\mathcal{A}
. The
i,j
component of
\tilde{G}
is given by
\innerproduct{2, i}{2, j}
and therefore

\displaystyle\tilde{G}=\frac{N-2}{N-1}I+\frac{1}{N-1}J,

(52)

where
J
is the matrix with all components being
1
. The eigenvalues of
J
are
0
with the multiplicity of
N-1
and
N
with the multiplicity of
1
. Therefore, the eigenvalues of
\tilde{G}
are
2
and
\frac{N-2}{N-1}
with the multiplicity of
1
and
N-1
, respectively. As a result, so are the eigenvalues of
G
. Since
\ket{S}
is the eigenvector with the eigenvalue
2
, we obtain

\displaystyle P_{2}\tilde{E}P_{2}\big|_{\mathcal{H}_{G}}=0,

(53)

which completes the proof. It is straightforward to see that
P_{2}\mathcal{E}_{\Delta t}^{\otimes N}[\rho(t)]P_{2}
is an operator in
\mathcal{H}_{G}
for any density matrix
\rho(t)
in the code space. Therefore, we may also see that
\tilde{E}\mathcal{E}_{\Delta t}^{\otimes N}[\rho(t)]
is vanishing.

Appendix D
Quantum Fisher Information of the Original Lindblad Equation

In this appendix, we calculate the quantum Fisher information (QFI) of the original Lindblad equation, Eq. (
2
), with the initial state
\ket{0}
and show that the uncertainty, Eq. (
28
), saturates the Quantum Cramér-Rao Bound.

Similar to the main text, we assume the measurement time
\tau
is short enough so that we can expand the density matrix up to the first order in
\epsilon\tau
and
\gamma\tau
.
The density matrix at time
\tau
is given by

\displaystyle\rho_{0}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}\rho_{i}(\tau),

(54)

\displaystyle\rho_{i}(\tau)

\displaystyle\simeq\ket{0}_{i}\!\!\bra{0}_{i}-\epsilon\tau\quantity(\sigma_i^y \cos\varphi+ \sigma_i^x \sin\varphi)-\frac{\gamma\tau}{2}\sigma_{i}^{z}.

(55)

The eigenvectors of
\rho_{i}(\tau)
are given by
\ket{0}_{i}
and
\ket{1}_{i}
with the eigenvalues
1
and
0
, respectively, up to
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon\tau,\gamma\tau)
.
Using these expressions, we can calculate the QFI for a single sensor,
\mathcal{F}_{i}
, as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{i}\sim\absolutevalue{\!\mel{0}{\partial_\epsilon\rho_i(\tau)}{1}}^{2}\sim\tau^{2}.

(56)

Therefore, the total QFI for
N
sensors and the total measurement time
T
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\text{total}}\sim N\frac{T}{\tau}\tau^{2}=N\tau T.

(57)

Using the Quantum Cramér-Rao Bound, the standard deviation of the estimator of
\epsilon
is bounded as

\displaystyle\delta\epsilon\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{F}_{\text{total}}}}\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{N\tau T}}.

(58)

The lower bound is indeed achieved by the Pauli-
Y
measurements on each sensor, as we have seen in Eq. (
28
).
