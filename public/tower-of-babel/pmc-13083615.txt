Baseline positional asymmetry and its association with planned tooth movements in clear aligner therapy
Authors: Tugba Haliloglu Ozkan, Elif Nadide Akay Polat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDigital treatment planning software in clear aligner (CA) therapy incorporates virtual simulations to generate planned tooth movements. While baseline tooth positions constitute one of the geometric inputs to digital setups, limited information is available regarding the association between pre-treatment positional asymmetry and the magnitude of planned movements, and how these associations vary by tooth and movement type.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 65 patients (390 anterior teeth) treated with Invisalign®. Baseline malpositions of anterior teeth were measured using 3D digital models in ClinCheck®, while planned movements—including rotation, angulation, extrusion, and intrusion—were extracted from the corresponding digital treatment plans. Baseline asymmetry between contralateral teeth was calculated to quantify movement complexity. General Linear Model (GLM) regression analyses were employed to assess the relationship between initial asymmetry and the magnitude of planned movements. Descriptive statistics and normality assessments were also performed.ResultsIn the 11–21 tooth pair, baseline asymmetry was significantly associated with planned extrusion (p = .002) and rotation (p < .001), but not intrusion (p = .190) or angulation (p = .270). For the 12–22 pair, significant associations were observed for intrusion (p = .008), angulation (p < .001), and rotation (p < .001), but not extrusion (p = .493). In the 13–23 pair, only extrusion was significantly associated with baseline differences (p < .001); no significant effects were observed for the other movement types.ConclusionBaseline positional asymmetry was associated with variation in planned extrusion, intrusion, and rotation movements within digital CA setups. These findings should be interpreted as descriptive associations rather than evidence of software-imposed constraints or internal planning strategies, and underscore the relevance of baseline geometry when interpreting digitally planned tooth movements.

Background

CA therapy has become a widely accepted alternative to fixed orthodontic appliances due to its esthetic, hygienic, and functional advantages [1]. A critical component of CA success is digital treatment planning, wherein sequential virtual movements are designed to guide teeth toward the desired final positions. Ideally, the magnitude of each planned movement should be appropriately scaled to the complexity of the initial malposition [2, 3].

In cases of substantial misalignment, larger or more complex corrective movements are required, and the treatment planning software should proportionally adjust its digital prescriptions. Failure to do so may result in undercorrection, increased need for refinements, longer treatment duration, and reduced clinical predictability [4]. Conversely, if the software overcompensates by exaggerating movements in difficult cases, this may lead to inefficient staging or excessive aligner wear, ultimately compromising treatment efficiency.

While existing literature has extensively evaluated the clinical accuracy of CA systems—by comparing planned versus achieved movements—few studies have examined the internal logic of how movements are planned in the first place [5, 6]. Most notably, discrepancies are often reported in vertical and rotational movements, where clinical outcomes frequently diverge from predictions [7, 8]. However, these findings primarily assess performance outcomes rather than evaluating whether planned movements were appropriately scaled in response to initial positional complexity.

Digital treatment planning systems used in CA therapy rely on proprietary algorithms, the internal logic of which remains undisclosed [3]. Consequently, it is unclear whether the planned tooth movements generated by the system scale proportionally to the magnitude or complexity of malposition. Specifically, no study has systematically evaluated whether planned movements are dynamically scaled based on baseline asymmetry between contralateral teeth. Investigating this question may provide insight into the relationship between baseline positional asymmetry and the distribution of planned corrective movements across different teeth and movement types.

Accordingly, the aim of this study was to evaluate whether the magnitude of planned tooth movements generated by the Invisalign® system corresponds to baseline positional asymmetry in anterior teeth. We hypothesized that greater discrepancies between contralateral teeth would be associated with larger planned movements, and that this relationship would vary depending on both tooth type and movement type.

Materials and methods

This retrospective study included 65 patients (390 anterior teeth) who underwent clear aligner therapy at a private clinic between 2023 and 2025. The study protocol was approved by the Ethical Committee (Ethical Number: 25/678), and all patient records were anonymized to ensure confidentiality. The sample comprised 35 women (52.2%) and 32 men (47.8%), with a mean age of 30.00 years (SD = 7.92; range = 16–45 years), reflecting a balanced distribution across sex and a wide age range. Patients were included if complete digital treatment plans were available for all anterior teeth, with at least one planned movement per tooth present in both contralateral teeth to allow valid paired comparisons. Only initial, auto-generated digital treatment plans were analyzed; cases in which the orthodontist had modified the planned movements prior to analysis were excluded. Additional exclusion criteria included missing teeth, systemic conditions affecting tooth movement, or incomplete records.

Digital treatment plans generated by Invisalign® (Align Technology, Santa Clara, CA, USA) were retrieved and systematically analyzed. Planned movements—including rotation, angulation, intrusion, and extrusion—were extracted from the digital plans using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA), while baseline malpositions were recorded to quantify movement difficulty.

Measurement of baseline tooth positions

For angulation measurements, frontal views of the baseline digital models were exported from the ClinCheck® software. Guideline grids were overlaid, and the images were imported into ImageJ for quantitative analysis. To ensure consistency between ClinCheck® exports and ImageJ measurements, calibration was performed in ImageJ for each image using the guideline grid provided by the ClinCheck® software. The distance between grid lines was used to set the measurement scale in ImageJ prior to analysis, and all subsequent measurements were performed using this calibrated scale. For each of the four incisors (11, 21, 12, 22), a line was drawn through the midpoint of the incisal edge and the midpoint of the gingival contour, representing the tooth’s long axis. This definition was chosen because the incisal and gingival midpoints represent stable and easily identifiable anatomical landmarks, allowing consistent long-axis determination across different teeth and patients. The angulation angle was determined as the angle between this line and a vertical reference. For the canines, the same procedure was applied using sagittal model views.

For extrusion and intrusion measurements, the same frontal images used for angulation were analyzed. The vertical distance from the midpoint of each incisal edge to a consistent reference point on the guideline grid was measured. Because the primary objective was to calculate baseline differences between contralateral teeth rather than absolute positions, a fixed absolute reference was not required—as long as the same reference level was applied within each pair.

For rotation measurements, occlusal views of the digital models were used. A line was drawn connecting the mesial and distal points of the incisal edge to define the incisal orientation. Rotation was measured as the angle between this line and a vertical reference line aligned with the model’s sagittal plane (Fig. 1). Alignment with the sagittal plane ensured a standardized rotational reference that could be applied consistently across all digital models.

Following these procedures, baseline differences were computed between contralateral teeth (e.g., tooth 11 vs. 21 or 12 vs. 22) for each movement type. Subsequently, the planned movement magnitudes for the same teeth were obtained directly from the ClinCheck software. All baseline measurements were independently repeated by two calibrated examiners, and intra-class correlation coefficients (ICCs) were calculated to assess inter-examiner reliability. The ICC values indicated strong agreement, with an average of 0.92 across all movement types.

Statistical analysis

A priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1 (F tests → Linear multiple regression) to determine the minimum required sample size. Assuming a medium effect size (f² = 0.25), α = 0.05, and power 1–β = 0.80, the required sample size was 55. Post hoc power analysis showed that for N = 67, the achieved power was 1–β = 0.88, indicating sufficient statistical power.

To examine the effect of baseline differences on planned movements, General Linear Model (GLM) regression analyses were performed using baseline values as predictors and planned movement differences as dependent variables. Descriptive statistics and normality tests were also evaluated. All analyses were conducted in SPSS (version 25, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R (version X.X, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Statistical significance was set at p < .05.

Additional analyses including demographic variables were performed to evaluate the potential influence of age and sex on planned movements. Discussion

The present study investigated the association between baseline positional asymmetry and the magnitude and direction of planned tooth movements in CA therapy. The findings demonstrated that the impact of initial asymmetry was not uniform across teeth or movement types, indicating tooth- and movement-specific association patterns within digital treatment plans. It is important to note that these findings are based solely on digitally planned tooth movements and do not provide direct insight into clinical accuracy, treatment predictability, or the actual achieved tooth movement. Rather than relying on assumptions regarding planning behavior, this analysis provides an objective description of digitally planned movements by quantifying how planned movement values vary in relation to baseline discrepancies. By characterizing these associations, the findings may contribute to a more informed interpretation of digital setups and support clinical awareness of variation in planned movement magnitudes across different teeth and movements.

The Invisalign® system was selected specifically for its ClinCheck® digital planning platform and proprietary algorithm, which represent the most widely used and clinically validated virtual setup system for CA therapy [9]. Accordingly, the present findings should be interpreted within the context of this specific planning environment and as descriptive observations derived from finalized digital treatment plans.

Methodologically, the study employed a within-subject paired design by comparing contralateral teeth sharing similar morphological and biomechanical environments This approach minimizes patient-level confounding factors, such as arch form, anchorage distribution, and biological variability, allowing baseline asymmetry to be examined as an isolated geometric parameter [10].

Evaluating baseline discrepancy and planned movement magnitude separately for each movement type (rotation, angulation, extrusion, intrusion) enabled a movement-specific analysis of digital planning outputs [10]. This approach does not imply differential prioritization or intent, but rather allows assessment of whether planned movements scale differently across movement categories. Rotational and angular movements have been reported to be less predictable in CA therapy, potentially due to limitations in aligner–tooth engagement and force delivery [11], whereas vertical movements may demonstrate more consistent scaling patterns [3].

For the central incisors (teeth 11 and 21), baseline asymmetry was significantly associated with differences in planned extrusion and rotation. In extrusion, the positive B coefficient (0.125) indicated that greater initial vertical asymmetry was associated with larger planned extrusion values for the tooth positioned higher in the arch. This finding reflects a proportional relationship between baseline discrepancy and planned movement magnitude, rather than evidence of adaptive or corrective intent. In contrast, the negative B coefficient observed for rotation (–0.279) suggests that increasing initial rotational discrepancy was associated with lower planned rotational movements. This inverse association indicates that planned rotation magnitude does not increase linearly with baseline discrepancy, highlighting a non-uniform scaling pattern rather than a deliberate or constraint imposed by the software. From a clinical perspective, this pattern suggests that in cases of pronounced rotational asymmetry of central incisors, clinicians may benefit from critically reviewing the planned rotational movements and considering manual adjustments during the digital setup, rather than relying solely on the automatically generated values. Previous studies reporting lower predictability for rotational movements in aligner therapy [5, 12, 13] provide relevant clinical context for interpreting this pattern, but causal inferences regarding planning logic cannot be drawn from the present data.

Together, these findings indicate that planned movements for central incisors exhibit a selective and non-uniform association pattern, with extrusion showing a scalable relationship with vertical discrepancies, while rotational corrections do not increase proportionally with greater baseline asymmetry. These movement-specific patterns are consistent with previous studies reporting differential adjustments across movement types in aligner systems, which have been discussed in the context of midline symmetry and occlusal balance within known biomechanical limits [14].

For the lateral incisors (teeth 12 and 22), baseline asymmetry showed significant associations with planned intrusion, angulation, and rotation, whereas no significant association was observed for extrusion. Among these, angulation demonstrated the strongest association with baseline discrepancy, indicating that planned angular adjustments varied more markedly with initial positional differences. Rotation also showed a notable association, while extrusion did not. These findings suggest that lateral incisors exhibit higher sensitivity to baseline discrepancies in certain movement dimensions—a finding that aligns with biomechanical expectations. The greater variability observed in lateral incisors may be related to their anatomical and biomechanical characteristics, such as reduced root surface area and anchorage, which have been previously discussed in the context of aligner biomechanics [15, 16]. The absence of a significant association for extrusion suggests that vertical discrepancies in lateral incisors may not be consistently reflected in planned extrusion values, or may be addressed through alternative planning considerations [17].

For the canines (teeth 13 and 23), baseline asymmetry was significantly associated only with planned extrusion, while angulation, rotation, and intrusion showed no significant associations. This finding indicates that variation in planned movements for canines was largely confined to the vertical dimension, whereas other movement types were less influenced by baseline discrepancies. Previous reports noting the biomechanical robustness and anchorage role of canines [1] provide useful context for understanding this pattern, but again, the present results should be interpreted as associations rather than mechanistic explanations. The observed extrusion scaling may reflect the relatively consistent control of vertical movements under aligner mechanics [2, 18].

Additional exploratory analyses including demographic variables indicated limited associations between sex and certain planned movements. However, these associations were not consistently observed across different tooth types or movement categories and should therefore be interpreted with caution. Age did not demonstrate significant effects on the evaluated movement types.

The potential influence of attachment presence on planned tooth movements was considered in the present study. However, because attachments were present in nearly all cases, their independent contribution could not be statistically evaluated and should therefore be considered when interpreting the observed associations.

Overall, the results demonstrate that planned tooth movements in digital CA setups vary according to tooth type and baseline positional asymmetry, with anterior teeth exhibiting movement-specific association patterns. Central and lateral incisors showed more pronounced variability across multiple movement types, whereas canines demonstrated more limited variation. These findings do not imply adaptive strategies, compensatory corrections, or a optimization behavior, but rather describe how planned movement magnitudes are distributed within digital treatment plans relative to baseline geometry. The results highlight the importance of interpreting digitally planned movements within their anatomical and biomechanical context, without overextending conclusions beyond the descriptive scope of the data.

Limitations

Some limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. Measurements were derived from 2D projections of 3D digital models, which may introduce projection-related effects. Projection angles and model positioning were carefully standardized to ensure consistency, and the current 2D approach is appropriate for the primary aim of evaluating baseline asymmetry and differences in planned movements. Therefore, findings should be interpreted as relative indicators within the digital setup rather than absolute spatial representations. In addition, clinical variables such as interproximal reduction, crowding severity, and malocclusion classification were not incorporated into the analytical model. While this allowed baseline positional asymmetry to be examined as an isolated geometric parameter, the absence of these factors should be considered when interpreting the findings in a broader clinical context.

Conclusion

These findings underscore the importance of carefully evaluating baseline tooth positions and symmetry during digital setup. The results indicate that planned movement magnitudes are not uniform across teeth, but show tooth- and movement-specific associations with initial positional asymmetry. Lateral incisors demonstrated the highest sensitivity to baseline discrepancies, reflecting a stronger association between initial positional differences and planned movement values compared with other anterior teeth. These patterns should be interpreted as descriptive associations rather than compensatory strategies or adaptive behavior of the planning software. Clinicians should consider tooth- and movement-specific variation in planned movements when interpreting digital setups and translating them into clinical protocols.
