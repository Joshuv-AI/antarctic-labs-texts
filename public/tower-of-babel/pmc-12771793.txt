A 30% high-fiber diet mitigates oxidative stress and inflammation while modulating cecal microbiota in rats undergoing high-intensity exercise
Authors: Seyed Zanyar Athari, Bahram Amoghli Tabrizi, Mansour Khakpour, Nardin Kianfar, Elaheh Khani, Aisa Abbasi, Mir Alireza Nourazar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHigh-intensity training (HIT) can induce inflammation and oxidative stress, posing challenges for athletes, while dietary fiber may offer protective benefits. This study examined the impact of a 30% increase in dietary fiber intake on oxidative stress, inflammation, hematological parameters, and gut microbiota in male Wistar rats subjected to HIT. Thirty-two rats (250 ± 20 g) were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 8 each): Control-NF (no training, normal diet), Control-Fib (no training, high-fiber diet), HIT-NF (HIT, normal diet), and HIT-Fib (HIT, high-fiber diet). HIT was performed at 90% VO₂max, five days per week for eight weeks. The high-fiber diet was enriched with wheat bran, providing 37–52% total dietary fiber, predominantly insoluble fractions (cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin) with minor soluble components (arabinoxylans and β-glucans). Antioxidant enzyme activities, inflammatory cytokines, hematological indices, and cecal bacterial profiles were evaluated using two-way ANOVA (diet×training) with Tukey’s post-hoc test. HIT significantly reduced catalase, SOD, and GPx activities and elevated TGF-β, IFN-γ, IL-17, and hsCRP levels, whereas fiber supplementation reversed these effects, restored leukocyte balance, and improved phagocytic activity. HIT also disturbed microbial composition by reducing Lactobacillus spp. and increasing coliform counts, while fiber partially normalized these alterations. Collectively, the findings provide novel evidence that a 30% high-fiber diet intake can serve as a practical nutritional strategy to mitigate physiological stress, inflammation, and microbiota imbalance induced by high-intensity exercise.Keywords: Fiber, Exercise overtraining, Inflammation, Gastrointestinal microbiome, Oxidative stress, Rat

Introduction

Exercise leads to a robust inflammatory response by initiating a complex cascade of inflammatory events, which depend on the type, intensity, duration, and familiarity of the exercise, as well as the age and clinical condition of the participants [1, 2].The occurrence of inflammation after high-intensity training (HIT) is one of the complications of professional athletes [1, 3].

HIT leads to an increased production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in the skeletal muscles due to elevated oxygen consumption, which can be 10–15 times higher than at resting conditions [4]. Excessive ROS can overwhelm antioxidant defenses, leading to oxidative stress and triggering inflammatory cytokine release [5, 6]. Although this transient inflammation supports adaptation, persistent activation may cause tissue injury and delayed recovery [3]. In addition, HIT can disrupt the gut microbiota, which plays a key role in modulating immune and inflammatory responses [7].

In order to prevent HIT complications, there are various strategies, one of which is dietary fiber consumption. Previous studies stated that dietary fiber has several positive effects on oxidative stress and systemic inflammation. Dietary fiber contains phenolic compounds, which can help reduce the risk of diseases related to oxidative stress by enhancing the body’s antioxidant capacity via improving the bioaccessibility and bioavailability of antioxidants [8]. Additionally, the intake of dietary fiber supports the growth of beneficial gut bacteria, leading to improvements in the intestinal microbiome. This positive change in gut flora can result in the production of short-chain fatty acids, which have anti-inflammatory properties, thereby reducing systemic inflammation [9].

However, despite suggestive evidence linking dietary fiber, gut microbiota, and inflammation, it remains unclear whether dietary fiber supplementation can specifically mitigate the combined effects of HIT on oxidative stress, inflammation, and microbial balance. Therefore, the present study evaluated the hypothesis that dietary fiber supplementation (30% wheat bran) attenuates HIT-induced oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, and dysbiosis in male rats. To address this, we examined antioxidant enzyme activities, inflammatory markers, and culture-based gut microbiota following HIT with or without fiber supplementation.

Materials and methods

Animals

This experimental laboratory intervention study was reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org). For this study, a total of 32 male Wistar rats, 8–10 weeks old and weighing approximately 250 ± 20 g, purchased from the Pasteur Institute of Iran (Tehran, Iran), were used. Rats were housed four per polypropylene cage with wood shavings and nesting material as environmental enrichment. Housing conditions were standardized at 21 ± 2 °C with 50–60% relative humidity and a 12 h light/dark cycle. Water and food were provided ad libitum. The experiment started after the rats had a week to adjust to their new environment. The animals were randomly divided into four equal groups (n = 8):

High Intensity Training (HIT) Protocol

Exercise intensity was verified individually for each rat using a graded treadmill exhaustion test (MazeRouter Automatic Treadmill for Rats and Mice, MazeRouter, Iran) conducted at baseline to determine maximal aerobic capacity, following the protocol described by Dor-Haim et al. The test began at a speed of 10 m/min with no incline, and both speed and slope were increased incrementally every 3 min until the animal reached exhaustion, defined as the inability to continue running despite gentle encouragement. The maximal workload achieved during this test was used to estimate VO₂max, and subsequent training sessions were performed at approximately 90% of this value, individually adjusted by speed and incline for each rat.

Accordingly, rats trained at speeds ranging from 22 to 25 m/min with a 10–15% incline. Exercise sessions were conducted 5 days per week for 8 weeks. The treadmill included separate lanes for each animal and a rear grid delivering a mild, constant electrical stimulus (0.3–2 mA) to maintain consistent running behavior without causing harm. Exercise intensity was gradually increased during the first 10 min of each session until rats reached the target level (≈ 90% VO₂max), after which they continued running at a steady state until exhaustion criteria were met. Exhaustion criteria (refusal to run despite gentle encouragement and repeated contact with the grid) were standardized across all animals to ensure comparability [10].

Diet with 30% Fiber (Fib)

The groups fed a diet enriched with 30% wheat bran (a source of fiber added to the regular food at a rate of 30 g/100 g) received more fiber than the regular diet for 8 weeks. To homogenize the diet, commercially available edible gelatin powder (food-grade, bovine origin) was dissolved in warm distilled water (10% w/v) and thoroughly mixed with the chow and wheat bran until a consistent texture was obtained. The control group received the same regular diet without wheat bran but prepared with an equal amount of gelatin solution. Daily food intake was monitored, and no significant differences in caloric consumption were observed across groups, ensuring that fiber supplementation rather than energy imbalance accounted for physiological changes [11].

Sampling

At the end of the study, the rats were anesthetized using ketamine (90 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg). Blood samples were taken by cardiac puncture. To control for diurnal variation and acute exercise effects, all blood and cecal content samples were collected 24 h after the final training session, between 08:00 and 10:00 a.m.

Blood biochemical analysis

Blood samples were collected 24 h after the final treatment. The samples were allowed to stand at room temperature for 30 min to clot and were then centrifuged at 3000 × g for 15 min. The obtained serum was aliquoted and stored at − 60 °C until analysis.

Oxidative stress enzymes

The activity of antioxidant enzymes Glutathione peroxidase (GPx), Superoxide dismutase (SOD), and Catalase (CAT) were measured from blood samples [12].

GPx activity was measured using the RANSEL kit, which employs cumene hydroperoxide as a substrate. The enzymatic reaction catalyzes the oxidation of glutathione, resulting in the conversion of oxidized glutathione to its reduced form, accompanied by the oxidation of NADPH to NADP+. This reaction was monitored spectrophotometrically at 340 nm and 37 °C, with results expressed in U/ml.

SOD activity was quantified using a commercial kit from ZellBio GmbH, following the manufacturer’s protocol. The assay measures the percentage inhibition of formazan dye production, which is generated from the tetrazolium-superoxide reaction. SOD activity was determined spectrophotometrically at 505 nm at 37 °C, with results reported as U/ml.

Catalase activity was evaluated according to the method of Beers and Sizer, which involves measuring the decrease in absorbance at 240 nm due to the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). The reaction mixture consisted of serum and phosphate buffer, with absorbance changes recorded using a UV spectrophotometer. Results were expressed as k/ml.

Inflammatory enzymes

ELISA kits from R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, were employed to determine the concentrations of murine cytokines, specifically high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (HSCRP) (Cat number: DY1744), Interleukin-17 (IL-17) (Cat number: DY4437), Transforming Growth Factor-beta (TGF-β) (Cat number: MB100B), and Interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) (Cat number: DY585) in the serum of the studied animals. The assays were conducted in strict accordance with the manufacturers’ protocols to ensure the accuracy and reliability of the results obtained.

Briefly, following sample preparation, the plates were coated with specific antibodies, and the samples were added to allow for binding. After incubation and washing steps to remove unbound substances, enzyme-linked secondary antibodies were introduced, followed by the addition of a substrate that produces a measurable signal. Then, the optical density was measured using a microplate reader at the specified wavelengths for each cytokine, and concentrations were determined by comparing the absorbance values to a standard curve generated from known cytokine concentrations [13].

Blood cells count

Approximately 1–2 mL of blood was drawn into EDTA-coated tubes to prevent coagulation. Blood samples were diluted 1:20 in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to prepare for analysis.

Neutrophil phagocytic factor

To assess the neutrophil phagocytic factor, Candida yeast was used. Whole blood, along with an anticoagulant, was added to the Candida sample and incubated at 37 ℃ for 15 min. A blood smear was then prepared, and the number of neutrophils containing Candida in their cytoplasm was counted.

Microbiota analysis

Samples also taken from the cecum of the rats were used for total bacterial counts, brain heart infusion (BHI) medium was used; for Lactobacillus counts, MRS agar was utilized; for total coliform counts, MacConkey agar was employed; and for Coliform counts, EMB agar was used. After culturing, incubation, and counting, the number of colony-forming units (CFU) was expressed logarithmically per gram of feces. Cecal contents were aseptically collected into sterile tubes and immediately processed. Serial tenfold dilutions were prepared and plated in duplicate on selective media: BHI for total bacteria, MRS agar for Lactobacillus spp., MacConkey agar for coliforms, and EMB agar for confirmation of Escherichia coli. Plates were incubated under appropriate aerobic or anaerobic conditions for 24–48 h, and colonies were expressed as log CFU/g feces. Quality control measures included duplicate plating for each sample and comparison with reference ATCC strains to ensure culture validity [14].

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA) and are expressed as mean ± 95% confidence intervals (CI). Two-way ANOVA was applied to evaluate the main effects of diet (NF vs. Fib) and exercise (Control vs. HIT), as well as their interaction. When significant effects were detected, Tukey’s post hoc test was used to correct for multiple comparisons. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Discussion

High-intensity training (HIT) induces several adverse effects, including heightened oxidative stress and systemic inflammation throughout the body. The current study found that HIT significantly elevated oxidative stress markers, as reflected by reduced catalase, superoxide dismutase (SOD), and glutathione peroxidase (GPx) activities compared to the non-fiber control group (Control-NF), and altered microbiota distribution, resulting in an increase in pathogenic coliform bacteria and a reduction in lactobacillus populations in the gut. These alterations induced systemic inflammation and elevated inflammatory indicators like TGF-β, IFN-γ, IL-17, and HSCRP in the studied rats. To ameliorate these complications, it was recommended to use 30% more dietary fiber, which was found to enhance oxidative stress markers, partially restore microbial balance, and ultimately diminish systemic inflammation.

Our findings are in agreement with recent studies highlighting the beneficial role of dietary fiber supplementation in exercise physiology. Likewise, Mancin et al. (2025) reviewed the role of fiber in sports nutrition, emphasizing its effects on gut barrier integrity, inflammation, and overall health in athletes [15]. Clauss et al. (2021) reviewed the interplay between exercise and gut microbiota, underlining that dietary modulation is a key factor in preventing dysbiosis during physiological stress [16].

During HIT, increased oxygen demand enhances mitochondrial respiration, leading to elevated ROS generation and oxidative stress, as reflected by reduced antioxidant enzyme activities (Catalase, GPx, SOD). This aligns with the hormetic model, in which excessive ROS surpass adaptive thresholds and cause cellular damage [17, 18]. Another significant consequence following HIT is alteration in the gut microbiota [19]. Although the present study focused on a treadmill-based high-intensity paradigm to induce systemic stress, contemporary perspectives in exercise science have shifted toward more integrative and joint-specific approaches. For example, Dhahbi et al. (2025) recently proposed a joint-by-joint training approach for injury prevention, contrasting with traditional focused strengthening. Such evolving paradigms emphasize the importance of tailoring exercise interventions to functional outcomes, which may complement nutritional strategies such as fiber supplementation [20]. The gut microbiota is a dynamic ecosystem shaped by food consumption and physical activity [21]. A diverse microbiome correlates with several health advantages, such as increased metabolic efficiency and enhanced immunological responses [22]. During vigorous physical exertion, blood circulation is diverted from the gastrointestinal (GI) tract to priorities perfusion to the muscles. This ischemia can result in gastrointestinal pain and disruptions, adversely affecting gut motility and the general health of the gut flora. Decreased blood circulation may foster conditions favorable for the proliferation of pathogenic bacteria while suppressing good varieties [23]. The current study demonstrated that HIT resulted in a rise in coliform bacteria and a decrease in lactobacillus bacteria, indicating bacterial proliferation favoring pathogens. Furthermore, it has been asserted that HIT can elicit an inflammatory response, marked by the secretion of cytokines and other inflammatory mediators. This inflammation can further undermine gut integrity and encourage dysbiosis, facilitating the proliferation of harmful bacteria like coliforms while suppressing helpful bacteria such as Lactobacillus [1].

In line with the present study, prior research indicates that both oxidative stress and alterations in gut flora might induce systemic inflammation, particularly during intense physical exertion [24]. Consistent with ROS-mediated oxidative stress as previously described, it can induce an inflammatory response, marked by the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines including IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β [25]. These cytokines may then reach the systemic circulation, resulting in a condition of systemic inflammation that lasts beyond the conclusion of the exercise session. HIT can modify the gut microbiome’s composition, promoting the proliferation of harmful bacteria like coliforms and diminishing good bacteria such as Lactobacillus. Dysbiosis can result in heightened intestinal permeability, permitting bacterial products and inflammatory mediators to infiltrate the circulation [26, 27]. The characteristic termed “leaky gut” facilitates the onset of systemic inflammation [28]. Furthermore, the modified gut microbiota might affect the immune system, resulting in a disparity between pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory responses. This imbalance may sustain the inflammatory state and elevate the risk of chronic inflammatory diseases [29]. TGF-β may induce the differentiation of Th17 cells, resulting in heightened IL-17 production. This can exacerbate inflammation and elevate levels of hsCRP, a liver acute-phase protein and a marker of systemic inflammation [30]. IFN-γ can regulate the effects of TGF-β and IL-17 by enhancing macrophage activation and affecting the equilibrium between pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory responses [31]. Increased IFN-γ levels may amplify the inflammatory response, potentially resulting in elevated hsCRP levels [32]. The interaction among these cytokines might lead to a prolonged inflammatory condition. The presence of IL-17 can sustain inflammation by recruiting immune cells to areas of tissue injury, whereas TGF-β may induce fibrosis and further tissue remodeling [33, 34]. The results of cell counting were also in line with the confirmation of enzyme results, indicating the occurrence of inflammation following HIT.

Increasing dietary fiber consumption has demonstrated enhancement of antioxidant defense systems inside the body, especially for systemic inflammation and oxidative stress [35, 36]. Dietary fibers are frequently linked to polyphenol chemicals, which possess significant antioxidant capabilities. Upon consumption of dietary fiber, these polyphenols are liberated in the gastrointestinal tract, where they might neutralize free radicals and mitigate oxidative stress [37]. High-fiber diets facilitate the proliferation of advantageous gut microbiota, resulting in the synthesis of SCFAs [38]. SCFAs have anti-inflammatory characteristics and can improve the body’s overall antioxidant level [39]. The fermentation of dietary fiber produces compounds that enhance antioxidant defenses [40]. Specific varieties of dietary fiber, including those from peas, sweet potatoes, and wheat bran, have demonstrated the ability to enhance the production of antioxidant enzymes in several organs. This is facilitated by the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) pathway, which governs the production of protective antioxidant proteins [41]. Dietary fiber can influence the immune response by elevating anti-inflammatory cytokines and reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines. The alteration in the cytokine profile results in decreased systemic inflammation, frequently linked to oxidative stress [42]. In an animal study, Williams et al. (2022) showed that oligofructose-enriched inulin increased SCFA levels and endurance capacity in mice, providing mechanistic support for our rodent findings [43]. The findings of the current investigation corroborate previous data, indicating that a 30% increase in dietary fiber may also elevate immunological levels in the examined rats.

The study’s findings indicate that higher dietary fiber causes alterations in microbial balance, which is crucial for sustaining gut health and avoiding dysbiosis. Prior research indicates that dietary fiber can enhance advantageous microbial populations, such as Lactobacillus, and an increase in dietary fiber can modify the gut microbiota composition, fostering a favorable microbial community [44].

Importantly, several recent studies in exercise physiology and nutrition research have confirmed that fiber supplementation can attenuate exercise-induced oxidative stress and inflammation. For instance, Qi et al. (2023) showed that higher dietary fiber intake is associated with improved immune function and reduced inflammatory biomarkers in humans [45]. Similarly, Tan et al. (2023) reviewed how dietary fiber-derived SCFAs help regulate mucosal homeostasis and immune responses. These findings strengthen our observation that wheat bran supplementation helped stabilize gut bacterial balance during HIT [46]. Moreover, a controlled trial in humans by Choe et al. (2025) indicated that fiber intake modulates inflammatory responses under metabolic or stress challenges, suggesting translational potential for our animal findings [47]. These protective effects of wheat bran may support post-exercise recovery by reducing oxidative stress and inflammation. However, recovery is multifactorial, influenced not only by nutritional strategies but also by sleep, circadian alignment, and environmental factors. For instance, Souissi et al. (2025) showed that evening blue light exposure timing significantly affects sleep quality, motor, and cognitive performance in young athletes, underscoring the complicated interaction between diet, exercise, and external factors in optimizing recovery [48].

The present study provides novel insights into how dietary fiber supplementation counteracts oxidative stress, inflammation, and microbial imbalance under HIT. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. The work was conducted exclusively in male rats, which may limit generalizability across sexes and species. Only a single fiber concentration (30% wheat bran) was tested, precluding dose–response evaluation. The relatively modest sample size (n = 6–8/group), together with occasional variability in assay sample numbers due to technical issues, may reduce statistical power, although post hoc analyses indicated adequacy for detecting major effects. Mechanistic endpoints such as short-chain fatty acid concentrations, antioxidant-related gene expression, and long-term physiological outcomes were not assessed. In addition, the microbiota was evaluated using culture-based enumeration of Lactobacillus spp. and coliforms, which provides information on key bacterial groups but not the overall microbial diversity or functional capacity. Furthermore, the study duration was relatively short, preventing evaluation of sustained metabolic or performance adaptations to dietary fiber supplementation during chronic HIT. Environmental factors such as housing conditions, diet composition beyond fiber content, and potential stress from training protocols may also have influenced physiological responses.

Future studies should therefore include larger cohorts with both sexes, explore different fiber types and doses, and incorporate high-throughput approaches such as 16 S rRNA sequencing, metagenomics, and metabolomics to capture comprehensive microbial and metabolic profiles. Integration of multi-omics techniques with longitudinal exercise models will help clarify the mechanistic pathways, particularly the microbiota–immune–oxidative stress axis through which dietary fiber modulates host responses to strenuous exercise. Ultimately, translating these findings into well-designed human trials will be essential to establish evidence-based dietary strategies for athletes and physically active populations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study highlights the pivotal role of dietary fiber in mitigating exercise-induced oxidative and inflammatory stress. Beyond its physiological benefits, fiber intake appears to preserve immune homeostasis and gut microbial stability under strenuous training conditions. These findings suggest that nutritional modulation particularly through adequate dietary fiber, could serve as a practical, non-pharmacological strategy to enhance recovery and protect systemic health in athletes exposed to high-intensity workloads.
