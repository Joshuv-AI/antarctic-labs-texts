End-of-life healthcare utilization and costs according to cause of death: a retrospective study
Authors: Delphine Bosson-Rieutort, Jean-Frédéric Boulianne, Juliette Duc, Sébastien Barbat-Artigas, Erin C. Strumpf
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ContextAging individuals are more likely to experience multiple health issues, resulting in increased and complex healthcare needs, particularly at the end-of-life (EoL). While several studies have shown that healthcare costs rose substantially during the final year and months of life, there is limited research on the EoL utilization and cost in Quebec, specifically using population-based data that span multiple healthcare settings and different causes of death.ObjectiveTo examine healthcare and social care utilization and cost during the last year of life of people who died in Quebec at the age of 66 and over, according to their cause of death.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective quantitative longitudinal study to analyze individuals’ healthcare utilization and cost during their last year of life between 2014 and 2018. Individuals were retrieved from the death registry and classified according to their cause of death as: cancer, organ failure, frailty (physical or cognitive) and other or unknown. Healthcare and social care utilization included: medical visits, emergency department visits, hospitalizations, homecare and long-term care (LTC). Costs were attributed to services either directly from administrative databases or estimated from public financial reports. We used generalized linear models (GLMs) to assess variations in service utilization and costs: Poisson or negative binomial models were employed for count outcomes based on data dispersion, and Gamma log link for costs.ResultsThe cohort included 21,255 individuals (52.6% women) with ~ 40% of deaths occurring among individuals aged 80–89 years. Nearly 50% of the cohort died from an organ failure, 30% of cancer and 15% of frailty. Cancer and organ failure were associated with high acute care use and costs, while frailty led to greater use of LTC and social care. Overall, the EoL the individual average cost was 34,467$ ($25,000-$53,000), depending on cause of death and type of services used. Notably, social services cost exceeds healthcare cost, especially for people who died from frailty, highlighting the financial burden of LTC accommodation.ConclusionThis study highlights distinct EoL healthcare and social services utilization and related costs among older adults, depending on the cause of death, age, and sex. These findings underscore the need for tailored care strategies that reflect different EoL trajectories.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06906-5.

Background

The global population is aging rapidly. By 2050, individuals aged 65 years and older are projected to represent 26.7% of the population in OECD countries, and between 21.4 and 23.4% in Canada by 2030 [1, 2]. This demographic shift poses significant challenges for healthcare systems, as older adults are more likely to experience multiple health conditions and functional decline, resulting in increased and often complex healthcare needs, particularly in the period leading up to death. In 2019, over half of individuals aged 65 and over across 36 OECD countries reported having fair or poor health [1]. Age-related illnesses like malignant tumours, heart disease, respiratory conditions, and dementia accounted for over 40% of all deaths in these countries [1]. In Canada, these conditions were among the top 10 causes of death in 2019, contributing to 70.6% of all mortalities [3]. Despite the impact of COVID-19, which became the fourth leading cause of death in 2021, cancer and heart disease remained the primary causes, with 35.7% and 57.6% of deaths respectively, occurring in individuals aged 80 and over [4]. In addition, the burden of chronic diseases increases with age as the prevalence of multiple chronic rises from 30% among individuals aged 40–49 to 52% among those aged 60–64 [5]. In Quebec, 45% of people aged 65 and older live with more than two chronic conditions, such as Alzheimer’s disease, metastatic cancer or hypertension [6].

As chronic conditions and life expectancy increase, healthcare needs intensify, particularly during the end-of-life (EoL), a period ranging from the last month of life to several years before death depending on the illness trajectory [7], as most individuals require substantial acute care during this period [8–12]. For instance, a study from Switzerland reported that 71% of individuals visited two to three healthcare facilities during the EoL period [13]. Similar trends has been observed in Canada, where healthcare use was 2.5 times higher among individuals aged 85 and older compared to those aged 65–69 in Ontario [8, 9]. The volume and type of healthcare services used at the EoL also vary significantly depending on clinical and contextual factors. A recent study conducted in Quebec, using multidimensional state sequence analysis to differentiate healthcare use patterns, revealed distinct care trajectories between individuals with dementia and those with metastatic tumours [14]. The latter received more palliative home care and palliative visits (50% to 77%) compared to those with dementia (13% to 19%). Conversely, individuals with dementia had higher emergency department (ED) visits rates, (24% to 30%) than those with metastatic tumour (16%) [14].

This variability is also reflected in healthcare expenditures. Comorbidities are a keydriver of elevated EoL costs [15, 16], and as the population continues to age, the financial burden is expected to increase. This concern has attracted growing attention from researchers and policymakers, particularly given the long-term implications for system sustainability [15, 17]. According to the Canadian Health Council, population aging was the fourth largest contributor to the rise in healthcare expenditures between 1998 and 2009, during which total spending nearly doubled, from $84 billion to $160 billion [18]. Projections by Clavet and colleagues suggest that aging will lead to additional public healthcare expenditures, of $24.7 billion by 2040 and $79.7 billion by 2060 [19]. While the proportion of total healthcare expenditures allocated to individuals aged 65 and older remained relatively stable between 2007 and 2017 (44.7% and 44.2%, respectively), per capita healthcare expenditures rose markedly with age. Specifically, average annual healthcare costs increased from $6,656 for those aged 65–69, to $8,467 for those aged 70–74, $11,324 for those aged 75–79, and $20,793 for individuals aged 80 and over [20]. A retrospective cohort study in Ontario estimated average healthcare costs at $53,661 per person in the last year of life, with a notable surge during the final 120 days preceding death [15].

In Canada, where healthcare is publicly funded and administered by provincial and territorial governments, hospitals play a central role the provision of EoL care. Between 50 and 70% of deaths occur in hospital settings [11, 21, 22], with fewer than half occurring in acute care settings [22]. Quebec recorded the highest hospital death rate among all Canadian provinces and territories in 2010 [21], and more recent data showed little improvement, with 70% of individuals in the cohort still dying either in hospital or during an ED visit [14]. Hospital death often represents the culmination of a complex EoL care trajectory, reflecting both patients’ clinical complexity and challenges in maintaining care outside hospital settings. This reliance on hospitals often contrasts with patients’ preferences. Studies have shown that over 90% of particularly seriously ill or older adults nearing the EoL in Canada lack access to palliative and end-of-life care despite most expressing a preference to receive such care at home [11, 23], and fewer than one in five received palliative care at home [24]. This gap contributes not only to misalignment with patients’ values but also avoidable acute care use and higher system costs as evidence from cohort studies has shown that healthcare expenditures rise substantially during the final months and year of life [24, 25], potentially due to hospitalisation costs.

While the ‘Triple Aim’ framework, introduced by Berwick et al. in 2008 (Berwick et al., 2008), has since expanded to include healthcare workers well-being [26] and health equity [27], reducing per capita healthcare costs remains a central priority guiding healthcare organizations’ efforts to provide timely, appropriate and efficient care. However, increasing complexity of care among older adults with multiple chronic conditions has made resource allocation more challenging. As the healthcare system faces rising demand and financial pressure, understanding how care and costs vary is essential to inform planning and ensure sustainability.

Despite these growing concerns, there is limited research on the cost of EoL care in Quebec, particularly studies using population-based data that span multiple healthcare settings, including ED, hospitals, long-term care facilities, and local community service centre. This study aims to examine healthcare and social care utilization and related cost during the last year of life of people who died in Quebec between 2014 and 2018, according to their cause of death, using provincial administrative health databases.

Methods

A retrospective quantitative longitudinal research study design was used to analyze individuals’ healthcare utilization and cost during their last year of life.

Study population and period

The study cohort included a 10% representative sample of individuals covered by Quebec’s public health insurance who died between April 1, 2014, and March 31, 2018, at age 66 or older. The age threshold ensured optimal capture of healthcare and social service use during the last year of life, leveraging Quebec’s universal drug coverage for those aged 65 and over. This 10% sample was provided by the Institut national d’excellence en santé et en services sociaux (INESSS) and was constructed to be representative of the Quebec decedent population with respect to key demographic and contextual variables, including age, sex, region of residence, and fiscal year of death. Due to data access and privacy regulations governing administrative data use, this representative subset was the only data made available for analysis. Full details of the study design are available in a previously published article [28].

Individuals’ characteristics data

Administrative and administrative health data were provided by the Régie de l’assurance maladie du Québec (RAMQ) and the Ministry of health (MSSS).

Personal socio-demographic information and causes of death were obtained from the Insured Persons Registration File (FIPA) and death registry (RED/D), which was fed mainly by the death certificate (SP-3). Date of death was obtained from the death register, and the date index was set as 365 days prior this date to retrieve all healthcare utilization one-year prior death. Age at death was categorized as follows: 66–69 years, 70–79 years, 80–89 years and 90 years and over. Causes of death were obtained using the single ICD-10 code representing the primary cause of death as certified and reviewed by the coroner within the death registry. This code was used as the basis for classification into five clinical categories from the Institut national de santé publique du Québec (INSPQ): cancer, organ failure, frailty (physical or cognitive), other, and unknown [29]. The INSPQ classification was based on several works [30–32] and defined each group as: a gradual evolution and a relatively clear terminal phase for cancer; a gradual decline, punctuated by episodes of acute deterioration and periods of recovery, and sometimes a sudden or unexpected death for Organ failure; and a gradual and prolonged decline, typical of frail elderly people or people with dementia for frailty(physical or cognitive). Classification was performed using a correspondence table developed that maps ICD-10 codes to each INSPQ category [29]. This method ensures systematic and standardized categorization aligned with our data provider and provincial data standards, facilitating comparability with other studies using administrative data. The full list of ICD-10 codes assigned to each category is provided in the supplement (Supplemental 1). An individual could only be in one category.

Healthcare services data

Healthcare utilization data were obtained from six anonymized administrative databases. Information on hospitalizations was sourced from the hospitalization registry (MED-ECHO) while date on ED visits came from the emergency department common databank (BDCU). Physician services were identified using the fee-for-service physician claims database (SMOD) with visits defined as all medical acts on the same day by the same physician, at the same institution, under the same claim. Outpatient visits were distinguished from those occurring during ED or hospital stays using facility identifiers and stay dates cross-referenced with BDCU and MED-ECHO. Services provided by healthcare professionals not remunerated on a fee-for-service basis, such as visiting hours, some homecare services and most of palliative care, were not available within the data. Interventions provided by local community service centers, hereafter called CLSCs, were obtained from the CLSC integration file (i-CLSC). CLSC are publicly funded organizations in Quebec that provide a broad range of community health and social services, including home care, palliative care and social support services. Long-term care (LTC) refers to residential long-term care facilities in Quebec, called Centres d’hébergement et de soins de longue durée (CHSLD), which provide 24-hour nursing and personal care for individuals with significant loss of autonomy. Accommodation data were retrieved from the Insured Persons Registration File (FIPA) where insurance plan codes 97 (Person accommodated) and 50 (Person eligible for the purchase program) were used to define LTC periods. For individuals whose accommodation began prior the index date (defined as one year before individuals’ death), the start date was set to the index date with a maximum of 365 days. All data were segmented into quarters, Q4 (9–12 months prior death) to Q1 (last 3 months of life).

Cost data

Healthcare costs were attributed to all services used during the last year of life, either directly from administrative databases (e.g., medical visits) or estimated from public financial reports for the other services. Cost attribution strategy was tailored to each data source.

Medical consultation costs were calculated by summing physician fees services within each billing request. Hospitalization costs were calculated using the relative intensity level of resources (NIRRU, niveau d’intensité relative des ressources utilisées), converted to Canadian dollars using the annual MSSS conversion factors for each fiscal year (supplemental 2). ED visit costs were extracted from AS-471 public financial reports (p.650, l.29, “average cost of the unit”), aggregated at the regional level by fiscal year to adjust for healthcare system restructuration during the study period. Long-term care costs were estimated using daily rates published in AS-478 financial reports and responses to the opposition parties available on the MSSS website. Daily rates were multiplied by the number of accommodation days for each participant (supplemental 3). Finally, estimating local community service costs required a more complex approach due to limited disaggregated data and structural reforms post-2015 that disrupted prior mission-based reporting (e.g. hospital center, LTC, CLSC). Because the new stratification often spanned multiple programs, a direct cost assignment was frequently not feasible. Cost estimation relied on activity centers, which provided varying levels of cost detail: average per-intervention, annual per-patient, or none. When possible, average intervention costs were used directly. When only annual per-patient costs were available, they were evenly distributed across the individual’s number of interventions for that year. Multiple interventions per day were consolidated into single daily entries when required. To capture care patterns over time, interventions were aggregated quarterly, and annual costs were proportionally distributed across quarters based on intervention counts. A full attribution example is provided in supplemental 4. Final costs reflect only publicly funded formal care. Informal (unpaid) caregiving, such as support provided by family members, was not capture in the administrative data and therefore could not be considered in this study.

Analyses

Following the assignment of service costs to each individual in the cohort, total and stratified costs were calculated at the individual-level. These included total annual costs, health-related costs, social care costs, and costs by service type. All values were adjusted for inflation using the 2018 Consumer Price Index as the reference year [33] (supplemental 5).

Descriptive analyses were conducted to characterize sociodemographic profiles, cause of death, and healthcare service utilization. These analyses reported frequencies, means and proportions of services use and associated costs. Group comparisons were performed using parametric tests (t-tests and ANOVA) or non-parametric alternatives (Wilcoxon and Kruskal-Wallis tests). When significant differences were observed, Tukey’s Honest Significant Difference test was applied for post hoc comparisons. To assess variations in service utilization on annual costs, generalized linear models (GLMs) were used. For count outcomes, Poisson or negative binomial models were employed based on the level of dispersion in the data. Model selection was guided by data availability and operational considerations, supported by a sequential stepwise modeling approach (both forward and backward) to identify significant covariates, guided by the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) to ensure model parsimony.

Cost analyses were conducted using a Gamma GLM with a log link function, selected for its ability to accommodate skewed cost distributions and zero-cost values. This approach avoided the transformation biases often associated with ordinary least squares regression [34, 35]. Models were adjusted for sex and age. Three primary cost categories were examined: total, health-related and social. Total costs encompassed all services. Health-related costs included ED visits, hospitalizations, inpatient and outpatient consultations, and “health” interventions provided by CLSC. Social care costs referred to social interventions provided by CLSC and LTC accommodation.

Results

Characteristics of the study population and healthcare utilization

Cohort characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Among the 21,255 individuals in the cohort, 11,184 (52.6%) were women and costs were missing for only 17 individuals. Average age at death was significantly higher for women than men (84.9 vs. 81.2 years, p < 0.001). Annual death counts remained stable throughout the study period. Approximately 40% of deaths occurred among individuals aged 80–89 years. Among those who died between ages 66–69 and 70–79, nearly 60% were men, while 70% of deaths at 90 years or older occurred in women. Almost half of all deaths were attributed to organ failure, with women accounting for 53% of them. The most pronounced sex difference was observed in deaths due to frailty (physical or cognitive), of which nearly 63% were women. This reflects the age distribution, with over 80% of such deaths occurring among individuals aged 80 years and older, who are predominantly women. For analytical purposes, individuals classified as “Other” and “Unknown” were grouped together to ease interpretation. Lastly, using ED and hospitalization data, we estimated that 9.2% of individuals died in ED (91.4% within 24 h of admission) and 11.8% died during a hospital stay (12.1% within 24 h of admission). Among hospital deaths, 22.6% had been admitted to a palliative care unit.

Healthcare utilization and costs

Of these entries, 66.7% were from the fee-for-service database, 16.1% from the i-CLSC database, 9.4% were from the ED database, 6% from the hospitalization one and finally, 1.8% were related to LTC accommodation. Cancer

Among the 21,255 individuals in the cohort, 6,302 died from a cancer (46.4% women). Over 75% were aged between 70 and 89 at the time of death. Overall, 92% visited the ED at least once (Table 2), with men more likely than women to do so (Table 3A). The estimated number of ED visits in the last year of life decreased with age, from 3.3 to 2.7 visits (Table 3A). Hospitalizations were also common, with 87.9% experiencing at least one. The adjusted model mirrored ED trends, men had more hospitalizations, and frequency decreasing with age at death (2.4 to 1.8) (Table 3B). Most of the individuals had a medical consultation (98.2%). Model estimates indicated women were more likely to consult general practitioners (GPs), while men were predicted to consult specialists more frequently (Table 3C and D). GP estimated consultations increased with age (6.9 to 10.2), while it decreased for specialists, men and women combined (21.9/25.2 to 8). Only 9.4% of individuals were housed in LTC, with no significant sex differences. However, women were predicted to stay longer than men (197.9 vs. 185.6 days) (Table 3E). All individuals benefited from local center interventions, with 96.8% benefiting from a “health” intervention and 58.6% from a “social” intervention. Adjusted models showed women had slightly more interventions than men (Table 3F). The number of CLSC interventions increased with age, with social interventions increasing more than health-related one for women (44-53.4 to 22.3–27) and men (41.3–50.1 to 20.9–25.4). As death approached, average number of health interventions rose, especially in the last quarter of life (Q1: 8.3 to Q4: 13.6), while social interventions decreased (Q1: 21.8 to Q4: 15).

Organ failure

Among the 10,422 individuals who died of organ failure, 53.1% were women and 65% were aged 70–89 at death. At least one ED visit was recorded for 85.5% and 73% had been hospitalized at least once. As with cancer, model estimates suggested higher utilization of ED and hospital services among men. In addition, both ED visits and hospital stays decreased with age in the last year of life, from 3.3 to 2.7 ED visits (Table 3A) and from 2.6 to 1.9 hospital stays. (Table 3B). A total of 94.7% of individuals sought medical advice. As previously observed, women were more likely to consult GPs whereas men more often consulted specialists. Estimated number of GP visits increased with age (6.6 to 9.8), while it decreased for specialists (16.5–19 to 6.4–7.4), both in men and women (Table 3C and D). LTC housing was noted for 25.3% of individuals, with no significant difference between men and women. However, women were predicted to be housed longer than men (259.9 vs. 243.7 days) (Table 3E). All individuals who died from an organ failure benefited from a local center, with 92.5% benefiting from health interventions and 63.8% of social interventions. The model showed slightly more interventions for women (Table 3F). With increasing age, number of CLSC interventions increased, but the increase in social interventions was greater than health-related one, for both women (53.6–65.1 to 27.13–32.9) and men (50.3–61.1 to 25.5–30.9). In the last quarter of life, health interventions increased (Q1: 8.5 to Q4: 11.2), while the average number of social interventions decreased modestly (Q1: 25.5 to Q4: 24.5).

Finally, the average individual cost of using services during the last year of life for those who died of organ failure was $33,179 ($16,832 for health costs and $29,173 for social care costs). GLMs marginal effects estimates showed total individual costs at $31,050, with healthcare and social care costs at $21,185 and $16,908 respectively (Table 4).

Frailty, physical or cognitive

Only 15% of the cohort died from frailty (physical or cognitive), with women comprising 62.8% of this group. At death, 61.3% were between 70 and 89 years old. Compared to other causes of death, this group had notably lower healthcare utilization: only 52.6% had at least one ED visit and 41.5% had stayed at least once in hospital. Model estimates indicated higher ED usage among men, with the number of visits decreasing with age in both sexes, from 2.9 to 2.4 for men and from 2.8 to 2.3 for women (Table 3A). For hospital stays, models showed men aged 66–69 had slightly higher rates than women (2.1 versus 2), but no sex difference was observed for those over 70 at the time of death (Table 3B). Medical consultations were recorded for 87.5% of individuals. As with other groups, women were more likely to consult a GP, while men were more likely to consult specialists (9.7 vs. 8.7) (Table 3C and D). GPs visits increased with age, while specialist visits decreased, regardless of biological sex (9.3 to 3.6 and 10.7 to 4.2). Individuals suffering from frailty were most often accommodated in LTC facilities, with 70.8% of the cohort having been accommodated at least once (Table 3E). Consistent with other groups, women were housed for longer duration, based on model estimates. All individuals benefited from local center interventions, with 83.3% benefiting from a health intervention and 83.4% from a social intervention. Again, the fitted model showed that women received slightly more interventions than men (Table 3F). Similarly, the number of interventions increased with age, with a steeper increase in social interventions than health interventions, for both women (55.7–67.7 and 28.2–34.2 respectively) and men (52.3–63.5 and 26.5–32.1 respectively). In the final months of life, health-related interventions increased (Q1: 8.6 to Q4: 11.6), while social interventions showed a slight decline (Q1: 28.6 to Q4: 26.7).

Service use in the last year of life for individuals who died from frailty (physical or cognitive) occurred an average cost of $57,981, with $8,471 for health costs and $56,435 for social care costs. Marginalized estimates showed adjusted cost at $53,310, with $11,166 for health and $32,260 for social services (Table 4). In-depth services analyses showed that the social care cost was mostly driven by the LTC accommodation rather than the CLSC social interventions.

Discussion

This study offers a comprehensive overview of healthcare and social service use in the last year of life among older adults in Quebec, distinguishing patterns by cause of death, sex and age. Based on a retrospective cohort of 21,255 individuals who died between 2014 and 2018, the results highlighted significant disparities in service use and associated cost among individuals who died from cancer, organ failure, or frailty (physical or cognitive). It is worth noting that the proportion of deaths occurring in hospital settings in our data (approximately 20%) is lower than commonly reported rates of 50–70%. This discrepancy is likely due to our reliance on death registry data, which is reviewed by coroners to resolve inconsistencies across sources, such as cases where a patient’s place of death is recorded differently in emergency and hospital records. Compared to hospital administrative data alone, this approach provides a more accurate classification of actual place of death in Quebec.

Our findings align with the cause of death being a main determinant of the EoL service utilization and cost. Individuals who died from a cancer or an organ failure showed higher use of acute care services than the rest of the cohort. In both groups, over 85% visited the ED at least once and over 70% were hospitalized. These individuals also showed higher average healthcare costs, reflecting the intensity of medical interventions typically associated with these conditions. In contrast, individuals who died from frailty (physical or cognitive), which include dementia and other age-related declines, had much lower rates of acute care use (only 52.6% visited the ED and 41.5% were hospitalized), but were far more likely to reside in LTC facilities (70.8% vs. 9.4% and 25.3% for the other groups) and to receive social interventions from CLSCs. This utilization aligns with clinical realities, where cancer and organ failure often involve acute deterioration, while frailty is marked by progressive functional decline [14, 30]. Consequently, individuals with frailty incurred the highest total and social care costs, averaging over $57,000, driven largely by prolonged LTC stays.

Age and sex were also key modifiers of healthcare and social services utilization and support the observed patterns. Women, who have a longer life expectancy than men (84.8 vs. 81.0 in 2018 in Quebec) [36], and have a higher prevalence of age-related conditions such as dementia (21.2% vs. 16.15%) were overly represented among those dying of frailty. This group was older on average and had longer durations of LTC housing and social services use. Men, on the other hand, were more likely to access hospital-based services and consult medical specialists, which was observed across all causes of death. The interaction between age, sex, and cause of death reinforces that healthcare planning must account not only for clinical diagnosis but also for demographic and social realities. For example, men’s higher reliance on specialist and emergency care, and women’s longer stays in LTC, suggest differing needs that may require targeted policy responses, such as increased investment in community-based specialist support or more tailored LTC programs for older women with cognitive decline.

Despite differences by cause of death, several common utilization patterns emerged. Across all groups, GP visits increased with age, while specialist consultations decreased. Health service use intensified in the final quarter of life, particularly for emergency and hospital services, in line with previous findings [11, 12, 25]. These findings were also consistent with a recent study reporting that 40.8% and 52.6% of the individuals who died from cancer or organ failure, respectively, visited the ED within the last 14 days of life [37]. This late-life surge in clinical intervention was not mirrored in social services as they tended to decrease as death approached. CLSC interventions followed a similar trend across groups as the number of interventions increased with age, especially for social services. Notably, deaths associated with frailty were linked to both higher volume and proportion of social interventions. This disparity emphasizes that the EoL care needs extend beyond clinical services, especially populations facing progressive decline rather than acute medical needs.

Individual cost patterns reflected these utilization differences. Overall, the average individual cost in the last year of life ranged from $25,000 to over $53,000, depending on the cause of death and the type of services used. Notably, social services cost exceeds healthcare cost in many cases, especially for people who died from frailty, highlighting the financial burden of LTC accommodation. These costs were consistent with those reported in Ontario, ranging from $19,568 (first quartile) and $66,875 (3rd quartile), with an average cost at $53,661 [15]. The observed increase in costs during the final months of life also aligns with findings from previous studies [15, 25]. Interestingly, our estimated annual costs were only slightly higher than those reported for the last 180 days of life in a multinational study comparing seven countries (Belgium, Canada, England, Germany, the Netherlands, Norway, and the United States). That study reported costs ranging from US $9,342 and US $21,840, with Canada exhibiting the highest expenditures [12]. However, the methodology used to estimate costs in our study differs substantially. Unlike Bekelman et al., who based their estimates on Case Mix Groups, our approach focuses on individualized cost assessments.

The findings underscore the importance of differentiated care strategies based on anticipated EoL health trajectories. For instance, reducing unnecessary hospital use in terminal and organ failure cases, where late-life hospitalizations are common, could improve quality of care and reduce costs. For frail individuals, maintaining community-based supports and delaying or reducing LTC admissions may be both cost-effective and more aligned with patient preferences [11], particularly as CLSC interventions were notably less expensive than LTC. However, it is important to acknowledge that increased reliance on community-based care may shift a substantial portion of care responsibilities and associated costs onto unpaid informal caregivers. These costs, including physical, emotional, and financial burdens, are not captured in administrative data and thus are not reflected in our cost estimates. This limitation underscores the complexity of evaluating the true economic impact of shifting care from institutional to community settings.

Strength and limitations

Administrative health data content

While administrative health databases provide comprehensive, population-level data on healthcare utilization across the province, offering a major strength in terms of sample size and representativeness, these data are primarily collected for administrative purposes, not research. Core variables such as data source and service date are mandatory, which supports the reliability of healthcare utilization measures. However, key sociodemographic variables such as ethnicity and socioeconomic status were not captured, limiting the scope of subgroup analysis. In this study, only age, sex and cause of death were available, restricting our ability to explore healthcare inequities related to social determinants of health. Beyond these data content limitations, additional challenges related to data management and methodological constraints are also present and are discussed in the following section. Another key limitation relates to the classification of individuals based on cause of death. In our study, categorization was based on a single ICD-10 code representing the primary cause of death, as recorded in the provincial death registry and certified by a coroner. This approach, while standardized and consistent with provincial data practices, does not account for contributory or secondary causes of death, which are particularly relevant in older populations. Consequently, individuals with complex clinical trajectories may be assigned to a single category that does not fully capture the range of conditions influencing end-of-life care. Furthermore, our classification was operationalized using a correspondence table developed by the INSPQ, which maps ICD-10 codes to broader clinical categories. While this method ensures comparability with other analyses conducted in Quebec, it may introduce heterogeneity within categories such as organ failure and frailty (physical or cognitive), whose definition has been slightly broadened compared to Lunney et al. [30] to include the work of Murray et al. [31] and Murtagh et al. [32].

Data managementand methodological challenge

Estimating costs for CLSC services presented significant methodological challenges due to data fragmentation and changes from the 2015 healthcare reform. The elimination of reporting categories (“missions”) complicated cost attribution as there were several overlaps in the budget breakdowns. To address this, we relied on the activity center data, which, despite inconsistencies in cost metrics, allowed a better cost estimation. Two cost attribution strategies were applied based on available metrics and a quarterly aggregation enabled temporal analysis of care intensity. These adaptations illustrate the complexity of cost estimation in fragmented systems and the necessity of systematic, flexible approaches.

ED and hospital data were the most robust, as they are routinely used by the health system for performance assessment. In contrast, data from the fee-for-service billing (SMOD) and local community service centers databases (i-CLSC) required some manipulation to be included. Medical acts were grouped to define a “visit” but misclassification (merging distinct visits or splitting one) may have occurred. However, the risk is minimal due to the use of multiple matching variables (date, patient, provider, location, billing ID), with negligible impact on the broader scale of this study. Another limitation is the potential underestimation of specialist visits, as visits that have taken place the same day as an emergency or hospitalization episode were excluded. This approach may have inadvertently omitted scheduled specialist appointments. However, this underestimation did not affect the cost estimates, as specialist billing fees, whether for outpatient or inpatient services, are submitted to the healthcare system regardless of the care setting and were therefore captured in the cost analysis. Nevertheless, in the future, a clearer distinction between those consultations is necessary as there is, to date, no actual reliable variable to distinguish those consultations. In addition, study period had to be restricted to fit the CLSC database creation and matching ID implementation which was effective in 2013 [38]. Finally, we acknowledge that the study period is short, and healthcare utilization could evolve over time and future research could explore this more formally, however some explanatory analyses showed no major variations in service use nor cost between fiscal year (data not shown).

Lack of palliative care and end-of-life information

A significant limitation of this study is the lack of reliable and complete information about palliative care within the administrative data, which constrained our ability to accurately describe the scope and delivery of palliative services. For instance, individuals may have received palliative care during the last days of their life in hospital settings that lack a designated palliative care unit, a common occurrence, resulting in no identifiable record of such care in the database. Furthermore, physicians providing palliative care services are often compensated through salaried or blended payment models rather than fee-for-service. Consequently, their activities are not systematically captured in the SMOD database. In addition, palliative homecare services, typically provided by physicians affiliated to CLSC, also tend to be underreported, as these providers are not administratively required to log their visits in this database. This gap may potentially lead to the misleading impression that physicians are minimally involved in EoL care and services or that access to palliative care is more limited than it is. In addition, the formal classification of patients under a “palliative care” profile is closely related to clinical judgment, experience and administrative protocols. In Quebec, a vital prognosis of three months is often required to qualify for palliative care, indirectly restricting access individuals suffering from frailty (physical or cognitive) for instance. Consequently, relying solely on administrative health data, without any expert judgement, may lead to the erroneous conclusion that few palliative care services are provided to these populations. In practice, many individuals with organ insufficiency or frailty (physical or cognitive) receive care under alternative administrative categories, such as “support for individuals with diminishing autonomy”. Finally, another limitation of this study is the exclusion of informal (unpaid) care from our analysis. Administrative databases do not capture support provided by family members or other informal caregivers, even though such care is especially prevalent in home and community settings. As a result, our cost estimates reflect only the formal healthcare system’s expenditures and may underestimate the full societal burden of end-of-life care. Future research is needed to enhance the identification and tracking of palliative care recipients within administrative health databases to more accurately reflect the real-world provision of EoL care, but also to incorporate all the components of EoL support, including family members and caregivers.

Conclusion

This study highlights distinct EoL healthcare and social services utilization and related costs among older adults in Quebec, shaped by cause of death, age, and sex. Cancer and organ failure were associated with high acute care use and costs, while frailty led to greater reliance on long-term and social care. These findings underscore the need for tailored care strategies that reflect different EoL trajectories. Gaps in palliative care data remain a key limitation, pointing to the importance of improving data systems to better capture the full scope of care and support effective, patient-centered planning.
