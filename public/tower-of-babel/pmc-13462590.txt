Asymmetric structural transitions in the icosahedral organization of Eastern equine encephalitis virus
Authors: Abhishek Bandyopadhyay, Lauren E. Williamson, Cameron D. Buchman, Thomas Klose, James E. Crowe, Richard J. Kuhn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Delivery of the viral genome into host cells is a critical step in successful viral infection. Alphaviruses achieve this step by fusing the viral and endosomal membranes under acidic conditions. This process requires significant structural changes in the alphavirus glycoprotein organization. Structural characterization of acidic pH-induced conformational changes in alphavirus virions has remained elusive due to the rapid, transient nature of these states, conformational heterogeneity, and particle aggregation. Antibody binding studies conducted at elevated temperatures or under acidic pH conditions have further revealed the presence of transitional epitopes that are inaccessible on alphaviruses at room temperature or neutral pH. In this report, we present structural snapshots of the conformational changes in the glycoproteins and nucleocapsid core of a prototypical alphavirus, Eastern equine encephalitis virus, caused by exposure to 40 °C or pH 5.6. These findings provide insights into the structural transitions that occur prior to viral fusion with the endosomal membrane. This approach has also allowed us to define the molecular basis for recognition of a pan-alphavirus epitope by a patient-derived human antibody.

Introduction

Alphaviruses are a group of arthropod-borne, positive-sense single-stranded RNA viruses that cause a variety of pathophysiological conditions in humans and animals1,2. Alphaviruses have been classified based on their pathologies. Representative arthritogenic alphaviruses include chikungunya virus (CHIKV), Ross River virus (RRV), and Mayaro virus (MAYV), which can cause a febrile illness that leads to debilitating arthritis and myalgia in infected individuals3. The encephalitic alphaviruses include Eastern equine encephalitis virus (EEEV), Western equine encephalitis virus (WEEV), and Venezuelan equine encephalitis virus (VEEV), which can cause severe encephalitis and neurological sequelae4–6.

Structural analyses of different alphaviruses have revealed remarkable organizational similarity among these enveloped particles7–9. Alphavirus particles exhibit T = 4 quasi-icosahedral symmetry, containing a nucleocapsid shell composed of 240 copies of the capsid protein, which encapsulates the ~11.7 kb RNA genome, and is surrounded by a glycoprotein shell embedded within a host-derived lipid bilayer. The glycoprotein shell consists of 80 trimeric spikes of heterodimers of two single-pass transmembrane glycoproteins, E1 and E2. The E2 glycoprotein is mostly responsible for recognition of cellular attachment factors and receptors. Once viral particles enter host cells through endocytosis, the E1 glycoprotein mediates endosomal membrane fusion under acidic pH conditions.

In addition to 240 copies each of the E1, E2, and capsid proteins, alphavirus particles contain non-stoichiometric amounts of the putative ion channel proteins, 6K and TF (transframe), which have yet to be structurally characterized10,11.

The ~30 kDa capsid protein is composed of N- and C-terminal domains, which are predicted to be intrinsically disordered or adopt a chymotrypsin-like fold, respectively. The capsid N-terminal domain interacts with the RNA genome. Part of the E2 C-terminal domain interacts with a hydrophobic cavity in the capsid C-terminal domain, contributing to the overall architecture of the double-layered assembly7–9.

The extracellular domain of the E1 glycoprotein consists of three β-barrel-like domains termed DI, DII and DIII. DII harbors the fusion loop, which facilitates class II-mediated viral fusion. The extracellular domain of the E2 glycoprotein is composed of three immunoglobulin-like domains termed A, B, and C, which are connected to each other by a β-ribbon connector with little secondary structure. The E1 or E2 extracellular domains are connected to their transmembrane helices through the stem region or D-loop, respectively. In virions, the E1 glycoprotein lies almost parallel to the viral membrane, while the E2 glycoprotein lies on top of the E1 glycoprotein, occluding most of the E1 glycoprotein. Furthermore, domain B of the E2 protein helps prevent premature exposure of the E1 fusion loop9,12.

Alphaviruses primarily use the clathrin-mediated endocytic route to enter host cells13–15. Upon entering a cell, progressive acidification in the endosomes leads to dissociation of the E2 and E1 glycoprotein heterodimers, E1 homotrimer formation, and fusion of the viral and endosomal membranes, subsequently releasing the nucleocapsid inside the cell16. This acidic-pH-dependent fusion process requires dramatic conformational changes of the E1 and E2 glycoproteins. A crystal structure of the E1 and E2 glycoprotein ectodomains of Sindbis virus (SINV) at pH 5.6 revealed that domain B and part of the β-ribbon connector of the E2 glycoprotein become disordered, thereby exposing the fusion loop17 on the E1 glycoprotein. Further structural changes lead to E1 homotrimer formation with significant rearrangement of domain interfaces, as revealed by the crystal structure of the post-fusion conformation of the Semliki Forest virus E1 glycoprotein18. These structural studies shed light on the conformational changes that occur in the extracellular domains of the E1 and E2 glycoproteins under acidic pH conditions, yet the structural changes that take place on an intact alphavirus virion have remained elusive19,20. Interestingly, several cryptic E1 glycoprotein epitopes, including some pan-alphavirus epitopes, of different alphaviruses are inaccessible to antibody recognition in intact virions at room temperature or neutral pH (pH 7.4). However, these epitopes become accessible at elevated temperatures—such as typical human body temperatures (37 °C) or febrile temperatures (up to 42 °C) reported in some patients suffering from EEEV infection21—or under acidic pH conditions (pH 5.6)22–24, strongly suggesting structural changes occur in virions exposed to these conditions.

In this report, we present the structural changes that occur at 40 °C or pH 5.6 in Sindbis virus (SINV)/EEEV particles. SINV/EEEV is a chimeric alphavirus that encodes the nonstructural proteins of SINV and the structural proteins of EEEV (strain FL93-939)25. This chimeric virus can be handled and studied in a BSL-2 containment facility, in contrast to wild type EEEV, which must be handled under BSL-3 conditions. For simplicity, we will refer to SINV/EEEV as EEEV hereafter. To capture the structural changes that occur in intact EEEV virions exposed to 40 °C or acidic pH conditions, we used Fab molecules of a patient-derived pan-alphavirus non-neutralizing anti-E1 glycoprotein antibody (EEEV-17923) or an E2 domain A targeting neutralizing antibody (EEEV-3326), respectively. Interestingly, the structural transitions captured under both conditions showed a similar trend, involving asymmetric movements of both the E1 and E2 glycoprotein extracellular domains, the E2 D-loop and E1 stem region, and the nucleocapsid core. The asymmetric nature of these structural transitions is characterized by comparatively larger structural changes in one arm of the asymmetric unit relative to the others. The differential exposure and recognition of a cryptic pan-alphavirus epitope on a single site in the asymmetric unit by EEEV-179 Fab and the movement of the spikes on intact virions under acidic pH conditions, provide opportunities to design pan-alphavirus fusion inhibitors that can target multiple clinically significant alphaviruses.

Results

Complex formation between EEEV and EEEV-179 Fab

EEEV-179 is a patient-derived non-neutralizing antibody that recognizes a pan-alphavirus epitope on the E1 glycoprotein and provides protection from both arthritogenic and encephalitic alphavirus infections by preventing viral egress from infected cells23. EEEV-179 Fab molecules did not form complexes with EEEV under native conditions, which is room temperature (23 °C−25 °C) and neutral pH (pH 7.4), but readily formed complexes at either 40 °C or under acidic pH conditions followed by back neutralization with a high pH buffer at room temperature. As mentioned before, the 40 °C was chosen to mimic high fever conditions reported in some patients suffering from EEEV infections21.

Structural basis for EEEV-179 recognition of a pan-alphavirus epitope on EEEV at 40 °C

To delineate the structural basis for EEEV-179 recognition of the pan-alphavirus epitope on EEEV, we determined the structure of the complex of EEEV and EEEV-179 Fab molecules formed at 40 °C using cryo-EM. The cryo-EM reconstruction of the complex reached a global resolution of 5.8 Å with icosahedral symmetry imposition. The icosahedral reconstruction revealed that the Fab molecules bind to the E1 glycoprotein in a tangential manner at the icosahedral two-fold (i2) toward the base of the q3 spike (Fig. 1A, B). Binding of Fab was not observed at the icosahedral five-fold (i5) region. The density of bound Fab molecules was discontinuous in the icosahedral reconstruction. The localized reconstruction map revealed that EEEV-179 binds to a relatively flat surface on the E1 glycoprotein adjacent to the DII fusion loop (Fig. 1D–F, Supplementary Fig. 1). Additional residues (S352 and F353) on DIII are within 5 Å of the Fab framework region of the variable domains (Fig. 1F, Supplementary Fig. 1). The tangential approach of EEEV-179 Fab towards its epitope leads to steric clashes between the N134 glycan of the E1 glycoprotein and the light chain variable domain of the Fab (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Prior alanine scanning mutagenesis and hydrogen-deuterium exchange mass spectrometry analyses were used to identify the footprint of EEEV-179 on the EEEV E1 glycoprotein23, which suggested that the antibody binds near the base of a spike, a finding corroborated by the structure.

In the top view of the atomic model, EEEV-179 engages the E1 glycoprotein near the fusion loop (Fig. 2A). The main interactions with the E1 glycoprotein are mediated by the heavy chain complementarity-determining region 3 (CDRH3) and light chain CDRs 1 (CDRL1) and 2 (CDRL2) of the Fab (Fig. 2B–D). The heavy and light chains bury a surface are of ~380 Å2 or ~571 Å2, respectively, on the E1 glycoprotein (Supplementary Fig. 2B). The EEEV-179 CDRs engage two ends of the DII region, with the CDRL1 and CDRL2 interacting with a region adjacent, but proximal to the fusion loop, while the CDRH3 wedges into a region distal to the fusion loop (Fig. 2B–D). The epitope-paratope interface between the EEEV E1 glycoprotein and EEEV-179 was resolved to a range between 4.0 to 4.5 Å (Fig. 2G–I). At this resolution range, it is not possible to determine the specific nature of the interactions between the CDRs and the epitope residues. However, the footprint can be determined and consists of both polar and non-polar residues, and to a lesser extent charged residues (Fig. 2C–E). The residues comprising the epitope are not completely conserved between the arthritogenic and encephalitic alphaviruses (Supplementary Fig. 1). Interestingly, most of the non-conserved residues interact with the CDRH3 (Fig. 2E and Supplementary Fig. 2B). The general positioning of the CDRs in the atomic model suggests a plausible mechanism for this interaction, which may involve Van der Waals interactions with the C-β atoms of these residues.

A recent study described a non-human primate-derived pan-alphavirus antibody (SKT05) targeting the same region on VEEV and WEEV virus-like particles (VLPs)27. The authors of that study suggested that binding to non-conserved residues at the epitope-paratope interface are accommodated through backbone hydrogen bonding with the CDRs of SKT05. Superimposition of the VEEV E1 glycoprotein in complex with SKT05 (PDB: 8EEU) onto the EEEV E1 glycoprotein bound to EEEV-179 shows that the VEEV epitope requires minor structural adjustments in certain residues, such as alternate side chain rotamers (VEEV E67 vs EEEV Q67) or slight movements (VEEV P70 vs EEEV S70), to engage with EEEV-179 (Fig. 2F). However, other epitope residues, such as C63 or E99, might need more than rotameric or minor structural changes to effectively bind EEEV-179.

Binding of EEEV-179 to only one site in the asymmetric unit

In the cryo-EM reconstruction of EEEV particles in complex with EEEV-179 Fab at 40 °C, we observed that the Fabs bound to only one site in the asymmetric unit. To probe this finding further, we reprocessed previously reported data of EEEV particles under native conditions (room temperature and neutral pH)9 and new data of EEEV particles alone at 40 °C (Fig. 1J–L) for structural comparisons of the locally reconstructed maps and models of the asymmetric unit (Fig. 1G–I). The structures generated in the absence of EEEV-179 Fabs were used as references to elucidate the structural transitions observed. To facilitate structural comparisons, we numbered the four arms of the asymmetric unit as follows: starting with the i3 arm (arm 1), followed by the q3 arm (arm 2) adjacent to the i3 spike, then the right-handed bend of the q3 spike (arms 3-4), and finally arm 3′ from the adjacent q3 spike (Fig. 3A). Superimposition of the E1 glycoprotein atomic models from the localized reconstruction maps of EEEV particles, either alone or in complex with EEEV-179 at 40 °C, revealed that, except for arm 4 of the asymmetric unit, none of the other arms exhibit an optimal epitope conformation compatible with EEEV-179 complex formation (RMSD = 0.029 Å) (Fig. 3B–E). We propose these observations explain why we did not observe any binding of EEEV-179 Fab molecules to the i5 region of EEEV particles at 40 °C. We also performed an analogous structural comparison with the asymmetric unit model of EEEV particles alone under native conditions and found that none of the arms have the optimal conformation for complex formation with EEEV-179 Fabs (Fig. 3F–I). Additionally, we measured the distances between the C-α atoms of the residues forming the epitope in the Fab complexed and native models (Supplementary Fig. 2C-D). The results show that, except for C63 and V80, all the other residues in the native model need to move by at least 1 Å, with A65, E99 and T101 requiring a movement of more than 2 Å, to adopt the conformation required for complex formation with EEEV-179. Altogether, the structural comparisons demonstrate that the epitope of EEEV-179 is not readily accessible on native virions, i.e., cryptic. However, when particles are exposed to 40 °C, the virus undergoes asymmetric structural changes within the icosahedral arrangement that results in differential or transitional exposure of the epitope, subsequently enabling complex formation with EEEV-179.

Acidic-pH induced complex formation between EEEV particles and EEEV-179 Fab

Since EEEV complexes with EEEV-179 Fabs were obtained at 40 °C, we next tested if exposure to acidic pH could also enable complex formation. As demonstrated previously, acidic pH treatment of alphavirus particles followed by neutralization with a high pH buffer (pH 8.0) generates particles that are largely infective and fusogenic with liposomes28. For acidic pH exposure, we chose pH 5.6 since EEEV particles readily undergo conformational changes at this pH29. To mitigate significant particle aggregation, we back neutralized the mixture of EEEV particles and EEEV-179 Fab molecules with a high pH buffer (stock concentration is 1 M Tris pH 8.0 and final concentration is 120 mM) after brief exposure to pH 5.6 conditions at room temperature. The sample prepared in this manner was termed ‘EEEV + EEEV-179 Fab-lpbn’, where ‘lpbn’ stands for low-pH-back-neutralized. The cryo-EM reconstruction of the complex revealed that the Fab molecules bound to the same arm 4 site on the asymmetric unit as seen for the complex obtained at 40 °C (Supplementary Fig. 3A–D). This finding strongly suggests that acidic pH conditions trigger asymmetric movements within the particle that differentially exposes the epitope on arm 4. The resolution of the epitope-paratope interface in these complexes is comparatively low (5 to 6 Å) (Supplementary Fig. 3D), which prevented detailed analyses of these interface interactions. Structural superimposition of the atomic models of asymmetric units of EEEV particles with EEEV-179 Fabs at 40 °C onto those of the complexes treated with ‘lpbn’ conditions shows minor structural differences at the backbone level (Supplementary Fig. 3E). However, given the relatively low resolution of the interface between the CDRs of the Fab and the E1 glycoprotein, this observation could simply mean that the approximate positioning of the Fab model is close to the one observed in the 40 °C model. To control for any structural differences between back neutralization treatment and Fab binding, we also reconstructed EEEV particles exposed to acidic pH followed by back neutralization (‘EEEV-lpbn’) in the absence of EEEV-179 Fabs (Supplementary Fig. 3F-G). A structural comparison of the asymmetric unit models of EEEV-lpbn and EEEV native samples revealed some structural differences across the entire asymmetric unit (RMSD = 1.52 Å). This suggests that the ‘lpbn’ protocol does not produce the exact placement of the structural proteins with respect to the untreated sample. Since the back neutralized complexes are unlikely to occur physiologically, we focused on the structural details obtained at 40 °C.

Low-resolution cryo-EM reconstructions of EEEV particles alone or in complex with EEEV-179 Fab at pH 5.6

Since the cryo-EM reconstruction of the ‘EEEV + EEEV-179-lpbn’ sample revealed the differential exposure of arm 4 of the asymmetric unit, we attempted to capture the structural transitions induced by acidic pH on EEEV particles alone or in the presence of EEEV-179 Fabs using cryo-EM. The samples were prepared by briefly mixing with a pH 5.6 buffer and loading onto glow-discharged lacey carbon grids within 30 seconds. The grids were then blotted and frozen. The resolutions of the reconstructions for both samples reached between 9 and 10 Å with icosahedral symmetry imposition (Supplementary Fig. 3H-I). Even though the samples did not show aggregation, the particles displayed significant heterogeneity. The 2D classes and the reconstructed volumes for both samples (Supplementary Fig. 11) showed that the particles overall resembled the structural arrangement of the glycoproteins seen in native EEEV, except with major flexibility of the spike arms. This finding suggests that the particles may represent early stages of the structural transitions that occur during viral fusion. The structural heterogeneity observed due to considerable movement of the glycoprotein extracellular domains prevented any resolution improvement through localized reconstruction and model building. Additionally, in the EEEV-179 Fab containing sample, we could not even identify the Fab molecule in the icosahedral reconstruction. Since these samples produced low-resolution reconstructions, they were designated as ‘EEEV at pH 5.6-low-res’ and ‘EEEV + EEEV-179 Fab at pH 5.6-low-res’.

Capturing an altered conformation of EEEV particles at pH 5.6

To minimize the structural heterogeneity of the spikes, we utilized a Fab molecule of a potently neutralizing antibody EEEV-3326, to restrict glycoprotein movement at pH 5.6. We mixed EEEV particles in a buffer at pH 7.4 with EEEV-33 Fabs in a pH 5.6 buffer, such that the pH of the resulting solution was 5.6. The sample was then loaded onto a glow-discharged lacey carbon grid, blotted, and frozen for subsequent analysis by cryo-EM. We chose EEEV-33 since it binds domain A of the E2 glycoprotein. Under acidic pH conditions, the E2 domain B and β-ribbon connector become highly flexible and disordered17. Thus, it was important to use an antibody targeting domain A of the E2 glycoprotein, but not domain B or the β-ribbon connector.

The cryo-EM reconstruction of the sample reached a global resolution of 6.4 Å with icosahedral symmetry imposition (Fig. 4A). Unlike the acidic pH samples described in the previous section, the characteristic structural features of the glycoprotein regions were clearly visible in the reconstruction. To analyze the acidic pH-induced structural changes in detail, we separated the asymmetric units through sub-particle extraction, 3D classification (with or without masks), and local refinement (Supplementary Fig. 12.I-III). Extracted sub-particles were first classified into 3 types, with one class having native EEEV-like structural features, one class depicting some structural heterogeneity (suggesting movement in the asymmetric unit), and the rest are highly heterogeneous or damaged. The class with some structural heterogeneity was classified further, and a relatively homogeneous population of sub-particles was identified and further refined through local refinement to an overall resolution of 4.7 Å (Fig. 4D). The population of this class represented about 5% of the extracted sub-particles. Given the resolution of the reconstruction, the backbone positions of the protein chains were resolved but not the side chains of individual residues. However, this volume displayed structural features distinct from the native asymmetric unit, and therefore, we refer to this asymmetric unit as asu* to indicate an altered conformation at pH 5.6. When viewed from the top, the atomic model of the asu* (Fig. 4E) radially expands when compared to the asymmetric unit model of native EEEV sample (Fig. 4F). The distance between the q3 arms increases by ~1 Å, and the distance of the i3 arm from the center of the q3 spike also increases by ~2.5 Å. Since these movements are induced by acidic pH, we computed the surface electrostatic potential of the EEEV E2/E1 glycoprotein extracellular domains at pH values of 7.4 or 5.6. The drop in pH caused drastic changes to the surface electrostatic potential (Fig. 4G-H), likely resulting in repulsion between different segments of the E2/E1 heterodimer and leading to the expansion observed.

The asymmetric units that displayed native-like features showed discontinuous density for the bound EEEV-33 Fabs at low contour levels, indicating low occupancy of the Fabs. Therefore, these sub-particles were further classified using masked 3D classification to separate Fab bound q3 and i3 spikes from unbound ones. Only one Fab molecule bound to each arm of either the q3 or i3 spikes. This finding is to be expected, since full occupancy of radially bound Fabs to E2 domain A on either the q3 or i3 spikes is unlikely due to steric clashes9. The refined maps for the q3 and i3 spikes bound to one EEEV-33 Fab molecule reached resolutions of 4.2-4.3 Å (Fig. 4I-L).

The models of EEEV-33 Fab bound q3 and i3 spikes at pH 5.6 are basically identical. The RMSD between the i3 arm (arm 1) of the asymmetric unit model of the EEEV-33 Fab bound to one arm of the q3 spike (arm 4) and that of the i3 arm bound to the Fab molecule is ~0.5 Å. EEEV-33 Fab binds to both domains A and B of E2 in a radial fashion (Supplementary Fig. 4B-C). The light chain predominantly interacts with domain A and the CDRH3 inserts into the cleft formed by E2 domains A, B and the E1 fusion loop. This region also interacts with receptors30–32. CDRH1 and CDRH2 are adjacent to the CDRH3 and interact with residues on E2 domain B (Supplementary Fig. 4C). The footprint of EEEV-33 includes residues from E2 domains A, B, and E1 fusion loop (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 4D). Superimposition of the EEEV-33 bound model at pH 5.6 to the native model, showed an RMSD of 0.2 Å (Supplementary Fig. 4E-F). In contrast, superimposition with the asu* model showed an RMSD of 2.3 Å (Supplementary Fig. 4G-H). These findings suggest that at pH 5.6 the EEEV-33 Fab bound trimeric spikes adopt a similar conformation to those at neutral pH, and the Fab restricts movement of these spikes. However, the asu* model shows distinct structural changes in the epitope region that prevents it from forming complex with EEEV-33 Fabs.

The expansion of the asu* with respect to the native asymmetric unit is highlighted through superimposition of the two models (Supplementary Fig. 5A–F). The top and side views of the models show discernible movements across the entire asu* in comparison to the native model. The glycoproteins in the asu* appear to move upwards from the viral membrane and laterally expand. Temperature-induced movements in the asymmetric unit of EEEV

EEEV-179 binds EEEV particles exposed to either 40 °C or acidic pH conditions in a similar manner. The conformation of the EEEV-179 epitope within arm 4 of the asymmetric unit at 40 °C or pH 5.6 is compatible for complex formation. These findings strongly suggest that similar structural transitions may occur in the virus at elevated temperatures and under acidic pH conditions. To probe this effect further, we compared the overall structural changes that occur in the asymmetric unit at 40 °C, asu*, and native conditions (room temperature and pH 7.4). Structural superimposition analyses revealed discernible structural differences between them, similar to those observed for particles exposed to acidic pH conditions (asu*) but not to the same extent. At 40 °C, arm 4 of the asymmetric unit exhibits comparatively pronounced structural changes. The E1 stem loop, and domains B and C of the E2 glycoprotein move in a similar way as seen for the acidic pH structure. In addition, the E1 and E2 transmembrane helices and the capsid C-terminal domains also move in the same way as in the asu* model (Supplementary Fig. 4I–L). Overall, the structural transitions seen in the 40 °C model provide an intermediate state snapshot between the neutral and acidic pH models.

Discussion

In this manuscript, we describe the movement of the structural proteins of EEEV, a prototypical alphavirus, exposed to elevated temperatures or acidic pH environments within the context of the double-layered icosahedral arrangement. Under physiological conditions, alphaviruses are exposed to acidic pH in the endosomal compartments and elevated temperatures, especially in febrile patients. Acidic pH-induced conformational changes in alphavirus glycoproteins are critical for viral and endosomal membrane fusion and delivery of the nucleocapsid inside host cells16–18. Additionally, temperature- and pH-dependent antibody binding to alphaviruses highlights the differential exposure of some epitopes under these conditions.22–24 Flaviviruses, such as dengue virus serotype 2 (DENV2), expand and transform from smooth to bumpy particles in a temperature-dependent manner33–36. So far, there are no reports of alphaviruses exhibiting large temperature-dependent conformational changes analogous to those exhibited by DENV2.

The cryo-EM reconstructions of EEEV particles alone or in complex with EEEV-179 Fabs at 40 °C illustrate the structural changes that occur in the virus due to temperature, and the subsequent exposure of the EEEV-179 epitope. EEEV-179 is a patient-derived non-neutralizing antibody that confers protection by blocking egress of newly formed alphavirus particles budding from the plasma membrane23. The epitope targeted by EEEV-179 is not readily accessible on EEEV virions under the native conditions tested here but is accessible on nascent particles budding from the plasma membrane. Our structural analysis revealed that the EEEV-179 Fab molecule forms a complex only at one site in the asymmetric unit (arm 4), highlighting the asymmetric nature of the exposure of the epitope under elevated temperature. This E1 epitope is not unique, as another antibody, SKT05, targets this same region on VEEV and WEEV VLPs27. However, SKT05 binding occurs under native conditions (i.e., room temperature and neutral pH). A structure of SKT05 in complex with EEEV virions or VLPs has yet to be reported, suggesting that this epitope is not easily accessible on intact EEEV virions or VLPs, but exposed on VEEV or WEEV VLPs.

Overall, the binding footprint of EEEV-179 is largely corroborated by previous epitope mapping studies. EEEV E1 glycoprotein residues A286, N361, and Y93 were identified through alanine-scanning mutagenesis studies of cell surface displayed mutant and wild-type EEEV structural proteins as critical for binding to EEEV-17923. However, in the structure reported here, none of these residues form any direct interaction with EEEV-179. It could be that these residues play an important role in stabilizing the conformation of the epitope on the plasma membrane or act allosterically.

The pan-alphavirus epitope described in this manuscript is only partially conserved at the residue level between the encephalitic and arthritogenic alphaviruses (Supplementary Fig. 1). The CDRH3 is the primary contributor interacting with the non-conserved residues at the epitope-paratope interface. Given the position of the CDRH3 in the structure, a possible mechanism for engagement involves Van der Waals interactions with the C-β atoms of these non-conserved residues. However, details of the interactions cannot be determined owing to the resolution range of the epitope-paratope interface. This binding mode differs from that reported for SKT05, where the authors suggest that SKT05 uses backbone hydrogen bonds for this interaction. One reason for these two different modes of binding could be due to the angle of approach, as SKT05 tilts about 29° more towards the viral membrane in comparison to EEEV-179 (Supplementary Fig. 2E).

The structural comparison of the model of arm 4 bound to EEEV-179 Fab with all the arms of the asymmetric unit models of native sample or EEEV at 40 °C sample revealed that only arm 4 at 40 °C displays the optimal conformation of the epitope region that is compatible with complex formation with the antibody. This finding prompted us to probe whether EEEV-179 Fab binds at the same site in the asymmetric unit under acidic pH condition using the previously described low-pH-back-neutralized (lpbn) protocol.

The structure of the ‘EEEV + EEEV-179-lpbn’ complex revealed that the EEEV-179 Fab binds to the same site on arm 4 of the asymmetric unit. Although the ‘lbpn’ complex is unlikely to occur under physiological conditions, the structure strongly suggests that under acidic pH conditions, there could be similar movements in arm 4, analogous to the ones observed for the 40 °C sample.

In order to capture structural transitions occurring in EEEV under acidic pH, we attempted to generate cryo-EM reconstructions of EEEV alone or in presence of EEEV-179 Fabs at pH 5.6. The cryo-EM reconstructions of these samples highlight the conformational flexibility of the E2/E1 extracellular domains induced by acidic pH conditions, leading to significant structural heterogeneity even before particle aggregation and yielding low-resolution reconstructions of the particles. An analogous study to capture acidic pH-induced structural changes in EEEV particles utilized grid-immobilized virus samples before exposure to low pH conditions. Unfortunately, the resolution of the reconstructions were limited to 16 to 20 Å37.

We overcame these technical hurdles by using the Fab molecules of a potently neutralizing antibody, EEEV-33, in an acidic pH buffer solution. The simultaneous exposure of EEEV particles to EEEV-33 Fab molecules and pH 5.6 buffer, locked some of the asymmetric units in a neutral pH-like conformation (1 out of 6), while a handful of the asymmetric units were altered (1 out of 20) [Supplementary Fig. 12 (II) D–G]. The vast majority of the asymmetric units (~80%) displayed significant structural heterogeneity. The observed heterogeneity highlights the relatively fast movement of the glycoproteins when exposed to acidic pH conditions. Interestingly, the icosahedral reconstruction of this sample revealed that the diameter of the virus expands by ~10 Å when comparing particles at pH 5.6 to those in native conditions. Since our structural analysis of the sub-particles corresponding to the asymmetric units of this acidic pH sample showed differential movements as well as heterogeneity, this increase in the diameter is the cumulative effect of all the asymmetric units put together. The structural comparison between the asu* and the native asymmetric unit reveals that, the arms of the q3 spike expand symmetrically by ~1 Å and the i3 arm moves away from the center of the q3 spike by ~2.5 Å in the asu*. These structural comparisons provide snapshots of the global movements that occur in an intact alphavirus under acidic pH conditions. The measurement of the movements in the asymmetric unit under native and acidic pH conditions reported here are based on the centers of mass of different domains and are relatively small given the resolution of the reconstruction. Nevertheless, the general trajectory of the movements observed is revealed in these structural comparisons. The detailed structural changes observed in the asymmetric unit under acidic ('asu'*, pH 5.6) versus native (neutral pH 7.4 at room temperature) conditions reveal how the glycoproteins in an intact alphavirus virion begin to move under acidic pH conditions prior to fusion. These conformational changes span the entire asymmetric unit.

It is important to note that in the absence of any other stabilizing factor that arrests the movement of the glycoproteins, exposure of EEEV particles to pH 5.6 conditions leads to much greater heterogeneity of the glycoprotein shell (Supplementary Fig. 3H-I). Given that we used EEEV-33 Fabs to stabilize the virus under acidic pH conditions (asu*), a limitation is to whether these structural changes captured are a direct effect of Fab binding to some of the asymmetric units. It is likely that EEEV-33 alters the equilibrium of the asymmetric units at pH 5.6, however, it is unlikely this Fab causes the specific structural changes induced by acidic pH, since we did not observe any EEEV-33 Fab bound to the asu*. The inability of EEEV-33 Fab to bind the asu* is likely due to the structural alteration of the epitope region. Therefore, EEEV-33 does not directly affect the specific conformation of asu* but indirectly affects the distribution of the asymmetric units as they undergo acidic pH induced conformational change. This is also supported by the observation that the EEEV 40 °C sample, which does not include EEEV-33 Fab molecules, exhibited similar structural changes to the asu* but to a lesser extent (Supplementary Fig. 4I–L).

The EEEV-33 Fab bound q3 and i3 spikes at pH 5.6 have similar conformations to native EEEV particles. Interestingly, among the four classes in the masked 3D classification of the q3 spikes, class 1 displayed native-like structure without any EEEV-33 Fab bound to it (Supplementary Fig. 12H). It is somewhat difficult to explain this result, but one possibility could be that these spikes are adjacent to the ones bound by EEEV-33 Fabs and thereby stabilized through proximity effects. It also raises the question as to why these spikes are not bound to any EEEV-33 Fab molecule. Although speculative, one potential explanation is that the sample was loaded onto the grid and frozen relatively quickly, leaving little incubation time for the Fabs to bind and saturate all available sites.

There are only three to five histidine residues in E2 and E1 glycoproteins, respectively, that are conserved across multiple alphaviruses (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 5A). Most of these residues are positioned at the interface between the E2 and E1 glycoproteins (Supplementary Fig. 5A). For example, the E2 residues H346 and H167 are at the E2 D-loop and E1 stem region, and ‘arch 2’ region of the β-ribbon connector and DII of the E1 glycoprotein interfaces, respectively. In contrast, H350 on the E2 glycoprotein points towards the viral membrane. Similarly, H387 of the E1 glycoprotein is critically positioned at the interface between domain C of the E2 glycoprotein and the E1 stem region. H231 on the E1 protein is adjacent to the E1 fusion loop and lies at the cleft between the E1 and E2 glycoproteins, a region important for receptor interactions31,32. H332 of the E1 glycoprotein is positioned at the interface between DI and DIII and is spatially close to H3 on DI. H125 of the E1 glycoprotein is not positioned at any interface between the glycoproteins but is positioned at the interface between neighboring spikes. In a high-resolution cryo-EM reconstruction of SINV, H125 forms a polar interaction with S41 of the E1 glycoprotein of the neighboring spike38. S41 is partially conserved across the arthritogenic and encephalitic alphaviruses, with threonine substituted in some viruses (Supplementary Fig. 1). Given the position of these conserved histidine residues within the E2/E1 heterodimer, they likely play a role in the pH-induced switching mechanism that governs the movement of the glycoproteins during viral fusion. The dramatic changes observed in the surface electrostatic potential of the E2/E1 heterodimer under acidic pH conditions likely cause repulsion between these residues and the ones surrounding them, ultimately driving the movements necessary for E2/E1 heterodimer dissociation and priming of the E1 glycoprotein for membrane fusion.

The asu* model of EEEV revealed surprising contraction of the nucleocapsid core toward the center of the virus when compared to the asymmetric unit model of the native sample (EEEV at pH 7.4, room temperature) (Movie 1). The glycoprotein and nucleocapsid core are linked through the transmembrane helices of the glycoproteins. These helices are more akin to springs, rather than rigid rods. Therefore, movement of the extracellular domains inevitably alters the small interface between the transmembrane helices. Here, we observed that the E2 transmembrane helix preceding the capsid protein interaction site extends and partially unwinds, likely due to alterations in the transmembrane helices interface caused by movements of the skirt region, E2 D-loop, and E1 stem region. Additionally, 3D variability analysis of the asu* map shows dramatic movements occur throughout the asymmetric unit. These movements span across the bilayer and lead to clashes between the C-terminal tail of the E2 and E1 transmembrane helices of neighboring spikes (Movie 2).

The contraction of the nucleocapsid core toward the center of the virus increases electrostatic repulsion within the core. Since the nucleocapsid region moves towards the center of the virus by 2.7 Å in the asymmetric unit, the diameter of the nucleocapsid decreases by 5.4 Å. This movement increases electrostatic repulsion by ~30 fold, since coulombic force is inversely proportional to the square of the distance between two charged points. Interestingly, the general architecture of the nucleocapsid core in the post-fusion state shows high structural heterogeneity and loss of icosahedral symmetry29. Furthermore, it has been reported that nucleocapsid cores within alphavirus particles can have both partially open or fully closed cores, and misalignment with the glycoprotein layer in some cases39. We postulate that, along with the structural heterogeneity of the nucleocapsid cores, this electrostatic repulsion due to the contraction might prime the cores for post-fusion disassembly. The interaction between the capsid protein and residues at the C-terminal portion of the E2 transmembrane helix contributes to the overall organization of an alphavirus particle in a metastable state, since the entire particle is capable of significant conformational change in a pH-dependent manner. However, this interaction needs to break during the fusion process to deliver the nucleocapsid cores inside the host cell. The localized reconstruction of the asu* revealed the presence of this interaction, which suggests that the viral bilayer may extend to accommodate the movement observed in the E2 transmembrane helices, while the nucleocapsid core compresses. The movements seen in these reconstructions also suggest possible alterations in the interface between the capsid protein and E2 C-terminal domain, the details of which are difficult to decipher owing to the resolution of this region (~5 Å).

Our structural analyses reveal that arm 4 of the asymmetric unit of EEEV undergoes comparatively greater structural changes when exposed to the conditions investigated in this study. We find that a pan-alphavirus epitope is exposed when particles were treated at 40 °C or back neutralized after acidic pH treatment, enabling EEEV-179 Fab molecule binding to arm 4 of the asymmetric unit. It is not immediately clear from these complexes the molecular basis for exclusive binding to arm 4 of the asymmetric unit. The intricate nature of the interplay of physicochemical forces that govern the movements of the E2/E1 glycoproteins within the context of the icosahedral symmetry is not yet understood to the extent necessary to explain the observed structural changes.

As depicted through the structural analyses presented in this study, the asymmetric movements that occur in an alphavirus particle at elevated temperatures or under acidic pH conditions lead to differential exposure of regions on the viral surface. A detailed understanding of the molecular forces that govern these movements have important implications for developing thermodynamic models of icosahedral virus disassembly and may also inform the energetics of multiprotein complex assembly and disassembly. Additionally, alphaviruses pose major threats to humans and other animals, and so far, a pan-alphavirus antibody that potently neutralizes both the arthritogenic and encephalitic alphaviruses has yet to be identified. Based on the studies described here, a neutralizing pan-alphavirus antibody must engage an epitope that is either fully or partially conserved while arresting the movements of the glycoprotein layer under acidic pH conditions. The results reported in this study highlight both aspects, the molecular basis of recognition of a partially conserved pan-alphavirus epitope and the movement in the glycoprotein layer under acidic pH conditions in the context of an intact virion. Both these findings provide a structural template for rational design of pan-alphavirus neutralizing antibodies targeting clinically relevant alphaviruses.

Methods

SINV/EEEV production and purification

SINV/EEEV was purified according to the previously described protocol with slight modifications9. Briefly, BHK-21 cells at ~85% confluency in six T-150 flasks (Corning) were inoculated with EEEV with a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 5 in Modified Eagle’s Medium (MEM; Gibco) supplemented with 2% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Cells were incubated at 37 °C and supernatant was collected between 16 and 18 h post-inoculation. Supernatant was then clarified of cell debris by Steritop filtration (Millipore). Solid PEG-6000 and NaCl were added to the clarified supernatant to a final concentration of 14% (w/v) and 1% (w/v), respectively. The mixture was gently swirled by hand to dissolve PEG and NaCl and then incubated overnight at 4 °C. The mixture was pelleted by centrifugation at 2,500 xg for 30 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was discarded and the pellet, containing SINV/EEEV particles, was gently resuspended in approximately 0.8 mL of 1x PBS (Phosphate Buffered Saline) buffer (pH 7.4). To purify the particles further, the resuspended pellet was gently layered on top of a 10 mL continuous OptiPrepTM (Sigma) gradient (0 to 90%) and was subjected to ultracentrifugation at 247,0000 xg for 1.5 h at 4 °C. The virus band was collected using a syringe and diluted in 10 mL of 1x PBS (pH 7.4) followed by concentration and buffer exchange into 1x PBS (pH 7.4) using an Amicon Ultra concentrator with a 100 kDa molecular weight cut-off (Millipore).

Fab molecule production and purification

Recombinant EEEV-179 or EEEV-33 variable genes were synthesized and cloned into a monocistronic pTwist CMV Betaglobin WPRE Neo mammalian expression vector containing the light chain or IgG1 CH1 constant region as previously described.23 EEEV-179, EEEV-179 Crystal Kappa (Fab_CK), and EEEV-33 Fab molecules were produced using the ExpiCHO-S (Thermo Fisher Scientific #A29127) expression system according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cell supernatants were purified using CaptureSelectTM CH1-XL affinity columns on an ÄKTA Pure 25 M chromatography system. Fab fragments were concentrated using 30 K MWCO Amicon® Ultra centrifugal filter units, then desalted, and buffer exchanged into 1× DPBS (Dulbecco’s Phosphate Buffered Saline) using 7 K MWCO Zeba desalting columns.

Sample preparation for cryo-EM

The different samples described in this manuscript were all prepared in a BSL-2 containment facility. Supplementary Table 1 lists the sample names, antibodies, temperature, pH, sample volume, and blot time for the different samples. The virion is EEEV in all cases. All the grids for cryo-EM data collection were prepared using glow-discharged lacey carbon grids and a Leica GP2 plunge freezing instrument. The blotted grids were plunged for freezing in liquid ethane. The concentration of EEEV particles was about 1 mg/mL, and EEEV-179 and EEEV-33 Fab stock concentrations were 2 and 3.5 mg/mL respectively.

X-ray crystallography of EEEV-179 Fab

After purification, EEEV-179 Fab_CK was transferred to 25 mM HEPES, pH 7.3, 100 mM NaCl using a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL gel filtration column and concentrated to 5 mg/mL. EEEV-179 Fab_CK was then crystallized in 0.3 M sodium fluoride and 15% PEG-3350 seeded with EEEV-179 Fab_CK crystal seeds. Crystals were cryoprotected using 30% glycerol for data collection. Diffraction data was collected at 100 K and λ = 0.97872 Å at the Life Sciences Collaborative Access Team beamline 21-ID-F at the Advanced Photon Source, Argonne National Laboratory. Data was processed and integrated using HKL300040. The structure was solved using molecular replacement via Phaser41 using an Alphafold42-generated model of EEEV-179 Fab_CK as the search model. Further structure refinement and modeling was completed using Refmac in the ccp4 suite43 and Coot44. The Ramachandran plot indicated that 96.12% of residues were in a favored orientation while only 0.46% of residues were considered outliers in the final model. Data collection and refinement statistics are listed in Supplementary Table 2. The crystal structure and the electron density map is shown in Supplementary Fig. 13.

Cryo-EM data processing, model fitting and refinement

For the samples described in this manuscript, data were processed using cryoSPARC45, except for the ‘EEEV + EEEV-179-lpbn’ sample, in which part of the processing was performed in Relion46. Patch Motion Correction, Patch CTF Estimations, particle picking, and extraction were all performed in the cryoSPARCLive sessions. Details of the processing of individual samples are described in the Supplementary information and Supplementary Figs. 6-12.

For the localized reconstruction maps of EEEV, EEEV at 40 °C, and ‘EEEV-lpbn’, and the asu* map, the previously published EEEV model (PDB ID: 6MX4) was fitted using Chimerax47 followed by subjecting the model to a Real_Space_Refinement protocol in Phenix48 using default settings. The model geometry and Ramachandran map were improved through iterative rounds of manual model building in Coot44 and Isolde49. The final model was subjected to another round of Real_Space_Refinement in Phenix.

For refinement of the glycoprotein models complexed with either EEEV-33 or EEEV-179 Fabs, we used a homology model of the EEEV-33 Fab molecule generated using Alphafold and a crystal structure of EEEV-179 Fab_CK molecule. The rest of the protocol for refinement remains the same as described in the previous paragraph. The details of the cryo-EM data collection parameters, processing, and model statistics are listed in Supplementary Tables 3–10.
