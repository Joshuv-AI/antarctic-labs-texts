Formal [4 + 2] combined ionic and radical approach of vinylogous enaminonitriles to access highly substituted sulfonyl pyridazines
Authors: Chanhyun Jung, Kwanghee Lee, Shanmugam Rajasekar, Ji-Youn Yim, Jaeuk Sim, Young Hee Lee, Jae-Hwan Kwak, Soonsil Hyun, Young Kee Kang, Mayavan Viji, Jae-Kyung Jung
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPyridazine derivatives hold significant interest due to their broad applications in pharmaceuticals and materials science, where they serve as valuable scaffolds for bioactive compounds and functional materials. Here, we report a formal [4 + 2] reaction for the synthesis of 5’-sulfonyl-4’-aryl-3-cyano substituted pyridazine compounds from the reaction between vinylogous enaminonitriles and sulfonyl hydrazides. The key features of our pyridazine synthesis include the transamidation of vinylogous enaminonitriles with sulfonyl hydrazide, radical sulfonylation of the resulting intermediate, and subsequent 6-endo-trig radical cyclization. This reaction proceeds smoothly to deliver a series of pyridazine derivatives in good to high yields. We also found that the sulfonyl group of the synthesized pyridazines can be transformed into C-, O-, or N-containing functional groups. A gram-scale experiment and a diverse transformation of synthesized pyridazines were also performed to validate the practicality of our developed process. In the synthesis of sulfonyl-substituted pyridazines, a 6-endo-trig cyclization via a radical pathway is both kinetically and thermodynamically favored over the cyclization via an ionic pathway, as supported by DFT calculations.Subject terms: Synthetic chemistry methodology, Diversity-oriented synthesis

Introduction

Pyridazine is an aromatic heterocyclic compound belonging to the diazine family, characterized by nitrogen atoms at the 1,2-positions1,2. Compared to other diazines, pyridazines exhibit higher pKa values (indicating greater basicity) and higher dipole moments1. considering pyridazine’s potential for hydrogen bonding and coordination with metals, as well as its increased polarity due to the presence of an additional nitrogen atom, which leads to the formation of more water-soluble salts or crystals, pyridazine can also be utilized as a bioisostere for benzene or pyridine3,4 for example, by employing the bioisostere concept, PZ-2891, which includes pyridazine and was developed as a PANK activator from the hit compound PZ-2863, demonstrates excellent pharmacokinetic properties and exhibits 100 times greater activity than PZ-2863 (Fig.1A)5,6 Pyridazine is the core scaffold of deucravacitinib7 (SOTYKTU®), sulfachloropyridazine and SR95191 (Fig. 1B)8,9 Consequently, there have been numerous efforts in recent decades to synthesize pyridazine scaffolds10–19.

In recent decades, push-pull enamine scaffolds such as enaminones and ylidenemalonitrile enamines have been widely employed for synthesizing diverse heterocycles or fluorescent probes (Fig. 1C (a, b))20–31. Our laboratory recently extended the push-pull enamines concept to vinylogous push-pull enamines, specifically a single-activated γ-functionalized enamino nitrile, which has been successfully utilized in heterocycle synthesis (Fig.1C (c))31.

Radical-involved cascade cyclization has emerged as a promising approach for obtaining functionalized and complex heterocycles with minimal reaction steps under environmentally benign conditions. One type of radical-radical coupling reaction involves the coupling of acyclic molecules to produce carbocyclic and heterocyclic scaffolds32–37. Notably, β, γ-unsaturated hydrazones have become increasingly recognized as essential and versatile motifs for producing a diverse array of substituted aza-heterocycles. Continued research efforts have been directed towards investigating the 5-exo-trig38–42 or 6-endo-trig43–46 cyclization of β, γ-unsaturated hydrazones to facilitate the synthesis of pyrazole, pyrazoline, dihydropyridazine, or pyridazine derivatives. For instance, the Xiao group reported a visible-light photocatalytic oxidative deprotonation electron transfer/TEMPO-mediated strategy for the catalytic N-radical cascade reaction of unsaturated hydrazones (Fig. 1D)46.

Inspired by these studies and continuing our interest in metal free synthesis31,47–52. We explored a formal [4 + 2] radical reaction using two components to synthesize heterocyclic compounds, leveraging the resemblance between β, γ-unsaturated hydrazones and our intermediates derived from our vinylogous push-pull enamine53–57. To this end, we disclose a TBHP-mediated radical-radical cascade reaction for constructing pyridazines via simultaneous C-N58,59 and C-S bond60–67 formation through 6-endo-trig cyclization. This mild protocol represents a broadly applicable synthesis of highly substituted sulfonyl pyridazines with good yields, which can further be transformed into highly functionalized pyridazines (Fig. 1E).

Results and discussion

To explore optimal conditions, we first investigated the reaction of ɣ-functionalized enaminonitrile 1a with commercially available tosyl hydrazide 2a, TBHP as the oxidant and KI as the catalyst. The reaction in toluene at 80 °C yielded an unexpected pyridazine product 3a (P1) in 46% (Table 1, entry 1), which was unambiguously confirmed by single crystal X-ray diffraction (CCDC number: 2094834). Encouraged by these results, we attempted to improve the yield of 3a. Preliminary screening of various additives indicated that TBAI was the most effective for product formation (71% yield, entry 3). Among the oxidants studied, TBHP was found to be the most suitable for obtaining 3a in high yield (entry 1), while the use of other peroxides significantly reduced the yield (entries 6–13). Organic oxidants produced moderate yields (entries 6–9). Inorganic peroxide oxidants resulted in higher yields compared to organic ones (entries 10–13) and the reaction with oxone as the oxidant displayed a good yield of 3a (68%, entry 13). Additionally, the yield varied depending on the amount of sulfonyl hydrazide, with the best yield obtained at a stoichiometric ratio of 2.0 eq. of 2a (entries 14–16). The highest yield was obtained when the reaction was carried out at 80 °C. (entries 17–18). Through solvent screening, toluene was identified as the most effective solvent for the reaction, even among nonpolar aprotic solvents (entries 19–25).

Having established the optimal reaction conditions, we expanded the substrate scope of the reaction, as summarized in Fig. 2. Initially, we examined reactions with various substituted sulfonyl hydrazides (Fig. 2A). Simple phenyl- or methoxyphenyl-substituted sulfonyl hydrazides afforded the desired products 3b-c in 75% and 71% yields, respectively. The presence of electron withdrawing groups, such as -CF3 and -NO2, did not hinder the reaction, yielding products 3d and 3e in 65% and 54% yields, respectively. Similarly, halogenated sulfonyl hydrazides provided the expected products in good yields (3f, 53% (F); 3g, 65% (Cl)). Notably, the reaction with dansyl sulfonyl hydrazide also provided the expected product 3h in 55% yield, demonstrating the suitability of this method for sterically bulky substituents. Sulfonyl hydrazide derived from thiophene also smoothly generated the anticipated product 3i in 63% yield. However, lower efficiency was observed when using alkyl sulfonyl hydrazide 3j.

Next, we investigated the scope of enaminonitriles under optimized conditions (Fig. 2B), which can be readily prepared31. Almost all enaminonitriles responded positively to our standard conditions and delivered the products in good yields. Enaminonitriles without any substitutions proceeded to generate the expected product 4a in 55% yield. Various electron-donating groups, including Me (4b, 63%), n-propyl (4c, 63%), iso-propyl (4d, 63%), methoxy (4e, 54%), and dioxymethylene (4n, 67%), also provided the corresponding products in good yields. Halogen-substituted enaminonitriles were compatible with the conditions and afforded the desired products in good yields (4f, 51% (F); 4g, 63% (Br); 4j, 68% (I)). Furthermore, good yields were obtained regardless of the position of the substituents on the benzene ring (4g, 63% (p-Br); 4h, 66% (m-Br); 4i, 59% (o-Br)). Additionally, various electron-withdrawing groups introduced in the enamines, smoothly afforded the corresponding products in good yields without any difficulties (4k–m, 52–63%). This optimized condition could be successfully extended to sterically hindered substrates (4o, 51%), and heterocycle-derived enaminonitriles (4p, 50%) as well.

Encouraged by the above results, we further extended our protocol to different electron-withdrawing groups (EWGs) on enamines. When ester-substituted enamines were treated with sulfonyl hydrazides containing methyl, methoxy, chloro, and trifluoromethyl groups, the target products were obtained in moderate yields (Fig. 2C, 5a–f, 32–43%). Notably, when a nitro group was used as an EWG, the reaction was highly effective in producing denitrated pyridazines via cleavage of the C-NO2 bond. Substituted sulfonyl hydrazides readily reacted with nitro enamines to provide denitrated pyridazines in higher yields (Fig. 2C6a–d, 66–80%)68.

Next, we aimed to test this hypothesis with a variety of enaminonitriles, which exhibited different reactivity patterns (Scheme 1). Replacement of the α-position hydrogen with a nitrile group (e.g. 7) resulted in decomposition of the starting material under our optimized conditions. while the enaminonitrile 8, with a nitrogen atom replacing carbon at the ɣ-position failed to provide the desired product. Surprisingly, when the γ-position was blocked with a chloro group, there was no negative effect, and the substrate smoothly underwent the reaction, furnishing the pyridazine product 4g in 75% yield69.

To gain a better understanding of the reaction pathways, we conducted controlled reactions (Scheme 2A). Initially, we carried out the reaction without both additive (TBAI) and oxidant (TBHP), which resulted in product 11a in 23% yield, instead of the expected pyridazine product (entry 1). This result implied that the tosylhydrazide initially underwent transamidation with dimethylamine, tautomerized to an α,β-unsaturated hydrazone, or underwent 1,6-addition and reduction, resulting in product 11a via the ionic mechanism70,71. The structure of compound 11 was confirmed by XRD analysis (CCDC number : 2389056). In addition, adding TBHP resulted in a slight increase in 41% (entry 2). Next, we aimed to investigate the correlation between sulfonyl hydrazide and oxidant TBHP. The reaction with a 1:4 ratio of these two reagents yielded both the pyridazine product (4c, 23%) and the tosyl α-substituted intermediate (12, 9%) (entry 3). By conducting the reaction with 2.0 equiv. of TBHP at lower temperature of 40°C, we were able to isolate key intermediates, including the non-conjugated hydrazone 13 (mixture, 22%), the conjugated hydrazone 14 (8%), and the desired pyridazine 4c (15%). Under these conditions, we also unexpectedly obtained N-aminopyridinium ylide 15b in 34% yield (entry 4). The formation of non-conjugated hydrazones 13 suggested that transamidation occurred in the first step. When conducting the above reaction at 80 °C, we obtained the expected pyridazine 4c in 18% yield, along with N-aminopyridinium ylide 15b in 48% yield (entry 5).

To identify the reaction intermediates, we conducted various control reactions. Initially, we performed a model reaction with non-conjugated hydrazone 13 under our standard conditions without tosyl hydrazide, resulting in the formation of pyridazine 4c in only 15% yield. This observation indicated that sulfonyl migration partially occurred in the initial radical process72. However, the yield of 4c increased up to 71% when 2.0 equivalents of external tosyl hydrazide (2a) was used (Scheme 2B). On the other hand, when 2.0 equivalents of benzene sulfonyl hydrazide (2b) instead of tosyl hydrazide (2a), were allowed to react with 13 under standard conditions, both the benzene sulfonyl-substituted pyridazine 4o and tosyl migrated product 4c were obtained in a 6:1 ratio (Scheme 2C). These outcomes could imply that i) intra-molecular sulfonyl migration occurred from the non-conjugated hydrazone 13, ii) 13 was an actual intermediate for this reaction and iii) 2.0 equivalents of sulfonyl hydrazide were required to achieve higher yields.

Based on the above control experiments, we also performed the standard reaction conditions without additive (TBAI), which afforded the expected sulfonylation product albeit in low yields (Scheme 2D, 11a–c, 26–41%). Similarly, we achieved the expected N-aminopyridinium ylides in moderate yields when the standard reaction conditions were performed only with 2.0 equivalents of oxidant TBHP (Scheme 2E, 15a–d, 41–48%). Considering the possibility of an ionic mechanism, we attempted the reaction using polar solvents. However, The yields were still low; we obtained the N-aminopyridinium ylides in 37% (DMF) and 7% (DMSO) yields, respectively. The structure of compound 15d was confirmed by single crystal X-ray diffraction (CCDC number: 2216818).

To determine whether the reaction proceeds via a radical mechanism, we conducted radical trapping experiments (Scheme 3A). The addition of free radical scavengers such as TEMPO, BHT, and DPE to our standard reaction conditions suppressed pyridazine formation to 0%, 15%, and 21%, respectively. These results clearly indicate that the sulfonyl radical is likely involved in this reaction and that it proceeds through a radical process. We also evaluated the feasibility of this reaction by performing gram-scale reactions, which efficiently afforded product 3a in 65% yield without significant loss. Similarly, starting from the HWE adduct, treatment with DMF-DMA afforded the enaminonitrile 1b31. After workup, without purification, our optimized reaction conditions afforded pyridazine 4b in 63% yield in two consecutive steps (Scheme 3B).

To expand the scope of synthesized pyridazines, various modifications were carried out (Scheme 3C). Treatment of pyridazine 3a with Zn in acetic acid resulted in the removal of the sulfonyl group, leading to the formation of the pyrrole derivative 16 in 82% yield73. The synthesized pyridazine could act as a diene. For instance, 3a undergoes an inverse electron demand Diels-Alder reaction (IEDDA) with 4-ethynyl anisole in the presence of a catalytic amount of ZnBr2, resulting in the tetra-substituted benzene derivative 17 in 61% yield74. And then the sulfonyl group of pyridazine 3a was successfully utilized as a partner in the Suzuki-Miyaura-cross-coupling reaction with 3,4-dimethoxy phenyl boronic acid, providing the coupling product 18 in 60% yield75. Further functionalization of the cyano groups in 4 g was achieved in the presence of potassium t-butoxide, resulting in the formation of an amide 19 in 57% yield, along with the replacement of the tosyl group by a t-butoxy group. Treatment of 4g with sodium methoxide or sodium azide resulted in the formation of the methoxy-substituted pyridazine 20 in 86% or azide-substituted pyridazine 21 in 93% yield, with the tosyl group being replaced by a methoxy or azide group, while the cyano group remained intact76.

Based on our experimental results and density functional theory (DFT) calculations, as summaraized in Figs. 3 and 4, a plausible reaction mechanism for the synthesis of pyridazine 3 is illustrated in Scheme 4. The transamidation of tosyl hydrazide with enaminonitrile (1) yields intermediate A (13), which then undergoes attack by a t-butoxy radical, generated from TBAI and TBHP, forming a radical intermediate B. This is followed by the attack of a tosyl radical and hydrogen transfer, leading to intermediate C. The radical pathway of C culminates in the formation of a six-membered pyridazine product. Specifically, N-radical formation in intermediate C, induced by a t-butoxy radical, followed by 6-endo-trig cyclization, results in intermediate E. Subsequent dehydrogenation and the consecutive elimination of a complex of tosylate anion and water produce the pyridazine product P1 (i.e., 3a). Alternatively, if intermediate C proceeds via an ionic pathway, the N-aminopyridinium ylide 15 is formed. In this route, the deprotonation of intermediate C by a hydroxide ion generates the cationic intermediate G, which undergoes protonation and subsequent 6-endo-trig cyclization to afford intermediate I. Finally, proton transfer of intermediate I efficiently yields the N-aminopyridinium ylide P2 (i.e.,15a). The placement of radicals in intermediates D and E along the radical pathway was confirmed through DFT calculations of spin densities (described in Figs. 3 and 4).

DFT calculations

To investigate the factors influencing selectivity in the synthesis of sulfonyl-substituted pyridazines, we conducted DFT calculations for the 6-endo-trig cyclization of γ-functionalized enaminonitrile 1a and tosyl hydrazide 2a via radical and ionic pathways, yielding 3a (entry 23, Table 1) and 15a (Scheme 2E), respectively, in toluene at 80 °C. All DFT calculations were performed at the M06-2X/def2-TZVP//M06-2X/6-31 + G(d,p) level of theory, employing the M06-2X functional77 and the polarizable continuum model (PCM)78 to account for solvation free energies in toluene, implemented in the Gaussian 09 program79. The enthalpic and entropic contributions were estimated by frequency calculations at the M06-2X/6-31 + G(d,p) level of theory. For all radical species, the unrestricted M06-2X (UM06-2X) functional was employed for geometry optimizations, frequency calculations, solvation free energy estimations, and single-point energy evaluations. The placement of radicals along the radical pathway was confirmed through DFT calculations of spin densities (i.e., excess α-spin densities). Computational details are described in Supplementary Data 5.

The furnished intermediate enaminonitrile A from 3a can adopt several possible conformations depending on the orientations of nitrile, phenyl and enamine groups (see Supplementary Fig. S2). Initially, species A undergoes a conformational change to form intermediate A1, which is subsequently attacked by a tBuO· radical, leading to the formation of radical intermediate A3 via the transition state ts1, with a barrier of 8.3 kcal/mol. The radical intermediate B is generated by the release of tBuOH from intermediate A3. Intermediate C is formed from intermediate B through a barrierless, concerted process involving the attack of the tosyl radical (Ts·) and a 1,3-hydrogen transfer. The structures of all local minima and transition states are provided in Supplementary Fig. S3, and the free-energy profile for the A-B-C pathway is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. S6.

The structures of intermediate C, transition states (ts11, ts12, ts21, and ts21), and corresponding products (P1 and P2) for radical and ionic pathways in toluene are also shown in Fig. 3. The transition states ts12 and ts21 correspond to the 6-endo-trig cyclizations for D → E via a radical pathway and H0 → I via an ionic pathway, respectively (see Fig. 4). All optimized structures of local minima and transition states along both pathways are depicted in Supplementary Figs. S4 and S5.

Additionally, we compared the structure of the product P1 with the X-ray structure of 3a in Table 1. The torsion angle θ, which defines the orientation of the tosyl group, is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. S7. The X-ray structure 3a was found to be 2.93 kcal/mol less stable than P1 in toluene. Notably, the optimized structure of 3a was isoenergetic to P1, but the torsion angle of the former exhibited an antisymmetric relationship compared to that of the latter, as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. S8. Hence, the local conformation of the tosyl group in the X-ray structure of 3a may be attributed to the transition from θ = – 49° (P1) to θ = 49° (the optimized X-ray structure), and subsequently to θ = 85° (X-ray structure). This transition is likely driven by crystal packing effects, which were not considered in the present calculations of isolated molecules.

The free-energy profiles of the two competing 6-endo-trig cyclization pathways in toluene at 80 °C are shown in Fig. 4. The relative Gibbs free energies were calculated according to the balanced equations for the reactant complex, intermediates, transition states, and product complex listed in Supplementary Table S2. The highest barriers (ΔG‡) were calculated to be 23.0 and 40.9 kcal/mol for transition states ts12 (i.e., D → E) and ts21 (i.e., H0 → I) along the radical and ionic pathways, respectively. This indicates that the radical pathway is kinetically favored by 17.9 kcal/mol over the ionic pathway for the 6-endo-trig cyclization. The second highest barriers were calculated to be 4.3 and 8.7 kcal/mol for transition states ts11 (i.e., the dehydrogenation of C to D) and ts22 (i.e., the proton transfer of I to the final product P2) along the radical and ionic pathways, respectively.

The relative free energies (ΔG) of the final product complexes of P1 and P2 compared to the starting complexes of intermediate C were calculated as –157.8 and –17.7 kcal/mol, respectively. This indicates that the product P1, formed via the radical pathway is 140.1 kcal/mol more stable than the product P2, formed via the ionic pathway. Moreover, the intermediates in the ionic pathway exhibited greater stability compared to the final product, with intermediates C4 and G being 39.7 kcal/mol and 39.0 kcal/mol more stable than the corresponding product P2, respectively. This suggests that intermediates C4 and G form a free-energy well, with a barrier of 85 kcal/mol to the transition state ts21 for the 6-endo-trig cyclization.

Hence, the DFT results suggest that the 6-endo-trig cyclization via the radical pathway is both kinetically and thermodynamically favored over the cyclization via the ionic pathway, yielding the pyridazine P1 (i.e., 3a) as a major product. This finding is consistent with our experimental results described above.

Conclusions

In summary, we have developed a novel method for the synthesis of sulfonyl-substituted pyridazines through radical cyclization, achieving highly substituted sulfonyl pyridazines in good to excellent yields. This reaction offers several advantages, including metal-free mild conditions, compatibility with various functional groups, and the ability to engage heterocycles and sterically hindered groups. The radical pathway facilitated the formation of new C–N and C–S bonds. Additionally, we demonstrated the synthetic utility of pyridazines in useful transformations and successfully conducted gram-scale reactions. Mechanistic investigations, supported by experimental studies and DFT calculations, revealed that the reaction proceeds via a radical mechanism to produce the pyridazine product, while an ionic pathway leads to the formation of N-aminopyridinium ylide products. We anticipate that this approach will serve as a powerful tool for the synthesis of highly substituted pyridazine scaffolds.
