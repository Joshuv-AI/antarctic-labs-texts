Near-optimal entanglement-communication tradeoffs for remote state preparation
Srijita Kundu, Olivier Lalonde

Abstract
We study the following task: Alice is given a classical description of a rank- k k projector P P on ℂ d \mathbb{C}^{d} , and Alice and Bob want to prepare the quantum state P / k P/k on Bob’s side using shared entanglement and classical communication. The general form of this task is known as remote state preparation (RSP). We give nearly-matching lower and upper bounds for the entanglement cost and communication cost for RSP of the states P / k P/k . Ours are the first nearly matching upper and lower bounds for RSP of mixed states, and in the special case of pure states, our lower bound outperforms the best previously known lower bound. Our results show that any pure entangled state that can be used to do RSP of these states with o ⁡ ( d ) o(d) bits of communication, can distill log ⁡ d \log d ebits of entanglement, and conversely, any state that can distill log ⁡ d \log d ebits of entanglement can be used to do RSP of these states efficiently. As applications of our results, we rederive a previously-known incompressibility result for states of the form P / k P/k , and give a new entanglement-assisted communication protocol for the equality function that uses 1 2 ​ log ⁡ n + O ⁡ ( 1 ) \frac{1}{2}\log n+O(1) many ebits, and O ⁡ ( 1 ) O(1) communication.

Introduction

A central goal of quantum information theory is to understand what resources are required to transmit quantum states between a sender and a receiver. There are various settings in which such a transmission can be performed. One setting is the task of
quantum teleportation
, in which the sender Alice holds a quantum state that is unknown to her and wants to transmit this to the receiver Bob. Alice and Bob pre-share an entangled quantum state which can be used as a resource for this task, and moreover, Alice can communicate with Bob using classical bits. One of the foundational results of quantum information theory is that a
d
-dimensional quantum state can be teleported using a
d
-dimensional maximally entangled state (i.e.,
\log d
many EPR pairs or ebits) shared between Alice and Bob, and
2\log d
bits of classical communication
[
8
]
, and that this is the optimal cost.

Moreover, it is known that the tasks of quantum teleportation and
entanglement distillation
are essentially equivalent. In entanglement distillation, Alice and Bob share some arbitrary (potentially mixed) entangled state, and want to distill a maximally entangled state of some dimension from this state, via local operations and classical communication (LOCC). It is easy to show that if some mixed entangled state can be used to teleport
d
-dimensional quantum states with some communication, then Alice and Bob can distill a
d
-dimensional maximally entangled state from this shared state, using the same communication as the teleportation protocol.

\log d+\log\log d+O(1)

1.1
Our results

In this work, we take a closer look at RSP with arbitrary pure states as the shared entanglement. We specifically study RSP protocols for
flat states
. A
d
-dimensional rank-
k
flat state is a mixed state of the form
\frac{P}{k}
, where
P
is a rank-
k
projector on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
(they are so called due to their spectrum being flat at
\frac{1}{k}
). We will call the set of all such states
G(d,k)
, and a protocol for remote state preparing these states will be called a
(d,k)
-RSP protocol.

Flat states are an important class of states in the context of RSP: they were the ensemble studied in
[
19
,
6
]
as well. Moreover, it can be seen that in any one-way communication protocol between Alice and Bob which involves shared EPR pairs projective measurements, the state on Bob’s side at the end of the protocol must be a flat state. Therefore, any protocol of this form is essentially doing RSP of a flat state, and it may be possible to use our results to prove bounds for such protocols.

1.1.1
Lower bound

Our main result shows lower bounds on the entanglement and communication cost of
(d,k)
-RSP protocols. The entanglement measure we use in our lower bound is smoothed entanglement min-entropy, denoted by
H^{\varepsilon}_{\min}(A)_{\sigma}
or
H^{\varepsilon}_{\min}(B)_{\rho}
when Alice and Bob share an entangled state
\ket{\sigma}^{AB}
. This quantity is a one-shot version of the more well-known measure entanglement entropy
H(A)_{\rho}
(or
H(B)_{\rho}
). Although this is only a useful entanglement measure for pure states, it is a stronger measure than the previously studied Schmidt rank, and is related to distillable entanglement for pure states. Moreover, it is possible to extend our result to lower bound the entanglement of formation for mixed entangled states used in RSP; we leave as an open question whether there is a connection to distillable entanglement as well in the mixed state case.

Our resource lower bounds are captured by the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1

(Combined version of Theorems
4.2
and
4.3
)
.

For all
\gamma>0
, any
(d,k)
-RSP protocol with relaxed average error
\varepsilon_{r}
,
m
bits of communication, and initial shared entangled state
\ket{\sigma}^{AB}
, must satisfy

H^{\delta+\gamma}_{\text{min}}(A)_{\sigma}\geq\log d-3\log(1/\gamma)-O(1)

where
\delta=F\left(\frac{k}{d}+O(\sqrt{\frac{m}{d}}),1-\varepsilon_{r}\right)
, and
F
is a truncated version of the fidelity function.
4
4

4

See Section
2
for a formal definition of
F
. What is important to know is that
F
is small if the first argument is much smaller than the second.
Moreover, for any (possibly mixed) shared state, the communication
m
must satisfy

m\geq\left\lfloor\log\frac{d}{k}\right\rfloor+\log(1-\varepsilon_{r}).

The relaxed error measure considered in Theorem
1.1
is among the weakest measures of correctness for RSP protocols (weaker than the average error considered in many other works), and our lower bound holds for protocols satisfying even this weak condition.

Note that in order for
H_{\min}^{\delta+\gamma}(A)_{\sigma}
to be meaningful, the smoothing parameter
\delta+\gamma
has to be smaller than
1
. This means that in order to get a nontrivial lower bound on entanglement for fixed
k
, we must have
m\ll d
, which is what we expect. Moreover, for such an
m
, the function
F
is only smaller than
1
if
k/d<1-\varepsilon_{r}
, i.e.,
\varepsilon_{r}<1-\frac{k}{d}
. This is also what we expect, because there is a trivial protocol achieving error
1-k/d
, in which there is no entanglement or communication and Bob always outputs the
d
-dimensional maximally mixed state.

Theorem
1.1
is an improvement over the result of
[
6
]
in several ways. Firstly, the entanglement measure we use is stronger; in fact, one open question in their work was to find a lower bound on entanglement cost stronger than Schmidt rank. Secondly, for protocols with average error
\varepsilon/2
, the communication cost + entanglement cost lower bound in their result is
\log d-3\log(1/(1-\varepsilon))-O(1)
, so they do not get any nontrivial bound on entanglement at all for protocols with
\log d
communication. On the other hand, our lower bound is nontrivial all the way up to
m
only being smaller than
d
by a constant factor (depending on the value of
k

[
6
]
do show an additional entanglement lower bound of
\log d-\frac{1}{2}\log k-O(1)
for protocols with optimal communication, but our lower bound also outperforms this.
In the special case where
k=1
and we are considering pure states, our entanglement lower bound is an improvement over the result of
[
11
]
due to our stronger measure.

Our result shows a connection between communication-efficient RSP and entanglement distillation, due to the fact that
H^{\varepsilon}_{\min}(A)_{\rho}
captures distillable entanglement for pure states. It is known due to
[
35
]
that there exists a one-way entanglement distillation protocol between Alice and Bob which distills
H^{\varepsilon}_{\min}(A)_{\sigma}-2\log(1/\varepsilon)-O(1)
many EPR pairs from their shared pure entangled state
\ket{\sigma}^{AB}
. Moreover, this rate of entanglement distillation is asymptotically optimal — it was shown in
[
7

that the number of EPR pairs that can be distilled from
\ket{\sigma}^{AB}
in the asymptotic i.i.d. setting is
H(A)_{\sigma}
(which
H^{\varepsilon}_{\min}(A)_{\sigma}
converges to). Previous results do not show such a connection, because it is not possible to distill EPR pairs up to the Schmidt rank of a pure entangled state.

1.1.2
Upper bounds

We also give efficient protocols for
(d,k)
-RSP that nearly match our communication and entanglement lower bounds. We give two different protocols: one where the entanglement cost is nearly-optimal and the communication cost is suboptimal (though not drastically so), and one whose communication cost matches exactly that of the best-known protocol in the average case but which requires slightly more entanglement than the first protocol.

Theorem 1.2
.

For all
d\geq k
and for all
\varepsilon>0
, there exist
(d,k)
-RSP protocols with worst-case error
3\varepsilon
, with the following parameters:

1.

A protocol which uses a maximally entangled state of local dimension
d
, and has communication
m=\log\frac{d}{k}+\log\log d+6\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+2\log\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+O(1)
;

2.

A protocol which uses a maximally entangled state of local dimension
O\left(\frac{d}{\varepsilon}\right)
, and has communication
m=\log\frac{d}{k}+3\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+2\log\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+O(1)

The first protocol in Theorem
1.2

1.1

It is also worth noting that the protocols in Theorem
1.2

In fact, the way we design these protocols is by designing protocols with average-case correctness first (because this is easier). (d,k)

Our protocols do take slight hits in communication cost when going through this average-case to worst-case reduction. The average-case version of our first protocol actually only has one-sided error, which means either Alice and Bob fail in the protocol and they know they have failed, or they succeed and in that case the state produced by Bob is exactly the target (see Theorem
3.4

1.1.3
Applications

First, as an application of our entanglement lower bound for
(d,k)
-RSP protocols, we rederive a result of
[
6
]
on the impossibility of visible compression for an ensemble of flat states. This is the main result of
[

[
6

Theorem 1.4
.

For any
\eta>0
, there exists
d_{0}
and
C>0
such that for all
(k,d,\varepsilon)
with
d\geq d_{0}
and
\varepsilon<1-\frac{k}{d}-\eta
, any visible
(d^{\prime},\varepsilon)
-compression scheme for the ensemble of all flat states in
G(d,k)
must satisfy

\log d^{\prime}\geq\log d-C.

This means that this ensemble cannot be compressed by more than a constant number of qubits.

Visible
(d^{\prime},\varepsilon)
-compression schemes are formally defined in Section
5.1
, but it essentially means a compression scheme where the compressor has a full description of the state to be compressed, rather than only having the ability to apply a CPTP map to a copy of the state. Our Theorem
1.4
is a bit stronger than the result of
[
6
]
because we show incompressibility for any nontrivial error tolerance, whereas
[
6
]
only gave this result for
\varepsilon\leq\frac{1}{2}
. The only downside of our result compared to theirs is that they prove the existence of a small finite ensemble of flat states which is incompressible, whereas our ensemble is essentially the whole of
G(d,k)
(which can be discretized into an extremely large but finite ensemble via an
\varepsilon
-net). We note however that the proof technique of our entanglement lower bound Theorem
4.3
(see Lemma
4.4
) can in fact yield the existence of an ensemble that isn’t too large (of size polynomial in
d
, let’s say) and which is still incompressible.

Additionally, as an application of our efficient
(d,k)
-RSP protocols, we give an entanglement-optimal bounded error protocol for the well-studied equality function on
n
bits. In
\mathrm{EQ}_{n}
, Alice and Bob are given inputs
x,y\in\{0,1\}^{n}
and want to communicate to determine whether these strings are equal or not. In the entanglement-assisted quantum communication setting, Alice and Bob pre-share entanglement and want to communicate classically in order to do this.

It is known that equality can be solved with
O(1)
communication with either shared randomness or shared entanglement. However, the most well-known entanglement-assisted protocol for this uses
\log n
ebits of shared entanglement (and these ebits are just used to generate shared randomness and then implement the best classical protocol).

We show that it is possible to halve the number of EPR pairs by using them in a genuinely quantum way. The
n
-dependence in the result is optimal, as it is known that
\frac{1}{2}\log n+O(1)
qubits of communication are both necessary and sufficient to compute the equality function with constant error probability
[
24
]
. Our result is a reproof of their upper bound, using our efficient RSP protocol.

Theorem 1.5
.

For all
\varepsilon>0
, there exists an entanglement-assisted protocol for
\mathrm{EQ}_{n}
with worst-case error probability
\varepsilon
which uses
\frac{1}{2}\log n+O(\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon})
shared EPR pairs and
O(\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon})
classical communication.

It should be noted that our result is slightly worse than the upper bound of
[
24
]
in that the
\varepsilon

1.2
Our techniques

1.2.1
Lower bound

Our communication lower bound is easy to derive: it just follows from the fact that flat states in
G(d,k)
can be used to send
\log\frac{d}{k}
bits of information. We’ll focus our attention on the proof of entanglement lower bound. Due to the fact that the entanglement min-entropy measure has not been considered before in the literature for proving entanglement lower bounds for RSP, our techniques here are quite different from existing techniques in the literature.

In order to derive a lower bound on
H_{\min}^{\varepsilon}(A)_{\sigma}
, we need to control the Schmidt spectrum of the initial shared state
\ket{\sigma}^{AB}
. The correctness condition of the protocol only tells us about the state of one of Bob’s registers at the end of the protocol, so we need to connect the spectrum of the initial state
\sigma
of the protocol to the spectrum of ensemble of final states
(p(c|P),\chi_{P,c})
for each possible message
c
Alice can send to Bob. Here
p(c|P)
is the probability of the message
c
when Alice’s input is
P
, and
\chi_{P,c}
is the final state of the protocol for this input and message. We can connect the spectrum of
\sigma
to the spectrum of this ensemble via the well-known result in
[
28
]
, which says that since the transformation is done via LOCC, the Schmidt spectrum of
\sigma
must be majorized by the ensemble average of the Schmidt spectra of
\chi_{P,c}
.

Moreover, we need to make use of the fact that the marginals of
\chi_{P,c}
on Bob’s target register are on average (over
c
and
P
) close to
\frac{P}{k}
. We’ll do this by employing the trick used to distill entanglement from a teleportation protocol: we’ll run the protocol in superposition over Alice’s input
P
, though this superposition will be over all of
G(d,k)
instead of just a basis.
6
6

6

For technical reasons, we actually need to do a finite version of this infinite superposition.
This will let us compare the spectrum of
\sigma
to the spectrum of the ensemble
(p(c),\rho_{c})
, where
\rho_{c}
the state
\chi_{P,c}
on Bob’s register averaged over
P
from the Haar measure in
G(d,k)
conditioned on the message being
c
. This is because the reduced state on Bob’s side for message
c
actually is
\rho_{c}
in the superposition protocol. These states are much easier to handle than the
\chi_{P,c}
states for arbitrary
P
.

We notice that if the averaging over
P
in the definition of
\rho_{c}
had been according to the Haar measure instead of the Haar measure conditioned on
c
, then the spectrum of
\rho_{c}
would straightforwardly have been like the maximally mixed state
\frac{I}{d}
(which has
\log d
min-entropy). This is because averaging
\frac{P}{k}
over the Haar measure gives
\frac{I}{d}
, and correctness requires that Bob register in
\chi_{P,c}
is close to
\frac{P}{k}
.
7
7

7

Relaxed average-case correctness requires something a bit weaker than this, but we’ll ignore this difference for now.
Now the actual averaging in
\rho_{c}
is conditioned on a particular
c
, but this can’t make things too bad. This is because
c
is
m
-bits long, and conditioning on a particular
c
changes probabilities by a multiplicative factor of at most
2^{m}
. This is not too bad when
m
is not too large. Using some tricks, it is possible to upper bound the spectrum of
\rho_{c}
(for most
c
) by what the spectrum would have been with Haar-averaging, along with an additive factor of
O(\sqrt{\frac{m}{d}})
. This in turn means that the spectrum of
\rho_{c}
is close to the spectrum of the maximally mixed state, with the closeness or smoothing term having a dependence on
O(\sqrt{\frac{m}{d}})
. This lets us lower bound the
H^{\gamma+\delta}_{\min}
of
\rho_{c}
on average over
c
, which lets us get the lower bound for
\sigma
.

1.2.2
Upper bound

Our first protocol is a generalization of the protocol of
[
11
]
to flat states. It is not too difficult to do this generalization, and we get the same entanglement and communication cost as
[
11
]
for pure states for this reason. We focus on the second protocol, which is more novel. We will first describe the average-case version of our second protocol, and then describe the average-case to worst-case reduction.

Second protocol, average-case.

This protocol can be described as a more refined version of the rejection sampling protocol that has appeared many times in the literature
[
19
,
5
,
6
]
. The idea behind rejection sampling is the following: suppose Alice and Bob share
N
copies of the maximally entangled state on
\ket{\Phi_{+}}
on
\mathbb{C}^{d}\times\mathbb{C}^{d}
. If Alice, on input
P
, measures in the basis
\{\overline{P},I-\overline{P}\}
on her half of each of these copies, and gets the outcome corresponding to
\overline{P}
on one copy, then due to the nature of the maximally entangled state, Bob’s residual state on his half of the corresponding copy will be
\frac{P}{k}
. Of course, Bob does not know which copy Alice succeeded on, so Alice has to send the index
i\in[N]
in order for him to output that register. The probability of Alice not obtaining outcome
\overline{P}
in any of the copies, and therefore this procedure failing, is
(\operatorname{Tr}((I-\overline{P})\ket{\Phi_{+}}\bra{\Phi_{+}}))^{N}=\left(1-\frac{k}{d}\right)^{N}
, due to
P
being a rank-
k
matrix. Taking
N=\frac{d}{k}\ln\frac{1}{\varepsilon}
makes this probability at most
\varepsilon
, and gives the communication and entanglement cost of this protocol that we’ve mentioned previously.

We notice that the most important thing in this analysis was the fact that the probability of Alice not succeeding in each iteration was
1-\frac{k}{d}
. This is true if Alice measures a fixed projector
\overline{P}
on her half of a maximally entangled state, but it’s also true if Alice measures a uniformly random rank-
k
projector on her half of any state. In particular, if she kept on measuring in the basis

Average-case to worst-case reduction.

Our average-case to worst-case reduction will use a similar idea of picking some good unitaries

1.3
Open problems

1.3.1
Extension of lower bound to mixed states

The most obvious open problem left by our work is to non-trivially extend our entanglement lower bound to mixed entangled states. There is a panoply of entanglement measures for mixed state entanglement. One such measure is entanglement of formation, and our result actually can be extended to lower bound the entanglement of formation for mixed states. Entanglement of formation of a mixed state
\sigma^{AB}
is defined as

E_{F}(\sigma^{AB})=\inf\left\{\sum_{i}p_{i}H(A)_{\psi_{i}}:\sigma^{AB}=\sum_{i}p_{i}\ket{\psi_{i}}\bra{\psi_{i}}^{AB}\right\},

where the infimum is taken over all convex combinations of pure states that equal
\sigma^{AB}
.
Since we can asymptotically lower bound entanglement entropy of pure states usable for RSP, we can asymptotically lower bound the entanglement of formation by lower bounding the entanglement entropy of each pure state in any convex combination (see e.g.
[
4
]
where employ such a strategy to test entanglement of formation of mixed states).

A more intriguing question is whether there is a connection between usability for efficient RSP and entanglement distillability for mixed states. Note that unlike pure states, where every entangled state has some amount of distillable entanglement, not all mixed entangled states have distillable entanglement. So for example, there exist states that have non-zero entanglement of formation but zero distillable entanglement. Moreover, there exist mixed entangled states that are useful in various ways, e.g. for violating Bell inequalities, which do not have distillable entanglement
[
34
]
.

The entanglement distillation result of
[
35
]
also works for mixed states — their protocol can distill
H^{\varepsilon}_{\min}(A|E)_{\sigma}-2\log(1/\varepsilon)-O(1)
many EPR pairs from a mixed entangled state
\sigma^{AB}
, where
H^{\varepsilon}_{\min}(A|E)_{\sigma}
is a conditional smoothed min-entropy and
\ket{\sigma}^{ABE}
is a purification of
\sigma^{AB}
. If our result could be extended to a lower bound on
H^{\varepsilon}_{\min}(A|E)_{\sigma}
for mixed entanglement, it would show that entangled states that are useful for doing RSP efficiently do have distillable entanglement, which would be a nontrivial result about mixed state entanglement.

1.3.2
Improvement of upper bounds

The protocols we construct for
(d,k)
-RSP are nearly but not exactly optimal. In particular, we suspect it should be possible to give a protocol which has
\log\frac{d}{k}+O(\log(1/\varepsilon))
communication cost and
\log d
ebit cost. So it remains open whether the
\log\log d
additive factor in the communication can be removed from our first protocol, or the additive
\log(1/\varepsilon)
can be removed from the ebit cost of our second protocol.

Another intriguing question is whether our protocols can be made computationally efficient.
[
3
]
give protocols that are worse than ours in terms of communication and entanglement cost, but their protocols are computationally efficient, essentially due to considering a computationally efficient version of the Convex Split Lemma (which they use analogously to how we use the Decoupling Theorem). It is plausible that our protocols can also be made efficient by considering efficient decoupling
[
16
]
.

Definition 2.1

(Schatten

The trace distance between two quantum states with density matrices

Fact 2.3

Given two states

\|\rho-\sigma\|_{\mathrm{tr}}=\sup_{P\text{ a projector}}\operatorname{Tr}(P(\rho-\sigma))

Definition 2.4

\mathsf{F}(\rho,\sigma)=\left[\operatorname{Tr}\left(\sqrt{\sqrt{\rho}\sigma\sqrt{\rho}}\right)\right]^{2}.

Definition 2.5

(Truncated fidelity function)

For

F(x,y)=\begin{cases}\left(\sqrt{xy}+\sqrt{(1-x)(1-y)}\right)^{2}&\text{ if }x\leq y\\

Lemma 2.6

The truncated fidelity function

Additionally, we prove the following.

Lemma 2.7
.

For all

\Delta\in[0,1-x_{0}]

F(x_{0}+\Delta,y_{0})-K\Delta^{2}\leq F(x_{0},y_{0})+O(K^{-1/3}).

We have:

\displaystyle F(x_{0}+\Delta,y_{0})-F(x_{0},y_{0})

\displaystyle\leq(2y-1)\Delta+2\sqrt{y(1-y)}\left(\sqrt{(x_{0}+\Delta)(1-x_{0}-\Delta)}-\sqrt{x_{0}(1-x_{0})}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\Delta+\sqrt{(x_{0}+\Delta)(1-x_{0}-\Delta)}-\sqrt{x_{0}(1-x_{0})}

Definition 2.8

(Min-entropy and Renyi

Definition 2.11

(Smoothed conditional min-entropy)

For

We have the following equivalent characterization of classical smoothed min-entropy.

Lemma 2.12
.

Given a probability distribution
p=\{p_{i}\}_{i}
, for all
\delta\in[0,1]
, we have that
H^{\delta}_{\min}(p)=\log(1/S^{*})
, where

S^{*}=\inf\left\{S:\sum_{i:\;p_{i}>S}(p_{i}-S)\leq\delta\right\}

Proof.

We first show that given the optimal value

, there is a distribution

such that

, and

. In fact

for all

\sum_{i}|q_{i}-p_{i}|=2\sum_{i:p_{i}>q_{i}}(p_{i}-q_{i})=2\sum_{i:p_{i}>S^{*}}(p_{i}-S^{*})=2\delta.

To show the other direction, suppose we have

such that

. Since

\sum_{i:p_{i}>S^{\prime}}(p_{i}-S^{\prime})\leq\sum_{i:p_{i}>S^{\prime}}(p_{i}-q_{i})\leq\sum_{i:p_{i}>q_{i}}(p_{i}-q_{i})\leq\delta.

This completes the proof.

2.4
Entanglement and LOCC

Fact 2.26

(Schmidt decomposition)
.

A pure entangled state
\ket{\psi}^{AB}
on registers
A
and
B
can be written as

\ket{\psi}^{AB}=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\ket{a_{i}}^{A}\ket{b_{i}}^{B},

where
\ket{a_{i}}^{A}
and
\ket{b_{i}}^{A}
are orthonormal states and
\lambda_{i}
are non-negative real numbers satisfying
\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{2}=1
, which are unique up to reordering. The above expression is called the Schmidt decomposition of the state
\ket{\psi}
.
\{\lambda_{i}^{2}\}_{i}
is a probability distribution called its Schmidt spectrum, and this is equal to the eigenvalues of the marginal states
\psi^{A}
and
\psi^{B}
. In what follows, the Schmidt spectrum will always be assumed to be sorted in descending order.

Definition 2.27

(Entanglement min-entropy)
.

For a pure entangled state
\ket{\psi}^{AB}
, its
\varepsilon
-smoothed entanglement min-entropy is equal to either
H^{\varepsilon}_{\min}(A)_{\psi}
or
H^{\varepsilon}_{\min}(B)_{\psi}
. It can be seen that both these quantities are equal to the
\varepsilon
-smoothed min-entropy of its Schmidt spectrum.

Entanglement min-entropy is a lower bound on distillable entanglement from a pure state, as we shall see below.

Definition 2.28

(Entanglement distillation)
.

An entanglement distillation protocol between two parties Alice and Bob, who start with an arbitrary initial shared entangled state
\rho^{AB}
, is a protocol utilizing only local operations and classical communication (LOCC) between the parties, which ends with Alice and Bob sharing the state
\sigma^{AB}
satisfying

\mathsf{F}(\sigma^{AB},\ket{\Phi_{+}}\bra{\Phi_{+}}^{AB})\geq 1-\varepsilon,

where
\ket{\Phi_{+}}\bra{\Phi_{+}}^{AB}
is the maximally entangled state on
AB
of rank
d
, for some
d
. The parameter
\varepsilon
is called the error of the protocol.

Fact 2.29

(
[
35
]
, Proposition 21)
.

For
\eta\in[0,\sqrt{\varepsilon})
, there exists a one-way
\varepsilon
-error entanglement distillation protocol between Alice and Bob who share the entangled state
\rho^{AB}
, utilizing classical communication only from Alice to Bob, yielding a maximally entangled state of rank
d
, for

\log d=H_{\min}^{\sqrt{\varepsilon}-\eta}(A|E)_{\rho}-4\log(1/\eta).

Here the register
E
is the purifying register in an arbitrary purification
\ket{\rho}^{ABE}
of
\rho^{AB}
.

If
\rho^{AB}
is a pure state, the entanglement distillation procedure from this theorem yields
H^{\sqrt{\varepsilon}-\eta}_{\min}(A)_{\rho}-4\log(1/\eta)
many EPR pairs. We’ll also note that inspecting the proof of Proposition 21 in
[
35
]
reveals that their protocol works fine with
H_{2}
instead of
H_{\min}
. So the actual lower bound on one-way distillable entanglement is
H_{2}^{\sqrt{\varepsilon}-\eta}(A|E)_{\rho}-4\log(1/\eta)
for mixed states, and
H_{2}^{\sqrt{\varepsilon}-\eta}(A)_{\rho}-4\log(1/\eta)
for pure states.

Finally, we will use a result characterizing when a pure entangled state can be transformed into an ensemble of pure entangled states via LOCC. In this scenario, we are considering implementing a measurement that gives the
i
-th outcome with probability
p_{i}
, and the post-measurement joint state when the
i
-th outcome is obtained is
\ket{\phi_{i}}
. We use
\{(p_{i},\ket{\phi_{i}}\bra{\phi_{i}})\}_{i}
to denote the outcome ensemble.

To state the result, we need the concept of majorization.

Definition 2.30
.

Let
x
and
y
be two
d
-dimensional vectors whose components are sorted in decreasing order, i.e,
x_{1}\geq x_{2}\geq\ldots\geq x_{d}
. We say that
x
majorizes
y
, denoted by
x\succeq y
, iff

\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}y_{i}\quad\forall k\in[d].

For vectors that are not arranged in descending order, we need to arrange them in descending order first before checking if the majorization condition is satisfied.

Fact 2.31

(
[
23
]
, see also
[
28
]
, Theorem 16)
.

Take
\ket{\psi}
to be a pure state and take
\{(p_{i},\ket{\phi_{i}}\bra{\phi_{i}})\}
to be an ensemble. Letting
\lambda
be the Schmidt spectrum of
\psi
and the
\mu_{i}
be those of the
\ket{\phi_{i}}\bra{\phi_{i}}
(all sorted in descending order), the transformation
\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}\rightarrow\{(p_{i},\ket{\phi_{i}}\bra{\phi_{i}})\}

2.5
Remote state preparation

In a remote state preparation setting, Alice gets a classical description of a quantum state. Alice and Bob share entanglement and do local operations and classical communication on their halves of it. The requirement is that at the end of the protocol, Bob should have a state close to Alice’s input state. This closeness can be measured in different ways, either in the worst-case or on average.

In general, remote state preparation protocols could have two-way communication, and this may be especially important in cases when the shared entangled state between Alice and Bob is mixed. This is because there exist mixed entangled states from which one can distill EPR pairs with two-way communication, but not one-way communication
[
10
]
. Correspondingly, there may be mixed entangled states with which RSP with efficient communication is possible in the two-way case but not the one-way case.

However, it is more common to consider RSP protocols with one-way communication in the literature, and we shall stick to this too, for convenience. In any case, we only consider pure entangled states in this work, and allowing two-way communication may not make much difference for pure states. However, our lower bound result does also work for protocols with two-way communication, as long as we replace the size of Alice’s message to Bob with the total size of the transcript. This is easy to see for our communication lower bound, but more tedious for the entanglement lower bound.

We describe the general form of an RSP protocol for preparing flat states
\frac{P}{k}
for
P\in G(d,k)
below. We call this a
(d,k)
-RSP protocol. We also introduce three different notions of error for such protocols.

Parameters.

•

Target dimension:
d

•

Target rank:
k

•

Classical message length:
m

Input.

Alice is given a classical description of a rank-
k
projector
P
on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
.

Protocol.

Alice and Bob initially share a pure entangled state
\ket{\sigma}^{AB}

1.

Alice performs an isometry
U_{P}:A\mapsto A^{\prime}C
, with the register
C
being of dimension
2^{m}
.

2.

Alice measures
C
and sends the measurement outcome

to Bob.

3.

, with

being of dimension

. For message

is
\ket{\chi_{P,c}}^{A^{\prime}B_{1}B_{2}}
, and its marginal on the target register

is
\chi^{B_{2}}_{P,c}

Error measures.

1.

\mathbb{E}_{P,c}(\|\chi^{B_{2}}_{P,c}-P/k\|_{\mathrm{tr}}).

\mathbb{E}_{P,c}\operatorname{Tr}((I-P)\chi^{B_{2}}_{P,C}).

In order to prove Theorem

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
P_{0}
be a fixed projector in

, and define the function

by:

f(P)=\|P/k-P_{0}/k\|_{\mathrm{tr}}.

Then,
f
is
\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2k}}\right)
-Lipschitz.

Proof.

For all
P,Q\in G(d,k)
, it holds

\displaystyle|f(P)-f(Q)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2k}\|P-Q\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2k}\sqrt{2k}\|P-Q\|_{2}

where the reverse triangle inequality was used for the first inequality and the fact that
P-Q
has rank at most
2k
was used in the second.
∎

This lets us derive the following isoperimetric inequality for
G(d,k)
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
A
be a measurable subset of
G(d,k)
and let
\mu(A)
be its measure under the Haar measure. Define the distance function
f_{A}:G(d,k)\to\mathbb{R}
by:

f_{A}(P)=\inf_{Q\in A}(\|P/k-Q/k\|_{\mathrm{tr}}).

There exists a universal constant
c^{\prime\prime}>0
such that, for all
t>0
,

\Pr[f_{A}(P)\geq t]\leq\exp(-c^{\prime\prime}dkt^{2}\mu(A)^{2}).

Proof.

Note first that it follows from Lemma
3.1
along with Fact
2.20
that
f_{A}
is
\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2k}}\right)
-Lipschitz. Given
t>0
, define the function
g_{t}:G(d,k)\to\mathbb{R}
by:

g_{t}(P)=\max\left(0,1-\frac{f_{A}(P)}{t}\right).

Note that
g_{t}(P)=1
if and only if
P\in\overline{A}
and
g_{t}(P)=0
if and only if
f_{A}(P)\geq t
. We have that
g_{t}
is
\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2k}t}\right)
-Lipschitz in view of Fact
2.20
. We also have that
\mathbb{E}[g_{t}(P)]\geq\mu(A)
, since in the set
A
,
f_{A}(P)=0
, and
g_{t}(P)
takes value

. This all gives:

\displaystyle\Pr[f_{A}(P)\geq t]

\displaystyle=\Pr[g_{t}(P)=0]

\displaystyle\leq\Pr[|g_{t}(P)-\mathbb{E}[g_{t}(P)]|\geq\mu(A)]

\displaystyle\leq\exp(-2c^{\prime}dkt^{2}\mu(A)^{2})

where Lemma
2.22
was applied in the last line. The statement follows.
∎

3.3
below, which lets us pick an appropriate choice of

.

Theorem 3.3
.

In the protocol
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
in Figure
2
, it is enough to take
N=O\left(\frac{\log 1/\delta}{\delta^{4}}\right)
.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon(P)
be the error of
\mathcal{P}
on input
P
, and set:

\varepsilon_{\delta/4}(P)=\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{N}\cap B(P,\delta/4)}(\varepsilon(Q)).

We claim that
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
will have have worst-case error at most
\varepsilon_{a}+\delta
if the unitaries
\mathcal{U}
are such that, for every
P^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}
,

\min_{i\in[N]}\varepsilon_{\frac{\delta}{4}}(U_{i}P^{\prime}U_{i}^{\dagger})<\varepsilon_{a}+\frac{\delta}{2}.

(1)

On input
P
,
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
then prepares

that is
\delta/2
-close to
P
up to distance
\varepsilon_{a}+\delta/2
. Therefore, the overall distance between
P
and Bob’s output on

is
\varepsilon_{a}+\delta
.

The existence of such a choice of unitaries
\mathcal{U}
will now be shown using the probabilistic method. It is enough to show that for a specific choice of

, if
\mathcal{U}
is chosen at random by selecting each

independently from the Haar measure, the probability that equation (
1

\frac{1}{|\mathcal{N}|}

\mathcal{N}

A=\left\{Q:\varepsilon_{\delta/4}(Q)<\varepsilon_{a}+\frac{\delta}{2}\right\}

In this section, we will give our first protocol for RSP of flat states with average-case error, proving the following theorem.

Theorem 3.4
.

For every
\varepsilon_{a}>0
, there exists a

Parameters.

Theorem 3.5
.

Such a choice of
\mathcal{U}
exists if we take
N=\Theta(\frac{d\log d}{k\varepsilon_{a}^{2}})
.

Proof.

With the choice of
N
in the lemma statement, the existence of an appropriate collection
\mathcal{U}
is shown using the probabilistic method. For a given choice of
\mathcal{U}
and a given choice of
P
, define
\Lambda(\mathcal{U}.P)
to be the spectral norm of
M
defined in equation (
2
) for a fixed
\mathcal{U}
and
P

\Lambda(\mathcal{U},P)=\left\|\frac{d}{kN}\sum_{i}U_{i}\overline{P}U_{i}^{\dagger}\right\|_{\infty}.

We want to show the existence of a collection of unitaries
\mathcal{U}
such that

\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[1-\frac{1}{\Lambda(\mathcal{U},P)}\right]<\varepsilon_{a}.

Note that since
\Lambda(\mathcal{U},P)\geq 1
always (since
M
has trace
d
), we get

\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[1-\frac{1}{\Lambda(\mathcal{U},P)}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}_{P}[\Lambda(\mathcal{U},P)]-1.

We will pick the elements of
\mathcal{U}
i.i.d. from the Haar measure, and show that

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{E}_{P}[\Lambda(\mathcal{U},P)]<1+\varepsilon_{a}.

This, by the probabilistic method, implies that there exists a choice of
\mathcal{U}
such that
\mathbb{E}_{P}[\Lambda(\mathcal{U},P)]
is at most

, which gives us the required average probability of the error outcome

P_{1},\ldots,P_{N}
to be independent uniformly random elements of

and defining the random variable

by

X=\left\|\frac{d}{kN}\sum_{i}P_{i}\right\|_{\infty}

we have that

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{E}_{P}[\Lambda(\mathcal{U},P)]=\mathbb{E}[X].

This expectation can now be estimated using the operator Chernoff bound (Fact
2.18
). Firstly, it is easy to see that for any

\mathbb{E}_{P_{i}}\left[\frac{d}{k}P_{i}\right]

\Pr\left[X>1+t\right]=\Pr_{P_{1},\ldots,P_{N}}\left[\frac{d}{kN}\sum_{i}P_{i}\not\preceq\left(1+t\right)I\right]\leq 2d\cdot\exp\left(-Kt^{2}\right).

t_{0}=\sqrt{\frac{\ln d}{K}}
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X]

\displaystyle=1+\int_{0}^{\infty}\Pr[X>1+t]dt

\displaystyle\leq 1+t_{0}+\int_{t_{0}}^{\infty}\Pr[X>1+t]dt

\displaystyle\leq 1+t_{0}+\int_{t_{0}}^{\infty}2d\cdot\exp(-Kt^{2})dt

\displaystyle\leq 1+t_{0}+\frac{d}{Kt_{0}}\exp(-Kt_{0}^{2})

\displaystyle=1+t_{0}+\frac{1}{Kt_{0}}

where Fact
2.14
was used in the second to last inequality. It can be seen that our choice of
N
makes this quantity at most
1+\varepsilon_{a}
, as desired.
∎

3.3
Decoupling with post-selection

In this section, we prove a technical result that will help us in proving the correctness of our second protocol, given in Section
3.4
. The technical result is a generalization of the well-known Decoupling Theorem, the standard version of which has numerous applications in quantum information. The most general version of the standard Decoupling Theorem without smoothing can be stated as follows.

Fact 3.6

(Decoupling Theorem,
[
15
]
)
.

Let
\rho^{AE}
be any state and
\mathcal{T}^{A\to A_{1}A_{2}}
be any CPTP map. Then,

\mathbb{E}_{U^{A}}\left[\left\|\mathcal{T}^{A\to A_{1}A_{2}}(U^{A}\rho^{AE}(U^{A})^{\dagger})-\tau^{A_{1}A_{2}}\otimes\rho^{E}\right\|_{1}\right]\leq 2^{-\frac{1}{2}H_{2}(A|E)_{\rho}-\frac{1}{2}H_{2}(B|A_{1}A_{2})_{\tau}},

where the expectation is taken over Haar-random
U^{A}
in
U(A)
, and the state
\tau
is given by
\tau_{A_{1}A_{2}B}=\mathcal{T}^{A\to A_{1}A_{2}}(\ket{\Phi_{+}}\bra{\Phi_{+}}^{AB})
.

When the CPTP map
\mathcal{T}^{A_{1}A_{2}}
is the partial trace over
A_{1}
, we get the following special case of the Decoupling Theorem. We state an upper bound on the Schatten
2
-norm as well as the
1
-norm in this case.

Fact 3.7

(See e.g. (
[
30
]
, equation 10.343))
.

For any state
\rho^{A_{1}A_{2}E}
with
A=A_{1}A_{2}
,
\dim(A_{1})=d_{1}
and
\dim(A_{2})=d_{2}
,

\mathbb{E}_{U^{A}}\left[\left\|\operatorname{Tr}_{A_{1}}(U^{A}\rho^{AE}(U^{A})^{\dagger})-\frac{I}{d_{2}}\otimes\rho^{E}\right\|_{2}\right]\leq\sqrt{\frac{2^{-H_{2}(A|E)_{\rho}}}{d_{1}}},

and hence

\mathbb{E}_{U^{A}}\left[\left\|\operatorname{Tr}_{A_{1}}(U^{A}\rho^{AE}(U^{A})^{\dagger})-\frac{I}{d_{2}}\otimes\rho^{E}\right\|_{1}\right]\leq\sqrt{\frac{d_{2}\cdot 2^{-H_{2}(A|E)_{\rho}}}{d_{1}}}.

Since applying a measurement and post-selecting on an outcome is not a CPTP map, it is not clear from Fact
3.6
that a decoupling-like result holds true when we apply a random unitary on a state, then apply a measurement and post-select on its outcome (since such an operation is not linear), which is what we will need in Section
3.4
. In this section, we prove such a result.

Theorem 3.8

(Decoupling with post-selection)
.

Let
\rho^{A_{1}A_{2}E}
be a quantum state on registers
A_{1}A_{2}E
, with
\dim(A_{1})=d_{1}
,
\dim(A_{2})=d_{2}
, and let
P
be a fixed rank-
k
projector on
\mathbb{C}^{d_{2}}
. For a unitary
U\in U(A_{1}A_{2})
, let
p(U,\rho)=\operatorname{Tr}(I^{A_{1}E}\otimes P^{A_{2}}(U\rho^{A_{1}A_{2}E}U^{\dagger}))
denote the probability of obtaining the
P
outcome on doing the measurement
\{P^{A_{2}},I^{A_{2}}-P^{A_{2}}\}
on the state
\rho^{AE}
. Then, when
U
is uniformly random over
U(A_{1}A_{2})
,

\mathbb{E}_{U}\left[p(U,\rho)\left\|\frac{P^{A_{2}}\left(\operatorname{Tr}_{A_{1}}\left(U\rho^{A_{1}A_{2}E}U^{\dagger}\right)\right)P^{A_{2}}}{p(U,\rho)}-\frac{P^{A_{2}}}{k}\otimes\rho^{E}\right\|_{1}\right]\leq k\cdot\sqrt{\frac{2^{-H_{2}(A_{1}A_{2}|E)_{\rho}}}{d_{1}d_{2}}}+C\sqrt{\frac{2^{-H_{2}(A_{1}A_{2})_{\rho}}}{d_{1}d_{2}}},

for some universal constant
C
.

The first state in the Schatten
1
-norm in the above expression is the state obtained after post-selecting on the outcome
P
after doing the measurement
\{P,I-P\}
in the
A_{2}
register of the state
\operatorname{Tr}_{A_{1}}(U\rho^{AE}U^{\dagger})
. The above theorem is essentially saying that when normalized by the probability of obtaining the outcome
P
in the first place, the post-selected state is close to the state
\frac{P^{A_{2}}}{k}\otimes\rho^{E}
. The closeness is to
\frac{P^{A_{2}}}{k}\otimes\rho^{E}
instead of
\frac{I^{A_{2}}}{d_{2}}\otimes\rho^{E}
like in Fact
3.7
because of the post-selection on the measurement outcome — the state collapses to the support of
P
after the measurement, and the expected probability of this happening is
\frac{k}{d}
. For our application, we will in fact only need Theorem
3.8
in the special case of the register
E
being empty. But we state and prove it here in this general form, since it may be of independent interest.

Before proving Theorem
3.8
, we prove two intermediary lemmas.

Lemma 3.9
.

If
A
is a
d\times d
Hermitian matrix and
P
is chosen uniformly at random in
G(d,k)
, it holds that the expectation of
\operatorname{Tr}(AP)
is
\frac{k\;\operatorname{Tr}(A)}{d}
and that, for all
t>0
,

\Pr\left[\left|\operatorname{Tr}(AP)-\frac{k\;\operatorname{Tr}(A)}{d}\right|>t\right]<\exp\left(-\frac{c^{\prime}dt^{2}}{\|A\|_{2}}\right).

Proof.

It is simple to check that the expectation of
g(P)
is
\frac{k}{d}
, since the expectation of
P
is
\frac{k}{d}I
. Moreover,

\displaystyle|\operatorname{Tr}(AP_{1})-\operatorname{Tr}(AP_{2})|

\displaystyle=|\operatorname{Tr}(A(P_{1}-P_{2}))|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\operatorname{Tr}(A^{2})\operatorname{Tr}((P_{1}-P_{2})^{2})}

\displaystyle=\|A\|_{2}\cdot\|P_{1}-P_{2}\|_{2}

where we have use the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality in the second line. The conclusion then follows from Fact
2.21
.
∎

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
\rho^{BE}
be any state such that
\dim(B)=d
, and let
P\in G(d,k)
. Given a unitary
U\in U(B)
, write
q(U,\rho)=\operatorname{Tr}(P^{B}U\rho^{BE}U^{\dagger})
. Then for uniform
U
in
U(B)
,

\mathbb{E}_{U}\left[q(U,\rho)\left\|\frac{P^{B}(U\rho^{BE}U^{\dagger})P^{B}}{q(U,\rho)}-\frac{P^{B}}{k}\otimes\rho^{E}\right\|_{1}\right]\leq\frac{k}{d}\left\|\rho^{BE}-\frac{I^{B}}{d}\otimes\rho^{E}\right\|_{1}+\frac{C}{\sqrt{d}}\left\|\rho^{B}-\frac{I^{B}}{d}\right\|_{2},

for some universal constant
C
.

Proof.

From the triangle inequality, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{U}\left[\left\|P^{B}(U\rho^{BE}U^{\dagger})P^{B}-\frac{q(U,\rho)P^{B}}{k}\otimes\rho^{E}\right\|_{1}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{U}\left[\left\|P^{B}U\left(\rho^{BE}-\frac{I^{B}}{d}\otimes\rho^{E}\right)U^{\dagger}P^{B}\right\|_{1}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{U}\left[\left\|\left(\frac{P^{B}}{d}-\frac{q(U,\rho)P^{B}}{k}\right)\otimes\rho^{E}\right\|_{1}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{U}\left[\left\|P^{B}U\left(\rho^{BE}-\frac{I^{B}}{d}\otimes\rho^{E}\right)U^{\dagger}P^{B}\right\|_{1}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{U}\left[\left\|\left(\frac{1}{d}-\frac{q(U,\rho)}{k}\right)P^{B}\right\|_{1}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{U}\left[\left\|P^{B}U\left(\rho^{BE}-\frac{I^{B}}{d}\otimes\rho^{E}\right)U^{\dagger}P^{B}\right\|_{1}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{U}\left[\left|\frac{1}{d}-\frac{q(U,\rho)}{k}\right|\right]\cdot\|P^{B}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{U}\left[\left\|P^{B}U\left(\rho^{BE}-\frac{I^{B}}{d}\otimes\rho^{E}\right)U^{\dagger}P^{B}\right\|_{1}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{U}\left[\left|\frac{1}{d}-\frac{q(U,\rho)}{k}\right|\right]\cdot k.

(3)

The first term in the right-hand side of (
3
) can be computed as follows: writing
\rho^{BE}-\frac{I^{B}}{d}\otimes\rho^{E}=M-N
for two PSD matrices
M
and
N
, we have
\|\rho^{BE}-I^{B}/d\otimes\rho^{E}\|_{1}=\operatorname{Tr}(M)+\operatorname{Tr}(N)
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{U}\left[\left\|P^{B}U\left(\rho^{BE}-\frac{I^{B}}{d}\otimes\rho^{E}\right)U^{\dagger}P^{B}\right\|_{1}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U}\left[\left\|PUMU^{\dagger}P\right\|_{1}+\left\|PUNU^{\dagger}P\right\|_{1}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U}[\operatorname{Tr}(PUMU^{\dagger}P)+\operatorname{Tr}(PUNU^{\dagger}P)]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left(\mathbb{E}_{U}\left[U^{\dagger}PU\right]M\right)+\operatorname{Tr}\left(\mathbb{E}_{U}\left[U^{\dagger}PU\right]N\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{d}(\operatorname{Tr}(M)+\operatorname{Tr}(N))

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{d}\left\|\rho^{BE}-\frac{I^{B}}{d}\otimes\rho^{E}\right\|_{1},

where in the fourth line we have made use of the fact that
\mathbb{E}_{U}[U^{\dagger}PU]=\frac{k}{d}I
.

The second term in equation (
3
) can be rewritten as
E_{U}[|\operatorname{Tr}((U^{\dagger}P^{B}U)\rho^{B})-k/d))|]
. Since
UP^{B}U^{\dagger}
is a uniformly random element of
G(d,k)
when
U
is uniformly random element of
U(B)
, the expectation can be taken over
G(d,k)
. Defining
Q=UPU^{\dagger}
, and letting
g(Q)=\operatorname{Tr}(Q\rho^{B})-k/d
, the second term is
\mathbb{E}_{Q}[|g(Q)|]
. It can be seen that

|g(Q)|=\left|\operatorname{Tr}\left(Q\left(\rho-\frac{I}{d}\right)\right)-\frac{k}{d}\operatorname{Tr}\left(\rho-\frac{I}{d}\right)\right|=\left|\operatorname{Tr}(AQ)-\frac{k}{d}\operatorname{Tr}(A)\right|

for
A=\rho-\frac{I}{d}
. Therefore, we can upper bound the probability that
|g(Q)|>t
from Lemma
3.9
. From this lemma, we get,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Q\sim G(d,k)}[|g(Q)|]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\Pr_{Q}[|g(Q)|>t]dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\Pr_{Q}\left[\left|\operatorname{Tr}(AQ)-\frac{k}{d}\operatorname{Tr}(A)\right|>t\right]dt

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{cd}{\|A\|_{2}}\cdot t^{2}\right)dt

\displaystyle=C\cdot\frac{\|A\|_{2}}{\sqrt{d}}

where Fact
2.15
was used in the final line. Putting in the value of
A
completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
3.8
.

Proof of Theorem
3.8
.

We can consider a random
U^{A_{1}A_{2}}
in
U(A_{1}A_{2})
that is of the form
(I^{A_{1}}\otimes V^{A_{1}})W^{A_{1}A_{2}}
, where
V^{A_{1}}
and
W^{A_{1}A_{2}}
are uniformly random in
U(A_{1})
and
U(A_{1}A_{2})
respectively. Since multiplying by
(I^{A_{1}}\otimes V^{A_{2}})
commutes with taking trace over
A_{1}
, we have,

\operatorname{Tr}_{A_{1}}\left(U^{A_{1}A_{2}}\rho^{A_{1}A_{2}E}(U^{A_{1}A_{2}})^{\dagger}\right)=V^{A_{2}}\operatorname{Tr}_{A_{1}}\left(W^{A_{1}A_{2}}\rho^{A_{1}A_{2}E}(W^{A_{1}A_{2}})^{\dagger}\right)(V^{A_{2}})^{\dagger}.

Letting
\rho^{A_{2}E}_{W}
denote
\operatorname{Tr}_{A_{1}}\left(W^{A_{1}A_{2}}\rho^{A_{1}A_{2}E}(W^{A_{1}A_{2}})^{\dagger}\right)
(and dropping the register superscripts from the operators), we also have,

p(U,\rho)=\operatorname{Tr}\left(PV\rho^{A_{2}E}_{W}V^{\dagger}\right)=q(V,\rho_{W}),

where
q(V,\rho_{W})
is the function from Lemma
3.10
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{U}\left[p(U,\rho)\left\|\frac{P\left(\operatorname{Tr}_{A_{1}}\left(U\rho^{A_{1}A_{2}E}U^{\dagger}\right)\right)P}{p(U,\rho)}-\frac{P}{k}\otimes\rho^{E}\right\|_{1}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{W}\left[\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[q(V,\rho_{W})\left\|\frac{P(V\rho^{A_{2}E}_{W}V^{\dagger})P}{q(V,\rho_{W})}-\frac{P}{k}\otimes\rho^{E}\right\|\right]\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{W}\left[\frac{k}{d}\left\|\rho^{A_{2}E}_{W}-\frac{I}{d_{2}}\otimes\rho^{E}\right\|_{1}+\frac{C}{\sqrt{d_{2}}}\left\|\rho^{A_{2}}_{W}-\frac{I}{d_{2}}\right\|_{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{k}{d_{2}}\mathbb{E}_{W}\left[\left\|\operatorname{Tr}_{A_{1}}\left(W\rho^{A_{1}A_{2}E}W^{\dagger}\right)-\frac{I}{d_{2}}\otimes\rho^{E}\right\|_{1}\right]+\sqrt{\frac{C}{d_{2}}}\mathbb{E}_{W}\left[\left\|\operatorname{Tr}_{A_{1}}\left(W\rho^{A_{1}A_{2}}W^{\dagger}\right)-\frac{I}{d_{2}}\right\|_{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{k}{d_{2}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{d_{2}\cdot 2^{-H_{2}(A_{1}A_{2}|E)_{\rho}}}{d_{1}}}+C\cdot\sqrt{\frac{2^{-H_{2}(A_{1}A_{2})_{\rho}}}{d_{1}d_{2}}},

where in the first equality we have used the law of total expectation, in the first inequality we have used Lemma
3.10
, and in the second inequality we have used both cases of Fact
3.7
. This proves the theorem.
∎

3.4
Second protocol: A refined rejection sampling procedure

The protocol we gave in Section
3.2
was both simple to analyze and was quite economical when it came to the entanglement cost. However, its communication cost was not optimal. In this subsection, we describe a more sophisticated
(d,k)
-RSP protocol with a slightly higher entanglement cost but whose communication cost matches that of the best-known protocol due to
[
20
]
(which itself is a generalization of a protocol for pure states due to
[
9
]
) of which it is a refinement. This protocol uses an extravagant amount of entanglement whereas the entanglement cost of our modified protocol is nearly optimal.

This second protocol, given in Figure
4
, will prove the following theorem.

Theorem 3.11
.

For every
\varepsilon_{a}>0
, there exists a
(d,k)
-RSP protocol with average error
\varepsilon_{a}
where Alice and Bob share a maximally entangled state with local dimension
O\left(\frac{d}{\varepsilon_{a}}\right)
, and Alice communicates
\log\frac{d}{k}+\log\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{a}}+O(1)
bits to Bob.

Together Theorems
3.11
and
1.3
imply item
2
of Theorem

.

Parameters.

•

A collection
\mathcal{U}=\{U_{1},\ldots,U_{N}\}
of unitaries on
\mathbb{C}^{d}

•

An ancilla size
r\geq 1

Protocol.

Alice and Bob share the standard maximally entangled state
\ket{\Phi_{+}}
on
A_{1}A_{2}B_{1}B_{2}
, where
A_{1}
and
B_{1}
are both of dimension
r
, and
A_{2}
and
B_{2}
are both of dimension

.

1.

Given input

1.1

She measures her share of the state according to the projective measurement
\{Q_{i},I-Q_{i}\}
, where
Q_{i}=U_{i}(I^{A_{1}}\otimes\overline{P}^{A_{2}})U_{i}^{\dagger}
.

1.2

If the first outcome was obtained, she sends
i
to Bob and exits the loop. If the second outcome was obtained and
i=N
, Alice sends
N+1
to Bob.

2.

If Bob receives
i\in[N]
from Alice, he applies the unitary
U_{i}^{T}
to
B_{1}B_{2}
, and makes
B_{2}
into the maximally mixed state otherwise.
B_{2}
then contains the approximation of
P/k
that is produced by the protocol.

Second average-case protocol for RSP of flat states

We now introduce notation to facilitate the analysis of the protocol. Take
P
to be Alice’s input. We will assume in the analysis that in all iterations in step 1 of the protocol, both outcomes have a nonzero probability of occurring: this will be the case with probability
1
for the collection of unitaries that we will exhibit. For all
i
, define
\rho_{i}
to be the residual state of
A_{1}A_{2}
right after the
i
-th iteration of the protocol failed, and set
p_{i}
to be the probability that the
i
-th iteration succeeded given that all previous iterations failed. From the Born rule, we have the expressions:

\displaystyle\rho_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{I^{A_{1}A_{2}}}{rd}

\displaystyle p_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}(Q_{i}\rho_{i-1})

\displaystyle\rho_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(I-Q_{i})\rho_{i-1}(I-Q_{i})}{1-p_{i}}.

Let also
\sigma_{i}
be the state of
B_{2}
given that the
i
-th iteration succeeded and Bob performed his unitary correction. Note that although the protocol is described as a series of projective measurements for notational ease, it could also be viewed as one single
(N+1)
-outcome generalized measurement. Since Alice and Bob started out in the standard maximally entangled state, the reduced density matrix of
B_{1}B_{2}
right before Bob performs the unitary correction is the conjugate of that of

, and is therefore

\widetilde{\sigma_{i}}^{B_{1}B_{2}}=\overline{\frac{Q_{i}\rho_{i-1}Q_{i}}{p_{i}}}=\overline{\frac{U_{i}(I\otimes\overline{P})U_{i}^{\dagger}\rho_{i-1}U_{i}(I\otimes\overline{P})U_{i}^{\dagger}}{p_{i}}}.

So that the residual state of
B_{2}
after Bob performed his unitary correction is:

\sigma_{i}=\frac{P(\operatorname{Tr}_{A_{1}}(\overline{U_{i}\rho_{i-1}U_{i}^{\dagger}}))P}{p_{i}}.

We then see that the expected error
\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{U},P)
of the protocol on Alice input

is given by:

\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{U},P)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(p_{i}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}(1-p_{j})\right)\left\|\sigma_{i}-\frac{P}{k}\right\|_{\mathrm{tr}}\right)+\left(\prod_{j=1}^{N}(1-p_{j})\right)\left(1-\frac{k}{d}\right),

(4)

where we have used that
\|\frac{I}{d}-\frac{P}{k}\|_{\mathrm{tr}}=1-\frac{k}{d}
for all rank-

P
(and this is the error when Alice sends
N+1

\varepsilon_{a}>0
, we want to exhibit values of

Theorem 3.12
.

For every
\varepsilon_{a}>0
, such a choice of

exists if we take

Proof.

It is enough to prove that with the choice of

By the probabilistic method, we can then select a

Note that because of the invariance of the Haar measure, the expectation under

P
. Let us then fix a particular one and use the notation
\rho_{i},Q_{i},p_{i},\sigma_{i}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{U}}\left[\left(\prod_{j=1}^{i}(1-p_{j})\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U_{1},\ldots,U_{i-1}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{U_{i}}\left[\left(\prod_{j=1}^{i}(1-p_{j})\right)\middle|U_{1},\ldots,U_{i-1}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U_{1},\ldots,U_{i-1}}\left[\left(\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}(1-p_{j})\right)\mathbb{E}_{U_{i}}\left[1-\operatorname{Tr}(Q_{i}\rho_{i-1})\mid U_{1},\ldots,U_{i-1}\right]\right]

\|\rho_{i}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{rd}\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}\frac{1}{1-p_{j}}.\leq\frac{1}{rd}\prod_{j=1}^{i}\frac{1}{1-p_{j}}.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{U}}[\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{U},P)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{U_{1},\ldots,U_{i-1}}\left[\left(\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}(1-p_{j})\right)\mathbb{E}_{U_{i}}\left[p_{i}\left\|\sigma_{i}-\frac{P}{k}\right\|_{\mathrm{tr}}\middle|U_{1}\ldots U_{i-1}\right]\right]+\left(1-\frac{k}{d}\right)\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{U}}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{N}(1-p_{j})\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle O\left(\frac{k}{rd}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\mathbb{E}_{U_{1},\ldots,U_{i-1}}\left[\sqrt{\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}(1-p_{j})}\right]\right)\right)+\left(1-\frac{k}{d}\right)^{N+1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle O\left(\frac{k}{rd}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{U_{1},\ldots,U_{i-1}}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}(1-p_{j})\right]}\right)\right)+\left(1-\frac{k}{d}\right)^{N+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O\left(\frac{k}{rd}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(1-\frac{k}{d}\right)^{\frac{i-1}{2}}\right)+\left(1-\frac{k}{d}\right)^{N+1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle O\left(\frac{k}{rd}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{k}{d}\right)^{\frac{i-1}{2}}\right)+\left(1-\frac{k}{d}\right)^{N+1}

where Jensen’s inequality was applied for the second inequality. Now taking
\sqrt{1-\frac{k}{d}}=x
, the summation in the first term in right-hand side above can be upper bounded as

\displaystyle\frac{k}{rd}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}x^{i}=\frac{k}{rd}\cdot\frac{1}{1-x}=\frac{k}{rd}\cdot\frac{1}{1-\sqrt{1-k/d}}\leq\frac{k}{rd}\cdot\frac{2d}{k}

using the inequality
1-\sqrt{1-y}\geq\frac{y}{2}
for
y\in[0,1]
. This means that

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{U}}[\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{U},P)]\leq O\left(\frac{1}{r}\right)+\left(1-\frac{k}{d}\right)^{N+1},

and so, taking taking
r=\Omega(\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{a}})
and
N=\frac{d}{k}\ln(1/\varepsilon_{a})
as in the theorem statement gives us the desired upper bound on the error.
∎

4
Resource lower bounds

In the previous section, we described protocols which achieved constant-error RSP of projectors in
G(d,k)
with
\approx\log\frac{d}{k}
bits of communication from Alice to Bob and
\approx\log d
EPR pairs. We now turn to showing that these are near-optimal. While proving the communication lower bound is simple, proving the entanglement lower bound is a lot more involved and takes up the bulk of the section.

We note that no lower bounds can be shown for protocols with
\varepsilon_{r}\geq 1-\frac{k}{d}
, as this can be achieved with no communication or entanglement at all by having Bob output the maximally mixed state. Provided that
\varepsilon_{r}
is smaller than this, we can prove a communication lower bound, which will be a simple consequence of the following result.

Fact 4.1

(See e.g. Theorem 4.1 of
[
36
]
)
.

For
n\geq 1
: consider the following task. Alice is given a uniformly random string
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
. Alice and Bob, who initially start out with an arbitrary maximally entangled state and have access to arbitrary shared randomness, run a two-way classical communication protocol. At the end of the protocol, Bob produces a string
x^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
. If
m
is an upper bound on the number of bits that were sent from Alice to Bob during the course of the protocol, we have

\Pr[x=x^{\prime}]\leq 2^{m-n}

We can then show our lower bound for the communication cost of
(d,k)
-RSP protocols.

Theorem 4.2
.

A
(d,k)
-RSP protocol with communication
m
and relaxed average-case error
\varepsilon_{r}
must satisfy

m\geq\left\lfloor\log\frac{d}{k}\right\rfloor+\log(1-\varepsilon_{r}).

Proof.

For
r=\lfloor{\frac{d}{k}}\rfloor
, let
P_{1},\ldots,P_{r}
be mutually orthogonal elements of
G(d,k)
. Complete them to a projective measurement with an additional projector
\tilde{P}
. Given the protocol in the statement of the proposition, consider the following protocol for transmitting
t\in[r]
from Alice to Bob. Using shared randomness, they sample a uniformly random unitary
U\in U(d)
; they run the original protocol with Alice input
UP_{t}U_{i}^{\dagger}
, and at the end of the protocol, Bob measures
B_{2}
according to the projective measurement
UP_{1}U^{\dagger},\ldots,UP_{r}U^{\dagger},U\tilde{P}U^{\dagger}
, getting outcome
t^{\prime}\in[r]
(if the last outcome is obtained, Bob picks
t^{\prime}
uniformly at random). We see that

1-\varepsilon_{r}\leq\Pr[t=t^{\prime}]\leq 2^{m-\lfloor\log\frac{d}{k}\rfloor}

Where
t
was encoded as a binary string and Fact
4.1
was applied. This gives the statement of the proposition.
∎

Note that this communication lower bound straightforwardly works even for RSP protocols with two-way communication and any amount of shared entanglement and randomness, and it lower bounds not only for the total communication of such a protocol, but the communication from Alice to Bob specifically.

Now we move on to proving a lower bound on the entanglement cost of
(d,k)
-RSP. In this regard, in addition to the condition
\varepsilon_{r}
not be too close to
1
, lower bounds can only be shown under the additional condition that
m=o(d)
. This is because, as noted in the introduction, there exists a good
(d,k)
-RSP protocol for relaxed error, namely the
\varepsilon
-net protocol, which is completely classical (i.e. require no entanglement) and which involves only
\Theta(d)
bits of communication. As it turns out, we can show that any protocol for which the bounds on both
\varepsilon_{r}
and communication are true, and which uses pure entanglement, must essentially be using
\log d-O(1)
EPR pairs.

Theorem 4.3
.

For all
\gamma>0
, any
(d,k)
-RSP protocol with relaxed average error
\varepsilon_{r}
,
m
bits of communication, and initial pure shared state
\ket{\sigma}^{AB}
, must satisfy

H^{\delta+\gamma}_{\text{min}}(A)_{\sigma}\geq\log d-3\log(1/\gamma)-O(1)

where
\delta=F\left(\frac{k}{d}+O(\sqrt{\frac{m}{d}}),1-\varepsilon_{r}\right)
, where
F
is the truncated fidelity function.

In view of Fact
2.29
, Theorem
4.3
implies that the initial entangled state is such that almost
\log d
many EPR pairs can be extracted out of it by LOCC up to a failure probability that is dependent on the smoothing. In particular, nothing is implied about the entanglement if
m=\Theta(n)
, in which case
\delta=1
, and the failure probability of the distillation protocol in Fact
2.29
is
1
.

4.1
The proof strategy

Our proof strategy for Theorem

(d,k)
-RSP protocol. Let
p

G(d,k)\times\{0,1\}^{m}

\ket{\chi_{c}}^{IA^{\prime}B_{1}B_{2}}

\rho^{B_{1}B_{2}}_{c}=\mathbb{E}_{P\sim p(\cdot\mid c)}[\chi^{B_{1}B_{2}}_{c,P}].

Moreover, let

\ket{\sigma}^{AB}

Lemma 4.4

The spectra
\lambda

\nu_{c}

\lambda\preceq\sum_{c}p(c)\nu_{c}.

To facilitate proving Lemma

to be determined later (along with the number

9
9

9

Ideally, we would run a uniform superposition over all

such that the superposition over them approximates the superposition over

In order to run the protocol in superposition, we introduce a new dimension-

on Alice’s side, initially in the uniform superposition. The original protocol is then carried out with Alice’s first operation controlled on

, i.e. the isometry

U=\sum_{i}\ket{i}\bra{i}^{I}\otimes U_{P_{i}}^{A}

where

in the original protocol. The rest of Alice and Bob’s operations in the superposition protocol are the same as in the original protocol. We will invoke Fact

Let
\tilde{p}

\ket{\chi_{c}}^{IA^{\prime}B_{1}B_{2}}=\sum_{i}\sqrt{\tilde{p}(i|c)}\ket{i}^{I}\ket{\chi_{P_{i},c}}^{A^{\prime}B_{1}B_{2}}.

The marginal state on the registers

\chi^{B_{1}B_{2}}_{c}=\sum_{i}\tilde{p}(i|c)\chi^{B_{1}B_{2}}_{P_{i},c}.

With this notation, we are now ready to prove Lemma

Proof of Lemma
4.4
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, we will show that for all

\sum_{i=1}^{l}\lambda_{i}\leq\left(\sum_{c}p(c)\sum_{i=1}^{l}\nu_{c,i}\right)+\varepsilon.

Since

Let
\tilde{\nu}_{c}
denote the eigenspectra of
\chi^{B_{1}B_{2}}_{c}
(which is equal to the Schmidt spectra of

\sum_{i=1}^{l}\lambda_{i}\leq\sum_{c}\tilde{p}(c)\sum_{i=1}^{l}\tilde{\nu}_{c,i}=\sum_{c}p(c)\sum_{i=1}^{l}\frac{\tilde{p}(c)}{p(c)}\tilde{\nu}_{c,i}.

We will show that, for some sufficiently large value of

, it is possible to choose

so that, for all

\left\|\rho^{B_{1}B_{2}}_{c}-\frac{\tilde{p}(c)}{p(c)}\chi^{B_{1}B_{2}}_{c}\right\|_{\mathrm{tr}}\leq\varepsilon.

(7)

Now the sum of the largest

\sup\{\operatorname{Tr}(M\omega):0\preceq M\preceq I,\mathrm{rank}(M)=l\}
. Moreover, the optimal

and the statement follows. We pick

\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{p}(c)}{p(c)}\chi^{B_{1}B_{2}}_{c}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{p(c|P_{i})}{p(c)}\rho^{B_{1}B_{2}}_{P_{i},c}

Theorem 4.5
.

Let

With this result, we can now prove Theorem

Proof of Theorem
4.3
.

Let
\varepsilon_{c}=\mathbb{E}_{P\sim p(\cdot|c)}\left[\operatorname{Tr}\left(\chi^{B_{1}B_{2}}_{c,P}(I^{B_{1}}\otimes(I-P)^{B_{2}})\right)\right]

From Lemma

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{l}\lambda_{j}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{c}p(c)\sum_{j=1}^{l}\nu_{c,j}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{c}p(c)F\left(\frac{k}{d}+A\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/p(c))}{d}}+\sqrt{\frac{l}{d}}\right),\;1-\varepsilon_{c}\right)

\displaystyle\leq F\left(\frac{k}{d}+A\sqrt{\frac{l}{d}}+\frac{A}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{c}p(c)\sqrt{\log(1/p(c))},\;1-\varepsilon_{r}\right)

\displaystyle\leq F\left(\frac{k}{d}+A\sqrt{\frac{l}{d}}+\frac{A}{\sqrt{d}}\cdot\sqrt{\sum_{c}p(c)\log(1/p(c))},\;1-\varepsilon_{r}\right)

4.2
The key semidefinite program

We first prove a result about the optimal value of a semidefinite program, which will be needed in our proof of Theorem

and

to be two nonzero projectors on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
with

\displaystyle\max_{\rho}

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}(P\rho)

(8)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}(Q\rho)\geq t

\displaystyle\rho\succeq 0,\operatorname{Tr}(\rho)=1

This subsection will be devoted to deriving the following lemma.

Lemma 4.6
.

The optimal value of program (
8
) is
F(\|PQP\|_{\infty},t)
, where

is the truncated fidelity function.

We start by noting that this program admits a pure optimal solution in view of the following simple lemma.
10
10

10

The natural extension of this result to three matrices is false ,because the expectation values of a single-qubit mixed state under the three non-identity Pauli matrices completely characterize it.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
A
and
B
be Hermitian matrices and let
\rho
be a mixed state. There exists a pure state
\ket{\psi}
such that:

\operatorname{Tr}(A\rho)=\braket{\psi|A|\psi}

\operatorname{Tr}(B\rho)=\braket{\psi|B|\psi}.

Proof.

Consider the spectral decomposition of
\rho
:

\rho=\sum_{i}p_{i}\ket{\psi_{i}}\bra{\psi_{i}}.

Let
M=A+iB
and set
a=\operatorname{Tr}(A\rho)
,
b=\operatorname{Tr}(B\rho)
, both of which are real because
A
and
B
are assumed to be Hermitian. Then,

a+ib=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho M)=\sum_{i}p_{i}\braket{\psi_{i}|M|\psi_{i}}

Now the set of numerical values of
\bra{\phi}M\ket{\phi}
is a convex set,
11
11

11

The most general form of this result is known as the Toeplitz-Hausdorff Theorem.
and therefore there exists
\ket{\psi}

. The result follows from comparing the real and imaginary parts.
∎

Next, we prove a special case of Lemma

.

Lemma 4.8
.

The statement of Lemma
4.6
holds when
P
and
Q
are rank-1 projectors on

Proof.

Assume without loss of generality that

and

\ket{\phi}=\alpha\ket{0}+\beta\ket{1}
and

. In view of Lemma
4.7

, where again
\gamma
can be assumed to be real and nonnegative without loss of generality. The objective function is then

, and the constraints are that
\gamma^{2}+|\delta|^{2}=1
and
\gamma^{2}\geq t
.

In the event that
\alpha^{2}\geq t
, setting
\gamma=\alpha
,
\delta=\beta
produces a value of 1, which is the highest possible. If not,

\alpha
and

Fact 4.9

(Jordan’s Lemma, see

Finishing the proof

B_{1}B_{2}
. Define the function
f:G(d,k)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by:

f(P)=\|Q(I^{B_{1}}\otimes P^{B_{2}})Q\|_{\infty}.

If
P
is sampled from the Haar measure on
G(d,k)
, then, for some universal constant
c^{\prime}>0
, we have that for all
t>0

\Pr\left[|f(P)-\mathbb{E}[f(P)]|\geq t\right]\leq\exp(-c^{\prime}dt^{2})

Proof.

Given
P_{1},P_{2}
, we have

\displaystyle|f(P_{1})-f(P_{2})|

\displaystyle\leq\|Q(I\otimes(P_{1}-P_{2}))Q\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\|I\otimes(P_{1}-P_{2})\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\|P_{1}-P_{2}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\|P_{1}-P_{2}\|_{2}

where the reverse triangle inequality was applied for the first inequality. The result then follows from the concentration inequality for Lipschitz functions in the Schatten
2
-norm in Fact
2.22
.
∎

We will need to calculate the expectation of
f(P)
in order to use the concentration inequality above. We do this in the following lemma.

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
Q
be a rank-
l
projector on
B_{1}B_{2}
. Defining
f:G(d,k)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by:

f(P)=\|Q(I^{B_{1}}\otimes P^{B_{2}})Q\|_{\infty}

We have that

\mathbb{E}_{P\sim\mu_{\text{Haar}}}[f(P)]\leq\frac{k}{d}+O\left(\sqrt{\frac{l}{d}}\right).

Proof.

We begin by noting that, using the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|Q(I\otimes P)Q\|_{\infty}]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{k}{d}\mathbb{E}[\|Q(I\otimes I)Q\|_{\infty}]+\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|Q\left(I\otimes\left(P-\frac{k}{d}I\right)\right)Q\right\|_{\infty}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{d}+\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|Q\left(I\otimes\left(P-\frac{k}{d}I\right)\right)Q\right\|_{\infty}\right].

All that remains to do is to bound the latter term, which is a routine exercise in random matrix theory. Take
\ket{v_{1}},\ldots,\ket{v_{N}}
to be a
(1/2)
-net over the complex sphere in the range of
Q
. This range is assumed to be of dimension
l
, so that we may take
N=C^{l}
as per Fact
2.24
, for some constant
C>1
. For
i\in[N]
, define the random variable
X_{i}
by

X_{i}=\left\langle v_{i}\middle|\left(I\otimes\left(P-\frac{k}{d}I\right)\right)\middle|v_{i}\right\rangle.

Setting
X=\max_{i}|X_{i}|
, Fact
2.25
gives that for any
P
,

\left\|Q\left(I\otimes\left(P-\frac{k}{d}I\right)\right)Q\right\|_{\infty}\leq 2X.

Because
\mathbb{E}[P]=\frac{k}{d}I
, it holds that
\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]=0
for all
i
. We can do calculations similar to those in Lemma
4.10
to show that
X_{i}
is
1
-Lipschitz in
P
. Then applying Fact
2.22
we have that, for any
t
,

\Pr[X_{i}>t]\leq\exp(-c^{\prime}dt^{2})

for all
i
. Then by a union bound we get,

\Pr[X>t]\leq N\exp(-c^{\prime}dt^{2}).

For any
t_{0}>0
, we can upper bound
\mathbb{E}[X]
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\Pr[X>t]dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t_{0}}\Pr[X>t]dt+\int_{t_{0}}^{\infty}\Pr[X>t]dt

\displaystyle\leq t_{0}+\frac{N}{2c^{\prime}t_{0}d}\exp(-c^{\prime}dt_{0}^{2})

where the bound in Fact
2.14
was used in the last inequality. Setting
t_{0}=\Theta\left(\sqrt{\frac{l}{d}}\right)
makes the second term in
O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right)
and hence proves the result.
∎

We can now complete the proof of the main theorem of this section.

Proof of Theorem
4.5
.

Let
G
and
\omega
and
\nu
be as in the statement of the theorem. A well-known characterization of the sum of the largest
l
eigenvalues is

\sum_{j=1}^{l}\nu_{j}=\sup_{0\preceq Q\preceq I,\text{rank}(Q)=l}\operatorname{Tr}(Q\omega).

Moreover, the optimal
Q
may be taken to be projector. Therefore, it is enough to bound
\operatorname{Tr}(Q\omega)
for any rank-
l
projector
Q
.
Let
Q
be such a projector. For
P\in G(d,k)
, let
f(P
) be as defined in Lemma
4.11
.
and set
t(P)=\operatorname{Tr}((I^{B_{1}}\otimes P^{B_{2}})\omega_{P})
. By definition, we have,
\mathbb{E}_{P\sim\mu}t(P)=1-\varepsilon
. Lemma
4.6
then gives:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}(Q\omega)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P\sim\mu}[\operatorname{Tr}(Q\omega_{P})]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{P\sim\mu}[F(f(P),t(P))]

\displaystyle\leq F(\mathbb{E}_{P\sim\mu}[f(P)],1-\varepsilon)

where the concavity of
F
was used for the second inequality. It is therefore enough to control the expectation of
f(P)
under
P\sim\mu
. We have already calculated its expectation under the Haar distribution in Lemma
4.11
. Using Fact
2.17
we therefore get for
r=\log K
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P\sim\mu}[f(P)]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{P\sim\mu_{\text{Haar}}}[f(P)]+\mathbb{E}_{P\sim\mu}\left[\left|f(P)-\mathbb{E}_{P\sim\mu_{\text{Haar}}}[f(P)]\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{k}{d}+O\left(\sqrt{\frac{l}{d}}\right)+2\left(\mathbb{E}_{P\sim\mu_{\text{Haar}}}\left[\left|f(P)-\mathbb{E}_{P\sim\mu_{\text{Haar}}}[f(P)]\right|^{r}\right]\right)^{1/r}.

Finally, we can upper bound the last term in the above expression using Fact
2.16
along with Lemma
4.10
. This gives us,

\left(\mathbb{E}_{P\sim\mu_{\text{Haar}}}\left[\left|f(P)-\mathbb{E}_{P\sim\mu_{\text{Haar}}}[f(P)]\right|^{2}\right]\right)^{1/r}\leq O\left(\sqrt{\frac{r}{d}}\right)\leq O\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log K}{d}}\right).

Putting everything together, we get,

\sum_{j=1}^{l}\nu_{j}=\operatorname{Tr}(Q\omega)\leq F\left(\frac{k}{d}+O\left(\sqrt{\frac{l}{d}}+\sqrt{\frac{\log K}{d}}\right),1-\varepsilon\right).\qed

Applications

5.1
Incompressibility of an ensemble of flat states

In this section, we prove our result about the impossibility of visible compression of an ensemble of flat states, as a corollary of our entanglement lower bound for RSP in Theorem
4.3
. First we state the definition of visible compression (without entanglement).

Definition 5.1

(Visible state compression without entanglement)
.

Fix an ensemble
\mathcal{E}=(\mu,\rho)
of quantum states in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. A visible
(d^{\prime},\varepsilon)
-compression scheme for
\mathcal{E}
is an encoding map
E:\mathbb{C}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}^{d^{\prime}}
and a decoding map
D:\mathbb{C}^{d^{\prime}}\to\mathbb{C}^{d}
such that
D
is CPTP (
E
can be any measurable map), and
d^{\prime}\leq d
, such that

\mathbb{E}_{\rho\sim\mu}\left[\|D\circ E(\rho)-\rho\|_{\mathrm{tr}}\right]\leq\varepsilon.

We now prove Theorem
1.4
, whose statement is recalled below.
See
1.4

Proof.

Such a compression procedure gives rise to a
(d,k)
-RSP protocol which consists in Alice preparing
E(\rho)
on her end and teleporting it to Bob, who then applies the decompression map
D
to get
D\circ E(\rho)
, and this is close to
\rho
. This protocol requires
\log d^{\prime}
EPR pairs and
2\log d^{\prime}
bits of communication, and the average error of the protocol is the same as that of the original procedure. Take
\eta>0
, and take
d_{0}
to be the smallest value of
d
such that if the second term in the first argument of the fidelity function in the statement of Theorem
4.3
is
A\sqrt{\frac{m}{d}}
, then

A\sqrt{\frac{2\log d}{d}}<\frac{\eta}{2}.

Setting
\delta=F\left(\frac{k}{d}+\frac{\eta}{2},1-\varepsilon\right)
, we see that
\delta<1
for any
\varepsilon
as in the statement of the corollary. Moreover, setting
\gamma=\frac{1-\delta}{2}
, and noting that
F
is monotonically increasing in the first argument, we can compute
H^{\delta+\gamma}
of
\log d^{\prime}
EPR pairs:

H^{\delta+\gamma}_{\text{min}}(A)_{\sigma}=\log d^{\prime}-\log(1-\gamma-\delta)=\log d^{\prime}-\log(1-\delta)+1.

Now Theorem
4.3
implies that

\log d^{\prime}-\log(1-\delta)+1\geq\log d+3\log(1-\delta)-3,

which can be rearranged to give the result.
∎

5.2
An entanglement-optimal bounded-error protocol for the equality function

We believe that the protocols we gave for
(d,k)
-RSP could turn out to be a useful subroutine for designing entanglement-assisted protocols in the future. Here we describe such a protocol for computing the equality function on
n
bits.

In the
\mathrm{EQ}_{n}
problem, Alice and Bob are given
x,y\in\{0,1\}^{n}
and need to determine whether they are equal. It is known that
n
bits (or
n
qubits in the absence of shared entanglement) of communication are necessary and sufficient to achieve this with probability
1
, but allowing for a small error probability
\varepsilon>0
changes the story significantly. It is known that, classically, the sharing of
\log n+O(\log\frac{1}{\epsilon})
public random bits, along with communication
\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+O(1)
(which crucially does not depend on
n
) are necessary and sufficient. Clearly, the same is possible if Alice and Bob share this many EPR pairs instead of random bits as they can be converted into random bits by measuring them in the computational basis.

Our protocol for
\mathrm{EQ}_{n}
will instead use
O(1)
communication and only
\frac{1}{2}\log n
many EPR pairs. Our result is formally restated below.
See
1.5

The protocol achieving Theorem
1.5
is given in Figure
5
.

Given.

•

A collection
\{P_{x}\}_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}
of elements of
G(d,k)

•

An error parameter
\varepsilon>0

Input.

Alice and Bob get inputs
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
and
y\in\{0,1\}^{n}
respectively.

Protocol.

1.

Alice and Bob run a
(d,k)
-RSP protocol with worst-case error
\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
given Alice input
P_{x}
.

2.

Bob does the
\{P_{y},I-P_{y}\}
measurement on the target register of the RSP protocol; he declares that
x=y
if he obtained the first outcome and
x\neq y
otherwise.

Figure 5:
An entanglement-assisted protocol for the equality function on
n
bits

It is easy to see that the worst-case error of the protocol in Figure
5
is at most

\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\max_{x\neq y}\frac{1}{k}\operatorname{Tr}(P_{x}P_{y}).

Therefore, we need to pick
\{P_{x}\}_{x}
such that
\max_{x\neq y}\operatorname{Tr}(P_{x}P_{y})\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
in order for the protocol to succeed with probability
1-\varepsilon
.
The following lemma shows that for
d=\Theta\left(\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\varepsilon^{3/2}}\right)
and
k=\Theta(\varepsilon d)
, such a choice of
P_{x}
-s exist. This proves Theorem
1.5
, assuming we use our Kraus operator protocol for
(d,k)
-RSP.

Lemma 5.2
.

Take
d\in\mathbb{N}
, and for
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, set
k=\lceil\frac{\varepsilon d}{2}\rceil
. For
m=\Theta(d^{2}\varepsilon^{3})
, there exist projectors
P_{1},\ldots,P_{2^{m}}\in G(d,k)
such that, for all
i\neq j
, we have

\frac{1}{k}\operatorname{Tr}(P_{i}P_{j})<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

Proof.

As is the rest of the paper, the existence of these projectors is shown using the probabilistic method. Note that for a fixed projector
P_{0}
, the function
f:G(d,k)\mapsto\mathbb{R}
given by
f(Q)=\frac{1}{k}\operatorname{Tr}(QP_{0})
has Lipschitz constant
\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}
. Therefore, in view of the concentration inequality in Fact
4.10
, if we sample
P_{1},\ldots,P_{N}
uniformly at random in
G(d,k)
, the probability that there exist
i\neq j
with
\frac{1}{k}\operatorname{Tr}(P_{i}P_{j})>\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
is bounded by

\binom{2^{m}}{2}\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{c^{\prime}}{4}dk\varepsilon^{2}\right).

This is smaller than
1
provided that

2^{2m}\leq\exp\left(-\frac{c^{\prime}}{8}dk\varepsilon^{2}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{c^{\prime}}{8}d^{2}\varepsilon^{3}\right)

which is satisfied for the value of
m
given in the statement of the lemma.
∎
