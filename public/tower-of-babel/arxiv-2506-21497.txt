Enhancing User Engagement in Socially-Driven Dialogue through Interactive LLM Alignments
Jiashuo Wang, Kaitao Song, Chunpu Xu, Changhe Song, Yang Xiao, Dongsheng Li, Lili Qiu, Wenjie Li

Abstract
Enhancing user engagement through interactions plays an essential role in socially-driven dialogues. While prior works have optimized models to reason over relevant knowledge or plan a dialogue act flow, the relationship between user engagement and knowledge or dialogue acts is subtle and does not guarantee user engagement in socially-driven dialogues. To this end, we enable interactive LLMs to learn user engagement by leveraging signals from the future development of conversations. Specifically, we adopt a more direct and relevant indicator of user engagement, i.e., the user’s reaction related to dialogue intention after the interaction, as a reward to align interactive LLMs. To achieve this, we develop a user simulator to interact with target interactive LLMs and explore interactions between the user and the interactive LLM system via i × \times MCTS ( M onte C arlo T ree S earch for i nteraction). In this way, we collect a dataset containing pairs of higher and lower-quality experiences using i × \times MCTS , and align interactive LLMs for high-level user engagement by direct preference optimization (DPO) accordingly. Experiments conducted on two socially-driven dialogue scenarios (emotional support conversations and persuasion for good) demonstrate that our method effectively enhances user engagement in interactive LLMs.



1
Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have recently made remarkable advancements, enabling their application across diverse domains, such as hypothesis generation
Zhou et al. 2024
and mental health counseling
Qiu and Lan 2024
. Many of these applications rely on interactive LLMs, where user engagement is essential in achieving various interaction goals
Liu et al. 2024
;
Furumai et al. 2024
.

Engagement can take on different meanings and indicators depending on the context, including the intensity and duration of emotion, attention, and other forms of involvement
Fredricks et al. 2004
. Conversation length can serve as a reasonable indicator in casual chitchat scenarios
Yu et al. 2017
, whereas emotional expression and problem recognition are likely more meaningful indicators in emotional support conversations
Chen et al. 2023
;
Holdsworth et al. 2014
. While engagement in chitchat has been explored in prior work
Yu et al. 2019
;
Yu et al. 2017
;
Yu et al. 2016
, the engagement in socially-driven dialogues (emotional support and persuasion for good), has been less directly investigated.

Prior research on conversational language models has focused on enhancing either models’ reasoning abilities over external knowledge
Deng et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2022
or their planning capabilities to design turn-level dialogue acts for smoother conversational flow
Yu et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2019
. These approaches are based on the logic that incorporating relevant knowledge and adopting appropriate dialogue acts can significantly increase the likelihood of engaging users. However, the relationship between user engagement and knowledge or dialogue acts is subtle rather than straightforward. For instance, while questions or informative content can encourage users to talk more and stay engaged, a poorly framed question or an overload of information may be off-putting
Chen et al. 2023
. In addition, the incorporation of external knowledge or dialogue acts may not be efficient in the era of LLMs.

A more direct and relevant indicator of user engagement is the user’s reaction that occurs after the interaction and is related to the dialogue’s intended goal. Unlike dialogue acts or domain knowledge, which can often be identified within a single turn or exchange, user engagement is shaped by the cumulative effect of the entire conversation. Therefore, future development of conversations can benefit interactive LLMs in learning response generations towards better user engagement.

This work aims to leverage user’s reaction related to the dialogue intention at the end of interactions to align interactive LLMs for enhanced user engagement. To achieve this, we collect interaction experiences between interactive LLMs and user simulators using a Monte Carlo Tree Search for interaction (
i
\times
MCTS
). Next, the level of user engagement is detected using rule-based methods and regular expression matching. Finally, interactive LLMs are optimized via Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
Rafailov et al. 2024
to increase the likelihood of generating responses that lead to stronger user engagement, while decreasing the likelihood of responses associated with weaker engagement.

For experiments, we select emotional support conversations and persuasion for good as evaluation tasks, as user engagement plays a crucial role in affecting user feelings, cognition and behaviors. When evaluating interactive LLMs optimized by the proposed method, we conduct both automated interactive evaluation and human subjective evaluation. Experimental results demonstrate that our method effectively enhances user engagement levels in interactions.

Our contributions can be summarized as follows:

•

This work focuses on enhancing user engagement in socially-driven dialogues, which has been less investigated directly in prior work.

•

Interactive LLMs are trained to learn from future dialogue outcomes, derived by
i
\times
MCTS
, via DPO to enhance user engagement.

•

Experimental evaluations on emotional support conversations and persuasion for good demonstrate the effectiveness of our approach.

2
Related Work

2.1
Engagement of Interactive LLMs

Interactive LLMs play a crucial role in various applications, including emotional support
Qiu and Lan 2024
;
Liu et al. 2024
and persuasive dialogue
Furumai et al. 2024
. Ensuring user engagement is critical in these conversations, as it fosters deeper connections and a better understanding of users.
To enhance engagement, prior research has primarily focused on improving models’ reasoning and planning capabilities.
Existing approaches can be broadly categorized into two strategies: (1) reasoning enhancement, which incorporates external knowledge to improve response quality
Deng et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2022
, and (2) planning-based response generation, which designs turn-level response strategies to guide conversation flow
Yu et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2019
.
However, as LLMs scale up, these methods may become less effective and computationally inefficient.
In this work, we take a more intuitive approach by leveraging user feedback in an interaction as a direct reward to align the LLM.
Specifically, we use the user’s final action or state at the end of the conversation as a criterion for measuring user engagement.
To achieve this, we enhance the model’s reasoning and planning capabilities using MCTS.

2.2
MCTS with LLM

MCTS is a heuristic search algorithm that has been successfully applied to tasks requiring complex reasoning and planning, such as Atari
Ye et al. 2021
and Go
Silver et al. 2016
. It explores the decision space by iteratively constructing a search tree, balancing exploration and exploitation to identify optimal actions.
Recently, MCTS has been integrated with LLMs to enhance their reasoning and planning capabilities.
It constructs a search tree by sampling model outputs based on the LLM’s policy.
Subsequently, paired outputs with higher and lower estimated rewards can be extracted and distilled into the LLM’s policy via supervised fine-tuning (SFT) or DPO
Rafailov et al. 2024
.
This approach has demonstrated success across various domains, including mathematical problem-solving
Wang et al. 2024b
;
Zhang et al. 2024
;
Feng et al. 2023
, scientific reasoning
Xie et al. 2024b
, and applications in programming and games
Zhou et al. 2023
.
Beyond the above domains, MCTS has been applied to conversational tasks. Beyond these domains, MCTS has also been applied to conversational tasks. For instance,
Li et al. 2024
and
Yu et al. 2023
utilize MCTS to enhance dialogue act planning, improving user-LLM interactions. Unlike these two approaches, we directly apply MCTS to optimize the interactive LLM itself, leading to greater effectiveness.

We decide to align interactive LLMs for enhanced user engagement using DPO

3.1
User Simulator and Engagement

User Simulator

We develop a user simulator to model a specific user’s immediate reactions and final engagement level after a conversation. The user simulator is expected to interact like a user given a user condition
\mathcal{U}
, contents of which vary depending on the scenarios.
\mathcal{U}
includes the user’s troubles, feelings, and thoughts in the context of emotional support conversation, while encompasses psychological and demographic information in persuasion for good. With
\mathcal{U}

i^{\text{th}}
turn based on the history conversation
\mathcal{C}
, formulated as follows:

\displaystyle o_{i}

\displaystyle=P_{u}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{C}_{i}),

(1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{i}

\displaystyle=\begin{aligned} &\begin{cases}\emptyset,&\text{if }i=0,\\
\{u_{0},a_{1},\dots,a_{i-1}\},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}\end{aligned}

o_{i}
represents the user simulator’s emotional & cognitive states and the response at the
i^{\text{th}}
turn in emotional support conversations, whereas in persuasion for good, it denotes only the response at the
i^{\text{th}}
turn.
\emptyset
indicate “” (empty). Throughout the interaction, the user simulator is designed to behave like a user in datasets. Therefore, we use a simple and effective method–supervised finetuning (SFT)–to train the user simulators.

Engagement

We extract contents from the end of the conversation to determine the level of user engagement. In emotional support conversations, a full expression of feelings and thoughts is considered as a sign of user engagement, denoted by 1. Otherwise, the engagement level is denoted by 0. Therefore, we use regular expression matching to detect whether there is still any negative feelings and thoughts in the user simulator’s emotional & cognitive states in its output. In persuasion for good, we use the donation amount to reflect the user engagement, and use regular expression matching to extract the decided donation amount from the last user utterance. The engagement level is
\frac{\$\text{donation}}{2}
, where $2 is the max donation amount.

3.2
MCTS for Interaction

Unlike mathematical or scientific problems
Zhang et al. 2024
;
Xie et al. 2024b
, scenarios focused on user engagement inherently involve interactions. Additionally, user engagement can be assessed at the end of the interaction. Thus, when performing
i
\times
MCTS
, we involve a user simulator.
Consequently, the tree comprises two types of nodes: user nodes and model nodes, with each model node always followed by a user node. Each node is an utterance. While user reactions can be diverse and could be modeled by generating multiple user utterances, we simplify the structure by considering only a single user node per model node.

i
\times
MCTS

As illustrated in
Figure

1
, similar to MCTS,
i
\times
MCTS
iteratively performs the following operations, iteractively:

Selection

i
\times
MCTS
recursively selects the model nodes from the root node by Upper Confidence Bound (UCB) bandit
Auer 2002
. That is:

\text{UCB}(a_{i})=\frac{Q(a_{i})}{N(a_{i})}+c\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\ln N(a_{i-2})}{N(a_{i})}},

(2)

where
N(\cdot)
is the visit counts for the model node,
c
represents an exploration constant, and
Q(a_{i})
is the total reward accumulated at node
a_{i}
.

Expansion

Once an expandable model node is selected and its next user node is generated, we generate
e
new model nodes using the interactive LLM
\pi_{\theta}
based on the conversation context. Therefore, the expansion width is
e
.
However, without pruning, the number of expanded nodes can grow exponentially with depth, leading to computational inefficiency. Therefore, we employ a simple pruning technique to constrain the search space and improve efficiency. Since understanding the user is crucial for engagement, we use the similarity between the user simulator’s state and the interactive LLM’s predicted state as a criterion to retain at most
K
nodes at each depth.

Rollout

User engagement is assessed at the end of the conversation.
If a user utterance indicates both conversation termination and engagement, the immediate reward is
1
; otherwise, it is
0
.

Back-propagation

The reward of the newly generated node is transmitted back to all its ancestors, updating their accumulated rewards accordingly, while their visit counts are increased by one. Given the inherent diversity of dialogue, we do not explore merely a single successful trajectory. Instead, we explore
K
trajectories up to a certain depth
T
or until an engagement signal is reached, and then we select the successful ones. Thus the reward can indicate the probability that the node can lead to a user engagement signal.

The
i
\times
MCTS
process generates a search tree, from which we extract and construct chosen-rejected model response pairs. The chosen and rejected responses are generated by
\pi_{\theta}
given the same conversation context. We refer to this set of pairs as
D_{p}
.

We adopt DPO
Rafailov et al. 2024
to enhance the engagement level of interactive LLMs. It is an off-policy preference learning algorithm that directly utilizes pairwise preference data to fine-tune the policy model.

We denote the preference dataset used for alignment as

The first part comprises the chosen-rejected model response pairs, i.e.,

4
Experimental Settings

4.1
Experiment Scenarios

For our experiments, we select tasks that meet the following criteria: (1) user engagement is crucial, and (2) the impact of an LLM’s engagement level can be directly observed at the end of the conversation. Based on these requirements, we choose emotional support dialogue and persuasion for good as our experimental tasks.

Emotional Support Conversation

The goal of emotional support conversation is to alleviate users’ negative feelings and help them understand and navigate their challenges
Liu et al. 2021
.
In conversations with users, it is essential to engage them, as this encourages the expression of their thoughts and emotions, ultimately enhancing their well-being.
Correspondingly, a key indicator of user engagement is whether the user has expressed all their negative thoughts and feelings.

Persuasion for Good

In the task of persuasion for good, the persuader aims to persuade the persuadee to donate to a specific charity
Wang et al. 2019
.
To successfully complete this task, the persuader must foster trust and inspire users to contribute.
In our experiment, we propose that the interactive LLM take on the role of the persuader. User engagement can then be measured by the final amount the user (persuadee) decides to donate.

4.2
Datasets

PsyDTCorpus & PsyQA

PsyDTCorpus is a multi-turn Chinese emotional support dialogue dataset introduced by
Xie et al. 2024a
. It is synthesized using GPT-4 to create digital twins of professional psychological counselors. This dataset comprises 5,000 conversations between a help-seeker and a supporter, with an average of 18.1 rounds per conversation.
We use GPT-4o
Hurst et al. 2024
to annotate the help-seeker’s emotional and cognitive states. This information is essential for pruning during the execution of
i
\times
MCTS
. Additionally, the user emotional and cognitive state at the end of the conversation serves as a signal of engagement. The annotation details can be found in
Section

A.1
.
In our experiment related to emotional support, we use this dataset to train the interactive LLM and user simulator model.

PsyQA
Sun et al. 2021
is a Chinese dataset of psychological health support in the form of the question and answer pair.
It is crawled from a Chinese mental health service platform, and contains 22,341 questions. Each question includes the help-seeker’s feelings and problems. We extract 4,000 questions as the user’s condition when executing
i
\times
MCTS
in the scenario of emotional support.

Persuasion For Good Corpus

The Persuasion For Good Corpus
Wang et al. 2019
is a dataset of multi-turn conversations designed to analyze persuasive strategies in donation-based dialogues. The dataset was collected via Amazon Mechanical Turk, where one participant attempts to persuade another to donate to a charitable cause. It consists of 1,017 conversations, and the average number of rounds per conversation is 10.43. This dataset also contains psychological and demographic information of the persuadee in each conversation. This information is presented in the (
attribute
,
value
) format, like (
extrovert
,
2.33
). This dataset is used to train the interactive LLM and user simulator model in the scenairo of persuasion for good.

4.3
Interactive LLM

Using the PsyDTCorpus and the Persuasion For Good Corpus, we train two interactive LLMs, denoted as the Supporter LLM and the Persuader LLM, respectively.
The Supporter LLM and Help-Seeker simulator are trained based on Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct
Yang et al. 2024
and Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct
Meta 2024
;
Dubey et al. 2024
via SFT with LoRA
Hu et al. 2022
, respectively.
We use different backbone models considering the language of their training datasets. Notably, when training the Supporter LLM, we ask the LLM to generate additional supporter response’s chain-of-though, which contains the predicted help-seeker’s emotional and cognitive states.

4.4
User Simulator

Corresponding to the above two interactive LLMs, we develop two user simulator models: the Help-Seeker Simulator and the Persuadee Simulator. Both models are trained using SFT with LoRA. For convenience, their backbone models are the same as their corresponding interactive LLMs.

As mentioned in
Section

3.2
, the user simulator is designed to behave according to the specified user condition. For the Help-Seeker Simulator, this condition includes the help-seeker’s troubles, feelings, and thoughts. Specifically, we use GPT-4o to extract and summarize this information from the conversation to represent the help-seeker’s condition.
For the Persuadee Simulator, the user condition consists of psychological and demographic attributes. We utilize GPT-4o to transform the information provided in the Persuasion for Good corpus into a natural language description of each persuadee in the dataset.

4.5
Pruning Technique

As mentioned in
Section

3.2
, we adopt pruning techniques to mitigate computational inefficiency as the depth of exploration increases. In the context of emotional support conversation, we maintain the top
K
trajectories based on the interactive LLM’s understanding of the help-seeker’s state. Specifically, we measure the cosine similarity between the emotional and cognitive state generated by the Help-Seeker Simulator
s_{\text{sim}}^{i-1}
and the state predicted by the Supporter LLM
s_{\text{llm}}^{i}
at the
i^{th}
turn. This similarity serves as a criterion for pruning less supportive trajectories, and is computed as:

\cos(s_{\text{sim}}^{i-1},s_{\text{llm}}^{i})=\frac{\mathbf{v}(s_{\text{sim}}^{i-1})\cdot\mathbf{v}(s_{\text{llm}}^{i})}{\|\mathbf{v}(s_{\text{sim}}^{i-1})\|\|\mathbf{v}(s_{\text{llm}}^{i-})\|},

(4)

where
\mathbf{v}
represents a function that computes the vector representation of the given text. Specifically, we utilize the all-MiniLM-L6-v2 model to generate these embeddings. A higher cosine similarity indicates a trajectory that contain more engaging and supportive responses.

For the task of persuasion for good, we use the persuadee’s sentiment as a criterion to retain the top
K
trajectories. In specific, we compute an accumulated sentiment score as follows:

\displaystyle ss_{i}

\displaystyle=\gamma*ss_{i-2}+\mathbf{VADER}(u_{i}),

(5)

\displaystyle ss_{0}

\displaystyle=0,

where
i
indicate the turn index,
\gamma
is a discount factor, and
\mathbf{VADER}(\cdot)
denotes the sentiment value computed by the VADER (Valence Aware Dictionary and sEntiment Reasoner) tool
Hutto and Gilbert 2014
. Higher sentiment scores indicate trajectories with a greater likelihood of leading to positive engagement.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we address the challenge of enhancing user engagement in interactive LLMs. We focuse on enhancing the engagement of interactive LLMs, emphasizing the purpose of interactions rather than their format. Unlike previous approaches, we use a more direct and relevant reward, derived from the user’s reaction after interactions, to optimize the interactive LLM. To gather user reactions, we develop a user simulator that behaves like a user under specific conditions. Our goal is to optimize the interactive LLM to foster greater engagement. Accordingly, we employ
i
\times
MCTS
to explore higher-quality interactions between the user simulator and the interactive LLM. Based on these interactions, we construct a preference dataset and apply DPO to align the model toward more engaging responses. We evaluated our method on emotional support conversations and persuasion for donation. Experimental results demonstrate that our approach significantly improves the engagement level of LLMs.

Limitations

In our work, we focus on general user engagement, optimizing the interactive LLM to generate responses that keep most users engaged. However, there is also room to develop a more personalized and engaging interactive LLM. In a personalized interactive LLM, predicting or simulating user behavior can be more challenging due to difficulties in data collection and the limitations of few-shot model training.

Ethics Statement

We acknowledge the potential for misuse of emotionally supportive and persuasive systems. These tools could be misappropriated to impersonate humans or manipulate users’ beliefs in harmful ways. To mitigate such risks, we strongly advocate for transparency, user consent, and regulatory oversight. Users should always be informed when interacting with AI systems rather than humans. We urge the development of clear regulatory frameworks, especially for high-stakes domains like mental health and donations, to ensure that persuasive targets remain aligned with ethical, factual, and legal standards.

In our experiments, we used open-source datasets, including PsyDTCorpus
Xie et al. 2024a
, PsyQA
Sun et al. 2021
, and Persuasion For Good
Wang et al. 2019
. These datasets were used in accordance with their respective licenses.
All personally identifiable information was removed from these datasets.
For the human ratings and user study, we prioritized the comfort and well-being of participants. Participants were informed about the purpose of our research and had the option to withdraw at any time if they felt uncomfortable.
Consequently, we confidently assert that our research is conducted in strict adherence to the ethical guidelines prescribed by the Association for Computational Linguistics (ACL).

A.3
Reliability of User Simulator

It is a fact that relying solely on LLMs to simulate user behaviors could still bring additional variances. However, our system benefits from the simulator by improving alignment with real user responses. LLM simulators can indeed provide human-like responses, helping alleviate data scarcity during training. Specifically, our target is only to simulate the user’s responses in some specific interactive scenarios (emotional support & persuasion for good) rather than modeling the full range of user behaviors.

We evaluated the consistency between the user simulator and real humans by presenting 50 conversation pairs (human-supporter vs. simulator-supporter) to 3 annotators. Each annotator labeled conversations as "human," "simulator," or "cannot identify," whose results are 48%: 32%: 20%.

A.4
Comparison Method Details

For Strategy Selection and Knowledge Selection, we ask GPT-4o to generated accurate strategies and relevant knowledge to assist the response generation. Then, we compute the similarities of the generated responses and the GPT-4o generated contents, and select the response with the highest similarity score as the final response.
