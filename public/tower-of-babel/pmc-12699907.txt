Inflammasome activation contributes to cisplatin resistance in ovarian cancer
Authors: Josiany Carlos de Souza, Tatiana Massariol Pimenta, Bárbara da Silva Martins, José Matheus Simões Padilha, Solenny Maria Silva Butzene, Milleny Ganho Marçal, Leticia Batista Azevedo Rangel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEpithelial ovarian cancer (EOC) is an alarming malignancy with frequent relapse and resistance to chemotherapy. Understanding the mechanism related to these phenotypes is urgent. Here, we investigated the roles of inflammasomes, NLRP1 and NLRP3, and pyroptosis in EOC progression and resistance to treatment.MethodsCell viability under cisplatin (CDDP) treatment was measured with diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT), and the IC50 values were calculated for the A2780, ACRP, and OVCAR3 cell lines. The levels of cytokines (interleukin-1β, IL-6, and TNFα) present in the supernatant were measured via ELISA. Caspase-1 activation was detected through the Caspase-Glo® 1 Assay. The cell death profile was determined via flow cytometry using Annexin V/PI staining, and the formation of pores in the cell membrane was measured using PI. A wound healing assay was used to investigate the effects of treatment with CDDP combined with a caspase-1 inhibitor (Ac-YVAD-CHO) on cell migration.ResultsIC50 values indicate increasing CDDP resistance across the following cell lines: A2780 (10.41 μM), ACRP (35.92 μM), and OVCAR3 (43.52 μM). Cytokine secretion and Caspase-1 activation were greater in treated cells than in untreated cells. CDDP-treated cells exhibited increased lytic cell death and pore formation. Caspase-1 inhibition during treatment with CDDP reduced wound closure, indicating reduced cell migration.ConclusionOur findings suggest that inflammasome activation and pyroptosis are mechanisms associated with ovarian cancer chemoresistance to CDDP, contributing to the devastating scenario of this disease. Targeting the NLRP1 and NLRP3 pathways could represent a promising strategy to improve OC treatment.Keywords: EOC, NLRP1, NLRP3, Caspase-1, Pyroptosis

Background

Ovarian cancer

Ovarian cancer (OC) is a deadly gynecological disease, mainly due to late diagnosis. After standard treatment, more than 80% of patients relapse, which results in a poor prognosis [67]. In 2022, 324,603 cases of ovarian cancer were diagnosed worldwide, while approximately 206,956 deaths occurred [21]. In the United States, the American Cancer Society estimated 20,890 new cases of ovarian cancer and 12,730 deaths from the disease in 2025 [63].

As a heterogeneous disease, OC is typically subdivided into histological subtypes that present different cells of origin, molecular compositions, and clinical aspects that impact prognosis and therapeutic approaches. [77, 83, 87]. The histological subtypes include non-epithelial OC, which is subdivided into germ cell and sex cord stromal cancers; rare small cell carcinoma; and ovarian sarcoma, which represents 10% of cases [11, 54]. Epithelial ovarian cancers (EOCs) account for approximately 90% of all ovarian malignancies [86] and are classified into four main subtypes according to the type of differentiation of the epithelial component: serous, clear cell, mucinous, and endometrioid [28, 43]. High-grade serous ovarian carcinoma (HGSOC) accounts for approximately 70% of diagnosed EOCs, endometrioid tumors account for 10%, clear cell tumors account for less than 10%, mucinous tumors account for 3%, and low-grade serous tumors account for less than 5% of all EOC cases [68].

In a study with 8,050 patients and a median follow-up of 91 months, Yang et al. [81] identified primary OC as the leading cause of death (80.3%), especially among patients with the serous subtype. Moreover, compared with serous carcinomas, endometrioid, mucinous and clear cell subtypes are associated with significantly better survival [81]. With respect to the origin of HGSOC, several studies have indicated that it develops in the fallopian tube from serous tubal intraepithelial carcinoma. Owing to their invasive capacity, these lesions can migrate to the ovary and adjacent tissues, where they accumulate additional genetic, molecular and histological alterations [26, 62, 65]. Thus, serous tubal intraepithelial carcinoma is considered a probable precursor of HGSOC [62].

Genetic characteristics

EOCs are subclassified into type I and type II tumors according to their genetic and molecular features, which influence tumor development [33]. In summary, type I tumors are commonly low grade, have a high frequency of mutations in the Ras pathway, generally do not have mutations in either p53 or BRCA, have a relatively normal karyotype, and are poorly responsive to platinum-based therapy (cisplatin, CDDP). On the other hand, type II EOCs are typically de novo invasive tumors; therefore, they are high grade; have wild-type Ras, BRCA and p53 mutations; have alterations in DNA copy number; and are usually responsive to platinum derivatives [29, 30, 71].

The various genetic characteristics of OC may differ according to its intrinsic and extrinsic features. The study of protein and gene expression, specifically concerning the chemoresistant or chemosensitive characteristics of OC, may result in new therapeutic targets, since protein expression levels vary according to apoptosis, DNA repair, and the cell cycle, among other cellular processes [1]. Identifying genetic mutations is important for effective chemotherapy and prognosis [16]. In HGSOC, the presence of four transcriptional subtypes (immunoreactive, differentiated, proliferative, and mesenchymal) was confirmed by studies using de novo classification [30], in addition to other studies showing that HGSOC has prominent intraepithelial lymphocytes, marked nuclear atypia, and abundant mitoses [50]. Furthermore, studies have shown that approximately 10–24% of CO cases are associated with germline mutations, with approximately 15% of patients carrying BRCA1 or BRCA2 mutations [61]. All these features contribute to OC heterogeneity and its impact on treatment outcomes.

Treatment with CDDP and inflammasome activation

Treatment for OC consists of surgical removal of the ovaries and the affected adjacent organs and chemotherapy. Currently, platinum-based agents and taxanes are used as neoadjuvant and adjuvant chemotherapies [47]. The choice of therapeutic approach and chemotherapy class depends on the immunohistological classification of the tumor, as well as the staging and intrinsic characteristics of the patient, such as comorbidities [2, 32, 55].

Regarding EOC, the focus of the current study, the therapy is based on platinum-derived chemotherapeutics, more specifically CDDP [77]. CDDP is a metallic agent capable of binding to DNA and forming adducts; if not repaired, DNA damage causes cells to undergo apoptosis [7, 14]. This process includes mitochondria-mediated apoptosis [27]. Despite its toxic effects, such as nephrotoxicity, peripheral neurotoxicity, and ototoxicity, CDDP has been established for OC treatment, among other solid tumors [88]. When starting chemotherapy, the HGSOC has a good response,however, approximately 75% of patients experience tumor recurrence and develop resistance to platinum derivatives [2, 7]. Studies have investigated several mechanisms of OC chemoresistance to CDDP. We highlight the inflammasome pathway and its components that play important roles in the expression and activation of cytokines, possibly related to the acquisition of resistance in OC.

This widely used chemotherapeutic agent induces cellular stress, including the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), mitochondrial dysfunction, and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress [88], which can activate inflammasomes. For example, CDDP has been shown to activate the NLRP3 inflammasome in various cell types, including cochlear marginal cells [82], renal tubular epithelial cells [15], triple-negative breast cancer [80] and ovarian cancer [36]. This process can lead to pyroptosis, a form of inflammatory cell death characterized by the release of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-18 [82]. Understanding these mechanistic connections between CDDP-induced cellular stress responses and inflammasome signaling is crucial for developing strategies to mitigate the side effects associated with CDDP therapy and enhance its therapeutic efficacy.

Inflammasomes and chemoresistance

Studies indicate that the inflammatory process surrounding the tumor microenvironment (TME) is a key factor associated with the development, progression and acquisition of a chemoresistant phenotype of EOC [37, 56, 72]. The inflammatory process begins with the activation of pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) in response to pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) or damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), which trigger the activation of cell signaling pathways that result in the release of proinflammatory cytokines [13, 49]. PRRs can be classified into the following classes: Toll-like receptors (TLRs), nucleotide-binding domain-like receptors (NLRs), RIG-I-like receptors (RLRs), C-type lectin receptors (CLRs), absent in melanoma II (AIM2), and pyrins [13, 19, 42, 59]. In this context, the ability of the NLRP1, NLRP3, and NLRC4 subtypes of the NLR receptor family to form large protein complexes called inflammasomes, which are important signaling components of the innate immune response and inflammation and are associated with the tumorigenesis process, has been well described in several studies [20, 45, 59, 70].

Inflammasome assembly can occur through two main pathways: canonical and noncanonical. In the canonical pathway, inflammasome formation occurs in two stages: In the preparation stage, stimuli such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS) induce the activation of TLRs, NLRs, and cytokine receptors, culminating in the activation of nuclear factor kappa B (NF-kB). NF-kB stimulates transcription processes, increasing the expression of NLRP3 and precursor forms of the proinflammatory cytokines interleukin (IL)−1β and IL-18. In the second stage, known as the activation stage, in the presence of secondary signals, such as extracellular ATP, K + ion efflux, or mitochondrial ROS (mtROS), NLRP3 oligomerizes and recruits the apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a caspase recruitment domain (ASC). This protein, in turn, recruits pro-caspase-1, forming the inflammasome complex [5, 10, 18, 20, 24, 45]. This complex is responsible for activating Caspase-1, inducing proteolytic cleavage that promotes the conversion of the proinflammatory cytokines pro-interleukin-1β and pro-interleukin-18 into their active forms, IL-1β and IL-18, respectively. The release of these cytokines into the extracellular domain induces the differentiation, recruitment, and proliferation of immune system cells [22, 25]. Furthermore, studies have shown that the function of IL-1β in the TME contributes to angiogenesis, tumor cell adhesion, and invasion into the extracellular matrix [74]. Helping with tumor development and metastasis.

Pyroptosis and chemoresistance

As part of the pathway involving the inflammasome, Caspase-1 activation is also responsible for the proteolytic cleavage of gasdermin D (GSDMD), which releases its N-terminal fragment that oligomerizes in the plasma membrane and initiates a form of programmed cell death known as pyroptosis. In this process, pores are formed in the cell membrane, leading to cell lysis [9, 25]. In noncanonical inflammasome assembly, the presence of lipopolysaccharides (LPS) in the cytosol induces “noncanonical” activation of the inflammasome, which results in the activation of caspase-4/5, which directly cleaves GSDMD, inducing a secondary process of cell death by pyroptosis [9, 10]. Pyroptosis is a highly inflammatory form of programmed cell death and has emerged as a critical component of the tumor dynamics of EOC, particularly in the context of CDDP resistance [39]. Pyroptosis also directly influences the TME by affecting immune cell infiltration and activation [75].

The differential expression of pyroptosis-related genes enables the classification of EOC into three distinct immunological profiles: immune-desert, characterized by low immune cell infiltration and local immunosuppression; immune-inflamed, characterized by substantial infiltration of activated T lymphocytes and a pronounced inflammatory response; and immune-excluded, in which immune cells are retained at the tumor periphery without effective penetration into the neoplastic tissue [38]. Recent studies have proposed a pyroptosis-related gene score (PRG score), in which lower values reflect reduced expression of these genes. Tumors with a low PRG score present a greater mutational burden, improved prognosis, and increased responsiveness to both chemotherapy and immunotherapy. These findings highlight pyroptosis as a potential target for TME remodeling and overcoming therapeutic resistance [38].

Lu and colleagues [40] emphasized that pyroptosis can be induced by various therapeutic strategies, including chemotherapeutic agents, natural compounds, and nanomaterials [40]. CDDP can trigger gasdermin E (GSDME)-mediated pyroptosis in tumor cells, suggesting that the modulation of gasdermin expression may influence tumor cell sensitivity to chemotherapy [85]. Moreover, combining chemotherapeutic agents with DNA methylation inhibitors can restore GSDME expression and promote pyroptosis in previously resistant tumor cells [34, 73, 79]. These therapeutic approaches aim not only to eliminate tumor cells but also to remodel the TME in a manner that supports antitumor immune responses. In summary, pyroptosis represents a critical pathway at the intersection of inflammation, CDDP resistance, and TME remodeling in EOC. A deeper understanding of the mechanisms regulating inflammasome activation and pyroptosis execution may open new avenues for developing adjuvant therapies aimed at reversing chemoresistance and enhancing treatment efficacy against EOC.

Here, we hypothesize that inflammasome activation is a key mechanism for OC resistance to CDDP. To understand the role of the inflammasomes NLRP1 and NLRP3, as well as pyroptosis, in OC chemoresistance to CDDP, we used 3 ovarian cancer cell lines. A2780, a cell line sensitive to CDDP. ACRP, a lineage with induced resistance to CDDP, is caused by treatment with CDDP [60]. OVCAR3, a multiresistant cell line, was also used in the experiments; this lineage has innate chemoresistance to CDDP. Briefly, cell viability was measured via MTT assays, and the IC50 was determined. The levels of the cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) present in the supernatant were quantified via ELISA. Caspase-1 activation was detected via the Caspase-Glo® 1 Assay. The cell death profile was determined by cytometry via Annexin V/PI, and the formation of pores in the cell membrane was detected via PI. To investigate the effects of treatment with CDDP alone or in combination with a caspase-1 inhibitor (caspase-1i, Ac-YVAD-CHO) on cell migration, we performed a wound healing assay.

Methods

Cell lines and cell culture

Three different cell lines were used in the experiments. A2780 (National Cancer Institute—INCa, Brazil), ovarian endometrioid carcinoma tumor cells from an untreated patient without mutations in p53. ACRP derived from A2780 when progressively treated with CDDP became resistant to the drug (Federal University of Espirito Santo). OVCAR3, which represents the HGSOC provided from malignant ascites of a patient with progressive ovarian carcinoma, is intrinsically resistant to CDDP, doxorubicin, and melphalan (Cell Bank of the Federal University of Rio de Janeiro, Brazil). The cell lines were cultured in DMEM (Gibco) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco), 1% (v/v) stabilized penicillin (100 units/mL), streptomycin (100 μg/mL) solution (Gibco, USA), and 1% (v/v) amphotericin B (Sigma‒Aldrich, Germany) and kept in an incubator at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2.

Resistance induction

To generate the ACRP cell line, a model of acquired resistance to CDDP, we treated the A2780 cell line with increasing doses of CDDP based on the protocol of Sherman-Baust et al. [60]. We gave the cells time to recover from the treatment before adding higher concentrations of CDDP. After several weeks of treatment, we measured resistance via the MTT assay.

MTT assay

The cytotoxic effect of CDDP (Acoord Farmaceutica) was investigated via a 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)−2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay (Sigma‒Aldrich, Germany) [53]. Briefly, cells were seeded (1 × 104 cells/well) on 96-well plates and allowed to adhere for at least 24 h. The cells were treated with CDDP at 0.78 µM to 100 µM for 24 h. The concentrations of CDDP used for each cell line are presented in Fig. 1. Drug-free medium or medium supplemented with 1X phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was added to the cells for the experimental controls. The plates were incubated with 0.5 mg/mL MTT solution for 4 h in the dark. The tetrazolium crystals were dissolved in DMSO. MTT absorbance was measured at 540 nm in a spectrophotometer (Epoch, Agilent Technologies, USA). GraphPad Prism software version 8.0 was used for the IC50 calculations.

The concentrations were between 0.78 µM and 100 µM, and the control conditions included only vehicle or 1X vehicle + PBS. N = 3. ns = nonsignificant; *p ≤ 0.05; **p ≤ 0.01; ***p ≤ 0.001; ****p ≤ 0.0001

Dosages of IL-1β, IL-6 and TNFα by ELISA

Interleukin-1β, IL-6, and TNFα secreted in ACRP-conditioned culture medium were quantified via ELISA (BD Biosciences, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The cytokine concentration was calculated via linear regression derived from the standard curve and is presented in pg/mL.

Detection of caspase-1 activation

To detect the activation of Caspase-1, we used the Caspase-Glo® 1 Assay Kit (Promega, USA). The cells were seeded in a 96-well plate. After 24 h, the cells were treated with CDDP for 24 h, the reagents were added, and the luminescence was measured with a Synergy® H1 Microplate Reader (Agilent Technologies, USA).

Flow cytometry analysis

A2780, ACRP, and OVCAR3 cell lines were seeded at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well in forty-eight-well plates and treated with CDDP at concentrations corresponding to the IC25, IC50, and IC75, as indicated in Table 1. After 24 h of treatment, the culture supernatant and adherent cells were collected and resuspended in binding buffer (10 mM HEPES, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM KCl, 1 mM MgCl₂, and 1.8 mM CaCl₂). The samples were incubated with Annexin V-FITC and subsequently stained with propidium iodide (PI) according to the manufacturer's instructions (Quatro G Biotecnologia, Brazil). Flow cytometric analysis was performed on a BD Accuri® C6 flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA), and 10,000 events per sample were recorded. The data were analyzed via FlowJo software, version 10.10.0.

Plasma membrane pore formation

PI was used to analyze pore formation in the membranes of cells treated with CDDP. Briefly, the cells were seeded at 1 × 104 cells/well in a black 96-well plate. After 24 h, the cells were treated with CDDP at inhibitory concentrations, and PI was added to the media at 3 μg/mL. Luminescence was detected for 6 h in a Synergy® H1 Microplate Reader (Agilent Technologies, USA).

Wound healing assay

The migration of cells treated with CDDP alone or in combination with a Caspase-1 inhibitor (Ac-YVAD-CHO) was assessed with a scratch assay. For experiments with A2780 and ACRP cells, 1 × 105 cells/well were seeded in twenty-four-well plates, and for OVCAR3 cells, 0.6 × 105 cells/well were seeded. The cells were cultured until they reached 80–90% confluency. Then, they were treated with mitomycin C (5 μg/mL) (Sigma), an agent that inhibits cell proliferation, and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h at 5% CO2. A 200 µL pipette tip was used to create a straight scratch across the center of each well. Then, the cells were washed with 1X PBS to remove detached cells, and fresh culture medium with treatments was added. Images were acquired at 0 h and 24 h after treatment for all the cell lines and at 48 h for OVCAR3 cells. An inverted microscope (ZEISS Microscopy; Carl Zeiss AG, Germany) was used, and images were captured via a microscopy imaging system.

The wound area was quantified via ImageJ 1.54p, an open-source platform for biological image analysis [57]. For each condition, three scratches were evaluated, with measurements taken at three arbitrarily defined points in each. Statistical analysis

All the results are presented as the means ± standard deviations (SDs) of at least three independent experiments. Statistical analyses were performed via GraphPad Prism version 8.0 for Windows. The statistical tests applied were unpaired Student’s t test, one-way ANOVA, or two-way ANOVA followed by the post hoc Bonferroni correction. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

ACRP cells increase their cytokine secretion when treated with CDDP

The levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNFα were detected via ELISA, which revealed that treatment with CDDP tends to increase the levels of proinflammatory cytokines in OC cells resistant to CDDP (ACRP) during drug treatment (Fig. 3). The decrease in the levels at IC75 can be partially explained by the lower number of viable cells. Interestingly, even at concentrations that kill approximately 50% of the cells, after 24 h, the cytokine levels were higher than those in the control condition. These findings indicate that CDDP-treated cells secrete more cytokines to stimulate the immune system.

Cells resistant to CDDP have increased levels of Caspase-1 activation

The activity of Caspase-1 is represented in Fig. 4, which shows how treatment with CDDP can increase the activation of the inflammasome pathway. Even when the cells were treated for 24 h and some were killed, the remaining cells presented increased enzyme activity. Curiously, the resistant cell lines ACRP and OCVAR3 presented higher levels of Caspase-1 activation than did the sensitive cell line A2780. These findings indicate a link between CDDP chemoresistance and inflammasome activation.

Lytic cell death is the main type of CDDP-resistant cell death

To evaluate the impact of treatment on the type of cell death, we conducted an Annexin V/PI assay. Pyroptosis emerged as the predominant form of cell death in CDDP-treated cells, with the exception of A2780 cells. For the lineage sensitive to CDDP, there was no difference between lytic and apoptotic cell death (Fig. 5). These findings indicate increased levels of lytic cell death in CDDP-resistant OC cells following treatment and align with the other data (Figs. 6 and 7). The predominance of pyroptosis over apoptosis implies that cell death is driven by inflammasome activation.

Treatment induces pore formation in treated cells

The detection of pore formation on the cell membrane was conducted via PI, which binds to DNA when the cell membrane becomes permeable. The luminescence levels were greater for the treated samples (Fig. 8). The small difference can be explained by the assay conditions, which were performed for 6 h outside the incubator. Even maintaining 37° in the reader and using media with HEPES. Initially, the values were similar; however, after 3 h, the cells treated with CDDP exhibited increased pore formation, indicating lytic cell death. The fact that the control condition does not follow this pattern can indicate that some cells undergo lytic cell death, but the degree of apoptotic death in the absence of treatment is higher.

Discussion

Chemoresistance in OCs can occur through several mechanisms. The cellular changes that characterize chemoresistance can hinder the influx and/or increase the efflux of CDDP, resulting in low concentrations of the drug in the cytoplasm [64]. Another resistance mechanism is the ability to repair DNA damaged by CDDP or gain tolerance to damage to genetic material without the need to trigger apoptotic pathways. In addition, cellular changes can make apoptotic pathways unfeasible [64, 69]. Moreover, the inflammatory TME is a key factor associated with the development, progression and acquisition of a chemoresistant phenotype of EOC, mainly due to the exacerbated production of inflammatory mediators, such as reactive oxygen species (ROS), cytokines and growth factors, which contribute to increased genomic instability, cell division, immune evasion, survival and cell transformation [8, 46, 76]. The evidence presented in this study suggests that inflammasome activation contributes to CDDP resistance in OC (Fig. 13).

Proposed mechanism: CDDP–induced stress promotes the generation of DAMPs, which activate the NLRP3 and NLRP1 inflammasomes. This leads to Caspase-1 activation, GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis, and the release of IL-1β and IL-18. In parallel, DAMP recognition also triggers NF-κB activation, increasing the transcription of pro-interleukins as well as the levels of the NLRP1 and NLRP3 components. Collectively, these events establish a proinflammatory TME that fosters therapeutic resistance in ovarian cancer

Three EOC cell lines were used to represent the most common OC group: one cell line sensitive to CDDP (A2780), one cell line with acquired resistance to CDDP (ACRP), and one cell line with innate resistance to CDDP (OVCAR3). OVCAR3 also represents the most common EOC type, the HGSOC. To identify the role of inflammasome activation in chemoresistance to CDDP, the IC50 was calculated, and the IC25 and IC75 were subsequently determined. These data demonstrate how CDDP can increase the secretion of cytokines in OC cells resistant to drugs. TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 are representative proinflammatory cytokines typically transcribed following NF-κB activation and are important transcription factors related to the inflammasome pathway [8, 23]. IL-1β was increased in ACRP-treated cells. This cytokine is a direct product of inflammasome NLRP1 and NLRP3 activation [3], helping in tumor progression throughout chronic inflammation [41]. IL-6 acts as a proinflammatory cytokine that promotes cell proliferation and inhibits apoptosis, metastasis, and chemoresistance in OC cells [66]. Gupta et al. [17] associated ovarian carcinogenesis with TNF-α levels in patient samples. Interestingly, TNF-α and IL-6 expression is more common in HGSOC than in endometrioid carcinoma [17]. In a mouse model of advanced OC, TNF-α signaling promoted tumor progression [6]. This evidence highlights the proinflammatory phenotype of OC. Ponton-Almodovar et al. [51] emphasized how the TME may influence the acquisition of chemoresistance in OC since several immune cells orchestrate cellular responses through the release of cytokines and chemokines [51].

The enzyme caspase-1 is a key component of the inflammasome pathway and is increased in OC cells under treated conditions. Curiously, the chemoresistant cell lines presented increased levels, indicating that Caspase-1 activation is increased in resistant live cells under treatment, which could indicate its importance in cell survival during chemotherapy. Additionally, lytic cell death was greater in ACRP and OVCAR3 cells than in control cells, indicating that pyroptosis occurs more frequently in these cell lines. Unlike apoptosis, pyroptosis is primarily mediated by the activation of Caspase-1 through inflammasomes such as NLRP3 and NLRP1, leading to the cleavage of GSDMD and the subsequent formation of pores in the cell membrane. This process ultimately results in the release of proinflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β and IL-18 [52]. Curiously, GSDMD is upregulated in OC [4]. Recent studies have demonstrated that NLRP3 overexpression is associated with CDDP resistance in EOC cells. Silencing of this inflammasome results in the reversal of the malignant phenotype by inhibiting cell proliferation, migration, and epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), as well as promoting apoptosis in CDDP-resistant cells [36]. These findings suggest that inflammasome activation may contribute to the maintenance of the TME, which favors chemoresistance.

Cell migration decreased when caspase-1 was inhibited in the treated cells, indicating that this pathway could be important for cell spreading. More significant differences were noted for OVCAR3. However, ACRP also promoted less migration in response to Caspase-1i monotherapy, which suggests that the ACRP pathway plays a role in cell migration even without CDDP. Studies investigating the role of NLRP3 inflammasome inhibition in OC cells and mice revealed a reduction in the cell proliferation rate and the suppression of EOC in vivo [48]. NLRP3 silencing causes antiproliferative, antimigrative, anti-invasive, and proapoptotic effects, and EMT is blocked in OC cells [36]. These findings indicate that inflammasome pathway modulation could represent an important target against OC, contributing to improved CDDP treatment outcomes.

Considering that CDDP often triggers inflammasome activation, thereby promoting inflammation and therapy resistance [82], exploring the clinical relevance of the NLRP3 [44] and NLRP1 [84] inflammasomes in cancer highlights their promise as molecular targets. In this context, inflammasome inhibitors have emerged as attractive strategies in OC treatment, particularly when combined with CDDP [31]. Notably, inflammasome blockade not only enhances CDDP efficacy but also may mitigate treatment-related side effects [35]. Consistent with these findings, the inhibition of NLRP3 reduces inflammation and sensitizes cancer cells to chemotherapy, including CDDP [36]. Recent studies combining CDDP, PARP inhibition (olaparib), an inflammasome-targeting agent (MCC950) and an IL-1β inhibitor (AS101) demonstrated synergistic antitumor activity, potentially through reshaping the TME, augmenting antitumor immunity, and promoting apoptosis [58].

In addition to being pharmacological inhibitors, phytochemicals have also shown promise as multitarget agents against the TLR/NF-κB/NLRP axis, reversing chemoresistance and improving responses to chemotherapy and immunotherapy in OC [12]. In support of the broader therapeutic potential of inflammasome modulation, in vivo mesothelioma models demonstrated that combining CDDP with an IL-1R antagonist (anakinra) significantly reduced the tumor burden compared with that with CDDP alone [78]. Together, these findings underscore the translational potential of targeting inflammasomes, particularly NLRP1 and NLRP3, as cotherapeutic strategies in OC. Future directions include the development of targeted delivery systems and predictive biomarkers to optimize patient selection, with the goals of enhancing therapeutic efficacy, reducing toxicity, and advancing personalized medicine approaches in gynecologic oncology.

While this study provides insights into the role of inflammasome signaling in CDDP resistance, several aspects merit further investigation. For example, we aimed to expand on these findings by exploring both the pharmacological inhibition of NLRP3 and the pharmacological inhibition of NLRP1. Preliminary data from our group suggest that the NLRP3 inhibitor MCC950 (TargetMol), when combined with CDDP, reduces cell viability (data not shown). Although Caspase-1 activation and lytic cell death were observed, future experiments assessing GSDMD cleavage as a marker of pyroptosis could provide additional confirmation of the mechanisms proposed. In the present work, we employed a Caspase-1 inhibitor to block the enzyme activated downstream of NLRP1 and NLRP3, which is important for GSDMD cleavage. Moreover, as our results were obtained in vitro, while these results provide important mechanistic insights, extending these observations to in vivo systems will be important to strengthen their translational relevance. Addressing these points in future research will serve to complement and expand upon the current findings.

Conclusions

This study revealed how the induction of chemoresistance in ACRP is promising and represents a good model of study, considering the similar IC50 values between this cell line and OVCAR3, which are known to have intrinsic resistance to CDDP. We observed increased secretion of cytokines in ACRP cells after treatment with CDDP. With respect to Caspase-1 activation, we observed that this enzyme has greater activity in treated cells, especially in chemoresistant cell lines (ACRP and OVCAR3). CDDP treatment dose-dependently increased the rates of lytic death in ACRP and OVCAR3 cells compared with the rate of apoptotic death. The Caspase-1 inhibitor, when combined with CDDP, decreased the cell migration capacity, indicating that the pathway is related to cell development and migration. Together, these data provide evidence of a potential association between OC chemoresistance to CDDP and inflammasome activation and pyroptosis. This pathway could represent a promising strategy to improve OC treatment.
