Diffuse high-grade glioma with gliomatosis cerebri pattern mimicking symmetric white matter disease
Authors: Salma Abouchiba, Zaid Ennasery, Hajar Ouazzani, Ismail Chaouche, Amal Akammar, Nizar El Bouardi, Meriem Haloua, Badr Alami, Moulay Youssef Alaoui Lamrani, Meryem Boubbou, Mustapha Maaroufi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiffuse gliomas with gliomatosis cerebri (GC) growth pattern are rare infiltrative tumors whose radiological presentation frequently mimics non-neoplastic white matter diseases. Strictly bilateral and symmetric involvement represents an exceptionally rare presentation, powerfully evoking leukodystrophy or metabolic leukoencephalopathy and constituting a major diagnostic pitfall. We report a 46-year-old woman with a 7-month history of progressive cognitive decline, dysarthria, and spastic paraparesis. CT demonstrated a hypodense corpus callosum lesion without enhancement. MRI revealed diffuse bilateral and remarkably symmetric T2/FLAIR white matter hyperintensities extending through the corpus callosum without gadolinium enhancement. Diffusion-weighted imaging showed focal restricted diffusion at lesion margins and within the splenium. MR spectroscopy demonstrated elevated choline (Cho/Cr ≈2), decreased N-acetylaspartate, prominent myo-inositol elevation, and a subtle creatine increase. Stereotactic biopsy confirmed high-grade diffuse glial proliferation with 5 mitoses per 10 high-power fields and IDH wild-type status. Unlike previously reported GC cases mimicking demyelinating disease with asymmetric multifocal involvement and low-grade histology, this case is distinguished by its strict bilateral symmetry and high-grade IDH wild-type biology. Symmetry does not exclude malignancy, and multimodal advanced MRI is essential to avoid diagnostic delay.Keywords: Gliomatosis cerebri, Diffuse glioma, White matter disease, MRI, Spectroscopy, Diffusion imaging

Introduction

Gliomatosis cerebri (GC) is recognized by the 2021 WHO Classification of CNS Tumors not as a distinct entity but as a growth pattern of diffuse infiltrative gliomas, defined by neoplastic glial infiltration of 3 or more cerebral lobes with frequent bilateral extension and possible involvement of deep gray matter, brainstem, and cerebellum [1]. The clinical presentation is heterogeneous, encompassing cognitive decline, behavioral changes, seizures, and focal neurological deficits evolving over weeks to months. Radiologically, GC is a well-established mimic of non-neoplastic white matter diseases, and several reports have documented cases initially attributed to demyelination, encephalitis, or vasculitis [2,3]. Relative preservation of the underlying architecture and the frequent absence of a discrete enhancing mass render GC one of the most radiologically deceptive brain tumors, creating substantial overlap with non-neoplastic white matter diseases, and definitive diagnosis relies on tissue biopsy [[2], [3], [4]].

The closest published parallel to our case is that of Mingomataj et al. [3], who described GC mimicking diffuse demyelinating disease confirmed by elevated myo-inositol on spectroscopy and biopsy revealing a low-grade (WHO grade II) infiltrating astrocytoma [3]. However, the bilateral involvement in that case was multifocal and non-symmetric. Our case presents with a strictly mirror-image bilateral symmetry, an even rarer pattern that far more powerfully evokes a hereditary or metabolic leukoencephalopathy, combined with high-grade IDH wild-type histology—a combination not previously documented in the literature.

Case presentation

A 46-year-old woman with no significant past medical history presented with a 7-month history of progressive neurological deterioration. Symptoms began with diffuse headaches, followed by dysarthria, psychomotor slowing, spatial disorientation, insomnia, and sphincter dysfunction. She had been incorrectly attributed a psychiatric etiology and treated with anxiolytics and antidepressants without improvement. One month before admission, her condition worsened markedly with progressive spastic paraparesis and gait instability. There was no history of fever, toxic exposure, or family history of neurological disease.

Neurological examination revealed frontal lobe dysfunction with cognitive impairment, dysarthria, and spastic paraparesis with bilateral pyramidal signs. Laboratory investigations, autoimmune panels, thyroid function, and full cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis including opening pressure, cell count, protein, glucose, microbiology, and immunological studies were all unremarkable, effectively excluding infectious and inflammatory etiologies.

Imaging findings

Noncontrast CT demonstrated a poorly defined hypodense lesion centered on the corpus callosum with bilateral periventricular extension, without mass effect or enhancement, raising initial suspicion of a demyelinating or toxic-metabolic process (Fig. 1).

Axial noncontrast brain CT images (A and B) demonstrate a focal hypodense lesion centered within the body of the corpus callosum (red circles). This abnormality is better delineated on coronal (E) and sagittal (F) reformatted images (arrows). Subtle, ill-defined bilateral periventricular and centrum semiovale hypodensity is also noted, representing an additional, more diffuse imaging feature of this case. Pre and postcontrast brain CT was performed. Following intravenous administration of iodinated contrast material, no enhancement is observed (C and D).

Brain MRI confirmed diffuse involvement of the corpus callosum, extending through its body, isthmus, and splenium, hypointense on T1 and markedly hyperintense on T2/FLAIR. The most diagnostically challenging feature was the strictly bilateral and symmetric T2/FLAIR hyperintensity of the periventricular and subcortical white matter of the frontal and parietal lobes, with mild expansion of affected white matter tracts. No gadolinium enhancement was observed and no spinal lesions were identified. Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) revealed focal restricted diffusion at lesion margins and within the splenium, with corresponding low ADC values, indicating areas of increased tumor cellularity within the diffusely infiltrative background (Fig. 2).

Sagittal T1-weighted (A), coronal T2-weighted (B), and axial FLAIR (C, D) images demonstrate that the focal abnormality initially identified on brain CT is clearly depicted on MRI, appearing as a T1 hypointense and markedly T2 hyperintense lesion (yellow arrows). However, this lesion is not isolated and is associated with extensive bilateral and symmetric T2/FLAIR hyperintense signal abnormalities involving the periventricular and subcortical white matter, distributed on both sides of the corpus callosum abnormalities. Diffusion-weighted imaging and corresponding ADC maps (E and F) show peripheral areas of diffusion restriction within these white matter lesions, as well as more pronounced restriction within the splenium of the corpus callosum (red arrows), suggesting regions of increased cellularity within a diffuse infiltrative process. The regions involved do not show enhancement on T1 SE after Gadolinium Administration (G and H).

MR spectroscopy (Multi-Voxel) performed with short echo time (TE = 35 ms) and long echo time (144 ms), without administration of a contrast agent, demonstrated a metabolic profile strongly supporting a tumoral process. Long echo time demonstrated elevated choline reflecting increased membrane turnover and decreased N-acetylaspartate (NAA) indicating neuronal loss, yielding a Cho/Cr ratio of approximately 2. Short TE additionally revealed prominent myo-inositol elevation, a marker of glial proliferation commonly elevated in diffuse infiltrative gliomas [5,6]. A subtle creatine increase relative to contralateral normal-appearing white matter was also noted, consistent with altered cellular energy metabolism [7] (Fig. 3).

Magnetic resonance spectroscopy performed with short echo time (TE = 35 ms) (A and B) and long echo time (TE = 144 ms) (C and D) demonstrates region-dependent metabolic profiles consistent with a diffuse glial tumor. Over the corpus callosum/splenium lesion, short TE acquisition (A and B) reveals a prominent elevation of myo-inositol, supporting glial proliferation, in keeping with a lower-grade metabolic profile. Over the left periventricular lesion, long TE acquisition (C and D) shows a clear increase in the choline peak associated with a decreased N-acetylaspartate (NAA) peak, resulting in an elevated Cho/NAA of 2.3 and a Cho/Cr ratio of approximately 2, in keeping with a higher-grade metabolic profile. A slight increase in creatine is also noted compared to normal brain parenchyma.

Histopathological findings

Given the progressive clinical course, the absence of a clear non-neoplastic etiology on laboratory and CSF investigations, and the multimodal imaging findings strongly suggesting a tumoral process, a stereotactic biopsy of the corpus callosum lesion was performed, targeting the area that exhibited the most diffusion restriction.

Multiple core biopsy specimens from the corpus callosum demonstrated a high-density glial tumor arranged in diffuse sheets on a fibrillary background, with relative preservation of the underlying neural architecture. Neoplastic cells were small to intermediate, displaying round to ovoid densely hyperchromatic nuclei, thick nuclear membranes, and marked anisokaryosis, dissociated by polymorphous inflammatory elements and thin-walled branching capillaries with regular endothelium. Mitotic figures were identified at 5 per 10 high-power fields. Ki-67 proliferation index and mitotic activity in the sampled tissue were not markedly elevated; the WHO Grade 4 designation for this tumor rests on its molecular profile rather than on these histological parameters alone (see Discussion). Occasional cells showed moderately abundant eosinophilic gemistocytic cytoplasm.

Immunohistochemistry demonstrated strong GFAP positivity and a Ki-67 proliferation index of approximately 15%-20%. IDH1 R132H immunostaining was negative; molecular analysis confirmed IDH wild-type status, classifying the tumor as adult-type diffuse glioma, IDH wild-type, with GC growth pattern, corresponding to WHO Grade 4 behavior [1] (Fig. 4).

(B) ×100: infiltrative neoplastic glial cells in a loose fibrillary stroma. (C) ×200: small to intermediate cells with hyperchromatic nuclei, thick nuclear membranes, marked anisokaryosis, and occasional gemistocytic differentiation; mitotic activity 5/10 HPF.

Clinical course and management

Following histopathological and molecular confirmation of a WHO Grade 4, IDH-wildtype diffuse glioma with a gliomatosis cerebri growth pattern, the case was discussed in a multidisciplinary tumor board. Given the diffuse, unresectable extent of disease, the patient was treated with radiotherapy. Management was subsequently transitioned to best supportive care.

Discussion

This case illustrates one of the rarest presentations of GC-pattern glioma: strictly bilateral and symmetric white matter infiltration closely simulating a leukodystrophy or metabolic leukoencephalopathy. The degree of symmetry is the defining feature that sets this case apart from most published reports. Compared to the closest analogue by Mingomataj et al. [3], 3 critical differences emerge. First, the white matter involvement in our case is mirror-image symmetric rather than multifocal and asymmetric, which is a far more powerful evocator of non-neoplastic etiologies and represents an even greater diagnostic trap. Second, IDH wild-type status confers WHO Grade 4 biological behavior in our tumor, a molecular criterion that applies regardless of the comparatively modest histological proliferation indices in the sampled tissue, with prognosis significantly worse than the low-grade astrocytoma described in the reference case. In GC-pattern gliomas, stereotactic biopsy may sample areas of lower histological grade within a biologically aggressive tumor, so molecular characterization (IDH status, TERT promoter mutation, CDKN2A/B deletion) rather than histological grade alone is essential for accurate grading and prognostication [1,4].

The differential diagnosis of bilateral symmetric white matter signal abnormalities is extensive, encompassing demyelinating diseases, leukodystrophies, toxic-metabolic encephalopathies, primary CNS lymphoma, and diffuse glioma.

Demyelinating diseases, particularly multiple sclerosis, typically produce multifocal ovoid periventricular lesions oriented perpendicular to the ventricles with dissemination in time and space, frequently with gadolinium enhancement during active phases [2]. The confluent, non-enhancing, and diffusely infiltrative pattern in our case is inconsistent with this diagnosis.

Leukodystrophies represent the most compelling alternative in this case given the striking symmetry. However, leukodystrophies typically affect younger patients, follow characteristic anatomical distribution patterns (eg, parieto-occipital predominance in X-ALD and frontal predominance in MLD), and are associated with specific metabolic or genetic markers [8]. The age of our patient, the absence of a family history, normal metabolic workup, and the progressive rapid clinical deterioration are inconsistent with most leukodystrophies.

Toxic-metabolic encephalopathies may produce symmetric white matter changes but are typically associated with a clear clinical context, often reversible, and associated with specific T2/FLAIR patterns (eg, posterior reversible encephalopathy and osmotic demyelination syndrome) [9]. The progressive, irreversible course in our patient argues strongly against these entities.

Primary CNS lymphoma may involve the corpus callosum and deep white matter but characteristically demonstrates marked diffusion restriction and uniform gadolinium enhancement, with a lipid-lactate peak on spectroscopy [10]. The non-enhancing, diffusely infiltrative appearance in our case is atypical for lymphoma.

HIV encephalitis also merits inclusion among the differential diagnoses of bilateral symmetric white matter disease, as it typically produces confluent, non-enhancing periventricular and deep white matter T2/FLAIR hyperintensity, with MR spectroscopy showing elevated choline, decreased NAA, and possible myo-inositol elevation that can overlap with the spectroscopic profile observed in our case [11]. In our patient, however, the absence of a recognized immunocompromised context, together with the focal corpus callosum lesion and areas of diffusion restriction, argued against this diagnosis.

Diffusion-weighted imaging provided a crucial diagnostic clue in this case. While gliomatosis cerebri typically demonstrates heterogeneous diffusion due to the coexistence of low-density infiltrative areas and higher-cellularity foci, the presence of focal restricted diffusion at lesion margins and within the splenium of the corpus callosum in our patient strongly favored a neoplastic process. Such findings are atypical for demyelinating disease in the non-acute phase and absent in most leukodystrophies [12].

MR spectroscopy was the single most informative advanced technique in establishing a tumoral diagnosis prior to biopsy. The elevation of myo-inositol is a particularly relevant marker in GC, as it reflects glial proliferation and has been identified as a potential indicator of gliomatosis cerebri growth pattern even when the Cho/Cr ratio is only mildly elevated [6]. The combination of elevated myo-inositol at short TE and an elevated Cho/Cr ratio of approximately 2 at long TE provides strong evidence for a diffuse glial tumor, as has been described in similar cases [3,13]. Perfusion MRI, unavailable in our patient, has also been shown to improve diagnostic accuracy in non-enhancing diffuse gliomas by identifying mildly elevated relative cerebral blood volume, contrasting with the normal rCBV of most white matter diseases [14].

The histological findings in the sampled corpus callosum/splenium tissue show a hypercellular diffuse glial proliferation with anisokaryosis, thick nuclear membranes, and mitotic and Ki-67 indices that, taken alone, are more in keeping with a lower-grade histological appearance, concordant with the lower-grade MR spectroscopic profile obtained from this same region. The absence of microvascular proliferation and necrosis in the sampled tissue does not exclude high-grade behavior in a focally biopsied GC-pattern tumor, as these features may be heterogeneously distributed and were not sampled from the more aggressive-appearing left periventricular component. This is precisely why, in GC-pattern gliomas, stereotactic biopsy may sample areas of lower histological grade within a biologically high-grade tumor, and why molecular characterization—rather than histological grade alone—is essential for accurate grading and prognostication [1,4]. IDH wild-type status, confirmed immunohistochemically and molecularly, is what places this tumor in the most aggressive category of adult diffuse gliomas—behavior equivalent to WHO Grade 4 glioblastoma—independent of the comparatively modest histological proliferation indices found on biopsy [1].

Conclusion

Diffuse glioma with GC growth pattern may exceptionally present as strictly bilateral and symmetric white matter infiltration, an even greater diagnostic challenge than the asymmetric bilateral forms previously reported, closely simulating leukodystrophies and metabolic leukoencephalopathies in which biopsy is rarely pursued early. Focal DWI restriction and a spectroscopic profile of elevated choline, reduced NAA, and prominent myo-inositol should raise strong suspicion for a tumoral process and prompt early histopathological confirmation. Symmetry does not exclude malignancy.
