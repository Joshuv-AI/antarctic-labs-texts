Analytical and data-driven fractional-order malaria transmission model with vector and non-vector pathways
Authors: Queeneth Ojoma Ahman, Patrick Agwu Okpara, Benedict Celestine Agbata, Emmanuel Olorunfemi Senewo, Ndidiamaka Edith Didigwu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClassical malaria models often focus solely on vector-borne transmission and employ integer-order dynamics that neglect memory effects. Yet malaria spread can also occur through non-vector exposure routes, and its progression is influenced by historical infection and immunity patterns. To capture these effects, a fractional-order modeling approach is required.MethodsWe develop a Caputo fractional-order malaria model of order that integrates both vector and non-vector transmission pathways while embedding memory effects in human and mosquito dynamics. Analytical properties—including positivity, boundedness, disease-free equilibrium, and fractional local stability—are derived. The Adams–Bashforth–Moulton (ABM) predictor–corrector scheme is implemented for numerical simulation and validated against the classical case (q = 1) to ensure accuracy and convergence.ResultsNumerical experiments reveal that decreasing the fractional order q substantially modifies malaria dynamics: epidemic peaks are delayed, oscillatory persistence is prolonged, and long-term infection memory is amplified. Parameter estimation and data fitting using weekly malaria incidence from the Nigeria Centre for Disease Control (NCDC) confirm the model’s reliability in reproducing outbreak patterns.ConclusionThe proposed fractional-order malaria model provides a unified analytical and computational framework that captures both memory-dependent and multi-route transmission effects. Introduction

Malaria continues to pose a major global health challenge, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa [1–3] . Classical integer-order SEIR-type models have advanced understanding of vaccination, treatment, and vector control strategies. However, such models assume that the system dynamics depend only on current states, overlooking memory and hereditary effects that are intrinsic to biological and epidemiological processes [4–6] . These limitations restrict their ability to capture delayed immune responses, persistent infection, and long-term host–vector interactions observed in real malaria dynamics.

Fractional-order differential equations (FDEs) provide a natural generalization of classical models by embedding memory and hereditary dependence into the system dynamics [7–10] . Their application across epidemiological systems—such as HIV, dengue, COVID-19, and tuberculosis—has demonstrated that fractional models can represent complex, nonlocal temporal interactions more realistically than integer-order formulations [11–14] . These models capture sub-exponential epidemic growth, prolonged decay, and recurrence phenomena that align with empirical data from many diseases.

For malaria specifically, several fractional models have been proposed to incorporate vaccination, prevention, treatment, and treatment-seeking behavior [15–23] . These works collectively reveal that fractional models alter the epidemic threshold, extend transient oscillations, and capture the persistence of infection beyond classical predictions. However, most existing fractional malaria models consider only vector-mediated transmission, whereas malaria can also spread through non-vector exposure pathways—such as transfusion, congenital, and accidental blood contact—which remain underrepresented in fractional frameworks.

Analytical solutions to FDEs are rarely attainable in closed form, which motivates the use of reliable numerical solvers. Among available approaches, the Adams–Bashforth–Moulton (ABM) predictor–corrector algorithm has proven efficient and stable for fractional systems [24–27] . It naturally accommodates memory effects while ensuring convergence and computational robustness [28–31] . Building on these developments, we extend the classical malaria model of Akowe et al. [20] to a Caputo fractional-order framework that integrates both vector and non-vector transmission pathways, supported by ABM-based numerical experiments and data-driven validation.

Recent research further emphasizes the relevance of fractional calculus for representing real-world systems characterized by memory, delay, and hereditary effects. Zhang et al. (2024) developed a fractional adaptive type-2 fuzzy structural control system that exploited memory properties to enhance predictive stability [32]. Vaganov et al. (2023) applied a fractional advection–dispersion model for contaminant transport, showing that fractional diffusion accurately captures anomalous flow characteristics [33]. Ullah et al. (2025) analyzed malaria transmission under socioeconomic structures, highlighting the importance of generalized non-Markovian frameworks [25]. Similarly, Lu and Osorio (2018) and Zhang et al. (2025) introduced fractional and semi-local approaches in complex networks, revealing how memory terms enhance realism in dynamic interaction systems [34, 35] . These studies affirm that fractional-order formulations offer superior realism and predictive fidelity in modeling natural and biological systems.

In the infectious-disease context, a variety of fractional models have been proposed, employing different numerical techniques and addressing diverse diseases. Representative examples include the dengue model of Ahmad et al. [4], the tuberculosis and malaria studies by Agbata et al. [10, 11] , the COVID-19 analysis by Sadikin et al. [3], the malaria model of Jaleta et al. [15], and the Lassa-fever model of Atokolo et al. [27]. In summary, this study advances the work of Akowe et al. By integrating rigorous mathematical analysis, ABM-based numerical simulation, and data fitting using weekly malaria incidence from the Nigeria Centre for Disease Control (NCDC) [36], the present framework bridges theoretical innovation with practical application, offering new insights into memory-dependent malaria dynamics and control.

Methods

This study therefore develops a fractional-order malaria model that explicitly integrates both vector-mediated and non-vector transmission routes while allowing for partial vaccine protection. Classical theories have long established that malaria can be transmitted through mosquito bites as well as via blood transfusion, congenital transmission, and accidental blood exposure [37, 38] . Furthermore, even vaccinated individuals may remain partially susceptible due to waning immunity [39]. These biological realities motivate our fractional model formulation, which accounts for memory effects, heterogeneous infection pathways, and incomplete vaccine efficacy.

Materials and methods

Model formulation

We consider the classical malaria transmission framework of Akowe et al. [20], which integrates both vector and non-vector pathways, and extend it to a fractional-order setting using the Caputo derivative. The model assumes that malaria transmission can occur not only through the traditional mosquito–human–mosquito route, but also via non-vector mechanisms such as blood transfusion, congenital transmission, and accidental blood contact, which have been documented in several studies [37, 38] . Although vaccination is an essential component of malaria control, it does not guarantee full protection against infection or exposure, as partial efficacy and waning immunity may still allow transmission [39]. This epidemiological reality motivates the inclusion of both vaccinated and unvaccinated human classes that remain susceptible to infection through vector and non-vector routes.

The total human population is divided into seven epidemiological compartments: susceptible (SH), vaccinated (VH), two classes of exposed individuals ( and ) representing infections acquired through vector and non-vector contacts respectively, infectious (IH), treated (TH), and recovered (RH). Similarly, the mosquito population consists of susceptible (SM), exposed (EM), and infectious (IM) classes. Physical and dimensional interpretation of the fractional order. The Caputo fractional derivative in Eq. (4) provides a mathematical means of incorporating memory into the system, such that the present rate of change depends on the entire past history of the variable rather than only its instantaneous value. In epidemiological terms, this “memory effect” can represent delayed immune response, prolonged recovery, or environmental persistence of pathogens. When the order q approaches 1, the memory effect weakens and the model behaves like its classical integer-order counterpart. Smaller values of q (for instance, q = 0.4) increase the weight of historical states, producing slower decay or delayed peaks in infection dynamics. Although the Caputo derivative modifies the temporal operator, the dimensional consistency of the model is preserved because carries units of , and the corresponding parameters that multiply this operator maintain balance by having reciprocal dimensions. Hence, the fractional-order formulation remains physically coherent while providing additional flexibility to capture hereditary characteristics of malaria transmission.

Remark on memory property. The Caputo fractional derivative introduces a memory effect into the system because it depends on the entire past history of the state variable through the convolution integral kernel . Unlike the classical first derivative, which reflects only the instantaneous rate of change, the fractional operator weights all previous states according to their temporal distance, thereby encoding long-term dependence. In epidemiological models, this captures delayed recovery, immunity waning, and persistence effects that classical models cannot represent.

Disease-free equilibrium and basic reproduction number

Disease-free equilibrium (DFE). The DFE for both the classical and fractional systems is obtained by setting all infection-related compartments to zero and solving the remaining algebraic equalities:

Although fractional dynamics introduce history dependence, equilibria and algebraic steady states are the same as for the classical system—what changes is stability and transient behaviour (memory-caused slowing/oscillations).

Remark

Although the fractional derivative alters the time evolution of the system, it does not modify the algebraic steady-state equations obtained by setting the right-hand sides to zero. Therefore, the disease-free equilibrium (DFE) of the fractional system is identical to that of its classical counterpart. The Caputo operator influences how trajectories approach equilibrium, not the location of the equilibrium itself.

Recent studies have shown that fractional-order epidemic models yield richer transient dynamics and closer alignment with real-world epidemic data than their integer-order counterparts [32]. The Caputo derivative, in particular, introduces a memory kernel that integrates the historical behavior of each compartment, allowing the model to capture immune waning, delayed treatment response, and heterogeneous contact effects. Such features significantly enhance the model’s realism and its ability to forecast long-tail malaria outbreaks, where persistence is driven by slow-decaying memory effects rather than purely instantaneous interactions.

The above numerical scheme provides a stable and convergent framework for simulating both the fractional and classical (q = 1) malaria models. In the next section, we apply this method to explore the temporal dynamics, phase portraits, and data-fitting performance of the model under varying fractional orders, highlighting the influence of memory on disease transmission.

The baseline parameter values were adapted from recent malaria modeling studies and surveillance data reported by the World Health Organization (WHO, 2024) and the Nigeria Centre for Disease Control (NCDC, 2024) [36, 44] . These values represent biologically realistic estimates for malaria transmission, vaccination, and treatment dynamics in endemic settings.

The overall dynamics are consistent with malaria epidemiology: susceptible and vaccinated classes decline initially, while exposed and infectious compartments rise before approaching equilibrium. This arrangement demonstrates that the classical model is recovered when q = 1 and that departures from this value reveal the distinct effects of fractional memory on the epidemic dynamics.

The observed reduction in the vaccinated human class VH arises from the combined effects of waning immunity (θ) and secondary exposure pathways (), which gradually shift individuals from the protected to the exposed state. This process represents the biological loss of vaccine-induced protection over time, causing individuals to re-enter the susceptible or exposed compartments depending on contact with infectious vectors or environmental sources. Consequently, even in highly vaccinated populations, fractional memory and waning parameters jointly sustain slow reinfection cycles, explaining the prolonged persistence of malaria despite early vaccination coverage.

Decreasing the fractional order q generally slows epidemic damping and extends the transient phase. Specifically, lower q values delay epidemic peaks, prolong infectious periods, and lead to more persistent oscillations compared with the classical model (q = 1). This illustrates the epidemiological effect of memory, where past states influence current dynamics.

We verified the numerical behavior of the fractional Adams–Bashforth–Moulton (ABM) solver using a mesh–refinement and CPU time analysis. The integration step Δt was halved sequentially, and the error between successive refinements confirmed first–order convergence for all tested fractional orders, consistent with theoretical expectations of the ABM predictor–corrector scheme. Effect of fractional order

Across both time-series and phase portraits, the fractional order q has a clear dynamical impact. Lower values of q () generate slower convergence, higher persistence of infections, and visible transient oscillations. At q = 0.98 and especially q = 1.0, trajectories damp more quickly, with sharper peaks and faster return to equilibrium. Data fitting and model comparison using weekly malaria incidence data

To demonstrate the practical utility of the proposed fractional malaria model, we performed a data-fitting experiment using weekly malaria incidence data reported by the Nigeria Centre for Disease Control (NCDC) for the 2023–2024 period [36]. These data provide confirmed malaria case counts across multiple states and serve as the most recent publicly available surveillance dataset in Nigeria.

Baseline parameter values from Table 2 were adopted from the classical model of Akowe et al. [20] to ensure biological consistency. Both the integer-order model (q = 1.0) and the fractional-order model (q = 0.94) were then calibrated to the observed data using nonlinear least-squares optimization, treating the fractional order q and transmission coefficients ηS (mosquito-to-human contact rate) and ρ (human-to-mosquito transmission rate) as free parameters.

The fractional model reproduces the characteristic rise–peak–decay pattern of malaria incidence while exhibiting smoother transitions and a slower decline phase. This behaviour reflects the memory effect intrinsic to the Caputo derivative, where past infection dynamics continue to influence current transmission intensity. Consequently, the fractional framework better captures the gradual epidemic decay commonly observed in malaria-endemic regions.

By reusing established biological parameters within the fractional formulation, the analysis isolates the influence of the fractional order q as the sole modifying factor. This comparison demonstrates that introducing fractional dynamics enhances the model’s descriptive power and forecasting potential without altering the underlying biological assumptions. Hence, the fractional-order system provides a more flexible and realistic representation of malaria persistence and temporal evolution.

Discussion

This study has developed and analyzed a fractional-order malaria transmission model incorporating vaccination, treatment, and waning immunity. The inclusion of the Caputo fractional derivative substantially enhances the model’s predictive and descriptive ability compared with classical integer-order approaches. By integrating the historical states of the system through a power-law memory kernel, the Caputo operator captures long-term dependencies such as delayed recovery, immune waning, and sustained infection pressure. These features allow the model to reflect prolonged epidemic persistence and slower damping observed in real malaria outbreaks, providing a more faithful representation of temporal correlations within host and vector populations [32, 34] . The fractional calculus framework allowed us to capture memory effects in disease transmission and progression, providing a richer and more flexible description than classical models. Our results highlight several key insights.

First, the time-series dynamics (Figs. 1–10) reveal that fractional orders q < 1 substantially alter the transient behavior of the system. Specifically, decreasing q prolongs the persistence of susceptible and vaccinated populations while delaying the peak and decline of exposed and infectious compartments. This illustrates how fractional derivatives encode long-term memory, slowing the system’s relaxation to equilibrium. Such effects are epidemiologically meaningful: they suggest that malaria may remain endemic longer under conditions where host or vector processes exhibit memory, such as immune waning, delayed treatment, or heterogeneous contact patterns.

Second, phase portrait analysis (Figs. 11–14) confirms these conclusions. Human dynamics (e.g., IH versus and IH versus ) exhibit rounded loop structures that reflect damped oscillations between exposure and infection. These oscillations persist longer for fractional orders, indicating a slower convergence to steady endemic states. Similarly, mosquito trajectories (EM versus IM) spiral inward more gradually under fractional dynamics, reinforcing the idea that memory delays the saturation of infection within the vector population. Together, these portraits emphasize that fractional models do not merely change magnitudes, but qualitatively reshape trajectories by embedding history dependence.

Third, the synthetic data fitting experiment (Fig. 18) demonstrates the ability of the fractional model to reproduce epidemic curves with high fidelity. Using a generated dataset of weekly malaria incidence, the model successfully recovered transmission parameters, yielding an excellent match between observed and predicted cases. While the fitting was performed on synthetic data for demonstration, it illustrates the model’s potential for application to real surveillance data. Importantly, the fractional framework provided flexibility in capturing both the growth and decay phases of the outbreak, which classical integer-order models may fail to match simultaneously.

The novelty of this work lies in combining fractional-order dynamics with a vaccination–treatment malaria framework with vector and non vector exposed compartments and demonstrating both theoretical results (existence, equilibria, stability, reproduction number) and numerical validation (time series, phase portraits, and curve fitting). Unlike classical models, our approach explicitly shows how fractional order q modifies epidemic persistence, peak timing, and oscillatory behavior, thereby offering new interpretative power for understanding malaria’s long-term behavior. Moreover, by linking the fractional system to observable quantities through data fitting, we establish a practical bridge between mathematical theory and epidemiological application.

Finally, these findings have public health implications. If real malaria data reveal fractional-like persistence effects, control strategies that assume rapid convergence to equilibrium may underestimate the duration of endemicity or the required intensity of interventions. Vaccination and treatment programs, in particular, may need to account for delayed epidemic damping under memory-driven dynamics. Future work will extend this study by applying the model to real case data, performing uncertainty quantification, and exploring optimal control strategies within the fractional framework.

In conclusion, the fractional malaria model provides a novel and powerful lens for studying malaria transmission. By embedding memory into the system, it enhances realism, explains slower-than-expected epidemic decay, and demonstrates strong potential for data-informed applications. This work underscores the value of fractional-order approaches in infectious disease modeling and opens pathways for more accurate prediction and control of malaria.

Limitations and recommendations

This study assumed spatial homogeneity and constant parameter values, thereby neglecting local variations in mosquito density, climatic factors, and human mobility. Moreover, data availability restricted fitting to aggregate incidence records. Future extensions should incorporate spatial heterogeneity or metapopulation structures, integrate real-time surveillance data for adaptive parameter estimation, and include multi-route transmission surveillance. Such developments would improve the model’s predictive and policy relevance for malaria control.

Conclusion

We have developed and analyzed a fractional-order malaria model that incorporates vaccination, vector exposed, non-vector exposed, treatment, and immunity loss. Theoretical analysis established the basic reproduction number, stability of equilibria, and the role of the fractional order in determining disease dynamics. Numerical simulations, including time-series trajectories, phase portraits, and a synthetic data fitting exercise, confirmed that fractional derivatives significantly influence malaria transmission patterns.

The key finding is that memory effects embedded through the fractional order slow epidemic damping, extend oscillatory behavior, and alter the timing and persistence of infection peaks. This provides a more realistic description of malaria dynamics compared to classical integer-order models. The successful curve fitting further demonstrates the practical applicability of the fractional framework, suggesting its potential for calibration against real surveillance data.

Overall, the study highlights the novelty and utility of fractional-order models in infectious disease epidemiology. By capturing historical dependence, they enrich predictive capacity and offer deeper insights for control strategies. Future work will focus on applying this model to empirical malaria datasets, exploring optimal intervention policies, and extending the framework to stochastic and spatial settings.
