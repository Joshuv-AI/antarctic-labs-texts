Predicting surface temperature in Lake Villarrica (Chilean Patagonia) using a long short-term memory model
Authors: Lien Rodríguez-López, David Bustos Usta, Lisandra Bravo Alvarez, Iongel Duran-Llacer, Luc Bourrel, Frederic Frappart, Roberto Urrutia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In this study, we analyze water-temperature time series was measured over 34 years, between 1986 and 2020, at the water surface at seven stations across Lake Villarrica (Southern Chile). The spring and summer seasons show an increment in the superficial temperature during the study period. The annual maximum temperature, ranging between 17.35 and 21.65 °C were observed in 1997 and 2009, respectively, while the annual minimum, ranging between 16.8 and 21.5 °C were observed in 2001 and 2009, respectively. In addition, we employ a machine learning based estimation model to predict surface temperatures in a South American lake spanning the period 1989 to 2021. Our model uses data in situ of physical, chemical, and biological parameters of lake quality water, along with meteorological data and spectral bands, including combinations of images from the Landsat 8 satellite, as input variables. The 7 lake monitoring stations were classified into 4 regions according to their geographical location: north, south, east, and west. Our findings demonstrate the exceptional performance of the long short-term memory (LSTM) model in accurately estimating temperatures across Lake Villarrica. The best results were obtained for the west region of the lake with good statistical metrics from the estimation model of RMSE = 2.79, Bias =−0.06, max error = 5.93, MSE = 7.83 and median absolute error (MedAE) = 2.13. This approach represents a significant advance in the integration of remote sensing and machine learning techniques to monitor and manage inland water systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23937-5.

Introduction

Climate change is causing a global increase in temperature, with significant consequences for both natural ecosystems and human societies1,2. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) has reported that the Earth’s average surface temperature has already increased by approximately 1 °C (1.8 degrees Fahrenheit) compared to pre-industrial levels and is projected to increase further in the coming decades3,4. This temperature increase is mainly due to the accumulation of greenhouse gases, such as carbon dioxide, in the Earth’s atmosphere due to human activities such as fossil fuel burning and deforestation5,6. The effects of this temperature increase include more frequent and severe heat waves, changes in precipitation patterns, sea level rise, and more intense storms7,8. These changes are already affecting communities around the world and are likely to have even greater impacts in the future.

Temperature plays a crucial role in the processes that take place in a lake, affecting everything from water circulation to the growth of aquatic organisms9,10. Temperature influences the physical properties of water, such as its density, viscosity, and thermal conductivity, which in turn affect the movement of water within the lake11,12. In addition, temperature influences the rates of biological processes, such as photosynthesis, respiration, and nutrient uptake, which are vital for the growth and survival of aquatic organisms13. Thus, changes in temperature can have significant impacts on the ecology of a lake, with consequences for the entire food web.

In addition, the temperature is a critical factor influencing primary productivity, which is the rate at which plants and other photosynthetic organisms produce organic matter through photosynthesis14,15. This process is the basis of all life on Earth, as it provides the energy and nutrients needed for the growth and survival of higher trophic levels16. The rate of primary productivity is strongly influenced by temperature, as warmer temperatures generally lead to higher rates of photosynthesis. Understanding the role of temperature in these processes is critical to managing and protecting freshwater resources.

Remote sensing methods have revolutionized the way lake surface water temperature (LSWT) is measured, allowing large-scale, non-invasive monitoring of aquatic systems17,18. A widely used remote sensing technique for measuring water surface temperature is thermal infrared remote sensing in passive sensors19,20. It consists of using a sensor to measure the thermal radiation emitted by the water surface, which is proportional to its temperature. This method can be carried out from the air, with airplanes or drones equipped with thermal cameras, or from space, with satellites equipped with thermal sensors. In addition, some satellites are equipped with microwave radiometers, which can measure water surface temperature from their microwave emissions21. Remote sensing methods offer several advantages over traditional methods, such as the ability to monitor large areas of water over time, without the need for physical contact with the water22,23.

There are several satellite-based platforms that measure the surface temperature of aquatic systems using algorithms. Among the most widely used are the Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS)23,24, the Landsat Thermal Infrared Sensor (TIRS)25,26, and the Visible Infrared Imaging Radiometer Suite (VIIRS)27,28. The algorithms used by each platform are slightly different, resulting in variations in accuracy and precision. In general, the choice of satellite platform depends on the specific needs of the user, such as the desired spatial and temporal resolution, as well as the environmental conditions of the aquatic system under study29. Regardless of the platform used, remote sensing provides a powerful tool for monitoring and managing aquatic systems, allowing researchers and managers to make more informed decisions about the health and sustainability of these important resources. The objective of this work is to analyse the spatial and temporal trends and behavior of the surface temperature of Lake Villarrica, in southern Chile, between 1986 and 2020, using Landsat satellite imagery and the LSTM estimation model. This research will contribute to providing useful data on the effects of climate change in Chilean lakes.

Materials and methods

Site description

Lake Villarrica is a large lake located in southern Chile, specifically in the Araucanía Region, between the Andes Mountains and the Coastal Range (Fig. 1). It has an approximate surface area of 174 km² and is located 227 m above sea level30. Its shape is elongated, with an approximate length of 22 km and a maximum width of about 8 km31. Its coastline is irregular and is marked by numerous bays, inlets, and islets. It is fed by several rivers, such as the Toltén, the Liucura and the Trancura, and its waters flow into the Pacific Ocean. The main morphological parameters of Lake Villarica are summarized in (Table 1). The landscape surrounding the lake is characterized by the presence of volcanoes, the Villarrica volcano being the most prominent. The volcano is an active stratovolcano, with a height of 2847 m, and is located on the eastern shore of the lake32. The Villarrica volcano is known for its frequent eruptions, which have resulted in the formation of a crater lake at the top of the volcano. The area surrounding Lake Villarrica is covered by dense forests, composed mainly of native trees such as araucaria, coigue and lenga. The lake is also surrounded by a varied flora and fauna, including several species of fish, birds, and mammals, such as the pudú, a small deer endemic to southern Chile. In general, Lake Villarrica and the surrounding landscape are characterized by their natural beauty and unique geological features, making it a popular destination for tourists and lovers of outdoor activities33,34.

In situ data

In situ measurements in the period 1986–2020 were carried out in several monitoring campaigns of the Chilean General Directorate of Water through the free web-based hydrometeorological service available online (http://www.dga.cl/servicioshidrometeorologicos/access) (accessed 20 January 2024). The first was reported in “Redefinición de la red mínima de lagos” by POCH AMBIENTAL S.A. in the study “Informe final nº 1. Analysis of phytoplankton in water samples. Tender Nº 1019-98-LE13”. The second was reported in the study “Analysis and reformulation of the monitoring network of the river lakes region” (DGA, 2020), and the third was carried out by the Eula Center in 2021. This downloadable database is available for 16 lakes that are part of the Minimum Lake Network (RML), in which Lake Villarrica is included. From it, Secchi disk depth (SD), chlorophyll a (Chl-a), surface temperature (°C), dissolved oxygen (OD), phosphorus and nitrogen total (PT and NT respectively), conductivity (Conduct) and turbidity (NTU) were obtained at 7 sampling stations (V1-V7). The monitored parameters were surface temperature (thermometry 2250 B standard methods 22 Ed, method used as a reference for temperature analysis according to the NCh 2313 compendium), surface Chl-a (fluorometric method), determination of total phosphorus ( 4500 P B Standard Methods 22 Edit. EAM), determination total nitrogen (4500-N C Standard Methods 22 Edit-EAM) and transparency (measured by SDD). At each of the lake stations, water samples were collected at 5 depths using a 5 L Niskin sampling bottle. Niskin 5 L sampling bottle. Samples were stored and transported in thermally insulated boxes, properly cooled with ice at a temperature of approximately 5 °C for subsequent analysis. Chemical analyses were performed at the chemistry laboratory of the Dirección General de Aguas de Chile (DGA). This laboratory is accredited by the Instituto Nacional de Normalización (National Standards Institute) for the Chilean Standard NCh ISO 17.025 of 2005.

Landsat satellite images

Preprocessing satellite images

This study used 25 Landsat 8 Collection 2 Level 1 images (L8, C2-L1) (see Table S1). The bands of the visible and infrared spectrum (Blue-B, Green-G, Red-R, Near Infrared-INR, Short Wavelength Infrared-SWIR1) were used from the Operational Land Imager (OLI) sensor at a spatial resolution of 30 m, and the thermal bands (TIRS 1, TIRS2) were used from the Thermal InfraRed Sensor (TIRS) at 100 m spatial resolutions. The images were downloaded from the Earth-Explorer site (https://earthexplorer.usgs.gov/), from United States Geological Survey (USGS). Landsat 8 was developed in collaboration with National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) and the United States Geological Survey (USGS). The study area of interest (Roi) was covered by trajectories 232/233 and row 87 of the imagery. The selected images met certain criteria, including low cloud cover (less than 12%) and proximity to the sampling date (within ± 9 days of satellite overpasses). Quality assessment (QA) bands were used to mask clouds, shadows, and cirrus clouds. Only cloud-free areas were included in the analysis.

To atmospherically correct images, the ACOLITE program (version 20211124.0) was used. ACOLITE incorporates atmospheric correction protocols and processing software developed at RBINS for water remote sensing applications. Dark Spectrum Fitting (DSF) and Exponential Extrapolation (EXP) algorithms were used for atmospheric correction for the bans of visible and infrared spectrum. The resulting values represent the surface level reflectance (ρs) for L8. X-Y band values were then extracted into a 3 × 3-pixel matrix. The lake contour was established using geospatial information obtained from the Dirección General de Aguas de Chile (DGA). Only the water body was considered for the analysis. In the case of thermal bands, they were also corrected in ACOLITE but using the Thermal Atmospheric Correction Tool (TACT). TACT is the thermal atmospheric correction tool for Landsat developed at RBINS. Retrieves ERA5 atmospheric profiles and uses libRadtran to calculate atmospheric transmittance and ascending and descending radiances. Currently, TACT generates the water surface temperature (WST) in each thermal band present in the Landsat sensor, assuming a constant (water) emissivity over the scene. This methodology can be consulted in detail in35.

LSTM models

Long short-term memory (LSTM) models are a type of recurrent neural network (RNN) that has been designed to handle the challenges of long-term dependencies in sequential data. Traditional RNNs can have difficulty retaining information over long periods of time, leading to the problem of vanishing gradients37. This is where gradients in the backpropagation algorithm become very small as they are propagated through many time steps, making it difficult to update the weights of the network. LSTM models address this issue by introducing memory cells and gating mechanisms that allow the model to selectively remember or forget information over time38.

Unlike standard recurrent neural networks, which can have trouble retaining information over the long term, LSTM networks are designed to be able to do so. They use a gating mechanism that allows them to control how much information they retain and how much they discard at each time step. In an LSTM network, each neural network unit has three main gates: the input gate, the forget gate and the output gate. These gates allow the network to decide what information to retain and what information to discard at each time step. The input gate decides what information is added to the network’s short-term memory. The forgetting gate decides what information is retained in long-term memory and what information is discarded. Finally, the output gate decides what information will be used to make predictions or decisions at that point in time39.

We applied a tanh activation function to scale values within a range of −1 to 1 and used the Adam optimizer during training. The input variables were constructed using the most recent lag (t-1) along with seasonal lags of 12 and 24 months (t-12, t-24) to explicitly incorporate both short-term dynamics and long-term seasonal patterns. The number of units in the LSTM layer was iteratively tuned based on RMSE and MSE performance metrics, resulting in the tailored unit allocations for each region: SOUTH (35), NORTH (40), WEST (30), and EAST (25). A dense layer was appended to the topology to generate final predictions. This configuration aligns with established architectures for time series forecasting using LSTMs40. Statistical analysis

A linear fit between Landsat and in situ data was performed following the methodology of23. The adjustment was performed by the least squares to find the relationship between the water surface temperature acquired by processing Landsat images and in situ measured data.

The data were analyzed at three temporal resolutions: monthly, seasonal, and annual measured temperatures. To quantify estimation errors, basic statistical analyses such as the coefficient of determination (R2), root mean square error (RMSE) and mean absolute error (MAE) were used.

K-fold cross-validation

After creating the models described in the section before, we had to determine the performance of each model. To this end, we applied the k-fold cross-validation (CV) method described by41, which is considered a statistical method to evaluate and compare algorithm performance by dividing data into two segments: one used for learning and the other to validate results without overlapping data instances between groups. We used the most applied alternative, known as k-fold, cross-validation with k = 5 folds42.

Mann Kendall and Sen´s slope tests

To analyze temperature and precipitation trends, the non-parametric Mann Kendall and Sen’s slope tests were applied, which do not require a normal distribution and can be applied to time series43–45. The level of significance used in all tests was a p value < 0.0546. The tests were applied to annual and seasonal precipitation, while temperature was only analyzed in summer and spring.

Discussion

LSWT is an essential climate variable that highlights regional weather and climate variability and trends. Temperature is a critical factor that significantly impacts various physical, chemical, and biological processes within aquatic ecosystems. Its influence extends to the solubility of gases, such as oxygen and carbon dioxide, which are indispensable for the survival of aquatic organisms. As temperature rises, the solubility of these gases decreases, resulting in warmer water containing less oxygen dissolved, thereby posing challenges for aquatic organisms’ respiratory processes. Moreover, temperature dictates the pace of essential chemical reactions, including nutrient cycling, photosynthesis, and respiration. These reactions, in turn, dictate nutrient availability, productivity levels, and decomposition rates within aquatic ecosystems, thus shaping food webs and biogeochemical cycles. In addition, temperature exerts a fundamental influence on the distribution and abundance of aquatic organisms. Various species of fish, invertebrates and algae have different temperature preferences for optimal growth and reproduction. Changes in temperature can alter the timing of life cycles, migratory patterns, and overall population dynamics of aquatic organisms. In addition, temperature influences the physical properties of water, such as density and viscosity, which in turn affect water movement and circulation patterns. These alterations in circulation subsequently influence the mixing of water masses, nutrient distribution, and overall ecosystem dynamics.

In the future, the results of this study will be used to evaluate the effects of temperature on primary producers in the lake, specifically when algal blooms appear in the lake during the summer. In addition to its importance in ecological processes, temperature is also a key factor in water management and conservation. Changes in temperature can indicate changes in the hydrological cycle, such as alterations in precipitation patterns or alterations in river and stream flow. Temperature monitoring can help identify water quality problems, such as thermal pollution caused by human activities such as power plants and industrial processes. Therefore, understanding the role of temperature in aquatic systems is crucial for maintaining ecosystem health, conserving biodiversity, and implementing sustainable water management practices.

Conclusions

This study analyzed surface water temperature time series collected over 34 years (1986–2020) at seven monitoring stations across Lake Villarrica. Results indicate a warming trend during spring and summer, with notable increases in surface temperature over the study period. The annual maximum temperatures ranged from 17.35 °C (1997) to 21.65 °C (2009), while the annual minimum temperatures ranged from 16.8 °C (2001) to 21.5 °C (2009). Additionally, we used 25 Landsat 8 satellite images, in situ water quality data, and meteorological records to estimate lake surface temperature using a combined data-driven algorithm.

However, the study has some limitations. The temporal resolution of satellite data was constrained by cloud cover and the revisit time of Landsat 8, which limited the number of usable scenes. In situ measurements were not always taken simultaneously with satellite overpasses, which may have affected model accuracy. Furthermore, the model’s performance varied spatially across the lake, suggesting that local environmental heterogeneity influences temperature estimations. Future work should focus on improving temporal alignment between satellite and in situ data, incorporating higher-resolution sensors or alternative satellite missions, and refining the model to better account for spatial variability in lake conditions.
