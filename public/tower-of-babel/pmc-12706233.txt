Determining the longitudinal associations between suicidal ideation and biopsychosocial factors in early to mid-adolescence: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Maddison Crethar, Amanda Boyes, Paul Schwenn, Lia Mills, Taliah Prince, Kassie Bromley, Michelle Kennedy, Christina Driver, Daniel F Hermens
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo determine whether a biopsychosocial model of suicidality, specifically sleep, nutrition, physical exercise, mindfulness, social connectedness, lower socioeconomic status (SES) and sex are uniquely associated with increased suicidal ideation, longitudinally over adolescence.DesignLongitudinal, prospective cohort study.SettingA structured self-report questionnaire was collected as part of the Longitudinal Adolescent Brain Study at the University of the Sunshine Coast’s Thompson Institute (Queensland, Australia) from July 2018 to January 2024.Participants159 Australian adolescents (n=91 female; 68 male) aged 12 to 17 years.Outcome measuresSelf-reported suicidal ideation was measured longitudinally. Data were also collected on self-reported lifestyle factors (sleep, nutrition, physical exercise, mindfulness and social connectedness), psychological distress, SES and sex. All measures were collected at 4-monthly intervals for each participant for up to 5 years (maximum of 15 time points).ResultsSignificant relationships were identified between increased suicidal ideation and poor sleep (OR 2.6, 95% CI 1.4 to 4.6, p=0.002), socioeconomic disadvantage (SES quintile 1: OR 6.3, 95% CI, 1.8 to 21.8, p=0.004; SES quintile 2: OR 8.7, 95% CI 1.4 to 56.2, p=0.022), psychological distress (OR 5.7, 95% CI 2.1 to 15.6, p≤0.001) and eating habits (β −0.08, 95% CI −0.2 to −0.0).ConclusionsPoor sleep, socioeconomic disadvantage, psychological distress and eating habits were all found to be significantly associated with increased adolescent suicidal ideation over time. These biopsychosocial factors should be considered in targeted interventions and policies for reducing adolescent suicidality. Further research should employ multilevel modelling to examine factor interactions and rigorously evaluate interventions targeting lifestyle factors and socioeconomic inequalities through randomised controlled trials and quasi-experimental designs.Keywords: Adolescent, MENTAL HEALTH, SLEEP MEDICINE, Suicide & self-harm, Psychological Stress

Introduction

Adolescence involves important concurrent neurobiological and emotional regulation skill development, making adolescents vulnerable to suicidal tendencies and other maladaptive behaviours.1 Suicidal ideation (SI), a precursor to suicidal behaviour (SB), has global prevalence rates ranging between 15 and 25% in adolescents.2 Adolescents, in particular male adolescents, are less likely to seek professional help to manage mental health concerns, compared with adults.3 Female adolescents experience a higher prevalence of SI, non-suicidal self injury and non-fatal SBs,4 5 while male adolescents have higher suicide rates.6

Previous research suggests lifestyle factors such as social connectedness, a healthy diet, physical activity, mindfulness and quality sleep may moderate suicidality; however, evidence is conflicting or lacking for adolescents.7,12 Maintaining a high-quality diet and good perceived social connectedness are associated with better physical and mental health in adolescents and young adults.8 11 13 Similarly, physical activity has been shown to be important in improving mood and reducing depressive symptoms,14 along with mindfulness, the practice of focusing awareness on the present moment, in adolescents.10 Furthermore, studies focused on self-reported perceptions of sleep quality have found better sleep quality to be associated with resilience, a protective factor against SI, while sleep abnormalities present as a risk factor in adults.15 Significant negative correlations have been found between sleep quality and quantity and the strength of suicidal feelings in adults,9 with further research needed to understand these factors in adolescents. While protective lifestyle factors demonstrate links to SI/SB, other factors such as socioeconomic status (SES) and psychological distress appear to mediate their effect.16

SES is defined by access to social resources, material assets and opportunities for societal participation, measuring factors like poverty and deprivation.17 Lower SES youth may experience stress from financial constraints and inadequate healthcare access16 18 19 contributing to poorer mental health outcomes and increasing vulnerability to psychological distress and suicidality.20 In Western countries, stressful life events, such as family breakdown and academic pressures, appear to increase psychological distress, which is linked to youth suicide.21

The biopsychosocial model of suicide provides a multifaceted approach to targeting biological, social and psychological factors in risk assessment.22 This model explains how an individual’s psychological risk and protective factors interact with biological and social factors to contribute to suicide risk.22 Figure 1 portrays a visual demonstration of the biopsychosocial factors contributing to suicidality.

Biopsychosocial model of suicide. SIDAS, Suicidal Ideation Attributes Scale.

Modifying lifestyle factors may mitigate suicidality; however, research has commonly targeted adult populations and there is conflicting evidence regarding the significance of lifestyle factors within an adolescent population.8 11 13 23 Cross-sectional analyses examining SI (during the past month) of participants from the Longitudinal Adolescent Brain Study (LABS; n=50; aged 12) found reduced social connectedness and increased sleep dysfunction were associated with increased Suicidal Ideation Attributes Scale (SIDAS) scores.23 Smaller associations were found between increased SI and reduced mindfulness, while physical activity and eating habits were not significant.23 Based on the research outlined above, this study included lifestyle factors.

Objective

This study aimed to explore the longitudinal relationships between lifestyle factors, SES and SI during adolescence, with the goal of identifying modifiable lifestyle factors that may prevent or reduce SI. The study examined how self-reported sleep, physical exercise, nutrition, mindfulness, social connectedness and a SES proxy (postcode) are associated with self-reported suicidal thoughts and behaviours in adolescence (between the ages of 12–17). In addition, the study investigated how sex, age and self-reported psychological distress may influence these associations. It was hypothesised that (1) poorer self-reported lifestyle factors (sleep, physical exercise, social connectedness, mindfulness and diet); (2) lower SES/higher disadvantage (estimated using National ABS postcode quintiles); and (3) female sex would be associated with increased SI in a sample of Australian 12-year-olds to 17-year-olds.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study used data collected between 18 July 2018 and 31 January 2024 from LABS at the University of the Sunshine Coast’s (UniSC) Thompson Institute. LABS is a prospective, cohort study that received ethics approval (A181064) from the UniSC Human Research Ethics Committee. Selection criteria included participants aged 12 to 15 years who were proficient in English and were not suffering from a major neurological disorder, intellectual disability or a sustained head injury. Participants completed assessments every 4 months until 17 years of age (maximum of 15 time points over 5 years). Participants were screened for eligibility prior to entry into the study. A trained researcher provided an outline of the study and answered any questions participants had prior to obtaining written consent to participate. Written consent to participate was obtained from participants and their caregiver/s during the initial visit and annually for the duration of the study. If the young person did not provide consent to participate, their decision took priority over parental consent.

Sample

As of 31 January 2024, there were a total of n=166 participants in LABS. This current study required participants to have reported SI data and have Queensland postcodes at the time of the analysis; therefore, the pool of participants was reduced to 159 (91 female, 68 male), with 899 data entries across time points 1–15.

Procedures

Participants completed demographics and self-report questionnaires at each time point (1–15) with a trained researcher. Design and recruitment details are described elsewhere.23 Questionnaires included SIDAS,24 Kessler-10 Psychological Distress Scale (K10),25 Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI),26 Social Connectedness Scale (SCS),27 Mindful Attention Awareness Scale for Adolescents (MAAS-A),28 Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ),29 Moderate-to-Vigorous Physical Activity (MVPA)29 and Vigorous Physical Activity (VPA).29 Further detail on each scale, including reliability and validity, is described in table 1.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were carried out using SPSS Statistics for Windows, V.29 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, USA).

Data cleaning

Raw data were checked, item/total scores contained no erroneous entries and all data were within acceptable ranges.

SES data processing

Participant postcodes were recoded into SES quintiles using the Socio-Economic Indexes for Areas (SEIFA) Australia 2021, specifically the Index of Relative Socioeconomic Advantage and Disadvantage.30 Grouping the ten deciles into five quintiles reduces variability and enables national comparison across Australian states.

Assumption checks

All primary outcome variables were checked for outliers (validity check, clinical plausibility and visual identification), with outliers identified on PSQI, K10, SIDAS, SES, FFQ, MAAS-A and SCS. Outliers were valid and within total score ranges. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov normality test showed that all measures were significantly non-normal. Visual inspection of frequency distributions, boxplots and Q-Q plots was used to determine the scales that displayed the strongest violation of normality (PSQI, K10, SIDAS, MAAS-A and SCS), and data for these scales were binarised (see table 3).31 SIDAS was recoded into ‘no’ versus ‘any SI categories’. FFQ and PA were less skewed and remained as continuous variables.

Generalised estimating equations (GEE) analyses

To examine the research questions, longitudinal associations between each of the lifestyle factors, psychological distress, sex, age and SES, with SIDAS were estimated using GEE. Age and sex were added to the model to control for them as potential confounds. A full GEE model was conducted with the following settings: the participant ID as the subject variable; TP as the within-subject variable; exchangeable and AR(1) working correlation matrices (to compare best model fit); a binomial distribution with a logit link (the logit link function allows the exponentiated coefficients to be interpreted as OR)32; SI as the dependent variable; sex, SES, sleep, mindfulness, social connectedness, psychological distress, eating habits, physical activity and age as independent variables; and χ2 statistics for model effect testing. Missing data were identified across the entire dataset (n=899) for self-reported sleep (n=898; 0.1% missing), mindfulness (n=897; 0.2% missing), social connectedness (n=898; 0.1% missing) and SES (n=895; 0.4% missing).

Patient and public involvement

None.

SI was binarised with the reference category set to 0 (no SI); thus, significant results are associated with increased SI. Significant associations were detected with SES, psychological distress and sleep. The odds of SI were 6.3 times higher in SES quintile 1 (most disadvantaged; OR=6.3 [95% CI 1.8 to 21.8]) compared with the reference group (SES quintile 5 (most advantaged)). Compared with this same reference group, the odds of SI were 8.7 times higher for SES quintile 2 (OR=8.7 [95% CI 1.4 to 56.2]). The odds of SI were 5.7 times higher in the medium/high psychological distress group (OR=5.7, [95% CI 2.1 to 15.6]) compared with the no/low psychological distress group. The odds of SI were 2.6 times higher in the poor sleep group (OR=2.6, [95% CI 1.4 to 4.6]) compared with the good sleep group. Lastly, the healthier eating habits were associated with lower SI (β=−0.08, [95% CI −.2 to −0.0]).

Discussion

We found support for the biopsychosocial model of SI in adolescents from a community sample, with sleep and eating habits as significant biological factors, psychological distress as a significant psychological factor and SES as a significant social factor. Our findings align with previous research demonstrating a significant association between poor sleep quality and increased SI.9 15 This finding partially supports hypothesis one. Sleep quality has emerged as a crucial factor in mental health, particularly in relation to resilience and susceptibility to SI.9 15 Previous studies involving adults have shown that healthy sleep patterns act as a protective factor against suicidal thoughts, while sleep disturbances pose a risk factor.9 15 Our study extends the evidence for the association between poor sleep and SI to adolescents. Due to the impact of sleep deprivation on cognitive function, promoting healthy sleep habits can serve as a modifiable intervention to reduce SI among adolescents. Also in partial support for hypothesis one, this study found that eating habits were significantly associated with self-reported SI. While this association between eating habits and SI has been reported in a cross-sectional adult cohort,8 the evidence is lacking or conflicting23 regarding the impact of diet on at-risk adolescents. This study provides support for the protective impact of maintaining a high-quality diet in reducing experiences of SI longitudinally from early to mid-adolescence.

Our findings of a significant association of psychological distress and SI in adolescence were consistent with those reported elsewhere.21 Social and economic factors play a crucial role in shaping mental health outcomes, with disadvantaged individuals facing an increased risk of mental disorder and associated adverse consequences. Income disparity has been identified as a direct contributor to mental health issues and psychological distress, with poorer communities often bearing a disproportionate burden compared with wealthier communities.19

Our findings also align with existing research indicating a significant association between lower SES and greater odds of experiencing SI, supporting hypothesis two. Individuals from low-income backgrounds often face formidable challenges in accessing necessities for a decent standard of living.33 Our study corroborates previous research highlighting the enduring association between lower SES and heightened risk of SI, even after controlling for variables such as age, sex and psychiatric morbidity.33 Socioeconomic factors contribute to this elevated risk through a combination of contextual and compositional influences.16 19

Previous research suggests sex differences among experiences of suicidal thoughts and behaviours, with female adolescents having a higher prevalence of SI and non-fatal SBs,4 5 while male adolescents have a higher prevalence of suicide rates.6 This study examined the presence and severity of suicidal thoughts and identified no sex differences in SI longitudinally, rejecting hypothesis three. Previous research also suggests that physical exercise, mindfulness, social connectedness and age may be related to SI across adult populations, with mixed findings for adolescents.3 7 8 11 12 However, this was not observed in the current study. This could be due to differences in statistical approaches; for example, our study used GEE modelling and a longitudinal dataset with fixed, short intervals (4 months), while others did not. Other contributing factors could be attributed to temporal dynamics, given that SI and its determinants can be influenced by a change in life circumstances, mental health status or social supports over time.

Limitations

When considering data for the relationship between SI and lifestyle factors, limitations of the study should be considered. First, participants’ likelihood of disclosing SI may be influenced by the risk of others finding out. Second, while postcodes are a suitable SES measure defined by the ABS, parental questionnaires may also be used to derive additional SES information, such as parental income, education and living standards. Further, this study had a small number of participants within SES quintiles 1 and 2 (influencing the large 95% CIs for the ORs noted in table 3), limiting the generalisability of the study. Thus, future research should target recruitment of participants in lower SES groups. The binarisation of factors may have influenced the findings, as previous studies with significant results included mostly continuous factors. Where there were no clear cut-off scores from either the scale or literature, the median score was used to binarise scales. This may have contributed to non-significant findings for social connectedness and mindfulness. This could have also influenced the large CIs for psychological distress, in addition to knowing the fluctuating levels of psychological distress experienced during the transitional period of adolescence.1 34 While GEE is an established statistical method, it is relatively new in the field of developmental psychology. Knowledge to date suggests the current sample size and number of observations provide appropriate power for the GEE analysis,35 36 and adequate precision to estimate population-average effects of moderate magnitude, as reflected in the sleep (OR ≈ 2.6) and psychological distress (OR ≈ 5.7) estimates. The parameters included in the models were kept to a minimum to reduce over-fitting and increase power;35 therefore, school type and grade were included only as descriptives and not covariates in the models.

Future directions

The current research examined the longitudinal relationships between lifestyle factors, SES and SI in adolescence; however, additional avenues for future investigation remain to be explored. First, different longitudinal analyses, such as multilevel modelling techniques, could be employed to examine how these factors interact and contribute to suicide risk in adolescents. Second, interventions targeting lifestyle factors and socioeconomic inequalities should be rigorously evaluated to determine their effectiveness in preventing SI in adolescent populations.8 For example, randomised controlled trials and quasi-experimental designs could be employed to assess the impact of interventions such as nutrition programmes, physical activity initiatives, mindfulness interventions and socioeconomic support programmes on reducing SI. Thirdly, future studies should consider school type and grade level as variables within multivariate analyses to better account for potential confounding effects. By advancing our understanding of the complex mechanisms underlying SI and identifying effective prevention strategies, we can work towards reducing the burden of suicide and promoting the mental health and well-being of adolescents.20

Conclusion

Adolescence is marked by profound biopsychosocial changes and can be a vulnerable period for the emergence of maladaptive behaviours, including SI. However, it is also a period of opportunity for intervention. The stark reality that suicide is the leading cause of death among Australian adolescents underscores the urgent need for comprehensive public health interventions. The findings of the current study suggest that in adolescents, sleep, psychological distress, SES and eating habits may be particularly important risk factors that should be targets for effective, tailored management strategies for adolescents experiencing suicidal thoughts and behaviours. This study has emphasised the multifaceted nature of SI in adolescence and the imperative need for holistic approaches that address both individual risk factors and broader social determinants. By advancing our understanding of these complex dynamics, more targeted and effective strategies to prevent suicide and promote mental well-being among adolescents can be developed.
