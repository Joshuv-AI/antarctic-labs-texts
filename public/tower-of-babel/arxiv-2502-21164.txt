Polynomial-Size Enumeration Kernelizations for Long Path Enumeration
Christian Komusiewicz, Diptapriyo Majumdar, Frank Sommer

Abstract
Enumeration kernelization for parameterized enumeration problems was defined by Creignou et al. [Theory Comput. Syst. 2017] and was later refined by Golovach et al. [J. Comput. Syst. Sci. 2022] to polynomial-delay enumeration kernelization. We consider Enum Long-Path , the enumeration variant of the Long-Path problem, from the perspective of enumeration kernelization. Formally, given an undirected graph G G and an integer k k , the objective of Enum Long-Path is to enumerate all paths of G G having exactly k k vertices. We consider the structural parameters vertex cover number, dissociation number, and distance to clique and provide polynomial-delay enumeration kernels of polynomial size for each of these parameters.



1
Introduction

Kernelization is one of the most important contributions of parameterized complexity to the toolbox for handling hard computational problems
[
9
,
12
,
19
]
. The idea in kernelization is to develop polynomial-time algorithms that shrink any instance
x
of a parameterized problem
L
with parameterization
\kappa
to an equivalent instance
x^{\prime}
, the size of which is upper-bounded by
g(\kappa(x))
for some function
g
.
The crucial innovation of kernelization is that the size bound
g
allows for a theory of the effectiveness of polynomial-time preprocessing, a goal that seems virtually unreachable in a nonparameterized setting. Accordingly, the aim is to obtain a kernelization with
g
growing as slowly as possible.

Originally limited to decision problems, several extensions of kernelization adapt the notion to optimization
[
30
]
, counting
[
26
,
29
]
, and enumeration problems
[
8
,
10
,
20
,
21
]
.
For enumeration problems, the focus of this work, a first attempt at a suitable kernelization definition, was to ask that
x^{\prime}
should not only be equivalent to
x
but furthermore that
x^{\prime}
contains
all
solutions of
x

[
10
]
. This definition has several drawbacks: First, it is restricted to subset-minimization problems. Second, the parameter is necessarily at least as large as the solution size. Finally, one may hope for kernels only in the case that the total number of solutions is bounded by a function of
\kappa(x)
. These drawbacks prompted Creignou et al.
[
8
]
to give a different definition of enumeration kernelization that asks for two algorithms: The first algorithm is the kernelization; it shrinks the input instance in polynomial time to the
kernel
, an instance whose size is bounded in the parameter. The second algorithm is the
solution-lifting
algorithm which allows to enumerate all solutions of the input instance from the solutions of the kernel. The crucial restriction for the solution-lifting algorithm is that it should be an FPT-delay algorithm, that is, the time spent between outputting consecutive solutions should be bounded by
f(\kappa(x))\cdot|x|^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
for some function
f
.
This definition gives the desired property that an enumeration problem has an FPT-delay algorithm if and only if it has an enumeration kernel
[
8
]
.
As discussed by Golovach et al.
[
20
]
, however, any enumeration problem with an FPT-delay algorithm automatically admits an enumeration kernel of
constant size
.
The crux of kernelization—providing a measure
g
of the effectiveness of data reduction—is undermined by this fact.
This led Golovach et al.
[
20
]
to define enumeration kernels whose solution-lifting algorithms are only allowed to have
polynomial
delay.
This particular running time bound has several desirable consequences
[
20
]
:
First, a problem has an enumeration kernel of constant size if and only if it admits a polynomial-delay enumeration algorithm.
This now directly corresponds to kernelization of decision problems, where a problem admits a kernelization of constant size if and only if it is polynomial-time solvable. Second, a parameterized enumeration problem admits an FPT-delay algorithm if and only if it admits a polynomial-delay enumeration kernel. Hence, the stricter delay requirement for the solution-lifting algorithm still suffices to capture all problems with FPT-delay. The size of this automatically implied kernel is, however, exponential in the parameter. This now makes it interesting to ask for polynomial-delay enumeration kernelization of
polynomial size
(henceforth
pd-ps kernel
). A further informal argument in favor of polynomial-delay enumeration kernels is that the limitation to polynomial delay often results in conceptually simple solution-lifting algorithms.
Thus, solutions that are not in the kernel are similar to the kernel solutions and the kernel essentially captures all interesting solution types.

Summarizing, a pd-ps kernel provides (i) a compact representation of all types of solutions to the input instance, and (ii) a guarantee that for every solution of the output instance, the collection of solutions that are not “contained” in the kernel can be enumerated easily, that is, in polynomial delay. A further advantage of polynomial-delay enumeration kernels, which we discuss in more detail in
Section

2
, is that they provide a means to bound the total running time that an enumeration algorithm spends on outputs without polynomial delay. In particular, one can observe that the smaller the kernel, the better this running time bound will be.

By the above, enumeration kernels with polynomial-delay solution-lifting algorithms are a desirable type of algorithms for hard parameterized enumeration problems and obtaining a small kernel size is an interesting goal in the design of these algorithms. A priori it is unclear, however, whether there are many problems for which we can find such kernelizations: The only currently known pd-ps kernels are given for enumeration variants of the
Matching Cut
and
d
-Cut
problem
[
20
,
31
]
, two graph problems that have received some interest but are certainly not among the most famous ones.
Thus, it is open whether there are further examples of pd-ps kernels, ideally for well-studied hard problems. In this work, we thus consider the following well-known problem as a candidate for application of the framework.

Enum Long-Path

Input:
An undirected graph
G=(V,E)
and an integer
k
.

Goal:
Enumerate all
k
-paths, that is, all paths having exactly
k
distinct vertices in
G
.

The corresponding decision version of the
Enum Long-Path
problem is the NP-complete
Long-Path
problem which asks if an undirected graph
G
has a
k
-path.

Long-Path
is well-studied from the perspective of parameterized complexity
[
3
,
14
,
17
,
18
,
22
]
and kernelization
[
5
,
6
,
24
,
25
]
.
In particular,
Long-Path
admits a polynomial kernel when parameterized by the vertex cover number, the distance to cluster, and the max-leaf number of the input graph
[
6
]
.
In contrast,
Long-Path
admits no polynomial kernel when parameterized by the solution size
k

[
5
]
or by the (vertex deletion) distance to outerplanar graphs
[
6
]
.

Our Contributions.

The two above-mentioned kernelization hardness results for
Long-Path
directly exclude pd-ps kernels for parameterization by solution size
k
or by distance to outerplanar graphs. We thus consider
Enum Long-Path
parameterized by two parameters that are lower-bounded by the distance to outerplanar graphs: the vertex cover number (
{{\sf vc}}
) and the dissociation number (
{{\sf diss}}
) of the input graph.
We also consider the distance to clique (
{{\sf dtc}}
) of the input graph which is incomparable to
{{\sf vc}}
and
{\sf diss}
.
We refer to
Fig.

1
for an illustration of the parameter relations and our results.

A hierarchy of parameters. A cyan box indicates polynomial sized enumeration kernels. A pink box indicates the non-existence of polynomial sized enumeration kernels. A yellow box indicates open status.
An edge from a parameter
\alpha
to a parameter
\beta

below
\alpha
means that there is a function
f
such that
\beta\leq f(\alpha)
in every graph.

Our first main result is the following.

Theorem 1.1

Enum Long-Path
parameterized by
{{\sf vc}}
admits an
\mathcal{O}(n\cdot m\cdot k^{2})
-delay enumeration kernel with
\mathcal{O}({{\sf vc}}^{2})
vertices.

The quadratic bound on the number of vertices is the same as for the known kernel for the decision problem
[
6
]
. Thus,
Theorem

1.1
generalizes the best decision kernel to the enumeration problem.
To prove
Theorem

1.1
we make use of (i) the “new expansion lemma”
[
16
,
19
]
and (ii) the enumeration of maximal matchings of a given cardinality with polynomial delay
[
27
,
28
]
.
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first application of the expansion lemma and its variants
[
16
,
19
,
37
]
in the context of enumeration algorithms.

More specifically, to compute the kernel, we use the new expansion lemma on an auxiliary bipartite graph where one partite set corresponds to pairs of vertices from the vertex cover and the other partite set corresponds to vertices of the independent set having degree at least two.
In the kernel, we preserve (1) all vertices from the vertex cover, (2) the vertices of the independent set of degree at least two identified by the new expansion lemma, and (3) a small number of degree-1 vertices of the independent set.
We then define a
signature
of a
k
-path
P
.
Roughly speaking the signature is a mapping of all vertices of the vertex cover of
P
to their position in
P
.
The signature is then used to define the notion of
equivalent paths
.
Since in the kernel there might be many equivalent
k
-paths, in the solution lifting algorithm we distinguish whether a
k
-path
P
is minimal or not.
If
P
is not minimal, we only output
P
, and otherwise, if
P
is minimal, we enumerate all equivalent
k
-paths containing at least one non-kernel vertex.
This ensures that each
k
-path of the input graph is output exactly once.
The expansion computed in the kernelization is essential for guaranteeing the delay of the solution-lifting algorithm:
whenever we replace an independent set of the kernel with a non-kernel one, the expansion ensures that this sequence can be extended to a
k
-path of
G
.

Our next result is a polynomial-delay enumeration kernel when the parameter is
{{\sf diss}}
, the dissociation number of the graph. This number is the vertex deletion distance to graphs where each connected component has at most two vertices.

Theorem 1.2

Enum Long-Path
parameterized by
{{\sf diss}}
admits an
\mathcal{O}({{\sf diss}}\cdot n)
-delay enumeration kernelization with
\mathcal{O}({{\sf diss}}^{3})
vertices.

Ideally, we again want to use the new expansion lemma in the kernelization algorithm.
We face a new issue, however:
The remaining vertices
I
of the input graph
G
which are not in the dissociation set
X
now form components of size 1 and 2.
Furthermore, in a
k
-path
P
between two vertices of
X
there can be one or two vertices from
I
. Moreover, vertices contained in size-2 components of
I
can also be used as single vertex between two vertices of
X
in
P
.
Consequently, we would need to employ the new expansion lemma for components of
I
of size 1 and size 2 individually.
This complicates the application of the expansion lemma.
Instead, we can achieve a polynomial kernel of cubic size much easier:
We distinguish
rare
and
frequent
vertices of
I
.
Roughly speaking, a vertex
u
is
rare
if there are only a small number of vertices with the same neighborhood with respect to
X
, and otherwise
u
is frequent.
In the kernel we preserve all rare vertices and sufficiently many frequent vertices.
Our solution lifting algorithm then uses similar ideas as the one for
{{\sf vc}}
.
Our approach even gives a kernelization for the more general
r
-
{{\sf coc}}
parameter, which is the vertex deletion distance to graphs whose connected components have size at most
r
.

Corollary 1

Enum Long-Path
parameterized by
r
-
{{\sf coc}}
for constant
r
admits an
\mathcal{O}(r
-
{{\sf coc}}\cdot n\cdot 2^{r})
-delay enumeration kernelization with
\mathcal{O}((r
-
{{\sf coc}})^{3})
vertices that can be computed in
\mathcal{O}(2^{r}\cdot r\cdot(r
-
{{\sf coc}})^{2}\cdot n)
time.

Ideally, we would like to provide kernels for even smaller parameters such as the vertex integrity or the treedepth of
G
. For these parameters, however, already decision kernels are unlikely due to the following folklore result. Here,
{\sf maxcp}
denotes the size of a largest connected component. This parameter is at least as large as vertex integrity and treedepth.

Proposition 1

Enum Long-Path
has no pd-ps kernel when parameterized by
{{\sf maxcp}}
unless
{\sf NP\subseteq coNP/poly}
.

Proof.

The disjoint union of
2^{t}
instances of
Long-Path
yields a trivial or-cross composition showing that
Long-Path
has no polynomial size compression for
{{\sf maxcp}}
unless
{\sf NP\subseteq coNP/poly}
.
This result transfers to
Enum Long-Path
.
∎

To obtain
Theorems

1.1
and
1.2
, we exploited that the length of a longest path can be upper-bounded by
\mathcal{O}({{\sf vc}})
and
\mathcal{O}({{\sf diss}})
, respectively.
This property does not hold for
{{\sf dtc}}
, the vertex deletion distance to cliques since the length of a longest path exceeds
{{\sf dtc}}
if the clique is larger than
{{\sf dtc}}
.

To resolve the issue of the unbounded path length, our kernelization works in 2 stages:
first the clique and parameter
k
are reduced simultaneously and second only clique vertices are removed.
Afterwards, we employ a marking scheme on the remaining clique similar to our marking scheme for
{{\sf diss}}
.
For the solution lifting algorithm, we also need to be more careful since the parameter
k^{\prime}
might be much smaller than
k
.
To ensure that each
k
-path is enumerated exactly once, we use two variants of the solution lifting algorithm. This gives our final main result, a polynomial-delay enumeration kernelization for the parameter
{{\sf dtc}}
.

Theorem 1.3

Enum Long-Path
parameterized by
{\sf dtc}
admits an
\mathcal{O}(n\cdot{\sf dtc}(G))
-delay enumeration kernelization with
\mathcal{O}({{\sf dtc}}^{3})
vertices.

We also show that our techniques can be adapted to
Enum Long-Cycle
.

Expansion Lemma.

Let
q
be a positive integer and
H
be a bipartite graph with bipartition
(A,B)
.
For

and
\widehat{B}\subseteq B

(i)

has one endpoint in
\widehat{A}
and the other endpoint in
\widehat{B}
,

(ii)

\widehat{A}
is incident to exactly

, and

(iii)

\widehat{B}
are incident to some edges of
M
.
A vertex of
\widehat{A}\cup\widehat{B}
is

.
By definition, all vertices of
\widehat{A}
are saturated by

\widehat{B}
may contain some unsaturated vertices.
Observe that a

-expansion of
\widehat{A}
onto
\widehat{B}
is just a matching of
\widehat{A}
onto
\widehat{B}
that saturates
\widehat{A}
.
We use the following proposition in our paper.

Proposition 2

(New

-Expansion Lemma - Lemma 3.2 of
[
16
]
)

Let

be a positive integer and
H
be a bipartite graph with bipartition
(A,B)
.
Then, there exists a polynomial-time algorithm that computes
\widehat{A}\subseteq A
and
\widehat{B}\subseteq B
such that there is a
q
-expansion of
\widehat{A}
onto
\widehat{B}
such that
(i)

N_{H}(\widehat{B})\subseteq\widehat{A}
, and

(ii)

|B\setminus\widehat{B}|\leq q\cdot|A\setminus\widehat{A}|
.

Given a graph
G

S\subseteq V(G)

if for every edge

The size of a vertex cover with minimum cardinality is called the

{{\sf vc}}(G)
.
A vertex subset
S
is a

[
34
]
if

has maximum degree at most one.
The

of a graph

, denoted by
{{\sf diss}}(G)
, is the size of a smallest dissociation set of

.
The

.
Given a graph

, let
{{\sf maxcp}}(G)
, denote the

of

.
In other words,

.

.

.

Parameterized Complexity and Kernelization.

A parameterized problem is a pair
(L,\kappa)
where
L\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
and
\kappa:\Sigma^{*}\to{\mathbb{N}}
is a computable function, called the
parameterization
or simply
parameter
.
A parameterized problem
(L,\kappa)
is said to be
fixed-parameter tractable
(or FPT in short) if an algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
that, given
x\in\Sigma^{*}
, decides in
f(k)\cdot|x|^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
-time if
x\in L
, where
f:{\mathbb{N}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{N}}
is some computable function. It is natural and common to assume that
\kappa(x)
is either simply given with
x
or can be computed in polynomial time from
x
.
Given a graph
G
, our parameterizations
{{\sf vc}}(G)
,
{{\sf diss}}(G)
, and
{{\sf dtc}}(G)
are NP-hard to compute, hence we only demand computability for
\kappa
. If one insists on polynomial-time computability, one may equivalently consider parameterization by the size of polynomial-time computable approximation solutions which are at most 3 times as large as
{{\sf vc}}(G),{{\sf diss}}(G)
, and
{{\sf dtc}}(G)
, respectively.

An important technique to design FPT-algorithms is kernelization or parameterized preprocessing.
Formally, a parameterized problem
(L,\kappa)
admits a
kernelization
if given
x\in\Sigma^{*}
, there is an algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
that runs in polynomial time and outputs an instance
x^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{*}
such that
(i)

x\in L
if and only if
x^{\prime}\in L
, and
(ii)

|x^{\prime}|+\kappa(x^{\prime})\leq g(\kappa(x))
for some computable function
g:{\mathbb{N}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{N}}
.
If
g(\cdot)
is a polynomial function, then
L
is said to admit a
polynomial kernel
.
It is well-known that a decidable parameterized problem is FPT if and only if it admits a kernel
[
9
]
.
For a more detailed discussion on parameterized complexity and kernelization, we refer to
[
9
,
12
,
19
]
.

Parameterized Enumeration and Enumeration Kernelization.

Creignou et al.
[
8
]
defined parameterized enumeration as follows.
An
enumeration problem
over a finite alphabet
\Sigma
is a tuple
(L,{\sf Sol})
such that

(i)

L\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
is a decision problem, and

(ii)

{\sf Sol}:\Sigma^{*}\rightarrow 2^{\Sigma^{*}}
is a computable function and
{\sf Sol}(x)\neq\emptyset
if and only if
x\in L
.
If
x\in L
, then
{\sf Sol}(x)
is the finite
set of solutions
to
x
.
A
parameterized enumeration problem
is a triple
\Pi=(L,{\sf Sol},\kappa(x))
where
(L,{\sf Sol})
is an enumeration problem and
\kappa:\Sigma^{*}\rightarrow{\mathbb{N}}
is the parameterization.

An enumeration algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
for a parameterized enumeration problem
\Pi
is a deterministic algorithm that given
x\in\Sigma^{*}
, outputs
{\sf Sol}(x)
exactly without duplicates and terminates after a finite number of steps.
For
x\in L
and
1\leq i\leq|{\sf Sol}(x)|
, the
i
-th delay
of
{\mathcal{A}}
is the time taken between outputting the
i
-th solution and the
(i+1)
-th solution of
{\sf Sol}(x)
.
The
0
-th delay
is the
precalculation time
, that is, the time taken from the start of the algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
to outputting the first solution.
The
postcalculation
time is the
|{\sf Sol}(x)|
-th delay, that is, the time from the last output to the termination of
{\mathcal{A}}
.
If an enumeration algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
guarantees that every delay is bounded by
f(\kappa(x))\cdot|x|^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
, then
{\mathcal{A}}
is an
FPT-delay enumeration algorithm
(
FPT-delay
algorithm).
If every delay is bounded by
|x|^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
, then
{\mathcal{A}}
is a
polynomial-delay enumeration algorithm
.
We will show kernelization results for the following definition of enumeration kernels.

(
[

]
)
.

A

(i)

For every instance

(ii)

for every

solution-lifting algorithm

{\mathcal{B}}
computes with delay polynomial in
|x|+|y|+\kappa(x)+\kappa(y)
, a nonempty set of solutions
S_{s}\subseteq{\sf Sol}(x)
such that
\{S_{s}\mid s\in{\sf Sol}(y)\}
is a partition of
{\sf Sol}(x)
.

The function
g(\cdot)
is the
size
of the polynomial-delay enumeration kernel.
If
g(\cdot)
is a polynomial function, then
\Pi
is said to admit a polynomial-delay enumeration kernel of
polynomial size
.

Observe that, by condition (
ii
) in the above definition,
x\in L
if and only if
y\in L
.
Observe from the definition that every polynomial-delay enumeration kernelization also corresponds to a kernel for the decision version of the corresponding problem.

Before stating our results, we show that the existence of polynomial delay enumeration kernels of a small size also entails running time guarantees concerning the non-polynomial part of the total running time. To put this bound into context, let us mention that the existence of an enumeration algorithm with FPT delay implies that there is an enumeration algorithm where the precalculation time is
f(\kappa(x))
and all further delays and the postcalculation time are polynomial
[
32
]
.
The bound for
f(\cdot)
is, however, usually prohibitively large. For polynomial-delay enumeration kernels we obtain the following.

Proposition 4

Let
\Pi=(L,{\sf Sol},\kappa)
be a parameterized enumeration problem such that there is an algorithm that outputs for any given
x\in\Sigma^{*}
the set
\sf Sol(x)
in
T(|x|)
time.
If
\Pi
admits a polynomial-delay enumeration kernel of size
g
, then
\Pi
admits an algorithm with
T(g(\kappa(x)))+|x|^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
precalculation time and polynomial delay.

Proof.

Since
\Pi
admits a polynomial-delay enumeration kernel, let
{\mathcal{A}}
be the kernelization algorithm and
{\mathcal{B}}
be the solution-lifting algorithm for the polynomial-delay enumeration kernel of
\Pi
.
The algorithm first uses
{\mathcal{A}}
to compute the kernel
y
of size
g(\kappa(x))
in
|x|^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
time.
Then it computes in
T(|y|)
time the set
\sf Sol(y)
.
This concludes the precalculation.
If
{\sf Sol}(y)=\emptyset
, then the algorithm stops without outputting any solution to the instance
x
.
Since
{\sf Sol}(x)\neq\emptyset
if and only if
{\sf Sol}(y)\neq\emptyset
, this step is correct.
Otherwise,
{\sf Sol}(y)\neq\emptyset
.

Then, the algorithm outputs for each
s\in\sf Sol(y)
using the solution-lifting algorithm
{\mathcal{B}}
the corresponding solutions
S_{s}\subseteq\sf Sol(x)
. The precalculation time is
T(|y|)+|x|^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
to compute the kernel, and all solutions in the kernel and the first solution output by the solution-lifting algorithm.
Next, all remaining solutions in
\sf Sol(x)
are output with polynomial delay, either as the next solution of some solution
S_{s}
or as the first solution of the next solution of
S_{s^{\prime}}
for the next solution
s^{\prime}
of
\sf Sol(y)
.
∎

The above is similar to decision problems where small kernels and exact exponential-time algorithms directly give concrete running time bounds for FPT-algorithms.

3
Parameterization by the Vertex Cover Number

In this section, we describe a polynomial-delay enumeration kernel for
Enum Long-Path
when parameterized by
{{\sf vc}}
, the size of a minimum vertex cover of the input graph. We first describe the kernelization algorithm and then the solution-lifting algorithm.
We assume without loss of generality that
G
has no isolated vertex, as isolated vertices cannot be part of any
k
-path for
k>1
and
Enum Long-Path
is trivial for
k=1
.

3.1
Marking Scheme and Kernelization Algorithm

To obtain our kernelization, we mark the vertices of
G
that we will keep in the kernel.
Given the input graph
G=(V,E)
and an integer
k
, we first invoke a linear-time algorithm
[
36
]
that outputs a vertex cover
X
of
G
such that
|X|\leq 2\cdot{{\sf vc}}(G)
.
We mark all vertices in
X
.
Now, let

.
We consider a partition

such that
I_{1}
is the set of all vertices of
I
that have degree one in
G
. We call the vertices of
I_{1}

pendant
.
We then construct an auxiliary bipartite graph

with bipartition
({{X}\choose{2}},I_{2})
as follows:
a vertex

is adjacent to a vertex
\{x,y\}\in{{X}\choose{2}}
in

if and only if

. This bipartite graph construction is inspired by Bodlaender et al.
[
6

who showed that the

-paths of

correspond to matchings in

Since

has no isolated vertex and

excludes the pendant vertices,

has no isolated vertex.
Then, we invoke

2

on the graph

\widehat{A}\subseteq\binom{X}{2}
and a set
\widehat{B}\subseteq I_{2}
such that there is a 3-expansion

of
\widehat{A}
onto
\widehat{B}
such that
(i)

and
(ii)

Furthermore, there exists a set

of size

such that each edge of

has exactly one endpoint in

, the vertices in
\widehat{B}
saturated by

We mark all vertices in

If no 3-expansion exists, that is, if
\widehat{A}=\emptyset
and thus also
\widehat{B}=B^{\prime}=\emptyset

I_{2}
in that case, and

is small by property
(ii)
of
Proposition

2

Otherwise, it is sufficient to mark the vertices

which are saturated by

, a notion of equivalence for

-paths in

and

. Then, in

1

k
-path

in
G
an equivalent one in
G^{\prime}
.

Moreover, for every
x\in X
, we mark one arbitrary pendant vertex
u\in I_{1}
such that
xu\in E(G)
.
Let

denote the set of all marked pendant vertices of

.
It is sufficient to mark one vertex of

per

since each

-path can contain at most one such vertex (in the beginning or the end).
Now,

is the subgraph induced by all marked vertices, that is,

.
Since

is an induced subgraph of
G
and by properties (i) and (ii) of
Proposition

2
(new

-expansion lemma), we may observe the following.

Observation 1.

Let
G^{\prime}
be the graph obtained from

after invoking the above marking scheme.
Then,
G^{\prime}
has
\mathcal{O}(|X|^{2})
vertices.
Moreover, any
k
-path of

is also a

-path of

Proof.

By construction, at most
|X|
pendant vertices of

are marked.
Hence,
|I_{1}^{+}|\leq|X|
.
Moreover, property (ii) of

2
implies that

Therefore,

has

vertices.

The second claim of the statement follows since

is an induced subgraph of

.
∎

We let

denote the independent set in

.
Observe that

consists of the marked pendant vertices

, the vertices

saturated by the 3-expansion

, and the vertices of

which have at least one neighbor in

.
Moreover, let

be the subgraph of

induced by

with bipartition
({{X}\choose{2}},(I_{2}\setminus\widehat{B})\cup B^{\prime})
.

We prove the statements in the given order.
For the first part, if

Otherwise, let
(\{x,y\},u)

(\{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\},u^{\prime})

Consider the edges

3.3
A Challenge: Avoiding Duplicate Enumeration

-path

of

k
-path

of

The natural idea is that we output

for a path

that is equivalent.
In case two distinct

and

of

-path

of

In such a case, if we enumerate all

-paths of

that are equivalent to a
k
-path

of

, then the same

-path of

To circumvent this issue, we introduce a lexicographic order on equivalent paths which allows to output equivalent paths only for solution paths that are minimal with respect to this order.

We formalize the order as follows. First, we assume some fixed total order
\prec
on the vertices of

and thus also on the vertices of

.
Now, for a path

consider the subsequence

of

containing only the vertices of

and call this the

.
In other words, the

We say that a path

when the

is lexicographically smaller than the

-sequence of

.

Lemma 3

Let

be a
k
-path in

.
There is an
\mathcal{O}(k\cdot(m^{\prime}+n^{\prime}))
-time algorithm that checks if

is the lexicographically smallest

-path among all

-paths in

with the same signature.

Proof.

Consider the

-sequence

of

.
For each vertex

in the

-sequence, starting with

-path

with the same signature and an

-sequence

such that

for all

.
The path

is lexicographically smallest with its signature if and only if the test fails for all

To perform the test efficiently for all given

Recall that

We add some further vertices that deal with the case that the

For each index

To see the running time, observe that

Next, we define the

Marking Scheme and Kernelization

Marking Scheme.

Our kernel consists of

and some connected components of

.
In order to detect the necessary connected components of

Then, the kernel comprises of all rare components and sufficiently many frequent components.

Formally, let

If

Otherwise, the pair
(u,w)

If for

Finally, if

Kernelization.

To obtain our kernel we exploit the marking scheme as follows:
We consider the graph

induced by all vertices of

, all rare vertices, and some of the frequent vertices.

of vertices from the modulator which is

4.3
Challenges

The objective of the solution-lifting algorithm is that “for each equivalence class
\mathcal{P}_{i}
we choose a unique

k
-path
P_{i}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{i}^{\prime}
that is used to enumerate all
k
-paths in
\mathcal{P}_{i}\setminus\mathcal{P}_{i}^{\prime}
with polynomial-delay and also

”.
For every other
k
-path
\widetilde{P_{i}}\in\mathcal{P}_{i}^{\prime}\setminus\{P_{i}^{\prime}\}
, the solution-lifting algorithm only outputs
\widetilde{P_{i}}
.
To make this algorithm work, we have to overcome the following challenges:

1.

Verify that
\mathcal{P}_{i}^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
, that is, for each equivalence class
\mathcal{P}_{i}
there exists at least one
k
-path
P^{\prime}
which is entirely contained in the kernel, that is, in
G^{\prime}
.

2.

Define the notation of
suitable

k
-paths of
G^{\prime}
.

3.

Given a
k
-path
P_{i}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{i}^{\prime}
, check whether
P_{i}^{\prime}
is a suitable
k
-path or not in polynomial time.

4.

Ensure polynomial delay for the enumeration of all
k
-paths in
\mathcal{P}_{i}\setminus\mathcal{P}_{i}^{\prime}
.

4.4
Resolving the Four Challenges

This section is devoted to describe how we resolve each of the above mentioned challenges.
The following lemma illustrates a crucial structural characterization that resolves the first challenge.

Lemma 6

If
\mathcal{P}_{i}
is a nonempty equivalence class of
k
-paths in
G
, then
\mathcal{P}_{i}^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
.

Proof.

Let
P_{i}\in\mathcal{P}_{i}
.
We prove the lemma by showing that there is a
k
-path
P_{i}^{\prime}
in
G^{\prime}
that is equivalent to
P_{i}
.
This is sufficient to prove that
\mathcal{P}_{i}^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
.
By definition for each
P_{i},\widehat{P_{i}}\in\mathcal{P}_{i}
,
{\textsf{sig}}_{P_{i}}={\textsf{sig}}_{\widehat{P_{i}}}
.
Therefore, both
P_{i}
and
\widehat{P_{i}}
coincide on all non-frequent vertices.
It means that we have
Z=V(P_{i})\setminus\mathcal{F}=V(\widehat{P_{i}})\setminus\mathcal{F}
where
\mathcal{F}
is the set of all frequent vertices.
And also for each
w\in Z
,
{\sf pos}_{P_{i}}(w)={\sf pos}_{\widehat{P_{i}}}(w)
.
This implies that both
P_{i}
and
\widehat{P_{i}}
have the same sequences of consecutive indices such that the vertices at these indices stem from
\mathcal{F}
(frequent vertices).
Let
g
now be such a sequence of length
t\in[0,2]
and let
v_{\ell}\in P_{i}\cap(X\cup\mathcal{R})
be the vertex left of this sequence and let
v_{r}\in P_{i}\cap(X\cup\mathcal{R})
be the vertex right of this sequence (by definition these vertices are in the modulator
X
).
Observe that the vertices
v_{\ell}
and
v_{r}
are
t
-frequent by definition.
Also, note that
v_{\ell}
or
v_{r}
might not exists, if
P
starts/ends with
g
; in particular both might not exist if
k\leq 2
.
Due to
Lemma

5
,
P_{i}
and
\widehat{P_{i}}
admit prolongation property.
We exploit this prolongation property to construct

as follows.
According to the construction of
G^{\prime}
(kernelization algorithm), there are at least

paths
Q_{1},Q_{2},\ldots,Q_{q}
in
G^{\prime}
of length

from

to

that are internally vertex-disjoint.
Since each
k
-path consists of at most
|X|
vertices from
X
, there are at most
|X|+1
sequences.
Since each such sequence contains at most
2
vertices, at most

paths

are hit by any

-path.
Hence, there is at least one path

which is not hit.
Thus there exists also some

which contains

as subpath.
∎

Defining the notion of suitable

-path in

.

Now, we move on to define the notion of “suitable

that addresses the second challenge.
Recall that

(the graph outputted by the kernelization algorithm) is an induced subgraph of the input graph

We maintain the relative ordering of the vertices in

as they were in

.
It is possible for us to encode these vertices using

We exploit this small encoding of the vertices in

as follows.

Informally speaking, a

Formally, let

be an equivalence class of the

(recall that

).
By definition of

only differ in some frequent vertices.
Let

.
Then,

that appears at the

.

.
Furthermore, observe that for any two distinct

Based on

, we define an ordering

.

Definition 8
.

Let

We write

We say

{\sf order}(P_{1}^{\prime})\triangleleft{\sf order}(P_{2}^{\prime})

Informally speaking,

Clearly,

This allows us to define the suitable

Definition 9

A path

Since

Check whether a

-path

Let

.
Informally, we try to find

Recall that each connected component of

In the following we do

(recall that they either have length

With this intuition, our next lemma illustrates a proof how we can check in polynomial time whether a given

Lemma 7

Let

.

{\sf order}(P_{i})
.
Additionally, we assume that we already verified that there is no lexicographically smaller

in a position of

Furthermore, let

be the corresponding

Parameterization by Distance to Clique

We present a polynomial-delay enumeration kernel for
Enum Long-Path
parameterized by the vertex deletion distance
{\sf dtc}(G)
of the graph
G
to a clique.
Again, for an input instance
(G,k)
we initially compute a
2
-approximation
X
of
{\sf dtc}(G)
by greedily adding
2K_{1}
s to
X
.
Let
\ell=|X|
and
C=V(G)\setminus X
be the remaining clique.
Again, we assume an arbitrary but fixed ordering
\prec
on the vertices of
C
.
We exploit the ordering
\prec
to define a minimal element in each equivalence class of
k^{\prime}
-paths in
G^{\prime}
which is then used in the solution-lifting algorithm.

In sharp contrast to the vertex cover number
{\sf vc}(G)
(see
Section

3
), and more general the dissociation number
{\sf diss}(G)
(see
Section

4
), the vertex deletion distance
{\sf dtc}(G)
to a clique does
not
provide any bound for the length
k
of a path.
Hence, in the kernelization we modify both the graph
G

and
the parameter
k
.

Strategy.

Initially, we employ a marking scheme similar to the one we used for the dissociation number
{\sf diss}(G)
(see
Section

4
).
More precisely, we mark sufficiently many vertices in
C
as
rare
and
frequent
such that all possible structures of a
k
-path can be preserved in the kernel.
Our kernelization comprises two steps:
First, we remove unmarked vertices of the clique and simultaneously we decrease
k
by the number of removed vertices.
This ensures that the new parameter
k^{\prime}
is bounded in
\ell\in\mathcal{O}({\sf dtc}(G))
.
Now, the remaining clique might still be unbounded in
{\sf dtc}(G)
.
Thus, in a second step, we remove unmarked vertices of the clique until also the size of the remaining clique is bounded in some function depending only on
{\sf dtc}(G)
.
It is necessary to do this in two steps since we have to ensure that all signatures are preserved (
k^{\prime}
does not get too small) and that we do not obtain an instance with no
k^{\prime}
-path (
k^{\prime}
is larger than the number of vertices in the kernel).

Our solution lifting algorithm uses similar ideas as the solution lifting algorithm for the dissociation number
{\sf diss}(G)
(see
Section

4
):
Initially, we define the
signature

\textsf{sig}_{P^{\prime}}
of each
k^{\prime}
-path
P^{\prime}
in
G^{\prime}
.
Afterwards, based on these signatures we define equivalence classes and define a suitable
k^{\prime}
-path of each equivalence class.
Then, we present a base version of the solution lifting algorithm
SolLift
.
This base version is adapted to obtain an algorithm
SolLiftTest
to check whether an
k^{\prime}
-path is suitable or not.
Afterwards,
SolLift
and
SolLiftTest
are used to verify that the equivalence classes of the reduced graph
G^{\prime}
are identical to those of the input graph
G
.
In contrast to
{\sf diss}(G)
, we cannot pick the suitable path
P^{\prime}
in
G^{\prime}
of an equivalence class to enumerate all
k
-paths in
G
having the same signature and output the path itself if
P^{\prime}
is not suitable:

P^{\prime}
has only length
k^{\prime}
and thus we have to add
k-k^{\prime}
vertices to
P^{\prime}
.
We need to do this in a clever way to ensure that each
k
-path in
G
is only enumerated once.
For this we use two further variants
SolLiftS
for suitable paths and
SolLiftNS
for non-suitable paths.
With each call of

we output
exactly one

-path having signature

5.1
Marking Scheme and Kernelization

Marking Scheme.

Similar to
{\sf diss}(G)
we mark sufficiently many common neighbors in
C
of two vertices in the modulator
X
, and we also mark sufficiently many neighbors in
C
of each vertex in
X
.
Let

and
p=2(\ell+1)
.
If

, and otherwise, mark

.
We treat the case

p
of these vertices (arbitrarily) as

Now, by

for at least one vertex

(see

Observation 3.

The above-described marking scheme marks at most

vertices as rare or frequent.

Proof.

For each pair of vertices in the modulator
X
at most
p
common neighbors in the clique are marked as rare or frequent.
This results in at most

marked vertices.
Furthermore, for each vertex in

p
neighbors in the clique are marked.
Thus, the claimed bound follows.
∎

Kernelization.

We may safely assume that
k\leq|V(G)|=|C|+|X|
since otherwise there is no
k
-path in
G
.
Let

We remove

with partition

.
Simultaneously, we set

.
Afterwards, if

arbitrarily, resulting in graph

with partition

.
Note that for the vertices in

, we maintain their relative ordering implied by the labels of all vertices in

.
Observe that for each removal there are sufficiently many unmarked vertices in

or
C^{\prime\prime}
since by

3

.

Lemma 9

has
\mathcal{O}({\sf dtc}(G)^{3})
vertices and

.

Proof.

Our kernelization algorithm ensures that

.

Furthermore, note that

Hence,

The statement follows since

∎

5.3
Challenges

The basic idea of the solution lifting algorithm is analog to the one for the dissociation number

(see

is

.

Check in polynomial time for some

, we need to remove the gap-symbols and add consecutive sequences of vertices from
C
which are not rare.
As discussed earlier, ideally one wants to insert each non-rare clique vertex in each gap; but this is not possible:
A vertex from the modulator
X
in
P_{i}^{\prime}
cannot have each vertex from
C
as predecessor or successor; only some frequent vertices are possible at this position ensuring the path property.
Because of this technical hurdle, our solution lifting algorithm consists of two phases, which we next describe informally.

Phase 1:

By definition, the predecessor and successor of a gap-symbol
g_{C}
in
\textsf{sig}_{P_{i}^{\prime}}
are two vertices from the modulator
X
.
Thus, we have to replace
g_{C}
by a common neighbor of these two vertices.
Similar, either the predecessor or the successor (not both, see
Definition

10
) of a gap-symbol
g_{N}
in
\textsf{sig}_{P_{i}^{\prime}}
is from the modulator
X
(this is well-defined).
Consequently, we have to replace
g_{N}
by a neighbor of this vertex.
Since we marked sufficiently many vertices as frequent, such replacements are always possible.
Let
Q_{i}^{\prime}
be the resulting
|\textsf{sig}_{P_{i}^{\prime}}|
-path.

Phase 2:

Now, we add further non-rare clique vertices to
Q_{i}^{\prime}
to obtain a
k
-path
P_{i}
.
More precisely, we add (possible empty) sequences of non-rare clique vertices between any two clique vertices of
Q_{i}^{\prime}
.

Next, we provide formal details for both phases.

Phase 1.

Recall that the aim of Phase
1
is to replace each gap-symbol
g_{N}
or
g_{C}
in
\textsf{sig}_{P_{i}^{\prime}}
by
exactly one
non-rare clique vertex.
Also, recall that the total number of gap-symbols in
\textsf{sig}_{P_{i}^{\prime}}
is at most
2\cdot|X|<p
; the threshold for marking clique vertices as rare or frequent.
Next, we describe how we replace each gap-symbol
g_{C}
and
g_{N}
.
We replace them from lowest to highest index in
\textsf{sig}_{P_{i}^{\prime}}
.
Let
L
be the current set of non-rare clique vertices we used for this replacement.
Initially,
L=\emptyset
.

Let
u,w\in X\cap P_{i}^{\prime}
be the predecessor and successor of
g_{C}
, respectively, and let
D_{C}=(N(u)\cap N(w)\cap C)\setminus(\mathcal{R}\cup L)
be the common neighbors of
u
and
w
in the clique which are not rare and not already used.
For each vertex
v\in D_{C}
, we branch in the possibility of replacing
g_{C}
with
v
.
Then, we add
v
to
L
.
We show that each of these branches leads to at least one valid extension which is a
k
-path of
G
.
For gap-symbol
g_{N}
we proceed similarly:
By definition of
g_{N}
, either its predecessor or successor (not both, see
Definition

10
), say
u
, in
P_{i}^{\prime}
is from the modulator
X
.
Let
D_{N}=(N(u)\cap C)\setminus(\mathcal{R}\cup L)
be the non-rare neighbors of
u
in the clique which are not already used.
Again, for each
v\in D_{N}
, we branch in the possibility of replacing
g_{N}
with
v
and we show that each of these branches leads to at least one valid extension, that is, a
k
-path in
G
.
Afterwards, we add
v
to
L
.

It remains to show that for each gap-symbol there is at least one vertex we can replace this gap-symbol with, that is,
|D_{C}|\geq 1
and
|D_{N}|\geq 1
for each step of the replacement.
By our definition of frequent vertices, we have
|D_{C}\cup L|\geq p
and
|D_{N}\cup L|\geq p
.
Since
L
contains at most one vertex for each gap-symbol in
\textsf{sig}_{P_{i}^{\prime}}
, we obtain that
|L|\leq 2\cdot|X|<p
.
Hence,
|D_{C}|\geq 1
and
|D_{N}|\geq 1
for each branching.
Thus, each branching leads to a
|\textsf{sig}_{P_{i}^{\prime}}|
-path in
G
containing no gap-symbols.

Phase 2.

Recall that the aim of this phase is to add non-rare clique vertices to
Q_{i}^{\prime}
to obtain
k
-paths of
G
having the same signature as
P_{i}^{\prime}
.
For this, we add (possible empty) sequences of (non-)kernel clique vertices to
Q_{i}^{\prime}
in the following cases:
a)
between two consecutive clique vertices from
Q_{i}^{\prime}
, and
b)
in the front/end of
Q_{i}^{\prime}
if the first/last vertex of
Q_{i}^{\prime}
is from the clique.
For this, let
p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{t}
with
t\leq|Q_{i}^{\prime}|+1
be the positions of
Q_{i}^{\prime}
such that
p_{i}
is the
i
th position in
Q_{i}^{\prime}
fulfilling
a)
or
b)
.

Let
L=C\setminus(\mathcal{R}\cup V(Q_{i}^{\prime}))
be the set of non-rare clique vertices not already contained in
Q_{i}^{\prime}
.
Note that
|L|\geq k-|Q_{i}^{\prime}|
, that is, there are sufficiently many vertices to add:
By definition of our kernel we have
|C\setminus C^{\prime}|\geq k-k^{\prime}
and by definition of path
Q_{i}^{\prime}
we know that all vertices in
V(P_{i}^{\prime})\setminus V(Q_{i}^{\prime})
are available in
L
and contained in
C^{\prime}
.

Intuitively, for each
Z\subseteq L
with
|Z|=k-|Q_{i}^{\prime}|
and each ordering
\pi
of

we enumerate all
k
-paths which can be obtained by adding the vertices in
Z
according to ordering
\pi
to
Q_{i}^{\prime}
in the positions
p_{1},\ldots,p_{t}
, that is, into each

we insert a (possibly empty) subsequent sequence of
\pi(Z)
such that each vertex in
Z
is assigned to exactly one position.

Now, we provide a precise description how Phase
2
works algorithmically.
The algorithm starts with the lexicographically smallest subset
L^{\prime}
of
L
having
k^{\prime}-|Q_{i}^{\prime}|
vertices and in each further iteration the algorithm considers the (unique) successor subset
L^{\prime}
according to this lexicographical order.
Furthermore, the algorithm starts with the lexicographical smallest ordering
\pi_{1}
of
L^{\prime}
and all orderings are sorted according to the lexicographical ordering of the resulting sequence.
In the following, let
Z
be such a set.
Consequently, in each further iteration the algorithm chooses the (unique) successor ordering
\pi_{j}
.
Note that all possibilities how many vertices of
Z
in order
\pi
are added into each positions
p_{1},\ldots,p_{t}
can be described by a vector

having

entries with values from

to

such that the sum of all entries is
|Z|
.
Let
\mathcal{V}
be the set of all such vectors and let

be an arbitrary but fixed ordering of

.
Now, for fixed vertices

and fixed ordering

the algorithm adds

in order
\pi
into positions
p_{1},\ldots,p_{t}
for all possibilities

in order of

.

Note that if
\textsf{sig}_{P_{i}^{\prime}}
is empty, then there is exactly one position
p_{1}
.
The resulting sequence
P
has length
k
.
In fact,
P
is also a
k
-path in
G
:
Q_{i}^{\prime}
is a path in
G
and between each two clique vertices of
Q_{i}^{\prime}
(and start/end if first/last vertex is from the clique), we add a consecutive sequence of clique vertices.
Thus, after each addition the resulting sequence is still a path.
We denote this algorithm as
SolLift
.

Check whether
P_{i}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{i}^{\prime}
is suitable.

Intuitively, the verification whether

is suitable is done by modifying the two phases of

as follows:
First, only kernel vertices from

can be added instead of all clique vertices in

.
Second, the output is a

-path of

, and not all

-paths in

For this, in both phases and each step of them only the vertex with smallest index is chosen.
This algorithm is referred to as

and the unique and well-defined output of

with input

Lemma 10

Given a

-path

of

-time algorithm

Proof.

Let
\textsf{sig}_{P_{i}^{\prime}}
be the signature of

.
Recall that in Phase 1 of

each gap-symbol is replaced by each possibility of a fitting non-rare clique vertex of

is modified as follows:

We now argue that

Phase 2 is modified as follows:
Let

be the non-rare kernel vertices of the clique which are not already used in path

.
Now, by definition,

is the lexicographically smallest

-path which can be outputted by Phase

of

More precisely, let

Let

be the vertices which which have a smaller index than

without the vertices which can be inserted in a smaller position.

Next, let

Observe that for each gap-symbol the set of candidate vertices of

Furthermore, positions

-path based on

∎

Main Theorem.

We now show that our solution lifting algorithm is correct and achieves polynomial delay.

See
1.3

Proof.

Size bound.
The size bound follows from

.

Next, we show that each
k
-path

of

In challenge

the set of vertices we can use to replace them can be computed in
\mathcal{O}(n)
time per gap-symbol, yielding a overall running time of
\mathcal{O}(n\cdot{\sf dtc}(G))
to compute these sets initially.
Each such candidate set is sorted ascendingly according to the indices.
Subsequently, all
|\textsf{sig}_{P_{i}^{\prime}}|
-paths having the same signature as
P_{i}^{\prime}
can be enumerated with a delay of
\mathcal{O}(n\cdot{\sf dtc}(G))
:
Define a lexicographical ordering of such
|\textsf{sig}_{P_{i}^{\prime}}|
-paths according to the orderings of the candidates sets, where the first criteria is the index of that vertex which replaced the first gap-symbol and so on.
Thus, all such
|\textsf{sig}_{P_{i}^{\prime}}|
-paths can be enumerated with
\mathcal{O}(n\cdot{\sf dtc}(G))
delay.

It remains to bound the delay of Phase 2.
The analysis of Phases 2.1 and 2.2 is similar, so we only analyze the delay for Phase 2.1; the only difference is that the output of Phase 2.1 is a
k^{\prime}
-path and that the output of Phase 2.2 is a
k
-path.
For this, let
Q_{i}^{\prime}
be a
|\textsf{sig}_{P_{i}^{\prime}}|
-path with
\textsf{sig}_{Q_{i}^{\prime}}=\textsf{sig}_{P_{i}^{\prime}}
outputted after Phase 1.
Furthermore, let
L=C\setminus(\mathcal{R}\cup V(Q_{i}^{\prime}))
be the set of non-rare clique vertices which can be added to
Q_{i}^{\prime}
.
Clearly,
L
is computable in
\mathcal{O}(n)
time and can be sorted ascendingly in linear time.
Also, positions
p_{1},\ldots,p_{t}
in which the vertices of
L
can be added without changing the signature of
Q_{i}^{\prime}
can be determined in
\mathcal{O}(k^{\prime})\in\mathcal{O}(n)
time.
Recall that initially,
SolLiftS
starts with the lexicographically smallest subset
L^{\prime}
of
L
having
k^{\prime}-|Q_{i}^{\prime}|
vertices and in each further iteration
SolLiftS
considers the (unique) successor subset
L^{\prime}
according to this lexicographical order.
Clearly, the first/next such subset
L^{\prime}
can be computed in
\mathcal{O}(n)
time.
Also, recall that
SolLiftS
starts with the lexicographical ordering
\pi_{1}
of
L^{\prime}
and all orderings are sorted according to the lexicographical ordering of the resulting sequence.
Consequently, in each further iteration
SolLiftS
chooses the (unique) successor ordering
\pi_{j}
.
Clearly, the first/next such ordering can be computed in
\mathcal{O}(n)
time.
Also recall that all possibilities of adding the vertices
L^{\prime}
in order
\pi
to positions
p_{1},\ldots,p_{t}
are described by a vector
\vec{v}
of length
t
and
\mathcal{V}
is the set of all such vectors and
\chi
is an arbitrary but fixed ordering of
\mathcal{V}
.
For each fixed
L^{\prime}
and fixed
\pi
,
SolLiftS
considers all vectors in
\mathcal{V}
according to
\chi
.
Clearly, the next such distribution of the vertices of
L^{\prime}
according to
\chi
can be computed in
\mathcal{O}(n)
time.
Thus, in overall
\mathcal{O}(n\cdot\log(n))
time the first/next/last
k^{\prime}
-path
P_{i}^{*}
is computed.
Hence, the delay of Phase 2.1 (and also Phase 2.2) is
\mathcal{O}(n)
.

Recall that if
P_{i}^{*}
is a path in
G^{\prime}
, not each
k
-path enumerated by Phase 2.2 is outputted: the unique
k
-path enumerated by
SolLiftNS
with input
P_{i}^{*}
is not outputted.
Thus, if
P_{i}^{*}
is a path in
G^{\prime}
, we check for each enumerated
k
-path
P_{i}
of
SolLiftS
whether
P_{i}=\texttt{SolLiftNS}(P_{i}^{*})
and only output
P_{i}
if
P_{i}\neq\texttt{SolLiftNS}(P_{i}^{*})
.
We now show that if
P_{i}
is not outputted (since
P_{i}
is the output of one call of
SolLiftNS
), then the next
k
-path
P_{i}^{\prime}
computed by
SolLiftS
is not the output of any call of
SolLiftNS
and is thus outputted.
If this property is verified, we can safely conclude that we only get an additional factor of 2 for the delay and thus we have shown that the overall delay of our algorithm is
\mathcal{O}(n\cdot{\sf dtc}(G))
.

It remains to show this property.
Assume that
P_{i}
is not outputted since
P_{i}
is the output of one call of
SolLiftNS
.
Recall that in
SolLiftNS
all non-rare clique vertices are added in the last possible position in ascending order.
Hence, in
SolLiftS
the last possible vector of
\mathcal{V}
according to
\chi
was used.
Consequently, the next ordering
\pi
of the vertices
L^{\prime}
is considered.
If there is another ordering, the subsequent ordering is not ascending and thus a
k
-path which is not the output of any call of
SolLiftNS
is produced.
So assume that there is no next ordering
\pi
.
This implies that
|L^{\prime}|=1
since the ascending order is the first order,
Hence, the algorithm now considers the next vertex in
C\setminus(\mathcal{R}\cup V(Q_{i}^{\prime}))
.
If such a vertex exists, then the subsequent
k
-path cannot be the output of a call of
SolLiftNS
and otherwise, if no such vertex exists, the algorithm
SolLiftS
terminates.
Thus, this property is verified.
∎

5.5
Extension to other Path and Cycle Variants

We now extend our positive result of

to

and the variants where all paths/cycles of length
at least

k

We start with

Proposition 6

Enum Long-Path At Least
parameterized by
{\sf dtc}(G)
admits an

-delay enumeration kernelization with

Proof.

The marking scheme, the kernelization, the definition of signature, the definition of the equivalence classes, and algorithm
SolLift
is unchanged.
Now, the argumentation that an equivalence class
\mathcal{P}_{i}
of the original instance is non-empty if and only if the corresponding equivalence class

of the kernel is non-empty is slightly different:
By algorithm

for each non-empty class

one obtains at least one
k
-path of
\mathcal{P}_{i}
.
Now, let

of length at least
k

Algorithm
SolLiftTest
computes a
k^{\prime}
-path

with the same signature.
Hence, if
\mathcal{P}_{i}\neq\emptyset
, then there exists at least one

-path in
\mathcal{P}_{i}^{\prime}
.
Thus, there is a one-to-one-correspondence between the classes
\mathcal{P}_{i}
and
\mathcal{P}_{i}^{\prime}
.
Since for each non-empty class
\mathcal{P}_{i}^{\prime}
contains at least one

-path, we can define the suitable path similar:
For

P_{1}^{\prime}\triangleleft P_{2}^{\prime}
and if

, then we use the existing definition described in
Section

5.4
.

Next we adapt algorithms
SolLiftNS
and
SolLiftS
.
For each
(k^{\prime}+z)
-path
P_{i}^{\prime}
in
G
, where

adds exactly
k-k^{\prime}
clique vertices to

5.4
.
Note that for distinct paths

and

of length at least

(P_{1}^{\prime})\neq

(P_{2}^{\prime})

and

have the same length, then the outputs are different as shown in

1.3
.
Let

be the suitable

-path of class
\mathcal{P}_{i}^{\prime}
.

Next, we adapt
SolLiftS
.
Phase 1 is not changed, that is, a
|\textsf{sig}_{P_{i}^{\prime}}|
-path

is computed where

is the suitable path of class
\mathcal{P}_{i}^{\prime}
.
For Phase 2, recall that
L=C\setminus(\mathcal{R}\cup V(Q_{i}^{\prime}))
is the set of candidate vertices which can be added in Phase 2.
In
Section

5.4
,
SolLiftS
for each

of size exactly
k-|Q_{i}^{\prime}|
, enumerated all
k
-paths where exactly the vertices of

where added to
Q_{i}^{\prime}
.
We enhance Phase 2 as follows:
Initially, all
k
-paths of
\mathcal{P}_{i}
are enumerated by using each

of size

.
Again, in Phase 2.1 we first compute a
k^{\prime}
-path
P_{i}^{\prime\prime}
and if

is a path in

we do not enumerate the output of

(P_{i}^{\prime\prime})

-paths of

The correctness is similar:

and

from the same equivalence class as the input.

6
Conclusion

We initiated a systematic study of enumeration kernels for the problems of finding paths (and cycles) with (at least)
k
vertices, leading to polynomial-delay enumeration kernels of polynomial size (pd-ps kernels) for the parameters
{{\sf vc}}
,
{{\sf diss}}
,
r
-
{{\sf coc}}
, and
{{\sf dtc}}
.
It is open whether our positive results can be extended to a pd-ps kernel for the parameters distance to cluster, feedback vertex set, or neighborhood diversity (for which even a polynomial-size kernel for the decision problem seems to be open).
More generally, it is open to develop pd-ps kernels for other well-studied problems.
