Limb salvage with brachial-to-radial bypass using a saphenous vein graft following severe crush injury: A case report
Authors: Fulya Topuz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSevere crush injuries to the upper limb can result in complex arterial damage and acute limb ischaemia, which often precludes primary repair. Introduction

Crush injuries to the upper limb are often accompanied by severe vascular, neurological and orthopaedic damage. Among these injuries, those affecting the brachial artery can rapidly progress to acute limb ischaemia if not promptly diagnosed and treated. Early recognition and timely revascularisation are essential for limb salvage.

Various surgical techniques have been described for managing traumatic arterial injuries to the upper limb, including primary repair, end-to-end anastomosis and interposition grafting using either an autologous vein or a synthetic graft. However, extensive arterial damage, contamination and poor local tissue conditions may limit the applicability of these conventional approaches.

Although arterial bypass using autologous vein grafts is a well-established technique for revascularisation of the lower extremities, its application in traumatic arterial injuries of the upper extremity is less commonly reported. In this case report, we present the successful salvage of a limb using a long-segment brachial-to-radial artery bypass with a reversed great saphenous vein graft following a severe crush injury.

Case presentation

A 21-year-old male was admitted to the emergency department with a severe crush injury to the proximal left upper extremity sustained at work. He was referred to the cardiovascular surgery department approximately 2.5 h after the injury. Physical examination revealed pallor and coldness of the left hand and forearm, with absent radial and ulnar pulses distal to the elbow. A marked motor deficit was noted, with muscle strength graded as 1/5, raising suspicion of an associated nerve injury.

Preoperative computed tomography angiography revealed an abrupt cessation of contrast flow in the distal segment of the brachial artery and no opacification of the radial or ulnar arteries below the elbow. This finding is consistent with acute traumatic arterial occlusion (see Fig. 1).

Computed tomography angiography of the left upper extremity. (A) An axial image showing an abrupt occlusion of the distal brachial artery. (B) A coronal reconstruction showing an absence of contrast opacification in the radial and ulnar arteries below the elbow.

The patient was taken urgently to the operating room due to clinical and radiological findings consistent with acute limb ischaemia and crush syndrome. Surgery was performed under general anaesthesia. Exploration through a longitudinal incision along the brachial sulcus revealed extensive traumatic damage extending distally from the distal half of the brachial artery, accompanied by severe soft tissue destruction and haematoma formation. Thromboembolic material was present in both the proximal and distal arterial segments, alongside significant muscle injury.

Given the long-segment arterial defect and severely compromised local tissue conditions, primary arterial repair was deemed unfeasible. Due to extensive arterial damage, a long autologous great saphenous vein graft was harvested from the right lower extremity, reversed, and prepared as a conduit. The length of the saphenous vein graft was tailored according to the extent of the arterial defect, ensuring a tension-free end-to-side anastomosis. Slight redundancy was intentionally maintained to accommodate limb movement and submuscular tunnelling, while avoiding excessive length that could predispose to kinking.

Simultaneously, the orthopaedic team performed internal fixation for a comminuted elbow fracture (see Fig. 3). Following this, thrombectomy was performed using a 3F Fogarty catheter, which was advanced approximately 20 cm proximally from the brachial artery until no more embolic material was retrieved. However, due to extensive arterial wall damage extending to the distal third of the radial artery, distal Fogarty crossing failed. Consequently, the proximal brachial artery segment with preserved endothelial integrity was selected as the entry site. Similar to the technique used in femoropopliteal bypass procedures, the graft was placed within a surgically created tunnel between muscle planes, providing a straight, tension-free course and protecting the conduit while minimising the risk of external compression or kinking. A brachial-to-radial artery bypass was performed using a reversed autologous great saphenous vein graft with end-to-side anastomoses to the brachial and radial arteries (see Fig. 2). The incision was partially extended distally, revealing severe damage to the radial artery along the exposed segment. Further extension was avoided to minimise additional soft tissue trauma and scarring. A more distal intact segment was identified through careful dissection, and the anastomosis was performed at the most proximal viable portion after confirming adequate back-bleeding. Immediately after completing the anastomoses and evacuating the air, distal arterial flow was observed to have been restored and graft patency was confirmed intraoperatively by Doppler ultrasonography and palpable distal pulses. Postoperatively, no additional positioning was required for the vascular reconstruction; however, excessive elbow flexion was avoided, and the limb was elevated to minimise swelling. The skin was left open, while the subcutaneous tissue was approximated with interrupted sutures. The fascia was not completely closed, and sterile dressings were applied to allow controlled decompression.

Postoperatively, the radial pulse remained palpable, and Doppler ultrasound revealed a three-phase flow pattern. The hand was warm, and there were no clinical signs of residual ischaemia. However, early functional recovery of hand movements was limited due to traumatic nerve injury, and the patient was referred to a specialised hand surgery centre.

At 4.5-month follow-up, graft patency was maintained with preserved distal perfusion. Wrist motion was preserved, and finger flexion and extension had largely recovered. Thumb opposition remained partially limited. No further vascular or surgical intervention was required, and functional recovery is ongoing with physiotherapy.

Discussion

Traumatic arterial injuries of the upper extremity account for a relatively small proportion of all peripheral vascular injuries; however, they are associated with substantial morbidity owing to the frequent coexistence of nerve, muscle, and osseous damage [1], [2]. Among upper extremity arterial injuries, the brachial artery has consistently been reported as the most commonly affected segment [1], [3]. Penetrating trauma remains the predominant mechanism. However, although less frequent, blunt and crush injuries are often accompanied by extensive soft tissue destruction, which makes surgical management considerably more complex [2], [3].

Although thromboembolic events are the main cause of acute upper limb ischaemia, traumatic mechanisms, particularly crush injuries, are characterised by long-segment arterial damage, contamination and associated musculoskeletal trauma [2], [3]. These factors often prevent primary arterial repair, requiring alternative revascularisation strategies. The choice of surgical technique is primarily dictated by the extent and nature of the arterial lesion. Primary end-to-end anastomosis is generally preferred for short arterial defects. However, when this approach is not feasible, autologous vein grafts are the preferred option [3], [4]. Previous series have demonstrated that interposition grafting with the great saphenous vein is the most commonly employed technique in upper extremity vascular trauma, offering superior resistance to infection and favourable long-term patency rates [1], [3].

Upper extremity bypass procedures are rarely performed and account for only a small proportion of all extremity revascularisations [5]. Nevertheless, selected reports have documented successful limb salvage and durable graft patency following in situ brachial-to-radial artery bypass [6]. In this case, the extensive destruction of a long segment of the brachial artery made primary repair impossible. However, adequate distal perfusion was achieved through a long-segment brachial-to-radial artery bypass using a reversed autologous great saphenous vein graft, resulting in successful limb salvage.

Although vein grafting is commonly used in upper extremity vascular trauma, most reported cases involve relatively short interposition grafts. In contrast, the present case required a long-segment bypass due to extensive arterial destruction caused by a crush injury involving both the arm and forearm. In addition, the graft traversed a joint region, requiring careful adjustment of graft length and positioning to prevent kinking during limb movement. Furthermore, the use of a submuscular interfascial tunnel provided a protected conduit course within a severely compromised soft tissue environment. These combined features distinguish this case from standard vascular repair techniques and highlight important surgical considerations in complex limb salvage scenarios.

Using end-to-side anastomosis preserved proximal arterial inflow while ensuring sufficient distal revascularisation. This approach is suitable for complex traumatic settings where restoring arterial continuity is not possible [6]. However, even in cases of successful revascularisation, the functional outcomes of upper extremity arterial reconstruction are often determined by the severity of associated nerve injuries [1], [3]. In line with existing literature, the presence of concomitant nerve damage limited functional recovery in this case, despite maintained graft patency and satisfactory distal perfusion.

In addition, ischemia time is a critical determinant of muscle viability. Although upper extremity ischemia is relatively better tolerated than lower limb ischemia due to richer collateral circulation, durations exceeding 6 h may still result in varying degrees of muscle injury [1], [7]. In the present case, the total ischemia time was approximately 7 h; despite this, and the severity of the crush injury, the functional outcome was favourable, with preserved wrist motion and largely recovered finger flexion and extension at 4.5-month follow-up. The only notable limitation was partial impairment of thumb opposition. No secondary surgical procedures were required, and functional recovery is ongoing with physiotherapy.

Although prolonged ischemia is a known risk factor for compartment syndrome, no clinical or intraoperative signs of increased compartment pressure were observed in this case. Therefore, fasciotomy was not performed. To mitigate the risk of postoperative swelling, the fascia was not completely closed, and the subcutaneous tissue was loosely approximated while the skin was left open, allowing controlled decompression. No signs of compartment syndrome developed during follow-up.

In cases of extensive crush injury, routing the vein graft through viable tissue provides a more protected bypass course than interposition grafting. This approach reduces the risk of graft kinking and avoids heavily damaged soft tissue planes, which is particularly beneficial in contaminated or crushed wounds. Furthermore, using a long saphenous vein graft, meticulously tunnelling through uninjured tissue planes, and performing tension-free end-to-side anastomoses probably helped achieve early graft patency and limb salvage in this complex traumatic setting.

The optimal sequence for vascular reconstruction and orthopaedic stabilisation in cases of traumatic extremity injury remains a matter of debate. In cases of prolonged ischaemia, the priority should be revascularisation, and temporary intravascular shunting may be considered to minimise ischaemic time [7], [8]. In this case, the simultaneous orthopaedic fixation performed during vein graft harvesting contributed to minimising the duration of ischaemia without delaying definitive vascular repair.

Traumatic arterial injuries to the upper limb present a significant surgical challenge due to their frequent association with complex damage to soft tissue, the nervous system, and the musculoskeletal system [1], [2]. Prompt revascularisation is essential to prevent irreversible ischaemic injury and limb loss [1], [7]. However, restoring arterial perfusion alone may not guarantee favourable functional outcomes in cases of severe nerve injury [1], [3]. These cases highlight the importance of a multidisciplinary approach involving cardiovascular surgeons, orthopaedists, hand surgeons, and rehabilitation specialists to optimise both limb salvage and functional recovery.

Conclusion

A brachial-to-radial artery bypass using an autologous saphenous vein graft can be an effective way of saving the limb in selected patients with crush-related upper-extremity arterial injuries when conventional repair techniques are not possible. While early revascularisation restores distal perfusion and helps prevent ischaemic complications, the extent of functional recovery depends on the severity of any associated neurological injury and the success of multidisciplinary postoperative management.

Clinical message/learning points

• Severe crush injuries to the upper limb may preclude primary arterial repair due to extensive tissue destruction and loss of a long segment of the vessel.

• Brachial-to-radial artery bypass using an autologous great saphenous vein graft is a reliable option for revascularisation when distal targets are required.

• Careful selection of the distal anastomosis site can help to restore hand perfusion while minimising further trauma to compromised tissues.

Creating a muscle-plane tunnel for long saphenous vein grafts, analogous to femoropopliteal bypass techniques, may reduce the risk of kinking or external compression in upper extremity trauma.

• Early confirmation of graft patency using Doppler ultrasonography and clinical examination is essential in the postoperative period.

• Successful limb salvage in such complex injuries depends on close multidisciplinary collaboration, particularly when there are also nerve or orthopaedic injuries.
