Endophytic entomopathogenic fungi: The next frontier in mycological biocontrol
Authors: Karan S. Patil, Ganesh B. Gathalkar, Ejaj K. Pathan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Entomopathogenic fungi (EPF) are eco-friendly alternatives to chemical pesticides. However, high costs, the instability of the formulations, sensitivity to environmental factors and variability in virulence limit adoption by farmers. We argue that these problems can be overcome by using EPF strains established as endophytes. The ability of endophytic entomopathogenic fungi (EEPF) to modulate the release of volatiles to attract the predators of insect pests positions them as dual-purpose biocontrol agents in agriculture. As endophytes, these fungi are persistent, lowering the costs for pest control. Endophytic entomopathogenic fungi can also promote plant growth and improve tolerance to abiotic stress. Research on the molecular mechanisms underlying plant tissue colonization by EEPF, their persistence in plants and virulence towards insects suggests that EEPF acquire virulence factors and metabolic versatility through horizontal gene transfer from plants. Therefore, establishing and maintaining EPF as endophytes within plants may compensate for the loss of virulence associated with repeated in vitro subculturing of EPF on artificial media. However, despite these potential advantages of EEPF, challenges still remain, such as variability in endophytic colonization under field conditions, host specificity, ecological risks, and scalability. This review critically evaluates these limitations, focusing on well-studied genera, such as Metarhizium, Beauveria, and Lecanicillium, and outlines future directions for improving the reliability of the application of EEPF. Introduction

Over the past two decades, entomopathogenic fungi (EPF) have emerged as biological control agents in agriculture (Faria and Wraight 2007; Jaronski and Mascarin 2017; Jaronski 2023). These fungi infect over 18 insect orders at all stages of development (Araújo and Hughes 2016). About 1,791 species of EPF across 90 to 100 genera have been reported (Mora et al. 2017; De Wint et al. 2024). They are distributed within Ascomycota, Basidiomycota, Blastocladiomycota, Chytridiomycota, Entomophthoromycotina, Microsporidia, Mucoromycota, and Oomycota (Hajek and St. Leger 1994; Boomsma et al. 2014). However, only 250 species have been developed and commercialized (St. Leger and Wang 2010; Mora et al. 2017; Couceiro et al. 2026).

This expansion of the market for entomopathogens is partly because they are considered environmentally safer than chemical insecticides (Aravinthraju et al. 2024), and partly due to advances in technologies for the mass-production of EPF spores (Ravensberg 2015; Mora et al. 2017; Mascarin et al. 2024).

In agriculture, EPF products play an important role in controlling insect pest populations. Insects comprise approximately 67% of known global fauna, with over one million described species within the Insecta (Stork 2018). Annual crop losses due to insect pests are estimated at 20% globally (Mateos Fernández et al. 2022). However, the field performance of EPF remains inconsistent. Virulence against insect pests varies with the strain of the specific EPF applied. Moreover, most EPF formulations are externally applied on crops. They are thus vulnerable to environmental variables such as humidity and UV-rays, and have limited persistence. To overcome these limitations, various strategies have been adopted. Advanced delivery systems, such as oil-based dispersions, enhance conidial attachment to the hydrophobic insect cuticle and provide protective lipids that reduce desiccation under low-humidity conditions (Lei et al. 2023). However, their application is often limited by potential phytotoxicity at high concentrations (Couceiro et al. 2026).

Microencapsulation, using biopolymers such as lignin and chitosan, effectively shields fungal propagules from UV-B radiation (Felizatti et al. 2021; Friuli et al. 2023). But the encapsulation process often results in reduced conidial viability and leads to delayed germination when in contact with the host (Braga et al. 2022). Moreover, for farmers, the processing and packaging of EPF spores increase costs. These factors limit the wide acceptance and long-term success of EPF (Bamisile et al. 2021; Couceiro et al. 2026).

In contrast, endophytic entomopathogenic fungi (EEPF) are symbiotic fungi that colonize plant tissues without causing disease symptoms while retaining the ability to infect and kill a wide range of insect pests (Mantzoukas and Eliopoulos 2020). Unlike plant pathogenic or saprophytic fungi, EEPF represent a specialized group of fungi with dual action: endophytic colonization in plants and necrotrophic parasitism on insects (Abdulle et al. 2021). When EPF systemically colonize plant tissues to act as endophytes, they are able to provide persistent protection from pests (Mateos Fernández et al. 2022). Once established as endophytes, EPF reduce dependence on externally applied chemical pesticides. As discussed in detail later in this review, they also augment plant resistance against biotic and abiotic stressors (Bamisile et al. 2021; Hu and Bidochka 2021; Aravinthraju et al. 2024).

Thus, EEPF have been proposed as a promising alternative to conventional chemical pesticides and even to periodically externally applied EPF. However, despite increasing interest in the application of EEPF in agriculture, critical gaps remain, including our limited understanding of the mechanisms governing stable endophytic colonization under field conditions, variability in host specificity, potential ecological risks associated with secondary metabolites, and challenges in large-scale application. Moreover, the relationship between fungal genomic traits and functional outcomes, such as virulence and endophytism, remains poorly resolved.

This review examines the distinctions between EEPF and conventional EPF. We synthesize current knowledge on their ecology, colonization strategies, and insect pathogenicity, with particular emphasis on well-studied genera such as Metarhizium, Beauveria, and Lecanicillium. Unlike previous reviews, we integrate ecological, physiological, and emerging molecular insights to identify key constraints, unresolved questions, and research priorities for the successful development and commercialization of EEPF-based pest management strategies.

Entomopathogenic fungi

Among EPF, Metarhizium, Beauveria, and Lecanicillium represent genera that have been extensively studied. The commercial use of these three as biocontrol agents has been reviewed (Irsad et al. 2023). All three are classified under the phylum Ascomycota. The following subsections highlight key similarities and differences among these three genera.

Metarhizium species

Metarhizium is cosmopolitan in its distribution (Mesquita et al. 2023) and targets a wide range of pest species across multiple orders such as Coleoptera, Lepidoptera, Diptera, Hemiptera and Orthoptera (Tupe et al. 2017; Kim et al. 2020).

Species such as Metarhizium anisopliae and Metarhizium robertsii are used in biocontrol programs worldwide (Shang et al. 2024). Field studies indicate that Metarhizium species can persist in the soil and rhizosphere for extended periods, from a few months to over a year, offering long-term pest control (Milner et al. 2003). For example, Metarhizium anisopliae showed persistence in the soil of a sugarcane field for up to 3.5 years. Throughout the study period, a small proportion of spores continued to be viable across all sites and formulations tested (Milner et al. 2003).

Metarhizium acridum represents a specialist lineage, with pathogenicity restricted primarily to Orthoptera. Metarhizium rileyi is used to control Noctuidae, major agricultural crop pests (Fronza et al. 2017). This ecological divergence reflects differences in host adaptation strategies and infection mechanisms (Hu et al. 2014; Stone and Bidochka 2020; Iwanicki et al. 2022).

Studies demonstrate that the Metarhizium species can establish endophytic colonization in diverse plants, such as tomato, maize, sorghum, soybean, potato, switchgrass, haricot beans and cassava (Sasan & Bidochka 2012; Greenfield et al. 2016; Liu et al. 2017; Ahmad et al. 2022), suggesting a potential link between broad plant host adaptability and endophytic competence.

Differences in plant host and insect host specialization as well as entomopathogenic virulence are associated with genomic features. Generalist species typically exhibit expanded gene families encoding proteases, chitinases, and gene clusters for secondary metabolite biosynthesis to facilitate the infection of diverse hosts (Hu et al. 2014; Stone and Bidochka 2020). Generalist entomopathogenic fungi, such as M. anisopliae, Metarhizium humberi, and B. bassiana strains, with wide host ranges, show expanded gene families for plant cell wall degradation (e.g., CAZymes) and insect cuticle penetration (e.g., subtilisin-like Pr1 proteases).

On the other hand, specialist strains, such as M. rileyi or M. acridum, have narrow-range of hosts and contracted genomes with fewer genes, limiting the choice of host to single insect orders, but often with higher infection efficiency within their target groups (Sánchez-Pérez et al. 2014; Staats et al. 2014; Gao et al. 2020). Such genomic attributes are closely linked to functional traits including virulence, environmental persistence, and the ability to establish endophytic associations (Hu et al. 2014; Stone and Bidochka 2020).

The genome size of the Metarhizium species examined so far typically ranges from 37 to 42 Mbp, encoding approximately 10,000 to 11,000 genes. The genomes are enriched with gene clusters responsible for secondary metabolite production and host adaptation (Iwanicki et al. 2022). For instance, the genome of the M. anisopliae E6 strain is approximately 38.5 Mbp, a size comparable to that of the host-generalist M. robertsii but significantly larger than the genomes of more specialized EPF such as Cordyceps militaris, whose genome size is about 32.2 Mbp. Variations in genome size in EPF represent a complex set of genes and gene clusters that facilitate ecological versatility, allowing the fungus to switch between saprophytic, entomopathogenic, and endophytic lifestyles. Specifically, the genome contains a unique reservoir of secreted proteins such as proteases and chitinases, and shows gene family expansions in serine and aspartic endopeptidases, essential for host cuticle degradation and environmental adaptation. These genomic features allow EPF to effectively switch their metabolic pathways to absorb various nutrients, whether surviving as plant endophytes or playing the role of insect pathogens (Staats et al. 2014).

The guanine and cytosine (GC) content in the genome of the M. anisopliae E6 strain is 51%, at par with other EPF such as M. acridum and B. bassiana. This uniformity in GC content across the lineage suggests conserved genomic architecture, even when these species evolved to infect distinct hosts (Staats et al. 2014; Pathan and Deshpande 2019) perhaps as a part of niche partitioning of hosts as seen in other kingdoms and ecosystems.

Beauveria species

The genus Beauveria has a wide range of insect hosts, including Coleoptera, Diptera, Hemiptera, Hymenoptera, Lepidoptera, and Orthoptera. Beauveria has been isolated from soils, insects, and plant tissues from different parts of the world (Kobmoo et al. 2021).

Field and greenhouse trials demonstrate the high efficacy of B. bassiana, the most studied and commercially exploited species, against whitefly species such as Bemisia tabaci and Trialeurodes vaporariorum Westwood (Sain et al. 2024). A study found that native isolates eliminated 66% of whitefly eggs and 65% of nymphs per cm2 on tomato leaflets, while the chemical insecticide, fenoxycarb (Syngenta Crop Protection, Monthey AG, Monthey, Switzerland), reduced whitefly eggs by 32% and nymph populations by 55% (Barra-Bucarei et al. 2020). Besides the whitefly species, B. bassiana has demonstrated efficacy against insects such as Ostrinia nubilalis, Pissodes castaneus, and Rhynchophorus ferrugineus (Gu et al. 2023).

The phylogenetic analysis of the most studied species in this genus, B. bassiana, showed ten evolutionary lineages among 102 isolates (Sain et al. 2024). The virulence of these strains varied greatly among whitefly hosts, but lacked tight host or geographic specificity, demonstrating substantial allelic variation (Sain et al. 2024). The endophytic existence of B. bassiana was reported in around 25 different plant species (Samal et al. 2023).

The Beauveria genome, ranging from approximately 33 to 37 Mbp, contains more than 10,000 predicted genes. Population genomics reveal marked intraspecific genetic structures with implications for adaptation and virulence (Lee et al. 2018).

Lecanicillium species

Lecanicillium, formerly known as Verticillium, is another leading endophytic entomopathogenic fungal genus. Based on phylogenetic investigation, several species of Lecanicillium have recently been reclassified as belonging to the genus Akanthomyces (Zhou et al. 2022). Lecanicillium is commonly present in insect environments worldwide. It is also reported to colonize crop plants (Gurulingappa et al. 2011).

Lecanicillium lecanii (Zimmerman) Viégas was reported as a potent strain for the control of insect pests and pathogens. In a field trial, biocapsules containing Lecanicillium saksenae and L. lecanii reduced aphid populations on cowpea by more than 90%. L. lecanii was also found to be effective against Aphis gossypii (Parvathi 2024).

Phylogenetic and morphological studies reveal eight distinct clades in Lecanicillium, indicating a clear and independent evolutionary history. The diversity seen in Lecanicillium lineages is due to long-term coevolution with insects and adaptation to different environments, rather than the result of a major geological crisis such as the Cretaceous-Tertiary extinction (Zhou et al. 2022). The genome size of the Lecanicillium sp. ranges from 31 to 36 Mbp with the largest genome comprising 11,009 predicted protein coding genes and 197 scaffolds (Harm et al. 2018).

Pre-contact stage

Entomopathogenic fungi produce volatile organic compounds (VOCs) that induce behavioural or physiological responses in potential hosts (Baverstock et al. 2009).

Cuticle contact and penetration

Most Ascomycete EPF, including Metarhizium and Beauveria species, infect insects via attachment. For attachment, surface proteins on fungal spores recognize compounds specific to insects. In M. robertsii (Bisch., Rehner & Humber), the adhesin, MAD1, plays a central role in the adhesion of the fungi to the cuticle (Wang and St. Leger 2007). In Beauveria, hydrophobins serve the same function.

After the fungal spores attach themselves to the host exoskeleton, they germinate, and release enzymes to break down the insect cuticle. Endophytic genera, such as Beauveria and Metarhizium species, produce chitinases and proteases (Hendra et al. 2026). These enzymes degrade and soften the insect cuticle.

The hyphal penetration of the insect’s chitinous exoskeleton may involve direct hyphal penetration or cuticle-penetrating appressoria, specialized structures for exerting mechanical pressure (Ortiz-Urquiza and Keyhani 2013; Chethana et al. 2021). During this stage, fungal components may stimulate the production of antimicrobial substances in the cuticle or elicit behavioral defenses such as grooming and thermoregulation (Roy et al. 2006).

Hemocoel colonization

After the fungi penetrate the cuticle, they come into contact with the insect hemolymph. In response to this fungal invasion, host cellular and humoral immune responses are activated. The fungi evade these defenses by modifying their own cell wall components to become blastospores. The blastospores produce metabolites that manipulate host behaviour, helping establish infection (Qu and Wang 2018). The fungi thus grow within the insect hemolymph as immune-evading hyphal bodies or in vivo blastospores (Wang et al. 2023). Not all EPF, however, follow this pattern. Some entomopathogens produce protoplasts, in vivo, or hyphae during host colonization (Elya and De Fine Licht 2021; Sacco and Hajek 2023).

The blastospores absorb nutrients in the host hemocoel and produce metabolites such as destruxins and lethal toxins such as beauvericin. These metabolites are insecticidal (Branine et al. 2019; Fite et al. 2023; Hendra et al. 2026).

Postmortem growth

Endophytic entomopathogenic fungi emerge from the dead host and sporulate on the surface of the cadaver. This stage completes the fungal life cycle and initiates new infection cycles. Figure 1 summarizes these EEPF-insect interactions.

The interaction between insects and EPF has coevolved over hundreds of millions of years. Fungi and insects are engaged in a long-standing evolutionary arms race, with insect pathogenicity arising multiple times, resulting in the development of conserved innate immune responses and behavioural defense mechanisms (Zhang et al. 2024). The degree of pathogenesis is influenced by the insect’s immune system. Insect immune responses, including cuticular defenses, cellular immunity, humoral pathways, and antimicrobial peptide production, play a critical role in determining the vigour of EPF infection. Endophytic entomopathogenic fungi and plant host interactions

In this section, we examine the interactions of EEPF with host plants to establish themselves as endophytes.

Molecular mechanisms of plant colonization

To colonize the host plant, EEPF release conidia or asexual spores that easily attach themselves to the host. Wang and St. Leger (2007) reported the role of the Metarhizium adhesin-like protein 2 (MAD 2) in the adhesion of M. anisopliae spores to plant surfaces. Interestingly, the expression of MAD2 was induced only in the plant microenvironment. The MAD2 knock-out mutant failed to adhere to the plant surface, highlighting the role of MAD2 in facilitating endophytic colonization by M. anisopliae.

When M. anisopliae spores are sprayed on leaves, hyphae emerge and enter the leaf apoplast. They can then spread to other parts of the plant, occasionally entering the xylem (Gómez-Vidal et al. 2006). Plants perceive microbial presence through pattern-recognition receptors that detect conserved microbial-associated molecular patterns. Endophytic colonization depends on the ability of EEPF to circumvent plant innate immune responses while establishing a mutually beneficial relationship. To persist within plant tissues, EEPF employ multiple evasion strategies, including the modification of chitin oligomers to limit the activation of chitin-specific receptors, the secretion of effector proteins that attenuate plant defense signalling, and the production of secondary metabolites that interfere with hormone-mediated defenses, particularly those regulated by jasmonic acid and salicylic acid pathways. To become an endophyte, the fungus selectively evades plant immune responses through molecular signalling and starts nutrient uptake (Hu and Bidochka 2021).

The plant-fungus association represents complex evolutionary events, ranging from epiphytic colonization to stable endophytic lifestyles (Branine et al. 2019). Genomic advances have begun to elucidate the molecular basis of this dual lifestyle, highlighting how certain fungi can simultaneously function as insect pathogens and plant mutualists (Samal et al. 2023).

Though direct evidence for long-term co-evolution between EPF and plant hosts remains limited (Elya and De Fine Licht 2021), there is evidence suggesting that mutual symbiotic relationships between plants and EEPF have evolved over more than 100 million years (Wang et al. 2016). Vega et al. (2009) demonstrated that entomopathogens can persist as endophytes and are more closely associated with plants than previously assumed. Considering the long-term interactions with plant hosts, it is not surprising that EEPF strains harbour additional plant-derived genes, supporting this view, a genome-wide analysis of seven Metarhizium strains revealed signatures of horizontal gene transfer (HGT), suggesting the acquisition of genes from bacteria, archaea, arthropods, plants, and even vertebrates (Hu et al. 2014). This point is discussed in more detail in the section, Mechanisms of improved virulence in EEPF.

Multifunctional roles of EEPF in plants

After establishing themselves within plant tissues, EEPF can confer systemic protection to plants against a wide range of insect pests, including chewing and sap-sucking species (Jaber and Ownley 2018). The nitrogen derived from the insects killed by EEPF is available to the host plant. Thus, fungi facilitate the provision of nutrients such as nitrogen in return for plant-derived carbohydrates. In some cases, EEPF provide protection against other fungal pathogens (Pathan and Deshpande 2019). Moreover, EEPF synthesize phytohormones such as auxins, gibberellins, and cytokinins and contribute to plant growth (Behie and Bidochka 2014). Thus, the relationship between EEPF and plant host is mutually beneficial.

Endophytic colonization primes plant immune systems by reprogramming the secondary metabolism and activating defense-related pathways (Shrivastava et al. 2015). This priming reduces host plant suitability for insect herbivores through enhanced repellence and toxicity, driven by elevated levels of phenolics, terpenes, flavonoids, alkaloids, and VOCs (Zaynab et al. 2018). Entomopathogenic endophytic fungi modulate defense-associated hormone signalling, reinforcing repellency to insect herbivores, and promoting plant growth (Zhang et al. 2024).

In a detailed study, González-Mas et al. (2021) examined how endophytic colonization by B. bassiana impacts the profile of VOCs in melon (Cucumis melo L.) and cotton (Gossypium hirsutum L.). They investigated VOC emissions from undamaged plants as well as from plants subjected to herbivory by sap-sucking aphids (Aphis gossypii Glover) (Hemiptera: Aphididae), and leaf-chewing caterpillars, specifically Spodoptera littoralis (Boisduval) and Spodoptera frugiperda (Smith) (Lepidoptera: Noctuidae). Across all feeding scenarios, compared with non-colonized controls, B. bassiana-colonized plants emitted a distinctly different blend of VOCs. Melon plants colonized by different strains of B. bassiana or Metarhizium brunneum released distinct VOC blends after aphid attacks, highlighting the importance of fungal strain identity and VOC responses that shape plant-fungus interactions (González-Mas et al. 2019b).

B. bassiana and M. brunneum altered VOC emissions in the presence as well as in the absence of the aphid, Aphis gossypii Glover, on melon (Cucumis melo L. var. Galia) plants (González-Mas et al. 2019a, b). These alterations likely arise from defense pathways activated by endophytic colonization (Rivas-Franco et al. 2020), often accompanied by increases in signalling molecules such as cis-jasmone and methyl jasmonate (Brunissen et al. 2010; Sobhy et al. 2017).

Volatile organic compounds are also emitted in response to pathogen infection (Ton et al. 2007; Sobhy et al. 2017). However, only a few studies have characterized the specific volatiles altered directly by the fungus or induced downstream by colonization (González-Mas et al. 2019b; Fingu-Mabola et al. 2020).

Some endophytic fungi release bioactive volatiles that can influence plant growth and defense regulation (Strobel et al. 2011; Li et al. 2014). Beauveria colonization can influence VOC blends by generating signals that attract the natural predators of the insect pests.

Entomopathogenic endophytic fungi are also actively involved in the biosynthesis and modulation of plant VOCs (Pineda et al. 2010; Kanchiswamy et al. 2015) and are known to modulate herbivore-induced VOC emissions, often resulting in enhanced attraction to parasitoids (Rostás and Turlings 2008; Sobhy et al. 2018). Volatile organic compounds play central ecological roles in attracting the natural enemies of herbivores, signalling herbivore attack, and coordinating defense responses within the plant, (Dicke and Baldwin 2010; Heil 2014). Endophyte colonization thus impacts herbivores directly and, indirectly, by attracting their natural enemies through plant-mediated mechanisms (Sword et al. 2017; Jaber and Araj 2018; González-Mas et al. 2019a, b). Thus, by reshaping VOC profiles, EEPF can influence insect population dynamics across multiple trophic levels (Gange et al. 2012).

Colonization by EEPF activates plant defense signaling pathways, including systemic acquired resistance and induced systemic resistance, involving the upregulation of genes associated with the production of defensive compounds such as phytoalexins, pathogenesis-related proteins, and antimicrobial secondary metabolites (Hendra et al. 2026). Thus, several EEPF taxa also suppress plant diseases (Jaber and Ownley 2018; Vega 2018; Dara 2019). Beauveria bassiana, for example, reportedly suppresses Zucchini yellow mosaic virus colonization in squash plants (Fingu-Mabola 2021; Sui et al. 2023). Since EEPF inhabit various internal plant tissues, including leaves, stems, roots, seeds, and fruits, they induce systemic resistance, enhancing the plant’s defense mechanisms against pests and pathogens (Panwar and Szczepaniec 2024).

Besides improving the immune system of plants as well as their defense mechanisms against various pathogens, EEPF synthesize secondary metabolites, such as phenols, alkaloids and peptides, which promote growth (Jha et al. 2023). For example, B. bassiana, and M. anisopliae promote nitrogen fixation (Behie and Bidochka 2014). In C3 plants that use the Calvin cycle for sugar synthesis, the respiratory metabolism of fungal endophytes in intracellular spaces is reported to boost leaf photosynthesis by increasing internal CO2 concentration (Suryanarayanan et al. 2022).

Phytohormones produced by EEPF not only promote plant growth but also help plants survive in contaminated soils (Daba et al. 2019). Studies demonstrate the effectiveness of EEPF colonization for remediating soil contaminants such as hydrocarbons, heavy metals, and industrial pollutants (He et al. 2020; Sharma and Kumar 2021). Endophytic entomopathogenic fungi such as Acremonium sp. and Mucor sp. help remediate and detoxify soil (He et al. 2020; Sharma et al. 2023).

The fungi boost crop growth, inducing shoot and root development, and providing crops with resistance against abiotic stressors such as salinity, drought, metals, and temperature (Singh et al. 2021).

Thus, EEPF play multiple roles: the induction of plant resistance against pathogens through induced systemic resistance, pathogenicity against insect pests, attracting natural predators of insect pests and the stimulation of plant growth through various physiological mechanisms (Hendra et al. 2026).

Implications of EEPF for insect pest control in agriculture

EEPF offer advantages over EPF, making them superior for sustainable long-term pest management (Mantzoukas and Eliopoulos 2020). Unlike chemical insecticides, EEPF do not leave harmful residues in the environment, minimizing contamination risks to soil and water (Gutiérrez et al. 2018). Unlike foliar treatment with other pest control agents, which are likely to be affected by environmental conditions such as heat, and humidity, necessitating multiple applications, EEPF colonize plant tissues naturally, providing long-term protection against insect pests. Thus, EEPF can be integrated into pest management to reduce the use of chemicals and to conserve beneficial/essential soil microbiota and arthropods (Mantzoukas and Eliopoulos 2020). The use of EEPF can be cost-effective in agriculture, simultaneously contributing to biodiversity enhancement and ecosystem stability (Mantzoukas and Eliopoulos 2020).

B. bassiana, for example, is effective in suppressing or controlling stem borers in coffee and sorghum by persisting in plant tissues for months, providing long-term protection (Panwar and Szczepaniec 2024). When deployed as an endophyte, B. bassiana demonstrated enhanced efficiency compared to conventional applications against pests such as the South American tomato pinworm, Tuta absoluta (Silva et al. 2020). Among the EEPF studied so far, B. bassiana has the widest host plant range (25 plant species), endophytically colonizing plants systemically, providing sustained resistance against insect pests and fungal pathogens (Samal et al. 2023).

Quantitative evidence demonstrates the effectiveness of EEPF in agricultural systems. The endophytic colonization of poppy by B. bassiana reduced feeding by the wasp, Iraella luteipes, by nearly 70%. In bananas, the endophytic establishment of B. bassiana led to nearly 60% mortality of banana root borer adults, reducing plant injury by up to 63% (Panwar and Szczepaniec 2024). The mortality rates of the cotton aphid were significantly increased in cotton treated with endophytic strains of Purpureocillium lilacinum and B. bassiana (Panwar and Szczepaniec 2024). Under greenhouse conditions, the mortality of spittlebug nymphs and adults feeding on plants inoculated with Fusarium multiceps and M. anisopliae reached up to 100%. More than 53% of nymphs and 59% of adults had fungi in their cadavers. In field conditions, more than 45% of insect cadavers contained the fungi (Silva et al. 2020).

When treated with B. bassiana, the survival of the coffee berry borer, Hypothenemus hampei (Ferrari) was reduced in tomato plants (Posada et al. 2007). Similarly, the colonization of tomato plants by B. bassiana decreased damage caused by Helicoverpa armigera (Qayyum et al. 2015). A mortality of approximately 50% along with reduced larval longevity of Tuta absoluta (Meyrick) on tomato leaves was also recorded (Klieber and Reineke 2016). Endophytic strains from maize are as effective as commercial entomopathogenic strains for the control of S. frugiperda (Aravinthraju et al. 2024). Field tests using EEPF to combat bovine ticks showed an increase in mortality of 32% compared to controls (Panwar and Szczepaniec 2024).

Endophytic colonization by B. bassiana and Akanthomyces muscarius in sweet pepper altered the life cycle and behavioral response of aphids, while maize seeds coated with the microsclerotia of Metarhizium species induced growth traits and reduced the survival of S. frugiperda (Panwar and Szczepaniec 2024). Conventional fungi lack such multitrophic interactions (González-Mas et al. 2021; Ahsan et al. 2024).

The ability to control a wide range of insect pests makes EEPF versatile biocontrol agents. When inhabiting plants, these fungi alter secondary metabolites and VOCs, and enhance plant resistance to pests and diseases, providing long-lasting protection from insect pests (Aravinthraju et al. 2024). They act either through direct infection or indirect plant-mediated mechanisms including repelling herbivores and attracting predators of pests as discussed in the earlier section. Conventional EPF lack such multitrophic interactions (González-Mas et al. 2021). Thus, EEPF serve as useful components in integrated pest management (IPM) strategies.

Compared to conventional EPF, EEPF spores are reported to exhibit rapid attachment to insect hosts due to plant-mediated enhancement in hydrophobins and adhesins, facilitating stronger adhesion (Tupe et al. 2017). As EEPF, M. anisopliae, for example, showed greater enzyme indices than seen in conventional EPF M. anisopliae strains (Kulkarni et al. 2008). When M. anisopliae spores are sprayed on crops infested with insect pests, enzymes such as chitinase and protease degrade the insect cuticle. Unlike EPF strains that rely solely on self-produced toxins, EEPF use additional plant-derived factors (Pathan and Deshpande 2019). This dual action of EEPF induces significantly higher mortality rates in insect pests than produced by EPF.

Entomopathogenic endophytic fungi were found to be more effective in key fungus-insect pathogenesis processes such as spore adhesion, germination, the enzymatic degradation of the insect cuticle, and the production of virulent metabolites than non-endophytic EPF. These improved virulent traits enable EEPF to achieve higher and more rapid insect mortality at relatively lower spore concentrations than non-endophytic counterparts (Fig. 3). The greater efficiency observed with EEPF may be attributed to their dual role as entomopathogens as well as endophytes (Silva et al. 2020). Given the effectiveness of EEPF in protecting plants from insect pests and invigorating plant growth and productivity, there have been attempts to isolate EEPF for mass production. In the following sections, we provide a brief summary of research on the isolation, maintenance and evaluation of EPF as well as EEPF for their insecticidal properties.

Isolation and maintenance of EEPF strains

Soil has been the major source for isolating EPF, particularly those belonging to the genera Beauveria and Metarhizium (Correa et al. 2022). Therefore, they were considered soil fungi. Soils that are undisturbed, from sparsely populated areas, such as mountains and forests, are preferable to minimize contamination from commercial strains. Moreover, in forest soils, the diversity of EPF is significantly higher than in agricultural environments (Wakil et al. 2013). Studies conducted on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau and the Gansu Corridor, China showed that there was a greater likelihood of isolating novel EPF strains in remote, relatively undisturbed areas than in highly disturbed regions (Orgiazzi et al. 2015; Dong et al. 2016). Therefore, minimally disturbed ecosystems are better for isolating effective, ecologically-adapted EPF strains.

During isolation, soil type, pH, field type, elevation, GPS coordinates, and annual climatic data are recorded for the precise documentation of each site. Soil is collected from a depth of 5–10 cm. Samples should be kept at room temperature if processed within 3 h. If there is a delay, the soil must be stored in dark condition at room temperature or refrigerated at 4 °C for up to a week (Liu et al. 2021).

When soil samples are collected from different fields, soil dilution is used to isolate strains (Kulkarni et al. 2008; Tupe et al. 2017). The suspension is spread on selective media plates containing peptone, glucose, streptomycin, tetracycline, cycloheximide, and dodine at pH 7.0. The plates are sealed and incubated at 28 °C. To ensure purity and avoid cross-contamination, the plates are monitored for fungal growth over two weeks and emerging fungal growth is excised and transferred to fresh media for an additional 7–10 days.

Colony and spore morphology are analysed under a microscope to identify the fungi. Pure cultures are then established by spreading spore suspensions on selective media to isolate individual colonies. Pure strains are maintained on potato dextrose agar slants (Pathan and Deshpande 2019).

The successive subculturing of fungi results in a considerable loss of virulence through strain degeneration, reduced enzymatic activity and phenotypic alterations (Chang et al. 2020; Zhao et al. 2022). A possible solution to this loss of virulence due to successive subculturing is re-isolation from the colonized tissues of insects or plants that naturally deter insects. This approach helps maintain the essential traits of the fungi, their viability and virulence.

Although traditionally regarded as soil-inhabiting organisms associated mainly with insect cadavers, some EPF also interact closely with plants. Such EPF can reside on the phylloplane, persist in the rhizosphere, and colonize internal tissues as true endophytes (Vega et al. 2008; Quesada Moraga 2020). When leaves fall on soil and degenerate, EEPF can survive as saprophytes and spores (Pathan and Deshpande 2019). EPF in the rhizosphere of plants, such as Azadirachta indica A. Juss., Annona reticulata L., Brassica campestris (B. rapa L), and Anacardium occidentale L., showed greater virulence (Dhale 2013).

Entomopathogenic endophytic fungi isolated from the rhizosphere of plants that are not attacked by chewing insects were more than twice as virulent as EPF isolated from soil. Such EEPF were effective even with one-tenth the number of spores (Kulkarni et al. 2008; Tupe et al. 2017; Pathan and Deshpande 2019; Pathan et al. 2021). The higher virulence was explained by the horizontal gene transfer of insecticidal traits from the plants to the fungi during the endophytic stage as described in the section, Mechanisms of improved virulence of EEPF.

It is not easy to distinguish between EPF and EEPF collected from the rhizosphere of plants. Therefore, we suggest rigorous testing of the virulence and other characteristics of EPF collected from soils and their ability to establish themselves as endophytes.

Evaluating insecticidal properties

The following subsections summarize commonly employed assays for assessing the insecticidal properties of EEPF and their suitability as biopesticides.

Direct methods for evaluating the virulence

Insect bioassays represent the most direct and widely used approach for assessing the insecticidal virulence of EEPF under controlled laboratory conditions. In such assays, insects are typically exposed to standardized concentrations of EEPF spores following established immersion or contact-based protocols, with appropriate untreated controls included for comparison (Vega et al. 2008; Butt et al. 2016). Virulence is subsequently assessed based on mortality rates, lethal time estimates, and confirmation of mycosis through post-mortem observation of fungal outgrowth.

Using standard insect bioassay protocols, B. bassiana and Clonostachys rosea were evaluated against the coffee pod borer, Hypothenemus hampei. The assays demonstrated that B. bassiana exhibited higher virulence, inducing rapid and complete mortality, than Clonostachys rosea (Link, Schroers). C. rosea showed comparatively lower virulence, achieving delayed and reduced mortality (Vega et al. 2008).

Standardized mortality correction and replication procedures must be applied to ensure robust assessment of fungal virulence. Building on this framework, subsequent studies have largely adopted similar standardized insect bioassay principles, incorporating only minor methodological modifications (Tupe et al. 2017; Pathan and Deshpande 2019). Such assays provide a foundational framework for comparing fungal virulence across isolates, hosts, and experimental conditions.

Egg-stage bioassays are widely used to evaluate the virulence of EEPF, with freshly laid eggs serving as the target life stage and hatching success or subsequent larval survival as key response variables. The effect of exposure to fungal spores on the early developmental stages of insect pests, such as the fall armyworm, S. frugiperda, has been reported. Post-exposure assessments typically extend to the larval stage, allowing the evaluation of delayed or trans-stadial effects on survival and development. Virulence is assessed through cumulative mortality and mycosis is confirmed via a characteristic fungal outgrowth on cadavers (Idrees et al. 2021).

Bioassays targeting second-instar larvae have also been employed based on comparable standardized exposure principles (Ruiz-Nájera et al. 2013; Idrees et al. 2022). These larval bioassays typically involve exposure to defined spore concentrations under controlled conditions, with mortality and fungal infection used as primary indicators of pathogenicity.

Field-level validation remains a critical bottleneck in the widespread adoption of EPF. In this context, Deka et al. (2021) demonstrated the successful translation of laboratory-identified virulence into field efficacy using a formulated B. bassiana isolate (BKN20) against the tea mosquito bug, Helopeltis theivora. The aqueous spore formulation significantly reduced shoot damage under micro-plot and multi-location field trials and performed comparably, or better than, the synthetic insecticide, thiamethoxam. The formulation exhibited no phytotoxic effects and was non-pathogenic to non-target natural enemies, underscoring its suitability for integration into pest management programs.

Such studies highlight the importance of formulation development and field validation in bridging the gap between laboratory virulence and practical biocontrol application.

Complementary methods for evaluating entomopathogenic virulence

While classical insect bioassays and whole plant (in planta) evaluations remain indispensable for determining EEPF virulence, they are often labour-intensive, host- and stage-specific, and limited in their ability to explain isolate-level variability or indirect, plant-mediated effects. Consequently, several complementary approaches have been developed that provide indirect and supportive indicators of virulence, enhancing the mechanistic and holistic interpretation of EEPF performance when used alongside conventional laboratory bioassays (Vega 2018; Sain et al. 2019).

Fungal fitness traits and lethal time metrics

Virulence differentiation among EEPF isolates can be substantially refined by incorporating fungal fitness traits such as mycelial growth rate, spore production, sporulation capacity, appressoria formation, and lethal time estimates (LT₅₀ and LT₉₀). Sain et al. (2019) demonstrated that isolates exhibiting comparable mortality levels against B. tabaci could differ markedly in infection dynamics, sporulation potential, and bio-efficacy indices when assessed using multiple bioassay systems. Their integrated bio-efficacy index, combining mortality, fungal growth characteristics, and lethal concentration values, proved more informative than mortality alone for ranking isolate virulence. Such parameters are, therefore, valuable supportive indicators of pathogenic potential, particularly for selecting strains with superior infection efficiency and persistence (Sain et al. 2019; Sharma et al. 2023).

Extracellular enzyme activity assays

Hydrolytic enzymes, including proteases, chitinases, and lipases, play a central role in the degradation of the insect cuticle and host invasion by EEPF. Quantitative enzyme activities have been shown to correlate strongly with the pathogenicity of EEPF with respect to insects. These enzymes are integral components of the infection process and are tightly linked to fungal pathogenic strategies (Gebremariam et al. 2022; Hong et al. 2024).

Among B. bassiana and M. anisopliae isolates, Gebremariam et al. (2022) reported significant inter-isolate variation in enzyme indices with some isolates producing higher levels of protease, chitinase, and lipase activities than others. These isolates consistently induced greater mortality at the nymphal as well as the adult stages of B. tabaci and Trialeurodes vaporariorum Westwood. The findings support enzyme profiling as a practical proxy for virulence screening, particularly during early-stage isolate comparison.

Molecular markers and gene expression analysis

Among complementary approaches, molecular tools are more likely to provide informative insights into EEPF virulence. Quantitative real time PCR (qPCR)-based methods enable the sensitive detection and quantification of fungal biomass in insect hosts, plant tissues, and soil, supporting assessments of colonization efficiency, persistence, and infection dynamics. Canfora et al. (2016) developed a culture-independent qPCR approach, using simple sequence repeat markers to accurately detect and quantify B. bassiana and Beaveria brongniartii (Sacc.) Petch in soil, following field application, demonstrating the utility of molecular markers for tracing fungal establishment and persistence.

Gene expression analyses contribute to the mechanistic understanding of virulence. Using RT-qPCR, Lobo et al. (2015) demonstrated that genes encoding secondary metabolite synthetases in B. bassiana, including those associated with tenellin and beauvericin biosynthesis, were actively expressed during insect infection and varied across infection stages and propagule types. Such expression patterns provide direct evidence that virulence-associated genes are transcriptionally regulated during insect host invasion. However, as emphasized in broader molecular reviews, these indicators are most robust when interpreted alongside phenotypic bioassays rather than as standalone measures of virulence (Hong et al. 2024).

Comparative genomics and secretome analysis

Comparative genomic studies reveal that a complex and dynamic repertoire of secreted proteins, cuticle-degrading enzymes, and secondary metabolite gene clusters underpins EEPF virulence. Staats et al. (2014) showed that M. anisopliae possesses a large and diverse predicted secretome, with many secreted protein-coding genes exhibiting differential expression during infection-related conditions. Phylogenomic analyses suggest that subsets of these secreted proteins have explicitly evolved in entomopathogenic lineages, underscoring their role in pathogenicity.

While genomic and secretome analyses provide predictive insights into virulence potential and host adaptation, functional validation remains essential to confirm their biological relevance (Staats et al. 2014; Hong et al. 2024).

Metabolomic profiling and bioactive compounds

Secondary metabolites are key contributors to insect mortality, immune suppression, and host manipulation. Shahbaz et al. (2024) highlighted the central role of metabolites, such as destruxins, beauvericins, hirsutellides, isarolides, and related compounds, in mediating EEPF pathogenicity. Metabolomic profiling enables the identification and comparison of these compounds across fungal isolates, helping explain differences in virulence intensity and host range. Although metabolite presence alone does not confirm virulence, such profiling provides important supportive evidence of pathogenic potential, particularly when integrated with enzymatic, molecular, and bioassay data.

Endophytic colonization efficiency and plant-mediated effects

In EEPF systems, colonization efficiency and persistence within plant tissues are critical determinants of effects on herbivorous insects. Moreover, the endophytic establishment of EPF is often localized and transient, yet sufficient to influence insect pest and plant responses (Kasambala et al. 2018; Vega 2018).

Experimental evidence demonstrated successful endophytic colonization of maize tissues by B. bassiana and M. anisopliae, as confirmed by fungal recovery assays and PCR-based detection (Liu et al. 2022). These endophytic associations were accompanied by measurable changes in plant growth. Molecular detection methods, therefore, play a key role in assessing EEPF persistence and functional relevance under field-relevant conditions. Fungal fitness traits, enzyme activity assays, molecular markers, genomic insights, metabolomic profiling, and colonization assessments provide complementary, mechanistic perspectives on EEPF virulence. While none of these approaches can replace classical insect bioassays and in planta evaluations, they function as robust supportive indicators that enhance isolate discrimination and biological interpretation. When integrated, these methods enable a more holistic and realistic assessment of EEPF performance, particularly within plant-mediated and indirect defense contexts central to sustainable agricultural biocontrol (Kasambala Donga et al. 2018; Sharma et al. 2023; Hong et al. 2024).

Methods for establishing EEPF in planta

Establishing EPF as endophytes in crop plants directly and indirectly helps control agricultural insect pests for long periods. In recent years, B. bassiana has been successfully established in wheat, pumpkin, radiata pine, sorghum, cacao, coffee, corn, banana, bean, date palm, jute, opium poppy, and tomato (Parsa et al. 2013). However, the rate and extent of colonization depend on growth conditions, cultivation features and the interactions of EEPF with other microbes (Vidal and Jaber 2015). Foliar spray

Spraying leaves with a suspension of fungal spores is a widely accepted technique for introducing EEPF into plant tissues (Pathan and Deshpande 2019; Liu et al. 2022). The plant is provided with distilled water as per its soil capacity, 24 h before spraying. During spraying, pot surfaces are covered with aluminium foil to reduce spore runoff. When the plant reaches the first true leaf stage (usually 14 days after the planting), a manual atomizer is used to spray the spore suspension on the axial leaf surface. To provide moisture for facilitating fungal growth, after spraying, the plants are kept in plastic bags for one day (Parsa et al. 2013; Pathan and Deshpande 2019).

Soil drenches

Drenching the soil at the base of the host plant with a spore suspension can establish B. bassiana and M. anisopliae as EEPF (Pathan and Deshpande 2019). Soil drenching leads to high rates of root colonization. EEPF colonize roots, frequently spread systemically to other plant parts and remain in the rhizosphere and root tissues for extended periods, improving plant development and offering continuous protection against the larvae of insects such as S. frugiperda. This method is compatible with both greenhouse and field conditions (Kasambala Donga et al. 2018; Ramos et al. 2020).

Although this method is simple, it has several limitations, such as low adsorption rates, microbial competition, and variable interactions with soil complexes, which reduce its reliability (Posada et al. 2007; Ahmad et al. 2024). In addition, the percentage of soil moisture is strongly influenced by irrigation, rainfall, and temperature, contributing to variability and resulting in lower consistency (Radová and Trnková 2010; Ahmad et al. 2024).

Seed treatment

Surface-sterilized seeds are coated with a fungal spore suspension 24 h before planting. When they germinate and grow, the fungi colonize the plant as endophytes, offering defense from the seedling stage onward (Jaber 2018; Altaf et al. 2023). Seed treatment is practical and widely used to establish EEPF such as B. bassiana and Metarhizium spp. in the host. It is a straightforward strategy, compatible with current seed-coating technology, and suitable for large-scale agricultural applications (Mantzoukas and Eliopoulos 2020).

Beauveria bassiana inoculated by seed treatment in pecan (Carya illinoinensis), a common crop in the USA, successfully eradicated the pest, Curculio caryae (Ramakuwela et al. 2020). However, the success of this method depends on seed type, soil moisture, temperature, and fungal strain. Moreover, native microbes might outcompete EEPF inoculated by seed treatment (Ramakuwela et al. 2020).

Root dipping

Root dipping is a common method to establish EEPF on host plants, particularly during the seedling stage. Three-week-old seedlings, with roots intact, are extracted from the growing medium. The roots are rinsed with sterile distilled water to remove soil and contaminants. Sometimes, the ends of the roots are trimmed for enhanced absorption. The cleaned roots are submerged in a fungal spore suspension for up to 24 h. The duration may vary depending on the strain. The fungal spores adhere to the roots of the host plant and begin colonization. After treatment, the seedlings are air-dried and planted in sterile soil or growth medium (Sinno et al. 2020; Geremew et al. 2024).

Root dipping has resulted in successful colonization, especially of root tissues, and sometimes of stems and leaves as well (Sinno et al. 2020; Geremew et al. 2024). Tomato seedlings (Solanum lycopersicum L.) treated with B. bassiana and M. robertsii using root dipping showed virulence on T. absoluta, a devastating tomato plant pest (Geremew et al. 2024). However, this approach is not practical for large-scale field applications. It is better suited for young seedlings that are transplanted than for mature plants or direct-seeded crops.

Stem injection

In stem injection, a hypodermic needle and syringe are used to inject a fungal spore suspension into the basal internode of the growing stem (Cherry et al. 2004). Compared to foliar spray and soil soaking, direct stem injection may achieve higher colonization in stem tissues (Bamisile et al. 2018; Ponchon et al. 2022).

Rhizome treatment

Rhizome treatment is a tested method to establish B. bassiana and can also be used to introduce other endophytes in host plants. The approach involves the removal of plants from a nutrient medium and dipping their rhizomes into a sufficient volume of spore suspension for 90 min. The plants are then planted in steam-sterilized loamy forest soil bags (Akello et al. 2007). The limitation of this method is that abiotic pressures might affect the viability of the fungal spores (Hallsworth and Magan 1999).

Radicle dressing

Radicle dressing involves applying fungal spores directly to the radicle (the embryonic root of a germinated seed). Posada and Vega (2005) established B. bassiana in Theobroma cacao (cocoa) seedlings by radicle dressing to provide the plant protection against Conopomorpha cramerella (cocoa pod borer). To establish B. bassiana, cocoa seeds were first surface sterilized and allowed to germinate in sterile condition until the radicle grew. The radicles were treated with fresh fungal spore suspension and seedlings were allowed to grow under lab conditions.

Table 1 provides a summary of the various methods used for establishing entomopathogens as endophytes.

Among the methods used, foliar spray was the most effective, with an approximately 80% rate of plant colonization. The spore concentration used can even be one log lower than that required for the other methods. However, spraying requires large coverage of the crop area. The total amount of spores needed may be lower in seed treatment and the process is less labour intensive. There is a need for comparative evaluation of the methods described above with the generalist entomopathogenic fungi on various crops.

Evaluation of endophytic colonization of EPF in host plants

To check whether EEPF have been established as endophytes and can function as insect control agents, leaf disc and in planta assays are used. Plants or plant parts are washed under running tap water. Plant parts, such as leaf, root and stem, are surface sterilized, cut into small pieces, inoculated on selective media plates and incubated for up to 2 weeks under controlled conditions. Plant sections showing fungal growth are excised and transferred to a fresh selective media plate to avoid cross-contamination with neighboring plant sections. Individual sporulating colonies are excised and transferred on selective media to obtain a single colony. The obtained pure cultures can be maintained on potato dextrose agar slants (Pathan et al. 2021).

Once EEPF colonization in the host plant is confirmed, the plant can be used as a feeding substrate for target insect stages. This evaluation ensures that the plant does not compromise fungal pathogenicity against target pests (Bamisile et al. 2021). By quantifying pest mortality and demonstrating plant protection synergy, efficacy testing strengthens IPM strategies (Aravinthraju et al. 2024). The studies cited in the context reveal indirect mechanisms including the induction of host plant defense, altered volatile profile and metabolite production that enhance pest deterrence (Clifton et al. 2018).

Standardized leaf disc assays were used to assess the lethal and sublethal effects of M. brunneum against the larvae of the herbivorous insect pest, S. littoralis. Larval mortality, feeding behaviour, and sublethal effects such as reduced fecundity and egg fertility were quantified, highlighting the potential of EEPF to exert direct and indirect impacts on S. littoralis (Garrido-Jurado et al. 2020).

In planta assays involve exposing insects directly to live plants colonized by EEPF. The assays are designed to evaluate the systemic plant-mediated effects of fungal colonization on insect performance, while accounting for background plant stress responses. In planta feeding trials extend bioassay results to greenhouse conditions by evaluating the insect control potential of EEPF under realistic plant-insect-fungus interactions. For instance, the insect control potential of B. bassiana and Purpureocillium lilacinum (Thom) have been assessed against Aphis gossypii through seed as well as stem inoculation approaches, revealing negative effects on the growth of aphid populations (Lopez et al. 2014; Gutiérrez et al. 2018). Such greenhouse-based assays demonstrate that endophytic colonization can significantly suppress aphid reproduction, underscoring the relevance of in planta approaches for evaluating the potential of EEPF for biocontrol (Lopez et al. 2014).

Mechanisms of improved virulence in EEPF

EEPF exhibit greater virulence against insect pests than their free-living counterparts, enabling plant protection and improved biocontrol efficacy (Branine et al. 2019). These enhancements are due to horizontal gene transfer and trait acquisition during plant-fungal interactions (Zhang et al. 2019; Pathan and Deshpande 2019). Understanding the mechanisms of transfer of traits from plants to fungi is crucial for optimising the utility of EEPF strains for the control of insect pests in agriculture (Vega 2018).

Horizontal gene transfer

A symbiotic fungus may acquire an insecticidal characteristic from a plant through horizontal gene transfer (HGT). Across 6 plant genomes, 14 candidate events were found, including 4 plants to fungi transfers and 5 fungi to plant transfers (Richards et al. 2009). Horizontal gene transfer between plants and fungi represents a rare but evolutionarily significant phenomenon that can contribute to fungal pathogenicity.

Colletotrichum spp. acquired plant-like subtilisin genes approximately 150–155 million years ago. These genes are upregulated in fungi during maize infection and may aid plant pathogenesis through molecular mimicry (Armijos Jaramillo et al. 2013). Rhizophagus irregularis, an arbuscular mycorrhizal fungus, obtained 37 genes from bacteria and plants, more than half of which are functionally expressed and essential for biological functions (Li et al. 2018). Over 90 genes transferred between the plant pathogenic fungi, Magnaporthales and Colletotrichum, exhibit widespread HGT events. These genes are especially associated with carbohydrate-activating enzymes, which help fungi break down plant cell walls (Qiu et al. 2016).

For instance, M. anisopliae strains isolated from the rhizosphere of Annona squamosa (custard apple) were more virulent against the pod-borer, H. armigera, than strains of M. anisopliae isolated from the rhizospheres of the other crop plants. The high virulence of M. anisopliae strains isolated from A. squamosa was attributed to horizontally acquired insecticidal peptides of plant origin. Genes coding for these plant-derived peptides as well as other genes of plant origin were detected in M. anisopliae. These genes were perhaps horizontally transferred to the fungus during its existence as endophyte in A. squamosa. Metarhizium anisopliae strains isolated from the rhizosphere of plants with insecticidal properties, such as neem, chili and papaya, also showed high virulence against H. armigera. The study suggests that the virulence of EEPF strains against insect pests is influenced by the source from which they are isolated (Pathan and Deshpande 2019). Evidence points to a shared genetic base and possible gene integration, which indicate HGT events (Barelli et al. 2016; Wang and Wang 2017). Enhancing the virulence of EEPF

Successive subculturing of EPF on artificial media can lead to the loss of their virulence against insect pests (Kulkarni et al. 2008; Hussien et al. 2021; Tomilova et al. 2023 ). Moreover, repeated in vitro subculturing on artificial media may induce random mutations or gene silencing in fungi (Chang et al. 2020). The loss of virulence due to repeated subculturing may be regained by sequential passage through the insect host or by genetic manipulation, such as the insertion of toxin-expressing genes (Kulkarni et al. 2008; Kidanu and Hagos 2019). For example, inserting toxin-producing genes enhances the effectiveness of EPF, increasing the mortality rate of target insect pests (Kidanu and Hagos 2019). Although such genetic manipulation offers a direct route to improve the entomopathogenic capabilities of EEPF, limited acceptability and socio-economic issues restrict their use.

An alternative strategy to create more virulent EEPF is to introduce EEPF into plants with insecticidal or insect-resistant traits and letting the fungi acquire and express plant-derived insecticidal pest resistance (or toxin-related) genes naturally (Fig. 4). There is evidence that shows that EEPF can stably acquire plant insecticidal genes and express those genes (Zhang et al. 2019; Vannier et al. 2019; Pathan and Deshpande 2019). Possible mechanisms of horizontal gene transfer (HGT) between plants and fungi

It is important to note that there are uncertainties. For example, the fungus contains internal defence mechanisms, such as repeat-induced point (RIP) mutations, which can silence or degrade the foreign DNA acquired through HGT. These RIP mutations can prevent the long-term accumulation of insecticidal traits (Hood et al. 2005; Gladyshev 2017; van Wyk et al. 2021). Moreover, endophytic colonization increased virulence against generalist pests but had a negligible effect on specialist pests, suggesting that these interactions are highly species-specific (Jaber and Ownley 2018). Such evidence suggests that the stability of horizontally acquired traits depends on the balance between gene acquisition, internal defence mechanisms, and selective pressures within the plant, the insect and the fungus. To overcome these issues, we need to understand how the traits can be transferred. We also need methods to ascertain whether the acquired traits are active.

Mechanisms of trait transfer

Trait transfer facilitated by an RNA-based mechanism involves the capture of plant mRNA molecules by fungal cells. The transfer of mRNA from Arabidopsis thaliana to the fungal pathogen, Botrytis cinerea, via extracellular vesicles has been demonstrated (Wang et al. 2024). The mRNA was reverse transcribed as a retrotransposon and integrated into the fungal genome via non-homologous end joining (Taylor and Bruenn 2009; Zhang et al. 2014; Wang et al. 2024). Such integration is likely to occur in repeat-rich regions. These repeat-rich regions act as hotspots for evolution and allow the stable expression of heterologous genes in fungal cells, as shown in Fig. 5a (Dong et al. 2015).

In rare cases, organelle capture or a transient fusion event may incorporate plant DNA into fungal cells via the transfer of mitochondria or plastids (Richards et al. 2011a). Grafting was used to study the mechanism involved in tobacco plant plastid genome transfer from cell to neighbouring cell via pores. This phenomenon provides a mechanistic framework for HGT (Fig. 5b). Once plant DNA enters a fungal cell via organelles, it gets integrated into and stably maintained in the fungal genome (Hertle et al. 2021).

Gene transfer may also occur through endocytosis or due to direct uptake of environmental DNA (eDNA) (Fig. 5c). The eDNA is released from damaged plant tissues, where fungal hyphae or germinated spores in the apoplast take up the eDNA along with water, nutrients, small molecules and organelles (Roca et al. 2004; Read et al. 2009). The integration of the eDNA occurs through non-homologous end joining (or, less frequently through homologous recombination). This phenomenon is intensified in an environment where plant debris and fungal hyphae coexist (Richards et al. 2011b; Qiu et al. 2016).

Though there are many hypotheses that seek to explain the mechanism of HGT, direct evidence is still lacking (Fitzpatrick 2012). The broader importance of HGT in fungal biology remains poorly understood and is likely to be underestimated (Malagnac et al. 2014). Nevertheless, this genetic phenomenon offers a promising avenue to enhance the potential of EEPF biocontrol capabilities.

Evaluation of trait transfer from host to EEPF

Horizontal gene transfer significantly contributes to enhancing virulence in EEPF by enabling the acquisition of adaptive genes from plants. Comprehensive omics profiling provides orthogonal evidence of HGT through sets of events such as irregular sequence composition, gene expression patterns, protein function, and metabolic outputs, essential for validating functional integration (Branine et al. 2019). This layered approach not only detects HGT events but also reveals their biological impact on host range expansion and biocontrol efficacy (Iwanicki et al. 2022).

Genomics

The genomics approach to assessing HGT in virulent fungal strains involves sequencing the whole genome. In the data obtained, genes that reveal unusual GC content, codon usage bias or unique sequence structure indicate a potential HGT event (Habig et al. 2024). Comparative genomics is then applied to identify closely related fungal species and the possible donor plant. The comparison is used to pinpoint genes that are found in the fungus but are lacking in related fungi, indicating that these genes may have been horizontally transferred from plants (Soanes and Richards 2014). After the comparison, potential genes are subjected to phylogenetic analysis. In the resulting phylogenetic trees, if a fungal gene clusters with plant homologs rather than with fungal genes, the possibility of an HGT event from the plant to the fungus is strongly supported (Slot and Rokas 2011; Pathan and Deshpande 2019). But the presence of a plant gene in a fungus does not necessarily mean that the gene will be expressed.

Transcriptomics and confirmation by RT-qPCR

To profile genome-wide gene expression in the fungus under different conditions, such as growth in pure culture, or under plant infection or abiotic stress, RNA is sequenced. The sequences reveal whether the candidate HGT genes, those that exhibit plant-like features in previous genomic analyses, are actively transcribed and how their expression varies depending on the environment. Consistent or inducible expression of plant-derived genes during plant interaction or stress supports their horizontal acquisition by EEPF and may have a role in virulence against insect pests (Fitzpatrick 2012).

To determine if these genes are expressed in more natural or complex settings, meta-transcriptomics can be employed. RNA extracted directly from environmental samples can help confirm the role of HGT genes beyond laboratory culture by demonstrating that the genes are not only present but are also actively transcribed in the EEPF within its ecological setting (Kuske et al. 2015; Marcelino et al. 2019).

Validating quantifying expression changes, observed in transcriptomics or meta-transcriptomics datasets, can be carried out with RT-qPCR, using gene-specific primers targeting HGT genes. The method helps verify if transcriptional induction or repression is substantial and repeatable across a range of experimental settings. This technique allows the precise quantification of gene expression levels, aiding the separation of real biological signals from technical noise (Ish-Shalom and Lichter 2010).

To perform a comparative transcriptomic analysis, RNA sequence reads from M. anisopliae strains were pre-processed and aligned against both the fungal reference genome and available A. squamosa transcription data. This approach identified 21 distinct transcripts of plant origin within virulent fungal strains including those for a non-ribosomal peptide synthetase and a plant cyclopeptide, providing evidence of HGT. The analysis also revealed significant deviation from the expected genomic G+C content of Metarhizium (Pathan and Deshpande 2019).

Proteomics

To detect and quantify proteins encoded by candidate HGT genes in fungi, quantitative and targeted proteomics (LC-MS/MS) can be used (Al-Amrani et al. 2021). Protein detection confirms active gene translation and can help localize the protein within the fungal cell and/or in secreted fractions (Corrêa et al. 2025). Comparative proteomics contrasting HGT positive or negative strains or mutants builds up evidence for functional HGT, especially when the protein is related to pest virulence or other plant-linked functions (Corrêa et al. 2025). Proteomic analysis revealed that M. anisopliae strains produced the insecticidal agents, cyclopeptide E and cyclopeptide F. These peptides showed homology to cyclopeptides from the A. squamosa plant, indicating plant-derived insecticidal traits in the virulent fungal isolate (Pathan and Deshpande 2019).

Metabolomics

Over 400 million years of co-evolution with their host plants, EEPF have developed unique biosynthetic pathways and metabolic mechanisms that allow the fungi to synthesize complex secondary metabolites originally thought to be exclusive to plants (Wen et al. 2022). Despite this discovery, it is estimated that only 16% of the 1.5 million fungal species have been described and studied in detail (Kharwar et al. 2011; Wen et al. 2022). Among them, EEPF have emerged as potential alternatives to plant-derived bioactive compounds, including polyketides, anthraquinones, quinones, and diketopiperazine derivatives (Wen et al. 2022). The metabolic profiles of wild-type and HGT-containing strains can be compared using untargeted metabolomics (LC-MS, GC-MS) to identify alterations in secondary metabolites derived from plant-like gene pathways (Witte et al. 2022). Metabolomics, along with other omics, allow screening for new secondary metabolites and help understand the regulation of biosynthetic pathways, providing insights into the roles that HGT plays.

Challenges and the way forward

The practical deployment of EEPF faces application-oriented constraints. Major challenges include inconsistent field performance due to environmental sensitivity to temperature, humidity, UV radiation, and soil conditions (Batta 2016; Quesada-Moraga et al. 2024), formulation instability, limited shelf life, and storage requirements (Mesquita et al. 2023). Concerns regarding non-target effects, particularly under direct spore exposure, regulatory complexity, and limited farmer acceptance further restrict large-scale adoption (Zimmermann 2008; Ravensberg 2011; Omuse et al. 2022).

Addressing these challenges requires a shift toward application-driven and systems-based strategies for EPF deployment. Improving formulation technologies is a priority, with emphasis on UV-protective carriers, controlled-release systems, and stabilizing additives to enhance shelf life and field persistence (Mesquita et al. 2023). Precision delivery approaches, optimized application timing, and reduced reliance on foliar spraying can minimize non-target exposure and improve ecological safety.

Endophytic establishment as a delivery strategy offers a distinct advantage over conventional external applications, as internally colonized EEPF provide long-term, systemic pest suppression while reducing environmental losses and non-target risks (Gathage et al. 2016; Jaber and Araj 2018; Pathan and Deshpande 2019). Integrating EEPF into IPM programs can enhance efficacy while conserving beneficial arthropods.

To ensure reliable performance, multi-location and multi-season field trials across diverse agro-climatic conditions are essential for developing robust application guidelines. Regulatory harmonization tailored to microbial biopesticides, coupled with farmer training and extension support, are critical for wider acceptance by the farming community (Chandler et al. 2011).

Finally, omics-based approaches can support application optimization by elucidating plant-fungus-insect interactions, environmental persistence, and safety, guiding the rational development of next-generation EEPF as durable and commercially viable biocontrol agents.

Conclusions

The use of EEPF represents a conceptual shift in biological pest control, moving from externally applied microbial agents toward internally established, plant-associated defenders. The capacity of EEPF to persist within plant tissues while selectively targeting insect pests positions them as integrative components of sustainable and precision-oriented agriculture, rather than transient alternatives to chemical pesticides.

Characteristic features of EEPF, such as ecological adaptability, functional flexibility, and biological promiscuity, enable their interaction with diverse plant hosts and insect pests in diverse environmental contexts. This adaptive capacity, potentially reinforced by HGT and metabolite-mediated signaling, allows EEPF to fine-tune their insecticidal potential while maintaining endophytic compatibility. Such flexibility distinguishes EEPF from conventionally applied entomopathogens and reinforces their promise as durable, system-level pest management tools. The identification of plant-derived genes within EEPF genomes, as summarized in this review, provides a framework for understanding the evolutionary acquisition of insecticidal traits and may guide future strain engineering strategies without necessitating transgenic interventions.

Advances in microbiology, biotechnology, and omics-driven approaches are creating opportunities to harness this diversity in a controlled and predictable manner. Maintaining entomopathogens as endophytes, rather than through repeated subculturing on artificial media, may help preserve virulence, metabolic competence, and host-adapted traits that are often lost during industrial propagation. In parallel, the concept of the sequential establishment of EEPF in different plant hosts with insecticidal properties presents a hypothesis-driven avenue to explore the adaptive enhancement of functional traits, provided such strategies are evaluated within robust ecological and biosafety frameworks.

Ultimately, the future of EEPF lies not in replacing chemical pesticides outright, but in redefining crop protection as a biologically integrated process, where adaptable microbial allies contribute to effective, sustainable, and socially acceptable agroecosystems. EEPF thus emerge as natural bodyguards within biologically integrated crop protection systems.
