Melioidosis: diverse clinical manifestations and patient outcomes in a case series
Authors: Ravi Kishor Allu, Raviteja Nalabothula, Kamala Kannan, Kumar Janardhanan Subramonia, Vaishnavi R
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Melioidosis, caused by the gram-negative facultative intracellular bacterium Burkholderia pseudomallei, is a complex and variable disease with a broad spectrum of clinical manifestations. It is primarily transmitted through exposure to wet-season soils or contaminated water. This case series presents five patients diagnosed with melioidosis, highlighting their diverse clinical presentations, diagnostic challenges, treatment approaches, and outcomes. The discussion includes a review of current literature on the epidemiology, pathogenesis, and management of melioidosis.

Introduction

Melioidosis, caused by Burkholderia pseudomallei, is a potentially life-threatening infectious disease endemic to Southeast Asia and northern Australia, with sporadic cases reported globally, particularly among travellers and immigrants from endemic regions [1, 2]. In recent years, it has been increasingly reported in India [3]. The disease presents with a wide range of clinical manifestations, from asymptomatic colonisation to severe multisystem involvement, including abscess formation, pneumonia, osteomyelitis, septic arthritis, and disseminated infection. It often mimics other infections, such as tuberculosis, pneumonia, and sepsis, leading to diagnostic challenges [4]. The organs most affected are the musculoskeletal system, liver, spleen, and genitourinary tract. Neurological involvement in melioidosis is termed neuromelioidosis, and it is extremely rare, with a high mortality rate.

The incubation period of melioidosis is highly variable, ranging from 1 day to 21 days, with a median of about 9 days. Burkholderia pseudomallei can remain dormant in hosts for extended periods, ranging from months to years, especially in individuals with immunocompromised status. This variability underscores the unpredictable nature of the disease and emphasizes the need for a proactive approach to early diagnosis and treatment [5].

Several risk factors predispose individuals to severe melioidosis, with diabetes mellitus being the most significant. Hyperglycaemia impairs neutrophil function, increasing susceptibility to B. pseudomallei infection and worsening outcomes [6]. Other underlying comorbidities, including chronic kidney disease, alcoholism, malignancy, and immunosuppression, further elevate the risk of severe disease and complications. The severity of the illness, comorbidities, and availability of quality medical care all affect the case fatality rate of melioidosis. Overall death rates in endemic areas vary between 10% and 40%, with the highest fatality rates occurring in septicaemic cases [7]. However, due to its wide range of symptoms, melioidosis is commonly misdiagnosed, leading to delays in treatment. This delay can significantly impact morbidity and mortality, underscoring the crucial role of accurate diagnosis and management in the field of infectious diseases.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a retrospective case series conducted at a tertiary care teaching hospital in India over a period of June 2024 to June 2025.

Inclusion criteria and data collection

All adult patients (≥ 18 years) with culture-confirmed Burkholderia pseudomallei infection during the study period were included. Clinical data including demographic details, comorbidities, presenting features, laboratory parameters, imaging findings, treatment details, and outcomes were retrieved from hospital medical records. Clinical timelines were described narratively within each case presentation. Patients with incomplete clinical data were excluded from the analysis.

Microbiological methods

Microbiological identification was initially performed using the VITEK 2 Compact automated system (bioMérieux, France). All isolates were subsequently confirmed by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) targeting the Type III Secretion System 1 (T3SS1) gene specific to Burkholderia pseudomallei. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed using the broth microdilution method and interpreted according to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines.

Reporting standards and limitations

This case series has been reported in accordance with CARE guidelines. Due to the retrospective nature of the study, standardized severity scoring systems such as SOFA or APACHE II were not consistently available for all patients.

Case 1

A 59-year-old male with Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM) presented with a ten-day history of fever, generalized myalgia, and gastrointestinal symptoms, including nausea, vomiting, decreased appetite, and abdominal distension for one week, along with decreased urine output. His long-standing, poorly controlled diabetes mellitus led to complications, including a diabetic foot ulcer with superimposed wet gangrene, necessitating amputation of the second toe. Additionally, he had previously undergone a right below-knee amputation due to diabetic complications. He also had a history of chronic alcohol use disorder. On admission, he was conscious, oriented, and afebrile but exhibited severe dehydration and pallor. His blood pressure was 130/80 mmHg, oxygen saturation was 99% on room air, and blood glucose was markedly elevated at 422 mg/dL. Cardiovascular and respiratory examinations were unremarkable, while an abdominal examination revealed a soft abdomen with normal bowel sounds.

Laboratory investigations showed significant abnormalities, including anaemia (haemoglobin 7.2 g/dL), leucocytosis with a neutrophil predominance (95.8%), thrombocytopenia (platelet count 81,000/cu.mm), and severe renal dysfunction (serum creatinine 7.7 mg/dL, blood urea nitrogen 106 mg/dL). Electrolyte disturbances included severe hyponatraemia (sodium < 120 mmol/L) and metabolic acidosis (bicarbonate 11 mmol/L). Liver function tests revealed mildly elevated bilirubin (total bilirubin 1.29 mg/dL) and transaminases (AST 189 IU/L, ALT 90 IU/L). The patient was initially managed with insulin infusion and intravenous fluids for diabetic ketoacidosis. Empirical intravenous antibiotics with piperacillin–tazobactam were initiated. Due to worsening renal function and oliguria, the patient underwent intermittent haemodialysis via a right internal jugular vein catheter.

Imaging studies, including computed tomography (CT) of the abdomen and ultrasound abdomen, revealed multiple liver abscesses, with the largest measuring approximately 6 cm × 5 cm with bilateral contracted kidneys. For the liver abscesses, percutaneous drainage with a pigtail catheter was performed. Microbiological cultures from blood, liver aspirate, and wound swabs confirmed the presence of Burkholderia pseudomallei, leading to a diagnosis of melioidosis, following which antibiotics were escalated to IV ceftazidime based on culture sensitivity results. Blood cultures were processed using an automated blood culture system. Initial organism identification was performed using an automated system and subsequently confirmed by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) targeting the Type III Secretion System 1 (T3SS1) gene specific to Burkholderia pseudomallei. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed using the broth microdilution method and interpreted in accordance with Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing showed that the isolate was susceptible to ceftazidime, meropenem, and trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole (Fig. 1A-D). Throughout treatment, the patient showed gradual clinical improvement. After completing the intensive phase of IV ceftazidime therapy for 4 weeks, and following significant recovery, he was discharged with oral eradication therapy with trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX) for 3 months. Supportive care, including dialysis and glycaemic control along with antibiotics was given during the hospital stay. The patient was discharged on oral TMP-SMX, with regular follow-up, and showed clinical improvement.

Case 2

A 36-year-old male farmer with a history of diabetes mellitus for one year presented with complaints of fever for ten days, progressive jaundice for four days, and difficulty walking for two days. The weakness primarily affected the proximal muscles of both lower limbs, with a gradual onset and worsening over time. On admission, he was drowsy, disoriented, and icteric, with a blood pressure of 90/60 mmHg, which improved with fluid resuscitation. Neurological examination showed normal tone in all four limbs, preserved power in the upper limbs, and asymmetrical lower limb weakness (left greater than right, proximal more than distal). Deep tendon reflexes were absent in the knees and ankles, while other systemic examinations were unremarkable.

Laboratory investigations revealed severe hyperglycaemia (capillary blood glucose of 380 mg/dL), hypokalaemia (potassium 2.5 mmol/L), thrombocytopenia (platelet count of 79,000/mm³), elevated total bilirubin (7.9 mg/dL), and impaired renal function (creatinine 2.3 mg/dL). He was initially suspected to have a tropical infectious disease such as scrub typhus or leptospirosis, which are common causes of acute febrile illness in endemic regions, and was treated with hepatoprotective agents, intravenous ceftriaxone, doxycycline, and supportive measures. Serology for scrub typhus was positive, and doxycycline therapy was continued as part of the treatment regimen. However, despite ongoing therapy, his fever persisted. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain revealed hyperintensities in the bilateral middle cerebellar peduncles with restricted diffusion and post-contrast enhancement in the bilateral cavernous sinus and infratemporal fossa (Fig. 2A-E). Whole-spine screening showed no abnormalities. Blood cultures subsequently grew Burkholderia pseudomallei, confirming the diagnosis of neuromelioidosis and antibiotic therapy was escalated to intravenous meropenem for the intensive phase. During hospitalization, the patient’s neurological condition worsened, progressing to quadriparesis with neck muscle weakness, which was consistent with the previously noted involvement of the brainstem and cerebellar pathways on MRI, ultimately leading to respiratory failure requiring ventilatory support.

Cerebrospinal fluid analysis showed mild pleocytosis with a total leukocyte count of 48 cells/mm³ (67% lymphocytes) and elevated protein levels, though culture results were negative. Nerve conduction studies revealed demyelination-predominant polyradiculoneuropathy with temporal dispersion and sural sparing.

Due to prolonged ventilatory dependence, the patient subsequently underwent tracheostomy for airway management and ventilatory support. He received four weeks of intensive intravenous meropenem therapy, followed by an eradication phase with oral trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX) for six months [8]. Supportive measures, including physiotherapy and tracheostomy care, played a crucial role in his recovery. Gradually, the patient showed significant clinical improvement, regained muscle strength, and was successfully weaned off ventilatory support. Tracheostomy decannulation was performed, and he was discharged on oral TMP-SMX with continued physiotherapy and regular follow-ups.

Case 3

A 58-year-old male with a known history of Stage 5 chronic kidney disease (CKD) on medical management presented with breathlessness and fever for seven days, along with bilateral lower limb swelling for one month. On admission, he was hypotensive and required inotropic support. Clinical examination revealed severe dehydration and pallor. His blood pressure was 90/60 mmHg, which improved with fluid resuscitation, and he had an elevated respiratory rate. Cardiovascular examinations were unremarkable, respiratory examination revealed bilateral crepts right more than left, while an abdominal examination revealed no significant abnormalities. Laboratory investigations showed severe azotaemia (serum urea 280 mg/dL, blood urea nitrogen 131 mg/dL, creatinine 10.3 mg/dL), metabolic acidosis (bicarbonate 14 mmol/L), and electrolyte imbalances, including mild hyponatraemia (sodium 132 mmol/L) and hyperkalaemia (potassium 5.3 mmol/L). Haematological findings included anaemia (haemoglobin 7.2 g/dL) and leucocytosis with a neutrophil predominance (WBC 15,550/cu.mm, neutrophils 96.6%). Computed tomography (CT) of the chest was performed and revealed right upper lobe consolidation and an initial diagnosis of right-sided pneumonia with CKD was made (Fig. 3A-C). The patient was initially managed with haemodialysis via a right internal jugular vein catheter and received intravenous fluids, inotropic support, and empirical IV antibiotics. Blood cultures subsequently confirmed the presence of Burkholderia pseudomallei, establishing the diagnosis of melioidosis, following which antibiotics were escalated to ceftazidime based on culture results. Despite intensive care measures, his condition deteriorated, requiring intubation due to progressive decline in consciousness (GCS deterioration) and worsening respiratory distress.

During hospitalization, the patient underwent two cycles of haemodialysis and received a packed red blood cell transfusion. However, he remained critically ill, requiring escalating inotropic support due to persistent hypotension. Despite aggressive management, he developed sudden bradycardia followed by cardiac arrest. Cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) was initiated per Advanced Cardiac Life Support (ACLS) protocols, but after six cycles, resuscitation was unsuccessful, and the patient was declared deceased. This case highlights the severe complications of melioidosis in an immunocompromised patient with CKD. Confirming Burkholderia pseudomallei through blood cultures underscores the importance of early recognition and targeted antibiotic therapy to improve outcomes in high-risk patients.

Case 4

A 57-year-old male with a history of Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus, systemic hypertension, Stage 5 chronic kidney disease (CKD) on haemodialysis, and hepatitis C presented with progressive swelling and pain in his left lower limb for one month, which later worsened and was suggestive of a severe soft tissue infection necessitating fasciotomy and wound debridement at an outside hospital, where he was intubated for the procedure (Fig. 4A, B). Postoperatively, the patient developed sepsis progressing to septic shock with multi-organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS) and was referred for further management on ventilatory support.

On admission in our hospital, the patient was intubated, sedated, and ventilated. He was hemodynamically unstable, with a blood pressure of 86/50 mmHg and a heart rate of 118 bpm. He was febrile (102.4 °F) and hypoxic, with an oxygen saturation of 89% on FiO₂ 60%, indicating significant respiratory compromise.

Laboratory investigations revealed hyperbilirubinaemia (total bilirubin 9.03 mg/dL, direct bilirubin 6.18 mg/dL), hypoalbuminemia (2.3 g/dL), and significant renal dysfunction (urea 101 mg/dL, creatinine 2.0 mg/dL). Electrolyte imbalances included hyponatraemia (sodium 129 mmol/L) and severe hypokalaemia (potassium 2.3 mmol/L). Haematological findings showed severe anaemia (haemoglobin 5.6 g/dL) and thrombocytopenia (platelet count 36,400/cu.mm), with coagulation abnormalities (prolonged PT 22.6 s, INR 1.67, APTT 45.5 s). Sepsis markers confirmed. Tissue cultures obtained from the affected site and blood cultures both yielded Burkholderia pseudomallei, following which targeted antibiotic therapy was initiated. Serology for anti-HCV was reactive. The patient was initially started on empirical intravenous piperacillin–tazobactam, which was subsequently escalated to intravenous meropenem in combination with trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole following culture reports. Histopathological examination was not performed, as the diagnosis was established based on microbiological culture results.

The patient required ongoing inotropic support with norepinephrine along with antibiotic therapy. Nephrology recommended urgent haemodialysis with platelet transfusion due to worsening thrombocytopenia and uraemia. Suspected right upper limb cellulitis was evaluated, and arterial and venous Doppler studies ruled out vascular compromise. Despite six cycles of haemodialysis, multiple blood transfusions, and antibiotic escalation, the patient’s condition continued to deteriorate.

During hospitalization, he developed sudden bradycardia, with a heart rate dropping to 40 bpm and blood pressure decreasing to 60/40 mmHg, followed by cardiac arrest. Cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) was initiated per ACLS protocol, including adrenaline administration and FiO₂ increase to 100%, but no return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) was achieved. After 30 min of resuscitative efforts, the patient was pronounced deceased. The final diagnosis included septic shock secondary to Burkholderia pseudomallei infection, multi-organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS), disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), left lower limb skin and soft tissue infection, and chronic kidney disease on haemodialysis. The cause of death was attributed to complications arising from septic shock, MODS, DIC, and CKD, despite intensive medical management.

Case 5

A 35-year-old male with a known 10-year history of Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM) presented with complaints of breathlessness and giddiness for three days, along with one episode of vomiting, cough with expectoration and acute onset altered level of consciousness for one day. He was brought to the emergency department in severe respiratory distress and was immediately intubated and shifted to the Critical Care Unit for mechanical ventilation. The patient had a history of right upper limb cellulitis secondary to Burkholderia pseudomallei, for which fasciotomy was done two months earlier but defaulted eradication phase treatment and lost follow up. On admission, his vitals were notable for tachycardia (pulse 130/min), hypotension (BP 90/60 mmHg), and capillary blood glucose of 450 mg/dL. He was initiated on insulin infusion for hyperglycaemia.

On examination, he was drowsy and altered (GCS E3VtM3-6T/15), intubated and mechanically ventilated (VC/AC mode) with FiO₂ of 40% and PEEP of 6 cm H₂O, maintaining SpO₂ at 98%. Initial blood investigations revealed leucocytosis (WBC 27,120/cu.mm, neutrophils 89.1%), elevated NT-ProBNP (1760 pg/mL), and metabolic acidosis. Platelet count was elevated (430,100/cu.mm). Procalcitonin was raised (0.88 ng/mL), suggestive of systemic bacterial infection. Cardiac evaluation showed global left ventricular hypokinesia with severe systolic dysfunction (EF 25–30%), consistent with acute decompensated heart failure and possible septic cardiomyopathy. Bedside chest X-ray revealed increased bronchovascular markings bilaterally and haziness in the basal regions, suggestive of pulmonary oedema.

MRI brain revealed multiple foci of diffusion restriction and T2/FLAIR hyperintensity in bilateral cerebellar peduncles, cerebellar hemispheres, pons, medulla, and along corticospinal tracts findings consistent with either multiple infarcts or cerebritis. These findings were suggestive of neuromelioidosis or septic emboli. A dilated perioptic CSF space also indicated raised intracranial pressure (Fig. 5A-F).

The patient was empirically started on intravenous piperacillin–tazobactam. Given the strong clinical suspicion of melioidosis reactivation, antimicrobial therapy was upgraded to high dose IV meropenem in view of suspected neuromelioidosis. Dual therapy with trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole was not initiated initially, as intravenous meropenem alone provides effective bactericidal coverage during the early intensive phase of severe disease. Seizure prophylaxis with IV levetiracetam was initiated. Supportive management included inotropic support with noradrenaline, insulin therapy, heart failure medications, and ventilatory care. Despite intensive care, the patient remained unconscious (GCS 2T/15) and dependent on mechanical ventilation (SIMV mode). A tracheostomy was planned on day 12 of intubation following clearance from cardiology and neurology teams. Despite aggressive management, he developed sudden bradycardia followed by cardiac arrest. Cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) was initiated per ACLS protocols, but after five cycles, resuscitation was unsuccessful, and the patient was declared deceased.

Discussion

This case series consists of five cases, each showcasing different clinical manifestations of melioidosis. The first case involved a 59-year-old male with gram-negative septicaemia and multiple liver abscesses. The second case featured a 36-year-old male who developed neuromelioidosis. The third case involved a 58-year-old male presenting with pneumonia and bacteraemia secondary to Burkholderia pseudomallei infection. The fourth case involved a 58-year-old male with sepsis secondary to left lower skin and soft tissue infection with septic shock, multi-organ dysfunction syndrome, and disseminated intravascular coagulation caused secondary to Burkholderia pseudomallei, further complicated by chronic kidney disease and hepatitis C, the fifth case represents a relapsing presentation of melioidosis. Out of the five cases, four patients had a history of Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM), with three patients having chronic kidney disease (CKD). These underlying conditions significantly contributed to the complexity of their clinical presentations and outcomes. Burkholderia pseudomallei was identified from blood cultures in all patients, and antimicrobial susceptibility testing performed using the broth microdilution method in accordance with Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines showed that all isolates were susceptible to ceftazidime, meropenem, and trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole, which guided the antimicrobial therapy used in these patients. Pneumonia is the most prevalent clinical manifestation of both acute and chronic melioidosis in adults. Skin infections, such as ulcers, abscesses, and maculopapular lesions, are also commonly observed. Genitourinary infections primarily involve prostatic abscesses [4, 9, 10]. Bacteraemia is present in approximately 50% of cases, and septic shock occurs in up to 25% of patients. Septic arthritis or osteomyelitis and encephalomyelitis are rare. Neuromelioidosis has a spectrum of presentation ranging from encephalomyelitis, meningitis, cerebral abscess, myelitis and epidural abscess. The clinical presentation can be in the form of brainstem encephalitis, cranial nerve palsies, peripheral motor weakness and flaccid paraparesis [9–15].

Melioidosis should be suspected in patients presenting with acute sepsis, community-acquired pneumonia, or chronic infection in the presence of risk factors such as diabetes, renal impairment, or immunosuppression, particularly when there is inadequate response to appropriate empirical antimicrobial therapy. In microscopy, Gram-negative bacilli with bipolar staining may be identified from the sputum, pus or wound swab of an infected patient [16].

Serology has a limited role in diagnosing melioidosis due to its unreliability, particularly in the acute phase [4]. The gold standard for diagnosis is the culture of Burkholderia pseudomallei from blood, sputum, urine, or tissue samples. Selective media such as Ashdown agar are particularly useful for isolating the organism from non-sterile clinical specimens such as sputum, pus, or wound swabs [17].

Other diagnostic techniques, such as polymerase chain reaction (PCR), rapid immunofluorescence microscopy, and lateral flow immunoassays, are not widely available in many settings. The treatment of melioidosis involves two main phases: an intensive phase with parenteral antibiotics to prevent mortality from severe illness, followed by an eradication phase with oral antibiotics to prevent relapse. Intravenous ceftazidime or meropenem forms the backbone of intensive phase therapy in melioidosis. In severe disease, including septic shock or deep-seated infections, meropenem is often preferred due to its enhanced bactericidal activity and better tissue penetration, to achieve adequate drug exposure at the site of infection. The choice of antibiotic and dosing was guided by disease severity and site of infection [4, 8, 18]. Empirical antibiotic therapy was subsequently de-escalated based on microbiological confirmation to ensure appropriate antimicrobial stewardship and reduce the risk of emergence of multidrug-resistant organisms.

For critically ill patients or those with central nervous system (CNS) involvement, meropenem is generally preferred over ceftazidime due to improved clinical outcomes. In patients with neurological involvement or deep-seated infection, combination therapy with trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX) may be added during the intensive phase to improve tissue penetration [8]. For non-critically ill patients without CNS infection, ceftazidime is effective, given at 50 mg/kg dose every 6–8 h. For non-pulmonary focal infections, such as those affecting the nervous system or prostatic tissue, the addition of TMP-SMX to the initial antibiotic regimen is recommended, as it offers excellent tissue penetration. The duration of the initial antibiotic therapy is generally at least 14 days, with longer courses indicated in more complicated or deep-seated infections. If blood cultures remain positive after 7 days or clinical deterioration occurs, further adjustments to the treatment are needed. Following the intensive phase, an oral regimen of TMP-SMX for at least three months is recommended to prevent relapse, with the dose typically 320 mg of the trimethoprim component orally twice daily for adults [8].

Prognosis is generally favourable with appropriate therapy, with reported mortality rates of less than 10% in some settings [9, 18].

However, it is higher in patients with neuromelioidosis and those with underlying conditions such as diabetes, chronic kidney disease, and alcohol use. Prevention in endemic areas includes avoiding skin contact with soil and surface water, particularly during the wet season and severe weather events [18]. Relapse can occur, particularly when eradication therapy is incomplete; therefore, early diagnosis and appropriate initiation of therapy are essential to reduce the risk of recurrence and improve clinical outcomes. The median duration from symptom onset to hospital presentation was approximately 10 days, whereas the median time to initiation of targeted therapy was 4 days, indicating delayed presentation and additional delay related to microbiological confirmation [Table 2]. Notably, early initiation of therapy based on clinical suspicion in one case reduced this delay. This case series has certain limitations. Being retrospective in nature, standardized severity scoring systems such as SOFA or APACHE II were not consistently available. Additionally, detailed clinical timelines and time-to-treatment analysis could not be systematically assessed, which may limit objective comparison of disease severity and outcomes across cases. Furthermore, the duration of intensive phase therapy in cases with neurological involvement was shorter than current guideline recommendations, as treatment was individualized based on clinical response and logistical considerations.

Conclusion

This case series highlights the protean manifestations and aggressive clinical course of melioidosis, underscoring its capacity to mimic a wide spectrum of infectious and inflammatory conditions. Through five diverse presentations ranging from liver abscess and pneumonia to neuromelioidosis and fulminant septic shock with multi-organ dysfunction, it demonstrates the unpredictable nature of Burkholderia pseudomallei infection, particularly in patients with underlying comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus and chronic kidney disease. Melioidosis remains an underdiagnosed yet potentially lethal disease. Heightened awareness among clinicians, especially in regions with rising incidence, coupled with robust microbiological support and adherence to treatment protocols, can significantly reduce morbidity and mortality. Early diagnosis, aggressive targeted therapy, and meticulous follow-up remain the cornerstones of improving outcomes in this formidable infection.
