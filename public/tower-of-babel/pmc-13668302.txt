Clinical, safety, and patient-reported outcomes associated with second-line and later therapies in primary biliary cholangitis: A systematic review
Authors: Marco Carbone, David Jones, Oyinkan Solanke, Rikke Brandt, Taryn Krause, Darren Asquith, Alex Pashley, Sonia Shaw, Emma Worthington, Jörn M Schattenberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Primary biliary cholangitis (PBC) is a rare, chronic, cholestatic disease that can result in cirrhosis, liver failure, and death. While biochemical markers such as alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and total bilirubin (TB) measure disease activity and progression, definitions of biochemical response vary. This systematic literature review (SLR) identified clinical studies for investigational and approved treatments in patients with PBC with inadequate response or intolerance to ursodeoxycholic acid requiring second-line (2L) or later (2L+) therapies.Methods:Electronic databases were searched on August 7, 2025 (MEDLINE; Embase) or September 30, 2025 (Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews; CENTRAL), and conference proceedings (2021–2025), bibliographies, and ClinicalTrials.gov were searched on August 15, 2025, following a pre-specified protocol (PROSPERO ID: CRD42023382262). Interventional and observational studies in the 2L/2L+ setting were prioritized for inclusion, and randomized controlled trials for extraction.Results:A total of 149 publications on 44 unique studies were included. Phase III trials evaluated elafibranor, seladelpar, budenoside, bezafibrate, and obeticholic acid. Various composite biochemical response endpoints were reported across 34% of studies, most frequently the POISE criteria (18%). Biochemical markers were assessed frequently (ALP: 89%; TB: 71%); however, upper limits of normal and response thresholds varied substantially. Response rates in placebo-controlled trials favored active treatment, although placebo responses were highly variable. Non-invasive fibrosis measures were reported in 16% of studies and showed small short-term changes. Patient-reported outcomes assessed pruritus (52%) and fatigue (32%) using a variety of scales.Conclusion:The evidence base for 2L/2L+ PBC therapies is extensive but heterogeneous in study designs, patient populations, and treatment assessment scales and outcome definitions, limiting cross-trial comparisons. This SLR provides a robust foundation for contextualizing study outcomes within the broader landscape and supporting clinical decision-making.Keywords: biliary, biological markers, cholangitis, clinical decision-making, fibrosis, pruritus, research design, second-line therapy

INTRODUCTION

Primary biliary cholangitis (PBC) is a rare, chronic, cholestatic liver disease characterized by the autoimmune destruction of intrahepatic bile ducts, resulting in cirrhosis and fibrosis.1,2 The pathology of PBC is largely unknown, and both environmental and genetic factors have been associated with increased risk of developing the disease.

3 PBC predominantly affects women aged ≥40 years, and while estimates of global PBC prevalence vary greatly, ranging from 1.91 to 40.2 per 100,000 individuals, global prevalence has increased over time.1,4,5,6,7 Fatigue and pruritus are the most common symptoms of PBC and can significantly reduce health-related quality of life (HRQoL).8 In addition, patients with PBC may be at a higher risk of cardiovascular, gastrointestinal, bone, and mental health disorders compared with the general population.9

Diagnosis of PBC is based on the presence of liver tests indicative of cholestatic hepatitis, particularly elevated levels of alkaline phosphatase (ALP), as well as serological positivity for anti-mitochondrial antibodies (AMA).1 Biochemical markers of cholestasis, such as elevated ALP, total bilirubin (TB), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and aspartate transaminase (AST), are used individually or as composite measures in clinical trials and are key indicators of disease stage and progression.10,11,12 In particular, ALP and TB are increasingly being recognized as markers to predict long-term outcomes in PBC, linked to an increased risk of liver transplantation and death,10,11 and often serve as primary efficacy endpoints in clinical trials of emerging PBC therapies.1,10,13 Beyond these biochemical markers, assessment of liver fibrosis and cirrhosis is required to fully understand the effects of individual treatments.

Ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) is currently the only approved first-line (1L) treatment for PBC. However, its biochemical efficacy is variable, with around 40% of patients experiencing an inadequate response that does not effectively prevent disease progression or poor clinical outcomes, and 3%–5% of patients have intolerable side effects.12,14,15 For patients who have an inadequate response to, or are intolerant of UDCA, treatments used in the second-line (2L) and later (2L+) settings include obeticholic acid (OCA) and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) agonists [elafibranor and seladelpar, and unlicensed fibrates (eg, fenofibrate and bezafibrate)].1,16,17 Both elafibranor and seladelpar have been recently approved as 2L treatments for PBC in several regions.18,19,20,21,22,23,24 These approvals were based on results of the phase III ELATIVE and RESPONSE trials, respectively, representing the first approved treatments in PBC in ~8 years.15,25,26 Before this, OCA, a selective farnesoid X receptor agonist, was approved as a 2L treatment for PBC in 2016.27,28 However, in 2024, the European Medicines Agency (EMA) concluded that the available evidence did not sufficiently confirm that the benefits of OCA outweigh its risks, resulting in its marketing authorization being revoked in the European Union (EU).29,30,31 OCA has since been voluntarily withdrawn from the United States, following a request from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA).32

There are currently a number of definitions of biochemical response that are used in clinical trial settings and in real-world practice in PBC. In addition, several patient-reported outcomes (PROs) are used to assess changes in critical components of PBC, including fatigue, pruritus, and HRQoL. Therefore, the evidence across different aspects of treatment effects and how they have been measured needs to be considered together to decipher any differences in therapeutic profiles and to evaluate potential benefits of PBC therapies, highlighting the need for comprehensive, evidence-based frameworks to inform decision-making. This systematic literature review (SLR) aimed to identify and provide a comprehensive evidence base of currently available clinical efficacy and safety data for investigational and approved treatments in the 2L/2L+ settings.

METHODS

Literature searches

This SLR was conducted with a pre-specified protocol, registered on PROSPERO (ID: CRD42023382262), following current best practices as detailed in the University of York Centre for Reviews and Dissemination’s (CRD) Guidance for Undertaking Reviews in Health Care.33 The original SLR was conducted in November 2022 and subsequently underwent 3 updates (in December 2023, June 2024, and August/September 2025, respectively).

Electronic databases were searched from their inception date (Supplemental Tables S1–S7, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C483), and hand-searching of key conference proceedings (from 2021 to 2025) and the bibliographies of any relevant systematic reviews and (network) meta-analyses (NMAs) were carried out. Conference proceedings before 2021 were not searched on the assumption that high-quality research would have since been published in full in a peer-reviewed journal.

For the original SLR, MEDLINE and Embase were searched via the OvidSP platform. The Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews (CDSR) and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) were searched through the Cochrane Library via the Wiley Online platform, and the Database of Abstracts of Reviews of Effects (DARE) was searched via the University of York CRD platform. All searches were conducted on November 21, 2022. For SLR Update I, MEDLINE and Embase were searched separately via the OvidSP Platform; CDSR and CENTRAL were searched through the Cochrane Library via the Wiley Online platform on December 18, 2023. For SLR Update II, MEDLINE, Embase, CDSR, and CENTRAL were searched simultaneously on June 19, 2024, via the OvidSP platform. For SLR Update III, MEDLINE and Embase were searched via the OvidSP Platform on August 7, 2025, and CDSR and CENTRAL were searched via the OvidSP Platform on September 30, 2025.

Supplementary searches of conferences, reference lists, and ClinicalTrials.gov were also conducted (Supplemental Tables S8 and S9, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C483).

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion and exclusion criteria were developed in line with the Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcomes (PICO) framework, as detailed in Table 1. Due to the high volume of publications identified, a two-stage prioritization strategy was applied to focus on the extraction of the evidence needed to support a health technology assessment (HTA) submission for elafibranor in PBC, by focusing on randomized controlled trials in the 2L/2L+ setting (Table 1). Each abstract and full text was screened against the eligibility criteria by 2 independent reviewers, and any disagreements were resolved by discussion, with a third reviewer if necessary, until a final decision was made.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Information and data, including study designs and characteristics, patient characteristics, efficacy, safety, liver fibrosis, and symptom-related and HRQoL-related PROs, were extracted into a pre-specified Microsoft Excel grid, in line with the University of York CRD guidelines.33 Absolute and relative change from baseline values and p-values were extracted, where reported.

The quality of all included studies was assessed using the quality assessment tool developed by the University of York CRD for randomized controlled trials (RCTs).34

Data extraction and quality assessment processes were conducted by a single reviewer and verified by a second reviewer. Any discrepancies were resolved by discussion, with a third reviewer if necessary, until a consensus was reached.

Overall, 149 publications reporting on 44 unique studies were prioritized for extraction in the SLR; a full list of included studies is included in Supplemental Table S10, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C483. In the original SLR, 353 publications were included, of which 94 publications reporting on 36 unique studies were prioritized for extraction. In SLR Update I, 48 additional publications were included, of which 17 novel publications reporting on 5 unique studies were prioritized for extraction. In SLR Update II, a total of 42 additional publications were included, of which 14 publications reporting on 1 unique study and 6 studies already captured in the SLR were prioritized for extraction. In SLR Update III, a total of 68 additional publications were included, of which 24 publications reporting on 2 novel studies and 6 studies already captured in the SLR were prioritized for extraction (Figure 2).

Study characteristics

An overview of the designs of the prioritized studies included in the SLR is presented in Table 2. In line with the prioritization strategy, all studies were RCTs in the 2L/2L+ treatment setting (n=44), of which 33 (75.6%) were published after 2010. Among the studies included, 20 were multi-center across multiple countries, 11 were multi-center within a single country, 5 were single-center, and the remaining 8 did not report study location. Seventeen studies were phase II, 6 were phase III, 3 were phase IV studies, and 18 did not report the study phase.

The most frequently reported interventions were bezafibrate (n=9), OCA (n=4), and seladelpar (n=4). Colchicine, elafibranor, fenofibrate, linerixibat, and methotrexate were all reported twice, and other drugs, including budesonide, saroglitazar, and setanaxib, were reported in one study each (Table 2). Studies had variable sample sizes ranging from 10 to 334 patients.

Patient population

Across the 44 studies, 40 reported sex, 17 reported race, and all reported age. Most patients were female (range: 60%–100%), predominantly White (range: 63%–100%), and the mean age range was 45.0–64.0 years (Supplemental Table S11, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C483).

Baseline disease characteristics

Disease staging at baseline was reported in 13 studies (29.5%; Supplemental Table S11, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C483), with several different scales used. The Ludwig scale and Scheuer scale were reported in 3 and 6 studies, respectively.41,43,45,46,50,53,54,59,60 The Nakamura scale was reported in 1 study,53,58and the method of disease staging was not reported in 3 studies.38,42,48 Disease duration before study entry was not consistently reported [16 studies (36.4%)], with a mean disease duration range of 2.7–12.8 years.53,58 Serum levels of biochemical markers were measured at baseline for ALP [n=38 studies (86.4%)], TB [n=33 (75%)], GGT [n=22 (50%)], and albumin [n=19 (48.2%)].61 Baseline values of measures of pruritus and fatigue were not as frequently reported [n=14 (34.1%), n=4 (9.1%), respectively].

Treatment history

Treatment history was reported in the majority of studies [32 studies (72.7%)], where concomitant UDCA treatment was reported in 31 studies (70.5%), with 81%–100% of patients receiving UDCA during the clinical trial period. Baseline daily UDCA dosage was reported in 20 studies (45.5%) with the mean dose ranging from 10.0 mg/kg to 17.3 mg/kg (Supplemental Table S12, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C483). Previous treatment with OCA and fibrates was reported in 3 (6.8%) and 2 studies (4.5%), respectively.

Biochemical response

Composite biochemical response endpoints were reported in 15 studies (34.1%; Table 3). Of these, 12 studies (27.3%) specified the criteria used to define response (Table 3), where 5 studies (18.2%) used multiple criteria.

Upper limit of normal (ULN) values for biochemical markers that were used in definitions of response were reported in 5 out of 6 phase III studies and varied between studies. For example, the ULN value for ALP was defined as 104 U/L for women and 129 U/L for men in Schattenberg 2021 (elafibranor), ELATIVE (elafibranor), and Hirschfield 2021 (budesonide),15,40,51,62 150 U/L in ENHANCE (seladelpar),63 and 116 U/L in RESPONSE (seladelpar).26 The ULN values for other biochemical markers, such as TB and ALT, also differed across studies (Supplemental Table S13, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C483).

POISE criteria, defined as ALP <1.67×ULN, with a reduction of ≤15% from baseline, and TB ≤ULN, were the most commonly used criteria, reported in 8 studies (18.2%), all of which were phase II (5 studies) or phase III (3 studies). Paris I criteria (defined as ALP <3.0×ULN, AST <2.0×ULN, and bilirubin <1 mg/dL) were reported in 5 studies (11.4%), and Paris II criteria (defined as ALP <1.5×ULN, AST <1.5×ULN, and bilirubin <1 mg/dL) in 6 studies (13.6%; Table 3). Barcelona criteria (defined as ALP normalization or >40% reduction) were reported in 4 studies (9.1%; Table 3). Toronto criteria (defined as ALP ≤1.67×ULN by 2 years of treatment), Toronto I criteria (defined as ALP <1.67×ULN), and Toronto II criteria (ALP <1.76×ULN) were reported in 2 studies (4.5%) each (Table 3). Other criteria, including complete biochemical response, modified versions of Toronto I, combinations of normalization of ALT, AST, GGT, or TB levels, and biochemical remission, were also reported.

While all placebo-controlled studies demonstrated a higher percentage of responders in treatment groups compared with placebo groups, notably, rates of response in placebo groups were highly variable between studies (Table 3).

Biochemical measures

ALP, TB, GGT, and albumin were the most commonly reported biochemical markers. Other measures included AST, ALT, indirect and conjugated bilirubin, serum tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), platelet counts, triglycerides, bile acid composition, IgA, IgG, and IgM, and changes in prothrombin.

Alkaline phosphatase

ALP was reported in 39 studies (88.6%; Table 4), of which 10 (22.7%) reported absolute baseline and end-of-study values. Changes from baseline in ALP, expressed as absolute and/or relative values, were reported across most treatments; changes were reported as mean values in 20 studies (43.2%) and as median values in 5 studies (11.4%; Table 4).

Total bilirubin

TB was reported in 31 studies (70.5%), of which 30 (68.2%) studies reported baseline and endpoint values, and/or change from baseline values (Table 4). Albumin

Albumin was reported in 12 studies (27.3%); change from baseline was reported in 10 studies, and only baseline and/or endpoint values were reported in 2 studies (Table 5). Pruritus

Alongside changes in biochemical markers and measures of liver fibrosis, PROs are available to evaluate treatment effects on debilitating symptoms of PBC, including pruritus. Pruritus PROs were reported in 23 studies (52.3%) using a variety of assessment tools to measure the severity or intensity of pruritus, or the impact of pruritus on patients’ HRQoL (Table 7). The visual analog scale (VAS) and the Worst-Itch Numeric Rating Scale (WI NRS) were the most frequently used to measure itch intensity, reported in 12 studies (27.3%) and 6 studies (13.6%), respectively (Table 7). In addition, the Itch domain of the PBC-40 and the 5-dimension (5D)-Itch questionnaire were most frequently used to measure pruritus-related impact on HRQoL, reported in 14 studies (31.8%) and 8 studies (18.2%), respectively (Table 7). Other measures, including bothersome itch, itch intensity (NRS), itch reporter outcome (RO), Patient Global Impression of Change (PGIC) and Severity (PGIS), monthly itch score, and sleep interference NRS, were less commonly used.

Fatigue

Fatigue PROs were reported in 14 (31.8%) studies. Long-term outcomes

Event-free survival (EFS) was reported in 1 (2.3) study, and mortality was reported in 18 studies (40.9%). Overall survival (OS) was reported in 2 studies (4.5%), and transplant-free survival (TFS) was reported in 3 studies each (2.3%).37

Safety outcomes

The incidence of adverse events (AEs) and serious AEs were reported in 23 (52.3%) and 25 (56.8%) studies, respectively. Treatment discontinuation due to AEs was reported in 22 studies (50.0%), and severe AEs were reported in 9 studies (20.5%). While safety outcomes may vary by drug class, gastrointestinal AEs were most commonly reported across all interventions, particularly diarrhea [n=23 studies (52.8%)], nausea [22 studies (50.0%)], and abdominal pain in 22 studies (50.0%). Neurological AEs of headache [n=23 (52.3%)], fatigue [n=21 (47.7%)], and dizziness [n=15 (34.9%)], as well as dermatological AEs of pruritus [n=21 (47.7%)] and rash [n=10 (22.7%)] were also commonly reported. Fractures were reported in 6 studies (13.6%).

Hepatic AEs of autoimmune hepatitis, hepatic cirrhosis, hepatic injury, hepatic failure, abnormal hepatic function, and hepatic encephalopathy were listed in 1 study (2.3%) each. Renal AEs, including acute kidney injury and renal impairment/dysfunction, were listed in 2 studies each (4.8%). Elevated transaminases were listed in 2 studies (4.5%), and hepatic enzyme increases were listed in 4 studies (9.1%). However, AEs of special interest data were not extracted in full.

Quality assessment

A summary of the quality assessment conducted for each study is presented in Supplemental Table S14, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C483. Appropriate randomization was reported in 22 studies (50.0%), and 5 additional studies (11.4%) reported randomization with no further details. Treatment allocation concealment was reported in 20 studies (45.5%), and blinding of outcome assessors was reported in 28 studies (63.6%).

DISCUSSION

This SLR captured a large body of relevant RCT data (149 publications reporting 44 unique studies) on the PBC treatment landscape in the 2L/2L+ setting, which provided a comprehensive evidence base for this review.

Several key biochemical markers of cholestasis were commonly reported across studies, including ALP, TB, and GGT. ALP is an integral component of various composite response criteria, and changes in ALP alone (presented as absolute change over time or relative changes from baseline), as well as ALP normalization, were often used as key endpoints. This reporting is in line with a shifting paradigm from using reduction to set multiples of ULN values (eg, 1.67×ULN) towards normalization as a treatment target. Assessment of biochemical markers varied across studies, with differences in assays used and definitions of ULN (Supplemental Table S13, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C483). Reporting of each biochemical marker also differed, with some studies reporting baseline and endpoint values, and others reporting percentage change or categorical changes from baseline, or normalization. In addition, baseline biochemistries varied markedly across study populations. As treatment response endpoints are often defined by reaching a set threshold of biochemical markers, higher baseline values and lower ULN values yield a lower likelihood of achieving a response because the relative reduction required to reach the threshold for response is larger.61 This effect is also evidenced by the variability in placebo responses observed in the RCTs captured here, highlighting the impact that differing baseline values and definitions of response may have on the interpretation of clinical data. Altogether, this variability presents challenges in evaluating the biochemical efficacy of different treatments in PBC, particularly given the lack of head-to-head trials for treatments in PBC.

Assessment of liver fibrosis and cirrhosis, alongside cholestatic and liver function markers, is useful to understand the potential impact of individual treatments on disease progression; NITs for fibrosis can provide some indication of a treatment effect. ELF, LSM, and PRO-C3 were the most commonly used NITs across the included studies; however, they were reported in less than a third of RCTs (n=11). Notably, among the 7 studies that reported LSM, values were not available for all patients included in these studies due to the limitations of costs, laboratory equipment, capabilities, and availability of technologies required for LSM evaluation (eg, FibroScan). Given that the included studies were largely RCTs, marked changes in NITs would not be expected within their relatively short timeframes, and the effects of novel treatments on disease progression and clinical outcomes, measured by progression-free survival (PFS) and TFS, will be established in ongoing open-label studies and with real-world data in the long term.67,68,69

Alongside improvements in liver biochemistry and slowing disease progression as a treatment goal in PBC, improvement in symptom burden has increasingly become a key focus for newer therapies. Pruritus and fatigue were the most often evaluated symptoms, but were not consistently reported. In addition, the PRO measures used to evaluate pruritus and fatigue, as well as the approaches to reporting these data, varied across studies, highlighting differences across studies in the evaluation of symptom burden.

Reported AEs were commonly gastrointestinal in nature, in line with the pathology of PBC, and treatment discontinuations due to AEs were not frequent. Notably, patient populations differed across studies due to differences in study design, disease characteristics, and eligibility criteria. Furthermore, potential variations in how AEs were defined and reported, relatively small population sizes, and contraindications of some drugs in different patients may influence AE outcomes; therefore, safety profiles across treatments cannot be compared.

The variability in study designs, timepoints of assessment, baseline patient and disease characteristics, and response endpoints, as evidenced in this review, as well as the lack of head-to-head trials, highlights the fragmented evidence base of current treatments in PBC. Consequently, clinicians face challenges in applying clinical trial results to inform personalized treatment decisions for individual patients. This heterogeneity of endpoints for clinical, safety, and PRO outcomes across studies emphasizes the importance of efforts such as the EASL/AASLD joint program for Global Consensus PBC Endpoints, which outlines consensus recommendations on endpoints, study of symptoms, and use of real-world evidence to advance the development of reliable surrogate endpoints for RCTs in PBC.70,71

Strengths and limitations

In this review, multiple electronic databases and supplementary reference lists of relevant SLRs and NMAs were searched following a systematic approach. Two reviewers performed each stage of study selection independently to minimize selection bias and ensure all relevant literature was captured and included. While the prioritization strategies implemented may have led to the exclusion of potentially relevant studies, they ensured that only the most pertinent evidence for the purpose of the review was included, given the high volume of literature available. The pre-defined protocol detailed the robust methodology employed here and enables systematic updating of the SLR in the future.

CONCLUSION

This SLR provides a comprehensive evidence base of key clinical efficacy and safety data for 2L/2L+ treatments in PBC. Due to the heterogeneity in study designs, response endpoints, eligibility criteria, and reporting, as well as in patient populations, direct comparisons of the relative effectiveness and safety of these therapies are limited. Furthermore, while some validated disease-specific PRO tools exist, heterogeneity in the selection and application of different measurement scales across studies introduces substantial variation in treatment outcome reporting. In light of this fragmented evidence base, further research is needed to inform decision-making in the context of the current 2L/2L+ landscape in PBC.
