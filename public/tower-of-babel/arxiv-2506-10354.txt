Revisiting mean estimation over ℓ p \ell_{p} balls: Is the MLE optimal?
Liviu Aolaritei ,, Michael I. Jordan ,, Reese Pathak ,, Annie Ulichney ,

Abstract
We revisit the problem of mean estimation in the Gaussian sequence model with ℓ p \ell_{p} constraints for p ∈ [ 0 , ∞ ] p\in[0,\infty] . We demonstrate two phenomena for the behavior of the maximum likelihood estimator (MLE), which depend on the noise level, the radius of the (quasi)norm constraint, the dimension, and the norm index p p . First, if p p lies between 0 0 and 1 + Θ ⁡ ( 1 log ⁡ d ) 1+\Theta(\tfrac{1}{\log d}) , inclusive, or if it is greater than or equal to 2 2 , the MLE is minimax rate-optimal for all noise levels and all constraint radii. On the other hand, for the remaining norm indices—namely, if p p lies between 1 + Θ ⁡ ( 1 log ⁡ d ) 1+\Theta(\tfrac{1}{\log d}) and 2 2 —there is a more striking behavior: the MLE is minimax rate- sub optimal, despite its nonlinearity in the observations, for essentially all noise levels and constraint radii for which nonlinear estimates are necessary for minimax-optimal estimation. Our results imply that when given n n independent and identically distributed Gaussian samples, the MLE can be suboptimal by a polynomial factor in the sample size. Our lower bounds are constructive: whenever the MLE is rate-suboptimal, we provide explicit instances on which the MLE provably incurs suboptimal risk. Finally, in the non-convex case—namely when p < 1 p<1 —we develop sharp local Gaussian width bounds, which may be of independent interest.

Introduction

This paper revisits the classical problem of mean estimation in the Gaussian sequence model in
\mathbf{R}^{d}
, subject to
\ell_{p}
(quasi)norm constraints.
Specifically, suppose we are given a Gaussian observation,

Y=\theta^{\star}+\sigma\xi,\quad\mbox{where}\quad\|\theta^{\star}\|_{p}\leqslant r,\quad\mbox{and}\quad\xi\sim\mathsf{N}\left(0,I_{d}\right).

(1)

We aim to estimate
\theta^{\star}
to optimal mean squared error, depending on the dimension
d
, the norm index
p\in[0,\infty]
, noise level
\sigma>0
, and radius
r>0
.
This problem is most easily motivated by viewing the
\ell_{p}
constraint as a form of prior knowledge on regularity of the (unknown) parameter
\theta^{\star}
that we seek to estimate. For instance, depending on the choice of
p
, these constraints correspond to the assumption that
\theta^{\star}
is sparse or that
\theta^{\star}
does not have very large entries.

The “nonlinearity phenomenon”

The seminal work of Donoho and Johnstone

Nonlinear and linear minimax risks:

To describe their result, recall the
(nonlinear) minimax mean squared error (MSE) for the model (
1
). It is given by

\mathcal{M}(\mathsf{B}^{d}_{p},\sigma)\coloneq\inf_{\widehat{\theta}}\sup_{\theta^{\star}\in\mathsf{B}^{d}_{p}}\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits_{Y\sim\mathsf{N}\left(\theta^{\star},\sigma^{2}I_{d}\right)}\Big[\|\widehat{\theta}(Y)-\theta^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\Big].

(2)

The infimum above is taken over all (measurable) functions
\widehat{\theta}\colon\mathbf{R}^{d}\to\mathbf{R}^{d}
. In contrast, the
linear
minimax MSE further restricts the infimum to mappings
Y\mapsto\widehat{\theta}(Y)
which are linear,

{\mathcal{M}^{\mathsf{L}}}(\mathsf{B}^{d}_{p},\sigma)\coloneq\inf_{\widehat{\theta}~\text{linear}}\;\sup_{\theta^{\star}\in\mathsf{B}^{d}_{p}}\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits_{Y\sim\mathsf{N}\left(\theta^{\star},\sigma^{2}I_{d}\right)}\Big[\|\widehat{\theta}(Y)-\theta^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\Big].

(3)

Of course, we have
\mathcal{M}\leqslant{\mathcal{M}^{\mathsf{L}}}
—the supremum risk of an optimal linear estimator bounds the minimax MSE from above. The
competitive ratio
for linear estimates, defined below, quantifies by how much the risk degrades when we restrict to linear estimators,

\mathfrak{C}^{\mathsf{L}}(\mathsf{B}^{d}_{p},\sigma)\coloneq\frac{{\mathcal{M}^{\mathsf{L}}}(\mathsf{B}^{d}_{p},\sigma)}{\mathcal{M}(\mathsf{B}^{d}_{p},\sigma)}.

(4)

Indeed, when
\mathfrak{C}^{\mathsf{L}}<\infty
, it provides the smallest constant
C\geqslant 1

such that
{\mathcal{M}^{\mathsf{L}}}\leqslant C\;\mathcal{M}
. That is, the competitive ratio quantifies the multiplicative gap in the error of the best linear estimate to that of a minimax optimal estimator, which is allowed to be (and often must be) nonlinear.

1.1.1
The necessity of nonlinear estimates

Donoho and Johnstone demonstrated
[
9
, Theorem 1]
that the competitive ratio for linear estimators satisfies

\lim_{d\to\infty}\mathfrak{C}^{\mathsf{L}}(\mathsf{B}^{d}_{p},\sigma)=\begin{cases}1,&\mbox{if}~2\leqslant p<\infty\\
\infty,&\mbox{if}~0<p<2\end{cases},

(5a)

where in the limit above, the noise level is taken as
\sigma\equiv\sigma(d)\coloneq C/\sqrt{d}
with
C>0
being any fixed constant. Furthermore, by Theorem 5 in the paper
[
9
]
, for
p\geqslant 2
, the competitive ratio satisfies, for sufficiently large
d
,

\mathfrak{C}^{\mathsf{L}}(\mathsf{B}^{d}_{p},\sigma)\asymp 1\quad\mbox{for all}~\sigma>0.

(5b)

Simply put, the results above demonstrate the following dichotomy:
if
p\geqslant 2
, then by relation (
5b
), linear estimates are—disregarding constant factors—minimax rate-optimal. On the other hand, if
p<2
, then the limit relation (
5a
) provides a sequence of noise levels for which linear estimates are suboptimal by a multiplicative factor that diverges as the dimension grows.

In fact, the suboptimality indicated by the limit relation (
5a
)—in the case the norm index satisfies
p\in(0,2)
—is even more striking. Indeed, it holds for a wide variety of scalings of the noise level.
To be more precise, Theorem 5 in the paper
[
9
]
and its strengthening via the results in the monograph
[
17
]
, refine the relations (
5
) as follows.
First, in sufficiently low noise or high noise, linear estimates are order-optimal. More precisely,
the relation (
5b
) extends to
p\in(0,2)
in that

\limsup_{d\to\infty}\mathfrak{C}^{\mathsf{L}}(\mathsf{B}^{d}_{p},\sigma)\asymp 1,\quad\mbox{if}~\sigma\ll\frac{1}{d^{1/p}}~\mbox{or}~\sigma\gg\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log d}}.

(6a)

In contrast, for a large variety of “moderate” noise levels, linear estimates are increasingly suboptimal as the dimension grows. More precisely,
the suboptimality result (
5a
) extends to other noise levels in the case
p\in(0,2)
as follows,

\lim_{d\to\infty}\mathfrak{C}^{\mathsf{L}}(\mathsf{B}^{d}_{p},\sigma)=\infty,\quad\mbox{if}~\sigma\gg\frac{1}{d^{1/p}}~\mbox{and}~\sigma\ll\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log d}}.

(6b)

To establish these results, Donoho and Johnstone show that the soft thresholding (ST) estimator, given below with its carefully chosen regularization level, achieves the minimax MSE
[
9
, Theorem 3]

\widehat{\theta}^{\,\mathsf{ST}}(Y)\coloneq\mathop{\rm arg\,min}_{\vartheta\in\mathbf{R}^{d}}\Big\{\,\|\vartheta-Y\|_{2}^{2}+2\lambda\|\vartheta\|_{1}\,\Big\}\quad\mbox{with}\quad\lambda\coloneq\sqrt{2\sigma^{2}\log(d\sigma^{p})}.

(7)

Notably, the cases
\sigma\ll d^{-1/p}
or
\sigma\gg(\log d)^{-1/2}
are uninteresting for the
\ell_{p}
mean estimation problem in that
attaining the minimax rate is “easy.” More rigorously, in the former regime,
\widehat{\theta}(Y)=Y
is order-optimal, and in the latter regime,
\widehat{\theta}(Y)=0
is order-optimal. To emphasize, in these cases, knowledge of the
\ell_{p}
constraint does
not
meaningfully impact the rate of estimation.
In summary, the essential takeaway from these results of Donoho and Johnstone is:

If
p\in(0,2)
, then nonlinear estimates are required to achieve the minimax rate

whenever knowledge of the
\ell_{p}
constraint can be leveraged to improve the worst-case MSE.

We show that the MLE
\Pi_{\mathsf{B}^{d}_{p}}(Y)
incurs suboptimal risk when estimating sparse signals on the boundary of the

ball. In such cases, the MLE shrinks towards denser vectors to fit the noise, which, due to the
\ell_{p}
constraint, forces it to also shrink coordinates corresponding to the true signal. This tradeoff leads to estimation error exceeding the minimax rate. See Figure

for a schematic illustration of this phenomenon.

In order to quantify this level of shrinkage, we show that the maximum likelihood estimator is a form of data-dependent shrinkage estimator.
To be more precise, fix
\lambda,t>0
. Let
\Psi_{p,\lambda}(t)
denote the unique positive solution, in
\psi
, to the equation,

\psi+\lambda\psi^{p-1}=t.

Observe that
\Psi_{p,\lambda}(t)
is precisely the proximal operator
[
27
]
of the function
x\mapsto\tfrac{\lambda}{p}x^{p}
evaluated at
t\geqslant 0
:

\Psi_{p,\lambda}(t)=\operatorname{\bf prox}_{\frac{\lambda}{p}(\cdot)^{p}}(t)=\mathop{\rm arg\,min}_{x}\left\{\tfrac{1}{2}(x-t)^{2}+\tfrac{\lambda}{p}x^{p}\right\}.

Viewing
\lambda
as fixed,
\Psi_{p,\lambda}
can be interpreted as a nonlinear shrinkage operator applied to

. As we show next, the MLE can then be interpreted, coordinatewise, in terms of this operator evaluated at
\lambda=\lambda^{\star}
, where
\lambda^{\star}
is data-dependent. The following lemma will be instrumental in proving the suboptimality of the MLE.

Lemma 1

(Variational characterization of the MLE,
p\in(1,\infty)
)
.

Suppose that
p\in(1,\infty)
, and take the parameter space
\Theta
to be the
\ell_{p}
ball
\mathsf{B}^{d}_{p}
.
Then, the maximum likelihood estimator satisfies the coordinatewise relations

\Pi_{\Theta}(Y)_{i}=\sign(Y_{i})\,\Psi_{p,\lambda^{\star}}\big(|Y_{i}|\big),\hskip 11.49994pt\mbox{for}~i=1,\ldots,d.

(19)

Here,
\lambda^{\star}
is defined by

\lambda^{\star}=\inf\left\{\,\lambda\geqslant 0\mid\sum_{i=1}^{d}\Psi_{p,\lambda}\big(|Y_{i}|\big)^{p}\leqslant 1\,\right\}.

(20)

We establish Lemma
1
via convex duality. We now provide an overview of the ideas underlying the proofs of Propositions
1
and
2

General proof idea:

Proposition
1
and Proposition
2
both follow from a two-step argument outlined below. In each case, we construct a particular sparse signal of the form
\theta^{\star}=\frac{1}{k^{1/p}}(\mathbf{1}_{k},0_{d-k})
, where
k<\frac{d}{2}
depends on
(\sigma,p,d)
. The key idea in both proofs is to leverage the coordinatewise condition,

|Y_{i}|=|\Pi_{\mathsf{B}^{d}_{p}}(Y)_{i}|+\lambda^{\star}|\Pi_{\mathsf{B}^{d}_{p}}(Y)_{i}|^{p-1},\quad\mbox{for all}~i=1,\ldots,d,

(21)

derived from Lagrange duality (see the proof of Lemma
1
). These conditions allow us to relate the magnitude of the “noise-only” coordinates of
Y
to a lower bound on the Lagrange multiplier,
\lambda^{\star}
, which controls the amount of shrinkage. In turn, this determines the estimation error on the “signal-plus-noise” coordinates. Our argument proceeds in two steps:

(i)

We establish that
\lambda^{\star}
is large, using the “noise-only” coordinates (
i
.
e
., the last
(d-k)
entries of

); and,

(ii)

By (i), as
\lambda^{\star}
is large, we show that the MLE must incur large estimation error in the “signal-plus-noise” coordinates (
i
.
e
., the first
k
entries of
Y
).

We now provide further details on the specific construction and reasoning used in each proof.

Proof sketch for Proposition
1
:

The relatively large noise level (i.e.,
\sigma\geqslant\frac{c}{\sqrt{q}d^{1/q}}
, for a constant

\theta^{\star}=e_{1}
. If
\lambda^{\star}\geqslant 1
and

\Pi_{\mathsf{B}^{d}_{p}}(Y)_{1}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}
, which implies from the fixed point equation (
21

(\theta^{\star}_{1}-\Pi_{\mathsf{B}^{d}_{p}}(Y)_{1})^{2}\geqslant\frac{1}{4}
. This is precisely what we will prove. To see this, we first apply (
21
) and some algebraic manipulations to show that
\lambda^{\star}=\|Y-\Pi_{\mathsf{B}^{d}_{p}}(Y)\|_{q}
. Since the noise is sufficiently large, we see that with large probability
Y\not\in\Theta(1)\mathsf{B}^{d}_{q}
, but by definition,
\Pi_{\mathsf{B}^{d}_{p}}(Y)\in\mathsf{B}^{d}_{p}\subset\mathsf{B}^{d}_{q}
,
which yields
\lambda^{\star}\gtrsim 1
by the triangle inequality. We refer to Figure
3
for an illustration of the technical steps mentioned above.

An important observation is that with large probability, we have
Y\not\in\mathsf{B}^{d}_{p}
and hence
\Pi_{\mathsf{B}^{d}_{p}}(Y)
lies on the boundary of the ball
\mathsf{B}^{d}_{p}
. Nonetheless, in order to fit the noise coordinates, the MLE returns a vector which is denser than the true
\theta^{\star}
, thereby inducing suboptimal error on the sparse signal components. We now turn to Proposition
2
.

Proof sketch for Proposition
2
:

We follow the same general strategy as in Proposition
1
, but now we must take
k>1
in order to balance the signal sparsity with the noise magnitude, which are the two crucial components of this argument. In this regime, it becomes necessary to select the parameter
k
as a function of
(\sigma,p,d)
, specifically by

k=k(\sigma,p,d)\asymp\Big(\frac{1}{\sigma^{q}d}\Big)^{\tfrac{p-1}{2-p}}\lor 1.

Recalling that
k
is the sparsity of
\theta^{\star}
, note that as
\sigma\to d^{-1/q}
this formula shows we are selecting sparser
\theta^{\star}
, since
k\to\Theta(1)
. On the other hand, as
\sigma\to d^{-1/p}
, we are selecting denser
\theta^{\star}
, since
k\to\Theta(d)
.
As a result, the hardest instance for MLE lies on the boundary of
\mathsf{B}^{d}_{p}
but is denser as the noise level shrinks. Having selected this choice of
k
, the argument now proceeds as follows. Though the noise level can be smaller in this setting, we still have
\|Y\|_{p}>1
with probability
\Omega(1)
, so the estimator lies on the boundary of the constraint set. The core idea is to show—via a pigeonhole-type argument—that there exists a sufficiently large subset of the “noise-only” coordinates in which the noise magnitude forces
\lambda^{\star}\gtrsim\sigma d^{1/q}
. Then, using a fixed-point argument and leveraging the identity (
21
), we conclude that the total estimation error in the “signal-plus-noise” coordinates is of order
\sigma d^{1/q}
for small
\sigma
and of constant order when
\sigma
is larger.

Worst-case risk of MLE, minimax risk, and competitive ratio:

The maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) for

\widehat{\mu}^{\sf MLE}(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})=\mathop{\rm arg\,min}_{\mu\in r\mathsf{B}^{d}_{p}}\Big\{\,\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|Y_{i}-\mu\|_{2}^{2}\,\Big\},

\mathcal{R}^{\mathsf{MLE}}_{n}(r\mathsf{B}^{d}_{p},\tau)\coloneq\sup_{\mu^{\star}\in r\mathsf{B}^{d}_{p}}\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits_{\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathsf{N}\left(\mu^{\star},\tau^{2}I_{d}\right)}\Big[\,\|\widehat{\mu}^{\sf MLE}(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})-\mu^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\,\Big].

\mathcal{M}_{n}(r\mathsf{B}^{d}_{p},\tau)\coloneq\inf_{\widehat{\mu}}\sup_{\mu^{\star}\in r\mathsf{B}^{d}_{p}}\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits_{\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathsf{N}\left(\mu^{\star},\tau^{2}I_{d}\right)}\Big[\,\|\widehat{\mu}(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})-\mu^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\,\Big].

\mathfrak{C}^{\mathsf{MLE}}_{n}(r\mathsf{B}^{d}_{p},\tau)\coloneq\frac{\mathcal{R}^{\mathsf{MLE}}_{n}(r\mathsf{B}^{d}_{p},\tau)}{\mathcal{M}_{n}(r\mathsf{B}^{d}_{p},\tau)}.

The minimax risk and the supremum risk of the MLE satisfy, respectively,

\mathcal{M}_{n}\asymp\frac{1}{n^{(1-\delta)/4}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathcal{R}^{\mathsf{MLE}}_{n}\asymp 1.

(33b)

Thus, the factor by which the MLE is suboptimal is given by

\mathfrak{C}^{\mathsf{MLE}}_{n}=\frac{\mathcal{R}^{\mathsf{MLE}}_{n}}{\mathcal{M}_{n}}\asymp n^{(1-\delta)/4}\to\infty,\quad\mbox{as}~n\to\infty,

again as Corollary
4
(i)
predicts.
∎

Note that the relations (
32
) and (
33
) were obtained by leveraging the analysis in Propositions
1
and Propositions
2
as well as the reductions from
n
samples to a single sample, as discussed in Section
3.2.1
above. We emphasize here the value of these nonasymptotic bounds; while Corollary
4
illustrates that the competitive ratio diverges upward as the dimension grows, by using the nonasymptotic bounds we gain further information: the rate at which this divergence occurs.

3.3
Connections with prior work

We discuss some connections between our work and the prior work on estimation over
\ell_{p}
balls and related work on the maximum likelihood estimator.

Estimation over
\ell_{p}
balls:

As mentioned in the introduction, the work of Donoho and Johnstone
[
9
]
revealed a
phase transition in which nonlinear estimates are necessary for minimax optimal estimation for
p<2
; see Section
1.1
for further details. Their work built on a literature studying the rate-optimality
[
25
,
16
,
11
,
10
]
and rate-suboptimality
[
28
]
of linear procedures in various statistical estimation problems.

The survey paper
[
38
]
also gives an account of some extensions of these results to other sparse sets of parameters. The monograph
[
17
]
additionally gave extensions of the results in
[
9
]
, including some nonasymptotic results. They also provide a detailed analysis of hard- and soft-thresholded estimates in the Gaussian sequence model.

The very recent paper
[
26
, Section 3.2.6]
studies the suboptimality of MLE, and demonstrates that for
p\in(1,2)
which is fixed, and independent of the dimension
d
, that MLE over
\ell_{p}
balls is minimax rate-suboptimal for the particular choice of
\sigma=d^{1-1/p}
, and for sufficiently large
d
. Unlike this work, their result does not characterize the full range of behaviors of rate-optimality or rate-suboptimality of the MLE, with regard to the parameters
(\sigma,r,p,d)
, nor does it provide nonasymptotic control on the risk of the MLE and/or the competitive ratio between the MLE and the minimax risk. From their work, for instance, it is unclear for which instances
(\theta^{\star},\sigma,p,d)
the MLE would exhibit its rate-suboptimality. That work also does not study non-convex case, when
p<1
, and also does not establish the rate-optimality of the MLE for
p\in[1,1+\Theta(\tfrac{1}{\log d})]
.

In a slightly different but related direction, the paper
[
24
]
studied a fixed-design linear regression setup with
\ell_{p}
parametric constraints. They demonstrated the suboptimality of the Lasso for certain design matrices via analysis of the regularization path and its dependence on the design matrix.

Suboptimality of the MLE:

The literature on the suboptimality of the MLE is vast. Indeed, many authors have shown that the MLE can be minimax rate-suboptimal in a variety of situations; see the overview papers by Le Cam
[
23
]
and Stigler
[
29
]
for a historical account as well as numerous examples.
It appears that the first example of the suboptimality of the MLE was pointed out by Birgé and Massart. They demonstrated
[
5
, Section 4.B]
the rate-suboptimality of the maximum likelihood estimate for
\alpha
-Hölder density estimation on the unit interval when
\alpha<1/2
.
Another rate-suboptimal example for density estimation is presented in Section 6.4 of the book
[
8
]
.
In a different direction, the paper
[
21
]
establishes the suboptimality of the MLE for estimating the support function of a convex body from noisy measurements. The paper
[
19
]
establishes the rate-suboptimality of the MLE for convex regression in high dimensions.

In the special setting when the observational model consists of a signal, known to lie in a convex compact set, plus additive Gaussian noise, there are still numerous examples of the rate-suboptimality of the MLE. For instance, the elliptical example in
[
39
, Lemma 2]
and the set constructed by Chatterjee in
[
7
, Proposition 1.5]
. These examples provided parameter spaces which shrink as the dimension grows for which the MLE exhibits rate-suboptimality. Additional examples were provided in the paper
[
26
]
, including for pyramids, ellipsoids, solids of revolution, and more.

Optimality of the MLE:

The maximum likelihood estimator (or equivalently, in Gaussian noise, the least squares estimate (LSE)) is known to be minimax rate-optimal in a variety of situations. The classical optimality theory of the MLE was based on regularity conditions such as differentiability in quadratic mean in the parametric case
[
22
,
31
]
or, in the nonparametric case, on entropy conditions (
e
.
g
., the convergence of Dudley’s (bracketing) entropy integral near the origin)
[
30
,
32
]
.

We now mention some concrete examples in which the optimality of the MLE was established. For instance, from the upper bounds in
[
37
, Theorem 2.3]
or
[
3
, Theorem 3.2]

and the lower bound in
[
4
, Corollary 5]
, the MLE was shown to be rate-optimal in isotonic regression. According to
[
35
, pp. 3007]
, the MLE is minimax rate-optimal for sufficiently regular elliptical parameter sets. The paper
[
15
, Section 3]
gave numerous additional examples for image and edge estimation, binary classification, multiple isotonic regression, and concave density estimation.

Chatterjee gave a general, sufficient condition for the optimality of the MLE
[
7
, Proposition 1.6]
; the author, however, gives the caveat that this condition “may be difficult to verify in examples.” This was expanded on in the recent work
[
26
]
. Their Corollary 2.19 demonstrates that the LSE is rate-optimal on a convex body if and only if a localized Gaussian width mapping has a controlled Lipschitz seminorm. This condition also appears quite difficult to verify in particular examples.

Variance and bias of the MLE:

Finally, we mention the paper
[
20
]
, whose results provide additional context and interpretation for our main lower bound results. The following result is essentially implicit in their work; see Appendix
C.2
for a complete and self-contained proof for our formulation.

Proposition 3

(Implicit in
[
20
]
)
.

There exists a constant
C\geqslant 1
such that for any
\sigma>0
, and any closed, convex set
\Theta\subset\mathbf{R}^{d}
, we have

\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits_{Y\sim\mathsf{N}\left(\theta^{\star},\sigma^{2}I_{d}\right)}\Big[\|\Pi_{\Theta}(Y)-\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits[\Pi_{\Theta}(Y)]\|_{2}^{2}\Big]\,\leqslant C\,\mathcal{M}(\Theta,\sigma).

That is, the variance of the MLE is minimax rate-optimal.

An immediate consequence of Proposition
3
is that if
\Theta\subset\mathbf{R}^{d}
is additionally
centrally symmetric
,
i
.
e
., if
\Theta=-\Theta
holds, then the MLE is minimax rate-optimal at the origin. Indeed, if
\Theta
is centrally symmetric, then as Gaussian noise is sign-symmetric,
it easily follows that the MLE is unbiased at the origin: for
\theta^{\star}=0
, we have
\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits_{Y\sim\mathsf{N}\left(\theta^{\star},\sigma^{2}I_{d}\right)}\Pi_{\Theta}(Y)=\theta^{\star}
, and hence by Proposition
3
the risk at
\theta^{\star}=0
is minimax rate-optimal.

In the context of our results, note that our constraint sets
\mathsf{B}^{d}_{p}
are centrally symmetric convex bodies, and hence we can only expect to derive lower bounds against the optimality of the MLE far from the origin. Indeed, and unsurprisingly given Proposition
3
, our lower bound results are attained on the boundary of
\mathsf{B}^{d}_{p}
; see Propositions
1
and Proposition
2
. Finally, by Proposition
3
, we can conclude that the squared
bias
, that is the quantity
\|\theta^{\star}-\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits_{\theta^{\star}}\Pi_{\mathsf{B}^{d}_{p}}(Y)\|_{2}^{2}
, must be larger than the minimax rate for the lower bound instances we derived.

3.4
Computation of the MLE

In this section, we discuss efficient methods for computing the MLE. We will handle the cases
p=0
,
p=\infty
, and
p>0
separately.

Computation of the MLE when
p=0
:

It is straightforward to compute a projection onto the set of
s
-sparse vectors. Indeed, for any
s\in[d]
and any
y\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
, the projection is nothing more than the
s
-largest entries of
y
by magnitude; i.e., we may take

\Pi_{\mathsf{B}^{d}_{0}(s)}(y)=\sum_{i\in\widehat{S}}y_{i}e_{i}\quad\mbox{for any}\quad\widehat{S}\in\mathop{\rm arg\,max}_{S\subset[d],|S|=s}\sum_{i\in S}y_{i}^{2}.

This can clearly be computed in polynomial time: simply sort the entries of
y
by magnitude, keep the largest
s
entries, and set the remaining entries to zero; ties may be broken arbitrarily.

Computation of the MLE when
p=\infty
:

It is just as straightforward to project onto an
\ell_{\infty}
-ball. For any
y\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
, the Euclidean projection of
y
onto
r\mathsf{B}^{d}_{\infty}
is obtained by clipping each coordinate to the interval
[-r,r]
. Specifically,

\Pi_{r\mathsf{B}^{d}_{\infty}}(y)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sign(y_{i})\min\left\{|y_{i}|,r\right\}e_{i}.

This can clearly be computed in polynomial time by coordinatewise clipping.

Computation of the MLE when
p\in(0,\infty)
:

For
p\geqslant 1
, the
projection is a convex program, and can be computed efficiently. In particular, see the paper
[
36
]
, which develops a method based on Lagrange duality and bisection that works for
p\geqslant 1
, in which case it can produce an (
\varepsilon
-approximate) solution in polynomial time. Moreover, for
p\in(0,1)
, this approach—while not guaranteed to converge—produces solutions with small duality gap.

. Inequality (ii) uses the following observation: if
s\geqslant d/2
, then

\min\{2s,d\}\log\frac{\mathrm{e}d}{\min\{2s,d\}}=d\leqslant 2s\leqslant 2s\log\frac{\mathrm{e}d}{s}.

On the other hand, if
s\leqslant d/2
, then

\min\{2s,d\}\log\frac{\mathrm{e}d}{\min\{2s,d\}}=s\log\frac{\mathrm{e}d}{2s}\leqslant 2s\log\frac{\mathrm{e}d}{s},

where final inequality uses that

\tfrac{\mathrm{e}d}{2s}\leqslant(\tfrac{\mathrm{e}d}{s})^{2}
. Passing to the supremum over
\theta^{\star}\in\mathsf{B}^{d}_{0}(s)
in display (
81
) yields the
desired inequality (
80
).

Lemma 11
.

For
1\leqslant k\leqslant d<\infty
, we have

\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\Big[\max_{|S|\leqslant k,S\subset[d]}\|\xi_{S}\|_{2}^{2}\Big]\leqslant 6k\log\frac{\mathrm{e}d}{k},

where
\xi\sim\mathsf{N}\left(0,I_{d}\right)
.

Fix
\lambda\in(0,1/2)
. By the concavity of the logarithm, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\Big[\max_{|S|\leqslant k,S\subset[d]}\|\xi_{S}\|_{2}^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\Big[\max_{|S|=k,S\subset[d]}\exp(\lambda\|\xi_{S}\|_{2}^{2})\Big]

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\binom{d}{k}+\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits[\exp(\lambda\chi^{2}_{k})]

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{\lambda}k\log\frac{\mathrm{e}d}{k}+\frac{1}{2\lambda}k\log\frac{1}{1-2\lambda},

where we have used the inequality
\binom{d}{k}\leqslant(\tfrac{\mathrm{e}d}{k})^{k}
and the moment generating function of the
\chi^{2}_{k}
random variate. Setting
\lambda=1/4
, we obtain the announced inequality

\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\Big[\max_{|S|\leqslant k,S\subset[d]}\|\xi_{S}\|_{2}^{2}\Big]\leqslant 4k\log\frac{\mathrm{e}d}{k}+(2\log 2)k<6k\log\frac{\mathrm{e}d}{k}.\qed

We fix

Signal and noise construction:

In the first regime (corresponding to Proposition

Maximum-likelihood estimator:

onto the unit

gives the projection

.

Soft-thresholding estimator:

Following
[
24
, Section 1.3]
and
[
9

7

Risk evaluation:

For each
d
in each regime, we compute the empirical MSE over 100 independent draws from the Gaussian sequence model for each estimator
\widehat{\theta}^{\text{MLE}}
and
\widehat{\theta}^{\text{ST}}

Appendix C
Background on the Gaussian sequence model

C.1
Minimax rate of estimation for
\ell_{p}
balls

The purpose of this section is to establish
the minimax rates provided in (
11
). We note that this result is implicit in the monograph
[
17
]
, albeit with constants that are difficult to track down explicitly.
The lower bound and upper bound involve the following explicit constants:

c_{\ell}\coloneq\frac{1}{868}\quad\mbox{and}\quad c_{u}\coloneq 6.

(82)

Control functions:

We define the so-called
control functions
, which we will establish as—apart from constant prefactors—the minimax MSE.
In the case
p\in[1,2]
, we define

\mathfrak{m}_{d,p}(\sigma)\coloneq\begin{cases}1&\sigma^{2}\geqslant\tfrac{1}{1+\log d}\\
(\sigma^{2}\log(\mathrm{e}d\sigma^{p}))^{1-p/2}&\sigma^{2}\in\big[\tfrac{1}{d^{2/p}},\tfrac{1}{1+\log d}\big]\\
\sigma^{2}d&\sigma^{2}\leqslant\tfrac{1}{d^{2/p}}\end{cases}.

For
p\geqslant 2
, we set
\mathfrak{m}_{d,p}(\sigma)=\sigma^{2}d\land d^{1-2/p}
.

Theorem 3

(Minimax rate of estimation for
\ell_{p}
balls)
.

For any
d\geqslant 1
,
p\in[1,\infty]
, and
\sigma>0
, the minimax mean squared error, as defined in display (
2
), satisfies the inequalities

c_{\ell}\,\mathfrak{m}_{p,d}(\sigma)\leqslant\mathcal{M}(\mathsf{B}^{d}_{p},\sigma)\leqslant c_{u}\,\mathfrak{m}_{p,d}(\sigma).

Above, the constants
c_{\ell},c_{u}
may be taken explicitly as in display (
82
).

The remainder of this section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
3
.

C.1.1
Proof of Theorem
3
for
p\geqslant 2

Upper bound:

In the case
p\geqslant 2
, it is straightforward to see

\mathcal{M}(\mathsf{B}^{d}_{p},\sigma)\leqslant\mathfrak{m}_{p,d}(\sigma),\quad\mbox{for every $d\geqslant 1,\sigma>0$}.

The bound follows immediately by considering either the estimate
\widehat{\theta}(Y)=0
, and the fact that
\rad(\mathsf{B}^{d}_{p})^{2}=d^{1-2/p}
,
or
\widehat{\theta}(Y)=Y
, and the fact that

\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\|\sigma\xi\|_{2}^{2}=\sigma^{2}d
, where
\xi\sim\mathsf{N}\left(0,I_{d}\right)
.

Lower bound:

We follow a standard reduction to hyperrectangles.
Note that
d^{-1/p}\mathsf{B}^{d}_{\infty}\subset\mathsf{B}^{d}_{p}
; for
p=\infty
, note that
d^{-1/p}=1
. Since
d^{-1/p}\mathsf{B}^{d}_{\infty}
is a
d
-fold Cartesian product of the intervals
[-d^{-1/p},d^{-1/p}]
, and the observation
Y\sim\mathsf{N}\left(\theta,\sigma^{2}I_{d}\right)
has independent coordinates, it follows that

\mathcal{M}(\mathsf{B}^{d}_{p},\sigma)\geqslant\mathcal{M}(d^{-1/p}\mathsf{B}^{d}_{\infty},\sigma)\\
=d\,\inf_{\widehat{\mu}}\sup_{|\mu|\leqslant d^{-1/p}}\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits_{z\sim\mathsf{N}\left(\mu,\sigma^{2}\right)}[(\widehat{\mu}(z)-\mu)^{2}]\geqslant\frac{2}{5}d\cdot\big(d^{-2/p}\land\sigma^{2}\big)=\frac{2}{5}\mathfrak{m}_{p,d}(\sigma),

where the penultimate inequality uses the bound (4.40) from the monograph
[
17
]
.

C.1.2
Proof of Theorem
3
for
p<2

Upper bound:

In the case
p<2
, the following bound holds by the same argument as in the case
p\geqslant 2
,

\mathcal{M}(\mathsf{B}^{d}_{p},\sigma)\leqslant 1\land\sigma^{2}d\quad\mbox{for every $d\geqslant 1,\sigma>0$}.

We used above
\rad(\mathsf{B}^{d}_{p})=1
, which holds as
1\leqslant p<2
. Thus, to establish the upper bound, it suffices to show that

\mathcal{M}(\mathsf{B}^{d}_{p},\sigma)\leqslant c_{u}\,\Big(\sigma^{2}\log(\mathrm{e}d\sigma^{p})\Big)^{1-p/2}\quad\mbox{when}~d\geqslant 1,~~\mbox{and}~~\sigma\in\Big[\frac{1}{d^{1/p}},\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\log d}}\Big].

To establish this bound, one can simply apply the result in Theorem 1 of
[
24
]
, noting that all the inequalities continue to hold for
1\leqslant p\leqslant 2
.

Lower bound:

For
d=1
, the lower bound is deduced from the case
p\geqslant 2
; indeed we have
\mathfrak{m}_{d,p}(\sigma)=\sigma^{2}\land 1
in this case.
Set
c\coloneq\tfrac{0.006}{1+\log 2}
. We obtain by tracing through the argument underlying inequality (11.46) in the monograph
[
17
]
that

\mathcal{M}(\mathsf{B}^{d}_{p},\sigma)=\sigma^{2}\mathcal{M}(\sigma^{-1}\mathsf{B}^{d}_{p},1)\geqslant c\,\Big\{\max_{1\leqslant k\leqslant d}k^{1-2/p}\land\sigma^{2}k\log(\mathrm{e}d/k)\Big\}.

(83)

Suppose that
\sigma\geqslant\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{1+\log(d)}}
.

Then taking
k=1
in the general lower bound (
83
), we obtain

\mathcal{M}(\mathsf{B}^{d}_{p},\sigma)\geqslant c\,(1\land\sigma^{2}\log(\mathrm{e}d))=c=c\,\mathfrak{m}_{d,p}(\sigma).

(84a)

Similarly, for
\sigma\leqslant\tfrac{1}{d^{1/p}}
, we take
k=d
,
and we obtain from (
83
) that

\mathcal{M}(\mathsf{B}^{d}_{p},\sigma)\geqslant c(d^{1-2/p}\land\sigma^{2}d)=c\sigma^{2}d=c\mathfrak{m}_{d,p}(\sigma).

(84b)

For
\sigma\in[\tfrac{1}{d^{1/p}},\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{1+\log d}}]
we note that

\mathcal{M}(\mathsf{B}^{d}_{p},\sigma)\geqslant c\max_{1\leqslant x\leqslant d}\left\lceil x\right\rceil^{1-2/p}\land\sigma^{2}\left\lceil x\right\rceil\log(\mathrm{e}d/\left\lceil x\right\rceil)\\
\geqslant\frac{c}{4}\max_{1\leqslant x\leqslant d}x^{1-2/p}\land\sigma^{2}x\log(\mathrm{e}d/x)=\frac{c}{4}\max_{\tfrac{1}{d^{1/p}}\leqslant\tau\leqslant 1}\tau^{2-p}\land\sigma^{2}\tau^{-p}\log(\mathrm{e}d\tau^{p}),

since the map
t\mapsto t\log(\mathrm{e}/t)
is nondecreasing on
[0,1]
and since
(\left\lceil x\right\rceil/x)^{1-2/p}=(x/\left\lceil x\right\rceil)^{2/p-1}\geqslant\frac{1}{4}
.
We now take

\tau=\sigma\sqrt{\log(\mathrm{e}d\sigma^{p})}.

Note that by assumption that
\sigma\in[\tfrac{1}{d^{1/p}},\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{1+\log d}}]
, we have

\frac{1}{d^{1/p}}\leqslant\sigma\leqslant\tau\leqslant\sqrt{\sigma^{2}\log(\mathrm{e}d)}\leqslant 1.

Thus, evaluating the general lower bound
with the choice of
\tau
given above, we find

\mathcal{M}(\mathsf{B}^{d}_{p},\sigma)\geqslant\frac{c}{4}\,\tau^{2-p}=\frac{c}{4}\mathfrak{m}_{d,p}(\sigma).

(84c)

Combining inequalities (
84a
), (
84b
), and (
84c
), we find that, since
\tfrac{c}{4}\geqslant\tfrac{1}{868}\eqcolon c_{\ell}
that

\mathcal{M}(\mathsf{B}^{d}_{p},\sigma)\geqslant c_{\ell}\,\mathfrak{m}_{d,p}(\sigma),

holds in the case
p\geqslant 2
for all
d\geqslant 1
and
\sigma>0
, as needed.

C.2
Proof of Proposition
3

We essentially follow the arguments of
[

with minor modifications to accommodate our formulation and to obtain explicit constants.

To simplify notation, let us set for

\widehat{\mu}(\xi)\coloneq\Pi_{\Theta}(\theta^{\star}+\sigma\xi),\quad h_{\eta}(\xi)\coloneq\|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)-\eta\|_{2},\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mu\coloneq\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits_{\xi\sim\mathsf{N}\left(0,I_{d}\right)}\widehat{\mu}(\xi).

Define

We will demonstrate that

\delta^{2}\leqslant 128991\,\mathcal{M}(\Theta,\sigma)

(85)

Note that the above inequality is equivalent to our claim.

Set
\mathscr{M}_{\delta}

\Theta

\mu

Specifically, for distinct

Here,

d(x,y)>\tau

Suppose that

\delta^{2}\leqslant c\sigma^{2}
for

\delta^{2}\leqslant c\min\{\sigma^{2},\diam(\Theta)^{2}\}\leqslant C_{1}\,\mathcal{M}(\Theta,\sigma).

(86)

Now suppose that

For the sake of contradiction, suppose that

\sigma^{2}\log|\mathscr{M}_{\delta}|<\tfrac{\delta^{2}}{16}

Note that for any

\sigma

\operatorname*{\mathbf{P}}\nolimits\Big\{\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits h_{\eta}(\xi)-h_{\eta}(\xi)>\sigma\sqrt{2\log(2|\mathscr{M}_{\delta}|)}\Big\}\leqslant\frac{1}{2|\mathscr{M}_{\delta}|}

Additionally, by Markov’s inequality we have

\operatorname*{\mathbf{P}}\nolimits\Big\{\widehat{\mu}(\xi)\in\Theta\cap(\mu+2\delta\mathsf{B}^{d}_{2})\Big\}\geqslant 1-\operatorname*{\mathbf{P}}\nolimits\Big\{\|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)-\mu\|_{2}^{2}>4\delta^{2}\Big\}\geqslant\frac{3}{4}.

Therefore, for some
\eta^{\star}\in\mathscr{M}_{\delta}

\operatorname*{\mathbf{P}}\nolimits\Big\{\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits h_{\eta^{\star}}(\xi)-h_{\eta^{\star}}(\xi)\leqslant\sigma\sqrt{2\log(2|\mathscr{M}_{\delta}|)}\land h_{\eta^{\star}}(\xi)\leqslant\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{8}}\Big\}\geqslant\frac{1}{4|\mathscr{M}_{\delta}|}>0.

Hence we have

\Big(\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits h_{\eta^{\star}}(\xi)\Big)^{2}\leqslant\Big(\sigma\sqrt{2\log(2|\mathscr{M}_{\delta}|)}+\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{8}}\Big)^{2}\leqslant\frac{\delta^{2}}{4}+4\log(2)\sigma^{2}+4\sigma^{2}\log|\mathscr{M}_{\delta}|.

Above we used the scalar inequality
(x+y)^{2}\leqslant 2(x^{2}+y^{2})

\displaystyle\delta^{2}

\displaystyle=\inf_{\eta\in\mathbf{R}^{d}}\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits\big[h_{\eta}(\xi)^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leqslant\operatorname*{\mathbf{E}}\nolimits[h_{\eta}^{\star}(\xi)]^{2}+\mathrm{Var}(h_{\eta}^{\star})

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\delta^{2}}{4}+\frac{1}{4}c\sigma^{2}+4\sigma^{2}\log|\mathscr{M}_{\delta}|\leqslant\frac{3\delta^{2}}{4}<\delta^{2}.

Above, we used the Gaussian Poincaré inequality
[

\mathrm{Var}(h_{\eta}^{\star})\leqslant\sigma^{2}
.
Consequently, we have

\frac{\delta^{2}}{16\sigma^{2}}\leqslant\log|\mathscr{M}_{\delta}|=\log M\Big(\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{8}},\Theta\cap(\mu+2\delta\mathsf{B}^{d}_{2}),\|\cdot\|_{2}\Big)\leqslant\sup_{\mu\in\Theta}\log M\Big(\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{8}},\Theta\cap(\mu+2\delta\mathsf{B}^{d}_{2}),\|\cdot\|_{2}\Big).

Hence, by Lemma

which we state below, and taking the parameters

, we have

\delta^{2}\leqslant C_{2}\,\mathcal{M}(\Theta,\sigma),

(87)

with

.
Combining the cases (

) and (
