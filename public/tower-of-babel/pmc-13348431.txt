Belonging-oriented classroom practices in K–12 schools: insights from interviews and classroom observations
Authors: Esra Töre
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Students' sense of belonging has been widely recognized as a critical factor influencing academic engagement, psychological well-being, and participation in school environments. However, existing research often examines classroom relationships, culturally responsive pedagogy, and participation structures separately, providing limited insight into how everyday classroom practices interact to support belonging. Guided by Self-Determination Theory and the literature on school belonging and psychological safety, this qualitative study explores how teachers construct classroom environments that may support belonging through daily pedagogical practices in K–12 settings. Data were collected through ten classroom observations and semi-structured interviews with ten educators working across preschool, primary, and middle school contexts. The data were analyzed using qualitative content analysis with iterative coding and thematic development. The findings revealed five interconnected dimensions of belonging-oriented classroom practices: inclusive and student-centered learning environments, community building and belonging, cultural representation and responsiveness, student voice, participation, and agency, and collaborative learning and social connectedness. These practices appear to support students' experiences of autonomy, competence, and relatedness while fostering psychological safety and meaningful participation, suggesting that belonging may emerge through the interaction of instructional accessibility, relational care, cultural recognition, participatory structures, and collaborative learning. By providing a practice-oriented perspective on how belonging may be enacted in everyday classroom life, this study contributes to research on inclusive education. It offers insights into how educators can design learning environments that may strengthen students' participation, engagement, and sense of belonging.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04758-1.

Introduction

A growing body of research in educational and social psychology highlights the central role of belonging in students’ academic, social, and psychological development [5, 41]. Sense of belonging refers to students’ perceptions of being accepted, valued, and included within a learning community [13]. In educational contexts, belonging has consistently been associated with positive outcomes, including academic motivation, engagement, persistence, and psychological well-being [28, 48]. Conversely, experiences of exclusion or marginalization can negatively affect students’ academic identity, participation, and emotional well-being [41]. As a result, belonging has become a central construct in contemporary discussions of inclusive and equitable education. In this context, inclusive education refers to the provision of equitable access, meaningful participation, and recognition of all students within the learning environment [1].

Classrooms represent a central context in which belonging is constructed because they are the primary sites of daily teacher–student interaction that shape students’ perceptions of membership in the learning community [45]. Research consistently demonstrates that classroom climate, including teacher–student relationships, emotional support, and opportunities for participation, strongly influences students’ sense of belonging (school belonging) and engagement [11, 35]. Positive teacher–student interactions and supportive classroom structures have been linked to stronger engagement and belonging across educational contexts [48]. In this sense, classroom practices function not only as instructional strategies but also as relational mechanisms through which students experience recognition, participation, and inclusion in the learning process.

One psychological mechanism that contributes to the development of belonging in classroom settings is psychological safety. Psychological safety refers to individuals’ perceptions that they can express ideas, ask questions, and take interpersonal risks without fear of embarrassment or negative consequences [12]. In educational contexts, psychologically safe classrooms encourage students to participate in discussions, share perspectives, and engage collaboratively with peers [16, 45]. Research suggests that classroom environments characterized by emotional support, predictable routines, and respectful teacher–student interactions foster psychological safety and participation [50, 52].

Belonging is closely connected to inclusive pedagogical practices and opportunities for student voice and participation [9, 22, 33]. Research shows that students’ sense of belonging is shaped not only by interpersonal relationships but also by whether classroom environments recognize and affirm students’ identities and experiences [51]. When classrooms acknowledge diverse perspectives and support meaningful participation, students are more likely to develop agency and connection to their learning communities [2, 31]. In this study, the term “diverse learners” refers to students with varied linguistic, cultural, socioeconomic, and ability backgrounds [49].

Several theoretical perspectives help explain how belonging is constructed in classroom environments. Research on culturally responsive teaching highlights the importance of integrating students’ cultural identities, languages, and experiences into instructional practices, suggesting that recognition of cultural diversity can strengthen students’ sense of belonging [15]. Scholarship on student voice and feminist pedagogy similarly emphasizes the importance of positioning students as active participants in learning processes and classroom dialogue [9, 25]. In addition, pedagogies of care highlight the role of caring teacher–student relationships in creating inclusive educational environments [43]. Finally, relational pedagogy emphasizes that classroom belonging emerges through everyday interactions and relationships between teachers and students [6]. Together, these perspectives provide a theoretical basis for understanding conditions associated with students’ sense of belonging.

Although existing research highlights the importance of teacher–student relationships, culturally responsive pedagogy, and student participation in fostering a sense of belonging, these elements are often examined separately in the literature. Studies have investigated teacher–student interactions [11, 35], cultural diversity climates [39], or collaborative participation structures [31] as predictors of belonging. However, these studies often examine specific classroom processes in isolation. As a result, the literature provides a more limited understanding of how these relational, pedagogical, and participatory practices interact within everyday classroom environments to shape students’ experiences of belonging.

This limitation is particularly relevant in K–12 education, where students’ experiences of inclusion and participation are shaped through daily classroom interactions, instructional routines, and teacher–student relationships [45]. Understanding how belonging develops, therefore, requires attention to the micro-level practices through which teachers organize learning environments, structure participation, and build relationships with students [2]. Qualitative approaches that integrate classroom observations with educator perspectives can provide important insight into how inclusive pedagogies are enacted in practice and how they shape students’ experiences of belonging [50, 51].

The present study addresses this gap by examining how teachers construct belonging-oriented classroom environments through everyday pedagogical practices. Drawing on qualitative data from teacher interviews and classroom observations across multiple K–12 school settings, the study explores how educators implement practices that support psychological safety, inclusive participation, and recognition of student identities [39]. By examining the everyday enactment of inclusive pedagogies, the study offers a practice-oriented understanding of how belonging may be cultivated in classroom environments [31]. Specifically, the study asks: How do teachers construct classroom environments that may support belonging through everyday pedagogical practices in K–12 settings? The theoretical perspectives informing this study are discussed in the following conceptual framework section, which outlines the psychological foundations guiding the analysis.

Theoretical framework

This study draws on two complementary theoretical perspectives: Self-Determination Theory (SDT) and research on school belonging and psychological safety. Together, these perspectives provide a psychological basis for understanding how belonging-oriented classroom practices shape students’ experiences of participation, recognition, and inclusion in K–12 settings.

Self-determination theory

Self-Determination Theory proposes that human motivation and well-being are shaped by the satisfaction of three basic psychological needs: autonomy, competence, and relatedness [36]. In educational settings, these needs are closely associated with students’ engagement, persistence, well-being, and quality of participation in classroom life [26, 34].

The need for autonomy refers to experiencing one’s actions as volitional and self-endorsed rather than externally controlled [36]. In classrooms, autonomy-supportive practices include offering meaningful choices, acknowledging students’ perspectives, and creating participation structures that allow students to exercise agency in their learning [17, 34].

The need for competence refers to feeling effective, capable, and able to meet learning demands [36]. Competence is fostered when teachers provide structure, differentiation, and accessible pathways for participation [46]. In K–12 classrooms, practices such as scaffolding, Universal Design for Learning, and differentiation support students’ perceptions of competence [3, 26].

The need for relatedness refers to feeling connected to, cared for by, and significant to others [36]. Among the three needs, relatedness is particularly important for belonging because it reflects students’ experiences of social connection and recognition in classroom communities [32]. Research across school contexts indicates that students are more likely to engage and participate when they perceive teachers and peers as supportive and when classroom environments foster relational safety [17].

Taken together, SDT provides an interpretive lens for understanding how classroom practices shape students’ experiences of autonomy, competence, and relatedness.

School belonging and psychological safety

The second framework guiding this study is the literature on school belonging and psychological safety. Sense of belonging is commonly defined as the experience of being accepted, respected, included, and supported within a social context such as school [2, 13]. In educational psychology, belonging has been linked to engagement, well-being, motivation, and persistence, while a lack of belonging is associated with withdrawal and reduced participation [5].

Belonging is not simply an internal feeling; it is shaped by everyday interactions, routines, and institutional messages about who is valued and recognized within a learning environment [51]. Research shows that belonging develops through multiple classroom processes, including supportive teacher–student relationships, equitable participation structures, cultural recognition, and opportunities for meaningful contribution [31, 39]. This is especially important for culturally and linguistically diverse students, where belonging is closely tied to whether students perceive their identities, languages, and backgrounds as visible and affirmed rather than marginalized [51].

A related construct is psychological safety, which refers to the perception that one can speak, participate, ask questions, and take interpersonal risks without fear of humiliation, exclusion, or negative consequences [12]. For students, psychological safety helps explain why some classroom contexts encourage voice and engagement, while others lead to silence, hesitation, or self-protection. Research suggests that emotional support, respectful communication, and inclusive participation structures contribute to psychologically safer classroom climates [48, 50].

Belonging and psychological safety are closely intertwined. Students are more likely to experience belonging when they feel safe enough to be visible, to contribute, and to participate as themselves [42]. Teacher behaviors that communicate respect and relational support can reduce social threat and strengthen students’ sense of belonging [35, 42]. This is particularly salient for students from marginalized, multilingual, or minoritized backgrounds, for whom belonging often depends on whether classroom norms make participation psychologically and culturally possible [39].

This theoretical perspective is central to the present study because it allows belonging-oriented classroom practices to be interpreted not only as instructional strategies but also as psychological conditions that support inclusion and participation in classroom communities. From this perspective, flexible environments, culturally responsive materials, relational routines, and structures for student voice matter because they shape whether students experience the classroom as a place where they are safe, recognized, and able to participate fully. Thus, the concepts of school belonging, and psychological safety provide the broader interpretive context for examining how inclusion is enacted in everyday classroom life.

Integrating the theoretical perspectives

Used together, SDT and the belonging/psychological safety literature offer a coherent theoretical basis for the present study. SDT explains how classroom practices support or constrain students' needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness, while belonging and psychological safety explain how these need-supportive conditions are experienced socially and emotionally in classroom communities [31]. This combined theoretical perspective allows teachers' everyday actions to be interpreted not only as pedagogical techniques but also as practices shaping students' motivation, participation, and sense of belonging. Psychological dimensions of belonging-oriented classroom practices

Research in educational psychology suggests that students’ sense of belonging may be shaped not only by interpersonal relationships but also by the instructional practices and participation structures that organize everyday classroom life [18]. In K–12 settings, classroom practices that support cognitive accessibility, psychological safety, and student voice may play an important role in shaping students’ participation and experiences associated with belonging [7, 37]. These dimensions can also be understood in relation to the satisfaction of students’ needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness, as outlined in Self-Determination Theory.

Inclusive pedagogy emphasizes instructional approaches that enable diverse learners (i.e., students from varied linguistic, cultural, socioeconomic, and ability backgrounds) to access and participate meaningfully in classroom learning [1]. Research indicates that when instruction accommodates diverse learning needs through differentiated instruction, flexible participation structures, and scaffolded learning pathways, students may be more likely to perceive classroom environments as supportive of competence and engagement [1, 27]. Such practices may help support participation in classroom learning and make it more accessible to students with diverse learning profiles.

Belonging is also closely related to the emotional climate of the classroom. Psychological safety refers to individuals’ perceptions that they can express ideas, ask questions, and participate in learning activities without fear of embarrassment or negative social consequences [12]. Research on classroom climate indicates that teacher practices may play a central role in creating environments where students feel safe to engage intellectually and socially with learning tasks [7]. When teachers emphasize respectful communication, predictable routines, and supportive interactions, students may be more likely to participate actively in classroom discussions and collaborative learning activities [14].

Opportunities for student voice and participation represent another important dimension of belonging-supportive classroom practices. Student voice refers to pedagogical practices that allow learners to express perspectives, contribute to classroom dialogue, and participate in shaping aspects of their learning experiences [10]. Classroom environments that incorporate collaborative discussions, peer learning activities, and opportunities for feedback allow students to participate in the co-construction of knowledge and strengthen their connection to the learning community [23, 54]. Peer collaboration may further support belonging by promoting social connectedness and shared participation in classroom learning [44, 54].

Method

Study design

This study employed a qualitative multiple case study design to examine how educators construct belonging-oriented classroom environments through everyday pedagogical practices. These practices were explored within broader school efforts related to diversity, equity, inclusion, and belonging (DEIB). Case study research allows in-depth investigation of contemporary educational practices within their real-world context and typically draws on multiple sources of evidence such as interviews and observations [40]. Grounded in an interpretivist paradigm, the study sought to understand educators' perspectives and classroom practices through two qualitative data sources: classroom observations and semi-structured interviews [53].

The researcher's positionality was also considered throughout the study. As a researcher examining classroom practices related to inclusion and belonging, the researcher maintained a reflective stance regarding potential biases and interpretations during data collection and analysis. Reflexive notes were kept during field observations and the coding process to support transparency and minimize interpretive bias.

This study focused on educators’ perspectives and classroom practices as a means of examining the instructional and relational conditions that may support students’ sense of belonging. While student perspectives were not directly collected, this design reflects a practice-oriented approach aimed at understanding how belonging is constructed through everyday pedagogical interactions.

Setting and participants

Participants and settings were selected using purposive sampling, with an emphasis on representing a range of school levels (preschool to middle school), educator roles, and instructional contexts. This variation was intentionally sought to capture developmental differences in how belonging-oriented practices are enacted across early childhood, primary, and middle school contexts. Purposeful sampling seeks information-rich cases that can illuminate the questions under study [30]. Interview participants were required to have at least three years of professional experience and prior engagement with initiatives related to diversity, equity, inclusion, and belonging. This criterion referred to prior involvement in school- or classroom-level practices aimed at fostering inclusive, equitable, and participatory learning environments.

Recruitment occurred through direct outreach and professional networks. Classroom observations were conducted in settings chosen to reflect variation in grade level, subject area, and student demographics, where available. A total of 10 educators participated in semi-structured interviews (Table 2), while 10 classroom environments were observed across preschool, primary, and middle school levels (Table 1). Data collection methods

Data were collected during the spring and fall semesters of 2025 using two qualitative techniques: classroom observations and semi-structured interviews. A structured observation protocol guided classroom observations, while interview protocols included open-ended questions designed to elicit participants' experiences with classroom practices related to belonging, inclusion, participation, and recognition.

Classroom observations were arranged in collaboration with school administrators. Observations were conducted across 10 classrooms over 4 full school days, focusing on instructional materials, classroom interactions, and environmental cues related to diversity and inclusion. Each classroom was observed for approximately 40 min, resulting in a total of 400 min of observation data. All observations were conducted by the primary researcher.

Interviews were conducted online with voluntary participation from ten educators, including teachers and school leaders. Each interview lasted approximately 20–30 min and was audio-recorded with participant consent. All interviews were transcribed verbatim and anonymized prior to analysis.

The semi-structured interview protocol and classroom observation guide used in the study are provided as supplementary materials (Additional file 1 and Additional file 2). The observation and interview protocols were initially developed within a DEIB framework; however, the analysis focused specifically on classroom practices that support students' sense of belonging. The DEIB framework was used to guide initial data collection due to its emphasis on inclusion, equity, and participation. However, the analysis was narrowed to belonging in order to provide a more focused examination of how these broader principles are enacted through classroom practices.

Data analysis

The data collected from classroom observations and educator interviews were analyzed using qualitative content analysis, a systematic method for coding and categorizing textual data to identify patterns and themes [40]. Data were analyzed through two main stages: coding and theme development.

Initial coding

Observation notes and interview transcripts were reviewed repeatedly to identify meaningful units of data. Initial open coding was conducted to generate preliminary categories. Coding was conducted iteratively by the researcher, and emerging categories were refined through repeated comparison across interview and observation data [4].

Theme development and integration

Codes were subsequently grouped into broader themes using axial coding procedures [47]. Observation and interview data were first analyzed separately and later compared to identify converging and diverging themes. This process supported triangulation across data sources, including classroom observations, semi-structured interviews, and field notes.

Throughout the analytic process, triangulation of methods and data sources was used to increase the trustworthiness and depth of findings. This aligns with recent methodological literature emphasizing the value of triangulation for enhancing the validity of case study research in educational settings [38, 40]. Pseudonyms and anonymized identifiers (e.g., P3, O7) were used throughout the analysis to protect participant confidentiality. Representative quotations are integrated throughout the findings to illustrate key ideas and provide insight into how belonging-oriented classroom practices are enacted across diverse instructional levels and roles.

Ethical considerations

This study received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Indiana University Bloomington (Protocol #25,002; approval date: December 17, 2024). The study was reviewed under expedited procedures as a minimal-risk behavioral and social science study involving interviews and classroom observations. Data collection was conducted exclusively in private schools due to access restrictions in public institutions. All research activities involving human participants adhered to established ethical guidelines for qualitative inquiry [19, 24]. All procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant institutional and national ethical guidelines.

Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. Interview participants signed voluntary consent forms and were informed of their right to withdraw at any time without penalty. For classroom observations involving minors, parental consent was obtained through school administrations, and families were provided with written information regarding the research purpose, procedures, and their child's participation.

All participants were given a confidentiality statement outlining how data would be used, stored, and protected. No real names were recorded; instead, anonymized codes (e.g., P1, O4) were used throughout the research process to protect participant identity and institutional privacy. Interview data and observation notes were stored securely on the researcher's password-protected, encrypted device, with access restricted to the researcher only [38].

To further ensure ethical rigor, the researcher introduced herself before every interview and observation. Participants were informed that their involvement was voluntary and that they could end the interaction at any time. For classroom observations, the researcher coordinated with teachers in advance to ensure alignment with classroom routines and minimize disruption to instructional activities.

Member checking was conducted by sharing interview summaries with participants for feedback and verification. Similarly, for classroom observations, follow-up communications were initiated with the teachers involved to clarify contextual details and confirm the accuracy of field notes. This process increased the study's credibility and respected participants' rights to accurate representation [19, 24].

Trustworthiness and rigor

To ensure methodological rigor and enhance the trustworthiness of the findings, this study followed the four criteria proposed by Guba and Lincoln [21]: credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability. An audit trail documenting data collection procedures, coding decisions, and theme development was maintained to enhance transparency and support the reproducibility of the study.

Credibility was supported through triangulation of data sources, including semi-structured interviews and classroom observations. Coding decisions were reviewed iteratively by the researcher, and emerging themes were refined through repeated comparison across the datasets [40].

Transferability was addressed through detailed descriptions of the research context, including school levels, classroom environments, and participant roles. Purposeful sampling across preschool, primary, and middle school settings further supported contextual variation, enabling readers to assess the potential applicability of the findings to other educational contexts [4].

Dependability was supported by maintaining an audit trail documenting key stages of data collection and analysis, including coding decisions and theme development [38].

Confirmability was enhanced through reflexive practices during the research process. The lead researcher maintained reflexive notes to document analytic decisions and monitor potential bias. All data were anonymized and securely stored to protect participant confidentiality [24].

Limitations of access and sampling

A key limitation of this study is the absence of student-reported data. While the study focused on educators’ perspectives and classroom observations to examine practices that may support belonging, it does not capture students’ subjective experiences directly. As belonging is inherently personal, future research should incorporate student voice to better understand how these practices are experienced by learners.

Although this study received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Indiana University Bloomington (IU IRB), access to public schools in the MCCSC district was restricted due to political sensitivities surrounding DEI-related initiatives. As district-level cooperation could not be secured, the study was limited to private school settings and relied on voluntary participation through individual teacher consent. These private school settings may also reflect particular socioeconomic (SES) conditions and resource availability, which should be considered when interpreting the findings.

While this provided insight into inclusive practices within these specific private school contexts, the absence of public-school data and restricted access to high school classrooms may have limited the breadth of observed belonging-oriented classroom practices and the generalizability of the findings. Future research should aim to include public school contexts and high school levels to broaden the scope and applicability of findings.

Thematic analysis of classroom observations and educator interviews revealed five overarching themes, each encompassing both observed and described practices.

Theme 1: inclusive and student-centered learning environments

This theme captures classroom practices designed to support diverse learners through flexible, student-centered, and inclusive instructional approaches. Both observations and interviews highlighted practices aimed at improving access to learning, supporting different learning styles, and promoting individualized learning pathways. Theme 1 consists of three sub-themes: Equity in access and differentiated instruction, student-centered and movement-based learning, and individual learning portfolios and daily goals.

Equity in access and differentiated instruction

Both observations and interviews indicated efforts to balance equal access to resources with differentiated instructional support. Classrooms were observed to provide shared learning resources and inclusive environments, as reflected in the note “equal materials and environment for all” (O1). At the same time, teachers emphasized the importance of recognizing diverse learning needs. One educator explained this perspective by noting, “We don’t say special education student… we just say diverse learners because every kid is diverse in how they learn” (P3). These examples illustrate how classrooms combined equitable access with differentiated support strategies.

Student-centered and movement-based learning

Observation and interview data showed that many classrooms encouraged active participation and flexible learning arrangements. Students were often able to choose materials and move freely within the classroom, described in the observation “they are getting their material and learn with what they want, teachers are facilitators” (O1). Teachers also described classroom structures that encouraged autonomy and self-reflection, as one educator noted: “Children can move around, sit and talk with whomever they want – but also learn to self-analyze their impact” (P7). These practices suggest that movement and flexibility appeared to support engagement and student agency.

Individual learning portfolios and daily goals

Observations indicated that individualized learning goals were integrated into classroom routines. Teachers monitored student progress through clearly defined learning objectives, reflected in the observation note “all students have learning aims and the teacher evaluates how they reach it” (O3). Interview participants described similar practices through project-based learning structures that allowed students to pursue personal interests. One educator explained, “Students chose what they wanted to work on… they presented projects to school board members and families” (P5). These examples illustrate how individualized learning goals were incorporated into everyday classroom practices. These practices were more prominently observed in early childhood and primary classrooms, where physical space was organized to allow movement between learning stations and flexible seating arrangements, compared to more structured seating patterns in middle school classrooms.

Theme 2: community building and belonging

This theme captures practices aimed at strengthening classroom community and supporting students' sense of belonging. Observations and interviews highlighted routines, classroom environments, and participation structures that encouraged inclusion and emotional safety. The theme includes three sub-themes: Shared routines and roles for belonging, flexible physical environments for psychological safety, and hearing quiet voices.

Shared routines and roles for belonging

Observation data indicated that shared classroom routines and responsibilities appeared to contribute to a sense of collective participation. For example, classrooms incorporated community practices such as “birthday celebration” (O2) and rotating responsibilities, including “water plant, librarian, etc.” (O6). Interview participants similarly emphasized the importance of collective responsibility in building classroom community. One educator explained, “Our classroom community works best if we all include each other and help each other” (P1). These practices illustrate how daily routines appeared to contribute to students’ sense of belonging.

Flexible physical environment for psychological safety

Classroom design also appeared to support students’ emotional comfort and participation. Observations described flexible classroom environments where students could choose learning spaces, reflected in the note “nothing is symmetric like life… they can choose the places where they feel belonging” (O8). Teachers similarly described intentional efforts to create accessible classroom spaces. As one educator explained, “We’ve worked to make sure our rooms are arranged so that they can access all of the parts of the classroom” (P4). These examples highlight how classroom environments were structured to support psychological safety and participation.

Hearing quiet voices

Both observations and interviews indicated that teachers attempted to support the inclusion of quieter students in classroom discussions. In one observation, a teacher intentionally invited quieter students to participate, described as “he gives quiet students a chance to speak… treating them according to their personalities” (O8). Teachers also described structured dialogue practices designed to support inclusive participation. For example, one educator noted, “We also do regular peace circles where we talk” (P4). These strategies illustrate how teachers sought to create opportunities for broader student participation. While these practices were observed across different grade levels, they were more explicitly structured in middle school classrooms, whereas in early childhood settings they were embedded more informally within daily routines. These practices included teacher prompting, structured turn-taking strategies, and the use of discussion formats such as circles, which provided more equitable opportunities for participation.

Theme 3: cultural representation and responsiveness

This theme reflects practices that recognize and integrate students' cultural and linguistic backgrounds into classroom learning. Observations and interviews indicated that cultural diversity was represented through instructional materials, classroom discussions, and language-support practices. The theme includes three sub-themes: Visual and linguistic cultural representations, critical cultural inquiry, and multilingualism and translation support.

Visual and linguistic cultural representations

Observation data indicated that classrooms incorporated visible representations of cultural diversity through instructional materials and displays. For example, one classroom included “flags from other cultures like Arabic ‘selam’ or yin-yang” (O5). Teachers also described intentional efforts to diversify classroom resources. As one educator explained, “Books we’re using represent a diversity of people… religions and races and cultures” (P4). These practices suggest that cultural diversity was reflected in both classroom environments and in instructional materials.

Critical cultural inquiry

Classroom instruction also included opportunities for students to reflect critically on cultural differences and historical processes. In one observation, students were encouraged to discuss global historical inequalities through the question “Why did Western Europe have the industrial revolution before others?” (O7). Interview participants similarly described integrating social justice discussions into classroom learning. One educator explained, “We were working on a social justice standards framework… tied to global issues like the UN sustainability goals” (P5). These examples illustrate how classrooms incorporated critical reflection on cultural and global issues.

Multilingualism and translation support

Observation data also showed efforts to support linguistic diversity in classrooms. In one case, multilingual learning materials were provided to support students’ language needs, described in the observation “one Asian and one black students have some different language documents for her” (O10). Teachers also described institutional resources designed to support multilingual learners. One participant explained, “We created an entire Spanish language section… we subscribe to databases that offer translation in many languages” (P9). These examples highlight efforts to support linguistic inclusion through both classroom instruction and institutional resources.

Theme 4: student voice, participation, and agency

This theme reflects practices that encourage student participation and voice within classroom communities. Observations and interviews indicated that students were provided with opportunities to share experiences, express opinions, and participate in classroom discussions. The theme includes three sub-themes: Space for student narratives, trust building through teacher self-disclosure, and choice and autonomy.

Space for student narratives

Observations showed that classroom routines often included opportunities for students to share personal experiences. For example, teachers invited students to reflect on their experiences through activities such as “taking feedback for the weekend – did you see anyone, how was it?” (O3). Teachers also described learning activities designed to connect classroom learning with students’ everyday lives. One educator explained, “We study a different continent each month… families bring materials or speak” (P1). These practices illustrate how personal and cultural narratives were incorporated into classroom learning.

Trust building through teacher self-disclosure

Observation data also showed that teachers occasionally shared personal experiences to build rapport with students. One observation recorded a teacher referencing personal life experiences during instruction, described as “giving an example of the teacher's life, childhood, or adult time” (O7). Teachers similarly described creating relational classroom environments grounded in trust and openness. As one participant explained, “We’re not here to judge… we’re here to work hard, have fun and learn a lot about each other” (P10).

Choice and autonomy

Observations indicated that students were sometimes given flexibility in how they participated in learning activities. For example, some students were allowed to engage in alternative tasks, as reflected in the observation “some students don’t want to attend activities; they are doing something different in another learning area” (O2). Teachers also described opportunities for student-led initiatives beyond the classroom. One educator explained, “Students in our school can create any type of club that they like” (P2). These practices illustrate how autonomy and participation were incorporated into classroom and school structures.

Theme 5: collaborative learning and social connectedness

This theme highlights classroom practices that emphasize collaboration, peer support, and positive social relationships. Observations and interviews indicated that learning environments often encouraged teamwork and informal social interactions among students. The theme includes two sub-themes: Collaboration through team-based projects and natural social interactions.

Collaboration through team-based projects

Observation data showed that students frequently engaged in collaborative learning activities. For example, one observation described a group project in which students worked together to design a scientific mechanism, noted as “they are preparing the slowest-moving mechanism… the whole team” (O9). Teachers similarly described collaboration as an intentional learning goal. One participant explained, “We wanted students to learn how to work together, to collaborate and solve real-world problems” (P5). These examples highlight the role of collaborative tasks in classroom learning.

Natural social interactions

Observations also documented informal interactions that contributed to classroom relationships. For instance, one observation described a moment where “a student came, showed the ladybug… the teacher said: ‘Cool’” (O8), illustrating spontaneous engagement between students and teachers. Teachers also described extracurricular activities that supported social connections within the school community. As one participant noted, “Clubs help them get to know other staff and students outside their classroom” (P8). These interactions suggest that informal social exchanges also contributed to supportive classroom relationships.

The model illustrates how classroom practices identified in this study, including inclusive pedagogy, community-building routines, cultural responsiveness, student voice, and collaborative learning, may support students' experiences of autonomy, competence, and relatedness. These need-supportive practices, in turn, contribute to the development of psychological safety and school belonging in K–12 classroom environments.

Building on these theoretical perspectives, the present study conceptualizes belonging-oriented classroom environments as shaped by the interaction of pedagogical practices and students' basic psychological needs. Discussion

This study examined how teachers construct belonging-oriented classroom environments through everyday pedagogical practices in K–12 settings. Findings from classroom observations and educator interviews suggest that belonging may emerge not from a single instructional strategy but from the interaction of instructional accessibility, relational support, cultural recognition, participatory structures, and collaborative learning practices enacted in everyday classroom practice. This interpretation aligns with research demonstrating that belonging develops through supportive teacher–student relationships and opportunities for meaningful participation [45]. Consistent with belonging literature, these findings also suggest that inclusion in educational settings involves more than physical presence; rather, belonging depends on whether students experience recognition, identity affirmation, and legitimate participation within classroom communities [39].

Findings from Theme 1 indicate that belonging appeared to be supported when students could access learning through differentiated instruction, flexible participation structures, and individualized learning goals. This finding aligns with Self-Determination Theory, which emphasizes that students' engagement and motivation are strengthened when classroom environments support perceptions of competence [26]. Research on inclusive pedagogy similarly demonstrates that differentiated instruction and flexible learning pathways enhance engagement and participation when instruction accommodates diverse learner profiles [1, 27]. More broadly, recent work suggests that responsive pedagogy functions not only as an instructional strategy but also as a condition for psychological inclusion, particularly for students whose needs are marginalized within standardized learning structures [20]. These findings therefore suggest that cognitive accessibility may function not only as a pedagogical adjustment but also as a condition of classroom membership.

Findings from Theme 2 show that classroom routines, shared roles, spatial flexibility, and deliberate support for quieter students appeared to contribute to environments in which belonging was supported. This finding is consistent with prior research demonstrating that belonging is strengthened in classrooms characterized by supportive teacher–student relationships, emotional security, and respectful interaction norms [11]. It also aligns with research on psychological safety in educational environments, which indicates that students participate more actively when classroom interactions reduce social risk and encourage open contribution [50, 52]. From a Self-Determination Theory perspective, such practices may support students' need for relatedness by creating relationally secure classroom environments that enable participation and engagement [32].

Findings from Theme 3 indicate that belonging appeared to be supported not simply because students' cultural and linguistic identities were represented in classroom materials, discussions, and learning activities. In the present study, cultural representation was supported through multilingual resources, culturally diverse materials, and instructional practices that encouraged critical reflection on cultural perspectives. These findings align with research on culturally responsive pedagogy, suggesting that students are more likely to feel a sense of belonging when their cultural identities and linguistic backgrounds are recognized as resources for learning [39]. Systematic and review-based studies similarly indicate that culturally responsive teaching practices foster belonging by connecting academic learning to students' cultural experiences and identities [8, 22, 29]. In this sense, culturally responsive practices appear to serve as mechanisms for identity affirmation, supporting students' participation and recognition within classroom communities. From this perspective, belonging appeared to be supported not simply because diversity was represented, but because students' identities were made usable within classroom learning processes.

Findings from Theme 4 indicate that opportunities for student voice, dialogue, and choice positioned students as active contributors within classroom learning communities. This finding is consistent with Self-Determination Theory, which suggests that autonomy-supportive environments strengthen motivation and engagement by allowing individuals to express their perspectives and exercise choice [34, 36]. Research on student voice further indicates that participatory classroom practices enhance students' sense of agency and strengthen their connection to learning communities [37]. In this respect, participatory structures may not simply reflect belonging but actively contribute to its development by positioning students as legitimate contributors to classroom knowledge construction. These findings suggest that student voice should be understood not only as an outcome of belonging but also as one of the classroom mechanisms through which belonging is produced.

Findings from Theme 5 indicate that collaborative learning activities, peer interaction, and informal social exchanges appeared to support students' experiences of belonging within classroom communities. Team-based learning activities, peer collaboration, and informal classroom interactions contributed to environments in which students experienced themselves as members of a shared learning community. These findings are consistent with research demonstrating that collaborative learning structures promote social connectedness and belonging by strengthening peer relationships and participation [44, 54]. More recent studies similarly suggest that belonging is reinforced in classroom contexts where cooperation and collective problem-solving are emphasized rather than competitive participation structures [31, 51]. From a psychological perspective, collaborative learning environments support belonging by simultaneously reinforcing relatedness and participation within classroom communities. These findings extend belonging research by suggesting that belonging is constructed not only through teacher–student relationships but also horizontally through peer collaboration and shared participation in classroom activities.

This study contributes to the literature by offering a practice-based account of how belonging is constructed through everyday classroom interactions. Rather than examining isolated factors, the findings integrate instructional, relational, cultural, and participatory dimensions into a unified perspective. This contribution lies in illustrating how these dimensions interact in practice, providing a more holistic understanding of belonging in classroom environments.

Taken together, these findings suggest that belonging-oriented classrooms may emerge through the convergence of instructional accessibility, relational support, cultural recognition, participatory learning structures, and collaborative interaction. This integrated perspective supports the complementary use of Self-Determination Theory and school belonging frameworks in understanding inclusive educational environments. In this sense, DEIB-related practices can be understood as a broader contextual layer, while belonging serves as the central analytic focus of the study. In this framework, instructional accessibility supports competence, participatory practices support autonomy, and relational and collaborative structures support relatedness [48]. These findings should also be interpreted in relation to the private school contexts in which the data were collected, which may reflect the contexts in which the data were collected. Such settings may reflect particular socioeconomic (SES) conditions, resource availability, and institutional flexibility, which can influence how belonging-oriented practices are enacted. At the same time, research on belonging and psychological safety highlights how these need-supportive conditions are experienced socially as recognition, inclusion, and emotional security within classroom communities [2, 42]. The contribution of the present study, therefore, lies in demonstrating how belonging is enacted through everyday classroom practices. Specifically, the findings show how instructional accessibility, relational classroom structures, cultural recognition, participatory learning practices, and peer collaboration interact to support students' experiences of autonomy, competence, and relatedness in classroom environments.

Implications

Theoretical implications

This study contributes to the literature on school belonging by providing a practice-oriented account of how belonging is enacted in everyday classroom environments. While prior research has primarily examined belonging as a psychological construct associated with engagement, motivation, and well-being, fewer studies have explored how belonging emerges through teachers' daily pedagogical practices. By integrating insights from Self-Determination Theory and research on school belonging, the findings demonstrate how classroom practices can function as need-supportive environments that sustain students' experiences of autonomy, competence, and relatedness.

The study also contributes to research on culturally responsive pedagogy by emphasizing that cultural inclusion extends beyond curricular representation. The findings suggest that belonging-supportive practices involve relational and participatory dimensions of teaching, including opportunities for voice, collaboration, and identity affirmation within classroom interactions. In this sense, belonging is not only an outcome of classroom climate but also a process shaped through everyday pedagogical decisions that structure recognition, participation, and interaction in learning environments.

Finally, the findings extend existing belonging research by illustrating how multiple dimensions of classroom practice including instructional accessibility, relational care, cultural representation, and participatory learning structures interact in shaping students' experiences of inclusion. This integrated perspective highlights belonging as a multidimensional process embedded in classroom practices rather than as a solely individual psychological perception.

Practical implications

The findings of this study offer several practical insights for educators and school leaders seeking to cultivate classroom environments that foster belonging.

First, the results highlight the importance of instructional accessibility. Practices such as differentiated instruction, flexible participation structures, and individualized learning goals can help ensure that students with diverse learning profiles can engage meaningfully in classroom activities. Designing instruction that allows multiple pathways to participation may therefore strengthen both academic engagement and perceptions of competence.

Relational classroom structures also appear critical for fostering a sense of belonging. Teachers can support a sense of belonging by establishing predictable routines, encouraging respectful classroom dialogue, and intentionally creating environments where students feel emotionally safe to participate. Small practices, such as assigning shared classroom roles, maintaining inclusive routines, and intentionally inviting quieter students to participate, can help reinforce students’ sense of belonging within the classroom community.

Culturally responsive classroom practices further support belonging by recognizing students' identities and experiences within the learning environment. Incorporating culturally diverse learning materials, multilingual resources, and opportunities for cultural dialogue can help create more inclusive classroom spaces. When students encounter their languages, cultures, and perspectives in classroom materials and discussions, they may be more likely to perceive the classroom as a place where their identities are valued.

Opportunities for student voice also play an important role in strengthening classroom belonging. Providing opportunities for students to express opinions, contribute to classroom discussions, and influence aspects of learning activities may strengthen students' sense of agency and connection to school. Classroom practices such as discussion circles, reflective dialogue, and participatory decision-making structures can support this process.

Finally, collaborative learning environments appear to strengthen students' social connectedness. Team-based learning activities, peer collaboration, and informal interaction opportunities can help students develop supportive relationships with peers, reinforcing a sense of shared participation in classroom learning. Structuring classroom learning around cooperation rather than competition may therefore contribute to stronger classroom belonging.

These implications should also be considered in relation to different school contexts. While the practices identified in this study were observed in private school settings with access to flexible resources, their implementation in public school contexts may require adaptation due to differences in class size, institutional constraints, and resource availability. Future research and practice should therefore consider how belonging-oriented practices can be adapted across diverse educational settings.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. First, the study was conducted exclusively in private school settings due to access restrictions in public schools within the MCCSC district. Because district-level permission for research related to diversity, equity, and inclusion initiatives could not be obtained, data collection relied on voluntary participation from private institutions. As a result, the findings reflect classroom practices in independent school contexts and may not fully represent instructional conditions or institutional structures present in public schools. Additionally, the findings reflect adult interpretations of classroom practices rather than students’ lived experiences, which may lead to potential discrepancies between observed practices and how belonging is actually experienced by students. Additionally, private school contexts often reflect particular socioeconomic (SES) conditions and access to resources, which may shape how belonging-oriented practices are implemented and experienced.

Second, classroom observations were limited to preschool, primary, and middle school levels due to site-level access constraints. Although some interview participants held roles extending into high school contexts, observational data did not include high school classrooms. Future studies should examine belonging-oriented practices across a broader range of educational levels. Third, the study relied on qualitative data from a relatively small sample of educators and classroom observations. While this design enabled in-depth exploration of classroom practices and educator perspectives, the findings are not intended to be statistically generalizable but rather analytically transferable to similar educational contexts. Instead, they provide contextually grounded insights into how belonging-oriented pedagogies are enacted in specific educational environments. Future research could expand this work by examining belonging-oriented practices in public school contexts, incorporating student perspectives, and exploring how classroom practices interact with broader institutional policies related to inclusion and equity.

Conclusion

This study explored how belonging-oriented classroom environments are constructed through everyday pedagogical practices in K–12 settings. The findings demonstrate that belonging may emerge through the interaction of inclusive instruction, relational classroom practices, culturally responsive pedagogy, student participation, and collaborative learning structures.

Rather than resulting from a single instructional strategy, belonging appears to develop through classroom environments that support students' participation, recognition, and social connection. Teachers, therefore, play a critical role in shaping the relational and psychological conditions that allow students to experience themselves as valued members of classroom communities. By illustrating how belonging is enacted through everyday classroom practices, this study contributes to the growing literature on inclusive education. It provides insights into how educators can design learning environments that support students' engagement, participation, and psychological well-being.

Taken together, the findings support the conceptual model presented in Fig. 1, suggesting that belonging-oriented classroom environments may emerge through the interaction of inclusive pedagogy, relational practices, cultural responsiveness, and participatory learning structures.
