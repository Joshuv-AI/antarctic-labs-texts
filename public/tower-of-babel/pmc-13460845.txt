Plasma Lidocaine Concentrations During Intravenous Lidocaine Infusion Therapy in the Pediatric Population—A Scoping Review
Authors: McKenna Postles, Amanda Barton, Nicholas West, Gillian Lauder
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundIntravenous lidocaine therapy (IVLT) is often used in perioperative multimodal analgesia due to its analgesic, anti‐hyperalgesic, and anti‐inflammatory effects. In adults, IVLT doses of 1–2 mg/kg/h produce plasma concentrations of 1–2 μg/mL, within the presumed therapeutic range of 1–5 μg/mL. The dose range, expected plasma concentrations, and presumed therapeutic range are not well defined in children. We aimed to review available pharmacokinetic data in pediatric patients receiving IVLT.MethodsWe searched MEDLINE, EMBASE, and CINAHL databases for studies reporting pharmacokinetic data for IVLT in healthy 1–18‐year‐olds, with plasma lidocaine or metabolite levels collected post‐infusion, excluding studies involving non‐therapeutic infusions or children with comorbidities affecting lidocaine metabolism. Bias was assessed using validated tools. We conducted narrative data synthesis due to reporting variability and summarized plasma lidocaine and/or metabolite levels and the incidence of reported adverse events.ResultsFour studies comprising 174 children were included. A study (n = 22) using a 1.5 mg/kg bolus over 30 min followed by a 1 mg/kg/h infusion for 6 h post‐surgery achieved highest mean drug concentrations of 2.82 ± 0.65 μg/mL immediately following surgery. Another study (n = 46) utilized a 1.5 mg/kg bolus over 5 min followed by a 2 mg/kg/h infusion and achieved peak concentrations of 4 to < 5 μg/mL. Two studies (n = 106) utilized a 1.5 mg/kg bolus and 1.5 mg/kg/h infusion until transfer to the post‐operative anesthesia care unit or six hours post‐operatively achieved concentrations that were detectable and < 5 μg/mL. The only adverse event reported was a single participant who experienced transient sensory disturbances.ConclusionExisting pharmacokinetic data for pediatric IVLT is extremely limited. Reported plasma concentrations were detectable and lower than presumed toxic levels with one reported adverse event. Large‐scale, multicenter research is needed to determine optimal pediatric dosing, elucidate dose response curves, and ensure safe and effective use of IVLT in the pediatric population.Trial RegistrationProspective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO): CRD42025636554

Introduction

Pain is an unpleasant sensory or emotional sensation resulting from the complex interaction of neuro‐inflammatory activity and complex peripheral and central nervous information‐processing networks [1]. Variability in pain perception and response to analgesic medication is related to factors such as age, sex, pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, and pharmacogenetic profiles, as well as cognitive, behavioral, cultural, and emotional factors [2, 3]. Clinical practice needs to be supported by a thorough understanding of the pharmacokinetics of analgesics to ensure safe and effective dosing regimens are used to appropriately manage pain and minimize uncertainties in pharmacokinetic responses, especially in the pediatric population [2, 3].

Multimodal analgesia aims to provide optimal comfort and minimize distress in the acute perioperative pain setting by utilizing a combination of analgesics that act by different mechanisms, thereby reducing the analgesic requirements of each type of medication and their risk of adverse effects [4]. Multimodal analgesic combinations may include simple analgesics (acetaminophen and ibuprofen), opioids, local anesthetics, adjuvant analgesics (such as NMDA receptor antagonists, alpha‐2 agonists, and gabapentinoids) with other non‐pharmacological strategies (such as rehabilitation, education, hypnosis, cognitive behavioral therapy, relaxation, imagery, controlled breathing, transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation, acupuncture, massage, and distraction techniques). A multimodal approach provides significant benefits, including reductions in pain intensity, opioid dose requirements, and opioid‐related adverse events [5, 6]. Intravenous lidocaine therapy (IVLT) has been used as an adjuvant analgesic agent for multimodal analgesia in both pediatric and adult postoperative pain management [7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12].

Lidocaine is an amide‐type local anesthetic that acts on voltage‐gated ion channels to impede signaling transmission within the peripheral and central nervous system [13]. Lidocaine has analgesic, anti‐hyperalgesia, and anti‐inflammatory effects and may reduce pain and postoperative nausea and vomiting [13]. In adults, lidocaine pharmacokinetics are well characterized. With bolus administration and infusions lasting less than 12 h, lidocaine displays biphasic elimination and linear pharmacokinetics with a terminal half‐life of about 100 min [14]. Lidocaine is metabolized rapidly in the liver to multiple metabolites: active monoethylglycinexylidide (MEGX), active glycinexylidide (GX), and inactive N‐ethylglycine (NEG). Children older than 6–7 months without hepatic, cardiac, or renal impairment eliminate intravenous lidocaine, similarly to adults, by metabolism and excretion of lidocaine and its metabolites [1].

The pharmacokinetics of intravenous lidocaine infusions in adults have been previously described [15]. Intravenous lidocaine infusion doses in the range of 1–2 mg/kg/h result in plasma concentrations of 1–2 μg/mL, which remain below presumed toxic levels of > 5 μg/mL and appear to impact postoperative pain with a logarithmic dose–response curve [16]. Target therapeutic plasma levels for acute pain management remain unknown, but ranges from 1 to 5 μg/mL appear to cause limited adverse effects [1]. Awake adult patients with plasma lidocaine levels above 5 μg/mL report early effects of local anesthetic system toxicity (LAST), such as perioral numbness, metallic taste, paresthesia, and tinnitus [1]. At higher plasma lidocaine levels (≥ 8 μg/mL), more severe symptoms of LAST occur, including visual or auditory disturbances, disassociation, muscle twitching, anaphylaxis, convulsions, coma, and respiratory or cardiac arrest [1].

The general principles of acute pain management in children recommend analgesics maintain stable drug plasma concentrations within the therapeutic window because subtherapeutic levels may lead to inadequate analgesia while supratherapeutic levels increase the potential for adverse effects [2, 17]. Therefore, pharmacokinetic profiles are required to understand and ensure standardized, effective, and safe IVLT pain management in the pediatric population. The aim of this review was to summarize the pharmacokinetic data collected from pediatric patients receiving IVLT and report on any associated incidences of LAST or adverse events.

Methods

Study Design and Registration

We conducted a systematic review of the literature. The review was registered with the Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) on 23 January 2025 (CRD42025636554) and was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [18]. The review protocol is accessible on PROSPERO.

Due to the lack of available data and the consequent inability to perform pharmacokinetic modeling, this review pivoted to be presented as a scoping review. For completeness, all available data have been summarized, including additional timepoints beyond the 8 h post‐IVLT collection points outlined in the initial systematic review protocol.

Eligibility Criteria

We included studies collecting pharmacokinetic data from otherwise healthy pediatric populations (1–18 years) receiving therapeutic IVLT, in which all reported plasma lidocaine or plasma lidocaine metabolite (NEG, MEGX, GX) levels were collected. We included both published and unpublished data obtained by contacting authors directly. There were no restrictions for study design, setting, or data. Literature reviews were used to identify any studies missed during the search process via backward citation searching.

We excluded studies that did not report pharmacokinetic data post‐IVLT or where lidocaine was not administered as a therapeutic intravenous infusion, for example, cases in which lidocaine was administered as a single bolus injection, or as an epidural. We excluded data reported from other age groups (< 1 year or > 18 years) and data collected from populations with comorbidities that may impair lidocaine metabolism, including cardiac, liver, and renal disease.

Information Sources

We searched MEDLINE (OVID interface, 1946 onwards), EMBASE (OVID interface, 1974 onwards), and the Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL, EBSCOhost interface, 1982 onwards) on 29 January 2025. To ensure thorough review and literature saturation, we backward citation searched through included studies and relevant reviews, contacted authors of identified studies for unpublished pharmacokinetic data, and continuously monitored ClinicalTrials.gov and PROSPERO for recently completed or ongoing trials or reviews to identify additional relevant studies. On 19 December 2025, prior to submitting our review for publication, we re‐ran our initial database search to ensure all available studies had been identified.

Search Strategy

Search strategy development was supported by clinical experts in pharmacology and anesthesiology and a Health Sciences Librarian experienced in systematic review searching within pediatrics, anesthesiology, and pharmacology. The search strategy included medical subject headings (MeSH) and keywords relevant to pediatric IVLT pharmacokinetics within the title, abstract, or author‐provided keywords (Table 1). The search was filtered to include studies of human subjects reported in the English language. Syntax and subject headings were adjusted to accommodate each database.

Selection and Data Collection Process

The first reviewer (MP) imported the search results from the databases into the reference manager, Zotero v6.0.37 (Digital Scholar, Vienna, VA), and removed duplicate entries. MP initially screened titles and abstracts using a broader inclusion criterion: the use of intravenous lidocaine in the pediatric population. We justified the use of one reviewer conducting this initial screening based on time/resource constraints and the broad initial criteria. MP exported the included results into Excel (Microsoft, Redmond, WA). MP and a second reviewer (AB) conducted independent, parallel screening of the full‐text articles of these results to determine study eligibility against the full inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Reviewers contacted authors for additional information to determine eligibility when necessary.

Study Outcomes

Primary Outcome

The primary outcome was plasma lidocaine levels and/or plasma lidocaine metabolite (NEG, MEGX, GX) levels at any time point from the start of IVLT. As lidocaine dosages and time points varied by study and the therapeutic ranges remain unknown, the objective was to summarize the regimes and their corresponding plasma lidocaine levels. The data extracted, if available, included patient characteristics (age, sex, weight, height, comorbidities, ethnicity), lidocaine dosages (mg/kg/h for infusions and mg/kg for accompanying boluses), plasma lidocaine and/or plasma lidocaine metabolite levels (μg/mL), and the time intervals at which they were measured from IVLT start. If sufficient data were available, studies were categorized into regimes that achieved plasma levels of: < 1 μg/mL; 1– < 2 μg/mL; 2– < 3 μg/mL; 3– < 4 μg/mL; 4– < 5 μg/mL; and ≥ 5 μg/mL.

Secondary Outcome

The secondary outcome was reported adverse effects of different plasma lidocaine concentrations. All adverse effects associated with lidocaine during and post‐IVLT have been reported including the incidence and signs/symptoms of LAST (perioral numbness, metallic taste, paresthesia, tinnitus, visual or auditory disturbances, disassociation, muscle twitching, anaphylaxis, convulsions, coma, and respiratory or cardiac arrest). As reporting of LAST varied by study, the outcome was summarized and reported based on the data provided.

Risk of Bias

The primary analysis included all eligible studies regardless of bias. To evaluate bias in individual studies, two authors (MP and AB) independently summarized study information using the Cochrane Risk‐of‐Bias tool for randomized trials, version 2 (RoB2) [19]. The domains of bias assessed for each study were bias arising from the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions as per the protocol, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported result [19]. Domains were assigned as a low risk, some concern, or high risk of bias based on domain‐specific signaling questions and used to generate an overall risk‐of‐bias judgment based on the criterion outlined in the RoB2 [19].

As the primary outcome consisted of objective numerical data (plasma levels), the reviewers prioritized the deviations from intended interventions, quantifying the effect of adherence to the intervention as per the protocol and missing outcome data to ensure the correct matching of regimes to plasma lidocaine (or metabolite) levels. Therefore, individual study bias was acknowledged during data synthesis, but did not influence numerical review findings unless (1) the IVLT regimen was misreported or unclear, (2) the plasma lidocaine (or metabolite) levels were misreported or unclear, or (3) deviations from intended interventions affected plasma lidocaine (or metabolite) levels.

Throughout data collection and bias evaluation, discrepancies between the two independent reviewers were resolved via discussion and, if no agreement could be reached, via consultation with a third reviewer (GL). All reviewers documented their individual assignments and reasoning for evaluation or reference by other reviewers at a later date. The final detailed risk of bias assessment was summarized into an RoB2 template (Data S1) [20].

Data Synthesis

An initial descriptive summary listed the included studies in a table outlining study design, participant characteristics, intervention (IVLT regimen), primary outcomes, and specific methods/reporting of extracted data. Results relevant to this review's primary outcome, plasma lidocaine or metabolite levels, were converted to consistent units and summarized in a table that matched study IVLT regimes with plasma levels achieved, associated incidences of adverse effects, including LAST, and the overall risk of bias. We did not conduct meta‐analyses or evaluate meta‐biases due to variability in IVLT regimens (timing and dosage of infusions and boluses) and limited study numbers. Therefore, this review evaluated its findings via a textual narrative synthesis of the results, including relationships within and between studies and robustness of the evidence, following the guidance from the Centre for Reviews and Dissemination [21].

The Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach does not recommend grading the overall quality of evidence in systematic reviews where no meta‐analysis was conducted; hence, an informal quality of evidence evaluation was conducted [22].

Results

Study Selection

A total of 871 search results were exported from MEDLINE, EMBASE, and CINAHL. Secondary searching prior to publication did not identify any additional results. Following removal of 197 duplicates, preliminary screening yielded 87 results referencing intravenous lidocaine use in the pediatric or unknown populations. Full‐text review and reverse citation searching of these results identified four studies meeting the inclusion criteria (Figure 1). Authors of these four studies were contacted for access to more detailed data.

PRISMA flow diagram [18].

Excluded Studies of Interest

Initial full‐text review and reverse citation searching yielded eight publications meeting the inclusion criteria for the review. However, five of the publications (two randomized controlled trials and 3 observational studies) reported on different outcomes from the same patient cohort, which was confirmed by study authors [23, 24, 25, 26, 27]. Therefore, we included the original randomized controlled trial in our review and excluded the other four publications.

Study Results

Four randomized trials, with a total of 174 participants in their lidocaine groups, were included in this review (Table 2). Two studies comprising 86 pediatric patients receiving IVLT produced pharmacokinetic data associated with a low risk of bias (Table 3). The first of these was a randomized, double‐blind, parallel‐group, controlled study that evaluated IVLT for the prevention of post‐operative vomiting in 46 pediatric patients undergoing elective tonsillectomy. It utilized a 1.5 mg/kg bolus five minutes prior to intubation, followed by a 2 mg/kg/h infusion during and until the end of surgery [28]. This regimen achieved plasma lidocaine concentrations of 4 to < 5 μg/mL, with a median (range) concentration of 3.94 (0.87–4.88) μg/ml at the end of surgery. No adverse effects of lidocaine or clinical evidence of LAST were reported.

The second study was a randomized, double‐blind study that assessed the effects of IVLT on the length of hospital stay after major abdominal surgery in 40 pediatric patients. It utilized a regimen of 1.5 mg/kg bolus 20 min prior to infusion followed by 1.5 mg/kg/h until six hours post‐surgery [29]. Plasma lidocaine levels were presented graphically and specific numbers were not given, but the authors specifically stated that toxic plasma lidocaine levels (< 5 μg/mL) were not reached and no serious lidocaine‐related adverse effects were reported.

Two studies of 88 pediatric patients produced pharmacokinetic data associated with some concerns for bias. The first of these was a randomized, double‐blind placebo‐controlled trial that evaluated the effect of IVLT on postoperative morphine requirements in children undergoing major spine surgery [23]. This study investigated a cohort of 22 pediatric patients who received a 1.5 mg/kg lidocaine bolus over 30 min before skin incision, followed by a 1 mg/kg/h lidocaine infusion lasting six hours after extensive spinal surgery. This regimen achieved mean drug concentrations of 2.82 ± 0.65 μg/mL immediately following surgery and 0.08 ± 0.08 μg/mL the morning after surgery. One patient experienced transient sensory disturbances in the extremity receiving the lidocaine infusion. Unpublished raw data provided by the authors showed that this regimen achieved plasma lidocaine concentrations of up to 4 to < 5 μg/mL. However, this data contained 23 participants rather than the reported 22, causing some concerns for bias, as reasons for exclusion of the 23rd participant remain unknown S1. Therefore, the raw data was not used in this review.

A second study with some concerns for bias was a randomized, double‐blind, controlled trial that investigated the effects of IVLT on hemodynamic response to tracheal intubation and metabolic‐hormonal responses during laparoscopic appendectomy in 66 pediatric patients [30]. This study utilized an initial bolus of 1.5 mg/kg over 5 min, followed by an infusion of 1.5 mg/kg/h until transfer to the post‐anesthesia care unit, achieving detectable plasma lidocaine concentrations less than 5 μg/mL from five minutes post‐intubation to immediately after surgery. Our RoB2 assessment associated these data with some concerns of bias, as data were presented in a graph, not a mean/median as prespecified, and one patient received a higher, unspecified lidocaine dosage, but was included in the investigators' analysis on the intention‐to‐treat principle [31].

Overall Quality of Evidence

According to the GRADE assessment for the quality of evidence, the risk of bias across studies remains unclear. Plausible bias that raises some concerns about the results is unlikely to lower the confidence in the effect of the estimate, due to the objectivity of the data collected. The consistency of the data is high, with homogenous results all less than 5 μg/mL. The data is direct, as pharmacokinetic data is inherently objective and pertains to the outcomes, intervention, and patient population of interest to this review. The limited study numbers make the validity of the estimated effect less certain. There may be a publication bias, due to pharmacokinetic data being taken as a safety measurement but not published. There was insufficient data to provide a pharmacokinetic profile of IVLT in the pediatric population.

Discussion

This scoping review aimed to summarize the available pharmacokinetic data associated with IVLT in the pediatric population. There was insufficient data to perform pharmacokinetic modeling. Four studies were identified that collected pharmacokinetic data from 174 patients, all utilizing regimens that resulted in plasma concentrations below toxic plasma levels (5 μg/mL) with one adverse event recorded. Available adult pharmacokinetic data indicate that IVLT in the 1–2 mg/kg/h dose range generally produces plasma concentrations of 1–2 μg/mL [16]. In the pediatric population studied in this review, similar dosing regimens were reported, but the plasma lidocaine concentrations were slightly higher. The pediatric regimens studied demonstrated average or median plasma lidocaine concentrations that exceeded 3 μg/mL and remained below 5 μg/mL.

Our review identified that there is currently a distinct lack of available pharmacokinetic data to create a pharmacokinetic profile of IVLT in the pediatric population despite its increasing off‐label use as a component of multimodal analgesia regimens [5]. Additionally, with the limited sample size and minimal reports regarding lidocaine adverse effects including LAST, conclusions regarding the safety of IVLT in the pediatric population cannot be made. Subgroup analysis of lidocaine levels by age grouping was also not possible with the available data.

Pediatric clinical research in general is more limited and faces greater ethical constraints than adult research [32]. Conducting pharmacokinetic research with lidocaine in the vulnerable pediatric population presents substantial ethical challenges as regulatory bodies impose stringent requirements to ensure that pediatric research meets the highest ethical standards. Any research must present a favorable risk‐benefit ratio with the prospect of direct therapeutic benefit or, at minimum, minimal additional risk beyond standard care. For lidocaine, an older generic drug with limited commercial incentive for industry‐sponsored trials, obtaining ethics approval is particularly difficult because Institutional Review Boards may question the justification of exposing children to additional study procedures when existing, although limited, data have guided clinical practice for decades [33].

One of the most significant practical barriers in pediatric pharmacokinetic studies is blood sampling. Pharmacokinetic studies require multiple, timed blood samples to characterize absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion profiles; an approach that is not practically feasible in children due to the concerns of repeated venipuncture [34]. The most pragmatic approach is to restrict serial blood sampling to children who already have indwelling arterial or central venous catheters for clinical purposes, typically those in the intensive care unit setting. However, this introduces selection bias, as these patients may have altered physiology (e.g., critical illness, hepatic hypoperfusion, altered protein binding) that does not represent the broader pediatric population.

Pediatric research historically receives less funding and priority than adult clinical research, with a higher likelihood of pediatric research studies being cross‐sectional or epidemiological rather than the robust RCTs more common in adult clinical research [35]. A 2020 descriptive analysis of pediatric drug data found that, in new drugs approved by Health Canada over a ten‐year period, pediatric‐specific information was available for only 47% of new drugs and a pediatric specific indication for use was present for only 28%. In drugs with pediatric dosing information, the most prevalent dosing age range is adolescents (12–17 years) [36]. Therefore, in order to provide pediatric patients with innovative medicines and address their medical needs, off‐label use of adult medications using weight‐based dosing regimens is common [32]. Extrapolation from adult data raises concerns related to both efficacy and safety, as pediatric patients are physiologically and developmentally distinct from adult patients. Within the pediatric age span (from neonates to adolescents), the pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamic profiles of pharmacotherapies also vary [32]. In order to extrapolate from adult data, researchers must lay out rigorous extrapolation plans that include similarity of disease and drug response between populations and knowledge gaps that need to be filled with clinical data from pediatric populations. They should also reflect the level of tolerable uncertainty associated with the drug, which varies within the context of disease severity and available treatments [37]. For lidocaine specifically, an older drug approved under different guidelines than those of newer, more‐strictly regulated pharmaceuticals, these risks and uncertainties, when used off‐label as an infusion, should be re‐evaluated under newer extrapolation guidelines.

Recommendations from the general principles of acute pain management in children that analgesics should be delivered to sit within the therapeutic plasma concentration window indicate that pharmacokinetic profiles need to be characterized to ensure safe IVLT pain management [2]. Detailed hourly blood sampling during and after IVLT is required to generate a comprehensive pharmacokinetic profile in the pediatric population. The dilemma between the need for pediatric clinical trials to inform safe clinical practice, on the one hand, and exposure of children to potential risks of research, on the other, complicates pediatric research and requires strategies to mitigate these risks. To mitigate the sampling burden, one approach would be to use sparse sampling designs where only two to four samples per patient are collected at strategically optimized time points [38]. Dried blood spot microsampling represents a promising alternative, requiring only 15–30 μL of capillary blood per sample, dramatically reducing the volume collected and enabling sampling from a heel prick or finger prick, with the utilization of topical anesthesia, rather than requiring invasive arterial or venous access [34]. By combining opportunistic sampling from indwelling lines with microsampling across multiple centers, a multicenter study could feasibly accumulate a rich dataset across age groups (infants through to adolescents) without imposing an unacceptable burden on individual participants.

Extrapolating adult lidocaine pharmacokinetic models to pediatric patients presents many challenges, mainly because children are not “small adults.” Two key mathematical tools, allometric exponents and sigmoidal maturation functions, are used together to bridge this gap, accounting for both body size and maturational changes in organ function. Extrapolation from adult data using allometric scaling and maturation models has been demonstrated with propofol, where initial adult pharmacokinetic models were scaled to children, then subsequently validated with relatively modest amounts of pediatric data [39]. Lidocaine is primarily metabolized by hepatic cytochrome P450 enzymes CYP1A2 and CYP3A4, both of which exhibit well‐characterized maturation profiles: CYP3A4 is relatively mature by 6 months of age, whereas CYP1A2 matures more slowly and may not reach adult activity levels until one to 2 years of age [40, 41]. Physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling platforms such as Simcyp Pediatric Simulator (Centara Inc., Radnor, PA) and PK‐Sim (Open Systems Pharmacology) can integrate age‐dependent changes in organ size, body composition, hepatic enzyme maturation, renal function, plasma protein binding, and cardiac output to predict lidocaine disposition across the pediatric age spectrum [42, 43, 44]. These models can generate prior predictions of concentration time profiles that inform optimal dosing regimens and sampling windows for the clinical study, thereby reducing the number of samples and patients needed to refine the model. However, PBPK models are only as reliable as their input data, and for lidocaine, there remain uncertainties around age‐related changes in hepatic blood flow, protein binding dynamics in critically ill children, and the pharmacological activity and toxicity of the MEGX metabolite, which necessitate clinical validation with real pediatric data [45]. A dual approach that uses pharmacokinetic predictions to design an optimally efficient limited blood sampling study, and then using the collected data to refine and validate the pharmacokinetic model represents the current best‐practice methodology advocated for pediatric drug development [46].

As individual centers are likely to recruit only small numbers of patients across the required age groups (infants, toddlers, pre‐school children, school‐age children, and adolescents), pooling data from multiple sites is essential to achieve adequate statistical power and data for modeling. Utilization of existing collaborative research networks would enhance the ability to pool data [47]. Standardized data collection using uniform bioanalytical methods validated across laboratories and agreed‐upon covariate definitions (e.g., how hepatic function is categorized in children) are prerequisites for meaningful data pooling. Using software that can accommodate variability between centers would enable parameter estimation even when individual center contributions are small [48].

A coordinated effort combining opportunistic clinical sampling, advanced microsampling techniques, PBPK‐informed study design, population pharmacokinetic modeling of pooled multicenter data, and collaborative governance structures represents the most feasible and scientifically rigorous pathway to establishing age‐appropriate, evidence‐based lidocaine infusion dosing regimens across the pediatric life span.

Limitations

The evidence included in this review was limited by the fact that the four reported studies were each performed at a single center in specific age groups, which may limit their generalizability. Due to the inherent constraints of conducting pediatric pharmacokinetic research, our review was limited by the amount and quality of data provided, as reporting of the data was difficult to interpret beyond a general range, estimation from a graph, or limited median values published. This inhibited the ability to conduct a robust meta‐analysis and to generate pharmacokinetic profiles to inform quality pediatric dosing recommendations. Finally, a single reviewer conducted the initial abstract screen due to resource constraints. This limitation was reduced via thorough reverse citation searching of literature reviews, continuous monitoring of study databases, and conducting the search two times to ensure eligible studies were not omitted.

Larger sample sizes of pediatric patients receiving IVLT in standardized protocols and appropriate sampling of lidocaine levels, and more robust extrapolation from adult data with the use of new pharmacokinetic techniques are required to optimize dosing and ensure the safest and most effective use in the pediatric population. Future research should aim to create age‐related dose response curves in both healthy patients and those with comorbidities that affect lidocaine pharmacokinetics. While it is believed that healthy children older than 6–7 months eliminate intravenous lidocaine similarly to adults, sampling multiple pediatric age groups, such as neonates (0–28 days), infants (> 28 days—1 year), toddlers (> 1–2 years), pre‐scool children (2–5 years), school‐age children (6–11 years), and adolescents (12–18 years) would be optimal to ensure accurate profiles due to anatomic and physiologic differences between these age groups and adults [1, 49]. Additionally, future research is required to determine if there is, in fact, an added benefit of IVLT in multimodal analgesia in different surgical populations to ensure the clinical relevance of its use and provide motivation for accurate pharmacokinetic modeling.

Conclusion

The limited available pharmacokinetic data prevented pharmacokinetic profiling for pediatric IVLT. The current use of IVLT in pediatric populations when administered as a 1.5 mg/kg bolus with 1–2 mg/kg/h infusion showed plasma concentrations which were generally higher than the corresponding adult data. Further pediatric research, using some of the suggested methodologies, is required to generate detailed pharmacokinetic profiles to determine safe plasma levels that confer therapeutic benefit.
