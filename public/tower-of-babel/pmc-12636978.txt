Assessment of β-blocker use and all-cause mortality in patients with myocardial infarction: a real-world analysis of the NHANES 1999–2018 database
Authors: Wang Ling, Hongde Li, Li Liu, Yansun Sun, Yannv Qu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the association between β-blocker use and all-cause mortality in a real-world cohort of patients with myocardial infarction (MI).MethodsThis study included 2308 patients with MI from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 1999–2018. The primary outcome was all-cause mortality. Multiple approaches were employed to control for confounding, including multivariable Cox regression, propensity score matching and inverse probability of treatment weighting.ResultsBased on data from 2308 patients with MI in the NHANES 1999–2018, with a mean follow-up of 84.6 months, this study evaluated the association between β-blocker use and all-cause mortality. The unadjusted analysis showed a significant protective effect (HR 0.81, 95% CI 0.68 to 0.96, p=0.0178). However, after multivariable adjustment for demographic, clinical and socioeconomic factors, as well as propensity score-based methods and inverse probability of treatment weighting, no significant association was observed (e.g., adjusted HR 1.04, 95% CI 0.87 to 1.25, p=0.6733). Stratified analyses did not reveal significant effect modification by any covariate (all p interaction value >0.05).ConclusionAfter adjustment for measured confounders, this analysis found no significant association between β-blocker use and all-cause mortality in this real-world MI cohort.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases remain a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, with myocardial infarction (MI) representing a significant contributor to this global health burden. Therefore, optimising long-term management and identifying therapeutic interventions that effectively mitigate mortality risk in this vulnerable population are of paramount importance.

Beta-adrenergic receptor blockers (β-blockers) have been a cornerstone of post-MI therapy for decades, established through numerous randomised controlled trials (RCTs) demonstrating their efficacy in reducing short- and long-term mortality, reinfarction and the development of heart failure.1 2 Some early meta-analyses indicated a reduction in mortality by approximately 23% within 2 years compared with control groups, providing a strong evidence base for their central role in acute myocardial infarction (AMI) guidelines.3 4 These early landmark trials, such as the Norwegian Multicentre Study Group and the Beta-Blocker Heart Attack Trial, provided robust evidence supporting their use, particularly in patients with reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF).5 Consequently, β-blockers are strongly recommended in major clinical guidelines for secondary prevention following MI.6 7

However, the landscape of MI management has evolved significantly with the advent of reperfusion therapies (eg, primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI)) and contemporary medical treatments, which may attenuate the incremental benefit of β-blockers observed in historical trials.8 The list of drugs for secondary prevention of MI is constantly updated, and the actual number of drugs taken by patients is increasing. Against the backdrop of modern treatment, it is very necessary to explore whether some classic drugs still have first-line recommendations. Recent research, including large-scale observational studies and updated meta-analyses, has begun to challenge the universal application of β-blockers in all patients with post-MI. Some analyses suggest that the survival benefits of β-blockers may be less pronounced or absent in specific subgroups, particularly in patients with preserved LVEF or without signs of heart failure.9 10 But the 2025 individual patient data meta-analysis of 22 earlier RCTs showed a significant 23% reduction in all-cause mortality and fewer recurrent MIs with β-blockers, with benefit strongest in the 40–49% LVEF stratum.11

Furthermore, the effectiveness of β-blockers in real-world settings can be influenced by a complex interplay of factors not always captured in controlled trials. Socioeconomic factors have been increasingly recognised as significant determinants of cardiovascular health and outcomes, potentially influencing treatment adherence, access to care and overall mortality risk.12

Given these evolving insights and the persistent need to validate therapeutic benefits in diverse, real-world populations, a comprehensive evaluation of β-blocker use and all-cause mortality in contemporary post-MI cohorts is warranted. This study aims to evaluate the association between β-blockers and all-cause mortality in a representative cohort of patients with MI using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES, 1999–2018).

Methods

Data source and study population

The NHANES is an ongoing, nationally representative survey managed by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/). Its central purpose is to track the health and nutritional status of the non-institutionalised civilian population of the USA. Data collection takes place in 2-year cycles and is made publicly available for research use. In each cycle, trained field teams gather information through three complementary channels: in-home interviews that collect self-reported demographic, socioeconomic, dietary and medical history data; standardised physical examinations conducted in mobile examination centres; and an extensive battery of laboratory tests on blood, urine and other specimens. These components together yield a rich, multilayered dataset that covers, among other areas, demographics, daily dietary intake, diagnosed medical conditions, laboratory test results and responses to detailed questionnaires.

Definition of the exposure variable

The exposure of interest was the use of β-blocker medications.

This was operationalised using data from the Prescription Medications section of the NHANES Questionnaire Data. Specifically, the following variables were extracted for each participant: RXQSEENBETA—a derived variable indicating whether the participant was identified as a β-blocker user based on their medication records; RXDDAYSBETA—the number of days the participant reported using the β-blocker medication; RXDCOUNTBETA—the number of different β-blocker medications the participant reported using.

Classification of exposure

Participants were classified as β-blocker users if the variable RXQSEENBETA indicated confirmed use. All other participants were classified as non-users. This created a binary exposure variable (user vs non-user) for the primary analysis.

Handling of missing data

Participants with missing or indeterminate data in the RXQSEENBETA variable were excluded from the analysis to ensure a clear comparison between defined user and non-user groups.

To obtain a sufficiently large sample size, we pooled data from 10 consecutive 20-year NHANES cycles (1999–2000 through 2017–2018). Starting with 40 033 NHANES records from 1999 to 2018 with the report of β-blocker use. After excluding 37 718 persons without AMI, we removed four individuals whose mortality data were missing and three individuals whose follow-up time was zero, leaving 2308 subjects for the final analytical cohort (figure 1).

Ethical considerations

Ethical oversight and participant protection are woven into every stage of NHANES. Each eligible individual receives a clear explanation of the study’s aims, procedures, risks and benefits and then signs a written informed consent form before any interview question is asked or any examination or laboratory test is performed. For the present secondary analysis, we strictly adhered to the NHANES data use guidelines, ensuring that all confidentiality requirements and analytical standards set by the National Center for Health Statistics were fully respected.

Outcome assessment

To determine vital status, each NHANES participant’s encrypted identifier was linked to the National Death Index (NDI) through 31 December 2018; any death recorded in the NDI by that date was counted as an all-cause mortality event, providing complete and uniform follow-up for every individual included in the analysis (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/data-linkage/mortality-public.htm). Each death certificate captured both the fact of death and its underlying cause. The shared unique identifier created a one-to-one match between NHANES and NDI records, guaranteeing complete and uniform follow-up. We calculated individual person-time (in months) from the date of the NHANES interview or examination to the date of death or to 31 December 2018, whichever came first.

Covariates

Several covariates were included a priori based on their known associations with either β-blocker use or mortality. Multivariable models were adjusted for the following covariates: demographic factors (age, sex, race/ethnicity, marital status, poverty to income ratio and three-level education), behavioural factors (four-category smoking, six-level alcohol consumption) and body mass index (BMI). Missing indicators were preserved in the analyses.

MI was defined on the basis of participants’ self-reported response to the following question: “Has a doctor or other health professional ever told you that you had a heart attack or myocardial infarction?”13 14

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as means±SDs for continuous variables and as frequencies (percentages) for categorical variables. Participants were stratified by survival status (survivors vs all-cause death) for baseline comparisons. Intergroup differences were assessed using analysis of variance for normally distributed continuous variables, the Kruskal-Wallis H test for non-normally distributed continuous variables and χ2 tests for categorical variables.

Differences across β-blocker usage groups were evaluated using t-tests (for normally distributed data), the Kruskal-Wallis H test (for skewed data) for continuous variables and χ2 tests for categorical variables.

HRs with corresponding 95% CIs for the association between β-blocker treatment and all-cause mortality were derived using Cox proportional hazards regression models. Multiple analytical approaches were employed, including multivariable Cox regression, propensity score (PS) matching and inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW), to address potential confounding.

Covariates were selected based on clinical relevance and included age, sex, marital status, education level, self-reported health status and annual income.

To address potential confounding, several analytical methods were applied, including multivariable Cox regression, PS matching and IPTW. Optimal full matching was performed using the Matchit package in R, with PS derived from a generalised additive model. Treatment effects were estimated using a generalised additive model with a logit link function. Cluster-robust SEs (estimated via the vcovCL() function from the R sandwich package) were used to account for pair membership in matched sets.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the findings: (1) Stratified analyses were performed across all covariates. Unadjusted and adjusted logistic regression models were used to calculate ORs for the association between β-blocker use and mortality in crude and stratified analyses. (2) IPTW was applied using PS to evaluate the relationship between β-blocker usage groups and the outcome of all-cause death.15 (3) Multiple imputation was used to handle missing data.16 (4) E-values were calculated to quantify the potential influence of unmeasured confounders on the association between green space and stroke risk.17 (5) A real-world comprehensive analysis was performed based on the duration of β-blocker use to evaluate its impact on all-cause mortality.

Results

Study population

This study included 2308 patients with MI from NHANES 1999–2018, with a mean follow-up of 84.6 months. Baseline characteristics are presented in table 1. Among all participants, the mean age was 68.73±11.50 years, 64.86% were male and 60.75% were non-Hispanic white. All-cause mortality occurred in 51.52% of the total population. Significant differences were observed between β-blocker users (n=345) and non-users (n=1963) in age (66.19±9.61 years vs 69.18±11.75 years, p<0.0001), ethnicity (p=0.0002), marital status (p=0.0298), BMI (p<0.0001), weight (p<0.0001) and waist circumference (p<0.0001). Interestingly, the mortality rate was significantly lower in β-blocker users compared with non-users (40.58% vs 53.44%, p<0.001). Online supplemental table S1 presents baseline characteristics stratified by β-blocker treatment duration. Baseline characteristics showed a substantial imbalance in actual treatment duration between the groups defined by the median split (standardised difference=0.6). Participants in the low-duration group (mean 905.3±645.4 days) were significantly younger (64.72±9.86 years vs 68.05±8.97 years, p=0.001) and had higher systolic blood pressure (69.02±12.92 mm Hg vs 65.07±12.53 mm Hg, p=0.007) compared with the high-duration group (mean 7770.3±17 296.3 days).

Factors influencing the risk of all-cause mortality

In the univariable analysis (table 2), β-blocker use was associated with a significant reduction in all-cause mortality (OR 0.81, 95% CI 0.68 to 0.96, p=0.0178). Several other factors were also significantly associated with mortality. Increasing age was a strong risk factor (OR 1.07, 95% CI 1.06 to 1.08, p<0.0001). Compared with non-Hispanic white participants, all other ethnic groups had significantly lower odds of mortality. Being widowed, divorced or separated was associated with higher risk (OR 1.52, 95% CI 1.35 to 1.71, p<0.0001) compared with being married. A higher BMI (OR 0.97, 95% CI 0.96 to 0.98, p<0.0001) and higher educational attainment were associated with protective effects.

Association of β-blocker use with all-cause mortality

In the multivariable analyses presented in table 3, the initial association between β-blocker use and reduced all-cause mortality observed in the crude analysis (HR 0.81, 95% CI 0.68 to 0.96, p=0.0178) was no longer statistically significant after adjustment (table 3). After adjustment for all listed covariates (age, sex, ethnicity, marital status, poverty to income ratio, education, smoking, alcohol use and BMI), the HR was 1.04 (95% CI 0.87 to 1.25, p=0.6733). Similarly, adjustment using the PS yielded an HR of 1.01 (95% CI 0.84 to 1.21, p=0.9356). Adjustment using a smoothed PS also showed a null association (HR 1.00, 95% CI 0.83 to 1.20, p=0.9672).

Stratified analysis of β-blocker use and all-cause mortality

Stratified analyses were performed to assess potential effect modification by baseline covariates (figure 2). No significant interaction was observed between β-blocker use and any covariate, as all p values for interaction exceeded 0.05. However, within certain subgroups, β-blocker use was associated with significantly reduced mortality. These included females (OR 0.70, 95% CI 0.51 to 0.96, p=0.029), widowed/divorced/separated individuals (OR 0.72, 95% CI 0.54 to 0.96, p=0.0259), those with a nearly poor poverty to income ratio (OR 0.69, 95% CI 0.52 to 0.92, p=0.012), participants with below high school education (OR 0.57, 95% CI 0.39 to 0.84, p=0.0048), never alcohol users (OR 0.60, 95% CI 0.37 to 0.99, p=0.0456) and those in the high diastolic blood pressure group (OR 0.65, 95% CI 0.47 to 0.89, p=0.0071).

Sensitivity analysis

One sensitivity analysis was implemented by examining the impact of treatment duration on all-cause mortality to assess the real-world effectiveness of β-blocker initiation. Baseline characteristics of β-blocker users stratified by treatment duration are presented in online supplemental table S1. Patients in the long-term treatment group were significantly older than those in the short-term group (68.05 years vs 64.72 years, p=0.001). Conversely, as expected, long-term users had significantly lower systolic blood pressure (65.07 mm Hg vs 69.02 mm Hg, p=0.007), likely reflecting both the cause and effect of sustained therapy. All-cause mortality was not statistically different between the groups in an unadjusted comparison (42.76% vs 38.86%, p=0.464). As shown in online supplemental table S2, longer β-blocker treatment duration was initially associated with a significant reduction in all-cause mortality in the crude analysis (HR 0.63, 95% CI 0.45 to 0.88, p=0.0065). However, after multivariable adjustment for all covariates, the association was attenuated and only marginally significant (HR 0.66, 95% CI 0.45 to 0.96, p=0.0304). Further adjustment using PS methods and IPTW yielded non-significant results across all estimates (eg, ATT: HR 0.89, 95% CI 0.53 to 1.49, p=0.6581).

Another sensitivity analysis was conducted using multiple imputation to handle missing data. The results were consistent with the primary complete-case analysis, yielding a null association between β-blocker use and mortality (adjusted HR 1.02, 95% CI 0.85 to 1.22, p=0.8536) (online supplemental table S3).

Discussion

The primary finding of this study, based on a nationally representative sample of 2308 patients with MI from NHANES 1999–2018, is that the association between β-blocker use and all-cause mortality is not significant after multivariable adjustment for demographic, clinical and socioeconomic factors. This result is further supported by PS-based methods and IPTW, which also showed a null association.

In 2024, the REDUCE-AMI investigators reported the first large, randomised, open-label trial devoted to patients with MI and preserved ejection fraction (LVEF≥50%) who were already receiving contemporary care. After a median follow-up of 3.5 years, routine β-blockers produced no discernible reduction in the primary composite of death or recurrent MI (HR 0.96, 95% CI 0.79 to 1.16).18 In the REBOOT trial, 4985 MI survivors with LVEF ≥40% were randomly assigned to continue or to stop/avoid β-blockers; after 3.5 years, the composite of death, reinfarction or heart failure admission was almost identical (HR 0.96, 95% CI 0.79 to 1.16), leading the authors to conclude that routine long-term β-blockers are unnecessary when systolic function is preserved.19 This is consistent with the notion that the benefit of β-blockers is largely confined to patients with reduced systolic function. By contrast, the 2025 individual patient data meta-analysis of 22 earlier RCTs that pooled 29 133 patients whose LVEF ranged from 30% to 49% showed a significant 23% reduction in all-cause mortality (HR 0.77, 95% CI 0.66 to 0.89) and 18% fewer recurrent MIs with β-blockers, with benefit strongest in the 40–49% LVEF stratum.11 These findings collectively emphasise the heterogeneity of β-blocker effects across different LVEF categories.

In a nationwide Korean cohort of 87 980 post-PCI survivors (38 246 with AMI and 49 734 with angina) between 2013 and 2017, β-blocker use was associated with significantly lower mortality in patients with AMI (HR 0.78, 95% CI 0.69 to 0.87) but not in those with angina (HR 1.07, 95% CI 0.98 to 1.16).20 This analysis used NHANES data (1999–2018) to evaluate the association between β-blocker use and all-cause mortality after MI in a real-world cohort aged 20–85 years. The cohort spans a wide calendar and age range and features long follow-up (mean 84.6 months), but lacks LVEF measurements. This absence of LVEF data limits our ability to stratify by systolic function. Thus, the study provides long-term, real-world evidence on post-MI β-blocker exposure across heterogeneous patients and treatment contexts. This study found that the initial association between β-blocker use and reduced all-cause mortality observed in the crude analysis (HR 0.81, 95% CI 0.68 to 0.96, p=0.0178) was no longer statistically significant after adjustment (HR 1.04, 95% CI 0.87 to 1.25, p=0.6733). Our findings, therefore, contribute to a growing body of evidence suggesting that the a priori assumed benefits of β-blockers may be less pronounced in the current therapeutic landscape.

Although the overall estimate was null, unadjusted subgroup analyses uncovered signals of benefit in several demographic strata: female sex (OR 0.70, 95% CI 0.51 to 0.96), limited education (OR 0.57, 95% CI 0.39 to 0.84) and a near poverty to income ratio (OR 0.69, 95% CI 0.52 to 0.92). These exploratory patterns were discussed in response to reviewer comments and highlight possible effect modification by socioeconomic factors. These differences were not preserved after formal interaction testing (all p interaction value >0.05), so they should be viewed as hypothesis generating rather than definitive. Nevertheless, they sit alongside emerging trial evidence—exemplified by REBOOT—that β-blockers may offer no advantage, or even possible harm, in certain populations such as women with preserved LVEF,19 21 underscoring the need to reappraise their routine use in contemporary post-MI care. These findings are noteworthy and warrant further investigation, as they might reflect underlying differences in disease presentation, comorbidities or adherence patterns in these specific populations.22 23 For example, studies have indicated that adherence to medical therapies may be lower in certain socioeconomic groups, and the protective effect of β-blockers might be more evident where it is truly needed and consistently used.24 It is crucial to interpret these subgroup-specific findings cautiously due to the lack of significant interaction, which means these associations could be driven by residual confounding within these smaller subgroups rather than a true differential effect of the medication. This reinforces the emphasis on the need for individualised β-blocker prescription based on patient phenotype and comorbidity burden.

Our study’s sensitivity analyses provide further context for the primary findings. A key challenge in observational research is confounding by indication, particularly the ‘healthy user’ bias, where patients who adhere to a medication may be inherently healthier.25 Our analysis of treatment duration sought to address this. Interestingly, we found that long-term β-blocker users were significantly older but had lower systolic blood pressure, potentially confounding the association. To mitigate this, multiple analytical approaches including multivariable adjustment, PS matching and inverse probability weighting were used, all converging on a null result. The number of β-blocker users (n=345) was relatively small, and the reported treatment durations showed extreme variability (905.3±645.4 days vs 7770.3±17 296.3 days), which could limit the robustness of this specific analysis. The unadjusted analysis of treatment duration initially showed a significant protective effect (OR 0.63, 95% CI 0.45 to 0.88, p=0.0065). However, similar to the main analysis, this association was substantially attenuated after multivariable adjustment and completely non-significant after using PS methods (eg, ATT: HR 0.89, 95% CI 0.53 to 1.49, p=0.6581). This suggests that the apparent benefit of long-term use is possibly explained by underlying patient characteristics, such as healthier lifestyle and better adherence to other treatments, rather than the medication itself. Furthermore, the consistency of our primary results with the multiple imputation sensitivity analysis (adjusted HR 1.02, 95% CI 0.85 to 1.22, p=0.8536) strengthens our conclusion. This consistency indicates that our findings are not dependent on the handling of missing data, reinforcing the robustness of the null association between β-blocker use and all-cause mortality in the overall study population. The small sample size of β-blocker users and the significant prescription differences observed are critical limitations that may have impacted the statistical power to detect a true effect, if one exists.26

Limitations

As an observational study, it is subject to residual confounding: the cohort’s population specificity is limited and may not generalise to non-US or trial populations. Importantly, detailed clinical data such as LVEF, MI subtype (ST-elevation myocardial infarction vs non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction), revascularisation status, presence of atrial fibrillation or heart failure symptoms, and β-blocker indication, dosage or adherence were unavailable in NHANES. These missing variables limit our ability to stratify the prognostic association of β-blocker therapy by key clinical characteristics and to personalise therapeutic implications. Medication exposure, dosing, adherence and indication details as well as timing of MI, revascularisation status and other disease-specific clinical data were incompletely captured, which could lead to potential misclassification and recall bias given the self-reported nature of NHANES medication data. Only 345 participants were recorded as β-blocker users, producing limited power for treated group and subgroup analyses and substantial heterogeneity in treatment duration and likely dosing/indication. Baseline medication status may not reflect changes during follow-up (initiation, discontinuation or dose changes), leading to exposure misclassification over time. Self-reported and survey-based measures entail potential measurement error and missing data. Finally, contemporaneous treatment data such as completeness of revascularisation, DAPT duration/intensity, statin intensity or RAASi use were insufficiently granular to fully account for modern background therapies that may modify the association between β-blocker use and mortality.

Conclusion

In conclusion, in the contemporary era of MI management, the use of β-blockers may not be significantly associated with a reduction in all-cause mortality in the overall population. The present findings, when interpreted alongside recent randomised and observational evidence, support the view that long-term β-blocker therapy should be personalised rather than routine. Our results align with a shift in the academic discussion around the broad application of β-blockers and underscore the need for new, large-scale RCTs to re-evaluate their role in the current treatment paradigm.
