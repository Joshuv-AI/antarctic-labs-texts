Effects of the artificial solidified crust on ground settlement during full scaffold construction in a coastal region
Authors: Kaizhe Shang, Long Chen, Yi Zhu, Chenlei Xie, Bingyi Li, Xing Ai Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe full scaffold construction method is widely used in cast-in-place box beam construction and is especially sensitive to foundation settlement in soft soil areas, which can significantly impact project quality. Based on the Zhejiang Shiyitang Expressway project, this study proposed a combination of superficial ground treatment and preloading method to reduce costs and improve construction efficiency. An artificial solidified crust layer (ASCL) with a depth of 1.5 m was constructed using in-situ solidification technology, achieving a bearing capacity of 180 kPa. Settlement, pore water pressure, and lateral displacement at various depths were monitored during the foundation preloading, scaffold preloading, and cast-in-place box beam loading stages through in-situ testing and numerical modeling. The ground settlement 30 days after concrete casting was 15.4 mm, with most of the settlement occurring during the foundation and scaffold preloading stages. The ASCL was included in the additional stress calculation, resulting in a 27% reduction in total settlement compared to conditions without the ASCL. The influence of permeability coefficient, soil compression modulus, loading mode, and load application area was analyzed using the validated numerical model. The final settlements were found to be similar for soils with varying permeability coefficients, indicating that the superficial ground treatment method is suitable for different soil types. The “effective range” of foundation preloading refers to the width between the bridge web and the bridge pile foundation.Keywords: Full scaffold construction, Artificial solidified crust layer, Finite element analysis, Multi-stage loading, SettlementSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Natural hazards

Introduction

As the social economy continues to develop rapidly, the demand for bridge construction has steadily increased, reflecting ongoing investments in infrastructure and the critical need to enhance transportation capacity. However, the widespread presence of soft soil foundations in many regions presents significant challenges to the progress of such projects1–8. The high compressibility, low bearing capacity, and complex characteristics of soft soils make foundation settlement control particularly difficult9,10, especially in managing differential settlement during the pouring process. Failure to control differential settlement can lead to cracks in the box girder, compromising both the quality and safety of the project11,12. Therefore, research into foundation settlement control techniques for soft soils is essential to ensure the long-term stability and reliability of infrastructure.

The conventional approach to bridge construction in soft soil areas typically involves using machinery, such as rollers, to compact the soil13–15; replacing in-situ soft soil with soil that meets the required standards; or leveraging terrain and geological conditions by appropriately arranging or extending the approach bridge to place the bridge abutment in areas with better soil quality or thinner soft soil16. However, these methods are often unsuitable for deep soft soil layers, where the compressibility of the underlying layers can still result in significant post-construction settlement. For instance, soil compaction may fail to achieve the desired settlement reduction when dealing with deep layers of loose or saturated soils. Similarly, soil replacement methods can incur high costs, especially when the soft soil extends to considerable depths, rendering the project financially unfeasible in many cases. Moreover, these solutions frequently fail to control differential settlement, leading to cracks and other structural issues in bridges and other infrastructure projects. As an alternative, the trestle method is often employed to address these challenges by providing a temporary construction platform suspended above the soft soil, thereby avoiding direct contact with unstable soil. The main construction steps include pre-treatment of the soft soil foundation, construction of the temporary trestle bridge, pile foundation construction, structural construction, and dismantling of the trestle bridge. Additionally, the movable scaffolding system (MSS) is used to advance to the next construction section via the guide rail or support system installed on the pier, allowing for efficient, rapid, and straightforward installation17,18. Although this method is suitable for various types of bridges, it is costly and not well-suited for curved sections.

In recent years, in-situ solidification technology has garnered significant attention as an effective method to improve the bearing capacity and mitigate settlement issues in soft soil areas. This technology involves powerful mixing to combine stabilizers with the soil, forming an in-situ ASCL of specific thickness and strength. This ASCL, along with the underlying subsoil, creates a double-layered foundation with enhanced bearing capacity19. Moreover, the ASCL exhibits a relatively large internal friction angle and low cohesion. Its presence diffuses stress, thereby increasing the horizontal bearing capacity of pile foundations as the thickness of the ASCL grows. Additionally, the settlement and lateral displacement of the soft soil foundation are significantly reduced. The in-situ solidified double-layered foundation offers several advantages over traditional methods, including lower construction costs, minimal environmental disruption, and the ability to treat deep soft soil layers that conventional techniques cannot effectively address. Consequently, this technology has been widely applied in dredged sludge reinforcement20, land reclamation21, embankments6, low-rise buildings22, and similar projects. Beyond its standalone application, in-situ solidification is often combined with other ground improvement techniques, such as surcharge preloading or vacuum preloading, to achieve superior results. Salehi and Sivakugan examined the effects of lime-clay modification on the consolidation behavior of dredged mud. Their findings demonstrated that mixing lime with dredged mud slurry before discharging it into a containment pond enhances the effectiveness of preloading on the dredged mud layer23. Wang et al. introduced a combined method of vacuum preloading and lime treatment to address insufficient soil consolidation24. Experimental results revealed that this method significantly increased the vane shear strength of all soil layers, particularly the deep layers, and accelerated the consolidation rate.

Based on a real-world bridge project in a mudflat area, this study investigates a combined foundation treatment scheme involving an artificial solidified crust layer and staged preloading to control post-construction settlement. Following the completion of bridge pile construction, in-situ soil within the designated area was solidified25,26, and a concrete surface layer was applied for preloading. Settlement during foundation preloading, scaffold preloading, and concrete casting was monitored using standard cubic concrete blocks (1 m × 1 m × 1 m) of the required weight. Previous studies have mainly examined the effects of individual factors, such as permeability coefficient or preloading method, on settlement behavior under loading conditions. However, the settlement evolution and deformation control mechanism of shallow-treated foundations reinforced by an artificial solidified crust layer, particularly under multi-stage construction loading, remain insufficiently understood27–32. To address this gap, the present study combines field monitoring, numerical simulation, and theoretical analysis to systematically investigate the settlement behavior of this combined treatment scheme under different construction stages, with particular attention to the effects of permeability coefficient30–38, loading mode39,40, and loading rate41. Through an engineering case study, the performance of a combined surface treatment and preloading scheme for full scaffold construction in coastal soft ground is evaluated, and the influences of staged loading and key soil parameters on settlement development and post-construction stability are clarified. The results offer both mechanistic insight and practical guidance for optimizing settlement control strategies in soft soil regions.

Geology and engineering condition

Geology

The Shiyitang Expressway connects with the Hangzhou Bay Sea-Crossing Bridge in Zhejiang Province, China. The entire expressway is designed as a girder bridge, with the cast-in-situ box girder constructed using the full scaffold method.

Figure 1 illustrates the distribution and key parameters of the soil layers. The soil predominantly consists of clay and sand, with clay primarily distributed between 0 and 90 m and below 105 m, and fine sand mainly found below 85 m. The range of various clay indicators is relatively broad, with unit weight typically between 16 and 20 kN/m3, cohesion ranging from 0 to 40 kPa, friction angle from 0–20°, and compression modulus between 0 and 15 MPa. In contrast, the fine sand layers exhibit relatively narrower parameter ranges, with unit weight generally around 20 kN/m3, cohesion close to 0–2 kPa, friction angle approximately 30–35°, and compression modulus mainly around 15–20 MPa.

Meanwhile, test results of the undisturbed sludge indicate that the in-situ sludge has a moisture content of 50–60% and contains a small amount of organic matter. The surface soil and the third soil layer consist of silty clay with low bearing capacities of 45 kPa and 60 kPa, respectively. The bearing capacities of other soil layers exceed 100 kPa.

Originally, Φ630 steel pipe piles with a length of 44.5 m were designed as the deep ground-treatment scheme for the temporary trestle foundation. However, this approach was cost-prohibitive and might also have adversely affected the newly constructed bridge piles. Therefore, a combined scheme consisting of superficial ground treatment and preloading was adopted in this project to reduce construction cost and improve construction efficiency.

Field test and numerical model arrangement

Monitoring instruments

An in-situ test was conducted to assess the effects of the ASCL, considering various stages of foundation preloading, scaffold preloading, and cast-in-place box beam loading. Instruments were embedded to monitor surface settlement, pore water pressure, and lateral displacement of the foundation. As shown in Fig. 4, two settlement nails were placed on top of the 16.5 m long and 9.9 m wide concrete layer—one at the center and the other at a corner of the layer. By processing the data from these settlement nails, the settlement values at the center and corner of the hardened concrete layer were obtained. Additionally, two earth pressure gauges and two pore water pressure gauges were installed at 2.4 m and 6.4 m depths on the right side of the center settlement nail. The vibrating wire earth pressure gauge (JTM-V2000B) had a diameter of 14 cm, a height of 3.5 cm, and a measurement range of 0-400 kPa. The vibrating wire pore water pressure gauge (JTM-V3000) had a diameter of 6 cm, a height of 20 cm, and a maximum measurement range of 600 kPa. A frequency meter with a precision of 0.1 Hz was used to record data from the earth pressure and vibrating wire pore water pressure gauges. The inclinometer (HY-808 F) included a PVC tube with a diameter of 70 mm and a length of 20 m, used to measure the lateral displacement of the subgrade with a precision of 1 mm. Two inclinometer tubes were installed on-site to monitor horizontal displacement, located at the center and near the short side at a depth of 15.5 m. Prior to use, the direction of the inclinometer guide groove was corrected, as shown in Fig. 4.

Test instrument layout (unit: cm).

Earth pressure gauges, pore water pressure gauges, and inclinometer tubes were installed using a drilling rig, which typically had a diameter of 90 mm. Steel cages were employed to position the gauges at different depths, and bentonite was used to fill the drilled holes.

In-situ loading test and the settlement characteristics

To prevent cracks caused by differential settlement, it is crucial to control the settlement deformation of the treated foundation during the casting process within a reasonable range. Therefore, the actual construction process involves foundation preloading, scaffold preloading, and pouring. During foundation preloading, settlement stabilizes through load application. The primary purpose of scaffold preloading is to eliminate joints in the support structure. To determine the characteristics of foundation settlement and deformation during different stages, field tests for both foundation preloading and scaffold preloading were conducted. These tests utilized standard cubic concrete blocks (1 m × 1 m × 1 m) corresponding to various loads. In the in-situ test, three-dimensional finite element numerical simulation software was used to replicate the construction processes of the first two steps and to model the casting process. The numerical model, constructed based on the test setup and results, was used to simulate the casting progress.

The simulated settlement at the corner point was higher than the measured value, while at the center point, the simulated value was lower. During the concrete pouring stage, the simulated settlements at the center and corner points were 15.4 mm and 16.7 mm, respectively.

Lateral displacement

Figure 9 presents the comparison between the measured and simulated horizontal displacements of two inclinometer pipes at the end of foundation preloading. Pipe-1, positioned on the long side of the preloading area, predominantly exhibits positive displacement (away from the preloading area). This is mainly because Pipe-1 was located near the boundary of the surcharge loading area, where the soil was more likely to undergo outward lateral squeezing under the applied load. With increasing depth, lateral displacement gradually diminishes, mirroring the distribution characteristics of additional stress. The maximum lateral displacement of Pipe-1 is 18 mm, with 10 mm observed at the bottom of the solidified layer. Pipe-2, situated at the center of the foundation treatment area, was installed after surface soil solidification. Field measurements for Pipe-2 predominantly show negative displacement (toward the preloading area), with a maximum of less than 3 mm. This behavior can be attributed to its location within the treated zone, where the surrounding soil was more strongly constrained by the loading area and the artificial solidified crust layer, resulting in slight inward soil movement under the combined effects of loading and boundary confinement. The simulation results for Pipe-2 exhibit minor fluctuations between positive and negative values, with a maximum error of less than 1 mm compared to the measured results, indicating that the simulation reasonably approximates the field conditions.

Comparison of settlement calculation results.

Figure 13 shows a significant difference in the settlement of topsoil depending on the presence of the ASCL. A comparison of settlement values 30 days after concrete pouring reveals that the calculated settlement of topsoil without an ASCL is 68 mm, whereas with an ASCL, it is 49.6 mm. Similarly, the simulated settlement values are 62 mm without the ASCL and 55 mm with it. Overall, the settlement of the top two soil layers with the ASCL is reduced by 27% and 11%, respectively.

The ASCL significantly reduces the settlement of the upper mucky soil. As it partially replaces the upper soil layer, the improved compression modulus of the ASCL decreases the compression deformation of the soil. The lower silty soil, which inherently possesses a high compression modulus, experiences reduced settlement primarily due to the decrease in additional stress resulting from forced diffusion, thereby further minimizing compression. Theoretical calculations reveal that the ASCL not only mitigates the compression deformation of the soft soil layer by reducing the compressibility of the upper soft soil but also decreases the additional stress on the lower soil through stress diffusion, ultimately reducing the total settlement of the foundation.

Analysis of influence parameters of foundation settlement

Permeability coefficient

The permeability coefficients of the overlying strata vary significantly44. The surface layer and the third layer exhibit the lowest permeability coefficient at 0.01391 m/day with the thickness 3 m and 43.5 m. Conversely, the second layer in the upper soil (21.8 m thick) has the highest permeability coefficient, recorded at 0.08986 m/day. This suggests that the settlement of the soil layer may be influenced by the relatively high permeability of the second layer. To assess the applicability of the preloading and full scaffold method in coastal beach areas, this section investigates the effect of varying permeability coefficient of the single soil layer on foundation settlement.

Soils with varying permeability coefficients were selected to compare settlement and settlement rate. The selected permeability coefficients are 1 m/day, 0.1 m/day, 0.01 m/day, and 0.0001 m/day, respectively. Figures 14 and 15 show that settlement gradually increases with the applied load, and the settlement rate increases first and then decrease at each loading stage. The next load can be applied once the settlement rate falls below 0.1 mm/h. The maximum settlement rate reaches 0.20 mm/h. Soils with higher permeability coefficients exhibit greater settlement rates and values. After the final load is applied, the settlement stabilizes over time. The total settlements for the three soils with higher permeability coefficients are 24.139 mm, 24.11 mm, and 23.811 mm, respectively, the final settlement for the soil with the lowest permeability coefficient is 22.611 mm. After the construction of the upper cast-in-place box girder, the foundation settlement within the first three days of the initial setting of concrete plays a critical role in determining the overall construction quality of the cast-in-place box girder. The post-construction settlement of the three types of soil with higher permeability coefficients are 1.522 mm, 1.522 mm and 2.048 mm, respectively, 19 days after loading. In contrast, the soil with the lowest permeability coefficient settled 7.806 mm after 19 days, which is significantly higher than the settlement of the soils with higher permeability coefficients. This discrepancy is primarily due to the lower permeability coefficient of the soil, which requires a much longer time for consolidation under load compared to soils with higher permeability coefficients. However, within three days after concrete placement, the settlements of the three types of soil with higher permeability coefficients are 0.103 mm, 0.109 mm, and 0.137 mm, respectively.

Soil compression modulus

In Figs. 14 and 15, the permeability coefficient varies by four orders of magnitude, ranging from 1 to 0.0001. Despite this substantial variation, the settlement value and settlement rate predicted by the model do not exhibit significant changes, which may be attributed to the large compression modulus of soil layer 2. To further investigate, the compression modulus of the lower soil layer was adjusted, and the results are presented in Fig. 16.

Influence curve of permeability coefficient on settlement of surface soil.

The variation in the permeability coefficient significantly influences soil settlement, and within the examined range and under the present numerical conditions, settlement generally tends to increase with increasing permeability coefficient45,46. However, an anomaly occurs when the permeability coefficient of soil with a modulus of 4 MPa falls within the range of 0.01 to 0.1. This anomaly may be attributed to the relatively low soil modulus, under which the permeability coefficient remains the dominant factor driving soil deformation. Consequently, it can be concluded that when the soil compression modulus is less than 6 MPa, the permeability coefficient is the primary factor affecting settlement, and the overall trend within the examined range generally follows the principle that settlement tends to increase with increasing permeability coefficient. Conversely, when the soil compression modulus is 6 MPa or higher, the compression modulus becomes the primary factor influencing settlement, while the effect of permeability coefficient still generally follows the same trend under the present numerical conditions. As the compression modulus increases, the settlement curves move downward overall, indicating a reduction in foundation settlement.

Loading mode

As discussed earlier, staged loading has increasingly supplanted the traditional single-stage loading method, proving to be more effective for soil consolidation. Consequently, this section will conduct a detailed analysis of the impact of these two different loading methods on foundation settlement.

Plaxis 3D software was employed to simulate the single-stage loading process, where a daily load of 45.3 kPa was applied continuously for 30 days. The foundation preloading process was modeled by setting a 9-day loading period, while the scaffold preloading process was simulated with a 3-day loading period.

The simulated settlement values at the surface (0 m) and bottom (− 3 m) of the powdery clay are presented in Fig. 17a. Using two different loading methods, the time-dependent settlement curves were obtained. The settlement values at the surface (0 m) of the topsoil under multi-stage loading and single-stage loading were found to be 55.03 mm and 53.4 mm, respectively. The comparison indicates a difference of 1.63 mm between the two loading methods at the surface level.

Settlement comparison diagram of two loading methods: (a) Comparison of settlement under two loading modes; (b) Settlement comparison of different loading modes at the end of different stages.

The comparative settlement values of soil layers at different depths at the end of various processes are illustrated in Fig. 17b. The total settlement from the initial stage is considered as the foundation preloading process, while the differential settlement between the scaffold preloading and concrete pouring stages is taken from the preceding stage. The results indicate that after step-by-step (multi-stage) loading and unloading, the settlement value is significantly smaller than that observed with one-time (single-stage) loading, with a reduction of approximately 25 mm.

The primary difference between the two loading methods lies in the distinct patterns of settlement change over time during the loading stage and the varying total differential settlement at the time of concrete pouring. In summary, this section provides recommendations on the applicability of different loading methods: For projects with a flexible construction schedule and stringent post-construction settlement requirements, multi-stage loading is recommended. Conversely, for projects with a relatively tight construction schedule and less stringent post-construction settlement requirements, single-stage loading is advisable. Although the settlement control effect of single-stage loading is not as effective as multi-stage loading, it can still meet the settlement control standards specified, while also offering considerable convenience during the preloading stage.

Load application area

The impact of the load application area on settlement is primarily determined by the width of the preload (i.e., the length of the load application area along the cross-sectional direction of the road). The preloading width on-site is 8.3 m for the bridge web and 9.2 m between the bridge pile foundations. In this study, the interval from the bridge web to the adjacent bridge pile foundation is defined as the “effective range” of preloading width, because within this range the surcharge directly affects the main settlement-control zone of the foundation. In this section, three points (8.5 m, 8.7 m, and 9.0 m) between the bridge web and the bridge pile foundation are analyzed, along with one point every 1 m between 9.2 m and 15 m. The corresponding curve is depicted in Fig. 18.

Foundation settlement with different preloading widths.

The results indicate that when the preloading width is within the range of the bridge web and the bridge pile foundation (i.e., 8.3 m to 9.2 m), the foundation settlement increases linearly with the increase in preloading width, with a steep slope: the settlement at 8.3 m is 71.72 mm 30 days after pouring, while at 9.2 m, it increases by 9% to 78.15 mm. However, when the preloading width extends beyond the bridge pile foundation, its influence on foundation settlement becomes negligible, with a maximum variation of less than 1%. This indicates that the “effective range” corresponds to the width interval in which preloading enlargement produces a pronounced settlement response; once the surcharge extends beyond the adjacent bridge pile foundation, the additional loaded area contributes little to settlement beneath the bridge structure. Finally, by comparing post-construction settlement across different preloading widths, it is evident that whether or not the preloading width falls within the “effective range”, the post-construction settlement remains relatively constant at approximately 4.82 mm.

Conclusions

Based on a real-world project in a mudflat area, this work investigates the settlement characteristics of foundations under shallow treatment. The effects of the permeability coefficient, soil compression modulus, loading mode, and load application area on foundation settlement and settlement rate were analyzed. The main conclusions are as follows:

When the upper mucky soil is solidified, the compressibility of the upper soft soil is reduced, thereby minimizing the compression deformation of the soft soil layer. Additionally, the presence of the ASCL induces stress diffusion within the foundation, which helps decrease the additional stress on the lower soil, consequently reducing settlement.
