Mass generation for the two dimensional O(N) Linear Sigma Model in the large N limit
Matías G. Delgadino, Scott A. Smith

Abstract
This work studies the O ⁡ ( N ) O(N) Linear Sigma Model on ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} under a scaling dictated by the formal 1 / N 1/N expansion. We show that in the large N N limit, correlations decay exponentially fast, where the acquired mass decays exponentially in the inverse temperature. In fact, each marginal converges to a massive Gaussian Free Field (GFF) on ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} , quantified in the 2 2 -Wasserstein distance with a weighted H 1 ​ ( ℝ 2 ) H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}) cost function. In contrast to prior work on the torus via parabolic stochastic quantization, our results hold without restrictions on the coupling constants, allowing us to also obtain a massive GFF in a suitable double scaling limit. Our proof combines the Feyel/Üstünel extension of Talagrand’s inequality with some classical tools in Euclidean Quantum Field Theory.

Introduction

Formally, given an integer
N\geq 2
, the
O(N)
Non-Linear Sigma Model on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a probability measure over vector-valued distributions
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{N}
with density

\frac{1}{\mathbf{Z}}\exp\bigg(-\frac{\beta}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\|\nabla\Phi(x)\|_{\mathbb{R}^{N\times 2}}^{2}dx\bigg)\prod_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\delta_{\mathbf{S}^{N-1}}(\Phi(x))d\Phi(x),

(1.1)

where
\beta>0
denotes the inverse temperature and

\mathbf{S}^{N-1}
is the
N-1
dimensional unit sphere. Despite being a fundamental model in the physics literature on Euclidean Quantum Field Theory (EQFT) c.f
[
57
,
13
]
, its rigorous mathematical construction remains a long-standing open problem in mathematical physics. A major difficulty is that the reference measure

\prod_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\delta_{\mathbf{S}^{N-1}}(\Phi(x))d\Phi(x)

(1.2)

lacks a clear definition. Indeed, an uncountable product of the Lebesgue measure is ill-defined, the typical difficulty in EQFT, but more significantly it is not clear how to interpret the constraint dictated by
\delta_{\mathbf{S}^{N-1}}(\Phi(x))
given that we expect the measure to be supported on
\Phi\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{N}
which can not be defined pointwise. One way to circumvent this obstruction is via lattice approximation of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where a countable number of spins are sampled from independent copies of the uniform distribution on
\mathbf{S}^{N-1}
exponentially weighted by a discretized version of the
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
semi-norm, this is known as the spin
O(N)
-model, c.f. Stanley
[
67
]
. This model has been studied extensively in the probability and statistical mechanics literature, but despite much progress, including a classical work of Gawedzki/Kupiainen
[
30
]
on the hierarchical approximation, the continuum limit as the lattice spacing tends to zero has never been constructed. Note
that the case
N=1
is better understood, and we refer the reader to the works
[
15
,
16
]

regarding the continuum limit of the 2d Ising model at the critical temperature.

An alternative way to regularize (
1.1
), which leads to a continuum approximation, is based on softening the restriction of
\Phi(x)
to
\mathbf{S}^{N-1}
via Gaussian approximation of the delta measure. For convenience, we first make the change of variables
\sqrt{\beta}\Phi\mapsto\Phi
, which normalizes the constant multiplying the
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
semi-norm in (
1.1
) and changes the radius of the sphere to
\sqrt{\beta}
in the constraint. Using the approximation

\displaystyle\delta_{\sqrt{\beta}\mathbf{S}^{N-1}}(\Phi(x))\approx\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{8\pi}}\text{exp}\bigg(-\frac{\lambda}{4}(\|\Phi(x)\|_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}^{2}-\beta)^{2}\bigg),

(1.3)

and approximating
\mathbb{R}^{2}
by a periodic torus
\Lambda_{L}
of volume

, one can formally obtain the Non-Linear Sigma Model as
\lambda,L\to\infty
, in the (still formal) density

d\nu_{L}\varpropto\exp\bigg(-\int_{\Lambda_{L}}\bigg(\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla\Phi(x)\|_{\mathbb{R}^{N\times 2}}^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{4}\big(\|\Phi(x)\|_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}^{2}-\beta\big)^{2}\bigg)dx\bigg)\prod_{x\in\Lambda_{L}}d\Phi(x),

(1.4)

which corresponds to the
Linear Sigma Model
on
\Lambda_{L}
, the main probability measure of interest in this manuscript.

The Linear Sigma Model is perhaps best known in the scalar setting
N=1
, which is usually referred to as the
\Phi^{4}_{2}
model. The model on
\Lambda_{L}
, which can be defined rigorously as a probability measure on
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Lambda_{L})^{N}
and denoted in this text by
\nu_{L}

[
54
]
, as exposed in the textbook
[
65
]
of Simon. The connection between the rigorous definition of the probability measure
\nu_{L}
and the formal action (
1.4

\Lambda_{L}
by a finite lattice
\Lambda_{L,\epsilon}
with spacing
\epsilon
such that
L\epsilon^{-1}\in\mathbb{N}
. The measure
\nu_{L,\epsilon}
on
(\mathbb{R}^{N})^{\Lambda_{L,\epsilon}}
is rigorously defined using the reference product Lebesgue measure
\prod_{x\in\Lambda_{L,\epsilon}}d\Phi(x)
with density proportional to

\exp\bigg(-\epsilon^{2}\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{L,\epsilon}}\bigg(\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla_{\epsilon}\Phi(x)\|_{\mathbb{R}^{N\times 2}}^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{4}:\big(\|\Phi(x)\|_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}^{2}-\beta\big)^{2}:dx\bigg)\bigg),

(1.5)

where
\nabla_{\epsilon}
denotes the discrete gradient and

denotes Wick renormalization relative to a GFF with

, see (
3.2
) for the precise definition. The measure
\nu_{L}
can be defined as the unique limiting law as

of a suitable extension of
\nu_{L,\epsilon}
to
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Lambda_{L})^{N}
, a perspective first advocated in the classical work
[

]
in the
N=1

The Linear Sigma Model

on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is then (tentatively) defined as an infinite volume limit

of
\nu_{L}

\lambda,\beta
, but we mostly omit this dependence to keep the notation as lean as possible.

Although subsequential limits of
\nu_{L}
as
L\to\infty
are known to exist, a number of fundamental questions about the limit points remain unsolved in the vector-valued setting
N\geq 2
. In particular, one would like to understand the following:

•

(Uniqueness) Do the measures
\nu_{L}
have a unique limit
\nu
as
L\to\infty
?

•

(Mass Gap) Do the limit point(s) have exponentially decaying correlations?

Closely related to the first question are the recent works
[
7
,
24
]
on uniqueness of the invariant measure for the infinite volume Langevin dynamic. For
N=1
,
[
7
]
established uniqueness as long as the two-point function is integrable, and in particular as long as the mass gap holds, whereas the techniques in
[
24

potentially apply to any

but currently only under a
perturbative assumption
such as
\beta=0
and
\lambda>0

One possible precise definition of mass gap, c.f.
[
24
,
33
]
, is to demand that for all
G,H\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, with their first two derivatives bounded, and test functions
g,h\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
it holds for each component

\big|\mathbb{E}^{\nu}[G(\Phi_{i}.g)H(\Phi_{i}.h(\cdot+z))]-\mathbb{E}^{\nu}[G(\Phi_{i}.g)]\mathbb{E}^{\nu}[H(\Phi_{i}.h(\cdot+z))]\big|\leq Ce^{-m|z|},

(1.6)

for all

, where the constant

is independent of

(but may depend on

). Here and throughout the article we use the notation

to denote the action of a linear form. Note that the rate of exponential decay
m=m(\lambda,\beta)
is referred to as the ‘mass’ and the property (

for the model, the idea being that the quartic potential ‘generates’ a mass, since for
\lambda=0

1.6
) is known to be false. Note that this is in contrast to the massive GFF, a canonical probability measure on
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, denoted in this work by
\mu_{m}
, which is the zero mean Gaussian process with covariance
(-\Delta+m^{2})^{-1}
and satisfies (
1.6
) with mass
m>0
.

The dimension of space plays an important role here, as the fundamental results of Fröhlich/Simon/Spencer
[
28
]
show that (
1.6

\lambda>0
and
\beta

[

which establishes the degeneration of the spectral gap in this case.
As noted in the introduction to
[
28
]

[

[

, correlation inequalities and a continuity argument in
[

[
24

However, in two dimensions the understanding of
N\geq 2
is incomplete and actually a different behavior is expected, as the symmetry group
O(N)
changes from discrete to continuous, as well as Abelian to Non-Abelian when
N\geq 3
. A fundamental conjecture on the mass gap of both the Linear and Non-Linear Sigma Model in two dimensions was formulated by Polyakov
[
58
]
. The most well-known form is for the spin
O(N)
model with
N\geq 3
, where exponential decay of correlations is expected for
all positive

\beta

[
56
]
for a survey on related progress in various lattice models. Furthermore, the mass
m
is conjectured to
decay exponentially in the inverse temperature
. More precisely, given an infinite volume limit formally corresponding to

\frac{1}{Z}\text{exp}\bigg(\sum_{x\sim y}\langle\Phi(x),\Phi(y)\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\bigg)\prod_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\delta_{\sqrt{\beta}\mathbf{S}^{N-1}}(\Phi(x))d\Phi(x),

(1.7)

the conjecture states that for all
z\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}

\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}\langle\Phi(0),\Phi(z)\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\lesssim\exp(-m|z|),\qquad\ln m\approx-\frac{2\pi\beta}{N-2}.

(1.8)

The predicted scaling of
m
with respect to
\beta
is connected to the expected asymptotic freedom of the Non-Linear Sigma Model, as it provides a guideline for how to tentatively renormalize the temperature in the spin
O(N)
model to obtain a continuum limit with a mass gap.
This deep conjecture is based on renormalization group heuristics in
[
58
]
which extend to a broader class of models including Yang-Mills in

, and we mention some related recent progress by Chatterjee
[
18
]
which obtains a massive (Gaussian) gauge field in a suitable scaling limit of the lattice Yang-Mills-Higgs model.

In the present work, we study mass generation in the

Some of the results in

were extended and strengthened in the significant works

[

]
also studies some similar questions as in
[

]
but from the viewpoint of hyperbolic stochastic quantization. Finally, we mention

The idea of using relative entropy bounds to understand mean field limits at stationarity is already present in the paper
[
1
]
of Ben-Arous/Zeitouni. Moreover, since the work of Jabin-Wang
[

]
, uniform bounds on the partition function have become fundamental to prove mean field limits for SDEs with singular interactions by relative entropy methods, see also the classical work of Ben Arous/Brunaud

extended the partition function bound and the mean-field limit to the attractive log-gas, also known as the Patlak-Keller-Segel system. We note that the logarithmic singularity of the log-gas matches the behavior of the Green’s functions in 2d. In fact, the techniques of Nelson

Despite the conceptual similarity with these recent developments for SDEs, the subtle behavior of the infinite volume limit

1\leq\mathbb{E}^{\mu_{m,L}^{\otimes N}}\left[\exp\bigg(-\frac{\lambda}{N}\int_{\Lambda}:\|Z_{\epsilon}(x)\|^{4}_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}:_{m}dx\bigg)\right]\leq C(\lambda,m,|\Lambda|),

Let us denote by

For the upper bound, we use Nelson’s argument, following the exposition of

As both integrals are clearly finite, the proof is complete.

We now turn to the argument for (

Definition 2.4

The Linear Sigma Model on

2.11

We now turn to the main result of this section, which proves that as

W_{H_{m}^{1}(\Lambda_{L})^{N}}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\inf\bigg\{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\|X_{1,j}-X_{2,j}\|^{2}_{H_{m}^{1}(\Lambda_{L})}\bigg|\,\,(X_{i,j})_{j=1}^{N}\sim\mu_{i},\,\,i=1,2\bigg\}^{\frac{1}{2}},

Remark 2.5
.

The normalization by

(2.39)

Indeed, by definition of the Wasserstein distance in terms of optimal couplings, it holds

W_{H_{m}^{1}(\Lambda_{L})^{N}}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})^{2}\geq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}W_{H_{m}^{1}(\Lambda_{L})}(P_{j}\mu_{1},P_{j}\mu_{2})^{2}.

Using that

The above scaling is also the natural one from the point of de Finetti/Hewitt–Savage convergence of probability measures on

3.1
The Gap Equation

Our starting point is to show that the measures
\nu^{N}_{L}
can equivalently be realized as the Linear Sigma Model
\nu^{N}_{m,L}
on
\Lambda_{L}
with respect to the strictly convex potential (
2.1
), as introduced in Section

, for a well chosen mass

. This mass will be used to define the limiting mass
\lim_{L,\,N\to\infty}m=m_{*}=m_{*}(\lambda,\beta)
in Theorem

. The non-linear equations satisfied by
m
and

in Theorem

are referred to as

, borrowing the terminology from the physics literature
[

]
.

Results of this type for
N=1
are attributed in
[

]
to R. Baumel, although it seems no publication ever appeared. The
gap equations
play an important role in
[

]
, where the infinite volume pressure is shown to be unique for a wide class of boundary conditions, as well as in the classical works
[
32
]
and
[
66
]
. We give here a slihtly different argument emphasizing relative entropy and functional inequalities.

Lemma 3.3
.

For any
\lambda>0
,
\beta\geq 0
, there exists a unique
m\in(0,1)
, which arises as the solution to the gap equation

\frac{m^{2}}{\lambda}+\bigg(1+\frac{2}{N}\bigg)\frac{1}{L^{2}}\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda_{L}^{*}}\left(\frac{1}{1+|\xi|^{2}}-\frac{1}{m^{2}+|\xi|^{2}}\right)=-\beta,

(3.4)

such that the probability measures
\nu^{N}_{L}
and
\nu^{N}_{m,L}
, defined in
Definition

3.1
and

2.4
respectively, are equal.

Remark 3.4
.

Before proceeding to the proof of Lemma
3.3
, we record the following elementary fact about equation (
3.4
). Namely, for any
\lambda,m>0
and
\beta\geq 0
, there exists a unique solution to (
3.4
) as a consequence of the Intermediate Value Theorem. Indeed, the LHS of (
3.4
) is a continuous increasing function of
m
. This follows by the representation

\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda_{L}^{*}}\left(\frac{1}{1+|\xi|^{2}}-\frac{1}{m^{2}+|\xi|^{2}}\right)=(m^{2}-1)\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda_{L}^{*}}\left(\frac{1}{(1+|\xi|^{2})(m^{2}+|\xi|^{2})}\right),

(3.5)

which expresses an absolutely convergent series. Furthermore, the LHS tends to
-\infty
as
m\to 0
, and takes a positive value of
\frac{1}{\lambda}
at
m=1
.

Remark 3.5
.

We now give some further motivation for (
3.4
) and a formal proof of Lemma
3.3
. Keeping in mind the formula (
3.2
), if

satisfies

\left(1+\frac{2}{N}\right)C_{\epsilon,L}^{1}+\beta=\left(1+\frac{2}{N}\right)C_{\epsilon,L}^{m_{\epsilon}}-\frac{m_{\epsilon}^{2}}{\lambda},

(3.6)

3.3

We now state the main quantitative input required for the proof of the above result. The key point is to have an estimate which scales optimally with the volume

\limsup_{L\to\infty}\frac{1}{L^{2}}W_{H^{1}_{m_{*}}(\Lambda_{L})^{N}}(\nu^{N}_{L},\mu_{m_{*},L}^{\otimes N})^{2}\leq\frac{C}{N}.

(3.39)

The first lemma is a comparison of two massive GFFs with differing masses, keeping in mind that the mass

Lemma 3.12
.

For
m
and
m_{*}
defined by the equations (

\limsup_{L\to\infty}\frac{1}{L^{2}}W_{H_{m}^{1}(\Lambda_{L})^{N}}(\mu_{m_{*},L}^{\otimes N},\mu_{m,L}^{\otimes N})^{2}\lesssim\lambda|\ln m_{*}|\cdot\frac{1}{N}.

(3.40)

Using independence, we first notice

\mathcal{H}(\mu_{m_{*},L}^{\otimes N}|\mu_{m,L}^{\otimes N})=N\mathcal{H}(\mu_{m_{*},L}|\mu_{m,L}),

hence we are left with estimating the right hand side. Using that the variance of both Gaussians diagonalize in the Fourier basis, and taking the limit of finite dimensional approximations (see for example Corollary 29 in
[

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L^{2}}\mathcal{H}(\mu_{m_{*},L}|\mu_{m,L})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2L^{2}}\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda_{L}^{*}}\frac{|\xi|^{2}+m_{*}^{2}}{|\xi|^{2}+m^{2}}-1+\ln\left(\frac{|\xi|^{2}+m^{2}}{|\xi|^{2}+m_{*}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2L^{2}}\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda_{L}^{*}}\frac{m_{*}^{2}-m^{2}}{|\xi|^{2}+m^{2}}+\ln\left(1+\frac{m^{2}-m_{*}^{2}}{|\xi|^{2}+m_{*}^{2}}\right).

where the series is easily seen to be absolutely convergent by Taylor expansion of

about the origin. Moreover, taking into account the identities

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{m_{*}^{2}-m^{2}}{|\xi|^{2}+m^{2}}+\ln\left(1+\frac{m^{2}-m_{*}^{2}}{|\xi|^{2}+m_{*}^{2}}\right)\;d\xi

\displaystyle=\pi\left(m^{2}-m_{*}^{2}+2m_{*}^{2}\ln\left(\frac{m_{*}}{m}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{m_{*}^{2}-m^{2}}{\xi_{1}^{2}+m^{2}}+\ln\left(1+\frac{m^{2}-m_{*}^{2}}{\xi_{1}^{2}+m_{*}^{2}}\right)\;d\xi_{1}

\displaystyle=\pi\frac{(m-m_{*})^{2}}{m},

(3.41)

we obtain from an elementary Riemmann sum estimate (

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L^{2}}\mathcal{H}(\mu_{m_{*},L}|\mu_{m,L})

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\left(m^{2}-m_{*}^{2}+m_{*}^{2}\ln\left(\frac{m_{*}}{m}\right)\right)+\frac{1}{L}\frac{(m-m_{*})^{2}}{m}

(3.42)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{L^{2}}\left(\frac{m_{*}^{2}-m^{2}}{m}+\ln\left(1+\frac{m^{2}-m_{*}^{2}}{m_{*}}\right)\right).

Recalling the definition (
3.31
) of
m_{**}
and using Lemma
3.7
, we obtain

\limsup_{L\to\infty}\frac{1}{L^{2}}\mathcal{H}(\mu_{m_{*},L}|\mu_{m,L})\lesssim m_{*}^{2}-m_{**}^{2}\lesssim\lambda|\ln m_{*}|\cdot\frac{1}{N},

where we used (
3.27
). See
[
60
]
for the same formula when the masses are fixed and independent of
L
. The desired estimate (
3.40
) now follows from (
3.3
) and Talagrand’s inequality, Proposition
2.8
.
∎The other key ingredient is to obtain bounds on
\mathcal{E}(\nu^{N}_{L}\mid\mu_{L}^{\otimes N})
that scale optimally with the volume
L^{2}
of the torus, uniformly in
N
. We start with a suitable uniform estimate on the partition function.

Lemma 3.13
.

There exists a constant
C_{\text{p.f.}}(\lambda,\beta)
such that uniformly in
L,N
it holds

0\leq\frac{1}{L^{2}}\log\mathbf{Z}_{L}^{N,\lambda}\leq C_{\text{p.f.}}(\lambda,\beta).

(3.43)

Furthermore, for every
\beta>0
, there exists a
\lambda_{0}(\beta)
such that for
\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}

C_{\text{p.f.}}(\lambda,\beta)\lesssim\lambda(\log\lambda)^{2}.

(3.44)

We decompose
\Lambda_{L}
into a union of
L^{2}
unit cubes, overlapping only at their boundaries, and label these cubes
C_{j}
for
j\in I_{L}
where
I_{L}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{2}
indexes the bottom left corner of the cube. By definition of the partition function, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}^{N,\beta,\lambda}_{L}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\mu_{m,L}^{\otimes N}}\exp\bigg({-\frac{\lambda}{N}\int_{T_{L}^{2}}:\|Z\|_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}^{4}:dx}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\mu_{m,L}^{\otimes N}}\prod_{j\in I_{L}}\exp\bigg({-\frac{\lambda}{N}\int_{C_{j}}:\|Z\|_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}^{4}:dx}\bigg).

By the Checkerboard estimate Lemma
D.3
, there exists a
p=p(m)
independent of
L
,
N
such that

\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}^{N,\beta,\lambda}_{L}

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{j\in I_{L}}\bigg\|\exp\bigg({-\frac{\lambda}{N}\int_{C_{j}}:\|Z\|_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}^{4}:dx}\bigg)\bigg\|_{L^{p}(d\mu_{m,L}^{\otimes N})}

\displaystyle=\bigg\|\exp\bigg({-\frac{\lambda}{N}\int_{C_{0}}:\|Z\|_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}^{4}:dx}\bigg)\bigg\|_{L^{p}(d\mu_{m,L}^{\otimes N})}^{L^{2}},

where the second inequality uses the translation invariance of
\mu_{m,L}
due to the periodic boundary conditions. Taking the logarithm and using Lemma
2.3
with
\Lambda=C_{0}
we obtain (
3.43
). Furthermore, for
\lambda\geq e
, by (
2.29
) we have

\log\left\|\exp\bigg({-\frac{\lambda}{N}\int_{C_{0}}:\|Z\|_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}^{4}:dx}\bigg)\right\|_{L^{p}(d\mu_{m,L}^{\otimes N})}\lesssim\lambda\bigg(|\log m|\vee(\log(\lambda p)\big)^{2}\bigg),

(3.45)

which implies (
3.44
). The claim now follows by taking
\lambda
large enough depending on
m
, which is in turn upper and lower bounded only in terms of
\beta
due to Remark
D.4
and Lemma
3.7
.
∎

Remark 3.14
.

We would expect that this bound actually holds for
\lambda_{0}
independent of
\beta
, say
\lambda_{0}=e
. Indeed, as a substitute for Checkerboard estimates, one could likely apply the conditioning approach of
[
36
]
to deduce a suitable factorization over cubes, modulo changing from periodic to Neumann boundary conditions. Combining this with the method of images, this would allow us to effectively take
p=1
in (
3.45
) and obtain an upper bound of the log of the partition function by
\lambda(\beta\vee(\log\lambda)^{2})
.
However, we also need the Checkerboard estimates in the proof of Proposition
3.15
below, and for that application we are not entirely sure if the approach of
[
36
]
suffices or not. Indeed, Theorem 3.1 from
[
36
]
is stated for a somewhat limited class of observables compared to the Checkerboard estimate Theorem A.1 from
[
36
]
.

We now turn to the final core quantitative ingredient, a uniform control on the relative entropy density of the measure
\nu^{N}_{L}
with respect to
\mu^{\otimes N}_{m,L}
. The following bound is similar to Lemma
2.10
in terms of the uniformity with respect to
N
, but with the improvement that it scales optimally with respect to the volume of
\Lambda_{L}
, which is crucial for obtaining uniform bounds on a suitable coupling later in Section
3.4
. To obtain the optimal scaling, we rely on a combination of the Checkerboard and Chessboard estimates. These tools were introduced in the context of the
\Phi^{4}_{2}
theory in the works
[
35
]
and
[
27
]
respectively. Since these seminal works, the Chessboard estimate has become an indispensible tool in the study of phase transitions of lattice gases, c.f.
[
28
]
. The Checkerboard estimate however seems to have received less attention, and we recall that it provides an elegant way of capturing the approximate independence of the massive GFF on disjoint (up to possible overlap on the boundary) regions. Specifically, after covering
\Lambda_{L}
with adjacent rectangles, it allows us to bound the expectation of a product of observables localized on these rectangles by a product of their
L^{p}
norms. The important point is that
p
is independent of the number of rectangles, and hence the volume
L^{2}
. In the present context, we need a Checkerboard estimate which is additionally uniform in
N
, and we give a short argument that this is indeed the case in Lemma
D.3
.

Proposition 3.15
.

The following bound holds

\sup_{N,L\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{1}{L^{2}}\mathcal{H}(\nu^{N}_{L}\mid\mu^{\otimes N}_{m,L})\lesssim C_{r.e.}(\lambda,\beta).

Furthermore, there exists
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{1}(\beta)
such that for
\lambda\geq\lambda_{1}(\beta)
it holds

\text{log}\big(C_{r.e.}(\lambda,\beta)\big)\lesssim\lambda(\log\lambda)^{2}.

(3.46)

To keep notation simple, we omit dependence of
\nu^{N}_{L}
on
\lambda
,
\beta
, but still keep track of these parameters in the multiplicative constants that arise in the bounds. As in the previous lemma, we introduce a cover of
\Lambda_{L}
by
L^{2}
unit cubes denoted
C_{j}
and indexed by their center
j\in I_{L}
. Start by noting that by Lemma
3.3
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L^{2}}\mathcal{H}(\nu^{N}_{L}\mid\mu_{m,L}^{\otimes N})=\frac{1}{L^{2}}\mathcal{H}(\nu^{N}_{m,L}\mid\mu_{m,L}^{\otimes N})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\lambda}{N}\frac{1}{L^{2}}\mathbb{E}^{\nu^{N}_{m,L}}\int_{\Lambda_{L}}:\|\Phi\|_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}^{4}:dx-\frac{1}{L^{2}}\text{log}\mathbf{Z}^{N}_{L}

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{\lambda}{N}\mathbb{E}^{\nu^{N}_{m,L}}\int_{C_{0}}:\|\Phi\|_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}^{4}:dx,

(3.47)

where we used translation invariance of
\nu^{N}_{m,L}
and
\mathbf{Z}_{L}^{N,\beta,\lambda}\geq 1
for the second term (by Jensen’s inequality, c.f. Lemma
2.3
).

Let
C_{0}
be the unit cube with the origin as its bottom left corner. Choose an integer
J
such that
2^{J}\leq L\leq 2^{J+1}
and define a tiling
(C_{j})_{j\in I_{J}}
of
2^{2J}
adjacent unit cubes via translations of
C_{0}
, disjoint except for possible overlaps at their boundaries, such that
\cup_{j}C_{j}=[-2^{J-1},2^{J-1}]^{2}\subset\Lambda_{L}
. By Lemma
D.2
, the following Chessboard estimate holds:

\displaystyle\bigg|\mathbb{E}^{\nu^{N}_{m,L}}\frac{1}{N}\int_{C_{0}}:\|\Phi\|_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}^{4}:dx\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\bigg(\mathbb{E}^{\nu^{N}_{m,L}}\underbrace{\prod_{j\in I_{J}}\frac{1}{N}\int_{C_{j}}:\|\Phi\|_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}^{4}:dx}_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\,G(\Phi)}\bigg)^{2^{-2J}}

(3.48)

where we omit dependence of
G
on
N
and
L
. Applying Holder’s inequality, Lemma
3.13
, and the lower bound
\mathbf{Z}_{L}^{N,\lambda,\beta}\geq 1
, we find that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\nu^{N}_{m,L}}G(\Phi)=\mathbb{E}^{\mu_{m,L}^{\otimes N}}\bigg[\frac{d\nu^{N}_{m,L}}{d\mu_{m,L}^{\otimes N}}G(Z)\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(\mathbf{Z}^{N,\beta,2\lambda}_{L})^{\frac{1}{2}}}{\mathbf{Z}^{N,\beta,\lambda}_{L}}\|G(Z)\|_{L^{2}(d\mu_{m,L}^{\otimes N})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\text{exp}\bigg(\frac{L^{2}}{2}C_{\text{p.f,}}(\beta,2\lambda)\bigg)\|G(Z)\|_{L^{2}(d\mu_{m,L}^{\otimes N})}.

(3.49)

To estimate
G(Z)
in
L^{2}(d\mu_{m,L}^{\otimes N})
, we use the Checkerboard estimate Lemma
D.3
to obtain a
p=p(m)
independent of
N,L
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\mu_{m,L}^{\otimes N}}\prod_{j\in I_{J}}\bigg|\frac{1}{N}\int_{C_{j}}:\|Z\|_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}^{4}:dx\bigg|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{j\in I_{J}}\bigg\|\bigg(\frac{1}{N}\int_{C_{j}}:\|Z\|_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}^{4}:dx\bigg)^{2}\bigg\|_{L^{p}(d\mu_{m,L}^{\otimes N})}

(3.50)

\displaystyle=\bigg\|\frac{1}{N}\int_{C_{0}}:\|Z\|_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}^{4}:dx\bigg\|_{L^{2p}(d\mu_{m,L}^{\otimes N})}^{2^{2J+1}}.

There is a slight technical issue regarding measurability which requires a suitable approximation argument before applying Lemma
D.3
, and this is carried out in Lemma
D.5
. Hence, by Lemma
2.2
we find that

\|G(Z)\|_{L^{2}(d\mu_{m,L}^{\otimes N})}^{2^{-2J}}\lesssim m^{-1}(2p-1)^{2}\lesssim m_{*}^{-5},

where we used Remark
D.4
and the lower bound
m\geq m_{*}
from Lemma
3.7
. Therefore, combining the above and using that
L^{2}2^{2J}\leq 2
we obtain

\sup_{N,L\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{1}{L^{2}}\mathcal{H}(\nu^{N}_{L}\mid\mu^{\otimes N}_{m,L})\lesssim\lambda\text{exp}\bigg(C_{\text{p.f,}}(\beta,2\lambda)\bigg)m_{*}^{-5}.

(3.51)

By choosing
\lambda
large enough depending on
\beta
, taking into account that
|\ln m_{*}|=O(\beta)
, we obtain the result.
∎

Remark 3.16
.

There should actually be significant cancellations between the two terms in the relative entropy. We would expect to obtain improved dependence of the final estimate on
\lambda
by exploiting this more carefully, perhaps by modifying the IBP arguments in
[

. In fact, related IBP arguments have recently been carried out in
[

, which should allow to explicitly compute the large

for fixed

and find its precise dependence on
\lambda,\beta
. It would be interesting to see if this could be made more quantitative at finite

, as this would allow to significantly weaken the scaling assumption between
\lambda
and

in Corollary
1.2
. However, at present the results in
[
64
]
are limited to the high temperature regime due to reliance on various a priori bounds established in
[

, so we leave to future work the possibility of improving on the

dependence of the bound.

With the above estimates we can now prove our main quantitative estimate.

By the monotonicity of
\|\cdot\|_{H^{1}_{\gamma}(\Lambda_{L})^{N}}
with respect to
\gamma
and the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle W_{H^{1}_{m_{*}}(\Lambda_{L})^{N}}(\nu^{N}_{L},\mu_{m_{*},L}^{\otimes N})^{2}\leq 2W_{H^{1}_{m}(\Lambda_{L})^{N}}(\nu^{N}_{L},\mu_{m,L}^{\otimes N})^{2}+2W_{H^{1}_{m}(\Lambda_{L})^{N}}(\mu_{m,L}^{\otimes N},\mu_{m_{*},L}^{\otimes N})^{2}.

(3.52)

Applying Talagrand’s inequality, Proposition
2.8
, followed by Proposition
3.15
,

\frac{1}{L^{2}}W_{H^{1}_{m}(\Lambda_{L})^{N}}(\nu^{N}_{L},\mu_{m,L}^{\otimes N})^{2}\leq\frac{2}{N}\cdot\frac{1}{L^{2}}\mathcal{H}(\nu^{N}_{L}\mid\mu_{m,L}^{\otimes N})\leq\frac{2}{N}\cdot C_{r.e}(\beta,\lambda).

(3.53)

Hence, dividing (
3.52
) by
L^{2}
and taking
L\to\infty
, the combination of (
3.53
) with Lemma
3.12
completes the proof.
∎

3.4
Proof of the Main Theorem

We now turn to the qualitative aspects of our proof.

Lemma 3.17
.

There exists an optimal coupling between
\nu^{N}_{L}
and
\mu^{\otimes N}_{m_{*},L}
in
W_{H^{1}_{m_{*}}(\Lambda_{L})^{N}}(\cdot,\cdot)
which is translation invariant.

We start by noticing that both measures
\nu^{N}_{L}
and
\mu^{\otimes N}_{m_{*},L}
are translation invariant. More concretely, for any
y\in\Lambda_{L}
, we consider the mapping
T_{y}:\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Lambda_{L})^{N}\to\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Lambda_{L})^{N}
, defined by
T_{y}(\Phi)=\Phi(\cdot+y)
, then
T_{y}^{\otimes N}\#\nu^{N}_{L}=\nu^{N}_{L}
and
T_{y}^{\otimes N}\#\mu_{L}^{\otimes N}=\mu_{L}^{\otimes N}
.
We consider
\Pi_{*}
the law of the optimal coupling between
\nu^{N}_{L}
and
\mu^{\otimes N}_{m_{*},L}
. By the observation above, we have that for any
y\in\mathbb{T}^{2}_{L}
, the measure
(T_{y}^{\otimes N}\otimes T_{y}^{\otimes N})\#\Pi_{*}
is also an admissible coupling. It is moreover, optimal because the
H^{1}(\Lambda_{L})^{N}
distance is independent of translations due to periodicity. We then define a coupling, which is also optimal, given by the average

\Pi^{**}=\frac{1}{L^{2}}\int_{\Lambda_{L}}(T_{y}^{\otimes N}\otimes T_{y}^{\otimes N})\#\Pi_{*}\;dy.

By construction this coupling is translation invariant, which completes the proof.
∎

By
Proposition

3.11
, there exists
L_{0}
independent of
N
such that for
L\geq L_{0}
we have

\frac{1}{L^{2}}W_{H^{1}_{m_{*}}(\Lambda_{L})^{N}}\big(\nu^{N}_{L},\mu_{m_{*},L}^{\otimes N}\big)^{2}\lesssim\frac{C}{N}.

(3.54)

By Lemma
3.17
, we can find some probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},{\mathbb{P}})
depending on
L
and random variables
\Phi_{L}^{N}\sim\nu^{N}_{L}
and
Z_{L}^{N}\sim\mu_{L}^{\otimes N}
such that
Y_{L}^{N}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\Phi_{L}^{N}(x)-Z_{L}^{N}(x)
is stationary and satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{NL^{2}}\mathbb{E}\int_{\Lambda_{L}}\big(m_{*}^{2}\|Y^{N}_{L}(x)\|_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}^{2}+\|\nabla Y^{N}_{L}(x)\|_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}^{2}\big)dx=W_{H^{1}_{m_{*}}(\Lambda_{L})^{N}}\big(\nu^{N}_{L},\mu_{m_{*},L}^{\otimes N}\big)^{2}\lesssim\frac{C}{N}.

(3.55)

By translation invariance this yields

\sup_{N,L,k}\mathbb{E}\int_{C_{k}}\big(m_{*}^{2}\|Y^{N}_{L}(x)\|_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}^{2}+\|\nabla Y^{N}_{L}(x)\|_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}^{2}\big)\lesssim C,

where
C_{k}=k+(0,1]^{2}
. Finally, recalling the definition of
\rho
from the notation section,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|Y^{N}_{L}\|_{H^{1}_{m_{*}}(\rho)^{N}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\mathbb{E}\int_{C_{k}}\rho(x)\big(m_{*}^{2}|Y^{N}_{L}(x)|^{2}+|\nabla Y_{L}^{N}(x)|^{2}\big)dx

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\rho(k)\mathbb{E}\|Y^{N}_{L}\|_{H^{1}_{m_{*}}(C_{k})}^{2}\lesssim\frac{C(\lambda,\beta)}{N}.

(3.56)

This implies the uniform in
L
bound

W_{H^{1}_{m_{*}}(\rho)^{N}}\big(\nu^{N}_{L},\mu_{m_{*},L}^{\otimes N}\big)^{2}\leq\frac{C(\lambda,\beta)}{N}.

As
L\to\infty
,
\mu_{m_{*},L}^{\otimes N}
converges weakly as a probability measure in
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))
to
\mu_{m_{*}}^{\otimes N}
. To see this, note that for any compactly supported test function
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, one can easily show the convergence of the co-variance operator

\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{m_{*},L}}\left[|\Phi.\phi|^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{L^{2}}\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda_{L}^{*}}\frac{|\widehat{\phi}(\xi)|^{2}}{m_{*}^{2}+|\xi|^{2}}\to\int\int\frac{|\mathcal{F}\phi(\zeta)|^{2}}{m_{*}^{2}+|\zeta|^{2}}\;d\zeta=\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{m_{*}}}\left[|\Phi.\phi|^{2}\right],

where
\widehat{\phi}
and
\mathcal{F}\phi
denote the Fourier series and the unitary Fourier transform of
\phi
, respectively. Given a periodic state
\nu^{N}
for the Linear Sigma Model, that is to say it is any accumulation point in
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))
with the narrow topology of the sequence
\nu^{N}_{L}
; without relabeling, we will assume that
\nu^{N}_{L}\rightharpoonup\nu^{N}
. By the previous observations, we get that the optimal pairing
\Pi_{L}^{\infty}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Lambda_{L})^{N}\times\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Lambda_{L})^{N})
between
\nu_{L}^{N}
and
\mu^{\otimes N}_{m_{*},L}
converges to
\Pi_{\infty}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{N}\times\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{N})
, a pairing between
\nu^{N}
and
\mu^{\otimes N}_{m_{*}}
. Lower-semicontinuity follows, and we get the bound

W_{H^{1}_{m_{*}}(\rho)^{N}}\big(\nu^{N},\mu_{m_{*}}^{\otimes N}\big)^{2}\leq\liminf_{L\to\infty}W_{H^{1}_{m_{*}}(\rho)^{N}}\big(\nu^{N}_{L},\mu_{m_{*},L}^{\otimes N}\big)^{2}\leq\frac{C(\lambda,\beta)}{N}.

∎Finally, we are ready to analyze the implications of Theorem
3.9
for observables to establish Theorem
1.1
. We will consider cylindrical functionals
F:\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\mapsto\mathbb{R}
of the form

\Psi\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\mapsto F(\Psi)=G(\Psi.g_{1},\cdots,\Psi.g_{m})

(3.57)

for some
m\geq 1
, smooth function
G:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
and
g_{1},\cdots,g_{m}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

Definition 3.18
.

We will say that a cylindrical functional
F
is
admissible
provided at least one of the following holds:

1.

All first derivatives of
G
are bounded.

2.

All second derivatives of
G
are bounded and
\mathbb{E}^{\mu_{m_{*}}}|\nabla G(Z)|^{2}<\infty
for
Z\sim\mu_{m_{*}}
.

We now show that the estimate (
1.11
) holds provided that
F
is admissible.

By Theorem
3.9
and Remark
3.10
,
for any
i\in[N]
we can find a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},{\mathbb{P}})
and random variables
\Phi_{i}\sim\nu^{N}_{i}
,
Z_{i}\sim\mu_{m_{*}}
such that
\Phi_{i}=Y_{i}+Z_{i}
and
\big(\mathbb{E}\|Y_{i}\|_{H^{1}_{m_{*}}(\rho)}^{2}\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq CN^{-\frac{1}{2}}
. Letting
F
be an admissible cylindrical function and assuming the first condition in Definition
3.18
holds, we may apply the fundamental theorem of calculus to obtain

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}F(\Psi)d(\nu^{N}_{i}-\mu_{m_{*}})(\Psi)\bigg|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\sum_{k=1}^{m}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\big[\partial_{k}G\big(\Phi_{i}.g_{1}+sY_{i}.g_{1},\cdots,\Phi_{i}.g_{m}+sY_{i}.g_{m}\big)Y_{i}.g_{k}\big]ds\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{m}\|\partial_{k}G\|_{L^{\infty}}\mathbb{E}|Y_{i}.g_{k}|\leq CN^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\|\partial_{k}G\|_{L^{\infty}}\|g_{k}\|_{H^{1}_{m}(\rho)^{*}},

(3.58)

which completes the proof. In the case where the condition in Definition
3.18
holds, the proof goes by a similar argument via second order Taylor expansion, which is left to the reader.
∎

Remark 3.19
.

Recall that we motivated the statement of the main result via the definition of decay of correlations
1.6
. As we mentioned in the introduction, the implicit constant
C_{F}
can be taken independently of
z
. Indeed, if we take
F
of the form
F(\Phi)=G(\Phi_{i}.g_{1},\Phi_{i}.g_{2}(\cdot+z))
, then the reader might worry that
\|g_{2}(\cdot+z)\|_{H^{1}_{m}(\rho)^{*}}
is not bounded uniformly in
z
. However, following the argument in the proof of Theorem
1.1
, we see this is not a problem. Indeed, by the construction in the proof of Theorem
3.9
, we can ensure that
Y_{i}
is translation invariant, which implies
\mathbb{E}^{\mu_{m_{*}}^{\otimes N}}|Y_{i}.g_{2}(\cdot+z)|=\mathbb{E}^{\mu_{m_{*}}^{\otimes N}}|Y_{i}.g_{2}|
, so the same implicit constant suffices for arbitrary
z
.

A
Some elementary integral estimates

We record here the proof of the elementary lemma used to track the dependence of the partition function on the coupling constant.

Lemma A.1
.

For
\lambda\geq e
it holds

\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{exp}\big(\lambda M-\text{exp}(M^{\frac{1}{2}})\big)dM\leq 50\lambda\text{exp}(16\lambda(\log\lambda)^{2}).

(A.1)

First, note that the contribution from
M\in[0,1]
can be bounded by
\text{exp}(\lambda)\leq\text{exp}(16\lambda(\log\lambda)^{2})
, so it suffices to consider the integral over
[1,\infty)
. Next observe that by Taylor expansion,
\lambda M-\frac{1}{2}\text{exp}(M^{\frac{1}{2}})\leq\lambda M-\frac{1}{2\cdot 4!}M^{2}\leq 0
for
M\geq 48\lambda
. Noting in addition that
\frac{1}{2}\text{exp}(M^{\frac{1}{2}})\geq\frac{1}{4}M
, we find

\int_{48\lambda}^{\infty}\text{exp}\big(\lambda M-\text{exp}(M^{\frac{1}{2}})\big)dM\leq\int_{48\lambda}^{\infty}\text{exp}(-\frac{1}{4}M)dM=4\text{exp}(-12\lambda)\leq 2\lambda.

(A.2)

Furthermore, on
[1,48\lambda]
, the function
f(M)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\lambda M-\text{exp}(M^{\frac{1}{2}})
has a strictly negative second derivative and a unique maximum at a critical
M_{*}
since
f^{\prime}(1)=\lambda-\frac{1}{2}e>0
and by Taylor expansion again,
f^{\prime}(48\lambda)\leq\lambda-\frac{1}{48}(48\lambda)^{\frac{3}{2}}<0
. At the critical point
M_{*}
it holds
\exp(\sqrt{M_{*}})=\frac{\lambda}{2}\sqrt{M_{*}}\leq\frac{\lambda}{2}\sqrt{48\lambda}\leq\lambda^{\frac{3}{2}}
, so that
M_{*}\leq 16(\log\lambda)^{2}
. Hence, we find

\int_{1}^{48\lambda}\text{exp}\big(\lambda M-\text{exp}(M^{\frac{1}{2}})\big)dM\leq(48\lambda-1)\text{exp}(16\lambda(\log\lambda)^{2}),

(A.3)

which yields (
A.1
) after summing the three bounds.
∎Furthermore, in the analysis of the various gap equations arising in Section 3, we need require some elementary bounds on Riemann sums of radially monotone functions.

Lemma A.2
.

Let
f:\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\to f(\xi)\in[0,\infty)
be smooth, radial, decreasing in
|\xi|
, and integrable. Then it holds that

\left(\frac{2\pi}{L}\right)^{2}\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda_{L}^{*}}f(\xi)\geq\frac{1}{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(\xi)d\xi.

(A.4)

Furthermore, the following bound holds

\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(\xi)d\xi-\left(\frac{2\pi}{L}\right)^{2}\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda_{L}^{*}}f(\xi)\bigg|\leq 3\cdot\left(\frac{2\pi}{L}\right)^{2}f(0)+4\cdot\left(\frac{2\pi}{L}\right)\int_{0}^{\infty}f(\eta_{1},0)d\eta_{1}.

(A.5)

For
\xi\in\frac{2\pi}{L}\mathbb{Z}^{2}
we define
Q_{\xi}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\xi+[0,\frac{2\pi}{L})^{2}
and note that for
\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\geq 0

\left(\frac{2\pi}{L}\right)^{2}f\left(\xi+\frac{2\pi}{L}e\right)\leq\int_{Q_{\xi}}f(\eta)d\eta\leq\left(\frac{2\pi}{L}\right)^{2}f(\xi),

(A.6)

by monotonicity, where
e=(1,1)
. Summing up the result we find from the upper bound in (
A.6
) that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(\eta)1_{\eta_{1},\eta_{2}\geq 0}d\eta\leq\left(\frac{2\pi}{L}\right)^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\xi=(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\in\Lambda_{L}^{*}\\
\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\geq 0\end{subarray}}f(\xi)\leq\left(\frac{2\pi}{L}\right)^{2}\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda_{L}}f(\xi),

(A.7)

since
f
is non-negative. The estimate (
A.4
) now follows directly from

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(\eta)d\eta=4\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(\eta)1_{\eta_{1},\eta_{2}\geq 0}d\eta.

(A.8)

To prove (
A.5
), first notice the upper bound

\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(\eta)d\eta-\left(\frac{2\pi}{L}\right)^{2}\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda_{L}^{*}}f(\xi)\leq 3\cdot\left(\frac{2\pi}{L}\right)^{2}f(0)+4\cdot\left(\frac{2\pi}{L}\right)^{2}\sum_{\xi_{1}\in\frac{2\pi}{L}\mathbb{Z},\xi_{1}>0}f(\xi_{1},0),

(A.9)

which follows from applying (
A.8
), the first inequality in (
A.7
), and the radial symmetry of
f
. Next we notice the lower bound

\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(\eta)d\eta-\left(\frac{2\pi}{L}\right)^{2}\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda_{L}^{*}}f(\xi)\geq-\left(\frac{2\pi}{L}\right)^{2}f(0)-4\cdot\left(\frac{2\pi}{L}\right)^{2}\sum_{\xi_{1}\in\frac{2\pi}{L}\mathbb{Z},\xi_{1}>0}f(\xi_{1},0),

(A.10)

which follows from a similar argument. Namely, summing over
\xi
in (
A.6
) we obtain

\int_{\mathbb{R}}1_{\eta_{1},\eta_{2}\geq 0}f(\eta)d\eta\geq\left(\frac{2\pi}{L}\right)^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\xi\in\Lambda_{L}^{*}\\
\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\geq 0\end{subarray}}f\left(\xi+\frac{2\pi}{L}e\right)=\left(\frac{2\pi}{L}\right)^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\xi\in\Lambda_{L}^{*}\\
\xi_{1},\xi_{2}>0\end{subarray}}f(\xi),

(A.11)

which can be combined with (
A.8
) and radial symmetry to obtain (
A.10
). The above upper and lower bounds therefore yield

\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(\xi)d\xi-\left(\frac{2\pi}{L}\right)^{2}\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda_{L}^{*}}f(\xi)\bigg|\leq 3\cdot\left(\frac{2\pi}{L}\right)^{2}f(0)+4\cdot\left(\frac{2\pi}{L}\right)^{2}\sum_{\xi_{1}\in\frac{2\pi}{L}\mathbb{Z},\xi_{1}>0}f(\xi_{1},0),

(A.12)

which implies (
A.5
) via the bound

\left(\frac{2\pi}{L}\right)\sum_{\eta_{1}\in\frac{2\pi}{L}\mathbb{Z},\eta_{1}>0}f(\eta_{1},0)\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}f(\eta_{1},0)d\eta_{1},

(A.13)

which follows again from monotonicity.
∎We now apply the above result to deduce a suitable lower bound on
m_{\epsilon}
, the unique solution to the gap equation on
\Lambda_{L,\epsilon}
.

Lemma A.3
.

For
\epsilon\leq 1
, the unique solution
m_{\epsilon}\in(0,1)
to (
3.6
) is uniformly bounded from below.

Recalling that the cutoff
\eta
takes the value
1
on the ball of radius
\frac{1}{2}
, we have the lower bound

\displaystyle C_{\epsilon,L}^{m_{\epsilon}}-C_{\epsilon,L}^{1}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{L^{2}}\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda_{L}^{*}}1_{|\epsilon\xi|\leq\frac{1}{2}}\bigg(\frac{1}{m_{\epsilon}^{2}+|\xi|^{2}}-\frac{1}{1+|\xi|^{2}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{4}\cdot\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{B(0,\frac{1}{2\epsilon})}\bigg(\frac{1}{m_{\epsilon}^{2}+|\xi|^{2}}-\frac{1}{1+|\xi|^{2}}\bigg)d\xi

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{8\pi}\ln m_{\epsilon}+\frac{1}{16\pi}\ln\bigg(\frac{1+4\epsilon^{2}m_{\epsilon}^{2}}{1+4\epsilon^{2}}\bigg).

(A.14)

Note that by convexity of
x\mapsto\ln(1+x)
we have
\ln\bigg(\frac{1+4\epsilon^{2}m_{\epsilon}^{2}}{1+4\epsilon^{2}}\bigg)\geq-4\epsilon^{2}
, hence we find

-\beta=\frac{m_{\epsilon}^{2}}{\lambda}+(1+\frac{2}{N})(C_{\epsilon,L}^{1}-C_{\epsilon,L}^{m_{\epsilon}})\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}+(1+\frac{2}{N})\big(\frac{1}{8\pi}\ln m_{\epsilon}+\frac{1}{2\pi}\epsilon^{2}\big)

(A.15)

Re-arranging, we find the lower bound
m_{\epsilon}\geq\text{exp}\big(-\frac{N}{N+2}(\beta+\lambda^{-1})-\epsilon^{2}\big)
,
which implies the uniform lower bound for
\epsilon\leq 1
.
∎We also need the following elementary application of the Poisson summation formula, which shows that the series on the LHS is monotonically decreasing in
m^{2}
and is used to deduce monotonicity of the solution to the finite volume gap equation with respect to

.

Lemma A.4
.

For
m>0
, the following identity holds

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\frac{m^{2}}{(m^{2}L^{2}+|2\pi k|^{2})^{2}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-sL^{2}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\frac{1}{4\pi}e^{-\frac{m^{2}}{4s}|k|^{2}}ds.

(A.16)

For any
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, the following identity follows from IBP and the change of variables
m^{2}s\mapsto s

\frac{m^{2}}{(m^{2}L^{2}+|2\pi k|^{2})^{2}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}m^{2}se^{-s(m^{2}L^{2}+|2\pi k|^{2})}ds=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-sL^{2}}\frac{s}{m^{2}}e^{-\frac{4\pi^{2}s}{m^{2}}|k|^{2}}ds.

(A.17)

By the Poisson summation formula, it holds

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\frac{s}{m^{2}}e^{-\frac{4\pi^{2}s}{m^{2}}|k|^{2}}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\frac{1}{4\pi}e^{-\frac{m^{2}}{4s}|k|^{2}}.

Hence, interchanging summation and integration we find the result.

B
Talagrand’s inequality for infinite dimensional Gaussian Measures

To keep the paper self-contained, we give a proof of the variant of Talagrand’s inequality needed in the present work. The argument given here is essentially the same as the one in
[
59
]
, as Peter Friz kindly informed us. We consider
\mu=\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)
the mean zero Gaussian measure with covariance operator
\Sigma:L^{2}(\Lambda_{L})\to L^{2}(\Lambda_{L})
a self-adjoint strictly positive compact operator in
L^{2}
. We consider
\{(\lambda_{k},e_{k})\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
the eigenvalue with their associated eigenfunctions which form orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\Lambda_{L})
. We consider a regularity scale
s\in(0,\infty)
and we will assume for simplicity that
\{e_{k}\}\subset H^{s}(\Lambda_{L})
are smooth, and
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(H^{-s}(\Lambda_{L}))
as a probability measure on the dual space
H^{s}(\Lambda_{L})
. The introduction of the regularity scale
s
is to avoid issues with the Donsker-Varadhan formula
[
23
]
, that is to say dual representation of the relative entropy functional. The main result of this section is Talagrand’s inequality
[
70
]
for Gaussians measures on
\mathcal{P}(H^{-s}(\Lambda_{L}))
.

First, we introduce some notation. We define the semi-norm (which might be infinite) associated to
\Sigma

\displaystyle\|\Phi\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\lambda_{k}}|\Phi.e_{k}|^{2}=\Sigma^{-1}\Phi.\Phi,

which is defined on
H^{-s}(\Lambda_{L})
. Next, we given
\mu,\,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(H^{-s}(\Lambda_{L}))
, we define the distance (possibly infinite)

W^{2}_{\Sigma^{-1}}(\mu,\nu)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int_{H^{-s}\times H^{-s}}\|\Phi-\Psi\|_{\Sigma_{M}^{-1}}^{2}\;d\pi(\Phi,\Psi),

(B.1)

where

\displaystyle\Pi(\mu,\nu)=\left\{\pi\in\mathcal{P}(H^{-s}\times H^{-s})\;:\;\int_{H^{-s}\times H^{-s}}F(\Phi)\;d\pi(\Phi,\Psi)=\int_{H^{-s}}F(\Phi)\;d\mu(\Phi)\right.

\displaystyle\left.\qquad\qquad\;\mbox{and}\;\int_{H^{-s}\times H^{-s}}F(\Psi)\;d\pi(\Phi,\Psi)=\int_{H^{-s}}F(\Psi)\;d\nu(\Psi)\quad\forall F\in C_{b}(H^{-s})\right\}

.

are the transference plans between
\mu
and
\nu
. Finally, we define the associated relative entropy functional

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(\nu|\mu)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\sup_{F\in C_{b}(H^{-s})}\int_{H^{-s}}F(\Psi)\;d\nu(\Psi)-\log\left(\int_{H^{-s}}e^{F(\Phi)}\;d\mu(\Phi)\right)

(B.2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle\int_{H^{-s}}\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}\log\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}\;d\mu&\nu\ll\mu\\
+\infty&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}

which does not depend on the operator
\Sigma
.

Proposition B.1
(Talagrand’s inequality).

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(H^{-s}(\Lambda_{L}))
be a Gaussian measure
\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)
with covariance operator
\Sigma
which is a strictly positive, self-adjoint, compact operator in
L^{2}(\Lambda_{L})
that admits a smooth diagonalizing basis
\{e_{k}\}\subset H^{s}(\Lambda_{L})
. Then, for any
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(H^{-s}(\Lambda_{L}))
, we have the inequality

W^{2}_{\Sigma^{-1}}(\nu,\mu)\leq 2\mathcal{H}(\nu|\mu).

(B.3)

After introducing notation, the proof of
Proposition

B.1
follows immediately from
Lemma

B.2
and
Lemma

B.3
below.

Notation.
Using that
\{e_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\subset H^{s}(\Lambda_{L})
, we can define
P_{M}:H^{s}(\Lambda_{L})\to\mathbb{R}^{M}
to be the encoding into the first
M
eigenfunctions. Namely, for any
\Phi\in H^{-s}(\Lambda_{L})
, we have

P_{M}(\Phi)=(\Phi.e_{1},...,\Phi.e_{M})\in\mathbb{R}^{M}.

We consider the sequence of measures
\mu^{M}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}P_{M}\#\mu=\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{M})\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{M})
, where

\Sigma_{M}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&\lambda_{2}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&\lambda_{M}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}.

We define the semi-norms associated to
\Sigma_{M}

\displaystyle\|v\|_{\Sigma_{M}^{-1}}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{k=1}^{M}\frac{1}{\lambda_{k}}|v_{k}|^{2}=\Sigma_{M}^{-1}v\cdot v,

which is defined on
\mathbb{R}^{M}
. We introduce the following notation for the anisotropic Wasserstein distance. Given
\mu^{M},\,\nu^{M}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{M})
, we define the distance (possibly infinite)

W^{2}_{\Sigma_{M}^{-1}}(\mu^{M},\nu^{M})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu^{M},\nu^{M})}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{M}\times\mathbb{R}^{M}}\|v-w\|_{\Sigma_{M}^{-1}}^{2}\;d\pi(v,w),

(B.4)

where
\Pi(\mu^{M},\nu^{M})\subset\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{M}\times\mathbb{R}^{M})
are the transference plans between
\mu^{M}
and
\nu^{M}
. Finally, we define the associated relative entropy functional

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(\nu^{M}|\mu^{M})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\sup_{F\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{M})}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{M}}F(w)\;d\nu^{M}(w)-\log\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{M}}e^{F(v)}\;d\mu^{M}(v)\right)

(B.5)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{M}}\frac{d\nu^{M}}{d\mu^{M}}\log\frac{d\nu^{M}}{d\mu^{M}}\;d\mu^{M}&\nu^{M}\ll\mu^{M}\\
+\infty&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}

which does not depend on the operator
\Sigma_{M}
.

Lemma B.2
.

Let
\mu,\,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(H^{-s}(\Lambda_{L}))
, for any
M\in\mathbb{N}
we consider
P_{M}\#\mu=\mu^{M}
,
P_{M}\#\nu=\nu^{M}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{M})
be their associated finite dimensional projections. Then, we have the limits

\lim_{M\to\infty}W^{2}_{\Sigma^{-1}_{M}}(\nu^{M},\mu^{M})=W^{2}_{\Sigma^{-1}}(\nu,\mu)\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\lim_{M\to\infty}\mathcal{H}(\nu^{M}|\mu^{M})=\mathcal{H}(\nu|\mu).

(B.6)

Step 1.
We show the monotonocity of the Wasserstein distance and the entropy with respect to the projection dimension. Namely, for any
M_{1},M_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
M_{1}\leq M_{2}
, we have the inequalities:

W_{\Sigma^{-1}_{M_{1}}}^{2}(\nu^{M_{1}},\mu^{M_{1}})\leq W_{\Sigma^{-1}_{M_{2}}}^{2}(\nu^{M_{2}},\mu^{M_{2}})\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\mathcal{H}(\nu^{M_{1}}|\mu^{M_{1}})\leq\mathcal{H}(\nu^{M_{2}}|\mu^{M_{2}}).

(B.7)

Moreover, the inequality still holds if
M_{2}=\infty
.

Proof of Step 1.
For simplicity, we show the proof with the notation for
M_{2}<\infty
, the generalization to
M_{2}=\infty
is analogous.
We start with the Wasserstein distance, we notice that for any transference plan
\pi\in\Pi(\mu^{M_{2}},\nu^{M_{2}})
between
\mu^{M_{2}}
and
\nu^{M_{2}}
, its projection
(P_{M_{1}}\times P_{M_{1}})_{\#}\pi\in\Pi(\mu^{M_{2}},\nu^{M_{2}})
is a transference plan between
\mu^{M_{1}}
and
\nu^{M_{1}}
. Hence, for any
\pi\in\Pi(\mu^{M_{2}},\nu^{M_{2}})
, the chain of inequalities is given by

\displaystyle W_{\Sigma^{-1}_{M_{1}}}^{2}(\nu^{M_{1}},\mu^{M_{1}})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{M_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{M_{1}}}\|v-w\|_{\Sigma_{M_{1}}^{-1}}^{2}\;d(P_{M_{1}}\times P_{M_{1}})_{\#}\pi(v,w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{M_{2}}\times\mathbb{R}^{M_{2}}}\|P_{M_{1}}v-P_{M_{1}}w\|_{\Sigma_{M_{2}}^{-1}}^{2}\;d\pi(v,w)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{M_{2}}\times\mathbb{R}^{M_{2}}}\|v-w\|_{\Sigma_{M_{2}}^{-1}}^{2}\;d\pi(v,w).

The inequality in (
B.7
) now follows by taking an infimum over
\pi\in\Pi(\mu^{M_{2}},\nu^{M_{2}})
.

For the entropy, we can use that any
F\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{M_{1}})
can be trivially extended to also belong to
\tilde{F}\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{M_{2}})
. Hence, we have the inequalities

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{M_{1}}}F(w)\;d\nu^{M_{1}}(w)-\log\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{M_{1}}}e^{F(v)}\;d\mu^{M_{1}}(v)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{M_{2}}}\tilde{F}(w)\;d\nu^{M_{2}}(w)-\log\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{M_{2}}}e^{\tilde{F}(v)}\;d\mu^{M_{2}}(v)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\mathcal{H}(\nu^{M_{2}}|\mu^{M_{2}}).

The inequality in (
B.7
) now follows by taking an supremum over
F\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{M_{1}})
.

Step 2.
We show the lower-semicontinuity

\displaystyle\liminf_{M\to\infty}W_{\Sigma^{-1}_{M}}^{2}(\nu^{M},\mu^{M})\geq W_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{2}(\nu,\mu)\quad\mbox{and}\quad\liminf_{M\to\infty}\mathcal{H}(\nu^{M}|\mu^{M})\geq\mathcal{H}(\nu|\mu).

(B.8)

Proof of Step 2.
We consider the inverse mapping to the projection
P_{M}^{-1}:\mathbb{R}^{M}\to H^{-s}(\Lambda_{L})
given by

P_{M}^{-1}v=\sum_{i=1}^{M}v_{i}e_{i}\in H^{-s},

and set up

\tilde{\mu}^{M}=P^{-1}_{M}\mu^{M}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\tilde{\nu}^{M}=P^{-1}_{M}\nu^{M}.

We notice that the measures
\tilde{\mu}^{M}
and
\tilde{\nu}^{M}
converge weakly to
\mu
and
\nu
as
M\to\infty
. This follows by continuity of the projection operator

\tilde{P}_{M}\Phi\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\langle\Phi,e_{i}\rangle e_{i}\to_{H^{-s}}\Phi,

and using Lebesgue dominated convergence

\int_{H^{-s}}F(\Phi)\;d\tilde{\mu}^{M}=\int_{H^{-s}}F(\tilde{P}_{M}\Phi)\;d\tilde{\mu}\to_{M\to\infty}\int_{H^{-s}}F(\Phi)\;d\tilde{\mu}.

We consider
\pi_{M}\in\Pi(\nu^{M},\mu^{M})
the optimal plan for
W_{\Sigma^{-1}_{M}}
, and take
\tilde{\pi}_{M}=(P_{M}^{-1}\times P_{M}^{-1})_{\#}\pi_{M}\in\mathcal{P}(H^{s}\times H^{s})
.
Then, we have the identity

W_{\Sigma^{-1}_{M}}^{2}(\nu^{M},\mu^{M})=\int_{H^{-s}\times H^{-s}}\|\Phi-\Psi\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{2}\tilde{\pi}_{M}(\Phi,\Psi).

As
H^{-s}
is a polish space, Prohorov’s implies that
\mu^{M}
and
\nu^{M}
are tight. This in turn implies that
\tilde{\pi}^{M}
is tight

\tilde{\pi}_{M}((K_{\varepsilon}^{\mu}\times K_{\varepsilon}^{\nu})^{c})\leq\tilde{\pi}_{M}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\times(K_{\varepsilon}^{\nu})^{c})+\tilde{\pi}_{M}((K_{\varepsilon}^{\mu})^{c}\times\mathcal{D}^{\prime})=\nu^{M}((K_{\varepsilon}^{\nu})^{c})+\mu^{M}((K_{\varepsilon}^{\mu})^{c})\leq 2\varepsilon.

Hence,
\tilde{\pi}^{M}
admits a convergent subsequence which has a limit
\pi^{\infty}\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
. Next, we show the lower-semicontinuity. For any
N\leq M
we have

W_{\Sigma^{-1}_{M}}^{2}(\nu^{M},\mu^{M})\geq\int_{H^{-s}\times H^{-s}}\|\tilde{P}_{N}\Phi-\tilde{P}_{N}\Psi\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{2}\tilde{\pi}^{M}(\Phi,\Psi),

Using weak convergence
\tilde{\pi}^{M}\rightharpoonup\pi^{\infty}
and the continuity of
\|\tilde{P}_{N}\Phi-\tilde{P}_{N}\Psi\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{2}
, we have that for any
N\in\mathbb{N}

\liminf_{M\to\infty}W_{\Sigma^{-1}_{M}}^{2}(\nu^{M},\mu^{M})\geq\int_{H^{-s}\times H^{-s}}\|\tilde{P}_{N}\Phi-\tilde{P}_{N}\Psi\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{2}\pi^{\infty}(\Phi,\Psi).

Taking supremum over
N
, we get the desired inequality

\liminf_{M\to\infty}W_{\Sigma^{-1}_{M}}^{2}(\nu^{M},\mu^{M})\geq\int_{H^{-s}\times H^{-s}}\|\Phi-\Psi\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{2}\pi^{\infty}(\Phi,\Psi)\geq W_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{2}(\nu,\mu).

For the relative entropy, we use the definition of the supremum (
B.2
). For any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
F\in C_{b}(H^{-s})
such that

\int_{H^{-s}}F(\Psi)\;d\nu(\Psi)-\log\left(\int_{H^{-s}}e^{F(\Phi)}\;d\mu(\Phi)\right)\geq\mathcal{H}(\nu|\mu)-\varepsilon.

Using the definition (
B.2
) for the relative entropy of
\tilde{\mu}^{M}
and
\tilde{\nu}^{M}
, we have

\mathcal{H}(\nu^{M}|\mu^{M})\geq\int_{H^{-s}}F(\tilde{P}_{M}\Psi)\;d\nu(\Psi)-\log\left(\int_{H^{-s}}e^{F(\tilde{P}_{M}\Phi)}\;d\mu(\phi)\right).

Taking limits and using that
\tilde{P}_{M}\Psi\to\Psi
in
H^{-s}
we have that for any
\varepsilon>0

\liminf_{M\to\infty}\mathcal{H}(\nu^{M}|\mu^{M})\geq\mathcal{H}(\nu|\mu)-\varepsilon,

and the conclusion follows.

Step 3.
The convergence now follows by combining
Step 1
and
Step 2
.

∎Next, we show the following known result for finite dimensional Gaussians.

Lemma B.3
.

Let
\mu^{M}=\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{M})\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{M})
a Gaussian measure with zero mean and covariance operator
\Sigma_{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
, then for any
\nu^{M}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{M})
we have the inequality:

W^{2}_{\Sigma^{-1}_{M}}(\nu^{M},\mu^{M})\leq 2\mathcal{H}(\nu^{M}|\mu^{M}).

(B.9)

Consider
\gamma\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{M})
the standard Gaussian measure. The classical Talagrand inequality
[
70
, Theorem 1.1]
states that for any
\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{M})
, we have the inequality

W_{I}^{2}(\rho,\gamma)\leq 2\mathcal{H}(\rho|\gamma).

(B.10)

We notice that

\gamma=\Sigma_{M}^{-1}\#\mu^{M},

and given
\nu^{M}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{M})
we define

\rho=\Sigma_{M}^{-1}\#\nu^{M}.

The result now follows from (
B.10
) and the identities

W_{\Sigma_{M}^{-1}}^{2}(\nu^{M},\mu^{M})=W_{I}^{2}(\rho,\gamma)\leq 2\mathcal{H}(\rho|\gamma)=2\mathcal{H}(\nu^{M}|\mu^{M}).

∎∎

C
Stroock’s formula

In this section, we provide the details for Stroock’s formula (
2.6
) of an inner product. This formula is well known for
F=G
:

\mathbb{E}F^{2}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j!}\|\mathbb{E}[D^{j}F]\|_{H^{\otimes j}}^{2}.

(C.1)

The inner product equation (
2.6
) we use in this paper follows immediately from the polarization identity

\mathbb{E}(FG)=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}(F+G)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\big(\mathbb{E}F^{2}+\mathbb{E}G^{2}\big).

(C.2)

Next, for completeness we formally derive the formula (
C.1
). For this we consider the orthonormal basis for
L^{2}_{\mu}(\Omega)
which is given by

\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha!}}\underbrace{\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}H_{\alpha_{i}}(Z.e_{i})}_{H_{\alpha}(Z)}\right\}_{\alpha}

where

\alpha\in\mathcal{A}=\{\alpha:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}\,|\quad\,\exists r\in\mathbb{N}\quad s.t.\quad\alpha_{i}=0\quad\;\forall i>r\}

is the set of multi-indices that vanish for all but finitely many values,
\alpha!=\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{i}!
, and
H_{\alpha_{i}}
denotes the standard Hermite polynomial of order
\alpha_{i}
. Taking the Chaos decomposition for
F
we have

F=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{c_{\alpha}}{\sqrt{\alpha!}}H_{\alpha}(Z)\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\mathbb{E}F^{2}=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}c^{2}_{\alpha}

On the other hand, we use that the Malliavin derivative is linear to compute the expectation in the following way

\mathbb{E}D^{j}F=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{c_{\alpha}}{\sqrt{\alpha!}}\mathbb{E}D^{j}H_{\alpha}(Z).

Let
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots)
be a multi-index with length
|\alpha|=j
. We define the associated index sequence
\mathbf{k}=(k_{1},\dots,k_{j})
by repeating each integer
i
exactly
\alpha_{i}
times:

\mathbf{k}=(\underbrace{1,\dots,1}_{\alpha_{1}},\underbrace{2,\dots,2}_{\alpha_{2}},\dots).

Computing the expectation of the Malliavin derivative of the basis element, we have:

\mathbb{E}\left[D^{j}H_{\alpha}(Z)\right]=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in S_{j}}e_{k_{\sigma(1)}}\otimes e_{k_{\sigma(2)}}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{k_{\sigma(j)}}&\text{if }|\alpha|=j,\\[12.0pt]
0&\text{if }|\alpha|\neq j,\end{cases}

where
S_{j}
is the set of permutations of
j
elements. Taking the norm and counting the repeated indexes we have

\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[D^{j}H_{\alpha}(Z)\right]\right\|^{2}_{H^{\otimes j}}=j!\alpha!

Hence, using orthogonality we get

\|\mathbb{E}D^{j}F\|_{H^{\otimes j}}^{2}=\left\|\sum_{|\alpha|=j}\frac{c_{\alpha}}{\sqrt{\alpha!}}\mathbb{E}\left[D^{j}H_{\alpha}(Z)\right]\right\|_{H^{\otimes j}}^{2}=j!\sum_{|\alpha|=j}c^{2}_{\alpha}

Re-arranging the terms, we get back the standard Stroock’s formula

\mathbb{E}F^{2}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j!}\|\mathbb{E}[D^{j}F]\|_{H^{\otimes j}}^{2}.

D
Construction and Properties of the Linear Sigma Model on the Torus

We now argue that
(\nu^{N}_{\epsilon})_{\epsilon>0}
is a Cauchy sequence in the total variation norm, uniformly in
N
. More precisely, we consider
\nu^{N}_{\epsilon}
as probability measures on
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\Lambda_{L})
endowed with the Borel topology and denote by
d_{\text{TV}}(\cdot,\cdot)
the corresponding total variation metric
given by

d_{\text{TV}}(\mu,\nu)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sup_{A\in\mathcal{F}}\big|\mu(A)-\nu(A)\big|,

(D.1)

where
\mathcal{F}
is the Borel sigma algebra over
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\Lambda_{L})
.

Lemma D.1
.

For any
\delta>1
, there exists a constant
C
depending on
\delta,m,\lambda
that that for all
\epsilon,\kappa<1

\sup_{N\geq 1}d_{\text{TV}}(\nu^{N}_{\epsilon},\nu^{N}_{\kappa})\leq C|\epsilon-\kappa|^{\delta}

(D.2)

We first argue that for all
\delta\in(0,1)
there exists a constant
C^{\prime}(\delta,\lambda,m)
such that for all
\epsilon,\kappa<1

\sup_{N\geq 1}\big|Z_{L,\epsilon}^{N}-Z_{L,\kappa}^{N}\big|\leq C^{\prime}|\epsilon-\kappa|^{\delta}.

(D.3)

To prove it, note that for all real numbers
a,b
it holds
|e^{a}-e^{b}|\leq(e^{a}+e^{b})|b-a|
, so that

\displaystyle\big|Z_{L,\epsilon}^{N}-Z_{L,\kappa}^{N}\big|

\displaystyle\leq|\lambda|\mathbb{E}^{\mu^{\otimes N}}\big[\big(e^{-\lambda V_{\epsilon}^{N}}+e^{-\lambda V_{\kappa}^{N}}\big)|V_{\epsilon}^{N}-V_{\kappa}^{N}|\big]

\displaystyle\lesssim\big(Z^{2\lambda,N}_{\epsilon}+Z^{2\lambda,N}_{\kappa}\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\big\|V_{\epsilon}^{N}-V_{\kappa}^{N}\big\|_{L^{2}(\Omega^{N})},

(D.4)

so the result follows from the upper bound for
Z_{\epsilon}^{N}
and the stability (
2.14
).

Let
\Phi_{\epsilon}^{N}\sim\nu^{N}_{\epsilon}
, then it holds that

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(\nu_{\epsilon}^{N}\mid\nu_{\kappa}^{N})=\mathbb{E}^{\nu^{N}_{\epsilon}}\text{log}\bigg(\frac{d\nu^{N}_{\epsilon}}{d\nu^{N}_{\kappa}}\bigg)=-\lambda\mathbb{E}^{\nu_{\epsilon}}\bigg[(V_{\epsilon}^{N}-V_{\kappa}^{N})(\Phi_{\epsilon}^{N})\bigg]+\text{log}\big(\frac{Z_{\kappa}^{N}}{Z_{\epsilon}^{N}}\big)

(D.5)

For the first term, we write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\nu_{\epsilon}}\bigg[(V_{\epsilon}^{N}-V_{\kappa}^{N})(\Phi_{\epsilon}^{N})\bigg]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{\epsilon}^{\lambda,N}}\mathbb{E}^{\mu^{\otimes N}}\big[\text{exp}(-\lambda V_{\epsilon}^{N})\big(V_{\epsilon}^{N}-V_{\kappa}^{N}\big)\big]

\displaystyle\leq\big(Z_{\epsilon}^{2\lambda,N}\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\big(\mathbb{E}|V_{\epsilon}^{N}-V_{\kappa}^{N}|^{2}\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq C|\epsilon-\kappa|^{\delta}.

For the second term, we use that since
Z_{\epsilon}^{N}\geq 1
, the mean-value theorem implies

\big|\text{log}Z_{\kappa}^{N}-\text{log}Z_{\epsilon}^{N}\big|\leq\big|Z_{\kappa}^{N}-Z_{\epsilon}^{N}\big|\leq C^{\prime}|\epsilon-\kappa|^{\delta}.

The result now follows from combining the two estimates with Pinsker’s inequality.

∎Our proof also relies on a suitable Chessboard estimate for the measure
\nu^{N}_{L}
, which we prove for completeness. Estimates of this type first appeared in
[
27
]
for
N=1
and a formulation (without proof) for general
N
is given in
[
45
]
. We refer to Theorem 5.8 in
[
10
]
for an analogous estimate for the lattice approximations.

Recalling the definition of the measure space
\Omega
and the associated canonical process
Z
, we define for each open (or closed) subset
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
the sigma algebra

\mathcal{F}_{C}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sigma\{Z.\varphi\mid\text{supp}\varphi\subset C\},

(D.6)

where for
\varphi=(\varphi_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
, the constraint
\text{supp}\varphi\subset C
means that
\text{supp}\varphi_{i}\subset C
for each
i\in[N]
.

Lemma D.2
.

The Chessboard estimate (
3.48
) holds.

Given a line
\Pi
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we denote by
\mathcal{R}_{\Pi}
the reflection across this line. We have a decomposition
\mathbb{T}_{L}=\mathbb{T}_{L}^{-}\cup\Pi\cup\mathbb{T}_{L}^{+}
, where
\mathcal{R}_{\Pi}
leaves
\Pi
invariant and
\mathcal{R}_{\Pi}\mathbb{T}_{L}^{-}=\mathbb{T}_{L}^{+}
. For an observable
F:\omega\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T}_{L})^{N}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
, we define
\mathcal{R}_{\Pi}F(\omega)=F(\mathcal{R}_{\Pi}\omega)
, where
\mathcal{R}_{\Pi}\omega.\varphi\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\omega.\mathcal{R}_{\Pi}\varphi
. The measure
\nu^{N}_{L}
is a limit of suitable extensions of the lattice approximations
\nu^{N}_{L,\epsilon}
. Reflection positivity of
\nu^{N}_{L,\epsilon}
follows easily from Corollary 5.4 in
[
10
]
, arguing as in the proof of Lemma 5.5 of
[
10
]
for the contribution of the measure from the discrete Gaussian free field. The measure
\nu^{N}_{L}
inherits reflection positivity in the limit
\epsilon\to 0
, which means that

(F,G)\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{T}_{L}^{+}},\nu^{N}_{L})\times L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{T}_{L}^{+}},\nu^{N}_{L})\mapsto\mathbb{E}^{\nu^{N}_{L}}(F\mathcal{R}_{\Pi}G)

(D.7)

is a symmetric, bi-linear form which is non-negative along the diagonal
F=G
. In particular, it satisfies the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality, so that for all
F\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{T}_{L}^{+}},\nu^{N}_{L})
it holds

\mathbb{E}^{\nu^{N}_{L}}F=\mathbb{E}^{\nu^{N}_{L}}(F\cdot 1)\leq\mathbb{E}^{\nu^{N}_{L}}(F\mathcal{R}_{\Pi}F)^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbb{E}^{\nu^{N}_{L}}(1\cdot\mathcal{R}_{\Pi}1)^{\frac{1}{2}}=\mathbb{E}^{\nu^{N}_{L}}(F\mathcal{R}_{\Pi}F)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(D.8)

Our plan is to repeatedly apply the above inequality with various choices of the plane
\Pi
. Namely, define for each
j=(j_{1},j_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\cap(-\frac{L}{2},\frac{L}{2})^{2}
the observable

F_{j,\epsilon}(\omega)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{N}\int_{C_{j}}:\|\omega_{\epsilon}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}^{4}:dx,

where
C_{j}
is the unit cube in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
having
j
as its bottom left corner, meaning the set of points
(j_{1}+t)e_{1}+(j_{2}+s)e_{2}
for
s,t\in[0,1]
. Each
F_{j,\epsilon}
is a Cauchy sequence in
L^{\infty-}(\Omega;\nu^{N}_{L})
as a consequence of (
2.14
), and therefore has a limit
F_{j}\in L^{\infty-}(\Omega;\nu^{N}_{L})
. We will make use of reflections across the lines
\Pi
of the form
\{x_{i}=a\}
where
a
is either zero or of the form
2^{\ell}
, where
2^{\ell+1}\leq\frac{L}{2}
. Note that

\mathcal{R}_{\{x_{i}=a\}}F_{j}=\delta_{i=1}F_{(a+1-j_{1})e_{1}+j_{2}e_{2}}+\delta_{i=2}F_{j_{1}e_{1}+(a+1-j_{2})e_{2}}.

(D.9)

Indeed, the fact that (
D.9
) holds with
F_{j}
replaced by the approximation
F_{j,\epsilon}
is a consequence of the commutativity of smooth Fourier truncation and reflection, that is
(\mathcal{R}_{\Pi}\omega)_{\epsilon}(x)=(\mathcal{R}_{\Pi}\omega_{\epsilon})(x)
, which uses the radial symmetry of the cutoff
\eta
. The plan is to repeatedly apply the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality, first along the lines
\{x_{1}=2^{\ell}\}
,
\{x_{1}=0\}
,
\{x_{2}=2^{m}\}
, and
\{x_{2}=0\}
in that order. To this end, we first argue by induction that for any
k

\mathbb{E}F_{0}\leq\bigg(\mathbb{E}\prod_{j_{1}=0}^{2^{k}}F_{j_{1}e_{1}}\bigg)^{2^{-k}}.

(D.10)

Indeed, the base case simply amounts to (
D.8
) applied with
F=F_{0}
along the plane
\{x_{1}=1\}
, taking into account (
D.9
) with
a=1
. Assuming inequality (
D.10
) holds for some
0\leq k
, we apply (
D.8
) with
F=\prod_{j_{1}=0}^{2^{k}}F_{j_{1}e_{1}}
along the line
\{x_{1}=2^{j+1}\}
to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}F_{0}

\displaystyle\leq\bigg(\mathbb{E}\prod_{j_{1}=0}^{2^{k}}F_{j_{1}e_{1}}\prod_{j_{1}=0}^{2^{k}}\mathcal{R}_{\{x_{1}=2^{j+1}\}}F_{j_{1}e_{1}}\bigg)^{2^{-(k+1)}}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\mathbb{E}\prod_{j_{1}=0}^{2^{k}}F_{j_{1}e_{1}}\prod_{j_{1}=0}^{2^{k}}F_{(2^{j+1}+1-j_{1})e_{1}}\bigg)^{2^{-(k+1)}}=\bigg(\mathbb{E}\prod_{j_{1}=0}^{2^{k+1}}F_{j_{1}e_{1}}\bigg)^{2^{-(k+1)}}.

This completes the proof of (
D.10
), and now applying (
D.8
) with
F=\prod_{j_{1}=0}^{2^{k}}F_{j_{1}e_{1}}
along the line
\{x_{1}=0\}
, we find

\mathbb{E}F_{0}\leq\bigg(\mathbb{E}\prod_{j_{1}=2^{-k}}^{2^{k}}F_{j_{1}e_{1}}\bigg)^{2^{-(k+1)}}.

Arguing inductively once more as above and reflecting along the lines
\{x_{2}=2^{j+1}\}
we obtain

\mathbb{E}F_{0}\leq\bigg(\mathbb{E}\prod_{j_{1}=2^{-k}}^{2^{k}}\prod_{j_{2}=0}^{2^{j}}F_{j_{1}e_{1}+j_{2}e_{2}}\bigg)^{2^{-(k+j+1)}},

(D.11)

so that finishing with one last application of (
D.8
) along the line
\{x_{2}=0\}
, we obtain the result.
∎Finally, we need a generalization of the Checkerboard estimate of Guerra/Rosen/Simon
[
36
]
, specifically Theorem A.1 from the Appendix. Compared to
[
36
]
, we need to argue that the exponent
p
can be chosen uniformly in
N
, which we do by induction and conditioning.

Lemma D.3
.

For all
m>0
, there exists a
p=p(m)
such that for all
L,N
the measure
\mu_{m,L}^{\otimes N}
has the following property.

For any rectangular array
C_{j}
of adjacent translates of a rectangle
C_{0}
such that
\Lambda_{L}=\cup_{j}C_{j}
, together with an array of random variables
(F_{j})_{j}
where
F_{j}
is
\mathcal{F}_{C_{j}}
measurable, the following inequality holds

\bigg\|\prod_{j}F_{j}\bigg\|_{L^{1}(d\mu_{m,L}^{\otimes N})}\leq\prod_{j}\|F_{j}\|_{L^{p}(d\mu_{m,L}^{\otimes N})}.

(D.12)

Remark D.4
.

By Fröhlich/Simon
[
27
]
Page 502, the exponent
p=p(m)
may be taken to be
\big(\frac{2}{1-e^{-m}}\big)^{2}
. For
m\in[0,1]
, we find that
p=O(m^{-2})
by taking into account the lower bound
1-e^{-m}\geq(1-e^{-1})m
.

The proof is by induction on
N
, as the base case follows from Theorem A.1 of
[
36
]
. Assuming the desired property holds for
N-1
, we write
\Phi=(\Phi_{1},\hat{\Phi})
and first apply the Checkerboard estimate in the variables
\hat{\Phi}
, which holds by our inductive assumption, then use the Checkerboard estimate for
N=1
in the variable
\Phi_{1}
to obtain

\displaystyle\int\bigg|\prod_{j}F_{j}(\Phi)\bigg|d\mu_{m,L}^{\otimes N}(\Phi)

\displaystyle=\int\int\bigg|\prod_{j}F_{j}(\Phi_{1},\hat{\Phi})\bigg|d\mu_{m,L}^{\otimes(N-1)}(\hat{\Phi})d\mu(\Phi_{1})

\displaystyle\leq\int\prod_{j}\bigg(\int|F_{j}(\Phi_{1},\hat{\Phi})|^{p}d\mu^{\otimes(N-1)}(\hat{\Phi})\bigg)^{\frac{1}{p}}d\mu(\Phi_{1})

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{j}\int\int\bigg|F_{j}(\Phi_{1},\hat{\Phi})\bigg|^{p}d\mu_{m,L}^{\otimes(N-1)}(\hat{\Phi})d\mu(\Phi_{1})

(D.13)

\displaystyle=\prod_{j}\|F_{j}\|_{L^{p}(d\mu_{m,L}^{\otimes N})},

(D.14)

which completes the proof.
∎Finally, we include some further technical details on the application of the Chessboard estimate used to bound the entropy density.

Lemma D.5
.

The Checkerboard estimate (
3.50
) holds.

Let us denote by
\Phi^{\kappa}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\Phi*\eta^{\kappa}
the re-scaled convolution with a standard mollifier
\tilde{\eta}
which is supported (in physical space) on a unit ball. Note that

\frac{1}{N}\int_{C_{j}}:\|Z\|_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}^{4}:dx=\lim_{\kappa\to 0}\frac{1}{N}\int_{C_{j}}:\|Z^{\kappa}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}^{4}:dx,

(D.15)

where the limit is in
L^{p}(d\mu_{m,L})
for any
p\in[1,\infty)
. For each cube
C_{j}
, we can choose a subset
C_{j}^{\kappa}\subset C_{j}
such that
I_{j,0}^{\kappa}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{N}\int_{C_{j}^{\kappa}}:\|Z^{\kappa}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}^{4}:dx
is
\mathcal{F}_{C_{j}}
measurable and
|C_{j}\setminus C_{j}^{\kappa}|\to 0
as
\kappa\to 0
. Defining
I_{j,1}^{\kappa}=I_{j}^{\kappa}-I_{j,0}^{\kappa}
, we find that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\mu_{m,L}^{\otimes N}}\prod_{j\in I_{J}}\bigg|\frac{1}{N}\int_{C_{j}}:\|Z^{\kappa}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}^{4}:dx\bigg|^{2}=\mathbb{E}^{\mu_{m,L}^{\otimes N}}\prod_{j\in I_{J}}(|I_{j,0}^{\kappa}|^{2}+|I_{j,1}^{\kappa}|^{2}+2|I_{j,0}^{\kappa}||I_{j,1}^{\kappa}|).

The above expectation can be written as a sum of expectations of products. The only contribution that survives in the
\kappa\to 0
limit is

\mathbb{E}^{\mu_{m,L}^{\otimes N}}\prod_{j\in I_{J}}|I_{j,0}^{\kappa}|^{2}\leq\prod_{j\in I_{J}}\bigg\|\bigg(\frac{1}{N}\int_{C_{j,\kappa}}:\|Z^{\kappa}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}^{4}:dx\bigg)^{2}\bigg\|_{L^{p}(d\mu_{m,L}^{\otimes N})},

and the RHS converges as
\kappa\to 0
to the RHS of (
3.50
). All remaining contributions tend to zero by Hölder’s inequality since they contain at least one factor of
I_{j,1}^{\kappa}
, which goes to zero in
L^{p}(d\mu_{m,L}^{\otimes N})
for every
p\in[1,\infty)
.
