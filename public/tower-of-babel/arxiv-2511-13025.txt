Reconstruction of Manifold Distances from Noisy Observations
Charles Fefferman, Jonathan Marty, Kevin Ren

Abstract
We consider the problem of reconstructing the intrinsic geometry of a manifold from noisy pairwise distance observations. Specifically, let ℳ \mathcal{M} denote a diameter 1 d-dimensional manifold and μ \mu a probability measure on ℳ \mathcal{M} that is mutually absolutely continuous with the volume measure. Suppose X 1 , … , X N X_{1},\dots,X_{N} are i.i.d. samples of μ \mu and we observe noisy-distance random variables d ′ ​ ( X j , X k ) d^{\prime}(X_{j},X_{k}) that are related to the true geodesic distances d ⁡ ( X j , X k ) d(X_{j},X_{k}) . With mild assumptions on the distributions and independence of the noisy distances, we develop a new framework for recovering all distances between points in a sufficiently dense subsample of M M . Our framework improves on previous work which assumed i.i.d. additive noise with known moments. Our method is based on a new way to estimate L 2 L_{2} -norms of certain expectation–functions f x ​ ( y ) = 𝔼 ​ d ′ ​ ( x , y ) f_{x}(y)=\mathbb{E}d^{\prime}(x,y) and use them to build robust clusters centered at points of our sample. Using a new geometric argument, we establish that, under mild geometric assumptions—bounded curvature and positive injectivity radius —these clusters allow one to recover the true distances between points in the sample up to an additive error of O ⁡ ( ε ​ log ⁡ ε − 1 ) O(\varepsilon\log\varepsilon^{-1}) . We develop two distinct algorithms for producing these clusters. The first achieves a sample complexity N ≍ ε − 2 ​ d − 2 ​ log ⁡ ( 1 / ε ) N\asymp\varepsilon^{-2d-2}\log(1/\varepsilon) and runtime o ⁡ ( N 3 ) o(N^{3}) . The second introduces novel geometric ideas that warrant further investigation. In the presence of missing observations, we show that a quantitative lower bound on sampling probabilities suffices to modify the cluster construction in the first algorithm and extend all recovery guarantees. Our main technical result also elucidates which properties of a manifold are necessary for the distance recovery, which suggests further extension of our techniques to a broader class of metric probability spaces.

Introduction

Let
(\mathcal{M},\mu,d)
be geodesic probability space. Suppose that we are given noisy versions of the distances
d^{\prime}(x_{j},x_{k})
for a set of points
\{x_{i}\}_{i\in[N]}
, which are i.i.d. samples of
\mu
. Assuming modest constraints on the distribution of the
d^{\prime}(x_{j},x_{k})
, this paper asks whether it is possible to approximate the pairwise geodesic distances
d(x_{j},x_{k})
for a smaller subset of the
x_{i}
which are sufficiently dense in
\mathcal{M}
. If
(\mathcal{M},d)
denotes a Riemannian manifold with geodesic distance, one can use these approximate distances to construct an approximation of
\mathcal{M}
in the Gromov-Hausdorff sense, see
[
11
]
.

There have been several previous approaches to learning abstract Riemannian manifolds from geodesic distance data. The Diffusion Maps
[
7
,
8
,
6
]
and EigenMap
[
2
]
algorithms build weighted graphs based on the distance information. These weights define a diffusion or differential operator on the manifold. By approximating the associated eigenfunctions, each point can be non-isometrically embedded into
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Further, the embeddings given by both approaches are used to approximate the metric in
[
19
]
. In
[
21
]
it is assumed that the manifold is covered by a single coordinate chart and we can access the coordinates for all sampled points as well as ”similarity measures”, which depend on the distances. The relationship between the underlying metric and the observed similarity measures is then modeled. The approximation of a Riemannian manifold in a Lipschitz sense based on access to pairwise distances between points in a dense net with small deterministic errors was treated in
[
11
]
.
[
12
]
addresses doing so when the distances are between a dense net of an open subset of the manifold with certain properties and a dense net of the whole manifold. In
[
13
]
, the approach of
[
11
]
was extended to noisy distances of the form
d(x_{j},x_{k})+\eta_{jk}
, where the
\eta_{jk}
are i.i.d. and we have access to a few of their moments.
[
13
]
also accounts for the case where a subset of the noisy distances are missing.

In this paper we develop methods for recovering the geodesic distances between a sufficiently dense set of points on
\mathcal{M}
given access to only noisy distances. Our sample size (number of draws from
\mu
) and runtime are comparable to
[
13
]
while significantly loosening assumptions on the noisy distances, foregoing much of the required independence and knowledge of moments (for a more detailed discussion of how our approach relates to that of
[
13
]
, see
Section

5
). Further, we are able to extend past the case of Riemannian manifolds to finite diameter geodesic probability spaces which satisfy key properties. These properties are characteristic of manifolds with curvature bounds, but can hold for more general spaces. Our methods take advantage of how certain statistics of the
d^{\prime}(x,y)
change as we vary the choice of
(x,y)
. It is possible to access these statistics by taking inner products of key functions against
\mu
and these inner products can in turn be approximated by taking averages of the noisy distances. Our main result is stated in Section
1.1
. Intuition for our approach is given in Section
1.2
. Our methods have potential applications to a diverse set of inverse problems, which are discussed in Section
1.3
.

1.2
Intuition

Our methods rely on constructing clusters of points which are close together. From there, we can approximate the average noisy distances between given points and all points in a given cluster and use these estimates to model the true distances between a sufficiently dense set of points on the manifold. However, a key conceptual impediment is that we make no strong assumptions on the distribution of
d^{\prime}(x_{j},x_{k})
for any pair of points. By contrast, in
[
13
]
it is assumed that
d^{\prime}(x_{j},x_{k})=d(x_{j},x_{k})+\eta_{jk}
where the
\eta_{jk}
are i.i.d. and we know their mean and variance, in which case it’s clear how our point-cluster distances would relate to the true distances between, say, a point in the cluster and our given point. In our case the meaning of this average is unclear and, moreover, it’s even less clear how one would go about constructing a cluster.

Consider
f(x,y)=\mathbb{E}d^{\prime}(x,y)
and let
f_{x}(y)=f(x,y)
. If we assume that the
d^{\prime}(x,y)\sim K_{d(x,y)}
and the
r\mapsto\mathbb{E}K_{r}
is a bilipschitz function, then the
f_{x}:\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}
are bilipschitz functions w.r.t. the intrinsic metric. Observe that, on account of
\mathcal{M}
having finite diameter, the
f_{x}
are bounded functions. This boundedness and the finite volume of
\mathcal{M}
imply that
f_{x}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{M},\mu)
where
\mu
denotes the uniform probability distribution. This
L^{2}
structure is the key ingredient in our algorithm.

To get a sense of how we can use this
L^{2}
structure to overcome the challenges mentioned in the first paragraph of this section, observe that we can approximate the inner product using our data. Take
x,y\in\mathcal{M}
and sample a set
Z=\{z_{i}\}
with the
z_{i}\sim\mu
i.i.d. For a pair
x,y\in\mathcal{M}
with
x\neq y
, consider the random variable

I(x,y)=\frac{1}{|Z|}\sum_{z\in Z}d^{\prime}(x,z)d^{\prime}(z,y)

and note that
\mathbb{E}I(x,y)=\langle f_{x},f_{y}\rangle
, where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the
L^{2}
inner product. Hence by sampling enough points we can approximate
\langle f_{x},f_{y}\rangle
for as many pairs
(x,y)
as we want to whatever degree of accuracy desired. What can we do with this information?

Consider the inner product
\langle f_{x},f_{y}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}(\|f_{x}\|_{2}^{2}+\|f_{y}\|_{2}^{2}-\|f_{x}-f_{y}\|_{2}^{2})
for
x,y\in\mathcal{M}
. Note that the
\|f_{x}-f_{y}\|_{2}^{2}
acts as a proxy for
d(x^{*},a)
. To see this, observe that taking
d(x,a)>3\delta
means that on a
\delta
-ball around
x
,
|f_{x}-f_{a}|
is above some constant. Lower bounding the volume of this ball, which can be done via a sectional curvature upper bound, gives a lower bound on
\|f_{x}-f_{a}\|^{2}_{2}
. This follows from the lower Lipschitz constant of
r\mapsto\mathbb{E}K_{r}
. Alternatively, for any given
c>0
, setting sufficiently
d(x,a)
small enough means that
|f_{x}-f_{a}|<c
everywhere on the manifold (follows from the upper Lipschitz constant of
r\to\mathbb{E}K_{r}
). Thus,
\|f_{x}-f_{y}\|_{2}^{2}
acts as a proximity sensor.

In the case that
\mathcal{M}
is a symmetric space we can take direct advantage of this property, since all the
f_{x}
have the same
L^{2}
norm, which we’re term
S
. As such, for any pair
x,y\in\mathcal{M}
,
\langle f_{x},f_{y}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}(\|f_{x}\|^{2}+\|f_{y}\|^{2}-\|f_{x}-f_{y}\|_{2}^{2})=S-\frac{1}{2}\|f_{x}-f_{y}\|_{2}^{2}
. Thus, the function
y\to\langle x,y\rangle
acts as a proximity sensor and we can use it to build clusters around any point
x\in\mathcal{M}
.

However, in the general case we need to deal with the changing values of the
\|f_{x}\|_{2}^{2}
. We can overcome this difficulty through optimization. Let
X^{*}=\arg\max_{x\in\mathcal{M}}\|f_{x}\|_{2}^{2}
. We can find a member
x^{*}\in X^{*}
by taking

(x^{*},y^{*})=\arg\max_{x,y\in\mathcal{M},x\neq y}\langle f_{x},f_{y}\rangle

Arguing based on the optimality of
x^{*}
and Lipschitz-ness of
x\to\|f_{x}\|^{2}_{2}
, it can be shown that the
\langle f_{x^{*}},f_{y}\rangle
can be used to distinguish points that are close to
x^{*}
from those that are far. Hence, we can use
y\mapsto\langle f_{x^{*}},f_{y}\rangle
and functions like it to build clusters, in this case around
x^{*}
.

This argmax idea is directly implemented in Algorithm 2 (
Theorem

4.1
). In fact, a slight variation of the argmax trick allows us to implement a simpler Algorithm 1 (
Theorem

1.1
).

As for relating the noisy point-cluster distances to true ones, having built these clusters the bilipschitz-ness of
r\mapsto\mathbb{E}K_{r}
can be utilized to approximate the original distances. This is formalized in Section
3.4
.

1.3
Potential Applications

The ability to reconstruct the intrinsic metric based on noisy pairwise distances is relevant to a broad class of inverse problems which attempt to recover material properties of some body based on observed or experimental data. For example, suppose I have two points in some inhomogenous medium. The time that it takes to traverse between these can be modelled by an underlying
travel-time metric
of a manifold (the medium). Hence, recovering the wave-speed function within the medium allows for the reconstruction of the metric. Recovering the travel-time metric accurately has applications in both medical and seismic imaging contexts
[
22
,
23
,
14
]
. Accurate reconstruction of the travel-time metric for seismic waves has the potential to unveil nuances in the Earth’s structure. For example, measurements of these travel times has shown that seismic waves within the inner core travel faster along the Earth’s spin-axis than in other directions
[
9
]
. Another application is magnetic resonance imaging based elastography, which measures mechanical properties of tissue based on its response to acoustic waves
[
14
]
. For a more detailed discussion of these topics, see Section 5 of
[
13
]
.

Existing methods to solve these inverse problems rely on measurements taken at the boundary to infer the intrinsic distances or travel times between points in the manifold
[
23
,
22
,
15
]
. As an example of such boundary measurements, one might probe tissue using an ultrasound machine, moving the transducer to get readings at different angles
[
22
]
. In addition to algorithmic progress, there is a line of analysis which studies the connection between boundary measurements of a wave equation and the distances between points in the interior
[
1
,
10
,
16
,
17
]
. However, random errors in the boundary measurements can propagate to the estimates of pairwise distances, reducing the efficacy of these approaches. In the context of ultrasound imaging, diffuse reverberation, in which the emitted sound wave reflects multiple times before returning to the transducer, is known to reduce the efficacy of sound speed estimation and Doppler imaging, among other techniques
[
4
,
15
,
20
]
. Thus, there is a need to develop methods which can either denoise the input data for these methods or reconstruct the Riemannian metric from their corrupt outputted distances. The techniques in this paper are relevant to the latter problem.

\|d^{\prime}(x,y)\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq C_{1}
;

•

(“Independence”)
\{d^{\prime}(x,y)\}_{\{x,y\}\subset X}
is a set of random variables such that for every
x\in X
, the variables
\{d^{\prime}(x,y)\}_{y\in X}
are independent conditioned on any subset of the other variables
\{d^{\prime}(y,z)\}_{y,z\neq x}
.

and furthermore whose expectation
f(x,y)=\mathbb{E}d^{\prime}(x,y)
satisfies the following properties:

•

(Bounded expectation)
|f(x,y)|\leq C_{2}
for all
x,y\in X
;

•

(Lower bound)
f(x,y)-f(x,z)\geq\sigma
for all distinct
x,y,z\in X
with
d(x,y)\geq d(x,z)+\varepsilon
.

•

(Uniform continuity) If
y,z\in X
satisfy
d(y,z)\leq\delta
, then
|f(x,y)-f(x,z)|\leq\frac{\kappa\sigma^{2}}{8C_{2}}
for all
x\neq y,z
.

Suppose that
N_{0}\geq C\frac{C_{1}^{4}+C_{2}^{4}}{\kappa^{2}\sigma^{4}c_{2}^{2}}\log\frac{N}{\theta}+\frac{C}{c_{1}^{2}}\log\frac{N}{\theta}
and
\alpha N\geq\frac{CC_{1}^{2}}{\phi\sigma^{2}}\log\frac{N_{0}}{\theta}
, where
C>0
is an absolute constant.
Then with probability
\geq 1-\theta
, we can compute numbers
A(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
in time
N^{3}N_{0}^{2}
such that:

1.

for every
x,y\in Y
, if
d(x,y)\leq r_{0}-8\varepsilon
, then
A(x,y)\neq\infty
;

2.

for every
x,y,z\in Y
satisfying
d(x,y)\geq d(x,z)+17\varepsilon
and
A(x,y),A(x,z)\neq+\infty
, we have
A(x,y)>A(x,z)
.

If all
p(x,y)=1
(the non-missing case), we can in fact compute numbers
A(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}
such that for all
x,y,z\in Y
and
d(x,y)\geq d(x,z)+17\varepsilon
, then
A(x,y)>A(x,z)
in time

. We also obtain slightly relaxed guarantees
N_{0}\geq C\frac{C_{1}^{4}+C_{1}^{2}C_{2}^{2}}{\kappa^{2}\sigma^{4}}\log\frac{N}{\theta}
and
\alpha N\geq\frac{CC_{1}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\log\frac{N_{0}}{\theta}
.

Remark 3.2
.

Think of
\sigma\sim\varepsilon
. It turns out that combining with
Proposition

, we need to take
N_{0}\sim\varepsilon^{-\max\{d,4\}}(d\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+\log\frac{1}{\theta})
,

, and

.

3.2
Proof of
Theorem

3.1
, non-missing case

3.2.1
Estimating norms from inner products

Definition 3.3
.

Fix a subset
Y\subseteq X
. For functions
f,g:Y\to\mathbb{R}
, we define

\langle f,g\rangle=\frac{1}{|Y|}\sum_{y\in Y}f(y)g(y).

The motivation for considering inner products is that they can be estimated effectively via the following concentration statement for inner products. The statement and proof technique may be of independent interest. The statement is not the most general possible in order to give a clean exposition; the reader is encouraged to relax the constraints of the proposition. The proof is deferred to
Section

6
.

Proposition 3.4

(Sub-Gaussian Hoeffding Inequality for Inner Products)
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
,
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
be sub-Gaussian random variables with means

\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]
,
\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}]
and Orlicz norms
\|X_{i}\|_{\psi_{2}}
,
\|Y_{i}\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq K
.
Suppose that:

•

Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
are mutually independent;

•

X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
are mutually independent conditioned on
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
;

•

\mathbb{E}[X_{i}\mid Y_{1},\cdots,Y_{n}]=\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]
,
|\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]|\leq L
, and
|\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}]|\leq L
for all
1\leq i\leq n
.

(These assumptions hold if
X_{1},\cdots,X_{n},Y_{1},\cdots,Y_{n}
are all mutually independent.)
Then for all
t\geq 0
,

\Pr\!\left(\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}Y_{i}-\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}])\right|\geq t\right)\leq 5\exp\!\left(-\frac{ct^{2}}{16K^{2}((K^{2}+L^{2})n+t)}\right),

where
c
is a universal constant.

Remark 3.5
.

If
X_{1},\cdots,X_{n},Y_{1},\cdots,Y_{n}
, then each
X_{i}Y_{i}
is exponential, hence we can use the sub-exponential Bernstein inequality. However, our approach works for our weaker mutual dependencies, at the cost of worse constants.

In our context, we have a noisy collection of vectors
\{f_{x}\}_{x\in X}
defined by
f_{x}(y)=d^{\prime}(x,y)
and by
Proposition

3.4
we can efficiently estimate inner products
\langle f_{x},f_{y}\rangle
with
x\neq y
; unfortunately, this is not enough to accurately determine norms
\|f_{x}-f_{y}\|^{2}
, which is the target quantity for determining which pairs
(f_{x},f_{y})
are close. Instead, we resort to estimating the norm via the following lemma.

Lemma 3.6

(Separation Lemma)
.

Fix
L,\delta>0
, and suppose that for every
x\in X
, we have a function
f_{x}:Y\to\mathbb{R}
. Suppose that:

•

(Bounded) Every
\|f_{x}\|_{2}\leq L
;

•

(Dense) For any
x\in X
, there exists
y\neq x\in X
with
\|f_{x}-f_{y}\|_{2}\leq\delta
.

Then for any
x\neq y
,

\frac{1}{2}\|f_{x}-f_{y}\|_{2}(\|f_{x}-f_{y}\|_{2}-2\delta)\leq\sup_{z\in X,z\neq x,y}|\langle f_{x}-f_{y},f_{z}\rangle|\leq L\|f_{x}-f_{y}\|_{2}.

Proof.

We first look at the left inequality. If
\|f_{x}-f_{y}\|_{2}\leq 2\delta
then it is trivial, so assume
\|f_{x}-f_{y}\|_{2}>2\delta
. Note that one of
|\left<f_{x},f_{x}-f_{y}\right>|\geq\frac{1}{2}\|f_{x}-f_{y}\|^{2}
or
|\left<f_{y},f_{x}-f_{y}\right>|\geq\frac{1}{2}\|f_{x}-f_{y}\|^{2}
; otherwise,

\|f_{x}-f_{y}\|^{2}=\left<f_{x},f_{x}-f_{y}\right>-\left<f_{y},f_{x}-f_{y}\right><\frac{1}{2}\|f_{x}-f_{y}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|f_{x}-f_{y}\|^{2}=\|f_{x}-f_{y}\|^{2}.

Without loss of generality, assume
|\left<f_{x},f_{x}-f_{y}\right>|\geq\frac{1}{2}\|f_{x}-f_{y}\|^{2}
. Using the dense assumption, pick
z\neq x,y
such that
\|f_{x}-f_{z}\|_{2}\leq\delta
. Then by Cauchy-Schwarz,

|\langle f_{x}-f_{y},f_{z}\rangle|\geq|\langle f_{x}-f_{y},f_{x}\rangle|-|\langle f_{x}-f_{y},f_{x}-f_{z}\rangle|\geq\frac{1}{2}\|f_{x}-f_{y}\|^{2}_{2}-\delta\|f_{x}-f_{y}\|_{2}.

This proves the first claim.

For the right inequality, we use Cauchy-Schwarz and our boundedness assumption to get, for all
z,w\in X
,

|\langle f_{x}-f_{y},f_{z}\rangle|\leq\|f_{x}-f_{y}\|_{2}\|f_{z}\|_{2}\leq L\|f_{x}-f_{y}\|_{2}.\qed

Remark 3.7
.

If our bounded assumption is
\|f_{x}\|_{\infty}\leq L
, then our result can be improved to
\leq L\|f_{x}-f_{y}\|_{1}
.

3.2.2
Cluster Construction

Now, we begin the proof of
Theorem

3.1
in the non-missing data case. For convenience, define
d^{\prime}(x,x)=0
and
f(x,x)=0
for all
x\in X
.

Definition 3.8
.

Fix a subset
Y\subset X
. We define

\mathcal{C}(x)=\{y\in X:\sup_{z\in X,z\neq x,y}\left|\frac{1}{|Y|}\sum_{v\in Y}(d^{\prime}(x,v)-d^{\prime}(y,v))d^{\prime}(z,v)\right|\leq\frac{1}{5}\kappa\sigma^{2}\}.

Note that
x\in\mathcal{C}(x)
.

Proposition 3.9
.

With probability at least
1-2|X|^{2}\exp(-|Y|\delta_{2}^{4}/(25600C_{1}^{2}(C_{1}^{2}+C_{2}^{2}+1))
, we have that for all
x\in X
,

B(x,\delta)\subset\mathcal{C}(x)\subset B(x,4\varepsilon).

(2)

After a precomputation cost of
|X|^{2}|Y|
, each cluster
\mathcal{C}(x)
can be computed in
|X|^{2}
time.

Proof.

If we precompute every
\sum_{v\in Y}d^{\prime}(x,v)d^{\prime}(y,v)
for each pair
x,y\in X
, each computation of
\frac{1}{|Y|}\sum_{v\in Y}(d^{\prime}(x,v)-d^{\prime}(y,v))(d^{\prime}(z,v)-d^{\prime}(w,v))
can be done in
O(1)
time. For each
y\in X
, it takes
O(|X|)
time to compute the supremum. Now we turn to proving (
2
).

Note that by independence,

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{|Y|}\sum_{v\in Y}d^{\prime}(x,v)d^{\prime}(z,v))\right]=\frac{1}{|Y|}\sum_{v\in Y}f(x,v)f(z,v)=\left<f_{x},f_{z}\right>,

where
f_{x}:Y\to\mathbb{R}
is defined by
f_{x}(y)=f(x,y)
.
Thus, by
Proposition

3.4
, with probability at least
1-5|X|^{2}\exp(-c|Y|\kappa^{2}\sigma^{4}/(25600C_{1}^{2}(C_{1}^{2}+C_{2}^{2}+1))
, we have that for all distinct
x\neq z\in X
,

\left|\frac{1}{|Y|}\sum_{v\in Y}d^{\prime}(x,v)d^{\prime}(z,v)-\left<f_{x},f_{z}\right>\right|\leq\frac{\kappa\sigma^{2}}{40}.

(3)

By using triangle inequality on a few applications of (
3
), we have for every distinct
x,y,z\in X
,

\left|\frac{1}{|Y|}\sum_{v\in Y}(d^{\prime}(x,v)-d^{\prime}(y,v))d^{\prime}(z,v)-\left<f_{x}-f_{y},f_{z}\right>\right|\leq\frac{\kappa\sigma^{2}}{20}.

(4)

Observe that
\|f_{x}\|_{2}=\sum_{y\in Y}|f(x,y)|^{2}\leq C_{2}
and for every
x\in X
, there exists
y\neq x\in X
such that
d(x,y)\leq\delta
, hence by theorem assumption,

\|f_{x}-f_{y}\|_{2}=\sqrt{\left(\frac{1}{|Y|}\sum_{z\in Y\setminus\{x,y\}}|f(x,z)-f(y,z)|^{2}\right)+\frac{1}{|Y|}\cdot 2f(x,y)^{2}}\leq\sqrt{\left(\frac{\kappa\sigma^{2}}{8C_{2}}\right)^{2}+\frac{2C_{2}^{2}}{|Y|}}\leq\frac{\kappa\sigma^{2}}{8C_{2}}+\frac{2C_{2}}{\sqrt{|Y|}}.

(5)

If
y\in B(x,\delta)
, then
\|f_{x}-f_{y}\|\leq\frac{\kappa\sigma^{2}}{8C_{2}}
using the same calculation as in (
5
), so by Lemma
3.6
and (
4
) (and using that
N_{0}=|Y|
is sufficiently large),

\sup_{z\in X,z\neq x,y}\left|\frac{1}{|Y|}\sum_{v\in Y}(d^{\prime}(x,v)-d^{\prime}(y,v))d^{\prime}(z,v)\right|\leq C_{2}\cdot\left(\frac{\kappa\sigma^{2}}{8C_{2}}+\frac{2C_{2}}{\sqrt{|Y|}}\right)+\frac{1}{20}\kappa\sigma^{2}\leq\frac{1}{5}\kappa\sigma^{2}.

If
d(x,y)\geq 4\varepsilon
, then by assumption we have
2C_{2}\geq\|f_{x}-f_{y}\|
and

\|f_{x}-f_{y}\|^{2}\geq\kappa\sigma^{2}
, hence by Lemma
3.6
, we have

\sup_{z\in X,z\neq x,y}\left|\frac{1}{|Y|}\sum_{v\in Y}(d^{\prime}(x,v)-d^{\prime}(y,v))d^{\prime}(z,v)\right|\geq\frac{1}{2}\kappa\sigma^{2}-2C_{2}\cdot\frac{\kappa\sigma^{2}}{8C_{2}}-\frac{1}{20}\kappa\sigma^{2}=\frac{1}{5}\kappa\sigma^{2}.

∎

Recall that we have conditioned on
d^{\prime}(x,y)
with
\{x,y\}\cap Y\neq\emptyset
; in particular, we have not conditioned on
d^{\prime}(x,y)
with
\{x,y\}\cap Y=\emptyset
.
We can ensure
\mathcal{C}(x)\cap Y=\emptyset
. Then, we compute the noisy averages (for
x,y\in Y
)

A(x,y):=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}(y)|}\sum_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}(y)}d^{\prime}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),

where
x^{\prime}=x^{\prime}(x)
is an arbitrary point in
\mathcal{C}(x)
that we pick once and for all. Note that

\mathbb{E}A(x,y)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}(y)|}\sum_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}(y)}\mathbb{E}d^{\prime}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}).

Lemma 3.10
.

\mathbb{E}A(x,y)-\mathbb{E}A(x,z)\geq\sigma
for all
x,y,z\in Y
with
d(x,y)\geq d(x,z)+17\varepsilon
.

Proof.

If
d(x,y)\geq d(x,z)+17\varepsilon
, then for every
x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}(x)
,
y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}(y)
, and
z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}(z)
, we have
d(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\geq d(x^{\prime},z^{\prime})+\varepsilon
by triangle inequality and the fact that
\mathcal{C}(x)\subset B(x,4\varepsilon)
, etc. Thus by the fact that an average of a sequence is between its minimum and maximum element and using our theorem assumption, we have

\mathbb{E}A(x,y)-\mathbb{E}A(x,z)\geq\min_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}(y)}f(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})-\max_{z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}(z)}f(x^{\prime},z^{\prime})\geq\sigma.\qed

Now by Theorem
2.2
, with probability
1-2|Y|^{2}\exp(-c\alpha N\sigma^{2}/C_{1}^{2})
, we have for all
x,y\in Y
,

\displaystyle\left|A(x,y)-\mathbb{E}A(x,y)\right|\leq\frac{\sigma}{3}.

Thus, if
d(x,y)\geq d(x,z)+17\varepsilon
, we get
A(x,y)>A(x,z)
, as desired.

Finally, to make the probability of success at least
1-\theta
, it suffices to ensure that:

\displaystyle 5|X|^{2}\exp(-c|Y|\kappa^{2}\sigma^{4}/(C_{1}^{4}+C_{1}^{2}C_{2}^{2}))\leq\frac{\theta}{2},

\displaystyle 2|Y|^{2}\exp(-c\alpha N\cdot\sigma^{2}/C_{1}^{2})\leq\frac{\theta}{2}.

One can check that the choices of
N_{0}
and
N
in the theorem statement work.

which implies

(7)

2.3

(8)

Let

\mathcal{C}(x)=\{y\in X:\frac{1}{|Y|}\sum_{z\in Y}m(x,z)m(y,z)\geq 1.5c_{1}\text{ and}\\
\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}v,w\in X\\

to compute

Finally, to make the probability of success at least

Algorithm 2: Regularized Optimization

We start with a sketch of our algorithm. We will sample three sets of points uniformly from
\mu
.
S_{1}
will be used for selecting our cluster “centers”,
S_{2}
will be used for running inner products, and
S_{3}
will be used to draw cluster points. Further, we will denote

. Let

. Assume that we have drawn
k-1

, where

denotes the i-th cluster and
n_{i}=|C_{i}|=n
. Consider the objective function

g_{k}(x,y)=\langle f_{x},f_{y}\rangle+\beta\min_{1\leq i\leq k-1}\mathbb{E}D_{i}(x)+\beta\min_{1\leq i\leq k-1}\mathbb{E}D_{i}(y)

,

, and

and then approximate

for all

, using the results to build

by selecting points where the objective is above a certain threshold. We will stop the algorithm when it can be detected that

(14)

We will force all cluster centers to be at least distance
c\varepsilon
from each other, for some small constant

. Thus, the maximal number of clusters

for which the algorithm still runs is upper bounded by a constant depending on
\varepsilon

\varepsilon

The algorithm works under general conditions on

and

4.1
Accessing Objective Functions

We have access to the objective function via

\tilde{g}_{k}(x,y)=L_{x,y}+\beta\min_{1\leq i\leq k-1}D_{i}(x)+\beta\min_{1\leq i\leq k-1}D_{i}(y)

where
L_{x,y}=\frac{1}{N_{2}}\sum_{z\in S_{2}}d^{\prime}(x,z)d^{\prime}(z,y)
. We want to achieve

|\tilde{g}_{k}(x,y)-g_{k}(x,y)|\leq\gamma

For this it is enough to have

\forall x,y\in S_{1},x\neq y,|L_{x,y}-\langle f_{x},f_{y}\rangle|\leq\frac{\gamma}{2}

(15)

\forall x\in S_{1},i\in\{1,\dots,k-1\},|D_{i}(x)-\mathbb{E}D_{i}(x)|\leq\frac{\gamma}{4\beta}

(16)

We first concern ourselves with achieving (
15
) with probability at least
1-\frac{\theta_{1}}{2}
. By union bound, it is enough to have (for arbitrary
x,y\in\mathcal{M}
)

\Pr[|L_{x,y}-\langle f_{x},f_{y}\rangle|>\frac{\gamma}{2}]<\frac{\theta_{1}}{2N_{1}^{2}}

(17)

For which it is enough to achieve (recall that
f_{x}(z)=\mathbb{E}d^{\prime}(x,z)
)

\Pr[|L_{x,y}-\frac{1}{N_{2}}\sum_{z\in S_{2}}f_{x}(z)f_{y}(z)|>\frac{\gamma}{4}]<\frac{\theta_{1}}{4N_{1}^{2}}

(18)

\Pr[|\frac{1}{N_{2}}\sum_{z\in S_{2}}f_{x}(z)f_{y}(z)-\langle f_{x},f_{y}\rangle|>\frac{\gamma}{4}]<\frac{\theta_{1}}{4N_{1}^{2}}

(19)

where it is worth noting that
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mu}(\mathbb{E}d^{\prime}(x,z)\mathbb{E}d^{\prime}(z,y))=\langle f_{x},f_{y}\rangle
. Using Proposition
3.4
for
t=\frac{N_{2}\gamma}{4}
and noting that
|\mathbb{E}d^{\prime}(x,y)|<L+C
and
\|d^{\prime}(x,z)\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq C_{3}
based on our assumptions, we have

\Pr[|L_{x,y}-\frac{1}{N_{2}}\sum_{z\in S_{2}}f_{x}(z)f_{y}(z)|>\frac{\gamma}{4}]\leq 5\exp\left(-\frac{cN_{2}\gamma^{2}}{256C_{3}^{2}(C_{3}^{2}+(L+C)^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\gamma)}\right)

Hence, the following is sufficient to achieve (
18
)

5\exp\left(-\frac{cN_{2}\gamma^{2}}{256C_{3}^{2}(C_{3}^{2}+(L+C)^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\gamma)}\right)<\frac{\theta_{1}}{4N_{1}^{2}}\iff\frac{256C_{3}^{2}(C_{3}^{2}+(L+C)^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\gamma)}{c\gamma^{2}}\log\frac{20N_{1}^{2}}{\theta_{1}}<N_{2}

Noting that
f_{x}(z)f_{y}(z)
varies between
L^{2}
and
(L+C)^{2}
for choices of
z\in\mathcal{M}
, Hoeffding’s inequality for bounded random variables (
Theorem

2.3
) gives

\Pr[|\frac{1}{N_{2}}\sum_{z\in S_{2}}f_{x}(z)f_{y}(z)-\langle f_{x},f_{y}\rangle|>\frac{\gamma}{4}]\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{N_{2}\gamma^{2}}{8((L+C)^{2}-L^{2})^{2}}\right)=2\exp\left(-\frac{N_{2}\gamma^{2}}{8C^{2}(2L+C)^{2}}\right)

Thus, in order to achieve (
19
) it is sufficient to have

2\exp\left(-\frac{N_{2}\gamma^{2}}{8C^{2}(2L+C)^{2}}\right)<\frac{\theta_{1}}{4N_{1}^{2}}\iff\frac{8C^{2}(2L+C)^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\log\frac{8N_{1}^{2}}{\theta_{1}}<N_{2}

Hence, in the case of small
\gamma
it is enough to have
N_{2}>A_{1}(C_{3}^{4}+(C_{3}^{2}+C^{2})(L+C)^{2})\gamma^{-2}\log\frac{A_{2}N_{1}^{2}}{\theta_{1}}
for absolute constants
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
. We now turn to obtaining (
16
). Assume that we have
\forall x\in S_{1},i\in\{1,\dots,k-2\},|D_{i}(x)-\mathbb{E}D_{i}(x)|\leq\frac{\gamma}{4\beta}
. We focus on achieving
\forall x\in S_{1},|D_{k-1}(x)-\mathbb{E}D_{k-1}(x)|\leq\frac{\gamma}{4\beta}
with probability at least
1-\frac{\theta_{1}}{2k_{max}}
. By union bound, it is enough to achieve (for arbitrary
x\in\mathcal{M}
)

\Pr[|D_{k-1}(x)-\mathbb{E}D_{k-1}(x)|>\frac{\gamma}{4\beta}]<\frac{\theta_{1}}{2N_{1}k_{max}}

(20)

Using Theorem
2.2
, we have

\Pr[|D_{k-1}(x)-\mathbb{E}D_{k-1}(x)|>\frac{\gamma}{4\beta}]<2\exp\left(-\frac{n\gamma^{2}}{32C_{3}^{2}\beta^{2}}\right)

Hence, achieving (
20
) comes down to

2\exp\left(-\frac{n\gamma^{2}}{32C_{3}^{2}\beta^{2}}\right)<\frac{\theta_{1}}{2N_{1}k_{max}}\iff\frac{32C_{3}\beta^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\log\frac{4N_{1}k_{max}}{\theta_{1}}<n

Thus, it is enough to have

for absolute constants

and

4.2
Obtaining a Cluster Center

Define
X_{k}^{*}=\arg\max_{x\in\mathcal{M}}g_{k}(x,x)
and
\hat{X}_{k}=\arg\max_{(x,y)\in S_{1}^{2},x\neq y}\tilde{g}_{k}(x,y)
. We want to ensure that any
(\hat{x}_{k},\hat{y}_{k})\in\hat{X}_{k}
has
\max_{z\in\mathcal{M}}g_{k}(z,z)-g_{k}(\hat{x}_{k},\hat{y}_{k})<4\gamma
. Suppose that
S_{1}
is an
\eta
-net of
\mathcal{M}
and select
x_{k}^{*}\in X_{k}^{*}
. Then there exists a pair
x,y\in S_{1}\cap B_{2\eta}(x_{k}^{*})
. Note that (for
x,y,z\in\mathcal{M}
)

\begin{split}\langle f_{z},f_{z}\rangle-\langle f_{x},f_{y}\rangle&=\langle f_{z},f_{z}\rangle-\langle f_{x},f_{z}\rangle+\langle f_{x},f_{z}\rangle-\langle f_{y},f_{z}\rangle+\langle f_{y},f_{z}\rangle-\langle f_{x},f_{y}\rangle\\
&=\langle f_{z},f_{z}-f_{x}\rangle+\langle f_{y},f_{z}-f_{x}\rangle+\langle f_{z},f_{x}-f_{y}\rangle\\
&\leq(L+C)C(2d(x,z)+d(x,y))\end{split}

(21)

and that
y\mapsto\min_{1\leq i\leq k-1}\mathbb{E}D_{i}(y)
is a C-Lipschitz function. These observations together tell us

\begin{split}g_{k}(x^{*}_{k},x^{*}_{k})-g_{k}(x,y)\leq&(L+C)C(2d(x,x^{*}_{k})+d(x,y))\\
&+C\beta(d(x,x^{*}_{k})+d(y,x^{*}_{k}))\\
\leq&(L+C)C(8\eta)+C\beta(4\eta)=\eta(8(L+C)C+4C\beta)\end{split}

(22)

In order to achieve
\max_{z\in\mathcal{M}}g_{k}(z,z)-g_{k}(x,y)<2\gamma
it is enough to have

\eta(8(L+C)C+4C\beta)<2\gamma\iff\eta<\frac{\gamma}{4(L+C)C+2C\beta}

Now we estimate
N_{1}
such that
S_{1}
is an
\eta
-net of
\mathcal{M}
with probability
\geq 1-\theta_{2}
. This can be done by
Proposition

2.5
but here’s another take which gives roughly the same answer. Take a maximal
\frac{\eta}{3}
-packing of
\mathcal{M}
. This set is a
\frac{2\eta}{3}

\mathcal{M}
(as otherwise you could add another ball to the packing). Hence, if you obtain at least one point in each ball, then you have a
\eta
-net of
\mathcal{M}

draws from

\frac{1}{\mathrm{VR}(\frac{\eta}{3})}\left(1-\mathrm{VR}(\frac{\eta}{3})\right)^{N_{1}}

where

is an upper bound on the number of balls and
\left(1-\mathrm{VR}(\frac{\eta}{3})\right)
is an upper bound on the probability that a given ball isn’t sampled on each independent draw. Hence, in order to bound this error probability by

, it is enough to have

N_{1}\geq\frac{\log\left(\theta_{2}\mathrm{VR}(\frac{\eta}{3})\right)}{\log\left(1-\mathrm{VR}(\frac{\eta}{3})\right)}

Now, let
(\hat{x}_{k},\hat{y}_{k})\in\arg\max_{x,y\in S_{1},x\neq y}\tilde{g}_{k}(x,y)
. Note that
\max_{z\in\mathcal{M}}g_{k}(z,z)-g(\hat{x}_{k},\hat{y}_{k})<4\gamma
because there exist
x,y\in S_{1}
satisfying
\max_{z\in\mathcal{M}}g_{k}(z,z)-g_{k}(x,y)<2\gamma
and the only pairs
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
that could have risen to be in
\arg\max_{(x,y)\in S_{1}^{2},x\neq y}\tilde{g}_{k}(x,y)
are those for which
\max_{z\in\mathcal{M}}g_{k}(z,z)-g(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})<4\gamma
. Now, observe that

\begin{split}\max_{z\in\mathcal{M}}g_{k}(z,z)-g_{k}(\hat{x}_{k},\hat{x}_{k})&\geq 0\\
\max_{z\in\mathcal{M}}g_{k}(z,z)-g_{k}(\hat{y}_{k},\hat{y}_{k})&\geq 0\end{split}

(23)

and also that

\displaystyle\max_{z\in\mathcal{M}}g_{k}(z,z)-g_{k}(\hat{x}_{k},\hat{y}_{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\max_{z\in\mathcal{M}}g_{k}(z,z)-g_{k}(\hat{x}_{k},\hat{x}_{k})\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left(\max_{z\in\mathcal{M}}g_{k}(z,z)-g_{k}(\hat{y}_{k},\hat{y}_{k})\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\|f_{\hat{x}_{k}}-f_{\hat{y}_{k}}\|_{2}^{2}<4\gamma

Hence we must have

.

For which it is enough to have

(L+C)C\delta+\beta C\delta<\gamma\iff\delta<\frac{\gamma}{C(L+C+\beta)}

∎

Before determining

Lemma 4.5

Let

Lemma 4.6
.

There exists a

4.6

From here we address how to obtain Assumption

Lemma 4.11

\mathcal{M}

We will now approximate the true distances between our cluster centers

\Pr(|A(\hat{x}_{i},\hat{x}_{j})-\mathbb{E}A(\hat{x}_{i},\hat{x}_{j})|>\omega)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{n^{2}\omega^{2}}{2C_{3}^{2}}\right)

for small enough
\varepsilon
(and hence small enough
\sigma
). We now show that we can use our cluster-cluster averages to produce Oracle

Lemma 4.13

For a pick of

5
Conclusion and Discussion

In this paper we develop several methods which allow us to recover the pairwise distances between points in a
\delta
-net of a diameter 1 geodesic probability space
(\mathcal{M},\mu,d)
satisfying certain key constraints when one only has access to noisy distances of a very general form. Further, in the case that
\mathcal{M}
is a d-manifold, this allow us to construct a Lipschitz approximation of
\mathcal{M}
by applying results from
[
11
]
. If
\mathcal{M}
is instead of finite diameter
\neq 1
, our methods allow us to approximate
\mathcal{M}
up to some scale. So for example, if
\diam\mathcal{M}=3
then our reconstructed pairwise distances will be those between the corresponding points on
\frac{1}{3}\mathcal{M}
. Modulo scaling, our methods achieve sample complexities and runtimes comparable to those in
[
13
]
.

5.1
Discussion of

1.1

We now show the setting of

1.1
. Recall that

Our starting point was the approach used in
[
13
]
to make clusters. Specifically
[
13

. Using properties intrinsic to the manifold,
[

showed that

, so it suffices to estimate

. If we have a good estimate for

, then for each

we can form a cluster
\mathcal{C}(x)
of close enough points to

and use this to estimate

for all pairs
(x,y)
(see Figure 1 in
[
13
]
). However, the method in
[
13
]
, which estimates
\int|\mathbb{E}d^{\prime}(y,x)-\mathbb{E}d^{\prime}(z,x)|^{2}\,d\mu(z)

\int\mathbb{E}|d^{\prime}(y,x)-d^{\prime}(z,x)|^{2}\,d\mu(z)
, only works
in the case of constant variance
. To estimate

, we develop a new estimator based on an argmax of inner products as in
Section

3.2.1

norm given inner product info has not been used before in our setting to the best of the authors’ knowledge and could lead to new algorithms for other problems.

Once we have estimated

, we can proceed as in the case of missing data of
[

]

\displaystyle k_{\Phi}(y,z)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{M}}|d(y,x)-d(z,x)|^{2}\Phi(y,x)\Phi(x,z)d\nu(x)

\displaystyle A_{\Phi}(y,z)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\Phi(y,x)\Phi(x,z)d\nu(x)

\displaystyle V_{\Phi}(y,z)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\psi(A_{\Phi}(y,x),k_{\Phi}(y,x))\Phi(z,x)d\nu(x)

\displaystyle W_{\Phi}(y,z)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\psi(A_{\Phi}(y,x),k_{\Phi}(y,x))\Phi(z,x)d\nu(x)

where

denotes the probability that

is not missing and

is a smooth approximation of the indicator function for a ball around

. The techniques developed in proving Theorem

produce approximations of integrals that are related to these (with the

replaced by

). The construction of the clusters

and

and

\frac{1}{|Y|}m(x,z)m(y,z)

in

Another special feature of our arguments is that we don’t work with integrals.
[
13
]
needed integrals and smooth functions, whereas we only need functions defined on the sample points (i.e. our

Thus, our method can be generalized to measure metric spaces that obey key assumptions characteristic of manifolds with bounded sectional curvature, but that are not quite manifolds.

We also point out that our manifold recovery method in the case of general
f(x,y)
is new; in particular, we hope the binary search paradigm in
Proposition

3.16
can find future applications in the realm of estimating distance ratios given a comparison oracle which tells you given two distances
d(x,y)
and

5.2
Discussion of
Theorem

4.1

In order to obtain well-spaced clusters in the proof of Theorem
4.1
, we make use of a regularized optimization that reflects the tradeoff between maximizing
\langle f_{x},f_{y}\rangle
and staying away from existing clusters, which is manifest in the
\min_{1\leq i\leq k-1}D_{i}(x)
term. A more intuitive approach to avoiding existing clusters would be to exclude all points in
S_{1}
within a
\tau
-ball of an existing cluster from our argmax. Although we don’t have access to metric balls around a given
\hat{x}_{i}
, we can use
\tilde{B}_{i}=\{D_{i}(x)<\tau^{\prime}\}
in place of
B(\hat{x}_{i},\tau)
for some threshold
\tau^{\prime}
. However, this approach runs into the issue that our selected argmax might lie on or close to the boundary of
\mathcal{M}\setminus\cup_{1\leq i\leq k-1}\tilde{B}_{i}
(where it is worth noting that
\mathcal{M}
has no boundary, but this choice of optimization restriction creates one). In this case, we might be able to increase our objective function by moving into
\cup_{1\leq i\leq k-1}\tilde{B}_{i}
, undermining our arguments. We could circumvent this issue by only adding points to the cluster that are also outside of the
\tilde{B}_{i}
. However, the
\tilde{B}_{i}
are not metric balls. On account of
D_{i}(x)
being the average noisy distance to points that are highly concentrated in the ball
B(\hat{x},5\sigma)
but which can have highly varying distributions within this ball, the set
\tilde{B}_{i}
macroscopically looks like a metric ball, but can have “swells” of order
\sigma
on its boundary. If
\hat{x}_{k}
is chosen to be near the boundary of a
\tilde{B}_{i}
, it is difficult to lower bound
\vol B(\hat{x}_{i},\epsilon)\setminus\cup_{1\leq i\leq k-1}\tilde{B}_{i}
and hence ensure that we obtain cluster points.

It’s also worth noting that, given the form of our
g_{k}(x,y)
, we need to take
\beta=O(\varepsilon^{-1})
for the analysis to work out, which forces
\delta=O(\varepsilon^{-3d})
instead of
O(\varepsilon^{-2d})
, propagating to a higher sample complexity and runtime. This begs the question if a different form of regularization could circumvent this issue.

5.3
Recovering the Manifold Scale

We now return to the topic of scaling and ask: can we guess the diameter? With certain a priori information about scale this is possible. For example, if we have access to the true distance
d(x,y)
for a one pair of points in our
\delta
-net then the true diameter can be approximated by
\frac{d(x,y)}{\tilde{d}(x,y)}
, where
\tilde{d}(x,y)
denotes our approximation under the assumption
\diam\mathcal{M}=1
. This is reminiscent of Erastothenes’ method for measuring the circumference of the Earth by measuring the angle of the sun’s rays in two cities at the same time of day, hence obtaining the angle between them along the surface of the Earth. This could be compared to the known distance between them on foot. In Erastothenes’ case he was modelling the Earth as a sphere, whereas in our case we can use our scale-invariant approximation of
\mathcal{M}
as a model. In either case, we’re using the shape of our space to determine its scale.

5.4
More Open Questions

1.

What are the optimal sample complexity and runtime we can achieve? A theoretical lower bound on complexity is
\varepsilon^{-d}
, the size of an
\varepsilon
-net on the sphere.

2.

Can we work with partial observations of the manifold, a potential large-noise version of
[
12
]
?

3.

Besides manifolds, what spaces are amenable to reconstruction from noisy distances using an extension of the framework in this paper?

4.

Can we extend the framework to work adaptively? A possible idea is to figure out coarse clusters, and use coarse cluster information to deduce finer clusters.
