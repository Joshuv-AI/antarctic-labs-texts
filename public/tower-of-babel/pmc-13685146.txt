Fetal cardiac interventions versus expectant management in congenital heart disease: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Peng Ning, Junhui Liu, Shuting Lin, Chenyue Zhao, Zhixian Ji, Silin Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo evaluate biventricular circulation (BVC), live birth, and postnatal mortality after fetal cardiac intervention (FCI) compared with expectant management, and to examine outcomes according to technical success of FAV.MethodsThis systematic review and meta-analysis followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library were searched from database inception through 31 December 2025 for observational FCI studiehis systematic review and meta-analysis followed PRISs. The primary analysis was limited to studies with an internal expectant-management comparator; eligible non-comparative FAV studies were considered only for the secondary technical-success analysis. Random-effects meta-analyses were performed in Stata, and heterogeneity was assessed using I².ResultsEleven comparative reports representing 11 independent study populations were included in the primary FCI-versus-expectant-management synthesis, comprising 775 fetuses (418 interventions and 357 controls). Overall FCI estimates included both technically successful and unsuccessful procedures. Among cohorts with available live-birth data, live birth occurred in 271/315 (86.0%) FCI cases and 287/319 (90.0%) controls, with no significant difference between groups (OR 0.680, 95% CI 0.350–1.320; p = 0.256). Postnatal death among live-born infants with available postnatal outcome data occurred in 53/271 (19.6%) in the FCI group and 94/287 (32.8%) in the expectant-management group and was lower after FCI (OR 0.457, 95% CI 0.280–0.746; p = 0.002). For FAV, postnatal death among live-born infants was lower (OR 0.419, 95% CI 0.216–0.813; p = 0.010), whereas BVC showed a non-significant trend (OR 2.260, 95% CI 0.970–5.290; p = 0.060). Fetal pulmonary valvuloplasty (FPV) showed no significant association with BVC (OR 2.430, 95% CI 0.880–6.760; p = 0.088) or postnatal death among live-born infants (OR 0.432, 95% CI 0.098–1.909; p = 0.268). Technically successful FAV was associated with higher odds of BVC and live birth compared with unsuccessful FAV. Fetal atrial septal intervention (FASI) analyses included live birth and postnatal death among live-born infants only. No statistically significant difference was found for live birth (OR 0.480, 95% CI 0.110–2.060; p = 0.325) or postnatal death among live-born infants (OR 0.433, 95% CI 0.127–1.482; p = 0.183).ConclusionsFAV was associated with lower odds of postnatal death among live-born infants and showed a non-significant trend toward improved BVC, while technically successful FAV was associated with higher odds of BVC and live birth than unsuccessful FAV. In contrast, comparative evidence for FPV and FASI remains sparse, and these findings should be interpreted as exploratory.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12884-026-09703-z.Keywords: Fetal cardiac intervention, Biventricular circulation, Fetal aortic valvuloplasty, Gestational and postnatal mortality

Introduction

Congenital heart disease (CHD) affects approximately 9.4 per 1,000 live births and remains a leading cause of neonatal morbidity and mortality [1]. Prenatal diagnosis enables early detection and delivery planning; however, for severe lesions, expectant management does not actively modify the natural history and disease progression may continue in utero [2]. Fetal cardiac interventions (FCI) are minimally invasive in utero procedures aimed at modifying disease progression and preserving cardiac function. The most commonly performed FCI procedures include fetal aortic valvuloplasty (FAV) for critical aortic stenosis, fetal atrial septal intervention (FASI) for hypoplastic left heart syndrome with a restrictive or intact atrial septum (HLHS-RAS/HLHS-IAS) [3–7], and fetal pulmonary valvuloplasty (FPV) for pulmonary atresia with an intact ventricular septum or critical pulmonary stenosis (PAIVS/PS).

Since the first successful percutaneous FAV reported by Maxwell et al. [8] in 1991, advances in technique, imaging, and patient selection have broadened the use of FCI [9, 10]. FAV may increase the likelihood of achieving a biventricular circulation (BVC) in selected fetuses, but survival benefits remain uncertain, and the procedure carries a risk of serious complications, including fetal demise [11]. Similar uncertainties exist for FASI and FPV in their respective indications [3, 4, 12].

Previous meta-analyses frequently relied on single-arm studies without an internal control group or on non–arm-based comparisons, in which outcomes for FCI and expectant management were extracted from different studies [3, 13–15]. Such approaches are inherently vulnerable to confounding and cannot adequately account for differences in baseline disease severity, case selection, and centre-specific practice patterns.

To address these gaps, we conducted a primary meta-analysis restricted to cohorts in which FCI and expectant management were compared within the same study population. A separate exploratory technical-success analysis additionally included FAV cohorts with extractable successful-versus-unsuccessful outcomes. Outcomes were pooled across independent cohorts to obtain more precise comparative estimates. To our knowledge, this is the first meta-analysis restricted to within-study comparisons of FCI and expectant management and the first quantitative synthesis of FAV outcomes according to technical success. This study aimed to clarify the association of FCI with key gestational and postnatal outcomes to inform clinical counselling and procedural decision-making.

Methods

This meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA guidelines and the recommendations outlined in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions. The PROSPERO registration number is CRD420251236069. The PRISMA checklist was followed in this review.

Eligibility criteria

We included observational studies that evaluated prenatal fetal cardiac intervention in fetuses with congenital heart disease. Eligible interventions included fetal aortic valvuloplasty, fetal pulmonary valvuloplasty, and fetal atrial septal intervention, including fetal atrial septoplasty and atrial septal stent placement. Studies were required to report at least one of the predefined outcomes: biventricular circulation, live birth, or postnatal death. Studies were eligible for the primary FCI-versus-expectant-management synthesis if they included an internal expectant-management comparator group. Studies without an expectant-management comparator were excluded from the primary comparative synthesis. However, for the exploratory secondary technical-success analysis, FAV reports without an expectant-management comparator were eligible when outcomes were reported separately for technically successful and unsuccessful procedures. One overlapping updated report meeting this criterion was retained for this analysis and did not add an independent study population. When multiple reports described overlapping study populations, reports were selected on an analysis-specific basis, and overlapping reports were not included simultaneously in the same pooled comparison.

We excluded case reports, conference abstracts, retracted publications, and studies in which outcome data for the relevant fetal cardiac condition could not be extracted separately. Overlapping reports that did not contribute unique analysis-specific data were excluded. When an overlapping report provided eligible data for a distinct secondary analysis, it was retained as an included report only for that analysis, did not add an independent study population, and was not pooled simultaneously with the overlapping report.

Information sources

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library from database inception to 31 December 2025. Two reviewers performed the search independently. To ensure completeness, the reference lists of all included studies and of relevant systematic reviews and meta-analyses were also screened manually. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus.

Search strategy

The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH), Emtree terms, and free-text keywords. No publication-date or language restrictions were applied. Reviews, editorials, comments, and letters were excluded. The following terms were used:

(fetal OR fetus) AND (cardiac OR heart) AND (intervention OR procedure OR surgery OR therapy OR “intrauterine intervention” OR “in utero intervention” OR “fetal cardiac intervention” OR FCI) AND (“aortic valvuloplasty” OR “pulmonary valvuloplasty” OR “atrial septoplasty” OR “balloon valvuloplasty” OR “fetal aortic valvuloplasty” OR “fetal pulmonary valvuloplasty” OR FAV OR FPV OR FASI) NOT (review OR editorial OR comment OR letter).

The detailed search strategies for each database, including subject headings (e.g., MeSH and Emtree), are provided in Appendix S1.

Definitions of clinical outcomes and procedural stratification

Clinical outcomes were defined prior to analysis to ensure consistency across studies. Technical success was defined according to each original publication; however, in general, it reflected successful balloon inflation across the target valve with improvement in antegrade flow. For FAV, this typically corresponded to balloon inflation across the aortic valve with demonstrable enhancement of antegrade aortic flow. BVC was defined as a postnatal circulation supported by separate functional left and right ventricles. When multiple time points were reported, outcomes at hospital discharge were prioritized. If discharge data were unavailable, the outcome as reported in the study was used. When the timing of BVC assessment was not specified, the reported value was accepted as final. Live birth was defined as an infant alive at the time of delivery. Postnatal death referred to mortality occurring between birth and hospital discharge.

For analyses comparing procedural outcomes, studies reporting FCI were analyzed overall and additionally stratified by procedure type (FAV, FPV, or FASI). For FAV, pairwise subgroup comparisons were conducted between (i) technical success vs. expectant management, (ii) technical failure vs. expectant management, and (iii) technical success vs. technical failure, where data were available. For comparative meta-analyses, postnatal death was analyzed using the number of live-born infants with known postnatal vital status in each study arm as the denominator. Fetuses that did not result in live birth were excluded from the postnatal-death risk set. Outcome availability was assessed separately for each study arm and endpoint. Studies or study arms lacking extractable comparative data for a given outcome were excluded only from that endpoint-specific meta-analysis. Study selection and data extraction

Study selection and data extraction were performed independently by two authors using the Rayyan web application. Titles and abstracts of all retrieved records were screened initially, and any study considered potentially relevant by either reviewer proceeded to full-text assessment. Full texts were then reviewed to determine eligibility according to the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Any discrepancies between reviewers were resolved by consultation with a third author.

For all studies meeting eligibility criteria, data were extracted independently and in duplicate, including study design, population characteristics, type of fetal cardiac intervention, comparator group, and reported clinical outcomes. Extracted outcomes included established measures of gestational and postnatal prognosis relevant to fetal cardiac intervention. Consensus was reached through discussion when differences occurred, ensuring accuracy and completeness of the final dataset. When necessary, authors were contacted to clarify ambiguous reporting or obtain missing information. Cases that did not report outcome data (e.g., BVC, live birth, or postnatal death) were excluded from analyses of the corresponding endpoint.

Assessment of risk of bias

Risk of bias was assessed independently by two reviewers using the ROBINS-I tool for non-randomized studies of interventions, covering bias due to confounding, selection of participants, classification of interventions, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of the reported result. Each domain was judged as low, medium, serious, or critical risk of bias. Overall risk of bias was determined by the highest risk rating across domains (worst-domain approach) in accordance with ROBINS-I guidance. For reporting, overall judgements were collapsed into three categories: low (ROBINS-I low), medium (ROBINS-I medium), and high (ROBINS-I serious or critical). Discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus.

Statistical analysis

Demographic and baseline characteristics were summarized descriptively. For postnatal-death analyses, event counts and denominators were extracted exclusively among live-born infants with known postnatal vital status. For each outcome, event counts were extracted for the intervention and comparator groups, and ORs with 95% CIs were pooled using random-effects (REML) models. Live birth and postnatal death among live-born infants were analyzed for overall FCI and by procedure type (FAV, FPV, and FASI) versus expectant management. BVC was analyzed separately for FAV and FPV and was not pooled across procedure types because these procedures address distinct cardiac pathologies and patient populations. Additional pairwise comparisons were performed among technically successful, technically failed, and expectant-management groups for FAV. Overall FCI analyses included all attempted interventions regardless of technical success or failure; technical-success analyses were performed separately for FAV when data were available. A continuity correction of 0.5 was applied when a study contained one or more zero cells. Heterogeneity was quantified using I², and results were presented in forest plots. All analyses were conducted in Stata/MP 18.0. For overall FCI outcomes reported by at least 10 cohorts, small-study effects were assessed visually using funnel plots and statistically using Egger’s regression test; p < 0.05 was considered evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry.

Results

Study selection

After full-text assessment, 72 reports were excluded. Twelve reports were included in the review, representing 11 independent study populations. Eleven comparative reports were included in the primary comparative quantitative synthesis, comprising 775 fetuses, including 418 interventions and 357 controls (Fig. 1) [4, 5, 7, 12, 16–22]. Friedman et al. [23], an overlapping report, contributed only to the secondary successful-versus-unsuccessful FAV analyses and did not add an independent study population.

General study characteristics

Twelve reports representing 11 independent study populations were included overall: 11 comparative reports contributed to the primary FCI-versus-expectant-management synthesis, and Friedman et al. [23], an updated overlapping report from the Boston FAV cohort, contributed only to the secondary successful-versus-unsuccessful FAV analysis and did not add an independent study population. The primary comparative dataset comprised 775 fetuses, with sample sizes ranging from 10 to 214 (Table 1). The largest cohort was reported by Kovacevic et al. [17] (n = 214), followed by Tulzer et al. [20] (n = 141); the remaining cohorts enrolled between 10 and 84 subjects. Of the 775 fetuses, 418 underwent FCI and 357 received expectant management. Procedure-specific intervention groups included 275 FAV, 67 FPV, and 76 FASI cases. BVC was analyzed separately for FAV and FPV cohorts with available data and was not pooled across procedure types; BVC was not analyzed for FASI. Live birth in the FCI group ranged from 4/4 to 88/103, compared with 3/5 to 25/25 in the control group. Postnatal death among live-born infants in the FCI group ranged from 0/9 to 20/40, compared with 0/15 to 20/24 in the control group.

Of the 775 fetuses included in the primary comparative dataset, 634 fetuses from 10 cohorts contributed to the live-birth meta-analysis. The 141 fetuses not included were all from Tulzer et al. [20] (103 FAV cases and 38 controls), because live-birth and postnatal-death data were unavailable for the control arm; consequently, the entire cohort could not contribute to the paired comparative meta-analyses of these endpoints. Among the 634 fetuses with available live-birth data, 558 were live-born and had known postnatal vital status, including 271 infants in the FCI group and 287 infants in the expectant-management group; these infants formed the denominator for the postnatal-death meta-analysis. The remaining 76 pregnancies did not result in live birth and were therefore not part of the postnatal-death risk set. Thus, the smaller postnatal-death denominator reflects the corrected restriction of the risk set to live-born infants rather than additional postnatal loss to follow-up. Meta-analysis of fetal cardiac intervention outcomes

Effect of fetal cardiac intervention versus expectant management

Because FAV and FPV address distinct cardiac pathologies and patient populations, BVC was not pooled across procedure types. Procedure-specific BVC results are reported in Sect. 4.1.1 and 4.1.2. Neither FAV nor FPV demonstrated a statistically significant improvement in BVC compared with expectant management, although both point estimates were directionally favorable. No significant difference was observed for live birth (10 cohorts; OR 0.680, 95% CI 0.350–1.320; I² = 6.950%; p = 0.256). FCI was associated with lower odds of postnatal death among live-born infants (10 cohorts; OR 0.457, 95% CI 0.280–0.746; I² = 1.840%; p = 0.002) (Table 2). Forest plots for the overall FCI outcomes are shown in Fig. 3A, B.

Effect of fetal aortic valvuloplasty versus expectant management

Six FAV cohorts comprising 557 fetuses were eligible for this subgroup analysis; endpoint-specific meta-analyses included five cohorts because not all cohorts reported each outcome. FAV was associated with a directionally higher likelihood of biventricular circulation compared with expectant management, although the difference did not reach statistical significance (OR 2.260, 95% CI 0.970–5.290; p = 0.060). No significant difference was detected for live birth (OR 0.920, 95% CI 0.450–1.880; p = 0.823). Postnatal death among live-born infants was lower in the FAV group (OR 0.419, 95% CI 0.216–0.813; I² = 0.000%; p = 0.010) (Table 2; Fig. 4A-C).

Forest plots for fetal aortic valvuloplasty versus expectant management: A postnatal biventricular circulation, B live birth, and (C) postnatal death among live-born infants

Effect of fetal pulmonary valvuloplasty versus expectant management

Two FPV cohorts, Hogan and Tworetzky [12, 22], were included in the FPV analysis, comprising 94 fetuses (Table 2). Given the small number of cohorts, the FPV findings should be interpreted cautiously. FPV showed no statistically significant differences from expectant management for BVC, live birth, or postnatal death among live-born infants. For BVC, the pooled OR was 2.430 (95% CI 0.880–6.760; I² = 0.000%; p = 0.088). Live birth also did not differ significantly between FPV and expectant management (OR 0.200, 95% CI 0.020–1.810; p = 0.154). For postnatal death among live-born infants, the pooled OR was 0.432 (95% CI 0.098–1.909; I² = 0.000%; p = 0.268). Detailed forest plots for these outcomes are provided in Figure S1.

Effect of fetal atrial septal intervention versus expectant management

Three FASI cohorts, Chaturvedi, Jantzen, and Yilmaz Furtun [4, 5, 7], were included in the FASI analysis, comprising 124 fetuses. FASI was analyzed only for live birth and postnatal death among live-born infants; BVC was not analyzed for FASI because it was not an applicable endpoint. Neither live birth (OR 0.480, 95% CI 0.110–2.060; p = 0.325) nor postnatal death among live-born infants (OR 0.433, 95% CI 0.127–1.482; I² = 42.970%; p = 0.183) differed significantly between FASI and expectant management (Table 2). Detailed forest plots for these outcomes are provided in Figure S2.

Outcomes according to technical success of fetal aortic valvuloplasty

This exploratory secondary analysis evaluated FAV cohorts with available technical-success stratification. Comparisons of successful or unsuccessful FAV with expectant management were restricted to cohorts with an expectant-management comparator, whereas successful-versus-unsuccessful comparisons additionally included FAV cohorts without an expectant-management arm when successful/unsuccessful-stratified outcomes were extractable. Biventricular circulation

Among FAV cases, technical success was associated with higher odds of biventricular circulation compared with unsuccessful FAV (3 reports; OR 5.695, 95% CI 2.037–15.922; I² = 0.000%; p = 0.001) (Table 3). The comparison of successful FAV versus expectant management was directionally favorable but attenuated, whereas unsuccessful FAV versus expectant management showed no significant improvement in BVC. Forest plots for these analyses are shown in Figure S3(A-C).

Live birth

Across the three comparisons, live-birth outcomes differed by procedural success (Table 3). Successful FAV was associated with higher live-birth odds than unsuccessful FAV (3 reports; OR 3.694, 95% CI 1.034–13.196; I² = 57.860%; p = 0.044). Comparisons of successful FAV versus expectant management and unsuccessful FAV versus expectant management were not statistically significant. Forest plots for these analyses are shown in Figure S4(A-C).

Postnatal death among live-born infants

For postnatal death among live-born infants, neither successful FAV versus expectant management (2 cohorts; OR 0.463, 95% CI 0.185–1.158; I² = 0.000%; p = 0.100) nor successful versus unsuccessful FAV (2 cohorts; OR 0.464, 95% CI 0.069–3.111; I² = 0.000%; p = 0.429) was statistically significant (Table 3). The unsuccessful FAV versus expectant-management comparison was based on one study (OR 1.412, 95% CI 0.155–12.875), so no pooled P value was estimated. The apparent difference in statistical significance between the overall FAV analysis and the successful-FAV analysis reflects differences in study availability and precision rather than opposing effect estimates. The overall FAV analysis included five cohorts and yielded an OR of 0.419 (95% CI 0.216–0.813), whereas the successful-FAV analysis included only two cohorts and yielded a similar point estimate (OR 0.463) but a wider 95% CI (0.185–1.158). Therefore, the available data do not indicate that unsuccessful procedures account for the lower postnatal mortality observed in the overall FAV analysis. These technical-success mortality comparisons remain underpowered and should be interpreted cautiously. Forest plots are presented in Figure S5(A-C).

Publication bias assessment

For overall FCI analyses, funnel plots were generated for live birth and postnatal death among live-born infants, each including 10 cohorts. Egger’s regression test did not indicate significant small-study effects for postnatal death among live-born infants (intercept = -0.830; p = 0.316) or live birth (intercept = -0.890; p = 0.236) (Figures S6 and S7). Publication bias was not assessed for BVC or procedure-specific analyses because fewer than 10 cohorts were available.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated FCI in severe CHD across 12 reports representing 11 independent study populations: 11 comparative reports with internal intervention and expectant-management cohorts and one overlapping updated FAV report used only in the secondary technical-success analysis. Procedure-specific analyses did not demonstrate statistically significant improvements in BVC for either FAV or FPV, although both estimates were directionally favorable. BVC was not pooled across FAV and FPV because these procedures address distinct cardiac pathologies and patient populations. Overall FCI was associated with lower postnatal mortality among live-born infants without a significant difference in live birth. The most consistent signals were observed for lower postnatal death among live-born infants after FAV and for improved BVC and live birth after technically successful FAV, whereas FPV and FASI remained less certain because of small numbers and endpoint-specific limitations.

BVC is widely recognized as an indicator of favorable long-term prognosis, and individual studies have reported post-FAV BVC rates ranging from 40% to 74% [11, 16, 17, 20, 24–31]. Prior meta-analyses compared intervention and control groups drawn from different studies rather than using within-study comparators and did not find a significant difference between FAV and expectant management [13]. In the present analysis, FAV showed a directionally higher likelihood of BVC, although this estimate did not reach conventional statistical significance. FAV did not significantly affect live birth rates, while postnatal death among live-born infants was lower in the intervention group. These findings suggest that FAV may be associated with lower early postnatal mortality among infants who were born alive, without evidence of improved live birth. This finding pertains specifically to mortality after live birth and should not be interpreted as evidence of improved overall fetal or perinatal survival; the magnitude of the BVC effect also remains sensitive to cohort selection.

In our analysis, successful FAV was associated with higher odds of BVC and live birth compared with unsuccessful FAV. However, the mortality findings require separate interpretation. Overall FAV versus expectant management was based on five cohorts, whereas successful FAV versus expectant management was based on only two cohorts. Although the latter did not reach statistical significance, its point estimate was similar to that of the overall FAV analysis (OR 0.463 vs. 0.419), but its confidence interval was substantially wider. This pattern is most consistent with reduced sample size and statistical precision rather than evidence that unsuccessful procedures contributed to the observed mortality association. Moreover, the unsuccessful-FAV versus expectant-management estimate was derived from a single study and was highly imprecise. Accordingly, mortality should not be interpreted as differing by technical success on the basis of the available data. For BVC and live birth, the findings nevertheless support the importance of careful candidate selection, meticulous pre-procedural planning, standardized intra-procedural technique, and performance in experienced centers.

Previous studies have suggested that FPV can improve BVC and promote ventricular growth, potentially offering benefits for selected patients with right-sided obstructive lesions [12, 15, 32–34]. In the present primary analysis, FPV was represented by only Hogan 2020 and Tworetzky 2009. No statistically significant differences were observed for BVC, live birth, or postnatal death among live-born infants. These findings should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of no benefit; rather, they indicate that the current internally controlled comparative evidence is sparse and likely sensitive to patient selection, fetal condition, procedural success, and postnatal management [34, 35]. Further prospective studies and standardized selection criteria are needed to identify which fetuses may derive benefit from FPV.

A previous meta-analysis of 746 fetuses with HLHS-RAS/HLHS-IAS reported that FASI reduced postnatal atrial septal restriction compared with expectant management, but did not show significant improvement in major survival outcomes, including live birth, neonatal mortality, or survival to discharge [3]. In the present analysis, FASI was evaluated using three internally controlled cohorts and was limited to live birth and postnatal death among live-born infants, because BVC was not an applicable endpoint for atrial septal intervention [4, 5, 7]. The pooled estimates did not show statistically significant differences in either live birth or postnatal death among live-born infants, although the direction of the postnatal-death estimate was favorable. These findings should not be interpreted as evidence of no benefit, because FASI cohorts primarily involved fetuses with HLHS and intact or restrictive atrial septum, or related left-sided obstructive lesions with left atrial hypertension, and outcomes are likely influenced by technical success, fetal lung disease, and candidate selection [4–7]. However, fetal pulmonary lymphangiectasia, MRI-defined nutmeg lung, pulmonary venous Doppler findings, and the severity of atrial-level restriction were not uniformly reported across studies; therefore, severity-based subgroup analyses could not be performed, and the FASI findings should be interpreted as exploratory.

A strength of this review is the use of within-study comparator groups, which may reduce confounding arising from differences between independently assembled intervention and control cohorts. To our knowledge, this is the first meta-analysis restricted to within-study comparisons of FCI and expectant management and the first quantitative synthesis of FAV outcomes according to technical success.

This review has several limitations. First, there is a clear lack of randomized controlled trials, and all included studies were observational in nature, which inherently increases the risk of bias. Second, post-intervention and postnatal outcomes may be affected by operator experience, fetal selection criteria, procedural strategies, and perinatal management practices across centers. Third, the FPV and FASI subgroup analyses included few cohorts, and the resulting conclusions should be considered exploratory. Fourth, technical-success-stratified postnatal mortality data were available from only two cohorts for the successful-FAV versus expectant-management comparison, resulting in limited statistical precision and precluding firm conclusions regarding mortality according to technical success. Fifth, one FAV cohort did not provide extractable comparative live-birth or postnatal-death data for both study arms and therefore did not contribute to these endpoint-specific meta-analyses. Although this represented study-level outcome unavailability rather than patient-level attrition within the analyzed cohorts, selective outcome reporting may still introduce availability bias.

Conclusion

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, FAV was associated with lower odds of postnatal death among live-born infants and showed a non-significant trend toward improved biventricular circulation. Technically successful FAV was associated with higher odds of biventricular circulation and live birth than unsuccessful FAV, whereas postnatal death among live-born infants did not differ significantly according to technical success. Evidence for FPV and FASI remains limited, and neither subgroup demonstrated consistent statistically significant improvements across the analyzed endpoints. Observed outcomes may vary according to patient selection, procedural success, and center expertise. Future research should focus on standardized selection criteria, optimized procedural timing and techniques, and prospective multicenter datasets with comparable outcome reporting.
