Abstract

We point out that, although SMEFT is constructed to be model-independent, this holds only if complete sets of operators are used. SMEFT analyses that focus on subsets of operators are only semi-model-independent. They probe classes of ultraviolet models, opening a window to the underlying new physics. This model dependence originates from the need to choose a weak basis, usually taken to be the down or up basis. This naturally leads to the introduction of a third SMEFT weak basis, the generic basis, which interpolates between the above two bases. Due to its generality, we argue that the generic weak basis is the most natural for new physics. In the down and up weak bases, only a specific product of the two transformation matrices from the weak to the mass basis is observable – the CKM matrix. However, in the generic weak basis, both transformation matrices are measurable in principle, possibly up to some phases.

Introduction —
Although the standard model (SM) of particle physics has been
enormously successful in describing the physics up to energy scales of

O({\rm TeV})
, it is not complete: it cannot explain a number of
observations (e.g., neutrino masses, dark matter, the baryon asymmetry
of the universe, etc.). We therefore conclude that there must be
physics beyond the SM.

The LHC has searched for new particles for many years, but none have
been observed. This implies that this new physics (NP), whatever it
is, must be heavy (or extremely weakly coupled). One approach to analyzing such NP is to build
models. Another approach uses model-independent effective
field theories (EFTs). When the NP is integrated out, one obtains the Standard Model EFT,
SMEFT
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
.
This EFT obeys the SM gauge symmetry,
SU(3)_{C}\times SU(2)_{L}\times U(1)_{Y}
and contains only SM particles. The leading-order (dimension-4) terms in SMEFT are those of the SM; higher-order terms are
suppressed by powers of the NP scale
\Lambda
.

As SMEFT is valid above the weak scale, its operators are defined
in the weak basis. That is, they are written as functions of the
fermions
q_{i}^{0}
(LH quark doublets),
\ell_{i}^{0}
(LH lepton doublets),

u_{i}^{0}
,
d_{i}^{0}
and
e_{i}^{0}
(all three RH singlets),
i=1,2,3
. (Here, LH
and RH denote left- and right-handed, and the superscript 0 indicates the weak basis.)

The
[U(3)]^{5}
“symmetry” —
It is often said that SMEFT possesses a
[U(3)]^{5}
“symmetry,” where
one
U(3)
describes transformations of the three generations of
q_{i}^{0}

among themselves, and the other
U(3)
s act similarly on
\ell_{i}^{0}
,
u_{i}^{0}
,

d_{i}^{0}
and
e_{i}^{0}

[
4

not

is transformed into a number of other operators, producing a new
Hamiltonian with the same operators as the original one, but in which
the new coefficients are linear combinations of the old coefficients.
The new Hamiltonian is therefore
equivalent
to the first
Hamiltonian.

To see this explicitly, consider the semileptonic SMEFT operator,

{\cal O}^{(1)}_{\ell q}=C^{(1)}_{\ell q}|_{prst}\,(\bar{\ell}_{p}^{0}\gamma_{\mu}\ell_{r}^{0})\,(\bar{q}_{s}^{0}\gamma^{\mu}q_{t}^{0})~.

(1)

To simplify things, we focus only on transformations of the quark fields. For a given
(p,r)
, the Hamiltonian is

{\cal H}=\sum_{s,t}C^{(1)}_{\ell q}|_{prst}\,(\bar{\ell}_{p}^{0}\gamma_{\mu}\ell_{r}^{0})\,(\bar{q}_{s}^{0}\gamma^{\mu}q_{t}^{0})~.

(2)

This corresponds to nine operators (
s,t=1,2,3
). We now perform a
U(3)
transformation on the LH quark fields:

q^{\prime 0}=S_{L}^{\dagger}\,q^{0}~.

(3)

The Hamiltonian becomes

\displaystyle{\cal H}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s,t}C^{(1)}_{\ell q}|_{prst}\,(\bar{\ell}_{p}^{0}\gamma_{\mu}\ell_{r}^{0})\,(\bar{q}_{i}^{\prime 0}\,(S_{L}^{\dagger})_{is}\,\gamma^{\mu}\,(S_{L})_{tj}\,q_{j}^{\prime 0})

(4)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}{\tilde{C}}^{(1)}_{\ell q}|_{prij}\,(\bar{\ell}_{p}^{0}\gamma_{\mu}\ell_{r}^{0})\,(\bar{q}_{i}^{\prime 0}\gamma^{\mu}q_{j}^{\prime 0})~,

where

{\tilde{C}}^{(1)}_{\ell q}|_{prij}=(S_{L}^{\dagger})_{is}\,C^{(1)}_{\ell q}|_{prst}\,(S_{L})_{tj}~.

(5)

We see that, after the transformation, the new Hamiltonian has the same operators as before the transformation, but the new coefficients are linear combinations of the old coefficients. This is what is meant when people refer to a
[U(3)]^{5}
“symmetry.”
We stress that this holds only if all nine SMEFT operators, i.e., all possible values of the weak (generation) indices
(s,t)
or
(i,j)
, are involved in the transformations.

The down and up bases —
As noted above, the dimension-4 operators in SMEFT are those of
the SM. Higher-order, non-SM operators can provide new contributions
to low-energy processes. Measurements of low-energy observables can
therefore give information about the coefficients of these SMEFT
operators. Below the weak scale, the physics is described by the
Weak Effective Theory (WET), obtained by further integrating out the SM
particles heavier than the
b
quark. The WET operators obey the

SU(3)_{C}\times U(1)_{em}
gauge symmetry. In order to get information
about the SMEFT operators from WET observables, it is necessary to
match SMEFT to WET.

One complication is that, while the SMEFT operators are defined in the
weak basis, the WET operators are in the mass basis. It is therefore
necessary to transform between the two bases. For quarks, this is done
as follows. The (leading-order) Yukawa terms in SMEFT are as in the SM:

{\bar{q}}_{i}^{0}\lambda^{u}_{ij}i\sigma_{2}Hu_{j}^{0}+{\bar{q}}_{i}^{0}\lambda^{d}_{ij}Hd_{j}^{0}~.

(6)

When the Higgs gets
a vacuum expectation value
v
, breaking the
SU(2)_{L}\times U(1)_{Y}
symmetry to
U(1)_{em}
, this leads to the up- and down-quark mass matrices
M_{u}\equiv\lambda^{u}v
and
M_{d}\equiv\lambda^{d}v
. Each of these is diagonalized by
a bi-unitary transformation. For
M_{u}
, we have
(S_{L}^{u})^{\dagger}M_{u}S_{R}^{u}=M_{u}^{diag}
. This implies that
U_{L}^{0}=S_{L}^{u}U_{L}
and

U_{R}^{0}=S_{R}^{u}U_{R}
, where
U\equiv(u,c,t)^{T}
and the absence of
superscripts indicates the mass basis. Similarly,
(S_{L}^{d})^{\dagger}M_{d}S_{R}^{d}=M_{d}^{diag}
, so that
D_{L}^{0}=S_{L}^{d}D_{L}
and
D_{R}^{0}=S_{R}^{d}D_{R}
, where
D\equiv(d,s,b)^{T}
. The Cabibbo-Kobayashi-Maskawa (CKM)
matrix is
V=(S_{L}^{u})^{\dagger}S_{L}^{d}
. The diagonalization of both mass matrices can only be done once the
electroweak symmetry is broken.

Above the weak scale, where SMEFT
is valid, we can use the
U(3)
transformations to diagonalize either

\lambda^{d}
or
\lambda^{u}
. We begin with a “generic” SMEFT
Hamiltonian in which neither of the Yukawa coupling matrices is diagonal. We
can use one
U(3)
symmetry to transform
U_{R}^{0}=S_{R}^{u}U_{R}
and
another for
D_{R}^{0}=S_{R}^{d}D_{R}
. The RH quarks in the SMEFT
Hamiltonian are now in the mass basis. At the SMEFT level, we can now
use another
U(3)
to transform
q^{0}\to S_{L}^{d}q^{0}
. This implies that
the down-quark Yukawa term
{\bar{q}}_{i}^{0}\lambda^{d}_{ij}Hd_{j}^{0}\to{\bar{q}}_{i}^{0}(S_{L}^{d})^{\dagger}_{ik}\lambda_{kl}^{d}(S_{R}^{d})_{lj}Hd_{j}={\bar{q}}_{i}^{0}\lambda_{ij}^{d,diag}Hd_{j}
. That is, the down-quark Yukawa coupling matrix
has been diagonalized. The upshot is that the LH down-quark weak
eigenstates are now the same as the mass eigenstates. In summary, we
have

q^{0}\to S_{L}^{d}q^{0}~,~\begin{pmatrix}u_{L}^{0}\\
d_{L}^{0}\end{pmatrix}\to\begin{pmatrix}V^{\dagger}\,u_{L}\\
d_{L}\end{pmatrix}~.

(7)

On the left we show the
U(3)
transformation applied to
q^{0}
and on
the right we show how the weak eigenstates
u^{0}_{L}
and
d^{0}_{L}
are
converted to the mass eigenstates
u_{L}
and
d_{L}
after symmetry
breaking.

Alternatively, we can transform
q^{0}\to S_{L}^{u}q
. This diagonalizes the
up-quark Yukawa coupling matrix, so that the LH up-quark weak eigenstates
are the same as the mass eigenstates. In this case, we have

q^{0}\to S_{L}^{u}q^{0}~,~\begin{pmatrix}u_{L}^{0}\\
d_{L}^{0}\end{pmatrix}\to\begin{pmatrix}u_{L}\\
V\,d_{L}\end{pmatrix}~.

(8)

independent of the convention. However, as we will see, the

7

Both of these bases are quite useful for matching SMEFT to WET,
because there is no dependence on the (unknown) transformation

– only the (known) CKM matrix is involved

Note, however, that

.
In these bases, there
are therefore no longer any unknown parameters coming from the

Subsets of SMEFT operators —
Above, we showed that, for a given SMEFT operator, the
[U(3)]^{5}
“symmetry” holds only if the Hamiltonian includes operators with all the values of the weak (generation) indices. However, it is often assumed that only a subset of such operators is present. In this case, things change substantially.

Consider again the SMEFT operator
{\cal O}^{(1)}_{\ell q}
of Eq. (
1
), and
suppose now that the generation indices take only the values
prst=2222
and
2233
, i.e.,
not all nine quark operators are present (and the leptonic indices are fixed). We are interested in the
matching of these
{\cal O}^{(1)}_{\ell q}
operators to WET. We have already described how
the quarks transform between the weak and mass bases. For the leptons,
we can again use one
U(3)
symmetry to transform
e_{R}^{0}=S_{R}^{e}e_{R}
,
so that the RH charged leptons in the SMEFT Hamiltonian are now in the
mass basis. For the LH leptons, if we neglect neutrino masses, we
can also take the LH charged-lepton fields in the weak basis to be the
same as in the mass basis.

In the down basis (Eq.
7
), the WET operators involve only two down-quark transitions, but nine up-quark transitions. For the two SMEFT operators, this gives

\sum_{k=2,3}C^{(1)}_{\ell q}|_{22kk}\left[\bar{\mu}_{L}\gamma_{\mu}\mu_{L}+\bar{\nu}_{\mu L}\gamma_{\mu}\nu_{\mu L}\right]\left[\bar{d}_{kL}\gamma^{\mu}d_{kL}+\bar{u}_{i,L}V_{ik}\gamma^{\mu}V^{\dagger}_{kj}u_{j,L}\right]~.

(9)

However, in the up basis (Eq.
8
), we have the opposite situation: the WET operators involve nine down-quark transitions, but only two up-quark transitions:

\sum_{k=2,3}C^{(1)}_{\ell q}|_{22kk}\left[\bar{\mu}_{L}\gamma_{\mu}\mu_{L}+\bar{\nu}_{\mu L}\gamma_{\mu}\nu_{\mu L}\right]\left[\bar{d}_{i,L}V^{\dagger}_{ik}\gamma^{\mu}V_{kj}d_{j,L}+\bar{u}_{kL}\gamma^{\mu}u_{kL}\right]~.

(10)

This shows that the predictions in the two bases are very different.

This should not come as a surprise. The down and up bases are defined by applying a
U(3)
transformation, either
S_{L}^{d}
or
S_{L}^{u}
, to
q^{0}
. But the new bases produced in this way are equivalent only if all the SMEFT operators are present. Here this is not the case, so the two bases are not equivalent from the beginning.

Let us be very clear about what we are saying: although the Hamiltonian with all nine SMEFT operators in the down basis is equivalent to the Hamiltonian with all nine SMEFT operators in the up basis, the Hamiltonian with only two SMEFT operators in the down basis is
not
equivalent to the Hamiltonian with only the
same
two SMEFT operators in the up basis. The point here is that, if one is analyzing a scenario in which only a subset of SMEFT operators is being used, one has to also specify the basis for the analysis. (This statement can be found elsewhere in the literature, see e.g., Refs.
[
10
,
11
]
.)

Semi-model-independent analyses —
There is another way to look at this. By construction, SMEFT is meant to be model-independent. But this holds only if full sets of operators are included. Once one considers only a subset of operators, this immediately raises the question: what forced the other operators to vanish? The answer is simple: although we are using the language of SMEFT, this is really an assumption about the underlying NP model. In the present case, the idea is that, when the NP is integrated out, the only
{\cal O}^{(1)}_{\ell q}
operators produced have indices 2222 and 2233 (though other SMEFT operators may also be present).

But this is not all. If one chooses to do the analysis in the down basis, one has also assumed that the down-quark Yukawa coupling matrix is diagonal when the NP is integrated out. A similar observation applies if the up basis is used.

The point is that, whenever an analysis uses only a subset of SMEFT operators – and most “SMEFT” analyses are of this type – it is effectively a model-like analysis. That is, the focus is really on that class of models that, when the NP is integrated out, (i) produces the chosen subset of SMEFT operators, and (ii) is naturally in the chosen (down or up) basis. Thus, even if the SMEFT language is used, the analysis is only semi-model-independent. In other words, there can be some model dependence in SMEFT.

Producing a subset of operators —
Here is an example of a NP model that produces the desired subset of
{\cal O}^{(1)}_{\ell q}
operators. The SM gauge symmetry is extended by a
U(1)^{\prime}
symmetry at the TeV scale, in which the
Z^{\prime}
gauge boson couples only to LH SM fermions. In the weak basis, its couplings are given by

-\mathcal{L}_{Z^{\prime}}\supset g^{\prime}\left[C_{\ell}^{i}\bar{\ell}_{i}^{0}\gamma^{\mu}\ell_{i}^{0}+C_{q}^{j}\bar{q}_{j}^{0}\gamma^{\mu}q_{j}^{0}\right]Z^{\prime}_{\mu}~,

(11)

where
C_{\ell}^{i}
and
C_{q}^{j}
are respectively the
U(1)^{\prime}
charges for the
i^{th}
and
j^{th}
generations of leptons and quarks. When the
Z^{\prime}
is integrated out at scale
\Lambda=M_{Z^{\prime}}
, three types of four-fermion operators are generated at tree level, those with four leptons, four quarks, and two leptons and two quarks:

-\frac{g^{{}^{\prime}2}}{2M_{Z^{\prime}}^{2}}\left[C_{\ell}^{i}C_{\ell}^{j}(\bar{\ell}_{i}^{0}\gamma^{\mu}\ell_{i}^{0})(\bar{\ell}_{j}^{0}\gamma_{\mu}\ell_{j}^{0})+C_{q}^{i}C_{q}^{j}(\bar{q}_{i}^{0}\gamma^{\mu}q_{i}^{0})(\bar{q}_{j}^{0}\gamma_{\mu}q_{j}^{0})+2C_{\ell}^{i}C_{q}^{j}(\bar{\ell}_{i}^{0}\gamma^{\mu}\ell_{i}^{0})(\bar{q}_{j}^{0}\gamma_{\mu}q_{j}^{0})\right]~.

(12)

The key point is that this defines the weak basis. Unlike in SMEFT, here the flavour indices have a physical meaning, i.e., these fermions are the eigenstates of the NP. If the
U(1)^{\prime}
charges are different for all three generations, then this basis is unique. We suppose that only
C_{\ell}^{2}
,
C_{q}^{2}
and
C_{q}^{3}
are nonzero, and that
C_{q}^{2}\neq C_{q}^{3}
, so that the only
{\cal O}^{(1)}_{\ell q}
operators produced have indices 2222 and 2233.

The generic basis —
Although this model produces the desired subset of
{\cal O}^{(1)}_{\ell q}
operators when the NP is integrated out, note that neither of the down-quark and up-quark Yukawa couplings is diagonal. Thus, these two SMEFT operators are defined in a new basis, different from the down or the up basis. We call this the “generic” basis.

Above, we argued that, whenever an analysis includes only a subset of SMEFT operators, it is necessary to also specify the basis for the analysis. Furthermore, these are fundamentally model-like analyses, in which the focus is on that class of models that (i) produces the chosen subset of SMEFT operators, and (ii) is naturally in the chosen basis. In the literature, almost all such analyses are done in the down or up basis. However, there are a great many (most?) models that are in neither the down nor up basis when the NP is integrated out. If one wants to explore the possible predictions when only a subset of SMEFT operators is present, it is necessary to also consider the generic basis.

In the generic basis,

\displaystyle q^{0}=\begin{pmatrix}u_{L}^{0}\\
d_{L}^{0}\end{pmatrix}

(13)

and the matching to WET is done via

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}u_{L}^{0}\\
d_{L}^{0}\end{pmatrix}\to\begin{pmatrix}S_{L}^{u}\,u_{L}\\
S_{L}^{d}\,d_{L}\end{pmatrix}~.

(14)

Given that only the
{\cal O}^{(1)}_{\ell q}
operators with indices 2222 and 2233 are present,
what is predicted for the WET operators? These operators involve nine down-quark transitions and nine up-quark transitions:

\sum_{k=2,3}C^{(1)}_{\ell q}|_{22kk}\left[\bar{\mu}_{L}\gamma_{\mu}\mu_{L}+\bar{\nu}_{\mu L}\gamma_{\mu}\nu_{\mu L}\right]\left[\bar{d}_{i,L}(S_{L}^{d})^{\dagger}_{ik}\gamma^{\mu}(S_{L}^{d})_{kj}d_{j,L}+\bar{u}_{i,L}(S_{L}^{u})^{\dagger}_{ik}\gamma^{\mu}(S_{L}^{u})_{kj}u_{j,L}\right]~.

(15)

This is like a combination of the down and up bases, except that
the coefficients are not the same. Here they involve elements of the
transformation matrices
S_{L}^{d}
and
S_{L}^{u}
, rather than the CKM matrix.

Extracting
S_{L}^{d}
and
S_{L}^{u}
—
This is potentially of great importance. Suppose that the coefficients of all these WET operators could be extracted
from measurements of low-energy observables. (Of course, this is very difficult in practice, but we are interested in what information is available in principle.)

Consider first those operators involving the down-quark transitions. Writing
C^{(1)}_{\ell q}|_{22kk}=(C^{(1)}_{q})_{kk}
for notational simplicity, the measurements of the coefficients of these WET operators provide the elements of the matrix
(S_{L}^{d})^{\dagger}C^{(1)}_{q}S_{L}^{d}
. With this, one can (numerically) find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of this matrix. Since
C^{(1)}_{q}
is Hermitian, the eigenvectors can be chosen (up to phases) to form an orthonormal basis. But the eigenvalues are the same as the eigenvalues of the original coupling matrix
C^{(1)}_{q}
. And if the eigenvectors of
C^{(1)}_{q}
are
W_{i}^{(a)}
with
a=1,2,3
, the eigenvectors of
(S_{L}^{d})^{\dagger}C^{(1)}_{q}S_{L}^{d}
are
W_{i}^{d(a)}=((S_{L}^{d})^{\dagger})_{ij}W_{j}^{(a)}
. Given that
C^{(1)}_{q}={\rm diag}(0,C_{22},C_{33})
, with
C_{22}\neq C_{33}
, we can easily compute its eigenvalues and eigenvectors (modulo phases). The relation between the eigenvectors
W_{i}^{d(a)}
and
W_{i}^{(a)}
then fixes
(S_{L}^{d})^{\dagger}
up to the three phases associated with the phase freedom in the definition of
W_{i}^{d(a)}
.

A similar exercise can be performed on the operators involving the up-quark transitions. This fixes
(S_{L}^{u})^{\dagger}
, again up to three phases. Finally, we use the relation
V=(S_{L}^{u})^{\dagger}S_{L}^{d}
. As the CKM matrix is known, this gives additional constraints on the six free phases, fixing three of them. Putting all this information together, we can obtain both
S_{L}^{d}
and
S_{L}^{u}
, up to three phases.
8
8

8

Considering more sets of SMEFT operators can determine both transformation matrices completely.

The bottom line is that, in this example, the transformation matrices

S_{L}^{d}
and
S_{L}^{u}
can in principle be extracted from the data! This
is in contrast to the SM, where these matrices are unmeasurable. In
the charged current, they only show up in the definition of the CKM matrix. And they cancel in the neutral current, so that
there are no tree-level flavour-changing neutral currents (FCNCs). This is
because all three generations of quarks have the same charges under

SU(2)_{L}\times U(1)_{Y}
. On the other hand, in SMEFT, the
couplings involving different generations can be different, and indeed
SMEFT allows FCNCs. So it should not come as a surprise that

S_{L}^{d}
and
S_{L}^{u}
are potentially measurable in SMEFT.

The above analysis was “top-down.” That is, we first defined the NP model at scale
\Lambda
and then worked out the low-energy consequences. But it is also possible to reverse the steps and perform a “bottom-up” analysis. The starting point is the measurement of the elements of the matrix
(S_{L}^{d})^{\dagger}C^{(1)}_{q}S_{L}^{d}
from the WET operators involving the down-quark transitions. We can again obtain the eigenvalues of this matrix, which are the same as the eigenvalues of the original coupling matrix
C^{(1)}_{q}
. With only the eigenvalues, we cannot say what
C^{(1)}_{q}
is. But this knowledge does allow us to say what it is
not
: any NP model that does not produce a
C^{(1)}_{q}
with these eigenvalues is ruled out.

Summary —
To recap, it is generally taken for granted that SMEFT analyses are model-independent. In this paper, we have shown that this is not necessarily true. In SMEFT operators involving fermions, the fermions have weak (generation) indices. If a given analysis involves full sets of such operators, i.e., the indices take all possible values, then the analysis is indeed model-independent. However, if only a subset of operators is used, the analysis is only semi-model-independent.

There are two issues here. First, the scenario is basis-dependent: the Hamiltonian with a subset of operators in one weak basis is not equivalent to the same Hamiltonian in another weak basis. In other words, it is necessary to specify the weak basis in which the analysis is done. Most analyses use either the down or up bases, in which the down-quark or up-quark Yukawa couplings are taken to be diagonal. Second, if only a subset of operators is used, this raises the question: what forced the other operators to vanish?

The point is that, whenever only a subset of operators is used, one is necessarily making an assumption about the underlying NP. To be specific, in the above scenario it is assumed that, when the NP particles are integrated out, the Hamiltonian is produced in a particular weak basis, and that only the desired subset of operators is generated. Note that this does not point to any one model of NP, but rather to a class of NP models.

Furthermore, when the NP is integrated out, it will generally not produce a Hamiltonian in the down or up weak basis, i.e., the down-quark or up-quark Yukawa couplings will not be diagonal. Instead, the operators will be defined in a new weak basis, the generic basis, in which neither of the Yukawa couplings is diagonal.

The transformation from the weak to the mass basis typically involves two transformation matrices involving the LH down and up quarks. In the down and up bases, only a specific combination of these matrices (the CKM matrix) appears. However, in the generic basis, both matrices appear and are measurable in principle, possibly up to some phases.
