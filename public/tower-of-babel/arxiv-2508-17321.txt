Invariant λ \lambda -translators for the Gauss curvature flow in Euclidean space
Muhittin Evren Aydin, Rafael López

Abstract
. A λ \lambda -translator is a surface in Euclidean space ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} whose Gauss curvature K K satisfies K = ⟨ N , v → ⟩ + λ K=\langle N,\vec{v}\rangle+\lambda , where N N is the Gauss map, v → \vec{v} is a fixed direction, and λ ∈ ℝ \lambda\in\mathbb{R} . In this paper, we classify all λ \lambda -translators that are invariant by a one-parameter group of translations and a one-parameter group of rotations.



1991 Mathematics Subject Classification
53A05, 53C21, 53C42

1.
Introduction

Let
\vec{v}
be a unit vector in Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. A surface
\Sigma
immersed in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is said to be a
\lambda
-translator
for the Gauss curvature flow if its Gauss curvature
K
satisfies

(1)

K=\langle N,\vec{v}\rangle+\lambda,\quad\vec{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{3},\quad|\vec{v}|=1,

where
N
is the unit normal vector of
\Sigma
. The vector
\vec{v}
is called the
speed
of the flow. For simplicity, we will refer to such surfaces as
\lambda
-translators. A
\lambda
-translator with
\lambda=0
is called a translator.
When
\lambda=0
, these translators correspond to solitons of the flow by Gauss curvature. In this situation, the surface
\Sigma
evolves under the Gauss curvature flow purely by translation in a fixed direction
\vec{v}
. That is, the family of surfaces
\{\Sigma+t\vec{v}:t\in\mathbb{R}\}
satisfies the property that, at each fixed
t
, the normal component of the velocity vector
\vec{v}
at each point is equal to the Gauss curvature at that point.

The study of the flow by Gauss curvature goes back to the work of Firey to model the shape of tumbling stones
[
14
]
. The generalization in higher dimensions is due to Tso
[
19
]
. See also
[
1
,
2
,
8
,
10
,
20
]
. Later, this was generalized to the flow by the powers of the Gauss curvature as discussed in
[
7
,
21
,
22
]
, and further improved in
[
3
,
4
,
6
,
8
,
9
,
11
,
15
]
.

Equation (
1
) is invariant by rigid motions of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. If the orientation on
\Sigma
is reversed, then
\Sigma
becomes a
\lambda
-translator with speed
-\vec{v}
. Hence, fixed the speed
\vec{v}
, the sign of
\lambda
cannot be prescribed. Examples of
\lambda
-translators are the following:

(1)

Planes. Given
\vec{v}
, any plane is a
\lambda
-translator with speed
\vec{v}
if
\lambda=-\langle N,\vec{v}\rangle
.

(2)

Circular cylinders whose rotation axis is parallel to
\vec{v}
. Here
\lambda=0
because
\langle N,\vec{v}\rangle=0
and
K=0
.

(3)

Rotational cones. If
\Sigma
is a rotational cone whose rotation axis is parallel to
\vec{v}
, and since
K=0
, then
\Sigma
is a
\lambda
-translator, where
\lambda=-\cos\theta
, and
\theta
is the inner angle of the cone.

In this paper we will investigate
\lambda
-translators that are invariant by two types of one-parameter groups of rigid motions of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
: the group of translations determined by a vector
\vec{w}
, and the group of rotations determined by a fixed axis
\vec{w}
. The objective of this paper is the classification of all
\lambda
-translators within these two classes of surfaces. Notice that in both of the above cases, there is not a
priori
relationship between the vector
\vec{w}
and the speed
\vec{v}
in the definition of a
\lambda
-translator. However, in either case, we will show that
\vec{w}
must be parallel to
\vec{v}
(Thm.
2.1
and Prop.
3.1
).

On the one hand, a cylindrical surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is defined as a surface invariant by a one-parameter group of translations. In Thm.
2.1
, we provide a complete classification of cylindrical
\lambda
-translators. On the other hand, cylindrical surfaces belong to two well-known classes of surfaces: ruled surfaces and translation surfaces. In Sect.
2
, we also study
\lambda
-translators of ruled and translation types. We classify in Thm.
2.2
the non-cylindrical ruled
\lambda
-translator showing that the Gauss curvature satisfies
K=0
and that the ruled surface is a tangent surface whose base curve is a general helix. Translation
\lambda
-translators are classified in Thm
2.4
proving that they must be cylindrical surfaces.

In Sects.
3
and
4
, we study
\lambda
-translators that are invariant by a one-parameter group of rotations, providing a complete classification in Thms.
3.3
,
4.1
, and
4.4
. As an immediate observation in Sect.
3
, and as illustrated by the above examples of
\lambda
-translators, we prove in Prop.
3.2
that all rotational surfaces generated by straight lines are
\lambda
-translators. Among the examples in our classification, and depending on the range of
\lambda
, Thms.
3.3
and
4.1
establish the existence of rotational
\lambda
-translators that intersect the rotation axis orthogonally. The construction of convex entire graphs is also included in Thm.
4.1
. In contrast, as a consequence of Thm.
4.1
, we observe the non-existence of closed rotational
\lambda
-translators.
For closed
\lambda
-translators, we prove in Thm.
4.2
that
\lambda\geq 0
and the genus is
0
(
\lambda>0
) or
1
(
\lambda=0
).

2.
Cylindrical
\lambda
-translators

Cylindrical surfaces belong to a wider class of surfaces called ruled surfaces. A ruled surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is constructed by moving a straight-line along a given curve. The straight-line and the curve are called the ruling and the base curve of the surface, respectively. If the rulings are all parallel straight-lines, then the ruled surface becomes cylindrical. A parametrization of a ruled surface
\Sigma
is

(2)

\Psi(s,t)=\gamma(s)+tw(s),\quad s\in I\subset\mathbb{R},t\in\mathbb{R},

where
\gamma\colon I\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
\gamma=\gamma(s)
, is the base curve parametrized by arc-length and

w(s)
is a unit vector field along
\gamma
which indicates the direction of rulings of
\Sigma
.

First, we consider the cylindrical
\lambda
-translators. In this case, the vector field
w(s)
in the parametrization (
2
) is a fixed vector
\vec{w}
. Then
\Sigma
is invariant by the group of translations defined by
\vec{w}
and the base curve
\gamma
can be chosen to lie in a plane orthogonal to
\vec{w}
. The following result classifies cylindrical
\lambda
-translators.

Theorem 2.1
.

Cylindrical
\lambda
-translators with speed
\vec{v}
are either planes orthogonal to
\vec{v}
(
\lambda=\pm 1
) or translators whose rulings are parallel to
\vec{v}
.

Proof.

Let
\vec{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a unit vector,
|\vec{v}|=1
. Any plane orthogonal to
\vec{v}
is a
(\pm 1)
-translator, meaning it satisfies Eq. (
1
) with
\lambda=\pm 1
. This proves the first part of the theorem.

Suppose now that
\Sigma
is a non-planar cylindrical surface and we will prove that
\lambda=0
. In such a case, we will also establish that the rulings are parallel to
\vec{v}
. By contradiction, suppose
\lambda\not=0
. The surface
\Sigma
is given by
\Sigma=\{\gamma(s)+t\vec{w}:t\in\mathbb{R},s\in I\}
, where
\gamma\colon I\subset\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
is parametrized by arc-length and lies in a plane orthogonal to
\vec{w}
. Since
K=0
for any cylindrical surface, Eq. (
1
) can be written as

(3)

\langle{\bf n}(s),\vec{v}\rangle+\lambda=0,

for all
s\in I
, where
{\bf n}
is the unit normal vector of
\gamma
as planar curve. Differentiating (
3
) with respect to
s
, we have

\kappa(s)\langle\gamma^{\prime}(s),\vec{v}\rangle=0
for all
s\in I
. Since
\Sigma
is non-planar,
\gamma
is not a straight-line, and therefore we have
\kappa\not=0
. We then deduce
\langle\gamma^{\prime}(s),\vec{v}\rangle=0
for all
s\in I
; that is,
\gamma
lies in a plane orthogonal to
\vec{v}
. This proves that
\vec{v}
and
\vec{w}
are parallel. As a consequence
{\bf n}(s)
is orthogonal to
\vec{v}
and Eq. (
3
) implies
\lambda=0
.

∎

We now classify the non-cylindrical ruled
\lambda
-translators. In the parametrization (
2
) we can assume that
w^{\prime}(s)\neq 0
on
I
. Moreover, we can choose the base curve
\gamma
to be the striction line which implies that
\langle\gamma^{\prime}(s),w^{\prime}(s)\rangle=0
, for every
s\in I
. The classification is the following.

Theorem 2.2
.

The only non-cylindrical ruled
\lambda
-translators with speed
\vec{v}
are the tangent surfaces whose base curves are general helices with axis
\vec{v}
. Moreover, the Gauss curvature satisfies
K=0
,
\lambda\neq 0
, and
\lambda\in[-1,1]
.

Proof.

Let
\Sigma
be a ruled surface which satisfies Eq. (
1
), and assume that
\Sigma
is non-cylindrical. Then,
\Sigma
admits the parametrization given by (
2
), with the condition
\langle\gamma^{\prime}(s),w^{\prime}(s)\rangle=0
, for every
s\in I
. We set

\alpha=\frac{(\gamma^{\prime},w,w^{\prime})}{|w^{\prime}|^{2}},

where
(\gamma^{\prime},w,w^{\prime})
is the determinant of the vectors
\gamma^{\prime}
,
w
, and
w^{\prime}
. A direct computation yields

K=-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{(\alpha^{2}+t^{2})^{2}},\quad N=\frac{\alpha w^{\prime}+tw^{\prime}\times w}{|w^{\prime}|\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+t^{2}}}.

We consider two separate cases:

(1)

Case
\alpha=0
. In this case
\gamma^{\prime}
is parallel to
w
, because
w^{\prime}
is orthogonal to both
\gamma^{\prime}
and
w
. Hence,
\Sigma
is a tangent surface. Let
{\bf n}
and
{\bf b}
denote the principal normal and binormal vector fields along
\gamma
, respectively. Then,
N=-{\bf b}
and Eq. (
1
) reduces to
\langle{\bf b},\vec{v}\rangle=\lambda
, where
|\lambda|\leq 1
. If
\lambda=0
, then
\Sigma
would be a plane, which contradicts our assumption. Differentiating both sides with respect to
s
, we obtain
\tau\langle{\bf n},\vec{v}\rangle=0
, where
\tau
is the torsion of
\gamma
. Note that
\tau\neq 0
, because otherwise
\Sigma
would again be planar. Consequently,
\langle{\bf n},\vec{v}\rangle=0
, which means that
\gamma
is a general helix with axis
\vec{v}
.

(2)

Case
\alpha\neq 0
. According to
[
3
, Theorem 6]
, a ruled surface with
K\neq 0
does not satisfy Eq. (
1
) when
\lambda=0
. Hence, by assuming
\lambda\neq 0
, we will arrive at a contradiction. Then, Eq. (
1
) can be written as

-\frac{\alpha^{2}+\lambda(\alpha^{2}+t^{2})^{2}}{(\alpha^{2}+t^{2})^{2}}=\frac{\alpha\langle w^{\prime},\vec{v}\rangle+t(w^{\prime},w,\vec{v})}{|w^{\prime}|\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+t^{2}}}.

By squaring both sides of this equation and passing to one side, we obtain

\sum_{n=0}^{8}A_{n}(s)t^{n}=0,

where
A_{n}(s)
are smooth functions on the variable
s
. Looking this equation as a polynomial equation on the variable
t
, we deduce that the coefficients
A_{n}
must all vanish. A computation of
A_{8}
gives

\lambda^{2}-\frac{(w^{\prime},w,\vec{v})^{2}}{|w^{\prime}|^{2}}=0.

Since
\lambda\neq 0
, it follows that
(w^{\prime},w,\vec{v})\neq 0
. Using
A_{7}=0
, we obtain

2\alpha\frac{\langle w^{\prime},\vec{v}\rangle(w^{\prime},w,\vec{v})}{|w^{\prime}|^{2}}=0.

Thus we deduce
\langle w^{\prime},\vec{v}\rangle=0
. Finally, from
A_{2}=0
and
A_{4}=0
we derive

\lambda\alpha^{4}(4+3\lambda\alpha^{2})=0,\quad\lambda\alpha^{2}(2+3\lambda\alpha^{2})=0,

which leads to a contradiction.

∎

Remark 2.3
.

It was proved in Prop. 2.4 that non-cylindrical ruled
\lambda
-translators have zero Gauss curvature. In this case, Eq. (
1
) becomes
\langle N,\vec{v}\rangle=-\lambda
. Therefore, the angle between the unit normal vector of the surface and the direction
\vec{v}
is constant. This implies that the surface belongs to the class of constant angle surfaces, a topic that has been extensively studied in differential geometry (see, for example,
[
5
,
12
,
13
,
18
]
). Recently, it was shown in
[
17
]
that a constant angle surface is the tangent surface on a general helix, which aligns with Prop. 2.4.

The second family of
\lambda
-translators we study consists of surfaces that can be expressed in separable variables in the form
z=f(x)+g(y)
, where
f:I\subset\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
and
g:J\subset\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
are smooth functions. A parametrization of the surface is

\Psi(x,y)=(x,y,f(x)+g(y))

and the surface is called a
translation surface
. Notice that the surface is obtained by the translation of the curve
x\mapsto(x,0,f(x))
along the curve
y\mapsto(0,y,g(y))
and vice-versa. In the following result, we classify all
\lambda
-translators that are of translation type. We point out that translation surfaces are graphs
z=f(x)+g(y)
on the
xy
-plane and there is no a
priori
relation between the
xy
-plane and the speed
\vec{v}
in the definition of a
\lambda
-translator.

Theorem 2.4
.

Cylindrical surfaces are the only
\lambda
-translators of the form
z=f(x)+g(y)
.

Proof.

Let
\Sigma
be a
\lambda
-translator of the form
z=f(x)+g(y)
. With respect to the
(x,y,z)
coordinates, let
\vec{v}=(v_{1},v_{2},v_{3})
be the speed of flow. If
\lambda=0
, the result was proved in
[
3
, Theorem 5]
.

We now consider the case
\lambda\not=0
. Assume, by contradiction, that
\Sigma
is not a cylindrical surface. Then there exists
(x_{0},y_{0})\in I\times J
such that
f^{\prime\prime}(x_{0})\not=0
and
g^{\prime\prime}(y_{0})\not=0
. Therefore, we can assume
f^{\prime\prime}g^{\prime\prime}\not=0
on a subset
\tilde{I}\times\tilde{J}
, where
\widetilde{I}
and
\widetilde{J}
are subintervals of
I
and
J
containing
x_{0}
and
y_{0}
, respectively. We restrict our study to
\tilde{I}\times\tilde{J}
. Eq. (
1
) can be expressed by

(4)

f^{\prime\prime}g^{\prime\prime}W_{1}^{-2}=W_{1}^{-1/2}W_{2}+\lambda,

where
W_{1}=1+f^{\prime 2}+g^{\prime 2}
and
W_{2}=-v_{1}f^{\prime}-v_{2}g^{\prime}+v_{3}
. We multiply both sides of Eq. (
4
) by
W_{1}^{2}/g^{\prime\prime}
, obtaining

f^{\prime\prime}=\frac{1}{g^{\prime\prime}}(W_{1}^{3/2}W_{2}+\lambda W_{1}^{2}).

Differentiating with respect to
y
,

(5)

0=-\frac{g^{\prime\prime\prime}}{g^{\prime\prime 2}}(W_{1}^{3/2}W_{2}+\lambda W_{1}^{2})+\frac{1}{g^{\prime\prime}}(3g^{\prime}g^{\prime\prime}W_{1}^{1/2}W_{2}-v_{2}g^{\prime\prime}W_{1}^{3/2}+4g^{\prime}g^{\prime\prime}\lambda W_{1}).

This equation can be written in the form
A+B\sqrt{W_{1}}=0
, where
A
and
B
are functions of
g,g^{\prime},g^{\prime\prime},W_{1},
and
W_{2}
. The identity
B^{2}W_{1}-A^{2}=0
is a polynomial in
f^{\prime}
of degree
8
, namely,

(6)

\sum_{n=0}^{8}C_{n}(y)f^{\prime}(x)^{n}=0.

Therefore, all coefficients
C_{n}
must vanish. By the computations of
C_{6},C_{7}
, and
C_{8}
, we obtain

\displaystyle C_{6}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle v_{2}^{2}-2\frac{g^{\prime}g^{\prime\prime\prime}}{g^{\prime\prime 2}}(3v_{1}^{2}-4\lambda^{2}),

\displaystyle C_{7}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2v_{1}v_{2}\frac{g^{\prime\prime\prime}}{g^{\prime\prime 2}},

\displaystyle C_{8}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{g^{\prime\prime\prime}}{g^{\prime\prime 2}}\right)^{2}(v_{1}^{2}-\lambda^{2})

We distinguish two cases.

(1)

Case
g^{\prime\prime\prime}\not=0
at some point
y_{0}
. We work around a subinterval
\widetilde{J}
around
y_{0}
. Then, from
C_{8}=0
, we get
v_{1}^{2}=\lambda^{2}
; in particular this implies
v_{1}\neq 0
. Hence,
C_{7}=0
yields
v_{2}=0
. Substituting this in
C_{6}=0
leads to the contradiction

2\lambda^{2}\frac{g^{\prime}g^{\prime\prime\prime}}{g^{\prime\prime 2}}=0.

(2)

Case
g^{\prime\prime\prime}=0
identically. From
C_{6}=0
we have
v_{2}=0
. Thus, for
5\leq n\leq 8
, the coefficients
C_{n}
vanish trivially. Moreover,
C_{4}=g^{\prime 2}(9v_{1}^{2}-16\lambda^{2})
, which implies
v_{1}\neq 0
; otherwise, from
C_{4}=0
, we would conclude
\lambda=0
. From the identity
C_{3}=18g^{\prime 2}v_{1}v_{3}
, it follows
v_{3}=0
. Finally, we arrive at the contradiction
C_{0}=-16\lambda^{2}(1+g^{\prime 2})^{2}=0
.

∎

3.
Rotational
\lambda
-translators

In the next two sections, we will study the
\lambda
-translators of rotational type, obtaining geometric properties of these surfaces. In this section, we study the relationship between the rotation axis and the speed
\vec{v}
of the
\lambda
-translator and we also consider the problem of existence of rotational
\lambda
-translators intersecting orthogonally the rotation axis.

The first result proves that the rotation axis and
\vec{v}
are parallel.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\Sigma
be a rotational surface. If
\Sigma
is a
\lambda
-translator, then
\Sigma
is a plane or its rotation axis is parallel to the speed
\vec{v}
.

Proof.

After a rigid motion of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, we can assume that the rotation axis
L
coincides with the
z
-axis. Let
\gamma\colon I\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
be the generating curve of
\Sigma
which we assume to be parametrized by arc-length,
\gamma(s)=(x(s),0,z(s))
,
s\in I
,
x(s)>0
. The surface
\Sigma
can be parametrized by

(7)

\Psi(s,t)=(x(s)\cos t,x(s)\sin t,z(s)),\quad s\in I,t\in\mathbb{R}.

The unit normal vector of
\Sigma
is
N=(-z^{\prime}\cos t,-z^{\prime}\sin t,x^{\prime})
, and the Gauss curvature is
K=\frac{z^{\prime}}{x}\kappa
, where
\kappa
is the curvature of
\gamma
. If the coordinates of the speed
\vec{v}
with respect to
(x,y,z)
are
\vec{v}=(v_{1},v_{2},v_{3})
, then Eq. (
1
) becomes

-v_{1}z^{\prime}(s)\cos t-v_{2}z^{\prime}(s)\sin t+v_{3}x^{\prime}(s)-\frac{z^{\prime}(s)}{x(s)}\kappa(s)+\lambda=0.

Since the functions
\{1,\sin t,\cos t\}
are linearly independent, their coefficients, which are functions on the
s
variable, must vanish identically in the interval
I
. Therefore

(8)

\begin{split}0&=v_{1}z^{\prime},\\
0&=v_{2}z^{\prime},\\
0&=v_{3}x^{\prime}-\frac{z^{\prime}}{x}\kappa+\lambda.\end{split}

We distinguish two cases.

(1)

There exists
s_{1}\in I
such that
z^{\prime}(s_{1})\not=0
. Then the first two equations of (
8
) imply
v_{1}=v_{2}=0
. This gives
\vec{v}=(0,0,\pm 1)
. Thus
\vec{v}
is parallel to the
z
-axis, proving the result.

(2)

Case
z^{\prime}=0
identically in
I
. Then
\gamma
is a horizontal line. This proves that
\Sigma
is a horizontal plane, completing the proof.

∎

From now, we will assume that the speed of the Gauss curvature flow is
\vec{v}=(0,0,1)
and in consequence the rotation axis is the
z
-axis. Following with the notation of Prop.
3.1
, since
\gamma
is parametrized by arc-length, there is a smooth function
\theta=\theta(s)
such that
x^{\prime}(s)=\cos\theta(s)
and
z^{\prime}(s)=\sin\theta(s)
. In particular,
\kappa(s)=\theta^{\prime}(s)
. From the last equation of (
8
), a rotational surface
\Sigma
parametrized by (
7
) is a
\lambda
-translator if and only if the functions
x(s)
and
z(s)
satisfy

(9)

\left\{\begin{split}x^{\prime}(s)&=\cos\theta(s)\\
z^{\prime}(s)&=\sin\theta(s)\\
\theta^{\prime}(s)&=x(s)\frac{\cos\theta(s)}{\sin\theta(s)}+\lambda\frac{x(s)}{\sin\theta(s)}.\end{split}\right.

In the next result, we investigate the rotational
\lambda
-translators whose generating curve is a straight-line.

Proposition 3.2
.

Any rotational surface generated by straight lines is a
\lambda
-translator. In particular, they are: horizontal planes (
|\lambda|=1
), rotational cones (
0<|\lambda|<1
) and circular cylinders (
\lambda=0
).

Proof.

Let
\Sigma
be a rotational surface about the
z
-axis and whose generating curve is a straight-line
\gamma
. Since
\theta^{\prime}=\kappa=0
, the function
\theta
is constant,
\theta(s)=\theta_{0}
. If
\gamma
is a horizontal straight-line, then
\Sigma
is a plane and we know that
\Sigma
is a
\lambda
-translator with
\lambda=\pm 1
. If
\gamma
is not a horizontal plane, then
\sin\theta_{0}\not=0
. Then (
9
) proves that
\Sigma
is a
\lambda
-translator for the choice of
\lambda
given by
\lambda=-\cos\theta_{0}
.
∎

We point out that planes and circular cylinders have constant mean curvature. In
[
16
]
, the second author has proved that these surfaces are the only
\lambda
-translators for the Gauss curvature flow with constant mean curvature.

In the second part of this section, we investigate those rotational
\lambda
-translators that intersect the rotation axis orthogonally. If the intersection with the
z
-axis occurs at
s=0
, the orthogonality condition is equivalent to the initial condition
\theta(0)=0
in the system (
9
). Consequently,
\sin\theta(0)=0
and
x(0)=0
, which implies that the third equation of (
9
) is not well-defined at
s=0
.

In order to prove the existence of (
9
), it is convenient to reparametrize the surface
\Sigma
. Since we require that
\gamma
meets the
z
-axis orthogonally,
\gamma
must be a graph on the
x
-axis. By using radial coordinates for
\Sigma
,
x=r
and
z=u(r)
, we have
\gamma(r)=(r,0,u(r))
, and the parametrization of
\Sigma
is
(r,t)\mapsto(r\cos t,r\sin t,u(r))
, where
u
is defined on some interval of
(0,\infty)
. With this parametrization, Eq. (
9
) becomes:

(10)

\dfrac{u^{\prime}(r)u^{\prime\prime}(r)}{r(1+u^{\prime}(r)^{2})^{2}}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1+u^{\prime 2}(r)}}+\lambda.

This equation is singular at
r=0
and standard ODE theory cannot directly apply. The orthogonality condition with the
z
-axis is expressed by
u^{\prime}(0)=0
. After a vertical translation, we can assume that the intersection point between
\gamma
and the
z
-axis occurs at the origin. To address the singularity at
r=0
we multiply (
10
) by
2r
and consider the initial value problem

(11)

\left\{\begin{split}\left(\dfrac{u^{\prime}(r)^{2}}{1+u^{\prime}(r)^{2}}\right)^{\prime}&=\frac{2r}{\sqrt{1+u^{\prime}(r)^{2}}}+2\lambda r,\quad\mbox{in }(r_{0},r_{0}+\delta)\\
u(r_{0})&=0,\quad u^{\prime}(r_{0})=0,\end{split}\right.

where
r_{0}>0
. When
r_{0}\not=0
, the standard theory of ODEs assures the existence of solutions of (
11
) for some
\delta>0
. In the following result, we establish the existence of solutions of (
11
) in the case
r_{0}=0
, for some
\delta>0
.

Theorem 3.3
.

For
r_{0}=0
, the solvability of (
11
) for some
\delta>0
is the following:

(1)

If

\lambda<-1
, then there are no solutions.

(2)

If
\lambda=-1
, the solution is
u(r)=0
.

(3)

If
\lambda>-1
, there is
R>0
such that the initial value problem (
11
) has a solution in the interval
[0,R]
.

Proof.

Suppose that (
11
) has a solution for
r_{0}=0
. By taking the limit as
r\to 0
and applying the L’Hôpital’s rule in (
10
), we obtain

u^{\prime\prime}(0)^{2}=1+\lambda.

This implies that
\lambda
must satisfy the inequality
\lambda\geq-1
.

If
\lambda=-1
, then it is trivial that
u(r)=0
is a solution.
Therefore, it remains to prove the existence of solutions when
\lambda>-1
. Define the functions
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to(-1,1)
and
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
by

f(x)=\frac{x}{\sqrt{1+x^{2}}},\quad g(x)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{1+x^{2}}}+2\lambda.

Note that the positivity of
g
depends on the value of
\lambda
. For example, when
\lambda\geq 0
,
g
is always positive. However, if
\lambda\in(-1,0)
, then
g
remains positive for every
x\in(\frac{\sqrt{1-\lambda^{2}}}{\lambda},\frac{\sqrt{1-\lambda^{2}}}{-\lambda})
.

In terms of the functions
f
and
g
, the equation in (
11
) can be written as
(f(u^{\prime})^{2})^{\prime}=rg(u^{\prime})
. From this identity we arrive at

(12)

u(r)=\int_{0}^{r}f^{-1}\left(\sqrt{\int_{0}^{s}tg(u^{\prime}(t))dt}\right)ds,

where
f^{-1}
is the inverse of
f
, defined by

f^{-1}\colon(-1,1)\to\mathbb{R},\quad f^{-1}(y)=\frac{y}{\sqrt{1-y^{2}}}.

Define the operator
\mathsf{T}
by

(\mathsf{T}u)(r)=\int_{0}^{r}f^{-1}\left(\sqrt{\int_{0}^{s}tg(u^{\prime}(t))dt}\right)ds.

It is clear that a
C^{2}
function
u
is a solution of (
11
) if
u
is a fixed point of the operator
\mathsf{T}
. The proof of the theorem consists of showing that
\mathsf{T}
is a contraction map on the Banach space
C^{1}([0,R])
endowed with the standard norm
\|u\|=\|u\|_{\infty}+\|u^{\prime}\|_{\infty}
. By applying the Banach fixed point theorem, the existence of a fixed point guarantees the desired solution. For this, we will prove that
\mathsf{T}
is a contraction on a closed ball
\overline{\mathcal{B}(0,\epsilon)}\subset C^{1}([0,R])
, for some
\epsilon,R>0
, which will be fixed later.

Notice that as long as
\lambda\geq 0
,
\mathsf{T}
is well-defined because the radicand in its definition is always positive. If
\lambda\in(-1,0)
we can ensure that the radicand remains positive by choosing a suitable upper bound for
\epsilon
; for example we may require
\epsilon<\frac{\sqrt{1-\lambda^{2}}}{-\lambda}
. Therefore,
|u^{\prime}|<\epsilon
because
\|u\|<\epsilon
.

Let
M=2+2\lambda>0
, which it is positive because
\lambda>-1
. Then
g(x)\leq M
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Fix
R>0
such that
R\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}
. Then we have

\int_{0}^{s}tg(u^{\prime}(t))\,dt\leq M\int_{0}^{s}t\,dt=M\frac{s^{2}}{2}\leq M\frac{R^{2}}{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}.

Thus, we can apply
f^{-1}
.

We prove that
\mathsf{T}
is a self-map on the closed ball
\overline{\mathcal{B}(0,\epsilon)}
for some
\epsilon>0
. Second, we demonstrate that
\mathsf{T}
is a contraction. Fix
\epsilon>0
, with
\epsilon<1
. In case that
\lambda\in(-1,0)
, we also assume
\epsilon<\frac{\sqrt{1-\lambda^{2}}}{-\lambda}
. The value of
R
will be determined later.

(1)

We prove that
\mathsf{T}(\overline{\mathcal{B}(0,\epsilon)})\subset\overline{\mathcal{B}(0,\epsilon)}
where
R
is given by

(13)

R<\min\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}},\frac{\epsilon}{2},\frac{\sqrt{2}\epsilon}{\sqrt{M(4+\epsilon^{2})}}\}.

Let
u\in\overline{\mathcal{B}(0,\epsilon)}
. We know that
\int_{0}^{s}tg(u^{\prime}(t))\,dt\leq 1/2
. By using that
f^{-1}
is an increasing function, we have

|(\mathsf{T}u)(r)|\leq\left|\int_{0}^{r}f^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\,ds\right|=\int_{0}^{r}ds=r\leq R\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}.

Similarly, we have
\int_{0}^{s}tg(u^{\prime}(t))\,dt\leq\int_{0}^{s}Mt\,ds=Ms^{2}/2
. Thus

|(\mathsf{T}u)^{\prime}(r)|\leq f^{-1}\left(\frac{s\sqrt{M}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\leq f^{-1}\left(\frac{R\sqrt{M}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2},

where we have used that
f^{-1}(R\sqrt{M/2})\leq\epsilon/2
thanks to (
13
).
Definitively, we have proved
\|\mathsf{T}u\|=|\|\mathsf{T}u\|_{\infty}+\|(\mathsf{T}u)^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon
.

(2)

We proceed in showing that
\mathsf{T}:\overline{\mathcal{B}(0,\epsilon)}\to\overline{\mathcal{B}(0,\epsilon)}
is a contraction. For this, we aim to establish the existence of a positive constant
\mu
, with
\mu<1
, such that

\|\mathsf{T}u-\mathsf{T}w\|\leq\mu\|u-w\|,

for all
u,w\in\overline{\mathcal{B}(0,\epsilon)}
. Let
L_{f^{-1}}
,
L_{g}
denote the Lipschitz constants of
f^{-1}
and
g
respectively when these functions are defined in the interval
[-\epsilon,\epsilon]
. We are going to bound each of the two summands in

\|\mathsf{T}u-\mathsf{T}w\|=\|\mathsf{T}u-\mathsf{T}w\|_{\infty}+\|(\mathsf{T}u)-(\mathsf{T}w)^{\prime}\|_{\infty}.

Let
u,w\in\overline{\mathcal{B}(0,\epsilon)}\subset C^{1}([0,R])
. For all
r\in[0,R]
, where
R
will be changed later, we have

\begin{split}|(\mathsf{T}u)(r)-(\mathsf{T}w)(r)|&\leq L_{f^{-1}}\int_{0}^{r}\left|\sqrt{\int_{0}^{s}tg(u^{\prime})\,dt}-\sqrt{\int_{0}^{s}tg(w^{\prime})\,dt}\right|ds\\
&\leq L_{f^{-1}}\int_{0}^{r}\frac{\int_{0}^{s}t|g(u^{\prime})-g(w^{\prime})|\,ds}{\sqrt{\int_{0}^{s}tg(u^{\prime})\,dt}+\sqrt{\int_{0}^{s}tg(w^{\prime})\,dt}}\,ds\\
&\leq L_{f^{-1}}L_{g}\|u-w\|\int_{0}^{r}\frac{s^{2}/2}{\sqrt{\int_{0}^{s}tg(u^{\prime})\,dt}+\sqrt{\int_{0}^{s}tg(w^{\prime})\,dt}}\,ds.\end{split}

Using the Taylor expansion of the function
g
, we have

\int_{0}^{s}tg(u^{\prime})\,dt=(1+\lambda)s^{2}+o(s^{2})

in
[-\epsilon,\epsilon]
.
Thus, we have

\begin{split}|(\mathsf{T}u)(r)-(\mathsf{T}w)(r)|&\leq L_{f^{-1}}L_{g}\|u-w\|\int_{0}^{r}\frac{s}{4\sqrt{1+\lambda}+o(1)}\,ds.\end{split}

From (
13
), we know that
R<\epsilon/2
. By taking
\epsilon
sufficiently small, there is a positive constant
C=C(\epsilon,\lambda)>0
such that
4\sqrt{1+\lambda}+o(1)\geq C(\epsilon,\lambda)
. Then

(14)

|(\mathsf{T}u)(r)-(\mathsf{T}w)(r)|\leq\frac{1}{C}L_{f^{-1}}L_{g}\|u-w\|\int_{0}^{r}s\,ds\leq\frac{R^{2}}{2C}L_{f^{-1}}L_{g}\|u-w\|.

Similarly, we derive

(15)

\begin{split}|(\mathsf{T}u)^{\prime}(r)-(\mathsf{T}w)^{\prime}(r)|&\leq L_{f^{-1}}\left|\sqrt{\int_{0}^{r}tg(u^{\prime})\,dt}-\sqrt{\int_{0}^{r}tg(w^{\prime})\,dt}\right|\\
&=L_{f^{-1}}\frac{\int_{0}^{r}t|g(u^{\prime})-g(w^{\prime})|\,ds}{\sqrt{\int_{0}^{r}tg(u^{\prime})\,dt}+\sqrt{\int_{0}^{r}tg(w^{\prime})\,dt}}\\
&\leq L_{f^{-1}}L_{g}\|u-w\|\frac{s^{2}/2}{\sqrt{\int_{0}^{s}tg(u^{\prime})\,dt}+\sqrt{\int_{0}^{s}tg(w^{\prime})\,dt}}\\
&\leq\frac{R}{C}L_{f^{-1}}L_{g}\|u-w\|.\end{split}

After choosing
R
to satisfy the condition (
13
), together with

R\leq\min\left\{\frac{\sqrt{C}}{\sqrt{2L_{f^{-1}}L_{g}}},\frac{C}{4L_{f^{-1}}L_{g}}\right\},

we obtain from (
14
) and (
15
)

\|\mathsf{T}u-\mathsf{T}w\|\leq\frac{1}{2}\|u-w\|,

which proves that
\mathsf{T}
is a contraction on
C^{1}
([0,R]).

To complete the proof, we show that the solution
u
extends with
C^{2}
-regularity at
r=0
. This was proved at the beginning of the proof. Indeed, by taking the limit as
r\to 0
and applying L’Hôpital’s rule to Eq. (
10
), we obtain

u^{\prime\prime}(0)^{2}=1+\lambda,

where
1+\lambda>0
because
\lambda>-1
. By the definition of
u
in (
12
), the function
u
implies that
u^{\prime\prime}(0)=\sqrt{1+\lambda}
.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

In (
12
) we can also replace the inverse of
f^{-1}
by
-f^{-1}
. In such a case, then
u^{\prime\prime}(0)=-\sqrt{1+\lambda}
. In fact, it is not difficult to see that if
\bar{u}
is the solution for Eq. (
11
) with
\bar{u}^{\prime\prime}(0)=-\sqrt{1+\lambda}
, then
\bar{u}(r)=-u(r)
for all
r
, where
u
is the solution with
u^{\prime\prime}(0)=\sqrt{1+\lambda}
.

4.
Geometric properties of rotational
\lambda
-translators

In this section we give a geometric description of the rotational
\lambda
-translators that intersect orthogonally the rotation axis. In the final part of this section, we will also study the case that the surface does not meet the rotation axis.

If the rotational
\lambda
-translator meets orthogonally the rotation axis, we know by Thm.
3.3
that
\lambda\geq-1
. If
\lambda=-1
, the surface is a horizontal planes.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\lambda>-1
. Let
\Sigma
be a rotational
\lambda
-translator with speed
\vec{v}=(0,0,1)
and parametrized by (
7
). Suppose that
\gamma
satisfies (
9
) with initial conditions

(16)

x(0)=z(0)=\theta(0)=0.

Let
[0,\omega)
be the maximal domain of
\gamma
. Up to a symmetry about the plane
z=0
, we have the following classification.

(1)

If
\lambda>0
then
\omega<\infty
and
\gamma
intersects the
z
-axis at
s=\omega
. This intersection is not orthogonal. Moreover,
x(s)<\sqrt{2}
for all
s\in I
.

(2)

If
\lambda=0
then
\omega=\infty
. The curve
\gamma
is asymptotic to the vertical line of equation
z=\sqrt{2}
. The function
z=z(s)
is strictly increasing with
\lim_{s\to\infty}z(s)=\infty
.

(3)

If
\lambda\in(-1,0)
, then
\omega=\infty
. The curve
\gamma
is an entire graph on
\mathbb{R}
where
\lim_{s\to\infty}x(s)=\lim_{s\to\infty}z(s)=\infty
and
\lim_{s\to\infty}\theta(s)=\cos^{-1}(-\lambda)
.

In all cases, the surface is convex.

Proof.

By the L’Hôpital rule we know that
\theta^{\prime}(0)^{2}=1+\lambda
. Then
\theta^{\prime}(0)
can take the value
\sqrt{1+\lambda}
or
-\sqrt{1+\lambda}
. However we have seen in Rem.
3.4
that the solutions for
\sqrt{1+\lambda}
and
-\sqrt{1+\lambda}
are symmetric with respect to each other about the
x
-axis. Without loss of generality, we assume
\theta^{\prime}(0)=\sqrt{1+\lambda}
. In such a case, the function
\theta(s)
is increasing at
s=0
and consequently, the functions
x(s)
and
z(s)
are also increasing at
s=0
.

Multiplying the third equation of (
9
) by
\sin\theta(s)
, we deduce a first integration. Indeed, it follows from (
16
) that

(17)

1-\cos\theta(s)=\frac{1}{2}x(s)^{2}+\lambda\int_{0}^{s}x(t)\,dt.

Notice that this identity can be also obtained from the first equation of (
11
).

(1)

Case
\lambda>0
. We aim to prove that the function
\theta(s)
crosses the value
\pi/2
. Assume that
\theta(s)\in(0,\pi/2)
; under this condition, the function
x(s)
is increasing. Fix
\bar{s}>0
, chosen sufficiently close to zero. From (
9
), and provided
\theta(s)\in(0,\pi/2)
and
s>\bar{s}
, we have

\theta^{\prime}(s)\geq\lambda\frac{x(s)}{\sin\theta(s)}\geq\lambda x(s)\geq\lambda x(\bar{s})>0.

This inequality proves that the growth of
\theta
is linear when
\theta(s)\in(0,\pi/2)
. Consequently, there exists a first value
s_{1}
such that
\theta(s_{1})=\pi/2
.

At
s=s_{1}
, we have
\theta(s_{1})=\pi/2
, thus the function
x(s)
attains a maximum at this point. Substituting into (
17
), we obtain

1=\frac{1}{2}x(s_{1})^{2}+\lambda\int_{0}^{s_{1}}x(t)\,dt>\frac{1}{2}x(s_{1})^{2}.

This inequality implies that
x(s_{1})<\sqrt{2}
, and hence
x(s)\leq x(s_{1})<\sqrt{2}
, for all
s\in[0,s_{1}]
.

It remains to prove that the solution curve
\gamma
meets the rotation axis again. For this, we focus in the functions
(x,\theta)
in the system (
9
) and consider the autonomous system

(18)

\left\{\begin{split}x^{\prime}&=\cos\theta\\
\theta^{\prime}&=\frac{x}{\sin\theta}(\cos\theta+\lambda).\end{split}\right.

Here we are assuming
(x,\theta)\in\mathbb{R}\times(0,\pi)
because of the denominator in
\theta^{\prime}
.
The singular point of the system (
18
) is
P=(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
. The linearized system at the singular point
P=(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
is

\begin{pmatrix}0&-\sin\theta\\
\frac{\cos\theta+\lambda}{\sin\theta}&\frac{-1-\lambda\cos\theta}{\sin^{2}\theta}\end{pmatrix}(P)=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
\lambda&0\end{pmatrix}.

Since the eigenvalues are pure complex numbers, namely
\pm\sqrt{-\lambda}
, then the point
P
represents a center in the phase plane: see Fig.
1
.

18
) with trajectories emanating from

(red).The singular point is

. Left: case
\lambda>0
. Right: case
\lambda\in(-1,0)
.

Since we are considering solutions of (
9
) where
\theta^{\prime}(0)>0
, the trajectory of (
18
) corresponding to the solution (
9
)-(
16
) moves on
\theta>0
. As we see in Fig.

, this trajectory turns around the center without to arrive to the value
\theta=\pi
. Thus the trajectory intersects the axis
x=0
in some value
\theta_{0}\in(\pi/2,\pi)
. This implies that the solution
\gamma
of (
9
)-(
16
) meets the
z
-axis at some point
s_{1}
with
\theta(s_{1})\in(\pi/2,\pi)
. In particular, the maximal domain of existence of (
9
)-(
16
) is finite and
\omega<\infty
.

(2)

Case
\lambda=0
. This situation is known and described in
[
4
]
. From Eq. (
17
), the maximal domain is
[0,\infty)
with
\lim_{s\to\infty}x(s)=\sqrt{2}
. The function
\theta
has sign in its domain which implies that
\gamma
is a convex curve.

(3)

Case
\lambda\in(-1,0)
. Instead to analyze the corresponding phase plane as in the case
\lambda>0
, the results can be obtained directly from (
9
). See the phase plane in Fig.
1
. The function
\theta
cannot attain the value
\pi/2
. Indeed, if
s_{1}
is the first point where
\theta(s_{1})=\pi/2
, then we would have
\theta^{\prime}(s_{1})\geq 0
. However from (
9
) we have
\theta^{\prime}(s_{1})=\lambda x(s_{1})<0
, which it is contradictory. The same argument proves that the function
\theta(s)
cannot go to the value
\pi/2
, and thus the function
\theta(s)
is away from the value
\pi/2
.

The image of
\theta(s)
remains in the interval
(0,\pi/2)
after
s=0
. Indeed, if
\theta(s)
decreases attaining the value
0
at
s_{2}
, then
\theta^{\prime}(s_{2})\leq 0
. However, from (
9
) we have
\lim_{s\to s_{2}}\theta^{\prime}(s)=\infty
. Similarly as in the previous discussion about the value
\pi/2
, the function
\theta(s)
cannot go to value
0
because in such a case,
\lim\theta^{\prime}(s)=\infty
as
\theta(s)\to 0
. As a consequence, if we fix
\bar{s}
close
0
, there are
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\mathbb{R}

0<\theta_{1}<\theta_{2}<\pi/2
such that
\theta_{1}\leq\theta(s)\leq\theta_{2}
for all

. This proves
\omega=\infty
and
\lim_{s\to\infty}x(s)=\lim_{s\to\infty}z(s)=\infty
.

We now prove the last statement. By dividing (
17

x(s)^{2}
and letting
s\to\infty
, the left hand-side of (
17
) is
0
. Then the L’Hôpital’s rule gives

-\frac{1}{2}=\lim_{s\to\infty}\frac{\lambda}{x(s)^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}x(t)\,dt=\frac{\lambda}{2\lim_{s\to\infty}x^{\prime}(s)}.

This gives
\lim_{s\to\infty}\cos\theta(s)=-\lambda
.

The last statement of the theorem is consequence that
\theta^{\prime}
cannot vanish, hence
\theta^{\prime}
is always is positive. This implies that the curvature of the planar curve
\gamma
has sign, in this case, is positive because
\theta^{\prime}(0)>0
. This is equivalent to say that
\gamma
is convex.
∎

Theorem
4.1
considers the case that the generating curve
\gamma
intersects orthogonally the rotation axis at the starting point

-translators if the curve

In case of existence of (non-rotational) closed
\lambda
-translators, we have a restriction on the sign of
\lambda
and on the genus of the surface.

Theorem 4.2
.

If
\Sigma
is a closed
\lambda
-translator, then
\lambda\geq 0
and the genus of
\Sigma
is
0
or
1
. Moreover,
\lambda=0
occurs only if the genus of
\Sigma
is
1
.

Proof.

Consider a plane
\Pi
, parallel to
\vec{v}
, such that
\Pi\cap\Sigma=\emptyset
. Moving the plane
\Pi
parallel to itself towards
\Sigma
, let
p\in\Sigma
the first point of contact. Then
K(p)\geq 0
and
N(p)
is orthogonal to
\vec{v}
. From Eq. (
1
), it follows that
\lambda\geq 0
.

On the other hand, if
g
is the genus of
\Sigma
, the Gauss-Bonnet theorem, in combination with (
1
), gives

4\pi(1-g)=\int_{\Sigma}K\,d\Sigma=\int_{\Sigma}\langle N,\vec{v}\rangle\,d\Sigma+\lambda\mbox{ area}(\Sigma).

Since for any closed surface, we have
\int_{\Sigma}\langle N,\vec{v}\rangle d\Sigma=0
, it follows that
4\pi(1-g)=\lambda\mbox{ area}(\Sigma)
. Since
\lambda\geq 0
, this proves
g\leq 1
. In particular,
\lambda=0
if and only if
g=1
.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

If the genus is
0
, the value of
\lambda
is determined by the Gauss-Bonnet formula, namely,

\lambda=\frac{4\pi}{\mbox{area}(\Sigma)}.

When
\lambda=-1
, Theorem

establishes that if the generating curve meets orthogonally the rotating axis, then the surface is a horizontal plane. In the following result we analyze the case
\lambda=-1
but now the surface meets orthogonally the

-plane. In terms of the generating curve
\gamma
, we have
\theta(0)=\pi/2
and
x(0)>0
. After a dilation, we can suppose

. Consider initial conditions

x(0)=1,\quad z(0)=0,\quad\theta(0)=\frac{\pi}{2}.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
\lambda=-1
. The solution

of (

has a branch asymptotic to a horizontal line and the other one ends at finite time.

Proof.

At

, from (

, hence

, the function

, neither the value

attains the value

. This proves that

takes all values in the interval

and a branch of the solution

satisfies

.
∎

In general, the study of solutions of (
