Climate change has already made the United States poorer
Authors: Derek Lemoine
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceClimate change has already begun altering weather around the world. Altering the weather is likely to affect the economy. Indeed, I show that climate change has already reduced United States income by making the country warmer. Because counties trade with each other, each U.S. county has been affected by how climate change altered temperatures throughout the United States, not just how it altered temperature locally. Statistical agencies could update these calculations annually.

Climate change is already here. Economists have long focused on projecting the costs of future climate change (1–5), as those are the costs that determine the value of reducing greenhouse gas emissions today. However, it is also important to understand the costs that climate change is already imposing on the economy. Knowledge of these ongoing costs could help with adaptation funding (6, 7), loss and damage compensation (8, 9), risk disclosure (10), regulation (11), and public awareness (12). And, of course, understanding the impacts of climate change today is a first step to understanding the impacts of future climate change. I here show that climate change has already reduced U.S. income over 2000 to 2019 by shifting daily temperatures within U.S. borders.

Projecting future impacts is extremely challenging, resulting in little consensus about either the best way to do so (5) or how large those impacts may be (13). Evaluating the impacts of climate change in recent years, from data we already have, should be more achievable. First, today’s economy is not so different from what it would have been in the absence of climate change or from what it was over the time period used to estimate the relationship between economic activity and weather. In contrast, when we project future impacts of climate change, we must project weather–economy relationships out of sample, to very different incomes, technologies, institutions, and policy environments. Second, climate change has not yet taken the world far beyond the temperatures experienced over past decades. As a result, linear effects estimated through reduced-form regression frameworks may suffice when calculating the costs of already-realized climate change. In contrast, we have to consider the potential for nonlinear effects when projecting future climate change, without much data to pin down either the nature or the degree of the nonlinearity.

The present paper develops an internally consistent framework for estimating how climate change-induced shifts in daily temperature have already affected income. There is a large literature on detection and attribution of climate change, in climate metrics (14) as well as in downstream natural and human systems (15, 16). Detection and attribution are especially challenging in human systems, which change relentlessly and for many reasons (16, 17). The present paper attributes a fraction of the past variation in U.S. counties’ income per capita to climate change. It uses a multistep approach to attribution: (15, 17) a regression framework attributes changes in income per capita over 1970 to 2019 to changes in local and regional temperatures, climate models attribute changes in temperatures over 2000 to 2019 to humanity’s effects on the climate, and a theoretical framework integrates these two attributions.

Much work in climate science attributes individual extreme weather events to climate change (18, 19), and some work has tallied the cost of climate change implied by these attribution studies (20). Prior work has also estimated how climate change has already affected mortality (21–25), agricultural productivity (26–29), and economic growth (30). All of these prior studies represent climate change as a series of localized, transient changes in weather. I advance this literature by formally showing how to account for the persistent and widespread nature of recent climate change, which has altered weather in all recent years and in all places. I show that this full accounting matters: the persistent and widespread nature of recent climate change made its effects on temperature many times more costly for U.S. income.

Theoretical Framework

I develop a formal framework that permits climate change to affect productivity, capital stocks, and prices. I derive the impact of climate change in terms that can be estimated from data within a linear regression framework.

I model income per capita in a location as a function of contemporary local weather, of local capital stocks, and of a local price index. The direct dependence of income per capita on contemporary weather reflects the possibility that weather impacts productivity.

The capital stocks encompass physical capital, natural resources, and knowledge (including technologies). Capital stocks depend on prior periods’ weather, on prior periods’ prices, and on prior periods’ beliefs about subsequent weather, including beliefs about the effect of climate change on weather trends. Agents updated prior periods’ beliefs from earlier observations of local weather and from earlier observations of weather in other locations that may be informative about trends in local weather. Current weather potentially affects capital stocks directly through degradation and indirectly through decisions about investment (for physical capital), management (for natural resources), and research (for knowledge). Beliefs and prices in earlier periods affected incentives to invest, to extract resources, or to undertake research.

The local price index depends on production around the country. It thus depends on aggregate capital stocks around the country and on weather around the country. Aggregate capital stocks, in turn, depend on contemporary weather around the country, on prior periods’ beliefs about subsequent weather around the country, and on prior periods’ national prices. Prior periods’ beliefs about subsequent weather around the country were updated from observations of earlier weather around the country.

I operationalize Eq. 1 as follows (see Materials and Methods for details). First, I obtain the effect of climate change on daily temperatures by comparing observed temperatures over 2000 to 2019 in U.S. counties to the average of climate models’ simulations of what temperatures would have been in the absence of human influences. Second, to obtain the effect of daily temperatures on the economy, I regress the year-on-year change in U.S. counties’ log income per capita over 1970 to 2019 on changes in current and lagged weather, county and year fixed effects, and quadratic state-level time trends, per Eq. 2. I categorize days as cold, cool, warm, or hot based on their average temperature (34). I construct annual weather variables corresponding to the number of cold, cool, warm, and hot days in a county (witn) and, in the base specification, corresponding to the inverse-distance-weighted average of the cold, cool, warm, and hot days in counties around the rest of the United States (Witn). I permit the effects of all weather variables to vary with a county’s baseline climate, income per capita, economic composition, and, entering quadratically so as to capture effects of coastlines, latitude and longitude. Finally, using Eq. 3, I combine the effects of climate change on recent U.S. daily temperatures with the effects of daily temperatures on U.S. income per capita to calculate each county’s percentage change in income per capita due to climate change in each year from 2000 to 2019. Aggregating county-level changes in income per capita using Eq. 4 then yields the percentage change in national income due to climate change over 2000 to 2019.

Results

Effects of Climate Change on Recent Daily Temperatures.

The first step is to obtain the effect of climate change on recent daily temperatures in the contiguous United States.

The Top Left panel in Fig. 1 plots the evolution of population-weighted national temperatures since 1950. The coldest days did not clearly trend one way or the other, cool days trended down over time, and warm and hot days both trended up over time. These trends reflect climate change and also reflect population shifting over time toward warmer counties.

The Top Right panel isolates the part due to climate change. It plots the effect of humans on realized temperatures, as the 10-y running mean of the difference between observed temperatures and the average of nine climate models’ simulated temperatures in the absence of human contributions to climate (Materials and Methods). Climate change appears to be responsible for part of the increase in warm and hot days and for part of the decrease in cool days, but climate change did not have a consistent effect on the coldest days.

The other four panels in Fig. 1 map the effect of climate change on the average number of days of each type per year over 2000 to 2019. Purple colors indicate that climate change decreased days of that type, and green colors indicate that climate change increased days of that type. Gold coloring indicates that a county does not have days of the indicated type, either with or without climate change. Climate change tends to reduce the incidence of cool and cold days while increasing the incidence of warm and hot days, but there is a lot of spatial heterogeneity in the effects of climate change. In particular, the number of warm days tends to decrease in favor of hot days among the hotter parts of the country, and there are areas of the Midwest in which climate change reduced the number of hot days and/or increased the number of cold days, reflecting the phenomenon known as the Midwestern warming hole (35, 36).

I next estimate how daily temperatures in the contiguous United States affect its income per capita.

Panel A depicts the effect of a transitory, isolated weather shock (Short-Run, Local), as determined by the estimated λ^nj (see Eq. 2). This illustrative calculation treats the indicated temperature variable as increasing in only one year and in only one county. For a fictional county with the average value of each covariate, point estimates suggest that an extra cold or cool day increases income per capita in that same year and location and an extra warm or hot day reduces income per capita in that same year and location. The effect of hot days is statistically significant. Counties with higher average annual income per capita tend to benefit more from cool and cold days and to be harmed more by warm and hot days. Counties with higher annual average temperatures (over 1950 to 1979) tend to benefit more from cool and cold days. Holding that annual average temperature constant, counties that are farther north benefit more from cold days and are harmed more by warm and hot days. [The effects of latitude and climate on hot days are noisy, which reflects a degree of collinearity among the two variables. Acting like a reduction in sample size, collinearity can increase the SE on some particular variable but does not bias either the point estimate or the SE (37).] Hot days are less harmful in counties that are farther west, which might reflect lower humidity in the west. Counties with a larger agriculture share of output tend to be harmed more by warm and hot days. In contrast, counties with a higher manufacturing share are harmed less by warm and hot days and benefit less from (or are even harmed by) cool and cold days.

Panel B depicts the effect of a persistent, isolated weather shock (Long-Run, Local), as determined by the estimated λ^nj+∑ℓ=15γ^njℓ (see Eq. 2). This illustrative calculation treats the indicated temperature variable as increasing in six straight years for a particular county. Point estimates now suggest that warm days are beneficial for the average county and that the effects of cold and cool days are now not sensitive to income. Otherwise, estimates are broadly consistent with the direction of short-run, local effects. Accounting for past years’ temperatures makes the estimates noisier.

Panel c depicts the effect of altering weather everywhere in a persistent fashion (Long-Run, Nationwide), as determined by the estimated λ^nj+Λ^nj+∑ℓ=15[γ^njℓ+Γ^njℓ] (see Eq. 2). This illustrative calculation assumes that every county in the contiguous United States receives an extra day of the indicated type—and does so for six years in a row. Effects on the average county become larger and noisier but are also largely consistent with the direction of short-run, local effects, with additional cold days now more clearly beneficial for the average county. However, several covariates now have the opposite effect on the consequences of warm and hot days. In particular, although greater income per capita and agricultural share increase exposure to local heat, they reduce exposure to widespread heat. This switch in sign for agricultural share is consistent with extreme heat reducing agricultural yields: local heat may reduce local income by reducing local yields (38), but widespread heat could raise the prices of U.S. agricultural products [through the combination of reduced yields and price-inelastic demand (39, 40)] and thereby increase producers’ income (41, 42). The most quantitatively important change is in the effect of latitude, as being farther north mitigates the effects of hot days in the long-run, nationwide calculation. This change is due to interactions between the lag of the national temperature index and latitude. As a consequence, whereas the short-run, local estimates suggest that localized heat benefits income in the southern United States, the long-run, nationwide estimates suggest that widespread heat costs the southern United States. The negative effects of extreme heat on southern counties through trade channels also appear in a recent structural model of climate impacts (43).

The short-run, local calculation (left column) suggests that climate change has reduced income per capita throughout the Great Plains and Midwest and increased income per capita in the Southeast and Southwest. Changes in each of the four temperature variables push in similar directions.

That calculation ignores the temporal structure of climate change. The middle column reports the long-run, local calculation. It permits the effects of climate change to reflect how climate change altered temperatures in earlier years, which might affect a location’s capital stocks. Total impacts on income per capita become more heterogeneous, with winners and losers both looking more extreme. But the pattern of winners and losers is broadly similar to the short-run, local calculation.

The right column reports the full calculation. It permits the effects of climate change on each county to also depend on how temperatures in other counties changed, reflecting how counties’ economies are linked through trade networks and how beliefs depend on broader weather patterns. Climate change now appears to have reduced income per capita in most of the United States. The more extreme losses were driven by changes in hot days, but changes in warm and cold days also contributed to losses. Much of the country benefited from changes in cool days, and the northernmost reaches of the country benefited from changes in hot days. A critical part of how climate change affects income per capita locally in any one county is how it affects temperatures elsewhere around the country, and thereby affects local prices and/or local investment.

From Eq. 4 in Materials and Methods, aggregate impacts on national income depend on how heterogeneous impacts on income per capita correlate with counties’ shares of national income. Fig. 4A plots the average impact of climate change on U.S. income over 2000 to 2019, via climate change shifting temperatures within the contiguous United States. The short-run, local calculation indicates that climate change reduced national income by 0.32% (SE: 0.25%). The long-run, local calculation produces noisier estimates. Its point estimate indicates that climate change reduced national income by 0.54% (SE: 1.0%). The full calculation indicates larger losses, with a point estimate of 12.1% (SE: 5.2%). Its 95% confidence extends to losses of 2.0%, which is outside the 95% CI for the short-run, local calculation. All calculations suggest that aggregate losses are primarily driven by changes in warm and hot days.

Fig. 4B assesses the robustness of average impacts over 2000 to 2019 to the regression sample and specification. The first row repeats the base specification’s results, from panel A. The other rows vary the regression sample or specification. The second row starts the sample in 1980. The third row ends the sample in 1999, just before the period over which I calculate impacts. The fourth row estimates the quadratic time trends at the county level rather than at the state level, and the fifth row drops all time trends. The sixth row replaces the year fixed effects with finer Census region-year fixed effects. The seventh row includes the year as an interacted covariate, thereby allowing the marginal effect of temperature to evolve over time. The next four rows keep only the latitude and longitude covariates, drop only the quadratic elements of the latitude and longitude covariates, drop all latitude and longitude covariates, and drop all covariates. The twelfth row models only cold and hot days, so that cool and warm days are absorbed into the omitted category. The thirteenth row does not weight the regression and the fourteenth row weights by log population instead of log income per capita. The final row constructs county-level temperature by weighting a county’s grid cells by area rather than by population.

The primary takeaways are robust: the short-run, local calculation suggests income losses of around 0.4%, the long-run, local calculation suggests income losses of around 1%, and the full calculation suggests income losses of around 12% with a 95% CI extending to income losses of around 2%. SI Appendix, Supporting Information D shows that estimates are also reasonably robust to including more lags of temperature. SI Appendix, Supporting Information E shows that estimates become more precise when clustering SE at less conservative levels used by prior literature. SI Appendix, Supporting Information G shows that estimating effects on output per capita (instead of income per capita) yields a larger point estimate than in the base specification. And SI Appendix, Supporting Information H shows that using individual climate models to define counterfactual climate (rather than averaging over nine climate models) generally yields similar results, with one model an outlier relative to the others.

A few changes do matter substantially. First, aggregating cool and warm days into the omitted category substantially reduces estimated losses under every calculation. Effects on mild weather are an important piece of climate change (44). Second, ending the estimation sample in 1999 increases the point estimate in the full calculation to a 25% reduction in income. Third, dropping the latitude and longitude covariates reduces estimated income losses under the full calculation, to 6%. Including linear latitude and longitude covariates increases estimated income losses most of the way back, to 9%. I use the specification with quadratic latitude and longitude covariates as the base specification because this form is justified by economic logic and because it fits the data appreciably better than specifications without this heterogeneity (note that the heterogeneous factors are identical between the estimation sample and impact calculations). Based on the within R2, the cartographic covariates account for 36% of the variation explained by all the covariates in the base specification. And the base specification is preferred by both Akaike and Bayesian Information Criteria to the specifications in Fig. 4 with subsets of covariates.

Fig. 4C assesses the robustness of the full calculation to the index of national weather patterns. The first row repeats the base specification’s results, from panel A. The base specification constructs a county’s nonlocal weather index as the inverse-distance-weighted average of temperatures everywhere else around the country. This index has the merits of being influenced by temperatures everywhere else in the country while being more sensitive to temperatures in the nearer counties that are likely to have tighter trade and social links to a county of interest.

However, other indices are plausible. Based on the “gravity” relationship between trade and distance from the international trade literature (45), the second row raises county-to-county distances to the fifth power before inverting them and taking the weighted average of nonlocal temperatures. The third through fifth rows average temperatures among counties whose centroids are within various radii of a given county’s centroid. And the sixth and seventh rows average temperatures among counties in the same state or Census region. As expected, changing the index of nonlocal weather primarily affects the full calculation. All of the alternate estimates still indicate income losses from climate change, but their estimates do tend to be both more precise and a few percentage points smaller than in the base specification. The smallest impacts arise from indices that account only for the very closest counties, whether by using gravity weights from international trade, by restricting attention to counties within 250 miles, or by restricting attention to counties within the same state (these three measures of nonlocal temperatures have pairwise correlations above 0.9). However, these three metrics probably overweight nearby counties, as counties within the United States are far more interconnected than are countries around the world. The more reasonable alternate indices suggest that climate change reduced U.S. income by around 7% over 2000 to 2019.

The final two rows in Fig. 4C construct indices based on mechanisms, weighting counties by state-to-state value of trade or by county-to-county social networking connections (Materials and Methods). Within the framework of Eq. 1, value of trade captures how weather in other locations may affect local prices, and social connectedness captures how weather in other locations may affect beliefs about local climate change and thus local investment. The regression with the trade measure explains about as much of the variation in the data as does the base specification but nearly twice as much of the variation as does a regression with the social connectedness measure. Adding the social connectedness measure to a regression with the trade measure does not explain much more of the variation in the data. Moreover, estimated income losses with the trade measure are similar to estimated income losses under the base specification (indicating robustness to constructing the index of nonlocal temperature from trade data), whereas estimated income losses with the social connectedness measure are half as large as in the base specification. These results suggest that price channels rather than belief channels drive the importance of national temperature patterns. Trade networks may be propagating the effects of climate change on U.S. counties’ temperatures to the rest of the United States, analogously to how they propagate weather shocks around the world (45). Future work should update these estimates as data on county-to-county trade links become available.

Discussion

I have estimated the impacts of climate change on recent years’ U.S. income via shifts in daily temperatures within the contiguous United States. I have accounted for the temporal and spatial structure of climate change using transparent reduced-form methods derived from a formal framework. Central estimates suggest that losses from climate change could already reach 12% of U.S. income. These estimates are comparable in magnitude to the estimated impact of proposed revisions to trade, monetary, and immigration policies (46).

I find that general equilibrium effects, arising from how climate change alters temperatures around the country, comprise a large fraction of the costs of climate change to date. This finding is reminiscent of concurrent work finding that national output is more sensitive to global temperature than to local temperature (47). However, that work argues that global temperature primarily matters via its effects on local extreme weather, not via its effects on local temperatures in other locations. The present paper finds that nonlocal temperatures matter, through trade connections. In addition, ref. 47 relies on time series variation in global temperature that affects all countries simultaneously. I here construct measures of national weather patterns that weight nearby temperatures more than distant temperatures, which enables me to use time fixed effects to absorb broader economic and climatic cycles.

My calculations come with several caveats. First, they are not a comprehensive accounting of the consequences of climate change. They omit effects on discrete weather events such as hurricanes, effects on precipitation that are not correlated with temperature, effects on nonweather channels such as sea level rise, and effects on outcomes such as mortality and amenity values.

Second, to implement my framework, I must take a stand on the measures of weather, on the lag structure of weather impacts, and on the index of nonlocal weather patterns. Future work could use model selection techniques to refine each assumption and to refine the set of covariates used to capture heterogeneity (48, 49).

Third, the present paper studies the effects of climate change on temperatures within U.S. borders. But climate change shifts weather around the world. Global weather patterns matter for economic output (45, 50–53), and international trade may be important to future climate change impacts, as has been shown for the agricultural sector (42, 54–57). Future work should expand the analysis to global weather and global economies.

Fourth, the conceptual framework misses the possibility of ex ante adaptation based on information other than weather realizations, such as information from climate models’ projections of future climate change (58, 59). Future work could assess whether that channel is quantitatively important.

Finally, estimates like the ones developed here could be updated annually. Assessing where climate change has caused economic losses could be important for figuring out where to direct adaptation funding, for compensation based on loss and damage frameworks, and for financial disclosure of climate risks. In addition, framing the past year’s temperature shifts in terms of lost income could make climate change more salient. It is possible that people notice the short-run, partial equilibrium effects of climate change, which I have shown cause relatively small aggregate income losses and even benefit parts of the country, but do not notice the long-run and general equilibrium effects of climate change, which I have shown increase aggregate income losses and transmit them around the country.

Materials and Methods

Code and data are available in the openICPSR repository, with DOI: https://doi.org/10.3886/E240311V1 (60).

Data.

I obtain data on county-level personal income per capita and population over 1969 to 2019 from the Bureau of Economic Analysis. County-level agricultural, resource, and manufacturing output shares also come from the Bureau of Economic Analysis. Agriculture corresponds to “Agriculture, forestry, fishing and hunting,” manufacturing corresponds to “Manufacturing,” and resource extraction corresponds to “Mining, quarrying, and oil and gas extraction.” I take a county’s latitude and longitude as the population-weighted average of its Census tracts’ centroids, using population as of 1990.

County-level temperature data over 1950 to 2019 follow prior work (38, 61). I first estimate the relationship between average daily temperature at a stable set of weather stations from the NOAA Global Historical Climatology Network and 4 km-by-4 km gridded temperature from the parameter-elevation regressions on independent slopes model (PRISM). I then use the estimated relationship to aggregate weather station observations of temperature to a 4 km-by-4 km grid. This method creates a record of daily data that accounts for the physical relationships modeled by PRISM while also limiting the scope for problematic measurement error arising from endogenous entry and exit of weather stations over time. I aggregate grid cells’ temperatures to county-average temperature, weighting cells by the population of their corresponding Census tracts in 1990 (in the base specification) or by area (in a robustness check).

I use four weather variables that follow ref. 34: the number of days in which a county’s population-weighted average temperature was below 20°F (< −6.7°C, “cold”), in 20 to 50°F (−6.7 to 10°C, “cool”), in 70 to 80°F (21.1 to 26.7°C, “warm”), and above 80°F (>26.7°C, “hot”). The number of days in 50 to 70°F (10 to 21.1°C) is the excluded category. These breakpoints are near the 5th, 40th, 75th, and 95th percentiles of the distribution of days in U.S. counties from 1951 to 2020 (34). I account for precipitation insofar as it is correlated with temperature, but I do not model precipitation directly, in part because climate models are not as skillful at simulating or attributing changes in precipitation (62, 63).

Climate model data come from the Detection and Attribution Model Intercomparison Project (DAMIP), which is part of the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6). I use the nine climate models that were accessible: CanESM5, CNRM-CM6-1, GFDL-ESM4, HadGEM3-GC31-LL, IPSL-CM6A-LR, MRI-ESM2-0, ACCESS-ESM1-5, MIROC6, and NorESM2-LM. The “hist-nat” experiments include only natural influences on the climate, in the form of solar variability and volcanic output (64).

I bias-correct model output following prior work (25, 65). First, for each of the nine climate models, I bilinearly interpolate model output to the Census tract level and compare its “hist” historical run to observed temperature in each Census tract over 2000 to 2019. For each month, I construct the average difference between observed temperature data and hist model temperature in that month. I also rank-order each day’s residual temperature within each month and then regress the resulting series of rank-ordered daily observations on the rank-ordered daily model output. This regression yields a coefficient that transforms modeled weather deviations for a given month into observed weather deviations. I then bring the months’ coefficients and the average differences over months to the hist-nat data, where I add the average month difference onto month-average temperature and then add the residual transformed by the coefficient from the rank-ordered regression. I thereby obtain a hist-nat series that is stripped of that model’s average biases at the monthly level and has variation among the days within a month similar to that observed in actual data. The difference between the average of the bias-corrected hist-nat simulations and historical data reflects the influence of humans on the climate, primarily through addition of carbon dioxide and other greenhouse gases. Finally, I aggregate Census tracts’ temperatures to the county level based on their population in 1990 (in the base specification) or on their area (in a robustness check). SI Appendix, Supporting Information H provides additional background on bias correction and depicts the corrections by climate model.

I employ two additional datasets in robustness checks for the construction of nonlocal temperature. First, I use trade data from the U.S. Bureau of Transportation Statistics Freight Analysis Framework (version 5.6.1) to weight counties based on the value of trade between them in 2017 (66). The trade data are at the state level. I drop all same-state trade and allocate trade value equally across all county pairs within a pair of states. Second, I use the county-to-county Social Connectedness Index to weight counties based on their number of Facebook connections, as a share of the counties’ total active Facebook users, as of April 2016 (67, 68).

Statistical Analysis.

In order to operationalize Eq. 1, I must estimate the coefficients λin, γins, Λin, and Γins. I recover these from the following regression:[2]Δlnyit=∑n=1N∑j=1JλnjXij[Δwitn]+∑n=1N∑j=1J∑ℓ=15γnjℓXij[Δwi(t−ℓ)n]+∑n=1N∑j=1JΛnjXijΔWitn+∑n=1N∑j=1J∑ℓ=15ΓnjℓXij[ΔWi(t−ℓ)n]+αi+δt+νs1t+νs2t2+ϵit,

where i indexes counties in the contiguous United States, t indexes years from 1970 to 2019, and Δ represents the difference operator. The left-hand side is the change in log income per capita from time t−1 to t. The weather variables witn capture the number of days in year t that county i’s population-weighted average temperature is in bin n. Differencing the dependent variable and the weather variables mitigates the potential for estimates to pick up spurious trends (69). I estimate distinct coefficients on each of five lags of differenced local and national weather, with SI Appendix, Supporting Information D assessing robustness to this choice. SI Appendix, Supporting Information I shows that the lags of a given differenced weather variable are not highly correlated with each other.

There are J covariates Xij interacted with the weather variables. These covariates permit heterogeneity in the effects of temperature, which corresponds to the i subscripts on the coefficients of Eq. 1. First, a constant picks up common effects of temperature. Second, a county’s average temperature over 1950 to 1979 picks up how warm and cold counties may have adapted differently to temperature. Third, a county’s average income per capita over the sample picks up how income can facilitate differential adaptation to temperature. Fourth, the log output shares of manufacturing, agriculture, and resource extraction in 2019 pick up how the composition of the economy alters its sensitivity to temperature. (I add 0.001 to each before taking the log.) Finally, latitude and longitude (along with their squares and linear interaction) pick up remaining influences from geographical location, including whether a county is close to a coast (which affects both humidity and the variability of temperature within a year). I standardize each covariate other than the constant.

I construct the index of nonlocal weather as the inverse-distance-weighted average of temperatures around the country:Witn=∑k≠i1dikwktn∑k≠i1dik,

where dik is the distance between the centroids of counties i and k. This nonlocal weather index captures the intuition that beliefs about future temperature in location i and prices in location i may be more sensitive to temperatures in nearby places than to temperatures in faraway places.

The αi are county fixed effects that absorb time-invariant differences across counties. The δt are year fixed effects that absorb US-wide economic conditions. The νs1 and νs2 capture state-specific quadratic time trends in income growth. Such trends could arise, for instance, from technological progress or migration that is unrelated to temperature and deviates from the national trend.

The ϵit is the error term. I two-way cluster SE at the year and state levels. By clustering at the year level, I allow for arbitrary correlation across all counties in the United States within a year. By clustering at the state level, I allow for arbitrary correlation across all years within each county and also for arbitrary correlation across all years between different counties in the same state. This two-way clustering assumes that a given county-year’s residuals are not correlated with the residuals of out-of-state counties in other years. SI Appendix, Supporting Information E shows that this level of clustering is more conservative than the levels used in prior literature that estimates effects of temperature in a panel of U.S. counties.

The base specification weights the regression by each county’s average log income per capita over the sample period. This weighting accounts for the possibility that measurement error is greater in lower income counties and also makes the regression estimates representative of the average unit of (log) income rather than the average county.

The λ and γ coefficients on their respective constants are identified from how temperatures (or a temperature trajectory) that were anomalous with respect to that county and year correlated with growth in income per capita that was also anomalous with respect to that county and year and was not explained by state-level trends. The Λ and Γ coefficients on their respective constants are identified from how being unusually close to counties whose temperatures were anomalous within a given year correlated with growth in income per capita that was anomalous with respect to that county and year and was not explained by state-level trends.

The coefficients on the covariates are identified by how the correlations described above vary cross-sectionally. The cross-sectional identification raises the possibility of unobserved confounding (5, 70, 71), as with other recent climate economics literature (e.g., refs. 49 and 72). That recent literature needs cross-sectional heterogeneity to be causally identified because the cross-sectionally varying characteristics evolve over time in their out-of-sample prediction exercises. In contrast, here the observed cross-sectionally varying characteristics maintain the same pattern in the counterfactual world as in the estimation sample, and it is plausible that potential unobserved confounds maintain their cross-sectional patterns in the counterfactual world as well.

All CI are calculated using the estimated covariance matrix from regression (2), accounting for potential correlations across coefficient estimates. CI for impact calculations do not account for uncertainty in climate models’ simulations, which tend to be a set of interrelated best guesses rather than independent samples from a distribution (73–76). SI Appendix, Supporting Information H shows that individual models yield estimates that are generally consistent with each other.
