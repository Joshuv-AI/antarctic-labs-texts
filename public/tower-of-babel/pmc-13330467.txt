Analysis and interpretation of the heterogeneity of community-based health insurance attributes and preferences in Senegal: evidence from a discrete choice experiment
Authors: Oumar Sagna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCommunity-based health insurance (CBHI) schemes have been widely promoted to improve financial protection and expand access to healthcare in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), particularly among populations working in the informal sector who lack access to formal health insurance. In Sub-Saharan Africa, CBHI has been implemented as part of broader efforts to advance universal health coverage (UHC), yet enrolment often remains low. In Senegal, CBHI schemes aim to expand insurance coverage among informal sector populations, but participation remains limited and uneven across geographic and socioeconomic groups. Evidence on how specific insurance design attributes influence enrolment decisions remains scarce. This study examines population preferences for CBHI attributes and their implications for scheme uptake.MethodsA discrete choice experiment (DCE) was conducted with 912 households in the Ziguinchor region using a stratified two-stage sampling approach. First, communities were selected across rural and urban areas. Second, households were randomly selected within each selected community. Lists of households were obtained from local administrative records, and respondents were randomly chosen from these lists. Interviews were conducted with household heads or adult household representatives. Prior to the main survey, the questionnaire and choice tasks were pilot-tested with a small sample of households to ensure clarity and comprehension. Mixed logit models were used to estimate preferences for six CBHI attributes, and policy simulations predicted enrolment under alternative scheme designs.ResultsChronic disease coverage (OR = 61.2; 95% CI: 46.5–81.7), transport availability (OR = 24.3; 95% CI: 17.1–33.1), and flexible payment options (OR = 6.0; 95% CI: 3.9–9.2) were the strongest determinants of enrolment. Significant heterogeneity in preferences was observed across population groups. Compared with urban households, rural households placed greater importance on transport availability and flexible payment mechanisms. Similarly, compared with high-income households, low-income households showed stronger preferences for flexible payment options, while high-income respondents preferred more comprehensive benefit packages. Scenario simulations predicted enrolment gains from 76.53% under the baseline DECAM model to 97.81% under an optimized scheme including chronic disease coverage, transport support, and flexible payment options. Willingness-to-pay also varied across income groups and locations.ConclusionsDesigning CBHI schemes around user preferences significantly improves predicted uptake and equity. Rather than uniform models, differentiated and preference-aligned insurance designs can drive substantial increases in enrolment and equity. Tailored insurance models that incorporate chronic disease services, address transport barriers, and allow flexible payment modalities are more likely to achieve inclusive enrolment. The inclusion of high-income households offers an opportunity for financial sustainability through cross-subsidization. These results offer actionable insights for Senegal and similar low-resource settings pursuing universal health coverage (UHC) through community-based mechanisms.Keywords: Community-Based Health Insurance, Discrete Choice Experiment, Health Financing, Universal Health Coverage, Willingness to Pay, Senegal, Informal Sector

Introduction

Community-based health insurance (CBHI) schemes have increasingly been promoted as important policy instruments for improving financial protection and expanding access to healthcare in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [1, 2]. In many LMICs, a large proportion of the population works in the informal sector and therefore lacks access to formal social health insurance systems.

As a result, households often rely heavily on out-of-pocket payments for healthcare, which can expose them to significant financial risks and limit access to essential health services. CBHI schemes have been introduced in several countries as a mechanism to pool risks and provide financial protection for populations that are otherwise excluded from formal insurance systems.

In Sub-Saharan Africa, CBHI has been widely promoted as part of broader strategies aimed at advancing universal health coverage (UHC) [3, 4]. Several countries have implemented CBHI initiatives to improve access to healthcare among informal sector populations and rural communities. However, despite these efforts, enrolment rates in many CBHI schemes remain relatively low, and participation levels vary considerably across regions and socioeconomic groups [4, 5]. Previous studies have highlighted multiple barriers to enrolment, including affordability constraints, limited awareness, perceived quality of care, and design features of insurance schemes that do not fully reflect user preferences.

In Senegal, where a large proportion of the population is employed in the informal sector and lacks access to formal social health insurance [6], CBHI represents a key policy instrument for advancing toward universal health coverage. Despite strong political commitment and institutional support, enrolment levels remain relatively low, with substantial disparities between rural areas (1.9%) and urban areas (11%) [7, 8]. These disparities highlight the need to better understand the factors influencing households’ decisions to enrol in CBHI schemes.

Despite the growing importance of CBHI in Senegal’s health financing strategy, limited empirical evidence exists on how specific insurance design attributes influence households’ decisions to enrol in CBHI schemes. In particular, little is known about the extent to which preferences for different insurance characteristics vary across population groups. Understanding such heterogeneity in preferences is essential for designing CBHI models that are both effective and equitable.

To address this gap, this study applies a Discrete Choice Experiment (DCE) to examine heterogeneity in preferences for key CBHI attributes among different population groups in Senegal. By identifying the relative importance of different insurance design features, the study aims to inform the design of more responsive and inclusive CBHI policies that can improve enrolment and contribute to progress toward universal health coverage.

Methods

Study setting and population

The study was conducted in Senegal, a lower-middle-income country located in West Africa with a population of approximately 18.1 million inhabitants and an annual population growth rate of about 2.9%. The country has a relatively young population, with more than 60% under the age of 25 years. Poverty remains a major challenge, affecting about 37.5% of the population, with higher prevalence in rural areas (53.6%) compared to urban areas (19.8%).

The health system in Senegal is organized in a pyramidal structure comprising three levels: the central level responsible for policy and regulation, the regional level coordinating health services, and the district level responsible for service delivery. Health services in Senegal are provided by a mix of public, private, and faith-based providers. The public sector plays a dominant role in primary healthcare delivery, particularly in rural areas, while private providers are more concentrated in urban settings. Access to healthcare services also varies significantly between urban and rural areas. Urban populations generally benefit from better availability of healthcare infrastructure, specialized services, and private providers. In contrast, rural populations often face multiple barriers to accessing healthcare, including longer travel distances to health facilities, transportation constraints, and shortages of qualified health personnel. The country had 79 health districts, 114 health centers, and more than 1,500 health posts serving as the backbone of primary health care delivery.

Health financing in Senegal relies on multiple sources including government funding, external partners, private sector contributions and households. According to national health accounts, household out-of-pocket expenditures remain a major source of health financing. Household spending represented about 41.8% of total health expenditure in 2022 and 43.0% in 2023, while public administration contributed around 19.8% and 21.3% respectively, and external partners accounted for approximately 22% and 23% of total health spending.

The Ziguinchor region was selected for this study because it represents a relevant policy setting for evaluating community-based health insurance initiative and is characterized by significant socioeconomic challenges. The region combines high poverty levels, low health insurance coverage, and ongoing efforts to expand CBHI schemes through both DECAM and UDAM models, making it an appropriate context to examine population preferences for insurance design attributes and potential enrolment responses, indeed the region has a population of over half a million people and exhibits one of the highest poverty rates in the country, with approximately 60% of residents living below the national poverty line. Health insurance coverage in the region remains particularly low, with more than 97% of the population lacking any form of health insurance.

A total of 912 households were selected for the study using a stratified two-stage sampling design to ensure representation of both rural and urban populations. In the first stage, communities were selected using stratified sampling based on their rural or urban classification. This stratification ensured adequate representation of different geographic contexts and socioeconomic conditions within the study area. In the second stage, households were randomly selected within each selected community. Lists of households were obtained from local administrative records, and respondents were randomly chosen from these lists. Interviews were conducted with household heads or adult household representatives.

Description of the two CBHI interventions

In response to these challenges, the Government of Senegal launched major reforms in 2012 aimed at expanding health insurance coverage among populations in the informal sector. Two CBHI models were introduced as part of this reform process: the DECAM1 and UDAM2 programs. These initiatives sought to strengthen community-based insurance through the development of mutual health organizations (MHOs) targeting both rural and urban populations.

The DECAM strategy, funded by USAID and WHO, operates under a decentralized model that emphasizes local governance, whereby each municipality is encouraged to host at least one MHO. These are managed by elected community volunteers and rely on passive premium collection mechanisms. Healthcare providers under DECAM are reimbursed on a fee-for-service basis.

In contrast, the UDAM model, supported by the Belgian Technical Cooperation Agency through the PAODES project, adopts a more centralized and professionalized approach. It establishes departmental insurance units across multiple municipalities, managed by a board of professionals including medical and financial experts. Premiums are actively collected by community agents, and provider payments are made through a capitation system [9].

Following the pilot phase (2012–2015), policymakers considered scaling up these models to improve universal health coverage (UHC) in the Ziguinchor region—home to over half a million people, with 60% living below the poverty line and 97.3% lacking health insurance coverage [7].

DCE design

The identification of attributes and their levels results from a wide literature review [10–12], discussions with the Ministry of Health expert committee3 and in-depth interviews with 40 key informants. Six attributes were selected for inclusion in the DCE: unit of enrollment (nuclear, extended family, community group), benefit package (comprehensive with or without chronic diseases), copayment levels (50%, 25%), transport availability, payment modality (cash, in-kind, combined), and annual premium level. These attributes were chosen based on a literature review and local context analysis. Initially, eleven attributes were identified: unit of enrolment, health services benefit package, copayment, transport, payment modality, premium level per person per year, frequency of premium payment, access to health facilities, governance modalities of the MHO, the management and initiative of MHO creation, and the geographical coverage of the MHO (Table 1).

Recognizing the cognitive challenges typically associated with discrete choice experiments (DCEs), especially among populations with high illiteracy rates, the DCE questionnaire was presented predominantly through visual images to facilitate comprehension (Fig. 1).

Example of a choice set (Version 1 of the questionnaire, question 18)

A pilot study was conducted in the Goudomp Health District (Sédhiou region), chosen for its socio-economic and demographic similarity to the study area. Thirty households from the informal sector participated, equally divided between rural and urban areas (n = 15 each).

The pilot study focused on testing the sequence and placement of visual images, measuring the average completion time and identifying the threshold for respondent fatigue, and verifying the internal validity of the questionnaire. Internal validity was assessed through two strategies:

Preference Monotonicity: Each questionnaire version included a deliberately dominant choice set (Choice Set #7). All respondents selected the dominant option, demonstrating the expected logical consistency. Following this, the dominant choice set was removed from the final version.

Preference Monotonicity: Each questionnaire version included a deliberately dominant choice set (Choice Set #7). All respondents selected the dominant option, demonstrating the expected logical consistency. Following this, the dominant choice set was removed from the final version.

Stability of Preferences: The same choice set (Choice Sets #1 and #8) was repeated to evaluate consistency. Identical responses between the two sets indicated stable preferences among respondents.

All respondents confirmed that the use of visual images significantly enhanced their understanding of the DCE tasks. However, they recommended adjustments to the sequence of images and minor modifications to the illustrations to improve clarity. These suggestions were incorporated into the final questionnaire version to optimize respondent engagement and data quality.

Data collection

Data were collected between June and September 2015 by trained enumerators using a structured questionnaire combined with a visual discrete choice experiment (DCE) instrument designed to facilitate comprehension among respondents with low literacy levels. Prior to the main survey, a pilot test was conducted in a small sample of households in a community with similar characteristics to the study area. The pilot allowed refinement of the questionnaire, testing of the DCE choice tasks, and assessment of respondents’ understanding of the attributes and levels included in the experiment. Enumerators underwent intensive training before data collection, which included sessions on the objectives of the study, ethical considerations, interview techniques, and detailed instructions on administering the DCE tasks. The training also included role-playing exercises and field practice to ensure standardization of procedures across interviewers. Visual aids were used to illustrate the attributes and levels of the insurance schemes, helping respondents better understand the hypothetical scenarios. During the interview, enumerators explained each attribute using visual cards before presenting the choice tasks. Respondents were then asked to select their preferred option in each choice scenario. The design and implementation of the DCE followed methodological recommendations described in previous studies, including Abiiro et al. (2014).

Face-to-face interviews were conducted at respondents’ households to ensure clarity of communication and to assist respondents when needed. Before each interview, enumerators explained the purpose of the study and obtained informed consent from participants.

In addition to the DCE choice tasks, the questionnaire collected information on respondents’ socio-demographic and economic characteristics, including age, gender, education level, marital status, household size, occupation, and household assets used as proxies for socioeconomic status. Data were also collected on respondents’ previous experience with health services and health insurance schemes. These variables were used to describe the study population and to explore heterogeneity in preferences during the econometric analysis.

Data analysis

The NGENE software was used to generate an orthogonal optimal design (OOD) [13], which consisted of 18 choice sets to assess the main effects plus one interaction effect between two attributes: health service benefit package and premium level per year. The 18 choice sets were randomly assigned into three versions, each containing six choice sets. Each choice set (Fig. 1) offered respondents a choice between two unlabeled alternatives and one opt-out option (no insurance), as enrolment in MOHs is not compulsory. The analysis from choices made in DCEs was carried out using random utility theory (RUT), developed by McFadden (1974) [14]. The probability that an individual chooses a specific model of an MOH corresponds to the probability that the utility derived from that option exceeds the utility derived from all other available alternatives. Uptake rates were predicted by applying the logit model across various combinations of attribute levels. Policy simulations were conducted to predict uptake under alternative scenarios.

Descriptive statistics

Of the 912 respondents, 50.4% were urban residents and 49.6% were rural. The median age was 48.1 years. A large proportion (42%) had no formal education. Most households (70%) earned less than 75,000 FCFA per month. Furthermore, 61.8% of households lived more than one hour from the nearest health facility, underscoring the importance of transportation as a barrier to access (Table 2).

Analysis by residence subgroup

Subgroup mixed logit models were estimated to examine differences in preferences between rural and urban respondents. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) are presented in Table 3

Among rural respondents (n = 452; 2,712 observations), the availability of transport for individuals living more than one hour from the nearest health facility showed the strongest association with enrolment preferences (OR = 11.01; 95% CI: 8.16–14.86). Flexible payment modalities were also strongly associated with enrolment preferences, particularly the combined in-kind and cash payment option (OR = 8.19; 95% CI: 6.96–9.64) and in-kind only payment (OR = 6.70; 95% CI: 5.68–7.90). Chronic disease coverage across all levels of the health system was also associated with higher enrolment preferences (OR = 5.87; 95% CI: 3.28–10.51).

Among urban respondents (n = 460; 2,760 observations), chronic disease coverage across all levels of the health system showed the strongest association with enrolment preferences (OR = 12.48; 95% CI: 10.22–15.24). Transport availability for individuals living more than one hour from the nearest health facility was also associated with enrolment preferences (OR = 6.78; 95% CI: 5.07–9.06). The combined in-kind and cash payment modality was associated with higher enrolment preferences (OR = 2.61; 95% CI: 2.17–3.14).

For co-payment levels, both groups showed higher enrolment preferences for lower cost-sharing. In rural areas, a 25% co-payment was associated with an OR of 1.67 (95% CI: 1.27–2.20), while no co-payment had an OR of 2.27 (95% CI: 1.77–2.92). In urban areas, the corresponding ORs were 1.58 (95% CI: 1.24–2.01) and 1.87 (95% CI: 1.48–2.37), respectively.

Interaction terms between premium level and chronic disease coverage across all levels of the health system were statistically significant in both groups (rural coefficient = 0.00120; p < 0.01; urban coefficient = 0.00094; p < 0.01).

Discussion

The findings of this study reinforce the strategic necessity of designing community-based health insurance (CBHI) schemes that are attuned to both the expressed preferences and socio-economic realities of target populations. Unlike generic one-size-fits-all approaches, our results underscore the importance of context-specific attributes—notably benefit content, transport availability, and payment modalities—that reflect real-world constraints and priorities.

A particularly striking result was the overwhelming importance of chronic disease coverage in shaping enrolment decisions. This preference, quantified with an odds ratio (OR) of 61.2 (95% CI: 46.5–81.7), not only confirms prior evidence from Abiiro et al. [17] and De Allegri et al. [18] but also sets a new benchmark for effect size in discrete choice studies of CBHI. The magnitude of this effect suggests that populations, even in resource-limited settings, are acutely aware of the long-term implications of chronic illness and are willing to prioritize schemes that offer financial protection for these conditions. Our integration of this attribute into scenario-based policy simulations further demonstrates its transformative potential: inclusion of chronic disease coverage contributed significantly to predicted enrolment gains in optimized models.

Transport availability emerged as another powerful determinant of insurance uptake, particularly among rural residents where physical access to health services remains a primary barrier. The OR of 24.3 (95% CI: 17.1–33.1) highlights that transport is more than a convenience; it is a structural enabler of service utilization. This aligns with findings from Criel et al. [19] and Basaza et al. [20] yet our study adds granularity by illustrating that this effect is not uniform across all socio-economic groups. It is especially potent among those geographically marginalized, suggesting that policy responses such as transportation stipends, mobile clinics, or embedded logistics support may yield high returns in coverage expansion.

Moreover, we found that flexible payment modalities allowing a mix of cash and in-kind contributions substantially increase the likelihood of enrolment, particularly among low-income and agriculturally dependent households. With an OR of 6.0 (95% CI: 3.9–9.2), this attribute highlights a pragmatic pathway for enhancing affordability without necessarily lowering premiums. This supports and expands on findings by Panda et al. [21] and Onwujekwe et al. [22], but our study is among the few to quantify this effect in West African settings using a nationally representative experimental design. These results stress that “how to pay” is as critical as “how much to pay”, especially in informal economies where liquidity is unpredictable.

While price sensitivity remains relevant particularly for middle-income groups the deterrent effect of higher premiums (OR = 0.59; 95% CI: 0.43–0.75) appears to be moderated by perceived value-for-money. In other words, individuals are less resistant to paying more when benefits are meaningful and tangible. This finding reinforces recommendations from Adebayo et al. [23] and supports a policy shift away from blanket premium subsidies toward strategic benefit enhancements that improve perceived value.

Another unique contribution of this study is the documentation of strong demand for comprehensive service and logistical support among high-income groups, a demographic often overlooked in CBHI literature. Our stratified analysis revealed an OR of 72.5 (95% CI: 52.3–94.1) for comprehensive coverage and 14.3 (95% CI: 10.1–18.6) for transport access in this subgroup. These unexpectedly high values suggest that CBHI is not exclusively relevant to the poor; rather, with appropriate design, such schemes can also attract wealthier individuals, creating opportunities for cross-subsidization and financial sustainability. This contradicts the common perception of CBHI as a “poverty-targeted” mechanism and open the door to broader, voluntary participation across socio-economic tiers [24–26]. While our study suggests that with appropriate design, CBHI schemes can attract wealthier individuals, the prevailing literature emphasizes that CBHI has traditionally been a mechanism to improve healthcare access for the poor. The limited participation of high-income groups in CBHI programs is often attributed to perceptions of inadequate service quality and limited benefits. Therefore, to broaden the appeal of CBHI across socio-economic tiers, significant adjustments in scheme design, benefit packages, and service quality may be necessary. These findings challenge the notion of CBHI as a poverty-targeted tool, instead positioning it as a potentially universal mechanism that can harness cross-subsidization.

In addition, our findings demonstrate clear heterogeneity in WTP for CBHI schemes based on income levels and geographic location. Higher-income households reported significantly greater WTP, with the high-income group reaching a median of 3,350 FCFA, compared to only 2,395 FCFA in the low-income group.

This underscores the direct relationship between purchasing power and financial engagement with health insurance. These findings are consistent with a growing body of global evidence on the heterogeneity of health insurance preferences. Like the results observed by Abiiro et al. [17] and De Allegri et al. [18] in Malawi, this study confirms that low-income and rural populations tend to express lower WTP, not necessarily due to undervaluing insurance, but due to constrained financial capacity. Likewise, Onwujekwe et al. [22] in Nigeria highlighted the importance of affordability mechanisms for rural households, recommending tiered pricing to improve coverage equity.

Furthermore, the observed urban-rural disparity echoes findings by Jehu-Appiah et al. [23] in Ghana and Basaza et al. [20] in Uganda, where higher urban WTP was associated with proximity to service providers and better information about insurance benefits.

These findings are also consistent with previous studies in Ethiopia [27] and Ghana [28], which reported positive associations between income and WTP, reflecting the income elasticity of demand for health insurance. These comparisons suggest a need for premium models that account for income variability to enhance equity and affordability. These disparities highlight the risks of a flat premium model, which may disproportionately affect vulnerable groups. Instead, tiered contributions, income-based subsidies, and geographically tailored interventions are needed to promote equity and improve uptake.

However, evidence from Burkina Faso suggests that willingness-to-pay (WTP) for community-based health insurance can vary depending on contextual factors. While some studies have shown that WTP may remain relatively high among lower-income groups when trust in the system and perceived quality of care is strong [29], De Allegri et al. reported lower WTP in more remote rural areas, highlighting the importance of service availability as a key determinant [30].

Geographic differences were also marked. Urban informal sector populations showed higher WTP than their rural counterparts, likely reflecting better access to health services and more consistent exposure to the benefits of insurance. These findings suggest that WTP is not uniformly distributed, and that the design and financing of CBHI schemes must be tailored to local realities. Similar patterns were reported in Kenya [31], where urban respondents were more willing to enroll due to better health infrastructure and information availability. These results indicate that spatial disparities should be addressed through context-specific policy responses.

The policy implications are substantial: a uniform premium model may unintentionally disadvantage rural and low-income populations. Instead, policymakers are advised to implement progressive contribution models, possibly including income-sensitive premiums or targeted subsidies, and to introduce localized interventions such as increased service availability in rural areas—to stimulate equitable enrolment. These recommendations align with international best practices and findings from multi-country analyses from Spaan et al., which advocate for flexibility and equity in health financing strategies to enhance universal health coverage outcomes [32].

These parallels support the view that geographic access, trust in service quality, and the economic environment are central determinants of WTP in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).

The observed heterogeneity in willingness-to-pay (WTP) across income and geographic lines underscores the need for differentiated policy interventions. A uniform premium structure may exacerbate inequities by placing a disproportionate financial burden on lower-income and rural populations. Policymakers should consider implementing progressive contribution mechanisms, such as income-based subsidies or tiered premiums, to ensure affordability while maintaining the financial sustainability of CBHI schemes. In addition, geographically tailored strategies such as increasing health service availability in rural areas or strengthening community awareness may improve the perceived value of insurance and stimulate broader enrolment across diverse contexts.

In summary, this study not only aligns with but also strengthens international evidence advocating for equity-oriented CBHI reform, reinforcing the call for differentiated premium policies and localized delivery models to bridge the gaps in WTP and enrollment.

From a methodological perspective, this study offers several innovations. The use of a discrete choice experiment (DCE) allowed for the rigorous quantification of trade-offs among competing attributes, while the incorporation of visual aids and pictorial choice sets ensured accessibility in low-literacy settings a rarely used approach in similar West African studies. Moreover, the income- and residence-stratified analyses enabled us to capture important preference heterogeneity, providing critical evidence for segmented policy strategies that move beyond a universal design toward more tailored insurance models. Altogether, our findings support a paradigm shift from standardized CBHI schemes to flexible, preference aligned designs. The simulation results showing predicted uptake levels as high as 97.81% under optimal configurations highlight the powerful implications of incorporating population preferences into benefit design. These findings are particularly salient for Senegalese health authorities and other LMIC policymakers seeking to expand health coverage in a sustainable, equitable manner. We recommend that future reforms prioritize the inclusion of chronic disease coverage, operational support for transport, and payment modality flexibility as foundational pillars of any inclusive and scalable CBHI strategy.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings.

First, the discrete choice experiment (DCE) relies on stated preferences derived from hypothetical scenarios, which may not perfectly reflect actual enrolment behavior in real-world settings. However, DCE methods are widely used in health economics to estimate individual preferences and policy trade-offs and provide valuable insights into how individuals may respond to alternative insurance designs.

Second, although the study sample was designed to capture both rural and urban households, the data were collected in a specific regional context. As a result, the findings may not be fully generalizable to all regions of Senegal or to other low- and middle-income countries with different health system structures.

Third, while the experimental design included several key attributes of community-based health insurance schemes, other contextual determinants of enrolment decisions, such as trust in institutions, past experiences with insurance programs, or perceptions of service quality, may also influence participation but were not directly captured in the choice experiment.

Despite these limitations, the study provides robust evidence on population preferences for CBHI design features and offers valuable insights for policy development aimed at improving enrolment and equity in health insurance schemes.

Conclusion

This study makes a compelling case for rethinking the design of community-based health insurance (CBHI) schemes in Senegal and similar LMIC settings. By rigorously quantifying user preferences through a discrete choice experiment and validating predicted uptake through scenario modeling, we demonstrate that insurance enrolment decisions are not merely cost-sensitive but are also deeply shaped by perceptions of value, accessibility, and adaptability. The exceptionally high odds ratios associated with chronic disease coverage, transport availability, and flexible payment modalities signal that these are not peripheral considerations—they are central levers for enrolment and retention. Notably, the observed magnitude of these effects exceeds those reported in comparable studies, suggesting that policy responsiveness to such preferences could unlock unprecedented gains in coverage. Moreover, the study reveals a nuanced segmentation of preferences across income and residence groups, challenging the notion that CBHI should exclusively target the poor. High-income respondents’ willingness to pay for convenience and comprehensiveness opens the door for voluntary cross-subsidization, which could enhance both financial viability and equity.

Importantly, these findings arrive at a pivotal time when Senegal and other countries are advancing toward universal health coverage. Our results provide timely, evidence-based guidance for designing CBHI models that are not only technically sound but also socially aligned. Rather than pursuing uniform schemes, future reforms should prioritize tailored benefit structures, logistical enablers, and adaptive contribution models that reflect the lived realities of diverse population groups. These insights are crucial for enhancing financial protection and ensuring sustainable enrolment, particularly among vulnerable and underserved groups. We recommend that future CBHI reforms prioritize chronic disease coverage, transport support, flexible payment options, and differentiated premium structures as core pillars of inclusive health financing in Senegal and similar LMIC contexts. By placing user preferences at the center of scheme design, this study advances both the scientific literature and the practical toolkit available to policymakers. It contributes a scalable methodological template for similar settings and highlights a path toward more inclusive, acceptable, and sustainable insurance systems that leave no one behind.
