Creation, implementation, and evaluation of an OER forensic chemistry CURE using high impact practices
Authors: Shainaz Landge, Debanjana Ghosh, Vijayalakshmi Ramasamy, Elizabeth C. Sargent
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The goal of this project was to develop and evaluate free, online Open-Educational Resource (OER) content for a forensic chemistry course, aligned with the Course-based-Undergraduate Research Experience (CURE) model of instruction. Through this project, we created supplementary materials including case studies, forensic chemistry lab techniques, and demo videos. The course also provided a platform for the students to interact with forensic experts. Nine high impact practices were incorporated and aligned with CURE. The materials covered in the course are hosted on the LibGuides platform for equitable access. While student perceptions of OER material quality varied, they were strongly correlated with student experience. Additional attitudinal results suggest student self-efficacy significantly increased in response to participation in this OER forensic chemistry CURE. Instructors who wish to adopt or adapt these course materials should provide structured support for digital tools so all students find them accessible, maximizing the effectiveness of this OER CURE.

Introduction

Undergraduate research experiences, including Course-based Undergraduate Research Experiences (CUREs), are widely recognized as high-impact educational practices that enhance student engagement, scientific thinking, and retention in STEM disciplines [1,2]. While traditional research experiences, such as faculty-mentored independent projects, are often limited to a small subset of students due to resource constraints and accessibility barriers [3], CUREs offer an inclusive alternative. By integrating authentic research directly into the curriculum, CUREs offer all students opportunities to engage in scientific inquiry, data analysis, and critical thinking within a structured course setting [4]. Consequently, they are particularly effective at fostering scientific identity and developing key professional skills in students [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9]].

Open Educational Resources (OER) further enhance the accessibility and equity of CURE-based instruction. OERs are freely available, high-quality teaching and learning materials that reduce financial barriers for students while promoting the dissemination of innovative pedagogical practices [[10], [11], [12]] and increasing student success [12,13]. The intentional integration of CURE-based instruction with OER materials offers a scalable, cost-effective model that supports all learners and fosters an inclusive academic environment [14].

To maximize these educational benefits, modern STEM curricula can intentionally layer multiple High-Impact Practices (HIPs) to improve student success [12,15,16]. The forensic chemistry course discussed here was designed to systematically incorporate nine distinct high-impact and professional practices: OERs, CUREs, collaborative assignments and projects, common intellectual experiences, research paper writing, poster presentations, oral presentations, real-life experience for solving crime, and meeting real scientists and investigators throughout the course. Blending these practices within an upper-level Forensic Chemistry course moves the curriculum beyond the limitations of traditional "cookbook" laboratory exercises and builds student confidence and capacity to hypothesize, review literature, engage in iterative experimentation, collaborate on data analysis, and generate meaningful scientific conclusions grounded in real-world applications.

Herein, we describe the development, implementation, and evaluation of a novel, OER-integrated forensic chemistry CURE instructional model, which cohesively combines the merits of authentic research with equitable access. The instructional materials provide a free, customizable resource designed to promote equity and serve as a blueprint for future course offerings.

Course context and design

[Omitted For Peer Review] University is a comprehensive Research (R2) Primarily Undergraduate Institution (PUI) and one of the largest universities in [Omitted for Peer Review]. While student interest in forensic science careers is growing rapidly among incoming freshmen, many students face significant financial roadblocks. The inability to afford textbooks and course supplies creates immense pressure on academic success, negatively impacting students' growth mindsets and sense of belonging [17,18]. To address this, we developed a specialized Forensic Chemistry course (Omitted for Peer Review) utilizing Open Educational Resources (OER) hosted on the university LibGuides platform [19]. This course serves chemistry, biochemistry, and biology majors, is offered face-to-face once per academic year, and requires prerequisites in general chemistry, analytical chemistry, and organic chemistry. As in a traditional forensic chemistry course, students are first introduced to the foundations of the discipline, including core chemical principles, forensic law, and analytical methodologies using traditional lecture formats and OER materials. These foundational topics align with our first three Student Learning Outcomes (SLOs):

SLO1: Apply foundational principles of chemistry and forensic science, including spectroscopy, spectrometry, chromatography, and sample preparation, to the analysis of physical evidence.

SLO2: Describe proper techniques for crime scene sketching, evidence collection, and documentation in accordance with legal and ethical standards.

SLO3: Analyze the chemical and physical properties of drugs, fingerprints, combustion, arson, explosives, and firearms to classify and interpret forensic evidence.

The CURE component then shifts students into an experiential, collaborative environment. Students immerse themselves in a real-life experience for solving crime by visiting a mocked-up crime scene to gather physical evidence following faculty-led investigative demonstrations (Fig. 1). Within this unit, students also utilize OER modules and video tours of crime scenes to catalogue evidence, document sample collection techniques, and guide their downstream sample analysis. SLO4: Design and execute a research-driven experimental plan as part of a Course-based Undergraduate Research Experience (CURE), incorporating prior findings, laboratory protocols, and necessary materials.

SLO5: Communicate research findings effectively through written, visual, and oral presentations, demonstrating an understanding of forensic methodologies and their broader implications.

To round out their professional development, students translate their laboratory findings into scholarly CURE deliverables through formal Research Paper Writing, a peer-reviewed Poster Presentation, and an Oral Presentation. This entire trajectory is contextualized through meetings with real scientists and investigators throughout the course, allowing students to interact with active professionals and see the direct, real-world impact of their academic work.

While it was not an original learning outcome associated with the pilot of this CURE, future instructors should consider adding an additional SLO related to student safety in the laboratory, such as “demonstrate an understanding of good laboratory practices and safety guidelines” as this is a critical component of a successful research experience.

Course schedule and modules

The forensic chemistry CURE was co-directed by two faculty members with research expertise in synthetic organic and analytical chemistry. This interdisciplinary leadership allowed the curriculum to bridge the chemical-biological interface, while simultaneously emphasizing forensic evidence recovery, data analysis, and hands-on instrumental troubleshooting (see Supplementary Materials for complete syllabus with course schedule). Students routinely operated a diverse suite of instrumentation introduced during lectures, including ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) spectroscopy, fluorescence spectroscopy, liquid chromatography, Raman spectroscopy, thin-layer chromatography (TLC), and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (see Supplementary Materials for General Consideration and Instrumentation used) depending on the final research project they chose. As deliverables, students authored a final research report styled as an American Chemical Society (ACS) manuscript, (a template was provided on forensic chemistry library guides), delivered an in-class oral defense, and presented their findings at a college-wide research symposium.

The course also heavily integrated Remote Mentoring of Undergraduate Students (ReMentURS) modules [20] paired with online quizzes to reinforce foundational knowledge and keep students engaged with the material. These online quizzes served as low-stakes, formative assessments that ensured students comprehend core concepts, such as information and data literacy as well as specific forensic science domains (e.g., fingerprinting, DNA, and drug analysis) before applying them to their CURE projects.

The 15-week curriculum was structured to scaffold student transition from foundational forensic concepts to independent investigators. Week 1 introduced disciplinary principles, and pedagogical expectations through lectures and with complementary readings guiding students to distinguish laboratory analysis from crime scene evaluation. Week 2 explored professional duties via the "Role of a Forensic Scientist" lecture and job description readings. During this week students also formed their CURE research teams using the Student Agreement Form for Group Projects (Supplementary Materials S1) and completed a literature quiz requiring them to locate and upload two primary, two secondary, and two tertiary sources to support their projects. Week 3 focused on scientific communication and included an Ink Analysis laboratory. During this week students also navigated literature using "THE ACRES" poster (Supplementary Materials Fig. S1), a sample peer-reviewed publication, the "Knowing the Plot of the Paper" worksheet, and the ReMentURS "How to Read a Peer-Reviewed Publication" template before drafting an initial abstract via the "Writing Impressive Abstracts (iABSTRACT)" video and template. Week 4 established research mechanics through an introductory audio lecture with a reflective statement, the “Basics of Science Literature Searches" resources, and EndNote online account setup tutorials, culminating in students uploading eight peer-reviewed publications for their project. Week 5 integrated evidence collection via "Crime Scene" video modules (Fig. 1) and account creation for ChemDraw and SciFinder; groups also applied a "Thinking about Titles" tutorial to deliver a 3-min research project presentation and submit two questions for a forensic scientist via a shared group document (Supplementary Materials S2).

In week 6 the curriculum shifts toward experimental methodology, data acquisition, and initial manuscript drafting with a biochemical "Fingerprint Techniques" lecture and Latent Evidence laboratory as well as continued project logistics; students submit technical forensic questions and present a 3-min group project timeline and chemical list using a Methods and Timeline Template (Supplementary Materials S3). Week 7 bridged raw data and communication via a "Making Data Bearable" lecture and a hands-on "Gummy Bear Data" Excel visualization exercise. Week 8 enhanced quantitative literacy through the "Graphing: Which graph to use?" tutorial and required teams to compile and upload their official Title, Abstract, and EndNote Bibliography into the group's assigned Manuscript Template. Week 9 provided industry insights via [Omitted for Peer Review] guest lectures on "DNA/Serology" and "Drugs on the Street," followed by an assessment quiz and the submission of CURE project's formal Procedure Section and finalized chemical/supply list into the manuscript template. Week 10 introduced formal manuscript architecture, utilizing dedicated instructional slides and a writing template to guide students in drafting their introduction sections, moving from broad forensic context down to specific experimental hypotheses and aims.

The final phase of the course emphasizes advanced forensic applications, data synthesis, and formal dissemination. Week 11 featured a toxicological "Lab on Drugs and Poisons" (Carolina Biological Supply) alongside the group submission of the finalized Materials and Methods template section to ensure reproducible documentation. Week 12 explored the anatomical and anthropological principles of Facial Reconstruction via instructional videos while introducing poster design through "Creating Scientific Posters" modules. Week 13 introduced Digital Forensics, covering the collection and analysis of electronic evidence such as metadata, network logs, and encryption. Students also analyzed and drafted their CURE results and discussion sections using dedicated slides and a standardized template. Week 14 paired a Trace Evidence Analysis laboratory with a primary dissemination milestone: presenting completed scientific posters to the broader academic community at a university-wide Annual Research Symposium. Week 15 concluded the semester with a formal oral presentation defense evaluated by the Oral Presentation Rubric (Supplementary Materials Fig. S2), individual contribution verification using the Student Lists Template (Supplementary Materials S4), and the final submission of a comprehensive, polished research manuscript formatted to American Chemical Society guideline, template already provided to students.

Student research deliverables

A core component of this CURE was the execution and presentation of original research. The inaugural cohort successfully completed the following projects: i) a study of caffeine's effects on DNA preservation, ii) analysis of different chemical degradations in saliva via Raman Spectroscopy, iii) comparative analysis of DNA collection techniques on heat-compromised surfaces, iv) determination of an unknown accelerant through GC-MS and bomb calorimetry, v) characterization of fingernail surface morphology exposed to common cleaning agents using SEM, vi) scientific analysis of trace evidence degraded by acid via ICP-MS, vii) hair identification of chemically treated hair using chromatography, and viii) determination of Effects of Nicotine in color treated hair with TLC (Supplementary Materials Fig. S3 - shown in bold). Students also did the final oral presentation on the research topics (Supplementary Materials Fig. S4).

The breadth of these research topics reflects the facilities and resources available at our institution. However, future instructors can easily implement pedagogical scaffolding [21] to limit student research to the bounds of their specific instructor expertise or institutional equipment access.

Assessment results

To evaluate the effectiveness of this instructional model, our research focused on four key objectives: 1) assess the perceived quality and academic integration of the developed OER materials based on student feedback, 2) evaluate shifts in student affective outcomes, specifically self-efficacy and science identity, following completion of the CURE, 3) monitor student content mastery and institutional retention through course performance data, and 4) characterize the relationships between OER material quality, integration, and overall student user experience. To address these objectives, we tracked course performance metrics and evaluated student feedback from two validated instruments: adapted versions of the College Student Persistence in the Sciences survey (Supplementary Materials S5) [22] and OER materials satisfaction survey (Supplementary Materials S6) [23].

Participant demographics and Institutional Review Board

The pilot version of the OER CURE was developed in Fall 2021 and delivered to an inaugural cohort of 33 undergraduate (30) and graduate (3) students in Spring 2022. Further demographic information is provided in supplemental materials Table S1. All participants signed an informed consent (see Supplementary Materials - Informed Consent form) document as authorized by the Federal Policy for the Protection of Human Subjects, Institutional Review Board (IRB), which also reviewed and approved this work as exempt from the full review ([Omitted for Peer Review] University IRB protocol H22333). All authors had access to de-identified data following the conclusion of the Spring 2022 semester.

Evaluation

Student performance was overwhelmingly positive with a course DFW rate of 0% for graduate students and 6.7% for undergraduate students; this undergraduate DFW is entirely represented by two students who withdrew from the course. All students who completed the CURE did so successfully with a grade of C or higher. Changes in self-efficacy and science identity were evaluated using a two-tailed Mann-Whitney U test to compare independent pre-intervention (n = 31) and post-intervention (n = 15) cohorts. As the evaluation of OER materials was delivered only as a post-semester survey, a one-sample Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Tests was used to compare student perceptions of OER quality, integration, and experience to a neutral benchmark (e.g., 3 on a 5-point scale). This analysis revealed that students found the quality of the OER to be significantly better than a neutral benchmark or standard textbook alternatives (mean = 3.96 vs 3.0; p = 0.002), that OER materials were explicitly integrated into assignments, lectures, and exams far beyond a neutral threshold (mean = 3.40 vs 2.5; p < 0.001), and that the digital OER format made executing day-to-day academic tasks significantly easier for the students compared to traditional alternatives (mean = 3.76 vs 3.0; p = 0.023). Discussion

Overall, the evaluation data present a coherent and mostly positive picture. Throughout the semester, students' self-efficacy increased significantly, even though their science identity remained statistically stable. Their end-of-course evaluations of the OER were positive across all three assessed areas of quality, integration, and experience, with each rated well above the neutral benchmark. The strong correlation between perceived quality and overall experience indicates that students viewed these attributes as interconnected parts of a single impression of the materials. When considered alongside course performance outcomes and measures of content mastery, these findings suggest that the OER suite supported satisfactory academic achievement and was perceived as a credible, well-integrated, and practical alternative to a commercial textbook.

It is critical to note that because all participants were exposed to the identical suite of open educational resources, the strong positive correlation between OER Quality and Student Experience does not reflect variation in the materials themselves, but rather variance in student perception. Simply making a course resource free is not enough. Since students have vastly different baseline digital literacies, some will inherently perceive an open-access platform as high-quality, while others will find it more frustrating to navigate compared to a commercial textbook. Students who struggled with digital navigation or visual clarity likely experienced higher extraneous processing, which hindered their self-reported ability to study effectively, manage time, and collaborate. Conversely, students who perceived the materials as highly enjoyable experienced a smoother workflow. For broader educational application, this highlights that merely adopting OER is insufficient; instructors must actively scaffold digital resource literacy to ensure all students perceive the materials as accessible, thereby maximizing the operational utility of the CURE.

Beyond digital literacy, the psychological outcomes of the curriculum provide deeper insight into student engagement. Self-efficacy, an individual's belief in their ability to achieve a specific goal, can serve as a strong predictor of task performance [24,25], and Course-based Undergraduate Research Experiences (CUREs) have previously been shown to positively impact this metric [7]. Similarly, science identity reflects the degree to which students relate to and feel like scientists, a factor strongly associated with long-term student persistence in STEM fields [26]. While our findings indicate this OER forensic chemistry CURE enhances student self-efficacy, no such enhancement of science identity was detected. Taken together, these improvements in student self-efficacy and OER materials ratings are encouraging indicators that students who complete this OER forensic chemistry CURE benefit not only from the reduced financial burden of zero-cost course material purchases but are also better positioned for academic success when sufficiently supported.

These localized outcomes are particularly relevant when situated within the broader challenges of higher education retention, where the "Leaky STEM Pipeline" puts students at high risk of leaving their institutions or degrees prematurely. This leak occurs at multiple points along the educational pathway, driven by a lack of engagement, inadequate support systems, and insufficient early research opportunities [27]. Despite a rapidly expanding job market and the promise of higher salaries, roughly half of students who declare a STEM major fail to graduate with a STEM degree [28]. These outcomes are worse among historically underrepresented student populations, who face systemic biases and limited access to resources [29,30]. To combat this attrition and ensure more students successfully navigate their way through the STEM pipeline, institutions must implement deliberate structural interventions. The CURE model is explicitly designed to address this by engaging students in authentic research experiences within a standard course format. Extensive literature confirms that early research exposure and access to equitable, freely available learning materials can positively influence a student's sense of belonging, confidence, and ultimate retention in science [2,12,31,32], particularly for groups historically underrepresented in STEM. By designing a forensic chemistry course that intentionally merges the CURE framework with an OER delivery platform, we provide a scalable, cost-effective model that supports student persistence, eliminates financial barriers, and serves as an adaptable, practical blueprint for instructors at other institutions.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be noted. The study tracked a single cohort in a forensic chemistry course with a small post-course sample (n = 15), less than half that of the pre-survey sample size (n = 31). This discrepancy limits our statistical power and generalizability and prevents us from conducting a paired analysis of individual learning trajectories. This attrition in response rate also introduces potential bias, as post-course respondents may represent the most engaged students. Additionally, without a traditional comparison group that did not complete the OER CURE, observed gains cannot be definitively isolated from OER CURE interventions, instructor effects, or natural student development over the semester. We also note that potential common-method bias was introduced by measuring the relationship between perceived material quality and student experience concurrently; however, the exceptionally high internal consistency of our instrument (Cronbach's Alpha > 0.9 for quality and student experience survey items) suggests students genuinely conceptualize material quality and their experience as highly integrated constructs. Future research will ideally deploy a streamlined, validated instrument across larger, more diverse samples utilizing controlled or longitudinal designs. Specifically, pairing pre- and post-course data at the individual level will clarify learning trajectories and mitigate the self-selection biases inherent in smaller samples. Finally, the finding that self-efficacy improved while science identity did not warrants further investigation. Given that science identity strongly predicts long-term STEM persistence, future studies will explore which specific CURE features best cultivate scientific identity and whether longer or more intensive research experiences are necessary to drive this growth.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that an OER-supported Course-Based Undergraduate Research Experience (CURE) in forensic chemistry can successfully replace commercial textbooks, eliminating a significant financial barrier without compromising academic rigor. Coupled with high overall course performance, students demonstrated significant gains in scientific self-efficacy over the semester. Furthermore, post-course evaluations revealed that students rated the OER materials well above neutral benchmarks for quality, course integration, and usability. Their unified judgment of material quality and user experience suggests that students do not separate what they are learning from how they are interacting with the digital platform. However, mixed spontaneous feedback during the semester underscores a critical caveat: the benefits of open educational resources depend on more than just free access. Because students perceive and navigate digital platforms differently, the success of an OER framework relies heavily on intentional digital literacy support and instructor scaffolding. Ultimately, this model offers a pragmatic, adaptable framework for STEM educators. By thoughtfully curating OER and intentionally developing students' resource literacy, educators can reduce costs, sustain high engagement, and make research-intensive learning more equitable and accessible for all students.
