Prognostic factors in resected non-small cell lung cancer with pathological N1 lymph node involvement: a survival analysis
Authors: Emrah Karcı, Fatma Mutlu, Berfin Kizildağ Aktaş, Nigar Alizada, Volkan Karaçam, Aydın Şanli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate the prognostic factors influencing survival in patients who underwent surgical treatment for early-stage non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and were found to have N1 lymph node metastasis on pathological examination.MethodsA total of 66 NSCLC patients who underwent anatomic lung resection (lobectomy, bilobectomy, or pneumonectomy) at our clinic between 2010 and 2020 and had a pathological stage of T1–2N1M0 disease were retrospectively analyzed. Clinical, demographic, histopathological, and surgical data, as well as survival times, were evaluated. Survival analyses were performed via the Kaplan–Meier method, and prognostic variables were assessed via univariate and multivariate analyses.ResultsA total of 66 patients, 60 males and 6 females, were included in the study. The mean age was 63.7 ± 8.3 years. The mean overall survival was 59.7 months, and the disease-free survival was 52.0 months. Squamous cell carcinoma histology, central tumor location, the presence of occult lymph node metastasis, and visceral pleural invasion were significantly associated with survival (p < 0.05). Cox regression analysis revealed that the histological subtype and the visibility of N1 metastasis were identified as independent prognostic factors.ConclusionPathological N1 lymph node involvement has a significant effect on the prognosis of patients with NSCLC. In particular, histological subtype and the presence of occult metastasis are key determinants of survival.

Introduction

Lung cancer continues to rank as one of the most common malignancies globally and is the primary contributor to cancer-related deaths [1]. Approximately 85% of these cases are classified as non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), which represents a biologically heterogeneous disease group. The prognosis of NSCLC is influenced by several factors, including tumor size, histological subtype, metastatic spread, and, most notably, lymph node involvement [2].

Lymph node involvement, denoted as the “N” descriptor in the TNM staging system, plays a pivotal role in determining the stage and prognosis of the disease. N1 involvement refers to metastasis to ipsilateral peribronchial and/or hilar lymph nodes without extension to the mediastinal nodes. Although the 8th edition of the TNM staging system has largely unchanged the N1 classification, recent studies have highlighted the heterogeneity within N1 disease itself. Factors such as the pattern of lymphatic spread, the number of involved nodal stations, and whether metastasis is clinically apparent or occult may significantly impact survival [3, 4].

Multiple studies in the literature have demonstrated that the prognostic significance of N1 disease varies according to subparameters such as the number of metastatic lymph nodes, number of nodal stations involved, clinical detectability of nodal metastasis (occult vs. obvious), and tumor location [5–7]. These features, in addition to their role in anatomical staging, provide valuable prognostic information and may guide therapeutic strategies in NSCLC.

In this context, evaluating the prognostic impact of specific characteristics of pathological N1 lymph node involvement is particularly relevant in early-stage NSCLC patients who are undergoing curative-intent surgical resection. Therefore, this study aimed to analyze the prognostic factors associated with survival in patients with pathological stage T1–2N1M0 NSCLC who underwent anatomic lung resection at our institution between 2010 and 2020.

Methods

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of our center on November 22, 2023, with protocol number 2023/37 − 11. A total of 66 patients who underwent anatomic lung resection for early-stage NSCLC at our institution between 2010 and 2020 and were pathologically staged as T1–T2N1M0 were included in the study. Patients who had received neoadjuvant therapy or who underwent sublobar resection (wedge resection or segmentectomy) were excluded.

Preoperative assessments included routine laboratory analyses, chest radiography, thoracic CT, PET-CT, and brain MRI. In addition, pulmonary function tests and fiberoptic bronchoscopy were performed as part of the standard staging protocol.

Age was analyzed as a continuous variable in the Cox proportional hazards model. For descriptive survival analyses, an optimal cutoff value of 63 years was determined using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and the Youden index. Follow-up time was calculated from the date of surgery to the date of death or last clinical visit.

Tumors were staged according to the 8th edition of the TNM staging system. Mediastinal staging was conducted in accordance with current ESTS and NCCN guidelines. Lymph nodes were considered clinically N1 (cN1) if the short-axis diameter was ≥ 10 mm on CT and/or if increased fluorodeoxyglucose uptake (SUVmax > 2.5) was observed on PET-CT. Invasive mediastinal staging with endobronchial ultrasound (EBUS) and/or cervical mediastinoscopy was performed selectively in patients with radiologically suspicious lymph nodes, central tumors, or tumors larger than 3 cm, in accordance with institutional practice and current guidelines. Patients with suspected N1 disease underwent additional mediastinoscopy to exclude occult N2 involvement before surgical resection. Occult N1 disease was defined as pathological N1 metastasis not detected by preoperative imaging or invasive staging, whereas clinically evident N1 disease was defined as nodal metastasis detected preoperatively by imaging and/or invasive staging procedures.

The tumor location was classified as central or peripheral on the basis of bronchoscopic findings. Preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative data were retrospectively collected from patient charts and hospital records. Demographic characteristics, surgical details, complications, and survival data were recorded.

Systematic lymph node dissection was performed in all patients according to IASLC and ESTS recommendations [8, 9]. Hilar and mediastinal nodal stations were systematically dissected, and all retrieved lymph nodes were examined histopathologically. The number of dissected lymph nodes and nodal stations was obtained from pathological reports. The lymph node ratio (LNR) was defined as the ratio of positive N1 lymph nodes to the total number of retrieved lymph nodes. Based on previous studies and SEER database analyses, LNR was categorized into three groups: ≤0.15, 0.16–0.5, and > 0.5. This approach ensured adequate nodal staging and minimized staging bias.

Statistical analyses were performed via IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables are presented as the means ± standard deviations (SDs), whereas categorical variables are presented as percentages. The distribution of continuous variables was assessed via the Shapiro–Wilk test. The independent samples t test was used for normally distributed variables, and the Mann–Whitney U test was used for nonnormally distributed variables. Comparisons of categorical variables were conducted via Pearson’s chi-square test, Fisher’s exact test, Yates’ correction, and Pearson’s exact chi-square test, as appropriate. Survival analyses were performed via the Kaplan–Meier method. A p value of < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

A total of 66 patients were included in the study. Among these patients, 60 (90.9%) were male, and 6 (9.1%) were female. The mean age was 63.7 ± 8.35 years. The median follow-up duration was 53 months (IQR 18–87.5). Thirty patients (45.5%) were aged 63 years or younger, whereas thirty-six patients (54.5%) were older than 63 years. Demographic and clinical characteristics of the patients

Histopathological analysis revealed squamous cell carcinoma in 34 patients (51.5%), adenocarcinoma in 27 patients (40.9%), and other histological subtypes—including large cell carcinoma, adenosquamous carcinoma, and sarcomatoid carcinoma—in 5 patients (7.6%). Regarding the type of surgical procedure, lobectomy was performed in 46 patients (69.7%), pneumonectomy in 16 patients (24.2%), and bilobectomy in 4 patients (6.1%). Thoracotomy and resection were performed in 61 patients (92.4%), whereas VATS was utilized in 5 patients (7.6%).

On the basis of bronchoscopic findings, peripheral tumor localization was identified in 49 patients (74.2%), and central localization was identified in 17 patients (25.8%). Peripheral localization was significantly more common among patients with adenocarcinoma (p < 0.001).

The mean SUVmax of the primary tumor on PET-CT was 9.06 ± 5.75. The SUVmax was significantly greater in patients with squamous cell carcinoma than in those with other histological subtypes (mean: 10.61, p = 0.039).

According to pathological staging, 25 patients (37.9%) were classified as T1, and 41 patients (62.1%) were classified as T2. However, this distribution was not associated with a statistically significant difference in survival outcomes (p = 0.144).

Systematic lymph node dissection was performed in all patients. The median number of retrieved lymph nodes was 13.5 (IQR: 10–19), with a mean of 14.8 ± 6.7. The median number of dissected nodal stations was 6.5 (IQR: 5–8), with a mean of 6.3 ± 1.8.

Survival analysis

The mean overall survival time was 59.7 months (range: 0.1–163 months; SD: 44.2), while the mean disease-free survival time was 52.0 months (SD: 45.2) (Fig. 1). By the end of the follow-up period, 29 patients (43.9%) were alive, and 37 patients (56.1%) had died.

Discussion

Our multivariate analysis demonstrated that squamous cell carcinoma histology and clinically evident N1 disease were independent predictors of overall survival. The poorer prognosis observed in patients with clinically evident N1 disease suggests that preoperatively detectable nodal metastasis may reflect a higher tumor burden and more aggressive tumor biology compared with occult nodal disease. Similarly, histological subtype remained an independent prognostic factor, which is consistent with previous reports indicating biological and metastatic differences between squamous cell carcinoma and adenocarcinoma.

The status of lymph node metastasis represents a pivotal determinant for both staging accuracy and survival outcomes in NSCLC patients. In the TNM staging system, the N1–3 classification provides more detailed prognostic information for extensive nodal involvement, whereas the prognostic value of isolated N1 disease remains less clearly defined. The N category in the 8th edition of the TNM classification remains unchanged from the 7th edition, prompting questions regarding the prognostic significance of N1 disease in the context of adverse features such as central tumor location, perineural invasion, and perinodal extension.

Several potential prognostic variables have been studied in NSCLC, including age, sex, tumor size, histological subtype, type of surgical resection, and use of adjuvant therapy. In a study by Taş et al. involving 110 lung cancer patients, overall survival was significantly worse in patients over the age of 60 [10]. Similarly, Onaitis et al. reported a 1.72-fold increased risk of death in patients aged ≥ 80 years compared with those aged 65–69 years (p < 0.001) [11]. In our cohort, no statistically significant differences were observed between patients aged ≤ 63 years and those aged > 63 years in terms of disease-free survival (DFS) or 5-year overall survival (OS).

Tumor location is also considered an important prognostic factor. Tumors arising in peripheral lung regions are generally detected at earlier stages, making complete surgical resection more feasible and improving patient prognosis. In contrast, centrally located tumors are frequently diagnosed at more advanced stages, often with aggressive biological characteristics and reduced operability [2, 12, 13]. In line with the literature, 74.2% of the tumors in our study were peripherally located, and 25.8% were central. Adenocarcinomas were significantly more likely to be peripherally located (92.6%) than other histological subtypes were. However, contrary to previous reports, centrally located tumors were associated with longer survival in our cohort (p = 0.013). This unexpected finding should be interpreted cautiously, as it may reflect the limited sample size, the predominance of squamous cell carcinoma among central tumors, and the single-center retrospective design, and requires validation in larger cohorts.

Histological subtypes are well known to impact patient prognosis. Holmes et al., in a study of T1N1 patients, reported significantly better survival in those with squamous cell carcinoma than in those with adenocarcinoma [5]. Similar findings were reported by Chansky et al. in a large cohort of 9137 patients with stage I–IIIA disease who underwent surgical resection, where squamous cell carcinoma was associated with more favorable outcomes [14]. Consistent with these results, our study demonstrated that patients with squamous cell carcinoma had significantly longer overall and disease-free survival than did those with adenocarcinoma (p = 0.007). The observed survival advantage in patients with squamous cell carcinoma may be linked to distinct biological growth patterns and metastatic behaviors. Unlike adenocarcinomas, which often exhibit an early propensity for hematogenous dissemination and distant metastasis even at smaller tumor sizes, squamous cell carcinomas tend to remain more localized or progress via lymphatic spread within the thorax. This fundamental difference in tumor biology likely explains why histological subtype emerged as a significant independent prognostic factor in our multivariate analysis.

Compared with the overall pattern of N1 metastasis, the distinction between occult and clinically evident nodal involvement has been less thoroughly studied. “Occult” metastases refer to cases in which lymph node involvement is not detected during preoperative clinical staging. In a study by Robinson et al. involving patients with clinical N0 NSCLC tumors smaller than 2 cm, the incidence of occult nodal metastasis was 14% [15]. Beyaz et al. similarly reported occult N1 metastases in 16.6% of clinical N0 patients, nearly half of whom had tumors < 3 cm in diameter [16]. In our cohort, 54 patients had occult N1 disease, and 12 had obvious N1 disease. Although no statistically significant difference in pathological stage was found between these groups (p > 0.05), both OS and DFS were significantly longer in patients with occult nodal metastasis (p < 0.05).

Previous studies have reported operative mortality rates of approximately 10% following pneumonectomy [17], with poorer outcomes observed after right-sided resections than after left-sided procedures, largely due to complications such as bronchopleural fistula and pulmonary edema [18, 19]. Batıhan et al. noted higher 5-year DFS rates in patients who underwent pneumonectomy, although no significant difference in OS was observed [20]. In our study, there was no statistically significant difference in either OS or DFS between patients who underwent lobectomy and those who underwent pneumonectomy (3 right, 13 left) (p = 0.771).

Involvement of the visceral pleura is widely recognized as an adverse prognostic feature in lung cancer patients. Such invasion can promote tumor spread into pleural and lymphatic structures, thereby increasing the likelihood of recurrence and distant metastasis [21, 22]. In a study by Kang et al. involving 439 patients, VPI was associated with higher rates of mediastinal lymph node involvement and reduced survival [21]. Another study involving 1281 NSCLC patients reported that the VPI was significantly correlated with tumor size, nodal involvement, and poor survival, identifying it as an independent negative prognostic factor [22]. In line with these findings, our results also revealed significantly lower OS and 5-year survival rates in patients with VPIs (p = 0.012 and p = 0.047, respectively). However, VPI lost its significance in the multivariate model, possibly due to collinearity or interactions with other clinicopathological variables.

The lymph node ratio (LNR) has been proposed as a prognostic marker in resected NSCLC, with higher LNR values generally associated with worse survival outcomes [23–26]. For example, an analysis of 4004 patients from the SEER database demonstrated that an increased LNR was linked to reduced survival [27]. Another study reported that patients with an LNR > 0.5 had a mortality risk comparable to that of patients with T3 disease [25]. However, LNR did not reach statistical significance in our cohort. This finding may be explained by the relatively homogeneous nodal burden in this N1-only population and the limited sample size, which may have reduced the discriminatory power of LNR. Therefore, LNR findings in this study should be interpreted cautiously and considered inconclusive. Larger prospective studies including N2 disease are warranted to clarify the prognostic value of LNR in nodal staging (p = 0.403).

This study has several limitations. The interval between overall survival and disease-free survival was relatively short, mainly because a substantial proportion of deaths occurred without documented recurrence (19 of 37 deaths), likely related to advanced age or comorbidities. These deaths represent competing risks and may have influenced cancer-specific outcome estimates. In addition, the retrospective single-center design may introduce selection bias, and the relatively small sample size limited statistical power, which may partly explain the wide confidence intervals and the lack of significance for some clinicopathological variables. Formal competing-risk analyses were not performed, and molecular data were unavailable for most patients. Nevertheless, the present study provides clinically relevant insights into the prognostic significance of occult versus clinically evident N1 disease.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that factors such as histological subtype, tumor localization, visceral pleural invasion, and the presence of occult versus manifest N1 metastasis significantly impact survival in patients with NSCLC. Notably, the observation that patients with occult N1 metastasis have better survival outcomes highlights the limitations of current staging modalities and underscores the critical role of thorough lymph node evaluation in guiding surgical decision-making.

In contrast, resection type, the LNR and the number of involved nodal stations were not found to have statistically significant effects on survival. These findings underscore the heterogeneous nature of N1 disease and support the need for individualized prognostic assessment in clinical practice.

To validate and expand upon these results, future large-scale, prospective, multicenter studies are warranted.
