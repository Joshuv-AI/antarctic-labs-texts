Emerging non-invasive tools for hair follicle assessment: a narrative review on hyperspectral and terahertz imaging
Authors: Debalina Bose, Rinky Kapoor, Debashree Das, Debraj Shome, Esmael Ahmed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hair loss, though often dismissed as cosmetic, reflects complex vascular, metabolic, structural, and inflammatory changes within the scalp microenvironment. Conventional diagnostic tools such as visual inspection, dermoscopy, trichoscopy, and biopsy remain limited in their ability to determine these evolving processes non-invasively. Recent advances in optical physics and biomedical engineering have introduced light- and wave-based imaging approaches that may expand scalp diagnostics beyond surface visualization. Hyperspectral imaging (HSI) and terahertz (THz) imaging have demonstrated comparable capabilities for assessing tissue biochemistry, microstructure, hydration dynamics, pigmentation, and vascular signatures in related dermatologic applications and may support exploratory investigation of scalp assessment after validation. However, direct clinical evidence supporting HSI and THz imaging specifically in hair-loss patients remains limited. Therefore, this review integrates available alopecia-related imaging evidence, mechanistic and modeling evidence relevant to hair biology, and extrapolated findings from broader dermatologic applications, including oncology and wound-healing research. Relevant literature was identified by structured searches of PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar, covering studies published from June 2008 to March 2026. The review analyzes how these technologies may identify indirect, non-invasive scalp-tissue indicators associated with follicular miniaturization, perifollicular inflammation, fibrosis, vascular compromise, hydration imbalance, and treatment response. By merging spectral and wave-derived data with AI-based analytics and quantitative biomarkers, HSI and THz imaging may, upon validation, contribute to a shift from subjective grading to dynamic exploratory physiological mapping of superficial scalp tissue. Nevertheless, clinical translation will require scalp-specific validation, standardized acquisition protocols, normative datasets among different hair types, and correlation with established trichoscopic, histological, and clinical endpoints. Overall, these technologies constitute promising but investigational frontiers in precision alopecia diagnostics and data-guided hair-loss management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10103-026-04990-6.

Introduction

Hair loss (alopecia) is a common and distressing condition with varied causes, including androgenetic alopecia (AGA), autoimmune disorders, nutritional deficiencies, and inflammatory scalp diseases [1]. Beyond cosmetic concerns, it entails significant psychosocial impact and remains diagnostically difficult due to the scalp’s complex genetic, hormonal, vascular, and immune interactions [2].

Conventional tools such as dermoscopy, trichoscopy, and scalp biopsy remain central to alopecia assessment but have important limitations, including operator dependence, limited depth or molecular information, sampling error, invasiveness in the case of biopsy, and the need for correlation with clinical and histopathological findings [3–5]. Dermoscopy and trichoscopy visualize only surface features, while biopsy, though a gold standard, is invasive and unsuitable for repeat use [6, 7]. Overlapping features between alopecia subtypes add to the complexity of diagnosis, pointing to the need for imaging approaches that can assess scalp structure and biochemistry objectively, repeatedly, and with minimal patient burden [8]. Advances in biophotonics, spectroscopy, and computational imaging are transforming dermatology into an evidence-based, technology-driven field. Non-invasive modalities such as optical coherence tomography and reflectance confocal microscopy provide structural and cellular information [9, 10], whereas HSI and THz imaging are currently being investigated for complementary functional and tissue-contrast mapping [11, 12]. By capturing biochemical and structural tissue signatures invisible to accepted methods and integrating them with AI-based analysis, these technologies may eventually support quantitative mapping of follicular density, vascularity, hydration, and inflammatory activity, provided these applications are confirmed through scalp-specific validation [13].

Importantly, this article is a narrative, hypothesis-generating review that synthesizes emerging concepts and early-stage evidence on HSI and THz imaging in hair loss diagnostics. It is not a primary clinical validation study and does not establish definitive diagnostic validity or clinical utility. Instead, it stresses mechanistic plausibility, technological capacities, and potential translational pathways, recognizing that the current evidence base remains preliminary and heterogeneous; accordingly, any clinical implications should be interpreted as exploratory and future-oriented, serving to identify priorities for future validation rather than established standards of care. Within this context, these developments may represent early steps toward shifting hair-loss diagnostics from qualitative assessment to more quantitative, advanced, and non-invasive visualization [14]. Light- and wave-based modalities are increasingly positioned as the clinician’s “new eyes,” enabling earlier detection, safer longitudinal monitoring, and more customized approaches to scalp and hair health [15].

Trichoscopy remains the frontline tool for evaluating surface morphology and hair-shaft and follicular signs, while scalp biopsy remains the reference standard when histopathological confirmation is required. Optical coherence tomography, reflectance confocal microscopy, and high-frequency ultrasound provide depth-resolved structural or cellular information and currently have more direct scalp-related evidence than HSI and THz imaging. Within this diagnostic framework, HSI and THz imaging should be considered investigational and potentially complementary modalities. Accordingly, this review evaluates their principles, available direct and indirect evidence, potential clinician-relevant applications, limitations, and requirements for scalp-specific validation and clinical translation.

Literature identification and selection

Although this article is presented as a narrative review, relevant literature was identified using structured searches of major databases, including PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar, covering studies published from June 2008 to March 2026. The search strategy incorporated both controlled vocabulary and free-text terms, using keyword combinations such as “hyperspectral imaging AND skin,” “terahertz imaging AND dermatology,” “alopecia AND imaging,” “scalp diagnostics AND optical imaging,” “hyperspectral imaging AND alopecia,” and “terahertz imaging AND hair loss.”

Priority was given to original studies, reviews, and technical reports directly addressing scalp imaging, hair disorders, alopecia diagnostics, HSI, THz imaging, and optical or spectroscopic assessment of skin and hair-bearing tissue.

Where alopecia-specific evidence was limited, relevant data from broader dermatologic and biomedical contexts, including skin cancer, wound healing, inflammation, vascular assessment, and tissue hydration analysis, were included to support mechanistic and translational interpretation. These indirect sources were clearly distinguished from direct scalp or alopecia evidence and interpreted cautiously, with explicit consideration of their usefulness and restrictions in hair-loss diagnosis. Studies were excluded if they were unrelated to imaging or spectroscopy, lacked relevance to dermatology or tissue diagnostics, were not available in English, or did not provide sufficient methodological or interpretive detail relevant to the scope of this review (Supplementary Table S1).

Imaging paradigm shift: light and waves in dermatology

Over the past decade, the convergence of dermatology and biomedical engineering has accelerated this transformation, fundamentally redefining the field of scalp diagnostics. Through harnessing the principles of reflection, scattering, absorption, and contrast generation, light- and wave-based modalities may offer knowledge of scalp morphology, vascularity, and follicular function; however, for HSI and THz imaging, these capabilities remain exploratory in alopecia until scalp-specific validation is available.

Early optical techniques such as Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT), Reflectance Confocal Microscopy (RCM), and high-frequency ultrasound have paved the way for this evolution [67]. Among these, OCT has exhibited exceptional promise. In evidence directly obtained from an alopecia study involving 32 subjects, both healthy individuals and patients with scarring and non-scarring alopecia, fibre-based swept-source OCT enabled visualization of hair follicles, sebaceous glands, and dermal structures across multiple scalp sites [16]. Structural parameters such as epidermal thickness and follicular density were quantified noninvasively, and correlated strongly with disease states [17]. Beyond structural assessment, dynamic OCT has differentiated inflammatory scalp conditions such as psoriasis, seborrheic dermatitis, and contact dermatitis by detecting vascular and architectural changes in vivo.

High-frequency ultrasound and RCM provide complementary perspectives, strengthening the diagnostic power of light- and wave-based modalities. A recent systematic review identified 42 scalp imaging studies, of which 27 employed such technologies, underscoring their growing clinical significance [14]. Parallel advances inside computational imaging and artificial intelligence further increase accuracy [37]. For instance, a study that directly used scalp imaging reported that a multi-network fusion object-detection model analyzing dermoscopic images of scalp psoriasis achieved diagnostic accuracy exceeding 91%, outperforming expert dermatologists when distinguishing psoriasis from seborrheic dermatitis. This high diagnostic validity is achieved in controlled experimental settings, with performance comparable to or exceeding expert evaluation in specific tasks. Within this advancing framework, HSI and THz imaging emerge as logical next-generation tools, extending the diagnostic frontier beyond OCT, ultrasound, and RCM into new spectral regimes. Unlike conventional imaging, which primarily captures structural or morphological information, HSI acquires spatially detailed data across hundreds of contiguous wavelength bands [53]. Each pixel contains both of the spatial coordinates and a reflectance or absorbance spectrum, thereby generating a biochemical “fingerprint” of tissue.

In HSI, broadband visible-near-infrared illumination is directed onto the scalp surface, where light engages with superficial scalp structures, hair shafts, follicular openings, perifollicular vasculature pigmentation, and water-containing tissue compartments. The reflected light is acquired by a hyperspectral camera or spectrometer, generating a spectral-spatial data cube in which each pixel contains both spatial information and a wavelength-dependent reflectance spectrum. These pixel-level spectral signatures can then be processed to derive biomarker maps related to melanin or pigmentation, hemoglobin and oxygenation, vascularity or erythema, and hydration-associated spectral traits. These features could serve as indirect markers of altered scalp microcirculation, perifollicular inflammation, follicular miniaturization, and early tissue remodelling, possibly supporting objective severity assessment and longitudinal treatment monitoring. HSI may also be combined with complementary modalities such as THz imaging, which provides contrast through a different region of the electromagnetic spectrum [55].

In HSI, relevant features may include reflectance intensity across selected wavelengths, spectral slopes, band ratios, chromophore-related indices, and spatial heterogeneity patterns. Although the direct clinical utility of HSI in alopecia remains unvalidated [54], an oncologic study of pigmented lesions in individuals with Caucasian skin reported 96.7% sensitivity for detecting cutaneous malignant melanoma, while specificity was limited, indicating that spectral signatures can distinguish subtle tissue differences under disease-specific conditions [38]. In addition, an ex vivo spectral-histology study used hyperspectral infrared imaging and infrared microspectroscopy to map longitudinal sections of human hair follicles and differentiate their biochemical layers. However, these findings show analytical practicability in excised tissue and do not establish diagnostic or monitoring applications for alopecia in vivo.

Terahertz imaging: working principles, quantitative signal assessment, and exploratory scalp assessment

THz imaging, operating in the ~ 0.1–10 THz frequency range, represents a fundamentally different diagnostic approach from optical and hyperspectral modalities. These non-ionizing electromagnetic waves are highly sensitive to water content and subtle structural alterations in tissue, making them suited to exploratory assessment of hydration- and structure-related changes in the scalp microenvironment that may be relevant to hair-loss pathology [42]. However, because THz spatial resolution is generally too coarse to resolve fine follicular compartments, such as the bulge region, dermal papilla, hair bulb, or perifollicular inflammatory infiltrates, its role in alopecia should be framed as indirect scalp-tissue mapping instead of direct microscopic imaging of follicles.

In practice, THz systems measure how pulses or continuous waves are reflected, transmitted, or absorbed by tissue. Contrast arises because hydrated, dense, or structurally modified tissue interacts with THz radiation differently than healthy tissue. This property has been successfully exploited in skin research: for example, in vivo imaging using a quantum-cascade-laser-based THz system achieved diffraction-limited resolution (~ 100 μm) over a 50 × 40 mm field and distinguished lesion types by mapping both amplitude and phase signals. Reviews of THz sensing regularly highlight its sensitivity to water content as the primary driver of contrast in living tissue [45].

Applied to hair-loss diagnostics, THz imaging is believed to provide indirect scalp-tissue markers instead of direct visualization of follicular microstructures; it can support exploratory assessment of disease progression and treatment response without tissue sampling [46]. Prospective studies in well-characterized alopecia cohorts are therefore required before THz-derived parameters can be regarded as clinically actionable biomarkers [56].

The THz imaging, low-energy terahertz waves are emitted toward the scalp and interact with superficial and subsurface tissue layers. The reflected or transmitted THz signals are subsequently analyzed using parameters such as amplitude, phase delay, absorption, and backscatter. These signal characteristics are sensitive to tissue water content, dielectric properties, and structural heterogeneity, allowing the generation of tissue-contrast outputs such as hydration-sensitive maps, dielectric or structural information, and regional tissue-mapping patterns. Within THz imaging, extracted features may include absorption coefficients, refractive index-related parameters, phase delay, backscatter intensity, and hydration-sensitive contrast patterns. From images to insights: quantitative biomarkers

The true value of advanced scalp imaging is found not only in acquiring high-resolution visuals but in extracting quantitative biomarkers, numerical parameters that objectively reflect scalp status, disease severity, and therapeutic response. Moving from descriptive visualization to reproducible measurement constitutes a key step toward precision dermatology in hair-loss disorders [49].

Several studies have identified structural biomarkers of alopecia. Clinical evidence derived directly from a scalp-specific study of twenty-seven volunteers demonstrates that magnetic resonance imaging in AGA displays reduced scalp and hypodermal thickness, along with shallower follicular depths [18]. Evidence from hair and scalp imaging studies indicates that trichoscopy-based analyses of hair density and shaft-diameter distributions can differentiate disease patterns in female-pattern hair loss. Integration of computational platforms further magnifies diagnostic capability [32]. A dermatology study that directly incorporated scalp/hair loss data demonstrated that deep learning can segment hair-bearing regions, calculate hair-loss area, and generate automated Severity of Alopecia Tool (SALT) scores, thereby enhancing reproducibility and facilitating quantitative disease tracking [19]. Beyond morphology, biochemical and transcriptomic profiling add depth by capturing disease-relevant molecular signatures that complement imaging-derived metrics. Evidence derived from analyses of affected human scalp tissue in patchy alopecia areata (AAP) shows clear gene expression alterations, characterized by upregulation of immune and chemokine pathways and the downregulation of keratin-related genes, reflecting concurrent immune activation and follicular structural compromise. Network-based analyses additionally identify CD2 as a central regulatory hub with possible therapeutic relevance. These biologically grounded findings broaden the biomarker framework into mechanistic, transcriptomic precision, reinforcing an integrated, quantitative approach to hair-loss assessment [30].

Clinically useful biomarkers must demonstrate reproducibility, correlation with validated endpoints, sensitivity to change, and biological plausibility, linking imaging findings to known follicular pathophysiology. Validated, quantitative biomarkers will ultimately shift hair-loss diagnostics from subjective assessment to numerical indices that quantify baseline severity, track therapy, and accelerate clinical trials through objective, reproducible endpoints. As imaging hardware matures, focus will turn toward building normative databases, standardising acquisition protocols, and smoothly integrating biomarker analytics into dermatology practice [50].

Building on this, a recent Scientific Reports study introduced an AI-driven grading framework for male pattern hair loss that uses a hair-loss area ratio metric. Leveraging evidence from patient-derived scalp images, the study demonstrated that high-precision Mask R-CNN segmentation achieved superior consistency compared with BASP grading, strengthening the value of quantitative, automated assessment in dermatologic diagnostics [33]. Continuing this trajectory, a study involving 761 images from 257 patients investigated early hair-loss detection using classical machine-learning classifiers applied to a 999-entry dataset incorporating demographic, hormonal, and lifestyle variables. The Random Forest model demonstrated the highest classification effectiveness (reported up to 99.8%) within this controlled dataset, with feature-importance analysis identifying age and glucose levels as key predictors, underscoring the multifactorial nature of alopecia [34]. However, this performance is dataset-specific and may reflect potential overfitting, with limited external validation across diverse populations, thereby restricting its generalizability to actual clinical settings.

More broadly, current evidence pertaining to AI-based hair diagnostics remains mostly speculative or conceptual and should therefore be interpreted with caution until validated in large-scale, alopecia-specific clinical cohorts. Although the integration of imaging, biometrics, and individual patient data yields a promising framework for individualized scalp assessment, the transition toward clinically reliable, data-driven, and individualized treatment strategies will depend on stringent validation, standardization, and realistic applicability [35].

The strength of available evidence varies considerably across modalities. Findings from direct human imaging studies provide relatively stronger clinical evidence. In contrast, AI-driven diagnostic models and computational evaluation, although promising, are primarily derived from controlled datasets and represent moderate levels of evidence demanding external validation. Evidence extrapolated from non-scalp applications, including oncology and wound-healing studies for HSI and THz imaging, represents a lower level of evidence and should be interpreted cautiously until confirmed in alopecia-specific clinical settings.

For AI-assisted HSI and THz scalp imaging, the expected learning targets must be predefined before model development. Models may learn spectral features, such as wavelength-specific reflectance or absorbance signatures related to hemoglobin, melanin, water, lipid, or oxygenation changes; spatial features, such as follicular-unit distribution, perifollicular erythema, hair-shaft emergence patterns, regional vascular heterogeneity, or areas of reduced follicular density; and multimodal features integrating spectral, spatial, clinical, and trichoscopic information. In longitudinal studies, AI may also learn temporal patterns, including changes within spectral biomarkers, hair density, shaft-diameter diversity, inflammatory activity, or treatment-response trajectories throughout time. For THz imaging, relevant learning targets may include hydration-sensitive reflectivity, phase-delay patterns, backscatter profiles, and regional tissue-contrast changes, but these should be interpreted as indirect tissue-level signals rather than as direct evidence of follicular microanatomy.

Training datasets for such models should be alopecia-specific, prospectively collected, and sufficiently diverse across alopecia subtype, disease severity, scalp region, skin tone, hair colour, hair density, hair-shaft thickness, sex, age, and treatment stage. Each image or spectral cube should be linked to well-defined ground-truth labels, including expert clinical diagnoses, trichoscopic features, standardized photographs, validated severity scores, hair-density and shaft-diameter measurements, treatment-response data, and histopathology when biopsy is ethically justified. Data should include raw and pre-processed spectral-spatial cubes, calibration metadata, illumination settings, acquisition geometry, device parameters, artefact annotations, and quality-control metrics such as signal-to-noise ratio. Model development should use predefined training, validation, and independent external test sets, ideally from multiple centres and devices, to reduce overfitting and assess generalizability across real-world scalp-imaging conditions.

Quantitative biomarkers can convert alopecia assessment from subjective visual grading into objective, reproducible measurements of disease severity, scalp tissue activity or indirect follicle-associated changes, and treatment response. In hair-loss disorders, imaging-derived parameters such as reduced scalp or hypodermal thickness, altered follicular depth, decreased hair density, increased shaft-diameter variability, perifollicular inflammatory signals, and fibrosis-related structural changes could provide measurable indicators of follicular miniaturization, disease activity, and irreversible follicular damage. Automated SALT scoring and AI-based hair-loss area ratios may further support longitudinal monitoring, assessment of therapeutic response, and standardization of clinical trial endpoints. However, biomarker utility depends on scalp-specific validation, standardized acquisition protocols, and correlation with established clinical, trichoscopic, histological, and biological endpoints.

Comparing the lens: conventional vs. emerging methods

Current alopecia assessment relies on clinical examination, standardized photography, trichoscopy, and scalp biopsy when histopathological confirmation is required [20–22, 72]. Trichoscopy remains the frontline imaging tool for evaluating hair-shaft and follicular surface features, whereas biopsy remains the reference standard for scarring or diagnostically ambiguous alopecia.

OCT, RCM, and HFUS provide depth-resolved structural, cellular, or vascular information that may complement trichoscopy in selected scalp disorders [16, 23–25]. These modalities currently have more direct scalp-related evidence than HSI and THz imaging but remain limited by device availability, specialized expertise, and incomplete standardization.

Nonetheless, traditional tools remain indispensable: trichoscopy is low-cost, widely available, and ideal for initial assessment, while biopsy continues to serve as the confirmatory standard [22]. Conversely, advanced imaging demands specialized instrumentation and expertise and lacks large-scale normative databases, both of which currently limit its routine integration into clinical workflows [52]. HSI and THz imaging currently lack alopecia-specific sensitivity, specificity, and receiver-operating characteristic data. Their diagnostic performance cannot yet be directly ranked against trichoscopy or biopsy in hair-loss cohorts. Available evidence is largely extrapolated from broader dermatologic, wound, oncology, and biomedical imaging applications; therefore, these modalities should be interpreted as investigational adjuncts requiring prospective scalp-specific validation

The anatomical scale of hair follicles limits the information obtainable from both modalities. Clinical HSI is primarily suited to surface and superficial dermal functional mapping, including pigmentary, vascular, erythematous, and hydration-associated features. THz imaging provides coarser, hydration-sensitive, and structural tissue contrast that may be relevant to regional water distribution and fibrosis-related remodelling. Neither modality currently provides sufficient spatial resolution or penetration to directly visualize fine follicular compartments such as the bulge, dermal papilla, hair bulb, or microscopic perifollicular infiltrates. They should therefore be regarded as adjunctive scalp-mapping technologies rather than direct microscopic follicle-imaging methods.

To aid conceptual comparison, this review proposes a non-validated Conceptual Scalp Imaging Readiness Index (SIRI), an evidence-informed, author-derived heuristic framework for comparing scalp imaging modalities across invasiveness, biomarker yield, AI-integration potential, and translational maturity [26]. Conventional techniques such as trichoscopy and biopsy remain clinically mature and accessible but provide limited biomarker-level information, whereas HSI and THz imaging may offer non-invasive, data-rich, and AI-compatible assessment, although alopecia-specific validation remains limited [20, 22, 47, 75]. It should not be interpreted as measured diagnostic performance, an externally validated scoring system, or a quantitative ranking of modalities for clinical adoption. Rather, it provides a conceptual roadmap for the transition from descriptive observation toward measurable, quantitative, and data-driven scalp diagnostics.

Translational frontiers: from lab prototypes to clinical practice

With structural, spectral, and wave-based imaging technologies advancing rapidly, alongside increasingly sophisticated automated biomarker extraction and AI-based analytical frameworks, the translational challenge now extends from technical proof of concept to disease-specific validation and eventual clinical integration. Moving from laboratory prototypes to routine scalp diagnostics requires parallel progress in hardware platforms, software ecosystems, regulatory validation, and integration into dermatology workflows [51]. In this context, HSI and THz imaging have shown important early advances toward clinical applicability.

One major translational development is the increasing miniaturisation and technical robustness of imaging platforms. Systems previously confined to optics laboratories are being adapted into more compact configurations suitable for clinical investigation. For example, a 2024 first-in-human study demonstrated a quantum-cascade-laser-based THz confocal system with approximately 100 μm diffraction-limited resolution and a 50 × 40 mm scan field, enabling in vivo differentiation among several skin lesion types [43, 60]. Dermatology-oriented HSI prototypes have likewise demonstrated progress toward compact acquisition systems, although their performance and practicality for routine scalp imaging remain to be established [40].

A second requirement for translation is the development of reliable computational processing pipelines. Spectral and wave-based modalities generate high-dimensional spatial-spectral and spatial-signal datasets that require standardized preprocessing, calibration, noise correction, segmentation, feature extraction, and automated interpretation. Real-time analytical workflows, including machine-learning-based classification or regression, may help convert raw imaging data into clinically interpretable outputs [36].

Although scalp-specific evidence remains limited, oncology and wound-healing research provides methodological precedents for translation. In oncology, HSI has been investigated for mapping tumor-associated differences in hemoglobin, oxygenation, and lipid-related spectral signatures [62], whereas THz imaging has demonstrated water-sensitive contrast between different superficial tissue states [61]. In wound research, HSI has been used primarily to assess tissue oxygenation and perfusion, while THz approaches have been explored to detect hydration changes and characterize superficial barrier-related abnormalities [44]. Some of these features may overlap indirectly with alopecia-associated changes, including perifollicular inflammation, barrier dysfunction, and tissue remodeling [27]. However, evidence from HSI in oncology or wound assessment and THz imaging in non-scalp skin pathology demonstrates technical feasibility and potential analytical workflows rather than diagnostic performance in alopecia. Dedicated scalp studies incorporating clinical, trichoscopic, and, where appropriate, histological correlation are therefore required. Translation to scalp and hair-loss diagnostics will require adaptation to curved, hair-bearing surfaces, including appropriate device ergonomics, motion compensation, reproducible positioning, and patient comfort during repeated imaging. Standardized acquisition protocols and normative datasets spanning diverse skin tones, hair characteristics, scalp regions, and alopecia phenotypes will also be necessary. Clinicians will require interpretable outputs generated within clinically practical timeframes and integrated with dermatology record systems and decision-support workflows. As discussed further in Sect. 8, progress will therefore depend on portable hardware, robust analytical pipelines, multicentre scalp-specific validation, and clinically interpretable reporting. Until these requirements are met, HSI and THz imaging should be regarded as investigational translational technologies rather than established tools for routine alopecia diagnosis or treatment monitoring [31, 57, 95].

Gaps and barriers to adoption: practical, regulatory, and clinical challenges

Despite rapid advances, several hurdles still limit the translation of HSI and THz imaging into routine hair-loss diagnostics. Despite their promising potential, HSI and THz imaging remain constrained by several interrelated technical, biological, and translational limitations that currently preclude routine clinical implementation in hair-loss diagnostics. Section 8 discusses these barriers.

Most studies remain proof-of-concept, rather than alopecia-specific research, leaving large-scale scalp validation scarce. The absence of databases of normative and THz spectra across alopecia subtypes, hair types, and ethnicities undermines reproducibility, introduces AI model bias, and complicates interpretation, leading to false results. Lack of standardized protocols, with variability in calibration, illumination, and scan depth, reduces comparability across centres [11, 59, 97, 98].

Moreover, a major limitation of the HSI imaging system for follicular diagnostics is its restricted penetration depth, which is sensitive to surface and superficial to mid-dermal optical signals and is therefore unlikely to directly visualize deeper follicular compartments [64, 68]. These indirect measures limit clinical diagnosis at a deeper level, such as the bulge, dermal papilla, and follicular bulb. The cost of commercial or research-grade HSI systems in the market lies in the range of 10,000 to over USD 100,000, with high-end systems exceeding basic multispectral devices [65].

THz imaging faces additional limitations as its penetration depth in hydrated biological tissues is restricted by strong water absorption, thereby limiting assessment of deeper scalp and follicular structures [69, 99]. Although explored in skin cancer, wound assessment and broader biomedical applications, its use in scalp and hair disorders remains exploratory, with limited follicular-specific evidence [56, 70]. These barriers are compounded by the need for specialised technical expertise for source-detector alignment, hydration-sensitive signal interpretation, and artefact management, limiting near-term use to research environments or specialised imaging centres [69, 100–106]. These scientific gaps are compounded by practical barriers such as the complexity of devices, cost of treatment, calibration requirements, and computational burden, which currently limit immediate adoption.

From a regulatory standpoint, before device approval, devices must demonstrate clear clinical value, reproducibility, and safety through multicentre trials [58]. Regulatory classification will depend on the intended clinical claim, jurisdiction, and degree of automation. If HSI or THz systems are used only for image acquisition, documentation, or research-use imaging, they may follow a lower-risk device pathway. However, if they are intended to detect, diagnose, stratify, monitor, or guide treatment decisions in alopecia, they would likely be regulated as active diagnostic medical devices. Where AI-based interpretation or automated classification is included, the software component may also require assessment as Software as a Medical Device or clinical decision-support software. Therefore, regulatory planning should define the intended-use statement early and distinguish between research-use imaging, adjunctive diagnostic support, and autonomous diagnostic claims.

Even after validation, successful adoption requires workflow integration in dermatology clinics currently optimised for trichoscopy and photography, which entails staff retraining, electronic health record (EHR) compatibility, and user-friendly outputs [96].

Overall, the translational pathway for HSI and THz imaging in alopecia will require staged development involving pilot studies, protocol development, repeatability assessment, small observational cohorts, multicenter validation, external validation of AI-derived biomarkers, and integration into tertiary dermatology or hair-specialist centres. At present, the review proposes a hypothetical construct that requires further study before it can be considered a formal instrument.

Clinical context: alopecia subtypes and imaging relevance

Given the limited availability of scalp-specific validation studies, the following section is intended as a conceptual overview to relate known pathological features of alopecia to the potential capabilities of HSI and THz imaging, rather than to define established clinical applications.

Androgenetic alopecia is characterized by progressive follicular miniaturization, in which terminal hairs gradually transform into finer vellus-like hairs, with reduced follicular size, a shortened anagen phase, and a prolonged telogen phase. The scalp microenvironment shows perifollicular microinflammation, subtle fibrosis, altered dermal papilla signaling, and reduced vascular support [107]. Correspondingly, these changes may manifest as reduced vascular and chromophore-related signals with subtle hydration and structural alterations, suggesting that HSI and THz imaging may offer greater value for disease activity assessment and treatment monitoring than for primary differential diagnosis.

Alopecia areata is a T cell-mediated autoimmune condition characterized by the loss of hair follicle immune privilege and targeted attack on anagen follicles. Histopathology shows a dense peribulbar lymphocytic infiltrate, often described as a swarm of bees, along with increased telogen follicles and follicular miniaturization. The microenvironment reflects immune dysregulation, cytokine signaling, and inflammatory mediators disrupting follicular cycling, with occasional pigment incontinence and matrix damage [108]. These features suggest that inflammation-related chromophore and oxygenation changes, along with localized hydration alterations, may be detectable, indicating greater utility for disease activity assessment and treatment monitoring rather than primary differential diagnosis.

Telogen effluvium is a non-scarring form of hair loss marked by a reactive shift of a large proportion of follicles from the anagen to the telogen phase, often triggered by systemic or physiological stress. Histologically, it is characterized by an increased number of telogen follicles without significant inflammation or follicular miniaturization. The scalp microenvironment remains largely preserved, with intact follicular architecture and minimal structural alteration. It is reversible because it disrupts the hair cycle, not the hair follicles themselves [109]. Accordingly, minimal changes in chromophore or hydration are expected, limiting diagnostic value, though these modalities may assist in monitoring recovery.

Scarring alopecias, or cicatricial alopecias, are a group of conditions that lead to permanent hair loss due to irreversible destruction of hair follicles and their replacement with fibrous scar tissue. Histologically, they show loss of follicular units, damage to the pilosebaceous apparatus, and replacement by connective tissue, often with perifollicular inflammatory infiltrates that may be lymphocytic or neutrophilic depending on the subtype. The scalp microenvironment is characterized by chronic inflammation, damage to the follicular stem cell niche, sebaceous gland loss, and progressive fibrosis, resulting in collapse of follicular architecture and loss of regenerative capacity [110]. These changes may correspond to fibrosis-related spectral alterations, reduced vascular signals, and alterations in hydration and tissue structure, suggesting that HSI and THz imaging may have greater utility for differential diagnosis and disease activity assessment.

Evolving hair-loss diagnostics: current standards and future directions

In routine clinical practice, alopecia diagnosis continues to rely on examination, trichoscopy, and, when required, scalp biopsy, which together serve as the current reference standards. Against this backdrop, it is important to note that there are still no large, scalp-focused studies directly comparing HSI or THz imaging with these established methods. Most available evidence comes from early-stage investigations or studies conducted in broader dermatologic settings, so its relevance to hair-loss conditions is still being established. As a result, these technologies should currently be viewed as promising but exploratory, with their clinical role likely to evolve alongside future, well-designed validation studies [111].

Looking ahead, the future of scalp diagnostics lies at the intersection of light, waves, and intelligence. Although HSI and THz imaging may offer complementary diagnostic insights, their applications in alopecia remain investigational rather than established clinical standards, and the proposed use of AI-assisted analysis to detect follicular stress before visible thinning requires validation in prospective alopecia studies.

In the short term, the most realistic goal is not immediate clinical deployment, but rather carefully designed pilot studies on human scalp or small observational cohorts focusing on protocol development, feasibility assessment, repeatability testing, and correlation with established clinical measures and treatment responses.

In the mid-term, the field will require multicenter validation, normative scalp databases, standardized acquisition conditions, and externally validated AI models. Integration into tertiary dermatology or hair-specialist centres may be feasible if acquisition time, device cost, and data-processing requirements are reduced. At this stage, HSI and THz imaging should be evaluated as adjunctive tools rather than replacements for clinical examination, trichoscopy, or histopathology.

In the long term, aspirational applications may include composite quantitative metrics such as a Proposed Conceptual Scalp Health Index, multimodal treatment-monitoring dashboards, portable chairside imaging platforms, and eventually smartphone-linked or remote monitoring systems. These concepts should be considered future research trajectories rather than current clinical capabilities. Their realization will depend on robust biomarker validation, usability testing, regulatory approval pathways, evidence of cost-effectiveness, and demonstration that such systems improve clinical outcomes or decision-making. Conclusion

HSI and THz imaging represent promising but still investigational approaches to hair-loss assessment. Their principal advantage over conventional diagnostic methods lies in the breadth, repeatability, and quantitative nature of the physiological information they may provide. Whereas trichoscopy captures visible hair-shaft and follicular-surface morphology and biopsy provides detailed histopathological information from a small, invasively sampled area, HSI may permit contactless, wide-field mapping of haemoglobin, oxygenation, vascularity, erythema, pigmentation, and hydration. THz imaging may offer a complementary assessment of tissue-water distribution, dielectric properties, fibrosis, and structural remodelling.

These features may make HSI and THz imaging better suited for repeated assessment of scalp physiology, disease activity, and treatment response than methods that rely mainly on visual pattern recognition or localized tissue sampling. Their combined use could extend scalp evaluation beyond static morphology by generating objective maps of functional and biochemical changes across broader scalp regions. Such capabilities may be particularly valuable for longitudinal monitoring, identification of subtle treatment-related changes, and development of quantitative imaging biomarkers. Their potential superiority, however, lies in the additional information they may generate rather than in currently demonstrated diagnostic accuracy, and they should therefore be regarded as adjuncts rather than replacements for trichoscopy or biopsy.

As direct clinical evidence supporting HSI and THz imaging in alopecia remains limited, future progress will require prospective alopecia-specific studies; standardized and calibration protocols; reproducible quantitative parameters; normative datasets encompassing diverse scalp regions, hair characteristics, and alopecia subtypes; and correlation with established clinical, trichoscopic, histological, and treatment-response endpoints. With such validation and appropriate workflow integration, HSI and THz imaging could provide a more objective, data-rich, and personalized framework for hair-loss assessment and monitoring.
