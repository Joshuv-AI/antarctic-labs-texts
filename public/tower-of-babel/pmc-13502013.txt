A Rosella-PLIN2 knock-in mouse reveals lipophagy and immunometabolic interplay in atherosclerosis
Authors: Thomas Laval, Nathan Joyce, Dominique M Boucher, Honora Labrana, Valérie Rochon, Christina Emerton, Mishita Dharia, Sabrina Robichaud, Victoria Lorant, My-Anh Nguyen, Michèle Geoffrion, Katey J Rayner, Derrick Gibbings, Lauryl M J Nutter, Ryan C Russell, Mireille Ouimet
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCytosolic lipid droplets (LDs) regulate lipid homeostasis, with abnormal LD dynamics linked to metabolic diseases like atherosclerosis. In macrophage foam cells, LDs undergo autophagic degradation via lipophagy, but the extent of this process in vascular smooth muscle cell (VSMC) foam cells remains unclear. To track lipophagy in real time, we developed a Rosella-PLIN2 (perilipin 2) biosensor by tagging PLIN2 with the fluorescent pH-biosensor Rosella. We show that proatherogenic lipoproteins and autophagy activators stimulate lipophagy in human macrophages. Targeting LDs with an LC3 fusion protein or LD-autophagy tethering compounds (LD-ATTECs) selectively enhanced lipophagy, promoting foam cell LD clearance. In an atherosclerosis model, Rosella-PLIN2 accurately tracked lipophagy in arterial foam cells, revealing distinct PLIN2 expression patterns in macrophage and non-leukocyte foam cells. We identified a lipophagy deficiency in VSMC foam cells and demonstrate that enhancing lipophagy promotes LD catabolism in primary VSMC foam cells. TREM2+ macrophages exhibited high lipid content and low lipophagy flux, whereas TREM2− macrophages had low lipid content and high lipophagy flux. Our findings highlight a cell-specific interplay between lipophagy and immunometabolism in arterial foam cells, unveiling novel therapeutic avenues for atherosclerosis. Introduction

Lipid droplets (LDs) are dynamic and ubiquitous organelles that store triglycerides and cholesterol esters, playing a central role in lipid metabolism. In response to environmental stressors, such as excess lipids or inflammatory signals, LDs accumulate in foam cells. Foam cells, immune-active lipid-laden cells that can profoundly reshape metabolic and inflammatory pathways, are the hallmark of atherosclerotic plaques that develop as a result of chronic inflammation and the accumulation of modified forms of cholesterol-rich low-density lipoproteins (LDL) [1,2]. Traditionally, these foam cells were thought to originate primarily from monocyte-derived macrophages [2]. However, compelling evidence from human and mouse studies challenges this dogma, revealing that vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) account for 30–70% of foam cells in atherosclerotic plaques [3–7]. Intriguingly, despite their shared foam cell identity, these two cell types exhibit distinct metabolic fates for modified LDL-derived cholesterol and LDs [8], underscoring the need to unravel their respective roles in atherosclerosis.

A promising strategy to mitigate cholesterol accumulation in plaques is to enhance cholesterol efflux from foam cells, the rate-limiting step of reverse cholesterol transport (RCT) – a potent anti-atherogenic process that facilitates cholesterol excretion via high-density lipoproteins (HDL) [9]. This process hinges on the release of free cholesterol from LDs, mediated by neutral cytoplasmic lipases or LIPA/LAL (lysosomal acid lipase A) following LD delivery to lysosomes [10]. Autophagy, a degradative pathway that traffics intracellular cargo to lysosomes, plays a crucial role in regulating macrophage LD breakdown and cholesterol efflux [10]. In macrophage foam cells, autophagy-mediated cholesterol efflux occurs through a selective process known as lipophagy, where LDs are tethered to autophagosomes via MAP1LC3 (microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3) and delivered to lysosomes [11,12]. Notably, several candidate lipophagy factors have been identified, many of which become dysregulated during atherogenesis, linking impaired lipophagy to disease progression [11,13–15].

Autophagy flux declines as atherosclerosis advances [6,16], with an even greater impairment observed in VSMC-derived foam cells compared to their macrophage counterparts [6]. However, critical questions remain, such as whether all plaque foam cells form bona fide LDs and how lipophagy is selectively regulated across distinct foam cell populations [17]. To this end, we developed a Rosella-PLIN2 (perilipin 2) lipophagy reporter by tagging the ubiquitous LD coat protein PLIN2 [18] with the pH-sensitive biosensor Rosella [19]. Leveraging this novel lipophagy reporter and selective autophagy enhancers, we show that lipophagy regulates macrophage foam cell immunometabolism. Furthermore, we introduce the Rosella-PLIN2 knock-in mouse – the first whole-body lipophagy reporter model – offering unprecedented insights into LD metabolism in vivo.

In a model of atherosclerosis, we show that macrophage and non-leukocyte foam cells exhibit distinct patterns of LD accumulation, accompanied by differential PLIN2 expression. Furthermore, we provide strong evidence that enhancing lipophagy drives LD catabolism in primary VSMC foam cells. We also identify foam cell subpopulations within atherosclerotic lesions that exhibit varying levels of lipophagy activity, such as Lipidhi Lipophagylo TREM2+ macrophages. Our findings uncover a cell-specific interplay between lipophagy and immunometabolism in arterial foam cells, while the Rosella-PLIN2 model provides a powerful tool for advancing the study of LD metabolism in health and disease.

Results

A new lipophagy biosensor

Several biosensors have been developed to monitor autophagy in live cells non-invasively, including Rosella, which leverages pH changes during autophagy [19]. Rosella consists of a tandem fusion of the pH-stable DsRed.T2 and the pH-sensitive pHluorin. Under neutral conditions both fluorophores fluoresce, yielding red and green signals, while in acidic lysosomes pHluorin is quenched, leaving only red fluorescence [19]. This enables tracking of autophagic cargo degradation via fluorescence microscopy and flow cytometry. Perilipins (PLINs) are key LD structural proteins, with PLIN2 being ubiquitously expressed [18] and the predominant LD coat protein in macrophage foam cells [10]. To selectively track lipophagy, we generated a Rosella-PLIN2 fusion protein reporter by tagging PLIN2 at its N terminus with Rosella (Figure 1(A)). Transient expression in oleic acid-treated HEK293 cells confirmed localization of Rosella-PLIN2 to cytosolic LDs (Figure 1(B)). Under basal conditions, LDs appeared as yellow rings, reflecting dual fluorescence at neutral pH and consistent with cytosolic LDs (Figure 1(B)). Upon autophagy induction with rapamycin, red-only puncta emerged within LysoTracker-positive lysosomes, validating that the biosensor reliably reports LD cargo delivery into the acidic compartment (Figure 1(B)). Quantification further demonstrated a significant rapamycin-induced increase in lipophagy flux (Figure 1(C)).

Next, we established a stably expressing Rosella-PLIN2 THP-1 monocytic cell line. As expected, Rosella-PLIN2 localized to the surface of LDs in differentiated THP-1 macrophages (Figure 1(D)). Aggregated LDL (agLDL) loading increased overall cellular LD content (Figure 1(E)), and consistent with this expanded LD pool, we also observed an increase in lipophagy flux (Figure 1(E)). Following agLDL loading, Rosella-PLIN2 THP-1 macrophages accumulated neutral lipids comparably to wild-type cells (Figure 1(G,H)). Upon rapamycin or agLDL treatment, the number of red-only puncta increased relative to controls, consistent with delivery of LD cargo to acidified lysosomes where pHluorin is quenched (Figure 1(I)).

The role of lysosomal acidification was confirmed with bafilomycin A1 (Baf-A1), a vacuolar-type H+ -ATPase inhibitor that prevented lysosomal acidification and thereby stabilized pHluorin fluorescence. In Baf-A1-treated cells, red-only puncta reverted to dual red/green fluorescence (Figure 1I), confirming that the reporter’s green quenching was acidification-dependent. Consistent with this mechanism, quantification showed that rapamycin strongly increased lipophagy flux, agLDL modestly increased it, and Baf-A1 suppressed both responses (Figure 1(J)). Thus, Rosella-PLIN2 provided a direct and sensitive readout of lipophagy flux, with Baf-A1 serving as a functional control to confirm that red-only events represented bona fide autophagic degradation.

Western blot analysis confirmed that Rosella-PLIN2 was expressed at the expected molecular weight without interfering with endogenous PLIN2 upregulation following agLDL loading (Figure 1(K)). Core autophagy flux markers, including LC3-I/II conversion, SQSTM1/p62, and phospho-ATG16L1 (p-ATG16L1), remained comparable between wild-type and Rosella-PLIN2 THP-1 macrophages (Figure 1(K)), confirming that reporter expression did not perturb basal autophagy.

Pharmacological and genetic manipulations further validated the biosensor. The MTOR inhibitor torin-1, like rapamycin, increased dsRed+/pHluorin− lipophagy events in oleic acid-loaded Rosella-PLIN2 THP-1 macrophages (Figure 1(L)). Colocalization with LysoTracker confirmed the lysosomal identity of these red-only puncta (Figure 1(L)). Recent studies implicate SPART/SPG20 [15], OSBPL8/ORP8 [13] and VPS4A [14] in selective LD turnover. siRNA-mediated knockdown of ATG5, SPART, OSBPL8 and VPS4A in Rosella-PLIN2 THP-1 macrophages reduced target mRNA by >80% (Figure 1(M)). In control siRNA cells, torin-1 treatment increased lipophagy (red-only puncta) (Figure 1(N,O)). However, knockdown of ATG5 abolished this effect, confirming dependence on core autophagy machinery (Figure 1(N,O)). Similarly, knockdown of SPART, VPS4A, and OSBPL8 attenuated torin-1-induced lipophagy (Figure 1(N,O)), supporting their role in macrophage lipophagy regulation. Collectively, these findings demonstrated that Rosella-PLIN2 faithfully reported lipophagy flux in live macrophages, provided a sensitive tool for dissecting lipid droplet turnover, and can be combined with genetic and pharmacological approaches to define regulatory mechanisms.

Selective activation of lipophagy promotes macrophage LD catabolism

A recent advancement in the field of lipophagy research was the development of the first specific lipophagy-inducing compounds, achieved by linking LD- and LC3-interacting molecules (Figure 2(A)). These LD-autophagy tethering compounds (LD-ATTECs) recruit LC3 to LDs, thereby promoting lipophagic LD degradation [20]. Within lysosomes, the hydrolysis of LD cholesterol esters by LAL generates free cholesterol, which can be exported via ABCA1-mediated efflux [10]. In parallel, free fatty acids derived from LD triglycerides and/or cholesterol esters through lipophagy can be channeled to mitochondria for β-oxidation [12]. In line with this, LD-ATTECs enhanced cholesterol efflux to lipid-poor APOA1 (Figure 2(G)) and increased oxygen consumption, indicating elevated mitochondrial respiration in LD-ATTEC-treated cells (Figure 2(H,I)).

To further investigate the potential of selective lipophagy activation to reverse lipid accumulation in macrophage foam cells, we utilized anti-GFP nanobodies fused to LC3 (anti-GFP-LC3) to target Rosella-PLIN2 LDs and promote autophagosome biogenesis at their surface (Figure 2(J)). We generated Rosella-PLIN2-expressing THP-1 cell lines stably expressing either anti-GFP alone or anti-GFP-LC3 under the control of a doxycycline-inducible promoter. As expected, doxycycline treatment significantly increased the expression of both anti-GFP and anti-GFP-LC3 nanobodies (Figure 2(K)). Both the anti-GFP and anti-GFP-LC3 are predicted to recognize the pHluorin (GFP) domain of Rosella-PLIN2 [21], but only anti-GFP-LC3 should initiate autophagosome biogenesis at the LD surface. We confirmed that anti-GFP and anti-GFP-LC3 localized to Rosella-PLIN2-tagged LDs, indicating successful targeting of the Rosella-PLIN2 pHluorin domain (Figure 2(l)). Notably, anti-GFP-LC3 expression upon doxycycline treatment led to a substantial increase in lipophagy events compared to cells expressing anti-GFP alone (Figure 2(M,N)). This was accompanied by a concomitant reduction in cellular LD content in cells expressing anti-GFP-LC3 as compared to anti-GFP alone (Figure 2(O)). Furthermore, oleic acid (OA)-loaded macrophages exhibited increased oxygen consumption following doxycycline-induced anti-GFP-LC3 expression compared to control (Figure 2(P)), reinforcing the findings observed with LD-ATTECs. Together, these results provided strong evidence that selective lipophagy activation, through either LD-ATTECs or anti-GFP-LC3 nanobodies, effectively promoted LD degradation, cholesterol efflux, and enhanced mitochondrial respiration in macrophage foam cells.

Enhancing lipophagy promotes an anti-inflammatory macrophage phenotype

TLR (toll-like receptor) signaling is implicated in macrophage LD accumulation within human atherosclerotic plaques [22]. Furthermore, TLR stimulation can regulate both macrophage autophagy and LD formation [23–26], suggesting that lipophagy may play a role in TLR-dependent modulation of macrophage LD metabolism. To investigate this, we stimulated Rosella-PLIN2 THP-1 macrophages for 24 h with TLR2 ligands Pam3Csk4 and lipoteichoic acid (LTA) from S. aureus, as well as the TLR4 ligand lipopolysaccharide (LPS). Notably, TLR2 ligands, but not LPS, significantly increased lipophagy, reaching levels comparable to those induced by torin-1 (Figure 3(A)). Unlike torin-1, however, TLR2 ligands did not enhance general autophagy flux at 24 h (Figure 3(B)), suggesting a selective effect on macrophage lipophagy.

Given the known role of LD dynamics in macrophage immune responses [27,28], our observation that lipophagy is modulated by TLR signaling in macrophage foam cells suggested a potential link between macrophage polarization and lipophagy. Supporting this, Rosella-PLIN2 THP-1 macrophages exhibited increased lipophagy events following M1-like polarization but not M2-like polarization (Figure 3(C-D)). To further investigate this, we induced the expression of anti-GFP-LC3 in Rosella-PLIN2 THP-1 macrophages preloaded with oleic acid and polarized them toward M1 or M2 phenotypes, using CD80 and MRC1/CD206 as respective markers (Figure 3(E-G)). Doxycycline treatment increased lipophagy flux in anti-GFP-LC3 but not anti-GFP expressing M1 and M2 Rosella-PLIN2 macrophages (Figure 3(E)). Upon doxycycline treatment, anti-GFP-LC3 M1-like macrophages displayed lower CD80 expression compared to anti-GFP-Ctrl (Figure 3(F)), while anti-GFP-LC3 M2-like macrophages exhibited upregulated MRC1/CD206 expression, with a more modest increase observed in M0 macrophages (Figure 3(G)). These findings revealed that lipophagy stimulation promoted an anti-inflammatory macrophage phenotype.

Generation of Rosella-PLIN2 biosensor knock-in mice to track lipophagy in vivo

To investigate the regulation of lipophagy during atherosclerosis development, we generated the first-ever lipophagy reporter mouse, leveraging our validated Rosella-PLIN2 biosensor. Using a knock-in strategy previously employed to generate tdTom-PLIN2 mice without histological alterations [29], we inserted the Rosella gene upstream of the endogenous Plin2 locus with a short linker sequence (Figure 4(A)). This innovative design enabled real-time tracking of lipophagy in vivo. Rosella-PLIN2 fluorescence was readily detectable in the livers of homozygous knock-in mice (Figure 4(B)). Mice fed a western diet exhibited higher hepatic Rosella-PLIN2 expression than chow-fed controls, reflecting the increased demand for PLIN2 to coat LDs storing excess dietary lipids (Figure 4(B)). Western blot analysis confirmed this observation, showing elevated hepatic Rosella-PLIN2 protein levels in western diet-fed mice compared to those on chow, with the magnitude of induction closely matching that of endogenous PLIN2 in wild-type mice (Figure 4(C)).

To validate the functionality of the Rosella-PLIN2 biosensor in macrophages, we isolated BMDMs from Rosella-PLIN2 mice and exposed them to agLDL. Rosella-PLIN2 expression was significantly increased in agLDL-loaded BMDMs compared to their unloaded counterparts (Figure 4(D)). Rosella-tagged LDs accumulated under these conditions, and distinct red-only puncta were observed co-localizing with lysosomes (Figure 4(E)), providing direct evidence of lipophagy. The pH sensitivity of the Rosella-PLIN2 reporter was further confirmed by treating cells with Baf-A1, which partially restored pHluorin fluorescence in lysosome-localized puncta and reduced lipophagy flux relative to controls (Figure 4(E,F)). To further assess biosensor functionality under enhanced lipophagy conditions, agLDL-loaded BMDMs were treated LD-ATTECs in APOA1-supplemented media. LD-ATTEC treatment significantly enhanced lipophagy while simultaneously reducing LD content compared to controls, an effect abolished in the presence of Baf-A1 (Figure 4(G,H)), confirming preserved lipophagy activity in Rosella-PLIN2 macrophages.

In line with our observations in THP-1 macrophages, lipophagy was further increased in M1-like polarized primary BMDMs relative to M0 and M2 phenotypes (Figure 4(I,J)), reinforcing the link between macrophage activation states and lipophagy regulation. Overall, our results established the Rosella-PLIN2 knock-in mouse as a novel tool for studying lipophagy dynamics in vivo. Consistent with findings in tdTom-Plin2 mice [29], our data confirmed that endogenous Rosella tagging of Plin2 did not alter LD metabolism, making this model a powerful new resource for investigating the role of lipophagy in cardiometabolic diseases.

PLIN2 is the predominant marker of foam cells in atherosclerosis

VSMCs constitute 30–70% of foam cells in human and murine atherosclerotic lesions [3–7]. As VSMCs transdifferentiate into macrophage-like foam cells, they acquire CD68 expression but lack the pan-leukocyte marker PTPRC/CD45, distinguishing them from macrophage foam cells [4,5,7,30]. Accordingly, macrophage and VSMC foam cells can be identified within atherosclerotic lesions as PTPRC/CD45+ CD68+ and PTPRC/CD45− CD68+, respectively.

To investigate whether VSMC-derived foam cells store lipids in bona fide LDs in vivo, we used Rosella-PLIN2 knock-in mice as reporters of PLIN2 expression. Hypercholesterolemia was induced in both wild-type and Rosella-PLIN2 mice through AAV8-gain-of-function PCSK9 overexpression and western diet feeding for 12 weeks. Analysis of aortic sinus lesions revealed that PTPRC/CD45+ CD68+, PTPRC/CD45+ CD68−, PTPRC/CD45− CD68+, and PTPRC/CD45− CD68− populations were present in similar proportions across genotypes and sexes (Figure 5(A-C)). In wild-type lesions, immunofluorescence for PLIN2 revealed that PTPRC/CD45− CD68+ cells comprised the majority of PLIN2+ foam cells (~25% of total plaque cells), followed by PTPRC/CD45+ CD68+ macrophages (~15%) (Figure 5(C)). Quantification of Rosella reporter expression (Rosella+ : DsRed+ pHluorin+) produced similar results (Figure 5(C)), confirming that the Rosella tag faithfully reflected endogenous PLIN2 expression within plaque subsets.

Although macrophages (PTPRC/CD45+ CD68+) were less abundant overall than VSMC-derived foam cells (PTPRC/CD45− CD68+), macrophages exhibited a higher proportion of PLIN2+ cells within their own population (~60%) (Figure 5(D)). Nonetheless, the much larger number of PTPRC/CD45− CD68+ cells (~50% of total plaque cells; Figure 5(B)) meant that they contributed the majority of total PLIN2+ cells in plaques (~25% of total plaque cells; Figure 5(C)), corresponding to ~65% of all PLIN2+ cells in plaques (Figure 5(E)), consistent with prior reports that most plaque foam cells originate from VSMCs [4,6]. Collectively, these findings indicated that macrophages were intrinsically more prone to forming PLIN2+ LDs, whereas VSMC-derived foam cells constituted the dominant PLIN2+ population in vivo due to their prevalence in atherosclerotic plaques.

Both PTPRC/CD45+ CD68+ and PTPRC/CD45− CD68+ foam cells in Rosella-PLIN2 mice exhibited comparable DsRed and pHluorin fluorescence intensities, detectable by widefield microscopy well above the autofluorescence observed in wild-type controls (Figure 5(F)). Mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of both DsRed and pHluorin was similar between foam cell subsets (Figure 5(F)), indicating comparable PLIN2 expression levels. As expected, Rosella-PLIN2 expression was rare among CD68− plaque cells (Figure 5(C,D)), consistent with CD68 as a marker of foam cells in atherosclerosis.

We next examined whether additional PLINs contribute to foam cell LD formation in plaque foam cells. Co-staining for PLIN3 alongside PLIN2 revealed that most PLIN3+ cells also expressed PLIN2 (Figure 5(G,H)). PLIN3+ PLIN2− cells were present but comprised only 2.8% of PTPRC/CD45+ CD68+ macrophages and 12.5% of PTPRC/CD45− CD68+ VSMC-derived cells (Figure 5(H)). Among all cells expressing at least one perilipin, these values corresponded to 3.2% and 20% of macrophage- and VSMC-derived subsets, respectively (Figure 5(H)). Notably, ~40% of PTPRC/CD45− CD68+ cells lacked detectable PLIN2 or PLIN3 (Figure 5(H)), suggesting that lipids in these cells are either stored outside classical LDs or within droplets coated by other PLINs, as reported in other cell types [31]. In summary, VSMC-derived foam cells formed abundant PLIN2+ LDs in vivo and represented the predominant PLIN2-expressing population within atherosclerotic plaques, while macrophages exhibited a higher intrinsic propensity for PLIN2+ LD formation. These findings validated the Rosella-PLIN2 knock-in model as a powerful tool for tracking LD dynamics and lipophagy across distinct foam cell lineages.

Lipophagy promotes LD degradation in VSMC foam cells

We next tested whether VSMCs possess the capacity to degrade LDs through lipophagy. Primary VSMCs were loaded with water-soluble cholesterol (WSC) to induce foam cell formation. WSC loading markedly increased neutral lipid content and robustly upregulated PLIN2 expression and coating on LDs (Figure S1). In WSC-loaded VSMCs, PLIN2 colocalized extensively with neutral lipids, with most LDs exhibiting PLIN2 positivity (Figure 5(I) and Figure S1). A comparable PLIN2-LD pattern was observed in WSC-loaded BMDMs (Figure 5(J) and Figure S1), indicating that both cell types generated bona fide PLIN2+ LDs under cholesterol-rich conditions.

To determine whether VSMC foam cells can actively catabolize LDs via lipophagy, we pharmacologically stimulated the pathway using LD-ATTECs. LD-ATTEC treatment significantly reduced LD abundance in WSC-loaded VSMCs (Figure 5(K)), mirroring the reduction observed in WSC-loaded BMDMs (Figure 5(L)). Consistent with the nonselective, neutral-lipid-binding Sudan moiety incorporated into LD-ATTECs, all LD subpopulations (PLIN2+ PLIN3+, PLIN2+ PLIN3−, PLIN2− PLIN3+, and PLIN2− PLIN3−) were similarly reduced in both VSMC and BMDM foam cells (Figure 5(K,L)). However, because PLIN2+ LDs represented the predominant LD population under these conditions, the majority of LDs undergoing degradation were PLIN2+. Together, these findings demonstrated that VSMC-derived foam cells not only formed authentic PLIN2+ LDs in vivo and in cholesterol-loaded cultures, but also possessed an intrinsic ability to remodel these lipid stores through lipophagy-driven LD degradation.

Impaired lipophagy in TREM2+ and pro-inflammatory macrophage foam cells

To quantify lipophagy flux in foam cell populations, we analyzed Rosella-expressing cells isolated from the aortic arches of 12-week western diet-fed Rosella-PLIN2 mice using flow cytometry. As previously described [6], aortic foam cells were identified based on granularity and neutral lipid content (Lipidhi: MDH+ SSChi), while Rosella+ cells were gated by pHluorin and DsRed fluorescence (Rosella+: DsRed+ pHluorin+) (Figure 6(A)). Validating this strategy, Rosella+ cells represented 55.8% of Lipidhi foam cells and 2.7% of Lipidlo non-foam cells (Figure 6(B)). The distribution of major atheroma cell types (endothelial: PTPRC/CD45− PECAM1/CD31+; non-leukocyte: PTPRC/CD45− PECAM1/CD31−; leukocyte: PTPRC/CD45+; non-myeloid leukocyte: PTPRC/CD45+ ITGAM/CD11b− FCGR1/CD64−; macrophage: PTPRC/CD45+ ITGAM/CD11b+ FCGR1/CD64+) was similar between total foam cells and Rosella+ cells, with <50% of either population comprising leukocytes (Figure 6(C)), consistent with prior studies [4,6]. Rosella expression was observed in ~85%, ~65%, and ~50% of macrophage, endothelial, and non-leukocyte foam cells, respectively (Figure 6(D)). Together with aortic sinus data (Figure 5), these findings indicated that PLIN2 expression is enriched in macrophages within atherosclerotic lesions.

We next quantified lipid content in Rosella+ cells classified by DsRed:pHluorin fluorescence ratios, designating subsets as Lipophagyhi or Lipophagylo. DsRed and pHluorin signals min-max scaled (0–1), and their ratio was calculated to represent lipophagy flux. Subsets were defined relative to the peak of this ratio distribution: cells above the peak were classified as Lipophagyhi, and those below as Lipophagylo. Thresholds were applied separately for each cell type to account for differences in size and lipid loading that influence dynamic range. Lipophagylo macrophage and endothelial foam cells contained significantly more neutral lipids than their Lipophagyhi counterparts, consistent with reduced LD catabolism (Figure 6(E)). In contrast, non-leukocyte foam cells showed minimal, non-significant differences in lipid content between Lipophagyhi and Lipophagylo subsets, suggesting impaired lipophagy in this population (Figure 6(E)). Ex vivo treatment of aortic digests with Baf-A1 supported these findings. Baf-A1 reduced the proportion of Lipophagyhi macrophages, as expected for functional flux, but had no effect on non-leukocytes, further confirming impaired lipophagy in this population (Figure 6(F)).

To determine if macrophage subpopulations with distinct inflammatory and metabolic profiles exhibit variations in lipophagy flux, we compared lipophagy flux and lipid content in Rosella+ macrophage foam cells expressing or lacking surface markers of inflammation such as MRC1/CD206, CD38, and TREM2, which have previously been linked to macrophage function in atherosclerotic lesions [22,32–35]. TREM2+ macrophage foam cells exhibited a higher lipid content than TREM2− cells but had significantly lower lipophagy flux (Figure 6(G,H)). In contrast, MRC1/CD206+ and CD38+ macrophages had higher lipophagy flux and lower lipid content, especially among TREM2− macrophages (Figure 6G,H). These findings indicated that macrophage lipophagy is highly heterogeneous and tightly regulated within atherosclerotic lesions.

To test if macrophage lipophagy heterogeneity is specific to the atheroma microenvironment, we elicited the recruitment of macrophage foam cells to the peritoneum of hypercholesterolemic Rosella-PLIN2 mice using thioglycolate, as previously described [36]. Consistent with our lesion data, lipophagy flux in TREM2+ and MHC-II+ macrophages was significantly lower compared to their TREM2− and MHC-II− counterparts, while CD38+ macrophages showed a much higher lipophagy flux (Figure 6(I)). These results suggested that TREM2+, CD38− and MHC-II+ pro-inflammatory macrophage foam cells exhibited impaired lipophagy, accompanied by lipid accumulation, regardless of tissue environment. Overall, our findings highlighted the dynamic lipid storage behavior of VSMC-derived foam cells in atherosclerosis and demonstrated the utility of the Rosella-PLIN2 model for studying LD formation and lipophagy flux in vivo across diverse foam cell populations.

Discussion

Autophagy is essential for regulating macrophage functions such as efferocytosis, cholesterol efflux, and inflammasome activation – key processes for cardiovascular health. In atherosclerosis, marked by lipid accumulation and inflammation in the arterial wall, impaired autophagy is observed as a critical event that accelerates disease progression [6,16,37]. The crucial role of macrophage LD metabolism in atherosclerosis is emphasized by studies showing reduced foam cell lipid burden and plaque size in mice lacking whole-body or bone marrow-specific expression of the LD-associated protein PLIN2 [38]. Similarly, organelle-specific autophagic pathways are gaining attention for their targeted impact on cardiometabolism [17,39,40]. However, the regulation of lipophagy, the selective autophagic degradation of LDs, remains poorly understood, especially in vivo.

In this study, we investigated the role of lipophagy in regulating foam cell immunometabolism. Using a novel Rosella-PLIN2 fluorescent lipophagy reporter, we successfully tracked lipophagy in human THP-1 macrophages and for the first time in vivo in a mouse model of atherosclerosis. We found that MTOR inhibition induced macrophage lipophagy, but that this effect could be blocked by disrupting the autophagic machinery or knocking down key lipophagy factors (VPS4A, SPART, OSBPL8) [13–15]. Furthermore, stimulation of lipophagy using LD-ATTECs or anti-GFP-LC3 chimeric nanobodies enhanced LD degradation, mitochondrial respiration and cholesterol efflux in macrophage foam cells, supporting the idea that lipophagy plays a central role in macrophage lipid metabolism, independent of other autophagy pathways [11].

Autophagy is modulated by cytokine- and TLR-mediated macrophage activation, influencing their inflammatory polarization [23,24,41]. Given that activated macrophages generate LDs [25,26,42], we hypothesized that lipophagy contributes to macrophage polarization. Indeed, lipophagy flux was upregulated in TLR2-stimulated and M1 macrophages, but not in M2 macrophages. Interestingly, lipophagy stimulation mitigated CD80 upregulation in M1 macrophages, suggesting that lipophagy provides negative feedback on pro-inflammatory activation; however, it remains to be determined whether overactivation of lipophagy could deplete lipid-derived mediators required to sustain inflammatory polarization. Conversely, stimulating lipophagy during M2 polarization enhanced MRC1/CD206 expression, consistent with the observation that lysosomal lipolysis sustains M2 activation [42]. Together, this data highlights the role of lipophagy in regulating macrophage foam cell immunometabolism, a novel therapeutic avenue for the treatment of atherosclerosis [43,44].

Research on LD metabolism in arterial foam cells has been limited by the lack of lipophagy-specific tools and factors [17]. To track LDs and lipophagy flux in vivo, we used the Rosella-PLIN2 biosensor in C57BL/6N mice by tagging endogenous Plin2 with Rosella, similarly to tdTom-PLIN2 knock-in mice [29]. Compared to the previously developed tdTom-PLIN2 reporter mouse, our Rosella-PLIN2 biosensor integrated a dual fluorescent, pH-sensitive module (DsRed.T2, which is pH-stable, and pHluorin, which is quenched in acidic environments) that allowed discrimination between cytosolic and lysosomal pools of PLIN2-tagged LDs. This allowed direct, ratiometric quantification of lipophagy flux and real-time tracking of LD delivery to lysosomes. Furthermore, the endogenous knock-in design ensured physiological PLIN2 expression and preserved basal autophagy flux, as validated in our cellular assays. The Rosella-PLIN2 knock-in mouse therefore represents the first in vivo model capable of reporting LD trafficking through the autophagy-lysosome pathway in real time with both spatial and functional resolution, surpassing conventional single-fluorophore models.

This approach enabled the fluorescent tagging of LDs and the quantification of lipophagy flux, allowing us to investigate the regulation of LD metabolism and lipophagy flux across arterial foam cell populations during atherosclerosis. Using CD68 and PTPRC markers, we identified macrophage and VSMC foam cells and quantified PLIN2 expression. We found that macrophage foam cells were highly enriched in PLIN2, while only half of VSMC foam cells were PLIN2+. In line with previous work [8], this supports the existence of a VSMC foam cell population that accumulates lipids outside of LDs, presumably inside LAL-defective lysosomes. Low LAL activity in VSMCs has been shown to underlie this phenotype, leading to defective hydrolysis of lipoprotein-derived cholesteryl esters and the retention of neutral lipids within lysosomes rather than cytosolic droplets. In both human and murine atheromas, intimal VSMCs exhibit markedly reduced LIPA expression and LAL protein compared with macrophages, limiting lysosomal lipid clearance and cholesterol efflux through the LXR-ABCA1 pathway [8]. The phenotypic differences between these newly identified subpopulations of VSMC foam cells may extend to other key cellular features, such as contractility or proliferative capacity.

Compared to macrophages, VSMCs have lower basal autophagy flux and impaired cholesterol efflux due to reduced ABCA1 expression [4]. However, pharmacological activation of autophagy with metformin increased ABCG1-mediated efflux to HDL [6]. Additionally, treatment of cholesterol-loaded VSMCs with HDL or APOA1 abolishes their macrophage-like phenotype, further supporting the atheroprotective potential of cholesterol efflux [45]. Here, stimulating lipophagy flux in primary VSMC foam cells with LD-ATTECs reduced their LD burden, suggesting that despite its low basal activity, lipophagy could be boosted to promote LD degradation in PLIN2+ arterial VSMC foam cells.

Consistent with our aortic sinuses data, flow cytometry analysis of aortic digests from atherosclerotic Rosella-PLIN2 mice revealed that almost all non-foam cells lacked Rosella, while about half of endothelial- and VSMC-like foam cells and 85% of macrophage foam cells expressed Rosella-PLIN2. Lipophagy was inversely correlated with lipid content in macrophage and endothelial foam cells, but not in VSMCs, suggesting a VSMC-specific defect in lipophagy. This finding aligns with our previous study reporting autophagy flux defects in VSMCs compared to macrophages during atherosclerosis progression [6]. LDs were recently implicated in endothelial dysfunction and inflammation, with neutral lipolysis in endothelial cells reported as being atheroprotective [46–48]. While confirming that arterial endothelial cells form bona fide LDs, our results point to endothelial lipophagy as a promising therapeutic target to prevent endothelial dysfunction and inflammation driving atherogenesis.

Emerging evidence identifies TREM2 as marker of a non-inflammatory, foamy macrophage subset in atherosclerotic plaques [49], and recent findings suggest it regulates foam cell formation and survival [32,50]. In our aortic digests from atherosclerotic Rosella-PLIN2 mice, TREM2+ macrophages displayed high lipid content and low lipophagy, suggesting a lipophagy defect that may contribute to their lipid burden. This is consistent with observations that TREM2 signaling inhibits autophagy via MTOR [51], potentially promoting foam cell formation in atherosclerosis. Our in vitro data indicated an interplay between lipophagy and macrophage polarization. Further analysis of arterial foam cell subsets based on CD38 (an M1-like marker) and MRC1/CD206 (an M2-like marker) expression revealed that TREM2− macrophages expressing CD38 and MRC1/CD206 had low lipid content and high lipophagy. Notably, we identified a TREM2− MRC1/CD206− CD38− subpopulation with altered LD metabolism like TREM2+ macrophages. Interestingly, CD38+ macrophages, despite containing LDs, wouldn’t typically be classified as foam cells due to their lipid content. CD38, as a target of LXR, regulates cholesterol metabolism within macrophage foam cells. Its deficiency or inhibition leads to increased cholesterol accumulation in lysosomes, exacerbating atherosclerosis [52,53]. Notably, Cd38 was one of the most significantly downregulated genes in macrophage foam cells from ldlr-/-trem2-/- atherosclerotic mice, compared to ldlr-/- Trem2+/+ mice [50]. This positions CD38 as potential marker of lipophagy-efficient macrophage foam cells and a regulator of cholesterol efflux during atherogenesis.

In conclusion, our findings reveal diverse foam cell subpopulations with heterogenous LD metabolism in atherosclerotic plaques, underscoring the need for targeted strategies in developing lipid metabolism-modulating therapies. The Rosella-PLIN2 mouse, with its ubiquitous PLIN2 expression, presents a promising tool for advancing research on LDs and lipophagy in cardiovascular diseases and beyond. This study highlights the critical role of lipophagy in macrophage immunometabolism, showing how macrophage foam cell LD degradation drives lipid metabolism and inflammatory profiles, offering new insights into atheroprotective mechanisms.

Materials and Methods

Generation of Rosella-Plin2 mice

The Rosella-PLIN2 line was generated at The Centre for Phenogenomics (TCP) under Animal Use Protocol numbers 0008, 0084 and 0329. All procedures on animals at The Center for Phenogenomics were reviewed and approved by TCP’s Animal Care Committee. TCP is certified by the Canadian Council on Animal Care and registered under the Animals for Research Act of Ontario. The Plin2em1Tcp allele (MGI:7489806), here called Rosella-PLIN2, has the Rosella open reading frame inserted between bases 86,586,916 and 86,586,917 of Chr4 (GRCm39). The Rosella-PLIN2 mouse line (C57BL/6N-Plin2em1Tcp, stock AFFT) is available from the Canadian Mouse Mutant Repository at The Center for Phenogenomics (Toronto, ON, Canada).

The Rosella-PLIN2 mouse line was made using adeno-associated virus to deliver the repair template [54] and electroporation to deliver Cas9 ribonucleoprotein [55]. The repair template consisted of 500-bp homology arms flanking the Rosella cDNA [19] between AAV ITRs and was synthesized and packaged into AAV by VectorBuilder (Chicago, IL, USA). On the day of embryo manipulation, 1200 ng/µL of Cas9 protein (Integrated DNA Technologies, 1,074,182) was complexed with 378 ng/µL Plin2 sgRNA (5’-AGCAGTAGTGGATCCGCAAC-3’) made using the EnGen sgRNA synthesis kit (New England Biolabs, E3322) in 1X Cas9 buffer (100 mM KCl, 20 mM HEPES, pH 7.2–7.4) for 10 min at 37°C and kept on ice until electroporation. C57BL/NCrl (Charles River Laboratories, strain code 027) mouse zygotes were collected from superovulated and mated females, treated with hyaluronidase to remove cumulus masses [56], then treated briefly with acid Tyrode’s [55], and placed in pre-equilibrated KSOMAA (Zenith Biotech, ZEKS-50) containing ~108 AAV/µL. Zygotes were co-incubated with AAV for 4 h at 37°C with 6% CO2 prior to being washed and placed in a 1:1 mixture of Cas9 RNP and Opti-MEM (ThermoFisher 31,985,062). Zygotes were electroporated with 12 × 30 V 1-msec pulses with 100 msec intervals. Electroporated zygotes were returned to KSOMAA media with AAV and incubated at 37°C with 6% CO2 overnight. Two-cell embryos were washed and then transferred into CD-1 (Charles River Labs, strain code 022) surrogate host mothers for gestation and birth.

Founders with the Rosella insertion were identified among born pups using genomic DNA for PCR with primers flanking the sgRNA target site and from inside the Rosella CDS to outside the 5’ homology arm, before quality control (QC). Template copy number was assessed by real-time PCR (ThermoFisher, Viia7) using a known single-copy DsRed knock-in mouse as a calibrator. Founders without extra template copies were then assessed by long-range PCR followed by long-read sequencing of PCR amplicons (Plasmidsaurus, San Francisco, CA, USA) to confirm site-specific integration and sequence integrity. Four founders that passed QC were bred to C57BL/6NCrl mice. Born pups were identified with the same PCRs and subjected to the same quality control as founders. N1 mice that passed QC were used to establish the Rosella-PLIN2 mouse line by intercrossing.

Animal experiments

All animal procedures were approved by the University of Ottawa Animal Care and Use Committee. Male and female C57BL/6NCrl or Rosella-PLIN2 mice were used for primary cell culture experiments and to monitor LD dynamics in foam cells during atherosclerosis development. Male and female mice aged 8 to 10 weeks were injected with 5 × 1011 particles of AAV-PCSK9 (University of Pennsylvania) in the peritoneum and fed a western Diet (Envigo, TD.88137) for 12, 16 or 24 weeks. Mice were randomly assigned to the respective cohorts as described. For the analysis of foamy peritoneal macrophages, 12-week western diet-fed Rosella-PLIN2 mice received an intraperitoneal injection of 1 mL 3% thioglycolate (BD Difco, B243010) 3 days prior to endpoint. The mice were anesthetized by isoflurane inhalation and euthanized by exsanguination. Blood was collected by cardiac puncture and mice were perfused with HBSS (Gibco, 14,175–095).

Plasmids

pLenti-Ef1a-Rosella-hPLIN2 was generated at the uOttawa Genomic Editing and Molecular biology (GEMb) facility. The coding sequence of hPLIN2 was amplified from a vector in the MGC Human ORFeome collection (Transomic Technologies) using oligonucleotides with sequence 5’–AAATCTGATCACATGGCATCCGTTGCAGTTGA-3’ and 5’–TGATTATCATATGACTAGTCCCGGGCTAATGAGTTTTATGCTCAGATCGCTGG-3.’ The Rosella dual fluorescent protein fusion construct was amplified from pAS1NB c Rosella I (Addgene, 71,245; deposited by Mark Prescott) [19] using primers 5’-ACTAGCCTCGAGGTTTAAACTACGGGCCACCATGGCCTCCTC-3’ and 5’–CAACGGATGCCATGTGATCAGATTTGTATAGTTCATCCATGCCT-3’). These two PCR amplicons were then inserted into EcoRI and BamHI cut pWPXLd by Gibson assembly [57] such that Rosella-hPLIN2 fusion expression is driven by the EF1a promoter. Inducible constructs expressing the GFP-nanobody alone or fused to LC3B (pInducer-GFPnano-Ctrl/LC3B) [21] were generated by PCR amplification of GFP-nanobody only and GFP-nanobody fusion constructs in pRGZCV using the following primer pair: 5’-CGCGGCCCCGAACTAGTCCAGTGTGGCGGCCGCGGATCCGCCG-3’ and 5’–CTAGACTCGAGCGGCCGCCACTGTGGGGGCGGAATTTGCGGCCGC-3,’ which were combined in equimolar ratios with BstXI cut pINDUCER20 [58] in an isothermal DNA assembly reaction. Lentivirus particles were generated by co-transfection of pLenti-Ef1a-Rosella-hPLIN2 or plasmids pInducer-GFPnano-Ctrl/LC3B with psPAX2 and pMD2.G (Addgene, 12,260 and 12,259; deposited by Didier Trono) in HEK293FT cells (ATCC, CRL-3216) using Lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagent (Invitrogen, L3000-008). Supernatants were collected 48 h post-transfection, and lentiviral particles were concentrated using Lenti-X Concentrator (Takara Bio, 631,231).

Cell culture

Bone marrow was isolated by perfusion of femurs and tibias from 7- to 12-week-old C57BL/6N mice and cultured in DMEM high glucose (Gibco, 11,965–092) with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, 12,483–020), 2 mM GlutaMAX (Gibco, 25,030–081), 20% L929-conditioned medium, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (Pen-Strep, Gibco, 15,140–122) for 7 days, with media change on day 3. After differentiation, BMDMs were replated in DMEM with 10% FBS, 10% L929-conditioned medium and Pen-Strep (BMDM media). Peritoneal macrophages were harvested from 8-week-old male and female C57BL/6N mice 3 days after intraperitoneal injection of 1 mL 3% thioglycolate and cultured in DMEM with 10% FBS and 1% Pen-Strep. Primary smooth muscle cells were isolated from the thoracic aorta of C57BL/6N or Rosella-PLIN2 mice. The aorta was digested for 20–30 min at 37°C (2 U/mL of Liberase TM [Roche, 05401127001], 2 U/mL of Elastase [Worthington Biochemical, LS002274-25 MG] in HBSS), as previously described [6]. Cells were plated on 0.1% gelatin (Sigma, ES-006-B) coated plates for the first passage, and cultured in DMEM high glucose, pyruvate (Gibco, 11,995,073) supplemented with 20% FBS, 1% Antibiotic-Antimycotic (Gibco, 15,240–062), 1x L-glutamine (Gibco, 25,030–081), 10 ng/mL of recombinant mouse LIF (LIF interleukin 6 family cytokine; PeproTech, 250–02-25UG) and 0.1 mM 2-mercaptoethanol (MP Biomedicals, 194,705).

THP-1 and HEK293T cells were purchased from and authenticated by ATCC (TIB-202 and CRL-3216 respectively) and confirmed mycoplasma-negative before experiments. THP-1 and HEK293T were respectively cultured in RPMI-1640 (Gibco, 11,875–093) and DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and Pen-Strep. THP-1 monocytes were differentiated into macrophages by incubation with 25 ng/mL PMA (ThermoFisher Scientific, J63916.MCR) for 72 h. Where specified, macrophages were transfected in OptiMEM media (Gibco, 31,985,070) with Human On-Target Plus siRNA SmartPools (Dharmacon) using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen, 13,778–150) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After 24 h, an equal volume of THP-1 media was added, and cells were incubated for another 24 h before lipid loading and treatments. Stable THP-1 cell lines (Rosella-PLIN2 and doxycycline-inducible anti-GFP-Ctrl and anti-GFP-LC3B) were generated by lentiviral transduction of monocytes in the presence of 8 µg/ml of polybrene (Sigma, H9268). Rosella-PLIN2 cells were selected by fluorescence-activated cell sorting using the BD FACS Aria IIIu (Beckton Dickinson), while anti-GFP-Ctrl and anti-GFP-LC3B THP-1 cell lines were selected with 500 µg/ml of G418 (ThermoFisher Scientific, 108,321-42–2).

Lipid loading and treatments

All macrophages were lipid-loaded by incubation with 50 µg/ml of agLDL for 24 h. Where indicated, differentiated THP-1 macrophages and HEK293T cells were lipid-loaded with 200 µM of oleic acid (OA) pre-complexed with fatty acid-free BSA (Sigma, O1383-1 G) at a 1:3 ratio. VSMCs were lipid-loaded with 35 μg/mL of cholesterol from mβ-CD-cholesterol complexes (Sigma, C4951) for 48 h in DMEM media supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% P/S. Where indicated, lipid-loaded cells were washed and incubated for 24 h in DMEM supplemented with 2 mg/ml of fatty acid-free BSA (Sigma, A6003). During this incubation, cells were treated with rapamycin (100 nM; Tocris, 1292), torin-1 (250 nM; Cayman, 10,997), LD-ATTEC4 (15 µM, synthesized as previously described [20] by Novalix, France) or doxycycline (100 ng/mL; Sigma, D9891-1 G), and, where specified, in presence of 50 μg/mL of human recombinant APOA1 or HDL (generated as previously described [59]).

For autophagy flux analyses, cells were treated for 2 h prior imaging or cell lysis with either chloroquine (30 µM, Sigma, C6628), bafilomycin A1 (75 nM, Invivogen, SML1661) or vehicle control (DMSO). For TLR stimulations, THP-1 macrophages were treated for 24 h in THP-1 media with LPS (100 ng/mL, O111:B4; Sigma, LPS25), Pam3Csk4 (500 ng/mL; Invivogen, 59,804,401) or lipoteichoic acid (100 ng/mL, LTA; Invivogen, NC9694097). For macrophage polarizations, cells were incubated for 24 h with 100 ng/mL of LPS plus 20 ng/mL of murine (PreproTech, 31,505,100 G) or human (R&D Systems, 285-IF-100) IFNG (M1), or with 20 ng/mL of murine IL4 (PreproTech, 214–14-5UG M2 BMDMs) or 20 ng/mL of human IL4 plus IL13 (PreproTech, 200–04-5UG and 200–13-2UG M2 THP-1).

Cell metabolism

Macrophages cultured and treated in Seahorse 96-well plates were washed twice and incubated in XF DMEM or RPMI assay medium (Agilent 103,575,100 or 103,576,100) supplemented with 11 mM XF glucose (Agilent 103,577,100), 1 mM XF pyruvate (Agilent 103,578,100) and 2 mM XF glutamine (Agilent 103,579,100) for 1 h at 37°C without CO2. Oxygen Consumption Rate (OCR) and Extracellular Acidification Rate (ECAR) were measured using the Seahorse XF Mito Stress Test Kit on the XFe96 analyzer according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Inhibitors were added at the following final concentrations: 1 µM of oligomycin, 3 µM of FCCP, 0.5 µM of Rotenone and 1 µM of antimycin A (all from Sigma). At the end of the measurements, live Hoechst 33,342 (Invitrogen) was added to the cells to stain the nuclei, and cells were imaged using the Cytation 5 automated widefield microscope. Cell nuclei were counted using the BioGen 5 software to normalize OCR and ECAR measurements.

Cholesterol efflux

Macrophages were loaded as previously described [59] with 50 μg/mL agLDL and 0.5 μCi/mL 3H-cholesterol (Perkin-Elmer, NET139001MC) in BMDM or THP-1 media for 24 h, followed by two washes in DPBS (Gibco, 14,190,250) and an overnight incubation in DMEM with 2 mg/mL fatty acid-free BSA. Cholesterol efflux to 50 μg/mL human recombinant APOA1 or HDL in the same media was carried out after 24 h in the presence of 15 μM of LD-ATTECs, 100 ng/mL of doxycycline, or vehicle control (DMSO). Supernatants were then removed, and cells were lysed in 0.5 M NaOH. Cell and supernatant radioactivity were quantified using the Hydex Sense plate reader (Gamble). Cholesterol efflux was calculated as a percentage of 3H-cholesterol in the supernatant/(3H-cholesterol in the supernatant + 3H-cholesterol in cells)x100%.

Immunostaining

VSMCs and BMDMs cultured on ibiTreat 18-well chamber slides (Ibidi) were treated as described, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (ThermoFisher Scientific, 043368.9 M) in 1X PBS (ThermoFisher Scientific, BP3994) at 37°C for 10 min, and washed five times with 1X PBS. After blocking and permeabilizing in 5% BSA with 0.1% Triton-X for 30 min at room temperature (RT), cells were incubated overnight at 4°C with anti-PLIN2 (Abcam, ab52356 1:400 or GP40 1:200) and anti-PLIN3 (Proteintech,10694–1-AP 1:200) in 1% BSA with 0.05% saponin. Cells were then incubated with anti-guinea pig IgG Alexa FluorTM 555 (Invitrogen, A21435, 1:1000) and anti-Rabbit IgG Alexa FluorTM Plus 647 (Invitrogen, A32733, 1:1000) secondary antibodies in 1% BSA with 0.05% saponin for 2 h at RT. Cells were washed three times in 1X PBS between all antibody incubations. Cells were counterstained with BODIPY 493/503 (Invitrogen, D3922) for 30 min at RT and with DAPI (Invitrogen, D1306) for 5 min at RT, then washed and left in 1X PBS for imaging.

Mouse hearts were embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound (OCT), flash frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C until cryosectioning. The embedded aortic sinuses were serial sectioned at a 10-µm thickness, and stored at −20°C. Sinuses were fixed for 10 min at RT with 4% PFA (or 3.7% PFA in HEPES pH 7 as described previously [60] for Rosella-PLIN2 hearts) and blocked/permeabilized with 10% Normal Horse Serum (NHS; Vector Labs, VECTS2000) with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS 1X for 30 min at RT. Then primary antibodies (Table 1) were diluted in 1% NHS in PBS 1X and incubated overnight on sinuses at 4°C in a moisture chamber. Secondary antibodies (Table 1) were diluted in 1% NHS in PBS 1X and incubated for 1 h at RT in a moisture chamber. The primary conjugated anti-CD68 antibody (Novus, NPB2-33337AF750; at 1:100) was diluted in 1% NHS and incubated for 2 h at RT in a moisture chamber. All antibody steps were followed by three washes in 1X PBS. The sections were counterstained with DAPI for 5 min at RT, washed and mounted on #1.5 coverslips with Prolong Glass Antifade Mountant (ThermoFisher, P36980). Sinuses were imaged using the inverted Zeiss AxioObserver 7 with the Zen Pro 3.1 software and using the 20x objective. Image processing and image analysis were done using the Zen Pro 3.1 software.

Flow cytometry

THP-1 macrophages were detached using enzyme-free Cell Dissociation Buffer (Gibco, 13,151,014) at 4°C and resuspended in FACS buffer (DPBS with 2% FBS and 1 mM EDTA [Invitrogen, 15,575,020]). To analyze atheroma foam cells, thoracic aortic arches from atherosclerotic mice were dissected, cleaned, and digested with 0.4 units/mL Liberase TM (Roche), 40 units/mL hyaluronidase (Sigma, H3506), and 20 units/mL DNAse (Sigma, DN25) in DPBS for 15–20 min at 37°C on a rotator. The resulting cell suspension was filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer, resuspended in FACS buffer, and stained for flow cytometry. When indicated, cells were pretreated with bafilomycin A1 before staining. Briefly, aortic arch digests were resuspended in high-glucose DMEM with 10% FBS and incubated with 75 nM bafilomycin A1 or DMSO vehicle for 2 h at 37°C. Following treatment, cells were washed twice with FACS buffer before staining. Peritoneal cells were collected by lavage of thioglycolate-injected mice with cold DPBS, followed by red blood cell lysis using 1X PharmLyse buffer (BD Biosciences, 555,899) and resuspension in FACS buffer. For antibody staining, cells were blocked with human or murine FcBlock (BD Biosciences, 564,220) and incubated with conjugated antibodies (Table 1) for 30 min at 4°C. Cells were then stained with Fixable Viability Stain 700 (BD Biosciences, 564,997; 1:1000) and Monodansylpentane (MDH, Abcepta, SM1000b; 1:1000) for 30 min at 4°C, washed in FACS buffer and acquired on a 5-laser Cytek Aurora spectral flow cytometer (Cytek Biosciences). Data were analyzed using SpectroFlo 3.3.0 (Cytek Biosciences) and FlowJo 10.8.2 (BD Biosciences).

qPCR

Cells were lysed in Trizol (Invitrogen, 15,596,026), and RNA was isolated using the Direct-zol RNA MiniPrep kit (Zymo, R2052). Reverse transcription was carried out with iScript Reverse Transcription Supermix (Bio-Rad, 1,708,841). qPCR was performed using the CFX Connect Real Time PCR detection System (Bio-Rad). Primer sequences are listed in Table 2. mRNA levels were quantified using the comparative CT method, with HPRT1 as the housekeeping gene.

Western blotting

Cells were lysed in 2X Laemmli Sample Buffer (Bio-Rad, 1,610,737) containing β-mercaptoethanol and boiled at 95°C for 5 min. Samples were run on 8%–16% Criterion TGX Stain Free Pre-cast Gels (BioRad, 5,671,103) and UV-activated for total protein quantification using the ChemiDoc XRS + System (Bio-Rad). Proteins were transferred onto 0.22 μm PVDF Membranes (Bio-Rad) using the Trans-Blot Turbo Transfer System (Bio-Rad). Immunoblotting for indicated proteins was performed with primary antibodies (Table 1) overnight at 4°C followed by horseradish peroxidase conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Proteins were developed using either Clarity (Bio-Rad, 1,705,061) or Clarity Max (Bio-Rad, 1,705,062) ECL Substrates and imaged on the ChemiDoc XRS + system (Bio-Rad).

Cell imaging

For live-cell microscopy, cells were cultured on ibiTreat 18-well chamber slides (Ibidi) and stained for 30 min at 37°C with MDH (1:10,000) or LysoTracker Deep Red (50 nM, Invitrogen, L12492) in FluoroBrite DMEM (Gibco, A1896701) (BMDMs and VSMCs) or phenol red-free RPMI (Gibco, 11,835–030) (THP-1) with 2 mg/mL fatty acid-free BSA before imaging. Live or fixed cells were imaged on the Zeiss LSM880 confocal microscope with Airyscan, using the 20x, 0.8 NA, Air, Plan-Apochromat or 63x, 1.4 NA, Oil, Plan-Apo (NA objectives) or on the Zeiss AxioObserver 7 inverted widefield microscope with the 20 × 0.8 NA Plan-Apochromat or 63 × 1.4 NA, Oil, Plan-Apochromat objectives, at 37°C and 5% CO2 or RT. To assess lipophagy flux at the single-cell level, we quantified lipophagy events per cell using the mito-QC Counter [61], or the DsRed:pHluorin fluorescence ratio per cell. Lipophagy events, LD area, and DsRed:pHluorin fluorescence ratios per cell were analyzed in ImageJ. For cells imaged live on the widefield Cytation imaging reader (BioTek), with a 20x objective at 37°C and 5% CO2, images were analyzed using the BioGen5 software.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SEM or mean ±95%CI and represent at least 3 independent experiments, unless otherwise noted. Statistical analysis was performed using Prism v10.2.0 (GraphPad Software Inc) and are detailed in the figure legends. Group differences were assessed using two-tailed unpaired Student’s t test, one-way or two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s, Dunnett’s, Sidak’s or Holm-Sidak’s multiple comparison corrections. Statistical significance is indicated in the figures as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001.
