An exact Ramsey number of large bipartite graphs versus odd wheel
Sayan Gupta, Kaushik Majumder

Abstract
. The Ramsey number for the pair of graphs 𝕂 1 , n \mathbb{K}_{1,n} (star) versus W m W_{m} (wheel) has been extensively studied. In contrast, the Ramsey number of 𝕂 2 , n \mathbb{K}_{2,n} versus wheel is not yet explored due to the structural complexity of 𝕂 2 , n \mathbb{K}_{2,n} . In this article, we have established an exact value of 𝕂 2 , n \mathbb{K}_{2,n} versus W m W_{m} for large n n and m m . In particular, we have proved R ⁡ ( 𝕂 2 , n , W m ) = 3 ​ n + 4 R(\mathbb{K}_{2,n},W_{m})=3n+4 if n n and m m are sufficiently large integers where n ≥ 4 ​ m n\geq 4m and m m is an odd integer. This also proves the W m W_{m} -goodness of 𝕂 2 , n \mathbb{K}_{2,n} . We have used a probabilistic method composed of structural analysis in our proof.

1.
Introduction

Let
G
and
H
be two graphs. The
Ramsey number

R(G,H)
is the minimum positive integer
N
such that for each graph
\Gamma
on
N
vertices either
\Gamma
contains a copy of
G
or its complement
\overline{\Gamma}
contains a copy of
H
(i.e.
\textup{copy}(H)
is a subgraph of
\overline{\Gamma}
). For a vertex
v
in
G
, we denote the set of neighbours of
v
in
G
as
N_{G}(v)
and its size
|N_{G}(v)|
is called degree with textbook notation
\deg_{G}(v)
. In the case of a bipartite graph
G
with bipartition
A\sqcup B
, if
v\in A
, then we simply denote the neighbours of it by
N_{B}(v)
as it does not contain any neighbour in
A
. A graph is
k
-connected
if the deletion of any set of vertices with size less than
k
does not make the graph disconnected. The minimum number of vertices needed to delete to make
G
disconnected is called the
connectivity
of the graph. We denote it by
\mathop{k}(G)
. The maximum and minimum degrees of a graph
G
are denoted by
\Delta(G)
and
\delta(G)
, respectively.
For a graph
G
, suppose its vertex set
V
decomposed into two sets
P\sqcup Q
, such that
\frac{|P|}{|V|}
is sufficiently small enough, in the sense that if
|V|\rightarrow\infty
, then
\frac{|P|}{|V|}\rightarrow 0
. In this setting, we may regard
Q
forms a
dense
or
good
set in
V
, while
P
behaves like a
null
or
bad
set. The presence of a bad set
P
complicates the analysis of the dependency tree. However, the dominant contribution comes from the dense set
Q
, which plays a central role in analysis. In this article, we have observed such decomposition in our proof. All the graphs we consider throughout this article are simple and undirected.

Burr proved a general lower bound on the Ramsey number for the pair of graphs
(G,H)
. His statement is the following:-

Theorem 1.1
.

[
5
, Theorem 1]

Let
G
be a connected graph and
H
be a graph satisfying
|V(G)|\geq\sigma(H)
where
\sigma(H)
is the size of the smallest colour class in a
\chi(H)
-colouring of
H
. Then

R(G,H)\geq(|V(G)|-1)(\chi(H)-1)+\sigma(H).

We say that a graph
G
is
H
-good
if equality holds in Burr’s lower bound for the pair
(G,H)
. This notion of goodness, introduced by Burr and Erdős in
[
6
]
, plays a central role in the study of Ramsey goodness. Roughly speaking, it identifies those pairs of graphs
(G,H)
for which Burr’s standard lower bound is in fact tight.

The study of Ramsey goodness has attracted significant attention, particularly in cases where
H

has a bounded chromatic number. Indeed, when
H
is a cycle or a tree graph, its chromatic number satisfies
\chi(H)\in\{2,3\}
, values that do not depend on
|V(H)|
. In such situations,
R(G,H)
grows linearly with
|V(G)|
. More generally, whenever
H
has a chromatic number
\chi(H)
that is independent of
|V(H)|
, the lower bound of Ramsey number
R(G,H)
remains linear in

|V(G)|
. Although it may still depend on the parameter
\sigma(H)
. If both
\chi(H)
and
\sigma(H)
are independent of
|V(H)|
, then the growth of
R(G,H)
is at least linear in
|V(G)|
. On the other hand, if
\chi(H)
is independent of
|V(H)|
but
\sigma(H)
depends on
|V(H)|
, then
R(G,H)
still grows at least linearly with
|V(G)|
, and the equality case becomes particularly rich and tractable. Concluding that
R(G,H)
is linear in
|V(G)|
requires establishing an upper bound of the same order. Our statement (Theorem
1.5
) shows that
R(\mathbb{K}_{2,n},W_{m})
, grows linearly with
|V(\mathbb{K}_{2,n})|=n+2
with explicit parametric dependence on
n
and
m
. In fact, Theorem
1.5
further establishes that
\mathbb{K}_{2,n}
is
W_{m}-
good, again with explicit dependence on
n
and
m
.

Determining which graphs are
H-
good for various families of graphs
H
, and understanding the structural reasons behind goodness, remains one of the central and challenging problems in graph Ramsey theory. Over the past several decades, substantial progress has been made for trees, cycles, and various sparse or nearly bipartite graphs; see, for example,
[
1
,
8
,
13
,
14
,
12
]
.

A well-studied example in the literature on Ramsey goodness is the pair
(\mathbb{K}_{1,n},W_{m})
, where
\mathbb{K}_{1,n}
denotes the star graph and
W_{m}
is the wheel graph on
m+1
vertices. The formal definition of the

on
m+1
vertices is the graph
\mathbb{K}_{1}\circledast C_{m}
. Here, the notation
\circledast
indicates the join of two graphs, that is
\mathbb{K}_{1}
is adjacent to all vertices of the cycle graph

. Recall that for each integer

,
\chi(W_{m})\in\{3,4\}
and
\sigma(W_{m})=1
. The general Ramsey number for the pair
(\mathbb{K}_{1,n},W_{m})
in the case of odd wheels was first determined in
[
7
]
for

, establishing that
\mathbb{K}_{1,n}
is
W_{m}
-good in this range. This bound was later improved to

in
[
3
]
. For further results on star–wheel Ramsey numbers, see
[
11
]
.

Theorem 1.2
.

[
7
, Theorem 2]

If
n\geq m-1
and
m\geq 3
is an odd integer, then

R(\mathbb{K}_{1,n},W_{m})=3n+1.

A
book
graph
\mathbb{K}_{2}\circledast\overline{\mathbb{K}_{n}}
contains
n+2
vertices and
2n+1
many edges, i.e.,
n
triangles sharing a common edge. It is denoted by
B_{n}
. The exact value of the book-wheel Ramsey number is the following.

Theorem 1.3
.

[
16
, Theorem 1]

If
m\geq 5n+3
and

, then

R(W_{m},B_{n})=2m+1.

This result proves that
W_{m}
is
B_{n}
-good. The motivating observation is that the graph
\mathbb{K}_{2,n}
is sandwiched between the star and book graphs. In particular,

\mathbb{K}_{1,n}\subset\mathbb{K}_{2,n}\subset B_{n}.

Here,
H\subset G
means a copy of the graph
H
is contained in
G
as a subgraph. A graph
G
is
\mathbb{K}_{1,n}
-free if and only if the maximum degree
\Delta(G)\leq n-1
. As a consequence, we get a lower bound on the minimum degree of
\overline{G}
. This helps to identify the embedding of particular structures inside
\overline{G}
. Whereas in the case of
\mathbb{K}_{2,n}
, we do not have that particular advantage due to its structural complexity and dependency. Hence, the Ramsey goodness of
\mathbb{K}_{2,n}
versus other graphs has not been explored yet in comparison to the star. The odd cycle graph goodness of
\mathbb{K}_{2,n}
has been established as follows:-

Theorem 1.4
.

[
10
, Theorem 1.8]

For each odd integer
m\geq 7
and for each integer
n\geq 2m+499
and
n\geq 3493
, then

R(\mathbb{K}_{2,n},C_{m})=2n+3.

However, in the case of the
\mathbb{K}_{2,n}
graph versus wheel graph, there is no such explicit study till now. Although we get a direct consequence of
\mathbb{K}_{2,n}
-goodness of

from Theorem

. Now it is an interesting question to ask whether
\mathbb{K}_{2,n}
is
W_{m}
-good or not. In this article, we answer this question in the affirmative for large

and

.

Theorem 1.5
.

If
n
and
m
are sufficiently large integers satisfying

, where

is an odd integer, then

R(\mathbb{K}_{2,n},W_{m})=3n+4.

.

and

are addressed in detail in the proof of the result. Briefly, using Lemma
2.2
, there exists a constant
K\in(0,\infty)
, and in our setting we take

\frac{1}{10}

It is quite challenging to study a
\mathbb{K}_{2,n}
-free graph

. Unlike star-free graphs, it does not provide us with any straightforward information regarding the minimum or maximum degree of
\overline{G}
. Although the fact lying in their neighbours of each

, is that

. Using this fact, we can deduce some lower bound on the minimum degree of
\overline{G}-\{x\}
for some

. In some cases, it is not sufficient to prove the embedding of the required structure inside
\overline{G}
. In our proof, we have used the

to deduce an upper bound on

. The intuition is: if

. This probabilistic aspect is explained in detail in the proof. This idea can also be generalised for a particular subset

with sufficiently many neighbours inside

. It automatically provides us with the existence of a vertex

in

.

2.
Preliminary Results: tracing an arc from the literature

The length of the smallest cycle in the graph is called the
girth
. The
circumference
is the length of the largest cycle. The
even circumference
and
odd circumference
of a graph
G
are the length of the largest even and odd cycle subgraph, respectively. In this article, we denote them by
\mathop{ec}(G)
and
\mathop{oc}(G)
respectively. A graph
G
of order
n
is pancyclic if it contains cycles of all lengths between
3
and
n
. A
weakly pan-cyclic
graph is a graph containing cycles of all lengths between its girth and circumference.

The first step of the proof is to show that a
\mathbb{K}_{2,n}
-free graph
G
on
3n+4
vertices contains a vertex
v
with degree sufficiently small. Consequently, this vertex contains a sufficiently large neighbourhood
\overline{H}
(say) in
\overline{G}
. We aim to show the existence of a
C_{m}
inside that. Together with
v
, it will form a
W_{m}
which leads to a contradiction. In order to show this, our first goal is to prove that
\overline{H}
is non-bipartite as our
m
is odd. We have explained the proof in detail in a later section. After this, we require a sufficient condition on the minimum degree of
\overline{H}
to guarantee the existence of the
C_{m}
. A few results related to pancyclic and weakly pancyclic graphs are very useful in this regard. However, in our case, we see that the degree conditions are not sufficient. Hence, we need the following two results to prove the existence of a
C_{m}
inside
\overline{H}
. The result by Haxell et al.
[
9
]
(Lemma
2.2
) is very similar to the results
[
4
]
on weakly pancyclic graphs. And it is one of the key results used in our proof.

Lemma 2.1
.

[
15
]

If
G
is
2
-connected and non-bipartite such that
\delta(G)\geq r\geq 3
and
|V(G)|\geq 2r+1
, then
\mathop{ec}(G)\geq 2r
and
\mathop{oc}(G)\geq 2r-1
.

Lemma 2.2
.

[
9

For each
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
K=K(\varepsilon)=O(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{5}})\in(0,\infty)
such that if

N\geq\frac{45K}{\varepsilon^{4}}=O\left(\frac{45}{\varepsilon^{9}}\right),

and

Lemma 2.3

[

Let
n\geq k\geq 2

then there exists an integer

The following lemma plays a key role in our proof, as it shows that the neighbourhood of the maximum-degree vertex in the graph we study is non-bipartite. This idea serves as a guiding principle throughout the article.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
G
be a bipartite graph with a bipartition
A\sqcup B
and
|N_{B}(a)|\geq d
for each
a\in A
. Then there exist at least two vertices
x,y\in A
such that

|N_{B}(x)\cap N_{B}(y)|>\frac{d^{2}}{|B|}-\frac{d}{|A|}.

(1)

Proof :.

We proceed with the proof using the first moment method. We perform a random experiment of choosing two vertices one by one without replacement from
A
. In this case, the sample space equals
\Omega=\{(x,y):x,y\in A;x\neq y\}
, which has size
|\Omega|=|A|(|A|-1)
. This yields the probability space
(\Omega,2^{\Omega},\Pr)
, where each singleton sample assigned equal probability, i.e.
\Pr(\{\omega\})=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}
for each
\omega\in\Omega
. We further construct the random variable
X:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, where for each
(x,y)\in\Omega

X(x,y)=|N_{B}(x)\cap N_{B}(y)|.

For a vertex
u\in B
, we construct the random variable
X_{u}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, where for each
(x,y)\in\Omega

\displaystyle X_{u}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}1&\textnormal{ if }u\in N_{B}(x)\cap N_{B}(y)\\
0&\textnormal{ else }&\end{array}\right..

Here,
X_{u}
forms a Bernoulli random variable with parameter
\Pr(X_{u}=1)
, where

{\textnormal{E}}[X_{u}]=\Pr(X_{u}=1)=\frac{|N_{A}(u)|(|N_{A}(u)|-1)}{|\Omega|}=\frac{|N_{A}(u)|(|N_{A}(u)|-1)}{|A|(|A|-1)}.

From the construction, it follows that
X=\underset{u\in B}{\sum}X_{u}
. Using the linearity of expectation, we have

{\textnormal{E}}[X]=\underset{u\in B}{\sum}{\textnormal{E}}[X_{u}]=\underset{u\in B}{\sum}\Pr(X_{u}=1)=\underset{u\in B}{\sum}\frac{|N_{A}(u)|(|N_{A}(u)|-1)}{|A|(|A|-1)}.

We further estimate
{\textnormal{E}}[X]
and while estimating we use Jensen’s inequality and the
duality formula

\underset{w\in A}{\sum}|N_{B}(w)|=\underset{u\in B}{\sum}|N_{A}(u)|
.

\displaystyle{\textnormal{E}}[X]=

\displaystyle\underset{u\in B}{\sum}\frac{|N_{A}(u)|(|N_{A}(u)|-1)}{|A|(|A|-1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underset{u\in B}{\sum}\frac{(|N_{A}(u)|-1)^{2}}{|A|(|A|-1)}+\underset{u\in B}{\sum}\frac{(|N_{A}(u)|-1)}{|A|(|A|-1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{|B|}{|A|(|A|-1)}\frac{\underset{u\in B}{\sum}(|N_{A}(u)|-1)^{2}}{\underset{u\in B}{\sum}1}+\frac{\underset{u\in B}{\sum}|N_{A}(u)|}{|A|(|A|-1)}-\frac{|B|}{|A|(|A|-1)}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{|B|}{|A|(|A|-1)}\left(\frac{\underset{u\in B}{\sum}(|N_{A}(u)|-1)}{|B|}\right)^{2}+\frac{\underset{w\in A}{\sum}|N_{B}(w)|}{|A|(|A|-1)}-\frac{|B|}{|A|(|A|-1)}\hskip 17.07182pt[\textnormal{Using Jensen's inequality}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{|B|}{|A|(|A|-1)}\left(\frac{\underset{u\in B}{\sum}|N_{A}(u)|-|B|}{|B|}\right)^{2}+\frac{\underset{w\in A}{\sum}|N_{B}(w)|}{|A|(|A|-1)}-\frac{|B|}{|A|(|A|-1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{|B|}{|A|(|A|-1)}\left(\frac{\underset{w\in A}{\sum}|N_{B}(w)|-|B|}{|B|}\right)^{2}+\frac{\underset{w\in A}{\sum}|N_{B}(w)|}{|A|(|A|-1)}-\frac{|B|}{|A|(|A|-1)}\hskip 17.07182pt[\textnormal{Using the duality formula}]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{|B|}{|A|(|A|-1)}\left(\frac{\underset{w\in A}{\sum}d-|B|}{|B|}\right)^{2}+\frac{\underset{w\in A}{\sum}d}{|A|(|A|-1)}-\frac{|B|}{|A|(|A|-1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{|B|}{|A|(|A|-1)}\left(\frac{d|A|}{|B|}-1\right)^{2}+\frac{d|A|}{|A|(|A|-1)}-\frac{|B|}{|A|(|A|-1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{|B|}{|A|(|A|-1)}\left(\frac{d^{2}|A|^{2}}{|B|^{2}}-2d\frac{|A|}{|B|}+1\right)+\frac{d}{|A|-1}-\frac{|B|}{|A|(|A|-1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}|A|}{|B|(|A|-1)}-\frac{2d}{|A|-1}+\frac{|B|}{|A|(|A|-1)}+\frac{d}{|A|-1}-\frac{|B|}{|A|(|A|-1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}|A|}{|B|(|A|-1)}-\frac{d}{|A|-1}>\frac{d^{2}}{|B|}-\frac{d}{|A|-1}>\frac{d^{2}}{|B|}-\frac{d}{|A|}.

This implies that there exists at least one
(x,y)\in\Omega
such that
X(x,y)>\frac{d^{2}}{|B|}-\frac{d}{|A|}
. This completes the proof.
∎

3.
Proof of the Theorem
1.5

We assume
m\geq 7
is an odd integer and
n\geq 4m
is sufficiently large. We construct the graph
G
, which is three disjoint unions of copies of
\mathbb{K}_{n+1}
. Then
\overline{G}
is the complete tripartite graph with each part having size
n+1
. From the construction, it is clear that
G\not\subseteq\mathbb{K}_{2,n}
and
\overline{G}\not\subseteq W_{m}
. This implies
R(\mathbb{K}_{2,n},W_{m})>3n+3
.

To establish
R(\mathbb{K}_{2,n},W_{m})=3n+4
, we assume that a graph
G
with
3n+4
many vertices does not contain a copy of
\mathbb{K}_{2,n}
. We show that its complement
\overline{G}
contains a
W_{m}
. On the contrary, let
\overline{G}
contain no
W_{m}
. We use the
\mathbb{K}_{2,n}
-freeness of
G
to obtain a certain upper bound on the minimum degree of
G
. Consequently, this shows that
\Delta(\overline{G})
is sufficiently large, which helps to prove the existence of a
C_{m}
inside the neighbourhood of the maximum degree vertex of
\overline{G}
. Along with the maximum degree vertex, we get a
W_{m}
in
\overline{G}
, which is a contradiction. Before proceeding, we need to prove some preliminary results that will be used to prove the main theorem.

Lemma 3.1
.

\Delta(\overline{G})\leq 2n+2=2(n+1)
.

Proof :.

If not, then let
\Delta(\overline{G})\geq 2n+3
. This implies that there exists a vertex
v\in V(G)
such that
|N_{\overline{G}}(v)|\geq 2n+3
. Since
G
is
\mathbb{K}_{2,n}
-free, we have
G[N_{\overline{G}}(v)]
is
\mathbb{K}_{2,n}
-free. Hence, from Theorem
1.4
, it follows that the graph
\overline{G}[N_{\overline{G}}(v)]
contains a copy of
C_{m}
. This implies the graph
\overline{G}
contains a copy of
C_{m}
. This
C_{m}
, along with the vertex
v
, forms a copy of wheel
W_{m}
, which is a contradiction.
∎

Recall that, here
\Delta(\overline{G})+\delta(G)=|V(G)|-1=3(n+1)
. Thus using Lemma
3.1
, it follows that
\delta(G)\geq(n+1)
. To establish the upper bound of
\delta(G)
, we use the first moment method.

Lemma 3.2
.

\delta(G)<\sqrt{3}(n+1)
.

Proof :.

If possible, let
\delta(G)\geq\sqrt{3}(n+1)
. We show using the first moment method that there exist
x,y\in V(G)
such that
|N_{G}(x)\cap N_{G}(y)|\geq n
. In contrast to Lemma
2.4
, the assumptions in the present setting differ, thereby warranting a reformulation of the argument; nonetheless, the underlying principle remains identical. We perform the same random experiment of choosing two vertices one by one without replacement. This yields the sample space

\Omega=\{(x,y):x,y\in V(G),x\neq y\}.

From the construction, it follows that
|\Omega|=|V(G)|(|V(G)|-1)=(3n+4)(3n+3)
. Here we consider the probability space
(\Omega,2^{\Omega},\Pr)
, where
\Pr(\{\omega\})=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}
for each
\omega\in\Omega
. We construct a random variable
X:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, where for each
(x,y)\in\Omega
,

X(x,y)=|N_{G}(x)\cap N_{G}(y)|.

For a vertex
v\in V
, we construct auxiliary random variable
X_{v}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, where for each
(x,y)\in\Omega
,

\displaystyle X_{v}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}1&\textnormal{ if }v\in N_{G}(x)\cap N_{G}(y)\\
0&\textnormal{ else }&\end{array}\right.,

From the construction, it follows that
X=\underset{v\in V}{\sum}X_{v}
. Here, we compute the distribution of
X_{v}
, for each
v\in V
,

\Pr(X_{v}=1)=\frac{|N_{G}(v)|(|N_{G}(v)|-1)}{|V(G)|(|V(G)|-1)}\geq\frac{\sqrt{3}(n+1)(\sqrt{3}(n+1)-1)}{(3n+4)(3n+3)}\geq\frac{n}{3n+4}.

Hence, using the linearity of expectation, we have
