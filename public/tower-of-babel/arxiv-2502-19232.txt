Quantum spherical functions of type χ \chi as Macdonald-Koornwinder polynomials
Stein Meereboer

Abstract
The theory of quantum symmetric pairs is applied to q q -special functions. Previous work shows the existence of a family χ \chi -spherical functions indexed by the integers for each Hermitian quantum symmetric pair. In this paper a distinguished family of such functions, invariant under the action of the Weyl group of the restricted roots, is shown to be a family of Macdonald-Koornwinder polynomials if the restricted root system is reduced or if the Satake diagram is of type 𝖠𝖨𝖨𝖨 𝖺 \mathsf{AIII_{a}} .

Introduction and overview

A beautiful result of Gail Letzter shows that quantum zonal spherical functions can be realized as Macdonald polynomials
[
23
]
. Her theory and results unify many case-by-case computations of Noumi, Sugatani, Dijkhuizen, Stokman, Koornwinder and others
[
29
]

[
30
]
,
[
28
]
,
[
19
]
,
[
34
]
. The connection between zonal spherical functions and
q
-special functions has examples in the more general setting of quantum spherical functions of type
\chi
. Koelink
[
15
,
Thm 7.1
]
and Noumi and Mimachi
[
29
,
Thm 4
]
show that quantum spherical functions of type
\chi
on the quantum analog of the symmetric pair
(SU(2),U(1))
can be interpreted as Askey-Wilson polynomials.

Classically, spherical functions of type
\chi
can be interpreted as hypergeometric functions, cf.
[
7
,
Thm 5.2.2
]
. This motivates the question whether quantum spherical functions of type
\chi
can be realized as
q
-hypergeometric functions. The main result of this paper gives as an affirmative answer to this question for all quantum symmetric pairs with reduced restricted root system and in type
\mathsf{AIII_{a}}
. The approach avoids any radial part calculations. Instead we rely on the identification in the zonal spherical case, which allows us to explicitly determine the orthogonality measure for quantum spherical functions of type
\chi
. Our identification avoids extensive case work.

As a consequence, we provide simple, uniform, formulas for the parameters appearing in the Macdonald-Koornwinder polynomials. The identification allows us to show properties of the orthogonal polynomials. With use of the
\imath
-bar involution of Bao and Wang
[
3
]
and the relative braid group action of Wang and Zhang
[
37
]
, we recover the known
q\to q^{-1}
symmetry of Koorwinder-Macdonald polynomials. Furthermore, in the reduced case we identify the Macdonald
q
-difference operators as operators arising form the center of the quantum group. Finally, tensor product decompositions yield relations for connection coefficients of Macdonald-Koornwinder polynomials.

This paper is organized as follows.

In Section
2
we give preliminaries about quantum groups, the quantized function algebra and quantum symmetric pairs including a review of character theory for Hermitian quantum symmetric pairs, cf.
[
27
]
.

Section
3
introduces our primary objects of study: spherical functions of type
\chi
, and Macdonald-Koornwinder polynomials. We also discuss the identification of zonal spherical functions with orthogonal polynomials, cf.
[
23
]
,
[
28
]
.

In Section
4
we construct, for each family of spherical functions of type
\chi
, a distinguished family of orthogonal polynomials. The essence of this construction is as follows: by viewing the space of
\chi
-spherical functions as a free module of rank one over the algebra of zonal spherical functions, we associate zonal spherical functions to each family of
\chi
-spherical functions. Using the Haar integral on the quantized function algebra and quantum Schur orthogonality we show that the polynomials are orthogonal with respect to a weight involving the orthogonality weight for zonal spherical functions and the fundamental spherical function of type
\chi
. Here the fundamental spherical functions are certain minimal elements in the space of spherical functions. Determining these polynomials thus reduces to identifying the fundamental spherical function.

Section
5
determines fundamental spherical functions by reducing to the rank one cases
\mathsf{AI}
and
\mathsf{AIV}
.

Combining the results of Sections
4
and
5
, Section
6
identifies the constructed orthogonal polynomials with Macdonald-Koornwinder polynomials based on the explicit expression of the weight for which the polynomials are orthogonal.

Finally, Section
7
applies the identification with orthogonal polynomials and quantum group techniques to establish three results for the orthogonal polynomials. First, we prove the invariance of orthogonal polynomials under sending
q\to q^{-1}
. Second, the Macdonald-Koornwinder
q
-difference operators are identified with central elements of the quantum group. Third, we derive relations for the connection coefficients of Macdonald-Koornwinder polynomials.

Quantum groups and quantum symmetric pairs

We introduce notation as in
[
25
]
. Let
\mathfrak{g}
be be a complex semisimple Lie algebra with Cartan matrix
C=(c_{ij})_{i,j\in\mathbf{I}}
. Let
D=\text{diag}(d_{i}\,:\,d_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 1},\,i\in\mathbf{I})
be a symmetrizer, meaning that
DC
is symmetric and
\gcd\{d_{i}\,:\,i\in\mathbf{I}\}=1
. We fix a set of simple roots
\Pi=\{\alpha_{i}\,:\,i\in\mathbf{I}\}
and a set of simple coroots
\Pi^{\vee}=\{\alpha^{\vee}_{i}\,:\,i\in\mathbf{I}\}
with corresponding root and coroot systems
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{R}^{\vee}
. The root lattice is denoted by
\mathbb{Z}\mathbf{I}=\oplus_{i\in\mathbf{I}}\mathbb{Z}\alpha_{i}
. The root lattice is equipped with the normalized Killing form
(\,,\,)
, scaled so that short roots have length
2
. The Weyl group
W
is generated by the simple reflections
s_{i}:\mathbb{Z}\mathbf{I}\to\mathbb{Z}\mathbf{I}
, for
i\in\mathbf{I}
defined by
s_{i}(\alpha_{j})=\alpha_{j}-c_{ij}\alpha_{i},
for
j\in\mathbf{I}
.

Let
q
be an indeterminate, and let
\mathbb{Q}(q)
denote the field of rational functions in
q
with coefficients in
\mathbb{Q}
. Denote by
\mathbb{F}
the algebraic closure of
\mathbb{Q}(q)
. We fix a group
homomorphism
\mathbb{Q}\to\mathbb{F}^{\times}
mapping
1\to q
. This corresponds to a consistent choice of rational
powers
q^{x}
of
q

(x\in\mathbb{Q})
. The field
\mathbb{F}
is equipped with a unique
\mathbb{Q}
-linear automorphism
\overline{\,\cdot\,}:\mathbb{F}\to\mathbb{F}
such that
q
is mapped to
q^{-1}
. Let
\mathbf{A}
be the smallest unital subring of
\mathbb{F}
for which
q
is contained in
\mathbf{A}
and such that every element in
\mathbf{A}
that is not contained in the ideal generated by
(q-1)
has a square root and is invertible, cf.
[
24
,
§1.
]
. Define
\mathsf{cl}:\mathbf{A}\to\mathbf{A}/(1-q)\mathbf{A}\cong\mathbb{C}
as the map
f\mapsto f(1)
. For each
i\in\mathbf{I}
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
, set

q_{i}:=q^{d_{i}},\qquad[n]_{i}:=\cfrac{q_{i}^{n}-q_{i}^{-n}}{q_{i}-q_{i}^{-1}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad[n]_{i}!:=\prod_{k=1}^{n}[k]_{i}.

Let
(Y,X,\langle\,,\,\rangle.\dots)
be simply connected root datum of type
(\mathbf{I},\cdot)
, cf.
[
25
,
2.2
]
. By definition, there are embeddings
\mathbf{I}\to X
,
i\mapsto\alpha_{i}
and
\mathbf{I}\to Y
,
i\mapsto\alpha_{i}^{\vee}
, such that
Y=\mathbb{Z}[\alpha_{i}^{\vee}]
and
X=\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}}(Y,\mathbb{Z})
, respectively. The pairing
\langle\,,\,\rangle
and the form
(\,,\,)
are related by the formula
\langle\alpha_{i}^{\vee},\alpha_{j}\rangle=\frac{2(\alpha_{i},\alpha_{j})}{(\alpha_{i},\alpha_{i})}
, for
i,j\in\mathbf{I}
. The lattice
X
is partially ordered by the rule

\lambda\leqslant\lambda^{\prime}\quad\text{if and only if}\quad\lambda^{\prime}-\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}\mathbf{I}.

The quantum group
\mathbf{U}
, associated to the root datum, is the unital associative algebra over
\mathbb{F}
, generated by the symbols
E_{i},F_{i}
for
i\in\mathbf{I}
and
K_{h}
for
h\in Y
. These generators satisfy the following relations for
i,j\in\mathbf{I},
and
h,h_{1},h_{2}\in Y:

\displaystyle K_{0}

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle K_{h_{1}}K_{h_{2}}

\displaystyle=K_{h_{1}+h_{2}},

\displaystyle K_{h}E_{i}

\displaystyle=q^{\langle h,\alpha_{i}\rangle}E_{i}K_{h},

\displaystyle K_{h}F_{i}

\displaystyle=q^{-\langle h,\alpha_{i}\rangle}F_{i}K_{h},

\displaystyle E_{i}F_{j}-F_{j}E_{i}

\displaystyle=\delta_{i,j}\cfrac{K_{i}-K_{i}^{-1}}{q_{i}-q_{i}^{-1}},

as well as the quantum Serre relations for
i\neq j
,

\displaystyle\sum_{r+s=1-a_{i,j}}(-1)^{s}E_{i}^{(r)}E_{j}E_{i}^{(s)}=\sum_{r+s=1-a_{i,j}}(-1)^{s}F_{i}^{(r)}F_{j}F_{i}^{(s)}=0.

Here we use the notation

K_{i}:=K_{d_{i}\alpha_{i}^{\vee}},\qquad E_{i}^{(n)}:=\frac{1}{[n]_{i}!}E_{i}^{n}\qquad\text{and}\qquad F_{i}^{(n)}=\frac{1}{[n]_{i}!}F_{i}^{n}.

The quantum group
\mathbf{U}
has the structure of a Hopf-algebra
(\mathbf{U},\triangle,\epsilon,\iota,S)
, cf.
[
11
,
Ch 4
]
. The conventions for the antipode, coproduct and counit are

\displaystyle\triangle(E_{i})

\displaystyle=E_{i}\otimes 1+K_{i}\otimes E_{i}

\displaystyle\triangle(F_{i})

\displaystyle=F_{i}\otimes K_{i}^{-1}+1\otimes F_{i}

\displaystyle\triangle(K_{h})

\displaystyle=K_{h}\otimes K_{h}

\displaystyle S(E_{i})

\displaystyle=-K_{i}^{-1}E_{i}

\displaystyle S(F_{i})

\displaystyle=-F_{i}K_{i}

\displaystyle S(K_{h})

\displaystyle=K_{-h}

\displaystyle\epsilon(E_{i})

\displaystyle=\epsilon(F_{i})=0

\displaystyle\epsilon(K_{h})

\displaystyle=1

for
i\in I
and
h\in Y
.

We use
Sweedler notation
to denote the coproduct, writing

\triangle(x)=x_{(1)}\otimes x_{(2)}\in\mathbf{U}\otimes\mathbf{U}.

(2.1)

1.

Let
\omega:\mathbf{U}\to\mathbf{U}
denote the unique algebra automorphism that satisfies

E_{i}\mapsto F_{i},\qquad F_{i}\mapsto E_{i},\qquad K_{i}\mapsto K_{i}^{-1},\qquad\text{ for all }i\in\mathbf{I}.

(2.2)

2.

Let
\varrho:\mathbf{U}\to\mathbf{U}
denote the unique algebra anti-automorphism that satisfies

E_{i}\mapsto F_{i},\qquad F_{i}\mapsto E_{i},\qquad K_{i}\mapsto K_{i},\qquad\text{ for all }i\in\mathbf{I}.

(2.3)

3.

Let
\overline{\,\cdot\,}:\mathbf{U}\to\mathbf{U}
denote the unique
\mathbb{Q}
-algebra automorphism that satisfies

E_{i}\mapsto E_{i},\qquad F_{i}\mapsto F_{i},\qquad K_{i}\mapsto K_{i}^{-1},\qquad q\mapsto q^{-1}\qquad\text{ for all }i\in\mathbf{I}

(2.4)

4.

For each
\boldsymbol{a}\in(\mathbb{F}^{\times})^{\mathbf{I}}
let
\Phi_{\boldsymbol{a}}:\mathbf{U}\to\mathbf{U}
denote the Hopf-algebra automorphism that satisfies

E_{i}\mapsto a_{i}^{1/2}E_{i},\qquad F_{i}\mapsto a_{i}^{-1/2}F_{i},\qquad K_{i}\mapsto K_{i},\qquad\text{ for all }i\in\mathbf{I}.

(2.5)

For each
i\in\mathbf{I}
, let
T_{i}:\mathbf{U}\to\mathbf{U}
denote the algebra automorphism corresponding to
T_{i,+1}^{\prime\prime}
as defined in
[
25
,
37.1
]
.
The operators
T_{i}
satisfy the braid relations. That is to say, if
w=s_{i_{1}}\dots s_{i_{n}}\in W
is a reduced expression, there exist a well-defined automorphism
T_{w}=T_{s_{i_{1}}}\dots T_{s_{i_{n}}}
of
\mathbf{U}
.

2.1
Finite dimensional weight modules

By convention
\mathbf{U}
-modules are left modules, unless stated otherwise.
A
\mathbf{U}
-module
M
is called a
weight
\mathbf{U}
-module
if
M
has a decomposition of the form

M=\bigoplus_{\lambda\in X}M_{\lambda},\qquad\text{where}\qquad M_{\lambda}=\{m\in M\,:\,K_{h}m=q^{\langle h,\lambda\rangle}m,\,\text{for all }h\in Y\}.

We say that a vector
v\in M
is a
weight vector of weight
\lambda
if
K_{h}v=q^{\langle h,\lambda\rangle}v
for each
h\in Y.

A weight
\mathbf{U}
-module
M
is called a
highest weight module
if there exists a weight vector
v\in M
such that
\mathbf{U}v=M
and
E_{i}v=0
for all
i\in\mathbf{I}.

The irreducible finite dimensional weight
\mathbf{U}
-modules are classified by the dominant weights
X^{+}
, cf.
[
25
,
3.5
]
.
The simple
\mathbf{U}
-module corresponding to a dominant weight
\lambda\in X^{+}
is denoted by
L(\lambda)
.
The theory of highest weight
\mathbf{U}
-modules has a natural analog for right
\mathbf{U}
-modules, cf.
[
25
,
3.5.7
]
.

Let
L(\lambda)
be an irreducible weight
\mathbf{U}
-module and
v_{\lambda}
be a highest weight vector. Then, there exists a
bar involution

\overline{\,\cdot\,}^{L(\lambda)}:L(\lambda)\to L(\lambda)
relative to
v_{\lambda}
satisfying

\overline{Xv_{\lambda}}^{L(\lambda)}:=\overline{X}v_{\lambda}\qquad\text{for}\qquad X\in\mathbf{U}.

This is well-defined by
[
25
, §19.3.4]
.
If it is clear from the context, we write
\overline{\,\cdot\,}^{L(\lambda)}=\overline{\,\cdot\,}
. Analogously, irreducible right weight
\mathbf{U}
-modules are equipped with bar involutions relative to chosen lowest weight vector.

If
\vartheta:\mathbf{U}\to\mathbf{U}
is an (anti)-automorphism and
M
is a left
\mathbf{U}
-module, we define
{}^{\vartheta}M
to be
\mathbf{U}
-module with underlying vector space
M
and such that the action is twisted by
\vartheta
.
Thus, if
\vartheta
is an automorphism
{}^{\vartheta}M
is a left
\mathbf{U}
-module, and if
\vartheta
is an anti-automorphism
{}^{\vartheta}M
is a right
\mathbf{U}
-module.

2.2
The quantized function algebra and bilinear forms

The
quantized function algebra

R_{q}(G)
is the subspace of
\mathbf{U}^{\ast}=\mathrm{Hom}(\mathbf{U},\mathbb{F})
spanned by matrix coefficients for finite dimensional weight
\mathbf{U}
-modules.
The quantized function algebra has the structure of a
Hopf-algebra
(R_{q}(G),\triangle,\epsilon,\iota,S)
which yields a Hopf-algebra duality between
\mathbf{U}
and
R_{q}(G)
, cf.
[
11
,
Ch 7
]
.

For each dominant weight
\lambda\in X^{+}
, the dual
L(\lambda)^{\ast}
becomes a right
\mathbf{U}
-module by defining

(fX)(v):=f(Xv)\qquad\text{where}\qquad X\in\mathbf{U},\,f\in L(\lambda)^{\ast},\,v\in L(\lambda).

Let
f\otimes v\in L(\lambda)^{\ast}\otimes L(\lambda)
, then we write the corresponding element of
R_{q}(G)
as
c^{L(\lambda)}_{f,v}.
If the module is clear from the context, we abbreviate this as
c_{f,v}
.
The quantized function algebra
R_{q}(G)
has the quantum Peter-Weyl decomposition

R_{q}(G)=\bigoplus_{\lambda\in X^{+}}L(\lambda)^{\ast}\otimes L(\lambda)

as a
\mathbf{U}
-bimodule, cf.
[
13
,
7.2
]
. For each weight
\lambda\in X^{+}
the
\mathbf{U}
-bimodule

L(\lambda)^{\ast}\otimes L(\lambda)

is referred to as a
Peter-Weyl block.

Next we describe an explicit realization of
L(\lambda)^{\ast}
via the Shapovalov form.
Define
L^{r}(\lambda)
as the simple twisted right module
{}^{\varrho}L(\lambda)
, where
\varrho
is the anti-automorphism defined in (
2.3
).
As observed by Masaki Kashiwara in
[
13
,
7.1.3
]
, for each dominant weight
\lambda\in X^{+}
, the module
L(\lambda)
is equipped with a unique non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form, relative to a chosen highest weight vector
v_{\lambda}\in L(\lambda)
, satisfying
(v_{\lambda},v_{\lambda})=1
and

(v,Xw)=(\varrho(X)v,w)\qquad\text{where}\qquad v,w\in L(\lambda),\,X\in\mathbf{U},

(2.6)

where
\varrho
is the anti-automorphism defined in (
2.3
).
This bilinear form, known as the
Shapovalov form
, provides an explicit isomorphism
L(\lambda)^{\ast}\cong L^{r}(\lambda)
as right
\mathbf{U}
-modules, cf.
[
13
,
Prop 7.2.2
]
.

2.3
Quantum symmetric pairs

In this section we introduce quantum symmetric pair coideal subalgebras following
[
21
]
and
[
16
]
.

For each subset
\mathbf{I}_{\bullet}\subset\mathbf{I}
, let
w_{\bullet}
denote the longest element in the parabolic subgroup
W_{\bullet}=\langle s_{i}:i\in\mathbf{I}_{\bullet}\rangle\subset W
.

Definition 2.1

(Satake diagram)
.

A
Satake diagram

(\mathbf{I}_{\bullet},\tau)
consists of a subset
\mathbf{I}_{\bullet}\subset\mathbf{I}
and an involutive diagram automorphism
\tau:\mathbf{I}\to\mathbf{I}
with

1.

\tau|_{\mathbf{I}_{\bullet}}=-w_{\bullet}
;

2.

If
i\in\mathbf{I}\setminus\mathbf{I}_{\bullet}
and
\tau(i)=i
then
\alpha_{i}(\rho_{\bullet}^{\vee})\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Here
\rho_{\bullet}^{\vee}
denotes the half sum of positive coroots relative to
\mathbf{I}_{\bullet}
.
∎

Given a Satake diagram
(\mathbf{I},\tau)
, we denote
\mathbf{I}_{\circ}:=\mathbf{I}\setminus\mathbf{I}_{\bullet}
and set

\mathbf{I}_{\mathrm{ns}}:=\{i\in\mathbf{I}_{\circ}\,:\,\tau(i)=i,\,\langle\alpha_{i}^{\vee},\alpha_{j}\rangle=0\text{ for all }j\in\mathbf{I}_{\bullet}\}.

Moreover, we associate an involution
\Theta=-w_{\bullet}\circ\tau
acting on
X,Y
and
\mathbb{Z}\mathbf{I}.

Denote
\mathbf{U}_{\bullet}=\mathbb{F}\langle E_{j},F_{j},K_{j}^{\pm}\,:\,j\in\mathbf{I}_{\bullet}\rangle
the quantum group assosiated to
\mathbf{I}_{\bullet}
and denote
\mathbf{U}_{\Theta}^{0}=\mathbb{F}\langle K_{h}\,:\,h\in Y,\,\Theta(h)=h\rangle
the
\Theta
-invariants of
\mathbf{U}^{0}=\langle K_{h}:\,h\in Y\rangle
.

Definition 2.2

(Quantum symmetric pair coideal subalgebra)
.

Let
\mathbf{B}_{\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{s}}
denote the
quantum symmetric pair coideal subalgebra
associated to the Satake diagram
(\mathbf{I}_{\bullet},\tau)
. It is the subalgebra of
\mathbf{U}
generated by
\mathbf{U}_{\bullet}
,
\mathbf{U}_{\Theta}^{0}
, and the elements

B_{i}^{\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{s}}=B_{i}=F_{i}+c_{i}T_{w_{\bullet}}(E_{\tau(i)})K_{i}^{-1}+s_{i}K_{i}^{-1},\qquad\text{for all}\qquad i\in\mathbf{I}_{\circ}.

We refer to
(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{B}_{\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{s}})
as a quantum symmetric pair (QSP), and to the algebra
\mathbf{B}_{\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{s}}
as a QSP-coideal subalgebra of
\mathbf{U}
.
The QSP-coideal subalgebra
\mathbf{B}_{\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{s}}
depends on a family of scalars
(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{s})\in(\mathbb{F}^{\times})^{\mathbf{I}_{\circ}}\times\mathbb{F}^{\mathbf{I}_{\circ}}
that satisfy

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle=\{\boldsymbol{d}\in(\mathbb{F}^{\times})^{\mathbf{I}_{\circ}}:d_{i}=d_{\tau(i)}\quad\text{if}\quad\langle\alpha_{i}^{\vee},\Theta(\alpha_{i})\rangle=0\}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathcal{S}

\displaystyle=\{\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathbb{F}^{\mathbf{I}_{\circ}}:t_{j}\neq 0\implies(j\in\mathbf{I}_{\mathrm{ns}}\text{ and }c_{ij}\in-2\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}\,\forall i\in\mathbf{I}_{\mathrm{ns}}\setminus\{j\})\}.\hfill

∎

By
[
16
, Prop 5.2]
QSP-coideal subalgebras
\mathbf{B}_{\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{s}}
are right coideal subalgebras, that is to say

\triangle(\mathbf{B}_{\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{s}})\subset\mathbf{B}_{\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{s}}\otimes\mathbf{U}.

For
\boldsymbol{s}=0
, the corresponding QSP-coideal subalgebra is denoted by
\mathbf{B}_{\boldsymbol{c}}
and is said to be
standard.

For each tuple
\boldsymbol{a}\in(\mathbb{F}^{\times})^{\mathbf{I}},
the coideal subalgebra
\Phi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\mathbf{B}_{\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{s}})
is a QSP-coideal subalgebra, here
\Phi_{\boldsymbol{a}}
is the automorphism from (
2.5
).
Thus, the group
(\mathbb{F}^{\times})^{\mathbf{I}}
acts on the set of parameters
(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{S})
.

A parameter
(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{s})
is called
specializable
if
(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{s})\in\mathbf{A}^{\mathbf{I}_{\circ}}\times\mathbf{A}^{\mathbf{I}_{\circ}}
and
\frac{c_{i}}{c_{\tau(i)}}+\mathbf{A}=(-1)^{\langle 2\rho_{\bullet}^{\vee},\alpha_{i}\rangle}+\mathbf{A}
for each
i\in\mathbf{I}_{\circ}
.
And a parameter
(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{s})
is called
quasi specializable
if it is in the
(\mathbb{F}^{\times})^{\mathbf{I}}
-orbit of a specializable parameter and
(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{s})\in\mathbb{Q}(q)^{\mathbf{I}_{\circ}}\times\mathbb{Q}(q)^{\mathbf{I}_{\circ}}
.

A Satake diagram
(\mathbf{I}_{\bullet},\tau)
is said to be
irreducible
if for each
i,j\in\mathbf{I}
, there exists
a sequence
i=i_{1}\dots,i_{r}=j\in\mathbf{I}
such that for each
k=1,...,r-1
, we have either

a_{i_{k},i_{k+1}}\neq 0
or
i_{k+1}=\tau(i_{k})
.

Remark 2.3
.

Throughout this paper, we assume that our parameters are quasi-specializable and that our Satake diagrams
(\mathbf{I}_{\bullet},\tau)
are irreducible.

Definition 2.4

(Shift of base point)
.

For each character
\chi\in\widehat{\mathbf{B}_{\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{s}}}
with
\chi|_{\mathbf{U}_{\bullet}}=\epsilon
we define the algebra isomorphism

\Psi_{\chi}:\mathbf{B}_{\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{s}}\to\mathbf{B}_{\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{t}},\qquad b\mapsto\chi(b_{(1)})b_{(2)}\qquad b\in\mathbf{B}_{\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{s}}.

(2.7)

It is referred to as a

, cf.
[

]
. The isomorphism
\Psi_{\chi}
maps
\mathbf{B}_{\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{s}}
to
\Psi_{\chi}(\mathbf{B}_{\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{s}})
, where
\Psi_{\chi}(\mathbf{B}_{\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{s}})=\mathbf{B}_{\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{t}}
is the QSP-coideal subalgebra with parameters

d_{i}=\chi(K_{i}^{-1}K_{\tau(i)})c_{i},\qquad t_{i}=\chi(B_{i}^{\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{s}}),\qquad\text{where}\qquad i\in\mathbf{I}_{\circ}.\qed

For each root
\alpha\in\mathcal{R}
, set
\widetilde{\alpha}=\frac{\alpha-\Theta(\alpha)}{2}
. The set

\Sigma=\{\widetilde{\alpha}\,:\,\alpha\in\mathcal{R},\,\Theta(\alpha)\neq\alpha\}

(2.8)

is called the
restricted root system
. The corresponding Weyl group is called the
relative Weyl group
and has an embedding in
W
, cf.
[
5
,
Sec 2.3
]
.

Definition 2.5
.

The number of
\tau
-orbits in
\mathbf{I}_{\circ}
is called the
rank
of the Satake diagram.
∎

Essential to many of our arguments is the reduction to the rank one case.

Definition 2.6

(Rank one reductions)
.

To each

\Psi_{\chi^{l}}:\mathbf{B}_{\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{s}}\to\Psi_{\chi^{l}}(\mathbf{B}_{\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{s}})

2.4

\Psi_{\chi^{l}}(\mathbf{B}_{\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{s}})

Analogous to the rank one case, cf. Section

, the fundamental spherical function
\varphi_{b^{l}}^{l,(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{s})}

\varphi_{b_{l}}^{l,(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{s})}=c_{f_{1},v_{1}}\cdots c_{f_{l},v_{l}},\qquad\qquad l\in\mathbb{Z},\,b_{l}\in\mathcal{B}^{+}(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{B}_{\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{s}},\chi^{l})

(7.11)

where each

\chi^{1}

\rho_{\chi^{i}}(\mathbf{B}_{\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{s}})

and

\displaystyle\varphi^{l,(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{s})}_{\lambda}c_{f_{l+1},v_{l+1}}\cdots c_{f_{l+m},v_{l+m}}

Let

denote the unique weight contained in the bottom of the
\chi^{m}
-well, cf. Lemma

Definition 7.13

The weights

\varGamma_{\lambda}=\mathcal{X}^{+}(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{B}_{\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{s}},\chi^{l+m})\cap(\lambda+\Lambda)

7.3.1
The case of
\mathsf{AI}

Since

The case of

(7.16)

\mathsf{AI}

Appendix A
Zonal spherical functions as Macdonald-Koornwinder

In

28
]
the zonal spherical functions for the

coideal subalgebras of type
\mathsf{AIII_{a}}
are identified with Macdonald-Koornwinder polynomials. These results are obtained in the context of
Noumi-Sugitani
coideal subalgebras. In this appendix we elaborate on the relevant parameters.

Let
\sigma\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geqslant 0}
and consider the solution
J^{\sigma}
to the reflection equation, as given in
[
28
,
2.14
]
. According to
[
21
,
Cor 6.6
]
there exists an associated QSP-coideal subalgebra
\mathbf{B}_{\boldsymbol{c}_{\sigma}}
with corresponding parameters
\boldsymbol{c}_{\sigma}=(c_{i})_{i\in I_{\circ}}
. The QSP-coideal subalgebra
\mathbf{B}_{\boldsymbol{c_{\sigma}}}^{\omega}=\omega(\mathbf{B}_{\boldsymbol{c_{\sigma}}})
has the same zonal spherical functions as the coideal subalgebra from
[
28
]
. To match the convention with
[
28
]
, let
i\in\mathbf{I}_{\scriptscriptstyle\otimes}
with
\chi_{1}(K_{i}K_{\tau(i)}^{-1})=q
.

Lemma A.1
.

For the parameters
\boldsymbol{c}_{\sigma}
of the QSP-coideal subalgebra
\mathbf{B}_{\boldsymbol{c_{\sigma}}}^{\omega}
it holds that
c_{i}c_{\tau(i)}^{-1}=(-1)^{m}q^{2\sigma}
.

Proof.

Using
[
21
,
Cor 6.6
]
the elements

L_{1,2}^{+}-q^{\sigma}S(L^{-}_{2,\tau(i)}),\quad\text{and}\quad L_{\tau(i)-1,\tau(i)}^{+}-q^{-\sigma}S(L^{-}_{l,\tau(i)-1})

are generators of
\mathbf{B}_{\boldsymbol{c}}^{\omega}
, here
L_{i,j}^{\pm}
denote the
L
-operators, cf.
[
14
,
8.5.2
]
. By
[
14
,
6.2.3 (65), 8.5.2
]
, it follows that
c_{i}c_{\tau(i)}^{-1}=(-1)^{m}q^{2\sigma}
.
∎

Proposition A.2
.

The restriction of
(\mathbf{B}^{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{c_{\sigma}}},\mathbf{B}_{\boldsymbol{c_{\sigma}}})
zonal spherical functions are Macdonald-Koornwinder polynomials with parameters

(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4},k_{5})=(1/2,\sigma+1/2,m-1,-\sigma,1).

Proof.

Let
\{\vartheta_{\lambda}:\lambda\in P^{+}(2\Sigma)\}
denote the family of zonal spherical functions for
(\mathbf{B}_{\boldsymbol{c_{\sigma}}},\mathbf{B}_{\boldsymbol{c_{\sigma}}})
, cf. Theorem
3.12
. According to
[
28
,
Thm 3.4
]
the restriction of
(\mathbf{B}_{\boldsymbol{c_{\sigma}}},\mathbf{B}_{\boldsymbol{c_{\sigma}}})

zonal spherical functions equal Macdonald-Koornwinder polynomials in base
q^{2}
with parameters
(1/2,\sigma+1/2,m-1,-\sigma,1).

Using Proposition
4.4
we can express
\mathrm{Res}(\vartheta_{\lambda})
as
P_{\lambda}(\mathrm{Res}(\phi_{1}),\dots,\mathrm{Res}(\phi_{k}))
for elementary zonal spherical functions
\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{k}
and a polynomial
P_{\lambda}
. Since
\{\vartheta_{\lambda}\triangleleft K_{-\rho}:\lambda\in P^{+}(2\Sigma)\}
is a scalar multiple of the family of zonal spherical functions for
(\mathbf{B}^{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{c_{\sigma}}},\mathbf{B}_{\boldsymbol{c_{\sigma}}})
, we see that
\mathrm{Res}(\vartheta_{\lambda}\triangleleft K_{-\rho})=P_{\lambda}(\phi_{1}\triangleleft K_{-\rho},\dots,\phi_{k}\triangleleft K_{-\rho})

\mathrm{Res}(\phi_{i}\triangleleft K_{-\rho}):=\phi_{i}^{\prime}

\mathrm{Res}(\vartheta_{\lambda}\triangleleft K_{-\rho})=P_{\lambda}(\phi_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\phi_{k}^{\prime})

\{\mathrm{Res}(\vartheta_{\lambda}\triangleleft K_{-\rho}):\lambda\in P^{+}(2\Sigma)\}

∎
