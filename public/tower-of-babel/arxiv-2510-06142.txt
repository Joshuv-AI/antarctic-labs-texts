On the generating series of the degree sequence
Quang-Khai Nguyen

Abstract
We study the generating series associated with the degree sequence of a monomial self-map of a projective toric variety. We establish conditions under which this series has its circle of convergence as a natural boundary, and hence is a transcendental, non-holonomic function. In the case of toric surfaces, our results are sharp; moreover, we answer a question of Bell by proving that their reductions modulo p \displaystyle p are transcendental for all but finitely many prime numbers p \displaystyle p .

Introduction

Degree sequences and the associated dynamical degrees play a central role in algebraic dynamics.
They are defined for arbitrary dominant rational maps on normal projective varieties over any field (see
[
DS05
,
Tru20
,
Dan20
]
).
As fundamental birational invariants, dynamical degrees govern key dynamical features of the system and measure its complexity.
Although their general behavior remains largely mysterious (cf.
[
BDJ20
]
), they can be explicitly computed and are known to be algebraic in certain situations, for instance, for dominant toric rational maps. In this paper, we restrict to this latter setting.
Dynamical degrees are defined via the asymptotic behavior of degree sequences, which therefore encode finer information and are, in principle, more difficult to study.
Our main objective is to show that, although dynamical degrees are comparatively simple and well understood in this case, the associated degree sequences may already display non-trivial complexity.
We capture this complexity through the study of the corresponding generating series.

Throughout this paper, we work in the following setting.
Let
\displaystyle X(\Sigma)
be a projective toric variety of dimension
\displaystyle d\geq 2
over an algebraically closed field of arbitrary characteristic. The variety
\displaystyle X(\Sigma)
is determined by a lattice
\displaystyle N\cong\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, its dual lattice
\displaystyle M
, and a complete fan
\displaystyle\Sigma
in
\displaystyle N_{\mathbb{R}}
. Let
\displaystyle\varphi:X(\Sigma)\dashrightarrow X(\Sigma)
be a toric dominant rational map. It corresponds to a homomorphism
\displaystyle M\to M
given by a matrix
\displaystyle A\in{M}_{d}(\mathbb{Z})
of non-zero determinant. Let
\displaystyle 0\leq k\leq d
be an integer and
\displaystyle D
an ample Cartier divisor on
\displaystyle X(\Sigma)
, then the
\displaystyle k
-degree sequence
is defined by the intersection numbers
\displaystyle\deg_{D,k}(\varphi^{n})=((\varphi^{n})^{*}D^{k}\cdot D^{d-k})
for all
\displaystyle n\geq 0
(we use the convention
\displaystyle\varphi^{0}=\mathrm{id}
). This sequence does not depend on the linear equivalence class of
\displaystyle D
. The
\displaystyle k
-dynamical degree
of
\displaystyle\varphi
is defined as
\displaystyle\lambda_{k}(\varphi):=\lim\deg_{D,k}(\varphi^{n})^{{1}/{n}}
, which is equal to the product of the
\displaystyle k
eigenvalues of largest modulus of
\displaystyle A
, cf.
[
HP07
,
FW12
,
Lin12
]
. We denote
\displaystyle\lambda_{k}(\varphi)
by
\displaystyle\lambda_{k}
, unless otherwise specified. Our main object of interest is the
generating power series

\Delta_{k,\varphi,D}(z):=\sum_{n\geq 0}\deg_{D,k}(\varphi^{n})z^{n}\in\mathbb{Z}[[z]],

which converges inside the open disk
\displaystyle\mathbb{D}(0,\lambda_{k}(\varphi)^{-1})
.

These series have been studied extensively in
[
AABM99
,
BHM03
]
when
\displaystyle k=1
and have been proven to be rational in many cases, most notably for regular maps and for birational surface maps. When the base field is
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}
, the dynamical degrees and the degree sequences are well-understood when the dynamical system
\displaystyle(X(\Sigma),\varphi)
can be lifted to a simplicial projective toric variety
\displaystyle X(\tilde{\Sigma})
and a
\displaystyle k
-stable
self-map
\displaystyle\tilde{\varphi}
in the sense that
\displaystyle\tilde{\varphi}^{n*}=\tilde{\varphi}^{*n}
for the induced pullbacks on
\displaystyle H^{2k}(X(\tilde{\Sigma}),\mathbb{R})
and for all
\displaystyle n\geq 0
, a property first introduced in
[
FS95
]
. A consequence of
\displaystyle k
-stability is that the degree sequences satisfy a
linear recurrence relation
(cf.
[
DF01
, Corollary 2.2]
and
[
LW14
, Proposition 6.3]
), which implies that
\displaystyle\Delta_{k,\varphi,D}
is a
rational function
. On the other hand, under certain conditions on
\displaystyle A
, it is shown in
[
BK04
,
HP07
,
LW14
]
that the degree sequences do not satisfy any linear recurrence, so that
\displaystyle\Delta_{k,\varphi,D}
is not rational.

A natural way to measure the complexity of power series is to place them within the following hierarchy:

\mathrm{Rat}\subset\mathrm{Alg}\subset\mathrm{Hol}\subset\mathrm{DiA}\subset\mathbb{C}[[z]].

(1)

Here,
\displaystyle\mathrm{Rat}=\mathbb{C}(z)\cap\mathbb{C}[[z]]
denotes the ring of rational power series,
\displaystyle\mathrm{Alg}
the ring of power series that are
algebraic
over
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}(z)
(i.e., those satisfying a polynomial equation with coefficients in
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}(z)
),
\displaystyle\mathrm{Hol}
the ring of
holonomic
power series (that is, those satisfying a linear differential equation with coefficients in
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}(z)
), and
\displaystyle\mathrm{DiA}
the ring of
differentially algebraic
power series, those satisfying an algebraic differential equation with coefficients in
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}(z)
.
Power series that do not belong to
\displaystyle\mathrm{Alg}
(resp.
\displaystyle\mathrm{DiA}
) are called
transcendental
(resp.
hypertranscendental
or
differentially transcendental
).
This classification also provides information about the corresponding coefficient sequences. Indeed, a power series is rational if and only if its coefficients satisfy a linear recurrence with constant coefficients, while it is holonomic if and only if its coefficients satisfy a linear recurrence relation with polynomial coefficients (see
[
Sta11
, Chapter 4]
and
[
SF99
, Chapter 6]
). Thus, more involved functional equations give rise to more complex coefficient sequences.
When restricted to power series with algebraic coefficients, the rings
\displaystyle\mathrm{Hol}
and
\displaystyle\mathrm{DiA}
are countable, and they play for arithmetic holomorphic functions a role analogous to that of the ring of periods among complex numbers
[
KZ01
]
. The classification (
1
) is also central in enumerative combinatorics, where the nature of a generating series reflects structural properties of the objects being counted (cf.
[
BM06
]
).
It has long been a classical problem to determine to which ring a given series arising from number theory, combinatorics, complex dynamics, or physics belongs.
Hilbert
[
Hil02
]
already observed that many important number-theoretic functions, such as the Riemann zeta function, do not belong to
\displaystyle\mathrm{DiA}
. For further results in this direction, we refer to
[
Sta80
,
BT94
,
SF99
,
ADH21
]
and the references therein.

A distinctive feature of holonomic convergent power series is that they admit analytic continuation to all but finitely many points of

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}
(see, for instance,
[
Sta80
, Theorem 2.1 and 4(a)]
). A natural strategy to prove that a power series is not algebraic or holonomic is therefore to show that it has infinitely many singularities. In the case of the series
\displaystyle\Delta_{k,\varphi,D}(z)
, we investigate its analytic properties in greater detail and establish a stronger result. Namely, we will prove that it admits the circle
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(0,{\lambda_{k}}^{-1})
as a
natural boundary
, i.e., it cannot be analytically continued at any point on
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(0,{\lambda_{k}}^{-1})
. Our main theorem, Theorem
A
, may be viewed as a generalization of
[
Fav03
, Théorème principal]
,
[
BK04
, Theorem 1.1]
,
[
HP07
, Proposition 7.6]
, and
[
LW14
, Theorem C]
. The existence of a natural boundary shows that even in relatively simple cases where the dynamical degrees can be computed explicitly, the associated degree sequences may nevertheless exhibit some complexity.

Theorem A
.

Let
\displaystyle\varphi:X(\Sigma)\dashrightarrow X(\Sigma)
be a dominant toric rational self-map of a projective toric variety of dimension
\displaystyle d\geq 2
, which corresponds to a matrix
\displaystyle A\in M_{d}(\mathbb{Z})
with
\displaystyle\det A\neq 0
. Let
\displaystyle D
be an ample Cartier divisor on
\displaystyle X(\Sigma)
. Assume that the eigenvalues of
\displaystyle A
are ordered as
\displaystyle|\mu_{1}|\geq|\mu_{2}|\geq|\mu_{3}|\geq\ldots\geq|\mu_{d}|
.
Let
\displaystyle 1\leq k\leq d-1
, and assume further that

•

\displaystyle|\mu_{k-1}|>|\mu_{k}|=|\mu_{k+1}|>|\mu_{k+2}|
,

•

\displaystyle\mu_{k}
and
\displaystyle\mu_{k+1}
are complex conjugate, and
\displaystyle\mu_{k+1}/\mu_{k}
is not a root of unity.

Then the series
\displaystyle\Delta_{k,\varphi,D}(z)
has the circle
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(0,{\lambda_{k}}^{-1})
as a natural boundary. In particular, the series
\displaystyle\Delta_{k,\varphi,D}(z)
is transcendental and non-holomonic over
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}(z)
.

Remark 1.1
.

There are several settings in which it has been shown that a power series is either rational or has its circle of convergence as a natural boundary. See, for instance,
[
BCR13
]
in the context of Mahler equations, and
[
BGNS23
,
BHN25
]
in the context of Artin-Mazur zeta functions arising from dynamics. It would be interesting to know whether a similar dichotomy also holds in our setting; in this direction, see Theorem
D
.
We stress, however, that proving the existence of a natural boundary does not imply that the corresponding function is differentially transcendental. Establishing the latter property usually requires more sophisticated algebraic tools and is often related to the existence of a functional equation (not of differential type) satisfied by the function in question (see
[
ADH21
]
). It would therefore be interesting to determine whether the generating series in Theorem
A
are differentially transcendental.

Remark 1.2
.

One can consider other variants of the generating series, such as the Artin-Mazur style zeta function

\zeta_{k,\varphi,D}(z):=\mathrm{exp}\Bigg(\sum_{n\geq 1}\dfrac{\deg_{D,k}(\varphi^{n})}{n}z^{n}\Bigg)

which also converges on
\displaystyle\mathbb{D}(0,\lambda_{k}^{-1})
. Observe that

\displaystyle z\dfrac{d}{dz}\log\zeta_{k,\varphi,D}(z)=\Delta_{k,\varphi,D}(z)-(D^{d})
. Therefore
\displaystyle\zeta_{k,\varphi,D}(z)
also has
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(0,\lambda_{k}^{-1})
as a natural boundary.

The Cesaro mean of the sequence
\displaystyle\deg_{D,k}(\varphi^{n})
is given by

\sum_{n\geq 1}\dfrac{\sum_{0\leq m\leq n-1}\deg_{D,k}(\varphi^{m})}{n}z^{n}.

A computation similar to Theorem
A
implies that under the same assumption, it also admits
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(0,\lambda_{k}^{-1})
as a natural boundary.

The proof of Theorem
A
proceeds as follows. First, we use the standard dictionary relating intersection theory in toric varieties and mixed volumes to compute the degrees, following
[
HS95
]
. It expresses the degree sequence as the values of
a piecewise linear function at the sequence of powers of an auxiliary matrix. Under our assumption on the eigenvalues of
\displaystyle A
, we are then able to reduce the whole problem to a two-dimensional situation where the auxiliary matrix
becomes conformal. At this stage, we need to control a series of the form
\displaystyle\sum h(\chi^{n})z^{n}
where
\displaystyle h
is piecewise linear on the complex plane, and
\displaystyle\chi
is a complex number whose argument is incommensurable with
\displaystyle 2\pi
. The key argument is to consider the Fourier series of the restriction of
\displaystyle h
to the unit circle, and to exploit the discontinuity of the derivative of
\displaystyle h
to infer that infinitely many of its Fourier coefficients
are non-zero. To conclude that the generating series has a natural boundary, we use in a crucial way that
these coefficients are non-zero along some arithmetic progression, which follows
from the Skolem-Mahler-Lech theorem.

In
[
BDJ20
]
, the authors construct the first example of a dominant rational self-map
\displaystyle f_{\mu}
of
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{2}
with transcendental dynamical degree
\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(f_{\mu})
. The map
\displaystyle f_{\mu}
is of the form
\displaystyle f_{\mu}=g\circ\varphi_{\mu}
, where

g:\mathbb{P}^{2}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{2},[x_{0}:x_{1}:x_{2}]\to[x_{0}(x_{1}+x_{2}-x_{0}):x_{1}(x_{2}+x_{0}-x_{1}):x_{2}(x_{0}+x_{1}-x_{2})],

is a birational involution of
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{2}
and
\displaystyle\varphi_{\mu}
is the monomial map associated with
\displaystyle A_{\mu}=\begin{pmatrix}{\operatorname{Re}\,}\mu&-\im\mu\\
\im\mu&{\operatorname{Re}\,}\mu\end{pmatrix}
where
\displaystyle\mu\in\mathbb{Z}[\mathrm{i}]
is a Gaussian integer whose argument is incommensurable
with
\displaystyle{2\pi}
. Recall from
loc. cit.
that
\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(f_{\mu})>\lambda_{1}(\varphi_{\mu})=\lambda_{2}(\varphi_{\mu})^{1/2}=\lambda_{2}(f_{\mu})^{1/2}
and we have the identity:

(1+\Delta_{1,f_{\mu},\mathcal{O}(1)}(z))(2-\Delta_{1,\varphi_{\mu},\mathcal{O}(1)}(z))=2

for
\displaystyle|z|<\lambda_{1}(f_{\mu})^{-1}
, cf. Formula (2.2) and Proposition 2.8 in
loc. cit.
. It is straightforward to deduce the following corollary by applying Theorem
A
and
[
BFJ06
, Main Theorem]
.

Corollary B
.

For any Gaussian integer
\displaystyle\mu
whose argument is incommensurable
with
\displaystyle{2\pi}
, the generating series

\displaystyle\Delta_{1,f_{\mu},\mathcal{O}(1)}(z)
of the map
\displaystyle f_{\mu}=g\circ\varphi_{\mu}
is meromorphic in the disk
\displaystyle\mathbb{D}(0,\lambda_{2}(f_{\mu})^{-1/2})
with a simple pole at
\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(f_{\mu})^{-1}
, and admits the circle
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(0,\lambda_{2}(f_{\mu})^{-1/2})

Let
\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}
denote the category of all normal projective varieties
\displaystyle X
over arbitrary fields
\displaystyle K

\displaystyle D
, and all dominant rational self-maps
\displaystyle f

that the set of degree sequences attached to elements of

In the two-dimensional case, Theorem

.

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{p}(z)

Remark 1.3
.

We stress that the study of reductions modulo
\displaystyle p
of power series in
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}[[x]]
is an active topic, another way to measure the complexity of the coefficient sequence. When such a power series is algebraic, all its reductions modulo
\displaystyle p
are necessarily algebraic. The converse, however, is false: there exist transcendental power series whose reductions are algebraic for every prime
\displaystyle p
. In particular, this intriguing phenomenon occurs for important families of holonomic power series arising from geometry (such as diagonals of algebraic power series
[
Del84
]
and some hypergeometric series
[

). It has proved useful in questions of functional transcendence and has also led to challenging open problems (see, for instance,
[

,
ABD19
,
ABC23
,
CFVM25
]
).

Remark 1.4
.

Note that by Remark
1.2
, the same dichotomy holds for the Artin-Mazur zeta function
\displaystyle\zeta_{1,\varphi,D}(z)
in the toric surface case. Similar results have been proved using the Pólya–Carlson theorem for other kinds of Artin-Mazur zeta functions, see
[
MT88
, Corollary 10.4]

and
[
BGNS23

for examples. However, the latter papers put conditions on the coefficients of the series that are not satisfied in our case.

Finally, we prove an intriguing rigidity result for the generating series of monomial surface maps, which follows from Theorem

[

]

To state our result, we need an auxiliary notion. A matrix
\displaystyle A\in M_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
is said to be

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}
if there exists no
\displaystyle A^{n}
-invariant line in
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}^{2}
for all
\displaystyle n\geq 1
, or equivalently,
\displaystyle A^{n}
has no rational eigenvalues. A toric rational self-map
\displaystyle\varphi

Theorem E
.

Let
\displaystyle\varphi
and
\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime}
be dominant toric rational self-maps of a projective toric surface
\displaystyle X(\Sigma)
that are absolutely irreducible over
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}
. Let
\displaystyle D
and
\displaystyle D^{\prime}
be ample Cartier divisors on
\displaystyle X(\Sigma)
. Assume that
\displaystyle\varphi
, with respect to
\displaystyle D
, and
\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime}
, with respect to
\displaystyle D^{\prime}
, have the same
\displaystyle 1
-degree sequence, i.e.,

\Delta_{1,\varphi,D}=\Delta_{1,\varphi^{\prime},D^{\prime}}.

Then there exists
\displaystyle u\in\{1,2,3,4,6\}
such that
\displaystyle\varphi^{u}
and

are semi-conjugate, that is,
\displaystyle f\circ\varphi^{u}=\varphi^{\prime u}\circ f

The difficulty of Theorem

\displaystyle\Delta_{1,\varphi,D}
and

to compare the coefficients of both sides. One can ask whether
\displaystyle\Delta_{1,f,D}=\Delta_{1,f,D^{\prime}}

\displaystyle D=D^{\prime}
, but it is unclear at this stage how to recover the line bundle from the generating series.

Toric geometry

In this section, we recall some materials from toric geometry and aim to interpret the degree sequences using mixed volumes, following
[
Oda93
]
.

A
toric variety
is a normal variety that contains an open dense torus, whose natural multiplicative action on itself extends to the whole variety.

2.1
Toric varieties

Let
\displaystyle d\geq 1
,
\displaystyle N
a free abelian group of rank
\displaystyle d
, and
\displaystyle M=\Hom_{\mathbb{Z}}(N,\mathbb{Z})
its dual lattice. We denote
\displaystyle N_{\mathbb{R}}:=N\otimes\mathbb{R}\cong\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\displaystyle M_{\mathbb{R}}:=M\otimes\mathbb{R}
the associated vector spaces equipped with the natural pairing
\displaystyle\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
, and similarly for
\displaystyle N_{\mathbb{Q}}
,
\displaystyle M_{\mathbb{Q}}
. We fix a basis of
\displaystyle N
and the dual basis for
\displaystyle M
.

A
polyhedral convex cone
in
\displaystyle N_{\mathbb{R}}
is a set of the form
\displaystyle\sigma=\Big\{\sum_{v\in S}\lambda_{v}v:\lambda_{v}\geq 0\Big\}
for a finite subset
\displaystyle S\subset N_{\mathbb{R}}
. A polyhedral convex cone
\displaystyle\sigma
is said to be
strongly convex
if
\displaystyle\sigma\cap(-\sigma)=\{0\}
, and said to be
rational
if
\displaystyle\sigma
is generated by a finite subset
\displaystyle S
of
\displaystyle N
. For the rest of this section, by cones we mean strongly convex rational polyhedral cones. The
dimension

\displaystyle\dim\sigma
of
\displaystyle\sigma
is the dimension of the vector space generated by
\displaystyle\sigma
. One-dimensional cones are also called
rays
. A cone is
simplicial
if it is generated by linearly independent vectors. A cone is
regular
if it is generated by a set which is part of a basis of
\displaystyle N
.

A
fan

\displaystyle\Sigma
in
\displaystyle N_{\mathbb{R}}
is a finite collection of cones such that every face of a cone in
\displaystyle\Sigma
is also a cone in
\displaystyle\Sigma
, and the intersection of two cones in
\displaystyle\Sigma
is a face of each. The
support
of
\displaystyle\Sigma
is defined as

\displaystyle|\Sigma|:=\displaystyle\bigcup_{\sigma\in\Sigma}\sigma
. We denote by
\displaystyle\Sigma(1)
the set of rays in
\displaystyle\Sigma
. A fan is said to be
simplicial
(resp.
regular
) if all of its cones are simplicial (resp. regular). A fan
\displaystyle\Sigma
is said to be
complete
if
\displaystyle|\Sigma|=N_{\mathbb{R}}
.
A fan
\displaystyle\tilde{\Sigma}
is a
refinement
of
\displaystyle\Sigma
if every cone in
\displaystyle\tilde{\Sigma}
is included in a cone of
\displaystyle\Sigma
.

Each fan
\displaystyle\Sigma
determines a toric variety
\displaystyle X(\Sigma)
of dimension
\displaystyle d
with a dense torus
\displaystyle\mathbb{G}_{m}^{d}
, and all toric varieties arise in this way. The variety
\displaystyle X(\Sigma)
is complete (resp. regular) if and only if
\displaystyle\Sigma
is complete (resp. regular). We say that
\displaystyle X(\Sigma)
is simplicial if
\displaystyle\Sigma
is simplicial.

We say that
\displaystyle\Sigma
is projective if
\displaystyle X(\Sigma)
is projective.

Let
\displaystyle\Sigma

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle X(\Sigma)

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}

It turns out that
\displaystyle\mathbb{G}_{m}^{d}

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}
-)
\displaystyle\mathrm{SF}(\Sigma)

-)support functions with respect to
\displaystyle\Sigma

\displaystyle v_{\rho}

\displaystyle\rho=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}v_{\rho}
. Every
\displaystyle h\in(\mathbb{Q}\text{-})\mathrm{SF}(\Sigma)
determines a
\displaystyle\mathbb{G}_{m}^{d}
-invariant (
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}
-)Cartier divisor
\displaystyle D(h):=\sum_{\rho\in\Sigma(1)}h(v_{\rho})D_{\rho}
. Conversely, every
\displaystyle\mathbb{G}_{m}^{d}
-invariant (
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}
-)Cartier divisor
\displaystyle D
can be regarded as a (
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}
)-Weil divisor of the form
\displaystyle D=\sum_{\rho\in\Sigma(1)}a_{\rho}D(\rho)
with
\displaystyle a_{\rho}\in\mathbb{Z}
(resp.
\displaystyle a_{\rho}\in\mathbb{Q}
), which defines a function
\displaystyle h_{D}\in(\mathbb{Q}\text{-})\mathrm{SF}(\Sigma)
by
\displaystyle h_{D}(v_{\rho})=a_{\rho}
for all
\displaystyle\rho\in\Sigma(1)
. This gives a one-to-one correspondence between (
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}
-)
\displaystyle\mathrm{SF}(\Sigma)
and
\displaystyle\mathbb{G}_{m}^{d}
-invariant (
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}
-)Cartier divisors. A
\displaystyle\mathbb{G}_{m}^{d}
-invariant
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}
-Cartier divisor
\displaystyle D
is ample if and only if
\displaystyle h_{D}
is
strictly convex
, i.e., it is a convex function and defined by different
\displaystyle m(\sigma)
for different
\displaystyle d
-dimensional cones
\displaystyle\sigma
in
\displaystyle\Sigma
. Thus
\displaystyle\Sigma
is projective if and only if there is a strictly convex function in
\displaystyle\mathrm{SF}(\Sigma)
.

A
rational polytope
in
\displaystyle M_{\mathbb{R}}
is the convex hull of finitely many points in
\displaystyle M_{\mathbb{Q}}
. A function
\displaystyle h\in\mathbb{Q}\text{-}\mathrm{SF}(\Sigma)
determines a (non-empty) rational polytope
\displaystyle P(h):=\{m\in M_{\mathbb{R}}:m\leq h\}\subset M_{\mathbb{R}}
. It follows that a
\displaystyle\mathbb{G}_{m}^{d}
-invariant
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}
-Cartier divisor
\displaystyle D
defines a rational polytope
\displaystyle P_{D}:=P(h_{D})
. Two
\displaystyle\mathbb{G}_{m}^{d}
-invariant
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}

\displaystyle D_{1}
and
\displaystyle D_{2}

\displaystyle h_{D_{1}}-h_{D_{2}}

\displaystyle N_{\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle P_{D_{1}}

\displaystyle P_{D_{2}}
. We note that
\displaystyle h\in\mathbb{Q}\text{-}\mathrm{SF}(\Sigma)

\displaystyle P(h)
is a polytope with non-empty

.

If
\displaystyle h\in\mathrm{SF}(\Sigma)

\displaystyle P(h)
is a

\displaystyle M
.

2.3
Toric rational maps and the pull-back operator

For a homomorphism
\displaystyle A:M\to M
, we denote by
\displaystyle A
the induced map
\displaystyle M_{\mathbb{R}}\to M_{\mathbb{R}}
, and by
\displaystyle\check{A}
the dual maps
\displaystyle N\to N
and
\displaystyle N_{\mathbb{R}}\to N_{\mathbb{R}}
.

Let
\displaystyle\Sigma
be a complete fan. A rational map
\displaystyle\varphi:X(\Sigma)\dashrightarrow X(\Sigma)
is said to be
toric
if it is equivariant under the action of the torus
\displaystyle\mathbb{G}_{m}^{d}
. Every group homomorphism
\displaystyle A:M\to M
with
\displaystyle A=(a_{ij})\in M_{d}(\mathbb{Z})
gives rise to the group homomorphism
\displaystyle\check{A}:N\to N
, which in turn determines the monomial map

\displaystyle\varphi:\mathbb{G}_{m}^{d}\to\mathbb{G}_{m}^{d},(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})\mapsto(z_{1}^{a_{11}}\cdots z_{d}^{a_{d1}},\ldots,z_{1}^{a_{1d}}\cdots z_{d}^{a_{dd}})

where
\displaystyle z_{1},\ldots,z_{d}
are coordinates on
\displaystyle\mathbb{G}_{m}^{d}
, hence a toric rational map
\displaystyle X(\Sigma)\dashrightarrow X(\Sigma)
. Conversely, all toric rational self-maps arise in this way. The map
\displaystyle\varphi:X(\Sigma)\dashrightarrow X(\Sigma)
is dominant if and only if
\displaystyle\det(A)\neq 0.
In this case, its topological degree is equal to
\displaystyle|\det(A)|
.

For a
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}
-Cartier divisor
\displaystyle D
on
\displaystyle X(\Sigma)
, its
pullback
via the toric rational map
\displaystyle\varphi
is defined as follows. We can find a regular refinement
\displaystyle\tilde{\Sigma}
of
\displaystyle\Sigma
so that the induced map
\displaystyle\tilde{\varphi}
is regular. It gives rise to a regular modification
\displaystyle\pi:X(\tilde{\Sigma})\to X(\Sigma)
, induced by the identity map
\displaystyle\id:M\to M
, such that
\displaystyle\tilde{\varphi}=\varphi\circ\pi
. The pullback
\displaystyle\varphi^{*}D
is defined as
\displaystyle\varphi^{*}D:=\pi_{*}\tilde{\varphi}^{*}D
which is only a
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}
-Weil divisor in general. This definition does not depend on the choice of
\displaystyle\tilde{\Sigma}
.

If
\displaystyle D=\sum_{\rho\in\Sigma(1)}h_{D}(v_{\rho})D(\rho)
is a
\displaystyle\mathbb{G}_{m}^{d}
-invariant (
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}
-)Cartier divisor which corresponds to
\displaystyle h_{D}\in(\mathbb{Q}\text{-})\mathrm{SF}(\Sigma)
and the polytope
\displaystyle P_{D}\subset M_{\mathbb{R}}
, then we have
\displaystyle\varphi^{*}D=\sum_{\rho\in\Sigma(1)}h_{D}(\check{A}(v_{\rho}))D(\rho)
, which is also a
\displaystyle\mathbb{G}_{m}^{d}
-invariant (
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}
-)Cartier divisor. Further,
\displaystyle\varphi^{*}D
corresponds to
\displaystyle h_{\varphi^{*}D}=h_{D}\circ\check{A}\in(\mathbb{Q}\text{-})\mathrm{SF}(\Sigma)
and
\displaystyle P(h_{D}\circ\check{A})=AP(h_{D})\subset M_{\mathbb{R}}
.

2.4
Mixed volumes and intersection numbers

Let
\displaystyle K_{1},\ldots,K_{d}
be convex bodies in
\displaystyle M_{\mathbb{R}}
. A theorem of Minkowski and Steiner says that the map given by the
Minkowski sum

\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},(r_{1},\ldots,r_{d})\mapsto\vol(r_{1}K_{1}+\cdots+r_{d}K_{d})

is a homogeneous polynomial of total degree
\displaystyle d
. The
mixed volume

\displaystyle\vol(K_{1},\ldots,K_{d})
of
\displaystyle K_{1},\ldots,K_{d}
is the non-negative number defined as the coefficient of the monomial
\displaystyle r_{1}\cdots r_{d}
, divided by
\displaystyle d!
.

By
[
Oda93
, p. 79]
, if
\displaystyle D_{1},\ldots,D_{d}
are
\displaystyle\mathbb{G}_{m}^{d}
-invariant
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}
-Cartier divisors on a complete toric variety
\displaystyle X(\Sigma)
, then the intersection product can be interpreted by mixed volumes as

(D_{1}\cdots D_{d})=d!\vol(P_{D_{1}},\ldots,P_{D_{d}})\in\mathbb{Q}.

Here, we recall that
\displaystyle P_{D_{1}},\ldots,P_{D_{d}}
are the polytopes associated with
\displaystyle D_{1},\ldots,D_{d}
, respectively.

2.5
Degree sequences

Let
\displaystyle X(\Sigma)
be a projective toric variety defined by a fan
\displaystyle\Sigma
in a lattice
\displaystyle N
of rank
\displaystyle d\geq 1
. Let
\displaystyle\varphi:X(\Sigma)\dashrightarrow X(\Sigma)
be a dominant toric rational map associated with a group homomorphism
\displaystyle A:M\to M
,
\displaystyle D
be an ample Cartier divisor on
\displaystyle X(\Sigma)
. Since every Cartier divisor is linear equivalent to a
\displaystyle\mathbb{G}_{m}^{d}
-invariant one, we may assume that
\displaystyle D
is
\displaystyle\mathbb{G}_{m}^{d}
-invariant. For
\displaystyle 1\leq k\leq d
, the
\displaystyle k
-degree sequence of
\displaystyle\varphi
can be rewritten as

\deg_{D,k}(\varphi^{n})=((\varphi^{n})^{*}D^{k}\cdot D^{d-k})=d!\vol(A^{n}P_{D}[k],P_{D}[d-k])\in\mathbb{Z}.

Here, the notation
\displaystyle Q[\ell]
stands for
\displaystyle\ell
times repetition of
\displaystyle Q
.

Recall that the
\displaystyle k
-dynamical degree of
\displaystyle\varphi
is
\displaystyle\lambda_{k}=\lambda_{k}(\varphi)=\lim\deg_{k,D}(\varphi^{n})^{1/n}\in[1,\infty).
Thus
\displaystyle\deg_{D,k}(\varphi^{n})\asymp\lambda_{k}^{n}
.

3
Some auxiliary results on piecewise linear functions

In this section, we present two auxiliary results for real-valued piecewise linear functions that will be useful later. The readers can skip this section on a first reading.

A piecewise linear function on a finite-dimensional real vector space
\displaystyle V
is a continuous 1-homogeneous function
\displaystyle h\colon V\to\mathbb{R}
such that there exists a complete (not necessarily rational) fan
\displaystyle\Sigma
of
\displaystyle V
and for each cone
\displaystyle\sigma\in\Sigma
a linear function
\displaystyle h_{\sigma}:V\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle h=h_{\sigma}
on the cone
\displaystyle\sigma
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Every piecewise linear function is Lipschitz.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle h:V\to\mathbb{R}
be a piecewise linear function with respect to some fan. We fix a norm
\displaystyle|\cdot|
on
\displaystyle V
. We can find some
\displaystyle C>0
sufficiently large so that if
\displaystyle v,w\in V
lie in the same cone, then
\displaystyle|h(v)-h(w)|\leq C|v-w|
. If
\displaystyle v
and
\displaystyle w
are not in the same cone, we can join them by a segment
\displaystyle v=v_{0},v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}=w
where
\displaystyle v_{i}
,
\displaystyle v_{i+1}
stay in the same cone for every
\displaystyle i=0,\ldots,n-1
. Therefore

|h(v)-h(w)|\leq\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}|h(v_{i})-h(v_{i+1})|\leq C\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}|v_{i}-v_{i+1}|=C|v-w|

as desired.
∎

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\displaystyle g:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
be a piecewise linear function. Let
\displaystyle\chi
be some non-zero complex number. We denote by
\displaystyle\theta
its argument. Then the power series
\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}g({\operatorname{Re}\,}(\chi^{n}),\im(\chi^{n}))z^{n}
converges inside the disk
\displaystyle\mathbb{D}(0,|\chi|^{-1})
and can be written as

\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\dfrac{a_{m}}{1-e^{\mathrm{i}m\theta}|\chi|z}

where
\displaystyle a_{m}\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\displaystyle\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}|a_{m}|<\infty
. Further, there is a linear recurrence sequence
\displaystyle\{d_{m}:m\in\mathbb{Z}\},
such that
\displaystyle a_{m}=\dfrac{d_{m}}{m^{2}-1}
for all
\displaystyle m\neq\pm 1
.

Proof.

Since
\displaystyle g
is piecewise linear, we have
\displaystyle|g({\operatorname{Re}\,}(\chi^{n}),\im(\chi^{n}))|=O(|\chi|^{n})
which yields that
\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}g({\operatorname{Re}\,}(\chi^{n}),\im(\chi^{n}))z^{n}
converges inside the disk
\displaystyle\mathbb{D}(0,|\chi|^{-1})
.

It is convenient to identify
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}
to make use of the conformal structure on
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}
, and view
\displaystyle g
as a 1-homogeneous continuous function on
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}
. Then

g({\operatorname{Re}\,}(\chi^{n}),\im(\chi^{n}))z^{n}=\sum_{n\geq 0}|\chi|^{n}g(e^{\mathrm{i}n\theta})z^{n}

inside the disk
\displaystyle\mathbb{D}(0,|\chi|^{-1}).

We decompose the function
\displaystyle g|_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}
in the Hilbertian basis consisting of
\displaystyle\{e^{\mathrm{i}mx}:m\in\mathbb{Z}\}
in
\displaystyle L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
. Since
\displaystyle g|_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}
is a piecewise linear function on
\displaystyle\mathbb{S}^{1}
, it is Lipschitz by Lemma
3.1
. Therefore, its Fourier series converges uniformly to
\displaystyle g|_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}
. In addition, since
\displaystyle g|_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}
is piecewise linear,
\displaystyle g|_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}
belongs to the Sobolev space
\displaystyle W^{1,2}(\mathbb{S}^{1}):=\{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1}):f^{\prime}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})\}
. Thus, for
\displaystyle x\in[0,2\pi]
, we may write

g(e^{\mathrm{i}x})=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{m}e^{\mathrm{i}mx}

for
\displaystyle a_{m}\in\mathbb{C}
and we have
\displaystyle\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}m^{2}|a_{m}|^{2}<\infty
by Parseval’s identity. It follows that
\displaystyle\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}|a_{m}|<\infty
. Therefore, for
\displaystyle z\in\mathbb{D}(0,|\chi|^{-1})
, we obtain

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}|\chi|^{n}g(e^{\mathrm{i}n\theta})z^{n}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{m}\sum_{n\geq 0}|\chi|^{n}e^{\mathrm{i}mn\theta}z^{n}=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{m}\dfrac{1}{1-e^{\mathrm{i}m\theta}|\chi|z}.

Now we compute
\displaystyle a_{m}
. Since
\displaystyle g
is piecewise linear, we can write

\displaystyle g(z)=b_{j}z+c_{j}\overline{z}
when
\displaystyle\arg z\in[\alpha_{j},\alpha_{j+1})
, where
\displaystyle 0=\alpha_{0}<\alpha_{1}<\ldots<\alpha_{t}=2\pi
are the break points on the unit circle
\displaystyle\mathbb{S}^{1}
for some
\displaystyle t\in\mathbb{N}
, see the picture below as an illustration.

\displaystyle\alpha_{0}=\alpha_{5}\mod 2\pi\mathbb{Z}
\displaystyle\alpha_{1}
\displaystyle\alpha_{4}
\displaystyle\alpha_{2}

\displaystyle\alpha_{3}

Since
\displaystyle g
is real-valued, we have
\displaystyle a_{-m}=\overline{a_{m}}
. For
\displaystyle m\neq\pm 1
, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle a_{m}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}g(e^{\mathrm{i}x})e^{-\mathrm{i}mx}dx=\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}\int_{\alpha_{j}}^{\alpha_{j+1}}b_{j}e^{\mathrm{i}(-m+1)x}+c_{j}e^{\mathrm{i}(-m-1)x}dx

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}b_{j}\dfrac{e^{\mathrm{i}(-m+1)\alpha_{j+1}}-e^{\mathrm{i}(-m+1)\alpha_{j}}}{\mathrm{i}(-m+1)}+\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}c_{j}\dfrac{e^{\mathrm{i}(-m-1)\alpha_{j+1}}-e^{\mathrm{i}(-m-1)\alpha_{j}}}{\mathrm{i}(-m-1)}.

For each
\displaystyle j=0,\ldots,t
, we denote
\displaystyle A_{j}=e^{\mathrm{i}\alpha_{j}}
, then

a_{m}=\dfrac{\mathrm{i}(-m-1)\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}b_{j}(A_{j+1}^{-m+1}-A_{j}^{-m+1})+\mathrm{i}(-m+1)\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}c_{j}(A_{j+1}^{-m-1}-A_{j}^{-m-1})}{m^{2}-1}.

Finally, set
\displaystyle d_{m}:=\mathrm{i}(-m-1)\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}b_{j}(A_{j+1}^{-m+1}-A_{j}^{-m+1})+\mathrm{i}(-m+1)\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}c_{j}(A_{j+1}^{-m-1}-A_{j}^{-m-1})
for
\displaystyle m\in\mathbb{Z}
, which is a power sum; thus, the sequence
\displaystyle\{d_{m}:m\in\mathbb{Z}\}
satisfy a linear recurrence relation as wanted.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

It is known that every linear recurrence sequence can be represented as values of a power sum
\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{h}\rho_{j}^{m}P_{j}(m)
for some distinct non-zero complex numbers
\displaystyle\rho_{j}
and some non-zero polynomials
\displaystyle P_{j}
. Such a representation is unique. The
degree
of such a power sum is defined as the maximal degree between
\displaystyle P_{j}
.

In Proposition
3.2
, the sequence
\displaystyle d_{m}
is represented by a power sum of degree at most 1.

are in
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

We will use the
surface area measure
to compute the mixed volume, following
[
Sch13
]
. We fix a scalar product for which the given basis of
\displaystyle N
forms an orthogonal basis, which gives rise to a Riemannian metric on
\displaystyle\mathbb{S}^{1}\subset N_{\mathbb{R}}
. If
\displaystyle Q
is a compact polyhedron in
\displaystyle M_{\mathbb{R}}
, the surface area measure
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(Q)
on
\displaystyle\mathbb{S}^{1}\subset N_{\mathbb{R}}
is defined as
\displaystyle\sum_{v}\mathrm{Vol}(F_{v})\delta_{v}
where
\displaystyle v
runs over the outer unit normal vectors of the facets
\displaystyle F_{v}
of
\displaystyle Q
, and
\displaystyle\delta_{v}
denotes the Dirac measure at
\displaystyle v
. Here, the volume of each facet is its one-dimensional volume, in other words, its length. By
[
Sch13
, Formulae (5.19) and (5.23)]
, we have

\mathrm{Vol}(u(P_{D}),P_{D})=\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}(h_{D}\circ\check{u})\mathcal{S}(P_{D})=\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{v}\mathrm{Vol}(F_{v})h_{D}(\check{u}(v))

where
\displaystyle\check{u}:N_{\mathbb{R}}\to N_{\mathbb{R}}
is the dual linear map of
\displaystyle u
, and
\displaystyle v
runs over the outer unit normal vectors of the facets
\displaystyle F_{v}
of
\displaystyle P_{D}
. We set
\displaystyle h(u):=\sum_{v}\mathrm{Vol}(F_{v})h_{D}(\check{u}(v))
for
\displaystyle u\in\Pi
.

Since all coefficients
\displaystyle\mathrm{Vol}(F_{v})
are positive, it is sufficient to show that for any
\displaystyle v
, the function
\displaystyle h_{v}(u):=h_{D}(\check{u}(v))
is piecewise linear and convex. Recall the fact that every convex piecewise linear function can be represented as the maximum of linear forms; thus,
\displaystyle h_{D}=\max\{l_{1},\ldots,l_{n}\}
for some linear forms
\displaystyle l_{i}
with coefficients in
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}
. It follows that

h_{v}(u)=\max\{L_{1}(u),\ldots,L_{n}(u)\}

where
\displaystyle L_{i}(u)=l_{i}(\check{u}(v))
is a linear form on the space
\displaystyle\Pi
. Thus
\displaystyle h_{v}
is a convex piecewise linear function on
\displaystyle\Pi
for every
\displaystyle v
. In addition, since the vector
\displaystyle\mathrm{Vol}(F_{v})v
have coefficients in
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}
(as
\displaystyle P_{D}
is a lattice polytope), the linear functions
\displaystyle\mathrm{Vol}(F_{v})L_{i}(u)=l_{i}(\check{u}(\mathrm{Vol}(F_{v})v))
have coefficients in
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}
. Therefore,
the piecewise linear functions
\displaystyle\mathrm{Vol}(F_{v})h_{v}=\max\{\mathrm{Vol}(F_{v})L_{i}\}
also have coefficients in
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}
, and so does
\displaystyle h
.

Since
\displaystyle A^{n}\in\Pi
for all
\displaystyle n\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\deg_{D,1}(\varphi^{n})=2!\mathrm{Vol}(A^{n}P_{D},P_{D})=h(A^{n})\text{ for all }n\geq 0.
Finally, if
\displaystyle h
is linear, then
\displaystyle\Delta_{1,\varphi,D}(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}h(A^{n})z^{n}
would be rational which contradicts our result in §
5.1
.
∎

\displaystyle 1\leq i<j\leq s

\displaystyle c_{i}=c_{j}

\displaystyle\ell\in(i,j)

\displaystyle c_{\ell}=c_{i}

\displaystyle\ell\not\in[i,j]

Replacing
\displaystyle h(z)

\displaystyle h(z)-(c_{i}z+\overline{c_{i}z})

\displaystyle c_{i}=c_{j}=0

\displaystyle h(z)=0
for all
\displaystyle z\in C_{i}\cup C_{j}

We set
\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}

\displaystyle\sigma_{j}

\displaystyle\bigcup_{\ell\in(i,j)}\sigma_{\ell}\subset\sigma

\displaystyle h

\displaystyle z_{i}

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}

\displaystyle(i,j)

We can merge consecutive closed subsets

, whence
\displaystyle\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\mu_{1},\mu_{1}^{\prime})/\mathbb{Q})\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
. Therefore
\displaystyle u\neq 5,8
since both
\displaystyle\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(e^{\mathrm{i}2\pi/5})/\mathbb{Q})
and
\displaystyle\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(e^{\mathrm{i}2\pi/8})/\mathbb{Q})
are cyclic of order
\displaystyle 4
. Thus,
\displaystyle u=12
and
\displaystyle\xi
is a 12th primitive root of unity. It is known that the minimal polynomial of
\displaystyle\xi
is
\displaystyle X^{4}-X^{2}+1
, and
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}(\xi)
contains only three quadratic subfields:
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{3})
,
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}(\mathrm{i})
, and
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3})
. Since
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}(\mu_{1})
and
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}(\mu_{1}^{\prime})
are two complex quadratic subfield of
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}(\xi)
which are disjoint over
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}
, one of them must be
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}(\mathrm{i})
, and the other is
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3})
. We may assume that
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}(\mu_{1})=\mathbb{Q}(\mathrm{i})=\mathbb{Q}(\xi^{3})
and
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}(\mu_{1}^{\prime})=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3})=\mathbb{Q}(\xi^{4})
. Thus there are
\displaystyle a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{Q}
such that
\displaystyle\mu_{1}=a+b\xi^{3}
and
\displaystyle\mu_{1}^{\prime}=c+d\xi^{4}=c-d+d\xi^{2}
.

Since
\displaystyle\lambda_{1}^{2}=\mu_{1}\overline{\mu_{1}}\in\mathbb{N}
,
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}(\lambda_{1})
is a real subfield of
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}(\xi)
. Therefore, there are two cases.

•

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}(\lambda_{1})=\mathbb{Q}
, i.e.,
\displaystyle\lambda_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
. From
\displaystyle\mu_{1}=\xi\mu_{1}^{\prime}
, we obtain

a+b\xi^{3}=\xi(c-d+d\xi^{2})=(c-d)\xi+d\xi^{3}.

Thus
\displaystyle a=0
and
\displaystyle b=c=d
; hence,
\displaystyle\mu_{1}=\pm b\mathrm{i}
, which contradicts the hypothesis
\displaystyle\arg\mu_{1}\not\in 2\pi\mathbb{Q}
.

•

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}(\lambda_{1})=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{3})
, i.e.,
\displaystyle\lambda_{1}=\lambda\sqrt{3}
for some
\displaystyle\lambda\in\mathbb{N}
. We have
\displaystyle 3\lambda^{2}=\lambda_{1}^{2}=\mu_{1}\overline{\mu_{1}}=a^{2}+b^{2}
. Since
\displaystyle-1
is not a square modulo 3, it follows that
\displaystyle a=b=\lambda=0
, which is absurd.

We conclude that
\displaystyle u\not\in\{5,8,12\}
. The theorem is proven.

Remark 5.10
.

Observe that the irreducibility assumption is necessary. Indeed, consider monomial self-maps
\displaystyle\varphi
and
\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime}
of
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{2}
associated with matrices
\displaystyle A=\begin{pmatrix}a&0\\
0&b\end{pmatrix}\text{ and }A^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}a&0\\
0&c\end{pmatrix}

where
\displaystyle a,b,c
are positive integers and
\displaystyle a>b>c
. Let
\displaystyle D=D^{\prime}=\mathcal{O}(1)
. Then
\displaystyle\Delta_{1,\varphi,\mathcal{O}(1)}(z)=\Delta_{1,\varphi^{\prime},\mathcal{O}(1)}(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a^{n}z^{n}=\dfrac{1}{1-az}.
However,
\displaystyle A^{u}
and
\displaystyle A^{\prime u}
are not conjugate for all
\displaystyle u>0
.
