Feasibility of a non-invasive skin biomechanical device for rapid assessment of pediatric dehydration: a case–control study
Authors: Yıldız Büyükdereli Atadağ, Tuba Saygılı, Zekiye Temizkan, Zeynep Şencan, Hamit Sırrı Keten
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDehydration in children remains a common yet potentially life-threatening clinical challenge, particularly in primary care and emergency settings where rapid, objective, and non-invasive assessment tools are limited. Skin biomechanics may offer a new way of measuring hydration status.ObjectiveTo investigate the utility of a non-invasive skin biomechanical measurement device (MyotonPro®) in differentiating hydration status in children aged 6 months to 6 years.MethodsIn this prospective case–control study conducted at Gaziantep University Hospital, 64 children were evaluated using the MyotonPro® device at three anatomical sites: 1.5 cm above the umbilicus, and 4 cm above the wrist on the posterior surfaces of both arms. Dehydration was defined by serum osmolality ≥ 290 mOsm/kg or urine specific gravity > 1.025. Oscillation frequency, dynamic stiffness, and elasticity were measured and compared between dehydrated (n = 32) and non-dehydrated (n = 32) participants. Laboratory results and World Health Organization clinical dehydration scale findings were analysed and compared with the device measurements.ResultsChildren in the dehydration group exhibited significantly lower oscillation frequency (18.75 ± 2.63 Hz vs. 20.64 ± 4.16 Hz; p = 0.034) and dynamic stiffness (430.06 ± 83.60 N/m vs. 499.78 ± 169.28 N/m; p = 0.042) in the right arm. Although similar results were observed in the left arm, there was no statistically significant difference. Biochemical markers such as creatinine, sodium, plasma osmolality, and urine specific gravity were significantly elevated in the dehydration group. Correlations were observed between MyotonPro® parameters and serum creatinine, BUN, and albumin levels.ConclusionMyotonPro® demonstrated significant differences in selected parameters (right arm oscillation frequency and stiffness), suggesting potential sensitivity to hydration status. However, its overall discriminative ability was limited. Therefore, MyotonPro® may serve as a supportive, but not standalone, tool in clinical decision-making for monitoring hydration levels, particularly in primary care and emergency settings.

Introduction

Dehydration in childhood remains a significant cause of morbidity and mortality. If diagnosis is delayed, hypovolemic shock and fatal complications may develop [1–3]. In routine clinical practice, diagnosis relies on physical signs such as skin turgor, capillary refill, and sunken eyes; however, these indicators alone lack sufficient sensitivity and specificity [3, 4]. The gold standard method - comparing body weight before and after rehydration - is often not feasible in many clinical settings [3, 5]. Clinical signs like decreased skin turgor may suggest dehydration and fluid-electrolyte imbalance; however, their correlation with other assessments has not been adequately established. In clinical practice, the most reliable and commonly used assessment for dehydration and electrolyte imbalance is the analysis of serum electrolyte levels [4]. Given the variability among clinical dehydration scales [5, 6], there is a clear need for new tools and/or methods capable of providing rapid and objective measurements.

The human skin, the body’s largest organ, possesses a viscoelastic structure [7]. Skin turgor is essentially a macroscopic reflection of skin biomechanics. With dehydration, cellular and extracellular fluid volume decreases, the collagen-elastin matrix becomes strained, and the skin stiffens [4, 8]. In recent years, various devices have been developed to quantitatively measure the mechanical properties of human skin [7, 9]. Among these, myotonometry - a biomechanical assessment device - stands out for its light weight, portability, and ease of use, making it favorable for clinical applications. The device applies a brief mechanical impulse to the skin surface and calculates parameters such as stiffness, frequency, and relaxation time based on the resulting microvibrations [10]. Studies on healthy adult skin and scar tissue have shown that MyotonPro measurements are reproducible both within and between assessors [10, 11]. Its non-invasive usage has also been explored in various skin pathologies, such as lymphedema monitoring, yielding promising differential diagnostic results [12]. In the pediatric population, the applicability of the device was demonstrated in a study involving children with cerebral palsy, where MyotonPro was used to monitor muscle stiffness and objectively demonstrate treatment response [13].

Currently available dehydration scales (such as the Gorelick Scale, the Clinical Dehydration Scale, and the World Health Organization scale) often fail to provide definitive results due to various limitations, making diagnosis particularly challenging in the pediatric population [3, 4]. Obtaining blood and urine samples from this age group is difficult, and waiting for laboratory results can be time-consuming. Yet, the rapid recognition and treatment of dehydration in children is of vital importance [1–3]. In primary care settings such as family health centers and emergency departments, the lack of reliable tools capable of quickly, non-invasively, and objectively assessing hydration status in pediatric patients may lead to treatment delays or unnecessary laboratory testing. Evaluating the potential of the MyotonPro® device - which can quantify skin biomechanics within seconds - constitutes the primary objective of our study.

Non-invasive whole-body tools such as bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) and its graphical extension, bioelectrical impedance vector analysis (BIVA), can classify hydration by modeling extracellular and intracellular fluid compartments, and have shown utility in pediatric dialysis cohorts, including receiver operating characteristic (ROC)-derived thresholds for overhydration [14, 15]. Yet their logistical requirements—such as electrode placement, standardized posture, and cooperation during measurement—make them less practical for immediate assessment in acutely ill young children [14, 15]. To address this gap, we evaluated whether a portable myotonometry device—capturing local skin biomechanical responses within seconds—could provide supportive information on hydration status. The study aimed to compare Myoton measurements taken from dehydrated and non-dehydrated children, and to analyse the relationship between these measurements and indicators of hydration obtained from laboratory tests.

Methods

Following approval from the ethics committee (27 February 2025, code KAEK-002-2) and the Gaziantep University Hospital chief physician (24 March 2025), data collection for this prospective case-control study was conducted over a two-month period between April and May 2025. The study was conducted at Gaziantep University Hospital, Gaziantep, Turkey, in the Department of Pediatrics. It included children aged 6 months to 6 years in order to minimize age-related variability in measurement protocols.

Eligible participants were individuals who had blood and/or urine tests requested by their clinician at the time of presentation, whose legal guardians had provided written informed consent, who had voluntarily agreed to participate and who had a Body Mass Index (BMI) < 30. Exclusion criteria included BMI ≥ 30, dermatological conditions or trauma at the skin sites to be measured, and refusal to participate.

A priori sample size was calculated in G*Power 3.1 for a two-sided independent-means comparison with α = 0.05 and power (1–β) = 0.80. Assuming a large effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.75), consistent with previously published Myoton studies reporting marked between-group differences in skin and tissue biomechanical parameters [11–13], the required sample was determined as 32 participants per group (total n = 64).

Recruitment was conducted consecutively: all eligible children presenting during the study period were screened, and those who met inclusion criteria and whose guardians provided informed consent were enrolled until the target sample size was reached.

The presence of dehydration was defined as fulfilling at least one of the following laboratory criteria: serum osmolality ≥ 290 mOsm/kg [16–18] or urine specific gravity >1.025 [19]. Participants not meeting these criteria were assigned to the control group. A total of 64 children were included, comprising 32 cases and 32 controls.

Laboratory data requested during routine clinical evaluations on the day of presentation were retrieved from the Hospital Information Management System. The research team performed physical measurements concurrently with sample collection for laboratory analysis.

Skin biomechanical properties were evaluated using the skin probe of the MyotonPro® device. Previous studies have demonstrated high internal consistency and test-retest reliability of MyotonPro® measurements in adult populations [20]. In this study, all measurements were conducted using the same device and standardized protocol [10–13]. Three anatomical sites were measured in each participant: the midline 1.5 cm above the umbilicus, the posterior surface of the right forearm 4 cm above the wrist, and the posterior surface of the left forearm 4 cm above the wrist. Each site was measured once per participant. Three primary parameters were assessed: Oscillation Frequency (Hz): Reflects resting tone of the skin. Dynamic Stiffness (N/m): Represents resistance to external force. Elasticity (Log Decrement): Indicates the ability of the tissue to return to its original shape after deformation.

All measurements were performed under standardized conditions using the same MyotonPro® device. To minimize observer-related variability, the device was operated by three clinician-researchers, all using their right hands. Since group assignment was based on laboratory results obtained afterward, the researchers conducting the MyotonPro® assessments were blinded to the dehydration status of the participants at the time of measurement.

Participant data included age, sex, height, weight, BMI, and any comorbid conditions. Clinical assessment included physical examination findings such as the World Health Organization (WHO) dehydration scale, body temperature, pulse, respiratory rate, and age-appropriate cognitive response levels.

Laboratory data were based on tests ordered by the attending clinician at the time of presentation. Measurements were taken immediately before the collection of blood samples, and the laboratory results were later retrieved from the patient files. All data were anonymized before analysis. Laboratory parameters used in the analysis included:

Complete Blood Count (CBC): white blood cell (WBC), red blood cell (RBC), hemoglobin (Hb), hematocrit (Hct), and platelets (PLT). Biochemistry: Glucose, urea, creatinine, total protein, albumin, C-reactive protein (CRP), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), sodium (Na⁺), potassium (K⁺), calcium (Ca²⁺), and chloride (Cl⁻). Plasma Osmolality (Calculated as 2 [Na⁺] + glucose (mg/dL)/18 + BUN (mg/dL)/2.8), Urine Specific Gravity [17].

Measurements were recorded immediately before blood/urine sampling, allowing real-time comparison of biomechanical data with laboratory markers.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 24. The normality of distributions was assessed via skewness and kurtosis values; non-parametric tests were used when the assumption of normality was not met. Independent samples t-test or Mann-Whitney U test was employed for comparisons of continuous variables between groups. Categorical variables were analysed using the Chi-square test. Correlations between paired data were assessed using Spearman correlation analysis. For variables with missing data, only complete data pairs were included. Logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify predictors differentiating between groups, with results reported using coefficients, standard errors, Wald tests, and Odds Ratios. Model fit and significance were evaluated accordingly. To assess the discriminatory power of skin biomechanical properties in differentiating between cases and controls, Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed. For each parameter, the Area Under the Curve (AUC), standard error, p-value, and 95% confidence intervals were calculated. ROC curves were presented graphically.

Results

A total of 64 children were included in the study, with 32 classified as the control group and 32 as the case group based on laboratory indicators of dehydration. The groups showed similar distributions in terms of age, sex, presence of comorbidities, and cognitive development level (Table 1). According to the WHO physical examination scale, clinical signs of dehydration were observed in three children in the case group; however, this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.076).

Skin biomechanical measurements

Among the skin biomechanical parameters measured using the MyotonPro® device from three anatomical regions (midline 1.5 cm above the umbilicus, posterior surfaces of the right and left arms), statistically significant differences were observed in the right arm. Specifically, the right arm oscillation frequency averaged 20.64 ± 4.16 Hz in the control group and 18.75 ± 2.63 Hz in the case group (p = 0.034). The dynamic stiffness of the right arm was 499.78 ± 169.28 N/m in the control group versus 430.06 ± 83.60 N/m in the case group (p = 0.042). No significant differences were found between groups for oscillation frequency, dynamic stiffness, or elasticity at the umbilical or left arm regions (p > 0.05) (Table 2).

Biochemical parameters

Significant differences in certain laboratory parameters were observed between groups: Creatinine levels were higher in the case group (0.57 ± 1.02 mg/dL), with this difference reaching statistical significance (p = 0.042). Sodium levels were 137.34 ± 1.52 mmol/L in the control group and 140.25 ± 3.47 mmol/L in the case group (p < 0.001). Plasma osmolality was found to be 283.67 ± 2.50 mOsm in the control group and 291.65 ± 6.85 mOsm in the case group (p < 0.001). Urine specific gravity was 1.013 ± 0.006 in the control group and 1.021 ± 0.010 in the case group (p = 0.001) (Table 2).

In the control group, significant correlations were observed between some skin biomechanical parameters and laboratory findings. Urea and BUN levels showed strong positive correlations with umbilical region oscillation frequency (r = 0.535 and 0.528) and dynamic stiffness (r = 0.637 and 0.634) (p < 0.01). Albumin levels correlated negatively with umbilical oscillation frequency (r = − 0.529, p < 0.01) and positively with right arm oscillation frequency (r = 0.522, p < 0.01). Body temperature correlated with right arm elasticity (r = 0.398, p < 0.05) and inversely with left arm dynamic stiffness (r = − 0.390, p < 0.05).

In the case group, significant correlations were also observed: Creatinine levels were positively correlated with oscillation frequency (r = 0.526) and dynamic stiffness (r = 0.454) of the left arm (p < 0.01). Glucose levels were significantly correlated with left arm elasticity (r = 0.437, p < 0.05). AST levels were positively correlated with right arm oscillation frequency (r = 0.364) and dynamic stiffness (r = 0.452).

Logistic regression and ROC analysis

Logistic regression analysis showed that oscillation frequency, dynamic stiffness, and elasticity values from the umbilicus and arm regions did not significantly differentiate between case (1) and control (0) groups (all p > 0.05) (Table 3). ROC analysis confirmed this, with the Area Under the Curve (AUC) values ranging between 0.362 and 0.514, indicating no significant discriminative ability (Table 4; Fig. 1).

Discussion

This study demonstrated that oscillation frequency and dynamic stiffness in the right arm were significantly lower in dehydrated children aged 6 months to 6 years (d ≈ 0.5; p < 0.05). These findings provide preliminary evidence that skin biomechanics in the pediatric population may be sensitive to hydration status. While the observed differences suggest a potential supportive role of myotonometry, the limited discriminative ability in logistic regression and ROC analyses indicates that these results should be interpreted cautiously and warrant further validation before clinical application.

Acute dehydration loosens the dermal viscoelastic matrix by reducing interstitial fluid and decreasing the tension in collagen-elastin fibers [4, 7, 8]. In this study, this relaxation was reflected as numerical reductions in parameters measured by the MyotonPro - specifically, oscillation frequency, which reflects resting skin tone, and dynamic stiffness, which indicates resistance to external forces - in the right arm of dehydrated children. These changes align with the known effects of fluid loss on the extracellular matrix and dermal elasticity and support the idea that skin biomechanics are responsive to systemic fluid balance.

The lack of significant differences in the umbilical and left arm regions may be attributed to anatomical factors, such as the thickness of subcutaneous fat in the umbilical region, and technical factors affecting measurement sensitivity. The discrepancy between right and left arms could be influenced by the right hand used by the investigator, suggesting that the choice of anatomical site and measurement side based on operator handedness is a critical methodological consideration. Although statistical significance was observed only in the right arm, similar (but non-significant) trends were noted in the left arm as well. This suggests that the sensitivity of MyotonPro to dermal fluid loss may be bilateral; however, statistical significance might not have been achieved on the left side due to the influence of measurement variances.

Aird et al. reported significant increases in quadriceps tone and stiffness with aging in men over 65, while our findings showed a significant decrease in stiffness and frequency in the right arm due to acute fluid loss. This contrast may reflect the opposing effects of aging (which increases pre-tension in the collagen–elastin matrix) and dehydration (which reduces it) [7, 20].

Eda et al. (2023), using the Cutometer-Dual MPA 580 rather than the MyotonPro in an exercise-induced acute fluid loss model, found a marked reduction in skin elasticity on the dorsum of the hand. This reduction was strongly correlated with biochemical hydration markers [8]. In contrast, our pediatric study identified significant reductions in oscillation frequency and dynamic stiffness using the MyotonPro, but not in elasticity. This discrepancy suggests that different devices (Cutometer vs. MyotonPro) may target distinct biomechanical components. The effects of hydration on tissue vary in detectability depending on the measurement technique and the selected anatomical region. Hence, the choice of biomechanical device and measurement site must be made carefully in both clinical and research settings.

Significant increases in sodium, creatinine, plasma osmolality, and urine specific gravity observed in this study are consistent with biochemical indicators of dehydration reported in the literature [1, 6, 16, 17]. Particularly, plasma osmolality ≥ 290 mOsm/kg has been described as an early warning sign of clinical deterioration in pediatric intensive care cohorts [16], underscoring the physiological impact of hydration status reflected in our findings. The fact that only three patients in the case group were clinically identified as dehydrated using the WHO physical examination scale suggests that this tool may have limited sensitivity.

Correlation analyses revealed positive associations between creatinine and both oscillation frequency and dynamic stiffness in the left arm, and a negative correlation between albumin and umbilical oscillation frequency. These findings suggest that viscoelastic parameters measured by MyotonPro® may be sensitive to both renovascular [21] and oncotic pressure [22] - related fluid dynamics. These findings also highlight the need for careful selection of anatomical regions when interpreting biomechanical measurements.

Strengths

A major strength of this study is the synchronization of skin measurements with laboratory sampling, allowing for direct comparison of biomechanical data with biochemical markers and eliminating timing bias. The use of the skin probe ensured reliable surface-level measurements, and consistent use of the same device and protocol minimized interobserver variability.

Limitations

Limitations include the single-center design, relatively small sample size, and the fact that measurements were only conducted using the right hand. The one-time nature of the measurements precluded repeatability analysis. Variability in subcutaneous fat thickness, tissue structure, and anatomical characteristics at the umbilicus and arm regions may have influenced the results. The limited choice of measurement sites constrains generalization to the entire body. Another limitation was the lack of direct comparison with other non-invasive hydration assessment methods such as bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) or bioelectrical impedance vector analysis (BIVA), which could have provided a broader context for interpreting the discriminative ability of MyotonPro®. Although regression and ROC analyses were conducted, the discriminative ability of MyotonPro® parameters was limited, and thus no statistically reliable cut-off values could be derived. To date, no validated cut-off values exist for hydration assessment in children using MyotonPro®. This novelty is a strength of the study, but also a limitation that warrants further validation studies.

Conclusion

This study is among the first to examine the use of a practical, non-invasive biomechanical device for evaluating pediatric dehydration. MyotonPro measurements were feasible, rapid, painless, and sensitive to hydration-related changes in the selected parameters. Yet, because the discriminatory power was limited in this study, it cannot be accepted as an independent diagnostic tool at this stage. For now, the MyotonPro® device should only be used as an adjunct to laboratory tests and clinical examinations. Nevertheless, the feasibility and sensitivity observed in this study highlight its promise and justify further evaluation in broader clinical contexts.
