Reversible Pebble Transducers
Luc Dartois, Paul Gastin, Loïc Germerie Guizouarn, Shankaranarayanan Krishna

Abstract
Deterministic two-way transducers with pebbles (aka pebble transducers) capture the class of polyregular functions, which extend the string-to-string regular functions allowing polynomial growth instead of linear growth. One of the most fundamental operations on functions is composition, and (poly)regular functions can be realized as a composition of several simpler functions. In general, composition of deterministic two-way transducers incur a doubly exponential blow-up in the size of the inputs. A major improvement in this direction comes from the fundamental result of Dartois et al. [ 10 ] showing a polynomial construction for the composition of reversible two-way transducers. A precise complexity analysis for existing composition techniques of pebble transducers is missing. But they rely on the classic composition of two-way transducers and inherit the double exponential complexity. To overcome this problem, we introduce reversible pebble transducers. Our main results are efficient uniformization techniques for non-deterministic pebble transducers to reversible ones and efficient composition for reversible pebble transducers.

Introduction

The theory of string transformations has garnered a lot of attention in recent years. The simplest kind of such transformations are
sequential functions
which are captured
by deterministic finite automata whose transitions are labelled by output words. An example of a sequential function is
f(w)=w^{\prime}
where
w,w^{\prime}
are words over an alphabet
\{a,b\}
, and

w^{\prime}
is obtained from
w
by replacing in
w
, each
a
with
bb
, and each
b
with
a
.

Rational functions
strictly extend sequential functions by allowing the underlying
automaton to be
unambiguous
instead of being deterministic. For instance,

f(ua)=au
where
a\in\Sigma,u\in\Sigma^{+}
which moves the last symbol of
the input to the first position is a rational function, and not
realizable by a sequential one.

While both sequential and rational functions are captured by ‘one-way’ transducers,

regular functions
are realized by two-way transducers where the input is scanned in both directions. Regular functions strictly extend rational functions; for instance
the regular function
f(w)=w^{R}
where
w^{R}
is the reverse of
w
is not realizable as a rational function. Regular functions are also equivalent to Courcelle’s word-to-word MSO transductions
[
9
,
13
]
,
streaming string transducers
[
1
]
as well as combinator expressions
[
2
,
3
,
6
,
11
]
.

Sequential, rational as well as regular functions are all transformations of
linear growth
, where the sizes of the outputs are linear in the sizes of the inputs.
More recently,
[
5
]
revisits functions of
polynomial growth
, that is, those whose output sizes are polynomial in the sizes of the input.
These functions, aptly called
polyregular functions
, date back to
[
12
,
15
]
.
A logical characterization for polyregular functions was given in
[
7
]
, namely, word-to-word MSO
interpretations.
MSO interpretations are equivalent to polyregular functions which are word-to-word functions recognised by deterministic, two-way pebble transducers
[
4
]
.

Prior work
[
12
,
4
]
on pebble transducers show that they are closed under composition, and are equivalent
to polyregular functions.
[
12
]

considers ‘basic’ pebble transducers where one can check the presence or absence of a certain pebble at the head position, along with the actions of dropping and lifting pebbles following a stack discipline. The main result of
[
12
]
is the closure under composition
of pebble transducers, as well as a
uniformization
of (non-determinsitic) pebble
to deterministic ones with the same number of pebbles.
The pebble transducers of
[
4
]
allow
comparison
tests
, namely, one can compare the positions on which pebbles are dropped, or the
position of the head and of a pebble.

[
4
]
gives high level ideas
for the closure under composition for pebble transducers with comparison tests.

Our focus in this paper is on the complexity aspects of the composition and uniformization
of pebble transducers.
A practical setting where composition is useful is while synthesizing pebble transducers
from polyregular functions
[
7
]
.
Polyregular functions
[
5
]

are defined as the smallest class of functions closed under composition that contains
sequential functions, the squaring function as well as the iterated reverse.
In general, the composition of (pebble-less) two-way transducers has at least a doubly
exponential blowup in the size of the input transducers
[
8
]
. An important class
of two-way transducers for which composition is polynomial are
reversible

transducers
[
10
]
.
Reversible transducers are those which are both deterministic and reverse-deterministic;
moreover they are expressively equivalent to two-way transducers
[
10
]
. This
makes reversibility a very attractive property for a transducer. However, the notion of
reversible pebble transducers has not been considered yet. Therefore it is unknown if
they have the same expressiveness as deterministic pebble transducers (polyregular
functions), and whether they are also amenable to an efficient composition like their
pebble-less counterparts. Our paper fills this gap.

Our Contributions.

1.

Reversible Pebble Transducers
. We define reversible pebble transducers
with equality tests allowing to check whether two pebbles are dropped on the same
position, in addition to the basic tests allowing to check if a pebble is dropped on the
current head position.

2.

Equivalent notions for pebble transducers
.
We show that pebble transducers with basic tests only, as well as those extended with
equality tests are equivalent.
More precisely, given a
k
-pebble transducer with equality tests having
n
states, we
show that we can construct an equivalent
k
-pebble transducer with basic tests having

\mathcal{O}(nk^{2})
states. The construction preserves both determinism and reverse-determinism.

3.

Composition of Reversible Pebble Transducers
. We show that reversible
pebble transducers are closed under composition. Given points 1,2 above, we allow
equality tests while studying composition. To be precise, given reversible pebble
transducers
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
with states
Q
,
Q^{\prime}
, having
n,m\geq 0
pebbles, we
construct a reversible pebble transducer
\mathcal{T}^{\prime\prime}
with
nm+n+m

pebbles and (i)
2\cdot|Q|\cdot|Q^{\prime}|
states when
m=0
, and (ii)

\mathcal{O}(|Q|^{m+2}\cdot|Q^{\prime}|\cdot(n+1)^{m+3})
states when
m>0
.

To compare with the existing composition approaches
[
12
]
and

[
4
]
, the number of pebbles we require for composition matches with
[
12
]
who showed that this is the optimal number of pebbles needed. Regarding the number of states,
[
12
]
relies on the composition of deterministic two-way transducers, which, as mentioned before, incurs at least a doubly exponential blowup. As can be seen from (ii) above, reversibility gives a much better complexity.
The high level description for composition in
[
4
]

does not give details on the number of states, and incurs
n
extra pebbles. As such,
we do not compare with them.

4.

Uniformization of non-deterministic Reversible Pebble Transducers
.
Given a non-deterministic pebble transducer
\mathcal{T}
with
k
pebbles and
n
states, we
construct a reversible pebble transducer
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
with
2^{\mathcal{O}((kn)^{2})}
states which
uniformizes the relation
[\![\mathcal{T}]\!]
computed by
\mathcal{T}
, i.e., computes a function

[\![\mathcal{T}^{\prime}]\!]\subseteq[\![\mathcal{T}]\!]
with same domain.

The uniformization
[
12
]
of non-deterministic to
deterministic pebble transducers is done using results from

[
14
]
, where uniformization is done via a sequence of
tranformations, namely: (
i
) transductions computed by a 0-pebble transducer are
definable using non-determinsitic MSO transductions, which can be uniformized using
deterministic MSO transductions, (
ii
) deterministic MSO transductions are
computed by deterministic two-way transducers. The uniformization for a given

k
-pebble transducer
[
12
]
is then computed as a
composition of a
k
-counting transduction and the deterministic two-way transducer
obtained in (
ii
). Due to (
ii
), this may incur a non-elementary
complexity, while our uniformization technique only has an exponential complexity.

5.

Polyregular functions as Reversible Pebble Transducers
.
Polyregular functions can be realized as (a) deterministic two-way pebble transducers

[
4
]
or, (b) as a composition of sequential
functions, squaring and iterated reverse functions
[
5
]
.
This results in two possibilities to compute a reversible pebble transducer for a
polyregular function.

a.
If the polyregular function is presented as a deterministic
two-way pebble transducers then we may use the uniformization construction of

\cref
item:uniformize to obtain an equivalent reversible pebble transducer.

b.

If a polyregular function
f
is given as a modular expression involving
sequential, squaring and iterated reverse functions, then one can directly synthesize a
reversible pebble transducer computing
f
using (
i
) the reversible transducers
for squaring and iterated reverse given in
\cref
fig:rev-squaring-v4,fig:iterRev
(
ii
) reversible transducers for the sequential functions obtained using

[
10
, Theorem 2]
, and (
iii
) our composition of the resultant
reversible pebble transducers. Note that the reversible machines in

\cref
fig:rev-squaring-v4,fig:iterRev are small with 6/5 states and 1/0 pebbles
respectively.
Once again, this is better than constructing first from the modular expression a
deterministic pebble transducer for function
f
and then uniformizing it to get a
reversible one.

2
Reversible Pebble Transducers

Pebble Transducers with Equality Tests.

In this section, we consider pebble transducers enhanced with equality
tests between pebble positions, in addition to the classical
query checking only if a pebble is present/absent at the head position.
The equality tests are inspired from
[
5
]
,
where
\leq
-comparisons between any two pebble positions, as well as between the head and
any pebble are allowed. Enhancing the basic tests, on whether a pebble is present/absent
at the head, by allowing to check for any pair of pebbles whether or not they are at the
same position is useful, especially when composing two pebble transducers.

A
k
-pebble automaton with equality tests (
k
-
\mathsf{PA}_{=}
) is given by a
tuple
\mathcal{A}=(Q,\Sigma,\delta,k,q_{i},q_{f})
where
Q
is a finite set of states,

\Sigma
is the finite input alphabet,

\delta
is the transition relation (see below),
and
q_{i},q_{f}\in Q
respectively are initial and final states.

Given an input word
u\in\Sigma^{*}
, we define
positions

\mathsf{pos}(u)=\{1,\ldots,|u|\}
. We visualise the input of a PA as a circular word
\#u
where

\#\notin\Sigma
is an endmarker reached when moving right (resp. left) from the last
(resp. first) position of
u
.
Keeping this in mind, we work with an
extended
set of positions

\mathsf{epos}(u)=\{0,1,\ldots,|u|\}
. For instance,

\begin{array}[]{ccccc}\#&a&b&c&d\\
0&1&2&3&4\end{array}
.

The head of the automaton during a run over
u
, occupies a position from
\mathsf{epos}(u)
,
denoted
\mathsf{h}
.
The states
Q
of the pebble automaton are partitioned into 3 disjoint sets,
Q_{+1}\uplus Q_{0}\uplus Q_{-1}
signifying movement of the head to the right, no movement of the head
and movement of the head to the left, respectively.
Given
\mathsf{h}\in\mathsf{epos}(u)
and a state
q\in Q
, we define

\mathsf{h}+q=\mathsf{h}
if
q\in Q_{0}
;

\mathsf{h}+q=(\mathsf{h}+1\mod(|u|+1))
if
q\in Q_{+1}
; and

\mathsf{h}+q=(\mathsf{h}-1\mod(|u|+1))
if
q\in Q_{-1}
.
The intuition is that if the head moves to the right of the last position of the word,
then it reads
\#
, and if the head moves to the left from
\#
, it reads the last
position of
u
.

Likewise, the pebbles are dropped on
\mathsf{epos}(u)
and follow a stack policy.
We denote by
\mathsf{peb}\in\mathsf{epos}(u)^{\leq k}
the stack of positions where
pebbles are dropped.

A configuration of a
k
-pebble automaton on the input word
u
is a tuple

C=(q,\mathsf{peb},\mathsf{h})\in Q\times\mathsf{epos}(u)^{\leq k}\times\mathsf{epos}(u)
.
In this configuration, the head of the automaton reads position
\mathsf{h}
, there are

|\mathsf{peb}|
pebbles dropped and the
i
-th pebble (
1\leq i\leq|\mathsf{peb}|
) is
dropped on position
\mathsf{peb}_{i}\in\mathsf{epos}(u)
.
The initial configuration is
(q_{i},\varepsilon,0)
and the accepting (final)
configuration is
(q_{f},\varepsilon,0)
. Hence, in order to accept, the automaton should
lift all pebbles and move back to the endmarker.
Wlog, we assume that
q_{i},q_{f}\in Q_{0}
with
q_{i}\neq q_{f}
, and there are no
transitions starting from
q_{f}
or going to
q_{i}
.

Transitions are of the form
t=(q,a,\varphi,\mathsf{op},q^{\prime})
with states
q,q^{\prime}\in Q
, input letter

a\in\Sigma_{\#}=\Sigma\cup\{\#\}
, test
\varphi
which is a conjunction of atoms of the form

(\mathsf{h}=\mathsf{p}_{i})
,
\neg(\mathsf{h}=\mathsf{p}_{i})
,
(\mathsf{p}_{i}=\mathsf{p}_{j})
or

\neg(\mathsf{p}_{i}=\mathsf{p}_{j})
(with
1\leq i,j\leq k
),
and operation
\mathsf{op}
of the form
1
1

1

We chose to
specify with index
i
of
\mathsf{drop}
and
\mathsf{lift}
which pebble is dropped or lifted.
This allows to get determinism or reverse-determinism without adding extra tests to
transitions. We could have simply used
\mathsf{drop}
and
\mathsf{lift}
at the expense of adding
tests to transitions to ensure (reverse-) determinism.

\mathsf{drop}_{i}
,
\mathsf{lift}_{i}
or
\mathsf{nop}
(with
1\leq i\leq k
).
Transition
t
is
enabled
at configuration
C=(q,\mathsf{peb},\mathsf{h})
(denoted

C\models t
) if:

1.

the letter read is
a
, i.e.,
a=(\#u)_{\mathsf{h}}

2.

\mathsf{peb},\mathsf{h}\models\varphi
: the test succeeds, where

•

\mathsf{peb},\mathsf{h}\models(\mathsf{h}=\mathsf{p}_{i})
if
1\leq i\leq|\mathsf{peb}|
and
\mathsf{peb}_{i}=\mathsf{h}
,

•

\mathsf{peb},\mathsf{h}\models(\mathsf{p}_{i}=\mathsf{p}_{j})
if
1\leq i,j\leq|\mathsf{peb}|
and

\mathsf{peb}_{i}=\mathsf{peb}_{j}
,

3.

\mathsf{peb},\mathsf{h}\models\mathsf{op}
: the operation can be executed where

•

\mathsf{peb},\mathsf{h}\models\mathsf{nop}
is always true,
and we let
\mathsf{nop}(\mathsf{peb},\mathsf{h})=\mathsf{peb}
,

•

\mathsf{peb},\mathsf{h}\models\mathsf{drop}_{i}
if
|\mathsf{peb}|=i-1
,
and we let
\mathsf{drop}_{i}(\mathsf{peb},\mathsf{h})=\mathsf{peb}\cdot\mathsf{h}
,

•

\mathsf{peb},\mathsf{h}\models\mathsf{lift}_{i}
if
|\mathsf{peb}|=i
and
\mathsf{h}=\mathsf{peb}_{i}
,
and we let
\mathsf{lift}_{i}(\mathsf{peb},\mathsf{h})=\mathsf{peb}_{1}\cdots\mathsf{peb}_{i-1}
.

When
C\models t
we have
C\xrightarrow{t}C^{\prime}=(q^{\prime},\mathsf{peb}^{\prime},\mathsf{h}^{\prime})
where

\mathsf{peb}^{\prime}=\mathsf{op}(\mathsf{peb},\mathsf{h})
and
\mathsf{h}^{\prime}=\mathsf{h}+q^{\prime}
.

Notice that the top pebble may be lifted from the pebble stack only when it is on the
position being read.
We often simply write
\mathsf{p}_{i}
(resp.
\neg\mathsf{p}_{i}
) for
the atomic test
(\mathsf{h}=\mathsf{p}_{i})
(resp.
\neg(\mathsf{h}=\mathsf{p}_{i})
).
Note that, if a transition
t=(q,a,\mathsf{p}_{i},\mathsf{drop}_{j},q^{\prime})

is enabled at some configuration
C
, then
i<j
and

C^{\prime}\models(\mathsf{p}_{i}=\mathsf{p}_{j})
where
C\xrightarrow{t}C^{\prime}
.

An equality test
\mathsf{p}_{i}=\mathsf{p}_{i}
allows to check if the size of the pebble stack
is at least
i
. This is not possible if we only use atoms of the form
\mathsf{p}_{i}
or

\neg\mathsf{p}_{i}
(which is not an expressivity problem since one may always store the
size of the pebble stack in the state of the automaton).

A run of
\mathcal{A}
is a sequence
C_{0}\xrightarrow{t_{1}}C_{1}\cdots\xrightarrow{t_{n}}C_{n}

where
C_{\ell}
are configurations on the input word
u
and
t_{\ell}\in\delta
are
transitions of
\mathcal{A}
. The run is initial if
C_{0}=(q_{i},\varepsilon,0)
and final if

C_{n}=(q_{f},\varepsilon,0)
. It is accepting if it is both initial and final.

The automaton is
deterministic
if for all pairs of transitions

t=(q,a,\varphi,\mathsf{op},r)
and
t^{\prime}=(q,a,\varphi^{\prime},\mathsf{op}^{\prime},r^{\prime})
, if
t
and
t^{\prime}
may be
simultaneously enabled (at some configuration
C
) then

t=t^{\prime}
.
The fact that an operation
\mathsf{op}
is enabled may be written as a test
\overline{\mathsf{op}}
:

\overline{\mathsf{nop}}=\mathsf{true}
,

\overline{\mathsf{drop}_{i}}=(\mathsf{p}_{i-1}=\mathsf{p}_{i-1})\wedge\neg(\mathsf{p}_{i}=\mathsf{p}_{i})
(with

\mathsf{p}_{0}=\mathsf{p}_{0}
identified with
\mathsf{true}
), and

\overline{\mathsf{lift}_{i}}=(\mathsf{h}=\mathsf{p}_{i})\wedge\neg(\mathsf{p}_{i+1}=\mathsf{p}_{i+1})
.
Then, the transitions
t
and
t^{\prime}
may be simultaneously enabled if and only if the test

\varphi\wedge\overline{\mathsf{op}}\wedge\varphi^{\prime}\wedge\overline{\mathsf{op}^{\prime}}
is satisfiable.
This gives a more syntactic definition of determinism.
In particular, a transtion
t
with operation
\mathsf{lift}_{i}
and a transition
t^{\prime}
with
atomic test
\neg\mathsf{p}_{i}
cannot be simultaneously enabled.

We define the reverse
\mathsf{op}^{r}
of an operation
\mathsf{op}
by

\mathsf{nop}^{r}=\mathsf{nop}
,
\mathsf{drop}_{i}^{r}=\mathsf{lift}_{i}
and
\mathsf{lift}_{i}^{r}=\mathsf{drop}_{i}
.
Notice that if
\mathsf{peb},\mathsf{h}\models\mathsf{op}
then

\mathsf{op}(\mathsf{peb},\mathsf{h}),\mathsf{h}\models\mathsf{op}^{r}
and

\mathsf{peb}=\mathsf{op}^{r}(\mathsf{op}(\mathsf{peb},\mathsf{h}),\mathsf{h})
.

A transition
t=(q,a,\varphi,\mathsf{op},q^{\prime})
is
reverse-enabled
at configuration

C^{\prime}=(q^{\prime},\mathsf{peb}^{\prime},\mathsf{h}^{\prime})
(denoted
C^{\prime}\models_{\mathsf{rev}}t
) if there is a configuration

C=(q,\mathsf{peb},\mathsf{h})
enabling
t
such that
C\xrightarrow{t}C^{\prime}
.
More explicitly, the only possibility for configuration
C
is given by

\mathsf{h}=\mathsf{h}^{\prime}-q^{\prime}
(corresponding to
\mathsf{h}^{\prime}=\mathsf{h}+q^{\prime}
), and

\mathsf{peb}=\mathsf{op}^{r}(\mathsf{peb}^{\prime},\mathsf{h})
(corresponding to
\mathsf{peb}^{\prime}=\mathsf{op}(\mathsf{peb},\mathsf{h})
).
Then,
C^{\prime}\models_{\mathsf{rev}}t

iff
\mathsf{peb}^{\prime},\mathsf{h}^{\prime}-q^{\prime}\models\mathsf{op}^{r}

and
\mathsf{op}^{r}(\mathsf{peb}^{\prime},\mathsf{h}^{\prime}-q^{\prime}),\mathsf{h}^{\prime}-q^{\prime}\models\varphi
.
In this case, we write
C=t^{-1}(C^{\prime})=(q,\mathsf{op}^{r}(\mathsf{peb}^{\prime},\mathsf{h}^{\prime}-q^{\prime}),\mathsf{h}^{\prime}-q^{\prime})
.
In particular, a transtion
t
with operation
\mathsf{drop}_{i}
and a transition
t^{\prime}
with atomic
test
\neg\mathsf{p}_{i}
and operation
\mathsf{nop}
cannot be simultaneously reverse-enabled.

The automaton is
reverse-deterministic
if for all pairs of transitions

t=(q,a,\varphi,\mathsf{op},r)
and
t^{\prime}=(q^{\prime},a,\varphi^{\prime},\mathsf{op}^{\prime},r)
, if
t
and
t^{\prime}
may be
simultaneously reverse-enabled (at some configuration
C^{\prime}
) then
t=t^{\prime}
.

The pebble automaton is
reversible
if it is both
deterministic
and

reverse-deterministic
.

A
k
-pebble transducer with equality tests (
k
-
\mathsf{PT}_{=}
)

\mathcal{T}=(Q,\Sigma,\delta,k,q_{i},q_{f},\Gamma,\mu)
is a
k
-pebble automaton
(
k
-
\mathsf{PA}_{=}
)

\mathcal{A}=(Q,\Sigma,\delta,k,q_{i},q_{f})
extended with an output alphabet
\Gamma
and an
output function
\mu
mapping each transition of
\mathcal{A}
to a word in
\Gamma^{*}
.
The semantics of
\mathcal{T}
is a relation

[\![\mathcal{T}]\!]\subseteq\Sigma^{*}\times\Gamma^{*}
consisting of all pairs
(u,v)

such that there is an accepting run

C_{0}\xrightarrow{t_{1}}C_{1}\cdots\xrightarrow{t_{n}}C_{n}
on
\#u
with

v=\mu(t_{1})\cdots\mu(t_{n})
.

A pebble transducer is deterministic (reverse-deterministic, reversible) whenever the
underlying pebble automaton is. The semantics of a deterministic (reverse-deterministic,
reversible) pebble transducer is a partial function.

Remark 1
.

Even though the semantics of 0-pebble transducers is different to that of two-way
transducers as defined in
[
10
]
, the two models are equivalent.
Given a transducer with
n
states using one semantics,
one can build a transducer realising the same relation using the other semantics,
with
\mathcal{O}(n)
states.
Moreover, determinism and reverse-determinism are preserved by this translation (see
\cref

A (basic) pebble automaton (

-
\mathsf{PA}
) or transducer (

-
\mathsf{PT}
) is one in
which equality tests of the form
(\mathsf{p}_{i}=\mathsf{p}_{j})
or

\overline{b}=(b_{1},\dots,b_{k})\in\{0,1\}^{k}
to denote a full test

a conjunction over all

of atoms

\mathsf{p}_{i}
if

if

.

3
Simulating equality tests of pebbles

In this section, we show the equivalence between a basic
k
pebble transducer
(
k
-
\mathsf{PT}
) and a
k
pebble transducer allowing equality tests
(
k
-
\mathsf{PT}_{=}
).
The precise statement is given by
\cref
thm:EqualityTests, followed by
a proof sketch, the full proof can be found in
\cref
app:equiv.

Theorem 4
.

Given a
k
-pebble transducer with equality tests
\mathcal{A}
having
n
states, one
can construct a basic
k
-pebble transducer
\mathcal{B}
with
n2^{k^{2}}
states such that

[\![\mathcal{A}]\!]=[\![\mathcal{B}]\!]
.

Moreover, if
\mathcal{A}
is deterministic (reverse-deterministic, reversible) then so
is
\mathcal{B}
.

Sketch of proof.

The main idea while simulating
\mathcal{A}
is to keep track of which pairs of pebbles
are on the same position in a coherent manner: that is, if
p_{i}=p_{j}
and
p_{j}=p_{\ell}

then we also have
p_{i}=p_{\ell}
. While constructing
\mathcal{B}
, we store the equalities of
the positions of the
k
pebbles as a
k\times k
boolean matrix
M
where

M_{i,i}=1
represents that the
i^{th}
pebble has been dropped, and
M_{i,j}=1
if the

i^{th}
and
j^{th}
pebbles are on the same position. Then if we denote by
Q
the
set of states of
\mathcal{A}
, the set of states of
\mathcal{B}
is
Q^{\prime}=Q\times\{0,1\}^{k^{2}}
.

We now explain how the information is used and updated.
Initially, no pebble is dropped, and the initial state of
\mathcal{B}
is
(q_{i},\overline{0})
where
\overline{0}
denotes the
k\times k
zero matrix and
q_{i}
is
the initial state of
\mathcal{A}
. Similarly, a run is accepting only when all pebbles
are lifted. Thus the final state of
\mathcal{B}
is
(q_{f},\overline{0})
where
q_{f}
is
the final state of
\mathcal{A}
.
Recall that basic pebble transducers can see whether a given pebble
is at the same position as the reading head, thus transitions of
\mathcal{A}
involving
a guard of the form
\mathsf{h}=\mathsf{p}_{i}
or
\neg(\mathsf{h}=\mathsf{p}_{i})
can be kept in

\mathcal{B}
. Tests of the form
\mathsf{p}_{i}=\mathsf{p}_{j}
are replaced by
\mathsf{true}
if

M_{i,j}=1
and by
\mathsf{false}
otherwise.
On transitions where there is no pebble lifting or dropping, the matrix
M
does not
need to be updated.
The
i^{th}
pebble
\mathsf{p}_{i}
can be dropped only if the
(i-1)^{th}

pebble is already dropped (that is,
M_{i-1,i-1}=1
) and the
i^{th}
one is not
(
M_{i,i}=0
). On dropping
\mathsf{p}_{i}
, the matrix
M
is updated by setting all
coefficients
M_{i,j}
and
M_{j,i}
to
1
if
j=i
or
(j<i
and
\mathsf{h}=p_{j})
, and
0

otherwise. Note
that to do this, each transition of
\mathcal{A}
with a
\mathsf{drop}_{i}
action is duplicated
into
2^{i-1}
disjoint transitions testing which subsets of
\mathsf{p}_{1},\dots,\mathsf{p}_{i-1}
are present at the head. Only one transition can then be fired,
depending on the bit vector generated by the pebbles at the current position.
Similarly, the
i^{th}
pebble
\mathsf{p}_{i}
can be lifted if and only if

\mathsf{h}=\mathsf{p}_{i}
and no larger pebbles are dropped
(i=k
or

M_{i+1,i+1}=0)
. On lifting
\mathsf{p}_{i}
, the matrix
M
is updated by setting
all coefficients of the
i^{th}
row and column to
0
. However to ensure the
preservation of reverse-determinism, each
\mathsf{lift}_{i}
transition is duplicated into

2^{i-1}
transitions that have complete and disjoint tests for the current bit vector
over pebbles
\mathsf{p}_{1},\dots,\mathsf{p}_{i-1}
.

Finally, suppose that
\mathcal{A}
is reversible. The only extra transitions added to

\mathcal{B}
are when a pebble is dropped or lifted. However, all the duplicate transitions
that have been added in
\mathcal{B}
have disjoint tests (enforced by disjoint bit vectors).
Thus
\mathcal{B}
is deterministic since
\mathcal{A}
is. On the other hand, consider we
want to reverse a
\mathsf{lift}_{i}
transition in
\mathcal{A}
. This means that the
i^{th}

pebble was at the previous position of the reading head, and further, there is only one
bit vector (enriching the
i
th row and column of
M
) which was valid at the previous
step. The correct predecessor matrix is then the only one where the
i^{th}
row and
column is coherent with the bit vector of the previous position.
∎

4
Composition of pebble transducers

The goal of this section is to give an efficient (in the size of the resulting transducer)
construction for the composition of pebble transducers. Since the construction is rather
involved, we start with two simpler cases.
The first one described in
\cref
sec:reversing shows how to compose a reversible

n
-pebble transducer with the reverse function

\mathsf{rev}\colon\Gamma^{*}\to\Gamma^{*}
which takes a string of letters and outputs
the sequence of letters in reverse order. For instance,
\mathsf{rev}(abac)=caba
.
The second simpler case is the composition of a reversible
n
-pebble transducer with a
deterministic
0
-pebble transducer. This case is addressed in

\cref
sec:simple-case. Finally, the general case is solved in

\cref
sec:general-case.

4.1
Reversing the output of a reversible pebble transducer

Let
\mathcal{T}=(Q,\Sigma,\delta,n,q_{i},q_{f},\Gamma,\mu)
be a reversible
n
-pebble
transducer, possibly with equality tests.
The goal is to construct a reversible transducer

\mathcal{T}^{r}=(Q^{\prime},\Sigma,\delta^{\prime},n,q^{\prime}_{i},q^{\prime}_{f},\Gamma,\mu^{\prime})
which outputs the reverse
of the string produced by
\mathcal{T}
:
\mathsf{dom}(\mathcal{T}^{r})=\mathsf{dom}(\mathcal{T})
and for all

u\in\mathsf{dom}(\mathcal{T})
, the string
[\![\mathcal{T}^{r}]\!](u)
is the reverse of the string

[\![\mathcal{T}]\!](u)
.

The set of states of
\mathcal{T}^{r}
is
Q^{\prime}=Q
but the polarity of the states is
reversed:
Q^{\prime}_{+1}=Q_{-1}
,
Q^{\prime}_{0}=Q_{0}
and
Q^{\prime}_{-1}=Q_{+1}
. The initial and final
states are exchanged:
q^{\prime}_{i}=q_{f}
and
q^{\prime}_{f}=q_{i}
.
The transitions are also reversed in the following way.
Let
t=(q,a,\varphi,\mathsf{op},q^{\prime})
be a transition of
\mathcal{T}
.
Then,
t^{r}=(q^{\prime},a,(\varphi,\mathsf{op})^{r},q)
is a transition of
\mathcal{T}^{r}
with

\mu^{\prime}(t^{r})=\mu(t)
. We define

(\varphi,\mathsf{op})^{r}=\mathsf{op}(\varphi),\mathsf{op}^{r}
: the operation is simply

\mathsf{op}^{r}
but the test
\mathsf{op}(\varphi)
depends on both
\varphi
and
\mathsf{op}
.
It remains to define
\mathsf{op}(\varphi)
. We want that

\mathsf{peb},\mathsf{h}\models\varphi
iff
\mathsf{op}(\mathsf{peb},\mathsf{h}),\mathsf{h}\models\mathsf{op}(\varphi)
,
assuming that
\mathsf{op}(\mathsf{peb},\mathsf{h})
is defined.
We let
\mathsf{op}(\varphi_{1}\wedge\varphi_{2})=\mathsf{op}(\varphi_{1})\wedge\mathsf{op}(\varphi_{2})

and
\mathsf{op}(\neg\varphi)=\neg\mathsf{op}(\varphi)
.
We let
\mathsf{nop}(\varphi)=\varphi
, and the remaining cases are given below

\displaystyle\mathsf{drop}_{\ell}(\mathsf{h}=\mathsf{p}_{i})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}(\mathsf{h}=\mathsf{p}_{i})&\text{if }i<\ell\\
\mathsf{false}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\mathsf{drop}_{\ell}(\mathsf{p}_{i}=\mathsf{p}_{j})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}(\mathsf{p}_{i}=\mathsf{p}_{j})&\text{if }i,j<\ell\\
\mathsf{false}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\mathsf{lift}_{\ell}(\mathsf{h}=\mathsf{p}_{i})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}(\mathsf{h}=\mathsf{p}_{i})&\text{if }i<\ell\\
\mathsf{true}&\text{if }i=\ell\\
\mathsf{false}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\mathsf{lift}_{\ell}(\mathsf{p}_{i}=\mathsf{p}_{j})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}(\mathsf{p}_{i}=\mathsf{p}_{j})&\text{if }i,j<\ell\\
(\mathsf{h}=\mathsf{p}_{i})&\text{if }i<j=\ell\\
(\mathsf{h}=\mathsf{p}_{j})&\text{if }j<i=\ell\\
\mathsf{true}&\text{if }i=j=\ell\\
\mathsf{false}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

We can easily check that
\mathsf{peb},\mathsf{h}\models\varphi
iff

\mathsf{op}(\mathsf{peb},\mathsf{h}),\mathsf{h}\models\mathsf{op}(\varphi)
when
\mathsf{op}(\mathsf{peb},\mathsf{h})
is defined.
Recall that, when
\mathsf{peb}^{\prime}=\mathsf{op}(\mathsf{peb},\mathsf{h})
is defined,
then
\mathsf{op}^{r}(\mathsf{peb}^{\prime},\mathsf{h})
is defined and we have

\mathsf{peb}=\mathsf{op}^{r}(\mathsf{peb}^{\prime},\mathsf{h})
.
With
t=(q,a,\varphi,\mathsf{op},q^{\prime})
and
t^{r}=(q^{\prime},a,\mathsf{op}(\varphi),\mathsf{op}^{r},q)
,
we deduce that

(q,\mathsf{peb},\mathsf{h})\xrightarrow{t}(q^{\prime},\mathsf{peb}^{\prime},\mathsf{h}^{\prime})\quad\text{iff}\quad(q^{\prime},\mathsf{peb}^{\prime},\mathsf{h}^{\prime}-q^{\prime})\xrightarrow{t^{r}}(q,\mathsf{peb},\mathsf{h}-q)

(1)

We deduce that, for
u\in\Sigma^{*}
, the following sequence is a run of
\mathcal{T}
on
{\#}u

(q_{1},\mathsf{peb}^{1},\mathsf{h}_{1})\xrightarrow{t_{1}}(q_{2},\mathsf{peb}^{2},\mathsf{h}_{2})\xrightarrow{t_{2}}(q_{3},\mathsf{peb}^{3},\mathsf{h}_{3})\cdots\xrightarrow{t_{m-1}}(q_{m},\mathsf{peb}^{m},\mathsf{h}_{m})

if and only if the following sequence is a run of
\mathcal{T}^{r}
on
{\#}u

(q_{1},\mathsf{peb}^{1},\mathsf{h}_{1}-q_{1})\xleftarrow{t_{1}^{r}}(q_{2},\mathsf{peb}^{2},\mathsf{h}_{2}-q_{2})\xleftarrow{t_{2}^{r}}(q_{3},\mathsf{peb}^{3},\mathsf{h}_{3}-q_{3})\cdots\xleftarrow{t_{m-1}^{r}}(q_{m},\mathsf{peb}^{m},\mathsf{h}_{m}-q_{m})

Now, the run of
\mathcal{T}
is accepting iff

(q_{1},\mathsf{peb}^{1},\mathsf{h}_{1})=(q_{i},\varepsilon,0)
and

(q_{m},\mathsf{peb}^{m},\mathsf{h}_{m})=(q_{f},\varepsilon,0)
.
Since
q_{i},q_{f}\in Q_{0}
, we deduce that the run of
\mathcal{T}
is accepting if and only
if the corresponding run of
\mathcal{T}^{r}
is accepting, i.e.,

(q_{m},\mathsf{peb}^{m},\mathsf{h}_{m}-q_{m})=(q^{\prime}_{i},\varepsilon,0)=(q_{f},\varepsilon,0)
and

(q_{1},\mathsf{peb}^{1},\mathsf{h}_{1}-q_{1})=(q^{\prime}_{f},\varepsilon,0)=(q_{i},\varepsilon,0)
.

We deduce that
\mathsf{dom}(\mathcal{T}^{r})=\mathsf{dom}(\mathcal{T})
and, by definition of
\mu^{\prime}
, for

u\in\mathsf{dom}(\mathcal{T})
, the string
[\![\mathcal{T}^{r}]\!](u)
is the reverse of the string

[\![\mathcal{T}]\!](u)
.

To show that
\mathcal{T}^{r}
is deterministic, consider a pair of transitions

t_{1}^{r}=(q^{\prime},a,(\varphi_{1},\mathsf{op}_{1})^{r},q_{1})
and

t_{2}^{r}=(q^{\prime},a,(\varphi_{2},\mathsf{op}_{2})^{r},q_{2})
where

t_{1}=(q_{1},a,\varphi_{1},\mathsf{op}_{1},q^{\prime})
and
t_{2}=(q_{2},a,\varphi_{2},\mathsf{op}_{2},q^{\prime})
are transitions of

\mathcal{T}
. Assume that
t_{1}^{r}
is enabled at some configuration

C^{\prime}=(q^{\prime},\mathsf{peb}^{\prime},\mathsf{h}^{\prime})
. Using (
1
), we deduce that

t_{1}
is reverse-enabled at configuration
C=(q^{\prime},\mathsf{peb}^{\prime},\mathsf{h}^{\prime}+q^{\prime})
.
Similarly, if
t_{2}^{r}
is enabled at
C^{\prime}
then
t_{2}
is reverse-enabled at

C
. Since
\mathcal{T}
is reverse-deterministic, we deduce that
\mathcal{T}^{r}
is deterministic.

We can prove similarly that
\mathcal{T}^{r}
is reverse-deterministic using the fact that

\mathcal{T}
is determinisitic. In particular, we show using (
1
) that
transition
t^{r}
is reversed-enabled at some configuration
C^{\prime}=(q,\mathsf{peb},\mathsf{h})

if and only if transition
t
is enabled at configuration
C=(q,\mathsf{peb},\mathsf{h}+q)
.
We deduce that
\mathcal{T}^{r}
is reversible.

4.2
Composition: Simple case

In this subsection, we prove the following result.

Theorem 5
.

Let
\mathcal{T}=(Q,\Sigma,\delta,n,q_{i},q_{f},\Gamma,\mu)

n
-pebble

\mathcal{T}^{\prime}

Wlog, we assume that transducer
\mathcal{T}

outputs at most one letter on each transition,

\mu(t)\in\Gamma\cup\{\varepsilon\}
for each transition
t
of

\mathcal{T}

n

1<i<n

We set
\mu(t_{i})=v_{i}

Since we maintain the tests
\varphi
and
\overline{\mathsf{op}}
in the sequence of

\mathcal{T}
writes
{\#}v
with

\mathcal{T}

\displaystyle Q^{\prime\prime}_{+1}

\displaystyle=(\widehat{Q_{+1}}\times Q^{\prime}_{+1})\cup(\widehat{Q_{-1}}\times Q^{\prime}_{-1})

\displaystyle Q^{\prime\prime}_{-1}

\displaystyle=(\widehat{Q_{+1}}\times Q^{\prime}_{-1})\cup(\widehat{Q_{-1}}\times Q^{\prime}_{+1})\,.

\displaystyle t^{\prime\prime}=(\hat{q},q^{\prime})

\displaystyle t^{\prime\prime}=(\hat{s},q^{\prime})

\displaystyle\text{if }q^{\prime}\in Q^{\prime}_{-1}

Set of generators.

Uniformizing Pebble transducers.

Another way to generate reversible pebble transducers is to start from a possibly non
deterministic pebble transducer and uniformize it by a reversible pebble transducer. This
section provides a procedure to do this, while preserving the number of pebbles used by

Appendix A
Pebbleless transducers

A
0
-pebble transducer can equivalently be seen as a two-way transducer.
In
[
10
]
, the authors define reversible two-way transducers, using a different
semantics than the one used here for
0
-pebble transducers.
In the following, we call a
0
-pebble transducer one using the semantics defined in our paper.
On the other hand, a two-way transducer refers to a machine using the semantics defined in
[
10
]
.

We now define the semantics of two-way transducers.
Here, the input word is not considered as circular, and instead of the
\#
symbol,
two different endmarkers are added,
{\mathop{\vdash}}
at the begining of the word and
{\mathop{\dashv}}
at the end.
We denote with
\Sigma_{\mathop{\vdash}}
(resp.
\Sigma_{\mathop{\dashv}}
,
\Sigma_{{\mathop{\vdash}}{\mathop{\dashv}}}
)
the alphabet
\Sigma\cup\left\{{\mathop{\vdash}}\right\}

(resp.
\Sigma\cup\left\{{\mathop{\dashv}}\right\}
,
\Sigma\cup\left\{{\mathop{\vdash}},{\mathop{\dashv}}\right\}
).
The positions for the head of the transducer are in between letters, instead of being on the letters.
The word

u=\begin{array}[]{cccccc}\#&a&b&c&d&\\
0&1&2&3&4&\end{array}

from our semantics becomes

\begin{array}[]{ccccccccccccc}&{\mathop{\vdash}}&&a&&b&&c&&d&&{\mathop{\dashv}}&\\
0&&1&&2&&3&&4&&5&&6\end{array}
.
For an in between position
\mathsf{h}\in\left\{0,\ldots,|u|+1\right\}
, we denote with
\mathsf{h}+1/2
the letter
on the right of the reading head.
For example,
u(2+1/2)=b
.
Similarly, for a position
\mathsf{h}\in\left\{1,\ldots,|u|+2\right\}
,

\mathsf{h}-1/2
is the letter on the left of the reading head.
A two-way transducer is a tuple
\mathcal{T}=\left(Q,\Sigma,\delta,q_{i},q_{f},\Gamma,\mu\right)
where

Q=Q^{+}\uplus Q^{-}
is a finite set of states,
with
Q^{+}
the set of
forward
states and
Q^{-}
the set of
backward
states;

\Sigma
is the alphabet;
q_{i},q_{f}\in Q^{+}
are respectively the (only) initial and accepting states;

\delta\subseteq Q\times\Sigma_{{\mathop{\vdash}}{\mathop{\dashv}}}\times Q
is the set of transitions;

\Gamma
is the output alphabet;

\mu\colon\delta\to\Gamma^{*}
is the output function.
By convention,

Because the word is not circular, the head cannot move past an end-marker,

A configuration of a two-way transducer on an input word

composed by the current state of the transducer and
the position of the reading head, with
\mathsf{h}\in\left\{0,\ldots,|u|+2\right\}

A transition

•

q\in Q^{-},p\in Q^{-},u(\mathsf{h}-1/2)=a,\mathsf{h}^{\prime}=\mathsf{h}-1

•

q\in Q^{-},p\in Q^{+},u(\mathsf{h}-1/2)=a,\mathsf{h}^{\prime}=\mathsf{h}

The two following lemmas prove that the two semantics are effectively equivalent, and that reversibility is preserved between them.

Lemma 21
.

Given a
0

of

of

First, notice that whithin the gadgets for each state,

Lemma 22
.

Given a (reversible) two-way transducer
T
with
n
states,
one can construct an equivalent
0
-pebble (reversible) transducer
P
with
n
states.

Proof.

Let
T=(Q^{+}\cup Q^{-},\Sigma,\delta,q_{i},q_{f},\Gamma,\mu)
.
The
0
-pebble transducer
P
is defined with the same set of states and transitions as

T
, with the following minor change.

First, we need to handle the position of the reading head and the changes on how the
moves are decided. We do so by maintaining the fact that the reading head in
P
is
always on the position to be read. Given a transition
(p,a,q)
in the two-way
semantics, the state
p
decides which position to read, and the state
q
decides which
position will be read next. It is either the next one if
q\in Q^{+}
or the previous one
if
q\in Q^{-}
. Then, keeping the same polarity for the states of
T
and the ones of

P
(except for
q_{i},q_{f}
), we obtain the same runs. More precisely, the states of

P
are partioned as
S_{+1}=Q^{+}\setminus\{q_{i},q_{f}\}
,
S_{0}=\{q_{i},q_{f}\}
and

S_{-1}=Q^{-}
.

We also keep essentially the same transitions in
P
as in
T
, simply replacing

{\mathop{\vdash}}
and
{\mathop{\dashv}}
in transitions of
T
with
\#
in transitions of
P
, since

0
-pebble transducers have only one endmarker
\#
standing for both
{\mathop{\vdash}}
and

{\mathop{\dashv}}
. We need to argue that this does not impact reversibility. In fact, the only
transitions in
T
reading
{\mathop{\vdash}}
are of the form
(p,{\mathop{\vdash}},q)
where
p\in Q^{-}\cup\{q_{i}\}
and

. For
{\mathop{\dashv}}
, these transitions are
of the form
\left(p,{\mathop{\dashv}},q\right)
where
p\in Q^{+}\setminus\{q_{i}\}

and
q\in Q^{-}\cup\{q_{f}\}
. So there is no overlap between transitions in
T

reading
{\mathop{\vdash}}
and those reading
{\mathop{\dashv}}
. Hence,

can acts as both

{\mathop{\vdash}}
and
{\mathop{\dashv}}

∎

Appendix B
Simulating equality tests of pebbles

In this section, we show that one can simulate a
k
-pebble transducer
\mathcal{A}
with equality tests using a basic
k
-pebble transducer
\mathcal{B}
. Since the main difference between the two is in the tests, one where we can easily check whether two pebbles are on the same position or not, we need to simulate this aspect in the basic pebble one. Let
\mathcal{A}=(Q,\Sigma,\delta,k,q_{0},q_{f},\Gamma,\mu)
be a
k
-
\mathsf{PT}_{=}
.

The idea is to come up with finite abstractions of the relative positions of pebbles.
Recall that basic pebble transducers allow tests that check if a pebble is on the head or not, so we can directly use these while constructing
\mathcal{B}
. In
\mathcal{B}
, we work with guards that are bit vectors
\in\{0,1\}^{k}
which represent a pebble configuration at a head position. Thus, the all 1-bitvector
(1,1,\dots,1)
denotes
all pebbles are at the head and represents the guard
\bigwedge_{i=1}^{k}(\mathsf{h}=\mathsf{p}_{i})

while the bitvector
(1,0,\dots,0)
where only the first bit is a 1 represents

\mathsf{h}=\mathsf{p}_{1}\wedge\bigwedge_{i=2}^{k}\neg(\mathsf{h}=\mathsf{p}_{i})
and so on. The guards
used in
\mathcal{B}
are therefore “complete” in the sense, for each transition
of
\mathcal{A}
testing
\mathsf{h}=\mathsf{p}_{j}
(or
\neg(\mathsf{h}=\mathsf{p}_{j})
),
we have
2^{k-1}
transitions in
\mathcal{B}
guarded by the
2^{k-1}
distinct bitvectors
where all of them have the
j
th bit 1 (resp.,
j
th bit 0) and all combinations
for others.

Thus, the key elements in the construction of
\mathcal{B}
are the following.

(a)

Extend the states of
\mathcal{A}
with a
k\times k
matrix
M
which tracks
the positions of all pebbles, in such a way that
M_{i,i}=1
iff
\mathsf{p}_{i}
is dropped,
and
M_{i,j}=M_{j,i}=1
iff
\mathsf{p}_{i},\mathsf{p}_{j}
are at the same position. Thus, the set of states goes from
Q
in
\mathcal{A}
to
Q\times M
in
\mathcal{B}
. In essence,
guards in
\mathcal{A}
of the form
\mathsf{p}_{i}=\mathsf{p}_{j}
are only enabled in
\mathcal{B}
from states of the form
(q,M)
where
M_{i,j}=M_{j,i}=1
.

(b)

As mentioned above, we have a blow up in the transitions where
we have one transition for each possibility of pebble arrangement for any head position.

We construct the basic pebble transducer
\mathcal{B}=(Q^{\prime},\Sigma,\delta^{\prime},k,q^{\prime}_{0},q^{\prime}_{f},\Gamma,\mu^{\prime})
as follows.

1.

Q^{\prime}=Q\times\{0,1\}^{k\times k}
.
We extend the set of states

Q
with information regarding the relative positions of the
k
pebbles.
A state in
Q^{\prime}
thus has the form
(q,\alpha)
where
\alpha=(\alpha_{i,j})_{1\leq i,j\leq k}
is a
k\times k
Boolean matrix, with the encoding that
\alpha_{i,j}=1
iff
\mathsf{peb}_{i}=\mathsf{peb}_{j}
, that is, both pebbles
\mathsf{p}_{i},\mathsf{p}_{j}
are at the same position.
\alpha_{i,i}=1
is an encoding of the fact that pebble
\mathsf{p}_{i}
has been dropped somewhere on the word. Finally,
(q,\alpha)\in Q^{\prime}_{i}
,
i\in\{+1,-1,0\}

iff
q\in Q_{i}
.

2.

The initial state
q^{\prime}_{i}=(q_{i},\overline{0})
where
\overline{0}
denotes the

k\times k
zero matrix. This signifies that no pebbles are dropped at the beginning.

3.

The final state
q^{\prime}_{f}=(q_{f},\overline{0})
. Recall that, for acceptance, all pebbles are lifted.

4.

The transitions
\delta^{\prime}
are defined in two stages.
For each transition

t=(q,a,\varphi,\mathsf{op},q^{\prime})
in
\mathcal{A}
, we define transitions
t^{\prime}=((q,\alpha),a,b,\mathsf{op},(q^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime}))
where
a\in\Sigma
,
b\in\{0,1\}^{k}

such that
(\alpha,b)
constitute a consistent abstraction pair : that is,
\alpha,b
are consistent with each other, the guard
\varphi
as well as the operation
\mathsf{op}
.

Basic Consistency of the pair (
\alpha,b
)

This is a basic consistency check done using invariants I1-I5. The component
\alpha
in source state and the bitvector guarding a transition from a source state
(q,\alpha))
must be consistent.

For each
\alpha
which is part of the source state
(q,\alpha)
in
t^{\prime}
,
an outgoing transition can be decorated with the bitvector
b
only if the following invariants (I1)-(I5) are true.

I1

The entries of
\alpha
are symmetric and transitive. It is easy to see that for
1\leq i,j\leq k
, if pebble
\mathsf{p}_{i}
and pebble
\mathsf{p}_{j}
are at the same position, then
\alpha_{i,j}=1=\alpha_{j,i}
. Likewise, if
\mathsf{p}_{i},\mathsf{p}_{j}
are not at the same position, then
\alpha_{i,j}=0=\alpha_{j,i}
. Likewise, if
\mathsf{p}_{i},\mathsf{p}_{j}
are at the same position, and
\mathsf{p}_{j},\mathsf{p}_{n}
are at the same position, then
\mathsf{p}_{i},\mathsf{p}_{n}
are at the same position. That is,

\alpha_{i,j}=1\wedge\alpha_{j,n}=1\Rightarrow\alpha_{i,n}=1
.

I2

For
1\leq i\leq k
,
(b_{i}=1)\Rightarrow(\alpha_{i,i}=1)
. Recall that
b_{i}=1
represents that pebble
\mathsf{p}_{i}
is on the head position, hence indeed it has been dropped implying
\alpha_{i,i}=1
.

I3

For
1\leq i,j\leq k
,
(b_{i}=1=b_{j})\Rightarrow\alpha_{i,j}=1
.
Indeed, if pebbles
\mathsf{p}_{i},\mathsf{p}_{j}
are both at the head position, they are at the same position. Note that by I1, we also get
\alpha_{j,i}=1
.

I4

For
1\leq i,j\leq k
,
\alpha_{i,j}=1\Rightarrow(\alpha_{i,i}=1\wedge\alpha_{j,j}=1\wedge b_{i}=b_{j})
. If pebbles
\mathsf{p}_{i},\mathsf{p}_{j}
are at the same position, then indeed they have both been dropped, and they are both either at the head position, or both are not on the head.

I5

(1\leq i<j\leq k\wedge\alpha_{j,j}=1)\Rightarrow\alpha_{i,i}=1
.
By the pebble stack policy, if a higher indexed pebble
\mathsf{p}_{j}
has been dropped, then indeed, all lower indexed pebbles
\mathsf{p}_{i}
have been dropped.

Transition Consistency :
\alpha,b\models\varphi,\mathsf{op}

Next, we present the consistency conditions

\alpha,b
and
\varphi

\mathsf{op}
.
Formally,

•

If
\varphi

\mathsf{h}=\mathsf{p}_{i}
, then
\alpha,b\models(\mathsf{h}=\mathsf{p}_{i})
if

.

•

If
\varphi
is an atom of the form
\mathsf{p}_{i}=\mathsf{p}_{j}
, then
\alpha,b\models(\mathsf{p}_{i}=\mathsf{p}_{j})
if
\alpha_{i,j}=1
.

We now proceed to define
\alpha,b\models\mathsf{op}

\mathsf{op}
and

. Here, we also specify the target
\alpha^{\prime}=\mathsf{op}(\alpha,b)

•

If
\mathsf{op}=\mathsf{nop}

\alpha,b\models\mathsf{nop}
for all
\alpha,b
. In this case,
\alpha^{\prime}=\alpha

•

\mathsf{op}=\mathsf{drop}~\mathsf{p}_{n}
.
\alpha,b\models\mathsf{drop}~\mathsf{p}_{n}
if
\alpha_{n,n}=0
and
\alpha_{n-1,n-1}=1
if

.
\alpha^{\prime}

First of all,
\alpha^{\prime}_{i,j}=1=\alpha_{i,j}

\alpha^{\prime}_{j,n}=0

.

•

\mathsf{op}=\mathsf{lift}~\mathsf{p}_{n}

5.

Finally, the output

in
\mathcal{A}
is the same as that associated with
t^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{B}
.

This concludes the construction of
\mathcal{B}
from
\mathcal{A}
. For a
\alpha,b
pair, checking if they are consistent with each other takes time linear in their sizes. Likewise, given a
\varphi
and
\mathsf{op}
, checking whether
\alpha,b\models\varphi,\mathsf{op}
takes time linear
in their sizes.

B.1
Configuration based consistency

\cref

sec:constr presented a construction of the transducer
\mathcal{B}

without equality checks, where the new transitions were obtained by adding on finite abstractions of the pebble space to the state, and expanding the transitions
to include an abstraction of the pebbles on the head.

To prove the correctness of our construction, we have to reason that from any configuration
C=(q,\mathsf{h},\mathsf{peb})
which enables a transition
t=(a,\varphi,\mathsf{op})
in
\mathcal{A}
, there is a configuration
C^{\prime}=((q,\alpha),\mathsf{h},\mathsf{peb})
in
\mathcal{B}
which enables the corresponding transition
t^{\prime}=(a,b,\mathsf{op})
and conversely. Towards this, we define
a map
\Phi=(\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2})
between configurations of
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
as follows.

Given a configuration
C=(q,\mathsf{h},\mathsf{peb})
enabling
t=(a,\varphi,\mathsf{op})
in
\mathcal{A}
,
\Phi(C)
is the unique configuration
C^{\prime}=((q,\Phi_{1}(\mathsf{peb})),\mathsf{h},\mathsf{peb})
of
\mathcal{B}
enabling
t^{\prime}=((q,\Phi_{1}(\mathsf{peb})),a,\Phi_{2}(\mathsf{h},\mathsf{peb}),\mathsf{op})
where
\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2}
respectively compute

\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{k\times k}
and the bitvector guard
b\in\{0,1\}^{k}
as follows.

\displaystyle\Phi_{1}(\mathsf{peb})=\quad\left\{\begin{array}[]{r l}\alpha_{i,j}=1&\text{if}~i,j\leq|\mathsf{peb}|\wedge\mathsf{peb}_{i}=\mathsf{peb}_{j}\\
\alpha_{i,j}=0&\text{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle\Phi_{2}(\mathsf{h},\mathsf{peb})=\quad\left\{\begin{array}[]{r l}b_{i}=1&\text{if}~i\leq|\mathsf{peb}|\wedge\mathsf{h}=\mathsf{peb}_{i}\\
b_{i}=0&\text{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

Lemma 23
.

Let
C=(q,\mathsf{h},\mathsf{peb})
be a configuration in
\mathcal{A}
.
The pair
(\Phi_{1}(\mathsf{peb}),\Phi_{2}(\mathsf{h},\mathsf{peb}))
satisfies basic
consistency.

Proof.

1.

First of all, the
\alpha
computed by
\Phi_{1}
is symmetric and transitive. Hence, (I1) is preserved.

2.

Next, consider
1\leq i\leq k
such that
i\leq|\mathsf{peb}|
and

h=\mathsf{peb}_{i}
. Then
\Phi_{2}
sets
b_{i}=1
. We also have
\mathsf{peb}_{i}=\mathsf{peb}_{i}
, and
\Phi_{1}
assigns
\alpha_{i,i}=1
. Now, for
i\leq|\mathsf{peb}|
if we do not have
h=\mathsf{peb}_{i}
, then

\Phi_{2}
sets
b_{i}=0
.
In both cases, (I2) is preserved by
\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2}
.

3.

Next, consider

1\leq i,j\leq|\mathsf{peb}|
and
\mathsf{h}=\mathsf{peb}_{i}=\mathsf{peb}_{j}
.
Then
\Phi_{2}
sets
b_{i}=1=b_{j}=1
. However,

\mathsf{h}=\mathsf{peb}_{i}=\mathsf{peb}_{j}\Rightarrow\mathsf{peb}_{i}=\mathsf{peb}_{j}

and
\Phi_{1}
sets
\alpha_{i,j}=1
. Noe suppose

\mathsf{peb}_{i}=\mathsf{peb}_{j}
, but
\mathsf{h}\neq\mathsf{peb}_{i}
(and

\mathsf{h}\neq\mathsf{peb}_{j}
). Then
b_{i}=0=b_{j}
. However,

\Phi_{1}
sets
\alpha_{i,j}=1
. In both cases, (I3) holds.

4.

Likewise, for
1\leq i,j\leq k
and
\mathsf{peb}_{i}=\mathsf{peb}_{j}
,
\Phi_{1}
assigns
\alpha_{i,j}=1
. Then clearly, either
\mathsf{h}=\mathsf{peb}_{i}=\mathsf{peb}_{j}
or

\mathsf{h}\neq(\mathsf{peb}_{i}=\mathsf{peb}_{j})
, and in both cases,
b_{i}=b_{j}

under
\Phi_{2}
. This shows that (I4) holds.

5.

Finally,
for all
1\leq j\leq|\mathsf{peb}|
,
\mathsf{peb}_{j}=\mathsf{peb}_{j}
. Thus,
for
i<j
,
\mathsf{peb}_{i}=\mathsf{peb}_{i}
by the stack discipline.

\Phi_{1}
assigns

\alpha_{j,j}=1
and
\alpha_{i,i}=1
, preserving (I5).

∎

Lemma 24
.

Let
C=(q,\mathsf{h},\mathsf{peb})
be a configuration in
\mathcal{A}
. For
any guard
\varphi
and operation
\mathsf{op}
in
\mathcal{A}
,

(\mathsf{h},\mathsf{peb})\models\varphi
(resp.,
(\mathsf{h},\mathsf{peb})\models\mathsf{op}
)
\Leftrightarrow(\Phi_{1}(\mathsf{peb}),\Phi_{2}(\mathsf{h},\mathsf{peb}))\models\varphi
(resp.,
(\Phi_{1}(\mathsf{peb}),\Phi_{2}(\mathsf{h},\mathsf{peb}))\models\mathsf{op}
). Finally,

\mathsf{op}(\Phi_{1}(\mathsf{peb}),\Phi_{2}(\mathsf{peb},\mathsf{h}))=\Phi_{1}(\mathsf{op}(\mathsf{peb},\mathsf{h}))
.

Proof.

(\mathsf{h},\mathsf{peb})\models\varphi,\mathsf{op}
has been defined earlier. We perform a case analysis on
\varphi
.

1.

\varphi=(\mathsf{h}=\mathsf{peb}_{i}
).
(\mathsf{h},\mathsf{peb})\models\varphi
iff
1\leq i\leq|\mathsf{peb}|
and
\mathsf{peb}_{i}=\mathsf{h}
. By construction of
\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2}
,

b_{i}=1
. By definition of transition consistency, we obtain
(\Phi_{1}(\mathsf{peb}),\Phi_{2}(\mathsf{h},\mathsf{peb}))\models\varphi
.

2.

\varphi=(\mathsf{peb}_{i}=\mathsf{peb}_{j}
).
(\mathsf{h},\mathsf{peb})\models\varphi
iff
1\leq i\leq|\mathsf{peb}|
and
\mathsf{peb}_{i}=\mathsf{peb}_{j}
. By construction of
\Phi_{1}
,

\alpha_{i,j}=1
. By definition of transition consistency, we obtain
(\Phi_{1}(\mathsf{peb}),\Phi_{2}(\mathsf{h},\mathsf{peb}))\models\varphi
.

We perform a case analysis on
\mathsf{op}
.

1.

\mathsf{op}=\mathsf{nop}
. This is trivial. In this case,

\mathsf{op}(\mathsf{peb},\mathsf{h})=\mathsf{peb}
. So,
\Phi_{1}(\mathsf{op}(\mathsf{peb},\mathsf{h}))=\Phi_{1}(\mathsf{peb})
.
This coincides with the definition of
\alpha^{\prime}=\mathsf{op}(\Phi_{1}(\mathsf{peb}),\Phi_{2}(\mathsf{peb},\mathsf{h}))
in
\mathcal{B}
since
\mathsf{nop}
does not change anything.

2.

\mathsf{op}=\mathsf{drop}~\mathsf{peb}_{n}
.
(\mathsf{h},\mathsf{peb})\models\mathsf{drop}~\mathsf{peb}_{n}
iff
|\mathsf{peb}|=n-1
. By construction of
\Phi_{1}
,
\alpha_{n,n}=0
as
n>|\mathsf{peb}|
and

\alpha_{n-1,n-1}=1
as
n-1\leq|\mathsf{peb}|
. By definition of transition consistency, we obtain
(\Phi_{1}(\mathsf{peb}),\Phi_{2}(\mathsf{h},\mathsf{peb}))\models\mathsf{op}
.

Now,
\mathsf{op}(\mathsf{peb},\mathsf{h})=\mathsf{peb}^{\prime}=\mathsf{peb}.\mathsf{h}
, and

|\mathsf{peb}^{\prime}|=n
. Then
\Phi_{1}(\mathsf{peb}^{\prime})
assigns
\alpha_{n,n}=1
,

\alpha_{i,n}=1
for all
\mathsf{peb}_{i}=\mathsf{peb}_{n}
and

\alpha_{n+1,n+1}=0
. By construction of
\mathcal{B}
, it can be seen that

\alpha^{\prime}=\mathsf{op}(\Phi_{1}(\mathsf{peb}),\Phi_{2}(\mathsf{h},\mathsf{peb}))
is precisely what we get above.

3.

\mathsf{op}=\mathsf{lift}~p_{n}
.
(\mathsf{h},\mathsf{peb})\models\mathsf{lift}~p_{n}
iff
|\mathsf{peb}|=n\geq 1
and
\mathsf{h}=\mathsf{peb}_{n}
. By construction of
\Phi_{2}
,
b_{n}=1
, and by
\Phi_{1}
,

\alpha_{n+1,n+1}=0
as
n+1>|\mathsf{peb}|
and
\alpha_{n,n}=1
as
n\leq|\mathsf{peb}|
.
By definition of transition consistency, we obtain
(\Phi_{1}(\mathsf{peb}),\Phi_{2}(\mathsf{h},\mathsf{peb}))\models\mathsf{op}
.

Now,
\mathsf{op}(\mathsf{peb},\mathsf{h})=\mathsf{peb}^{\prime}=\mathsf{peb}_{1}\dots\mathsf{peb}_{n-1}
.

\Phi_{1}(\mathsf{peb}^{\prime})
assigns
\alpha_{i,j}=0
for
i,j\geq n
as
n>|\mathsf{peb}^{\prime}|
,

\alpha_{i,j}=1
for all
i,j\leq|\mathsf{peb}^{\prime}|=n-1
and
\mathsf{peb}_{i}=\mathsf{peb}_{j}
.
The definition of
\mathcal{B}
precisely assigns the same to
\alpha^{\prime}=\mathsf{op}(\Phi_{1}(\mathsf{peb}),\Phi_{2}(\mathsf{h},\mathsf{peb}))
.

Thus,
(\mathsf{h},\mathsf{peb})\models\varphi,\mathsf{op}

\Rightarrow

(\Phi_{1}(\mathsf{peb}),\Phi_{2}(\mathsf{h},\mathsf{peb}))\models\varphi,\mathsf{op}
.
\cref
figcommu shows the commutation
of applying
\mathsf{op}
and
\Phi
.

Conversely, assume
(\Phi_{1}(\mathsf{peb}),\Phi_{2}(\mathsf{h},\mathsf{peb}))\models\varphi,\mathsf{op}
.
Let
\alpha=\Phi_{1}(\mathsf{peb}),b=\Phi_{2}(\mathsf{h},\mathsf{peb})
.
We have to show that
\mathsf{h},\mathsf{peb}\models\varphi,\mathsf{op}
.
First we consider
\varphi
.

1.

\varphi=(\mathsf{h}=\mathsf{peb}_{i})
. By definition,
\alpha,b\models\varphi
if
b_{i}=1
.
By the definition of
\Phi_{2}(\mathsf{h},\mathsf{peb})
,
b_{i}
is set to 1 when
\mathsf{h}=\mathsf{peb}_{i}

and
i\leq|\mathsf{peb}|
. These are precisely the conditions needed for
\mathsf{h},\mathsf{peb}\models\varphi
.

2.

\varphi=(\mathsf{peb}_{i}=\mathsf{peb}_{j})
. By definition,
\alpha,b\models\varphi
if
\alpha_{i,j}=1
. By construction of
\Phi_{1}(\mathsf{peb})
,
\alpha_{i,j}
is assigned 1
when
1\leq i,j\leq|\mathsf{peb}|
and
\mathsf{peb}_{i}=\mathsf{peb}_{j}
. These are precisely the conditions needed for
\mathsf{h},\mathsf{peb}\models\varphi
.

Now, we consider
\mathsf{op}
.

1.

\mathsf{op}=\mathsf{nop}
. In this case, neither the pebble stack nor the head is affected. Hence,
if
\alpha,b\models\mathsf{op}
, we also have
\mathsf{h},\mathsf{peb}\models\mathsf{op}
.

2.

\mathsf{op}=\mathsf{drop}~\mathsf{peb}_{n}
. By definition,
\alpha,b\models\mathsf{op}
if
\alpha_{n,n}=0,\alpha_{n-1,n-1}=1
. By definition of
\Phi_{1}
,
\alpha_{n-1,n-1}
is assigned 1 when
\mathsf{peb}_{n-1}=\mathsf{peb}_{n-1}
and
n-1\leq|\mathsf{peb}|
; also,
\alpha_{n,n}
is assigned 0 when
n>|\mathsf{peb}|
and
\neg(\mathsf{peb}_{n}=\mathsf{peb}_{n})
. Together,

n-1\leq|\mathsf{peb}|
and
n>|\mathsf{peb}|
gives us
|\mathsf{peb}|=n-1
.
The definition of
\Phi_{1}
also ensures that
\mathsf{peb}_{i}=\mathsf{peb}_{i}
for all
i\leq n-1
. These are precisely the conditions needed for

\mathsf{h},\mathsf{peb}\models\mathsf{drop}~\mathsf{peb}_{n}
.

3.

\mathsf{op}=\mathsf{lift}~\mathsf{peb}_{n}
. By definition,
\alpha,b\models\mathsf{op}
if
b_{n}=1
and
\alpha_{n+1,n+1}=0
,
n<|\mathsf{peb}|
. By the definition of
\Phi_{1}(\mathsf{peb})
and
\Phi_{2}(\mathsf{h},\mathsf{peb})
,
b_{n}
is assigned 1 when

n\leq|\mathsf{peb}|
and
\mathsf{h}=\mathsf{peb}_{n}
. Likewise,
\alpha_{n+1,n+1}
is assigned 0
when
n+1>|\mathsf{peb}|
or
\neg(\mathsf{peb}_{n+1}=\mathsf{peb}_{n+1})
.
n\leq|\mathsf{peb}|
and

n+1>|\mathsf{peb}|
together say that
|\mathsf{peb}|=n
.
By definition of
\mathsf{h},\mathsf{peb}\models\mathsf{op}
, we see that

\mathsf{h},\mathsf{peb}\models\mathsf{lift}~\mathsf{peb}_{n}
since we have
|\mathsf{peb}|=n
and
\mathsf{h}=\mathsf{peb}_{n}
.

Thus,
(\Phi_{1}(\mathsf{peb}),\Phi_{2}(\mathsf{h},\mathsf{peb}))\models\varphi,\mathsf{op}

\Rightarrow

(\mathsf{h},\mathsf{peb})\models\varphi,\mathsf{op}
.
∎

Lemma 25
.

(\alpha,b)
be a pair satisfying basic consistency. Then

\mathsf{h},\mathsf{peb}
such that
(\alpha,b)=(\Phi_{1}(\mathsf{peb}),\Phi_{2}(\mathsf{h},\mathsf{peb}))
.

Proof.

Given
(\alpha,b)
, first we construct
|\mathsf{peb}|=\max\{i\mid\alpha_{i,i}=1\}
.
Recall that
\alpha
is an equivalence relation on
\{1,2,\dots,|\mathsf{peb}|\}
. Let
[i]
,
1\leq i\leq|\mathsf{peb}|
denote the equivalence class of
i
under
\alpha
.
[i]=\{j\mid\alpha_{i,j}=1\}
.

For any word
\#u
such that
|u|>|\mathsf{peb}|
,
define

.
f
is a function
which assigns members of
[i]
to a unique position on
\#u
. For
i,j\in\{1,2,\dots,|\mathsf{peb}|\}
,
i\sim j
iff
f(i)=f(j)
. Define

\mathsf{peb}_{j}=f(j)
, for
1\leq j\leq|\mathsf{peb}|
. For all
j\in[i]

\mathsf{peb}_{j}=\mathsf{peb}_{i}
since
f(j)=f(i)
. With this definition
of
\mathsf{peb}
, we show that
\Phi_{1}(\mathsf{peb})=\alpha
. Let the
k\times k
Boolean matrix defined by
\Phi_{1}
be called
\beta
. We will show that
\alpha=\beta
.

By definition,

\Phi_{1}
assigns
\beta_{i,j}=1
iff
\mathsf{peb}_{i}=\mathsf{peb}_{j}
for
1\leq i,j\leq|\mathsf{peb}|
. By the construction of
\mathsf{peb}
, we have
\mathsf{peb}_{i}=\mathsf{peb}_{j}
iff
f(i)=f(j)
iff
i\sim j
. Since
\alpha_{i,j}=1

i\sim j

\alpha=\beta
. Hence,
\alpha=\Phi_{1}(\mathsf{peb})
.

Next, we define
\mathsf{h}
using
\alpha,b
, and construct

.

First, let us consider the case when
b=\overline{0}
. In this case,
define
\mathsf{h}=v
where
v\in\{0,1,\dots,|u|\}
is such that
v\neq f(i)
for any
i
.
Otherwise, define
\mathsf{h}=f(i)
if
b_{i}=1
. Notice that, by definition
of
f
,
f(i)=f(j)
for all
j\in[i]
; this gives
b_{j}=b_{i}=1
for all
j\in[i]
.
Finally, notice that for all
j\in[i]
,
b_{j}=b_{i}
since
(\alpha,b)
satisfies basic consistency.

Now by definition of
\Phi_{2}
,
b^{\prime}_{i}=1
if
i\leq|\mathsf{peb}|
and
\mathsf{h}=\mathsf{peb}_{i}=f(i)
, and
b^{\prime}_{i}=0
when
\mathsf{h}=v\notin f(\{1,2,\dots,|\mathsf{peb}|\}
(
\mathsf{h}\neq\mathsf{peb}_{i}
for all
i
). We now show that
b^{\prime}=b
.
By construction,
b^{\prime}_{i}=1
when
\mathsf{h}=f(i)
, and this is true when
b_{i}=1
.
In this case we also have
b^{\prime}_{j}=1
for all
j\in[i]
: then we know also that

b_{j}=1
, since
b_{i}=b_{j}
for all
j\in[i]
. Finally,
b^{\prime}_{i}=0
when
\mathsf{h}\neq f(i)
for any
i
. That is,
\mathsf{h}\neq\mathsf{peb}_{i}
for any
1\leq i\leq|\mathsf{peb}|
; in this case,

b_{i}=0
as well. Thus,
b^{\prime}=b=\Phi_{2}(\mathsf{h},\mathsf{peb})
.

i=1,2
in
\mathcal{B}
be enabled from

C^{\prime}=((q,\alpha),\mathsf{peb},\mathsf{h})
.
From the above, we know that there is a unique
t_{i}
in
\mathcal{A}
corresponding to each
t^{\prime}_{i}
.
Let
t_{i}=(q,a,\varphi_{i},\mathsf{op}_{i},q^{\prime}_{i})
.

From
\cref
lem:conf, we know that there exists
\mathsf{peb},\mathsf{h}
such that
\alpha=\Phi_{1}(\mathsf{peb}),b=\Phi_{2}(\mathsf{peb},\mathsf{h})
.
By
\cref
lem:trans we know that
\alpha,b\models\varphi_{i}\Rightarrow(\mathsf{h},\mathsf{peb})\models\varphi_{i}
, and
\alpha,b\models\mathsf{op}_{i}\Rightarrow(\mathsf{h},\mathsf{peb})\models\mathsf{op}_{i}
. If
t_{1},t_{2}
are enabled from
C=(q,\mathsf{peb},\mathsf{h})
, then by determinism of
\mathcal{A}
,
we have
t_{1}=t_{2}
. Hence,
\mathsf{op}_{1}=\mathsf{op}_{2}=\mathsf{op}
. Also, by
\cref
lem:trans,
\alpha^{\prime}_{i}=\mathsf{op}_{i}(\alpha,b)=\mathsf{op}(\alpha,b)
for
i=1,2
.
This implies
t^{\prime}_{1}=t^{\prime}_{2}
. Thus,
\mathcal{B}
is deterministic.
∎

Lemma 28
.

\mathcal{B}
is reverse deterministic if
\mathcal{A}
is reverse deterministic.

Proof.

Assume
\mathcal{A}
is reverse deterministic.
Let
t^{\prime}_{i}=((q_{i},\alpha_{i}),a,b,\mathsf{op}_{i},(q^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime}))
for
i=1,2
be two transitions
in
\mathcal{B}
. If
t^{\prime}_{1},t^{\prime}_{2}
are both simultaneously reverse enabled, we want to show that

t^{\prime}_{1}=t^{\prime}_{2}
.
Assume that we have configurations
C^{\prime}_{1},C^{\prime}_{2},C^{\prime}
such that

C^{\prime}_{1}=((q_{1},\alpha_{1}),\mathsf{peb}^{1},\mathsf{h}^{1})\xrightarrow{t^{\prime}_{1}}C^{\prime}=((q^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime}),\mathsf{peb}^{\prime},\mathsf{h}^{\prime})
, and
C^{\prime}_{2}=((q_{2},\alpha_{2}),\mathsf{peb}^{2},\mathsf{h}^{2})\xrightarrow{t^{\prime}_{2}}C^{\prime}=((q^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime}),\mathsf{peb}^{\prime},\mathsf{h}^{\prime})
.

1.

By
\cref
lem:conf, we know that
\alpha_{1}=\Phi_{1}(\mathsf{peb}^{1}),\alpha_{2}=\Phi_{1}(\mathsf{peb}^{2})
.
Also,
\mathsf{h}^{1}=\mathsf{h}^{2}=\mathsf{h}^{\prime}-q^{\prime}=\mathsf{h}

2.

By
\cref
lem:run, corresponding to
t^{\prime}_{1},t^{\prime}_{2}
, for
i=1,2
we
have the transitions

(q_{i},\mathsf{peb}^{i},\mathsf{h})\xrightarrow{t_{i}=(q_{i},a,\varphi_{i},\mathsf{op}_{i},q^{\prime})}(q^{\prime},\mathsf{peb}^{\prime},\mathsf{h}^{\prime})
in
\mathcal{A}
.
Since
\mathcal{A}
is reverse deterministic, we know that

t_{1}=t_{2}
.
t_{1}=t_{2}
implies that
\mathsf{op}_{1}=\mathsf{op}_{2}
,
\varphi_{1}=\varphi_{2}
,
q_{1}=q_{2}
.

3.

From
\cref
sec:reversing, we know that
\mathsf{peb}^{i}=\mathsf{op}^{r}(\mathsf{peb}^{\prime},\mathsf{h})
for
i=1,2
. That is,
\mathsf{peb}^{1}=\mathsf{peb}^{2}
.

4.

Hence,
\alpha_{1}=\Phi_{1}(\mathsf{peb}^{1})=\Phi_{1}(\mathsf{peb}^{2})=\alpha_{2}
.

5.

Thus we have
t^{\prime}_{1}=t^{\prime}_{2}
.

Hence,
\mathcal{B}
is reverse deterministic.
∎

Theorem 29
.

The basic
k
-pebble transducer
\mathcal{B}
constructed above is deterministic and reversible if
\mathcal{A}
is. Further,
[\![\mathcal{B}]\!]=[\![\mathcal{A}]\!]
.

Proof.

\cref

lem:run,lem:det show that every run in
\mathcal{A}
has a corresponding
unique run in
\mathcal{B}
and conversely. By construction of
\mathcal{B}
, the outputs of corresponding transitions
in
\mathcal{B}
are the same as those of
\mathcal{A}
.
∎
