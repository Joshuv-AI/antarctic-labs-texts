Implicit biases change more slowly in larger cities
Authors: Andrew J. Stier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceImplicit biases remain a contributor to discrimination despite widespread endorsement of equality. Cities are associated with lower implicit bias levels, but how do these biases change over time? Here, I develop a mathematical theory for how cities’ social environments shape the speed at which implicit biases change over time, and the effectiveness of interventions to reduce them. I demonstrate that this model fits empirical data across a large-scale observation dataset and experimental intervention data in US cities. If this model captures some of the causal mechanisms, then the results suggest that changes in implicit biases can be understood as a collective phenomenon mediated by cultural information and how that information is introduced into human social networks and processed by individuals.

Though most people endorse equality, discrimination persists in the United States. This is partly due to implicit biases—attitudes and beliefs that operate without conscious intention (1–5), but may be in conscious awareness (6–8). Implicit biases related to race, skin tone, gender, sexuality, weight, age, and disability status are associated with police violence (5, 9, 10), poor medical care (5, 11–14), worse educational outcomes (5, 15), and worse access to career opportunities (16). As a result, implicit biases have become a central topic in discussions around structural discrimination (17, 18) and bias-reduction interventions have proliferated globally (19) with a goal of inducing cultural change that results in more equal societies.

However, there has been limited work that explains how, why, and at what speed societal-level patterns of implicit biases change. The few existing studies document changes over time in large-scale observational data (20–22) and have found that sexuality, race, and skin tone biases in the United States decreased by 65%, 26%, and 25% between 2007 and 2020, while e.g., age biases, have remained stable (21, 23); similar, though smaller decreases are observed globally (20). Despite these reductions, recent evidence has shown that implicit bias trainings have limited long-term efficacy (24–26). Why then, have some implicit biases decreased dramatically across the United States while others remain unchanged, and what principles can guide the development of effective interventions? The primary barrier to answering these questions is a lack of theoretical models that are falsifiable, provide quantitative testable predictions, and propose mechanistic targets that can be experimentally tested.

To begin to fill this gap, I propose a theory of implicit bias change that can serve as a foundation to guide future research and the development of effective interventions. This theory, which is the main contribution of the present work, is a quantitative mathematical model that describes how implicit biases vary spatially, how they change over time, and focuses on implicit biases in cities. This is motivated by past research from social psychology (25, 27–38) and urban science (39–45) demonstrating that cities shape social contexts in consistent ways across cultures and human history.

The main psychological hypothesis of this theory is that the primary individual-level mechanism of implicit biases change relevant to societal-level dynamics, is change in exposure to out-group individuals. While decades of experimental and observational evidence support this (25, 27–38) as one of many individual-level psychological mechanisms of implicit bias change (46–50), I do not directly test it here. Instead, I test predictions of a mathematical model that describes the consequences of this individual-level mechanism for cities of different population sizes. In doing so, I combine individual-level psychological mechanisms with emergent, well established aggregate mechanisms (51) which describe how different cities set the rates of out-group exposure (42, 45).

Instead of falling prey to the “ecological fallacy” (52) of reverse inference from group dynamics to individual mechanism, I start from well-established 1) individual-level psychological (25, 27–38) and 2) societal-level emergent (42, 45) mechanisms and ask what the consequences of these mechanisms are in aggregate. I demonstrate that the predictions of the proposed model hold at different levels of aggregation. Namely, I confirm the model’s predictions for individual-level responses to an implicit bias intervention (Fig. 5), differing speeds of implicit bias change across cities (Fig. 3), and dynamics of implicit bias change across the entire United States (Fig. 4). To do so, I use observational data from ≈14 million individual implicit association tests (IATs) and experimental data from 1,150 participants.

Direct causal evidence of the type provided by controlled lab-based experiments is often out of reach for real-world complex environments such as cities. However, consistent alignment between the proposed model’s predictions and empirical measurements across different levels of aggregation, from individuals to the entire United States, strengthens the utility of proposed model as a foundational component of future frameworks for describing and understanding the societal-level dynamics of implicit bias change.

An Urban Scaling Model of Implicit Bias Change.

I start my mathematical analysis from the point of view of urban scaling theory (41, 42). Its mathematical models describe cities as spatially extended socioeconomic networks which are facilitated and structured by cities’ built environments. In this model, urban social networks form as the result of balancing the costs of housing and the movement of people and goods over cities’ infrastructure networks with the benefits of social and economic interactions (42). These models derive average properties of cities as scaling relationships as a function of city population size, N (41). These derived relationships explain patterns of innovation (39), depression (53), incomes (41), road lengths (41), and many others (42), and are empirically supported across modern cultures and in ancient cities as distant as 300 BCE (42, 44). In the case of the number of per-capita social interactions, k, the derived scaling relationship takes the form of k∼Nδ, where δ=16; a prediction that has been empirically confirmed (45).

In previous work, my colleagues and I distilled two principles that shape implicit bias levels across cities. First, lower bias levels are the result of more intergroup interactions (25, 27–34): one of many mechanisms (46–50) determining implicit bias levels, but the one most relevant for understanding changes in implicit biases in an urban context. Second, low bias levels are the result of out-group expertise (35–38), meaning that rates of change slow down as exposure increases. These considerations lead to an expression for the average implicit bias level in a city of:[1]bi=b0(k0Niδ)−α,

where i indexes different cities, b0 is the bias prefactor which sets the baseline bias level, k0 is the exposure prefactor which sets the baseline intergroup exposure level, and α>0 is the learning rate which results in diminishing marginal returns to exposure typical of expertise-based learning (54). Quantities that exponentiate N, e.g., −αδ in Eq. 1, are called scaling exponents. In previous work that focused on implicit racial biases we found α≈0.25 which indicates that every doubling of exposures results in an approximately 16% decrease in bias levels (55).

Expertise-based learning is a more abstracted mechanism than other recently proposed psychological mechanisms of implicit bias change. Specifically, recent work contrasts co-occurrence-based associative learning with relational-based propositional learning models of implicit bias change (46–49, 56), which, more concretely than expertise-based learning, predict differing conditions under which implicit biases change. In the supplement, I demonstrate that well-known mathematical models of associative learning (56) and a mathematical model of one propositional framework, the common currency model (47), admit largely identical expertise-based learning curves consistent with Eq. 1.

The main difference for societal dynamics of implicit bias change between the propositional and associative frameworks, then, is the different predictions they make about which types of exposures lead to implicit bias change (46–49). However, since the mathematical model that I develop does not depend on these details, I default to an abstracted definition of out-group exposure: exposure that leads to changes in biases. That is, the model I develop below does not depend on what types of exposures, environmental signals, or cultural information lead to implicit bias change, just that there are some such exposures and signals.

To build this model, I add a third, dynamical principle to exposure and expertise: Changes in biases result directly from changes in intergroup exposure. That is, for any particular individual, ∂tb0=0. This principle is motivated by previous observational and experimental work associating low implicit bias levels with more out-group exposure (25, 27–38). Further, I focus specifically on changes in exposure due to cultural information. This is motivated by three observations.

First, experimental interventions using simulated interactions with out-group exemplars result in reduced bias levels, albeit temporarily (24, 50). Second, implicit racial biases decreased by approximately 26% from 2007 to 2020 (21, 22) even though residential segregation, which is a barrier to face-to-face interactions (55), only decreased by ≈10% during the same time period (57). Third, Fig. 1 shows that between 2010 and 2020, average implicit bias levels are strongly negatively correlated with the amount of representation of various groups in movies (58) (Spearman’s rank order correlation, rs,Black−White=−0.72, P=0.006, n=13; rs,White−Asian=−0.73, P=0.026, n=9; rs,gender−career=−0.86, P<0.001, n=13; rs,gender−science=−0.83, P=0.001, n=13; Fig. 1). In other words, the large reduction in implicit racial biases between 2007 and 2020 co-occurred with large changes in cultural exposures (representation in film) and small changes in face-to-face exposures (proxied by segregation). This suggests that modeling changes in cultural information, e.g., representation in movies, television, advertisements, news, and social media posts, etc, is a better target for understanding and explaining the dynamics of implicit bias change.

Consequently, I ask two questions about the dynamics of cultural information that lead to changes in intergroup exposure: 1) How is new cultural information introduced into a city population? 2) How much is introduced?

I cover all of the mathematically possible answers to these questions, which describe emergent, city-level mechanisms rather than new individual-level psychological mechanisms. For the first question, cultural information might enter a population in a city-size independent way—e.g., during major cultural events where a large fraction of a city’s population is rapidly exposed to new information—or the introduction of new cultural information might be city-size-dependent as is typically seen in processes involving social influence (59), including invention (39), and disease transmission (60) where introduction is socially constrained (Fig. 2, Bottom Left). For the second question, the amount of cultural information introduced might be greater than peoples’ cognitive capacity (61) to process it, so that they must choose a fraction of new information to engage with; alternatively, cognitive capacity might be much higher than the typical cognitive load introduced by new cultural information (Fig. 2, Top Left) so that urban inhabitants can process all available new information.

These two modes of information processing and information introduction result in three scaling relationships relating the speed at which implicit biases change to city population size (SI Appendix, Text):[2]|∂tbi(t)|∼Ni−αδ,[3]|∂tbi(t)|∼Ni−δ−αδ,[4]|∂tbi(t)|∼Ni−2δ−αδ.

These equations predict that the speed at which implicit biases change is slower in larger cities. In other words, the theory predicts that implicit biases are harder to change in large cities, regardless of how cultural information is introduced into or processed by a population. A corollary of this prediction is that small perturbations to exposure, like those that might occur during a one-off bias reduction intervention (24, 50) will fade more quickly in larger cities.

I next consider how these predictions impact macroscale patterns of change across multiple cities. To do so, I take a set of cities that initially follow Eq. 1 and let them evolve according to one of Eqs. 2–4. I then ask what scaling exponents and prefactors would be measured at a later time, Δt. Explicitly, I find values of b0′ and β′ that satisfy:[5]b0′Niβ′≈b0Ni−αδ+Δt∂tbi

Some algebra reveals that there are two characteristic ways in which the implicit biases of an urban system can change over time, when starting from Eq. 5 and substituting in one of Eqs. 2–4 (SI Appendix, Text). First, the system can rotate around an intermediate value of N. That is, biases will increase (decrease) in smaller cities and decrease (increase) in larger cities (Fig. 2, Bottom Right). Second, biases in all cities can shift up or down together (i.e., β′(0)=β′(Δt), Fig. 2, Top Right).

These two modes of implicit bias change are equivalent to the statement that the slope and intercept of the scaling line, β′ and log b0′, have either a negative linear relationship or are uncorrelated. Specifically, the first mode of implicit bias change, ∂tbi∼Ni−jδ−αδ, with j∈{1,2}, results in[6]Δln b0′Δβ′=−1jδ−⟨ln Ni⟩,

where ⟨·⟩ is the mean operator. Conversely, in the second mode of implicit bias change, ∂tbi∼Ni−αδ, and β′ and log b0′ are uncorrelated. I empirically measure this below by calculating the angle, θ, between successive points in ln b0′ and β′ phase space via θ= tan−1Δln b0′/ Δβ′.

Implicit Biases Scale with City Population Size.

To test these predictions, I use data from the publicly available Project Implicit dataset (62), which has ≈14 million implicit association test (IAT) responses from individuals in U.S. metropolitan areas between 2010 and 2023. Implicit association tests present participants with stimuli from different categories. Participants are given a pairing of stimulus categories, and during each trial, they are asked to classify a single stimulus correctly. The degree of implicit association is measured as a difference in reaction times between contrasting category pairings, normalized to each individual’s typical spread in reaction times (1–3). For example, in the Black-White race IAT, test takers are presented with “good” and “bad” words (e.g., “Cheerful,” “Magnificent,” “Angry,” “Scorn”) and White and Black faces. If participants are slower to classify stimuli when White is paired with “good,” they have a Black-good bias. If participants are slower to classify stimuli when Black is paired with “good,” they have a White-good bias (1, 3).

As in previous work (55), I find that implicit bias levels generally decrease with city population (β=−0.016, t=−4.316, P<0.001, n=5,191 for the ordinary least squares fit to b∼Nβ across all biases and years, SI Appendix, Fig. S1). Across 11 implicit biases (SI Appendix, Table S1), implicit biases levels from Black-White, skin tone, gender-science, and Asian-White IATs have scaling exponents β<0. Age and disability IAT data have scaling exponents greater than zero (βage=0.013, SE=0.002, P<0.001; βdisability=0.013, SE=0.005, P=0.012), though this can be explained by the fact that larger cities tend to have fewer older inhabitants and fewer inhabitants living with disabilities (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Data from other IATs show no significant difference in bias levels between large and small cities. This may be due to limited data or city size not directly structuring out-group exposure levels relevant to these biases.

Bias Changes More Slowly in Larger Cities.

To measure how quickly biases change, I take moving window averages of the same Project Implicit data within each city and then compute the rate of change over the moving window. Fig. 3 illustrates the city-population dependence of the speed of implicit biases change, i.e., γ in |∂tb|∼Nγ. Values of γ less than 0 indicate that biases change more slowly in larger cities while values of γ greater than 0 indicate that biases change more quickly in larger cities. With a moving window size of 30 d, I find that all implicit biases change more slowly in larger cities than in smaller cities: γ<0 (95% bootstrap over cities CI, CI, for the fraction of the distribution of γ that is less than zero, ∈[0.95, 1.0]). These distributions are also consistent with the predictions of γ=−2δ−αδ≈−0.38 and γ=−δ−αδ≈−0.21. Specifically, the 95% bootstrap over cities CI for all biases overlaps with both predictions (γ∈[−0.423, −0.189], results for individual IATs are in SI Appendix, Table S2).

When combining data across biases, city population explains 14.4% of the variance in how quickly biases change across cities with the R2 for individual biases ranging from 13.4% to 25.4% (Fig. 3). Results are similar for different moving window sizes, data inclusion thresholds (SI Appendix, Tables S3–S11), and a more restrictive definition of city boundaries that only includes core metropolitan counties (SI Appendix, Tables S12–S14). Simulations show that more sophisticated, model-based time series methods applied previously to Project Implicit data (20–22) are unable to accurately detect different speeds of implicit bias change because they amplify the noise from small and fluctuating sample sizes for individual cities (SI Appendix, Fig. S3).

System-Wide Changes in Urban Implicit Biases.

I next examine macroscale changes in implicit biases. To do so, I fit a line to the logarithm of city population versus the logarithm of the bias level in a moving window (Fig. 2). At each time point, the log–log line has a slope, β′, and intercept log b0′. I plot the slopes against the intercepts and examine trajectories in this phase space plot over time. The trajectories in this phase space are predicted by Eq. 6 and SI Appendix, Eq. S20 and are illustrated schematically in Fig. 2. Fig. 4 illustrates the empirical phase space trajectories. There is a strong negative correlation between β′ and log b0′ as predicted by the theory (Fig. 4, Left, mean centering data by year and combining across all biases, rs=−0.99, P<0.001, n=8,114).

Fitting these mean-centered phase space data to Eq. 6 with j=1 or j=2 provides a test between the two different modes of implicit bias change that predict a negative correlation in phase space. The coefficient of determination, i.e., R2, for the |∂tb|∼N−2δ−αδ best fit line in the Left panel of Fig. 4 is significantly higher (R2=0.982, 95% bootstrap CI=[0.978, 0.985]) than the corresponding one for |∂tb|∼N−δ−αδ (R2=0.897, 95% bootstrap CI=[0.888, 0.904]). This suggests that the primary mode of implicit bias change involves limited cognitive capacity and unconstrained introduction (Fig. 2).

This conclusion is recapitulated by examining the trajectories between successive phase space points (Fig. 4, Middle and Right). Rather than fitting the average phase space trajectory (Fig. 4, Left), I examine point-by-point temporal microtrajectories of implicit bias change in phase space. I focus on the angle of each microtrajectory, θ, which is predicted by Eq. 6. I find that θ, is strongly peaked around θ=93.85 degrees (95% bootstrap CI, θ∈[93.73, 93.93]) for all biases (Fig. 4, Insets and SI Appendix, Table S15). This overlaps the distribution predicted by Eq. 6 for θ when |∂tb|∼N−2δ−αδ (θ∈[93.09, 93.74]). However, it does not overlap the distribution predicted by Eq. 6 for θ when |∂tb|∼N−δ−αδ (θ∈[92.66, 93.13]). These findings further support the conclusion that the primary mode of implicit bias change involves limited cognitive capacity and unconstrained introduction.

Although θ is strongly peaked around the mode of implicit bias change involving limited cognitive capacity and unconstrained introduction (Fig. 4, Insets), there are some instances of θ≈90 degrees (SI Appendix, Table S15). This value of θ, is predicted when cultural information is introduction-constrained but not limited by cognitive capacity. I examine where in the phase space plots shown in Fig. 4, Middle and Right microtrajectories with θ=90 occur. I find that microtrajectories with θ=90 are significantly more likely when moving between phase space clusters (Fig. 4, Middle and Right) than when moving within a cluster (Z test for difference in proportions, Δprobability,86.83−89.97deg=−0.027, Z(673,138)=2.81, P=0.005; SI Appendix, Fig. S4 and Table S16; results for individual biases in SI Appendix, Tables S17–S23).

In other words, microtrajectories which move between clusters are more likely to be consistent with implicit bias change where cultural information is introduction-constrained but not limited by cognitive capacity; while microtrajectories that stay within clusters are more likely to be consistent with implicit bias change where cognitive capacity is limited and cultural information is not introduction-constrained.

These between-cluster microtrajectories seem to be a characteristic of sustained long-term decreases in biases, as evidenced by the observation that biases which changed more between 2010 and 2023 have phase space clusters which are more spread out (rs=−0.77, P<0.001, n=10; SI Appendix, Fig. S5, compare Fig. 4Middle and Right). The mathematical model predicts that a greater spread between phase space clusters occurs as the result of more or more intense microtrajectories with θ=90. Together, these findings suggest that although introduction-constrained but not cognitive-capacity-limited implicit bias change is relatively infrequent, it may be necessary to sustain long-term change. Such instances might correspond to “viral” cultural events that spread through urban social networks and highly capture people’s attention.

Consequences for Bias Interventions.

Finally, I turn to the prediction that the effects of interventions to reduce biases will fade more quickly in larger cities. To test this prediction, I recruited 1,150 participants between the ages of 18 and 89 from small cities (median population 153,411) and large cities (median population 13,012,469) in the United States. Participants first completed a Black-White IAT, then read one of 10 counterstereotypic stories similar to that in refs. 24 and 50, and then completed a second IAT. Each counterstereotypic story presented participants with a vividly described second-person scenario in which they face a stressful situation (e.g., a terminal illness or homelessness) and are saved by an out-group member. Finally, participants were asked to return 6 h, 12 h, and 24 h later to complete follow-up IATs or 12 h, 24 h, 60 h, and 216 h later to complete follow-up IATs. After the initial data collection, participants who completed no follow-ups beyond 24 h were asked to return for an additional final follow-up. Participant demographics are shown in SI Appendix, Table S21.

As can be seen in Fig. 5, the counterstereotypic story induced a 22% decrease in implicit racial biases across all participants (tpaired(1150)=−8.64, P<0.001). As predicted, this reduction in biases faded more quickly in larger cities than in smaller cities. Specifically, implicit racial biases remained lower in participants from small cities during the first day (tpaired(223)=−2.11, P=0.036), at the 24 h follow-up (tpaired(304)=−5.19, P=<0.001), at the follow-up at approximately 60 h, i.e., ≈2.5 d (tpaired(197)=−3.77, P<0.001), and at the follow-ups at approximately 216 h, i.e., ≈9 d (tpaired(180)=−4.87, P<0.001). In contrast, implicit racial biases for participants from large cities returned to baseline by the first follow-up during the first day (tpaired(182)=0.31, P=0.75) and were also at baseline at the 60 h (tpaired(136)=−0.52, P=0.60) and 216 h follow-ups (tpaired(150)=−0.81, P=0.42). This occurred even though the counterstereotypic story had similar effects on participants from large cities (Δ|b|=−0.151, tpaired(461)=−5.55, P<0.001) and small cities (Δ|b|=−0.148, tpaired(650)=−6.26, P<0.001). Interestingly, participants from large cities showed persistent reductions in bias at only the 24 h follow-up (tpaired(211)=−2.40, P=0.017), possibly due to postsleep memory consolidation of the emotional counterstereotypic narrative (63). Finally, changes in bias levels from baseline were positively correlated with the number of contacts in participants’ cell phones at 12 h—for participants from small cities from whom biases were reduced by the intervention—at the first follow-up (rs(n=132)=0.196, P=0.024), indicating that the effect of the intervention persisted longer for participants with fewer contacts (SI Appendix, Tables S25 and S26). To summarize, the effect of the intervention faded more quickly for participants with more access to cultural information, as proxied by the number of contacts in their cell phone and the population size of their city.

Discussion.

As modern societies claim to strive toward equal access to opportunity, it is important to build theories that can facilitate critical examinations of barriers to equity and interventions to reduce these barriers. Although the foundational models in many areas of social science are not quantitative (64), making sense of newly available large datasets of human behavior requires new quantitative frameworks (65).

Here, I contribute to this framework by developing a mathematical model of the dynamics of implicit bias change based on decades of observations from experimental psychology and urban science. The present work is a crucial step in understanding the basic dynamics and mechanisms of implicit biases, and it provides essential building blocks for developing new theories that can help us understand why, how, and when implicit biases change, and how we can design lasting interventions to reduce them. The broad success of this model in predicting observational and experimental data across scales suggests that such future work must take local context into account to be successful.

While there has been previous discussion of the role of context in shaping implicit biases (28, 66, 67), this work has argued that contextual effects overwhelm individual differences. The model and the empirical tests of its predictions in the present work suggest instead that implicit biases change due to how individuals experience and accumulate cultural information, and that the pacing and content of cultural information are structured by context.

This reveals important facts about how implicit biases change over time. First, on average, biases change more slowly in larger cities even though larger cities facilitate more opportunities for diverse interactions (68, 69) and larger cities tend to have lower biases in the first place (55). The model proposed here suggests that this is because 1) implicit biases are associated with low out-group expertise and 2) larger cities facilitate many more interactions than smaller cities (41, 42, 45). As a result, a greater exposure to bias-changing cultural information is needed to generate the same change in implicit biases in a large city as in a small city because of both higher social interaction rates and lower bias levels in larger cities. In contrast, a naive extrapolation of previous implicit bias research would suggest that implicit biases change more quickly in larger cities precisely because of point 2).

Second, the effects of one-off interventions to reduce biases fade more quickly in participants with greater access to cultural information. Here, this resulted in longer-lasting effects in participants from smaller cities and participants with fewer contacts stored in their cell phones. This matches the observation of slower changes in biases in larger cities, since larger cities facilitate a faster pace of interactions and exposure to cultural information (39, 42, 45). As a result, implicit biases are harder to change in individuals living in large cities since this rapid exposure reinforces existing cultural and societal contexts. This observation runs counter to previous results (24, 50) which suggested that the effects of interventions are too brief for practical purposes. Instead, in small cities or with individuals with less access to cultural information, interventions may be effective if administered close enough to relevant outcomes [e.g., hiring decisions (16)]. These results demonstrate that interventions must match intensity and frequency to individual and social contexts to be effective, and should serve as a basic design principle for future interventions.

The results of the present work also suggest next steps to better understand the recent global reduction in some implicit biases (5, 20, 21). First, experiments are needed that can clarify what features of naturalistic exposures (e.g., which aspects of movie roles, viral clips, novels, etc.) lead to changes in implicit bias levels (46, 47, 49). Second, future work should focus on developing a better understanding of when and why certain pieces of cultural information are rapidly disseminated in real-world urban settings [as opposed to online (70–72)], while other information has more structured dissemination. Third, while the present work validates the existence of environmental influences on individual bias change (Fig. 5) and characterizes the average dynamics of bias change for entire cities (Fig. 3), future work is needed to characterize individual differences in trajectories of implicit bias change in response to repeated cultural exposures in naturalistic and experimentally controlled settings. Finally, replicating the observational and experimental results beyond the United States will help to reinforce our understanding of implicit biases globally and help reveal further moderating factors beyond city population. We may expect such replications to corroborate the findings of the present work based on past work in urban scaling which generalized similar patterns and dynamics beyond the United States across cultures and time (41–44).

At a time when the focus on reducing implicit biases to create more equitable societies has come into question, it is necessary to develop interdisciplinary quantitative theories that explain the origins and dynamics of implicit biases and how they translate into differential access to opportunity. Such models are necessary prerequisites if we hope to rigorously evaluate the societal impacts of implicit biases and to design effective interventions that lastingly reduce them.

Materials and Methods

Ethics Approval.

The use of Project Implicit IAT data in the present study was approved by the University of Chicago Social Sciences Institutional Review Board (IRB23-0796). Project Implicit data collection was approved by the University of Virginia Institutional Review Board for the Social and Behavioral Sciences. The bias intervention experiment was approved by the University of Chicago Social Sciences Institutional Review Board (IRB25-0519).

Observational IAT Data.

All IAT Data are publicly available and were downloaded from https://osf.io/y9hiq/. These data are coded at the participant level, a fraction of which includes geographic identifiers for state and county. Biases were assessed by the Dbiep metric which consists of the latency difference between compatible and incompatible blocks of the IAT, divided by the pooled SD. For example, in the racial IAT, higher and positive scores indicate an implicit bias toward White faces while lower and negative scores indicate an implicit bias toward Black faces.

U.S. Census Data.

All census data is publicly available and was downloaded from data.census.gov. Five-year population estimates for U.S. cities defined as combined statistical areas (CBSAs) were downloaded from table B01003. Five-year estimates of the number of city inhabitants in each age group were downloaded from table S0101. Five-year estimates of disability status were downloaded from table S1810. In order to map between county and CBSAs, delineation files for 2020 were downloaded from the United States Office of Budget and Management from https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/metro-micro/about/delineation-files.html.

Film Media Representation Data.

Data on representation in film media was downloaded from the GitHub repository for (58) from https://github.com/dbamman/movie-representation/blob/main/data/ci_by_year.txt. In that study, the authors used machine learning to extract faces from 2,307 DVDs released between 1980 and 2022. They inferred gender by matching faces to attestations of gender on Wikidata. They inferred race and ethnicity via human ratings on 90% of the dataset and extrapolation to the remaining 10%.

Measuring Rates of Change.

In order to measure how quickly implicit biases change in different cities, only Project Implicit data from participants who shared their geographic information was retained. Next, data from cities with at least 250 responses over 14 y was retained. From these data, a moving average of the number of responses, mean Dbiep score, and the SD in scores were computed for various window sizes from a minimum of 30 to a maximum of 360 d. Next, rates of change were calculated for each city by a weighted least squares regression of Dbiep versus time, over a moving window of the same size. Regression weights were the inverse of the squared SE of the mean Dbiep at each time point. Observations of rates of change were retained if the regression-derived P-value was less than 0.05. Additionally, observations of rates of change were discarded regardless of P-value if|∂tb|<M⟨σ⟩medianW(W2−1)12,

where M is a data inclusion factor that took on values of 1, 1.5, or 1.96, ⟨σ⟩median is the median of the SE of the mean for observations in the window of size W. The factor of W(W2−1)12 comes from the fact that the SE of a regression slope is equal to the SD of the residuals (which is less than or equal to ⟨σ⟩median) divided by (xi−⟨x⟩)2. This last expression returns the desired dependence on W when the xi are evenly spaced from 1 to W.

The measured rate of change and the SE estimate of the rate of change from the regression were retained for the next processing step. Finally, the scaling exponent for rates of change was estimated via weighted least squares regression of ln |∂tb| versus lnN across a moving window of the same size, W. The inverse of the SEs from the previous step were used as weights. For each IAT type, multiple comparison testing was used to retain significant scaling exponents across time points at a significance threshold of 0.001. To estimate CIs for the mean of the distribution and the percent of the distribution below 0, 1,000 bootstrap resamples were used, where each iteration resamples over which cities’ entire time series of ∂tb are included.

Measuring Scaling Parameters Over Time.

To measure the scaling exponent and scaling prefactor over time, an ordinarily least squares regression to ln b versus lnN was performed over a moving window. Parameter estimates with regression P-values less than 0.05 were retained. The angle of trajectories in phase space was calculated by the quadrant aware inverse tangent function. To align parallel trajectories with opposite signs, 180 degrees was added to negative angles. CIs were computed by 5,000 bootstrap resamples.

Phase Space Clustering and Angles.

The number of phase space clusters for each IAT was found using KMeans clustering and scree plots. Three procedures were applied to refine these clusters. First, the horizontal distance between clusters was computed and clusters with a horizontal distance of less than 5 were combined. with cluster regression slopes, m, and cluster regression intercepts, c. Second, with the new number of clusters, KMeans was run again, and individual points were reassigned to the cluster for which they were the closest to the best fit regression line. Finally, outliers from each cluster were removed to a new cluster by iteratively fitting a regression line to the cluster and moving points outside 2 times the SD of the residuals to their own cluster.

To measure whether trajectories at certain angles were more likely to be within or between clusters, all angles were first converted to an angle relative to horizontal by taking 90−|90−θ|. Next these angles, each representing a phase space trajectory, were assigned a label of between or within cluster, only considering start and end points not in one of the residual outlier clusters. Finally, these angles were binned into 10 equal-count bins and then a two-sided test for a difference in proportions based on a normal distribution z test was carried out for each bin.

Experimental Data.

Experimental data was collected via a Qualtrics survey and participants were recruited via the Prolific platform. Informed consent was obtained from all participants and the data collection procedures were approved by the University of Chicago Social Sciences Institutional Review Board (IRB25-0519). Participants were randomized to read one of ten counterstereotypic narratives during the initial phase of the experiment. The experiment and analysis plans were preregistered https://aspredicted.org/rnbm-6ptg.pdf. After the initial collection of 266 participants, the collection was extended to an additional 884 and additional follow-ups up to 9 d from the initial phase were added. All other analyses and procedures remained identical to what is described in the preregistration. The implicit association test was implemented with the MinnoJS qualtrics IAT script https://minnojs.github.io/minnojs-blog/qualtrics-iat/. The Dbiep score was calculated as the average between the practice and test block Dbiep scores. Scores for each block were computed as the difference in reaction time between incongruent and congruent trials divided by the pooled SD of reaction time for each participant. Because of the high variance in return times on the same day as the initial survey, data from the 6 and 12 h timepoints were combined.
