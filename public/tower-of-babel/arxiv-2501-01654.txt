Fundamental polytope for the isometry group of an alcove
Lucas Seco, Arthur Garnier, Karl-Hermann Neeb

Abstract
. A fundamental alcove 𝒜 \mathscr{A} is a tile in a paving of a vector space V V by an affine reflection group W aff W_{\mathrm{aff}} . Its geometry encodes essential features of W aff W_{\mathrm{aff}} , such as its affine Dynkin diagram D ~ \widetilde{D} and fundamental group Ω \Omega . In this article we investigate its full isometry group Aut ⁡ ( 𝒜 ) \Aut(\mathscr{A}) . It is well known that the isometry group of a regular polyhedron is generated by hyperplane reflections on its faces. Being a simplex, an alcove 𝒜 \mathscr{A} is the simplest of polyhedra, nevertheless it is seldom a regular one. In our first main result we show that Aut ⁡ ( 𝒜 ) \Aut(\mathscr{A}) is isomorphic to Aut ⁡ ( D ~ ) \Aut(\widetilde{D}) . Building on this connection, we establish that Aut ⁡ ( 𝒜 ) \Aut(\mathscr{A}) is an abstract Coxeter group, with generators given by affine isometric involutions of the ambient space. Although these involutions are seldom reflections, our second main result leverages them to construct, by slicing the Komrakov–Premet fundamental polytope 𝒦 \mathscr{K} for the action of Ω \Omega , a family of fundamental polytopes for the action of Aut ⁡ ( 𝒜 ) \Aut(\mathscr{A}) on 𝒜 \mathscr{A} , whose vertices are contained in the vertices of 𝒦 \mathscr{K} and whose faces are parametrized by the so-called balanced minuscule roots, which we introduce here. In an appendix, we discuss some related negative results on stratified centralizers and equivariant triangulations.

The idea is to observe that a nontrivial involution

Thus,
\Pi_{0}

\phi_{0}

\mathscr{L}

.

\mathscr{K}

[
1

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}v_{0}=v_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}(\alpha_{i}+\alpha_{n+1-i})&\text{if $\Phi=A_{n},n\geq 2$},\\[5.0pt]

1.1.
Preliminaries

A set of
n+1
vectors
\{p_{0},p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}\}\subseteq V
is in general position if any of its proper subsets is l.i. A simplex
\sigma
of
V
is the convex hull of a set of
n+1
vertices in general position,
\sigma=\conv(\{p_{i}\})
. There exists a unique point
q_{*}
equidistant to the vertices of
\sigma
, it is the center of the sphere
in which
\sigma
in inscribed.
Also, there exists a unique point
p_{*}
equidistant to the faces of

\sigma
, it is the center of the sphere inscribed in
\sigma
, which is tangent to each face of
\sigma
at a unique point.
Each vertex
p_{i}
of
\sigma
has an opposite face
\sigma(p_{i})=\conv(\{p_{j}\mid j\neq i\})
with unit inward normal
\nu_{i}
. Furthermore, the unit normals
\nu_{0},\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{n}
are in general position.

In the Euclidean group of rigid motions
\mathrm{E}(V)=V\rtimes\mathrm{O}(V)
, the vector space
V
is identified with the subgroup of translations via
t_{\gamma}(H)=H+\gamma
for
\gamma\in V
. Since
V
is normal in
\mathrm{E}(V)
, the projection to the linear part

(6)

\pi:\mathrm{E}(V)\rightarrow\mathrm{O}(V)

is a surjective homomorphism, equivariant w.r.t. the
\mathrm{O}(V)
-action by conjugation,
that is

(7)

\pi(\phi\zeta\phi^{-1})=\phi\pi(\zeta)\phi^{-1}

for
\zeta\in\mathrm{E}(V)
and
\phi\in\mathrm{O}(V)
.

An affine reflection group
W_{\mathrm{aff}}
of

is generated by the reflections on the hyperplanes of a locally finite and infinite collection

of affine hyperplanes of

that is invariant w.r.t. reflection on each one of its hyperplanes (see Chap. V of
[
1
]
). An alcove is then the closure of a connected component of the Stiefel diagram
V\setminus\bigcup_{H\in\mathscr{H}}H
.

The group

acts simply transitively on the set of alcoves
and any alcove is a fundamental domain for the action of

in
V
. We assume from now on that this action is irreducible so that alcoves are simplices.
Fix an alcove
\mathscr{A}
through the origin, which we will call a fundamental alcove.
Its inwards unit normals give rise to a Coxeter diagram
\widetilde{C}
.
We actually have more structure. Indeed, successive reflections on two successive paralell hyperplanes of

(8)

\mathscr{A}:=\{x\in V\mid(\forall i\in I)\ (\alpha_{i},x)\geq 0,\penalty\ (\alpha_{0},x)\leq 1\}.

It follows that

.

Clearly,

contains the Weyl group

Consider the

\Phi

of

2.
Affine diagram automorphisms and minuscule roots

In this section we describe how the affine diagram automorphisms and the minuscule roots are intimately related.

Recall the highest root
\alpha_{0}=\sum_{i>0}m_{i}\alpha_{i}
. We say that the root
\alpha_{j}
, or the coweight
\varpi^{\vee}_{j}
, is
minuscule
when the corresponding coefficient is
m_{j}=1
. Recall that (
17
)

\widetilde{W}:=W\cap\Aut(\widetilde{D})

acts simply transitively on the set of systems of simple roots
contained in
\{-\alpha_{0}\}\cup\Pi
. Since any such system has
n
elements, it is either
\Pi
or of the form

(19)

\Pi_{j}=\{-\alpha_{0},\,\alpha_{i}:\,i\neq j\}

The main result of this section is the following.

Theorem 2.1
.

\Pi_{j}
is a system of simple roots for
\Phi
if and only if the root
\alpha_{j}
is minuscule. In this case,
\alpha_{j}
is the lowest root of
\Pi_{j}
.

Proof.

\Pi_{j}
is clearly a linear independent set of vectors that spans
V
.
If
m_{j}=1
, we claim that every root
\beta\in\Phi
is
{\mathbb{Z}}
-spanned by
\Pi_{j}
with coefficients of the same sign. In fact, w.l.o.g.
\beta
is negative w.r.t.
\Pi
, thus it can be obtained bottom up from the lowest root
-\alpha_{0}
w.r.t.
\Pi
as

\beta=-\alpha_{0}+\sum_{i}c_{i}\alpha_{i}=-\alpha_{0}+c_{j}\alpha_{j}+\sum_{i\neq j}c_{i}\alpha_{i}=(c_{j}-1)\alpha_{j}+\sum_{i\neq j}(c_{i}-m_{i})\alpha_{i}

for integers
c_{i}\geq 0
, where
c_{i}-m_{i}\leq 0
. On the rightmost side, the
\alpha_{j}
coefficient of
\beta
in the
\Pi
-basis is
c_{j}-1\leq 0
, it follows that
c_{j}\in\{0,\,1\}
.
If
c_{j}=0
, then

\beta=-\alpha_{0}+\sum_{i}c_{i}\alpha_{i}

has non-negative coefficients on the
\Pi_{j}
basis.
If
c_{j}=1
, then

\beta=\sum_{i\neq j}(c_{i}-m_{i})\alpha_{i}

has non-positive coefficients on the
\Pi_{j}
basis.
In both cases the claim hold, thus
\Pi_{j}
is a system of simple roots.

Reciprocally, if
\Pi_{j}
is a system of simple roots then there exists unique integer coefficients
c_{i}
of the same sign such that

\displaystyle\alpha_{j}=c_{0}(-\alpha_{0})+\sum_{i\neq j}c_{i}\alpha_{i}

Since in the rightmost side the coefficient of
\alpha_{j}
in the
\Pi
-basis is
-c_{0}m_{j}
, it follows that
-c_{0}m_{j}=1
so that

-c_{0}=m_{j}=1
, thus
\alpha_{j}
is minuscule.

If
\Pi_{j}
is a system of simple roots we claim
\alpha_{j}
is its lowest root. In fact,
subtracting
\alpha_{i}
,
i\neq j
, from
\alpha_{j}
amounts to
\alpha_{j}-\alpha_{i}
, which is never a root, since
\Pi
is a simple system. Furthermore, subtracting
-\alpha_{0}
from
\alpha_{j}
amounts to
\alpha_{j}+\alpha_{0}
, which is never a root, since
\alpha_{0}
is the highest root of the simple system
\Pi
.
∎

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
\alpha_{j}
be a minuscule root. Then there exists a unique

v_{j}\in W
such that

(20)

v_{j}\Pi=\Pi_{j}

or, equivalently, such that
v_{j}\in\widetilde{W}
and

v_{j}(-\alpha_{0})=\alpha_{j}
.

Proof.

Since
\alpha_{j}
is minuscule,
\Pi_{j}
is a system of simple roots, by Theorem
2.1
. The uniqueness of
v_{j}
then comes from the simple transitivity of
W
on the set of systems of simple roots of
\Phi
.
Now let
v_{j}\in W
such that
v_{j}\Pi=\Pi_{j}
. Then
v_{j}
maps the lowest root of
\Pi
to the lowest root of
\Pi_{j}
so that, by Theorem
2.1
,
v_{j}(-\alpha_{0})=\alpha_{j}
, thus
v_{j}\in\widetilde{W}
.
Reciprocally, if
v_{j}\in\Aut(\widetilde{D})
then

v_{j}(\Pi\cup\{-\alpha_{0}\})=\Pi\cup\{-\alpha_{0}\}

If
v_{j}(-\alpha_{0})=\alpha_{j}
then we can remove

\{-\alpha_{0}\}
from the leftmost parenthesis and
\{\alpha_{j}\}
from the rightmost parenthesis to get
v_{j}\Pi=\Pi_{j}
, as claimed.
∎

The next result gives a topological characterization of the minuscule roots in terms of the affine Dynkin diagram.

Corollary 2.3
.

For a simple root
\alpha_{j}

(a)

\alpha_{j}
is minuscule.

(b)

It can be mapped to
-\alpha_{0}

\widetilde{D}
.

(c)

The diagram
\widetilde{D}\setminus\{\alpha_{j}\}
is
isomorphic to
D
.

Proof.

If
\alpha_{j}
is minuscule, then Corollary
2.2

implies that

is such that
f(\alpha_{j})=-\alpha_{0}
. It restricts to a diagram isomorphism
f:\widetilde{D}\setminus\{\alpha_{j}\}\rightarrow\widetilde{D}\setminus\{-\alpha_{0}\}=D
, so (a) implies (b), and (b) is equivalent to (c).
To see that (c) implies (a), assume that
there exists a diagram isomorphism
f:\widetilde{D}\setminus\{\alpha_{j}\}\rightarrow D
. Then
\widetilde{D}\setminus\{\alpha_{j}\}
is a
system of simple roots for
\Phi
. Since the roots of this diagram are

Appendix A
Stratified centralizers

Here we discuss some related negative results on stratified centralizers and equivariant triangulations.

Given a subgroup
G\subseteq\mathrm{E}(V)
denote the centralizer of
x\in V
by

G_{x}=\{g\in G\mid gx=x\}

In many instances, one wishes to know how
G_{x}
varies when
x
varies within a fundamental domain of
G
(see Remark
A.7
).
Suppose that
G
has a fundamental domain given by a polytope
\mathscr{F}
, which is stratified by its
k
-dimensional faces,
k=0,1,\ldots,n
.
The
regular face
of
x\in\mathscr{F}
is the intersection of all the faces of
\mathscr{F}
containing
x
. Note that the regular face of a
vertex of
\mathscr{F}
is the vertex itself.

Definition A.1
.

We say that
G
has
stratified centralizers
w.r.t. the fundamental polytope
\mathscr{F}
if
G_{x}=G_{y}
whenever
x,y\in\mathscr{F}
have the same regular face.

This is well known to hold for the affine Weyl group
W_{\mathrm{aff}}
w.r.t.
\mathscr{A}
. Indeed,
(W_{\mathrm{aff}})_{x}
is generated by the reflections in the top dimensional faces of
\mathscr{A}
containing the regular face of
x\in\mathscr{A}
(see Proposition V.§3.1(vii) p.80 of
[
1
]
).

What about the extended Weyl group
W_{\mathrm{ext}}
? For the Komrakov–Premet domain
\mathscr{K}
we will see, by presenting counterexamples, that this is not always the case. We start with some preparatory remarks. Recall from Section 1 that

(53)

W_{\mathrm{aff}}=Q^{\vee}\rtimes W\qquad W_{\mathrm{ext}}:=P^{\vee}\rtimes W=\Omega\rtimes W_{\mathrm{aff}}

Proposition A.2
.

For
x\in\mathscr{A}
, we have

(54)

(W_{\mathrm{ext}})_{x}=\Omega_{x}\rtimes(W_{\mathrm{aff}})_{x}.

Proof.

By (
53
),
\zeta\in W_{\mathrm{ext}}
decomposes as
\zeta=\omega\eta
, for
\omega\in\Omega
and
\eta\in W_{\mathrm{aff}}
. Since
x\in\mathscr{A}
,

\zeta x=x
implies that
\eta x=\omega^{-1}x\in\mathscr{A}
so that,
\eta x=x
(see Proposition V.§3.2(I) p.80 of
[
1
]
). It follows that
\omega x=x
, which proves our claim.
∎

Since
W_{\mathrm{aff}}
has stratified centralizers w.r.t.
\mathscr{A}
and since the regular face of
x\in\mathscr{K}
is contained in the regular face of
x\in\mathscr{A}
(which is not necessarily of the same dimension), it follows that
W_{\mathrm{aff}}
has stratified centralizers w.r.t.
\mathscr{K}
. Proposition
A.2
then implies the following.

Corollary A.3
.

The extended Weyl group
W_{\mathrm{ext}}
has stratified centralizers w.r.t.
\mathscr{K}
if and only if its fundamental group
\Omega
does.

Consider the Kac coordinates of
\mathscr{A}
given by

[b_{0},b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}]=b_{1}\varpi^{\vee}_{1}+\ldots+b_{n}\varpi^{\vee}_{n}\quad\text{with}\quad b_{0},b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}\geq 0,\quad b_{0}+m_{1}b_{1}+\cdots+m_{n}b_{m}=1

The top dimensional faces of
\mathscr{A}
are given by

\left(\alpha_{i},\,[b_{0},b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}]\right)=b_{i}=0\quad(i=1,\ldots,n),\qquad\left(\alpha_{0},\,[b_{0},b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}]\right)=m_{1}b_{1}+\cdots+m_{n}b_{m}=1

thus, have Kac coordinates
b_{i}=0

(i=0,\ldots,n)
.
Furthermore,
\mathscr{K}
has top dimensional faces
H^{0}_{j}\cap\mathscr{K}
given by

\displaystyle\left(\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{j},\,[b_{0},b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}]\right)=m_{1}b_{1}+\cdots+m_{n}b_{m}+b_{j}=1\quad(j\in J)

thus, with Kac coordinates
b_{0}=b_{j}

(j\in J)
.

Theorem A.4
.

For type
A_{n}
, the extended Weyl group

has stratified centralizers w.r.t. the Komrakov–Premet domain

if and only if

is prime.

Proof.

For type

we have

and recall from Table

that

generated by the cyclic permutation

\omega_{1}[b_{0},b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}]=[b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{n},b_{0}]

[

For each

In other words, if an element of

Now, when
