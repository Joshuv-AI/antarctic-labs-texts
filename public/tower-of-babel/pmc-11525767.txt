Use of extracorporeal membrane oxygenation during a decompressive hemicraniectomy for severe traumatic brain injury: illustrative case
Authors: Dan Y Draytsel, Justin Oh, Christopher T Tanski, Fadar Oliver Otite, Ethan Burke, Syed Ali, Fenghua Li, Timothy Beutler
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDDecompressive hemicraniectomy (DHC) can be a life-saving treatment for patients with severe traumatic brain injury (TBI) with a focal mass lesion who develop refractory elevated intracranial pressure (ICP). Nonetheless, successful completion of this procedure requires maintaining hemodynamic and respiratory stability. Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) use in patients with respiratory or cardiac failure is well described in the literature and has become routinely used in patients with refractory hypoxia unresponsive to traditional mechanical ventilation strategies, but few cases of its use have been reported in the neurosurgical literature.OBSERVATIONSHerein, the authors describe a unique case in which a man presented after an unwitnessed fall that caused severe TBI without a focal mass lesion. The patient subsequently developed medically refractory elevated ICP secondary to traumatic cerebral edema. He required DHC, but the procedure could only be completed safely with the utilization of intraoperative ECMO.LESSONSAs more is learned about the techniques and pitfalls of ECMO, its indications are rapidly expanding. The case presented describes the safe use of ECMO during a major neurosurgical procedure, showing that the technique can be completed safely and offering a therapeutic option for effectively addressing refractory hypoxia and hypercarbia in patients with severe TBI who require urgent or emergency neurosurgical procedures. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE24264

Decompressive hemicraniectomy (DHC) is a life-saving procedure that involves removing a large portion of the skull and opening the underlying dura to relieve intracranial pressure (ICP).1–3 Primary DHC is completed in response to a focal mass lesion such as a large traumatic intracranial hemorrhage or large stroke. The use and indications for primary DHC are well described in the literature and guidelines. The role of secondary DHC, surgery done in response to medically refractory intracranial hypertension in the absence of a focal mass lesion, has been more controversial.

The 2020 update to the brain injury guidelines on DHC recommended a large frontotemporal DHC, not less than 12 cm × 15 cm in diameter, to reduce mortality and improve neurological outcomes. Secondary DHC for late (> 72 hours) refractory intracranial hypertension was also recommended to improve mortality rates and favorable outcomes, whereas early secondary DHC (< 72 hours) was only recommended to improve mortality. Both early and late secondary DHC have been shown to be effective at reducing ICP and the intensive care unit length of stay.4

Although DHC has been shown to decrease mortality in patients, the risk of severe disability is significant, with quality of life emerging as an important consideration when decisions regarding surgery are made.1, 2, 5 After traumatic brain injury (TBI), DHC is associated with lower mortality than that following medical management but with higher rates of vegetative state or severe disability.2, 5 For intractable ICP after aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), early intervention, young age, and progressive brain edema without radiological signs of infarction have been found to be associated with better long-term outcomes.5–8

Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) is a life-saving therapy for respiratory and cardiac failure, first used in neonates in the 1970s.9 An ECMO machine pumps blood through a series of cannulas from the patient’s body to an artificial lung, which adds oxygen and removes carbon dioxide, replacing the function of both the lungs and the heart.10 There are two main types of ECMO support, venovenous and venoarterial. Venovenous ECMO (VV-ECMO) is primarily used either when the lungs cannot provide enough oxygen to the body despite supplemental oxygen or when the lungs cannot remove carbon dioxide despite the help of a mechanical ventilator. Venoarterial ECMO is used when the heart cannot pump enough blood to the body.10 Intraoperative ECMO has been gaining popularity for lung transplantation surgeries, replacing cardiopulmonary bypass as the most prominent approach.11 Given advances in ECMO technology, the potential prophylactic use of ECMO for lung protection during critical periods of severe illness is also being considered.11 During ECMO, blood is circulated outside of the body, where it encounters a large surface area of nonendothelial biosurface, which results in a prothrombotic state.12 Therefore, ECMO is traditionally used in conjunction with anticoagulation, though heterogeneity exists in anticoagulation practices and monitoring. Based on a recent cross-sectional survey of ECMO centers, unfractionated heparin (UFH) is the most preferred anticoagulation agent. UFH is then titrated based on activated coagulation time, activated prothrombin time, or anti-Xa test results. However, there are no established guidelines, and each center is encouraged to formulate an algorithm that works best for them.12 Because of the need for anticoagulation, craniotomy or craniectomy is often considered a relative contraindication.

Herein, we describe a unique case in which a male presented after an unwitnessed traumatic fall that caused severe TBI without a focal mass lesion who developed medically refractory elevated ICP. Initial DHC for ICP relief was aborted due to hypoxic and hypercarbic respiratory failure during positioning. A second attempt at surgery was successful with the use of intraoperative ECMO, with the ECMO team at the bedside.

Illustrative Case

A 25-year-old male with no significant past medical history was admitted after an unwitnessed traumatic fall from three stories and having been found unconscious at the scene. The patient underwent bystander cardiopulmonary resuscitation at the scene per emergency medical services (EMS), but a pulse was present at the time of EMS’ arrival. Upon arrival to the hospital, the patient was comatose and intubated because his Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score was low (score of 3). Initial preoperative computed tomography (CT) of the head showed a left occipital skull fracture with extension to the skull base, SAH in the anterior interhemispheric fissure, and evidence of diffuse cerebral edema (Fig. 1). CT angiography showed no evidence of vessel abnormality and good flow in the intracranial vessels. The initial trauma survey was unremarkable except for moderate soft tissue injury. Physical examination with sedation revealed the absence of brainstem reflexes with fluctuating pupils, trace extension of his right upper extremity, and a GCS score of 4T (eyes 1, verbal 1T, motor 2). An intracranial monitor was placed for ICP and brain tissue oxygenation monitoring. His brain tissue partial pressure of oxygen ranged from 17 to 29 mm Hg preoperatively. Medical management consisted of a tiered approach. The head of the bed was kept elevated, and the ventilator was titrated to maintain an arterial blood partial pressure of carbon dioxide level between 33 and 37 mm Hg. His sedation was optimized using video electroencephalography to titrate fentanyl, Versed, and propofol infusions to achieve a burst suppression pattern. The patient was also paralyzed and cooled to 35°C. Hyperosmolar therapy (mannitol and hypertonic saline boluses) was used to treat ICP elevations. The patient also had an external ventricular drain placed for continuous cerebrospinal fluid diversion.

Despite these measures, the patient’s ICP continued to be elevated and became more difficult to control. Secondary right-sided DHC was offered for the medically refractory intracranial hypertension. An initial attempt at the surgery was aborted due to hypoxia and hypercarbic respiratory failure that occurred when the patient was positioned supine for surgery. The likely cause of hypoxia was thought to be traumatic pulmonary contusions versus aspiration pneumonia resulting in acute respiratory distress syndrome. A chest radiograph at the time of the initial attempt revealed a significant interval increase in bilateral pulmonary opacities, with the right side greater than the left side (Fig. 2). This was likely exacerbated by restrictive lung ventilation from the patient's obese body habitus. Bronchoscopy was completed during the initial surgery attempt and was negative for evidence of lung infection and airway blockage. The patient was brought back to the operating room a second time with plans for initiating VV-ECMO prior to the DHC to allow for adequate oxygenation and ventilation during the surgical procedure. The DHC was performed with adequate oxygenation and ventilation maintained throughout the procedure. There was no administration of prophylactic or therapeutic anticoagulation during surgery given the high risk of intraoperative and postoperative hemorrhage. Postoperative CT revealed appropriate decompression with stable multicompartmental intracranial hemorrhages with no new intracranial bleeding or increase in midline shift (Figs. 3 and 4). Postoperative ICP and brain tissue oxygenation values were noted to be improved, and sedation could be weaned. ECMO was continued after surgery to optimize brain tissue oxygenation while the patient’s lung pathology had time to improve. ECMO was terminated 7 days postoperatively as the patient stabilized from a respiratory perspective. The ECMO circuit was maintained in the postoperative setting without the use of anticoagulation to minimize the risk of secondary intracranial hemorrhage, and there was minimal disruption of the circuit due to clotting. The patient’s neurological examination improved over time, and his sedation was weaned. He was successfully extubated on hospital day 27 and weaned off supplemental oxygen. Postextubation, he had a strong cough and was hypophonic but was able to whisper his name. The patient went on to have a percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy tube placed for dysphagia. One month after being admitted to the neurosurgical ward, he was transferred to acute inpatient rehabilitation. He recovered well with intensive rehabilitation and underwent cranioplasty placement 4 months after his DHC. Postoperative examinations after his cranioplasty showed that he had full strength in his bilateral upper and lower extremities. He was able to ambulate independently and carry out premorbid activities of daily living.

Discussion

Observations

There is limited literature on the use of ECMO in neurosurgical patients. ECMO intrinsically carries a risk of thrombotic complications requiring the use of anticoagulation, and invasive neurosurgical procedures or pathologies are a relative contraindication to its use. However, patients with severe TBI often present with multisystem injuries that must be treated in tandem. Our case report highlights the unique use of ECMO in an emergency setting of persistently elevated ICPs refractory to tiered medical management of TBI.

Current literature is limited to the use of ECMO postoperatively in patients who developed hemodynamic instability or refractory hypoxia following a neurosurgical procedure. The first involves a case report of a massive pulmonary embolism after revision lumbar fusion requiring ECMO due to hemodynamic instability from right heart strain.19 There are two case reports demonstrating the use of postoperative ECMO after a DHC in pediatric and adult patients.18 More recently, a patient with a ruptured cerebral aneurysm required ECMO prior to a diagnostic angiogram and craniectomy for clipping.13

Despite the complicated hospital course in the cases, these reports describe good neurological outcomes in critically ill patients. Our case is unique in that ECMO was specifically initiated because the patient’s body habitus and surgical positioning exacerbated his already tenuous respiratory status. A more recent study was published by Helmy et al. that retrospectively analyzed patients with neurosurgical pathologies that required ECMO intervention during or after surgery.14 Of the 24 patients included in that study, 15 (63%) survived to hospital discharge, and 12 of the 15 patients had good neurological outcomes.

Of the case reports mentioned, Yen et al. revealed that therapeutic anticoagulation was intentionally not administered to reduce the risk of hemorrhagic complications after a DHC.15 Hwang et al. reported that anticoagulation was initiated at the time of ECMO to reduce the risk of associated thrombotic complications.13 Yen et al. described standard frequent neurological assessments along with continuous electroencephalography monitoring while on ECMO and for 3 days postcannulation to monitor the patient’s neurological status.15 In our case, the decision was also made to withhold the use of continuous therapeutic anticoagulation. There is very limited reporting on the risks of intracranial hemorrhagic or thrombotic events in neurosurgical patients undergoing ECMO.

However, we can draw from the current literature about ECMO without the use of systemic anticoagulation. A recent systematic review by Olson et al. analyzed thrombosis and bleeding outcomes in adult patients undergoing ECMO therapy without systemic anticoagulation.16 Thrombotic events occurred in 9.5% of patients, while severe bleeding events occurred in 27.9% of patients.

Lessons

The case presented here is unique among the previously reported cases because ECMO was initiated prior to DHC and was used specifically to complete the surgical procedure. Our case illustrates several lessons regarding the use of ECMO in neurosurgical procedures, including the importance of case selection, neurological monitoring, individualized treatment protocols, and consideration of novel applications of established treatments.

The first lesson from this case regards the importance of case selection. This case demonstrated that ECMO could be effectively initiated and used for cardiopulmonary support in a critically ill TBI patient with medically refractory ICP. Assessment of polytrauma injuries and preinjury comorbidities is important when counseling families on expected hospital courses and functional outcomes.

Second, this case highlights the importance of developing effective multimodal neurological monitoring strategies for patients placed on ECMO. Patients on ECMO are at risk for developing adverse neurological events, including intracranial hemorrhage, acute ischemic stroke, seizures, diffuse cerebral edema, and hypoxic brain injury. Therefore, there is a need for neurological monitoring for patients who are on ECMO. This can be challenging due to the severity of critical illness and the need for deep sedation and/or paralysis. The literature on this topic remains limited and lacks consensus on the best monitoring practices. Given how important neurological and functional outcomes are for clinical decision-making, the development of standardized multimodal neurological monitoring is important in the care of ECMO patients.17

Third, this case highlights the importance of developing individualized and tailored treatment protocols for each patient that are guided by evidence-based guidelines. Both TBI and ECMO have been shown to increase the risk of acute thrombosis, yet no thrombotic complications were seen in our patient, even without the use of anticoagulation. This is a key observation, showing that providers must exercise judgment and weigh the risks and benefits of treatment options.

Finally, this case shows the importance of novelty and innovation. ECMO has been around for many years and has been used with increased frequency in other specialties. There have been few reports of its use in neurosurgical procedures due to the relative contraindication of requiring systemic anticoagulation. Only one previously published case described the use of ECMO intraoperatively for a patient undergoing a DHC, but this case is unique because the patient was put on ECMO specifically to complete the DHC safely. This case highlights the circumstances, time line, and outcomes for such a procedure and adds to this body of literature showing that ECMO can be used effectively to achieve good surgical and neurological outcomes.

Our patient was able to undergo a successful DHC for severe TBI with refractory intracranial hypertension, despite his hypoxic and hypercapnic respiratory failure. He was able to be successfully monitored without any adverse bleeding events and decannulated once hemodynamically stable. After recovery from acute respiratory failure and treatment of his intracranial hypertension, his neurological status improved. He could be discharged to acute inpatient rehabilitation and eventually was discharged home. To our knowledge, this is the first report of its kind that used ECMO during a DHC and provides evidence of the success of intraoperative ECMO during neurosurgical procedures.
