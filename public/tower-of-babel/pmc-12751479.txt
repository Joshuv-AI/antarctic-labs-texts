Relationship of dietary macronutrient intake with the relative abundance of bifidobacteria and lactobacilli in breast milk of normal-weight mothers compared to the obese: a case-control study
Authors: Fatemeh Ammari, Seyedeh Neda Mousavi, Zahra Abbaspour rad, Davoud Afshar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLactobacillus and Bifidobacterium are members of the probiotic bacteria with many health benefits. Breast milk is an important source of these beneficial genera, which are affected by several factors. In the present study, the population of these genera was measured in milk of obese vs. normal-weight lactating women and their relationship with dietary macronutrient’s intake was investigated.MethodsTwenty women with high body mass index (BMI) enrolled as obese (≥ 30 kg/m2) and 20 women with normal BMI (18.5–24.9 kg/m2) were selected as controls. Breast milk samples were collected 4 months after delivery (due to stabilization of milk microbiome). Dietary information was collected using a food frequency questionnaire. DNA was extracted from milk. The population of Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus was measured against 16 S rRNA gene amplification method.ResultsBifidobacterium was significantly higher in breast milk of controls than the obese (p = 0.04). Bifidobacterium was 89% lower in breast milk of obese mothers than the controls, adjusting for the assessed parameters (p < 0.001). Pre-pregnancy weight showed a significant effect on the population of Bifidobacterium (p < 0.001). Energy, protein, carbohydrates, total fiber, soluble and insoluble fiber showed a positive effect on Bifidobacterium population, while dietary fat showed a negative effect (p < 0.001 in all cases).ConclusionsThe breast milk population of Bifidobacterium is associated with maternal weight. Maternal dietary intake of energy, carbohydrate, protein and fibre showed a positive effect on this genus, while dietary fat showed a negative effect.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40795-025-01226-8.

Introduction

Breast milk is a rich source of nutrients and bioactive compounds, including antimicrobial proteins and some oligosaccharides, as a source of prebiotics [1, 2]. It has now been proven that breast milk contains bacteria, which play an important role in colonizing the infant’s gut after birth [3–5]. The gut microbiota determines the maturity of immune, neuroendocrine and cardiovascular systems [6]. One study showed that the composition of breast milk microbiota in obese mothers is different than the normal-weights and it is affected by the sex of infant [7]. Another study reported that the Staphylococcus, Streptococcus, Corynebacteria, and Propionibacteria population remained unchanged, while the Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus were variable in the milk of mothers [8]. Bifidobacterium, anaerobic bacilli, related to the Actinobacteria family that is commonly colonized in various tissues in the body, such as the oropharynx, colon, genitourinary tract, and breast milk [9]. The presence of Bifidobacterium in the gut has beneficial effects on health [10]. Some medical effects are related to the Bifidobacterium population such as preventing the intestinal infections, preventing or reducing diarrhea, reducing disorders caused by liver failure, reducing blood cholesterol levels, and reducing cancer compounds. Moreover, this genus has immunological effects by strengthening the immune system and reducing the effects of lactose intolerance [11]. Lactobacilli are members of the Firmicutes family which are considered as the main component of milk flora. This genus has positive effects by preventing infectious diseases and balancing the infant’s intestinal flora through producing antimicrobial compounds [12]. The beneficial effect of bifidobacteria and lactobacilli as the probiotics has been highlighted in previous studies; however, there is no study to measure their population in human milk after delivery in Iranian population. The population of bacteria is varying depending on the subject’s ethnicity, genetic, geographical status, lifestyle, maternal anthropometrics and the time of lactation. The present study was designed to compare the breast milk biofidobacteria and lactobacilli population in obese lactating mothers compared to the normal-weight, living in Iran.

Materials and methods

The present study has been ethically approved by the ethics committee of Zanjan University of Medical Sciences under the code of IR.ZUMS.REC.1403.083. All procedure has been performed based on the Helsinki guidelines. Sampling was performed in spring and summer 2024, to omit seasonal effects on the dietary choices and bacterial composition of milk. Participants were included in the study after obtaining oral and written informed consent and based on the inclusion criteria. A total of forty women on the fourth month of lactation were enrolled, which half of them were obese (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2), and others were normal-weight (18.5 ≤ BMI ≤ 24.9 kg/m2). Mothers who delivered term and single baby, aged 18–40 years, exclusively breastfeeding, attending to the health centers for the child growth assessment were participated. Participants, who were not smoking, addicted to substances, or alcohol/ any drug consumers were included. Healthy participants without any diagnosed disease or disorder were matched on the route of delivery. Half of participants delivered vaginally and others by the cesarean section in both groups. Participants were allowed to withdraw from the study at any stage. Participants who consumed probiotic, prebiotic, symbiotic or antibiotics at least during the past two months were excluded. Mothers with a large or small baby for gestational age were also excluded. The study diagram has been shown in (Fig. 1).

Anthropometric measures of mothers and infants were recorded. Dietary intake was assessed by a food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) (See supplementary file). All samples were collected from Zanjan city, Iran, as previously reported. Milk samples were gathered after breast sterilization with chlorhexidine 2%, from both breasts. The DNA extraction and the amplification were conducted according to the previous method described by Karami, et al. [13]. The bacterial primers are presented in Table 1. The primers were verified in the primer-BLAST database of the National Centre for Biotechnology Information (NCBI). ABI Step-One sequence detection system (Applied Biosystems, California, USA) was used for the qPCR. A total volume including 10 µL of SYBR Green Master Mix (Amplicon, Denmark), 4 µL of DNA template, and 1 µL of each forward and reverse primer. 4.5 µL purified water free of DNA and RNA was prepared in a microtube. The cycle threshold (CT) values normalized to 16 S rRNA as a negative control. The initial denaturation included one cycle: 95 °C for 10 min. The amplification profile included 40 three-step cycles, including denaturation, annealing, and extension steps: 95 °C for 30 s, 60 °C for 30 s and 72 °C for 30s. The final extension was provided in one cycle: 72 °C for 30 s. Sample size and statistical analysis

Considering the power of 80% and type 1 error, based on a previous study [7] on the bacterial profile of human milk, twenty participants were considered in each group. Participants were matched by race, geographical region, age, stage of breastfeeding, breastfeeding method (exclusive breastfeeding), weight gain during pregnancy, and delivery method (cesarean/natural). Dietary information was inserted into the N4 software (Nutritionist 4, USA). Data analysis was performed by SPSS software, version 18. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to assess the distribution form of data. In the normally distributed data, an independent sample t-test was used to determine differences between the two groups. However, the Mann-Whitney test were used for the non-normal distributed-one. Qualitative variables were compared by the chi-squared test. A linear mixed model was used to determine the effect of each assessed parameter on the milk phyla. The Pearson correlation test was used to assess the two-way correlation between energy and macronutrient intake adjusted for energy on the bacterial genera.

Due to the significant difference between the amount of energy intake in two studied groups, intake of macronutrients including carbohydrates, fat, protein, total fiber, soluble and insoluble fiber were adjusted. After adjusting, the Bifidobacterium population in obese lactating mothers was 89% lower than the controls. Pre-pregnancy weight showed a significant effect on the Bifidobacterium population, and the current weight of mothers during breastfeeding reduced this population by 35%. Energy, protein, carbohydrates, total fiber, soluble and insoluble fiber showed a significant positive effect on the Bifidobacterium population (p < 0.001 in all comparisons), while dietary fat showed a negative effect (p < 0.001). In addition, none of the assessed factors showed a significant effect on the Lactobacillus population, neither in the obese nor in the normal group. Results of the correlation analysis showed that there was a significant direct correlation between energy-adjusted insoluble fiber and the population of Bifidobacterium in milk of the control group (r = 0.86, p < 0.001). In the obese group, a significant direct correlation was observed between energy intake and the population of Bifidobacterium (r = 0.56, p = 0.01). In the controls, a significant direct correlation was observed between energy-adjusted dietary insoluble fiber and population of Bifidobacterium (r = 0.95, p < 0.001). Moreover, a significant positive correlation was observed between energy-adjusted dietary insoluble fiber with the population of Lactobacillus in breast milk of obese mothers (r = 0.54, p = 0.01).

Discussion

The first study was carried out to investigate the relationship between dietary macronutrient intake and the amount of Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus in breast milk of mothers with normal BMI compared to the obese. All participants had the same genetics and race. All mothers exclusively breastfeed their infants. The anthropometric indices of infants had no significant difference between the two groups at the birth and time of sampling (4 months after birth). The results of the present study showed a statistically significant difference between the two groups in terms of daily calorie, carbohydrate, and total fibre intake. In terms of the bacterial genera, the amount of Bifidobacterium was significantly higher in the controls than in the obese. After adjusting for the effects of the baseline parameters, the Bifidobacterium population in obese lactating mothers was 89% lower than the controls. Pre-pregnancy weight showed a significant effect on the Bifidobacterium population. Maternal current weight was negatively associated with the population of Bifidobacterium. Energy, protein, carbohydrates, total fiber, soluble and insoluble fiber showed a positive effect on Bifidobacterium population, while dietary fat showed a negative effect on it. In addition, none of the assessed factors showed a significant effect on the Lactobacillus population. In general, there was a significantly direct correlation between the amount of insoluble fibre intake and Bifidobacterium population. In the obese participants, a significant direct correlation was observed between the amount of energy intake and Bifidobacterium. In the controls, a significant direct correlation was observed between the amount of insoluble fiber and this genus. Moreover, a significant positive correlation was observed between the amount of dietary insoluble fibre and the Lactobacillus population in the obese group. Despite the initial notions of the sterility of breast milk, breast milk is now recognized as a rich source of micronutrients, immunoglobulins, beneficial bacteria, and many other compounds, which have many benefits for health. Mature breast milk contains 6–7% protein, 42% carbohydrate, and 50% fat per 100 mL, and provides approximately 65–70 kcal of energy per 30 mL [14]. The bacteria in breast milk, as the first food source for the infant, determine the composition of the infant’s gut microbiome, which can play a very important role in the infant’s health/disease status. The gut microbiota is formed in the early stages of life and is influenced by internal factors such as delivery method, infant feeding method (breast milk/formula), weaning period, and environmental factors such as antibiotic use, maternal weight, physical activity, and diet. The mother’s delivery method, weight, and diet are among the most important factors affecting the composition of the milk bacteria during lactation [15]. One of the most important species belonging to the phylum Actinobacteria is Bifidobacteriumceae and Choriobacteriaceae, which have probiotic roles in the body and have many health benefits. Bifidobacterium is a subfamily of this bacterial phylum and is influenced by genetics, environment, diet, lifestyle, and intestinal physiology. Therefore, increasing the population of this bacterial genus can have many health benefits [16, 17]. Lactobacillus is from the class of Bacilli and belongs to the Firmicutes phylum. This bacterial genus also has many health benefits. However, in the present study, only a significant correlation between dietary insoluble fiber and the population of this bacterium was observed only in obese individuals. Therefore, it can be concluded that higher consumption of insoluble fibre in obese lactating mothers was able to show a positive relationship with the population of Lactobacillus in breast milk. As we know, microbiota is affected by age, race, geographical region, dietary composition, type and amount of food consumed, air pollution, and type of medication consumed, especially antibiotics, maternal breastfeeding status (exclusive breastfeeding or simultaneous use of formula milk), maternal exposure to pregnancy complications such as gestational hypertension, preeclampsia, gestational diabetes, and delivery method (cesarean/natural). Therefore, there is a need to conduct studies in all countries and even Iranian cities that have different climates and different dietary patterns. In the present study, participants were matched in terms of race, geographical region, age, stage of breastfeeding, breastfeeding method (exclusive breastfeeding), weight gain during pregnancy, and delivery method (cesarean/natural). They were evaluated in terms of dietary intake, and the amount of calories, carbohydrates, and total fiber intake was different between the two groups. In general, the composition of breast milk changes with each feeding of the infant, and at the beginning of breastfeeding, the amount of water in the milk is higher. The amount of fat in the milk is lower, which is called foremilk, and then at the end of each feeding, the amount of fat in the milk increases and it turns into complete and high-fat milk, which is called handmilk. In this way, the infant is automatically satiated as the fat content increases. The composition of breast milk depends on the mother’s food intake, so that even the type and amount of fat affects the type and amount of fat in the breast milk [18]. In the first six months after delivery, milk production is 750 mL per day. The mother’s daily energy requirement in the first six months of breastfeeding is 500 kcal more than the calories required in the pre-pregnancy period. According to the recommended dietary allowance (RDA), 15 g of additional protein is required for the mother in the first six months [19]. Carbohydrates are the main source of daily energy for the mother, which also helps in milk production. Carbohydrates are classified into three forms: monosaccharides, disaccharides, and polysaccharides. Monosaccharides include glucose (blood sugar), fructose (fruit sugar), and galactose (milk sugar linked to glucose). Major disaccharides include sucrose (table sugar), lactose (milk sugar), and maltose (malt sugar). The main polysaccharide in food is starch, the food sources of which are bread, rice, starchy vegetables, including potatoes, pumpkins, etc. Dietary fibre is a subset of carbohydrates that is classified into two categories: soluble and insoluble forms. Soluble fibre such as gum and pectin help reduce cholesterol and reduce appetite, and insoluble fibers such as cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin help relieve constipation by increasing water absorption in the intestine [20]. Importantly, dietary fiber is not digestible in the upper gastrointestinal tract and reaches the large intestine, where it is affected by colonic bacteria and produces metabolites called short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs). Acetate, propionate, and butyrate are the most abundant SCFAs produced in the gut (85% of the total SCFAs produced), and their production rates vary depending on the type and amount of bacteria living in the intestine. The roles of SCFAs in the body include increasing water and sodium absorption, increasing colonic cell proliferation, increasing metabolic energy, increasing colonic blood flow, stimulating the autonomic nervous system, increasing the production of digestive hormones, and producing beneficial flora that helps reduce pathogens [21]. The results of the present study differ from a study conducted in Brazil on 94 samples of breastfeeding mothers in terms of the relationship between the intake of polyunsaturated fatty acids and linoleic acid in mothers’ diet and the amount of Bifidobacterium in breast milk [22]. In this study, a significant direct correlation was observed between these two dietary factors and the amount of Bifidobacterium in milk, while dietary intakes of Poly-Unsaturated Fatty Acids (PUFAs) showed no significant association with this bacterium in our study. One of the reasons for this discrepancy could be related to the geographical area of ​​the studied samples, race, and type of food consumption. Differences in microbiota composition in people which can be related to the race are the main reason for the disparity across various studies. On the other hand, milk samples were collected in different months of breastfeeding, which could affect the results. It was also not stated whether the infants were exclusively breastfed or fed with complementary formulas. This is because even a single use of formula milk and a decrease in breast milk consumption can affect the breast milk bacterial composition. Another study was conducted on milk samples of mothers who were in the first trimester of breastfeeding and lived in Colombia [23]. This study showed an inverse relationship between dietary intakes of simple carbohydrates which are found in processed and refined sugars with the population of Bifidobacterium in breast milk. In our study, total dietary carbohydrate had a positive effect on the population of Bifidobacterium. One of the reasons for the difference in results could be the difference in geographical region, type of food consumed, and genetics of the individuals. However, the important point is that in the aforementioned study, simple carbohydrate intake showed this negative relationship with the Bifidobacterium population. However, in our study total carbohydrate intake, which also includes dietary fibre, showed a positive association with the population of this bacterium. A study in China has shown that the bacterial population of milk is different in residents of two different regions, which indicates the importance of geographical region in determining the composition of milk microbiota and reminds us of the need to conduct this type of study even in different cities of the same country [24]. The delivery method is also a very important factor in determining the milk microbiota. A recent study assessed the effect of delivery method on the milk microbiota one month after delivery and resulted in dysbiosis of the milk microbiota, reducing the population of beneficial bacteria and increasing pathogens in cesarean Sect [25]. In the present study, the number of women who delivered by cesarean section was equal to that of women who delivered vaginally in both groups, so the effect of delivery method on the composition of the milk microbiota was controlled. The present study was an observational study on an Iranian population living in a mountainous region. Similar to other studies, the present study has limitations. The design of the present study cannot determine a cause-and-effect relationship between macronutrient intake and the amount of Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus in milk. The results of this study cannot be generalized to other regions of Iran because race, geographical environment, and food consumption can affect the results. Relative abundance was assessed only at the level of two bacterial genera, and bacterial changes at the species, phylum, and examination of other bacterial genera can complement the results of the present study. In addition, it is recommended that future studies collect stool samples from infants to more accurately determine the relationship between these two bacterial genera in breast milk and the infant gut microbiome. Clinical trials using diets or supplements to alter the composition of breast milk microbiota are recommended for future studies. Since the effects of changes in the infant gut microbiome are persistent, it is recommended to follow these infants into early childhood and examine the relationship of these bacteria with child growth and anthropometric indicators. In general, the maternal diet during pregnancy and lactation has lasting effects on the metabolism of the next generation. Even following an appropriate diet after weaning cannot eliminate the detrimental effects of diets during pregnancy and lactation.

Conclusions

The population of milk Bifidobacterium differed between the obese and normal-weight mothers in the fourth month of breastfeeding. Mothers with a normal weight had higher levels of Bifidobacterium than the obese mothers. The pre-pregnancy weight had a significant effect on milk Bifidobacterium. The amount of dietary energy, proteins, carbohydrates, and fibre, including soluble and insoluble, showed a positive effect on the population of Bifidobacterium, while the amount of fats had a negative effect. There was also a significant direct correlation between the amount of insoluble fiber and milk Lactobacillus population. More studies are needed to reach a concise result.
