Contrasting temporal dynamics of fluorescence and photoacoustic signals from Cetuximab-IRDye800 conjugate in EGFR-overexpressing tumors
Authors: Mohammad A. Saad, Derek Allen, Allison Sweeney, Marvin Xavierselvan, Srivalleesha Mallidi, Tayyaba Hasan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Molecular fluorescence imaging is effective for tumor diagnosis but limited by low-depth profiling, which can be addressed by photoacoustic (PA) imaging. However, PA imaging has low sensitivity due to microenvironment-induced effects on exogenous contrast agents. Accordingly, the temporal and biophysical determinants of PA contrast in Cetuximab-IRDye800 conjugates should be performed to complement fluorescence-based diagnostics. In this study, we compare the temporal dynamics of PA and fluorescence signals from a Cetuximab-IRDye800 conjugate in a tumor xenograft model. We demonstrate that while fluorescence signal increases steadily over time after administration of Cetuximab-IRDye800, PA signal peaks early (~75% higher at 3 hours), followed by a decrease (~24% higher at 24 hours). Mechanistic analysis revealed formation of H-aggregates with Cetuximab-IRDye800 conjugation, which results in enhanced PA contrast, while receptor-mediated endocytosis disrupts these aggregates, reducing PA signal intensity over time. These findings underscore the complementary nature of PA and fluorescence imaging and emphasize timing as a critical factor for capturing peak PA contrast for tumor diagnostics.

INTRODUCTION

Fluorescence imaging, either through monitoring inherent variations in autofluorescence of diseased and healthy tissues or through externally administered fluorescent contrast agents, has been shown to improve diagnosis and tumor margin delineation during surgical resection (1–4). This is exemplified by the use of several fluorescent contrast agents [methylene blue, 5-aminolevulinic acid, and indocyanine green (ICG)] for imaging sentinel lymph nodes, vascular and lymphatic pathologies, and tumor tissues during surgical procedures (5–7). However, fluorescence imaging of externally administered contrast agents is limited by their nonspecific accumulation, leading to inaccurate diagnosis and margin definition (8). In this context, conjugating fluorophores to molecular-targeted agents has been shown to improve specificity (9–13), and several clinical studies are being conducted to study the efficacy of molecular-targeted fluorophores in head and neck cancer (NCT01987375), pancreatic cancer (NCT02736578), prostate cancer (NCT02048150), breast cancer (NCT01508572), and rectal cancer (NCT01972373).

In addition, lower depth profiling associated with fluorescence imaging has also been a limitation in applications which require real-time navigation (8). Complementing fluorescence with photoacoustic (PA) imaging provides the much-needed depth profiling to enhance spatial resolution (14–16). PA imaging relies on excitation of chromophores using nanosecond pulsed lasers, which induce transient thermal expansions that can be detected using ultrasound transducers (17). In essence, PA imaging combines optical and ultrasound imaging often proving advantageous due to the combined benefits of the two imaging modalities—sensitivity of optical imaging with the increased depth profiling of ultrasound imaging. PA imaging is in clinical trials to evaluate its diagnostic efficacy for several types of cancers including breast cancer (NCT03897270), thyroid cancer (NCT04248166), colon and rectal tumors (NCT04339374), and head and neck cancers (NCT04428515) amongst others. However, contrast-free PA imaging has limited sensitivity to molecular changes and reduced specificity in delineating tumors from healthy tissues. Several contrast agents have been proposed for PA imaging, but they lack tumor specificity due to nonspecific accumulation. More recently, molecular-targeted contrast agents, reported primarily for fluorescence imaging, have been studied for PA imaging in preclinical tumor models and clinical studies for in vivo and ex vivo identification of lymph node metastasis (18–21). However, contrast for PA imaging in clinical studies has been underwhelming (21).

In the present study, we compare the PA and fluorescence imaging capabilities of a Cetuximab-IRDye800 conjugate in a subcutaneous tumor xenograft model. Cetuximab is a clinically approved anti–epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)–targeted antibody, and Cetuximab-IRDye800 is in clinical trials for intraoperative fluorescence imaging of several cancer types including head and neck cancers (12, 22, 23). EGFR is a promising molecular marker, which has been used as a targeting molecule for imaging and therapeutic purposes due to its overexpression in several cancer types including head and neck cancers, pancreatic cancers, ovarian cancers, etc. (9, 11, 16). In addition, several near-infrared (NIR) dyes, including IRDye800, have been used as conjugates with Cetuximab for multimodal fluorescence and PA imaging (24, 25). Most commonly used fluorophores have also been used as contrast agents for PA measurements due to a notable quantum yield of nonradiative decay. Fluorescence properties of dyes depend on the radiative transfer of energy from the excited singlet state (S1) to the ground state (S0). In contrast, PA properties depend on the nonradiative transfer of energy either from the excited singlet state (S1) or the excited triplet state (T1), achieved through an intersystem crossing. In principle, NIR dyes with high fluorescence quantum yields (ΦFL) are often preferred for fluorescence-based imaging, while dyes, characterized by a relatively higher nonradiative quantum yield (ΦNR; quantified as 1 – ΦFL), can be used as PA contrast agents. Of the several fluorescent dyes in clinical use, ICG (ΦFL = 0.12) and IRDye800CW (ΦFL = 0.15) (26) are relatively better PA contrast agents and have been explored extensively for PA imaging. The imaging contrast of these agents can be significantly altered depending on their aggregation status, concentration, environment, and protein binding (19, 27, 28).

In the present study, using a Cetuximab-IRDye800 conjugate, we report that conjugation of IRDye800 to an antibody, even at the clinically relevant degree of labeling (DOL) of 2, facilitates its H-aggregation, thereby enhancing PA signal intensity. This has severe implications when applied to in vivo tumor imaging, as the PA contrast reduces when the Cetuximab-IRDye800 conjugate undergoes cellular internalization and degradation in the lysosomes. As conventional wisdom holds that prolonged incubation times (2 to 5 days in clinical studies) for antibody-dye conjugates are necessary for capturing peak fluorescence signal–to–background ratio, we demonstrate that this may negatively influence PA contrast. Through comparative microscopic tumor tissue analysis, we show that although the distribution of Cetuximab-IRDye800 in tumor sections is low at early time points, it, however, is specific to the tumor tissues. For these reasons, implementing Cetuximab-IRDye800–based PA imaging at early time points, postinfusion, may be ideal for diagnostic applications such as sentinel lymph node imaging to identify occult metastases.

RESULTS

Cetuximab-IRDye800 synthesis

Cetuximab-IRDye800 synthesis was performed by a previously established protocol (29–31) with slight modifications as described in Fig. 1A. The Food and Drug Administration–approved anti-EGFR antibody, Cetuximab, was used for specificity and targeting (32), and the synthesis protocol was optimized to incorporate IRDye800 with different DOLs. The DOL for IRDye800 was 2.25 ± 0.09, 4.20 ± 0.13, 4.93 ± 0.19, and 7.20 ± 0.29, achieved using dye-to-antibody ratios of 4, 8, 10, and 15, respectively (Fig. 1D). As shown in Fig. 1B, Cetuximab-IRDye800 showed absorption peaks corresponding to both Cetuximab and IRDye800. The purity of the Cetuximab-IRDye800 conjugates was confirmed by SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE; Fig. 1C), and as shown, the IRDye800 molecules were bound to both the heavy and light chains of the antibody with minimal (<2%) free dye in the solution. Increasing the IRDye800-to-Cetuximab ratio in the reaction mix led to a slight decrease in the conjugation efficiency of IRDye800 (Fig. 1D).

Contrasting temporal dynamics of tumor PA and fluorescence signals after Cetuximab-IRDye800 administration

Tumors were developed by subcutaneous implantation of A431 cells in the flank of Swiss nu/nu mice. After 2 weeks, tumor-bearing mice were imaged to record the background fluorescence and PA signal, followed by intravenous administration of Cetuximab-IRDye800 with a DOL of 2 (sample 1:2; Fig. 1D). The treated mice were then imaged immediately after administration (within 5 min for PA imaging) and at 1, 3, 6, 12, 18, and 24 hours postinjection for both fluorescence and PA imaging (Fig. 2A). As shown in Fig. 2B, mice treated with Cetuximab-IRDye800 showed an increase in IRDye800 fluorescence postinjection. Quantification of fluorescence images revealed a significant increase (~8000-fold) in fluorescence signal 6 hours post–Cetuximab-IRDye800 injection (0.0090 × 107 for preinjection versus 73.29 × 107 at 6 hours; P < 0.0001; preinjection versus 6 hours) (Fig. 2C and fig. S1A). The fluorescence signal showed a steady increase till the 18-hour time point (~14,000-fold increase over preinjection levels) beyond which it appeared to be saturated (Fig. 2C and fig. S1A). The first derivative of the fluorescence accumulation curve suggests that ~70% of tumor accumulation occurs within the first 6 hours of Cetuximab-IRDye800 injection (fig. S1B). To evaluate the specificity and sensitivity of the fluorescence signal, we performed receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis on the tumor fluorescence at different time points (fig. S1, C to I). While area under the ROC curve for preinjection (0.5), 1 hour (0.33), and 3 hours (0.69) postinjection was low, 6 hours post–Cetuximab-IRDye800 administration revealed a significantly higher area under the ROC curve (0.99; P = 0.0013), which further increased to 1 for the 12-, 18-, and 24-hour time points (Fig. 2D). This suggested that Cetuximab-IRDye800 fluorescence–based tumor detection is accurate at the 6-hour time point and beyond that. Our results also indicate that while the tumor fluorescence signal increases rapidly within 1 hour post–Cetuximab-IRDye800 administration, the specificity of the signal can only be achieved by 6 hours possibly due to clearance of the antibody from the nontumor tissue (background signal). This is in agreement with previous clinical and preclinical studies, which suggest that the time between infusion and tissue sampling does not influence intratumoral uptake of antibody-dye conjugates (33) but influences tumor-to-background fluorescence ratios due to clearance of the conjugates from healthy tissues (34).

We also performed longitudinal multispectral PA imaging of A431 subcutaneous tumors over 24 hours postinjection (Fig. 2A). PA images were acquired at four wavelengths (705, 780, 850, and 900 nm) to enable spectral unmixing of endogenous hemoglobin (Hb) from exogenous IRDye800 signal. At each time point, we captured three to five tumor cross-sectional frames per mouse, with the central frame positioned at tumor center and additional frames acquired laterally to comprehensively assess spatial heterogeneity in probe distribution and hemodynamic parameters. Representative longitudinal PA images demonstrate spatiotemporal evolution of both endogenous and exogenous contrast over 24 hours following Cetuximab-IRDye800 administration (Fig. 2 and fig. S2). Images are coregistered with ultrasound (grayscale) and overlaid with raw PA intensity at 705 nm (Fig. 2E) and 780 nm (Fig. 2G) plus spectrally unmixed signals for total hemoglobin (HbT) (fig. S2A) and IRDye800 (fig. S2B). B-mode ultrasound images provide anatomical context and confirm consistent tumor morphology across time points, establishing that observed PA signal changes reflect molecular and physiological alterations rather than anatomical changes. The PA signal intensity at 705-nm wavelength (characteristic of H-aggregated IRDye800) showed robust signal at baseline (preinjection), reflecting strong Hb absorption. Normalized PA intensity at 705 nm increased initially at 5 min (P = 0.1085), followed by sustained elevation peaking at 6 hours [1.15 ± 0.30 arbitrary units (a.u.), preinjection versus 6 hours; P = 0.0035], and then declined significantly by 24 hours (0.83 ± 0.20 a.u., preinjection versus 24 hours; P = 0.0229) (Fig. 2F). The 780-nm wavelength exhibited similar patterns: early increase, sustained elevation through 6 hours (1.15 ± 0.30 a.u., preinjection versus 6 hours; P = 0.0017), and declined by 24 hours (1.02 ± 0.27 a.u., preinjection versus 24 hours; P = 0.9950) (Fig. 2H).

HbT images, derived from spectral unmixing of oxygenated and deoxygenated Hb, revealed intriguing temporal dynamics. At baseline, tumors displayed moderate HbT signal. Notably, from 5 min through 6 hours, bright focal regions appeared, suggesting increased blood volume or Hb concentration. These heterogeneous features were particularly prominent at 1 to 3 hours and may represent enhanced perfusion or vascular pooling following Cetuximab-IRDye800 administration. The PA signal intensity became more homogeneous at later time points (12 to 24 hours), with overall signal diminishing slightly. Small darker regions within some tumors likely correspond to necrotic or poorly vascularized areas. Quantitative HbT measurements confirmed early vascular perturbations with significant increases at 3 hours (preinjection versus 3 hours; P < 0.0001) postinjection (fig. S2C). Tumor oxygen saturation (sO2) remained stable through 18 hours but showed significant increase at 24 hours (preinjection versus 24 hours; P < 0.0001) compared to baseline (fig. S2D).

IRDye800 imaging revealed rapid antibody-dye conjugate accumulation kinetics. At baseline, minimal IRDye800 signal was detected (fig. S2, B and E), as expected. An intense signal appeared by 5 min postinjection, with focal hotspots suggesting areas of high vascular delivery or preferential EGFR-mediated binding. Signal intensity peaked within 1 to 3 hours (1.72 ± 1.61 a.u., preinjection versus 3 hours; P = 0.0338) post–Cetuximab-IRDye800 administration, with extensive distribution throughout the tumor volume showing marked spatial heterogeneity—distinct regions of very high intensity interspersed with moderate or no signal. This heterogeneous pattern reflects variable vascular delivery, regional differences in EGFR expression density, and potential barriers to antibody penetration including stromal density and interstitial fluid pressure. IRDye800 showed a decrease at 6 hours but remained substantially elevated above baseline. By 12 to 18 hours, IRDye800 signal became progressively diffuse and diminished, returning near baseline by 24 hours (1.24 ± 0.82 a.u., preinjection versus 24 hours; P > 0.9999). Quantitative analysis of baseline-corrected IRDye800 PA signal confirmed these observations, revealing rapid tumor accumulation with significantly higher signal as early as 5 min (preinjection versus 5 min; P = 0.0501) (fig. S2E). Signal remained elevated at peak levels through 1 to 3 hours (preinjection versus 3 hours; P = 0.0338)—approximately 75% above baseline. Following peak, IRDye800 signal gradually decreased by 6 hours (preinjection versus 6 hours; P = 0.3194) before returning to baseline by 12 to 24 hours (preinjection versus 24 hours; P > 0.9999). Frame-based quantification captured substantial spatial heterogeneity in Cetuximab-IRDye800 distribution, evidenced by visible signal intensity variations across tumor regions.

A comparison of PA intensity signals for multiple chromophores revealed important spatial relationships. Bright IRDye800 hotspots at early time points (5 min to 3 hours) spatially correlated with enhanced HbT signal, suggesting that high blood volume facilitates efficient antibody delivery. This supports vascular delivery as a critical determinant of antibody accumulation in initial hours postinjection. Conversely, regions with persistently low IRDye800 signal corresponded to poorly vascularized or necrotic regions, appearing as signal voids in HbT images. The 705- and 780-nm signals maintained more consistent intensity than IRDye800, with gradual temporal profiles and a PA signal peaking at 6 hours.

For a more robust analysis, we averaged all frames per animal and performed Friedman repeated-measures tests on animal-level means. At 705 nm, the overall temporal trajectory across all time points was significant (χ2 = 13.20, P = 0.040, n = 6 animals), and the decline phase from 6 to 24 hours was highly significant (χ2 = 12.56, P = 0.0014, n = 6 animals), with the 6- to 24-hour decrease confirmed by paired Wilcoxon test (P = 0.031) and paired t test (P = 0.003) (table S1). At 780 nm, the same temporal trends were observed but did not reach statistical significance at the animal level due to the limited sample size (n = 5 to 9) (table S2). Individual pairwise comparisons (e.g., pre versus 6 hours) were similarly underpowered at the animal level, which is a recognized limitation of small-animal longitudinal imaging studies. To further validate temporal variations in the fluorescence and PA signal intensity, we performed a two-way mixed-effects model analysis of variance (ANOVA) with modality (fluorescence versus PA unmixed IRDye800 signals) and time. Our results revealed a statistically significant variation (P = 0.0228; table S3), confirming that fluorescence and PA signals exhibit temporal variations following Cetuximab-IRDye800 administration. A similar trend of an early peak in PA signal intensity has been reported previously with a different molecular-targeted dual imaging probe (trastuzumab conjugated to BHQ3 and AF488) (14).

EGFR targeting is required for sustained PA signal enhancement

To determine whether the observed temporal PA signal pattern is specific to EGFR-targeted uptake or occurs with any antibody-dye conjugate accumulation in tumors, we performed control experiments using immunoglobulin G (IgG)–IRDye800 (a non-EGFR–targeting antibody conjugated with IRDye800) in the same A431 tumor model (fig. S3A). Fluorescence imaging of IgG-IRDye800–treated tumors showed progressive accumulation over time (fig. S3, B and C), demonstrating that the nontargeted conjugate does accumulate in tumors, likely via enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect. Quantification revealed significant increases in tumor fluorescence at later time points (pre versus 12 hours: P = 0.0388; pre versus 18 hours: P = 0.0317), confirming passive tumor accumulation.

We performed longitudinal PA imaging in A431 tumor-bearing mice receiving an IRDye800-conjugated isotype antibody (IgG-IRDye800). The representative PA images at 705 nm showed sustained bright signal through early-to-mid time points, peaking visually at 5 min and 1 hour before returning to baseline by 3 hours. Quantitative analysis revealed significant increases at 5 min (preinjection versus 5 min; P = 0.0032) and 1 hour (preinjection versus 1 hour; P = 0.0014) but importantly a nonsignificant change at the 3-hour and the 6-hour (P = 0.9977 and P = 0.5949, respectively) time point. However, a significant decline below baseline was observed by 24 hours (fig. S4, A and B). Similarly, 780-nm PA images demonstrated high signal intensity at the early time points, with sustained brightness from 5 min through 1 hour before returning to baseline by 3 hours. Significant elevations were observed at 5 min (preinjection versus 5 min; P = 0.0306) and 1 hour (preinjection versus 1 hour; P = 0.0005), with return toward baseline at later time points (fig. S4, C and D). HbT images showed bright focal regions at 1 hour, indicating localized increased blood volume—visually similar to the Cetuximab-IRDye800 group. This suggests that antibody administration itself, independent of dye conjugation, induces vascular responses. Quantitative HbT analysis revealed pronounced early response: significant increases at 5 min (preinjection versus 5 min; P = 0.0269) and 1 hour (P < 0.0001) before returning to baseline (fig. S4, E and F). The sO2 response pattern differed substantially from Cetuximab-IRDye800–treated animals. An initial significant decrease occurred at 5 min (P = 0.0382) from baseline (fig. S4G)—followed by recovery and subsequent significant increases at 12 and 24 hours (P < 0.0001 for both preinjection versus 12 and 24 hours). This biphasic response—initial deoxygenation followed by delayed hyperoxygenation—was not observed in the Cetuximab-IRDye800 group.

In addition, IgG-IRDye800–administered animals displayed predominantly lower IRDye800 signal throughout all time points, fluctuating around baseline levels. No bright hotspots or sustained elevation were apparent, contrasting sharply with the marked signal in Cetuximab-IRDye800–treated animals. A subtle, transient elevation was observed at 5 min but rapidly returned to baseline by 1 to 3 hours. Quantitative analysis confirmed minimal IRDye800 PA signal throughout 24 hours (fig. S4, H and I). A transient significant elevation at 5 min (P = 0.0111) rapidly returned to preinjection levels by 1 hour and remained at baseline through 24 hours (all ns versus preinjection). This confirms IRDye800 detection specificity and validates that robust signals in the Cetuximab-IRDye800 group were attributable to IRDye800-conjugated antibody rather than autofluorescence or nonspecific background.

These control experiments demonstrate that (i) passive tumor accumulation via EPR effect is insufficient to produce sustained PA signal enhancement, (ii) the temporal PA signal pattern observed with Cetuximab-IRDye800 (peak at 1 to 6 hours followed by return to baseline) is EGFR specific and possibly requires receptor-mediated uptake, and (iii) receptor-mediated endocytosis potentially determines both the PA signal peak (via maintaining H-aggregation of the IRDye800 during cell surface binding and early uptake) and gradual fluorescence signal increase through dequenching via aggregate disruption during lysosomal degradation.

Variations in intratumoral distribution of Cetuximab-IRDye800 with time

On the basis of the temporal discrepancy between the fluorescence and PA signal maxima, we hypothesized that the decrease in PA signal from the 6-hour to the 24-hour time point may be due to variations in microscopic tumor localization rather than macroscopic tumor accumulation of the Cetuximab-IRDye800 conjugate. To assess the intratumoral localization of Cetuximab-IRDye800, we used a Cetuximab-IRDye800 conjugate with a molecule of Alexa Fluor 647 (Cet-IRDye-AF) incorporated to enable ex vivo imaging of the Cetuximab-IRDye800 conjugate. The synthesis and characterization of Cetuximab-IRDye-AF is shown in fig. S5. The DOL was 1.87 ± 0.17 and 0.87 ± 0.07 for IRDye800 and AF647, achieved using dye to antibody ratios of 4 and 2, respectively (fig. S5D). As shown in fig. S5B, Cetuximab-IRDye-AF showed absorption peaks corresponding to both AF647 and IRDye800. There was a spectral overlap between the two dyes where IRDye800 contributed to the AF647 absorption and a correction factor of 0.11 was applied for calculating AF647 concentrations. The interference of AF647 absorption with that of IRDye800 at 780 nm was minimal. The purity of the Cetuximab-IRDye-AF conjugate was confirmed by SDS-PAGE (fig. S5C), and as shown, both dyes were bound to the heavy and light chains of the antibody with minimal (<2%) free dye in the solution. Increasing the IRDye800-to-Cetuximab ratio in the reaction mix led to a marked decrease in the conjugation efficiency of AF647. The conjugation efficiency of IRDye800, however, was not affected with increasing IRDye800-to-Cetuximab ratio (fig. S5D). The Cetuximab-IRDye-AF conjugate showed similar temporal variations in the fluorescence and PA signal, with a steady increase in fluorescence signal for both IRDye800 (fig. S6, B to D) and AF647 (fig. S7) and an initial increase followed by a decrease in PA signal for IRDye800 (fig. S6, E to H).

The tumor tissues from Cetuximab-IRDye-AF–injected mice were harvested at either 6 or 24 hours postinjection and evaluated for microscopic distribution of Cetuximab-IRDye-AF conjugate. Uninjected mice tumors were used as controls for thresholding fluorescence signals. At both 6 and 24 hours post–Cetuximab-IRDye-AF administration, tumor fluorescence signal was significantly higher as compared to the fluorescence signal in the overlying skin or dermal tissue and surrounding muscle tissue (fig. S8), suggesting specificity of the Cetuximab-IRDye-AF conjugate, even at an early time point. At 24 hours postinjection, the mean tumor fluorescence intensity per pixel showed an increase, although not significant, as compared to 6 hours (Fig. 3C; P = 0.4661 for 6 hours versus 24 hours). In addition, the percentage tumor coverage of Cetuximab-IRDye-AF was higher (Fig. 3D), although not significant, suggesting higher distribution of the conjugate at 24 hours postinjection (P = 0.1124 for 6 hours versus 24 hours). We then calculated Cetuximab-IRDye-AF intensity normalized for vasculature (Cetuximab-IRDye-AF–to–CD31 ratio) to account for variations in the vasculature in the different sections. Simil r to the previous observations on Cetuximab-IRDye-AF intensity in the tumor and its percentage tumor coverage, the Cetuximab-IRDye-AF–to–CD31 ratio showed an increase (not significant; P = 0.0767 for 6 hours versus 24 hours) at 24 hours as compared to the 6-hour time point (Fig. 3E). We also calculated the Cetuximab-IRDye-AF distance from the nearest blood vessel (CD31) as a metric to quantify distribution. The Cetuximab-IRDye-AF distance from the nearest blood vessel was significantly higher in the 24-hour group as compared to the 6-hour group (P = 0.0323) (Fig. 3F). This suggests that at the 24-hour time point slightly more Cetuximab-IRDye-AF can enter the tumor and extravasate into deeper tumor layers farther away from the blood vessels. The Cetuximab-IRDye-AF–to–EGFR ratio, a parameter used to determine the number of available EGFR sites bound by the Cetuximab-IRDye-AF, also showed a slight but nonsignificant increase at 24 hours as compared to that at 6 hours (P = 0.1434) (Fig. 3G).

These data suggest that a 24-hour gap between infusion and tissue sampling increases microscopic in vivo fluorescence signal due to enhanced uptake and distribution of Cetuximab-IRDye-AF conjugate. The percentage tumor coverage of Cetuximab-IRDye-AF, Cetuximab-IRDye-AF intensity normalized for vasculature, and Cetuximab-|IRDye-AF–to–EGFR ratio all showed a significantly higher value for the 24-hour time point when compared to the preinjected controls. Although these parameters showed an increase at the 6-hour time point as well, the difference was not significant except for the mean tumor fluorescence intensity per pixel (Fig. 3C), which showed a significant increase at the 6-hour time point as well. These results do support the in vivo observations of increase in macroscopic tumor fluorescence with time; however, they failed to address the decrease in PA signal from the 6-hour to the 24-hour time point.

Variations in subcellular Cetuximab-IRDye-AF localization with time

To understand the contrasting variations in IRDye800 fluorescence and PA signals, we postulated that these differences could be due to variations in the microenvironment of the IRDye800 molecules. To gain insight into Cetuximab-IRDye-AF subcellular localization, we incubated Cetuximab-IRDye-AF with A431 cells in vitro and imaged for localization at different time points. As shown in Fig. 4A, the Cetuximab-IRDye-AF binds to A431 cell surface within an hour, followed by gradual endocytosis and localization in the lysosomes. This was analyzed by calculating the Pearson’s colocalization coefficient, which showed a gradual increase in lysosomal colocalization with incubation time. The lysosomal localization was found to be significantly higher at 24 and 48 hours post–Cetuximab-IRDye-AF incubation in comparison to that at the 1-hour time point (P = 0.0041 for 24 hours and P = 0.0002 for 48 hours versus 1 hour) (Fig. 4B).

In a separate experiment, we prepared cell lysates from Cetuximab-IRDye-AF–treated A431 cells at different time points and subjected them to SDS-PAGE analysis (Fig. 4D). As shown in Fig. 4D, bands corresponding to the free dyes (AF647 and IRDye800) appear at the 12-hour time point, suggesting that at the initial time points, the Cetuximab-IRDye-AF is either accumulated on the cell surface or is in an early stage of the receptor-mediated endocytosis pathway (Fig. 4C). As receptor-mediated endocytosis (RME) involves several steps including exposure to low pH followed by degradation through enzymatic action, we believe that the disruption of H-aggregates may be initiated during the early stages of RME through low-pH–mediated denaturation of the antibody. The relative band intensity of the free dyes increased at the 24-hour time point as compared to that observed at 6 hours post–Cetuximab-IRDye-AF incubation, suggesting degradation of Cetuximab-IRDye-AF upon lysosomal localization (Fig. 4E). These data suggest that the decrease in PA signal as observed in the in vivo study may possibly be due to the lysosomal localization, denaturation at low pH, and degradation of the Cetuximab-IRDye-AF conjugate.

Lysosomal uptake and cleavage of Cetuximab-IRDye800 reduces PA signal intensity

Having established the critical time points associated with Cetuximab-IRDye800 binding and cellular uptake, we performed fluorescence and PA imaging on A431 and T-47D (low expression of EGFR) cell phantoms pretreated with Cetuximab-IRDye800 for either 1, 3, 6, 12, 18, or 24 hours. Cetuximab-IRDye800–treated tumor cell phantoms were prepared by embedding 107 cells in gelatin and pouring that in a well of a 96-well plate (Fig. 5, A and B). Figure 5C shows the IRDye800 fluorescence signals from Cetuximab-IRDye800–treated A431 cells, which, as expected, increased with increase in incubation time (Fig. 5D). Cetuximab-IRDye800–treated T-47D cell phantoms also showed an increase in fluorescence signal (Fig. 5E), but the increase was significantly lower as compared to that observed for A431 tumor phantoms. PA signals in Cetuximab-IRDye800–treated A431 phantoms, in contrast, peaked early, at the 6-hour time point for both the wavelengths of 705 nm (Fig. 5, F and G) and 780 nm (Fig. 5, I and H), and decreased thereafter, consistent with the in vivo dynamics of PA signal fluctuations. In contrast, PA signals at both 705 and 780 nm showed no significant increase for the Cetuximab-IRDye800–treated T-47D cell phantoms, suggesting lower uptake due to the low EGFR expression in these cells. These findings suggested that peak PA signal is achieved at early time points when the Cetuximab-IRDye800 conjugate is intact and prolonging incubation results in Cetuximab-IRDye800 degradation in the lysosomes, leading to a drop in PA signal.

Antibody conjugation promotes H-aggregation and enhances PA signal of IRDye800

To further test our hypothesis that an intact Cetuximab-IRDye800 conjugate results in a higher PA signal, we first recorded the fluorescence and PA signal intensity for free IRDye800 and Cetuximab-IRDye800 with increasing concentrations. As shown in Fig. 6 (A, C, and G), IRDye800 fluorescence and PA signal showed a linear increase with an increase in concentration. At similar concentrations, Cetuximab-IRDye800 fluorescence was significantly lower than the free IRDye800 (Fig. 6, A and C), possibly due to homoquenching. However, in contrast, the PA signal from the Cetuximab-IRDye800 conjugate was significantly higher than the free IRDye800, suggesting that conjugation to an antibody improves PA signal amplitude (Fig. 6G).

To further confirm this, we synthesized Cetuximab-IRDye800 with increasing IRDye800 payload (Fig. 1D) and evaluated the effect of trypsinization on IRDye800 fluorescence and PA signal intensity. As shown in Fig. 6B, IRDye800 fluorescence signal decreased with an increase in DOL of IRDye800 on the antibody, possibly due to an increase in dipole-dipole coupling, leading to quenching of fluorescence signal at higher IRDye800 payloads. The fluorescence was, however, restored to a comparable value after trypsinization (Fig. 6, B and D). In contrast, PA signal at both 705 nm (Fig. 6, E and H) and 780 nm (Fig. 6, F and I) showed a gradual increase with increasing DOL but decreased with trypsinization (Fig. 6, E, F, H, and I). These results suggest that molecular stacking of IRDye800, upon antibody conjugation, contributes to an increased PA signal amplitude (35). The H-aggregation of IRDye800 is characterized by a shoulder at ~705 nm in the absorption spectra (Fig. 7B) of the Cetuximab-IRDye800 conjugate and increases with increasing IRDye800 loading on the antibody (Fig. 7C). While the H-aggregation appeared to increase even beyond a loading ratio of 5:1 (IRDye800 Cetuximab), the PA signal amplitude appeared to have plateaued at a loading ratio of 5:1 (Fig. 6, H and I). DISCUSSION

While fluorescence-based diagnostics have been in clinical practice for a few decades, there are certain limitations associated with the current practice of using fluorophores for detecting and guiding tumor resection surgeries. Two major limitations are the nonspecific accumulation of fluorophores and the inherent optical limits of penetration (few millimeters) of NIR light used for fluorescence measurements. With recent advances in the development of therapeutic antibodies and antibody drug conjugates, several NIR fluorophore-antibody conjugates have been reported and are in clinical trials for detection and guiding tumor resection surgeries of which EGFR-targeted IRDye800 has been the most common with several clinical trials evaluating its efficacy in different tumor types (36). In addition, the use of complementary imaging technologies can possibly overcome limitations associated with fluorescence imaging.

This study discusses the potential of Cetuximab-IRDye800 as a molecular-targeted PA contrast agent. While the utility of Cetuximab-IRDye800 as a molecular-targeted fluorescence contrast agent is well established, its utility for PA imaging has been limited by several factors including low sensitivity for accurate detection. Nishio et al. (18) demonstrated ex vivo PA imaging of lymph node metastasis using Pan-IRDye800 conjugates; however, these results were not extendable to clinical studies possibly due to the lower light fluence permitted in the clinic and hence the lower detection limit of the imaging system (21). It has been identified that PA imaging is limited with its sensitivity, and efforts have thus been made to develop novel agents with improved contrast for PA imaging (29, 37, 38).

In this study, we highlight the importance of timing PA imaging postinjection of a Cetuximab-IRDye800 conjugate. We show that antibody conjugation promotes H-aggregation of IRDye800, which enhances PA signal intensity. When applied to in vivo and in vitro tumor phantom imaging, capturing PA signal peak requires imaging at early time points when the Cetuximab-IRDye800 conjugate is intact. However, if imaging is delayed, which usually is preferred in fluorescence-based imaging, then the PA signal intensity decreases because of possible denaturation and trypsinization of antibody-dye conjugate during the RME pathway. This observation may have major implications in the way PA imaging is being performed in the clinic, which thus far has yielded underwhelming results (21). The study proposes a straightforward strategy, emphasizing on shorter time frames between infusion and imaging of Cetuximab-IRDye800, to potentially capture PA contrast at a time point when it is high. We also propose an alternative to enhance this signal further through increasing the DOL to 5 instead of the clinically used DOL of ~2. PA imaging capability of Cetuximab-IRDye800 with a DOL of 5 was, although, not tested in the current study but is expected to be higher than that obtained for Cetuximab-IRDye800 with a DOL of 2, based on our in vitro studies. The importance of timing as proposed in this study is backed by other studies for different dyes—BHQ3 (14) and ICG (19). For BHQ3-fluorescein trastuzumab conjugates, imaging at 8 hours postinjection yielded a higher PA signal in Human Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor 2 (HER2)–positive subcutaneous H2N tumors. The observed decrease in PA signal after the 8-hour time point was attributed to the metabolization of BHQ3 as has been demonstrated in earlier studies (39). We assume that the metabolization of BHQ3 is possibly following RME of the BHQ3-fluorescein trastuzumab conjugate as it is observed in the time frames of the RME-based Cetuximab-IRDye800 degradation observed in our study. In the study by Wilson et al. (19), RME of antibody-ICG conjugate was used to an advantage where the shift in the ICG absorption spectrum, and hence the spectroscopic PA signal, during cellular endocytosis and subsequent degradation of the B7-H3-ICG conjugate was exploited to differentiate between free B7-H3-ICG, nonspecific controls, and B7-H3-ICG bound and internalized to its molecular target (19). However, unlike ICG, which shows a shift in absorption peak depending on its antibody bound or unbound status, IRDye800 shows an appearance of a shoulder peak at around 700 nm. This shoulder peak, however, is relatively lower as compared to the primary absorption peak at 780 nm (at the DOLs tried in this study). As degradation of the H-aggregates results in decrease in PA signal intensity across all wavelengths, we hypothesize that the increased thermal conversion efficiency of the H-aggregated IRDye800 enhances the PA signal over a broad range, including 780 nm. The PA spectral changes for Cetuximab-IRDye800–administered mice bearing subcutaneous A431 tumors and in vitro A431 phantoms are presented in fig. S9 (A and B). As the system complexity increases from free Cetuximab-IRDye800 (fig. S9C) to cellular phantoms (fig. S9B) and in vivo tumors (fig. S9A), the spectra become progressively noisier, and the absorption features associated with IRDye800 (approximately 705 and 780 nm) broaden and are often observed as shoulders rather than distinct, well-resolved peaks. This behavior is expected and widely observed in complex biological systems. Spectral broadening and reduced peak definition occur because of multiple factors including heterogeneous optical environments, scattering, depth-dependent fluence attenuation, and signal-to-noise limitations inherent to in vivo imaging. However, as evident, both 705-nm and 780-nm signals decrease equally with time, suggesting that H-aggregation is important to sustain high PA signal amplitude across all wavelengths. In addition, the formation of H-aggregation does not increase the PA signal intensity at 705 nm over 780 nm as shown in the normalized PA spectra for Cetuximab-IRDye800 synthesized with different DOL (fig. S9C). Consequently, 780 nm emerges as an optimal wavelength for PA imaging. This choice is further supported by its spectral separation from the dominant Hb absorption bands and the comparatively greater light penetration depth at 780 nm. This effect may be restricted to dyes such as IRDye800, ICG, and BHQ3 that have a propensity for aggregation accompanied by changes in absorption spectra (40, 41). For dyes with a lower propensity to aggregate, such as naphthalocyanine dyes with significant axial and peripheral substitutions (42), temporal variations in PA signal are not observed, as demonstrated in our previously published studies (37).

While our study demonstrates a possible methodology for capturing higher PA signals from Cetuximab-IRDye800, currently being evaluated in the clinic for fluorescence imaging, there were a few limitations that must be addressed before this could be taken further. First, the accuracy of PA imaging–based detection at such early time frames is a concern primarily due to the possible presence of unbound Cetuximab-IRDye800. In microscopic evaluation of overlying skin and adjacent muscle tissue, we did not observe Cetuximab-IRDye-AF conjugate signal. However, in vivo imaging of IRDye800 fluorescence did suggest an improvement in the tumor-to-background ratio from the 6-hour to the 24-hour time point, indicating the presence of Cetuximab-IRDye-AF in relatively low quantities in the muscle and skin tissue at the 6-hour time point. These low amounts may, however, be below the detection limit of PA imaging but can contribute, as sensitivity of imaging systems is improved. Second, the distribution of Cetuximab-IRDye-AF as observed in ex vivo histopathological evaluation was lower at the 6-hour time point compared to the 24-hour time point. Cetuximab-IRDye-AF distribution between the two time points was compared by monitoring the Cetuximab-IRDye-AF coverage area in the tumor cross sections, the Cetuximab-IRDye-AF–to–EGFR ratio to indicate the number of EGFR sites (as revealed by ex vivo EGFR staining) labeled by Cetuximab-IRDye-AF, distance of Cetuximab-IRDye-AF from the nearest CD31 positive blood vessel, and the Cetuximab-IRDye-AF–to–CD31 ratio. All these parameters showed a lower value (although not significant) at the 6-hour time point as compared to the 24-hour time point, with the exception of Cetuximab-IRDye-AF distance from the nearest CD31-positive blood vessel, which was significantly lower. This is in good correlation with the in vivo fluorescence imaging results where IRDye800 fluorescence at 6 hours was lower as compared to the 24-hour time point. However, the ex vivo distribution studies were based on AF647 fluorescence imaging, which may have been quenched for the 6-hour tumor samples. We therefore believe that the differences between the microscopic Cetuximab-IRDye-AF distribution parameters at the 6-hour and the 24-hour time points may be even lower as observed in this study. Third, the susceptibility of cyanine dyes including ICG and IRDye800 to photobleaching under nanosecond laser illumination for PA imaging is another critical factor. Duffy et al. (42) have demonstrated an ~50% photobleaching in IRDye800 when exposed to 30 min of continuous PA imaging. When performing three-dimensional (3D) imaging of tumors across multiple wavelengths, the extended PA laser exposure exacerbates photobleaching effects and may have affected signal amplitude at the 24-hour time point due to imaging at earlier time points. Fourth, the tumors studied in this study were fairly big, close to ~5 mm, and therefore had a larger Cetuximab-IRDye800 accumulation and hence high enough PA signal that could be detected. Future studies should focus on identifying lymph node metastasis using the Cetuximab-IRDye800 conjugate and assess whether microscopic tumors could be efficiently detected using PA imaging at early time points.

With this study, we attempt to emphasize the importance of timing for capturing a high PA signal intensity, and we believe this could be further improved through one of the following three strategies: (i) As discussed earlier, increasing the IRDye800 DOL to 5 or more can possibly enhance PA signal amplitude. (ii) The high receptor expression in A431 tumors used here may have posed a significant binding-site barrier limiting the extent of migration, distribution, and accumulation of Cetuximab-IRDye800 conjugate, thus decreasing the PA signal. Preinjecting animals with free Cetuximab can possibly help in improving the PA signal amplitude further (43). (iii) Performing photodynamic priming (PDP) before Cetuximab-IRDye800 conjugate administration can help in increased accumulation of the conjugate at relatively early time points, thereby enhancing PA signal intensity (44). This has been exemplified in preclinical oral cancer models where PDP at 75 J/cm2 expedited interstitial accumulation of Cetuximab-IRDye800 by 10.5-fold and increased its fractional tumor coverage by 49.5%, 1 hour after administration (45).

In summary, this study underscores the critical importance of timing in PA imaging protocols using Cetuximab-IRDye800 and provides mechanistic evidence explaining why acquisition at early time points can capture peak PA signal. Furthermore, we outline several practical strategies to possibly enhance PA signal amplitude. Our findings demonstrate that fluorescence and PA signals arise from distinct physical states and microenvironmental contexts of the same molecular probe, Cetuximab-IRDye800. Consequently, optimal imaging time points differ between modalities, reflecting the evolving biochemical and biophysical environments encountered by the Cetuximab-IRDye800 conjugate in vivo. These results emphasize the fundamentally complementary nature of fluorescence and PA imaging. Leveraging this complementarity has the potential to meaningfully refine the clinical deployment of Cetuximab-IRDye800, particularly in lymph node imaging for the detection of occult metastases.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Synthesis of Cetuximab-IRDye800 and Cetuximab-IRDye800-AF647 conjugate

The human anti-EGFR antibody (Cetuximab, ERBITUX from Eli Lilly) was obtained from Premium Health Services (Columbia, MD, USA). Before initiating the conjugation reaction, the antibody was filtered by passing through a 0.22-μm syringe filter, followed by estimating the concentration using a Pierce BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The conjugation reaction was initiated by adding N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) ester of IRDye800 (LI-COR Biosciences, Lincoln, NE, USA) at a molar excess of 4, 8, 10, and 15, respectively, to 2 ml of Cetuximab (~2 mg/ml). The reaction was allowed to proceed for 4 hours under continuous stirring at room temperature, followed by purification using a 7-kDa MWCO Zeba Spin Desalting Column (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) preequilibrated with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The purified Cetuximab-IRDye800 conjugate was then concentrated using a 30-kDa Amicon Ultra-15 Centrifugal Filter Unit (MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA, USA). The final purified Cetuximab-IRDye800 conjugate was stored at 4°C. Synthesis of the Cetuximab-IRDye-AF conjugate was performed similarly using a molar excess of 4 and 2 for the IRDye800 and AF647 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), respectively. Synthesis of the isotype-IRDye800 was performed by reacting to the human IgG1 kappa recombinant isotype control antibody (clone: 5Vk2/37.7H, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) with a fourfold molar excess of IRDye800, followed by purification and characterization as reported earlier. The DOL for Iso-IRDye800 was found to be 1.97 ± 0.23.

Characterization of Cetuximab-IRDye800 and Cetuximab-IRDye-AF conjugate

The DOL of IRDye800 on Cetuximab was calculated by monitoring the absorption of Cetuximab-IRDye800 and Cetuximab-IRDye-AF conjugate, using a Thermo Fisher Scientific Evolution 300 ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), at 780 nm using the molar extinction coefficient of 270,000 M−1 cm−1 for IRDye800 at 780 nm in 1:1 PBS:methanol. AF647 concentration was calculated using the molar extinction coefficient of 239,000 M−1 cm−1 at 650 nm in PBS with an 11% correction factor for IRDye800 at 650 nm. Cetuximab concentration was calculated using a Pierce BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

SDS-PAGE analysis was performed by mixing the Cetuximab-IRDye800 or Cetuximab-IRDye-AF conjugate with 1× Laemmli buffer (Boston BioProducts, Milford, MA, USA), heating at 95°C for 5 min and loading it on a 4–20% Mini-PROTEAN precast polyacrylamide gel (Bio-Rad, Hercules, California, USA). The separated bands were visualized using a ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, California, USA). Image analysis and quantification were performed using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health).

Comparative fluorescence studies for free IRDye800 and Cetuximab-IRDye800 were performed by diluting the stock of the dye or conjugate in PBS and mixing it with 1.2% agarose in a 1:1 ratio, followed by overlaying it on an agarose (1.2%, 100 μl) bed in a well of a 96-well plate. The gels were allowed to solidify and imaged in hyperspectral mode using an IVIS imaging system using an IVIS Lumina Series III In Vivo Imaging System (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA). Baseline spectra of free IRDye800 phantoms were also measured and used for unmixing and generating unmixed composite images of IRDye800 in the Cetuximab-IRDye800 phantoms. Quantification of fluorescence intensities was performed using the Living Image software provided by the manufacturer.

PA imaging was performed using a VisualSonics Vevo 2100 LAZR system (VisualSonics, Fujifilm, ON, Canada). The 96-well plate with the phantoms was submerged in double-distilled water, and the PA transducer (LZ220), with bubble-free ultrasound gel applied to it, was placed in close proximity to the sample containing phantoms. Image analysis was performed using the built-in software provided by VisualSonics, Fujifilm (ON, Canada). Imaging parameters—laser power, PA gain, persistence, and frame rate—were kept constant across different measurements. For PA imaging analysis and quantification, the upper 1 mm of the well with the phantom embedded was used.

For trypsinization studies, Cetuximab-IRDye800 at a concentration of 20 μM IRDye800 equivalent was mixed with 1% trypsin and incubated at 37°C for 24 hours. Following this, phantoms were prepared and imaged as described previously.

Generation of subcutaneous A431 xenograft tumors

The human epidermoid carcinoma line [A-431, American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), Manassas, VA, USA] was cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Corning, Corning, NY, USA), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and a mixture of penicillin (50 IU/ml) and streptomycin (50 μg/ml) (Corning, Corning, NY, USA). All animal studies were performed in accordance with protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) (IACUC protocol number: 2019N000041). For tumor implantation, confluent cell cultures were trypsinized, counted, and resuspended in DMEM. A total of 2.5 × 106 cells in 50 μl of 50% Matrigel was implanted subcutaneously in 6- to 8-week-old athymic nude mice (The Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, ME, USA). After 2 weeks, the animals were imaged for background fluorescence and PA signal, followed by intravenous injection of Cetuximab-IRDye800 or Cetuximab-IRDye-AF (~150 μg of Cetuximab equivalent) through the lateral tail vein. The animals were imaged again at 5 min (PA imaging only) and 1, 3, 6, 12, 18, and 24 hours. A cohort of animals injected with Cetuximab-IRDye-AF was euthanized at 6 and 24 hours each, respectively, for histopathological evaluation of tumor tissues.

In vivo fluorescence measurements were carried out on isoflurane anesthetized mice in hyperspectral mode using an IVIS Lumina Series III In Vivo Imaging System (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA). Baseline spectra of free AF647 and IRDye800 phantoms were used for unmixing and generating unmixed images of IRDye800 and AF647. Quantification of fluorescence intensities was performed using the Living Image software provided by the manufacturer.

PA imaging was performed using a VisualSonics Vevo 2100 LAZR system (VisualSonics, Fujifilm, ON, Canada). Mice were anesthetized and placed in prone position with bubble-free ultrasound gel applied on the tumors. The PA transducer (LZ220) was placed in proximity to the tumor, and images were acquired either in a single plane, multiwavelength mode, or in 3D mode (three to five frames per mouse) with a central frame positioned at the tumor center and one to two additional frames captured on either side. Images were obtained at predefined wavelengths (705, 780, 850, and 900 nm) to enable spectral unmixing of endogenous Hb signals and IRDye800 signals. Coregistered B-mode images were acquired for identifying tumor planes between different imaging time points. Imaging was performed at preinjection baseline and at 5 min (0.08 hours) and 1, 3, 6, 12, 18, and 24 hours postadministration of Cetuximab-IRDye800 conjugates. Imaging parameters—laser power, PA gain, persistence, and frame rate—were kept constant across all measurements and time points. Raw data were exported as “.raw” files from the Vevo LAB software and then imported to MATLAB. Using custom scripts built upon the base code provided by VisualSonics, the raw data were converted to “.mat” files for subsequent image and data processing. The Hb and dye content were unmixed from the multiwavelength data using the linear least-squares method (46) via the lsqnonneg MATLAB function. The molar extinction coefficients for oxygenated and deoxygenated Hb were gathered from Prahl (47), while the IRDye800 dye molar extinction coefficients were taken directly from the spectrophotometer readings described in the section above. For display purposes only, PA images were thresholded to reduce background noise and improve visual clarity. Threshold values were determined objectively by measuring the mean PA signal intensity in a 50 × 50 pixel region adjacent to but outside the tumor boundary. Display thresholds were set at this baseline level and applied consistently across all time points: 2 a.u. for HbT and 10 a.u. for wavelength-specific PA signals at 705 and 780 nm. Quantitative analysis was performed on all pixels within the tumor region of interest (ROI), regardless of their PA signal amplitude. Tumor ROIs were manually defined on the basis of coregistered B-mode ultrasound anatomical boundaries and maintained consistently across all time points. This approach ensures unbiased quantification of HbT, StO2, and IRDye800 signal intensity, as no pixels were excluded from analysis based on PA amplitude.

Quantification was performed across the whole-tumor cross-sectional frame using pixel-wise averaging for IRDye800 and HbT amplitude values, with each frame treated as an individual data point (three to five data points per mouse) to account for tumor heterogeneity and capture spatial variations in dye distribution and Hb content across different tumor regions, providing a more comprehensive assessment of drug uptake patterns.

Immunofluorescence staining and image analysis

Cetuximab-IRDye-AF–injected mice were euthanized in accordance with the IACUC-approved procedures. Tumor tissues were collected and embedded in Tissue-Tek O.C.T. compound (Sakura, Torrance, CA, USA) and frozen until further use. Tumor tissues were then cryosectioned at a thickness of 5 μm, fixed with acetone, and stained using primary anti-EGFR (EP38Y, Abcam, Cambridge, United Kingdom) and anti-CD31 (MEC 13.3, Woburn, MA, USA) antibodies, at dilutions of 10 and 0.15 μg/ml, respectively, followed by secondary Goat anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) Highly Cross-Adsorbed Secondary Antibody Alexa Fluor Plus 488 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and Goat anti-Rat IgG (H + L) Cross-Adsorbed Secondary Antibody Alexa Fluor 546 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) antibodies at dilutions of 400 μg/ml each. Rabbit IgG monoclonal (EPR25A, Abcam, Cambridge, United Kingdom) and Rat IgG2a kappa Isotype Control (eBR2a, eBioscience, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) were used as isotype control. The sections were counterstained with ProLong Glass Antifade Mountant with NucBlue Stain (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and imaged using a Hamamatsu NanoZoomer slide scanner (Hamamatsu Photonics, Shizuoka, Japan). Image acquisition settings were consistent for each tumor section, and images were acquired for four channels, which contained 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI), EGFR (fluorescein isothiocyanate), CD31 (tetramethyl rhodamine isothiocyanate), as well as the endogenously injected free and Cetuximab-IRDye-AF–bound Alexa Fluor 647. Images were separated into their constituent channels using the NDP.View2 application and subsequently loaded into MATLAB. Control mouse sections without injected Cetuximab-IRDye-AF were used to determine threshold values for true Cetuximab-IRDye-AF signal. For EGFR and CD31 signal, isotype antibodies were used for threshold setting. For each slide, the tumor, skin, and muscle tissue sections were identified in accordance with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), EGFR, and DAPI stains. After the different tissue types were identified, the thresholds were applied, and the images were cropped on the basis of the specific tissue type of interest. Mean Cetuximab-IRDye-AF intensity and percent area were determined via image matrix calculations. For determining the separation between components of different fluorescent channels, the pdist2 MATLAB function was used.

Fluorescence and PA imaging of in vitro tumor phantoms

Tumor phantoms were prepared as described in our previous studies (29, 37, 38). T-47D cells were used as a negative control due to their low EGFR expression (48). T-47D (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 (Corning, Corning, NY, USA), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and a mixture of penicillin (50 IU/ml) and streptomycin (50 μg/ml). Briefly, A431 or T-47D cells were seeded in 150-mm dishes and treated with Cetuximab-IRDye800 for the indicated time points following which cells were collected by gently scraping by a cell scraper, counted, and resuspended in 5% gelatin. The resuspended cells (1 × 107 in a final volume of 50 μl) were immediately poured in a well of a 96-well plate previously prelaid with 10% gelatin (250 to 300 μl) to minimize reverberations from the base of the 96-well plate during PA imaging. The tumor phantoms were allowed to solidify and imaged within 24 hours. IVIS imaging and PAI were performed as described earlier. For PAI analysis and quantification, the upper 1 mm of the well with the tumor cell phantom embedded was used.

Confocal imaging for assessing Cetuximab-IRDye-AF localization

A431 cells were seeded onto glass bottom 35-mm cell culture dishes at a density of 300,000 cells per dish. Cetuximab-IRDye-AF was added at an AF647 equivalent concentration of 250 nM. Thirty minutes before the end of incubation, cells were incubated with LysoTracker Green DND-26 (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA) at a concentration of 50 nM, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, counterstained with Hoechst (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and visualized under a confocal microscope (Olympus FV1000, Shinjuku City, Tokyo, Japan). Quantitative analysis of colocalization were performed after converting images to 8-bit and subtracting background using a rolling ball radius of 50 through ImageJ (National Institutes of Health).

SDS-PAGE analysis

A431 cells were seeded onto 35-mm cell culture dishes at a density of 300,000 cells per dish. Cetuximab-IRDye-AF was incubated at an AF647 equivalent concentration of 250 nM, and the cells were collected by gently scraping by a cell scraper at the end of the desired incubation period and pelleted. Cell pellets were lysed using radioimmunoprecipitation (RIPA) lysis and extraction buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and protein estimation in the cell lysate was performed using a Pierce BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). A total of 20 μg of cell lysate was then mixed with 1× Laemmli buffer (Boston BioProducts, Milford, MA, USA) and heated at 95°C for 5 min, followed by loading on a 4–20% Mini-PROTEAN precast polyacrylamide gel (Bio-Rad, Hercules, California, USA). The separated bands were visualized using a ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, California, USA). Image analysis and quantification were performed using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health). Briefly, background subtraction was performed on all band intensities, and intensities from bands in a single lane (excluding the band corresponding to the aggregate) were used as a measure of total dye in the cells. The fraction of intact antibody and free dye was then calculated by dividing their respective intensities from the total dye intensity from the corresponding lane.

Statistical analysis

Details of statistical analysis, including the statistical significance and n numbers, for all experiments are reported in the figure legends. Statistics were performed using GraphPad Prism (v10) software. Shapiro-Wilk test was used to test for normal distribution, and parametric tests were used if the P value obtained for all the experimental groups was more than 0.05. For in vivo PA imaging data, PA signal intensities at 705 and 780 nm were normalized to preinjection baseline values for each animal. Frame-level analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test, with each cross-sectional frame treated as an individual data point (n = 1 to 4 frames per animal, five to nine animals per time point). The variation in frame numbers across animals resulted from differences in tumor size and exclusion of frames containing imaging artifacts. To validate temporal dynamics between PA and fluorescence modalities, a two-way mixed-effects model ANOVA was performed with modality and time as factors (table S3). To address potential concerns regarding statistical nonindependence of frames within the same animal, an additional animal-level analysis was performed by averaging all frames per animal per time point. Because the same animals were imaged longitudinally, the Friedman repeated-measures test was used to assess overall temporal dynamics, with post hoc paired comparisons performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test and paired t test as reported in tables S1 and S2.
