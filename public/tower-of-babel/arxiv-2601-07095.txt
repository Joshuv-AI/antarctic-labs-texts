Score-Based VAMP with Fisher-Information-Based Onsager Correction
Tadashi Wadayama 1 , Takumi Takahashi 2

Abstract
We propose score-based VAMP (SC-VAMP), a variant of vector approximate message passing (VAMP) in which the Onsager correction is expressed and computed via conditional Fisher information, thereby enabling a Jacobian-free implementation. Using learned score functions, SC-VAMP constructs nonlinear minimum mean square error (MMSE) estimators through Tweedie’s formula and derives the corresponding Onsager terms from the score-norm statistics, avoiding the need for analytical derivatives of the prior or likelihood. When combined with random orthogonal/unitary mixing to mitigate non-ideal, structured or correlated sensing settings, the proposed framework extends VAMP to complex black-box inference problems where explicit modeling is intractable. Finally, by leveraging the entropic central limit theorem (CLT) , we provide an information-theoretic perspective on the Gaussian approximation underlying state evolution (SE) , offering insight into the decoupling principle beyond idealized independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) settings, including nonlinear regimes.



I
Introduction

High-dimensional inverse problems—estimating a signal vector
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
from noisy, possibly nonlinear measurements
\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
—are ubiquitous in modern science and engineering, spanning fields from wireless communications and compressed sensing to medical imaging and computational photography.
Traditionally, these problems have been approached using two distinct paradigms: model-based iterative algorithms and data-driven deep learning approaches.

Model-based algorithms, particularly
approximate message passing (AMP)

[
1
]
and closely related approaches such
vector AMP (VAMP)

[
2
]
and
orthogonal AMP (OAMP)

[
4
]
, offer powerful and analytically tractable frameworks for signal recovery.
Under ideal conditions—such as
independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.)
Gaussian sensing matrices or
right-rotationally invariant (RRI)
matrices—their asymptotic performance is rigorously characterized by a scalar recursion known as
state evolution (SE)
, which accurately predicts the trajectory of the reconstruction error.
In this work, we deliberately focus on the Bayes-optimal formulation of
VAMP
, which combines strong empirical performance with a broad range of practical applicability,
i.e.
,
RRI
sensing matrices.

However, Bayes-optimal
VAMP
faces significant challenges in real-world scenarios.
Deriving the optimal
minimum mean square error (MMSE)
denoiser and its associated divergence
(the Onsager correction term

VAMP

) typically requires analytical knowledge of both the prior distribution

and the likelihood

.
In practice, the observation model itself is often highly structured or constrained—for example, through convolutional operators, array manifolds, or forward models based on
partial differential equations (PDEs)

[

]
.
Moreover, in many modern applications, the prior is complex (
e.g.
, natural images), and the front-end observation involves deterministic nonlinearities (
e.g.
, sensor saturation or optical processes) in real-world systems.
Under such conditions, the analytical derivation of optimal estimators and the associated
SE
becomes computationally intractable or may no longer be valid.

In contrast, data-driven approaches, such as
deep unfolding (DU)

[
29
,
30
,
31
,
32
]
, replace the analytical components of iterative algorithms with trainable parameters or trainable neural networks.
While empirically successful, these methods often operate as black boxes: they typically lack the theoretical guarantees enjoyed by their model-based counterparts, and it is often unclear whether performance bottlenecks arise from fundamental physical limits or from insufficient training.
Furthermore, training such end-to-end models frequently requires massive datasets of paired input-output samples
(\bm{x},\bm{y})
, which may not always be available or easy to generate in practice, particularly when the forward model corresponds to a complex physical system.

In this paper, we bridge this gap by proposing
score-based VAMP (SC-VAMP)
,
a framework that integrates the analytically tractable structure of
VAMP
with the expressive power of score-based models
[
23
]
.
Our key insight is that the essential components of Bayes-optimal
VAMP
—namely, the nonlinear
MMSE
estimator and the Onsager correction term—can be completely characterized by the
score function

\nabla\log p(\cdot)
and the
Fisher information

J=\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla\log p(\cdot)\|^{2}]
.
This abstraction enables a unified treatment of both linear and nonlinear problems under a single mathematical formalism, while also providing a conceptual bridge between statistical-physics-inspired analyses, Bayesian inference, and information-theoretic viewpoints in a coherent manner.

By leveraging
denoising score matching (DSM)

[
21
]
, we learn these quantities directly from data, thereby enabling the construction of approximately optimal estimators without requiring explicit analytical expressions for the underlying density functions.
Moreover, we show that, in the classical linear Gaussian setting,

SC-VAMP
reduces to standard Bayes-optimal
VAMP
and recovers the same
SE
fixed point and mutual information.
As a result,
SC-VAMP
remains fully consistent with existing theory, while extending its applicability far beyond linear Gaussian models.

The contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:

•

Data-Driven Generalization of Bayes-optimal VAMP:

We formulate
SC-VAMP
, in which the denoiser is implemented via Tweedie’s formula using a learned score function, and the Onsager correction is computed from the estimated Fisher information.
When combined with random orthogonal/unitary mixing (
i.e.
, continuous-valued random mixing) to handle non-ideal sensing settings,
SC-VAMP
extends
VAMP
to arbitrary complex priors and nonlinear observation models while retaining the core message-passing structure, without requiring explicit analytical derivatives.

•

Jacobian-Free Onsager via Fisher Information (Score-Centric View of VAMP):

We show that the Onsager correction can be expressed solely in terms of the conditional Fisher information along the pseudo-
additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN)
channel,
e.g.
,
\alpha(v)=1-\frac{v}{N}J
, where the Fisher information
J
is estimated from the squared norm of the conditional score.
This eliminates the need for explicit Jacobian/divergence evaluations, which are typically required in
AMP
/
VAMP
when using complex or learned denoisers.
As a result, we obtain a unified score-centric interface in which both
the posterior mean update (via Tweedie’s formula) and the Onsager term are determined by the same object—the conditional score.
We further provide complementary rationales based on Stein’s identity and on an information-theoretic route (via de Bruijn and I–MMSE relationship), offering an interpretable view of the Onsager term as an information-consistency (curvature) correction.

•

Theoretical Rationale via Entropic CLT:

We provide an information-theoretic perspective on the Gaussian approximation underlying
SE
. By leveraging the entropic
central limit theorem (CLT)

[
19
]
and the results by Tulino and Verdú
[
15
]
, we interpret the linear mixing step in
SC-VAMP
as a “Gaussianizer,” which tends to reduce the non-Gaussianity of estimation errors in terms of
Kullback-Leibler (KL)
-divergence.
This information-theoretic viewpoint, when combined with random orthogonal/unitary mixing, offers insight into the decoupling principle in nonlinear and structured settings.

II-C

Related Works

The proposed
SC-VAMP
framework is closely related to a broad line of research on iterative inference algorithms originating from the
Thouless–Anderson–Palmer (TAP)
equations
[
36
]
introduced in spin-glass theory, as well as to their asymptotic analyses.
The concept of the Onsager reaction term, originally introduced in the
TAP
equations, has since been abstracted and understood as an essential correction that ensures self-consistency in large-scale iterative inference algorithms, thereby enabling an asymptotic characterization via
SE
.
Representative algorithms that can be regarded as culminating points of this development are
AMP
and
VAMP
, together with
SE
as the asymptotic analytical framework that is intrinsically paired with these algorithms.

AMP
and
VAMP
can be formulated as top-down algorithmic constructions based on generalized error models
[
2
,
37
,
38
,
39
]
.
The rigorous validity of
SE
for these algorithms has been established under appropriate matrix ensembles and regularity conditions, building on arguments closely related to conditioning techniques developed by Erwin Bolthausen in the context of spin-glass theory.
At the same time, these algorithms admit an alternative interpretation as large-system limits of Bayesian
belief propagation (BP)
(
i.e.
, the sum–product algorithm) on dense graphs
[
40
]
.
From this perspective, the Onsager reaction term can be interpreted as a correction that compensates for correlations induced between messages during iterative updates.
This structural interpretation is consistent with the classical result that the
TAP
equations can be derived from
BP
in fully connected Ising models, and it can also be explained from the viewpoint of
expectation-consistent (EC)
approximations
[
41
]
and approximate free-energy minimization
[
42
]
.

In particular, in Bayes-optimal settings where the prior distribution and the likelihood are matched to the true generative model, the self-consistent fixed-point equations obtained via the replica method
[
43
,
44
]
(within the regime where the replica-symmetric assumption is valid) coincide with the fixed points of
SE
.
In this case, the
SE
recursion can be interpreted as a successive-substitution procedure for solving these fixed-point equations.
When the fixed point is unique and
SE
converges, the asymptotic solution reached by the algorithm coincides with the Bayes-optimal estimator in the large-system limit
[
37
,
38
]
.
In this sense, Bayes-optimal
AMP
and Bayes-optimal
VAMP
can be viewed as occupying a conceptual nexus at which top-down algorithmic design principles rooted in spin-glass theory intersect with bottom-up interpretations based on Bayesian approximate inference.

These developments are also closely connected to earlier notions of decoupling and scalar-equivalent channel representations in coding theory, such as density evolution for
low-density parity-check (LDPC)
codes
[
25
]
and the turbo principle
[
24
]
.
In particular, the turbo principle—originally introduced as an algorithmic heuristic with empirically favorable convergence behavior—relies on the exchange of extrinsic information between constituent modules and is commonly analyzed via scalar-equivalent channels and
extrinsic information transfer (EXIT)
charts
[
45
]
.
From a modern perspective, it is precisely the statistical-mechanics-inspired framework that provides a rigorous theoretical foundation for such principles, enabling discussions of optimality and convergence in asymptotic regimes, including both large-system limits in inference problems and infinite-length limits in coding theory.

Together with rigorous asymptotic analyses based on
SE
,

AMP
and
VAMP
have been extended to
generalized linear models (GLMs)

[
3
,
46
,
47
,
48
,
49
]
, providing powerful and analytically tractable inference frameworks.
However, with only a few highly restricted exceptions, it remains difficult to derive closed-form algorithms for the complex prior distributions and observation models with deterministic nonlinearities that commonly arise in practical engineering problems.

In a related but distinct line of work, learning-based denoisers have been
integrated into
AMP
/
VAMP
-type architectures.

Plug-and-play (PnP)

[
5
]
and
regularization-by-denoising (RED)

[
6
,
7
]
replace model-based priors with powerful image denoisers, achieving strong
empirical performance in practice.
Furthermore, numerous deep-unfolded
AMP
/
VAMP
architectures have been proposed, in which denoisers are implemented as neural networks and trained in an end-to-end manner.

Deep unfolding (DU)

[
29
]
and its applications to communication systems
[
30
]
demonstrate that unfolding a small number of message-passing iterations
into a trainable network can yield remarkably strong empirical performance
[
31
,
32
]
.
However, these approaches often come at the cost of a clean theoretical interpretation grounded in
SE
.
Since learned denoisers and their associated Jacobians (Onsager terms) are not explicitly tied to any underlying probabilistic model, it becomes difficult to quantitatively assess how far the algorithmic performance lies from information-theoretic limits, or whether
SE
remains valid in such settings.

II-D

Our Contributions

Against this background, score-based generative models and
DSM

[
21
,
23
]
have recently emerged as a powerful frameworks for learning gradients of log-densities (

While several works have explored the use of learned score functions for denoising, sampling, and as building blocks for Bayesian inference, most of these approaches are not explicitly connected to the statistical estimation theory underlying

Our work can be viewed as

As a result, this work substantially extends the applicability of

, establishing a framework that reconciles learning-based nonlinear estimation with asymptotic analyses via

In addition, the theoretical foundations of this work—most notably the integral representations of

[

These connections trace back to Stam’s use of the heat equation

due to Brown

and Barron

, and were ultimately unified by the I–

.

III
Score-Based VAMP

In this section, we present the theoretical framework underlying
SC-VAMP
.
By reformulating Bayes-optimal
VAMP
through the lens of score functions and Fisher information, we obtain a unified algorithmic framework in which optimal nonlinear estimators are constructed
via Tweedie’s formula.
This formulation extends the classical linear setting to general nonlinear regimes, while preserving a principled and analytically interpretable structure.

III-A

Score-Based Representation of SISO Modules

We begin by describing a
SISO
module, which serves as a fundamental building block of the proposed
SC-VAMP
presented below.
The
SISO
module acts as an estimator: given an input vector
\bm{x}_{in}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, representing the prior information, an (optional) observation vector
\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
, and an input variance
v_{in}>0
, , it produces an extrinsic output
\bm{x}_{out}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and an extrinsic variance
v_{out}>0
.

The score-based representation of a
SISO
module used
in the
SC-VAMP
is given as follows:

\displaystyle\bm{x}_{post}

\displaystyle=\bm{x}_{in}+v_{in}s(\bm{x}_{in}|\bm{y}),

(1)

\displaystyle\bm{x}_{out}

\displaystyle=\frac{\bm{x}_{post}-\alpha(v_{in})\bm{x}_{in}}{1-\alpha(v_{in})},

(2)

\displaystyle v_{out}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha(v_{in})}{1-\alpha(v_{in})}v_{in},

(3)

\displaystyle\alpha(v_{in})

\displaystyle=1-\frac{v_{in}}{N}J_{X_{in}|Y},

(4)

where
\bm{x}_{post}
denotes the posterior mean, and
\alpha(v_{in})
is the Onsager coefficient.
The (conditional) score function
s(\cdot|\cdot)
is defined by

s(\bm{x}_{in}|\bm{y})\equiv\nabla_{\bm{x}_{in}}\log p_{X_{in}|Y}(\bm{x}_{in}|\bm{y}).

(5)

The corresponding conditional Fisher information is

\displaystyle J_{X_{in}|Y}\equiv{\mathbb{E}}[\|s(\bm{x}_{in}|\bm{y})\|^{2}],

(6)

where
Y
and
X_{in}
denote the random variables corresponding to the observation vector and the input to the
SISO
module, respectively.
In addition, the posterior variance is given by

\displaystyle v_{post}=v_{in}\alpha(v_{in}).

(7)

The expressions above describe a
SISO
module that depends on
\bm{y}
.
For a
SISO
module that does not use
\bm{y}
(
i.e.
,
denoiser module
, we use the unconditional score function:
s(\bm{x}_{in})\equiv\nabla_{\bm{x}_{in}}\log p_{X_{in}}(\bm{x}_{in})
is place of
s(\bm{x}_{in}|\bm{y})
.
The derivation and further discussion of these relations are provided in the following subsections.

Beyond computational savings, expressing the Onsager correction through conditional Fisher information also suggests an information-geometric interpretation: the strength of the correction can be viewed as being governed by the local curvature of the induced posterior.

III-B

Score-based VAMP (SC-VAMP) Algorithm

The proposed
SC-VAMP
algorithm operates by iteratively applying two
SISO
modules, that exchange extrinsic information in the form of mean vectors and variances.
We refer to these modules as Module A and Module B, whose operations are specified below.

Module A (Observation Module):
This module typically incorporates the observation
\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
together with the channel conditional
probability density function (PDF)

p_{Y|X}
or an associated likelihood function.
Given inputs
\bm{x}_{in,A}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
v_{in,A}\in\mathbb{R}
, it computes extrinsic outputs
\bm{x}_{out,A}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
v_{out,A}\in\mathbb{R}
using the conditional score function
s(\bm{x}_{in}|\bm{y})
:

\displaystyle\bm{x}_{out,A}

\displaystyle=\frac{v_{in,A}s(\bm{x}_{in,A}|\bm{y})+(1-\alpha_{A}(v_{in,A}))\bm{x}_{in,A}}{1-\alpha_{A}(v_{in,A})},

(8)

\displaystyle v_{out,A}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{A}(v_{in,A})}{1-\alpha_{A}(v_{in,A})}v_{in,A},

(9)

\displaystyle\alpha_{A}(v_{in,A})

\displaystyle=1-\frac{v_{in,A}}{N}J_{X_{in,A}|Y},

(10)

with message updates

\displaystyle\bm{x}_{in,A}

\displaystyle=\bm{x}_{out,B},\quad v_{in,A}=v_{out,B}.

(11)

Module B (Denoiser Module):
This module typically incorporates prior information through the prior distribution
p_{X}
.
Given inputs
\bm{x}_{in,B}
and
v_{in,B}
, it computes extrinsic outputs
\bm{x}_{out,B}
and
v_{out,B}
using the score function
s(\bm{x}_{in})
, which does not depend on
\bm{y}
:

\displaystyle\bm{x}_{out,B}

\displaystyle=\frac{v_{in,B}s(\bm{x}_{in,B})+(1-\alpha_{B}(v_{in,B}))\bm{x}_{in,B}}{1-\alpha_{B}(v_{in,B})},

(12)

\displaystyle v_{out,B}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{B}(v_{in,B})}{1-\alpha_{B}(v_{in,B})}v_{in,B},

(13)

\displaystyle\alpha_{B}(v_{in,B})

\displaystyle=1-\frac{v_{in,B}}{N}J_{X_{in,B}},

(14)

with message updates

\displaystyle\bm{x}_{in,B}

\displaystyle=\bm{x}_{out,A},\quad v_{in,B}=v_{out,A}.

(15)

The two modules exchange extrinsic messages iteratively for a fixed number of iterations
T
or until the extrinsic parameters converge.
The algorithm is typically initialized by setting the output of Module B at iteration
t=0
to initial values.
Specifically, we set
\bm{x}_{out,B}^{(0)}=\bm{0}
and
v_{out,B}^{(0)}=v_{init}
, where
v_{init}
denotes the variance of the prior distribution or, more generally, a large initial variance for numerical stability.

The

We consider the problem of estimating an unknown signal

We assume two types of soft information regarding

Soft-Input 1 (Prior Information):
An observation of

(16)

This can be interpreted as observing

An realization of

is denoted by
\bm{x}_{in}

Soft-Input 2 (Observation Information):
An observation
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
follows:

X\sim p_{X},\quad Y\sim p_{Y|X}.

(17)

A representative example is a nonlinear measurement model in which the signal
X
is passed through a nonlinear mapping
f:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{M}
and corrupted by additive Gaussian noise
W\sim{\cal N}(\bm{0},\sigma_{N}^{2}\bm{I}_{M})
,
i.e.
,

Y=f(X)+W.

(18)

where we assume that
X
,
Z
, and

In the following, we first derive the posterior mean (

,

\mathbb{E}[X|X_{in}=\bm{x}_{in},Y=\bm{y}]

III-C
2
Conditional Tweedie’s Formula

The core component of the
SISO
module is the
MMSE
estimator given by the posterior expectation
\mathbb{E}[X|X_{in}=\bm{x}_{in},Y=\bm{y}]
.
This estimator admits a concise representation in terms of the score function, which can be viewed as a conditional generalization of Tweedie’s formula
[
11
,
12
]
to the case where side information
Y
is available.

The posterior expectation is derived as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X|\bm{x}_{in},\bm{y}]

\displaystyle=\int\bm{x}p(\bm{x}|\bm{x}_{in},\bm{y})d\bm{x}

(19)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p(\bm{x}_{in},\bm{y})}\int\bm{x}p(\bm{x}_{in},\bm{y}|\bm{x})p(\bm{x})d\bm{x}

(20)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p(\bm{x}_{in},\bm{y})}\int\bm{x}p(\bm{x}_{in}|\bm{x})p(\bm{y}|\bm{x})p(\bm{x})d\bm{x},

(21)

where the last equality follows from the conditional independence
X_{in}\perp Y
given
X
.
Here, we focus on the observation model
p(\bm{x}_{in}|\bm{x})
, whose
PDF
is given by

p(\bm{x}_{in}|\bm{x})=\frac{1}{(2\pi v_{in})^{N/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\|\bm{x}_{in}-\bm{x}\|^{2}}{2v_{in}}\right).

(22)

Taking the partial derivative with respect to
\bm{x}_{in}
, we obtain

\displaystyle v_{in}\nabla_{\bm{x}_{in}}p(\bm{x}_{in}|\bm{x})

\displaystyle=-(\bm{x}_{in}-\bm{x})p(\bm{x}_{in}|\bm{x}),

which can be rearranged as

\bm{x}p(\bm{x}_{in}|\bm{x})=\bm{x}_{in}p(\bm{x}_{in}|\bm{x})+v_{in}\nabla_{\bm{x}_{in}}p(\bm{x}_{in}|\bm{x}).

(23)

Substituting this identity into the numerator of the posterior expectation integral yields

\displaystyle\int\bm{x}p(\bm{x}_{in}|\bm{x})p(\bm{y}|\bm{x})p(\bm{x})d\bm{x}

\displaystyle=\int\left(\bm{x}_{in}p(\bm{x}_{in}|\bm{x})+v_{in}\nabla_{\bm{x}_{in}}p(\bm{x}_{in}|\bm{x})\right)p(\bm{y}|\bm{x})p(\bm{x})d\bm{x}

\displaystyle=\bm{x}_{in}\underbrace{\int p(\bm{x}_{in}|\bm{x})p(\bm{y}|\bm{x})p(\bm{x})d\bm{x}}_{p(\bm{x}_{in},\bm{y})}

\displaystyle+v_{in}\int\nabla_{\bm{x}_{in}}p(\bm{x}_{in}|\bm{x})p(\bm{y}|\bm{x})p(\bm{x})d\bm{x}.

In the second term, exchanging integration and differentiation (under standard regularity conditions) gives

\displaystyle\int\nabla_{\bm{x}_{in}}p(\bm{x}_{in}|\bm{x})p(\bm{y}|\bm{x})p(\bm{x})d\bm{x}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\bm{x}_{in}}\int p(\bm{x}_{in}|\bm{x})p(\bm{y}|\bm{x})p(\bm{x})d\bm{x}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\bm{x}_{in}}p(\bm{x}_{in},\bm{y}).

Combining these results, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X|\bm{x}_{in},\bm{y}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p(\bm{x}_{in},\bm{y})}\left(\bm{x}_{in}p(\bm{x}_{in},\bm{y})+v_{in}\nabla_{\bm{x}_{in}}p(\bm{x}_{in},\bm{y})\right)

\displaystyle=\bm{x}_{in}+v_{in}\frac{\nabla_{\bm{x}_{in}}p(\bm{x}_{in},\bm{y})}{p(\bm{x}_{in},\bm{y})}

\displaystyle=\bm{x}_{in}+v_{in}\nabla_{\bm{x}_{in}}\log p(\bm{x}_{in},\bm{y})

\displaystyle=\bm{x}_{in}+v_{in}\nabla_{\bm{x}_{in}}(\log p(\bm{x}_{in}|\bm{y})+\log p(\bm{y}))

\displaystyle=\bm{x}_{in}+v_{in}s(\bm{x}_{in}|\bm{y}),

where the last equality follows from
\nabla_{\bm{x}_{in}}\log p(\bm{y})=0
.
This completes the derivation of the conditional Tweedie’s formula, corresponding to (
1
); therefore, in the following, we denote the posterior mean by
\bm{x}_{post}=\mathbb{E}[X|\bm{x}_{in},\bm{y}]
, which serves as the
MMSE
estimate of
X
given the soft inputs
X_{in}
and
Y
.

III-C
3
Derivation of Extrinsic Output with Onsager Correction

The information passed from a
SISO
module to the subsequent stage is required to be
extrinsic
, meaning that the contribution of the input information (prior) should be appropriately removed.
In this subsection, we derive (
2
), which realizes this extrinsic message construction, based on a Gaussian approximation and its reproducibility properties.

Specifically, we approximate the posterior estimate (total information) produced by the
SISO
module by a Gaussian distribution
{\cal N}(\bm{x}_{post},v_{post}\bm{I}_{N})
with mean
\bm{x}_{post}
and variance
v_{post}
.
Under this approximation, the posterior distribution can be expressed as the product (equivalently, the sum of precisions) of the Gaussian input distribution
{\cal N}(\bm{x}_{in},v_{in}\bm{I}_{N})
and an extrinsic Gaussian distribution
{\cal N}(\bm{x}_{out},v_{out}\bm{I}_{N})
generated within the module
[
42
]
.
This leads to the relations:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{v_{post}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{v_{in}}+\frac{1}{v_{out}},

(24)

\displaystyle\frac{\bm{x}_{post}}{v_{post}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\bm{x}_{in}}{v_{in}}+\frac{\bm{x}_{out}}{v_{out}}.

(25)

Here, the posterior estimation error variance
v_{post}
can be interpreted as the per-symbol
MMSE
in regimes where a scalar-equivalent channel approximation is accurate.
Introducing the Onsager coefficient
\alpha
, defined via the average divergence of the estimator (as discussed later), we write

v_{post}=v_{in}\alpha(v_{in}),

(26)

where
\alpha(v_{in})<1
reflects the effective variance reduction induced by the denoising operation.

III-C
4
Derivation of Extrinsic Variance
v_{out}

Substituting (
26
) into (
24
) and rearranging terms, the extrinsic variance
v_{out}
is obtained by solving

\displaystyle\frac{1}{v_{out}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{v_{in}\alpha(v_{in})}-\frac{1}{v_{in}}=\frac{1-\alpha(v_{in})}{v_{in}\alpha(v_{in})}

(27)

This yields (
3
).
The resulting expression serves as the variance update equation for the extrinsic message and is consistent with the variance recursion that appears in
SE
analyses under appropriate decoupling assumptions.

III-C
5
Derivation of Extrinsic Mean
\bm{x}_{out}

Similarly, multiplying both sides of (
25
) by
v_{post}=v_{in}\alpha(v_{in})
gives:

\bm{x}_{post}=\alpha(v_{in})\bm{x}_{in}+\frac{v_{in}\alpha(v_{in})}{v_{out}}\bm{x}_{out}

(28)

Using (
27
), the coefficient simplifies to

\frac{v_{in}\alpha(v_{in})}{v_{out}}=v_{in}\alpha(v_{in})\cdot\frac{1-\alpha(v_{in})}{v_{in}\alpha(v_{in})}=1-\alpha(v_{in}),

(29)

which leads to

\bm{x}_{post}=\alpha(v_{in})\bm{x}_{in}+(1-\alpha(v_{in}))\bm{x}_{out}.

(30)

Solving this expression for
\bm{x}_{out}
yields the extrinsic output equation with Onsager correction in (
2
).
This operation corresponds to the Onsager correction, which subtracts the input contribution (
\alpha\bm{x}_{in}
)
from the posterior mean and rescales the residual accordingly.

III-C
6
Derivation of the Onsager Coefficient
\alpha
(Divergence Route)

We first recall a standard derivation of the Onsager coefficient
\alpha
that is commonly used in the literature.
In the
VAMP
framework,
\alpha
is defined as the average divergence of the
MMSE
estimator
\eta(\bm{x}_{in})\equiv\bm{x}_{in}+v_{in}s(\bm{x}_{in}|\bm{y})
, namely,

\alpha(v_{in})\equiv\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\bm{x}_{in}}\cdot\eta(\bm{x}_{in})]=1+\frac{v_{in}}{N}\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\bm{x}_{in}}\cdot s(\bm{x}_{in}|\bm{y})].

(31)

Applying Stein’s identity
[
14
]
,
\mathbb{E}[\nabla\cdot s]=-\mathbb{E}[\|s\|^{2}]
,
the divergence term can be rewritten in terms of the squared norm of the score function (
i.e.
, conditional Fisher information), yielding:

\displaystyle\alpha(v_{in})

\displaystyle=1-\frac{v_{in}}{N}\mathbb{E}[\|s(\bm{x}_{in}|\bm{y})\|^{2}]

(32)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{v_{in}}{N}J_{X_{in}|Y},

(33)

which leads to (
4
). It is well-known that we can easily derive the identity (
26
)
directly from the definition of the Onsager coefficient (
31
).

III-C
7
Derivation of Onsager Coefficient
\alpha
(Information-Theoretic Route)

It is also possible to show the identity on the Onsager coefficient (
26
) via another
information-theoretic route
.
From the conditional de Bruijn’s identity
[
17
]
, the derivative of the conditional
MI
satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dv_{in}}I(X;X_{in}|Y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}J_{X_{in}|Y}-\frac{N}{2v_{in}}.

(34)

On the other hand, the same derivative can be expressed via the I–MMSE relationship
[
13
]
as

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dv_{in}}I(X;X_{in}|Y)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2v_{in}^{2}}\mathrm{mmse}(v_{in}),

(35)

where
\mathrm{mmse}(v_{in})\equiv\mathbb{E}[\|X-\mathbb{E}[X|X_{in},Y]\|^{2}]
denotes the
MMSE
of estimating
X
from
X_{in}
and
Y
.
Equating (
34
) and (
35
) yields

\displaystyle\mathrm{mmse}(v_{in})=Nv_{in}-v_{in}^{2}J_{X_{in}|Y}.

(36)

Recalling that the posterior variance
v_{post}
is the per-symbol
MMSE
, we obtain

\displaystyle v_{post}=\frac{1}{N}\mathrm{mmse}(v_{in})

\displaystyle=v_{in}-\frac{v_{in}^{2}}{N}J_{X_{in}|Y}

(37)

\displaystyle=v_{in}\left(1-\frac{v_{in}}{N}J_{X_{in}|Y}\right)

(38)

\displaystyle=v_{in}\alpha(v_{in}).

(39)

If the Onsager coefficient
\alpha(v_{in})
is defined as the correction factor in the relation (
39
), the same expression for
\alpha(v_{in})
can be obtained without explicitly evaluating the divergence.
Alternatively,
\alpha(v_{in})
can be interpreted as a constraint required to satisfy the information-theoretic consistency between the
MMSE
and Fisher information.

III-D

Recovery of Bayes-optimal VAMP for Linear Observations

We confirm that the score-based formulation proposed in this paper recovers the standard Bayes-optimal
VAMP

[
2
]
in the special case of linear Gaussian observations,
i.e.
,
standard linear models (SLMs)
.

Consider the observation model given by a
SLM
:

\bm{y}=\bm{A}\bm{x}+\bm{n},\quad\bm{n}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\sigma_{w}^{2}\bm{I}).

(40)

The input to the
SISO
module is
\bm{x}_{in}=\bm{x}+\bm{z},\bm{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},v_{in}\bm{I}_{N})
.
In this setting, the random variables
(\bm{x},\bm{y},\bm{x}_{in})
follow a joint Gaussian distribution, and the corresponding conditional score function is therefore linear.

In this system, the posterior mean of
p(\bm{x}|\bm{x}_{in},\bm{y})
,
i.e.
, the
MMSE
estimate, is given by the well-known Wiener filter,
i.e.
,
linear MMSE (LMMSE)
estimator:

\hat{\bm{x}}_{LMMSE}=\left(\frac{1}{v_{in}}\bm{I}+\frac{1}{\sigma_{w}^{2}}\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{A}\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{v_{in}}\bm{x}_{in}+\frac{1}{\sigma_{w}^{2}}\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{y}\right).

(41)

On the other hand, working backward from Tweedie’s formula
\eta(\bm{x}_{in})=\bm{x}_{in}+v_{in}s(\bm{x}_{in}|\bm{y})
, the score function in this case must satisfy

s(\bm{x}_{in}|\bm{y})=\frac{\hat{\bm{x}}_{LMMSE}-\bm{x}_{in}}{v_{in}}.

(42)

This expression is linear in
\bm{x}_{in}
.

In standard
VAMP
, for a linear estimator of the form
\eta(\bm{x}_{in})=\bm{K}\bm{x}_{in}+\bm{c}
, the Onsager coefficient is calculated using the trace of the Jacobian as

\alpha_{VAMP}=\frac{1}{N}\Tr\left(\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial\bm{x}_{in}}\right)=\frac{1}{N}\Tr(\bm{K}).

(43)

From (
41
), the gain matrix
\bm{K}
in this case, using the posterior covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}_{post}=(v_{in}^{-1}\bm{I}+\sigma_{w}^{-2}\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{A})^{-1}
, is given by

\bm{K}=\frac{1}{v_{in}}\bm{\Sigma}_{post}.

(44)

Accordingly, the Onsager coefficient used in standard
VAMP
can be obtained by

\alpha_{VAMP}=\frac{1}{Nv_{in}}\Tr(\bm{\Sigma}_{post}).

(45)

Next, we check the score-based definition of the Onsager coefficient in (
31
), and have the following identity:

\displaystyle\alpha_{score}

\displaystyle=1+\frac{v_{in}}{N}\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\bm{x}_{in}}\cdot s(\bm{x}_{in}|\bm{y})]

\displaystyle=1+\frac{v_{in}}{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla_{\bm{x}_{in}}\cdot\left(\frac{\eta(\bm{x}_{in})-\bm{x}_{in}}{v_{in}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=1+\frac{v_{in}}{N}\left(\frac{1}{v_{in}}\mathbb{E}[\nabla\cdot\eta]-\frac{1}{v_{in}}\mathbb{E}[\nabla\cdot\bm{x}_{in}]\right)

\displaystyle=1+\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}[\Tr(\bm{K})]-\frac{1}{N}N

\displaystyle=1+\alpha_{VAMP}-1

\displaystyle=\alpha_{VAMP}.

(46)

This calculation shows that, in the linear Gaussian setting, the Onsager coefficient obtained from the score-based formulation coincides exactly with that used in standard
VAMP
.
Therefore, the formulation of the
SC-VAMP
is a complete generalization of standard Bayes-optimal
VAMP
.

III-E

Recommended Implementation: Efficient Estimation of Fisher Information

A significant computational advantage of the proposed
SC-VAMP
framework over standard
VAMP
lies in the estimation of the Onsager correction term
\alpha(v_{in})
.
In standard
VAMP
, calculating
\alpha(v_{in})
requires evaluating the divergence of the denoiser
\eta(\cdot)
, defined as
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\partial\eta_{n}}{\partial x_{in,n}}
.
For deep neural network-based denoisers, computing this divergence typically necessitates
automatic differentiation (AutoDiff)
to obtain the Jacobian trace, which is computationally expensive and memory-intensive, especially for high-dimensional signals.

In contrast,
SC-VAMP
circumvents the need for explicit derivative computations by leveraging Stein’s identity, which relates the divergence to the Fisher information.
Consequently, the Onsager coefficient can be determined solely from the squared norm of the score function output.
We propose the following efficient Monte Carlo estimation procedure using mini-batch processing, which is recommended for practical implementations.

Let
\mathcal{B}\equiv\{(\mathbf{x}_{in}^{(i)},\mathbf{y}^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{B}
be a mini-batch of
B
input samples at a given iteration, where
\mathbf{x}_{in}^{(i)}
denotes the input to the
SISO
module (prior or likelihood) and
\mathbf{y}^{(i)}
denotes the observation (if applicable).
The Fisher information is estimated empirically as the average squared
\ell_{2}
-norm of the learned score function outputs:

\hat{J}_{\theta}=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}\left\|\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{in}^{(i)}|\mathbf{y}^{(i)})\right\|^{2},

(47)

where
\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(\cdot)
represents the learned score network.
Using this estimate, the estimate of Onsager coefficient is computed as

\hat{\alpha}(v_{in})=1-\frac{v_{in}}{N}\hat{J}_{\theta}.

(48)

This approach reduces the computational complexity from the order of Jacobian computations to simple forward propagation followed by a norm calculation.

III-F

Utilization of Pre-Trained Denoisers

In many practical scenarios, a high-performance denoiser
\eta_{\text{opt}}(\cdot)
(
e.g.
, a pre-trained deep neural network like DnCNN or DRUNet) may already be available.
In such cases, it is possible to bypass the explicit training of a score model by deriving the
implicit score
directly from the denoiser outputs.
Based on Tweedie’s formula (
1
), the score function corresponding to the denoiser
\eta_{\text{opt}}
can be retrieved as

\hat{\mathbf{s}}(\mathbf{x}_{in})=\frac{\eta_{\text{opt}}(\mathbf{x}_{in})-\mathbf{x}_{in}}{v_{in}}.

(49)

Assuming
\eta_{\text{opt}}
closely approximates the
MMSE
estimator, this implicit score
\hat{\mathbf{s}}
is a consistent estimator of the true score
\nabla\log p(\mathbf{x}_{in})
.
Consequently, Stein’s identity holds, and the Fisher information required for the Onsager correction can be estimated by substituting (
49
) into the Monte Carlo estimator:

\hat{J}_{\eta}=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}\left\|\frac{\eta_{\text{opt}}(\mathbf{x}_{in}^{(i)})-\mathbf{x}_{in}^{(i)}}{v_{in}}\right\|^{2}.

(50)

This hybrid approach allows
SC-VAMP
to leverage existing
state-of-the-art (SotA)
denoisers while retaining the efficient, derivative-free computation of the Onsager term.
Note that numerical stability requires care when
v_{in}
becomes extremely small, although in typical VAMP operation, the convergence behavior usually prevents singularity issues.

IV
Optimality in Scalar Gaussian Channels

It is well established from the decoupling principle of standard
VAMP
that, in linear inverse problems, the iterative process can be characterized by an equivalent scalar Gaussian channel (
i.e.
, an
AWGN
channel) at each iteration.
Under the assumption of decoupling and Gaussian inputs, it therefore
suffices to examine whether the proposed score-based update rules recover the
MI

I(X;Y)
in this scalar Gaussian setting.
Accordingly, this section focuses on the analysis of the basic scalar
AWGN
case.

IV-A

Optimality Theorem

In this subsection, we explicitly derive the
SE
fixed point by applying the
SC-VAMP
update rules (
1
)-(
4
) to a scalar Gaussian channel.
The following theorem states that the resulting
SE
fixed point achieves the
MI
of the underlying channel.

Theorem 1

(Optimality in Scalar Gaussian Channels)
.

Consider a scalar Gaussian channel
Y=X+Z
, where
X\sim\mathcal{N}(0,P)
and noise
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
.
The
MI
associated with the fixed point of
SC-VAMP
satisfies

I_{\mathsf{VAMP}}=I(X;Y)=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1+\frac{P}{\sigma^{2}}\right),

(51)

where
I_{\mathsf{VAMP}}
denotes the
MI
corresponding to the
SE
fixed point.

IV-B

Proof

We analyze the updates of Module A (Likelihood) and Module B (Prior) in sequence.

IV-B
1
Module A (Likelihood)

The input to Module A is given by
X_{in,A}=X+W_{A}
, where
W_{A}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,v_{in,A})
.
For the Gaussian likelihood
p(y|x_{in})
, the conditional score function is linear and given by

s_{A}(x_{in}|y)=\frac{y-x_{in}}{\sigma^{2}+v_{in,A}}.

(52)

The corresponding Fisher information
J_{A}
with respect to
x_{in}
is constant:

J_{A}=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}+v_{in,A}}.

(53)

Using the definition of the Onsager coefficient in Eq. (
4
), we obtain:

\displaystyle\alpha_{A}(v_{in,A})

\displaystyle=1-v_{in,A}J_{A}

(54)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{v_{in,A}}{\sigma^{2}+v_{in,A}}=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\sigma^{2}+v_{in,A}}.

(55)

Substituting
\alpha_{A}
into the variance update rule (
3
), the extrinsic variance becomes

v_{out,A}=v_{in,A}\frac{\alpha_{A}}{1-\alpha_{A}}=v_{in,A}\frac{\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\sigma^{2}+v_{in,A}}}{\frac{v_{in,A}}{\sigma^{2}+v_{in,A}}}=\sigma^{2}.

(56)

Thus, Module A outputs an extrinsic variance equal to the noise variance
\sigma^{2}
, independent of the input variance.

IV-B
2
Module B (Prior)

The input to Module B is given by
X_{in,B}=X+W_{B}
, where
W_{B}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,v_{in,B})
.
For the Gaussian prior
X\sim\mathcal{N}(0,P)
, the score function is given by

s_{B}(x_{in})=-\frac{x_{in}}{P+v_{in,B}}.

(57)

Similarly, the corresponding Fisher information is
J_{B}=\frac{1}{P+v_{in,B}}
.
Using (
4
), the Onsager coefficient becomes

\alpha_{B}(v_{in,B})=1-v_{in,B}J_{B}=\frac{P}{P+v_{in,B}}.

(58)

Applying the variance update rule (
3
) yields

v_{out,B}=v_{in,B}\frac{\alpha_{B}}{1-\alpha_{B}}=P.

(59)

Thus, Module B outputs an extrinsic variance equal to the signal power
P
.

IV-B
3
Fixed Point and Mutual Information

The
SE
recursion
v_{in,B}^{(t)}=v_{out,A}^{(t-1)}
immediately converges to the fixed point
v_{in,B}^{*}=\sigma^{2}
.
At this fixed point, the effective channel seen by Module B is
X_{in,B}=X+Z_{eff}
, where
Z_{eff}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
.
The resulting
MI
is given by

\displaystyle I_{\mathsf{VAMP}}

\displaystyle\equiv I(X;X_{in,B})=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1+\frac{P}{v_{in,B}^{*}}\right)

(60)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1+\frac{P}{\sigma^{2}}\right)=I(X;Y).

(61)

This analytically confirms that the
MI
associated with the
SE
fixed point coincides with the true
MI

I(X;Y)
.

IV-B
4
Consistency of the Estimator

While Theorem
1
establishes consistency at the level of
the second moment, the
SC-VAMP
also correctly recovers the optimal point estimator (first moment).
To verify this, we examine the extrinsic mean messages at the fixed point.
For Module A, from (
52
) and (
55
), the extrinsic update rule (
8
) yields

x_{out,A}=\frac{v_{in,A}\left(y-x_{in}\right)+v_{in,A}x_{in,A}}{v_{in,A}}=y.

(62)

Thus, the extrinsic message from the likelihood module reduces to the observation itself.
For Module B, which employs a zero-mean prior, the extrinsic message is
x_{out,B}=0
.
Consequently, at the fixed point, Module B receives
x_{in,B}=y
with effective noise variance
v_{in,B}^{*}=\sigma^{2}
.
The final estimate produced by the algorithm is therefore

\hat{x}_{\mathsf{VAMP}}=\mathbb{E}[X\mid X_{in,B}=y]=\frac{P}{P+\sigma^{2}}y.

(63)

This coincides with the
LMMSE
estimator (Wiener filter) for the scalar Gaussian channel, indicating consistency in both information-theoretic limits and point estimation accuracy.

Remark 1

(Generalization to Vector Linear Gaussian Models)
.

The result established in Theorem
1
for the scalar case naturally extends to vector linear Gaussian models
\bm{y}=\bm{A}\bm{x}+\bm{z}
with Gaussian priors.
A fundamental property of
VAMP
is that, for an
RRI
matrix
\bm{A}
in the large-system limit, the estimation problem asymptotically decouples into a collection of parallel, independent scalar Gaussian channels.
Since the proposed score-based update rules recover the optimal behavior for each underlying scalar Gaussian channel, the
SC-VAMP
algorithm is theoretically consistent with asymptotically achieving the corresponding
MI
in the vector setting.
In particular, the resulting
MI
is given by
I(\bm{X};\bm{Y})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\log(1+\frac{\lambda_{i}P}{\sigma^{2}}),
where
\lambda_{i}
denotes the eigenvalues of
\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{A}
.

V
Data-Driven Approach for SC-VAMP

When the analytical form of the score function is available, both the denoiser and the Onsager coefficient can be derived in closed form.
For instance, in linear observation models, the score function admits an explicit analytical expression.
A key advantage of
SC-VAMP
is that, even when such
analytical forms are unavailable, both the denoiser and the
Onsager coefficient can instead be obtained through a learned
score model.

V-A

Denoising Score Matching (DSM)

This becomes particularly powerful when the score function
s(\bm{x}_{in})=\nabla_{\bm{x}_{in}}\log p(\bm{x}_{in})
(for Module B)
or
s(\bm{x}_{in}|\bm{y})=\nabla_{\bm{x}_{in}}\log p(\bm{x}_{in}|\bm{y})
(for Module A) is not analytically tractable, which is common
in problems involving complex signal priors
p_{X}
or nonlinear observation models
p_{Y|X}
.
In such settings, a
data-driven
approach to inverse problems becomes feasible through learned score models.

In these cases, the true score function can be approximated by a parameterized model, typically a deep neural network
\bm{s}_{\theta}(\cdot)
, trained using
denoising score matching (DSM)
.
The core idea of
DSM

[
20
,
21
]
is to learn parameters
\theta
by minimizing the expected squared difference between the network output and the true score.
Although the true score
\nabla_{\bm{x}_{in}}\log p(\bm{x}_{in})
is unknown,
DSM
yields a tractable training objective.
For the denoiser module (Module B) with input noise variance
v_{in}
, the
DSM
objective is

\displaystyle J_{DSM}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim p_{X},\bm{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,v_{in}\bm{I}_{N})}\left[\left\|\bm{s}_{\theta}(\bm{x}+\bm{z})-\nabla_{\bm{x}_{in}}\log p(\bm{x}_{in}|\bm{x})\right\|^{2}\right]

(64)

where
\bm{x}_{in}=\bm{x}+\bm{z}
.
The conditional score
\nabla_{\bm{x}_{in}}\log p(\bm{x}_{in}|\bm{x})
corresponds to the Gaussian noise model
\mathcal{N}(\bm{x}_{in}|\bm{x},v_{in}\bm{I}_{N})
, and is given analytically by

\nabla_{\bm{x}_{in}}\log p(\bm{x}_{in}|\bm{x})=-\frac{\bm{x}_{in}-\bm{x}}{v_{in}}=-\frac{\bm{z}}{v_{in}}.

(65)

As a result, the objective simplifies to the computationally tractable loss:

\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim P_{X},\bm{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,v_{in}\bm{I}_{N})}\left[\left\|\bm{s}_{\theta}(\bm{x}+\bm{z})+\frac{\bm{z}}{v_{in}}\right\|^{2}\right],

(66)

which requires only samples from the prior
P_{X}
.
A similar objective can be derived for learning the conditional score
\bm{s}_{\theta}(\bm{x}_{in}|\bm{y})
.
It should be noted that a learned score function with small
v_{in}
is unstable
if the prior of
\bm{x}
is not differentiable,
e.g.
, a mixture of the Dirac’s delta functions. For such a case,
Gaussian smoothing for
\bm{x}
could mitigate the problem.

V-B

Estimating Fisher Information

Once the network
\bm{s}_{\theta}
is trained, it serves as a

plug-in
score model within the
SC-VAMP
algorithm.
The posterior mean is then computed via Tweedie’s formula as

\bm{x}_{post}=\bm{x}_{in}+v_{in}\bm{s}_{\theta}(\bm{x}_{in}|\bm{y}).

(67)

The Onsager coefficient
\alpha(v_{in})
is estimated using the Fisher information associated with the
learned
score model.
Given the learned score model
s_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot)
, the Fisher information can be approximated by Monte Carlo estimation:

\displaystyle J_{\theta}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}\|s_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_{in,i}|\bm{y}_{i})\|^{2}

(68)

\displaystyle\simeq\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{s}_{\theta}(X_{in}|Y)\|^{2}],

(69)

\displaystyle\alpha_{\theta}(v_{in})

\displaystyle=1-\frac{v_{in}}{N}J_{\theta},

(70)

where
\bm{x}_{in,i}\sim p_{X_{in}}
and
\bm{y}_{i}\sim p_{Y}
denote samples drawn from the corresponding distributions.
In practice,
J_{\theta}
can be estimated empirically during the
VAMP
iterations by calculating the average squared norm of the network’s output vector.
This data-driven procedure enables the proposed
SC-VAMP
framework to be applied to a much broader class of complex and nonlinear inference problems.

VI
Decoupling Principle and State Evolution

The empirical success of iterative algorithms such as
VAMP
and AMP is underpinned by the
decoupling principle
.
In the large-system limit (
M=\delta N\to\infty
with a fixed ratio
\delta=M/N
), this principle states that the original
N
-dimensional vector problem statistically decomposes into
N
parallel, independent scalar Gaussian channels.
This decomposition is fundamental, as it enables the iterative dynamics of the algorithm to be tracked by a simple one-dimensional recursion known as
SE
.
In this section, we discuss the structural conditions that facilitate this decoupling—specifically, statistical symmetry and mechanisms that suppress short-range correlations, such as locally tree-like factor graphs or high-dimensional random mixing—and provide an information-theoretic justification for the Gaussian
approximation underlying
SE
.

VI-A

Decoupling for SC-VAMP

For
SC-VAMP
, the decoupling principle implies that the input
to the nonlinear
SISO
module (denoiser)
\eta(\cdot)
at iteration
t
can be modeled as
\bm{x}_{in}^{(t)}=\bm{x}+\bm{e}^{(t)}
, where
\bm{x}
is the true signal and
\bm{e}^{(t)}
is an error vector that is asymptotically
i.i.d.
Gaussian,
\bm{e}^{(t)}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},v^{(t)}\bm{I}_{N})
.

This decoupling allows the algorithm’s performance to be tracked by
SE
, which reduces the high-dimensional dynamics to the scalar recursion of the variance
v^{(t)}
.
The
SC-VAMP
framework—defined through Tweedie’s formula and the Fisher
information–based Onsager term—naturally adopts
SE
as a central theoretical tool.
However, such decoupling is not universally guaranteed.
In general, the validity of the decoupling principle relies on two
key ingredients: (i) a form of statistical symmetry across coordinates, and (ii) a mechanism that suppresses short-range correlations, such as locally tree-like factor graphs or high-dimensional random mixing (
e.g.
,
RRI
linear transforms).

VI-B

Statistical Symmetry

The first requirement, often referred to as
identically distributed
, is a direct consequence of statistical symmetry, or permutation invariance.
If the system—such as the prior
p_{X}
, the observation operator
\bm{A}
, and the
nonlinear function
f
—is statistically invariant under permutations of its coordinates, then no coordinate
i
is distinguished from any other coordinate
j
.
Consequently, the marginal distributions of the error components
e_{i}^{(t)}
and
e_{j}^{(t)}
must be identical:

P(e_{i}^{(t)})=P(e_{j}^{(t)})\quad\forall i,j.

(71)

This symmetry implies that tracking a single scalar variance
v(t)={\mathbb{E}}[e_{i}^{2}]
is sufficient, provided that the error components are also independent.

VI-C

Suppressing Short-Range Correlations

The second ingredient is the suppression of short-range statistical dependencies between messages.
In sparse message-passing systems, this requirement is realized when
the underlying
factor graph (FG)
is
locally tree-like

[
25
]
.
In iterative message-passing algorithm, correlations between messages arises from

short cycles (loops)
in the graph. If a message sent by a node can return
to itself via a short path (
e.g.
,
A\to B\to C\to A
), then the messages received by node
A
from
B
and
C
will be correlated, as they share a recent common ancestor.
Such correlations violate the independence assumption underlying the decoupling principle.

A graph is said to be
locally tree-like
if, in the limit
N\to\infty
, the probability of the existence of any short cycle vanishes.
In this case, messages arriving at a node are asymptotically independent.
Consequently, in sparse graphical models, local tree-likeness serves as the canonical mechanism for suppressing short-range correlations and ensuring effective decoupling.
In dense linear models, such as standard
VAMP
, an analogous effect can be interpreted, from a Bayesian inference perspective, as being achieved through
high-dimensional random mixing, as discussed in the next subsection.

VI-D

Achieving Decoupling in Practice

There exist practical systems that satisfy the two conditions.
Representative examples include the following.

VI-D
1
Component-wise Functions (
e.g.
,
GLMs
)

When the nonlinearity acts component-wise,
i.e.
,
y_{i}=f(x_{i})
, the
associated
FG
is inherently symmetric and perfectly sparse, with no cycles.
This represents the simplest setting in which the decoupling principle holds.

VI-D
2
Right-Rotationally Invariant Matrices (
e.g.
, standard VAMP)

For dense matrices used in
VAMP
, the key property is
right-rotationally invariant (RRI)
, meaning that the matrix’s right singular vectors are uniformly (Haar) distributed over the orthogonal group.
Unlike standard
AMP
which relies on the
i.i.d.
nature of matrix entries,
VAMP
leverages this rotational symmetry.
As a result, the estimation error vector remains isotropic (statistically symmetric) across iterations.
Under standard large-system assumptions for
RRI
matrices and Lipschitz denoisers, while it is not immediate that the joint distribution of all error coordinates is
i.i.d.
, any finite-dimensional subset of coordinates becomes asymptotically
independent and identically distributed
.
This weaker, marginal form of decoupling is sufficient to support the use of scalar
SE
in the sense of an empirical fixed-point analysis, without explicitly relying on a locally tree-like graph structure.

VI-D
3
Random Interleaver (
e.g.
, Turbo/LDPC Codes)

This provides a key mechanism for handling structured systems.
Consider a function
{f}
that represents a locally coupled system, in which each output component depends only on a small subset of input variables (
i.e.
, the Jacobian of
{f}
is sparse).
A typical example is convolution, which is sparse but highly structured and often lacks both statistical symmetry and a locally tree-like
FG
due to fixed local patterns.
By
sandwiching

{f}
with random permutations (interleavers),
i.e.
,
{f}^{\prime}(\bm{x})={\pi}^{-1}{f}({\pi}\bm{x})
, one can simultaneously:

•

Restore symmetry: The resulting system becomes statistically permutation-invariant.

•

Enforce tree-likeness: The interleaver disrupts fixed local cycles in
{f}
, yielding a random sparse graph that, with high probability, becomes locally tree-like as
N\to\infty
.

This principle underlies the validity of density evolution for Turbo and
LDPC
codes.

VI-D
4
Random Modulation (random orthogonal/unitary matrices)

This approach can be viewed as a generalization of random interleaving to linear inverse problems.
As discussed in recent studies
[
8
]
, for an arbitrary channel matrix
\bm{A}
—which may be deterministic and structured—right-multiplication by a random unitary matrix
\bm{\Xi}
(random modulation) yields an equivalent
channel
\bm{J}=\bm{A}\bm{\Xi}
.
The transformed matrix
\bm{J}
shares the same singular values as
\bm{A}
but has asymptotically Haar-distributed singular vectors.
As a result, the estimation error becomes statistically isotropic.
From an inference perspective, this random modulation can be interpreted as placing
\bm{J}
in the same
universality class
as
RRI
matrices, suggesting that
SE
predictions remain accurate even for structured channels.
Conceptually, this operation acts as a generalized linear interleaver that
whitens
spatial correlations in the channel.

VI-E

Information-Theoretic Perspective via Entropic CLT

SE
tracks the evolution of second-order statistics, such as the variance
v(t)
, under the implicit assumption that the effective estimation error can be approximated by a Gaussian distribution.
Importantly, the validity of
SE
does not require convergence of the full joint distribution of the error vector to a Gaussian law; rather, it relies on a weaker form of convergence, namely the empirical convergence of finite-dimensional marginals, which is sufficient to characterize pseudo-Lipschitz observables via a law-of-large-numbers effect.
From this perspective, the entropic
CLT
provides a complementary, information-theoretic viewpoint on the Gaussian approximation commonly invoked in
SE
analyses.
Whereas the classical
CLT
guarantees convergence in distribution, the entropic
CLT
establishes a stronger notion of convergence in terms of information measures.
Specifically, Barron
[
19
]
showed that for a sum of independent random variables, the
KL
-divergence to the corresponding Gaussian distribution decreases monotonically as the number of summands increases.
Tulino and Verdú
[
15
]
further analyzed this phenomenon using the I-MMSE relationship,, quantifying the reduction of non-Gaussianity from an information-theoretic standpoint.

In
SC-VAMP
, the linear mixing step—implemented via multiplication by an orthogonal matrix
\bm{V}^{T}
or a random modulator
\bm{\Xi}
—forms weighted sums
of the
N
input error components.
Under the decoupling assumption, whereby these inputs are asymptotically independent, the entropic
CLT
provides an information-theoretic perspective suggesting that the distribution of each output component becomes increasingly Gaussian in the
KL
-divergence sense as the system size
N
grows.
From this viewpoint, the linear mixing step can be interpreted as acting as
Gaussianizer
, attenuating structured non-Gaussian effects introduced by the nonlinear denoiser
Accordingly, for each fixed coordinate
i
and any fixed iteration index
t
, the marginal distribution of the effective noise may approach a Gaussian distribution in the sense that

D_{\text{KL}}(P_{e_{i}^{(t)}}\|\mathcal{N}(0,v^{(t)}))\to 0\quad\text{as }N\to\infty,

(72)

under appropriate independence and mixing assumptions.
While such entropic convergence is not required for
SE
to hold, it offers additional intuition for the Gaussian approximation commonly employed in
SE
and for the use of second-order moments as effective summary statistics in empirical fixed-point analysis.

More generally, one may insert random orthogonal/unitary transforms and their inverses around both modules A and B, so that each nonlinear block operates in a randomly mixed coordinate system while the overall end-to-end mapping remains unchanged.
Such double-sided random mixing has the potential to homogenize and decorrelate the estimation errors seen by both modules, thereby facilitating decoupling, even when both the “channel” and “prior” modules involve complex nonlinearities.
A rigorous characterization of these schemes is left for future work.

Along a related direction,
SE
-aware batch normalization
and
deep-unfolded Onsager corrections
may allow
SE
to serve as a useful design guideline even beyond the classical large-system setting.
In particular,
SE
-based theory may offer systematic insights into improving performance in nonlinear and finite-dimensional systems with correlated sources.

Linear Observation System

In this subsection, we present numerical experiments to validate the proposed

\bm{y}=\bm{A}\bm{x}+\bm{n}

\bm{n}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\sigma_{n}^{2}\bm{I}_{M})

The signal follows a

p_{X}(x)=(1-\rho)\delta(x)+\rho\cdot\mathcal{N}(x;0,\sigma_{x}^{2}),

(73)

The

\bm{A}=\bm{U}\bm{D}\bm{V}^{\top}

\bm{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}

The experimental parameters are the following: signal dimension

2

We employ a mini-batch implementation with batch size
B=200
to estimate the Fisher information required for the Onsager correction.
Specifically, the Fisher information

is estimated as the sample average of the squared score norm over the mini-batch:

\hat{J}=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}\|\bm{s}(\bm{x}_{\mathrm{in}}^{(i)}|\bm{y}^{(i)})\|^{2},

where
\bm{s}(\cdot|\cdot)
denotes the score function.
This mini-batch estimation corresponds to (
47
) in the proposed framework and enables efficient computation of the Onsager coefficient
\alpha
without explicit Jacobian calculations.

VII-B

Correlated Prior with Learned Score Function

In this subsection, we demonstrate the effectiveness of
SC-VAMP
with a
learned
score function for a correlated prior distribution.
Unlike the previous experiment using analytical scores, this experiment validates the complete data-driven pipeline including
DSM
training and mini-batch Stein calibration.

VII-B
1
Correlated 2D Gaussian Prior

We consider a structured prior where the signal
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is partitioned into
N/2
pairs of correlated Gaussian variables.
Each pair
(x_{2k-1},x_{2k})
for
k=1,\ldots,N/2
follows a bivariate Gaussian distribution:

\begin{pmatrix}x_{2k-1}\\
x_{2k}\end{pmatrix}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\bm{0},\begin{pmatrix}\sigma^{2}&\xi\sigma^{2}\\
\xi\sigma^{2}&\sigma^{2}\end{pmatrix}\right),

(74)

where
\sigma^{2}
is the marginal variance and
\xi\in(-1,1)
is the correlation coefficient.
This prior captures pairwise dependencies that cannot be efficiently exploited by element-wise denoisers, making it a suitable testbed for evaluating learned score functions that respect the correlation structure.

VII-B
2
Pairwise Score Network Architecture

To capture the pairwise correlation structure, we employ a neural network that processes pairs of variables jointly.
The score network
s_{\theta}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
takes as input a pair
(r_{1},r_{2})
along with the noise level
\sigma
, and outputs the corresponding score vector
(s_{1},s_{2})
.

The network architecture consists of a
multi-layer perceptron (MLP)
with the following structure:

•

Input layer:
3
neurons (pair values
r_{1},r_{2}
and noise level
\sigma
)

•

Hidden layers:
3
fully-connected layers with
128
neurons each

•

Activation function: Softplus

•

Output layer:
2
neurons (score values
s_{1},s_{2}
)

VII-B
3
Denoising Score Matching Training

The score network is trained via
DSM
.
Given samples
\bm{x}_{0}
from the prior and noise
\bm{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\sigma^{2}\bm{I})
with
\sigma
uniformly sampled from
[\sigma_{\min},\sigma_{\max}]=[0.01,3.0]
, the
DSM
loss is:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DSM}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}_{0},\sigma,\bm{z}}\left[\left\|s_{\theta}(\bm{x}_{0}+\bm{z},\sigma)+\frac{\bm{z}}{\sigma^{2}}\right\|^{2}\right].

(75)

Training is performed using the Adam optimizer with learning rate
10^{-3}
and cosine annealing schedule over
20,000
iterations with batch size
256
.

VII-B
4
Mini-batch Stein Calibration

A key component of our implementation is the mini-batch Stein calibration
[
26
]
, which enforces a necessary Stein moment constraint via a scalar rescaling.
For each
SISO
module evaluation, we compute a scalar calibration coefficient
c
using the Stein identity:

c=\frac{-N}{(1/B)\sum_{i=1}^{B}\bm{r}^{(i)\top}s_{\theta}(\bm{r}^{(i)},\sigma)},

(76)

where
\{\bm{r}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{B}
is the current mini-batch of inputs.
This calibration ensures that
\mathbb{E}[\bm{r}^{\top}(c\cdot s_{\theta}(\bm{r},\sigma))]=-N
, which is a necessary condition for the score function of an
N
-dimensional Gaussian-corrupted distribution.
The calibrated denoiser output is then computed as:

\bm{x}_{\mathrm{post}}=\bm{r}+v_{\mathrm{in}}\cdot c\cdot s_{\theta}(\bm{r},\sqrt{v_{\mathrm{in}}}),

(77)

where
v_{\mathrm{in}}
is the input variance. This mini-batch calibration is applied consistently in both the
SC-VAMP
algorithm and the
SE
computation, ensuring fair comparison.

VIII
Conclusion

In this paper, we have proposed the
SC-VAMP
framework, a data-driven approach to high-dimensional inverse problems.
By integrating message-passing algorithms with learned score-based models, we have
established a framework that broadens the applicability of standard
VAMP
to settings that go beyond the classical linear Gaussian case.

A fundamental advantage of the proposed method is its universality with respect to the underlying probability distributions.
Unlike standard
VAMP
, which often relies on linear Gaussian assumptions or requires analytical derivation of denoisers and their Jacobians,
SC-VAMP
leverages learned score functions to construct (near) optimal nonlinear
estimators in a data-driven manner.
This enables applications to a broader class of problems, including:

•

Arbitrary priors:
Correlated, complex, data-driven priors, such as natural images or semantic latent representations, for which analytical modeling is challenging.

•

General nonlinear observations:
Systems governed by deterministic nonlinear maps
\bm{y}=f(\bm{x})
, such as sensor saturation, optical nonlinearities, or black-box physical processes.

Through this abstraction,
SC-VAMP
can be viewed as a natural extension of
VAMP
from specialized linear Gaussian settings toward more general inference scenarios encountered in practice.

Our analysis also provides a score-based reinterpretation of standard
VAMP
,
unifying the estimator update and the Onsager correction through conditional score and Fisher quantities, which offers a principled interface for extending

VAMP
to learned modules.
While
SC-VAMP
is formulated using score functions in this paper, extending the framework to velocity-field framework (
e.g.
, flow matching
[
34
]
and rectified flow
[
35
]
) is a promising future direction.
Since there exists a one-to-one algebraic correspondence between the score function and the velocity field under a given probability path, an
SC-VAMP
–like algorithm may be constructed using a velocity-field network to implement the
MMSE
estimator.
In practice, velocity-field training is often observed to be more stable than score-based training, particularly in low-noise regimes.
Deriving an
SC-VAMP
formulation within the rectified flow paradigm would be
an interesting future problem, as it could bridge message-passing algorithms with the principles of
L_{2}
optimal transport.

Hybrid Approach with Automatic Differentiation

While the data-driven approach using
DSM
described in Section
V
is powerful for unknown or complex priors, many engineering applications involve a well-defined, deterministic observation model.
In such cases, treating the observation process as a black box to be learned might be inefficient.
In this Appendix section, we propose a
hybrid
architecture that combines the learned score function for the prior (Module B) with a computation-based
MMSE
estimator with
AutoDiff

[
33
]
for the observation model (Module A).

-A

Differentiable Observation Models

Consider the nonlinear observation model:

\bm{y}=f(\bm{x})+\bm{n},\quad\bm{n}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\sigma_{n}^{2}\bm{I}_{M}),

(78)

where
f:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{M}
is a known, differentiable function representing the physical measurement process (
e.g.
, channel nonlinearity, partial differential equation solver
[
28
]
,
or rendering equation).
Unlike the prior distribution
p_{X}
, the conditional log likelihood
p(\bm{y}|\bm{x})
is explicitly known:

\log p(\bm{y}|\bm{x})=-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{n}^{2}}\|\bm{y}-f(\bm{x})\|^{2}+\text{const}.

(79)

Modern deep learning frameworks (
e.g.
, PyTorch, TensorFlow, JAX) allow
for the efficient computation of the gradient of this likelihood with respect to
\bm{x}
using
AutoDiff
, specifically via the
vector-Jacobian product (VJP)
, without explicitly instantiating the Jacobian matrix:

\nabla_{\bm{x}}\log p(\bm{y}|\bm{x})=\frac{1}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}[\bm{J}_{f}(\bm{x})]^{\top}(\bm{y}-f(\bm{x})),

(80)

where
J_{f}(\bm{x})
denotes the Jacobian matrix of
f(\bm{x})
at
\bm{x}
.

-B

Posterior Sampling for MMSE Estimation

For Module A, the goal is to compute the
MMSE
estimate (or equivalently, the conditional score
s_{A}(\bm{x}_{in}|\bm{y})
) given the input
\bm{x}_{in}=\bm{x}+\bm{z}
, where
\bm{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},v_{in}\bm{I})
.
The
MMSE
estimator is given by the posterior expectation:

\bm{x}_{post}=\mathbb{E}[\bm{X}|\bm{x}_{in},\bm{y}]=\int\bm{x}\frac{p(\bm{y}|\bm{x})\mathcal{N}(\bm{x}_{in}|\bm{x},v_{in}\bm{I})}{p(\bm{y},\bm{x}_{in})}d\bm{x}.

(81)

Since the integral involving the nonlinear
f(\bm{x})
is generally intractable,
we can approximate this expectation using Monte Carlo sampling driven by Langevin dynamics.
The gradient of the log-posterior required for Langevin sampling is given by

\nabla_{\bm{x}}\log p(\bm{x}|\bm{x}_{\text{in}},\bm{y})=\nabla_{\bm{x}}\log p(\bm{y}|\bm{x})-\frac{\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{\text{in}}}{v_{\text{in}}}.

(82)

Specifically, we employ the
unadjusted Langevin algorithm (ULA)
to generate samples.
The discrete-time update rule at iteration
k
with step size
\delta
is given by

\displaystyle\bm{x}^{(k+1)}

\displaystyle=\bm{x}^{(k)}+\delta\left(\nabla_{\bm{x}}\log p(\bm{y}|\bm{x}^{(k)})-\frac{\bm{x}^{(k)}-\bm{x}_{in}}{v_{in}}\right)

\displaystyle+\sqrt{2\delta}\bm{z}^{(k)},

(83)

where
\bm{z}^{(k)}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I}_{N})
is standard Gaussian noise.
By repeating this update for
K
steps (possibly with a decaying step size schedule), the sequence approaches the target posterior distribution.
Since the first term inside the parenthesis is computed efficiently via
AutoDiff
as described in (
80
), the entire sampling process remains computationally tractable.
We thus can generate samples
\bm{x}^{(k)}\sim p(\bm{x}|\bm{x}_{\text{in}},\bm{y})
and approximate

\bm{x}_{post}=\mathbb{E}[\bm{X}|\bm{x}_{in},\bm{y}]\approx\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bm{x}^{(k)},

(84)

where
K
is the number of samples.
The estimated value of
\bm{x}_{post}
can be used in the
SISO
module instead of (
1
).

This hybrid approach allows the
SC-VAMP
algorithm to leverage the exact physical model
f(\bm{x})
without training a separate score network for the likelihood, while still utilizing the powerful learned score
s_{\theta}(\bm{x})
for the complex prior in Module B.

-C

Computational Complexity

One potential challenge of the proposed hybrid approach is the computational cost associated with the iterative Langevin sampling, especially when the forward operator
f(\bm{x})
involves heavy numerical simulations (
e.g.
, solving
PDEs
as in
[
28
]
).
However, this cost can be significantly mitigated by leveraging the iterative nature of
SC-VAMP
.
Since the estimate
\bm{x}_{in}
changes gradually across
SC-VAMP
iterations, we can employ a
warm-start
strategy, initializing the Langevin chain with the current input
\bm{x}_{in}
or the samples from the previous iteration.
This strategy drastically reduces the number of steps
K
required for burn-in.
Furthermore, the sampling process is inherently parallelizable.
By evaluating
f(\bm{x})
for multiple particles simultaneously on a
graphics processing unit (GPU)
, the wall-clock time can be kept within a reasonable range for many practical applications.
Ultimately, this approach offers a trade-off: it incurs a higher computational cost compared to simple linearization (
e.g.
, Extended
VAMP
) but provides a more accurate score estimation in strongly nonlinear regimes where linearization fails.
