Color and orientation feature-attention sensors for efficient recognition
Authors: Hongzhao Wu, Xiangwei Su, Cheng Zhang, Tianjiao Zhang, Yiqi Chen, Zheng Bian, Xinlong Zeng, Chaokai Zhu, Maoxin Tian, Jialei Miao, Caijing Liang, Yang Xu, Bin Yu, Yang Chai, Yuda Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A key challenge in visual search is that target objects defined by specific features are often mixed with distractors. This demands intelligent vision systems capable of performing selective feature acquisition instead of randomly or exhaustively scanning the environment. Here, we report a feature-attention vision sensor that integrates a semantic and geometric “dual-attention” mechanism directly at the hardware level. The sensor features reconfigurable spectral responsivity from the visible to near-infrared range, enabling tunable color attention with a spectral rejection ratio of up to 2 × 104. Furthermore, we implement tunable orientation attention through an intensity-responsivity matrix. In automatic driving, the feature-attention vision sensor recognizes no stopping sign with 99.52% accuracy comparable to RGB cameras while reducing data generation by 66.7%. This approach mimics this biological “sense-only-the-useful” strategy, eliminating the need for bulky optical filters and postprocessing algorithms and offering a promising pathway for the realization of compact, resource-efficient intelligent vision systems.

INTRODUCTION

Efficient visual scene understanding relies heavily on the selective extraction of target features. In visual search tasks, target objects defined by mission-specific features are often embedded among visually similar distractors, necessitating substantial computational resources for accurate discrimination. Conventional RGB camera operates on a “capture-all-then-discard” principle, recording all optical information regardless of its necessity (Fig. 1A), which leads to substantial data redundancy and high computational costs (1–6). Alternatively, traditional grayscale camera maps multidimensional optical information onto a single intensity scale, which results in the critical loss of optical information. This mismatch between the acquisition modes of conventional sensors and the task-specific demand for targeted optical information inevitably leads to either severe data redundancy or critical information loss.

The human visual system has a “sense-only-the-useful” strategy to selectively extract objects of interest from complex visual stimuli according to target feature (Fig. 1B) (7–12). When we search for a specific kind of object, for example, a red O among red and green Qs, we adopt the feature attention. In the search, we first ignore the green letters (color attention) and then discriminate between the shapes O and Q (orientation attention). The adjustment of visual attention according to the object feature allows us to efficiently capture and process visual information by focusing on task-relevant features while suppressing irrelevant input (8, 10, 13). Consequently, it is highly desirable to construct an intelligent image sensor capable of performing precise, feature-attention–driven sensing of task-relevant information directly at the physical sampling stage (Fig. 1, C and D) (14–29).

In the context of visual scene understanding, color encodes critical cues regarding material properties and object categories (semantic information), while orientation delineates geometric structures and spatial relationships (geometric information). Fundamentally, these two types of visual information are indispensable features for perception, effectively addressing the elemental questions of “what” and “where,” respectively. Reliable scene interpretation relies on the synergistic integration of these features through bidirectional constraints and coinference; relying solely on either color or geometry is often insufficient. For instance, in autonomous driving, traffic signs among regulatory signs (red), warning signs (yellow), and guide signs (blue) are morphologically similar and distinguishable primarily by color, whereas red regulatory signs, such as no standing versus no stopping (Fig. 1, A and B), share identical chromatic features but are differentiated by their distinct geometric contours. Ultimately, robust visual perception relies on the synergistic modulation of attention to both semantic (color) and geometric (orientation) dimensions.

In this work, we report a feature-attention vision sensor that integrates a semantic and geometric “dual-attention” mechanism directly at the hardware level. A three-terminal image sensor allows the device’s physical properties to be modulated independently: The gate terminal reconfigures heterojunction band alignment for switchable color attention, and the drain terminal adjusts intensity responsivity for tunable orientation attention. The device exhibits a high spectral rejection ratio of up to 2 × 104 (between 480 and 1064 nm), ensuring precise feature attention. In autonomous driving, our feature-attention imaging sensor accurately and efficiently captures color and orientation features of no stopping signs, thereby suppressing the interference and reducing the data volume by 66.7%, as well as achieving high recognition accuracy up to 99.52%. Our approach represents a fundamental paradigm shift from the conventional capture-all-then-discard model to a highly efficient sense-only-the-useful strategy, paving the way for compact, intelligent vision systems capable of real-time, in-sensor feature extraction.

RESULTS

Top-down attention mechanism

The top-down attention mechanism enables humans to selectively focus on targets by having task-relevant features. When attention is directed toward a target, sensory input from that target is enhanced, while input from competing stimuli is suppressed. This selective modulation optimizes computational resource allocation, facilitating efficient and accurate visual processing. Concisely, the core of top-down attention of human visual system is to selectively capture information. Therefore, to design a feature-attention intelligent photodetector, the device should display the ability to capture the selected information based on the target feature.

For color attention, the device should selectively capture color information, that is, to enhance the responsivity to specific color and suppress the responsivity to other colors. In other words, we need to design a device that can reconfigure the spectral responsivity according to the attention (Fig. 1C). Two-dimensional materials have abundant band structures with rich electric-field tunability, which can be used to design and build high-quality heterojunction based on band alignment (30–32). Type-II heterojunction presents varied band alignment structures and carrier transport characteristics under electric field, which is conducive to realizing color attention based on reconfigurable spectral responsivity (25). For orientation attention, the device should selectively capture orientation information, that is, to enhance the response to specific orientation and suppress the response to other orientations. Inspired by the receptive field of human visual system with tunable orientation response (fig. S1) (7), we design a receptive field to in situ extract orientation features. Figure 1D illustrates the convolutional computation (input ⊗ receptive field) implemented in the sensor array for 45° orientation attention. The output image highlights the 45° orientation features while suppressing the response to other orientations. The in-sensor orientation attention requires that the photodetectors within the array must exhibit varying intensity responsivity (weights; Fig. 1E) and be arranged along a specific orientation (Fig. 1F). Compared with two-terminal counterparts, the three-terminal field-effect transistor offers a superior degree of physical tunability, providing a platform to integrate orientation attention alongside intrinsic color selectivity. Figure 1F presents the circuit diagram and corresponding optical microscope image of the fabricated sensor array, aiming at simultaneous color and orientation dual-attention imaging.

The alignment between the visual sensor’s output (S) and human attention (F) serves as a metric for evaluating the sensor’s ability to capture relevant information of interest. The presence of high response at attention focus in imaging results can be described with normalized scanpath saliency (NSS) calculated by the following equation (33, 34)NSS=1N∑i=1NS¯(i)×F(i)(1)N=∑iF(i)=255 and S¯=S−μ(S)σ(S)(2)where N is the total number of human attention positions, μ(S) is the mean value of visual sensor imaging, and σ(S) is the standard deviation. The NSS is significantly greater than zero when the imaging result corresponds to the human top-down attention focus, exhibiting that the sensor can realize feature-attention imaging. Conversely, NSS approaches zero when imaging results show insignificant correlation with human top-down attention focus. The presence of low response at nonattention focus in imaging results can be quantified with specificity calculated by the following equation (35)Specificity=TNTN+FN(3)where TN is the number of nonattention focus pixel with low response and FN is the number of nonattention focus pixel with high response. The specificity is significantly close to 1 when the imaging result corresponds to the human top-down attention focus, exhibiting that the sensor can suppress the response of nonattention focus. Conversely, specificity is close to 0 when imaging results show insignificant correlation with human top-down attention focus. Therefore, feature-attention imaging can increase the NSS and specificity of imaging results, enabling the sensor to reduce interference from redundant data and efficiently capture and process image information. The detailed computing method of NSS and specificity is depicted in text S1.

Reconfigurable spectral responsivity for color-attention imaging

We design a three-terminal field-effect transistor based on type-II heterojunction, aiming at color attention and orientation attention. Figure 2A depicts the schematic of a three-terminal photodetector, with the MoTe2/MoS2 heterojunction as channel and the 285-nm-thick SiO2/Si as the gate structure. The Au electrodes are patterned on the MoTe2/MoS2 heterojunction as the source and drain terminals. The gate-source voltage Vgs can electrostatically tune the band alignment of MoTe2/MoS2 heterojunction, and the drain-source voltage Vds can amplify the photocurrent, working as two control terminals to select color and orientation information, respectively. A corresponding optical microscope image of the heterojunction is also provided in Fig. 2A, where the MoTe2 (4.7 nm thick; fig. S2B) and MoS2 (8.5 nm thick; fig. S2B) flakes are depicted with dashed blue and red lines, respectively. To confirm material quality, we performed the Raman measurements (fig. S3), indicating the formation of a high-quality van der Waals (vdW) heterojunction (36, 37).

The electrical properties of the MoTe2/MoS2 heterojunction were displayed in fig. S4. We further measured the transfer characteristics of the MoTe2/MoS2 heterojunction device at drain-source voltage Vds = 1 V under dark conditions (Fig. 2B). The drain bias is applied to MoTe2, while MoS2 is connected to the ground. The heterojunction device exhibits typical Gaussian-type antiambipolar response (Imax = 74 nA at Vgs = −19 V), giving rise to the negative differential transconductance gm (inset of Fig. 2B) (38). The gm = 0 appears at the same location as the Gaussian peak (Vgs = −19 V).

The transfer curves can be divided into four regions depending on the carrier accumulation profiles when changing the gate electric field (Fig. 2B). Band alignment diagrams of the four regions are schematically demonstrated in fig. S5 (38). In region I, holes accumulate in the P-channel MoTe2, while the fully depleted N-channel MoS2 completely turns off the entire heterojunction, resulting in the low junction current. In region II, as Vgs increases, the Fermi level gradually shifts up into the bandgap, leading to a moderate decrease of the hole concentration in the MoTe2 channel accompanied by a significant increase in the electron concentration in the MoS2 channel. This results in the higher junction current. The region III refers to a stage in which the depletion of holes in the MoTe2 channel is faster than the accumulation of electrons in the MoS2 channel, leading to a decrease of the junction current. In region IV, electrons accumulate in the N-channel MoS2, while the fully depleted P-channel MoTe2 completely turns off the entire heterojunction, resulting in the low junction current. The device exhibits Vgs-tunable band alignment, which is essential for color attention based on reconfigurable spectral responsivity.

Previously reported image sensors for in-sensor computing typically have a fixed spectral responsivity. The reconfigurable spectral responsivity is desirable for color attention. We first characterized the photoresponse of the individual MoS2 and MoTe2, both showing strong photoresponse within the visible spectrum range (fig. S6). The MoTe2 exhibits strong photoresponse within near-infrared range, expanding the junction response spectrum. To determine the reconfigurable spectral responsivity, we first measure the R-Vgs curves of MoTe2/MoS2 vdW heterojunction under 480- and 700-nm light illumination (Fig. 2C). The spectral responsivity, R, was calculated using the following equationR=IphP(4)where the photocurrent is defined as Iph = Ilight − Idark, with Ilight and Idark representing Ids with and without light illumination, respectively, and P represents the incident light intensity. When Vgs is less than −15 V, the responsivity under 480-nm light illumination is smaller than that under 700-nm light illumination. When Vgs increases to be greater than −15 V, the responsivity under 480-nm light illumination becomes larger than that under 700-nm light illumination. The Vgs-dependent responsivity between 480 and 700 nm exhibits the potential of the device to realize reconfigurable spectral responsivity for color attention. To further verify the reconfigurable spectral responsivity, we extracted the responsivity matrix at different incident light wavelengths and Vgs, exhibiting a spectral responsivity with giant gate voltage tunability in the visible and infrared (IR) spectrum range (Fig. 2D and fig. S7). When Vds is 1.2 V, it can be observed that as Vgs increases, the maximum of the spectral responsivity gradually shifts from 480 to 700 nm (Fig. 2D). When Vds is −1.2 V, the spectral responsivity can reach maximum in IR spectrum range at Vgs = 10 V (fig. S7). These results demonstrate that our device has the capability to reconfigure the spectral responsivity for color attention.

In traffic signs, red feature conventionally signifies prohibition and danger, whereas blue feature denotes guide and reminder. IR imaging circumvents the perceptual blind spots of visible light, enabling the sensor to operate reliably across all weather conditions. Collectively, these color features provide essential cues for real-world recognition systems to operate dependably. Thus, a high spectral rejection ratio to realize red attention, blue attention, and IR attention is highly desirable. Figure 2E depicts the reconfigurable spectral responsivity by tuning Vgs/Vds for red attention, blue attention, and IR attention. When Vgs = −50 V and Vds = 1.2 V, the device displays the maximum responsivity under 480-nm light illumination with high spectral rejection ratio with R480/R700 and R480/R1064 reaching 31 and ∼2 × 104, respectively (Fig. 2E, blue line). This enables the photodetector to realize blue attention, suppressing the input of other color information. Similarly, when Vgs and Vds are set to 0 and 1.2 V, respectively, the device demonstrates peak responsivity under 700-nm light illumination, achieving a spectral rejection ratio of 1.7 and 8 for R700/R480 and R700/R1064, respectively (Fig. 2E, red line). This allows the photodetector to realize red attention, attenuating signal input from nonred wavelengths. By applying Vgs to 10 V and reversing the Vds to −1.2 V, the device responds most strongly to 1064-nm light illumination with spectral rejection ratio (R1064/R480 and R1064/R700) reaching 3.5 and 2.7, respectively (Fig. 2E, pale teal line), enabling the photodetector to realize IR attention and suppress the input of visible information. In addition, fig. S8 shows that the color attention by setting a Vgs is immune to spectral cross-talk or macroscopic peak shift (e.g., from 480 nm to another color). The Vgs-reconfigurable spectral responsivity can be understood via analysis of the band properties of the MoTe2/MoS2 heterojunction (Fig. 2, F and G). When Vgs < 0 V, MoTe2 becomes p-type dominant, and the Fermi level continuously shifts downward away from the valence band maximum. Electrons in the valence band occupy lower-energy states, requiring substantially higher excitation energy to generate photogenerated carriers, consistent with Burstein-Moss effect (39). Shorter wavelengths of light can excite more photogenerated carriers and hence blueshifts the spectral responsivity (Fig. 2F). When Vgs > 0 V, the interlayer recombination of photogenerated carriers will limit the photoresponsivity. The interlayer recombination is limited by the supply of electrons from MoS2. Therefore, longer wavelengths of light cause more interlayer recombination and hence redshifts the spectral responsivity (Fig. 2G). Besides, we also characterized the noise-equivalent power of the device under different incident light wavelengths at Vgs = −50, 0, and 10 V, exhibiting low noise levels and high sensitivity of the device (fig. S9). Beyond the red and blue channels in the visible range, the heterojunction photodetector exhibits the potential for multicolor attention, successfully resolving distinct spectral channels including blue, red, and yellow (fig. S8). This versatile visible spectral tunability enables the device to adapt to broader and more spectrally complex scenarios.

On the basis of the reconfigurable spectral responsivity, we demonstrate that photodetector can mimic the attention adjustment to different color information in human vision system, thus realizing color-attention imaging without relying on additional optical filters. Schematic of the imaging system with our photodetector is shown in fig. S10. We demonstrate proof-of-concept color-attention imaging based on a single-pixel scanning method. When aimed to capture red feature, we set Vgs as 0 V and Vds as 1.2 V, thereby the responsivity of red light has been enhanced, while the responsivity of other colors has been suppressed. Thus, in the imaging of colored “ZJU” (Fig. 2H, top plane), the red “Z” has been precisely captured by the photodetector, while the signals of the blue “J” and the near-infrared “U” have been attenuated. To evaluate the quality of the red-attention imaging, we calculate the NSS and the specificity of the imaging results. The resulting NSS value of 1.3447 demonstrates that our photodetector has high response within the red region. The resulting specificity value of 1 shows that our photodetector has low response beyond the red region. The red-attention imaging matches well with the red features, demonstrating that our photodetector can mimic the red focus of human attention and reduce the interference of information from other wavelengths. Conversely, to capture blue feature, setting Vgs to −50 V and Vds to 1.2 V enhances the responsivity of blue light and suppresses the responsivity of other colors. Under this condition, photodetector selectively detects the blue components, whereas signals from the red Z and the near-infrared U are effectively minimized (Fig. 2H, middle plane). This enables blue-attention imaging, greatly reducing interference from nonblue color information. Similarly, we calculate the NSS and specificity of the blue-attention imaging results. Validated by the significant deviation from 0 in NSS, reaching 1.4104, our photodetector has high response within the blue region. The specificity up to 1 indicates that our photodetector has low response beyond blue region. Figure 2H (bottom plane) displays the color-attention imaging at near-infrared range (Vgs = 10 V and Vds = −1.2 V). An NSS of 1.2532 validates the device’s high sensitivity in the IR domain. This performance is complemented by excellent spectral selectivity—evidenced by a specificity of 1—indicating effective suppression of signals outside the target IR range. With direct evidence seen in the precise alignment between the color-attention imaging and the color features in ZJU, we demonstrate that our photodetector can mimic the top-down human attention.

Overall, our photodetector has reconfigurable spectral responsivity without additional optical filters. The visible color-attention imaging enables more efficient and precise extraction of the color feature, and the IR attention solves the inherent sensing gap of visible light. This capability is particularly critical for analyzing surface properties in semantically complex scenarios—such as those encountered in autonomous driving—where the robust and efficient recognition of color features is a prerequisite for reliable decision-making.

Tunable orientation response for orientation-attention imaging

Besides Vgs-dependent color-attention imaging, the in-sensor orientation feature extraction is essential for orientation-attention imaging. We propose the reconfigurable sensor array and perform the in-sensor convolutional computation. The Vds-dependent light intensity responsivity acts as the weight matrix. To determine the Vds-dependent light intensity responsivity, we first characterized the Iph-Vds curves under 480- and 700-nm light illumination, with corresponding incident light intensity of 190 and 158 mW/cm2, respectively. Figure 3A demonstrates that the Iph of photodetector can be linearly adjusted under a constant light intensity. This exhibits the potential of the device to realize tunable light intensity responsivity, which is the basis of the in-sensor weight modulation. To realize image information collection, the photodetector must work under different light intensities. As shown in Fig. 3 (B and C), we replot the power-dependent photocurrent under 480- and 700-nm light illumination at a certain Vgs (480 nm at Vgs = −50 V and 700 nm at Vgs = 0 V) and fit these curves with the following equationIph=WPα(5)where P represents the light intensity and W and α are fitted parameters. Table S1 exhibits the value of the fitted W and α at different Vds. All the curves have great goodness-of-fit coefficient of determination (R2; 0.96 under 480 nm and 0.99 under 700 nm).

The fitted curves under the same wavelength light illumination have the same α, while curves under the same wavelength light illumination at different Vds have different W. Therefore, the α is only related to the light wavelength, and the W is only related to the Vds. We define the W as the light intensity responsivity. As Vds increases, the light-intensity–independent W rises, leading to an increase in Iph. The linear Vds-dependent W ensures that we can precisely tune the light intensity responsivity of the device by adjusting the Vds (Fig. 3D), thus realizing in-sensor weight tuning. The same α ensures high-quality collection of image information with fewer parameters. Figure 3 (E and F) depicts the spectral responsivity at different Vds, demonstrating that adjusting Vds does not change Vgs-dependent spectral responsivity. Besides, we also fit the curves under 1064-nm light illumination with different light intensity and characterize the spectral responsivity at different Vds (fig. S11 and table S2), showing the same linear tunability. Detailed discussion on curve fitting can be found in text S2.

On the basis of the Vds-dependent light intensity responsivity, tunable orientation response can be realized by setting Vds in a sensor array to mimic receptive field. Figure 4A shows the dual-attention workflow of our vision sensor. We can configure the Vds of the sensor array for the orientation-attention imaging and Vgs for color-attention imaging in parallel. We demonstrate proof-of-concept feature-attention imaging based on a single-pixel scanning method with a 3 × 3 sensor array (Fig. 4A). The definition of orientation by the coordinate system of the sensor plane is depicted in fig. S12. When aiming to capture the information of red and 45° (Fig. 4B), we set Vgs to 0 V and configure Vds of the receptive field as 45°, thereby the corresponding information will be captured, while the information of other orientations and colors will be suppressed. Vds configuration and the orientation mechanism can be found in fig. S13 and text S3. To evaluate the quality of the feature-attention imaging, we calculate the NSS and the specificity of the imaging results. The resulting NSS value of 3.3 demonstrates that our sensor has high response within the red and 45° region. The resulting specificity value of 1 demonstrates that our photodetector has low response beyond the red and 45° region (table S3). The strong match between the red- and 45°-attention imaging and the feature of the diamond’s upper left edge demonstrate that our sensor can mimic the color and orientation focus of human attention. When aimed at capturing the blue and 135° information (the lower left edge of the diamond), we set the Vgs to −50 V and configure the Vds of the receptive field as 135° (Fig. 4C). In this way, sensor selectively detects the blue and 135° components, whereas signals from other colors and orientations are effectively attenuated. Similarly, validated by the significant deviation from zero of NSS, reaching 3.49, our sensor has high response within the blue and 135° region. The specificity up to 1 indicates that our photodetector has low response beyond blue and 135° region (table S3). With direct evidence seen in the precise alignment between the blue- and 135°-attention imaging and the feature of the diamond’s lower left edge, we can realize color-attention and orientation-attention imaging, reducing the interference of information from other colors and orientations. The size of the receptive field can be increased to improve the orientation resolution, but a large receptive field can blur out high-frequency orientation information. A comprehensive discussion about the array size selection is provided in text S4 (figs. S14 and S15 and table S4).

The modulation of light responsivity required for orientation attention is inherently coupled to the selected color channel (table S1). Figure S16 illustrates the specific Vds configurations required to execute 45° feature attention under the blue and red channels, respectively. Securing the exact same spatial orientation necessitates completely different Vds mapping strategies depending on the selected color channel. This unequivocally proves that the orientation parameters are intrinsically determined by, and physically coupled to, the chosen color channel. Although the inherent Vds and Vgs parameters coupling, the device can also be configured to independently execute exclusive color attention or exclusive orientation attention. The detailed operational workflows for implementing these single-feature–attention modes are explicitly illustrated in fig. S17.

In summary, our photodetector has Vds-dependent light intensity responsivity. We can realize orientation-attention imaging by setting Vds in a sensor array to mimic receptive field and dynamically reconfiguring the weight matrix of the receptive field. Orientation-attention imaging enables the efficient and precise capture of orientation features, a capability that is particularly critical for global contour detection and shape analysis in geometrically complex scenarios. In addition, configuring the Vds of the array also has the potential to achieve other functions, such as contrast enhancement, denoising, etc. The integration of color attention and orientation attention thus greatly enhances the functional versatility of device and reduces the postprocessing steps and the complexity of the system. Our strategy paves the way for compact and intelligent vision systems capable of real-time in-sensor feature extraction, facilitating downstream tasks such as object detection, classification, and tracking.

Efficient perception of color and orientation distinctive feature

Figure 5 (A and B) illustrates a scenario that machine vision systems in autonomous vehicles need to recognize traffic signs “no stopping” on the road. We have used the feature-attention vision sensor for the imaging of traffic signs, compared with conventional RGB camera and grayscale camera. No stopping sign has distinct red and 45° features compared to other traffic signs, which are also the main basis for computer recognition. Therefore, accurate and efficient capture of red and 45° features is important for the recognition of the no stopping sign.

Figure 5C illustrates the schematic of the imaging system incorporating the feature-attention vision sensor and the optical microscope of the classification system. The imaging setup comprises a single-pixel system, a transimpedance amplifier (TIA), a data acquisition (DAQ) system, and a computer (PC). The classification system consists of a field-programmable gate array (FPGA) deploying a convolutional neural network (CNN). In operation, the feature-attention vision sensor has been set into red-attention and 45°-attention mode, in which Vgs is 0 V and the Vds of the receptive field is configured as 45°. The feature-attention vision sensor can selectively capture the red and 45° features and suppress the interference of redundant information. Images acquired by the feature-attention vision sensor from the imaging system are transmitted to the classification system for recognition. Figure 5D (top plane) illustrates the data transmission latency from the PC to the FPGA, which is 89.8 ms. As a control experiment, images acquired from RGB camera and grayscale camera have also been transmitted to the FPGA, and the data transmission latencies are 267.6 and 89.8 ms, respectively. This is because the feature-attention vision sensor reduces data volume to just 33.3% of that required by a standard RGB camera (Fig. 5D, bottom plane), thereby facilitating low latency.

A bespoke traffic sign dataset was imaged using the feature-attention vision sensor, a grayscale camera, and an RGB camera. The image data acquired from each sensor were subsequently input into the same CNN architecture to evaluate training efficiency and recognition performance (fig. S18 to S21). Figure 5E demonstrates the recognition accuracy of the imaging and classification system. The feature-attention vision sensor achieves a recognition accuracy of 99.52%, which is commensurate with the RGB camera (100%) and outperforms the grayscale camera (87.43%) by a margin of 12.09%. Crucially, this high performance is realized with a data footprint identical to that of the grayscale device, representing only 33.3% of the volume generated by the RGB system. These results demonstrate that the proposed sensor suppresses data generation by 66.7% without compromising recognition accuracy. Figure S20 quantifies the recall and precision metrics for the feature-attention vision sensor, RGB camera, and grayscale camera. The proposed sensor achieves a recall of 99% and a precision of 98.6%—levels commensurate with the RGB camera—thereby demonstrating precise and reliable target recognition. Crucially, the device greatly outperforms the grayscale camera, exhibiting improvements of 25.5% in recall and 31.7% in precision.

To understand the mechanism behind this efficient but accurate classification of feature-attention sensor, we analyzed the intermediate feature maps at the Pool2 layer (Fig. 5F). Our feature-attention sensor achieves hardware-level feature disentanglement via a dual-attention strategy: Color attention amplifies semantic information in the red channel, while orientation attention isolates geometric features along the 45° orientation, which effectively decouples target features from background interference. In contrast, grayscale camera suffers from severe “feature collapse” by projecting multidimensional optical information onto the intensity information. This compression homogenizes semantically distinct traffic signs into indistinguishable annular contours within deep network layers. Consequently, our results demonstrate that introducing color (semantic) and orientation (geometric) attention directly at the sensor edge overcomes the semantic and geometric information loss of intensity-based classification.

Overall, our feature-attention vision sensor implements in-sensor feature extraction without relying on optical components or complex algorithm resources. Figure 5G presents a radar chart comparing the performance indices of the different sensor modalities. The feature-attention vision sensor outperforms the conventional grayscale camera across all quality metrics—including accuracy, recall, precision, specificity, and NSS—while maintaining a comparable data volume. Crucially, when compared with the standard RGB camera, our device achieves commensurate accuracy, recall, and precision but exhibits superior specificity and NSS. This performance is realized with a significantly lower data volume than the RGB system, collectively highlighting the distinct advantages of the feature-attention architecture in balancing robust recognition with data efficiency. This validates the efficacy of the feature-attention vision sensor’s sense-only-the-useful strategy within autonomous driving scenarios. In addition, this strategy will also provide advantages in industrial inspection applications, such as the automated optical inspection (AOI) of high-resolution displays (fig. S22). Ultimately, the architecture offers a promising pathway toward intelligent vision systems capable of efficient scene interpretation and robust decision-making in resource-constrained settings.

DISCUSSION

In conclusion, we have developed a feature-attention vision sensor that successfully implements a bioinspired sense-only-the-useful strategy to overcome the data redundancy bottlenecks of conventional imaging. By synergizing tunable color and orientation attention directly at the hardware layer, this hardware-level modulation enables efficient recognition (99.52% accuracy) in autonomous driving scenarios while suppressing data volume by 66.7%. By eliminating the reliance on external optical components and complex postprocessing, this work provides a scalable and efficient hardware platform for next-generation intelligent vision systems operating in resource-constrained environments.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Device fabrication

The devices were fabricated on commercially heavily doped p-type Si wafer with a 285-nm SiO2 layer. Few-layer MoTe2 and MoS2 were obtained through mechanical exfoliation. First, few-layer MoTe2 was exfoliated onto a polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) stamp and transferred onto the substrate under an optical microscope-assisted aligned transfer system. Then, few-layer MoS2 were exfoliated onto a PDMS stamp and transferred onto the MoTe2 under an optical microscope-assisted aligned transfer system. The source/drain contacts were defined using laser direct writing technology, and then Au (40 nm) was deposited using thermal evaporation for the contacts. The fabrication of heterojunction array was described in text S5.

Device characterization

All the devices were electrically characterized by an integrated optoelectronic test platform. The device was placed on the optoelectronic coupling probe station (Meta Technology) to characterize its properties under ambient laboratory conditions [25°C, 1 atm (101.325 kPa)]. The electrical characteristics were measured by the source meter (Keithley 2636B). Meanwhile, the incident lights with different wavelengths were provided with a xenon lamp light source (Meta Technology).
