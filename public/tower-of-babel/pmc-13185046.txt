Determinants of the choice of delivery mode in pregnant women: a convergent parallel mixed-methods study protocol
Authors: Shirin Osouli-Tabrizi, Somayeh Abdolalipour, Fatemeh Ahadi Yulgunloo, Mahsa Maghalian, Mojgan Mirghafourvand
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe mode of delivery is one of the most critical decisions in maternal care, influenced by psychological, social, cultural and medical factors. Despite the known benefits of vaginal delivery, the caesarean section rate (40%–55%) in Iran remains significantly higher than the WHO’s recommended level (10%–15%). Fear of childbirth, anxiety, social norms and misinformation are among the key determinants shaping women’s delivery preferences. Understanding these factors is essential for developing effective interventions to promote informed decision-making and reduce unnecessary caesarean sections. Thus, the study aims to assess the determinants of the choice of delivery mode in pregnant women.Methods and analysisThis study will employ a convergent parallel mixed-methods design to comprehensively investigate the determinants of delivery mode choice among pregnant women. In the quantitative phase, a cross-sectional study will be conducted among 768 primigravid women attending health centres in Tabriz, Iran. Data will be collected using standardised instruments such as the Childbirth Fear Questionnaire, Depression, Anxiety and Stress Scale (DASS-21) and researcher-developed questionnaires addressing sociocultural, environmental and educational factors. Statistical analyses will include descriptive and inferential tests such as χ2 and multivariate logistic regression. In the qualitative phase, semistructured interviews will be conducted with purposively selected participants to explore their experiences, beliefs and perceptions related to childbirth and delivery mode choice. Data will be analysed using conventional content analysis. The findings from both phases will be integrated during the interpretation stage to provide a comprehensive understanding of the phenomenon.Ethics and disseminationThis study has received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of Tabriz University of Medical Sciences, Tabriz, Iran (IR.TBZMED.REC.). Study findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed journals and scientific conferences. The study started in August 2025 and will continue until 2027. A similar study design will be implemented in a national, multicentre setting during this period.

Introduction

Pregnancy and childbirth are among the most significant and transformative experiences in a woman’s life, and the decision regarding the mode of delivery is one of the most critical choices women face during this period.13 This decision not only impacts the physical health of the mother and newborn but can also have profound long-term psychological and social effects, in addition to significantly influencing the mother’s quality of life.4 5

Natural childbirth has long been recognised as an optimal method aligned with women’s physiological design. Extensive scientific research has confirmed that vaginal delivery offers numerous maternal benefits, including faster recovery and reduced postpartum complications. However, in certain medically indicated cases, caesarean section becomes an essential life-saving intervention when either the mother or newborn is at risk.68 A woman’s right to choose her mode of delivery is a key component of respectful maternity care in modern obstetrics, as it promotes the health of both mother and newborn.9

Women’s preferences regarding delivery method are influenced by various psychological, cultural, social, economic and medical factors, while environmental elements, values, knowledge, perspectives and individual personality also play significant roles.911 Despite reductions in maternal mortality in developed countries, anxiety and fear of childbirth particularly among first-time mothers remain prevalent.12 Specifically, one in five pregnant or postpartum women experiences one or more types of anxiety disorders or anxiety-related conditions during this period.13 A rate substantially higher than the prevalence of depression, which affects approximately 6%–12% of women during the perinatal period.14 15 Anxiety disorders encompass all major anxiety disorders (such as generalised anxiety disorder, panic disorder, agoraphobia, social anxiety and specific phobias), in addition to obsessive-compulsive disorder and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). Although the latter two are now classified separately, many researchers continue to categorise them as anxiety and related disorders.16

A key domain of anxiety, both in primiparous and multiparous women, is fear of childbirth. Childbirth-related fears such as fear of pain, medical interventions or potential harm to the infant are common and can be intense. Although positive emotions towards pregnancy and childbirth generally outweigh negative ones, in some cases, negative emotions particularly fear of childbirth become predominant.17 Fear of childbirth is also associated with numerous adverse outcomes, including pregnancy avoidance, miscarriage, heightened pain perception during labour, prolonged labour duration,17 increased likelihood of emergency or elective caesarean delivery,18 postpartum depression, PTSD, elevated psychological stress19 and impaired mother–infant bonding.20 In some instances, the severity and persistence of this fear significantly impair psychosocial functioning, establishing it as a clinically valid psychological indication for caesarean section.17

The role of healthcare professionals and midwives is also highly influential in this decision-making process. Midwifery counselling and the information provided to pregnant women, particularly recommendations from healthcare specialists as trusted sources, can shape women’s perspectives regarding the safety and benefits of different delivery modes.21

Furthermore, women’s prior childbirth experiences and those of family and friends can significantly influence their preferences. Women with positive previous vaginal delivery experiences are more likely to choose this method again, whereas negative experiences may steer them towards caesarean section.22 23 Psychological and emotional factors constitute another dimension of this decision-making process. Fear of childbirth complications, concerns about neonatal health and potential impacts on maternal physical condition can all shape women’s decisions.24 Additionally, cultural beliefs, values and traditions can substantially influence attitudes towards delivery methods and subsequent decision-making.25 Finally, economic factors may play a decisive role in delivery mode selection.26 The results of a study conducted in northern Iran indicate that women’s choice of mode of delivery is strongly influenced by cultural, social, traditional and religious beliefs, rather than being based solely on medical considerations. In this study, caesarean section was perceived by some women as a safer option that protects physical health and carries social prestige, while normal vaginal delivery was viewed as a lower cost and healthier method and also as a symbol of women’s strength and capability. The influence of family members, social networks, others’ childbirth experiences and social imitation played a significant role in shaping women’s attitudes towards delivery options. In addition, traditional and religious beliefs framed vaginal delivery as a sacred process, a manifestation of divine power and a factor contributing to women’s health. Overall, these findings suggest that promoting positive attitudes towards vaginal delivery requires attention to cultural and religious contexts as well as correction of existing misconceptions.10

Overall, multiple inter-related factors within each society can influence women’s delivery choices and contribute to rising caesarean rates.27 Based on findings from cross-sectional studies, largely conducted in specific regional contexts particularly Iran and Ethiopia, advanced maternal age, urbanisation, higher education levels, physician recommendation for caesarean delivery, antenatal bleeding and inadequate information are key determinants of women’s preference for elective caesarean section.2830 Similarly, a study conducted in Jiangsu Province, China, found that over half of women delivered by caesarean, with higher rates in urban areas compared with rural areas. Women living in central Jiangsu, those with higher socioeconomic status and those who received more prenatal care visits were more likely to have caesarean deliveries. While some of these factors have also been reported in studies from other settings, the existing evidence remains uneven, and there is limited comparative research examining whether these drivers operate similarly across different countries and healthcare systems. This highlights an important gap in the literature and underscores the need for further cross-cultural and multinational studies to better understand the global versus context-specific determinants of women’s delivery mode preferences.3133 A systematic review conducted in East Africa showed that most women prefer vaginal delivery (75%), while only 25% favoured caesarean section. The study also found that factors such as antenatal care follow-up, satisfaction with previous childbirth, place of residence, occupation, planned pregnancy, history of spontaneous abortion, current pregnancy-related problems, discussion with a partner and type of hospital were significantly associated with women’s preference for delivery mode. These findings indicate that the choice of delivery method in East Africa is influenced by multiple social, economic and care-related factors, highlighting the need for a comprehensive approach to better understand these determinants and promote safe vaginal delivery while reducing unnecessary caesarean sections.34

According to WHO reports, the ideal global rate for caesarean delivery should range between 10% and 15% of all births. This benchmark, established through extensive research, represents the level at which its medical benefits including saving maternal and neonatal lives are maximised. The WHO emphasises that rates exceeding this threshold typically do not lead to improved maternal or neonatal outcomes and may instead introduce unnecessary complications. While the global caesarean rate is rising, some countries report figures substantially higher than the recommended level.35 The worldwide average for caesarean sections stands at approximately 21%.36 However, Iran’s caesarean rate significantly exceeds both the global average and WHO recommendations. Reported caesarean rates in Iran range from 40% to 55% of all births, with notably higher percentages in major urban centres and private hospitals.37 38

However, opportunities exist to mitigate these adverse effects. Previous research has demonstrated that psychotherapy and educational interventions such as counselling by maternity care providers or hospital-based childbirth education can reduce fear of childbirth in pregnant women.39

Several studies conducted in Iran have investigated factors influencing women’s preferences for delivery mode using various methods. An analytical cross-sectional study in Tehran (2016) involving 400 pregnant women found that occupation, education, parity, prior delivery method and participation in childbirth education classes were significantly associated with preference for vaginal delivery. A qualitative study among primiparous women with previous vaginal birth experience identified themes such as ‘internal control’, ‘external control’ and ‘support’ as key determinants of positive or negative childbirth experiences, highlighting the importance of woman-centred care. Furthermore, a mixed-methods systematic review of studies conducted in Iran (1990–2019) included 65 quantitative studies and 26 qualitative studies, revealing that despite low individual preference for caesarean section among nulliparous women, national CS rates remain high (47.9%), reflecting the influence of health professional and system-level factors.4042 These studies demonstrate that while several quantitative and qualitative studies have explored delivery preferences in Iran, there is a limited number of mixed-methods studies. Moreover, most existing research—particularly quantitative studies—has focused on isolated determinants (eg, individual psychological, social or clinical factors) rather than examining how multiple domains interact, and primary mixed-method studies that integrate women’s lived experiences with measurable factors remain scarce. Understanding how psychological, social and systemic factors interact to influence women’s choices remains incomplete, underscoring the rationale for the present mixed-methods study.

In summary, women’s decision-making regarding delivery mode results from a multidimensional interplay of psychological, cultural, social and experiential factors. Anxiety and fear of childbirth, alongside cultural beliefs, social network recommendations and the quality of interactions with healthcare providers, play fundamental roles in shaping this choice. Although both quantitative and qualitative studies have been conducted on this topic, there is no comprehensive study that examines all the factors influencing the choice of delivery mode. The findings from such studies could pave the way for developing effective educational and counselling programmes, enhancing communication between pregnant women and healthcare teams and ultimately reducing unnecessary caesarean deliveries while improving maternal and neonatal health outcomes.

Methods and analysis

Study design

This study employs a convergent parallel mixed-methods design within a pragmatist paradigm. Mixed-methods research encompasses various typologies, with decisions regarding the specific design addressing sequence, priority and theoretical perspective being made in response to the research questions.43

To integrate the quantitative (QUAN) and qualitative (QUAL) data, a triangulation approach was employed. This strategy involves combining findings from both QUAN and QUAL sources to enhance the comprehensiveness and validity of the study. The results from QUAN and QUAL analyses were examined together to identify areas of agreement and convergence, providing a more robust understanding of the phenomenon under investigation. By interpreting QUAN and QUAL data in a complementary and integrated manner, the study ensures that insights from both methodological perspectives contribute to a coherent overall interpretation.44

In this mixed-methods design, qualitative and quantitative data are collected and analysed concurrently yet independently. The two approaches typically receive equal priority and weight. Data analysis is conducted separately for each component, with integration occurring during the final interpretation phase. The discussion subsequently focuses on the extent and nature of convergence between the quantitative and qualitative findings45 (figure 1).

First phase: quantitative research

This phase of the study will employ a cross-sectional survey design to investigate the determinants of delivery mode choice among pregnant women. The study will be conducted following approval from the Ethics Committee of Tabriz University of Medical Sciences.

Sampling method

A cluster sampling method will be utilised. After obtaining ethical approval and the necessary permissions from the provincial and district health authorities, the study will be conducted in Tabriz city, East Azerbaijan province, Iran. Iran has an integrated primary healthcare system delivered through a network of public health centres under the supervision of the Ministry of Health and Medical Education. In Tabriz city, there are 145 provincial and district health centres serving defined catchment populations and providing comprehensive primary healthcare services, including health education and maternal and reproductive health services. Antenatal care is provided as part of this routine and integrated service package. One-quarter of the health centres will be randomly selected using the website www.random.org. The researcher will then visit the selected centres to extract a list of primigravid women in their third trimester, along with their contact numbers, from the SIB system (Integrated health information system). A proportional random sample of pregnant women will be selected from each centre.

Recruitment and data collection

The researcher will contact selected women by phone, explain the study’s objectives and procedures and invite them to participate. Those expressing interest will be screened for inclusion and exclusion criteria during the same call. Potentially eligible participants will be asked to attend their local health centre at a scheduled time. During this visit, the study aims and methods will be explained in detail. Willing participants will provide written informed consent before completing the study questionnaires, which will be administered in person through face-to-face interviews.

Data management

A comprehensive data management plan will be implemented to ensure reproducibility and participant privacy. All data will be collected and stored in SPSS (.sav) format on secure institutional servers, with regular backups. Personal identifiers will be removed or replaced with unique codes, and any potentially identifying information will be masked. Metadata and a detailed codebook will document variable definitions, coding and data transformations.

Instruments

Instruments include demographic and obstetric information (maternal age, age at marriage, age at first pregnancy, education, occupation, spouse’s education and occupation, number of pregnancies, history of abortion, previous delivery mode, current gestational age), Fear of Childbirth Scale, Depression, Anxiety and Stress Scale (DASS-21), Environmental, Cultural and Social Factors Questionnaire, Questionnaire on the Role of Midwifery and Healthcare Provider Education, Questionnaire on Women’s Knowledge of the Advantages and Disadvantages of Cesarean and Vaginal Delivery. The demographic and obstetric, environmental/cultural/social, provider education and knowledge questionnaires are researcher developed. To ensure validity, the instruments will undergo content and face validation by 10 faculty members of the Nursing-Midwifery School before the study. Revisions will be made based on their feedback.

Follow-up

Participants will be followed until after delivery. The actual delivery mode will be ascertained via a follow-up phone call and recorded in a specific checklist.

Sample size

The required sample size for this study was calculated using the standard formula for estimating a population proportion. With a 95% confidence level (Z=1.96), an anticipated caesarean section prevalence of 50% (p=0.5) to maximise sample size and a margin of error of 0.05, the initial sample size was determined to be 384 participants. Because cluster sampling will be used, the sample size was adjusted for the design effect by doubling it, resulting in a final sample size of 768 participants.

Scales and data collection

Questionnaire 1: demographic and obstetric information

This researcher-developed questionnaire consists of 10–15 items and collects demographic and obstetric data, including maternal age, age at marriage, age at first pregnancy, educational attainment, occupation, spouse’s education and occupation, number of pregnancies, history of abortion, previous delivery mode and current gestational age.

Questionnaire 2: psychological factors—fear of childbirth and perceived stress

Childbirth Fear Questionnaire

The Childbirth Fear Questionnaire (CFQ), developed by Fairbrother et al,47 was used to assess fear of childbirth. This 40-item instrument evaluates eight distinct domains: social shame, pain, analgesia, fear of birth mode, physical health of mother and baby, bodily changes, sexual function and medical interventions. The CFQ employs a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 to 4, where: 0 indicates ‘no fear’, 4 indicates ‘severe fear’.

Validity assessment

Content validity

Content validity will be quantitatively and qualitatively assessed by a panel of 10 specialists. The qualitative assessment will evaluate phrasing, wording, item relevance and placement. Quantitatively, the content validity ratio (CVR) and content validity index (CVI) will be calculated based on expert ratings.49

Face validity

Face validity will be evaluated using qualitative and quantitative methods. Qualitatively, cognitive interviews with a sample of 10 target women will assess comprehension, wording clarity and item relevance. Quantitatively, the Item Impact Score will be calculated using a 5-point importance Likert scale. An item will be considered valid if its Impact Score, derived from the formula Impact Score=Frequency (%)×Importance, exceeds 1.5.50 51

Construct validity

Construct validity will be examined through exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA).

An EFA will be conducted on the first dataset using principal component analysis with varimax rotation to identify the factor structure. Criteria such as factor loadings ≥0.4, Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO)≥0.7, and a significant Bartlett’s test (p<0.05) will be applied. Due to cultural and linguistic differences, modifications to the factor structure or subscale names may be made based on statistical and theoretical evidence, while attempting to preserve the original four-factor structure.48 52

A CFA will then be performed on a completely independent second dataset to evaluate the model fit and confirm the factor structure identified in the EFA. This two-sample approach ensures robust construct validation and avoids overfitting, following standard psychometric practices.

Reliability assessment

Reliability will be determined through both test–retest reliability, analysed via the intraclass correlation coefficient and internal consistency, measured using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient.

Depression, Anxiety and Stress Scale

The DASS-21, developed by Lovibond in 1995,53 is a self-report instrument designed to measure the related negative emotional states of depression, anxiety and stress. The scale comprises 21 items, divided equally into three subscales, with each subscale containing seven items. The total score for each subscale is derived from the sum of its respective items. Responses are recorded on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (‘Did not apply to me at all’) to 3 (‘Applied to me very much, or most of the time’). As the DASS-21 is the short form of the original 42-item scale, the final score for each subscale must be multiplied by two. The resulting scores are then interpreted using the corresponding severity classification table provided in the manual. Reliability coefficient of 0.77 has been reported for the DASS-21.

Questionnaire 3: environmental, cultural and social factors

This researcher-developed questionnaire consists of 12 items assessing societal attitudes towards vaginal and caesarean delivery, spousal and social network recommendations, cultural norms, environmental and social conditions and resource availability. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale (strongly disagree (0), disagree (1), neutral (2), agree (4), strongly agree (5).

Questionnaire 4: role of midwifery and healthcare provider education

This 7-item researcher-developed questionnaire evaluates the perceived usefulness of counselling and education provided by midwives and healthcare staff regarding delivery mode choice.

Questionnaire 5: women’s knowledge of advantages and disadvantages of caesarean and vaginal delivery

This 11-item researcher-developed questionnaire measures women’s understanding of the risks and benefits of caesarean and vaginal delivery, satisfaction with information received and perceived need for further education.

Validation of researcher-developed instruments

All researcher-developed questionnaires will undergo content and face validation by 10 faculty members of the Nursing-Midwifery School. Revisions will be made based on their feedback to ensure clarity, relevance and comprehensiveness.

Content validity of the questionnaire

To ensure the relevance, clarity and comprehensiveness of the questionnaire items, content validity will be evaluated using both the CVI and the CVR. A panel of experts in obstetrics, midwifery and maternal health will review all items. The CVR for each item will be calculated based on the experts’ assessment of whether the item is ‘essential’, ‘useful but not essential’ or ‘not necessary’, according to Lawshe’s method. Items with CVR values below the acceptable threshold will be revised or removed. The CVI will be calculated to assess the relevance, clarity and simplicity of each item on a 4-point scale, and the overall CVI will be determined by averaging the item-level scores. This process will ensure that the questionnaire accurately reflects the constructs under study and will be appropriate for the target population.

Data analysis

Collected quantitative data will be analysed using SPSS Statistics (V.26). Descriptive statistics (mean, SD, frequency and percentage) will summarise demographic and obstetric characteristics as well as psychological, environmental, cultural and social factors influencing delivery mode choice. In bivariate analysis, associations between categorical independent variables and the mode of delivery will be assessed using the χ2 test, while independent t-tests will be conducted for continuous variables. For multivariate analysis, all variables that show a significant association in bivariate analysis (p<0.2) or are considered theoretically important based on previous literature will be included in the multivariate logistic regression model. This approach allows us to analyse the simultaneous effect of multiple independent variables, including maternal age, education, occupation, parity, history of abortion, fear of childbirth, stress, depression, anxiety, social and cultural factors, knowledge about delivery modes and perceived role of healthcare providers on the outcome (preference or actual delivery mode). Variables that do not meet the p<0.2 threshold and are not theoretically essential will not be entered into the model. ORs and 95% CIs will be reported for each predictor. Model fit will be assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test, and multicollinearity will be evaluated using variance inflation factors.

Second phase: a qualitative study

Sample size and sampling method

In the qualitative phase, participants will be selected purposively from among women who completed the quantitative survey, with the aim of achieving maximum variation in key characteristics such as age, parity, education level and previous childbirth experience. Initial anxiety will be assessed using the anxiety subscale of the DASS-21 and the CFQ, and these scores will be used to guide sampling for the interviews. Specifically, women will be selected across the spectrum of anxiety and childbirth fear (low, moderate and high levels) to ensure that a wide range of experiences and perspectives related to delivery mode choice are captured.

Research setting

The interviews will be conducted at health centres within the participants’ own communities. This will provide a comfortable, safe and accessible environment, allowing participants to share their experiences freely.

Sample size in the qualitative phase

The qualitative sample size will be guided by data saturation, continuing interviews until no new information emerges. Based on previous studies, saturation is generally reached between 9 and 17 interviews, with 10–15 in-depth interviews planned for this study.54

Data collection methods

The present study will be conducted using a convergent mixed-methods design. In this design, quantitative and qualitative data are collected simultaneously, analysed independently and then integrated and compared during the interpretation phase. Qualitative data will be collected through in-depth, semistructured individual interviews using open-ended questions. In the qualitative component, participants will be purposefully selected to ensure maximum variation in characteristics such as anxiety levels, age, parity, education and previous childbirth experience. The interviews will be conducted by the researcher, who is a trained female with experience in qualitative maternal health research, and participants will be encouraged to freely express their views and experiences. The interview guide will be developed based on a review of the relevant literature.46 The interview will begin with a broad opening question: ‘Please tell me about your overall feelings during this pregnancy’. ‘Subsequent questions will adapt to participants’ responses and may include: Have you experienced any worries or anxiety during this period? Could you elaborate? Has this feeling changed at specific times? What are your views on vaginal delivery? What is your opinion about caesarean section? Had you thought about your delivery mode before becoming pregnant? Why or why not? What factors have influenced your decision on delivery mode? (eg, doctor’s advice, prior experience, family/peer influence, media, fear of pain, etc) Has anyone in your family or social circle influenced your choice? How? Do you have any fears about the process of childbirth? What are they related to? What are your expectations of healthcare providers (doctors, midwives, nurses) during delivery? What measures do you believe could help women make more informed decisions about their delivery mode? If you have previous childbirth experience, how has it influenced your current decision?’ Follow-up probing questions such as ‘What do you mean by that?’, ‘Can you explain further?’ or ‘Could you give an example?’ will be used to explore responses in greater depth. Throughout the interview, the researcher will document non-verbal cues (eg, tone of voice, facial expressions, posture) in a dedicated form, noting the time and location of each session.

In the qualitative phase, the study will be reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist to ensure transparent reporting of participant selection, the interview process and data analysis. In-depth semistructured interviews will be conducted with a selected group of participants, and thematic analysis will be performed to identify key themes and patterns.

Data analysis method

Data will be analysed using qualitative content analysis with a conventional approach, as outlined by Graneheim and Lundman.55 This method involves a systematic process where themes are derived through careful coding, categorisation and interpretation, rather than emerging spontaneously.56 The process involves the following five stages: (1) transcribing each interview verbatim immediately after completion. (2) Reading through the transcripts multiple times to gain a comprehensive understanding. (3) Dividing the text into meaning units, summarising these units and coding them. (4) Grouping codes into subcategories and categories based on similarities and differences. (5) Extracting themes that represent latent content and underlying concepts within the data. The analysis will be supported by MAXQDA V.24.11 software for organising interview transcripts and codes.

Methodological rigour in qualitative research

To ensure the scientific rigour of the qualitative component, this study will adhere to four established trustworthiness criteria: credibility, dependability, confirmability and transferability.57 58

Credibility

Credibility, reflecting confidence in data and analytical processes, will be enhanced through several strategies. These include prolonged engagement with the research phenomenon, continuous data examination and member checking where participants verify transcript accuracy and preliminary interpretations. Additionally, peer debriefing with supervisors and consultants will provide critical external perspectives, while establishing rapport and utilising semistructured interviews will encourage open, detailed responses from participants. All interviews will be documented and monitored by a second researcher to ensure methodological consistency.55 59

Dependability

Dependability, addressing data stability over time and conditions (analogous to reliability in quantitative research), will be achieved through comprehensive audit trails. This includes detailed documentation of all research decisions and analytical processes. Furthermore, external qualitative researchers will review coded data and categories to assess consistency in interpretation, with any substantially divergent categories undergoing collaborative re-examination until consensus is reached.

Confirmability

Confirmability, concerning objectivity and neutrality of findings, will be strengthened through multiple approaches: researcher bracketing of preconceptions, external auditing, publication of results in peer-reviewed journals and explicit documentation of data interpretation rules. A complete audit trail will be maintained, comprising: (1) raw data (field notes, interview transcripts), (2) analytical documentation (reduction diagrams, methodological memos) and (3) process records (consultation notes, peer-review feedback). This transparency enables external verification of interpretive processes and demonstrates absence of systematic bias.

Transferability

Transferability, pertaining to the potential applicability of findings to other contexts, will be supported through thick, contextualised descriptions of both research settings and participant characteristics. While qualitative researchers can suggest potential transferability, ultimate determination rests with readers assessing contextual similarity to their own environments. This study will provide sufficient methodological and contextual detail to enable informed judgements regarding potential transferability.55

Mixed phase

Following Creswell and Plano Clark’s conceptualisation of integration, this study achieved integration by linking the methods of data collection and analysis. Specifically, integration was accomplished through merging, whereby QUAN and QUAL data were brought together during the interpretation phase. Using a triangulation approach, findings from QUAN and QUAL analyses were examined concurrently to identify areas of convergence and agreement. This merged interpretation enabled a more comprehensive and robust understanding of the phenomenon under investigation by allowing complementary insights from both data strands to inform the overall conclusions.44 60

This study employs a concurrent triangulation design, collecting and analysing quantitative and qualitative data simultaneously yet independently, assigning equal priority to both datasets. The quantitative data will undergo statistical analysis using SPSS software, while the qualitative data will be examined through conventional content analysis. Integration will occur during the interpretation phase, where statistical findings from the quantitative phase (including anxiety levels, fear of childbirth and other delivery mode determinants) will be systematically compared and synthesised with themes emerging from qualitative interviews (encompassing women’s experiences, beliefs and perceptions of childbirth). This integrative process will identify convergence, complementarity and contradiction between datasets, enabling a richer interpretation of the psychological, social, cultural and structural factors influencing women’s delivery mode decisions. In this convergent parallel design, quantitative and qualitative findings will first be analysed separately and then integrated during the interpretation stage using a merging approach. Quantitative results—such as levels of childbirth fear, anxiety and their associated determinants—will be compared and contrasted with qualitative themes that capture women’s experiences, beliefs and social context. Integration will be achieved through narrative weaving and, where appropriate, joint display tables that juxtapose quantitative findings with qualitative themes. This approach will not involve transforming qualitative data into numerical variables or converting quantitative data into qualitative categories. The final integrated interpretation, presented in the discussion section, will provide a comprehensive understanding of the phenomenon, ultimately establishing a foundation for conceptual frameworks and practical recommendations regarding educational interventions, counselling approaches and health policy development in maternal and neonatal care.

Discussion

Despite the marked increase in caesarean section rates both in Iran and globally, most existing research has relied on quantitative approaches and superficial analyses, often overlooking the profound psychological, social and cultural dimensions underlying women’s decision-making regarding delivery mode. Although a number of qualitative studies have been conducted, comprehensive evidence synthesising determinants of delivery mode choice remains limited.6165 The present study will employ a convergent parallel mixed-methods design to address this research gap, aiming to identify the deeper determinants influencing delivery mode choice and to provide a more holistic understanding of this complex phenomenon.

Meta-ethnographic review conducted by Hamilton et al66 demonstrated that continuous, empathetic support from healthcare teams particularly through providing accurate information about the benefits and risks of different delivery methods and discussing reasons for prior caesarean sections can effectively promote informed decision-making and reduce unnecessary elective caesareans. Complementing this, a study by Stoll et al involving 3680 Canadian students with no prior childbirth experience showed that over 90% of participants preferred vaginal delivery, while only 9% favoured caesarean section. Trust in the body’s ability to undergo natural birth was a key factor in this preference, whereas fear of childbirth and lack of confidence in physiological capabilities were the main drivers for choosing caesarean delivery. Given that more than 90% preferred vaginal birth, no educational intervention was provided in this study. Nevertheless, these findings highlight the importance of evidence-based education, particularly for younger populations prior to pregnancy, to enhance trust in natural childbirth and address fears or misconceptions.67

Recent studies have further highlighted the significant role of sociocultural components in this decision-making process. Zewudu et al30 identified that dissatisfaction with previous care, insufficient knowledge about caesarean delivery, history of spontaneous abortion, urban residence and problems in the current pregnancy were all associated with a preference for caesarean section. Similarly, Ayalew et al 29 reported that residence location, antepartum haemorrhage, previous caesarean section and misinformation about childbirth were influential factors. Parallel findings have been observed in domestic studies; Rajabe et al28 demonstrated that a woman’s initial delivery preference significantly influences her subsequent choice, with factors such as maternal age, spouse’s education level, number of live births and pre-existing maternal attitudes towards childbirth being pivotal. Furthermore, Heydari et al68 noted that advanced maternal age, higher education and urban living were correlated with caesarean selection, with physician recommendation emerging as one of the most critical determinants for elective caesarean delivery.

In summary, women’s decisions regarding delivery mode are shaped by a complex interplay of psychological, social, cultural and relational factors. Fear of vaginal birth, misconceptions about its risks, and a lack of accurate information represent the most substantial barriers to choosing vaginal delivery. Conversely, knowledgeable and respectful communication between women and healthcare providers enhances trust in vaginal birth and mitigates associated anxiety.

This study protocol is designed to conduct an in-depth investigation into the lived experiences and attitudes of Iranian pregnant women concerning delivery mode choice. The anticipated findings are expected to yield a more comprehensive and profound understanding of the factors influencing women’s decision-making, thereby establishing a foundational platform for future research in the realms of both vaginal and caesarean delivery.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethical considerations

This study has received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of Tabriz University of Medical Sciences, Tabriz, Iran (IR.TBZMED.REC.1404.302). Written informed consent will be obtained from all participants involved in the quantitative and qualitative phases of the study. Participants will be informed that their personal information and identities will be kept strictly confidential and that they may withdraw from the study at any stage without any consequences.

Dissemination plan

The findings from this study will be disseminated through publications in peer-reviewed journals and presentations at national and international conferences focusing on reproductive and women’s health. Key results and practical recommendations will be communicated to healthcare professionals and policymakers through targeted workshops and policy briefs coordinated by Tabriz University of Medical Sciences. To promote public engagement, plain-language summaries of the research outcomes will also be shared via local health centres and social media platforms, aiming to raise awareness among women of reproductive age.

These dissemination activities are intended to ensure that the evidence generated informs clinical practice, guides reproductive health policy and supports women in making informed and confident decisions regarding their preferred mode of delivery.
