The continuity of Beurling density and Beurling dimension of spectra of a class of self-affine spectral measures
Zi-Yun Chen, Zhi-Yi Wu, Min-Min Zhang

Abstract
. It is well-known that the Beurling dimension of the spectra of certain singularly continuous spectral measures possesses an intermediate property. In this paper, we establish that for a class of self-affine spectral measures μ \mu , both the Beurling dimension and Beurling density of their spectra attain full flexibility simultaneously. Specifically, for any t ∈ ( 0 , dim H w ( supp ⁡ ( μ ) ) ] t\in(0,\dim_{H}^{w}(\supp(\mu))] and s ∈ [ 0 , ∞ ) s\in[0,\infty) , there exists a spectrum Λ := Λ t , s \Lambda:=\Lambda_{t,s} of μ \mu satisfying dim B ​ e ( Λ ) = t and D t + ​ ( Λ ) = s \dim_{Be}(\Lambda)=t\quad\text{and}\quad D_{t}^{+}(\Lambda)=s where dim H w \dim_{H}^{w} denotes the pseudo Hausdorff dimension, dim B ​ e \dim_{Be} denotes the Beurling dimension and D t + D_{t}^{+} denotes the t t -Beurling density. These results provide new insights into the structure of the spectra for a singularly continuous spectral measure.

Introduction

We say that
\mu

\mathbb{R}^{d}

\mu
is a probability measure on

forms an orthonormal basis for

The theory of spectral measure originated from the well-known fact that the exponential functions

[
18

first proved that the standard middle-fourth Cantor measure

is a spectral measure and since then, the spectrality of self-similar measures, self-affine measures and Moran measures has been extensively explored; see

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Spectrality and spectra

For a probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, its Fourier transform is defined as usual,

\hat{\mu}(\xi)=\int e^{-2\pi i\xi x}\mathrm{d}\mu(x).

It is easy to see that a discrete set
\Lambda
is an orthogonal set of
\mu
if and only if

(\Lambda-\Lambda)\setminus\{0\}\subseteq\mathcal{Z}(\hat{\mu}),

(2.1)

where
\mathcal{Z}(f):=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\colon f(x)=0\}
. Since the properties of orthogonal sets are invariant under translations, we always assume that
0\in\Lambda
, and hence
\Lambda\subseteq\Lambda-\Lambda
.

From (
1.1
) we have

\hat{\mu}_{R,B}(\xi)=\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}m_{B}((R^{t})^{-j}\xi),~\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{2},

where

m_{B}(x)=\frac{1}{\#B}\sum_{b\in B}e^{-2\pi i\langle b,x\rangle}.

It is easy to see that the zero set of
m_{B}
is

\mathcal{Z}(m_{B})=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}\xi_{1}\\
\xi_{2}\\
\end{pmatrix}\colon\xi_{1}\in\frac{2\mathbb{Z}+1}{2},\xi_{2}\in\mathbb{R}\right\}

and therefore

\begin{split}\mathcal{Z}(\hat{\mu}_{R,B})&=\{\xi\colon\hat{\mu}_{R,B}(\xi)=0\}=\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}(R^{t})^{k}\mathcal{Z}(m_{B})\\
&=\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}\left\{\begin{pmatrix}2^{k}&0\\
k2^{k-1}&2^{k}\\
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\xi_{1}\\
\xi_{2}\\
\end{pmatrix}\colon\xi_{1}\in\frac{2\mathbb{Z}+1}{2},\xi_{2}\in\mathbb{R}\right\}\\
&=\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}\left\{\begin{pmatrix}2^{k}\xi_{1}\\
k2^{k-1}\xi_{1}+2^{k}\xi_{2}\\
\end{pmatrix}\colon\xi_{1}\in\frac{2\mathbb{Z}+1}{2},\xi_{2}\in\mathbb{R}\right\}\\
&=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}\xi_{1}\\
\xi_{2}\\
\end{pmatrix}\colon\xi_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\},\xi_{2}\in\mathbb{R}\right\}.\end{split}

(2.2)

For any
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
, let
\beta\colon A\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be an arbitrary function with
\beta(0)=0
, and we define

\Lambda_{A,\beta}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}n\\
\beta(n)\\
\end{pmatrix}\colon n\in A,\beta(n)\in\mathbb{R}\right\}.

(2.3)

Based on the zero set of
\hat{\mu}_{R,B}
, we have the following simple conclusion.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\Lambda
be a subset of
\mathbb{R}
with
0\in\Lambda
. Then
\Lambda
is an orthogonal set of
\mu_{R,B}
if and only if there exist
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
and function
\beta\colon A\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
\Lambda=\Lambda_{A,\beta}
, where
\Lambda_{A,\beta}
is defined as in (
2.3
).

Proof.

It is trivial.
∎

To prove the completeness, following the idea of Parseval’s identity method, Jorgensen and Pedersen
[

]
gave a criterion by using the function

Q_{\Lambda}(\xi):=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}|\hat{\mu}(\xi+\lambda)|^{2}.

Theorem 2.2
.

[

Let

The following theorem involving the spectrality of

[

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
\beta\colon\mathbb{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be an arbitrary function and let
\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z},\beta}
be defined as in (
2.3
). Then
\Lambda_{\beta}:=\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z},\beta}
is a spectrum of
\mu_{R,B}
.

Proof.

By an easy calculation, for
\xi=(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})^{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
where
a^{t}
denotes the transpose of a vector
a\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
\hat{\mu}_{R,B}(\xi)=e^{-\pi i\xi_{1}}\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}\cos(\frac{\pi\xi_{1}}{2^{j}})=e^{-\pi i\xi_{1}}\frac{\sin(\pi\xi_{1})}{\pi\xi_{1}}
and then if
\xi_{1}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Z}
,

\begin{split}Q_{\Lambda_{\beta}}(\xi)&=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\beta}}|\hat{\mu}_{R,B}(\xi+\lambda)|^{2}\\
&=\sum_{\lambda_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|\frac{\sin(\pi(\lambda_{1}+\xi_{1}))}{\pi(\lambda_{1}+\xi_{1})}\right|^{2}\\
&=1,\end{split}

(2.4)

where the last equality holds by the known identity
\sum_{\lambda_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{1}{(\lambda_{1}+\xi_{1})^{2}}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{\sin^{2}(\pi\xi_{1})}
. By Theorem
2.2
, we have that
\Lambda_{\beta}
is a spectrum of
\mu_{R,B}
.
∎

2.2.
Beurling density and Beurling dimension

Let
\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a countable set. For
r>0,
the
upper
r
-Beurling density
of
\Lambda
is defined by

D_{r}^{+}(\Lambda)=\limsup\limits_{h\to\infty}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\#(\Lambda\cap B(x,h))}{h^{r}},

where
B(x,h)
is the open ball centered at
x
with radius
h
.
It is easy to prove that there is a critical value of
r
where
D_{r}^{+}(\Lambda)
jumps from
\infty
to
0
. This critical value is defined as the
Beurling dimension
of
\Lambda
, or more precisely,

\displaystyle\dim_{Be}(\Lambda)=\inf\{r:D_{r}^{+}(\Lambda)=0\}=\sup\{r:D_{r}^{+}(\Lambda)=\infty\}.

(2.6)

Equivalently,

\displaystyle\dim_{Be}(\Lambda)=\inf\{r:D_{r}^{+}(\Lambda)<+\infty\}=\sup\{r:D_{r}^{+}(\Lambda)>0\}.

(2.7)

Now, we prove Theorem
1.2
. To prove it we need the following two theorems.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
\mu_{R,B}
be the self-affine measure generated by the matrix and the digit set defined in (
1.2
). Then for any orthogonal set
\Lambda
of
\mu_{R,B}
, we have

\dim_{Be}(\Lambda)\leq\dim_{H}^{w}(\supp(\mu_{R,B}))=\frac{\ln\#B}{\ln\lambda_{\min}}=1,

where
\lambda_{\min}
is the minimum moduli of the eigenvalues of
R
.

Proof.

It is a direct corollary of Theorem 1.3 in
[
29
]
.
∎

Theorem 2.5
.

There exists a spectrum of
\mu_{R,B}
with Beurling dimenison zero.

Proof.

Let
L=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\\
\end{pmatrix}\right\}
.
Then it is easy to see that

H=\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\begin{bmatrix}e^{-2\pi i\langle R^{-1}b,l\rangle}\end{bmatrix}_{b\in B,l\in L}

is unitary. By Theorem 1.3 in
[
3
]
, we obtain the desired result.
∎

By Theorems
2.4
and
2.5
, it remains to prove that the upper bound can be attained; this part will be deferred to the proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Let
\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a countable set. For
r>0,
the
upper
r
-density
of
\Lambda
is defined by

B_{r}^{+}(\Lambda)=\limsup\limits_{h\to\infty}\frac{\#(\Lambda\cap B(0,h))}{h^{r}}.

Similar to the Beurling dimension, we can define the
Banach dimension
of
\Lambda
by

\displaystyle\dim_{Ba}(\Lambda)=\inf\{r:B_{r}^{+}(\Lambda)=0\}=\sup\{r:B_{r}^{+}(\Lambda)=\infty\}.

(2.8)

Equivalently,

\displaystyle\dim_{Ba}(\Lambda)=\inf\{r:B_{r}^{+}(\Lambda)<+\infty\}=\sup\{r:B_{r}^{+}(\Lambda)>0\}.

(2.9)

We end this section with the following lemma which will be needed in the following sections.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
\Lambda_{\beta}
be defined as in (
2.5
). Suppose
\beta(n)
has polynomial or exponential growth and is symmetric about the origin. Then
\dim_{Be}(\Lambda_{\beta})=\dim_{Ba}(\Lambda_{\beta})
.

Proof.

In this two cases, for any
h>0
, observe that the number of points
\Lambda_{\beta}
in
B(x,h)
for an arbitrary
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is less than or equal to the number of points
\Lambda_{\beta}
in
B(0,h)
, i.e.,

\#(\Lambda_{\beta}\cap B(x,h))\leq\#(\Lambda_{\beta}\cap B(0,h)).

This follows because according to the definition of
\beta
, the set
\Lambda_{\beta}
becomes increasingly sparse as points move away from the origin. Then for any
r>0
,
D_{r}^{+}(\Lambda_{\beta})\leq B_{r}^{+}(\Lambda_{\beta})
.

Conversely,
B_{r}^{+}(\Lambda_{\beta})\leq D_{r}^{+}(\Lambda_{\beta})
trivially. Thus, the critical values
r
where
D_{r}^{+}(\Lambda_{\beta})
and
B_{r}^{+}(\Lambda_{\beta})
transition between
0
and
\infty
must coincide. Hence,
\dim_{Be}(\Lambda_{\beta})=\dim_{Ba}(\Lambda_{\beta})
. This completes the proof.
∎

Proofs of Theorems
1.3
and

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

When
t=0
, let
\beta(n)=\text{sgn}(n)\cdot 2^{|n|},n\in\mathbb{Z}
, where
sgn
is the symbolic function. For
r>0
, take
h>0
, there exists
n\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
2^{n}<h\leq 2^{n+1}
. Then

\displaystyle\frac{\#(\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z},\beta}\cap B(0,h))}{h^{r}}\leq\frac{\#(\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z},\beta}\cap B(0,2^{n+1}))}{2^{nr}}\leq\frac{2n}{2^{nr}}\rightarrow 0~\text{as}~h\to\infty,

which implies that

\displaystyle B_{r}^{+}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z},\beta})=\limsup\limits_{h\to\infty}\frac{\#(\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z},\beta}\cap B(0,h))}{h^{r}}=0,

and thus
\dim_{Ba}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z},\beta})=0
. By Lemma
2.6
,
\dim_{Be}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z},\beta})=\dim_{Ba}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z},\beta})=0
. This is an explicit constructive proof of Theorem
2.5
.

When
t=1
, let
\beta(n)=n,n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Now we prove
\dim_{Be}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z},\beta})=1
. By Theorem
1.2
we only need to prove that
\dim_{Be}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z},\beta})\geq 1
. Take
h>0
, there exists
n\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
n<h\leq n+1
. Then for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

\displaystyle\frac{\#(\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z},\beta}\cap B(x,h))}{h}\geq\frac{\#(\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z},\beta}\cap B(x,n))}{n+1}.

Then

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{\#(\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z},\beta}\cap B(x,h))}{h}\geq\frac{\#(\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z},\beta}\cap B(0,n))}{n+1}=\frac{\lfloor\sqrt{2}n\rfloor}{n+1}\geq\frac{\sqrt{2}n-1}{n+1},

which implies that
D_{1}^{+}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z},\beta})\geq\sqrt{2}>0
. Here and in the sequel,
\lfloor x\rfloor
denotes the largest integer that is no greater than
x
. Therefore,
\dim_{Be}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z},\beta})\geq 1
. This implies that the upper bound in Theorem
1.2
can be attained.

We are left to prove the case that
t\in(0,1)
. Let
\beta(n)=\text{sgn}(n)|n|^{\frac{1}{t}}
. First, we prove
\dim_{Be}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z},\beta})\leq t
. Take
h>0
,

\displaystyle\frac{\#(\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z},\beta}\cap B(0,h))}{h^{t}}\leq\frac{2h^{t}}{h^{t}}\leq 2,

which implies that
\dim_{Ba}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z},\beta})\leq t
. On the other hand,

\displaystyle\frac{\#(\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z},\beta}\cap B(0,h))}{h^{t}}\geq\frac{\sqrt{2}h^{t}}{h^{t}}=\sqrt{2},

which implies that
\dim_{Ba}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z},\beta})\geq t
. Then
\dim_{Ba}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z},\beta})=t
. By Lemma
2.6
again,
\dim_{Be}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z},\beta})=\dim_{Ba}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z},\beta})=t
.

\square

Proof of Theorem

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.
We divide the proof into two cases.

Case I.
s=0
. For
0<t\leq 1
, we construct the spectrum
\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z},\beta}
by defining the function as follows:

\beta(n)=\mathrm{sgn}(n)|n|^{\frac{1}{t}}\cdot\log|n|,\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}.

We need to verify that
\dim_{Be}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z},\beta})=t
and
D_{t}^{+}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z},\beta})=0
.

For a point
\begin{pmatrix}n\\
\beta(n)\end{pmatrix}\in\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z},\beta}
, its distance to the origin is:

\sqrt{n^{2}+\left(|n|^{\frac{1}{t}}\cdot\log|n|\right)^{2}}.

Hence,

\#(\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z},\beta}\cap B(0,h))\leq 2\left(\frac{h}{\log h}\right)^{t}.

Then

\frac{\#(\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z},\beta}\cap B(0,h))}{h^{t}}\leq\frac{2\left(\frac{h}{\log h}\right)^{t}}{h^{t}}=\frac{2}{(\log h)^{t}}.

As
h\to\infty
, it follows that:

B_{t}^{+}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z},\beta})=\limsup_{h\to\infty}\frac{2}{(\log h)^{t}}=0.

In this case, similar to Lemma
2.6
,
D_{t}^{+}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z},\beta})=B_{t}^{+}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z},\beta})=0
. This implies that
\dim_{Be}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z},\beta})\leq t
.

Next, we prove
\dim_{Be}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z},\beta})\geq t
. For any
r<t
, for sufficiently large
h
,

\#(\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z},\beta}\cap B(0,h))\geq 2\left(\frac{\frac{1}{2}h}{\log h}\right)^{t}.

and then

\frac{\#(\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z},\beta}\cap B(0,h))}{h^{r}}\geq 2\cdot(\frac{1}{2})^{t}\cdot h^{t-r}\cdot(\log h)^{-t}\to\infty\quad(h\to\infty).

Thus,
D_{r}^{+}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z},\beta})=\infty
for
r<t
.
Hence,
\dim_{Be}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z},\beta})=t
.

Case II.
0<s<\infty
. Let
t\in(0,1]
. Define the function
\beta:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
as:

\beta(n)=\text{sgn}(n)\cdot\left(\frac{2|n|}{s}\right)^{1/t}.

We will show that
\dim_{Be}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z},\beta})=t
and
D_{t}^{+}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z},\beta})=s
.

For
\begin{pmatrix}n\\
\beta(n)\end{pmatrix}\in\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z},\beta}
to lie in
B(0,h)
, it must satisfy:

n^{2}+\left(\frac{2|n|}{s}\right)^{2/t}\leq h^{2}.

Then
\left(\frac{2|n|}{s}\right)^{2/t}\leq h^{2}
, which implies that
|n|\leq\frac{s}{2}h^{t}
.

Thus, the number of points in
B(0,h)
is:

\#(\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z},\beta}\cap B(0,h))\leq 2\left\lfloor\frac{s}{2}h^{t}\right\rfloor\leq sh^{t}.

Then, we have

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{\#(\Lambda\cap B(x,h))}{h^{t}}\leq\frac{sh^{t}}{h^{t}}=s.

Hence,
D_{t}^{+}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z},\beta})\leq s
. On the other hand, for sufficiently large
h
,

\#(\Lambda\cap B(0,h))\geq 2\left(\frac{s}{2}h^{t}-\delta(h)\right)=sh^{t}-2\delta(h),

where
\delta(h)=o(h^{t})
. This implies that
D_{t}^{+}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z},\beta})\geq s
and thus
D_{t}^{+}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z},\beta})=s
.

Since
\#(\Lambda\cap B(x,h))\approx sh^{t}
, it follows that

\dim_{Be}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z},\beta})=t.

Here,
\approx
means that there exist positive constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
such that
c_{1}b\leq a\leq c_{2}b
.

\square

Remark 4.1
.

When
t
is
0
,
s
can only be infinity.

5.
Further questions

In this paper, we only consider
R=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\
0&2\\
\end{pmatrix}
for the digit set
B=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\\
\end{pmatrix}\right\}
. There are many interesting problems left in the other general cases.

For example,
R=\begin{pmatrix}a&c\\
0&b\\
\end{pmatrix}

with
a\in 2\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
,
b\in 2\mathbb{Z}+1\setminus\{\pm 1\}
and
c\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
,
B
is the same as in this paper. In this case, it is known that the corresponding self-affine measure
\mu_{R,B}
is spectral
[
22
]
. In general, the spectral structure is more complicated and is hard to show all of spectra explicitly like this model in this paper. A natural question is the following

Question
Do all the results established in this paper retain their validity for the above model?
