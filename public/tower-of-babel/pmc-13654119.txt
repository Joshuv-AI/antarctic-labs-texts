Single-view echocardiography-based deep learning for pulmonary hypertension detection: a multi-level supervised and explainable approach
Authors: Qiumeng Xi, Hebin Liu, Xinpeng Dai, Jiayi He, Qizhi Xu, Yidan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEarly identification of pulmonary hypertension (PH), particularly mild disease, remains challenging. We aimed to develop and validate a deep learning (DL) model for noninvasive PH screening using single-view transthoracic echocardiography (TTE), without reliance on Doppler measurements.MethodsIn this retrospective study, apical four-chamber (A4C) TTE cine loops were collected between January 2023 and June 2025. A total of 144 patients with PH confirmed by right heart catheterization (RHC), 17 RHC-confirmed non-PH patients, and 95 non-PH controls defined by TTE under strict inclusion criteria were included. An additional temporally independent validation cohort of 40 RHC-confirmed subjects was enrolled between December 2025 and May 2026. Multiple DL architectures were evaluated for detecting PH. Model performance was assessed at both sample and patient levels using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), sensitivity, and specificity.ResultsA total of 256 patients with 318 TTE examinations were included for model development and independent validation, and an additional 40 RHC-confirmed subjects with 40 TTE examinations were included for temporal validation. The multi-level supervised model achieved the best performance among the evaluated architectures, with a patient-level AUROC of 1.000 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.998–1.000], sensitivity of 0.954, and specificity of 1.000 in the independent validation cohort. In the temporal validation cohort, the model achieved an AUROC of 0.932 (95% CI: 0.843–0.992), with sensitivity of 0.875, specificity of 0.813, and accuracy of 0.850. In an analysis of 50 RHC-confirmed subjects, the DL model achieved an AUROC of 0.937 (95% CI: 0.865–0.987) for distinguishing mild PH from non-PH, with sensitivity of 0.857 and specificity of 0.864, compared with AUROCs of 0.607–0.825 for conventional echocardiographic parameters.ConclusionsWe developed an interpretable DL framework based on single-view A4C echocardiography for noninvasive PH assessment. The model showed promising performance, including preserved sensitivity for mild PH. Further multicenter prospective validation is required before clinical implementation.

Introduction

Pulmonary hypertension (PH) is a progressive pulmonary vascular disorder characterized by elevated mean pulmonary artery pressure (mPAP) >20 mmHg, ultimately leading to right heart failure and even death if left untreated (1). Due to the mild and nonspecific nature of early symptoms, PH is frequently diagnosed after a prolonged delay of more than two years (2). Such delays are strongly associated with increased morbidity, mortality, and irreversible right ventricular remodeling (3,4). Right heart catheterization (RHC) remains the gold standard for the definitive diagnosis of PH; however, its invasive nature and substantial resource requirements limit its feasibility as a widespread screening tool (5). Echocardiography is therefore recommended as the first-line noninvasive modality for PH evaluation, integrating tricuspid regurgitation (TR) velocity with multiple indirect signs of elevated pulmonary pressures(6). Nonetheless, TR signals are unobtainable or unreliable in up to one-third of patients (7). Furthermore, conventional echocardiographic interpretation is operator-dependent and susceptible to inaccuracies related to arrhythmias, pulmonic stenosis, or severe TR (8). These limitations highlight an unmet clinical need for a precise, effective, and truly noninvasive screening strategy for PH, particularly for mild disease characterized by subtle structural alterations.

Artificial intelligence offers a promising solution for detecting PH based on echocardiographic data. Several prior studies have explored machine learning (ML)-based approaches for noninvasive PH detection using structured echocardiographic measurements and clinical variables (7,9,10). However, these approaches rely heavily on manually derived parameters and do not directly exploit the rich spatiotemporal information embedded in raw echocardiographic images. More recent efforts have shifted toward deep learning (DL) models applied to raw echocardiographic data, including multi-view or multimodal architectures that capture ventricular morphology and motion patterns (11,12). Despite encouraging results, several barriers limit their clinical translation. Many models require multiple echocardiographic views, increasing acquisition complexity and reducing practicality in real-world screening settings. In addition, some studies lack RHC-confirmed ground truth, and most predominantly focus on advanced-stage PH, with limited evaluation of mild disease, where structural abnormalities are subtle and detection is particularly challenging.

Compared with other imaging modalities, echocardiography uniquely provides dynamic and functional information of the heart in a widely accessible and noninvasive manner. In this study, we systematically developed and evaluated multiple DL architectures for PH detection using single-view echocardiographic images. We aimed to identify an optimal model that achieves robust diagnostic performance, particularly for mild PH, while maintaining clinical interpretability, thereby facilitating practical and scalable PH screening in routine clinical practice. We present this article in accordance with the STARD reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2026-0786/rc).

Methods

Study design and population

A study overview is shown in Figure 1. Our study targeted patients who underwent suspicion-based PH screening between January 2023 and June 2025 in the Department of Pulmonary and Critical Care Medicine at Beijing Chaoyang Hospital. Eligible patients were retrospectively identified and classified as PH or control. PH diagnosis was based on resting RHC. Patients with an mPAP >20 mmHg were included in the PH group, whereas those with mPAP ≤20 mmHg were categorized as controls. From an initial pool of 332 patients, those lacking an apical four-chamber (A4C) view (n=76) or presenting with inadequate A4C image quality (n=12), those with RHC acquired under non-resting conditions (e.g., stress testing) (n=76), and those with a TTE-RHC interval exceeding 30 days (n=7) were excluded. Multiple TTE examinations acquired at different time points from the same individual were included, with each TTE paired with a corresponding RHC performed at the same time point; however, to prevent data leakage, all examinations from a given patient were allocated exclusively to either the training or validation cohort. In total, 144 PH patients (with 205 TTE examinations) were included in model development cohort. Considering that most patients undergoing RHC were diagnosed with PH, we constructed a control group. Specifically, the control cohort initially included 265 patients who underwent TTE between May 2025 and June 2025 but did not undergo RHC, exhibited no symptoms or signs suggestive of PH and showed no evidence of PH during at least 6 months of clinical follow-up. After exclusion of 18 patients due to inadequate image quality, 9 patients due to the absence of an A4C view, and 143 patients with abnormal TTE findings other than mild valvular regurgitation, a total of 95 patients with 95 TTE examinations were finally included in the control cohort.

Inclusion-exclusion flowchart for the study population. A4C, apical four-chamber; mPAP, mean pulmonary arterial pressure; PH, pulmonary hypertension; RHC, right heart catheterisation; TTE, transthoracic echocardiography.

PH severity was defined based on mPAP: mild PH was defined as 20< mPAP ≤35 mmHg, moderate PH as 35< mPAP ≤45 mmHg, and severe PH as mPAP >45 mmHg (5,13). The dataset was randomly divided into training and validation cohorts at a 7:3 ratio, using stratified random sampling to preserve the distribution of controls and PH severity categories across datasets.

In addition, a retrospective temporal validation cohort was assembled to further evaluate model performance in a more recent clinical setting. Consecutive patients who underwent paired TTE and RHC examinations between December 2025 and May 2026 were screened. After excluding 8 patients because of inadequate A4C image quality, 40 patients with 40 TTE examinations were included, comprising 24 RHC-confirmed PH patients and 16 RHC-confirmed non-PH subjects.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Chaoyang Hospital Affiliated to Capital Medical University (No. 2023-ke-264). Given the retrospective nature of the study and use of data acquired during routine clinical care, the requirement for individual informed consent was waived. All data were anonymized prior to analysis to ensure patient confidentiality.

Echocardiography and preprocessing

All echocardiographic recordings were performed by experienced operators according to the guideline recommendations (6) using a commercially available ultrasound system (EPIQ 7 C, Philips Healthcare, MA, USA) equipped with X5-1 and S5-1 phased-array transducers, and measurements were collected retrospectively from echocardiographic reports. For the retrospective temporal validation cohort, in addition to the Philips platform, echocardiographic cine loops were also acquired using the ACUSON Origin system (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) equipped with 5V-1 and 5Z-1 phased-array transducers.

To ensure data quality and consistency across model inputs, all echocardiographic videos underwent standardized preprocessing prior to model training. Raw DICOM cine loops were converted to a bitmap format and split into frame sequences, and grayscale information was extracted to preserve the principal anatomical features of the heart. Non-diagnostic elements (e.g., textual labels, measurement lines, and graphical annotations) were automatically removed to minimize interference from non-structural information during feature extraction. In the process of cardiac cycle extraction, temporal analysis was performed to automatically isolate a complete cardiac cycle from each echocardiographic video, which was used as an individual input sample for the model. Each sample consisted of a 10-frame A4C image sequence extracted from a complete cardiac cycle. When multiple complete cardiac cycles were available for the same patient, each cycle was treated as a separate sample. Furthermore, to reduce variability related to imaging conditions, all frames were rescaled to a uniform spatial resolution of 520×700 pixels, and pixel intensities were normalized using a mean of 0.5 and a standard deviation of 0.5. This step was executed automatically by the data preprocessing module during model loading.

Model development and evaluation

Multiple DL architectures were explored, including convolutional neural network models and a transformer-based model. Specifically, the evaluated architectures comprised ResNet, Vision Transformer (ViT) and a ResNet50-based multi-level supervised model. Briefly, the ResNet50-based multi-level supervised model employed a cascaded segment-then-classify architecture, where ventricular segmentation probability maps were explicitly used as spatial priors to guide downstream classification. This design enforces anatomical awareness in the classification process and enhances interpretability by linking diagnostic decisions to underlying structural abnormalities. Hyperparameters for model training are summarized in Table S1.

The ResNet50-based multi-level supervised model was designed as a cascaded segment-then-classify framework. The encoder was initialized with ImageNet-pretrained weights and modified to accept a 10-channel input, with each channel corresponding to one frame from a complete cardiac cycle. Frames were stacked along the channel dimension rather than processed sequentially, allowing the network to learn spatiotemporal features using 2-dimensional convolutions without recurrent or 3-dimensional layers. The encoder generated a stride-32 feature map, which was passed to a transposed-convolution decoder to produce pixel-wise segmentation masks for three anatomical classes: background, right ventricle (RV), and left ventricle (LV). The resulting softmax segmentation probability maps were then used as spatial priors for the classification head, which consisted of two convolutional layers followed by adaptive average pooling and fully connected layers. This design was intended to incorporate anatomical information into PH classification and improve model interpretability.

Online data augmentation was applied during training to improve generalization, including random horizontal flips, small rotations (±5°), and slight scaling (±10%). The total loss combined classification and segmentation losses. The classification task used standard cross-entropy loss, whereas the segmentation loss was weighted according to class frequency, with weights of 0.2, 1.0, and 0.9 for background, RV, and LV, respectively. No explicit class weighting was applied to the classification loss, because patient-level stratification during cross-validation preserved the original PH and control distribution.

During model development, five-fold cross-validation was performed at the patient level. In each fold, all samples from the same patient were assigned to the same fold to avoid data leakage. Four folds were used for training and the remaining fold was used for validation. Early stopping was based on validation area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC). Candidate models were compared according to cross-validation performance in the development cohort, and the final model was selected before evaluation in the independent validation and temporal validation cohorts.

For each echocardiographic examination, multiple complete cardiac cycles could generate several 10-frame samples. The model generated a PH probability for each sample, and sample-level probabilities from the same examination were averaged to obtain the final examination-level prediction. For patient-level evaluation, probabilities from all available samples belonging to the same patient were averaged to generate the final patient-level probability. For binary PH classification, the optimal probability threshold was determined using the Youden index in the model development cohort. This fixed threshold was then applied unchanged to the independent validation and temporal validation cohorts without re-optimization.

Model performance was assessed at both the sample and patient levels. The primary performance metric was AUROC. Additional metrics included accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, F1 score, and Brier score. The 95% confidence intervals for AUROC and other performance metrics were estimated using non-parametric bootstrapping with 1,000 resamples. Calibration was assessed using the Brier score and calibration curves.

The final model was subsequently evaluated in both independent validation cohort and the temporal validation cohort, neither of which was involved in model training, cross-validation, model selection, or threshold determination. Model performance was assessed at both the sample and patient levels using the same metrics described above. The probability threshold determined in the model development cohort was applied to both validation cohorts without re-optimization.

Sensitivity analyses

To address potential labeling bias and baseline imbalance related to the control group definition, two sensitivity analyses were performed in the independent validation cohort. First, the analysis was restricted to subjects with RHC-confirmed PH or non-PH. Second, an age- and sex-matched analysis was performed. PH patients were matched to non-PH subjects using exact matching on sex and nearest-neighbor matching on age without replacement. Model performance in both sensitivity analyses was evaluated using the fixed probability threshold determined in the model development cohort. Standardized mean differences (SMDs) were calculated to assess the balance of age and sex before and after matching.

Subgroup analysis

To further evaluate model performance across clinically relevant subgroups, predefined subgroup analyses were conducted in the pooled evaluation cohort, which included subjects from both the independent validation cohort and temporal validation cohort. Subjects were stratified by age (<65 and ≥65 years), sex, and body mass index (BMI <25 and ≥25 kg/m2). For these subgroups, AUROC, sensitivity, and specificity were calculated to assess the robustness of model performance.

In addition, severity-stratified analyses were performed among patients with RHC-confirmed PH. PH severity was categorized as mild, moderate, or severe according to the definitions described above. To further evaluate model performance in early-stage PH, an exploratory severity-stratified analysis was conducted in RHC-confirmed PH patients from the combined evaluation cohorts, including the independent validation cohort and temporal validation cohort. Within each severity group, predicted probability distributions were examined, and sensitivity was calculated using the fixed probability threshold determined in the model development cohort, without threshold re-optimization.

Comparison with conventional echocardiographic parameters for detecting mild PH

The ability of the DL model to detect mild PH was compared with that of conventional echocardiographic parameters. This analysis included RHC-confirmed subjects from the combined evaluation cohorts, consisting of patients with mild PH and non-PH subjects. The evaluated conventional echocardiographic parameters included basal RV diameter, TAPSE, TR velocity, pulmonary artery systolic pressure (PASP), and TAPSE/PASP. For threshold-based analysis, conventional echocardiographic criteria were defined as follows: basal RV diameter ≥41 mm, TAPSE ≤17 mm, TR velocity ≥2.8 m/s, PASP ≥35 mmHg, and TAPSE/PASP ≤0.32 (5,6). The DL model was evaluated using the fixed probability threshold determined in the model development cohort. AUROC, sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and precision were calculated for the DL model and each conventional echocardiographic parameter. For AUROC calculation, patients with non-measurable values for a given conventional parameter were excluded. For threshold-based analyses, non-measurable conventional echocardiographic parameters were treated as negative for the corresponding criterion. For patients with mild PH, the hemodynamic and echocardiographic profiles of correctly classified and misclassified cases by the DL model were summarized descriptively.

Explainability analysis

To assess the explainability and physiological plausibility of the model’ s decision-making process, Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) was applied to each frame of the A4C cine-loops (14). This approach enabled visualization of the activated regions contributing to the classification, thereby highlighting the key structures and dynamic features leveraged by the model. This approach allowed us to assess the physiological plausibility of the model’s predictions.

Statistical analysis

The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov- Smirnov test. Variables following a normal distribution were analyzed with independent-samples t-tests and reported as mean ± standard deviation. For non-normally distributed variables, the Mann-Whitney U test was applied, and the results were presented as median with interquartile range in the format M (Q1, Q3). Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages, and between-group comparisons were performed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test when appropriate. A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using Python (version 3.9.0).

Results

Study population

Overall, 256 patients with 318 TTE examinations were included. Among them, 144 patients contributed 205 paired TTE-RHC examinations that were confirmed as PH by RHC, and 17 patients contributed 18 paired examinations that were classified as non-PH controls by RHC. In addition, 95 TTE-confirmed normal controls with no echocardiographic evidence of PH were included. At the timepoint level, PH examinations were obtained at a median age of 58.0 (49.0–67.0) years, with 40.5% from male patients, whereas RHC- confirmed non-PH examinations were obtained at a median age of 57.0 (50.5–69.3) years, with 33.3% from males. TTE-confirmed normal control examinations were obtained at a median age of 38.0 (33.0–49.0) years, with 31.6% from males. Based on this superior performance, this model was selected as the backbone and subsequently evaluated on the independent validation set. The multi-level supervised model achieved an AUROC of 1.000 (95% CI: 0.998–1.000) in the independent validation set at the patient level, with sensitivity and specificity of 0.954 (95% CI: 0.898–1.000) and 1.000 (95% CI: 1.000–1.000), respectively (Figure 3A,3C). The model provided well-calibrated probability estimates at the patient level, with a Brier score of 0.024 (95% CI: 0.007–0.048). In addition, the distribution of predicted probabilities at the patient level demonstrated clear separation between PH and non-PH cases (Figure S3).

In the temporal validation cohort, the model achieved an AUROC of 0.932 (95% CI: 0.843–0.992), with sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy of 0.875, 0.813, and 0.850, respectively (Table 3 and Figure S4).

Sensitivity analyses

Two sensitivity analyses were performed in the independent validation cohort, including an RHC-confirmed subset analysis and an age- and sex-matched analysis. In the RHC-confirmed subset including 71 subjects, comprising 65 PH patients and 6 non-PH subjects, the model achieved an AUROC of 1.000 (95% CI: 1.000–1.000), with sensitivity and specificity of 0.954 (95% CI: 0.897–1.000) and 1.000 (95% CI: 1.000–1.000) respectively. After age- and sex-matching, 76 subjects were included, comprising 38 PH patients and 38 non-PH subjects. Matching reduced the SMD from 0.793 to 0.386 for age and from 0.085 to 0.000 for sex. In the matched cohort, the model achieved an AUROC of 1.000 (95% CI: 1.000–1.000), with sensitivity and specificity of 0.947 (95% CI: 0.864–1.000) and 1.000 (95% CI: 1.000–1.000), respectively. Detailed results are shown in Tables S6,S7.

Subgroup analysis

The multi-level supervised model showed consistent performance across clinically relevant subgroups in the pooled evaluation cohort, which included subjects from both the independent validation cohort and the temporal validation cohort (Figure 4). The model showed consistently high discriminative performance across these subgroups, with AUROCs of 0.993 (95% CI: 0.981–1.000) in female patients and 0.975 (95% CI: 0.921–1.000) in male patients, 0.994 (95% CI: 0.983–1.000) in patients aged <65 years and 0.947 (95% CI: 0.842–1.000) in those aged ≥65 years, and 0.990 (95% CI: 0.975–0.999) in patients with BMI <25 kg/m2 and 0.989 (95% CI: 0.962–1.000) in those with BMI ≥25 kg/m2. Sensitivity and specificity were generally stable across subgroups, although specificity was relatively lower in male patients and patients aged ≥65 years. Among RHC-confirmed PH patients, severity-stratified analysis showed a sensitivity of 0.857 (95% CI: 0.714–0.964) in mild PH, 0.947 (95% CI: 0.842–1.000) in moderate PH, and 0.976 (95% CI: 0.929–1.000) in severe PH. Detailed subgroup results are shown in Table S8. Predicted probability distributions across PH severity levels showed a progressive upward shift from mild to severe PH (Figure S5).

Comparison with conventional echocardiographic parameters for detecting mild PH

In the mild PH analysis, 50 RHC-confirmed subjects from the pooled evaluation cohort were included, comprising 28 patients with mild PH and 22 non-PH subjects. The DL model achieved an AUROC of 0.937 (95% CI: 0.865–0.987), which was higher than those of basal RV diameter, TAPSE, TR velocity, PASP, and TAPSE/PASP.

Using the fixed threshold of 0.434, the DL model correctly identified 24 of 28 mild PH patients, with sensitivity of 0.857 (95% CI: 0.714–0.964), specificity of 0.864 (95% CI: 0.706–1.000), accuracy of 0.860 (95% CI: 0.760–0.940), and precision of 0.889 (95% CI: 0.750–1.000). Compared with conventional echocardiographic criteria, the DL model showed higher overall discriminative performance. Detailed results are presented in Table S9. The hemodynamic and echocardiographic profiles of correctly classified and misclassified mild PH cases are summarized in Table S10. Because only four false-negative cases were observed, this comparison was descriptive and no formal hypothesis testing was performed.

Discussion

In this study, we developed and validated a DL algorithm based on single-view A4C TTE cine loops for noninvasive PH assessment. The proposed multi-level supervised model integrates ventricular segmentation and PH classification into a unified framework, enabling automated extraction of cardiac anatomical features and estimation of PH probability from standard A4C images. The model showed high discriminative performance in the independent validation cohort and maintained promising performance in the temporally independent validation cohort. Additional sensitivity and subgroup analyses further supported the stability of model performance across different evaluation settings. Notably, although sensitivity was lower in mild PH than in moderate or severe PH, the model correctly identified most mild PH cases and outperformed conventional echocardiographic parameters for distinguishing mild PH from RHC-confirmed non-PH subjects.

With the rapid development of DL techniques, increasing efforts have been made to explore automated identification of PH using a variety of diagnostic modalities, including phonocardiograms (15), 12-lead electrocardiograms (16,17), and chest radiographs (9,18). Although many of these approaches demonstrate reasonable discriminative performance for PH detection, several limitations remain. In particular, models based on electrocardiography and cardiac auscultation generally report AUROC below 0.90 (15-17). In contrast, chest radiography-based models have achieved higher AUROC values exceeding 0.90; however, such approaches inevitably expose patients to ionizing radiation, limiting their suitability for repeated assessments. Moreover, As a radiation-free and noninvasive modality, TTE remains central to the diagnosis and risk stratification of PH in current clinical practice (19-21). However, standardized echocardiographic assessment for PH typically requires the acquisition of multiple views (5), which is time-consuming and labor-intensive, and diagnostic accuracy remains highly operator-dependent. In this context, artificial intelligence approaches based on echocardiography may be particularly well suited for large-scale and repeated screening. Building on these considerations, we developed a DL model using routinely acquired single-view two-dimensional A4C echocardiographic images, aiming to reduce operator dependence while maintaining diagnostic performance and scalability for PH screening.

In the independent validation cohort, the model showed a clear separation of predicted probabilities between PH and non-PH cases, with most predictions distributed near either end of the probability spectrum. This pattern suggests that the model learned discriminative imaging features associated with PH rather than relying solely on marginal threshold-dependent decisions. The low Brier score and calibration curve further provided complementary information on the agreement between predicted probabilities and observed PH status. In the temporally independent validation cohort, model performance decreased compared with the independent validation cohort but remained clinically meaningful, which is expected given the stricter RHC-confirmed control definition, temporal separation from the model development cohort and additional vendor-related imaging heterogeneity. Together, these findings support the potential value of single-view A4C-based DL assessment as a noninvasive aid for PH detection.

Beyond overall diagnostic performance, the observed severity-dependent gradient in predicted probabilities suggests that the model captures pathophysiologically relevant information beyond binary classification. The wider dispersion of predicted probabilities in mild PH likely reflects the heterogeneity of early-stage disease, whereas the consistently high probabilities observed in moderate and severe PH indicate more stable and pronounced RV remodeling patterns. Clinically, PH often remains unrecognized in its early stages because patients are asymptomatic or present with nonspecific symptoms, resulting in delayed diagnosis until advanced disease, commonly at World Health Organization functional class III or IV (22,23). Earlier identification may help prompt timely evaluation and management before irreversible disease progression occurs. In the present study, the multi-level supervised model showed higher overall discriminative performance than conventional echocardiographic parameters for distinguishing mild PH from RHC-confirmed non-PH subjects. These findings suggest that single-view A4C-based DL assessment may provide complementary information for mild PH detection.

In parallel, model explainability is a critical consideration for the clinical adoption of artificial intelligence approaches. To address this challenge, the proposed segment-then-classify architecture was designed to explicitly separate anatomical localization from disease classification. By performing ventricular segmentation prior to classification, the model bases its predictions on spatial probability distributions of cardiac structures rather than raw image intensity alone, thereby aligning model decision-making with prior studies that emphasize assessment of RV structure and function in PH (24-27). Grad-CAM visualizations of our model demonstrated a predominant focus on the RV in patients with PH, which aligns with prior artificial intelligence studies across different imaging modalities. Both the Cardiac Magnetic Resonance-based model proposed by Cheng et al. (28) for mPAP prediction and the echocardiography-based Chamber Attention Network developed by Sun et al. (29) consistently highlighted the RV as a dominant contributor to PH-related model predictions. In addition, patient-level predictions were derived by aggregating probabilities across multiple cardiac cycles, which better reflects routine clinical decision-making and reduces the impact of beat-to-beat variability. This hierarchically supervised design enables robust performance while maintaining clinical interpretability.

Limitation

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single center with a relatively small sample size in a specialized PH referral setting, which may have introduced selection bias. Consequently, patients with mild PH were underrepresented, which likely contributed to the lower sensitivity observed for mild PH compared with more advanced disease. Second, owing to the limited availability of RHC-confirmed PH-negative cases, a hybrid labeling strategy combining RHC and TTE was adopted. While RHC-confirmed cases were used as the reference standard whenever available, individuals with negative TTE findings were included as controls under strict inclusion criteria to minimize misclassification. Although this approach is not conventional, it has been reported in prior studies (9,18,30) and may increase dataset diversity, potentially enhancing model generalizability. Nevertheless, despite rigorous quality control procedures and a minimum follow-up period of six months, the inclusion of patients with very early or subclinical PH cannot be completely excluded. To address this issue, we performed sensitivity analyses in the independent validation cohort, including an RHC-confirmed subset analysis and an age- and sex-matched analysis. These sensitivity analyses should be interpreted cautiously because of the limited number of subjects in some subgroups, particularly the small number of RHC-confirmed non-PH subjects. However, the temporal validation cohort provided complementary evidence, as all enrolled subjects underwent RHC, and both PH and non-PH diagnoses were determined according to RHC findings. In addition, age and sex distributions were relatively balanced between groups in this cohort. The cohort also included images acquired from two ultrasound vendors, and most examinations were performed using Siemens systems. These findings partly mitigate concerns regarding labeling bias and baseline imbalance, but larger multicenter prospective cohorts with more RHC-confirmed non-PH subjects and multi-vendor representation remain necessary. Third, despite demonstrating robust performance in PH classification, the proposed model is currently not designed to differentiate among PH subtypes. Future studies with larger, more diverse cohorts are warranted to enable the development and validation of models for PH subtype classification.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we developed a Doppler-free DL framework for PH assessment using single-view A4C echocardiographic images. The model showed promising performance and preserved sensitivity for mild PH, supporting its potential as a noninvasive screening aid. Further multicenter prospective validation is required before clinical implementation.
