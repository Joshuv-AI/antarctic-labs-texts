Seasonal dynamics and species diversity of Anopheles mosquitoes in malaria endemic districts of Southern Odisha India
Authors: Muhammed Mustafa Baig, Divya Teja Koppula, Dilip Kumar Panigrahi, B Vijayakumar, Dolly Choudhary, Premalatha Acharya, Manoj Patnaik, K Gunasekaran, Ashwani Kumar, Manju Rahi, A N Shriram
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMosquitoes of the Anopheles, Aedes, and Culex genera are responsible for transmitting major vector-borne diseases. Malaria remains a significant public health concern in Odisha, primarily due to the state’s conducive environment for Anopheles mosquito breeding. This study, conducted between March 2021 and February 2023 across 11 traditionally hyper-endemic districts in southern Odisha, aimed to assess seasonal variations in Anopheles diversity, composition, and abundance. A total of 10,807 Anopheles mosquito’s species were collected manually indoors (house dwellings and cattle sheds) and outdoors (burrows, vegetation, tree holes, and culverts). Morphological identification revealed 18 Anopheles species. An. subpictus was the predominant species during the summer of 2021, with (328; 42.99%), and during the rainy season, with (1151; 46.60%), although its prevalence declined in subsequent years. An. culicifacies, a primary malaria vector, exhibited a consistent presence with (780; 31.58%) in the rainy season of 2021 and (798; 38.35%) in the rainy season of 2022. An. varuna remained scarce during summer and rainy seasons but peaked sharply in winter, with the highest prevalence in winter 2021–2022 (730; 35.56%) and winter 2022–2023 (485; 25.18%). Diversity indices (Shannon’s, Simpson’s, Pielou’s) and Correspondence Analysis identified Ganjam as the district with the highest species diversity (1.26–2.2). Seasonal variation had a statistically significant impact on species diversity (p < 0.001), surpassing the influence of district level factors. These findings show that seasonality strongly influences Anopheles populations and highlight the need for localized, evidence-based vector control. Monitoring of mosquito diversity is vital for shaping malaria interventions suited to Odisha’s transmission ecology.Keywords: Malaria, Odisha, Shannon’s, Simpson’s, And pielou’s, Anopheles diversitySubject terms: Zoology, Ecology, Diseases, Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Mosquitoes significantly transmit diseases like malaria, dengue, chikungunya, Zika, Japanese encephalitis, West Nile Viruses, Yellow fever and Lymphatic Filariasis1. Over 150 mosquito species from the Anopheles, Aedes, and Culex genera can be carriers for these infections2. Notably, only female mosquitoes take blood meals for the development and maturation of their eggs. Their bites can cause itchiness, redness, and potentially serious illness if scratched3. These differences among species arise from variations in their biology, behavior, and physiology4.

Mosquitoes with longer lifespans can transmit diseases more efficiently because the pathogen has more time to multiply inside them5. Mosquitoes that prefer feeding on humans (anthropophilic) are more likely to spread diseases between people because they come into contact with infected individuals6.

India faces significant challenges with malaria, particularly in Odisha7. Among the state’s 30 districts, eleven southern districts Nawarangpur, Rayagada, Balangir, Nuapada, Ganjam, Gajapati, Kandhamal, Kalahandi, Koraput, Boudh and Malkangiri have been severely affected by high incidences of falciparum malaria over the years. These eleven districts alone reported 66.5% of the malaria cases and 52.0% of the malaria deaths of the State in 2017. Health officials reported 41,739;25,503; 23,770; and 41,971 malaria cases in 2020, 2021, 2022 and 2023, respectively8 Odisha’s wet, hilly forests with streams, together with its hot, humid climate and high rainfall, create ideal malaria transmission conditions by promoting mosquito survival and longevity. These environmental conditions particularly favor Anopheles mosquitoes, the region’s primary malaria vectors9. Inland forested areas support a greater diversity and abundance of mosquito species compared to coastal regions, due to seasonal variations in temperature, humidity, and rainfall that enhance mosquito reproduction and population growth10.

Studies in Odisha’s coastal regions have identified a higher diversity of anopheline vectors compared to other Culicine mosquitoes11. Tracking mosquito diversity is crucial for disease control, especially as climate change creates more mosquito habitats12,13. Changes in mosquito behavior can also impact disease spread. Understanding which mosquito species can be effective disease vectors to predict and prevent outbreaks is imperative, particularly for emerging or expanding diseases14,15.

Various factors determine mosquito habitats, including preferred breeding grounds and habitat types, which influence mosquito interactions with disease pathogens in infective human hosts and their potential to spread them further. Human activities, such as urbanization, deforestation, irrigation, and water storage practices, can alter mosquito habitats, ecology and biology16.

Anopheles mosquitoes, the primary malaria vectors, prefer to breed in various types water bodies- natural or man-made, sunny or shady, temporary or permanent17. Female Anopheles mosquitoes transmit malaria by carrying Plasmodium parasites such as falciparum, vivax, malariae and ovale18,19. Malaria transmission is influenced by factors including the number of mosquito species and populations, land cover, and terrain20. In Odisha, the primary malaria vectors are An. fluviatilis and An. culicifacies, with An. annularis playing a lesser and secondary role21,22.

Studying, monitoring and intervention in mosquito populations is crucial, as these vectors transmit numerous viruses and parasites responsible for severe diseases and significant mortality in humans and animals23. Insecticides are widely used as part of mosquito control interventions, including indoor residual spraying (IRS), long-lasting insecticidal nets (LLINs), and larviciding; however, their extensive application has led to the emergence of insecticide resistance, which poses a substantial challenge to controlling diseases such as malaria. Insecticide resistance in mosquitoes has emerged as a major challenge to vector control programs, reducing the efficacy of commonly used insecticides such as pyrethroids, organophosphates, carbamates, and DDT (Dichloro-diphenyl-trichloroethane)24,25.

This resistance can lead to the survival of mosquitoes despite interventions, increasing the risk of disease transmission26. Monitoring mosquito populations is essential for tracking insecticide resistance and selecting the most effective insecticides27. Additionally, understanding mosquito diversity is critical for public health risk assessment and the implementation of vector control measures28.

Our study aimed to investigate the seasonal effects on Anopheles mosquitoes across 11 districts in southern Odisha (Rayagada, Nabarangpur, Kalahandi, Nuapada, Balangir, Boudh, Kandhamal, Gajapati, Ganjam, Malkangiri, and Koraput). Additionally, we sought to generate data on mosquito species diversity, composition (types of Anopheles present), and abundance (number of mosquitoes) across these districts, considering their distinct environmental characteristics.

Methods

Study area

Our study was conducted in Odisha, located along the Bay of Bengal in the eastern part of India, between latitudes 17.31°N and 22.31°N and longitudes 81.31°E and 87.29°E (Fig.1).

The map was modified by the authors to add labels, scale bar, and north arrow for visualization purposes only.

We focused specifically on 11 southern districts of Odisha state (Rayagada, Nabarangpur, Kalahandi, Nuapada, Balangir, Boudh, Kandhamal, Gajapati, Ganjam, Malkangiri, and Koraput) which are known for their high malaria incidence.

Within each districts, sampling was conducted across three ecotypes, classified according to topography: (i) hilltop villages, situated on slopes and ridges where perennial streams are the primary water source; (ii) foothill villages, located within 0.5–1 km of the hill base; and (iii) plain villages, located at least 2 km away from the foothills, where wells, ponds, and paddy fields constitute the primary water sources and serve as essential breeding habitats for anopheline mosquitoes. Thus, all three ecotypes were represented in each study district, enabling mosquito diversity and seasonal dynamics comparison by both district and ecotype. This region experiences three distinct seasons: dry summer (March–June), wet rainy season (July–October), and dry winter (November–February). The annual maximum and minimum temperatures are 33.74 °C (92.73ºF) and 22.05 °C (71.69ºF), respectively, with yealy average of 109.64 rainy days (30.04% of the year)29. Climatic conditions influence the creation of breeding sites and mosquito growth, affecting the overall trend of Anopheles species in the region30. Malaria is a significant health issue in this area, with Plasmodium falciparum being the most prevalent parasite (90% of cases). Malaria incidence peaks from July to September and again in November and December. The primary malaria vectors in this region are An. culicifacies and An. fluviatilis.

Adult mosquito collection, and morphological identification

Adult Anopheles mosquitoes were collected across 63 villages in 11 districts between July 2021 and February 2023 (except in Gajapati and Rayagada, surveyed from July 2021 to February 2023 due to administrative reasons. We collected 10,807 Anopheles mosquitoes during the study period. The seasons I, II, III, IV, V and VI correspond to the periods summer (Mar-Jun 2021), Rainy (Jul–Oct 2021), Winter (Nov 2021-Feb 2022), Summer (Mar-Jun 2022), Rainy (Jul-Oct2022), Winter (Nov 2022-Feb 2023) respectively.

Diurnal indoor resting collections (IDRC)

Anopheles mosquitoes were collected manually using oral aspirators and torchlight between 06:00–07:30 h. In each village, six human dwellings (HDs) and three cattle sheds (CSs) were sampled in each village. Each technical personnel spent 10 min in each location, collecting resting mosquitoes from walls, roofs, and eaves in each location.

Diurnal outdoor resting collections (ODRC)

Twelve artificial pit shelters were constructed at fixed sites around each village, representing all directions around villages. Collections were carried out by spending five minutes in each shelter. Additional outdoor resting sites such as bushes, rock hollows, culverts and tree holes were also inspected31.

Nocturnal light trap collections

Modified CDC miniature light traps (6-V DC motor, 2.5-V bulb) were deployed from dusk to dawn in each of the six selected villages per district. Six traps were used per collection night: two in HDs, two in CSs, and two outdoors. Collections were performed fortnightly32.

The total number of mosquitoes collected from indoors (HD + CS) and outdoors using the two methods, aspirator and light trap, were considered for estimating the vector mosquitoes’ resting behaviour (endophilic or exophilic). The data on the abundance of vector mosquitoes were analyzed by season.

To gain a comprehensive understanding of mosquito activity, we also collected mosquitoes landing on people both indoors and outdoors throughout the night. Man-landing mosquito collections were conducted once every two months in a randomly chosen village from those visited across the districts. Each hour, the team carefully recorded which mosquitoes landed on human volunteers. Finally, each mosquito species was identified by examining its morphological characteristics under a stereo-microscope. Established scientific keys, including those by Christopher’s taxonomic keys for anophelines33 was used to identify each mosquito species.

Statistical analysis

We analyzed the diversity of mosquito species using PAST software, version 4.03. The abundance of data on each collected mosquito species was entered into the software for each district and season. These data were then used to calculate three complementary ecological indices: Shannon–Wiener Diversity Index (H′), Simpson Dominance Index (D), and Pielou’s Evenness Index (J′) were used to assess the richness and evenness, dominance, and evenness among species respectively. Multiple indices provide a more robust and comprehensive assessment of mosquito community structure, as each index captures different aspects of diversity that a single index alone cannot fully represent, we used a two-way ANOVA statistical test to determine whether mosquito diversity differed between seasons or districts. If the initial ANOVA revealed a significant difference (p-value less than 0.05), we conducted additional post hoc tests (Bonferroni) to identify specific groups with of disagreements. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant, indicating that the results are unlikely to be due to chance.

A combination of indices was employed to effectively evaluate mosquito diversity to provide complementary insights into species richness, abundance, and evenness. This study utilised three widely recognised indices: Shannon’s Diversity Index, Simpson’s Index, and Pielou’s Evenness Index. These indices were selected for their established relevance in ecological studies, particularly those focused on species distribution, species richness and biodiversity assessments. To facilitate comparisons of mosquito species diversity across locations, these diversity indices were computed as described below:

Shannon’s diversity index (H′)

Measures overall species diversity, accounting for both richness and relative abundance. Higher values indicate a more diverse community, whereas lower values indicate dominance by a few species34,35.

Simpson’s index (D)

Assesses dominance and the contribution of common versus rare species. Higher values indicate a more even species distribution, while lower values reflect strong dominance by one or a few species36. This is particularly relevant for understanding potential disease transmission risks from dominant malaria vectors such as An. culicifacies and An. fluviatilis.

Pielou index (J’)

Evaluates how evenly individuals are distributed across species, with values close to 1 indicating equal abundance and lower values reflecting uneven distribution37. Together, these indices allowed us to assess whether specific districts or seasons were dominated by a few vector species or supported more balanced mosquito assemblages.

Rarefaction

We used rarefaction to compare the number and variety of mosquito species between different environments and assess how well the sampling captured the actual diversity. Rarefaction accounts for the number of mosquitoes collected and estimates the potential species richness in a given area. Initially, collecting more mosquitoes usually leads to a rapid increase in the number of species found. As the collection continues, the curve flattens out as only rare species are discovered after most of the common species have been identified.

Correspondence analysis (CA)

Correspondence Analysis (CA) was used to determine if there were differences in the composition of Anopheles mosquito species across the 11 districts. CA also helps identify whether environmental factors significantly influence these differences in mosquito communities.

Rainfall data

Total monthly rainfall data were collected from the Odisha Weather Monitoring Systems (OWMS) for all study districts38. Mean rainfall for each season was calculated using the formula: Mean rainfall per season = Total Rainfall/Number of days per season (120 days)​

The lag mean rainfall per season was used to assess its relationship with mosquito species abundance and diversity indices, providing insights into the effects of rainfall on vector populations and community structure.

Cluster analysis

Cluster analysis was performed to assess the species abundance and species richness similarity across the 11 study districts. The analysis utilized number of anopheline mosquitoes, number of species, season and the district were used to done the cluster analysis. STATA 14.2 was used to done the analysis. Similarity measures were calculated using the complete linkage method, and the resulting clustering patterns were visualized through a dendrogram.

Ethical approval

The study was approved by Institutional Human Ethics Committee (IHEC) vide letter no. IHEC-1020/N/J dated 4th February,2020.

Most abundant species

The study revealed seasonal variations in the dominance of different Anopheles mosquito species (Fig.1; Table1 ). An. subpictus was the most common species, comprising (328;42.99%) of mosquitoes in summer and (1151;46.60%) in rainy 2021, though its numbers declined significantly in subsequent seasons. An. culicifacies, a primary malaria vector, exhibited a consistent presence with (780; 31.58%) in the rainy season of 2021 and (798; 38.35%) in the rainy season of 2022. An. varuna remained scarce during summer and rainy seasons but peaked sharply in winter, with the highest prevalence in winter 2021–2022 (730; 35.56%) and winter 2022–2023 (485; 25.18%). An. culicifacies (the primary vector) and An. subpictus (not incriminated as a vector in the study area), constituted a high percentage of the total mosquitoes collected during the first two seasons. Other species, such as An. fluviatilis (the primary vector) and An. nigerrimus (a secondary malaria vector), were predominantly found during the winter. Rare species such as An. ramsayi and An. pallidus were observed in low numbers.

Seasonal abundance

In Seasons I & II (summer and rainy-2021) An. culicifacies, An. subpictus and An. vagus collectively accounted for nearly 90% of the mosquito population. In Season III (winter 21–22), An. fluviatilis, An. jeyporiensis and An. varuna together made up 61% of the overall abundance. In seasons IV and V (summer and rainy 2022), An. culicifacies and An. vagus contributed more than 60%. In Season VI (winter 22–23), An. culicifacies, An. fluviatilis, An. jeyporiensis, An. nigerrimus and An. varuna collectively represented 76% of the total species abundance.

Overall abundance

An. culicifacies, An. vagus and An. subpictus contributed more than 70% of the mosquito population in both summer and rainy seasons. In the winter season, An. fluviatilis, An. varuna and An. jeyporiensis together contributed 57%. Overall, An. culicifacies (2654;24.6%), An. subpictus (1675;15.5%), An. vagus (1604;14.8%) and An. varuna (1452;13.4%) together accounted for nearly 70% of the total catch across all seasons.

Diversity indices

To analyze changes in mosquito diversity over the study period, we utilized line graphs with error bars representing 95% confidence intervals. We calculated three different diversity indices for each district, considering data across two years and three seasons (summer, rainy, and winter). This approach allowed us to examine how the variety of mosquito species fluctuated within each district depending on the season and year.

Shannon index

From Summer 2021 to Winter 2023, diversity consistently and gradually increased, peaking in the Winter of 2022–2023 across all districts, with mean Shannon Index values around 2. Districts such as Balangir, Ganjam, Kalahandi, Malkangiri, and Nuapada consistently exhibited higher diversity values (ranging from 1.09 to 2.2) across seasons, indicating high diversity in these districts. In contrast, the Boudh and Kandhamal districts showed lower diversity in the first two seasons with a gradual increase from the third season onward. Some districts, including Gajapati, Koraput, Nabarangpur and Rayagada demonstrated fluctuating diversity patterns, with values oscillating between high and low across seasons. Overall, the increasing trend in diversity suggests the potential suitability of environmental conditions supporting a greater variety of mosquito species in most districts over time.

Shannon indices were notably high in Malkangiri during seasons I (summer 2021), III (winter 2021-22) and VI (winter ); in Kalahandi during season II (rainy 2021); in Nabarangpur in season IV (summer 2022); in Koraput in season V (rainy 2022); and Ganjam during season VI (winter ).

To assess the diversity of mosquito species across different districts and seasons, we calculated the average Shannon index with a 95% confidence interval. This index accounts for the number of mosquito species present and their relative abundance within a particular location and time (Fig. 2).

Mean with 95% Confidence Interval for Shannon Index in different seasons from 2021 to 2023.

The mean Shannon diversity indices range varied from 1.16 to 1.94 over the study period. Significant seasonal variations were observed (ANOVA, P < 0.05), but no significant differences between districts were detected. In the first year (2021–2022), winter exhibited significantly higher indices than both summer (P < 0.001) and the rainy season (P = 0.001), with no significant difference between summer and rainy (P = 0.220). In the second year (2022–2023), winter again showed significantly higher indices than the rainy (P = 0.037), while no differences were found between summer and the rainy season (P = 0.999) or between summer and winter (P = 0.999). The second-year summer season had significantly higher indices than the first year (P < 0.001), but no differences were observed between the years for the rainy (P = 0.463) and winter (P = 0.999) seasons.

Simpson index

The Simpson Index, ranging from 0 (low diversity) to 1 (high diversity), provides insights into mosquito

diversity across districts and seasons.

Districts such as Ganjam, Kalahandi, Koraput, Malkangiri, and Nabarangpur demonstrated consistently moderate to high diversity throughout most seasons, with Simpson’s Index values ranging from 0.60 to 0.87. This indicates a relatively even distribution of mosquito species within these regions. Similarly, Balangir and Naupada exhibited moderate values of the Simpson Index, with values ranging between 0.56 and 0.84. The Boudh and Kandhamal districts had the lowest overall diversity in Seasons 1 and II (summer and rainy-2021), with values ranging from 0.42 to 0.75, suggesting dominance by a few mosquito species. In Gajapati and Rayagada even though collection was not done in Season I (summer 2021), subsequent seasons showed moderate diversity, with values ranging from 0.52 to 0.83.

Simpson index was high in Malkangiri during Season I, (summer 2021), III (winter ) and VI (winter ), in Kalahandi during Seasons II & IV (rainy 2021 and summer 2022); in Koraput during Season I (summer 2021), and in Kalahandi during season V (rainy 2022). This study utilized the Simpson index to assess both the variety and evenness of mosquito species across different districts and seasons. (Fig. 3)

Mean with 95% Confidence Interval for Simpson Index during different seasons between 2021 and 2023.

The range of the mean Simpson diversity indices varied from 0.62 to 0.82 over the study period, with significant differences observed between seasons but no differences between districts. In the first year, winter had significantly higher indices than summer (P < 0.001), while no significant differences were found between summer and the rainy season (P = 0.225) or between the rainy and winter seasons (P = 0.357). In the second year, winter had higher indices than the rainy season (P = 0.343) and summer (P = 0.999), though the differences were not statistically significant. Comparing the two years, the summer season had significantly higher indices in the second year compared to the first year (P < 0.001). Although the rainy (P = 0.999) and winter (P = 0.999) seasons also showed higher diversity indices in the second year, the differences between the years were not statistically significant.

Pielous index

Mosquito diversity varied across districts and seasons, with seasonal variations having a decisive influence than the location. Districts like Kalahandi and Kandhamal consistently maintained a balanced distribution of mosquito species throughout the year, reflected in their high Pielou’s Indices. Conversely, districts like Balangir exhibit seasons with more even distributions, such as in summer 2021, where the index peaked at 0.99. The Gajapati district experienced uneven distribution in certain seasons, with a notably low index value of 0.00 in some instances. Seasonal fluctuations in evenness are also observed, as seen in Boudh, where the index dropped during the 2021 rainy season but recovered by the summer of 2022 (Fig.4).

Mean with 95% Confidence Interval for Pielous Index during different seasons between 2021 and 2023.

Overall, these findings emphasize the importance of considering the number of mosquito species (diversity) and their evenness within a location for a better understanding of mosquito communities. This information is valuable for managing ecosystems and adapting to environmental changes.

Pielou’s index was high in the Balangir during Season I, Kalahandi during Seasons II and III, Boudh during Season IV, and Kandhamal during Seasons V and VI.

The range of mean Pielou’s diversity indices varied from 0.50 to 0.73 over the study period, with significant differences observed between both seasons and districts. In the first year, summer had significantly higher indices than the rainy season (P = 0.001), At the same time no significant differences were found between summer and winter (P = 0.421) or between the rainy and winter seasons (P = 0.649). In the second year, winter showed significantly higher indices than rainy (P = 0.030), but no differences were observed between summer and the rainy season (P = 0.326) or between summer and winter (P = 0.999). In comparison, between the two years, no significant differences were found across the summer (P = 0.999), rainy (P = 0.999), or winter seasons (P = 0.999).

Rarefaction curve

We employed rarefaction curves to compare the adequacy of our sampling and the number of mosquito species across seasons. This technique estimates the expected number of species by randomly resampling the collected mosquito pool multiple times. A steeper initial rise in the curve indicates a rapid increase in new species discovery as more samples are included. The curve’s later flattening suggests that we were more likely to collect the same species repeatedly, indicating a sufficient sampling effort. According to these curves, winter (2021–2022) and the rainy season (2022) exhibited the highest estimated species richness, followed by the rainy season (2021), winter (2022–2023), and summer (2022). Summer 2021 showed the least species richness. Overall, mosquito species richness was highest in winter and lowest in summer. All seasons reached asymptotic line confirming adequate sampling effort (Fig. 12).

Correspondence analysis

Correspondence Analysis (CA) was employed to explore the relationship between mosquito species and the seasons in which they were collected. This statistical method positions species and seasons based on their associations, creating a visual representation. The CA biplot displays two main axes explaining most variation (96.9% and 3.1%). The positioning of species on the biplot indicates patterns; An. subpictus is associated with summer and rainfall, while An. aconitus, An. barbirostris, An.fluviatilis and other species are more linked to winter seasons. Summer and Rainy seasons (2022–2023) appear to be associated with species, viz., An. annularis and An. culicifacies (Fig.13).

Discussion

The current study looked at how the diversity of Anopheles mosquitoes changed with the seasons in 11 districts in southern Odisha. These districts are Rayagada, Nabarangpur, Kalahandi, Nuapada, Balangir, Boudh, Kandhamal, Gajapati, Ganjam, Malkangiri, and Koraput. The study also produced detailed information on the species variety, composition, and abundance of Anopheles mosquitoes in these districts32,39, each of which has unique environmental characteristics. Understanding these factors is important to understand the dynamics of vectors, which can facilitate targeted vector control strategies and efforts to control malaria40. Over the study period, a total of 10,807 Anopheles mosquitoes were collected across all seasons, representing 18 species. An. subpictus was the most dominant species during both summer (328;42.99%) (March–June) and the rainy season (1151; 46.60%) (July–October) of 2021, followed by a significant decline thereafter. An. culicifacies, a primary malaria vector in the region, consistently constituted 30–38% of the population during summer and the rainy season. In contrast, An. varuna exhibited peak abundance during the winter of 2021 and 2022 (730;35.56%). Species diversity varied across districts, with Ganjam consistently showing the highest diversity (1.26–2.2). Statistical analysis indicated that seasonal fluctuations significantly influenced mosquito diversity (p < 0.001), suggesting that seasonality significantly shapes mosquito communities more than district-specific environmental factors.

The results provide an understanding of the impact of seasonal changes and ecological differences on mosquito distribution, which would enable the development of an efficient strategy for vector surveillance and management in this region.

Seasonal variations in mosquito communities

Our study revealed significant seasonal variations in mosquito diversity, with winter exhibiting the highest overall diversity, and summer displaying the lowest. This pattern was consistent across all districts, suggesting that seasonal factors such as temperature and rainfall, substantially influence mosquito populations than geographic location. While the present study area encompasses distinct ecological niches - hilltop, foothill, and plain ecotypes- these localized conditions, including variations in temperature, humidity, and breeding site availability, likely significantly impact the proliferation of specific mosquito species. As poikilotherms, mosquitoes are highly sensitive to temperature fluctuations. Increased temperatures lead to higher biting rates, accelerating disease transmission cycles, shorter gonotrophic cycles, resulting in increased reproductive output, enhanced fertility. However extreme temperatures can negatively impact egg production and hatchability and accelerated development of immature stages41–43.

Furthermore, climate-sensitivity influences mosquito breeding site availability. Environmental conditions, including temperature variability and climate change, can significantly impact the ecology and phenology of Anopheles vectors and their disease transmission potential44. Along with the correlation of rainfall and species richness other environmental variables such as temperature fluctuations, and humidity was also affecting the development of the mosquito’s life cycles and survival rates45,46.

To our knowledge, only three previous studies have investigated Anopheline diversity in India: Meghalaya47,48 and in Assam49.

Our analysis of lagged seasonal rainfall data demonstrated a positive correlation between rainfall and species richness, as well as Shannon and Simpson indices. Increased rainfall provides more breeding opportunities, leading to higher species richness and abundance. This finding underscores the critical link between precipitation patterns and the abundance and diversity of mosquito populations.

Dominance of An. subpictus and seasonal variations

An. subpictus emerged as the most prevalent, exhibiting significant dominance during the summer and rainy seasons, with high proportions of 328; 42.99% and 1151; 46.60%, respectively. A strong correlation was observed between the abundance of An. subpictus and the presence of temporary muddy ponds, which were abundant during periods of significant rainfall. The subsequent decrease in rainfall led to the evaporation of these ponds, demonstrating the critical influence of environmental variables, such as rainfall, on the abundance and distribution of this species. While An. subpictus is not currently considered a primary malaria vector50, evidence suggests that particular sibling species within the An. subpictus complex, particularly sibling species B, have been implicated as key vectors in coastal regions51. Furthermore, research indicates that An. subpictus may have the potential to transmit malaria parasites under favorable environmental conditions52–55.

Complementary roles of major vectors

Other major malaria vectors in Odisha, such as An. fluviatilis and An. culicifacies, exhibited varying seasonal abundances. An. fluviatilis was more prevalent during the winter, favoring habitats such as freshwater streams, river edges, and paddy fields. An. culicifacies dominated during the summer and rainy seasons, thriving in ponds with higher algal content and waterlogged paddy fields. This seasonal shift in dominant species suggests a relayed transmission pattern, where one species assumes the primary role of transmission while the other becomes less active, ensuring the continuity of the malaria transmission cycle56,57. The coexistence of these species across various ecological zones, including An. minimus, An. subpictus, and An. annularis, contributes to a complex and continuous malaria transmission window in Odisha58–60.

These species’ observed seasonal and ecological distribution and their genetic diversity and adaptability, underscores the critical need for targeted and stratified vector control strategies for effective malaria control in the region. These fluctuations highlight the complex interplay between environmental conditions and mosquito populations61–63.

Diversity, Indices, and species distribution

The study employed various diversity indices -Shannon’s Index, Simpson’s Index, and Pielou’s Index- to assess mosquito diversity, species distribution, and dominance across districts and seasons. Our findings confirmed the observed seasonal trends, with Shannon’s Index showing increased diversity in winter season and lower diversity in the summer and rainy seasons. Simpson’s Index, reflecting both species richness and evenness and Pielou’s Index, indicating species distribution balance, also revealed seasonal and spatial variations. For example, districts like Ganjam consistently exhibited high diversity (1.26–2.2), while others showed significant seasonal fluctuations. Additionally, Pielou’s evenness index revealed variations in the species distribution across both space (districts) and time (seasons). Some districts, like Kalahandi, maintained a balanced distribution of mosquito species year-round, while others displayed seasonal fluctuations. When analyzing the diversity indices, we used the Bonferroni pairwise comparison test to account for multiple comparisons between seasons and districts. This test helps ensure the statistical significance of the observed differences.

Our findings confirmed seasonal trends, with Shannon’s index indicating higher diversity in winter and lower diversity in summer and rainy seasons. Similar results were observed in Maharashtra studies, highlighting the impact of seasonal changes on mosquito diversity64. Winter conditions often support a greater variety of mosquito species due to favourable environmental factors, such as reduced temperatures that may limit dominant species and allow for a broader range of adaptive species to thrive.

Simpson’s index, reflecting both species richness and evenness, was lower in summer compared to winter season in this study. A study in southeastern Mexico also found lower abundance and diversity in the dry season compared to the cooler seasons65. This pattern is consistent across various habitats and districts, with higher diversity observed during the rainy and cold seasons. The findings emphasize the influence of seasonal changes and habitat types on mosquito diversity, underscoring the need for targeted vector control measures during periods of lower diversity.

Pielou’s index, which measures species distribution balance, revealed variations across districts and seasons. Some districts, like Kalahandi, maintained a balanced distribution of mosquito species year-round, while others exhibited seasonal fluctuations.

Implications for public health

These findings have significant implications for public health and malaria control in Odisha. Identifying the prevalent mosquito species and understanding their seasonal variations is crucial for implementing targeted vector control strategies. Different mosquito species exhibit varying capacities to transmit malaria parasites and viruses, prioritizing control efforts based on the dominant species within a specific study area is essential66. The observed seasonal changes in mosquito diversity emphasize the need for regular surveillance and the implementation of integrated vector control measures throughout the year to adapt to the dynamics of the mosquito population. While the widespread use of Long-Lasting Insecticidal Nets (LLINs) is fully justified in these districts of Odisha, the integration of complementary control measures such as Indoor Residual Spraying (IRS) with effective insecticides, and larval source management (LSM) strategies, with appropriate seasonal positioning, as informed by this study, can significantly enhance the effectiveness of vector control efforts.

Limitations

While the influence of rainfall on Anopheles species diversity is addressed, this study did not account for other potential environmental variables, such as temperature fluctuations and human activity, that also play a significant role in influencing mosquito abundance and diversity. Future research incorporating these factors could provide a more comprehensive understanding of the ecological drivers influencing Anopheles diversity and distribution.

Conclusion

This study, conducted in endemic districts of Odisha, demonstrated that climatic conditions and seasonal variations significantly influence mosquito density and species richness. These findings provide valuable insights into the dynamic nature of mosquito populations in the region, highlighting their responsiveness to seasonal and geographic fluctuations. A deeper understanding of these complex dynamics of mosquito populations is essential for developing effective and adaptable vector control strategies to mitigate the burden of mosquito-borne diseases, particularly malaria. Continuous surveillance of mosquito diversity will generate critical data to support evidence-based planning and implementation of targeted control measures, tailored to the specific transmission dynamics observed in Odisha. Such an approach will not only enhance vector management strategies but also contribute to broader malaria elimination efforts, ultimately strengthening public health interventions in endemic settings.
