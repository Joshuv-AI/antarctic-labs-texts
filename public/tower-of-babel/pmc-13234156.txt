Whole-exome sequencing increases variant detection compared to karyotyping and CMA in an unselected FGR cohort
Authors: Jianzhen Liu, Keng Lin, Zhuowen Mai, Yongshan Zhang, Xichong Li, Xiangrong Meng, Hongzhen Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aims to systematically evaluate the diagnostic yields of karyotyping, chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA), and whole-exome sequencing (WES) in an unselected cohort of fetuses with fetal growth restriction (FGR). A total of 129 FGR fetuses were included, of which 64 underwent all three genetic tests, enabling a head-to-head comparison. Among these 64 cases, WES detected genetic variants in 25.0% (16/64), compared to 9.4% (6/64) for CMA and 3.1% (2/64) for karyotyping. Notably, in cases negative by both karyotyping and CMA, WES provided an additional diagnostic yield of 15.6%. Even in isolated FGR, WES identified variants in 23.6% (13/55) of cases. Follow-up data at one year of age revealed that infants born after FGR had an increased risk of postnatal growth retardation (16.1%) and delayed language development (9.7%). This study demonstrates that in a real-world, phenotypically unselected FGR cohort, WES substantially increases variant detection, particularly in cases where conventional testing is negative.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47909-5.Subject terms: Diseases, Genetics, Medical research, Molecular biology, Molecular medicine

Introduction

Fetal growth restriction (FGR) refers to a condition in which the fetus fails to achieve its genetic growth potential in utero1,2, often manifested as an estimated fetal weight (EFW) or abdominal circumference (AC) below the 10th percentile for the corresponding gestational age. The incidence of FGR is approximately 3–7% of all pregnancies and is the second most common cause of perinatal mortality3,4. FGR also increases the risk of various long-term adverse outcomes5. Therefore, detailed ultrasound examination, genetic counseling, and prenatal diagnosis should be performed upon diagnosis of FGR6,7.

The occurrence of FGR is influenced by multiple factors, including maternal clinical conditions, genetic abnormalities, and placental factors. Fetal genetic abnormalities play a significant role among these. Aneuploidy and copy number variations (CNVs) are the most common genetic abnormalities in FGR, with chromosomal abnormalities accounting for at least 15–20% of FGR cases8,9. In contrast, for fetuses with structural anomalies, the diagnostic yield of conventional karyotyping is even higher, reported to be nearly 32%10,11, and when combined with chromosomal microarray (CMA), the detection rate increases by an additional 3–10%12–14. This disparity highlights the different genetic architectures underlying FGR and structural anomalies, and underscores the need for a tailored diagnostic approach in FGR.

The development of whole-exome sequencing (WES) has provided a technical means for exploring the etiology of FGR. The American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) recommends WES as an effective supplement when karyotyping and CMA yield negative results15.

Based on the above background, while the value of these techniques is recognized, previous studies have largely relied on comparisons of individual technologies and have predominantly focused on FGR cases with structural abnormalities, leaving the genetic etiology of isolated FGR and the optimal stepwise strategy in a general FGR population less defined. This study aims to retrospectively analyze the clinical data of 129 FGR fetuses to systematically evaluate the abnormal detection rates and distribution characteristics of karyotyping, CMA, and WES in the diagnosis of FGR. Importantly, this study does not advocate for WES as a first-line test for all FGR cases, but rather seeks to define its role within a stepwise diagnostic algorithm—particularly as a valuable complement when conventional testing (karyotyping and CMA) yields negative results. We further explore the results on pregnancy outcomes and analyze the short-term development of live-born infants. By comparing the diagnostic efficacy of conventional and advanced genetic testing techniques, this study aims to provide more targeted clinical evidence for the prenatal genetic diagnostic strategy of FGR and offer references for genetic counseling and pregnancy management.

Results

Clinical characteristics of participants

A total of 129 fetuses were diagnosed with FGR according to the fetal growth curves for Asian populations established by the NICHD (Fig. 1). The median maternal age was 31 years (range: 19–43 years), with 38 cases (29.5%) being of advanced maternal age (≥ 35 years). The median gestational age at diagnosis was 25 weeks (range: 17–35 weeks). Pregnancy history was distributed as follows: 31 primiparas (24.2%), 81 multiparas (62.8%), and an additional 17 cases (13.2%) involved pregnant individuals with prior pregnancies but no live births. Adverse pregnancy history was reported in 8 cases (6.2%), including recurrent miscarriage, previous termination due to fetal anomalies, and stillbirth. Detailed descriptions of adverse pregnancy histories are provided in Supplementary Table S1. Furthermore, both parents were identified as carriers of thalassemia in 5 cases (3.9%). Genetic abnormalities detected by karyotyping, CMA, and WES in FGR Cohorts

As shown in Table 3, the positive detection rates varied among the different testing methods applied to the cohort. Chromosomal karyotype analysis was successfully performed in 127 patients (excluding two cases of culture failure), with a positive detection rate of 5.5% (7/127). The seven abnormal karyotypes identified included three cases of numerical abnormalities: one case of trisomy 21, one case of trisomy 18, and one case of trisomy 22 mosaicism (with a mosaicism level of 10%). Structural abnormalities were observed in four cases: one case of functional trisomy 21 due to a Robertsonian translocation der(21;21); one case involving a derivative chromosome of the short arm of chromosome 3 (p26.1); and one case with a defined duplication in the long arm of chromosome 10 (q11.22q11.23). Additionally, one case exhibited an increase in the heterochromatin region of the long arm of chromosome 16 (16qh+).

CMA detected chromosomal abnormalities in 13 out of 129 tested individuals, yielding an overall detection rate of 10.1%. According to the ACMG classification guidelines, the findings included seven pathogenic abnormalities, two likely pathogenic abnormalities, and four VUS. Among the pathogenic findings were two cases of trisomy 21, one case of trisomy 13, one case of trisomy 22 mosaicism, one case of trisomy 18 mosaicism, one deletion at 15q26.2–q26, and one deletion at 7q36.2. The two likely pathogenic findings consisted of a 12q23.1–q24.11 deletion and a single-copy deletion in the Xp22.33/Yp11.32 region, specifically reported as arr[hg19] XP22.33 or YP11.32 (313,342–1,234,634 or 263,342–1,184,634)x1.

Compared with conventional karyotyping, CMA demonstrated a higher overall diagnostic yield (10.1% vs. 5.5%). This increase was attributable to CMA’s ability to identify additional aneuploidies (including one case of trisomy 21 and one case of trisomy 13 not detected by karyotyping) as well as submicroscopic copy number variations beyond the resolution of conventional cytogenetics. These findings highlight the clinical utility of CMA in reducing diagnostic omissions associated with the limited resolution of karyotype analysis.

Among the 129 FGR patients, 64 underwent trio‑based WES. Genetic variants were detected in 16 cases, yielding a detection rate of 25%. These findings included pathogenic, likely pathogenic, and variants of uncertain significance. Even though less than half of the cohort received WES, it identified 9 additional cases with reportable variants compared with karyotype and 3 additional cases compared with CMA (Table 3).

To further delineate the complementary diagnostic value of different genetic tests in FGR, we performed a paired comparison (McNemar test) in the 64 cases that underwent all three investigations (Fig. 2). WES demonstrated a significantly higher detection rate (25.0%, 16/64) than karyotyping (3.1%, 2/64; P = 0.003). In contrast, the difference between CMA (9.4%; 6/64) and karyotyping or CMA and WES were not statistically significant (9.4% vs. 3.1%; P = 0.375 ; 9.4% vs. 25%; P = 0.063). In a hypothetical stepwise diagnostic pathway—starting with karyotyping, followed by CMA in karyotype-negative cases—CMA provided an incremental yield of 6.3%. Moreover, WES identified additional pathogenic variants in 15.6% of cases that were negative on both karyotyping and CMA, highlighting its substantial additive diagnostic role.

Pregnancy outcomes and neonatal characteristics

Of the 129 pregnancies, pregnancy outcomes were collected for 123 patients (Table 4). Among these, 18 cases (14.6%) were terminated based on parental decision, 2 cases (1.6%) resulted in intrauterine fetal death, and 103 (83.7%) cases ended in live births. Among the 103 live births, there were 58 vaginal deliveries (56.3%) and 45 cesarean Sect. (43.7%). Regarding gestational age at delivery, 23 (22.3%) were preterm births (< 37 weeks) and 80 (77.7%) were full-term deliveries (≥ 37 weeks). The median birth weight was 2670 g. Among these 103 live births, the majority (66%, n = 68) had a birth weight of ≥ 2500 g, while 26.2% (n = 27) fell into the 1500–2499 g category, and 7.8% (n = 8) were of very low birth weight (< 1500 g).

Follow-up data at one year postnatal

A one-year follow-up was conducted for 31 infants, among whom one preterm infant (34 weeks+) was reported to have passed away during hospitalization after birth. Among the remaining 30 infants, 20 (64.5%) exhibited normal or good development, while 10 (32.3%) showed abnormalities. Five infants (16.1%) exhibited postnatal growth retardation, all of which manifested as underweight or failure to meet growth standards. Three infants (9.7%) experienced delayed language development, primarily characterized by an inability to vocalize or delayed vocalization. Regarding special diseases or clinical abnormalities, two infants (6.5%) were diagnosed with protein allergy. Motor development was normal in all 30 infants (100%), with no abnormal cases reported (Table 5).

Differential detection rates of genetic tests in isolated and non-isolated FGR

To assess whether concurrent ultrasound abnormalities influence the genetic diagnostic yield in FGR, we stratified the cohort into two groups: isolated FGR and FGR with associated structural anomalies or ultrasound soft markers. We then compared the positive detection rates of karyotyping, CMA, and WES between these groups.

Among 127 cases with available karyotyping results, the positive detection rate was 3.9% (4/102) in the isolated FGR group and 12.0% (3/25) in the FGR-with-abnormalities group, with no statistically significant difference between the two (Fisher’s exact test, P = 0.137). Of the 129 cases tested by CMA, positive findings were identified in 6.8% (7/103) of isolated FGR cases compared with 23.1% (6/26) of cases with concurrent abnormalities—a difference that reached statistical significance (Fisher’s exact test, P = 0.024). Among the 64 patients who underwent trio-based WES, positive variants were detected in 23.6% (13/55) of isolated FGR cases and 33.3% (3/9) of cases with additional ultrasound findings, though the difference was not significant (Fisher’s exact test, P = 0.679). Notably, even in isolated FGR, WES yielded a diagnostic rate of nearly one-quarter, underscoring its potential clinical utility in the absence of sonographic abnormalities, particularly in high-risk pregnancies.

Discussion

This study aims to systematically evaluate the diagnostic yields of karyotyping, CMA, and WES in an unselected cohort of fetuses with FGR. The results indicate that WES demonstrates significantly increased variant detection compared to conventional genetic testing methods. We identified five pathogenic variants in genes including FGFR3, FKBP10, NF1, G6PD, and GJB2. While variants in FGFR3 and FKBP10 are directly linked to skeletal dysplasias that can secondarily cause FGR16–19, the association of NF1, G6PD, and GJB2 with FGR is less direct. For instance, NF1 is associated with neurofibromatosis type 120, which may not always present with intrauterine growth restriction. These findings highlight that WES uncovers monogenic disorders, some of which may have a direct causal relationship with FGR, while others represent secondary findings with implications for postnatal management. Therefore, the clinical utility of WES extends beyond simply explaining the FGR etiology to providing a comprehensive genetic diagnosis for the fetus.

Further analysis reveals that WES provides substantial and quantifiable incremental diagnostic value in cases with negative results from conventional testing. Within a hypothetical stepwise diagnostic pathway of “karyotyping → CMA → WES,” WES contributed an additional diagnostic yield of 15.6% in cases where both karyotyping and CMA were negative. This incremental gain aligns with and further refines previous reports. While studies such as those by Chen et al. (2025) and Petrovski et al.21,22 have demonstrated the value of WES in selected cohorts—namely, karyotype/CMA-negative fetuses or those with structural anomalies—their designs may introduce selection bias by excluding cases with positive conventional findings. In contrast, our study enrolled a phenotypically unselected cohort of 129 FGR fetuses, from which 64 cases underwent all three genetic tests, enabling a relatively unbiased head-to-head comparison that reflects real-world clinical practice. By precisely quantifying the incremental yield of WES in karyotype/CMA-negative cases within this unbiased framework, our findings underscore the reproducible and robust additive value of WES in the stepwise genetic evaluation of FGR. Besides, most studies have focused on FGR with structural abnormalities, leaving the genetic etiology of isolated FGR and the understanding a general FGR population less defined.

Notably, even in the subgroup of isolated FGR—cases without other ultrasound structural abnormalities or soft markers—WES detected variants in 23.6% of cases. This finding strongly supports and extends the observations of Shi et al. (2025), who reported an 11.9% overall detection rate by WES in CMA-negative isolated FGR, with rates rising to 33.3% in those with short long bones23. Our data demonstrate that even in the absence of specific sonographic clues like shortened long bones, a substantial proportion of isolated FGR cases harbor monogenic etiologies detectable by WES. This challenges the prevailing clinical assumption that isolated FGR has a low likelihood of a genetic etiology and highlights the significant and consistent diagnostic potential of WES across phenotypic spectra. Therefore, integrating WES into the prenatal diagnostic workflow for FGR, particularly for isolated cases as suggested by growing evidence, holds promise for substantially improving etiological diagnosis rates, enabling more precise genetic counseling and pregnancy management.

In cases where both karyotype analysis and CMA yielded negative results, WES successfully identified multiple definitive monogenic pathogenic variants, providing crucial etiological diagnoses for FGR (Table 6). For instance, WES detected nonsense mutations in the NF1 gene associated with neurofibromatosis type 1, mutations in the FGFR3 gene linked to skeletal dysplasia, pathogenic variants in the FKBP10 gene causing severe osteogenesis imperfecta, as well as mutations in the G6PD and GJB2 genes involved in metabolic and auditory functions, respectively. These cases encompass various inheritance patterns, including denovo dominant mutations, recessive disorders, and X-linked inheritance, demonstrating the robust capability of WES in elucidating monogenic etiologies that conventional testing fails to identify. These diagnoses not only directly informed clinical decision-making—including pregnancy management, postnatal monitoring, and genetic counseling—but also further underscore the importance of integrating WES into the diagnostic workflow for FGR, particularly in cases with negative conventional test results, to achieve precise etiological diagnosis and improve the management of fetuses and newborns.

Our cohort data offer encouraging prognostic benchmarks for clinical counseling. The overall live birth rate of 83.7% (103/123) aligns with contemporary series demonstrating that, under structured antenatal surveillance, most FGR fetuses can be carried to viability21. Neonatal anthropometry further supports cautious optimism: 66% of live-born infants achieved a birth weight ≥ 2500 g, while only 7.8% were of very low birth weight (< 1500 g). This distribution suggests that a substantial proportion of growth-restricted fetuses maintain a baseline growth trajectory in utero.

Most notably, developmental follow-up at one year of age indicated generally positive early trajectories. Among 31 infants assessed, 64.5% exhibited age-appropriate growth and neurodevelopment. Crucially, gross motor development was normal in 100% of these children, providing early reassurance regarding a key domain of neurological integrity. These findings are consistent with studies highlighting the potential for favorable outcomes in appropriately monitored FGR cohorts24. They underscore that FGR, particularly when detected and managed in a tertiary care setting, is not synonymous with adverse developmental destiny.

Despite advancements in diagnostic technology, significant risks and uncertainties persist that cannot be overlooked. Among infants with abnormal follow-up outcomes, 16.1% (5/31) exhibited “failure to meet weight standards or growth retardation.” Additionally, 9.7% (3/31) of infants experienced delayed language development. Although the proportion is low (2.9%, 3/103), congenital structural malformations such as inguinal hernia, pulmonary sequestration, and cleft lip and palate were still observed among live-born infants. Furthermore, 6.5% (2/31) of infants were diagnosed with protein allergy during follow-up, suggesting that infants with FGR may possess specific immunological or metabolic susceptibilities.

Furthermore, two cases in this study illustrate the current diagnostic complexities and technological limitations. Case 51 exhibited a heterozygous deletion in the 9q33.1 region detected by both CMA and WES, which was classified as a VUS. Although parental verification excluded benign polymorphisms, the lack of clear genotype-phenotype correlation data prevented definitive assessment of its pathogenicity. However, the fetus ultimately experienced intrauterine death, highlighting the extreme complexity of interpreting VUS in the prenatal setting. In clinical practice, VUS is often reported conservatively, yet its potential biological significance cannot be ignored when it coexists with severe phenotypes such as FGR or adverse outcomes. Case 66 was diagnosed with FGR accompanied by high-risk serological markers at 18 weeks of gestation, yet QF-PCR, karyotyping, and CMA detected no abnormalities. The fetus ultimately experienced intrauterine death. The stark contrast between this “completely negative” diagnostic result and the severe outcome suggests that existing conventional technologies have blind spots and may miss pathogenic mechanisms such as balanced complex chromosomal rearrangements, non-coding regulatory mutations, epigenetic abnormalities, or mitochondrial DNA mutations. These cases indicate that the current understanding of the complete etiological spectrum of FGR remains incomplete. Future strategies should integrate long-read sequencing, whole-genome sequencing, and epigenomic analyses to overcome these existing diagnostic limitations.

Given the substantial incremental diagnostic yield (15.6%) of WES in cases with negative karyotype and CMA results—and its ability to identify causative variants in nearly one-quarter of isolated FGR cases—we recommend incorporating WES into the diagnostic algorithm for FGR, particularly for cases with negative conventional testing or isolated presentation. A stepwise “karyotype → CMA → WES” approach can systematically enhance etiological diagnosis, reducing diagnostic uncertainty and delay.

For cases involving VUS, genetic counseling must extend beyond variant report interpretation. Establishing a collaborative framework involving obstetrics, fetal medicine, genetics, and neonatology is essential. Counseling should dynamically integrate genetic findings with serial ultrasound phenotyping, enabling more accurate assessment of a VUS’s potential clinical impact and supporting families with balanced, individualized risk-based decision-making.

It is crucial to communicate clearly to families that negative results from even the most advanced genetic tests do not eliminate fetal risk or guarantee a favorable outcome. Cases in this study with negative results but adverse outcomes underscore the presence of non-genetic or currently undetectable etiologies. Therefore, all FGR fetuses, regardless of genetic test results, warrant intensive prenatal surveillance. Families should be counseled about residual risks to ensure fully informed shared decision-making.

While this study provides valuable insights into the genetic etiology of FGR, several limitations must be acknowledged when interpreting the results. First, the single-center retrospective design and modest sample size (n = 129, with only 64 undergoing WES) may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of our findings, particularly regarding the diagnostic yield of WES in unselected FGR populations. Second, the follow-up duration in this study was limited to one year postnatally, and the assessment was standardized by age. Third, our study defined FGR primarily by EFW and AC below the 10th percentile, without a systematic sub-classification based on biometric patterns (e.g., symmetric vs. asymmetric). Besides, our study did not assess uniparental disomy (UPD), a known contributor to FGR through its effects on imprinted genes. The techniques used in this study are not designed to detect UPD or methylation abnormalities. Therefore, the potential role of UPD in our FGR cohort remains unexplored, representing a diagnostic blind spot.

To address these limitations and advance the field, future studies should prioritize multi-center prospective cohorts with standardized genetic testing protocols to validate our findings in larger, more diverse populations. A longer and more strategically timed follow-up (e.g., at key developmental stages like preschool age) would provide more clinically meaningful insights into long-term outcomes. Future prospective studies should incorporate such longitudinal follow-up designs. As different FGR subtypes may have distinct etiologies, future studies should incorporate such detailed classification. Technologically, the integration of more comprehensive approaches—such as long-read sequencing for complex structural variants, whole-genome sequencing to capture non-coding regions, and epigenomic analyses—will be crucial to illuminate current diagnostic blind spots.

Methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective cohort study was conducted on fetuses who underwent prenatal diagnosis between January 2018 and December 2024. Fetuses were included if they met the following criteria: (1) Prenatal diagnosis of FGR, defined as an EFW and/or AC below the 10th percentile for gestational age according to the Asian population-specific fetal growth standards established by the National Institute of Child Health and Human Development (NICHD). (2) Underwent at least one of the following genetic tests: chromosomal karyotyping, CMA, or WES. (3) Informed consent for the use of clinical data for research purposes was obtained from all participants.

Clinical data were extracted from electronic medical records and included the following: maternal age, obstetric history (primiparous, multiparous, previous pregnancy without delivery), history of adverse pregnancy outcomes, and parental genetic background. Gestational age at diagnosis of FGR, ultrasound findings (classified as isolated FGR, FGR with structural abnormalities, or FGR with ultrasound soft markers), and results of prenatal serum screening for common aneuploidies were also recorded. Pregnancy outcomes included live birth, termination of pregnancy, intrauterine fetal death, gestational age at delivery, and mode of delivery. Data on birth weight, presence of congenital malformations, and results of the one-year postnatal follow-up were collected. Follow-up assessments included physical growth parameters, achievement of neurodevelopmental milestones (language and motor development), and diagnosis of any specific diseases.

Ethical approval and consent to participate

This study was carried out following the Helsinki Declaration’s ethical guidelines. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Guangzhou Huadu District Maternal and Child Health Care Hospital (Approval No.: 2025-056).

Chromosomal Karyotyping

Performed on cultured amniocytes or chorionic villus samples using standard G-banding techniques. Karyotypes were described according to the International System for Human Cytogenetic Nomenclature (ISCN).

CMA

Genomic DNA from amniotic fluid was analyzed using the Affymetrix Cytoscan 750 K array to detect CNVs. The assay was conducted according to standard protocols and reported following ISCN guidelines. Reporting criteria were as follows: benign or likely benign CNVs were generally not reported. For clinically significant CNVs, a threshold of ≥ 100kbp (≥ 50 markers) was applied. Variants of uncertain significance (VUS) in prenatal samples were reported only if deletions were ≥ 500 kb or duplications ≥ 1 Mb. Homozygous regions ≥ 10 Mb were reported if potentially clinically relevant. For CNV interpretation, we referred to public databases including the Database of Genomic Variants (DGV), ClinGen, and DECIPHER.

WES

For cases requiring further molecular diagnosis, trio-based WES (fetus and both parents) was performed. Genomic DNA was enriched using the Nadon WES 2.0 Pro capture kit, targeting the coding exons and flanking regions (within 10 bp of exon boundaries) of approximately 4,000 genes associated with human diseases. The gene list was primarily based on the ClinGen database (updated September 22, 2022), including genes with “strong” or “definitive” evidence for disease association, supplemented by additional important genes recommended by the first edition of the “Prenatal Exome Sequencing Genetic Counseling and Reporting Standards.”

Sequencing was performed on an Illumina platform with paired-end reads. The mean sequencing depth was > 100× for target regions, with > 98% of target bases achieving at least 20× coverage. Bioinformatics analysis, including alignment to the human reference genome (GRCh37/hg19), variant calling, and annotation, was performed using a standardized pipeline.

Detected variants were filtered against population databases, including gnomAD, 1000 Genomes, and dbSNP, to exclude those with a minor allele frequency > 1%. Variants were prioritized based on predicted pathogenicity using multiple in silico prediction tools, including SIFT, PolyPhen-2, MutationTaster, PROVEAN, REVEL, and splice-site prediction tools such as Human Splicing Finder and MaxEntScan. Inheritance patterns and phenotypic relevance to the fetal ultrasound findings were also considered.

Variant classification followed the ACMG/AMP guidelines [PMID: 25741868] and the ACMG points to consider document for prenatal exome sequencing [PMID: 31911674]. Reference databases including ClinVar, OMIM, and HGMD were consulted for variant interpretation. Reportable findings included pathogenic variants, likely pathogenic variants, and VUS. The reporting of VUS was based on a comprehensive assessment integrating clinical context.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 17.0 software. Descriptive statistics were presented as median (range) for continuous variables and frequency (percentage) for categorical variables. The positive detection rates of karyotyping, CMA, and WES were calculated as the proportion of cases with reportable findings among the total tested cases.

Differences in detection rates between isolated and non-isolated FGR groups were compared using Fisher’s exact test. In the subset of patients who underwent all three tests, the overall difference in detection rates was assessed using Cochran’s Q test. If the Q test was significant (P < 0.05), post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using McNemar tests, with P-values adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni method. A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
