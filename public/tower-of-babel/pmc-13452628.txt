Course of Portal Hypertension and Its Prognostic Impact After Liver Transplantation

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundWhile portal hypertension (PH) typically resolves after liver transplantation (LT), persistence of PH may affect post‐transplant outcomes. We assessed the evolution of PH after LT and its impact on adverse outcomes.MethodsWe recorded clinical, laboratory, and imaging parameters of LT recipients between 2016 and 2022 in Vienna and Zagreb. Persistent features of CSPH were defined as the presence of portosystemic collaterals, platelet count (PLT) ≤ 110 G/L, and/or splenomegaly (≥ 13 cm). PH‐related clinical events were defined as variceal bleeding, ascites requiring intervention, hepatic hydrothorax, portal vein thrombosis, or liver‐related death within 12 months post‐LT. Post‐LT outcomes were analyzed using landmark Cox regression for 3 months post‐LT.ResultsOf 645 LT recipients (76% male, median age 59 years) listed with a median MELD of 15 points and PLT of 99 G/L, features of CSPH were present in 537 (83.3%) at baseline. At year 1 after LT, MELD improved to 9 (IQR 7–12), PLT increased to 160 G/L (IQR 120–207), and features of CSPH persisted in 251/453 classifiable patients (55.4%). PH events occurred in 75 (11.6%) and 84 (13%) deaths. In a landmark analysis from 3 months post‐LT (n = 521, 29 deaths), persistent thrombocytopenia (< 110 G/L) at M3 independently predicted mortality (aHR 2.31, 95% CI 1.01–5.30, p = 0.048) after adjustment for MELD, age, CRP, and pre‐LT TIPS.ConclusionsWhile PH improves after LT, features of CSPH persisted in 55% of classifiable patients and PH‐related clinical events occurred in 11.6% within the first year after LT. Persistent thrombocytopenia (< 110 G/L) at 3 months post‐LT independently predicted post‐LT mortality (aHR 2.31, 95% CI 1.01–5.30, p = 0.048).Keywords: liver transplantation, platelet dynamics, portal hypertension, post‐transplant outcomes, thrombocytopenia

Introduction

Portal hypertension (PH) is a frequent complication of advanced chronic liver disease (ACLD) [1] and presents one of the key disease‐driving mechanisms in patients with ACLD [2]. Liver transplantation (LT) may be necessary in ACLD due to acute or chronic liver failure. Usually, PH resolves promptly after LT. However, PH and associated complications may also persist and consequently lead to post‐LT complications such as refractory ascites, hydrothorax and variceal bleeding [3]. The prevalence/persistence of PH after LT and its complications is estimated 7% [4] and has a major negative impact on patient survival with reported 1‐year post‐LT survival rates of 75.8% (vs. 92.3% without PH) [4, 5].

PH can be of prehepatic, intrahepatic and posthepatic origin with pre/post‐hepatic PH being commonly related to surgical complications (hepatic venous outflow or portal vein inflow obstruction at the level of anastomosis), while intrahepatic PH is due to parenchymal graft diseases [6]. Portal vein thrombosis (PVT) and portal vein anastomotic stenosis (PVAS) are the most frequent prehepatic causes leading to PH after LT. PVT is an uncommon but serious complication with a reported incidence of around 3% in adult transplant recipients [7]. Risk factors for post‐LT PVT include preoperative PVT, surgical stenosis, splenectomy and portosystemic shunts [8]. The inferior vena cava stenosis is a rare posthepatic cause of PH and commonly presents with de novo or persisting ascites after LT. It occurs early in the posttransplant period due to early surgical complications, or years after LT due to neointimal hyperplasia or perivascular fibrosis.

Moreover, intrahepatic PH after LT usually results from progressive graft diseases, which rapidly cause graft fibrosis/cirrhosis. Causes are heterogeneous and include graft rejection, cholangitis, post‐transplant metabolic liver disease, recurrence of the underlying liver disease etiology, or de novo viral hepatitis (B, C and E) [9].

In contrast to well‐established guidelines for PH management prior to LT [10], high‐level evidence‐based recommendations for the treatment of post‐LT PH and associated complications are lacking.

Methods

Study Population and Study Design

This multicenter study was conducted as an explorative retrospective cohort study in patients undergoing LT at two European centers between 01/2016 and 12/2022. We included patients ≥ 18 years receiving LT between 01.01.2016 and 31.12.2022 at the Vienna General Hospital and University Hospital Merkur, Zagreb. Patients were excluded if any of the following criteria were present: (i) age < 18 years, (ii) re‐Transplantation, (iii) presence of polycystic liver disease, (iv) extrahepatic malignancy and (v) acute liver failure. Demographic, clinical, imaging data and laboratory values were retrieved from patient's medical records and collected at listing for LT prior to LT, 3–6 months after LT and 12 months post‐LT. PH was managed according to national [11] and international guidelines [10]. The vast majority of liver grafts in both centers were whole‐liver grafts from donation after brain death (DBD); split‐liver and living‐donor transplants represented a small minority of cases.

Laboratory Values

Missing laboratory values were not imputed, and all analyses were conducted based on available data. The number of available cases for each parameter is reported in the respective tables. Variables with incomplete data are indicated with the proportion of available cases. Sensitivity analyses were performed where appropriate to assess the potential impact of missing data on key findings. Platelet count at 3 months post‐LT was classified into three pre‐specified clinically grounded categories: normalization (≥ 150 G/L, reference), partial recovery (110–149 G/L), and persistent thrombocytopenia (< 110 G/L), aligned with Baveno VII criteria for CSPH features. Platelet values were used as measured throughout all analyses without any cap or transformation.

Definition of CSPH Features and PH‐Associated Events

Features of CSPH were defined symmetrically at baseline (time of listing), 3 months post‐LT, and 12 months post‐LT as the presence of at least one of the following: (i) portosystemic collaterals on cross‐sectional imaging, (ii) platelet count ≤ 110 G/L, and/or (iii) splenomegaly defined as longitudinal spleen diameter ≥ 13 cm. The 110 G/L platelet threshold is aligned with the Baveno VII criteria for CSPH features. Varices were excluded from the symmetric longitudinal definition because systematic endoscopic surveillance was performed only at baseline; varices at baseline are retained as a descriptive parameter in Table 1. De novo features of CSPH were defined as the appearance of any of the three CSPH‐features criteria at 12 months post‐LT in patients without CSPH features at LT. PH‐related clinical events within 12 months post‐LT were defined as the occurrence of variceal bleeding, ascites requiring intervention (grade 2–3 per Baveno VII), hepatic hydrothorax, portal vein thrombosis, and/or liver‐related death. Decompensated cirrhosis at the time of listing was defined clinically as the presence of any of the following: clinically significant ascites (grade 2–3), variceal bleeding, and/or hepatic encephalopathy, consistent with Baveno VII criteria [10]. Severe surgical complications were recorded descriptively but were not included in the landmark analyses of post‐3‐month outcomes because, by definition, these events can only occur before the M3 landmark.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R 4.2.3 (R Core Team, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and GraphPad Prism 10.4.1 (GraphPad Software, CA, USA). Missing values were not imputed; available‐case numbers are reported in the tables. Categorical variables were reported as absolute (n) and relative frequencies (%), whereas continuous variables as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range [IQR]), as appropriate. Student's t‐test was used for group comparisons of normally distributed variables and Mann‐Whitney‐U‐test for non‐normally distributed variables. Group comparisons of categorical variables were performed using either Chi‐squared or Fisher's exact tests, as appropriate. Follow‐up time was calculated as the time from liver transplantation to the date of portal hypertensive event, death, or last follow‐up at the General Hospital Vienna or the Merkur University Hospital Zagreb using the Reverse Kaplan‐Meier Method. The endpoints assessed were overall mortality and portal hypertensive events/liver‐related death within 12 months post‐LT. For analyses of portal hypertensive events/liver‐related death, patients were censored at the time of non‐liver‐related death or last follow‐up.

For analyses in which platelet count at 3 months post‐LT served as the exposure variable, we applied a pre‐specified landmark framework with the time origin reset to 3 months post‐LT, excluding patients with events before the landmark, censored before 3 months, or without a documented 3‐month platelet measurement. Platelet count at 3 months was classified into three pre‐specified categories aligned with Baveno VII: normalization (≥ 150 G/L, reference), partial recovery (110–149 G/L), and persistent thrombocytopenia (< 110 G/L).

Uni‐ and multivariable Cox regression analyses were used to estimate hazard ratios. The pre‐specified multivariable model included the M3 platelet category, MELD at listing, age, CRP, and pre‐LT TIPS, chosen for biological relevance and appropriate events‐per‐variable ratio. Model performance was summarized using Harrell's concordance index and the time‐dependent AUC at 12 months post‐landmark. A sensitivity analysis excluding patients with pre‐LT TIPS was performed. Cumulative incidence functions were estimated using the Aalen‐Johansen method, treating death without a prior PH‐event as a competing risk. Survival curves were compared by log‐rank test. Sankey plot illustrated CSPH‐features trajectories from baseline to 12 months post‐LT.

The MELD score in this study refers to the original Model for End‐Stage Liver Disease (MELD) score used by Eurotransplant for organ allocation [12]. The level of significance was set at a 2‐sided p‐value < 0.05.

Ethical Considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and its amendments and was approved by the local ethics committee (EK: 2185/2023), which waived the requirement of written informed consent for the retrospective analysis.

Results

Patient Characteristics at Time of Listing for LT

A total of 645 patients undergoing LT between 2016 and 2022 at two centers (Zagreb: n = 321; Vienna: n = 324) were included in this study. A detailed overview of the patient selection process is shown in Figure 1. The overall cohort predominantly consisted of male patients (76%), with a median age of 59 years (IQR: 52–64). When comparing the two centers, the Vienna cohort was slightly younger with a median age of 57 years compared to 60 years in the Zagreb cohort and had a higher proportion of male recipients (81% vs. 71%).

Patient flow chart.

The majority of patients (n = 492, 76%) were decompensated at time of listing and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) was present in 37% (n = 241) of the cohort. The leading underlying etiology was alcohol‐related liver disease (ALD), accounting for 46% of cases, followed by viral hepatitis in 15% and Metabolic Dysfunction‐associated Steatotic Liver Disease (MASLD) in 9%. At the time of listing, the median MELD score was 15 (IQR: 11–19), with 51% of patients yielding a MELD score of ≥ 15 and 3% presenting with MELD scores of 30 or higher. Most patients were classified as Child‐Turcotte‐Pugh (CTP) stage B (50%) or C (31%), with a median CTP score of 8 (IQR: 7–10). Laboratory values indicated advanced liver disease in the majority of LT recipients, with 76% of patients presenting thrombocytopenia (< 150 G/L) and a median platelet count of 99 G/L. Laboratory markers of liver function were comparable between the Vienna and the Zagreb cohorts (see baseline characteristics in Table 1 and Supporting Information S1: Table ST1).

PH Before and After LT

Features of CSPH were highly prevalent at listing, with 537 patients (83.3%) fulfilling at least one criterion (collaterals, PLT ≤ 110 G/L, or splenomegaly ≥ 13 cm). PH‐related complications were frequently observed, with 61% of patients presenting with varices, 58% with ascites, and 15% with a history of variceal bleeding at baseline. In total, 7% of patients had undergone transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt (TIPS) placement prior to LT, with a marked difference between the two centers, as the procedure had been performed in 13% of the Vienna cohort and 1% of the Zagreb cohort (Supporting Information S1: Table ST1). Features of CSPH decreased substantially after LT, from 537/645 (83.3%) at baseline to 323/551 (58.6%) at 3 months and 271/526 (51.5%) at 12 months. Among the 537 patients with CSPH features at baseline, 453 could be classified at 12 months; of these, CSPH features persisted in 251 (55.4%) and resolved in 202 (44.6%). A Sankey plot of patient trajectories between baseline and 12‐month follow‐up is shown in Figure 2. De novo features of CSPH occurred in 19 of 108 patients (17.6%). Splenomegaly showed only minimal regression after LT, persisting in a substantial proportion of patients. Notably, post‐LT platelet recovery did not differ across spleen‐trajectory groups (decreasing, stable, or increasing spleen diameter; p = 0.518) (Supporting Information S1: Figure SF2).

Evolution of Clinical and Laboratory Parameters Pre/post LT

A significant improvement in clinical and laboratory parameters was observed following LT. The median MELD score decreased from 16 (IQR: 13–21) prior to LT to 9 (IQR: 7–12) at 12 months post‐LT. Platelet count showed substantial recovery, rising from a median of 90 G/L pre‐LT to 160 G/L at 12 months post‐LT. Similarly, albumin levels improved from 33 g/L before LT to 42 g/L one year after LT. In addition, bilirubin and INR levels normalized post‐LT. These findings highlight the significant reversal of liver dysfunction following LT, as detailed in Table 2, Figure 3 and Supporting Information S1: Table ST3.

Liver‐Related Events During Follow‐Up

During the 12‐month follow‐up period, 84 patients (13.0%) died, resulting in a 12‐month survival rate of 87% (Supporting Information S1: Table ST2, Figure SF1). Despite an overall improvement in liver function, 75 patients (11.6%) experienced PH‐related events or liver‐related death within the first 12 months after LT (Supporting Information S1: Table ST2, Figure 4B). Prognostic Impact of Platelet Dynamics in LT Recipients

Platelet count dynamics emerged as a key predictor of clinical outcomes. Using a pre‐specified landmark framework from 3 months post‐LT (n = 521 patients with 29 deaths, n = 513 with 25 PH‐events/liver‐related deaths), patients were stratified into three platelet categories at M3: normalization (≥ 150 G/L, n = 320, 59.9%), partial recovery (110–149 G/L, n = 113, 21.2%), and persistent thrombocytopenia (< 110 G/L, n = 101, 18.9%). In the multivariable Cox model adjusted for MELD, age, CRP, and pre‐LT TIPS, persistent thrombocytopenia at M3 was independently associated with post‐landmark mortality (aHR 2.31, 95% CI 1.01–5.30, p = 0.048, Figure 4A) and showed a similar direction of effect for PH‐related clinical events/liver‐related death (aHR 2.06, 0.82–5.19, p = 0.126), see Table 3. In a pre‐specified sensitivity analysis excluding the 45 patients with pre‐LT TIPS, the effect was preserved and numerically slightly stronger (aHR 2.51, p = 0.032 for mortality).

Surgical Complications

Severe surgical complications within 90 days post‐LT occurred in 141 patients (21.9%) and are reported descriptively in Supporting Information S1: Table ST2; these events were by definition excluded from the landmark analyses.

Discussion

Our analysis of 645 LT recipients provides valuable insights into the evolution of PH after LT, and its prognostic implications.

While features of CSPH resolved or attenuated in the majority of patients after LT, 55% continued to show at least one feature at 12 months. Although MELD improved substantially, MELD and PH reflect conceptually distinct processes—MELD captures hepatocellular synthetic and excretory function, whereas CSPH is a hemodynamic phenomenon—and MELD improvement should not be interpreted as direct resolution of PH. Accordingly, post‐LT MELD was not associated with post‐landmark outcomes in our analysis.

This underscores the importance of continuous post‐transplant PH monitoring, particularly in light of evidence suggesting that even subclinical portal hypertension (HVPG values ≥ 6 mm Hg) can contribute to disease progression in LT recipients, especially those with HCV recurrence after LT [13]. Interestingly, while portosystemic collaterals usually vanish or decrease after PH‐targeted therapy [14], splenomegaly often persists. Persistence of CSPH features has repeatedly been shown to be associated with unfavorable prognosis, e.g. in patients with primary biliary cholangitis or with autoimmune hepatitis [15].

One of the most notable findings is the prognostic significance of platelet count trajectories in the early post‐LT period. Using a pre‐specified landmark framework starting at 3 months post‐LT, patients with persistent thrombocytopenia (< 110 G/L) at M3 had an approximately two‐fold higher risk of mortality compared to patients with normalized platelet counts (≥ 150 G/L), independent of MELD, age, CRP, and pre‐LT TIPS; a similar effect direction was observed for PH‐related clinical events and liver‐related death. These estimates were robust in a pre‐specified sensitivity analysis excluding patients with pre‐LT TIPS, and in a time‐dependent ROC analysis, the M3 platelet category outperformed MELD, age, and CRP as a predictor of post‐landmark mortality, supporting the notion that post‐transplant platelet recovery captures prognostic information not reflected in synthetic liver function scores. Importantly, the prognostic utility of the M3 platelet trajectory is by design restricted to outcomes occurring after the 3‐month landmark, since early post‐LT complications (biliary leaks, hepatic artery thrombosis, sepsis) may themselves contribute to suboptimal platelet recovery at M3. A longer‐term prognostic role is supported by our competing‐risks analysis, which showed an early and sustained divergence between patients with persistent thrombocytopenia and those with partial or complete recovery.

While Eyraud et al. [16] previously described persistent thrombocytopenia after LT, our study is the first to establish its prognostic implications. The mechanisms underlying post‐LT thrombocytopenia are multifactorial and incompletely understood, including PH‐related causes (splenic or graft sequestration due to persistent PH) and non‐PH‐related mechanisms such as immune‐mediated thrombocytopenia, impaired thrombopoietin synthesis, viral infection, medications, or hemodilution [17, 18, 19, 20].

Clinically, these findings argue for systematic platelet monitoring post‐LT as a simple tool for early risk stratification. For patients with declining or persistently low platelets, we suggest a tiered diagnostic approach; (i) closer clinical and laboratory follow‐up during the first post‐LT year; (ii) a low threshold for Doppler ultrasound to exclude vascular causes of persisting PH; (iii) non‐invasive assessment by liver and spleen stiffness where available; (iv) endoscopic screening, particularly in patients with a history of pre‐LT variceal bleeding; and (v) hemodynamic confirmation (HVPG or EUS‐guided portal pressure gradient) in cases of diagnostic uncertainty.

Several limitations warrant consideration. These include potential selection bias due to the retrospective study design and the inability to fully adjust for all potential confounders affecting platelet counts, such as medication effects or infections. Imaging follow‐up was not protocolized, limiting spleen and collateral documentation at post‐LT timepoints. Sarcopenia, liver and spleen stiffness, and liver graft type were not systematically available. The relatively low median MELD at listing (reflecting the high HCC‐MELD‐exception proportion) and the low MASLD prevalence may limit generalizability. Severe surgical complications occurring within the first 3 months post‐LT were excluded from the landmark prognostic analyses, as they could not be validly predicted by the 3‐month platelet measurement; reverse causality between early complications and platelet recovery cannot be excluded. Finally, because early post‐LT surgical complications and the M3 platelet measurement temporally overlap, our prognostic inferences from M3 platelets are explicitly restricted to outcomes after the landmark.

In conclusion, persistent features of CSPH after LT are more frequent than previously recognized and predispose to PH‐related complications. Persistent thrombocytopenia at 3 months post‐LT is a simple, independent prognostic marker for post‐landmark mortality and PH‐related events, supporting its use for refined post‐transplant surveillance.
