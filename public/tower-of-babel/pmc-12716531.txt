Human papillomavirus vaccine uptake and associated factors among in-school adolescent girls in Harari regional state, Eastern Ethiopia: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Hayat Tesfaye, Haymanot Mezmur, Nesredin Ahmed, Gezahegn Tesfaye, Agumasie Semahegn
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe Human Papillomavirus (HPV) vaccine has offered a great promise to reduce the cervical cancer burden, yet its uptake remains limited, and the current evidence is mixed and inconclusive. This study aims to assess HPV vaccine uptake and its associated factors among adolescent school girls.DesignA school-based cross-sectional study.SettingSchools in Harari regional state, Eastern Ethiopia.Participantstotal of 416 female adolescent students, from 1 April 2024 to 30 April 2024.OutcomeUptake of HPV vaccination.MethodsA multi-stage sampling technique was used to select study participants. A structured interviewer-administered questionnaire was used for data collection. The collected data were entered into Epidata V.3.1 and analysed by using STATA V.17.0. A logistic regression model was used to assess the associations between dependent and independent variables. An adjusted OR with 95% CIs was used to declare a significant association.ResultAdolescent girls’ uptake of the HPV vaccine was 49.26% (95% CI 42.65% to 56.59%) Being urban residents (AOR=2.59; 95% CI 1.61 to 4.18), having a government-employed mother (AOR=2.60; 95% CI 1.12 to 6.05), availability of health promotion in their vicinity (AOR=4.17; 95% CI 2.01 to 8.66), having a good knowledge of cervical cancer and HPV infection (AOR=1.64; 95% CI 1.05 to 2.57) were significantly associated with uptake of HPV vaccineConclusionNearly half of the eligible in-school adolescent girls received the HPV vaccine. Being an urban resident, having a government-employed mother, the availability of health promotion in their vicinity, and having a good knowledge of cervical cancer and HPV infection were significantly associated with the uptake of HPV vaccination. Expanding health promotion and raising awareness of cervical cancer and HPV infection among rural adolescents could increase HPV vaccine uptake.

Background

Globally, cervical cancer is one of the major public health problems and the most common sexually transmitted infections among the female population.1 It is the fourth most common cancer affecting women and accounts for about 7.5% of cancer-related deaths in women globally.2 More than 99% of cervical cancer is caused by persistent infection with the oncogenic or high-risk sexually transmitted types of Human papillomavirus (HPV).3

The introduction of HPV vaccination marks a significant advancement in the fight against cervical cancer, aiming to prevent infections from HPV strains that contribute to a considerable incidence of cervical and other cancers.4 The HPV vaccine was first introduced in 2006 by WHO.5 Six HPV vaccines are now approved: three bivalent, two quadrivalent and one nonavalent vaccine. They all have shown great effectiveness in preventing infections with HPV strains 16 and 18, which account for over 70% of cervical cancer worldwide.6 7 The nonavalent vaccination provides further protection against five additional cancer-causing HPV types, while the quadrivalent vaccine protects against wart-causing HPV types 6 and 11.8

HPV vaccination is primarily targeted at young teenage girls aged 9 to 14 to maximise the vaccine efficacy by ensuring it is administered before the initiation of sexual activity.9 The WHO recommends that girls aged 9 to 14 years receive one or two doses of HPV vaccination.6 The protection provided by these vaccines lasts for many years, with follow-up data indicating sustained immunity over a decade post-vaccination.10 11

Although millions of people worldwide have received the HPV vaccination, which is safe and effective with few side effects, acceptance of the vaccine is still low in many areas, especially in low-income and middle-income nations.12 The global coverage for the first dose of HPV vaccine in girls only increased from 20% in 2022 to 27% in 2023.6 According to a systematic review and meta-analysis, only 28.53% of teenage girls in sub-Saharan Africa have taken the HPV vaccination.13 These disparities underscore the importance of advocating for political commitment to support vaccination efforts and integrating vaccination programmes into school health initiatives.14 15

Despite the proven safety and efficacy of the HPV vaccine, which has been administered to millions worldwide with minimal adverse effects, uptake remains low in many regions, particularly in low-income countries like Ethiopia.13 Ethiopia’s first nationwide immunisation campaign in December 2018 against HPV reached over 2.4 million girls.16 In Ethiopia, the uptake of HPV vaccination among female students is 48.6% in Mettu,17 31.65% in South West Shoa18 and 61.2% in Nekemte city.19 There remains a significant gap in meeting the global objective of vaccinating 90% of female adolescents against HPV by the age of 15, which is essential for the elimination of cervical cancer by 2030.20

Although HPV vaccine uptake has been studied in various Ethiopian settings, this study adds distinct value by focusing on in-school adolescent girls aged from 14 to 18 in Harari region, Eastern Ethiopia, a region with unique sociocultural dynamics and limited information regarding the uptake of HPV vaccination. It explores locally relevant determinants such as parental awareness and school-based education, offering insights not captured in broader national analyses. Importantly, the findings provide timely, actionable insights for regional health authorities and school-based vaccination programmes, supporting Ethiopia’s efforts to improve HPV vaccine coverage through context-specific strategies. Methods

Study design and setting

A school-based cross-sectional study was conducted from 1 April 2024 to 30 April 2024, Harari region, eastern Ethiopia. This study was conducted in Harari Regional State, located in Eastern Ethiopia, approximately 526 kilometers east of Addis Ababa. Nine woredas, comprising three rural and six urban areas, make up the region. In Harar, there are 20 primary-level and seven secondary-level governmental schools. Harar’s health infrastructure includes two public hospitals, eight health centres and private clinics, many of which are equipped to provide routine immunisation services and adolescent and youth-friendly care.21 The full study protocol is available as online supplemental material S1 and has been cited accordingly to ensure transparency and reproducibility (online supplemental Material S1). The study protocol was not pre-registered in any public trial registry.

Population and eligibility

The source population consisted of female adolescent girls aged 14–18 years enrolled in the 2023/24 academic year in Harari Regional State during the study period. Adolescent girls aged between 14 and 18 years who were enrolled in selected government primary and secondary schools in Harari Regional State were the study population. The age group of 14 to 18 years was selected because the vaccine is administered to adolescents within this age range in Ethiopia. Adolescent girls who were unable to respond to a questionnaire due to illness and enrolled in the evening class were excluded.

Sample size and sampling procedure

The sample size for the study was determined by using single population proportion formula with a prevalence (p) estimate of HPV-vaccine 44.4%;22 5% of marginal error (d) and 95% confidence level.

Finally, by considering 10% as a non-response rate, the calculated final sample size was 416.

Sampling techniques

A multi-stage sampling method was used. In the first stage, seven out of 20 primary schools and three out of seven secondary schools in the Harari region were selected using a simple random sampling method. In the second stage, the sample size was proportionally allocated according to the number of adolescent girls within the school. Within each school, the study participants were selected using a simple random sampling technique from official roster. A total of 3534 female students (2082 from primary school and 1452 from secondary school) were enrolled in those selected primary and secondary schools in the 2023/24 academic year.

Data collection methods

Six BSc nurses collected data using a structured and pretested face-to-face interviewer-administered questionnaire. The questionnaire was adopted by reviewing previous literature.2224 The questionnaire was initially prepared in English, then translated into Afaan Oromo and Amharic, and subsequently retranslated into English to check its consistency. The questionnaire includes sociodemographic characteristics like age, family occupation, educational status and household income, and assessment of the HPV vaccine uptake, attitude and overall knowledge of cervical cancer, HPV infection and HPV vaccine.

Measurement of outcome and exposure

Adolescents who had received at least one dose of the HPV vaccine were considered to have had uptake of the HPV vaccine.25

Knowledge about cervical cancer and HPV infection was assessed using 10 items, with each correct answer earning 1 point and an incorrect answer receiving a score of 0. Those scoring at or above the mean are classified as having good knowledge of cervical cancer and HPV infection, and those scoring below the mean are considered to have poor knowledge of cervical cancer and HPV infection.26

Knowledge about the HPV vaccine was measured by using 10 knowledge items, with each correct answer earning 1 point and an incorrect answer receiving a score of 0. Those scoring at or above the mean are classified as having good knowledge of the HPV vaccine, and those scoring below the mean are considered to have poor knowledge of the HPV vaccine.26

Attitude towards the HPV vaccine was assessed using five items. Adolescents who have a positive attitude towards the HPV vaccine were those who scored above the mean of attitude-assessing questions. In contrast, those who scored below the mean of attitude-assessing questions were categorised as having a negative attitude towards the HPV vaccine.26

Data processing and analysis

The collected data were entered into Epidata V.3.1 and cleaned and analysed by using STATA V.17.0. The findings of the study were presented in the form of tables, figures and narration. Mean, Median and SD were used to summarise the continuous variables. Bivariable and multivariable analyses were performed to ascertain factors associated with the uptake of the vaccine. Those variables with a p value of <0.25 in the bivariable analysis were considered candidates for a multivariable logistic regression model. Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness model fitness and multicollinearity were checked. The mean variance inflation factor was 1.7, which did not show multicollinearity among independent variables. Adjusted ORs with their 95% CIs were computed to measure the strength and direction of associations, and statistical significance was declared at p<0.05.

Data quality assurance

The questionnaire was prepared in English, translated into Afaan Oromo and Amharic, and then back to English by two translators to ensure consistency. It was pretested on 5% of the sample size in one non-selected school to check instrument consistency, adequacy of variables and question order. Modifications were made based on the findings. Internal consistencies were checked for each composite index score. Data collectors and supervisors were trained by the principal investigator on how to use the questionnaire and approach participants. The completed questionnaires were checked for completeness by supervisors at the data collection site and by the principal investigator.

Patient and public involvement

There was no patient and public involvement.

A total of 404 school-aged adolescent girls participated in the study, with a 97.11% response rate. The majority of 233 (57.7%) of the participants were in the age range of 14–15 years, with a mean age of 15.18 and a SD of ±0.85 years. Two hundred twenty-six (55.94%) of the participants living in urban areas, 253 (62.62 %) were Muslim by religion, and 260 (64.36%) were living with both parents (table 1).

Uptake of human papillomavirus

Of the total respondents, 199 (49.26 %) with 95% CI (42.65% to 56.59%) had taken the HPV vaccine. Among those who received the HPV vaccine, 103 (51.76%) had received the full two doses. The top motivator for the HPV vaccine was pre-health information provided in school health education (73; 36.68%), followed by health professionals’ advice (46; 23.12%), while peer influence and family support accounted for 23 (11.56%) and 19 (9.55%), respectively. The most common reasons for not getting the HPV vaccine were not being informed of the date of the vaccination (34.48%) and fear of becoming infertile (15.76%). Other reasons, such as fear of needle, fear of side effects and belief that they are not sexually active, account for 14.29%, 10.84%, 11.10% and 6.40%, respectively.

Factors associated with uptake of HPV vaccine

After controlling for possible confounders, being urban resident (AOR=2.59; 95% CI 1.61 to 4.19), having a government-employed mother (AOR=2.60; 95% CI 1.12 to 6.05), availability of health promotion in their vicinity (AOR=4.17; 95% CI 2.01 to 8.66), having good knowledge of cervical cancer and HPV infection (AOR=1.64; 95% CI 1.05 to 2.57) were significantly associated with uptake of HPV vaccination (table 4).

Discussion

This study aimed to assess the uptake of HPV vaccination and its associated factors among in-school adolescent girls in Harar, eastern Ethiopia. Overall, nearly half of the study participants received HPV vaccination. Being an urban resident, having a government-employed mother, availability of health promotion in their vicinity, having a good knowledge of cervical cancer and HPV infection were significantly associated with uptake of HPV vaccination.

The uptake of HPV vaccination in this study (49.26%) was comparable with previous studies conducted in Ethiopia, such as in Mettu town (48.6%)17 and Bahir Dar (45.3%).27 However, the uptake of HPV vaccination in this study is relatively higher compared with studies conducted in Uganda (19.6%)28 and Singapore (9.8%).29 The disparity could be attributed to differences in coverage strategy and cultural attitudes towards vaccination in the country.30 31 Additionally, the difference may result from variations in approaches to quantifying HPV vaccine uptake; for instance, the Ugandan study required three doses, while this study counted any prior vaccination.

The uptake of HPV vaccination in our study is relatively lower compared with the findings of the study conducted in Germany (59.6%)32 and Malaysia (77.9%).33 This disparity can be attributed to several factors, including limited access to healthcare facilities, insufficient awareness about the vaccine and cultural beliefs that may contribute to vaccine hesitancy.34 35 Developing culturally sensitive interventions and engaging with local communities to tailor communication strategies can foster greater vaccine acceptance.

In this study, residence has shown a statistically significant association with HPV uptake as those who are living in urban areas were 2.6 times more likely to receive the vaccine compared with those who grew up in rural areas. This finding is consistent with the study conducted in Western Ethiopia19 and Uganda, Kampala.36 This might be due to the exposure of urban adolescents to media and having more access to healthcare facilities than the rural ones. The other possible justification might be poor message delivery system of the responsible body and lack of awareness by the students, which also might be due to less vaccine availability in the rural area.31 37

This study found that respondents whose mothers are government employees are more likely to take the HPV vaccine. Consistent with this study finding, another study found that the odds of knowing the HPV vaccine were higher among parents of girls who worked as government employees.38 39 Adolescents whose mother was employed to a government-owned institution might have better access to media (print, social and mass media) as well as being more likely to be literate and exposed to HPV vaccination information. Educated communities are better equipped to engage in discussions about health policies, support public health initiatives and participate in collective actions that promote community well-being. They can also leverage their knowledge to easily navigate healthcare systems, ensuring timely vaccinations and other preventive measures.40 41

In this study, the availability of promotion in the adolescent’s vicinity was associated with the vaccine uptake. In congruence with this finding, a study from Ambo in Ethiopia reported that availability of awareness creation (even if for an irregular/short period) helped them to gain more information about the benefits of the vaccine, which in turn influenced their decision to take the vaccine.42 Other studies43 44 also highlighted the importance of targeted awareness programmes and accessible healthcare services in improving vaccination rates. By providing information and promoting the benefits of the HPV vaccine, these initiatives can significantly impact public health outcomes.

This study revealed that adolescents who had good knowledge about cervical cancer and HPV infection were more likely to receive HPV vaccination compared with their counterparts. This study is supported by a study conducted in south-west Ethiopia45 and Brazil.46 The possible justification is that by understanding the severity of HPV, its transmission methods and preventive measures, as well as having knowledge about the disease and how to prevent it, adolescents are more likely to be encouraged to receive the vaccine and protect themselves against infection.23 47 In this manner, the findings in this study will be a significant input for policymakers to understand coverage by region and expand the service by designing interventions based on the identified factors. This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, its institutional-based design restricts the generalisability of results to the broader community population of the same age group. Second, the cross-sectional nature of the study precludes any inference of causal relationships between the outcome and independent variables. Third, HPV vaccination status was assessed through verbal self-report during face-to-face interviews. Although efforts were made to cross-check responses using vaccination cards, the available registers were not consistently updated, which may have introduced recall or reporting bias. Fourth, the study was limited to governmental schools, which were selected because they enrol the majority of adolescent girls in the Harari region and primarily serve lower-income and middle-income households. This focus enhances relevance for public health programming but may limit comparability with private school populations. Fifth, students who were acutely ill or enrolled in night school were excluded based on practical and ethical considerations. Ill students were excluded to avoid placing undue burden and to ensure informed, voluntary participation. Night school attendees, often older, working adolescents with different exposure to school-based health education, were logistically difficult to access and represented a distinct demographic. Including them would have required a separate sampling frame and could have compromised the study’s focus on the standard in-school adolescent population targeted by Ethiopia’s HPV vaccination strategy. Future studies should assess HPV vaccine uptake among marginalised groups and across both public and private schools to inform more equitable programme design.

Conclusion

The uptake of the HPV vaccine by in-school adolescent girls was found to be low, and far lower than the national HPV vaccine coverage target. Factors that positively influenced uptake include urban residence, having a government-employed mother, availability of health promotion in their vicinity, and having good knowledge of cervical cancer and HPV infection. We suggest an intervention tailored to adolescent behaviour, alongside expanding outreach health promotion initiatives and raising awareness of cervical cancer and HPV infection targeting rural adolescents to improve the HPV vaccine uptake.
