Precedence-Constrained Decision Trees and Coverings
Michał Szyfelbein, Dariusz Dereniowski

Abstract
This work considers a number of optimization problems and reductive relations between them. The two main problems we are interested in are the Optimal Decision Tree and Set Cover . We study these two fundamental tasks under precedence constraints, that is, if a test (or set) X X is a predecessor of Y Y , then in any feasible decision tree X X needs to be an ancestor of Y Y (or respectively, if Y Y is added to set cover, then so must be X X ). For the Optimal Decision Tree we consider two optimization criteria: worst case identification time (height of the tree) or the average identification time. Similarly, for the Set Cover we study two cost measures: the size of the cover or the average cover time. Our approach is to develop a number of algorithmic reductions, where an approximation algorithm for one problem provides an approximation for another via a black-box usage of a procedure for the former. En route we introduce other optimization problems either to complete the ‘reduction landscape’ or because they hold the essence of combinatorial structure of our problems. The latter is brought by a problem of finding a maximum density precedence closed subfamily, where the density is defined as the ratio of the number of items the family covers to its size. By doing so we provide 𝒪 ∗ ​ ( m ) \mathcal{O}^{*}(\sqrt{m}) -approximation algorithms for all of the aforementioned problems. The picture is complemented by a number of hardness reductions that provide o ⁡ ( m 1 / 12 − ϵ ) o(m^{1/12-\epsilon}) -inapproximability results for the decision tree and covering problems. Besides giving a complete set of results for general precedence constraints, we also provide polylogarithmic approximation guarantees for two most typically studied and applicable precedence types, outforests and inforests. By providing corresponding hardness results, we show these results to be tight.

Consider a set
\mathcal{H}
of

\mathcal{T}

Each test

\mathcal{H}

As a result of executing a test

Without formally stating an optimization criterion we refer to the above as the
Optimal Decision Tree
(
DT
) problem.
(For formal statements of our problems see Section
2

In this work we generalize this process by considering precedence relation between tests given as an arbitrary partial order
(\mathcal{T},\preceq)

A test

can be performed only if all its predecessors have been performed previously (to which we refer as
precedence-closed
solution).
On the applicability side, the problem includes numerous use cases, some of them in the area of biology
3
.
For example hypotheses may represent possible conditions or diseases and tests may represent medical procedures. In such case it may happen that some tests are less invasive or expensive than others, and should be performed firstly. Additionally, it can also happen that a given test is a prerequisite for another test, e.g., a blood test may be required before performing a biopsy. At last it might also be the case that a large test consists of multiple stages, each with its own outcomes, and the later stages can be performed only after the earlier ones.

The aforementioned precedence constraints make the decision tree problem significantly more challenging. In a classical setup (without such constraints) the usual way of proceeding is to select at each step a test that maximizes some measure of information gain (e.g., reduction in entropy or number of hypotheses pairs). However, when precedence constraints are present, it may happen that the most informative test cannot be chosen at the current step, as some of its predecessors have not been performed yet.
To address this issue we use a series of ‘reductions’ between other optimization problems i. e., using a particular algorithm for one problem as a subroutine for the other.
Particularly, we study set covering with precedence:
We are given a set
\mathcal{U}
of
n
items, a collection
\mathcal{S}
of
m
subsets of
\mathcal{U}
, such that
\bigcup_{S\in\mathcal{S}}S=\mathcal{U}
, a partial order
(\mathcal{S},\preceq)
on these subsets and a parameter
0<f\leq 1
.
We say that a subfamily
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{S}

covers
at least
f
-fraction of items from
\mathcal{U}
if
\mathopen{}\left|\bigcup_{C\in\mathcal{C}}C\right|\geq f\cdot n
.
We ask for a
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
that covers at least
f\cdot n
items from
\mathcal{U}
and is
precedence-closed
, that is, for each
x\in\mathcal{C}
and each
y\in\mathcal{S}
such that
y\preceq x
it holds
y\in\mathcal{C}
. We measure the quality of such
\mathcal{C}
by either its size
\mathopen{}\left|\mathcal{C}\right|
or the average time it takes to cover an item from
\mathcal{U}
(assuming some order of sets in
\mathcal{C}
closed under the precedence constraint).
We show how to reduce the optimal decision tree to the above set covering. Then we develop approximation algorithms for the set covering problems themselves. The approximation algorithms that we propose en route, e.g. for precedence constrained set cover, are of independent interest.

Preliminaries and problem definitions

Partial orders and precedence constraints.

Throughout this paper, we work with POSETs
(X,\preceq)
, where
\preceq
is a reflexive, transitive, and antisymmetric binary relation.
For elements
x,y\in X
, we say that
y
is a
predecessor
of
x
if
y\preceq x
, and that
x
is a
successor
of
y
.
A subset
S\subseteq X
is
precedence-closed
if for all
x\in S
and all
y\in X
such that
y\preceq x
, it holds that
y\in S
.
A sequence
(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{k})
of elements from
X
is
precedence-closed
if for any
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
such that
x_{i}\preceq x_{j}
, we have
i<j
. We consider several special types of partial orders: An
inforest
is a partial order in which every element has at most one successor. An
outforest
is a partial order in which every element has at most one predecessor.

Decision trees.

In decision tree problems, the goal is to design a
strategy

\mathcal{S}
understood as an adaptive algorithm, that based on the previous replies, gives questioner the next test to be performed.
This strategy is typically represented as a
decision tree
, a rooted tree
D
in which internal nodes represent tests to be performed, edges represent replies and each leaf represents a hypothesis.
Formally, a decision tree is defined recursively as follows.
If
r=\mathopen{}\left\{H_{1},\ldots,H_{l}\right\}\in\mathcal{T}
is the first test performed by
\mathcal{S}
, then
r
is the root of
D
.
For each reply
H_{i}
,
i\in\mathopen{}\left\{1,\ldots,l\right\}
, take inductively defined decision tree
D_{i}
derived from the tests done by
\mathcal{S}
when the reply to
r
is
H_{i}
.
Then,
D
is obtained by making the roots of
D_{1},\ldots,D_{l}
the children of
r
, and the edge connecting
r
to the root of
D_{i}
is labeled with the reply
H_{i}
.
To complete the inductive definition, if
\mathcal{S}
is a trivial strategy that outputs the target, then the corresponding tree
D
is a leaf labeled with the target.
It should be remarked that it is possible for a test to appear multiple times in the decision tree.
However, with a slight abuse of notation we use tests as nodes of
D
whenever there is no ambiguity. When the tests are subject to a precedence relation
(\mathcal{T},\preceq)
, a decision tree
D
is
precedence-closed
if for every node
v
in
D
, the set of tests on the path from the root to
v
forms a precedence-closed sequence so that a test
t
is executed only if all its predecessors in
(\mathcal{T},\preceq)
have already been performed

Cost measures for decision trees.

Let
\operatorname{tests}(D,v)
denote the sequence of tests on the path from the root to
v
in
D
including
v
. The cost of identifying a hypothesis
h
using a decision tree

is
\texttt{COST}\mathopen{}\left(D,h\right)=\mathopen{}\left|\operatorname{tests}(D,v)\right|
, where
v
is the leaf of
D
that represents identifying

as the target.
We consider two cost measures for decision trees: the worst-case cost
\texttt{COST}_{W}\mathopen{}\left(D\right)=\max_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\texttt{COST}\mathopen{}\left(D,h\right)
and the average-case cost
\texttt{COST}_{A}\mathopen{}\left(D\right)=\sum_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\cdot\texttt{COST}\mathopen{}\left(D,h\right)
.

Throughout the paper we use

Let

for a family of sets

For a family of sets

For a sequence

A permutation

Theorem 9

If there is a polynomial-time bicriteria

Proof.

Consider a call to

(2)

4
Max-Density Precedence-Closed Subfamily

In order to solve
f-PCSC
and
f-PCMSSC
we firstly solve two essential problems:
MDPCS
and
b-MDPCS
which serve as a way to find precedence-closed subfamilies of sets which cover many elements with respect to their size. We will then use such subroutines as an oracle in order to construct greedy, set-cover like approximation algorithms for our problems.
By
44
, the following solution to
MDPCS
on instance
\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{G},\preceq\right)
provides a
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\left(\sqrt{m}\right)
-approximation: pick
\mathcal{A}
to be the best solution among

\mathopen{}\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\operatorname{closure}[S],S\in\mathcal{G}}\mathopen{}\left\{\Delta\mathopen{}\left(\operatorname{closure}[S]\right)\right\}
and
\mathcal{S}
.
We will refer to such
\mathcal{A}
as a
greedy
solution.

When
\max_{S\in\mathcal{G}}\Delta\mathopen{}\left(\operatorname{closure}[S]\right)\geq 1
, then the approximation factor of the greedy can also be bounded by
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\left(\sqrt{n}\right)
.
Additionally, we show that a slightly modified greedy rule achieves an
b
-approximation for the parametrized version of the problem,
b-MDPCS
.
An
\mathcal{A}
that satisfies

\mathcal{A}=\mathopen{}\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\operatorname{closure}[S],S\in\mathcal{G},\mathopen{}\left|\operatorname{closure}[S]\right|\leq b}\mathopen{}\left\{\Delta\mathopen{}\left(\operatorname{closure}[S]\right)\right\}

will be called a
b
-greedy
solution to
b-MDPCS
.

Theorem 11
.

Let
\mathcal{A}^{*}
be an optimal solution to
b-MDPCS
and let
\mathcal{A}
be
b
-greedy.
Then,
\Delta\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{A}\right)\geq\frac{\Delta\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{A}^{*}\right)}{b}
.

Proof.

We utilize a partial argument of
44
for
MDPCS
. Let
k=\mathopen{}\left|\mathcal{A}^{*}\right|\leq b
.
For each
S\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
, by the greedy rule we trivially have that
\Delta\mathopen{}\left(\operatorname{closure}[S]\right)\leq\Delta\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{A}\right)
.
Therefore:

\mathopen{}\left|\operatorname{cov}\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{A}^{*}\right)\right|\leq\sum_{S\in\mathcal{A}^{*}}\mathopen{}\left|\operatorname{cov}\mathopen{}\left(\operatorname{closure}[S]\right)\right|\leq\Delta\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{A}\right)\sum_{S\in\mathcal{A}^{*}}\mathopen{}\left|\operatorname{closure}[S]\right|\leq\Delta\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{A}\right)\cdot k^{2},

where the first inequality is by the definition of union. We have that:

\frac{\Delta\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{A}^{*}\right)}{\Delta\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{A}\right)}=\frac{\mathopen{}\left|\operatorname{cov}\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{A}^{*}\right)\right|}{k\cdot\Delta\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{A}\right)}\leq k\leq b,

which by rearranging gives the desired inequality.
∎

The above theorem gives a
b
-approximation algorithm for the
b-MDPCS
. Note that for large values of
b
this might be much worse than the
\sqrt{m}
-approximation provided for the non-parametrized version of the problem. In the original version of the above argument it could be shown that if one additionally considers a candidate solution which is the whole
\mathcal{S}
, then the approximation of greedy is
\min\mathopen{}\left\{k,m/k\right\}
. However, since we enforce additional condition on the size of the dense subfamily this might not be a feasible solution. Note that however, for our needs, the
b
-approximation will be sufficient. We leave as an open question whether one can obtain a
o\mathopen{}\left(m\right)
approximation for
b-MDPCS
in case when
k=\Theta(m)
.

Algorithm 2

The

Lemma 12
.

Let

be the cover returned by Algorithm

\mathopen{}\left|\mathcal{C}\right|\leq\mathopen{}\left(\sqrt{m\cdot H_{n}}+1\right)\cdot b

Else, when

Lemma 13
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be the cover returned by Algorithm
2
. Then,
\mathopen{}\left|\operatorname{cov}\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{C}\right)\right|\geq\mathopen{}\left|\operatorname{cov}\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\textup{opt}}\right)\right|

Proof.

If

, then the algorithm returns
\mathcal{C}_{l}=\mathcal{S}
and trivially covers at least as many elements as
\mathcal{C}_{\textup{opt}}
.

Otherwise, we assume that the the while loop is being executed until the number of covered elements is at least
\mathopen{}\left|\operatorname{cov}\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\textup{opt}}\right)\right|

\mathopen{}\left(\sqrt{m\cdot H_{n}}+1\right)\cdot b
. By doing so, we show that when the cost of the cover

exceeds
\sqrt{m\cdot H_{n}}\cdot b

\mathopen{}\left|\operatorname{cov}\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\textup{opt}}\right)\right|
.

Let
R_{i}=\mathcal{U}\setminus\operatorname{cov}\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{C}_{i}\right)
be the set of uncovered elements after the
i
-th iteration; take

.
Note that
\mathopen{}\left|\mathcal{C}_{i}\right|=\sum_{j=1}^{i}\mathopen{}\left|\mathcal{A}_{j}\right|
. For any covered element
u\in\mathcal{U}
, let

be the first iteration in which
u
is covered, i.e.,

. We set the

of

to be

.
Let
t
be the index of the first iteration of the while loop when
\mathopen{}\left|\operatorname{cov}\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{C}_{t}\right)\right|\geq\mathopen{}\left|\operatorname{cov}\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\textup{opt}}\right)\right|
.
(Note that such

exists since we are analyzing Algorithm

under the assumption that the loop works indefinitely, i.e., until all items are covered).
Let
k=\mathopen{}\left|\operatorname{cov}\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{C}_{t-1}\right)\right|\leq\mathopen{}\left|\operatorname{cov}\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\textup{opt}}\right)\right|
.
Order the elements of
\operatorname{cov}\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\textup{opt}}\right)
as

in the order in which they are covered by the algorithm with ties broken arbitrarily (i.e., if

, then

).

Claim 1
.

For each
j\in\mathopen{}\left\{1,\ldots,k\right\}
,
c(u_{j})\leq b^{2}/(k-j+1)
.

Proof.

Consider the iteration
i(u_{j})
in which
u_{j}
is covered. Since
\mathcal{C}_{\textup{opt}}
is a precedence-closed family, we know that during iteration
i(u_{j})
, there exists a precedence-closed family
\mathcal{C}_{\textup{opt}}
that covers at least
k-j+1
elements of
R_{i(u_{j})-1}
of size at most
\mathopen{}\left|\mathcal{C}_{\textup{opt}}\right|\leq b
. Since the algorithm selects a
b
-approximation to

during iteration

, we have that:

\Delta\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{A}_{i(u_{j})},R_{i(u_{j})-1}\right)\geq\frac{\Delta\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\textup{opt}},R_{i(u_{j})-1}\right)}{b}\geq\frac{k-j+1}{\mathopen{}\left|\mathcal{C}_{\textup{opt}}\right|\cdot b}\geq\frac{k-j+1}{b^{2}},

where the first inequality is by the approximation guarantee of the algorithm and the greedy choice, the second inequality is by definition of density, and the last inequality is by the budget constraint on
\mathcal{C}_{\textup{opt}}
. Rearranging the above inequality yields:

c(u_{j})=\frac{\mathopen{}\left|\mathcal{A}_{i(u_{j})}\right|}{\mathopen{}\left|\operatorname{cov}\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{A}_{i(u_{j})},R_{i(u_{j})-1}\right)\right|}=\frac{1}{\Delta\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{A}_{i(u_{j})},R_{i(u_{j})-1}\right)}\leq\frac{b^{2}}{k-j+1}

∎

Using Claim
1
, the sum of prices of all elements in
\mathcal{C}_{t-1}
is bounded by

.
By the price definition,

.
Since
\mathopen{}\left|\mathcal{A}_{t}\right|\leq b
,
\mathopen{}\left|\mathcal{C}_{t}\right|\leq\mathopen{}\left|\mathcal{C}_{t-1}\right|+\mathopen{}\left|\mathcal{A}_{t}\right|\leq b^{2}H_{n}+b

Recall that we are considering the case when

\mathopen{}\left|\mathcal{C}_{t}\right|\leq\mathopen{}\left(\sqrt{mH_{n}}+1\right)b
.
This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

\mathopen{}\left|\operatorname{cov}\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\textup{opt}},R_{l-1}\right)\right|\geq k/2
.
Hence by (
3

\mathopen{}\left|\mathcal{A}_{l}\right|\leq\frac{2\gamma n}{k}\mathopen{}\left|\mathcal{C}_{\textup{opt}}\right|
.
This gives
\mathopen{}\left|\mathcal{C}_{l}\right|=\sum_{i=1}^{l}\mathopen{}\left|\mathcal{A}_{i}\right|\leq\frac{4\gamma n}{k}\cdot\mathopen{}\left|\mathcal{C}_{\textup{opt}}\right|=\frac{4\gamma}{f}\cdot\mathopen{}\left|\mathcal{C}_{\textup{opt}}\right|
.
Moreover,
\mathopen{}\left|\operatorname{cov}\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{C}_{l}\right)\right|\geq k/2
, which means the algorithm covers at least half of the required elements.
This proves the theorem.

5.4
Special cases of precedence constraints

Theorem 21
.

There exist polynomial-time bicriteria
\mathopen{}\left(1,\frac{e}{e-1}\right)
-approximation algorithms for
b-PCSC
and
f-PCSC
when the precedence constraints form an inforest.

Proof.

The proof is by showing that this problem is reducible to a standard budgeted maximum coverage problem (with no precedence constraints) with non-uniform cost of sets. In this setup including a set
S
into the cover may impose cost
c\mathopen{}\left(S\right)
. For this a
\mathopen{}\left(1,\frac{e}{e-1}\right)
-approximation algorithm is known
39
. Observer that for precedence constraints of the inforest type, for any two sets
S,W
if
\operatorname{closure}[S]\cap\operatorname{closure}[W]\neq\emptyset
, then one is a descendant of the other in the inforest, i.e., either
S\preceq W
or
W\preceq S
. Therefore we can create an equivalent not precedented instance in the following way: for each set
S
in the precedence-constrained instance, we create a new set
S^{\prime}
in the new instance, such that
S^{\prime}=\bigcup_{W\in\operatorname{closure}[S]}W
and
c\mathopen{}\left(S\right)=\mathopen{}\left|S^{\prime}\right|
. It is easy to see that any feasible solution in the new instance corresponds to a feasible solution in the original instance with the same cost and coverage, and vice versa. Thus, by Corollary
16
the theorem follows.
∎

This implies the following result for
f-PCMSSC
via carefull examination of Theorem
20
.

Corollary 22
.

There exist polynomial-time bicriteria
\mathopen{}\left(864\cdot\mathopen{}\left(\frac{e}{e-1}\right)^{3}+1,\frac{e}{e-1}\right)\approx\mathopen{}\left(3421.7,\frac{e}{e-1}\right)
-approximation algorithms for
f-PCMSSC
when the precedence constraints form an inforest.

Theorem 23
.

There exist polynomial-time bicriteria
\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\left(\log n\right),4\right)
-approximation algorithms for
b-PCSC
,
f-PCSC
and
f-PCMSSC
when the precedence constraints form an outforest.

Proof.

The proof is by showing that this problem is reducible to
GSO
on tree metrics.
The latter admits a
\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\left(\log n\right),4\right)
-approximation algorithm
32
.
It should be noted that they give a worse approximation ratio of
\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\left(\log^{2}n\right),4\right)
, however their algorithm consists of embedding a general metric into a tree metric with
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\left(\log n\right)
distortion.
Since our metrics is already a tree metric, we can skip this step and obtain the improved approximation ratio.

We create the metric as follows: we introduce a root node
r
and for each set
S\in\mathcal{S}
we add a representative node
v_{S}
connected to its every successor in the outforest with an edge of length
1
.
Additionally, we connect each maximal element of the outforest to
r
with an edge of length
1
. Finally, for each element
u\in\mathcal{U}
we create a group
X_{u}=\mathopen{}\left\{v_{S}\mid u\in S\right\}
which consists of all representatives of vertices containing
u
. Since the resulting metric is a tree metric, any tour returned by the algorithm traverses each edge either
0
or
2
times.
Moreover, since the tour starts at the root, we have that if a representative of a set
W
is visited, the representatives of all of its predecessors in the outforest have been visited.
Therefore, any feasible solution to
GSO
on this metric corresponds to a feasible solution to
b-PCSC
on the original instance with the same cost and coverage, and vice versa. Thus, by Corollary
16
and Theorem
20
the theorem follows.
∎

Theorem 24
.

Suppose there exists a constant
C>0
and
\epsilon\in(0,1)
such that for all
S\in\mathcal{G}
,

\mathopen{}\left|\operatorname{closure}[S]\right|\leq C\cdot\mathopen{}\left|\operatorname{cov}\mathopen{}\left(\operatorname{closure}[S]\right)\right|^{\epsilon}
(
\epsilon
-shallow ancestry).
Then a greedy solution provides an
\mathcal{O}(n^{\epsilon})
-approximation for the
MDPCS
problem.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{A}^{*}
be an optimal solution and let
\mathcal{A}
be a greedy solution (cf. Section
4
).
Denote for brevity
\delta=\max_{S\in\mathcal{G}}\Delta\mathopen{}\left(\operatorname{closure}[S]\right)
.
There are two cases:

1.

If
\delta\geq n^{1-\epsilon}
, then, we observe that
\Delta\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{A}\right)=\delta\geq n^{1-\epsilon}
. Since we can cover at most
n
elements with at least one set, we have
\Delta\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{A}^{*}\right)\leq n
. Therefore:

\frac{\Delta\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{A}^{*}\right)}{\Delta\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{A}\right)}\leq\frac{n}{n^{1-\epsilon}}=n^{\epsilon}

2.

Else, if
\delta\leq n^{1-\epsilon}
, we proceed as follows: By definition of density, for any
S\in\mathcal{G}
it holds
\mathopen{}\left|\operatorname{cov}\mathopen{}\left(\operatorname{closure}[S]\right)\right|\leq\delta\cdot\mathopen{}\left|\operatorname{closure}[S]\right|
. Combining this with the
\epsilon
-shallow ancestry condition, we have that for all
S\in\mathcal{G}
,
\mathopen{}\left|\operatorname{cov}\mathopen{}\left(\operatorname{closure}[S]\right)\right|\leq\delta\cdot C\cdot\mathopen{}\left|\operatorname{cov}\mathopen{}\left(\operatorname{closure}[S]\right)\right|^{\epsilon}
. Rearranging this inequality, we get that
\mathopen{}\left|\operatorname{cov}\mathopen{}\left(\operatorname{closure}[S]\right)\right|\leq(\delta\cdot C)^{\frac{1}{1-\epsilon}}
. We have that:

\mathopen{}\left|\operatorname{cov}\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{A}^{*}\right)\right|=\mathopen{}\left|\bigcup_{S\in\mathcal{A}^{*}}\operatorname{cov}\mathopen{}\left(\operatorname{closure}[S]\right)\right|\leq\sum_{S\in\mathcal{A}^{*}}\mathopen{}\left|\operatorname{cov}\mathopen{}\left(\operatorname{closure}[S]\right)\right|\leq\mathopen{}\left|\mathcal{A}^{*}\right|\cdot(\delta\cdot C)^{\frac{1}{1-\epsilon}}.

Therefore,

\Delta\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{A}^{*}\right)=\frac{\mathopen{}\left|\operatorname{cov}\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{A}^{*}\right)\right|}{\mathopen{}\left|\mathcal{A}^{*}\right|}\leq(\delta\cdot C)^{\frac{1}{1-\epsilon}}.

By the greedy choice,
\Delta\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{A}\right)\geq\delta
and by assumption
\delta\leq n^{1-\epsilon}
. Thus:

\frac{\Delta\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{A}^{*}\right)}{\Delta\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{A}\right)}\leq\frac{(\delta\cdot C)^{\frac{1}{1-\epsilon}}}{\delta}=C^{\frac{1}{1-\epsilon}}\cdot\delta^{\frac{\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}}\leq C^{\frac{1}{1-\epsilon}}\cdot(n^{1-\epsilon})^{\frac{\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}}=C^{\frac{1}{1-\epsilon}}\cdot n^{\epsilon}.

∎

t_{S}
that gives a reply
\mathopen{}\left\{h_{u}^{i}\right\}
if
u\in S
and all other hypotheses belong to a reply
H_{t_{S}}
when
u\notin S
. The precedence constraints on tests are the same as the precedence constraints on sets, i.e., if
S_{1}
must be selected before
S_{2}
in
f-PCSC
, then
t_{S_{1}}
must be selected before
t_{S_{2}}
in
PCWCDT
. Notice, that any valid decision tree for the constructed instance of
PCWCDT
is a path of tests (ignoring leaves) since performing any test
t_{S}
either returns a response containing one of the hypotheses
h_{u}^{i}
, thus ending a process or returns
H_{t_{S}}
, which is a singular possible response leading to the next test. Note that even when the candidate set contains only representatives of one element
u
, we still need to perform a test to distinguish between
h_{u}^{0}
and
h_{u}^{1}
, which could not be a case if there was only one hypothesis per element.
Therefore, any valid decision tree for the constructed instance of
PCWCDT
corresponds to a valid selection of sets in
f-PCSC
and vice versa. Moreover, the cost of the decision tree is equal to the number of selected sets. Thus, since the size of the reduction is linear any
\alpha
-approximation for
PCWCDT
would yield an
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\left(\alpha\right)
-approximation for
f-PCSC
.
∎

Theorem 26
.

Approximating
PCACDT
is as hard as approximating
f-PCMSSC
.

Proof.

We use the same reduction as in the previous theorem. Note that since we doubled the number of hypotheses in the construction, the cost of any decision tree in the constructed instance for
PCACDT
has twice the number of selected sets in
f-PCMSSC
. However, this does not affect the structure of the reduction and thus any
\alpha
-approximation for
PCACDT
would yield an
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\left(\alpha\right)
-approximation for
f-PCMSSC
.
∎

6.3
General precedence constraints

In this section we show strong inapproximability results for
PCWCDT
and
PCACDT
with general precedence constraints by reducing from the Planted Dense Subgraph Conjecture which is a widely believed statement about hardness of detecting a dense component within an Erdős-Renyi graph
9
;
44
.

Conjecture 6.31

(Planted Dense Subgraph (PDS) Conjecture)
.

For any constants
\beta<\alpha
and any
k\geq\sqrt{N}
, there is no polynomial time algorithm that can distinguish between the following two distributions of graphs with any advantage
\epsilon>0
:

•

With probability 1/2, an Erdős-Renyi graph
G(N,N^{\alpha-1})
.

•

With probability 1/2, an Erdős-Renyi graph
G(N,N^{\alpha-1})
with a planted subgraph of size
k
and edge density
k^{\beta-1}
.

Using this conjecture
44
have showed the following inapproximability results for
f-PCMSSC
:

Theorem 6.32
.

For any
\epsilon>0
,
f-PCMSSC
cannot be approximated within a factor of
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\left(m^{1/6-\epsilon}\right)
nor
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\left(n^{1/12-\epsilon}\right)
condition to Planted Dense Subgraph Conjecture.

By Theorem
26
we immediately obtain the following results:

Corollary 6.33
.

For any
\epsilon>0
,
PCACDT
cannot be approximated within a factor of
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\left(m^{1/6-\epsilon}\right)
nor
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\left(n^{1/12-\epsilon}\right)
condition to Planted Dense Subgraph Conjecture.

Below we show that a similar reduction can also be used to obtain the same inapproximability result for
f-PCSC
. The construction and analysis are almost the same as the one in
44
but we include it here for completeness. In the reduction we will often make use of the Chernoff Bound which is as follows:

Theorem 6.34

(Chernoff Bound)
.

Let
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{n}
be independent random variables taking values in
\mathopen{}\left\{0,1\right\}
such that for every
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
,
\mathbb{P}[X_{i}=1]=p
. Let
X=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
and
\mu=\mathbb{E}[X]=pn
. Then, for every
\delta\in(0,1)
it holds that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[X\leq(1-\delta)\mu]

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\frac{\delta^{2}\mu}{2}},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[X\geq(1+\delta)\mu]

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\frac{\delta^{2}\mu}{3}}.

We have the following theorem:

Theorem 6.35
.

For any
\epsilon>0
,
f-PCSC
cannot be approximated within a factor of
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\left(m^{1/6-\epsilon}\right)
nor
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\left(n^{1/12-\epsilon}\right)
condition to Planted Dense Subgraph Conjecture.

Proof 6.36
.

Construction of the reduction.

We start by choosing appropriate parameters for our reduction. Let
k=\sqrt{N}
,
\alpha=1/2
and
\beta=1/2-\gamma
for some
\gamma>0
to be determined later. In our reduction we will embed the structure of the input graph into the precedence constraints, while the universum elements will enforce the covering requirement.

Given a graph
G=(\{1,\ldots,N\},\mathcal{E})
as input to the Planted Dense Subgraph problem, we construct an instance of
f-PCSC
as follows. For each vertex
v\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
of
G
, we create
\lambda
representative sets
V_{v,i}
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,\lambda\}
, where
\lambda
is an appropriately chosen natural number to be specified later. For each edge
uv\in\mathcal{E}
, we create one representative set
E_{uv}
which must be preceded by all representatives of both
u
and
v
. That is, for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,\lambda\}
, we have
V_{u,i}\preceq E_{uv}
and
V_{v,i}\preceq E_{uv}
.

Next, we define the universum of elements to be covered. Let
U=\{0,1,\dots,n\}
for some appropriately chosen natural number
n
to be determined later. Every vertex representative set contains only the special element
0
, that is,
V_{v,i}=\{0\}
for all
v\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
and
i\in\{1,\ldots,\lambda\}
.

To define the contents of edge representative sets, we use a randomized construction based on a parameter
p\in(0,1)
. For each vertex
v\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
, we define an auxiliary set
U_{v}\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}
by including each element
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
in
U_{v}
independently with probability
p
. That is, for each
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and each
v\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
, we have
\mathbb{P}[j\in\mathcal{U}_{v}]=p
. The edge representative set
E_{uv}
is then defined to cover the intersection of the auxiliary sets, that is,
E_{uv}=(U_{u}\cap U_{v})
.

From this construction, we can compute the following expected values:

•

\mathbb{E}[\mathopen{}\left|U_{v}\right|]=pn
for each vertex
v
,

•

\mathbb{E}[\mathopen{}\left|\{v\colon j\in\mathcal{U}_{v}\}\right|]=pN
for each element
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,

•

\mathbb{E}[\mathopen{}\left|E_{uv}\right|]=\mathbb{E}[\mathopen{}\left|U_{u}\cap U_{v}\right|]=np^{2}
for each edge
uv
.

The intuition behind this construction is as follows: we choose
p
to be the smallest value such that if a planted component exists, then with high probability it covers all of
U
using only edge representatives from the planted component (and their required predecessors). On the other hand, if no planted component exists, then we show that any solution must use significantly more sets to cover
U
, thus establishing the desired inapproximability ratio.

Choice of parameters.

Let
\mathcal{P}
denote the planted component if it exists. Since
\mathcal{P}
consists of
k=\sqrt{N}
vertices, for any element
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, the expected number of vertices in
\mathcal{P}
whose auxiliary sets contain
j
is
\mathbb{E}[\mathopen{}\left|\{v\colon j\in\mathcal{U}_{v},v\in V(\mathcal{P})\}\right|]=p\sqrt{N}
. Therefore, the expected number of unordered pairs of vertices in
\mathcal{P}
where both auxiliary sets contain
j
is:

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\left\{(v,u)\colon j\in\mathcal{U}_{v},j\in\mathcal{U}_{u},v,u\in V(\mathcal{P}),v<u\right\}\right|\right]=\binom{p\sqrt{N}}{2}.

Since each edge in
\mathcal{P}
exists independently with probability
k^{\beta-1}=(\sqrt{N})^{-1/2-\gamma}=N^{-1/4-\gamma/2}
, the expected number of edge representative sets from
\mathcal{P}
that cover element
j
is:

\mathbb{E}[\mathopen{}\left|\{E_{uv}\colon j\in E_{uv},uv\in E(\mathcal{P})\}\right|]=\binom{p\sqrt{N}}{2}\cdot N^{-1/4-\gamma/2}\geq\frac{p^{2}\cdot N}{4}\cdot N^{-1/4-\gamma/2}=\frac{p^{2}\cdot N^{3/4-\gamma/2}}{4}.

For this expected value to be sufficiently large (specifically, to be
\Theta(\log^{2}N)
), we choose:

p=32\cdot N^{-3/8+\gamma/4}\cdot\log N
.
We have
\mathbb{E}[\mathopen{}\left|\{E_{uv}\colon j\in E_{uv},uv\in E(\mathcal{P})\}\right|]\geq 64\cdot\log^{2}N
, which will allow us to apply concentration bounds effectively.

Case 1: Planted component exists.

Assume that the input graph contains a planted component
\mathcal{P}
. We show that there exists a solution to
f-PCSC
using only sets corresponding to vertices and edges in
\mathcal{P}
that covers all elements in
U
with high probability, and that this solution has cost
O(\lambda\cdot\sqrt{N}+N^{3/4-\gamma/2})
.
Consider the solution that selects all vertex representatives
V_{v,i}
for all
v\in V(\mathcal{P})
and all
i\in\{1,\ldots,\lambda\}
, together with all edge representatives
E_{uv}
for all edges
uv\in E(\mathcal{P})
. The special element
0
is covered by any vertex representative, so we focus on covering elements in
\{1,\dots,n\}
.

Fix an arbitrary element
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. We first bound the number of vertices in
\mathcal{P}
whose auxiliary sets contain
j
. Since
\mathbb{E}[\mathopen{}\left|\{v\colon j\in\mathcal{U}_{v},v\in V(\mathcal{P})\}\right|]=p\sqrt{N}
, by Chernoff’s bound (Theorem
6.34
) with
\delta=1/2
, we have:

\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\{v\colon j\in\mathcal{U}_{v},v\in V(\mathcal{P})\}\right|\leq\frac{p\sqrt{N}}{2}\right]\leq e^{-p\sqrt{N}/8}=e^{-4\cdot N^{1/8+\gamma/4}\cdot\log N}=N^{-4\cdot N^{1/8+\gamma/4}},

which is exponentially small.

Next, for large enough
x
, we have
\binom{x}{2}\geq x^{2}/4
. Therefore, the expected number of unordered pairs of vertices in
\mathcal{P}
where both auxiliary sets contain
j
is at least
p^{2}\cdot N/4
. Again by Chernoff’s bound, we have:

\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\{(u,v)\colon j\in\mathcal{U}_{u},j\in\mathcal{U}_{v},u,v\in V(\mathcal{P}),u<v\}\right|\leq\frac{p^{2}\cdot N}{16}\right]\leq N^{-4\cdot N^{1/8+\gamma/4}}.

Now, each such pair
(u,v)
corresponds to an edge in
\mathcal{P}
with probability
N^{-1/4-\gamma/2}
. Since edge existence is independent of the auxiliary set memberships, the expected number of edge representatives in
\mathcal{P}
that cover
j
is:

\mu:=\mathbb{E}[\mathopen{}\left|\{E_{uv}\colon j\in E_{uv},uv\in E(\mathcal{P})\}\right|]\geq\frac{p^{2}\cdot N}{16}\cdot N^{-1/4-\gamma/2}=64\cdot\log^{2}N.

Applying Chernoff’s bound once more, we obtain:

\mathbb{P}[\mathopen{}\left|\{E_{uv}\colon j\in E_{uv},uv\in E(\mathcal{P})\}\right|\leq\mu/2]\leq e^{-\mu/8}=e^{-8\log^{2}N}=N^{-8\log N}.

By the union bound over all
n
elements in
\{1,\dots,n\}
, the probability that some element is not covered by the edge representatives in
\mathcal{P}
is at most
n\cdot N^{-8\log N}
. We will choose
n=\Theta(N^{3/4})
later, which makes this probability negligible for large
N
.

Finally, we bound the number of sets in this solution. The number of vertex representative sets is exactly
\lambda\cdot\sqrt{N}
. For the edge representatives, observe that:

\mathbb{E}[\mathopen{}\left|E(\mathcal{P})\right|]=\binom{\sqrt{N}}{2}\cdot N^{-1/4-\gamma/2}\leq\frac{N}{2}\cdot N^{-1/4-\gamma/2}=\frac{N^{3/4-\gamma/2}}{2}.

By Chernoff’s bound with
\delta=1
, we have:

\mathbb{P}[\mathopen{}\left|E(\mathcal{P})\right|\geq N^{3/4-\gamma/2}]\leq e^{-N^{3/4-\gamma/2}/6},

which is extremely small. Therefore, with high probability, the total number of sets in the solution is at most:

\lambda\cdot\sqrt{N}+N^{3/4-\gamma/2}.

Case 2: No planted component.

Now assume that the input graph does not contain a planted component, so it is drawn entirely from
G(N,N^{-1/2})
. We show that any solution to
f-PCSC
must use significantly more sets to cover all elements in
U
with high probability.

Consider any solution to
f-PCSC
that selects representatives corresponding to some set
X\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N\}
of vertices with
|X|=x
. Due to the precedence constraints, this solution must include all
\lambda x
vertex representatives
V_{v,i}
for
v\in X
and
i\in\{1,\ldots,\lambda\}
. After selecting these vertex representatives, the solution can then select edge representatives
E_{uv}
for edges
uv
where both
u,v\in X
.

We first bound the number of edges among the vertices in
X
. Since the graph is drawn from
G(N,N^{-1/2})
, each potential edge exists independently with probability
N^{-1/2}
. For any fixed set
X
of size
x
, the expected number of edges with both endpoints in
X
is:

\mu_{E}:=\binom{x}{2}\cdot N^{-1/2}\leq\frac{x^{2}}{2\sqrt{N}}.

There are at most
\binom{N}{x}\leq N^{x}
possible sets
X
of size
x
. By Chernoff’s bound with
\delta=1/2
, the probability that a specific set
X
contains more than
3\mu_{E}/2
edges is at most
e^{-\mu_{E}/12}
. By the union bound over all possible sets of size
x
, the probability that some set of size
x
has more than
3\mu_{E}/2
edges is at most:

N^{x}\cdot e^{-\mu_{E}/12}=N^{x}\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{24\sqrt{N}}\right).

For this probability to be at most
N^{-8}
, we would need
\mu_{E}\geq 12(x+8)\log N
, which translates to
x\geq 500\sqrt{N}\log N
. However, we are interested in the regime where
x\leq N^{(5-2\gamma)/8}
. For such values of
x
and sufficiently large
N
(note that
N^{(5-2\gamma)/8}<N^{5/8}<\sqrt{N}
for large
N
), we can safely assume that with overwhelming probability, any set
X
of size
x\leq N^{(5-2\gamma)/8}
contains at most
x^{2}/\sqrt{N}
edges.

Next, we bound how many elements can be covered by these edge representatives. By construction, each edge representative
E_{uv}
covers the elements in
(U_{u}\cap U_{v})
. The size of
U_{u}\cap U_{v}
has expectation
\mathbb{E}[\mathopen{}\left|U_{u}\cap U_{v}\right|]=np^{2}
. By Chernoff’s bound, the probability that a single edge representative covers more than
2np^{2}
elements from
\{1,\dots,n\}
is at most
e^{-np^{2}/12}
.

The total number of edge representatives available after selecting vertices in
X
is at most
x^{2}/\sqrt{N}
. By the union bound, the probability that at least one of these edge representatives covers more than
2np^{2}
elements from
\{1,\dots,n\}
is at most:

\frac{x^{2}}{\sqrt{N}}\cdot e^{-np^{2}/12}.

Recall that
p^{2}=(32)^{2}\cdot N^{-3/4+\gamma/2}\cdot\log^{2}N=1024\cdot N^{-3/4+\gamma/2}\cdot\log^{2}N
. For the above probability to be negligible, we need
n\geq 200\cdot\log N/p^{2}
, which gives:

n\geq\frac{200\cdot\log N}{1024\cdot N^{-3/4+\gamma/2}\cdot\log^{2}N}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{N^{3/4-\gamma/2}}{\log N}\right).

This condition is satisfied by choosing
n=\Theta(N^{3/4})
.

Now, suppose
x\leq N^{(5-2\gamma)/8}
. Then, with high probability, after selecting
\lambda x
vertex representatives and at most
x^{2}/\sqrt{N}
edge representatives, the total number of elements from
\{1,\dots,n\}
that are covered is at most:

\displaystyle 2np^{2}\cdot\frac{x^{2}}{\sqrt{N}}

\displaystyle=2048\cdot n\cdot N^{-3/4+\gamma/2}\cdot\log^{2}N\cdot\frac{x^{2}}{\sqrt{N}}=2048\cdot n\cdot N^{-5/4+\gamma/2}\cdot\log^{2}N\cdot x^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2048\cdot n\cdot N^{-5/4+\gamma/2}\cdot\log^{2}N\cdot N^{(5-2\gamma)/4}=2048\cdot n\cdot\log^{2}N.

Since
n=\Theta(N^{3/4})
, we have
2048\cdot n\cdot\log^{2}N=o(n)
for sufficiently large
N
. This means that when
x\leq N^{(5-2\gamma)/8}
, at most
o(n)
elements from
\{1,\dots,n\}
are covered, leaving
\Omega(n)
elements uncovered.

To cover all elements in
U
, any solution must eventually select enough sets to cover these remaining
\Omega(n)
elements. However, due to the precedence constraints, to reach edge representatives that can cover many new elements, the solution must first select many vertex representatives. Specifically, to significantly increase coverage beyond what
x\leq N^{(5-2\gamma)/8}
vertices provide, the solution must select at least
\lambda\cdot N^{(5-2\gamma)/8}
vertex representatives plus at least
N^{(5-2\gamma)/4}
edge representatives. Therefore, with high probability, the cost of any solution that covers all elements in
U
is at least:

\lambda\cdot N^{(5-2\gamma)/8}+N^{(5-2\gamma)/4}.

Comparison and conclusion.

We now compare the costs in the two cases to establish the inapproximability ratio. By choosing
\lambda=N^{1/4}
(or any
\lambda\geq N^{1/4}
) and letting
\gamma
approach
0
we have that the ratio between the costs in Case 2 and Case 1 is at least:

\frac{\lambda\cdot N^{(5-2\gamma)/8}+N^{(5-2\gamma)/4}}{\lambda\cdot\sqrt{N}+N^{3/4-\gamma/2}}=\Omega\mathopen{}\left(\frac{N^{5/8}}{N^{1/2}}\right)=\Omega\mathopen{}\left(N^{1/8}\right).

Since we chose
n=\Theta(N^{3/4})
, we have that the gap is
\Theta(N^{1/8})=\Theta((N^{3/4})^{1/6})=\Theta(n^{1/6})
. By choosing
\gamma
sufficiently small, we can make this gap arbitrarily close to
n^{1/6}
.
Furthermore, the total number of sets
m
in the constructed instance is
m=\lambda\cdot N+|\mathcal{E}|
. For a graph drawn from
G(N,N^{-1/2})
, the number of edges is highly concentrated around
\binom{N}{2}\cdot N^{-1/2}=\Theta(N^{3/2})
. With
\lambda=N^{1/4}
, we have
m=\Theta(N^{3/2})
. Therefore,
n=\Theta(N^{3/4})=\Theta(m^{1/2})
, and the gap can also be expressed as:

\Theta(N^{1/8})=\Theta((N^{3/2})^{1/12})=\Theta(m^{1/12}).

Thus, any polynomial-time algorithm for
f-PCSC
with approximation factor better than
\mathcal{O}(n^{1/6-\epsilon})
or
\mathcal{O}(m^{1/12-\epsilon})
for any
\epsilon>0
could distinguish between the two cases of the Planted Dense Subgraph problem with significant advantage. This completes the proof.

Therefore we immediately have that:

Corollary 6.37
.

For any
\epsilon>0
,
PCWCDT
cannot be approximated within a factor of
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\left(m^{1/6-\epsilon}\right)
nor
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\left(n^{1/12-\epsilon}\right)
condition to Planted Dense Subgraph Conjecture.
