Exploring factors influencing medical specialty choice among undergraduates in The Gambia: a situated expectancy-value theory approach
Authors: Andrea Nicaise Bikay, Oke-Oghene P Akpoveso, Chijioke Joel Nweke, Emmanuel C Ekeoma, Priscilla B Tambe, Abubakar Kabiru
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe selection of a medical specialty is a critical juncture in the professional development of medical students, with implications for individual career satisfaction and for the distribution of the national health workforce. In low-resource settings such as The Gambia, aligning students’ specialty choices with healthcare needs is essential for addressing persistent shortages in key areas. Situated-Expectancy-Value Theory (SEVT) offers a robust framework to understand how expectations of success and the perceived value of different specialties influence decision-making. This study applies SEVT to explore contemporary factors shaping specialty choice among Gambian medical students.MethodA cross-sectional survey was conducted among clinical-year medical students at two medical schools in The Gambia: the University of The Gambia and the American International University. A structured questionnaire, developed using the Expectancy-Value Theory framework, assessed 19 motivational factors across expectancy, value, and cost domains. Data were analyzed using SPSS software. The Fisher–Freeman–Halton test was conducted to identify associations between demographic variables and specialty preferences.ResultsA total of 150 students participated in the study, with 58.7% female and 41.3% male participants. Aggregation of participants’ first to third specialty choices showed that surgery was most frequently selected (n = 94), followed by internal medicine (n = 78) and obstetrics and gynaecology (n = 51). Radiology, Ear, Nose, and Throat (ENT), and pathology had similarly low preferences, while Basic sciences and psychiatry recorded the lowest frequency as first-choice specialties. Based on the expectancy-value theory framework, significant motivating factors were intrinsic (P = 0.049) and attainment/perceived meaningfulness (P = 0.047). The Fisher–Freeman–Halton test also revealed a statistically significant association between gender and specialty choice(P = 0.012). The student’s academic level also significantly influenced specialty selection (p < 0.001). No significant association was found between socio-demographic characteristics such as parental income, education, age, nationality, or area of residence and specialty choice.ConclusionThis study identifies internal medicine, surgery, obstetrics, and gynaecology as the most preferred specialties, with gender and level of clinical exposure emerging as key factors influencing medical specialty choice. Our findings further reveal that specialty decisions are shaped by intrinsic motivation, such as intellectual challenges in the specialty, and desire to provide community service, and by attainment values, including role models in the selected specialty and perceived prestige of the specialty. The study provides insight into how students’ personal values, sociocultural expectations, and educational context in The Gambia shapes their career aspirations. It also highlights gendered patterns in specialty preferences. We propose that aligning students’ interests through early exposure and culturally responsive teaching beyond traditional career counselling could better support informed specialty choices and contribute to workforce planning. Implications for policy implementation and workforce planning are discussed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09163-1.

Introduction

In The Gambia, concerns about the future medical workforce are no longer abstract projections but an immediate policy problem. As a lower-middle-income country with a high burden of infectious and non-communicable diseases, The Gambia faces chronic underfunding, limited training capacity, specialist outmigration, and challenging living conditions for health workers [1–3]. Recent workforce estimates indicate that in 2021, the country had only 79 specialist doctors, with 89% concentrated in the Greater Banjul area, leaving rural regions critically underserved [4]. According to the 2022–2026 Human Resources for Health Strategic Plan, there are only 1 of 7 required psychiatrists, 4 of 10 anaesthesiologists, 1 of 14 ophthalmologists, and no general surgeons, whereas 10 of 11 obstetrician-gynaecologist posts are filled, underscoring an imbalance across specialties [4]. These structural constraints are compounded by an emerging pattern of medical specialty preferences that appears poorly aligned with national health priorities. For instance, Bittaye et al. reported that Gambian medical students predominantly favour Surgery, Obstetrics and Gynaecology, Internal Medicine, and Paediatrics, whereas Anaesthesia and the Basic Medical Sciences received little interest [3]. Such skewed patterns in specialty choice risk perpetuating persistent gaps in critical services and undermining efforts to sustain capacity development within the health system. These local challenges sit within a wider global crisis in health workforce distribution. The World Health Organization projects a shortfall of approximately 11 million health workers by 2030 [1]. This deficit is not only numerical but also spatial and disciplinary, with stark imbalances between urban and rural areas and between highly sought-after and neglected specialties. For instance, in high-income countries such as Japan and the USA, clinical and surgical specialists cluster in urban areas [5], prompting proposals to increase the proportion of family physicians to improve rural access and equity [6].

In middle-income countries such as Brazil, physician density ranges from 0.52 per 1,000 population in economically disadvantaged northern regions to over 2.0 per 1,000 in wealthier south-eastern states [7], and despite implementing new programmes, inequalities persist [7]. The situation is even more concerning in lower-middle-income countries, where physician densities are frequently below 10.8 per 10,000 population, with rates in sub-Saharan Africa as low as 2.9 (2.1–4.0) per 10,000 [5]. These shortages and maldistribution are exacerbated by several systemic factors, including the misalignment of education and employment strategies with population health needs, inadequate incentives for practice in underserved areas, and ongoing health worker migration (“brain drain”) [1]. Consequently, these workforce gaps directly contribute to increased mortality from conditions such as neglected tropical diseases and malaria, enteric infections, maternal and neonatal disorders, and diabetes and kidney diseases [2].

Recognising these challenges, governments and global agencies have introduced a range of policies to improve the effective distribution of the health workforce. Here, high-income and low-and middle-income countries implement strategies such as rural recruitment and training tracks [8], and community-oriented medical schools [9, 10]; these strategies aim to channel graduates towards underserved areas. For example, Thailand and Nepal have tied government scholarships to compulsory rural service [8, 11], while Cuba and South Africa have community-oriented medical schools that train doctors for underserved areas [9].

Similarly, the Gambian government is strengthening medical education by establishing new postgraduate programmes in collaboration with international partners and promoting community-oriented training [3, 12]. However, many of these strategies implicitly assume that increasing training numbers or offering incentives will automatically translate into more doctors entering underserved specialties or rural practice. The persistent under-representation of specific specialties, including family medicine, psychiatry, anaesthesia, and basic sciences, remains evident [4].This observation suggests that the assumption is overly simplistic.

Literature suggests that a complex interplay of individual, institutional, and societal factors shape medical students’ specialty choices. Gender is known to influence specialty choices across countries, with female medical students inclined to paediatrics, obstetrics, and gynaecology, and male counterparts preferring specialties such as Neurosurgery and orthopaedic surgery [13–16].

Evidence from a systematic review indicates that surgery and internal medicine are among the most commonly preferred specialties globally; however, the factors associated with these preferences vary by context [13].

In studies from occidental settings, lifestyle considerations and work–life balance are more frequently reported as influential, whereas personal interest in the discipline appears to be a common driver of specialty choice across contexts. Importantly, this review compared occidental and non-occidental countries as broad categories, without undertaking stratified analyses of low-income settings [13]. As a result, the applicability of these findings to low, resource-constrained health systems, such as The Gambia, remains unclear.

Beyond this contextual gap, existing research on medical specialty choice is often atheoretical and frequently treats preferences as static and individualistic, interpreted primarily through personal “interest” or “lifestyle” narratives [17–20]. This approach under-represents how students’ choices are shaped by situated experiences, such as limited role models in certain specialties, perceived risk of unemployment, and family expectations, and how these experiences interact with structural factors, such as training availability and scholarship schemes. Consequently, there is a critical gap in theoretically informed research examining specialty choices among medical students in an under-resourced context, such as The Gambia.

To address this, the present study is guided by Situated Expectancy-Value Theory (SEVT), which conceptualizes achievement-related choices as shaped by individuals’ expectations of success, the value they attach to different options, and the perceived cost of pursuing them within a specific context [12]. SEVT has been widely applied to educational and career decisions and is well-suited to examining how Gambian medical students navigate specialty choices. Guided by this framework, the study aims to:

Overall, this paper examines specialty choice among clerkship-phase medical students in The Gambia, using Situated Expectancy–Value Theory (SEVT) as a lens to explore specialty preferences. It focuses on gender patterns in specialty preference and associations with SEVT-related motivational constructs. In doing so, it seeks to generate theoretically informed insights that may support educational and policy strategies for building a more balanced specialist workforce, addressing contextual healthcare needs, and aligning with students’ evolving career aspirations.

Situated expectancy-value theory as a framework for exploring medical specialty selection

Eccles’ Expectancy-Value Theory (EEVT) posits that career choice is influenced by an individual’s perceived likelihood of success, weighed against the costs of training, self-belief in their abilities, anticipated benefits, and the personal significance attached to the chosen career [21]. However, the current study draws on Situated Expectancy-Value Theory (SEVT) to extend this model by emphasising that these expectations explore the motivational and contextual factors influencing medical students’ specialty choices. SEVT is an extension of the original expectancy-value model, modified to account for how individuals’ motivations are shaped not only by internal beliefs but are formed and reconstructed within specific social, cultural, and institutional contexts [22]. In this study, SEVT is conceptualised as a framework in which students’ expectancy for success and four components of task value (intrinsic, utility, attainment, and cost) operate within the broader medical learning context to shape career choice (Fig. 1) [23–25].

Expectancy refers to an individual’s belief about the likelihood of achieving a desired outcome or goal [25]. Value, on the other hand, encompasses the importance or significance an individual places on achieving the outcome, which can vary depending on factors such as intrinsic interest, personal goals, and perceived rewards. The aforementioned factors could also determine the value and cost associated with pursuing a particular medical specialty [25].

In medical education research, common factors influencing specialty choice in low- and middle-income countries have been grouped into five categories: career-related influences, personal determinants, life fulfilment, interpersonal influences, and educational factors [26]. There is evidence that contextual changes, such as curricular reforms that enhance specialty-specific exposure during the preclinical years, have been associated with increased student interest in those specialties [27]. Moreover, a study by Ladha et al. [28] demonstrated that specialty preferences evolve, with choices for surgical specialties more strongly associated with attainment values such as prestige. In addition, preferences for primary care tended to reflect expectancy-related factors, such as match confidence, and family-related considerations that map onto both cost and attainment values. Thus, empirically identified factors [19, 29–31], matched to SEVT constructs, directly informed the development and a priori organisation of the 19 motivational items used in this study.

Methods

Study design

A descriptive cross-sectional survey was conducted between 1 July and 31 August 2024 among clinical-phase medical students at the only two Gambian medical schools at the time: the American International University West Africa (AIUWA) and the University of The Gambia (UTG). Data were collected using a pre-tested, self-administered questionnaire distributed in both electronic and hard-copy formats.

Questionnaire and construct design

The questionnaire contained four sections: sociodemographic characteristics, specialty preferences drawn from 15 disciplines [18, 28–30], and students were asked to rank their top three preferred specialties, indicating when they made a definitive decision regarding their preferred specialty; 19 items derived from literature assessing motivational influences on specialty choice(see supplementary Fig. 1), each rated “yes”, “maybe”, or “no”; and an open-ended space for additional comments [18, 28–30]. Each item was classified under each construct according to its definition in the literature (supplementary Fig. 1). In brief, Expectancy included factors such as appraisal of one’s own skills and aptitude and the perceived likelihood of successfully entering a residency program, which imply an expectancy for success [31]. Intrinsic value included intellectual challenge in the specialty and the desire to contribute to society through health promotion [32, 33]. Attainment value reflecting identity-based motivations [24, 34] included items such as role modelling/mentoring. Utility value [32, 35] encompassed items such as a job opportunity. Cost [24, 36, 37] included items such as the length of residency.

The draft items were reviewed by two medical education experts and piloted with 10 non-study students; the wording was refined for clarity before full deployment.

Participants and sampling

The target population comprised all fifth-, sixth-, and final-year students in clinical rotations at AIUWA and UTG (N = 214). Using convenience sampling, we invited all eligible students in lecture theatres, hospital wards, and their classes’ official social-media groups to complete the questionnaire. Students in pre-clinical years or those who declined consent were excluded from the survey.

Statistical analysis

For analysis, the specialty choices were categorised into five groups [38, 39]. :

As shown in Supplementary Table 3, the 19 motivational items were grouped according to Situated Expectancy–Value Theory to form construct-level variables (Expectancy, Intrinsic Value, Attainment Value, Utility Value, and Cost), and each construct was analysed independently. Analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables were reported as means with standard deviations. Associations between students’ first-choice specialty and categorical predictors (i.e., sex and academic year), as well as the influence of the dichotomised Expectancy–Value Theory constructs, were examined using the Fisher–Freeman–Halton test. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical approval

Approval was obtained from the Ethics and Research Committee (AERC) of the American International University of West Africa, The Gambia.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of respondents

A total of 150 fully completed questionnaires were returned out of 214 distributed, yielding a response rate of 70.1%. The majority of respondents (140; 93%) were aged 20–29 years. Regarding gender distribution, 88 respondents (58.7%) identified as female, while 62 (41.3%) identified as male. The participants were primarily from West Africa, with the majority originating from The Gambia (59.3%), followed by Nigeria (24.7%) and Cameroon (6.7%), and smaller proportions from non-African countries (1.3%). In terms of institutional affiliation, 74% of respondents were enrolled at the University of The Gambia, while 26% were enrolled at the American International University, West Africa. Most participants were in the later years of their medical education: 46.7% in the fifth year, 30.7% in the sixth year, and 22% in their final year, reflecting substantial participation by students engaged in active clinical or clerkship rotations. The majority of students were unmarried (94.0%), and 77.3% reported residing in urban areas of The Gambia (Table 1).

The majority of participants identified as Muslim (58%), followed by Christian (42%). Parental education levels revealed a gender-based difference: 50% of male parents versus 42% of female parents had attained a high school certificate; 24.0% of male parents and 30.7% of female parents held a first degree; and 34.0% of male parents compared to 18.7% of female parents had completed a Master’s degree. These findings suggest that male parents were more likely to have attained higher levels of education than female parents (Supplementary Table 1).

Only 23% of participants reported having a relative with a medical degree, indicating limited familial exposure to the medical profession. In terms of parental income, there was a relatively even distribution across monthly income categories ranging from less than USD 166.67 to USD 694.44, suggesting that participants were drawn from a range of socio-economic backgrounds. Notably, 58.7% of students reported receiving financial support from government or non-governmental organisations (NGOs), which may reflect the limited economic capacity of many families to fund medical education independently (Supplementary Table 1).

Distribution of specialty preferences among participants

Based on the aggregation of participants’ first to third specialty choices, the most frequently selected specialties were Surgery (n = 94), Internal Medicine (n = 78), and Obstetrics and Gynaecology (n = 51). Specialties such as Academic Medicine, Orthopaedics, Public Health, and General Practice demonstrated relatively similar frequencies, though they ranked lower than the top three. The least preferred specialties included Radiology, Pathology, Anaesthesia, Ophthalmology, and Basic Sciences (Fig. 2).

Further examination, shown in Table 3, suggests that female participants were more likely to prefer Clinical specialties over Surgical specialties (58.4% vs. 31.2%), whereas male participants were more likely to prefer Surgical specialties over Clinical specialties (42.9% vs. 33.9%). In other specialty categories, a higher proportion of male participants selected Diagnostic (5.4%) and Other specialties (14.3%) than females (2.6%). In contrast, Preventive Medicine was slightly more favoured by female participants (5.2%) than by their male counterparts (3.6%).

Association between first specialty choice and year of study

As shown in Table 3, fifth-year students demonstrated a stronger preference for Clinical specialties compared to Surgical specialties (57.4% vs. 39.3%) and other fields. However, this trend shifted among sixth-year students, who showed a greater preference for Surgical specialties over Clinical specialties (42.2% vs. 31.1%). In contrast, seventh-year (final-year) students displayed a pattern similar to that of fifth-year students, with a higher preference for Clinical specialties over Surgical ones (55.6% vs. 18.5%).

Notably, sixth-year students demonstrated the highest first-choice preference for Surgical specialties (42.2%) and higher first-choice selection of Preventive Medicine (11.1%), and Other specialties (13.3%) compared with both fifth- and seventh-year students. Overall, the top specialty choice among participants was associated with year of study (p < 0.001).

Association between medical students’ first specialty choice and attainment and intrinsic value components of situated expectancy-value theory

The Fisher–Freeman–Halton test indicated associations between motivational constructs and specialty choice (Table 4). There was a significant association between intrinsic motivation and specialty choice (p = 0.049). Among participants reporting high intrinsic motivation, 49.3% selected clinical specialties and 42.3% selected surgical specialties. Similarly, attainment value was significantly associated with specialty choice (p = 0.047), with 69.6% of participants reporting high attainment value selecting clinical specialties compared with 26.1% selecting surgical specialties. This pattern suggests a stronger alignment between clinical specialty preference and identity-related motivation. In contrast, 50% of students reporting a medium level of attainment value selected surgical specialties. Approximately 1.5%–4.2% of participants reporting high intrinsic motivation selected preventive, diagnostic, and other specialties; however, these differences were not statistically significant (p > 0.05). None of the remaining SEVT components were significantly associated with participants’ most preferred specialty (p > 0.05).

Discussion

The study assessed the factors influencing medical specialty choices among medical students in The Gambia, using the Situated Expectancy-Value Theory (SEVT) as a framework. A similar study was conducted in 2012 by Bittaye et al. [3]. Like the comparison study, when the frequency of first to third specialty choices was aggregated, our study showed that Surgery, Obstetrics and Gynaecology, and Internal Medicine had high frequency selection, with Surgery showing the highest cumulative frequency. Previous studies in Kenya and India observed a similar trend [29, 40].

When specialties were categorised as clinical and surgical, our results indicated a possible overlap in students’ first to third choices between these categories (Table 2). Despite the overlap, the data show a strong female preference for Obstetrics and Gynaecology (categorized as a clinical specialty). This is consistent with the data reported by Bittaye et al. This preference for Obstetrics and Gynaecology has also been observed in other studies [13, 41]. In the Bittaye study, factors uniquely influencing female students included focus on urgent care, physician–patient interaction, and the perceived prestige of the specialty. These factors show motivational patterns that also broadly align with those observed in our study.

Given that our study excluded preclinical students, whereas the Bittaye study included a majority of preclinical female participants, this consistent pattern of female preference for Obstetrics and Gynaecology in both studies, despite a 12-year gap, suggests that female students often develop an early interest in Obstetrics and Gynaecology. This pattern aligns with findings from Kamijo et al. [41] in Japan.

In contrast to the Bittaye study, which reported that 81% of male respondents preferred Internal Medicine, our findings show a higher proportion of male students favouring surgical specialties (42.9%) over clinical specialties (33.9%). This suggests a shift in male specialty preferences within the Gambian context. Moreover, the unique factors influencing male choice in the Bittaye study, such as perceived individual competence and focus on urgent care, differ slightly from the motivational patterns observed for males in our cohort.

This shift may reflect changes in national health policies between 2012 and 2020 that prioritised human-resource development and introduced new incentives. During this period, several residency programmes were established, including Obstetrics and Gynaecology, General Surgery, and Internal Medicine in collaboration with the Medical Research Council, The Gambia [42], Family Medicine [43], and a Master’s in Public Health. These developments, together with more recent policies aimed at establishing a Postgraduate College of Surgeons and Physicians (2020–2030) [44], were not in place at the time of the Bittaye study and may have influenced students’ evolving specialty preferences.

Gender-based patterns in specialty preference were also observed in a study from Kenya [29], reinforcing the idea that such trends may be common across several African contexts. Importantly, these patterns are not unique to Africa. A recent systematic review from Western countries similarly highlights gendered specialty preferences, suggesting that these choices may reflect broader societal expectations, particularly the value female students place on meaningful clinical engagement and the perceived social significance of the specialty. While male students are more often associated with career-focused or high-intensity professional trajectories [15, 45].

As mentioned earlier, our study focused exclusively on students in the clinical (clerkship) phase of training, where exposure to different specialties is more direct and likely to inform decision-making [46]. Accordingly, an evolving trend was observed within the Clinical specialty category: interest peaked in the fifth year, declined in the sixth year, and rose again in the final (seventh) year, suggesting a temporary broadening of preferences mid-clerkship. In this study context, the participants in the final year are in the third year of core rotations, where it has been observed that the majority of students solidify their specialty choice during third-year core rotations [46, 47]. Consistent with the theoretical underpinnings of Situated Expectancy–Value Theory, it is plausible that this shift reflects the influence of students’ clinical learning experiences on their motivation for specialty choice [48]. In essence, increased clinical exposure may heighten awareness of cost (e.g., workload and lifestyle demands), stimulate intrinsic value (e.g., enjoyment of specific rotation experiences), shape attainment value (e.g., team dynamics, supervisory relationships, and perceived professional identity), and influence expectancy for success (e.g., confidence in ability or perceived likelihood of succeeding) within a specialty. While these mechanisms were not directly measured in the present study, future qualitative research would be well-positioned to explore how such motivational beliefs evolve.

Furthermore, this shift in sixth-year preferences may signal a critical point for curricular enhancement and targeted interventions to support more diverse and informed specialty considerations [47]. Such influence has been reported in Japan, where students’ interest in General Medicine increased following clinical rotations in the specialty [49]. Taken together, our findings reinforce the value of early practical, immersive experiences in real-world clinical settings as key drivers in the formation of specialty interests.

Finally, the motivations underlying participants’ specialty choices were interpreted through the lens of Situated Expectancy Value Theory (SEVT), as informed by relevant literature. Fisher’s analysis (Table 4) revealed significant associations between specialty choice and the components of interest and attainment value. Lee et al. [50] argue that the attainment component of SEVT is closely tied to identity; in other words, individuals are motivated to pursue goals that align with how they see themselves.

Given that attainment value was significantly associated with preferences for clinical specialties, while high intrinsic value was distributed across both clinical and surgical specialties, and considering the significant association between sex and the choice of these specialties observed in our findings, future research should examine whether these motivational patterns differ by gender. Although our current analyses did not directly test this, it represents an important direction for further study. From an SEVT perspective, the observed preference for clinical disciplines may reflect identity alignment and perceived personal significance (attainment value) among students selecting these fields. Such patterns may also be shaped by broader societal norms or contextual healthcare needs that reinforce interest in specialties perceived as more relational or patient-centred, such as obstetrics and gynaecology. In addition, evidence from other fields suggests that intrinsic interest can shape entry into and persistence within particular professional pathways [50]; supporting the need for future research to examine whether and how gender moderates the relationship between motivational constructs and medical specialty choice.

Although direct studies exploring Gambian doctors’ expectations to succeed in surgical residencies are limited, international studies suggest that female students’ lower interest in surgical specialties is influenced by factors such as male dominance in surgical departments, lack of female role models, concerns about work–life balance and fertility, and a challenging male-dominated work environment [45, 51].

Not all studies report similar gendered patterns. For example, in China [52] most male students (63.6%) preferred surgery, while female students showed a broader distribution of interests, with internal medicine as the most common choice. In contrast, a study from Mexico found no significant gender differences in specialty preference [53]. However, both studies explored gender-linked psychological traits and motivations, yielding further insight: female students scored higher on traits such as emotionality, aesthetic appreciation, and modesty, attributes potentially aligned with strong physician-patient relationships, which are important for clinical specialists, while male students scored higher on inquisitiveness, a facet of the Openness to Experience domain, which is largely associated with intrinsic motivation [53]. Additionally, it was noted that female students were motivated by specialty-specific knowledge and a sense of achievement, while intellectual challenge was a stronger driver for male students [52]. When viewed through the SEVT framework, these findings suggest that intrinsic motivation (e.g., interest, intellectual challenge) and attainment value may relate differently to specialty choice across male and female students. Consequently, addressing gender underrepresentation in specialties may require efforts to reframe or broaden the professional identity and socio-cultural meaning associated with the target disciplines [54].

To our knowledge, this is the first study to view medical specialty choice through the lens of SEVT, though it has several limitations. Intrinsic Value (P = 0.049) and Attainment Value (P = 0.047) lie close to the conventional significance threshold and should therefore be interpreted with appropriate caution; these findings are indicative rather than definitive. The use of self-administered questionnaires permitted the identification of associations but not causal relationships. For example, students’ specialty choices may have been influenced by limited exposure at the time of the survey. While the study explores intended career preferences, it also does not capture actual enrolment into specialty training. Additionally, the survey may not have captured all influences on specialty choice. The unique context of expatriates, often with limited English proficiency and little understanding of the local language, may shape specialty decisions in ways not accounted for by SEVT. Another key limitation of this study is the challenge of accurately measuring motivational constructs, such as attainment value and intrinsic motivation, with structured questionnaires. Bearing in mind that these constructs are often deeply intertwined with individuals’ identities and shaped by cultural, familial, and situational influences, attempts to isolate them through survey items may lead to conceptual overlap or obscure the origins of why certain tasks are perceived as important, particularly in the context of SEVT [24]. For instance, an item such as “desire to provide community service” may reflect intrinsic motivation related to personal fulfilment, or it may indicate an attainment value influenced by culturally embedded career expectations. Similarly, interest may be situational and emergent rather than stable or generalisable [22]. These complexities suggest that standardised measures may not fully capture the dynamic, context-dependent nature of motivation. Accordingly, ongoing studies are adopting longitudinal and qualitative approaches to address gaps in understanding how cultural identity shapes the motivations underpinning specialty choice [22, 55, 56]. Convenience sampling used in this study may limit representativeness and introduce selection bias, thereby reducing the generalisability of the findings. However, the study achieved a response rate of 70.1%, which strengthens the credibility of the results by reducing the likelihood of systematic non-response bias. Nevertheless, certain groups may still be overrepresented while others remain underrepresented, and the findings should be interpreted with all these limitations in mind.

Implications for curriculum design and workforce development in The Gambia

In light of the discussions above, these findings may contribute to bridging the persistent gap between policy formulation and the intended policy outcomes, particularly within specialist workforce planning. First, according to the Gambian national policy, the continuing burden of neonatal mortality and maternal health complications may reinforce the societal value placed on Obstetrics and Gynaecology [44]. This may explain the sustained female preference for this specialty through alignment with patient-centred roles [3, 57]. In addition, the expansion of training pathways, incentives, and clearer postgraduate opportunities may strengthen the utility and expectancy components of motivation, potentially increasing interest in surgical specialties [49, 58, 59]. Given the observed association between sex and specialty choice in our sample, future research should examine whether these contextual influences operate differently across male and female students.

Furthermore, the continued prominence of Surgery, Obstetrics and Gynaecology, and Internal Medicine as the most preferred specialties since 2012 suggests that current strategies may be insufficient to achieve a balanced specialist workforce. This has important implications for under-subscribed fields such as ENT, radiology, and sonography, which remain low-interest despite policy commitments to strengthen these services through human resource and infrastructural development.

From an SEVT perspective, our findings suggest that students may ascribe lower expectancy, utility, and attainment value, and higher perceived cost, to less visible specialties. Addressing these gaps may therefore include: alignment with intrinsic and attainment value factors. For the latter interventions, such as early awareness campaigns in secondary schools, community education to enhance societal appreciation for these specialties, enhanced preclinical exposure, structured mentorship, and targeted incentives can be implemented. Furthermore, incorporating research modules within under-subscribed specialties may also increase perceived technical challenge and academic appeal, thereby strengthening intrinsic interest and utility value among students [57]. Such efforts may help build a more balanced, context-responsive specialist workforce.

Finally, our findings contribute to SEVT by showing how contextual opportunity structures, particularly the availability of specialty training pathways, may shape students’ specialty preferences through expectancy- and value-related mechanisms in a resource-constrained setting. At the same time, they underscore the importance of contextually grounded curriculum design that goes beyond conventional career counselling, in supporting students’ career choices [3]. In the Gambian context, this may involve adopting culturally responsive pedagogy, including longitudinal mentoring with guided reflection by respected clinicians, structured clinical exposure (e.g. supervised gap-year experiences), and an emphasis on community impact during counselling and clinical learning experiences [60]. Such approaches may be more effective than traditional counselling alone, as they reflect local religious and social hierarchies, high power distance, and socioeconomic uncertainty. They also align with our findings that perceived meaningfulness and personal interest play a key role in specialty choice. In practical terms, we suggest a shift from episodic, information-focused counselling toward longitudinal mentoring, experiential learning opportunities, and guided reflection embedded within the curriculum. This may equip students to make specialty choices that are both contextually and personally meaningful. Incorporating these measures could play an essential role in addressing the ongoing challenge of healthcare workforce imbalance in the country.

Conclusion

This study identifies gender and year of clinical exposure as key factors influencing medical specialty choice. Our findings largely align with previous evidence in The Gambia, confirming persistent preferences for Surgery, Obstetrics and Gynaecology, and Internal Medicine, while highlighting a shift in male students’ interest toward surgical specialties. By applying SEVT, our findings suggest that students’ decisions may be shaped by intrinsic motivation and attainment values. These insights have practical implications for medical education policy and postgraduate training, including establishing and accrediting sustainable programs, early clinical exposure that offers hands-on, enjoyable experiences, structured mentorship, community engagement, targeted incentives for under-subscribed specialties, and context-responsive teaching. Such strategies may foster a more balanced and sustainable health workforce. Further studies are underway to explore the role of attainment value and intrinsic motivation in shaping specialty choice within the Gambian context.
