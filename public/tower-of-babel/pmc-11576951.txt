CYP4BN4v7 regulates the population density dependent oocyte maturity rate in bean beetles
Authors: Qianquan Chen, Yongqin Li, Zheng Fang, Qingshan Wu, Leitao Tan, Qingbei Weng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The bean beetle (Callosobruchus maculatus) clearly exhibits population density-dependent polymorphism. Cytochrome P450 (CYP) is involved in many aspects of the physiological activities of insects. However, the role of CYP in population density-dependent polymorphisms remains unknown. The terminal oocyte maturity rate of high-population-density individuals (H) was faster than that of low-population-density individuals (L). A total of 56 CYP-like genes were identified from transcriptomic and genomic data, including seven clan 2 CYP-like genes, seven mitochondrial CYP-like genes, 19 clan 3 CYP-like genes, and 23 clan 4 CYP-like genes. Gene duplication might occur in CYP9Z4-like, CYP345A1-like, CYP345A2-like, CYP349A1-like, CYP349A2-like, and CYP4BN4-like. Thirteen and two CYP-like genes were up-regulated and down-regulated, respectively, in H. Among these CYP-like genes, CYP4BN4v7-like was the most abundant CYP. CYP4BN4v7-like was more highly expressed in the head than in the thorax and abdomen. Its mRNA levels in the head, thorax, and abdomen were greater in H than in L. After RNA interference decreased its mRNA level, the terminal oocyte maturity rate decreased. Moreover, the expression level of insulin-like peptide 1 (ILP1), which plays a vital role in regulating terminal oocyte development, decreased in the head. In conclusion, CYP4BN4v7-like modulated the population density-dependent terminal oocyte maturity rate by regulating the expression of ILP1.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-79866-2.

Population density fluctuations are prevalent, and the benefits and costs of possessing a phenotype vary at different population densities1. Consequently, many insects can sense population density and adjust their phenotypes, including behavior, body color, wing morphology, sensilla morphology, developmental rate, hormone metabolism, immunity, and reproduction2. For example, Locusta migratoria exhibits clear phenotypic plasticity depending on population density3. Population density can regulate females’ sexual maturity rate and egg size4. Moreover, eggs generated from females live under high population density, hatch more uniformly, and develop faster5. The improvement in the sexual maturity rate and development rate could accelerate population outbreaks by shortening the generation time. In addition to these traits, population density can regulate the expression of genes related to physiological stress such as cytochrome P450 (CYP)6,7; however, few studies explore the role of CYP in density-dependent polymorphism.

CYP is one of the largest gene superfamilies in living organisms, including bacteria, fungi, plants, and animals8. The expansion and diversification of CYPs are common in insects; for example, 48, 84, 90, 111, 143, 164, and 204 CYPs are encoded by Apis mellifera, Bombyx mori, Drosophila melanogaster, Anopheles gambiae, Tribolium castaneum, Aedes aegypti, and Culex quinquefasciatus, respectively9,10. CYPs participate in many aspects of physiological activities in insects. CYPs are mainly involved in the detoxification of xenobiotics, including plant allelochemicals and synthetic insecticides11–16. Microarray-based methods revealed that xenobiotic-inducible CYPs belonged to CYP3, CYP4, and mitochondrial clans in D. melanogaster17,18. With the help of CYPs, T. castaneumis resistant to five classes of insecticides and fumigants that are used to manage it9. Furthermore, CYPs regulate the biosynthesis of hydrocarbons deposited on epicuticles as waterproofing agents by terrestrial insects19,20. CYPs play important roles in chemical communication and behavior via the catalysis and hydroxylation of fatty acids and the degradation of pheromones21. Additionally, CYPs are involved in development, metamorphosis, and reproduction22,23. Currently, most researchers focus on revealing the role of CYPs in detoxification24, but few researchers have unveiled the role of CYPs in reproduction25.

Bean beetles (Callosobruchus maculatus) are world-wide distributed stored-product insects that can complete their life history on 35 species’ seeds and adapt to novel hosts in a few generations26. At suitable temperatures, they can reproduce for 11–12 generations every year26. They exhibit clear density-dependent polymorphism, i.e., individuals appear in active form and nonactive form under high population density and low population density, respectively. There are differences in behavior, morphology, chemical constitution of the body, physiology, and reproduction between the active form and nonactive form27,28. The transition from inactive form to active form can lead to population outbreaks, however, the molecular mechanism of the transition remains unknown.

Insulin-like peptides (ILPs) are widely distributed in invertebrates. In D. melanogaster, ILPs are expressed mainly in the nervous system29. In addition, ILPs are expressed in the other tissues, including midgut, imaginal discs, salivary glands, fat body, and ovary30. They play important roles in many physiological processes, including metabolism, development, immunity, feeding behavior, stress resistance, diapause, lifespan, and reproduction29. Four ILPs are identified in bean beetles. And ILP1is expressed mainly in the head31. The expression levels of ILP1-3 in the head, ILP2-3 in the thorax, ILP1 as well as ILP3in the abdomen are regulated by the population density31. RNA interference experiments prove that each ILP can regulate terminal oocyte maturity rate31. ILPs regulate vitellogenin (Vg) synthesis by controlling the phosphorylation of FOXO, which is a transcriptional repressor32.

In the present study, bean beetles were used to explore the role of CYP in population density-dependent plasticity in reproduction. A total of 56 CYP-like genes were identified from the genomic data and RNA sequencing data of the bean beetle. Thirteen and two CYP-like genes were upregulated and downregulated, respectively, in high population-density individuals. The RNA interference results showed that CYP4BN4v7-like regulated the terminal oocyte maturity rate.

Results

Oocyte maturity rate

The length of the terminal oocyte can be used to indicate the sexual maturity rate of a female4. In twelve hours after adult emergence, the terminal oocyte length of high-population-density individuals (H) was significantly longer than that of low-population-density individuals (L) (Fig. 1A, B, n = 15 for each group; t-test, P = 0.0066).

Identification and phylogenetic analysis of CYP

A total of 56 CYP-like genes were identified in the bean beetle (Fig. 2). Most CYP-like genes have conserved domains, including the Heme-binding domain, PERF domain, helix-K, and helix-I (Fig. S1). Seven clan 2 CYP-like genes were identified, including CYP303A1-like, CYP15-like, CYP305A1-like, CYP18A1-like, CYP306A1-like, CYP307A1-like, and CYP307B1-like (Fig. 2A). Seven mitochondrial CYP-like genes were identified, including CYP301A1-like, CYP49A1-like, CYP302A1-like, CYP314A1-like, CYP315A1-like, CYP334B1v1-like, and CYP334B1v2-like (Fig. 2B). Two versions of CYP334B1-like were detected in bean beetle, and the amino acid sequence identities, positives (different amino acids had similar chemical properties) and gaps between CYP334B1v1-like and CYP334B1v2-like were 53%, 75% and 0%, respectively.

A total of 19 clan 3 CYP-like genes were identified in bean beetles, including CYP9AD1-like, CYP345D2-like, CYP348A1-like, CYP6BS1-like, CYP6BR1-like, CYP6BK2-like, CYP6BQ6-like, CYP6BQ9-like, CYP6BQ10-like, two CYP6BQ5-like, two CYP345A1-like, three CYP345A2-like, and three CYP9Z4-like (Fig. 2C). Among these CYP6 genes, CYP6BQ5v1-like had the lowest amino acid sequence identity with CYP6BQ9-like, while CYP6BQ6-like had the highest amino acid sequence identity with CYP6BQ10-like (Table S1.1). Among the three CYP9Z4-like genes, CYP9Z4v1-like had the lowest amino acid sequence identity with CYP9Z4v3-like, and CYP9Z4v2-like had the highest amino acid sequence identity with CYP9Z4v3-like (Table S1.2). Among the five CYP345A-like genes, CYP345A2v2-like had the lowest amino acid sequence identity with CYP345A1v2-like, while CYP345A2v1-like had the highest amino acid sequence identity with CYP345A2v2-like (Table S1.3). Taken together, several versions of CYP6BQ5-like, CYP9Z4-like, CYP345A1-like, and CYP345A2-like were detected in bean beetles, which suggested that gene duplication might occur in these genes.

A total of 23 clan 4 CYP-like genes were identified in bean beetles, including CYP350C1-like, CYP352A1-like, CYP352A2-like, CYP4AA1-like, CYP4G14-like, CYP4G7-like, CYP4BN2-like, CYP4BN7-like, three CYP349A2-like, five CYP349A1-like, and seven CYP4BN4-like (Fig. 2D). CYP349A2v1-like shared 52% and 34% amino acid sequence identity with CYP349A2v2-like and CYP349A2v3-like, respectively (Table S1.4). Among the five CYP349A1-like genes, CYP349A1v2-like shared the highest amino acid sequence identity with CYP349A1v3-like, while CYP349A1v3-like shared the lowest amino acid sequence identity with CYP349A1v5-like (Table S1.5). Among the seven CYP4BN4-like genes, CYP4BN4v6-like shared the highest amino acid sequence identity with CYP4BN4v5-like, while CYP4BN4v7-like shared the lowest amino acid sequence identity with CYP4BN4v2-like (Table S1.6). The sequence alignment of the seven versions of CYP4BN4-like showed that the region from 350 to 580 was more conserved than the remaining region (Fig. S2).

Expression levels of CYP

Among clan 2 CYPs, the expression levels of CYP307B1-like and CYP306A1-like were the lowest and highest, respectively (Fig. 3A). Population density had no significant effect on the expression levels of clan 2 CYPs (Fig. 3A). Among the mitochondrial CYPs, the expression levels of CYP334B1v1-like and CYP302A1-like were the lowest and highest, respectively (Fig. 3B). Compared to those in L, the expression levels of CYP334B1v1-like and CYP334B1v2-like were up-regulated in H.

Among clan 3 CYPs, the mRNA levels of CYP9Z4v2-like and CYP9Z4v3-like were the lowest and highest, respectively (Fig. 3C). The expression levels of CYP9Z4v2-like, CYP345A1v2-like, and CYP345A2v3-like were up-regulated in H; however, the expression levels of CYP6BS1-like and CYP345A2v1-like were down-regulated in H.

For clan 4 CYPs, the expression level of CYP349A1v3-like was the lowest (Fig. 3D). The mRNA levels of CYP349A1v3-like, CYP349A1v4-like, CYP4BN7-like, CYP350C1-like, CYP349A1v2-like, CYP349A2v3-like, CYP4BN4v2-like, and CYP4BN4v7-like were up-regulated in H (Fig. 3D). The mRNA level of CYP4BN4v7-like was the most abundant, and its FKPM values reached to 602.0 and 416.2 in H and L, respectively (Fig. 3D).

Gene ontology analysis of CYP

CYPs were involved in several cellular components, molecular functions, and biological processes (Fig. S3A). Differentially expressed CYPs between H and L were enriched in cellular components including membrane, intracellular membrane-bounded organelle, membrane-bounded organelle, and endoplasmic reticulum (Fig. S3B). The main molecular functions of these genes were oxidoreductase activity, binding, monooxygenase activity, and steroid hydroxylase activity. These genes were involved in biological processes, including oxidation-reduction processes, response to organic cyclic compounds, response to insecticides, fatty acid omega-oxidation, sterol metabolic processes, retinoid metabolic processes, mating, cellular hormone metabolic processes, and drug response (Fig. S3B). In particular, CYP4BN4v7, the most abundant CYP, was involved in mating. Based on expression level and GO analysis results, we focused on studying the function of CYP4BN4v7-like in population density-dependent plasticity in reproduction.

Function of CYP4BN4v7-like in oocyte development

Samples from H were used to reveal tissue-specific expression of CYP4BN4v7-like. The expression level of CYP4BN4v7-like in the head was greater than that in the thorax and abdomen (Fig. 4A) (n = 6 for each group, ANOVA, P = 0.0021). The cephalic mRNA level of this gene was significantly greater in H than in L (Fig. 4B) (n = 5 for each group, t-test, P = 0.0018). Similarly, compared with those in L, the mRNA levels of this gene in the thorax, and abdomen were up-regulated in H (Fig. 4C, thorax, n = 5 for each group, t-test, P = 0.0075; Fig. 4D, abdomen, n = 8 for each group, t-test, P = 0.019). To determine its function in terminal oocyte development, a double-stranded RNA targeting CYP4BN4v7-like (dsCYP4BN4v7) was designed. Compared to those after dsGFP injection, the mRNA levels of CYP4BN4v7-like in the head, and abdomen decreased by approximately 90% after dsCYP4BN4v7 injection (Fig. 4E) (head, n = 5 for each group, t-test, P < 0.001; abdomen, n = 6 for each group, t-test, P < 0.001). After the dsCYP4BN4v7 injection, the abdomen became blacker (Fig. 4F), and the terminal oocyte length decreased (Fig. 4G, H) (n = 15 for each group, t-test, P< 0.0001). Owing to each insulin-like peptide (ILP) can regulate the terminal oocyte development in bean beetles31, the mRNA levels of all ILPs were determined after dsCYP4BN4v7 injection. The expression level of ILP1 was significantly decreased in the head (Fig. 5A, n = 6 for each group, t-test, P = 0.0056); however, the expression levels of the remaining ILPs in the head were not significantly changed (Fig. 5B-D). None of the ILP expression levels in the abdomen significantly changed (Fig. 5E-H). ILP1is mainly expressed in the head, and its expression level in the head is 289.53 times greater than that in the thorax or abdomen31. However, the mRNA levels of the remaining ILPs in the head were approximately three times greater than those in the thorax or abdomen. Moreover, the mRNA level of ILP1was much greater than that of the remaining ILPs in the head31. RNA interference experiments show that ILP1can regulate terminal oocyte maturity rate31. Taken together, these findings indicated that CYP4BN4v7-like modulated terminal oocyte development by regulating the expression of ILP1.

Discussion

Bean beetles could adjust their terminal oocyte maturity rate according to population density. At a low population density, there were enough seeds for females to lay eggs, and females laid more eggs to increase the number of their offspring28. At a high population density, there were not enough seeds for females to lay eggs, and females had to compete for seeds to lay eggs28. Females who had a faster sexual maturity rate could lay eggs earlier. As a result, their offspring might have advantages in development. When each seed was occupied by several eggs, females dumped some eggs to avoid each seed being occupied by too many eggs. In short, females adjusted their maturity rate to increase the fitness of their offspring.

Clan 2 CYPs are involved in development and metamorphosis. For example, CYP307A is required for the biosynthesis of 20-hydroxyecdysone (20E) in D. melanogaster22. On the contrary, CYP15A1 is involved in catalyzing the epoxidation of methyl-farnesoate to the juvenile hormone in Diploptera punctata33,34. CYP18A1 plays a key role in metamorphosis via the inactivation of steroid hormones in D. melanogaster35. The expression level of CYP306A1 was positively correlated with the ecdysteroid titer in T. castaneum9; hence, CYP306A1 might be involved in ecdysteroid biosynthesis. CYP303A1 is involved in the development of external sensory organs to receive mechanosensory and chemosensory stimuli in D. melanogaster36. Population density did not affect the expression levels of clan 2 CYPs. Taken together, clan 2 CYPs might regulate the development and metamorphosis of bean beetles. However, they do not play key roles in population density-dependent polymorphisms in bean beetles. Among mitochondrial CYPs, gene duplication might occur in CYP334B1-like. Both CYP334B1-like genes were up-regulated in H. In T. castaneum, the mRNA level of CYP334B1 was low at the larval stage, and it increased at the adult stage, which suggested that CYP334B1might be involved in development and reproduction9. It seemed CYP334B1-like might contribute to the differences in development and reproduction between H and L in bean beetles.

Clan 3 CYPs are involved in the detoxification of xenobiotics. For example, CYP9AQ2 is involved in deltamethrin detoxification in L. migratoria37,38. In bean beetles, three versions of CYP9Z4-like were identified, which suggested that gene duplication might occur. With more copies of CYP9Z4-like, bean beetles might be more efficient in deltamethrin detoxification than L. migratoria. In addition, CYP9Z4v2-like was up-regulated at high population densities, which indicated that H might have a stronger ability in deltamethrin detoxification than L. CYP6BQ9 is responsible for the majority of deltamethrin resistance in T. castaneum39. In bean beetles, five CYP6BQ-like genes were identified, including CYP6BQ6-like, CYP6BQ9-like, CYP6BQ10-like, and two versions of CYP6BQ5-like. These CYP6BQ-like genes could contribute to deltamethrin resistance for bean beetles. CYP345A1 is involved in the detoxification of several kinds of insecticides in T. castaneum40. Two CYP345A1-like, and three CYP345A2-like were identified in bean beetles. At the same time, CYP345A1v2-like, and CYP345A2v3-like were up-regulated in H. Gene duplication in CYP345A-like could enhance detoxification of insecticides for bean beetles, and up-regulation of CYP345A-like in H could enhance detoxification of insecticides for H. Overall, gene duplication of clan 3 CYPs could contribute to detoxification of insecticides for bean beetle, and up-regulation of clan 3 CYPs could furtherly enhance the resistance to insecticides for H.

RNA Sequencing data showed that CYP4BN4v7-like was the most abundant CYP in the abdomen, and the expression level of CYP4BN4v7-like was significantly higher in H than that in L. Furthermore, GO analysis revealed that CYP4BN4-like was involved in mating. Expression level and GO analysis results suggested that CYP4BN4v7-like might play a key role in regulating population density-dependent plasticity in reproduction. Seven versions of CYP4BN4-like were detected in bean beetles, and CYP4BN4v2-like together with CYP4BN4v7-like were up-regulated in H. Among these CYPs, the expression level of CYP4BN4v7-like was the highest. Compared to L, CYP4BN4v7-like was up-regulated in the head, thorax, and abdomen of H, which suggested that population density could regulate the expression of CYP4BN4v7-like. Terminal oocyte length can be used to indicate the sexual maturity rate of L. migratoria and bean beetle4,31. Female bean beetles are fully mature from 24 to 36 h after emergence, they were collected for RNA extraction and measurement of terminal oocytes in twelve hours after adult emergence31. L. migratoria is much larger than bean beetle in body size, as a result, females of the former take much longer time to mature. The females of L. migratoria are fully mature at approximately 12 and 16 days after adult emergence under high population density and low population density, respectively. On the 4th, 8th, and 12th day after female adult emergence, the terminal oocyte length of high population density females is longer than that of low population density females. Both results from beetles and L. migratoria show that the length of terminal oocyte before fully mature can be used to indicate sexual maturity rate. Further RNA interference experiments showed that the knockdown of CYP4BN4v7-like led to a decrease in the terminal oocyte maturity rate of the bean beetles. These results indicated that CYP4BN4v7-like could regulate population density-dependent plasticity in reproduction in bean beetles. In addition to CYP4BN4v7-like, there were 12 CYPs up-regulated in H. These 12 CYPs might be involved in regulating population density-dependent plasticity in reproduction, which requires further study.

Previous study shows that all ILPs can regulate terminal oocyte development in bean beetle31, hence, the expression level of each ILP was determined after the knockdown of CYP4BN4v7-like. The expression level of ILP1 in the head decreased by 50% after the knockdown of CYP4BN4v7-like; however, the expression levels of the remaining ILPs in the head were not significantly changed. None of the ILP expression levels in the abdomen significantly changed after the knockdown of CYP4BN4v7-like. ILP1is mainly expressed in the head, and its expression level in the head is much greater than that in other tissues31. Population density can regulate the expression levels of ILP1-3 in the head, ILP2-3 in the thorax, ILP1 as well as ILP3 in the abdomen31. RNA interference experiments show that ILP1can regulate terminal oocyte maturity rate31. Taken together, ILP1can regulate population density-dependent plasticity in the terminal oocyte maturity rate. ILPs promote the phosphorylation of FOXO. FOXO can bind the control region of vitellogenin to work as a transcriptional repressor, however, after phosphorylation, FOXO cannot bind the control region of vitellogenin and subsequently, vitellogenin synthesis is initiated32. Taken together, these findings indicate that CYP4BN4v7-like modulates the population density-dependent plasticity in terminal oocyte maturity rate by regulating the expression of ILP1. However, how CYP4BN4v7-like regulates the expression of ILP1 requires further study. Exploring the effect of CYPs on the sexual maturity rate not only expands our understanding of density-dependent polymorphisms but also sheds light on pest management.

Conclusions

Compared to L, H had a faster terminal oocyte maturity rate. A total of 56 CYP-like genes were identified, and four conserved domains were characterized in CYPs. Gene duplication might occur in CYP9Z4-like, CYP345A1-like, CYP345A2-like, CYP349A1-like, CYP349A2-like, and CYP4BN4-like. Thirteen and two CYPs were up-regulated and down-regulated in H, respectively. CYP4BN4v7-like was the most abundant CYP and was up-regulated in H. CYP4BN4v7-like modulated the terminal oocyte maturity rate by regulating the expression of ILP1.

Materials & methods

Insects

Bean beetles were maintained in the School of Life Sciences, at Guizhou Normal University28,31. They were reared with mung beans in a tissue culture a glass bottle (0.2 L), which was covered with gauze to avoid escape and to provide air for breathing. Two pairs of low-population-density beetles were reared with approximately 100 seeds in a bottle to build a low-density population. Similarly, 20 pairs of high-population-density beetles were reared in a bottle with approximately 100 seeds to build a high-density population. All beetles were reared at 28 ± 2℃ with a 14:10 light: dark photoperiod and 50–70% humidity. Before the experiments, the bean beetles were reared for five generations.

Measurement of terminal oocyte length

The adults are fully mature from 24 to 36 h after emergence, thus female beetles were collected for RNA extraction and measurement of terminal oocytes in 12 h after adult emergence31. Some female beetles were immediately placed in liquid nitrogen and then stored at −80℃ for RNA extraction. The remaining females were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution for three days. Each ovary is composed of several ovarioles. The ovary was separated into single ovariole. The terminal oocyte locates at the tip of the ovariole. Pictures of terminal oocytes were acquired with a microscope (SZX7; Olympus, Japan). The length of terminal oocytes was measured on the pictures with cellSens software (SZX7; Olympus, Japan)31. All terminal oocytes from the same female were measured, and then the lengths of these terminal oocytes were averaged as a value. Fifteen females (biological replications) were used for each group (high population density vs. low population density) or each treatment (dsGFP vs. dsCYP4BN4v7).

Identification of CYP in bean beetles

The abdomens from more than ten females were pooled together as one replicate, and three biological replicates were performed for each group (high population density vs. low population density). Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol Reagent (Thermo Fisher). The RNA quality and quantity were measured with a NanoDrop 2000 and an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100, respectively. The paired-end libraries were synthesized with a TruSeq stranded mRNA sample preparation kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol. All sequencing was conducted with Illumina HiSeq 2500 sequencing technology with a maximum read length of 2 × 150 bp (GenBank BioProject number: PRJNA1120775). The quality of the reads was assessed with FastQC v.0.10.1. Low-quality reads and ambiguous nucleotides (reads with more than 5% N bases) were filtered with Trimmomatic v.0.3641. At the same time, adapter sequences were trimmed with Trimmomatic v.0.3641. The remaining high-quality data were used for de novo transcriptome assembly with Trinity v.2.5.142,43. Clean reads were aligned to the unigene library using Bowtie v.2.3.444. The expression levels of the unigenes were calculated using RSEM v.1.3.045. The expression level of each unigene was represented by the fragments per kilobase per million mapped reads (FPKM) value. Differential expression analysis of samples was carried out with DESeq2 v.1.18.1 using default settings46. Unigenes with a minimal fold change of 1.5 and a false discovery rate < 0.0147 were considered significantly differentially expressed between H and L.

The protein sequences of the CYPs of T. castaneum were downloaded from the cytochrome P450 homepage (http://drnelson.uthsc.edu/CytochromeP450.html) and NCBI9. Given that the study provided RNA Sequencing data for the abdomen of female bean beetles, RNA Sequencing data for the whole body of bean beetles, including different developmental stages (larvae, pupae, and adults) and both sexes (male, and female) (BioProject accession number: PRJNA309272; GenBank accession number: GEUD00000000), were downloaded from NCBI48. The protein sequences of T. castaneum were used as queries to identify CYPs of bean beetles using BioEdit software with default settings49. The significance value was 1e−20. Because the genome of the bean beetle was decoded, the protein sequences (without specific gene names) were downloaded from NCBI (GenBank accession number: GCA_900659725.1)50. BLASTP was used to identify the CYPs of bean beetles from the protein sequence library. A comprehensive comparison between the transcriptome and genome results was performed to determine the most reliable CYPs. In addition, protein sequences from the genome annotation were used to verify gene duplication.

Phylogenetic analysis of CYP

The protein sequences of the CYPs of D. melanogaster, T. castaneum, and A. mellifera were downloaded from the cytochrome P450 homepage and NCBI. The protein sequences of these three species were aligned with the CYPs of bean beetles via ClustalW alignment in Molecular Evolutionary Genetic Analysis software version 11 (MEGA 11)51. The pair-wise alignment was conducted with a gap opening penalty of 10 and a gap extension penalty of 0.1. Multiple alignments were performed with a gap opening penalty of 3.0 and a gap extension penalty of 1.840. Neighbor-joining trees were constructed with 1,000 bootstrap replications. The Poisson model was selected as the substitution model. The amino acid sequence identities, positives (different amino acids had similar chemical properties), and gaps between two proteins were calculated using BioEdit software49.

Identification of conserved motifs

Conserved domains of CYP-like genes of bean beetles were identified using Multiple Expectation Maximization for Motif Elicitation (MEME, http://meme-suite.org/)52. The amino acid sequences of these conserved domains including the heme-binding domain, PERF domain, helix-K, and helix-I, were extracted, and sequence logos were created with WEBLOGO (http://weblogo.berkeley.edu/)53.

Gene ontology analysis of CYPs

The CYPs of bean beetle were subjected to gene ontology (GO) annotation with Blast2GO (http://www.blast2go.com/)54. These genes were divided according to the following criteria: cellular component, molecular function, and biological process.

Quantitative real-time PCR and RNA interference

The females were divided according to their head, thorax, and abdomen. Six heads were pooled together as one replicate, and five or six biological replicates were performed for each group (high population density vs. low population density) and treatment (dsGFP vs. dsCYP4BN4v7)31. Similarly, the thorax and abdomen samples were processed according to the head samples. Total RNA was extracted from the samples with TriQuick Reagent (Solarbio). The concentration and quality were measured with a Biotek Epoch2. Two µg of total RNA was used to synthesize cDNA with FastKing gDNA Dispelling RT Supermix (Tiangen). The mRNA levels were determined with Talent qPCR PreMix (SYBR Green) (Tiangen) and normalized to those of the ribosome protein 494,31. PCR amplification was carried out with the Thermo QuantStudio 3. Melting curve analysis was used to confirm the specificity of the amplification. Relative mRNA levels were calculated with the 2−ΔΔCt method. The sequences of the primers are listed in Table S2.

CYP4BN4v7-like was amplified from cDNA with gene-specific primers (Table S2). The PCR products were subsequently cloned and inserted into the PGEM-T Easy vector (Promega, Madison, USA). The T7 promoter sequence was added to the 5’ end of the forward primer (T7-F) and reverse primer (T7-R). One double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) synthesis template was amplified with the vector containing the sequence of CYP4BN4v7-like using the combination of T7-F and R, and the other template was amplified using the combination of F and T7-R. DsRNAs were generated with the T7 RiboMAX™ Express Large Scale RNA Production System (Promega). DsRNA targeting the green fluorescent protein (dsGFP) was used as a control. High population density individuals were used for injection. At the pupal stage (approximately three days before adult emergence), approximately one µg of dsRNA was injected into each bean beetle through the abdomen using a nanoliter injector 2000 (World Precision Instruments) with a microglass needle. Female beetles were collected for silencing efficiency analysis and measurement of terminal oocytes in 12 h after adult emergence.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using the SAS 9.0 software. The normality of the data was checked using the Shapiro-Wilk test. The equality of the data variances was checked using the Folded F test. For normally and homogenously distributed data, t-test and one-way ANOVA was used for two-group and three-group comparisons, respectively. For data that did not conform to a normal distribution and/or equal variance, the Kruskale-Wallis test was used for comparisons. Differences were considered significant if P < 0.05. The data are presented as the mean ± SEM (standard error of the mean).
