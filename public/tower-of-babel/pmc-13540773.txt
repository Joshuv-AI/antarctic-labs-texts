Surgical strategy to minimize dominant hemisphere retraction in an asymmetric giant tuberculum sellae meningioma with contralateral anterior cerebral artery positioning: A case report
Authors: Uygun Altibayev, Jakhongirmirzo Yoldoshev, Gayrat Kariev, Alvaro Campero
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Large asymmetric tuberculum sellae meningiomas are technically demanding anterior skull base lesions because of their close relationships with the optic apparatus and the anterior cerebral artery–anterior communicating artery complex (ACA-AComA). Although tumor lateralization is commonly considered during approach selection, it may be insufficient as an isolated determinant in cases with contralateral vascular displacement and marked peritumoral edema involving the dominant frontal lobe. In selected complex cases, integrating vascular anatomy, edema distribution, and baseline functional vulnerability may help refine surgical planning.Case description: We report the case of a 44-year-old male patient with an asymmetric giant tuberculum sellae meningioma measuring 52 × 49 × 49 mm, associated with optic chiasm compression, contralateral displacement of the ACA-AComA complex, and pronounced peritumoral edema of the dominant left frontal lobe. Clinically, the patient presented with progressive visual deterioration and moderate frontal-executive cognitive impairment. Neuropsychological testing did not independently determine the surgical corridor, but complemented imaging by confirming baseline dominant frontal network vulnerability. Based on the combined assessment of vascular geometry, edema distribution, tumor size, and functional considerations, an extended bifrontal craniotomy was selected to allow early bilateral visualization of the anterior cerebral arteries and to reduce additional traction-related stress on the dominant edematous frontal lobe. Controlled cerebrospinal fluid management was performed using intraoperative ventricular drainage followed by postoperative lumbar drainage. Maximally safe near-total resection was achieved without vascular complications. A very thin adherent tumor layer was intentionally preserved along the densely adherent ACA-AComA region, with no radiographically appreciable residual enhancing tumor on early postoperative magnetic resonance imaging. Early postoperative mild frontal-executive worsening was observed, followed by recovery to baseline by 3 months, together with gradual improvement in visual function.Conclusion: This case illustrates that, in selected giant asymmetric tuberculum sellae meningiomas, surgical planning may benefit from explicit consideration of contralateral ACA-AComA displacement, dominant frontal edema, and preoperative frontal-executive vulnerability rather than tumor lateralization alone. The favorable postoperative course should be interpreted cautiously and should not be considered evidence of superiority of the bifrontal approach over unilateral transcranial or endoscopic routes. This report provides a hypothesis-generating example of functional–vascular surgical planning in a complex anterior skull base tumor.

INTRODUCTION

Tuberculum sellae meningiomas are anterior skull base tumors that remain surgically demanding because of the narrow operative corridor and their close anatomical relationships with the optic nerves, optic chiasm, and anterior cerebral artery-anterior communicating artery complex (ACA-AComA). These features increase the risk of visual deterioration and vascular injury, making the principal surgical objectives maximally safe tumor resection, decompression of the visual apparatus, and preservation of critical neurovascular structures.[1-6,8,10,12,15,18,19,21,25]

Selection of the optimal surgical approach for tuberculum sellae meningiomas is generally individualized and may be influenced by tumor size, direction of extension, optic canal involvement, preoperative visual status, vascular relationships, frontal sinus anatomy, surgeon experience, and the relative advantages of transcranial or endoscopic corridors.[3,8-10,12,15,17,19,25,28,29] Although tumor lateralization is often considered during operative planning, in large and asymmetric lesions, the side of maximal tumor extension does not necessarily correspond to the safest surgical trajectory. In such cases, the relationship between the tumor and the ACA-AComA complex, the degree and distribution of peritumoral edema, and the functional vulnerability of the frontal lobes may become particularly relevant factors in approach selection.

The microanatomy of the ACA-AComA complex is highly variable, and early identification of arterial segments may be important when the tumor capsule is densely adherent to displaced vessels. Unilateral transcranial, endoscopic endonasal, and other limited approaches may be appropriate in selected tuberculum sellae meningiomas; however, when the ACA-AComA complex is displaced contralaterally or partially obscured by the tumor, a unilateral corridor may provide limited control of contralateral vascular structures. Under these specific anatomical conditions, an extended bifrontal interhemispheric approach may be considered one possible strategy to obtain early bilateral vascular visualization and to distribute retraction forces away from the more vulnerable frontal lobe.[4,15,17,24,28,29] This should not be interpreted as a general superiority of the bifrontal route, but rather as an anatomy-dependent option in selected cases.

Postoperative cognitive changes after anterior skull base surgery are also relevant to operative planning, particularly when the dominant frontal lobe is affected by edema or chronic compression. Such changes are likely multifactorial and may reflect varying contributions of surgical manipulation, frontal lobe retraction, edema evolution, perfusion changes, fatigue, medications, and postoperative recovery dynamics.[16,27] Preoperative cognitive screening may therefore complement anatomical imaging by identifying baseline frontal-executive vulnerability, although it should not be regarded as an independent determinant of the surgical corridor.

Here, we present the surgical decision-making process in a patient with an asymmetric giant tuberculum sellae meningioma associated with contralateral displacement of the ACA-AComA complex and marked peritumoral edema of the dominant left frontal lobe. In this case, operative planning was guided not by tumor lateralization alone but by the combined assessment of vascular geometry, anticipated interhemispheric traction, dominant frontal lobe vulnerability, and the need for controlled cerebrospinal fluid management. Given the severity of peritumoral edema, intracranial pressure reduction was achieved through intraoperative ventricular puncture of the frontal horn, followed by postoperative lumbar drainage.

The specific contribution of this report is not the general concept of individualized approach selection, which is well established in skull base surgery, but the explicit prioritization of three converging factors in a single complex case: Contralateral ACAAComA displacement, ipsilateral dominant frontal edema, and preoperative frontal-executive vulnerability. This case is therefore presented as a hypothesis-generating illustration of functional–vascular surgical planning in a selected giant asymmetric tuberculum sellae meningioma, rather than as evidence of superiority over alternative approaches.

CASE PRESENTATION

A 44-year-old male patient (T.Q.) was admitted to the Republican Specialized Scientific and Practical Medical Center of Neurosurgery, Tashkent, Uzbekistan, with a 6-month history of progressive memory impairment, headaches, speech difficulties, gradual deterioration of visual function, decreased olfaction, and generalized weakness. His past medical history was unremarkable, with no previously diagnosed systemic disease, prior cranial surgery, or known oncological history. He was not taking any regular medications before admission. There was no history of alcohol abuse or recreational drug use. On admission, the patient’s functional status, assessed using the Karnofsky Performance Scale, was 80.

The patient was right-handed according to the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory. In combination with the presence of expressive language disturbances, this supported left hemispheric dominance. Formal neuropsychological assessment was performed as part of the preoperative evaluation to characterize baseline cognitive status. These findings did not independently determine the surgical corridor, but complemented neuroimaging by confirming preoperative frontal-executive vulnerability of the dominant hemisphere.

Ophthalmological assessment

Preoperative ophthalmological examination revealed bilateral reduction in visual acuity: oculus dexter; right eye (OD) 0.3 [approximately 20/60] and oculus sinister; left eye (OS) 0.4 [approximately 20/50]. Goldmann perimetry demonstrated incomplete asymmetric bitemporal hemianopia, more pronounced on the right side, consistent with compression of the inferior aspect of the optic chiasm. Fundoscopic examination showed asymmetric papilledematous changes, clinically graded as Grade III in the left eye and Grade II in the right eye, together with early temporal pallor of both optic discs, suggesting evolving secondary optic atrophy.

Baseline optical coherence tomography (OCT; Disc 3D with retinal nerve fiber layer [RNFL] analysis) demonstrated structural involvement of both optic nerves. The most prominent finding was thinning of the RNFL in the superior quadrants bilaterally, consistent with chronic chiasmal compression [Figure 1]. These preoperative OCT findings were interpreted as evidence of chronic optic pathway involvement and were used to characterize the severity of preoperative visual compromise, rather than as a postoperative measure of recovery.

Optical coherence tomography of the optic nerve heads with retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) analysis demonstrating asymmetric papilledema—Grade III in the left eye (OS; oculus sinister) and Grade II in the right eye (OD; oculus dexter)—and bilateral thinning of the superior RNFL quadrants, consistent with chronic chiasmal compression.

Thickness and deviation maps demonstrated pathological changes in the corresponding regions, whereas clock-hour RNFL analysis revealed predominant fiber thinning in the 11–1 o’clock sectors. This pattern was consistent with chronic compression of the inferior aspect of the optic chiasm. The temporal sectors were relatively preserved, corresponding to the clinical pattern of incomplete bitemporal hemianopia.

The cup-to-disc ratio did not reach values typical of glaucomatous optic neuropathy. However, in combination with marked RNFL thinning, these findings suggested evolving secondary optic atrophy related to prolonged chiasmal compression. On OCT B-scans, no significant elevation of the optic discs was observed, supporting a transition from predominantly congestive changes toward an atrophic stage.

Overall, the preoperative ophthalmological profile reflected asymmetric papilledematous involvement of the optic nerve heads, more pronounced in the left eye, together with signs of developing secondary optic atrophy. These findings were concordant with the neuroimaging evidence of chronic chiasmal compression and suggested that structural optic nerve damage might limit the potential for complete visual recovery. Because postoperative OCT was not performed, this interpretation was based on baseline preoperative OCT findings and should be considered cautiously.

Neuroimaging

Preoperative noncontrast and contrast-enhanced brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated a large extra-axial mass arising from the tuberculum sellae region. The lesion had well-defined margins, a predominantly solid structure, and intense homogeneous contrast enhancement, radiologically consistent with meningioma. The tumor measured 52 × 49 × 49 mm, corresponding to a giant tuberculum sellae meningioma [Figure 2].

Sagittal and coronal images demonstrated marked suprasellar extension of the lesion with elevation and deformation of the optic chiasm. The mass compressed the floor of the third ventricle and produced mild distortion of the adjacent cerebrospinal fluid pathways, without radiological evidence of acute obstructive hydrocephalus. Axial images revealed an asymmetric predominant extension of the tumor toward the left side, accompanied by pronounced peritumoral edema involving the left frontal lobe.

T2-weighted sequences confirmed extensive vasogenic edema within the left frontal lobe, with compression of adjacent cortical and subcortical structures. On contrast-enhanced T1-weighted images, the tumor showed uniform enhancement and a broad dural attachment at the tuberculum sellae, consistent with a radiological diagnosis of meningioma.

The lesion was in close anatomical relationship with the optic nerves, optic chiasm, and proximal anterior cerebral artery segments. These findings indicated a complex neurovascular configuration rather than simple tumor lateralization alone and were considered relevant for preoperative assessment of visual, vascular, and frontal lobe traction-related risks.

Overall, neuroimaging findings were consistent with an asymmetric giant tuberculum sellae meningioma with suprasellar extension, optic chiasm compression, marked left frontal peritumoral edema, and close proximity to the anterior circulation vessels.

Diffusion-weighted imaging demonstrated increased signal intensity within the left frontal lobe corresponding to the region of pronounced peritumoral edema. However, no corresponding diffusion restriction was identified on apparent diffusion coefficient maps, supporting the interpretation of vasogenic edema and excluding acute ischemic injury at the preoperative stage [Figure 3].

Multislice computed tomography angiography (CTA) of the brain demonstrated close spatial relationships between the tumor and the arteries of the anterior circulation. Sagittal, axial, and coronal reconstructions revealed rightward displacement of the proximal segments (A1) of both anterior cerebral arteries secondary to tumor mass effect. Deformation of the anterior communicating artery was also observed, without evidence of vascular occlusion [Figure 4].

Three-dimensional CTA reconstruction demonstrated asymmetric spatial configuration of the vascular structures, with right-sided predominance of the ACA-AComA complex and preserved patency of the major cerebral arteries. Visualization of both A1 segments suggested close apposition to the tumor capsule, indicating a potentially increased risk of intraoperative vascular injury with a unilateral surgical approach.

EEG recordings revealed moderate diffuse abnormalities of bioelectrical activity with predominance of slow-wave components in the frontal leads, more pronounced on the left. Neuropsychological status

Preoperative neuropsychological assessment was performed to characterize the patient’s baseline cognitive status and to evaluate frontal-executive function in the context of marked left frontal peritumoral edema. The assessment revealed moderate cognitive impairment, predominantly of frontal– subcortical type.

On the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), the total score ranged from 21 to 23 out of 30, consistent with moderate cognitive decline. The most pronounced deficits were observed in attention, executive functions, working memory, and processing speed, whereas orientation and basic mnemonic functions were relatively preserved.

On the trail-making test (TMT), completion times for Part A ranged from 60 to 75 s and for Part B from 140 to 180 s. These values exceeded age-adjusted normative ranges and indicated psychomotor slowing, reduced visuomotor coordination, and impaired cognitive flexibility and attentional shifting. The disproportionate prolongation of TMT-B compared with TMT-A supported the presence of executive dysfunction, consistent with involvement of medial basal frontal and frontostriatal networks.

Collectively, these findings were interpreted as a moderate frontal-executive neuropsychological syndrome, most likely related to chronic compression of frontal structures and disruption of frontal network integration. Given the patient’s right-handedness, expressive language disturbance, and presumed left hemispheric dominance, the preoperative cognitive profile was considered an additional marker of functional vulnerability of the dominant frontal lobe.

Importantly, neuropsychological testing did not independently determine the surgical corridor. Rather, it complemented neuroimaging by confirming baseline frontal-executive vulnerability in a patient who already demonstrated marked left frontal edema and complex ACAAComA displacement. Therefore, these findings reinforced the surgical objective of minimizing traction-related stress on the dominant left frontal lobe during approach selection and intraoperative planning.

Surgical technique

Selection of the surgical strategy was based on the combined assessment of anatomical, vascular, radiological, and functional factors rather than on tumor lateralization alone. The following features were considered relevant during preoperative planning:

Contralateral displacement of the ACA-AComA complex and close apposition of the proximal anterior cerebral artery segments to the tumor capsule on CTA, raising concern for limited contralateral vascular control through a unilateral corridor.

Marked peritumoral edema involves the dominant left frontal lobe, suggesting increased susceptibility to traction-related functional disturbance.

Preoperative frontal-executive impairment on the MoCA and TMT did not independently determine the surgical corridor but support the presence of baseline functional vulnerability of frontal networks.

Large tumor size and asymmetric extension made early bilateral vascular visualization an important consideration during operative planning.

The convergence of these factors supported the selection of an extended bifrontal craniotomy as an individualized operative strategy for this specific anatomical and functional setting. The aim was to obtain early bilateral visualization of the anterior cerebral arteries, reduce uncontrolled traction on the dominant edematous frontal lobe, and allow controlled management of cerebrospinal fluid dynamics.

Given the asymmetric tumor configuration, contralateral positioning of the proximal anterior cerebral artery segments, and marked peritumoral edema of the left frontal lobe, surgical planning was guided by vascular geometry and dominant frontal lobe vulnerability in addition to tumor lateralization. This rationale favored an extended bifrontal approach, while acknowledging that unilateral or endoscopic approaches may be appropriate in other tuberculum sellae meningiomas depending on the specific anatomical configuration.

The patient was positioned supine with the head fixed in slight extension on a headrest. To reduce intracranial pressure, facilitate brain relaxation, and minimize frontal lobe retraction, a temporary ventricular drain was placed into the frontal horn of the right lateral ventricle before craniotomy.

Two burr holes were placed parallel to the superior sagittal sinus, anterior to the bregma. One burr hole was created bilaterally at the intersection of the temporal line and coronal suture, additional lateral key burr holes were placed, and an auxiliary burr hole was made above the glabella, taking into account the patient’s small frontal sinus volume. After connecting the burr holes, an extended bifrontal bone flap was fashioned. The dura mater was opened in a U-shaped fashion with its base oriented toward the superior sagittal sinus.

Interhemispheric dissection was performed under microscopic visualization with gentle separation of the frontal lobes. Retraction was applied cautiously and predominantly from the right frontal side when needed, with the intention of reducing additional stress on the edematous dominant left frontal lobe. Initial internal debulking of the central tumor component was then performed to decrease tumor volume and facilitate safer capsule mobilization.

Subsequently, the anterior cerebral arteries and their branches were identified under direct microscopic visualization. Both proximal and distal ACA segments were carefully dissected from the tumor surface, where a safe arachnoid plane was available. Dense adhesion of the proximal ACA segments to the tumor capsule was encountered intraoperatively, confirming the preoperative concern raised by CTA regarding the complex vascular relationship of the lesion [Figure 9].

This operative strategy allowed early delineation of the spatial relationships between the tumor capsule and the ACA-AComA complex before major tumor mobilization. It also provided the opportunity for early bilateral vascular visualization and vascular control if required. Retraction was applied cautiously and predominantly toward the right frontal lobe, with the intention of reducing additional mechanical stress on the edematous dominant left hemisphere.

After central tumor debulking and right-sided identification of both anterior cerebral arteries, microsurgical mobilization of the tumor capsule was continued under direct visualization. Intraoperative contact ultrasound Doppler assessment of the A1 and A2 segments of both anterior cerebral arteries was performed. Vessel identification was achieved using microscopic anatomical landmarks of the suprasellar region and the ACA-AComA complex.

Intraoperative Doppler demonstrated asymmetry of flow velocities within the anterior cerebral artery segments, with higher velocity in the right A2 segment than in the left A2 segment: 64 cm/s versus 49 cm/s, respectively. Reduced flow parameters were also observed in the left A1 segment, consistent with the preoperative CTA finding of left A1 hypoplasia. These Doppler data were interpreted descriptively and were not used as independent evidence of hemispheric perfusion status. However, they were concordant with the preoperative vascular anatomy and reinforced the importance of careful bilateral identification and gentle microsurgical handling of the ACA-AComA complex.

Tumor removal was performed in a stepwise fashion, beginning with internal decompression using an ultrasonic aspirator, followed by gradual circumferential mobilization of the tumor capsule. In the suprasellar region, meticulous microdissection was performed to separate the tumor from the optic nerves and optic chiasm while preserving arachnoid planes wherever possible.

A maximally safe near-total resection was achieved under direct microscopic visualization. In areas of dense adhesion to both A1 segments of the anterior cerebral arteries and the anterior communicating artery, a very thin adherent tumor layer was intentionally preserved to avoid vascular injury. Thus, the extent of resection was classified as near-total rather than gross-total resection. After removal of the main tumor volume, coagulation of the dural attachment at the tuberculum sellae was performed. Hemostasis was secured, the anterior cranial fossa was sealed, and the dura mater was closed in a watertight fashion.

Before replacement of the bone flap, the temporary ventricular drain from the right frontal horn was removed, and a small dural defect was additionally reinforced to reduce the risk of cerebrospinal fluid leakage. Before final fixation of the bone flap, the frontal sinus was re-irrigated; its cavity was packed with a fat graft and covered with aponeurosis, followed by standard repositioning and fixation of the bone flap.

Given the extensive bifrontal exposure, opening of the frontal sinus, and pronounced preoperative frontal edema, a lumbar drain was placed 14 hours postoperatively as part of the planned cerebrospinal fluid management strategy. This was intended to support controlled postoperative cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) diversion and reduce the risk of CSF-related wound complications.

RESULTS

Early postoperative course and neurological status

During the early postoperative period, the patient’s general condition remained stable. Consciousness was preserved, and no new focal neurological deficits were observed. Neurological status did not deteriorate compared with the preoperative baseline.

Ophthalmological evaluation performed on postoperative day 3 demonstrated preservation of visual function at the preoperative level, with visual acuity of OD 0.3 [approximately 20/60] and OS 0.4 [approximately 20/50]. Incomplete asymmetric bitemporal hemianopia persisted, but no early postoperative worsening of the visual field defect was detected. No clinical evidence of progression of papilledematous changes was observed.

Follow-up ophthalmological examinations at 4 weeks and 3 months demonstrated gradual improvement in visual function. At 4 weeks, visual acuity improved to OD 0.5 [approximately 20/40] and OS 0.6 [approximately 20/33], accompanied by partial regression of the bitemporal visual field defect. At 3 months, further improvement was documented, with visual acuity reaching OD 0.6 [approximately 20/33] and OS 0.7 [approximately 20/28], together with reduced severity of chiasmal syndrome on perimetric assessment.

Postoperative OCT was not performed; therefore, structural postoperative changes in the RNFL were not assessed. Visual recovery was evaluated using visual acuity and perimetric follow-up.

Follow-up neuroimaging

Postoperative brain CT performed 11 hours after surgery demonstrated expected postoperative changes with formation of a resection cavity in the suprasellar region. No intracerebral or subarachnoid hemorrhage was detected. There was no significant progression of left frontal peritumoral edema, no clinically relevant mass effect, and no imaging evidence of acute obstructive hydrocephalus [Figure 10].

Contrast-enhanced MRI performed 52 h postoperatively demonstrated expected postoperative changes after maximally safe near-total tumor resection. Intraoperatively, a very thin adherent tumor layer had been intentionally preserved along the densely adherent ACA-AComA region to avoid vascular injury. Therefore, the extent of resection was classified as near-total resection, corresponding most appropriately to Simpson Grade IV. Although this intraoperative finding precluded classification as gross-total or Simpson Grade II resection, no radiographically appreciable residual enhancing tumor was identified within the operative field on early postoperative MRI. Adequate decompression of the Chiasmal–Suprasellar region was achieved. No pathological contrast enhancement suggestive of measurable residual disease was detected. Regression of left frontal peritumoral edema was observed, together with mild expected postoperative soft tissue changes [Figure 11].

Neuropsychological outcome

On postoperative day 3, repeat neuropsychological assessment demonstrated mild transient worsening of frontal-executive performance. The MoCA score decreased slightly to 20–21/30 compared with the preoperative range of 21–23/30. On the TMT, moderate slowing was observed, with completion times of 65–75 s for Part A and 150–170 s for Part B. Clinically, these changes were manifested by a mild reduction in initiative and psychomotor speed.

These early postoperative cognitive changes were considered transient in the clinical course; however, their exact mechanism could not be determined from a single case. Potential contributing factors include frontal lobe manipulation, retraction-related network disturbance, postoperative edema dynamics, transient perfusion changes, fatigue, medication effects, and general postoperative recovery.

By postoperative day 7, partial improvement in cognitive performance was observed. The MoCA score increased to 21–22/30, TMT-A improved to 60–65 seconds, and TMT-B improved to 140–155 s. The patient was discharged home in satisfactory condition.

At the 4-week follow-up, further improvement in neuropsychological parameters was documented, with MoCA scores of 22–23/30, TMT-A completion times of 55–60 s, and TMT-B completion times of 130–140 s, accompanied by clinical regression of frontal symptoms.

At 3 months postoperatively, neuropsychological status had largely returned to the preoperative baseline level. MoCA scores reached 23–24/30, TMT-A completion times improved to 50–55 s, and TMT-B completion times improved to 120– 130 s. No clinically significant frontal-executive deficit was detected at this follow-up. These findings suggest recovery of early postoperative cognitive worsening but should not be interpreted as definitive evidence that the selected surgical strategy independently determined the favorable cognitive outcome [Figure 12].

Histopathological findings

Histological examination of the resected tumor specimen revealed morphological features consistent with fibrous meningioma, including prominent collagen-rich stromal architecture and spindle-shaped tumor cells arranged in fascicular patterns. No significant cytological atypia, necrosis, brain invasion, or increased mitotic activity was identified.

Immunohistochemical analysis was not performed; therefore, the MIB-1/Ki-67 labeling index was not assessed. The diagnosis was established based on characteristic routine histopathological features. Collectively, these findings were consistent with fibrous meningioma, corresponding to World Health Organization (WHO) Grade I [Figure 13].

Plan for long-term follow-up

Long-term follow-up is planned to monitor for delayed tumor recurrence or growth of any radiographically occult residual tumor, as well as to assess visual and neuropsychological recovery. The patient will undergo contrast-enhanced brain MRI at 6 and 12 months after surgery and annually thereafter, unless earlier imaging is indicated by new neurological, visual, or cognitive symptoms. Ophthalmological follow-up, including visual acuity testing and perimetry, is planned at the same intervals, with optical coherence tomography to be repeated if clinically indicated. Neuropsychological follow-up will also be continued at 6 and 12 months, and then according to clinical status, with particular attention to frontal-executive function. Considering the confirmed histological diagnosis of fibrous meningioma, WHO Grade I, the maximally safe near-total resection, the absence of radiographically measurable residual enhancing tumor on early postoperative MRI, and the patient’s stable postoperative course, adjuvant radiotherapy has not been considered at this stage according to our institutional practice. Continued MRI surveillance remains planned to detect any delayed recurrence or residual tumor growth.

DISCUSSION

Tuberculum sellae meningiomas are surgically demanding anterior skull base tumors because of their close anatomical relationships with the optic nerves, optic chiasm, pituitary stalk, and ACA-AComA complex.[2-6,10,12,15,18,19,25] The main surgical objectives are maximal safe resection, decompression of the visual apparatus, preservation of vascular structures, and avoidance of unnecessary frontal lobe manipulation.

Approach selection for tuberculum sellae meningiomas is inherently individualized. Tumor size, direction of extension, optic canal involvement, vascular displacement or encasement, frontal sinus anatomy, preoperative visual status, brain edema, and surgeon experience may all influence the choice between unilateral transcranial, bifrontal/interhemispheric, and endoscopic endonasal routes.[3,8-10,12,15,17,19,24,25,28,29] Therefore, tumor lateralization represents only one component of surgical planning and should not be considered an isolated determinant of the operative corridor, particularly in giant or markedly asymmetric lesions.

In the present case, the tumor extended predominantly toward the left side; however, the operative strategy was not based on tumor lateralization alone. Preoperative CTA demonstrated rightward displacement and asymmetry of the ACA-AComA complex, whereas MRI showed pronounced peritumoral edema involving the dominant left frontal lobe. In addition, preoperative neuropsychological assessment demonstrated frontal-executive impairment, which was interpreted as an additional marker of dominant frontal network vulnerability rather than an independent determinant of the surgical corridor.

The specific contribution of this case is the explicit integration of three converging factors during operative planning: contralateral ACA-AComA displacement, ipsilateral dominant frontal edema, and preoperative frontal-executive vulnerability. This combination favored an extended bifrontal approach in this particular anatomical and functional setting, with the aim of obtaining early bilateral vascular visualization and reducing additional retraction-related stress on the dominant edematous frontal lobe.

An extended bifrontal craniotomy was therefore selected [Table 1]. In this case, the approach provided bilateral exposure of the ACA-AComA complex before major tumor mobilization and allowed retraction to be applied cautiously and predominantly toward the nondominant right frontal side when necessary. Early identification of proximal and distal ACA segments helped clarify the relationship between the tumor capsule and adjacent vessels and guided subsequent microsurgical dissection. However, this observation should be interpreted as case-specific and should not be regarded as evidence that the bifrontal approach is generally superior to unilateral or endoscopic approaches. In appropriately selected tuberculum sellae meningiomas, unilateral transcranial or endoscopic endonasal routes may remain suitable and effective options.

An important component of the operative strategy was CSF dynamics management. In the setting of a large suprasellar lesion and pronounced edema of the dominant left frontal lobe, preoperative lumbar drainage was avoided because caudal CSF decompression might theoretically increase intracranial shift or vascular tension in selected cases. Instead, intraoperative ventricular puncture of the frontal horn was used to achieve controlled brain relaxation during the approach. Postoperative lumbar drainage was subsequently instituted after a wide bifrontal craniotomy and frontal sinus opening as part of the planned CSF management strategy, with the intention of supporting controlled postoperative CSF diversion and reducing CSF-related wound complications.[24]

The transient frontal-executive disturbances observed in the early postoperative period should be interpreted cautiously. Although the mild decline in MoCA and TMT performance on postoperative day 3 was followed by gradual improvement over subsequent weeks and return to baseline by 3 months, the mechanism of this cognitive fluctuation cannot be determined from a single case. Possible contributing factors include frontal lobe manipulation, retraction-related network disturbance, postoperative edema dynamics, transient perfusion changes, fatigue, medication effects, and general postoperative recovery. Therefore, the observed cognitive recovery may be consistent with transient frontal network dysfunction, but it should not be considered definitive evidence that the selected surgical strategy independently protected cognitive function.

Gradual postoperative improvement in visual function was consistent with effective decompression of the chiasmal– suprasellar region. However, the extent of visual recovery after chronic chiasmal compression is influenced by multiple factors, including duration of symptoms, preoperative visual impairment, optic nerve structural damage, and RNFL status. In the present case, preoperative OCT demonstrated RNFL thinning, suggesting chronic optic pathway involvement. Because postoperative OCT was not performed, the role of RNFL loss as a limiting factor for visual recovery was inferred from baseline preoperative OCT findings and should be interpreted cautiously.[20,22,23,26]

In keeping with the available literature, this clinical case suggests that, in asymmetric giant tuberculum sellae meningiomas, surgical decision-making based solely on tumor lateralization may be insufficient.[3,8-10,12,15,17,19,24,25,28,29] This observation should be understood as a case-based institutional interpretation that is consistent with the available literature on individualized approach selection, vascular relationships, visual pathway status, and cognitive vulnerability in anterior skull base surgery. In selected complex cases, a broader multifactorial assessment incorporating contralateral ACA-AComA displacement, peritumoral edema distribution, visual pathway involvement, and baseline frontal-executive vulnerability may help refine surgical planning.[7,11,13,14] In the present patient, this integrated assessment supported the selection of an extended bifrontal approach in a specific anatomical and functional context. However, because this report describes a single case, these observations should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and should not be extrapolated as evidence of the general superiority of the bifrontal route over unilateral transcranial or endoscopic approaches, which remain appropriate options depending on tumor configuration, vascular anatomy, optic canal involvement, and surgeon experience.

Although this is a single-case experience, it provides a hypothesis-generating illustration of functional–vascular surgical planning for a complex asymmetric tuberculum sellae meningioma. The proposed strategy may be useful as an operative planning concept in selected cases, but its reproducibility and effect on visual, vascular, and cognitive outcomes require evaluation in larger comparative series.

CONCLUSION

In giant asymmetric tuberculum sellae meningiomas, tumor lateralization alone may be insufficient for selecting the safest surgical corridor. This case illustrates that operative planning may benefit from the combined assessment of vascular geometry, peritumoral edema distribution, and functional vulnerability of the dominant frontal lobe.

In the present patient, contralateral displacement of the ACA-AComA complex, marked edema of the dominant left frontal lobe, and preoperative frontal-executive impairment favored selection of an extended bifrontal approach with planned cerebrospinal fluid management. This strategy allowed early bilateral visualization of the anterior cerebral arteries, controlled tumor debulking, and maximally safe near-total resection with no radiographically appreciable residual enhancing tumor on early postoperative MRI.

The favorable postoperative visual and cognitive course observed in this case should be interpreted cautiously and should not be considered evidence of the superiority of the bifrontal approach over unilateral transcranial or endoscopic routes. Rather, this case provides a hypothesis-generating example of functional–vascular surgical planning in a selected complex anterior skull base tumor. Larger comparative studies are required to determine whether this planning concept can improve visual, vascular, or cognitive outcomes in broader patient populations.
