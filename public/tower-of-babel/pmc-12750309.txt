Experimental design and control methods for dose rate optimization in a synchrotron‐based proton therapy beamline
Authors: Jason Tang, Daniel Liu, Jacob Mathew, Sabrina Campelo, Lawrence Bronk, Paige Taylor, Yuting Li, Fada Guan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUltra‐high dose rate radiotherapy (typically defined >40 Gy/s) has shown promise for enhanced normal tissue sparing while maintaining tumor control, termed the FLASH effect when this biological response is observed. However, precise dose rate control remains a technical challenge in proton therapy systems, particularly for research applications investigating dose rate effects.PurposeTo develop and validate a passive beam modulation technique using tungsten scatterers for controllable dose rate adjustment in a synchrotron‐based proton therapy beamline without modifying accelerator parameters.MethodsWe investigated dose rate modulation using tungsten foils of varying thicknesses (0.1‐3.5 mm) positioned upstream in an 87.2 MeV experimental proton beamline. Monte Carlo simulations using Geant4 were performed to model the dose rate variation as a function of the tungsten scatter's thickness. Experimental validation was conducted using an Advanced Markus ionization chamber for dose rate measurements and EBT‐XD radiochromic films for lateral dose profile analysis. Five tungsten thicknesses were tested with full‐spill deliveries (∼1400 monitor units (MUs), and ∼100 milliseconds (ms) pulse width).ResultsAn inverse exponential relationship between dose rate and scatterer thickness was observed, with measured dose rates ranging from 288.9±0.7 Gy/s (0.1 mm tungsten) to 10.2±0.2 Gy/s (3.5 mm tungsten). Experimental measurements validated the accuracy of Monte Carlo predictions at the standard condition (1400 MU delivered in 100 ms) by falling within the 95% confidence intervals. Lateral beam profiles demonstrated progressive broadening with increased scatterer thickness, and the film measurements showed good agreements with Monte Carlo simulation (<2% difference at beam center).ConclusionsTungsten scatterer thickness modulation provides a practical, controllable method for dose rate adjustment spanning conventional to FLASH regimes. This passive approach enables precise dose rate control for preclinical radiobiological research without requiring modifications of accelerator structure and parameters.

INTRODUCTION

Proton therapy has emerged as a promising modality in radiation oncology due to its distinct physical properties, particularly its Bragg peak behavior, which allows for highly conformal dose distributions with minimal exit dose to surrounding healthy tissues. 1 This characteristic enables superior sparing of normal tissues and critical structures compared to photon‐based radiotherapy and other conventional modalities. Additionally, proton therapy may offer radiobiological advantages through enhanced relative biological effectiveness (RBE) compared to photon‐based radiotherapy, though current clinical practice applies a constant RBE of 1.1 despite experimental evidence of spatial and beam quality such as linear energy transfer (LET) and microdosimetry‐dependent RBE variations along the beam path. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5

In recent years, interest in proton therapy has been further amplified by investigations into ultra‐high dose rate radiotherapy, which has demonstrated potential to enhance normal tissue sparing while maintaining tumor control. When this enhanced normal tissue sparing is observed at ultra‐high dose rates (typically >40 Gy/s), the biological phenomenon is termed the FLASH effect. 6 However, it is important to note that ultra‐high dose rate delivery does not automatically guarantee FLASH effects, as the biological response depends on multiple factors beyond dose rate alone. This distinction was highlighted in a recent preclinical study using approximately 200 Gy/s 7 passive‐scattering proton therapy in mice, which failed to demonstrate the expected FLASH effect despite achieving ultra‐high dose rates. 8 Such findings question the universality of the 40 Gy/s threshold and motivate systematic exploration of dose rate parameters to identify optimal conditions for improved preclinical and clinical outcomes.

Despite these promising developments, a significant barrier to clinical translation of ultra‐high dose rate proton therapy lies in the technical limitations of beam delivery systems, particularly in controlling the ultra‐high dose rates required for investigating potential FLASH effects. Existing proton beamlines face substantial challenges in dose rate modulation due to limitations in spot delivery times, beam stability at ultra‐high dose rates, and the inherent pulsed time structure of synchrotron accelerator output. 9 , 10 Current strategies to modulate dose rates often involve adjusting beam current or modifying accelerator parameters, which may not be feasible in all clinical contexts and can adversely affect other critical beam characteristics including energy stability, temporal beam structure, and spatial beam uniformity.

To address this challenge, we adopted a passive beam modulation technique that can be implemented without altering the primary accelerator system or adjusting beam current. This approach utilizes scatterers positioned upstream to modify the spatial and temporal characteristics of the proton beam. By increasing the scatterer thickness, one can broaden the lateral beam profile and effectively reduce the proton fluence and dose rate through beam attenuation and enhanced multiple scattering, without requiring modifications to upstream beamline components or accelerator parameters.

In this study, we propose and investigate tungsten scatterer thickness modulation as a practical and effective approach for dose rate control in proton therapy. Through a combination of Monte Carlo simulations using Geant4 and experimental validation using advanced dosimetric techniques, we evaluate the quantitative relationship between scatterer thickness and dose rate, as well as its effects on beam spatial distribution. This work aims to establish the experimental infrastructure necessary for systematic preclinical studies investigating the biological effects of varying proton dose rates, ultimately supporting the identification of optimal dose rate parameters for clinical translation of ultra‐high dose rate proton therapy. 11

METHODS

Experimental rationale and background

In a prior experimental setup, a 0.3 mm tungsten foil (ρ = 19.3 g/cm3) was employed as the first scatterer (retractable) in a synchrotron‐based horizontal proton beamline (Hitachi, Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) for preclinical studies, 10 as illustrated in Figure 1. This beamline delivers a single beam energy of 87.2 MeV (nominal energy provided by manufacturer). When the first scatterer is deployed, the dose rate at the isocenter is reduced from approximately 1,000 Gy/s to 150 Gy/s. 9 , 10

Experimental beamline with extractable first scatterer and external second scatterer.

Inspired by this configuration, the current study employs a strategy of varying tungsten scatterer thicknesses to systematically modulate the dose rate, as shown in Figure 1. During experiments with the external second scatterer, the original first scatterer was retracted from the beamline to isolate the effects of the variable thickness scatterer.

Monte carlo simulation

Geant4 12 , 13 Monte Carlo simulations of different tungsten scatterer thicknesses were performed based on our previously validated Monte Carlo model of this proton beamline. 10 Figure 2 illustrates the Monte Carlo modeling of geometry and particle trajectories (100 primary proton histories) without any scatterer and with different second tungsten scatterer thicknesses, clearly demonstrating the increased lateral scattering of the proton beam with increasing tungsten foil thickness. For quantitative dose rate calculations and predictions presented in this study, 108 histories were simulated to ensure adequate statistical precision (<1% statistical uncertainty in the dose calculations).

Monte Carlo modeling of the geometry and particle trajectories (100 primary proton histories) without any scatterer and with different second scatterer (made of tungsten) thicknesses. Color scheme for particles: protons – blue, electrons – red, photons – green, and neutrons – yellow.

Experimental setup

A custom experimental configuration was developed to evaluate dose rate modulation using varying tungsten scatterer thicknesses in the horizontal beam delivery system. The 87.2 MeV proton beam was extracted from the synchrotron with typical full spill durations of 2 seconds and pulse‐on times of approximately 100 milliseconds.

The physical arrangement in the beamline, from entrance to exit, included the following components in sequence: an entrance window (0.05 mm thick stainless‐steel foil), a profile monitor (multi‐wire ion chamber), a reference monitor (multi‐wire ion chamber), an external tungsten scatterer (varying thickness), a large‐aperture collimator (10 cm diameter), a 1 mm plastic water‐equivalent buildup layer, an Advanced Markus ionization chamber (PTW, Freiburg, Germany) without its protective cap, and a downstream plastic water phantom (Figure 3A).

(A) Illustration of entrance dose measurement using Advanced Markus chamber (protection cap off). (B) Illustration of entrance proton beam profile measurement using EBT‐XD film.

The tungsten scatterer was positioned 14 cm downstream from the entrance window to optimize beam scattering while maintaining sufficient drift distance for beam broadening. Five distinct tungsten foil thicknesses were tested: 0.1 mm, 0.4 mm, 1.0 mm, 2.0 mm, and 3.5 mm. Actual measured thicknesses were 0.100±0.001 mm, 0.414±0.002 mm, 1.020±0.006 mm, 2.031±0.028 mm, and 3.565±0.028 mm, respectively, measured at four different locations on each foil using a digital micrometer (Mitutoyo America Corp., Aurora, IL).

Measurements of dose rate and beam profile

For dose rate measurements, full proton beam spills were delivered with approximately 1400 monitor units (MU) within each ∼100 ms pulse. The Advanced Markus ionization chamber was used for dose measurements. Pulse structures and delivery times were recorded using an oscilloscope (Tektronix TDS 3014B) to determine the average dose rate within each pulse. All measurements were performed in triplicate to assess reproducibility. Statistical analysis included calculation of standard deviations and 95% confidence intervals (CI).

Notably, MU in this experimental synchrotron‐based system is calibrated under specific reference conditions: beam ID 401 with the first scatterer (0.3 mm tungsten foil) in position, at 5 mm depth in a solid water phantom, using an Advanced Markus chamber positioned perpendicular to the central beam axis without its protective cap. Under these calibration conditions, 1 MU corresponds to 1.11 cGy.

For spatial dose distribution measurements, Gafchromic™ EBT‐XD films (12 cm × 10 cm, Ashland Specialty Ingredients G.P., Wilmington, DE) were positioned between the buildup layer and the Advanced Markus chamber (Figure 3B). Approximately 16 Gy was delivered to the center of each film. The Advanced Markus chamber remained in position during film irradiations to validate that the delivered dose was consistent with expectations, as the film center dose and chamber reading should be comparable. Note that beam profile measurements were not performed for the 2.0 mm tungsten scatterer configuration.

We evaluated the agreement between Monte Carlo calculations and film measurements across dose profiles using 1D gamma analysis, performed with the open‐source software PyMedPhys (version 0.39.3). 14 The dose threshold was set to 10% of the maximum dose in the reference dataset for global gamma index (γ) calculation. Gamma analysis criteria of 2%/1 mm were employed, with film measurements serving as the reference dataset and Monte Carlo calculations as the evaluation dataset.

Film analysis

The irradiated films were scanned 48 hours post‐irradiation using an Epson Expression 10000XL flatbed scanner (Epson America, Inc., Los Alamitos, CA) at 254 dpi resolution. The red‐channel optical density was converted to dose using a four‐parameter Rodbard (NIH) calibration function established with known dose standards. The calibration was performed using uniform dose irradiation from a clinical spot‐scanning beamline at the entrance region for 87.2 MeV proton beams, accounting for the beam quality dependence of EBT‐XD film response. 15

Film dose values were normalized by the monitor units (MU) of each delivery, and the resulting film dose profiles were compared with Monte Carlo predicted dose profiles for validation.

RESULTS

Dose rate modulation

A clear inverse exponential relationship was observed between tungsten scatterer thickness and measured dose rate. As shown in Figure 4, increasing the scatterer thickness from 0.1 mm to 3.5 mm (nominal) resulted in a progressive reduction in dose rate for full‐spill deliveries of approximately 1400 monitor units (MU). The measured dose rate decreased from 288.9±0.7 Gy/s with the thinnest scatterer (0.1 mm, nominal) to 10.2±0.2 Gy/s with the thickest scatterer (3.5 mm, nominal), representing a dose rate reduction of over 28‐fold across the tested thickness range.

The experimental measurements at 1 mm depth (water equivalent) showed excellent agreement with Monte Carlo predictions at 21 different tungsten thicknesses, with measured values consistently falling within or very close to the 95% confidence intervals. The percentage differences between measured and predicted values ranged from ‐4.1% to ‐8.2%, indicating a systematic but small underestimation by experimental measurements. Only for the 0.1 mm (nominal) tungsten thickness was the mean measured dose rate (288.9 Gy/s) slightly below the lower boundary of the 95% confidence interval (289.2 Gy/s). For all other thicknesses, measured dose rates fell within the 95% confidence intervals of the Monte Carlo predictions.

Monte Carlo simulations also evaluated the dose‐averaged LET values at the measurement position, which were 0.8, 0.8, 0.9, 1.0, and 1.3 keV/µm for measured tungsten thicknesses of 0.100, 0.414, 1.020, 2.031, and 3.565 mm, respectively. These values all remain in the low‐LET range. Based on our previously published radiobiological data, such small variations in LET within this low range do not produce significant differences in clonogenic cell survival, 2 indicating that the biological effects observed in future experiments will be primarily driven by dose rate rather than LET variations.

Spatial dose distribution

Spatial dose distribution of the beam spot was assessed using EBT‐XD films positioned downstream of tungsten scatterers of varying thickness. The lateral beam profiles at 1 mm depth (water equivalent) demonstrated significant broadening with increased scatterer thickness, consistent with enhanced multiple scattering of 87.2 MeV protons in tungsten.

Figure 5 shows the comparison between film‐measured spot profiles and Monte Carlo predicted dose profiles, normalized by monitor units (MU). Excellent agreement was observed between simulated and measured lateral dose profiles, with differences within a 2% margin across all tested configurations. For measured tungsten thicknesses of 0.100 mm, 0.414 mm, 1.020 mm, and 3.565 mm, the measured film center doses (x from ‐1 mm to +1 mm) were 1.3%, 0.7%, 1.1%, and 0.9% lower than Monte Carlo calculated doses, respectively. Note that beam profile measurements were not performed for the 2.031 mm (measured) tungsten scatterer configuration. Beam profile measurements were performed for four selected thicknesses (0.100, 0.414, 1.020, and 3.565 mm, measured) to demonstrate the beam broadening trend and maintain optimal figure presentation. The 1D gamma passing rates are 100%, 100%, 99.5%, and 95.4% for the four selected tungsten thicknesses, respectively.

The close agreement between experimental and simulated dose distributions validates both the experimental methodology and the Monte Carlo modeling approach. These findings confirm the efficacy of tungsten scatterer thickness modulation as a passive method for dose rate control, with excellent predictability through Monte Carlo simulation.

DISCUSSION

Significance of the dose rate modulation

The broad range of dose rates achieved in this study (10.2 to 288.9 Gy/s) makes the tungsten scatterer approach particularly valuable for radiobiological research, where systematic investigation of dose rate effects requires precise control over delivery parameters to determine whether FLASH effects can be achieved. Additionally, the beamline can be operated to deliver conventional dose rates by deploying the retractable first scatterer (0.3 mm tungsten foil), which reduces the entrance dose rate to <1 Gy/s. This configuration provides an ideal control condition for radiobiological experiments comparing conventional and ultra‐high dose rate effects.

The ability to modulate dose rates across such a wide range without modifying accelerator parameters represents a significant advantage for research applications. The passive scattering approach demonstrated in this study offers several advantages over active dose rate modulation techniques. By avoiding modifications to accelerator parameters, beam stability and reproducibility are maintained, which is crucial for both research consistency and safety. The exponential relationship between scatterer thickness and dose rate provides predictable control, enabling precise targeting of specific dose rate regimes.

Compared to our passive scattering approach, active dose rate modulation through beam current adjustment offers certain advantages: no additional scattering material is required, beam transmission efficiency is preserved, and dose rates can be adjusted electronically without hardware changes. However, active modulation presents significant technical challenges, including maintaining beam stability across different current settings, ensuring consistent beam optics and energy throughout the current range, and potentially requiring accelerator control system modifications that may not be permitted in clinical facilities. The passive tungsten scatterer method, while less efficient due to beam attenuation, provides highly reproducible dose rate control without affecting accelerator parameters, making it particularly suitable for research facilities where consistent beam conditions are critical and accelerator modifications are restricted. For future clinical implementation, active modulation would likely be preferred, but for current preclinical research investigating dose rate effects, the simplicity and reliability of passive modulation offer practical advantages.

Technical limitations

While the passive scattering approach demonstrates excellent dose rate control, it involves important trade‐offs that must be considered. Thicker scatterers result in significant beam loss through attenuation and scattering, reducing overall beam utilization efficiency. While this may be acceptable for research applications where dose rate precision is prioritized over efficiency, clinical implementation would require consideration of treatment time implications and potentially favor active dose rate modulation methods that preserve beam utilization.

An important safety consideration for passive scattering is the potential increase in neutron production from nuclear interactions in the tungsten. However, Monte Carlo simulations revealed that neutron dose contribution to the target volume (20 cm × 20 cm × 7 cm water phantom) remained below 0.1% for all tested tungsten thicknesses, indicating negligible radiobiological impact.

Notably, the measured dose rates consistently show slightly lower values than Monte Carlo predictions across all tungsten thicknesses. This systematic difference likely arises from minor discrepancies between the idealized simulation conditions including perfect geometry and NIST standard tungsten material properties in Geant4 and the actual experimental setup and tungsten foil composition. Despite this small systematic offset, the measurements fall within the 95% confidence intervals of the predictions, validating our methodology.

Future radiobiological studies

The wide dose rate range and excellent reproducibility demonstrated in this study make the tungsten scatterer approach well‐suited for systematic radiobiological investigations. The ability to deliver doses spanning conventional to ultra‐high dose rate regimes with the same beam delivery system enables controlled studies of dose rate effects on cellular and tissue responses. This capability is essential for determining optimal dose rate thresholds and understanding the mechanisms underlying potential FLASH effects.

Future work will focus on expanding this methodology toward biologically driven applications. Planned developments include deploying a robotic arm system for automated positioning of 96‐well cell culture plates during targeted irradiation, 10 enabling selective irradiation of individual wells for high‐throughput biological experiments. 16 Additional investigations will examine cellular responses to varying dose rates using the optimized delivery strategies developed in this study.

CONCLUSIONS

This study successfully demonstrates that tungsten scatterer thickness modulation provides a practical and controllable method for dose rate adjustment in synchrotron‐based proton therapy systems. The inverse exponential relationship between scatterer thickness and dose rate enables precise control across a clinically relevant range spanning conventional to ultra‐high dose rate regimes. The established experimental infrastructure provides a foundation for systematic investigation of dose rate‐dependent biological responses and optimization of ultra‐high dose rate radiotherapy protocols.
