Maternal liver function and adverse pregnancy outcomes in Chinese women with HIV: a cohort study
Authors: E Wu, Jun-Tao Ni, Lijun Xu, Yin-wei Chen, Xiao-Hui Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUnderstanding maternal liver function in human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)-positive pregnancies is essential for improving maternal and infant health and preventing mother-to-child transmission. HIV-positive pregnant women are at risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes (APO). While liver damage in HIV-infected individuals is well known, the impact of maternal circulating liver function markers on APOs remains underexplored. This study investigated the association between liver function markers and APO in HIV-infected pregnant women in China.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study analyzed data from the China prevention of mother-to-child transmission program (2011–2024), assessing maternal alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and total bilirubin (TBil) levels during pregnancy. Multivariate-adjusted binary logistic regression was conducted to estimate odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). The main outcome measures were low birth weight or preterm birth (LBW/PTB), small for gestational age (SGA), and overall APO.Results1096 HIV-infected pregnant women were included in this study. We recorded 330 cases of APO, including LBW/PTB (n = 177) and SGA (n = 199). A significant U-shaped association was found between maternal ALT levels and overall APO and LBW/PTB risk (p for non-linearity < 0.001), with the higher risks at both low (< 10 U/L) and high (≥ 30 U/L) levels. Compared to ALT at 20–29 U/L, the ORs (95% CI) of overall APO were 1.91 (1.16–3.13) for ALT < 10 U/L and 2.35 (1.27–4.35) for ALT ≥ 30 U/L. Elevated AST concentrations were linked to higher risks of overall APO, LBW/PTB and SGA. Moreover, a positive correlation was observed between TBil levels and LBW/PTB. Notably, the association between maternal liver function markers (ALT, AST) and APO was trimester-dependent, emerging significantly only during the third trimester.ConclusionsOur findings suggest that maternal circulating liver function markers are important predictors of APO in HIV-infected pregnant women in China. Background

Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection remains a significant global public health concern, with an estimated 37.7 million people infected with HIV worldwide in 2020 [1], and approximately 1.4 million HIV-infected women giving birth each year [2]. Previous research has indicated that maternal HIV infection may increase the risk of multiple adverse pregnancy outcomes (APO) [3], such as preterm birth (PTB), low birth weight (LBW), and small-for-gestational-age (SGA) infants [4, 5]. Therefore, identifying modifiable risk factors among pregnant women with HIV is crucial for preventing APO in this vulnerable population.

Existing research has predominantly focused on the risks of APO associated with antiretroviral therapy (ART) in HIV-infected pregnant women. There is well-documented evidence linking ART to gestational hypertension, dyslipidemia, PTB, and elevated hepatic enzymes [6–11]. However, the relationship between maternal liver dysfunction and APO in this population remains underexplored. Chronic HIV infection, along with long-term ART exposure, may predispose individuals to hepatic injury through mechanisms such as metabolic dysregulation, immune activation, and drug-induced hepatotoxicity [12, 13]. Notably, Maurice et al. reported a striking prevalence of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (> 35%) among people living with HIV, underscoring the vulnerability of this population to liver-related morbidity [14]. Biomarkers including alanine aminotransaminase (ALT), aspartate aminotransaminase (AST), and total bilirubin (TBil) serve as critical proxies for hepatic function, reflecting metabolic capacity, detoxification efficiency, and systemic immune-nutritional status [15, 16]. Elevated aminotransferases, indicative of hepatocellular injury, signal impaired hepatic regulation of local and systemic inflammatory responses through distinct molecular mechanisms. These inflammatory cascades, both localized and systemic, are being increasingly recognized as independent etiological contributors to PTB [17]. Similarly, hyperbilirubinemia (elevated TBil) compromises maternal-fetal bile acid transport, increasing the risks of cholestasis-associated preterm labor and fetal distress [18]. A study from China highlighted the prevalence (9.57%) of liver function impairment during pregnancy and identified it as an independent risk factor for PTB. Abnormalities in ALT, AST, and TBil levels have been linked to a two-fold increase in the risk of PTB [17]. These findings underscore the critical role of maternal liver function in pregnancy outcomes. In the context of HIV infection, where the immune system is under constant challenge and metabolic capacity may be compromised [19, 20], monitoring these liver function markers becomes crucial for understanding the intricate relationship between HIV-infected maternal liver function and APO. Despite these insights, due to challenges in recruiting and longitudinal monitoring this high-risk cohort, data characterizing liver function-APO associations in HIV-infected pregnancies remain scarce.

This study investigates the association between maternal liver function markers (ALT, AST, TBil) and adverse outcomes in HIV-infected pregnant women in China.

Methods

Study participants

This retrospective cohort study analyzed data from the Prevention of Mother-to-Child Transmission (PMTCT) of HIV database in Zhejiang Province, China. In the China PMTCT program, women were screened for HIV, syphilis, and hepatitis B virus during their first antenatal health care (ANC) visit and at delivery [21]. HIV was diagnosed at a local disease control centre. HIV-positive women were provided antiviral therapy immediately, followed by medical staff in local maternal and children’s health hospitals and required hospital delivery. Moreover, these women were recommended to have liver function testing during their first ANC visit. In general, pregnant women had at least five ANC visits, while those with high-risk or abnormal indicators had a higher number of visits. The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Women’s Hospital School of Medicine Zhejiang University (IRB-20220378-R). All patients signed informed consent.

The minimum required sample size was calculated using a cohort study design and the formula shown below. The outcome incidence rates were estimated as p1 = 0.2 for the exposed group, and p2 = 0.1 for the non-exposed group [22, 23]. The parameter p was defined as the mean incidence rate across both groups, and q was calculated as q = 1 − p, with a power of 1 − β = 0.90, and a two-sided significance level of α = 0.05. The minimum sample size required for the study was determined to be 266 participants.

Formula:

In this study, a total of 1458 HIV-infected pregnant women were recruited from China between January 2011 and March 2024. Women with multiple pregnancies (n = 10), those missing information on infant birth weight and sex (n = 22), and those with no liver function marker measurements during pregnancy (n = 330) were excluded. Finally, we included 1096 HIV-infected pregnant women in this study (Fig. 1). Supplementary Table S1 summarises the characteristics of the subjects excluded from the analysis.

Flowchart of study participants

Exposure assessment

Maternal circulating liver function markers, including serum ALT, AST, and TBil, were measured during routine antenatal examinations. For the participants in this study, blood samples were drawn at varying time points across pregnancy. Among the 1,096 included women, 586 were tested before 28 weeks of gestation and 845 at 28 weeks or later, some women contributed measurements in both periods. Serial measurements from all available time points for each participant, spanning from early pregnancy to delivery, were used to calculate a longitudinal mean value for each liver function marker.

Outcome measurement

The primary outcome was the overall incidence of APO, including LBW, PTB, SGA, perinatal death, fetal macrosomia, and post-term infants. Notably, these APO subtypes—particularly LBW/PTB and SGA—are not mutually exclusive and may overlap. While LBW is defined as a birth weight of less than 2,500 g, PTB is defined as birth at < 37 gestational weeks. SGA refers to infants whose birth weight is below the 10th percentile for their gestational age, and hence, they are smaller for the length of pregnancy [24]. While perinatal death is defined as stillbirth at and after 28 weeks of pregnancy (or fetal weight ≥ 1000 g), neonatal death is death within 7 days of birth. Fetal macrosomia refers to newborns whose weight at birth is ≥ 4000 g. Post-term infants are newborns born at ≥ 42 weeks of gestation. Birth weights were obtained from the medical records. The gestational age was determined using either the last menstrual period or an early ultrasound examination in pregnancy [25, 26].

Assessment of covariates

Covariates were selected based on prior knowledge and a review of previous research, including age at pregnancy, age at HIV diagnosis, education (primary and below, junior high school, senior high school, college and above), gravidity (1, 2–3, ≥ 4), parity (0, 1, ≥ 2), and infant sex (female, male). All data for covariates were collected during pregnancy.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables conforming to a normal distribution are depicted as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas those exhibiting a skewed distribution are presented as median (P25, P75). Categorical variables are presented as frequencies (%). Multivariable-adjusted restricted cubic splines (RCS) with three knots at the 10th, 50th, and 90th centiles [27], were employed to analyse the dose-response relationship of ALT, AST and TBil levels with adverse outcomes. Binary logistic regression was used to estimate the odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) of adverse outcomes. Model 1 was not adjusted, whereas Model 2 was adjusted for demographic characteristics (age at pregnancy, age at HIV diagnosis, and education). Model 3 was further adjusted for maternal and child characteristics (gravidity, parity, and infant sex). The variance inflation factor (VIF) was used to assess multicollinearity among the variables in the logistic model. The VIF values for the variables in this study’s logistic model were below 5, indicating the absence of multicollinearity. Multiple interactions were performed to test the interaction term beta in the binary logistic regression model [28]. Furthermore, we conducted stratified analyses to examine effect modification by education, gravidity, parity, among others.

Sensitivity analyses were performed to validate the robustness of the results. First, serum ALT, AST, and TBil concentrations were categorised based on the deciles. Second, given the evolution of clinical guidelines, ART regimens, and maternal health interventions between 2011 and 2024, we stratified the data into distinct temporal periods (2011–2015, 2016–2020, 2021–2024) to determine if the observed associations were consistent under evolving clinical practices. Third, given that antiviral medication use during pregnancy may be an important confounder, we repeated the primary analysis with additional adjustment for antiviral use during pregnancy (yes or no). Fourth, giving that using mean concentrations of liver function markers across pregnancy may obscure important temporal variations. Hence, we reran the primary analyses by calculating mean concentrations of liver-function markers during gestational age < 28 weeks (n = 586) and gestational age ≥ 28 weeks (n = 845). Fifth, we further excluded HIV-infected pregnant women who were co-infected with hepatitis B/C virus (n = 20) or syphilis (n = 28) during pregnancy to control for potential confounders. Sixth, considering that combining LBW and PTB might obscure some important outcomes, we repeated the primary analysis with LBW and PTB examined separately. Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.1.3). Statistical significance was set at a two-tailed p-value < 0.05. The Benjamini–Hochberg method was implemented to correct for multiple comparisons, maintaining a false discovery rate (FDR) of 0.1 [27, 29].

Results

Basic characteristics of the study population

Data for 1096 HIV-infected pregnant women are shown in Table 1. The mean (± SD) maternal age at enrolment was 28 (± 5.8) years. APO were recorded in 330 women (overall rate, 30.1%). Among HIV-infected mothers who experienced an APO, most had 2–3 prior gravidities, were nulliparous, delivered female infants, and were more likely to have a junior high school education. The most common APO was SGA (199 cases, 18.2%), followed by LBW/PTB (171 cases, 15.6%), fetal macrosomia (24 cases, 2.2%), postmature infants (17 cases, 1.6%), and perinatal deaths (6 cases, 0.5%) (Supplementary Table S2).

Liver function markers

Of the 1096 women, 96.2% reported ALT within 0–40 u/L, 96.6% documented AST within 0–40 u/L, and 91.0% had TBil within the normal range (1.7–17.1 µmol/L). The majority of the HIV-infected women (87.3%) had circulating levels of liver function markers during pregnancy within the normal range (Supplementary Table S3). Compared to those without APO, HIV-infected women who experienced an APO appeared to have a higher proportion of elevated ALT and AST levels outside the normal reference ranges (Table 1).

Dose-response association

Multivariate-adjusted RCS analysis revealed a U-shaped nonlinear dose-response association between ALT concentrations and the risk of overall APO and LBW/PTB (all p-values for nonlinearity < 0.05; all p-values for overall significance < 0.05). ALT concentrations around 20 u/L were associated with the lowest risks of overall APO and LBW/PTB (Fig. 2). We therefore used 10, 20, and 30 u/L as cut-off points to categorise ALT concentrations (Supplementary Table S4). Moreover, there appeared to be a null correlation between ALT concentrations and SGA, as the dose-response relationship showed a flat line (p for nonlinearity = 0.596; p for overall significance = 0.096). Notably, the RCS analysis also indicated an increased risk of LBW/PTB with increasing AST concentrations, and the rate of increase in OR slowed down at an AST concentration of approximately 25 U/L (p for overall significance = 0.021). Based on these findings, we use 15, 25, and 35 u/L as cut-off points to categorise AST concentrations into four groups. Additionally, the risks of LBW/PTB increased with increasing TBil concentrations and tended to level off at TBil concentrations > 15 µmol/L. Therefore, we used 5, 10, and 15 µmol/L as cut-points to categorise TBil concentrations.

Association between liver function marker categories and adverse outcomes

The same trends were observed when maternal circulating liver function marker categories were used instead of continuous values. In the multivariable-adjusted logistic regression model 3, compared with HIV-infected pregnant women with ALT between 20 and 29 u/L, those with ALT < 10 u/L or ALT ≥ 30 u/L were associated with higher risks of overall APO [OR (95%CI): 1.91 (1.16–3.13) and 2.35 (1.27–4.35))] and LBW/PTB [2.47 (1.20–5.07), and 5.14 (2.31–11.44)]. Moreover, compared to AST levels < 15 u/L, circulating AST concentrations ≥ 35 u/L were more significantly associated with increased risks of overall APO [2.18 (1.17–4.05)], LBW/PTB [2.13 (1.03–4.40)], and SGA [2.40 (1.15–4.98)], as analyzed in Model 3. Additionally, model 3 indicated that low TBil levels had a protective effect on LBW/PTB, compared to HIV-infected pregnant women with TBil < 5 µmol/L, those with TBil ≥ 15 µmol/L had a 2.25-fold higher risk of LBW/PTB [3.25 (1.34–7.85)] (Table 2). Notably, a null correlation was observed between the TBil categories and overall APO and SGA. Similar results were observed for Models 1 and 2 (Supplementary Table S5).

Subgroup analyses and sensitivity analyses

Multivariable-adjusted RCS analysis supported a U-shaped association between ALT and overall APO and LBW/PTB, alongside a protective effect of low TBil. These associations were most pronounced in primiparous, lower-educated women delivering female infants (p for interaction < 0.001; Fig. 3). Furthermore, the correlations between AST or TBil and overall APO, as well as SGA, were stronger in female infants (Supplementary Figs. S1 and S2). In the sensitivity analyses, similar results were obtained when we (a) categorised the concentrations of maternal circulating liver function markers according to deciles (Supplementary Table S6), (b) stratified the data into distinct temporal periods (2011–2015, 2016–2020, 2021–2024) (Supplementary Fig. S3), (c) further adjusted for antiviral medication in the models (Supplementary Table S7), (d) excluded pregnant women with co-infections (hepatitis B, C, or syphilis) (Supplementary Table S8), or (e) analyzed LBW and PTB as separate outcomes (Supplementary Fig. S4). However, the mean concentrations of liver function markers in each trimester showed that ALT and AST were associated with adverse outcomes only in the third trimester (≥ 28 weeks of gestation) (Fig. 4).

Discussion

An analysis of 1096 HIV-infected pregnant women revealed a “U-shaped” dose-response correlation between ALT concentrations and the overall APO and LBW/PTB risk, with ALT levels of 20–29 u/L associated with a lower risk of adverse outcomes. The “U-shaped” dose-response correlation suggests that optimal levels may reflect balanced liver function essential for proper fetal development. Additionally, the lower the AST concentration, the lower the risk of adverse outcomes. An AST concentration < 15 u/L was linked to a lower risk of overall APO, LBW/PTB, and SGA. And TBil concentration was positively associated with LBW/PTB risk, and TBil concentration of < 5 µmol/L had a protective effect against LBW/PTB. Furthermore, stronger associations were observed in certain subgroups. Of note, the significant association of adverse outcomes with elevated ALT and AST levels only in the third trimester of pregnancy may be attributed to the dynamic physiological demands of pregnancy. In late pregnancy, maternal metabolic and detoxification loads peak to support rapid fetal growth, which may amplify the effects of subclinical hepatocellular injury on placental insufficiency and fetal malnutrition [30, 31]. These findings extend previous studies on the overall status of liver markers during pregnancy, highlighting the importance of enhanced liver function screening during pregnancy, especially in the third trimester.

HIV-infected pregnant women have higher risks of APO and abnormal liver functions [32, 33]. A study from Chengdu, China, found that the incidences of PTB and LBW among HIV-negative pregnant women were 6.82% and 4.65%, respectively, which were substantially lower than the LBW/PTB incidence in the HIV-positive group in this study (15.6%) [3]. Moreover, liver diseases have become the most common complication in HIV-infected individuals [34]. Some studies have reported a 12% incidence of liver fibrosis in overweight/obese women in the Netherlands, but only a few studies have focused on pregnant women [35, 36]. This study revealed that in pregnant women with HIV, the incidence of at least one abnormality in liver function markers is approximately 12.7%, which is lower than previously reported rates in HIV patients (14%–23%) [12, 36, 37]. The lower number of women with abnormal liver function markers in our study could be attributed to enhanced pre-pregnancy and pre-marriage testing. In China, the government provides free pre-pregnancy and pre-marriage testing [21]. For example, there are about 100 maternal and child health care institutions in Zhejiang Province that offer pre-pregnancy and pre-marital screening, and the coverage rate has reached more than 90% for years. However, the incomplete investigation of liver function markers in this study should be addressed in future studies.

Studies on the association between overall APO and liver function are limited. Previous studies have reported the impact of liver dysfunction during pregnancy on adverse outcomes such as PTB and large for gestational age birth [38, 39]. A study involving 36,755 Chinese pregnant women showed that abnormal levels of ALT (≥ 201 u/L), AST (≥ 201 u/L), or TBil (≥ 34.3 µmol/L) significantly increased the risk of PTB, with unadjusted risk ratios of 3.52, 4.06, and 4.12, respectively [17]. Additionally, a study of 2322 Korean women revealed a null association between elevated ALT levels (> 95th percentile) in early pregnancy and risks of SGA [40]. Our findings are in agreement with those of previous studies. ALT abnormalities may affect the metabolic intrauterine environment and thus explain LBW/PTB, whereas SGA may involve unique metabolic adaptations, such as fetal insulin resistance or altered amino acid transport, that are less influenced by maternal ALT levels [41].

Various mechanisms have been proposed to explain the role of abnormal liver function in APO. The liver plays a crucial role in regulating systemic and local inflammatory responses through various molecular mechanisms [42]. Although the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying PTB are not fully understood, a limited number of studies have shown that both systemic and local inflammation may be potential causes. PTB has been shown to be associated with excessive production of proinflammatory cytokines [43]. HIV infection is characterized by persistent immune activation and chronic inflammation. This pro-inflammatory milieu (elevated cytokines such as TNF-α) can directly induce hepatocyte apoptosis and exacerbate liver injury from other etiologies, such as drug toxicity or metabolic dysfunction [44, 45]. In pregnancy, this effect may be amplified as the physiological immune modulation of gestation interacts with HIV-associated inflammation, creating a synergistic burden on hepatic function [46, 47]. This heightened inflammatory state likely lowers the threshold for hepatocyte injury, contributing to the observed elevations in AST and ALT and increasing the risk of APO. Furthermore, while ART is essential for viral suppression, it may exert complex effects on the liver. Specific ART regimens are associated with hepatotoxicity; for instance, nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NRTIs) such as stavudine and zidovudine can inhibit mitochondrial DNA polymerase-γ, leading to oxidative stress and hepatocellular damage, while non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NNRTIs) and protease inhibitors (PIs) may also contribute to hepatic steatosis and enzyme elevations [48, 49]. Additionally, certain PIs, notably atazanavir, are known to inhibit the hepatic enzyme UDP-glucuronosyltransferase 1A1 (UGT1A1), a pharmacologic effect that impairs bilirubin conjugation and leads to unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia [50]. The convergence of HIV-related chronic inflammation, ART-related metabolic alterations (including both direct hepatotoxicity and effects on bilirubin metabolism), and the increased physiological demands of pregnancy creates a unique milieu. This complex interplay can differentially impact liver enzymes and is likely a key contributor to the increased risk of APOs observed in this special population.

ALT is predominantly localized in the cytoplasm of hepatocytes, making it a more specific marker for hepatocellular injury [51]. In this study, the U-shaped association between ALT and APO may reflect a dual pathophysiology specific to hepatic parenchymal stress. Low ALT levels during pregnancy may reflect physiological adaptations or altered hepatic metabolism rather than solely indicating decreased synthetic function [52]. In the context of HIV infection and long-term ART exposure, where the immune system is persistently challenged and metabolic capacity may be compromised, extremely low ALT levels might indicate insufficient hepatic biosynthetic or metabolic adaptation, potentially compromising fetal growth and development [19]. Conversely, high ALT levels may indicate liver damage or inflammation. Both low and high ALT levels may adversely affect birth outcomes. In contrast, AST is found in both the cytoplasm and mitochondria of hepatocytes and is also present in significant amounts in other tissues such as cardiac muscle, skeletal muscle, kidneys, and brain [51].Low concentrations of AST within the normal range have antioxidant effects and contribute to amino acid balance, leading to good liver health and metabolic stability [53–55], and lower AST levels may signify a healthier liver environment, supporting better pregnancy outcomes. The monotonic increase in risk with higher AST levels may integrate signals from broader systemic processes. This could include contributions from not only liver involvement but also systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, or subclinical mitochondrial toxicity—factors highly relevant in a population on ART [56, 57]. Lower TBil levels within the normal range have a protective effect, which could be attributed to the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties of bilirubin [58, 59]. Lower TBil levels may indicate a balance in antioxidant capacity, mitigating oxidative stress that could negatively affect fetal growth and development. Additionally, high TBil levels are indicative of liver failure or decompensated cirrhosis [13].

The strength of this study lies in its focus and robust sample size. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to explore the dose-response relationship between maternal liver function markers and APO in Chinese HIV-infected pregnant women. These findings provide valuable insights for clinical practice in vulnerable populations, particularly pregnant women and patients with liver damage who are also HIV-infected. Moreover, this study comprehensively evaluated multiple liver function markers and their associations with APO. This thorough assessment provides a holistic view of the impact of liver function on pregnancy outcomes in women with HIV infection. The large and representative sample size of the study population makes our findings reliable and generalisable. Despite its strengths, this study has some limitations. First, owing to the cohort study design and its retrospective nature, causal relationships between maternal liver function markers and adverse outcomes cannot be definitively established. Further interventional studies are required to confirm these associations. In addition, future prospective studies with more frequent liver function testing and detailed data on potential confounders are needed to confirm and extend these findings. Second, although we adjusted for potential confounding factors, the possibility of residual confounding by unmeasured variables, such as HIV-specific factors (e.g., viral load, CD4 + T-cell count, and specific antiretroviral regimens), maternal characteristics (e.g., body mass index, nutritional status, and other chronic conditions such as diabetes or hypertension), pre-existing liver disease or impaired liver function, and behavioral factors (e.g., alcohol consumption), cannot be excluded and may have influenced the results [60, 61]. This should be prominently addressed in the design of subsequent studies. Third, while our sensitivity analysis accounted for antiviral therapy, it did not incorporate adjustments for specific drug regimens, initiation timing, dose adjustments during treatment, or adherence levels. These limitations highlight the need for future pharmacoepidemiologic studies with granular treatment data. Finally, it is important to note that our findings are derived from the PMTCT of HIV database in Zhejiang Province, China. The observed association between liver function and adverse pregnancy outcomes may not be directly generalizable to non-HIV populations or to other geographical settings with differing epidemiological profiles and healthcare systems. Therefore, replicating this research in diverse populations and settings is essential to confirm these associations and to enhance the external validity and clinical applicability of the findings.

Conclusions

Our findings indicate an association between liver function markers and APO in HIV-infected pregnant women. A “U-shaped” nonlinear association was seen between ALT levels and overall APO and LBW/PTB, with an increased risk observed at both low (< 10 U/L) and high (≥ 30 U/L) levels. In addition, higher AST (≥ 35 u/L) or TBil levels (≥ 15 µmol/L) were found to increase the risk of adverse outcomes. Our study highlights the significance of strengthening maternal liver function monitoring during pregnancy, especially in the third trimester, in HIV-infected women. We believe that our findings can guide clinical practice in China to optimize maternal and infant health outcomes in this vulnerable population.
