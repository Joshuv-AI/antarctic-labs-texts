Beta-blockers after myocardial infarction with preserved ejection fraction—reexamining established paradigms?
Authors: Agata Galas, Paweł Krzesiński
Published: 2026

Beta-blockers (BBs), according to the current European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines for the management of acute coronary syndromes (ACS), are recommended with a class IIa, level B indication in patients after myocardial infarction (MI) irrespective of left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) (1). They also remain first-line antianginal therapy (class I, level B indication), reflecting their established role in reducing myocardial oxygen demand and mitigating ischemia-related symptoms (2). Even when percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) has successfully addressed the culprit lesion, it cannot be assumed that all potential sources of anginal symptoms have been eliminated. A range of alternative pathophysiological mechanisms—including microvascular dysfunction, coronary vasomotor abnormalities, or residual diffuse atherosclerotic disease—may continue to contribute to ischemia. Furthermore, BBs have been associated with a reduced risk of developing heart failure (HF) among patients with hypertension. Nevertheless, their preventive effect appears to be age-dependent, with benefits comparable to those of other antihypertensive therapies in patients younger than 65 years, whereas a diminished effect has been observed in older individuals (3). This clinical reality underscores why BB have traditionally been viewed as a foundational component of post-MI management, extending beyond simple hemodynamic control to a broader strategy of symptom modulation and secondary prevention.

The historical foundation for recommendations (1), however, rests largely on evidence generated in an era preceding contemporary PCI. The pivotal trials underlying guideline statements (4-7) were published decades ago, at a time when routine early revascularization, high-intensity statin therapy, and comprehensive secondary prevention strategies were far less standardized than they are today. However, pooled data showed that treatment with BB following an MI reduced the risk of death by more than 20% (4). Interestingly, already in 2014 the authors of a meta-analysis reported that, in contemporary practice for MI, BB no longer confer a mortality benefit, but are associated with a reduction in recurrent MI and angina (in the short term), at the expense of a higher incidence of HF, cardiogenic shock, and drug discontinuation, and they suggested that guideline authors should reconsider the strength of recommendations for routine BB use after MI (6,7). Consequently, the applicability of these data to modern post-MI populations, particularly those with preserved LVEF, remained uncertain. Notably, even the authors of the ESC guidelines (1) explicitly acknowledged this evidence gap and emphasized the need for dedicated randomized trials to clarify benefits of BB therapy in patients with a history of MI and preserved LVEF.

This call for contemporary data has been central to ongoing debate and has set the stage for the recently published in The New England Journal of Medicine meta-analysis (8). In this meta-analysis of five studies (REBOOT, REDUCE-AMI, BETAMI, DANBLOCK, and CAPITAL-RCT) encompassing 17,801 patients with prior MI with preserved LVEF and no other indication for BB therapy, BB use was not associated with reductions in all-cause mortality, recurrent MI, or the development of HF (8). Importantly, these findings are consistent with those reported by Gomes et al. in a meta-analysis of post-MI patients with preserved LVEF, which similarly failed to demonstrate a clear prognostic benefit of long-term BB therapy in this population (9).

In contrast, patients with LVEF of 40–49% appear to derive benefit from BB, as demonstrated by a meta-analysis of the REBOOT, BETAMI, DANBLOCK, and CAPITAL-RCT trials, which reported a significant reduction in a composite endpoint of all-cause death, recurrent MI, and HF in patients with mildly reduced ejection fraction (HFmrEF) (10). This consistency across contemporary meta-analyses further strengthens the rationale for reassessing routine BB use in patients without reduced LVEF or other established indications.

These findings raise an important clinical question regarding the optimal long-term management of post-MI patients with preserved LVEF, in whom neither BB nor sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT-2 inhibitors) have consistently demonstrated prognostic benefit. Taken together, these findings suggest that, in post-MI patients with preserved LVEF and no other indication for BB therapy, greater emphasis may need to be placed on comprehensive secondary prevention measures, including intensive lipid-lowering therapy, risk-factor modification, and cardiac rehabilitation (11,12). In the study by Kristensen et al., the primary endpoint (all-cause death, recurrent MI, or HF) occurred in 717 patients (8.1%) in the BB group and in 748 patients (8.3%) in the non-BB group during a median follow-up of 3.6 years. HF was observed in 75 (0.84%) and 87 (0.96%) patients, respectively, which appears inconsistent with prior observations indicating that new-onset HF develops in 20–30% of patients within the first year after discharge for MI (13-15). Importantly, the interpretation of these remarkably low event rates depends on how HF was defined and ascertained across the included trials. Hospitalization for HF, outpatient HF diagnosis, imaging-based ventricular dysfunction, and adjudicated clinical HF events represent distinct manifestations of the disease and may substantially influence reported incidence rates. Therefore, given that HF outcomes in the analyzed studies were primarily captured as hospitalization events, the reported incidence may not fully reflect the overall burden of HF, particularly when considering cases diagnosed and managed in outpatient settings. Although these reference data are somewhat dated, populationbased studies indicate a substantial decline in the prevalence of HF after MI over time: between the periods 1996–1998 and 2005–2007, the prevalence of HF after MI fell from 28.1% to 16.5% (13). Furthermore, in a large cohort of 63,853 patients discharged alive without HF, 8,058 (12.6%) were subsequently hospitalized with or died because of HF during a median follow-up of 3.2 years, yielding HF incidence rates of 31 per 1,000 person-years in men and 46 per 1,000 person-years in women, with the highest rates occurring during the first 6 months of follow-up and then stabilizing thereafter (14). Overall, HF incidence in these population-based cohorts was substantially higher than that reported in the pooled trials, likely reflecting, at least in part, the highly selected, lower-risk trial population. This difference should be considered when interpreting the generalizability of the findings. In one of the trials included in the meta-analysis (CAPITAL-RCT) (16,17), enrolling patients treated between 2010 and 2014, thus already reflecting somewhat outdated but still contemporary practice—the combined endpoint of death, recurrent MI, or HF occurred in 6.8% of patients receiving carvedilol versus 7.9% in the non-BB group over a median follow-up of 3.9 years among patients with mildly reduced LVEF indicating a relatively low absolute event rate. In contrast, while mortality and HF rates remained reassuringly low, any revascularization occurred in 20.3% of the carvedilol group and 17.7% of the non-BB group; however, this difference was not statistically significant (P=0.65) (16). Repeat revascularization may reflect greater underlying coronary disease burden rather than represent a causal risk factor for HF (18,19). In a large study involving 337,274 patients, recurrent MI was a strong independent predictor of subsequent HF (20). Although repeat revascularization rates were similar between treatment groups, this observation should be interpreted with caution, as revascularization decisions are influenced by multiple clinical and anatomical factors and cannot be considered a direct measure of anti-anginal efficacy. Nevertheless, these findings may warrant further investigation in future studies evaluating the broader clinical impact of long-term BB therapy in contemporary post-MI populations.

Therefore, the low incidence of HF observed in the contemporary trials may reflect not only advances in post-MI management but also differences in HF definitions, ascertainment strategies, and event adjudication procedures. Several other questions arise in this context. Firstly, could HF have been underdiagnosed by being recognized only in patients with overt clinical events requiring hospitalization, rather than through systematic ambulatory follow-up, careful symptom assessment, and imaging‑based criteria in an outpatient setting? In the study by Faridi et al., 64.2% of new-onset HF cases following MI were identified in outpatient settings (20). Secondly, might the underrepresentation of women in the study population have influenced the observed outcomes, given well-established sex-related differences in HF incidence, symptom presentation, and response to therapy (21,22)? Finally, could the incidence of de novo atrial fibrillation (AF)—often associated with sympathetic overactivity and potentially modified by BB—have been higher in the non-BB group? Additionally, the relatively short median follow-up in the presented meta-analysis, may be insufficient to capture the full trajectory of late-onset HF. Collectively, these observations underscore the need for cautious interpretation of the available evidence and suggest that the true long-term burden of HF and arrhythmic complications following MI may be underestimated in contemporary randomized trials. Furthermore, the cohort study by Gho et al. underscores the substantial burden of HF post-MI, reporting that one in four survivors of a first MI develop HF within 4 years (23). In this context, the cornerstone of post-MI management should not be a blanket continuation or withdrawal of BB, but rather an individualized, risk-guided approach, incorporating LVEF, symptom status, comorbidities, age, and long-term prognosis, with careful reassessment over time. Similarly, the EMPACT-MI trial did not demonstrate a significant reduction in the primary composite endpoint among post-MI patients at increased risk of HF. Nevertheless, the observed reduction in HF hospitalizations highlights the ongoing search for effective strategies to improve outcomes in this population, particularly as the role of long-term BB therapy continues to be reassessed (11).

Clinical practice should evolve in response to emerging evidence, with the ultimate goal of providing the best possible care for patients. Future modifications of guideline recommendations may influence prescribing patterns in clinical practice. However, the extent to which clinicians will incorporate emerging evidence into routine care remains uncertain and will likely depend on individual patient characteristics, physician experience, and the accumulation of further evidence, not only because of established prescribing habits but also because of the persistent belief that BB continue to provide meaningful clinical benefit despite evolving evidence.

In conclusion, contemporary evidence no longer supports the automatic continuation of long-term BB therapy in all patients following MI with preserved LVEF. Rather, treatment decisions should be individualized, taking into account patient characteristics, comorbidities, symptom burden, and the presence of other established indications for BB therapy. While BB remain an important therapeutic option in selected patients, their routine use as a universal post-MI strategy in individuals with preserved LVEF should be reconsidered in light of contemporary evidence. Further adequately powered randomized trials are needed to identify patient subgroups that may continue to derive meaningful clinical benefit.
