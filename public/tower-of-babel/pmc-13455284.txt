The application of mini T-plate and high-strength suture wires in the surgical treatment of posterior cruciate ligament avulsion fractures: a retrospective case series
Authors: Chenhao Sun, Shihan Wang, Pengtao Chen, Yao Lu, Kuijing Han, Pei Zhang, Yilang He, Yuan Liang, Yongchao Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPosterior cruciate ligament (PCL) avulsion fractures at the tibial insertion can cause posterior knee instability and accelerate osteoarthritis. Meyers-McKeever type II/III fractures require surgery. Traditional open approaches are traumatic, while arthroscopic techniques are limited by equipment, learning curves, and fixation efficacy.ObjectiveTo evaluate the short-term clinical outcomes and feasibility of a combined vertical medial posterior incision approach using a small T-plate and high-strength suture fixation for isolated PCL avulsion fractures.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 21 patients with isolated Meyers-McKeever type II/III PCL tibial avulsion fractures treated with posteromedial longitudinal mini-incision fixation using a mini T-plate combined with high-strength suture. Operative parameters, fracture healing, knee ROM, Tegner-Lysholm score, IKDC score, posterior drawer test, and complications were assessed.ResultsThe mean surgery duration was 81.5 ± 20.9 min, with an average intraoperative blood loss of 33 ± 11 ml. All fractures achieved anatomical reduction, with an average healing time of 10.9 ± 1.6 weeks. At the final follow-up (mean 10.6 ± 5.2 months), knee ROM improved significantly from 76.5°±12.1° preoperatively to 132.2°±4.2° (P < 0.05). Both Tegner-Lysholm scores (from 45.5 ± 9.3 to 95.5 ± 2.8) and IKDC scores (from 35.9 ± 8.9 to 96.8 ± 1.5) showed statistically significant improvements (P < 0.05). The posterior drawer test was negative in all patients, and no infection, implant failure, or gastrocnemius weakness was observed.ConclusionPosteromedial longitudinal mini-incision fixation with a mini T-plate combined with high-strength suture achieved satisfactory clinical outcomes for PCL tibial avulsion fractures. The proposed 5T concept summarizes the main practical advantages of this technique. In this descriptive case series (Level IV evidence), the technique achieved satisfactory short-term clinical outcomes for PCL tibial avulsion fractures, particularly in patients with small or comminuted fragments. Further prospective comparative studies are needed to validate its long-term clinical value.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-026-03853-9.Keywords: Posterior medial longitudinal incision, Homemade small T-plate, PCL tibial avulsion, Internal fixation

Introduction

The Posterior Cruciate Ligament (PCL) is essential for maintaining posterior stability, and tibial avulsion fractures account for approximately 20% of knee ligament injuries [1]. Such injuries can result in posterior tibia laxity, leading to abnormal joint stress distribution and accelerated osteoarthritis progression [2, 3]. According to the Meyers-McKeever classification, type II (partially displaced) and type III (completely displaced) fractures necessitate surgical intervention to restore knee stability because of the challenges associated with anatomical repositioning [4, 5, 6].

Currently, treatment methods include traditional posterior surgery and arthroscopic techniques. Traditional posterior surgery is associated with a high risk of neurovascular injury and slow prolonged postoperative recovery due to extensive soft tissues dissection, whereas arthroscopic techniques are minimally invasive but require advanced technical skills, specialized equipment, and a steep learning curve, thereby limiting their use in primary care settings [7, 8, 9]. Furthermore, existing fixation devices (e.g., screws and suture anchors) may have limited effectiveness for small or comminuted fragments, and there is no consensus regarding standardized implant selection. Modified posterior approaches (e.g., oblique incision and inverted L-shaped posteromedial approaches) have also been introduced, with reports of reduced soft-tissue trauma and favorable follow-up outcomes [10].

In this study, we selected a posteromedial longitudinal straight incision because it provides direct access to the avulsion bed through an intermuscular interval while limiting soft-tissue stripping and avoiding the technical complexity of arthroscopy [11, 12]. We combined a mini T-plate with high-strength sutures to address both fragment compression and ligament traction, particularly in small or comminuted fragments where isolated screw fixation may be less feasible [2, 4, 13]. The aim of this study was to evaluate the clinical efficacy and safety of this technique for isolated PCL tibial avulsion fractures (Figs. 1C and 2A-B).

Methods

Patient

With the approval of the Ethics Committee of Subei People’s Hospital, this single-center retrospective study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Inclusion criteria: (1) Acute isolated Meyers-McKeever type II/III PCL tibial avulsion fractures diagnosed by computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). (2) Absence of concomitant ligamentous injuries or neurovascular compromise. (3) Complete follow-up records (≥ 6 months). Exclusion criteria: (1) Chronic fracture (> 2 weeks from injury to surgery). (2) Severe osteoporosis (T-score ≤-2.5) or active systemic infections. During the study period (March 2016 to September 2023), a total of 34 patients with acute isolated PCL tibial avulsion fractures were treated at our institution. Of these, 13 were excluded: 7 had follow-up less than 6 months, 3 had concomitant posterior lateral corner injury, 2 had previous ipsilateral knee surgery, and 1 had chronic fracture (> 2 weeks). The remaining 21 patients formed the study cohort (Fig. 3). 21 eligible patients (17 males, 4 females; mean age 42.6 ± 9.3 years, range: 38–52 years) were enrolled. These cases included 12 left and 9 right, with 6 type II and 15 type III fractures. Mean time from injury to surgery was 4.5 ± 2.6 days (range: 2–10 days). Preoperative management included thromboprophylaxis with low-molecular-weight heparin, standard anteroposterior and lateral knee radiographs, high-resolution CT (0.6-mm slices) for fracture morphology assessment, and 3.0-T MRI to evaluate ligament integrity (Figs. 4F-H and 5A-D).

Surgical technique

All procedures were performed through a posteromedial longitudinal straight incision using a homemade titanium alloy mini T-plate and high-strength polyethylene sutures. The mini T-plate was a commercially available titanium alloy plate (Double Medical Technology Inc., Xiamen, China) with the following typical dimensions: arm length 12–16 mm, stem length 10–14 mm, thickness 1.5–2.0 mm, and 3–10 screw holes of 2.0–2.7 mm in diameter (three holes in the arms, 3–7 holes in the stem). Intraoperatively, the plate was bent using pliers to match the contour of the posterior tibial cortex. The arm length and hole orientation were occasionally trimmed with a cutting pliers to accommodate fragment size, and 2.0–2.7 mm cortical screws were used for fixation. (Fig. 1). Following anesthesia induction, patients were positioned prone on a radiolucent operating table with a pneumatic tourniquet (300 mmHg). The knee was maintained in 20°-30° flexion using a sterile bolster. A 4-cm longitudinal incision was created, extending from the popliteal fossa to the medial border of the gastrocnemius (Fig. 4A). Blunt dissection through the intermuscular interval between the medial gastrocnemius head and semimembranosus allowed lateral retraction of these structures, preserving neurovascular integrity while exposing the posterior knee capsule. Longitudinal capsulotomy was performed to visualize the avulsed PCL fragment (Fig. 4B). Meticulous debridement removed fracture hematoma and interposed soft tissues. The PCL insertion was secured with a locking suture, leaving two free suture ends (Figs. 1B and 4C-D). Under direct visualization with 30° knee flexion, anatomical reduction was achieved. One or both free suture limbs were placed beneath the T-plate. The mini T-plate was then fixed to the posterior tibial insertion site of the PCL with a 3.5-mm cortical screw. The suture was passed through the plate hole and tied securely to reinforce fixation. (Figures 1C and 5E). Intraoperative fluoroscopic verification in both anteroposterior and lateral views confirmed implant positioning and screw length appropriateness. The capsule was closed with interrupted 2 − 0 absorbable sutures, followed by placement of a closed suction drain. Layered closure was performed for subcutaneous tissue and skin using 4 − 0 absorbable sutures.

Postoperative rehabilitation

Postoperative management included pain control, drainage care, and a standardized rehabilitation protocol. The drainage tube was removed 24 h after surgery. Isometric quadriceps exercises were initiated immediately after surgery. Passive knee flexion (0°-60°) was performed on the 2nd postoperative day, with limited weight-bearing for 4 weeks. A gradual transition to partial weight-bearing was made from 4 to 8 weeks, and full weight-bearing was permitted after 8 weeks. Return to unrestricted daily activities was recommended after 24 weeks. Fracture healing was assessed on both anteroposterior and lateral radiographs. Radiographic union was defined as: (1) disappearance of the fracture line or presence of only a vague sclerotic line across the fracture site on at least two orthogonal views, and (2) evidence of trabecular bridging across the fracture site. For patients with equivocal radiographic findings, CT scan was performed to confirm union. All assessments were made by two independent radiologists; any disagreement was resolved by consensus.

Assessment indicators

The primary outcome indicators were (1) radiographic healing time, assessed by X-ray and CT; (2) knee range of motion (ROM), measured with a goniometer; (3) IKDC and Tegner-Lysholm scores for knee function assessment; and (4) posterior drawer test results for stability evaluation. Secondary metrics included operative time, intraoperative bleeding, complication rate, and a heel lift test via single-leg stance to assess gastrocnemius strength. All postoperative clinical assessments, including range of motion measurement, posterior drawer test, and functional scoring (IKDC and Tegner-Lysholm), were performed by a single orthopaedic attending physician who was not involved in the surgical procedures and was blinded to the study objectives. The posterior drawer test was performed with the knee at 90° flexion; the result was recorded as negative if the tibial step-off was within 2 mm compared with the contralateral side. No instrumented laxity measurement was used.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous data were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x̄±s), and paired t-tests were used to compare preoperative and final follow-up measurements. For key continuous outcomes, 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the mean at final follow-up were calculated. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical tests were exploratory. No adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied, although the p-values for the three primary outcomes remained below a Bonferroni-corrected threshold of 0.0167. The study was not powered a priori for hypothesis testing; therefore, p-values should not be interpreted as evidence of clinical superiority over other techniques.

Results

All incisions healed primarily without complications (infection, deep vein thrombosis, or implant failure). Mean operative time was 81.5 ± 20.9 min (range: 45–100 min) with blood loss of 33 ± 11 ml. Radiographic union occurred at 10.9 ± 1.6 weeks (range: 9-12.5 weeks). At final follow-up (mean 10.6 ± 5.2 months): Knee ROM improved from 76.5°±12.1° to 132.2°±4.2° (95% CI: 130.3° to 134.1°; P < 0.05). Tegner-Lysholm scores increased from 45.5 ± 9.3 to 95.5 ± 2.8 (95% CI: 94.2 to 96.8; P < 0.05). IKDC scores improved from 35.9 ± 8.9 to 96.8 ± 1.5 (95% CI: 96.1 to 97.5; P < 0.05).

All patients exhibited negative posterior drawer tests and preserved gastrocnemius strength (Table 1).

Discussion

PCL tibial avulsion fractures require stable anatomical reduction because residual displacement or posterior instability may lead to persistent knee dysfunction and accelerate degenerative changes [1, 3, 14, 15]. Although both traditional open reduction and arthroscopic fixation have been widely used, each has inherent limitations in routine clinical practice [3, 16]. Conventional posterior open approaches often require wider soft-tissue dissection, whereas arthroscopic techniques, despite being minimally invasive, are technically demanding, equipment-dependent, and less accessible in primary hospitals. Therefore, there remains a practical need for a fixation strategy that balances limited surgical trauma, feasible fixation strength, and broad clinical applicability.

The present technique was developed precisely to address this gap. Through a posteromedial longitudinal mini-incision, the fracture bed can be exposed through an intermuscular plane with relatively limited soft-tissue disruption, while direct visualization facilitates anatomical reduction. More importantly, the combination of a mini T-plate and high-strength suture provides a composite fixation construct rather than simple point fixation. This may represent the key technical advantage of the procedure. The T-plate offers buttress and compression for the osseous fragment, whereas the suture component helps control the ligament-bone junction and disperses stress across the avulsed fragment [13, 17]. This configuration may be particularly valuable in cases with relatively small fragments, marginal comminution, or suboptimal screw purchase.

The clinical results of this study support the effectiveness of this concept. All patients achieved fracture union, posterior drawer testing was negative at final follow-up, and both ROM and functional scores improved significantly. These findings suggest that the technique can achieve not only radiographic healing but also restoration of knee stability and functional recovery. From a practical perspective, the consistently favorable healing and functional outcomes indicate that this procedure provides sufficient fixation stability to support postoperative rehabilitation and progressive recovery.

Compared with purely screw-based fixation, the current method may offer broader adaptability for fracture morphology, especially when the fragment is too small or fragile for secure screw compression alone [13, 17]. Compared with arthroscopic fixation, this approach preserves the advantages of direct reduction and straightforward fixation while avoiding the steep learning curve and equipment dependence of advanced arthroscopic procedures [14, 16]. Therefore, rather than replacing all existing techniques, this mini-incision composite fixation method may serve as a valuable alternative option, particularly in institutions where arthroscopic resources or expertise are limited [16].

An additional strength of this technique lies in its translational value. The procedure does not require specialized arthroscopic instrumentation, and the surgical steps are relatively intuitive for trauma surgeons familiar with posteromedial knee exposure [11, 18]. In addition, the implant configuration is economical and widely available. Although a formal cost analysis was not performed, avoiding arthroscopic instrumentation and using commonly available implants may reduce resource requirements and improve feasibility in primary and regional hospitals [16]. For this reason, the technique may be especially meaningful in primary and regional hospitals, where a reproducible and dependable fixation method is often more important than procedural sophistication alone. In this sense, the present method is not merely a modified approach, but a practically relevant solution tailored to real-world clinical settings.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a retrospective single-center study with a limited sample size and no control group, which inherently carries selection bias. Second, we excluded 13 patients (primarily due to incomplete follow-up), which may introduce attrition bias and potentially overestimate treatment effects. Third, the follow-up duration (mean 10.6 months) is insufficient to assess long-term outcomes such as post-traumatic osteoarthritis, late implant failure, or maintenance of knee stability. Fourth, the posterior drawer test is examiner-dependent and subjective; no instrumented laxity measurement was used. Fifth, multiple outcome measures (ROM, Lysholm, IKDC) were tested without adjustment for multiple comparisons; although all p-values remained below a Bonferroni-corrected threshold of 0.0167, the statistical analysis should be considered exploratory. Sixth, functional scores at final follow-up showed minimal variability and approached ceiling values, suggesting a potential ceiling effect that limits sensitivity. Seventh, several potential confounders were not controlled: patient compliance with rehabilitation, fragment size and comminution, injury mechanism and severity, and surgeon experience over the 7-year study period. Eighth, the mini T-plate was manually contoured intraoperatively and not a standardized commercial implant, which limits reproducibility across centers. Therefore, our conclusions are restricted to short-term outcomes in this cohort, and the technique should not be claimed as superior or more reliable than other fixation methods without further comparative studies.

Despite these limitations, the present study suggests that posteromedial mini-incision fixation using a mini T-plate combined with high-strength suture achieved satisfactory short-term clinical and radiographic outcomes in this cohort. These findings suggest that the technique is feasible and may be considered as one treatment option, but its reliability and reproducibility require further validation in prospective comparative studies.

Conclusion

Posteromedial longitudinal mini-incision fixation with a mini T-plate combined with high-strength suture achieved satisfactory clinical outcomes for PCL tibial avulsion fractures. The proposed 5T concept summarizes its main practical advantages, and the technique may represent a feasible and reproducible treatment option, particularly in resource-limited settings. Further prospective comparative studies are required to validate its long-term clinical value.
