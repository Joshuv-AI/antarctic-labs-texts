Reevaluation of prognostic and severity indicators for COVID-19 patients in the emergency department
Authors: Yi-Jie Zhang, Xiao-Yan Liu, Wan-Xin Xu, Ya-Peng Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsThis study aimed to re-evaluate whether the scoring systems, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) were effective in predicting prognosis and severity of COVID-19 patients in the emergency department (ED).MethodsCOVID-19 patients enrolled in this retrospective study divided into the death (DEA) and survival (SUR) groups, the severe/critical (SC) and non-severe/critical (non-SC) groups. The Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE) II, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA), National Early Warning Score (NEWS) and CCEDRRN COVID-19 Mortality Score were calculated. The neutrophil, lymphocyte and platelet counts were extracted from the first routine blood examination, and NLR and PLR were calculated accordingly. Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve and logistic regression were performed.ResultsAll the scoring systems, as well as NLR and PLR, significantly increased in both the DEA and SC groups. The ROC curve showed that the CCEDRRN COVID-19 Mortality Score had the highest predictive value for mortality and severity (AUC 0.779, 0.850, respectively), which outperformed the APACHE II, SOFA and NEWS. NLR presented better predictive ability for severity (AUC 0.741) than death (AUC 0.702). The APACHE II, NEWS and CCEDRRN COVID-19 Mortality Score were positively correlated with both prognosis and severity, whereas NLR only with severity.ConclusionThe NEWS and CCEDRRN COVID-19 Mortality Score were reconfirmed for early and rapid predicting the poor prognosis and severity of COVID-19 patients in ED, especially the CCEDRRN COVID-19 Mortality Score with the highest discrimination capacity, and NLR was more appropriate for predicting the severity.Keywords: COVID-19, APACHE II, SOFA, NEWS, CCEDRRN COVID-19 mortality score, NLR, PLR, prognosis, severity

Introduction

Since 2019, the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) pandemic has threatened life, as well as social and economic security. With the continuous mutation, the SARS-CoV-2 variant Omicron has become the main epidemic worldwide due to its more contagious and vaccine escape mutations [1]. After the liberalization of the epidemic prevention policy in December 2022, the number of cases of SARS-CoV-2 infections, almost all initial infections, has surged, posing new challenges to medical care. For physicians in the emergency department (ED), it is crucial to quickly diagnose, identify severity and provide appropriate treatment in the shortest possible time.

As known, age, obesity and chronic diseases such as hypertension, diabetes, etc. are high-risk groups for severe coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19). Furthermore, several biomarkers, such as C-reactive protein (CRP), D-dimer, ferritin, troponin I, interleukin-6, the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), the platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) and so on, can be used as prognostic and severity significance in COVID-19 [2–9]. Particularly, NRL has been confirmed as an indicator to be associated with severity and effectively predict mortality of COVID-19 [4,6,7]. Meanwhile, several existing and novel early warning scoring systems, such as the National Early Warning Score (NEWS) and its derivatives, the Modified Early Warning Score (MEWS), the Rapid Emergency Medicine Score (REMS), the Canadian COVID-19 Emergency Department Rapid Response Network (CCEDRRN), have been validated to predict poor prognosis and disease deterioration in COVID-19 patients [10–17]. Of which, the NEWS and its derivatives have been widely validated and recommended for triage decisions in patients with COVID-19 for its predictive value for adverse outcomes [18]. The CCEDRRN COVID-19 Mortality Score exhibited excellent predictive value in ED and in-hospital mortality [17].

Upon considering the majority of patients in the ED are elderly with many underlying comorbidities, the underlying disease exacerbation and its resulting complications can also contribute to a poor prognosis for COVID-19 patients. In light of the complexity of these patients, it is imperative to further validated whether the SARS-CoV-2 mutation, frequency of infection, variations in epidemic periods and regions affect the efficacy of these prognostic predicators. This study retrospectively analysed the COVID-19 patients presenting to the ED of our hospital and further analysed the predictive value of indicators for prognosis and severity including the NLR, PLR, NEWS and CCEDRRN COVID-19 Mortality Score, as well as Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE II) and Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) scores.

Methods

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at the Department of Emergency of Beijing Shijitan Hospital. All procedures adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Beijing Shijitan Hospital (No. IIT2024-008-002), and the consent was approved for waiver due to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of anonymized data.

Patients

All adult patients (≥ 18 years) presenting to the ED with a positive RT-PCR SARS-CoV-2 pharyngeal swab for the first time and complete clinical data were enrolled from December 7, 2022, to June 1, 2023. Exclusion criteria: patients less than 18 years old, patients previously infected with COVID-19, patients abandoning treatment, patients lost follow-up due to automatic discharge, transfer to other medical institutions, or other reasons. Patients were followed up for 28 days, or until discharge if more than 28 days (Figure 1).

Flow diagram of the study.

The included and excluded patients presenting to the emergency department (ED), as well as the patients in each group, were shown in the flow diagram.

Based on the National Institutes of Health guidelines, all patients were classified into five distinct types: Asymptomatic or Presymptomatic Infection, Mild, Moderate, Severe and Critical [19]. All patients were divided into two groups according to prognosis: the death (DEA) group and survival (SUR) group. Meanwhile, the severe/critical (SC) and non-severe/critical (non-SC) groups were classified according to type to assess the predictive value for severity (Figure 1).

Data collection and scoring systems

Data on age, sex and co-existing chronic diseases (such as cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, diabetes mellitus, chronic lung diseases, chronic kidney diseases, malignancy and immune system diseases) were collected. APACHE II, SOFA, NEWS and CCEDRRN COVID-19 Mortality Score were calculated at ED arrival. NEWS ranged from 0 to 20 comprises of pulse rate, respiratory rate, body temperature, systolic blood pressure, oxygen saturation and need for oxygen supplement [20]. The CCEDRRN COVID-19 Mortality Score ranged from −1 to 19 comprises of age, sex, arrival from (Home or community/No fixed address/Institution), arrival mode, chest pain, moderate/severe liver disease, arrival respiratory rate, mode and level of oxygen in emergency department [17]. Details of the two scores were listed in Table S1. The neutrophil (N), lymphocyte (L) and platelet (Plt) counts were extracted from the first routine blood examination and the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) were calculated accordingly.

Statistical analysis

For normally distributed continuous variables, the data are presented as the mean ± SD or median and interquartile range if not. To compare continuous variables. Statistical comparisons between the two groups were performed using the two-tailed Student’s t-test for normally distributed continuous variables or the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed continuous variables. Categorical data presented as frequency and percentage were compared using the chi-squared test. Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves were created, the areas under the curve (AUC) were calculated to assess the predictive power, and the optimal cut-off values were obtained according to Youden’s index. The predictive value was classified based on the AUC as excellent discrimination (AUC value: 0.99-0.9), very good discrimination (AUC value: 0.89-0.8), good (AUC value: 0.79-0.7), moderate (AUC value: 0.69-0.6), or poor (AUC value: < 0.6) [21]. Meanwhile, Odds ratios (OR) and their 95% CI were estimated using logistic regression to determine the association of indicators with prognosis and severity. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. The data were analysed using SPSS 20.0.

Results

General patient characteristics

A total of 364 patients with COVID-19, 218 males (59.9%) and 146 females (40.1%), were enrolled. Patients ranged in age from 25 to 101 years (78.44 ± 15.29 years), of which 313 (85.99%) patients were ≥ 65 years old. As shown in Table 1, higher proportion of males was in the DEA and SC groups, and the age was significantly higher than those in the SUR and non-SC groups, respectively. Of all the patients, 158 (43.41%) died of all causes, 220 (60.44%) were classified as severe or critical. The results of intergroup comparison of comorbidity were shown in Table 1. In general, 49 (13.46%) had no comorbidity, 93 (25.55%) patients had one chronic disease as described above, 93 (25.55%) had two and 129 (35.44%) had three or more chronic diseases.

ROC curve and logistics regression analysis

The ROC curve was used to evaluate the value of the indicators for predicting poor prognosis and severity. The results showed that the APACHE II, SOFA, NEWS and CCEDRRN COVID-19 Mortality Score could all effectively predict poor prognosis and severity, especially the NEWS and CCEDRRN COVID-19 Mortality Score (Figure 2A-1, B-1, Table 3): APACHE II (AUC 0.745, cut-off =11.5), SOFA (AUC 0.731, cut-off =2.5), NEWS (AUC 0.763, cut-off =5.5) and CCEDRRN COVID-19 Mortality Score (AUC 0.779, cut-off =11.5) for death; APACHE II (AUC 0.794, cut-off =9.5), SOFA (AUC 0.784, cut-off =2.5), NEWS (AUC 0.820, cut-off =5.5) and CCEDRRN COVID-19 Mortality Score (AUC 0.850, cut-off =11.5) for severity. NLR (AUC 0.741, cut-off =6.529) and PLR (AUC 0.714, cut-off =235.489) presented better predictive values for severity than death (Figure 2A-2, B-2, Table 3). In addition, the predictive value of N was moderate, with the value of AUC 0.681 for death and 0.683 for severity (Figure 2A-2, B-2, Table 3). The Plt value indicated a moderate ability to predict the severity (Figure 2B-2, Table 3). Intriguingly, the results showed that the value of L was able to predict survival (AUC 0.609) and non-severity (AUC 0.668), but not death or severity (Figure 2A-3, B-3, Table 3). Discussion

SARS-CoV-2 has been raging worldwide since 2019, posing a significant threat to human life and health. With a deeper understanding, more optimal treatment strategies and drugs have been developed to prevent and treat SARS-CoV-2 infection. Nonetheless, high mortality still threatens severe/critical COVID-19 patients. Additionally, SARS-CoV-2 infection attacks not only the respiratory system but also the cardiovascular, clotting, digestive, nervous and immune systems, contributing to a poor prognosis. In the ED, elderly patients account for a large proportion, often complicated by a variety of chronic diseases characterized by atypical symptoms and rapid disease progression. Therefore, prompt and readily accessible indicators for predicting prognosis and severity are critical for these patients. Some inflammatory markers, such as D-dimer and ferritin, may have potential prognostic value for COVID-19 [8,9], but their application is limited due to non-routine examination items in some healthcare settings and/or the long time required for testing. Previous studies have demonstrated that CRP, NLR and PLR, which are simple to calculate and readily available, can be used as predictors of prognosis and severity for COVID-19 patients [5–7]. Moreover, some early warning scoring systems have also been confirmed to be valuable in predicting mortality and ICU admission [10–15,17], but rarely in severity. Therefore, this study respectively analysed the biomarkers and early warning scoring systems simultaneously, and re-evaluated the capacity in predicting severity and prognosis for COVID-19 patients in the ED.

The probability of severe COVID-19 is higher in people aged ≥ 65 years, and in those with certain chronic medical conditions [19]. The increased susceptibility of older people to COVID-19 may be attributed to their weaker immune defense [22], more comorbidities [22], ageing-related chronic pro-inflammatory status of the immune system with persistent low-grade innate immune activation, and higher levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines [22–24]. In this study, 85.99% of the enrolled patients were older than 65 years, and 86.54% had one or more chronic medical diseases. The ages of the DEA and SC groups were significantly higher than those of the SUR and non-SC groups. Moreover, previous studies have shown that men have a higher risk of infection, disease severity, intensive care unit (ICU) admission and death than women [25,26], which may be related to weaker immunity due to genetic and hormonal factors [24], the effects of serum testosterone [27,28], lifestyle that predisposes to infection (such as smoking) [24], and higher levels of ACE2 on the endothelium of the pulmonary vessels [22]. Our study also showed significantly more male patients in both the DEA and SC groups, which is consistent with previous studies.

The APACHE II and SOFA scores, which are the most used risk scores to predict mortality in the ICU, have been demonstrated in many studies to be effective tools in predicting mortality in ICU patients with COVID-19 [29–33]. However, both scoring systems are more extensive and time-consuming, not suitable for quick discrimination, especially in times of surging patient numbers and relative scarcity of medical resources during the COVID-19 pandemic. The NEWS, launched in 2012 as an early warning scores was confirmed to be effective at identifying/stratifying acutely ill patients with COVID-19 and their deterioration [13–16]. Nevertheless, the study in Sudan suggested that none of the triage scores including the NEWS2 assessed demonstrated sufficient accuracy to be used clinically likely due to differences in the health care system and population [34]. Carr E et al. highlighted that NEWS2 had poor-to-moderate discrimination for medium-term COVID-19 outcome, and a supplemented model adding eight routinely collected blood and physiological parameters (supplemental oxygen flow rate, urea, age, oxygen saturation, CRP, estimated glomerular filtration rate, neutrophil count, NLR) could improve discrimination [14], albeit at the cost of increased time consumption. Most noteworthy, a multicenter observational study in Canada developed and validated that the CCEDRRN COVID-19 Mortality Score, a simple score including eight variables available on ED arrival, could be used for level-of-care discussions with patients and accurately predicting death in situations of critical care resource constraints with the excellent AUC value of 0.92 [17]. This findings need to be further validated in other countries. In this study, the APACHE II, SOFA, NEWS and CCEDRRN COVID-19 Mortality Score were simultaneously reevaluated for the ability to predict the poor prognosis and severity of COVID-19 patients presenting in the ED. The results demonstrated that APACHE II and SOFA scores were effective predictors of mortality as well as severity in COVID-19 patients, with an AUC of 0.731-0.794, especially APACHE II score which positively correlated with prognosis and severity shown in logistic regression analysis, but not SOFA. Notably, the NEWS and CCEDRRN COVID-19 Mortality Score showed higher discrimination capacity in mortality and severity, especially in severity with the AUG value >0.8, and was positively correlated with both prognosis and severity, which outperformed the APACHE II and SOFA scores. The CCEDRRN COVID-19 Mortality Score had the highest predictive value of all indicators, although the AUC value was lower than in the previous study. This difference may be related to different countries, inclusion/exclusion criteria, sample size and so on.

Coronavirus infection can cause physiological stress on the human body, which is characterized by elevated levels of cortisol and catecholamines, which in turn causes leukocytosis and lymphopenia [35]. NLR, as the ratio of neutrophils to lymphocytes, has been proven to predict prognosis, severity, and mortality in many conditions, such as hepatocellular cancer, breast cancer, neonatal sepsis and bloodstream infections [4]. Recent studies have also demonstrated that NLR can well predict the severity and poor prognosis of COVID-19, and is superior to neutrophils, lymphocytes and PLR [4–7]. A systematic review and meta-analysis indicated that the AUC of NLR was 0.81 (95% CI 0.77–0.84) for predicting severity and 0.86 (95% CI 0.83–0.89) for predicting mortality [4]. According to our results, neutrophil count was significantly elevated, accompanied by lymphocyte degradation both in the DEA and SC groups, and platelet was only elevated in the SC group. The predictive value of neutrophils for prognosis and severity as well as platelet count for severity was poor-moderate. Conversely, lymphocytes were able to predict survival and non-severity, but not death or severity. The predictive values of NLR and PLR improved compared to those of N, L and Plt. Notably, NLR (AUC value 0.741) and PLR (AUC value 0.714) were good predictors of severity. However, the value of predicting death was even lower (AUC value 0.633-0.702), which could mainly contribute to other factors associated with death, such as age, comorbidities, etc. The subgroup analysis revealed that the predictive power of NLR was affected by disease type, ages and so on [4]. Additionally, logistic regression analysis consistently indicated that only NLR had a good positive correlation with the severity of COVID-19. In summary, our study confirmed that NLR could be used as effective predictors, especially for severity in COVID-19 patients in the ED.

This study was conducted in a relatively large group of patients; however, there were limitations. All patients enrolled with COVID-19 for the first time were all from the ED, and severe/critical patients accounted for a large proportion. Specific patient structures may have contributed to bias in the results, despite being a true reality. Second, this was a single-center study, which needs to be confirmed by further multi-center studies with larger samples.

Conclusion

This study re-evaluated the predictive value of indicators in patients with COVID-19 for the first time in the ED. The results revealed that The NEWS and CCEDRRN COVID-19 Mortality Score, as simple early warning scoring systems, were reconfirmed for early and rapid clinical screening in COVID-19 patients. The CCEDRRN COVID-19 Mortality Score had the highest discrimination capacity which outperformed the APACHE II, SOFA and NEWS in predicting the poor prognosis and severity of COVID-19 patients in the ED. NLR was more appropriate for predicting the severity of COVID-19 patients in the ED.
