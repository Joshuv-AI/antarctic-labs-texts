Attitudes toward cancer screening and its’ associated factors: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Naeimeh Sarkhani, Reza Negarandeh, Negin Farhoudi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCancer is one of the leading public health challenges globally. Despite the availability of effective screening methods and treatments, participation in cancer screening programs remains low in many regions. Evidence suggests that individuals’ attitudes toward screening significantly influence their participation. This study aimed to investigate attitudes toward cancer screening and its associated factors.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted on 450 individuals aged 30 to 70 years who visited comprehensive health centers in southern Tehran from September to December 2024. A multistage sampling method was used: one health center was randomly selected from each of the five districts, and eligible participants were consecutively recruited. Data were collected using a demographic questionnaire and the Persian version of the Attitude Scale for Cancer Screening (ASCS-P15). Statistical analysis was done using the Mann–Whitney U test, Kruskal–Wallis test, and Spearman correlation. A significance level of P < 0.05 was considered.ResultsThe median (interquartile range) of attitudes toward cancer screening was 63 (19.25), with a range of scores achieved from 16 to 75. Significant associations were found between attitude scores and gender, education level, monthly income, and family cancer history (P < 0.05). Men reported more positive attitudes compared with women. Participants with a family history of cancer also demonstrated significantly higher overall attitude scores than those without such a history. The “Willing Attitude” subscale was significantly associated with family cancer history, income, education, employment status, and age. The “Unwilling Attitude” subscale was linked to family cancer history, income, and using fast food (P < 0.05).ConclusionAlthough the overall attitude toward cancer screening was positive among the study population, low participation rates suggest that other barriers may exist. Future research should focus on identifying these barriers to improve the effectiveness and reach of screening programs.Keywords: Attitude, Screening, Cancer, Iran

Introduction

Cancer is rapidly expanding as one of the leading causes of mortality worldwide [1]. It is projected that the global cancer burden will reach 28.4 million cases by 2040. The increase in cancer burden is expected to be more pronounced in developing countries (rising from 64% to 95%) compared to developed nations (from 32% to 56%), primarily due to risk factors such as tobacco use, unhealthy diets, and physical inactivity [2]. In Iran, similar to global trends, the incidence of cancer has been on the rise. In 2020, more than 70,000 new cancer cases and over 79,000 cancer-related deaths were reported in the country [3]. According to estimates by the International Agency for Research on Cancer, the most prevalent cancers among women in Iran are breast, stomach, colorectal, lung, and thyroid cancers, while the most common cancers among men include stomach, prostate, lung, colorectal, and bladder cancers [4].

In response to the high incidence and mortality rates of cancer in Iran, the Iranian Ministry of Health launched the National Comprehensive Cancer Control Program in 2007. This program includes initiatives for prevention and early detection, such as screening programs for breast, colorectal, prostate, and cervical cancers [5]. Cancer screening is a critical preventive measure for reducing disease burden and cancer-related mortality, ultimately improving patient survival rates [6]. All types of health services—such as laboratory tests and clinical examinations—that are conducted primarily among at-risk populations before the onset of symptoms and signs are considered cancer screening [7]. Screening is a multi-step care process that involves patients, organizations, and healthcare providers [8]. The goal of screening is to identify people with abnormalities that suggest specific cancer or precancer that have not caused any symptoms and to refer them promptly for diagnosis and treatment [9]. The World Health Organization recommends population-based screening programs for the early detection of breast, cervical, and colorectal cancers [10]. In Iran, according to the National Early Cancer Detection Program guideline developed in late 2022, cervical and breast cancer screening begins at the age of 30, while colorectal cancer screening starts at the age of 50 [11]. Cancer screening programs are designed to reduce disease-related complications and outcomes, prevent recurrence and metastasis, and enhance quality of life. Therefore, participation in such programs is of critical importance [7].

Although a range of cancer screening modalities is available within the community, participation rates remain suboptimal [12, 13]. Considerable research has been dedicated to elucidating the factors contributing to this underutilization. Studies have identified numerous barriers to screening participation, including cultural beliefs about disease, perceived invulnerability, lack of personal responsibility for health, financial constraints, fear of positive results, negative attitudes toward cancer, limited social support, and levels of religiosity [12–15]. Furthermore, a growing body of literature underscores the pivotal influence of attitudes toward cancer screening, with evidence indicating a significant association between individuals’ attitudes and their engagement in screening behaviors [16, 17].

Attitude is a psychological tendency expressed by evaluating a particular entity with some degree of favor or disfavor. It encompasses an individual’s feelings, beliefs, and behavioral intentions toward an object, person, or situation, and influences how they respond to related experiences or information [18]. Specifically, having a positive attitude serves as a supportive factor for an individual’s overall health, and the nature of this attitude influences how a person copes with cancer [19]. Kassie et al. demonstrated in their study that the use of cervical cancer screening tests was 6.1 times higher among women with a positive attitude toward screening tests [20]. Considering the rising incidence of cancer, its complications and consequences, the high cost of treatment, and the evidence that a positive attitude toward cancer and screening methods contributes to early detection, timely treatment, and better outcomes, the importance of addressing attitudes toward cancer screening has become increasingly evident.

Moreover, in Iran, no comprehensive study has specifically examined attitudes toward cancer screening and the associated factors. Most existing studies have focused on attitudes toward screening programs for specific cancer types, such as breast cancer [21], cervical cancer [22], or colorectal cancer [23], or have assessed attitudes toward cancer as a disease [5]. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate individuals’ attitudes towards cancer screening and its associated factors.

Research question

What is the level of attitude toward cancer screening among Iranian adults, and what are the associated factors?

Methods

Design

This cross-sectional study was conducted from October to December 2024 in comprehensive health centers located in the southern districts of Tehran.

Setting and sample

The study sample included all adults aged between 30 and 70 years referring to comprehensive health centers in South Tehran affiliated with Tehran University of Medical Sciences. This range was chosen to align with the National Early Cancer Detection Program guideline and because it represents the target population for which the research questionnaire was developed and validated. The sample size was estimated using the formula for calculating the mean of a single group [24]. Based on the results of a pilot study, Zα/2 was considered as 1.96, the standard deviation (S) as 11.02, the type I error (α) as 0.05, and the precision (d) as 1.1. Using the formula N = (Zα/2)² × S²/d², the required sample size was calculated to be 400 participants. Considering a 10% non-response rate [25], the final sample size was set at 444 individuals. Inclusion criteria were: men and women aged 30–70 years, residents of the five target districts, and absence of severe physical, cognitive, or learning disabilities. A multistage sampling method was employed. First, one health center was randomly selected from each of the five southern districts of Tehran (Districts 10, 11, 16, 17, and 19). In the second stage, eligible participants who were present at the selected centers during the study period were consecutively selected, reaching a total of 450 participants.

Data collection

The researcher conducted on-site visits to each of the five selected centers to facilitate participant recruitment. The questionnaires were self-administered by the participants at the comprehensive health centers in the five southern districts of Tehran during their visit. The researcher remained available at each location to provide clarification or assistance with the questionnaire as needed. The estimated time required to complete the questionnaire was approximately 10 to 15 min.

Data collection instruments

Data were collected using two instruments: (1) A researcher-made questionnaire on demographic characteristics and factors associated with attitudes toward cancer screening, and (2) the Persian version of the Attitude Scale for Cancer Screening (ASCS-P15) [26]. The first questionnaire included variables such as age, gender, marital status, employment status, income level, educational attainment, smoking history, regular physical activity (engaging in moderate- to vigorous-intensity physical activity for at least 150 min per week), using fast food, history of chronic disease, family history of cancer, history of cancer screening, and the time of the last screening. These items were derived based on a review of the relevant literature. The validated Persian version of the Attitude Scale for Cancer Screening, a self-administered scale, was used to assess attitudes toward cancer screening [26]. The original scale (ASCS-15) was developed in 2020 by Öztürk et al. in Turkey for use among adults aged 30–70, and all psychometric evaluations were conducted by the original developers [7]. The ASCS-15 was translated into Persian and psychometrically validated by Sarkhani et al. in 2023. The face and content validity of the Persian version were confirmed. Unlike the original unidimensional scale, the Persian version identified two subscales: Subscale 1, labeled “Willing Attitude,” includes 9 items (items 1–9), and Subscale 2, labeled “Unwilling Attitude,” includes 6 items (items 10–15). The reliability of this instrument has been reported to exceed 0.80 [26]. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale (5 = Strongly agree, 4 = Somewhat agree, 3 = Neither agree nor disagree, 2 = Somewhat disagree, 1 = Strongly disagree). When calculating the total score, six items (items 10–15) must be reverse-scored. The total score ranges from 15 to 75, with scores closer to 15 indicating a more negative attitude toward cancer screening, and scores closer to 75 indicating a more positive attitude. Accordingly, in the “Willing Attitude” subscale, scores closer to 45 reflect greater willingness or a more positive attitude toward undergoing cancer screening, whereas in the “Unwilling Attitude” subscale, scores closer to 6 indicate less willingness or a more negative attitude. As the instrument measures attitudes, it does not employ a predefined cut-off score. In this study, to evaluate the reliability of the instrument, the test–retest method was employed. The questionnaire was administered twice to a sample of 20 eligible participants with a 10-day interval between administrations. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 24, and the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated based on a two-way mixed-effects model, single-rating, absolute-agreement type. The ICC values less than 0.5 indicate poor reliability, values between 0.5 and 0.75 indicate moderate reliability, values between 0.75 and 0.9 indicate good reliability, and values greater than 0.90 indicate excellent reliability [27]. The ICC value was 0.86, with a 95% confidence interval (CI) ranging from 0.64 to 0.92, indicating good reliability. Additionally, Cronbach’s alpha for internal consistency was calculated at 0.88.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Approval for the study was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Tehran University of Medical Sciences, Tehran, Iran (Ethics Code: IR.TUMS.FNM.REC.1402.230). Prior to data collection, the research objectives and the contents of the questionnaire were clearly explained to all participants. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant. For illiterate participants, the study objectives and procedures were thoroughly explained, and written informed consent was obtained from their legal guardians. Only individuals who provided informed consent were invited to complete the questionnaire. The researcher assured all participants of the confidentiality of their responses and emphasized that the data would be used solely for research purposes.

Statistical analyses

Data were analyzed using Jamovi software version 2.3. Both descriptive statistics (frequency, percentage, interquartile range, median, mean, and standard deviation) and inferential statistics were employed. For items with missing responses, the missing values were replaced by the mean score of all available responses for that specific item. Based on the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, the distribution of the variable “attitude toward cancer screening” and its subscales was not normal (p < 0.05). Therefore, the Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare differences in attitude scores between binary variables such as gender, smoking, regular exercise, family history of cancer, and screening history. The Kruskal–Wallis test was used to examine differences in attitude scores across multi-level variables, including marital status, employment status, monthly income, level of education, using fast food, history of chronic disease, and last cancer screening time. The results are interpreted as differences in median scores between groups. In the case of statistically significant results, pairwise comparisons were performed to identify which specific groups differed. Spearman’s correlation coefficient was applied to assess the relationship between age (a continuous variable) and the attitude toward cancer screening and its subscales. Similarly, Spearman’s correlation coefficient was also used to examine the relationship between the Willing Attitude and Unwilling Attitude scores. The strength of correlations was interpreted based on the Spearman correlation coefficient (r) as follows: weak (0.1 ≤ |r| < 0.3), moderate (0.3 ≤ |r| < 0.5), strong (0.5 ≤ |r| < 1.0), and perfect (|r| = 1) [27]. A significance level of p < 0.05 was considered in all analyses.

Significant differences in overall attitudes toward cancer screening were observed according to gender, family history of cancer, monthly income, and level of education. Specifically, men reported more positive attitudes compared with women. Participants with a family history of cancer also demonstrated significantly higher overall attitude scores than those without such a history. Analysis of the Willing Attitude subscale revealed significant associations with family history of cancer, monthly income, level of education, employment status, and age. Post hoc comparisons indicated that participants with a diploma education level showed more favorable attitudes compared with those who were illiterate. Moreover, individuals with adequate monthly income reported significantly higher positive attitudes compared to those with inadequate income. The Unwilling Attitude subscale was significantly associated with family history of cancer, monthly income, and using fast food. Post hoc analysis showed that participants who consumed fast food monthly had a more favorable attitude than those who consumed it weekly. Spearman’s correlation coefficient showed a weak but significant negative correlation between age and Willing Attitude (p < 0.05). No other significant correlations were observed between age and the overall attitude score (p > 0.05) or unwilling attitude (p > 0.05) (Table 1).

As presented in Table 2, the median overall attitude score toward cancer screening was 63 (IQR = 19.25), with observed scores ranging from 16 to 75, indicating a generally positive attitude among participants. Likewise, the Willing Attitude subscale demonstrated a median score of 42 (IQR = 6), further reflecting predominantly positive attitudes. Conversely, the Unwilling Attitude subscale yielded a median score of 28 (IQR = 5), suggesting comparatively lower levels of negative attitudes toward cancer screening. Additionally, Spearman’s correlation analysis revealed a moderate positive correlation between Willing and Unwilling Attitudes (r = 0.45, p < 0.001), suggesting that higher scores in one subscale were associated with higher scores in the other.

Discussion

The present study is the first study conducted in Iran to investigate individuals’ attitudes towards cancer screening and its associated factors. In our study, the overall attitude score toward cancer screening was positive. A positive attitude toward cancer screening appears promising for reducing the global burden of cancer, as it can significantly influence individuals’ participation in screening programs. Recent studies have also reported similar levels of positive attitudes toward cancer screening [6–8]. Findings from international research indicate that attitudes toward cancer screening are generally positive across many societies. For example, a study conducted in China by Huang et al. reported that individuals at high risk held a positive attitude toward colorectal cancer screening [9]. Similarly, evidence from a review study indicated that while ethnic minorities in China generally expressed positive attitudes toward cancer screening, such favorable perceptions were not consistently translated into preventive health behaviors or actual participation. Indeed, most studies reported screening attendance rates of less than 40% [28]. Additionally, a study in France demonstrated that both smokers and non-smokers expressed positive attitudes toward lung cancer screening [29]. In a systematic review, Sahu et al. also reported that the Indian population had favorable attitudes toward cancer screening methods [6].

However, some studies have highlighted negative attitudes toward cancer screening [21, 30]. For instance, a systematic review conducted that examined international studies up to 2021 revealed that women’s attitudes toward breast cancer screening were predominantly negative, this being identified as one of the main barriers to participation in screening programs [21]. Previous research also indicated that adults with chronic illnesses, limited cancer knowledge, and the belief that cancer is unpreventable had lower attitudes toward cancer screening, highlighting the impact of misconceptions and health status on screening behavior [30]. Several factors may contribute to explaining the positive attitude toward cancer screening observed in the present study. These include the educational level of participants — with the highest proportion holding university degrees — access to screening services, and the implementation of national cancer control programs. In addition, the influence of social desirability on responses may have played a role. Furthermore, employment status appeared to be a noteworthy variable that could enhance a positive attitude toward cancer screening.

In our study, similar to the findings of several previous studies, no statistically significant associations were found between attitude toward cancer screening and variables such as age, marital status, employment status, smoking history, regular physical activity, healthy eating habits, history of chronic diseases, prior cancer screening experience, and time since last screening [8, 31, 32]. However, a significant association was observed between participants’ gender and their attitude toward cancer screening, with men displaying more positive attitudes than women. This finding is consistent with a study conducted in Konya, Turkey, involving 334 participants aged 30–70 years, which reported that women had more negative attitudes toward cancer screening compared to men [7]. In contrast, a study conducted in Saudi Arabia found that women exhibited highly positive attitudes toward cancer screening [33]. Attitude plays a crucial role in shaping individual behavior, and positive attitudes are more likely to be reflected in actual health-related behaviors compared to negative ones [6]. Therefore, promoting positive attitudes among women toward cancer screening may encourage more consistent participation in screening programs.

In the present study, individuals with diploma education level exhibited more positive attitudes toward cancer screening compared to those who were illiterate or had only primary or secondary education. This finding is consistent with the results of several studies [8, 33]. This phenomenon can be explained by the assumption that individuals with higher education levels tend to have more positive attitudes because they are generally more health-conscious and have better access to information, enabling them easier access to evidence regarding cancer screening. However, a study conducted among 254 municipal employees in Ankara found no significant relationship between education level and attitudes toward cancer screening [34]. Moreover, higher education does not always guarantee a more positive attitude toward cancer screening. For instance, Öztürk et al. found that as education level increased, positive attitudes toward cancer screening actually diminished [7].

In this study, individuals with sufficient income demonstrated more positive attitudes toward cancer screening compared to those with insufficient or somewhat sufficient income. Supporting this, a previous study indicated that people with social security and incomes equal to or exceeding their expenses had more positive attitudes toward cancer screening than those with low income [8]. Conversely, studies reported no significant association between income status and attitudes toward cancer screening [32, 34]. Consistent with the present study’s findings, A study in northern Ethiopia showed that individuals with a monthly income above 1500 Ethiopian birr had more positive attitudes toward cervical cancer screening compared to those with lower incomes [35]. Socioeconomic factors such as income influence participation in cancer screening [8]. It is hypothesized that the more positive attitude of higher-income individuals may be due to their more frequent visits to healthcare facilities and greater access to information through various channels such as mass media (television and radio) and social media. Therefore, individuals with lower monthly incomes require special emphasis in the design and implementation of interventions aimed at improving positive attitudes toward cancer screening.

The findings of this study also indicated a significant relationship between participants’ attitudes toward cancer screening and a family history of cancer. In contrast, several studies have reported no significant effect of family history on attitudes toward cancer screening [6, 30, 34]. A study assessing attitudes toward cancer screening among 349 patients yielded results similar to the present study, reporting a positive impact of family history on screening attitudes [31]. This finding can be explained by the fact that individuals with a family history of cancer tend to have more information about cancer screening and pay greater attention to the risk of genetic transmission. It is important to raise awareness in the community that although individuals may be healthy, they might still be at risk of developing cancer due to unmodifiable factors such as genetics. Participation in organized screening programs can facilitate early detection and improve outcomes.

Considering that the instrument used in this study encompassed two subscales—Positive attitude toward cancer screening (referred to as willing Attitude) and negative attitude toward cancer screening (referred to as unwilling Attitude)—the results indicated that individuals with a positive attitude toward screening simultaneously expressed a lower degree of negative attitude. Among these factors, family history of cancer and monthly income were identified as common determinants across both attitude subscales toward screening, underscoring the critical role of these variables in shaping individuals’ attitudes toward cancer screening.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, it is the first investigation of attitudes toward cancer screening and its associated factors among Iranian adults aged 30 to 70 years. Second, it was conducted with an adequate sample size. However, the study also has the following limitations:

Firstly, it was conducted solely among the urban population attending comprehensive health centers in the southern districts of Tehran; therefore, caution should be exercised when generalizing the findings to rural populations or urban populations with different socioeconomic statuses. Secondly, due to illiteracy among some participants, the questionnaire items were administered by an interviewer, which may have introduced information bias. We recommend that future studies be conducted across diverse populations throughout the country.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that the overall attitude toward cancer screening is positive. Gender, educational level, monthly income, and family history of cancer were significantly associated with participants’ attitude scores. The positive attitude observed in this study may be attributed to ongoing awareness campaigns and regular educational efforts conducted by health center professionals. Despite the favorable attitude toward cancer screening, previous studies indicate that the actual uptake of screening remains low. Therefore, the key question is: What factors hinder participation in screening programs despite the generally positive attitudes? Therefore, it is recommended that researchers conduct studies to identify barriers to participation in cancer screening and that officials use this information to increase the rate of public participation in screening programs.
