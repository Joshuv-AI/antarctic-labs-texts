Primary Tracheal Rosai‐Dorfman Disease Coexisting With Active Pulmonary Tuberculosis
Authors: Shijie Yang, Shaoling Li, Wenwen Sun, Ye Gu, Li Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRosai‐Dorfman disease (RDD) rarely involves the trachea, and active pulmonary tuberculosis (TB) has not been reported with tracheal RDD. A 35‐year‐old woman presented with progressive cough, wheezing, and shortness of breath for 1 month. Chest CT revealed a tracheal mass causing > 90% airway narrowing and left lung nodules suggestive of TB. Emergency bronchoscopic resection using high‐frequency electrocautery relieved the obstruction. Histopathology showed emperipolesis and immunohistochemistry was consistent with RDD. Bronchial aspirate was positive for Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex by culture and GeneXpert, without rifampicin resistance. Standard anti‐TB therapy was given. Two‐month CT and one‐year follow‐up bronchoscopy and CT showed a patent airway without recurrence. The patient remained symptom‐free with no other organ involvement. This is the first report of primary tracheal RDD coexisting with active pulmonary TB. Bronchoscopic resection combined with anti‐TB therapy achieved durable disease control at 1 year.Keywords: airway obstruction, bronchoscopy, Rosai‐Dorfman disease, trachea, tuberculosis

Introduction

Rosai‐Dorfman disease (RDD) represents a rare histiocytic proliferation of idiopathic origin. Although extranodal manifestation is observed in approximately 40% of cases, tracheobronchial involvement remains exceptionally scarce, with fewer than 30 instances documented to date [1, 2]. Clinically, airway RDD frequently mimics malignant tumours and may lead to severe airway obstruction requiring urgent intervention [3]. Because the clinical and radiological features are nonspecific, histopathological examination remains essential for establishing the diagnosis. Bronchoscopic resection is generally considered the preferred treatment for localized endobronchial disease [3, 4]. Although RDD has been reported in association with tuberculous lymphadenitis, tracheal RDD occurring with active pulmonary tuberculosis has not been previously described [2, 5]. Here, we present such a case with one‐year follow‐up and discuss the current evidence regarding the diagnosis, differential diagnosis, treatment, and prognosis of airway RDD.

Case Report

A 35‐year‐old Chinese woman with no past medical history presented with progressive cough and dyspnea for 1 month. The cough was dry initially, then with white sputum, worse at night, with wheezing. She denied fever, night sweats, or weight loss.

On examination, inspiratory stridor and reduced left lung breath sounds were noted. No lymphadenopathy or organomegaly.

Laboratory tests: normal CBC, CRP 3.5 mg/L, ESR 22 mm/h, T‐SPOT.TB negative. Arterial blood gas (room air): pH 7.46, PaCO2 34 mmHg, PaO2 58 mmHg, SaO2 89%. Chest CT (Figure 1A) showed a 1.5‐cm mass on the left lower tracheal wall above the carina, narrowing the lumen > 90%, and nodular opacities with calcifications in the left upper lobe. Pulmonary function tests were not performed due to instability.

Combined chest CT and bronchoscopic findings. (A) Pretreatment chest CT: Tracheal mass (arrow). (B) Bronchoscopy before treatment: Obstructing polypoid mass. (C) Immediate post‐resection: Patent airway. (D) One‐year CT: No recurrence. (E) One‐year bronchoscopy: Patent lumen. (F) One‐year autofluorescence bronchoscopy: Magenta discoloration (biopsy: Chronic inflammation).

Emergency flexible bronchoscopy (Figure 1B) revealed a vascular polypoid mass nearly occluding the airway. Urgent therapeutic bronchoscopy under general anaesthesia: the mass was resected using a high‐frequency electrosurgical snare (40 W, blended mode), followed by base electrocoagulation (Figure 1C). The airway was immediately restored, and symptoms improved. No complications. Specimens were sent for pathology and bronchial aspirate (BA).

Histopathology showed fibrovascular tissue with lymphocytes, plasma cells, neutrophils, and large histiocytes. Emperipolesis was observed. Immunohistochemistry: S‐100+, CD68+, CD163+, CK+, Ki67~10%; negative for CD30, CD15, CMV. Acid‐fast and fungal stains negative. Diagnosis: RDD (Figure 2).

Pathological features of tracheal Rosai‐Dorfman disease and post‐treatment biopsy. (A) Low magnification: Inflammatory granulation tissue (arrow) with dense lymphoplasmacytic and histiocytic infiltration; (B) High magnification: Histiocyte‐like cells with emperipolesis (arrow); (C) CD163 staining: Un‐stained lymphocytes within histiocyte‐like cells; (D) Post‐intervention biopsy: No residual tumour.

BA grew Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex on liquid culture; GeneXpert MTB/RIF was positive without rifampicin resistance.

Anti‐tuberculosis therapy: rifampicin 450 mg/day, isoniazid 300 mg/day, ethambutol 750 mg/day, pyrazinamide 1250 mg/day for 2 months, followed by rifampicin and isoniazid for 4 months. No corticosteroids or immunomodulators were given.

At 1 year, the patient was asymptomatic. CT (Figure 1D) showed no tracheal lesion. Bronchoscopy (Figure 1E) revealed a patent airway with smooth mucosa. Fluorescence bronchoscopy showed a magenta area (Figure 1F), but biopsy demonstrated only chronic inflammation (Figure 2D). Anti‐TB therapy had been completed 6 months earlier. No lymph node, skin, bone, or CNS involvement was found.

Discussion

This report describes a rare case of primary tracheal RDD associated with active pulmonary tuberculosis (TB). The patient underwent bronchoscopic resection followed by standard anti‐tubercular therapy, and no recurrence was detected during one year of follow‐up.

The diagnosis of RDD is primarily based on histopathological and immunophenotypic findings rather than clinical or radiological features. Current consensus recommendations emphasize that the hallmark of RDD is the presence of large histiocytes exhibiting emperipolesis, accompanied by an immunophenotype characterized by S‐100, CD68, and CD163 positivity, while Langerhans cell markers (CD1a and langerin) are typically negative. Although emperipolesis may be inconspicuous in some cases, the combination of characteristic morphology and immunohistochemistry remains the diagnostic cornerstone [1, 2, 6]. In our patient, the diagnosis was supported by the presence of prominent emperipolesis together with diffuse S‐100, CD68 and CD163 expression, while the clinical evaluation showed no evidence of systemic disease, consistent with localized extranodal RDD.

Because tracheal RDD usually presents as a polypoid endoluminal mass with nonspecific imaging findings, distinguishing it from other causes of benign tracheal masses before biopsy is challenging [2, 3, 4]. Important differential diagnoses include inflammatory myofibroblastic tumour, inflammatory polyps, tracheobronchial amyloidosis, papilloma, IgG4‐related disease, granulomatous disorders, and other histiocytic disorders such as Langerhans cell histiocytosis [1, 2, 3, 4]. Although imaging and bronchoscopic appearance may help narrow the differential diagnosis, none of these findings is sufficiently specific [2, 4]. Histopathological examination together with immunohistochemistry therefore remains essential for establishing the diagnosis and excluding other benign or malignant conditions [1, 2]. For patients presenting with an isolated tracheal mass, tissue biopsy should be performed whenever feasible because clinical, radiological, and bronchoscopic findings alone are insufficient to establish a definitive diagnosis.

The clinical course of RDD is heterogeneous and largely depends on the extent and location of disease. While some patients experience spontaneous regression without treatment, others develop persistent or recurrent disease requiring repeated intervention [1, 2, 6]. Localized extranodal RDD generally follows a more favourable course than multifocal or disseminated disease, whereas involvement of critical organs such as the central airway may lead to significant morbidity because of mechanical obstruction rather than aggressive biological behaviour [1, 2, 6]. Although airway RDD is uncommon, published case series suggest that local recurrence is not unusual, particularly after incomplete resection, underscoring the importance of long‐term surveillance even in patients with an initially favourable response [3, 6]. Our patient remained free of recurrence during 1 year of follow‐up, consistent with the relatively favourable course reported in patients with localized endoluminal disease.

Treatment of RDD should be individualized according to disease extent, anatomical location, symptom severity, and the presence of organ dysfunction. Current consensus recommendations suggest that observation may be appropriate for asymptomatic patients with limited disease, whereas active treatment is indicated for symptomatic or organ‐threatening lesions. Available therapeutic options include corticosteroids, surgical excision, radiotherapy, conventional immunosuppressive agents, and, more recently, targeted therapies for selected patients with refractory or disseminated disease [1, 2]. For localized airway RDD, however, bronchoscopic intervention is generally considered the preferred initial approach because it provides both definitive tissue diagnosis and immediate relief of airway obstruction while preserving normal airway anatomy [3, 4]. In the present case, the lesion was confined to the tracheal lumen without evidence of systemic involvement. Complete bronchoscopic resection rapidly restored airway patency, and no additional treatment for RDD was required. This favourable outcome supports previous observations that localized endoluminal disease can often be successfully managed with endoscopic treatment alone [3, 4].

Published case series and consensus recommendations suggest that the prognosis of RDD is generally favourable in patients with localized disease, particularly when complete local treatment can be achieved, whereas multifocal or disseminated disease is associated with a higher risk of relapse and may require systemic therapy [1, 2, 6]. In our patient, complete bronchoscopic resection was followed by an uneventful clinical course, and no recurrence was detected during 1 year of follow‐up. The coexistence of active pulmonary tuberculosis makes this case particularly unusual. Although RDD has occasionally been reported in association with tuberculous lymphadenitis, pulmonary tuberculosis accompanying primary tracheal RDD has not previously been described [5]. Whether this represents a causal association or a coincidental finding remains uncertain. Nevertheless, in regions where tuberculosis is prevalent, clinicians should remain alert to the possibility of concomitant infection when evaluating atypical tracheal lesions. Continued long‐term surveillance is recommended because recurrence has been reported even after apparently complete local treatment [1, 3].

In conclusion, primary tracheal RDD should be included in the differential diagnosis of obstructive airway lesions. Histopathology remains essential for diagnosis, and bronchoscopic resection can provide effective local control. The possibility of coexisting tuberculosis should not be overlooked, particularly in regions where TB is prevalent.
