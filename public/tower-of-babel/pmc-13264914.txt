Ontologies and zoonotic risk-related knowledge, attitudes and practices: a cross-sectional survey in Bolivia, Brazil, Chile and Guatemala
Authors: Katja Radon, Caroline Kuhn, Carlos Fernando Pinto Navia, Carlos Roberto Vásquez-Almazán, Denise Siqueira de Carvalho, Luciana Salini Abrahão Pires, Magda Clara Vieira da Costa-Ribeiro, Marcia Adler, María Soledad Burrone, Maria Teresa Solís-Soto, on behalf of KAP Wildlife project team
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo examine whether Indigenous Peoples’ and Local Communities’ (IPLC) ontologies are associated with knowledge, attitudes and practices (KAP) related to wildlife cohabitation and zoonotic disease transmission in biodiversity-rich areas of Latin America.DesignCross-sectional household survey using a standardised KAP questionnaire. Ontologies were classified using latent class analysis. Associations between ontology classes and outcomes were assessed using multivariable logistic regression models.SettingUrban, rural and protected areas in biodiversity-rich regions of Bolivia, Brazil, Chile and Guatemala.ParticipantsA total of 2903 individuals aged ≥10 years were recruited through random household sampling (response rate 85%).Primary and secondary outcome measuresPrimary outcomes were defined according to the KAP framework. Knowledge outcomes comprised combined knowledge of zoonotic disease transmission from wildlife to humans and knowledge of zoonotic risks associated with wildlife trade. Perceived training needs related to zoonotic disease prevention were analysed as a secondary knowledge outcome measure. Attitudes were measured through risk perception, operationalised as concern about zoonotic disease transmission. Practices included self-reported hunting and slaughtering of wildlife.ResultsThe analysis identified three distinct ontology classes: Relational environmentalism (52% of the population), characterised by strong spiritual connections to animals and a tendency to protect wildlife; Dualistic environmentalism (28%), with a weaker spiritual connection to animals but a commitment to wildlife conservation; and Neutral (20%), demonstrating little spiritual connection to animals and a neutral attitude towards wildlife conservation. In the logistic regression analyses, both environmentalism groups exhibited greater knowledge of zoonotic transmission and concern about outbreaks, with members of the Relational class demonstrating higher levels of these attributes. Furthermore, members of the Dualistic environmentalism class were less likely to have close contact with animals.ConclusionsIn Latin America’s biodiversity-rich regions, individuals whose ontology aligns with environmentalism appear to demonstrate a heightened awareness of zoonoses, particularly those who adhere to a Relational environmentalism perspective. Consequently, the integration of IPLC cultural knowledge holds potential to enhance wildlife conservation measures and contribute to the mitigation of disease transmission. Further research is needed to explore causal pathways and the integration of culturally grounded approaches into public health interventions.Keywords: Climate Change, Cross-Sectional Studies, INFECTIOUS DISEASES

Introduction

Latin America is one of the most biodiverse regions globally, with over 40% of the world’s species endemic to the region.1 2 However, biodiversity is under significant threat from human activities such as deforestation, mining, monoculture farming and unregulated wildlife exploitation.3,5 Over the past five decades, Latin America has experienced the highest wildlife population declines worldwide, with a 95% reduction, largely driven by land-use changes that increase the risk of zoonotic spillover.6 7

Indigenous Peoples and Local Communities (IPLCs) play a crucial role in conservation efforts, possessing deep ecological knowledge and fostering spiritual ties to ecosystems. Many IPLC knowledge systems have been described as reflecting relational human–nature ontologies, rooted in animism, that emphasise interconnectedness rather than the nature-culture divide introduced by European colonial perspectives.8,10 Many Indigenous traditions across Latin America, including Andean, Amazonian and Mayan cosmologies, perceive humans, animals and natural entities as inherently alive and sentient.11,14 This perspective informs conservation practices and has influenced legal frameworks such as the ‘rights of nature’ in some Latin American countries.15

Despite their stewardship, IPLCs face increasing challenges from poverty, extractivism, climate change and conservation approaches that often exclude local participation.16 Meanwhile environmental activism centred on animal protection is gaining traction in Latin American countries, particularly among younger, urban populations, though it often emphasises market-driven lifestyle changes (eg, veganism) rather than systemic ecological integration.17 In contrast, many IPLCs depend on wildlife for food and income, leading to human-wildlife interactions and potential conflicts, such as livestock predation and crop destruction.18 These interactions, exacerbated by deforestation and habitat fragmentation, also heighten the risk of zoonotic disease transmission, with approximately 60% of emerging infectious diseases being zoonotic, the majority originating from wildlife.19 Zoonotic transmission is rarely linear; instead, multiple species, including domestic and wild animals, often serve as hosts.20 The emergence of SARS-CoV-2 has underscored the need for a deeper understanding of human-wildlife interactions to mitigate future risks.

Ontologies shape human–wildlife interactions and influence zoonotic risk.21 22 However, the role of relational ontologies, such as animism, in contrast to dualistic naturalistic perspectives, remains largely unexplored. It is hypothesised that these ontologies may function as both barriers and enablers for zoonosis prevention.21 22 Studies suggest that IPLC-managed areas experience lower deforestation rates and greater forest regeneration, contributing to biodiversity conservation and reduced zoonotic risk.23

Despite these insights, however, there has been a paucity of research examining whether different ontologies are actually associated with distinct knowledge, attitudes and practices (KAP) towards wildlife in Latin America. This study therefore investigates this relationship, using data from the cross-sectional epidemiologic component of the ‘Knowledge, Attitudes and Practices towards the Risk of Zoonotic Diseases, Wildlife Trade and Wildlife Consumption in Latin America’ study.

Materials and methods

A detailed description of the overall project has been published previously.24 The cross-sectional design, including study areas, participant selection, survey instruments, variables and data analysis procedures, is summarised below.

Study areas and population

Four countries’ areas were selected for their diverse climate, biodiversity, wildlife and culture (figure 1). In Brazil, the project was conducted in the Atlantic Forest, a region with 5% of the world’s vertebrate species. It involved three rural communities representing the population of the region: Caiçaras, Quilombolas and Castelhanos-colony. In Chile, the project focused on the O'Higgins region, centred on the Paredones river basin. It encompassed the communities of Navidad (coast), Doñihue (valley) and Machalí (mountain). In Bolivia, the project was conducted in the Department of Chuquisaca, in the Municipality of Presto, within the El Palmar Integrated Management Natural Area. Quechua communities inhabit this region. In Guatemala, the project was implemented in the Ecoregion Lachua, a vast tropical wetland area in 49 indigenous communities of the Maya-Q'eqchi’ people.

A two-stage cluster sampling procedure was conducted in each area to include 900 participants, with a random sample of Enumeration Areas achieving 300 in rural, urban and protected areas. We invited all household members aged 10 years or older into the study, with 100 individuals in three age groups (children, adults and elderly) in each area. The sample size considered the balance between feasibility of implementing fieldwork and statistical robustness, so the minimum sample size for developing a multivariable prediction model with binary outcomes was considered, considering at least 100 people per group.25 Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. For participants under 16 years of age, written informed consent was obtained from a parent or legal guardian, and verbal or written assent was obtained from the minor participant in accordance with local ethical requirements.

Community members participated in the study through the survey, which was designed to capture their experiences, priorities and practices related to zoonotic risks and wildlife use.

Study instruments

The standard questionnaire on KAP was adapted from previous studies.26 27 The English version of the questionnaire is provided in the online supplemental file S2 and includes questions on demographics, education, occupation and medical history, and zoonotic disease-related KAP.24 The instrument was translated into local languages and then piloted in each country (≈20) to assess clarity, with feedback used to revise it in the final questionnaire. The interviews, conducted between September 2022 and September 2024 by trained interviewers who were fluent in the local languages, lasted approximately 30 min, and responses were standardised and coded for entry into Epi Info or Open Data Kit. Participant data were pseudonymised by assigning unique identification codes and stored on a secure platform compliant with applicable data protection regulations.

Patient and public involvement

Community representatives and members of Indigenous and local communities were engaged during the preparatory phase of the study to introduce the research aims and ensure the cultural appropriateness of the survey. The questionnaire was piloted and refined based on feedback regarding clarity, language, contextual relevance and the time required for completion. Translations into local languages were reviewed by bilingual experts to ensure comprehensibility.

Prior to fieldwork, the study was presented to local authorities and through local media channels. Community representatives facilitated access to households and supported recruitment by informing residents about the study. Participants were not involved in determining the outcome measures, which were defined by the research team.

Dissemination includes sharing results with participating communities through locally appropriate formats, such as community meetings, radio broadcasts and the distribution of culturally appropriate educational materials.

Data analysis

The analysis was done by IBM SPSS V.29 software. For the latent class analysis (LCA), we used the R package poLCA via the R plug-in. The study population was restricted to those with complete data on all indicator variables used for the LCA. The LCA was then used to classify ontologies based on the patterns of the following six statements regarding the attitude towards wildlife, all scored on a 3-point Likert scale (‘strongly disagree’/’disagree’, ‘neutral’, ‘agree’/’agree’:

Humans are part of the animal world.

Humans are part of the animal world.

(Some) wild animals are connected with my ancestors.

(Some) wild animals are connected with my ancestors.

Animals see themselves as humans and have their own culture.

Animals see themselves as humans and have their own culture.

I fear wild animals, we have to protect humans from wild animals.

I fear wild animals, we have to protect humans from wild animals.

We have to protect wild animals from humans.

We have to protect wild animals from humans.

Wild animals are beneficial to humans.

Wild animals are beneficial to humans.

The selection of the model was guided by the principle of parsimony, with simplicity, interpretability, and model fit based on the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) being given consideration. Given the robustness of the results, the implementation of multiple-group models (by country) was deemed unnecessary and was thus excluded to enhance simplicity. Additionally, the LCA was repeated, with dichotomised indicator variables (‘strongly disagree’/’disagree’/’neutral’ vs ‘agree’/’strongly agree’). Furthermore, we performed a country-specific LCA to account for heterogeneity between countries.

We compared the ontology classes identified via LCA across variables and potential confounders, using absolute and relative frequencies and χ² tests. Subsequently, we examined the associations between class membership and KAP. Frequencies were calculated for each category, followed by crude logistic regression models adjusted only for the sampling variables. Finally, fully adjusted models were developed, incorporating both the sampling variables and potential confounders. As sensitivity analyses, we re-ran the final models using country-specific latent classes, both with and without adjustment for country. To assess potential overadjustment, we repeated the models excluding Indigenous identification from the final models. Finally, we stratified the models for the primary outcomes by country. In addition, we considered the lack of knowledge about transmission from animals to humans for specific zoonotic diseases as outcome (Yellow fever, Chikungunya, Hantavirus, Leptospirosis, Rabies, Brucellosis, Salmonellosis, Bovine Tuberculosis and COVID-19).

This study adhered to the Consensus-Based Checklist for Reporting of Survey Studies guideline.28 The completed checklist is provided in the online supplemental file S3.

Results

A total of 3011 face-to-face interviews were conducted (~85% of the eligible population). Of these, 2970 had complete data on the LCA variables. Most interviews were conducted in Bolivia (N=900), followed by Guatemala (N=841). In Brazil, no approval for participation of children was obtained; in Chile, families were reluctant to let children take part. Therefore, only 622 interviews with adults and elderly were conducted in Brazil and 607 in Chile. The comparison of sociodemographic variables revealed statistically significant differences between countries in all variables (pχ²<0.001). In Guatemala, more males (56%) than females (44%) participated, while the reverse was true in the other countries. The lowest levels of educational attainment were observed in Bolivia and Guatemala, with one in three lacking formal education, compared with around 8% in Chile and 11% in Brazil. Furthermore, in the former countries, a higher percentage of the population with indigenous ancestry was noted (>90%), in comparison to 7% in Chile and 21% in Brazil (online supplemental table S1). The missing data showed no association with other variables, so it is possible to assume it was missing at random (data not shown).

Incorporating the indicator variables (illustrated in online supplemental file S1: table S2 according to country) into the LCA, the BIC was lowest for the seven-class solution (online supplemental file S1: table S3 and figures S1-S4). The most substantial decrease was observed between the two-class and three-class solutions, after which BIC gradually declined with diminishing returns until reaching its minimum. Beyond the three-class solution, the additional classes became increasingly challenging to interpret, as they introduced finer but less meaningful subdivisions. Consequently, we selected the three-class solution for subsequent analyses.

Latent class 1 (‘neutral’; prevalence 21%) comprised participants who exhibited a neutral response to most items. Latent class 2 (‘dualistic environmentalism’; 29%) was constituted by participants who were inclined to support animal protection without disclosing cultural and spiritual ties to ecosystems. Latent class 3 (‘relational environmentalism’; 52%), included those for whom animals were spiritually significant and morally important, viewed as a source of utility (online supplemental file S1: figure S2). The distribution of the three ontology classes differed according to country, area, age, level of education, and self-identification with indigenous groups (p<0.05; table 1).

Discussion

We explored people’s knowledge of wildlife and zoonotic diseases in urban, rural and protected areas in Bolivia, Brazil, Chile and Guatemala. Participants who aligned with environmentalist classes exhibited higher levels of knowledge about zoonotic diseases and greater concern about disease outbreaks in comparison to those with a neutral class. Concurrently, individuals categorised within the relational environmentalism class were also more likely to engage in practices related to animals.

The link between relational environmentalism and knowledge or attitudes towards zoonotic disease transmission is consistent with previous research highlighting the importance of indigenous knowledge systems in biodiversity conservation and ecosystem management.19 Indigenous communities often possess an intricate understanding of human-wildlife interactions due to their deep cultural and spiritual ties to nature.29 30 This traditional ecological knowledge (TEK) is often transmitted orally through generations.31 Likewise, the dualistic environmentalism class’s prioritisation of animal protection is congruent with prevailing trends in global environmental activism.17 32

The ontology classes identified were not exclusively associated with indigenous ancestry. This is in alignment with a conceptual shift from the utilisation of the term ‘Indigenous Communities’ to the more inclusive and comprehensive use of IPLCs. Prior research suggests that many rural and local communities retain their TEK due to their dependence on natural resources.33 The One Health approach emphasises the integration of IPLCs’ knowledge in the prevention of zoonotic diseases, acknowledging that their health perspectives are shaped by cultural traditions and lived experiences.34 This study supports the argument that KAP towards zoonotic risk and wildlife coexistence are shaped more by ontologies and ecological interactions than by ethnic or political self-identification alone.35

The finding that formal education plays a role in shaping knowledge about zoonotic diseases aligns with broader public health literature.36 Education has been consistently linked to increased health literacy, empowering individuals to recognise risks and adopt preventive measures. Conversely, the observed gaps in knowledge and training among participants with lower education levels highlight persistent inequities in access to information and resources. In that sense, it is possible that the children have not yet consolidated positions on the subject.

Country-specific results revealed significant contextual differences in zoonotic disease awareness. These discrepancies may stem from differences in public health campaigns, levels of wildlife exposure, environmental and cultural factors, or the representation of IPLCs in health education initiatives.37 The intersection of anthropogenic changes and human–wildlife interactions further amplifies zoonotic disease risks, particularly in Brazil, where expanding urbanisation has altered pathogen transmission dynamics.38 Traditional hunting and wildlife use practices play a role in shaping interactions with wildlife. Communities in Guatemala, for example, rely on wildlife for protein and use animals in cultural rituals, practices that foster both knowledge and risks related to zoonotic diseases. In addition, the specific zoonotic diseases considered differ by country in terms of prevalence. This emphasises the need for regionally adapted, culturally sensitive participatory education campaigns that account for local epidemiological risks, cultural perspectives and existing knowledge gaps, balancing tradition with public health priorities.13 16

Limitations of this study include its cross-sectional design, which is useful for identifying associations but precludes causal inference about relationships among ontologies, KAP. For instance, practices such as hunting are shaped by a complex interplay of cultural, economic and structural factors; therefore, attributing them solely to ontological orientations would constitute an oversimplification. Although the study implemented strategies to favour the representativeness of the study population and therefore the generalisation of the results, through random sampling and achieving a high percentage of response (≈ 85%), it is possible that some groups are underrepresented (eg, people with less interest in wildlife protection issues or zoonotic risks); therefore, the association between the Neutral Class in relation to KAP could be underestimated. Furthermore, some regions and populations were underrepresented in this study. For example, no community in a protected area was included in Chile. Similarly, the exclusion of children in Chile and Brazil due to parental refusal might have skewed age-related findings. Furthermore, although the study applied a two-stage cluster sampling design, identifiers for Enumeration Areas and households were not retained in the analytical dataset. Therefore, clustering at these levels could not be accounted for in the models. This may have resulted in an underestimation of SEs.

The reliance on self-reported data can introduce bias. Participants may be reluctant to disclose sensitive behaviours, such as illegal hunting, or may exaggerate their knowledge of zoonotic diseases to align with perceived social expectations. In interpreting hunting practices, the legal context should also be considered. For instance, in Brazil, commercial hunting is prohibited under environmental legislation; however, subsistence hunting by traditional communities – such as the Quilombola39 - is permitted. Furthermore, Indigenous identity was assessed through self-identification and may not fully capture the multidimensional and context-dependent nature of Indigeneity in Latin America. Identity is shaped by historical, cultural and political factors; therefore, misclassification or socially influenced responses cannot be excluded. However, self-identification represents the standard and widely accepted approach to assessing Indigenous belonging.40 41 The use of local interviewers may, in some cases, have exacerbated these biases rather than mitigated them. Despite the provision of comprehensive training to interviewers and the extensive piloting of questionnaires, linguistic and cultural variations may still have influenced the interpretation of certain questions. Variations in educational levels and systems between countries may also have influenced participants’ responses. For instance, the lower formal education levels in Bolivia and Guatemala may have contributed to lower reported training on zoonotic diseases, while cultural knowledge passed down through Indigenous traditions may have compensated for this lack of formal education in certain contexts. However, it does not fully explore systemic barriers, such as access to healthcare, education or government-led wildlife management initiatives, which might have shaped the observed disparities between countries and communities. On the other hand, the study did not explore in depth some elements that might better explain the characteristics of the ontology groups, such as media exposure, type of job, which could be explored in future studies.

In addition, local events occurring during the study period—such as spectacled bear (Tremarctos ornatus) attacks in Bolivia, avian influenza outbreaks in Chile and the dengue fever epidemic in Brazil—likely influenced participants’ responses. These incidents may have temporarily heightened awareness of specific zoonotic risks or shifted attitudes toward wildlife conservation,42 introducing contextual variability that may limit generalisability. Additionally, potential bias may have been introduced through the way we defined certain variables. To address this, we applied alternative definitions in our sensitivity analyses. Furthermore, given the diversity of cultures, response behaviours and environmental contexts, there is a compelling argument for the use of country-specific LCA or for the stratification of analyses by country. However, the main findings were largely corroborated by our sensitivity analyses. The use of LCA avoided oversimplifying responses, providing nuanced insights into the diversity of perceptions within and across regions. Results were robust to a variety of alternative LCA definitions indicating the validity of the findings.

The study may provide valuable insights into the interplay between ontologies and KAP regarding zoonotic diseases in four Latin American regions. We were able to involve remote communities that are difficult to reach from major cities, including protected areas with which local universities have longstanding collaborations. Local involvement was prioritised during data collection. Trained local interviewers fluent in Indigenous languages and dialects conducted surveys, reducing potential biases due to language barriers or cultural misunderstandings. This approach also ensured cultural appropriateness in communication, fostering trust with participants and improving the reliability of the data. We might, however, be criticised for using Indigenous identities and knowledge systems and reducing them to ‘romanticised’ concepts which do not meet the real social and political conditions.43 We tried our best to include IPLCs’ perspectives in our study. The findings presented here are enriched by the qualitative component of the project, which largely supports our results (Data not shown). Collaborative efforts with the communities are currently underway to develop intervention approaches that aim to further explore the interplay between ontologies, behaviours and health outcomes.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that ontologies are associated with variation in zoonotic disease-related KAP across diverse Latin American settings. While ontologies do not determine behaviour, they appear to represent one dimension shaping how individuals perceive and engage with wildlife-related risks. Integrating cultural and relational perspectives into One Health approaches may therefore strengthen zoonotic risk communication and community-based prevention strategies. However, given the cross-sectional design, further research is required to better understand the mechanisms linking ontology and practice.
