Fermi detection of γ \gamma -rays from the radio-quiet Seyfert galaxy NGC 3281 Facilities: Fermi -LAT, Swift-BAT ScienceTools (v2.0.8) and FermiPy ( Wood et al. 2017 )
Jun-Rong Liu, Hua Feng, Jian-Min Wang

Abstract
We report the detection of significant γ \gamma -ray emission with 𝐹𝑒𝑟𝑚𝑖 \it Fermi -LAT from the radio-quiet Seyfert 2 galaxy NGC 3281, with a luminosity of 5.9 ​ ( ± 1.7 ) × 10 41 ​ erg ​ s − 1 5.9\,(\pm 1.7)\times 10^{41}\rm\,erg\,s^{-1} at a significance of 6.22 ​ σ 6.22\,\sigma (TS = 42.81 42.81 ). The power-law photon index is 2.61 ​ ( ± 0.24 ) 2.61~(\pm 0.24) , indicative of a soft spectrum. The star formation activity in NGC 3281 is insufficient to explain its γ \gamma -ray luminosity based on the empirical relation between the infrared and γ \gamma -ray luminosities observed in other sources. The multiwavelength spectrum can be explained as due to inverse Compton scattering by relativistic electrons in the corona or jet of seed photons from the corona, disk and torus. The source is Compton-thick and attenuation of GeV photons due to pair production in the corona is nonnegligible (with an optical depth of about 10). The intrinsic γ \gamma -ray luminosity is inferred to be 3.4 × 10 42 3.4\,\times 10^{42} and 2.2 × 10 41 ​ erg ​ s − 1 2.2\,\times 10^{41}\rm\,erg\,s^{-1} for the corona and jet model, respectively. The observed γ \gamma -ray and radio luminosities is roughly consistent with the known correlation between the two quantities, among the lowest luminosity regime. The jet origin is valid only if the radio emission is dominated by the jet.



I
Introduction

Radio-loud active galactic nuclei (AGNs) can display strong
\gamma
-ray emission, due to inverse Compton scattering by electrons in the relativistic jet
(
Blandford et al. 2019
, e.g., see review in)
.
GeV emission is also seen in some radio-quiet AGNs.
For example, AGNs with a strong star formation rate (SFR) are often significantly detected in
\gamma
-rays
(
Ackermann et al. 2012a
;
Ajello et al. 2020
)
.

Liu et al. 2025
found evidence for
\gamma
-ray emission in the corona of AGNs from a carefully selected sub-sample in the Swift BAT AGN Spectroscopic Survey (BASS).
These are nearby, radio-quiet, and ultra hard X-ray bright AGNs, whose jets are not powerful enough to explain the observed GeV emission.
There are also signatures of
\gamma
-ray emission below the GeV band arguably originated in the AGN corona from NGC 1068
(
Ajello et al. 2023
)
and NGC 4945
(
Murase et al. 2024
)
.

The AGN corona is expected to be a factory of
\gamma
-ray emission
(
Inoue et al. 2019
;
Inoue et al. 2021
)
in light of its non-thermal activity found in radio observations
(
Inoue & Doi 2018
)
.

Bhattacharyya et al. 2003
;
Bhattacharyya et al. 2006
have found that the proton-proton collisions in the inner region of an accretion disk would produce electrons, positrons, and
\gamma
-rays.
An ultra high energy bump above TeV is expected due to injection of non-thermal particles into a magnetized corona
(
Romero et al. 2010
)
.

\gamma
-rays from AGN coronae have been searched in the past.
Observations with the Energetic Gamma Ray Experiment Telescope (EGRET) gave a
2\sigma
upper limit on the
\gamma
-ray flux from 22 Seyfert galaxies selected by X-ray
(
Lin et al. 1993
)
.

Cillis et al. 2004
utilized a stacking technique on a sample of Seyfert galaxies away from the Galactic plane and found no significant result (
<2\sigma
) with EGRET data.

Teng et al. 2011
analyzed the
\it Fermi
Large Area Telescope (LAT) data on 491 Seyfert galaxies detected by Swift BAT and found that only two nearby objects, NGC 1068 and NGC 4945, were detected with
\gamma
-ray emission, consistent with starburst activity.
For those undetected objects,
Teng et al. 2011
gave a flux upper limit
\sim 2\times 10^{-11}\rm\,ph\,s^{-1}\,cm^{-2}
above 1 GeV.

Ackermann et al. 2012b
searched
\it Fermi
-LAT data of 120 hard X-ray-selected Seyfert galaxies again and found no significant
\gamma
-ray emission in the GeV band.
However, only 2.1 and 3 years of the
\it Fermi
-LAT data were analyzed in
Teng et al. 2011
and
Ackermann et al. 2012b
, respectively.
Recently, sub-GeV
\gamma
-rays from NGC 4945 were reported by
Murase et al. 2024
and explained as due to the magnetically powered corona model, while its GeV emission was explained by star formation activity.
In a similar case of NGC 1068, a two-component model, with hadronic emission in the vicinity of the central supermassive black hole (SMBH) and starburst activity, is proposed to explain the low-energy (below
\sim 500
MeV) and high-energy (above
\sim 500
MeV)
\gamma
-ray spectral energy distribution (SED), respectively
(
Ajello et al. 2023
)
.
Another radio-quiet galaxy, NGC 4151, may have shown
\gamma
-ray emission, which can be explained as due to jet, coronal activities
(
Inoue & Khangulyan 2023
)
, or the ultra fast outflows
(
Ajello et al. 2021
)
, despite the fact that for this
object
(
Peretti et al. 2023
, NGC 4151, see)
the origin of its
\gamma
-ray emission is uncertain since there is a blazar within the error radius of the
\gamma
-ray position
(
Ballet et al. 2023
;
Murase et al. 2024
)
.

In this work, we studied the
\gamma
-ray properties of another radio-quiet Seyfert galaxy, NGC 3281, using
\it Fermi
-LAT data accumulated in around 16 years.
The target was selected from a
\gamma
-ray survey of the BASS sample
(
Liu et al. 2025
)
.
NGC 3281 is a spiral Seyfert 2 galaxy (R.A.
=157\fdg 9670
, Decl.
=-34\fdg 8537
) with a redshift based distance of
D=48.1
Mpc
(
Koss et al. 2022a
)
.
It is detected by Swift-BAT with a hard X-ray luminosity of
L_{\rm 14-195\,keV}=2.4\times 10^{43}\,\rm erg\,s^{-1}

(
Oh et al. 2018
)

and by Swift-XRT with a soft X-ray luminosity of
L_{\rm 0.3-10\,keV}=9.7\times 10^{41}\rm\,erg\,s^{-1}

(
Evans et al. 2020
)
.
Its nuclear region is Compton-thick with a column density of
N_{\rm H}=2.0\times 10^{24}\,\rm cm^{-2}

(
Zhao et al. 2021
)
.
The mass of SMBH is
M_{\bullet}=1.66\times 10^{8}\,M_{\odot}
derived from velocity dispersion
(
Koss et al. 2022b
)
.
The bolometric luminosity is derived as
L_{\rm Bol}=2.3\times 10^{44}\rm\,erg\,s^{-1}
, assuming a 14-150 keV bolometric correction of 8
(
Ricci et al. 2017
)
, consistent with that derived based on [O
iii
]
(
Dall’Agnol de Oliveira et al. 2023
)
.

We explain the
\gamma

III
Modeling

We try to interpret the
\gamma
-ray emission in NGC 3281 as a result of inverse Compton scattering by high energy electrons in the corona and jet, respectively.

III.1
Corona model

In an AGN corona, electrons can be accelerated approaching the speed of light by shocks
(
Drury 1983
;
Blandford & Eichler 1987
;
Inoue et al. 2019
)
or magnetic reconnection
(
Sironi & Spitkovsky 2014
;
Chael et al. 2017
)
.
The energy spectrum of the non-thermal electrons is given by

\frac{{\rm d}N}{{\rm d}\gamma_{\rm e}}=n_{0}\gamma_{\rm e}^{-p}e^{-\gamma_{\rm e}/\gamma_{\rm max}}\;,

(1)

where
\gamma_{\rm e}
is the Lorentz factor,

\gamma_{\rm max}
is the cutoff Lorentz factor,

p
is the spectral index assumed to be 2,
and
n_{0}
is the normalization.
The total number density
n_{\rm e}
can be obtained by integrating
{\rm d}N/{\rm d}\gamma_{\rm e}
from
\gamma_{\rm min}=1
to infinity.

Emission from the corona, disk, and torus provides a seed photon field for inverse Compton scattering (
\gamma
-ray production) as well as pair production (
\gamma
-ray attenuation).
The corona emission dominates in the X-ray band, typically in the energy range of
\sim
0.1-100 keV, and can be described by a power-law distribution with an exponential cutoff,
L_{\nu,\rm cor}=L_{0}(E/E_{\rm c})^{\alpha}e^{-E/E_{\rm c}}
, where
\alpha
is the spectral index,
E_{\rm c}
is the cutoff energy, and
L_{0}
is the normalization.
The corresponding number density of photons is
n_{\rm ph}(\nu)=L_{\nu,\rm cor}/(4\pi R^{2}h\nu c)
, where
h
is the Planck constant,

c
is the speed of light,
and
R
is the corona radius.
We adopt
R=10\,R_{\rm g}
, where
R_{\rm g}=GM_{\bullet}/c^{2}
is the gravitational radius and
G
is the gravitational constant.
For the accretion disk, the standard disk
(
Shakura & Sunyaev 1973
)
model with radiation peaked in optical and UV is assumed.
A radius of
R=10^{3}\,R_{\rm g}
is assumed to calculate the number density of photons.
For the torus, the template spectrum in
Sanders et al. 1989
is used. The emission peaks in infrared and is assumed to be confined within a radius of
R=10^{6}\,R_{\rm g}
.
The SEDs of the three components are displayed in Figure
4
.
Their normalizations are found by fitting with the data.

Pair production attenuates
\gamma
-rays originated in the corona
(
Inoue et al. 2019
)
.
Therefore, the model flux can be calculated as a result of inverse Compton scattering by high energy electrons of seed photons (see Appendix
A
), subject to attenuation caused by pair production (see Appendix
B
), and then compared with data using the Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) fit.

For the X-ray fit, we adopted the NuSTAR spectrum previously reported in
Zhao et al. 2021
.
As the NuSTAR spectrum cannot well constrain the absorption column density due to limited low energy coverage, we fixed
N_{\rm H}
at
8\times 10^{23}
and
2\times 10^{24}\,\rm cm^{-2}
, respectively, the bounds of
N_{\rm H}
found by
Zhao et al. 2021
.
The cutoff energy
E_{\rm c}
is fixed at 500 keV
(
Zhao et al. 2021
)
.
The X-ray and
\gamma
-ray SED and best-fit models are shown in Figure
3
.
As one can see,
N_{\rm H}
at the upper bound provides a better fit in the GeV band than the lower one does.
Thus, we fixed
N_{\rm H}=2\times 10^{24}\,\rm cm^{-2}
in the subsequent analysis.
The multiwavelength SED and best-fit model is shown in Figure
4
.
The X-ray spectral model parameters are derived as
\alpha=-0.79
and
L_{0}=9.2\times 10^{22}\rm\,erg\,s^{-1}\,Hz^{-1}
.
Correspondingly, the differential luminosity at 0.91 keV and integrated luminosity from 2 to 10 keV are
2.9\times 10^{42}\rm\,erg\,s^{-1}
and
6.5\times 10^{42}\rm\,erg\,s^{-1}
, respectively.
The best-fit parameters are listed in Table
2
.

We obtain the intrinsic
\gamma
-rays luminosity
L_{\rm 0.1-300\,GeV}^{\prime}=3.4\,\times 10^{42}\rm\,erg\,s^{-1}
and
\gamma
-ray to X-ray luminosity ratio
\xi_{\rm nth}=L_{\rm 0.1-300\,GeV}^{\prime}/L_{\rm 14-195\,keV}\approx 14\%
.
If
\gamma
-rays are a proxy for non-thermal electrons (synchrotron radiation is not considered here) and X-rays for thermal electrons,
\xi_{\rm nth}
then represents the fractional energy of non-thermal electrons.
The observed ratio
L_{\rm 0.1-300\,GeV}/L_{\rm 14-195\,keV}=2.4\%
is consistent with a previous observational upper limit of 0.1
(
Ackermann et al. 2012b
)
.

We calculated the optical depth by integrating the pair production cross section in Appendix
B
.
The optical depth as well as the transmission fraction as a function of energy are shown in Figure
7
.
As one can see, the optical depth at 1 GeV is
\sim
10, indicating that the attenuation due to pair production cannot be ignored in our case.
We note that a consistent optical depth can be obtained if one applies Eq. (1) in
Murase et al. 2024
for a simplified estimation.

III.2
Jet model

NGC 3281 exhibits a radio core with extended jet-like features, as shown in a 1.4 GHz image obtained with the Australian Square Kilometre Array Pathfinder
(
Johnston et al. 2008
, ASKAP;)
in Figure
5
.

Given the 5 GHz and
B

(
Ulvestad & Wilson 1989
;
Véron-Cetty & Véron 2010
)
, the source has a radio-to-optical luminosity ratio
\mathcal{R}_{\rm rB}\equiv L_{\nu,\rm 5GHz}/L_{\nu,\rm B}=7.3

(
Ulvestad & Wilson 1989
;
Véron-Cetty & Véron 2010
)
, classified as a radio-quiet AGN
(
Kellermann et al. 1994
,
\mathcal{R}_{\rm rB}<10
;)
.
As
\mathcal{R}_{\rm rB}
is significantly influenced by the subtraction of host galaxy emission, especially for Seyfert galaxies
(
Ho & Peng 2001
)
,
we also calculated the radio loudness against the hard X-ray band, defined as
\mathcal{R}_{\rm rX}\equiv L_{\rm 1.4GHz}/L_{\rm 14-195\,keV}=1.3\times 10^{-5}

(
Teng et al. 2011
;
Ackermann et al. 2012b
)
, which also aligns with the classification of a radio-quiet AGN, characterized by
\mathcal{R}_{\rm rX}<10^{-4}

(
Ackermann et al. 2012b
)
or
\mathcal{R}_{\rm rX}<2.0\times 10^{-5}

(
Teng et al. 2011
)
.
Here
L_{\rm 1.4\,GHz}
is adopted from
Condon et al. 1998
.

A simple one-zone homogeneous jet model
(
Inoue & Takahara 1996
)
is adopted to calculate the radio and
\gamma
-ray SEDs, assuming the same power-law spectral distribution described in Eq. (
1
).
We adopted the same seed photon field outlined in §
III.1
.
The jet radius, referring to the distance to the SMBH, is set as a free parameter in the fit.
The seed photon density at the jet radius is calculated assuming an inverse square law if the component radius is smaller than the jet radius.
The synchrotron radiation and synchrotron self-Compton scattering are calculated following
Inoue & Takahara 1996
and the external inverse Compton scattering is derived from Eq. (
A1
).
The pair production process is unimportant and ignored in this case with a large jet radius.The radio and
\gamma
-ray SEDs are fitted simultaneously.
The fit includes four free parameters,
n_{0}
,
\gamma_{\rm max}
,
R
(jet radius), and
B
(jet magnetic field strength).
In principle, these parameters can be determined from the synchrotron self-absorption frequency, peak frequency of synchrotron radiation, radio luminosity, and
\gamma
-ray luminosity.
The best-fit results are also listed in Table
2
.
The intrinsic
\gamma
-ray luminosity in the jet model is
2.2\,\times 10^{41}\rm\,erg\,s^{-1}
.

IV
Discussions

Here we report a significant detection of
\gamma
-ray emission from the radio-quiet AGN in NGC 3281 with
\it Fermi
-LAT, with
L_{\rm 0.1-300\,GeV}=5.9\,(\pm 1.7)\times 10^{41}\rm\,erg\,s^{-1}
at a significance of
6.22\,\sigma
.
The
\gamma
-ray emission, as well as the multiwavelength SED, can be explained in the context of inverse Compton scattering by high energy electrons in the corona or in the jet of seed photons from the torus, disk and corona.
In the corona model, the seed photons mainly come from the corona and disk component, with the corona component having a slightly larger contribution.
In the jet model, the seed photons are dominated by disk photons.

In the corona model, pair production becomes important at energies around GeV and above (Figure
4
).
The ratio of intrinsic to attenuated
\gamma
-ray luminosity at 100 MeV and 1 GeV is about
0.07
and
0.19
, respectively.
Cascade would occur following annihilation of GeV photons with keV photons in the corona, and produce secondary sub-GeV photons due to inverse Compton scattering
(
Murase et al. 2012
;
Murase et al. 2020
;
Murase et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
.
To estimate this effect, we calculate the positron/electron spectra following Eq. (3.25) in
Aharonian 2004
and the subsequent inverse Compton spectra using Eqs. (
A1
-
B4
) in the Appendix.
The emergent SED converges after a few iterations, leading to a flux enhancement by a factor of approximately
4.3
at 100 MeV.
Thus, if the cascade process is considered, a lower electron density by a factor of about
4.3
would be sufficient to account for the observed data.
An accurate calculation, however, needs to solve the radiative transfer equation by incorporating the photon generation term due to cascade.
Consequently, Eqs. (
B3
) and (
B4
) are inapplicable in their current form.
This is beyond the scope of this work and will be addressed in the future.

As discussed above, the star formation activity and outflows could be other possible causes of
\gamma
-ray emission.
Star-forming galaxies display significant
\gamma
-ray emission with a luminosity scaled with the infrared luminosity
(
Ajello et al. 2020
)
, suggesting that their
\gamma
-ray emission is driven by star forming processes, possibly due to interaction between high energy cosmic rays and the interstellar medium
(
Paglione et al. 1996
;
Abdo et al. 2010
;
Ackermann et al. 2012a
;
Ajello et al. 2020
)
.
We estimate the contribution from star formation to
\gamma
-ray emission using the
L_{0.1-800\,\rm GeV}-L_{\rm 8-1000\,\mu m}
relation established by
Ajello et al. 2020
.
The infrared luminosity,
L_{\rm 8-1000\,\mu m}=6.0\times 10^{10}\,L_{\odot}
, is quoted from the IRAS Faint Source Catalogue
(
Moshir et al. 1990
)
.
The expected
\gamma
-ray luminosity,
L_{\gamma,\rm\,SFG}={2.9\,(\pm 0.6)\times 10^{40}\rm\,erg\,s^{-1}}
, is about an order of magnitude below the observed luminosity, indicating that the contribution from star formation activity is negligible.
In theory,
\gamma
-rays can be produced by interaction between AGN-driven outflows and the interstellar medium
(
Faucher-Giguère & Quataert 2012
;
Wang & Loeb 2016
;
Zhu et al. 2024
)
,
AGN torus
(
Wang 2004
;
Inoue et al. 2022
)
,
or clouds
(
Huang et al. 2024
)
.
Possible observational evidence for
\gamma
-rays produced by ultra fast outflows is reported
(
Ajello et al. 2021
;
Sakai et al. 2025
)
.
However,
McDaniel et al. 2023
searched galaxies hosting molecular outflow for
\gamma
-ray emission, but found no evidence that the outflow is directly accelerating cosmic rays.

Dall’Agnol de Oliveira et al. 2023
studied the ionized gas outflows from NGC 3281 using ALMA CO(2–1) observations, and estimated a power of
\dot{E}=(0.045-1.6)\times 10^{40}\,\rm erg\,s^{-1}
. This is much lower than the
\gamma
-ray luminosity measured in this work, indicating that the
\gamma
-rays from NGC 3281 can not be dominated by the outflows.
Thus, these processes are unlikely to account for the observed
\gamma
-ray emission seen in NGC 3281.

If the
\gamma
-ray emission is indeed due to a jet, it should appear as a weak version of radio-loud AGN.
A correlation between
L_{\rm 1.4\,GHz}
and
L_{\rm 0.1-100\rm GeV}
has been found for radio-loud AGNs

(
Inoue 2011
;
Di Mauro et al. 2014
;
Fukazawa et al. 2022
)
.
Such a relation may extend to radio-quiet AGNs.
We plotted NGC 3281 in the same diagram (Figure
6
) and found that it aligns with the correlation, among the lowest luminosity regime in the sample.
This resembles the Fanaroff–Riley0 (FR0) galaxies, which possess compact radio jets and also show a correlation between their radio and
\gamma
-ray emission consistent with the relation seen in canonical FR radio galaxies
(
Khatiya et al. 2024
)
.
On the other hand, such a low radio luminosity may have other origins than low-power jets
(
Panessa et al. 2019
)
.
NGC 3281 has outflows with a power of
(0.045-1.6)\times 10^{40}\,\rm erg\,s^{-1}
,
which might contribute to the observed radio flux
(
Dall’Agnol de Oliveira et al. 2023
)
.
Synchrotron emission in the corona is another possibility.
In radio-quiet galaxies, the radio emission follows a trend of
L_{\rm 1.4\,GHz}/L_{\rm 14-195\,keV}\sim 10^{-5}
, arguably attributed to corona emission
(
Laor & Behar 2008
;
Smith et al. 2020
)
.
NGC 3281 shows a consistent radio-to-X-ray luminosity ratio (
1.3\times 10^{-5}
, see §
III.2
), suggesting that one should be cautious about the radio origin.
In this regard, the jet model is valid only if the radio emission is dominated by relativistic jets.
In the jet model, we have assumed that the size of the emission region approximates the distance to the SMBH, parameterized with the same
R
.
In reality, the emission size (
R_{\rm e}
) may be smaller than the distance (
R_{\rm d}
).
According to Eq. (
A1
), the
\gamma
-ray luminosity has a scaling relation
L_{\nu}^{\prime}\propto R_{\rm e}^{3}R_{\rm d}^{-2}n_{\rm e}
.
Therefore, one should keep the caveat in mind that if the actual emission size is smaller than the distance, the true electron density will be higher than that quoted in Table
2
.

Appendix A
Inverse Compton scattering process

In the model of inverse Compton scattering, the
\gamma
-ray spectrum can be expressed as
(
Blumenthal & Gould 1970
;
Inoue & Takahara 1996
)
,

L_{\nu}^{\prime}=R^{3}h\nu\int_{\nu_{\rm min}}^{\nu_{\rm max}}\int_{\gamma_{\rm min}}^{\gamma_{\rm max}}f(\nu,\nu_{i},\gamma_{\rm e})\frac{{\rm d}N}{{\rm d}\gamma_{\rm e}}n_{\rm ph}(\nu_{i}){\rm d}\gamma_{\rm e}{\rm d}\nu_{i},

(A1)

where

R
is the size of emission region,

\nu_{i}
is the incident photon frequency,

\nu_{\rm min}=10^{13}\,\rm Hz
and
\nu_{\rm max}=10^{21}\,\rm Hz
are the minimum and maximum of the incident photon frequencies, respectively,

\nu
is the
\gamma
-ray frequency,

\gamma_{\rm e}
is the electron Lorentz factor,

dN/d\gamma_{\rm e}
is the spectrum of the electron in Eq. (
1
),

\gamma_{\rm min}
is the minimum of the electron Lorentz factor,

n_{\rm ph}(\nu_{i})
is the spectrum of incident seed photons number density derived from the photon field described in §
III.1
.
The function

f(\nu,\nu_{i},\gamma_{\rm e})=\frac{2\pi r_{\rm e}^{2}c}{\gamma_{\rm e}^{2}\nu_{i}}\left[2\,q\,\ln q+(1+2q)(1-q)+\frac{(\Gamma_{e}q)^{2}(1-q)}{2(1+\Gamma_{e}q)}\right],

(A2)

determines the spectrum shape after an incident photon is scattered by a relativistic electron
(
Blumenthal & Gould 1970
)
,
where
r_{\rm e}=e^{2}/m_{\rm e}c^{2}
is the classical electron radius,

e
is the electron charge,

m_{\rm e}
is the electron mass,

\Gamma_{e}=4h\nu_{i}\gamma_{\rm e}/m_{\rm e}c^{2}
, and
q=\nu/4\gamma_{\rm e}^{2}\nu_{i}(1-h\nu/\gamma_{\rm e}m_{\rm e}c^{2})
.

Appendix B
pair production process

The
\gamma
-rays produced in the corona would be attenuated due to pair production (
\gamma\gamma^{\prime}\rightarrow e^{+}e^{-}
) interactions.
The total cross section of
\gamma\gamma^{\prime}
pair production process is given by
Aharonian 2004
,

\sigma_{\gamma\gamma}(\nu,\nu_{i})=\frac{3\,\sigma_{\rm T}}{2s^{2}}\left[\left(s+\frac{1}{2}\ln s-\frac{1}{6}+\frac{1}{2\,s}\right)\ln\left(\sqrt{s}+\sqrt{s-1}\right)-\right.\left.\left(s+\frac{4}{9}-\frac{1}{9\,s}\right)\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{s}}\right],

(B1)

where
\sigma_{\rm T}
is the Thomson cross section,

s\equiv h^{2}\nu\nu_{i}/m_{\rm e}^{2}\,c^{4}
,

h\nu
is the energy of the
\gamma
-rays,
and
h\nu_{i}
is the energy of the incident target photon.

\sigma_{\gamma\gamma}(\nu,\nu_{i})
reaches its maximum value
\approx 0.22\,\sigma_{\rm T}
at
s=3.5
.
For photons with
h\nu=1\rm\,GeV
, the most efficient pair production occurs with target photons with
h\nu_{i}=0.91
keV,
which is in the Swift-XRT energy range
(
Burrows et al. 2005
)
.
The optical depth to pair production is given by

\tau_{\gamma\gamma}=\int_{\nu_{\rm min}}^{\nu_{\rm max}}n_{\rm ph}(\nu_{i})\sigma_{\gamma\gamma}(\nu,\nu_{i})R{\rm d}\nu_{i}=\int_{\nu_{\rm min}}^{\nu_{\rm max}}\left(\frac{L_{\nu_{i}}}{4\pi R^{2}ch\nu_{i}}\right)\sigma_{\gamma\gamma}(\nu,\nu_{i})R{\rm d}\nu_{i},

(B2)

where

n_{\rm ph}(\nu_{i})
is the spectrum of the soft photon number density,
and
L_{\nu_{i}}
is the soft X-ray luminosity density.

\tau_{\gamma\gamma}
due to the three components is shown in the left panel of Figure
7
.
The fraction of high-energy photons remaining after attenuation is given by the factor
(
Dermer & Menon 2009
)
,

\zeta{(\nu)}=\left(\frac{3}{\tau_{\gamma\gamma}}\right)\left[\frac{1}{2}+\frac{{\rm exp}(-\tau_{\gamma\gamma})}{\tau_{\gamma\gamma}}-\frac{1-{\rm exp}(-\tau_{\gamma\gamma})}{\tau_{\gamma\gamma}^{2}}\right]\;,

(B3)

shown in the right panel of Figure
7
.
Finally, we derive the
result of the attenuated
\gamma
-ray luminosity spectrum

L_{\nu}=\zeta{(\nu)}L_{\nu}^{\prime}.

(B4)
