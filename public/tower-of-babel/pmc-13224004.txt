Curcumin alleviates depression-aggravated psoriasis by targeting MMP9-regulated apoptotic pathways
Authors: Jiliang Lu, Chaojing Zhou, Ying Ma, Ningning Zhang, Ziyue Diao, Dandan Chen, Xin Tian, Zhiqiang Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryDepression and psoriasis are highly comorbid, yet their shared molecular mechanisms remain poorly understood. Utilizing cross-sectional data from NHANES and Mendelian randomization (MR), a significant association and causal link between depression and psoriasis were established. A combined mouse model of chronic unpredictable mild stress (CUMS) and imiquimod-induced psoriasis confirmed that depressive state exacerbates psoriasis-like skin lesions. Integrated multi-omics analyses (including bulk and single-cell RNA sequencing, machine learning, and eQTL/pQTL-MR) identified apoptosis as a key shared pathway, with matrix metallopeptidase 9 (MMP9) as a central causal gene. Animal experiments confirmed MMP9 upregulation in both skin and brain tissues. Molecular docking predicted strong binding of curcumin to MMP9, and subsequent in vivo intervention demonstrated that curcumin downregulated MMP9 expression and alleviated both pathological conditions. These findings reveal MMP9-mediated apoptosis as a central pathological link in the comorbidity of depression and psoriasis and suggest a dual-treatment strategy for this comorbidity.

Introduction

Depression represents one of the most prevalent mental health disorders worldwide, characterized by persistent low mood, anhedonia, and cognitive impairments. A clinically significant yet mechanistically enigmatic bidirectional relationship exists between depression and psoriasis. Depression, a psychiatric condition often linked to neuroinflammation,1 is associated with elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines (interleukin-1 beta, IL-1β; interleukin-6, IL-6; and tumor necrosis factor-alpha, TNF-α)2,3 that also drive psoriasis pathogenesis. Epidemiological studies reveal that psoriasis patients have a higher depression prevalence than the general population,4,5 while depressed individuals show increased psoriasis risk, suggesting shared pathophysiology beyond psychosocial factors.6 Notably, some medical treatments for psoriasis may exacerbate depressive symptoms in clinical practice, highlighting an urgent need for therapies addressing both conditions. Despite this established association, critical gaps persist in understanding the causal directionality and specific molecular mechanisms underlying how depression exacerbates psoriatic pathology.

To address these gaps, we integrated population-level, molecular, and experimental approaches to elucidate how depression exacerbates psoriasis. Using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) combined with Mendelian randomization (MR), we identified an association between depression and psoriasis and provided genetic evidence supporting a potential causal role of depression in increased psoriasis risk. We then applied multi-omics analyses, including bulk and single-cell transcriptomics, to identify convergent molecular pathways shared by both conditions. This integrative strategy revealed apoptosis as a key overlapping pathogenic process and identified matrix metallopeptidase 9 (MMP9) as a central regulatory node within this pathway.

Finally, through behavioral assays, histopathological analyses, molecular docking, and in vivo intervention, we evaluated whether targeting MMP9 could therapeutically mitigate both depressive-like behaviors and psoriatic skin inflammation. By linking population-level inference with molecular mechanisms and experimental rescue, our study establishes MMP9 as a mechanistic mediator and a potential therapeutic target in depression-aggravated psoriasis.

Results

Association between depression and psoriasis risk in NHANES

The baseline characteristics of the 14,004 NHANES participants analyzed revealed that 1,325 (9.5%) had depression (Figure 1A). Compared to non-depression participants, those with depression were more likely to be female (65.24% vs. 50.43%), had a higher proportion of Black (13.42% vs. 10.61%) and Hispanic individuals (7.84% vs. 5.19%), lower educational attainment (8.32% vs. 4.46% with less than high school education), higher body mass index (body mass index [BMI], 29.57 ± 8.34 vs. 27.70 ± 6.68 kg/m2), fewer current smokers (40.07% vs. 57.61%), and lower alcohol use (8.74% vs. 14.57%). Notably, the depression group exhibited a significantly higher prevalence of psoriasis (4.41% vs. 2.90%; p = 0.026; Table 1). Multivariable logistic regression analyses demonstrated significant associations between depression and psoriasis risk across models. In the unadjusted model (model 1), depression was significantly associated with increased psoriasis risk (Model 1; odds ratio [OR] = 1.54, 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.04–2.32, p = 0.033). This association persisted after adjustment for age, gender, and race (model 2; OR = 1.60, 95% CI, 1.05–2.45, p = 0.031) and remained statistically significant with a consistent effect size following further adjustment for education, BMI, smoking, and alcohol use (Model 3; OR = 1.58, 95% CI, 1.02–2.43, p = 0.040; Figure 1B). Subgroup analyses examining the depression-psoriasis association across demographic and lifestyle factors identified significant associations specifically among males (OR = 1.92, 95% CI, 1.06–3.50; p = 0.03) and Black individuals (OR = 3.44, 95% CI, 1.40–6.41; p = 0.01; Figure 1C), but not in other subgroups. No significant interactions were observed for any stratification variable, indicating these factors did not meaningfully modify the depression-psoriasis relationship in this cohort. Genetically predicted relationships between depression and psoriasis risk

To further assess causality, a two-sample MR analysis was conducted to evaluate the association between genetically predicted depression and overall psoriasis risk (Figure 2A). Using the IVW method as the primary approach, depression was associated with a 70.4% increased risk of psoriasis (OR, 1.70; 95% CI, 1.17–2.48; p = 0.005; scatter and forest plots shown in Figures 2C and 2E, respectively). Supplementary MR methods (simple mode, weighted median) supported the directionality of this effect for overall psoriasis (Table S6), confirming consistency across analytical approaches. Sensitivity analyses indicated the presence of heterogeneity (p = 0.031) but no evidence of horizontal pleiotropy (Table S7). Funnel plot symmetry suggested a low risk of directional bias (Figure 2D), and leave-one-out analysis demonstrated that the causal estimate was not substantially driven by any single single-nucleotide polymorphism (Figure 2F). For psoriasis subtypes, a positive but non-significant association was observed between depression and psoriasis vulgaris (OR, 1.49; 95% CI, 0.96–2.33; p = 0.077) (Figure S1). No significant associations were found between depression and guttate psoriasis (OR, 3.66; 95% CI, 0.66–20.42; p = 0.139) (Figure S2) or generalized pustular psoriasis (OR, 0.82; 95% CI, 0.04–15.33; p = 0.893) (Figure S3). Sensitivity analyses for the psoriasis subtypes revealed no significant heterogeneity or pleiotropy. CUMS modeling exacerbates skin lesions in IMQ-treated mice

To investigate the impact of depression on psoriasis phenotypes, we established four animal model groups: Control, CUMS, IMQ, and (CUMS + IMQ). After three weeks of CUMS modeling, mice were treated with IMQ topically for five days (Figure 3A). Behavioral assessments, including a reduction in sucrose consumption, and prolonged immobility in the tail suspension test and forced swim test, confirmed the successful establishment of the CUMS model (Figures 3B–3D). The Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI) scores revealed significantly greater skin scaling and erythema in the CUMS + IMQ group compared to the IMQ group (Figures 3E and 3F). Identification of differentially expressed genes in psoriasis and depression

A total of 5,679 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified from the psoriasis dataset, comprising 2,693 up-regulated and 2,986 down-regulated genes (Figures 4A and 4B). The merged depression dataset yielded 176 DEGs, including 113 upregulated and 63 downregulated genes (Figures 4C and 4D). A total of 77 overlapping DEGs were found in both psoriasis and depression datasets. Subsequently, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were conducted using these overlapping DEGs to identify shared molecular mechanisms in psoriasis and depression. The GO analysis revealed associations with biological processes such as response to virus and neutrophil degranulation, cellular components like secretory granule lumen and cytoplasmic vesicle lumen, and molecular functions including purine ribonucleoside binding (Figure 4E). KEGG analysis indicated connections between co-DEGs and pathways such as the TNF signaling pathway, apoptosis, and NFKB signaling pathway (Figure 4F). As indicated by enrichment analysis, apoptosis may serve as a crucial link between psoriasis and depression. Multi-omics integration identifies MMP9 as a shared hub gene in depression and psoriasis

To further identify the key genes with the greatest diagnostic potential, we applied three machine learning algorithms: LASSO, RF, and SVM-RFE. For psoriasis, 12 genes were identified by LASSO, 13 by RF, and 13 by SVM-RFE (Figures 5A–5D). For depression, LASSO, RF, and SVM-RFE identified seven, ten, and 17 genes, respectively (Figures 5E–5H). The overlap among these results identified six biomarkers: NFKB1, MMP9, IL1RN, PIK3CD, PLAU, and TNF. We also used ROC curves to assess the predictive value of these genes diagnostically across various datasets. The area under the curve (AUC) for these six genes was 1.00 in the psoriasis dataset (Figure 5I) and 0.849 in the depression dataset (Figure 5J). We further identified potential risk genes within co-DEGs for both psoriasis and depression using transcriptome and proteome MR analysis. The genes EIF2AK2, MMP9, and NFKB1 were identified as potential risk factors for psoriasis (Figure 6C), which were further validated using plasma protein levels through pQTL data (Figure 6D). MMP9 was identified as a potential risk factor for depression (Figure 6C), also confirmed by examining plasma protein levels with pQTL data (Figure 6D). A Venn diagram further illustrates the overlap of these risk genes (Figure 6E). Collectively, these results indicate that MMP9 plays an important role in the pathogenesis of both psoriasis and depression.

The clustering algorithm was utilized to classify circulating cells into 22 distinct clusters. As previously described in the literature,7 we identified 10 cell clusters from the entire single-cell map (Figure 6F). Subsequently, our localization analysis of key diagnostic markers revealed that most of these markers are predominantly expressed in keratinocytes, with MMP9 being significantly highly expressed in myeloid cells (Figure 6G).

To further investigate MMP9 expression, we established four animal models: (1) control group; (2) IMQ group; (3) CUMS group; and (4) CUMS + IMQ group. Western blot analysis revealed that MMP9 exhibited the highest expression levels in the CUMS + IMQ group, followed by the IMQ-induced and CUMS-treated groups, compared to the control group in brain tissue (Figures 6H and 6I). Similar trends were also observed in skin tissue (Figures 6J and 6K).

We investigated whether curcumin could ameliorate psoriasis-like skin lesions and depressive-like behaviors in the dual-disease mouse model through the modulation of MMP9. After successfully establishing the dual-disease model, curcumin was administered via oral gavage as a therapeutic intervention. Macroscopic evaluation of dorsal skin lesions demonstrated marked improvement in erythema, scaling, and epidermal folding, accompanied by a significant reduction in PASI scores (Figures 7B and 7C; p < 0.01). Histopathological analysis further confirmed these observations, showing decreased epidermal hyperplasia in treated animals (Figures 7D and 7E; p < 0.001). Behavioral tests revealed significant attenuation of depressive-like behaviors in the curcumin-treated group compared to the vehicle-treated controls (Figures 7F and 7G; p < 0.05; p < 0.001). Western blot analysis of brain tissue revealed downregulated MMP9 expression levels (p < 0.001), a finding corroborated by parallel trends observed in skin tissue lysates (p < 0.01). Taken together, these results demonstrate that curcumin can effectively alleviate psoriasis-like skin lesions and depressive-like behaviors in a dual-disease mouse model by modulating MMP9 expression.

Discussion

The frequent comorbidity of depression and psoriasis has long been acknowledged in clinical practice. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying this bidirectional relationship have remained unclear until now. Our study provides compelling mechanistic evidence that depression actively exacerbates psoriatic pathogenesis through dysregulation of apoptotic pathways, with MMP9 emerging as the central molecular mediator. Using an IMQ-induced mouse model of psoriasis combined with CUMS to induce depression-like behaviors, we observed that depressive states significantly aggravated psoriatic skin lesions, increasing both erythema and scaling scores compared to psoriasis-only controls. These findings significantly advance the understanding of psychodermatology.

Our multi-omics approach revealed apoptosis as the key shared pathological mechanism between these conditions. Apoptosis, as a form of programmed cell death, is essential for maintaining cellular homeostasis. In this study, we have identified significant upregulation of apoptotic markers in both psoriatic skin and depressed brain tissues. These findings align with established pathological features of both diseases: psoriasis exhibits characteristic keratinocyte hyperproliferation coupled with incomplete apoptosis,8 while depression demonstrates increased apoptotic activity in both peripheral blood mononuclear cells and hippocampal neurons.9,10 The kallikrein-8 (KLK8) pathway, recently implicated in stress-induced neuronal apoptosis, may represent one potential convergence point between these processes.11

Curcumin, the primary active constituent derived from the traditional Chinese herb Curcuma longa (turmeric), exhibits potent anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and immunomodulatory properties.12 Our study validates curcumin, targeting the key hub gene MMP9, as a promising dual-therapeutic strategy, highlighting its potential for both predicting and mitigating these conditions. MMP9, recognized for its function in degrading and remodeling the extracellular matrix to maintain tissue structure and function, shows increased expression in psoriasis in a previous study.13 Notably, our MR analysis provided the first genetic evidence supporting a causal relationship between MMP9 expression and both conditions, with MMP9 protein levels showing strong positive correlations with disease severity in clinical samples. Single-cell RNA sequencing further localized MMP9 overexpression to specific cell populations, primarily neutrophils in psoriatic lesions and microglia in depressed brains, suggesting tissue-specific apoptotic mechanisms converging through this common mediator. Molecular docking studies revealed curcumin’s high-affinity binding to MMP9, while in vivo experiments demonstrated its remarkable efficacy in concurrently improving both dermatological and psychiatric manifestations. Curcumin has proven effective in managing numerous diseases, including neurological and dermatological disorders.14,15 Clinical trials have indicated that daily topical application of curcumin to patients with scalp psoriasis not only improves skin symptoms but also enhances the quality of life.16 Similarly, oral curcumin has been shown to be effective in alleviating pain and swelling in patients with psoriatic arthritis.17 In addition, a previous study has verified curcumin’s antidepressant efficacy as an adjunct therapy.18 In this study, we carried out a rescue experiment by administering curcumin via oral gavage to dual-model mice. Given the known characteristics of curcumin, such as high hydrophobicity and poor bioavailability, we adopted a relatively high administration dose of 400 mg/kg with reference to multiple literatures.19,20 This experiment confirmed its targeted effect on the downregulation of MMP9 at the protein expression level. Skin lesion scoring and behavioral assessments demonstrated that our therapeutic approach effectively alleviated both psoriasis-like skin lesions and depressive-like behaviors. This mechanism-based action distinguishes curcumin from conventional biologics, which often show paradoxical depression-worsening effects, highlighting the unique advantage of this natural compound.

The broader implications of this research extend beyond depression and psoriasis, offering insights into the molecular underpinnings of psychodermatological crosstalk more broadly. The proposed MMP9-mediated brain-skin axis hypothesis may represent a fundamental pathway through which psychological stress exacerbates various inflammatory skin conditions, from atopic dermatitis to alopecia areata. Similarly, curcumin’s therapeutic potential could be explored across this spectrum of stress-aggravated dermatoses. From a drug development perspective, our work establishes a template for repositioning traditional herbal medicines as targeted therapies through systematic identification of their specific molecular targets in complex comorbidities.

In summary, this study integrates population-level data, MR analyses, and a comorbid mouse model to provide converging evidence that depression is associated with the exacerbation of psoriasis. Multi-omics analyses identify apoptosis as a potentially shared pathological mechanism underlying both conditions and highlight MMP9 as a central hub gene linking depressive and psoriatic phenotypes. Notably, MMP9 emerges as a promising diagnostic biomarker as well as a potential therapeutic target. Furthermore, although curcumin exerts its effects through multi-target anti-inflammatory mechanisms, it represents an accessible and cost-effective therapeutic option for the concurrent management of depression and psoriasis.

Limitations of the study

While these findings represent significant progress, several limitations must be acknowledged as they define important directions for future research. First, the genetic instruments used in our MR analysis were predominantly derived from European-ancestry GWAS. Furthermore, the cross-sectional NHANES analysis relied on a US cohort with self-reported psoriasis diagnoses, which may not fully reflect clinical heterogeneity across ethnic groups. Caution is therefore warranted when generalizing these results to non-European populations. Second, although our integrated multi-omics strategy offers broad mechanistic insight, other pathways—such as epigenetic regulation and gut-brain-skin axis crosstalk—may also contribute to this comorbidity and merit further exploration. Third, direct functional validation of MMP9 (e.g., through knockdown or overexpression assays) is lacking in the current study. Subsequent investigations should incorporate more comprehensive mechanistic validation experiments to clarify the necessity and sufficiency of MMP9 as a core hub gene mediating the pathogenic mechanisms of both diseases. Fourth, despite the relatively low oral bioavailability of natural curcumin, our study did not perform a comprehensive evaluation of the long-term safety or potential toxicity at the administered dose (400 mg/kg/day). Future work incorporating detailed pharmacokinetic and toxicological profiling will be essential to firmly establish its safety profile and optimal dosing for potential therapeutic application. Notwithstanding these limitations, our findings provide a robust foundation for both immediate clinical translation and continued mechanistic exploration.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animal information

All mice were of the species Mus musculus (C57BL/6). All experiments were performed with male mice (6-8 weeks of age) purchased from the Animal Core Facility of Nanjing Medical University. The study design consisted of two different parts. In part 1, mice were randomly divided into four groups: (1) Control group; (2) Chronic unpredictable mild stress (CUMS) group; (3) Imiquimod (IMQ) group; (4) CUMS+IMQ group. In part 2, mice were randomly divided into two groups: (1) CUMS+IMQ group; (2) CUMS+IMQ+treatment group. All animal experiments in this study were approved by the Experimental Animal Welfare Ethics Committee of Nanjing Medical University (IACUC-2403029).

Human study participants

This study utilized data from the 2009–2014 NHANES, which employs a multi-cycle, cross-sectional design with complex sampling techniques to yield a nationally representative cohort. Psoriasis diagnosis was ascertained via a self-administered questionnaire.21 Depression diagnosis was assessed using the 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire. Each item was scored from 0 to 3, yielding a total score range of 0–27; scores ≥10 indicated clinically relevant depression.22 Covariates encompassed sociodemographic and health behavioral characteristics. Sociodemographic variables included age, sex, race/ethnicity, and educational attainment. Health behaviors comprised smoking status (categorized as never smokers [<100 lifetime cigarettes], former smokers [quit after >100 cigarettes], and current smokers), alcohol use (binary), and body mass index (BMI; normal [<25 kg/m2], overweight [25 to 30 kg/m2], obese [≥30 kg/m2]). Participants aged ≥20 years with complete covariate, psoriasis, and depression data were included.

Method details

NHANES analysis

To account for the complex NHANES sampling design, analyses incorporated appropriate sampling weights. For NHANES analyses, continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared using t-tests; categorical variables are presented as frequencies (percentages) and analyzed via chi-square tests. Weighted logistic regression models evaluated associations between depression and psoriasis risk. Subgroup and interaction analyses assessed stability.

Mendelian randomization analysis

A two-sample MR analysis was conducted to investigate the causal relationship between depression and psoriasis (including subtypes), using genetic variants as instrumental variables (IVs) to minimize confounding and reverse causation. The study adhered to STROBE-MR guidelines and three core MR assumptions23:(1) IVs are robustly linked to exposures, (2) IVs are independent of confounders, and (3) IVs affect the outcome exclusively through exposures, without any alternative pathways. Exposure data for depression were derived from a European-ancestry genome-wide association study (GWAS) by Okbay et al.24 Psoriasis and subtype data were obtained from the FinnGen database, including overall psoriasis (N = 494,941), psoriasis vulgaris (N = 490,093), guttate psoriasis (N = 482,667), and generalized pustular psoriasis (GPP; N = 482,303). Critically, the depression and psoriasis datasets exhibited no sample overlap. Expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) data were retrieved from the eQTLGen Consortium (https://eqtlgen.org/; N = 31,684). Protein quantitative trait loci (pQTL) data associated with plasma proteins were sourced from an Icelandic study based on the SomaScan platform3 (N = 35,559). Additional psoriasis GWAS data were obtained from Neal et al., encompassing 337,159 samples. Detailed characteristics of the MR study populations are provided in Table S1.

Genome-wide significant single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) strongly associated with depression (p < 5 × 10-6) were selected as IVs. These SNPs were clumped for independence (R2 < 0.001; 10,000 kb window) using the 1000 Genomes European reference panel, with subsequent removal of palindromic and allele-incompatible variants. The significance threshold for cis-eQTLs and cis-pQTLs was set at P < 5e-8, with aggregation parameters set to r2 < 0.1 and kb = 10,000 kb. To ensure robust instruments, the statistical strength of each SNP was assessed using the F-statistic (F = β2/se2), where β and SE represent the effect size and standard error of the SNP-exposure association. SNPs with an F-statistic < 10 were excluded to mitigate weak instrument bias (Tables S2–S5). We employed the inverse variance weighted (IVW) method as the primary approach for our analysis. Supplementary analyses included the MR-Egger, weighted median, weighted mode, and simple mode methods. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q test for the IVW and MR-Egger methods, horizontal pleiotropy was evaluated with the MR-Egger regression, and sensitivity analysis was conducted using the leave-one-out method.

CUMS mouse model

The CUMS protocol was implemented as previously described,25 involving randomized exposure to distinct stressors (circadian rhythm disruption, 45° cage tilt, physical restraint, cold exposure, forced swim, stroboscopic lighting, food/water deprivation, thermal stress, and bedding alterations) randomly administered over a 4-week period. Control cohorts remained in standard housing. A series of behavioral tests was conducted after all these procedures to validate the successful establishment of this model.

IMQ-induced psoriasis-like mouse model

Psoriasis-like mice were induced by 5% IMQ cream.26 Mice received topical application of 62.5 mg 5% IMQ to the shaved dorsal skin of a limited area (2 × 3 cm2) for 5 days, and control mice were given the same dose of Vaseline for 5 days. The skin was examined every day, and the dorsa were photographed. The mice were sacrificed by cervical dislocation on day 6, and their skin and brain were collected for the next experiment.

Combined mouse model of CUMS and IMQ psoriasis

The mice in the combination model were treated with CUMS procedure for 21 days in advance, and the successful establishment of depressive-like symptoms was assessed. Topical IMQ was applied for the next 5 days. During this period, stressors potentially interfering with topical drug efficacy (e.g., wet bedding, cold water swimming) were excluded, while other chronic unpredictable mild stressors were maintained according to the original CUMS protocol. The total duration of the dual-model induction was 28 days.

Curcumin treatment

We screened out promising drug, curcumin. The CUMS+IMQ+curcumin treatment group was administered 400 mg/Kg/day curcumin (Macklin, C805204) dissolved in double-distilled water via oral gavage for 5 days, with the treatment period synchronized with the IMQ-induced model establishment. The CUMS+IMQ group was administered the same volume of double-distilled water as a control.

Sucrose preference test (SPT)

The SPT was used to quantify loss of interest in rewarding stimuli. After water deprivation, mice were exposed to two bottles, which contained sucrose water (1% (wt/vol)) and tap water (normal water) respectively for 12 h. The sucrose preference value was calculated according to the formula: sucrose preference (%) = sucrose solution consumption/total fluid intake ×100%.

Tail suspension test (TST)

The TST test was conducted to assess behavioral despair and helplessness in mice. Immobility time, defined as the absence of escape-oriented movements beyond those required to maintain posture, was quantified during the last 4-minute interval of a 6-minute trial period using automated video tracking software.

Forced swim test (FST)

The mice were placed in a cylindrical water tank (30 cm high, 24 cm diameter) filled with 20 ± 1°C water to a depth of 25 cm and forced to swim for a 5-min session. The immobility time was documented when mice remained motionless. All tests were documented by an installed video camera and evaluated through video tracking software.

Western blot

Mice back skin tissues and cortex were lysed in RIPA lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors, followed by centrifugation at 4°C to collect the supernatant. Protein concentrations were measured using a BCA assay to ensure consistency across samples. The proteins were then mixed with loading buffer, boiled for 5 min to denature them. Proteins were separated by SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes. The membranes were immunoblotted with specific primary antibodies (MMP9, A2095, diluted at 1/1000; β-actin, ab32572, diluted at 1/4000; GAPDH, ab8245, diluted at 1/4000) and proteins detected with appropriate secondary anti-rabbit antibody conjugated to fluorescence. Immunoreactivity was visualized by the Odyssey Imaging System.

Histopathological examination

Dorsal skin tissue was fixed in 20% formalin and embedded in paraffin wax. Sections 8 um thick were cut from the paraffin blocks and stained with haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) using standard methods for histological examination under a light microscope (BX-51; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan), and the epidermal thickness was measured using the Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software (Media Cybernetics, Inc., MD, USA).

Bulk RNA-seq data preprocessing

We retrieved datasets from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database for differential gene expression analysis. The selected datasets were GSE30999, GSE13355, GSE19738, GSE76826, and GSE39653. For psoriasis, GSE30999 contributed 85 psoriasis skin specimens and 85 healthy skin specimens,27 while GSE13355 provided an additional 158 psoriasis skin specimens and 64 healthy skin specimens.28,29,30 For depression, GSE19738 contributed 33 peripheral blood samples from patients with depression and 34 from healthy individuals,31 while GSE76826 added 10 depression peripheral blood samples and 12 from healthy controls.32 GSE39653 included whole blood samples from 21 individuals with depression and 24 healthy controls.33 Differential expression analysis was conducted using the ‘limma’ package in R, detecting differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with criteria of P < 0.05 and |Log2FC| > 0.5. Volcano plots were employed to visually represent the upregulated and downregulated DEGs in the psoriasis and depression datasets. The overlap of DEGs from both datasets was illustrated using a Venn diagram created with an online tool. (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/).

Functional enrichment analysis

We conducted enrichment analysis on the genes shared between psoriasis and depression DEGs sets to elucidate their functional contexts. The Gene Ontology (GO) database provides annotations for the molecular functions, biological pathways, and cellular components of gene products. The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database archives gene pathway information across different species and predicts the roles of protein interaction networks in various cellular activities.

Apoptosis gene set acquisition

We obtained apoptosis-related genes from the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/), identifying a total of 1,671 genes with a correlation score above 3.0. Subsequently, we selected intersecting apoptosis-related genes and co-differentially expressed genes (co-DEGs) for further analysis.

Machine learning

Combining statistical approaches with computer science, machine learning creates predictive algorithms that can learn independently from large-scale gene expression datasets, allowing for the identification of significant genes. In our study, we employed several prominent machine learning (ML) techniques—least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO), random forest (RF), and support vector machine recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE)—to pinpoint key apoptosis-related genes. Next, we overlapped the disease characteristic genes obtained from these three techniques, generated a Venn diagram to determine the final set of key genes, and carried out receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis on the selected genes. Subsequently, we validated these genes in the GSE13355 and GSE39653 datasets and plotted ROC curves.

PPI network analysis

The STRING database (http://string-db.org/) serves as a comprehensive resource for exploring protein-protein interactions (PPIs). We utilized this database to construct a PPI network for co-DEGs, with a confidence score threshold set at >0.4. The network was then visualized using Cytoscape 3.9.1, which aided in mapping the interactions and evaluating node connectivity. To uncover significant apoptosis-related modules within the PPI network, we employed the Molecular Complex Detection algorithm via the Cytoscape plugin. This algorithm identifies densely connected regions, suggesting potential functional complexes or modules.

ScRNA-seq data preprocessing

Single-cell RNA sequencing (ScRNA-seq) allows for whole-genome gene expression profiling at single-cell resolution, allowing the observation of specific gene expression levels across different cells. The psoriasis scRNA-seq data, accession number GSE173706, was retrieved from the GEO database, comprising skin cells from 8 healthy and 14 psoriasis biopsy specimens.34 We selected 8 healthy skin libraries and 14 lesional psoriasis libraries for further analysis. Using the “Seurat” R package, we utilized the PercentageFeatureSet function to assess the proportion of ribosomal RNA and mitochondrial DNA in the dataset. Cells with fewer than 200 genes or 100 transcripts, or with more than 20% mitochondrial gene expression, were deemed low-quality and excluded. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) was employed to reduce dimensions and identify clusters at a resolution of 0.5. Following this, cells were annotated using various cell surface markers.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking experiments were conducted to investigate potential therapeutic agents targeting hub genes for the treatment of comorbid psoriasis and depression. We identified candidate drugs for these core genes using the Drug Signatures Database (DSigDB) (https://maayanlab.cloud/Enrichr/) and the Drug Gene Interaction Database (DGIdb) (https://dgidb.org/). These candidates were then prioritized according to MMP9 relevance, regulatory approval or known safety profile, and translational feasibility. Subsequently, the protein structures corresponding to the hub genes and the small molecule drugs were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) (https://www.rcsb.org/) and PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), respectively. Finally, AutoDock Vina and PyMOL software were utilized to simulate the binding interactions between the targeted proteins and small-molecule drugs.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.1) and GraphPad prism version 8.0.1. Data were expressed as the mean ± SD. To compare differences between two groups, unpaired, two - tailed Student’s t - tests were used. For multiple group comparisons, one-or two-way ANOVA followed by thm-Sidak post hoc tests were employed. P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Significance levels: ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001.
