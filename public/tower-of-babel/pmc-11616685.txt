In vivo dynamic tracking of cerebral chloride regulation using molecularly tailored liquid/liquid interfacial ultramicro iontronics
Authors: Chaoyue Gu, Fanzhen Kong, Sen Liang, Xiang Zhao, Bingjie Kong, Tianhe Jiang, Jianan Yu, Qi Li, Yuqing Lin, Shuo Bai, Yuanhua Shao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Chloride ion, a pivotal cerebral anion involved in neuronal inhibition, is implicated in various neurodegenerative diseases. Conventional direct faradaic detection based on electron transfers at solid electrode/solution interfaces has been proven ineffective due to the electrochemically inactive nature of Cl−. Here, we present an approach involving molecularly tailored liquid/liquid interfacial ultramicro iontronics (L/L-UIs) supported at ultramicropipettes filled with organic gel containing lipophilic bis-thioureas ionophores, which represents the first application of amperometric methodology based on electrochemical facilitated ion transfers reactions at a soft L/L ultramicrointerface to achieve in vivo sensing of electrochemically inactive ions, and dynamically tracking cerebral Cl− in vivo. Furthermore, evidence of dynamic neuronal Cl− regulation via KCC2 modulated through GABAB receptors was provided, further substantiating GABAB receptor–mediated Cl−-related neuronal inhibition. The proposed L/L-UIs have notable potential for in situ tracking of other crucial electrochemically inactive ions or ionized biomolecules in vivo, thereby facilitating the study of brain diseases and the diagnosis and treatment of related disorders.

INTRODUCTION

Electrochemically inactive ions (ECIIs), usually refer to as ions cannot undergo redox reactions in the matrix within potential window, play crucial roles in the physiological activities of the brain (1, 2). For example, chloride ion (Cl−) is closely involved in neuronal inhibition (3). Monitoring neuronal excitation through action potentials is relatively straightforward, but directly monitoring neuronal inhibition, especially in cases of shunting inhibition where the inward flow of negatively charged Cl− is equivalent to the outward flow of positive current, is challenging (4, 5). However, the dynamic fluctuation of Cl− concentration can reflect the neuronal inhibition process in real time, providing a potential approach for monitoring neuronal inhibition. Previous evidence suggests that the abnormalities in Cl−-related neuronal inhibition in central nervous system are closely related to various neurodegenerative diseases, such as Alzheimer's disease (AD) and epilepsy (see Fig. 1A), of which the level of cerebral Cl− of human are decreased (2 to 10% versus normal range of 120 to 132 mM) (3, 6, 7). Therefore, dynamically tracking cerebral ECIIs, specifically Cl−, is of great significance for exploring the pathology of neurodegenerative diseases at the molecular level and providing more scientific and effective diagnosis and treatment programs.

Despite the continuous development of various fluorescent probes, the limitations of tissue penetration depth, autofluorescence, and blood-brain barrier have made dynamic optical tracking of cerebral ECIIs in vivo very difficult (8). To date, various implanted micro/nanoelectrochemical biosensors with high temporal and spatial resolution, as well as exceptional sensitivity, have been developed, enabling the analysis of cerebral electrochemically active chemicals and providing insights into brain function and activity (9–13). However, the methodologies for dynamically tracking cerebral ECIIs in vivo remain limited. Direct faradaic detection of cerebral ECIIs based on electron transfers (ETs) at conventional solid electrode/solution interfaces has failed due to the redox potentials of ECIIs exceeding the potential window of water decomposition under physiological conditions. It is difficult for Cl− to undergo ET reaction at such interfaces, because the oxygen evolution reaction in aqueous solution will occur preferentially. Although recognition units such as enzymes and aptamers have been found to improve the selectivity detection of ECIIs, the available natural enzymes and aptamers are still limited, and these recognition processes are usually irreversible, making them unsuitable for dynamic tracking of cerebral ECIIs (2, 14). In addition, the mechanical stability of the detection interface is also an important factor affecting the long-term dynamic tracking of cerebral ECIIs. Therefore, development of new electrochemical sensing methods with wide applicability is highly desirable to enable dynamic tracking of cerebral ECIIs, e.g., Cl−, in vivo.

Compared to traditional electrochemical methods based on ETs at solid electrode/solution interfaces, ion transfers (ITs) reaction at liquid/liquid (L/L) interfaces, which refer to interfaces composed of two immiscible electrolyte solutions, have emerged as highly promising analytical strategies for the detection of ions (15, 16). However, ITs at an L/L interface are usually thermodynamically unfavorable and lack specificity, resulting in the unobserved interfacial current signals within the potential window and susceptibility to interference from other ions (17). Nevertheless, by incorporating an ionophore into the organic phase, the thermodynamically unfavorable transfer of hydrophilic ions from an aqueous solution to the organic phase becomes possible, enabling facilitated ITs (FITs) processes with high selectivity through heterogeneous ion-ionophore recognition (17–19). Previous studies have used β-octafluoro-meso-octamethylcalix[4]pyrrole as an ionophore in 1,2-dichloroethane (DCE) to facilitate the transfer of hydrophilic redox-inactive anions at an L/L ultramicrointerface supported by an ultramicropipette (20). Inspiration from these findings, determining the FITs of ECIIs at the ultramicropipette-supported L/L interface could provide a methodology for dynamically tracking cerebral ECIIs in vivo.

In this study, we present the design of molecularly tailored L/L interfacial ultramicro iontronics (L/L-UIs) supported by an ultramicropipette filled with an organic phase containing lipophilic bis-thioureas ionophores with exceptional selectivity and sensitivity (Fig. 1B). Unlike electronics, iontronics (also called ionotronics) relies on ions as carriers of signals (21). To address the dynamic issues of L/L ultramicrointerfaces, we have incorporated gel matrices into the organic phase to increase their mechanical stability (22). By molecularly tailoring the L/L ultramicrointerface with specifically designed bis-thioureas ionophores (IPECl−1, IPECl−2, and IPECl−3), we achieved the thermodynamically favorable and reversible process of FITs of Cl− at the L/L ultramicrointerface supported on the L/L-UIs. The thermodynamic parameters, such as the stoichiometric ratio, the association constant logβ, and diffusion coefficient D were obtained. To the best of our knowledge, the amperometric methodology based on electrochemical FITs reactions at a soft L/L ultramicrointerface is the first application to achieve the sensing of ECIIs in vivo, as well as the first dynamic tracking of cerebral Cl− in vivo. The L/L-UIs successfully enabled highly selective and dynamic electrochemical tracking of Cl− in different brain areas of AD mice and epileptic rat models, providing evidence of dynamic neuronal Cl− regulation via the K+-Cl− cotransporter 2 (KCC2) modulated through γ-aminobutyric acid subtype B receptors (GABABRs), further supporting the GABABR-mediated Cl−-related neuronal inhibition in the AD mouse models (Fig. 1C) (23). The facile and reliable electrochemical L/L ultramicrointerface sensing platform exhibited great advantages for dynamically tracking other electrochemically inactive substances in the brain, particularly beneficial for evaluating brain processes at the molecular level.

RESULTS

Design and characterization of Cl− sensing platform

In this work, bis-thioureas were chosen as the ionophore of Cl− due to their prominent performance in anion binding (24, 25). As seen in Fig. 2 (A to C), three distinct bis-thioureas ionophores (IPECl−1, IPECl−2, and IPECl−3) were designed and synthesized, and characterized by 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), 13C NMR, and mass spectrometry (MS; figs. S1 to S9). The molecular design strategy involved incorporating a lipophilic, relatively flexible m-xylene spacer that incorporates H-bond donor groups, bis-thioureas, which act as recognition units toward H-bond acceptor for Cl−. The lipophilic scaffolds promote solubility in organic phase, enabling the molecules to act as anion carriers in organic solution. By regulating the electron-withdrawing substituents of the bis-thioureas, highly selective recognition of Cl− might be achieved (26). Specifically, the bis-thioureas moiety features distinct electron-withdrawing N-aryl groups, namely, p-nitrophenyl (red, resulting in IPECl−1), p-trifluoromethylphenyl (green, resulting in IPECl−2), and 3,5-bis(trifluoromethyl)phenyl (blue, resulting in IPECl−3). The charge density distributions were analyzed using density functional theory (DFT) calculations, revealing that Cl− has localized negative charges (Fig. 2, D to F). Further, p-nitrophenyl of IPECl−1 exhibited a notably higher negative surface charge and enhanced electron-absorbing properties that increase the acidity of H-atoms on the thioureas moiety of the receptor, consequently enhancing the H-bond affinity and binding strength.

The specific recognition abilities of three distinct bis-thioureas ionophores toward Cl− were assessed through DFT calculations and electrochemical measurements. As depicted in Fig. 2 (G to I), all three types of bis-thioureas displayed notable ability to interact with Cl− and the bond lengths of the hydrogen bonds formed between Cl− and the three IPEs were evaluated. Notably, the H-bond distances between Cl− and IPECl−1 and Cl− and IPECl−2 range from 2.20 to 2.38 Å, while IPECl−3 featuring a 3,5-bis(trifluoromethyl)phenyl substitution was measured at 2.49 Å, indicating a relatively weak interaction compared to the other two ionophores. To further investigate the distinguishing characteristics of the three bis-thiourea ionophores, the energy gap (Eg) between the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) of the ion-ionophore complexation reaction were evaluated. The Eg provides insights into the energy barrier that electrons need to overcome in the tunneling model (27). In these calculations, Cl− contributes to the HOMO orbital, while the ionophores mainly for the LUMO orbital. The calculated Eg values between IPECl−1, IPECl−2, IPECl−3, and Cl− were found to be 0.20, 0.24, and 0.23 eV, respectively, indicating that IPECl−1 exhibits a higher capability to bind with Cl− compared to the other two ionophores (Fig. 2J). Furthermore, the change in Gibbs free energy (ΔG) for the complexation reaction was calculated and illustrated in Fig. 2K. The ΔG values between IPECl−1, IPECl−2, IPECl−3, and Cl− were −13.3, −10.5, −11.7 kcal/mol, respectively. The results indicated that the ΔG value for IPECl−1 was more negative, suggesting that the thiourea moiety in IPECl−1 has a greater propensity to form bonds with Cl− compared to those in IPECl−2 and IPECl−3. The ΔG calculations were consistent with the HOMO-LUMO Eg calculations, further supporting the conclusion that IPECl−1 exhibits superior recognition capabilities for Cl− from a thermodynamic standpoint.

The utilization of IT reactions at L/L ultramicrointerfaces, constructed on the basis of micro/nano pipettes, offers a viable approach for fabricating amperometrically interfacial sensing platform. From a thermodynamic perspective, the half-wave potential (∆owϕ1/2) for the FIT reaction between Cl− and L in the case of a 1:1 (n = 1) ratio can be expressed as (28, 29)Δowϕ1/2=ΔowϕCl−0′+RTzFln(βL[Cl-])(2)where ∆owϕCl−0′ represents the formal potential of Cl− transfer across the L/L ultramicrointerface, z denotes the transferred charge of Cl−, βL is the association constant in the DCE phase, and [Cl−] indicates the concentration of Cl− in the aqueous phase. ITs voltammetry offers the possibility of specific sensing by providing distinctive signals corresponding to electrochemically inactive species at a specific ∆owϕ1/2. This property makes it suitable for constructing amperometric L/L-UIs, and its selectivity can be tailored by the polarization potential.

To confirm the Cl− recognition capability of IPECl at the L/L ultramicrointerface, cyclic voltammetry (CV) experiments were conducted using electrochemical cell 1. Negative current in the measurements corresponds to the transfer of negative charge from the aqueous phase to the DCE phase or positive charge from the DCE phase to the aqueous phase (17). The transfer potentials of FITs were calibrated using tetramethylammonium cation (TMA+) as an internal reference ion (30, 31). Figure 3B illustrated the voltammetric response in the absence of IPECl (black curve), displaying the characteristic potential window. However, in the presence of IPECl, a well-defined steady-state negative current ranging from 0 to −0.27 V was observed, indicating the FIT process of Cl− at such L/L interfaces. It is worth noting that the concentration of IPECl exceeds that of Cl−, making the diffusion of Cl− the rate-determining step of FITs (20, 32). The forward steady-state wave observed in the voltammogram, as shown in the symmetrical waveform, is controlled by the hemispherical diffusion of Cl− from the aqueous phase to the L/L interface with the formation of [IPE + Cl−] complex. This corresponds to the transfer process facilitated by interfacial complexation (TIC) mechanism (28, 29). Conversely, during the reversed scan, the voltammetric response reflects the hemispherical diffusion of the [IPE + Cl−] complex away from the ultramicrointerface, representing transfer through the interfacial dissociation (TID) mechanism (28, 29). The TIC and TID processes indicate that the binding and dissociation of the ionophores with Cl− at the L/L interface are chemically reversible processes, enabling dynamic tracking of Cl−.

The ∆owϕ1/2 and interfacial transfer current were meticulously recorded. As depicted in Fig. 3 (C and D), IPECl−1 exhibited a lower ∆owϕ1/2 and a higher transfer current, indicating its pronounced affinity for complex Cl− and remarkable ability to facilitate Cl− at the L/L interface. The driving force for Cl− transfer facilitated by different IPECl ionophores was evaluated by determining the difference in Gibbs transfer energy change (∆∆owGtr) (32). From the intercepts (figs. S10 to S12), the logβ were calculated as 7.82, 7.40, and 7.12 for IPECl−1, IPECl−2, and IPECl−3, respectively. Ultimately, these results demonstrate that the Cl− transfer process facilitated by IPECl−1 exhibits higher thermodynamically stability compared to IPECl−2 and IPECl−3. This enhanced thermodynamic stability contributes to the excellent recognition capabilities and exceptional electrochemical properties of IPECl−1 at the L/L ultramicrointerface.

We also used an electrochemical MS (EC-MS) technique, which used hybrid ultramicroelectrodes combined with pulse ionization to investigate interfacial reactions (33–36). The EC-MS can assess the interfacial complexation stoichiometry between the ionophore and Cl− using electrochemical cell 2. The experimental setup (shown in Fig. 4A) involved a dual-barrel micropipette system, one of which was filled with KCl aqueous solutions immobilized by agar gel, while the other contained DCE solutions of ionophore and supporting electrolyte (BTPPATPBF5). An L/L ultramicrointerface was formed at the tip of the micropipette, which was subjected to a suitable potential applied via an external DC power supply to initiate the FIT reaction. The same electrochemical cell 2 also functioned as the MS nanospray, propelled by a piezoelectric pistol, which could inject electrogenerated products and intermediates of FIT reactions directly into the mass spectrometer for analysis. When the voltage was turned off, we detected the presence of [IPECl−1 − H]− at mass/charge ratio (m/z) 495 and [IPECl−1 + Cl]− at m/z 531 (fig. S13). Note that the detection of [IPECl−1 + Cl]− resulted from the trace amount of Cl− originating from glassware or organic solution, which could complex with IPECl−1. The intensity ratio of the m/z 531 ion to the m/z 495 ion (I531/I495) was measured at 1.8. Upon setting the interfacial potential to −0.2 V (Fig. 4, B and C), IPECl−1 recognized Cl− at the L/L ultramicrointerfaces and facilitated their transfer from the organic phase to the agar phase, leading to an obvious increase in the [IPECl−1 + Cl]− signal with a simultaneous decline in the [IPECl−1 − H]− signal (I531/I495 = 12.8), which was seven times larger than that of the potential off (fig. S13). As depicted in the inset, the detected signals of m/z 531 displayed isotopic distributions similar to the theoretical values, exhibiting a characteristic peak in the isotope distribution associated with Cl− binding. To further interpret the peaks, we conducted collision-induced dissociation (CID) analysis. The fragment ions observed at m/z 495 corresponded precisely to the loss of HCl group (m/z 36) from parent ions m/z 531, further confirming the complexation was [IPECl−1 + Cl]−. Using the same methodology, we measured the intensity ratio of the [IPECl−2 + Cl]− at m/z 577 to [IPECl−2 − H]− at m/z 541 (I577/I541) to be 5.5 times higher after applying the potential compared to before (Fig. 4, D and E, and fig. S14). Similarly, the ratio I713/I677 resulting from the FIT reactions between IPECl−3 and Cl− showed a 5.6-fold increase (Fig. 4, F and G, and fig. S15). We confirmed the assignment of m/z 577 and m/z 713 peaks through CID analysis, which demonstrated the loss of the HCl group, providing solid evidence for the formation of [IPE + Cl]− complexes. Consequently, we observed interfacial recognition and facilitated transfer of Cl− by the EC-MS technique, unveiling the formation of 1:1 stoichiometry complex through the formation of four hydrogen bonds between the three distinct IPEs and Cl− in the interfacial layer.

Electrochemical performance of L/L-UIs in vitro

The mechanical stability of L/L interfaces supported at ultramicropipettes is crucial for the dynamic tracking of Cl− in vivo. However, the dynamic nature of L/L interfaces poses challenges in utilizing pipette-supported soft interfaces as probes for in vivo sensing. Challenges arise from phase mixing during the implantation process and electrode contamination caused by proteins in complex biological environments. To address these issues, we developed gel ultramicroelectrodes by incorporating polyvinylchloride (PVC) to solidify the organic phase (33). Despite an increase in iR drop due to the gel matrix, downsizing the L/L interface to the ultramicroscale will effectively minimize the iR drop. Additional, enhanced mass transfer at the ultramicroscale interface notably improves analytical sensitivity (37, 38). Among the tested ionophores, IPECl−1 exhibits stronger complexation and recognition toward Cl−, making it more suitable for sensing Cl−. Consequently, IPECl−1 was selected for the subsequent electrochemical quantitative monitoring of Cl− levels. We integrated the Cl− carrier IPECl−1 with gel ultramicropipettes to fabricate L/L-UIs, enabling selective sensing of target Cl−.

In this study, we thoroughly characterized the ultramicropipettes (fig. S16) and L/L-UIs (Fig. 5A). The environmental scanning electron microscopy (ESEM) image in Fig. 5A clearly shows a well-shaped orifice and complete filling of the micropipette tip with PVC gel, which is promising for electrochemical measurements. Figure 5B demonstrated the Cl− transfer with an initial potential of 0 V, followed by a steady-state response from 0 to −0.28 V (∆owϕ1/2: −143 mV), indicating that the gel introduction has negligible compromise on recognition performance, apart from a decline in the diffusion coefficient (D) of IPEs. The D values of three IPEs in the gel of organic solution were also calculated, which were (6.1 ± 0.2) ×10−7 cm2 s−1, (1.8 ± 0.1) ×10−7 cm2 s−1, and (3.3 ± 0.3) ×10−7 cm2 s−1, respectively (fig. S17 to S19) (33, 39).

The stability of L/L-UIs was evaluated by comparing them with silanized L/L-UIs, in which the organic phase was not immobilized by PVC gels. Differential pulse voltammetry (DPV) was used to analyze the electrochemical performance of the developed L/L interface sensing platform. Figure 5C showed that after six repeated measurements, the currents recorded from the silanized L/L-UIs exhibited a notable increase compared to the gel-based L/L-UIs, indicating better stability of the latter ones. The long-term stability of L/L-UIs in aqueous solution was also evaluated, and the electrochemical current response showed little change (<9.8%) after recording the i-t response of Cl− sensing with the L/L-UIs for 8 hours (Fig. 5D), confirming their relative stability in aqueous solution. L/L-UIs also exhibited excellent stability under bovine serum albumin (BSA) conditions (Fig. 5E), demonstrating their antibiofouling ability.

To investigate the specificity detection of Cl− in physiological environments, the DPV responses of various potentially interfering biological related analytes were examined in a level of brain, including anions (SO42−, H2PO4−, HCO3−, ClO4−, NO3−, I−, Br−, F−, Ac−, and OH−), metal ions (K+, Na+, Ca2+, Mg2+, Cu2+, Zn2+, Co2+, Fe2+, Fe3+, Cd2+, and Ni2+), amino acids (Ser, Val, Arg, His, Gly, Cys, Ile, Tyr, Glu, Lys, and Thr), and other biological species (DA, AA, UA, lactic, glucose, and Dopac) (40). As shown in Fig. 5F and figs. S20 to S23, the L/L-UIs exhibited good selectivity for the detection of Cl− with negligible interference (<3.2%) from other analytes. Besides, L/L-UIs for Cl− sensing were not influenced by the fluctuation of pH and temperature (figs. S24 and S25).

The current variation of L/L-UIs against various concentrations of Cl− was evaluated by DPV to assess the amperometric electrochemical responsiveness. Figure 5G illustrated the observation of peak at −141 mV, which was attributed to the FIT of Cl− at the L/L interface. Within the concentration range of 0.5 to 200 mM (Fig. 5H), the current peak (Ip) displayed a pronounced and linear increment in response to logarithm of concentration of Cl− [Ip = −5.06 (±0.16)LogC − 1.82 (±0.23), R2 = 0.992], affirming the quantitative determination capability of the developed L/L-UIs. The limit of detection of this method was about 0.1 mM, determined by three standard deviations. Following this, the absolute sensitivity and relative sensitivity were computed based on the current’s dependence on various concentrations of Cl−. The maximum absolute sensitivity of the L/L-UIs was −2.12 pA mM−1 (fig. S26), and the maximum relative sensitivity was 328% mM−1 (fig. S27). The precision (δC) of the L/L-UIs, which represents the minimum fluctuation in the concentration of Cl− that the sensor can distinguish, was evaluated by the following equation (41)δC=(∂C∂I)δI(6)where ∂C/∂I and δI represent the inverse of the slope in the corresponding calibration plots of I (pA) versus Cl− concentrations (mM) and the SD of the averaged currents, respectively. The minimum precision of the L/L-UIs was about 0.013 mM (fig. S28), which means that the L/L-UIs could sensitively monitor the small change of Cl− concentration.

The reproducibility of the L/L-UIs was characterized by cycling DPV tests in solutions with two different Cl− concentrations under the same experimental conditions. Figure 5I showed that after 12 cycles of consecutive testing, the current values of L/L-UIs were fully reversible without notable hysteresis, with reproducibility better than 97%, demonstrating excellent reproducibility and reversibility, which is crucial for dynamic tracking of Cl−.

In vivo cerebral Cl− level measurement by L/L-UIs

On the basis of the promising electrochemical results of L/L-UIs for Cl− sensing in vitro, we further investigated their application in vivo detection capabilities. We selected AD and epilepsy, which are neurodegenerative conditions closely associated with Cl− levels in the brain, as representative models to examine Cl− level alterations by implanting L/L-UIs in specific brain regions including the hippocampus, cortex, and striatum. Figure 6 (A to D) showed the DPVs results obtained using L/L-UIs in normal and AD models. In the normal mouse model, the baseline Cl− levels were detected at 102.5 ± 4.1 mM in the hippocampus, 71.8 ± 3.9 mM in the striatum, and 68.9 ± 3.2 mM in the cortex (n = 6). In contrast, the Cl− levels in the hippocampus of AD mice exhibited a notable decrease (69.4 ± 3.4 mM) compared to the normal brain, while relatively consistent values 69.3 ± 3.3 and 67.1 ± 3.7 mM were observed in the striatum and cortex, respectively (n represents different mice).

In the epilepsy rat model, Cl− levels decreased to 70.1 ± 3.6 mM in the hippocampus compared to the normal brain’s level of 105.2 ± 4.6 mM (Fig. 6, E and F). The striatum showed a Cl− level of 66.3 ± 4.4 mM, contrasting with the normal rat brain’s level of 80.2 ± 3.4 mM, while the cortex exhibited a level of 64.5 ± 4.0 mΜ compared to 78.5 ± 3.3 mM in the normal rat brain (n = 6, n represents different rats) (Fig. 6, G and H). In addition, we compared the L/L-UIs method at the L/L interface with the conventional Volhard’s method for Cl− determination in microdialysates from the rat brain’s hippocampus (table S1 and fig. S29). The results demonstrated good agreement between the two methods (n = 3), further confirming the accuracy of L/L-UIs for Cl− determination.

Overall, these findings indicate significantly lower Cl− levels in the hippocampus of both AD mouse and epilepsy rat models compared to normal models, suggesting the critical involvement of the hippocampus in Cl− dysregulation. To assess the biocompatibility of L/L-UIs, we conducted hematoxylin and eosin staining experiments on brain slice extracted from AD mice implanted with L/L-UIs (see fig. S30). The results revealed a well-aligned distribution of neural cells, providing compelling evidence that L/L-UIs exhibited high biocompatibility and had no discernible detrimental effects on the integrity of live brains. Moreover, major organ slices extracted from rats implanted with L/L-UIs showed no obvious pathological changes (fig. S31), indicating good biocompatibility and suitability for long-term monitoring in vivo. Consequently, the amperometric-type electrochemical biosensor using L/L-UIs supported at the L/L interface successfully assessed Cl− levels in live rat brains with AD and epilepsy models.

Dynamic monitoring Cl− regulation in AD model

Under normal circumstances, Cl− are regulated by ion channels, such as the KCC2 channel, to maintain a concentration difference between the inside and outside of neurons (42, 43). However, in certain neurodegenerative diseases such as AD and epilepsy, the function of GABABRs, which can regulate the function of the KCC2 channel, might become abnormal. Specifically, under normal conditions, GABABRs facilitate the outward flow of Cl− by regulating the function of the KCC2 channel, thus maintaining a normal excitatory-inhibitory balance (44, 45). However, in neurodegenerative diseases, the abnormal function of GABABRs in their activated state can lead to dysregulation of the KCC2 channel in its inhibitory state, resulting in an accumulation of intracellular chloride concentration ([Cl−]i) in neurons (46, 47). As a GABABRs antagonist, CGP 35348 can inhibit the function of GABABRs, potentially interfering with Cl− regulation. Previous studies have explored the use of CGP 35348 in neurodegenerative disease models and found that it may have effects on improving cognitive function and alleviating disease pathology (48, 49). However, further investigation to verify the specific pathway through which CGP 35348 regulates Cl− level in neurodegenerative diseases is still needed, which is important for the treatment of related disorders. Therefore, we choose AD disease as a pathological model for our research.

The in vivo long-term stability of our developed L/L sensing platform, the L/L-UI, should be investigated before they could be used for long-term and real-time monitoring of Cl− regulation. As shown in fig. S32, no obvious fluctuation or drift of the currents of the L/L-UI were observed during the continuous monitoring for 3.5 hours. Subsequently, this L/L-UI was transferred to an in vitro electrochemical cell 5, and the results indicated that it could still sensitively distinguish the test solutions with Cl− concentrations of 20 and 100 mM (as shown in fig. S33). Benefiting from the improved mechanical stability of the L/L ultramicrointerfaces after immobilization by PVC gel, L/L-UIs showed great potential for long-term monitoring of Cl− in vivo. Thus, the L/L-UIs were expanded to dynamically track the Cl− level in mice brain with an AD model followed by CGP 35348 injection, which has never been reported.

In this experiment, 500 μM CGP 35348 (5 μl min−1 for 60 s) was exogenously infused into the mice hippocampus to inhibit the GABABRs, and the L/L-UIs were implanted nearby this injection site to in situ track the dynamics of Cl−. The change in electrochemical signals recorded by the L/L-UIs in the hippocampus after CGP 35348 injection was monitored by chronoamperometry to record the dynamics change of Cl−. As shown in Fig. 7A (top), with −0.15 V applied potential, a prompt decrease in the current response was recorded with the L/L-UIs after the local microinfusion of CGP 35348 and reached a plateau at 100 s. However, for the control experiment, negligible variation was obtained (Fig. 7A, middle), further confirming the chloride ion effusion caused by CGP 35348–induced Cl− regulation. Moreover, when the L/L-UIs were applied to +0.05 V, a potential where Cl− cannot be facilitated transfer at the L/L interface, no obvious current was recorded (Fig. 7A, bottom), further verifying the monitored response was indeed from the Cl− transfer.

We subsequently verified the above electrochemical results with immunofluorescence staining analysis using confocal laser scanning microscopy to verify the pathway of CGP 35348 regulated Cl− level (Fig. 7, B and C). KCC2, GABABRs, and nuclei were respectively labeled for immunofluorescence experiments with the secondary antibodies coupled to green, red, and blue fluorophores, respectively. As depicted in Fig. 7B, after being treated with CGP 35348, a notable decline (78.8%) of red area density (GABABRs) was recorded contrast to the control trail, while green area density (KCC2) was apparently increased (66.8%), revealing the inhibitory effect of GABABRs and activated expression of KCC2, demonstrating the efflux of Cl− highly associated with the increase in blocker of GABABRs, further supporting the GABABRs-mediated chloride ion influx/efflux pathway by regulation of the KCC2 (Fig. 7D). The present study revealed that GABABRs could be disordered in the AD brain, which leads to serious neuronal damage, and such GABABRs antagonist like CGP35348 can alleviate this damage through the CGP 35348–induced GABABRs/KCC2 regulation pathway to further protect neuronal cells effectively.

DISCUSSION

In summary, we demonstrate the excellent Cl− recognition capabilities of bis-thioureas (IPECl−1) as ionophores for the fabrication of L/L-UIs at the L/L interface, resulting in the development of an amperometric Cl− electrochemical platform. The interfacial complexation-dissociation process was verified by thermodynamic characterization and CV confirmed a 1:1 stoichiometry complex, as verified by EC-MS. In comparison to prevailing Cl− detection methodologies, including liquid chromatography, fluorescence spectroscopy, colorimetry, and electrochemical techniques based on solid electrode/solution interfaces (table S2) (50–61), the L/L-UIs based on FIT reactions at a soft L/L ultramicrointerface enabled us to in situ, highly selectively and dynamically monitor of Cl− in living rodent models. The remarkable electrochemical properties displayed by the L/L-UIs allowed for dynamics tracking of Cl− within specific brain regions of AD and epilepsy disease models, thereby enabling direct detection of inhibitory neural signals and facilitating the evaluation of brain processes at the molecular level. Moreover, we confirmed the GABABRs-mediated chloride ion influx/effusion pathway. L/L interface tailored with highly selective ionophores for amperometric ion sensing is a promising advanced and reliable sensing methodology that can potentially be extended to other important electrochemically inactive ions or ionized biomolecules in vivo.

The method developed based on the L/L-UI holds substantial value for neuroscience research. It could help deepen the understanding of inhibitory neurotransmission mechanisms, reveal the relation between Cl− abnormalities and neurological disorders (such as epilepsy and depression), and provide crucial data for optimizing neuromodulation techniques. By monitoring how external stimuli like electrical stimulation or optogenetics affect dynamic fluctuation of Cl− level, the platform can enhance the precision and efficacy of neuromodulatory therapies, such as deep brain stimulation. In addition, it facilitates precise mapping of neural circuits and introduces a high-resolution research method that could capture subtle neural activities not easily detected by traditional approaches, advancing both the diagnosis and treatment of neurological diseases.

Although the method based on L/L-UI offers in vivo dynamic tracking electrochemically inactive Cl− and substantial benefits in neuroscience research, our work still has some limitations. In the future, it is anticipated that array electrodes with many specific ion sensors will be capable of high-throughput real-time monitoring of multiple ions, thereby capturing the dynamic changes associated with various neural activities. This advancement necessitates not only innovative material and sensor designs but also the integration of AI tools for comprehensive data analysis and interpretation. Concurrently, the incorporation of robotic technology can substantially mitigate human errors during surgical procedures and ensure precise L/L-UI positioning within the brain. Future research endeavors also would focus on the integration and miniaturization of the monitoring system to facilitate long-term, precise wireless tracking of electrochemically inactive ions in vivo.

Theoretical analysis

All the calculations were performed using the Gaussian 16 package. The geometry optimizations were carried out in DCE solvent (ε = 10.125) using the solvation model density (SMD) (64) model at the M062X (65) level of theory with a def2-SVP (66) basis set for all atoms. Frequencies were computed analytically at the same level of theory to confirm whether the structures are minima (no imaginary frequencies) or transition states (only one imaginary frequency). Key transition-state structures were confirmed to connect the correct reactants and products by intrinsic reaction coordinate calculations (67, 68). To obtain better accuracy, energies for the optimized geometries were recalculated using M062X single-point calculations with a larger basis set, which is def2-TZVP (69, 70) basis set for all other atoms. The final free energies reported in the article (ΔGsol) are the large basis set single-point energies with Gibbs free energy correction (at 298.15 K).

Fabrication of agar-gel hybrid ultramicroelectrodes

Dual-barrel micropipettes were made from borosilicate glass capillaries (outer diameter, 1.5 mm; length, 10 cm; Sutter Instrument Co., USA) based on a P-2000 CO2 laser–based puller (Sutter Instrument Co.). The pulling program was set as follows: HEAT 550, FIL 4, VEL 30, DEL 135, and PULL 20. To make the outer surface lipophilic, the outer wall and the thin band of the dual-barrel micropipettes were silanized (71). Agar-gel hybrid ultramicroelectrodes were fabricated using the method reported previously (72). The aqueous solutions were added with agar (2%, w/w) and then heated to about 100°C until the agar dissolved and the gel became transparent. The hot gel solution was backfilled into one channel of the micropipettes using a small syringe. After cooling down to the room temperature (22° ± 2°C), the micropipettes were examined with the optical microscope to ensure that no air bubbles were inside the micropipettes. Then, another channel of the silanized micropipettes was injected with an organic phase, which would cover the outer surface and have a nice contact with the gel phase.

Fabrication of the L/L-UIs

Micropipettes were made from borosilicate glass capillaries BF150-75-10 (outer diameter = 1.5 mm, inner diameter = 0.86 mm, and length = 10 cm, Sutter Instrument Co., Novato, CA) based on a P-97 puller (Sutter Instrument Co.). The pulling program was set as follows: HEAT 550, PULL 0, VEL 15, and TIME 200. The micropipettes were characterized by CV and SEM to obtain the radius of the tip (fig. S16). The radius can be calculated by the steady-state diffusion limiting current (iss) (62)iss=3.35πnFDCr(7)where n is the transferred charge, F is the Faraday constant, and D and C are the diffusion constant (5.2 × 10−6 cm2/s) (73) and the bulk concentration of DB18C6. The radius of micropipette was about 1.7 μm obtained from the CV, consistent with the SEM image.

The L/L-UIs were fabricated based on micropipettes using the method, which was similar to the previous report and the amount of PVC was a consideration of gelation time (74). The organogel was prepared by adding PVC in DCE solution (10%, m/v) and heated to about 80°C until the PVC dissolved. Using a small syringe, the hot organogel was backfilled into the micropipettes. After cooling down to the room temperature (22° ± 2°C), the micropipettes were examined with the optical microscope to ensure that no air bubbles were inside the micropipettes. The morphology of the L/L-UIs was characterized by the ESEM.

In vivo experiments

The in vivo experimental protocols were approved by Animal Care and Use Committee of State Key Laboratory of Biochemical Engineering, Institute of Process Engineering, Chinese Academy of Sciences, Beijing, China (no. IPEAECA2023036 and IPEAECA2023121). The male C57BL mice, AD model mice (26 to 30 g), and adult male Sprague-Dawley rats (240 to 280 g) were purchased from SPF (Beijing) Biotechnology Co. Ltd. The epilepsy rat models were prepared according to the previous literatures (75, 76). In the in vivo experiments of mice, the mouse was anesthetized using chloral hydrate (25 mg ml−1) with an initial dose of 500 mg kg−1 (i.p.). When anesthetized and stunned, the mouse was fixed on the stereotaxic frame (Shenzhen Rwdls Biotechnology Co. Ltd). Then, the mouse was pushed off the hair, cut off the scalp, and drilled for implantation of electrodes. According to standard stereotaxic operations, the L/L-UIs was implanted in the cortex [anteroposterior (AP) = 2.0 mm from bregma, mediolateral (L) = 1.2 mm, and dorsoventral (V) = 1.5 mm below dura], the left striatum (AP = 0.5 mm anterior to bregma, L = 2.0 mm, and V = 3.0 mm below dura), and the hippocampus (AP = 2.3 mm from bregma, L = 2.1 mm, and V = 1.0 mm below dura). The Ag/AgCl reference electrode was implanted near the working electrode. The L/L-UIs equipped with a microinjected capillary (75 μm inner diameter and 150 μm outer diameter) was parallelly co-implanted into the hippocampus (AP = 2.3 mm from bregma, L = 2.1 mm, and V = 1.0 mm below dura). The microinjection of artificial cerebrospinal fluid (pH 7.4) or CGP 35348 solution at a flow rate of 5 μl min−1 was administered by a programmable syringe pump with gas-impermeable syringes. To evaluate the accuracy of the proposed method, the microdialysate from the hippocampus of the mouse brain was collected according to previous procedure (77).

For the in vivo experiments of rats, the L/L-UIs was implanted in the cortex (AP = 0.2 mm from bregma, L = 5.6 mm, and V = 2.0 mm below dura), the left striatum (AP = 2.0 mm anterior to bregma, L = 2.5 mm, and V = 6.0 mm below dura), and the hippocampus (AP = 5.0 mm from bregma, L = 5.0 mm, and V = 1.5 mm below dura). All electrochemical experiments were obtained and collected by CHI 760e electrochemical workstation. During the whole operation, additional doses of 100 mg kg−1 (i.p.) chloral hydrate solution was injected as needed, and the body temperature of the rats was always maintained at 37°C with a homothermic blanket.

Statistical analysis

Experimental data are presented as means ± SD, and all experiments were conducted with at least three parallel samples. All statistical analyses were performed with the IBM SPSS statistics version 25. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to inspect the normality and homogeneity of variance of all the data. Statistical significance of all comparison results obtained in this study was determined by performing two-tailed unpaired Student’s t test for two-group comparisons and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by a Tukey post hoc analysis for multiple-group comparisons, with the significance threshold of *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001.
