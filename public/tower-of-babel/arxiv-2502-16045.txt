Sharp Lower Bounds for Dyadic Square Functions of indicator functions of sets
Natanael Alpay, Paata Ivanisvili

Abstract
. We study lower bounds for dyadic square functions of indicator functions. Remark 1.13
.

Although we formulate our results for Borel sets, all statements extend verbatim to arbitrary Lebesgue measurable sets. Indeed, for every Lebesgue measurable set

The purpose of the next section, which also served as the original motivation for this project, is to explore the parallels and distinctions between lower bounds for discrete gradients of Boolean functions on the hypercube (the so-called
edge isoperimetric inequalities

1.1.
Discrete gradient on the hypercube

Another way to model dyadic martingales is through the hypercube. Let
n\geq 1
, and consider the
n
-dimensional hypercube
\{-1,1\}^{n}
equipped with uniform probability measure. Let
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
be independent identically distributed Bernoulli symmetric
\pm 1
random variables. Set
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
. For any
f:\{-1,1\}^{n}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
, the sequence

\displaystyle f_{0}=\mathbb{E}f(x);

\displaystyle f_{1}(\varepsilon_{1})=\mathbb{E}(f(x)|x_{1}=\varepsilon_{1});

\displaystyle f_{2}(\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2})=\mathbb{E}(f(x)|x_{1}=\varepsilon_{1},x_{2}=\varepsilon_{2});

\displaystyle\ldots

\displaystyle f_{n}(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n})=\mathbb{E}(f(x)|x_{1}=\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,x_{n}=\varepsilon_{n})=f(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n}).

defines a dyadic martingale
\{f_{k}\}_{k=0}^{n}
. Notice that here

\displaystyle f_{0}=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}=\pm 1}f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}),

\displaystyle f_{1}(\varepsilon_{1})=\frac{1}{2^{n-1}}\sum_{x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}=\pm 1}f(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}),

\displaystyle\ldots

\displaystyle f_{n-1}(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n-1})=\frac{f(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n-1},1)+f(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n-1},-1)}{2}.

Next, we define discrete derivatives. For each
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
we set

\displaystyle D_{j}f(x)=\frac{f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{j-1},x_{j},x_{j+1},\ldots,x_{n})-f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{j-1},-x_{j},x_{j+1},\ldots,x_{n})}{2}.

Let
\nabla f(x)=(D_{1}f(x),\ldots,D_{n}f(x))
, and for each
\beta\geq 1
set
\beta
- gradient to be defined as

\displaystyle|\nabla f|_{\beta}(x)=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}|D_{j}f(x)|^{\beta}\right)^{1/\beta}.

In general the functions
S_{\beta}(f)
and
|\nabla f|_{\beta}
are not comparable. One may ask: how different are they? Let us make a couple of observations.

1.

If
f
is boolean, i.e.,
f(x)=\mathbbm{1}_{A}
for some
A\subset\{-1,1\}^{n}
then
|\nabla f|_{\beta}
equals to
(|\nabla f|_{1})^{1/\beta}
up to a constant factor. Indeed, since
|2D_{j}f|\in\{0,1\}
, we have

\displaystyle|\nabla f|_{\beta}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}|2D_{j}f|\right)^{1/\beta}=2^{\frac{1}{\beta}-1}(|\nabla f|_{1})^{1/\beta}.

Therefore for boolean
f
we always have

(1.9)

\displaystyle\|\,|\nabla f|_{\beta}\,\|_{p}=2^{\frac{1}{\beta}-1}\|\,|\nabla f|_{1}\,\|_{p/\beta}^{1/\beta}.

The identity (
1.9
) shows that studying lower bounds on
\|\,|\nabla f|_{\beta}\,\|_{p}
is the same as studying lower bounds on
\|\,|\nabla f|_{1}\,\|_{q}
with
q=p/\beta
.

2.

Talagrand’s isoperimetric inequality with “two-sided gradient” says that there exists a universal constant
C>0
such that for any
A\subset\{-1,1\}^{n}
we have

(1.10)

\displaystyle\||\nabla\mathbbm{1}_{A}|_{1}\|_{q}\geq C(|A|^{*})^{1/q}\log\frac{1}{|A|^{*}}

holds for all
q\geq 1/2
. The estimate (
1.10
) is sharp up to a universal constant factor
C
. Moreover, as soon as

there is no lower bound on
\||\nabla f|_{1}\|_{q}
in terms of
|A|
independent of

(see
[
3
]
and references therein). See
[
9
]
about the state of the art of the best constant

in (

Notice that when

the left-hand side in (
1.10

A
and
A^{c}
, and it gives the lower bound of the edge boundary of

in terms of the size of

[

Combining (

2p\geq\beta\geq 1

\displaystyle\|\,|\nabla\mathbbm{1}_{A}|_{\beta}\,\|_{p}\geq C(|A|^{*})^{1/p}\left(\log\frac{1}{|A|^{*}}\right)^{1/\beta}.

The reader should compare (

obtained in Theorems

and

3.

We have

\displaystyle(S_{\beta}f(x))^{\beta}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}|f_{k}-f_{k-1}|^{\beta}=|\mathbb{E}(D_{1}f(x)|x_{1})|^{\beta}

\displaystyle+|\mathbb{E}(D_{2}f(x)|x_{1},x_{2})|^{\beta}+\ldots+|\mathbb{E}(D_{n}f(x)|x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})|^{\beta}.

Thus we obtain

\displaystyle\||\nabla\mathbbm{1}_{A}|_{1}\|_{1}\geq\|S_{1}(\mathbbm{1}_{A})\|_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{Theorem\penalty\ \ref{mth02}}}}{{\geq}}B_{1,1}(|A|)

However, the equality cases in the edge-isoperimetric inequality (see

, we have

(2.13)

Q\!\left(\int_{0}^{1}f\right)\leq\int_{0}^{1}S_{1,N}(f)+\int_{0}^{1}Q(f_{N}).

For
N=0
, we have
f_{0}=\int_{0}^{1}f
, so (
2.13
) is
immediate.
Assume now that (
2.13
) holds for some
N\geq 0
.
Fix a dyadic interval
I\in\mathcal{D}_{N}
, and let
I^{-},I^{+}
be its two dyadic
children. Write

a=\langle f\rangle_{I^{-}},\qquad b=\langle f\rangle_{I^{+}}.

Then

f_{N}|_{I}=\frac{a+b}{2},\qquad f_{N+1}|_{I^{-}}=a,\qquad f_{N+1}|_{I^{+}}=b.

Applying (
2.12
) to
a
and
b
, we obtain

Q\!\left(\frac{a+b}{2}\right)\leq\frac{Q(a)+Q(b)}{2}+\left|\frac{a-b}{2}\right|.

Multiplying by
|I|
gives

\int_{I}Q(f_{N})\leq\int_{I}Q(f_{N+1})+|I|\left|\frac{a-b}{2}\right|.

On the other hand, since
f_{N+1}-f_{N}
is constant on each child of
I
,

\int_{I}|f_{N+1}-f_{N}|=\frac{|I|}{2}\left|a-\frac{a+b}{2}\right|+\frac{|I|}{2}\left|b-\frac{a+b}{2}\right|=|I|\left|\frac{a-b}{2}\right|.

Hence

\int_{I}Q(f_{N})\leq\int_{I}Q(f_{N+1})+\int_{I}|f_{N+1}-f_{N}|.

Summing over all
I\in\mathcal{D}_{N}
, we get

\int_{0}^{1}Q(f_{N})\leq\int_{0}^{1}Q(f_{N+1})+\int_{0}^{1}|f_{N+1}-f_{N}|.

Combining this with the induction hypothesis yields

\displaystyle Q\!\left(\int_{0}^{1}f\right)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}S_{1,N}(f)+\int_{0}^{1}Q(f_{N})

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}S_{1,N}(f)+\int_{0}^{1}|f_{N+1}-f_{N}|+\int_{0}^{1}Q(f_{N+1})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}S_{1,N+1}(f)+\int_{0}^{1}Q(f_{N+1}),

which proves (
2.13
) for
N+1
.
Now let
N\to\infty
. Since
f=\mathbbm{1}_{A}
, dyadic martingale convergence
gives

f_{N}\to f\qquad\text{a.e. on }{[0,1)}.

Because
f\in\{0,1\}
a.e. and
Q(0)=Q(1)=0
, we have

Q(f_{N})\to 0\qquad\text{a.e. on }{[0,1)}.

Since
Q
is continuous on
[0,1]
, it is bounded, so dominated convergence
implies

\int_{0}^{1}Q(f_{N})\to 0.

Also,
S_{1,N}(f)\uparrow S_{1}(f)
pointwise, so by monotone convergence,

\int_{0}^{1}S_{1,N}(f)\to\int_{0}^{1}S_{1}(f).

Passing to the limit in (
2.13
), we obtain

Q(|A|)=Q\!\left(\int_{0}^{1}f\right)\leq\int_{0}^{1}S_{1}(f)=\|S_{1}(\mathbbm{1}_{A})\|_{1}.

This proves the lemma.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.8
.

Applying Lemma
2.1
with
Q=B_{1,1}
, we obtain

(2.14)

\|S_{1}(\mathbbm{1}_{A})\|_{1}\geq B_{1,1}(|A|)

for every Borel set
A\subset{[0,1)}
.
We next compute the square function explicitly for an interval. Fix

x\in[0,1]
, and set

A=[0,x),\qquad f=\mathbbm{1}_{[0,x)}.

For each
n\geq 1
, let

x\,2^{n-1}=m+r,\qquad m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},\quad r\in[0,1).

If
r=0
, then
x
is an endpoint of some dyadic interval of level
n-1
,
so
f
is constant on each such interval and therefore

d_{n}=0.

Assume now that
0<r<1
. Then for dyadic intervals of level
n-1
on which

f
is constant contribute nothing to
d_{n}
, and the only possible nonzero
contribution comes from the unique dyadic interval

I=\left[\frac{m}{2^{n-1}},\frac{m+1}{2^{n-1}}\right)

whose interior contains
x
.
Let
I^{-},I^{+}
be the two dyadic children of
I
.

If
0<r\leq\frac{1}{2}
, then

\langle f\rangle_{I^{-}}=2r,\qquad\langle f\rangle_{I^{+}}=0,\qquad\langle f\rangle_{I}=r.

Hence

\int_{I}|d_{n}|=\frac{|I|}{2}\,|2r-r|+\frac{|I|}{2}\,|0-r|=|I|\,r=\frac{r}{2^{n-1}}.

If
\frac{1}{2}<r<1
, then

\langle f\rangle_{I^{-}}=1,\qquad\langle f\rangle_{I^{+}}=2r-1,\qquad\langle f\rangle_{I}=r.

Therefore

\int_{I}|d_{n}|=\frac{|I|}{2}\,|1-r|+\frac{|I|}{2}\,|2r-1-r|=|I|\,(1-r)=\frac{1-r}{2^{n-1}}.

Combining the two cases, we obtain

\|d_{n}\|_{1}=\frac{\min\{r,1-r\}}{2^{n-1}}=\frac{\operatorname{dist}(2^{n-1}x,\mathbb{Z})}{2^{n-1}},\qquad n\geq 1.

Thus, by monotone convergence,

\displaystyle\|S_{1}(\mathbbm{1}_{[0,x)})\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|d_{n}|=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\|d_{n}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\operatorname{dist}(2^{n-1}x,\mathbb{Z})}{2^{n-1}}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{\operatorname{dist}(2^{m}x,\mathbb{Z})}{2^{m}}=T(x).

Hence

\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\subset{[0,1)}\ \mathrm{Borel}\\
|A|=x\end{subarray}}\|S_{1}(\mathbbm{1}_{A})\|_{1}\leq\|S_{1}(\mathbbm{1}_{[0,x)})\|_{1}=T(x).

On the other hand, taking the infimum in (
2.14
), we get

\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\subset{[0,1)}\ \mathrm{Borel}\\
|A|=x\end{subarray}}\|S_{1}(\mathbbm{1}_{A})\|_{1}\geq B_{1,1}(x).

Since
B_{1,1}(x)=T(x)
on
[0,1]
by
[
17
, Theorem 7]
, we conclude that

\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\subset{[0,1)}\ \mathrm{Borel}\\
|A|=x\end{subarray}}\|S_{1}(\mathbbm{1}_{A})\|_{1}=B_{1,1}(x)=T(x),

which proves the theorem.
∎

Proof of Proposition
1.10
.

Using the Takagi representation (
1.8
)
and the symmetry
T(x)=T(1-x)
, it is enough to consider

u:=x^{*}\in(0,1/2].

Set

N:=\left\lfloor\log_{2}\frac{1}{u}\right\rfloor\implies 2^{-N-1}<u\leq 2^{-N}.

For
0\leq n\leq N-1
we have
2^{n}u\leq 1/2
, and so

\operatorname{dist}(2^{n}u,\mathbb{Z})=2^{n}u.

Thus

\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\frac{\operatorname{dist}(2^{n}u,\mathbb{Z})}{2^{n}}=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}u=Nu.

For the tail, since
\operatorname{dist}(t,\mathbb{Z})\leq 1/2
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}\frac{\operatorname{dist}(2^{n}u,\mathbb{Z})}{2^{n}}\leq\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}\frac{1/2}{2^{n}}=2^{-N}\leq 2u.

Therefore

Nu\leq T(u)\leq Nu+2u.

Since
u=x^{*}
, and

N\leq\log_{2}\frac{1}{u}<N+1.

We get

T(x^{*})=x^{*}\log_{2}\frac{1}{x^{*}}+O(x^{*}).

This finishes the proof of Proposition
1.10
.
∎

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.4
and Remark
1.6

In this section we prove Theorem
1.4
. Thus, for every pair
(\alpha,\beta)
with
\beta\geq 1\geq\alpha>0
, we will show that
\|S_{\beta}(\mathbbm{1}_{A})\|_{\alpha}\geq B_{\alpha,\beta}(|A|)
for every Borel set
A\subset{[0,1)}
. We will also prove
Remark
1.6
under the standing continuity assumption on the
supersolution.

Recall that for
f\in{L^{1}([0,1))}
we define
f_{n}=\mathbb{E}(f\mid\mathcal{F}_{n})
and
d_{n}=f_{n}-f_{n-1}
, and for
N\geq 1
,

S_{\beta}(f)=\lim_{N\to\infty}S_{\beta,N}(f)\quad\text{where}\quad S_{\beta,N}(f)=\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}|d_{n}|^{\beta}\right)^{1/\beta},.

We begin with the finite-level Bellman iteration.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
Q,M:[0,1]\times[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
be Borel measurable
functions such that

(3.15)

Q\!\left(p+a,\,(|a|^{\beta}+q^{\beta})^{1/\beta}\right)+Q\!\left(p-a,\,(|a|^{\beta}+q^{\beta})^{1/\beta}\right)\geq 2Q(p,q)

for all
p\in[0,1]
, all
q\geq 0
, and all
a\in\mathbb{R}
with

p\pm a\in[0,1]
. Assume also that

Q(p,q)\leq M(p,q)\qquad\text{for all }p\in[0,1],\ q\geq 0.

Then for every
N\geq 1
and every
\mathcal{F}_{N}
-measurable function

f:[0,1]\to[0,1]
,

(3.16)

Q\!\left(\int_{0}^{1}f,\ q\right)\leq\int_{0}^{1}M\!\left(f,\,(S_{\beta,N}(f)^{\beta}+q^{\beta})^{1/\beta}\right).

Proof.

For
0\leq n\leq N
, define

\Phi_{n}=\sum_{I\in\mathcal{D}_{n}}|I|\,Q\!\left(\langle f\rangle_{I},\ q_{I}^{(n)}\right),

where

q_{I}^{(n)}=\left(q^{\beta}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}|d_{k}|_{I}^{\beta}\right)^{1/\beta},

and
|d_{k}|_{I}
denotes the constant value of
|d_{k}|
on
I
. This is
well-defined because
d_{k}
is
\mathcal{F}_{k}
-measurable and hence is
constant on every
I\in\mathcal{D}_{n}
whenever
k\leq n
.

For
n=0
we have

\Phi_{0}=Q\!\left(\int_{0}^{1}f,\ q\right).

We claim that

\Phi_{n-1}\leq\Phi_{n}\qquad\text{for every }1\leq n\leq N.

Fix
I\in\mathcal{D}_{n-1}
, and write
I^{-},I^{+}
for its two dyadic
children. Set

p_{I}=\langle f\rangle_{I},\qquad a_{I}=\frac{\langle f\rangle_{I^{+}}-\langle f\rangle_{I^{-}}}{2}.

Then

\langle f\rangle_{I^{-}}=p_{I}-a_{I},\qquad\langle f\rangle_{I^{+}}=p_{I}+a_{I}.

Moreover, on
I^{-}
and
I^{+}
we have

d_{n}=-a_{I}\quad\text{and}\quad d_{n}=a_{I},

respectively. Hence

q_{I^{-}}^{(n)}=q_{I^{+}}^{(n)}=\left((q_{I}^{(n-1)})^{\beta}+|a_{I}|^{\beta}\right)^{1/\beta}.

Applying (
3.15
) with
p=p_{I}
,
a=a_{I}
, and
q=q_{I}^{(n-1)}
, we obtain

\displaystyle|I|\,Q(p_{I},q_{I}^{(n-1)})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|I|}{2}\,Q\!\left(p_{I}-a_{I},\ q_{I^{-}}^{(n)}\right)+\frac{|I|}{2}\,Q\!\left(p_{I}+a_{I},\ q_{I^{+}}^{(n)}\right)

\displaystyle=|I^{-}|\,Q\!\left(\langle f\rangle_{I^{-}},q_{I^{-}}^{(n)}\right)+|I^{+}|\,Q\!\left(\langle f\rangle_{I^{+}},q_{I^{+}}^{(n)}\right).

Summing over all
I\in\mathcal{D}_{n-1}
gives
\Phi_{n-1}\leq\Phi_{n}
.
Therefore

Q\!\left(\int_{0}^{1}f,\ q\right)=\Phi_{0}\leq\Phi_{N}.

Since
f
is
\mathcal{F}_{N}
-measurable, it is constant on each

J\in\mathcal{D}_{N}
, so
\langle f\rangle_{J}=f|_{J}
. Also,

q_{J}^{(N)}=\left(S_{\beta,N}(f)^{\beta}+q^{\beta}\right)^{1/\beta}\quad\text{on }J.

Thus, using
Q\leq M
,

\displaystyle\Phi_{N}

\displaystyle=\sum_{J\in\mathcal{D}_{N}}|J|\,Q\!\left(f|_{J},\ (S_{\beta,N}(f)^{\beta}+q^{\beta})^{1/\beta}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{J\in\mathcal{D}_{N}}|J|\,M\!\left(f|_{J},\ (S_{\beta,N}(f)^{\beta}+q^{\beta})^{1/\beta}\right)=\int_{0}^{1}M\!\left(f,\ (S_{\beta,N}(f)^{\beta}+q^{\beta})^{1/\beta}\right).

This proves (
3.16
).
∎

We now verify that the Bellman candidate (
3.17
) satisfies the required three-point inequality.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\beta\geq 1\geq\alpha>0
, and let
B:[0,1]\to[0,\infty)
satisfy the
two-point inequality (
1.3
) and the boundary condition

B(0)=B(1)=0.

Define

(3.17)

Q_{B}(p,q)=\left(B(p)^{\beta}+q^{\beta}\right)^{\alpha/\beta}.

Then
Q_{B}
satisfies the three-point inequality (
3.15
). Moreover,

Q_{B}(0,q)=Q_{B}(1,q)=q^{\alpha}\qquad\text{for all }q\geq 0.

Proof.

The endpoint identities are immediate from
B(0)=B(1)=0
.
Set

r=\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\in(0,1].

Fix
p\in[0,1]
,
q\geq 0
, and
a\in\mathbb{R}
such that
p\pm a\in[0,1]
.
Let

u=B(p+a)^{\beta}+|a|^{\beta},\qquad v=B(p-a)^{\beta}+|a|^{\beta}.

By the two-point inequality (
1.3
), written with
x=p+a
and

y=p-a
, we have

\frac{u^{r}+v^{r}}{2}\geq B(p)^{\alpha}.

If
r=1
, then
\alpha=\beta
, and therefore

\frac{u+v}{2}\geq B(p)^{\beta}.

Adding
q^{\beta}
to both sides yields

\frac{(u+q^{\beta})+(v+q^{\beta})}{2}\geq B(p)^{\beta}+q^{\beta},

which is exactly (
3.15
) for
Q_{B}
.

Assume now that
0<r<1
. Define

\psi(t)=\left(\frac{(u+t)^{r}+(v+t)^{r}}{2}\right)^{1/r}-t,\qquad t\geq 0.

We claim that
\psi
is nondecreasing on
[0,\infty)
.
For
t>0
, let
Y
be the random variable taking the values
u+t
and

v+t
with probability
1/2
. Then

\psi(t)=\left(\mathbb{E}Y^{r}\right)^{1/r}-t,

and differentiation gives

\psi^{\prime}(t)=\left(\mathbb{E}Y^{r}\right)^{1/r-1}\mathbb{E}Y^{r-1}-1.

Since
Y>0
almost surely and the power means are monotone in the exponent,

\left(\mathbb{E}Y^{r}\right)^{1/r}\geq\left(\mathbb{E}Y^{r-1}\right)^{1/(r-1)}.

Because
r-1<0
, raising both sides to the power
r-1
reverses the
inequality, and we obtain

\left(\mathbb{E}Y^{r}\right)^{(r-1)/r}\leq\mathbb{E}Y^{r-1}.

Therefore

\left(\mathbb{E}Y^{r}\right)^{1/r-1}\mathbb{E}Y^{r-1}\geq 1,

so
\psi^{\prime}(t)\geq 0
for all
t>0
. Hence
\psi
is nondecreasing on

[0,\infty)
.
Taking
t=q^{\beta}
and using the inequality at
t=0
, we get

\psi(q^{\beta})\geq\psi(0)\geq B(p)^{\beta}.

That is,

\left(\frac{(u+q^{\beta})^{r}+(v+q^{\beta})^{r}}{2}\right)^{1/r}\geq B(p)^{\beta}+q^{\beta}.

Raising both sides to the power
r
yields

\frac{(u+q^{\beta})^{r}+(v+q^{\beta})^{r}}{2}\geq\left(B(p)^{\beta}+q^{\beta}\right)^{r},

which is precisely (
3.15
) for
Q_{B}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.4
and Remark
1.6
in the continuous case.

Let
B:[0,1]\to[0,\infty)
be continuous, satisfy the two-point
inequality (
1.3
), and satisfy

B(0)=B(1)=0.

Define

Q_{B}(p,q)=\left(B(p)^{\beta}+q^{\beta}\right)^{\alpha/\beta}.

By Lemma
3.2
, the function
Q_{B}
satisfies the three-point
inequality (
3.15
).

Let
A\subset{[0,1)}
be Borel, and set

f=\mathbbm{1}_{A},\qquad f_{N}=\mathbb{E}(f\mid\mathcal{F}_{N}).

Then
f_{N}
is
\mathcal{F}_{N}
-measurable,
0\leq f_{N}\leq 1
, and

\int_{0}^{1}f_{N}=\int_{0}^{1}f=|A|.

Also, the first
N
martingale differences of
f_{N}
agree with those of

f
, and therefore

S_{\beta,N}(f_{N})=S_{\beta,N}(f).

Applying Lemma
3.1
with
Q=M=Q_{B}
and
q=0
, we obtain

\displaystyle B(|A|)^{\alpha}=Q_{B}(|A|,0)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}Q_{B}\!\left(f_{N},S_{\beta,N}(f)\right)=\int_{0}^{1}\left(B(f_{N})^{\beta}+S_{\beta,N}(f)^{\beta}\right)^{\alpha/\beta}.

Since
0<\alpha/\beta\leq 1
, we have

(u+v)^{\alpha/\beta}\leq u^{\alpha/\beta}+v^{\alpha/\beta}\qquad\text{for all }u,v\geq 0.

Hence

B(|A|)^{\alpha}\leq\int_{0}^{1}B(f_{N})^{\alpha}+\int_{0}^{1}S_{\beta,N}(f)^{\alpha}.

Now
f_{N}\to f=\mathbbm{1}_{A}
almost everywhere, and since
0\leq f_{N}\leq 1
,

B
is continuous on
[0,1]
and vanishes at
0
and
1
, dominated
convergence gives

\lim_{N\to\infty}\int_{0}^{1}B(f_{N})^{\alpha}=0.

Also,
S_{\beta,N}(f)\uparrow S_{\beta}(f)
pointwise, so by monotone
convergence,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\int_{0}^{1}S_{\beta,N}(f)^{\alpha}=\int_{0}^{1}S_{\beta}(f)^{\alpha}=\|S_{\beta}(\mathbbm{1}_{A})\|_{\alpha}^{\alpha}.

Therefore

B(|A|)^{\alpha}\leq\|S_{\beta}(\mathbbm{1}_{A})\|_{\alpha}^{\alpha},

and hence

\|S_{\beta}(\mathbbm{1}_{A})\|_{\alpha}\geq B(|A|).

Taking
B=B_{\alpha,\beta}
proves Theorem
1.4
. The same
argument applies to every continuous supersolution

\widetilde{B}_{\alpha,\beta}
satisfying the two-point inequality
(
1.3
) and the boundary conditions

\widetilde{B}_{\alpha,\beta}(0)=\widetilde{B}_{\alpha,\beta}(1)=0.

This proves Remark
1.6
in the continuous case.
∎

4.
Proof of Theorem
1.1
and Proposition
1.2

In this section we complete the proof of Theorem
1.1
in the Borel-set setting. We continue to work with the dyadic filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{n})
on
[0,1)
, and for a Borel set
A\subset{[0,1)}
we define
S_{2}(\mathbbm{1}_{A})
through the dyadic martingale generated by
\mathbbm{1}_{A}
.

We will reuse the tree-iteration framework from Section
3
.
The difference in the present case is that our Bellman function only satisfies an endpoint obstacle condition, rather than a global obstacle condition (due to the fact that
f
is boolean).
Accordingly, we first show the following dyadic endpoint-obstacle variant of Lemma
3.1
.

Corollary 4.1
.

Let
Q:[0,1]\times[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
be a Borel measurable function satisfying

(4.18)

Q\!\left(p+a,\sqrt{q^{2}+a^{2}}\right)+Q\!\left(p-a,\sqrt{q^{2}+a^{2}}\right)\geq 2Q(p,q)

for all
p\in[0,1]
, all
q\geq 0
, and all
a\in\mathbb{R}
such that
p\pm a\in[0,1]
. Let
\phi:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
be a Borel measurable function satisfying

(4.19)

Q(0,q)\leq\phi(q^{2}),\qquad Q(1,q)\leq\phi(q^{2})\qquad\text{for all }q\geq 0.

Then for every
A\in\mathcal{D}
,

Q(|A|,0)\leq\int_{0}^{1}\phi\!\big(S_{2}(\mathbbm{1}_{A})^{2}\big).

Proof.

The proof is the same tree iteration as in Lemma
3.1
, with
f=\mathbbm{1}_{A}
.
Since
A\in\mathcal{D}
, there exists
N
such that
f
is
\mathcal{F}_{N}
-measurable.
Applying the proof of Lemma
3.1
with
\beta=2
and
q=0
, we obtain

Q(|A|,0)\leq\Phi_{N},

where
\Phi_{N}
is the terminal Bellman sum over dyadic atoms of level
N
.

At the terminal level
N
, the function
f
takes only the values
0
and
1
on dyadic atoms, and moreover
d_{n}=0
for all
n>N
, so

S_{2}(f)=S_{2,N}(f).

Thus, on each dyadic atom
J\in\mathcal{D}_{N}
, the endpoint obstacle condition (
4.19

Q\!\left(f|_{J},\ S_{2}(f)|_{J}\right)\leq\phi\!\left(S_{2}(f)|_{J}^{2}\right).

Summing over all such
J
, we obtain

\Phi_{N}\leq\int_{0}^{1}\phi\!\big(S_{2}(\mathbbm{1}_{A})^{2}\big),

and therefore

Q(|A|,0)\leq\int_{0}^{1}\phi\!\big(S_{2}(\mathbbm{1}_{A})^{2}\big),

as claimed.
∎

We now construct the Bellman function needed for the sharp lower bound.
Let
(B_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a standard Brownian motion started at
B_{0}=p\in[0,1]
, and

\tau:=\inf\{t\geq 0:\,B_{t}\notin(0,1)\}

be its first exit time from
(0,1)
.

Let
\phi:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
be concave, such that for every
r\in[0,1]
and
s\geq 0

(4.20)

\mathbb{E}_{r}\!\bigl[\,|\phi(s+\tau)|\,\bigr]<\infty.

Set

(4.21)

U_{\phi}(p,q):=\mathbb{E}_{p}\!\big[\phi(q^{2}+\tau)\big],\qquad p\in[0,1],\ q\geq 0.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\phi:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
be concave satisfing (
4.20
).
Let
U_{\phi}
be defined by (
4.21
). Then

U_{\phi}(0,q)=U_{\phi}(1,q)=\phi(q^{2}),\qquad q\geq 0,

and for every
p\in[0,1]
, every
q\geq 0
, and every
a\in\mathbb{R}
such that

p\pm a\in[0,1]
,

U_{\phi}\!\left(p+a,\sqrt{q^{2}+a^{2}}\right)+U_{\phi}\!\left(p-a,\sqrt{q^{2}+a^{2}}\right)\geq 2\,U_{\phi}(p,q).

Proof.

The boundary condition is immediate. Indeed for
p\in\{0,1\}
, then
\tau=0
almost
surely, hence

U_{\phi}(p,q)=\mathbb{E}_{p}[\phi(q^{2}+\tau)]=\phi(q^{2}).

To prove the inequality, note that it is invariant under
a\mapsto-a
, so it
is enough to consider
a\geq 0
. The case
a=0
is trivial, so assume
a>0
.

Define

\sigma:=\inf\{t\geq 0:\,|B_{t}-p|=a\},\quad A_{+}:=\{B_{\sigma}=p+a\},\quad A_{-}:=\{B_{\sigma}=p-a\}.

Because
p\pm a\in[0,1]
and Brownian paths are continuous, the process must hit
one of the points
p-a
or
p+a
before it can exit
(0,1)
. So
\sigma\leq\tau
a.s.

Define the martingale
M_{t}:=(B_{t}-p)^{2}-t
.
By optional stopping at
t\wedge\sigma
,

\mathbb{E}_{p}[M_{t\wedge\sigma}]=\mathbb{E}_{p}[M_{0}]=0,

so

\mathbb{E}_{p}[t\wedge\sigma]=\mathbb{E}_{p}[(B_{t\wedge\sigma}-p)^{2}].

Since
|B_{t\wedge\sigma}-p|\leq a
, the right-hand side is bounded by
a^{2}
.
Letting
t\to\infty
and using monotone convergence on the left and dominated
convergence on the right, we obtain

\mathbb{E}_{p}[\sigma]=\mathbb{E}_{p}[(B_{\sigma}-p)^{2}]=a^{2}.

Now we use the symmetry of Brownian motion when it first hits one of the two
points
p-a
or
p+a
. Under
\mathbb{P}_{p}
, the shifted process

X_{t}:=B_{t}-p

is standard Brownian motion starting at
0
. Brownian motion is symmetric, so

X_{t}
and
-X_{t}
have the same distribution. Also, replacing
X
by
-X
does
not change the stopping time

\sigma=\inf\{t\geq 0:\,|X_{t}|=a\},

because
\sigma
only depends on
|X_{t}|
. On the other hand, this reflection
swaps the two events of first hitting
p\pm a
. In particular, for every nonnegative measurable function

h
,

\mathbb{E}_{p}[h(\sigma)\mathbbm{1}_{A_{+}}]=\mathbb{E}_{p}[h(\sigma)\mathbbm{1}_{A_{-}}]=\frac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{p}[h(\sigma)].

By applying this identity to the positive and negative parts, the same formula
holds for every integrable function
h
.

For each
r\in[0,1]
, define

G_{r}(s):=\mathbb{E}_{r}[\phi(s+\tau)],\qquad H_{r}(s):=\mathbb{E}_{r}[|\phi(s+\tau)|],\qquad s\geq 0.

These are finite by assumption. Moreover,
G_{r}
is concave on
[0,\infty)
.
Indeed, if
s_{1},s_{2}\geq 0
and
\theta\in[0,1]
, then concavity of
\phi
gives

\phi\!\left(\theta(s_{1}+\tau)+(1-\theta)(s_{2}+\tau)\right)\geq\theta\,\phi(s_{1}+\tau)+(1-\theta)\,\phi(s_{2}+\tau)

almost surely. Taking
\mathbb{E}_{r}
yields

G_{r}(\theta s_{1}+(1-\theta)s_{2})\geq\theta G_{r}(s_{1})+(1-\theta)G_{r}(s_{2}).

By the strong Markov property at time
\sigma
,

\mathbb{E}_{p}\!\left[\,|\phi(q^{2}+\tau)|\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}\right]=H_{B_{\sigma}}(q^{2}+\sigma)=\mathbbm{1}_{A_{+}}H_{p+a}(q^{2}+\sigma)+\mathbbm{1}_{A_{-}}H_{p-a}(q^{2}+\sigma).

Taking expectations yields

\mathbb{E}_{p}\!\bigl[\mathbbm{1}_{A_{+}}H_{p+a}(q^{2}+\sigma)\bigr]+\mathbb{E}_{p}\!\bigl[\mathbbm{1}_{A_{-}}H_{p-a}(q^{2}+\sigma)\bigr]=\mathbb{E}_{p}\!\bigl[|\phi(q^{2}+\tau)|\bigr]<\infty.

Applying the reflection identity twice with

h(\sigma)=H_{p\pm a}(q^{2}+\sigma)
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}_{p}[H_{p\pm a}(q^{2}+\sigma)]<\infty.

Since
|G_{r}|\leq H_{r}
, it follows that

G_{p+a}(q^{2}+\sigma),\qquad G_{p-a}(q^{2}+\sigma)

are integrable.

Now apply the strong Markov property at time
\sigma
to
\phi(q^{2}+\tau)
,

\mathbb{E}_{p}\!\left[\,\phi(q^{2}+\tau)\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}\right]=G_{B_{\sigma}}(q^{2}+\sigma)=\mathbbm{1}_{A_{+}}G_{p+a}(q^{2}+\sigma)+\mathbbm{1}_{A_{-}}G_{p-a}(q^{2}+\sigma).

Taking expectations and using the reflection identity for the integrable
functions
h(\sigma)=G_{p+a}(q^{2}+\sigma)
and

h(\sigma)=G_{p-a}(q^{2}+\sigma)
, we get

U_{\phi}(p,q)=\frac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{p}\!\bigl[G_{p+a}(q^{2}+\sigma)\bigr]+\frac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{p}\!\bigl[G_{p-a}(q^{2}+\sigma)\bigr].

Since each
G_{r}
is concave and
\mathbb{E}_{p}[\sigma]=a^{2}
, Jensen’s inequality
gives

\mathbb{E}_{p}\!\bigl[G_{r}(q^{2}+\sigma)\bigr]\leq G_{r}(q^{2}+\mathbb{E}_{p}[\sigma])=G_{r}(q^{2}+a^{2}).

By definition of
G_{r}
,

G_{r}(q^{2}+a^{2})=\mathbb{E}_{r}[\phi(q^{2}+a^{2}+\tau)]=U_{\phi}\!\left(r,\sqrt{q^{2}+a^{2}}\right).

Applying this with
r=p+a
and
r=p-a
, we conclude that

U_{\phi}(p,q)\leq\frac{1}{2}\,U_{\phi}\!\left(p+a,\sqrt{q^{2}+a^{2}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\,U_{\phi}\!\left(p-a,\sqrt{q^{2}+a^{2}}\right).

Multiplying by
2
proves the claim.
∎

As an immediate consequence of Corollary
4.1
, we obtain the lower bound for dyadic sets.

Corollary 4.3
.

For
\phi(t)=\sqrt{t}
, define

(4.22)

\tilde{U}(p,q):=\mathbb{E}_{p}\sqrt{q^{2}+\tau},\qquad p\in[0,1],\ q\geq 0.

For every
A\in\mathcal{D}
,

\|S_{2}(\mathbbm{1}_{A})\|_{1}\geq\tilde{U}(|A|,0)=\mathbb{E}_{|A|}\sqrt{\tau}.

Proof.

Apply Corollary
4.1
with
Q=\tilde{U}
, and use Lemma
4.2
.
∎

We now pass from dyadic sets to arbitrary Borel sets.

Proposition 4.4
.

For every Borel set
A\subset{[0,1)}
,

\|S_{2}(\mathbbm{1}_{A})\|_{1}\geq\tilde{U}(|A|,0).

Proof.

Let
f=\mathbbm{1}_{A}
, and define

f_{n}:=\mathbb{E}(f\mid\mathcal{F}_{n}),\qquad A_{n}:=\{f_{n}>1/2\}.

Then
A_{n}\in\mathcal{F}_{n}\subset\mathcal{D}
. Since
f_{n}\to f
almost everywhere and
f\in\{0,1\}
almost everywhere, we have

\mathbbm{1}_{A_{n}}\to\mathbbm{1}_{A}\qquad\text{almost everywhere.}

Hence
|A_{n}\triangle A|=\|\mathbbm{1}_{A_{n}}-\mathbbm{1}_{A}\|_{1}\to 0

by dominated convergence.
By the pointwise reverse triangle inequality in
\ell^{2}
,

|S_{2}(u)-S_{2}(v)|\leq S_{2}(u-v).

Therefore

\big|\|S_{2}(\mathbbm{1}_{A_{n}})\|_{1}-\|S_{2}(\mathbbm{1}_{A})\|_{1}\big|\leq\|S_{2}(\mathbbm{1}_{A_{n}}-\mathbbm{1}_{A})\|_{1}.

By Cauchy–Schwarz and orthogonality of martingale differences,

\|S_{2}(h)\|_{1}\leq\|S_{2}(h)\|_{2}=\|h-\mathbb{E}h\|_{2}\leq\|h\|_{2}.

Applying this to
h=\mathbbm{1}_{A_{n}}-\mathbbm{1}_{A}
, we get

\big|\|S_{2}(\mathbbm{1}_{A_{n}})\|_{1}-\|S_{2}(\mathbbm{1}_{A})\|_{1}\big|\leq|A_{n}\triangle A|^{1/2}\to 0.

Since
A_{n}\in\mathcal{D}
, Corollary
4.3
gives

\|S_{2}(\mathbbm{1}_{A_{n}})\|_{1}\geq\tilde{U}(|A_{n}|,0).

Thus it remains to note that
|A_{n}|\to|A|
and that
p\mapsto\tilde{U}(p,0)
is continuous, so passing to the limit yields

\|S_{2}(\mathbbm{1}_{A})\|_{1}\geq\tilde{U}(|A|,0).

∎

Remark 4.5
.

For every
0<\alpha\leq 2
, the function

\phi_{\alpha}(t)=t^{\alpha/2}

is increasing and concave on
[0,\infty)
.
Hence the candidate function

U_{\alpha}(p,q):=\mathbb{E}_{p}\big(q^{2}+\tau\big)^{\alpha/2}

satisfies the same one-step inequality

U_{\alpha}\!\left(p+a,\sqrt{q^{2}+a^{2}}\right)+U_{\alpha}\!\left(p-a,\sqrt{q^{2}+a^{2}}\right)\geq 2U_{\alpha}(p,q),

together with the boundary condition

U_{\alpha}(0,q)=U_{\alpha}(1,q)=q^{\alpha}.

Therefore Corollary
4.1
gives the Brownian lower bound in

L^{\alpha}
for the full range
0<\alpha\leq 2
:

\|S_{2}(\mathbbm{1}_{A})\|_{\alpha}^{\alpha}\geq\mathbb{E}_{|A|}\tau^{\alpha/2}\qquad\text{for every }A\in\mathcal{D}.

The lower bound extends from dyadic sets to arbitrary Borel sets

A\subset[0,1)
for every
0<\alpha\leq 2
. Indeed, if
A_{n}\in\mathcal{D}

approximates
A
as in Proposition
4.4
, then

\|S_{2}(\mathbbm{1}_{A_{n}})-S_{2}(\mathbbm{1}_{A})\|_{\alpha}\leq\|S_{2}(\mathbbm{1}_{A_{n}}-\mathbbm{1}_{A})\|_{2}\to 0,

since
\alpha\leq 2
, and
p\mapsto\mathbb{E}_{p}\tau^{\alpha/2}
is continuous
on
[0,1]
. Therefore

\|S_{2}(\mathbbm{1}_{A})\|_{\alpha}^{\alpha}\geq\mathbb{E}_{|A|}\tau^{\alpha/2}\qquad\text{for every Borel set }A\subset[0,1).

Moreover one could show that this bound is sharp by similar steps as in Lemma
4.9
below.

We next show the explicit formula and asymptotic behavior of
\tilde{U}(p,q)
.

Lemma 4.6
.

We have

(4.23)

\tilde{U}(p,0)=\mathbb{E}_{p}\sqrt{\tau}=\frac{2\sqrt{2\pi}}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\sin((2k+1)\pi p)}{(2k+1)^{2}},

and

\mathbb{E}_{p}\sqrt{\tau}\asymp p^{*}\log_{2}\frac{1}{p^{*}},\qquad p^{*}:=\min\{p,1-p\}.

In particular, the map
p\mapsto\tilde{U}(p,0)
is continuous on
[0,1]
.

Proof.

Let

u(t,p):=\mathbb{P}_{p}(\tau>t).

Then

\mathbb{E}_{p}\sqrt{\tau}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{-1/2}u(t,p)\,dt.

The function
u
solves the Dirichlet heat problem on
(0,1)
with initial data
u(0,p)=1
.
(This follows from the correspondence between killed Brownian motion and the Dirichlet heat equation; see
[
21
, Theorem 7.44 and Theorem 7.45,]
.)
Hence

u(t,p)=\frac{4}{\pi}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\sin((2k+1)\pi p)}{2k+1}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(2k+1)^{2}\pi^{2}}{2}t\right).

Moreover,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{4}{\pi}\frac{1}{2k+1}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{-1/2}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(2k+1)^{2}\pi^{2}}{2}t\right)\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{2\sqrt{2\pi}}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2k+1)^{2}}<\infty.

Thus the series may be integrated termwise, which gives

\mathbb{E}_{p}\sqrt{\tau}=\frac{2\sqrt{2\pi}}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\sin((2k+1)\pi p)}{(2k+1)^{2}}.

This series is absolutely and uniformly convergent in
p
, and therefore
p\mapsto\tilde{U}(p,0)
is continuous.
To obtain the asymptotic, set
x=\pi p
. Then

(4.24)

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\sin((2k+1)x)}{(2k+1)^{2}}=\mathrm{Cl}_{2}(x)-\frac{1}{4}\mathrm{Cl}_{2}(2x),

where

(4.25)

\mathrm{Cl}_{2}(x):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\sin(nx)}{n^{2}}=-\int_{0}^{x}\log\!\bigl(2\sin(t/2)\bigr)\,dt

is the Clausen function of order two; see
[
18
]
.
Using the expansion

\log\!\bigl(2\sin(t/2)\bigr)=\log t+O(t^{2})\qquad(t\downarrow 0),

we obtain

\mathrm{Cl}_{2}(x)=x\log\frac{1}{x}+O(x).

Therefore

(4.26)

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\sin((2k+1)x)}{(2k+1)^{2}}=\frac{x}{2}\log\frac{1}{x}+O(x),

and consequently

\mathbb{E}_{p}\sqrt{\tau}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\,p\log\frac{1}{p}+O(p)\qquad(p\downarrow 0).

By symmetry under
p\mapsto 1-p
, the same estimate holds near
1
, which proves

\mathbb{E}_{p}\sqrt{\tau}\asymp p^{*}\log_{2}\frac{1}{p^{*}},

because when
p
is separate from the endpoints of the interval
[0,1]
then both functions are positive, separated form zero and infinity.
∎

Lemma 4.7
.

Let

p^{*}:=\min\{p,1-p\}.
Then for every
p\in[0,1]
,

\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\,\ln 2\;p^{*}\log_{2}\frac{1}{p^{*}}\;\leq\;\tilde{U}(p,0)\;\leq\;\frac{4\sqrt{2}\,G}{\pi^{3/2}}\,p^{*}\log_{2}\frac{1}{p^{*}},

where

G=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{(2k+1)^{2}}

is Catalan’s constant.
Both constants are sharp:

\lim_{p^{*}\downarrow 0}\frac{\tilde{U}(p,0)}{p^{*}\log_{2}\frac{1}{p^{*}}}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\,\ln 2,

and equality in the upper bound holds at
p=\frac{1}{2}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.6
, we can write

\tilde{U}(p,0)=\mathbb{E}_{p}\sqrt{\tau}=\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{\pi^{3/2}}\,S(p),\quad\text{where}\quad S(p):=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\sin((2k+1)\pi p)}{(2k+1)^{2}}.

By equation (
4.24
) using
x=\pi p
, we have

S(p)=\mathrm{Cl}_{2}(\pi p)-\frac{1}{4}\mathrm{Cl}_{2}(2\pi p),

where
\mathrm{Cl}_{2}(x)
is given by (
4.25
).
Moreover, since

\sin((2k+1)\pi(1-p))=\sin((2k+1)\pi p)

for every
k\geq 0
, we have

S(1-p)=S(p),\qquad p\in[0,1].

Hence it is enough to work on
[0,1/2]
, where
p^{*}=p
.

Differentiating the Clausen representation on
(0,1)
gives

S^{\prime}(p)=-\pi\log\!\Bigl(2\sin\frac{\pi p}{2}\Bigr)+\frac{\pi}{2}\log\!\bigl(2\sin(\pi p)\bigr).

Using

\sin(\pi p)=2\sin\frac{\pi p}{2}\cos\frac{\pi p}{2},

we obtain

S^{\prime}(p)=\frac{\pi}{2}\log\!\Bigl(\cot\frac{\pi p}{2}\Bigr).

Since
S(0)=0
, integration yields

S(p)=\int_{0}^{\pi p}\frac{1}{2}\log\!\Bigl(\cot\frac{t}{2}\Bigr)\,dt.

Differentiating once more, and using

\frac{d}{dx}\log\!\Bigl(\cot\frac{x}{2}\Bigr)=-\frac{1}{\sin x},

we get

S^{\prime\prime}(p)=-\frac{\pi^{2}}{2\sin(\pi p)},\qquad 0<p<1.

We split the proof into three steps.

Step 1: Lower bound.

For
0<p\leq 1/2
,

L(p):=S(p)-\frac{\pi}{2}p\log\frac{1}{p}.

Then

L^{\prime\prime}(p)=-\frac{\pi^{2}}{2\sin(\pi p)}+\frac{\pi}{2p}.

Since
\sin(\pi p)\leq\pi p
on
[0,1/2]
, it follows that

-\frac{\pi^{2}}{2\sin(\pi p)}\leq-\frac{\pi}{2p}\implies L^{\prime\prime}(p)\leq 0

and so

L
is concave on
(0,1/2]
.

Taking
p\downarrow 0
and using (
4.26
) we get

L(p)\to 0
as
(p\downarrow 0).

Also,

L\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}\Bigr)=S\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}\Bigr)-\frac{\pi}{4}\log 2=G-\frac{\pi}{4}\log 2>0.

Therefore the continuous extension of
L
to
[0,1/2]
is concave, equals
0
at
0
, and is positive at
1/2
.
Therefore

L(p)\geq 0\qquad\text{for all }p\in\Bigl[0,\frac{1}{2}\Bigr].

Thus
by symmetry,

S(p)\geq\frac{\pi}{2}p^{*}\log\frac{1}{p^{*}},\qquad 0\leq p\leq 1.

Multiplying by
\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{\pi^{3/2}}
and using

\log\frac{1}{p^{*}}=(\ln 2)\log_{2}\frac{1}{p^{*}},

we obtain

\tilde{U}(p,0)\geq\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\,\ln 2\;p^{*}\log_{2}\frac{1}{p^{*}}.

Step 2: Upper bound.

Set

A:=\frac{2G}{\ln 2},

and for
0<p\leq 1/2
, define

V(p):=S(p)-A\,p\log\frac{1}{p},\quad\text{then}\quad V^{\prime\prime}(p)=-\frac{\pi^{2}}{2\sin(\pi p)}+\frac{A}{p}.

Now
\sin(\pi p)\geq 2p
on
[0,1/2]
, so

-\frac{\pi^{2}}{2\sin(\pi p)}\geq-\frac{\pi^{2}}{4p}\quad\implies\quad V^{\prime\prime}(p)\geq\frac{A-\pi^{2}/4}{p}.

Since

A=\frac{2G}{\ln 2}\approx 2.6434
and
\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}\approx 2.4674,

we have
A>\pi^{2}/4
, and therefore

V^{\prime\prime}(p)>0\qquad\text{for all }p\in\Bigl(0,\frac{1}{2}\Bigr].

So
V
is convex on
(0,1/2]
.

Taking
p\downarrow 0
and using (
4.26
) we get

V(p)\to 0
as
(p\downarrow 0).

Moreover,

V\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}\Bigr)=S\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}\Bigr)-A\cdot\frac{1}{2}\log 2=G-\frac{2G}{\ln 2}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\ln 2=0.

Thus the continuous extension of
V
to
[0,1/2]
is convex and vanishes at both endpoints. Hence

V(p)\leq 0\qquad\text{for all }p\in\Bigl[0,\frac{1}{2}\Bigr].

Therefore
by symmetry,

S(p)\leq A\,p^{*}\log\frac{1}{p^{*}},\qquad 0\leq p\leq 1.

Multiplying by
\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{\pi^{3/2}}
and using again

\log\frac{1}{p^{*}}=(\ln 2)\log_{2}\frac{1}{p^{*}},

gives

\tilde{U}(p,0)\leq\frac{4\sqrt{2}\,G}{\pi^{3/2}}\,p^{*}\log_{2}\frac{1}{p^{*}}.

Step 3: Sharpness.

The sharpness of the lower constant follows from the asymptotic already proved in Lemma
4.6
:

\tilde{U}(p,0)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\,p\log\frac{1}{p}+O(p)\qquad(p\downarrow 0).

Dividing by
p\log_{2}(1/p)=\frac{1}{\ln 2}p\log(1/p)
yields

\lim_{p\downarrow 0}\frac{\tilde{U}(p,0)}{p\log_{2}\frac{1}{p}}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\,\ln 2.

By symmetry, the same limit holds as
p\uparrow 1
, so the lower constant cannot be improved.

For the upper bound, equality holds at
p=\frac{1}{2}
, because

S\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}\Bigr)=G

and therefore

\tilde{U}\Bigl(\frac{1}{2},0\Bigr)=\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{\pi^{3/2}}\,G=\frac{4\sqrt{2}\,G}{\pi^{3/2}}\cdot\frac{1}{2}=\frac{4\sqrt{2}\,G}{\pi^{3/2}}\,\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}\Bigr)\log_{2}2.

This proves sharpness of the upper constant.
∎

We now introduce the extremal profile in the Borel class:

\mathcal{F}(p):=\inf\Bigl\{\|S_{2}(\mathbbm{1}_{A})\|_{1}:\ A\subset{[0,1)}\ \text{Borel},\ |A|=p\Bigr\},\qquad p\in[0,1].

Lemma 4.8
.

For all
p,q\in[0,1]
,

|\mathcal{F}(p)-\mathcal{F}(q)|\leq|p-q|^{1/2}.

In particular,
\mathcal{F}
is continuous on
[0,1]
.

Proof.

Fix
p,q\in[0,1]
and
\varepsilon>0
. Choose a Borel set
A\subset{[0,1)}
with
|A|=p
and

\|S_{2}(\mathbbm{1}_{A})\|_{1}\leq\mathcal{F}(p)+\varepsilon.

Assume first that
q\geq p
. Since Lebesgue measure is nonatomic, there exists a Borel set
E\subset{[0,1)}\setminus A
with
|E|=q-p
. Set
B:=A\cup E
. Then
|B|=q
and

|A\triangle B|=q-p.

So,

\big|\|S_{2}(\mathbbm{1}_{A})\|_{1}-\|S_{2}(\mathbbm{1}_{B})\|_{1}\big|\leq|A\triangle B|^{1/2}=|p-q|^{1/2}.

Hence

\mathcal{F}(q)\leq\|S_{2}(\mathbbm{1}_{B})\|_{1}\leq\mathcal{F}(p)+\varepsilon+|p-q|^{1/2}.

Letting
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
gives

\mathcal{F}(q)\leq\mathcal{F}(p)+|p-q|^{1/2}.

Interchanging the roles of
p
and
q
proves the claim.
∎

It remains to prove the matching upper bound.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
p_{m}=k_{m}/2^{m}\in(0,1)
and set
a_{m}=2^{-m}
. Then there exists a Borel set

A_{m}^{\infty}\subset{[0,1)}
with
|A_{m}^{\infty}|=p_{m}
such that

\|S_{2}(\mathbbm{1}_{A_{m}^{\infty}})\|_{1}=a_{m}\,\mathbb{E}\sqrt{\tau_{m}},

where
\tau_{m}
is the exit time of the dyadic lattice walk started at
p_{m}
.
Moreover, if
p_{m}\to p\in(0,1)
, then

a_{m}\,\mathbb{E}\sqrt{\tau_{m}}\to\mathbb{E}_{p}\sqrt{\tau}.

Proof.

Let
(r_{k})_{k\geq 1}
be the Rademacher system on
[0,1)
, give by

r_{k}(x):=\operatorname{sgn}\bigl(\sin(2^{k}\pi x)\bigr),\qquad x\in{[0,1)}.

Define

X_{0}^{(m)}:=p_{m},\qquad X_{n}^{(m)}:=X_{n-1}^{(m)}+a_{m}r_{n},\qquad n\geq 1.

Let

\tau_{m}:=\inf\{n\geq 0:\ X_{n}^{(m)}\in\{0,1\}\},\qquad A_{m}^{\infty}:=\{X_{\tau_{m}}^{(m)}=1\}.

Since

A_{m}^{\infty}=\bigcup_{n\geq 0}\bigl(\{\tau_{m}=n\}\cap\{X_{n}^{(m)}=1\}\bigr),

the set
A_{m}^{\infty}
is Borel.
The stopped process
X_{n\wedge\tau_{m}}^{(m)}
is a bounded martingale with values in
[0,1]
. Since

X_{\tau_{m}}^{(m)}\in\{0,1\}\implies X_{\tau_{m}}^{(m)}=\mathbbm{1}_{A_{m}^{\infty}}.

Hence optional stopping gives

|A_{m}^{\infty}|=\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau_{m}}^{(m)}]=X_{0}^{(m)}=p_{m}.

Again by optional stopping,

\mathbb{E}(\mathbbm{1}_{A_{m}^{\infty}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n})=X_{n\wedge\tau_{m}}^{(m)}.

Hence

d_{n}=X_{n\wedge\tau_{m}}^{(m)}-X_{(n-1)\wedge\tau_{m}}^{(m)}=a_{m}r_{n}\,\mathbbm{1}_{\{n\leq\tau_{m}\}}.

Therefore

S_{2}(\mathbbm{1}_{A_{m}^{\infty}})^{2}=\sum_{n\geq 1}|d_{n}|^{2}=a_{m}^{2}\tau_{m},

and so

\|S_{2}(\mathbbm{1}_{A_{m}^{\infty}})\|_{1}=a_{m}\,\mathbb{E}\sqrt{\tau_{m}}.

To prove convergence, let
Y_{m}
be the continuous linear interpolation of the walk
(X_{n}^{(m)})_{n\geq 0}
on the time scale
a_{m}^{2}
, and define

\theta_{m}:=\inf\{t\geq 0:\,Y_{m}(t)\notin(0,1)\}.

Then
\theta_{m}=a_{m}^{2}\tau_{m}
. By Donsker’s invariance principle, the processes
Y_{m}
converge weakly to Brownian motion started at
p

[
10
]
. By Skorokhod representation
[
4
]
, we may see them on a common probability space so that

Y_{m}\to B\qquad\text{almost surely uniformly on compact time intervals}

We claim that

\theta_{m}\to\tau\qquad\text{almost surely.}

Fix a sample point
\omega
for which the above uniform convergence holds and such that Brownian motion, after hitting
0
or
1
, leaves the interval
[0,1]
immediately; this is a standard almost sure property of Brownian motion. Let
\varepsilon>0
. By continuity of
B(\cdot,\omega)
on
[0,\tau(\omega)-\varepsilon]
, there exists
\delta>0
such that

B_{t}(\omega)\in[\delta,1-\delta]\qquad\text{for all }0\leq t\leq\tau(\omega)-\varepsilon.

Hence for all sufficiently large
m
,

Y_{m}(t,\omega)\in(0,1)\qquad\text{for all }0\leq t\leq\tau(\omega)-\varepsilon,

so

\theta_{m}(\omega)\geq\tau(\omega)-\varepsilon.

On the other hand, if
B_{\tau(\omega)}(\omega)=0
, then by the above boundary regularity there exists

t_{\varepsilon}\in[\tau(\omega),\tau(\omega)+\varepsilon]
such that

B_{t_{\varepsilon}}(\omega)<0.

Similarly, if
B_{\tau(\omega)}(\omega)=1
, there exists

t_{\varepsilon}\in[\tau(\omega),\tau(\omega)+\varepsilon]
such that

B_{t_{\varepsilon}}(\omega)>1.

Uniform convergence on
[0,t_{\varepsilon}]
then implies that for all sufficiently large
m
,

Y_{m}(t_{\varepsilon},\omega)\notin(0,1),

and therefore

\theta_{m}(\omega)\leq t_{\varepsilon}\leq\tau(\omega)+\varepsilon.

Since
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary, it follows that
\theta_{m}(\omega)\to\tau(\omega)
.
On the other hand, the process

D_{n}\subseteq D_{n+1}
. It suffices to show that,
B_{n+1}\mid_{D_{n}}=B_{n}
for all
n\geq 1
.
Let
x\in D_{n}
, then
x=\frac{k}{2^{n}}
for some
0\leq k\leq 2^{n}
.
Using Lemma
A.1
, we have

\displaystyle B_{n+1}(x)

\displaystyle=(n+1)\frac{k}{2^{n}}-\frac{1}{2^{n}}F\left(2k\right)=(n+1)\frac{k}{2^{n}}-\frac{1}{2^{n}}\left(k+2F(k)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{2^{n}}k+\frac{k}{2^{n}}-\frac{k}{2^{n}}-\frac{1}{2^{n-1}}F(k)=B_{n}(x).

Hence the limit exists.

To prove (ii), we first show that the limit function
P:D\mapsto[0,\infty)
defined on
D
indeed satisfies
P(0)=P(1)=0
, and the two-point inequality (
1.3
) with
(\alpha,\beta)=(1,1)
, that is

(A.32)

\displaystyle\left|\frac{x-y}{2}\right|+\frac{P(x)+P(y)}{2}\geq P\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)\quad\text{for all}\quad x,y\in D.

Note that indeed
P(0)=B_{1}(0)=0
, and
P(1)=B_{1}(1)=0
, so we show the two-point inequality.
Pick arbitrary points
x,y\in D
. Then there exists
n\geq 1
such that
x,y,\frac{x+y}{2}\in D_{n+1}
.
It suffices to show

(A.33)

\left|\frac{x-y}{2}\right|+\frac{B_{n+1}(x)+B_{n+1}(y)}{2}\geq B_{n+1}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right).

Without loss of generality, assume
x\geq y
. Then there exist
k
and
l
such that
0\leq l\leq k\leq 2^{n+1}
,

x=\frac{k}{2^{n+1}},\quad y=\frac{l}{2^{n+1}},\quad\text{and}\quad k+l\quad\text{is even}.

Rewriting (
A.33
) using the definition (
1.7
) for
B_{n}
, we get

\frac{k-l}{2^{n+2}}+\frac{(n+1)(k+l)}{2^{n+2}}-\frac{F(k)+F(l)}{2^{n+1}}\geq\frac{k+l}{2^{n+2}}(n+1)-\frac{F\left(\frac{k+l}{2}\right)}{2^{n}},

the above line is
equivalent to

\frac{k-l}{2}+2F\left(\frac{k+l}{2}\right)\geq F(k)+F(l).

Using the identity

F(2k)=k+2F(k)
from Lemma (
A.1
), the above inequality simplifies to

F(k+l)\geq F(k)+F(l)+l,

which is true by Lemma
A.2
.

We are left to show that
P
is the pointwise maximal function defined on dyadic numbers
D\subset[0,1]
, satisfying the boundary condition
P(0)=P(1)=0
and the two-point inequality (
A.32
).

For
n\geq 1
, let
\{0,1\}^{n}
be the hypercube of dimension
n
. The vertices
x,y\in\{0,1\}^{n}
are joined by an edge if
x=\left(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\right)
and
y=\left(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\right)
differ by exactly one coordinate. Denote such an edge by
(x,y)
.
Let
A\subset\{0,1\}^{n}
and
A^{c}:=\{0,1\}^{n}\backslash A
, we call
y\in A^{c}
a neighbor of
x\in A
if it joins
x
by an edge.
Define the edge boundary of
A
by
\nabla A:=\left\{(x,y):x\in A,y\in A^{c}\right\}
, and the function

h_{A}(x)=\begin{cases}0&{\rm{if}}\;\;x\in A^{c},\\
\text{number of neighbors of $x$}&{\rm{if}}\;\;x\in A.\end{cases}

The quantity
\mathbb{E}h_{A}(x)
, where
x\sim\mathrm{unif}(\{0,1\}^{n})
, has the property
\mathbb{E}h_{A}(x)=\mathbb{E}h_{A^{c}}(x)
, and we have
\mathbb{E}h_{A}(x)=\frac{|\nabla A|}{2^{n}}
.
It is known (see
[
3
]
) that

\min_{A\subset\{0,1\}^{n},\,|A|=t}\mathbb{E}h_{A}(x)=P(t)\quad\text{for all}\quad t\in D_{n}.

Here
|A|=\mathbb{P}(x\in A)
is the uniform probability measure.
It suffices to show that

\mathbb{E}h_{A}(x)\geq Q(|A|)

for any
Q:{D}\to\mathbb{R}
that satisfies
Q(0)=Q(1)=0
and (
A.32
).

Let
f={\mathbbm{1}}_{A}
be the indicator function of a set
A\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
, for
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, denote
x^{i}=(x_{1},\cdots x_{i-1},1-x_{i},x_{i+1},\cdots x_{n})
and denote
(\cdot)_{+}=\max(\cdot,0)
.
We prove the inequality

(A.34)

\mathbb{E}h_{A}=\mathbb{E}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(f(x)-f(x^{i}))_{+}\geq Q(|A|)

by induction on
n
.
For
n=1
, we have
\mathbb{E}h_{A}=\frac{|\nabla A|}{2}
. We verify that for all
A\in\{0,1\}
, (
A.34
) holds. Let
A=\emptyset
(the case
A=\{0,1\}
is similar), then
\mathbb{E}h_{A}=0=Q(0)
.
Let
A=\{1\}
(the case
A=\{0\}
is similar), then
\mathbb{E}h_{A}=\frac{1}{2}
,
Since
Q
satisfies (
A.32
), and
|A|=\frac{1}{2}
, we have

Q\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)=Q\left(\frac{1+0}{2}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{Q(1)+Q(0)}{2}=\frac{1}{2}=\mathbb{E}h_{A}.

Assume now that the statement holds for
n-1
. For
\bar{x}=(x_{2},\dots,x_{n})\in\{0,1\}^{n-1}
, define

A_{i}:=\{\bar{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n-1}:(i,\bar{x})\in A\},\qquad i=0,1,

and write

f_{i}(\bar{x}):=\mathbbm{1}_{A_{i}}(\bar{x}),\qquad i=0,1.

Here and below,
|A_{i}|=\mathbb{P}(\bar{x}\in A_{i})
denotes the uniform probability measure on
\{0,1\}^{n-1}
, so

|A|=\frac{|A_{1}|+|A_{0}|}{2}.

For
k=1,\dots,n-1
, let
\bar{x}^{\,k}
denote the point obtained from
\bar{x}
by flipping its
k
-th coordinate. Also define

h_{A_{i}}(\bar{x}):=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\bigl(f_{i}(\bar{x})-f_{i}(\bar{x}^{\,k})\bigr)_{+},\qquad i=0,1.

Since

h_{A}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bigl(f(x)-f(x^{j})\bigr)_{+},

we split the sum into the first coordinate and the remaining coordinates:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}h_{A}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\bar{x}}\mathbb{E}_{x_{1}}\bigl(f(x)-f(x^{1})\bigr)_{+}+\mathbb{E}_{\bar{x}}\mathbb{E}_{x_{1}}\sum_{j=2}^{n}\bigl(f(x)-f(x^{j})\bigr)_{+}.

For the first term, using
(a-b)_{+}+(b-a)_{+}=|a-b|
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bar{x}}\mathbb{E}_{x_{1}}\bigl(f(x)-f(x^{1})\bigr)_{+}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\bar{x}}\Bigl(\bigl(f_{1}(\bar{x})-f_{0}(\bar{x})\bigr)_{+}+\bigl(f_{0}(\bar{x})-f_{1}(\bar{x})\bigr)_{+}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\bar{x}}\bigl|f_{1}(\bar{x})-f_{0}(\bar{x})\bigr|.

For the remaining coordinates, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bar{x}}\mathbb{E}_{x_{1}}\sum_{j=2}^{n}\bigl(f(x)-f(x^{j})\bigr)_{+}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\bar{x}}\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\bigl(f_{1}(\bar{x})-f_{1}(\bar{x}^{\,k})\bigr)_{+}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\bar{x}}\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\bigl(f_{0}(\bar{x})-f_{0}(\bar{x}^{\,k})\bigr)_{+}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}h_{A_{1}}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}h_{A_{0}}.

Therefore

\mathbb{E}h_{A}=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\bigl|f_{1}-f_{0}\bigr|+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}h_{A_{1}}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}h_{A_{0}}.

By Jensen’s inequality,

\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\bigl|f_{1}-f_{0}\bigr|\geq\frac{1}{2}\bigl|\mathbb{E}f_{1}-\mathbb{E}f_{0}\bigr|=\frac{1}{2}\bigl||A_{1}|-|A_{0}|\bigr|.

By the induction hypothesis,

\mathbb{E}h_{A_{1}}\geq Q(|A_{1}|),\qquad\mathbb{E}h_{A_{0}}\geq Q(|A_{0}|).

Hence

\mathbb{E}h_{A}\geq\frac{1}{2}\bigl||A_{1}|-|A_{0}|\bigr|+\frac{1}{2}\bigl(Q(|A_{1}|)+Q(|A_{0}|)\bigr).

Since
Q
satisfies (
A.32
),

Q\!\left(\frac{|A_{1}|+|A_{0}|}{2}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}\bigl(Q(|A_{1}|)+Q(|A_{0}|)\bigr)+\frac{1}{2}\bigl||A_{1}|-|A_{0}|\bigr|.

Therefore

\mathbb{E}h_{A}\geq Q\!\left(\frac{|A_{1}|+|A_{0}|}{2}\right)=Q(|A|).

This completes the induction and proves (ii).

Lemma A.3
.

For all
x\in D
, we have the following dyadic symmetry

P(x)=P(1-x).

Proof.

Define

L(x):=P(1-x)
for
x\in D.

Then

L(0)=P(1)=0,\qquad L(1)=P(0)=0.

Also, if
x,y\in D
, then

\displaystyle L\!\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=P\!\left(1-\frac{x+y}{2}\right)=P\!\left(\frac{(1-x)+(1-y)}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{P(1-x)+P(1-y)}{2}+\frac{|(1-x)-(1-y)|}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{L(x)+L(y)}{2}+\frac{|x-y|}{2}.

Hence
L
satisfies the endpoint condition and (
A.32
) on
D
.
By part (ii), since
P
is pointwise maximal on
D
, we get

L(x)\leq P(x),\qquad x\in D.

Applying this with
1-x
in place of
x
yields

P(x)=L(1-x)\leq P(1-x),

while applying it at
x
gives

P(1-x)=L(x)\leq P(x).

Therefore
P(x)=P(1-x)
for all
x\in D.

∎

To prove (iii), we equivalently prove that for all
x,y\in D
such that
|x-y|<\frac{1}{2^{m}}
for some
m\geq 1
, we have
|P(x)-P(y)|\lesssim\frac{m}{2^{m}}
.
The proof of the claim is given in the next section.

Lemma A.4
.

The function
P
defined on
D
, extends to a uniquely defined continuous function on
[0,1]
.

Proof.

By part (iii), for
x,y\in D
with
|x-y|\leq 2^{-m}
, we have

|P(x)-P(y)|\leq C\frac{m}{2^{m}}.

Equivalently, if
0<|x-y|\leq h\leq 1/2
, then choosing
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that

2^{-(m+1)}<h\leq 2^{-m},

we obtain

|P(x)-P(y)|\leq C\frac{m}{2^{m}}\lesssim h\log_{2}\!\Big(\frac{1}{h}\Big).

Since
h\log_{2}(1/h)\to 0
as
h\to 0^{+}
, it follows that
P
is uniformly
continuous on
D
. Because
D
is dense in
[0,1]
,
P
extends uniquely to a
continuous function on
[0,1]
.

∎

To prove (iv), by Lemma
A.4
,
P
extends uniquely to a continuous function on

[0,1]
. By abuse of notation, we denote this extension again by
P
.

By Lemma
A.3
, we already know that

P(x)=P(1-x),\qquad x\in D.

Since both sides are continuous on
[0,1]
and agree on the dense set
D
, we obtain

P(x)=P(1-x),\qquad x\in[0,1].

Next, we verify that

P(x)+x=2P(x/2),\qquad x\in D.

Pick any
x\in D_{n}
. Then
x=\frac{k}{2^{n}}
for some
n\geq 1
and some integer
k
with
0\leq k\leq 2^{n}
. Since
x\in D_{n}
, we have
P(x)=B_{n}(x)
, and since
\frac{x}{2}=\frac{k}{2^{n+1}}\in D_{n+1}
, we also have

P(x/2)=B_{n+1}(x/2)
. By the definition of
B_{n}
,

\displaystyle P(x)+x

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{nk}{2^{n}}-\frac{1}{2^{n-1}}F(k)\right)+\frac{k}{2^{n}}=\frac{(n+1)k}{2^{n}}-\frac{1}{2^{n-1}}F(k),

while

\displaystyle 2P\!\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=2\left(\frac{(n+1)k}{2^{n+1}}-\frac{1}{2^{n}}F(k)\right)=\frac{(n+1)k}{2^{n}}-\frac{1}{2^{n-1}}F(k).

Hence

P(x)+x=2P(x/2).

This proves (iv) on
D
.

To prove part (v), define

g(x):=\begin{cases}x\log_{2}\!\left(\frac{1}{x}\right),&x\in(0,1],\\[2.84526pt]
0,&x=0,\end{cases}\qquad\widetilde{g}(x):=g(1-x),

and using
\tilde{g}(X)=g(1-x)
define

B(x):=\max\{g(x),\widetilde{g}(x)\}=x^{*}\log_{2}\!\left(\frac{1}{x^{*}}\right),\qquad x\in[0,1].

Clearly,
B(0)=B(1)=0.

We first show that
g
satisfies (
A.32
) on
[0,1]
. By symmetry, the same will then
hold for
\widetilde{g}
.

If
x=0
or
y=0
, the inequality is immediate. If
x=y
, it is trivial. Thus assume

0<x\leq y\leq 1.

Write

y=(1+t)x,\qquad t\geq 0.

Then

\frac{x+y}{2}=\frac{(2+t)x}{2},\qquad\frac{|x-y|}{2}=\frac{tx}{2}.

A direct computation gives

\displaystyle g\!\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)-\frac{g(x)+g(y)}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{x}{2}\Bigl((2+t)\Bigl(1-\log_{2}(2+t)\Bigr)+(1+t)\log_{2}(1+t)\Bigr).

Therefore it is enough to prove that

(2+t)\Bigl(1-\log_{2}(2+t)\Bigr)+(1+t)\log_{2}(1+t)\leq t,\qquad t\geq 0.

Equivalently,

\phi(t):=(1+t)\log_{2}(1+t)-(2+t)\log_{2}(2+t)+2\leq 0.

Now

\phi(0)=0,

and

\phi^{\prime}(t)=\log_{2}\!\left(\frac{1+t}{2+t}\right)\leq 0,\qquad t\geq 0.

Hence
\phi(t)\leq 0

satisfies (
A.32
) on
[0,1]
.

By symmetry,
\widetilde{g}(x)=g(1-x)
also satisfies (
A.32
). We now claim that the

the same properties. Indeed, if

A.32

\displaystyle w\!\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\max\!\left\{u\!\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right),\,v\!\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\max\!\left\{\frac{u(x)+u(y)}{2}+\frac{|x-y|}{2},\frac{v(x)+v(y)}{2}+\frac{|x-y|}{2}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\max\{u(x),v(x)\}+\max\{u(y),v(y)\}}{2}+\frac{|x-y|}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{w(x)+w(y)}{2}+\frac{|x-y|}{2}.

Applying this with
u=g
and
v=\widetilde{g}
, we conclude that

B(x)=x^{*}\log_{2}\!\left(\frac{1}{x^{*}}\right)

satisfies the endpoint condition and (
A.32
) on
[0,1]
, hence also on
D
.

By part (ii), since
P
is pointwise maximal on
D
, we obtain

P(x)\geq B(x)=x^{*}\log_{2}\!\left(\frac{1}{x^{*}}\right),\qquad x\in D.

By continuity of
P
from part (iii), the same inequality holds for all
x\in[0,1]
.

It remains to prove the claimed equality cases. From part (iv), taking
x=1
, we get

P(1)+1=2P(1/2).

Since
P(1)=0
, it follows that

P(1/2)=\frac{1}{2}.

Now we prove by induction that

P(2^{-k})=\frac{k}{2^{k}},\qquad k\geq 1.

This is true for

. Assume it holds for
k-1
. Applying part (iv) with

x=2^{-(k-1)}
, we obtain

P(2^{-(k-1)})+2^{-(k-1)}=2P(2^{-k}).

Hence, by the induction hypothesis,

2P(2^{-k})=\frac{k-1}{2^{k-1}}+\frac{1}{2^{k-1}}=\frac{k}{2^{k-1}},

so

P(2^{-k})=\frac{k}{2^{k}}.

By the symmetry proved in part (iv),

P(1-2^{-k})=P(2^{-k})=\frac{k}{2^{k}}.

Finally,

2^{-k}\log_{2}(2^{k})=\frac{k}{2^{k}},

so equality holds at

x=2^{-k}\qquad\text{and}\qquad x=1-2^{-k}.

This proves (v).

To prove part (vi), by Lemma
A.4
, the function
P:D\to[0,\infty)

[0,1]

.

P\!\left(\frac{x_{n}+y_{n}}{2}\right)\leq\frac{P(x_{n})+P(y_{n})}{2}+\frac{|x_{n}-y_{n}|}{2}.

Appendix B
Proof of Theorem

.iii

In this section, we verify part (iii) of Theorem
1.9
. It suffices to show that there exists a universal constant
C>0
such that for any
m\geq 1
if
|x-y|<2^{-m}
where
x,y\in D
, then
|P(x)-P(y)|\leq Cm/2^{m}

For any

,

The condition

Using the definition of

\displaystyle|B_{n}(x)-B_{n}(y)|=

\displaystyle\left|B_{n}\left(\frac{k}{2^{n}}\right)-B_{n}\left(\frac{l}{2^{n}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\frac{(n+1)(k-l)-(F(2k)-F(2l))}{2^{n}}\right|.

For such

(B.35)

\left|\frac{n(k-l)-(F(2k)-F(2l))}{2^{n}}\right|\leq C\frac{m}{2^{m}}.

Recall

, and

s(i)

.
From
[
11

F(l)=\max_{0\leq m\leq l/2}(F(m)+F(l-m)+m).

For any integer

where

We further make the following observations.

Lemma B.1
.

For any integer
k>0
and integer
p

0\leq p\leq 2^{k}

F(2^{k}+p)=k2^{k-1}+p+F(p).

Proof.

It is easy to see that

F(2^{k})=\sum_{j=0}^{2^{k}-1}s(j)=k2^{k-1},

and for all
0\leq j<2^{k}
we have

s(2^{k}+j)=1+s(j).

Let
0\leq p\leq 2^{k}
. We have

\displaystyle F(2^{k}+p)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{2^{k}+p-1}s(j)=\sum_{j=0}^{2^{k}-1}s(j)+\sum_{j=2^{k}}^{2^{k}+p-1}s(j)

\displaystyle=F(2^{k})+\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}s(2^{k}+j)=k2^{k-1}+\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}\left(1+s(j)\right)

\displaystyle=k2^{k-1}+p+F(p).

This finishes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Lemma B.2
.

For any
0<k\leq 2^{n}
of the form

k=2^{k_{1}}+2^{k_{2}}+\cdots+2^{k_{T}},

where

F(k)=\sum_{j=1}^{T}(k_{j}+2(j-1))2^{k_{j}-1}.

Let
p=k-2^{k_{1}}
, then

.
Hence by Lemma

(B.37)

F(k)=F(2^{k_{1}}+(k-2^{k_{1}}))=k_{1}2^{k_{1}-1}+(k-2^{k_{1}})+F(k-2^{k_{1}}).

Take
p=k-2^{k_{1}}-2^{k_{2}}
, then
p<2^{k_{2}}
Using Lemma
B.1
again, we have

(B.38)

\displaystyle F(k-2^{k_{1}})

\displaystyle=F(2^{k_{2}}+(k-2^{k_{1}}-2^{k_{2}}))

\displaystyle=k_{2}2^{k_{2}-1}+(k-2^{k_{1}}-2^{k_{2}})+F(k-2^{k_{1}}-2^{k_{2}}).

Combining (
B.37
) and (
B.38
), we obtain

\displaystyle F(k)

\displaystyle=k_{1}2^{k_{1}-1}+(k-2^{k_{1}})+k_{2}2^{k_{2}-1}+(k-2^{k_{1}}-2^{k_{2}})+F(k-2^{k_{1}}-2^{k_{2}})

\displaystyle=k_{1}2^{k_{1}-1}+(2^{k_{2}}+\cdots+2^{k_{T}})+k_{2}2^{k_{2}-1}+(2^{k_{3}}+\cdots+2^{k_{T}})

\displaystyle\;\;+F(k-2^{k_{1}}-2^{k_{2}}).

Iterating this process, we obtain

\displaystyle F(k)

\displaystyle=k_{1}2^{k_{1}-1}+(2^{k_{2}}+\cdots+2^{k_{T}})+k_{2}2^{k_{2}-1}+(2^{k_{3}}+\cdots+2^{k_{T}})+

\displaystyle\quad\cdots+k_{T-1}2^{k_{T-1}-1}+2^{k_{T}}+F(2^{k_{T}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{T}k_{j}2^{k_{j}-1}+\sum_{j=2}^{T}(j-1)2^{k_{j}}

∎

Lemma B.3
.

Let
0\leq l<k\leq 2^{n}
such that
|k-l|\leq 2^{n-m}
; consider the binary representations

\displaystyle l=2^{l_{1}}+2^{l_{2}}+\cdots+2^{l_{R}},\qquad k=2^{k_{1}}+2^{k_{2}}+\cdots+2^{k_{T}},

where
k_{1}>k_{2}>\cdots\geq 0
and
l_{1}>l_{2}>\cdots>l_{R}\geq 0
.
Then, for a fixed
p>0
, if
l_{i}=k_{i}
for all
i=1,\dots,p-1
and
k_{p}\geq l_{p}+2
, we have

k_{p}\leq n-m+1.

Proof.

Fix
p>0
, such that
l_{i}=k_{i}
for all
i=1,\ldots,p-1
, and
k_{p}\geq l_{p}+2
.
Using the finite geometric series formula, we have

(B.39)

2^{k_{p}-1}-1=2^{k_{p}-2}+2^{k_{p}-3}+\cdots 2^{0}\geq 2^{l_{p}}+2^{l_{p}-1}+\cdots+2^{0}.

From (
B.39
) we have

\displaystyle|k-l|

\displaystyle=2^{k_{p}}+\cdots+2^{k_{T}}-(2^{l_{p}}+\cdots+2^{l_{R}})

\displaystyle\geq 2^{k_{p}}-(2^{k_{p}-1}-1)=2^{k_{p}-1}+1.

Since
|k-l|\leq 2^{n-m}
, it follows
2^{k_{p}-1}+1\leq 2^{n-m}
. Hence,

k_{p}-1\leq n-m.

This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Lemma B.4
.

Let
0\leq l<k\leq 2^{n}
, such that
|k-l|\leq 2^{n-m}
, and having the binary representations

\displaystyle l

\displaystyle=2^{l_{1}}+2^{l_{2}}+\cdots+2^{l_{R}},\qquad k

\displaystyle=2^{k_{1}}+2^{k_{2}}+\cdots+2^{k_{T}},

where

n\geq k_{1}>k_{2}>\cdots\geq 0,\qquad n\geq l_{1}>l_{2}>\cdots>l_{R}\geq 0.

Fix
p>0
and assume

l_{i}=k_{i}\quad\text{for }i=1,\dots,p-1,\qquad k_{p}=l_{p}+1.

Let
M\geq 1
be maximal such that

l_{p+j}=k_{p}-(j+1),\qquad j=0,\dots,M-1.

Equivalently, either
p+M>R
, or else

l_{p+M}\leq k_{p}-(M+2).

Then

(B.40)

p<T\Longrightarrow k_{p+1}\leq n-m+1.

Moreover,

(B.41)

k_{p}-M\leq n-m+1.

If
p+M\leq R
, then

(B.42)

l_{p+M}\leq n-m+1.

Proof.

Assume first that
p<T
, so that
k_{p+1}
is defined. Since

l_{i}=k_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,p-1
and
k_{p}=l_{p}+1
, we have

\displaystyle|k-l|

\displaystyle=2^{k_{p}}+2^{k_{p+1}}+\cdots+2^{k_{T}}-(2^{l_{p}}+\cdots+2^{l_{R}})

\displaystyle\geq 2^{k_{p}}+2^{k_{p+1}}+\cdots+2^{k_{T}}-(2^{k_{p}-1}+\cdots+2^{0})

\displaystyle=2^{k_{p}}+2^{k_{p+1}}+\cdots+2^{k_{T}}-(2^{k_{p}}-1)

\displaystyle\geq 2^{k_{p+1}}.

Since
|k-l|\leq 2^{n-m}
, it follows that
k_{p+1}\leq n-m
, and hence
(
B.40
) holds.

Next we prove (
B.41
). By maximality of
M
, the block

2^{l_{p}}+2^{l_{p+1}}+\cdots+2^{l_{p+M-1}}

is exactly

2^{k_{p}-1}+2^{k_{p}-2}+\cdots+2^{k_{p}-M}.

Therefore

\displaystyle|k-l|

\displaystyle=2^{k_{p}}+\cdots+2^{k_{T}}-\bigl(2^{k_{p}-1}+\cdots+2^{k_{p}-M}\bigr)-\sum_{j=p+M}^{R}2^{l_{j}}.

If
p+M>R
, then the last sum is empty, so

|k-l|\geq 2^{k_{p}-M}.

If
p+M\leq R
, then by maximality of
M
we have

l_{p+M}\leq k_{p}-M-2
, and hence

\sum_{j=p+M}^{R}2^{l_{j}}\leq 2^{k_{p}-M-2}+2^{k_{p}-M-3}+\cdots+2^{0}=2^{k_{p}-M-1}-1.

Thus in either case,

|k-l|\geq 2^{k_{p}-M-1}.

Since
|k-l|\leq 2^{n-m}
, we obtain

k_{p}-M\leq n-m+1,

which proves (
B.41
).

Finally, if
p+M\leq R
, then by maximality of
M
,

l_{p+M}\leq k_{p}-M-2.

Combining this with (
B.41
) gives

l_{p+M}\leq n-m-1\leq n-m+1,

which proves (
B.42
).
∎

Lemma B.5
.

For
M\geq 1
, we have

\sum_{j=1}^{M}j2^{-j}=2-2^{-M}(M+2).

Proof.

Indeed, recall that

\sum_{j=0}^{M}x^{j}=\frac{1-x^{M+1}}{1-x}.

Differentiating both sides and then multiplying by
x
, yields

\sum_{j=1}^{M}jx^{j}=x\frac{\Big(-(M+1)x^{M}(1-x)+(1-x^{M+1})\Big)}{(1-x)^{2}}.

Substituting
x=2^{-1}
, the identity follows.

We first dispose of the endpoint cases. If one of
x,y
equals

,

By symmetry in
