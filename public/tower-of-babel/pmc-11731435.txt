Investigating the effect of yoga-based educational interventions on the volume and composition of breast milk in premature infant’s mothers admitted to neonatal intensive care unit: A randomized controlled trial
Authors: Fatemeh Karimi, Parvin Aziznejadroshan, Zahra Akbarian Rad, Hossein-Ali Nikbakht, Durdi Qujeq
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:This study examined the effects of yoga-based educational interventions on the volume and composition of breast milk in premature infants’ mothers admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU).MATERIALS AND METHODS:A randomized controlled trial was conducted on 78 primiparous mothers whose premature infants were less than 34 weeks and were hospitalized in the NICU of Ayatollah Rouhani Hospital from February 2021 to November 2022. Mothers were assigned to a control group and an experimental group, that is, yoga, using the block randomization method. Mothers in the experimental group performed yoga for 30 minutes under the supervision of a yoga therapist every morning for six consecutive days. The volume of the mother’s milk was measured using an electric pump, while its composition was assessed using a spectrophotometer according to the instructions of the Pars Azmoun Standard Kit.RESULTS:The results of the analysis on the remaining 76 samples showed a difference in the means of the volume of breast milk after the intervention with 11.39 and 33.84 ml for the control and yoga group, respectively.there was a significant difference between the amount of breast milk in the control and yoga groups (P < 0.001). Also, the average difference in the amount of breast milk compounds was not significantly different in any of the cases.CONCLUSIONS:The yoga technique is effective in increasing the volume of breast milk in mothers with premature infants. Therefore, it is recommended to increase the volume of breast milk in the NICU.

Introduction

According to the definition of the World Health Organization (WHO), babies who are born before the 37th week from the first day of the last menstrual period are considered premature.[1] 15 million premature babies are born in the world annually, which is more than 10% of all the babies who are born.[2]

Premature birth is one of the causes of infant deaths, and it is the second cause of death in children under 5 years of age after pneumonia.[3] Clinical and scientific advances in the field of neonatal medicine have continuously improved the survival of premature infants, especially those with low birth weight. One of the main goals is the early and sufficient feeding of premature infants.[4] Human milk has been recommended as an optimal and exclusive source of primary nutrients for infants, which includes strong immune factors and unique combinations of nutrients that play a role in providing the growth and development needs of infants, especially premature ones.[5] The importance of breastfeeding is associated with a decrease in the prevalence of late-onset sepsis, necrotizing enterocolitis, chronic lung disease, premature retinopathy, shorter length of hospitalization, and reduction in rehospitalization. These benefits are related to natural breastfeeding and the transfer of maternal antibodies through milk.[6]

In low- and middle-income countries, only 37% of children are exclusively breastfed in the first 6 months of life.[7] The study of Naja F et al. (2022)[8] showed that the prevalence of exclusive breastfeeding in Qatar (based on a telephone questionnaire) was 26% in 2019. In the study of Baba Ahmadi et al. (2021),[9] the prevalence of exclusive breastfeeding in the first 6 months of a baby’s life is 49% based on a meta-analysis in Iran. At the 65th World Health Assembly, WHO members set and stated the goals for 2025, when at least 50% of infants under 6 months are supposed to be exclusively breastfed.[10]

In the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU), mothers’ inability to have enough contact with their infants for breastfeeding, insomnia, stress, and anxiety negatively affects breastfeeding and hormonal factors.[11] Successful exclusive feeding is strongly influenced by the amount of milk production. The decrease in milk production in the first days after birth is considered an obstacle to breastfeeding.[12] Mothers of premature infants are three times more at risk of insufficient milk production than those of term infants.[13] Milk production in mothers of premature infants and exclusive feeding can be affected by various factors, such as stress, health problems, delay in breastfeeding, and being away from the infant.[4] The secretion of prolactin for milk synthesis and oxytocin for milk secretion is required for successful lactation. Anxiety and stress of the mother prevent the release of oxytocin and prolactin. These two hormones cause milk to flow from the milk ducts to the nipple cells, so when the mother is relaxed, this can lead to an increase in milk ejection.[14] In recent years, non-pharmacological methods have focused on increasing the prolactin secretion of mothers with premature infants and increasing breast milk production.[11] There are several ways to increase breast milk, one of which is doing yoga. Yoga is a unique combination of movements useful for improving physical health, respiration, and relaxation, which can provide mental peace.[15] Yoga movements can improve the functioning of endocrine glands and blood circulation in the body and brain.[16]

In the study of Wildan et al. (2011),[15] it was shown that after performing yoga for six consecutive days, there was an increase in the average milk production per lactation in lactating mothers 1–6 months after giving birth. Also, the research results of Dabas et al. (2019)[13] indicated that the use of the relaxation method plays an important role in reducing stress and anxiety and improving milk production. However, Varisoglu’s study showed that music had no effect on increasing the volume of breast milk and there was no statistically significant difference between the intervention and control groups.[17]

Material and Methods

Study design and setting

This randomized controlled clinical trial was performed on 78 primiparous mothers whose preterm infants (under 34 weeks) were born during a 10-month period from February 2021 to November 2022 in the NICU of Ayatollah Rouhani Hospital in Babol, Iran.

Study participants and sampling

Primiparous mothers were randomly assigned to one of the two study groups, that is, control (N = 39) and experimental (yoga; N = 39), using a computer random number generator and a strategy of permuted blocks with a size of 6.

The sample size was specified according to the objectives of the study. Informed by the study of Wildan et al.[15] regarding the effect of yoga interventions on increasing the volume of breast milk, taking into account the comparison of the average and the standard deviation difference (effect size 0.5), the confidence level of 95%, and the power of 80% study, and using G Power software version 3[18] with a two-sided assumption, 34 people are required in each group, which means a total of 68 people for two groups; given the ten percent dropout, 78 people were determined as the final sample size.

Randomization and blinding

Primiparous mothers were randomly assigned to one of the two study groups, that is, control (N = 39) and experimental (yoga; N = 39), using a computer random number generator and a strategy of permuted blocks with a size of 6. To maintain randomization during the study, the allocation concealment method was performed. Thus, to which group the next person would be assigned was not known. They were only consecutively revealed in the final expulsion phase by the study monitor assigned to the center. The first author approached the participants.

The blinding of nurses who implemented the intervention was not possible given the nature of the intervention. All outcomes in this study were measured through laboratory instruments. Due to the characteristics of the study intervention, blinding was applied only to a group of researchers who were laboratory evaluators and data analysts. In the case of incomplete blinding, the authors made sure that the outcome was not likely to be influenced by a lack of blinding. This study followed the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines for reporting randomized controlled trials [Figure 1].

CONSORT diagram

Regarding the inclusion criteria, primiparous mothers were selected based on the hospitalization time of premature infants (gestational age (GA) <34 weeks) in the NICU of Ayatollah Rouhani Hospital. Interventions were performed for mothers on days 4–6 after delivery.

The mother’s willingness to breastfeed and the possibility of the mother’s presence for breastfeeding at least once a day in the hospital were taken into account.

The exclusion criteria were mothers with a history of exercise or yoga in the past 3 months, refusal to participate, absence of the mother even for 1 day during the 6 days of the study, infant death during the study, infants with GA >34 weeks, drug abuse or oral galactagogues, and mother–child dyads with known contraindications for breastfeeding.

Data collection tool and technique

Mothers in the control group, who were on days 4–6 postpartum, participated in a routine breastfeeding training class taught by a skilled nurse working in the NICU. First, they washed their hands and sat on a comfortable chair in the breastfeeding room. How the electric breast pump works was explained in the ward for the mothers of both groups. As the quality of breast milk changes during the day and the amount of milk fat is lower in the morning, this process takes place around noon as the stability of milk fat is higher at this time.[19] Moreover, this time period was compatible with mothers’ training time. For all mothers in the control group, milk was collected by an electric breast pump after the end of the breastfeeding routine training and measured in milliliters in a container under the supervision of the first researcher; the volume of milk was recorded in the questionnaire.

The mothers of the yoga group, who spent 4–6 days after giving birth, were asked to go to a quiet and peaceful room equipped for training yoga relaxation techniques from the first to sixth sessions by the yoga instructor; the techniques include the following:

The primary objective of this study was to examine the effect of yoga on breast milk volume in mothers of premature infants. Breast milk was collected on the first and sixth days of the study around noon (11:30–12:00) using a German-made mamivac electric breast pump. The volume of mother’s milk was measured by the first researcher and recorded in the questionnaire in both groups using a Mandala glass container (capacity 100 cc) made in Switzerland based on milliliters.

The secondary aim was to determine the effect of yoga-based educational interventions on fat, total protein, albumin, calcium, and phosphorus in the breast milk of mothers with premature infants. For this purpose, two milliliters of breast milk was collected on the first and sixth days of the study from both groups and transferred to the Biochemistry Laboratory. The biochemical indicators of breast milk were compared in terms of fat (cholesterol and triglyceride), albumin, total protein, calcium, and phosphorus using a spectrophotometer made in the UK model (UNICO 9344) with accurate mg/DL measurement; this was performed by a permanent expert of the laboratory blindly according to the instructions of the Pars Azmoun Standard Kit.

All outcomes in this study were measured by laboratory instruments. Due to the characteristics of the study intervention, blinding was applied only to a group of researchers who were laboratory evaluators and data analysts.

Data analysis

The descriptive information of the patients was reported using the central index of the mean and the dispersion index of the standard deviation or in the form of numbers and percentages. To check the related statistical tests, first, the normality of the data was checked using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test; if it was normal, parametric tests, for example, paired t-test, were used.

When the data were not normal, Wilcoxon’s test was used to check the desired results in groups and also to check the relationship between a quantitative variable in two dependent situations. Next, the independent t-test was used to compare the average of variables with two categories of qualitative variables. Also, the chi-square test was used to check the relationship between two qualitative variables. If the conditions of the chi-square test were not appropriate, Fisher’s test was used. Also, to compare the composition of breast milk after the intervention in the two groups, and to consider the sizes before the intervention, the analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) test was considered. All analyses were performed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software version 23. A significant level was considered for all analyses (P < 0.05). The infant’s demographic information (fetal age, birth weight, and infant’s weight on the first day of study) and the mother’s characteristics (age and weight) were collected from the hospital records.

Results

The total number of mothers who gave birth in the 10 months from February 2021 to November 2022 was 1,510, but the total number of mothers whose infants were admitted to the NICU was 246. Of 246 mothers, 168 mothers were excluded from the study. Finally, 76 mothers, that is, 38 people in the control group and 38 people in the yoga group, were analyzed [Figure 1].

There was no significant difference between the two groups in terms of maternal age, infant’s birth weight, fetal age, maternal weight, and infant’s weight on the first day of the study [Table 1].

Discussion

Based on the findings of this research, yoga-based educational interventions increased the volume of breast milk in mothers with premature infants hospitalized in the NICU. Although the difference in the mean values of breast milk factors, including triglyceride, calcium, and phosphorus, was not statistically significant, it was significant in terms of quantity; however, it had no effect on other breast milk compounds, such as cholesterol, albumin, and total protein. A study by Wildan et al. (2011)[15] entitled “the benefits of yoga in increasing milk production in lactating mothers with infants of 1-6 months” showed that on the sixth day the volume of mothers’ milk significantly increased after starting yoga movements; in addition, as yoga can help to calm the mind of lactating mothers and increase their self-confidence, it had an effect on breastfeeding and infant care. The results of that study are in line with those of the present, yet they differ both in terms of the control group and the target group.

A study was also conducted by Dabas et al. (2019)[13] under the title “the effect of sound relaxation method on stress and anxiety and milk output among postpartum mothers of hospitalized neonates: A randomized controlled trial.” In this study, the intervention group used the sound relaxation method under the guidance of yoga therapy, and the control group received the usual care. The findings showed a significant reduction in maternal stress and anxiety scales and an increase in milk output in the intervention group compared with that of the control group. The result of this study showed that the use of relaxation techniques with the help of sound had a significant role in reducing stress and anxiety and improving milk ejection; this is in line with the result of this study.

SefidHaji et al. (2022)[20] conducted a study entitled “effect of lullaby on volume, fat, total protein and albumin concentration of breast milk in premature infants’ mothers admitted to NICU: A randomized controlled trial.” This study showed that the volume and composition of breast milk, including fat, total protein, and albumin, were greater when mothers with premature infants listened to the mother’s lullaby along with the mother looking at the photograph of her own baby instead of merely listening to the mother’s lullaby by the mother. The results of Sefidhaji et al.’s study are consistent with those of our study in terms of increasing the volume of breast milk. However, the findings were not significant in terms of the composition of breast milk in our study, unlike those of Sefidhaji et al.

Boybay and Yayan (2022)[21] examined the effect of postpartum yoga on breastfeeding self-sufficiency and maternal attachment in primiparous mothers. Their study showed a significant increase in breastfeeding self-sufficiency and improvement in maternal attachment in the intervention group; in this respect, it is consistent with our study that used the effect of yoga as an independent variable. The difference between the two, however, was that they investigated its effect on self-sufficiency in breastfeeding and the improvement of maternal attachment instead of measuring the effect of yoga on the quality and quantity of mothers’ milk.

Varişoğlu et al. (2020)[17] conducted a study entitled “the effects of listening to music on breast milk production by mothers of premature newborns in the neonatal intensive care unit: A randomized controlled study.” The results showed that there was no difference between the music group and the control group in terms of breast milk production. However, the final cortisol levels of the music group were significantly lower compared with those in pretest measurements. Also, the anxiety level score in the intervention group was statistically significant compared with that of the control group. The results of their study were different from those of the current study. This difference is probably due to the different working methods between the two studies; they include the duration of the intervention, that is, 3 days in Varişoğlu’s intervention and 6 days in our study, as well as the intervention time of 15 minutes in Varişoğlu’s study and 20 minutes in our study.

Limitation and recommendation

We would like to emphasize that our study has some limitations. In this study, ANCOVA was performed to adjust the baseline values and other variables in the study. Also, given the small number of target groups in this study, it is suggested that future studies be conducted with a larger sample size. We acknowledge the limitations of conducting a single-center trial.

Conclusion

The findings of this research showed that yoga-based educational interventions led to an increase in the volume of breast milk in mothers with premature infants admitted to the NICU. It is expected that health workers hold a yoga-based exercise program for breastfeeding mothers and provide motivation to lactating mothers to follow yoga as one of the alternatives that can be performed to facilitate the production of breast milk.

Ethical consideration

This study was conducted with the aim of determining the effect of educational interventions in yoga on the volume, fat, total protein, albumin, calcium, and phosphorus of breast milk in mothers of premature infants admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) (Ayatollah Rouhani Teaching Hospital, Babol, Iran). This study was approved by the university research ethics committee with the code of ethics of IR.MUBABOL.HRI.REC.1400.169, and the number of Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (IRCT) at the National Registry of Clinical Trials is IRCT20220104053619N1.
