Assessment of patient preferences in digital health trials: procedure development and implementation in a preference-based trial
Authors: Jürgen Barth, Anita V Thomae, Anna K Tietjen, Claudia M Witt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo develop and evaluate a standardised, fully automated assessment procedure for patient treatment preferences suitable for use in randomised controlled trials (RCTs) with remote or digital health interventions.DesignMultiphase developmental study followed by implementation in a fully randomised preference trial (FRPT).SettingUniversity and outpatient cancer care settings in Switzerland; implementation in RCTs with a remote digital health intervention.Participants50 medical students for pilot testing; 11 patients with cancer for qualitative testing; 124 adult patients with cancer experiencing fatigue or sleep problems in an FRPT with a remote digital health intervention.Primary and secondary outcome measuresPrimary outcome: feasibility and applicability of a standardised digital preference assessment procedure. Secondary outcomes: distribution and strength of treatment preferences, reasons for preference.ResultsA procedure with three items for assessing treatment preferences was developed and refined based on pilot testing, stakeholder meetings and patient interviews. The stepwise procedure ensured that study participants with an initially unclear preference were finally able to report their preference. The majority of students (91.7%) indicated their preference already in the first assessment step. Similar in the RCT phase, 110 of 124 participants (88.7%) indicated their preference in the first step. Preference drivers included interest, perceived ease and conceptual appeal in addition to expected treatment benefits.ConclusionsOur fully automated standardised preference assessment with three items appears feasible in trials with digital health interventions. Future trials with two active treatment conditions should consider implementation of such a preference assessment to evaluate non-specific treatment effects driven by the match of patients’ preferences with the respective treatment.Trial registration numbersDRKS00035596; DRKS00035541; SNCTP000006209.

Introduction

Patient preferences can modify treatment effects in both randomised controlled trials (RCTs) and clinical practice. Multiple meta-analyses of RCTs have demonstrated that treatment effects are larger when patients were allocated according to their preferences compared with being assigned to non-preferred treatment with standardised mean difference (SMD) of about SMD 0.20 to SMD 0.30.1 2 One proposed explanation of this additional effect is the resentful demoralisation in patients receiving a treatment contrary to their preference. Such participants may perceive the assignment as unfair, potentially leading to lower adherence to the study intervention.3

There are established clinical designs to specifically investigate the impact of patient preferences on clinical outcomes in trials.4 In partially randomised patient preference trials (PRPTs) only those participants without preference are randomised, while those with a preference receive their preferred treatment.5 Doubly randomised preference trials employ a two-stage randomisation: in the first stage, patients are either randomised to a choice or no-choice condition. In the second stage, those participants in the choice condition receive their preferred treatment, while those in the no-choice condition were allocated randomly to the respective treatments.6 In fully randomised preference trials (FRPTs), patients initially state their preference but are then randomised regardless of their preference.7

Assessing patient preferences is challenging, and we identified four considerations for developing a preference assessment procedure: (a) the amount of information about treatments provided before the assessment, (b) the format of response options, (c) the underlying concept of preference and (d) the extent of individual human support offered before the preference assessment.

The first consideration is the amount of information provided prior to the preference assessment. In clinical trials, informed consent forms typically offer a basic description of the treatment and are followed by the preference assessment. However, a systematic review about FRPT reports that, beyond the basic information, about half of the studies provided patients with additional detailed descriptions of the treatments before the preference assessment8 to allow a sound preference statement.

The second consideration is the format of response categories. Including a ‘no response’ or ‘neither/nor’ option is appropriate for PRPT as participants selecting this option would be allocated to the study via randomisation. In FRPT, however, a ‘no response’ category does not permit an analysis of the impact of preferences on treatment outcomes. Nevertheless, some studies included this response option,911 proposing two strategies: (a) categorising such participants as ‘match’ with either treatment, assuming the absence of a strong preference for a specific treatment or (b) creating a separate category (match, mismatch and no preference) in the statistical model. Some other studies used scales and applied arbitrary cut-offs to create dichotomous preference categories.12 Such an approach has certain limitations, as participants might exceed the cut-off for both treatments or may have very similar ratings for both treatments.

The third consideration is how the concept of preference is defined. Some authors infer preference by asking patients about the expected treatment benefit for each treatment.9 10 13 While this captures outcome expectations,14 it may not fully represent patient preferences, which also consider factors like required effort, perceived fit with past experiences or personal values. One example with a broader scope is the Treatment Acceptability and Preference (TAP) (ie, including logic of the treatment, individual suitability, effectiveness and convenience).15 While the TAP has excellent psychometric properties, the categorical preference assessment did not overcome the problem that individuals may lack a clear preference. This resulted in about one quarter of study participants without a clear preference.

The fourth consideration concerns the extent of individual human support in the preference assessment procedure. Some procedures include treatment providers in supporting patients’ decisions by explaining different treatment options.1618 Others rely on specific standardised descriptions about the treatments without any human support.9

In the context of fully remote digital health trials, there are specific challenges since patients lack the opportunity to discuss treatment options with the study personnel. This may partially explain why only a few digital health trials have employed preference designs since then2022 with a considerable variety between assessment procedures and a lack of details in the description, which limits reproducibility.

Therefore, this study aimed to develop a digital standardised assessment procedure for patient preferences that ensures the identification of a preferred option and can be used in fully remote preference trials across a variety of settings and treatments. Second, the study aimed to evaluate the application of the procedure in a fully remote digital health intervention RCT. We describe the entire development process and important changes on the way to the final preference assessment procedure. For a successful application in a digital health intervention trial our assessment procedure should follow these principles: (1) the preference assessment procedure must work without requiring participants to seek additional information; (2) the description about the treatment options given in advance should be balanced in terms of persuasiveness; (3) the preference assessment procedure must result in a preference for one treatment option and should facilitate the decision process by guiding the patient through a structured, stepwise process. The option ‘no preference’ should be excluded as it is not compatible with implementation in an FRPT and (4) the preference assessment should be conducted in a self-report format.

Methods

Based on our literature search, a first version of the preference assessment was developed. This initial preference assessment procedure was tested and applied in three study phases and revised in each phase (see figure 1). Phase 1: testing in a medical student population and collecting feedback from a stakeholder advisory board, Phase 2: iterative testing and refinement of the preference assessment with patients with cancer and medical students and Phase 3: application of a final version of the preference assessment procedure in an FRPT.

Phase 1: test with medical students and feedback from advisory board

In this phase, we aimed to evaluate the feasibility and applicability of the preference assessment procedure (version 1) with three subsequent items (online supplemental 1). The first two items included besides the two treatment options also the option ‘I do not know’, whereas the third item offered only the two treatments as response options. Moreover, the quality and the persuasiveness of the written information about the treatments provided prior to the preference assessment were evaluated. We conducted a cross-sectional pilot study within a student course (September to December 2022). A total of 50 medical students of the University of Zurich (third-year Bachelor and first-year Master) self-enrolled in the elective course ‘Complementary Medicine’. All participants voluntarily selected this course, were informed about the pilot study and agreed to the collection of data (online supplemental 2). On the eLearning platform OpenLMS a case vignette depicting a cancer patient experiencing cancer-related fatigue (CRF) was presented to the students followed by the preference assessment to be completed by each student. Students were asked to employ the perspective of the patient in the case vignette while completing the preference assessment. They could indicate their preference between two available treatment options for CRF, namely acupressure or mindfulness, after receiving a short written information about acupressure and mindfulness. The short description of the two available treatment options included statements on their evidence base and working mechanisms. Descriptions were similar in tonality, length (about 120 words) and covered the same topics (effort for patients, description of the intervention, dosage, evidence). Students evaluated the quality of the arguments (on a 5-point numeric rating scale ranging from ‘very bad’ to ‘very good’) and the persuasiveness of arguments in the written information (on a 5-point numeric rating scale ranging from ‘not at all convincing’ to ‘very convincing’). The information was intended to have similar scores for both treatment options.

The feasibility of the preference assessment was evaluated by analysing the responses to the sequential three items. Good feasibility was assumed if a large proportion of participants would indicate their preference without forcing a decision at item 1 or 2, indicating that the assessment was easy to use.

An online meeting with the project team and five persons from the stakeholder advisory board (two health professionals, one researcher and two people with cancer experience as patient or relative) was conducted on 17 November 2022, to collect feedback about version 1 of the preference assessment. After a presentation of the findings from the student pilot study the stakeholder advisory board was engaged in a discussion about the preference assessment procedure. The moderators (JB and CMW) encouraged any critical feedback. The feedback was integrated into the preference assessment as well leading to version 2 (online supplemental 1).

Phase 2: test with patient population and medical students

In this second phase, the assessment procedure version 2 was tested with patients to evaluate the construct validity, usability and the reasoning process during completion (see version 2 in online supplemental 1). We conducted two rounds of think aloud online interviews in German language. The interviews were conducted by JB or AKT, two researchers with a background in either psychology or public health. In the first round conducted in August 2022, we interviewed two patients from the stakeholder advisory board. In the second round, we conducted interviews with cancer patients. A convenience sampling strategy was applied. Patients were approached for the interview (1) via the institute’s website and (2) by staff in an outpatient clinic for complementary and integrative medicine. Patients from the outpatient clinic had already an established relationship with our institution but had not been in contact with the interviewers before.

All interested participants received detailed study information about the interview procedures via email (online supplemental 3). Patients who agreed to participate were scheduled for an online interview conducted via Microsoft Teams. To simulate actual trial conditions, the full preference assessment procedure (written information on treatment options (online supplemental 4) and the preference assessment version 2 (online supplemental 1) was implemented in a digital format on the eLearning platform Open LMS. After providing oral informed consent, participants were given fictitious login credentials to access the Open LMS platform in order to ensure their anonymity on the platform. Participants were then asked to share their screen with the interviewer. Before proceeding to the assessment, participants received background information on the study and instructions for the interview procedure on the Open LMS platform. The interview guideline is available in online supplemental 5. After clarifying any remaining questions with the study participants, the preference assessment procedure was started. Participants first received written information about the two available treatment options, followed by the preference assessment items. Using a think-aloud approach, patients were encouraged to verbalise their thoughts while reading the written information and responding to the three items of the preference assessment. The interviewer asked follow-up questions to further explore participants’ reasoning and to understand differences in response patterns (ie, different responses about preferences between questions) (online supplemental 5). During the interviews, the interviewer took handwritten notes capturing participants’ understanding of the questions, difficulties encountered, and their thought processes when formulating responses. No audio or video recordings were made in order to foster a trusting and open interview atmosphere. The interviews lasted around 30 min.

After these interviews with the patients, we asked students to evaluate two different options of items 3 (online supplemental 6). 25 Medical students at the University of Zurich (third-year Bachelor and first-year Master), enrolled in the elective course ‘Placebo and Nocebo’, evaluated these options during a lecture on patient preferences (May 2024) using an anonymous mobile voting system (Mentimeter), following a brief introduction to the assessment procedure. The best option for item 3 was then implemented in the preference assessment and three patients were interviewed with think aloud similar to the initial testing. After that version 3 of the preference assessment was created.

Phase 3: final version of the preference assessment and application in an FRPT

In the last phase, we applied the preference assessment procedure (version 3 in online supplemental 1) in a fully digital FRPT with two intervention groups and an additional waitlist control group. The aim of this trial is to assess the effectiveness of acupressure and a mindfulness-based technique, delivered via an eLearning course, in alleviating fatigue and sleep problems, and to investigate whether patient preferences modify intervention effectiveness. Patients were randomised to receive either an eLearning course to learn a mindfulness-based technique (treatment A) or an eLearning course to learn acupressure (treatment B) or learn their preferred self care technique (waitlist control) after 6 weeks. The first two active treatment conditions can be considered as FRPT since patients were randomised independent of their preference. The waitlist condition can be considered as modified preference-based group since patients received their treatment of choice after the waiting period of 6 weeks. In the study patients with cancer (first or recurrent cancer diagnosis in the last 5 years, independent of stage and type of cancer) experiencing either fatigue or sleep problems were included.23

Procedure

Study participants registered themselves for the trial. The trial was announced via flyer, social media and healthcare providers. Recognising that our study and interventions may raise additional questions for interested patients, we provided a Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ) section on the study website (www.icanselfcare.ch). After providing informed consent (online supplemental 7) and the screening/eligibility questionnaire, the participants completed the standardised preference assessment procedure (version 3 in online supplemental 1) at baseline (preference for mindfulness-based technique vs acupressure). Participants also indicated the strength of their preference (‘How strong do you prefer acupressure over mindfulness’ and vice versa on a numeric rating scale from 0 (not at all) to 10 (very strong)) as well as reasons for their preference. Participants were randomised in a 1:1:1 ratio using block randomisation with variable block lengths, stratified for preference, into one of three groups (acupressure, MBT, waitlist). The sequence was generated in R (V.4.3.2) by a statistician, who was not further involved in the study. Participants were randomised disregarding their reported preference. In consequence, participants in the intervention groups had a 50% chance to receive their preferred treatment (match).

No post-intervention outcomes were assessed in this study; these will be evaluated as part of a larger clinical trial, as well as the impact of receiving a preferred vs non-preferred treatment in the two active treatment groups (excluding the waitlist). For this study, we used baseline data about Quality of Life (FACT G7),24 digital skills, prior experiences with acupressure or mindfulness, duration of cancer, sleep problems or fatigue, and sociodemographic variables.

Analysis

For the evaluation of the feasibility of the preference assessment version 1 as well as the evaluation of the argument quality and persuasiveness of arguments, we used descriptive statistics (phase 1). The cognitive interviews with patients were summarised in a written way per patient, summarised thematically across patients and the research team discussed consequences of the interviews for the revision of the items (phase 2).25 A critical aspect in the feedback of patients was the coherence between responses to the items of the preference assessment. Differences in response patterns were considered as sign to modify a question to capture similar content like other questions. No formal power analysis was conducted since no formal coding was foreseen. Our main interest was to identify major problems in three items and we would have about 80% power with 10 patients to detect major problems with a prevalence of 15%.26 In phase 3, descriptive statistics of baseline characteristics for the patient population from the preference trial were provided (phase 3). For this explorative analysis, we aimed to include about 100 participants from the larger ICanSelfCare trial to evaluate the application of the preference assessment. No formal power calculation was performed for the explorative analysis, but the sample size allows to include a broad spectrum of patients (age, sex, etc). For the items of the preference assessment, counts and percentages were reported. The strength of the preference per treatment was analysed with descriptive statistics. For each self care technique, we presented the reasons for this preference with counts and percentages. All analyses were conducted with either SPSS 25.0 or R (2025.05.0+496)

Results

Phase 1: test with medical students and feedback from advisory board (version 1)

A total of 48 students completed the preference assessment and evaluated both the quality of the arguments and the persuasiveness of the provided description. Overall, 56.3% voted for acupressure as the preferred treatment option for the patient in the case vignette. The majority of students (91.7%) indicated their preference already in the first item, 6.3% in the second item, and one person in the last item. The quality of the arguments of the descriptions was perceived equal for both treatments with a mean score of 3.3±0.7 (mean±SD) for acupressure and 3.3±0.9 for mindfulness. The persuasiveness of the descriptions was also rated similarly between acupressure and mindfulness (acupressure 3.5±0.9 and mindfulness 3.2±0.9).

In summary, the procedure was applicable in the scenario of the student course. Advisory board members supported the stepwise preference assessment, noting its appropriateness even though only a minority of students have selected ‘I do not know’ in response to the first item. Advisory board members nevertheless supported the use of an ‘I do not know’ response option in the first two items of the preference assessment.

Based on further discussions, the items of version 1 were refined by using the term ‘learning’ as a treatment in all items for a better fit with the eLearning environment of the trial. We also replaced the term ‘therapy’ by ‘self care technique’ to emphasise that patients will practice on their own and the treatment will not be received as passive intervention. We also decided to reverse the perspective in item 3 (recommendation from a friend vs recommendation to a friend) to capture the preference of the person filling in the assessment.

Phase 2: testing with patient population and students (version 2)

11 patients were interviewed (age range 38–58, nine females, two males, max. duration of the interview 30 min). The patients from the Stakeholder Advisory Board (SAB) supported the idea of including an ‘I don’t know’ response option to avoid negative reactions caused by forcing a choice. They also noted that preferences may be driven by curiosity rather than a belief in the superiority of a specific treatment.

In the think aloud interviews patients’ feedback was integrated to version 2 of the preference assessment after six interviews as one major issue was identified. A notable finding was that patients expressed a different preference in the third item (‘What would you recommend to a friend with similar complaints’) compared with the first two when all items were presented. Patients explained this discrepancy by perceiving the third item as distinct from the preceding questions. They further explained that the term ‘friend’ implies a specific individual. However, treatment recommendations depend on individual characteristics which can vary across different ‘friends’. They also found the item misleading since patients would be more likely to recommend to friends a treatment they already tried rather than a novel treatment, but would prefer a novel treatment for themselves to extend their experiences based on their curiosity. Moreover, they found it difficult to come to a general statement since recommendations might differ depending on the specific friends’ characteristics and needs. In consequence, item 3 was inconsistent with the objective to capture patient’s own preference, as reflected in the first two items.

Two newly developed alternative options for a new third item (online supplemental 1) were tested in a student population and 17 out of 25 students voted for the item ‘Imagine that the computer will now randomly determine which self care technique you would learn: Which result would you prefer?’ as stronger related to the other two items. Another finding from the interviews was that patients prefer visuals aids and concise summaries to better understand the two treatment options. In response, we integrated a table in the written information provided that contrasts the two treatment options side by side. Three additional interviews were then conducted to pilot version 3 of the preference assessment. All patients had no difficulties in responding to the three questions of the preference assessment and they responded in the same pattern to each item. The new item and the additional table were then implemented in the revised preference assessment (version 3.0, online supplemental 1). No further modifications were made to the preference assessment procedure.

Discussion

In this study, we systematically developed a preference assessment procedure based on previous preference trials and with the aim to transfer it into the context remote digital health intervention trials. By involving stakeholders, we improved the initial version of our assessment procedure and implemented it successfully in a clinical trial. The final version of the fully-automated standardised assessment procedure ensures the identification of a preferred option and seems well suited for a variety of other treatments and digital health settings since the items can be easily adapted to any preference trial.

Previous research showed that high health literacy goes along with the wish to become involved in the clinical decision making prior to a treatment.27 However, this does not necessarily imply that patients with low health literacy have no preference, since preferences are not only based on rational considerations but also affected by many other psychological constructs (ie, coping style, health beliefs, personality).28 This suggests preferences do not solely reflect considerations of the most beneficial treatment in a specific situation (ie, expectation14) but also individuals’ considerations about their perceived fit to the treatment. As shown in our study, judgements about preferences are based on concepts like curiosity or convincingness of the treatment concept as shown in figure 3.

The study has some limitations that should be acknowledged. In the absence of similar stepwise assessment procedures, we developed new items specifically for this purpose. This approach can be challenged since many decisions must be taken. However, we are confident that our stepwise development process combined with stakeholder involvement and pre-testing resulted in a robust and useful instrument. A second limitation are the characteristics of our patient population. Many of them have a higher education and digital skills were quite good. A replication of our assessment procedure in other vulnerable groups with lower health literacy is therefore needed.

Conclusion

Our carefully developed automated standardised preference assessment appears feasible in trials with digital health interventions and guides the patient to identify a preferred option. Future trials with two active treatment conditions should consider implementation of such a preference assessment to evaluate non-specific treatment effects driven by the match of patients’ preferences and the respective treatment.
