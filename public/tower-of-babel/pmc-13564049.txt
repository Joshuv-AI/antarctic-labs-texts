Nontuberculous mycobacterial infections in the setting of immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy: A case series
Authors: Joelle N Friesen, Cassandra Braun, Ashley Egan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractImmune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have revolutionized cancer therapy. While ICIs have shown efficacy across a range of malignancies, they have also been associated with immune-related adverse events (irAEs) and there is increasing concern that ICIs may predispose patients to opportunistic infections. We report six cases of clinically significant nontuberculous mycobacterial (NTM) infections occurring during or after ICI therapy. Key findings include the prevalence of underlying lung disease in patients diagnosed with NTM infection (including primary pulmonary malignancy or metastases), predominance of anti-PD-1/PD-L1 exposure in our cohort, wide range of NTM species identified, wide temporal windows between ICI therapy and NTM infection, and overall good outcomes after NTM-directed therapy with four of five patients who received NTM-directed therapy alive at one year after NTM diagnosis. We recommend that clinicians maintain a high index of suspicion for NTM infections in the differential for patients who have received ICI therapy and we recommend ongoing investigation into the potential correlation between ICI therapy and NTM infection.Keywords: Immune checkpoint inhibitor, ICI, Nontuberculous mycobacterial infection, NTM

Introduction

Cancer therapy has been revolutionized by immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), first FDA-approved in early 2011. These agents target PD-1, PD-L1, CTLA-4, and LAG-3 to enhance T-cell-mediated tumor destruction. ICIs are now widely utilized across a range of malignancies with proven efficacy, albeit potential for immune-related adverse events (irAEs) which result from dysregulated immune activation and can affect nearly any organ system. Approximately 40 % of patients receiving anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapy and approximately 60 % receiving anti-CTLA-4 therapy experience irAE(s) with varying severity [1,2]. These irAEs are typically treated with immunosuppressive regimens.

There is increasing concern that ICIs may predispose patients to infection through a variety of mechanisms. Two mechanisms have been proposed: 1) infections related to immunosuppression and 2) infections related to heightened immune dysregulation [3]. The incidence of infection while taking ICIs is generally reported in the 18–27% range and includes a broad range of organisms including bacterial, viral, and fungal pathogens [[4], [5], [6], [7]].

There are emerging anecdotal reports of increased development or reactivation of mycobacterial infections during or after ICI therapy [8,9]. This is supported by a study demonstrating that mice deficient in PD-1 are more sensitive to M. tuberculosis (MTB) and have subsequent increased mortality, potentially because the PD-1 pathway helps control inflammatory responses to MTB infection [10]. In M. tuberculosis (MTB) infection, PD-1 expression increases in multiple immune cell populations and disruption of PD-1-mediated regulation can impact cytokines such as TNF-α and IFN-γ, promoting mycobacterial proliferation [8,9,11].

Case reports increasingly suggest a relationship between ICI administration and non-tuberculous mycobacterial (NTM) infections as well, yet this relationship remains poorly understood although similar mechanisms to MTB infection may contribute. We describe the clinical characteristics, temporal relationships, and outcomes of NTM infections occurring during or after ICI therapy in a single-center case series.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a retrospective case series of adult patients at a large academic referral center from January 1, 2012 to December 31, 2024. Patients were included if they were ≥ 18 years old, received at least one ICI for malignancy, and had been diagnosed with an NTM infection during or after ICI therapy. Exclusion criteria included pregnancy, incomplete data, or microbiologic findings deemed clinically insignificant. NTM infection was considered clinically significant when positive cultures correlated with radiographic findings and/or symptoms as determined by pulmonology or infectious disease specialists. Patients could be seen inpatient, outpatient, or both settings. IRB approval was waived by our hospital committee and all cases were anonymized.

Data collection, outcomes, and analysis

Data extracted from the electronic medical record included demographics, pulmonary history, malignancy characteristics, ICI exposure, NTM details, and clinical outcomes.

Pulmonary history included smoking status, underlying lung disease, and pulmonary function tests within one year of ICI initiation when available.

Malignancy variables included cancer type, metastatic status, lung involvement, diagnosis date, and relapse status. ICI variables included agent class, immune-related toxicities, immunosuppressive treatment, concurrent oncologic therapies, and whether ICI therapy was held after NTM diagnosis.

Temporal variables included duration of ICI exposure before NTM diagnosis, time from ICI initiation to infection, time from ICI discontinuation to infection when applicable, and duration of NTM-directed therapy.

Outcomes included clinical response to infection treatment, survival at 90, 180, and 365 days, and cause of death.

Descriptive analyses were performed, including mean and range for continuous variables and frequencies (percentages) for categorical variables.

Results

Patient characteristics

Of 137 records reviewed, six clinically significant NTM cases during or after ICI therapy were identified. Exclusions included ruled-out NTM (n = 100), indeterminate QuantiFERON tests (n = 14), planned but uninitiated ICI therapy (n = 8), likely culture contaminants (n = 6), and inadequate data (n = 2).

Mycobacterial infection characteristics

None of the patients had a known history of prior NTM infection. Three were found to have M. abscessus, one had M. kansasii, one had M. chelonae, and one had M. avium complex and M. chimaera. Four had pulmonary involvement of infection; this included two with new FDG-avid lung consolidations, one with new bilateral bronchial wall thickening and nodular consolidation with groundglass opacities, and one with a new pleural effusion. Mean WBC at time of NTM diagnosis was 7.8 × 109/L (range 4–10 ×109/L); mean lymphocytes at time of NTM diagnosis were 1.35 × 109/L (range 0.60–2.20 ×109/L). All six patients had a diagnosis made through at least one positive mycobacterial culture; see Table 3 for details of sites involved.

Temporal characteristics

The average number of days of ICI therapy received prior to NTM diagnosis was 534 days (range 71–1583). The average number of days from ICI therapy initiation to NTM diagnosis was 871 (range 80–1991) and the average number of days from ICI discontinuation to NTM diagnosis was 182 (range 0–918). Of note, half of the patients had ICI therapy ongoing at the time of NTM diagnosis (0 days from discontinuation). Outcomes

Four patients had symptoms at the time of NTM diagnosis that were attributed at least in part to NTM infection; symptoms improved with treatment in all patients that were treated. Four patients had new chest CT findings that all improved after NTM treatment; the fifth was not treated and the sixth did not have new radiographic findings at the time of NTM diagnosis. NTM cultures were negative after treatment in both patients in whom they were checked after antibiotic completion. All patients were alive at 90 days after NTM diagnosis. Four patients were alive at 180 days and 365 days after infection diagnosis. Both deaths in our cohort were attributed to metastatic lung cancer. Outcomes are summarized in Table 4.

Discussion

In this single-center case series of adult patients with NTM infection during or after ICI therapy, we observed several key patterns: frequent underlying lung disease, predominance of anti-PD-1/PD-L1 exposure, diverse NTM species, and generally favorable outcomes after NTM-directed therapy.

Our cohort had a strong pattern of underlying lung disease. This included one-third of patients with known COPD, two-thirds with PFT abnormalities, two-thirds with prior lung radiation, and two-thirds with lung involvement of malignancy. Overall, five out of six patients had some form of underlying structural lung abnormality. Of those five, four had pulmonary involvement of NTM infection; the sixth patient without known underlying lung abnormality also had NTM pulmonary involvement. This is consistent with the general understanding that impaired lung function and/or altered lung architecture are predisposing factors for pathogenic mycobacterial infection [3,12]. This is also consistent with other case reports of NTM infections in the setting of ICI use; the lung is involved in the majority of cases [3,13]. These findings suggest that pre-existing structural lung abnormalities may represent a critical risk factor in the development of clinically significant NTM infection among patients receiving ICIs. One patient in our cohort had a line-associated NTM infection that was diagnosed via catheter-tip culture and exclusion of any other infectious causes of cellulitis which conversely serves as a reminder that not all NTM infections are pulmonary.

Amongst the six cases of NTM infection in the setting of ICI use, five involved use of anti-PD-1 therapy and all six were exposed to either anti-PD-1 or PD-L1 blockade. It is unclear whether this reflects increased susceptibility to mycobacterial infections when the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway is targeted compared with CTLA-4 or LAG-3 or whether it simply mirrors the higher frequency of anti-PD-1/PD-L1 ICI use in contemporary oncology practice, particularly in non-small cell lung cancer which affected half of our cohort. As previously mentioned, animal studies have suggested that blockade or genetic deficiency of the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway contributes to an exaggerated inflammatory response in the setting of M. tuberculosis infection; this effect may extend to NTM infections as well [10,14]. Disruption of the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway is hypothesized to result in maladaptive hyperinflammation that permits mycobacterial persistence and/or proliferation. There are not currently robust data demonstrating that CTLA-4 or LAG-3 inhibitors independently confer increased risk of clinically significant mycobacterial infections, although this may reflect limited study availability and/or lower utilization compared to PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors.

The mechanisms underlying predisposition to mycobacterial infection in the setting of ICI use remain widely debated. Studies conflict on whether immunosuppression administered for irAEs is the primary driver of infection risk or whether immune dysregulation intrinsic to ICIs is the primary underlying etiology. Interestingly, a narrative review from Vaddi et al. proposes that ICIs may confer a degree of protection against mycobacterial infections in patients with greater degrees of immunodeficiency while facilitating mycobacterial infection in those with more intact immune systems, hypothesizing that there is a goal median degree of immune system activation for optimal protection against mycobacterial infections and that ICIs can either help or hinder individual immune systems in finding this balance [11]. As outlined above, there is increasing discussion of opportunistic infections due to dysregulated immunity (ITI-DI). Del Castillo et al. performed a retrospective study in 2016 of over 700 patients who received ICI therapy and found that administration of corticosteroids and/or infliximab were statistically significant risk factors for development of infection [15]. This influenced the 2018 European Society of Clinical Microbiology and Infectious Disease consensus statement, which concluded that infections in the setting of ICI use were likely attributable to immunosuppressive therapy utilized for irAEs rather than mechanisms intrinsic to ICIs [16]. However, several subsequent studies have challenged this conclusion, reporting no statistically significant correlation between infection occurrence and immunosuppressive exposure in patients receiving ICI therapy [[4], [5], [6]]. In contrast, at least one further study identified a correlation between corticosteroid exposure and infection in patients receiving ICIs [7]. In our case series, half of patients had exposure to steroids and/or infliximab for at least three months prior to NTM diagnosis for the treatment of irAE(s), while the other half of patients had no documented immunosuppressive medication exposure. Of the patients who received chemotherapy and/or other targeted non-ICI therapy, only one had this ongoing at the time of NTM diagnosis and the others had completed their chemotherapy courses at least four months prior to NTM diagnosis, reducing the chance of immunosuppression from concomitant chemotherapy acting as a confounder for this case series. Although the small sample size precludes definitive conclusions, this distribution suggests that clinically significant NTM infection may arise in both immunosuppressed and non-immunosuppressed patients receiving ICIs. This observation supports a potential dual-mechanism framework in which infection risk may be mediated by secondary immunosuppression for irAE management and/or immune dysregulation directly induced by checkpoint inhibition. These mechanisms are likely not mutually exclusive and may interact in susceptible hosts, particularly those with underlying structural lung disease.

Five different NTM species were identified among the six patients in our study, representing greater diversity than many prior reports, in which M. avium complex and/or M. abscessus predominate [3,12,13]. M. abscessus was the most commonly identified species in our cohort, present in half of patients. The diversity of species identified suggests that susceptibility in the setting of ICI therapy is not limited to a single pathogen and that host-related vulnerability may play a larger role than organism-specific virulence.

Our data suggest no clear temporal window for NTM risk during or after ICI therapy. Although prior studies report most infections within one year of ICI initiation, the mean interval in our cohort exceeded two years (range 80–1991 days) [3,6,13]. Infection also occurred months to years after ICI discontinuation, suggesting potential prolonged susceptibility, although further investigation is needed. This widened temporal window for impact of ICIs is in line with data suggesting that onset and persistence of immune-related events secondary to ICI therapy may occur months or even years after the ICI is discontinued [[17], [18], [19]].

Overall, patients in our cohort had favorable clinical outcomes following NTM treatment. Five of six patients received antimycobacterial treatment, and all patients who completed at least one year of therapy per guidelines demonstrated improvement in symptoms and/or radiographic findings when present, and were alive at least 365 days after NTM diagnosis. Both deaths in our cohort were attributed to progressive metastatic lung cancer rather than infection. While these findings are reassuring, they should be interpreted cautiously given the small sample size and limited microbiologic follow-up. Repeat cultures following treatment completion were not obtained in several patients, limiting assessment of sustained microbiologic clearance.

Strengths included a high-volume ICI center, detailed longitudinal data, and strict criteria for clinically significant NTM infection. Limitations include small sample size, single-center design, predominantly White population, and retrospective methodology limiting causal inference. NTM infection can also be difficult to distinguish radiographically and clinically from cancer progression, pneumonitis, and ICI sarcoid-like reaction, requiring a high degree of suspicion and thorough diagnostic evaluation, likely leading to underdiagnosis of NTM infection in the setting of ICI use. Future studies would benefit from multi-center collaboration with larger sample sizes to better evaluate incidence and identify independent risk factors as well as development of registries to more systematically capture mycobacterial infections in patients receiving ICIs.

Conclusions

We present a series of six cases of NTM infections in the setting of ICI use. Key findings include prevalence of underlying lung disease, predominance of anti-PD-1/PD-L1 exposure, presence of both immunosuppressed and non-immunosuppressed patients, diverse NTM species, wide temporal window of infection, and overall good outcomes after NTM-directed therapy. We recommend clinicians maintain a high level of suspicion for NTM infection in patients with new symptoms and/or radiographic findings who have received ICI therapy regardless of ICI timing or duration and regardless of if they have also received immunosuppressive medications.
