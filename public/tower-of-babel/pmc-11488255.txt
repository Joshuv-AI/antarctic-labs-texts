The relationship between professional identity and professional development among special education teachers: a moderated mediation model
Authors: Yueming Pi, Minjie Ma, Aixin Hu, Tingzhao Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe professional development of special education teachers has received increasing attention in recent years, and identity has been identified as a protective factor in this context. However, only a limited amount of information is available regarding the potential mediators and moderators of this relationship. A major objective of this study was to determine (a) whether self-efficacy mediates the relationship between professional identity and professional development and (b) whether perceived social support moderates the indirect relationship between professional identity and professional development among special education teachers. A total of 2668 special education teachers participated in this study and completed measures relating to their professional identity, self-efficacy, perceived social support, and professional development. The study found that (1) professional identity is significantly and positively related to the professional development of special education teachers, while self-efficacy and perceived social support are similarly positively related to their professional development; (2) the relationship between professional identity and professional development is partially mediated by teachers' self-efficacy; and (3) perceived social support moderates the indirect effects of professional identity on special education teachers' professional development via self-efficacy. Based on the findings of this study, it appears that high perceived social support may be a protective factor concerning the professional development of special education teachers with low self-efficacy since these special education teachers appear to require more professional development.Keywords: Professional identity, Professional development, Special education teachers, Moderated mediation

Introduction

Special education teachers are professionals who perform their educational and teaching duties for the benefit of disabled children in schools, which means that their educational objects are disabled students, their teaching methods are unique, and their knowledge structure is diverse. As educators, special education teachers have a duty to ensure that the education of children with disabilities is of the highest caliber possible. Some studies have shown that special education teachers face the challenges of understaffing, low student–teacher ratios, attrition, and a lack of professional certifications, which makes it difficult for schools to employ highly qualified special education teachers to educate students with disabilities [1–3]. For example, 69,400 special education teachers provided educational services to 919,800 children with disabilities in China in 2021 [4]. The government expects special education teachers to provide appropriate education, assessment, intervention, and technical support to children with disabilities and has set higher professional standards for teaching in this context [5]. For example, one might cite the Special Education Teachers Professional Standards of China, the Special Education Professional Ethical Principles, and the Special Educator Preparation Standards of the United States of America. A special education professional learning background, internship experience in special education schools, and passing the recruitment exam held by the education bureau are now requirements for new special education teachers. Special education instructors will be under intense competition since their professional titles will be evaluated using the same criteria and grades as regular school teachers. Despite this fact, special education teachers face higher standards and challenges in the classroom, which can lead to stress and burnout due to overwork and a lack of appreciation, thus negatively affecting their sense of professional advancement and the quality of education they provide [6]. As a result, policy-makers, reformers, educators, and researchers are increasingly concerned about the professional transformation and development of special education teachers [7].

Professional development refers to teachers’ awareness of the need to continuously acquire teaching skills, expertise, values, and personal qualities to continually adapt to the education system and improve students’ learning abilities [8]. Furthermore, teachers should maintain a professional stance as they strive to improve the quality of their teaching and maintain a positive social reputation [9]. Therefore, professional development is necessary both for teachers to do their jobs and for students to grow [10]. Research has confirmed that effective professional development not only promotes special education teachers' teaching practices, professional learning, morale, and careers [11–13] but can also contribute to the academic achievement of children with disabilities as well as their attitudes toward general learning and vocabulary learning [14, 15]. Although a great deal of research has been conducted in this area over recent decades, specialized research on the professional development of special education teachers that focuses on the real-world needs, characteristics, and models of professional development for special education teachers remains scarce [16–18]. To inspire instruction and intervention efforts, it is necessary to gain a deeper understanding of the factors that influence special education teachers' professional development and the mechanisms underlying that influence. Research has found that social factors affect special education teachers' professional development due to a lack of systemic support, professional learning opportunities, inadequate resources, and structural constraints [19–21]. Professional development is also influenced by psychological factors such as the confidence of special education teachers, their interests, their attitudes, and the level of emotional and behavioral difficulties associated with the children with disabilities whom they teach. However, very few studies have examined the role of professional identity in the professional development of special education teachers [22].

Professional identity is defined as a complex cognitive structure in which teachers develop into professional actors based on their existing work experiences, which encompasses their professional interests, perceptions of teaching and student learning, and professional perspectives, to answer the questions "Who am I?" and "Who do I want to be?" [23, 24]. Special education teachers' positive educational beliefs, emotional attitudes, and professional value identity orientations are all represented by their professional identity, which are self-reflective and meaningful constructs that represent the educational practices that special education teachers use with students who have disabilities. The development of the personal and societal values, emotions, and conceptions required for teacher professionalization and professional learning have always depended on teachers' professional identities as they have always been vital to their continual learning, teaching, and self-improvement [25–27]. Based on this theory, numerous qualitative studies have confirmed that identity contributes to our understanding of teachers’ professional development and that a positive and stable professional identity is a protective factor concerning the professional learning and development of special educators [28, 29]. Special education teachers' work engagement and teaching practices are driven by learning and reflecting on knowledge, professional values, and multiple roles [30]. These studies have suggested that professional identity may play an important role in the professional development of special education teachers.

Numerous contributions have been made to the study of professional identity and professional development; however, the mediating and moderating mechanisms that underlie the relationship between special education teachers' professional identities and their professional development have largely been unexplored. Identifying the mediating and moderating mechanisms that underlie this relationship is critical to improving our understanding of special education teachers' professional development and to the task of developing effective interventions in this context. Furthermore, given that special education teachers are most likely to encounter dilemmas concerning professional development, they face more demanding tasks and workloads, more individualized educational clients, and higher professional demands than regular teachers [31]. The majority of research on professional development has focused on school teachers in general, and more research should be conducted to investigate special education teachers.

To address these issues, this study examined a sample of Chinese special education teachers to investigate a conceptual model that featured professional identity and professional development as two interrelated psychological variables. First, teacher self-efficacy was considered to mediate the relationship between professional identity and professional development. Second, perceptions of social support were considered to moderate the indirect relationship between professional identity and professional development via teacher self-efficacy.

The mediating role of teachers’ self-efficacy

According to self-efficacy theory, self-efficacy is an individual's belief in their ability to accomplish specific tasks assigned to them. It encompasses abilities derived from cognition, society, and behavior, which have a role in shaping individual behavior and achieving goals [32]. This concept has been widely applied in practice, and self-efficacy is of great significance in improving work attitudes and enhancing work performance. On the one hand, identity can affect the self-efficacy, job satisfaction, and work efficiency of teachers, and self-efficacy will be adjusted along with changes in identity status. According to Erikson’s identity theory, identity refers to the perception of theories, attitudes, and beliefs about the self and is a psychological attribute developed by an individual's relationship with his or her environment [33]. It is known that teachers' identity style is significantly correlated with self-efficacy [34, 35], and identity style can predict teacher self-efficacy [36]. When a teacher embraces their own identity, it is a recognition of their own talents, and the higher the level of identity recognition, the stronger their self-efficacy beliefs will be [37]. Additionally, longitudinal research has discovered that professional identity influences teachers' self-efficacy, job satisfaction, and productivity and that teachers' clear autonomous role orientation and social testing effectively support the development of self-efficacy [38, 39].

On the other hand, teacher's self-efficacy refers to expectations and beliefs regarding his or her judgments of expected outcomes, which can influence students’ academic achievement as well as teacher's level of professional competence [40]. The self-efficacy of special education teachers is closely correlated with the status of children with disabilities and their learning behaviors, and the level of self-efficacy affects teachers' teaching behaviors and the quality of instruction [41, 42]. Empirical studies have found that high self-efficacy drives professional development and enhances teachers' enthusiasm for teaching, learning goals, teaching ideas and methods, instructional design, and organizational management [43, 44]; low self-efficacy, on the other hand, is related to burnout and represents a risk factor for leaving the profession, and teachers with low self-efficacy have difficulty identifying strengths and areas for professional development [45, 46]. Furthermore, studies on the existence of an interaction between self-efficacy and professional development have found similar results, with the findings of most such studies being consistent with the claim that self-efficacy predicts professional development [47, 48]. To date, no studies have examined the mediating role of self-efficacy in the relationship between professional identity and professional development among special education teachers.

The moderating role of perceived social support

Social support is a basic and universal human need that can help teachers reduce psychological stress, relieve tension, reduce burnout, and improve social adjustment [49]. Special education teachers would likely benefit from receiving social support from family, friends, and significant others who can encourage openness and sharing of emotions with others [50, 51]. The government has issued a series of policies to ensure the cultivation of special education teachers, improve their status and treatment, and improve professional qualification certification, such as the Compulsory Education Law of China, Regulations on Education for Persons with Disabilities promulgated by The State Council of China, Special Education Teachers Professional Standards promulgated by the Ministry of Education of China, etc. According to the social support gain model theory, appreciating social support satisfies individual psychological needs, provides a sense of security and positive emotional experiences, and enhances self-confidence and self-esteem [52]. The higher the level of emotional experience and satisfaction that special education teachers derive from feeling respect, support, and understanding from society, the more they are inclined to develop a positive identity and the more they strive to understand and adapt to their professional role [53]. Because teachers’ perceptions of identity are a cornerstone of professional development practice [54], it can be hypothesized that teachers with high levels of perceived social support are more motivated to learn professionally than teachers with low levels of perceived social support, which makes them more capable to engage in professional development. That is, perceived social support predicts professional development. Consistent with this view, previous empirical research has suggested that teachers are more likely to achieve professional development due to the subjective perceptions of social support that influence their behavior and development [55].

In addition, perceived social support may moderate the indirect relationship between the professional identity and professional development of special education teachers. This claim is based on the inconsistent findings of studies that have used professional identity as a single predictor of teachers’ professional development [56]. Taking professional identity in isolation as a self-perception or psychological factor can explain only a small fraction of the variance in a specific, overtly human behavior. Thus, without any other relevant information regarding an individual's cognitive characteristics, it is difficult to determine definitively whether professional identity is a strong predictor of behavior. The relationship between the professional identity and professional development of special education teachers may be explained more effectively by considering social support to be a cognitive trait. Individuals' emotional experiences and perceptions of social support may contribute to the relationship between identity and professional development. In support of this view, one recent study found that special education teachers' professional identity and professional development require support and resources and that social support may prevent the negative outcomes of a lack of sense of identity and professional competence [57]. To date, previous research has neither examined social support as a predictor of special education teachers' professional development nor quantified the moderating effect of social support on the indirect relationship between professional identity and special education teachers' professional development.

The theory foundation and present study

The foundational theory

The Theory of Planned Behaviour (TPB for short) is a theoretical model of the endogenous influences on behavior proposed by Ajzen [58]. The theory of planned behavior suggests that behavior attitude, subjective norm and perceived behavioral control work together to influence an individual's behavior intentions and ultimately motivate the individual to engage in actual behavior. The present study employs the TPB as a guiding framework to elucidate the processes underlying the professional development of special education teachers(see Fig. 1). Professional identity, self-efficacy, and perceived social support as factors influencing professional development intentions and behaviors. The professional identity of an individual serves as the logical foundation for their behavior, influencing their attitude. Social support and expectation from significant others contribute to the formation of subjective norm, while self-efficacy plays a crucial role in perceived behavioral control. Accordingly, this study identified special education teachers' attitude as perceptions and emotions about professional identity, subjective norm as perceptions of the degree to which professional development is supported and accepted, and perceived behavioral control as an assessment of professional competence and influencing roles.

Guiding framework for the study

The present study

In this study, we tested a conceptual model according to which professional identity serves as a protective factor concerning special education teachers' professional development. The purpose of this study was twofold: (a) to determine whether special education teachers' self-efficacy mediates the relationship between professional identity and professional development and (b) to verify whether the indirect link between special education teachers' professional identity and professional development via self-efficacy is enhanced by their perceptions of social support. In summary, these two research questions constitute a moderated mediation model. This integrated model allows both mediation effects (i.e., what is the relationship between professional identity and professional development among special education teachers?) and moderating effects (i.e., under what conditions is the moderating effect weakest or strongest?) to be addressed. Methods

Participants

A random sampling method was used to conduct a questionnaire survey of teachers working for a total of 140 special education schools in eight provinces in China. Each school randomly selected 20 teachers and distributed paper questionnaires to them. After the questionnaires were collected, 132 blank and invalid questionnaires were removed, and 2668 valid questionnaires were obtained, for an effective recovery rate of 95.29%. As shown in Table 1, total of 32.43% of subjects were male, whereas 67.57% of subjects were female. The average working experience of the subjects was 6.32 years (SD = 0.63, Range = 1–20); 52.99% of the teachers had worked for less than 5 years, 21.56% had worked for 5–10 years, and 25.45% had worked for more than 10 years. Regarding the subjects' degree, including a postgraduate degree in the total number of subjects about 13.44%, college degree accounted for 65.48%, and junior college and high school accounted for 21.08%.

Measures

Professional identity scale

The Professional Identity Scale [59] was used to measure special education teachers’ actual perceptions of professional identity. The scale included 12 items and the three dimensions of teaching the subject (e.g., "I consulted with colleagues about the subject"), teacher-student relationship (e.g., "I respect pupils in my class"), and role perceptions (e.g., "I commit to service for disabled children"). The scale was scored on a 7-point Likert scale, with 1 to 7 representing "1 = very negative to 7 = very positive"; a higher average score indicates a higher degree of professional identity. A Chinese version of the scale was developed in the current study. Initially, the scale was translated from English to Chinese, and then the content of the scale was reviewed and suggestions for changes were made. Following this, the scale was translated back into English. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the whole scale and the 3 dimensions were 0.79 (whole scale), 0.68 (subject one teaches), 0.72 (relationship with pupils), and 0.63 (role conception). The results of the validation factor analysis were satisfactory, and the fit indices of the model were χ2/df = 3.11, RMSEA = 0.08, GFI = 0.88, AGFI = 0.91, CFI = 0. 91, NFI = 0. 92, RMR = 0.04.

Professional development survey

The Survey of Characteristics of Professional Development [60] was used to assess the professional development characteristics of teachers working in special education schools. This scale includes 51 items and the five dimensions of teachers’ content knowledge (14 items, e.g., “I became more confident in my ability to answer student questions about a topic”), coherence (9 items, e.g., “My professional development experiences were aligned with our school’s mission and vision”), collective participation (15 items, e.g., “I collaborated with colleagues to design flexible groups based on student need”), active learning in the classroom (7 items, e.g., “I wrote assessments to match state learning standards”), and active learning beyond the classroom (6 items, e.g., “I practiced a new skill under simulated conditions”). The scale was scored on a 7-point Likert scale, with 1 to 7 representing "1 = almost never true to 7 = almost always true”; a higher average score indicates a higher degree of professional development. The scale was translated into Chinese through collaboration with special education experts and excellent translators through forward translation, back translation, and cultural adaptation processes, and conducted reliability and validity tests, which was ultimately used in this study. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of the whole scale and the five dimensions were 0.80 (whole scale), 0.69 (content knowledge), 0.74 (coherence), 0.68 (collective participation), 0.77 (active learning in the classroom), and 0.78 (active learning beyond the classroom). The results of the validation factor analysis were good, and the fit indices of the model were χ2/df = 3.20, RMSEA = 0.08, GFI = 0.87, AGFI = 0.91, and CFI = 0.88, NFI = 0.92, RMR = 0.04.

Teachers' self-efficacy scale

The Teachers Sense of Efficacy Scale was used to assess special educators’ beliefs regarding their competencies, which was was proposed by Tschannen-Moran et al. [61] and adapted into Chinese by Yu et al. [62]. This scale comprises 24 items across three dimensions: classroom management efficacy (e.g., "How much can you do to calm a student who is disruptive or noisy? "), student engagement efficacy (e.g., " How much can you do to get through to the most difficult students? "), teaching strategy efficacy (e.g., "How well can you respond to difficult questions from your students? "). Each dimension includes eight items. The scale was scored on a 7-point Likert scale, with a range of 1 to 7 indicating "strongly disagree" to "strongly agree"; a higher average score indicates a higher level of the teacher’s self-efficacy. The reliability test showed that the Cronbach alpha coefficients for the total scale, classroom management dimension, student engagement dimension, and teaching strategies dimension were 0.79, 0.73, 0.76, and 0.79, respectively. The results of the validation factor analysis were good, and the fit indices of the model were χ2/df = 2.90, RMSEA = 0.07, GFI = 0.92, AGFI = 0.93, and CFI = 0. 92, NFI = 0.90, RMR = 0.04.

Perceived social support scale

The Perceived Social Support Scale was used to measure special education teachers’ emotional experiences and level of satisfaction with social support, which was developed by Zimet et al. [63] and adapted into Chinese by Wang et al. [64]. This scale included 12 self-rated items, and the three dimensions were family support (e.g., "I can talk about my problems with my family"), friend support (e.g., "I have friends with whom I can share my joys and sorrows") and significant other support (e.g., "There is a special person in my life who cares about my feelings"); each dimension included 4 items. The scale was scored on a 7-point scale, with 1 to 7 indicating "strongly disagree" to "strongly agree"; higher whole-scale scores indicated higher levels of social support. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the total scale and the three dimensions were 0.83, 0.70, 0.74, and 0.61, respectively. The results of the validation factor analysis were good, and the fit indices of the model were χ2/df = 3.50, RMSEA = 0.06, GFI = 0.89, AGFI = 0.90, and CFI = 0. 93, NFI = 0.92, RMR = 0.04.

Procedure

Two sessions of data collection were conducted; the first session included 1590 questionnaires that were distributed from April to May 2021, while the second session included 1210 questionnaires that were distributed from September to October 2021, for a total of 2800 questionnaires. The surveyors were trained to read the questionnaire instructions to the subjects to ensure that the data collection process was standardized and that the subjects responded uniformly. As a reward for completing the questionnaire, the subjects received a gift (e.g., a notebook, thumb drive, or book).

Data analyses

This study has adopted IBM SPSS 24.0 software and the PROCESS 3.3 macro program for all data analyses. Once all the questionnaires had been collected, all the data were processed in the following way: (1) SPSS 24.0 was used to test the scales of the four variables for common method bias, after which descriptive statistics were calculated and correlation analysis was applied to the four variables. (2)We applied Model 4 of macro program PROCESS in SPSS to examine the mediation effect of teachers' self-efficacy. In addition, the bootstrap test was conducted, and the resultant 95% confidence intervals were inspected to examine the significance of mediating effect. (3) We applied Model 4 of macro program PROCESS in SPSS to examine moderated mediation effect of perceived social support. In addition, the significance of the effect was tested using the bootstrap method, and a 95% bias-corrected confidence interval set was used to indicate a significant impact [65]. At last, simple slope tests were used to further explain specific moderating effect.

The moderating effect of perceived social support

Further bias-corrected percentile tests using the bootstrap method revealed that the indirect effect of professional identity on special education teachers' professional development via self-efficacy was moderated by perceived social support (See in Table 5). For special education teachers with high levels of perceived social support, the indirect effect of self-efficacy on special education teachers' professional development was moderated by perceived social support, b = 0.05, SE = 0.01, 95% CI [0.04, 0.10]; for special education teachers with low levels of perceived social support, professional identity did not affect professional development through self-efficacy, b = 0.09, SE = 0.02, 95% CI [-0.03, 0.06]. These results provide partial support for the moderated mediation model proposed in this study. The mediating role of self-efficacy was moderated by perceived social support, and the moderating role of perceived social support was reflected in the second half of professional identity's impact on special education teachers' professional development via self-efficacy.

Discussion

The correlation between multiple variables

First off, the research results indicate that professional identity has a significant positive predictive effect on the professional development of special education teachers, which is consistent with existing research. Because the professionalization of teaching practice is anchored in professional identity [66]. When a teacher considers themselves a professional, they will think about how to grow professionally. On the other hand, the findings are in line with previous studies and show a connection between self-efficacy and identity, professional development for special education teachers, teachers' self-efficacy is a component of professional identity and willingness to undertake professional development willingness [67]. Positive beliefs and self-confidence in teacher self-efficacy are intrinsic to forming professional development intentions and fulfilling professional commitments.

Secondly, there is a positive correlation between perceived social support and professional development, identity, and self-efficacy. When teachers perceive that schools provide learning and training opportunities and that teachers collaborate, it may affect their professional development, help to understand the professional identity of educators, and establish a positive self-efficacy [68].

The mediating role of teachers' self-efficacy

This study found that the self-efficacy of special education teachers is significantly promoted by professional identity, and it has a positive predictive effect on the professional development of special education teachers. That is, self-efficacy mediates the relationship between professional identity and professional development among special education teachers.

These findings support social identity theory [69], which suggests that an individual's sense of belonging to and identification with a social identity is a key factor in limiting negative emotions and negative behaviors among special education teachers. Special education teachers' perceptions of professional identity increase their level of self-motivation concerning professional development behaviors, which prevents special education teachers from developing negative professional development beliefs and behaviors. This study clarifies the question of why positive professional identity positively predicts special education teachers' professional development. Previous research by educational psychologists has focused on the constructs and characteristics associated with special education teachers' professional identities, whereas developmental psychologists have emphasized the burnout and self-efficacy of special education teachers. Research in these two areas has developed virtually independently, and the potential connections between these two fields have not been considered. This study integrates research from both fields to shed light on special education teachers' professional development. The integrated mediation model suggests that protective factors related to professional identity (e.g., role perceptions, teacher-student relationships) may enhance some protective personal traits (e.g., self-efficacy), thereby increasing special education teachers' behavioral tendencies toward professional development.

In addition to the overall mediation result, each link in the mediation model is noteworthy. In terms of the first stage of the mediation process (i.e., professional identity → self-efficacy), our findings support the claim that positive identity is positively related to special education teachers' self-efficacy [70, 71]. This finding is consistent with self-efficacy theory [32] and identity theory; accordingly, special education teachers who have a positive identity are more likely to be individuals with high self-esteem and a positive self-concept and are more likely to engage in positive behaviors [72]. We know that the formation process of professional identity is the process of special education teachers internalizing professional norms, beliefs, values, and codes of conduct. Special education teachers tend to have positive cognitive and affective experiences of teaching effectiveness and develop positive mental representations [73]. In addition, special education teachers' concerns may vary at different stages of development, so they were busy coping, but when they acquired special education knowledge and skills, they can adapt to the role switching and show high self-efficacy beliefs [74, 75].

Concerning the second stage of the mediation model (i.e., self-efficacy → professional development), this study showed that self-efficacy is positively associated with special education teachers’ professional development. This finding is in line with the motivation-competency beliefs-values model of teacher professional development [76], which posits that people with appropriate levels of motivation and positive competency beliefs (e.g., high self-efficacy) are more likely to achieve professional development. Given that self-efficacy is a further development of an individual's motivation and competency beliefs, it makes sense to apply this model to explain why some special education teachers may be more specialized than others. Self-efficacy can both mediate direct experiences, emotional arousal, and alternative experiences to promote positive cognitive ratings of competence and improve special education teachers' knowledge and skill learning, teacher-student interactions, and school affiliation behaviors [46, 77]. Teachers with high self-efficacy are not only highly motivated to learn but also believe that they can increase competence through learning and are more likely to actively engage in educational practice and professional learning to improve the quality of classroom relationships and instruction [78–80]. This finding is consistent with the conclusions of previous research, which has suggested that self-efficacy is a factor that contributes to the professional development of special education teachers [81].

The moderating role of perceived social support

The results of the study indicated that perceived social support predicts the professional development of special education teachers. This result is consistent with the conclusions of previous research, which has suggested that teachers with high perceived social support engage in activities that may meet their professional development needs [55, 82]. Due to the intrinsic nature of professional development for special education teachers, this process is inseparable from external support, which causes individuals with high levels of perceived social support to be more motivated and engaged in professional development efforts [83].

Additionally, this study found that perceived social support moderates only the pathway between self-efficacy and special education teachers' professional development (second-stage moderation). There is a significant relationship between self-efficacy and professional development for special education teachers with high perceived social support. According to the social support hypothesis, special education teachers with high perceived social support tend to evaluate their competence, teaching effectiveness, and self-worth positively [84], and such perceived social support may have more influence on their tendency to seek professional development and their social adaptation [85]. Concerning students with special educational needs, professional development for special education teachers is not achievable without professional support or teachers’ ability to share knowledge and experience, learn together, and participate in curriculum development [28, 86]. In particular, novice special education teachers, who are disadvantaged by their limited expertise, tend to develop informal supportive relationships with professionals, colleagues, and family members to provide "on-demand" support for instructional or personal issues (e.g., emotional support) that arise in the context of their daily work and to provide opportunities for teacher professional development and learning [53, 87]. In addition, students with disabilities can not only bring job stress but also emotional support to special education teachers [3, 88]. Establishing a high-quality teacher-student relationship means intimate relationships with each other, and the growth and progress of students with disabilities can make teachers feel happy and successful [89, 90]. Special education teachers with lower levels of perceived social support are less willing to engage in professional development and therefore do not demonstrate a significant relationship between self-efficacy and professional development. These findings may explain the inconsistent results of previous studies regarding the relationship between self-efficacy and professional development. As a consequence, perceived social support could be used as an indicator of whether teachers with negative self-perceptions and evaluations (e.g., low self-esteem) engage in professional development activities.

Contrary to our expectations, perceived social support does not moderate the relationship between professional identity and special education teachers' self-efficacy. This result can be explained in terms of the powerful role that identity plays in teachers' lives. According to self-determination theory (Deci and Ryan, 2000), identity is a universal value need, and without addressing this fundamental psychological need, individuals cannot obtain positive levels of motivation and competence ratings. However, if identity needs are met, teachers can obtain a stable sense of self-efficacy regardless of how much social support they perceive to be providing for them.

Limitations and directions for future research

The study has the following limitations. First, this study is a cross-sectional study, which has limitations concerning the inference of causal relationships among variables. Future research should conduct longitudinal studies to confirm any causal hypotheses. Second, emotion and culture are the potential influencing factors and background of special education teachers' identity and career development, but the research is insufficient. Future research should pay attention to the impact of emotion and culture on special education teachers. Third, the study used measurement instruments that apply to teachers in general to assess the characteristics of the professional development of special education teachers. Future research should use specialized assessment instruments designed to investigate the professional development of special education teachers to explore the model that we developed in this study.
