Thiamine administration in septic shock: towards finding the target population
Authors: Jacob Vine, John H Lee, Ari Moskowitz, Michael W Donnino
Published: 2024

Dear editor,

We would like to thank Pereira et al. for their letter regarding our recently published article [1] in which we performed a post hoc analysis of our two recent Phase II clinical trials on thiamine administration in septic shock, Thiamine in Septic Shock Trial (TSS) (NCT01070810) [2] and The Thiamine for Renal Protection in Septic Shock Trial (TRPSS) (NCT03550794) [3].

The authors now share data from their study re-analyzed for renal endpoints similar to our post-hoc analysis [4]. Pereira analyzed 108 patients who were not receiving renal replacement therapy (RRT) at the time of enrollment for the primary endpoint of need for RRT and found no statistical difference between thiamine and placebo groups. Of note, this endpoint was different from our analysis of alive and RRT-free and, as such, does have to contend with the bias of competing risk. To this end, the authors reported a numerically lower mortality rate in the thiamine group in their original cohort so competing risk of death could confound the need for RRT (i.e., those who died might have needed RRT but never had the opportunity). Their original trial also reported higher creatinine levels at baseline in the thiamine group but the numbers are not published [5]. In addition, as this is sub-analysis of patients who did not have end-stage renal disease (ESRD) upon presentation, evaluation of baseline differences between groups especially on renal parameters would be important but is not available.

The authors performed a second analysis after excluding those with acute kidney injury (AKI) at the time of enrollment, which resulted in removing more than 50% of the original study population and leaving 43 patients in the analysis. With this small subgroup, they found no difference in the odds of developing AKI or needing RRT between the two groups. No baseline characteristics are provided for these subgroups so whether there was a baseline imbalance remains unknown.

We thank the authors for sharing a re-analysis of their randomized trial but do caution that there were some structural concerns that might preclude more definitive conclusions. We do agree with the authors that evidence at this time is inconclusive about whether thiamine is effective in septic shock overall and further agree that the group most likely to benefit appears to be the deficient cohort.
