Unveiling oncogene-induced senescence: a new frontier in prostate cancer prognosis and therapy
Authors: YunZhen Peng, Xin Qiu, JunJie Cai, YongZhong Li, XueYan Wei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundProstate cancer (PCa) is the most common tumor in the male urogenital system. Radical prostatectomy, radiation, chemotherapy and endocrine therapy are common clinical treatment methods. Oncogene-induced senescence (OIS) is a special type of cellular senescence believed to have anti-tumor effects, may serve as an initial barrier for preventing tumor growth and development. As of yet, the effects of OIS on the occurrence, development, prognosis of prostate cancer and its related mechanisms are poorly understood, so further study is urgently needed.Materials and methodsGene transcriptomic data and clinical information data for PCa were downloaded from TCGA and GEO databases, OISGs were obtained from the Reactome database. We identified OIS-related subtypes based on consistent cluster analysis. Univariate Cox regression analysis was performed to identify PR-OISGs. LASSO regression analysis finally screened prognosis-related key genes and constructed a risk score model. Patients were classified into high-/ low-risk groups based on the median risk score. We assessed the TMB, immune cell infiltration level, immune checkpoint expression and drug sensitivity between high-/ low-risk groups. A nomogram was constructed and validated using calibration curves and clinical decision curves. RT-qPCR and IHC staining analysis were performed to verify the mRNA and protein expression levels of target genes.ResultsThe study identified two OIS-associated subtypes of PCa. We screened seven PRGs and established a risk model for PCa patients. It was found that BFS (biochemical recurrence-free survival) was significantly lower in high-risk group. Patients in the high-risk group showed greater immune infiltration and high expression of immune checkpoint, as well as higher TMB. Furthermore, stratifying the risk score appropriately allowed the predictive nomogram model to accurately predict the outcome of prostate cancer patients. Also, docetaxel and Olaparib sensitivity was higher in the high-risk group, whereas bicalutamide was more sensitive in the low-risk group. IHC and PCR confirmed CDK6 was highly upregulated in PCa.ConclusionOur study screened seven genes with potential value for predicting long-term survival of patients with PCa and developed a prognostic model. These findings are expected to guide future development of effective therapies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03893-2.

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) is a common type of malignant carcinoma in the urinary system. Prostate cancer demonstrates the highest incidence and mortality rates among urothelial malignancies [1] (Sung H et al., 2021). Globally, it constitutes the second most prevalent solid tumor and the fifth leading cause of cancer-related mortality in males [2] (Gandaglia G et al., 2021). Substantial geographic variation exists in prostate cancer incidence and mortality globally. According to the 2020 GLOBOCAN analysis by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), Nordic Europe has the highest incidence of PCa, while South-Central Asia has the lowest. Prostate cancer mortality rates demonstrate significant global heterogeneity, with the Caribbean exhibiting the highest documented rates and South Asia the lowest [2]. (Gandaglia G. et al., 2021).

The etiology of prostate cancer is multifactorial, genetic susceptibility, ethnic factors and family history of cancer are recognized as risk factors for initiation potential in prostate cancer [2] (Gandaglia G. et al., 2021). Epidemiological evidence consistently demonstrates that prostate cancer incidence escalates markedly with advancing age, with over 85% of initial diagnoses occurring in individuals aged ≥ 60 years [3, 4] (Siegel RL et al., 2022; Bray F et al., 2018).

The essential treatments for PCa include radical prostatectomy (RP), radical radiotherapy (RT), endocrine therapy, targeted therapy, chemotherapy, and so on. RT and RP are the two main treatments for limited prostate cancer [5] (Rebello RJ et al., 2021). Prostate cancer pathogenesis is pathogenically linked to androgen receptor (AR) signaling dynamics, thus androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) is one of its effective therapeutic strategies, which is widely used in clinical practice [6] (Teo MY et al., 2019). For advanced or metastatic prostate cancer, ADT constitutes the foundational first-line systemic intervention [7] (Morris MJ et al., 2018).

Biochemical recurrence (BCR) signifies the initial phase of disease progression preceding clinical recurrence in prostate cancer, with definitional criteria therapy-dependent. For radiotherapy-treated patients, BCR is universally defined per Phoenix criteria as a post-nadir prostate-specific antigen (PSA) rise of ≥ 2 ng/mL above the nadir value [8] (Roach M et al., 2006). Following RP, BCR is defined per American Urological Association (AUA) criteria as a confirmed rise in prostate-specific antigen (PSA) to ≥ 0.2 ng/mL on two consecutive measurements [9] (Cookson MS et al., 2007). However, approximately one-quarter to one-half of patients developed BCR after the definitive treatment [10] (Van den Broeck et al., 2019). BCR represents a critical transitional phase in prostate cancer progression. Post-BCR therapeutic options remain limited, underscoring an urgent need to identify novel therapeutic targets and strategies that enhance treatment efficacy and extend metastasis-free survival.

Cell senescence is a physiological process that antagonizes malignant transformation, which safeguard tissue homeostasis [11] (Gorgoulis V et al., 2019). Dysfunction of senescence-critical genes, particularly those regulating cell cycle arrest pathways, compromises genomic stability and predisposes organisms to oncogenic transformation [12] (Schmitt CA et al., 2022). Oncogene-induced senescence (OIS) constitutes a tumor-suppressive mechanism wherein sustained oncogenic activation triggers irreversible cell cycle arrest, thereby preventing malignant transformation of genomically compromised cells [13] (Courtois-Cox S et al., 2008). Substantial evidence establishes OIS as a potent tumor-suppressive mechanism, functioning as a critical early-stage barrier against oncogenesis by halting the proliferation of pre-malignant cells [14] (Mittermeier C et al., 2020). Histopathological analyses of early-stage human prostate cancer specimens have revealed molecular signatures consistent with cellular senescence [15] (Chen Z et al., 2005). However, the prognostic significance of OIS-related genes in prostate cancer patients remains incompletely characterized, warranting further investigation to elucidate their clinical implications.

This study systematically investigated the association between OIS and clinical outcomes in prostate cancer patients. We established novel prognostic biomarkers predictive of biochemical recurrence, with potential implications for refining risk stratification in clinical management.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition and preprocessing

UCSC Xena (https://xenabrowser.net/datapages/) provided us with transcriptome data (TCGA-PRAD) and corresponding clinicopathological information on 481 prostate tumor samples and 51 normal samples. Transcriptome data were fragments per kilobase million (FPKM) in RNA-seq, we obtained FPKM values as the final result of our RNA-Seq analysis. GSE54460 (gene expression files from the GPL11154 platform), GSE46602 (gene expression files from the GPL570 platform), and GSE116918 (gene expression files from the GPL25318 platform) were downloaded from GEO (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/), a total of samples from 390 tumors and 14 normals were included. Clinicopathological characteristics including age, prostate specific antigen (PSA), Gleason score (GS), clinical T stage, BCR time, and BCR status were collected (Supplementary Table 1).

OIS-related signaling pathways (ID: R-HSA-2559585.6) and 33 OIS regulators were downloaded from the Reactome database (https://reactome.org/PathwayBrowser/). Initially, we downloaded 33 OIS genes, but we were not able to detect AGO1, AGO3, or AGO4 genes in the GSE54460 data, so we used the remaining 30 genes for further analysis (Supplementary Table 2). Since all data were downloaded from public databases, no additional ethical approval was required.

Identification of oncogene-induced senescence subtypes

A network of protein-protein interactions (PPIs) was constructed using the STRING database (http://string-db.org). Data from 481 tumor samples from TCGA-PRAD, 36 tumor samples from GSE46602, and 106 tumor samples from GSE54460 were combined into an entire cohort using a “Combat” package to eliminate batch effects. ComBat (Combined Estimation of Batch Effects) uses empirical Bayesian methods to adjust for batch effects in data. It can handle multiple batches and adjusts for location and scale for each feature (gene, protein, etc.). A total of 522 PRAD samples were finally analyzed after removing samples with missing BCR time and status data.

Principal component analysis (PCA) Statistical analysis and hierarchical clustering were used to perform unsupervised analysis on normalized and preprocessed data. Unsupervised hierarchical clustering analysis of OIS regulators was performed using the “ConsensusClusterPlus” R package. A cumulative distribution function plot was used to determine the number of clusters to be used. Expression differences between subtypes were illustrated in the heatmap and PCA plots. “ComplexHeatmap” and “grid” packages created the heatmap, which was visualized using “Heatmap”. PCA plots are constructed by the “tidyverse” package and visualized by the “ggbiplot” function.

Construction and verification of OIS-related prognostic signature

All tumor samples with missing BCR information were deleted. An analysis of univariate Cox regression was conducted to determine the prognostic value of OISGs by using the “coxph” algorithm and the result is presented through a forest plot, A P ≤ 0.05 was used for defining Prognosis-related genes (PRGs). The gene expression difference between prostate carcinomas and normal prostate tissue was compared using a box plot.

Then, LASSO-Cox analysis was used to develop a prediction model based on PRGs. We ran LASSO Cox regression using the “glmnet” package. We calculated the regression coefficients using multivariate regression. In this study, patients were divided into a training set (n = 363) and a testing set (n = 156) on a 7:3 ratio randomly, and GSE116918 as an external validation set. In the training set, the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression analysis was used to correct the risk of overfitting and narrow the target gene range. The risk score of each sample was calculated based on the LASSO regression analysis by the following formula: Risk score = Σ (Expi*βi), in which Expi represents the gene expression value and βi represents the regression coefficient (Supplementary Table 3).

Based on the median risk score, we divided patients into high-risk and low-risk subgroups. An analysis of Kaplan-Meier (log-rank test) survival probabilities was conducted to compare the survival of high-risk and low-risk participants. An area under the ROC curve (AUC) and the characteristics of the ROC curve were used to evaluate the prognosis prediction. Boxplots of Wilcox tests were employed to compare risk scores between the two groups. Analyses of multivariate and univariate Cox regression were conducted to evaluate the independent prognostic factors. Survival curves were generated using the K-M method in the R package “survival”, and ROC curves were generated using the R package “timeROC”.

Comprehensive assessment of riskscore and clinical parameters among PCa patients

To further determine if there is a correlation between the risk score and the prognostic factors, Pearson’s chi-squared test was used to compare the differences across the various clinicopathologic characteristics in all cohorts. Heatmap has been created using the R package “pheatmap” to display correlations between prognostic risk scores and clinical features. We generated box plots using R software’s ggboxplot packages. To identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in high- and low-risk groups, the “Limma” package in R was used. Genes with |logFC|>1 and p < 0.01 were differentially expressed.

Construction and validation of the predictive nomogram

Based on the multivariate Cox stepwise regression analysis, we screened PSA, GS, T stage, and risk score as prognosis-related characteristics. On this basis, by utilizing the R packages “rms” and “regplot”, we developed a clinicopathological nomogram on oncogene-induced senescence to predict the survival of PCa patients.

Next, we used the concordance index (C-index) to evaluate the prediction accuracy of this model, and an analysis of calibration curves was carried out to assess calibration. Decision curve analysis was used to evaluate the clinical efficacy of the nomogram. ROC analyses were performed as previously described.

Functional enrichment analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) categories and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways were carried out by the “clusterProfiler” R package. The bubble diagram was drawn by the “ggplot2” package, significantly enriched GO terms and pathways are those with a corrected FDR adjusted p value < 0.05. After that, we conducted Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) using the “GSVA” software package in RStudio to examine the expression differences across risk groups. The “msigdbr” package was utilized to download the gene sets related to “c2” and “KEGG” as input preparation files, the “GSVA” algorithm was used to obtain corresponding pathways for the samples. Differential analysis was conducted using the “limma” package with screening criteria of | logFC | >1 with a 0.05 adjusted p-value. We visualized the 20 top differentially enriched pathways.

Immune infiltration analysis

To determine whether the OIS-related prognostic signature may play a crucial role in immune infiltration in prostatic carcinoma, 22 types of infiltrating immune cells were analyzed. In this study, we compared immune cell infiltration in the tumor microenvironment (TME) in high- and low-risk groups and between two subtypes using “CIBERSORT” and “ssGSEA” algorithms, respectively. The correlation between tumor immune cell infiltration and prognostic risk score was analyzed using Spearman’s correlation and significant correlations were plotted using R package corrplot. To evaluate the ratio of 28 immune cell subcategories in the high-risk and low-risk groups, the ssGSEA (Single Sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis) algorithm was employed by using the package “GSVA”. We analyzed the correlation between immune cell genes and gene signature using a heatmap.

Forecast of immunotherapy reactions

Furthermore, we compared the expression differences of common immune checkpoints in each group by Wilcoxon test to predict the benefit from immunotherapy, including IDO1, CTLA-4, TIGIT, PDCD1, PDCD1LG2, CD28, CD226, and PVR. The results of the appeal are visualized using the “ggplot2” and “ggpubr” packages. Pearson’s correlation test was used to examine the correlation between immune cell immune checkpoints and Risk score. Correlation plots were generated in R using the corrplot, ggpubr, and ggExtra packages.

Tumor mutation burden (TMB) analysis

We downloaded simple nucleotide variation (SNV) data from the TCGA database for prostate patients. Maftools R software package was used for visualizing the analysis of somatic mutation data. Due to the lack of mutation information for some patients, a total of 380 patients were included in the mutation landscape analysis. Waterfall plots were used to show the top 20 mutations in the high and low-risk groups in terms of mutation frequency.

Chemotherapy susceptibility estimates based upon riskscores

The semi-inhibitory concentration (IC50) values of common anti-tumor drugs for PCa patients between the two groups were calculated by the pRRophetic package.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

The Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database (https://www.proteinatlas.org/) is an open-source database that comprehensively contains protein expression in almost all tissues and organs of the human body. As part of this study, the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database (accessed on 17 January 2024) was adopted to validate protein expressions of the selected 7 prognostic related genes from the prognostic model for analyzing and comparing the protein expression of the target genes.

Real-Time quantitative PCR

We collected tumor and normal tissues from five patients with PCa undergoing surgery at the Third Affiliated Hospital of Southern Medical University (Guangzhou, China). According to WHO classification, they had diagnostic criteria and did not receive any preoperative treatment. Each patient provided informed consent and ethical approval to the Ethics Committee of Southern Medical University before they were included in the study (Ethics approval number:2022-lunshen-044). Our workflow ensured tissue accuracy through: All tumor and paired normal prostate tissues were collected during radical prostatectomy by experienced urologists under direct supervision by pathologists; Postoperative H&E validation by dual blinded pathologists; Tumor cell content was quantified by pathologists during H&E evaluation. A minimum tumor purity threshold of ≥ 70% was enforced for all tumor samples included in RT-qPCR/IHC analyses; Strict exclusion of cancer/HGPIN/BPH from “normal” tissues. These measures mitigate heterogeneity-related confounders and underpin the biological validity of our molecular data. Before using, store these tissues at -80 °C. RT-qPCR was used as a further method of verifying the expression of prognostic genes in tissue samples. Trizol (Invitrogen, Trizol) was used to extract total RNA, and a reverse transcription PCR kit (Takara, RR047A) was used to reverse transcription. In this test, PCR is performed as prescribed by the manufacturer (Takara, RR091A). A reference gene for the internal comparison was b-actin. By comparing the mRNA expression levels to b-actin, we quantified the relative mRNA expression level. A list of primer sequences can be found in the supplementary Table 4.

Statistical analysis

All bioinformatics analyses and statistical tests were performed by R software (version 4.1.1) together with GraphPad Prism v7.00. The Wilcoxon test or independent sample t-test was used for all continuous variables and the chi-square test was used for qualitative data comparison. The nonparametric Wilcoxon test was used for comparison between the two groups, and the Kruskal Wallis test was utilized for multiple comparisons. Survival analysis was performed using the K-M curve and log-rank test. Statistical p-values below 0.05 were considered significant. In vitro experiments, each group repeated at least three independent experiments. Throughout all figures, statistical significance thresholds are uniformly denoted as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001. In vitro experiments, each group repeated at least three independent experiments.

On the basis of a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network map obtained from the online database, STRING, we evaluated 33 genes associated with OIS (Oncogene-Induced Senescence) in the Reactome database. The constructed PPI map, as shown in Fig. 2A, demonstrates the closely interrelated nature of the individual genes. The TCGA-PRAD cohort was then analyzed to determine the frequencies of somatic mutations in 33 OIS-related genes (OISGs). A graph (Fig. 2B) shows the mutation distribution of the 20 most frequently mutated genes, TP53 having the highest mutation frequency (72%), followed by ERF (8%), MOV10 (4%), and RB1(4%). We next analyzed mRNA expression levels of OISGs in gene expression profiling data sets from prostate cancer and normal tissues. As indicated in Fig. 2C, most of the OISGs were identified to be significantly differentially expressed between tumor and normal tissues.

Identification of OIS subtypes and their characteristics in prostate cancer

To eliminate the heterogeneity of individual datasets, we combined three different datasets (TCGA-PRAD, GSE46602 and GSE54460) into a complete cohort of 623 tumor samples. With the exception of genes not detected in the GEO cohort, we identified 30 OIS regulators in this study. A cluster analysis was conducted using ConsensusClusterPlus, which results are graphically represented using Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF) curves and delta area plots. Based on the CDF and Delta area curve, the cluster was determined to be the most stable clustering when k = 2 (Supplementary Fig. 1A). According to the consensus clustering, 623 samples were distinguished into two distinct subtypes: subtypes A (n = 473) and subtype B (n = 150) (Fig. 3A). Principal component analysis (PCA) demonstrated the existence of transcriptional profile differences between subtypes A and B (Fig. 3B). A heatmap shows the expression levels of OISGs as well as the clinical characteristics of the two subtypes (Fig. 3C).

Establish and validation of the OIS prognosis model based on OISGs

Biochemical recurrence (BCR) was chosen as a prognostic indicator in this study due to the high 5-year survival rate for prostate cancer patients and the small sample size of deaths. After removing samples with missing BCR data from the sampling, 519 tumor samples were included in this study. To assess the prognostic value of OISGs, we applied univariate COX regression analysis which screened out 11 genes associated with prognosis in prostate cancer patients (Fig. 4A). We split the data into a training set (n = 363) and a testing set (n = 156) in a ratio of 7:3 randomly. Following that, we conducted LASSO regression analysis on the 11 previously acquired genes (Figs. 4B-C) and screened out 7 genes significantlyassociated with prognosis (Supplementary Table 3). According to the expression levels of these 7 genes and the LASSO regression coefficients, a risk score was calculated as follows: risk score = (0.0174 * ERF expression) + (0.4390 * MOV10 expression) + (-0.1388 * TP53 expression) + (-0.2608 * CDK6 expression) + (0.1937 * CDKN2D expression) + (0.3784*CDKN2B expression) + (0.4098 * CDKN2C expression). Kaplan-Meier analyses were conducted to analyze the impact of these genes on PCa patient survival (Fig. 4D-J). Results indicated that poor prognosis is associated with high levels of CDKN2B, CDKN2C, CDKN2D, ERF and MOV10, as well as low levels of CDK6 and TP53. We also found that those in higher risk groups were more likely to progress BCR (Fig. 4K).

Verification of seven prognostic OIS-related genes in PCA tissues

Further tests were performed on prostate cancer and normal prostate tissues for the expression of seven prognostic OIS-related genes. Firstly, an analysis of the protein expression levels of the seven genes was conducted using the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) online databases (www.proteinatlas.org). Immunohistochemical (IHC) analyses showed CDK6 and CDKN2B were strongly positively expressed in prostate cancer tissues (Fig. 7A-B), whereas it was negatively or weakly expressed in normal prostate tissues. As the most frequently mutated tumor suppressor gene, TP53, a higher level of expression can be observed in normal tissues than in tumors (Fig. 7C). Secondly, we performed RT-qPCR to verify the mRNA expression of seven signature genes on prostate cancer specimens and benign prostate tissues from the laboratory of the Third Affiliated Hospital, Southern Medical University. A significant upregulation of CDK6, MOV10, and TP53 was observed in cancer cells (Fig. 8A-C), whereas no significant differences were found in the expression of the remaining genes.

Independent prognostic analysis and construction of the nomogram

In uni- and multivariate Cox regression analyses, the risk model’s independent prognostic value and assessment of other clinical features as independent prognostic factors were evaluated. The N stage was not included in univariate and multivariate COX regression analyses due to the absence of some patients with the N stage. Multiple risk factors including riskscor, Gleason Score (GS), PSA, and T stage were enrolled together to carry out univariate and multivariate Cox analyses for BFS in TCGA- PRAD and GEO cohorts. According to univariate and multivariate regression analyses, prognostic risk score, GS as well as T-stage, especially T3-4, are independent prognostic variables (Fig. 9A-B).

The risk score, PSA, T stage and GS were selected into the nomogram by a stepwise Cox regression model (Fig. 9C). The probability of 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year BFS in prostate cancer patients was predicted by calculating the set values of each score in the nomogram. The performance of the nomogram can be assessed by Harrell’s concordance index(C-index), which yielded an averaged C-index of 0.806 (95%confidence interval: 0.767–0.845). Subsequently, a calibration curve was drawn to assess the accuracy of the nomogram. As shown in Fig. 9D, the calibration curve showed that the predicted values were consistent with the observed values. Decision curve analysis (DCA) for comparing the difference between the nomogram and single independent clinical parameter (Fig. 9E). We further explored the relationship between the clinical information and risk scores (Fig. 9F-I), the results showed that higher risk scores were indeed associated with worse clinical characteristics.

Functional enrichment analysis and gene set variation analysis

To further explore differences in gene function and pathway between the high- and low-risk groups, we applied the “limma” package to obtain differentially expressed genes (DEGs) based on a filter criterion of |logFC|>1 and p < 0.05. Finally, we obtained a total of 119 DEGs. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis and Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) were then performed based on these DEGs. Based on GO analysis, DEGs are classified into three main functions, including biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC) and molecular functions (MF). The biological processes (BP) of these genes included symbiotic interaction, regulation of peptidase activity, collagen-containing extracellular matrix, endoplasmic reticulum lumen, extracellular matrix structural constituent and protease binding. In terms of molecular function (MF), the target genes were significantly enriched with GO terms involved in molecule binding functions. From the perspective of the cellular component (CC), there was an enrichment of DEGs with GO terms related to collagen-containing extracellular matrix, endoplasmic reticulum lumen, secretory granule lumen, etc. (Fig. 10A).

Somatic mutations and immune landscape

In cancer, tumor mutation burden (TMB) is an index for evaluating the frequency of mutations in genes. We profiled the mutation landscape of the low-risk and high-risk groups to examine the relationship between the OIS-related gene signature and the mutation levels in PCA patients. As illustrated by the waterfall plot, the high-risk group had a higher nucleotide variation rate (63.59% vs. 52.55%, Fig. 12A and B). In accordance with waterfall plot findings, the box plot analyses also showed that TMB was higher in the high-risk group than in the low-risk group (Fig. 12C). As a result of this study, the risk score was found to reflect genomic instability and TMB level in patients with PCA, indicating its potential to predict the response to ICIs.

Drug sensitivity analysis

In addition, we analyzed the differences in sensitivity to commonly used antineoplastic drugs between the two groups of patients using the “pRRophic” package (Supplementary Fig. 2). A drug’s sensitivity can be measured by the half inhibitory concentration (IC50), the smaller the IC50, the more sensitive the drug. In pharmacological profiling, we observed significantly lower IC50 values (p < 0.05) for Bortezomib, Camptothecin, Cyclopamine, Dasatinib, Docetaxel, Doxorubicin, Elesclomol, Gefitinib, Imatinib, Methotrexate, Nilotinib, Paclitaxel, Parthenolide, Pazopanib, Rapamycin, Shikonin, Sunitinib, Vinblastine, and Olaparib in the high-risk group, indicating enhanced therapeutic sensitivity in these patients. Conversely, significantly reduced IC50 values (p < 0.05) for Axitinib, Bicalutamide, Bosutinib, and Pyrimethamine were observed in the low-risk group, demonstrating greater drug efficacy in this cohort. Bicalutamide is a commonly used drug in endocrine therapy for prostate cancer patients, our research found that PCa patients in the low-risk group were more sensitive to bicalutamide (Fig. 12D). Docetaxel (DTX) is a widely used chemotherapeutic agent for the treatment of prostate cancer, we then compared the IC50 of DTX between two subgroups. Considering the result, we speculate that patients in the high-risk group may be more sensitive to DTX therapy (Fig. 12E). In recent years, PARP inhibitors, such as Olaparib, have shown great promise in the treatment of prostate cancer. There was a lower IC 50 in the high-risk group, indicating a higher sensitivity to targeted drugs (Fig. 12F). Taken together, these results suggest that OIS-related genes are correlated with the sensitivity of multiple drugs.

Discussion

Several studies indicate that cellular senescence contributes to cancer development and progression. Telomerase-dependent replicative senescence, OIS and stress-induced senescence are the main pathways leading to cellular senescence [16, 17] (Campisi J, 2005; Kuilman T et al., 2010). Among these, replicative senescence was the first to be identified. A study by Hayflick and Moorhead found that human embryonic fibroblasts cultured in vitro cease to proliferate after a limited number of cell divisions, also known as the Hayflick limit [18] (Hayflick L et al., 1961). This replicative senescence can be attributed to telomere attrition [19] (Harley CB et al., 1990). OIS, which is often regarded as an early, cell-intrinsic tumor-suppressor mechanism, does not require telomere depletion at the onset, unlike replicative senescence. Researchers have discovered that OIS plays a dual role in the development of cancer, exhibiting anticancer effects during the early stages of tumor formation and pro-cancer effects as the cancer grows [20] (Rattanavirotkul N et al.,2021).

Cancer-related deaths in aging men are due mainly to prostate cancer, the most common urological neoplasm. In recent years, the incidence of prostate cancer has shown an increasing trend [21] (Culp MB et al., 2020). A study by Van den Broeck et al. found that patients with PCa who develop BCR after radical treatment are at risk for distant metastasis and significantly related to cancer-related death [10] (Van den Broeck T et al.,2019). Pertinently, previous data suggests that prostate cancer are closely related to Androgens and androgen receptor (AR) [22] (Jamroze A et al., 2021). It has been shown that cellular senescence is associated with prostate hyperplasia [23] (Fiard G et al., 2021), however, whether it is associated with the malignance of prostate cancer and the mechanism underlying them are not clear. It is therefore critical to study the effects of OIS on prostate cancer and its mechanisms so that they can be used as a reference for the prevention and treatment of prostate cancer in the future.

There are many studies on prognostic models related to cellular senescence and most of them are informative, but there are few studies on OIS at present. Our extensive literature search revealed a prognostic model was constructed by Gao C et al. based on 11 OIS-associated lncRNAs, indicating OIS might affect the microenvironment of HCC [24] (Gao C et al., 2022). As can be seen, our research topic has clinically significant for the research standpoint.

Based on the TCGA and GEO databases, this study screened OIS-related genes and found that the expression levels of ERF, MOV10, TP53, CDK6, CDKN2D, CDKN2B, and CDKN2C genes were significantly correlated with prostate cancer occurrence. It was believed that the above genes could predict prostate cancer prognoses effectively. Following this, a risk scoring model was constructed using the expression levels and risk coefficients of these seven genes. Considering the estimation of the model’s predictive power and the comparison of the characteristics of high- and low-risk groups, we anticipate the model to be helpful for predicting PCa prognosis and guiding individuals’ treatment and clinical decisions.

Unlike pure bioinformatics approaches, we performed PCR experiments against the screened seven signatures as part of our study. Results showed that prostate cancer tissues had significantly higher MOV10, CDK6, and TP53 mRNA levels than normal prostate tissues, while no significant differences were found in mRNA expression levels of ERF, CDKN2D, CDKN2B, and CDKN2C genes between tumor tissues and normal tissues. Secondly, MOV10 and TP53 expression levels in tumor samples were significantly higher than those in normal samples, while CDKN2B expression levels in tumor samples were significantly lower, based on the differential expression analysis of OISGs in the TCGA-PRAD cohort. As compared to normal samples, tumor samples did not show any difference in the expression levels of ERF, CDK6, CDKN2D, and CDKN2C genes.

Based on the results outlined above, aside from CDK6 and CDKN2B genes, the results of the remaining genes were consistent with RT-qPCR analysis. Similarly, previous related studies found that CDKN2D, CDKN2B and CDKN2C gene expression differences were not significant between prostate cancer tissues and normal paracancerous tissues [25] (Park DJ et al.,1997), which was consistent with our findings. Additionally, the HPA online database was used to analyze immunohistochemical staining images, we found that prostate cancer tissues expressed CDK6 and CDKN2B strongly positively, whereas normal prostate tissues expressed TP53 more strongly, and the expression level of the remaining genes was not significantly different. As our interpretation was based on public databases, some proteins’ expression levels did not match those found from our analysis.

The core mechanism is the critical role of post-transcriptional regulation, mRNA levels do not invariably exhibit linear correspondence with protein abundance due to intricate post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms.

Our RT-qPCR data showed no statistically significant difference in mRNA expression of CDKN2B gene between cancerous and adjacent normal tissues. However, HPA IHC demonstrated “strong positive” protein expression in tumor tissues. This indicates significant post-transcriptional regulation of CDKN2B in prostate cancer, leading to substantially increased protein abundance.

While RT-qPCR revealed significant upregulation of TP53 mRNA in tumors, HPA IHC showed stronger TP53 expression in normal prostate tissues. This aligns with the canonical TP53 regulatory paradigm in cancer: In normal cells, wild-type p53 protein undergoes rapid degradation via E3 ubiquitin ligases (e.g., MDM2), maintaining low protein levels despite detectable basal mRNA expression. In prostate cancer, frequent TP53 loss-of-function mutations occur. Mutant p53 proteins: (i) Lose transactivation capacity. (ii) Exhibit conformational changes that resist MDM2-mediated degradation. (iii) Accumulate in nuclei due to prolonged half-life [26, 27] (Macedo-Silva C et al.,2023, Liu Zet al.,2022).

In addition, Sample Heterogeneity and Technical Disparities, Methodological distinctions, Divergent significance criteria, Inherent limitations of the database may all lead to differences in expression.

CDKN2B, CDKN2C and CDKN2D are cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors (CDKIs) which collectively belong to the INK4 family encoded by the CDKN2 gene family, as well as important tumor suppressors. The downregulation of their expression may be related to tumor formation [28, 29] (Amani J et al.,2021; Campbell GJ et al.,2020). They also participate in DNA damage repair, cellular senescence and apoptosis by binding to the cyclin-CDK complex [30] (Cánepa ET et al.,2007). According to a related study, CDKN2C is closely associated with pheochromocytoma and medullary thyroid carcinoma [31] (van Veelen W et al.,2009). CDK inhibitors, particularly those that inhibit CDK4 and CDK6 enzyme activity, are currently approved for treating metastatic hormone receptor-positive breast cancer by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) [32] (Zhang M et al.,2021). However, a related study revealed that the above three genes did not differ significantly between prostate cancer and paracancerous tissues [25] (Park DJ et al.,1997), which is consistent with our findings.

The Cyclin-Dependent Kinase 6 (CDK6) is a classical cell cycle protein kinase that phosphorylates and inactivates the retinoblastoma protein (Rb) to initiate the cell cycle transition from G1 phase to S phase [33] (Nebenfuehr S et al., 2020). There is growing evidence that CDK6 plays a significant role in tumor development by regulating /apoptosis, senescence and proliferation of cells [34, 35] (Gao X et al., 2020; Wang H et al.,2017). A study has found that hematological malignancies can develop as a result of CDK6 dysfunction [36] (Chilosi M et al., 1998). In addition, CDK6 deficiency is strongly associated with breast cancer and melanoma development [37, 38] (Goel S et al., 2016; Read J et al., 2016). The role of CDK6 in prostate cancer has been reported. In Chen X et al.‘s study, CDK6 expression was significantly higher in prostate cancer tissues than in normal tissues, and CDK6 inhibition delayed prostate cancer progression [39] (Chen X et al.,2022), similar to our findings.

In epidermis repair and regeneration, ERF is a well-known transcription factor that plays a role in epithelial repair. With strong transcriptional repression activity, it is one of the most widely expressed ETS members, besides this, it has been shown to inhibit cell transformation induced by ETS and suppress tumourigenesis associated with ETS2 [40, 41] (Liu D et al.,1997; Sgouras DN et al.,1995). As one of the largest families of signal-dependent transcriptional regulators, ETS genes mediate cellular proliferation, differentiation and tumour formation. A recent study suggests that co-deletion of Capicua (CIC, another transcription factor) and ERF may contribute to prostate cancer development [42] (Gupta N et al., 2022). A study also found that losing ERF activity leads to activation of the androgen receptor pathway, which results in prostate cancer [43] (Bose R et al., 2017).

MOV10 (Moloney Leukemia Virus 10) is an interferon-induced RNA unwinding enzyme [44] (Cuevas RA et al., 2016) involved in a variety of cellular functions, including binding RNA [45] (Castello A et al., 2012), involvement in mRNA degradation [46] (Gregersen LH et al., 2014), inhibition of tumor progression [47] (El Messaoudi-Aubert S et al., 2010), inhibition of human immunodeficiency virus type 1 (HIV-1) replication [48] (Burdick R et al., 2010) and anti-RNA virus activity [44] (Cuevas RA et al., 2016), among others. A recent study has found that human Burkitt’s lymphoma and cervical cancer cells express MOV10 at levels 2–3 times higher than normal cells [49] (Nakano M et al., 2009). In our study, MOV10 expression levels were significantly higher in prostate cancer cells than in normal prostate cells. However, no further studies are currently underway to assess MOV10’s role in prostate cancer.

In addition to being a transcription factor, TP53 (also called p53) is a putative tumor suppressor gene that plays an important role in mediating cellular senescence and preventing further tumor cell proliferation [50] (Rufini A et al., 2013). In addition, most studies have identified the involvement of p53 in cellular autophagy [51] (Crighton D et al., 2006), repair of genetic damage [52] (Gatz SA et al., 2006), oxidative stress response [53] (Bensaad K et al., 2005) and regulation of cellular senescence [54] (Kortlever RM et al., 2006). It has been shown that p53 mutation is extremely common in tumors, approximately half of all malignancies undergo p53 mutations during progression [55] (Soussi T et al., 2005). The mutation of p53 results in loss of tumor suppressor function, allowing tumor cells to escape immune surveillance [56] (Blagih J et al., 2020), resulting in cancer progression [57] (Vousden KH et al., 2007). Recent research demonstrates that in prostate cancer, mutant p53 (mutp53) drives the formation of an immunosuppressive microenvironment, thereby accelerating progression to castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) [58] (Bian Z et al., 2025), they also comprehensively profiled the immune microenvironment of prostate cancer, delineating three distinct immunophenotypic clusters. This classification facilitates the identification of patients amenable to immunotherapy. Notably, TP53 mutations were predominantly enriched within the non-immunoreactive cluster and conferred resistance to immunotherapy, correlating with diminished clinical efficacy [59] (Meng J et al., 2021). It has been found in our study that prostate cancer tissue expresses the TP53 gene at a significantly higher level than normal tissue, and that the mutation frequency of this gene is significantly higher than other genes.

Immune cells are important components of the tumor microenvironment (TME) and play a key role in tumor development [60] (Hinshaw DC et al., 2019). In terms of tumor immune infiltration, CD8 + T cells, helper T cells, natural killer cells, neutrophils and macrophages were significantly upregulated in high-risk patients, all of these cells are involved in immune-related processes, including the presentation of antigens, the production of cytotoxins, the suppression of tumors and the defense against infections, according to our findings. Researchers have shown that innate immune cells (such as macrophages, dendritic cells, neutrophils, and natural killer cells) and adaptive immune cells (such as T cells and B cells) influence TME to promote tumor growth [60] (Hinshaw DC et al., 2019). Meng et al. demonstrated a significant disparity in immune infiltration profiles between prostate cancer (PCa) and control tissues. Plasma cells, regulatory T cells (Tregs), and mast cells were implicated in PCa progression, whereas M2-polarized macrophages emerged as an independent prognostic predictor in PCa patients [61] (Meng J et al., 2019). We found that TP53 and MOV10 are highly expressed in prostate cancer tissues and show a positive correlation with immune cell expression. In addition to it, patients at high risk have a greater infiltration of immune cells. It appears from the above results that during the occurrence and development of prostate cancer, the OIS-genes may affect tumor-infiltrating immune cells in the tumor microenvironment, which in turn influences tumor progression.

The tumor mutational burden (TMB) is a measure of the number of mutations in tumors. The more frequently tumor cells undergo mutations, the more tumor antigens are carried on their surface and the more likely it is to activate the immune system [62] (Jardim DL et al., 2021). Previous studies have shown that TMB is positively correlated with immunotherapy effectiveness, therefore, TMB detection can be used to predict the efficacy of immunotherapy in cancer patients, making it a promising biomarker for cancer immune checkpoint blockade therapy (ICB) [63, 64] (Maleki Vareki S, 2018; Chan TA et al., 2019). In our research, a higher TMB as well as a significant rise in the expression of common immune checkpoints, including CTLA4, PD1, and PD-L1, were found in the high-risk cohort, which may inform therapeutic methods in prostate cancer and predicting efficacy.

Chemotherapy is often employed as a last-resort treatment for androgen-independent prostate cancer. However, unfortunately, it has proven rarely successful in advanced disease, necessitating the development of superior treatment modalities. p53, a sequence-specific transcription factor encoded by the TP53 gene, is upregulated in response to DNA-damaging stressors including ultraviolet radiation, cytotoxic agents, and hypoxia. Existing evidence indicates that TP53 functionality governs chemotherapeutic sensitivity in malignant cells, advanced prostate cancer cells lacking functional p53 protein exhibit heightened resistance to chemotherapy or radiation. Treatment with DNA-damaging agents (e.g., chemotherapeutic drugs, irradiation) may further promote progression in cancers harboring mutant p53 [65]. (Chappell WH et al., 2012)

In studies on drug sensitivity, previous research has found that upregulation of CDK6 may represent a crucial mechanism enabling breast cancer cells to overcome fulvestrant-mediated growth inhibition. In patients with advanced ER-positive breast cancer exhibiting high CDK6 expression in metastatic lesions, progression-free survival (PFS) following fulvestrant treatment is significantly shortened.

Regarding pharmacological treatment, we found that patients in the high-risk group were more sensitive to common antineoplastic drugs such as paclitaxel, docetaxel, doxorubicin, methotrexate, rapamycin and vincristine, which may provide a reference for prostate cancer treatment. Since AR is strongly implicated in prostate cancer, anti-androgens are used in its treatment. Androgen depletion therapy (ADT) is the first line of treatment for advanced PCa in clinical practice. Bicalutamide is one of the first-generation antagonists, which is commonly used as a long-term treatment for prostate cancer. Further, we calculated the IC50 values for Bicalutamide between low- and high-risk groups. As we can see, IC50 values were much lower in the low-risk group, indicating that low-risk patients are more drug-sensitive to bicalutamide than high-risk patients. However, as prostate cancer patients progressed to castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC), bicalutamide’s effectiveness decreased [66] (Cornford P et al., 2021). As a consequence, we suggest that OIS genes may play a pivotal role in the progression of advanced prostate cancers that resist castration. In addition to ADT, chemotherapy is a vital treatment option for PCa. Among the commonly used chemotherapeutic agents for prostate cancer, docetaxel improves overall survival for patients with metastatic, de-escalation-resistant prostate cancer [67, 68] (Tannock IF et al., 2004; Petrylak DP et al., 2004), which is widely used in clinical practice. A high-risk group of patients had a higher drug sensitivity to docetaxel, according to our study. Following ADT, taxane-based chemotherapy as well as immunotherapy with Sipuleucel-T, FDA-approved PARP inhibitors (Opalarib and Rucaparib) for the treatment of Castration-Resistant Prostate Cancer [69] (Schaeffer E et al., 2021). Currently, PARP-1 inhibitors are approved for treating ovarian, breast and prostate cancers harboring mutations in the BRCA gene. In comparison with enzalutamide and abiraterone, Olaparib was associated with longer progression-free survival and better patient-reported endpoints [70] (de Bono J et al., 2020). Olaparib has been approved by the FDA for patients with metastatic goitre-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC) who have progressed after treatment with enzalutamide and/or abiraterone based on the results of the PROfound trial [70] (de Bono J et al., 2020). Therefore, we further explore the sensitivity of two subgroups to Olaparib. The results of our drug sensitivity analyses found that high risk group confers high sensitivity to Olaparib. The above findings can be informative to some extent in terms of individualized prostate cancer treatment.

In spite of this, there still exist several limitations in our study. First, the retrospective design and exclusive reliance on public databases introduce inherent selection biases, necessitating validation through prospective multicenter cohorts and mechanistic interrogation using in vitro/vivo models. Second, experimental validation was restricted to PCR-based assays, warranting deeper mechanistic exploration through functional studies (e.g., gene knockdown/overexpression) and pathway analyses. The RT-qPCR validation cohort (n = 5 paired tumor/adjacent tissues) had limited statistical power despite detecting significant differential expression of key genes (CDK6, MOV10, TP53; p < 0.05) under a paired-sample design. Although this approach provides preliminary target validation, larger independent cohorts are warranted for confirmation. Additionally, absence of protein-level corroboration (e.g., Western blot/IHC) precludes assessment of post-transcriptional regulation effects (e.g., CDKN2B). Besides, our drug sensitivity analysis based on the pRRopheric algorithm is derived from cancer cell line data and fails to simulate the complexity of the tumour microenvironment. These predictions are purely computational speculations and must be validated through in vitro and in vivo experiments before their clinical significance can be considered. Their value primarily lies in prioritising candidate screening for future mechanistic studies.At last, elevated PSA levels in a small number of patients did not necessarily cause tumor progression, so the use of BCR for predicting patient prognosis was not completely absolute [10] (Van den Broeck et al., 2019). In the case of appeals, our findings would be more accurate and informative if appeal issues were improved.

Conclusion

This study developed and independently validated a prognostic risk model incorporating seven oncogene-induced senescence genes (OISGs), which significantly stratifies biochemical recurrence risk in prostate cancer patients. Furthermore, we demonstrate that these OISGs correlate with differential immune cell infiltration patterns. Our findings are expected to lead to new ideas for guiding prostate cancer integrated treatment and mitigate BCR risk and ultimately improve clinical outcomes.
