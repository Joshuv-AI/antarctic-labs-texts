The relationship between disability, anxiety and depression in older adults with chronic disease comorbidities: the mediating role of frailty
Authors: Honghong Wen, Qingyun Mao, Zhixia Jiang, Heting Liang, Yumeng Zhang, Shaoting Yang, Yunting Li, Yan Xiong, Xiaoli Yuan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe global aging crisis has intensified concerns about disability, frailty, anxiety, and depression in older adults, particularly those with multimorbidity. However, the interrelationships among these factors remain not fully understood. This study investigates the mediating role of frailty between disability and anxiety/depression in this population.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted from October 2022 to January 2023, enrolling 4,372 older adults with multimorbidity from 3 tertiary hospitals, 5 secondary hospitals, and 3 community health centers in Zunyi, China. Data were collected using validated instruments: Activities of Daily Living (ADL), the Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS), Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7), Geriatric Depression Scale-Short Form (GDS-15), and Frailty Phenotype. Spearman correlation, binary logistic regression, and mediation analysis (SPSS PROCESS 3.5) with Bootstrap testing (5,000 resamples) were employed.ResultsAmong participants, 437 (10.0%) exhibited disability. Significant correlations were observed between disability and anxiety/depression (r = 0.32–0.41, p < 0.001). Frailty partially mediated the disability-anxiety (50.45% of total effect) and disability-depression (48.17%) relationships (p < 0.001).ConclusionFrailty serves as a critical mediator between disability, anxiety and depression in older adults with chronic disease comorbidity. Targeted interventions addressing disability and frailty may significantly mitigate anxiety and depression, thereby improving mental health and quality of life in this vulnerable population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06610-4.

Introduction

China has one of the world’s fastest - aging populations [1]. In 2022, 14.9% of the population was 65 and older, and this is expected to reach 26.1% by 2050 [2]. Given this rapid aging, studying aging-related health issues is crucial for medicine and societal sustainability. Older adults often face psychological problems due to physical aging, chronic diseases, worsening social relations, and economic factors [3–6]. Anxiety and depression are common among them [7, 8], and can severely affect their quality of life, even leading to suicide [9]. The rise in global life expectancy has increased chronic disease co - morbidity [10], raising the incidence of anxiety and depression in older adults. Wang et al. found that 11.85% of older adults with chronic comorbidities had anxiety symptoms, compared to 9.72% without, with the former 1.399 times more likely [11]. Thus, identifying factors influencing these mental health issues is vital for elderly care.

With aging, older adults experience degeneration of organs and tissues, leading to reduced daily activity and possible disability [12]. Those with chronic comorbidities are at a much higher disability risk [13]. Chronic diseases lower exercise capacity, and inflammation worsens physical harm. Drug treatment and nursing add to the burden. There is a strong link between daily activity (ADL), anxiety, and depression [14]. Declining ADL causes frustration, and combined with chronic disease discomfort, reduced self - efficacy, and less social interaction, it increases the risk of anxiety and depression in older adults [15, 16]. Chronic comorbidities are closely related to disability [17], with more diseases leading to higher disability prevalence, which impacts the elderly’s quality of life and mental health. The combined effect of chronic comorbidities and disability on older adults’ mental health is complex, but research in this area is scarce.

Frailty, prevalent among older adults, causes physiological, psychological, and social deterioration. Impaired physical function makes older adults more vulnerable to anxiety and depression [18, 19]. This decline reduces physical activity, impairs mobility and daily function, and compromises nutritional status and muscle strength [20]. In older adults, frailty commonly coexists with chronic diseases; their shared physiological pathways drive complex health issues. As a state of diminished physiological reserve, frailty reflects dysregulation across multiple systems, including musculoskeletal, nervous, and endocrine. Chronic conditions like cardiovascular, cerebrovascular, and cognitive disorders inflict cumulative stress on bodily systems, amplifying the physiological underpinnings of frailty. This reinforces the strong link between disability and frailty, particularly in maintaining ADL. Although some studies [6, 12] have examined the impact of chronic diseases on frailty and disability, research lacks in-depth theoretical models and empirical evidence regarding their combined effects on older adults’ mental health, specifically anxiety and depression. For instance, Shanshan Yu’s study [21] showed that chronic heart failure patients’ exercise intolerance due to cardiac insufficiency worsened physical limitations, exacerbating nutritional deficits and muscle atrophy in frailty. The dual burden of disease and frailty also increased psychological distress, elevating anxiety and depression prevalence. These research [22–24] findings strongly confirm the mutual influence of frailty, disability, and chronic disease comorbidity at the physiological level and their severe impact on the mental health of older adults. However, it is important to note that frailty is a reversible dynamic syndrome. Older adults’ identification of frailty and effective intervention can reverse or slow the occurrence of multi-dimensional declines. Therefore, this study investigates the mediating role and nature of frailty between disability and anxiety and depression in the context of chronic disease comorbidity. This not only helps uncover the pathogenesis of mental health disorders in older adults but also provides a scientific basis for developing personalized intervention measures.

This study aims to explore frailty’s mediating role between disability, anxiety, and depression, innovatively incorporating chronic comorbidities as a background factor. By examining these relationships, it contributes to comprehensively understanding the mechanisms connecting disability and mental health problems. The study proposes two hypotheses (Fig. 1): (1) disability significantly impacts anxiety and depression; (2) frailty mediates the relationship among disability, anxiety, and depression.

Methods

Research design and subjects

This study adopted a cross-sectional research design. Convenience sampling method was used to select older adults with comorbid chronic diseases from three tertiary hospitals, five secondary hospitals, and three community health service centers in Zunyi City, Guizhou Province from October 2022 to January 2023 as the research subjects. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) aged ≥ 60 years old; (2) without a history of severe mental illness, conscious, and with normal communication ability; (3) giving informed consent and voluntarily participating in the study; (4) suffering from two or more chronic diseases. The exclusion criteria included: (1) those with severe diseases who could not cooperate; (2) those suffering from only one chronic disease. This study has been reviewed and approved by the Ethics Review Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Zunyi Medical University (Ethics Review No.: KLL2022-814), and was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Each participant freely consented to participate in the study and provided informed consent. Finally, a total of 4,372 elderly patients with comorbid chronic diseases were included in this study.

Measures

General information questionnaire

A self-designed general information questionnaire was used, which included items such as gender, age, educational level, marital status, children, type and mode of residence, social support, the number of chronic diseases, etc. At the same time, alcohol consumption and smoking habits were evaluated to comprehensively understand the health-related behaviors in the living background of the older adults.

Chronic disease questionnaire

Using the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) [25] questionnaire, the survey assessed 15 chronic diseases: hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes/elevated blood glucose, malignant neoplasms (e.g., cancer), chronic lung diseases, liver disorders, heart disease, stroke, kidney diseases, gastric/digestive system disorders, emotional/psychiatric problems, memory-related disorders, Parkinson’s disease, arthritis/rheumatic diseases, and asthma. Participants were asked, “Has a doctor ever diagnosed you with any of the following diseases?” Older adults indicated whether they had one or more conditions, and the number of comorbidities was recorded.

Assessment of disability: activities of daily living (ADL) scale

This scale was developed by Sidney Katz in 1963 [26], and it has high reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.951) [27]. It is used to evaluate the self-care ability of older adults. The scale covers basic skills such as using the toilet, dressing, taking a bath, etc. The total score of the scale is 100 points, and the higher the score, the stronger the independence. A score above 60 indicates basic self-care ability, a score between 60 and 40 means partial assistance is needed, a score between 40 and 20 indicates a great deal of assistance is required, and a score below 20 means complete dependence.

Assessment of anxiety: the self-rating anxiety scale (SAS)

This scale was developed by W.K. Zung in 1971 [28] and is widely applied in the fields of clinical and psychological research. It consists of 20 items, comprehensively covering aspects such as anxious emotions, physiological reactions, and behavioral manifestations, and serves as a self-rating tool for evaluating an individual’s anxiety level. A 4-point rating scale is used to assess the frequency of anxiety symptoms. Fifteen items in the scale are scored positively, and five items are scored negatively. A total score of ≥ 50 points is regarded as indicating the presence of anxiety. The internal consistency of this scale, Cronbach’s α, is 0.937 [29].

Assessment of depression: the geriatric depression scale-short form (GDS-15)

This scale was designed by Sheikh and Yesavage in 1982 [30] and simplified from the GDS-30 to enable the rapid assessment of depressive symptoms in the elderly. The scale consists of 15 items, covering symptoms such as low mood, reduced activity, irritability, withdrawal, a sense of miserable happiness, and negative self-evaluation. A response of “yes” is scored as 1 point, and a response of “no” is also scored as 1 point. If the total score is ≥ 6 points, it suggests the possible presence of depressive symptoms. The internal consistency of this scale, Cronbach’s α, is 0.745 [31].

Assessment of frailty: the frailty scale (FRAIL)

This scale was proposed by Morley JE and others in 2012 [32] and is a simple and practical tool for assessing the frailty status older adults. It consists of 5 questions: fatigue, difficulty climbing stairs, difficulty walking 500 m, suffering from more than 5 diseases, and weight loss of more than 3 kg. A score of “yes” is given 1 point, and a score of “no” is given 0 point. The total score ranges from 0 to 5 points. A score of 0 indicates no frailty, a score of 1 to 2 points indicates pre-frailty, and a score of 3 points or above indicates frailty. The Cronbach’s α of this scale is 0.826 [33].

Data collection

This study adopted a questionnaire survey administered by five trained assessors. Prior to data collection, the research team addressed potential issues in questionnaire administration and established standardized guiding language. Assessors used unified and fixed language to explain the study’s purpose, significance, and completion procedures to patients and families. Questionnaires were administered after obtaining informed consent from older adults with chronic comorbidities and their families. After obtaining the informed consent of older adults patients with comorbid chronic diseases and their families, the questionnaires were filled out. Mainly, the patients filled out the questionnaires by themselves. For patients who lacked the ability to fill out the questionnaires independently, they could orally state the answers and the assessors would fill them out on their behalf. During the filling process, the assessors were present throughout the whole process to promptly answer the questions of the patients and their families. A total of 4,820 questionnaires were distributed, with 88 invalid ones excluded, leaving 4,732 questionnaires collected. Ultimately, 4,732 valid questionnaires were included, resulting in a 98% effective return rate.

Statistical methods

Data were entered and analyzed using SPSS 29.0 (IBM Corporation, NY, USA). Demographic characteristics were presented as numbers and percentages or mean ± standard deviation (SD). The normality of the data was tested using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. For measurement data that conformed to a normal distribution, the t-test or analysis of variance was used for comparisons between groups; for data that did not conform to a normal distribution, the rank-sum test was applied. Spearman correlation analysis was used to explore the correlations among the main research variables. After adjusting for confounding factors, binary logistic regression was used to estimate the association between disabled or normal older adults and anxiety/depression. Finally, SPSS PROCESS 3.5 was used to establish a simple mediating model of frailty in the effect of disability on anxiety/depression. The mediating effect of frailty was further tested using the Bootstrap method. If the 95% confidence interval (CI) did not include 0, it indicated the presence of a mediating effect. All statistical tests were two-tailed tests, and p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participants’ characteristics

The average age of the 4,372 older adults with comorbid chronic diseases was 72.57 ± 7.22 years old, and there were 2,239 males (51.2%). Among them, 437 older adults (10.0%) had comorbid disabilities, 1,278 (29.2%) had anxiety, and 532 (12.1%) had depression. The differential analysis showed that age, gender, educational level, place of residence, and social support had a significant impact on anxiety symptoms; age, educational level, place of residence, and social support had a significant impact on depressive symptoms. Association between the presence of ADL and anxiety or depression in older adults with chronic disease Comorbidity

A binary logistic regression was used to estimate the association between the presence of disability and anxiety or depressive symptoms. A stratified multiple models of binary logistic regression was used to estimate the association between the presence of disability and anxiety or depressive symptoms. As shown in Table 3, Model 1 explored the association between the presence of disability and anxiety or depressive symptoms after adjusting for relevant information such as marital status, living arrangement, number of children, alcohol consumption, and smoking. The results showed that compared with older adults without disability, older adults with disability had a significantly increased risk of developing anxiety symptoms (OR = 0.28, 95% CI: 0.24–0.33) and also had a significantly increased risk of developing depressive symptoms (OR = 0.27, 95% CI: 0.24–0.31). On the basis of Model 1, Model 2 added age, gender, educational level, place of residence, and social support. The results showed that older adults with disability also had a significantly increased risk of developing anxiety and depressive symptoms. When frailty was further included in this model, the results showed that older adults with both disability and frailty had an even more significant risk of developing anxiety and depression. These results indicate that the ability to perform daily activities is a major predictor of anxiety and depressive symptoms in the older adults. In addition, the forest plot of Model 3 for anxiety is shown in Fig. 2, and the forest plot of Model 3 for depression is shown in Fig. 3. The forest plots indicate that gender, education, alcohol consumption, social support, disability, and frailty are contributing factors to anxiety symptoms in the older adults; smoking, alcohol consumption, living arrangement, social support, disability, and frailty are contributing factors to depression in the older adults. Analysis of the mediating effect of frailty between disability and anxiety or depression in elderly people with chronic disease comorbidity

The Process 3.5 developed by Andrew F. Hayes was used to construct a simple mediating model to examine the relationship between disability and anxiety or depression. A Bootstrap test was also conducted to verify the mediating role of frailty (Fig. 4 and Fig. 5). The research results showed that frailty played a partial mediating role between disability and anxiety. Therefore, after adjusting for control variables such as gender, education, alcohol consumption, and social support, the total effect of disability on anxiety was − 0.111 (p < 0.001). When frailty was used as a mediating variable, the direct effect decreased significantly (β = −0.056, p < 0.001). The total indirect effect was − 0.056 (95% CI: −0.131, −0.100), and the ratio to the total effect was 50.45% (−0.056/−0.111). At the same time, in the depression model, the research results also showed that frailty partially mediated the association between disability and depression. Of course, after adjusting for control variables such as smoking, alcohol consumption, living arrangement, and social support, we found that the total effect of disability on depression was − 0.050 (p < 0.001). When frailty was introduced into the model as a mediating variable, the direct effect also decreased significantly (β = −0.026, p < 0.001). Discussion

Amid global efforts to promote healthy aging, safeguarding mental health has emerged as a critical priority for improving quality of life in older adults [34]. In this study, we explored the mechanisms through which frailty influences the relationship between disability and anxiety/depression in the context of chronic disease comorbidities. Our findings revealed two key insights: (1) disability directly impacts the development of anxiety and depression, and (2) frailty partially mediates this relationship. These results offer a refined perspective for mental health research targeting older adults with chronic disease comorbidities, addressing a critical research gap and providing insights to advance healthy aging and enhance the quality of life for older adults.

Direct association between disability and anxiety/depression in older adults with chronic comorbidities

Our results indicate that disability directly influences the process of anxiety and depression, suggesting that older adults with lesser degrees of disability experience lower levels of anxiety and depressive symptoms, which is in line with prior research [35]. In the Global Burden of Disease, Injuries, and Risk Factors Study, anxiety and depression rank among the top 25 contributors to global disability-adjusted life years (DALYs). In the present study, the prevalence rates of disability, anxiety, and depression among older adults with chronic disease comorbidities were 10%, 29.2%, and 12.1%, respectively, aligning with findings from previous research [36–38]. This indicates that these conditions are common in older adults with chronic disease comorbidities, highlighting the shared challenges in managing their health.A Meta - analysis [37]on Chinese older adults with chronic diseases shows they experience an earlier and faster decline in ADL. Due to the long - term nature of chronic diseases, with low cure rates, easy recurrence, and high disability rates, older adults’ physical conditions deteriorate, leaving them less confident to manage disease - related negative effects, thus making them more prone to severe psychological issues [39–41]. Studies [42, 43] on chronic diseases and disability in older adults show that those with conditions like cognitive impairment, extrapyramidal disorders, and cerebrovascular disease experience progressive decline in self-care ability. Such individuals are more susceptible to anxiety, depression, and other negative emotions, which in turn exacerbate disability—creating a vicious cycle with the underlying disease. This stems from the close link between chronic disease progression/prognosis and daily activities: following disease onset, patients face not only physical burdens but also lifestyle disruptions and financial stress, collectively increasing vulnerability to anxiety and depression. Moreover, disability restricts older adults’ activity ranges, detaching them from their social circles, reducing social participation, and causing serious loneliness. Declining self - care ability also forces dependence on others for daily tasks and outings. These significant life changes can trigger anxiety and depression [44]. In R. Schulz’s review [45] on caregiver support, it’s highlighted that caregiving negatively impacts caregivers’ physical and mental health, putting them at risk of low quality of life and heightened isolation. This is particularly evident when caring for disabled older adults, as it can disrupt family routines and daily life, leading to uneven distribution of family responsibilities, tension, and conflict among family members. Under such circumstances, older adults are also prone to developing anxiety and depression. This indirectly underscores the critical role of daily activity training for older adults with chronic disease comorbidities in preventing disability. Because regular daily physical activity can strengthen muscles, effectively counteract activity limitations caused by disease progression, and reduce the risk of disability and mental health problems. As per Alizadeh et al.‘s [46]. study, daily physical activity also stimulates the secretion of various antidepressant factors, such as irisin, hypothalamic secretin−1, neuropeptide (NPY), and tryptophan. Thus, proper exercise brings physical and mental pleasure, reducing anxiety and depression. Therefore, improving and protecting older adults’ daily activity abilities is crucial for preventing mental health issues and mitigating the psychological consequences of disability.

Frailty mediates the association between disability and anxiety/depression in older adults with chronic comorbidities

Our study shows that frailty partially mediates the impact of disability on anxiety and depression in older adults, indicating that disability may influence mental health through frailty. Previous studies [47, 48] have shown that disability is linked to frailty, which in turn correlates with anxiety and depression. Our research highlights how disability, frailty, anxiety, and depression interact to accelerate psychological decline—particularly in individuals with multiple chronic conditions. Oxidative stress and malnutrition serve as key drivers in this process: these physiological mechanisms underlie both frailty progression and chronic disease pathogenesis. For example, research [49] in older adults with cardiovascular disease has shown oxidative stress markers independently associate with frailty, damaging tissues via multiple pathways [50]. In chronic disease contexts, nutritional deficits (e.g., serum protein, vitamin, and trace element deficiencies) exacerbate immune dysfunction, muscle wasting/atrophy, and functional impairment. Tazzeo et al. [51]noted that age-related declines in muscle strength and mass are key contributors to frailty, while GONG et al. [52]found a fourfold increased risk of depression in frail elderly individuals and a similar risk of frailty in those with depression, indicating a bidirectional association. Hiriscau et al. [53]further supported that frailty is linked to higher anxiety scores. For older adults with chronic comorbidities, chronic pain and helplessness reduce social participation, leading to loneliness and isolation, which, combined with financial and psychological burdens, create a vicious cycle of disability, frailty, and mental health issues [54]. For older adults with chronic comorbidities, chronic pain and helplessness reduce social participation, leading to loneliness and isolation. Combined with financial and psychological burdens, these factors create a vicious cycle of disability, frailty, and mental health issues [54]. Thus, as daily functioning declines and frailty progresses in this population, anxiety and depression symptoms worsen significantly. Our study confirms a strong link between frailty and disability, with potential bidirectional causality between frailty and anxiety/depression—findings that may complicate frailty’s mediating role. Theoretically, however, frailty remains a key marker of physical decline and likely serves as an important mediator. Further research is needed to fully evaluate how disability influences mental health via frailty [55].

The potential physiological link between disability, frailty, and anxiety/depression is complex. It may stem from the interplay of neurobiological and inflammatory mechanisms. Neuroimaging shows that frailty is closely tied to brain structural changes, particularly reduced gray matter volume in areas linked to depression symptoms, such as the thalamus, precentral gyrus, anterior cingulate cortex, and ventral striatum. These changes may disrupt synaptic plasticity, affecting emotional regulation and cognitive function, and increasing depression and anxiety risk [56].

Inflammatory markers like C-reactive protein and interleukin−6 are significantly associated with the occurrence and progression of frailty and disability [57]. elevated inflammatory markers in frail individuals might trigger depressive symptoms by influencing neurotransmitter metabolism and activating the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis [58]. thus, addressing inflammation through lifestyle interventions can mitigate age - related diseases and improve overall health.

Conclusion and limitations

Our large-sample study confirms the significant association between disability, anxiety, and depression among older adults with chronic disease comorbidities, and highlights the critical mediating role of frailty in this relationship. However, several limitations should be considered. First, the cross-sectional design inherently restricts causal inference, and reverse causality (e.g., depression potentially contributing to frailty and disability) cannot be ruled out. Longitudinal studies are strongly recommended to clarify temporal sequences and establish causal pathways. Second, the use of self-reported measures for disability, frailty, anxiety, and depression may introduce recall bias and social desirability bias. Future research should incorporate objective assessments, such as laboratory-based biomarkers of frailty (e.g., IL-6, hs-CRP, HbA1c, GDF-15, DHEAS). Third, although we adjusted for multiple known confounders, residual confounding by unmeasured variables remains possible. Furthermore, future studies would benefit from including a broader range of modifiable mediators across physiological, psychological, and social domains to identify additional intervention targets. Finally, the generalizability of our findings may be limited to older adults in similar socio-cultural and regional contexts, such as those in Zunyi, China.

Despite these limitations, this study underscores the importance of addressing frailty in the management of mental health among comorbid older adults. It provides a foundation for the development of integrated interventions and offers valuable insights for public health policies aimed at promoting healthy aging and reducing the societal burden of late-life disability and mental disorders.
