Myeloid cell reprogramming drives enhanced defense against Streptococcus pneumoniae lung infection following exposure to commensal Prevotella
Authors: Sara N. Stoner, Eric D. Larson, Sam Fulte, Steven C. Shaw, Erin R. Fish, Edward N. Janoff, Matthias Mack, Sarah E. Clark
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceEven though Streptococcus pneumoniae is the most common cause of bacterial lung infections, most people who are exposed to this bacterium can successfully defend against infection. We investigated a mechanism for this natural resilience against infection by exploring how the immune system is educated by lung exposures to a common airway bacterium, Prevotella. We found that Prevotella alters the protective functions of immune cells recruited to the lungs after S. pneumoniae challenge, resulting in improved immune-mediated bacterial clearance. This study reveals the cell type–specific transcriptional changes which occur in the lungs during S. pneumoniae infection, and how these are altered in a setting of improved infection defense following Prevotella exposure.

Growing evidence points to the respiratory tract microbiome as a key determinant of pneumonia outcomes (1–3). The upper respiratory tract acts as a microbial reservoir, with commensal bacteria from the oropharynx serving as the primary source of transient microbial exposures in the lower airway (4, 5). Greater microbial diversity in the upper and lower airway correlates with improved survival in pneumonia patients (6–8), whereas depletion of obligate anaerobes, including oral commensals (6), through antibiotic treatment or chronic oxygen therapy is linked to worse outcomes (9).

Prevotella species are core members of the oral microbiome and are consistently among the most abundant bacterial taxa detected in the lungs of healthy individuals (4, 10). Higher Prevotella abundance is inversely associated with respiratory pathogens in both the upper airway (8, 11) and the lungs (4, 12, 13). Specifically, the species Prevotella melaninogenica is enriched in healthy individuals compared to patients suffering from pneumonia, acute respiratory distress syndrome, and exacerbations of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (11, 14, 15). In one report, P. melaninogenica was the most discriminatory species distinguishing healthy subjects from those with Streptococcus pneumoniae (pneumococcal) pneumonia (8). Beyond correlations with health, Prevotella are increasingly recognized as immunomodulatory. Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) samples enriched with Prevotella exhibit increased numbers of activated neutrophils and higher Th17 cell frequencies (16, 17), and lung transplant recipients with more diverse microbial profiles containing Prevotella have elevated expression of IL-10 (18). A single dose of P. melaninogenica, in combination with two other oral commensals, increased airway clearance of S. pneumoniae from the lungs of mice at 24 h and up to 14 d postexposure to the commensal mixture (19). These findings suggest that Prevotella may shape lung immune tone and infection susceptibility.

To mechanistically address the link between Prevotella and improved pneumonia outcomes, we previously determined that pre-exposure to live or heat-killed P. melaninogenica promoted rapid clearance of S. pneumoniae from the lungs (20), with a similar effect observed against Staphylococcus aureus, demonstrating a protective function against multiple respiratory pathogens (21). Here, scRNA-seq was used to define the transcriptional immune landscape during S. pneumoniae infection in susceptible vs. resistant hosts, with resistance modeled by improved pathogen clearance following P. melaninogenica priming.

Results

P. melaninogenica Exposure Alters the Myeloid Cell Response to Pneumococcal Pneumonia.

Mice primed with heat-killed (HK) P. melaninogenica 24 h prior to S. pneumoniae challenge had significantly reduced S. pneumoniae burdens at 24 h postinfection compared to unprimed mice (Fig. 1A). Single-cell RNA sequencing was performed on lung tissue of naïve mice, P. melaninogenica-exposed mice, and S. pneumoniae–infected mice with or without prior exposure to P. melaninogenica (Fig. 1B). Clustering analysis identified 30 distinct cell populations in lung tissue (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A–D). Myeloid cell populations included monocytes, macrophages, neutrophils, dendritic cells, and basophils (Fig. 1 C and D). Broad changes among several myeloid cell populations were apparent in response to both S. pneumoniae infection and P. melaninogenica exposure, including large expansions in recruited monocytes and neutrophils with corresponding reductions in tissue-resident macrophages (Fig. 1E). Two neutrophil populations were detected, both of which were increased in P. melaninogenica-exposed and S. pneumoniae–infected mice compared to naïve mice. Recruited immune cells detected at baseline in naïve mice are likely in the marginated vascular compartment of the lungs, as reported elsewhere (22). Neutrophil recruitment increased in both P. melaninogenica-exposed and S. pneumoniae–infected mice, but was lower in mice pre-exposed to P. melaninogenica with enhanced S. pneumoniae clearance (Fig. 1F). Inflammatory monocytes were recruited to the lung tissue in P. melaninogenica-exposed mice and in even greater numbers in S. pneumoniae–infected mice (Fig. 1F). As with neutrophils, numbers of recruited inflammatory monocytes were lower with dual-bacterial exposure than with either alone. Tissue-resident alveolar macrophages (AMs), prominent at baseline, were depleted in both P. melaninogenica-exposed and S. pneumoniae–infected mice, alone and in combination, as anticipated following an acute inflammatory stimulus (23, 24), but more subtly with P. melaninogenica alone (Fig. 1F). A minority population of macrophages with high expression of cell division genes was present in all conditions (SI Appendix, Fig. S2), consistent with prior reports of a proliferative AM subpopulation (24).

Of note, two distinct populations of monocyte-derived macrophages (MDM) were identified which differed proportionally between groups; a prominent C1qa+ MDM population selectively enriched in S. pneumoniae–infected mice and a smaller Cxcl3+ MDM population predominantly enriched in P. melaninogenica-exposed mice (Fig. 1F). Partition-based graph abstraction (PAGA) clustering and pseudotime analysis revealed that C1qa+ MDMs clustered closely with immature monocytes, whereas Cxcl3+ MDMs clustered closer to the mature, tissue resident-like macrophages (Fig. 1G). Additionally, the prominent C1qa+ MDMs expressed high levels of pro-inflammatory “M1-like” signature genes, while the Cxcl3+ MDMs and tissue resident-like macrophage populations showed greater expression of pro-resolution “M2-like” signature genes (Fig. 1H). This pattern aligns with reports indicating that recruited macrophage populations exhibit an M1-like phenotype, and tissue-resident macrophages are more M2-like during acute lung injury (24, 25).

Given our prior finding that toll-like receptor 2 (TLR2) signaling was required for P. melaninogenica–induced neutrophil activation and lung clearance of S. pneumoniae (20), we examined Tlr2 expression patterns. Tlr2 was primarily expressed in myeloid cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A), focusing our analysis on myeloid cell types to identify gene expression pathways associated with P. melaninogenica-mediated protection. Unbiased pathway enrichment analysis of S. pneumoniae–infected mice with vs. without P. melaninogenica priming revealed distinctly enriched immune activation pathways, including TNF signaling and antigen presentation, in myeloid cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S3B). Differential signaling pathway enrichment was also apparent among other nonmyeloid cell types including epithelial cells and adaptive immune cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S3C).

Immune Priming by P. melaninogenica Induces a Coordinated Recruitment of Myeloid Cells.

Myeloid cell recruitment over time was examined in P. melaninogenica-primed mice via flow cytometry (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A). Neutrophils and Ly6C+ monocytes and MDMs were recruited to the lung tissue as early as 6 h postexposure to P. melaninogenica, with persistence at 24 h (Fig. 2A). This cellular recruitment coincided with elevated concentrations of neutrophil-recruiting chemokines CXCL1, CXCL2, and CXCL5 as well as the monocyte chemoattractant CCL2 in lung BAL fluid (Fig. 2B). CellChat analysis, which predicts cell–cell communication networks, in all treatment groups combined revealed multiple significantly enriched chemokine signaling pathways. For neutrophil-recruiting chemokines, Cxcl2-Cxcr2 and Cxcl3-Cxcr2 signaling pathways were both significantly enriched, with the Cxcl3+ MDM population predicted as a major source of CXCL2 sensed by Cxcr2-expressing neutrophils. CellChat analysis also identified the Ccl6-Ccr1 signaling pathway as significantly enriched, with several macrophage populations predicted to signal to Ccr1-expressing neutrophils (Fig. 2 C–E). Cxcl1 signaling networks were not significantly enriched. Stratification by condition showed that Cxcl2, Cxcl3, and Ccl6 were highly expressed in macrophage populations from P. melaninogenica-exposed mice, particularly the Cxcl3+ MDMs which were selectively enriched in this group, consistent with the high neutrophil cell counts observed in P. melaninogenica-exposed mice.

Neutrophils were predicted to engage in autocrine signaling, as they expressed both chemokines and their corresponding receptors (Fig. 2C), suggesting that neutrophils contribute to their own recruitment through positive feedback. Cxcl5, which encodes a neutrophil-recruiting chemokine produced by epithelial cells, was expressed by goblet cells and predicted to signal to Cxcr2-expressing neutrophils (Fig. 2F). Last, Ccl2, which encodes a monocyte-recruiting chemokine, was expressed by fibroblasts and C1qa+ MDMs, and was predicted to signal to Ccr2-expressing inflammatory monocytes (Fig. 2G). Condition-specific analysis revealed that fibroblasts from S. pneumoniae–infected mice expressed high levels of Ccl2 (Fig. 2G), correlating with the increased inflammatory monocyte counts observed in these mice compared to other groups. Both the C1qa+ MDMs and Cxcl3+ MDMs also expressed the T cell–recruiting chemokines Cxcl16, Cxcl10, and Cxcl9, predicted to signal to Cxcr6 and Cxcr3-expressing regulatory T cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 B–D). These data highlight a coordinated signaling program with predicted contributions from macrophages and epithelial cells to the recruitment of innate immune cells to the lungs following P. melaninogenica exposure.

P. melaninogenica Induces Neutrophil Transcriptional Profiles Associated with Enhanced Phagocytosis and Killing of S. pneumoniae.

We previously reported the unique capacity for P. melaninogenica to enhance S. pneumoniae clearance, as 24 h exposure to other commensal bacteria or Escherichia coli lipopolysaccharide (LPS) did not provide the same protective benefit (20). Here, we found that neutrophils purified from the lungs of P. melaninogenica-exposed mice exhibited an increased capacity to kill S. pneumoniae compared to neutrophils from mice treated with E. coli LPS (Fig. 3A). Neutrophil serine protease activity, a requirement for S. pneumoniae killing after P. melaninogenica exposure, is similarly low in neutrophils of nonprimed and E. coli LPS-treated mice (20). Therefore, PBS treatment was used as a nonprimed, negative control for neutrophil functional assays moving forward. We used an in vivo flow cytometry-based uptake assay to assess the ability of lung neutrophils to phagocytose S. pneumoniae. Neutrophil phagocytosis of S. pneumoniae was significantly increased in the lungs of P. melaninogenica-primed mice (Fig. 3B). In addition to phagocytic and killing capacity, neutrophils were analyzed for surface expression of CD18, an integrin subunit which together with CD11b forms complement receptor 3 (CR3), important for phagocytosis of iC3b-opsonized bacteria (26). Neutrophil expression of CD18 was significantly higher in P. melaninogenica-primed mice (Fig. 3C and SI Appendix, Fig. S5A), correlating with the increased phagocytosis and S. pneumoniae killing. Of note, C3 and other complement associated genes were upregulated in neutrophils and macrophages in P. melaninogenica-primed mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S5B).

Unbiased analysis of the two neutrophil populations identified by scRNA-seq revealed significant enrichment for several gene ontology (GO) terms involved in immune defense in P. melaninogenica-exposed mice (Fig. 3D). To investigate differences in phagocytosis and toll-like receptor signaling associated genes, a module scoring function was applied to assess expression of selected gene sets in aggregate per condition. Module scoring analysis identified a significant upregulation of both phagocytosis signature genes and toll-like receptor signaling genes (Fig. 3E), which stimulate neutrophil activation, phagocytosis, and reactive oxygen species production (27), in P. melaninogenica-exposed mice. Module scoring also indicated increased maturation associated genes in the Mmp9+ neutrophil population, which was smaller in total number compared to the largely expanded Csf1+ neutrophil population, and significantly elevated migration signature genes in both neutrophil populations in P. melaninogenica-exposed mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S5C). Several neutrophil granule genes were reduced in the Csf1+ neutrophil population of P. melaninogenica-primed mice (Fig. 3F), which may indicate regulation of granule synthesis in more matured neutrophils with sufficiently accumulated granules (28, 29). These findings suggest that P. melaninogenica upregulates antibacterial defense genes in lung neutrophils.

TNF Signaling Drives Enhanced Neutrophil-Mediated Clearance of S. pneumoniae Following P. melaninogenica Priming.

In BAL fluid from mice exposed to HK P. melaninogenica, TNFα was the highest measured cytokine, produced as early as 2 h postexposure to P. melaninogenica (Fig. 4A). Neutrophils were the primary Tnf expressing cells, with expression also noted in monocyte and macrophage populations, and Tnf expression was elevated in P. melaninogenica-exposed mice (Fig. 4 B and C). TNFα is recognized by TNF receptor 1 (TNFR1) and TNFR2, which are encoded by the Tnfrsf1a and Tnfrsf1b genes, respectively. As expected, Tnfrsf1a was broadly expressed across different lung cell types, while Tnfrsf1b was primarily expressed by myeloid cells and T regulatory cells (30) (Fig. 4D). Both TNF receptors were highly expressed by neutrophils. Using CellChat analysis to infer cell–cell interactions, neutrophils were predicted to respond to TNFα produced by Cxcl3+ MDMs and from themselves in an autocrine fashion (Fig. 4E). By module scoring analysis, both neutrophil populations in P. melaninogenica-exposed mice exhibited significant upregulation of TNF signaling pathway associated genes (Fig. 4F). Linear correlation demonstrated a significant positive correlation between expression of phagocytosis-related and TNF signaling pathway genes in the Mmp9+ neutrophil population of P. melaninogenica-exposed mice (Fig. 4G).

To test whether TNFα signaling regulates S. pneumoniae phagocytosis, neutrophils were isolated from the lungs of wildtype and Tnf−/− mice 24 h after P. melaninogenica exposure and incubated with pre-opsonized, FITC-labeled HK S. pneumoniae using an ex vivo uptake assay. In the absence of TNFα, neutrophil phagocytosis of S. pneumoniae was significantly reduced (Fig. 4H). To determine which TNFα receptors were necessary for enhanced S. pneumoniae phagocytosis, uptake of FITC-labeled HK S. pneumoniae was evaluated in neutrophils purified from wildtype, Tnfrsf1a−/−, or Tnfrsf1b−/− mice following P. melaninogenica priming. While phagocytic capacity was retained in neutrophils lacking TNFR1, S. pneumoniae phagocytosis was significantly reduced in neutrophils lacking TNFR2 compared to neutrophils from WT mice (Fig. 4 I and J). Neutrophils lacking either TNF or TNFR2 also exhibited significantly reduced serine protease activity (Fig. 4 K and L). Neutrophil expression of reactive oxygen species, which also contributes to intracellular bacterial killing, was unaffected by the absence of TNFα (SI Appendix, Fig. S5D). Loss of TNFα or TNFR2 did not impact neutrophil frequencies in naïve or P. melaninogenica-exposed mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 E and F). These data identify TNFα signaling through TNFR2 as a previously unrecognized regulator of neutrophil serine protease activity and bacterial uptake in the lung during acute pneumococcal pneumonia.

Protective Immune Priming Reduces the Pneumococcal-Induced Type I IFN Response.

In mice infected with S. pneumoniae alone, we identified expression of several genes associated with canonical innate immune activation during pneumococcal infection (SI Appendix, Fig. S6A). Using pathway enrichment analysis as an unbiased approach to identify the top differentially enriched signaling pathways, type I and type II interferon associated pathways emerged as the dominant gene ontology pathways across myeloid cell populations in S. pneumoniae–infected mice (Fig. 5A). Interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) were also among the top upregulated genes in the C1qa+ MDM population which was selectively enriched in S. pneumoniae infected mice (Fig. 5B) and broadly among myeloid cells (Fig. 5C). Correspondingly, expression of type I interferon receptor genes Ifnar1 and Ifnar2, as well as type II interferon receptor gene Ifngr1, were higher in myeloid populations compared to other cell types (SI Appendix, Fig. S6B). Notably, ISGs were expressed at lower levels in mice exposed to P. melaninogenica prior to S. pneumoniae infection (Fig. 5C), in which enhanced S. pneumoniae clearance was observed. While similar interferon-associated genes were upregulated during S. pneumoniae infection, particularly among C1qa+ MDMs and neutrophils, module scoring analysis indicated a significant reduction in the expression of both type I and type II interferon signature genes in these cells, as well as inflammatory monocytes and dendritic cells, in mice primed with P. melaninogenica prior to S. pneumoniae infection (Fig. 5D).

Expression of Stat1, which controls expression of interferon-stimulated genes (31), was upregulated to a greater degree in all cell types in the lungs of S. pneumoniae–infected mice compared to P. melaninogenica-exposed mice (Fig. 5E). In contrast, suppressor of cytokine signaling 3 (Socs3), a negative regulator of the JAK/STAT signaling pathway (32), was increased to a greater degree in myeloid cells and T cells of P. melaninogenica-exposed mice compared to S. pneumoniae–infected mice (Fig. 5F). Across myeloid cell populations, MAPK and ERK signaling associated genes were upregulated in mice primed with P. melaninogenica prior to S. pneumoniae infection, compared to mice infected with S. pneumoniae alone (Fig. 5G). Similarly, network pathway analysis revealed ERK1 and ERK2 cascade signaling as a top upregulated pathway in neutrophils from mice primed with P. melaninogenica before S. pneumoniae infection, compared to several interferon signaling related pathways dominating neutrophils in unprimed mice infected with S. pneumoniae (Fig. 5H). Of note, MAPK and ERK1/2 signaling occur downstream of TLR2 (33), shown to be required for P. melaninogenica-mediated protection (20). Additionally, genes involved in chemotaxis and phagocytosis, processes known to be regulated by MAPK-ERK signaling (34–36), were enriched in P. melaninogenica-primed mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S5G). These data suggest that the improved immune-mediated clearance of S. pneumoniae in mice exposed to P. melaninogenica involves an overall transcriptional shift away from an interferon signaling-dominant profile toward a pro-phagocytic profile associated with MAPK/ERK signaling.

Transcriptional Changes in Monocytes and Macrophages Associated with Increased Phagocytic Capacity.

We next analyzed the transcriptional differences in macrophages from P. melaninogenica primed mice. In C1qa+ MDMs, lysosome and lytic vacuole organization genes were enriched in mice exposed to P. melaninogenica prior to S. pneumoniae infection, compared to mice infected with S. pneumoniae alone (Fig. 6A). Lysosome organization genes were broadly upregulated across all monocyte and macrophage populations in mice exposed to P. melaninogenica prior to S. pneumoniae infection (Fig. 6B). Similarly, unbiased pathway enrichment analysis identified several pathways associated with bacterial killing in AMs from mice exposed to P. melaninogenica before S. pneumoniae infection, including lysosomal acidification and reactive oxygen species metabolic process associated genes (Fig. 6C). Network pathway analysis confirmed enrichment in lysosome regulation and positive regulation of defense response genes in AMs for P. melaninogenica-primed, but not unprimed, mice infected with S. pneumoniae (Fig. 6D). Bacterial phagocytosis has been shown to increase expression of lysosomal genes in macrophages (37). To determine the functional correlates of the lysosome-associated transcriptional differences, we performed an in vivo phagocytosis assay to compare S. pneumoniae uptake in mice with or without P. melaninogenica exposure. Assessment of phagocytic capacity revealed a significant increase in phagocytosis of FITC-labeled HK S. pneumoniae for both recruited Ly6C+ monocytes and MDMs as well as resident AMs in mice primed with P. melaninogenica (Fig. 6E).

Monocyte and Macrophage Populations Contribute to P. melaninogenica–Induced Clearance of S. pneumoniae.

Given their increased bacterial uptake, we sought to understand whether recruited monocytes and macrophages directly contribute to enhanced clearance of S. pneumoniae in P. melaninogenica-primed mice. To address this question, mice were treated with an anti-CCR2 antibody, which selectively depleted CCR2+ myeloid cells, leading to a significant reduction in Ly6C+ recruited monocytes and macrophages without affecting resident AMs or recruited neutrophil populations (Fig. 6 F and G). In the absence of recruited monocytes and macrophages, P. melaninogenica exposure was no longer protective against S. pneumoniae infection (Fig. 6H). Notably, neutrophil activation, as measured by CD18 surface expression and intracellular TNFα, was not affected by anti-CCR2 treatment (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 A and B), suggesting that regulation of neutrophil-mediated bacterial killing was not the mechanism responsible for CCR2-dependent enhanced protection against S. pneumoniae infection in P. melaninogenica-primed mice. Together, these data reveal a previously unrecognized dependence on recruited monocytes and macrophages for commensal-enhanced protection against pneumococcal pneumonia.

Discussion

Using a preclinical model recapitulating associations between Prevotella aspiration and improved outcomes in critically ill patients, we demonstrate that P. melaninogenica dramatically alters the pulmonary transcriptional innate immune response to S. pneumoniae. The gene expression profile of P. melaninogenica-exposed mice in myeloid cells was enriched in antibacterial, pro-phagocytic pathways, in contrast to the antiviral, interferon-dominant expression profile observed in nonprimed mice with unresolved infection. Macrophages and monocytes of Prevotella-primed mice exhibited functional increases in phagocytic capacity and directly participated in S. pneumoniae clearance. Further, we identified an essential role for TNFR2 in neutrophil-mediated protection during pneumococcal pneumonia, mediated by increased neutrophil phagocytosis and serine protease activity. Together, these findings illustrate a paradigm for improved antibacterial defense through commensal-directed myeloid cell reprogramming.

In contrast to P. melaninogenica and other airway Prevotella species, we previously found that Prevotella intermedia was not protective and failed to induce a similar TLR2-dependent neutrophil TNFα response as the “protective” species (20). Consistent with this observation, others report species-specific TLR activation in epithelial cells, as Prevotella histicola activated TLR2 and TLR5, while Prevotella nigrescens selectively activated TLR2 (38, 39). Unlike P. melaninogenica, P. intermedia is frequently associated with periodontal abscesses, harbors several virulence factors, and upregulates host adherence-associated receptor Ptafr, correlating with increased S. pneumoniae burdens (40, 41). Here, we focused on defining the transcriptional reprogramming induced by exposure to P. melaninogenica to uncover the transcriptional changes associated with improved S. pneumoniae clearance, compared to less robust infection defense.

To our surprise, the dominant transcriptional signature enriched in myeloid cells during S. pneumoniae infection without Prevotella priming was an antiviral associated interferon response. S. pneumoniae triggers type I interferon signaling via the pore-forming toxin pneumolysin (42, 43). Interferon signaling can protect against S. pneumoniae infection by increasing alveolar epithelial cell survival and myeloid cell activation (44, 45) and Ifnar−/− mice exhibit increased S. pneumoniae burdens (42, 46), suggesting that the complete absence of type I interferon signaling is harmful. However, excessive type I interferon signaling is widely documented to increase susceptibility to pneumococcal pneumonia and worsen pneumonia outcomes, particularly in the context of viral coinfections (47–50), and type I interferon can negatively regulate phagocytosis (44, 48, 49). These findings suggest that the magnitude of the type I interferon response may determine whether it is beneficial or detrimental to S. pneumoniae clearance. In myeloid cells from P. melaninogenica primed mice, reduced overall ISG expression corresponded with elevated expression of MAPK and ERK signaling genes, aligning functionally with increased S. pneumoniae uptake by lung neutrophils and macrophages in P. melaninogenica-primed mice. TLR2 and MAPK signaling are associated with enhanced phagocytosis and pathogen killing by innate immune cells in other infection models (51, 52). Together, these data suggest that an interferon dominant response is maladaptive for efficient S. pneumoniae clearance, compared to the TLR2 and MAPK/ERK signaling program induced by Prevotella priming.

The recruitment of monocyte-derived macrophages to the lungs is a hallmark of severe pneumonia, with direct contributions to alveolar injury (53–55). We observed striking differences among the two transcriptionally distinct MDM populations identified by scRNA-seq between mice infected with S. pneumoniae compared to those primed with P. melaninogenica. The C1qa+ MDM population which dominated S. pneumoniae–infected mice expressed significantly higher ISGs and may therefore serve as a key source of the maladaptive interferon response observed in susceptible mice. In people with severe bacterial pneumonia, recent reports indicate a central role for circulating C1qa-expressing monocytes in driving deleterious inflammation (56, 57). In contrast, the Cxcl3+ MDM population selectively enriched by P. melaninogenica exposure transcriptionally resembled mature tissue resident-like macrophages and expressed chemokines that contribute to neutrophil recruitment, suggesting these two populations have distinct impacts on the immune landscape during infection. Other examples of enhanced defense against bacterial pneumonia following immune priming by microbial-derived factors, gut commensals, or other pneumococcal serotypes are all mechanistically linked to reprogramming in resident macrophages, namely AMs (58–62). Here, our findings provide insight regarding how distinct MDM subpopulations recruited during infection can alter the protective host response.

Neutrophils undergo several maturation states following tissue migration and during bacterial infection (63), with two dominant populations frequently reported (64, 65). Neutrophil cell numbers were lower in S. pneumoniae–infected mice after P. melaninogenica priming, consistent with lower pathogen burdens and a resolving inflammatory response. Of the two neutrophil populations we identified, Csf1+ neutrophils expressed higher levels of inflammatory genes, including those involved in TLR signaling, phagocytosis, and TNF signaling, in response to P. melaninogenica. The independent contributions of TNFR1 vs. TNFR2 signaling during infection are largely unclear. Here, we demonstrate that TNFR2 is required for enhanced phagocytosis and serine protease production in neutrophils after P. melaninogenica priming. A protective role for TNFR2 has previously been found during skin infection with S. aureus, where TNFR2 promoted activation of neutrophil ROS and vital NETosis (66). In contrast, we found that TNFR2 signaling increased neutrophil phagocytosis and serine protease activity without altered ROS, suggesting that the protective functions of TNFR2 signaling are context-dependent. While P. melaninogenica exposure can directly induce neutrophil production of TNF⍺ ex vivo, additional tissue-specific cells or signals are required for enhanced S. pneumoniae killing (20). Here, we cannot rule out the contribution of TNFR2 expression on other cells for enhanced neutrophil antimicrobial function. Together, these findings highlight TNF signaling through TNFR2 as a critical regulator of neutrophil serine protease activity, which is a key mechanism of S. pneumoniae killing (67, 68).

This study has several limitations to consider. Many immune defense mechanisms are regulated posttranscriptionally; therefore, gene expression data were complemented with protein analysis and functional assays whenever possible. While single dose pre-exposure models are commonly used to delineate immune priming mechanisms (19, 69–71), future work could expand these findings using multiple smaller doses over an extended period, mimicking continuous exposure to aspirated oral commensals. Another limitation is the use of HK bacteria, as recent work demonstrates that lower airway enrichment of oral commensals correlates with the detection of immune-modulating short-chain fatty acids (72), and that P. melaninogenica is metabolically active in the murine lung (72). These findings provide initial evidence that aspirated oral commensals are not merely transient passengers but active participants shaping the lung immune environment.

Clinically, escalating evidence suggests that airway anaerobes including Prevotella should be spared during treatment of critically ill patients, based on the strong associations between anaerobes and improved survival (9, 73–75). While arguments for this approach are often centered on the potential contributions of gut anaerobes, a recent study concluded that oral and lung anaerobes are the most powerful microbial indicator of improved survival in critically ill patients (76). In the same study, Prevotella inversely correlated with acute respiratory failure and markers of tissue injury including soluble TNFR1 (76). This observation is in alignment with the protective TNF signaling through TNFR2, rather than pro-apoptotic TNFR1, observed in P. melaninogenica exposed mice. Our data, together with the clinical correlation between P. melaninogenica abundance and reduced lower airway infections (8, 11, 15), point to an important role for P. melaninogenica aspiration in directing lung immune homeostasis and defense against bacterial pneumonia. Further work is needed to optimize Prevotella-mediated enhanced defense in a clinical setting, with the potential to reduce overall pneumonia mortality.

Materials and Methods

C57BL/6J (WT), B6.129S-Tnf tm1Gkl/J (Tnf−/−), C57BL/6-Tnfrsf1atm1lmx/J (Tnfrsf1a−/−), and B6.129S2-Tnfrsf1btm1Mwm/J (Tnfrsf1b−/−) mice were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory. For infections, mice were administered 107 CFU S. pneumoniae i.t. in 50 μL PBS. For P. melaninogenica exposures, mice were administered 50 μL i.t. 106-108 CFU equivalents of heat-killed (HK) bacteria in PBS 24 h prior to live S. pneumoniae infection. For CCR2+ monocyte depletion, mice were intraperitoneally injected with either 20 μg MC-21 antibody (77) or rat IgG2B isotype control (Bio X Cell) in 200 μL PBS 24 h prior and immediately before treating with HK P. melaninogenica i.t. Depletion of CD11b+CCR2+ myeloid cells was confirmed via flow cytometry. Detailed descriptions of remaining materials and methodology including bacterial strains and preparations, flow cytometry, cytokine and chemokine analysis, functional assays, single cell RNA sequencing, and data analysis, is provided in SI Appendix, SI Materials and Methods section.
