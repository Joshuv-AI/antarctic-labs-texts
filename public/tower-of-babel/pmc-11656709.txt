Byproduct-to-Host Ratios for Assessing the Accessibility of Mineral Resources
Authors: Titouan Greffe, Max Frenzel, Tim T. Werner, Gavin Mudd, Peng Wang, Manuele Margni, Cécile Bulle
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Mineral resources are essential for reaching net-zero ambitions by 2050. There is a rising diversity of metals in electricity generation and storage technologies, as well as for mobility technologies. However, little is known about the future supply of minor elements historically mined in low volumes such as indium, tellurium, germanium, or tantalum. Those minor elements are found in lower concentrations in the ores of major elements and therefore rarely form economic deposits on their own. Such elements are often produced as byproducts of a host (or “target commodity”, which underpins the bulk of a mine’s profitability) in ore, e.g., in porphyry ore, tellurium is a byproduct where copper is the host. As a result, the primary supply of those minor elements depends on the supply of the major elements. Such dependency has not been accounted for in scenarios of the mineral supply. To address this gap, we developed a methodology to harmonize scattered data of mineral resource estimates and to calculate the mass ratio between the byproduct and the host in ores and concentrates, called the byproduct-to-host (BtH) ratio. We collected crude ore tonnage and element grades, among other key data, from the state-of-the-art literature and publicly available mining company reports. Our data set covers 3422 deposits across 141 countries providing 22 275 BtH ratios. The future supply of minor elements can be derived by multiplying the primary production of host elements by the developed BtH ratios, noting the limitations of data representativity. The open-access nature of this work facilitates the enrichment and update of this data set in the coming years.

Introduction

Since the industrial revolution, there has been a rising demand and diversity of elements used in society,1,2 and this trend is expected to continue beyond 2050.3−5 More than half of the elements needed by modern society are mined as by-/coproducts of either other metals6 or fossil fuels.7 Constraints on current and future supplies of mineral resources are mostly estimated for major elements like copper, aluminum, lead, nickel, zinc, and some energy transition metals like lithium.4,5,8−11 Historically, these major elements have had larger markets. Elements are defined as “minor” when they have been historically mined in lower volumes (i.e., lower than dozens of kilotons per year). These minor elements are generally found in lower concentrations in the ores of major elements,6,12 from which sufficient quantities can be extracted to satisfy demand of major elements. Minor elements, therefore, rarely form economic deposits on their own. Despite numerous discussions on the importance of a secured future supply of minor elements, assessments of their supply has been limited.13−16 Mineral resource estimates for minor elements are hampered by a lack of robust data,15,16 leading to high uncertainties. An element in a deposit can be defined either as byproduct, coproduct, or main product (i.e., host).17 While there are no explicit definitions of those terms in the various mineral resource reporting codes,15 they are commonly understood as defined by Campbell.17 A byproduct has “no effect on the decision about the appropriate profit-maximizing level of production for the mining operation” while a coproduct has an effect on the decision for mine production.17 A main product is the only element influencing the production of the mine.17 In this article, we use “host” to designate a main product, as per Nassar et al.6 Other terms are used in the literature such as attractor element in refs (18) and (19) or target commodity. Some elements, like cobalt, can be produced either as a byproduct in nickel laterite ores or as a host, as in the hydrothermal and volcanogenic deposit of Bou Azzer district, Morocco.20 Therefore, the status of a minor element as either host, coproduct, or byproduct depends on the source (e.g., type of ore, slag) from which it is recovered.21,22

This status can change depending on the supply demand balance of both the host and the byproduct. It is common for many byproduct elements like cobalt to exhibit higher market price volatility than their hosts.15,23,24 Given that the markets for the minor metals market are often small, the influence of an individual producer can be large. Therefore, it can be risky for a mining company to increase the recovery of the byproduct since it can flood the market and drive prices down. Consequently, it is rare that mining companies enter such markets due to the high uncertainty of increasing their revenues.25 If a mining company enters the market of a byproduct and the contribution of the element to overall revenues is sufficiently large (i.e., at least 20%, as proposed by Nassar et al.6), then the market price of the byproducts can influence the production in the mine. As a result, the status of an element can then switch from byproduct to main product.17,26

There have been some preliminary analyses of the dependence of a byproduct on a host. Nassar et al.6 performed the first global analysis of economic companionality, which represents the share of global production of a byproduct link to a given host, including the economic companionality of 62 metals in 2008. Graedel et al.27 recently expanded the analysis to helium, fluorine, and silicon, which were not included in the original study. This economic companionality does not provide a ratio of minor element concentrations to each of its hosts in geological settings. Moreover, this companionality is calculated on the basis of revenues generated by the host and the cost of operations to extract it in each mine. It addresses the question “how dependent for profitable operation is [the byproduct] in [the] mine on the revenue of the other metals recovered from that operation”.6

McNulty and Jowitt16 used the Nassar et al.6 results and multiplied them with historical primary production (using USGS and BGS data), to obtain byproduct-to-host (BtH) production ratios, considering this companionality (i.e., how the byproduct is dependent on the revenues from the host) as a proxy of the share of the total production of a minor element linked to each companion major element. However, the adequacy of the use of the companionality of Nassar et al.6 to derive such BtH ratios is debatable, since it is the result of both the production ratios and price ratios in 2008. Taking the example of copper, the byproduct-to-copper ratio should ideally be calculated using only the extraction of ore from mines where copper is a host and from which the byproduct is recovered, possibly at another stage on the supply chain. It would also be preferable to estimate the BtH ratio based only on the physical quantity of byproduct recovered relative to the extraction of the host, instead of including economic relationships in the calculation of the ratio. Moreover, BtH ratios calculated per the method of McNulty and Jowitt are based on past production data and do not provide insights into the potential supply of byproducts in the future. Other studies providing BtH ratios have focused on one byproduct–host pair such as tellurium–copper28 or indium–zinc.29

Byproducts can be recovered at different stages of the supply chain (Figure 1), e.g., from ores, slimes, or slag. Tellurium is mainly recovered from copper anode slimes at the electrorefining stage,28,30 gallium is commonly recovered during hydrometallurgical processing of iron-rich precipitate originating from sulphidic zinc ores and from the Bayer liquor during processing of bauxite in smelters,31−34 germanium from coal fly ash and zinc-dominant ores,35 scandium from red mud,36 and indium from zinc-dominant ores.29 For an overview of a mineral supply chain, readers are referred to Frenzel et al.37

There is a need for exhaustive estimations of mineral resource availability in mines (presence of an element in an ore/brine) and their accessibility, i.e., the fraction of the element potentially recoverable.16,38−41 The mining industry has developed standardized methods to assess the extent of mineral resources. Two primary categories, which can be split into subcategories, can be used to classify a mineral deposit: mineral reserves and resources.13,42 Although the definitions of both mineral reserves and resources vary across extractive industries and countries,41 they are commonly understood as follows: mineral reserves are the portion of measured and indicated resources that are recoverable at a profit. Therefore, this considers the so-called modifying factors such as losses during extraction and legal, social, and environmental factors, among others.13 Resources have reasonable prospects for eventual economic extraction and are generally estimated by geologists.13 The difference between the “recoverable part at a profit” and the “reasonable prospects” is the result of the use of the modifying factors.13 Resource estimates are generally larger, since they do not account for technological losses, and may have a larger uncertainty than that of mineral reserves.42

The first barrier has been overlooked in many studies, including those defining criticality.44,45 Ignoring that this barrier can be easily overcome under favorable economic conditions (e.g., the increase of byproduct market price) has led to unjustified fear of supply disruption. The supply potential of currently classified “critical” elements such as gallium, germanium, and indium in numerous studies,46 especially in the US and European context,16 is at least twice the current rates of production, without any change in host demand.32,33,35 The first barrier can be overcome by increasing the global processing capacity of the smelters and by increasing recovery efficiency at each smelter and refining plant. Once the maximum global recovery efficiency is reached (i.e., all smelter and refinery plants reach their maximum recovery capacity), then the demand of the host should increase to drive an increased extraction of crude ore containing both host and byproducts. This latter barrier may not be overcome if the host and byproducts are not being jointly consumed (i.e., consumed in the same products), or if the market price of the byproducts does not increase substantially.

To the best of our knowledge, and of other experts,47 there is no open-access data set that discloses harmonized estimates of BtH ratios in worldwide deposits. In this study, we aim to produce such a data set, in compliance with the FAIR (findable, accessible, interoperable, and reusable) principles.48,49 Jasansky et al.50 recently published an open database on metal mining. They also disclosed data on ore reserves in 668 deposits for 25 metals. Our work complements theirs by developing a comprehensive data set of 3422 deposits with mineral resource estimates, covering 63 elements (including metals, fossils, phosphate, and helium).

In the following section, we describe the methodology to consistently develop BtH ratios and the literature review strategy to collect data for a comprehensive set of elements. For each byproduct–host pair, we provide the distribution of ratios across all deposits, where the content of both elements could be estimated.

Methods

To derive BtH ratios, we need to know the overall content of the byproduct in resources and where it is recovered on the supply chain. At each stage of the supply chain, there is a fraction of the “available” quantity of the byproduct (i.e., total content in the ore/brine/fossil source) that is “potentially accessible”. The “potentially accessible” BtH ratio informs on this relative quantity of byproduct if all supply chains reach the highest possible recovery rate (i.e., the fraction of inflow recovered at the output of a process). The “accessible” BtH ratio provides this relative quantity given the current state of the global supply chain, i.e., what is currently technically and economically extractable. The output flows of the host and byproduct from the supply chain are ready to be used as industrial commodities. The global demand of a host element like copper is met by this output flow from the supply chain (h5 in Figure 1). In this study, we provide “available”, “potentially accessible”, and “accessible” BtH ratios. The “available” BtH ratio is the mass ratio of the host to the byproduct in the ore or in the brine before extraction (Θ1 in Figure 1). The “potentially accessible” BtH is the mass flow ratio of the host and the byproduct net of losses, using estimates of highest possible recovery rate at each stage of the supply chain. The “accessible” BtH is the mass ratio of the host and the byproduct net of losses using estimates of the current recovery rates (θ5 in Figure 1). BtH ratios are calculated for as many metal pairs across as many deposits and supply chains as possible for which data are available. The three methodological steps for data collection, data processing, and calculation of BtH ratios are described below.

Data Collection of Deposits (Type, Tonnage, Grade) and Recovery Rates along the Supply Chain

Data collection was performed via online search of keywords combining “global assessment of reserves”, “global production”, “recovery of”, “meta-analysis”, “review”, and “main deposits of” with the name of each element listed in SI 1. The snowball method was employed, involving expanding data sets from key references and consulting their bibliographies to find other relevant references.51 When data for a given deposit for which we knew at least its name and its location were missing, such as its type, its ore tonnage, or its elements grade, we searched for publicly available annual, technical, or sustainability reports of mining companies operating the deposit. We manually extracted the relevant information in a spreadsheet file when identifying a data point (e.g., missing quantity of an element grade or new deposit) not already reported in our data set.

Abiotic resources are extracted from different sources, which are generally crude ore or brine or in a few cases from oil and coal fly ash. We collected information about the concentrations of the element in those sources. When several data sets were available for a given deposit about the mineral resource estimate and the concentration of each element, we prioritized the most recent data and the most comprehensive one among all sources of data identified.

Recovery rates for each element extracted from a given deposit and from transformation processes along the respective supply chains were retrieved as per the following. When available, we used element-and-deposit-specific milling recovery rate, e.g., coming from the S&P database52 for 70 deposits, such as the milling recovery rate of copper (80.7%) and gold (84.5%) in the Boddington mine in Australia.52 If site-specific data were not available, we used deposit-specific recovery rate average values, e.g., 80% of all elements are recovered from deposit A, from literature reviews.53−55 As an example, the recovery rate of lithium from brines (including mining and refining losses) is 57% while the recovery rate of lithium from spodumene ore is 59.5%. The recovery rate at other stages of the supply chain were also retrieved from the literature (see SI 1 and 4). Ores of individual mines are rarely processed in isolation and are blended at metallurgical plants.56 Facing large data gaps, we had to assume that smelting recovery rates of an element (e.g., copper) found in different ores in different deposits are the same, regardless of its deposit of origin. This assumption is reasonable since concentrates often have to fulfill specific quality criteria (minimum grade and below-threshold levels of contaminants like arsenic). The data decision tree for recovery rate values is presented in Figure 6.2 in SI 6.

Data Processing

Our data processing methodology consists of (i) data harmonization, (ii) unit conversion, (iii) filling data gaps, and (iv) host element identification. The first three steps are similar to the methodology employed in ref (50) to process their ore reserves data.

Harmonization

Information on the name of the deposit was entered as reported in the most data-exhaustive article, providing both tonnage and element grades. In some cases, there were several different words referring to the same item. We then harmonized the information from the literature by using predefined categories to entries of the name of an element, units and countries, via a concordance table (see SI 2). Data on ore tonnage, grades, the status of the deposit, recovery rates, and milling capacity for a deposit can come from different data sources. These sources may not name the deposit the same way; therefore, we had to map deposit names (see Figure 6.1 in SI 6).

Conversion of Units

The various units used in different data sets were converted with concordance tables (SI 2). The content of each element was generally obtained by multiplying the grade (in grams/ton of ore, ppm, or %) by the ore tonnage, expressed in metric tons. A stoichiometric conversion was realized if the content of an element in the reference was provided for its oxide. As an example, Kalashnikov et al.57 reported the content of La2O3 in kg, so we converted the content of La2O3 into the content of lanthanum (La) by multiplying by the stoichiometric conversion factor, which is 0.852. Such a conversion is also applied to the host if its content is reported in the oxide state. The ore tonnage was converted to metric tons (Mt) if not already reported like this.

Filling Data Gaps

The quantity of elements not reported in the reference source was added by using the grade of the element multiplied by the tonnage of the deposit. As an example, Sb, Bi, Co, Cd, Se, and Te contents in the Lac des Iles massive sulfide deposit are not reported in Mudd and Jowitt.58 We used grade estimates of those missing elements from ref (12) then multiplied by the tonnage reported in Mudd and Jowitt58 to include their quantity for this deposit in the data set.

Identification of Potential Host in Deposits

For deposits where the host is not identified as such by the company in the data sources, we determined the host as the element having the highest contribution to revenues. Revenues are obtained by multiplying the total quantity of each element by its 10 year average market price from 2010 to 2018 (SI 2). If the contribution of the element to revenues is above 20%, it is considered as a coproduct, in the vein of Nassar et al.6 In this study, an element is classified as a host when it is defined as the largest contributor to total revenues in each deposit.

Results

The newly harmonized data set contains 3422 deposits, covering 141 countries, of which 2733 report the quantity of more than one element allowing the determination of at least one BtH ratio in each deposit (except for five deposits, for which the host could not be determined). The representativity of mineral resource estimates in our data set is presented in the discussions (Data Representativity section). Overall, 7425 “available”, “potentially accessible”, and “accessible” BtH ratios are generated in this data set which are made available in SI 1 and further analyzed below.

Our 5th–95th percentile ranges should be close to a BtH ratio that would consider both actual known and unknown deposits, unless unknown large deposits with a BtH ratio out of this range are discovered. Among those 12 pairs, three (Ag–Cu, Mo–Cu, and Re–Cu) have many outliers. These outliers are for small deposits with BtH ratios out of the range of larger deposits. Regarding Cu–Ni, the distribution is centered on the values from Buckton South (Canada) and Talvivaara (Finland) deposits due to their large host tonnage (353 and 4071 kt, respectively) compared to other deposits in the data set.

The number of hosts is larger than what is originally reported by Nassar et al.,6 although they focused on metals and therefore did not include phosphate rock or fossil fuel deposits. Here, we consider that when we have a mineral resource estimate of an element in a deposit, then it contributes to the revenues of the mine. This is an assumption since this estimate may not come from a mining company report but from additional literature. In reality, the element may generate revenues further down the supply chain and may not be considered as a host by the mining company when extracted. The calculated global extractable BtH ratios range from less than one nanogram (10–9) to more than one ton (103) per kg of host element. Pairs having an extractable BtH ratio higher than one is due to a market price difference between the host and the byproduct that outweighs the mass difference, which applies to, e.g., the copper–gold pair. Discussion

This research work generated a data set that provides harmonized values of byproduct quantities linked to the production of a host by deposit and by supply chain stages. It helps to shed light on current levels of companionality of metal extraction and transformation. It can be used to estimate prospective primary production flows of byproducts at both global and regional scales by multiplying the primary production flows by our accessible BtH ratios. This data set of BtH ratios is built with mineral resource estimates representative of the current economic situation and is assumed to be the current best estimate. It is, however, possible that some of the current byproducts become coproducts or hosts in the long term if the market price of the host decreases and the market price of the byproduct increases. However, given the current large gap in the contribution to revenues between the host and the byproduct, it is unlikely that such changes occur in the short- to medium-term. Still, there are inherent uncertainties when those BtH ratios are used in studies analyzing the long-term supply of metals.

Data Representativity

We evaluate the representativity of our data set considering alternative estimates of global mineral resources of each element, as there is no other comprehensive open-access data set at the deposit level. The comparison of mineral resources at the country level with other data on deposits is out of the scope of this article. It is not feasible to do such a comparison for half of the elements, as only production at the country level is reported, which in most cases does not correspond to sites of extraction. In our database, we compiled deposit-level mineral resource estimates for 63 elements. We first compared the sum of tonnage of each element with the main reference estimates from the literature,42,59,60 and we classified elements into four categories: (1) the nine elements for which our database is more exhaustive than the “best” reference estimates; (2) the 22 elements for which our database is of the same order of magnitude as the main reference estimates; (3) the eight elements for which our database covers less than 20% of the global production reported in the reference estimates; and (4) the 21 elements for which there are no global mineral resource estimates and hence are more exhaustively documented in our database (Figure 5a).

Qref,b and Qref,h are estimated as the “best” resource estimate up to date of the byproduct and the host, respectively. Most of these data are from refs (42), (59), and (60) (see SI 3 for details). A representativity score close to zero means that the total tonnage of either the byproduct or the host (or both) is low compared to the “best” resource estimate we identified. Representativity results of BtH ratios are presented in Figure 5b and are available in more detail in SI 7. The representativity of more than half of the pairs could not be assessed (“NA” in Figure 5b) due to missing alternative mineral resource estimates for at least one element of the pair (meaning our database is more exhaustive than the other estimates for those pairs). Fifty-eight pairs have a representativity score above 100%, such as Cu–Ni, Ag–Cu, or Ag–Mo, meaning that both the host and byproduct tonnage reported in our data set are above the world resource estimate in the “best” alternative reference data set.

BtH ratios of pairs where both byproduct and host belong to category 1 are based on the most exhaustive and representative available data. Since BtH ratios are based on mineral resource estimates, rather than reserves, we suggest that BtH ratios of those pairs should be close to a “future reassessed” BtH ratio in the short- to medium-term if no supergiant deposits with BtH ratios deviating from our estimation are discovered in the next decade. BtH ratios of pairs combining an element from category 2 with an element of category 1, 2, or 4 can be considered as based on strongly representative data (even if less exhaustive) if we assume that our sample of deposits representing 20% of global mineral resources is sufficient to represent the distribution of ores containing the element. BtH ratios of pairs composed of one element of category 4 and one element of category 1 or 4 can be considered as based on the best available data (as noncompatibility data is available elsewhere for category 4 metals), which does not mean it is exhaustive and representative of world deposits (which remain poorly documented). Any pair implying an element of category 3 has a poor representativity and is based on nonexhaustive data. Additional data collection of mineral resource estimates of this element is required to make the BtH ratio, implying this element is more robust.

We also evaluated the representativity of recovery rate data, which is used for the calculation of the “accessible” BtH ratio. Across all deposits, 95% of such data used is not specific to the concentration of the element in the ore, to the deposit, or to the type of deposit (see SI 1 for details) due to data gaps and missing reliable extrapolation methods. It is valuable to document the potential maximum recovery rate per deposit, which could be addressed in future research.

Limitations and Uncertainties

Quantities of some elements in mineral deposits have remained unreported by mining companies. We encourage future research to fill in those data gaps, either by collecting geochemical data from scientific studies, such as Frenzel et al.,32,33,35 or onsite de novo data following statistical analysis guidelines by Frenzel.61

There are unavoidable biases included in our data set of BtH ratios. The first bias is that byproducts have a higher probability of being reported in deposits where their grade is high (and therefore potentially contributing to revenues), and the second is that we only have access to the mean value of the grade but not its distribution in each deposit (which is thought to be log-normal in most cases).62 The “accessible” BtH may evolve as it is influenced by the recovery rate of the two elements on their supply chain, which can change with market price variation and evolution of technologies, while the “available” BtH ratios can change only with more complete data (new deposits added to the data set or new mineral resource estimates in deposit already included). Therefore, the “available” BtH can be interpreted as the maximum “ideal” ratio between the byproduct and the host if no losses occur on the supply chain, the “accessible” BtH is the business-as-usual estimate while keeping current practices, and the potentially accessible BtH is what would optimistically be possible with the best currently available technologies. Finally, we rely on publicly shared mineral resource estimates in commercial reports, where detailed information on the methodology used to compile data leading to mineral resource estimates is often lacking, as highlighted by Mudd et al.,15 and the associated uncertainty of these estimates is rarely, if ever, quantified.63,64

As such, these reporting issues lead to data gaps in our study. We identified thousands of potential deposits, mainly when comparing our list of deposit names with those listed in the Owen et al.65 and Macklin et al.66 data sets, where data collection and harmonization of ore tonnage and element grade would be needed to include them in our data set (SI 1, “Data Gaps” sheet). We searched for data on the 15 largest (ranked by ore tonnage) remaining deposits for each commodity, but we did not find information about element grades and therefore decided to stop our investigation and data collection. Despite the advances made on resource estimates for 3422 deposits compared to the literature,50 significant efforts are still needed to improve data coverage for missing deposits among the estimated 14 thousand active and 150 thousands abandoned mines estimated globally66 (Macklin et al.66 report 22 609 active and 159 735 abandoned mines in the main text, but this includes some double counting, e.g., the Insizwa mine in South Africa is reported four times). Our compiled data set provides BtH ratios using actual mineral resource estimates. Those estimates may change with exploration or new assessments of deposits by mining companies. Moreover, the global weighted mean BtH ratios may change if BtH ratios of giant or supergiant deposits (i.e., deposits with metal tonnage 10 to 100 times larger than large deposits67) not yet included in this data set largely deviate from BtH ratios of included deposits. To make this data set more complete and mature, it is crucial to (i) report the presence of elements in ores for mining companies, even if they do not contribute to their revenues, and (ii) centralize data of mineral resource estimates from all deposits to facilitate harmonization and enhance the robustness and completeness of the data set. This data set is obviously open to future updates and to be completed by experts of the field.

To reach that goal, we think it is key to adopt an international resource identifier (iri) to label a deposit. An iri is a permanent web link that refers to metadata such as the name of the deposit, its geographic coordinates, or the name of the owner at a given year. This would help to avoid the reference to the same deposit with multiple different labels. Considering the identified substantial data gaps, we join the call for the launch of a centralized interoperable public data repository that would facilitate the data collection and harmonization of mineral resource estimates and other key data such as location of mines or annual production.68−70

Applications of the Data Set

Our data set provides harmonized mineral resource data for over 3000 deposits, targeting recovery rates and byproduct-to-host ratios. These could be of particular interest for researchers in the fields of industrial ecology, economic geology, circular economy, criticality, and life cycle assessment, as well as to mining industry and mineral processing practitioners. Our data set facilitates the evaluation of the interdependency of primary production flows of metals. It also facilitates the quantification of losses along the supply chain with the collected recovery rate data. For instance, most material flow analysis studies derive stock and flows of resources without integrating any supply constraints,71,72 therefore assuming supply always matches the demand of resources. The BtH ratios developed enable considerations of dependency on (production) flows and not only on stocks. We recommend using our “accessible” BtH ratio to determine future production flows of byproducts, noting the constraints of data representativity.
