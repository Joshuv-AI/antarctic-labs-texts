Diagonal Scaling: A Multi-Dimensional Resource Model and Optimization Framework for Distributed Databases
Shahir Abdullah, Syed Rohit Zaman

Abstract
Modern cloud databases present scaling as a binary decision: scale-out by adding nodes or scale-up by increasing per-node resources. This one-dimensional view is limiting because database performance, cost, and coordination overhead emerge from the joint interaction of horizontal elasticity and per-node CPU, memory, network bandwidth, and storage IOPS. As a result, systems often overreact to load spikes, underreact to memory pressure, or oscillate between suboptimal states. We introduce the Scaling Plane , a two-dimensional model in which each distributed database configuration is represented as a point ( H , V ) (H,V) , with H H denoting node count and V V a vector of resources. Over this plane, we define smooth approximations of latency, throughput, coordination overhead, and monetary cost—providing a unified view of performance trade-offs. We show analytically and empirically that optimal scaling trajectories frequently lie along diagonal paths: sequences of joint horizontal–vertical adjustments that simultaneously exploit cluster parallelism and per-node improvements. To compute such actions, we propose DiagonalScale , a discrete local-search algorithm that evaluates horizontal, vertical, and diagonal moves in the Scaling Plane and selects the configuration minimizing a multi-objective function subject to SLA constraints. Using synthetic surfaces, microbenchmarks, and experiments on distributed SQL and KV systems, we demonstrate that diagonal scaling reduces p95 latency by up to 40%, lowers cost-per-query by up to 37%, and reduces rebalancing by 2–5 × \times compared to horizontal-only and vertical-only autoscaling. Our results highlight the need for multi-dimensional scaling models and provide a foundation for next-generation autoscaling in cloud database systems.



I
Introduction

Distributed databases power mission-critical services such as financial applications, e-commerce platforms, logistics systems, and global-scale social networks. Their performance and availability depend on the ability to scale elastically in response to changing workloads. However, while elasticity is widely advertised as a core feature of cloud-native data systems, the mechanisms and assumptions behind autoscaling remain simplistic and are often suboptimal in practice.

I-A

Limitations of the Binary Scaling Paradigm

Modern databases expose two principal scaling dimensions:

1.

Horizontal scaling
(scale-out): add or remove nodes.

2.

Vertical scaling
(scale-up): increase or decrease per-node CPU, RAM, storage IOPS, or network bandwidth.

Nearly all real-world systems—including Spanner
[
6
]
, DynamoDB
[
7
]
, Bigtable
[
3
]
, Cassandra
[
11
]
, CockroachDB
[
4
]
, and YugabyteDB
[
12
]
—treat these two scaling axes as
independent
, leaving operators and cloud controllers to choose between scale-out or scale-up based on limited heuristics.

This dichotomy is problematic. Scale-out increases cluster parallelism but also increases consensus overhead, tail latency, network saturation, compaction backlog, and the likelihood of disruptive data rebalances. Scale-up improves per-node throughput and reduces local contention, but offers no relief for hotspots spread across partitions and eventually hits hardware ceilings.

I-B

Workload Diversity Demands Multi-Dimensional Reasoning

Applications exhibit widely varying characteristics:

•

read-heavy workloads with skewed access distributions,

•

write-intensive workloads sensitive to commit latency,

•

mixed workloads with large working sets,

•

workloads with diurnal patterns or unpredictable surges.

A scaling policy that works for CPU-saturated workloads can fail disastrously for memory-bound workloads. A policy that works for strong-consistency write-heavy loads (e.g., Spanner) may be inefficient for eventually consistent KV systems (e.g., Dynamo).

We show that many workloads benefit from
coordinated
, not independent, adjustments in horizontal and vertical resources.

I-C

The Scaling Plane: A Unified View of Elasticity

This work introduces the
Scaling Plane
, a model where each configuration of a distributed database is represented as a point
(H,V)
, where
H

•

We show that these surfaces are neither monotonic nor separable in
H

I-D

Diagonal Scaling: A Missing Abstraction

We reveal that optimal scaling paths frequently follow
diagonal
directions—scaling both
H
and
V

•

pure scale-out (horizontal-only),

•

pure scale-up (vertical-only).

Surprisingly, even small vertical adjustments can dramatically reduce the need for horizontal expansion, reducing the magnitude of rebalances and improving stability.

III
The Scaling Plane Model

We now formalize the
Scaling Plane
, a two-dimensional analytical model for reasoning about elasticity in distributed databases. The goal is to build a compact mathematical representation that captures the dominant behavioral trends of modern systems such as Spanner, DynamoDB, Bigtable, Cassandra, CockroachDB, and YugabyteDB. While real systems contain many second-order effects, the Scaling Plane provides a sufficiently accurate baseline for optimization and autoscaling.

III-A

Resource Space Definition

A distributed database configuration is represented as a point
(H,V)
, where:

•

H\in\mathbb{N}
is the number of nodes.

•

V=(c,r,b,s)
is the vector of per-node CPU, RAM, network bandwidth, and storage IOPS.

We define the resource space:

\mathcal{S}=\{\,(H,V):H\geq 1,\;c,r,b,s>0\,\}.

This models the elasticity region exposed by cloud providers:
H
is discrete (integer node counts), while
V
is chosen from a small finite set of instance types.

III-B

Node-Intrinsic Latency

Node latency arises from processing requests using CPU, memory, network stack, and storage engine. We approximate it with a weighted harmonic form:

L_{\text{node}}(V)=\frac{\alpha}{c}+\frac{\beta}{r}+\frac{\gamma}{b}+\frac{\delta}{s},

where
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta
are sensitivity constants.

This form captures:

•

the strong impact of CPU on compute-bound operations,

•

the influence of RAM on working-set and caching behavior,

•

the role of network bandwidth in replication and RPC,

•

the effect of storage IOPS on LSM-tree compaction and log flushes.

This is consistent with observations for Spanner
[
6
]
, Bigtable
[
3
]
, and CockroachDB
[
4
]
.

III-C

Coordination Latency

Coordination cost increases with cluster size due to consensus protocols, global timestamps, metadata synchronization, and topology changes. We model coordination latency as:

L_{\text{coord}}(H)=\eta\log H+\mu H^{\theta},

where
0<\theta<1
creates a super-logarithmic but sub-linear growth curve, matching empirical measurements in distributed consensus literature
[
2
]
.

III-D

Total Latency

We define:

L(H,V)=L_{\text{node}}(V)+L_{\text{coord}}(H).

This implies:

\frac{\partial L}{\partial H}>0\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\partial L}{\partial\|V\|}<0.

III-E

Throughput Surface

Per-node throughput is:

T_{\text{node}}(V)=\kappa\cdot\min(c,r,b,s).

Cluster throughput accounts for diminishing returns:

T(H,V)=H\cdot T_{\text{node}}(V)\cdot\phi(H),

where:

\phi(H)=\frac{1}{1+\omega\log H},

reflects increased coordination overhead and replication costs.

III-F

Coordination Overhead Surface

Write-heavy workloads impose additional overhead. Let
\lambda_{w}
be the write arrival rate.

K(H,V)=\rho\cdot L_{\text{coord}}(H)\cdot\frac{\lambda_{w}}{T(H,V)}.

This captures the intuition that coordination overhead:

•

increases with write load,

•

decreases when throughput increases,

•

rises with larger cluster size.

III-G

Monetary Cost Surface

If
C_{\text{node}}(V)
is the cloud cost for an instance with resources
V
, total cost is:

C(H,V)=H\cdot C_{\text{node}}(V).

III-H

Multi-Objective Optimization

We define the objective:

F(H,V)=\alpha L(H,V)+\beta C(H,V)+\gamma K(H,V),

subject to:

\displaystyle L(H,V)

\displaystyle\leq L_{\max},

\displaystyle T(H,V)

\displaystyle\geq T_{\min}.

This creates a two-dimensional non-convex optimization problem.

III-I

Surface Geometry

The surfaces
L(H,V)
,
T(H,V)
,
C(H,V)
, and
K(H,V)
collectively create a geometric structure resembling valleys and ridges:

•

L
decreases along
V
but increases along
H
,

•

T
increases but saturates with
H
and
V
,

•

C
increases linearly with
H
and superlinearly with
V
,

•

K
grows with
H
and shrinks with
V
.

Key Insight:

The minimum of
F
is rarely on the axis-aligned edges (pure scale-up or pure scale-out). Instead, minima lie inside the plane, along
diagonal trajectories
.

Fig. 1:
The Scaling Plane, showing horizontal, vertical, and diagonal scaling paths. Diagonal paths traverse interior regions where many surfaces reach their minima.

IV
Diagonal Scaling Theory

Diagonal scaling refers to coordinated changes in
H
and
V
. We now provide a deeper theoretical justification.

IV-A

Scaling Trajectories

Consider a trajectory:

\tau(t)=(H(t),V(t)),

where both
H
and
V
increase with
t
. Let
m=\frac{dH}{d\|V\|}
be the slope.

Goal:
Find
m^{\ast}
minimizing:

F(H(t),V(t)).

IV-B

Gradient Alignment Condition

The gradient of
F
is:

\nabla F=\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial H},\frac{\partial F}{\partial\|V\|}\right).

A local optimum along a trajectory direction
(1,m)
is reached when:

\nabla F(H^{\ast},V^{\ast})\cdot(1,m^{\ast})=0.

Thus the optimal diagonal direction
(1,m^{\ast})
aligns with
-\nabla F
.

IV-C

Lemma: Axis-Aligned Scaling is Rarely Optimal

Lemma 1.

If
\frac{\partial F}{\partial H}\neq 0
and
\frac{\partial F}{\partial\|V\|}\neq 0
, then the optimal direction is neither horizontal nor vertical.

Proof Sketch.

If optimal scaling were horizontal, direction vector would be
(1,0)
. But:

\nabla F\cdot(1,0)=\frac{\partial F}{\partial H}\neq 0,

contradiction.

Similarly for vertical scaling.
Thus diagonal scaling is required.

\square

IV-D

Proposition: Existence of Interior Minima

Proposition.

If
L
decreases in
V
, increases in
H
,
C
increases in both, and
K
increases in
H
but decreases in
V
, then
F
has at least one interior stationary point
(H^{\ast},V^{\ast})
.

Sketch.

Intermediate value theorem + monotonicity properties guarantee a point where partial derivatives vanish simultaneously.

—

Explore neighbors around

Examine horizontal, vertical, and diagonal moves.

4.

Penalize disruptive moves proportional to expected rebalancing.

5.

Accept moves only if

We adopt a multi-layered methodology that combines synthetic analytical surfaces, microbenchmarks on distributed SQL and KV systems, and dynamic experiments simulating real-world traffic patterns.

Our methodology seeks to answer four core questions:

Does diagonal scaling outperform horizontal- and vertical-only autoscaling across a diverse set of workloads?

3.

Does
DiagonalScale

IX
Conclusion

We introduced diagonal scaling as a foundational abstraction for elastic distributed databases. By modeling cluster configurations as points in a two-dimensional Scaling Plane and deriving analytic latency, throughput, cost, and coordination surfaces, we demonstrated the inherent multi-dimensionality of scaling decisions.

Our
DiagonalScale
algorithm performs discrete local search in this space, evaluating horizontal, vertical, and diagonal neighbors. We prove monotone convergence, provide stability guarantees, and demonstrate effectiveness across synthetic and empirical settings.

Diagonal scaling delivers lower latency, reduced cost, and dramatically improved stability compared to traditional horizontal- and vertical-only autoscaling. This work establishes a path toward more intelligent, multi-dimensional autoscaling controllers for cloud-native data systems.
