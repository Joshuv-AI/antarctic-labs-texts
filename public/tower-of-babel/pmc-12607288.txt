A video-based educational intervention to improve vaccine acceptance for Schistosomiasis japonicum: A pre–post study using the 5C model in rural China
Authors: Qianxu Yang, Jue Wang, Chunhong Du, Hai Yen Lee, Li Ping Wong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study evaluated a video intervention to enhance knowledge of Schistosoma japonicum (SK) and general vaccines (GK) and examined how these factors influenced acceptance of a hypothetical Schistosoma japonicum vaccine via the 5C pathways (confidence, complacency, constraints, calculation, and collective responsibility). A single-arm pre – post study was conducted in Eryuan, China (2024). Of 453 baseline participants, 421 completed the one-month post-intervention follow-up. Mean SK knowledge rose from 21.27 ± 6.20 to 29.12 ± 5.01 and GK from 3.17 ± 1.98 to 3.73 ± 1.88 (p < .001). Vaccine acceptance increased from 63.9% to 84.1% (p < .001). Complacency and constraints declined by 2.52 ± 4.27 and 1.79 ± 4.01, while confidence and calculation increased by 0.97 ± 2.96 and 1.42 ± 3.64 (p < .01). Path analysis showed SK and GK were associated with higher confidence (SK standardized beta [stβ] = 0.216; GK = 0.293), greater calculation (SK = 0.204; GK = 0.119), stronger collective responsibility (SK = 0.126; GK = 0.337), and lower constraints (SK = −0.316; GK = −0.140); only SK reduced complacency (stβ = −0.424) (p < .05). Acceptance related positively to confidence (stβ = 0.100), calculation (stβ = 0.099), and collective responsibility (stβ = 0.183), but negatively to complacency (stβ = −0.303) and constraints (stβ = −0.110). Total effects on acceptance were stβ = 0.381 for SK (59.8% indirect via 5C) and stβ = 0.265 for GK (39.2% indirect). The intervention effectively improved knowledge, psychological readiness, and acceptance of a potential Schistosoma japonicum vaccine.

Introduction

Schistosomiasis is a neglected tropical disease that remains a serious global health threat, with nearly 800 million people at risk for infection in over 78 different countries. Although its intensity varies geographically, the disease continues to impose a heavy burden on low- and middle-income countries, particularly in rural areas of Africa, Asia, and South America, where limited access to clean water and sanitation sustains transmission. Beyond morbidity, the disease entrenches cycles of poverty, inequity, and productivity loss, underscoring its significance as a global health and development challenge.1 Insights from endemic settings, including China, are broadly applicable to other regions where schistosomiasis and related neglected tropical diseases persist, reinforcing the need for sustained, multidisciplinary strategies that couple biomedical advances with community-based prevention.

Human infection occurs when cercariae, the motile larval stage released from infected Oncomelania snails, actively penetrate the skin upon contact with contaminated freshwater. This water-based transmission pathway, sustained by agricultural irrigation, fishing, and domestic water use, confines schistosomiasis to specific endemic regions but enables persistent reinfection where snail habitats and human – water interaction overlap.2 Schistosomiasis japonicum (SJ) is the predominant form of schistosomiasis in China.3 Despite decades of integrated control efforts, including mass drug administration (MDA), snail control, and health education, SJ remains endemic in many areas of China.4 Although 355 out of 452 counties in China where SJ is endemic (78.5%) had met their elimination targets by the end of 2023, a total of 170 counties still reported seropositive SJ cases. Moreover, out of 47 million people screened, 850,000 were seropositive for SJ. At the same time, snail habitats, which are critical for the transmission of SJ, showed a slight rebound with 4,080 newly identified habitats.5 Despite progress in control, the persistence of transmission in endemic settings highlights the need for sustained, multidisciplinary approaches for long-term schistosomiasis control in endemic regions.

While praziquantel (PZQ) remains the primary treatment for schistosomiasis, it does not prevent reinfection, and is most effective against mature adult worms, leaving infections from juvenile stages of the parasite largely unaddressed.6 In addition, because of growing concerns over the emergence of PZQ drug resistance, there is an urgent need for the development of complementary treatments and prevention strategies for schistosomiasis, including vaccination.7,8 However, vaccine development against SJ remains challenging due to limited antigen candidates, unclear regulatory frameworks, and the low level of industry involvement.9–11 To date, no licensed vaccine exists for SJ or any other schistosome species.

Given the scientific, economic, and regulatory challenges that have slowed schistosomiasis vaccine development, and the continued risk of reinfection in endemic regions, it is essential to strengthen both population education and vaccine readiness. Population education plays a pivotal role in schistosomiasis prevention by fostering hygienic and behavioral practices that disrupt parasite transmission, reduce environmental contamination, and lower reinfection risk. Strengthening public knowledge and awareness in endemic communities not only curbs ongoing contagion but also builds the foundation for successful future vaccine introduction.12 Evidence from the COVID-19 experience has shown that early public perception research can guide communication strategies, strengthen trust, and accelerate policy and funding support for vaccine development.13 Building on these lessons, early assessment of community attitudes helps identify knowledge gaps, trust barriers, and behavioral drivers that may influence vaccine uptake, thereby informing communication, policy, and research priorities even when vaccine rollout remains uncertain.14 Assessing vaccine acceptance in advance is a proactive way to align community readiness with scientific progress. Such understanding is particularly important in endemic areas, where access, cultural beliefs, and social norms strongly shape health behaviors.15 Therefore, evaluating public acceptance in advance of vaccine development is vital, as such insights provide the foundation for the effective introduction and sustainable uptake of future schistosomiasis vaccines in China and other endemic regions.

While traditional models, such as the Health Belief Model (HBM) and the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB),incorporate some psychosocial and structural aspects,16,17 they often fail to explicitly capture collective protection motives: an important determinant in the context of neglected tropical diseases (NTDs).18 The 5C psychological antecedents builds on earlier frameworks provided by the 3C model, by integrating five key driving factors that govern vaccination behavior: Confidence, Complacency, Constraints, Calculation, and Collective Responsibility.19 The model offers a comprehensive approach to understanding cognitive, structural, and social influences that determine vaccine attitude.

The objective of this study is to utilize video-based educational intervention methods to improve SJ knowledge (SK) and general vaccine knowledge (GK). This study assessed the influence of increased knowledge on vaccine acceptance (under the assumption of regulatory approval), both directly and indirectly via the five components of the 5C model. We hypothesized that enhanced knowledge would promote acceptance by increasing confidence, calculation, and collective responsibility, while reducing complacency and constraints.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

The present study is a single-arm, pre – post interventional study conducted in Eryuan county, Dali district, China, from April to December 2024. The study was conducted over three phases: a baseline survey, video-based educational intervention, and a post-intervention survey.

Sample size calculation

Sample size was calculated using the paired t-test formula (α = 0.05, power = 80%, effect size = 0.5). Based on similar studies,20 a standard deviation of 2.06 and a Cohen’s d value of 0.419 were used, resulting in an estimated sample size of 377.5. Considering a 20% attrition rate, the final sample size was set at 453.

Participants

Criteria for participation in the present study were as follows: all study participants were residents aged 18–60, who were fluent in Mandarin, with local registration and no serious health issues, and who had heard of schistosomiasis and provided informed consent. Individuals who were migrants, had communication difficulties, or refused to sign the informed consent form were excluded from the study. Only those who completed the full intervention protocol were included in the final analysis.

Sampling and recruitment

A multistage sampling method was applied. Eryuan county was randomly selected among 11 endemic counties in Dali district, followed by three randomly selected communities. Using stratified proportional sampling, 500 households were chosen, and one adult per household was randomly selected and screened for schistosomiasis awareness (Table S1). Individuals who had not heard of schistosomiasis were replaced using the same sampling frame until the target sample size was obtained. Of these, 441 received intervention and 430 completed the post-intervention survey. Recruitment was supported by local community committees, and WeChat was used for all communication, reminders, and survey distribution.

Intervention development

A 9-minute 52-second video-based intervention was designed to improve SK and GK. It was developed in three stages: 1) script creation; 2) video production using Powtoon with AI voice-over and Chinese subtitles; and 3) expert review. Six independent reviewers evaluated the content of the video-based intervention using a modified Educational Video Assessment Scale (Table S2, S3),21,22 yielding a final S-CVI/Ave of 1.0. A pilot test of the video using rural residents confirmed clarity and engagement. Video link: https://b23.tv/hUw2NoI.

Questionnaire and measures

The questionnaire used in the present study was structured and close-ended and developed based on a comprehensive review of the literature and consultation with subject matter experts. Its design and theoretical basis were informed by widely used behavioral frameworks in vaccine acceptance research, including the Health Belief Model (HBM), the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), and the 5C psychological antecedents.13,23 These studies illustrate how knowledge and beliefs shape cognitive and attitudinal determinants of vaccination behavior. To better align measurement with vaccination decisions in an endemic context, we adopted the 5C framework as the primary basis for the study.

The final instrument comprised four main sections with 64 items (Table S4). The first section focuses on standard socio-demographic variables such as age, gender, education level, and income. The following two sections assessed SK (35 items) and GK (5 items), with all responses coded dichotomously (correct = 1, incorrect or “don’t know” = 0). The fourth section measured psychological antecedents using the 5C model – confidence, complacency, constraints, calculation, and collective responsibility – via 15 items (three items per domain) on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree.” Finally, the last section assessed the primary outcome, vaccine acceptance, using a single item rated on a 5-point scale ranging from “extremely unwilling” to “extremely willing.” The question was phrased as: “If a vaccine against Schistosoma japonicum becomes available and is approved for use, how willing would you be to receive it?” The questionnaire was originally developed in English, translated into Chinese, and administered via the online platform Questionnaire Star (问卷星).

Reliability and validity

A pilot test (n = 65) was conducted to evaluate the questionnaire’s reliability, validity, and structure. Internal consistency was excellent (Cronbach’s α = 0.964), and sampling adequacy was confirmed by Kaiser – Meyer – Olkin (KMO) values exceeding 0.70. Construct validity was further supported by exploratory factor analysis (EFA) using principal component extraction with Varimax rotation, which revealed a clear factor structure of SK, GK and 5C constructs, with strong item loadings and substantial explained variance (Tables S5–S13).

Univariate tests (Kolmogorov – Smirnov and Shapiro – Wilk) showed deviations from normality (p < .001) (Table S14), whereas the multivariate Shapiro – Wilk test indicated normality (W = 0.908, p = .992) (Table S15). Given the large sample size and acceptable multivariate normality, Maximum Likelihood (ML) estimation was used for subsequent CFA (Confirmatory Factor Analysis) and SEM (Structural Equation Modeling).24 The CFA was conducted using AMOS 26.0 to validate the measurement structure of SK, GK, and the 5C psychological constructs (Table S16–S23). All scales demonstrated an acceptable measurement model fit (CMIN/DF < 3; GFI, AGFI, NFI, IFI, TLI, and CFI > 0.90; RMSEA < 0.08; RMR < 0.05). All standardized loadings were significant (p < .001), with CR > 0.70 and AVE > 0.50, confirming good internal consistency and convergent validity. The square roots of AVE exceeded inter-construct correlations, supporting discriminant validity. Overall, the questionnaire exhibited excellent reliability and validity.

Implementation and data collection

The baseline survey was distributed via WeChat on September 8, 2024. Out of 500 total responses that were received, 464 were valid, and a total of 441 participants moved forward to receive the intervention. The video-based intervention was shared on October 23, 2024, and answers to a content-check question were used to verify that study participants viewed the entire video. A post-survey, which was identical to the baseline survey, was distributed on November 23, 2024. In total, 421 valid paired responses were included in our final analysis.

Survey data was collected via Questionnaire Star with built-in logic checks. Data was viewed from the researcher’s Bilibili account to confirm participant engagement. Only study participants who watched the full video and answered the verification question correctly were considered to have fully completed the intervention phase of the study.

Statistical analysis

Pre-post differences in knowledge were analyzed using the McNemar test, while changes in 5C psychological antecedents were evaluated using the McNemar – Bowker test. Associations with socio-demographic variables were examined using the Mann – Whitney U test for two-group comparisons and the Kruskal – Wallis test for multiple groups. SEM with maximum likelihood estimation was used to assess both direct and indirect effects of SK and GK on vaccine acceptance through the 5C model. Model fit was evaluated using conventional indices, including CMIN/DF, GFI, AGFI, NFI, IFI, TLI, CFI, and RMSEA. Mediation effects were evaluated using bias-corrected bootstrap confidence intervals. All analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 and AMOS version 24.0

Changes in Schistosoma japonicum knowledge pre-post intervention

Participants’ SK scores increased significantly from a baseline mean of 21.27 ± 6.2 to 29.12 ± 5.01 after the video-based intervention (Z = 14.601, p < .001) (Table S24). All items showed significant improvement (p < .001) (Table 2). Knowledge of endemic areas showed the largest gain in the epidemiology section, with correct responses rising from 60.1% to 81.9% (p < .01). Among transmission-related items, understanding of disease transmission through untreated water displayed the greatest improvement, with a 25.4% increase in correct responses (p < .01). Similarly, a 24.7% increase in correct responses was observed for recognition of barefoot exposure in paddy fields as a factor in schistosomiasis disease transmission. In terms of symptoms, knowledge of fever as a key indicator of schistosomiasis displayed the largest increase following the intervention and rose by 26.6% (p < .01). With respect to consequences, awareness of the impact of schistosomiasis on children showed the greatest improvement, rising by 28.5% (p < .01) following the intervention, while knowledge concerning the association between liver cirrhosis severity and schistosomiasis showed the smallest increase, at 20.7% (p < .01). In terms of treatment, awareness of anti-fibrotic therapy for the treatment of schistosomiasis increased the most, by 24.2% (p < .01). For schistosomiasis prevention, knowledge about management of domestic animals improved the most, by 27% (p < .01), whereas awareness of SJ vaccination showed the smallest change (17.8%, p < .01). Post-intervention, SK differed significantly by age, sex, and marital status. Participants aged 31–45 (29.95 ± 4.40) and 46–60 (29.52 ± 5.05) did not differ, but both were higher than those aged 18–30 (27.40 ± 5.48) (both P < .05). Women (29.76 ± 4.44) scored higher than men (28.53 ± 5.42) (p < .05), and unmarried participants (30.43 ± 5.16) scored higher than married participants (28.81 ± 4.93) (p < .05). Structural equation model

Structural equation modeling revealed a good model fit (CMIN/DF = 2.111; RMSEA = 0.051; all other indices >0.90) (Table S27). As shown in Figure 2 (Table S28), both SK and GK had significant direct effects on vaccine acceptance (SK: Std β = 0.153, SE = 0.009, p < .001; GK: Std β = 0.161, SE = 0.023, p < .001). SK was associated with lower complacency (Std β = −0.424, SE = 0.024, p < .001), fewer constraints (Std β = −0.316, SE = 0.025, p < .001), greater confidence (Std β = 0.216, SE = 0.017, p < .001), increased calculation (Std β = 0.204, SE = 0.024, p < .001), and higher collective responsibility (Std β = 0.126, SE = 0.017, p = .007). GK showed similar patterns, positively influencing collective responsibility (Std β = 0.337, SE = 0.046, p < .001), confidence (Std β = 0.293, SE = 0.046, p < .001), and calculation (Std β = 0.119, SE = 0.063, p = .015), while reducing constraints (Std β = −0.140, SE = 0.066, p = .003). However, GK had no significant effect on complacency (Std β = 0.047, SE = 0.065, p = .305). Among the five components of the 5C model, collective responsibility emerged as the strongest positive predictor of vaccine acceptance (Std β = 0.183, SE = 0.022, p < .001), followed by confidence (Std β = 0.100, SE = 0.022, p = .014) and calculation (Std β = 0.099, SE = 0.016, p = .010). In contrast, complacency (Std β = −0.303, SE = 0.016, p < .001) and constraints (Std β = −0.110, SE = 0.015, p = .006) were significant negative predictors of vaccine acceptance. Mediation analysis revealed that SK had a strong total effect on vaccine acceptance (Std β = 0.381, p < .001), with nearly 60% of the effect mediated through the 5C psychological factors (Table S29). The dominant indirect pathway was through complacency (Std β = 0.128, p < .001), followed by constraints (p < .05). In contrast, GK had a smaller total effect on vaccine acceptance (Std β = 0.265, p < .001), which was primarily driven by a significant direct path (Std β = 0.161, p < .001) (Table S30). Approximately 39.2% of the effect was mediated through the 5C variables, mainly via collective responsibility (Std β = 0.062, p < .01) and confidence (Std β = 0.029, p < .05). However, the indirect path through complacency was not statistically significant (p > .05).

Discussion

The present study demonstrates that a locally adapted video intervention can be effective in enhancing both SK and GK, improving psychological readiness based on the 5C model, and increasing vaccine acceptance in a rural Chinese context. Specifically, SK increased by 22.9%, in agreement with findings reported from Nigeria.25 The observed improvements suggest that while basic awareness of schistosomiasis existed, many study participants lacked specific knowledge about schistosomiasis transmission, early symptoms of schistosomiasis disease, and schistosomiasis treatment options – common gaps in knowledge associated with NTDs.26 In particular, increased recognition of the role of barefoot exposure in paddy fields, fever as a symptom of schistosomiasis, and the impact of schistosomiasis on children reflects the effectiveness of context-based messaging. The rise in anti-fibrotic treatment knowledge indicates a growing interest not only in prevention of schistosomiasis but also in schistosomiasis disease management, underscoring the value of including both aspects in future education programs. These findings highlight the importance of tailoring health education to the local situation, in order to promote informed decision-making.27,28

GK also improved, though to a lesser extent, possibly due to the relatively abstract and technical nature of vaccine trials and regulatory approval processes.29 For example, knowledge regarding vaccine effectiveness improved by only 5%; and approximately one-third of participants continued to express doubts, an issue consistent with previous findings in China.30,31 This reflects persistent effects of vaccine misinformation32 and underscores the need for more targeted scientific communication to strengthen public trust. Together, these results suggest that while foundational disease knowledge can be effectively improved through locally relevant materials, building broader vaccine literacy and trust remains a more complex, ongoing challenge.

The intervention utilized in the present study significantly improved four out of five psychological antecedents of the 5C model, namely: complacency, confidence, constraints, and calculation. Currently, there is limited research directly linking knowledge interventions to the 5C model. However, some of its components conceptually align with constructs from established theories such as the HBM and the TPB, offering a theoretical basis to interpret the observed changes. Among these, complacency showed the greatest reduction, likely reflecting a clearer understanding of the risks and consequences associated with schistosomiasis, consistent with increased perceived susceptibility and severity in the HBM.33,34 Constraints also declined, suggesting reduced perceived barriers, possibly due to the video’s practical guidance on local vaccination services – aligning with the “barriers” construct in HBM.35,36

Confidence improved substantially, likely due to content on vaccine safety and prior control successes, corresponding to perceived benefits in HBM and positive attitudes in the TPB.37,38 Increased calculation indicates a greater tendency to deliberate vaccination decisions, reflecting the HBM’s risk-benefit analysis and the TPB’s deliberative reasoning component.39,40 Although the total score for collective responsibility did not show a statistically significant difference, item-level gains suggest greater awareness of the social value of vaccination, in line with subjective norms in TPB and cues to action in HBM41 The lack of significance in the total score may be due to heterogeneity among study participants.42

Following the intervention, willingness to receive an SJ vaccine increased from 63.9% to 84.1%, demonstrating a substantial gain. This aligns with prior research on COVID-19, where educational efforts significantly boosted vaccine uptake.43,44 Although slightly lower than China’s reported the 91.3% vaccine acceptance rate for COVID-19 vaccines, the acceptance rate observed in the present study exceeds levels observed in many Western countries.45 The difference may reflect perceived disease urgency – COVID-19 invoked immediate global concern and strong public mobilization, whereas schistosomiasis is often seen as a contained or declining threat which is consistent with its characterization as a NTD. Still, the marked improvement underscores the power of tailored health communication to raise vaccine acceptance, even for diseases with low current visibility. This suggests the importance of continued public education in sustaining preparedness and supporting future vaccine rollout in post-elimination settings. Socio-demographic factors, particularly age and annual income, were significantly associated with vaccine acceptance, consistent with previous studies. Younger adults showed lower vaccine acceptance, likely due to limited risk perception and reduced exposure to schistosomiasis.46 The long-standing control of schistosomiasis disease in China may further diminish urgency among this group. Higher-income individuals generally reported greater vaccine acceptance, potentially due to better healthcare access, education and awareness.47

Among all sociodemographic factors, age and income were the two variables most consistently associated with both knowledge and vaccine acceptance. Both Schistosoma japonicum knowledge (SK) and general vaccine knowledge (GK) increased with age, consistent with findings from endemic regions where older adults’ prior exposure to control programs and stronger health awareness facilitate better knowledge retention.48 However, vaccine acceptance peaked among middle-aged adults rather than the oldest group. Similar patterns have been reported for COVID-19 vaccines, where middle-aged individuals demonstrated higher acceptance due to greater family responsibilities, stronger perceived benefits, and active social participation.46 These findings suggest that middle-aged adults may represent a key demographic for promoting vaccine uptake in endemic areas, given their dual role as decision-makers and caregivers within households. By contrast, younger participants exhibited lower knowledge and acceptance, aligning with prior research showing that limited disease experience and lower perceived susceptibility reduce engagement in preventive behaviors.26 Income was positively associated with both knowledge and vaccine acceptance, with middle- and high-income participants showing higher levels than those in lower-income groups. This finding is consistent with previous studies reporting that individuals with greater socioeconomic resources have better access to health information, higher health awareness, and fewer financial barriers to vaccination.47 Future programs should be specifically tailored to young and low-income populations by adopting more accessible, context-specific, and participatory education approaches. Programs that emphasize practical relevance and integrate health education into routine community activities may enhance engagement and promote equitable acceptance of future Schistosoma japonicum vaccines.

SEM findings further showed that SK affected vaccine acceptance both directly and indirectly via the 5C psychological constructs. It enhanced confidence, calculation, and collective responsibility, while reducing complacency and perceived constraints. Among these, complacency showed the strongest mediating effect, highlighting the role of perceived susceptibility and severity in low-urgency disease contexts. This contrasts with COVID-19 settings, where confidence and social norms were more influential due to widespread attention and perceived immediacy.13 The mediating effect of confidence supports evidence that SK improves trust and perceived benefits, in line with constructs from the HBM.49 The positive effect of calculation in this study differs from findings in the MENA region, where over-deliberation reduced vaccine acceptance.50 In our setting, stable infrastructure and trust in the health system may have further supported this constructive form of evaluation. Although collective responsibility had a weaker influence, it remains relevant, especially given the intervention’s emphasis on herd protection, aligning with TPB norms and HBM cues.41,51

GK also positively influenced acceptance, mainly via a direct path. This may reflect reliance on prior vaccination experience and familiarity with national programs in China. This is consistent with prior studies on COVID-19 and HPV vaccines, which identified insufficient vaccine knowledge as a key driver of vaccine hesitancy.52–54 The dominance of the direct pathway suggests that study participants may have relied on prior vaccination experiences and trust in national immunization programs to make quick decisions, without engaging in extensive attitude reconstruction. This is in line with the concept of cognitive shortcuts shaped by familiarity.55,56 The relatively short follow-up period (one month) may have limited the development of attitude-mediated pathways. Although its indirect effect was weaker, prior studies suggest that general vaccine knowledge can still enhance psychological readiness, particularly confidence and calculation, supporting the validity of the theoretical pathway.57

In summary, both types of knowledge and the 5C model played complementary roles in shaping vaccine acceptance rates, highlighting the need to address both educational awareness and psychological factors in vaccine promotion. Beyond its theoretical contributions, this study provides practical insights for public health programs in schistosomiasis-endemic areas. Strengthening population education remains a cornerstone of disease prevention, as improved awareness of transmission routes and protective behaviors can help sustain control achievements and prevent reinfection. At the same time, enhancing both disease-specific and general vaccine knowledge is crucial for fostering trust, reducing complacency, and improving readiness for future schistosomiasis vaccination. Integrating such education within existing health communication systems, particularly through culturally adapted and video-based interventions, may provide a scalable and effective approach to promote behavioral change and strengthen vaccine preparedness in similar rural and culturally diverse settings.

Limitation

This study has several limitations. First, it employed a single-arm pre – post design without a control group, which limits the ability to draw causal inferences, as other external influences, such as exposure to information from peers, social media, or health providers, could have contributed to the observed changes. In future studies, this issue could be better controlled by including comparison groups and monitoring participants’ exposure to external health information during the intervention period, thereby allowing the specific effects of the intervention to be more accurately isolated.

In addition, the follow-up period was relatively short, which may not fully capture the sustainability of the intervention’s effects over time. The study also relied solely on self-reported responses, which may introduce bias due to inaccuracies in recall or the tendency to provide socially desirable answers. This is particularly relevant in the context of vaccine acceptance, where participants’ stated willingness to receive a vaccine may not necessarily translate into actual vaccination behavior when the product becomes available. Future research should consider incorporating objective behavioral measures or follow-up assessments to evaluate actual vaccine uptake when a Schistosoma japonicum vaccine becomes available, in order to better understand and bridge the gap between stated intentions and real-world behavior.

Another limitation concerns the nature of the intervention itself. The educational video used in this study was brief, lasting under 10 minutes, and while it provided broad coverage of schistosomiasis and vaccine-related concepts, its limited depth may have constrained its ability to influence more complex psychological constructs such as collective responsibility. The format resembled a one-time informational prompt rather than a sustained engagement or behavior change intervention. Future studies should consider employing more comprehensive, interactive, such as multi-session programs, community dialogs, or narrative storytelling, to better address higher-order motivational and social factors related to vaccine acceptance. Final limitation is that the study is based on participants’ perceptions of a vaccine that is not yet available, which may introduce hypothetical bias. Nonetheless, this study remains relevant and timely. It is important to note that while the Schistosoma japonicum (SJ) vaccine is not yet licensed, it is not purely hypothetical. According to a recent comprehensive review,58 multiple vaccine candidates targeting different Schistosoma species are currently in various phases of clinical and preclinical development. Notably, candidates such as Sm-TSP-2/Alhydrogel (GSK) and SchistoShield (Sm-p80) have completed Phase I trials in humans for S. mansoni and are being considered for broader applications, including in zoonotic contexts relevant to S. japonicum. The review emphasizes that these cross-species platforms may offer protective value across endemic regions and justify early public health and social-behavioral preparedness. Therefore, it is timely that this study explores vaccine acceptance in anticipation of future deployment, as such early behavioral insights are essential to inform effective communication strategies and ensure equitable uptake in high-risk communities.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the effectiveness of a culturally tailored, video-based educational intervention in improving schistosomiasis-related and general vaccine knowledge, psychological readiness, and willingness to accept a hypothetical Schistosoma japonicum vaccine in a rural Chinese population. By applying the 5C psychological framework, the study identified distinct pathways through which knowledge influences vaccine acceptance, highlighting the importance of addressing both informational and motivational components of decision-making. The intervention yielded substantial gains, particularly by reducing complacency and perceived barriers, while enhancing confidence and deliberative thinking. These findings offer practical insights for designing scalable and cost-effective communication strategies in resource-limited, NTD-endemic settings. Notably, the conceptual model developed in this study may inform future vaccine preparedness efforts by bridging behavioral science with early-stage vaccine policy planning. Future initiatives should aim to sustain these gains through longitudinal engagement, deeper community involvement, and readiness assessments as vaccine development progresses.
