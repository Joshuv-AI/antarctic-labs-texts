Trends, Risk Factors, and Future Burden of Female Breast Cancer in China and Other G20 Countries, 1990–2050: A Comparative Global Burden of Disease 2023 Analysis
Authors: Xubin Dong, Xiyu Kang, Zeyu Wu, Guangyu Li, Jiaxian Yue, Jiaxiang Liu, Qingyao Shang, Tianxiao Wang, Dongmin Yang, Jiayi Ma, Huiming Wu, Chenxuan Yang, Xin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study analyzes the burden of female breast cancer in China and other G20 countries from 1990 to 2023, characterizes its trends and risk factors, and projects the burden to 2050.MethodsUsing Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2023 estimates, we evaluated age-standardized rates of incidence, mortality, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), the mortality-to-incidence ratio (MIR), and trends (estimated annual percentage change, EAPC); applied Das Gupta decomposition, sociodemographic index (SDI) gradient, attributable-risk, and socioeconomic-inequality analyses, with stratification by premenopausal (20–54 years) and postmenopausal (≥55 years) age groups; and used autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) models to forecast to 2050.ResultsIn 2023, China’s age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) was 31.6 per 100,000, the second lowest among G20 members (range: India 29.4 to France 114.6), yet China recorded the largest absolute burden (342,725 cases and 76,589 deaths). Between 1990 and 2023, China’s age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR) declined far more rapidly than in the rest of the G20 (EAPC −1.92 vs −0.23), whereas its ASIR rose only modestly (EAPC 0.40 vs 1.05); China’s MIR fell to the eighth lowest by 2023. Across members, SDI correlated positively with ASIR (r = 0.74) but inversely with MIR (r = −0.84). Decomposition attributed China’s rising deaths chiefly to population aging, partly offset by declining age-specific rates. Rates in the postmenopausal age group exceeded those in the premenopausal age group in all 19 members. By 2050, China’s ASIR is projected to remain broadly stable, while absolute cases rise by approximately 131%.ConclusionsAcross the G20, female breast-cancer burden is undergoing an epidemiological transition: absolute burden is rising as populations grow and age, while age-standardized mortality is declining in most members, with marked heterogeneity across the G20. The burden is shifting toward older women in the postmenopausal age group, while the mortality-to-incidence ratio remains higher in lower-SDI members. These patterns argue for G20-coordinated, equity-focused prevention and survival care.

Introduction

Female breast cancer is the most frequently diagnosed cancer in women worldwide, accounting for an estimated 2.3 million new cases and 670,000 deaths in 2022.1 Its burden is rising but unevenly distributed across countries at different stages of socioeconomic development, with incidence and mortality following divergent trajectories that a single global figure conceals. More broadly, the global cancer landscape is undergoing substantial shifts in incidence, mortality, and geographic distribution.2 The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2023 breast-cancer analysis provides 34 years of country-level estimates spanning 1990–2023, together with forecasts to 2050.3 These estimates open a post-2023 window for tracking national progress toward Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) target 3.4, which calls for a one-third reduction in premature mortality from non-communicable diseases by 2030.4

China stands out within the G20. In 2022, female breast cancer was the second most commonly diagnosed cancer among Chinese women, after lung cancer,5 and in 2023 it accounted for approximately 343,000 incident cases, the largest absolute number among G20 economies. Over recent decades, breast-cancer incidence in China has risen even as mortality has improved, amid national policy emphasis on prevention, early detection, and treatment.6 This period also coincided with a substantial evolution of cancer treatment, from cytotoxic chemotherapy toward targeted and immune-based therapy.7 How these trends compare with those of other major economies, and what drives them, remain incompletely characterized. Modifiable risk factors, including adiposity, alcohol use, smoking, and dietary exposures, shape this burden, yet their relative magnitude and direction remain uncertain across the G20. Adiposity, in particular, has a menopause-dependent relationship with breast cancer: higher body-mass index (BMI) is inversely associated with premenopausal disease yet positively associated with postmenopausal disease.8,9 Reconciling this menopause-dependent association with population-level attributable-burden estimates that treat high BMI as a harmful exposure motivates burden analyses stratified by menopausal status.

The Group of Twenty (G20) provides a policy-relevant comparator for such an assessment: its health ministers’ meetings have made the G20 a platform for coordinated action on global health priorities, including noncommunicable diseases and universal health coverage.10 The G20 comprises 19 sovereign member states (Argentina, Australia, Brazil, Canada, China, France, Germany, India, Indonesia, Italy, Japan, Mexico, the Republic of Korea, the Russian Federation, Saudi Arabia, South Africa, Türkiye, the United Kingdom and the United States of America) together with two regional members, the European Union and, since 2023, the African Union. Benchmarking China against the other G20 economies situates its breast-cancer transition within the economic and health-system context most relevant to coordinated prevention policy.

Existing evidence, however, addresses these dimensions only in part. The official GBD 2023 breast-cancer study described global, regional, and national burden, attributable risks, and forecasts across 204 countries but, by design, did not foreground any single country.3 Recent country-led analyses have each advanced part of this question, though none has addressed all of it: a comparative GBD 2023 description of China alongside India and the United States reported China’s distinctive rising-incidence, falling-mortality pattern but without a full G20 frame or menopausal analyses.11 A China–G20 comparison projected trends to 2040 on the earlier GBD 2021 release, descriptively.12 Consequently, for a China-centered G20 framework built on GBD 2023, no study has yet jointly delivered country-level sociodemographic-index (SDI) gradients in incidence and relative mortality burden, burden stratified by menopausal status using dedicated age-standardized data, and population forecasts to 2050. Our analysis extends this evidence in three respects. First, it applies a G20-specific comparative frame, with China as the index country, to country-level GBD 2023 estimates that the official breast-cancer analysis reports without foregrounding any single country. Second, it moves the China–G20 comparison from the GBD 2021 to the GBD 2023 release and extends the projection horizon from 2040 to 2050. Third, it adds a demographic–epidemiological decomposition of the 1990–2023 change, a stratification by menopausal status, and a cross-country assessment of socioeconomic inequality. Although the G20 is not a homogeneous epidemiological region, its members represent major economies that differ substantially in population structure, exposure profiles, health-system capacity, and data quality.

Building on GBD 2023, we therefore positioned China’s female breast-cancer burden within the G20 across 1990–2050. We benchmarked the 2023 burden against the other 18 G20 member states, the G20 aggregate, and the global level, and characterized its multi-measure profile. We then quantified structural trends and compared China with the rest of the G20, decomposed the 1990–2023 change in burden into demographic and epidemiological contributions, characterized country-level SDI change and 2023 SDI gradients in incidence and relative mortality burden, and profiled the modifiable risk-factor portfolio relative to G20 peers. Finally, we projected the burden to 2050 and stratified it by menopausal status using dedicated age-standardized data.

Materials and Methods

Data Sources

We extracted GBD 2023 female breast cancer estimates for 1990–2023 from the Global Health Data Exchange GBD Results Tool.3 All 19 sovereign G20 member states were included, together with the Global and G20-aggregate levels; the two regional members, the European Union and the African Union, were excluded because GBD 2023 reports estimates by country and territory and provides no separate estimates for them. The G20 estimates are supranational aggregates provided by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME) and were not obtained by summing or averaging member-state estimates; because they are reported as an aggregate, they do not represent the burden or temporal patterns of individual G20 members.

Six measures (Incidence, Deaths, Prevalence, disability-adjusted life years [DALYs], years of life lost [YLLs], and years lived with disability [YLDs]) were obtained as both counts and age-standardized rates (ASR) per 100,000 women across 22 age groups, the age-standardized rates being those published by IHME for GBD 2023, obtained by direct standardization of age-specific rates to the GBD standard population and expressed per 100,000 women, each with its 95% uncertainty interval (UI); this standard population applies to all age-standardized rates reported in this study. Attributable burden for the seven GBD-defined risk factors (high body-mass index, diet high in red meat, alcohol use, secondhand smoke, low physical activity, smoking, and high fasting plasma glucose) was extracted on the same grid of location, measure, age group, and year, following the GBD comparative risk assessment framework.13 SDI values were taken from the IHME GBD 2023 SDI series (1950–2023) for descriptive country-level SDI context and contemporaneous 2023 SDI-gradient analyses. Burden by menopausal status was obtained from the dedicated IHME GBD 2023 age-standardized menopausal-status release, using the 20–54-year band as a premenopausal proxy and the ≥55-year band as a postmenopausal proxy, for Incidence, Deaths, and DALYs in 1990 and 2023.

Temporal-Trend Analysis

ASR trajectories were summarized with three complementary approaches. The estimated annual percentage change (EAPC) was derived from an ordinary-least-squares regression of log-transformed ASR on calendar year, with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) from the slope standard error. Segmented (broken-line) regression (R package segmented, K = 0 to 4 selected by the Bayesian information criterion [BIC]) yielded segment-specific and weighted average annual percentage change (AAPC) for all 19 member states.14 For graphical display of trajectory shape, four members were selected to span the observed range of trajectories: China as the index country, Saudi Arabia (the largest increase in the age-standardized incidence rate across the G20), the United States (among the two steepest declines in that rate), and Japan (a high-income member with rising rates). Age-period-cohort (APC) net drift was estimated by fitting an APC model with reference period 2010.15 To compare country-specific trends, each G20 country’s EAPC for the age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) and the age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR) was compared with the mean EAPC of the remaining 18 G20 member countries using z tests based on the country CI-derived standard error and the between-country standard error of the rest-of-G20 mean. P values were adjusted within each measure using the Benjamini–Hochberg false-discovery-rate (FDR) procedure and displayed as q values. A trend in an age-standardized rate was classified as increasing or decreasing when the EAPC point estimate and both bounds of its 95% CI lay entirely above or entirely below zero, respectively, and as stable otherwise.

Decomposition of Burden Change

The 1990–2023 change in Incidence, Deaths, and DALYs was partitioned by the Das Gupta decomposition method into contributions of population growth, population aging, and epidemiological rate change (population size treated as an additional factor within Das Gupta’s general cross-classification framework; canonical cross-classification weights, additive by construction, with a residual below 0.001% of the total change attributable to floating-point rounding), reported as both absolute contributions and percentages of the total change.16,17

Sociodemographic-Context Analysis

We plotted country-level SDI in 1990 and 2023 for each G20 member. For 2023, we assessed cross-sectional Pearson correlations between country-level SDI and two age-standardized breast-cancer indicators: ASIR and the mortality-to-incidence ratio (MIR; ASMR/ASIR),18 the latter used as a complementary population-level indicator. MIR was interpreted as an ecological measure of mortality relative to incidence rather than as an individual-level case-fatality or survival measure. Because MIR is influenced by cancer registration completeness, diagnostic practices, mortality certification, stage distribution, and treatment availability, it was not treated as a direct measure of survival, treatment effectiveness, or health-system performance. These analyses remained ecological and were not interpreted as causal effects of SDI. The slope index of inequality (SII) and the concentration index (CI) were used to measure absolute and relative health disparities across the 19 member states.19 Member states were ranked by their SDI in the corresponding year and assigned the midpoint fractional rank, each member state weighted equally. The SII is defined as the absolute difference in the regression-line values between SDI rank 1 and SDI rank 0 and was estimated by regressing the age-standardized rate on the fractional rank. The CI measures relative inequality by fitting a Lorenz concentration curve of the cumulative distribution of burden against the cumulative distribution of population ranked by SDI, and was estimated with the convenient-regression estimator, in which twice the variance of the fractional rank multiplied by the rate divided by its mean is regressed on the fractional rank.20 Positive values indicate that the burden is concentrated in higher-SDI member states. Both indices were computed for the ASIR and the ASMR in 1990 and 2023, with 95% confidence intervals from the corresponding regressions; the 1990-to-2023 change in the CI is reported as the difference between the two point estimates, with a 95% confidence interval taken from the percentile distribution of 2000 bootstrap replicates in which the 19 member states were resampled with replacement.

Risk-Factor Analysis

For each G20 country, 2023 attributable deaths were tabulated across the seven risk factors. Consistent with GBD analyses of breast-cancer burden attributable to dietary and metabolic risks,21 factor-specific estimates are reported separately for each risk factor and were not normalized into shares of a common total. Because GBD attributable deaths incorporate mediation (for example, high BMI acting partly through high fasting plasma glucose) and overlapping exposures, raw factor-specific values must not be summed as mutually exclusive components. Per-risk-factor EAPC and the China-versus-G20-median percentage change in attributable deaths from 1990 to 2023 were derived, with the per-factor percentage change expressed relative to the 1990 value and the G20 reference summarized as the median and interquartile range across the 19 member states.

Menopausal Stratification

Following the approach of the GBD 2023 breast-cancer analysis, menopausal status was defined using age as a proxy: the premenopausal age group comprised females aged 20–54 years and the postmenopausal age group comprised females aged 55 years and older. These are age bands rather than measured menopausal status, and the two strata are referred to throughout as the premenopausal and postmenopausal age groups.22 ASIR and ASMR in the premenopausal and postmenopausal age groups were compared across the G20 for 2023 and, for China, across 1990–2023.23 Postmenopausal and premenopausal rates in 2023 were compared across the 19 member states with one-sided paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests.

Projection to 2050

Primary projections of the age-standardized incidence and mortality rates to 2050 used autoregressive integrated moving-average (ARIMA) models24 fitted to the 1990–2023 age-standardized rate series in log space, with the order (p, d, q) selected by the Akaike information criterion and 95% prediction intervals obtained from the forecast distribution. ARIMA has been evaluated against age-period-cohort and Bayesian alternatives for forecasting age-standardized cancer incidence.25 Absolute case projections combined the projected ARIMA rates with log-linearly extrapolated age-specific population structures. The univariate time-series forecasting approach follows established practice for GBD rate series.26

Software, Reporting, and Ethics

Analyses used R 4.5.3 (segmented and Epi) and Python 3.12 (pandas, numpy, statsmodels, scipy, and matplotlib). Reporting follows the Guidelines for Accurate and Transparent Health Estimates Reporting (GATHER) for the burden-estimation component.27 As a secondary analysis of publicly available aggregate and summary-level data, the study was not pre-registered; ethical review was exempt under Article 32 of the Chinese Measures for Ethical Review of Life Science and Medical Research Involving Human Subjects, as detailed in the Ethics Approval and Consent to Participate section.

Results

China’s 2023 Burden Within the G20

Age-standardized incidence varied geographically across the G20, with the highest rates concentrated among Western European and North American members and the lowest among South and Southeast Asian members (Figure 1A), whereas elevated age-standardized mortality appeared in several high-income members as well as in lower-SDI members (Figure 1B and Table 1). Across the four age-standardized measures of incidence, mortality, DALYs, and prevalence, China consistently ranked in the lower percentiles within the G20 (Figure 1C and Table S1), clustering with other middle-SDI economies and distinct from the high-rate Western members. In 2023, age-standardized incidence varied more than three-fold across the G20, from 114.6 per 100,000 (95% UI: 98.4 to 134.0) in France to 29.4 (95% UI: 22.8 to 37.1) in India, with China’s ASIR (31.6 per 100,000; 95% UI: 25.4 to 38.2) the second lowest of the 19 G20 member states, below the G20-member median of 49.4 and above only India (Figure 1C and Table 1). Despite this low rate, China recorded the largest absolute incident burden of any G20 member, with 342,725 incident cases (95% UI: 270,872 to 413,171), a consequence of its population size (Figure 1D). China’s MIR (0.21) ranked eighth lowest of the 19 G20 member states (just behind Italy), within the lower band led by Japan, the Republic of Korea, France, the United States, Australia, and Canada, indicating a comparatively low ratio of deaths to incident cases, whereas the highest ratios clustered among lower-resource members such as South Africa and India (Figure 1E and Table S2). Temporal Trends, 1990–2023

Across 1990–2023, China’s ASIR increased modestly (EAPC 0.40, 95% CI 0.28 to 0.52), below the rest-of-G20 mean of 1.05, and this country-versus-rest difference was not significant after FDR correction (q = 0.104) (Figure 2A and Table S3). Incidence trends diverged widely across the G20: the ASIR rose fastest in Saudi Arabia (EAPC 3.96), the Republic of Korea (3.56), and Indonesia (2.73), yet fell in several high-income members, most steeply in Canada (−1.17) and the United States (−1.16). China’s ASMR, by contrast, declined markedly (EAPC −1.92, 95% CI −2.09 to −1.75) against a rest-of-G20 mean of −0.23, differing from the remaining G20 distribution after FDR correction (q < 0.001) (Figure 2B). China’s was the third-steepest mortality decline in the G20, behind only the United Kingdom (−2.06) and Canada (−1.97), whereas the ASMR continued to rise most in Saudi Arabia (2.42), Indonesia (1.90), and India (1.68). Both rates rose in Japan (ASIR EAPC 2.29, 95% CI 2.04 to 2.54; ASMR EAPC 1.01, 95% CI 0.89 to 1.14), the Republic of Korea and Saudi Arabia, the only three of the eleven members with a 2023 SDI of 0.80 or above in which mortality rose. The same pattern of faster mortality decline held for DALYs (EAPC −2.05, 95% CI −2.23 to −1.87) and YLLs (EAPC −2.24, 95% CI −2.43 to −2.05). Segmented regression confirmed a deceleration in China’s incidence trajectory, with the annual increase slowing from 1.48% in 1990–1998 to 0.03% in 1998–2020 before an upturn to 4.98% in 2020–2023 (breakpoints 1998 and 2020). Across the G20, these segmented ASIR trajectories spanned a wide range (Figure 2C): the United States showed the steepest decline (AAPC −1.00, 95% CI −1.06 to −0.93), Saudi Arabia the steepest increase (AAPC 3.71, 95% CI 3.43 to 3.98), and Japan a continuing but decelerating rise (AAPC 2.40, 95% CI 2.21 to 2.59), with breakpoint years and segment-specific changes for all 19 members reported in Table S4. China’s APC net drift for mortality was negative (Figure 2D). China’s MIR more than halved, from 0.43 in 1990 to 0.21 in 2023 (EAPC −2.3), a steeper improvement than the G20 average (0.37 to 0.29; EAPC −0.8), so that China moved from a higher ratio than the G20 in 1990 to the eighth lowest among the 19 members by 2023. Across the G20, the MIR declined in every member between 1990 and 2023, most steeply in the Republic of Korea (−61%) and China (−51%), the only two members exceeding 50% (Table S2). Because MIR is influenced by cancer registration completeness, diagnostic practices, and mortality certification as well as by outcomes after diagnosis, these declines describe mortality falling relative to incidence rather than a measured change in survival or case fatality. Together, these trends defined an incidence–mortality decoupling that was more pronounced in China than across the G20 as a whole. Demographic and Epidemiological Decomposition

China’s breast-cancer deaths rose from 49,616 (95% UI: 40,599 to 62,432) in 1990 to 76,589 (95% UI: 64,031 to 90,580) in 2023. Decomposition analysis revealed that all three drivers of the 1990–2023 change in incidence acted in the same direction in China (population growth 31%, aging 45%, epidemiological change 24%) (Figure 3A and Table S5). For deaths, the modest net rise was instead the sum of partly offsetting contributions: population growth (77%) and aging (134%) drove deaths upward, whereas declining age-specific death rates (the epidemiological component) acted in the opposite direction (−111%), the three summing to the net change (Figure 3B and Table S5). The DALY decomposition was directionally identical but larger (growth 116%, aging 158%, epidemiological change −174%). Across the G20, the epidemiological contribution to changing deaths diverged in sign along the SDI gradient (Figure 3C and Table S5): China’s aging (134%) was offset by a large epidemiological improvement (−111%); the United States showed the most extreme offset (aging 353%, epidemiological change −739%); whereas in other members the epidemiological component instead added to deaths (Saudi Arabia 38%, India 34%, with aging contributing only 10% and 18%, respectively). Sociodemographic Context and SDI Gradients

Country-level SDI among G20 members remained heterogeneous in 2023, ranging from 0.607 in India to 0.892 in the Republic of Korea; China’s SDI increased from 0.465 in 1990 to 0.728 in 2023 (an increase of 0.262), below most high-income G20 members (Figure 4A). Across the 19 G20 members, 2023 SDI correlated positively with ASIR (Pearson r = 0.74, P < 0.001) but inversely with the MIR (r = −0.84, P < 0.001) (Figure 4B and C; Table S6). Thus, higher-SDI members tended to have higher recorded incidence, whereas lower-SDI members had a higher ratio of mortality to incidence. Cross-country inequality in both measures narrowed over the study period (Figure 4D). For incidence, the SII decreased from +99.8 per 100,000 (95% CI +58.5 to +141.1) in 1990 to +61.7 (+28.6 to +94.8) in 2023, while the CI decreased from +0.322 (+0.189 to +0.455) to +0.168 (+0.078 to +0.258) (change in CI −0.154, bootstrap 95% CI −0.259 to −0.035), indicating that incidence remained concentrated in higher-SDI members but less strongly than in 1990. For mortality, the SII decreased from +21.0 (+8.0 to +33.9) to −0.3 (−8.8 to +8.2) and the CI from +0.184 (+0.070 to +0.298) to −0.003 (−0.090 to +0.084) (change in CI −0.187, −0.282 to −0.075), indicating that the socioeconomic gradient in mortality was no longer distinguishable from zero by 2023. Risk Factors

High BMI and high fasting plasma glucose accounted for a substantial and growing share of attributable breast-cancer deaths across most G20 members (Figure 5A and Table S7). China’s risk-attributable mortality rose across all seven factors between 1990 and 2023, led by high BMI (up 200%), which reached 4251 attributable deaths in 2023 (95% UI: 162 to 8565), and low physical activity (up 94%), with diet high in red meat (up 59%) and high fasting plasma glucose (up 55%; 5180 deaths, 95% UI: 3589 to 7745) also increasing. Relative to the G20 median, China’s increases were at or below the peer norm for most factors and notably lower for high fasting plasma glucose (55% vs 151%) and smoking (13% vs 61%), indicating that China’s growing metabolic risk burden was broadly typical of, rather than exceptional within, the G20 (Figure 5B). Burden by Menopausal Age Group

Rates in the postmenopausal age group (≥55 years) exceeded those in the premenopausal age group (20–54 years) in every G20 member in 2023, with median post/pre ratios of 3.6 for ASIR and 7.6 for ASMR (Wilcoxon P < 0.001 for both) (Figure 6A and B; Table S8). The larger proportional mortality decline in the premenopausal age group is consistent with greater relative gains among younger women; causal attribution to screening or treatment was not directly evaluated. Projections to 2050

Under the ARIMA model, China’s ASIR was projected to remain broadly stable to 2050, declining 4.3% to 30.2 per 100,000 (95% prediction interval 23.6 to 38.7), while absolute incident cases were projected to rise to approximately 792,000, an increase of 131% driven by population aging and growth (Figure 7A and Table S9). China’s ASMR was projected to decline by 11.7% to 5.96 per 100,000 by 2050 (95% prediction interval 3.3 to 10.9) (Figure 7B). This divergence between declining age-standardized incidence and rising absolute case numbers was shared by China and six other G20 members and is consistent with the decomposition above. As an external check on the GBD-based estimates, China’s female breast-cancer rates were benchmarked against national cancer registry-based estimates for China in 2022.28 For 2022, the GBD-derived age-standardized incidence rate (31.7 per 100,000) lay within 4% of the registry-based value (33.0 per 100,000), the incident-case count differed by approximately 5% (340,300 vs 357,200), and the age-standardized mortality rate was of comparable magnitude (6.8 vs 6.1 per 100,000). The residual differences are consistent with the distinct standard populations and estimation approaches of the two sources.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study is the first to characterize the burden of female breast cancer in China within a dedicated G20 framework built on GBD 2023. Female breast cancer underwent a clear epidemiological transition: incidence rose while mortality fell, particularly in China. In 2023, China’s age-standardized incidence ranked among the lowest in the G20 yet produced the largest absolute case burden in the group. This incidence–mortality decoupling, a pattern that may arise from greater case detection alongside lower case fatality,29 mirrors the global pattern of rising prevalence with declining death and DALY rates.30 Unlike the United States, which is declining across all indicators, or India, which is rising rapidly, China combines rising incidence with one of the steepest mortality declines in the G20,11 consistent with the policy emphasis on prevention, early detection, and treatment under Healthy China 2030.6 China’s low MIR, despite its middle-SDI position, is an ecological measure of mortality relative to incidence rather than a direct measure of survival: higher recorded incidence with lower mortality and MIR may reflect stronger surveillance, broader diagnostic recognition, and more complete registration as well as better outcomes after diagnosis, so incidence trends should not be interpreted in isolation.

Although age-standardized rates are improving, the absolute burden is set to grow. Decomposition attributed China’s 1990–2023 rise in deaths chiefly to population aging and growth, only partly offset by epidemiological improvement, and the ARIMA projections indicate that age-standardized rates will plateau or decline only modestly in China and several peers even as absolute incident cases rise steeply. This divergence between declining rates and rising case numbers implies that rate-based optimism understates the service-delivery demand that population aging will impose, a caution that holds in China and other high-baseline settings.

Within the G20, country-level SDI gradients suggest that equity priorities extend beyond occurrence toward relative mortality burden. In 2023, higher-SDI members tended to have higher recorded incidence, whereas lower-SDI members showed higher MIR; mortality is becoming disproportionately concentrated in lower-SDI countries, consistent with earlier GBD analyses of breast-cancer burden and attributable risk factors across SDI levels.30 These patterns indicate a shift in equity priorities: diagnostic intensity and risk exposure may shape incidence in high-SDI settings, but timely diagnosis and access to treatment remain central in lower-SDI settings, where population-based survival is lowest.31 The inequality indices place this shift on a common scale: the mortality gradient present across the G20 in 1990 was no longer distinguishable from zero by 2023, whereas incidence remained concentrated in higher-SDI members. These gradients bear directly on the WHO Global Breast Cancer Initiative’s goal of reducing breast-cancer mortality by 2.5% per year32 and on SDG target 3.4.4

The inverse, menopause-dependent association of higher BMI with breast cancer is not new. Decades of observational evidence have established that higher BMI is protective against breast cancer before menopause yet harmful after it.8,9 Proposed explanations include pathways linked to early-life adiposity and, after menopause, aromatase-driven estrogen exposure.33,34 Molecular evidence further indicates that a substantial share of the genes most frequently mutated in breast cancer is expressed differentially between premenopausal and postmenopausal patients, a distinction that age bands alone cannot capture.35 This menopause-dependent pattern is consistent with high BMI’s standing as the fastest-rising attributable risk for breast-cancer deaths in China: the population-level attributable burden plausibly reflects adult and postmenopausal adiposity acting through aromatase-driven estrogen exposure. Our stratification by menopausal age group, built on dedicated age-standardized data, shows the absolute burden concentrating in the postmenopausal age group, in which higher adiposity is the harmful exposure. Because a growing share of this burden falls on older women in the postmenopausal age group,23 sustaining the mortality decline will require screening and survival-care strategies that extend to older age groups rather than focusing only on younger cohorts.

Our analysis is consistent with previous GBD-based studies and extends them. The official GBD 2023 study forecast global and national burden to 2050 but did not foreground the G20, and a recent comparison by Feng and colleagues used the earlier GBD 2021 release with descriptive joinpoint and ARIMA models.3,12 We instead frame China’s transition within the G20 on GBD 2023. Country-specific trajectories within the G20 were heterogeneous. In contrast to the general pattern of declining mortality among high-income members, Japan showed rising age-standardized incidence and mortality across the study period. Candidate explanations include the comparatively late introduction and lower uptake of organized mammography screening, birth-cohort shifts in reproductive and lifestyle exposures, and rising adiposity;36 aggregate GBD estimates cannot distinguish among them, and this divergence warrants further investigation. Where our mortality trajectory differs from that earlier analysis, the gap reflects inter-cycle revisions between GBD 2021 and GBD 2023 and our G20-specific frame. For the G20, this means sustaining primary prevention37 while closing the survival gap in lower-SDI members. Prevention on this scale also depends on long-term surveillance of metabolic risk, for which digital and wearable monitoring technologies offer an increasingly practical route.38

The strengths of our analysis include a dedicated G20 frame on the most recent GBD 2023 release, a demographic-epidemiological decomposition, and menopausal-status stratification using dedicated age-standardized data. This study also has several limitations. First, GBD estimates are modeled from heterogeneous inputs and inherit their uncertainty, particularly for data-sparse members; country-specific forecasts should therefore be read cautiously. Second, menopausal status was defined using age as a proxy (20–54 and ≥55 years) rather than measured menopause; age at menopause is heterogeneous across individuals and populations, country-specific cutoff ages would be preferable, and misclassification around the age at menopause is therefore unavoidable. Third, MIR should be considered with caution because cross-country differences may reflect not only survival and treatment differences but also cancer registration, death registration, and data completeness; although MIR is commonly used as a population-level proxy of cancer outcomes in global epidemiological studies, it should not be regarded as a direct measure of survival or case fatality. Fourth, GBD reports a limited set of attributable risk factors and does not include tumor subtype or stage, constraining the granularity of cross-country comparison. Finally, the projection baseline spans the COVID-19 pandemic, which may have transiently affected case ascertainment and health-service delivery during 2020–2023; although the GBD framework smooths short-term perturbations, estimates spanning the pandemic years warrant careful interpretation.

Conclusions

Across the G20, female breast-cancer burden is undergoing an epidemiological transition: absolute burden is rising as populations grow and age, while age-standardized mortality is declining in most members, with marked heterogeneity across the G20. The burden is shifting toward older women in the postmenopausal age group (≥55 years), while the mortality-to-incidence ratio remains higher in lower-SDI members. China exemplifies this transition, combining a low age-standardized rate with the largest absolute burden and one of the fastest mortality declines in the group; its incidence is projected to fall only modestly to 2050, yet absolute cases are projected to rise by approximately 131% as the population grows and ages. These findings support G20-coordinated, equity-focused breast-cancer control that pairs metabolic primary prevention with urgent investment in early diagnosis and access to treatment for lower-SDI members.
