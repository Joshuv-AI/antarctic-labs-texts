Optimization of 68Ga-lactosylated human serum albumin (LSA) for PET-based quantitative evaluation of hepatic functional reserve in liver fibrosis models
Authors: Boeun Park, Ji Yong Park, Kisoo Pahk, Jin Sil Kim, Chanmin Joung, Jung Woo Byun, Ran Ji Yoo, Ji Seon Mun, Kyungsil Chae, Jae Min Jeong, Sungeun Kim, Jin Chul Paeng, Yun-Sang Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate preoperative assessment of hepatic functional reserve (HFR) and future remnant liver function is essential for reducing postoperative morbidity and mortality after liver surgery, yet current clinical methods remain indirect and provide limited spatial and quantitative information. Here, we developed an asialoglycoprotein receptor (ASGPR)-targeted tracer for positron emission tomography (PET), [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-lactosylated human serum albumin ([68Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA), and systematically optimized the degree of lactosylation for hepatic imaging. Comparative evaluation of lactosylated human serum albumin constructs bearing 7 or 12 lactose moieties [LSA (7) and LSA (12)] identified LSA (12) as the preferred construct with more favorable hepatocyte targeting in vivo. [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA was radiolabeled with high efficiency within 10 min at room temperature and showed favorable radiochemical stability in phosphate-buffered saline and serum. The optimized precursor was further formulated as a lyophilized kit, enabling rapid one-step 68Ga labeling, reproducible preparation, and storage stability. In thioacetamide-induced rat liver fibrosis models, including untreated, spontaneous recovery, and pirfenidone-treated groups, [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA PET/CT enabled single-time-point quantitative assessment of hepatic function, with 60 min post-injection selected as the optimal imaging time point. Hepatic uptake and liver-to-heart ratios were markedly reduced in fibrotic livers and progressively restored in recovery and treatment models. PET-derived parameters correlated with histological fibrosis, ASGPR expression, fibrogenic and apoptotic markers, and serum liver injury biomarkers. In contrast, 99mTc-mebrofenin required serial imaging to evaluate hepatobiliary kinetics, highlighting mechanistic and workflow differences between transport-based scintigraphy and receptor-associated PET uptake imaging. Reduced hepatic uptake was also reproduced in a fibrotic mouse model. In addition, single-dose toxicity testing showed favorable acute tolerability of non-radioactive NOTA-LSA. Together, these findings support the feasibility of [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA as a reproducible and quantitative PET platform for HFR imaging with translational potential.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12951-026-04827-y.Keywords: Hepatic functional reserve, Lactosylated serum albumin, Asialoglycoprotein receptor, Liver fibrosis

Background

The preoperative assessment of hepatic functional reserve (HFR) and future remnant liver (FRL) function is crucial for safe liver resection because post-hepatectomy liver failure remains a major cause of postoperative morbidity and mortality [1, 2]. Conventional liver function assessment relies on biochemical parameters and clinical grading systems such as the Child–Pugh and Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) scores [3, 4]. Dynamic quantitative tests include clearance-based approaches such as the indocyanine green (ICG) clearance test [5]. However, these conventional methods remain indirect and may not accurately reflect regional hepatic function. In particular, the predictive value of ICG-based assessment for post-hepatectomy liver failure remains limited when used alone, and ICG-derived indices can be influenced by factors other than intrinsic hepatocellular function [6, 7].

Nuclear medicine approaches provide an attractive alternative because they enable image-based monitoring and quantitative evaluation of both total and regional liver function [7–9]. Among these, technetium-99m (99mTc)-mebrofenin hepatobiliary scintigraphy has been increasingly used for preoperative functional assessment before major liver surgery and transplantation [9–11]. Preoperative 99mTc-mebrofenin scintigraphy has been shown to improve risk estimation for postoperative liver failure and, particularly in patients with uncertain parenchymal quality, may provide more relevant functional information than CT volumetry alone [10]. Another representative strategy is receptor-targeted imaging using asialoglycoprotein receptor (ASGPR)-directed tracers such as 99mTc-labeled galactosyl human serum albumin (GSA) scintigraphy [9, 12]. Because ASGPR is abundantly and selectively expressed on hepatocyte surfaces and mediates endocytosis of ligands containing terminal galactose or lactose residues, ASGPR-mediated uptake has been proposed as a surrogate marker of functional hepatocyte mass [9, 12].

Despite their utility, these clinically established tracers are based on single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT), which has inherent limitations relative to positron emission tomography (PET) in terms of spatial resolution and absolute quantification [9]. In addition, quantitative analysis in SPECT-based workflows may require relatively complex index derivation and region-of-interest delineation, potentially increasing inter-operator and inter-center variability [9, 11].

In our previous study, we developed 68Ga-labeled neolactosylated human serum albumin for PET imaging of hepatic ASGPR and demonstrated favorable in vivo stability with liver uptake comparable to that of GSA-based tracers [13]. However, the degree of lactosylation on the albumin scaffold was not systematically optimized, limiting detailed evaluation of ligand-density-dependent functional performance. In the present study, we therefore optimized the number of lactose moieties in an imaging-guided manner while ensuring radiolabeling reproducibility, serum stability, and suitability for kit formulation. We further established a lyophilized one-step 68Ga-labeling kit and evaluated its storage stability over time. To validate functional performance, we applied [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA PET/CT to thioacetamide (TAA)-induced rat liver fibrosis models, including recovery and treatment groups, and determined the optimal imaging time point for quantitative HFR assessment. PET-derived parameters, including liver uptake and liver-to-heart ratios, were correlated with histological fibrosis, ASGPR expression, and serum liver injury markers to determine how well PET signals reflect functional hepatocyte preservation and fibrosis severity. Finally, a single-dose toxicity study was conducted to support translational feasibility. Accordingly, this study was designed to establish an ASGPR-targeted PET platform for quantitative HFR assessment with a clinically practical lyophilized kit formulation (Scheme 1).

The lyophilized NOTA-LSA kit enables rapid one-step radiolabeling with 68Ga for clinical application. The lactosylated albumin selectively binds to asialoglycoprotein receptors (ASGPR) on hepatocytes, allowing specific uptake in functional liver tissue. In normal liver, high ASGPR expression results in increased tracer accumulation, whereas reduced ASGPR expression in fibrotic liver leads to decreased uptake. This differential uptake enables quantitative assessment of liver function using PET imaging

Methods

Materials

Albumin (recombinant human serum albumin, Exbumin®) was purchased from InVitria (Junction City, KS, USA). (p-SCN-Bn)-NOTA was obtained from FutureChem (Seoul, Korea). A BCA protein assay kit was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Rockford, IL, USA). Sterile 0.2 μm syringe filters (Acrodisc®, 25 mm, Supor® membrane) were purchased from Pall Corporation (USA). PD-10 size-exclusion columns were purchased from Cytiva (Seoul, Korea). Whatman No. 1 paper was purchased from VWR Scientific (Philadelphia, PA, USA). ITLC-SG plates were purchased from Agilent Technologies, Inc. (Santa Clara, CA, USA). Normal saline for injection was supplied by Seoul National University Hospital. All other reagents and chemicals were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA), unless otherwise stated. The molecular weights of HSA and its conjugates were determined by MALDI-TOF/TOF mass spectrometry using a TOF/TOF 5800 system (AB Sciex, Foster City, CA, USA). Hydrodynamic diameter and zeta potential were measured using a Zetasizer Nano ZS90 (Malvern Instruments, Malvern, UK), morphology was analyzed using a transmission electron microscope (JEM-1400 Flash, JEOL, Tokyo, Japan), and UV–visible absorbance was measured using a NanoDrop ND-1000 spectrophotometer (NanoDrop Technologies, Wilmington, DE, USA). A 68Ge/68Ga generator (1.85 GBq at calibration; Cyclotron Co., Obninsk, Russia) was used to obtain 68Ga. Radio-TLC analyses were performed using an AR-2000 radio-TLC scanner (Bioscan, Washington, DC, USA). Radioactivity was measured using a dose calibrator (Capintec, Ramsey, NJ, USA). Rat PET/CT imaging was performed using a small-animal PET/CT scanner at the National RI New Drug Center, whereas mouse PET imaging was performed using a SimPET system (Brightonix Imaging, Seoul, Korea). A dedicated small-animal SPECT/CT system (NanoSPECT/CT Plus, Mediso Ltd., Budapest, Hungary) located at the Molecular Imaging Center, Seoul National University, was used for in vivo SPECT/CT imaging.

Optimization and characterization of NOTA-LSA

NOTA-LSA was synthesized using a modified method described previously [7]. Albumin (130 mg, 2000 nmole/5 mL) dissolved in 0.1 M potassium phosphate buffer (pH 8.2) was reacted with α-lactose monohydrate (200 mg, 555 µmole) and sodium cyanoborohydride (200 mg, 3183 µmole) which was then incubated at room temperature for 4 days. To compare liver targeting by lactose number to albumin, each aliquot (26 mg, 400 nmole/1 mL) was collected and purified with 0.1 M Sodium carbonate buffer (pH 9.2) over reaction time of 0.5, 1, 2, 4 day to give LSA (22.1 mg, 6060 nmole/2 mL) and stored at 4 °C until further reaction. (p-SCN-Bn)-NOTA (9.3 mg, 3.6 µmole) was reacted with LSA solution and incubated at room temperature for 12 h. The mixture was then purified with PBS to give NOTA-LSA (18.8 mg, 284.6 nmole/4 mL). For additional confirmation in liver section, the fluorescent dye, fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC, 1.1 mg, 2846 nmole/1 mL) dissolved in DMSO was reacted with NOTA-LSA solution, incubated at room temperature for 2 h, and the mixture was then purified with PBS. All purification was performed using a desalting column (PD-10).

Radiochemistry

Radiolabeling of [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA followed the protocols as previously described [7]. 68GaCl3 (370 MBq/3 mL) was eluted from a 68Ge/68Ga generator using 0.1 M HCl, and the pH was adjusted to 4.5-5 with 1 mL of 1 M Sodium acetate buffer (pH 5.3). The 68GaCl3 solution (123 MBq/1 mL) was then mixed with NOTA-LSA (1.5 mg/1 mL) and incubated for 10 min at room temperature. The radiolabeling efficiency was determined by radio thin layer chromatography (radio-TLC) using instant thin layer chromatography-silica gel (ITLC-SG) and 0.1 M citric acid (Rf values: [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA = 0.0-0.1; free 68Ga = 0.9-1.0). The radiochemical stability of [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA was evaluated in PBS and human serum at 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 h. [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA was 10-fold diluted with PBS or human serum and incubated at 37 °C, and analyzed by radio-TLC.

Establishment of thioacetamide (TAA)-induced liver fibrosis and treatment models

Six weeks old male Sprague-Dawley rats (260 ~ 280 g body weight) were used for the experiment (Koatech, Pyeongtaek, Korea). Rats were housed under barrier conditions on a 12-h light/dark cycle with free access to water and food. All experimental protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Korea University College of Medicine (Approval No. KOREA-2021-0125). All the methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. After a week of acclimation, rats were injected intraperitoneally with vehicle (0.9% normal saline) or TAA (300 mg/kg, 12 mg/mL in saline) three times a week for 28 days. During liver fibrosis induction, rats were housed under constant veterinary supervision and humanely euthanized if drastic changes in health status were reported.

Rats were orally treated once a day with either pirfenidone (CA-M493285, Toronto Research Chemicals, Toronto, ON, Canada) at a dosage of 300 mg/kg dissolved in 0.5% carboxymethylcellulose (CMC, C4888, MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA) at a concentration of 30 mg/mL, or they were administered the vehicle (CMC, 10 mL/kg) simultaneously with the commencement of TAA injection.

Validation and characterization of the liver fibrosis model

Histopathology. Rats were humanely euthanized after four weeks of TAA administration and PET/CT imaging. After euthanasia, the liver tissues-embedded paraffin blocks were prepared following the standard protocol [14]. Then, 4.5 μm thickness liver sections were stained with hematoxylin, eosin, and Masson’s trichrome based on general procedure [15]. All stained tissue images were acquired with Zeiss Axio Scan Z1 (Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed as previously described [14]. The following primary antibodies were used for IHC: anti-asialoglycoprotein receptor 1/HL-1 (ASGR1) antibody (1:200 dilution, ab127896, Abcam, Cambridge, United Kingdom), and anti-α-SMA (1:400 dilution, ab7817, Abcam). For secondary antibodies, Anti-goat IgG Alexa Fluor 488 and 594 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) were used. All IHC images were obtained with Zeiss Axio Scan Z1 (Carl Zeiss).

Before PET/CT imaging, blood was collected with femoral artery cannulation, as described in [16]. The levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), direct bilirubin (DBIL), and total bilirubin (TBIL) in serum were measured with a FUJI DRI-Chemiclinical Chemistry Analyzer (FUJI DRI-CHEM 4000i, Fuji Film, Tokyo, Japan).

Total RNA from liver tissues extracted with the RNeasy Fibrous Tissue Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) and qRT-PCR was performed as previously described [17, 18]. The sequences of transcriptome-specific primers were described previously [18].

In vivo PET/CT imaging of [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA

PET/CT imaging studies of [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA biodistribution were performed with a small animal PET/CT scanner. Animals were anesthetized with 1.5–2.5% isoflurane and injected with 9.2 ± 1.8 MBq (0.5 mg/200 µL) of 68Ga radiolabeled NOTA-LSA or NOTA-HSA (n = 3/each group) via the tail vein. For attenuation correction and anatomical localization, micro-CT imaging was obtained before PET imaging using 50 kVp X-ray voltage with 0.16 mAs of anode current. To optimize the imaging acquisition time, three time points were selected for evaluation. CT scans and PET images were acquired from the heart to the kidneys at 30, 60, and 120 min post-injection (10 min, one-bed scan). After comparison of the serial PET images, 60 min post-injection was selected as the optimal imaging time point. PET images in the subsequent experiments were acquired for 20 min from the heart to the kidneys at 60 min after injection of [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA, using an energy window of 400–600 keV. For image analysis, PET and CT data were reconstructed using Nucline NanoScan software (version 3.00.021.0000). The standardized uptake value (SUV) was calculated as (MBq/ml in tissue)/(MBq of the injected dose)/(body weight (g)) using the same volume of interest (VOI) of heart for blood pool and liver. For quantitative image analysis, VOIs were manually placed on reconstructed PET/CT images using the CT component for anatomical guidance. Hepatic VOIs were placed within the liver parenchyma while avoiding large vessels, liver edges, and obvious artifacts, and blood-pool VOIs were placed in the cardiac blood-pool region while avoiding spillover from adjacent myocardium or liver activity. The same VOI strategy was applied across animals and imaging time points to ensure consistent quantification. Partial-volume correction was not applied because the analysis was performed using relatively large organ-level VOIs rather than small focal lesions. For rat PET/CT analyses, organ-level SUVmean values were used to calculate liver uptake, blood-pool activity, liver-to-heart ratios, and HFR values. SUVmax and SUVpeak were not used for rat organ-level quantification.

99mTc-mebrofenin

99mTc-mebrofenin was prepared using a commercially available lyophilized kit (Cellbion Mebrofenin for Injection, Korea). Each vial contained 45.0 mg of mebrofenin (USP) along with excipients including gluconate (0.44 mg), sodium hydroxide, hydrochloric acid (for pH adjustment), and sterile water for injection.

For radiolabeling, sodium pertechnetate (99mTcO₄⁻) solution was added to the vial under aseptic conditions and allowed to react according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The resulting 99mTc-mebrofenin solution was used for intravenous administration.

In vivo SPECT/CT imaging of 99mTc-mebrofenin

To further validate the performance of [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA, in vivo SPECT/CT imaging with the clinically established hepatobiliary tracer ⁹⁹ᵐTc-mebrofenin was performed in normal rats and TAA-induced fibrotic rats at 6 weeks. Animals were fasted for 24 h prior to imaging to minimize gastrointestinal uptake. 99mTc-mebrofenin was prepared using a commercially available kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions and administered via intravenous injection. SPECT/CT imaging was conducted using a dedicated small-animal SPECT/CT system. CT scans were acquired for anatomical reference, followed by SPECT acquisition covering the thoracoabdominal region. Serial images were acquired at 10, 20, 30, and 60 min post-injection to evaluate hepatic uptake and hepatobiliary excretion kinetics. Imaging parameters were set according to the system specifications, and data were reconstructed using the manufacturer-provided software.

Synthesis and scale-up of NOTA-LSA

Exbumin® (217.6 mg, 3.30 µmol) dissolved in 0.1 M potassium phosphate buffer (6.4 mL, pH 8.0) was reacted with α-lactose monohydrate (320 mg, 890 µmol) and sodium cyanoborohydride (320 mg, 5.09 mmol) dissolved in 0.1 M potassium phosphate buffer (1.6 mL, pH 8.0), which was then incubated at room temperature for 4 days with gentle stirring on a rotary mixer. The reaction mixture was purified with 0.1 M sodium carbonate buffer (pH 9.2) to give LSA, which was stored at 4 °C until further use. p-SCN-Bn-NOTA (15.7 mg, 28.0 µmol) dissolved in 0.1 M sodium carbonate buffer (0.8 mL, pH 9.2) was added to the LSA solution and incubated at room temperature for 24 h. The crude NOTA-LSA solution was then purified with 1 M sodium acetate buffer (pH 5.0) for the production batch used for kit development to give acetate-buffered NOTA-LSA suitable for subsequent [68Ga]Ga labeling and lyophilization. The final protein concentration was determined by BCA assay, and the solution was stored at 4 °C until kit formulation. All purification was performed using a PD-10 column.

Preparation of lyophilized NOTA-LSA kit vials

Lyophilized NOTA-LSA kits were prepared from the acetate-buffered NOTA-LSA stock solution. The NOTA-LSA solution in 1 M sodium acetate buffer (pH 5.0) was adjusted to a final protein concentration of 2 mg/mL based on the BCA assay and passed through a sterile 0.2 μm syringe filter under aseptic conditions. The filtered solution was dispensed as 1.0 mL aliquots into prewashed glass vials and lyophilized for more than 40 h until a dry, intact cake was obtained. Before sample loading, the freeze-dryer was operated in pre-freeze mode with the venting valve open for at least 2 h to cool the chamber and shelves. After filling, the vials were placed on the shelves and partially stoppered during lyophilization. At the end of the cycle, the chamber was backfilled with high-purity nitrogen (99.999%), the stoppers were fully inserted, and the vials were sealed with aluminum caps. The lyophilized NOTA-LSA kit vials were stored at 4 °C in the dark and used at different storage times for evaluation of radiolabeling efficiency and radiochemical stability.

Characterization of NOTA-LSA

The degree of functionalization (DOF), defined as the number of ligands per mole of albumin, was calculated by matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization time-of-flight (MALDI-TOF) mass spectrometry (MS) analysis. Before analysis, albumin samples (10 µg/10 µL) were mixed with sinapinic acid (SA) matrix solution (1:1, v/v) in 50% acetonitrile containing 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA); 1 µL of the mixture was spotted onto the target plate, air-dried, and analyzed by MALDI-TOF MS in linear positive-ion mode at an acceleration voltage of 25 kV. Mass spectra were acquired over an m/z range of 50,000–100,000 Da by averaging signals from 2,500 laser shots. The morphology and particle size of albumin samples were determined by transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Albumin samples were diluted in distilled water (0.5 mg/mL), sonicated for 5 min, and a single drop was placed onto 400-mesh holey carbon-coated copper grids for 5 min. Excess solution was removed with filter paper, and the grids were dried overnight before imaging. The TEM protocol was adapted from our previously reported methods for morphological characterization of albumin-based nanoplatforms [19, 20]. TEM images were acquired at an acceleration voltage of 80 kV, and particle size was measured using ImageJ software with at least 50 particles analyzed for each sample. No additional heavy-metal staining, including OsO₄ staining, was applied because sufficient contrast for morphological assessment was obtained under the optimized sample-preparation and imaging conditions. The hydrodynamic diameter and surface charge of albumin samples were measured using a dynamic light scattering (DLS) system. Samples were diluted with distilled water (size: 1–10 mg/mL; zeta potential: 0.1–1 mg/mL) and sonicated for 1 min before measurement. All measurements were conducted at room temperature in triplicate and averaged. Protein concentration was quantified using a BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and the molar concentration of albumin was calculated from the measured protein concentration and the molecular weight of each sample. The UV–visible absorption spectra of HSA, LSA, and NOTA-LSA were recorded using a NanoDrop ND-1000 spectrophotometer (NanoDrop Technologies, Wilmington, DE, USA). Samples were prepared in distilled water at 0.5 mg/mL, and spectra were acquired over 200–320 nm at room temperature using distilled water as a blank. Baseline-corrected spectra were used for comparison of the overall absorption profiles.

Radiochemical properties of kit formation

[68Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA was prepared using generator-eluted 68GaCl3 under standard kit-based labeling conditions. 68GaCl3 was eluted from a 68Ge/68Ga generator using 0.1 M HCl, and 3.0 mL of the eluate was directly added to a lyophilized NOTA-LSA kit vial. The vial was gently mixed until the lyophilized cake was completely dissolved and incubated for 10 min at room temperature. Radiolabeling efficiency was determined by radio-thin layer chromatography (radio-TLC) using instant thin layer chromatography-silica gel (ITLC-SG) and 0.1 M citric acid. After development, the strips were dried and analyzed using an AR-2000 radio-TLC scanner. Under these conditions, [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA remained at the origin (Rf = 0.0-0.1), whereas free 68Ga migrated to the solvent front (Rf = 0.9-1.0). To evaluate the maximum labeling capacity according to kit concentration, lyophilized NOTA-LSA kits were prepared at different protein concentrations of 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, and 5.0 mg/mL. Each kit vial was radiolabeled with generator-eluted 68GaCl3 as described above, and radiochemical purity was determined by radio-TLC. The maximum labeling capacity for each kit concentration was defined as the highest applied activity that resulted in a radiochemical purity of at least 95% under the standard labeling conditions. The in vitro radiochemical stability of [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA prepared from the lyophilized kit was assessed in human serum. The radiolabeled product was mixed with human serum at a sample-to-serum volume ratio of 1:5 and incubated at 37 °C. Aliquots were collected at 10 min, 30 min, 1 h, 2 h, and 3 h, diluted 1:20 with PBS to reduce viscosity and improve chromatographic resolution, and analyzed by radio-TLC. Radiochemical stability was expressed as the percentage of radioactivity remaining in the protein-bound fraction over time.

Shelf-life evaluation of the lyophilized NOTA-LSA kit

To evaluate the storage stability and potential shelf life of the lyophilized NOTA-LSA kit, kit vials were stored at 4 °C in the dark and tested at predetermined time points of 1, 3, and 6 months after preparation. At each time point, a kit vial was radiolabeled by addition of 3.0 mL of generator-eluted 68GaCl3 and incubated for 10 min at room temperature, as described above. Radiochemical yield and radiochemical purity were determined by radio-TLC using ITLC-SG and 0.1 M citric acid. In addition, the in vitro radiochemical stability of [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA prepared from stored kits was evaluated in human serum under the conditions described above. The shelf life of the kit was defined as the storage period during which the kit maintained a radiochemical purity of at least 95% under the standard labeling conditions. During this period, [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA also retained radiochemical stability in human serum over the tested incubation time.

Single-dose toxicity study

A single-dose intravenous toxicity study was conducted in Sprague–Dawley rats to assess the acute toxicity of non-radioactive NOTA-LSA. Rats (8 weeks old; male and female; n = 5 per sex per group) were randomly assigned to a vehicle control group or to NOTA-LSA groups receiving 0.103, 1.030, or 10.300 mg/kg by a single intravenous tail-vein injection. Animals were observed for 14 days for mortality, clinical signs, and body weight changes, followed by gross necropsy at study termination. Statistical analyses were performed using Bartlett’s test, one-way ANOVA or the Kruskal–Wallis test, and Dunnett’s or Dunn’s multiple comparison test, as appropriate. The approximate lethal dose (ALD) was estimated on the basis of the overall toxicological findings.

In vivo simPET imaging in normal and fibrotic mouse models

To verify that the lyophilized NOTA-LSA kit provided in vivo performance comparable to that of the liquid precursor, SimPET imaging was performed in normal and TAA-induced fibrotic C57BL/6 mice. On each experimental day, normal and fibrotic mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and positioned side by side on the scanner bed. [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA prepared from the lyophilized kit was administered intravenously via the tail vein at a comparable injected activity (approximately 50 ± 8 µCi; 1.85 ± 0.30 MBq) and injection volume. Sequential static scans were acquired immediately after injection and at 0.5, 1, and 2 h post-injection using the SimPET system with the same acquisition and reconstruction parameters applied across groups. Volumes of interest were placed over the liver and an intravascular blood-pool region, and liver SUVmax and liver-to-blood uptake ratios were calculated for each mouse. SUVpeak was not used in the mouse PET analysis.

Statistics

All statistical analyses were performed using the GraphPad Prism software (version 5.0) and MedCalc (version 20.218). All data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with a Tukey post-hoc test was used to compare three or more groups. Normality distribution was evaluated with the Shapiro-Wilk test. For comparisons between the control and fibrotic liver group, Welch’s t-test and Mann-Whitney test were used. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant Scheme 1.

Results

Optimization and characterization of NOTA-LSA

The conjugation of the ligand to the amine group of the lysine residue on albumin was a two-step process (Fig. 1A). First, α-lactose for ASGPR target was conjugated to the albumin via reductive amination. Second, SCN-NOTA for 68Ga chelation and FITC were conjugated to the albumin via thiourea bond formation. The ratio of ligand per mole of albumin, degree of functionalization (DOF) was calculated from the average molecular weight difference of HSA, LSA(n), and NOTA-LSA(n) based on MALDI-TOF MS spectra, taking into account the theoretical mass increase of lactose (+ 325.3 Da) and NOTA (+ 450.5 Da) (n = DOF of lactose, notation n = 12) (Fig. 1B; Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. S1A). In this study, to optimize the number of LSA, we quantified the number of lactose moieties introduced onto the albumin surface as a function of reaction time. Notably, since the number of available amine groups on the albumin surface is known to be approximately 15–20 [19], we confirmed that lactose functionalization reached saturation after 8 days of reaction, as evidenced by time-course sampling (Supplementary Fig. S1B and S1C). Importantly, the reaction-time-dependent increase in DOF indicated that ligand density could be tuned in a controlled and reproducible manner, while the saturation behavior suggested a practical upper limit for surface modification on the albumin scaffold. Specifically, we collected LSA samples by reaction time over 4 days to compare image differences in liver uptake as a function of lactose number. In our previous studies, we demonstrated that the in vivo biodistribution of albumin varies depending on the degree of surface amine utilization [19]. In particular, increased surface modification led to a higher level of non-specific liver uptake. Furthermore, in subsequent studies introducing mannose, we found that the efficiency of the albumin platform, in terms of exploiting sugar-mediated selectivity, was maximized when more than six saccharide units were conjugated [20]. Similarly, in studies using another monosaccharide, glucose, the highest targeting selectivity was observed when more than six sugar moieties were introduced [21]. Together, these observations indicate that ligand density is a critical design variable for sugar-mediated hepatic targeting, because insufficient ligand presentation may limit receptor engagement whereas excessive surface modification can alter biodistribution and increase nonspecific liver accumulation [19–21]. Based on these findings, the present study aimed to achieve a surface modification degree greater than six. To comparatively evaluate the effect of higher modification levels, we selected degrees of modification of 7 and 12 for investigation. In particular, considering the two-step strategy required for radiolabeling or fluorescent labeling, we defined the maximum degree of surface modification as a reaction condition corresponding to approximately half of the available amine groups being occupied. In this context, LSA (7) was selected as a moderate ligand-density construct above the apparent targeting threshold, whereas LSA (12) represented a higher yet still practical level of multivalent lactose presentation suitable for subsequent biological comparison.

As comparison samples, we used HSA, LSA (7), and LSA (12), which had similar DOFs for NOTA in each sample (DOF; lactose = 7.0 ± 0.4 and 12.1 ± 0.4, NOTA = 1.5 ± 0.2). Maintaining comparable NOTA loading across constructs was important to ensure that subsequent differences in radiolabeling or in vivo behavior could be interpreted primarily as the effect of lactose density rather than chelator content. TEM and DLS were used to determine the size distribution and morphology of HSA, LSA, and LSA-NOTA (Table 2; Fig. 1B–E). The TEM images showed evenly distributed spherical morphologies with diameter of approximately 8–10 nm (HSA = 8.16 ± 2.25, LSA = 9.76 ± 2.40, NOTA-LSA = 8.47 ± 1.09). Number-weighted DLS measurement also revealed similar hydrodynamic diameters of approximately 7–9 nm (HSA = 7.65 ± 1.15, LSA = 7.44 ± 1.13, NOTA-LSA = 8.20 ± 1.10), and the zeta potential tended to decrease as the ligands bound to amine group of albumin (HSA = − 12.3 ± 5.13 mV, LSA = − 13.1 ± 5.20 mV, NOTA-LSA = − 14.5 ± 6.54 mV). These findings indicate that sequential ligand conjugation did not markedly alter the structural or colloidal properties of the albumin scaffold, supporting the interpretation that subsequent biological differences arise primarily from ligand presentation rather than gross physicochemical instability. Taken together, these data establish LSA (7) and LSA (12) as structurally comparable albumin-based constructs differing primarily in ligand density, thereby enabling systematic evaluation of multivalent ASGPR-targeting effects in subsequent in vivo studies.

Characterization of the TAA-induced liver fibrosis model

To evaluate the functional performance of [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA, a TAA-induced liver fibrosis model was established. As shown in Fig. 2A, histological analysis revealed marked collagen deposition and increased α-SMA expression in fibrotic liver compared with normal controls, indicating activation of hepatic stellate cells. In contrast, ASGR1 expression, a representative marker of functional hepatocytes, was significantly reduced in fibrotic liver. These findings were further supported by qPCR analysis (Fig. 2B), which showed significant upregulation of fibrosis-related markers, including α-SMA and collagen types I, III, and V, in the fibrosis group. Apoptosis-related markers, including caspase-3, -8, and − 9, were also markedly increased, whereas ASGR1 expression was significantly decreased. In addition, serum biochemical analysis (Fig. 2C) demonstrated significantly elevated levels of AST, ALT, total bilirubin, and direct bilirubin in the fibrosis model compared with the sham group, confirming impaired liver function. Collectively, these results validate the successful establishment of a liver fibrosis model with both structural and functional impairment, suitable for subsequent evaluation of HFR using PET imaging.

To determine the optimal imaging time point, preliminary studies were conducted using LSA (7) and LSA (12), with PET images acquired at 30 min, 1 h, and 2 h after intravenous injection. A key consideration was identifying the time point that provides sufficient image contrast while accounting for the half-life of gallium. In our previous study, imaging-based analysis was performed at 15 min post-injection, while ex vivo biodistribution was evaluated up to 1 h [7]. Based on these findings, we designed the present study to compare imaging at 30 min, 1 h, and 2 h. Notably, both LSA (7) and LSA (12) exhibited an increase in the liver-to-heart (L/H) ratio over time. At 30 min, the L/H ratio showed a 2.5-fold difference between LSA (7) and LSA (12), which increased to 2.8-fold at 1 h, and then decreased to 2.5-fold at 2 h. This kinetic pattern suggests that 1 h post-injection provides a favorable balance between sufficient hepatic accumulation and reduced blood-pool activity, whereas the decreased relative contrast at 2 h may reflect the combined effects of radioactive decay and tracer washout. Therefore, 1 h post-injection was selected as the optimal static imaging time point on the basis of liver-to-heart contrast, blood-pool clearance, and practical compatibility with the ⁶⁸Ga imaging window. In particular, considering the half-life of gallium (~ 67 min), the 1 h time point represents the most appropriate balance between signal retention and imaging contrast. This result suggests that 1 h post-injection provides a favorable balance between hepatic target retention and blood-pool clearance, thereby maximizing discrimination between constructs with different ASGPR-targeting efficiency [7]. Notably, even without applying precise anatomical segmentation and instead using arbitrarily defined spherical ROIs for each organ, distinctly different uptake patterns were observed, with differences sufficiently pronounced to be clearly distinguishable (Supplementary Fig. S3).

PET/CT imaging was performed to evaluate the biodistribution of [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA and its capability for quantitative assessment of HFR. First, the effect of lactose density on hepatocyte targeting was investigated using HSA, LSA (7), and LSA (12) (Fig. 3A, B). While non-lactosylated HSA showed comparable uptake in the liver and blood pool, increasing the degree of lactose functionalization resulted in significantly enhanced liver uptake with reduced blood retention (Liver/Heart ratio; LSA (12): 8.34 ± 1.63 > LSA (7): 2.56 ± 0.32 > HSA: 1.25 ± 0.31). These findings support the interpretation that lactose density contributes to ASGPR-associated hepatic accumulation and that higher lactose density improves hepatocyte-associated uptake. The clear separation among HSA, LSA (7), and LSA (12) suggests that ligand-density optimization at the molecular design level translated into differential in vivo hepatic uptake performance.

Next, LSA (12) was applied to evaluate liver function across disease models with varying severity (Fig. 3C–E). Compared to normal sham animals, fibrotic rats exhibited markedly reduced hepatic uptake, approaching blood pool levels (Liver/Heart; Sham: 8.34 ± 1.63 vs. Model: 1.06 ± 0.02). Notably, hepatic uptake progressively increased in both spontaneous recovery and pirfenidone-treated groups (Model: 1.06 ± 0.02 < Natural healing: 3.02 ± 0.41 < Pirfenidone: 5.81 ± 0.37), reflecting functional restoration of hepatocytes. These trends are consistent with the interpretation that reduced hepatic uptake reflects loss of functional hepatocyte mass and receptor availability in fibrotic liver, whereas partial recovery of uptake reflects restoration of hepatocyte-associated function [8, 9]. The stepwise increase in hepatic uptake from the fibrosis model to the natural healing and pirfenidone-treated groups indicates that [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA PET can capture graded changes in hepatic functional status rather than merely distinguishing normal from diseased liver.

Based on these results, HFR was quantitatively derived using the liver-to-heart ratio normalized to the sham group. HFR was calculated as follows: HFR (%) = [(L/H ratio of each animal) / (mean L/H ratio of sham animals)] × 100, where the L/H ratio was defined as liver uptake divided by heart blood-pool uptake. The calculated HFR values clearly distinguished disease severity and treatment response (Table 3, Model: 12.7%, Natural healing: 36.2%, Pirfenidone: 69.7%), demonstrating that [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA PET enables robust and quantitative evaluation of liver function. Importantly, hepatic uptake alone showed strong discriminatory power, suggesting a simplified and practical metric for HFR assessment without complex normalization [1, 4, 5, 10]. This finding is particularly relevant from a translational perspective, because it suggests that a simple static uptake-based metric may be sufficient for functional stratification without relying exclusively on more complex dynamic analysis [4–23].

Comparison with ⁹⁹ᵐTc-mebrofenin SPECT/CT

Previous studies using 99mTc-mebrofenin have demonstrated that assessment of hepatic function relies on dynamic hepatobiliary transport kinetics, requiring serial imaging to capture both hepatic uptake and biliary excretion patterns [4–24]. In particular, delayed hepatobiliary excretion, reflected by prolonged gallbladder visualization, has been reported in liver fibrosis models [11, 22, 25]. This pattern indicates that 99mTc-mebrofenin primarily reflects hepatobiliary transport behavior rather than a direct receptor-based measure of hepatocyte function. However, as shown in the present TAA-induced liver fibrosis model, although 99mTc-mebrofenin imaging could distinguish impaired excretion kinetics compared with normal controls, such differences were only discernible through serial imaging (Fig. 4). This kinetic dependence contrasts with [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA PET, in which hepatic function can be estimated from a single optimized static time point based on hepatic tracer uptake [7–9]. This kinetic dependence makes it challenging to assess hepatic functional reserve using a single time-point image. Moreover, due to the intrinsic limitations of SPECT imaging, including lower spatial resolution and limited quantitative accuracy, precise evaluation of disease severity remains difficult [4, 11, 22]. This distinction likely reflects the different biological processes interrogated by the two tracers: 99mTc-mebrofenin reflects hepatobiliary transport and excretion, whereas [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA reflects ASGPR-associated hepatocyte recognition and uptake [4, 9, 22, 23]. In contrast, [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA PET enables single-time-point quantitative assessment of hepatic function, providing a simplified PET-based approach for evaluating HFR [1, 4, 5, 10, 21]. From a translational perspective, this is particularly relevant because assessment of hepatic functional reserve requires reliable quantification of preserved hepatocyte function in addition to anatomical liver volume [1, 4, 5, 10]. This simplified workflow may support reproducible HFR assessment in future studies, although direct clinical comparison with transport-based SPECT approaches remains necessary [4–23].

Scale-up synthesis, kit formulation, and radiochemical characterization of NOTA-LSA

For clinical translation, process conditions for large-scale production and kit formulation were established based on the optimized two-step conjugation strategy that preserved albumin size, morphology, and colloidal stability at small scale. This large-scale process maintained the ligand-density regime identified as optimal in small-scale experiments, thereby preserving the multivalent ASGPR-targeting design rather than merely reproducing the chemistry at higher batch size. In this context, a plant cell–derived recombinant human serum albumin (Exbumin®) was employed as the starting material to minimize potential immunogenicity concerns associated with protein-based therapeutics [14]. The use of plant cell–derived recombinant HSA is also relevant for clinical translation because it reduces potential concerns related to human- or animal-derived serum contaminants and provides a more standardized source material for scalable kit production. LSA and NOTA-LSA were generated by sequential lactose conjugation and NOTA functionalization, and the synthetic procedure was readily scalable to the batch size required for kit manufacture (Fig. 5A; Table 4). MALDI-TOF MS demonstrated a stepwise increase in centroid mass from HSA to LSA and subsequently to NOTA-LSA, confirming efficient chemical modification with average degrees of functionalization of 11.09 for lactose and 2.10 for NOTA per HSA molecule (Table 4). The physicochemical properties of the albumin derivatives were largely preserved after scale-up conjugation: DLS showed comparable hydrodynamic diameters for HSA, LSA, and NOTA-LSA (7.462 ± 1.019, 7.505 ± 1.191, and 7.412 ± 0.948 nm, respectively), and NOTA-LSA displayed a low polydispersity index with a slightly negative zeta potential, indicating favorable colloidal behavior suitable for kit formulation (Fig. 5B; Tables 4 and 5). UV–visible spectroscopy showed comparable absorption profiles for HSA, LSA, and NOTA-LSA, indicating that lactose and NOTA conjugation and subsequent kit formulation did not markedly alter the albumin backbone or overall solution integrity, while BCA assay of the acetate-buffered NOTA-LSA stock used for kit production confirmed a final protein concentration of 10.55 mg/mL after dilution correction (Fig. 5C, Fig. S5).

On the basis of these characterization data, NOTA-LSA was formulated as a lyophilized kit and its radiolabeling performance with ⁶⁸Ga was evaluated (Fig. 5D–F). Across the tested kit concentrations, the maximum labeling capacity was comparable, allowing labeling of approximately 20 mCi per vial while maintaining radiochemical purity of at least 95% (Fig. 5D). The 0.5 and 1 mg/mL kits achieved high radiochemical stability in human serum at 37 °C, with radiochemical purity remaining above 96% for up to 3 h (Table 6; Fig. 5E, F). LSA constructs prepared with different lactose densities for ligand-number optimization also showed similarly high labeling efficiency and serum stability under the same conditions, enabling the establishment of a unified, kit-compatible labeling protocol based on our previously reported method [7]. In addition, the single-vial, room-temperature ⁶⁸Ga-labeling procedure and preserved performance after refrigerated storage support a simplified and standardized workflow that is well suited to routine PET radiopharmacy practice and future multicenter implementation. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the scale-up process yields chemically well-defined NOTA-LSA with preserved physicochemical properties and robust, kit-based radiolabeling performance. Thus, the scaled-up NOTA-LSA kit couples a mechanistically optimized multivalent ASGPR-targeting design with a clinically practical radiopharmaceutical format, providing a ready-to-use platform for quantitative assessment of hepatic functional reserve in subsequent in vivo studies.

Radiochemistry and kit-based radiolabeling performance of [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA

The radiolabeling efficiency of [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA reached approximately 100% within 10 min at room temperature, as confirmed by radio-TLC showing a single peak at the origin with negligible free ⁶⁸Ga. The radiolabeled product maintained radiochemical purity above 85% in PBS and human serum for up to 4 h at 37 °C, indicating sufficient stability over the intended imaging period (Fig. 1G, H and Supplementary Fig. S2). These results indicate that the precursor itself possesses favorable chelation efficiency and radiochemical stability under mild labeling conditions, providing a robust solution-phase basis for subsequent kit development. Building on these solution-based results, the lyophilized NOTA-LSA kit enabled simple one-step preparation of [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA by direct addition of 3 mL of generator-eluted ⁶⁸GaCl₃ without further pH adjustment, achieving near-quantitative radiolabeling within 10 min at room temperature in a single vial. This finding indicates that lyophilization preserved the intrinsic chelation efficiency of the precursor, allowing the optimized solution-phase radiochemistry to be directly translated into a kit-based format. The ability to achieve near-quantitative labeling within 10 min at room temperature without additional pH adjustment also highlights the operational simplicity of the kit and supports its suitability for routine radiopharmacy workflow. The maximum labeling capacity depended on the NOTA-LSA content per vial, reaching 24.4 and 25.2 mCi for the 0.5 and 1 mg/mL kits and 19.97 and 19.92 mCi for the 2 and 5 mg/mL kits, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S6). These data suggest that formulation performance was not improved simply by increasing precursor concentration, and that the 0.5 and 1 mg/mL kits represented a more practical balance between labeling capacity and radiochemical stability. Among these, the 0.5 and 1 mg/mL formulations provided the most favorable balance between labeling capacity and stability, maintaining radiochemical purity above 96% in human serum at 37 °C for up to 3 h (Table 6; Fig. 5E, F). The maintained radiochemical purity over the imaging window supports the interpretation that subsequent in vivo uptake patterns predominantly reflect tracer-specific biodistribution rather than rapid radiometal dissociation. For shelf-life evaluation, the production batch used for kit manufacture showed a BCA-determined protein concentration of 13.16 mg/mL, and kits stored at 4 °C for 1, 3, and 6 months retained high labeling efficiency and post-labeling serum stability on radio-TLC analysis (Supplementary Figs. S7–S10). Retention of labeling efficiency and serum stability after refrigerated storage for up to 6 months supports the practical feasibility of batch production, storage, and decentralized on-site use. Collectively, these findings indicate that the lyophilized NOTA-LSA kit preserves the high radiolabeling efficiency observed in the precursor solution while providing a robust, time-efficient, and radiochemically stable platform for routine preparation of [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA. Taken together, these results support the lyophilized kit as a simplified, reproducible, and clinically practical radiopharmaceutical format for translational evaluation of ASGPR-targeted hepatic function imaging.

Single-dose intravenous toxicity of NOTA-LSA

A single-dose intravenous toxicity study was conducted to evaluate the acute safety of non-radioactive NOTA-LSA in Sprague–Dawley rats, and the overall study design is summarized in Table 7. Animals received a single tail-vein injection of NOTA-LSA at 0.103, 1.030, or 10.300 mg/kg and were monitored for 14 days. Toxicological interpretation is summarized in Table 8, and detailed outcomes by dose group are presented in Table 9. No mortality occurred in any treatment group during the observation period, and no treatment-related clinical signs were observed at any tested dose level. Body weight changes in both males and females remained within the normal range without statistically significant treatment-related differences compared with the vehicle control group, and gross necropsy and histopathological examination revealed no treatment-related abnormalities at study termination. A transient episode of chromodacryorrhea was observed in one male rat in the high-dose group; this finding resolved spontaneously during follow-up and was considered incidental rather than treatment related.

Although this study was limited to single-dose acute toxicity, the absence of treatment-related findings supports continued preclinical development of the formulation. In vivo simPET imaging in normal and fibrotic mouse models

Using the same batch of lyophilized NOTA-LSA kit that had undergone radiochemical stability and toxicity testing, we next applied [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA to a TAA-induced mouse fibrosis model to re-validate its functional performance in a second species (Figs. S11 and 6). In simPET imaging, normal mice showed rapid hepatic uptake with prominent liver activity by 0.5 h post injection, whereas TAA-treated mice showed lower hepatic uptake, with the clearest visual separation between groups observed at 0.5 h (Fig. S11A, B). Quantitative analysis confirmed that liver SUVmax at 0.5 h was significantly reduced in fibrotic mice compared with normal controls, whereas differences at 0 h, 1 h, and 2 h were not statistically significant (Fig. 6), indicating that 0.5 h was the most informative single time point for discriminating normal and diseased liver in this model. Although the optimal static imaging time point differed between rats and mice, both models showed the clearest group discrimination after partial blood-pool clearance rather than immediately after injection, underscoring the importance of species-specific imaging time-window optimization for functional assessment.

To further assess the biological relevance of this time point, we examined correlations between liver SUVmax and conventional tissue and serum biomarkers, including ASGR1, α-SMA, AST, and ALT (Table 10). Among all imaging time points, 0.5 h SUVmax showed the strongest and most consistent relationships, displaying a positive correlation with ASGR1 expression and negative correlations with α-SMA, AST, and ALT, whereas early (0 h) and later (1–2 h) measurements exhibited weaker or non-significant associations. The directionality of these correlations supports the interpretation that 0.5 h hepatic uptake serves as an imaging surrogate of preserved functional hepatocyte integrity [8, 9]. These findings further suggest that the 0.5 h PET signal reflects a favorable balance between ASGPR-mediated hepatocyte uptake and blood-pool clearance, thereby improving discrimination between preserved receptor-mediated hepatic uptake and fibrosis-associated functional loss [8, 9]. Although the sample size was limited and the study was not designed to define definitive cut-off values, the concordant trends across multiple independent biomarkers support the interpretation that [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA reflects fibrosis-associated hepatic functional impairment rather than nonspecific tracer distribution.

Discussion

Accurate assessment of HFR remains a major challenge in liver surgery because currently available clinical and functional tests do not fully capture the spatial and biological heterogeneity of diseased liver tissue. This limitation is clinically important because post-hepatectomy liver failure (PHLF) remains a major determinant of postoperative morbidity and mortality, and preoperative decision-making increasingly depends on reliable estimation of future remnant liver function rather than liver volume alone [1–6]. In the present study, we developed and optimized an ASGPR-targeted albumin-based PET platform, [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA, and demonstrated its feasibility for quantitative evaluation of hepatic dysfunction in TAA-induced liver fibrosis models. Compared with our previous proof-of-concept work [7], the current study extends the platform by systematically optimizing ligand density on the albumin scaffold, establishing a lyophilized one-step 68Ga-labeling kit, and validating PET-derived readouts against histological, molecular, and biochemical markers of liver injury.

A key finding of this study is that the degree of lactosylation critically influenced hepatic targeting performance. Comparative evaluation of LSA (7) and LSA (12) showed that increased lactose density enhanced liver uptake and improved discrimination from the blood pool, indicating that ligand density is a major determinant of ASGPR-mediated targeting efficiency. In the context of nanobiotechnology, this observation highlights the importance of multivalent ligand presentation, whereby optimized ligand density may enhance receptor binding avidity and promote receptor-mediated endocytosis [25, 26]. Furthermore, the use of human serum albumin as a biocompatible carrier provides a structurally defined scaffold for ligand presentation while preserving favorable systemic pharmacokinetics [27]. These findings underscore ligand-density tuning as an important design variable in ASGPR-targeted molecular imaging systems and help explain the more favorable in vivo performance of the optimized construct.

Another important result is that [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA enabled quantitative assessment of hepatic dysfunction at a single optimized imaging time point. In the rat fibrosis model, hepatic uptake and liver-to-heart ratios were markedly reduced in fibrotic liver and progressively restored in spontaneous recovery and pirfenidone-treated groups, indicating that PET signal intensity reflected functional impairment and recovery in parallel with histological and biochemical changes. Moreover, PET-derived parameters correlated with collagen deposition, α-SMA expression, ASGR1 expression, apoptosis-related markers, and serum liver injury markers. These correlations support the interpretation that [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA uptake reflects preservation of functional hepatocyte mass and receptor availability rather than nonspecific tracer accumulation alone. From a translational perspective, this is relevant because preoperative planning should ideally incorporate both the quantity and quality of the future remnant liver rather than rely on volumetry alone [1, 4, 5, 10].

Although ICG clearance is widely used for clinical assessment of hepatic functional reserve, it provides a global clearance-based estimate and does not directly depict the spatial distribution of liver function. In addition, ICG-derived indices may be influenced by hepatic blood flow, biliary excretion, cholestasis, and systemic hemodynamic factors, which can complicate the interpretation of intrinsic hepatocellular function. In contrast, [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA PET provides tomographic, region-based quantitative information on hepatic tracer uptake, allowing assessment of functional heterogeneity within the liver and potentially enabling more direct evaluation of regional hepatic reserve. However, because ICG clearance was not directly measured in the present animal models, the current study does not establish direct superiority over ICG-based assessment. Rather, our findings suggest that [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA PET may complement conventional global liver function tests by providing spatially resolved and quantitatively reproducible information on ASGPR-associated hepatic function.

The present data also highlight an important distinction between [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA PET and conventional hepatobiliary scintigraphy. 99mTc-mebrofenin is a clinically established tracer that has shown utility for estimating future remnant liver function and predicting postoperative risk, and hepatobiliary scintigraphy remains one of the best-studied imaging-based functional tests in this setting [4, 5, 11–13, 22, 23]. However, the interpretation of mebrofenin imaging depends heavily on dynamic hepatobiliary uptake and excretion kinetics and often requires serial acquisition and relatively complex analysis [4, 11–15, 22, 23]. Recent clinical studies continue to support the value of mebrofenin scintigraphy or dynamic SPECT/CT in selecting patients at risk of clinically relevant PHLF, but they also reinforce the workflow complexity inherent to transport-based imaging approaches [12–15, 22, 23]. In our study, 99mTc-mebrofenin differentiated diseased from normal animals primarily through delayed excretion patterns, whereas [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA provided a single-time-point uptake-based quantitative readout of hepatic function. This difference likely reflects the distinct biology interrogated by the two tracers: mebrofenin reflects hepatobiliary transport and excretion, whereas [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA reflects ASGPR-mediated hepatocyte recognition and uptake.

Although HSA-based tracers can also be labeled with ⁹⁹ᵐTc for SPECT imaging [28], ⁶⁸Ga was selected in the present study to establish a PET-based quantitative platform for HFR assessment. Compared with SPECT, PET generally provides higher spatial resolution, greater sensitivity, and more robust quantitative capability, which are important for region-based assessment of hepatic functional reserve. In addition, ⁶⁸Ga is compatible with NOTA-mediated generator-based radiolabeling and enables rapid kit-based preparation without the need for an on-site cyclotron. Although the relatively short physical half-life of ⁶⁸Ga may limit delayed imaging or long-distance distribution, it is well matched to the rapid one-step labeling procedure and the early hepatic uptake kinetics of [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA, supporting practical same-day imaging within a clinically feasible 30–60 min acquisition window.

This ASGPR-centered mechanism is particularly relevant in chronic liver injury. ASGPR expression is largely restricted to hepatocytes and has long been considered a marker of differentiated hepatocyte function [8, 9]. Reduced [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA accumulation in fibrotic liver is therefore consistent with diminished functional hepatocyte mass and reduced receptor-mediated endocytic capacity. In the present study, reduced hepatic uptake of [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA was accompanied by decreased ASGR1 expression in fibrotic liver, and correlation analysis demonstrated positive associations between PET-derived hepatic uptake parameters and ASGR1 expression. However, because hepatic PET uptake may also be influenced by functional hepatocyte mass, perfusion, and tracer delivery, the PET signal should be interpreted as an integrated imaging readout of ASGPR-associated hepatic function rather than as a direct one-to-one measurement of receptor density. The concordance between PET-derived parameters, ASGR1 expression, fibrosis burden, and biochemical markers further supports this interpretation. In this regard, [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA may serve as a receptor-based molecular imaging biomarker of hepatic reserve rather than a purely transport-based tracer.

The biodistribution findings also require consideration in relation to potential off-target uptake. In the tracer optimization experiment, non-lactosylated HSA showed comparable liver and blood-pool activity, whereas increasing lactose density resulted in higher liver-to-heart ratios and reduced blood retention. This pattern suggests that hepatic accumulation of [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA was primarily driven by lactose-mediated hepatocyte targeting rather than nonspecific albumin distribution alone. In the advanced fibrosis model, hepatic uptake decreased toward blood-pool levels, which is consistent with impaired functional hepatocyte mass and reduced ASGPR-associated uptake capacity rather than increased off-target accumulation as the dominant mechanism. Nevertheless, because whole-body ex vivo biodistribution, receptor-blocking experiments, and dosimetry analysis were not performed in the present study, minor off-target uptake in extrahepatic organs cannot be completely excluded. Future studies incorporating whole-body biodistribution analysis and receptor-blocking or competitive inhibition experiments will be required to more rigorously define the off-target profile of [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA.

The translational features of this platform are also noteworthy. In addition to solution-based radiolabeling, we established a lyophilized NOTA-LSA kit that enabled rapid one-step 68Ga labeling with high radiochemical efficiency and favorable serum stability. The lyophilized kit maintained favorable radiolabeling performance during the evaluated storage period, supporting the feasibility of kit-based preparation. Nevertheless, further studies will be required to evaluate long-term kit stability under different storage temperatures and humidity conditions, as well as batch-to-batch reproducibility under GMP-compatible manufacturing conditions. These additional evaluations will be important for defining shelf life, transport conditions, and quality-control specifications for future clinical translation. The clinical translation of targeted nanomaterials is often hindered by synthetic and formulation complexity [29]. In this context, the retained labeling performance of the lyophilized kit during storage supports a simplified and standardized workflow that may reduce operator-dependent variability and facilitate future multi-center evaluations. Such kit-based preparation is an important advantage for broader clinical deployment because it lowers procedural complexity and may improve reproducibility across sites. Furthermore, the favorable acute tolerability observed in the single-dose toxicity study provides preliminary support for continued translational development.

Cross-species validation in a fibrotic mouse model further strengthened the robustness of the imaging mechanism. Reduced hepatic uptake was reproduced across species, supporting the generalizability of ASGPR-targeted PET readouts in fibrotic liver. This consistency suggests that the platform captures a shared biological feature of chronic liver injury rather than a species-specific biodistribution artifact.

From a clinical translation perspective, determination of an appropriate imaging time point will be important for standardized quantitative assessment. In the present study, hepatic uptake was clearly visualized within 30–60 min after injection across rodent models, suggesting that [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA enables practical imaging within a clinically feasible time window compatible with the physical half-life of ⁶⁸Ga. Importantly, quantitative discrimination between normal and fibrotic liver was achieved using a single optimized acquisition time point, supporting the potential clinical utility of a simplified PET workflow. The difference in the most informative imaging time point between rats and mice likely reflects species-related differences in tracer kinetics, body size, blood-pool clearance, and hepatic physiology. Therefore, the 30–60 min window should be regarded as a practical candidate range for initial clinical protocol optimization rather than as a fixed human imaging time point. Further first-in-human or pilot clinical studies using serial or dual-time-point PET acquisition will be required to determine the optimal imaging protocol and quantitative parameters in humans.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, all imaging experiments were performed in preclinical fibrosis models, and the clinical performance of [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA in human liver disease remains to be determined. In addition, direct head-to-head benchmarking against established clinical standards, including ICG clearance, MELD-based clinical indices, ⁹⁹ᵐTc-mebrofenin kinetic parameters, and functional liver volume measurements, was not performed. Therefore, the current findings should be interpreted as supporting the feasibility of [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA PET as a complementary PET-based quantitative platform for HFR assessment rather than establishing its superiority over existing methods. Furthermore, although the lactose-density dependence, hepatic biodistribution pattern, and correlations with ASGR1 expression support ASGPR-associated hepatocyte uptake, receptor-blocking studies, competitive inhibition experiments, and ASGPR-deficient models were not performed. Therefore, receptor-specific uptake cannot be considered conclusively proven, and future mechanistic studies will be required to distinguish ASGPR-mediated uptake from nonspecific albumin distribution, altered hepatic perfusion, and fibrosis-associated changes. Second, although the TAA model reproduces key structural and functional features of chronic liver injury, it does not reflect the full etiologic and metabolic diversity of human liver disease, including metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis/nonalcoholic steatohepatitis, viral hepatitis, and alcohol-associated liver disease. Third, the present study was designed to evaluate imaging performance and biological correlation rather than to directly predict surgical outcomes such as PHLF. Future studies using liver resection models and prospective clinical cohorts will be required to determine whether PET-derived HFR parameters can predict PHLF risk and improve preoperative surgical decision-making. Finally, although hepatic uptake alone showed strong discriminatory capability, additional studies will be needed to determine the most robust quantitative parameter for clinical implementation and to assess inter-center reproducibility. In addition, although serial static imaging at 30, 60, and 120 min supported the selection of 60 min as a practical optimized imaging time point in rats, full dynamic PET acquisition, time–activity curve analysis, and compartmental kinetic modeling were not performed. Moreover, some imaging experiments, including tracer optimization and mouse validation studies, were performed with relatively small animal cohorts, and formal a priori power calculations were not conducted. Therefore, the present results should be interpreted as exploratory preclinical feasibility data, and larger adequately powered studies will be needed to validate diagnostic performance, define robust cut-off values, and support clinical translation. Finally, the toxicity evaluation was limited to a single-dose acute toxicity study of non-radioactive NOTA-LSA, and repeated-dose toxicity, immunogenicity, pharmacokinetics, radiation dosimetry, and regulatory-grade safety assessments were not performed. These studies will be required before clinical translation of [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA. In addition, partial-volume correction was not applied, and future studies using standardized segmentation protocols and multicenter reproducibility analyses will be needed to further improve quantitative robustness.

Despite these limitations, the present study provides several advances over previous work. It identifies ligand-density optimization as a critical determinant of ASGPR-targeted albumin imaging performance, demonstrates that [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA PET enables practical single-time-point quantitative assessment of hepatic function, and shows that the tracer can be prepared in a clinically relevant lyophilized kit format with favorable radiochemical and preliminary safety profiles. Taken together, these results support [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA as a promising molecular imaging platform for quantitative HFR assessment and justify further translational and clinical investigation.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed and optimized [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA as an ASGPR-targeted, albumin-based PET platform for quantitative evaluation of HFR. The optimized construct showed reproducible synthesis, favorable radiochemical stability, and successful lyophilized kit formulation, enabling rapid one-step radiolabeling with translational practicality. In TAA-induced liver fibrosis models, [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA enabled single-time-point quantitative assessment of hepatic function, whereas comparison with ⁹⁹ᵐTc-mebrofenin SPECT highlighted mechanistic and workflow differences between receptor-associated PET uptake imaging and transport-based hepatobiliary scintigraphy. Importantly, hepatic uptake alone showed strong potential as a simplified and practical imaging parameter for HFR evaluation. Collectively, these findings support [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-LSA PET as a reproducible and quantitative molecular imaging platform for HFR assessment with translational potential.
