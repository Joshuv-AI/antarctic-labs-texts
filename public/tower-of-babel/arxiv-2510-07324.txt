Geodesics in the Deep Linear Network
Alan Chen

Abstract
. We derive a general system of ODEs and associated explicit solutions in a special case for geodesics between full rank matrices in the deep linear network geometry. In the process, we characterize all horizontal straight lines in the invariant balanced manifold that remain geodesics under Riemannian submersion.



1.
Introduction

Let
\mathbb{M}_{d}
,
\mathfrak{M}_{d}
,
\Symm_{d}
,
\mathbb{P}_{d}
, and
O_{d}
denote the sets of all, invertible, symmetric, positive definite, and orthogonal
d\times d
matrices respectively.

1.1.
The Deep Linear Network.

The deep linear network (DLN) is a phenomenological model of the training dynamics of neural networks
[
1
,
2
,
6
,
9
]
. Given a depth
N
and width
d
, in its simplest form the DLN consists of the “upstairs” (high-dimensional) parameter space of
N

d\times d
matrices
\mathbb{M}_{d}^{N}
and a “downstairs” (low-dimensional) observable space
\mathbb{M}_{d}
, linked by the product map
\phi:\mathbb{M}_{d}^{N}\to\mathbb{M}_{d}
that sends

(1.1)

\mathbf{W}=(W_{N},\ldots,W_{1})\mapsto X=W_{N}W_{N-1}\cdots W_{1}.

Indeed,
\mathbb{M}_{d}^{N}
has
Nd^{2}
degrees of freedom while
\mathbb{M}_{d}
only has
d^{2}
, modeling overparameterization without the complications of nonlinearities and bias terms in traditional neural networks. The DLN is used to explicitly analyze the interplay of overparameterization with gradient descent of an energy function
E:\mathbb{M}_{d}\to\mathbb{R}
. The gradient flow in parameter space

(1.2)

\dot{\mathbf{W}}=-\nabla_{\mathbf{W}}E(X)=-\nabla_{\mathbf{W}}E(\phi(\mathbf{W}))

can be written out explicitly via the chain rule as

(1.3)

\dot{W}_{p}=-(W_{N}\dots W_{p+1})^{T}dE(X)(W_{p-1}\dots W_{1})^{T}

for all
1\leq p\leq N
, where
dE(X)\in\mathbb{M}_{d}
is given elementwise by
dE(X)_{ij}=\frac{\partial E}{\partial X_{ij}}
.

1.2.
Balancedness and Riemannian Geometry

Interestingly, the DLN gradient flow (Equation
1.2
) has invariant manifolds with associated metrics that illustrate the geometric structure underlying the DLN. One such invariant manifold is defined by the constraints

(1.4)

W_{p+1}^{T}W_{p+1}=W_{p}W_{p}^{T}

for all
1\leq p\leq N-1

[
2
, Theorem 1]
. We further restrict to when all coordinates are rank
d
and denote this manifold
\mathcal{M}
, the balanced manifold. When
\mathbf{W}\in\mathcal{M}
,
X=\phi(\mathbf{W})\in\mathfrak{M}_{d}
.

It is not hard to show that in
\mathcal{M}
,
W_{p}
have the same singular values for all
1\leq p\leq N
and the right singular vectors of
W_{p+1}
align with the left singular vectors of
W_{p}
for
1\leq p\leq N-1
. Thus, given the SVD of a matrix
X=Q_{N}\Sigma Q_{0}
, we can parameterize any
\mathbf{W}\in\mathcal{M}
such that
\phi(\mathbf{W})=X
using just
(\Lambda,Q_{N},Q_{N-1},\ldots,Q_{0})
, where
\Lambda=\Sigma^{\frac{1}{N}}
and
W_{p}=Q_{p}\Lambda Q_{p-1}^{T}
are obtained by the SVD for all
1\leq p\leq N
. Therefore, there exists a bijective map

(1.5)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\xi:\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}\times O_{d}^{N+1}&\to\mathbb{M}_{d}^{N},\\
(\Lambda,Q_{N},Q_{N-1},\ldots,Q_{0})&\mapsto(W_{N},W_{N-1},\ldots,W_{1}).\end{split}

Because
\mathcal{M}
is embedded within the flat
(\mathbb{M}_{d}^{N},F)
where
F
is the Euclidean metric, we can induce the natural metric
\iota
on
\mathcal{M}
from
F
. Downstairs, we can define a metric
g^{N}
on
\mathfrak{M}_{d}
as follows: for any
Z\in T_{X}\mathfrak{M}_{d}
,

(1.6)

\|Z\|_{g^{N}}^{2}=\Tr\left(Z^{T}\mathcal{A}_{N,X}^{-1}(Z)\right),

where
\mathcal{A}_{N,X}:T_{X}\mathfrak{M}_{d}^{*}\to T_{X}\mathfrak{M}_{d}
is the linear operator that maps from 1-forms to the tangent space with explicit form

(1.7)

Z\mapsto\sum_{p=1}^{N}\left(XX^{T}\right)^{\frac{N-p}{N}}Z\left(X^{T}X\right)^{\frac{p-1}{N}}.

g^{N}
is crucial in the DLN because it can be used to express the training dynamics of the end-to-end matrix
X
as a Riemannian gradient flow
[
3
, Section 4]
. Furthermore, these metrics in tandem provide a geometric perspective on the DLN model from
[
10
, Theorem 8]
and
[
11
, Theorem 36]
, which we elaborate on in Section
3.4.1
:

Theorem 1.1

(
\phi
is a Riemannian Submersion)
.

The end-to-end product map
\phi:\mathcal{M}\to\mathfrak{M}_{d}
is a Riemannian submersion from
(\mathcal{M},\iota)
to
(\mathfrak{M}_{d},g^{N})
.

[
5
, Section 4]
derive explicit formulas for geodesics in the Bures-Wasserstein geometry by identifying a Riemannian submersion structure from invertible matrices to the positive definite matrices. We present an alternative proof of the same geodesic formulas that also generalizes to the DLN, yielding the general ODEs for geodesic in
(\mathfrak{M}_{d},g^{N})
. Furthermore, Theorem
1.1
implies that the DLN also has a Riemannian submersion structure with
(\mathcal{M},\iota)
,
(\mathfrak{M}_{d},g^{N})
, and
\phi
, which we leverage to solve the general equations in a specific case.

2.
Main Results

Our main results are a statement of ODEs for general geodesics in
(\mathfrak{M}_{d},g^{N})
and explicit solutions when the singular vectors of the endpoints are related by a single matrix
Q\in O_{d}
. The solutions are obtained by characterizing horizontal straight lines in
\mathcal{M}
in a manner similar to the classical theory of confocal quadrics
[
7
]
.

Let
f_{\alpha}^{[1]}(\Lambda)
where
\Lambda=\diag(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{d})
denote the matrix

(2.1)

\left(f_{\alpha}^{[1]}(\Lambda)\right)_{ij}=\begin{cases}\frac{\lambda_{i}^{\alpha}-\lambda_{j}^{\alpha}}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}}&i\neq j,\\
\alpha\lambda_{i}^{\alpha-1}&i=j.\end{cases}

We discuss the origin of this matrix in Section
3.3
.

Theorem 2.1

(DLN Geodesic Equations)
.

X(t)=U(t)\Sigma(t)V(t)^{T}
is a geodesic between
A
and
B
on
(\mathfrak{M}_{d},g^{N})
if it satisfies the Dirichlet boundary value problem

(2.2)

\begin{cases}\dot{X}=\mathcal{A}_{N,X}(P),\\
\dot{P}=-U\left(\sum_{p=1}^{N}\left[f_{\frac{N-p}{N}}^{[1]}\left(\Sigma^{2}\right)\circ M_{1}\right]\Sigma+\Sigma\left[f_{\frac{p-1}{N}}^{[1]}\left(\Sigma^{2}\right)\circ M_{2}\right]\right)V^{T},\\
X(0)=A,\\
X(1)=B,\end{cases}

where
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
are the symmetric matrices

(2.3)

\displaystyle M_{1}

\displaystyle=(U^{T}PV)\Sigma^{\frac{2(p-1)}{N}}(U^{T}PV)^{T}

(2.4)

\displaystyle M_{2}

\displaystyle=(U^{T}PV)^{T}\Sigma^{\frac{2(N-p)}{N}}(U^{T}PV).

We discover these equations via direct computation in Hamiltonian coordinates (Section
3.3
). Using Riemannian submersion, we are then able to derive explicit solutions to these equations for particular endpoints. As an important intermediate result, we characterize the straight lines in
\mathcal{M}
.

Lemma 2.2

(Characterization of Straight Lines on
\mathcal{M}
)
.

Let
\mathbf{A}=\xi(\Lambda,Q_{N},\ldots,Q_{0})
and
\mathbf{B}=\xi(\tilde{\Lambda},\tilde{Q}_{N},\ldots,\tilde{Q}_{0})
be the endpoints of interest. Let

(2.5)

R_{p}=\tilde{Q}_{p}^{T}Q_{p}\in O_{d}.

Then, the line

(2.6)

\mathbf{W}(t)=(1-t)\mathbf{A}+t\mathbf{B}

lies in the balanced manifold for all
t\in[0,1]
:

(1)

if
R_{p+1}=R_{p-1}
for all
1\leq p\leq N-1
.

(2)

if and only if

(2.7)

(R_{p+1}-R_{p-1})M^{T}

are skew symmetric, where

(2.8)

M=\tilde{\Lambda}^{-1}R_{p}\Lambda

for all
1\leq p\leq N-1
.

We are now ready to state the second main result. Let
A=U\Sigma V^{T}
and
B=\tilde{U}\tilde{\Sigma}\tilde{V}^{T}
be the endpoints in
\mathfrak{M}_{d}
of the geodesic.

Theorem 2.3

(Explicit Formulas for DLN Geodesics)
.

If there exists
Q\in O_{d}
such that
\tilde{U}=UQ
and
\tilde{V}=VQ
,

(1)

there exists
\mathbf{A}\in\phi^{-1}(A)
and
\mathbf{B}\in\phi^{-1}(B)
such that
x(t)=(1-t)\mathbf{A}+t\mathbf{B}
is a geodesic in
(\mathcal{M},\iota)
.

(2)

the geodesic in
(\mathfrak{M}_{d},g^{N})
between
A
and
B
is given by

(2.9)

\begin{split}X(t)=&\left((1-t)U\Sigma^{\frac{1}{N}}+t\tilde{U}\tilde{\Sigma}^{\frac{1}{N}}Q^{T}\right)\\
&\quad\left((1-t)\Sigma^{\frac{1}{N}}+tQ\tilde{\Sigma}^{\frac{1}{N}}Q^{T}\right)^{N-2}\\
&\quad\left((1-t)\Sigma^{\frac{1}{N}}V^{T}+tQ\tilde{\Sigma}^{\frac{1}{N}}\tilde{V}^{T}\right).\end{split}

Remark 2.4
.

Examples of when
A
and
B
satisfy this right-left rotation condition is if they are both symmetric or if they have the same singular vectors (
Q=\text{Id}
).

Remark 2.5
.

When
A
and
B
are diagonal with positive entries (fractional powers are well defined), the geodesic simplifies to

(2.10)

\displaystyle\begin{split}X(t)&=\left((1-t)A^{1/N}+tB^{1/N}\right)^{N}\\
&=\sum_{p=0}^{N}\binom{N}{p}(1-t)^{p}t^{N-p}A^{p/N}B^{(N-p)/N}.\end{split}

This simplification can be shown by applying the theorem and expanding the geodesic formulas using that diagonal matrices commute. The limiting behavior as
N\to\infty
(recognizing
X(t)
is the expectation of
A^{S}B^{1-S}
where
S
is the average of
N
Bernoulli
(1-t)
random variables) is

(2.11)

X(t)=A^{1-t}B^{t}.

We discuss this striking simplification more in Appendix
A
.

The two important tools we will use to prove Theorem
2.3
in Section
3.4
are (1) the characterization of straight lines on
\mathcal{M}
(Lemma
2.2
) and (2) an orthonormal basis for the horizontal space in
\mathcal{M}
constructed by
[
10
,
11
, Theorem 34]
(Lemma
3.10
and see
[
9
]
for an exposition). Straight lines are automatically geodesics on
(\mathcal{M},\iota)
because
\iota
is induced from the Euclidean metric on
\mathbb{M}_{d}^{N}
. We will then send a subset of these straight lines to new geodesics in
(\mathfrak{M}_{d},g^{N})
to uncover the explicit formulas for geodesics as stated.

3.
Proofs

3.1.
Geodesics Review

Definition 3.1

(Geodesic)
.

A geodesic between
A,B\in\mathcal{M}
on
(\mathcal{M},g)
is an extrema of the action

(3.1)

\mathcal{A}[x]=\int_{0}^{1}g_{x(t)}(\dot{x}(t),\dot{x}(t))dt

over the set of paths

(3.2)

\mathcal{S}=\{x:[0,1]\to\mathcal{M}|x(0)=A,x(1)=B,x\in\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,1])\}.

The minimizing
x
is known to satisfy the Euler-Lagrange equations

(3.3)

\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L(x,\dot{x})}{\partial\dot{x}}=-\frac{\partial L(x,\dot{x})}{\partial x}.

The dual of the Lagrangian optimization problem is the Hamiltonian formulation that reveals an equivalent set of equations of motion. Given a Lagrangian
L(x,\dot{x})
, the Hamiltonian is the Legendre transform of
L(x,\cdot)

(3.4)

\displaystyle H(x,p)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\dot{x}}(\langle p,\dot{x}\rangle-L(x,\dot{x})).

It is not hard to see that the change of variables is
p=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{x}}
when
\dot{x}
is maximizing. The corresponding canonical equations to the Euler-Lagrange equations are

(3.5)

\displaystyle\dot{x}

\displaystyle=\partial_{p}H(x,p),

(3.6)

\displaystyle\dot{p}

\displaystyle=-\partial_{x}H(x,p).

3.2.
The Bures-Wasserstein Geometry

The Bures-Wasserstein geometry
[
5
]
is a special case of the DLN model when
N=2
and
W_{1}=W_{2}^{T}
. The parameter space becomes
\mathfrak{M}_{d}
whereas the observable space is
\mathbb{P}_{d}
. The map
\pi:\mathfrak{M}_{d}\to\mathbb{P}_{d}
is a Riemannian submersion that maps

W\in\mathfrak{M}_{d}\mapsto X=WW^{T}\in\mathbb{P}_{d}.

There are
d^{2}
degrees of freedom in parameter space compared to only
\binom{d+1}{2}
dimensions downstairs as
X
is symmetric. The downstairs metric, now denoted
g^{\text{BW}}
, at a point
X\in\mathbb{P}_{d}
reduces to a very simple form

(3.7)

g^{\text{BW}}(Z_{1},Z_{2})=\frac{1}{2}\Tr(Z_{1}^{T}\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{X}(Z_{2})),

where
\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{X}(Z)
is the Lyapunov operator that maps
Z
to the matrix that solves

(3.8)

\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{X}(Z)X+X\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{X}(Z)=Z.

It is also clear that
\mathcal{L}_{X}(Y)=XY+YX
. An important quantity in the Bures-Wasserstein geometry is the geometric mean of two matrices.

Definition 3.2

(Geometric Mean)
.

Given
A,B\in\mathbb{P}_{d}
, the geometric mean of
A
and
B
(denoted by
A\#B
) is

(3.9)

A\#B=A^{1/2}(A^{-1/2}BA^{-1/2})^{1/2}A^{1/2}.

Lemma 3.3

(Geometric Mean Properties)
.

We will use two key facts about the geometric mean, both of which can be proven directly from the definition.

(1)

A\#B
is the unique positive definite solution
M
to the Riccati Equation

(3.10)

B=MA^{-1}M.

(2)

A(A^{-1}\#B)=(AB)^{1/2}
and
(A^{-1}\#B)A=(BA)^{1/2}
.

As aforementioned, the Bures-Wasserstein geometry acts as a motivating example for our results. Specifically, we present a derivation of the explicit formulas for geodesics by solving the Hamiltonian equations of motion directly that we will generalize to the DLN (although we will not solve the equations explicitly).

Theorem 3.4

(Bures-Wasserstein Geodesics)
.

The geodesic path
X:[0,1]\to\mathbb{P}_{d}
between
A,B\in\mathbb{P}_{d}
in
(\mathbb{P}_{d},g^{\text{BW}})
satisfies the boundary value problem

(3.11)

\begin{cases}\dot{X}=\mathcal{L}_{X}(P),\\
\dot{P}=-P^{2},\\
X(0)=A,\\
X(1)=B,\end{cases}

and has explicit form given by

(3.12)

X(t)=(1-t)^{2}A+t^{2}B+t(1-t)\left((AB)^{1/2}+(BA)^{1/2}\right).

Proof of Theorem
3.4
.

To begin, recall that
T_{X}\mathbb{P}_{d}\cong\text{Symm}_{d}
and denote
Y=\dot{X}
. In order to construct the change of variable from Lagrangian into Hamiltonian coordinates, we must first compute
D_{Y}[L(X,Y)](H)
where
H\in T_{X}\mathbb{P}_{d}
.

\displaystyle D_{Y}\left[\frac{1}{2}\Tr(Y^{T}\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{X}(Y))\right](H)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Tr(D_{Y}[Y^{T}\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{X}(Y)](H))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Tr(H^{T}\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{X}(Y)+Y^{T}\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{X}(H))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(g_{X}(H,Y)+g_{X}(Y,H))

(3.13)

\displaystyle=\Tr(\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{X}(Y)H)

where the second to last equality holds because
H
and
Y
are in
T_{X}\mathbb{P}_{d}
and the last equality follows from the cyclic property of trace. Thus, we obtain that
P=\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{X}(Y)
and that
Y=\mathcal{L}_{X}(P)
. Now, we can compute the Hamiltonian using the cyclic property of trace and the definition of
P
and
\mathcal{L}_{X}
as

\displaystyle H(X,P)

\displaystyle=\Tr(P^{T}\mathcal{L}_{X}(P))-\frac{1}{2}\Tr(\mathcal{L}_{X}(P)\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{X}(\mathcal{L}_{X}(P)))

(3.14)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Tr(P\mathcal{L}_{X}(P))=\Tr(P^{2}X).

The system of differential equations is now clear from the form of
H(X,P)
. We can solve them to find exact formulas for
X
and
P
. In particular,
\dot{P}=-P^{2}
implies

\displaystyle-P^{-1}\dot{P}P^{-1}

\displaystyle=I

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}(P^{-1})

\displaystyle=I

(3.15)

\displaystyle P^{-1}(t)

\displaystyle=tI+P^{-1}_{0}.

Thus,
P(t)=(tI+P^{-1}_{0})^{-1}
. Factoring out
P^{-1}_{0}
from the interior term reveals that
P(t)
is a time derivative:

\displaystyle P(t)

\displaystyle=(P^{-1}_{0}(tP_{0}+I))^{-1}

(3.16)

\displaystyle=(tP_{0}+I)^{-1}P_{0}

(3.17)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}\log(tP_{0}+I).

The first differential equation can be expanded as

(3.18)

\displaystyle\dot{X}=X(t)P(t)+P(t)X(t).

Motivated by the similarity of this equation to the Lax equation, we make the ansatz for the solution form of
X
as

(3.19)

X(t)=e^{C(t)}X_{0}e^{D(t)},

which after plugging in and matching coefficients gives that
\dot{C}(t)=\dot{D}(t)=P(t)
and implying that

(3.20)

\displaystyle C(t)=D(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}P(s)ds

(3.21)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{d}{ds}\log(sP_{0}+I)ds

(3.22)

\displaystyle=\log(tP_{0}+I).

Substituting
C
and
D
back into the ansatz gives that

\displaystyle X(t)

\displaystyle=\exp\{\log(tP_{0}+I)\}X_{0}\exp\{\log(tP_{0}+I)\}

\displaystyle=(tP_{0}+I)X_{0}(tP_{0}+I)

(3.23)

\displaystyle=(tP_{0}+I)A(tP_{0}+I).

Applying the final unused condition that
X_{1}=B
helps find
P_{0}
:

(3.24)

B=(P_{0}+I)A(P_{0}+I),

which from Lemma
3.3
implies that

(3.25)

P_{0}=(A^{-1}\#B)-I.

Finally, substituting
P_{0}
into our solution formula for
X(t)
, we conclude that

(3.26)

\displaystyle X(t)

\displaystyle=(t((A^{-1}\#B)-I)+I)A(t((A^{-1}\#B)-I)+I)

(3.27)

\displaystyle=(t(A^{-1}\#B)+(1-t)I)A(t(A^{-1}\#B)+(1-t)I)

(3.28)

\displaystyle=(1-t)^{2}A+t^{2}B+t(1-t)((A^{-1}\#B)A+A(A^{-1}\#B))

(3.29)

\displaystyle=(1-t)^{2}A+t^{2}B+t(1-t)\left((AB)^{1/2}+(BA)^{1/2}\right).

∎

3.3.
General Equations from Hamiltonian

We will now generalize the preceding calculation to the DLN. First, recall functional calculus on symmetric matrices
[
4
, Chapter 2]
. If
S=Q\Lambda Q^{T}
is a symmetric matrix and
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a 1D functional,
f
can be extended to the symmetric matrices as (abusing notation)

(3.30)

f(S)=Qf(\Lambda)Q^{T}=Q\text{diag}(f(\lambda_{1}),f(\lambda_{2}),\dots,f(\lambda_{d}))Q^{T}.

Then, the differential of
f(S)
at
S
evaluated in the direction
Z
is exactly

(3.31)

Df[S](Z)=Q\left(f^{[1]}(\Lambda)\circ\left[Q^{T}ZQ\right]\right)Q^{T}

where
f^{[1]}
is the special matrix given elementwise by the conditional

(3.32)

f^{[1]}(\Lambda)_{ij}=\begin{cases}\frac{f(\lambda_{i})-f(\lambda_{j})}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}}&i\neq j\\
\lim_{\lambda_{i}\to\lambda_{j}}\frac{f(\lambda_{i})-f(\lambda_{j})}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}}&i=j.\end{cases}

f^{[1]}_{\alpha}
(Equation
2.1
) now clearly comes from Equation
3.32
if
f(x)=x^{\alpha}
. We also have the following derivative computation.

Lemma 3.5

(Derivatives of
(XX^{T})^{\alpha}
)
.

Let
X=U\Sigma V^{T}
be the singular value decomposition of
X
. Then,

(3.33)

\displaystyle D_{X}\left[\left(XX^{T}\right)^{\alpha}\right](Z)

\displaystyle=U\left[f_{\alpha}^{[1]}\left(\Sigma^{2}\right)\circ\left(\Sigma\tilde{Z}^{T}+\tilde{Z}\Sigma\right)\right]U^{T}.

(3.34)

\displaystyle D_{X}\left[\left(X^{T}X\right)^{\alpha}\right](Z)

\displaystyle=V\left[f_{\alpha}^{[1]}\left(\Sigma^{2}\right)\circ\left(\Sigma\tilde{Z}+\tilde{Z}^{T}\Sigma\right)\right]V^{T}.

where
\tilde{Z}=U^{T}ZV
.

Proof.

We write out the proof for the first result - the second is similar. Notice that
XX^{T}
is a symmetric matrix with diagonalization
U\Sigma^{2}U^{T}
. Thus, we apply functional calculus along with the chain rule to find that

(3.35)

D_{X}\left[\left(XX^{T}\right)^{\alpha}\right](Z)=U\left[f_{\alpha}^{[1]}\left(\Sigma^{2}\right)\circ\left(U^{T}\left(D_{X}\left[XX^{T}\right](Z)\right)U\right)\right]U^{T}.

Clearly, we know that

(3.36)

D_{X}\left[XX^{T}\right](Z)=XZ^{T}+ZX^{T}.

Rewriting
Z
as
\tilde{Z}
in the singular vector coordinates via the transformation
Z=U\tilde{Z}V^{T}
nets the final simplification that gives the form in the Lemma statement.
∎

We also prepare another intermediate result that will be useful for algebraic manipulation.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
K
and
M
be symmetric matrices,
\Sigma
be a diagonal matrix, and
A
be an arbitrary matrix. Then, we have the following identities.

(3.37)

\displaystyle\Tr([K\circ(\Sigma A^{T})]M)

\displaystyle=\Tr([K\circ(A\Sigma)]M)=\Tr(\Sigma[K\circ M]A),

(3.38)

\displaystyle\Tr([K\circ(\Sigma A)]M)

\displaystyle=\Tr([K\circ(A^{T}\Sigma)]M)=\Tr([K\circ M]\Sigma A).

Proof.

Again, we will just prove for the first equation and note the second follows from almost identical steps. Let’s first prove that the first expression is equal to the last. We will approach by expanding into coordinates, following Einstein notation convention where repeated indices are summed.

\displaystyle\Tr([K\circ(\Sigma A^{T})]M)

\displaystyle=K_{ij}\sigma_{i}(A^{T})_{ij}M_{ji}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{i}K_{ij}M_{ji}A_{ji}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{i}K_{ij}M_{ij}A_{ji}

(3.39)

\displaystyle=\Tr(\Sigma[K\circ M]A).

Similarly, we can prove the equality of the second expression and the third expression using the symmetry of
K
.

\displaystyle\Tr([K\circ(A\Sigma)]M)

\displaystyle=K_{ij}A_{ij}\sigma_{j}M_{ji}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{j}K_{ij}M_{ji}A_{ij}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{j}K_{ji}M_{ji}A_{ij}

(3.40)

\displaystyle=\Tr(\Sigma[K\circ M]A).

∎

Following similar steps to the Bures Wasserstein case, we can find the Hamiltonian on the manifold
(\mathfrak{M}_{d},g^{N})
to be

(3.41)

H(X,P)=\frac{1}{2}\Tr\left(P^{T}\mathcal{A}_{N,X}(P)\right).

Using these coordinates, we will now obtain the general equations on the manifold
(\mathfrak{M}_{d},g^{N})
through direct computation, proving Theorem
2.1
.

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

This first differential equation follows from exactly the same computation as the Bures-Wasserstein case (Equation
3.2
) but with
\mathcal{L}^{-1}
replaced by
\mathcal{A}^{-1}_{N,X}
. The only nontrivial equation is the second equation and the associated computation of
D_{X}[H(X,P)](Z)
. We begin by applying Lemma
3.5
.

(3.42)

\displaystyle D_{X}[H(X,P)](Z)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{p=1}^{N}\Tr\left(T_{U}+T_{V}\right),

(3.43)

\displaystyle T_{U}=P^{T}U\left[f_{\frac{N-p}{N}}^{[1]}\left(\Sigma^{2}\right)\circ\left(\Sigma\tilde{Z}^{T}+\tilde{Z}\Sigma\right)\right]U^{T}PV\Sigma^{\frac{2(p-1)}{N}}V^{T},

(3.44)

\displaystyle T_{V}=P^{T}U\Sigma^{\frac{2(N-p)}{N}}U^{T}PV\left[f_{\frac{p-1}{N}}^{[1]}\left(\Sigma^{2}\right)\circ\left(\Sigma\tilde{Z}+\tilde{Z}^{T}\Sigma\right)\right]V^{T}.

To clean up further, we also write
P
in terms of the singular value coordinates by setting
\tilde{P}=U^{T}PV
, leaving just

(3.45)

\displaystyle T_{U}

\displaystyle=\tilde{P}^{T}\left[f_{\frac{N-p}{N}}^{[1]}\left(\Sigma^{2}\right)\circ\left(\Sigma\tilde{Z}^{T}+\tilde{Z}\Sigma\right)\right]\tilde{P}\Sigma^{\frac{2(p-1)}{N}}

(3.46)

\displaystyle T_{V}

\displaystyle=\tilde{P}^{T}\Sigma^{\frac{2(N-p)}{N}}\tilde{P}\left[f_{\frac{p-1}{N}}^{[1]}\left(\Sigma^{2}\right)\circ\left(\Sigma\tilde{Z}+\tilde{Z}^{T}\Sigma\right)\right].

Applying the trace identities from Lemma
3.6
to Equations
3.45
and
3.46
, where we note
f^{[1]}_{\alpha}(\Sigma^{2})
is symmetric, and substituting in
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
from Equation
2.3
reveal

(3.47)

\displaystyle D_{X}[H(X,P)](Z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{p=1}^{N}\Tr\left(2\Sigma\left[f_{\frac{N-p}{N}}^{[1]}\left(\Sigma^{2}\right)\circ M_{1}\right]\tilde{Z}+2\left[f_{\frac{p-1}{N}}^{[1]}\left(\Sigma^{2}\right)\circ M_{2}\right]\Sigma\tilde{Z}\right)

(3.48)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=1}^{N}\Tr\left(\left(\Sigma\left[f_{\frac{N-p}{N}}^{[1]}\left(\Sigma^{2}\right)\circ M_{1}\right]+\left[f_{\frac{p-1}{N}}^{[1]}\left(\Sigma^{2}\right)\circ M_{2}\right]\Sigma\right)\tilde{Z}\right)

(3.49)

\displaystyle=\Tr\left(V\sum_{p=1}^{N}\left(\Sigma\left[f_{\frac{N-p}{N}}^{[1]}\left(\Sigma^{2}\right)\circ M_{1}\right]+\left[f_{\frac{p-1}{N}}^{[1]}\left(\Sigma^{2}\right)\circ M_{2}\right]\Sigma\right)U^{T}Z\right)

This lets us conclude that the second canonical equation in the Hamiltonian formulation is

(3.50)

\dot{P}=-U\sum_{p=1}^{N}\left(\left[f_{\frac{N-p}{N}}^{[1]}\left(\Sigma^{2}\right)\circ M_{1}\right]\Sigma+\Sigma\left[f_{\frac{p-1}{N}}^{[1]}\left(\Sigma^{2}\right)\circ M_{2}\right]\right)V^{T}.

∎

However, unlike the Bures-Wasserstein case (Theorem
3.4
), we find that this final form is not particularly amenable to direct solution, despite the commutator structure and relation to the singular vector coordinates
U
and
V
. Thus, we turn to special cases, where we will be able to find explicit formulas by leveraging the inherent geometric structure of the DLN model.

3.4.
Explicit Solutions from Submersion

3.4.1.
Riemannian Geometry Background

Recall the following important definitions and results from Riemannian geometry
[
5
]
.

Definition 3.7

(Vertical and Horizontal Spaces)
.

Let
(\mathcal{M},g)
and (
\mathcal{N},h)
be linked by a differentiable map
\pi:\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{N}
. Then, there exists a direct sum decomposition of the tangent space at any point
m\in\mathcal{M}
via the differential of
\phi
.

(3.51)

T_{m}\mathcal{M}=\ker D\pi[m]\oplus(\ker D\pi[m])^{\perp}=\mathcal{V}_{m}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{m},

where
\mathcal{V}_{m}
and
\mathcal{H}_{m}
denote the vertical and horizontal spaces at
m
respectively.

Definition 3.8

(Riemannian Submersion)
.

Let
(\mathcal{M},g)
and
(\mathcal{N},h)
be two Riemannian manifolds and let
T_{m}\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{V}_{m}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{m}
be the direct sum decomposition of the tangent space at
m\in\mathcal{M}
. A mapping
\pi:\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{N}
is a Riemannian submersion if and only if the differential
D\pi[m]:T_{m}\mathcal{M}\to T_{\pi(m)}\mathcal{N}
satisfies the following for all
m\in\mathcal{M}
:

(1)

D\pi[m]
is surjective (
\pi
is a smooth submersion).

(2)

D\pi[m]
restricted to the horizontal space is an isometry i.e.
\|z\|_{g}=\|D\pi[m](z)\|_{h}
for all
z\in\mathcal{H}_{m}
.

A core fact underlying
[
5
, Section 4]
and Theorem
2.3
is that understanding the horizontal space of a submersion allows us to identify geodesics that are preserved under the submersion.

Theorem 3.9

(Submersions Preserve Horizontal Geodesics)
.

Let
(\mathcal{M},g)
and
(\mathcal{N},h)
be Riemannian manifolds and
\phi:\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{N}
be a Riemannian submersion. Let
x:[0,1]\to\mathcal{M}
be a geodesic between
A,B\in\mathcal{M}
in the geometry
(\mathcal{M},g)
so that
x^{\prime}(0)\in\mathcal{H}_{A}
i.e. is horizontal. Then,

(1)

x^{\prime}(t)
is horizontal for all
t\in[0,1]
.

(2)

\phi\circ x
is a geodesic in
(\mathcal{N},h)
between
\phi(A)
and
\phi(B)
and is the same length as
x
.

Thus, if we are able to classify horizontal geodesics in
(\mathcal{M},\iota)
, Theorem
3.9
will automatically give explicit formulas for geodesics in
(\mathfrak{M}_{d},g^{N})
through
\phi
, the submersion. The simplest class of geodesics in
(\mathcal{M},\iota)
are straight lines. Thus, we begin with a proof of Lemma
2.2
that characterizes the straight lines in
\mathcal{M}
.

Proof of Lemma
2.2
.

We wish to find the condition that
\mathbf{W}(t)=(1-t)\mathbf{A}+t\mathbf{B}
is in
\mathcal{M}
for all
t\in(0,1)
if
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\in\mathcal{M}
. Fix
p\in\{1,2,\ldots,N-1\}
. We would like to show that

(3.52)

W_{p+1}(t)^{T}W_{p+1}(t)=W_{p}(t)W_{p}(t)^{T},

where
W_{p}(t)=(1-t)W_{p}(0)+tW_{p}(1)
for all
p
. Expanding the algebra out and using that
W_{p}(0)=A_{p}
and
W_{p}(1)=B_{p}
are both in the balanced manifold, we find that
\mathbf{W}(t)\in\mathcal{M}
for all
t\in(0,1)
if and only if

(3.53)

B_{p+1}^{T}A_{p+1}+A_{p+1}^{T}B_{p+1}=B_{p}A_{p}^{T}+A_{p}B_{p}^{T}.

Now, we use the parameterizations of
A
and
B
to expand into the orthogonal matrix components.

(3.54)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\tilde{Q}_{p}\tilde{\Lambda}(\tilde{Q}_{p+1}^{T}Q_{p+1})\Lambda Q_{p}^{T}&+Q_{p}\Lambda(Q_{p+1}^{T}\tilde{Q}_{p+1})\tilde{\Lambda}\tilde{Q}_{p}^{T}\\
&=\tilde{Q}_{p}\tilde{\Lambda}(\tilde{Q}_{p-1}^{T}Q_{p-1})\Lambda Q_{p}^{T}+Q_{p}\Lambda(Q_{p-1}^{T}\tilde{Q}_{p-1})\tilde{\Lambda}\tilde{Q}_{p}^{T}.\end{split}

Rewriting using
R_{p}
notation and left and right multiplying by
\tilde{Q}_{p}^{T}
and
Q_{p}
respectively gives

(3.55)

\displaystyle\tilde{\Lambda}(R_{p+1}-R_{p-1})\Lambda

\displaystyle=-R_{p}\Lambda(R_{p+1}-R_{p-1})^{T}\tilde{\Lambda}R_{p}.

The sufficient condition (
R_{p+1}=R_{p-1}
for all
1\leq p\leq N-1
) clearly results in this condition being satisfied for all
t\in(0,1)
. From here, it is a simple rewriting in terms of
M
to get the final equivalent condition:

(3.56)

\displaystyle R_{p+1}-R_{p-1}

\displaystyle=-M(Q_{p+1}-Q_{p-1})^{T}M^{-T}.

(3.57)

\displaystyle(R_{p+1}-R_{p-1})M^{T}

\displaystyle=-M(Q_{p+1}-Q_{p-1})^{T}.

The structure demonstrates that the difference

(3.58)

R_{p+1}-R_{p-1}

behaves like a skew symmetric matrix
after
changing into coordinates given by
M
(or essentially
R_{p}
).
∎

We now state an orthonormal basis for the horizontal space in
\mathcal{M}
computed in
[
10
, Section 6]
and
[
11
, Theorem 34]
. Let

(3.59)

\mathbf{M}\in\mathcal{M},\mathbf{M}=\left(Q_{N}\Lambda^{\frac{1}{N}}Q_{N-1}^{T},Q_{N-1}\Lambda^{\frac{1}{N}}Q_{N-2}^{T},\dots,Q_{1}\Lambda^{\frac{1}{N}}Q_{0}^{T}\right)

so that
\mathbf{M}=\xi(\Lambda,Q_{N},Q_{N-1},\ldots,Q_{1})
and
\Lambda=\diag(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{d})
. Furthermore, let
q_{p,i}
denote the
i
th column of
Q_{p}
. Then, define the following special rank one elements of
\mathbb{M}_{d}^{N}
:

(3.60)

\begin{cases}\mathbf{l}^{k}=\left(l^{k}_{N},l^{k}_{N-1},\ldots,l^{k}_{1}\right),\\
l^{k}_{s}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}q_{s,k}q_{s-1,k}^{T},\end{cases}

(3.61)

\begin{cases}\mathbf{u}^{k,l,0}=\left(u_{N}^{k,l,0},u_{N-1}^{k,l,0},\ldots,u_{1}^{k,l,0}\right),\\
u^{k,l,0}_{s}=\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{k}^{2}-\lambda_{l}^{2}}{\lambda_{k}^{2N}-\lambda_{l}^{2N}}}\lambda_{k}^{s-1}\lambda_{l}^{N-s}q_{s,l}q_{s-1,k}^{T},\end{cases}

and

(3.62)

\begin{cases}\mathbf{u}^{k,l,N}=\left(u_{N}^{k,l,N},u_{N-1}^{k,l,N},\ldots,u_{1}^{k,l,N}\right),\\
u^{k,l,N}_{s}=\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{k}^{2}-\lambda_{l}^{2}}{\lambda_{k}^{2N}-\lambda_{l}^{2N}}}\lambda_{k}^{N-s}\lambda_{l}^{s-1}q_{s,k}q_{s-1,l}^{T}.\end{cases}

Lemma 3.10

(Orthonormal Basis for Horizontal Space in
\mathcal{M}
)
.

(3.63)

\begin{cases}\mathbf{l}=\{l_{k}|1\leq k\leq d\},\\
\mathbf{u}^{0}=\{u^{k,l,0}|1\leq k<l\leq d\},\\
\mathbf{u}^{N}=\{u^{k,l,N}|1\leq k<l\leq d\}\end{cases}

form a basis for
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{M}}=(\ker\phi^{*}[\mathbf{M}])^{\perp}
.

Remark 3.11
.

Note a matrix
\mathbf{Z}\in\mathbb{M}_{d}^{N}
is in
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{M}}
(span of
\mathbf{l}
,
\mathbf{u}^{k,l,0}
, and
\mathbf{u}^{k,l,N}
) if and only if there exists a set of coefficients
\alpha\in\mathbb{M}_{d}
such that

(3.64)

Q_{p}\alpha Q_{p-1}^{T}=Z_{p}

for all
1\leq p\leq N
.

Now that we understand both the straight lines and horizontal space in
\mathcal{M}
, we can use
\phi
and Theorem
3.9
to construct new explicit formulas for geodesics on
\mathfrak{M}_{d}
.

Proof of Theorem
2.3
.

It is sufficient to first construct
\mathbf{A}\in\phi^{-1}(A)\subseteq\mathcal{M}
and
\mathbf{B}\in\phi^{-1}(B)\subseteq\mathcal{M}
such that

(3.65)

x(t)=(1-t)\mathbf{A}+t\mathbf{B}\in\mathcal{M}\text{ for all }t\in[0,1]

because
\iota
is induced by the flat Frobenius metric. Then, we will show that
x^{\prime}(t)
is horizontal at
t=0
by proving that

(3.66)

\frac{d}{dt}\left((1-t)\mathbf{A}+t\mathbf{B}\right)=-\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B}\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{A}}

or that equivalently
\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{A}}
and
\mathbf{B}\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{A}}
because
\phi^{*}
is linear. Finally, Theorem
3.9
can be applied, implying that
\phi\circ x
is a geodesic in the downstairs space between
\phi(\mathbf{A})
and
\phi(\mathbf{B})
and giving the explicit formula as desired.

We begin by identifying the correct parameterizations for the upstairs endpoints. Consider the following parameterization for
\mathbf{A}
:

(3.67)

\mathbf{A}=\xi\left(\Sigma^{\frac{1}{N}},U,I,\ldots,I,V\right)

where
\xi
is the map given in Equation
1.5
since
\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{M}
. Clearly,
\phi(\mathbf{A})=A
. Furthermore, Lemma
3.10
gives a set of orthonormal basis vectors for the horizontal space
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{A}}
:

(3.68)

\displaystyle l^{k}_{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\begin{cases}u_{k}e_{k}^{T}&s=N\\
e_{k}v_{k}^{T}&s=1\\
e_{k}e_{k}^{T}&\text{o/w}\end{cases},

(3.69)

\displaystyle u^{k,l,0}_{s}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_{k}^{\frac{2}{N}}-\sigma_{l}^{\frac{2}{N}}}{\sigma_{k}^{2}-\sigma_{l}^{2}}}\sigma^{\frac{s-1}{N}}\sigma_{l}^{\frac{N-s}{N}}\begin{cases}u_{l}e_{k}^{T}&s=N\\
e_{l}v_{k}^{T}&s=1\\
e_{l}e_{k}^{T}&\text{o/w}\end{cases},

(3.70)

\displaystyle u^{k,l,0}_{s}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_{k}^{\frac{2}{N}}-\sigma_{l}^{\frac{2}{N}}}{\sigma_{k}^{2}-\sigma_{l}^{2}}}\sigma^{\frac{N-s}{N}}\sigma_{l}^{\frac{s-1}{N}}\begin{cases}u_{k}e_{l}^{T}&s=N\\
e_{k}v_{l}^{T}&s=1\\
e_{k}e_{l}^{T}&\text{o/w}\end{cases}.

By Remark
3.11
,
\mathbf{Z}\in\mathbb{M}_{d}^{N}
is in
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{A}}
if and only if there exists
\alpha\in\mathbb{M}_{d}
such that

(3.71)

\displaystyle U\alpha

\displaystyle=Z_{N},

(3.72)

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=Z_{p},2\leq p\leq N-2,

(3.73)

\displaystyle\alpha V^{T}

\displaystyle=Z_{1}.

It is immediately obvious that
\mathbf{A}
is in its own horizontal space
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{A}}
using the coefficient matrix
\alpha=\Sigma^{\frac{1}{N}}
.

Now, also consider

(3.74)

\mathbf{B}=\xi\left(\tilde{\Sigma}^{\frac{1}{N}},\tilde{U},Q,\ldots,Q,\tilde{V}\right),

where
Q=U^{T}\tilde{U}=V^{T}\tilde{V}
by assumption. Clearly,
\phi(\mathbf{B})=B
. Furthermore,
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
satisfy the sufficient condition of Lemma
2.2
since
Q^{T}=\tilde{U}^{T}U=\tilde{V}^{T}V
and
R_{p}
is constant for all
p
. Thus, the line
(1-t)\mathbf{A}+t\mathbf{B}
is indeed the geodesic between
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
in
(\mathcal{M},\iota)
.

Now we will show
x^{\prime}(0)
is horizontal for the chosen
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
. We have already shown that
\mathbf{A}
is in
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{A}}
. By Equation
3.66
, it is now sufficient to show that
\mathbf{B}\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{A}}
as well. We thus return to solving the system of equations in Remark
3.11
with
\mathbf{Z}=\mathbf{B}
:

(3.75)

\displaystyle U\alpha

\displaystyle=\tilde{U}\tilde{\Sigma}^{\frac{1}{N}}Q^{T},

(3.76)

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=Q\tilde{\Sigma}^{\frac{1}{N}}Q^{T},

(3.77)

\displaystyle\alpha V^{T}

\displaystyle=Q\tilde{\Sigma}^{\frac{1}{N}}\tilde{V}^{T},

and has the obvious solution given by the middle equations of
\alpha=Q\tilde{\Sigma}^{\frac{1}{N}}Q^{T}
which is consistent with the others by definition of
Q
. Thus,
\mathbf{B}\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{A}}
.

Since we have shown that both
\mathbf{B}
and
\mathbf{A}
are in
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{A}}
and that the geodesic linking them is a straight line, it follows from Equation
3.66
that
x^{\prime}(0)
is horizontal. Applying Theorem
3.9
, we conclude that
\phi\circ x
is a geodesic in
(\mathfrak{M}_{d},g^{N})
of the same length as
x
. The explicit formula comes directly from applying
\phi
to
x
between our parameterized
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
.
∎

Appendix A
Informal Note on Equation
2.11

The geodesic in Equation
2.11
also appears in another geometry
[
4
]
when the endpoints are positive definite diagonal matrices. In particular, we endow
\mathbb{P}_{d}
with the trace metric: at a matrix
P\in\mathbb{P}_{d}
and tangent vector
Z\in T_{P}\mathbb{P}_{d}
, we let

(A.1)

\|Z\|_{\Tr}^{2}=\Tr(P^{-1}ZP^{-1}Z).

Consider
P
positive definite and diagonal, and let
Z
commute with
P
(e.g. also diagonal). By the properties of trace,

(A.2)

\|Z\|_{\Tr}^{2}=\Tr(ZP^{-2}Z).

Between any matrices
P_{0},P_{1}\in\mathbb{P}_{d}
, the geodesic is exactly
P_{0}^{1-t}P_{1}^{t}
. Interestingly,
g^{N}
as
N\to\infty
coincides with the trace metric under these assumptions. Consider a variant of
\mathcal{A}_{N,\cdot}
with an additional
\frac{1}{N}
factor in front so that a
N\to\infty
limit exists. Then, indeed, if
P
is diagonal and commutes with
Z
,

(A.3)

\mathcal{A}_{N,P}(Z)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{p=1}^{N}(P^{2})^{\frac{N-p}{N}}Z(P^{2})^{\frac{p-1}{N}}=(P^{2})^{\frac{N-1}{N}}Z.

The limit as
N\to\infty
is just
P^{2}Z
. Thus,
\mathcal{A}^{-1}_{\infty,P}=P^{-2}Z
and we conclude that

(A.4)

\|Z\|_{g^{\infty}}=\Tr(Z\mathcal{A}^{-1}_{\infty,P}Z)=\Tr(ZP^{-2}Z)=\|Z\|_{\Tr}.

More generally, if
X\in\mathfrak{M}_{d}
is symmetric, for all
Z\in T_{X}\mathfrak{M}_{d}
where
X
and
Z
commute, we have that this agreement between
g^{\infty}
and the trace metric. Since along the geodesic
X(t)
between
A
and
B

\dot{X}\in T_{X}\mathfrak{M}_{d}
is always diagonal, we have agreement between the trace metric and DLN metric for the entire geodesic. Contrast this with the smallest nontrivial depth,
N=2
, where if
X
is symmetric we know already that the DLN metric coincides with the Bures Wasserstein metric for symmetric tangent vectors.
