Morphological and reproductive trait analysis for assessment of genetic diversity in Chrysanthemum morifolium Ramat.
Authors: Madhu Bala, Komalpreet Kaur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe present investigation was conducted at the Punjab Agricultural University, Ludhiana, during 2022–23 to evaluate twenty genetically diverse chrysanthemum (Chrysanthemum morifolium Ramat.) genotypes demonstrating cut, loose, and pot culture categories that were grouped further into early, mid, and late maturity groups. The study aimed to assess morphological and reproductive attributes. Significant variations were observed in vegetative parameters, including plant height, spread, branching and stem girth. From the given results, it was noticed that the Ratlam Selection and Solan Shringar recorded maximum plant height, while Punjab Mohini exhibited the highest branching. Flowering traits also varied markedly; early-flowering genotypes consistently showed earliest flower bud initiation, whereas late-flowering types required longer durations for bud initiation. Punjab Mohini was found to be excelled in flower yield, producing the highest number of flowers per plant. Ray floret characteristics also varied, with Jaya showing highest number of florets. Considerable diversity was also observed in flower forms, colours, and RHS colour chart values. Reproductive biology studies indicated that highest pollen viability and pistil receptivity were achieved predominantly on the third day after anthesis, with Bidhan Purna and Haldi Ghati recording the highest values. Overall, genotypes such as Ratlam Selection, Punjab Mohini, Jaya, and Winter Queen emerged as promising for commercial exploitation and breeding.Keywords: Chrysanthemum, Flowering attributes, Genotype variability, Pollen viability, Pistil receptivity

Introduction

Chrysanthemum (Chrysanthemum morifolium Ramat.), a member of the family Asteraceae, is one of the most important ornamental crops cultivated worldwide for its remarkable aesthetic appeal, wide adaptability, and diverse utility. It is commonly referred to as the "Autumn Queen" or the "Queen of the East," and also attained global recognition as a major cut flower and exhibition plant [15, 20]. The genus is hexaploid in nature (2n = 54) with a basic chromosome number x = 9 [59]. It originated in Asia and North eastern Europe and holds historical, cultural, and commercial significance. The etymology of the word is derived from the Greek terms chrysos (golden) and anthos (flower), which collectively symbolize its traditional golden-yellow blooms. In India, it is commonly known as "Guldaudi" and is highly valued due to the wide diversity among its cultivars in terms of growth habit, bloom size, form, and colour [40, 47].

Globally, chrysanthemum ranks as the fourth most important cut flower and the fifth most traded potted plant, occupying nearly 9.77% of the cut flower market share [28, 33]. Trade statistics indicate that it is the second most traded cut flower species worldwide, next only to roses [8]. In India, it is extensively cultivated in Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, and Telangana, covering nearly 42.63 thousand hectares and contributing approximately 644.94 thousand metric tonnes of loose flowers and 39.19 thousand metric tonnes of cut flowers annually [1]. Loose-flowered types are particularly popular for garland-making, bouquets, venis, and religious offerings, while standard types with large, sturdy stems are extensively used for cut flower arrangements and exhibitions [4, 18].

Biologically, chrysanthemum is a qualitative short-day plant in which flowering is induced by photoperiod. Under long-day conditions, the plant remains in a vegetative phase, exhibiting increased plant height and vegetative growth, while transition to flowering occurs as day length decreases [45]. In natural conditions, wild-type chrysanthemums bloom during autumn and winter seasons [23]. Tall and upright cultivars are preferred for cut flowers and background planting, whereas dwarf and compact types are suitable for pot culture or foreground planting in landscapes [61].

Despite its economic and ornamental importance, chrysanthemum improvement faces certain challenges. Although substantial efforts have been directed toward germplasm collection and varietal development, the availability of comprehensive information on the genetic variability, flowering responses, and yield-contributing attributes of diverse genotypes remains limited in India. Novelty in traits such as flower colour, shape, size, plant architecture, and post-harvest longevity is consistently demanded in the market, emphasizing the necessity of systematic evaluation and classification of genotypes [24]. Such characterization not only assists in identifying superior cultivars for direct commercial exploitation but also provides a basis for breeding programs aimed at developing novel hybrids with desirable traits. Genetic diversity plays a crucial role in determining the extent of variability in morphological and yield-contributing traits. Variations in key characters such as flower weight, number of flowers per plant, and bloom size directly influence overall flower yield and quality. Analyzing the relationships between yield and its component traits is essential for understanding the selection potential of these characters [48]. Furthermore, the reproductive biology of chrysanthemum genotypes encompassing pollen viability and pistil receptivity on the stigma varies considerably among genotypes in relation to their reproductive capacity and significantly influences the genetic structure and size of populations [43]. Apart from the morphological and yield attributes, reproductive biology traits such a pollen viability and pistil receptivity are also vital for successful fertilization and seed set, which directly influence the hybridization efficiency and the development of new genotypes. High pollen viability ensures the effective male fertility, which extended period of pistil receptivity enhances the likelihood of successful pollination, thereby contributing to higher genetic recombination and improved breeding efficiency in chrysanthemum [5, 60].

Such analyses are valuable in identifying the traits most closely associated with yield and in selecting promising genotypes for breeding. The present investigation was undertaken considering the economic importance, rising consumer demand, and the limited systematic information available on chrysanthemum genotypes. The study aimed to evaluate the twenty diverse genotypes for their morphological, flowering and reproductive attributes to assess the extent of genetic variability and to identify promising genotypes with high ornamental and yield potential.

Materials and methods

Experimental site and design

The present research was carried out at Research Farm, Department of Floriculture and Landscaping, Department of Plant Breeding and Genetics, Punjab Agricultural University, Ludhiana during 2022—23. The site is located in the North-Western Plain Zone of India (30º 54’ N, 75º 48’, with an elevation of 247 m AMSL) and experiences a subtropical climate. Summers (April-June) are hot and dry with temperature rising upto 45—46º C, while winters (December-January) are cold with temperatures occasionally dropping below 5º C. The average annual rainfall is about 700 mm and the type of soil is sandy loam with moderate fertility and good water retention capacity.

Planting material

A total twenty genetically diverse genotypes of chrysanthemum (Angel, Punjab Anuradha, Baggi, Bidhan Purna, Bidhan Tarun, Haldi Ghati, Jaya, Kalpana, Kelvin Mandarin, Kusum, Maghi, Mars, Mother Teresa, Punjab Mohini, Ratlam Selection, Solan Shringar, Vijay Kiran, Winter Queen, Yellow Charm, Yellow Delight) having commercial significance were selected from the germplasm maintained at chrysanthemum block, PAU, Ludhiana for morphological evaluation (Fig. 1). The grouping of all the 20 genotypes into three classifications was done on the basis of their commercial purposes (cut, loose and pot culture) and response to flowering (early, medium and late).

Classification of 20 diverse genotypes of chrysanthemum on the basis of morphological, maturity group and reproductive traits

Selection, preparation of cutting and its planting

Healthy and disease-free mother plants representing 20 genotypes were used for the preparation of terminal stem cuttings (5–8 cm in length). The cuttings were treated with indole-3-butyric acid (IBA) @ 500 mg/-L and planted in protrays containing burnt rice husk as the rooting medium during the first week of July. The protrays were maintained under 50% shade net conditions and irrigated twice daily to ensure high humidity. Root initiation occurred within 20 days, after which the rooted cuttings were transplanted in the first week of August into 8-inch earthen pots filled with a sterilized soil and vermicompost mixture (1:1 v/v). Each plant was properly tagged with its respective cultivar name, and uniform cultural practices, including irrigation, nutrient application, and plant protection measures, were followed to ensure healthy growth and flowering.

Intercultural operations and harvesting

All recommended cultural practices of PAU, including weeding, pinching, staking, and irrigation, were uniformly followed to ensure proper growth and flowering. From bud initiation to full bloom (October), observations on morphological, vegetative, flowering, and yield traits were recorded. Flowers from cut and loose-flower genotypes were harvested weekly from October to January for yield assessment, while pot-cultured genotypes were maintained for display.

Observations recorded

From each replication, five plants per genotype were randomly selected and tagged at the onset of floral bud initiation to facilitate systematic recording of morphological observations, encompassing vegetative growth, flowering and reproductive attributes.

Vegetative attributes

Plant height, spread and number of primary branches

Plant height, plant spread, and the number of primary branches were recorded at the stage of first flower bud initiation to assess the vegetative growth performance of the genotypes. Plant height (cm) was measured from the basal region of the stem to the apical growing point of plant using a meter scale, and the mean values were expressed in centimetres. Plant spread (cm) was determined by measuring the canopy diameter in two directions (north–south and east–west) with the help of a meter scale, and the average of the two readings was taken as the mean plant spread. The number of primary branches was recorded by counting the total branches arising from the main stem and the mean value was computed.

Stem girth (mm) and internodal length (cm)

Stem girth (mm) of each plant was measured at the middle portion of the main stem using a vernier caliper, and the mean values were computed and expressed in millimetres. Internodal length (cm) was determined by measuring the distance between two consecutive nodes on the main stem with the help of a meter scale, and the average values were expressed in centimetres.

Flowering attributes

Days to first flower bud appearance, colour break stage and 50% flowering

Days to first flower bud appearance were recorded as the number of days from transplanting to the emergence of the first visible flower bud on five tagged plants of each genotype, and the mean values were computed. Days to colour break stage were determined by recording the number of days from transplanting to the appearance of visible colour on flower buds, using observations from five plants per replication, and the average values were calculated. Days to 50% flowering were recorded as the number of days from transplanting to the opening of 50% of flowers in each genotype, based on observations from tagged plants across all replications, and the mean values were worked out.

Bud diameter, flower diameter and number of flowers per plant

Bud diameter (mm) was measured using a vernier caliper, and the mean values were computed and expressed in millimetres. Flower diameter (cm) was recorded at full bloom by measuring the diameter of five randomly selected flowers in each genotype with the help of a scale, and the average values were expressed in centimetres. The number of flowers per plant was determined by counting the total number of flowers produced per plant throughout the flowering season in each replication, and the mean values were calculated.

Number of ray florets/flower and its thickness

The number of ray florets per inflorescence was recorded by counting florets from five randomly selected flowers in each genotype, and the average value was computed. The thickness of ray florets was measured using a vernier caliper, and the mean was expressed in millimeters (mm).

Flowering duration and shelf /vase life (days)

Flowering duration (days) was calculated as the number of days from the onset of blooming to the fading of more than 75% of flowers on each tagged plant within a replication, and the mean values were worked out for each genotype. Shelf /vase life (days) was assessed by harvesting freshly opened flowers or buds showing colour from each genotype. The duration for which cut flowers retained freshness (vase life) and loose flowers maintained acceptable quality (shelf life) was recorded, and the average values were computed.

Flower yield/plant (g)

In loose-flower genotypes, flower yield per plant was estimated by recording the cumulative flower weight from successive pickings on five tagged plants per replication, from the first harvest until the end of the flowering season. The mean yield was expressed in grams (g) per plant.

Flower form and its colour (RHS)

The flower form of each genotype was categorized on the basis of the arrangement and alignment of ray and disc florets. The colour of both ray and disc florets was recorded with reference to the Royal Horticultural Society (RHS) Colour Chart for accurate classification.

Reproductive attributes

Pollen viability (%)

Pollen viability across genotypes was determined following the procedure outlined [17]. Freshly dehisced pollen grains were collected from flowers between the first and fourth day after anthesis during afternoon hours using a fine brush. The pollen was carefully transferred onto clean glass slides, to which a drop of 1% acetocarmine solution was added before gently placing a cover slip, ensuring the exclusion of air bubbles. Viable pollen grains, owing to the presence of dehydrogenase activity, absorbed the stain and appeared pink, whereas non-viable grains remained unstained or yellowish. After one hour of staining, the slides were examined under a compound microscope, and the proportion of viable pollen grains was calculated using the formula described [52].

Pistil receptivity

Pistil receptivity, a critical reproductive trait influencing the success of pollination and fertilization, was assessed in the present investigation following the method [27]. Flowers from each genotype were sampled at four developmental stages, spanning the first to the fourth day after anthesis. Stigmas were carefully excised from the ray florets of freshly harvested flowers and mounted on clean glass slides. A drop of 6% hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) solution was placed on each stigma, followed by the gentle placement of a cover slip. Observations were made under a compound microscope, where the release of oxygen bubbles from the stigmatic surface indicated positive receptivity. The intensity of bubbling was used as the basis for scoring and categorization of receptivity levels.

Statistical analysis

The data related to vegetative and flowering attributes were analyzed by using the Completely Randomized Design (CRD) along with three replications. The analysis was performed by using the R software [9]. For the analysis of reproductive parameters, the mean ± standard deviation and treatment means were compared using Tukey’s Honest Significant differences (HSD) test at p ≤ 0.05 in Minitab software.

Results

Vegetative attributes

Plant height (cm)

The significant variability in plant height was recorded among the evaluated genotypes across different categories at the stage of first bud appearance (Table 1, Fig. 2). In the cut flower category, maximum plant height (55.33 cm) was recorded in Maghi, while the minimum plant height (31.33 cm) noticed in Yellow Delight across the late, early and mid-flowering groups, respectively. Within the loose flower category, significant variation in plant height was observed. The maximum plant height (65.33 cm) were recorded in Ratlam Selection, while minimum (27.00 cm) was recorded in Bidhan Tarun, within the mid, late and early flowering group, respectively. Among the pot culture genotypes, the maximum plant height (49.00 cm) was recorded in Kalpana, while minimum (16.00 cm) was recorded in Yellow Charm, within the mid, late and early flowering group. The overall results indicated that, across all the categories, the maximum plant height was recorded in mid and late flowering genotypes, with Ratlam selection and Maghi recorded the maximum plant height, while the minimum plant height was recorded in Yellow Delight, Bidhan Tarun and Yellow Charm, respectively.

Plant spread (cm)

The significant variations were recorded among the different genotypes with respect to plant spread (Table 1; Fig. 2). Within the cut flower category, Vijay Kiran exhibited the highest plant spread (35.30 cm), while lowest plant spread (25.33 cm) was observed in the genotype Jaya across the early, mid and late group. In the loose flower category, the maximum plant spread was observed in Ratlam Selection (38.67 cm), whereas minimum (27.67 cm) was recorded in Baggi within the mid, late and early group. Among the pot culture genotypes, the maximum plant spread was noticed in Winter Queen (35.00 cm), while the minimum (22.00 cm) was recorded in Bidhan Purna across the late, mid and early group. From the given results, it was across all categories, the maximum plant spread was observed in Ratlam Selection and Winter Queen, whereas the minimum plant spread was found in Bidhan Purna and Maghi.

Number of branches

The significant results were recorded among the different genotypes with respect to the number of branches per plant across the different categories (Table 1; Fig. 2). The number of branches per plant was found highest (8.00) in Jaya, whereas Yellow Delight and Kelvin Mandarin recorded comparatively lowest number of branches per plant (3.67 each) respectively, across the mid, late and early group (Table 1). In the case of loose flower category, the higher number of branches was recorded in Baggi (8.00), while Solan Shringar produced a slightly lower number of branches (6.80), respectively within the mid, early and late flowering group. Among the pot culture genotypes, Punjab Mohini yielded the highest number of branches (8.67), whereas comparatively lowest number of branches per plant were observed in Kusum (4.33) and Haldi Ghati (5.33) respectively, across the early, mid and late flowering group. From the overall results, it was concluded that the higher number of branches were recorded in Punjab Mohini and Jaya, while the lowest was recorded in Yellow Delight and Kusum.

Stem girth (mm) and internodal length (cm)

Significant variation was observed among the genotypes with respect to stem girth and internodal length (Table 1). The maximum stem girth was recorded in Maghi (4.23 mm), whereas the minimum was observed in Yellow Delight (2.55 mm). A similar trend was noted for internodal length, where the longest internodes were recorded in Jaya (3.27 cm), while the shortest internodes were observed in Yellow Delight (2.27 cm), respectively.

Flowering attributes

Days to first flower bud appearance (DBTA) and colour break stage (DTCB)

The significant variation was observed in terms of number of days taken to first bud initiation across the genotypes (Table 2). In the cut flower group, the minimum (87 days) number of days taken to bud appearance was recorded in Yellow Delight, while maximum (98 days) number of days taken in Maghi, across early, mid and late flowering group. Among loose flower genotypes, Bidhan Tarun recorded the earliest (87 days) bud appearance, while Solan Shringar required the maximum (115 days) number of days. In the pot culture group, minimum number of days was observed in Punjab Anuradha (67 days), while Kalpana showed the maximum number of days (101.67 days). Overall, the results indicate that early-flowering genotypes consistently exhibited earlier bud appearance, whereas mid and late flowering groups required a longer duration for first bud appearance.

Flowering duration (days) and response in weeks

The flowering duration varied considerably among the genotypes (Table 2). In the cut flower category, the maximum flowering duration was recorded in Yellow Delight and Maghi (33 days), while the minimum was recorded in Jaya (23.67 days). Among loose flower types, the longest flowering duration was exhibited in Ratlam Selection (37 days), whereas the shortest was observed in Solan Shringar (26.00 days). In pot culture genotypes, Winter Queen recorded the maximum flowering duration (37 days), while the minimum was noted in Kalpana (22.67 days), closely followed by Angel (23 days). Flowering duration was highly genotype-dependent, with late and some early-flowering genotypes showing extended flowering periods, while certain mid-flowering genotypes exhibited shorter durations.

The flowering response in weeks also varied significantly among the genotypes (Table 2). In the cut flower category, maximum flowering response was recorded in Maghi (14 days), while minimum was recorded in Yellow Delight (12.43 weeks). In the loose flower category, Solan Shringar (16.43 weeks) exhibited the maximum response in weeks, while minimum was recorded in Bidhan Tarun (12.43 weeks). In the pot culture category, the maximum flowering response was recorded in Kalpana (14.52 weeks), while the minimum was recorded in Punjab Anuradha (9.57 weeks), respectively. These results indicate that flowering response in weeks is strongly influenced by the flowering group and genotype, with late-flowering genotypes generally showing extended flowering periods, while early-flowering genotypes tend to have shorter overall responses with less weeks of response group.

Bud diameter (mm) and flower diameter (cm)

Bud diameter varied significantly among the different chrysanthemum genotypes (Table 3). In the cut flower group, the largest (10 mm) bud diameter was recorded in Yellow Delight, whereas the smallest (7 mm) was observed in Maghi. Among loose flower genotypes, Ratlam Selection produced the maximum (12.51 mm) bud diameter, while Solan Shringar had the minimum (7.64 mm) bud diameter. In the pot culture category, Winter Queen showed the highest (10.51 mm) bud diameter, while the lowest (5.49 mm) was noted in Yellow Charm, respectively. Overall, the mid-flowering group exhibited the maximum bud diameter (12.51 mm) in Ratlam Selection, whereas the minimum (5.49 mm) was recorded in Yellow Charm in the early flowering group.

Overall, the results indicate that the third day after anthesis was the most critical interval, during which the majority of genotypes attained their maximum pollen viability, whereas a few genotypes maintained higher viability up to the fourth day.

Discussion

Vegetative attributes

Plant height is an important growth parameter, reflecting vegetative vigour and exerting a substantial influence on the overall yield potential of a crop [53, 55]. In the present study, observed variability in plant height among the different genotypes may be due to genetic differences and their interaction with prevailing environmental conditions [11, 22]. The significant genotypic variation observed in plant height among the different genotypes can be attributed due to enhanced meristematic activity resulting from accelerated cell division and elongation during the early growth phase. This process is often associated with higher photosynthetic activity, enabling greater assimilate production and partitioning towards vertical growth [58]. Additionally, nutritional status of plants plays a pivotal role, particularly the uptake of nitrogen, which contributes to chlorophyll synthesis, thereby improving photosynthetic efficiency. These assimilate, in turn, contribute to the biosynthesis of key phytohormones such as auxins, gibberellins, and cytokinins, which are directly involved in the stimulate stem elongation and internodal growth [12, 47].

These findings are in line with earlier reports in chrysanthemum, where variation in plant height was linked to differences in photosynthetic efficiency, assimilate partitioning, and genetic makeup of the genotypes [39, 48].

Plant spread is an important vegetative parameter that determines the architecture and photosynthetic efficiency of a plant, ultimately influencing yield and quality. The significant variation observed among the genotypes may be attributed to their genetic makeup, which regulates branching pattern and canopy development [38]. In the present study, genotypes such as Vijay Kiran, Ratlam Selection, and Winter Queen exhibited wider plant spread, which could be associated with the production of a greater number of secondary and lateral branches. Increased branching enhances leaf production, and as leaves are the primary functional units for photosynthesis, this leads to improved assimilate availability, thereby supporting higher vegetative growth and flower yield [7]. The wider angle between primary and secondary branches at the coupling points also contributes to enhanced canopy spread, as reported earlier [41].

The variation in plant spread among different genotypes is further governed by additive gene action [34] as well as the adaptability of the genotypes to prevailing environmental conditions [53]. These results are in close agreement with earlier findings in chrysanthemum, where significant variability in plant spread was reported to be due to differences in branching capacity, genetic factors, and adaptability [26, 34, 49].

Branching is a vital vegetative parameter as it determines canopy spread, leaf area development, and ultimately flower yield potential. The variation in the number of branches observed in the present study can be attributed primarily to genetic differences among the genotypes, which influence their inherent growth rates and capacity for shoot initiation [51]. Genotypes such as Jaya, Baggi, and Punjab Mohini exhibited a higher branching pattern, which could be linked to their superior genetic potential for vegetative vigour and better utilization of assimilates. An increased number of branches per plant leads to greater leaf production, thereby enhancing photosynthetic activity and strengthening the source–sink relationship, which ultimately contributes to higher yield [6]. Apart from genetic factors, environmental conditions such as temperature, relative humidity, and light intensity also play a crucial role in regulating branching, as they affect the expression of inherent genetic traits [40]. For instance, chrysanthemum is a short-day plant, and genotypes grown during the summer season tend to produce more primary branches due to longer day lengths and favourable temperatures, as also reported [31]. Earlier studies have similarly highlighted genotypic variability in branching in chrysanthemum [21, 47], dendranthema species [2, 11], gladiolus [46], and china aster [30]. These variations have often been linked to differences in the genetic makeup, capacity for reserve food storage, and adaptability to soil and climatic conditions [3].

Stem girth and internodal length are important vegetative attributes that influence plant vigour, stability, and canopy development. The observed differences among genotypes can primarily be ascribed to their genetic constitution, which regulates growth patterns and morphological expression [10]. Genotypes such as Maghi and Vijay Kiran, which recorded thicker stems, may possess greater assimilate partitioning towards secondary growth, thereby enhancing stem robustness. A higher stem girth is often associated with increased leaf area and photosynthetic activity, which promotes carbohydrate accumulation and root growth, further reinforcing vegetative development [16]. Similar genotypic variations in stem girth in chrysanthemum have also been documented earlier [2, 6]. With respect to internodal length, Jaya and Maghi exhibited longer internodes, which directly contributed to their higher plant height, suggesting a positive correlation between these two traits. Elongated internodes are often regulated by the hormonal balance of gibberellins and auxins, coupled with favourable environmental conditions, which promote enhanced cell elongation and division. This relationship between internodal length and plant height has also been emphasized in earlier studies by Pasha et al. [35].

Flowering attributes

The differences observed in the time taken for bud initiation across genotypes may be attributed due to inherent genetic variability, which regulates the earliness or delayed flowering as an intrinsic varietal character. These results are consistent with earlier reports in chrysanthemum [36], dahlia [29], and other ornamental crops [44]. Furthermore, genotypic expression interacts with prevailing environmental conditions, thereby influencing the onset of floral initiation. Earliness in bud initiation is a desirable trait in floricultural crops, as it facilitates the capture of early markets, extends the duration of flower availability, and enhances profitability [51]. Thus, the marked variation in bud initiation time observed across chrysanthemum genotypes underscores the role of genetic constitution and genotype–environment interactions in determining flowering behaviour. These findings provide valuable insights for selecting genotypes suitable to specific production systems and market demands.

The variability in time to colour break among genotypes can be attributed to differences in genetic makeup and their interaction with environmental conditions during the crop season. Earliness in colour break is closely associated with early bud initiation and is often facilitated by efficient carbohydrate utilization, nutrient uptake, and favourable climatic conditions such as shorter day lengths and lower temperatures, which collectively promote accelerated floral development [56]. Similar trends in flowering behaviour have also been reported earlier in chrysanthemum and other ornamentals crops [11, 47].

The variation in days to 50% flowering among genotypes may be attributed to differences in genetic constitution and the interaction between genotype and prevailing environmental conditions [51]. Most genotypes exhibited adaptability to local conditions, and early flowering appeared to be facilitated by earlier bud initiation and colour break, supported by favourable climatic factors and nutrient availability. Such variation has also been reported previously in chrysanthemum [19, 49, 50].

Flowering duration is a critical trait, as it determines the availability of blooms over time and thereby influences their commercial value in the market [37]. The observed differences in flowering span among genotypes can be attributed to inherent genetic variability as well as genotype × environment interactions, which regulate floral development and senescence [11]. Earlier studies in chrysanthemum under varied climatic conditions have also reported similar variation in flowering duration [25, 47, 51].

The variation in flower diameter may primarily be ascribed to genotypic differences, which are further influenced by prevailing environmental conditions and their interactions [14]. Flower size is largely a varietal characteristic, but it may also be influenced by source-sink relationships, moisture status, and the number of flowers produced per plant. Similar findings in chrysanthemum were reported by Singh et al. [47] and Parmar et al. [34]. The variability in flower production can be attributed to both genetic and environmental factors. Genotypes producing more branches generally yield a higher number of flowers due to greater photosynthate production and dry matter accumulation [12, 54]. Similar variations have also been documented in chrysanthemum [32, 39, 42, 47, 51].

Significant variation in the number of ray florets per plant was recorded among chrysanthemum genotypes. This variation may be attributed to genotypic differences that regulate physiological processes such as amino acid and protein synthesis, which are essential for floral development [3]. Similar observations in chrysanthemum have also been reported [47].

A wide diversity of flower forms was documented across the genotypes. Variation in flower form is largely determined by the genetic constitution of the plant and gene interactions regulating floral development. Similar findings have been reported in chrysanthemum [4, 13]. A wide range of flower colours was observed among the genotypes. Variation in flower colour is mainly attributed to genetic factors and pigment composition, with anthocyanins responsible for red–purple shades, and chalcones and aurones imparting yellow hues [14]. Significant variability in shelf and vase life was observed among the evaluated genotypes. These differences can be ascribed to genetic makeup, carbohydrate accumulation, and cultivar sensitivity to ethylene, which regulate postharvest physiology [57]. These factors interact with environmental influences such as temperature, wind velocity, and humidity, thereby impacting turgidity, transpiration, and the breakdown of stored food reserves [47]. Longer shelf life has been associated with lower transpiration and evaporation rates under favourable microclimatic conditions [53].

Variation in flower weight was recorded exclusively among loose flower genotypes. Higher floral yield is often associated with greater plant height, increased primary branching, higher leaf count, and larger leaf area, which enhance photosynthetic efficiency and ultimately increase the number of flowers per plant [6, 11]. Moreover, water absorption capacity relative to transpiration rates also plays a role in determining flower biomass [10]. The present findings are in agreement with earlier reports attributing variation in flower weight to genotypic differences in flower size, disc prominence, and number of well-developed ray florets [49].

Significant variation in pollen viability was observed among chrysanthemum genotypes across successive days after anthesis. Most genotypes, including Bidhan Purna, Ratlam Selection, Yellow Charm, and Winter Queen, attained peak viability on the third day, whereas Baggi, Jaya, Kelvin Mandarin, and Solan Shringar reached maximum viability on the fourth day. Certain genotypes, such as Haldi Ghati and Punjab Mohini, maintained high viability (≥ 90%) as early as the second day. These differences are likely due to genotypic variation in flower form, anthesis timing, and disc floret morphology, which influence pollen hydration, nutrient utilization, and metabolic activity. The observed decline in viability over time can be attributed to pollen dehydration and accelerated metabolic processes post-anther dehiscence, consistent with previous reports [62]. Overall, the third day after anthesis represents the optimal interval for pollen collection in most genotypes. Pistil receptivity also varied among genotypes, with most attaining maximum receptivity on the third day after anthesis (e.g., Haldi Ghati, Winter Queen, Punjab Mohini). Some genotypes, including Punjab Mohini and Yellow Charm, showed peak receptivity on the second day, while Kelvin Mandarin and Winter Queen maintained high receptivity up to the fourth day. Early stages exhibited shorter pistils confined within the corolla tube, whereas on the third day, stigmatic lobes fully expanded and bifurcated to form a Y-shaped surface conducive for pollen deposition. Differences in receptivity are influenced by floral morphology, genotype, and the timing of anthesis, highlighting the importance of synchronizing pollen viability and pistil receptivity for successful hybridization [59, 62].

Conclusion

The comprehensive evaluation of twenty chrysanthemum genotypes revealed the considerable genetic variability in vegetative growth, flowering behaviour, floral morphology, and reproductive biology. Distinct genotypes demonstrated the superiority in specific attributes- Ratlam Selection and Maghi enhanced plant vigour, Punjab Mohini showed profuse branching and high flower yield, Jaya excellent in ray floret production, and Winter Queen in flowering duration highlighting their potential for targeted commercial utilization. This marked variation in floral forms, colour shades, and RHS chart groupings further emphasized the rich ornamental diversity within the studied germplasm. Based on the flowering behaviour, the genotypes were classified into three distinct maturity groups: early, medium and late. The early flowering groups included Punjab Mohini, Yellow Charm, Kusum, Vijay Kiran, Yellow Delight, Bidhan Tarun, Punjab Anuradha, Bidhan Purna etc., the medium group included Jaya, Kelvin Mandarin, Baggi, Ratlam Selection, Mother Teresa, Kalpana etc., while the late group consisted of Maghi, Solan Shringar and Winter Queen. Reproductive biology observations indicated that the third day after the anthesis represented the most favourable stage for achieving maximum pollen viability and pistil receptivity. This period was identified as the critical for effective pollen-pistil interaction, leading to higher success rates. Such information is invaluable for optimizing hybridization techniques and enhancing breeding efficiency in chrysanthemum. Overall, the findings not only identified promising genotypes suitable for cut, loose, and pot flower markets but also provided a robust genetic foundation for future breeding strategies aimed at improving yield, floral quality, and aesthetic appeal in chrysanthemum, thereby contributing to the diversification and advancement of the floriculture industry.
