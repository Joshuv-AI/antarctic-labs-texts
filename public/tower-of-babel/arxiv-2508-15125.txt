Modeling pandemics
John F. Dawson, Fred Cooper, Efstathios G. Charalampidis

Abstract
We review several models for pandemics that plagued the USA and the world in the past decade. Methods of data fitting are reviewed and several types of microscopic and rate equation models are discussed and numerically solved. This paper was written in March of 2021 and newer data is now available; however some of the models and techniques we used to numerically study these models are still of interest. Several appendices discuss in detail these models. I
Introduction

As a result of the COVID-19 pandemic, numerous mathematical models of the pandemic have recently been proposed and debated in both the press and in scientific publications. One way to think about these models are to categorize them according to the way they work. One can divide them into two major groups: those models that simply try to predict future conditions based on statistical models fit to past conditions, and those that are based on transition dynamics. In the first category is William Farr’s original proposal (1844) that epidemics follow a bell-shaped curve so that after peak infection is reached, the epidemic will “progressively fall through the same steps.” One fits the data to a normal distribution curve. Obviously one can choose a different model curve for the epidemic and simply fit your favorite curve to the data or to the log of the data. A recent model by Morte
et al.

Della Morte et al. 2020
chooses a model curve, inspired by renormalization group techniques, and fits the curve to the observed number of cases available to date. The model from the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME) at UW appears to belong to this first category
Jewell et al. 2020
, although apparently modified somewhat by social distancing models. The model used mortality data rather than infection rate data which is considered by some to be more reliable. The IHME model has been widely quoted in the press and by the president’s task force, largely because they are willing to make predictions.

In the second category are epidemiological models often based on SEIR (susceptible-exposed-infectious-recovered) dynamics, with numerous modifications to account for social distancing. We discuss these types of models in Sec.
III.2
. A recent model by Dandekar and Barbastathis
Dandekar and Barbastathis 2020
(the “MIT” model) using machine learning techniques belongs to this category. We discuss this model below. The MRC center of the Imperial College of London model is also based on epidemiological considerations as well as statistical analysis.

A third method (Girona
Girona 2020
) applies stochastic algorithms to individuals players in a sample population to create the pandemic in a two-dimensional grid. This model is based on reaction rates and is discussed below.

SEIR models

The basic SEIR model assumes a homogeneous population described by
the following consituants:

S

with reactions and reaction rates given by:

S+I\xrightarrow{\beta}E+I,\quad E\xrightarrow{\sigma}I,\quad I\xrightarrow{\gamma}R\>.

(2)

Here after contact with an infected individual (
I
), susceptible (
S
) individuals enter into the exposed class (
E
) at a transmission rate
\beta
per person which progress to the infected class (
I
) at an infection or incubation rate
\sigma
and which in turn are eventually removed (
R
) from the dynamics at a rate
\gamma
. In this model, the removed either become immune or die. Rate equations for these processes are:

\displaystyle\derivative{S}{t}

\displaystyle=-\beta\,S(t)I(t)/N\>,

(3a)

\displaystyle\derivative{E}{t}

\displaystyle=\beta\,S(t)I(t)/N-\sigma\,E(t)\>,

(3b)

\displaystyle\derivative{I}{t}

\displaystyle=\sigma\,E(t)-\gamma\,I(t)\>,

(3c)

\displaystyle\derivative{R}{t}

\displaystyle=\gamma\,I(t)\>,

(3d)

where the total population number
N=S(t)+E(t)+I(t)+R(t)
is conserved.
There are three parameters in this model,
(\beta,\sigma,\gamma)
, in addition to the total population number
N
. One can split the removed category into a recovered (now called
R
) and dead (
D
) by replacing Eq. (
3d
) with the equations,

\derivative{D}{t}=f\gamma\,I(t),\quad\derivative{R}{t}=(1-f)\,\gamma\,I(t)\>,

(4)

where
f
is the fraction of infected individuals that die. The recovered are assumed to be immune. The total number of individuals are now given by
N=S(t)+E(t)+I(t)+R(t)+D(t)
, and include the dead. The cumulative number of infected individuals
C(t)
are given by
C(t)=I(t)+D(t)+R(t)
, and the new infections per day by

\derivative{C}{t}=\sigma\,E(t)\>.

(5)

For the COVID-19 case, the transmission time
1/\beta
is approximately 2–3 days if no control measures are taken. The incubation time
1/\sigma
is approximately 7–14 days and the recovery or death time
1/\gamma
also seems to be about 7–14 days. The death rate in the US is about 5%. For the Ebola epidemic in West Africa, the death rate was a shocking 50%. The basic infection (reproduction) number is defined to be
\mathcal{R}_{0}=\beta/\gamma
and the basic incubation number is
\mathcal{I}_{0}=\sigma/\gamma
. The initial conditions are also important. Here
N
is the total population number which in the case of the US is about 330 million people. For New York City it is about 8 million, for Wuhon China about 11 million. These are big numbers compared to the number of infected individuals at
t=0

S_{0}=N-I_{0}

S(t)\approx S_{0}\approx N
. So it is a reasonable to set
S(t)=N+F(t)
, where
F(t)
is a number comparable to the populations of exposed, infected, and removed individuals. With this approximation, the SEIR equations (
3

\displaystyle\derivative{F}{t}

\displaystyle=-\beta\,I(t)\>,

(6a)

\displaystyle\derivative{E}{t}

\displaystyle=\beta\,I(t)-\sigma\,E(t)\>,

(6b)

\displaystyle\derivative{I}{t}

\displaystyle=\sigma\,E(t)-\gamma\,I(t)\>,

(6c)

\displaystyle\derivative{D}{t}

\displaystyle=f\gamma\,I(t),\quad\derivative{R}{t}=(1-f)\,\gamma\,I(t)\>,

(6d)

III.2.1
Machine learning models

The model of Dandekar and Barbastathis
Dandekar and Barbastathis 2020
(referred to as the MIT model) uses a neural network to find the best fit for a quarantine function. They start with a simplified SIR model, eliminating the incubation period state
E
, so as to reduce the number of parameters. Instead of allowing the reproduction rate
\mathcal{R}
to become a function of time, they introduce a quarantine fraction
Q(t)=\gamma\,q(t)
and a quarantine population
T(t)=Q(t)\,I(t)=\gamma\,q(t)\,I(t)
, so that the SIR model equations become:

\displaystyle\derivative{S}{t}

\displaystyle=-\beta\,S(t)I(t)/N\>,

(9a)

\displaystyle\derivative{I}{t}

\displaystyle=\beta\,S(t)I(t)/N-\gamma\,[1+q(t)]\,I(t)\>,

(9b)

\displaystyle\derivative{R}{t}

\displaystyle=\gamma\,I(t)\>,

(9c)

\displaystyle\derivative{T}{t}

\displaystyle=\gamma\,q(t)\,I(t)\>.

(9d)

In dimensionless time
\tau=\gamma\,t
, these SIR equations are described by a single parameter
\mathcal{R}_{0}=\beta/\gamma
and an unknown quarantine function
q(t)
. The problem is then to find the best quarantine function, which they implement by using machine learning with a neural network. That is they set
Q(t)=\mathrm{NN}[\,W,U(t)\,]
, where
U(t)=\{\,S(t),I(t),R(t),T(t)\,\}
and
\mathrm{NN}
is a neural network consisting of an activation function which connects all the functions represented by
U(t)
at time
t
to the quarantine fraction
Q(t)
, which is in itself a solution of (
9
). They use a neural network consisting of a “2-layer densely connected network with 10 units in the hidden layer and the ReLU activation function.” The network results in 64 weights
W_{i}
which are tuned to obtain the best fit to the data. The neural network is used to both solve the model differential-algebraic equation (DAE) system and the error loss by a local sensitivity analysis to the data (See Cao
et al.

Cao et al. 2003
and Rackauckas
et al.

Rackauckas et al. 2020
). This is a very sophisticated calculation using the
JULIA
computer language, developed at MIT.
(See Rackauckas
et al.

Rackauckas et al. 2019
and the
JULIA
website
https://julialang.org/
for further details.)

III.3
Geographical models

The extension of SEIR-type models to include movement of individuals in a geographical region is straightforward. However to simplify the dynamics, we will use the simpler SIR model described by the reactions,

S+I\xrightarrow{k_{1}}2\,I,\quad I\xrightarrow{k_{2}}0,\quad S\xrightarrow{k_{3}}0,\quad 0\xrightarrow{k_{4}}S\>.

(10)

Here we have allowed for a death (or recovery) rate
k_{2}
for the infected population and death
k_{3}
and
birth
k_{4}
rates for the susceptible population. Dead or recovered individuals do not enter into the dynamics. The
d
-dimensional region is divided into cells labeled by the index
i
, so that populations are now labeled by the set
\{\,S_{i}(t),I_{i}(t)\,\}
. The susceptible and infected populations are allowed to jump into neighborhood regions with rates
d_{S}
and
d_{I}
respectively,

I_{i}(t)\xrightarrow{d_{I}}I_{i\pm 1}(t),\quad S_{i}(t)\xrightarrow{d_{S}}S_{i\pm 1}(t)\>.

(11)

Neighboring regions can be as far away as New York and Florida, as these regions are connected by exchanging populations. We assume that the recovered are immune. Passing over to a continuum density description, we set

\phi_{S}(\mathbf{x},t)=S_{i}(t)/L^{d},\quad\phi_{I}(\mathbf{x},t)=I_{i}(t)/L^{d}\>.

The total population density is now given by
\rho_{0}=N/L^{d}
.
Then the continuum version of the SIR equations (
3
) becomes:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi_{I}(\mathbf{x},t)

\displaystyle=[\,D_{I}\laplacian-\mu\,]\,\phi_{I}(\mathbf{x},t)+\lambda\,\phi_{S}(\mathbf{x},t)\,\phi_{I}(\mathbf{x},t)\>,

(12a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi_{S}(\mathbf{x},t)

\displaystyle=[\,D_{S}\laplacian-\nu\,]\,\phi_{S}(\mathbf{x},t)-\lambda\,\phi_{S}(\mathbf{x},t)\,\phi_{I}(\mathbf{x},t)+f\>,

(12b)

Here
\lambda=k_{1}L^{d}=\beta/\rho_{0}
,
\mu=k_{2}L^{d}
,
\nu=k_{3}L^{d}
,
f=k_{4}
,
D_{S}=2d_{S}/h^{2}
, and
D_{I}=2d_{I}/h^{2}
. In this model, dispersion takes place as a random walk. We recognize Eqs. (
12
) as being a set of coupled non-linear Schrödinger equations in imaginary time, but with
real
“wave functions.” We can add recovered, dead, and accumulated infection density by the equations,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi_{R}(\mathbf{x},t)

\displaystyle=(1-g)\,\mu\,\phi_{I}(\mathbf{x},t)\>,

(13a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi_{D}(\mathbf{x},t)

\displaystyle=g\,\mu\,\phi_{I}(\mathbf{x},t)\>,

(13b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi_{C}(\mathbf{x},t)

\displaystyle=\mu\,\phi_{I}(\mathbf{x},t)\>,

(13c)

which do not enter into the dynamics. Here
g
is the fraction of infected individuals that die.

Homogeneous and steady state solutions

Ignoring the recovered population, homogeneous and steady state solutions exist for the rest of the SIR model. Setting
\phi_{\alpha}(x,t)=\phi_{\alpha}
, they are given by solutions of the equations:

\displaystyle\nu\,\phi_{I}-\lambda\,\phi_{S}\,\phi_{I}

\displaystyle=0\>.

(14a)

\displaystyle\nu\,\phi_{S}+\lambda\,\phi_{S}\,\phi_{I}

\displaystyle=f\>,

(14b)

There are two sets of solutions of (
14
) given by

\displaystyle\phi_{I}^{(R)}=0,\quad\phi_{S}^{(R)}=\frac{f}{\nu}\>,

(15a)

\displaystyle\phi_{I}^{(B)}=\frac{f}{\mu}-\frac{\nu}{\lambda},\quad\phi_{S}^{(B)}=\frac{\mu}{\lambda}\>.

(15b)

Solutions (
15a
) are labeled “red,” and those in (
15b
) “blue.” The red solutions consist of a uniform population of susceptible individuals and no infected individuals, which is obviously stable. It is usual to set the birth and death rates of susceptible individuals numerically equal, in which case
f=\nu
and
\phi_{S}^{(R)}=1
. It is also useful to set
\mu=\nu+\kappa
, with
\kappa>0
. With this notation, blue solutions are possible if

\frac{f}{\mu}-\frac{\nu}{\lambda}=\frac{\nu}{\mu\lambda}\,(\lambda-\mu)=\frac{\nu\,(\lambda-\nu-\kappa)}{\lambda\,(\nu+\kappa)}\geq 0\>.

(16)

In this case, both infected and healthy individuals can coexist. A plot of

(in red) and

(in blue) as a function of

and

is shown in Fig.
9(a)
. In some regions
\phi_{I}^{(B)}
is greater than

.

III.3.2
Stability analysis

Let us define

\Phi(x,t)=\begin{pmatrix}\phi_{I}(x,t)\\
\phi_{S}(x,t)\end{pmatrix},\quad\Phi_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}\phi_{I}\\
\phi_{S}\end{pmatrix},\quad\delta\Phi=\begin{pmatrix}\delta\phi_{I}\\
\delta\phi_{S}\end{pmatrix}\>,

(17)

where
\Phi_{0}
are solutions of the homogeneous and steady state solutions (
14
). Then the stability of the solutions of the rate equations can be studied by setting

\Phi(x,t)=\Phi_{0}+\delta\Phi\,{\rm e}^{{\rm i}(kx-\omega t)}\>,

(18)

and expanding the result to first order in
\delta\Phi
. This gives an equation of the form,

\displaystyle G^{-1}_{k,\omega}[\,\Phi_{0}\,]\,\delta\Phi=0\>,

(19a)

\displaystyle G^{-1}_{k,\omega}[\,\Phi_{0}\,]=\begin{pmatrix}-{\rm i}\omega+D_{I}k^{2}+\mu-\lambda\,\phi_{S}\>,&-\lambda\,\phi_{I}\\
\lambda\,\phi_{S}\>,&-{\rm i}\omega+D_{S}k^{2}+\nu+\lambda\,\phi_{I}\end{pmatrix}\>.

(19b)

Non-trivial solutions are possible if

\displaystyle\det{ G^{-1}_{k,\omega}[\, \Phi_0 \,] }=-\omega^{2}-{\rm i}\,B_{k}\,\omega+C_{k}=-(\omega-\omega_{k}^{(+)})\,(\omega-\omega_{k}^{(-)})=0\>.

(20)

where

\displaystyle B_{k}

\displaystyle=(D_{I}+D_{S})\,k^{2}+\lambda\,(\phi_{I}-\phi_{S})+\mu+\nu\>,

(21a)

\displaystyle C_{k}

\displaystyle=D_{I}D_{S}\,k^{4}+[\,D_{I}(\nu+\lambda\phi_{I})+D_{S}(\mu-\lambda\phi_{S})\,]\,k^{2}+\lambda(\mu\phi_{I}-\nu\phi_{S})+\mu\nu\>.

(21b)

and

\omega_{k}^{(\pm)}={\rm i}\,\frac{B_{k}}{2}\pm\rm i\,\sqrt{\Bigl(\frac{B_{k}}{2}\Bigr)^{2}-C_{k}}\>.

(22)

III.3.3
Temporal stability

Homogeneous and oscillatory solutions are found for
k=0
when
B_{0}=0
and
C_{0}<0
.
From (
21
), this requires

\displaystyle\nu+\mu+\lambda\,(\,\phi_{I}-\phi_{S}\,)=0\>,

(23a)

\displaystyle\mu\,\nu+\lambda\,(\mu\,\phi_{I}-\nu\,\phi_{S})\leq 0\>.

(23b)

For the red state,
\phi_{S}=f/\nu
and
\phi_{I}=0
, so that (
23a
) required that
\lambda f/\nu=\nu+\mu
, in which case
C_{0}=\nu(\mu-\lambda)=-\nu^{2}\leq 0
is always satisfied so that the red states are temporaly stable. The oscillation frequencies are
\omega_{0}^{(\pm)}=\pm\nu
. The blue state is unstable for all values of the parameters. This temporal instability is called Hopf bifurcation.
This oscillation is illustrated in Fig.
7(b)
where the simulation was started in the Red state where
\phi_{S}=1
and
\phi_{I}=0
, with
\nu=f=1/100
, but winds up asymptotically in the Blue state with
\phi_{S}=0.5
and
\phi_{I}=0.02

1/\nu=100
days. In order to have oscillations
\nu
and
f
must be non-zero — that is oscillations require a constant entering and leaving of the physical region, which is probably to be expected in any epidemic. The rate of the entering and leaving govern the the oscillation period. Note the steady increase in deaths in this scenario.

III.3.4
Spacial stability

Inhomogeneous and stable steady state patterns can be set up if certain conditions are met. These patterns are called Turing patterns — the condition under which the patters emerge are called Turing bifurcations. For patterns to emerge, we must have
C_{k}\leq 0
. The critical value is when
C_{k_{c}}=0
, or from (
21b
) when

D_{I}D_{S}\,k_{c}^{4}+[\,D_{I}(\nu+\lambda\,\phi_{I})+D_{S}(\mu-\lambda\,\phi_{S})\,]\,k_{c}^{2}+\lambda\,(\mu\phi_{I}-\nu\phi_{S})+\mu\nu=0\>.

(24)

As a function of
k^{2}
,
C_{k}
is a parabolic curve with positive curvature,

\displaystyle\partialderivative{C_k}{k^2}\Big|_{k_{c}}

\displaystyle=2\,D_{I}D_{S}\,k_{c}^{2}+[\,D_{I}(\nu+\lambda\,\phi_{I})+D_{S}(\mu-\lambda\,\phi_{S})\,]\>,

(25a)

\displaystyle\partialderivative[2]{C_k}{(k^2)}\Big|_{k_{c}}

\displaystyle=2\,D_{I}D_{S}>0\>.

(25b)

The minimum of the curve is at

k_{c}^{2}=-\frac{D_{I}(\nu+\lambda\,\phi_{I})+D_{S}(\mu-\lambda\,\phi_{S})}{2\,D_{I}D_{S}}\>.

(26)

Substituting this into (
24
) gives:

\displaystyle[\,D_{I}(\nu+\lambda\,\phi_{I})+D_{S}(\mu-\lambda\,\phi_{S})\,]^{2}

\displaystyle=4\,D_{I}D_{S}\,[\,\mu\,\nu+\lambda\,(\mu\,\phi_{I}-\nu\,\phi_{S})\,]

(27)

\displaystyle\hskip-40.00006pt=4\,D_{I}(\nu+\lambda\,\phi_{I})\,D_{S}(\mu-\lambda\,\phi_{S})-4\,D_{I}D_{S}\,\lambda^{2}\,\phi_{I}\,\phi_{S}\>.

Choosing the negative sign for the square root and substitution into (
26
), the critical wave number is given by

k_{c}=\sqrt{\frac{\mu\,\nu+\lambda\,(\mu\,\phi_{I}-\nu\,\phi_{S})}{4\,D_{I}D_{S}}}

(28)

Simplifying (
27
) gives

\displaystyle-D_{I}^{2}(\nu+\lambda\,\phi_{I})^{2}+

\displaystyle 2\,D_{I}(\nu+\lambda\,\phi_{I})\,D_{S}(\mu-\lambda\,\phi_{S})-D_{S}^{2}(\mu-\lambda\,\phi_{S})^{2}

(29)

\displaystyle=4\,D_{I}D_{S}\,\lambda^{2}\,\phi_{I}\,\phi_{S}\>,

which can be written as

[\,D_{I}(\nu+\lambda\,\phi_{I})-D_{S}(\mu-\lambda\,\phi_{S})\,]^{2}=4\,D_{I}D_{S}\,\lambda^{2}\,\phi_{I}\,\phi_{S}\>.

(30)

For the red state, (
30
) becomes:

D_{I}\,\nu=D_{S}(\mu-\lambda)\quad\mbox{$\Rightarrow$}\quad\frac{\mu-\lambda}{\nu}=\frac{D_{I}}{D_{S}}\>,

(31)

which defines the Turing line. For the red state, from (
28
) along this line the critical wave number is given by

k_{c}=\sqrt{\frac{\nu\,(\mu-\lambda)}{4\,D_{I}D_{S}}}=\frac{\nu}{2D_{S}}\>.

(32)

For the blue state,

\nu+\lambda\,\phi_{I}=\frac{\nu\lambda}{\mu},\quad\lambda\,\phi_{S}=\mu\>.

(33)

Subsituting this into (
29
) gives the condition,

4\,\frac{\mu^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}\,\Bigl(\frac{\lambda-\mu}{\nu}\Bigr)=\frac{D_{I}}{D_{S}}\>.

(34)

The Turing curves are shown in Fig.

.
Both of these states can exhibit Turing patterns.

III.4
Stochastic models

Stochastic models of processes that lead to rate equations like (
12

Assuming that the processes are Markovian, one can either solve the master equation numerically for population number using an algorithm, such as the Gillespie algorithm, for each process and reaction selected at random, or develop rate equations for the densities, called
Langevin equations
, which includes internal noise generated by the microscopic model. Details of this derivation are given in Appendix
B
. The Gillespie algorithm is discussed in Appendix
D
.

In appendix

(35a)

with noise functions,

\displaystyle\eta_{I}(x)

IV
Applications

IV.1
Fitting models to data

The problem of applying SEIR models to epidemics is that data is either unreliable or incomplete. One would like to be able to predict the course of an epidemic at the beginning of the process so that corrective measures can be taken early on. This is not always possible. Ideally modelers would like to have data on the number of cases, the number of deaths, the number of recovered, and if possible the number of infected or hospitalized per day as a function of date. This data is very difficult to get, even from organizations like the CDC or the WHO which
should
provide it.
2
2

2

I have found it particularly difficult to obtain data from these organizations.

Fitting data requires the minimization of a cost function, usually taken to be a least-squares function of the form,

L(p)=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{d}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{m}}|\,y_{j}(t_{i},p)-d_{j}(i)\,|^{2}\>,

(37)

where the sum goes over the
N_{d}
data points
d_{j}(i)
at
t_{i}
. Here
y_{i}(t,p)
is a vector of model population dimension
N_{m}
, satisfying the differential algebraic equations

\dot{y}_{j}=f_{j}(y,p,t),\quad y_{j}(0,p)=y_{j,0}(p),\quad j=1,2,\dotsc,N_{m}\>,

(38)

and where
p=(\,p_{1},p_{2},\dotsc,p_{N_{p}}\,)
is a vector containing the
N_{p}
the parameters in the problem. Note that the initial conditions
y_{j,0}(p)
can depend on the parameters also. Minimization of (
37
) with solutions of the differential equations (
38
) is a classic problem in applied mathematics and analysis. We describe here a gradient method given in papers by Petzold and co-workers
Li and Petzold 2000
;
Cao et al. 2003
. In this method, we start by expanding (
37
) to first order about a point
p
,

L(p+\Delta p)=L(p)+\sum_{k=1}^{N_{p}}\partialderivative{L(p)}{p_k}\,\Delta p_{k}+\dotsb

(39)

Now choosing

\Delta p_{k}=-h\,\partialderivative{L(p)}{p_k}\quad\mbox{$\Rightarrow$}\quad L(p+\Delta p)-L(p)=-h\,\sum_{k=1}^{N_{p}}\Big|\partialderivative{L(p)}{p_k}\Bigr|^{2}<0\>,

(40)

we move the point
p
to a value which gives a smaller value of
L(p)
. Here
h
is the step size.
Differentiating (
37
) with respect to the parameters and setting the result to zero gives

\partialderivative{L(p)}{p_k}=2\sum_{i=1}^{N_{d}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{m}}\,[\,y_{j}(t_{i},p)-d_{j}(i)\,]\,s_{jk}(t_{i},p),\quad s_{jk}(t,p)=\partialderivative{y_j(t,p)}{p_k}\>.

(41)

This necessitates finding the solution of not only the original equations (
38
), but the set of differential equations for for
s_{ij}(t,p)
, given by

\dot{s}_{ij}(t,p)=\partialderivative{f_i(y,t,p)}{y_k}\,s_{kj}(t,p)+\partialderivative{f_i(y,t,p)}{p_j},\quad s_{ij}(0,p)=\partialderivative{y_{i,0}(p)}{p_j}\>.

(42)

So we must find the simultaneous solution of Eqs. (
38
) and (
42
) with initial conditions which, in general, can be included as one of the parameters. Then we start with estimated values of
p_{k}
and compute (
41
) at the data points
t_{i}
, then new values of
p_{k}
are found from (
40
), and the process started all over again.

Petzold has developed a computer code called
DASPK3.0

Li and Petzold 2000
to do this for large-scale differential algebraic systems. Our SEIR fitting problem is not a large scale system, so the code seems to be over kill in our case.

In practice often one fits the log of the population numbers and data rather than the numbers themselves. Various authors did different things.
Althaus
Althaus 2014
used the
ODE
solver in the R-programming language to solve the SEIR equations and the
optim
optimizer to find the best fit, without explaining what he optimized.
Tang,
et al.

Tang et al. 2020
used a Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method combined with an adaptive Metropolis-Hastings (M-H) algorithm to find the best fit, again without any details.
The MIT group
Dandekar and Barbastathis 2020
used the local adjoint sensitivity method of Petzold
et al.

Cao et al. 2003
and Rackauckas
et al.

Rackauckas et al. 2020
for the machine learning algorithm and the
ADAM
optimizer in the
JULIA
computation environment. Smirnova
et al.

Smirnova et al. 2019
took a different tack and converted the set of ODE’s to a linear Volterra equation which was then solved iteratively for
\beta(t)
.

IV.3
Application to the US COVID-19 pandemic

Application of the SEIR model to the COVID-19 epidemic in the US is difficult since the number of cases are still increasing, although the number of new cases are plateauing or even decreasing.

When this paper was written (March, 2021), the US was still in the middle of the epidemic and the number of cases were still increasing. So at that time, application of the SEIR model to the COVID-19 epidemic in the US was difficult. Regional differences did develop which the simple SEIR model cannot emulate. However the methods used here could be incorporated into models which better capture the current situation.

B.1
The master equation

The master equation formalism assumes that the space in which reactions take place can be divided into a
d
-dimensional hyper-cubic lattice of cells and that each cell can be treated as a coherent entity. Reactions occur only if the objects involved are in the same cell. They are assumed to react independently. If the underlying processes are Markovian, they can be described by a probability distribution function
P(\,\mathbf{N},t\,)
, where
\mathbf{N}=\{\,N_{a}(i),N_{b}(j),\dotsc\,\}
is a vector describing the number of objects
N_{\alpha}(i)
of species
\alpha=(a,b,\dotsc)
at a site

\frac{\partial P(\mathbf{N},t)}{\partial t}=\sum_{r}\,\bigl[\,a_{r}(\mathbf{N}-\mathbf{S}_{r})\,P(\mathbf{N}-\mathbf{S}_{r},t)-a_{r}(\mathbf{N})\,P(\mathbf{N},t)\,\bigr]\>,

(63)

where

is the stoichiometric vector of reaction
r

is the propensity function (reaction rate), determined by the number and kind of objects present before the reaction. From (

is conserved.

The master equation suggests introducing an occupation number algebra with annihilation and creation operators

4

B.3
The Doi shift

A simple trick, discovered by Doi
Doi 1976
, provides a probabilistic interpretation of the state vector. Doi found that if all creation operators are translated by one unit leaving the annihilation operators unchanged, the overlap of the transformed state vector with the vacuum is conserved. The non-hermitian operator
\hat{D}
which does this is given by

\hat{D}=\exp\Bigl\{\,-\sum_{i,\alpha}\hat{a}_{\alpha}(i)\,\Bigr\}\>,

(79)

so that

\displaystyle\hat{D}^{-1}\,\hat{a}_{\alpha}(i)\,\hat{D}

\displaystyle=\hat{a}_{\alpha}(i)\>,

(80a)

\displaystyle\hat{D}^{-1}\,\hat{a}^{{\dagger}}_{\alpha}(i)\,\hat{D}

\displaystyle=1+\hat{a}^{{\dagger}}_{\alpha}(i)\>.

(80b)

The Doi-shifted state vector is defined by,

\displaystyle\ket*{ \tilde{\Psi}(t) }=\hat{D}^{-1}\ket*{ \Psi(t) }

\displaystyle=\prod_{\alpha,i}\sum_{n_{\alpha}(i)=0}^{\infty}\!P(\mathbf{n},t)\,\hat{D}^{-1}\,\bigl[\,\hat{a}^{{\dagger}}_{\alpha}(i)\,\bigr]^{n^{\phantom{{\dagger}}}_{\alpha}(i)}\,\hat{D}\,\hat{D}^{-1}\,\ket{ \bzero}

(81)

\displaystyle=\prod_{\alpha,i}\sum_{n_{\alpha}(i)=0}^{\infty}\!P(\mathbf{n},t)\,\bigl[\,1+\hat{a}^{{\dagger}}_{\alpha}(i)\,\bigr]^{n^{\phantom{{\dagger}}}_{\alpha}(i)}\,\ket{ \bzero}\>,

and the overlap of the vacuum and the shifted state vector is given by

\innerproduct*{ \vb{0} }{ \tilde{\Psi}(t) }=\prod_{\alpha,i}\sum_{n_{\alpha}(i)=0}^{\infty}\!P(\mathbf{n},t)=1\>,

(82)

and is conserved. We can define a Doi-shifted vacuum by
\bra{\tilde{\vb{0}}}=\bra{\vb{0}}\hat{D}^{-1}
so that
\innerproduct*{ \vb{0} }{ \tilde{\Psi}(t) }=\innerproduct*{ \tilde{\vb{0}} }{ \Psi(t) }
.
The Schrödinger equation (
68
) for the Doi-shifted state vector becomes

\partialderivative{\ket*{\tilde{\Psi}(t)}}{t}=-\tilde{H}[\,\hat{\mathbf{a}}^{{\dagger}},\hat{\mathbf{a}}\,]\ket*{\tilde{\Psi}(t)}\>,

(83)

where the shifted Hamiltonian is

\displaystyle H[\hat{\mathbf{a}}^{{\dagger}},\hat{\mathbf{a}}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\expectationvalue{i,j}}\,\bigl\{\,d_{I}\,(\,\hat{a}^{{\dagger}}_{I}(i)-\hat{a}^{{\dagger}}_{I}(j)\,)\,(\,\hat{a}_{I}(i)-\hat{a}_{I}(j)\,)

\displaystyle\hskip 40.00006pt+d_{S}\,(\,\hat{a}^{{\dagger}}_{S}(i)-\hat{a}^{{\dagger}}_{S}(j)\,)\,(\,\hat{a}_{S}(i)-\hat{a}_{S}(j)\,)\bigr\}

(84)

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt+\sum_{i}\bigl\{\,k_{1}\,[\,\hat{a}_{S}^{{\dagger}}(i)-\hat{a}_{I}^{{\dagger}}(i)\,]\,(\,\hat{a}_{I}^{{\dagger}}(i)+1\,)\,\hat{a}_{S}(i)\,\hat{a}_{I}(i)+k_{2}\,\hat{a}_{I}^{{\dagger}}(i)\,\hat{a}_{I}(i)

\displaystyle\hskip 40.00006pt+k_{3}\,\hat{a}_{S}^{{\dagger}}(i)\,\hat{a}_{S}(i)-k_{4}\,\hat{a}_{S}^{{\dagger}}(i)\,\bigr\}\>.

Appendix D
Microscopic equations

There are different ways to implement a stochastic method for directly simulating the master equation. One method is to choose a very small but fixed time step and using a probability function and random numbers to decide for each step which reaction takes place, starting from some initial condition. The process is repeated starting from the same initial conditions and ensemble averages computed. In 1976, Gillispie
Gillespie 1976
;
Gillespie 1977
realized that the problem with this method was that for most of the steps, nothing happens. So if one could estimate the value of a time step in which nothing happens, one could take steps when something really happens. In addition, updates of occupation numbers at all sites can be done in single pass. The way this works is the following. Let
a_{i}(\mathbf{n})
be the reaction rates for the
i^{\text{th}}
process including a jump process, so that
a(\mathbf{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{q}a_{i}(\mathbf{n})
is the reaction probability per unit time that something happens. So then the probability that nothing happens in a time between
t

P(\Delta t)=1-a(\mathbf{n})\,\Delta t+\dots={\rm e}^{-a(\mathbf{n})\,\Delta t}\>,

(113)

where
P(\Delta t)=r

r

\Delta t=\frac{\ln(1/r)}{a(\mathbf{n})}\>.

(114)

Selecting a random number
r

One is guaranteed (more or less) that during this time, no interactions will take place. So one can then take this step and then decide, using new random numbers, which process
do
take place. The algorithm then update the occupation numbers using the stochastic vectors of the reaction for all the reaction elements at all sites. Then one recomputes
a(\mathbf{n})
and find a new step to take. Specifically, the Gillespie algorithm is the following steps
Erban et al. 2007
:

1.

Set the time
t=0
and the initial occupation values for the reactants
n_{1}(0)
.

2.

Generate two random numbers
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
uniformly distributed in
(0,1)
.

3.

Compute the propensity function
a_{i}(\mathbf{n})
for each reaction and the sum over all reactions:

a(\mathbf{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{q}a_{i}(\mathbf{n})\>.

(115)

4.

Using (
114
), compute the time
\Delta t
when the next reaction takes place and update the time,
t=t+\Delta t
.

5.

Compute
which
reaction takes place by finding the value of
j
where

\frac{1}{a(\mathbf{n})}\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}a_{i}(\mathbf{n})\leq r_{2}<\frac{1}{a(\mathbf{n})}\sum_{i=1}^{j}a_{i}(\mathbf{n})\>.

(116)

Then the
j^{\text{th}}
reaction takes place. Since
r_{2}
is a random number it doesn’t matter how the reactions are ordered. Then update the occupation numbers of reactants and products of the
j^{\text{th}}
reaction.

6.

Go back to step 2 and continue to the end time.

There is only one reaction per time step so one does not have to recompute all the propensity functions at each time step, but can just update only those that are changed by the reaction selected.
