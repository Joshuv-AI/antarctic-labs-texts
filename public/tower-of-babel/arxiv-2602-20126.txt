Adaptation to Intrinsic Dependence in Diffusion Language Models
Yunxiao Zhao, Changxiao Cai

Abstract
Diffusion language models (DLMs) have recently emerged as a promising alternative to autoregressive (AR) approaches, enabling parallel token generation beyond a rigid left-to-right order. Despite growing empirical success, the theoretical understanding of how unmasking schedules—which specify the order and size of unmasked tokens during sampling—affect generation quality remains limited. In this work, we introduce a distribution-agnostic unmasking schedule for DLMs that adapts to the (unknown) dependence structure of the target data distribution, without requiring any prior knowledge or hyperparameter tuning. In contrast to prior deterministic procedures that fix unmasking sizes, our method randomizes the number of tokens revealed at each iteration. We show that, for two specific parameter choices, the sampling convergence guarantees—measured by Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence—scale as O ~ ​ ( 𝖳𝖢 / K ) \widetilde{O}(\mathsf{TC}/K) and O ~ ​ ( 𝖣𝖳𝖢 / K ) \widetilde{O}(\mathsf{DTC}/K) respectively. Here, K K is the number of iterations, and 𝖳𝖢 \mathsf{TC} and 𝖣𝖳𝖢 \mathsf{DTC} are the total correlation and dual total correlation of the target distribution, capturing the intrinsic dependence structure underlying the data. Importantly, our guarantees hold in the practically relevant parallel-sampling regime K < L K<L where L L is the token sequence length. These results significantly improve upon prior convergence theories and yield substantial sampling acceleration for low-complexity distributions. Overall, our findings unveil the adaptivity of DLMs to intrinsic data structures and shed light on the benefit of randomized unmasking sizes in inference schedule design.



1
Introduction

Diffusion models
(
49
;
18
;
50
)
have become the cornerstone of modern generative modeling in continuous domains such as images and audio
(
12
;
45
)
.
Motivated by these successes, recent work has extended diffusion-based generation to discrete data, most notably natural language, giving rise to
diffusion language models
(DLMs)
(
46
;
40
)
.
Unlike autoregressive (AR) paradigms, which decode tokens sequentially in a fixed left-to-right order, DLMs allow multiple tokens to be generated in parallel and in arbitrary orders, offering a promising route toward faster and more flexible inference in large language models (LLMs).

State-of-the-art DLMs are built on
masked diffusion models
(MDMs)
(
1
;
6
;
48
)
.
An MDM introduces an absorbing state called
mask
and consists of two complementary processes. The forward (masking) process transforms a token sequence
X^{(0)}
drawn from a data distribution
p_{\mathsf{data}}
into a fully masked sequence
X^{(K)}
by gradually masking tokens:

\displaystyle X^{(0)}\,\overset{\mathsf{mask}}{\longrightarrow}\,X^{(1)}\,\overset{\mathsf{mask}}{\longrightarrow}\,X^{(2)}\,\overset{\mathsf{mask}}{\longrightarrow}\,\cdots\,\overset{\mathsf{mask}}{\longrightarrow}\,X^{(K)}.

The reverse (unmasking) process seeks to reverse the forward process and iteratively converts a fully masked sequence
Y^{(0)}
into a fresh sample
Y^{(K)}
from the data distribution:

\displaystyle Y^{(0)}\,\overset{\mathsf{unmask}}{\longrightarrow}\,Y^{(1)}\,\overset{\mathsf{unmask}}{\longrightarrow}\,Y^{(2)}\,\overset{\mathsf{unmask}}{\longrightarrow}\,\cdots\,\overset{\mathsf{unmask}}{\longrightarrow}\,Y^{(K)}.

The core mechanism of the reverse process is to choose a subset of indices to unmask at each iteration and sample their values conditioned on the currently revealed context.
In principle, the idealized update would draw from the true conditional joint distribution of the tokens to be unmasked given the already unmasked tokens.
Hence, DLM sampling relies on a
mask predictor
that learns these conditional distributions from finite training samples drawn from the target distribution.
One can view the mask predictor as a counterpart of the score estimator in continuous diffusion models
(
51
)
that enable the transformation from noise to data.

However, exact reverse sampling is computationally prohibitive, since the joint distribution of multiple tokens is generally intractable in high-dimensional settings.
To address this issue, practical DLMs typically adopt a conditional independence approximation
(
40
;
54
)
, using the
product of conditional marginal distributions
to approximate the true conditional joint distribution of masked tokens.
In other words, the mask predictor is trained to learn the conditional marginal distributions of individual unmasked tokens, and predict each masked token independently given the unmasked context.

1.1
Trade-off between sampling efficiency and accuracy

A key appeal of DLMs is their potential to break the sequential bottleneck of AR generation. A neural network-based mask predictor can simultaneously compute the conditional marginal distributions for all masked positions in a single forward pass, making it possible to generate multiple tokens in parallel at each iteration.

This parallelism, however, comes with a fundamental statistical price. Since we approximate the conditional joint by a product of conditional marginals and sample each newly unmasked token independently given the
same
partially revealed context, this approximation ignores dependencies among simultaneously generated tokens. Therefore, it induces an inevitable sampling error—even when the true conditional marginals are available.

Consequently, given a fixed pre-trained mask predictor, the sampling efficiency and accuracy of DLM sampling is governed by the
unmasking schedule
, which specifies how many tokens to reveal and which positions to choose at each iteration.
For example, unmasking all tokens in a single iteration maximizes parallelism but incurs the largest dependence-induced error, whereas unmasking one token at a time eliminates this error but reduces sampling to an effectively AR procedure.

Designing unmasking schedules that achieve high-quality generation without sacrificing the computational gains of parallel decoding is therefore a central challenge for DLMs.
Although a range of heuristics have been explored in practice, such as uniform random schedules
(
48
;
46
;
40
)
and confidence-based procedures
(
3
)
, a principled theoretical understanding of DLM sampling remains limited.

Prior theory on DLM sampling.

Recent work has begun to theoretically investigate this speed-accuracy trade-off by analyzing the sampling error in the regime where the number of sampling iterations
K
is smaller than the sequence length
L
.

The work of
29
provided the first convergence analysis for DLM sampling, focusing on the uniform random unmasking scheme. It prescribes a batch-size schedule
s_{1},\dots,s_{K}
with
s_{k}\geq 1
and
\sum_{k=1}^{K}s_{k}=L
, and at iteration
k
, unmasks
s_{k}
tokens selected uniformly at random from the remaining masked tokens.
When the unmasking sizes are balanced across iterations (i.e.,
s_{k}\asymp L/K
for all
k
), they show that the sampling convergence rate, measured by KL divergence, is bounded by

\displaystyle O\bigg(\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{L}I(X_{i};X_{-i})\bigg),

where
I(X_{i};X_{-i})
is the mutual information between the
i
-token
X_{i}
and the rest
X_{-i}\coloneqq(X_{j})_{j\neq i}
under the data distribution
X\sim p_{\mathsf{data}}
.
Similar bounds were also established in
24
later.

Recent work of
10
sharpened the picture by giving an exact characterization of the sampling convergence rate.
The authors then proposed an unmasking schedule that assumes access to accurate estimates
\widehat{\mathsf{TC}}
and
\widehat{\mathsf{DTC}}
of the
total correlation
(TC) and
dual total correlation
(DTC) of the data distribution (see the formal definitions in Section
2
).
They proved that this scheme achieves the
sampling convergence rate

\displaystyle O\bigg(\frac{\log L}{K}\min\{\widehat{\mathsf{TC}},\widehat{\mathsf{DTC}}\}\bigg).

Since the TC and DTC satisfy

\mathsf{TC}(X)+\mathsf{DTC}(X)=\sum_{i=1}^{L}I(X_{i};X_{-i}),

this result improves upon that of
29
and demonstrates that, for structured distributions where
\min\{\mathsf{TC}(X),\mathsf{DTC}(X)\}
is much smaller than
\sum_{i}I(X_{i};X_{-i})
, appropriately designed unmasking schedules can significantly improve sampling speed.

Gap.

Despite these theoretical advances, several critical limitations remain.
First, the analysis in
29
did not take intrinsic data structures such as weak dependence into account. The sampling error of the uniform unmasking strategy scales with the sum of mutual information
\sum_{i}I(X_{i};X_{-i})
, which can be suboptimal for structured data distributions.
For instance, in regimes where
I(X_{i};X_{-i})\gtrsim 1
—commonly encountered in practice—the sampling error grows linearly with
L
, making it ineffective for generating long sequences.

While the unmasking schedule proposed by
10
can achieve improved sampling guarantees that depend on the intrinsic structure of the data distribution, it requires reliable estimates of the information-theoretic quantities such as TC, DTC, or related information curves, which are typically unavailable for real-world high-dimensional data distributions.
In many cases, accurately estimating these quantities may require as much (or more) data than training the DLM itself. Consequently, their schedule is best viewed as an information-theoretic benchmark rather than a directly implementable procedure.

This gap motivates the question that we explore in this paper:

Can we design an unmasking schedule that adapts to unknown data structure without requiring any prior knowledge?

1.2
Our contributions

In this paper, we answer the above question affirmatively by proposing a distribution-agnostic, randomized unmasking schedule, revealing how intrinsic dependence structure in the data can be harnessed to speed up sampling.

Specifically, we develop a randomized unmasking strategy that, at each iteration, first randomly selects the unmasking set size from a carefully designed distribution and then unmasks the corresponding number of tokens uniformly at random.
Crucially, the schedule depends only on the sequence length
L
and the number of iterations
K
, and requires no prior knowledge or estimation of the target distribution.

We prove that given a pre-trained mask predictor, the resulting sampler achieves expected KL-sampling error bounds that depend on intrinsic information-theoretic measures. With two specific parameter choices, we obtain sampling convergence guarantees

\displaystyle\frac{\mathsf{TC}(X)}{K}\log L\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{\mathsf{DTC}(X)}{K-\log(L-1)-1}(\log(L-1)+1),

where
\mathsf{TC}(X)
and
\mathsf{DTC}(X)
denote the TC and DTC of the data distribution, respectively.
Notably, these guarantees hold in the practically relevant parallel generation regime
K<L
.
When the target distribution has low complexity, i.e.,
\min\{\mathsf{DTC}(X),\mathsf{TC}(X)\}\ll L
— frequently encountered in many practical applications — our results reveal that DLMs can automatically leverage the favorable low-dimensional structures, without distribution-specific knowledge, estimation, or tuning.
More broadly, these findings deliver the first theory for the dependence-adaptivity of DLMs, and strengthen the state-of-the-art convergence theory.
Finally, we note that the core design choice of our approach is randomizing unmasking sizes instead of fixing them in advance.
This may shed light on the unmasking schedule design for DLMs in practice.

1.3
Related work

Diffusion language models.

Early attempts to bring diffusion models to language modeling operated on the continuous embeddings of discrete tokens and applied Gaussian diffusion in continuous spaces
(
36
)
.
A major shift came with discrete diffusion models, where
1
formulates discrete diffusion as a continuous-time Markov chain (CTMC) with categorical transition kernels, providing a unified framework that encompasses uniform-noise, absorbing (mask), and other structured corruption processes.
The work of
39
proposed score entropy discrete diffusion, introducing a score entropy objective to learn concrete scores and improving diffusion language modeling performance.
Recently, MDMs have proven particularly effective for language generation
(
46
;
48
)
, and scaling these approaches has led to performance competitive with autoregressive models
(
40
;
23
;
52
;
55
;
59
)
. Beyond mask-based corruption, uniform-noise discrete diffusion has also been explored
(
47
)
.

Unmasking schemes.

A common practical unmasking strategy follows a two-stage template: first specifies a size schedule and then selects that many positions, often uniformly at random, from the remaining masked tokens. Popular deterministic size schedules include cosine schedules
(
48
)
and log-linear schedules
(
46
;
39
)
.
A different line of heuristics is to unmask “easy” tokens first, using either confidence-based rules that prioritize positions with high predicted probability
(
40
;
56
;
54
)
or entropy-based rules that select positions with low predictive entropy
(
3
)
.
However, such greedy choices can also be myopic: committing early to low-entropy tokens can increase uncertainty for the remaining positions in later iterations by removing informative context or amplifying dependency effects. To address this limitation, more structured approaches have been proposed, including learning unmasking policies via reinforcement learning
(
21
)
and schedule design via planning or search procedures
(
15
)
.

Theory for diffusion models.

Our results connect to a well-developed literature on convergence guarantees for continuous diffusion samplers
(
25
;
9
;
26
;
7
;
4
;
34
;
32
)
, which studies given a pre-trained model, how fast the sampling iterate converges to the target distribution in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
as the number of iterations
K
increases.
State-of-the-art theory
(
22
;
58
)
establishes convergence rates of
\widetilde{O}(D/T^{2})
in KL divergence and
\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{D}/K)
in total variation distance, and a variety of provably accelerated samplers have been developed
(
28
;
31
;
16
;
19
;
30
;
35
)
.
The adaptivity to (unknown) low-complexity structure has also been studied recently
(

;
37
;
20
;
42
;
27
)
, improving the dependence on the ambient dimension
D
to some intrinsic dimension
d
of the data distribution.
Thus for
d\ll D
, continuous diffusion samplers can exploit low-dimensional structures to accelerate sampling.
In contrast, convergence theory for discrete diffusion models remains comparatively nascent. Existing analysis typically requires the number of iterations to scale at least with the data dimension or sequence length
(
8
;
38
;
14
;
44
;
43
)
, and therefore does not accommodate the parallel-generation regime of DLMs.
Beyond convergence guarantees, end-to-end statistical guarantees by tracking the error contributions from both the training and sampling phases have also been developed
(
41
;
53
;

;
5
;

;

)
.

Concurrent work.

In a concurrent and independent work,
13
propose a
\tau
-leaping-based sampler for MDMs under a CTMC formulation. Their method also adapts to intrinsic data structure, with sampling convergence rate characterized by the TC and DTC of the target data distribution.
Our emphasis is complementary: we study the explicit design of unmasking schedules directly in discrete-time DLMs, without invoking a CTMC perspective.
In addition, our iteration complexity does not scale with the vocabulary size, while the
\tau
-leaping approach incurs a dependence on the vocabulary size.
Furthermore, our theoretical analysis directly tracks the error propagation across unmasking iterations, which may be of independent interest for studying DLM samplers.

2
Preliminaries

In this section, we review the basics of MDMs and DLMs.

Forward (masking) process.

Let
X^{(0)}=(X_{1}^{(0)},X_{2}^{(0)},\dots,X_{L}^{(0)})\in\mathbb{X}^{L}
be a token sequence drawn from the data distribution
p_{\mathsf{data}}
.
The forward process corrupts
X^{(0)}
over
K
steps by progressively replacing tokens with the mask symbol
\mathsf{M}
until all indices are masked.

Specifically, let
T=(T^{(1)},T^{(2)},\ldots,T^{(K)})
be a partition of the index set
[L]
,
i.e.,
T^{(i)}\cap T^{(j)}=\varnothing
for
i\neq j
and
\cup_{k=1}^{K}T^{(k)}=[L]
. For each
k\in[K]
,
T^{(k)}\subseteq[L]
represents the set of token positions newly masked at iteration
k
, and we define
M^{(k)}\coloneqq\cup_{j=1}^{k}T^{(j)}
the cumulative set of masked positions up to iteration
k
.

Let
X^{(k)}
denote the partially masked sequence after
k
masking steps.
Specifically, we update the sequence
X^{(k)}
from
X^{(k-1)}
by masking the tokens at positions in set
T^{(k)}
:

\displaystyle X^{(k)}_{i}=\begin{cases}\mathsf{M},&\text{if }i\in T^{(k)},\\
X^{(k-1)}_{i},&\text{if }i\notin T^{(k)}.\end{cases}

Equivalently, tokens of the original sequence
X^{(0)}
at positions in
M^{(k)}
are masked in
X^{(k)}
, i.e.,

X^{(k)}=\mathcal{P}_{[L]\setminus M^{(k)}}(X^{(0)}).

After
K
steps, the sequence is fully masked, i.e.,
X^{(K)}=(\mathsf{M},\mathsf{M},\ldots,\mathsf{M})\in\overline{\mathbb{X}}^{L}
.

Mask predictor training.

DLMs generate text by learning to reverse the above forward process, thereby transforming a fully masked sequence into a new sample from
p_{\mathsf{data}}
.
This time-reversal is realized by a mask predictor, which aims to learn the conditional distribution of masked tokens given the unmasked ones at each step of the forward process.

As discussed in Section
1
, directly modeling the joint conditional distribution of multiple masked tokens can be intractable in high dimensions. Therefore, DLMs adopt a factorized approximation.
Concretely, for
i\in[L]
, we denote the true conditional marginal of
X_{i}^{(0)}
given an arbitrary set
S
of unmasked tokens (under the data distribution
p_{\mathsf{data}}
) as

\displaystyle p^{\star}_{i}(x\mid z_{S})\coloneqq\mathbb{P}\big\{X^{(0)}_{i}=x\mid X^{(0)}_{S}=z_{S}\big\}

(2)

for any
S\subseteq[L]\setminus\{i\}
,
z_{S}\in\mathbb{X}^{|S|}
, and
x\in\mathbb{X}
.
The goal is to build an estimator
\widehat{p}_{i}(\cdot\mid\cdot)
for each conditional marginal
p^{\star}_{i}(\cdot\mid\cdot)
and construct a mask predictor
\widehat{p}=\prod_{i=1}^{L}\widehat{p}_{i}
.

The mask predictor
\widehat{p}
is trained via maximizing the log-likelihood of the masked tokens given observed tokens (or equivalently, minimize the KL divergence between the data and sampled distributions):

\displaystyle\max_{p=\prod_{i=1}^{L}p_{i}}\mathbb{E}_{X^{(0)},S,\tau}\Bigg[\frac{L}{|M^{(\tau)}|}\sum_{i\in M^{(\tau)}}\log p_{i}\big(X^{(0)}_{i}\mid X^{(\tau)}\big)\Bigg].

(3)

Here,
X^{(0)}
is a training sample drawn from
p_{\mathsf{data}}
,
S=(S^{(1)},\dots,S^{(K)})
is a sequence of unmasking sets forming a partition of
[L]
,
\tau
is an iteration index,
M^{(\tau)}=\cup_{k=\tau}^{K}S^{(k)}
is the set of masked tokens at step
\tau
, and
X^{(\tau)}=\mathcal{P}_{[L]\setminus M^{(\tau)}}(X^{(0)})
is the corresponding partially masked sequence. The expectation is over
X^{(0)}\sim p_{\mathsf{data}}
, the random unmasking sets
S
drawn from a certain schedule, and the random time
\tau\in[K]
sampled according to
\mathbb{P}\{\tau=k\mid S\}=|S^{(k)}|/L
.
Equivalently, each unmasking set
S^{(k)}
corresponds to the masking set
T^{(K-k+1)}
in the forward process, and the objective is to predict conditional marginals of masked tokens at various intermediate masking levels.
In practice, this objective is approximated by averaging over finite training samples.

As a note, one can verify that the product of the true conditional marginals
\prod_{i=1}^{L}p^{\star}_{i}
defined in (
2
) is the optimal solution to the above minimization problem (
3
).

Reverse (unmasking) process.

After a mask predictor
\widehat{p}
is trained, a
K
-step DLM sampling procedure generates a token sequence of length
L
by iteratively unmasking a masked sequence as follows.

Beginning at step
0
, we initialize the sequence
Y^{(0)}=(\mathsf{M},\mathsf{M},\ldots,\mathsf{M})\in\overline{\mathbb{X}}^{L}
and the cumulative unmasking set
U^{(0)}=\varnothing
.
Then, for each iteration
k=1,2,\ldots,K
, we repeat the following two steps:

1.

Unmasking set selection.
We first select a subset
S^{(k)}
from the currently masked positions
[L]\setminus U^{(k-1)}
according to some policy
\pi^{(k)}
, which defines a distribution over all the subsets of
[L]\setminus U^{(k-1)}
.

2.

Token sampling.
Next, given the previous iterate
Y^{(k-1)}
, we sample the tokens from set
S^{(k)}

independently
and
simultaneously
, using the learned mask predictor
\widehat{p}=\prod_{i=1}^{L}\widehat{p}_{i}
:

\displaystyle\widehat{Y}_{i}\sim\widehat{p}_{i}\big(\cdot\mid Y^{(k-1)}\big),\quad\forall\,i\in S^{(k)}.

(4)

We then update the sequence
Y^{(k)}
by filling in the newly sampled tokens:

\displaystyle Y^{(k)}_{i}=\begin{cases}\widehat{Y}_{i},&\text{if }i\in S^{(k)},\\
Y^{(k-1)}_{i},&\text{if }i\notin S^{(k)},\end{cases}

(5)

and update the cumulative unmasking set

U^{(k)}=U^{(k-1)}\cup S^{(k)}
.

The final iterate
Y_{\mathsf{out}}\coloneqq Y^{(K)}
is outputted as the generated sample.

It is worth noting that given a pre-trained mask predictor
\widehat{p}
and a fixed sequence of unmasking sets
S=(S^{(1)},S^{(2)},\ldots,S^{(K)})
the distribution of the output sample
Y_{\mathsf{out}}
is uniquely determined.
Therefore, we may write
Y_{\mathsf{out}}=Y_{\mathsf{out}}^{S}
to emphasize the dependence of the generated sample on a specific sequence of unmasking sets
S
.

More generally, since the unmasking set sequence
S
is selected according to the unmasking scheme
\pi=(\pi^{(1)},\pi^{(2)},\ldots,\pi^{(K)})
, the distribution of the generated sample
Y_{\mathsf{out}}
is fully characterized by
\pi
(along with
\widehat{p}
) through the induced distribution over
S
.

Information-theoretic measures.

Finally, we review several information-theoretic measures that will be used in this paper. Let
X=(X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{L})\in\mathbb{X}^{L}
be a random sequence with joint distribution
p_{X}
.

For any sets
S,T\in[L]
, we use
H(X_{S})
to denote the entropy of the random sequence
X_{S}=(X_{i})_{i\in S}
, and define the conditional entropy of
X_{S}
given
X_{T}
as

\displaystyle H(X_{S}\mid X_{T})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p_{X_{T}}}[H(X_{S}\mid X_{T}=z)].

(6)

The
total correlation
(TC) of
X_{S}
is defined as:

\displaystyle\mathsf{TC}(X_{S})\coloneqq\sum_{i\in S}H(X_{i})-H(X_{S}).

(7)

Intuitively, TC measures the global dependence among the variables in
X_{S}
by quantifying the distance between the joint distribution
p_{X_{S}}
and the fully factorized distribution
\prod_{i\in S}p_{X_{i}}
.
The
conditional total correlation
of
X_{S}
given
X_{T}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathsf{TC}(X_{S}\mid X_{T})\coloneqq\sum_{i\in S}H(X_{i}\mid X_{T})-H(X_{S}\mid X_{T}).

(8)

The
dual total correlation
(DTC) of
X_{S}
is defined as:

\displaystyle\mathsf{DTC}(X_{S})\coloneqq H(X_{S})-\sum_{i\in S}H\big(X_{i}\mid X_{S\setminus\{i\}}\big).

(9)

DTC has been shown to characterize how well the distribution can be approximated by a sparse mixture of product distributions
(
2
;
10
)
.
Moreover, when each
X_{i}
is determined by the rest, all the conditional entropies are zero, and the DTC equals the entropy, i.e.,
\mathsf{DTC}(X_{S})=H(X_{S})
.

The
conditional dual total correlation
of
X_{S}
given
X_{T}
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathsf{DTC}(X_{S}\mid X_{T})=H(X_{S}\mid X_{T})-\sum_{i\in S}H\big(X_{i}\mid X_{(S\setminus\{i\})\cup T}\big).

(10)

Finally, we use
H(X)
and
H(p_{X})
interchangeably to denote the entropy of
X
, and likewise for other information-theoretic quantities.

3
Main results

In this section, we present our proposed unmasking scheme along with its theoretical guarantee on the sampling error measured by KL divergence.

3.1
Unmasking scheme

We propose a distribution-agnostic randomized unmasking procedure, denoted by
\pi=\pi(K,[L])
, that unmasks a length-
L
sequence over
K
iterations.
For clarity, we describe the scheme in a slightly more general form
\pi(K,\mathcal{I})
, where
\mathcal{I}\subseteq[L]
represents the set of indices that need to be unmasked.

At a high level,
\pi(K,\mathcal{I})
operates recursively. In the first iteration
t=1
, it samples an unmasking set
S^{(1)}\subseteq\mathcal{I}
. It then applies the same procedure
\pi(K-1,\mathcal{I}\setminus S^{(1)})
to the remaining masked positions
\mathcal{I}\setminus S^{(1)}
for the next
K-1
iterations, and generates the unmasking sets
S^{(2)},\ldots,S^{(K)}
sequentially.

The key design choice is how to sample the first unmasking set
S^{(1)}
from
\mathcal{I}
. We do this in two stages:

•

Sample a batch size.
We first draw
\ell=|S^{(1)}|
by assigning weights
w_{\ell}(K,|\mathcal{I}|)
for
\ell\geq 1
.

•

Sample a subset of that size.
Conditioned on
\ell
, we choose
S^{(1)}
uniformly at random among all
\ell
-subsets of
\mathcal{I}
.

The batch-size distribution is governed by a scalar coefficient
f(K,L^{\prime})
(defined for
K\geq 1
and
L^{\prime}\geq K
), which will later appear as the leading factor in our KL sampling error bounds for the unmasking schedule
\pi
.
Importantly, for fixed
(K,|\mathcal{I}|)
, the weights
\{w_{\ell}(K,|\mathcal{I}|)\}_{\ell\geq 1}
depend only on coefficients
f(K-1,\cdot)
from the previous recursion level
K-1
. As a result, the entire schedule can be computed inductively starting from the base case
K=1
.

General framework.

We now formalize the general construction of the unmasking scheme
\pi(K,\mathcal{I})
in detail.
This construction provides a template for a family of randomized unmasking schemes, parameterized by the coefficient
f(K,L^{\prime})
and the associated batch-size weights
\{w_{\ell}(K,|\mathcal{I}|)\}_{\ell\geq 1}
.
We will later specify these quantities in two concrete ways, leading to a TC-adaptive scheme
\pi_{\mathsf{tc}}
and a DTC-adaptive scheme
\pi_{\mathsf{dtc}}
.

Base case (
K=1
).

For any index set
\mathcal{I}
with
|\mathcal{I}|\geq 1
, the scheme
\pi(1,\mathcal{I})
simply unmasks all tokens in
\mathcal{I}
in a single iteration, i.e.,
\mathbb{P}\{S^{(1)}=\mathcal{I}\}=1
.
In addition, we initialize the coefficient
f(1,L^{\prime})
for all
1\leq L^{\prime}\leq L
.

Recursion (
K\geq 2
).

Assume that the scheme
\pi(K-1,\mathcal{J})
has been defined for any index set
\mathcal{J}
with
K-1\leq|\mathcal{J}|\leq L
, and that the coefficient
f(K-1,L^{\prime})
has been defined for all
K-1\leq L^{\prime}\leq L
.
We now construct
\pi(K,\mathcal{I})
for any index set
\mathcal{I}
with
K\leq|\mathcal{I}|\leq L
.
For notational convenience, we denote by
L^{\prime}:=|\mathcal{I}|
the size of set
\mathcal{I}
to be unmasked.

•

Step 1: sample the first unmasking set
S^{(1)}
.

Since we must leave at least
K-1
positions masked for the remaining
K-1
iterations, the first batch size must satisfy
|S^{(1)}|\in\{1,2,\ldots,L^{\prime}-K+1\}
.
We assign each possible size
\ell
a nonnegative weight

\displaystyle w_{\ell}(K,L^{\prime}),\quad\forall\,\ell=1,2,\ldots,L^{\prime}-K+1,

(11)

which is determined by the coefficient
f(K-1,\cdot)
at level
K-1
,
and define the normalizing constant

\displaystyle\Psi(K,L^{\prime})\coloneqq\sum_{\ell=1}^{L^{\prime}-K+1}w_{\ell}(K,L^{\prime}).

(12)

Using these weights, we draw the size of the first unmasking set
|S^{(1)}|
according to

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{|S^{(1)}|=\ell\}=\frac{w_{\ell}(K,L^{\prime})}{\Psi(K,L^{\prime})},\quad\forall\,\ell=1,2,\ldots,L^{\prime}-K+1.

Conditioned on
|S^{(1)}|=\ell
, we then sample
S^{(1)}
uniformly at random among all subsets of
\mathcal{I}
with size
\ell
.

Finally, we compute the level-
K
coefficient
f(K,\cdot)
based on the previously defined
f(K-1,\cdot)
and the normalizing constant
\Psi(K,\cdot)
at level
K
.

•

Step 2: recurse on the remaining indices.

Having sampled the first set
S^{(1)}
, we apply the same procedure to the remaining masked set
\mathcal{I}\setminus S^{(1)}
for the next
K-1
iterations.
Concretely, for
k=2,3,\ldots,K
, we set

\displaystyle\pi^{(k)}(K,\mathcal{I})=\pi^{(k-1)}(K-1,\mathcal{I}\setminus S^{(1)}).

(13)

Remark 1
.

The unmasking scheme
\pi(K,\mathcal{I})
is completely specified by the number of iterations
K
and index set
\mathcal{I}
. In particular, it does not require prior knowledge of the data distribution
p_{\mathsf{data}}
, estimation of information-theoretic quantities such as TC and DTC, or hyperparameter tuning.

Unlike procedures that fix the unmasking sizes
|S^{(1)}|,\dots,|S^{(K)}|
in advance
(
29
;
10
)
, our scheme randomizes the unmasking size at each iteration. As we show in the analysis, this randomization is crucial for adapting to the unknown dependence structure of
p_{\mathsf{data}}
while remaining distribution-agnostic.

TC/DTC-adaptive schemes.

With this general construction in place, we now present two concrete unmasking schemes by specifying the coefficients
f(K,L^{\prime})
and batch-size weights
\{w_{\ell}(K,L^{\prime})\}_{\ell\geq 1}
, which yield sampling convergence guarantees in terms of TC and DTC, respectively.

•

TC-adaptive scheme.

We first introduce the TC-adaptive unmasking scheme, denoted by
\pi_{\mathsf{tc}}(K,\mathcal{I})
.

For
K=1
, we initialize the coefficient
f_{\mathsf{tc}}(1,L^{\prime})
by setting

\displaystyle f_{\mathsf{tc}}(1,1)\coloneqq 0,\qquad\text{and}\qquad f_{\mathsf{tc}}(1,L^{\prime})\coloneqq 1,\quad\forall\,2\leq L^{\prime}\leq L.

(14)

For
K\geq 2
and
L^{\prime}\geq K
, we define the batch-size weights by

\displaystyle w_{1}^{\mathsf{tc}}(K,L^{\prime})

\displaystyle\coloneqq 1,

(15a)

\displaystyle w_{\ell}^{\mathsf{tc}}(K,L^{\prime})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\prod_{i=1}^{\ell-1}\frac{(L^{\prime}-i)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-i)}{1+(L^{\prime}-i-2)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-i-1)},\quad\forall\,\ell=2,\ldots,L^{\prime}-K+1,

(15b)

with the normalizing constant

\displaystyle\Psi_{\mathsf{tc}}(K,L^{\prime})\coloneqq\sum_{\ell=1}^{L^{\prime}-K+1}w_{\ell}^{\mathsf{tc}}(K,L^{\prime}).

(16)

Finally, we update the coefficient
f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K,L^{\prime})
according to

\displaystyle f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K,L^{\prime})\coloneqq 1-\frac{1+(L^{\prime}-2)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-1)}{\Psi_{\mathsf{tc}}(K,L^{\prime})}.

(17)

•

DTC-adaptive scheme.

For the DTC-adaptive scheme, denoted by
\pi_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K,\mathcal{I})
, we initialize the coefficient
f_{\mathsf{dtc}}(1,L^{\prime})
as

\displaystyle f_{\mathsf{dtc}}(1,L^{\prime})\coloneqq\frac{L^{\prime}-1}{L-L^{\prime}+1},\quad\forall\,1\leq L^{\prime}\leq L.

(18)

For
K\geq 2
and
L^{\prime}\geq K
, the batch-size weights are chosen as

\displaystyle w_{1}^{\mathsf{dtc}}(K,L^{\prime})

\displaystyle\coloneqq 1,

(19a)

\displaystyle w_{\ell}^{\mathsf{dtc}}(K,L^{\prime})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\prod_{i=1}^{\ell-1}\frac{(L-L^{\prime}+i)\,f_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-i)}{1+(L-L^{\prime}+i+2)\,f_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-i-1)},\quad\forall\,\ell=2,\ldots,L^{\prime}-K+1,

(19b)

with the normalizing constant

\displaystyle\Psi_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K,L^{\prime})\coloneqq\sum_{\ell=1}^{L^{\prime}-K+1}w_{\ell}^{\mathsf{dtc}}(K,L^{\prime}).

(20)

The coefficient
f_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K,L^{\prime})
is then updated according to

\displaystyle f_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K,L^{\prime})\coloneqq-1+\frac{1+(L-L^{\prime}+2)\,f_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-1)}{\Psi_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K,L^{\prime})}.

(21)

To build some intuition for these two constructed schemes, we plot the empirical mean unmasking size
\mathbb{E}[|S^{(k)}|]
at each iteration
k
in Figure
1
.
As can be seen, the TC-adaptive scheme
\pi_{\mathsf{tc}}
unmasks more tokens in early iterations and progressively fewer later on. In contrast, the DTC-adaptive scheme
\pi_{\mathsf{dtc}}
starts with smaller unmasking sizes and gradually increases the batch size as sampling proceeds.
Intuitively,
\pi_{\mathsf{tc}}
front-loads unmasking because TC primarily penalizes within-batch dependence, while
\pi_{\mathsf{dtc}}
back-loads unmasking because low-DTC often reflects a smaller number of degrees of freedom that, once revealed, reduce the conditional dependence of the remaining tokens and allow larger parallel updates in later iterations.

Finally, we briefly discuss the computational cost of sampling unmasking sets
from the proposed scheme.
After a one-time
O(KL)
dynamic-programming precomputation of
f(K,L^{\prime})
for
K\leq L^{\prime}\leq L
, the batch size
|S^{(k)}|
can be drawn in
O(1+|S^{(k)}|)
time per iteration via inverse-transform sampling, and the corresponding set can be sampled uniformly in overall
O(L)
time (e.g., via reservoir
sampling). Consequently, generating all unmasking sets costs
O(K+L)
time after precomputation, and thus our strategy introduces essentially no additional overhead.

3.2
Theoretical guarantee

We proceed to present the sampling guarantees of the proposed unmasking schemes
\pi_{\mathsf{tc}}
and
\pi_{\mathsf{dtc}}
, stated in terms of the KL divergence between the data distribution
p_{\mathsf{data}}
and the distribution
p_{Y_{\mathsf{out}}}
of the sampled sequence after
K
iterations.

Following prior analysis of DLM sampling convergence
(
29
;
10
)
, it is useful to separate
two sources of error: (i) prediction error due to using an imperfect mask predictor, and (ii) intrinsic sampling error due to parallel decoding, which persists even with access to true conditional marginals.

We begin by quantifying the quality of the learned mask predictor used during the token generation process.
Recall the training objective from (
3
) in Section
2
,
whose optimal solution
p^{\star}
corresponds to the product of the true conditional marginals
p_{i}^{\star}
defined in (
2
).

Definition 1
.

For any mask predictor
\widehat{p}=\prod_{i=1}^{T}\widehat{p}_{i}
, define its prediction error under an unmasking schedule
\pi
as

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\mathsf{pred}}(\pi)

\displaystyle\coloneqq{\mathbb{E}}_{X^{(0)},S,\tau}\Bigg[\frac{L}{|M^{(\tau)}|}\sum_{i\in M^{(\tau)}}\log p_{i}^{\star}\big(X^{(0)}_{i}\mid X^{(\tau)}\big)\Bigg]-{\mathbb{E}}_{X^{(0)},S,\tau}\Bigg[\frac{L}{|M^{(\tau)}|}\sum_{i\in M^{(\tau)}}\log\widehat{p}_{i}\big(X^{(0)}_{i}\mid X^{(\tau)}\big)\Bigg],

(22)

where

M^{(\tau)}=\cup_{k=\tau}^{K}S^{(k)}
is the set of masked tokens at step
\tau
and
X^{(\tau)}=\mathcal{P}_{[L]\setminus M^{(\tau)}}(X^{(0)})
is the corresponding partially masked sequence.
The expectation is taken over the data sample
X^{(0)}\sim p_{\mathsf{data}}
, unmasking sets
S=(S^{(1)},S^{(2)},\ldots,S^{(K)})\sim\pi
, and iteration index
\tau\in[K]
sampled according to

\mathbb{P}\{\tau=k\mid S\}=|S^{(k)}|/{L}
.

Intuitively,
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{pred}}(\pi)
measures the average log-likelihood suboptimality of the learned mask predictor
\widehat{p}
relative to the optimal solution
p^{\star}
, evaluated on the partially masked sequences encountered under schedule
\pi
. When the optimal mask predictor is used, we have
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{pred}}(\pi)=0
for any unmasking strategy
\pi
.

We now state our main results regarding the KL sampling error of our proposed unmasking schemes.
To begin with, Theorem
1
provides the KL sampling error bound of the unmasking scheme
\pi_{\mathsf{tc}}
.

Theorem 1
.

Given any mask predictor
\widehat{p}
, the KL divergence between the data distribution and the sampled distribution produced by the unmasking schedule
\pi_{\mathsf{tc}}(K,[L])

satisfies, for any
2\leq K\leq L
,

\displaystyle\mathsf{KL}(p_{\mathsf{data}}\,\|\,p_{Y_{\mathsf{out}}})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\pi_{\mathsf{tc}}}\big[\mathsf{KL}(p_{\mathsf{data}}\,\|\,p_{Y_{\mathsf{out}}^{S}}\big)\big]\leq\frac{H_{L-K+1}-1}{K+H_{L-K+1}-2}\mathsf{TC}(p_{\mathsf{data}})+\varepsilon_{\mathsf{pred}}(\pi_{\mathsf{tc}}).

(23)

Here,
p_{Y_{\mathsf{out}}^{S}}
is the sampled distribution conditioned on a fixed sequence of unmasking sets
S=(S^{(1)},S^{(2)},\ldots,S^{(K)})
,

H_{n}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{n}i^{-1}
denotes the
n
-th harmonic number for
n\geq 1
, and

\varepsilon_{\mathsf{pred}}(\pi_{\mathsf{tc}})
is the prediction error of the mask predictor
\widehat{p}
defined in (
22
).

The next theorem provides an analogous KL sampling error bound for the DTC-adaptive unmasking scheme
\pi_{\mathsf{dtc}}
.

Theorem 2
.

Given any mask predictor
\widehat{p}
, the KL divergence between the data distribution and the sampled distribution produced by the unmasking schedule
\pi_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K,[L])

satisfies, for any
H_{L-1}<K\leq L
,

\displaystyle\mathsf{KL}(p_{\mathsf{data}}\,\|\,p_{Y_{\mathsf{out}}})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\pi_{\mathsf{dtc}}}\big[\mathsf{KL}(p_{\mathsf{data}}\,\|\,p_{Y_{\mathsf{out}}^{S}}\big)\big]\leq\frac{H_{L-1}}{K-H_{L-1}}\mathsf{DTC}(p_{\mathsf{data}})+\varepsilon_{\mathsf{pred}}(\pi_{\mathsf{dtc}}).

(24)

Here,
p_{Y_{\mathsf{out}}^{S}}
is the sampled distribution conditioned on a fixed sequence of unmasking sets
S=(S^{(1)},S^{(2)},\ldots,S^{(K)})
,
H_{n}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{n}i^{-1}
denotes the
n
-th harmonic number for
n\geq 1
, and

\varepsilon_{\mathsf{pred}}(\pi_{\mathsf{dtc}})
is the prediction error of the mask predictor
\widehat{p}
.

In words, Theorems
1
–
2
demonstrate that the KL sampling error decomposes into two components: (i) a dependence measure
\mathsf{TC}(p_{\mathsf{data}})
or
\mathsf{DTC}(p_{\mathsf{data}})
, which measures the intrinsic complexity of the target distribution
p_{\mathsf{data}}
, and (ii) an estimation component
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{pred}}
that captures the suboptimality of the learned mask predictor.
Notably, our convergence theory covers the practically critical regime
K<L
, where multiple tokens are generated per iteration. This provides theoretical support for the key promise of DLMs—parallel decoding with controlled degradation in sampling accuracy.

We now discuss several important implications.

•

Iteration complexity.

Since
\log n\leq H_{n}\leq 1+\log n
for all
n\geq 1
, the sampling convergence rate of
\pi_{\mathsf{tc}}
in (
23
) can be simplified to

\displaystyle\frac{\mathsf{TC}(p_{\mathsf{data}})}{K+\log(L-K+1)-2}\log(L-K+1)\leq\frac{\mathsf{TC}(p_{\mathsf{data}})}{K}\log L

for any
K\leq L-7
.
In particular, when the optimal mask predictor is used (i.e.,
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{pred}}=0
), the number of sampling iterations required to achieve
\varepsilon
-accuracy in KL divergence is at most

\displaystyle\frac{\mathsf{TC}(p_{\mathsf{data}})}{\varepsilon}\log L.

Similarly, the sampling error bound in (
24
) demonstrates that the iteration complexity of
\pi_{\mathsf{dtc}}
to achieve
\varepsilon
-accuracy in KL divergence is upper bounded by

\displaystyle\bigg(\frac{\mathsf{DTC}(p_{\mathsf{data}})}{\varepsilon}+1\bigg)\big(\log(L-1)+1\big)

As a consequence, when
\min\{\mathsf{TC}(p_{\mathsf{data}}),\mathsf{DTC}(p_{\mathsf{data}})\}\ll L
, our schedule yields a substantial speedup over one-by-one AR decoding and DLM unmasking schemes that requires
\Theta(L)
iterations.

•

Adaptivity to intrinsic dependence.

Notably, such sampling acceleration is achieved without requiring prior knowledge of the data distribution or hyperparameter tuning. This reveals the feasibility of dependence-adaptive DLM sampling that automatically exploits the low-complexity structure of data to achieve faster convergence.

•

Improvement over fixed-size schedules.
The work of
10
shows that there exists a distribution
p_{0}
such that, even with access to the true conditional marginals, any unmasking strategy with a fixed-size schedule requires at least
\Omega(\varepsilon^{-1}\min\{\mathsf{TC}(p_{0}),\mathsf{DTC}(p_{0})\}\log L)
iterations to achieve
\varepsilon
-accuracy in KL divergence.
Therefore, our randomized unmasking schemes can be more efficient than fixed-size schedules in the worst case.

•

Unmasking schedule choice.
Our results suggest a simple guideline for choosing the unmasking schedule:

\pi_{\mathsf{tc}}
is preferable when TC is the smaller intrinsic complexity measure, while
\pi_{\mathsf{dtc}}
is preferable when DTC is smaller. More broadly, the two schedules provide complementary adaptivity guarantees, offering a principled way to match the DLM sampler to the dominant dependence structure of the target distribution.

Finally, we discuss representative settings where TC and DTC are significantly smaller than the ambient sequence length
L
, which illustrates the practical relevance of our results.

•

Low-TC distributions.

Consider the parity (checksum) distribution of
X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{L})
where
X_{1},\dots,X_{L-1}
are i.i.d.
\mathsf{Unif}(\{0,1\})
random variables and

X_{L}=X_{1}\oplus X_{2}\oplus\cdots\oplus X_{L-1}
.
It is easy to compute
H(X_{i})=1
for
i\in[L]
and
H(X)=L-1
.
Since each variable is determined by the remaining
L-1
variables, we have

\mathsf{TC}(X)=\sum_{i=1}^{L}H(X_{i})-H(X)=1
,
\mathsf{DTC}(X)=H(X)-\sum_{i=1}^{L}H\big(X_{i}\mid X_{-i}\big)=L-1
, and
\sum_{i=1}^{L}I(X_{i};X_{-i})=L
.
This example illustrates that the distribution with a weak “checksum-like” constraint has very small TC.
More generally, small TC arises in distributions that are close to independent except for a limited amount of shared information, or a small number of global constraints.

•

Low-DTC distributions.

Consider a latent vector
U\in\{0,1\}^{d}
with i.i.d.
\mathsf{Unif}(\{0,1\})
entries, and let

X\;=\;GU\in\{0,1\}^{L},

where
G\in\{0,1\}^{L\times d}
has rank
d
(over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
). Then
X
is uniform over a

d
-dimensional linear subspace of
\{0,1\}^{L}
, and hence
H(X)=d
. By the definition

\mathsf{DTC}(X)=H(X)-\sum_{i=1}^{L}H(X_{i}\mid X_{-i})
, we immediately obtain

\mathsf{DTC}(X)\leq H(X)=d.

In particular, when
d\ll L
, the distribution has small DTC even though it may exhibit strong
global constraints. For instance, if every coordinate
X_{i}
is a nontrivial linear function of
U

(so that
H(X_{i})=1
for all
i
), then

\mathsf{TC}(X)=\sum_{i=1}^{L}H(X_{i})-H(X)=L-d

can be large. More generally, small DTC arises in distributions driven by a low-dimensional latent source (e.g., low-rank or manifold structure), where the intrinsic degrees of freedom are much smaller than the sequence length
L
.
We refer to
13
for a detailed discussion of distributions with small DTC.

Analysis

This section outlines the proof strategy for Theorem
1
and Theorem
2
.

4.1
Analysis for TC-adaptive scheme (Proof of Theorem
1
)

Step 1: Decoupling prediction and sampling errors.

We first separate the error due to an imperfectly learned mask predictor from the error that is intrinsic to parallel unmasking.

To this end, we introduce an auxiliary (idealized) sequence
(\overline{Y}^{(0)},\overline{Y}^{(1)},\dots,\overline{Y}^{(K)})
, which uses the same unmasking strategy
\pi_{\mathsf{tc}}(K,[L])
as the practical sampler
Y
, but replace the learned mask predictor
\widehat{p}
with the optimal mask predictor
p^{\star}
(the product of true conditional marginals under
p_{\mathsf{data}}
).

Similar to
Y
, we denote by
\overline{Y}_{\mathsf{out}}\coloneqq\overline{Y}^{(K)}
the output of the idealized sampler and write
\overline{Y}_{\mathsf{out}}=\overline{Y}_{\mathsf{out}}^{S}
when we need to highlight the dependence on the unmasking sets
S=(S^{(1)},S^{(2)},\ldots,S^{(K)})
.

As shown in
29
, the difference between the expected KL errors of
Y_{\mathsf{out}}
and
\overline{Y}_{\mathsf{out}}
is exactly the prediction error
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{pred}}
defined in (
22
):

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\pi_{\mathsf{tc}}}\Big[\mathsf{KL}\big(p_{X^{(0)}}\,\|\,p_{Y_{\mathsf{out}}^{S}})\Big]-\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\pi_{\mathsf{tc}}}\Big[\mathsf{KL}(p_{X^{(0)}}\,\|\,p_{\overline{Y}_{\mathsf{out}}^{S}}\big)\Big]=\varepsilon_{\mathsf{pred}}(\pi_{\mathsf{tc}}),

(25)

For completeness, we include a proof in Appendix
B.1
.
Consequently, it suffices to analyze the expected KL sampling error of the output of the idealized sampler
\overline{Y}_{\mathsf{out}}
.

In what follows, we write
X=X^{(0)}
and
\overline{Y}=\overline{Y}_{\mathsf{out}}=\overline{Y}^{(K)}
for convenience of presentation, so that
p_{X}=p_{\mathsf{data}}
.

Step 2: Recursive decomposition of KL error.

We first notice that when a set of tokens is generated independently using a product of conditional marginals, the resulting discrepancy from the true joint distribution is precisely captured by the TC of those tokens.
This is formalized in the following lemma, whose proof can be found in Appendix
B.2
.

Lemma 1
.

Fix an arbitrary masked index set
\mathcal{I}\subseteq[L]
and condition on the already generated tokens
X_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}=x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}
. For any set
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{I}
, the KL divergence between the true joint conditional distribution and the sampled distribution of a single-step sampler for tokens in
\mathcal{S}
satisfies

\mathsf{KL}\Big(p_{X_{\mathcal{S}}\mid X_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}}(\cdot\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\,\big\|\,\prod_{i\in\mathcal{S}}p_{X_{i}\mid X_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}}(\cdot\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\Big)=\mathsf{TC}\big(p_{X_{\mathcal{S}}\mid X_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}}(\cdot\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\big).

Our key observation is that the expected KL sampling error of
\overline{Y}
admits a clean recursion across iterations. After the first batch is unmasked, the remaining
K-1
steps form the same problem on the remaining positions, conditioned on what has already been unmasked.
This yields the following recursive decomposition; its proof is deferred to Appendix
A.2
.

Lemma 2
.

Fix an arbitrary unmasking scheme
\pi
and masked index set
\mathcal{I}
with
K\leq|\mathcal{I}|\leq L
.
Conditioned on the already unmasked tokens
X_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}=x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\pi(K,\mathcal{I})}\Big[\mathsf{KL}\big(p_{X_{\mathcal{I}}\mid X_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}}(\cdot\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\,\|\,p_{\overline{Y}_{\mathcal{I}}^{S}\mid\overline{Y}_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{c}}}}(\cdot\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\big)\Big]

\displaystyle\quad=\mathbb{E}_{S^{(1)}}\Big[\mathsf{TC}\big(p_{X_{S^{(1)}}\mid X_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}}(\cdot\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\big)\Big]

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbb{E}_{S^{(1)},x_{S^{(1)}}}\bigg[\mathbb{E}_{S^{(2:K)}}\Big[\mathsf{KL}\Big(p_{X_{\mathcal{I}\setminus S^{(1)}}\mid X_{S^{(1)}\cup\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}}(\cdot\mid x_{S^{(1)}\cup\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\,\big\|\,p_{\overline{Y}_{\mathcal{I}\setminus S^{(1)}}^{S^{(2:K)}}\mid\overline{Y}_{S^{(1)}\cup\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}}(\cdot\mid x_{S^{(1)}\cup\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\Big)\Big]\bigg],

where the expectation on the second line is over
S^{(1)}\sim\pi^{(1)}(K,\mathcal{I})
,
x_{S^{(1)}}\sim p_{X_{S^{(1)}}\mid X_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}}(\cdot\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})
, and
S^{(2:K)}\coloneqq(S^{(2)},S^{(3)},\ldots,S^{(K)})\sim\pi(K-1,\mathcal{I}\setminus S^{(1)})
.

We remind the reader of the notation that the superscript
S
in
\overline{Y}^{S}
highlights the dependence on the fixed sequence of unmasking sets
S=(S^{(1)},S^{(2)},\ldots,S^{(K)})
. When we apply the schedule
\pi(K,\mathcal{I})
to unmask tokens in
\mathcal{I}
,
\overline{Y}_{\mathcal{I}}^{S}
depends on the unmasking sets
S
and
\overline{Y}_{\mathcal{I}\setminus S^{(1)}}^{S^{(2:K)}}
depends on the unmasking sets
S^{(2:K)}
.

Step 3: Exact characterization of KL error in terms of TC.

With this recursion in hand, we show that our specific choice of the first-step size distribution in
\pi_{\mathsf{tc}}
makes the overall expected KL sampling error proportional to the TC of the remaining conditional distribution.
Particularly, the proportionality factor is precisely the coefficient
f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K,|\mathcal{I}|)
defined in (
17
).

This is formalized in the following lemma, whose proof is deferred to Appendix
A.3
.

Lemma 3
.

For any masked index set
\mathcal{I}\subseteq[L]
with
K\leq|\mathcal{I}|\leq L
, conditioned on the already unmasked tokens
X_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}=x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}
, the unmasking scheme
\pi_{\mathsf{tc}}(K,\mathcal{I})
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\pi_{\mathsf{tc}}(K,\mathcal{I})}\Big[\mathsf{KL}\Big(p_{X_{\mathcal{I}}\mid X_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{c}}}}(\cdot\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\,\|\,p_{\overline{Y}^{S}_{\mathcal{I}}\mid\overline{Y}_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{c}}}}(\cdot\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\Big)\Big]=f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K,|\mathcal{I}|)\cdot\mathsf{TC}\big(p_{X_{\mathcal{I}}\mid X_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{c}}}}(\cdot\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\big).

(26)

In particular, by setting
\mathcal{I}=[L]
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\pi_{\mathsf{tc}}(K,[L])}\Big[\mathsf{KL}\big(p_{\mathsf{data}}\,\|\,p_{\overline{Y}^{S}_{\mathsf{out}}}\big)\Big]=f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K,L)\cdot\mathsf{TC}(p_{\mathsf{data}}).

(27)

We now briefly outline the proof of Lemma
3
, which proceeds by induction on the number of iterations
K
.

The base case
K=1
is immediate since the scheme unmasks all tokens at once, and the KL sampling error reduces to the TC of the conditional distribution.

Now suppose the claim holds for
K-1
. Combining Lemma
2
with the induction hypothesis, we can express the target error,

\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\pi_{\mathsf{tc}}(K,\mathcal{I})}\big[\mathsf{KL}(p_{X_{\mathcal{I}}}(\cdot\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\,\|\,p_{\overline{Y}_{\mathcal{I}}^{S}\mid\overline{Y}_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{c}}}}(\cdot\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}))\big]
,
as an expectation over the first unmasking set
S^{(1)}
consisting of two contributions: (i) the TC incurred when unmasking the first batch, and (ii) the remaining
(K-1)
-step error, which by the induction hypothesis reduces to a scaled TC of the residual problem. Concretely, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{S^{(1)}}\Big[\mathsf{TC}\big(p_{X_{S^{(1)}}\mid X_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{c}}}}(\cdot\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\big)\Big]+f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,|\mathcal{I}|-|S^{(1)}|)\cdot\mathbb{E}_{x_{S^{(1)}}}\Big[\mathsf{TC}\big(p_{X_{\mathcal{I}\setminus S^{(1)}}\mid X_{S^{(1)}\cup\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}}(\cdot\mid x_{S^{(1)}\cup\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\big)\Big],

where the second expectation is over
x_{S^{(1)}}\sim p_{X_{S^{(1)}}\mid X_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}}(\cdot\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})
.

Recall that our strategy first randomly selects the size
|S^{(1)}|
and then chooses
S^{(1)}
uniformly at random among all subsets of that size.
Hence, by the definition of TC, this expectation can be written as a weighted sum of entropies of subsequences of
X
, indexed by the possible first-step sizes
\ell=|S^{(1)}|
:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Psi_{\mathsf{tc}}(K,|\mathcal{I}|)}\sum_{\ell=1}^{|\mathcal{I}|-K+1}w_{\ell}^{\mathsf{tc}}(K,|\mathcal{I}|)a_{\ell},

(28)

where weights
w_{\ell}^{\mathsf{tc}}(K,|\mathcal{I}|)
and normalizing constant
\Psi_{\mathsf{tc}}(K,|\mathcal{I}|)
are defined in (
15
) and (
16
), respectively, and
a_{\ell}
is a linear combination of entropies of
\ell
- and
(\ell+1)
-subsets, given by

\displaystyle\frac{\ell}{|\mathcal{I}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}H\big(p_{X_{i}\mid X_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}}(\cdot\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\big)-f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,|\mathcal{I}|-\ell)\cdot H\big(p_{X_{\mathcal{I}}\mid X_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}}(\cdot\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\big)

\displaystyle\quad-\alpha_{\ell}\sum_{\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{I}\,:\,|\mathcal{A}|=\ell}H\big(p_{X_{\mathcal{A}}\mid X_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}}(\cdot\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\big)+\beta_{\ell}\sum_{\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{I}\,:\,|\mathcal{B}|=\ell+1}H\big(p_{X_{\mathcal{B}}\mid X_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}}(\cdot\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\big)

for some coefficients
\alpha_{\ell},\beta_{\ell}
(exact expressions can be found in Appendix
A.3
) depending on
f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,|\mathcal{I}|-\ell)
, which are used to determine the weights
w_{\ell}^{\mathsf{tc}}(K,|\mathcal{I}|)
.

If the unmasking size
\ell=|S^{(1)}|
are fixed deterministically, these subset-entropy terms generally do not cancel and one is left with distribution-specific residual terms. In contrast, the chosen weights
\{w^{\mathsf{tc}}_{\ell}\}_{\ell\geq 1}
over possible sizes in our schedule make these subset-entropy contributions telescope across
\ell
, leaving precisely the TC term,

\mathsf{TC}\big(p_{X_{\mathcal{I}}\mid X_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}}(\cdot\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\big)=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}H\big(p_{X_{i}\mid X_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}}(\cdot\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\big)-H\big(p_{X_{\mathcal{I}}\mid X_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}}(\cdot\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\big),

multiplied by the coefficient
f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K,|\mathcal{I}|)
.
Therefore, this telescoping mechanism is the core reason that our randomized unmasking strategy enables distribution-agnostic adaptivity.

This completes the induction and establishes Lemma
3
.

Step 4: Bounding coefficient
f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K,L)
.

By (
27
), it remains to bound the coefficient
f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K,L)
. This is established by the following lemma; with proof deferred to Appendix
A.4
.

Lemma 4
.

For any
K\geq 2
and
K\leq L^{\prime}\leq L
, the coefficient
f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K,L^{\prime})
satisfies

\displaystyle f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K,L^{\prime})\leq\frac{H_{L^{\prime}-K+1}-1}{K+H_{L^{\prime}-K+1}-2}.

(29)

where
H_{n}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{n}i^{-1}
denotes the
n
-th harmonic number.
In particular, one has

f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K,L)\leq\frac{H_{L-K+1}-1}{K+H_{L-K+1}-2}.

(30)

To conclude, combining (
25
), (
27
), and (
30
) yields Theorem
1
.

4.2
Analysis for DTC-adaptive scheme (Proof of Theorem
2
)

We proceed to present the proof of the convergence theory of the DTC-adaptive scheme
\pi_{\mathsf{dtc}}
in Theorem
2
. It follows a similar high-level structure as the TC case in Section
4.1
.
Therefore, we only highlight the key differences in the analysis, while omitting details that are essentially the same as in the TC case.

In Step 1, we decouple the prediction error from the parallel sampling errors via (
25
).

In Step 2, we invoke the recursive decomposition of the KL sampling error (Lemma
2
), aiming to relate the KL error to the TC and DTC of the remaining conditional distribution.

The main technical difference in the DTC case arises in the following Step 3.

Step 3: Characterization of KL error in terms of TC and DTC.

Unlike the TC-adaptive scheme
\pi_{\mathsf{tc}}
, the expected KL sampling error of the DTC-adaptive scheme
\pi_{\mathsf{dtc}}
generally cannot be characterized solely in terms of DTC.
This is natural since the one-step error incurred in the first iteration is exactly the TC of the tokens unmasked in the first batch (see Lemma
1
), which generally cannot be tightly controlled by DTC alone.
Instead, we show that the choice of the first-batch size distribution in
\pi_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K,\mathcal{I})
yields an expected KL error controlled by a linear combination of TC and DTC of the remaining conditional distribution.
Additionally, the proportionality factor is given by
f_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K,|\mathcal{I}|)
defined in (
21
).
In particular, when
\mathcal{I}=[L]
, the TC term vanishes and the KL sampling error becomes proportional to the DTC of the data distribution.

This is summarized by the following lemma; with proof deferred to Appendix
A.5
.

Lemma 5
.

For any masked index set
\mathcal{I}\subseteq[L]
with
K\leq|\mathcal{I}|\leq L
, conditioned on the already unmasked tokens
X_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}=x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}
, the DTC-adaptive unmasking scheme
\pi_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K,\mathcal{I})
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\pi_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K,\mathcal{I})}\Big[\mathsf{KL}\big(p_{X_{\mathcal{I}}\mid X_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{c}}}}(\cdot\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\,\|\,p_{\overline{Y}_{\mathcal{I}}^{S}\mid\overline{Y}_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{c}}}}(\cdot\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\big)\Big]

\displaystyle\quad\leq f_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K,|\mathcal{I}|)\bigg(\frac{L-|\mathcal{I}|}{|\mathcal{I}|}\cdot\mathsf{TC}\big(p_{X_{\mathcal{I}}\mid X_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{c}}}}(\cdot\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\big)+\frac{L}{|\mathcal{I}|}\cdot\mathsf{DTC}\big(p_{X_{\mathcal{I}}\mid X_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{c}}}}(\cdot\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\big)\bigg).

(31)

In particular, when
\mathcal{I}=[L]
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\pi_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K,[L])}\big[\mathsf{KL}(p_{\mathsf{data}}\,\|\,p_{\overline{Y}^{S}_{\mathsf{out}}})\big]\leq f_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K,L)\cdot\mathsf{DTC}(p_{\mathsf{data}}).

(32)

The proof strategy of Lemma
5
follows the same inductive template as Lemma
3
for the TC-adaptive scheme. Concretely, we express the target KL error as a weighted sum of entropies of subsequences of
X
. The key difference is that the weights
\{w_{\ell}^{\mathsf{dtc}}\}_{\ell\geq 1}
are designed to make these subset-entropy terms telescope into a linear combination of TC and DTC, rather than collapsing to DTC alone, as one might hope by direct analogy with the TC case.

Step 4: Bounding coefficient
f_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K,L)
.

Equipped with (
32
), we are left to control the coefficient
f_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K,L)
. This is established by the following lemma; with proof deferred to Appendix
A.6
.

Lemma 6
.

For any
K\geq 2
and
K\leq L^{\prime}\leq L
, the coefficient
f_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K,L^{\prime})
satisfies

\displaystyle f_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K,L^{\prime})\leq\frac{\min\{L^{\prime}-K,\frac{L}{K}(H_{L-1}-H_{L-L^{\prime}})\}}{L-\min\{L^{\prime}-K,\frac{L}{K}(H_{L-1}-H_{L-L^{\prime}})\}}.

(33)

where
H_{n}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{n}i^{-1}
denotes the
n
-th harmonic number and we set
H_{0}\coloneqq 0
for convenience.
In particular, for
K>H_{L-1}
, we have

f_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K,L)\leq\frac{\min\{L^{\prime}-K,\frac{L}{K}H_{L-1}\}}{L-\min\{L^{\prime}-K,\frac{L}{K}H_{L-1}\}}\leq\frac{H_{L-1}}{K-H_{L-1}}.

(34)

Putting (
25
), (
32
), and (
34
) together finishes the proof of Theorem
2
.

6
Discussion

This work has made progress toward a theoretical understanding of how DLMs can exploit intrinsic dependence structure to accelerate sampling.
Our main finding is that, without requiring distribution-specific knowledge or tuning, one can design a distribution-agnostic unmasking schedule that adapts to unknown structure and achieves minimal sampling error governed by TC, an information-theoretic measure of global dependence.
Beyond the convergence theory, our result highlights a concrete algorithmic principle: randomizing the unmasking sizes across iterations can enable structure-adaptive performance.

Before concluding, we point out several directions for future investigation.
First, it is natural to ask whether one can design an adaptive unmasking schedule that is simultaneously optimal in the small-TC and small-DTC regimes. This would sharpen our understanding of sampling acceleration for DLMs and potentially yield a single schedule that performs well across a wider range of distributions.
Second, our theory treats the mask predictor as given, whereas in practice it is trained under a particular masking distribution and then deployed under an inference-time schedule.
Thus developing an end-to-end analysis that couples training with sampling serves as an important research direction.
Third, our schedule chooses which positions to unmask uniformly at random conditioned on the unmasking size, while practical samplers often use state-dependent heuristics (e.g., confidence/entropy) to pick positions.
It would be valuable to characterize the sampling performance of such heuristics theoretically.

\pi_{\mathsf{tc}}(K,\mathcal{I})
decomposes as:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\pi_{\mathsf{tc}}(K,\mathcal{I})}\Big[\mathsf{KL}\big(p_{X_{\mathcal{I}}}(\cdot\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\|p_{\overline{Y}_{\mathcal{I}}^{S}}(\cdot\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\big)\Big]=\mathbb{E}_{S^{(1)}\sim\pi^{(1)}_{\mathsf{TC}}(K,\mathcal{I})}\big[\mathsf{TC}(X_{S^{(1)}}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\big]

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}_{S^{(1)}\sim\pi^{(1)}_{\mathsf{TC}}(K,\mathcal{I}),\,x_{S^{(1)}}\sim p_{X_{S^{(1)}}}(\cdot\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})}\Big[\mathbb{E}_{S^{(2:K)}\sim\pi_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,\mathcal{I}\setminus S^{(1)})}\big[\mathsf{KL}\big(p_{X_{\mathcal{I}\setminus S^{(1)}}}(\cdot\mid x_{S^{(1)}\cup\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\|p_{\overline{Y}_{\mathcal{I}\setminus S^{(1)}}^{S^{(2:K)}}}(\cdot\mid x_{S^{(1)}\cup\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\big)\big]\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{S^{(1)}\sim\pi^{(1)}_{\mathsf{TC}}(K,\mathcal{I}),x_{S^{(1)}}\sim p_{X_{S^{(1)}}}(\cdot\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})}\big[\mathsf{TC}(X_{S^{(1)}}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})+f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-|S^{(1)}|)\mathsf{TC}(X_{\mathcal{I}\setminus S^{(1)}}\mid x_{S^{(1)}\cup\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{S^{(1)}\sim\pi^{(1)}_{\mathsf{tc}}(K,\mathcal{I})}\big[\mathsf{TC}(X_{S^{(1)}}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})+f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-|S^{(1)}|)\mathsf{TC}(X_{\mathcal{I}\setminus S^{(1)}}\mid X_{S^{(1)}},x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\big],

(35)

where the first equality comes from Lemma
2
, the second applies the inductive assumption, and the third exploits the definitions of conditional total correlation and conditional entropy.

To evaluate the right-hand side of (
35
), recall the two-stage construction of the first unmasking set
S^{(1)}
: its cardinality
\ell=|S^{(1)}|
is drawn with probability
w^{\mathsf{tc}}_{\ell}(K,L^{\prime})/\Psi^{\mathsf{tc}}(K,L^{\prime})
for
\ell=1,2,\ldots,L^{\prime}-K+1
, after which
S^{(1)}
is selected uniformly at random from all size-
\ell
subsets of
\mathcal{I}
.
Accordingly, we introduce
a_{\ell}=a_{\ell}(K,L^{\prime})
, the average over all such size-
\ell
subsets, for each
1\leq\ell\leq L^{\prime}-K+1
:

\displaystyle a_{\ell}(K,L^{\prime})\coloneqq\frac{1}{\binom{L^{\prime}}{\ell}}\sum_{S^{(1)}\subseteq\mathcal{I}:|S^{(1)}|=\ell}\Big(\mathsf{TC}\big(X_{S^{(1)}}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}\big)+f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-\ell)\mathsf{TC}(X_{\mathcal{I}\setminus S^{(1)}}\mid X_{S^{(1)}},x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\Big).

(36)

The expected KL sampling error from (
35
) can then be written as the following weighted sum

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\pi_{\mathsf{tc}}(K,\mathcal{I})}\Big[\mathsf{KL}\big(p_{X_{\mathcal{I}}}(\cdot\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\|p_{\overline{Y}_{\mathcal{I}}^{S}}(\cdot\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\big)\Big]=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L^{\prime}-K+1}\mathbb{P}\{|S|=\ell\}\cdot a_{\ell}=\frac{1}{\Psi^{\mathsf{tc}}(K,L^{\prime})}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L^{\prime}-K+1}w^{\mathsf{tc}}_{\ell}a_{\ell}.

(37)

Our task therefore reduces to bounding the weighted sum
\sum_{\ell=1}^{L^{\prime}-K+1}w^{\mathsf{tc}}_{\ell}a_{\ell}
.

Step 2: Express
\sum_{\ell}w^{\mathsf{tc}}_{\ell}a_{\ell}
as a telescoped sum.

Expanding the total correlation and conditional total correlation in their entropic forms, we can reformulate
a_{\ell}
from (
36
) as:

\displaystyle a_{\ell}

\displaystyle\overset{(\mathrm{i})}{=}\frac{1}{\binom{L^{\prime}}{\ell}}\sum_{S^{(1)}\subseteq\mathcal{I}:|S^{(1)}|=\ell}\Bigg(\sum_{i\in S^{(1)}}H(X_{i}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})-H(X_{S^{(1)}}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})

\displaystyle\quad\quad+f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-\ell)\bigg(\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}\setminus S^{(1)}}H(X_{j}\mid X_{S^{(1)}},x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})-H(X_{\mathcal{I}\setminus S^{(1)}}\mid X_{S^{(1)}},x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\bigg)\Bigg)

\displaystyle\overset{(\mathrm{ii})}{=}\frac{1}{\binom{L^{\prime}}{\ell}}\sum_{S^{(1)}\subseteq\mathcal{I}:|S^{(1)}|=\ell}\Bigg(\sum_{i\in S^{(1)}}H(X_{i}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})-H(X_{S^{(1)}}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})

\displaystyle\quad\quad+f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-\ell)\bigg(\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}\setminus S^{(1)}}H(X_{\{j\}\cup S^{(1)}}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})-(L^{\prime}-\ell-1)H(X_{S^{(1)}}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})-H(X_{\mathcal{I}}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\bigg)\Bigg)

\displaystyle\overset{(\mathrm{iii})}{=}\frac{\ell}{L^{\prime}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}H(X_{i}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})-f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-\ell)H(X_{\mathcal{I}}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})

\displaystyle\quad-\underbrace{\frac{1+(L^{\prime}-\ell-1)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-\ell)}{\binom{L^{\prime}}{\ell}}}_{=:\alpha_{\ell}}\sum_{S^{(1)}\subseteq\mathcal{I}:|S^{(1)}|=\ell}H(X_{S^{(1)}}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\frac{(\ell+1)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-\ell)}{\binom{L^{\prime}}{\ell}}}_{=:\beta_{\ell}}\sum_{T\subseteq\mathcal{I}:|T|=\ell+1}H(X_{S^{(1)}}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}).

(38)

Here, (i) expands the total correlation and conditional total correlation into sums of entropies; (ii) applies the standard identity
H(X\mid Y)=H(X,Y)-H(Y)
; (iii) relies on a double-counting argument:
\sum_{S^{(1)}\subseteq\mathcal{I}:|S^{(1)}|=\ell}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}\setminus S^{(1)}}H(X_{\{j\}\cup S^{(1)}}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})=(\ell+1)\sum_{S^{(1)}\subseteq\mathcal{I}:|S^{(1)}|=\ell+1}H(X_{T}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})
, because each
(\ell+1)
-element set
T
is obtained by adjoining any one of its
\ell+1
members to its complement subset.

For the entropy sums in
a_{\ell}
to cancel telescopically upon forming the weighted combination
\sum_{\ell=1}^{L^{\prime}-K+1}w^{\mathsf{tc}}_{\ell}a_{\ell}
, we require that the weights
w^{\mathsf{tc}}_{\ell}
satisfy

\displaystyle w^{\mathsf{tc}}_{\ell}\beta_{\ell}=w^{\mathsf{tc}}_{\ell+1}\alpha_{\ell+1},\quad\forall\,l=1,2,\ldots,L^{\prime}-K.

This telescoping requirement yields the recurrence

\displaystyle w^{\mathsf{tc}}_{\ell+1}=w^{\mathsf{tc}}_{\ell}\frac{(L^{\prime}-\ell)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-\ell)}{1+(L^{\prime}-\ell-2)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-\ell-1)},\quad\forall\,l=1,2,\ldots,L^{\prime}-K.

(39)

Initializing with
w_{1}=1
and iterating, we arrive at

\displaystyle w^{\mathsf{tc}}_{\ell}=\prod_{i=1}^{\ell-1}\frac{(L^{\prime}-i)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-i)}{1+(L^{\prime}-i-2)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-i-1)},\quad\forall\,l=1,2,\ldots,L^{\prime}-K+1,

(40)

which coincides precisely with the weight formula specified in (
15
).

Step 3: Compute entropy coefficients.

Having established (
38
), the next task is to determine the entropy coefficients that appear in the weighted sum
\sum_{\ell=1}^{L^{\prime}-K+1}w^{\mathsf{tc}}_{\ell}a_{\ell}
.

•

We start by evaluating the coefficient multiplying
-H(X_{\mathcal{I}}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})
, namely
\sum_{\ell=1}^{L^{\prime}-K+1}w^{\mathsf{tc}}_{\ell}f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-\ell)
. The key observation is the following rearrangement of
f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-\ell)
:

\displaystyle f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-\ell)=

\displaystyle\frac{(L^{\prime}-\ell)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-\ell)}{1+(L^{\prime}-\ell-2)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-\ell-1)}\Big(1+(L^{\prime}-\ell-2)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-\ell-1)\Big)-(L^{\prime}-\ell-1)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-\ell).

Applying this identity, the coefficient evaluates to:

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{L^{\prime}-K+1}w^{\mathsf{tc}}_{\ell}f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-\ell)\overset{(\mathrm{i})}{=}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L^{\prime}-K}w^{\mathsf{tc}}_{\ell}f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-\ell)

\displaystyle\overset{(\mathrm{ii})}{=}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L^{\prime}-K}w^{\mathsf{tc}}_{\ell}\bigg(\frac{(L^{\prime}-\ell)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-\ell)}{1+(L^{\prime}-\ell-2)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-\ell-1)}-(L^{\prime}-\ell-1)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-\ell)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+\frac{(L^{\prime}-\ell)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-\ell)}{1+(L^{\prime}-\ell-2)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-\ell-1)}(L^{\prime}-\ell-2)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-\ell-1)\bigg)

\displaystyle\overset{(\mathrm{iii})}{=}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L^{\prime}-K}w^{\mathsf{tc}}_{\ell+1}-\sum_{\ell=1}^{L^{\prime}-K}\Big(w^{\mathsf{tc}}_{\ell}(L^{\prime}-\ell-1)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-\ell)-w^{\mathsf{tc}}_{\ell+1}\big(L^{\prime}-(\ell+1)-1\big)f\big(K-1,L^{\prime}-(\ell+1)\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L^{\prime}-K}w^{\mathsf{tc}}_{\ell+1}-\Big(w_{1}(L^{\prime}-2)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-1)-w^{\mathsf{tc}}_{L^{\prime}-K+1}(K-2)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,K-1)\Big)

\displaystyle\overset{(\mathrm{iv})}{=}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L^{\prime}-K+1}w^{\mathsf{tc}}_{\ell}-1-(L^{\prime}-2)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-1).

(41)

Here, (i) follows from the vanishing property
f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K,K)=0
for every
K\geq 1
; (ii) substitutes the rearrangement of
f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-\ell)
derived above;
(iii) invokes the weight definition in (
40
) and reorganizes into a telescoping sum; (iv) applies the initialization
w_{1}=1
together with
f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K,K)=0
.

•

It remains to handle the coefficient of
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}H(X_{i}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})
appearing in (
38
). Using the expression for
\alpha_{1}
from (
38
), the coefficient can be computed as:

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{L^{\prime}-K+1}w^{\mathsf{tc}}_{\ell}\frac{\ell}{L^{\prime}}-w_{1}\frac{1+(L^{\prime}-2)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-1)}{L^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L^{\prime}-K+1}w^{\mathsf{tc}}_{\ell}-\frac{1}{L^{\prime}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L^{\prime}-K+1}w^{\mathsf{tc}}_{\ell}(L^{\prime}-\ell)-w_{1}\frac{1+(L^{\prime}-2)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-1)}{L^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(i)}}}{{=}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L^{\prime}-K+1}w^{\mathsf{tc}}_{\ell}

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{L^{\prime}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L^{\prime}-K}\Big(w^{\mathsf{tc}}_{\ell}(L^{\prime}-\ell)\big(1+(L^{\prime}-\ell-1)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-\ell)\big)-w^{\mathsf{tc}}_{\ell}(L^{\prime}-\ell)(L^{\prime}-\ell-1)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-\ell)\Big)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{L^{\prime}}w^{\mathsf{tc}}_{L^{\prime}-K+1}(K-1)-w_{1}\frac{1+(L^{\prime}-2)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-1)}{L^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(ii)}}}{{=}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L^{\prime}-K+1}w^{\mathsf{tc}}_{\ell}-\frac{1}{L^{\prime}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L^{\prime}-K}\Big(w^{\mathsf{tc}}_{\ell}(L^{\prime}-\ell)\big(1+(L^{\prime}-\ell-1)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-\ell)\big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-w^{\mathsf{tc}}_{\ell+1}\big(L^{\prime}-(\ell+1)\big)\big[1+\big(L^{\prime}-(\ell+1)-1\big)f\big(K-1,L^{\prime}-(\ell+1)\big)\big]\Big)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{L^{\prime}}w^{\mathsf{tc}}_{L^{\prime}-K+1}(K-1)-w_{1}\frac{1+(L^{\prime}-2)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-1)}{L^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(iii)}}}{{=}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L^{\prime}-K+1}w^{\mathsf{tc}}_{\ell}

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{L^{\prime}}\Big(w_{1}(L^{\prime}-1)\big(1+(L^{\prime}-2)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-1)\big)-w^{\mathsf{tc}}_{L^{\prime}-K+1}(K-1)\big(1+(K-2)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,K-1)\big)\Big)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{L^{\prime}}w^{\mathsf{tc}}_{L^{\prime}-K+1}(K-1)-w_{1}\frac{1+(L^{\prime}-2)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-1)}{L^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(iv)}}}{{=}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L^{\prime}-K+1}w^{\mathsf{tc}}_{\ell}-\big(1+(L^{\prime}-2)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-1)\big).

(42)

Here, (i) decomposes
(L^{\prime}-\ell)
as

(L^{\prime}-\ell)(1+(L^{\prime}-\ell+1)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-\ell))-(L^{\prime}-\ell)(L^{\prime}-\ell+1)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-\ell);

(ii) employs the weight recurrence from (
40
), which gives

w^{\mathsf{tc}}_{\ell}(L^{\prime}-\ell)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-\ell)=w^{\mathsf{tc}}_{\ell+1}\big[1+\big(L^{\prime}-(\ell+1)-1\big)f\big(K-1,L^{\prime}-(\ell+1)\big)\big];

(iii) collapses the resulting telescoping sum; (iv) substitutes
w_{1}=1
and leverages
f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K,K)=0
for all
K\geq 1
.

Step 4: Putting everything together.

Assembling the coefficient formulas (
41
) and (
42
) and inserting them back into (
38
), all intermediate entropy sums cancel telescopically, and only the total correlation over
\mathcal{I}
given
x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}
remains:

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{L^{\prime}-K+1}w^{\mathsf{tc}}_{\ell}a_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\sum_{\ell=1}^{L^{\prime}-K+1}w^{\mathsf{tc}}_{\ell}-1-(L^{\prime}-2)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-1)\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}H(X_{i}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})-H(X_{\mathcal{I}}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\bigg)

\displaystyle=\bigg(\sum_{\ell=1}^{L^{\prime}-K+1}w^{\mathsf{tc}}_{\ell}-1-(L^{\prime}-2)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-1)\bigg)\mathsf{TC}(X_{\mathcal{I}}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}).

(43)

Substituting (
43
) into the KL error expression (
37
) yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\pi_{\mathsf{tc}}(K,\mathcal{I})}\Big[\mathsf{KL}\big(p_{X_{\mathcal{I}}}(\cdot\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\|p_{\overline{Y}_{\mathcal{I}}^{S}}(\cdot\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\big)\Big]=\frac{1}{\Psi^{\mathsf{tc}}(K,L^{\prime})}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L^{\prime}-K+1}w^{\mathsf{tc}}_{\ell}a_{\ell}

\displaystyle\qquad=\bigg(1-\frac{1+(L^{\prime}-2)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-1)}{\Psi^{\mathsf{tc}}(K,L^{\prime})}\bigg)\mathsf{TC}(X_{\mathcal{I}}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})

\displaystyle\qquad=f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K,L^{\prime})\mathsf{TC}(X_{\mathcal{I}}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}),

(44)

with the final step following from the definition of
f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K,L^{\prime})
in (
17
).
This closes the inductive argument and finishes the proof of Lemma
3
.

A.4
Proof of Lemma
4

By the definition of
f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K,L^{\prime})
(
17
), we only need to prove

\frac{\Psi^{\mathsf{tc}}(K,L^{\prime})}{1+(L^{\prime}-2)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-1)}\leq\frac{K+H_{L^{\prime}-K+1}-2}{K-1},

(45)

from which it follows directly that

f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K,L^{\prime})=1-\frac{1+(L^{\prime}-2)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-1)}{\Psi^{\mathsf{tc}}(K,L^{\prime})}\leq 1-\frac{K-1}{K+H_{L^{\prime}-K+1}-2}=\frac{H_{L^{\prime}-K+1}-1}{K+H_{L^{\prime}-K+1}-2}.

Our strategy is to establish (
45
) via strong induction over
(K,L^{\prime})
, ordered by the sum
K+L^{\prime}
.
Using the expressions for
w_{\ell}^{\mathsf{dtc}}
from (
19
) and
\Psi_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K,L)
from (
12
), one can write

\displaystyle\Psi^{\mathsf{tc}}(K,L^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L^{\prime}-K+1}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell-1}\frac{(L^{\prime}-i)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-i)}{1+(L^{\prime}-i-2)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-i-1)}.

Base case 1:
K=2
.

We begin by verifying the claim when the number of batches is at its smallest, namely
K=2
.

Setting
K=2
, note that
f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,\ell)=f_{\mathsf{tc}}(1,\ell)=1
for every
\ell\geq 2
. Each factor in the product that defines
\Psi^{\mathsf{tc}}(2,L^{\prime})
then reduces to

\frac{(L^{\prime}-i)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(1,L^{\prime}-i)}{1+(L^{\prime}-i-2)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(1,L^{\prime}-i-1)}=\frac{L^{\prime}-i}{L^{\prime}-i-1},

so the product collapses telescopically to

\prod_{i=1}^{\ell-1}\frac{L^{\prime}-i}{L^{\prime}-i-1}=\frac{(L^{\prime}-1)(L^{\prime}-2)\cdots(L^{\prime}-\ell+1)}{(L^{\prime}-2)(L^{\prime}-3)\cdots(L^{\prime}-\ell)}=\frac{L^{\prime}-1}{L^{\prime}-\ell}.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\frac{\Psi^{\mathsf{tc}}(2,L^{\prime})}{1+(L^{\prime}-2)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(1,L^{\prime}-1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+(L^{\prime}-2)\cdot 1}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L^{\prime}-1}\frac{L^{\prime}-1}{L^{\prime}-\ell}=\frac{1}{L^{\prime}-1}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L^{\prime}-1}\frac{L^{\prime}-1}{L^{\prime}-\ell}=\sum_{j=1}^{L^{\prime}-1}\frac{1}{j}=H_{L^{\prime}-1},

which verifies the claim for
K=2
.

Base case 2:
L^{\prime}=K\geq 2
.

We now handle the boundary case where
L^{\prime}
equals
K
.

Observe that for
L^{\prime}=K
, one has
\Psi^{\mathsf{tc}}(K,K)=1
, while the induction hypothesis gives
f\big(K-1,L^{\prime}-(L^{\prime}-K+1)\big)=f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,K-1)=0
. It follows that

\frac{\Psi^{\mathsf{tc}}(K,K)}{1+(K-2)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,K-1)}=\frac{1}{1+(K-2)\cdot 0}=1=\frac{K+H_{1}-2}{K-1}=\frac{K-1}{K-1}

and

f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K,K)=1-\frac{\Psi^{\mathsf{tc}}(K,K)}{1+(K-2)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,K-1)}=0.

This settles the diagonal base case.

Inductive step:
L^{\prime}>K>2
.

Suppose (
45
) is valid for every pair
(K^{\prime},L^{\prime\prime})
satisfying
K^{\prime}+L^{\prime\prime}<K+L^{\prime}
. Our goal is to verify it for
(K,L^{\prime})
with
K\geq 3
and
L^{\prime}>K
, which amounts to showing

\displaystyle\Psi^{\mathsf{tc}}(K,L^{\prime})\leq\big(1+(L^{\prime}-2)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-1)\big)\cdot\frac{K+H_{L^{\prime}-K+1}-2}{K-1}.

(46)

A key ingredient is the following recurrence linking
\Psi^{\mathsf{tc}}(K,L^{\prime})
to
\Psi^{\mathsf{tc}}(K,L^{\prime}-1)
:

\displaystyle\Psi^{\mathsf{tc}}(K,L^{\prime})-1

\displaystyle=\frac{(L^{\prime}-1)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-1)}{1+(L^{\prime}-3)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-2)}\Psi^{\mathsf{tc}}(K,L^{\prime}-1).

(47)

We postpone its derivation to the end of this subsection.

Taking (
47
) as given, we carry out the induction argument.
By the inductive assumption applied to the pair
(K,L^{\prime}-1)
, we obtain

\frac{\Psi^{\mathsf{tc}}(K,L^{\prime}-1)}{1+(L^{\prime}-3)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-2)}\leq\frac{K+H_{L^{\prime}-K}-2}{K-1}.

Substituting this estimate into (
47
) yields the following upper bound on
\Psi^{\mathsf{tc}}(K,L^{\prime})
:

\displaystyle\Psi^{\mathsf{tc}}(K,L^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{(L^{\prime}-1)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-1)}{1+(L^{\prime}-3)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-2)}\cdot\bigl(1+(L^{\prime}-3)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-2)\bigr)\cdot\frac{K+H_{L^{\prime}-K}-2}{K-1}

\displaystyle=1+\frac{(L^{\prime}-1)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-1)(K+H_{L^{\prime}-K}-2)}{K-1}.

Therefore, proving (
46
) reduces to verifying that

1+\frac{(L^{\prime}-1)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-1)(K+H_{L^{\prime}-K}-2)}{K-1}\leq\frac{(1+(L^{\prime}-2)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-1))(K+H_{L^{\prime}-K+1}-2)}{K-1}.

After multiplying through by
(K-1)
and reorganizing, this becomes

(K-1)+(L^{\prime}-1)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-1)(K+H_{L^{\prime}-K}-2)\leq\big(1+(L^{\prime}-2)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-1)\big)(K+H_{L^{\prime}-K+1}-2).

(48)

Isolating the factor
f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-1)
and simplifying, (
48
) can be rewritten as

f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-1)\big[(L^{\prime}-1)(K+H_{L^{\prime}-K}-2)-(L^{\prime}-2)(K+H_{L^{\prime}-K+1}-2)\big]\leq(K+H_{L^{\prime}-K+1}-2)-(K-1)=H_{L^{\prime}-K+1}-1.

The bracketed expression admits the following simplification:

\displaystyle(L^{\prime}-1)(K+H_{L^{\prime}-K}-2)-(L^{\prime}-2)(K+H_{L^{\prime}-K+1}-2)

\displaystyle\qquad=(K+H_{L^{\prime}-K}-2)+(L^{\prime}-2)\big(H_{L^{\prime}-K}-H_{L^{\prime}-K+1}\big)

\displaystyle\qquad=(K+H_{L^{\prime}-K}-2)-\frac{L^{\prime}-2}{L^{\prime}-K+1}

\displaystyle\qquad=K-2+H_{L^{\prime}-K+1}-\frac{1}{L^{\prime}-K+1}-\frac{L^{\prime}-2}{L^{\prime}-K+1}

\displaystyle\qquad=K-3+H_{L^{\prime}-K+1}-\frac{K-2}{L^{\prime}-K+1}.

with the second and third equalities following from the harmonic number identity
H_{L^{\prime}-K+1}=H_{L^{\prime}-K}+(L^{\prime}-K+1)^{-1}
.
Combining the above, it remains to establish that

\Big(K-3+H_{L^{\prime}-K+1}-\frac{K-2}{L^{\prime}-K+1}\Big)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-1)\leq H_{L^{\prime}-K+1}-1.

(49)

To complete the argument, invoke the induction hypothesis for
(K-1,L^{\prime}-1)
— valid because
(K-1)+(L^{\prime}-1)<K+L^{\prime}
— to deduce that

f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-1)\leq\frac{H_{(L^{\prime}-1)-(K-1)+1}-1}{(K-1)+H_{(L^{\prime}-1)-(K-1)+1}-2}=\frac{H_{L^{\prime}-K+1}-1}{K-3+H_{L^{\prime}-K+1}}.

Using this, the left-hand side of (
49
) is bounded above by

\displaystyle\Big(K-3+H_{L^{\prime}-K+1}-\frac{K-2}{L^{\prime}-K+1}\Big)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-1)

\displaystyle\leq(K-3+H_{L^{\prime}-K+1})f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-1)

\displaystyle\leq(K-3+H_{L^{\prime}-K+1})\cdot\frac{H_{L^{\prime}-K+1}-1}{K-3+H_{L^{\prime}-K+1}}=H_{L^{\prime}-K+1}-1.

This confirms (
48
), thereby closing the inductive step and concluding the proof of Lemma
4
.

Proof of the identity (
47
).

We now provide the derivation of the identity (
47
). Note that

\displaystyle\Psi^{\mathsf{tc}}(K,L^{\prime})-1

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L^{\prime}-K+1}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell-1}\frac{(L^{\prime}-i)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-i)}{1+(L^{\prime}-i-2)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-i-1)}-1

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=2}^{L^{\prime}-K+1}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell-1}\frac{(L^{\prime}-i)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-i)}{1+(L^{\prime}-i-2)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-i-1)},

since the
\ell=1
summand equals
1
(empty product).

Proceeding further, we obtain

\displaystyle\Psi^{\mathsf{tc}}(K,L^{\prime})-1

\displaystyle=\frac{(L^{\prime}-1)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-1)}{1+(L^{\prime}-3)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-2)}\sum_{\ell=2}^{L^{\prime}-K+1}\prod_{i=2}^{\ell-1}\frac{(L^{\prime}-i)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-i)}{1+(L^{\prime}-i-2)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-i-1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{(L^{\prime}-1)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-1)}{1+(L^{\prime}-3)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-2)}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L^{\prime}-K}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell-1}\frac{(L^{\prime}-1-i)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-1-i)}{1+(L^{\prime}-1-i-2)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-1-i-1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{(L^{\prime}-1)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-1)}{1+(L^{\prime}-3)f_{\mathsf{tc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-2)}\Psi^{\mathsf{tc}}(K,L^{\prime}-1),

Here, the first step separates out the
i=1
factor from the product, and the second step follows from a change of summation and product indices.
This completes the proof of (
47
).

A.5
Proof of Lemma
5

We will prove Lemma
5
by induction on the number of iterations
K
. For any index set of currently masked tokens
\mathcal{I}
with
K\leq|\mathcal{I}|\leq L
and conditioned on already generated tokens
x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}
, we aim to show that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\pi_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K,\mathcal{I})}\Big[\mathsf{KL}\big(p_{X_{\mathcal{I}}}(\cdot\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\,\|\,p_{\overline{Y}_{\mathcal{I}}^{S}}(\cdot\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\big)\Big]\leq f_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K,|\mathcal{I}|)\bigg(\frac{L-|\mathcal{I}|}{|\mathcal{I}|}\mathsf{TC}\big(X_{\mathcal{I}}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}\big)+\frac{L}{|\mathcal{I}|}\mathsf{DTC}\big(X_{\mathcal{I}}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}\big)\bigg).

To simplify the notation, we denote
L^{\prime}\coloneqq|\mathcal{I}|
throughout the proof.
Note that all distributions below are conditioned on
x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}
.

A.5.1
Base case

When
K=1
,
\pi_{\mathsf{dtc}}(1,\mathcal{I})
simply unmasks all tokens in
\mathcal{I}
in a single iteration.
So the sampling distribution is the product of conditional marginal distributions. By Lemma
1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\pi_{\mathsf{dtc}}(1,\mathcal{I})}\Big[\mathsf{KL}\big(p_{X_{\mathcal{I}}}(\cdot\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\,\|\,p_{\overline{Y}_{\mathcal{I}}^{S}}(\cdot\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\big)\Big]

\displaystyle\qquad=\mathsf{KL}\Big(p_{X_{\mathcal{I}}}(\cdot\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\,\|\,\prod_{i\in\mathcal{I}}p_{X_{i}}(\cdot\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad=\mathsf{TC}\big(X_{\mathcal{I}}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}\big)

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{(L^{\prime}-1)(L-L^{\prime})}{(L-L^{\prime}+1)L^{\prime}}\mathsf{TC}\big(X_{\mathcal{I}}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}\big)+\frac{L}{(L-L^{\prime}+1)L^{\prime}}\mathsf{TC}\big(X_{\mathcal{I}}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}\big)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\frac{(L^{\prime}-1)(L-L^{\prime})}{(L-L^{\prime}+1)L^{\prime}}\mathsf{TC}\big(X_{\mathcal{I}}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}\big)+\frac{(L^{\prime}-1)L}{(L-L^{\prime}+1)L^{\prime}}\mathsf{DTC}\big(X_{\mathcal{I}}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}\big)

\displaystyle\qquad=f_{\mathsf{dtc}}(1,L^{\prime})\bigg(\frac{L-L^{\prime}}{L^{\prime}}\mathsf{TC}\big(X_{\mathcal{I}}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}\big)+\frac{L}{L^{\prime}}\mathsf{DTC}\big(X_{\mathcal{I}}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}\big)\bigg),

where the inequality holds since
\mathsf{TC}\big(X_{\mathcal{I}}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}\big)\leq(L^{\prime}-1)\mathsf{DTC}\big(X_{\mathcal{I}}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}\big)
by Lemma
7
in Appendix
B.3
, and the last equality holds since the definition
f_{\mathsf{dtc}}(1,L^{\prime})=\frac{L^{\prime}-1}{L-L^{\prime}+1}
for any
1\leq L^{\prime}\leq L
.

A.5.2
Inductive step

Assume that the claim holds for
K-1
. We will show that it also holds for
K
.

Step 1: Bound KL error by a weighted sum
\sum_{\ell}w_{\ell}^{\mathsf{dtc}}b_{\ell}
.

By Lemma
2
, conditioned on
x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}
, the expected KL error of
\pi_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K,\mathcal{I})
admits the following decomposition:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\pi_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K,\mathcal{I})}\Big[\mathsf{KL}\big(p_{X_{\mathcal{I}}}(\cdot\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\,\|\,p_{\overline{Y}_{\mathcal{I}}^{S}}(\cdot\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\big)\Big]

\displaystyle\quad=\mathbb{E}_{S^{(1)}}\big[\mathsf{TC}(X_{S^{(1)}}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\big]+\mathbb{E}_{S^{(1)},\,x_{S^{(1)}}}\bigg[\mathbb{E}_{S^{(2:K)}}\Big[\mathsf{KL}\big(p_{X_{\mathcal{I}\setminus S^{(1)}}}(\cdot\mid x_{S^{(1)}\cup\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\,\|\,p_{\overline{Y}_{\mathcal{I}\setminus S^{(1)}}^{S^{(2:K)}}}(\cdot\mid x_{S^{(1)}\cup\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\big)\Big]\bigg].

where
S^{(1)}\sim\pi_{\mathsf{dtc}}^{(1)}(K,\mathcal{I})
,
x_{S^{(1)}}\sim p_{X_{S^{(1)}}}(\cdot\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})
and
S^{(2:K)}\sim\pi_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K-1,\mathcal{I}\setminus S^{(1)})
. Applying the induction hypothesis to the inner expectation yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{S}\Big[\mathsf{KL}\big(p_{X_{\mathcal{I}}}(\cdot\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}),\|\,p_{\overline{Y}_{\mathcal{I}}^{S}}(\cdot\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\big)\Big]

\displaystyle\quad\leq\mathbb{E}_{S^{(1)}}\big[\mathsf{TC}\big(X_{S^{(1)}}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}\big)\big]+\mathbb{E}_{S^{(1)},x_{S^{(1)}}}\bigg[f_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-|S^{(1)}|)\bigg(\frac{L-L^{\prime}+|S^{(1)}|}{L^{\prime}-|S^{(1)}|}\mathsf{TC}\big(X_{\mathcal{I}\setminus S^{(1)}}\mid x_{S^{(1)}},x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}\big)

\displaystyle\hskip 280.00043pt+\frac{L}{L^{\prime}-|S^{(1)}|}\mathsf{DTC}\big(X_{\mathcal{I}\setminus S^{(1)}}\mid x_{S^{(1)}},x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}\big)\bigg)\bigg]

\displaystyle\quad=\mathbb{E}_{S^{(1)}}\bigg[\mathsf{TC}\big(X_{S^{(1)}}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}\big)+f_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-|S^{(1)}|)\bigg(\frac{L-L^{\prime}+|S^{(1)}|}{L^{\prime}-|S^{(1)}|}\mathsf{TC}\big(X_{\mathcal{I}\setminus S^{(1)}}\mid X_{S^{(1)}},x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}\big)

\displaystyle\hskip 230.00035pt+\frac{L}{L^{\prime}-|S^{(1)}|}\mathsf{DTC}\big(X_{\mathcal{I}\setminus S^{(1)}}\mid X_{S^{(1)}},x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}\big))\Big],

(50)

where the last equality follows from the definitions of conditional total correlation and conditional dual total correlation.

To analyze the right-hand side of (
50
), recall that we generate the first unmasking set
S^{(1)}
in two steps. First, we sample its size
\ell=|S^{(1)}|
with probability
w^{\mathsf{dtc}}_{\ell}(K,L^{\prime})/\Psi^{\mathsf{dtc}}(K,L^{\prime})
for
\ell=1,2,\ldots,L^{\prime}-K+1
. Given
\ell
, we then choose
S^{(1)}
uniformly at random among all size-
\ell
subsets of
\mathcal{I}
.
As a result, let us define
b_{\ell}=b_{\ell}(K,L^{\prime})
as the uniform average over all size-
\ell
subsets for each
1\leq\ell\leq L^{\prime}-K+1
:

\displaystyle b_{\ell}(K,L^{\prime})\coloneqq\frac{1}{\binom{L^{\prime}}{\ell}}\sum_{S^{(1)}\subseteq\mathcal{I}\,:\,|S^{(1)}|=\ell}\Big(

\displaystyle\mathsf{TC}(X_{S^{(1)}}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})+\frac{L-L^{\prime}+\ell}{L^{\prime}-\ell}f_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-\ell)\mathsf{TC}(X_{\mathcal{I}\setminus S^{(1)}}\mid X_{S^{(1)}},x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})

\displaystyle+\frac{L}{L^{\prime}-\ell}f_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-\ell)\mathsf{DTC}(X_{\mathcal{I}\setminus S^{(1)}}\mid X_{S^{(1)}},x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\Big).

(51)

We can then express the expected KL sampling error on the right-hand side of (
50
) as a weighted sum

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\pi_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K,\mathcal{I})}\Big[\mathsf{KL}\big(p_{X_{\mathcal{I}}}(\cdot\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\,\|\,p_{\overline{Y}_{\mathcal{I}}^{S}}(\cdot\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\big)\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{L^{\prime}-K+1}\mathbb{P}\{|S^{(1)}|=\ell\}\cdot b_{\ell}=\frac{1}{\Psi^{\mathsf{dtc}}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L^{\prime}-K+1}w^{\mathsf{dtc}}_{\ell}b_{\ell}.

(52)

Therefore, this suggests we control the weighted sum
\sum_{\ell=1}^{L^{\prime}-K+1}w^{\mathsf{dtc}}_{\ell}b_{\ell}
.

Step 2: Express
\sum_{\ell}w^{\mathsf{dtc}}_{\ell}b_{\ell}
as a telescoped sum.

To this end, plugging in the definitions of conditional TC and DTC, we can rewrite

defined in (

\displaystyle b_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\binom{L^{\prime}}{\ell}}\sum_{\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{I}\,:\,|\mathcal{S}|=\ell}\Bigg(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}H(X_{i}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})-H(X_{\mathcal{S}}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{L-L^{\prime}+\ell}{L^{\prime}-\ell}f_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-\ell)\bigg(\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}\setminus\mathcal{S}}H(X_{j}\mid X_{\mathcal{S}},x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})-H(X_{\mathcal{I}\setminus\mathcal{S}}\mid X_{\mathcal{S}},x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})\bigg)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{L}{L^{\prime}-\ell}f_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-\ell)\bigg(H(X_{\mathcal{I}\setminus\mathcal{S}}\mid X_{\mathcal{S}},x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}\setminus\mathcal{S}}H\big(X_{j}\mid X_{\mathcal{I}\setminus\{j\}},x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}\big)\bigg)\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\binom{L^{\prime}}{\ell}}\sum_{\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{I}\,:\,|\mathcal{S}|=\ell}\Bigg(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}H(X_{i}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})-H(X_{\mathcal{S}}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{L-L^{\prime}+\ell}{L^{\prime}-\ell}f_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-\ell)\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}\setminus\mathcal{S}}H(X_{j}\mid X_{\mathcal{S}},x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})+f_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-\ell)H(X_{\mathcal{I}\setminus\mathcal{S}}\mid X_{\mathcal{S}},x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{L}{L^{\prime}-\ell}f_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-\ell)\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}\setminus\mathcal{S}}H\big(X_{j}\mid X_{\mathcal{I}\setminus\{j\}},x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}\big)\Bigg).

As

for any random variables

\displaystyle b_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\binom{L^{\prime}}{\ell}}\sum_{\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{I}\,:\,|\mathcal{S}|=\ell}\Bigg(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}H(X_{i}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})-\big[1+(L-L^{\prime}+\ell+1)f_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-\ell)\big]H(X_{\mathcal{S}}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{L}{L^{\prime}-\ell}f_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-\ell)\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}\setminus\mathcal{S}}H\big(X_{j}\mid X_{\mathcal{I}\setminus\{j\}},x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}\big)\Bigg),

(53)

where we use the fact that

Now, notice that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\ell}\frac{1}{\binom{L^{\prime}}{\ell}}\sum_{\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{I}\,:\,|\mathcal{S}|=\ell}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}H(X_{i}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{I}\,:\,|\mathcal{S}|=\ell}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}\setminus\mathcal{S}}H\big(X_{\{j\}\cup\mathcal{S}}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}\big)

(55)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L^{\prime}-\ell}\frac{1}{\binom{L^{\prime}}{\ell}}\sum_{\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{I}\,:\,|\mathcal{S}|=\ell}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}\setminus\mathcal{S}}H\big(X_{j}\mid X_{\mathcal{I}\setminus\{j\}},x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L^{\prime}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}H\big(X_{j}\mid X_{\mathcal{I}\setminus\{j\}},x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}\big).

(56)

Here, (

\mathcal{I}

with size

is equivalent to removing one element from a set with size

\ell+1
ways. (
56

\mathcal{I}
is equivalent to uniformly selecting a set

Substituting (
54

\displaystyle b_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{\ell}{L^{\prime}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}H(X_{i}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})-\frac{1+(L-L^{\prime}+\ell+1)f_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-\ell)}{\binom{L^{\prime}}{\ell}}\sum_{\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{I}\,:\,|\mathcal{S}|=\ell}H(X_{\mathcal{S}}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{(\ell+1)}{\binom{L^{\prime}}{\ell}}\frac{L-L^{\prime}+\ell}{L^{\prime}-\ell}f_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-\ell)\sum_{\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathcal{I}\,:\,|\mathcal{T}|=\ell+1}H(X_{\mathcal{T}}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})+f_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-\ell)H(X_{\mathcal{I}}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{L}{L^{\prime}}f_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-\ell)\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}H\big(X_{j}\mid X_{\mathcal{I}\setminus\{j\}},x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{\ell}{L^{\prime}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}H(X_{i}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})+f_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-\ell)H(X_{\mathcal{I}}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})-\frac{L}{L^{\prime}}f_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-\ell)\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}H\big(X_{j}\mid X_{\mathcal{I}\setminus\{j\}},x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}}\big)

where we use the fact that (1)
w^{\mathsf{dtc}}_{1}

Step 3: Compute entropy coefficients.

With the expression (
61
) in hand, it remains to compute the entropy coefficients in the above expression.

•

Let us begin with the coefficient for
H(X_{\mathcal{I}}\mid x_{\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{c}}})
, i.e.,
\sum_{\ell=1}^{L^{\prime}-K}w^{\mathsf{dtc}}_{\ell}f_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-\ell)

A.6.1
Base case 1:

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{L^{\prime}-1}\frac{1}{L-L^{\prime}+\ell}\geq\frac{(L^{\prime}-1)^{2}}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{L^{\prime}-1}(L-L^{\prime}+\ell)}.

•

Sub-case 2a:
p(K-1,L^{\prime}-1)=\frac{L}{K-1}(H_{L-1}-H_{L-L^{\prime}+1})
.

Since
p(K-1,L^{\prime}-1)\leq L^{\prime}-K<L
by definition, the condition implies that
H_{L-1}-H_{L-L^{\prime}+1}<K-1
. Hence, we know that

\frac{p(K-1,L^{\prime}-1)}{L-p(K-1,L^{\prime}-1)}=\frac{H_{L-1}-H_{L-L^{\prime}+1}}{K-1-(H_{L-1}-H_{L-L^{\prime}+1})}>0.

Together with (
74
), this yields that the left-hand side of (
73
) is at most

\frac{L\big(K-1-(H_{L-1}-H_{L-L^{\prime}})\big)\,(H_{L-1}-H_{L-L^{\prime}+1})}{K\big(K-1-(H_{L-1}-H_{L-L^{\prime}+1})\big)}.

This is upper bounded by
p(K,L^{\prime})=\frac{L}{K}(H_{L-1}-H_{L-L^{\prime}})
if and only if

\big(K-1-(H_{L-1}-H_{L-L^{\prime}})\big)\,(H_{L-1}-H_{L-L^{\prime}+1})\leq(H_{L-1}-H_{L-L^{\prime}})\,\big(K-1-(H_{L-1}-H_{L-L^{\prime}+1})\big),

or equivalently,

(K-1)(H_{L-1}-H_{L-L^{\prime}+1})\leq(K-1)(H_{L-1}-H_{L-L^{\prime}}).

This holds since
H_{n}
is increasing in
n
. Hence, (
73
) holds in Sub-case 2a.

•

Sub-case 2b:
p(K-1,L^{\prime}-1)=L^{\prime}-K
.

In this case, one has

\frac{p(K-1,L^{\prime}-1)}{L-p(K-1,L^{\prime}-1)}=\frac{L^{\prime}-K}{L-L^{\prime}+K}.

Combining this with (
74
), we can control the left-hand side of (
73
) by

\frac{L\big(K-1-(H_{L-1}-H_{L-L^{\prime}})\big)(L^{\prime}-K)}{K(L-L^{\prime}+K)}.

This is upper bounded by
p(K,L^{\prime})=\frac{L}{K}(H_{L-1}-H_{L-L^{\prime}})
if and only if

\big(K-1-(H_{L-1}-H_{L-L^{\prime}})\big)(L^{\prime}-K)\leq(H_{L-1}-H_{L-L^{\prime}})(L-L^{\prime}+K),

or equivalently,

(K-1)(L^{\prime}-K)\leq(H_{L-1}-H_{L-L^{\prime}})L.

Since the condition
p(K-1,L^{\prime}-1)=L^{\prime}-K
means that
\frac{L}{K-1}(H_{L-1}-H_{L-L^{\prime}+1})\geq L^{\prime}-K
, we obtain

L(H_{L-1}-H_{L-L^{\prime}})=L(H_{L-1}-H_{L-L^{\prime}+1})+\frac{L}{L-L^{\prime}+1}>(K-1)(L^{\prime}-K),

which verifies the desired inequality.
Thus (
73
) also holds in Sub-case 2b.

•

Combining these two sub-cases establishes (
73
) in Case 2.

Finally, combining Cases 1 and 2 concludes that (
73
) holds for
(K,L^{\prime})
, thereby finishing the inductive step. The proof of Lemma
6
is now complete.

Proof of the identity (

).

It remains to prove the identity (

).
Recall the definition of
\Psi_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K,L^{\prime})
, we can write

\displaystyle\Psi_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K,L^{\prime})-1

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L^{\prime}-K+1}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell-1}\frac{(L-L^{\prime}+i)f_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-i)}{1+(L-L^{\prime}+i+2)f_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-i-1)}-1

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=2}^{L^{\prime}-K+1}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell-1}\frac{(L-L^{\prime}+i)f_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-i)}{1+(L-L^{\prime}+i+2)f_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-i-1)},

where the last equality follows from the fact that the
\ell=1
term in the sum is

.

We can further derive

\displaystyle\Psi_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K,L^{\prime})-1

\displaystyle=\frac{(L-L^{\prime}+1)f_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-1)}{1+(L-L^{\prime}+3)f_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-2)}\sum_{\ell=2}^{L^{\prime}-K+1}\prod_{i=2}^{\ell-1}\frac{(L-L^{\prime}+i)f_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-i)}{1+(L-L^{\prime}+i+2)f_{\mathsf{dtc}}(K-1,L^{\prime}-i-1)}

B.3
Relationship between TC and DTC

Lemma 7
.

For any random vector
X=(X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{n})
, we have

\mathsf{TC}(X)\leq(n-1)\mathsf{DTC}(X).

Proof.

Consider the
n
cyclic permutations
\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{n-1}
of
[n]
, where
\sigma_{j}(k)=((k+j-1)\bmod n)+1
. Applying the chain rule of entropy to each cyclic permutation
\sigma_{j}
gives

\displaystyle H(X_{[n]})=\sum_{k=1}^{n}H\big(X_{\sigma_{j}(k)}\mid X_{\sigma_{j}(1)},\ldots,X_{\sigma_{j}(k-1)}\big).

(75)

Summing (
75
) over all
n
cyclic permutations yields

\displaystyle n\,H(X_{[n]})=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\sum_{k=1}^{n}H\big(X_{\sigma_{j}(k)}\mid X_{\sigma_{j}(1)},\ldots,X_{\sigma_{j}(k-1)}\big).

(76)

Now we examine the contribution of each
X_{i}
to the right-hand side. Across the
n
cyclic permutations,
X_{i}
appears exactly once in each position
k=1,\ldots,n
. In particular:

•

When
k=1
:
X_{i}
is conditioned on nothing, contributing
H(X_{i})
.

•

When
k=n
:
X_{i}
is conditioned on all others, contributing
H(X_{i}\mid X_{[n]\setminus\{i\}})
.

•

For
k=2,\ldots,n-1
:
X_{i}
is conditioned on some strict subset of
[n]\setminus\{i\}
.

Since conditioning reduces entropy, i.e.,
H(X_{i}\mid X_{T})\geq H(X_{i}\mid X_{[n]\setminus\{i\}})
for any
T\subseteq[n]\setminus\{i\}
, the
n-2
middle terms are each lower bounded by
H(X_{i}\mid X_{[n]\setminus\{i\}})
. Therefore, for each
X_{i}
, its total contribution satisfies

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}H(X_{\sigma_{j}(k)}\mid X_{\sigma_{j}(1)},\ldots,X_{\sigma_{j}(k-1)})\geq H(X_{\sigma_{j}(1)})+(n-1)H(X_{\sigma_{j}(1)}\mid X_{[n]\setminus\{i\}}).

(77)

Summing (
77
) over all
i\in[n]
and substituting into (
76
), we obtain

\displaystyle n\,H(X_{[n]})\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}H(X_{i})+(n-1)\sum_{i=1}^{n}H(X_{i}\mid X_{[n]\setminus\{i\}}).

(78)

Rearranging (
78
) gives

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}H(X_{i})-H(X_{[n]})\leq(n-1)\bigg(H(X_{[n]})-\sum_{i=1}^{n}H(X_{i}\mid X_{[n]\setminus\{i\}})\bigg).

This is equivalent to the desired inequality:

\mathsf{TC}(X)\leq(n-1)\,\mathsf{DTC}(X).

∎
