Postoperative gait training using the Hybrid Assistive Limb for pediatric spinal intramedullary cavernous malformation: illustrative case
Authors: Taku Matsuda, Hideo Mure, Yuki Masuda, Hiroshi Kagusa, Mai Azumi, Nori Sato, Tetsuya Matsuura, Yasushi Takagi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDPediatric spinal intramedullary cavernous malformations are rare, and their optimal management requires timely surgical intervention and structured postoperative rehabilitation.OBSERVATIONSAn 11-year-old female presented with acute back pain, followed by progressive paraplegia and urinary retention. MRI revealed an intramedullary lesion at T8. The patient’s neurological status deteriorated during hospitalization, prompting emergency resection. Histopathological examination confirmed the presence of a cavernous malformation. Postoperatively, conventional physiotherapy was initiated, and robot-assisted rehabilitation using an exoskeletal robot began on postoperative day 14, with 10 sessions completed. Exoskeletal robot-assisted training enabled early standing and gait practice despite significant paresis. The patient showed progressive improvements in muscle strength, gait function, and activities of daily living. The bladder dysfunction resolved, and the patient regained independent ambulation with a T-cane before transfer for further rehabilitation. At 6 months, the patient had regained full strength in all muscle groups and was able to ambulate independently without the need for a cane.LESSONSExoskeletal robot-assisted rehabilitation may enhance postoperative recovery in pediatric patients with spinal intramedullary cavernous malformations by promoting early mobilization, improving gait patterns, reducing spasticity, and supporting autonomic function. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE26448

Spinal intramedullary cavernous malformations are uncommon in children, and their true prevalence remains unclear. Previous reports have suggested that these lesions account for a very small fraction of pediatric spinal pathologies, with estimates around 0.18%.1 Timely surgical intervention is essential for achieving a favorable prognosis.2

Rehabilitation is another important determinant of patient outcome. Robot-assisted rehabilitation can enhance functional recovery, and robotic systems have been introduced for conditions such as neuromuscular diseases, spinal cord injuries, and stroke.3–5 Among these systems is the Hybrid Assistive Limb (HAL; CYBERDYNE, Inc.), an exoskeleton-type robot designed for rehabilitation, which assists motion by detecting bioelectrical signals from the skin generated by the patient’s intention to move their limbs. A lower limb HAL can enable patients, even those with severe paresis, to initiate standing and gait training at an earlier stage. Our institution has incorporated the HAL into the rehabilitation programs for patients with stroke and those undergoing postoperative recovery after cerebrospinal surgeries associated with motor paralysis, such as surgeries for brain arteriovenous malformations, brain tumors, and spinal tumors.

Although several studies have suggested the potential benefits of rehabilitation after spinal surgery,6 none have specifically described its use in patients undergoing surgery for cavernous malformations. In this report, we present a pediatric case of intramedullary cavernous malformation in which postoperative rehabilitation using HAL was associated with meaningful improvements in gait function and activities of daily living.

Illustrative Case

An 11‑year‑old female experienced a sudden onset of severe back pain. Three days later, the patient developed gait disturbances and urinary retention. Initial MRI performed at a local orthopedic clinic revealed an abnormal intramedullary signal. Subsequently, the patient was referred to our hospital.

The patient first presented with paraplegia. Manual muscle testing (MMT) demonstrated the following grades (right/left): iliopsoas 4/3, quadriceps 4/4, hamstrings 4/4, tibialis anterior 4/4, extensor hallucis longus 4/4, and triceps surae 4/4. Sensory examination revealed impaired pain and temperature sensation below the T10 level. In addition, bladder and rectal dysfunctions were observed. MRI demonstrated an approximately 7-mm intramedullary lesion with abnormal signal intensity at the T8 level (Fig. 1). The low intensities on both T1- and T2-weighted imaging suggested an acute hematoma, and a spinal intramedullary cavernous malformation was suspected. A semiurgent surgery had been scheduled for approximately 2 weeks after admission; however, on day 10, the patient experienced a sudden worsening of paraplegia (MMT grades [right/left]: iliopsoas 2/3, quadriceps 2/3, hamstrings 2/3, tibialis anterior 2/3, extensor hallucis longus 2/2, and triceps surae 2/2). An emergency operation was performed via a posterior median sulcus approach, with entry through the dorsal columns under somatosensory evoked potential monitoring. The mass was completely removed; the intraoperative findings are shown in Fig. 2A–C. Histopathological examination confirmed the diagnosis of spinal intramedullary cavernous malformation (Fig. 2D). Because the patient exhibited significant postoperative paraplegia, discharge home was not feasible, and continued inpatient rehabilitation was required. Therefore, HAL-assisted gait training was performed during the inpatient rehabilitation period. Conventional physiotherapy was continued, and HAL rehabilitation was initiated twice weekly beginning 14 days after surgery. The patient was able to stand with assistance at the start of HAL training (Video 1A). Initially, standing training was performed in the STAND mode (Video 1B), followed by walking ­training in the CVC (Cybernic Voluntary Control) mode (Video 1C). A total of 10 HAL sessions were conducted. No adverse events related to the HAL-assisted rehabilitation were observed. The patient’s muscle strength gradually recovered, and 67 days after surgery, the patient was transferred to another hospital for further rehabilitation. At the time of transfer, the patient was able to walk with a T-cane (Video 1D). MMT grades had improved to iliopsoas 4/4, quadriceps 4/4, hamstrings 4/4, tibialis anterior 4/4, extensor hallucis longus 4/4, and triceps surae 4/4, and the bladder and rectal dysfunctions had resolved. The postoperative MR images are shown in Fig. 3. The patient was discharged from the hospital 3 months postoperatively and was independent in activities of daily living. Six months after surgery, the motor function and activities of daily living remained stable (Video 1E), with muscle strength graded 5/5 on MMT of all muscles, and the patient was able to ambulate independently without the need for a cane. Discussion

Early postoperative mobilization is key for preventing disuse and improving functional outcomes.7 Some case reports have described the effectiveness of HAL rehabilitation in postoperative patients with spinal disease, such as ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament, arteriovenous fistula, ependymoma, and spinal cord injury.6 However, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of a pediatric patient demonstrating good recovery after HAL rehabilitation following spinal cavernous hemangioma resection.

In HAL training, the device detects bioelectrical signals from the patient’s muscles and assists movement accordingly. These signals are sent back to the CNS as sensory input, facilitating activation of impaired neural pathways and promoting recovery of motor function. This concept, known as the interactive biofeedback (iBF) theory, was originally demonstrated in animal studies8 and offers potential advantages over conventional rehabilitation approaches.9 The incorporation of HAL-assisted rehabilitation into physiotherapy enables early mobilization, even in patients with limited muscle strength, and facilitates the initiation of gait training. Wall et al. reported longer walking distances for patients undergoing HAL training compared with conventional training.10

The HAL system provides additional benefits beyond improvements in motor function. Spasticity is thought to result, in part, from the loss of descending inputs from the CNS to the central pattern generator. By facilitating voluntary movement through iBF, HAL may help restore this input to reduce spasticity. In this context, early initiation of HAL-assisted gait training may contribute to the prevention of increased spasticity.

Furthermore, Brinkemper et al. reported that exoskeleton-assisted training can improve bowel and bladder function.11 In the present case, although urinary retention was observed at first presentation, bladder function gradually recovered during HAL gait training, allowing successful removal of the urinary catheter and discontinuation of urinary catheterization. Although causality cannot be established based on a single case, early initiation of HAL gait training might have contributed to this improvement.

Individuals with spinal cord injury have an increased risk of developing osteoarthritis, potentially due to factors such as progressive spasticity and abnormal gait patterns.12 Gait training with the HAL system may improve walking patterns and promote physiological movement;13 thus, learning a proper gait style through the early initiation of HAL-assisted gait training may help reduce the long-term risk of osteoarthritis.

Safdar et al. observed improvement in 36%–69% of patients undergoing resection of spinal cavernous malformation on long-term follow-up, highlighting the importance of appropriate timing of surgical intervention for favorable outcomes.2 Liu et al. identified several predictors of poor neurological outcomes in spinal cavernous malformations, including symptom duration (≥ 26 months), small lesion size (≤ 5 mm), complete intramedullary location, and preoperative American Spinal Injury Association Impairment Scale (AIS) grade B or C.14 In addition, Rauschenbach et al. reported that early surgical intervention for intramedullary hemorrhages is associated with improved postoperative outcomes.15

In the present case, despite the presence of unfavorable prognostic factors, including intramedullary type and AIS grade C, the patient showed a good prognosis. We attribute this, at least in part, to timely surgical intervention following symptom progression. Furthermore, Cai et al. reported that younger patients tend to have more favorable outcomes than older patients, suggesting that age may also have contributed to the observed recovery.16

Liang et al. analyzed outcomes in 96 patients with intramedullary spinal cavernous malformations using the Aminoff-Logue Disability Scale, which evaluates gait and urinary function, at baseline and during long-term follow-up.17 Among the cohort, 29 patients were classified as grade 4 preoperatively, and none improved to grade 1 during follow-up.17 In contrast, our patient improved from grade 4 preoperatively to grade 1 during follow-up. This notable recovery suggests that HAL-assisted rehabilitation may have synergistic effects when combined with surgical treatment.

Limitations

This report describes a single case without a control, which limits the generalizability of the findings.

Observations

This pediatric patient with an intramedullary spinal cavernous malformation experienced rapid neurological deterioration but showed remarkable recovery after emergency resection, followed by early initiation of HAL-assisted rehabilitation. This early training enabled safe mobilization despite severe paresis and was associated with improvements in gait, muscle strength, and bladder function. To our knowledge, this is the first report of postoperative HAL-assisted rehabilitation in a child with spinal intramedullary cavernous malformation, highlighting the potential role of this tool in enhancing postoperative recovery.

Patient Perspective

The patient and the patient’s family reported that early gait training with HAL increased the patient’s confidence and motivation during recovery.

Lessons

HAL-assisted rehabilitation may be a useful adjunct after surgical treatment of spinal cavernous malformations. In addition to supporting motor recovery, it may help reduce spasticity and improve bladder function, potentially contributing to improved overall outcomes.
