Cervical flexion–induced myelopathy (Hirayama disease): A radiology case report
Authors: Balamurugan Ulaganathan, Arul Sakthi Priya Pandian, Eswaramoorthy Sellamuthu, Usha Jayagurunathan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHirayama disease is a self-limiting cervical flexion myelopathy that typically affects adolescent and young adult males presenting with unilateral or asymmetric distal upper-limb weakness and atrophy. Early diagnosis is important because avoiding neck flexion with cervical collar immobilization may limit ongoing spinal cord injury and selected patients may require surgery. We report a 19-year-old male with 1 year of left-hand weakness involving the middle and ring fingers with progressive difficulty in daily activities. Neurologic examination revealed distal hand muscle wasting and weakness without sensory loss or upper motor neuron signs. Abnormalities were observed in Nerve conduction studies. Cervical MRI demonstrated subtle lower cervical cord atrophy and focal intramedullary T2 hyperintensity in the neutral position. Dynamic flexion MRI showed anterior displacement of the posterior dura with widening of the posterior epidural space, prominent epidural flow voids, and post-contrast enhancement consistent with engorged epidural venous plexus. The patient was managed conservatively with a cervical collar and activity modification; he remained clinically stable at 12-month follow-up. This case emphasizes the neutral-position MRI clues that should prompt flexion imaging and provide a reproducible dynamic MRI technique description relevant to radiology practice.Keywords: Hirayama disease, Cervical flexion myelopathy, Dynamic MRI, Monomelic amyotrophy, Epidural venous plexus

Introduction

Hirayama disease (HD) is a rare nonfamilial neurological disorder which affects the inferior motor neurons most commonly involving the C7-T1 spinal nerves. It is also known as juvenile muscular atrophy of the distal upper extremity or monomelic amyotrophy with highest prevalence among Asian population. In 1959, Hirayama describe this disease as a clinically different from other motor neuron diseases. It is characterized by insidious onset weakness and atrophy of the distal upper limb muscles, predominantly in adolescent and young adult males [1]. Hirayama disease clinically presents as unilateral or bilateral, asymmetric weakness and muscle atrophy of C7-T1 myotomes without sensory loss. The disease is known for its initial progression followed by spontaneous arrest after several years.

The most accepted pathophysiologic mechanism involves a mismatch in growth between the vertebral column and the dural sac [2]. During neck flexion, the posterior dural sac may shift anteriorly, causing compression or flattening of the lower cervical cord and posteriorly leading to expansion of the epidural space with engorgement of the posterior internal vertebral venous plexus. Recurrent compression and venous congestion lead to chronic microcirculatory disturbance, preferentially affecting the anterior horn cells. Because these hallmark changes can be absent in neutral position, dynamic (flexion) MRI is essential for diagnosis [3,4].

Early recognition and conservative management with cervical collar immobilization and ergonomic modification may reduce the flexion-related cord compression. Surgery can be considered for progressive or severe cases.

Case report

A 19-year-old gentleman presented with 1-year history of progressive weakness of the left middle and ring fingers, later involving the left hand and forearm. He reported difficulty with activities requiring grip strength and fine motor control. No neck pain or decreased sensation was reported. There was no history of trauma, surgery, poliomyelitis, toxin exposure, or chronic systemic illness.

On physical examination marked wasting of the left thenar and hypothenar muscles with mild wasting of forearm muscles were noted. Strength of intrinsic muscles of the hand was decreased (3/5). Noticeable worsening of weakness was demonstrated during Cold paresis test. Intact sensation to pinprick, fine touch, crude touch, and proprioception. Deep tendon reflexes were normal and symmetrical.

Motor nerve conduction studies of ulnar and median nerve showed reduced compound muscle action potential with preserved distal latencies and conduction velocities. Sensory nerve conduction studies were normal.

Imaging findings

Cervical spine MRI was performed on a 3T scanner in neutral and flexion positions. Flexion was achieved using positioning support to obtain approximately 30–40° of neck flexion and maintain a stable position during acquisition. Sagittal T2-weighted, axial T2-weighted images in neutral and flexion position along with post-contrast sagittal T1-weighted images were acquired in flexion to assess posterior epidural enhancement. On Sagittal T2-weighted imaging in neutral position, there was subtle focal lower cervical cord atrophy/flattening, maximal at C5–6 (Fig. 1 A). On axial T2-weighted imaging in neutral position at C5-6 level, there was focal intramedullary T2 hyperintensity predominantly involving the left anterior horn and anterior paramedian cord, compatible with chronic ischemic injury or gliosis. No significant degenerative canal stenosis was present (Figs. 2A and B). In flexion position sagittal T2-weighted images shows dural shift with anterior displacement of the posterior dural sac, maximal at C5–6, extending approximately from C4–C7. Crescentic widening of posterior epidural space with prominent serpiginous flow voids consistent with engorged epidural venous plexus (Fig. 1B) was noted. Increased flattening during flexion with persistent intramedullary T2 hyperintensity was also seen. The laminodural space which is the distance between the lamina and the posterior dural sac significantly increases during flexion MRI, measuring 5.1 mm.

Note on clinical–radiologic correlation: The patient’s distal hand-predominant weakness suggested involvement of C8–T1 myotomes. In Hirayama disease, maximal dynamic compression may occur at a mid-to-lower cervical vertebral level while affecting anterior horn cells corresponding to lower cervical segments due to segmental-vertebral level mismatch and the craniocaudal extent of dural shift during flexion.

Treatment and follow-up

The patient was managed conservatively with Cervical collar immobilization, activity modification and ergonomic counselling to avoid prolonged neck flexion during studying/phone use. Physiotherapy with hand strengthening exercises was also recommended. At 12-month follow-up, the patient reported no further functional decline, and examination showed no progression of weakness or muscle wasting. No surgical intervention was required.

Discussion

This case illustrates radiologically important features of Hirayama disease and highlights the importance of dynamic MRI. Hirayama disease should be a part of the differential diagnosis for young male patients with symptoms of upper extremity weakness associated with muscle wasting. The exact pathogenesis of the disease remains to be unclear. The imbalance between the growth of the vertebral column and the growth of the spinal canal causes anterior displacement of the posterior cervical dura compressing the spinal cord against the vertebral bodies resulting in myelopathy is one such proposed theory. Another speculation is that the increased laxity of the dura mater which is anchored superiorly at C2-C3, allows for increased cord movement and concomitant anterior displacement with neck flexion, resulting in repetitive cervical microtrauma that leads to micro ischemia of the anterior horn cells.

Hirayama disease often has an insidious onset of progressive weakness and muscular wasting of the upper extremity. The disease appears to be self-limiting, reaching a stable state approximately 3 to 5 years after its onset, a characteristic feature that differentiates this entity from other known motor neuron diseases. Early diagnosis and intervention are crucial for managing Hirayama disease, despite its self-limiting nature. The disease is often unilateral, affecting the left side more than the right, Hirayama disease also presents bilaterally in 10% of cases. Typically, the weakness is in the C7-T1 myotome distribution, affecting extension, flexion, abduction, and adduction of the fingers and wrist, as well as pronation and supination of the forearm. The atrophy accompanying the muscle weakness, sometimes referred to as oblique amyotrophy has a characteristic distribution. It often affects the hand and the ulnar forearm, including the thenar, hypothenar, and interosseous muscles distinctively sparing the brachioradialis muscle. Symptoms can worsen with flexion of the neck and sometimes with cold temperatures, commonly referred to as cold paresis.

The neutral position MRI showed subtle lower cervical cord atrophy and focal anterior horn predominant T2 hyperintensity with no findings of significant spondylotic stenosis, an imaging pattern that should prompt to perform flexion MRI. Flexion imaging confirms the diagnosis by demonstrating the characteristic anterior displacement of the posterior dural sac with widening of the posterior epidural space, prominent epidural flow voids, and contrast enhancement representing engorged epidural venous plexus. According to Chen et al, loss of attachment from posterior dural sac and subjacent lamina is the most valuable diagnostic finding of Hirayama disease at neutral-position MR imaging [3]. Sonwalkar et al. stress the importance of dynamic post contrast study for evaluation of suspected cases of Hirayama disease [5].

Electrophysiology strengthened diagnostic certainty and helped narrow the differentials. Normal sensory nerve conduction and absence of conduction block helps in excluding peripheral entrapment neuropathies. Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis is excluded due to absence of UMN signs clinically, absence of bilateral involvement of lateral corticospinal tract on imaging and lack of widespread EMG abnormalities.

Cervical collar immobilization, being the first-line treatment, is beneficial for most patients [6]. It helps in progression of Hirayama disease by restricting the neck movement and preventing spinal cord compression injury during flexion [7]. Exercise therapy is also advised for patients with Hirayama disease to improve the strength of cervical spine muscles and their functioning. Surgical intervention is indicated when there is disease worsening, intolerance of the cervical collar, no improvement with conservative treatment, or serious symptoms such as severe spinal cord atrophy and positive pyramidal signs [8].

Conclusion

Hirayama disease should be considered in young patients with unilateral distal upper-limb weakness and atrophy. Subtle lower cervical cord atrophy or focal anterior horn–region T2 hyperintensity on neutral MRI should trigger dynamic flexion MRI, which can demonstrate diagnostic anterior dural shift and epidural venous engorgement. Early diagnosis enables timely conservative management with cervical collar immobilization and activity modification, potentially limiting progression.
