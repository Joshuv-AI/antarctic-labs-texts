Immunodominance hierarchy of influenza A haemagglutinin antigenic sites: an observational cohort study across distinct exposure histories
Authors: José Luis Martínez-Guevara, Tsoi Ying Lai, Mitali Mishra, Minh Thu Hoang, William Shea, Caroline Katherine Page, Pamela Sinywimaanzi, Edgar Simulundu, Richard Eric Rothman, Andrew Pekosz, Katherine Zela Joan Fenstermacher, Catherine Gayle Sutcliffe, Viviana Simon, Peter Palese, Weina Sun, Irene González-Domínguez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundAntibody responses to influenza A viruses are strongly focused on immunodominant antigenic sites within the haemagglutinin (HA) head domain. Although immunodominance hierarchies have been described for H1 and H3 subtypes in adults, it remains unclear whether these patterns are shaped by first early-life exposure or by cumulative influenza infections or vaccinations over time.MethodsWe evaluated HA antigenic site immunodominance using haemagglutination inhibition (HI) assays with panels of H1 and H3 mutant viruses lacking individual classical antigenic sites. Sera from two human cohorts with different exposure histories were analysed: adults from New York City with mixed exposures through vaccination and infection, as well as children and adults living in rural Zambia exposed only through infection. Analyses of children with presumed first exposure were conducted to assess how initial influenza exposure shapes immunodominance hierarchies.FindingsAcross both cohorts, H1-specific antibody responses were consistently dominated by antigenic sites Sb and Ca2, regardless of age, geography, or exposure history. For H3, sites A and B shared immunodominance. The first influenza A infection in children was associated with the establishment of these hierarchies. Importantly, influenza B infection did not alter the immunodominance profiles of influenza A H1 or H3 haemagglutinin.InterpretationImmunodominance hierarchies of influenza A haemagglutinin antigenic sites are established early in life following first exposure and appear not to be substantially affected by diverse exposure histories.FundingThis project was financially supported by Center for Excellence on Influenza Research and Response, the Center of Excellence for Influenza Research and Surveillance, the Collaborative Influenza Vaccine Innovation Centers programme as well as institutional funds from the Center for Vaccine Research and Pandemic Preparedness, and the National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases.Keywords: Protection, Epitope targeting, Antibody prevalence, Original antigenic sin, Immune imprinting, Humoural immunity

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched major biomedical literature databases to identify relevant articles published up to May 2026 using combinations of the terms influenza, haemagglutinin, immunodominance, antigenic sites, haemagglutination inhibition, immune imprinting, original antigenic sin, H1N1, and H3N2. We also reviewed the reference lists of relevant articles. Previous studies have shown that antibody responses to influenza A virus target a few dominant sites within the haemagglutinin (HA) head domain. Whether these patterns are shaped by first infection in early life or by cumulative exposures through infection and vaccination has remained unclear. Direct human evidence linking early influenza exposure to long-term HA antigenic immunodominance has been limited.

Added value of this study

Using sera from two distinct cohorts: (1) adults from New York City with mixed vaccination and infection histories and (2) adults and children from rural Zambia exposed only through infection, we demonstrate that HA immunodominance hierarchies of HA antigenic sites are established after the first influenza A infection and remain stable into adulthood. H1-specific responses consistently focus on Sb and Ca2 sites, while H3 responses are dominated by sites A and B. Influenza B infection did not affect these established patterns.

Implications of all the available evidence

Early-life exposure shapes long-lasting antibody responses that preferentially target a limited number of antigenic sites and are largely resistant to modification by subsequent infections. These results help explain the narrow focus of conventional influenza vaccines and highlights the need for vaccine designs that account for early immune imprinting to achieve broader and more durable protection.

Introduction

Influenza A viruses remain a major global public health threat due to their rapid antigenic evolution and ability to cause recurrent seasonal epidemics.1, 2, 3 The viral haemagglutinin (HA) glycoprotein is the principal target of neutralising antibodies and plays a central role in virus attachment, entry into host cells, and immune recognition.4,5 Based on phylogenetic and antigenic properties of HA, influenza A viruses are classified into 19 distinct HA subtypes that cluster into two major phylogenetic groups: group one HAs includes 13 subtypes (H1, H2, H5, H6, H8, H9, H11, H12, H13, H16, H17, H18, and H19) whereas group two HAs comprises six subtypes (H3, H4, H7, H10, H14, and H15). Both phylogenetic groups have contributed substantially to human disease.5,6

All HA proteins share a conserved structure composed of a globular head and a conserved stalk domain.5 Antibody responses are strongly biased towards the HA head, which contains variable immunodominant antigenic sites that are the primary targets of neutralising antibodies.4,5 Each antigenic site contains many epitopes structurally defined as the amino acids on the antigen that contact amino acids of the antibody.7 For H1 (group one HA), five major antigenic sites have been defined: the specific site a (Sa), the specific site b (Sb), the constant site a1 (Ca1), the constant site a2 (Ca2), and the constant site b (Cb) (Fig. 1A).10,11 Similarly, five sites (A–E) have been described for H3 (group two HA) (Fig. 1B).12, 13, 14

Haemagglutination inhibition (HI) titres represent a well-established correlate of protection against influenza virus infection and provide a functional measure of antibody targeting of HA head epitopes.15 Using HI assays, we and others have previously described the immunodominant hierarchies of the antigenic sites for both H1 or H3 in polyclonal serum samples from human adults. For H1, analyses of HI-active antibodies have demonstrated that the immunodominance hierarchy is typically dominated by site Sb, followed by Ca2.16,17 One study suggested that site Sa may also contribute to immunodominant antibody responses.18 For H3, analyses in human cohorts have identified sites A and B as key immunodominant regions; however, it remains unclear whether immunodominance is consistently driven by one of these sites or whether sites A and B can exhibit comparable dominance depending on cohort-specific factors.19, 20, 21, 22

Moreover, it remains unknown whether the observed immunodominance hierarchies of H1 and H3 antigenic sites in adults are primarily shaped by host factors such as early-life initial exposure via infection or vaccination or instead reflect the cumulative effects of repeated influenza exposures over a lifetime.

In this study, we systematically evaluated the immunodominance of HA antigenic sites for both H1 and H3 using HI assays with sera sourced from two human cohorts with distinct influenza virus exposure histories during a similar time frame. One cohort consisted of adults with unknown, but likely mixed, influenza exposures through vaccinations and infections, whereas the second cohort comprised mostly children and some adult donors whose influenza exposure occurred exclusively through infection. Here, we examined whether immunodominance hierarchies are shaped predominantly by early-life imprinting or by cumulative exposures by analysing the immunodominance profiles of immunologically naïve children following influenza infection. Together, this work provides a comparative analysis of antigenic site immunodominance across both H1 and H3 phylogenetic groups and offers new insight into the host and exposure-related determinants that govern antibody targeting of influenza virus haemagglutinin.

Methods

Human cohorts

Ethics

Two human cohorts were used for this work:

The first cohort (designated as the USA cohort) comprised sera obtained from 59 participants enrolled in an IRB-approved, observational study (Protection Associated with Rapid Immunity to SARS-CoV-2, STUDY-20-00442)23 performed at the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai in New York City. All participants provided written informed consent prior to data and sample collection. This study adheres to the ethical principles of the 2024 Declaration of Helsinki. Sera were selected from study visits occurring during either the 2021–2022 or the 2022–2023 winter season. Participant individual data including age and sex assigned at birth is reported in Supplementary Table S1. Sera were stored at −80 °C until use.

The second cohort (designated as the Zambia cohort) consisted of paired serum samples obtained from 83 participants enrolled in a respiratory infection surveillance programme established at Macha Hospital in Southern Province, Zambia. The hospital is in a rural area populated primarily by subsistence farmers. The respiratory infection surveillance programme established at Macha Hospital in Southern Province of Zambia as part of the Johns Hopkins Center for Excellence in Influenza Research and Surveillance (JHCEIRS) was approved by the Johns Hopkins School of Medicine Institutional Review Board (IRB00168163), the Macha Research Trust Institutional Review Board (E.2018.02), and the Zambian National Health Research Authority. Adult participants and parents or legal guardians of child participants provided written informed consent prior to data/sample collection. In addition, children 12–15 years of age provided written assent.

The samples were collected in 2019 and 2021 from patients testing positive for influenza A or influenza B infections by polymerase chain reaction (PCR). The Zambia cohort samples were not collected based on a defined “winter season” as influenza circulation in this region does not follow the same seasonality observed in the USA cohort. Instead, influenza A virus activity in Zambia typically occurs during the colder months (approximately May–August), whereas influenza B virus activity is more commonly observed from the late cold season into the hot season (approximately August–December).24,25 A baseline blood sample was collected within 10 days of illness onset and a follow-up blood sample collected between 18 and 54 days after enrolment. Age, PCR test results, sex assigned at birth, year and month of sample collection, and the collection times for baseline and follow-up samples are reported in Supplementary Table S2. The serum obtained from blood samples was stored at −80 °C until use.

Biospecimen handling

Prior to HI assay, all serum samples were treated with Vibrio cholerae receptor-destroying enzyme (RDE) (II) “SEIKEN” (Hardy Diagnostics, Santa Maria, CA, USA, Cat. No. 370013) as described previously.26 Briefly, serum was mixed with RDE in a 1:3 ratio (vol/vol). RDE treated samples were incubated at 37 °C for 18–20 h and the reaction was stopped by the addition of 2.5% sodium citrate solution (Fisher scientific, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA, Cat. No. BP327-1) in a 1:3 ratio (vol/vol). The samples were then heat treated at 56 °C for 30 min. Serum was finally diluted with sterile 1X phosphate-buffered saline pH 7.4 (PBS) (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA, Cat. No. 10010023) to reach a final dilution of 1:10.

Panel of recombinant H1 viruses

The methods and description of the generation of mutant viruses (H1-ΔSa, H1-ΔSb, H1-ΔCa1, H1-ΔCa2, and H1-ΔCb) have been previously published.18 The mutant viruses were constructed by substitution of the classic antigenic sites (Sa, Sb, Ca1, Ca2, and Cb) of the H1 A/Michigan/45/2015 strain with the corresponding heterologous sequences of H5 (A/Vietnam/1203/2004). Each mutant virus contained five or more amino acid substitutions within one antigenic site, while the other four sites were not altered (Supplementary Figure S5). The viruses were rescued as 7:1 reassortants expressing the HA mutant protein with all other segments from the A/Puerto Rico/8/1934 (PR8) strain. Viruses were grown in 10-day-old embryonated chicken eggs for 48 h at 37 °C, then haemagglutination assays were performed to confirm the growth of each virus before freezing at −80 °C. Before the experiments, the sequence of the HA segments from each virus was confirmed by Sanger sequencing.

For HI assays, we also used a PR8 virus expressing a chimeric cH5/1 HA, in which the entire head domain of the A/Michigan/45/2015 HA was replaced with an H5 head domain, as well as a PR8 mutant virus expressing a mosaic HA in which all defined antigenic sites of the A/Michigan/45/2015 HA were replaced with H5-derived sequences (mH5/1); both viruses have been described previously.18

Panel of recombinant H3 viruses

Rescue and characterisation of H3 mutant viruses (H3-ΔA, H3-ΔB, H3-ΔC, H3-ΔD, and H3-ΔE) lacking one antigenic site have been previously described.22 Briefly, the mutant viruses were created by replacing the classic antigenic sites (A, B, C, D, and E) of the H3 A/Hong Kong/4801/2014 strain with the corresponding heterologous sequences of H10 (A/Jiangxi-Donghu/346-1/2013). Each mutant virus contained three or more amino acid substitutions within one antigenic site, while the other four sites were intact (Supplementary Figure S6). The viruses were rescued as 7:1 reassortants expressing the H3 mutant protein in the PR8 backbone. We also employed a PR8 virus expressing a chimeric cH10/1 HA in which the entire head domain of A/Hong Kong/4801/2014 HA was replaced with an H10 head domain and a PR8 virus expressing a mosaic mH10/1 HA in which all classic antigenic sites were replaced with H10 sequences; both viruses have been described previously.22

Viruses were grown in 10-day-old embryonated chicken eggs for 48 h at 37 °C, growth was confirmed by haemagglutination assays, each virus was saved at −80 °C, and the sequence of the HA segment from each virus was confirmed by Sanger sequencing.

Haemagglutination inhibition (HI) assay

Chicken red blood cells (LAMPIRE Biological Laboratories, Pipersville, PA, USA, Cat. No. 7201403) were washed in PBS and resuspended at a concentration of 0.5% red blood cells. Two-fold serial dilutions of the RDE-treated human samples were done in 25 μl across a 96–well V-bottom plate. Allantoic fluid containing wild-type or mutant viruses was diluted to eight HA-units and then incubated in equal volumes with the RDE-treated human serum (25 μl each) for 30 min at 25 °C. Fifty microlitres of chicken red blood cells (0.5% in PBS) were then added. Finally, the plates were incubated for 30 min at 4 °C and the HI titres were visually determined.

Statistics

Only samples with detectable HI activity against the wild-type viruses were included in antigenic site immunodominance analyses. Categorical variables (age, sex, and infection) used to describe the cohorts are reported as counts and percentages. Haemagglutination inhibition (HI) titres, which represent the primary outcome measurement, are reported as reciprocal serum dilutions. The limit of detection of the HI assay was defined as a titre of 10, corresponding to the initial 10-fold dilution obtained after RDE treatment of serum samples. HI titre distributions are presented using box-and-whisker plots with individual data points overlaid. Box plots display the median and interquartile range, with whiskers indicating the range of the data. Friedman test (non-parametric test for paired data) was used to compare HI titres between wild-type and mutant viruses within individuals. This test was selected due to the non-normal distribution of the data, as assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and the Shapiro–Wilk test. When appropriate, post hoc comparisons were conducted using Dunn's multiple comparisons test. Rank distributions derived from the Friedman test are visualised using violin plots to illustrate the distribution and density of the data across conditions. A p-value ≤0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10.0.2 for Mac OS X; GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA).

Role of funders

The funders of the study had no role in the study design, data collection, data analyses, interpretation, writing of the report, or decision to submit it for publication.

Results

Characteristics of human cohorts

To assess the immunodominance of HA antigenic sites in representative viruses from both phylogenetic groups of influenza A, we analysed HI titres from human serum samples obtained from two distinct cohorts. Most participants in the USA cohort were between 30 and 39 years of age (39%). Additional age groups included individuals aged 18–29 years (5%), 40–49 years (24%), 50–59 years (13%), and older than 60 years (19%). The cohort included similar numbers of female (49%) and male (51%) participants, with sex defined based on sex assigned at birth (Fig. 2A and Supplementary Table S1).

Immunodominance analysis of influenza A antigenic sites from group one and group two HA proteins in individuals with distinct influenza exposure histories

To assess the immunodominance of influenza A antigenic sites from group one and group two HAs in human cohorts, we performed HI assays using two panels of mutant viruses. The first panel of mutants consisted of H1-based viruses (group one HA) expressing the HA of A/Michigan/45/2015. In each H1 mutant virus, a single antigenic site was replaced (“silenced”) with a heterologous sequence derived from H5. The second panel of mutants consisted of H3-based viruses (group two HA) expressing the HA of A/Hong Kong/4801/2014, in which individual antigenic sites were replaced (“silenced”) with heterologous sequences derived from H10. The contribution of each antigenic site was determined by comparing serum HI titres against wild-type (Wt) HA viruses containing all classical antigenic sites (H1-Wt or H3-Wt) with HI titres against mutant viruses lacking a single antigenic site. A significant reduction in HI titres against a given mutant relative to its corresponding Wt virus was interpreted as evidence for the presence of antibodies targeting that specific antigenic site.

HI assays performed on samples from both cohorts revealed that many sera did not display HI activity against either the H1 or H3 viruses. Serum samples from one participant in the USA cohort (USA_Donor_35) and 36 participants in the Zambia cohort (Zambia donors: 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 19, 20, 22, 23, 24, 26, 27, 28, 30, 31, 34, 35, 38, 46, 49, 50, 52, 61, 64, 65, 66, 67, 69, 71, 74, 75, 76, 80, 81, 82, and 83) did not display HI activity against the H1 viruses tested. Similarly, serum samples from one participant in the USA cohort (USA_Donor_7) and 13 participants in the Zambia cohort (Zambia donors: 3, 5, 6, 8, 9, 18, 29, 44, 45, 56, 60, 68, and 73) did not exhibit HI activity against the H3 viruses tested (Supplementary Figures S1–4). To enable evaluation of antigenic site immunodominance for each HA subtype, only serum samples with HI activity were included in the analyses. Supplementary Table S3 lists the samples included in each immunodominance analysis.

Analysis of sera from the USA cohort with the H1 mutant panel, revealed significant reductions in HI titres against mutants lacking the Sb (H1-ΔSb) or Ca2 (H1-ΔCa2) sites compared with H1-Wt, indicating Sb and Ca2 as the dominant antigenic sites (Fig. 2C). Consistent with this observation, follow-up sera from the Zambia cohort also showed a significant reduction in the HI titres against H1-ΔSb and H1-ΔCa2 (Fig. 2E). We also observed a significant reduction in HI titres against H1-ΔSa in follow-up sera from the Zambia cohort, suggesting an additional contribution of Sa to the immunodominant antibody response after infection (Fig. 2E). In both cohorts, HI titres against mutants lacking Ca1 (H1-ΔCa1), or Cb (H1-ΔCb) were comparable to those against H1-Wt, indicating that these sites were not immunodominant. No detectable HI activity was observed for any serum sample against the cH5/1 virus, which contains a complete H5 head domain, suggesting that antibodies against the head domain of the A/Michigan/45/2015 strain did not cross-react with the H5 head domain. In addition, HI activity against the mosaic virus expressing the mH5/1 HA, in which all defined antigenic sites were replaced, was largely abolished, confirming the effective removal of the original immunodominant antigenic sites (Fig. 2C and E). Rank-based comparisons derived from the Friedman test used to determine the statistical significance between groups are shown in Supplementary Figure S7.

For H3, site B emerged as the most immunodominant site in the USA cohort, as evidenced by the significant reduction of HI titres against H3-ΔB, compared with H3-Wt (Fig. 2D). Additionally, although median HI titres (red lines shown in Fig. 2D) were comparable between H3-Wt and H3-ΔA, paired statistical analysis using the Friedman test indicated a statistical consistent reduction in HI titres across individuals when site A was removed, as reflected in the rank distributions calculated by the Friedman test (Supplementary Figure S7), indicating a systematic reduction in HI titres within-donors (Fig. 2D), regardless of overlapping absolute median values of the groups. In contrast, HI titres against mutants lacking site C (H3-ΔC), site D (H3-ΔD) or site E (H3-ΔE) were comparable to those against H3-Wt, indicating that these sites were not immunodominant (Fig. 2D). Similarly, the Zambia cohort follow-up sera collected after infection displayed significant reductions in HI titres against H3-ΔA and H3-ΔB, demonstrating a defined immunodominance profile led by sites A and B (Fig. 2F). HI titres against mutants lacking sites C (H3-ΔC), D (H3-ΔD), or E (H3-ΔE) were comparable to those against H3-Wt, suggesting a lack of immunodominance for these sites (Fig. 2F). Rank-based comparisons calculated from the Friedman test are shown in Supplemental Figure S7.

In both cohorts, no serum sample exhibited detectable HI activity against the cH10/3 virus, which contains a complete H10 head domain, confirming the absence of cross-reactive antibodies targeting H10. Finally, the significant reduction in HI activity against the mosaic virus expressing mH10/3 HA validated the successful removal of classical antigenic sites (Fig. 2D and F).

Immunodominance of antigenic sites of influenza A group one and group two HA proteins are established as early as the first exposure

We next investigated whether immunodominance hierarchies of influenza A HA antigenic sites are shaped by first exposure during early childhood or instead reflect the cumulative effects of repeated influenza virus exposures over time. To address this, we restricted the immunodominance analysis to children (≤12 years old) from the Zambia cohort with no detectable baseline HI titres against the wild-type viruses who subsequently exhibited seroconversion following PCR-confirmed influenza A infection (Fig. 3A). Children ≤12 years old were selected for this analysis, as they are the individuals most likely to be undergoing a primary influenza A exposure.

In children with no detectable HI-reactive antibodies against H1 mutant viruses at baseline, influenza A infection induced an H1 immunodominance profile primarily driven by the Sb and Ca2 antigenic sites, as judged by a significant reduction in HI titres against H1-ΔSb and H1-ΔCa2 compared with H1-Wt. In contrast, HI titre against H1-ΔSa, H1-ΔCa1, and H1-ΔCb were similar to those against H1-Wt, suggesting that these sites were not immunodominant early in life (Fig. 3B, Supplementary Table S4).

Analysis of H3 antigenic site immunodominance in children with no detectable HI activity against H3 viruses at baseline showed that influenza A infection elicited an immunodominance profile dominated by antigenic site A, as indicated by a significant reduction in HI titres against H3-ΔA (Fig. 3C, Supplementary Table S4). In contrast, although we observed a trend towards reduced HI titres against the H3-ΔB virus, this reduction was not statistically significant (p-value = 0.1085) (Fig. 3C). Rank-based comparisons derived from the Friedman test used to determine the statistical significance between groups are shown in Supplementary Figure S8.

Influenza B infection does not affect the immunodominance of antigenic sites of influenza A group one and group two HA proteins

Given that a subset of donors from the Zambia cohort tested positive for influenza B infection, we investigated whether this kind of infection influenced the immunodominance of antigenic sites on influenza A HAs (Fig. 4A). As shown in Fig. 4B and C, all participants with confirmed influenza B infection exhibited high HI titres against the tested H1 and H3 viruses at baseline. Notably, influenza B infection did not induce substantial changes in the HI titres displayed by these donors against either the H1 or H3 mutant panels.

Consistent with our previous findings, the immunodominance hierarchy for H1 in the Zambia cohorts remained dominated by antigenic site Sb as indicated by the significant reduction of HI titres against H1-ΔSb. Additionally, although not statistically significant, we also observed a trend towards reduced HI titres for H1-ΔSa and H1-ΔCa2.

For H3, we also observed trends towards reduced HI titres against H3-ΔA and H3-ΔB; however, these reductions were not statistically significant. Rank-based comparisons derived from the Friedman test used to determine the statistical significance between groups are shown in Supplementary Figure S9.

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the immunodominance hierarchies of HA antigenic sites from influenza A viruses belonging to both major phylogenetic groups and assessed how these hierarchies are shaped by exposure history and age. By using two human cohorts with markedly different influenza exposure backgrounds, we provide evidence that the immunodominance hierarchies of both H1 and H3 HA head epitopes are dominated by specific antigenic sites regardless of immune history or geographic origin. Across both cohorts, the immunodominance profile of H1 HA was consistently dominated by antigenic sites Sb and Ca2. Of note, site Sa also contributed to H1 immunodominance in the Zambia cohort but not in the USA cohort. Although the reason for this difference is unclear, it may be related to differences in the characteristics of the cohorts analysed. The USA cohort comprised only adults with mixed influenza exposures, whereas the Zambia cohort primarily consisted of children (83%) and some adults (17%) exposed exclusively through influenza infection. For H3 HA, site B and site A were the most immunodominant antigenic sites. Our findings corroborate prior reports identifying these sites as dominant targets of HI-active antibodies.16,17,19,22 Consistent with the younger age and likely a more limited influenza exposure history, we observed a lower overall seropositivity against both H1 and H3 viruses in the Zambia cohort as compared with the USA cohort, that did not seem to change the immunodominance pattern between the two cohorts beyond the Sa contribution. Notably, influenza B infection did not alter the immunodominance hierarchies of influenza A HA antigenic sites, suggesting that heterologous influenza B virus infections are not sufficient to remodel pre-existing HA-specific immunodominance patterns.16,17,19,22 Importantly, the H1 mutant viruses used in this study have been validated using panels of human monoclonal antibodies, demonstrating that disruption of an individual antigenic site selectively abolishes recognition to that site while preserving binding to the remaining ones.27 Therefore, the observed reduction in HI titres in serum samples using the mutant virus panels should be interpreted as a loss in HI activity to one site and not as a consequence of structural cross-disruption between antigenic sites or the presence of bispecific antibodies targeting multiple epitopes.

Moreover, the HI assay may not provide a linear measure of epitope occupancy or the contribution of individual antibody populations. Instead, it may represent a threshold-based functional readout, in which inhibition is achieved only when sufficient HA molecules are occupied to prevent red blood cell haemagglutination. Consequently, relatively modest changes in the fraction of functional HA molecules or in the composition of the polyclonal antibody repertoire can produce disproportionate reductions in measured HI titres, including transitions from robust inhibition to titres below the limit of detection as seen in many donors in the cohort (Raw HI titre values of USA cohorts [Data Raw_1] and Raw HI titre values of Zambia cohorts [Data Raw_2]) and also in previous works.16, 17, 18,22 Additional studies isolating monoclonal antibodies from these complex polyclonal mixtures can bring light to this matter.

Three-dimensional structural comparison of the most consistent immunodominant antigenic regions found in H1 (Sb, Ca2) and H3 (site B, site A) HAs shows that Sb and site B are located in an exposed α-helix adjacent to the receptor-binding site (RBS) pocket, whereas Ca2 and site A are located in analogous protruding loops extending from the local HA molecular surface (Fig. 5).28 From a biophysical perspective, these regions are solvent-exposed and close to the RBS which may confer a functional advantage during immune selection and promote the expansion of these B cell clones. These same regions have been associated with higher predicted mutation rates in the HA.29,30 Previous mutagenesis studies from our group demonstrated that Sb and Ca2 in H1 and site B and site A in H3 are highly tolerant to insertions, highlighting the structural flexibility of these regions to accommodate new amino acid changes.31,32 Furthermore, key amino acid substitutions contained within these regions have been associated with antigenic drift of H1N1 and H3N2 viruses.33,34 Altogether, these observations support a model in which structural exposure and mutational tolerance jointly explain why Sb/Ca2 (H1) and site A/site B (H3) repeatedly emerge as dominant antigenic regions.

On the other hand, the role of the rest of the antigenic regions remains unclear. Their flexibility,31,32 mutation rate,1 and HI activity nonetheless suggest ongoing relevance to influenza virus evolution. Recent studies suggest the need of epistatic mutations as key factors for viral fitness and virus immune evasion,35, 36, 37 suggesting that adaptive mutations within Sb/Ca2 or sites B/A may depend on compensatory changes elsewhere in the HA. Additionally, residual HI activity was detected against the H1 and H3 mosaic viruses lacking all five classically defined antigenic sites. Currently, the reason for this residual HI activity is unclear, but one possibility may be related to antibodies directed against non-classical epitopes within HI active sites that can still contribute to haemagglutination inhibition, including conserved domains like the RBS.18,22

Influenza virus-specific antibody responses are largely oligoclonal and biased, with fewer than 10 clonotypes often accounting for up to ∼60% of the serum antibody response that is recalled and expanded after repeated exposures.38,39 The current results support that, whether at first exposure to H1 or H3 or after repeated infections and hybrid immunity, the pattern of immunodominance remains largely stable. These findings imply that most humans maintain antibody responses that are focused mainly on a limited number of HA head regions, which constrains and shapes their influenza A-specific immunity over the life course. Recent characterisations of human influenza antibody repertoires have identified preferential use of specific VH3-family genes in antibodies targeting HA head epitopes, many of which provide strain-specific immunity.40, 41, 42 The combination of high epitope accessibility and the large pool of naïve B cells that can readily recognise these surfaces may explain the universal preference for these immunodominant head regions.

Finally, our findings are consistent with a recent work by Sun et al.,27 which demonstrated that early-life B cell imprinting can constrain the development of broadly cross-reactive B cell responses, particularly those targeting the conserved HA stalk. In line with these observations, our results indicate that early influenza exposure also establishes a stable hierarchy of immunodominant antigenic sites within the HA head domain. Together, these findings support a model in which immune imprinting not only limits antibody breadth but also defines the specificity of the response by preferentially focussing antibodies on a restricted set of antigenic sites.

This study has several limitations. First, we focused on HI-active antibodies to define immunodominance, using two previously developed mutant-virus panels based on historical vaccine strains, A/Michigan/45/2015 and A/Hong Kong/4081/2014. Antigenic drift has occurred between these reporter strains and the circulating viruses over the years that likely shaped the participants’ antibody repertoires, so antibodies targeting epitopes not present or not preserved in these two reporter viruses may have been underrepresented in this study. Second, HI assays primarily measure antibodies that interfere with receptor binding and therefore do not capture non-HI neutralising antibodies or antibodies that act predominantly through Fc-mediated effector functions, which may also contribute substantially to protection. Third, although our cohorts include diverse age groups, sex assigned at birth and exposure histories, some subgroup analyses were based on relatively small sample sizes, limiting the power to detect more subtle differences. Fourth, at present, we do not have sufficient information regarding the specific influenza A subtype (H1 or H3) responsible for infection in each donor or information on influenza exposure history of the cohorts. As a result, we were unable to reliably assess how infection with group 1 viruses may influence group 2 immunodominance, or vice versa, nor could we identify the reason for the differences in seropositivity between cohorts. Additional serology analyses on those samples might bring light to this matter. Fifth, although most participants showed stable or increased HI titres between baseline and follow-up, a small proportion of Zambia donors exhibited decreases in follow-up HI titres relative to baseline (Supplementary Figure S10). These minor discrepancies between first and second blood serum collection highlight the inherent variability of the HI assay and should be considered when assessing assay robustness.

Furthermore, while our study includes two cohorts with markedly different geographic and exposure backgrounds, the generalisability of these findings to other populations should be considered with caution. Both cohorts represent specific epidemiological contexts (an urban, vaccination-experienced population in New York City and a predominantly infection-only, rural population in Zambia) which may not fully capture the diversity of influenza exposure histories globally. For instance, populations with different viral circulation patterns may exhibit variations in immunodominance profiles. Nevertheless, the consistency of the immunodominance hierarchies observed across these two distinct cohorts suggests that the dominance of specific HA antigenic sites may reflect fundamental immunological and structural constraints. Future studies including additional cohorts from diverse geographic regions and longitudinal designs will be important to further validate the extent to which these patterns are broadly conserved. Finally, direct comparison of individual antigenic profiles and their potential roles as correlates of protection were beyond the scope of this study. Larger, longitudinal studies incorporating expanded antigen panels and complementary functional assays are needed to clarify how demographic factors, antigenic drift, repeated exposures, and vaccination shape immunodominance and influenza immunity over the lifespan.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that immunodominance hierarchies of influenza A HA antigenic sites are established early after first exposure, conserved across diverse exposure histories, and are resistant to modification by heterologous influenza infections. These findings provide new insight into how structural and repertoire-level constraints channel antibody responses toward specific HA head regions and thereby influence both individual immunity and the antigenic evolution of influenza A viruses.
