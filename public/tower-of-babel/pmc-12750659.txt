Emergency decompression of rare tension hydrothorax with obstructive shock: a case series
Authors: Nileena George, Devin Dromgoole, Bradley Kraft, Joel Kravitz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTension hydrothorax is a life-threatening condition caused by a massive buildup of fluid in the pleural space, compressing the mediastinum and compromising cardiac output. It causes obstructive shock and can lead to cardiac arrest. Early detection and rapid intervention are essential to prevent cardiorespiratory collapse.Case seriesThis is a case series of three patients who presented with a tension hydrothorax and signs of hemodynamic instability. Initial imaging showed a complete white-out of the chest with a mediastinal shift, which required an emergent chest tube thoracostomy.ConclusionAlthough decompressive interventions are common, courses and awareness can minimize potential complications when managing emergency conditions such as tension hydrothorax, which remains a medical challenge.Keywords: Tension hydrothorax, Obstructive shock, Malignancy, Emergency management, Video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS)

Introduction

Tension hydrothorax is a rare, life-threatening condition characterized by the accumulation of fluid in the pleural space causing increased intrathoracic pressure and cardiovascular compromise. Malignant pleural effusions are a common complication in advanced cancer, often leading to large unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions. However, progression to tension physiology from atraumatic pleural effusions or hemothoraces is rare. This case series.

highlights three cases of tension hydrothorax that presented to a community emergency department (ED). The diagnostic hurdles, emergency management, and clinical course of these three rare cases are discussed in this paper. Informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to publication.

Case #1

A 61 year-old male presented to the Emergency Department (ED) with shortness of breath, hypoxia and hypotension. The patient had lethargy and dyspnea for one week, acutely worsening over the past 4 days. Initial vital signs were: HR 145 bpm in atrial fibrillation, BP 98/67 mmHg, 97% Sp on 15 L NRB, RR 24 breaths per minute. On examination, he was somnolent and ill-appearing with ashen skin, in severe respiratory distress with accessory muscle use and retractions. Breath sounds were absent on the left side. Portable chest x-ray revealed “white out” of the left lung with significant tracheal deviation to right (Fig. 1a). An 18-gauge long spinal needle was inserted at the left 2nd intercostal space midclavicular line with immediate return of blood (Fig. 1b). The patient’s respiratory status continued to deteriorate and intubation was performed while simultaneously a right femoral central venous line was placed. A 28-Fr chest tube was placed on the left side at the midclavicular line with immediate expulsion of 3,000mL of bright red pleural fluid. The chest tube was subsequently clamped to prevent further drainage. The patient became hypotensive and tachycardic requiring norepinephrine drip for blood pressure augmentation.

Preliminary interpretation of Computed Tomography Angiography (CTA) of the Chest was inconclusive for possible type B aortic dissection and the patient was transferred to a tertiary referral center via air for possible vascular surgery intervention (Fig. 1c). As such, Massive Transfusion Protocol was activated and the patient was given 4U (units) Packed Red Blood Cells (PRBC), 2U platelets, and 1U Fresh Frozen Plasma (FFP) while awaiting transfer. Final CTA reading showed no evidence of aortic dissection, but narrowing of left upper lobe pulmonary artery branch due to surrounding and invading pathologic soft tissue mass. The patient required an additional left-sided chest tube due to large output and rapidly reaccumulating pleural effusion.Cytology revealed exudative effusion, likely from Stage IV prostate cancer. He was extubated two days after admission and discharged fifteen days later with a plan for outpatient malignancy work up.

Case #2

A 74 year old female with past medical history of Stage 1B lung adenocarcinoma, status post right upper lobe lobectomy, presented to the ED with shortness of breath. The patient had worsening shortness of breath for the past week, prompting activation of Emergency Medical Services (EMS). Upon EMS arrival, she was found to be experiencing new-onset atrial flutter with rapid ventricular response at a rate of 160 bpm. She was treated with diltiazem 0.25 mg/kg in the field without improvement. Upon arrival, the patient was found to still be in atrial flutter at 160 bpm. She was initially rate controlled after an additional 0.35 mg/kg diltiazem bolus and initiation of a titratable diltiazem infusion. However, the patient ultimately required an amiodarone infusion to control her rate in the ED. Despite normalization of her heart rate with amiodarone, she continued to decompensate. She became progressively hypoxic and hypotensive, with blood pressure reading as low as 95/74 mmHg.

The patient was found to have decreased lung sounds on the right during her initial examination. She was ultimately found to have a large right sided pleural effusion with almost complete opacification of the lung on chest x-ray (Fig. 2a) later confirmed by CT (Fig. 2b). Imaging was concerning for tension physiology given that the patient had severe mediastinal shift. As such, an emergent right sided chest tube was placed with immediate improvement in the patient’s vital signs and subjective symptoms (Fig. 2c). The chest tube produced nearly 2 L of serosanguinous fluid following initial placement. The patient was admitted to the Intensive Care Unit (ICU). Pleural fluid cytology ultimately revealed malignant cells, positive for adenocarcinoma. While admitted, the patient underwent video assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) guided biopsy of a lung nodule found on CT which confirmed the diagnosis of adenocarcinoma. Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) later revealed suspected metastasis to the brain. The patient was stabilized and ultimately discharged to subacute rehab, where she began chemotherapy with weekly right-sided thoracentesis. After seven months of chemotherapy, the patient elected hospice.

Case #3

A 48 year old female with a past medical history of anoxic brain injury and.insulin-dependent diabetes presented to the ED with shortness of breath. The patient was being treated for pneumonia outpatient over the last three days, however she had acutely worsening dyspnea that prompted EMS evaluation. On EMS arrival, the patient had an oxygen saturation of 82% Sp that improved to 92% Sp with 15 L/min via nonrebreather. Physical examination revealed decreased lung sounds on the left. The patient was tachypneic and tachycardic at a rate of 150 bpm with a blood pressure of 169/85 mmHg. Broad spectrum antibiotics were administered with an IV crystalloid fluid bolus.

A portable chest x-ray demonstrated complete opacification of the left hemithorax with rightward mediastinal shift, suggestive of a massive pleural effusion (Fig. 3a). The patient’s work of breathing began to increase and was trialed on bilevel positive airway pressure (BiPAP), however began to demonstrate signs of altered mental status and the decision was made to emergently intubate. A CTA chest was obtained to further characterize the pleural effusion and mediastinal shift (Fig. 3b). A 24-Fr chest tube was inserted and returned approximately 1.5 L of yellow fluid throughout the ED course. A repeat chest x-ray showed an almost complete resolution of left hemithorax opacification and mediastinal shift (Fig. 3 d). The patient’s pleural fluid culture grew Streptococcus intermedius and was continued on a course of ceftriaxone. During her hospital course she had a recurrence of the left pleural effusion requiring the placement of an additional chest tube. CT scan revealed a loculated effusion requiring VATS for decortication and partial pleurectomy (Fig. 3c). She was eventually discharged to subacute rehab after a three week hospital stay.

Discussion

Pathophysiologically, a massive pleural effusion (MPE) works similarly to a. massive hemothorax or a tension pneumothorax, with the essential difference being that the latter is an accumulation of air. The elevated intrathoracic pressure gives a tamponade-like effect, compromising cardiac output and diastolic function [1].

Pleural effusions, hemorrhagic or otherwise, are subdivided into transudative or. exudative based on Light’s criteria [2]. Unilateral effusions are typically exudates, resulting from localized capillary permeability caused by conditions such as pneumonia or other pulmonary infections, cancer and pancreatitis. Transudates, by contrast, are more often bilateral, arising from systemic causes such as fluid overload, cirrhosis or congestive heart failure, among others.

While there are no definitive studies looking at pleural fluid with tension physiology specifically, some epidemiological data exists with respect to massive pleural effusion etiology [3]. Porcel and his colleagues conducted a large retrospective study of over 750 pleural effusions filling over 2/3 of the hemithorax. In a 10 year retrospective review, malignancy was the cause of the MPE in over half the cases, with parapneumonic effusions and emphysema causing 22%. Tuberculosis (TB) seems to be a common cause of MPE, though it’s true incidence tends to vary based on population and location. TB was seen in 12% of all MPE cases in Porcel’s review, though, as high as 37% in a review of patients with MPE in Pakistan [4]. Another smaller series revealed that MPE’s are more likely to be due to malignancy or cirrhosis and less likely to be infectious or congestive heart failure.

Hemothorax is defined as a collection of blood in the pleural space, is most frequently traumatic in origin, with non-traumatic causes being far less common [5]. The fluid hematocrit is not an absolute diagnostic criteria, as a true hemothorax can be diluted with pleural fluid in a couple of days, resembling a hemorrhagic effusion [6]. One series placed traumatic causes at approximately 75%, with iatrogenic causes at almost 25%, and spontaneous causes under 1%, though this was in a series of elderly patients [7]. Given the higher incidence of thoracic trauma in younger patients, the relative proportion of spontaneous hemothorax is likely even smaller. As a result, the literature surrounding spontaneous hemothoraces is relegated mostly to case reports or small case series [6, 8, 9].

Causes of hemothorax are threefold: coagulopathic, vascular or neoplastic, without a true understanding of their relative frequency due to their rarity. Although unconfirmed, it is likely that coagulopathy represents the most common cause of SHTX, if only for the degree of usage of anticoagulants in the population. With anticoagulation, either minimal trauma or. spontaneous rupture of small pleural blood vessels can lead to a hemothorax. Case reports have related spontaneous hemothorax to pulmonary embolism8, hemophilia and other coagulopathies [10]. Administration of anticoagulants, including warfarin, heparin and tissue plasminogen activator (TPA), causing spontaneous hemothorax have all been reported [8, 11].

Hemothoraces of vascular origin can be due to various causes. Cases of death with.severe or massive hemothorax are most often due to aortic disease [12], and almost always affect the left pleura. The smaller ascending aorta has a propensity to bleed into the pericardium rather than either pleural space. While surgery is indicated for aortic aneurysms and ascending. aortic dissections, thoracic drainage should be approached with extreme caution and likely be deferred; thoracic drainage would serve as a low pressure outflow for the hemorrhage and likely lead to clinical instability. Vascular malformations, either in systemic diseases (like hereditary telangiectasia) or as isolated lesions, can cause hemothoraces. However, the arteriovenous malformations usually result in intrabronchial rupture, causing hemoptysis clinically, with hemothorax being less common.

Multiple types of neoplasia have been described to cause both hemothorax as well as hydrothorax, including lung cancer (most commonly), bone and soft tissue tumors, vascular tumors like angiosarcomas, germ cell tumors, thymus, and (rarely) hepatocellular carcinoma. Despite the suspected obvious association, primary lung cancer is not a common cause of spontaneous hemothorax (SHTX), with few case reports in the literature [13, 14]. Thymic tumors and angiosarcomas both have rare reports of hemothorax, arising from direct invasion of the pleural space by the cancer itself.

In the ED, the diagnosis is made largely on clinical and radiographic grounds. In the case of MPE or massive hemothorax causing opacification of the entire lung field, special attention should be paid to the patient’s position during the X-ray. A patient lying supine may have a concomitant pneumothorax which could be missed as air layers out above the fluid over the course of the lung field, as it did in our first patient. If the suspicion is present, a lateral film or a film with the patient even partially sitting up may show air along with the effusion.

With respect to management, treatment options depend to a large extent on the magnitude of the fluid collection as well as its rapidity, the stability of the patient, and the proximate cause. Most pleural effusions do not require emergent thoracentesis in the ED unless respiratory or circulatory collapse is present. Correction of coagulopathy is needed for every cause of hemothorax. With smaller fluid collections (less than 300 cc’s), conservative management may.

be indicated. Reviews of traumatic hemothoraces suggest that volumes below 300 cc’s were somewhere between 72 and 92% likely to eventually resolve without intervention [15].

Conversely, non-traumatic hemothorax may require different considerations in terms of ascertaining etiology. Tube thoracostomy is considered the first line management options for almost all hemothoraces. Early surgical approach would certainly be favored in the case of massive hemothorax, defined as more than 500 cc of blood drained in the first hour or 200–300 cc/hr in subsequent hours.While tube thoracostomy is almost always necessary in cases of symptomatic MPE, care should be given to avoid further complications such as pneumothorax or re-expansion pulmonary edema (RPE). As such, the American College of Chest Physicians counsel draining no more than 1 L of fluid at any one time [16] with the British Thoracic Society setting a limit of 1.5 L [17], although a small retrospective analysis did suggest no significant increases in RPE (or other complications) when more volume was removed [18]. In the absence of tension, thought could be given to using a pigtail catheter - the decreased flow rate would allow steady equilibration of pleural and pulmonary pressure over time and thus reduce the risk of re-expansion induced edema, although neither of our reported cases would have been amenable to this consideration. This could be considered with stable patients with known prior effusions where the medical etiology is understood.

While drainage may pose risk in certain clinical scenarios, the clot, if left undrained, will organize in the pleural space, making it even harder to remove. The clotting process is of greatest concern along the visceral and parietal pleura. After formation of the fibrin clot, the membrane. thickens due to fibroblast proliferation, a process that takes an estimated 7–10 days. As a result, a hemothorax that is not removed after approximately 10 days will be very difficult to remove and will likely require decortication.

Conclusion

Massive pleural effusion and tension hydrothoraces, in the absence of trauma, present a unique set of therapeutic and diagnostic challenges. This case series and subsequent discussion highlights and reviews the dilemmas in caring for these severely ill patients.
