An image encryption scheme using PRESENT-RC4, chaos and secure key generation
Authors: Krishna Kumar, Satyabrata Roy, Dev Puri, Ravinder Kumar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
As the demand for secure visual data transmission grows, specifically concerning the areas of medical imaging, surveillance, and secure communications, image encryption has emerged as a popular research area. Traditional ciphers such as Rivest Cipher 4 (RC4) are fast but present challenges in the form of a biased keystream along with weak key scheduling. For this reason, the use of RC4 alone is undesirable. In this paper, a hybrid scheme is discussed for encrypting digital images that uses RC4 and combines it with chaotic transformations, along with nonlinear cryptography, to help enhance the security of normal RC4. In the first stage of the proposed scheme, a secure 256-bit key is generated using a Password-Based Key-Derivation Function (PBKDF2) and HMAC-SHA256, which then is expanded using a novel scheme. The original Keystream Generation Algorithm used to generate the keystream in RC4 undergoes modifications. The keystream is further augmented using a Multiply-With-Carry (MWC) pseudo-random sequence to provide a more secure keystream while mitigating bias. The proposed scheme employs a unique process of encryption that employs pixel-wise substitution in a preliminary step using the PRESENT S-box, followed by a stage of spatial shuffling using Arnold’s Cat Map with the added step of diffusion to increase entropy and statistical randomness. All the results metrics demonstrate that the proposed scheme dilutes the known weaknesses presented in normal RC4, while simultaneously exhibiting strong secure image encryption capabilities, with desirable entropy, NPCR and UACI.

Introduction

The expansion in digital communication has lead to the protection of visual content becoming extremely important in the field of medical imaging, secure communications, surveillance and copyrights, ensuring protection against cyber crimes such as data theft or information leakage1. Digital images being rich in redundant data and spatial correlation, require specialized encryption techniques to ensure confidentiality and integrity while transmission over communication channels.

Some well-known works have been discussed in recent years2345, incorporating various techniques and mechanisms for image encryption with the use of specialized ciphers and chaos, in order to achieve encryption standards. Many of these researches aim to introduce chaos as a useful layer for creating randomness along with other encryption mechanisms678, while others implement hybrid encryption schemes to eliminate the vulnerabilities present in the existing works91011.

Recent studies highlight the growing need for efficient image encryption, especially with the rise of cloud-based surveillance and IoT applications. Conventional methods often face limitations in resource usage, now a days many researchers to explore lightweight schemes using chaotic maps like Logistic and Arnold’s Cat Map and cellular automata which offer strong security with minimal computational overhead1213.

This research aims to incorporate the use of RC4 stream cipher14 with proposed modifications in the keystream, along with pixel scrambling mechanism using Arnold Cat Map15 for better diffusion. The scheme also uses the PRESENT S-Box16 as an additional layer for encryption and achieving security. The secure key generation and expansion process ensures that key related security is not compromised during the encryption process, providing integrity to the mechanism.

The paper presents a hybrid image encryption scheme to overcome the limitations of RC4 cipher and its inherent vulnerabilities, integrating chaos theory, nonlinear substitutions, and modified keystream generation techniques. The scheme begins with a secure key generation using a well-known Key Derivation Function, based upon a user-entered password (PBKDF2)17 with HMAC-SHA256 as the hash function. Further, the key is expanded using a proposed Key-Expansion Algorithm, such that the 256-bit key is expanded to match the length of input image bytes. The expanded key is then passed through a variation of the Keystream Generation Algorithm, based on RC4 but improved for increased randomness. This algorithm, aside from the expanded key, uses an external Pseudo-Random Number Sequence derived from the Multiply-With-Carry (MWC) PRNG18. The use of this integration provides more efficient shuffling and eliminates the keystream bias issue common with regular RC4.

The actual encryption process works in three essential phases: (i) a nonlinear encryption algorithm applied to the image bytes utilizing the keystream, expanded key, and the MWC sequence; (ii) pixel shuffling via Arnold’s Cat Map to add spatial diffusion; and (iii) an additional pixel diffusion scheme to further randomize the pixel values and maximize statistical uniformity. Each of the image channels (R, G, B) is treated independently, ensuring encryption uniformity for the entire image. The proposed framework not only eliminates the weaknesses of RC4 but also creates a secure and efficient image encryption system for practical use.

In combination, all these steps generate an image that is encrypted with a high level of entropy, it has good disruption of the correlation between the pixels, and other standard cryptographic evaluation metrics are at satisfactory levels. Related works

With rapid growth of digital communication and storage systems, increasing demand of protecting privacy of visual data has established image encryption as a crucial area of research in multimedia security. Traditional encryption algorithms can face several challenges related to image data, including redundancy in data, large volume and real time processing capabilities. Recently, hybrid crypto-systems based on combination of chaos theory, permutation-diffusion networks, and various lightweight modifications suitable for resource-constrained environments with standard ciphers have been studied.

This section discusses about some related works in the field of image encryption, implementing various techniques to achieve desired results. Many of the works are oriented towards using chaos-based image encryption technique, by using one or more chaotic maps as a useful approach for creating randomness in the ciphered image after encryption. The hybrid implementation of chaotic techniques along with other approaches have proved to be an efficient way for encrypting images. For example, the author in19 proposes a Multiple Image Encryption (MIE) algorithm for satellite imagery by integrating hyperchaotic systems, Singular Value Decomposition (SVD), counter mode RCS, a chaos-based Hill cipher, and a custom S-box generated via a modified Blum Blum Shub (BBS) algorithm. In20, a Multiple Chaos Based Encryption is proposed for IoT devices, using a combination of Henon Map and 2D Logistic Map. It combines pixel shuffling and key-mixing approaches to achieve high confusion and diffusion properties.

Similarly, Kumar et. al. in21 integrates Arnold’s Cat Map for pixel Scrambling, Elliptical Curve Cryptography (ECC) for encryption process and a generic algorithm to optimize ECC Key. The algorithm shows fast encryption with high entropy and low correlation on standard hardware. In22, the author proposed a Blockchain-based Chaotic Arnold Map Encryption Scheme (BCAES), which uses Henon and Arnold’s Cat Map along with orthogonal matrix transformations into a multi-phase encryption system for secure storage and transmission of medical images in cloud environments. The entropy values nearing 7.999 along with other test results prove that the technique was efficient enough for required purpose.

Jithin et. al. in23 have combined Arnold’s Cat Map based encryption with DNA sequence operations for encoding, along with a novel Mandelbrot-set-based conditional shift algorithm for confusion and diffusion of RGB channels separately. With this technique, DNA logic and Mandelbrot set enable strong confusion and diffusion on color images. In24, the author presents an asymmetric image encryption scheme using RSA to generate keys for a generalized Arnold map. The encryption involves XOR-based pixel diffusion, cyclic row-column confusion, and additive mode diffusion, all performed twice for strong encryption.

Masood et. al in8 have used a hybrid image encryption scheme using three chaotic systems–Arnold Cat Map for permutation, Newton-Leipnik system for PRNG, and Logistic Gaussian Map for dynamic S-box generation. Plain image-dependent SHA-512 hash is used to derive chaos parameters. The scheme shows excellent statistical strength and speed. A grayscale image encryption method is proposed in7, which uses hybrid chaotic maps (Logistic-Tent-Sine combinations) for diffusion and Arnold’s cat map for confusion. It extends the original image with chaos-generated data and applies XOR, transformation, and exchange in the diffusion phase. The chaotic maps are selected adaptively based on correlation in the plain image.

Sabir et al. in5 presents a multi-layer color image encryption technique combining Random Matrix Affine Cipher (RMAC), Reality Preserving 2D Fractional Hartley Transform (RP2DFrHT), and 2D Arnold map. RMAC provides security in coordinate and geometric domains, RP2DFrHT ensures real-valued encrypted data for easier storage/transmission, and Arnold map enhances security by scrambling pixels.

The authors in3 proposes a lightweight, real-time capable image encryption technique using a novel pixel shuffling method followed by Logistic and Arnold’s Cat chaotic maps applied independently on each RGB channel. The shuffled image is XORed with a key derived via Logistic Map, then further transformed with Arnold Cat Map for final encryption. It emphasizes efficiency, key sensitivity, and robustness under cryptanalysis and data loss. Similarly, in6, the model uses ECC (via ECDH and ElGamal) with 3D/4D Arnold Cat maps for scrambling, providing both image encryption and authentication. Images are scrambled in blocks, encrypted using ECC keys, and verified with digital signatures based on hash values. It is evaluated on both grayscale and RGB images, showing excellent randomness, low correlation, and strong robustness to attacks.

The authors in25 combines RC4 with a chaotic logistic map (CLM) for image encryption. The CLM generates a key stream, which is XORed with the image bytes after permutation. The method ensures lossless encryption, high key sensitivity, and uniform histogram distribution. Similarly, Jiang et. al. in26 applies RC4, pixel shuffling, and steganography to encrypt biomedical cell images. The method ensures no loss of critical image data (viability, morphology) post-decryption.

Munir et. al. in27 enhances RC4 by integrating multi chaotic maps (Logistic, Henon-Sine) for KSA and PRGA stages. The chaotic maps generate pseudorandom numbers to improve key scrambling and keystream generation. The method is tested for image encryption performance. The authors in28 The paper proposes a two-stage encryption method: permutation using the chaotic Gingerbreadman map and substitution using RC4A. The Gingerbreadman map shuffles pixels, while RC4A XORs pixel values with keystreams. The method is tested for security and robustness.

Jabbar et. al in29 proposes a triple-encryption algorithm combining RC4, Zigzag transformation, and Elliptic Curve Cryptography (ECC) for secure image encryption. The paper achieves high NPCR and low correlation, indicating strong encryption. The author in2 have intertwined RC4 cipher and chaotic maps for their proposed algorithm, by first using chaos as confusion for scrambling pixels, while the RC4 cipher is used to alter the values of pixels through row-wise and column-wise transformations. The entropy value was shown to be near ideal with a high NPCR, however the UACI values could still be improved. The shortcomings in some of the works have led to our proposed three-phase mechanism, which combines PBKDF2-based key expansion, Multiply-With-Carry RC4-based keystream modification, and Arnold Cat Map diffusion and addressing bias vulnerabilities while being computationally efficient. The proposed mechanism aims to overcome the present vulnerabilities in the existing hybrid RC4 systems for security, robustness and deployability. Prerequisites

RC4 Cipher

RC4 (Rivest Cipher 4) is a well-known cipher, originally proposed by Ron Rivest, for its simple implementation and fast execution14. The cipher works on two subroutines, one dealing with the scheduling of the encryption key and the other for the generation of pseudo-random sequence14. The PRGA produces a keystream output. Further it utilizes an XOR operation to encrypt the given data. Being an efficient cipher, RC4 was deployed in protocols like WEP, SSL/TLS, WPA etc. However, several vulnerabilities were found with its working mechanism.

One such vulnerability was the biased keystream generated from the pseudo-random sequence1442, i.e. some bytes appeared more frequently than others. This was a major flaw in the working mechanism, as biased keystream would make statistical attacks feasible. Moreover, RC4 has a weak key scheduling algorithm43 that makes it prone to key recovery attacks. If the same key is used repeatedly, it allows the attacker to decode patterns and recover the originally present data. Hence RC4 today is considered a cryptographically broken cipher.

However, many researches have proposed methodologies to combine the cipher with other approaches101131, that can incorporate diffusion in the encryption mechanism. One such approach introduces the chaos-based encryption by implementing chaotic maps along with the RC4 cipher. This research also aims to tackle the vulnerabilities of the cipher by combining it with Arnold’s Cat Map scrambling, a secure key generation mechanism and a proposed modification in the keystream generation, along with an effective encryption process.

Arnold’s Cat Map

The proposed encryption scheme uses Arnold’s Map as an additional layer for adding diffusion. It is a 2D chaotic map, widely adopted for image encryption, steganography, watermarking and related fields15. The map was presented by Vladimir Arnold regarding the ergodic theory, wherein, a chaotic behaviour was observed in the dynamical systems. In44, the authors analyze the cycle structure of the generalized discrete Arnold’s Cat Map in binary arithmetic domains, specifically over \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbb {Z}_{2^e}$$\end{document}. It is shown that the period distribution of the Cat Map follows a power-law distribution with a fixed exponent when the arithmetic precision e is sufficiently large. Furthermore, the number of cycles of any given length stabilizes to a constant as e increases. The Cat Map defines a bijective mapping on its domain, and every node in its functional graph belongs to exactly one cycle, implying the absence of transient states. As the arithmetic precision e increases, the structure of the functional graph evolves in a predictable manner, where the cycles expand incrementally from those in smaller domains–for example, cycles in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbb {F}_{e+1}$$\end{document} are systematically derived from the cycles in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbb {F}_e$$\end{document}.

Arnold’s Cat Map is a widely adopted technique for image scrambling due to its easy implementation and effective recovery. It is utilized for the process of pixel scrambling, while maintaining the integrity of the original image, without any information loss45. The map operates by shuffling the pixel coordinates and performing a linear transformation on an image repeatedly, according to the number of iterations defined. Proposed scheme

The proposed model aims to incorporate the use of some conventional approaches of image encryption with proposed modifications, in order to address the vulnerabilities present within the components. The model is applied on RGB images namely, Sailboat, Baboon, Peppers and Airplane, each of size 512 x 512, wherein, encryption of each channel takes places independently, recombining all the channels at last to get the required encrypted image.

Encryption process

The scheme begins with a secure key generation phase, in which we derive a key of 256 bits by applying the PBKDF250 with HMAC-SHA2565152, as the underlying hash function. The key is further expanded using a key expansion mechanism to generate blocks of 32 bytes each, appending to the original key iteratively to derive an expanded key, equal to the length of image bytes. The process further continues with a Keystream Generation Algorithm, which is based on a modified approach of the keystream generation process of RC4 cipher14. The input to the Keystream Generation Algorithm are the expanded key and an external Pseudo-Random Sequence, generated via an algorithm called Multiply-With-Carry PRNG. This is done to introduce better shuffling of the generated keystream and tackle the vulnerability of biased keystream, which is present in the RC4 cipher.

The encryption process operates on three main mechanisms (i) Encryption Function on the original image bytes (ii) Shuffling pixels of the encrypted image via Arnold’s Cat Map15 (iii) Pixel Diffusion. While (i) and (ii) ensure high entropy value for the encrypted image, (iii) makes diffusion uniform for the entire image, such that small changes to a pixel in the digital image will give a completely unique ciphered image.Fig. 3Encryption Process.

Encryption Process.

Firstly, image bytes are obtained for each channel from the original image. Then we apply the encryption function to each channel byte separately, which takes the inputs as the channel bytes, the expanded key, the obtained keystream and the Pseudo-Random Sequence of numbers. Further, we make use of the PRESENT S-Box16 as discussed in section 3.3 as an additional layer for the encryption process. After encryption, each channel is reshaped back into a 2D array.

During the second phase, Arnold’s Cat Map is used for scrambling each encrypted channel, shuffling the pixel positions and enhancing diffusion (section 3.2). At the last stage, a Pixel Diffusion mechanism is applied to the scrambled-ciphered image to maximize the diffusion effect and maintain its uniformity for the entire image.

Key generation

The initial derivation of the key process incorporates the use of a PBKDF2, as stated in17 with HMAC-SHA2565152, as the hash function (see Fig. 4). It is used to provide strength to the password and make them resistant against dictionary attacks. PBKDF2 is based on a pseudorandom function (HMAC-SHA256 in the proposed scheme) which is then used iteratively to the password entered by user, to generate the key of the desired length. The key size generated is 256 bits or 32 bytes in our proposed scheme.Fig. 4Key Generation using PBKDF2 and HMAC-SHA256.

Key Generation using PBKDF2 and HMAC-SHA256.

The aim is to strengthen password security by hashing the input repeatedly using the HMAC-SHA256 hash function. HMAC processes the password by computing two hashes – the inner and the outer hash. Firstly, SHA-256 is applied to the input password padded with an inner pad (ipad), while the second layer hashes the result with an outer pad (opad). The formula for HMAC-SHA256 is given by 3:3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} HMAC(K, M) = \text {Hash}\left( \left( K \oplus \text {{opad}} \right) \parallel \text {Hash}\left( \left( K \oplus \text {{ipad}} \right) \parallel M \right) \right) , \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where K refers to the key (password in PBKDF2), M refers to the message (salt + counter), ipad and opad are the inner and outer padding respectively, and ’Hash’ is SHA-256 operation. PBKDF2 then begins by taking inputs as password P, a unique salt value S, c being the count of iterations, and the key length which is desired dkLen. It is to be noted that the iteration count should be sufficiently high for the key derivation process, with a minimum of 1000 iterations, ranging up to 10,000,000 iterations, as recommended by53. Also, the salt should be at least 128 bits and should be randomly generated.

The process starts by computing the value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$U_1$$\end{document} using the HMAC-SHA256 hash, where the password will act as the key and the salt, after concatenation with a counter (starting from 1), functions as the message. Key expansion

The key derived from PBKDF2 in section 4.1 is further expanded to match the length of image bytes to be encrypted, which can be demonstrated through Algorithm 4.

The process begins by initializing an empty expanded key list and computing the initial state using SHA-256, which acts as the seed for subsequent block generation. New blocks, each of 32 bytes, are generated iteratively by firstly performing concatenation of the current state and a counter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(|\text {expanded\_key}| \mod 256)$$\end{document} and further hashing the result. A block undergoes further transformation in the mixed_block phase, where each byte is bitwise rotated based on its position (left shift by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(i \mod 8)$$\end{document} and right shift by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(8 - (i \mod 8), \mod 256)$$\end{document} to add non-linearity. The rotated byte is then XORed with the corresponding byte, to ensure key sensitivity. The transformed mixed block is appended to expanded_key, and the state is updated with this block.

Once the desired length is met, each byte of the expanded key is XORed with the previous byte. The resulting key is the expanded state of key derived from PBKDF2, which has the same length as the bytes of image to be encrypted. By implementing SHA-256 and bitwise operations, it achieves security properties like avalanche effect, strong diffusion, and resistance to brute-force attacks.

Pseudo-random sequence generation

The proposed scheme makes use of an externally generated PRN sequence through a Pseudo Random Number Generator (PRNG) called Multiply-with-Carry (MWC), originally proposed by George Marsaglia, It is used to generate long sequences of random numbers. The result so obtained has good statistical behaviour, to ensure true randomness and unpredictability54.

The mechanism is based on a combination of multiplication and carry operation to compute the next random number.

The next random number \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_n$$\end{document} can be computed through 7 :7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} X_n = a \cdot X_{n-1} + C_{n-1}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}while the carry update is computed through 8:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} C_n = \left\lfloor \frac{a \cdot X_{n-1} + C_{n-1}}{m} \right\rfloor . \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_n$$\end{document} is the generated sequence, a refers to the multiplier, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_n$$\end{document} is the carry term, and m is the modulus. The modulus m is equal to the power of 2, such that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$m = 2^b$$\end{document}, where the value of b is 32 for the proposed scheme. It works well because of its high spectral score and performs well in lattice structure tests for higher dimensions.

The value of initial carry c chosen is 1013904223, which comes as a combination with multiplier a from55. The value is co-prime with the modulus m, which means that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gcd (c,2^{32})=1$$\end{document}. Also, the chosen carry value is large enough to prevent early stagnation while also small enough to avoid computational overflow when combined with multiplier a.

Encryption function

Fig. 6Encryption Function.

Encryption Function.

The encryption function is applied to each channel bytes separately (see Fig. 6), which takes the inputs as the channel bytes, the expanded key (section 4.1), the Pseudo-Random Sequence of numbers (section 4.1) and the obtained keystream (section 4.1). We make use of the PRESENT S-Box (section 3.3) as an additional layer for the process of encryption.

For each channel byte of the image, a diffusion value is calculated as the sum of keystream value, MWC generated pseudo-random number and the previous output, computed for modulo 256. Further, the image byte is XORed with the calculated diffusion value and modular addition is performed with the corresponding byte from the expanded key. After we obtain the encrypted channel byte, we perform bitwise left circular shift to the individual byte, where the rotation amount is determined by the subsequent key byte (modulo 8). This operation enhances diffusion and ensures that similar plaintext values encrypt to different ciphertext values.

Following the rotation of channel bytes, each byte is then substituted using the PRESENT S-Box (section 3.3), where each 4-byte nibble is masked with a substituted value. The resulting channel byte is stored as encrypted data and previous output is updated to this new encrypted byte, ensuring that encryption of new each byte is dependent on the previously encrypted byte. The process continues till all the channel bytes are encrypted.

Decryption function

Fig. 8Decryption Function.

Decryption Function.

Figure 8 shows the decryption function applied to the channel bytes, recovered from the reversal of Arnold’s Cat Map. The function assumes four inputs, mainly the channel bytes which are encrypted, the expanded key used during encryption, the MWC generated pseudo-random sequence and the obtained keystream. Firstly, diffusion value is calculated for decryption as the sum of keystream value, MWC generated pseudo-random number and the previous output, computed for modulo 256. Further, the decrypted byte is XORed with the calculated diffusion value and modular subtraction is performed with the corresponding byte from the expanded key. A bitwise right circular shift operation is performed on the individual byte where the rotation amount is determined by the subsequent key byte (modulo 8). Once the decrypted byte is recovered, we reverse the PRESENT S-Box on each byte, where every 4-byte nibble is back-substituted with its original value. After iterating through all bytes, the recovered bytes for each channel are recombined back to original image.

An algorithm’s key space depends on the size of secret key61. The scheme proposed in this paper has a secure key space, governed by the 256-bit key generated from the PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA256. This process of key derivation makes sure that even poor passwords are stretched into a cryptographically secure key, giving a theoretical key space of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$2^{256}$$\end{document} (around \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1.16 \times 10^{77}$$\end{document} possible keys), comparable to AES-256 and regarded as computationally secure against comprehensive search attacks using current technology. Key sensitivity

Structure Similarity Index Measure (SSIM)

The Structural Similarity Index (SSIM) is a perceptual metric used to quantify the similarity between two images by comparing their structural information, luminance, and contrast1. Unlike PSNR, which is based on absolute error metrics, SSIM evaluates the perceptual closeness of images. It is mathematically defined as:20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text {SSIM}(x, y) = \frac{(2\mu _x \mu _y + C_1)(2\sigma _{xy} + C_2)}{(\mu _x^2 + \mu _y^2 + C_1)(\sigma _x^2 + \sigma _y^2 + C_2)} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu _x$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu _y$$\end{document} are the mean intensities of images x and y, respectively; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma _x^2$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma _y^2$$\end{document} are their variances; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma _{xy}$$\end{document} is the covariance between the two images; and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_1$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_2$$\end{document} are small constants to stabilize the division.

Number of Pixels Change Rate (NPCR)

NPCR (Number of Pixels Change Rate) is a key metric used in image encryption to evaluate the sensitivity of an encryption process to minute changes in the plaintext image. It measures how much a ciphered image changes when a single pixel is modified in the plain (original) image9.

Mathematically, NPCR is defined as:22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text {NPCR} = \frac{\sum _{i=0}^{m-1} \sum _{j=0}^{n-1} D(i,j)}{m \times n} \times 100\% \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D(i,j) = {\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} 1 & \text {if } C_1(i,j) \ne C_2(i,j) \\ 0 & \text {if } C_1(i,j) = C_2(i,j) \end{array}\right. }$$\end{document}Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_1$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_2$$\end{document} denote the two encrypted images–one obtained from the original image and the other from the image with one pixel changed. The function D(i, j) checks whether the pixel values at position (i, j) in the two ciphered images differ.

To compute NPCR, the proposed algorithm is first used to encrypt a plain image. Then, one pixel in the original image is altered slightly, and the modified image is again encrypted using the same algorithm. The two resulting ciphered images are then compared pixel by pixel to calculate the NPCR. A stronger encryption algorithm should yield an NPCR value greater than 99%.

Conclusion

The paper presents a secure hybrid image encryption scheme to counter the already known weaknesses of RC4 stream cipher. Developed using chaos theory, Arnold’s Cat Map, nonlinear substitution, PRESENT S-box, and modified Multiply-With-Carry (MWC) type PRNG keystream generation algorithm; the proposed scheme provides security, diffusion, and statistical performance. Key generation and expansion use PBKDF2 and HMAC-SHA256, ensuring that the key generation remains secure and scalable. In addition, the multi-stage scheme gives strong resistance against common cryptographic attacks, such as brute force, statistical and differential attacks. Experimental results reveal an entropy value more than 7.999, significant pixel correlation deviation, and acceptable NPCR (>99%) and UACI (around 33%). With a wide testing over fourteen evaluation parameters including the NIST Test Suite, the results show that the proposed method performs excellently.

Overall, the proposed scheme provides a robust, optimal, and secure framework for image encryption, thus becoming the best candidate for used scenarios that require protection of visual data. Future works may involve investigation into the possibilities of expanding the proposed scheme for video encryption, work on optimizing the proposed scheme computational efficiency for real-time scenarios, and further analysis of the adaptive chaos-based tuning of parameters for enhanced security.
