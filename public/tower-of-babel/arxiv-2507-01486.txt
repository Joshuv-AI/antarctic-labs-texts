Spin Systems Coupled to Photons: A Case study of Phase Transition in a One-Dimensional Classical Ferromagnetic Ising Spin Chain at Finite Temperature
Shuntaro Otake, Motoaki Bamba

Abstract
Although one-dimensional classical spin chains do not exhibit phase transitions, we found that a phase transition does occur when they are coupled to a cavity photon mode. This provides one of the simplest examples demonstrating that finite-temperature superradiant phase transitions can emerge from long-range fully connected interactions mediated by photons and interactions within the material.



I
Introduction

A hundred years ago, Ising, in his doctoral dissertation, showed that a one-dimensional classical ferromagnetic spin chain does not exhibit a magnetic phase transition
Ising 1925
. However, it is nowadays widely known that magnetic phase transitions occur in two or more dimensions, and since then the Ising model has been positioned as one of the simplest, crucial models for studying phase-transition phenomena in many-body systems.

Furthermore, the strong interaction between matter and light, which is expected to be realized in cavity systems in recent years, is expected to lead to the discovery and understanding of new emergent phenomena. The ultrastrong light-matter coupling
Forn-Díaz et al. 2019
;
Kockum et al. 2019
has, over the past decade, moved beyond theoretical concepts and has been realized in a variety of experimental systems, including phonons
Faust and Henry 1966
;
Zhang et al. 2021
;
Barra-Burillo et al. 2021
, inter-subband transitions in semiconductor quantum wells
Günter et al. 2009
, superconducting circuits
Niemczyk et al. 2010
;
Yoshihara et al. 2017
, organic molecules
Schwartz et al. 2011
, electron cyclotron motion (Landau-level transitions)
Scalari et al. 2012
;
Bayer et al. 2017
, molecular vibrations
George et al. 2016
, surface plasmon polaritons
Mueller et al. 2020
and magnons
Golovchanskiy et al. 2021
. This progress has opened promising avenues for potential applications in both physics and chemistry
Forn-Díaz et al. 2019
;
Kockum et al. 2019
;
Nagarajan et al. 2021
.

Of special interest is the realization of a superradiant phase transition (SRPT)
Hepp and Lieb 1973
, which remains a demanding and unresolved issue.
This phenomenon refers to the spontaneous emergence of a coherent amplitude in the electromagnetic field due to light–matter interactions under thermal equilibrium.
Although lasers also exhibit spontaneous coherence, they are generated by an inverted population of matter, that is, under non-equilibrium conditions. The concept of SRPT was first theoretically proposed in 1973
Hepp and Lieb 1973
through the analysis of the Dicke model (which will be shown in Eq. (
3
))
Dicke 1954
. Although the existence of a phase transition in the Dicke model (where interactions whitin the material are neglected) has been mathematically proven, the mechanism by which a superradiant phase transition is triggered in realistic materials remains to be fully understood.

The Dicke model describes an ensemble of independent two-level atoms confined in a cavity. Two-level atoms can be described in terms of spins.
Therefore, in this study, we adopt a model of spin systems inside a cavity.
We demonstrate that a model in which a classical Ising spin chain with ferromagnetic interactions is coupled to cavity photons serves as an almost exactly solvable system exhibiting the SRPT. This can be regarded as a novel emergent phenomenon induced by ultrastrong light–matter coupling in a material system that would not exhibit a phase transition on its own.
Furthermore, we point out that this perspective is also applicable to conventional models such as the Dicke model. Finally, we contrast our results with those of a previous study
Rohn et al. 2020
, which examined a closely related Hamiltonian at zero temperature.

II
Model

We consider a typical lattice in which spins are located. Let the total number of spins be
N
. The spin at site
i\in\{1,2,\dots,N\}
is generally given by the set
(\sigma_{i}^{x},\sigma_{i}^{y},\sigma_{i}^{z})
where for each
i
,
\sigma_{i}^{\mu}\,(\mu=x,y,z)
represents the Pauli matrices whose eigenvalues are
\pm 1
.
Let us denote the Hamiltonian of the spin system as
H_{\text{spin}}
.
For example, a typical classical Ising model is given by

H_{\text{Ising}}=\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}J_{ij}\sigma_{i}^{z}\sigma_{j}^{z}.

(1)

The summation
\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}
represents the sum over the nearest-neighbor spins, and
J_{ij}
is the interaction constant between spins, where a positive value indicates an antiferromagnetic interaction and a negative value indicates a ferromagnetic interaction.

Here, let us introduce a model in which the spin system is coupled to photons. The Hamiltonian
H
is assumed to be given by the following:

H=H_{\text{spin}}+\omega a^{\dagger}a+\frac{g}{\sqrt{N}}(a+a^{\dagger})\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}^{\mu}\quad(\mu=x,y,z).

(2)

Here,
\hbar=1
, and
a^{\dagger}
and
a
are the creation and annihilation operators for photons with frequency
\omega
, satisfying the usual commutation relation
[a,a^{\dagger}]=1
. Furthermore, the most important parameter in this model is
g
, which represents the coupling strength between the photons and the spins.
\mu
is determined by the specific system. For example, the Dicke model for a collection of two-level atoms,
\mu
is taken as
x
, and it is expressed using the resonance frequency of the atom
\omega_{0}
(
H_{\text{spin}}=\omega_{0}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}^{z}
) as follows
Hepp and Lieb 1973
;
Dicke 1954
:

H_{\text{Dicke}}=\omega_{0}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}^{z}+\omega a^{\dagger}a+\frac{g}{\sqrt{N}}(a+a^{\dagger})\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}^{x}.

(3)

At this point, let’s define the thermodynamic quantities in this system.
Hereafter, the Boltzmann constant
k_{\text{B}}
is set to 1.
Given Hamiltonian
H
, the expectation value of a physical quantity
O
at finite temperature
T=\beta^{-1}
is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{O}=\Tr{\frac{e^{-\beta H}}{Z} O},

(4)

\displaystyle Z=\Tr{e^{-\beta H}}.

(5)

Here,
Z
is the partition function of the system. As an example, the magnetization in the
\mu
-direction and photon number normalized by the number
N
of spins of the system at finite temperature are defined, respectively as

\displaystyle m=\Tr{\frac{e^{-\beta H}}{Z} \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \sigma_i^{\mu}},

(6)

\displaystyle n=\Tr{\frac{e^{-\beta H}}{Z} \frac{1}{N}\,a^\dagger a}.

(7)

In this paper, we first show that, in general, the coupling between spins and photons can be divided into two contributions: long-range
,
fully connected spin-spin interactions mediated by photons and quasi-particles that behave as free bosons.

To diagonalize the Hamiltonian
H
given by Eq. (
2
), we transform it as follows:

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=H_{\text{spin}}+\omega\left(a+\dfrac{g}{\omega\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}^{\mu}\right)^{\dagger}\left(a+\dfrac{g}{\omega\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}^{\mu}\right)

\displaystyle\quad-\dfrac{g^{2}}{N\omega}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}^{\mu}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=H_{\text{spin}}+\omega b^{\dagger}b-\dfrac{g^{2}}{N\omega}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}^{\mu}\right)^{2}.

(8)

Here,
b=a+\dfrac{g}{\omega\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}^{\mu}
is an operator that satisfies the typical bosonic commutation relation
[b,b^{\dagger}]=1
, and represents a quasi-particle in this model. This quasi-particle does not interact with the spins, so it is a free boson.
Using the property of the Pauli operators,
(\sigma_{i}^{\mu})^{2}=1
, we can transform the expression as follows:

H=H_{\text{spin}}-\dfrac{g^{2}}{N\omega}\sum_{i\neq j}\sigma_{i}^{\mu}\sigma_{j}^{\mu}+\omega b^{\dagger}b-\frac{g^{2}}{\omega}

(9)

Figure 2:
When the spin system couples to a cavity photon, a long-range, all-to-all ferromagnetic interaction emerges between the spins. In the figure, a specific spin highlighted in red interacts with the other spins not only through the original internal spin-spin interactions
J_{ij}
, but also via a fully-connected intractions
-\frac{g^{2}}{N\omega}
mediated by photons.

The second term is the fully connected term, which means that there exists a long-range ferromagnetic interaction in the
\mu
-direction between all distinct spins. It is noteworthy that this term is proportional to the square of the coupling strength
g
. In this way, the interaction between the cavity photons and the spin system can be separated into a fully connected interaction between spins and a free boson.

The mean-field solution of a fully connected classical spin system is known to become exact in the thermodynamic limit
N\to\infty

Sitter 1937
. For convenience, a proof is provided in Appendix
A
.
Therefore, in the strong coupling limit, where the second term dominates the system, the mean-field treatment of the fully connected term becomes exact. From here on, in this paper, we assume that
N
is sufficiently large such that the thermodynamic limit holds. Furthermore, we assume the system is in a thermal equilibrium at finite temperature
\beta^{-1}
.

Here, for each spin, let us define the following quantity as the fluctuation of the magnetization:

\displaystyle\delta m_{i}=\sigma_{i}^{\mu}-m.

(10)

The mean-field approximation is analytically equivalent to neglecting the square term of the fluctuation of the magnetization. In this case, the mean-field approximation for the all-to-all coupling term can be obtained as follows:

\displaystyle\sum_{i\neq j}\sigma_{i}^{\mu}\sigma_{j}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\neq j}\left(m^{2}+(\delta m_{i}+\delta m_{j})\,m+\delta m_{i}\delta m_{j}\right)

(11)

\displaystyle\simeq\sum_{i\neq j}\left(m^{2}+(\delta m_{i}+\delta m_{j})\,m\right)

(12)

\displaystyle=-N(N-1)m^{2}+2(N-1)m\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}^{\mu}

(13)

\displaystyle\simeq-N^{2}m^{2}+2Nm\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}^{\mu}.

(14)

Here, we have used the fact that
\sum_{i\neq j}1=N(N-1)\simeq N^{2}
,
\sum_{i\neq j}(\sigma_{i}^{\mu}+\sigma_{j}^{\mu})=2(N-1)\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}^{\mu}\simeq 2N\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}^{\mu}
.
By applying the mean-field approximation to the fully connected term in this way, it becomes a simple sum of individual spins, making the analysis easier. Let us call the mean-field approximation applied only to the fully connected term a partial mean-field approximation. The partial mean-field approximation corresponds to ignoring the term
\dfrac{g^{2}}{N}\sum_{i\neq j}\delta m_{i}\delta m_{j}=\dfrac{g^{2}}{N}\sum_{i\neq j}(\sigma_{i}^{\mu}-m)(\sigma_{j}^{\mu}-m)
. Thus it is rigorously justified not only in the strong coupling limit but also in the weak coupling limit where the coupling strength is sufficiently small, as this term is negligible.

On the other hand, in the SRPT, a cavity electromagnetic field spontaneously emerges.
Therefore, it is essential to evaluate the creation and annihilation operators of the cavity photons, as well as the expectation value of the photon number. Since
\expectationvalue{b}=0
and
\expectationvalue{\sum_{i=1}^N \sigma_i^\mu}=Nm
at any finite temperature, given the relationship between
a
and
b
, we get

\expectationvalue{a}=\expectationvalue{a^\dagger}=-\dfrac{gm}{\omega}\sqrt{N}.

(15)

As can be seen from this, the emergence of a finite magnetization is accompanied by the development of a macroscopic electromagnetic field in the cavity. This is the superradiant phenomenon in the present model.

The expectation value of
\expectationvalue{a^\dagger a}
satisfy

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{a^\dagger a}

\displaystyle=\Tr\left(a^{\dagger}a\,\frac{e^{-\beta H}}{Z}\right)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{e^{\beta\omega}-1}+\frac{g^{2}}{N\omega^{2}}\expectationvalue{\left( \sum_{i=1}^N \sigma_i^\mu\right)^2},

(16)

where we have taken into account the Bose-Einstein distribution
\expectationvalue{b^\dagger b}=\dfrac{1}{e^{\beta\omega}-1}
. Interestingly, the squared magnetization appears here as well.
By applying a partial mean-field approximation to the all-to-all interaction term, in the same manner as in the magnetization calculation, we obtain the following:

\expectationvalue{a^\dagger a}=\dfrac{1}{e^{\beta\omega}-1}+\dfrac{Ng^{2}m^{2}}{\omega^{2}}\simeq\dfrac{Ng^{2}m^{2}}{\omega^{2}}.

(17)

Therefore,

n\simeq\dfrac{g^{2}m^{2}}{\omega^{2}}=\left(\frac{\expectationvalue{a}}{\sqrt{N}}\right)^{2}.

(18)

This serves as the order parameter in the present model, and it holds at any finite temperature within the range where the partial mean-field approximation is valid.
The positive square root of
n
determines the amplitude
{\expectationvalue{a}}/{\sqrt{N}}
of the normalized electromagnetic field in the cavity.
Furthermore, since the term in Eq. (
14
) induces a phase transition at finite temperature and the magnetization acquires a finite value, it can be roughly understood that the system undergoes a transition to the SRPT phase with finite
n
when the coupling strength
g
is sufficiently large.

In the next section, let us consider a system that is analytically tractable for further discussion.

III
One-Dimensional Classical Ferromagnetic Spin Chain Coupled to a Cavity

As a specific example of a spin system, let’s consider a one-dimensional ferromagnetic classical spin chain. This system is a specific example of the typical classical Ising model Eq. (
1
). The Hamiltonian is expressed as follows:

\displaystyle H_{\text{1d-Ising}}=-J\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}^{z}\sigma_{i+1}^{z}.

(19)

Here, the spin-spin interaction
J_{ij}
is taken to be negative, and the interaction between sites is assumed to be
-J
, independent of the positions of the sites. Additionally, periodic boundary conditions are assumed, such that
\sigma_{N+1}^{z}=\sigma_{1}^{z}
.

As is well known, this one-dimensional classical spin chain can be completely solved analytically and does not exhibit a magnetic phase transition at any finite temperature
Ising 1925
. On ther other hand, in systems with more than two dimensions, below the critical temperature, the magnetization acquires a finite value, and the so-called
Z_{2}
symmetry, where the system remains invariant even when the spins of the entire system are reversed as
\sigma_{i}^{z}\to-\sigma_{i}^{z}
, is broken. The exact solution for the magnetization when a classical external magnetic field
h
is applied to the system is given by the following expression, where the Hamiltonian is defined as
H=H_{\text{1d-Ising}}-h\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}^{z}

Ising 1925
:

\displaystyle m=\dfrac{\sinh(\beta h)}{\sqrt{(\sinh(\beta h))^{2}+e^{-4\beta J}}}

(20)

In the following, we consider a model where this classical magnetic field is quantized as cavity photons. The Hamiltonian of the system is given by:

H=-J\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}^{z}\sigma_{i+1}^{z}+\omega a^{\dagger}a+\frac{g}{\sqrt{N}}(a+a^{\dagger})\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}^{z}.

(21)

This corresponds to a specific example of the Hamiltonian given in Eq. (
2
) with
H_{\text{spin}}=H_{\text{1D-Ising}}
and
\mu=z
.
Meanwhile, Ref.
Rohn et al. 2020
focused on the model with
\mu=x
, analyzed perturbatively at zero temperature.
Even if the spins of the entire system are reversed, this Hamiltonian is not invariant. However, if both the reversal of the spins
\sigma_{i}^{z}\to-\sigma_{i}^{z}
of the entire system and the ”reversal of the photons”
a\to-a
are performed simultaneously, the Hamiltonian remains invariant. Let’s call this symmetry the
Z_{2}
symmetry of the system.
By applying the same procedure as in the previous section, we diagonalize the Hamiltonian as follows.

\displaystyle H=-J\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}^{z}\sigma_{i+1}^{z}-\dfrac{g^{2}}{N\omega}\sum_{i\neq j}\sigma_{i}^{z}\sigma_{j}^{z}+\omega b^{\dagger}b+\text{const}.

(22)

Furthermore, applying the partial mean-field approximation to the fully connected terms results in the following effective Hamiltonian for the spins:

\displaystyle H_{\text{eff}}=-J\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}^{z}\sigma_{i+1}^{z}-\dfrac{2g^{2}m}{\omega}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}^{z}

(23)

By interpreting
\dfrac{2g^{2}m}{\omega}
as a classical external magnetic field, the system is effectively equivalent to a one-dimensional classical spin chain under an applied classical field. Therefore, utilizing the exact solution Eq. (
20
) for the magnetization, we obtain the following self-consistent equation for the magnetization:

m=\frac{\sinh\left(2\beta\dfrac{g^{2}}{\omega}m\right)}{\sqrt{\sinh^{2}\left(2\beta\dfrac{g^{2}}{\omega}m\right)+e^{-4\beta J}}},

(24)

that is,

\sinh^{2}\left(2\beta\dfrac{g^{2}}{\omega}m\right)=\frac{m^{2}e^{-4\beta J}}{1-m^{2}}.

(25)

From this self-consistent equation, we can obtain a magnetic phase transition where the magnetization acquires a finite value in the low-temperature and strong-coupling regime.
The dependence of the order parameters
m
and
n
on the coupling strength
g
is shown in Fig.
3
.
Surprisingly, the critical temperature
T_{\text{c}}
can be determined from the self-consistent equation within the range where the partial mean-field approximation is justified:

T_{\textbf{c}}=\dfrac{2g^{2}}{\omega}\exp(\dfrac{2J}{T_\text{c}}).

(26)

Hence,

T_{\text{c}}=\dfrac{2J}{W\left(\dfrac{\omega J}{g^{2}}\right)}\underset{g\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\dfrac{2g^{2}}{\omega}.

(27)

Here,
W(z)
denote the Lambert’s W function satisfying
W(z)\exp W(z)=z
. The critical coupling constant for this system at finite temperature
T=\beta^{-1}
is

g_{\text{c}}=\sqrt{\frac{\omega}{2\beta}}e^{-\beta J}\underset{T\to 0}{\longrightarrow}0.

(28)

The results shown in Fig.

are obtained with
\omega
= 1 fixed. The white region indicates the paramagnetic phase where the magnetization vanishes.

As seen in Fig.

, a ferromagnetic phase accompanied by superradiance appears in the strong-coupling, low-temperature regime, as indicated by the colored regions, whereas the white region corresponds to the phase where both the magnetization and the cavity field are zero.

The red line represents the exact critical temperature within the regime discussed, while the black line corresponds to the critical temperature in the case of
J=0
. In both the strong- and weak-coupling limits, the red line asymptotically approaches the black line.

Moreover, the dependence of the critical temperature on
J
in the low-temperature region exhibits an exponential behavior. This suggests that, for the experimental detection of superradiance, not only the coupling constant of strength
g
with the cavity but also the strength
J
of interactions within the material plays an important role.

The case of
g=0
reproduces the known result that the system remains in the paramagnetic phase at any finite temperature, with no phase transition occurring
Ising 1925
.

The procedure shown above makes the picture of superradiant phase transition more clear even in the well-known Dicke model
Dicke 1954
.
Applying a partial mean-field approximation to the spins in Dicke model yields the following form.

H_{\text{Dicke Spin}}\simeq\omega_{0}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}^{z}-\dfrac{2g^{2}m^{x}}{\omega}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}^{x}

(29)

Here,
m^{x}
denotes the magnetization in the
x
-direction. When
g
becomes sufficiently large and the second term dominates, it can be seen that a superradiant phase transition occurs at finite temperature, where magnetization along the
x
-direction emerges. In this case as well, it is a typical second-order phase transition. The critical coupling constant in this simple effective Hamiltonian can be exactly obtained as follows. (See Appendix
B
for the proof.)

g_{\text{c}}=\sqrt{\dfrac{\omega\omega_{0}}{2}\coth{\beta\omega_{0}}}\underset{T=\beta^{-1}\to 0}{\longrightarrow}\sqrt{\dfrac{\omega\omega_{0}}{2}}.

(30)

This is consistent with what is shown in Ref.
Kirton et al. 2019
.

Moreover, Ref.
Rohn et al. 2020
discussed the zero-temperature behavior of the following Hamiltonian, closely related with that in the present study:

H=-J\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}^{z}\sigma_{i+1}^{z}+\omega a^{\dagger}a+\frac{g}{\sqrt{N}}(a+a^{\dagger})\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}^{x}.

(31)

Applying a partial mean-field approximation to spins in this Hamiltonian yields:

H_{\text{eff}}\simeq-J\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}^{z}\sigma_{i+1}^{z}-\dfrac{2g^{2}m^{x}}{\omega}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}^{x}\underset{g\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}-\dfrac{2g^{2}m^{x}}{\omega}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}^{x}.

(32)

In the same manner as discussed above, in the limit where the coupling
g
becomes large and the second term dominates, a second-order phase transition at finite temperature is expected to occur also in this Hamiltonian. However, numerical calculations at absolute zero temperature reported in Ref.
Rohn et al. 2020
have confirmed a first-order phase transition.

Since this Hamiltonian possesses a
Z_{2}\times Z_{2}
symmetry
Rohn et al. 2020
, which differs from that of our model, the difference may arise from the fact that the two systems belong to different universality classes.

Therefore, our results strongly suggest the existence of a tricritical point, where the first-order and second-order phase transition lines meet, at finite temperature, or that the transition point at zero temperature serves as such a point.

IV
Conclusion

The one-dimensional classical ferromagnetic spin chain is known as a model that does not exhibit a magnetic phase transition at finite temperature. However, in a modified version where the external magnetic field is quantized as a cavity photon field—i.e., a model coupled to cavity photons—a second-order magnetic phase transition does occur, accompanied by a finite expectation value of the cavity electromagnetic field, known as the superradiant phase transition (SRPT). This model strongly suggests that the emergence of superradiant order originates from the all-to-all interactions mediated by photons. Furthermore, analytical expressions for the magnetization, electromagnetic field, and critical temperature are found to hold exactly, at least in the limits of infinitely strong and infinitely weak coupling. These results rely on two facts: the mean-field approximation becomes exact for fully connected interactions, and the one-dimensional classical spin chain with an external field is exactly solvable. A similar second-order phase transition can be observed in the Dicke model, which is well known and can be revalidated using the approach presented in this study. It is also observed in the Hamiltonian studied by Rohn et al.
Rohn et al. 2020
. Furthermore, when combined with their previous perturbative study at zero temperature, these findings suggest the possible existence of a tricritical point.

Appendix A
Derivation Showing that the Mean-Field Approximation Becomes Exact in the Fully Connected Model

In this appendix, we evaluate the partition function of the fully connected model described by the Hamiltonian

H=-\dfrac{g^{2}}{N\omega}\sum_{i\neq j}\sigma_{i}^{\mu}\sigma_{j}^{\mu}.

(33)

Here, Let the partition function be denoted by
Z
, which is given by

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle=\Tr{\exp\left(\beta\dfrac{g^2}{N\omega} \sum_{i \neq j} \sigma_i^{\mu} \sigma_j^{\mu} \right)}

(34)

\displaystyle\simeq\Tr{\exp\left(\beta\dfrac{g^2}{N\omega} \left(\sum_{i=1}^N \sigma_i^\mu\right)^2 \right)}

(35)

The approximation used here becomes exact in the thermodynamic limit
N\rightarrow{\infty}
. Furthermore, by using the relation
\exp(bx^2)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dfrac{da}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp(-a^2+2a\sqrt{b}x)
,

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle=\Tr{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dfrac{da}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp(-a^2+2a\sqrt{\dfrac{\beta g^2}{N\omega}}\sum_{i=1}^N \sigma_i^\mu)}

(36)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dfrac{da}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp(-a^2 N\log(2\cosh(2a\sqrt{\dfrac{\beta g^2}{N\omega}})))

(37)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\sqrt{\dfrac{\beta Ng^{2}}{2\pi\omega}}dm\exp(-N\left(\dfrac{\beta g^2}{\omega}m^2-\log(2\cosh(\beta\dfrac{2g^2}{\omega}m))\right))

(38)

In the thermodynamic limit
N\rightarrow{\infty}
, the saddle-point condition leads to

\dfrac{\partial}{\partial m}\left(\dfrac{\beta g^{2}}{\omega}m^{2}-\log(2\cosh(\beta\dfrac{2g^2}{\omega}m))\right)=0

(39)

Thus, we get

m=\tanh(2\beta\dfrac{g^2}{\omega}m)

(40)

This corresponds exactly to the case of
J=0
in the self-consistent equation Eq. (
24
) which is derived in the main text using a partial mean-field approximation.

Appendix B
Derivation of the critical coupling constant from the mean-field approximated Dicke model

Here, we show that the critical coupling constant Eq. (
30
) can be derived from the partial mean-field approximated Dicke model Eq. (
29
).

Letting
h_{\text{eff}}
be
\sqrt{\omega_{0}^{2}+h_{x}^{2}}
,
h_{x}=\dfrac{2g^{2}m^{x}}{\omega}
the partition function par spin can be written as follows.

Z_{\text{one spin}}=\exp(-\beta h_\text{eff})+\exp(\beta h_\text{eff})=2\cosh(\beta h_\text{eff}).

(41)

By applying the relation

m^{x}=\dfrac{1}{\beta Z_{\text{one spin}}}\dfrac{\partial Z_{\text{one spin}}}{\partial h_{x}}

(42)

we get

m^{x}=\dfrac{h_{x}}{h_{\text{eff}}}\tanh(\beta h_\text{eff})

(43)

Thus, we arrive at the following self-consistent equation.

\sqrt{\omega_{0}^{2}+\left(\dfrac{2g^{2}m^{x}}{\omega}\right)^{2}}=\dfrac{2g^{2}}{\omega}\tanh\left(\beta\sqrt{\omega_{0}^{2}+\left(\dfrac{2g^{2}m^{x}}{\omega}\right)^{2}}\right)

(44)

At the critical point,
m^{x}=0
,
g=g_{\text{c}}
holds, so

\omega_{0}=\dfrac{2g_{\text{c}}^{2}}{\omega}\tanh(\beta\omega_0)

(45)

and we get Eq. (
30
).
