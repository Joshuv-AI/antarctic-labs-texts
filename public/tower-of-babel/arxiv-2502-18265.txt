A Competitive Posted-Price Mechanism for Online Budget-Feasible Auctions
Andreas Charalampopoulos, Dimitris Fotakis, Panagiotis Patsilinakos, Thanos Tolias

Abstract
We consider online procurement auctions, where the agents arrive sequentially, in random order, and have private costs for their services. The buyer aims to maximize a monotone submodular value function for the subset of agents whose services are procured, subject to a budget constraint on their payments. We consider a posted-price setting where upon each agent’s arrival, the buyer decides on a payment offered to them. The agent accepts or rejects the offer, depending on whether the payment exceeds their cost, without revealing any other information about their private costs whatsoever. We present a randomized online posted-price mechanism with constant competitive ratio, thus resolving the main open question of (Badanidiyuru, Kleinberg and Singer, EC 2012). Posted-price mechanisms for online procurement typically operate by learning an estimation of the optimal value, denoted as OPT \mathrm{OPT} , and using it to determine the payments offered to the agents. The main challenge is to learn OPT \mathrm{OPT} within a constant factor from the agents’ accept / reject responses to the payments offered. Our approach is based on an online test of whether our estimation is too low compared against OPT \mathrm{OPT} and a carefully designed adaptive search that gradually refines our estimation.

Introduction

We consider a model of procurement auctions, introduced by Singer

f:2^{\mathcal{N}}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

B

In the algorithmic problem, the buyer is aware of the agent costs
c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}
and aims to compute a subset
S\subseteq\mathcal{N}
of agents (a.k.a. a solution) that maximizes
f(S)

In the mechanism design setting of

The mechanism must be budget-feasible with respect to the payments

Conceptually, individual rationality and truthfulness require that the payments are large enough to cover the agent costs and incentivize truthful reporting, while budget feasibility requires that the payments are kept low, so that the value of the solution is maximized.

1.1
Contribution and General Approach

In this work, we present a randomized constant-competitive posted-price mechanism for online procurement auctions with monotone submodular valuations, thus resolving the main open question of Badanidiyuru et al.
[
17
]
. More formally, we show the following:

Theorem 1.1

There is a universally truthful
O(1)
-competitive randomized posted-price mechanism for budget-feasible online procurement auctions with secretary agent arrivals and monotone submodular buyer’s valuations.

The mechanism of Theorem
1.1
satisfies budget-feasibility with certainty, and is individually rational and universally (and obviously) truthful
2
2

2

A randomized mechanism is universally truthful if it can be expressed as a probability distribution over truthful deterministic mechanisms. A mechanism is
obviously truthful

[
18
]
if it has an equilibrium in obviously dominant strategies. A strategy is
obviously dominant
if the best outcome under any possible deviation from it is no better than the worst outcome under the given strategy.
,
with the latter properties following directly from the definition of posted-price mechanisms, where the agents to act in their best interest when selecting whether to accept the payment offered or not.

Posted-price mechanisms for online procurement typically operate by learning an estimation of the optimal value
\mathrm{OPT}
(usually referred to as a
threshold
, for brevity)
\hat{t}
and using it to determine the payments (a.k.a.
prices
) offered to the agents. We restrict our attention to
linear-price
mechanisms, where the price offered to agent
i
is
p_{i}=f_{S}(i)B/\hat{t}
, where
B
is the buyer’s budget,
\hat{t}
is the mechanism’s current threshold,
S
is the subset of agents selected in the solution up to
i
’s arrival, and
f_{S}(i)=f(S\cup\{i\})-f(S)
is the marginal value due to
i
’s inclusion in the solution.

One can show (see e.g., the proof of Lemma
12
or
[
17
, Lemma 4.4]
) that linear-price mechanisms with thresholds
\hat{t}
that remain within a constant fraction of
\mathrm{OPT}
for a constant fraction of the agent sequence are constant-competitive. Hence, the main challenge in the design of efficient linear-price mechanisms (and the main difference between them) is how to maintain a threshold
\hat{t}
that mostly remains within a constant fraction of
\mathrm{OPT}
.

The posted-price mechanism of Badanidiyuru et al.
[
17
, Section 4]
uses linear prices and a fixed threshold
\hat{t}
determined before the first offer to an agent. In Theorem
3.1
, we show that the competitive ratio of any linear-price mechanism that selects a fixed threshold without any knowledge of the agent costs is
\Omega(\log n)
. Hence, we consider
adaptive linear-price
mechanisms based on a threshold that evolves over time.

Our approach is inspired by the constant-competitive adaptive linear-price mechanism of
[
17
, Section 3]
for symmetric submodular valuations and agents sampled independently from an unknown distribution.
As in
[
17
, Section 3]
, our approach is based on an online test (cf. Mechanism
2
, usually referred to as
TestThreshold
) that efficiently decides if the current threshold
\hat{t}
is less than
\mathrm{OPT}/4
. TestThreshold comes with an one-sided guarantee: with constant probability, TestThreshold succeeds (i.e., it responds that the current threshold
\hat{t}\leq\mathrm{OPT}/4
), if it is applied with any threshold
\hat{t}\leq\mathrm{OPT}/4
to a random subset of agents of size
\Omega(v_{\text{max}}n/\mathrm{OPT})
, where
v_{\text{max}}=\max_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\{f(\{i\})\}
is the maximum value of an agent (Property
2
). If TestThreshold is applied with a threshold
\hat{t}>\mathrm{OPT}/4
, there is no guarantee on its response. If TestThreshold fails (i.e., it responds that the current threshold
\hat{t}>\mathrm{OPT}/4
), we become aware that
\hat{t}
is rather too large and should be decreased; otherwise, TestThreshold contributes
\Omega(v_{\text{max}}\hat{t}/OPT)
to the total value of the mechanism.

The high level idea of our approach is to first obtain a rough estimation of
\mathrm{OPT}
and then apply binary search, guided by TestThreshold, to the possible range of
\mathrm{OPT}
defined by our initial estimation. At the conceptual level, our approach consists of three periods:

Learning
v_{\text{max}}
:

As in standard secretary algorithms, we start with a
learning period
that considers a constant fraction of the agents, where we do not make any offers and aim to estimate
v_{\text{max}}
with constant probability. We note that
v_{\text{max}}\leq\mathrm{OPT}\leq n\cdot v_{\text{max}}
.

Binary Search:

Using TestThreshold, we apply binary search to the set
\{v_{\text{max}},2v_{\text{max}},4v_{\text{max}},\ldots
,
2^{\log n}v_{\text{max}}\}
of different estimations of
\mathrm{OPT}
expressed as a power of
2
times
v_{\text{max}}
. This period aims to compute a threshold
\hat{t}
within a constant fraction of
\mathrm{OPT}
. Our application of binary search requires
\Theta(\log\log n)

phases
, each consisting of
O(\log\log n)
negatively-dependent
rounds
where we apply TestThreshold (Appendix

0.B.4
). Hence, we can prove that using
O((\log\log n)^{2})
applications of TestThreshold to the next constant fraction of agents, we end up with a threshold
\hat{t}\geq\mathrm{OPT}/8
with constant probability (Lemma
9
).

Exploitation:

Applying TestThreshold to the last constant fraction of agents, where every time the test succeeds
\hat{t}
is doubled, and every time the test fails
\hat{t}
is halved, we collect an expected total value that is within a constant fraction of
\mathrm{OPT}
(Lemma
10
).

The three-period approach above comprises a constant-competitive posted-price mechanism for online budget-feasible procurement, but only under a
large market assumption
. Specifically, in order to show a constant competitive ratio, we need to assume that
\mathrm{OPT}/v_{\text{max}}=\Omega((\log\log n)^{2})
, i.e., that the optimal solution consists of sufficiently many agents.
Such large market is necessary in order to guarantee that each of the
O((\log\log n)^{2})
applications of TestThreshold considers a random subset of agents of size
\Omega(v_{\text{max}}n/\mathrm{OPT})
. This is necessary, so that we can prove that the entire period of Binary Search succeeds with constant probability.

We can deal with the case where
\mathrm{OPT}/v_{\text{max}}=o((\log\log n)^{2})
by applying Dynkin’s secretary algorithm
[
21
]
with constant probability, which results in competitive ratio of
O((\log\log n)^{2})
(which can be improved to
O(\log\log n)
with a careful analysis).

To remove the large market assumption and obtain a constant-competitive mechanism, we assume that
\mathrm{OPT}\geq\beta v_{\text{max}}
, where
\beta
is a sufficiently large constant. We define a power tower sequence as
\psi_{1}=\beta
and
\psi_{j+1}=2^{\psi_{j}}\cdot\psi_{j}
and apply TestThreshold with thresholds
\psi_{j}v_{\text{max}}
in order to determine a pair of consecutive intervals
[\psi_{j}v_{\text{max}},\psi_{j+1}v_{\text{max}})
and
[\psi_{j+1}v_{\text{max}},\psi_{j+2}v_{\text{max}})
such that
\mathrm{OPT}
is included in their union. We show how to compute such a pair of intervals with constant probability by
O(\log^{\ast}n)
applications of TestThreshold to a constant fraction of the agent sequence in total (Lemma
20
).
Selecting one of these intervals
[\psi_{j}v_{\text{max}},\psi_{j+1}v_{\text{max}})
and
[\psi_{j+1}v_{\text{max}},\psi_{j+2}v_{\text{max}})
at random and applying Binary Search and Exploitation, as described above, to the chosen interval (instead of the interval
[\beta v_{\text{max}},nv_{\text{max}})]
initially considered) satisfies the required large market property and results in a constant-competitive posted-price mechanism, are required by Theorem
1.1
.

Model, Definitions and Preliminaries

A set function
f:2^{\mathcal{N}}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is
non-decreasing
(often referred to as
monotone
) if for all
S\subseteq T\subseteq\mathcal{N}
,
f(S)\leq f(T)
. A function
f
is
submodular
if
f
has non-increasing marginal values, i.e., for all
S\subseteq T\subseteq\mathcal{N}
and all
i\not\in T
,
f(T\cup\{i\})-f(T)\leq f(S\cup\{i\})-f(S)
. We let
f_{S}(i)=f(S\cup\{i\})-f(S)
denote the marginal value of
i
with respect to
S
. In this work, we consider valuation functions
f:2^{\mathcal{N}}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
that are normalized, i.e. have
f(\emptyset)=0
, monotone and submodular.

A valuation function
f
is accessed by
value queries
, which for any given set
S\subseteq\mathcal{N}
, return
f(S)
. We let
v_{\text{max}}=\max_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\{f(\{i\})\}
denote the maximum value of any agent. In the following, all logarithms are base-
2
unless stated otherwise.

Budget-Feasible Procurement Auctions and Mechanisms.

We consider procurement auctions with a set
\mathcal{N}=\{1,\ldots,n\}
of agents. Each agent
i
has a private cost
c_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
for participating in the solution. The buyer has a budget
B\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and aims to maximize a monotone submodular valuation
f:2^{\mathcal{N}}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, subject to the constraint that the sum of payments to the agents must be at most
B
.

A (deterministic)
direct revelation mechanism

\mathcal{M}=(A,p)
is a pair consisting of an allocation function and a payment function. The allocation function
A:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}\to 2^{\mathcal{N}}
maps a bid vector
\vec{b}=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})
submitted by the agents to a solution
A(\vec{b})=S\subseteq\mathcal{N}
. The payment function
p:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}
computes the payments
p(\vec{b})
allocated to the agents for the solution
A(\vec{b})
. A randomized mechanism is a probability distribution over deterministic mechanisms.

Given a mechanism
\mathcal{M}=(A,p)
, each agent
i
aims to maximize their
utility

u_{i}(\vec{b})
through their bid. It is
u_{i}(\vec{b})=p_{i}(\vec{b})-c_{i}
, if
i
is included in the solution
A(\vec{b})
, and
u_{i}(\vec{b})=p_{i}(\vec{b})
, otherwise.

A mechanism is
budget-feasible
if for every bid vector
\vec{b}
,
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}p_{i}(\vec{b})\leq B
. A mechanism is
individually rational
, if for every bid vector
\vec{b}
and all agents
i\in\mathcal{N}
,
u_{i}(\vec{b})\geq 0
. Hence,
p_{i}(\vec{b})\geq 0
for all agents
i\in\mathcal{N}
, and
p_{i}(\vec{b})\geq c_{i}
for all agents
i
included in the solution
A(\vec{b})
. Due to individual rationality and the budget constraint, we always let
p_{i}(\vec{b})=0
for all agents
i\not\in A(\vec{b})
.

A mechanism is
truthful
, if reporting their cost
c_{i}
is a dominant strategy for every agent
i
, i.e., for all
i\in\mathcal{N}
, all
b\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and every bidding vector
\vec{b}_{-i}
of the other agents,
u_{i}(\vec{b}_{-i},c_{i})\geq u_{i}(\vec{b}_{-i},b)
.

For randomized mechanisms, that we consider in this work, we require that the mechanism is budget feasible and individually rational
with certainty
and
universally truthful
, i.e., the mechanism is a probability distribution over deterministic truthful mechanisms.

Online Budget-Feasible Procurement Auctions.

In the online setting, agents arrive sequentially and the decision about whether the present agent
i
is included in the solution and
i
’s payment is
irrevocable
and is taken upon
i
’s arrival, using only information about the agents having arrived before
i
and without any knowledge about future agent arrivals whatsoever.

In this work, we consider an online procurement auctions with
secretary arrivals
, where the set
\mathcal{N}
of agents is chosen adversarially and the agents arrive in random order (formally, the agents’ arrival order is drawn uniformly at random from all permutations of
\mathcal{N}
).

Posted-Price Mechanisms.

We restrict our attention to (online) sequential
posted-price
mechanisms. A posted-price mechanism decides on the price
p_{i}
of each agent
i
upon
i
’s arrival and makes
i
a take-it-or-leave-it offer. Agent
i
accepts or rejects the offer, depending on whether
p_{i}\geq c_{i}
or not, without revealing any other information about their private cost
c_{i}
whatsoever. If agent
i
accepts the offer,
i
is included in the mechanism’s solution and the budget currently available decreases by
p_{i}
. Otherwise, we let
i
’s price be
p_{i}=0
and agent
i
is discarded.

Randomized posted-price mechanisms are individually rational and universally (and obviously) truthful, because among the two options available (accept or reject the mechanism’s offer), the agents select the option that maximizes their utility
\max\{0,c_{i}-p_{i}\}
. Moreover, posted-price mechanisms are budget feasible, assuming that each price offered doed not exceed the budget currently available.

We further restrict our attention to
linear-price
mechanisms, which maintain a threshold
\hat{t}
and offer a price
p_{i}=f_{S}(i)B/\hat{t}
to every agent
i
(assuming that
p_{i}
does not exceed the budget currently available). The threshold
\hat{t}
is an estimation of the optimal value and is maintained based on the marginal values of previous agents and their accept / reject decisions.

We say that a linear-price mechanism is
adaptive
, if it may use a different threshold for determining the linear price offered to each agent, and
non-adaptive
, if the mechanism decides on a fixed threshold before its first offer to an agent and uses it for all remaining agents.

Competitive Ratio.

We evaluate the performance of our online mechanisms using the competitive ratio
[
32
]
. We compare the performance our our mechanism against the offline optimum, denoted
\mathrm{OPT}
, which is the value of an optimal solution
S^{\ast}\subseteq\mathcal{N}
that maximized
f(S^{\ast})
subject to
\sum_{i\in S^{\ast}}c_{i}\leq B
. We note that
\mathrm{OPT}=f(S^{\ast})
is defined with respect to a computationally unrestricted algorithm with full advance knowledge of the set
\mathcal{N}
of agents and their costs
c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}
.

In our setting, the competitive ratio
cr
of an algorithm is the worst-case ratio, over all possible sets of agents
\mathcal{N}
, of
\mathrm{OPT}
divided by the algorithm’s expected cost
\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{ALG}]
(where the expectation is taken over both the agents’ arrival order and the algorithm’s random choices).

3
A Logarithmic Lower Bound for Non-Adaptive Linear-Price Mechanisms

To motivate the use of non-adaptive linear-price mechanisms, we first prove the following:

Theorem 3.1

Every randomized non-adaptive linear-price mechanism is at least
\frac{\log n}{2}
-competitive.

Proof
(Proofsketch.)

We consider a family of instances
\mathcal{I}=\{I_{0},I_{1},...,I_{\log n}\}
. Every instance consists of
n
value-private cost pairs
(v,c)
, where for instance
I_{i}
,
v=1
and
c=B/2^{i}
, for every
i\in\{0,1,...,\log n\}
.

We construct a probability distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{I}}
over the family
\mathcal{I}
of instances. For every
i=0,1,2,\ldots,\log(n)-1
, we let
I_{i}
occur with probability
p_{i}=1/2^{i+1}
in
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{I}}
. We let
I_{\log n}
occur with probability
p_{\log n}=1/n
in
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{I}}
, so that the sum of
p_{i}
s is equal to
1
. To conclude the proof, we show that the expected value of any deterministic non-adaptive linear-price mechanism on
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{I}}
is at most
1
, while the expected value of the optimal solution on
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{I}}
is
\frac{\log n}{2}+1
. Then, the theorem follows from Yao’s principle (
[
32
, Chapter 8.4]
and
[
33
]
). We provide a detailed proof in Section
0.A
.

The key idea behind Theorem
3.1
is that a non-adaptive linear-price mechanism has to decide on a fixed threshold
\hat{t}
before getting any information about the agents’ private costs (which for posted-price mechanisms is obtained only by offering prices to the agents and collecting their accept / reject responses). Hence, essentially the best approach of non-adaptive linear-price mechanisms is to learn
v_{\max}
and select a threshold
\hat{t}
from
\{1,2,4,\ldots,2^{\log n}\}
uniformly at random (which is what the posted-price mechanism of
[
17
, Section 4]
actually does). Hence, to improve on the logarithmic competitive ratio of
[
17
, Section 4]
, we next present an adaptive linear-price mechanism.

4
Posted-Price Mechanism: Building Blocks and Outline of the Analysis

We next present the main steps of our posted-price
LM-Mechanism
and the key ideas of its analysis.
LM-Mechanism
assumes that
\mathrm{OPT}/v_{\text{max}}
is sufficient large (LM stands for
Large Market
). E.g., it suffices that
\mathrm{OPT}/v_{\text{max}}\geq 10^{7}
. In Section
9
, we use Dynkin’s algorithm
[
21
]
and an adaptation of the posted-price mechanism in
[
17
, Section 4]
to deal with the case where
\mathrm{OPT}/v_{\text{max}}<10^{7}
.

As explained in Section
1.1
,
LM-Mechanism
consists of
4
periods. Each of the first three periods “consumes” a constant fraction of the agent sequence (and at most a constant fraction of the budget
B
) and provides the next period with a more refined estimation of
\mathrm{OPT}
. More specifically,
LM-Mechanism
proceeds along the following periods:

Period 1 – LearningMaxValue:

The first period calls Mechanism
3
, usually referred to as LearningMaxValue, which
examines about
1/3
of the agent sequence and returns the maximum value of an agent in this part. We let
\mathcal{E}_{1}
denote the event that the value returned by
LearningMaxValue
is indeed
v_{\text{max}}
, which occurs with probability
1/3
. Assuming
\mathcal{E}_{1}
, we have a first rough estimation of
\mathrm{OPT}
, since
v_{\text{max}}\leq\mathrm{OPT}\leq n\cdot v_{\text{max}}
.

Period 2 – PowerTowerSearch:

The second period calls Mechanism
4
, usually referred to as PowerTowerSearch,
which decides on two consecutive intervals
A=[a_{\min},a_{\max}]
and
B=[b_{\min},b_{\max}]
such that
a_{\max}=2^{\frac{a_{\min}}{v_{\text{max}}}}a_{\min}
and
b_{\max}=2^{\frac{b_{\min}}{v_{\text{max}}}}b_{\min}
. PowerTowerSearch then selects one of the two intervals uniformly at random and returns its endpoints as
t_{\min}
and
t_{\max}
. We let
\mathcal{E}_{2}
denote the event that
\mathrm{OPT}\in A\cup B
and
\mathcal{E}_{5}
denote the event that conditional on
\mathcal{E}_{2}
, the right interval of
A
and
B
is chosen.
In Section
6
, we discuss the implementation of
PowerTowerSearch
and prove that
\mathcal{E}_{2}
occurs with probability at least
0.9
.

Period 3 – BinarySearch:

The third period calls Mechanism
5
, usually referred to as BinarySearch, which performs binary search in the interval
[t_{\min},t_{\max}]
and returns
t_{\text{init}}
. Assuming a proper execution of BinarySearch, which is implied by event
\mathcal{E}_{3}
formally defined later on, we have that
\mathrm{OPT}/4\leq t_{\text{init}}\leq O(\log\log(t_{\max}/t_{\min}))\mathrm{OPT}
. The analysis of
BinarySearch
is presented in Section
7
.

Period 4 – Exploitation:

The fourth period calls Mechanism
6
, usually referred to as Exploitation, which performs an adaptive search, starting with
t_{\text{init}}
, on possible values of the mechanism’s linear-price threshold that are powers of
2
(see also
[
17
, Section 3]
). Assuming its proper execution, which is also implied by event
\mathcal{E}_{3}
, Exploitation collects
\Omega(\mathrm{OPT})
value from its part of the agent sequence. The analysis of
Exploitation
is presented in Section
8
.

Different periods, as they run, update
LM-Mechanism
’s state with consists of its current solution
S
, the total value collected so far
val
, the budget available
B_{\text{rem}}
and the sequence of agents not yet considered
\mathcal{N}_{\text{rem}}
.
Each of the last three periods examine a (relatively small) constant fraction of the agent sequence. We let
\mathcal{E}_{4}
denote the event that the total number of agents examined by the
4
periods of
LM-Mechanism
is at most
n
(which along with events
\mathcal{E}_{2}
and
\mathcal{E}_{3}
also imply that the total budget expended is at most
B
). The correctness of
LM-Mechanism
is implied by event
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap\mathcal{E}_{2}\cap\mathcal{E}_{3}\cap\mathcal{E}_{4}\cap\mathcal{E}_{5}
. The correctness probability of
LM-Mechanism
is analyzed in Section
4.2
.

The two key technical ingredients in the analysis of
LM-Mechanism
is a partitioning of the last
3
periods into phases and rounds (as depicted Fig.
1
) and
TestEstimate
, which is applied to each round and tests if the current threshold is too large or too small.
We next discuss their main properties.

4.1
Partitioning into Rounds and Phases

To partition the agent sequence into rounds, we employ a standard technique
[
34
]
, where round lengths are drawn from a binomial distribution to ensure independence among agents and that the resulting prefix of the sequence is a
random subset
of
\mathcal{N}
, where each agent participates with a given probability (often referred to as the round’s
participation probability
and denoted by
q
).

Formally, for any suffix
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{N}
of the agent sequence and any
a\in(0,1)
, an
a
-round
(or simply a
round
, if
a
is clear from the context) is a prefix of
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
with length drawn from a binomial distribution
\mathcal{B}(|\mathcal{N}^{\prime}|,a)
with parameters
|\mathcal{N}^{\prime}|
and
a
. We usually refer to
a
as the the
length parameter
(or the
length probability
) of the round.
Along with the random arrival order of the agents, selecting the length of a round as a binomially distributed random variable ensures that every two agents in
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
are included in the given round independently with probability
a
(which allows us to analyze the properties of each round using standard concentration inequalities).

Given the agent sequence
\mathcal{N}
, we define a sequence of
\kappa\geq 1
rounds with length parameters
a_{1},\ldots,a_{\kappa}
as follows: we select the length
x_{1}
of the
1
st round from the binomial distribution
\mathcal{B}(n,a_{1})
, let
n_{1}=n-x_{1}
be the number of agents remaining, select the length
x_{2}
of the
2
nd round from
\mathcal{B}(n_{1},a_{2})
,
\ldots
, let
n_{i}=n_{i-1}-x_{i}
be the number of agents remaining after the
i
-th round, select the length
x_{i+1}
of the
(i+1)
-th round from
\mathcal{B}(n_{i},a_{i+1})
,
\ldots
, select the length
x_{\kappa}
of the
\kappa
-th round from
\mathcal{B}(n_{\kappa-1},a_{\kappa})
. The following analyzes the distribution of the resulting random subsets of agents:

Lemma 1

For any
\kappa\geq 1
, we consider a sequence of round lengths distributed binomially as described above with length parameters
a_{1},\ldots,a_{\kappa}
. Then, each agent
b\in\mathcal{N}
is included in round
\kappa
independently of the other agents with participation probability

q_{\kappa}=a_{\kappa}\prod_{i=1}^{\kappa-1}(1-a_{i})

(2)

Proof
(Proofsketch.)

The proof consists of two parts. First we observe that due to secretary agent arrivals, the probability that an agent belongs to any fixed interval of length
n_{i}
of an agent sequence of length
n
is
n_{i}/n
. Applying standard properties of the binomial distribution repeatedly, we derive the desired participation probability. Next, we use secretary agent arrivals and the binomial distribution of round lengths and show that the probability that any two agents belong to round
\kappa
is
q_{\kappa}^{2}
.
A detailed proof is given in Appendix
0.B.1
.

In our mechanism, we draw multiple rounds with significantly varying lengths, which complicates the calculation of each round’s participation probability using (
2
). The following lower bound on the participation probability allows us to argue about the properties of each round using its length parameter
a
. The proof requires the precise definition of the length parameters of all rounds considered by our mechanism and is deferred to Appendix
0.B.2
. For the proof, we use that the length parameter of every round is
o(1)
and that their sum over all rounds is significantly less than
1
(which also allows us to show that event
\mathcal{E}_{4}
occurs with probability at least
0.97
, see Lemma
20
in Appendix
0.B.5
).

Lemma 2

The participation probability of any round with length parameter
a
is at least
6a/(10e)
.

Good, Dense Rounds and Phases.

Our goal is to use TestThreshold on rounds in order to assess wether a threshold
t
underestimates the true optimum. To this end, we will call an
\left(a,t\right)
-round, a round with length parameter
a
on which we test a threshold
t
. For our test to be correct consistently we need the expected number of agents included in both the optimal solution and our
\left(a,t\right)
-round to be at least a fixed constant, i.e.
a\geq\frac{v_{\text{max}}}{\mathrm{OPT}}
. Since we don’t know the value of
\mathrm{OPT}
and
t
is our estimate of it, we say that an
\left(a,t\right)
-round is ‘good’ if
a\geq 81\,e\,v_{\text{max}}/t
.

We allocate a budget
B_{j}=3\,C\cdot a_{j}\cdot B
to every round
j
with length parameter
a_{j}
, where
C=1/(8e)
is a fixed constant used in the following sections. For each round
j
with set of agents
\mathcal{N}_{j}
and length parameter
a_{j}
, we let
S^{\ast}_{j}\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{j}
be the set of agents in round
j
with maximum value
f(S^{\ast}_{j})
subject to the budget constraint
\sum_{i\in S_{j}^{\ast}}c_{i}\leq B_{j}
, and let
\mathrm{OPT}_{j}=f(S^{\ast}_{j})
be the optimal value of round
j
.

We say that an
\left(a_{j},t_{j}\right))
-round
j
is
dense
if
\mathrm{OPT}_{j}\geq C\cdot a_{j}\cdot\mathrm{OPT}
. Intuitively, a round is dense if it gets a fair share (with respect to its length parameter) of the optimal value. The following shows that ‘good’ rounds are dense with good probability.

Lemma 3

A ‘good’
\left(a_{j},t_{j}\right)
-round
j
, such that
t_{j}\leq\mathrm{OPT}
, is dense with probability at least
0.9
.

The high level intuition behind the proof of Lemma
3
is that a good round is a random set that includes, in expectation, sufficiently many agents of the optimal solution. The requirement on the round being ‘good’ is mostly technical and is used in the proof of Lemma
3
. Lemma
3
is key to the analysis of
LM-Mechanism
and can be found in Section
0.B.3
.

Applying
TestEstimate
to a dense round with threshold
t\leq\mathrm{OPT}/4
should verify that
t
is not too large and can be increased. To amplify the confidence of our test, we apply
TestEstimate
to a sequence of rounds. A
\left(\delta,a,t\right)
-phase
is a sequence of
\delta

a
and all testing the same threshold

(see also Fig.
1

We note that
TestEstimate
is always applied with the same threshold

to every round of a given phase.
A
\left(\delta,a,t\right)
-phase is
dense
if at least
\delta/2
of its rounds are dense. In Appendix

A

-phase, consisting of ‘good’ rounds and with

1-\exp(\frac{-4\cdot\delta}{45})

For the proof of Lemma
4

Remark 1

We should highlight that the randomness in the agent arrivals and the internal randomness of
LM-Mechanism
are only used (i) to partition the agent sequence into rounds consisting of random subsets of agents; and (ii) to select the right interval between
A
and
B
in Period
2
. Everything else in
LM-Mechanism
is deterministic. In fact, once the rounds are formed,
TestEstimate
and the other parts of
LM-Mechanism
operate without assuming anything about the order in which the agents arrive. ∎

\mathcal{E}_{1}

Lemma 5

Event

Proof

In Appendix

\mathbb{P}\!\left[\mathcal{E}_{2}\right]\geq 0.9

4.3
Estimation Testing

The key building block of our
LM-Mechanism
is an online test, usually referred to as
TestThreshold
, which is applied to a
(\delta,a,t)
-phase.
TestThreshold
, whose pseudocode can be found in Mechanism
2
, determines whether:

it is possible to collect value at least
C\cdot a\cdot\hat{t}
, where
C=1/(7e)
by applying linear-pricing with threshold
\hat{t}
to a round with length parameter
a
.

TestThreshold
determines the set
\mathcal{N}_{\text{round}}
of agents of each round by drawing their sizes from a binomial distribution
\mathcal{B}(|\mathcal{N}_{\text{rem}}|,a)
. Then, it offers every agent
b\in\mathcal{N}_{\text{round}}
the corresponding linear price
p_{b}=f_{S}(b)B/\hat{t}
and collects value
f_{S}(b)
, if the price
p_{b}
is accepted. We write that
TestThreshold
succeeds in a round, if it collects value at least
C\cdot a\cdot\hat{t}
from the present round, and fails, otherwise. We write that
TestThreshold
succeeds in a phase (or just succeeds) when at least half of the rounds are successful.

TestThreshold
is the only point where
LM-Mechanism
makes offers to the agents, collects value and expends budget. Periods
2
and
3
determine the length parameters and the number of phases to which
TestThreshold
is applied, and use their success / fail responses to guide our adaptive search for a good estimate of
\mathrm{OPT}
. Then, Period
4
applies
TestThreshold
to phases with appropriate length parameters in order to collect a total value of
\Omega(\mathrm{OPT})
.
If during the execution of
LM-Mechanism
, either the budget or the agent sequence is exhausted, or an agent with value larger than
v_{\text{max}}
is found, event
\mathcal{E}
is violated and
TestThreshold
(and
LM-Mechanism
) abort (without any value).

were included in the optimal solution but not in
S_{j}
. This can happen for one of two reasons: either their private costs exceeded the mechanism’s offer, i.e.,
c_{b}>f_{S_{j}}(b)\,B\,/\,\hat{t}
, or

was insufficient to make an appropriate offer to

. However, by design, only the first case can occur. Due to our linear pricing scheme, no agent is ever offered a price larger than
v_{\text{max}}\,B\,/\,\hat{t}\leq\frac{B}{1024}
, where the inequality holds because we always use thresholds
\hat{t}\geq 1024\,v_{\text{max}}
. Combining this with Lemma
6
, we conclude that all agents
b\in S_{j}^{*}\setminus S_{j}
declined our offer.

We can rewrite the sum of the values of these agents as:

\sum_{b\in S_{j}^{*}\setminus S_{j}}f_{S_{j}}(b)=\sum_{b\in S_{j}^{*}\setminus S_{j}}\frac{f_{S_{j}}(b)}{c_{b}}\cdot c_{b}<\sum_{b\in S_{j}^{*}\setminus S_{j}}\frac{\hat{t}}{B}\cdot c_{b}\leq\hat{t}\cdot\frac{B_{j}}{B}=3\cdot C\cdot a_{j}\cdot\hat{t}

(5)

The second to last inequality follows from the hypothesis
c_{b}>f_{S_{j}}(b)\,B\,/\,\hat{t}
and the last inequality follows from the fact that
S_{j}^{*}
is budget-feasible with respect to
B_{j}=3\cdot C\cdot a_{j}\cdot B
.

Using monotonicity and submodularity of the valuation function we have:

f(S_{j}^{*})-f(S_{j})\leq f(S_{j}^{*}\cup S_{j})-f(S_{j})\leq\sum_{b\in S_{j}^{*}\setminus S_{j}}f_{S_{j}}(b)

(6)

Applying (
5
) to (
6
), we get that
f(S_{j})\geq f(S_{j}^{*})-3\cdot C\cdot a_{j}\cdot\hat{t}
. Finally, using the hypothesis that round
j
is dense, and hence
f(S^{\ast}_{j})\geq C\cdot a_{j}\cdot\mathrm{OPT}
, we obtain that:

f(S_{j})\geq C\cdot a_{j}\cdot\mathrm{OPT}-3\cdot C\cdot a_{j}\cdot\hat{t}

(7)

Since
\hat{t}\leq\frac{\mathrm{OPT}}{4}
,

f(S_{j})\geq 4\cdot C\cdot a_{j}\cdot\hat{t}-3\cdot C\cdot a_{j}\cdot\hat{t}=C\cdot a_{j}\cdot\hat{t}
.
Thus,
TestThreshold
it collects value at least
C\cdot a_{j}\cdot\hat{t}
and succeeds.

The Lemma above extends to phases the following way:

Lemma 7

and returns

Conclusions

In this work, we have introduced a randomized, constant-competitive posted-price mechanism for online procurement auctions, thus resolving the main open question of Badanidiyuru, Kleinberg and Singer
[
17
]
. Our findings demonstrate that in online procurement auctions, sequential posted-price mechanisms can be as powerful, in terms of the asymptotics of their competitive ratio, as seal-bid mechanisms, whose performance has been extensively studied. We note that despite our mechanism is elaborate to design and analyze, its interface with the agents is simple and transparent.

In addition to improving the constants in our analysis, an interesting direction for further research is whether our refined adaptive search can be generalized and applied to other posted-price mechanisms, such as combinatorial auctions with submodular bidders
[
35
,

,

]
, towards improved competitive ratios.
