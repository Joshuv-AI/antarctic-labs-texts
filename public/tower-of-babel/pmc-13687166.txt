Intraoperative ventilation settings in Germany: an observational multicentre cross-sectional study
Authors: Linda Grüßer, András Keszei, Rolf Rossaint, Berit Zingraf, Julia Wallqvist, Sebastian Goldmann, Mark Coburn, Andreas Güldner, Thomas Hachenberg, Christian Hönemann, Thea Koch, Bert Mierke, Martin Schuster, Hermann Wrigge, Julia Schuler, Jan Larmann, Ana Kowark, NO-HARM Study Group, German Society of Anesthesiology and Intensive Care (GSAIC) Trials Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeIntraoperative lung protective ventilation and, in inhalational anesthesia, minimal fresh gas flow (FGF) are recommended. It remains unclear how these recommendations are transferred to clinical routine. We aimed to assess corresponding anesthesia practices in Germany and raise awareness of optimal ventilation settings.MethodsWe performed a pragmatic prospective observational national cross-sectional assessment of intraoperative ventilation settings in Germany, followed by an educational intervention and follow-up assessment. The intervention consisted of a pocket card designed in a Delphi-process. The patient-centred primary endpoint was tidal volume (VT)·kg−1 ideal body weight (IBW), and the environment-centred primary endpoint was FGF in inhalational anesthesia. In a post hoc analysis, we grouped departments into quintiles of baseline VT and FGF, and explored changes after the intervention.ResultsOver 240 of 1,148 anesthesiology departments participated (21%). We collected data from 2,198 operating rooms in the first (preintervention) and 2,106 in the second (postintervention) assessment (96% response rate). The mean (standard deviation) VT·kg−1 IBW was 7.4 (1.4) mL in the first assessment vs 7.3 (1.3) mL in the second assessment. The median [interquartile range (IQR)] FGFs were 0.60 [0.50–0.85] L·min−1 vs 0.50 [0.45–0.75] L·min−1, respectively. Exploratory multilevel regression analysis did not show an effect of the intervention. In the anesthesiology departments whose baseline ventilation settings were in the highest quintile, the intervention was associated with improvements in both VT and FGF.ConclusionsThis large-scale clinical study of intraoperative ventilation settings in Germany found that most anesthesiologists already followed current recommendations. Therefore, for the entire cohort, no major improvements were observed. The educational intervention might improve ventilation settings in anesthesiology departments whose baseline practice deviates strongly from current recommendations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s12630-026-03172-w.Keywords: anesthesia, climate change, fresh gas flow, lung-protective ventilation, ventilation settings, volatile anesthetics

Primum non nocere is the guiding principle in medicine. Intraoperative lung-protective ventilation and in inhalational anesthesia, the use of sevoflurane rather than desflurane and low fresh gas flow (FGF) settings are recommended for prevention of postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs) and for environmental stewardship, respectively.1–6

Excessive tidal volumes (VT) or high driving pressures are associated with PPCs, resulting in increased postoperative morbidity, mortality, and health care use.7 A multicentre observational study reported an increased risk of PPCs for 28.4% of 9,864 patients undergoing surgery.8 Currently available evidence suggests a VT of 6–8 mL·kg−1 ideal body weight (IBW), a low to moderate positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP), avoidance of unnecessarily high inspiratory oxygen concentrations, and normocapnia.2,9 Nevertheless, data indicate that the transition of these recommendations into daily routine could be improved.10–12

The choice of volatile anesthetic (VA) and FGF settings contribute to the overall carbon footprint of hospitals and thus climate change. Commonly used VAs are potent greenhouse gases with 100-year global warming potentials (reference CO2 = 1) ranging from 144 (sevoflurane) to 2,590 (desflurane).13,14 Data on current sustainable anesthesia practices are scarce but show untapped potential.15 In a worldwide survey, 64% of 1,285 anesthesiologists reported preferably using VAs. Only 49% used an FGF < 1.0 L·min−1, and only 15% reported using an FGF < 0.5 L·min−1.16 USA data showed mean (standard deviation [SD]) FGFs of 1.9 (0.9) L·min−1 in 831 desflurane cases and 2.5 (0.9) L·min−1 in 5,361 sevoflurane cases, respectively.17 Strategies targeting the choice of anesthetics and FGF settings have the potential to reduce emissions without compromising patient safety.4,18 Our previous single-centre data showed that the use of low FGF increased from 55% to 75% after implementation of educational interventions.19

In Germany, approximately 26,000 anesthesiologists perform an estimated 17 million cases per year.20,21 Surprisingly, it remains unknown which VTs, FGFs, and other ventilation settings are routinely used.

We conducted a pragmatic observational multicentre study, “Intraoperative Fresh Gas Flow and Ventilation Settings in Germany (NO-HARM),” with the aim to evaluate intraoperative FGF and ventilation settings used by anesthesiologists across different types of hospitals in Germany in daily routine. We hypothesized that VT in patients with ventilated lungs and FGF settings in inhalational anesthesia cases might be optimized. By providing information on best practices, we aimed to aid the implementation of improved ventilation settings in between two cross-sectional assessments.

Methods

Study design and ethical approval

We conducted a pragmatic prospective observational multicentre cross-sectional assessment of intraoperative ventilation parameters, followed by an educational intervention, and a follow-up cross-sectional assessment in Germany on 15 March 2023 and 16 June 2023, respectively.

A waiver was approved from the leading centre’s Independent Ethics Committee on 28 November 2022 (EK 22/384; Independent Ethics Committee, Faculty of Medicine, RWTH Aachen University, Aachen, Germany) and from participating centres’ responsible authorities according to local regulations. The study was performed following the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was prospectively registered in the German Clinical Trials Register (DRKS00029368). A data analysis and statistical plan was written and posted on a publicly accessible server before data were accessed. Reporting adheres to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines.22

Setting and participants

We screened all hospitals with an anesthesiology department and an e-mail address available for the department head listed in the Germany Hospital Directory.1 We then contacted the department heads via e-mail regarding study participation (N = 1,148). We included all operating rooms (ORs) with an anesthesiologist in charge in participating departments at 9:00 a.m. on the data collection day. There were no exclusion criteria regarding setting and participants.

Study flow and data collection

The heads of participating departments provided information in an online questionnaire on the number of hospital beds, ORs, employed physicians (including residents) and specialist anesthesiologists, and the presence of sustainability projects. On 15 March 2023 at 9:00 a.m., when we assumed that most patients would be in the maintenance phase of general anesthesia, all anesthesia-related parameters were collected anonymously on paper-based case report forms (CRFs) (Electronic Supplementary Material [ESM] eAppendix 1). To make study participation feasible for hospitals without dedicated research infrastructure, anesthesiologists in charge of each OR in the study recorded the data at the given time point. Data collection was restricted to a minimum to ensure anonymity of the patient and anesthesiologist. Each hospital received coded CRFs to allow adjustments on the hospital level in the statistical analysis. After the first assessment, all departments received hard-copy pocket cards with information regarding optimal intraoperative ventilation settings for distribution among their coworkers, on their own responsibility. After 3 months, on 16 June 2023, a second identical cross-sectional assessment took place.

All CRFs were submitted to the coordinating study centre after the respective assessment and were digitized in a Microsoft® Excel spreadsheet for Office365 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA) by trained study staff. All entries were independently reviewed by another trained study staff member for transmission discrepancy and plausibility.

Educational intervention

The pocket card was developed through a three-round remote Delphi process involving eight expert members (M. C., A. G., T. H., C. H., T. K., B. M., M. S., H. W.) of the German professional societies. Experts first provided input on patient-centred and environmental-centred anesthesia settings. The coordinating team (L. G., A. K., R. R.) synthesized their statements into concrete recommendations for standard ventilation settings in common patients, which the experts then reviewed. Last, all experts and the coordinating team agreed upon a final version of the pocket card (ESM eAppendix 2).

Outcomes

All variables and outcome measures are described in detail in the study protocol and the statistical analysis plan (ESM eAppendices 3 and 4). The primary outcome measures were 1) VT·kg−1 IBW in all patients whose lungs were ventilated in volume control (VC), pressure control (PC), or pressure support (PS) modes and 2) FGF in all inhalational anesthesia cases during the maintenance phase. We excluded cases with cardiopulmonary bypass for both primary outcome assessments. We further excluded patients with one-lung ventilation from analysis of VT·kg−1 IBW. Secondary outcomes comprised the type of hypnotics used, number of patients with lungs ventilated with VT ≥ 10 mL·kg−1 IBW, ventilation pressures, end-tidal carbon dioxide (EtCO2), fraction of inspired oxygen (FIO2), respiratory rate, average FGF in the induction phase, and use of neuromuscular and temperature monitoring.

Sample size

The sample size considerations were based on assumptions regarding the number of departments that were expected to agree to participate. According to the German Federal Statistical Office, there were 1,235 hospitals with anesthesiology specialists in Germany in 2019.23 We estimated that 30% of all anesthesiology departments with an average of six ORs would participate, which would result in 2,223 included cases per cross-sectional assessment. We assumed that 75% of these involved patients with ventilated lungs and that 50% would be available for FGF analyses. On the basis of previously published data, we assumed an SD between 1.4 and 2.1 mL·kg−1 for VT and between 1 and 1.5 L·min−1 for FGF.11,17,24,25 The expected width of a 95% confidence interval for a mean VT with a sample size of 1,500 was 0.14 to 0.21 mL·kg−1 and, for a mean FGF with a sample size of 1,000, was 0.12 to 0.19 L·min−1.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed after database cleaning and plausibility checks. Descriptive statistics are presented as mean (SD) or median [interquartile range (IQR)] for continuous variables, and number (percentage) for categorical variables.

Using an exploratory multilevel linear regression model, we assessed changes in log-transformed FGF and VT while adjusting for potential predicting factors as outlined in the statistical analysis plan (ESM eAppendix 4). We checked model assumptions of linear relationship with predefined variables graphically. In post hoc analyses, we explored changes in subgroups of departments defined by quintiles of VT and FGF settings at baseline.

For missing data on categorical variables, we applied a separate category in the models. Missing data on continuous variables were handled using multiple imputations by chained equations using predictive mean matching. We used Rubin’s rule to pool parameter estimates.

All analyses were performed using R Core team 4.1.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Models were fitted using the brms package (Version 2.18.0),26 and the mice package (Version 3.14.0)27 was used for imputations.

Bias

One designated anesthesiologist from each department received all information regarding the study and was instructed to briefly inform the team about the cross-sectional anonymous and voluntary data collection. Thus, the anesthesiologists were unaware of the study details to minimize the risk of modifications to standard practice. This approach was carefully weighed against data collection by a dedicated research team, which would be complicated for smaller hospitals. We aimed at minimizing nonresponder bias by making study participation as effortless as possible and limiting data collection to a minimum. To decrease the risk of detection bias, data collection was restricted to objective variables that are routinely documented in daily practice in anesthesia protocols or displayed on the anesthesia machine.

Results

Of 1,148 contacted departments, 246 (21%) participated in the first (preintervention) assessment and 242 of these 246 (98%) in the second (post intervention) assessment. Most were located in North-Rhine Westphalia (n = 71; 29%), consistent with the German population distribution (ESM eAppendix 5). The majority of departments were from hospitals with ≤ 300 beds (n = 88; 36%) and 301–600 beds (n = 87; 35%). Most cases (n = 1,957; 45%) were contributed by hospitals with > 900 beds. The median [IQR] number of physicians who worked at the respective departments was 26 [14–48]; the median [IQR] number of specialists was 15 [10–25].

In total, 4,479 ORs were running at the two cross-sectional assessments, and data were collected in 4,304 (96%) of them (2,198 in the first and 2,106 in the second assessment) (Fig. 1). Centres contributed a mean (SD) of 9 (8) CRFs in each assessment. Physicians’ median [IQR] anesthesia experience in the first and second assessment was 6 [2–18] years and 6 [2–16] years, respectively. Female physicians accounted for 46% and 45%, respectively. Patient and perioperative characteristics were similar in both assessments (Table 1).

In our multilevel exploratory regression model, the estimated VT·kg−1 IBW was 7.5 mL (95% predictive interval [PI], 5.2 to 9.7) in the first assessment. In the second assessment, VT·kg−1 IBW was 0.1 mL (95% PI, 0.0 to 0.2) lower (ESM eAppendix 6). Female sex (0.5 mL; 95% credible interval [CrI], 0.4 to 0.6), higher body mass index (0.3 mL; 95% CrI, 0.3 to 0.4), PS ventilation modes (0.4 mL; 95% CrI, 0.1 to 0.6), higher driving pressures (0.1 mL; 95% CrI, 0.1 to 0.2), lower EtCO2 levels (−0.2 mL; 95% CrI, −0.3 to −0.2), and a lower respiratory rate (−0.3 mL; 95% CrI, −0.4 to −0.3) were associated with higher VT·kg−1 IBW. In departments that were initially in the highest quintile, VT·kg−1 IBW were 0.6 mL (95% CrI, 0.4 to 0.8) lower in the second assessment (ESM eAppendix 7).

Out of 3,833 general anesthesia cases, 2,360 (62%) received VAs. Among these, 1,927 (82%) had patients that were in the maintenance phase, and these cases were analyzed for the environmental-centred primary outcome FGF (Table 4). Discussion

In this large-scale observational study analyzing intraoperative ventilation data from over 240 hospitals ranging from small to large institutions across Germany, we found that, in the two cross-sectional assessments, mean (SD) VT·kg−1 IBW was 7.4 (1.4) mL vs 7.3 (1.3) mL, and median [IQR] FGF settings in inhalational anesthesia cases during maintenance were 0.60 [0.50–0.85] L·min−1 vs 0.50 [0.45–0.75] L·min−1. These data indicate that overall, most anesthesiologists already followed current recommendations; exploratory multilevel regression analysis did not show an effect of the educational intervention. Nevertheless, our findings indicate improvements in departments with high VT and FGF settings at baseline.

Real-world evidence on intraoperative ventilation settings is limited. In a French study, 18% of 2,960 patients’ lungs were ventilated with high VT > 10 mL·kg−1 IBW.11 In 330,000 patients in a large international database, VT·kg−1 decreased from 8.9 to 8.2 mL from 2008 to 2013. The proportion of patients with lungs ventilated with > 10 mL eventually settled at 14%.10 In our study, most anesthesiologists already applied VT according to lung-protective ventilation strategies before the educational intervention. Only 3–4% of patients’ lungs were ventilated with VT ≥ 10 mL·kg−1 IBW. Similar to what was previously shown, these patients more often were female, were shorter in height, and had abnormal body weight classes.10,11 Interestingly, we found 12.8% of children with lungs ventilated with VT ≥ 10 mL·kg−1 IBW compared with 2.6% of adults. Comparable data are currently lacking.

In our study, ventilation pressures were in the recommended ranges in the first (preintervention) assessment, which might explain the lack of relevant changes in the second. Given the overall low rates of missing data, the large share of implausible data concerning the peak inspiratory and driving pressures is surprising and may reveal the need for further educational initiatives. Nevertheless, information on PEEP levels was available in 98% of analyzed cases and showed that zero PEEP (ZEEP) was only applied in 3% of ventilated patients, which is considerably less than the 70% with ZEEP in a French cohort in 2012.11 This may be explained by growing evidence of benefits of individualized PEEP levels since then.2 In general, ventilation parameters did not significantly differ between the first and second assessment, which may indicate that patient-centred ventilation practices were established in most departments’ clinical routines at baseline. It is worth noting that there may be a potential to reduce FIO2 settings, but as our study did not collect data on peripheral oxygen saturation or partial pressure of oxygen levels, this cannot be evaluated. Importantly, our study did not assess the effect of intraoperative ventilation settings on postoperative patient outcomes. Findings from the large IMPROVE, PROVHILO, iPROVE, PROBESE, and DESIGNATION randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are challenging to merge and to transfer to daily practices owing to differences in the patient population, the interventions, and the control groups.28–32 Choosing a control group that truly reflects common contemporary practice is essential10,33 and is now available with the NO-HARM data set. Recent evidence suggests that further factors such as intraoperative EtCO2 or mechanical power are associated with postoperative outcomes.34–36 In general, intraoperative optimization alone may be insufficient to improve clinical outcomes, and future work should focus on perioperative strategies.32

In contrast to patient-centred ventilation settings, our study revealed an untapped potential when it comes to environmental-centred anesthesia settings. Of course, a median FGF of 0.6 L·min−1 in the overall cohort already in the first assessment is a commendable finding when compared with other larger observational studies.17 Nevertheless, in most cases, FGF could be easily reduced to < 0.5 L·min−1. Our findings suggest that departments with initially high FGFs particularly profited from the simple educational intervention.

In contrast to FGF settings during the maintenance phase, estimated FGFs in the anesthesia induction phase were considerably higher. Even though the pocket card highlighted the VA-saving potential in the wash-in phase, the estimated median [IQR] FGF in this phase was only reduced from 2.00 [1.00–3.00] to 1.50 [1.50–2.85] L·min−1. In the absence of comparable data from other large-scale studies, this underlines the need for more specific guidance in daily routine. So far, only a few studies have investigated the safety of low FGF in the wash-in phase, and more data are needed to formulate standardized algorithms.37,38 Evidence from the USA indicated that, while 25.5% out of more than 12,000,000 outpatient surgeries would have been amenable to peripheral nerve blocks, these were applied in only 3.3% of cases.39 The proportion of procedures feasible with only regional anesthesia in our data set is unknown, but the proportion of 5% in the broad surgical mix suggests that there may be room for a wider use of regional anesthesia. Educational initiatives concerning the expansion of regional anesthesia seem warranted, as the latter provides benefits for patients and the environment.40–44 Encouragingly, the use of desflurane had already been restricted in the studied cohort from the beginning. If departments had stopped buying desflurane after receiving the pocket card, this is unlikely to be reflected in the second assessment, as they may have been using the remaining supplies. Of note, there were only eight cases with nitrous oxide usage in the overall data set, which is considerably less than reported in smaller studies by others.45,46 Implementing sustainable patient-centred anesthesia practices requires both systemic and behavioural changes.47 Low-cost, targeted educational interventions may help increase organizational readiness.48 Recently, a nationwide bottom-up self-regulatory approach to reducing VA usage in favour of total intravenous anesthesia was initiated in the Netherlands. This model included patient safety studies, a national inventory of anesthetic drug usage, the development of a national guideline, and incorporation into control audits.49 While the outcomes are outstanding, future studies should address not only anesthesia staff but also the surgical counterparts and patients to ensure clinical feasibility, acceptability, and long-term sustainability in the OR.47

This study is unique in that it recruited more than 240 anesthesiology departments at all types of German hospitals to participate in a project aiming at assessing and improving anesthesia practices. We show that a nationwide data collection project is possible and can result in a robust data set with negligible missing data. To achieve a comprehensive picture of anesthesia practices across all types of hospitals, we had to choose a pragmatic study design. Despite our precautions, this increases the risk for observation bias. Nevertheless, anesthesiologists reported not applying temperature monitoring in one third of general anesthesia cases, even though this is recommended in German guidelines,50 and 50% did not apply neuromuscular monitoring even though they used neuromuscular blockers in mechanically ventilated patients. This may indicate that standard practices were not purposely changed for data collection.

We acknowledge that the before-and-after comparisons only generate assumptions of a potential relationship between the educational intervention and ventilation settings. An RCT with longer follow-up times would be required to ensure robustness regarding the intervention efficacy and practice changes over a longer term. We may have underestimated the overall effect of the educational intervention as our post hoc analyses indicate that improvements may be more prominent in settings where anesthesiologists are less aware of current recommendations. Nevertheless, these findings should be interpreted with caution, as they are prone to regression to the mean. Another limitation is that we initially did not consider the usage of closed-loop systems. In the postintervention assessment, we found that these were used in 54 (3%) of inhalational anesthesia cases during the maintenance phase, with a median [IQR] FGF of 0.40 [0.21–0.63] L·min−1 (n = 43). Their potential benefit should be further investigated. We cannot exclude a selection bias, as departments that previously prioritized up-to-date practices were naturally more inclined to participate in NO-HARM, which may also be reflected in the large share of teaching hospitals in the data set. Our results may therefore not be generalizable to nonteaching hospitals. Despite a participation rate below the estimated 30%, we achieved a sample size close to the estimated target. Over 10,000 anesthesiologists and residents worked at the respective departments, showing that we captured a relevant snapshot of standard practices across Germany. The anonymous and restricted data collection impeded collection of detailed patient data and query management. Simplification of data protection and ethical approval processes is needed for future large-scale digitized research in Germany.

Overall, our data provide a robust snapshot of real-world anesthesia practices in Germany, but further research is necessary on the European level.

Conclusions

This large-scale national observational study captured data on anesthesia practices in daily routine from over 240 German anesthesiology departments representing hospitals of varying sizes in two cross-sectional assessments. The majority of patients’ lungs were ventilated with adequate VT·kg−1 IBW and low FGF settings at baseline before our educational intervention. In departments with higher VT and FGF settings at baseline, our approach might help realize untapped potential. Our data suggest that future initiatives should focus on further implementation of minimal and metabolic FGF settings, exploring lower FGFs in the induction phase and leveraging the usage of regional anesthesia. More research on the association of intraoperative ventilation settings and patient outcomes as well as sustainable anesthesia practices are recommended and should be facilitated by regulatory authorities.
