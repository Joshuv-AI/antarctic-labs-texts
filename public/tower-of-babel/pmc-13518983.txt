Mapping the role of artificial intelligence in health-related stigma: a scoping review
Authors: Tianqi Song, Jack Jamieson, Wataru Akahori, Han Meng, Shuqi Wang, Yi-Chieh Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStigma remains a major barrier to equitable health and well-being, while artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly recognized as a tool with both potential and risk in addressing this challenge. However, research on AI and stigma is fragmented across disciplines, hindering a unified understanding of their intersection. To consolidate existing evidence, we conducted a scoping review of 11,769 records published between 2016 and 2025 and identified 70 studies examining the relationship between AI and health-related stigma. Four research themes emerged: AI measuring stigma (n = 42, 60%), stigma influencing AI use (n = 15, 21%), AI increasing stigma (n = 9, 13%), and AI reducing stigma (n = 4, 6%). Most studies focused on mental health disorders, revealing an imbalance in attention to other health conditions. Across studies, we observed inconsistent definitions of stigma, limited cross-cultural perspectives, and few evaluations of real-world AI applications. Addressing these gaps will be critical for developing responsible and equitable AI systems that mitigate rather than reinforce health stigma across broader societal and health contexts.Subject terms: Diseases, Health care, Psychology, Psychology, Scientific community

Introduction

Health-related stigma, a social process marked by the exclusion and devaluation of individuals or groups associated with particular health conditions1, remains a pervasive barrier to equitable health. Stigma discourages help-seeking2,3, reinforces structural inequities4, and limits access to social participation and opportunity5. Reducing stigma has therefore become a central objective in global health policy and clinical practice. Alongside these efforts, digital technologies are increasingly used to support prevention, treatment, and health communication, raising new questions about how technology can both reproduce and mitigate stigma6–11.

AI has further transformed this landscape. Machine-learning and natural-language models have been applied to detect stigmatizing language12 and to design automated interventions through conversational agents13,14. Yet the same technologies can unintentionally amplify bias and discrimination15–17. With the rise of large language models, new ethical concerns have emerged about misrepresentation, data misuse, and loss of trust among populations already vulnerable to stigma18–22. The dual capacity of AI to either reduce or reinforce social exclusion underscores the need to examine how stigma manifests within the design, deployment, and perception of AI systems in health contexts23.

Despite the importance, little is known about the broader intersections between AI and health-related stigma. In particular, there is limited understanding of how stigma is measured, reinforced, mitigated, or otherwise shaped through AI technologies, and conversely, how existing forms of health-related stigma influence the design, deployment, and adoption of AI systems in health contexts. This bidirectional dynamic is of particular concern because existing biases can be amplified and reintroduced into society through AI systems, thereby reinforcing health-related stigma at scale. Previous reviews have concentrated on stigma measurement or intervention within specific domains such as mental health or HIV24–28, leaving AI-centric mechanisms largely unexplored.

To fill this gap, this scoping review systematically maps research at the intersection of AI and health-related stigma. We sought to clarify the roles AI plays in relation to health-related stigma, whether as a tool for measurement, an intervention mechanism, a potential source of stigma exacerbation, or a system shaped by stigmatizing structures. We further characterized the technological and contextual features of this literature, including AI system types, health domains, stigma forms, and study designs. Finally, we synthesized evaluation approaches and empirical findings to identify methodological patterns, conceptual gaps, and priorities for advancing inclusive and socially responsible AI in health contexts. Importantly, this review examines health-related stigma across diverse settings, including public discourse and social media, AI system design and evaluation, and clinical encounters, rather than focusing exclusively on stigma within healthcare systems. We adopt this broad scope because AI plays distinct roles across these contexts, from measuring stigma in online platforms to potentially amplifying bias through algorithmic systems, and a comprehensive mapping of the field requires examining these varied settings together.

Following PRISMA guidelines29, 70 studies published between 2016 and 2025 were identified across medicine, computer science, psychology, and the social sciences.

The synthesis characterizes how AI is used to measure and reduce stigma, as well as how it can increase stigma and how stigma influences AI adoption. By mapping these patterns across disciplines, the review identifies key gaps and highlights opportunities for developing more inclusive and responsible AI in health contexts.

Results

Study selection

A total of 27,552 records were identified across ten databases. After removing duplicates, 11,769 records were screened based on title and abstract. Of these, 519 reports were sought for retrieval; 20 could not be retrieved, leaving 499 reports assessed for eligibility through full-text review. Following full-text screening, 442 reports were excluded. In addition, 13 studies were identified through reference searches and screened independently. In total, these processes resulted in a final sample of 70 included studies (Fig. 1). The included publications spanned multiple disciplines, including medicine, computer science, psychology, and the social sciences.

Study characteristics

Publications ranged from 2016 to 2025, with a general upward trend peaking in 2022–2023 before declining in the most recent years (Fig. 2a); the lower count for 2025 may be partially attributable to the search cut-off date (September 2025). Early publications were predominantly focused on AI measuring stigma, particularly through NLP-based analyses of social media. Studies on stigma influencing AI use and AI increasing stigma began appearing from around 2019 onward, while AI reducing stigma emerged only from 2020, reflecting the growing scope and maturity of the field.

Natural language processing (NLP) and machine learning (ML) were the most frequently used AI approaches (n = 40 and n = 37 of studies, respectively), primarily applied to text classification, sentiment detection, and prediction of stigma-related outcomes. Around one-quarter of the studies (n = 20) examined AI-based services such as chatbots, virtual agents, or diagnostic tools, while a smaller subset focused directly on evaluating language models or generative systems (n = 6).

Health contexts were dominated by mental health-related stigma (n = 53), followed by infectious diseases (n = 9), metabolic disorders (n = 7), and neurological conditions (n = 6). Stigma types were most often categorized as public stigma (n = 29) or self-stigma (n = 14), though over one-quarter of studies (n = 22) did not specify the target stigma. Supplementary Section 3 provides a consolidated reference of stigma scales and codebooks used across the reviewed studies, which may serve as a practical guide for researchers selecting appropriate measurement instruments in future work.

AI measuring stigma

Figure 4a illustrates two main types of research questions (RQs) in applying artificial intelligence to the study of health-related stigma. The first uses AI to detect and characterize stigma in public discourse (Fig. 4a, left). Early applications automated the identification and quantification of stigmatizing content, enabling large-scale analyses of social media data that were previously infeasible with manual methods34,95–97. Figure 4b shows the typical workflow, where natural language processing and machine-learning methods were used to analyze large-scale digital corpora for stigmatizing language. In the data collection stage, most studies gathered stigma-related text from large-scale digital corpora. The most commonly used data source was Twitter (now rebranded as ‘X’; we retain the term ‘Twitter’ to align with the terminology in cited research) (n = 12)31,32,38,44,46,50,52,55–57,60,63, followed by Reddit (n = 7)30,36,40,49,59,65,67, Weibo (n = 3)34,43,48, and Facebook (n = 2) 39,42, to detect and quantify stigmatizing language. During data pre-processing, text was prepared for model input through content analysis43, sentiment analysis31,40,44,45,54,60, and data annotation31,34,35,39,42,45. In this context, sentiment analysis refers to extracting sentiment scores as features for subsequent model training, rather than as a research outcome in itself. Annotation approaches varied: some studies derived coding schemes from established psychological scales31,34,35, whereas others employed crowd-sourced labeling to construct training data39,42,45. In the model training stage, three types of algorithms were commonly employed: clustering or topic modeling to identify dominant themes, classification to categorize text as stigmatizing or non-stigmatizing, and regression to identify linguistic or demographic features most associated with stigma attitudes. Finally, in data analysis, studies evaluated AI model performance using metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score34,43,45,51,52,55,62, and characterized stigma through linguistic34,36,43, sentiment45,56,60, and demographic analyses35,59. Here, sentiment analysis served as an outcome evaluation, examining sentiment patterns to characterize the nature of stigmatizing language.

The reviewed studies primarily examined the prevalence and characteristics of stigma within digital corpora. Reported prevalence varied widely, from less than 1%46,49,65 to over 40%55,60, depending largely on the health condition analyzed. Stigma related to obesity was typically rare (about 1–2% of posts in relevant datasets), whereas schizophrenia-related content showed much higher rates, reaching up to 47%46,49,55. Linguistic and sentiment analyses indicated that stigmatizing posts often contained exclusionary or negative expressions, while demographic analyses revealed systematic variation across gender, race, and lived experience with illness35,54,59.

Stigma influencing AI use

Perceptions of stigma substantially shape how individuals engage with AI-based health tools, including chatbots, diagnostic systems, and digital therapy applications (Fig. 5a). These findings are largely derived from quantitative approaches, including online surveys18,19,78,82 and controlled user experiments72,75,81,83.

Key research questions and findings in Stigma Influencing AI Use studies.

Panel (a) shows the key research questions, and panel (b) summarizes the findings. Solid borders denote factors directly related to stigma (e.g., absence of human judgment, data-driven bias), whereas dashed borders represent general facilitators of AI adoption not specific to stigma (e.g., perceived usefulness, remote access).

Across interview and survey studies, two contrasting patterns emerged (Fig. 5b, left). For some users, the perceived anonymity of AI offered a “stigma-free” environment that encouraged disclosure and use22,74. Others, however, hesitated to engage with such tools due to concerns that AI might reproduce or amplify stigma. Reported barriers included fears of biased algorithms and data misuse19,21, structural inequities that could disadvantage minority groups, and inadequate privacy protections20,73.

Quantitative studies further provide mixed evidence on whether stigma influences the adoption of AI-based counseling tools (Fig. 5b). Two survey studies produced divergent results: one reported no significant association between self- or social stigma and AI chatbot adoption78, whereas another found a weak positive correlation between stigma and favorable attitudes toward AI therapists18. Experimental work compared preferences for AI versus human clinicians and found that participants generally favored human counselors72,75. However, willingness to use AI tools increased when the health condition was stigmatized or personally sensitive.

AI increasing stigma

A growing number of studies have identified ways in which AI systems may inadvertently amplify health-related stigma (Fig. 6a). These investigations examined whether AI technologies themselves, or individuals who use AI-assisted applications, could reinforce stigma toward certain health groups.

Figure 6b summarizes the main findings. Bias evaluations of language models showed that prompts referencing stigmatized conditions such as “mental illness” or “disability” produced more negative or fearful responses than neutral terms84–86. Embedding analyses found that vector representations of stigmatized concepts clustered in negative semantic spaces87, and image-generation models reproduced reductive or harmful visual stereotypes of illness88,89.

In an experimental vignette study, healthcare professionals exposed to machine learning-based predictive assessments demonstrated shifts in stigma-related judgments, reporting greater fear and lower anger toward patients, along with reduced attributions of personal responsibility, compared to psychosocial assessments91.

AI reducing stigma

Lastly, a small but emerging body of research has explored whether AI can be used to actively reduce stigma through interactive interventions (Fig. 7). These studies employed conversational agents that engaged users in dialogues about mental health, leading to measurable reductions in stigmatizing attitudes. The interaction drew on contact theory, which proposes that structured interaction with members of a stigmatized group can reduce prejudice and improve attitudes99, and took three forms: vignette-sharing, where the agent narrated first-person experiences of living with a health condition14; question-and-answering, where participants engaged in open dialogue with the agent14,92; and cooperation tasks, where participants engaged in a cooperative task with the AI agent (e.g., cooperative learning)93.

The effects were most pronounced when agents shared first-person narratives, modeled non-stigmatizing perspectives14,92,93. Extending beyond the general population, recent work has adapted conversational agents as interactive educational tools to reduce stigma among healthcare providers94. While encouraging, this evidence remains preliminary and is largely derived from small-scale experimental studies.

Discussion

This scoping review highlights a growing but uneven body of research examining the intersection between AI and health-related stigma. While interest in this topic has increased rapidly over the past decade, the field remains dominated by studies that use AI to identify and measure stigma rather than to understand or intervene in its social dynamics. Consequently, AI currently functions more as an analytic tool than as an active means to reduce stigma and promote equity in diverse health contexts.

A notable finding is the disproportionate focus on mental health conditions, which account for nearly three-quarters of the reviewed studies. This concentration reflects both the high global prevalence of mental disorders100 and the extensive research attention they have attracted, but constrains the generalizability of existing knowledge to other health domains. Other conditions long recognized in stigma research, such as leprosy101,102, epilepsy103,104, and chronic physical illness, are rarely represented. Differences in the linguistic and affective framing of stigma across diseases suggest that each health condition carries distinct social meanings and communicative patterns. Expanding the evidence base beyond mental disorders would clarify how AI can be adapted to address diverse manifestations of stigma and reveal condition-specific design considerations for equitable health technologies.

Another major gap concerns the scope of AI modalities used to study or assess stigma. Nearly all included studies rely on natural-language data, whereas multimodal approaches that incorporate speech76, image88,89, or video analysis are almost absent. This text-centric focus partly reflects the maturity of available technologies: NLP methods were well-established earlier in the review period, whereas high-quality multimodal models capable of reliably analyzing speech, images, and video have only become widely accessible more recently. Nevertheless, this imbalance is critical because stigma often operates through visual and auditory cues (e.g., facial expressions, bodily appearance, or tone of voice) that fall outside text analysis. Early investigations of image-generation and speech-recognition models demonstrate that these systems can encode harmful stereotypes76,88,89, underscoring the urgency of expanding bias evaluation to multimodal models105. A comprehensive understanding of AI-related stigma will require methods capable of capturing its multimodal nature.

Conceptual ambiguity further limits progress. Definitions and labeling criteria for “stigma” vary widely across studies, with many datasets adopting binary classifications or unreported annotation procedures. The absence of standardized frameworks weakens reproducibility and obscures comparisons across algorithms and health domains. Clear reporting of labeling criteria, transparency about annotation processes, and engagement with people who have lived experience of stigmatized conditions are needed to ensure conceptual validity and ethical sensitivity59,106,107. Building consensus around theory-driven definitions of stigma69,70 would also support more consistent evaluation of how AI influences stigmatizing mechanisms.

Evidence of real-world impact remains scarce. Most investigations evaluate model performance or short-term laboratory interventions rather than long-term outcomes in clinical or community settings. Consequently, little is known about how AI applications affect perceptions of stigma among patients, professionals, or the public. Integrating quantitative bias auditing with qualitative and field-based methods could illuminate how algorithmic design choices translate into social consequences. Longitudinal and task-specific evaluations will be essential to determine whether AI can genuinely alleviate, rather than reproduce, inequities in health communication and care delivery.

Cultural and demographic representation also remains limited. Stigma is inherently shaped by social and cultural context108,109, yet most existing studies rely on homogeneous samples and Western-centric data sources. Very few adopt cross-cultural comparisons or include participants from minoritized groups. Developing culturally representative datasets, recruiting diverse populations, and designing cross-cultural annotation protocols could improve the cultural validity of AI systems and reduce risks of reproducing global bias110,111. Moreover, as existing stigma interventions often adopt a one-size-fits-all approach14,92–94, future work could leverage AI’s capacity for personalization to tailor interventions to different cultural and social contexts.

This review has its limitations. First, as is typical with scoping reviews, this study does not critically address specific research questions related to health stigma and AI112. Instead, it provides a broad overview of ongoing work and future opportunities. Compared to systematic reviews or meta-analyses on mental health stigma and technology interventions27, this review may lack the detailed evaluation and depth typically found in such approaches. Future work could address this by focusing on specific aspects of AI or health-related stigma, conducting systematic reviews such as27,113,114 to analyze targeted research questions, evaluate the quality of studies, and synthesize findings that can directly inform practice and policy. Second, while our search strategy covered multiple databases and spanned cross-disciplinary research, we excluded gray literature, such as non-peer-reviewed works. This decision was made to maintain consistent inclusion criteria across disciplines and align with prior scoping review methods115–117. We acknowledge that this approach inherently omits potentially relevant insights from gray literature98,118. Future research could address this limitation by expanding the scope to include gray literature, which may offer additional insights and enrich the findings. Third, the search keywords were primarily derived from previous reviews on AI in health119–121 and health-related stigma24–27. The two-tier structure, which combined broad health descriptors with condition-specific terms, was designed to ensure that less-represented conditions remained eligible for capture, and the observed predominance of mental health studies likely reflects the current focal points of the field rather than a search artifact alone. Nevertheless, the reliance on conditions prominent in existing reviews may introduce selection bias, particularly the predominance of mental health studies in our sample. Future work could adopt a more systematic keyword selection framework, for example by drawing on established disease burden indicators such as Disability-Adjusted Life Years (DALYs; 122), to ensure broader coverage of underrepresented yet heavily stigmatized conditions.

This review indicates that advancing the study of AI and health-related stigma will require closer integration of computational, clinical, and social perspectives. Moving beyond text-based analytics toward multimodal, cross-cultural, and patient-centered approaches will be essential for designing equitable and trustworthy AI systems. By bridging analytic insight with lived experience, future work can transform AI from a passive observer of social bias into a proactive instrument for promoting inclusion and dignity in relation to health.

Methods

This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines29 and followed the methodological framework123. The process involved four stages: literature identification, eligibility screening, inclusion, and synthesis. A formal review protocol was not preregistered due to the exploratory nature of this scoping review. As this review analyzed existing published literature and did not involve primary data collection from human participants or animals, ethics approval and informed consent were not required.

Search strategy

To capture the intersection of AI, health, and stigma, we constructed a search strategy spanning these three domains. The initial keyword set was derived from prior reviews on AI in health119–121 and on health-related stigma24–27. We then iteratively refined the terms through pilot searches to achieve an appropriate balance between recall and precision:

AI-related terms ranged from broad descriptors (e.g., “artificial intelligence”, “machine learning”, “deep learning”) to specific systems and applications (e.g., “robot”, “chatbot”, “decision support”), reflecting the substantial terminological variation observed in prior reviews119–121.

AI-related terms ranged from broad descriptors (e.g., “artificial intelligence”, “machine learning”, “deep learning”) to specific systems and applications (e.g., “robot”, “chatbot”, “decision support”), reflecting the substantial terminological variation observed in prior reviews119–121.

Health-related terms were structured across two tiers: broad descriptors (e.g., “health”, “disease”, “patient”) designed to capture studies involving conditions not explicitly enumerated, and condition-specific terms representing health conditions frequently associated with stigma in prior syntheses (e.g., “HIV”, “depression”, “substance use”)24–26. This two-tier design ensured that studies on less-represented conditions remained eligible for inclusion through the broad descriptors, even when not explicitly named in the condition-specific tier. Broader categorical terms (e.g., “mental illness,” “mental disorders”) were initially tested during pilot searches. However, when combined with AI- and stigma-related keywords, these terms produced a high volume of irrelevant records, substantially reducing search precision. Ten databases covering computing, health, and social sciences were searched: ACM Digital Library, IEEE Xplore, PubMed, PsycInfo, Cochrane, EMBASE, CINAHL, Scopus, ScienceDirect, and Web of Science. Queries were applied to the title, abstract, and keyword fields; equivalent search fields were used for databases lacking these options. The complete database-specific queries are detailed in Supplementary Section 2. Searches included records published from 2012 onward, corresponding to the period following the introduction of deep learning, which marked a major inflection point in modern AI development124. All searches were completed in September 2025. After de-duplication, 11,769 unique records were identified for screening.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they (i) discussed or implemented an AI algorithm or application, (ii) addressed a health-related condition or context, and (iii) explicitly examined stigma or related constructs such as stereotype, discrimination, or prejudice. These criteria were not restricted to studies situated within healthcare systems; studies examining health-related stigma in public discourse, AI system evaluation, or technology adoption contexts were equally eligible.

Papers were excluded if they were not peer-reviewed, not written in English, or were unrelated to health. Literature reviews, opinion pieces, and conference abstracts were also excluded. We removed studies focusing exclusively on COVID-19-related stigma because specialized reviews in that area already exist125,126. However, papers that examined other forms of health stigma during the pandemic were retained. A full description of the inclusion and exclusion criteria is provided in Supplementary Section 4.

Two authors independently screened all titles and abstracts, followed by full-text reviews for the remaining records, using the PRISMA flow shown in Fig. 1. Discrepancies were resolved by discussion until consensus was reached. Of the 519 reports sought for retrieval from database searches, 20 were not retrieved, leaving 499 assessed for full-text eligibility; 442 were excluded, yielding 57 studies from database searching.

Reference lists of included studies were examined to identify additional papers not captured by the database searches, yielding 13 further records, which were screened independently following the same process. The final sample comprised 70 studies.

Data extraction and synthesis

Each included study was charted using a standardized data-extraction template capturing publication year, country, study design, AI technique, data source, health domain, stigma type, and principal findings. Two reviewers jointly coded a subset of studies (30%) to calibrate the coding framework, after which the remaining papers were coded independently with periodic cross-checks to ensure consistency. Coding focused on four analytic dimensions: health-related, AI-related, stigma-related, and study-related characteristics. The study-related characteristics captured each study’s research questions (RQs), methods, measures, and key findings, with particular attention to what is currently understood, and what remains unclear about stigma in each context.

Quantitative summaries were generated to describe publication trends, disciplinary distributions, and AI methods. Health conditions were categorized using the World Health Organization’s International Classification of Diseases, 11th Revision (ICD-11), and AI technologies were mapped according to the European Commission AI taxonomy127. In addition, we categorized the reviewed studies by disciplinary publication venue. Categories were assigned based on Scopus’s All Science Journal Classification codes (https://www.elsevier.com/products/scopus/content), mapped to broader groupings following the Standard Research Classification (https://aria.stats.govt.nz/aria/#ClassificationSearch:q=ANZSRC). Because venues may be associated with multiple subject codes, we adopted a multi-label classification approach; that is, a single study may be assigned to more than one discipline. For example, the Journal of Affective Disorders34 is coded as “2700 Medicine” and “3200 Psychology” in Scopus, and therefore maps to both “Medical and Health Sciences” and “Psychology and Cognitive Sciences.” In contrast, the Proceedings of the AAAI Conference on Artificial Intelligence86 is coded only as “1700 Computer Science,” mapping solely to “Information and Computing Sciences.” For venues not indexed in Scopus, two authors independently assigned discipline codes based on the venue’s stated scope.

Findings were synthesized narratively to identify major research patterns and conceptual gaps. Specifically, studies were grouped into four thematic areas-AI measuring stigma, stigma influencing AI use, AI increasing stigma, and AI reducing stigma-which together illustrate the spectrum of how AI interacts with health-related stigma. Quantitative distributions and representative examples are summarized in Section “Results” and corresponding figures.
