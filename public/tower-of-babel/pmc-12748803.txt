Antioxidant and anticancer potentiality of F13 fraction of Pleurotus sajar caju against cancer cell lines and in silico analysis
Authors: Koushik Pandey, Madhuparna Ghosh, Swapan Kumar Ghosh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Novel drugs, isolated from natural compounds, are currently being tested and sought out, and here, Pleurotus sajar caju was considered as a source for potentially novel drugs. The current study focuses on identifying the bioactive compounds of partially purified fractions (F1–F20) from PSME (Pleurotus sajar caju methanolic extract) in order to demonstrate the antioxidant activity and anticancer activity of F13 on cervical, lung, and breast cancer cell lines with mechanisms and also in silico study of selected compounds. The results of the qualitative analysis of PSME exhibited that phenol, flavonoid, carbohydrate, and alkaloid contents were in high (+++) amounts. FT-IR analysis of the extract showed several functional groups, like O–H stretching for carboxylic acids, C–H stretching for alkanes and alkyl groups, etc. The PSME underwent column chromatography for fractionation and partial purification, obtaining 20 fractions (F1–F20). Fractions were tested for antioxidant content and activity, and network analysis of the co-occurrence pattern within the antioxidant content and activity of different fractions of PSME was done by R software, and F13 was judged as the best fraction. The F13 had the highest radical scavenging activity with EC50 values of 21.65 ± 0.81 µg.mL−1 by DPPH (1,1 diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl), and it also had maximum scavenging activity among other fractions by the FRAP (ferric reducing antioxidant power) method. Total phenolic content, flavonoid content, and ascorbic acid content of F13 were maximum among other fractions. TLC analysis of the F13 fraction revealed six distinct spots with Rf values (0.36, 0.52, 0.65, 0.74, 0.36, and 0.87), but three spots matched perfectly with the Rf values of the standards of quinine (0.37), quercetin (0.74), and p-coumaric acid (0.87). The cytotoxicity effect of F13 from PSME at the highest concentration of 1500 µg.mL−1 on the HeLa cell line was 90.66 ± 3.05% cell growth inhibition, compared to other cell lines at 24 h. The F13 induced apoptosis and LDH leakage. It upregulated gene expression of Caspase 3 & 9 and P53 and downregulated BcL2 genes of all cell lines, as proved by our Western blotting experiment. In silico analysis exhibited that quercetin and p-coumaric acid strongly bound with receptors BcL2 and Caspase 3, inhibiting BcL2 function and enhancing Caspase 3 function. In conclusion, the present study has demonstrated that F13, having six bioactive compounds, was a highly potent antioxidant and anti-breast, anti-lung, and anti-cervical cancer agent. After in vivo and clinical trials in humans, it would be an important product for next-generation drug preparation for cancer management.

Introduction

Beyond the health benefits of items originating from plants or animals, mushrooms have been acknowledged as a valuable nutritional source and functional food1–4. Mushrooms have been recognized as a "third food kingdom" by scientists, who are exploring their potential as “functional foods”5–7. To investigate the antioxidant and other bioactive compounds in mushrooms, scientists apply sophisticated methods such as UV–Vis spectrophotoscopy, HPTLC (high-performance thin-layer chromatography), HPLC (high-performance liquid chromatography), column chromatography, FT-IR (Fourier transform infrared), GC–MS (gas chromatography mass spectra), LC–MS (liquid chromatography mass spectra), ID/2D/3D NMR (nuclear magnetic resonance), flash chromatography, and others. Nearly 2–11 million fungal species on Earth have been estimated8,9,10,11, including 150,600 described fungal species12,13, and 14,000–16,000 mushroom species14–17.

Furthermore, it has been revealed that 2000 species are utilized medicinally17,18. As a result, mushrooms have been investigated as a possible source for the creation of medications to treat our numerous diseases17,19–22,23]. Pleurotus sajar caju belongs to the Pleurotaceae family, the Agaricales order, and the Basidiomycota phylum, and this mushroom has been taken in this study as a sample for a source of antioxidant and anticancer agents because of the following points: i. It is among the edible mushrooms that are most commonly grown. ii. It is the third most cultivated mushroom worldwide, and its cultivation and popularity have increased dramatically. iii. It has a high concentration of bioactive metabolites, which are the most significant unexplored source of potent and innovative pharmaceutical products. iv. It has nutraceutical and culinary qualities24,25, v. It possesses antiviral, anti-thrombotic, anti-hypertensive, anti-cholesterol, anti-atherosclerosis, and immunomodulatory qualities26–28, and vi. Despite this fact, very few studies of P. sajar caju on its anticancer activity in cervical, lung, and breast cancer cell lines have been conducted. According to Ren29 polysaccharide extracts of Pleurotus eryngii, P. austral, and 14 other mushroom species exhibited direct anticancer activity against the C32 and WM-266–4 cell lines. On the other hand, the cancer disease is knocking on every door, and even at least one family member is being affected by this disease. As per WHO (2020), cancer is the main cause of human death globally. The American Cancer Society (ACS) released Global Cancer Statistics, 2024, and reported30 that the worldwide cancer occurrence in 2022 was near about 20 million cancer cases, and 9.7 million cancer death cases had been reported due to this disease, and scientists assessed that by 2050, 35 million people will be affected by this disease in 185 countries31. Current Global Cancer Statistics (2025 Update) exhibited that an estimated 10 million people die due to cancer each year. Globally in 2020, the position of lung cancer was 1st, followed by breast cancer, while cervical cancer was in 8th position32–34 but Cancer Statistics (2025 Update) showed the same thing. Approximately 40% of cancer occurred due to improper diet, obesity, alcohol consumption, and smoking (https://soulivity.com/health/world-cancer-day-2025-advancing-the-fight-against-cancer). Furthermore, epidemiology showed us that the highest percentage (49.2%) of cancer incidence was in Asian countries32,33. For the treatment of cancer, single or combinations of some treatments such as chemotherapy, operative, radiotherapy, immunotherapy, and photodynamic therapy have been used for long time, but they are causing a variety of side effects and complications in cancer patients, highlighting the critical need for novel, effective, and less toxic therapeutic approaches1. Specifically, Pleurotus sajar caju (PSC) presenting anticancer effect against colorectal cancer (CRC) cell lines like HCT11634, S-180 and Hep-235. Regarding mycochemistry of this mushroom, some molecules (polysaccharides, proteins, quinones etc.) in fruitbody have been reported36. The important thing to explore how compounds of Pluerotus sajar caju (PSC) induce apoptosis, for inhibition of growth of a panel of cancer cell line like MCF 7, HeLa, A549. The effect of this mushroom or its extract or fraction as a broad spectrum anticancer was not found by any other scientists. Therefore, we have tried to full fill this knowledge gap. This study investigated the potential of the cultivated mushroom Pleurotus sojar caju for its bioactive compounds, antioxidant activity, and anticancer properties against HeLa (cervical cancer), MCF7 (breast estrogen-positive cancer), and A549 (non-small cell lung cancer) cell lines, and further investigated the in-silico study to evaluate the binding affinity, energy, and stability of F13 product compounds as ligands, with the focus on their interaction with the BcL-2 and Caspase-3 protein receptors, given their critical roles in apoptosis.

Materials and methods

Mushroom collection and identification

The cultivated Pleurotus sajar caju basidiocarps were collected from the Suvodra farm mushroom cultivation unit in Halisahar, District 24-Parganas (S), and conveyed to the laboratory in a biodegradable polythene bag. All morphological features, such as colour and shape, were recorded, and anatomical features were noted and identified utilizing published keys37,38 (www.mycobank.org). A voucher specimen of this mushroom was preserved in the Mycopathology Herbarium, Ramakrishna Mission Vivekananda Centenary College (Autonomous), Kolkata-700118.

Solvent extraction

The mushroom fruit bodies of various ages were thoroughly washed with tap water before being air-dried in a 45 °C oven for 48 h. The dried fruit bodies were cut into pieces and ground into powder using a mixer grinder.

Dip method

100 g of dried mushroom powder was weighed, then dipped in the appropriate ratio (1:10) of methanol and kept under stirring conditions at 45 °C in an incubator for 3 days. As methanol is a polar solvent, it extracts the majority of bioactive compounds, and it is also less costly. This is because the methanol was used as a solvent for extraction. Then, the mixture was passed through Whatman No. 4 and Whatman No. 1 filter papers serially, and the filtrate was collected in a beaker. The solvent from the filtrate or extract was removed using a rotary vacuum evaporator at 40 °C, and the remaining solvent was removed using a lyophilizer. The resulting powder, known as PSME (Pleurotus sajar caju methanolic extract), was stored in an airtight container at 4 °C in a refrigerator.

Mycochemical screening or qualitative test of PSME

In this current study, myco-chemical screening was used to identify the myco-compounds of PSME sample.

Alkaloid test

0.2 g of powder of PSME was taken in a test tube, and a drop of 2% sulfuric acid was added to it, and the test tube was slightly heated for 2 min. It was then dribbled into a different test tube and mixed with 2–3 drops of the Dragendroffs reagent (a mixture of the two solutions: 0.5 g of bismuth nitrate with 10 mL of HCl and 4 g of KI with 5 mL of distilled water). An orange-red precipitation formation indicated the presence of alkaloid39.

Steroid test

In a test tube, 0.2 g of PSME powder was taken, and it was treated with 2–3 drops of acetic anhydride. Concentrated H2SO4 was poured to the test tube through the inner wall drop by drop. The appearance of a brown ring on test tube indicated the presence of steroids40.

Terpenoid test

0.2 g of powder of PSME was taken in a test tube, and then 2 mL chloroform and 3 mL of concentrated sulphuric acid (90%) were poured in the test tube and mixed nicely41. Then, a reddish-brown colour was generated in the mixture that indicated the existence of terpenoid.

Flavonoid test

In a test tube, 0.2 g of powder of PSME was taken, and it was dissolved by adding 4 mL of distilled water. Afterward, diluted sodium hydroxide (1 M solution) was poured into the test tube. It yielded a yellow solution. Then a few drops of dilute acid (10% hydrochloric acid) were added to this test tube; as a result, the yellow color disappeared, that indicated the existence of flavonoid42.

Saponins test

0.2 g of powder of PSME was taken in a test tube, and 2 mL of distilled water was added to it, and the test tube was shaken vigorously for about 5 min. Afterward, the test tube was kept for 30 min. Then, the appearance of honeycomb froth, indicated the existence of saponin39.

Tannin test

In a test tube, 2 mL of distilled water was taken, and 0.2 g of powder of PSME was added to it, and the powder was dissolved nicely, and then the mixture was warmed to 50 °C in a water bath. After warming the mixture, 2 drops of 1% FeCl3 were added to the mixture, then a dark green solution appeared, and it indicated the existence of tannin43,44.

Phenol test

0.2 g of powder of PSME was taken in a test tube, and 2 mL of distilled water was added to it, and the powder was dissolved. Then, 2 drops of 5% FeCl3 solution (neutral) were poured in the test tube. A deep blue color appeared, and it indicated the existence of phenol41. Here, a neutral FeCl3 solution was freshly prepared by pouring diluted solution of sodium hydroxide to a ferric chloride solution drop by drop until a small permanent brown precipitation was seen. Then, the solution was filtered, and clear filtrate was collected and used for the phenol test.

Phlobatannin test

0.2 g of powder of PSME was dissolved in 2 mL of water in a test tube. Then, 3 mL of dilute HCl (10%) were added to this solution. A red precipitate appeared, and it signaled the existence of phlobatannins39.

Fourier transform infrared spectrophotometer (FTIR) analysis of PSME

This analysis is done to determine the types of functional groups of compounds. The wavelength of light absorbed is characteristic of the chemical bond, as shown in the annotated spectrum. 10 mg of powder of PSME were mixed in 100 mg of KBr Pellet, and it was subjected to an FT-IR spectroscope with a scan range of 400 to 4000 cm-1 45. A chromatogram was generated, and each peak was identified with the help of web database.

Fractionation of PSME extract by column chromatography

Fractionation or partial purification of PSME was carried out by column chromatography46. A glass column was washed with hexane, a cotton layer was fitted at the bottom. This column was packed with 1:7 silica gel (60–120 mesh) for complete separation, and the column was charged with solvents. The solvent was eluted all the way to the column bed, and the dried extract was loaded into the column. Afterward, a layer of cotton was kept over the charged matter for its protection. The charged column was kept for 4 h to ascertain the complete saturation and removal of air bubble from column, yielding a static bed.

The charged column was eluted with several mobile phases (gradient elution technique) in the following ratio of hexane and methanol: 9:1, 8:2, 7:3, 6:4, 5:5, and 0:10. A total of 20 fractions were collected in separate 20 (5 mL capacity) glass tubes by an automatic fraction collector and each fraction (2 mL) was concentrated separately with the help of a vacuum rotary evaporator (Superfit, Model: PBV-7D) and lyophilized, and the preliminary antioxidant activity was determined using the antioxidant content and activity analysis.

Antioxidant content of different fractions of PSME

Total phenol content (TPC) assay

Folin–Ciocalteu’s reagent protocol was applied to estimate the TPC of each fraction powder (F1-F20)47. 0.1 mL Folin–Ciocalteu’s reagent (0.5 N) was taken in a clean test tube, and 0.5 mL (1 mg.mL-1) of fraction solution was added to it and mixed nicely, and kept at room temperature (RT) (30 °C) for 15 min. Afterward, the mixture was added to 2.5 mL of saturated sodium carbonate solution and kept at RT for 30 min. The OD (optical density) was taken at 760 nm wave length by a UV–Vis spectrophotometer. The result was denoted as mg of Gallic acid equivalent (GAEs) per g of fraction powder of PSME and data presented as the average of three replicates with standard deviation (SD).

Total flavonoid (TFC) content assay

A gold standard protocol48 was used to calculate TFC of different fractions (F1-F20). In a test tube, equal parts 2% AlCl3 (made in absolute methanol) and sample of fraction powder (1 mg.mL-1) were taken, mixed finely and kept for 10 min at RT. The absorbance at 415 nm was measured after 10 min by UV–Vis spectrophotometer against a blank/ control (Extraction solvent). Finally, the results were expressed as milli-gram (mg) of Quercetin equivalents (QEs) per g of fraction powder. and was represented as the mean of three replicates with SD.

Total ascorbic acid content (TAAC) assay

The TAAC was measured by the Folin Ciocalteu reagent protocol described by Jagota and Dani49. 0.5 mL of the sample solution (fraction) (mg.mL-1) was rapidly combined with 0.8 mL of 10% trichloroacetic acid in a glass test tube, stored in a refrigerator for 5 min., and then centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 min. 0.2 mL of solution was taken in a test tube from the mixture and it was added by 10 mL distilled water to dilute to 2 mL. To the diluted fraction powder mixture, 0.2 mL of diluted Folin-Ciocalteu reagent (1:9) was mixed, and it was kept for 10 min at RT (30 °C). The absorbance at 760 nm was subsequently measured using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer. Finally, the results were expressed as mg of ascorbic acid equivalents (AAEs) per g of fraction powder of PSME, and data presented as average of three replicates with SD.

Antioxidant activity analysis of different fractions of PSME

Antioxidant activity test by DPPH method

DPPH (1,1 diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl) method proposed by Bondet et al.50 was used to determine antioxidant capacity of each fraction (sample). The concentration of each fraction sample solution ranged from 10–100 µg. mL-1, and the concentration of the DPPH solution in methanol was 0.4 mM. Here, 0.9 mL of DPPH solution (0.4 mM) was taken in test tubes separately and it was added by 100 µL of each concentration of fraction powder separately. A control set without sample was run. The reaction mixture was incubated for 2 h in a dark condition at RT (30 ± 2 °C), and then the mixture was subjected to take OD at 517 nm wave length by a UV–Vis spectrophotometer. The scavenge potentiality of all fractions was ultimately measured. The synthetic antioxidant BHA (butylated hydroxyanisole) was used as positive control. Antioxidant activity test by FRAP (ferric reducing antioxidant power) method

FRAP is a method applied to determine the antioxidant activity of a sample by evaluating its capacity to reduce ferric ions (Fe3+) to ferrous ions (Fe2+)51. The 80 µL of fraction powder (sample) was taken in a test tube, and then 2 mL of FRAP reagent was added to it, and the mixture was incubated for 10 min at 37 °C. The mixture was subjected to take OD at 593 nm wave length by a UV–Vis spectrophotometer. Concentrations ranged from 100 to 1000 µMs of FeSO4 7H2O were prepared and their absorbance were taken at 593 nm by a UV–Vis spectrophotometer to make a standard curve. The results were denoted as the concentrations of FeSO4 7H2O with equivalent antioxidant activity.

The interaction network analysis among antioxidant contents (TPC, TAA, TFC) and antioxidant activity (DPPH and FRAP)

The most common way to visualize relationships among entities (F1-F20, TPC, TFC, TACC, DPPH, and FRAP within the network) was done through networks (graphs) by using R software (version 4.2.0)52. All data of entities were loaded in R-software. Here network consisted of nodes (denoted as circles with entities) and edges (denoted as lines for co-relationship or interaction). R-studio was used for visualization. Only statistically significant correlations with Pearson’s r > 0.6 and p value < 0.05 were considered.

TLC (thin layer chromatography) profiling of F13 from PSME

In brief, pre-coated silica gel 60 F254 plates (E. Merck) were taken and, pencil lines were marked 1.5 cm from one edge of the plates. 20 µL of F13 solution (0.5%) and standard samples were loaded in spots on the pencil line using thin capillary pipettes. Then, the plates were placed in a development chamber with solvent systems [a 40:40:20 ratio of ethyl acetate, chloroform, and hexane (standardized by trials)], and solvent system was allowed to run upward until about 1 cm from the top end. The time of solvent run was noted. Afterward, the TLC plates were taken off from the chamber and dried, and subjected to UV scanner (254 nm and 366 nm) for examining spots in the plate. The chromatograms that resulted were photographed. After that, we labelled the chromatogram spots53,54. Anti-cancer activity

Here only F13 product, out of 20 fractions was selected and evaluated for its anticancer potentiality. Selection was done on the basis of its best antioxidant potentiality and antioxidant content.

Cell culture

The human breast (MCF7), lung (A459), cervical cancer cell line (HeLa), at passage number 30, 20 and 23 respectively which were purchased from The National Centre for Cell Science (NCSS), Savitribai Phule Pune University, Pune, India] were cultured separately in 75-mm tissue culture flasks in DMEM (Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium) (supplemented with L-glutamine, 10% (v/v) foetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 µg. mL-1 streptomycin, and 250 IU/l penicillin (all purchased from HiMedia Laboratories Pvt. Cultures were passaged weekly for maintenance, and the culture medium was changed twice a week55.

Cytotoxicity/cell proliferation assay

A MTT assay was used to evaluate the activity of F13 from PSME on cell proliferation in the MCF7, A549 and HeLa cell lines (reagents procured from HiMedia Laboratories Pvt. Ltd.)19. 10 × 104 cells were seeded in each well of a 96 well plate with fresh DMEM medium amended with 10% FBS and antibiotics. The plates were incubated in 5% CO2 and at 37 °C temperature in a CO2 incubator for 24 h under moist condition until the cells reached 80–90% confluence. Following that, the culture was washed with 10% PBS and exposed with F13 fraction dissolved in DMEM at various concentrations (250, 500, 750, 1,000 and 1500 μg. mL-1) before being incubated at 37 °C, in a 5% CO2 with 95% air atmosphere. DMEM was used as the vehicle control (negative). Adriamycin (Doxorubicin) (Concentration 2.0, 3.0 and 5.0 μg. mL-1) as positive control. After 24 h incubation, cells were subjected to wash with PBS (Phosphate Buffered Saline), and 100 µL of 500 µg/mL MTT solution (dissolved in phenol red-free DMEM) was poured into each well. Then, the plates with cells were kept for 3 h, and after that the medium was discarded from each well, and 100 µL dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was added for dissolving the crystals. A microplate reader (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc., Hercules, CA, USA) was used to read the plate at an absorbance of 570 nm. Then, the plates were subjected to read under 570 nm wave length by a microplate reader (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc., Hercules, CA, USA)56. Normal human cell line, HEK 293 T was also treated by F13 in the same way, and effect of it was noted57. The percentage of cytotoxicity was calculated by the formula:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\% Cell cytotoxicity}=100-\left[(\frac{\text{ Ac}-\mathrm{At}}{\mathrm{Ac}} )\times100\right]$$\end{document}whereas Ac = Absorbance of the control. At = Absorbance of the test.

The IC50 value (i.e., the concentration that caused 50% cell death) was determined by plotting a dose–response graph of the cytotoxicity values obtained. In each experiment at least thrice, replica was taken and each experiment was repeated by at least three times.

Cell and nuclear morphology examined under a phase contrast and inverted fluorescence microscope

To examine the morphology of cancer cells, 2 × 105 cells /well of each cancer cell line and a normal human cell line (HEK 293 T) were cultured into 6-well plates separately for 24 h in CO2 incubator at 5% CO2 and at 37 °C temperature with moist condition, Then the cells were exposed to some concentrations of F13 from PSME (250, 500, 750, and 1,000, 1500 µg.mL-1) separately where DMEM used as vehicle control and after 24 h, we examined cellular morphology under broad field compound and phase contrast microscope and photos of the cells were taken by camera fitted in an Olympus phase contrast microscope (at a magnification of × 20; Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan. To examine the nuclear morphology, treated cells were subjected to stain with fluorochrome strain (DAPI) and investigated under inverted fluorescence microscope58.

Apoptosis analysis

The nuclear morphology of three cell lines treated by different concentrations (250, 500, 750 µg. mL-1) of F13 fraction separately was examined after they were stained with DAPI to determine whether or not apoptosis had occurred. Protocol used here was followed from Ghosh et al.50. Using an inverted fluorescent microscope, each experiment involved the observation and counting of apoptotic cells. In each experiment, at least five optical fields containing a total of 200 cells were counted19. The percentage of apoptotic cells was calculated as follows: percentage of apoptotic cells = [number of apoptotic cells / total cells counted (usually 200)] × 100%.

Lactate dehydrogenase assay

Cancer cell lines were cultured separately in in 96 wells of a plate (4 × 103 cells/well) in medium DMEM medium amended with 10% FBS for 24 h with 5% CO2 at 37 °C temperature. After washing the cells with PBS, 100 µL of each concentration (250, 500, 750 µg.mL-1) of F13 from PSME was poured separately into the wells, and the cells were incubated for 24 h. and the LDH (lactate dehydrogenase) kit was used to perform the assay (Thermo Fisher Sci. Ltd, USA). A 100 µL of (10:1000) sample was taken and mixed with working reagent and kept for 1 min at 37 °C, and absorbance was taken at 340 nm and calculated the percentage of LDH leakage as per manufacturer’s instructions53.

Western blot analysis for protein expression

750 µg.mL-1 of F13 from PSME extract was separately used to treat each cell lines, HeLa, MCF7 and A549 (2 × 105) cells for 24 h. Treated cells were harvested and lysed in RIPA buffer a (Abcam, USA) to collect the corresponding proteins. The effects of F13 on the expression of specific genes like p53 (tumour guard), apoptotic Caspase-3, Caspase-9, and anti-apoptotic gene BcL2 (Santacruze Biotechnology, USA) were noted and recorded. Proteins were noticed by incubation with the corresponding primary antibodies, followed by blotting with the HRP-conjugated secondary antibody by Western blotting. The blots were then visualization using Luminol (Bio-Rad, USA) and photos of each blot was taken. Image J software was used to measure the intensity of each blot. The obtained data were plotted in a bar diagram59.

Molecular docking or in silico analysis

Preparation of receptor

The crystal form (3D) of Caspase-3 (PDB id: 4qu8) and BcL2 (PDB id: 4MAN) were found in protein data base (PDB) and they were downloaded from it, saved (http://www.rcsb.org/) and visualized by Discovery Studio Visualizer (Biovia Discovery Studio2024). The active site (FT site) was then identified by Vajda Lab (Vajda Lab at Boston University). Each receptor protein was loaded in Autodoc Vina (https://vina.scripps.edu/) software to delete all imported foreign substances like water and heteroatoms but Kollman charges and polar hydrogens were added. and amino acid sequences, if missing, were recovered by this software. Images of prepared receptors were observed in chimera 1.16 and saved. Thus. target receptor proteins were prepared for molecular docking purpose.

Preparation of ligand

Two important compounds, like quercetin and p-coumaric acid, identified by TLC, were selected as ligands for molecular docking against receptors like Caspase 3 and BcL2. These two compounds were downloaded from Pubchem and Drugbank in SDF format, were then converted from SDF to mol by Open Babel software, and then they were imported into Swissdock and PyRx for molecular docking.

Docking of receptor and ligand

The automated docking tools like Swissdock, PyRx, etc. were used for the molecular docking analysis. Before molecular docking, the protein structures were saved in pdb files. Pre-calculation of atomic affinities was done using AutoGrid. In case of BcL2 and Quercetin interaction the grid box size was: X: Y: Z = 20:20:20 and the center was: X: Y: Z = -29: − 6: − 5. In case of BcL2 and p-coumaric acid interaction the grid box size and center well like BcL2 and Quercetin interaction. In case of Caspase 3 and quercetin interaction the grid box size was: X: Y: Z = 20:20:20 and the center was: X: Y: Z = 3: 105: − 103. In case of BcL2 and p-coumaric acid interaction the grid box size and center well like Caspase 3 and quercetin interaction. The Protein–ligand dock complexes were analyzed by Swissdock software for post-docking studies, and visualized by the Biovia Discovery Studio Visualizer 2024, to get different sorts of 3D structure as well as 2D orientations and saved as image form60.

Statistical analysis

We analysed the data obtained from experiments by GraphPad Prism 5 (GraphPad Software, Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA), and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare concentration differences. Duncan multiple range test (DMRT)61, was also done. The results showed here were means ± standard error/ standard deviation (± SE/ SD) of data from triplicate experiments. The figures were representative for at least triple separate experiments. A statistically significant difference was analyzed at P < 0.05 level.

Evaluating binding energy in the ligand-receptor complex

Molecular docking studies were used to elucidate binding interactions, the binding mode, and stability between compounds (quercetin and p-coumaric acid) and targets (BcL2 and Caspase 3) using PyRx, Autodoc Vina software and Swissdoc. The docking data for quercetin and p-coumaric acid with the receptor molecules Caspase-3 and BcL2 were summarized in Table 6, which showed that quercetin was predicted to bind to the active-site cavity of the BcL2 with a binding energy of − 7.09 kcal/mol, which was the highest. Meanwhile, the p-coumaric acid interacted with the target receptor (BcL2) with good binding energies ranging from − 6.84 kcal/mol (Table 6). This indicated that the binding energy/affinity between the target receptor and each of the two compounds was high. As a result, the apoptosis regulator BcL2 was downregulated. Similarly, data from Table 6 showed that quercetin and p-coumaric acid had high binding energy (− 6.75 and − 5.8 kcal/mol, respectively) with Caspase 3 receptors, thus they after binding with Caspase 3 activate it. Discussion

The majority of consumers prefer the antioxidant qualities of mushrooms, which are known to be rich in antioxidants. Fogarasi et al.62 recently evaluated the health effects of bioactive secondary metabolites, particularly phenolic compounds from mushrooms. For some time, researchers have been working on the antioxidant content and activity of different solvent extracts (ethanolic, methanolic, hexane, etc.) of different mushrooms and also have found their anticancer potential on different human cancer cell lines20–22,53,57. Numerous bioactive substances, including tannins, saponins, flavonoids, terpenoids, phenolics, alkaloids, cardiac glycosides, anthocyanins, and betacyanin, were detected in varying concentrations by quantitative phytochemical analysis of various solvent extracts of Pleurotus ostreatus63. Our findings showed that PSME had high concentrations of phenol, flavonoids, carbohydrates, and alkaloids. The methanolic extract of P. ostreatus had the highest DPPH radical scavenging activity (87.67%) at 500 µg.mL-1 63. Generally, a higher phenolic content of any food, extract, or drug indicates its increased antioxidant potential, which is beneficial for human health64. Phenolic compounds have been widely studied for their various biological activities, including anticancer effects. The synergistic interactions among different bioactive compounds contribute to the enhanced antioxidant potentiality of extract64. Antioxidant activity bioassay showed that the EC50 of ethanolic extract of P. florida (antioxidant activity) was 41.17 ± 1.42 µg. mL−1 20, while our methanolic extract fractions (F1-F20) showed their antioxidant activity by DPPH, but F13 exhibited the lowest EC50, which indicated its highest antioxidant activity, which was statistically the same (p > 0.05 level) as synthetic antioxidant BHA. Therefore, the F13 fraction could replace the synthetic antioxidant BHA. Very recently, Ghosh et al.60 fractionized AAEAE (Astraeus asiaticus ethyl acetate extract) by column chromatography and that yielded 20 (F1-F20) fractions, but F12 showed a very strong antioxidant scavenging activity (EC50 value) than other fractions. The EC50 values in the DPPH assay ranged from 0.46 to 13.17 µg. mL-1 of solvent extracts of P. ostreatus64,65. Furthermore, F13 contained a good amount of total phenols, flavonoids, and ascorbic acid. While Pandey and Ghosh20 estimated total phenolic content (7.98 ± 0.78 mg GAEs/g), total flavonoid content (2.031 ± 0.024 mg QE/g), and total ascorbic acid content in the ethanolic extract (0.167 ± 0.03 mg AAEs /g) and our results were comparable to theirs, except total ascorbic acid. The methanol extract of P. ostretus showed total phenol as 8.87 ± 0.06 mg/100 g64. Correlation analysis results showed that Fe2 + scavenging and total phenolic content were strongly correlated with total antioxidant capacity66. In a network or network correlation map (a star-shaped interaction diagram showed the relationship among F1-F20 fractions of PSME and antioxidant contents and assays (DPPH and FRAP) is shown. Fractions that are connected to more antioxidant parameters might have broader antioxidant potential. For instance, F12, F11, and F19 have multiple connections, suggesting they contributed significantly to various antioxidant activities. The fractions connect more strongly (via blue lines) to a parameter like DPPH or FRAP, indicating that particular fraction has potent activity in that assay. The network analysis showed that the F13 from PSME was the best among other fractions with respect to antioxidant content and activity, as it was connected with antioxidant contents and activity by deep and broader blue lines, which indicated strongest correlation with statistically significance. Network analysis for finding correlation among metabolites throughout the 94 tomato cultivars was established67. Similarly, other scientists applied this network analysis to make relationships among multifactors68. Menaga et al.69 fractionized ME of P. florida by column chromatography, like our experiment, and the PF5 fraction was purified, and it had good antioxidant activities. Gebru et al.65 found that the antioxidant activity was better when P. ostreatus was grown on a mixture of substrates (straws of wheat and barley, husks of field pea, faba bean, and sawdust) than on individual substrates. Our mushroom was grown in wheat straw, and its antioxidant was comparable to P. ostreatus65. In vitro antioxidant activities (DPPH) of ME of Pleurotus djamor were very satisfactory as investigated by Sudha et al.70. Jeena et al.71 observed that fruiting bodies of Pleurotus (P. sojor caju, P. ostreatus, and P. sapidus) showed good antioxidant activity and reducing power. Through FT-IR, we observed that PSME contained phenolic compounds, and our analytical TLC experiment showed that F13 was a mixture of six compounds (3 phenolic compounds like quinine, quercetin, and coumaric acid and 3 unidentified compounds). Similarly, Menaga et al.69 found that their PF5 of ME of P. florida was one phenolic compound, like 3-methoxy-4-hydroxy cinnamic acid (ferulic acid). Antioxidant substances scavenge reactive oxygen species (ROS) that damage plasma membrane, DNA, etc. so, they protect cells from harmful effect of ROS72 (Wang et al. 2011). At the same way, some workers have been analysed their activity as anticancer agents. Application of antioxidant supplementation is now an emerging strategy for managing cancer disease; some clinical trials have been done to record the function of antioxidant supplements in cancer management or prevention73. However, the anticancer mechanism of antioxidant substances like flavonoids, flavones, etc., is still unclear to us. It was postulated that antioxidant substances might inhibit the growth of cancer cells, induce apoptosis, prohibit angiogenesis, and inhibit metastasis74. For instances, some dietary antioxidants, originated from some plant extracts, were able to induce apoptosis in cervical cancer cells75. In general, the compounds that have high antioxidant activity shows anti-cancer activity76. A positive correlation was noted between the antioxidant activity and anticancer potential of trihydroxyflavone against the lung cancer cell line (A549 cell line)77. Similarly, antioxidant compound quercetin was reported as anticancer drugs by apoptosis and suppression of cell proliferation of breast, lung, oral and prostate cancer78. But it is not true in all cases77, because scientists have found that some antioxidants may have adverse effects. Therefore, the relationship between antioxidants and cancer was not straight but complex, because in some cases they were both protective and anticancer, and in some cases, they showed harmful effects, depending on the specific type of antioxidant, concentration or dosage, and individual factors72.

In our cytotoxicity assay, F13 was the best for antioxidant activity and was found to inhibit the growth of all three cancer cell lines (HeLa, MCF7, and A549); at the highest concentration (1500 µg. mL-1), it showed more than 80% inhibition of all three cell lines at 24 h, and cellular morphological changes like rounding of cells, blebbing of cell membranes, and nuclear fragmentation and chromatin condensation happened due to exposure to F13. Similarly, the PF5 of P. florida showed potent inhibitory effects on the growth of A549 lung cancer cells69. Cells treated with 1250 µg. mL-1 of ethanolic extract of P. florida revealed 75.22, 77.77, and 84.65% growth inhibition of HeLa at the 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h time points, respectively, and also found the same cellular and nuclear morphological changes20. F12 fraction of ethyl acetate extract of another edible mushroom, A. asiaticus, at dose dose-dependent manner (250–1500 µg. mL-1) showed antiproliferative activates against three cancer cell lines (HeLa, A549, and MCF7)60. The methanolic extract of P. ostreatus was found to inhibit the growth of breast cancer (MCF7 and MDA-MB-231) and colon cancer (HT-29 and HCT-116) cell lines79 and these authors reported that this extract of the fruit body of P. ostreatus changed the morphology of MCF7 and HT-29 cells by breaking DNA and chromatin condensation, which indicated the occurrence of apoptosis80. In our Western blotting experiment, we found that F13 upregulated Caspase 3 and 9 and downregulated anti-apoptotic BcL2 genes of all three cancer cell lines, which indicated that F13 induced apoptosis by increasing apoptotic proteins like Caspase 3 & 9 and reducing BcL2 proteins. We also noted that p53 (tumor guard protein) of all three cancer cells was also enhanced by exposure to F13. According to Jedinak and Sliva79, the inhibition of growth of human colon and breast cancer cells happened through both p53-dependent and p53-independent pathways by exposure to extract of P. ostreatus.

Furthermore, in our LDH leakage assay we found that our F13 from PSME was able to allow the leakage of LDH in all three cancer cell lines (MCF7, A549 and HeLa), which indicated the good anticancer nature of F13. Similarly, Sharvit et al.80 reported that the ethyl acetate extract of Cyathus striatus was able to allow the leakage of LDH from the pancreatic cancer cell line. Ghosh et al.81 recorded the effect of. methanolic extract of A. bisporus had positive effect on LDH leakage on MCF7, HeLa and MDA-MB-231cancer cell lines. Six compounds within F13 acted as anticancer agents, as evident from our different experimental findings, like inhibition of cell growth of all three cancer cell lines (MTT assay), cellular and nuclear changes, apoptosis assay, and LDH leakage from all three cancer cells. Moreover, the mechanism behind apoptosis was the upregulation of the apoptotic genes/proteins Caspase 3 & 9 and p53 and the simultaneous downregulation of the antiapoptotic gene/protein BcL2, as it was proven from our Western blotting experiments results. A lot of work on phenolic compounds have been done for their several biological activities, including anticancer, anti-inflammatory effects63. Quercetin, one kind of flavonoid, have been reported to have antioxidant activity, having in different vegetables82,83. Several studies confirmed the ability of quercetin to potentiate the efficacy of anticancer drugs by apoptosis and suppression of cell proliferation of breast, lung, oral, and prostate cancer84. Hisaka et al85 for the first time examined antitumor effects of quercetin on thirteen liver cancer cell lines (13 HCC) in vitro. p-coumaric acid, one kind of phenolic compound or heterocyclic compound belonging to the class of benzo-alpha-pyrone86, soluble in ethanol and chloroform87, and upon consumption, coumarins are assimilated well through the gastrointestinal tract88, and it has both antioxidant and anticancer property89–92. Since quinine isolation in the nineteenth century, a large number of related natural products have been isolated and identified93. Most of these compounds and their derivatives exhibited significant biological activities, such as antitumor, antimalarial, anti-oxidant and other effects94–97. Ren29 states that since several of the compounds have been demonstrated to work in combination, it is desirable to examine the anti-proliferative effects of the entire mushroom extract rather than merely its constituent parts. The anticancer efficacy of our F13 fraction (quinin, quercetin, p-coumaric acid, etc.) was most likely caused by the chemicals’ synergistic effect. But in comparison with Doxorubicin, F13 from PSME, having a higher dose, had a lesser positive effect in the inhibition of all three cancer cell lines; it might be because F13 was of edible mushroom origin and was not pure, but doxorubicin was quite pure.

Molecular docking (MD) is a modern tool to know the interaction mode, energy, affinity, and stability of binding between receptor and ligand or drug98. Hydrogen bonding, van der Waals interaction, Pi-Alkyl and Pi sigma interaction (hydrophobic interaction) and other interacting energies are involved in doc complex but primary forces for this purpose are hydrogen bonding and van der Waals99. From our experiments, it was resolved that our F13 fraction contained phenolic compounds (quercetin and p-coumaric acid), and it was invariably determined that these compounds acted as ligands against Caspase 3 & 9 and BcL2 and p53 proteins. From literature100–102, we have noticed that these ligands inhibited the function of BcL2. In our experiment, the binding energies of two ligands (quercetin and p-coumaric acids) against BcL2 were satisfactory. Docking analysis have indicated that these two compounds were inhibitory to BcL2. Similarly, worker reported good the binding energy of solsodine (-6.16 kcal/mol) against BcL2103. On the other hand, we known that, Caspase-3, the cysteine protease family protein, triggers apoptosis104,105. In silico analysis showed us that quercetin and p-coumaric acid had high binding energy with Caspase 3. So, F13 acted as a potential upregulator of Caspase-3, leading to apoptosis, which was endorsed by other workers101,106. The study provides a comprehensive investigation of the antioxidant and anticancer properties of the F13 fraction from Pleurotus sajar caju, including robust in vitro and in silico analyses, which enriches the current understanding of mushroom-derived bioactive compounds in cancer management. As the literature survey revealed, no human clinical trial has been done on Pleurotus sajar-caju; therefore, clinical trials of F13 in humans are required to understand its potentiality and safety and also for drug development. Every research work may have some limitations, and limitations in this work include i. unable to identify three compounds of F13, out of six by TLC, ii. lack of LC–MS/ GC–MS analysis of F13, iii. lack of study of cell cycle arrest by this fraction. iii. lack of application of this F13 in animal model for studying anticancer potentiality. But our present findings invariably made an inroad for future work.

Conclusions

After critical discussion, we concluded that our F13 fraction from P. sajar caju contained six compounds (quinin, quercetin, p-coumaric acid, etc.). Most likely, their synergistic effect was responsible for the anticancer activity. Its anticancer effect on cancer cell lines, such as HeLa, MCF7, and A549, was demonstrated by cytotoxicity assays, changes in cellular and nuclear morphology, upregulation of Caspase 3, 9, and p53 genes, and downregulation of BcL2 genes, as well as leakage of LDH from cancer cell lines. There was no toxic effect of this F13 fraction, as evident from the experiments where our normal cell line (HEK 293 T) was under exposure to F13 for 24 h; no inhibition of cell growth was noted. In addition, molecular docking exhibited good binding energy of quercetin and p-coumaric acid with BcL2 and Caspase 3, so they influenced upregulation of Caspase 3 & 9 but downregulation of BcL2, and thus induced and enhanced apoptosis. Therefore, the F13 fraction from P. sajar caju is a good source for antioxidant and anticancer compounds, and it may be applied in cancer management after animal and human trials.
