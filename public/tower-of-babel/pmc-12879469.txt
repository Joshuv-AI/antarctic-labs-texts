Psychosocial correlates of co-workers’ responses to alcohol problems in the workplace - a cross-sectional survey among medical personnel in Poland
Authors: Justyna Klingemann, Harald Klingemann, Agnieszka Borowiec, Halina Sienkiewicz-Jarosz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe health of medical staff impacts the quality of patient care. Work-related alcohol issues are in this regard well-documented, linking consumption to adverse outcomes. However, healthcare workers’ responses to perceived alcohol problems, which are crucial for Employee Assistance Programs (EAPs) success, remain under-researched.MethodsThe study surveyed 655 Polish healthcare workers in autumn 2024 by computer assisted personal interviewing (CAPI). The quota sample included 50% hospital staff and 50% from other facilities, based on the Medical Personnel Data portal. Instruments used included the Copenhagen Psychosocial Questionnaire (COPSOQII, measuring workplace stress and support) and the Rapid Alcohol Problems Screen (RAPS4), covarying with co-workers’ responses to alcohol issues in the workplace.ResultsIn hypothetical scenarios, respondents were more likely to take action in relation to problems with alcohol (85%) compared to real situations (72%), with the most common response being direct confrontation with the colleague under suspicion. Organizational support, particularly from supervisors, significantly influenced responses, with higher support correlating with a greater likelihood of direct or indirect action, though no predictor remained significant in real-life situations. Additionally, professional hierarchy played a role, as lower-status medical professionals were more cautious in responding, and treatment effectiveness optimism influenced indirect responses in hypothetical cases but had no impact in real situations.ConclusionsThis study examines how personal attitudes, workplace norms, and perceived organizational social support shape medical professionals’ responses to alcohol-related issues. While many recognize the risks involved, a gap exists between hypothetical and real-life interventions. Trust in superiors fosters proactive responses, highlighting the need for clear policies, targeted training, and EAPs (Employee Assistance Programs) to be implemented in order to balance accountability with employee dignity and privacy.Keywords: Alcohol-related problems, Workplace, Health professionals, Organizational social support

Background

Helping professions, including doctors, nurses, psychologists and other medical professionals, face heightened expectations from the public, who anticipate that they will deliver high-quality care. However, even healthcare professionals are not immune to personal health challenges; there is a paucity of research, for instance, on the health of medical staff, and analyses of situations in which a doctor or nurse has become a patient are rare [1, 2]. The health of medical staff, including their mental health, is of both individual and systemic value, as it is a vital indicator of the quality of patient care. Work-related alcohol problems have been extensively documented in the literature, with numerous studies highlighting the correlation between alcohol consumption and various adverse outcomes, including accidents at work, staff turnover, reduced work efficiency, presenteeism, absenteeism, and conflicts between employees [3–6]. It is particularly noteworthy that employees who experience alcohol-related problems or consume alcohol on the premises of a medical facility present a significant risk to themselves and others. Their own well-being is jeopardized, workplace relationships with colleagues are adversely impacted, and the well-being of patients is endangered. It is a matter of concern that employees in the medical sector are also affected by mental health issues, including those resulting from alcohol consumption. This is a particularly salient issue in light of the increasingly stressful environment that is created as a consequence of the labour shortage in healthcare systems in many countries. In fact, it is predicted [7] that alcohol-related impairment among certain healthcare professions will increase. Research indicates that individuals in high-pressure occupations involving shift work, demonstrate a higher prevalence of risky drinking behaviours [8, 9]. However, a study conducted on hospital staff in Greece [10] revealed that nurses exhibited a greater propensity for protective health behaviours compared to doctors, who often do not adhere to their own recommended guidelines for preventing health issues. A study of the Polish healthcare sector reveals that doctors have the highest prevalence of alcohol abuse (12.6%), followed by nurses (3.2%) and other healthcare professionals (7.4%) [11].

Concurrently, the social esteem held by the Polish populace towards medical professionals - alongside firefighters, miners and university professors - has remained consistently high since the 1990s [12]. This phenomenon is a contributory factor to the reluctance of individuals to disclose alcohol-related problems, due to the perceived or real risk of losing their employment if such issues were to become public knowledge. The occupational context of healthcare professionals working with patients necessitates a heightened level of scrutiny with regard to alcohol [4, 13]. Shame and the fear of stigmatization represent additional significant barriers to disclosure. Those working in the medical sector who experience alcohol dependence may encounter stigmatization, resulting in discrimination in hiring, promotion, and accessing full employment benefits, as well as inequity in workplace social interactions [14].

The relational aspects of addiction and alcohol abuse mean that, despite attempts by individuals to conceal it, a problem with alcohol is observed by those in their immediate environments. Research conducted in Poland indicates that employees within the medical and education sectors have expressed a sense of helplessness when confronted with addiction in colleagues [4]. Furthermore, the willingness to act may be influenced by the position in the job hierarchies [15]. Consequently, there is a need to identify effective strategies to address the discrepancy between intention and action. The attitude of managers towards organizational alcohol policies is of crucial importance, as they frequently exert a significant influence on the implementation process [8]. In a Swedish study, 27% of managers and 8% of other employees from a range of sectors, including the medical sector, stated that they had expressed concern about colleagues due to their alcohol consumption. The awareness of the response path was found to be contingent on the role within the organisation, with almost all managers (94%) being knowledgeable about the employee policy on alcohol use and abuse and the majority (98%) expressing confidence in their ability to respond in the event of an incident. In contrast, this level of awareness and confidence was not uniform across all employees, with only 63% and 70% of other employees, respectively, demonstrating comparable levels of awareness and preparedness [16].

The aim of this study was to explore the medical community’s beliefs about alcohol-related problems and norms regarding alcohol use in the workplace, and to understand the determinants of responses to these problems in the medical sector. In the context of the medical sector, the issue of alcohol use has been identified as a salient but understudied area. The study presented here addresses this research gap.

Methods

Study design

The study was observational, cross-sectional in nature and was conducted using a survey method, i.e. structured interviews.

Study population

The survey was conducted using the computer-assisted personal interviewing (CAPI) method1 on a nationwide random and stratified sample of 655 employees of the Polish health sector. Half of the respondents (51%) were employed in hospitals, while the remainder were engaged in various other healthcare settings, including general and specialised medical practices, physiotherapy, emergency, and nursing and midwifery. The data on the proportions of doctors, nurses and midwives, and physiotherapists were estimated on the basis of the Medical Personnel Data portal, and the proportions of the other medical groups (i.e. psychologists, paramedics) were assumed at 5% each. The selection of respondents was conducted in accordance with the assumed proportions. Within this group, 78.3% of individuals identified as female. The average age of the survey respondents was 39.9 years, with a standard deviation of 9.2 years. A significant proportion of the participants were nurses and midwives, constituting 53.1% of the sample. Doctors accounted for 27.3% of the healthcare workers surveyed. Finally, the survey participants had an average of 11.3 years of professional experience (with a standard deviation of 9.9) (Table 1).

Measures

The survey comprised a series of questions relating to the consumption of alcohol in the workplace. This included both hypothetical and actual responses regarding situations in which individuals suspected a colleague to be under the influence of alcohol in the workplace, optimism about alcohol treatment effectiveness, and alcohol-related workplace norms. These norms were based on the study by Geuijen et al. [15]. The questionnaire also incorporated questions designed to identify problematic alcohol use, as indicated by the Rapid Alcohol Problems Screen (RAPS4) [17]. In order to facilitate a more nuanced analysis, the following descriptive and explanatory categories are distinguished: ‘direct response’ (I would talk only to the person; I would talk to the person and discuss it with colleagues; I would talk to the person and discuss it with my superior; I would talk to the person and discuss it with colleagues and my superior), ‘indirect response’ (I would discuss it only with my superior; I would discuss it with my superior and with colleagues) and ‘no direct/indirect response’ (I would not react; I would discuss it only with colleagues; other; don’t know). The multiple response option allowed for various combinations.

The RAPS4 was developed by Cherpitel [17] for the purpose of detecting current alcohol dependence (4 items: remorse, amnesia, performs and starter). A positive response to any of the four questions is considered positive on the RAPS4. The instrument has been utilised in Poland in an emergency setting study [18], and in comparative international population studies [19]. Due to the skewness of the distribution, a dichotomous variable was used in the selected analyses (0 points = no addiction; 1–4 = addiction).

The second, revised version of the Copenhagen Psychosocial Questionnaire (COPSOQII), developed by Pejtersen et al. [20] and validated in Polish by Baka [21], includes subscales on social support from colleagues (3 items; Cronbach’s α = 0.788) and social support from supervisor (3 items; Cronbach’s α = 0.999). These scales were used to measure specific perceived support in the organization. All items have five response options on Likert-type scales. Furthermore, we merged both COPSOQ subscales [20, 21] as a measure for overall support within the organization (Cronbach’s α = 0.844).

Finally we used a question “Have you ever been diagnosed yourself with addiction” to provide some qualitative insight in employers’ response.

Statistical analyses

In order to identify relationships between qualitative variables, crosstabulations and chi-square tests were used. In order to determine relationships between various types of response and quantitative variable/scale, such as social support, years of practice, and addiction treatment, optimism, one-way ANOVA analyses were employed. The quantitative variables were found to deviate from a normal distribution (as indicated by the Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests), with the exception of the variable which was analysed in the context of real behaviour. The bias was deemed acceptable for the variables “support from co-workers” and “support from supervisors” (as determined by the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test); however, it was deemed too large for the variables “years in practice” and “RAPS4”. Consequently, the Kruskal-Wallis test was employed to corroborate the outcomes of the ANOVA analysis. The Levene Test indicates homogeneity of variance in the variables, excluding “organisational social support” in analyses related to the hypothetical situation and RAPS in both. To confirm the ANOVA results, the Welch Test was used. Multivariate logistic regression analyses employing the enter method were conducted to identify significant predictors of the type of hypothetical and actual response when individuals suspected a co-worker to be under the influence of alcohol in the workplace.

Results

Type of response

Respondents were asked first how they would react if they had a suspicion that a co-worker was under the influence of alcohol in the workplace, followed by a question asking how they did react when they were confronted with this problem in a real situation. The second question was only addressed to respondents who had experienced such a situation, i.e. answered ‘yes’ to the question: ‘Have you ever had the impression that a colleague was under the influence of alcohol or other psychoactive substances at work’. While imagining a hypothetical situation only 15% said they would not act, while in an experienced real situation 28% refrained from an active response (Table 2). In both cases, the active respondents primarily reached out to the colleague assumingly under the influence only (34%/26%), followed by (15.6%/12.7%) who would discuss the problem with a superior only (15.6%/12.7%). The latter possibly reflects a certain caution in sharing a suspicion with colleagues and potentially stigmatizing the person in question. The former passive behaviour might reflect a lack of trust that the situation could be handled successfully and without stigmatizing the person in question.

The distribution of these composite variables showed a reluctance to disclose the problem in the presence of the supervisor both in the hypothetical and real situations (indirect response 18.5%/19.4%). At the same time contacting the person in question was hypothetically seen as necessary by a majority (56%, see Fig. 1). The problem-solving potential of this aspect is significantly diminished when being confronted with a real situation (41%) with an equal increase of ‘no direct/indirect response’ to 39.6%. The following analysis will highlight the organizational and individual barriers to exploit and apply the hypothetical ‘good will’ in a real situation.

Hypothetical and real approaches upon a presumption of substance use of a colleague

Organizational covariates

We pursued the assumption organizational social support, openness and trust in work relationships would influence the reaction towards the alcohol problems of a colleague. Accordingly, we merged both COPSOQ subscales [20, 21] as an indicator of overall organisational social support (Cronbach’s α = 0.844). Besides perceived social support, ‘years of practice’ as an indirect correlate of familiarity with the organizational formal and informal policies was taken into consideration. A one-way ANOVA showed a statistically significant difference between at least two groups in support from co-workers (F(2,587) = 4.765, p = 0.009), support from supervisors (F(2, 632) = 17.080, p = 0.001) and organisational social support (F(2, 581) = 10.417, p < 0.001). At the same time there was no statistically significant difference in work experience between any groups (F(2,652) = 0.191, p = 0.826). A Bonferroni Test for multiple comparisons showed that the mean value of support from co-workers was significantly different between direct response and no-response groups (p = 0.043, 95% C.I. = 0.0048, 0.4485), and the indirect response no-response groups (p = 0.011, 95% C.I. = 0.0616, 0.6259). There was no statistically significant difference between the direct response and indirect response groups. The mean value of support from supervisors was significantly different between the direct response and indirect response groups (p = 0.008, 95% C.I. = -0.6033, -0.0690), the direct response and no-response group (p < = 0.001, 95% C.I. = 0.1578, 0.6433), and the indirect response and no-response groups (p < = 0.001, 95% C.I. = 0.4300, 1.0434). A Tamhane Test for multiple comparisons found that the mean value of organisational support was significantly different between the direct response and no-response groups (p = 0.005, 95% C.I. = 0.0682, 0.4822) and the indirect response and no-response groups (p < = 0.001, 95% C.I. = 0.2397, 0.8087). There was no statistically significant difference between the direct and indirect response groups (the results of pairwise comparisons were confirmed by the Kruskal-Wallis test).

To summarize: In a hypothetical situation, the perceived support across the three subscales favours significantly both the decision to take direct or indirect action. Only the support from the supervisor shows a significantly difference between direct response and indirect response; the latter showing a higher level of support than the former (see Table 3).

In a second step, a one-way ANOVA was performed to determine the relationship between the type of response in the experienced real situation and support from co-workers, support from supervisors, organizational social support and years of practice. A one-way ANOVA was performed to determine the relationships between the type of response in a real situation in which the co-worker was under influence of alcohol or another substance and “support from co-workers”, “support from supervisors”, “organisational social support” and “years in practice”. It revealed that there was a statistically significant difference between at least two groups in support from supervisors (F(2, 128) = 6.633, p = 0.002). A one-way ANOVA revealed that there was not a statistically significant difference in support from co-workers (F(2, 118) = 0.427, p = 0.653), and organisational support (F(2, 118) = 2.637, p = 0.076), and years in practice between any groups (F(2,131) = 0.222, p = 0.801). A Bonferroni Test for multiple comparisons found that the mean value of support from supervisors was significantly different between the direct response and indirect response groups (p = 0.006, 95% C.I. = -1.4664, -0.1874), and the indirect response and no-response groups (p = 0.002, 95% C.I. = 0.2754, 1.5504). The results of pairwise comparisons were confirmed by the Kruskal-Wallis test.

To summarize: In a real situation only the support from the supervisor showed a significant difference between indirect response and each of the others: direct response and no-response (see Table 3). Organizational conditions perceived as supportive therefore significantly influence coping with alcohol problems in the workplace.

In another step, we tested the hypothesis that the status/type of work/position in the medical organization will guide the type of response to alcohol problems therein. A first comparison between professional groups shows almost no differences between the three groups of medical staff in a hypothetical situation. However, in the real situation the response pattern of the ‘other medical professionals’ changes markedly, depicting a cautious way of responding, with 47% who would respond at all and only 29% including the person in question in a proactive response (by comparison, 45% of the doctors and 41.6% of the nurses and midwives said they would do so) (see Fig. 2). The lower status of this group within the organizational hierarchy could explain this tendency to not get involved. However, there are no significant relationships between types of reaction and professional groups in the hypothetical and real situations.

Individual problem-related predictors of response

The following exploration is based on the assumption that addiction-related attitudes, individual alcohol problems and norms will influence the type of response to alcohol problems in the workplace. Table 5 shows the significant differences between professional groups when it comes to the ‘tolerance’ of alcohol in the workplace setting. While 94%-to-97% of the other groups consider drinking alcohol in the workplace to be unacceptable, a higher tolerance among doctors emerged, with 12.8% of them accepting the drinking of alcohol in the workplace. The acceptance of alcohol consumption eight hours before work is shared by almost a quarter of all respondents.

Discussion

The present study set out to explore the medical community’s beliefs about alcohol-related problems and norms regarding alcohol use in the workplace, with a view to understanding the correlates of responses to these problems in the medical sector. The response to assumed and/or experienced alcohol problems in the workplace is influenced by a number of factors, including alcohol-related norms and behaviour, as well as the general helping behaviour and organisationals social support. While ample research addresses the ‘top-down’ perspective of the implementation of EAPs, the analysis presented here highlights the ‘bottom-up’ perception of employees in the particularly safety-sensitive, high-pressure medical sector. As part of a wider study within mental healthcare, the alcohol-specific analysis is able to draw also upon general contextual aspects such as organizational social support. Joining individual and organizational aspects represents the strength of the study.

We hypothesised that attitudes regarding addiction, personal alcohol problems and norms would influence how individuals respond to problems related to alcohol use in the workplace. The present study’s sample reveals that almost a quarter of respondents accept drinking alcohol eight hours prior to beginning work. A parallel can be drawn with the findings of a Dutch study among doctors [15], which revealed that the overwhelming majority of them not only deemed alcohol consumption at work to be unacceptable (99%), but also during standby duties (91%) and within the eight hours preceding work (77%).

We recognize a big potential of willingness to help and intervene when alcohol at the workplace becomes a problem when looking at a hypothetical situation. The application of this goodwill in a real situation meets several barriers, including stigmatization and lack of information about and awareness of potential support. Even though the optimistic view of treatment for addiction promotes proactive behaviour, it can be accompanied by insecurity and a lack of information. Similarly to the Dutch study mentioned, the majority of doctors indicated that they would take action if they suspected substance use in a colleague, but in the group that had experienced such a situation only two thirds had actually taken action. The reluctance to intervene is frequently explained by the bystander effect, i.e. the expectation that someone else will take care of the problem [15].

The level of stigma attached to seeking help and other barriers needs to be addressed by future research [22–25]. The relatively high level of willingness to respond to real cases - even without a clear vision about the possibly negative consequences for the person in question - could be explained by the specificity of the high-risk medical context and the anticipation of harm to others (here patients), which outweighs concerns for the person with the alcohol problem. To date, work-related alcohol use and harm to others have been studied at a general population level [26].

In view of the fact that medical professionals are considered to be occupying safety-sensitive roles, the imposition of strict no-tolerance policies with regard to alcohol use is both appropriate and necessary [14]. Nonetheless, this approach can be implemented without compromising employee privacy, autonomy, and dignity. Among the recommended employer-initiated interventions are the establishment of a clear written workplace policy on alcohol use, employee education to improve knowledge about alcohol-related risks, training of managers to keep them updated with the most recent workplace alcohol policies, and identification of early-warning signs of impaired performance. The implementation of employee assistance programmes, which facilitate the confidential treatment of affected workers, is also recommended [27–30]. Research indicates that managers in larger organisations that have implemented alcohol policies are more likely to respond to such situations (provided they have the relevant knowledge) [8].

The assumption that organizational social support - openness and trust in work relationships - can influence a reaction to the alcohol problems of a colleague has been confirmed in the hypothetical scenario - the perceived level of support favoured significantly both the decision to take direct or indirect action. However, in a real situation only support from supervisors showed a significant difference between indirect response and each of the other forms. The inclusion of perceived organisational social support and trust in this study shows that structural factors, in addition to individual characteristics, widen our understanding of the ways in which alcohol problems are coped with in the workplace. While most of the literature adopts a top-down perspective (e.g. effects of testing programs and focus on legislation and guidelines) [31, 32], this study highlights the co-worker perspective, including the perception of the organizational context. A contextual approach and the inclusion of various stakeholder positions is seen as essential for the success of EAPs [33, 34].

Limitations

The present study collected no information concerning existing EAPs or other forms of support with which the respondents were acquainted and had access to. However, it can be reasonably assumed that the institutions included in the study did not offer EAPs, as research conducted in the Polish context has indicated that the proportion of medium and large workplaces offering assistance to employees with alcohol problems is very low [4, 35]. Furthermore, attempts to include the type of employment (full-time or part-time) as an indicator of identification with the workplace and group cohesion failed because, in Poland, the majority of doctors are employed by multiple institutions.

This omission limits our ability to evaluate how the availability of EAPs could have shaped intervention behaviours. If EAPs were accessible and effectively communicated, they might have provided respondents with a more straightforward, safer, and trusted way to address suspected alcohol-related issues. This could potentially reduce the gap between their hypothetical willingness to act and their actual responses in real situations. Findings from other workplace settings suggest that well-implemented alcohol policies and EAPs can enhance managers’ readiness to intervene, especially when supported by adequate training and clearly defined procedures [8, 27, 28, 34]. Conversely, the absence of such structures—or limited awareness of them—may lead to increased hesitation, reliance on informal approaches, or inaction driven by concerns about stigma and uncertainty regarding procedural outcomes [14, 22, 23].

Conclusions

This study sheds light on the complex interplay between personal attitudes, workplace norms and organizational social support in shaping responses to alcohol-related problems among medical professionals. While a considerable proportion of respondents recognise the risks associated with alcohol consumption before and during work, a discrepancy emerges between their readiness to intervene in hypothetical scenarios and their actual responses in real-life situations. Key barriers to intervening include stigma, lack of awareness and the bystander effect. A supportive organisational social support, particularly trust in superiors, plays a crucial role in encouraging proactive responses to alcohol-related issues. The findings emphasise the necessity for clear workplace alcohol policies, targeted training, and Employee Assistance Programs (EAPs) to cultivate a culture of accountability, while ensuring the protection of employees’ privacy and dignity. Future research should further explore structural and contextual factors influencing intervention behaviours, ensuring a holistic approach to addressing alcohol use in safety-sensitive professions.
