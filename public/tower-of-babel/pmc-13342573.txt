Differential oxidation of surimi actomyosin by malondialdehyde and heme: spectroscopy and molecular simulation
Authors: Hao Zhu, Qingchun Zhang, Benjamin K Simpson, Yanbo Wang, Yicheng Ding, Shulai Liu, Shichen Zhu, Xuxia Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPro-oxidative factors compromise surimi gel quality by inducing protein denaturation and aggregation. This study elucidated the interactions between malondialdehyde (MDA) and hemin with surimi actomyosin through multispectral analysis and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. The results suggested that MDA and hemin induced actomyosin oxidation by altering protein conformation, surface hydrophobicity, and the microenvironment of aromatic residues, especially under low-pH/high-temperature conditions. The MD results confirmed that MDA weakly interacted with myosin owing to its flexible backbone and limited hydrophobicity (ΔE = +0.24 kcal/mol), whereas hemin forms stable interactions (ΔE = −33.64 kcal/mol), likely due to its central iron atom, rigid porphyrin ring, and polar groups. Under co-exposure, hemin facilitated the relocation of MDA into deeper binding pockets, forming a synergistic network driven by hydrogen bonding, van der Waals forces, and hydrophobic interactions (ΔE = +0.05 kcal/mol for MDA; −28.71 kcal/mol for hemin). These findings provide molecular-level insights into surimi quality deterioration and support strategies to mitigate protein oxidation during processing. Subject terms: Biochemistry, Biophysics, Chemistry

Introduction

Surimi-based products are widely favored by consumers due to their desirable texture and unique taste. However, various physicochemical changes involving surimi production steps, especially protein oxidation—significantly compromise product quality1. Protein oxidation modifies amino acid residues and peptide side chains, leading to detrimental effects on gelling ability2, color stability3, water-holding capacity, and digestibility4. These oxidative processes are driven by residual pro-oxidation factors, including lipid oxidation products, metal ions, pro-oxidant enzymes, and metmyoglobin, each contributing to surimi actomyosin oxidation through distinct mechanisms5. Amongst these factors, malondialdehyde (MDA), a secondary lipid oxidation product, and hemin, a prosthetic group of myoglobin (Mb) and hemoglobin, are two major endogenous pro-oxidants that are challenging to eliminate in surimi. MDA promotes protein carbonylation and aggregation via nucleophilic addition6, whereas hemin accelerates oxidation either through direct coordination with actomyosin or via redox reactions involving Fe ion release7–10.

Moreover, the pro-oxidative effects of MDA and hemin are highly dependent on environmental conditions, particularly pH and temperature fluctuations during surimi processing, including rinsing and thawing. When surimi is prepared from live or freshly chilled fish, slaughter-induced stress typically causes a rapid pH decline to approximately 6 before rinsing. To mitigate this, alkaline rinsing agents such as sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO₃) are commonly used, which not only weaken the binding affinity of lipids and Mb to muscle proteins11, but also elevate the surimi pH to around 8.3. Additionally, frozen surimi undergoes thawing, a process in which the temperature can rise to 20 °C12. Both decreasing pH and increasing temperature accelerate hemin release and aggregation, promoting the gradual dissociation of Fe ions from its coordinated porphyrin framework13–15. Similarly, the interactions between MDA and protein are also affected by these environmental conditions16.

Although MDA and hemin are well-established as important endogenous pro-oxidants, and pH and temperature on their activity have been documented17–20, systematic investigations into their synergistic and condition-dependent impacts on surimi actomyosin structural stability under realistic processing conditions (i.e., rinsing, thawing, postmortem acidification) are still lacking. Furthermore, the molecular-level mechanisms by which these specific pro-oxidants interact with and alter myosin structure under such dynamic processing environments remain poorly understood. This knowledge deficit substantially impedes the formulation of targeted approaches to control oxidation and enhance surimi quality in the practical production process. Studies specifically designed to uncover both the structural modifications induced by MDA and hemin and their underlying interaction mechanisms with actomyosin across these critical processing parameters represent both a scientific necessity and an innovative research direction.

Therefore, this study was designed to investigate the effects of MDA and hemin on the structural characteristics of surimi actomyosin under simulated processing conditions, including different pH (6.0, 8.0) and temperature (0 °C, 20 °C) conditions. To achieve this goal, an unusually broad but unified analytical framework was employed, including intrinsic and synchronous fluorescence, ultraviolet–visible (UV–Vis) absorption spectroscopy, fluorescence excitation–emission matrix (EEM), confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM), and 100-ns molecular dynamics (MD) simulations; thus, providing a multi-dimensional mechanistic picture from microstructure to molecular interaction energies. To our knowledge, the present work also provides the first comprehensive MD-based comparison of MDA vs hemin binding energetics and mechanisms, quantifying their distinct free energies, and reporting detailed interaction networks. Prior studies noted their oxidative roles but did not resolve molecular-level differences in myosin binding. Overall, the results may provide fundamental insights into actomyosin oxidation mechanisms under realistic surimi processing conditions, while offering valuable theoretical guidance for quality optimization in surimi-based products manufacturing.

Results and discussion

Intrinsic fluorescence spectra

Fluorescence changes are closely related to aromatic amino acid residues (e.g., Trp and Tyr), typically embedded within the hydrophobic core of proteins. Upon oxidation-induced conformational changes, these residues may become exposed to the solvent, leading to altered fluorescence intensity and shifts in the emission maximum (λmax)21. A red shift generally reflects increased environmental polarity, whereas a blue shift reveals re-embedding into hydrophobic regions22. At pH 6 and 0 °C (Fig. 1A), fluorescence intensity increased in all oxidation treatment groups compared to the NC group, with the lowest intensity of 2063. The MDA and Hemin groups had intermediate intensity of 3159 and 3197, respectively, while the MDA/Hemin group showed the highest (5614). Correspondingly, λmax red-shifted from 322 nm (NC) to 328 nm (MDA/Hemin), indicating increased exposure of aromatic residues and enhanced environmental polarity. The fluorescence intensity decreased from 5961 (NC) to 3385 (MDA/Hemin), with a λmax blue shift when the temperature increased to 20 °C at pH 6 (Fig. 1E), indicating that elevated temperature promoted small molecule-induced reaggregation, re-embedding residues into the hydrophobic core. A similar trend was also observed when the temperature increased from 0 °C to 20 °C at pH 8 (Fig. 1I, M). Although the MDA group showed a modest increase in fluorescence intensity relative to NC at pH 8 and 0 °C, this rise is still attributed to the initial exposure of aromatic amino-acid residues during the early stage of progressive oxidation22. Besides, the fluorescence intensity differences among all groups at pH 8 were smaller than those at pH 6, indicating reduced structural disruption under alkaline conditions23. The comparable excitation of aromatic residues in the Hemin and MDA/Hemin groups suggests that their synergistic pro-oxidative effect was weakened under alkaline environments24. These findings implied that the MDA/Hemin system exhibited the strongest oxidative impact, while NC showed the weakest. Oxidative treatment at low temperature initially induces structural unfolding and exposure of hydrophobic residues. As the temperature rises, continued action of small molecules promotes protein reaggregation, re-embedding these residues and reducing fluorescence intensity. This process reflects an “Exposure-quenching” behavior25.

UV–Vis absorption spectra

The UV–Vis absorption spectra of grass carp actomyosin (240–360 nm, Fig. 1 B, F, J, N) reflect protein conformation changes, particularly the stability of tertiary structures26. The characteristic absorption peak at 280 nm originates from aromatic amino acid residues and indicates the polarity of their surrounding microenvironment. Consistent with the intrinsic fluorescence results, oxidative treatments triggered conformational alterations in actomyosin. At pH 6.0 and 0 °C, absorbance was highest in the MDA/Hemin group, followed by Hemin and MDA, with the NC group lowest. Upon heating to 20 °C, absorbance declined across all groups, decreasing progressively from NC to MDA/Hemin. At pH 8.0 and 0 °C, MDA showed the highest absorbance, followed by MDA/Hemin, NC, and Hemin. Increasing the temperature to 20 °C caused a further decline across all groups, with absorbance consistently decreasing from NC to MDA/Hemin. These results suggest that oxidative stress induced by small molecules and elevated temperature initially promotes the exposure of aromatic residues from the hydrophobic core into a more polar environment, followed by partial reburial due to protein reaggregation, leading to reduced absorbance27. The MDA/Hemin group caused the greatest disruption to tertiary structure, while the NC group remained largely unaffected. Moreover, absorbance changes were more pronounced under acidic conditions, indicating lower structural stability of actomyosin at pH 628.

Synchronous fluorescence spectra

The synchronous fluorescence spectra of actomyosin (Fig. 1C, G, K, O for Trp; Fig. 1D, H, L, P for Tyr) reveal the polarity of the microenvironment surrounding the fluorophores. A wavelength interval (Δλ) of 60 nm corresponds to Trp excitation and 15 nm to Tyr29. For Trp residues, λmax remained unchanged across treatments, with no notable red or blue shifts. Variations were mainly observed in fluorescence intensity, following trends consistent with intrinsic fluorescence and UV–Vis results. The limited shift in λmax may be attributed to the strong hydrophobicity of Trp and its burial within the hydrophobic protein core30. In contrast, Tyr residues exhibited higher environmental sensitivity. In addition to changes in intensity, λmax shifts were more pronounced. At pH6 and 0 °C (Fig. 1Ⅰ-D), λmax gradually red-shifted across treatments, with the largest change in the MDA/Hemin group, suggesting increasing exposure of Tyr residues to a polar aqueous environment. When the temperature rose to 20 °C (Fig. 1Ⅱ-H), λmax blue-shifted in all groups, with the greatest reversal observed in the MDA/Hemin treatment, suggesting oxidation-induced reaggregation and partial reburial of Tyr residues31. At pH8 (Fig. 1Ⅲ-L and 1Ⅳ-P), both Trp and Tyr showed less variation in fluorescence intensity and λmax, indicating a more stable actomyosin conformation and weaker oxidative effects under alkaline conditions.

EEM

EEM spectroscopy is a highly sensitive technique commonly used to characterize protein conformational changes. When fluorophores such as Trp and Tyr become exposed from hydrophobic regions to the aqueous environment, fluorescence intensity changes, suggesting that the protein undergoes unfolding or aggregation32. The characteristic peak at λex = 280.0 nm and λem = 330.0 nm further reflects their typical emission behavior33. Figure 2 shows the EEM spectra of actomyosin under different oxidative treatments using a consistent color scale. At pH6 and 0 °C (Fig. 2A–D), the NC group exhibited the lowest fluorescence intensity, while the MDA and Hemin groups showed moderate increases. The MDA/Hemin group displayed the highest intensity. When the temperature rose to 20 °C (Fig. 2E–H), the NC group showed the strongest signal, whereas the MDA/Hemin group had the weakest. The enhanced maximum fluorescence intensity in the treatment groups suggests continuous exposure and quenching of aromatic amino acid residues34. At pH 8 (Fig. 2I–P), the fluorescence intensity at 0 °C rose from the NC group to MDA, then declined in the Hemin and MDA/Hemin groups. At 20 °C, the intensity continuously decreased from NC to MDA/Hemin, consistent with the intrinsic fluorescence and UV–Vis absorption results. Moreover, actomyosin exhibited more pronounced fluorescence intensity changes under acidic conditions, with the highest signal reaching 279,365 in Fig. 2Ⅱ-E. In contrast, alkaline conditions conferred higher structural stability, with less residue exposure. This suggests that MDA and hemin exert stronger structural effects in acidic environments35. These results further suggest that oxidation induced by small molecules may first promote conformational changes that expose aromatic residues, and then, under further influence of processing conditions, lead to a fluorescence enhancement–quenching pattern36.

CLSM

CLSM images of FITC-labeled actomyosin are shown in Fig. 3, where green fluorescence indicates the spatial distribution of the protein37. In the NC group, protein droplets were the smallest in size and displayed a highly uniform, compact distribution (Fig. 3A), which helps prevent droplet aggregation during storage, thus enhancing stability38. The MDA group showed large protein aggregates, with a markedly reduced number of dispersed droplets (Fig. 3B). Under the action of MDA, the protein structure unfolds, exposing internal hydrophobic groups to the external environment, thereby promoting oxidative aggregation39. Meanwhile, as a nucleophilic cross-linker, it directly promotes both inter- and intramolecular cross-linking of actomyosin via Michael addition, substantially increasing its average particle size40. In contrast, the Hemin group exhibited flocculent clusters (Fig. 3C). Hemin accelerates disulfide bond formation by enhancing free radical generation41. The MDA/Hemin group exhibited both large aggregates and flocculent structures (Fig. 3D), indicating that the two small molecules can simultaneously disrupt the spatial organization of the protein. This suggests a possible synergistic effect that promotes oxidative aggregation of actomyosin42.

Conformational changes of the complexes

Myosin is the primary component of surimi actomyosin, accounting for over 60% of the total protein content, and plays a critical role in gel formation during processing43. To explore the interactions with small molecules and identify potential binding sites, the representative 3D structural snapshots were obtained for each myosin–ligand complex (Fig. 4Ⅰ). All systems retained an overall folded architecture to some extent during the simulations. Notably, hemin remained stably bound to a surface-accessible pocket throughout the simulation, indicating higher binding stability, whereas MDA occasionally dissociated from its binding region and diffused transiently, suggesting weaker and less persistent interactions44. As shown in Fig. 4I, noticeable deformation and reorientation of the long helices near the N/C-termini were observed. Consistently, time-resolved secondary-structure analysis suggested that the most evident rearrangements occurred in these terminal helical regions, which displayed intermittent bending and partial helix-to-coil/turn transitions (Supplementary data 1). Because similar terminal fluctuations were also present in the NC trajectory, these variations may partly reflect the intrinsic flexibility of end segments, while ligand binding may further modulate the extent and patterns of these local structural rearrangements45.

The free energy landscapes (FELs) of the complexes were constructed using root mean square deviation (RMSD) and radius of gyration (Rg) as reaction coordinates, with red regions representing high free energy states and blue regions indicating low-energy conformations46. In the NC system (Fig. 4IIA), the low-energy region is relatively narrow and concentrated, with two separated energy minima. This indicates that the protein mainly fluctuates around two low-energy conformational regions, reflecting a relatively stable folded state. In contrast, the MDA (Fig. 4IIB) and Hemin (Fig. 4IIC) systems exhibit more expanded energy surfaces, and the reduced low-energy regions suggest that stable conformations are less concentrated, with more frequent transitions among different states47. The MDA/Hemin co-treated system (Fig. 4IID) shows the most dispersed and broadly distributed energy basins, indicating a further broadened conformational distribution48. Moreover, given that the free-energy color scale on the right is consistent across panels, the color distributions provide a visual reference: the NC group shows relatively weaker high-energy coloration, followed by the MDA group, whereas the MDA/Hemin group shows stronger high-energy coloration, with a less prominent green-to-yellow transition band. It should be noted that MDA undergoes alternating association and dissociation during the simulations. Because the FEL is derived from statistics over the entire trajectory, this dynamic behavior may be associated with changes in the sampled conformational distribution and, together with its interplay with hemin, contribute to the observed differences in the basin patterns.

MD simulation parameters analysis

RMSD is a critical metric for evaluating the structural stability of proteins during MD simulation, particularly after ligand binding. In this study, the RMSD of the myosin backbone was monitored to assess the equilibration behavior and dynamic stability of each system49. As shown in Fig. 5A, the backbone RMSD increased rapidly in the early stage, indicating overall conformational relaxation from the initial model50. The increase then gradually diminished, and the RMSD profile reached a plateau after approximately 60 ns. During the late stage, it fluctuated around system-specific mean levels without an apparent long-term drift, suggesting that the trajectories remained relatively stable while retaining some backbone flexibility51. Based on the late-stage plateau behavior in Fig. 5A, the last 5 ns of each trajectory were used for binding free energy and protein–ligand interaction analyses.

Root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) was used to evaluate the local flexibility of individual amino acid residues during MD simulations by quantifying atomic deviations from their time-averaged positions, thereby reflecting how ligand binding may affect protein dynamics52. As shown in Fig. 5B, most residues exhibited RMSF values below 0.5 nm across all systems, indicating restrained backbone motions. Meanwhile, higher fluctuations were observed in several segments, mainly within the 120–220, 360–450, and 600–780 regions, suggesting localized flexibility distributed along the sequence. To facilitate interpretation, the sequence segments that contacted hemin or MDA during the simulations were highlighted in Fig. 5B using dashed boxes. Importantly, ligand contacts can influence RMSF in two typical ways: when a ligand forms relatively persistent local noncovalent constraints, the conformational freedom of the associated residues decreases, and RMSF remains relatively low; whereas when ligand binding occurs in surface-exposed regions or shallow grooves and is accompanied by conformational motions, the corresponding segment may exhibit higher RMSF53. As indicated in Fig. 5B, the MDA-contact segment (approximately 600–780) tended to show enhanced fluctuations, whereas the hemin-contact segments (approximately 120–220 and 360–450) showed comparatively moderate fluctuations, suggesting different extents of local dynamic influence by the two ligands. Notably, a pronounced increase in RMSF was observed toward the C-terminus (approximately 800 and beyond) in all systems, including NC, suggesting that this feature is at least partly associated with the intrinsically higher flexibility of the tail segment and reduced end constraints, rather than being primarily driven by ligand binding or a ligand-specific disruption of a long helical region54.

Rg is a key metric for assessing protein compactness, representing the average distance of atoms from the geometric center. Lower Rg values generally reflect a more compact and stable conformation55. As shown in Fig. 5C, Rg values for all systems initially decreased from 3.7 to 3.3 nm and then plateaued, indicating global conformational relaxation and reorganization from the initial model56. After approximately 60 ns, the Rg trajectories showed no apparent long-term drift and fluctuated around system-specific mean levels. Notably, within this late-stage regime (60–100 ns), the MDA/Hemin system exhibited relatively larger Rg fluctuations than the other systems.

Solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) variations during MD simulations provide valuable insights into the binding modes and conformational adaptations of protein–ligand complexes. Lower SASA values generally indicate a more compact protein structure with reduced exposure of hydrophobic regions57, whereas higher values reflect a more open conformation58. As shown in Fig. 5D, during the final 40 ns of simulation, the NC system showed the lowest SASA values, suggesting that myosin without ligand binding maintained a compact conformation with less exposure of non-polar residues and overall lower hydrophobicity. This aligns with spectroscopic data indicating increased exposure of aromatic residues upon ligand binding, consistent with small molecule-induced dynamic structural changes in myosin59.

Contact surface area (CSA) quantifies the interfacial contact between a protein and a ligand60. A larger CSA usually indicates broader interfacial contact, which can be accompanied by local conformational adjustment and may influence the stability of the contacted regions47. In the MDA/Hemin system, the CSA between MDA and myosin stayed at a low level, fluctuating mainly around 0.5 nm² and dropping close to zero in a small part of the trajectory (Fig. 5E). This pattern suggests that the MDA–myosin interface is limited and that a large contact area is not maintained for long. The MDA system showed a similar low CSA profile (Fig. 5I), with a longer period where the contact was close to zero. In contrast, hemin showed a much larger CSA in the co-treatment system, fluctuating around 4.5 nm² with relatively small variations (Fig. 5F). The Hemin system displayed a comparable CSA level and variation range (Fig. 5J), indicating that hemin forms a more extensive and persistent interface with myosin.

Hydrogen bonds (H-bonds) contribute to protein–ligand binding by linking myosin residues with ligand functional groups and reflect the persistence of specific interfacial contacts61. In the co-treatment system, MDA formed 0 to 2 H-bonds with myosin, and the occupancy remained low for most of the simulation (Fig. 5G), indicating weak and short-lived hydrogen bonding. In the MDA system, H-bond occupancy was even lower, consistent with the CSA results (Fig. 5K). By comparison, hemin maintained 2 to 5 H-bonds for extended periods in the co-treatment system (Fig. 5H), and a similar H-bond level was observed in the Hemin system (Fig. 5L). Together with the CSA results, these data indicate that hemin sustains a more stable interfacial interaction network with myosin than MDA62.

Binding free energy analysis

Binding free energies were calculated using the MM/PBSA method and averaged over the final 5 ns of each trajectory to represent the stable late-stage regime after the initial conformational relaxation. In the MDA–myosin complex, the binding was weak (Fig. 5I), with ΔE(Binding) = 0.24 kcal/mol. Energy decomposition revealed that electrostatic interactions predominated, with ΔE(Elec) of −0.72 kcal/mol, suggesting hydrogen bond formation between the carbonyl groups of MDA and polar residues in myosin63. In contrast, van der Waals contributions were limited (ΔE(vdW) = −0.42 kcal/mol). The polar solvation energy (ΔE(Polar Solv) = 1.44 kcal/mol) was the main unfavorable factor, likely due to the high solvent accessibility of the binding region, which increases susceptibility of water molecules64. The non-polar solvation energy (ΔE(SASA)) was only −0.09 kcal/mol, further indicating insufficient hydrophobic encapsulation. Structurally, MDA is a small linear ligand with a limited hydrophobic surface. Although it contains two carbonyl groups capable of hydrogen bonding, it lacks an extensive hydrophobic surface and sufficient multipoint confinement to support stable, multipoint interactions. As a result, MDA exhibits predominantly weak, transient interactions, leading to short residence times and unstable positioning within the binding pocket. These characteristics suggest limited noncovalent stabilization of the MDA–myosin interface under the present simulation conditions65.

In contrast, the Hemin–myosin complex exhibited significantly stronger binding affinity (Fig. 5J). The ΔE(Binding) was −33.64 kcal/mol, and the energy profile remained stable throughout the simulation, indicating a well-preserved binding conformation66. Van der Waals interactions were the predominant driving force (ΔE(vdW) = −47.30 kcal/mol), suggesting that the rigid porphyrin ring of hemin fits efficiently into the hydrophobic pocket of myosin. Electrostatic interactions also contributed substantially (ΔE(Elec) = −25.52 kcal/mol), likely arising from interactions between the peripheral carboxyl groups of hemin and polar residues of the protein. The non-polar solvation energy (−ΔE(SASA) = − 5.54 kcal/mol) further supported effective hydrophobic shielding at the interface. Although the polar solvation energy (ΔE(Polar Solv) = 44.72 kcal/mol) was unfavorable, its impact was limited due to the dominant favorable interactions. Structurally, hemin features a central iron atom, a rigid porphyrin backbone, and multiple polar substituents, giving rise to distinct amphiphilicity20. This structural arrangement enables hemin to form stable, long-lasting interactions with myosin, while also enabling redox reactivity such as radical generation and electron transfer. These properties may promote local conformational loosening and potentially compromise overall structural stability67.

In the MDA/Hemin co-treated system, the two small molecules were bound to distinct regions of myosin, and their binding behaviors were further optimized (Fig. 5K, L). For MDA, the ΔE(Binding) decreased to 0.05 kcal/mol, accompanied by reduced energy fluctuations. Although ΔE(Elec) and ΔE(vdW) were close to zero, indicating weak direct interactions, the polar solvation energy decreased to 0.10 kcal/mol, suggesting reduced hydration interference and a more defined local binding environment. Structural analysis revealed that MDA shifted from a surface-exposed position to an internal groove, forming hydrogen bonds with LYS407 and GLY632, thereby adopting a more defined binding mode. Meanwhile, hemin retained favorable binding characteristics under co-treatment, with a ΔE(Binding) of −28.71 kcal/mol. The van der Waals and electrostatic energies (−43.17and − 28.45 kcal/mol, respectively) were slightly lower than in the Hemin-only system but still indicated strong binding. The non-polar solvation energy (−5.28 kcal/mol) and polar solvation energy (48.18 kcal/mol) together suggest moderate hydrophobic enhancement at the interface. The stable embedding of hemin likely induced local conformational rearrangements, facilitating the formation of a new binding pocket for MDA. Collectively, MDA and hemin formed a complementary binding network that enhanced binding stability and strengthened their combined interaction with myosin. The study further demonstrates a synergistic oxidation mechanism between hemin and MDA, directly supported by the structural and energetic analyses. This idea was previously proposed but not experimentally or computationally verified.

Interaction sites and binding mechanisms of the complexes from MD simulations

In the MDA–myosin complex, the ligand is bound within a surface-exposed groove in a flexible protein region (Fig. 6A-I). The 2D interaction map (Fig. 6A-III) revealed that ASP749 acted as the sole hydrogen bond donor, forming two hydrogen bonds with the two carbonyl groups of MDA. Neighboring residues SER747, ILE748, and MET775 engaged in van der Waals interactions, providing additional support to the binding pocket. These residues were mainly polar and located on the protein surface, indicating that the interaction was predominantly hydrogen bond–driven, with weak hydrophobic contributions and no buried hydrophobic core68. The 3D binding conformation (Fig. 6A-II) further showed that MDA aligned nearly parallel to the protein surface, forming an open and loosely constrained complex. This spatial configuration, lacking dense multipoint non-covalent interactions, suggests limited structural stability and a higher propensity for conformational fluctuation or dissociation. Notably, MD simulations did not capture direct interactions between MDA and aromatic residues. However, initial docking results revealed that the aldehyde groups on MDA formed dual hydrogen bonds with the amino group of TYR755. This discrepancy likely reflects conformational rearrangements during the simulation that disrupted the original hydrogen bond network. In the hemin–myosin complex, the ligand was surrounded by multiple amino acid residues, forming a well-defined binding pocket69. As shown in the 2D interaction diagram (Fig. 6B-III), TYR134 formed a directional hydrogen bond with the phenolic hydroxyl of hemin, likely contributing to ligand orientation and stabilization. Several residues, including ILE114, ASN187, TYR128, ARG190, LYS129, LYS678, and THR185, participated in van der Waals interactions. Although weak individually, these short-range interactions were distributed across multiple sites, collectively stabilizing the complex. Additionally, PRO127, ASN126, and GLY183 formed C–H interactions, further reinforcing the noncovalent binding network. The pocket also contained non-polar (e.g., valine and leucine) and aromatic residues (e.g., tryptophan), which interacted with the hydrophobic region of hemin through Alkyl and Pi–Alkyl interactions. These hydrophobic forces facilitated embedding hemin into hydrophobic regions of the protein, as supported by spectroscopic evidence showing increased exposure of internal hydrophobic residues upon ligand binding. The 3D binding conformation (Fig. 6B-II) further confirmed that hemin was deeply embedded in the myosin hydrophobic cavity. The porphyrin ring, containing the central metal ion, was closely aligned with the protein interior, indicating strong spatial complementarity and binding specificity. Hemin binds to myosin through hydrogen bonding, van der Waals forces, C–H interactions, and hydrophobic effects, collectively inducing protein conformational changes and potentially contributing to oxidation-related processes20.

In the co-treatment system, MDA and hemin bind to distinct regions of myosin (Fig. 6C-I). Compared to the MDA-only complex, where binding primarily occurred at polar surface residues, MDA in the combined system relocated to a region between LYS407 and GLY632 (Fig. 6C-III). It formed two stable hydrogen bonds with the side-chain amino group of LYS407 and the backbone carboxyl group of GLY632, establishing a directional interaction network. ALA628 and GLU630 contributed C–H···O interactions, while ALA629 and GLU631 formed van der Waals contacts, offering additional—though relatively weak—noncovalent stabilization. The 3D model (Fig. 6C-II) further revealed that MDA was embedded within the protein interior, forming multipoint contacts with adjacent chain segments and occupying a more compact binding site. Meanwhile, hemin maintained its typical embedded configuration, forming alkyl and π–alkyl interactions with nonpolar residues (e.g., VAL133, PRO127, LEU131, and ILE114), π–π stacking with TRP130, and hydrogen bonding with TYR134, ASN126, and ASN187 (Fig. 6C-III). Its binding position was nearly identical to that in the single-ligand system, suggesting continued occupancy of a high-affinity site, consistent with multispectral results. Structural and energetic analyses of these systems suggest that hemin plays a dual role in its interaction with myosin. It not only directly binds via hydrophobic embedding and multiple noncovalent interactions but also induces local conformational rearrangements or structural stabilization, thereby indirectly enhancing the binding affinity and spatial accommodation of MDA. Consequently, MDA relocates from a polar surface region to a more structured, interaction-rich site, with its binding mode evolving from primarily hydrogen bonding to a synergistic network involving hydrogen bonds, C–H···O interactions, and hydrophobic contacts. Under oxidative stress, hemin may facilitate MDA-induced oxidative modification of actomyosin, promoting a cooperative mechanism that accelerates protein carbonylation, cross-linking, aggregation, and functional deterioration66. Identifying residue-specific interaction sites provides molecular targets for future modification or mutation studies, and the mechanistic insights offer practical relevance for industrial quality control and oxidation mitigation in surimi production. It should be noted that pKa calculations and constant-pH MD simulations were not included in this study. Based on the key interaction sites identified in Fig. 6, the binding discussed here mainly involves Asp, Lys, Arg, and Tyr residues, which are generally unlikely to undergo drastic charge-state switching within the pH 6–8 range and under low-temperature conditions. However, local microenvironments may still shift residue pKa values. Thus, keeping protonation states fixed may limit residue-specific interpretation of pH- and low-temperature–dependent ligand interactions. Future work will incorporate pKa profiling (with and without ligands) and, where appropriate, constant-pH simulations.

Overall, both MDA and hemin induced oxidative modifications in surimi actomyosin, with hemin exhibiting significantly stronger pro-oxidative activity. These small molecules demonstrated synergistic effects in disrupting protein conformation by promoting the exposure of internal hydrophobic residues and altering the local microenvironment. Under mild conditions (pH 8, 0 °C), fluorescence initially increased before subsequent quenching, whereas harsher conditions (pH 6, 20 °C) resulted in direct fluorescence quenching. MD simulations further revealed distinct binding behaviors: MDA formed only two hydrogen bonds with Asp749 and exhibited weak interaction with myosin, as reflected by a low binding energy ΔE(Binding) = 0.24 kcal/mol, indicating minimal multipoint noncovalent stabilization. In contrast, hemin established stable hydrogen, alkyl, and π–alkyl interactions with Tyr134, Pro127, and adjacent aromatic residues. It was embedded within a hydrophobic pocket, displaying strong binding specificity (ΔE(Binding) = –33.64 kcal/mol). Co-treatment with both compounds promoted conformational loosening, accompanied by increased surface hydrophobicity and reduced structural compactness. Notably, hemin may not only occupy high-affinity binding sites but also induce localized conformational changes that facilitate MDA binding. Although this study elucidated the structural impacts of pro-oxidative small molecules and their combined treatment on protein conformation, quantitative characterization of surimi quality attributes, such as color and texture, is still lacking. In future work, specific indicator measurements could be combined with proteomic approaches to more systematically clarify the association mechanisms between conformational changes and functional properties, as well as flavor development.

Methods

Chemicals

1,1,3,3-Tetraethoxypropane (TEP, ≥97% purity) and hemin (≥90% purity) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Saint Louis, MO, USA). All other analytical-grade reagents were obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Preparation of MDA and hemin stock solutions

MDA stock solution was prepared following the method of Chen et al.63. Briefly, 8.4 mL of TEP, 10 mL of 5 M HCl, and 31.6 mL of distilled water were mixed to obtain a 50 mL solution. The mixture was magnetically stirred at 40 °C in the dark for 40 min until a yellow color developed. After cooling to ambient temperature (25 ± 1 °C), the pH was adjusted to 7.0 using 6 M NaOH. The solution was then diluted to 250 mL with KCl buffer (1.2 M, 20 mM K2HPO4, 20 mM KH2PO4, pH 7.0). The obtained MDA solution was diluted 1 × 105 times, and the absorbance of the MDA solution was measured at 267 nm by using a molar extinction coefficient of ε = 31,500 M−1 cm−1, and the concentration was determined.

Hemin stock solution was prepared as described by Zhang et al.10. Specifically, 0.163 g of hemin was dissolved in 30 mM NaOH to prepare a 10 mM solution, which was stored at 4 °C in the dark. The stock solution was then diluted to 1 mM using KCl buffer (1.2 M, 20 mM K2HPO4, 20 mM KH2PO4, pH 7.0). The resulting solution was stored at 4 °C in the dark.

Preparation of surimi and actomyosin

Surimi was prepared following the method of Liu et al.70. Fresh grass carp, which had been slaughtered by the vendor at the market before purchase, were obtained from a local aquatic market in Deqing County, Zhejiang Province, and immediately transported to the laboratory on ice. Accordingly, no live vertebrate animals were used in this study. After deboning and evisceration, the fish were processed twice using a vertical meat extractor (XZC-220, Hangzhou Saixushipin Co., Ltd., China) to obtain 200–250 g of minced meat. The mince was rinsed twice with a solution containing 0.4% NaHCO₃ and 0.1% NaCl at a 1:5 (w/v) ratio, with each cycle lasting 5 min and the temperature kept below 4 °C. After three rounds of dehydration, the moisture content was adjusted to approximately 81%. The sample was then weighed, recorded, and stored at 4 °C for further use.

Actomyosin was prepared following the method of Benjakul et al.71. Briefly, 3 g of surimi was homogenized in 40 mL of chilled 0.6 M KCl solution (20 mM K2HPO4, 20 mM KH2PO4, pH 7.0) using a homogenizer (IKA T-10, Wilmington, NC, USA) for 4 min. During homogenization, the sample was processed in 20 s intervals with 20 s rests to prevent overheating, while the sample beaker was placed on ice throughout. The pH of the homogenate was adjusted to 7.0 and held on ice for 30 min, followed by centrifugation at 5000 × g, 4°C for 30 min. To precipitate actomyosin, three volumes of chilled deionized water were added to the supernatant, and the mixture was centrifuged at 5000 × g at 4 °C for 20 min. The resulting precipitate was redissolved in an equal volume of chilled 1.2 M KCl solution (20 mM K2HPO4, 20 mM KH2PO4, pH 7.0) with stirring for 30 min at 4 °C. After removing insoluble material via centrifugation (5000 × g, 20 min, 4 °C), the final supernatant was collected as the actomyosin solution.

Preparation of oxidative treatment systems

Actomyosin was mixed with KCl buffer (1.2 M, 20 mM K2HPO4, 20 mM KH2PO4, pH 7.0) and adjusted to a final protein concentration of 10 mg/mL. MDA and hemin stock solutions were appropriately diluted and added to achieve concentrations of 5 mg/mL protein and 0.001 mM for both MDA and hemin. Actomyosin without any additives served as the negative control group (NC). The actomyosin + MDA mixture was defined as the MDA group, actomyosin + hemin mixture as the Hemin group, and actomyosin + MDA + hemin mixture as MDA/Hemin group (total oxidant concentration: 0.001 mM; MDA to hemin molar ratio = 1:9). The final concentration of MDA and hemin in actomyosin was 2 mg/kg and 2 mg/100 g, respectively, according to their actual dosage in surimi72. Each treatment was divided into two aliquots, and pH was adjusted to either 6.0 or 8.0 using 1 M HCl or 1 M KOH. All samples were then incubated at either 0 °C or 20 °C for 24 h with continuous shaking. Oxidation was terminated by the addition of 1% butylated hydroxytoluene. All samples were dialyzed at 4 °C for 48 h using 10 kDa molecular weight cutoff membranes to remove unreacted small molecules. The resulting solutions were lyophilized and stored at 4 °C for subsequent analysis.

Fluorescence spectroscopy

Intrinsic fluorescence spectra of actomyosin were recorded at ambient temperature (25 ± 1 °C) using a fluorescence spectrophotometer (RF-5301, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan), following the method of Zhu et al.20. A blank sample consisting of KCl buffer (1.2 M, 20 mM K2HPO4, 20 mM KH2PO4, pH 7.0) without protein was used for baseline correction. For each measurement, 2 mL of actomyosin solution (0.5 mg/mL, including both control and oxidized samples) was transferred to a 1 cm path length quartz cuvette. The excitation wavelength was set at 283 nm, and emission spectra were scanned from 300 to 450 nm. Both excitation and emission slit widths were maintained at 5 nm. Detector sensitivity was set to automatic mode.

Synchronous fluorescence spectra were recorded by maintaining a constant wavelength interval (Δλ) between excitation (λex) and emission (λem) wavelengths. Two Δλ values were applied: 15 nm for tyrosine (Tyr) and 60 nm for tryptophan (Trp). Both excitation and emission slit widths were set to 2.5 nm. Emission spectra were scanned from 220 to 450 nm using an excitation wavelength of 280 nm.

UV–Vis spectral analysis

UV–Vis absorption spectra of actomyosin were recorded at ambient temperature (25 ± 1 °C) using a UV-1800 spectrophotometer (Shimadzu Co., Ltd., Kyoto, Japan), following the method of Zhang et al.21. A blank was prepared with 1.2 M KCl buffer (20 mM K2HPO4, 20 mM KH2PO4, pH 7.0) without protein. For each measurement, 2 mL of actomyosin solution (0.5 mg/mL) was placed in a quartz cuvette with a 1 cm path length. Spectra were scanned from 200 to 450 nm at a rate of 50 nm/min.

EEM

Three-dimensional fluorescence spectra of actomyosin were recorded at ambient temperature (25 ± 1 °C) using a fluorescence spectrophotometer (F-4500, Hitachi, Japan), following the method of Zhang et al.21. A blank was prepared using 1.2 M KCl buffer (20 mM K2HPO4, 20 mM KH2PO4, pH 7.0) without protein. For each measurement, 2 mL of actomyosin solution (0.1 mg/mL, including control and oxidized samples) was placed in a quartz cuvette with a 1 cm path length. Excitation and emission slit widths were set at 5 nm and 10 nm, respectively. Excitation wavelengths were scanned from 200 to 400 nm, and emission wavelengths from 200 to 500 nm. The slit widths were set at 5 nm for excitation and 10 nm for emission.

CLSM

The microstructure of actomyosin was observed using a CLSM (FV1200, Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan), as described by Niu et al.30. A total of 400 μL of protein solution (1 mg/mL) was thoroughly mixed with 110 μL of FITC dye solution (1 mg/mL) and incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 30 min. After staining, 20 μL of the mixture was placed on a two-well microscope slide, and a coverslip was gently applied, ensuring no air bubbles remained between the sample and the coverslip. The microstructure was then visualized using an excitation wavelength of 488 nm, an emission range of 500–550 nm, and a magnification of ×100.

MD simulations

Molecular docking between grass carp myosin and the small molecules hemin and MDA was performed using AutoDock Vina73. The amino acid sequence of grass carp myosin was obtained from the UniProt database (UniProtKB ID: B0FYX7) and modified as necessary based on the work of Yang et al.74. Among the structural models available, the AlphaFold-predicted structure was used as the receptor due to its most complete residue coverage. The molecular structures of MDA and hemin were obtained from the ZINC database (http://zinc.docking.org/substances/ZINC000005178380/) and the RCSB PDB database (https://www.rcsb.org/), respectively, as previously reported by Shen et al.75 and Zhu et al.20. Prior to docking, ligand charges were assigned using Gasteiger charges in AutoDock Tools 1.5.6, and all ligand structures were converted to PDBQT format. The myosin structure was similarly converted after adding polar hydrogen atoms and calculating charges. The entire protein was defined as the docking search space. A grid box of 94 × 68 × 92 Å³ was set with a center at (x, y, z) = (6.232, 4.065, 3.495), and the configuration files were generated accordingly. Ten independent docking runs were conducted, and the conformation with the highest binding affinity was selected for subsequent analysis. Protein-ligand interactions were visualized and analyzed using Discovery Studio Client (version 2021, Dassault Systèmes BIOVIA, San Diego, CA, USA) and PyMOL (version 2.5, Schrödinger, LLC, New York, NY, USA; https://www.pymol.org, including structure display), interaction analysis, and trajectory animation. All software used in this study was properly licensed for academic research purposes. MD simulations were carried out using GROMACS 202176. The AMBER99SB-ILDN force field was applied to the protein, and the General AMBER Force Field77 was used for the small molecules. Four systems were constructed: (1) myosin alone (NC), (2) myosin–MDA complex (MDA), (3) myosin–hemin complex (Hemin), and (4) myosin–MDA–hemin complex (MDA/Hemin). Each selected docking conformation was placed at the center of a cubic simulation box with a minimum distance of 1.2 nm between the complex and the box edges. The systems were solvated in 0.15 M NaCl and electrically neutralized. Energy minimization was performed using the steepest descent algorithm (50,000 steps; convergence criterion of 1000 kJ mol−1 nm−1). Position-restrained equilibration was conducted for 500 ps under both NVT and NPT ensembles at 298.15 K and 1 atm. A 100 ns production run was performed with a 2 fs time step, and snapshots were saved every 100 ps. Temperature and pressure were controlled using the V-rescale thermostat and Parrinello-Rahman barostat, respectively. Neighbor searching employed the Verlet cutoff scheme, with a cutoff of 1.2 nm for both Coulombic and van der Waals interactions. Long-range electrostatics were handled using the Particle Mesh Ewald method. Periodic boundary conditions were applied, and the LINCS algorithm was used to constrain hydrogen bonds, enhancing simulation stability and accuracy. Binding free energy (ΔE(Binding)) was calculated using the MM/PBSA method implemented in GROMACS 2021 with the g_mmpbsa package78. For the myosin–MDA–hemin system, non-specific interactions between ligands were corrected during the calculation. Statistical analysis

All software used in this study was properly licensed for academic research purposes. Graphing and data visualization were performed using Origin 2021 (OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA), and all experiments were conducted in triplicate.
