Real-world pharmacokinetics of venetoclax in patients with acute myeloid leukemia in Japan
Authors: Minoru Kanaya, Yuji Mukai, Goichi Yoshimoto, Mirei Kobayashi, Sayaka Kajikawa, Toma Suzuki, Naoki Miyashita, Shinpei Harada, Takashi Ishio, Emi Yokoyama, Koh Izumiyama, Makoto Saito, Masanobu Morioka, Akio Mori, Toshihiro Matsukawa, Masahiro Onozawa, Mitsuru Moriyama, SungGi Chi, Kosuke Doki, Takanori Teshima, Yosuke Minami, Masato Homma, Takeshi Kondo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•Plasma VEN concentration in patients with AML is potentially helpful in predicting prolonged neutropenia.•A low WBC count and impaired renal function heighten the risk of elevated VEN, causing prolonged neutropenia.

Introduction

Historically, the prognosis of acute myeloid leukemia (AML) in older patients ineligible for intensive chemotherapy has been poor.1 Venetoclax (VEN), an orally bioavailable B-cell lymphoma 2 (BCL-2) inhibitor, demonstrated modest activity as a single agent in patients with refractory and relapsed AML, even at higher doses of 1200 or 800 mg, which are higher than the standard 400 mg dose.2 However, VEN administered in combination with azacitidine (AZA)3 or low-dose cytarabine (LDAC)4 showed robust efficacy in patients with untreated AML who were ineligible for intensive chemotherapy, suggesting a synergistic effect of VEN when combined with AZA. Nevertheless, severe and prolonged neutropenia was identified as one of the most critical adverse events of both regimens.2 Such prolonged neutropenia leads to opportunistic infections, ultimately hindering the achievement of longer treatment cycles. Therefore, prevention of neutropenia is crucial for achieving better outcomes in VEN-containing regimens; however, definitive risk factors for prolonged neutropenia remain poorly understood. Several approaches have been explored to mitigate prolonged neutropenia, including shortening the duration of VEN administration5, 6, 7 and implementing therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM).8, 9, 10

The pharmacokinetics (PK) of VEN could potentially be a key factor in predicting neutropenia. The most extensive analysis of VEN PK to date involved nearly 800 samples from 5 phase 1 to 3 clinical trials of VEN in various hematological malignancies, including AML.8 The aforementioned study, in patients with AML, demonstrated no significant exposure-response relationship between VEN exposure and the probability of achieving a complete response (CR) or complete remission with incomplete hematological recovery (CRi) in AZA-VEN and VEN-LDAC regimens. Regarding safety, a shallow, although not statistically significant, relationship was observed between VEN exposure and treatment-emergent grade ≥3 neutropenia. Interestingly, the study also reported that Asian patients tended to have higher plasma VEN concentrations and were more likely to experience treatment-emergent grade ≥3 neutropenia. Moreover, VEN in combination with hypomethylating agent regimens has been associated with neutropenia and thrombocytopenia, with the latter often being prolonged in patients receiving concomitant azole antifungal prophylaxis.11

Based on these observations, we hypothesized that PK evaluation of VEN is necessary, especially in Asian populations, for managing adverse events associated with VEN combination therapy for AML, particularly AZA-VEN. Accordingly, we conducted this PK study for AZA-VEN/LDAC-VEN regimens to elucidate risk factors for high plasma VEN concentrations and, ultimately, for prolonged neutropenia.

Methods

Study designs

We conducted a prospective observational study to monitor the PK of VEN and to analyze its impact on treatment efficacy and the incidence of neutropenia. Adult patients (aged ≥18 years) with newly diagnosed or relapsed/refractory AML, who were candidates for treatment with either AZA-VEN or VEN-LDAC, were enrolled. The AZA-VEN regimen consisted of VEN (400 mg daily, with dose adjustments for concomitant azole antifungal use as described below) and AZA (75 mg/m2 subcutaneously daily for 7 days). Similarly, the VEN-LDAC regimen involved VEN (600 mg daily, with dose adjustments for concomitant azole antifungal use) and LDAC (20 mg/m2 subcutaneously daily for 10 days). VEN doses were adjusted for concomitant use of azole antifungal agents according to published recommendations.12,13 Specifically, in the AZA-VEN arm, the VEN dose was reduced from 400 to 50 mg for patients concomitantly receiving voriconazole or posaconazole, and to 200 mg for those receiving fluconazole and isavuconazole. The Japanese prescribing guidelines for VEN (from AbbVie) strongly recommend a dose adjustment from 400 to 50 mg when coadministering strong CYP3A4 inhibitors, such as voriconazole and posaconazole, a distinct approach from that adopted in the United States and Europe. This study was conducted in collaboration with the HM-SCREEN (managed by the National Cancer Center Hospital East, Japan) and Hokkaido Leukemia Net. The study protocol adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the ethics committee of Aiiku Hospital (University Hospital Medical Information Network [UMIN] registry number: UMIN000047371).

Definitions

Response to therapy was evaluated according to the 2022 European LeukemiaNet (ELN) criteria.14 The composite complete remission (CRc) rate was defined as the sum of CR and CRi. For the analysis focused on patients with CRc, we selected those who achieved or maintained CRc after VEN-containing regimens, irrespective of their pretreatment disease status, and for whom VEN PK analysis was performed during the corresponding treatment cycle. Patients without CR were defined as those who did not achieve CRc after VEN-containing regimens, and whose PK samples were collected during the relevant treatment cycle. Adverse events, including neutropenia and thrombocytopenia, were assessed and graded according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events, version 5.0. The duration of cytopenia was defined as the time interval from the onset of blood cell count decrease to the first day of confirmed recovery to above a predefined threshold. The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using serum creatinine levels, patient age, and sex.15

PK study

Key PK parameters assessed included trough concentration, maximum plasma concentration (Cmax), and the area under the plasma concentration–time curve from 0 to 12 hours (AUC0-12). Theoretically, VEN concentrations are anticipated to achieve steady state within 7 days of dosing. Therefore, blood samples for the determination of VEN plasma concentration were collected by venipuncture into potassium EDTA–containing tubes on, or after, day 7 of VEN administration. Samples were obtained immediately before VEN dosing (0 hours [C0] or trough) and at 6 hours (C6), and 12 hours (C12) after dosing. The PK data generally consisted of 3 samples (C0, C6, and C12) per patient, which we refer to as a "3-point PK data set." Multiple PK measurements in the same patient were permitted by the study protocol. Plasma VEN concentrations were determined using a liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry method.16,17 PK parameters for VEN at steady state (after day 7) were estimated using noncompartmental methods in R (version 4.3.2) with the “ncar” package (version 0.4.5). Estimated parameters included Cmax, Tmax (time to maximum plasma concentration), and AUC0-12. For the statistical analyses of PK parameters, the PROC MIXED procedure in SAS software, version 9.4, was used.

Statistical analysis

Correlations between clinical parameters and clinical outcomes after VEN treatment were assessed using the Pearson product-moment correlation coefficient. For comparisons between 2 groups with data assumed to follow a Gaussian distribution, paired or unpaired t tests were used, as appropriate. All P values were 2-sided, and statistical significance was set at P value <.05. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.4.2 (GraphPad Software, LLC, San Diego, CA).

Results

A total of 76 Japanese patients with newly diagnosed (n = 53) or relapsed/refractory (n = 23) AML were enrolled. All 76 patients contributed at least 1 3-point PK data set. An additional 5 patients provided a second set, resulting in a total of 81 samples in the 3-point PK data sets for analysis. Overall, 241 blood samples were collected for VEN concentration measurement, with 2 samples missing from the final evaluation.

Baseline patient characteristics (n = 76) and the 3-point PK data set (n = 81 samples) are summarized in Tables 1 and 2. Of these samples, 53 (69.6%) were from patients with newly diagnosed AML, and 23 (30.3%) were from patients with relapsed/refractory AML. Most samples (n = 75 [98.7%]) were obtained from patients receiving the AZA-VEN regimen, whereas only 1 sample (1.3%) was from a patient treated with the VEN-LDAC regimen. Median VEN cycles with blood concentration monitoring was 1 (range, 1-12). The number of samples collected during the first cycle of VEN-containing regimens was 58 (71.6%). Among 3-point PK data set, 44 (54.3%) data sets were from patients who had received concomitant antifungal prophylaxis. Specifically, 13 (16.0%) patients in the PK data set received fluconazole or voriconazole, and 29 (35.8%) received echinocandin-based antifungal prophylaxis. Granulocyte colony-stimulating factor was administrated to 48 (59.3%) within the PK data set. After VEN-containing treatment, CRc was achieved in patients corresponding to 44 samples (54.3% of the 81 analyzed samples, assuming all were evaluable for response). To investigate a potential association between VEN PK and hematological adverse events, we evaluated the correlation between the duration of grade ≥3 neutropenia (neutrophil count of <1000/μL) and VEN PK parameters (trough, Cmax, and AUC0-12). This specific analysis focused on patients within the PK data set who achieved CRc (n = 44), comprising CR patients (n = 38) and CRi patients (n = 6), after the VEN-containing regimen. A significant positive correlation was observed between the duration of grade ≥3 neutropenia and AUC0-12 (Pearson r = 0.338; P = .028). However, no significant correlations were found with VEN trough concentrations (Pearson r = 0.252; P = .108), Cmax values (Pearson r = 0.276; P = .070; Figure 3), or C6 values (Pearson r = 0.293; P = .056; supplemental Figure 4). In addition to grade 3 neutropenia, we investigated the relationship between grade 4 neutropenia and VEN PK. No correlation was observed between the duration of grade 4 neutropenia and VEN PK (supplemental Figure 5). We also evaluated the correlation between grade 3 thrombocytopenia (platelet count of <50 000/μL to 25 000/μL) and VEN PK parameters, specifically among CR cases. Next, we stratified the PK data set into 2 groups: 1 characterized by neutrophil recovery within 28 days, and the other by prolonged neutrophil recovery (beyond 28 days). We then compared these groups with respect to VEN PK parameters. No significant differences in these VEN PK parameters were observed between the 2 groups (supplemental Figure 6).

Subsequently, we investigated potential risk factors for higher plasma VEN concentrations. We considered the hypothesis that a high tumor burden, potentially reflected by white blood cell count (WBC) might correlate with increased BCL-2 expression and subsequently greater VEN consumption, whereas renal impairment (indicated by eGFR) has been suggested to affect CYP3A4 activity, potentially by suppressing hepatic enzymes.17

Based on these considerations, patients who achieved CRc (n = 42) were stratified using baseline WBC counts and eGFR. First, we assessed the correlation between eGFR and plasma VEN concentrations. As we anticipated, eGFR was inversely correlated with plasma VEN concentrations, particularly Cmax and AUC0-12 (r = ˗0.377, P = .012 and r = ˗0.328, P = .034, respectively; supplemental Figure 7). Next, cutoffs were defined by the median baseline WBC count (3000/μL) and eGFR (60 mL/min per 1.73 m2). This stratification categorized patients into a “WBClow/eGFRlow” group (n = 9) and a combined “others” group (comprising WBChigh/eGFRhigh, WBChigh/eGFRlow, and WBClow/eGFRhigh; n = 33). Patients in the WBClow/eGFRlow group exhibited significantly higher AUC0-12 values (P = .037) than those in the combined “others” group. However, no significant differences were observed for plasma VEN trough concentrations or Cmax values between these groups (Figure 5A-C). In summary, these data indicate that patients with both low WBC counts and low eGFR at baseline experienced higher systemic VEN exposure (AUC0-12) and more prolonged neutropenia. This profile may represent an important risk factor for increased toxicity in patients with AML receiving VEN-containing regimens.

Discussion

This study represents, to our knowledge, one of the largest real-world investigations (76 patients, 241 samples, and an 81-sample PK data set) of VEN PK to date. We demonstrated that VEN PK, particularly the AUC0-12, was associated with neutrophil recovery after AZA-VEN treatment for AML, consistent with previous reports.8,10 We identified that patients with both low baseline WBC counts and low eGFR were at risk for higher VEN exposure and, consequently, prolonged neutropenia. In contrast to our findings, a large cohort PK study from clinical trials by AbbVie8 reported that mild to severe renal impairment did not appear to significantly affect VEN exposure, suggesting that the impact of renal impairment alone on plasma VEN concentrations might be minimal. However, it is recognized that nonrenal drug clearance can be affected by decreased renal drug clearance, because renal failure may influence hepatic drug metabolism by either inducing or suppressing hepatic enzymes.19, 20, For instance, in vitro experiments demonstrated that the activity of CYP3A4, the primary enzyme for VEN elimination, was reduced by ∼80% in human liver cells when cocultured with serum from patients with chronic kidney disease.21 This finding indicated that hepatic drug clearance via CYP3A4 could be impaired by chronic kidney disease.19, 20, 21 Regarding the WBC count, we hypothesized that BCL-2 inhibitors might be consumed more readily in patients with a high tumor burden, potentially leading to lower systemic VEN concentrations. Theoretically, a high leukemic tumor burden could lead to increased expression of the target BCL-2 protein, resulting in greater VEN binding and drug consumption, thereby potentially decreasing plasma VEN concentrations. Although these hypotheses require further validation, our findings suggest that the combination of low circulating WBCs (potentially indicating a low tumor burden) and impaired renal function may serve as a useful marker for predicting higher VEN exposure and prolonged neutropenia.

We considered it desirable to focus on clinically important and frequently occurring adverse events to leverage VEN blood concentrations in clinical practice. The most clinically critical adverse event in VEN management is cytopenia. In this study, 37 of 44 cases (84%) achieving CRc experienced grade 4 neutropenia, whereas all 44 cases experienced grade 3 neutropenia. Grade 3 and 4 thrombocytopenia was observed in 57% and 39% of cases, respectively. We therefore concluded that it would be appropriate to select grade 3 neutropenia, the most frequently occurring hematological adverse event in patients, as the primary end point for this study.

In this Japanese real-world cohort, plasma VEN concentrations appeared to be higher than those reported in several previous studies.8,9,12,22, 23, 24 This observation underscores the potential importance of evaluating VEN PK specifically in Asian patients, who may tend to exhibit higher plasma VEN concentrations. Consistent with a previous report,8 our data indicate that AUC0-12, rather than trough concentrations or Cmax, better reflects hematological toxicities. Although Kawedia et al9 demonstrated that VEN trough levels correlated well with AUC, our analysis of the correlation between AUC0-12 and C0, C6, or C12 revealed that C6 showed the strongest correlation with AUC0-12. Our analysis showed that VEN trough levels trended to correlate with grade 3 neutropenia, but there was no strong correlation observed between trough levels and grade 3 thrombocytopenia. In contrast to the trough level, Cmax and C6 demonstrated a better correlation with both neutropenia and thrombocytopenia. Therefore, these parameters represent strong candidates for use as surrogates for AUC. Although trough level monitoring is often considered more practical than AUC, C6, and Cmax determination for TDM, further PK analyses are warranted to clarify and optimize VEN TDM strategies.

Regarding the coadministration of VEN with antifungal drugs, particularly CYP3A4-inhibiting triazoles, we compared VEN concentrations among 3 groups: patients without concomitant antifungal therapy, those receiving triazoles, and those receiving echinocandins. Consequently, no significant differences were observed in VEN trough, Cmax, and AUC0-12 values among these 3 groups in our cohort. It is common practice in Japan to reduce the VEN dose from 400 mg to 50 mg when coadministered with potent CYP3A4 inhibitors, such as voriconazole and posaconazole. We interpret this finding to mean that the standard dose adjustments, as also suggested by Kawedia et al,9 were effective in preventing excessive VEN accumulation. This speculation is supported by a PK study from India, in which VEN 50 mg with posaconazole demonstrated response rates and adverse events comparable with those with standard VEN 400 mg without any CYP3A4 inhibitors.25 However, it is important to note that only 17% of the total cohort received azole antifungal agents, and most of these cases involved fluconazole. Furthermore, because the sample size is relatively small, this conclusion must be considered with certain limitations.

In terms of therapeutic efficacy, our data demonstrated no clear relationship between plasma VEN concentrations and the CRc rate. The sensitivity of AML to AZA-VEN is often stratified by genetic risk classifiers specific to VEN-containing regimens such as prognostic risk signatures and ELN 2024 Less-Intensive, which differ from the ELN 2022 risk classification system.26, 27, 28 This may explain why we did not observe a significant difference in VEN concentrations between the CRc and non-CRc groups.

This study has several limitations. First, limitations in health insurance coverage in Japan restricted comprehensive genetic testing for all patients. Routine somatic mutation testing was limited to FLT3-ITD and NPM1, which is insufficient for complete risk stratification according to ELN 2022 criteria or ELN 2024 Less-Intensive criteria. Because this study did not primarily focus on survival outcomes, we believe the impact of this limitation on our main PK findings was not substantial. Second, although this multicenter prospective study included 76 patients (81 samples), the sample size may not have been sufficient to definitively evaluate all aspects of the clinical significance of VEN PK. Further real-world evidence with larger cohorts is needed for a deeper understanding of the clinical implications of VEN PK. Third, this cohort included patients treated with both AZA-VEN and VEN-LDAC. However, only 1 patient (1 sample) received the VEN-LDAC regimen. Therefore, we believe our findings predominantly reflect VEN PK within the more widely used AZA-VEN regimen.

In conclusion, this study provides new insights into VEN PK in patients with AML receiving VEN-containing regimens, primarily AZA-VEN. Our findings highlight the need for careful attention to patients presenting with both low WBC counts and low eGFR at baseline, because they may be at higher risk for increased VEN exposure and associated toxicity. We suggest that VEN PK monitoring may be warranted to minimize hematological adverse events and achieve safer AZA-VEN management in patients with AML.
