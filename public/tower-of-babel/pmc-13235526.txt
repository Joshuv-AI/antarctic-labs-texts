Bivalent RSVpreF effectiveness against RSV-associated hospital or emergency department care over two seasons: a retrospective test-negative case control study
Authors: Sara Y. Tartof, Negar Aliabadi, Evan Zasowski, Gabriella Goodwin, Jeff M. Slezak, Vennis Hong, Timothy B. ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundSince RSV vaccine licensure in 2023, vaccine effectiveness (VE) studies demonstrated first season protection against severe RSV disease including RSV-related hospitalization/emergency department (ED) visits. Here, we estimate VE over two seasons, by RSV subtype, and for additional high-risk subgroups.MethodsThis retrospective test-negative case–control study included adults ≥60 years at Kaiser Permanente Southern California with lower respiratory tract disease (LRTD) or acute respiratory illness (ARI) hospitalization/ED during 24 November 2023─09 April 2024 and 05 November 2024─26 April 2025. In primary analyses, cases were RSV-positive and controls were negative for: RSV, SARS-CoV-2, influenza, and positive for a non-vaccine-preventable pathogen. Exposure was bivalent RSVpreF receipt ≥21 days before LRTD/ARI. VE was calculated using adjusted odds ratios from multivariable logistic regression. “First season after vaccination” includes events in the same RSV season as vaccination; “second season” includes events in 2024–25 among those vaccinated in 2023–24.FindingsVE against RSV-associated LRTD hospitalizations/ED was 81% (95% CI: 68–89) and 76% (95% CI: 48–89) for first and second seasons after vaccination, respectively. Limiting to those vaccinated in 2023/2024, VE was 87% (95% CI: 37–97) 0–<6 and 77% (95% CI: 54–89) 6–18 months post-vaccination. Protection persisted across both seasons for adults aged ≥75 years, those with risk factors, frailty, immunocompromise, and by RSV A/B. VE in immunocompromised adults appeared to decrease more quickly: 74% (95% CI: 37–89) first season to 54% (95% CI: −48 to 85) second season.InterpretationRSVpreF vaccination was associated with protection against RSV-associated severe respiratory illness, overall and among the most vulnerable patients. VE appeared to decline modestly across two seasons in most populations, this finding was more pronounced among immunocompromised adults. Our VE estimates indicate potentially substantial public health benefit of RSV vaccination.FundingThis was a Pfizer-sponsored study.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV) is a leading cause of severe respiratory infections in older adults, leading to substantial morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs. To evaluate evidence before this study, we conducted a search on PubMed and Embase from inception to 07 May 2026 using the following search terms: RSV vaccine AND (effectiveness OR efficacy) AND adult. Clinical trials have shown the efficacy of RSV vaccines in preventing RSV-related lower respiratory tract disease (LRTD) across multiple RSV seasons but were not powered to demonstrate efficacy against emergency department (ED) visits or hospitalization. Multiple real-world vaccine effectiveness (VE) studies have demonstrated that protein subunit RSV vaccines (i.e., bivalent RSVpreF, RSVpreF3) are effective against severe LRTD and acute respiratory illness (ARI) outcomes, including those requiring hospitalization/ED, and effective among key patient populations of interest, such as immunocompromised individuals and among older age groups through the first RSV season post-vaccination. Data are beginning to emerge regarding real-world VE of protein subunit RSV vaccines across two RSV seasons. In one prospective CDC study, VE against RSV-related ARI hospitalizations decreased from 69% to 48% in the first and second seasons after vaccination, respectively, among the overall study population, and from 54% to 11% among the immunocompromised. A separate CDC study showed VE against RSV-associated hospitalization declining from 84% to 41% over two RSV seasons among all older adults, and from 75 to 44% among the immunocompromised. In a Veterans Healthcare study, VE against documented RSV infection decreased from 83% to 58% from the first month of vaccination over 18 months among the overall population, and from 75% to 40% among the immunocompromised. While these studies employed different methodology and populations, they all demonstrated that VE declines over time, and to a greater extent among the immunocompromised.

Added value of this study

This study evaluated bivalent RSVpreF VE across two seasons against RSV-related hospitalization/ED visits with ARI or LRTD in a population with high quality vaccine exposure data. Further, swab salvage was used to boost the representativeness of the tested cohort. It provides a comprehensive evaluation of RSVpreF VE by time since vaccination that showed modest decline over 18 months. The findings extend results from a single season, demonstrating sustained RSVpreF effectiveness within key subgroups of interest, such as immunocompromised individuals and among older age groups and by RSV subtype. Beyond duration of protection, we also add novel data on effectiveness among frail adults and among subgroups of adults age 60–74 years who are defined by the Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices as high risk for severe RSV.

Implications of all the available evidence

Bivalent RSVpreF confers protection against severe respiratory illness due to RSV, across two seasons, including among those most vulnerable. The estimated public health and economic impact from RSV vaccination on averted hospitalization/ED encounters across multiple RSV seasons could be substantial. This underscores the importance of RSV vaccination as a routine preventive health measure among vulnerable populations and efforts to promote widespread vaccination coverage in these populations is warranted. Although confidence intervals were wide in the second season among the immunocompromised, these findings support further study of revaccination timing, particularly in this high-risk population.

Introduction

Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV) is a leading cause of severe respiratory infection in older adults resulting in substantial morbidity, mortality, and healthcare cost. Estimates in older adults indicate RSV causes over 159,000 hospitalizations and 119,000 emergency department visits (ED) annually in the U.S. and as many as 787,000 hospitalizations and between 22,000–47,000 in-hospital deaths annually in high-income countries.1,2 While already high, these values likely underestimate the true RSV burden because published incidence data can include infrequent testing in older adults, low assay sensitivity, frequent reliance on single specimen collection, and use of case definitions that exclude some RSV disease (e.g., influenza-like illness which requires fever).1, 2, 3, 4, 5 Older adults are at higher risk of severe RSV disease, including hospitalization and other serious outcomes. Among older adults hospitalized, 15–25% require intensive care unit admission and/or respiratory support6, 7, 8, 9 and 4–8% may die. 7, 8, 9 Further, mortality within one year of RSV hospitalization may be as high as 25%.9 In addition to the clinical burden of RSV, the annual economic burden of RSV in older adults is estimated to be in the billions in the U.S. alone.10,11

Preventive measures against RSV disease in older adults were limited until vaccines were approved by the US Food and Drug Administration and European Medicines Agency in 2023. These vaccines, including RSVpreF (Pfizer's Prefusion F protein-based bivalent protein subunit RSV vaccine), showed efficacy in preventing RSV lower respiratory tract disease (LRTD) across multiple RSV seasons in phase III clinical trials.12, 13, 14, 15 Notably, the trials did not accrue enough ED visits or hospitalizations to generate efficacy estimates against these severe outcomes. Since licensure, multiple real-world vaccine effectiveness (VE) studies have evaluated VE against more severe outcomes such as hospitalizations for RSV-related acute respiratory illnesses (ARI), LRTD, or cardiorespiratory illnesses; hospitalization events with supplemental oxygen use, critical illness, or death; and among important subgroups such as immunocompromised individuals, those at high risk of severe RSV disease, and older age groups.16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22 While these studies have documented that protein subunit vaccines (i.e., bivalent RSVpreF, RSVpreF3) confer protection against severe outcomes at the end of the first season post-vaccine implementation, limited data are available on durability of protection and VE by RSV subtype and confirmation of existing findings in different healthcare systems with distinct sociodemographic distribution and geography and among additional clinically important subgroups (e.g., by frailty status) are needed.18,23

The objective of this analysis was to extend the evaluation of RSVpreF VE across two seasons, assessing VE against RSV-related hospitalization/ED visits with ARI or LRTD, overall and by time points since vaccination. The study was conducted in a setting with access to high quality vaccine exposure data and swab salvage to boost the representativeness of the tested cohort because of limited clinical testing to identify RSV. We additionally evaluated VE against ARI/LRTD among key subgroups, including by RSV subtype, age categories and high-risk subgroups (e.g., frail), as well as against severe outcomes.

Methods

This was a retrospective test-negative case–control study of adults aged ≥60 years at Kaiser Permanente Southern California (KPSC) with an ARI or LRTD hospitalization/ED visit during 24 Nov 2023─9 Apr 2024 or 5 Nov 2024─26 Apr 2025. KPSC is an integrated healthcare system with more than 4.7 million members reflective of the racial/ethnic and sociodemographic diversity of the underlying population.24,25 The index date for the ARI or LRTD hospitalization/ED visit was defined as the date of admission for the encounter. Participants were required to have ≥1 year of health plan membership prior to the ARI or LRTD (allowing for a 45-day membership gap to account for delays in membership renewal) to determine medical history. ARI and LRTD were defined by ICD-10 discharge code (Supplemental Tables S1 and S2) in any position; LRTD is a subset of ARI and was the primary analysis population. We included LRTD/ARI hospitalizations/ED with RSV-tested nasal/nasopharyngeal swabs collected 14 days prior to 3 days after admission. Swabs were tested using Roche Diagnostics Cobas eplex RP2 multiplex respiratory pathogen panel, covering 16 viral and 2 bacterial targets. ED were defined as admission and discharge from the ED < 2 days. ED ≥ 2 days or with direct hospital transfer were defined as hospitalizations. Hospitalizations starting in inpatient settings required a duration ≥24 h.

The primary analysis included the first LRTD/ARI encounter per patient and per season. Multiple LRTD/ARI hospitalizations/ED comprised a single encounter if occurring within ≤30 days of each other.

To reduce bias associated with infrequent standard-of-care (SOC) RSV testing, this study included RSV results obtained from both SOC testing and from specimens salvaged from LRTD/ARI where testing was ordered for SARS-CoV-2 and/or influenza only. Salvage and SOC specimens were collected based on the same clinical criteria and tested on the same multiplex panel, and cases were defined as RSV positive without coinfection. RSV cases coinfected with another pathogen from the multiplex panel were excluded. Separate analyses were conducted with two sets of prespecified controls. The primary analysis used “strict” controls which were negative for RSV, human metapneumovirus (hMPV), and other vaccine preventable diseases (VPD) (influenza and SARS-CoV-2), and positive for ≥1 non-VPD pathogen [i.e., adenovirus, coronavirus (229E, HKU1, NL63, OC43), human rhinovirus/enterovirus, Parainfluenza 1–4, Chlamydia pneumoniae, Mycoplasma pneumoniae]. “Broad” controls were RSV-negative. The ‘strict’ approach was designed to reduce bias in VE estimates by: (1) mitigating reduced PCR sensitivity in older adults when only a single specimen is collected, through requiring detection of at least one non-VPD pathogen3; (2) limiting confounding from correlated behaviors that influence uptake of influenza and COVID-19 vaccines by excluding influenza- and SARS-CoV-2–positive controls26; and (3) accounting for potential cross-protection of hMPV by RSVpreF through exclusion of hMPV-positive controls.27

Exposure was RSVpreF receipt ≥21 days before the LRTD/ARI. This 21-day window was selected to mitigate the risk of including events from adults whose symptoms developed during the 14-day antibody development window and therefore had not yet had adequate time for vaccine to confer protection. As such an additional 7 days were added to account for viral incubation,28 symptom onset, and ED/hospital admission.29 Patients receiving non-RSVPreF RSV vaccine (e.g., RSVPreF3 or mRNA RSV vaccine) and those receiving RSVpreF <21 days before the ARI or LRTD were excluded. KPSC members received RSV vaccines at no cost. KPSC electronic health records captured all vaccinations administered within the health system and were supplemented with vaccine administration data from California's Immunization Registry (CAIR) to which healthcare providers are legally required to report RSV vaccinations.30 The small number of patients (n = 3) who received a second dose of RSVpreF were treated as vaccinated, with timing of vaccination starting at the date of first dose.

RSVpreF was administered at seven study hospitals and their 82 associated medical offices and was the sole RSV vaccine product at these locations. Other KPSC non-study hospitals (n = 8) were verified by internal comparisons to be equivalent regarding patient population demographics and clinical characteristics. There were no differences in vaccination practices between study and non-study hospitals. RSV unvaccinated hospitalization/ED events among an eligible population in non-study sites where RSV testing was conducted and RSVPreF was not available were also included.

Adjusted odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for first dose of RSVpreF were estimated from multivariable logistic regression and used to calculate VE (VE = (1−OR) x 100). Findings from phase 3 studies, expert opinion, and published studies of clinical/biologic factors were used to identify potential covariates for inclusion in the model. Covariates were assessed for completeness, distributions, and bivariate associations with the outcome, exposure, and each other. Variables were selected based on a combination of a priori decisions and a qualitative assessment of empirical relationships. Age, biological sex at birth, self-reported race/ethnicity, and Charlson comorbidity index were pre-specified as important confounders incorporated into all models. Other model-specified covariates are listed in Table 1, with immunocompromise defined in the Supplement.16,22 The only adjustment variable with missing values was race/ethnicity, which was combined as a Non-Hispanic Multiple/Other/Unknown category. Analyses were done separately for LRTD and ARI. COVID-19 vaccination was not included in models due to high collinearity with RSV vaccination (r = 0.78). VE was estimated against the first ARI or LRTD hospitalization/ED; by age; by race/ethnicity, immunocompromise, for those with severe ARI or LRTD (defined as hospitalization/ED events requiring oxygen supplementation); for those at high risk of severe disease based on underlying comorbidities (asthma, COPD, CHF, coronary artery disease, other chronic lung, cardiac, renal, and liver diseases, diabetes mellitus, neurologic conditions, stroke, autoimmune disorders, immunocompromising conditions and medications, HIV, AIDS, cancers, organ transplant, and blood disorders); by frailty status Results

Of 152,758 patients with LRTD hospitalization/ED at study sites during two RSV seasons (2023–2024 and 2024–2025), 88,547 (58.0%) had a nasal/nasopharyngeal swab collected; of these, 26,171 (29.6%) were RSV-tested (7896 salvage specimens, 18,275 SOC specimens). After applying eligibility criteria and including unvaccinated RSV-tested patients from non-study sites, 10,997 LRTD events with tested specimens were included in the broad control study population (Fig. 1), where 5382 (48.9%) were RSV-tested as part of SOC, and 5615 (51.1%) were salvaged. A total of 1521 (13.8%) patients with LRTD were vaccinated with RSVPreF across both seasons, with 159 (10.5%) vaccinations occurring during the 2023–2024 season and 1362 (89.5%) occurring in the 2024–2025 season. Overall, 24 of 760 cases (3.2%) and 1497 of 10,237 broad controls (14.6%) were vaccinated against RSV.Fig. 1Flowchart of Lower Respiratory Tract Disease Analytic Study Population, Kaiser Permanente Southern California, Combined Seasons 2023─2025. Overall, patient characteristics were similar between patients with salvaged versus SOC specimens. However, compared to SOC specimens, salvaged specimens were more likely to be from patients from racial or ethnic groups other than non-Hispanic White, those with ED presentation, those with higher NDI, and those receiving RSVpreF, and other factors (Supplemental Table S3). Compared to those tested for RSV, LRTD ED/hospitalizations with no swab collected or not RSV-tested were more likely to be from non-Hispanic White patients, were less likely to have supplemental oxygen given, and were more likely to be unvaccinated, among other factors. The proportion RSV positive between SOC (7.3%) and salvaged specimens (6.5%) was similar.

The majority of the LRTD population had comorbidities (93.8% had ≥1 Charlson comorbidity), 55.8% had chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), 22.6% were immunocompromised, and 61.1% were aged ≥75 years (Table 1). Patients with an RSV-related LRTD hospitalization/ED were more likely to be female and older compared to strict and broad controls and more likely to be hospitalized compared to strict controls. Patients with LRTD receiving RSVPreF were older and had a higher Charlson comorbidity index than those who remained unvaccinated. The ARI population had similar characteristics and differences by case–control (Supplemental Table S4) and RSVPreF vaccination status.

Of the 10,727 LRTD hospitalizations/ED encounters that tested negative for RSV, 9170 episodes were ineligible for the strict control group: 1123 tested positive for hMPV or a VPD (321 hMPV, 279 SARS-CoV-2, and 543 influenza; non-mutually exclusive) and 8047 tested negative for all pathogens. Of the 1557 eligible episodes that tested positive for a non-VPD pathogen, 15 were removed after restricting to the first episode per patient per season, leaving 1542 episodes in the final strict control group. The distribution of patient characteristics was similar to those of the broad controls (Table 1). The strict analysis cohort included 2317 LRTD events. Overall, 25 of 775 cases (3.2%) and 275 of 1542 strict controls (17.8%) were vaccinated against RSV (Table 1).

First season after vaccination

Comparing 767 cases to 1468 strict controls, adjusted VE against LRTD was 81% (95% CI: 68–89) with vaccinees receiving RSVpreF a median 84 (IQR: 52–126) days before LRTD (Fig. 2). VE against severe LRTD was 85% (95% CI: 71–92) using strict controls. In the analysis comparing 752 cases to 9836 broad controls (i.e., all RSV-negative controls), adjusted VE was similar: 80% (95% CI: 67–88), with vaccinees receiving RSVpreF a median 84 (IQR: 51–122) days before LRTD. VE remained high among the oldest age groups (≥75 years: 81% [65–90]), immunocompromised patients (74% [37–89]), highly frail patients (65% [29–83]), and against both RSV subtypes (A: 77% [56–88]; B: 79% [52–90]) (Fig. 2). Using strict controls, VE against RSV-associated ARI hospitalization/ED in the first season after vaccination was 83% (95% CI: 73–89) with a median 81 (IQR: 53–126) days before ARI, similar to that observed for LRTD, and 83% (95% CI: 73–89) when using broad controls (Supplemental Figure S1). Second season after vaccination

Comparing 324 cases to 765 strict controls, adjusted VE against LRTD was 76% (95% CI: 48–89) with vaccinees receiving RSVpreF a median of 372 (IQR: 342–403) days before LRTD (Fig. 2). VE against severe LRTD was 76% (95% CI: 42–91) using strict controls. In an analysis comparing 313 cases to 4772 broad controls (i.e., all RSV-negative controls), adjusted VE was similar, 71% (95% CI: 41–86), with vaccinees receiving RSVpreF a median 384 (IQR: 342–428) days before LRTD. VE remained similar in magnitude among those ≥75 years: 76% [40–90])), highly frail patients (81% [15–96]), and against both RSV subtypes (A: 76% [41–90]; B: 77% [2–95]) (Fig. 2). The point estimate for VE among immunocompromised patients appeared to decline more rapidly over time than the immunocompetent (immunocompromised 2nd season VE: 54% [−48 to 85] and 1st season VE: 74% [37–89] versus immunocompetent 2nd season VE: 86% [55–96] and 1st season VE: 86% [72–93]), although confidence intervals were wide for the immunocompromised second season. VE against the main ARI outcomes as well as stratified VE estimates were similar to those against LRTD (Supplemental Figure S1). VE against RSV-associated LRTD in the hospital setting (84% [54─95]) was higher than the main estimate for the combined hospital/ED setting, while VE against LRTD in the ED setting was lower (60% [−33 to 88]). Similarly, VE against RSV-associated ARI in the hospital setting was higher (76% [41─90]) than for the ED outcome (62% [6─85) (Supplemental Table S5). However, the confidence intervals were wide for the ED outcomes.

Time since vaccination

There were 761 cases and 1374 strict controls vaccinated in the 2023/2024 RSV season included in the analyses of VE durability over time. Among those, VE declined from 87% (37–97) from 0 to <6 months following vaccination to 77% (54–89) in the 6–18 months following vaccination (Fig. 3). In an analysis using broad controls, VE similarly declined from 84% (34–96) to 71% (43–85). VE against ARI also declined, from 89% (52–98) from 0 to <6 months following vaccination to 70% (46–83) in the 6–18 months following vaccination with strict controls. Combined seasons VE

Across both RSV seasons combined, and including vaccinations across both seasons, overall VE against LRTD hospitalization/ED and severe LRTD using strict controls was 80% (69–87), and 82% (69–89%), respectively (Supplemental Figure S2). Using broad controls, VE was similar: 78% (66–86) and 81% (67–89) against LRTD hospitalization/ED and severe LRTD, respectively. VE remained comparable to overall estimates among most strata, however with somewhat lower VE among immunocompromised patients (69% [36–85]) and those with high frailty (70% [41–84]). VE estimates against ARI hospitalization/ED were largely consistent with those for LRTD (Supplemental Figure S3).

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses using an exposure definition of RSVpreF receipt ≥14 days prior to ARI or LRTD, using data from study sites only, and with estimation modeling splines for age and date of encounter did not result in meaningful changes to VE estimates, with highly overlapping confidence intervals (Supplemental Table S6).

Discussion

Bivalent RSVpreF demonstrated protection against both ARI and LRTD hospitalizations/ED visits due to RSV in the first and second seasons after vaccination, across combined seasons, and by time since vaccination. The burden of comorbidities among those with ARI and LRTD events was very high, and protection was also demonstrated among the most vulnerable adults, including those with frailty, the oldest age, high-risk conditions, as well as immunocompromised patients, and by RSV subtype.

VE against RSV-LRTD hospitalizations/ED demonstrated modest decline (5─10%) over time using two complementary approaches. In the seasonal analyses, VE against RSV-LRTD was 81% in the first and 76% in the second season after vaccination. In analyses by time since vaccination among adults vaccinated in 2023–2024, VE against RSV-LRTD was 87% at 0–<6 months and 77% at 6–18 months post-vaccination. Although confidence intervals overlapped, both approaches support durable protection extending through at least two RSV seasons, with only modest attenuation over time. These results are consistent with findings from the pivotal clinical trial which showed 89% first-season VE14 and 78% second-season VE13 against RSV-LRTD.

The present analysis with two seasons of LRTD/ARI events updates our previously published VE estimates following the first season post-vaccination. This earlier study documented slightly higher VE's of 89% against RSV-LRTD16 hospitalizations/ED and 92% against RSV-ARI22 hospitalizations/ED. The modestly lower VE in the current study likely occurred because of a longer average follow-up (LRTD:84 days, ARI:81 days) compared to the earlier analyses (LRTD:51 days, ARI:56 days) rather than any significant change in vaccine performance.13,14 With this additional follow up time, our first season VE is now similar to that from a recent randomized phase 4 pragmatic trial evaluating bivalent RSVpreF in a Danish cohort (83% VE against respiratory tract disease hospitalizations), which, owing to its randomized interventional design, reduced exposure misclassification.20

Our VE against ARI was 83% and 70% against hospitalization/ED for those in their first and second seasons after vaccination, respectively, representing a decline of 13% compared to LRTD decline of 5%. Comparable results were seen in our time since vaccination analysis for RSV-ARI, (0─6 months: 89%; 6─18 months: 70%) and in CDC's IVY Network,32 which estimated 67% first season after vaccination, and 54% for the second season, representing a 13% decline. While the rate of decline is similar, our ARI VE point estimates are higher than those reported in IVY. This may be due to differences in exposure classification, as our study included comprehensive EHR vaccination documentation supplemented with capture of vaccination given outside of KPSC through KPSC's linkage with its state vaccine registry, whereas the IVY study relied on various immunization record sources including self-report. Indeed, a recent analysis of methods of ascertaining RSV vaccination status among older adults found that in states with mandatory IIS vaccination inclusion policies, such as California, IISs detected more RSV vaccinations as compared to EHRs or self-report,33 thus KPSCs seamless linkage to the state registry provided high-confidence exposure information. Differences between studies may also reflect varying severity of comorbidities or immunocompromising conditions in patient groups. Our findings can also be compared with caution with those reported for bivalent RSVpreF in the Veterans Health Administration (VHA) target trial emulation study, which found VE of 81% in the first month after vaccination, declining to 56% over 18 months. This 25% absolute difference, while higher than that reported in our analysis and IVY's, is notably capturing a different set of events as the VHA includes any medically attended RSV infection and encompasses milder disease, whereas our study and IVY's include all RSV-related respiratory infections requiring ED or hospitalization encounters.23 Clinical trial data indicates VE for less severe outcomes declines more quickly.13

Subgroup analyses for both LRTD and ARI showed similar trends across seasons among the oldest adults aged ≥75 years, those with risk factors, and adults with high frailty, though some estimates in the second season after vaccination had wide confidence intervals due to fewer available events for analysis. Frail adults are at high risk for severe RSV,34 yet vaccine efficacy estimates for frail individuals were not generated in pivotal registrational trials, due to accrual of too few events.15,35 Our analyses are among the first to report that RSVpreF confers protection against ARI hospitalizations/ED visits by all levels of frailty in a real-world population, with 69%–93% VE in the first season after vaccination, and 52–85% VE in the second season after vaccination although some estimates in the second season were imprecise due to small numbers of events. This is generally comparable to the first season 73% VE found in a VHA analysis for combined protein subunit vaccines, which used Care Assessment Need (CAN) scores approximating frailty through predictive models for 1-year mortality.19 The VHA study demonstrated greater decline among those with higher CAN scores over 18 months (79%–42%) as compared to those with the lowest CAN scores (89%–63%).23 Notable differences between our study and the VHA is that the latter's included RSV infections in any medical setting, and their population was predominantly male. By comparison, our study included over 50% women and evaluated RSV respiratory events in higher acuity hospitalized/ED settings. We also estimated 81% and 83% VE against RSV-ARI and LRTD, respectively, among 60–74-year-olds with high-risk conditions in the first season after vaccination, decreasing to 78% and 81% in the second season after vaccination. These results support current vaccination recommendations, as 60–74-year-olds with high risk are recommended for vaccination by the Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices36 and other vaccine advisory committee's recommendations worldwide.37, 38, 39

Bivalent RSVpreF VE among immunocompromised adults was 71% and 74% for ARI and LRTD, respectively, in the first season after vaccination, decreasing to 38% and 54% in the second season, although confidence intervals were wide for the second season. The first season findings align with first season analyses of bivalent RSVpreF VE from Denmark and England, where VE among immunocompromised adults was 61% and 73%, respectively, though the analysis from England was limited to those 75–79 years of age, in accordance with their vaccine technical committee reccomendations.40 Notably, the confidence intervals for the Danish estimate were wide, and while neither of these studies was specifically designed to evaluate this subgroup, they do provide additional context for bivalent RSVpreF performance in this vulnerable patient population. As RSVpreF rollout in England and for the Danish pragmatic study was one year after introduction in the United States, second season estimates are not yet available from either setting. We found that VE among the immunocompromised decreased more in the second season after vaccination compared to the immunocompetent, though precision was limited. Lower VE in the second season may reflect faster rate of decline in this high-risk group as was seen in CDC and VHA VE studies that combined RSV vaccines. CDC's VISION study reported VE against RSV-hospitalization among immunocompromised adults of 75% (58–85) 14–179 days post vaccination, decreasing to 44% (21–60) in the subsequent 180–588 days,41 and their IVY study reported that VE for combined products decreased from 54% (−2 to 79) to 11% (−51 to 48) over 2 seasons.32,41 Finally, the VHA study reported VE from 75% (52–89) in the first month after vaccination to 40% (24–53) over 18 months among the immunocompromised.23 Taken together, these data raise the hypothesis that immunocompromised adults may benefit from shorter revaccination intervals than immunocompetent adults, a question that will require confirmation in future studies and in the context of evolving recommending body's deliberations. Initial studies of RSVpreF3 in non-immunosuppressed populations did not show additional benefit with annual revaccination,12 but ongoing studies evaluating different revaccination intervals and higher-risk groups will be informative for future policy decisions.

RSV subtype analyses indicated that RSVpreF, a bivalent formulation including equal parts RSV A and RSV B prefusion F glycoproteins, was similarly protective for both of these antigenic subgroups, as was seen in the pivotal trial.13 We demonstrated highly consistent VE by subtype in the first season after vaccination: 77% against RSV A and 79% against RSV B for LRTD. There was little decline in VE in the second season: 76% against RSV A and 77% against RSV B. The IVY study reported RSVpreF VE against ARI hospitalizations of 57% and 81% for RSV A and B, respectively, which was similar to this study's ARI results: 83% against RSV A and 81% against RSV B for ARI. Additional follow-up time in our study will be helpful to detect longer term differences in VE changes by subtype. Longer-term follow-up over additional RSV seasons will be important to confirm whether subtype-specific VE remains stable over time.

This study includes both strengths and limitations. Important strengths include our accuracy of immunization data with minimal exposure misclassification, our use of specimen salvage to boost power and generalizability of RSV case detection, our systematic use of a multi-pathogen panel allowing us to generate estimates against RSV by subtype and also define a strict control group which minimizes bias, and inclusion of a socio-demographically diverse study population. KPSC uses regionally standardized protocols that limit oxygen therapy to members with evidence of substantial respiratory compromise, making the study's severe ARI and LRTD case definition more meaningful clinically than some other settings. Limitations include lower RSV vaccine uptake in the first season after vaccine availability at KPSC which resulted in reduced power for some subgroup analyses for the second season of follow up. Sample size limitations also contributed to the possibility of sparse-data bias in some VE estimates and precluded our ability to measure VE among those with low frailty in the first season. Despite inclusion of a comprehensive set of covariates, it is possible that our estimates do not fully account for unmeasured confounding and residual confounding may be present from data-driven covariate selection and categorization. Our ARI/LRTD definitions relied on discharge codes plus testing, without chart validation or symptom abstraction, so syndrome misclassification and imperfect balance between cases/controls remains possible. Further, a minor limitation was the exclusion of 41 hospitalization events <24 h that would have been appropriate to include in a combined hospitalization/ED outcome, however, this would represent a small addition to the over 16,000 events that were analyzed. There were 3 patients who received a second RSVpreF dose within one year after the first dose, however, based on randomized clinical trial data showing no increase in vaccine efficacy from a 12 month booster dose, inclusion of these patients is unlikely to alter our findings.42 We include the combined two-season analyses as a point of comparison with other analyses.18,41 However, our combined two-season analyses are not a representative average of the effect across two seasons as 89.5% of all vaccinations occurred in the second season, resulting in estimates that are weighted towards first-season effectiveness. The study also relies on data from a single health system.

Conclusions

Bivalent RSVpreF confers protection against severe RSV-associated respiratory illnesses, across two seasons, for both RSV subtypes, and among the most vulnerable patient populations. Our VE estimates translate to substantial public health benefit of RSV vaccination. Using an estimated annual incidence of RSV-associated ARI hospitalizations of 347 per 100,000 older adults, an 80% VE over two seasons would translate to approximately one RSV-related ARI hospitalization or ED encounter averted for every 180 older adults vaccinated. On this basis, given 173,859 RSVpreF doses have been administered at KPSC as of November 10, 2025, 900 serious RSV-associated ARI events (∼500 hospitalizations and 400 ED encounters) may have been prevented over two seasons. Given protection continues with modest decline into a second RSV season, further additional public health and economic impact in future years could be substantial. Longer-term follow-up will be essential to define VE trajectories beyond two seasons.
