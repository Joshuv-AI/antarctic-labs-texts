Potent halt of SARS-CoV-2 replication using clitocine-induced emergent-viral-RNA editing and disruption
Authors: Amgad M. Rabie, Imane G. El Idrissi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Potently inhibiting the functions of the replication proteins of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), or inducing erroneous editing of newly replicated SARS-CoV-2 RNA strands during replication, is among the most effective anti-SARS-CoV-2 approaches. Nucleosides and their phosphorylated derivatives (i.e., nucleotides) are considered ideal options for stopping various infections of coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19). Clitocine (CLT) is a phosphorylatable natural nucleoside analog that is isolated mainly from two edible mushrooms, Clitocybe inversa and Leucopaxillus giganteus, and it is uniquely characterized by a nitro group on its pyrimidine ring. CLT and many of its derivatives are well known mainly for their potent anticancer activities. Leveraging the use of its favorable and rare chemical structure in medicinal chemistry and drug development, herein, we biochemically evaluated and computationally explained the potential capacity of CLT to mutate and disable the emergent viral RNA strands during the coronaviral replication processes against the SARS-CoV-2 particle and two of its essential replication proteins, the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) and 3′-to-5′ exoribonuclease (ExoN) enzymes, which are crucial for a complete and functioning replication machinery. The in vitro anti-RdRp, anti-ExoN, and anti-SARS-CoV-2 assays demonstrated the potent inhibitory activities of the CLT molecule and its expected major active metabolite, CLT triphosphate (CLT-TP), on coronaviral replication, with very interesting 50% inhibitory concentration (IC50) values against the nirmatrelvir-resistant SARS-CoV-2 variant “NRSV” (0.19, 0.20, and 0.38 µM for CLT and 0.15, 0.18, and 0.34 µM for CLT-TP, respectively), using the structurally similar and approved anti-SARS-CoV-2 medication molnupiravir (MNP) and its known active triphosphate metabolite, β-D-N4-hydroxycytidine 5′-triphosphate (NHC-TP), respectively, as reference drugs. The subsequent in silico simulations and computations, by implementing mainly the relevant molecular docking and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, adequately expounded these biochemical findings. Through this new study, we also succeeded in uncovering and explaining the molecular-level mechanistic hallmarks of CLT/CLT-TP with respect to disrupting and impairing the coronaviral replication, as well as militating COVID-19 in a comprehensive way. In conclusion, the presented results need to be further confirmed by robust preclinical and clinical studies, along with the use of CLT as a promising pioneering parent anti-SARS-CoV-2 nucleoside analog by medicinal chemists for the synthesis of a wide spectrum of anti-RdRp nitronucleoside derivatives with ExoN-resistant chain-terminating activities (i.e., with ExoN-proofreading-escaping capacities) for optimum activity against coronaviruses.

Introduction

In 2026, for the seventh consecutive year, the ongoing impacts of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) and the related coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) continue to pose significant health challenges for the global population. As a result, finding highly effective antiviral medicines (natural or synthetic) that can efficiently combat or limit the various stages of the SARS-CoV-2 life cycle and trip, which begins with the invasive stages “infection”, progresses through the reproductive stages “viral replication”, and primarily ends with the pathogenic stages “disease manifestation” (mainly the clinical pictures of COVID-19 and long coronavirus disease “Long COVID”), represents a major challenge for medicinal chemists, clinical pharmacologists, and other relevant scientists.1,2 Respective attempts from academic research centers, pharmaceutical companies, and others in this concern have led to some moderately potent approved anti-COVID-19 drugs that specifically act to interrupt the coronaviral replication or reproduction stages, e.g., remdesivir, favipiravir, molnupiravir (MNP), nirmatrelvir, ritonavir, ensitrelvir, and obeldesivir (some of which are previously used compounds).1 The scaffolds of all of these molecules share a structural property of having nitrogenous heterocycles, and, in addition, the majority of these molecules have nucleoside-like structural properties.

From our viewpoint, molecules having considerable nucleosidic architectures and characteristics are regarded as golden molecules in the ongoing therapeutic battles against coronaviruses. According to the previous important findings by Rabie and his various research teams,3–12 we believe and hypothesize that nucleoside-like compounds could act as effective anticoronaviral drug candidates mainly via two integrated strategies or modes of action (i.e., two complementary anticoronaviral theories). The first strategy is the nucleoside-mimicry camouflage approach (NMCA), while the second is the noncarcinogenic genotoxic effects approach (NGEA). Both pharmacological mechanisms of action represent highly effective antiviral therapeutic tactics. In the NMCA tactic, we leverage the considerable degree of structural similarity of the administered nucleoside-like molecule with the known four typical RNA nucleosides and their corresponding nucleotides to significantly deceive replication-related enzymes such as the viral RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) protein (the nonstructural protein-12/7/8 “nsp12–nsp7–nsp8” complex) and the 3′-to-5′ exoribonuclease (ExoN) protein (the nonstructural protein-14/10 “nsp14–nsp10” complex), causing distortion and misrepresentation of the newly replicated coronaviral RNA strand via several mechanisms (e.g., the error catastrophe mechanism).13,14 Nucleotide analogs are presently utilized for the treatment of most RNA-viral diseases, including COVID-19, with acceptable degrees of success.15 Nucleotide analog prodrugs (either synthetic or natural) are usually intracellularly activated to the metabolic 5′-triphosphate form. They are incorporated into the emergent viral RNA of the target virus by the viral polymerase (RdRp), thereby terminating or corrupting intact RNA generation that is needed for reproduction of the virus. On the other hand, in the NGEA tactic, we leverage the cell-type specificity of the coronaviral infection, since a virus can specifically infect certain target human cells, and almost no others, due to the suitability and appropriateness of the gene expression patterns in these target cells for this viral infection. Based on this breakthrough concept, noncarcinogenic genotoxic agents or even approved medicines with tolerated genotoxic side effects can be used to temporarily modify the gene expression patterns in the target host cells by altering the ordinary three-dimensional (3D) genome architecture, which is expected to significantly decrease and impair the susceptibility of target host cells to the respective viral infections, enabling these target cells to provisionally become ‘nontarget cells’ for the relevant virus.15–17 Presently, nearly all drugs (mainly nucleoside-like small-molecule compounds) reported to exhibit preventive and/or curative actions against COVID-19 possess genotoxic side effects (direct genotoxicity) or regulate many human genes (indirect genotoxicity), which strongly supports our proposed new anticoronaviral theory.1,17 According to this therapeutic anticoronaviral theory, which represents a milestone in anticoronaviral approaches, numerous known and approved medicines with genotoxic side effects can potentially be employed to inhibit all coronaviral infections. Drugs that act almost exclusively via this tactic are expected to have a significant additional advantage over those that act solely via the aforementioned first tactic (the NMCA strategy), as they will not cause drug resistance in SARS-CoV-2 and other coronaviruses because these drugs principally target human cells (through several tracks) rather than viral particles.

Clitocine (CLT, its preferred IUPAC name is (2R,3R,4S,5R)-2-[(6-amino-5-nitropyrimidin-4-yl)amino]-5-(hydroxymethyl)oxolane-3,4-diol; Fig. 1) is a phosphorylatable natural nucleoside analog that is isolated mainly from two common and edible mushrooms, Clitocybe inversa and Leucopaxillus giganteus, i.e., a natural mycocompound.18 It is uniquely characterized by a nitro group on a pyrimidine ring (it is an atypical nitro-containing pyrimidine nucleoside, in which a ribofuranosyl moiety is attached to the pyrimidine nucleus via an exocyclic amino group linked to the 4-position of the nitrogenous base), representing an unrivalled chemical scaffold in nucleoside design and research in medicinal chemistry.18 This unique and pioneering nucleoside analog and many of its synthesized isomers and derivatives proved to have some potent beneficial biological activities, such as anticancer, insecticidal, antiinflammatory, and antiviral effects, as well as effects on the cardiovascular system.18 The uniqueness of the molecular structure of CLT comes mainly from being a nitronucleoside with an exocyclic N-glycoside, which might open relevant medicinal chemistry insights and directions for proposed novel modes of action in anticancer and antiviral research.

Chemical structures of CLT, CLT-TP, MNP, and NHC-TP.

CLT is expected to be metabolized into several phosphorylated products inside the human body, ending with the triphosphate metabolite (CLT triphosphate or CLT-TP; Fig. 1), which is the major bioactive metabolite of CLT (as with known indigenous nucleosides).3–5,18 Rationally, the anticoronaviral potential of CLT could be suggested and investigated since some principal coronaviral enzymes, such as RdRp and ExoN, are direct molecular targets for nucleoside analogs. The considerably conserved nature of these two coronaviral enzymes19 has always been very appealing to our research teams during our continuous journey,4–8,11,12 including the currently presented work, to find successful coronavirucidal agents, since we are primarily striving to achieve universal (i.e., general and nonspecific) attack on all coronaviruses, irrespective of their type, species, and variant (including the current coronaviruses, e.g., SARS-CoV-2, and possibly emerging ones, e.g., SARS-CoV-3).

For several coronaviruses, natural resistance to nucleotide analogs is usually provided by a coronaviral 3′-to-5′ exonuclease heterodimer (ExoN), which can effectively remove terminal analogs in most cases.4–8,11,12 Furthermore, mutations that emerged in the catalytic domains of coronaviral reproduction proteins, such as RdRp and the two coronaviral proteases, the main protease (Mpro) and the papain-like protease (PLpro), could confer substantial resistance against a wide range of treatment inhibitors, as in the case of nirmatrelvir (i.e., the nirmatrelvir-resistant SARS-CoV-2 variant “NRSV”).20 Therefore, besides investigating the two aforementioned anticoronaviral therapeutic strategies, NMCA and NGEA, evaluating the potential inhibitory activities of CLT and CLT-TP on the highly conserved catalytic regions of the aforementioned coronaviral proteins would add an extra advantage (especially if these activities are of significant values) to the total anticoronaviral effects of CLT, making it a possible broad-spectrum anticoronaviral drug that acts on almost any coronavirus, irrespective of its type and changed/mutated proteins. In this traditional enzyme inhibitory mechanism of action, we evaluate the capacity of CLT/CLT-TP molecules to act mainly as irreversible blockers, while, in the NMCA strategy, we explore their capacities to act mainly as competitive inhibitors. On the other hand, some studies proved that CLT could act as a noncarcinogenic genotoxic agent (i.e., through the NGEA strategy) on human cells.21 Based on all the previous facts and knowledge, we tried in the current drug-discovery study, using the biological methods supported by the computational estimations and exegeses, to evaluate, analyze, and explain the pharmacological activities of CLT to act as an effective anti-SARS-CoV-2 agent (and, consequently, as an anti-COVID-19 medicine) as well as a broad-spectrum anticoronaviral agent by acting via one or more of the aforementioned anticoronaviral therapeutic strategies or modes of action. MNP (an approved anti-RdRp drug for COVID-19 treatment; Fig. 1) and its major bioactive metabolite, the triphosphate metabolite, β-D-N4-hydroxycytidine 5′-triphosphate (NHC-TP; Fig. 1) were used as reference anticoronaviral agents for comparison with CLT and CLT-TP, respectively.

Materials and methods

In vitro bioevaluation

Manufacturer's specifications of the assayed compounds

The investigational compound CLT (CAS Registry Number: 105798-74-1) was purchased from the DC Chemicals company (https://www.dcchemicals.com; Shanghai, China; Catalog Number: DC11851, purity: >98%), and its active metabolite CLT-TP was purchased from the same company (DC Chemicals) by custom synthesis (purity: >97%). On the other hand, the reference anti-SARS-CoV-2 drug MNP (CAS Registry Number: 2349386-89-4) was purchased from the Biosynth Carbosynth company (Carbosynth Ltd, Berkshire, U.K.; Catalog Number: AE176721, purity: ≥98%), and its active metabolite NHC-TP (CAS Registry Number: 34973-27-8) was purchased from the MedChemExpress company (MCE, MedChemExpress LLC, New Jersey, U.S.A.; Catalog Number: HY-135867, purity: 98.02%). The ultrapure control solvent dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; CAS Registry Number: 67-68-5) was purchased from a known Egyptian local distributor, El-Gomhouria Company For Drugs (El-Gomhouria Co. For Trading Drugs, Chemicals & Medical Supplies, Mansoura Branch, Egypt; purity: ≥99.9%, anhydrous form).

In vitro dual anti-RdRp/ExoN activities assay (SARS-CoV-2-RdRp-Gluc reporter assay)

The structures of the RdRp and ExoN proteins are considerably conserved among the different types of coronaviruses;19 thus, the findings of the current assay could reflect the degree of the broad-spectrum anticoronaviral nature and potential of the compounds tested in this biochemical enzymatic assay (the SARS-CoV-2-RdRp-Gluc reporter assay).22,23 This reliable assay is a relatively straightforward biochemical virological test. First, the well-known 293T cells (ATCC CRL-3216) were kept in pure fresh Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM; Gibco) with exactly 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco), then they were cultured in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 at 37 °C. Later, the HEK293T cells were properly transfected using Vigofect transfection reagents (Vigorous Biotech), adhering to the manufacturer's detailed specific instructions. The necessary plasmid DNAs, antibodies, and reagents were purchased (the majority were from Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd “Shanghai, China” and Cell Signaling Technology “CST”, Inc. “Danvers, Massachusetts, U.S.A.”; with only a few others obtained from known regional suppliers), handled, and processed as guided by the procedures outlined and described in the referenced literature protocols.22,23 The investigated compounds (CLT and CLT-TP), positive-control drugs (MNP and NHC-TP), and negative-control solvent (DMSO) used in the assay are as defined and listed in Subsection 2.1.1. Initial pilot trials were performed on the evaluated compounds to establish the optimal concentration with which to initiate the current biochemical assay. Based on the results of these trials, stock solutions of the tested compounds were prepared by dissolving each of the four compounds, CLT, CLT-TP, MNP, and NHC-TP, in DMSO to obtain a final stock concentration of 100 µM of each compound. Furthermore, to fulfill the various measurements, ten gradual dilutions (concentrations of 50, 25, 10, 5, 1, 0.50, 0.25, 0.10, 0.05, and 0.01 µM) of these stock solutions were prepared. DMSO measurements were also conducted to render all experiments placebo-controlled. Traditional western blot tests (for the collected transfected HEK293T cells), modern real-time reverse transcription–polymerase chain reaction “RT-PCR” tests (for the extracted total RNA of transfected HEK293T cells), and routine cell viability tests (using the Cell Counting Kit-8 “CCK8”, Beyotime) were carried out in the same way as in the standard steps of the authentic assay.22,23 The various stages and steps of the present in vitro enzymatic assay were carefully performed in accordance with the same authentic literature protocol and method, but with almost all the used proteins modified to be identical to the SARS-CoV-2 Omicron-B.1.1.529.5 lineage.22,23 It is important to note that the HEK293T cells were transfected in the current SARS-CoV-2-RdRp-Gluc reporter assay with the CoV-Gluc, nsp12, nsp7, and nsp8 plasmid DNAs (absence of ExoN) at a ratio of 1 : 10 : 30 : 30 and with the CoV-Gluc, nsp12, nsp7, nsp8, nsp10, and nsp14 plasmid DNAs (presence of ExoN) at a ratio of 1 : 10 : 30 : 30 : 10 : 90.22,23 For the measurements, a stock solution of the highly sensitive bioluminescent substrate coelenterazine-h was prepared by dissolving coelenterazine-h in very pure analytical-grade absolute ethanol to an exact concentration of 1.022 mM, as directed in the original assay.22,23 Since coelenterazine-h in the solution form is highly heat/light/moisture-sensitive and susceptible to degradation, its stock solution should be stored under the manufacturer's appropriate specified conditions; it should be kept in a dark airtight container at a temperature of −20 °C or lower (proper storage in a cool, dark, and dry place) to maintain its activity. To prepare a fresh coelenterazine-h solution for each assay, the stock solution was diluted in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to a concentration of 16.7 µM and carefully incubated in the dark at room temperature for 30 min (directly before each assay).22,23 For the luminescence measurement steps, 10 µL of the supernatant was added to each well of a white and opaque 96-well plate, followed by injection of 60 µL of the freshly-prepared 16.7-µM coelenterazine-h solution.22,23 The emitted luminescence was precisely measured for 0.5 s with a high-performance Berthold Centro XS3 LB 960 microplate luminometer.22,23

In vitro biological anti-SARS-CoV-2 activities assay (including the cytotoxic activity assay)

Based on several validated original procedures reported in the literature,3–11 the present in vitro anti-COVID-19 multiassay (including the cytotoxicity test) was precisely designed for the estimation of the net anti-SARS-CoV-2 capacities of CLT and other potential anticoronaviral compounds. For safety purposes, the procedures were implemented in a specialized biosafety level-3 (BSL-3) laboratory in Italy after obtaining the required routine ethical and safety institutional approvals from the relevant authorities. The tested SARS-CoV-2 strain (the isolated specimen), the SARS-CoV-2 Omicron-B.1.1.529.5 lineage, was previously confirmed to be an NRSV strain through the relevant assay prior to being preserved in the research biobank. The starting titer of the stock coronavirus (107.25 TCID50 mL−1; TCID50 “50% tissue culture infectious dose” is the amount of virus required to infect 50% of a cell culture, representing the infectious viral titer and reflecting the degree of infectivity of a virus in cells) was prepared after three serial passages in Vero E6 cells (ATCC CRL-1586) in infection media (DMEM supplemented with 4.5 g L−1d-glucose, 100 mg L−1 sodium pyruvate, 2% FBS, 100 000 U L−1 penicillin–streptomycin “Pen-Strep”, and 25 mM N-(2-hydroxyethyl)piperazine-N′-ethanesulfonic acid “HEPES”). The investigated compounds (CLT and CLT-TP), positive-control drugs (MNP and NHC-TP), and negative-control solvent (DMSO) used in this assay are, exactly as in the previous enzymatic assay, as defined and listed in Subsection 2.1.1. As with the previous enzymatic assay, initiatory pilot assays were implemented on the tested compounds to establish the optimal concentration with which to initiate the in vitro anti-SARS-CoV-2 and cytotoxicity tests. Accordingly, stock solutions of the tested compounds were prepared by dissolving each of the four compounds, CLT, CLT-TP, MNP, and NHC-TP, in DMSO to obtain a final concentration of 100 µM of each compound. Further, to conduct the measurements, ten gradual dilutions (serial concentrations of 50, 25, 10, 5, 1, 0.50, 0.25, 0.10, 0.05, and 0.01 µM) of these stocks were prepared. DMSO measurements were also conducted to render all experiments placebo-controlled. To evaluate the total or net anti-SARS-CoV-2 activity of each of the target compounds CLT/CLT-TP as compared to that of the positive-control drugs MNP/NHC-TP (along with that of the negative-control solvent DMSO), respectively, at the in vitro level, Vero E6 cells were pretreated with each of the five compounds diluted in infection media for 1 h prior to infection by the aforementioned viral lineage at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.02 (the Vero E6 cells were carefully infected with the aforementioned coronaviral sample). All the investigated compounds were maintained with the virus inoculum throughout the 2-h incubation period. The inoculum was removed after incubation, and the cells were overlaid with infection media containing the diluted investigated compounds (in serial concentrations, as enumerated before). After 48 h of incubation at 37 °C, the supernatants were collected to determine the viral loads using the TCID50 assay or the quantitative real-time RT-PCR “qRT-PCR” assay (TaqMan Fast Virus 1-Step Master Mix). Cytotoxicity assessment of each of the five tested compounds was performed in Vero E6 cells as well, employing the well-known CellTiter-Glo luminescent cell viability assay (Promega).

Statistical analysis of the results of implemented bioassays

Dose–response curve data for all the assays mentioned above are presented as “the mean (µ) ± the standard error of the mean (SEM)” from three consecutive measurements (triplicate measurements). Final % inhibition values were calculated by subtracting the % inhibition observed with the negative-control solvent DMSO at the corresponding concentration. All inhibitory concentration (e.g., the 50% inhibitory concentration “IC50” and the 90% inhibitory concentration “IC90”) values were determined by nonlinear regression analysis using the GraphPad Prism software (Version 9.0.2). All resulting data were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

In silico computational explanation

Download and preparation of SARS-CoV-2 protein/ligand files

The precise 3D crystallized structures of the targetable SARS-CoV-2 RdRp and ExoN proteins were directly obtained, using the “fetch” command in the PyMOL software (the PyMOL Molecular Graphics System, Version 2.6, Schrödinger, LLC; licensed installed copy in 2025), from the free Protein Data Bank (PDB) repository with the two PDB identifiers “IDs” 7OZU for RdRp (found at: https://www.rcsb.org/structure/7ozu) and 7MC6 for ExoN (found at: https://www.rcsb.org/structure/7mc6). To simulate the catalytic structure of both enzymes in their natural environment, the two enzymatic proteins, 7OZU and 7MC6, were downloaded, as they are, along with their protein cofactors, i.e., were downloaded cocrystallized in the complex forms nsp12/7/8 and nsp14/10, respectively. Within PyMOL, nonprotein atoms (such as RNA strands, solvent molecules, and ions) were removed where necessary. The proteins were then exported in “.pdb” format. Using AutoDockTools in the AutoDock Vina software (Version 1.2.3, licensed installed copy in 2025), the proteins were imported, checked (full checking for any errors), assigned Gasteiger charges, and exported in “.pdbqt” format for use in the blind docking procedures. On the other hand, the exact 3D structures of the investigated ligands were directly downloaded from the PubChem website (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov): CLT (CID: 129111), MNP (CID: 145996610), and NHC-TP (CID: 147591). Because the 3D structure of CLT-TP (CID: 158063810) was not available, the two-dimensional (2D) structure was downloaded instead, and the 3D coordinates were easily generated using PyMOL. As with the aforementioned protein files, all ligand structures, in their pure neutral forms, were then converted into the “.pdbqt” format to be ready for use in the blind docking procedures.

Execution of blind molecular docking

In this process, we aimed to conduct an undirected docking of the investigated molecules, CLT, CLT-TP, MNP, and NHC-TP, in the RdRp and ExoN enzymes of SARS-CoV-2. The current blind docking simulations were performed with AutoDock Vina (the version is stated above) through MetaScreener (https://github.com/bio-hpc/metascreener).24 This included implementing individual blind docking runs and generating a final blind docking consensus by analyzing and assessing the different distributions of binding energies and their structural clusters of poses. Generally, the various simple steps of the entire docking process were essentially performed following the thorough instructions provided in the relevant literature methodology.12,25 It is important to state that the molecular docking software settings and adjustments in the current protocol not only adhered to the normal physiological pH (human blood pH) but also to other pH states in the human body where the virus could also be present (this is why the triphosphate groups and nucleosidic nitrogen atoms of the relevant ligands appear protonated in some docking outputs; see Fig. 5–8 later).

Execution of molecular dynamics (MD) simulations

To investigate and understand the dynamics of the previous molecular docking results, the best pose of each of the considered locations was subjected to MD simulations with the MD engine Desmond included in the Maestro Suite of Schrödinger (Schrödinger, Inc.).12,25,26 In addition, the relevant apoenzyme was used as the standard configuration for the baseline comparison of some MD parameters in each case. To start, the protein–ligand files were imported from the MetaScreener's output and then prepared with the Maestro's Protein Preparation Workflow (adhering primarily to the appropriate default instructions and options of the relevant literature protocols)12,25 with the highly accurate OPLS4 (Optimized Potentials for Liquid Simulations 4) force field. Using the Maestro System Builder tool, all complexes were moved to minimize the volume and placed in a box with a buffer size of 10 × 10 × 10 Å. The box was then filled with sodium and chloride ions to neutralize charges and reach a concentration of 0.15 M of sodium chloride (to be similar to the predominant range of the molar concentration of sodium chloride in the human body), and with water using the rigid single-point charge (SPC) model as a solvating water model. Simulations were conducted for a duration of 100 ns at a temperature of 300 K and a pressure of 1 atm, using the ensemble NPT and the force field OPLS4. They were then thoroughly analyzed using the Maestro's Simulation Interactions Diagram. Final Simulation Interactions Diagram reports were used for the computational assessment, exploration, and comparison of the relevant various structural and dynamic properties and variables of the investigated protein–ligand complexes, e.g., protein and ligand root-mean-square deviations (protein and ligand “protein–ligand” RMSDs; measured in Å), protein and ligand root-mean-square fluctuations (RMSFs; measured in Å), interactions fractions (%), number of protein–ligand contacts (# of total contacts), radius of gyration (rGyr; measured in Å), number of intramolecular H-bonds (intraHB), molecular surface area (MolSA; measured in Å2), solvent-accessible surface area (SASA; measured in Å2), and polar surface area (PSA; measured in Å2), in order to support and explicate the experimental biological findings.

Results and discussion

Biochemical inhibitory activities against SARS-CoV-2 RdRp

As mentioned earlier, the primary focus of the present preclinical research study is on exploring the potential capacities of the natural nitronucleoside compound CLT to effectively inhibit and disrupt the enzymatic proteins that are responsible for coronaviral replication and reproduction, specifically the two crucial enzymes, RdRp and ExoN proteins. Coronaviral RdRp inhibition comes to the forefront of promising and successful anti-SARS-CoV-2 therapeutic strategies.27 Therefore, the current SARS-CoV-2-RdRp-Gluc reporter assay was performed to evaluate these possible capacities. The resulting data showed that the four tested compounds, being similar nucleoside-like molecules, act in almost the same way against the SARS-CoV-2 polymerase. For this anti-RdRp behavior, CLT (IC50 = 0.19 µM) and its metabolite CLT-TP (IC50 = 0.15 µM) demonstrated slightly better inhibitory activities than MNP (IC50 = 0.20 µM) and its metabolite NHC-TP (IC50 = 0.19 µM), respectively, in the absence of the SARS-CoV-2 exonuclease (the same behavior was observed in the presence of the exonuclease, as clearly reported in Table 1). CLT and CLT-TP suppressed the SARS-CoV-2 RdRp's replicative performance with exceptionally low IC50 values, demonstrating the potent inhibitory actions of CLT/CLT-TP against the coronaviral replication machinery. Furthermore, these considerably strong effects clarify that CLT might act against the SARS-CoV-2 RdRp enzyme, at least partially, through the nucleoside mimicry mechanism (NMCA), exactly as previously suggested in the present study's rationale.

From our viewpoint, the unique presence of a highly active nitro group (with two amino groups at its two sides in the nucleobase ring), with its strong electron-withdrawing properties and distinct polar nature, in the CLT/CLT-TP molecules is thought to contribute significantly to their better performance when compared to the MNP/NHC-TP molecules (which lack this group), respectively, in strongly binding to the active and catalytic regions of the RdRp protein (and possibly of the coronaviral components as a whole). This superior binding affinity might lead to more effective inhibition of the coronaviral replication. Interestingly, the oxidative, polar, and electron-withdrawing features of the nitro group make the CLT molecule more reactive in many ways, e.g., (1) making it more susceptible to nucleophilic chemical attacks (interactions) in the biological environments; (2) making it capable of participating in several noncovalent interactions, including hydrogen bonding and π-hole interactions; (3) making it capable of being involved in coordinative interactions with the different metal ions that critically affect the coronaviral life cycle; (4) making it involved in metabolic reduction processes with the reduction of the nitro group and formation of reactive intermediates that can actively interact with the coronaviral targets (like RNA, proteins, and enzymes) and the human targets (like RNA and genes “NGEA mechanism”); (5) making it capable of being used as a hypoxia-activated prodrug, which can be purposed to be activated in hypoxic (low-oxygen) environments that are usually found in COVID-19 and most coronaviral diseases; (6) making it capable of having self-immolative linkers (spacers) for drug-delivery systems that can be designed for COVID-19 patients (as an example); (7) making it exhibit a wide range of antimicrobial bioactivities, including antiviral effects; and (8) making it able to disrupt viral biological processes, leading to the death of SARS-CoV-2 or coronaviral particles. All of the preceding examples of merits can be viewed as a rational basis for the beneficial and superior usage of natural and synthetically designed nitronucleosides as anticoronaviral agents over classical nucleosides. Moreover, the presence of a reactive free amino group at the nucleobase ring of the CLT/CLT-TP molecules, unlike the corresponding MNP/NHC-TP molecules (which have a hydroxyl-substituted amino group at the nucleobase ring), might slightly strengthen their binding affinities to the coronaviral RdRp protein through enhanced and denser hydrogen bonding formation and other favorable interactions as compared to the MNP/NHC-TP molecules. The CLT/CLT-TP molecules also have a nucleobase-exocyclic secondary amino group (linked to the sugar moiety) that is not present in the MNP/NHC-TP molecules and might contribute to better relevant binding forces.

Biochemical inhibitory activities against SARS-CoV-2 ExoN

One of the smartest anticoronaviral therapeutic tactics is one that targets disabling the crucial proofreading enzyme, ExoN, and taking it out of service, since coronaviruses that lack the ExoN enzyme or have it considerably disrupted (e.g., inhibited) are massively susceptible to lethal mutagenesis, which leads to coronaviral irreproduction (inability to properly reproduce) and death.28,29 The difference between the inhibitory activities of a certain molecule against RdRp in the presence of ExoN and in its absence could be interpreted as a measure of this molecule's capacity to evade ExoN's proofreading performance (the capacity is inversely proportional to this difference), i.e., the inhibitory activity of a certain molecule against the RdRp–ExoN system could also be generally considered as the inhibitory activity of this molecule against ExoN.11Fig. 3 and Table 1 display the dose (molar concentration)–response (% inhibition) curves and the final specific IC50 values obtained from the biochemical anti-SARS-CoV-2-RdRp/ExoN assay, in the presence of ExoN activity, for the tested compounds (CLT, CLT-TP, MNP, and NHC-TP), respectively.

The resulting data showed that the final inhibitory activities of the four tested compounds against the SARS-CoV-2 RdRp (in the presence of ExoN activity, simulating the natural coronaviral environment) differ principally according to every molecule's ability to inhibit or evade the SARS-CoV-2 ExoN. As logically expected, these activities were found to be lower than those primarily attained in the absence of the ExoN enzyme. For this anti-ExoN behavior, CLT (IC50 = 0.20 µM) and its metabolite CLT-TP (IC50 = 0.18 µM) demonstrated significantly better inhibitory activities than MNP (IC50 = 0.27 µM) and its metabolite NHC-TP (IC50 = 0.23 µM), respectively, in the presence of the SARS-CoV-2 polymerase. CLT and CLT-TP suppressed the SARS-CoV-2 ExoN's proofreading performance with exceptionally low IC50 values, demonstrating the potent inhibitory actions of CLT/CLT-TP against two of the most important enzymes (RdRp and ExoN) of the entire coronaviral replication machinery. In addition, as previously deduced with the SARS-CoV-2 RdRp enzyme, these clearly powerful effects indicate that CLT and its derivatives might act against the SARS-CoV-2 ExoN enzyme, at least in part, through the NMCA mechanism, i.e., the current data might partially prove that the NMCA mechanism is involved in these inhibitory processes. Again, the unrivaled presence of a highly active nitro group, with two interactive amino groups at its two sides in the nucleobase ring, in the CLT/CLT-TP molecules is believed to significantly contribute to their better performance when compared to the respective MNP/NHC-TP molecules in blocking the active and catalytic sites of the ExoN protein. This superior blockade might lead to more effective evasion of the coronaviral proofreading and more successful inhibition of the coronaviral replication as a whole.

From the values in Table 1, it can be proposed that if the IC50 values of a nucleoside/nucleotide analog against the RdRp enzyme alone and against the RdRp enzyme in the presence of the ExoN enzyme are less than 1 µM and close to each other, the nucleoside/nucleotide analog inhibitor is more effective against the SARS-CoV-2 replication machinery (in general and also when compared to another nucleoside/nucleotide analog inhibitor, respectively). From the results, we can also suggest that an ideal potent anti-SARS-CoV-2 agent of the anti-RdRp nucleoside analog class should have a ratio of “IC50(RdRp+ExoN)/IC50(RdRp)” that is very close to 1 and less than 2. As this ratio decreases, the tested compound may have a higher capacity, in most cases, to succeed in inhibiting the SARS-CoV-2 replication more efficiently. The nucleoside analog compound CLT displayed the lowest “IC50(RdRp+ExoN)/IC50(RdRp)” ratio (1.05, very close to 1) among the four tested compounds, which is smaller than that of its reference nucleoside analog drug “MNP” (1.35, the highest ratio among the four tested compounds). Similarly, CLT's active nucleotide metabolite “CLT-TP” displayed a slightly smaller “IC50(RdRp+ExoN)/IC50(RdRp)” ratio (1.20) than that of its reference active nucleotide metabolite “NHC-TP” (1.21). The presently detected very promising capacities of CLT and CLT-TP to inhibit the RdRp and ExoN activities of the coronaviral-2 Omicron variant (away from the actual virally infected environment) also necessitate examining and evaluating the net inhibitory activities of both compounds against the entire viral reproduction in the in vitro anti-SARS-CoV-2 assays (see next subsection). This represents a second stage in our exploratory journey for the possible therapeutic positioning/purposing of CLT (and its effective derived nitronucleosides/nitronucleotides) to be used as potent anti-SARS-CoV-2 and anti-COVID-19 medications.

Revealing mechanistic hallmarks of CLT/CLT-TP on disrupting and impairing coronaviral replication

As a work team, combining simulative theoretical modeling with actual experimental data was crucial to achieving our goals and findings. In almost all our previous and current relevant studies, by a combination of theoretical simulations of the RdRp and ExoN proteins with experimental facts, we were able to capture intermediate states of these proteins (alone as well as bound with the target ligands) that were previously unseen or impossible to see from the practical viewpoint, since they occur in time intervals of femtoseconds or picoseconds. This integrated strategy allowed us to deeply understand the coronaviral infection and replication processes at an atomic level, which, as a result, effectively helped us to design highly fittable and successful therapies against SARS-CoV-2. After achieving the promising combined biological–computational outcomes indicated above, it has become imperative for us to find the exact or most prominent mechanism(s) of CLT's anticoronaviral action. This obviously necessitated further exploration of the CLT's binding modes with the targeted proteins RdRp/ExoN and its druggability over them, hoping to provide valuable reference and data for the development of potent anti-SARS-CoV-2/anticoronaviral therapies.

We hypothesize that CLT strongly disrupts SARS-CoV-2 RNA replication mainly via a dual mode of action. First, it inhibits the relevant replication proteins, RdRp and ExoN enzymes, by potently binding to their major catalytic sites. Second, it generates impaired new viral RNA strands, i.e., false RNA strands, through incorporation into the newly produced strands. For the first mechanism, it is important to know that nucleotide analogs could effectively evade coronaviral ExoN cleavage (this cleavage is needed for the excision of mismatched nucleotides/nucleotide analogs that are incorporated into the coronaviral nascent RNA) by having a nucleobase that does not interact with the ExoN protein residues His95, Gln145, or Phe146 (due to the essential roles of these amino acids in recognizing the base moiety and thus pulling the molecules of the incorporated nucleotide analogs into a preferable structural conformation for cleavage).30 This critical prerequisite is available in the case of CLT, as proved by the biochemical–computational findings indicated above. In addition, a chemically edited (chemically modified) ribose moiety in the nucleotide analog molecule could also significantly aid this cleavage evasion and the first mechanism.30 It is expected that removing or blocking both the 2-hydroxyl and 3-hydroxyl groups (or at least one of them) of the ribosyl moiety of the CLT molecule via a molecular optimization strategy in drug design (to obtain new analogs of the current ribose-containing CLT molecule) will significantly modulate and increase the molecule's resistance against the incisional activity of the ExoN enzyme (i.e., enhance the molecule's ability to escape ExoN proofreading) and, consequently, will greatly improve the CLT's original anticoronaviral activities in the newly designed derivatives.3–5,30

On the other hand, the second mechanism (Fig. 13) could be attributed to the considerable resemblance to the normal RNA nucleosides cytidine (C) and uridine (U) in their molecular structure. Consequently, this structural similarity greatly increases the susceptibility of CLT to be phosphorylated (in the presence of ATP, which is converted to adenosine diphosphate “ADP”) by the uridine–cytidine kinase (UCK) into CLT monophosphate (CLT-MP). CLT-MP is also expected to be phosphorylated by other kinases, the cytidine monophosphate (CMP) and uridine monophosphate (UMP) kinases (CMPK and UMPK, respectively), into CLT diphosphate (CLT-DP), which is further phosphorylated by the cytidine diphosphate (CDP) and uridine diphosphate (UDP) kinases (CDPK and UDPK, respectively) into CLT-TP, the major active metabolite of CLT (by analogy to indigenous nucleosides/nucleotides). This sequential phosphorylation phase is followed by a competitive editing phase in which the CLT-TP molecules effectively compete with the cytidine triphosphate (CTP) and uridine triphosphate (UTP) molecules in the newly replicated SARS-CoV-2 RNA strands formed by the enzymatic catalysis of SARS-CoV-2 RdRp, giving inactive, damaged, and corrupted pseudostrands with very resistant capacities against the corrective proofreading actions of the SARS-CoV-2 ExoN enzyme (escaping the ExoN proofreading) due to the expected prior ExoN inhibition via the aforementioned first mechanism. Guided by this integrated bimodal hypothesis and rational design, CLT/CLT-TP molecules were proposed to effectively halt SARS-CoV-2/coronaviral replication and infection.

Demonstrating synergistic anti-COVID-19 actions (including actions for aiding Long COVID syndrome treatment) of CLT

As a source of nitric oxide (NO), CLT can also perform five additional synergistic actions against COVID-19 and even Long COVID or post-COVID sequelae (i.e., five integrated anti-COVID-19/anti-Long-COVID effects beyond the combined inhibitory and mutative actions currently being investigated against coronaviruses). CLT can: (1) have direct antiviral toxic effects;18,31 (2) increase the production of NO to permit adequate activation of the guanylate cyclase (GC) enzyme, thus, enhancing the synthesis of cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP) and, consequently, boosting the activation of protein kinase G (PKG) and retaining the normal human cellular signaling pathways again;18,31 (3) act as a coronary vasodilator that may target delivery of NO to host cells infected by SARS-CoV-2;31 (4) correct vascular endothelial dysfunctions in SARS-CoV-2-induced cardiovascular diseases;31 and (5) strongly bind the major catalytic site of SARS-CoV-2 PLpro and effectively inhibit the virus according to some reported studies.31 Being a ribonucleoside/ribonucleotide and adenosine analog, CLT might be able to fight and suppress coronaviral infection-induced tumors via its reported anticancer actions (mainly, cancer cell apoptosis and tumor growth inhibition).18 This could also be considered another supplemental effect added to CLT's aforementioned effects that can be collectively repurposed for the management and treatment of Long COVID. Conclusions and future therapeutic perspectives

With each new viral pandemic, nucleosidic heterocyclic compounds come to the top of the therapeutic scene as one of the best drug options for effective treatment. CLT is a promising mycotherapeutic agent with the unique structural feature of being a nucleoside with a nitro group in its nucleobase. Being a natural and well-tolerated compound, it represents a potential anticoronaviral option that is favorable and superior to most of the recent synthetic antiviral nucleoside analog drugs. The current combined in vitro/in silico results promisingly revealed that this nitromycocompound, CLT, and its major active triphosphorylated metabolite, CLT-TP, showed significant outperformance over the anticoronaviral-RdRp/anti-COVID-19 drug, molnupiravir, and its major active triphosphorylated metabolite, NHC-TP, respectively, in the net anticoronaviral performance and effectiveness. The current and previous research findings regarding the significant inhibitory binding actions of CLT against many of the conserved catalytic regions of the key players in the coronaviral reproduction and lifecycle (especially the PLpro, RdRp, and ExoN enzymes; pan-coronaviral inhibition) support our hypothesis that CLT can act as a potent pan-coronaviral inhibitor (i.e., an efficient broad-spectrum anticoronaviral agent), working by more than one of the anticoronaviral therapeutic strategies, including the mutagenic activities of CLT/CLT-TP against the newly emerged coronaviral RNA strands through the CLT-TP's capacities to be successfully incorporated into these strands. The most apparent limitation of the present research outcomes is the lack of extensive in vivo/clinical investigations on CLT, particularly regarding the anti-SARS-CoV-2/anti-COVID-19 activities and the prominent mechanisms of anticoronaviral action. This pharmacological restriction or issue can be readily resolved and addressed in the near future through implementing a variety of well-designed research projects (including clinical trials) in this regard. Furthermore, from a medicinal chemical perspective, the presence of several active moieties in the molecular structure of CLT offers a tremendous opportunity for structural optimization, via appropriate chemical synthetic derivatization, to significantly enhance the pharmacodynamic/pharmacokinetic properties of this compound into more potent, efficient, and bioavailable anti-SARS-CoV-2/anti-COVID-19 structures.18,32 In any case, the cytotoxicity of CLT, demonstrated in several cancer cell lines,18,21,33 must be considered when determining the final therapeutic antiviral doses of this drug, assuming CLT receives clinical approval.
