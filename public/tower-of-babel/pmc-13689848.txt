History of Presentation

A 50-year-old woman with a history of hypertension and hypothyroidism presented to the emergency department with acute chest pain after sustaining a bee sting to the posterior neck while washing her car. Within minutes, she developed diffuse urticaria, periorbital swelling, facial edema, dizziness, vomiting, and severe substernal chest pain radiating to the left arm.

On arrival, she was anxious and in acute distress, but vital signs were stable. Physical examination revealed diffuse urticaria, periorbital edema, and mild jugular venous distension. Lung auscultation demonstrated diffuse wheezing, and cardiac exam showed a regular rhythm without murmurs.

Management

Initial therapy targeted both anaphylaxis and possible ACS. The patient received intramuscular epinephrine (0.3 mg), intravenous methylprednisolone (125 mg), intravenous diphenhydramine (50 mg), aspirin (325 mg loading dose, then 81 mg daily), clopidogrel (300 mg loading dose, then 75 mg daily), therapeutic enoxaparin (1 mg/kg every 12 hours), and intravenous nitroglycerin infusion.

After coronary angiography confirmed normal coronaries, anticoagulation was discontinued. Management was refocused on vasospasm control with nitrates and calcium channel blockers. β-blockers were avoided to prevent worsening vasospasm and interference with epinephrine. Morphine was also avoided because of the risk of histamine release. The patient remained under joint cardiology and allergy/immunology supervision for the remainder of her admission.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Chest pain resolved within 48 hours. She was discharged on a calcium channel blocker, nitrate, and angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor. An epinephrine autoinjector was also prescribed. Allergy follow-up was arranged for venom immunotherapy evaluation. At 3-month follow-up, she remained asymptomatic, and electrocardiogram (ECG) demonstrated resolution of abnormal ST-segment findings and repeated echocardiogram showed improvement in ventricular function.

Discussion

Background

Kounis syndrome, first described in 1991, is an ACS triggered by allergic or hypersensitivity reactions. Three variants have been described: Type I, characterized by coronary vasospasm in angiographically normal arteries; Type II, involving plaque erosion or rupture in pre-existing atherosclerotic disease; and Type III, associated with coronary stent thrombosis.1,2 Our patient most likely represents Type I Kounis syndrome, in which mast cell–mediated release of inflammatory mediators induced coronary vasospasm and myocardial injury despite the absence of obstructive coronary artery disease.3

Pathophysiology

Bee venom contains allergenic proteins, including phospholipase A2, melittin, and hyaluronidase, which can trigger mast cell and basophil activation with release of histamine, leukotrienes, prostaglandins, and platelet-activating factor.4,5 These mediators promote coronary vasoconstriction, endothelial dysfunction, and platelet activation, explaining myocardial ischemia, troponin elevation, and transient regional wall-motion abnormalities in the absence of fixed coronary stenosis.4,6, 7

Differential Diagnosis

Although the clinical presentation strongly suggested Type I Kounis syndrome, the diagnosis remains presumptive, and other causes of myocardial infarction with nonobstructive coronary arteries (MINOCA) should be considered.8 Acute viral myocarditis, hypersensitivity myocarditis, Takotsubo syndrome, coronary thromboembolism, and spontaneous coronary vasospasm unrelated to allergic reactions were considered in the differential diagnosis.8,9 Hypersensitivity myocarditis deserves consideration because it may also occur following allergic reactions and present with chest pain, elevated cardiac biomarkers, and transient left ventricular dysfunction. However, the immediate onset of chest pain within minutes of the bee sting, concomitant urticaria and angioedema, objective evidence of myocardial injury, transient regional wall-motion abnormalities, and angiographically normal coronary arteries favored presumed Type I Kounis syndrome.1,3 Takotsubo syndrome was considered less likely because echocardiography demonstrated focal anterior wall hypokinesis rather than the characteristic apical or midventricular ballooning pattern.8 Coronary vasospasm was not directly demonstrated angiographically but was inferred from the clinical presentation in the absence of obstructive coronary artery disease.

Management

Current American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association and European Society of Cardiology guidelines for ACSs and chest pain do not provide disease-specific recommendations for Kounis syndrome. Consequently, management requires balancing the priorities of anaphylaxis treatment with established ACS therapies.

•Epinephrine: Remains the cornerstone of anaphylaxis management but should be administered with caution because it may exacerbate coronary vasospasm and myocardial ischemia.

The absence of formal guideline recommendations highlights the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion and adopting a multidisciplinary approach to diagnosis and management.

Preventive measures

Preventive strategies include allergen avoidance, prescription of epinephrine autoinjectors, and referral for allergy/immunology evaluation, including consideration of venom immunotherapy. Long-term cardiology follow-up is also important to document recovery of ventricular function and guide ongoing cardiovascular risk management.

Study Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging, an important diagnostic modality in the evaluation of MINOCA, was not available at our institution and therefore was not performed. Consequently, microvascular ischemia, myocarditis, and other inflammatory causes of MINOCA could not be definitively excluded, limiting diagnostic certainty.9 Serum tryptase and total immunoglobulin E levels were not obtained during the acute presentation and therefore additional evidence of mast-cell activation could not be assessed. Previous ECGs were unavailable, precluding determination of whether the left bundle branch block was chronic or new. Furthermore, because left bundle branch block is associated with secondary repolarization abnormalities, the asymmetric T-wave changes observed on the admission ECG should be interpreted cautiously and were not considered diagnostic of myocardial ischemia in isolation.

Clinical Importance

Although underrecognized, registry data estimate that Kounis syndrome occurs in approximately 1.1% of hospitalizations for allergic, hypersensitivity, or anaphylactic reactions.4 Its true incidence may be higher because of underdiagnosis and overlap with other causes of ACS. Failure to recognize Kounis syndrome may lead to unnecessary interventions, prolonged antithrombotic therapy, or inappropriate medication selection; conversely, delayed treatment of anaphylaxis may be life-threatening.

Educational Value

This case highlights the need for awareness among emergency physicians, hospitalists, and cardiologists. Recognizing the overlap between allergic and cardiovascular presentations is essential to avoid misdiagnosis and guide appropriate therapy. Incorporating Kounis syndrome into the differential diagnosis of ACS, particularly after allergic exposures, may improve diagnostic accuracy and prevent mismanagement.

Conclusions

This case demonstrates that ACSs may arise from immunologic mechanisms, not solely from atherosclerosis, highlighting the complex interplay between the immune and cardiovascular systems. Recognition of this entity is essential because its presentation may closely mimic conventional myocardial infarction, yet its underlying pathophysiology and management differ substantially. Increased awareness of Kounis syndrome can facilitate timely diagnosis, appropriate treatment, and improved patient outcomes while avoiding unnecessary invasive procedures and prolonged antithrombotic therapy.
