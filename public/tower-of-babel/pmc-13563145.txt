Digital Health Approaches to Geriatric Rehabilitation: A Narrative Paper
Authors: Changwon Moon, Pamela Bregasi, Aminreza Khandan, Bijan Najafi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rapid population ageing is widening the gap between geriatric rehabilitation needs and constrained care capacity. Digital health offers new ways to deliver rehabilitation beyond traditional settings, but its role and limitations in geriatric practice remain unclear. To provide a narrative synthesis of how digital health can support geriatric rehabilitation, highlighting advances, challenges, and future directions. We conducted a narrative review of digital health approaches in geriatric rehabilitation, informed by the authors’ clinical and technical expertise, synthesizing evidence on telemedicine, mobile health, wearable sensors, exergames, extended‑reality platforms, and artificial intelligence (AI)-enabled systems across rehabilitation domains. Digital health interventions can support continuous monitoring, motor and cognitive training (including gamified exercise), behavioral self‑management, and remote clinician oversight. Studies in selected older populations show feasibility, safety, and short‑ to medium‑term improvements in mobility, frailty, pain, and cardiorespiratory fitness. However, the evidence base is fragmented, often excludes frail older adults and those with cognitive or multisensory impairment, and frequently focuses on surrogate sensor‑derived outcomes, with limited long‑term data on independence, falls, or quality of life. Persistent challenges include usability and digital‑literacy barriers, the “grey digital divide,” limited interoperability and workflow integration, and unresolved issues of data governance, privacy, and algorithmic transparency. Digital health has potential to augment geriatric rehabilitation and support healthy ageing, but its benefits are unlikely to be equitably realized without intentional design and implementation. Priorities include human‑centered and co‑design approaches, pragmatic trials, integration with rehabilitation workflows, and robust ethical and regulatory frameworks for data‑driven and AI‑enabled care.

GRAPHICAL ABSTRACT

INTRODUCTION

People today are living longer than any previous generation: by 2030, around one in six people worldwide will be aged 60 years or older, and by 2050 this group is expected to reach about 2.1 billion, with the majority living in low‑ and middle‑income countries [1,2]. Yet these extra years of life are not automatically healthy. Global data show that gains in life expectancy have outpaced improvements in healthy life expectancy, so many older adults spend a substantial number of years living with disease, disability, and reduced functional capacity [1-4]. Multimorbidity driven by non‑communicable diseases, together with geriatric syndromes and polypharmacy, creates a complex clinical picture that is poorly served by episodic, disease‑centered care. In line with the World Health Organization (WHO) concept of healthy ageing, defined as developing and maintaining the functional ability that enables well-being in older age, health systems must address this complexity through a fundamental reorientation of care. They should move toward models that are proactive, person-centered, and integrated. This shift requires geriatric rehabilitation, secondary prevention, and support for independent living at home to be treated as central priorities rather than peripheral concerns [1,2,5]. Rather than intervening only after acute illness or functional decline, services should anticipate risk, preserve intrinsic capacity, and adapt the environment to sustain autonomy. This also depends on improved coordination across primary care, hospital, community, and social services, allowing older adults to experience continuous support rather than fragmented episodes of treatment. However, growing demand, persistent workforce shortages, rising hospital and long‑term care costs, and geographic and socioeconomic barriers to access have created a widening gap between the need for rehabilitation and the services actually delivered, leaving large segments of older adults without timely or adequate rehabilitation support [6,7].

Within this context, digital health is a key lever to promote healthy ageing at scale and to narrow the rehabilitation gap. WHO describes digital health as a field of knowledge and practice focused on how digital technologies are developed and used to improve health and strengthen health systems, extending earlier notions of electronic health (eHealth) and mobile health (mHealth) to include connected devices, data‑driven applications, artificial intelligence (AI), and other emerging tools [8]. The WHO Global Strategy on Digital Health explicitly links these technologies to the goal of “healthy lives and well‑being for all at all ages,” emphasizing their role in person‑centered, integrated care [8]. For older adults, digital solutions can support healthy ageing by enabling remote and hybrid geriatric rehabilitation (telerehabilitation and telemedicine), home‑based exercise and self‑management programs delivered via mHealth applications, continuous monitoring of mobility, falls risk and vital signs through wearable and ambient sensors, cognitive and physical training, and platforms that reduce loneliness by maintaining social participation [9]. At the same time, the “grey digital divide” [10], defined as persistent disparities in access to devices and connectivity, digital skills, and trust in technology between older people and the rest of the population, remains a major barrier and risks worsening health inequities if not systematically addressed through age‑friendly design, co‑creation with older adults, and targeted digital inclusion policies [11]. This narrative review therefore examines how digital health can be harnessed to advance healthy ageing and geriatric rehabilitation, delineates key considerations for equitable clinical implementation, and outlines future directions for integrating digital technologies into rehabilitation pathways for older adults.

Brief history of digital health care

Although the term digital health is relatively recent, technology-mediated care has been evolving for more than a century (Fig. 1). In 1905–1906, Willem Einthoven transmitted electrocardiograms over telephone lines between the hospital and his laboratory (“telecardiograms”), showing that clinically useful physiological signals could be recorded and interpreted at a distance [12]. In the 1920s–1940s, physicians used ship‑to‑shore radio to advise crews and passengers at sea, an early form of telemedicine that extended specialist expertise far beyond hospitals [13]. From the 1960s onwards, telemetry systems developed for air‑force pilots and NASA astronauts enabled continuous monitoring of multiple vital signs in extreme environments, with later spin‑offs for intensive care and remote patient monitoring on Earth [14]. Together, these early experiments demonstrated that diagnosis, monitoring, and physician‑supervised care could safely move beyond the traditional clinic, including into remote and underserved settings [15].

From the late 20th century, attention shifted from isolated telemedicine pilots to the broader digitalization of health systems. The progressive adoption of electronic medical records (EMRs) from the 1990s onward created a digital backbone for storing and exchanging clinical information, while national and international eHealth strategies promoted interoperable health IT and health information exchange. In parallel, the public internet transformed how people access health information: by 2012, about 72% of U.S. adults who used the internet reported looking online for health information in the previous year, substantially reducing the traditional information asymmetry between clinicians and patients [16]. Secure patient portals, online appointment systems, and early mHealth applications laid the groundwork for today’s data-rich digital health ecosystem.

In parallel, wearable computing evolved from simple body-worn tools to the sensor-rich devices that now underpin much of digital health. Early examples include portable timepieces such as Peter Henlein’s early-16th-century pendant clocks and pocket watches, which made personal timekeeping a wearable capability [17,18], and 17th-century Chinese abacus rings, miniature counting devices worn on the finger by traders for on-the-go arithmetic [18]. In the early 1960s, Edward Thorp and Claude Shannon built what is widely regarded as the first wearable computer: a covert analog device that used toe-operated switches concealed in shoes and body-worn electronics to predict roulette outcomes [19]. The 1970s then brought pocket-sized electronic calculators and calculator watches; the Busicom Handy LE-120A (1971) was among the first truly pocket-sized calculators integrating processor, display, keys, memory, and battery in a single handheld unit, an architecture that foreshadowed later mobile computers and medical devices [20].

During the 1980s and 1990s, academic groups began to explore general-purpose wearable computing. Steve Mann’s backpack [21], mounted systems and later smartwatch together with Edgar Matias’s 1994 wrist computer with a half-QWERTY keyboard [22], showed that continuous, body-worn computing could support everyday tasks such as communication, note-taking, and personal information management. At the same time, personal digital assistants (PDAs) such as the Apple Newton and PalmPilot brought handheld computing into mainstream use [23]. Early wearable mixed-reality platforms in the early 2000s, such as EPFL’s sub-1-kg system that used a PDA or ultra-mobile PC as the core unit, connected to a head-mounted display, inertial tracker, and wireless interfaces [24], illustrated how compact computers could orchestrate multiple on-body sensors and displays in real-world environments.

Advances in microelectromechanical systems (MEMS) then enabled small, low-power inertial measurement units (IMUs) to be embedded in wearable nodes [25]. By the early 2000s, IMU-based systems were being developed for human motion analysis and gait assessment, providing an alternative to camera-based motion capture and opening the way for wearable movement analysis in clinical and rehabilitation settings [25]. Consumer electronics subsequently took wearable sensing to scale: Fitbit, founded in 2007 with its first tracker released in 2009, popularized continuous step counting and activity tracking for the general population [26]. Pebble’s e-paper smartwatch, launched via Kickstarter in 2012, established many of the interaction patterns of modern smartwatches [27]; and Google Glass, announced in 2012 and released to early adopters from 2013 before a broader public launch in 2014, explored head-mounted augmented reality (AR) and hands-free access to digital information [28].

Wearable electronics have since moved ever closer to the skin. Around 2011, John A. Rogers and colleagues introduced ultrathin “epidermal electronic systems”—stretchable, tattoo-like platforms that laminate onto the epidermis, match its mechanical properties, and can record electrophysiological signals, temperature, and strain while remaining comfortable for prolonged wear [29]. These devices also demonstrated wireless, battery-free operation through inductive coupling, foreshadowing today’s flexible electronic tattoos and smart patches for continuous, unobtrusive monitoring of cardiac, neuromuscular, and other physiological signals [29].

These hardware innovations underpin the current wave of digital health. Modern smartphones and wearables now function as ubiquitous sensing and communication hubs, enabling mHealth applications, continuous home monitoring, and “aging in place” solutions that capture activity, sleep, gait, and cardiovascular parameters, particularly relevant for older adults and people living with chronic conditions [30-34]. Cloud platforms and interoperable health information systems allow these data streams to be integrated with EMRs and clinician dashboards, while national digital health strategies—such as the WHO Global Strategy on Digital Health [1,8,15]—explicitly link these technologies to the goal of healthy lives and well-being for all at all ages. The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic then acted as a catalyst, triggering a rapid, large-scale expansion of telehealth and remote monitoring and demonstrating that a substantial share of outpatient care can be delivered virtually when regulatory and reimbursement barriers are relaxed [35-37].

The most recent phase adds AI and edge computing to this infrastructure [38]. Machine-learning models trained on data from wearables and other digital devices can now detect arrhythmias and structural heart disease, quantify gait and balance, estimate clinical impairment scores, and drive adaptive, closed-loop rehabilitation and coaching [38,39]. Running these models partly on-device and partly in the cloud enables real-time feedback, longitudinal tracking of functional trajectories, and remote clinician oversight at scale. For aging populations and individuals undergoing rehabilitation, this convergence of wearable sensing, connectivity, and AI is central to emerging models of proactive, data-driven, and person-centered digital health care.

Characteristics of the geriatric population

“Geriatric” is not merely a chronological label, but a distinct physiological, functional, and social phenotype shaped by the biology of aging. Aging is characterized by a progressive loss of physiological integrity and narrowing of homeostatic reserves, leading to impaired function and heightened vulnerability to seemingly minor stressors [40,41]. Musculoskeletal (MSK) decline, including sarcopenia and osteoporosis, compromises strength, balance, and gait, increasing fall and fracture risk and directly shaping rehabilitation targets [42]. Digital biomarkers for frailty, such as gait speed or variability measured via smartphones or wearables, may enable early detection and guide targeted interventions [43,44]. Sensory impairments and neurocognitive changes further constrain how older adults interact with devices and follow complex instructions [45,46]. Multimorbidity adds an additional layer of complexity. Geriatric patients often have multiple chronic conditions and polypharmacy, increasing risks of non-adherence and complications, highlighting the role of technology like smart pillboxes and reminder applications [47].

At the same time, many older adults experience a ‘double burden’ of social and digital exclusion: reduced social participation and loneliness, coupled with lower digital literacy, physical and cognitive barriers to device use, financial constraints, and heightened concerns about privacy and data security [48,49]. Nevertheless, when digital tools are perceived as useful for independence, health management, and family contact, and when adequate training is provided, many older adults are willing and able to adopt them [50].

CONSIDERATIONS FOR APPLYING DIGITAL HEALTH CARE IN GERIATRIC REHABILITATION

Purpose of digital health

Digital health technologies fulfill multiple complementary roles in geriatric rehabilitation. Their capacity to extend care beyond traditional clinical environments allows for continuous, individualized, and scalable interventions tailored to the complex needs of older adults.

Wearables for comprehensive geriatric assessment: mobility, frailty, and cognitive function

Wearable sensors enable continuous, objective assessment of mobility in real-world settings, beyond traditional gait laboratories. Miniaturized IMUs worn on the trunk or feet quantify spatiotemporal gait, postural control, and basic postures and transitions during daily life, typically using a single pendant or waist-worn sensor with minimal training and without dedicated lab infrastructure (Fig. 2) [25,51-53]. These systems can also support digital biomarkers of frailty, functional decline, and resilience [54-56]. Pendant-based accelerometers characterize 24‑hour patterns of activity and sedentary behavior from walking, standing, sitting, lying, and postural transitions, yielding metrics that discriminate frailty status and improve classification beyond clinic-based assessments [57]. Brief upper-extremity tests instrumented with a wrist-worn gyroscope use 20‑second elbow flexion–extension to generate a sensor‑based frailty index correlated with grip strength and physical activity, feasible in small spaces, at home, or via telemedicine [58-60]. Sensor-derived mobility features have also been combined into dynamic indicators of resilience; early changes in cadence, walking time, and transitions have been summarized into a chemotherapy resilience index that predicts non‑resilience soon after treatment initiation [61].

Wearables also provide quantitative markers of cognitive function and cognitive frailty [60,62-64]. Instrumented gait and balance assessments reveal subtle abnormalities, particularly under dual‑task conditions, captured by IMU‑based measures such as stride‑to‑stride variability and double‑support time that serve as digital biomarkers of cognitive frailty [63-66]. To avoid the space and safety constraints of dual‑task walking, motoric–cognitive tests based on a 20‑second elbow flexion–extension task, recorded by a single wrist‑worn sensor or standard video camera, yield AI‑derived “frailty meter” and “motoric‑cognitive meter” scores suitable for remote and in‑home assessment [58,60].

At the foot and skin level, wearable pressure and temperature sensors complement whole‑body mobility metrics by monitoring plantar loading and tissue health, enabling identification of high‑risk pressure patterns, real‑time offloading prompts, and multimodal monitoring of plantar temperature and step counts for biofeedback and remote clinician oversight [30,34,67-69]. Digital wound‑care platforms, including thermography mats and connected socks, integrate these sensor data with symptom reporting, automated alerts, and photo or video diaries to facilitate early detection of complications and timely intervention for older adults managing wounds at home [30,34,67,70,71].

Overall, these advances show that wearable technologies can deliver scalable, low‑burden assessment of mobility, frailty, resilience, cognitive function, and tissue integrity outside specialized gait laboratories, using simple form factors and automated analytics that are well suited to in‑home and diverse geriatric care settings. Because these assessments can be conducted under unsupervised conditions in real-world daily environments, wearable technologies also hold substantial potential for the quantitative analysis of a wide range of geriatric-related characteristics.

Motor training and feedback

Digital platforms also support structured therapeutic training by providing interactive, real-time feedback [72-74]. Balance rehabilitation programs often incorporate weight-shifting exercises, virtual obstacle navigation, and sway-targeting routines delivered via visual, auditory, or vibrotactile cues [72-75]. Gait retraining applications utilize techniques such as foot progression angle correction through haptic feedback and virtual reality (VR)-mediated environments to enhance motor planning and adaptability [74-76]. mHealth applications expand access to multicomponent exercise programs—including resistance, flexibility, balance, and aerobic modalities—allowing comprehensive rehabilitation in home settings with minimal supervision [77,78].

Using these digital platforms, interactive and real-time feedback can be applied to support structured therapeutic training in multiple ways (Fig. 3). First, wearable-based interactive exercise platforms use wearable inertial sensors (e.g., attached to the ankle, wrist, or trunk) to guide and quantify home exercises by providing real-time feedback on movement quality, speed, and range of motion [79]. Second, VR-based exergames transform exercises into immersive games or guided workouts, thereby enhancing engagement. These programs are useful for balance, body awareness, upper-limb, or aerobic training and can be applied even in some bedbound patients [80]. Third, extended-reality platforms, including AR- or mixed reality (MR)-based exercises, can be implemented to overlay visual cues or tasks onto the real environment, thereby guiding movements, dual-task training, and functional activities [81]. Next, smart environments and ambient exercise tools, such as instrumented floors or mats, smart treadmills, or sensor-enabled home setups, can track gait, balance, and daily functional movements, effectively transforming routine activities into therapeutic exercises [82]. In addition, AI-driven coaching and adaptive exercise platforms, combined with wearables, cameras, or smart mirrors, can provide personalized exercise prescriptions, real-time form correction, and adaptive difficulty based on performance, thereby improving exercise adherence [83]. Furthermore, robotic- and exoskeleton-assisted interactive exercise can be employed using home- or clinic-adapted robotic devices that provide assistance or resistance for repetitive, task-oriented movements, along with interactive feedback and progressive training [84]. Finally, telerehabilitation solutions enable clinicians to remotely supervise and coach exercise sessions via telemedicine, while integrating sensors or applications to monitor adherence and performance [85].

Cognitive–motor integration and motor learning

Body awareness, sensing where our limbs are and how they move, is often described as a “sixth sense” [86]. It depends on proprioception, which integrates signals from muscles, joints, skin, and the vestibular and visual systems to maintain an internal map of the body in space and to support stable gait and balance [87]. Modern motor-control theories propose that the brain builds internal forward models that predict the sensory consequences of movement; discrepancies between predicted and actual feedback (sensory prediction errors) drive motor adaptation and learning [88]. In diabetic and chemotherapy‑induced peripheral neuropathy, loss or distortion of somatosensory input from the feet and lower limbs degrades this internal model and is associated with impaired gait, reduced balance, and increased fear of falling and fall risk [89-96]. Neurorehabilitation technologies aim to restore this body–brain loop by making motor errors perceptible and actionable. Advanced systems embed motor‑learning principles, delivering real‑time visual, auditory, or haptic feedback that highlights mismatches between intended and executed movements, prompting recalibration of internal models and the acquisition of more efficient, stable movement strategies. By amplifying, substituting, or restructuring sensory cues, they can partially compensate for noisy or absent afferent signals in neuropathy and help “reconnect” body and mind [97]. To further train cognitive–motor integration, many platforms incorporate dual‑task paradigms, obstacle negotiation, and visuomotor perturbations; randomized trials show that such motor–cognitive training can improve gait and balance, enhance executive function, and reduce fall risk in older adults, including those with cognitive vulnerability, with additional benefits for mood and confidence [74-76,98].

Behavioral self-management and adherence support

Facilitating self-management is a core advantage of digital health interventions. For example, smart insoles provide multimodal alerts that prompt behavioral adjustments during daily activities. Adherence to these prompts has been shown to depend on sufficient alert frequency with improved compliance [69]. mHealth platforms also incorporate behavioral reinforcement strategies such as progress tracking, personalized goal setting, progress visualizations, rewards, and motivational messaging grounded in behavioral therapy principles [78]. Digital wound-care systems similarly leverage patient-entered data, daily reminders, and automated risk alerts to improve adherence to offloading, dressing changes, and clinical follow-up, demonstrating the cross-condition applicability of digital self-management tools in geriatric populations [67].

Information exchange and clinical integration

Digital health also improves information flow between patients and clinicians. Automated symptom reporting, daily sensor-based summaries, and structured digital diaries provide clinicians with actionable insight into patients’ home-based functioning. In wound care, such platforms have reduced communication delays, improved triage decisions, and supported early identification of complications—principles readily transferrable to geriatric rehabilitation workflows [34,67]. For successful clinical integration, technologies must align with existing rehabilitation pathways, be interoperable with eHealth records, and fit within clinician workflows. Adequate clinician training and institutional support are essential to ensuring safe implementation, particularly when care models rely on remote monitoring or telerehabilitation.

Feasibility of digital health

Digital health interventions can be practical and safe for older adults when they are grounded in user‑centered design, accessibility, and appropriate clinical oversight. Technology‑supported home‑based exercise programs using tablets, smartphones, and wearable sensors have shown good feasibility, usability, and adherence in frail and pre‑frail older adults, even with minimal face‑to‑face supervision [78,99,100]. Gamified exercise interventions (i.e., exergame) that combine structured physical activity with interactive visual feedback, personalized scoring and progression, motivational messages or notifications, and other game‑design elements are now widely used for balance, strength, and cognitive training in community‑dwelling, prefrail, and frail older adults [97,101]. Multiple trials and systematic reviews report that exergaming is generally safe, well tolerated, and perceived as enjoyable, with good short‑term adherence and measurable improvements in balance and functional performance [77,97,102,103]. The game‑like context, immediate performance feedback, and goal‑setting elements are particularly helpful for sustaining motivation during unsupervised, in‑home training. From a safety perspective, technology‑augmented programs have shown favorable profiles across heterogeneous geriatric populations [104]. Randomized trials of treadmill training combined with VR in older adults with a history of falls, mild cognitive impairment (MCI), or Parkinson’s disease report greater reductions in fall risk than conventional treadmill training without an increase in serious adverse events [76]. Sensor-based balance training has also been safely implemented in older adults with various comorbidities, including diabetic peripheral neuropathy, chronic kidney disease requiring hemodialysis, and chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy with impaired proprioception, without reports of training-related injuries or symptom exacerbation [74,78,91,97]. Reviews of active exergames in older adults also conclude that adverse events are rare and that these interventions are broadly feasible in both outpatient and home settings [103].

However, important feasibility constraints remain. Interventions that depend on sensory augmentation, such as vibrating or “stochastic resonance” insoles, require a minimum vibration perception threshold at the plantar surface. Trials in older adults and people with diabetic peripheral neuropathy show that a sizeable fraction of screened participants cannot reliably perceive even strong vibratory stimuli, which limits eligibility for these neuromodulatory approaches [105]. Smart insole systems for diabetic foot ulcer (DFU) prevention further illustrate pragmatic issues: studies report challenges with device durability, the need for repair or replacement, variable long‑term wear time, and difficulties maintaining continuous connectivity [106]. Console‑based exergame hardware also introduces ergonomic constraints. The Wii Fit Balance Board (Nintendo) is a small, fixed platform (51×31 cm) that may not accommodate wide‑based stance or protective stepping strategies in very frail users [74]. Camera‑based systems such as the Xbox Kinect (Microsoft) typically require 2–3 m of unobstructed space and a clear line of sight to the whole body, making it difficult to position a chair, rail, or walker directly in front of the participant for safety in small apartments [74]. In contrast, minimalist wearable solutions, such as a pendant or wrist‑worn inertial sensor, or smart insoles providing simple visual, auditory, or vibrotactile cues through a phone or watch, can be used while seated or standing, with or without assistive devices, and do not require a dedicated play space or specialized gaming hardware. These properties make wearable‑driven feedback particularly attractive for scalable, unsupervised, in‑home rehabilitation in routine geriatric care, if interventions are tailored to the user’s sensory capacities, living environment, and digital literacy.

Usability of digital health

Usability is a pivotal factor in the successful implementation of digital health technologies in geriatric rehabilitation, directly influencing uptake, sustained engagement, and clinical effectiveness. Older adults often face age-related sensory, cognitive, and motor limitations, making intuitive, low-complexity design essential. High-usability systems prioritize minimal cognitive burden, clear visual interfaces, and accessible interactions. Mobile applications tailored for older users frequently incorporate large touch targets, high-contrast displays, voice-guided navigation, and simplified step-by-step workflows. Such design features have enabled unsupervised use of mHealth platforms with minimal drop-off, even in prefrail populations [78].

Patient-reported outcomes consistently reflect positive user experiences. In trials involving interactive, sensor-based balance training, participants have emphasized the clarity provided by real-time visual and auditory feedback, which not only supports accurate task execution but also enhances self-awareness and engagement with therapy goals [84]. Quantitative assessments of technology acceptance further reinforce these observations. Users of smart pressure monitoring insoles, for example, rated the systems as practical, enjoyable, and suitable for integration into daily routines, reflecting both ergonomic design and functional relevance [69]. Importantly, these platforms support passive monitoring without imposing additional burden, making them particularly advantageous in populations with fatigue or mobility limitations.

However, usability limitations persist, particularly when using commercial video-gaming technologies not specifically developed for clinical populations. Systems such as the Wii Fit Balance Board or Xbox Kinect are constrained by factors including limited weight capacity, sensitivity to lighting and line-of-sight issues, and insufficient adaptability for progressive motor rehabilitation. These limitations can reduce the precision of motion tracking and the therapeutic specificity of feedback, potentially compromising outcomes in individuals requiring fine-tuned interventions. In contrast, purpose-built rehabilitation technologies—such as clinical-grade motion sensors and adjustable training interfaces—address these challenges by allowing personalized calibration, graduated task difficulty, and multi-sensory feedback modalities aligned with therapeutic goals [73].

Finally, optimizing usability in digital health solutions for older adults requires close alignment with geriatric-specific needs. Co-design approaches involving end-users, clinicians, and caregivers can ensure that technologies are not only accessible but also functionally meaningful and clinically effective in real-world settings.

Effectiveness of digital health

Digital health interventions have demonstrated clinically meaningful efficacy across diverse geriatric populations, including frail older adults, individuals with sensory impairments, and those at high risk of falls. These technologies have been shown to improve critical rehabilitation outcomes such as postural stability, gait performance, fall risk reduction, and overall frailty status.

For instance, in neuropathic conditions, sensor-based biofeedback systems deliver real-time feedback on joint and trunk motion, effectively compensating for impaired proprioception. In individuals with diabetic peripheral neuropathy, such training has led to significant reductions in postural sway—up to 58% in center-of-mass sway, 63% in ankle sway, and 72% in hip sway—with additional improvements observed under eyes-closed conditions and in mental health–related quality-of-life scores [73]. Comparable benefits have been reported among older cancer survivors with chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy, where interactive sensor-based balance training yielded significant sway reductions across multiple stance conditions, particularly among those with the poorest initial balance or more severe neuropathic symptoms [91]. Additional sensor-based motor-adaptation training programs have also shown significant clinical benefit. In a randomized trial of older adults with chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy, interactive balance training using wearable sensors produced significant reductions in hip, ankle, and center-of-mass sway across multiple stance conditions, with excellent safety and acceptability despite high baseline impairment [91]. Complementing these neuromodulatory approaches, pressure-monitoring smart insoles equipped with real-time alerts have also demonstrated clinically meaningful behavioral effects in older adults. In a three-month study, frequent alerting improved adherence to offloading and enhanced protective gait responses [69]. Collectively, these findings illustrate how sensor-based digital feedback systems can support safe, adaptive behavior in populations with sensory or functional impairments. The effects of digital health across a broader range of conditions will be addressed in the following section.

CLINICAL EVIDENCE OF DIGITAL HEALTH IN GERIATRIC REHABILITATION

Neurological/cognitive rehabilitation

Stroke

A robust and growing body of evidence supports the addition of digital modalities, such as VR, game-based exercise, smart gloves and sensor-assisted telerehabilitation platforms, to conventional post-stroke rehabilitation. Large systematic reviews and meta-analyses indicate that VR and interactive gaming, when added to usual care or alone, produce small-to-moderate improvements in upper-limb function, activity limitation, balance but not in gait speed, participation, quality-of-life [107]; However, the same review judged the overall certainty of evidence as “low to moderate” due to heterogeneity in interventions and methodological limitations. Moreover, a 2025 meta-analysis of “computer-mediated therapies” for stroke including immersive VR (IVR) and non-immersive VR (NIVR) found that both led to improvements in upper-limb activity and activities of daily living, with IVR showing a trend toward larger effect sizes than NIVR [108]. Safety data from the Cochrane review were relatively reassuring. Among the 23 studies that monitored adverse events, only a small number reported mild events, with transient dizziness being the adverse event most consistently associated with VR use [108].

Parkinson’s disease

Wearable sensors such as IMUs, smartwatches/insole and smartphone-based monitoring have increasingly been employed to capture real-world, high-frequency data on gait, tremor, and daily motor fluctuations in Parkinson’s disease, enabling more granular phenotyping than periodic clinic assessments [109,110]. Although large-scale randomized controlled trial (RCT) data remain limited, observational studies suggest this continuous monitoring supports better medication titration, relapse detection, and individualized therapy adjustments such as cueing and physiotherapy [111]. Intervention-wise, cueing devices (audio/visual/haptic) plus home-based balance exergames have, in small to moderate cohorts, reduced freezing-of-gait episodes, decreased walking task completion time, and improved gait parameters including stride, step frequency and postural stability [112]. In addition to motor symptom monitoring, recent digital health approaches have increasingly expanded toward the quantitative assessment of non-motor and subtle functional manifestations of Parkinson’s disease. Emerging studies have demonstrated that voice analysis using machine-learning algorithms can objectively detect hypophonia, dysarthria, altered speech rhythm, and other speech abnormalities that may reflect cognitive and affective dysfunction beyond overt motor impairment [113]. Similarly, AI-based video and computer-vision analyses have enabled markerless quantification of facial masking (hypomimia), bradykinesia, postural instability, and movement asymmetry using standard smartphone or webcam recordings, potentially allowing scalable remote assessment in home and community settings [114]. These multimodal digital biomarkers may facilitate earlier detection of symptom progression, continuous longitudinal monitoring, and more personalized rehabilitation strategies by integrating speech, facial expression, gait, and behavioral data into ecologically valid real-world assessments.

MCI

Computerized cognitive training (CCT) and digital brain-training platforms have been trialed in older adults with MCI or early dementia. A meta-analysis of 18 RCTs in MCI found small-to-moderate effect sizes for global cognition, memory, working memory, and a small effect for executive function [115]. Another updated meta-analysis identified that in individuals with MCI, supervised CCT significantly improved verbal, visual, and working memory, while unsupervised CCT produced smaller but measurable gains in verbal memory [116]. Moreover, among community-dwelling older adults with MCI, information and communication technology (ICT)-based cognitive interventions were associated with improvements in standardized cognitive scores, reduced depressive symptoms, and potential quality-of-life gains [117]. Nonetheless, a systematic review focusing on CCT for preventing dementia in MCI populations noted that available trials are small, heterogenous, and of low to very low quality, with no data demonstrating prevention of dementia onset or sustained long-term cognitive benefit [118]. Therefore, while CCT shows promise for improving cognitive domains in MCI or early dementia in the short to medium term, definitive evidence for long-term benefit, and durability remains lacking.

MSK rehabilitation

Frailty, sarcopenia, fall prevention

Digital approaches for frailty and sarcopenia include remotely supervised progressive resistance training delivered through application or telerehabilitation platforms, exergames and motion-capture supervised balance programs, and wearable sensor feedback for gait/balance monitoring [119]. Meta-analyses of home-based tele-exercise and exergaming show improved gait speed, balance test scores and other functional outcomes in older adults when adherence is adequate [120]. Recently, we developed Tele-Exergame (Fig. 4), a self-administered wearable-sensor system delivering virtually guided, interactive motor–cognitive exercises [78]. It provides voice guidance, real-time motion tracking and feedback, emphasizes body perception, and enables remote monitoring to support adherence and safety. In our recent RCT, we found that improvements were observed in cognitive and balance outcomes in the Tele-Exergame group. Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) increased by about one point at week 6. Balance showed a stronger response: postural sway area was nearly halved at week 6 and remained lower at week 12, whereas similar trends in controls were not significant. Despite cognitive impairment and limited technology experience, participants reported high acceptance, with perceived usefulness 78% and intention to use 82% (unpublished, ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT05235113). AI pose-estimation systems (camera-based) enable clinicians to remotely monitor exercise quality and progression, and early studies of AI-enhanced telerehabilitation report comparable functional improvements to supervised in-person programs [121]. Pose-estimation telerehabilitation platforms developed in Korea, such as those commercialized by EverEx and Neofect, have been piloted in hospital and community programs targeting older adults with frailty or postoperative weakness. Implementation requires structured safety screening, caregiver support for high-fall-risk patients, and clear protocols for emergency escalation and fall-mitigation when delivering home-based or unsupervised digital exercise to frail older adults [122]. Beyond monitoring gait speed, balance, or exercise adherence, recent studies have begun exploring whether computer-vision and pose-estimation approaches can also estimate muscle strength and muscle power from functional movement tasks. In particular, sit-to-stand performance analyzed using 2D or 3D pose-estimation algorithms has shown promise as a surrogate marker of lower-extremity muscle function in older adults [123]. Hwang et al. [124] recently demonstrated that sit-to-stand power derived from 2D pose estimation was significantly associated with conventional measures of muscle strength and physical performance in older adults, suggesting that camera-based assessment may provide a scalable and low-burden method for screening sarcopenia and functional decline. These developments suggest that computer vision may expand the role of digital rehabilitation beyond exercise supervision and mobility monitoring toward quantitative evaluation of muscle function and functional reserve in real-world geriatric settings.

Chronic MSK pain, fibromyalgia, and long COVID

Digital care pathways for chronic MSK pain, typically combining app‑based exercise therapy, education, behavioral support, and remote coaching, have produced clinically meaningful reductions in pain and improvements in function across multiple randomized trials and large real-world cohorts [125]. Large pragmatic cohort studies of commercial digital MSK programs report rapid and sustained reductions in pain scores and functional gains at 6–12 weeks, with many users achieving clinically meaningful improvement by 3 months [126]. Claims‑based observational analyses extend these findings to downstream utilization. Compared with matched patients receiving in‑person physical therapy (PT), participants in a digital MSK program had substantially lower odds of opioid initiation and fewer opioid prescriptions over 12 months [127], and a recent nationwide matched‑cohort study found ~58% lower overall MSK surgery rates and ~82% lower rates of low‑value procedures, accompanied by reduced health care utilization and spending [128].

Adjunctive digital wearables and neuromodulation devices, particularly wearable transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS), add another layer of nonpharmacologic support. A RCT of a calf‑worn wearable TENS device in fibromyalgia showed modest but significant improvements in disease impact, pain, and functional interference compared with sham, with larger effects in individuals with higher pain sensitivity [129]. A pilot randomized trial in people with long COVID and fibromyalgia‑like symptoms found that high‑dose wearable TENS over 4 weeks reduced pain interference and improved fatigue and gait parameters relative to low‑dose stimulation [130]. Collectively, these studies support the potential of digital MSK programs, supplemented by wearable neuromodulation, to deliver clinically relevant pain and functional benefits in chronic MSK and related post‑viral pain syndromes.

Economic evaluations and real-world cost analyses suggest potential cost offsets (fewer imaging studies, lower procedure rates, reduced absenteeism) with digital MSK programs, but long-term independent cost-effectiveness studies are still limited and further unbiased health-economic research is needed [131]. Meta-analyses and systematic reviews of mHealth and software-based interventions for chronic low back pain and other MSK disorders report overall small-to-moderate pooled effects on pain and function, but heterogeneity between trials and intervention types is substantial [132]. Finally, publication bias and industry sponsorship are acknowledged concerns in this field; therefore, interpretation of vendor-reported outcomes should be balanced with independent RCTs, registry data, and systematic reviews when forming clinical or policy decisions [133].

Postoperative rehabilitation

Digital care pathways after joint replacement are increasingly using diverse forms including virtual- PT with avatars, smart-device–assisted telerehabilitation, asynchronous app-based exercise libraries, markerless motion-capture telerehabilitation, and AR/VR enhanced rehab modules rather than solely videoconference-based remote PT. Meta-analyses and randomized trials examining these varied digital modalities suggest that telerehabilitation can deliver functional outcomes comparable to, and in some measures better than, conventional center-based or in-person PT programs in selected patients [134,135]. For example, a large meta-analysis (25 RCTs, 4,402 patients) focusing on total knee arthroplasty (TKA) found that telerehabilitation, including smart device-assisted rehabilitation, resulted in statistically significant improvements over conventional rehabilitation in pain, passive flexion range of motion, quadriceps strength, and lower cost [136]. A recent protocol publication describes use of markerless human pose estimation (via standard cameras/smartphones) to deliver standardized exercise programs and real-time posture/motion feedback after meniscus surgery, with potential for scalable rehabilitation without specialized hardware [137].

Cardiopulmonary rehabilitation

Home‑based and telehealth‑delivered cardiac rehabilitation and pulmonary rehabilitation (CR/PR) delivered through smartphone apps, wearable sensors, and tele‑consultations has now been tested in multiple randomized trials and real‑world cohorts. A recent meta‑analysis of 16 RCTs (n=4,557) in heart‑failure patients found that home‑based cardiac telerehabilitation significantly improved peak VO2, 6‑minute walk distance, health‑related quality of life, and reduced rehospitalization compared with usual care, without any loss of left‑ventricular function relative to center‑based CR [138]. A complementary meta‑analysis focusing on home‑based cardiac telerehabilitation supported by wearable electrocardiogram (ECG)/heart‑rate monitors (>1,300 participants) showed additional gains in cardiac function and emotional well‑being compared with controls [139]. These findings align with consensus statements from major cardiology societies, which now recognize home‑based CR as an evidence‑based alternative for patients who cannot attend center‑based programs.

Similar trends are emerging in oncology and pulmonary care. A 2024 meta‑analysis of telehealth exercise‑based rehabilitation in cancer survivors (10 RCTs, n=855) reported significant improvements in cardiorespiratory fitness and physical activity, with high retention and low rates of serious adverse events [140]. In cardio‑oncology, single‑arm telerehabilitation programs for hematologic malignancy survivors have demonstrated good adherence (78%–80%), clinically meaningful gains in peak VO2, and no serious exercise‑related events [141]. Most recently, our group evaluated a 12‑week telehealth pulmonary rehabilitation (TPR) program for U.S. veterans with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) delivered via VA Video Connect: 44 of 51 enrolled veterans (86%) completed the program, with a mean 41‑m (≈16%) increase in 6‑minute walk distance, faster Timed Up‑and‑Go and Sit‑to‑Stand performance, marked improvements in COPD‑specific quality‑of‑life scores, and no reported falls or exercise‑related injuries [99].

Beyond supervised exercise, digital health tools are increasingly used for remote patient monitoring and early detection of decompensation. For example, a recent AI- and wearable-based platform demonstrated feasibility in continuously monitoring post-discharge heart-failure (HF) patients and used machine-learning models to predict clinical deterioration with high sensitivity and specificity, indicating the potential for proactive, data-driven management to prevent rehospitalization [142]. In this multicenter study, a chest‑worn multisensor patch plus cloud‑based machine‑learning analytics monitored patients for three months after HF hospitalization and predicted impending HF rehospitalization with 76%–88% sensitivity and 85% specificity, with alerts occurring a median of 6.5 days before admission [142]. This illustrates how combining wearables with AI‑driven analytics could shift CR/PR from reactive to proactive care. At the same time, qualitative work and technology‑acceptance reviews in older cardiac patients show that usability, adherence, and satisfaction are generally high when programs use simple interfaces, clear instructions, remote technical support, and flexible, individualized exercise prescriptions—supporting the feasibility and acceptability of digital CR/PR even in older adults with multimorbidity [143].

Other conditions

Dysphagia

Telepractice models, in which speech-language pathologists deliver assessment and therapy remotely via synchronous videoconferencing, have proven feasible and acceptable for older adults with neurogenic or post-operative dysphagia and can maintain continuity of care when in-person services are limited [144]. Surface electromyography biofeedback applied to swallowing exercises allows clinicians to give real-time feedback on swallowing muscle activity and has been associated with improved swallow biomechanics and patient-reported swallowing function in several trials and pilot studies [145,146]. Game-based and digitally augmented swallowing programs improve engagement and adherence in older patients, which in turn mediates functional gains in some single-site randomized trials and feasibility studies [144]. Finally, practical deployment in geriatric rehabilitation requires attention to safety such as aspiration risk triage, caregiver-assisted positioning and device handling, broadband/tech support, and pathways for rapid escalation to in-person swallowing evaluation when clinical red flags appear

DFU

Sensorized remote monitoring (plantar temperature mats, smart socks, and smart insoles) is the most mature digital approach for DFU prevention, with studies showing that persistent focal temperature asymmetry or “hotspot” detection frequently precedes ulceration and can be used to trigger early clinical review or activity off-loading [70]. Our group has contributed to the development and evaluation of sensor-based DFU prevention pathways, arguing that daily foot monitoring combined with rapid clinical triage could extend “ulcer-free days” in remission cohorts, and publishing both feasibility work and programmatic evaluations supporting reduced recurrence risk when integrated into care [30,34,35,67,68]. Additionally, image-based wound assessment using smartphone photography and AI permits remote triage, objective area/volume tracking, and clinician review that can accelerate healing decisions and reduce unnecessary travel for frail older patients [67,147]. Randomized and observational program data suggest remote monitoring approaches, including temperature mats, smart socks, image review, and clinician workflow, are associated with earlier detection of pre-ulcerative changes, fewer severe ulcer events in high-risk cohorts, and potential reductions in downstream resource use, although meta-analyses caution heterogeneity in study designs and emphasize the need for randomized effectiveness studies and cost-effectiveness analyses [148].

CURRENT CHALLENGES AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

Despite rapid growth in digital health technologies, their integration into geriatric rehabilitation is still constrained by patient heterogeneity, multimorbidity, fragmented workflows, and unequal access to care. Systematic reviews and international consensus work emphasize that eHealth for geriatric rehabilitation is promising but characterized by clinical heterogeneity, modest study quality, and limited reporting on usability or long‑term outcomes in the frailest patients [149,150]. Without deliberate attention to these issues, digital interventions risk improving short‑term surrogate metrics without translating into durable functional gains, reduced disability, or equitable outcomes for older adults (Fig. 5).

Limited generalizability, fragmented evidence, and heterogeneous outcomes

Systematic reviews of digital health in geriatric rehabilitation show that many trials exclude individuals with cognitive impairment, severe sensory loss, or complex multimorbidity, limiting generalizability to “real‑world” geriatric rehabilitation populations [150]. Interventions, settings, and comparators are highly diverse, and outcomes often focus on short‑term biomechanical or sensor‑derived metrics (e.g., sway, gait variability, step counts) rather than hard endpoints such as falls, hospitalization, institutionalization, functional independence, or participation. Follow‑up periods are frequently limited to weeks or a few months, making it difficult to determine whether early improvements are sustained [150].

Future work should prioritize inclusive, adequately powered trials and pragmatic hybrid effectiveness–implementation designs that actively recruit individuals with cognitive, sensory, and mobility impairments rather than excluding them [151]. Standardized core outcome sets that combine digital biomarkers with outcomes that matter to older adults and clinicians, including falls, independence, participation, health‑related quality of life, and caregiver burden are needed, alongside longer follow‑up and linkage to routinely collected data. Adaptive protocols and stratified analyses can then help identify which subgroups benefit most from specific digital interventions [152,153].

Usability barriers and digital literacy

Age‑related declines in vision, hearing, dexterity, and executive function, together with digital literacy gaps, remain major barriers to adoption and sustained use of digital tools [154].

These barriers can be addressed through the application of geriatric-centered design principles and implementation strategies. Recommended strategies include: high‑contrast interfaces, large touch targets, simplified navigation, and multimodal (visual, auditory, haptic) feedback; structured onboarding, brief hands‑on training augmented with AI chatbots, voice-enabled solution, and ongoing technical support; co‑design with older adults, caregivers, and clinicians; and provision of devices, connectivity, or community “access hubs” for those without reliable internet or hardware [155]. Emerging AI‑based personalization and conversational agents may help adapt difficulty, coaching, and reminders to individual needs, but must be implemented with clear guardrails for safety, transparency, and workload [149].

Integration into clinical workflows

Many digital health solutions are implemented as stand‑alone pilots rather than embedded into established rehabilitation pathways. Common problems include lack of interoperability with eHealth records, unclear clinical responsibility for monitoring sensor or app data, limited time and training for clinicians, and uncertain reimbursement models [67,150,156]. These factors can increase clinician workload and documentation burden instead of relieving it, undermining long‑term adoption. Future implementation should focus on tightly integrating digital platforms with electronic health records (EHRs) and existing rehabilitation workflows; defining clear roles and escalation protocols for data review; and using automated triage, alerts, and dashboard summaries to surface only clinically actionable information [35].

Data governance, privacy, and ethical concerns

Continuous monitoring of mobility, physiology, and home environments generates large volumes of sensitive data. Ethical analyses in geriatric digital health underscore three recurring concerns: equity (who benefits and who is left out), privacy and surveillance, and data responsibility (who controls, secures, and is accountable for data use and algorithmic decisions) [157-159]. These issues are particularly salient for older adults with cognitive impairment, limited digital literacy, or dependence on caregivers. Future digital health strategies for older adults should adopt privacy‑by‑design and security‑by‑design principles; provide clear, comprehensible consent processes and options to opt out; ensure that algorithms are transparent, explainable, and routinely audited for bias; and delineate shared responsibilities among patients, caregivers, clinicians, health systems, and technology vendors [159].

Psychosocial and organizational determinants of adoption

Finally, improving adoption of digital health in geriatric rehabilitation cannot rely on usability enhancements alone. Telehealth and digital health adoption studies consistently show lower use among the oldest old, those with lower education or income, rural residents, ethnic minority groups, and individuals with limited broadband access, reinforcing a “digital divide” that risks amplifying existing health inequities [49]. Implementation studies in home‑based and institutional rehabilitation show that success depends on the broader psychosocial and organizational context: older adults’ fears and motivations, caregiver burden, clinician attitudes, leadership support, and local resources [160]. Interventions that embed digital tools within supportive ecosystems, linking family caregivers, community services, and multidisciplinary rehabilitation teams are more likely to achieve sustained engagement and real‑world effectiveness. Future directions include applying implementation‑science frameworks (e.g., Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research [CFIR]; Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, and Maintenance [RE‑AIM]) to design and evaluate digital geriatric rehabilitation programs; investing in interdisciplinary education for clinicians on digital and AI‑enabled care; building caregiver‑centered support infrastructures; and fostering organizational cultures that view digital health as a tool to enhance, rather than replace, relationship‑centered geriatric care [49,151,156].

CONCLUSION

Digital health has emerged as a transformative enabler in geriatric rehabilitation, offering scalable, personalized, and data-driven solutions to address the growing gap between care needs and available resources. Wearable sensors, mobile applications, telerehabilitation, and AI-assisted platforms facilitate continuous monitoring, motor and cognitive training, self-management, and enhanced patient–clinician communication. Evidence to date demonstrates that these technologies can improve functional outcomes, adherence, and quality of life across diverse geriatric populations. Despite these advances, challenges remain. Methodological heterogeneity, the grey digital divide, accessibility limitations, and data governance concerns continue to constrain widespread adoption. Future efforts must prioritize human-centered design, inclusive trial methodologies, interoperability, and integrated care pathways that consider psychosocial, caregiver, and organizational factors. Moreover, AI and emerging digital modalities require ethical, safe, and contextually adapted frameworks for deployment.
