Bacteriophage Therapy in Cystic Fibrosis: Emerging Strategies for Managing Chronic Pseudomonas aeruginosa Infections
Authors: Ayona Puthenpurayil, Lauren Gordon, Joanne L Fothergill
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPseudomonas aeruginosa is the leading cause of chronic respiratory infection in people with cystic fibrosis (pwCF) and remains a major driver of morbidity and premature mortality. The unique environment of the CF lung promotes persistent colonisation, biofilm formation, and the emergence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) phenotypes, severely limiting the effectiveness of conventional antibiotic therapies. Despite aggressive and prolonged antibiotic regimens, eradication of chronic P. aeruginosa infection is rarely achieved, and repeated exposure accelerates resistance development. Consequently, there is an urgent need for alternative or adjunctive therapeutic strategies. Bacteriophage (phage) therapy has re-emerged as a promising approach for treating MDR P. aeruginosa infections due to its strain specificity, self-amplifying activity, and capacity to disrupt biofilms. Importantly, phage-driven selection pressure can impose fitness costs on bacteria, including reduced virulence and increased susceptibility to antibiotics, providing a potential route to antimicrobial resensitisation. However, translating phage therapy into routine clinical practice faces challenges, particularly in the context of CF lung disease, where heterogeneous bacterial populations, thick mucus, biofilms, and host immune responses complicate treatment efficacy. This scoping review synthesises current understanding of P. aeruginosa pathogenesis in CF, focusing on antibiotic resistance mechanisms, virulence factors that function as phage receptors, and the evolutionary trade-offs exploited by phage therapy, including the role of phage-encoded depolymerases that degrade protective biofilms and bacterial defence mechanisms that drive phage resistance. We critically evaluate clinical evidence supporting phage use in pwCF, highlighting key limitations in existing studies, and priorities for future research required to establish phage therapy as a viable, regulated treatment option.Keywords: Pseudomonas aeruginosa, bacteriophage, cystic fibrosis, receptors, antibiotic resistance

Researchers are therefore exploring new treatment options, and one of the most encouraging is bacteriophage (phage) therapy. Phages are naturally occurring viruses that selectively infect and kill specific bacteria. They can multiply quickly at the site of infection and, through specialised proteins, help break down the protective layer surrounding bacterial communities. In some cases, when bacteria become resistant to phages, they may become less harmful or more vulnerable to antibiotics, potentially restoring the effectiveness of existing treatments. However, using phages to treat pwCF is still challenging. The lungs contain thick mucus, complex bacterial communities and immune responses that may influence how well phages work.

This scoping review explains how P. aeruginosa causes and sustains infections in pwCF and explores how phages may help overcome some limitations of current treatments. We review current evidence for phage therapy in CF, discuss challenges that may affect its success, and highlight the research needed before it can be widely used in clinical practice. Although further clinical trials are required, phage therapy has the potential to become an important addition to existing treatments for chronic CF lung infections and may help address the growing challenge of antibiotic resistance.

Pseudomonas aeruginosa: A Persistent Respiratory Pathogen

Pseudomonas aeruginosa is a Gram-negative opportunistic pathogen responsible for severe acute and chronic infections, particularly in immunocompromised individuals and hospitalised patients.1,2 Its clinical importance stems from a combination of intrinsic resistance to multiple antibiotic classes, a remarkable capacity to acquire additional resistance determinants, and the ability to survive within and adapt to hostile host environments.3,4 As a result, P. aeruginosa has been designated a priority pathogen by the World Health Organisation.5

In respiratory disease, P. aeruginosa is particularly problematic due to its capacity to colonise damaged airways, form biofilms, and evade both antimicrobial and immune-mediated clearance.6,7 These traits encourage its dominance in chronic lung infections and make eradication difficult even with intensive antibiotic therapy. This challenge is most evident in Cystic Fibrosis (CF), where P. aeruginosa infection often becomes lifelong.8 As a result, P. aeruginosa remains one of the most clinically significant pathogens in CF and a major focus of therapeutic development. This scoping review therefore focuses on the mechanisms underlying P. aeruginosa persistence, biofilm formation and antimicrobial resistance in CF and evaluates bacteriophage (phage) therapy as a complementary therapeutic strategy that exploits bacterial evolutionary trade-offs to restore antibiotic susceptibility and improve clinical outcomes.

P. aeruginosa Infection in Cystic Fibrosis (CF)

CF is a genetic disorder caused by mutations in the CF transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) gene, resulting in impaired ion transport and dehydrated airway surface liquid.9,10 This leads to thick, viscous mucus, defective mucociliary clearance, and chronic airway inflammation, collectively creating an environment highly permissive to bacterial persistence.11 P. aeruginosa is the predominant cause of chronic respiratory infection in people with CF (pwCF), with up to 70% of patients developing long-term colonisation over their lifetime.2,12 The prevalence of P. aeruginosa infection increases with age, affecting approximately 5–20% of children aged ≤2 years and 40–50% of adults aged 30–45 years.13 Early (acute) infections are often eradicated with antibiotics; however, repeated acquisition of genetically distinct strains from environmental sources is common during childhood. Over time, one adapted strain typically dominates, establishing chronic infection characterised by biofilm growth, mucoid phenotypes, and extensive within-host evolution. This progression from acute to chronic infection is a defining feature of CF lung disease and has important implications for treatment responses and disease outcome.14

Chronic P. aeruginosa infection in pwCF is strongly associated with recurrent pulmonary exacerbations, progressive lung function decline and increased mortality.15 This burden is further emphasised by reports linking P. aeruginosa infection to a 2.6-fold increase in 8-year mortality among children with CF.16 Once established, eradication is rarely successful, reflecting the combined impact of antibiotic resistance, biofilm-mediated tolerance, and immune evasion. Notably, most studies describing strain dominance and adaptation rely on cross-sectional sampling, limiting insight into the temporal dynamics of within-host evolution that may influence therapeutic response.17 This is particularly relevant when interpreting clinical trial outcomes, where evolutionary trajectories within individual patients may differ substantially from population-level trends captured by cross-sectional designs.

P. aeruginosa populations associated with CF are highly diverse and whilst most strains are acquired from the environment, transmissible epidemic lineages have circulated within and between CF centres. Several epidemic or transmissible lineages, including the Liverpool Epidemic Strain (LES), Prairie Epidemic Strain and Danish epidemic lineages, have been associated with persistence, antimicrobial resistance and, in some settings, poorer clinical outcomes.18,19 Importantly, P. aeruginosa does not remain genetically or phenotypically static following establishment of chronic infection. Prolonged persistence within the CF airway drives extensive within-host evolution and diversification, including selection for mucoidy and biofilm-associated growth, altered motility and virulence, hypermutability, antimicrobial resistance and metabolic adaptation.20 Consequently, individual patients may harbour multiple co-existing P. aeruginosa subpopulations with distinct phenotypes and antimicrobial resistance profiles. This diversity has particular relevance to bacteriophage therapy. Phage susceptibility can vary substantially between strains and between isolates recovered from the same patient, while chronic adaptation can alter surface structures used as phage receptors. The presence of heterogeneous populations raises an important practical limitation for personalised phage therapy: susceptibility testing of a single colony may not capture the diversity of phage susceptibility within a chronically infected airway, potentially resulting in selection of phages that target only a subset of the infecting population. A further limitation is that there is currently no universally accepted definition or standardised method for determining clinically relevant phage susceptibility. Conventional plaque-based assays performed under planktonic laboratory conditions may poorly predict phage activity against slow-growing, mucoid or biofilm-associated populations within CF sputum.

Despite highly effective CFTR modulators such as elexacaftor/tezacaftor/ivacaftor improving respiratory symptoms and reducing bacterial burden in eligible individuals, chronic P. aeruginosa infections persist and continue to evolve across the wider pwCF population, highlighting that while modulator therapy enhances clinical outcomes, it does not fully eradicate established airway infections.21 Furthermore, some individuals are ineligible for modulators, particularly those with Class I CFTR mutations for whom infection management remains almost entirely dependent on conventional antimicrobial treatment, underscoring the need for novel therapies across all CF genotypes.

Multidrug-Resistance (MDR) and Evolutionary Constraints

Management of P. aeruginosa infection in CF relies on antipseudomonal antibiotics, including beta-lactams (eg, meropenem), aminoglycosides (eg, tobramycin), fluoroquinolones (eg, ciprofloxacin) and, as a last resort option, polymyxins (eg, colistin), administered alone or in combination depending on infection severity and susceptibility profiles.22,23 However, prolonged and repeated use of these antibiotics has accelerated the emergence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) P. aeruginosa strains, particularly in adult pwCF with cumulative antibiotic exposure.3,24 MDR is typically defined as resistance to at least one agent in three or more antimicrobial classes.25 Antibiotic overuse creates a selective pressure favouring bacteria that possess antibiotic-resistance genes (ARGs), enabling them to survive in the presence of antibiotics that would have previously been effective.26 In CF, MDR P. aeruginosa contributes to reduced antibiotic efficacy, more frequent pulmonary exacerbations, and irreversible lung function decline.27 Recognition of carbapenem-resistant P. aeruginosa as a “critical” priority pathogen underscores the urgency of developing alternative therapeutic strategies.28 Current treatment paradigms remain largely reactive, relying on high antibiotic intensity with limited success in clearing chronic colonisation; in the absence of eradication, treatments may themselves act as drivers of further resistance. No current treatments address the evolutionary drivers of resistance.20 This has renewed interest in non-antibiotic interventions, including phage therapy for managing chronic MDR infections in pwCF.

An additional challenge in managing chronic P. aeruginosa infection is the role of evolutionary constraints in shaping the emergence of MDR. While antibiotic exposure drives selection for resistant strains, resistance evolution is restricted and is often associated with trade-offs in bacterial fitness, virulence, or metabolic efficiency. Certain resistance mechanisms may reduce growth rate or impair competitiveness in the absence of antibiotics, while adaptation to one antimicrobial can increase susceptibility to another through collateral sensitivity. Understanding these evolutionary constraints is increasingly recognised as important in the development of sustainable treatment strategies, as therapies that exploit fitness trade-offs or limit adaptive pathways may help suppress the emergence and persistence of MDR strains. Consequently, evolution-informed therapies may not only reduce bacterial burden but also influence the evolutionary trajectories available to P. aeruginosa, potentially slowing the development of further resistance.

Antibiotic Resistance Mechanisms in P. aeruginosa and Implications for Phage-Driven Resensitisation

The persistence of P. aeruginosa in the CF lung is underpinned by a combination of intrinsic, acquired, and adaptive resistance mechanisms that collectively reduce antibiotic efficacy.3,4 While these mechanisms complicate treatment, they also create exploitable evolutionary vulnerabilities that can be targeted by phages.

Intrinsic and Acquired Resistance

Intrinsic resistance in P. aeruginosa arises from low outer membrane permeability, constitutive efflux pump activity, and the production of antibiotic-inactivating enzymes.3,20 Restricted porin-mediated uptake limits intracellular antibiotic accumulation, while resistance-nodulation-division (RND) efflux pumps such as MexAB-OprM and MexXY-OprM actively expel diverse antimicrobial agents.21,22 In parallel, chromosomal encoded β-lactamases and aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes degrade or modify antibiotics before they reach their targets.26,27

Acquired resistance further amplifies these defences through mutations in regulatory and structural genes or via horizontal gene transfer (HGT) of ARGs carried on plasmids, integrons, and prophages.29,30 In CF, where chronic infections persist for years to decades, prolonged within-host colonisation may increase opportunities for HGT events, facilitating the gradual accumulation of resistance determinants that may not occur as frequently in acute infections.31 Loss or modification of the OprD porin, for example, is a dominant mechanism of carbapenem resistance in clinical isolates, while mutations in efflux pump regulators drive overexpression and MDR.32,33 Resistance mechanisms in P. aeruginosa are reviewed in detail by Langendonk et al.34

Adaptive Resistance and Tolerance in the CF Lung

Beyond stable genetic resistance, P. aeruginosa exhibits adaptive resistance in response to environmental stressors prevalent in the CF lung, including hypoxia, nutrient limitation, and antibiotic exposure.35 Biofilm development and the formation of persister cells confer transient tolerance to antibiotics without genetic resistance mutations.7,36 These phenotypes contribute to treatment failure and relapse, particularly during chronic infection. Importantly, most resistance studies rely on planktonic models that poorly reflect the polymicrobial, biofilm-dominated CF lung environment, limiting their translational relevance.6,14 Some studies have begun to address this by using in vitro biofilm models and artificial sputum media, which better recapitulate the oxygen gradients, altered nutrient conditions and viscosity characteristic of the CF lung.37

Phage-Driven Evolutionary Trade-Offs and Antimicrobial Resensitisation

While antibiotic pressure typically selects for increasingly resistant phenotypes, phage predation can drive alternative evolutionary trajectories. Resistance to phages often arises through modification or loss of bacterial surface structures that function as phage receptors, including lipopolysaccharide (LPS), type IV pili (T4P), and flagella (Figure 1).38,39 Crucially, these structures are linked to key bacterial functions including virulence and motility. As a result, phage-resistant mutants may incur fitness costs, such as reduced virulence, impaired biofilm formation, and increased susceptibility to antibiotics - a phenomenon described as collateral sensitivity or resensitisation.40–45 These trade-offs provide a strong rationale for integrating phages into treatment strategies for MDR P. aeruginosa, particularly when combined with antibiotics to exploit phage-antibiotic synergy (PAS).46,47 However, the extent of these fitness trade-offs depends on the specific resistance mutation, bacterial strain and selective pressure. In some cases, phage-resistant mutants retain full fitness and virulence despite acquiring resistance.48 Thus, phage-driven resensitisation is not a universal outcome and may require experimental validation in clinically relevant strains before therapeutic use.49

Virulence Factors Relevant to Phage Therapy: Focus on Phage Receptors

P. aeruginosa expresses a wide array of virulence factors that promote colonisation, immune evasion, and persistence in the CF lung.50 From a phage therapy perspective, those virulence determinants that also function as phage receptors are of particular importance, as they directly shape phage host range, resistance evolution, and therapeutic efficacy. The diversity of these phage receptors across P. aeruginosa strains circulating within the CF lung means that even prior to resistance evolution, initial phage host range may be restricted, reinforcing the importance of strain-matched personalised phage selection and cocktail design. Virulence factors of P. aeruginosa have been reviewed in detail by Swain et al.51

Among these receptors, LPS is a major component of the outer membrane and a common target of lytic phages infecting P. aeruginosa.28 Structural modifications to LPS can confer phage resistance; however, these changes often compromise membrane integrity, reduce virulence, and increase susceptibility to antibiotics and host immune factors.38,52 In the CF lung, LPS alterations have also been linked to impaired biofilm architecture, suggesting that phage selection against LPS may weaken bacterial persistence. Similarly, T4P are multifunctional surface appendages involved in adhesion, twitching motility, and biofilm initiation.53,54 They also serve as receptors for multiple P. aeruginosa phages and are frequently targeted during phage therapy.39 Loss or modification of T4P to evade phage infection can reduce motility and colonisation efficiency, potentially attenuating virulence and impairing biofilm development in the CF lung. Flagella represent another important phage receptor that contributes to motility, chemotaxis, and early biofilm formation and is recognised by both the host immune system and certain phages.55 Phage-driven selection against flagellar structures may reduce bacterial dissemination and inflammatory stimulation but can also alter immune recognition dynamics, highlighting the need to balance therapeutic benefit with potential host-pathogen interactions.

Beyond receptor modification, receptor accessibility can be influenced by the emergence of mucoidy during chronic CF infection. The mucoid phenotype is characterised by overproduction of alginate, a key biofilm matrix component.56,57 Alginate can mask underlying phage receptors and physically impede phage access to bacterial cells.58 However, phages encoding alginate depolymerases can degrade this component, restoring access to receptors and enhancing bacterial clearance.59,60 Not all phages encode these specific depolymerases, and the absence of this activity may limit phage efficacy against mucoid P. aeruginosa isolates that dominate in chronic lung infections in CF, representing an important consideration in phage selection for this patient group. Furthermore, depolymerase activity is generally substrate-specific and activity against purified polysaccharides or laboratory biofilms does not necessarily predict effective degradation of the chemically and structurally heterogeneous matrix encountered in CF sputum. This limitation could be addressed by genetically engineering phages to carry specific depolymerases, but such genetic modifications raise additional regulatory and environmental concerns that may hinder clinical use.

Biofilms, Quorum Sensing, and Persister Cells: Barriers and Opportunities for Phage Therapy

Biofilm formation is a defining feature of chronic P. aeruginosa infection in pwCF and a major contributor to antibiotic tolerance.6 The structural integrity of P. aeruginosa biofilms is primarily maintained by three exopolysaccharides (EPS) including Pel, Psl and alginate, which act as physical barriers to antibiotics, phages, and host immune cells. The relative contribution of each EPS to biofilm structure depends on the infection stage and strain genetics; Pel and Psl are mainly utilised during early infection, while alginate becomes the dominant matrix component in mucoid strains, particularly during chronic infection. These shifts directly impact phage selection, as the effectiveness of depolymerase encoding phages depends on targeting the dominant EPS component in a given infection.61,62 Biofilm development is regulated by quorum sensing (QS), a bacterial cell-to-cell communication system that coordinates population density-dependent gene expression. Biofilms also harbour persister cells, a metabolically inactive bacterial subpopulation capable of surviving antibiotic exposure and subsequently repopulating the infection following treatment.63,64

The biofilm-associated properties present considerable challenges for phage therapy. As effective phage replication is dependent on active host cell metabolism, the dormant state of persister cells may limit phage replication within established biofilms and subsequent lysis of embedded bacteria. However, certain phages have been shown to infect deep dormant persister cells, which is important when considering phage therapy for chronic lung infections characterised by dense, mature biofilm communities.65,66 Additionally, given that QS regulates the expression of surface-exposed phage receptors, changes in QS activity within biofilms may affect receptor availability and therefore phage infection efficacy.67 Despite these barriers, phages can exploit biofilm heterogeneity and replicate within bacterial aggregates, particularly when equipped with depolymerases.7,58 Furthermore, phage-mediated biofilm disruption can increase antibiotic penetration and resensitise bacteria to previously ineffective treatments, supporting the rationale for combined phage-antibiotic therapy.46,47,66

The polymicrobial composition of CF biofilms may also influence phage efficacy. Although P. aeruginosa often becomes the dominant CF pathogen, it coexists within biofilms alongside other microorganisms, including Staphylococcus aureus, Haemophilus influenzae, Stenotrophomonas maltophilia, Burkholderia spp., anaerobes and fungi such as Aspergillus fumigatus. Interactions between these organisms can modify biofilm architecture, potentially limiting phage access to target bacteria through increased matrix production and spatial shielding. Moreover, the host specificity of phages may alter microbial composition by selectively reducing susceptible bacterial populations, potentially facilitating the expansion of non-target organisms. Therefore, understanding phage activity within the complex polymicrobial communities of the CF airway remains an important translational challenge.68–71

Phage Therapy: Principles, Advantages, and Limitations

Phage therapy employs strictly lytic phages (Figure 2) to selectively infect and lyse bacterial pathogens and has re-emerged as a favourable strategy for managing MDR P. aeruginosa infections. Lytic phages are preferred for therapeutic use as they replicate within and destroy their bacterial hosts without integrating into the bacterial genome, thereby avoiding HGT of virulence or ARGs - a particular concern in P. aeruginosa, where temperate phages are prevalent.72–74 Unlike antibiotics, phages replicate at the site of infection, exhibit narrow host specificity, and largely spare commensal microbiota.75,76 These properties are particularly advantageous in pwCF, where repeated and prolonged antibiotic exposure accelerates resistance development and microbial dysbiosis contributes to disease progression. Another key benefit of phages in this context is their reliance on bacterial surface receptors that are also central virulence and persistence determinants.39 In addition to direct antibacterial activity, phages may promote antimicrobial resensitisation through receptor-mediated evolutionary trade-offs.38,52

Two main replication strategies of phages: lytic and lysogenic cycle. In the lytic cycle, the phage injects its genetic material into the bacterial host and hijacks the host cell machinery to synthesise viral components. These components assemble into new phage virions, which are released by bacterial cell lysis. In contrast, the lysogenic cycle (exclusive to temperate phages) involves the integration of the phage genome into the host chromosome as a prophage which is passed on to daughter host cells during replication. Despite this promise, phage therapy faces important limitations. Phage host range is often strain-specific, necessitating personalised or cocktail-based approaches.79 However, the clinical implementation of personalised phage therapy presents logistical considerations. Effective treatment relies on rapid phage susceptibility testing of patient isolates and access to well-curated phage libraries or biobanks containing phages active against a range of clinically relevant pathogens. For individuals with severe MDR infections, the time required for isolate characterisation and phage matching may delay treatment. Therefore, standardised testing methods and rapid phage-selection workflows will be essential for the wider adoption of personalised phage therapy in clinical practice.80

Although phage therapy has primarily been investigated for chronic MDR infections, its use during acute infection should also be explored. Early intervention may reduce bacterial burden before chronic colonisation, within-host adaptation and antimicrobial resistance emerge. Phage therapy has demonstrated efficacy in preclinical animal models of acute P. aeruginosa infection in CF; however, clinical evidence in this setting remains limited.81–83

In addition, pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) parameters remain poorly defined, particularly for inhaled delivery within the CF lung environment, where mucus viscosity, biofilm architecture and heterogeneous bacterial density impede phage diffusion and access to bacterial targets.84,85 Regulatory barriers and limited Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) capacity further constrain widespread implementation of phage therapy, particularly in the UK.86 Existing regulatory frameworks designed for biological medicines can be challenging to navigate and as such, there are no licensed phage products in the US, UK and EU.87 These complexities mean clinical use is still confined to unlicensed compassionate use rather than a scalable route to routine implementation.88,89

Bacterial Resistance to Phages and Evolutionary Steering

Bacteria can evolve resistance to phages through receptor modification, activation of intracellular defence systems, or acquisition of phage-derived resistance elements.90 Beyond receptor-based resistance, P. aeruginosa employs intracellular defence systems including restriction–modification (RM) systems, CRISPR–Cas immunity, abortive infection pathways, and cyclic-oligonucleotide-based anti-phage signalling systems (CBASS) to degrade phage nucleic acids or trigger programmed cell death.91–94 In response, phages have evolved diverse counter-defence strategies, including anti-CRISPR and anti-CBASS proteins that neutralise bacterial immunity and restore infectivity.95,96 Although many of these anti-phage defence systems are well characterised individually, there is a lack of understanding of how these may impact phage therapy and how multiple defences interact, coordinate, and evolve together as a multi-layered immune strategy against phage infection.97

Rather than representing a limitation, the evolutionary arms race between bacteria and phage provides a therapeutic opportunity. Rational phage selection and cocktail design can deliberately exploit resistance-associated trade-offs, forcing bacterial populations into less virulent and more treatable evolutionary states.79 The dynamic interplay between bacteria and phage has been reviewed by Ibrahim and Aranjani.98 However, the therapeutic value of phage-driven evolutionary steering should not be assumed: resistance can arise through multiple genetic routes, not all of which impose clinically meaningful fitness costs, and compensatory evolution may diminish initially favourable trade-offs during prolonged infection.

Phage Cocktails and Phage-Antibiotic Synergy (PAS)

To overcome narrow host range and limit resistance emergence, phage cocktails combining multiple lytic phages are widely proposed. By targeting distinct bacterial receptors, cocktails increase therapeutic breadth and reduce the likelihood that single mutational events confer resistance.79,99 In P. aeruginosa, cocktails directed against LPS, T4P, and flagella are particularly relevant given the central role of some of these structures in CF lung colonisation and their dual function as phage receptors. However, cocktail formulations may also provoke a stronger adaptive immune response and facilitate the emergence of cross-resistance across phage types within the cocktail.100 Data from controlled clinical trials evaluating this possibility remain limited.

Another increasingly compelling strategy is phage-antibiotic combination therapy, which exploits PAS. Sublethal concentrations of certain antibiotics can enhance phage replication, while phage-mediated selection can drive bacteria toward phenotypes with restored antibiotic susceptibility.46,47 This reframes AMR as an evolvable and exploitable vulnerability, rather than a fixed endpoint. Nevertheless, continuous co-administration of phages and antibiotics has limitations. Antibiotic-mediated bacterial killing can reduce the available host population below the proliferation threshold, the minimum bacterial cell density needed to sustain productive phage replication, thereby limiting phage amplification before infection is fully resolved.89

Despite this, strong in vivo evidence for phage-antibiotic combination therapy has emerged. Ashworth et al demonstrated that phage steering within the lung environment can drive pan-resistant P. aeruginosa toward less virulent and more treatable phenotypes, enabling infection clearance despite extensive baseline resistance.42 These findings align with resistance-breaking frameworks proposed by Langendonk et al (2021), which conceptualise AMR as a modular, environmentally conditioned process shaped by metabolic state, envelope composition, and host-derived pressures.34

Therapeutic Challenges in the Cystic Fibrosis (CF) Lung Environment

The CF lung presents a uniquely hostile environment, characterised by immunological and physical barriers that collectively undermine phage efficacy. Phages interact with the mammalian immune system in complex and context-dependent ways. Phage-mediated bacterial lysis can enhance bacterial clearance by increasing exposure of bacterial debris to phagocytosis and immune recognition, while in some settings phage-mediated killing continues within phagocytes.101,102 Therefore, effective bacterial clearance often depends on the synergy between phages and innate immune cells, specifically neutrophils which eliminate residual and phage-resistant bacterial subpopulations. However, this synergy is disrupted in the CF lung. CFTR dysfunction impairs multiple aspects of neutrophil function including chemotaxis, phagosomal acidification and oxidative killing. Furthermore, neutrophil elastase released into the CF airway can cleave specific bacterial surface receptors, potentially reducing phage adsorption. Therefore, rather than supporting effective pathogen clearance, neutrophils in pwCF can contribute to progressive airway inflammation and tissue damage.103,104

Phages are also subject to recognition and clearance by both innate and adaptive immune responses. Phages can be phagocytosed by macrophages and dendritic cells, and repeated dosing may induce phage-specific antibodies that neutralise phages and undermine long-term efficacy.105,106 However, in CF, macrophage function is further compromised, with CF macrophages exhibiting impaired phagocytic killing, altered inflammatory signalling and delayed resolution of inflammation.103 Rapid bacterial lysis can also release endotoxins and other bacterial contaminants, triggering inflammatory responses similar to those observed with bactericidal antibiotics.107 As a result, rigorous purification of therapeutic phage preparations is essential to reduce the risk of treatment-associated inflammatory reactions.108 In pwCF, where chronic inflammation already drives lung damage, these multifaceted immune-phage interactions represent a vital and insufficiently characterised determinant of therapeutic success and are likely to contribute to the transient efficacy observed across clinical studies to date.

These immunological barriers are further compounded by the physical and microbiological properties of the CF lung, which pose challenges for phage delivery and sustained activity. The CF lung is characterised by thick mucus, spatially structured biofilms and diverse bacterial populations.58,104 Effective therapy requires phages to penetrate mucus and biofilms, access viable bacterial cells, and encounter sufficient host density to sustain replication. As discussed previously, depolymerase-producing phages may overcome these barriers by improving biofilm penetration and enhancing bacterial clearance.59,60 However, chronic infection is characterised by extensive bacterial debris and receptor diversity, which may sequester phages and reduce effective dosing.104 Moreover, the abundance of hypermutable P. aeruginosa strains in the CF lung under continuous antibiotic pressure may accelerate the rate of phage resistance.103 These barriers emphasise the complexity of achieving durable phage activity in the CF lung and support the use of personalised phage cocktails or combination approaches.

Clinical Evidence, Implementation Challenges, and Future Directions

Clinical evidence supporting phage therapy in respiratory infection is growing but remains limited by a lack of large-scale, randomised controlled trials. Compassionate-use cases and early-phase trials report favourable safety profiles, including inhaled and intravenous administration, with few adverse effects.109–111 Terlizzi et al conducted a systematic review of phage therapy in pwCF focusing on its safety and therapeutic potential for chronic airway infections. The authors report that the available clinical evidence suggests that phage therapy is generally well tolerated in pwCF and may provide clinical benefit, particularly in infections caused by MDR P. aeruginosa. Across reported cases, phage administration was associated with reductions in bacterial burden and, in some instances, improvements in clinical status without significant adverse events. Nebulised delivery may be particularly advantageous for chronic CF lung infections because it enables high phage concentrations to be delivered directly to the site of infection while minimising systemic exposure. However, considerable differences in treatment regimens, administration routes and outcome measures were noted. Overall, the review supports phage therapy as an advantageous adjunctive strategy for CF respiratory infections, while emphasising the need for rigorously designed trials to optimise delivery approaches and standardise therapeutic protocols. A landmark step towards addressing these limitations was the BX004-A (1b/2a) clinical trial, the first in-human randomised controlled trial of a nebulised phage cocktail for chronic P. aeruginosa infection in pwCF. The trial demonstrated that the phage cocktail was well tolerated and resulted in a significant reduction in sputum bacterial load. Nebulisation effectively delivered the phages to the lower respiratory tract. However, the study’s small sample size (nine pwCF) limited conclusions regarding clinical efficacy.113 Unfortunately, phase 2b of the clinical trial was discontinued due to unexpectedly high rates of adverse events, a finding that not only halted further developments of this treatment but also raises questions about safety, particularly around dosing and delivery. This contrasts with the widely available data indicating that phage therapy is generally safe, highlighting the challenges of translating promising early findings into licensed phage products.

Despite encouraging clinical observations, the efficacy of phage therapy remains difficult to evaluate. While observational evidence may be subject to publication bias, positive outcomes have been reported consistently across multiple independent studies. Nevertheless, most of the available evidence is derived from compassionate-use cases and small early-phase trials that vary considerably in study design, treatment duration and outcome measures. Many studies employ personalised phage cocktails selected against individual patient isolates, limiting the generalisability of findings and making direct comparisons challenging. Variability in treatment response may also reflect differences in infecting bacterial strains, phage susceptibility and the complex polymicrobial environment of the CF lung. Interpretation of efficacy is further complicated by the use of concurrent antibiotics, making it difficult to attribute clinical outcomes to phage therapy alone. Consequently, larger randomised controlled trials with standardised clinical and microbiological endpoints are required to determine which patients are most likely to benefit from phage therapy and under what treatment conditions.118–120

Beyond efficacy, important uncertainties remain regarding the safety of phage therapy. These include immune-mediated phage clearance, inflammatory responses associated with phage administration and bacterial lysis, the emergence of phage-resistant bacterial populations during therapy, and the poorly understood environmental consequences of widespread phage application. Moreover, due to the limited number of clinical trials conducted to date, comprehensive safety data and documentation of adverse events remain scarce. Further studies are therefore required to fully characterise the long-term safety profile of phage-based therapeutics.101,102,121

Conclusion

P. aeruginosa remains a dominant and clinically challenging pathogen in CF due to its capacity for biofilm formation, immune evasion, and MDR. Phage therapy offers a compelling complementary strategy that disrupts biofilms and drives antimicrobial resensitisation. However, successful clinical translation will require deeper mechanistic insight into phage-bacteria-host interactions within the CF lung, optimisation of phage selection and delivery, and integration of evolutionary endpoints into clinical trials. Unlike previous reviews focused primarily on phage efficacy, this review integrates current understanding of receptor-mediated evolutionary trade-offs, phage-host interactions within the CF lung, and the challenges associated with clinical implementation of phage therapy.

Despite extensive scientific interest, major gaps remain that continue to limit progress towards routine clinical implementation. Current evidence is dominated by compassionate-use cases, small observational studies, and early-phase trials with limited standardisation in dosing strategies, delivery methods, microbiological endpoints, and patient selection. Future clinical trials will require larger multicentre designs with harmonised outcome measures that extend beyond short-term bacterial clearance to include longitudinal assessment of resistance evolution, changes in virulence, biofilm disruption, and durability of therapeutic response. Importantly, most existing studies rely on single time-point sampling and fail to capture the dynamic within-host evolution of P. aeruginosa populations during treatment, limiting understanding of how phage-driven selective pressures shape long-term adaptation in the CF lung. Another knowledge gap is the impact of highly effective CFTR modulator therapy on phage efficacy. CFTR modulators alter mucus properties, inflammation and bacterial burden, substantially changing the CF lung environment in which phages must act. As the majority of pwCF now receive modulator therapy, understanding how these treatments affect phage activity should be incorporated into future clinical trial design.58,122

In parallel, there are substantial manufacturing and regulatory barriers. These obstacles are particularly relevant for personalised phage therapy, where treatment may require rapid identification, characterisation, and formulation of patient-specific phage cocktails. Increased investment from public funding bodies, industry and national healthcare systems will therefore be essential to establish sustainable phage libraries, biobanks, manufacturing pipelines and coordinated clinical trial networks capable of supporting large-scale translational research.

Emerging advances in synthetic biology and phage engineering may further expand the potential of phage-based approaches. Genetically engineered phages capable of enhanced biofilm degradation, broader host range, reduced bacterial resistance emergence or delivery of antimicrobial payloads represent a promising next generation of therapeutics. CRISPR-Cas-engineered phages can selectively target antibiotic resistance or virulence genes, while depolymerase-enhanced phages and engineered lysin delivery systems may improve activity against highly adapted mucoid strains that complicate chronic lung infections. Additionally, artificial intelligence and machine learning may accelerate phage therapy development by enabling rapid phage-host matching for personalised therapy and optimising phage cocktail design for clinical trials.123 However, these technologies introduce additional safety, manufacturing and regulatory considerations, including concerns regarding genetic stability, environmental release and regulatory approval pathways. Therefore, meticulous preclinical evaluation will be required before engineered phages can be translated into clinical practice.124 Furthermore, for AI-based host prediction, the key problem may not simply be model sophistication but training data quality. This could be affected by inconsistent host-range assays, limited representation of clinical CF isolates and rapidly evolving bacterial populations. A prediction model would currently likely be limited by poor/limited phenotypic datasets.

Ultimately, the future success of phage therapy in CF and beyond will depend not only on demonstrating clinical efficacy, but also on developing integrated translational frameworks that combine evolutionary biology, microbiology, engineering, regulatory science, and precision medicine. Public and patient involvement will also be important in shaping future phage therapy trials, especially given the treatment burden in pwCF and the need to incorporate patient-relevant outcomes including quality of life, treatment acceptability, and frequency of hospital attendance. Patient-centred target product profiles for phage therapy to treat CF lung infections have recently been published.125 Addressing these interdisciplinary challenges will be critical to advancing phage therapy from compassionate use toward a scalable, licensed and evidence-based treatment strategy for chronic P. aeruginosa infection in pwCF. More broadly, phage therapy may offer benefits beyond bacterial killing by steering bacterial evolution towards less virulent and more treatable phenotypes, thereby improving treatment outcomes and helping preserve the effectiveness of existing antibiotics.
