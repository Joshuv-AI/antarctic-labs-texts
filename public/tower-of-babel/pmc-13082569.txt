Endoscopic Ultrasound as a Diagnostic Tool in Obscure Gastrointestinal Bleeding: Left Gastric Artery Pseudoaneurysm Mimicking Gastric Varices
Authors: Chidera Onwuzo, Avleen Kaur, Sharifeh Almasaid, Bishnu Sapkota
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObscure gastrointestinal bleeding is recurrent or persistent bleeding after negative esophagogastroduodenoscopy and colonoscopy. We report an 81-year-old man with hematochezia and acute anemia after nondiagnostic esophagogastroduodenoscopy/colonoscopy and tagged red blood cell scan. Enteroscopy revealed gastric bleeding with folds suspicious for gastric varices. Endoscopic ultrasound demonstrated prominent submucosal vessels and a ∼3 cm perigastric cystic lesion near the pancreas. Portal venous-phase computed tomography identified a pseudoaneurysm along the lesser curvature arising from a left gastric artery branch. Catheter angiography confirmed the lesion and coil embolization achieved durable hemostasis.

INTRODUCTION

Obscure gastrointestinal bleeding (OGIB) is defined as persistent or recurrent gastrointestinal (GI) bleeding with negative findings on initial esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) and colonoscopy.1 It accounts for about 5% of all GI bleeding cases.2 When standard bidirectional endoscopy is unrevealing, guidelines recommend a sequential approach: repeat endoscopy if suspicion is high, followed by video capsule endoscopy, deep enteroscopy (eg, balloon-assisted), and cross-sectional imaging such as computed tomography angiography (CTA) or computed tomography enterography; conventional angiography is reserved for active brisk bleeding or therapeutic intervention.3

While most OGIB sources are mucosal (eg, angioectasias, erosions/ulcers, neoplasms), rare extraluminal vascular lesions can cause intraluminal hemorrhage and escape endoscopic detection. Visceral arterial pseudoaneurysms are uncommon but dangerous focal arterial wall defects that risk catastrophic rupture if unrecognized.4 Common causes include chronic pancreatitis, trauma/iatrogenic injury, and peptic ulcer disease.4 In pancreatitis-related cases, splenic artery involvement is typical, while left gastric artery (LGA) pseudoaneurysms are exceptionally rare.4

We describe an unusual case of OGIB in an elderly patient where endoscopic ultrasound (EUS), initially performed to evaluate suspected gastric varices, identified a perigastric vascular anomaly. This was confirmed as an LGA pseudoaneurysm on CTA and managed with trans arterial coil embolization.

CASE REPORT

An 81-year-old man presented with dizziness, fatigue, bright red blood per rectum for 24 hours. History included alcohol-related chronic pancreatitis, duodenal ulcer, gastroesophageal reflux disease, hypertension and tobacco use. He denied melena, hematemesis. No nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug, anticoagulant, or antiplatelet use. Laboratory test results reveal worsening anemia with hemoglobin falling to 7 g/dL from a baseline of ∼12 g/dL, with normal liver function tests, platelet count, and coagulation profile. CTA of the abdomen and pelvis suggested cirrhosis/portal hypertension (enlarged mesenteric veins) but did not identify an active bleeding source (Figure 1); he received transfusion and gastroenterology was consulted.

Initial computed tomography angiography images. Axial section in the early arterial (A) and venous phases (B) show mild inflammation around the mid pancreatic body, posterior to the stomach. Few nonprominent tortuous veins are noted around the stomach wall. No pseudoaneurysm or extravasation.

Initial EGD and colonoscopy were unrevealing and no evidence of bluish, tortuous, or nodular submucosal vessels were noted, and a tagged red blood cell scan (with repeat imaging 24 hours later) failed to localize bleeding. Enteroscopy showed blood in the gastric body, prompting exchange to a standard gastroscope, which showed oozing from the gastric body/fundus (Figure 2) with prominent folds difficult to distend (Figure 2). Epinephrine was injected to achieve temporary hemostasis and improve visualization. Given concern for gastric varices, EUS revealed prominent submucosal vessels and a complex ∼3 cm cystic lesion between the gastric body and pancreatic body demonstrating vascular flow (Figure 3). Additional temporary hemostasis was achieved with EndoClot. The varices were not amenable to banding and he was transferred to the intensive care unit on octreotide drip and intravenous proton pump inhibitor.

Esophagogastroduodenoscopy evaluation. (A) Showing active oozing in the gastric body and fundus. (B) Showing prominent and poorly distensible folds.

EUS evaluation. (A and B) EUS showing submucosal vascular structures consistent with gastric and perigastric varices. (C) EUS showing a 3 cm complex cystic lesion located between the posterior gastric body and pancreas. (D) EUS Doppler showing vascular flow in the identified cystic lesion. EUS, endoscopic ultrasound.

A portal venous-phase computed tomography was obtained to assess for portal venous thrombosis and better characterize the peripancreatic lesion, demonstrating a suspected pseudoaneurysm along the lesser curvature arising from a gastric arterial branch with surrounding hemorrhagic material, without active extravasation (Figure 4). Interventional radiology performed angiography, which identified a pseudoaneurysm arising from a branch of the LGA (from the gastrohepatic trunk off the celiac axis) (Figure 5), and successful coil embolization was performed (Figure 5). The patient remained hemodynamically stable after embolization without recurrent bleeding or needing repeat transfusion. He was discharged home on post procedure day 2 with an outpatient gastroenterology follow-up that he missed, but followed up with his primary care provider a week later and denied any further bleeding episodes or post-procedural complications.

Multiple prominent venous varicose are noted around the stomach. Inflammatory changes are again noted in the pancreatic body and posterior stomach wall. (C) Axial image in early arterial phase shows pseudoaneurysm, likely arising from a branch of gastric artery.

DISCUSSION

This case highlights a significant challenge in assessing OGIB: submucosal or perigastric vascular structures observed during endoscopic procedures can appear like gastric varices, yet not all lesions that resemble varices are indicative of portal hypertension. In patients without confirmed cirrhosis or those presenting with unusual imaging, clinical characteristics, or other features, different possibilities should be explored, such as pseudoaneurysm, arterialized collaterals, or vascular abnormalities linked to pancreatitis or ulcer disease.5 Importantly, brisk upper GI bleeding can present as hematochezia, and intermittent bleeding can further reduce the sensitivity of tagged red blood cell scanning and standard endoscopic examinations, prompting escalation to advanced diagnostics per guideline-driven algorithms.3

Guidelines for suspected small bowel bleeding prioritize video capsule endoscopy, deep enteroscopy, and cross-sectional imaging after negative EGD/colonoscopy, tailoring test selection to hemodynamic status and the likelihood of active bleeding.3 EUS is not routinely positioned as a first-line test in OGIB algorithms; however, EUS offers unique strengths when bleeding is suspected to originate from the gastric wall, perigastric region, or pancreas-adjacent vasculature. It provides high-resolution evaluation of submucosal and extraluminal structures and, with Doppler, can characterize flow patterns that differentiate venous varices from arterial lesions.6 In this patient, EUS performed for presumed gastric varices identified complex perigastric vascularity and an adjacent cystic-appearing lesion, raising suspicion for an alternative vascular source and prompting definitive vascular imaging.

Visceral arterial pseudoaneurysms are uncommon overall, but their clinical relevance is disproportionate: rupture risk is high, and hemorrhage may be rapidly fatal without timely intervention.4 In pancreatitis-associated pseudoaneurysms, enzymatic autodigestion and inflammation weaken vessel walls; the splenic artery is most often involved, while LGA pseudoaneurysms represent a small minority.4 Similarly, peptic ulcer disease can erode into adjacent arterial structures, creating pseudoaneurysm formation and hemorrhage.7 Because LGA pseudoaneurysms are rare and may bleed intermittently, they can mimic more common entities (eg, varices, ulcer bleeding) and remain occult through multiple standard investigations.

CTA is widely used to detect pseudoaneurysms and define arterial anatomy, and catheter angiography remains the gold standard because it provides both precise localization and the opportunity for immediate endovascular therapy.4 In our case, CTA demonstrated a pseudoaneurysm near the lesser curvature arising from an LGA branch, and angiography confirmed the lesion and enabled successful coil embolization consistent with preferred contemporary management favoring minimally invasive endovascular approaches when feasible.4

Although most LGA pseudoaneurysms are diagnosed by CTA/angiography, EUS-based diagnosis has been reported previously, including a classic case describing EUS identification of an LGA pseudoaneurysm with an aneurysmogastric fistula.8 Beyond diagnosis, EUS-guided vascular interventions (eg, targeted injection or embolization techniques) have emerged as potential options in select scenarios particularly when endovascular access is challenging or when a lesion is well visualized from the GI lumen though expertise, equipment, and institutional protocols vary.6 Our case shows that when endoscopic findings suggest gastric varices but the clinical picture is atypical, EUS with Doppler can prompt reconsideration of the diagnosis and raise suspicion for an arterial lesion. This should expedite CTA and angiography, enabling definitive endovascular therapy.
