Effects of modified site for radial artery cannulation on the stability of arterial blood pressure monitoring in patients undergoing elective surgery (MoSRAC): protocol for a randomised controlled trial
Authors: Xuan Yang, Hui-Ming Liang, Ya-Bin Huang, Shan Li, Yi Chen, Tong-Feng Luo, Wei Wang, Jing Li, Ying-Yin Zhao, Yu Jin, Fan Wang, You-Can Yao, San-Qing Jin, Zhi-Nan Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionInvasive arterial blood pressure monitoring is critical for perioperative and critically ill patients, yet traditional radial artery cannulation near the wrist joint is prone to catheter dysfunction (eg, kinking, occlusion) due to positional changes, compromising accuracy and patient safety. This trial hypothesises that modifying the cannulation site to 1.5–2.5 cm proximal to the radial styloid process may enhance catheter stability.Methods and analysisThis is a prospective, parallel-group, randomised, controlled, analyst-blinded trial. A total of 486 participants (243 per group) will be enrolled at the Sixth Affiliated Hospital, Sun Yat-sen University. Eligible patients (18–75 years, American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status I–III, requiring elective surgery with radial artery cannulation) will be randomised 1:1 to the modified group (1.5–2.5 cm proximal to the radial styloid process) or the conventional group (traditional site). The primary outcome is the incidence of arterial catheter dysfunction (defined by criteria such as blood sampling difficulty, position-dependent waveform or improved waveform post-square wave test). Secondary outcomes include frequency of catheter dysfunction, damping abnormality rate, first-puncture success rate, number of arterial punctures, arterial cannulation time, complication incidence and blood pressure measurement differences.Ethics and disseminationThis study protocol (V.4.0) was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Sixth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University in Guangzhou, China on 2 September 2025. The first participant was recruited on 15 September 2025, with an estimated completion date of 31 December 2025. Informed consent will be obtained from all participants. Findings will be published in peer-reviewed journals.Trial registration numberNCT06566456.

Introduction

Blood pressure monitoring is one of the most fundamental and critical methods for assessing cardiovascular system function, serving as a cornerstone in the care of anaesthetised and critically ill patients. Current blood pressure monitoring techniques are primarily categorised into two types: non-invasive blood pressure (NIBP) monitoring and invasive arterial blood pressure (IABP) monitoring. NIBP measurements, however, are susceptible to interference from multiple factors (eg, positional changes, patient agitation) and require longer measurement durations. Consequently, their sensitivity and specificity are insufficient to meet the demands of perioperative patients with rapid or significant blood pressure fluctuations, obese patients and other populations requiring precise circulatory monitoring.15

IABP monitoring involves inserting an arterial catheter into vessels such as the radial, ulnar, axillary or femoral artery, with direct blood pressure measurement achieved via a pressure transducer. Compared with NIBP, IABP provides more accurate and sensitive readings, enabling continuous, beat-to-beat arterial pressure measurements and waveform analysis, which has established it as the ‘gold standard’ for blood pressure monitoring.6 As early as 1993, the Australian Incident Monitoring Study highlighted that IABP outperforms NIBP in the early detection of intraoperative hypotension.7 Additionally, arterial cannulation facilitates convenient blood sampling for blood gas analysis and other laboratory tests8; for patients with conditions such as pathological obesity or limb disabilities, IABP may even represent the sole feasible method for continuous circulatory assessment.9 The radial artery is the most commonly used site for arterial cannulation in anaesthesia due to its superficial location, relatively fixed course and reduced risk of distal ischaemia compared with other puncture sites (eg, ulnar, brachial or femoral arteries).10

However, arterial catheter dysfunction—manifested as catheter kinking, dislodgment or lumen occlusion (impeding blood withdrawal)—is a prevalent issue in IABP monitoring.1114 This dysfunction not only compromises the accuracy of IABP measurements but also poses significant threats to perioperative patient safety. Frequent catheter replacement further increases healthcare providers’ workload and patient burden. Our research group conducted a questionnaire survey during the 2014 National Anesthesiologists Conference focusing on arterial catheter occlusion; results revealed that 93.9% of anaesthesiologists had encountered partial catheter occlusion, and 79.3% had experienced complete occlusion. Alarmingly, cases were reported where delayed vasopressor administration due to catheter dysfunction necessitated emergency patient rescue.15 Thus, enhancing catheter stability and obtaining more accurate arterial waveforms has become a critical challenge for clinical anaesthesiologists.

Clinically, radial artery cannulation is conventionally performed at the site of maximal pulsation medial to the radial styloid process.9 16 However, this location is adjacent to the wrist joint, making it prone to catheter obstruction during intraoperative positional changes. Our survey further indicated that 92.7% of responding anaesthesiologists identified positional shifts as a key contributor to catheter occlusion.15 Alves et al also reported that wrist movement may increase the risk of radial artery pressure line dysfunction, requiring more frequent dressing changes and elevating the probability of puncture-site infection.12 These findings prompted our hypothesis: Could adjusting the cannulation site reduce the impact of wrist movement on arterial line stability? Imbriaco et al conducted a prospective non-randomised cohort study in adult ICU patients, comparing radial artery cannulation at 4–10 cm versus 0–3.9 cm from the wrist joint.17 Their results demonstrated that cannulation at 4–10 cm proximal to the wrist joint significantly prolonged catheter retention time within the vessel. Conversely, another study emphasised that the safe zone for radial artery cannulation in adults spans 4 cm from the wrist to the region where the radial artery runs parallel to the radial nerve, minimising the risk of radial nerve injury.18 A randomised controlled trial was carried out to compare three different cannulation sites: Site 1 (targeting the radial styloid process), Site 2 (located midway between Sites 1 and 3) and Site 3 (situated at the distal one-third of the forearm). The findings demonstrated that, specifically among male patients, Site 2 was linked to the highest success rate on the first attempt, the fewest number of cannulation attempts, the shortest total cannulation time and the lowest complication rate.19 Yücel et al also conducted a similar study and reported that, taking into account the high removal rate in the wrist region and the elevated failure rate at the forearm midpoint, the distal quarter of the forearm might be recognised as ‘the optimal insertion site’ for ultrasound-guided radial artery cannulation.20 However, the above-mentioned studies did not proceed to further evaluate the impact of different cannulation sites on the functionality of intraoperative arterial catheters.

Building on these observations, we propose that radial artery cannulation 2 cm proximal to the traditional site (ie, 1.5–2.5 cm above the radial styloid process) may reduce the impact of wrist movement on catheter stability, thereby securing a more reliable arterial access.

This study aims to investigate the impact of radial artery cannulation at 1.5–2.5 cm proximal to the radial styloid process on perioperative arterial catheter function, arterial cannulation procedure, blood pressure measurement accuracy and complications, using the traditional cannulation site as a control. By publishing this protocol, we aim to enhance research transparency and provide a methodological reference for future investigations in arterial catheter management.

Methods and Analysis

Objective

This study aimed to investigate the effects of the modified radial artery cannulation site on perioperative arterial catheter function, arterial cannulation procedure, the accuracy of perioperative blood pressure measurements and complications, while also seeking to improve the accuracy and stability of IABP measurements through this modification.

Design and setting

This trial protocol follows the recommendations of the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) 2025 reporting guideline. This is a prospective, parallel-group, randomised, controlled, analyst-blinded, superiority trial. Eligible patients will be randomly assigned to the intervention group or the control group at a 1:1 allocation ratio. The trial is conducted at the Sixth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University in Guangzhou, China. The study flow chart is presented in figure 1.

Treatment

Preoperative Education

1 day prior to surgery, researchers will screen potential participants based on elective surgery application forms. They will then conduct face-to-face verbal explanations with these individuals to clarify the study protocol and objectives, obtain informed consent and have participants sign the informed consent form (Online supplemental material 1). No incentives were offered to participants for their involvement in this study.

Arterial Cannulation Procedure

Pre-cannulation Preparation

Routine monitoring of ECG, NIBP and pulse oximetry will be implemented. Before completing arterial cannulation, the blood pressure cuff will be placed on the contralateral upper limb, set to automatic measurement mode with a 5-minute interval. Following successful arterial cannulation, the cuff will be moved to the same upper limb as the radial artery catheter, switched to manual measurement mode and used for NIBP measurement as needed. The blood pressure sensor tubing (B. Braun Medical (Suzhou) Co., Ltd., China) will be pre-flushed with normal saline and connected to the arterial pressure monitoring line of the monitor. Zero-point adjustment and calibration will be performed. The patient will be positioned supine, with the puncture-side upper limb extended, abducted and palm-up. A gauze roll will be placed under the patient’s wrist to maintain slight dorsiflexion. Routine disinfection and drape placement will be performed. For awake patients, 1 mL of 2% lidocaine will be administered for local anaesthesia at the puncture site prior to arterial puncture; for patients under general anaesthesia, arterial puncture will be performed directly.

Group assignment

Modified group: the radial artery cannulation site is 1.5–2.5 cm proximal to the radial styloid process.

Control group: the radial artery cannulation site is the most prominent pulsation area of the radial artery adjacent to the radial styloid process.

Radial Artery Cannulation

Based on this grouping strategy, under ultrasound guidance, arterial cannulation will be performed by a designated senior attending physician with >5 years of experience in vascular access, using QuickFlash Radial Artery Catheterization Set (Arrow International LLC, USA). The insertion approach is standardised and not at the discretion of the operator.

Prior to cannulation, the skin insertion site will be marked at the allocated study position: 1.5–2.5 cm proximal to the radial styloid process for the intervention group, and the traditional distal site (the most prominent pulsation area of the radial artery adjacent to the radial styloid process) for the control group. The ultrasound probe will be placed in a transverse orientation to identify the radial artery, and the operator will make every effort to distinguish the adjacent radial nerve though anatomical variation may occasionally prevent definitive visualisation of the nerve in all patients. After confirming the allocated insertion site, the probe position is adjusted to centre the radial artery image on the ultrasound screen.

The indwelling catheter is inserted at an angle of 45° to 60° relative to the skin surface. During insertion, the operator will gently adjust the catheter position and manoeuvre the ultrasound probe to ensure the needle tip is clearly visualised on the screen throughout the procedure. As the needle tip is advanced toward the radial artery, the probe angle is adjusted dynamically to maintain continuous visualisation of the needle tip. When a flashback of arterial blood is observed in the needle hub, the puncture angle is lowered, and the guidewire is advanced into the artery lumen. The catheter sheath is then advanced over the guidewire into the artery, after which the needle and guidewire are removed. Pulsatile blood flow from the catheter hub confirms successful cannulation. The catheter is then secured and connected to the pressure transducer for continuous arterial pressure monitoring. The arterial catheter will be flushed with normal saline immediately after connection to ensure line patency.

Timing will start on needle insertion. Once the first arterial blood pressure waveform appears on the monitor, radial artery cannulation is deemed complete and timing will cease. The arterial tubing will be securely affixed using 3M transparent dressing, and the arterial catheter will be flushed.

Following successful cannulation, the vertical distance between the cannulation site and the radial styloid process will be measured. Prior to surgical preparation, both upper limbs will be positioned adducted and retracted to resting positions. During surgery, both upper limbs will be placed adducted and tucked alongside the trunk, with the wrists maintained in neutral natural positions without excessive flexion or extension, to minimise excessive extension or pronation stress at the cannulation site.

Before the patient leaves the operating room, the patient’s radial artery catheter will be removed. Local compression will then be applied to achieve haemostasis for 3 min, followed by the application of a pressure dressing with a cotton pad.

Concomitant Care and Procedure Regulations

To eliminate potential interference with arterial catheter function and ensure the reliability of primary outcome judgement, clear specifications for concomitant care and other procedures during the arterial catheter indwelling period are established. All medically necessary routine clinical care that does not affect catheter stability is permitted, including standard perioperative anaesthesia management, regular medication for underlying diseases (such as hypertension, diabetes and hyperlipidaemia), necessary symptomatic treatment for adverse events unrelated to arterial cannulation and routine perioperative examinations that do not involve displacement or compression of the catheterised arm. All permitted concomitant procedures and medication will be fully recorded in the case report form for subsequent analysis.

Procedures that may affect catheter stability or interfere with the judgement of catheter dysfunction are strictly prohibited during the indwelling period, including excessive bending or compression of the catheterised arm, other interventional operations performed on the same arm and arbitrary adjustment of the arterial catheter position by non-study medical staff. This ensures that the observed difference in catheter function between the two groups is only associated with the puncture site variable, avoiding additional bias in result analysis.

Follow-up

Figure 2 provides details of the schedule of enrolment, interventions and assessments. During the arterial cannulation procedure, document the number of puncture attempts, duration of arterial catheter insertion and procedure-related complications. Following successful arterial cannulation, perform square wave testing at 30-minute intervals (with each square wave persisting for 2–3 s). Prior to each test: (1) record baseline IABP values; (2) conduct the square wave test, then re-record IABP values once the waveform stabilises; (3) observe and document incidents of abnormal arterial catheter function and damping status. Afterwards, initiate NIBP monitoring on the same arm with IABP, record NIBP values and flush the arterial line to mitigate interference from cuff inflation during NIBP measurement on IABP monitoring. Square wave testing may also be performed when the arterial waveform appears flat, with all specified observations documented until catheter removal.

The schedule of enrolment, interventions and assessment

Post-catheter removal, follow-up assessments will be conducted on postoperative days 1, 2 and 3 to evaluate the cannulation site for post-puncture complications. For patients requiring prolonged arterial cannulation (eg, those admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) postoperatively), daily catheter care will be performed per standard nursing protocols, which includes daily follow-up visits: (1) repeated square wave testing; (2) documentation of damping status, the difference in direct arterial pressure before and after testing, and any complications; and (3) recording of total catheter indwelling duration.

Outcomes

Primary outcomes

We will choose the incidence of arterial catheter dysfunction as the primary outcome.12

Incidence of arterial catheter dysfunction: the proportion of cases with abnormal function relative to the number of enrolled participants in each group.

Arterial catheter dysfunction should meet at least one of the following criteria: (a) difficulty in blood sampling: When drawing arterial blood samples for blood gas analysis, the sample cannot be aspirated smoothly through the arterial pressure monitoring line, necessitating repeated flushing of the line to obtain an adequate sample; or arterial catheter occlusion occurs and a new arterial pressure monitoring pathway must be established based on surgical or anaesthetic requirements; (b) position-dependent waveform or flushing: repositioning of the arterial catheter or wrist is required to obtain a satisfactory arterial waveform, or smooth flushing can only be achieved following such repositioning; (c) improved waveform post-square wave test: after a square wave test (conducted every 30 min or when the arterial waveform becomes flat), the arterial waveform shows significant improvement compared with its pre-flush state.

Secondary outcomes

1. Frequency of arterial catheter dysfunction: mean number of catheter instability events per subject per hour.

2. Damping abnormality rate and frequency.

Damping abnormality rate: the ratio of total cases with over-damping or under-damping to the total number of enrolled patients per group.

Damping abnormality frequency: mean number of over-damping or under-damping events per subject per hour.

3. First-puncture success rate: the ratio of cases with successful first-time arterial cannulation to the number of enrolled cases per group. A successful first puncture is defined as achieving arterial access and catheter placement without needle withdrawal or repositioning after initial skin entry.

4. Number of arterial punctures: total number of arterial punctures performed before successful cannulation. One puncture is defined as withdrawing the needle to the subcutaneous layer and reinserting it during the cannulation process.

5. Arterial cannulation time: defined as the interval from the first skin entry of the needle to the first display of an arterial blood pressure waveform on the monitor.

6. Complication incidence: encompasses complications during puncture and catheter indwelling (eg, hematoma, bleeding, ecchymosis) and post-puncture complications (eg, bleeding, haematoma, distal ischaemia, local infection, peripheral nerve injury, unintended catheter dislodgment).2124 The observation window is 0–3 days post-surgery.

A haematoma is a localised swelling resulting from blood extravasation into surrounding tissues following vascular rupture. Bleeding refers to minor oozing that can be controlled with local compression alone. Ecchymosis describes subcutaneous or submucosal patchy haemorrhage presenting as red or purplish-red discolouration with a diameter exceeding 5 mm. Distal ischaemia, marked by inadequate fingertip blood circulation, may manifest as numbness, pain or pale/purplish skin discolouration. Local infection typically presents as redness, swelling, warmth and tenderness at the puncture site. Peripheral nerve injury, involving impaired nerve conduction, may manifest as loss of active movement in muscles innervated by the affected nerve, sensory deficits, deformity, elevated skin temperature or irritative neuralgia.

7. Difference in IABP before and after square wave test.

8. Difference between IABP and NIBP.

For ICU-admitted patients: daily follow-up will be conducted. The following will be recorded: primary outcome measures, secondary outcome measures (2), (6) and (7). Additionally, the duration of catheter indwelling in the ICU, occurrence of unintended arterial catheter dislodgment during indwelling, catheter removal due to clinical stability (no longer requiring direct arterial pressure monitoring) and each patient’s final disposition will be documented.

Sample size calculations

This trial used a superiority test for sample size calculation. In the 100-case preliminary experiment results, the incidence rate of abnormal arterial catheter function was 30% in the control group and 12% in the modified group. With a predefined superiority margin Δ of −6% (actual observed Δ of −18%), using a type I error rate (α) of 0.025 and statistical power (1−β) of 0.90, statistical calculations determined that 231 participants per group would be required to detect this difference in a one-sided superiority test. Considering a conservatively estimated 5% dropout rate (higher than the 3% dropout rate observed in our pre-experiment), the final enrolled sample size was set at 243 patients per group, with a total of 486 patients. The sample size calculation was performed using R software (V.4.5.1).

Recruitment

Trained researchers will oversee the enrolment of study participants using a consecutive sampling approach. Patients scheduled for surgery will be screened from the surgical scheduling system 24 hours prior to their procedure to identify those meeting the inclusion criteria. Eligible individuals will be provided with comprehensive study information through both verbal explanations and written information sheets. Once written consent to participate is obtained, participants will undergo screening to complete a questionnaire. This questionnaire will include personal contact details, demographic data and a detailed medical history. Throughout the study period, all participants retain the right to withdraw from the research at any time, with no adverse impact on their ongoing medical care.

Randomisation, masking and blinding

Eligible patients will be randomly assigned to the modified group (experimental group) or conventional group (control group) in a 1:1 ratio via simple randomisation. The random sequence will be generated by an independent statistician using R software (V.4.5.1). Group assignments will be stored in sealed opaque envelopes with the study assistant. Each envelope will be labelled with the enrolment number on the exterior and contain both the enrolment number and the assigned group on the interior. Following the patients’ enrolment order, the study assistant will assign corresponding numbers, open the envelopes and inform the principal investigator of the group assignment results. Personnel involved in participant recruitment, intraoperative outcome observation and statistical data analysis will be kept blinded. Emergency unblinding to intervention allocation is only permitted when required for serious adverse event reporting and ethical submission, to support life-saving emergency medical treatment for participants, for data verification by the institutional review board or regulatory authorities or for necessary pre-unblinding data checks after database locking; all emergency unblinding operations will be fully documented and reported to the trial steering committee within 24 hours, and any unblinded statistical analysts will be replaced to eliminate potential analysis bias.

Data collection and management

Data will be manually recorded using standardised paper case report forms (CRFs) to ensure consistency. A dedicated Data Preservation Team will be responsible for overseeing data handling, with strict adherence to data protection legislation to guarantee secure storage. All raw data (including completed CRFs) will be stored in locked, fireproof cabinets within a restricted-access facility at the Sixth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University, with access limited to authorised team members only.

To minimise entry errors, a dual manual verification process will be implemented: two independent members of the Data Preservation Team will cross-check and confirm data accuracy immediately after collection. Anonymous datasets (with identifiers removed) will be archived in a secure physical storage room designated for research data at the Sixth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University to facilitate future auditing or secondary analysis.

The Data Preservation Team will also manage key tasks, including: designing and updating CRFs, conducting blinded data reviews (to ensure completeness and consistency), organising data filing according to study timelines and maintaining detailed logs of data access or transfers. All data handling processes will be documented in paper-based records and stored alongside the original CRFs.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables with normal or approximately normal distributions will be presented as mean (SD), while non-normally distributed continuous variables will be reported as median (IQR). Categorical variables will be presented as frequencies and percentages (n, %). Group differences will be evaluated using Student’s t-test for normally distributed continuous variables, the Mann-Whitney U test for non-normally distributed continuous variables, and either Pearson’s χ² test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Repeated measures data will be analysed using a Generalised Linear Mixed-Effects Model.

Risk ratios with 95% CIs will be computed via log-binomial or Poisson regression. Risk differences with 95% CIs will be calculated using binomial or Poisson regression. For count endpoints, Poisson regression will be used as the primary analysis, with negative binomial regression as the alternative if overdispersion is detected. Differences in means for normally distributed continuous variables with 95% CIs will be calculated using linear regression or generalised estimation equations. Median differences with 95% bootstrap CIs will be used for skewed continuous variables.

The incidence of arterial catheter dysfunction is designated as the sole prespecified confirmatory primary endpoint of this trial. Between-group comparisons will use a one-sided superiority test, with the overall 0.05 type I error rate split equally to set the significance threshold at p<0.025. All secondary endpoints are prespecified as exploratory, to be analysed using two-sided tests with a threshold of p<0.05. Safety endpoints, as a predefined subset of secondary endpoints, include complications during puncture, catheter indwelling and post-puncture complications. All safety endpoints are categorical adverse event endpoints, and between-group comparisons will adopt the prespecified categorical variable analytical methods specified above, with two-sided tests at a significance threshold of p<0.05.

Analyses will be performed in both the intention-to-treat (ITT) set and the per-protocol (PP) set. In the ITT analysis, no imputation will be applied to missing endpoint data for the primary and secondary outcome analyses; sensitivity analyses limited to the primary endpoint will be conducted, including multiple imputation, best-case and worst-case extreme value imputation to assess result robustness. In the PP analysis, no imputation will be applied to missing data. No subgroup analyses are prespecified or planned for this trial. All statistical analyses will be conducted using R software (V.4.5.1), with the following R packages: stats (V.4.5.1), epiR (V.2.0.72), mice (V.3.17.0) and glmmTMB (V.1.1.10).

Monitoring, Safety and Audit

The monitoring, safety management and audit of this single-centre study will be jointly overseen by the research team, Ethics Committee and Data Safety Monitoring Committee (DSMC) to ensure trial quality and participant safety.

The Principal Investigator (PI) will assume overall responsibility for coordinating trial operations, including data collection, protocol implementation and communication with relevant stakeholders. Recruitment progress is monitored biweekly via a pre-defined traffic light system, with targeted acceleration measures activated promptly if recruitment rates fall below the anticipated target to ensure sample size adequacy.

The DSMC, composed of experts in clinical research, statistics and ethics, will review recruitment progress, conduct interim monitoring of safety and efficacy data, assess any unforeseen effects of the intervention and recommend to the PI whether trial modifications or termination are needed based on data analysis. The DSMC will hold its first meeting prior to trial initiation, with subsequent meetings scheduled at least twice annually (frequency may be adjusted based on trial progress).

The Ethics Committee will perform annual audits of the trial to ensure compliance with ethical guidelines, good clinical practice and regulatory requirements. Investigators at the single centre will be responsible for promptly documenting and responding to serious adverse events (SAEs) and suspected unexpected serious adverse reactions (SUSARs). All SAEs and SUSARs must be reported to the CI, Ethics Committee and DSMC in accordance with regulatory timelines (eg, within 24–48 hours for urgent events).

Protocol dropout

Reasons for withdrawing from the trial include the patient withdrawing their informed consent.

Patient and public involvement

Patient and public involvement representatives have been involved in the formulation of the cannulation procedure and postoperative follow-up plan, including determining the patient’s position during cannulation and the feasibility of postoperative follow-up timing, to ensure patient acceptability.

Discussion

IABP monitoring is indispensable for precise haemodynamic management during complex surgical procedures, particularly cardiac or major vascular surgery, where it guides vasoactive drug titration and controlled hypotension.25 However, catheter instability-induced blood pressure data distortion, whether underestimation or overestimation, poses catastrophic risks.26 Underestimation (eg, due to partial occlusion or kinking) may lead to unnecessary fluid resuscitation or vasopressor use, increasing cardiac load, pulmonary oedema risk and drug-related adverse events. Conversely, overestimation (eg, from tip wall apposition or thrombus) masks true hypotension, delaying intervention and endangering vital organ perfusion.27 28 Ensuring radial artery catheter patency and stability is thus critical for reliable monitoring and patient safety.

Anatomical location is a key determinant of radial artery catheter instability. The traditional puncture site, adjacent to the radial styloid process within the wrist joint’s range of motion, is vulnerable to displacement from wrist flexion, extension, pronation or supination. Such movements can irritate vessel walls, induce spasm or cause catheter angulation, leading to waveform damping or signal loss.12 The modified cannulation site (1.5–2.5 cm proximal to the radial styloid process) addresses this limitation by avoiding the wrist joint’s mechanical stress centre, minimising catheter disruption from hand movements. Beyond enhanced stability, puncture sites distal to the wrist joint correlate with lower post-procedure infection rates12 13 17 and improving patient comfort by avoiding the sensitive wrist area and enabling natural wrist positioning, which may enhance long-term comfort.

This study acknowledges potential limitations. First, as a single-centre study, generalisability may be constrained. However, with a large sample size, coverage of most major surgery types and flexible anaesthesia plans, this study’s conclusions will have broad clinical applicability. Second, the modified site might have a lower initial cannulation success rate than the traditional site, potentially biasing outcomes. To mitigate this, we will standardise ultrasound-guided puncture techniques and restrict cannulation to dedicated experienced personnel, minimising operator variability.

In summary, this trial addresses a critical gap in invasive monitoring by targeting anatomical vulnerability—a modifiable risk factor for catheter instability. By rigorously evaluating the modified cannulation site, we aim to advance haemodynamic monitoring practices and optimise patient safety.
