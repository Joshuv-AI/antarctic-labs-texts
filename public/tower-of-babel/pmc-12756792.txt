Fluorophore Design via Generative Modeling in a Curated Subspace
Authors: Bomin Kim, Islambek Ashyrmamatov, Umit V. Ucak, Juyong Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
We present a fluorescence-oriented molecular design strategy that integrates multiple generative models within a chemically constrained and photophysically relevant subspace. Rather than relying on large-scale data sets or model-specific tuning, our approach builds on a curated training set constructed exclusively from atom-in-SMILES (AIS) fragments spanning chromophore-like molecules. The pipeline accommodates reinforcement learning–based ReLeaSE, which employs AIS-based tokenization, MolDQN, which utilizes a graph-based molecular representation, and the combinatorial optimization–based MolFinder, which operates on SMILES strings, each guided by a semiheuristic objective function targeting excitation energy, oscillator strength, and fluorophore similarity. Despite the compact training corpus, the pipeline yields structurally novel and optically active molecules, validated via QM calculations at the sTDA/CAM-B3LYP level. Our findings demonstrate that strategic data curation and property-driven integration can enable general-purpose models to succeed in specialized discovery tasks such as de novo fluorophore design.

Introduction

Recent advancements in artificial intelligence (AI) have revolutionized the field of molecular design. Reinforcement Learning (RL), in particular, has demonstrated notable potential in guiding molecule generation through sequential decision-making, optimizing for desired physical and chemical properties. − Among the diverse application areas, generating fluorescent compounds stands out as a compelling target, largely because multiple interrelated factors such as excitation energy, oscillator strength, quantum yield, and Stokes shift must all be balanced simultaneously. , Achieving optimal fluorescence means adjusting several molecular features at the same time such as how electrons are distributed, how flexible or rigid the molecule is, and what kinds of substituents are attached. These factors influence each other in complex ways, so improving one property such as brightness can easily worsen another such as stability. Because of this interdependence, there are no simple rules or linear trends to follow, making the design problem highly multidimensional and well suited for AI-based exploration. The past decade has seen rapid growth in fluorescence-based chemical sensors, which have broad applications in bioimaging and spectroscopy, further motivating the need for efficient discovery pipelines.

A prominent early contribution is ChemTS by Yang et al., an efficient Python library that integrates Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) with a recurrent neural network (RNN) to explore chemical space without predefined fragments. Sumita et al. extended this framework by coupling ChemTS with DFT simulations to discover and experimentally validate photofunctional molecules with target excitation energies, later scaling it with massive parallelization to generate a novel naked-eye–detectable fluorescent compound; their latest work, ChemTSv2, introduced customizable reward functions and parallel MCTS to accelerate molecular discovery. − Other advances include a multiconstraint generator based on conditional transformers and reinforcement learning, an adversarial generative model that produces fluorescent compounds with strong oscillator strength and charge-transfer properties, and FLAME, a modular AI framework combining large-scale databases and predictive models for fluorophore design. Although significant progress has been made, the de novo design of fluorescent molecules continues to pose challenges due to the above-mentioned nonlinear relationship between molecular structure and fluorescence-related properties. Existing AI-assisted models struggle to balance molecular diversity, synthesizability, and complexity, while high-throughput quantum chemical calculations, even with parallelization, are still computationally demanding.

In this study, we systematically investigated three avant-garde molecular generation algorithms: ReLeaSE and MolDQN, both grounded in reinforcement learning, and MolFinder, a combinatorial optimization algorithm. We devised a pipeline that integrates ReLeaSE, MolDQN, and MolFinder, all operating on a shared curated data set built from chromophore-inspired fragments. MolDQN and MolFinder retained their native input formats (2D molecular graphs and SMILES, respectively), while we extended ReLeaSE to incorporate atom-in-SMILES (AIS) tokenization, enriching the chemical representation landscape. Our main goal was to generate fluorescent compounds using these distinct generative approaches, with molecular optimization in each model guided by a semiheuristic objective function.

The primary contribution of this work is methodological engineering of chemical data and scoring: by restricting generator and predictor token space to chromophore-derived AIS fragments and by crafting a photophysics-aware reward, we show that general-purpose generative models can be repurposed for fluorophore design without bespoke architectures. The generated molecules were comprehensively evaluated on (i) photophysical properties (excitation energy E and oscillator strength f) predicted by our ML models and cross-checked by single-point sTDA­(CAM-B3LYP)/def2-SVP calculations; (ii) scaffold diversity via Krüger-style ring-system fragmentation; (iii) synthetic accessibility using SAScore, SCScore, RAScore, and SYBA (all range-normalized and inverted where lower is better); and (iv) molecular complexity using Bertz, Barone, Böttcher, and a Spacial index. These metrics were selected to span key dimensions including photophysical plausibility (E, f), structural novelty (scaffold analysis), synthetic accessibility, and molecular size and topology. We note their known limitations: synthetic accessibility and complexity scores are heuristic and not absolute predictors of laboratory feasibility; scaffold counts depend on fragmentation rules; and ML-predicted ω and f are only used directionally and are validated with sTDA before interpretation. Final screenings were further validated through quantum mechanical calculations to confirm the photophysical relevance of top candidates.

Results and Discussion

Model Overview

To provide an overview of our strategy, Figure illustrates the complete setup, showing how ReLeaSE, MolDQN, and MolFinder are integrated with curated data sets and demonstrate their respective molecular representations and predictive components. An expanded explanation of the workflows shown in Figure (step-by-step descriptions for ReLeaSE, MolDQN, and MolFinder; AIS tokenization details; and generator–predictor training protocol) is provided in the Supporting Information. We have revised the ReLeaSE model’s input-output structure, replacing traditional SMILES tokenization with the AIS scheme for inputs, and translating AIS tokens back to SMILES for outputs. We feed AIS tokens to ReLeaSE to reduce the ambiguity of plain-SMILES tokenization and to make the sequence model more sensitive to substructures; outputs are then detokenized to canonical SMILES for downstream compatibility. This change is an I/O wrapper in ReLeaSE and requires no architectural edits, unlike MolDQN and MolFinder, where the input representation is tightly coupled to graph actions/featurization and thus harder to replace.

Illustration of our refined molecular modeling approach, drawing from the ZINC and PubChemQC databases. Using the AIS tokenization scheme, the ReLeaSE model synergizes generative and predictive networks, while the MolDQN model modifies 2D SMILES graphs to achieve desired molecular properties. MolFinder leverages the CSA algorithm and SMILES representation for prediction. Property prediction utilizes both a Random Forest model and a multilayer perceptron (MLP) with multitask loss. All predictive methods utilize AIS-based binary fingerprinting for optimized molecular representation and analysis.

In essence, the ReLeaSE merges the strengths of two deep neural networks:the generative and the predictive models. Although trained individually, these interconnected networks collaborate during the optimization phase. The MolDQN model interprets SMILES as 2D graphs and then alters atoms, bonds, or molecular groups, informed by the Markov decision process, until the molecule manifests desired properties. We have retained the original structure of MolDQN.

MolFinder, designed for molecular property optimization, is fundamentally a generative tool. It is based on the conformational space annealing algorithm, operates on the SMILES-based representation, and generates new molecules through crossover and mutation operations on SMILES strings (See model details in the Supporting Information).

For the predictive components of the models, the elements of the AIS basis set shape the dimensions of the fix-length binary vector, serving as the input data structure. We employed two models to predict excitation energy and oscillator strength: The Random Forest-based (RF) algorithm (Scikit-learn version 0.21) and a multilayer perceptron (MLP) incorporating multitask loss. The final loss was the sum of mean squared errors (MSE) between predictions and PubChemQC references, vertical excitation energies and oscillator strengths computed by TD-DFT­(B3LYP)/6–31+G­(d) on B3LYP/6–31G­(d) geometries in GAMESS.

Basis Set and Data Set Curation

For our generative models training, the data set is derived from the ZINC20 database, providing a vast pool for the examination of potential bioactive compounds. We started with a data set comprising approximately 350 million organic molecules. In addition, we had a small collection of (nearly 9000) chromophores and fluorophores forming the backbone of our investigation because of their capacity to absorb and emit light. This set served solely to derive a basis set made of atom-in-SMILES (AIS) fragments, an atomic environment , based tokenization scheme designed to address the inherent ambiguities associated with conventional SMILES tokenization. Our AIS basis contains 501 unique tokens, of which 17 are SMILES topology markers (syntax that encodes connectivity/stereochemistry rather than elements). Examples include branch parentheses “(” and “)”, ring-closure indices “1–9”, bond-order symbols “–”, “=”, and “#”, E/Z markers “/” and “\”, and the aromatic-bond “:”. These markers guide how atoms are connected but do not correspond to physical atoms. The extensive ZINC data set was subjected to an exhaustive analysis as each molecule was scrutinized to ensure that all of its fragments are members of AIS basis set, leading to the isolation of the most germane molecules. A MaxMin diversity picker, using ECFP4 descriptors (Morgan radius = 2; 2048-bit) with Tanimoto dissimilarity, was applied to the curated ZINC20 pool, yielding a ∼2.25 million-molecule training set.

The training data set for our predictive models originates from PubChemQC, a leading database for first-principle quantum chemistry calculations. The excited states are computed using time-dependent density functional theory (TD-DFT) at the B3LYP/6–31+G* level, facilitated by GAMESS. We processed GAMESS outputs to extract optimized structures, vertical excitation energies, and oscillator strengths for roughly 1.8 million molecules. Using the same AIS basis (derived from our 9k chromophore/fluorophore corpus) that we applied to ZINC20, we curated PubChemQC and obtained 500,000 molecules with photophysical labels. Because both data sets are characterized by the identical AIS basis set, the generator and predictor operate in the same molecular subspace, aligning the generator’s token space with the predictor’s input domain.

Objective Function

Fluorescence in molecules are determined by several factors, including its excitation energy, oscillator strength, quantum yield and Stokes shift. In this study, we specifically focused on excitation energy and oscillator strength, positing these factors as key indicators of a molecule’s fluorescence potential due to their fundamental roles in dictating fluorescence properties. An efficient fluorescence in a molecule often necessitates a relatively high oscillator strength for the corresponding transition. For organic molecules, an excitation energy that falls within the ultraviolet to the visible spectrumt, typically ranging from 1.5 to 6.0 eV, favors the fluorescence. We designed an objective function rewarding the generation of molecules with attributes aligning with ideal fluorescent properties.

We use BODIPY as a canonical fluorophore reference. The target value for the HOMO → LUMO vertical excitation is taken from TD-DFT­(CAM-B3LYP)/TZP (ADF STO basis), 3.137 eV, which is higher than the experimental value (∼2.46 eV). Several hybrid/RSH functionals (B3LYP, PBE0, wPBEh, CAM-B3LYP) yield comparable energies for this state, but CAM-B3LYP provides a bright energy–intensity pair and long-range correction; using B3LYP instead would not change our conclusions. In our framework, the reference serves as a directional prior guiding generation toward higher-energy, brighter transitions rather than as an absolute benchmark. This parameter was chosen to align with the ideal attributes for efficient fluorescence in organic molecules. In addition, our function aims to maximize the average structural similarity of the generated molecules to a reference set of 2900 fluorophores. Overall, rather than making architectural changes, our approach focuses on training corpus and the mathematical form of the objective function: by restricting token space to chromophore derived AIS fragments and by weighting excitation energy, oscillator strength and fluorophore similarity, we convert general-purpose generators into effective fluorophore design engines.

Predicted Photophysical Properties and Scaffold Diversity

To assess the predicted photophysical property profiles of the generated molecules, Figure juxtaposes the distributions of excitation energy and oscillator strength against those of the PubChemQC-500 K reference set, a basis indicating structural diversity. For interpretability, we define a positive outcome as high oscillator strength with excitation energies near the reference region (within the 1.5–6.0 eV window), while the PubChemQC-500 K curves are shown only as a general-purpose reference distribution drawn from a structurally diverse data set without fluorophore/chromophore emphasis. Regarding oscillator strength (f) distributions and associated statistics, PubChemQC baseline exhibited a median value around 0.30. Both MolDQN-RF and MolFinder-RF models generate molecules with notably higher f values, clustering around 1.0, whereas their MLP counterparts yield moderately lower strengths (e.g., MolFinder-MLP centers near 0.60). While the ReLeaSE-MLP distribution aligns more closely with the natural reference profile, ReLeaSE-RF significantly underperforms, with a mean f around 0.2. This suggests suboptimal fluorophore design, despite achieving relatively higher excitation energies. Overall, these results indicate that the MolFinder and MolDQN modelsparticularly their RF-trained variantsare more effective at generating molecules with desirable oscillator strength properties.

In terms of excitation energy, PubChemQC exhibits a broad distribution centered around 5.2 eV. The MolDQN and MolFinder models demonstrate tightly clustered excitation energies in the range of 2.5 to 4.0 eV, with much narrower variances. Such behavior suggests that these models consistently generate molecules with excitation energies closer to the design target (near BODIPY’s 3.13 eV) rather than mimicking the wide variability in natural chromophores. ReLeaSE-RF displays a significantly higher mean excitation energy of 6.93 ± 0.88 eV, often falling outside the optimal fluorescence window (1.5–6.0 eV). ReLeaSE-MLP exhibits a distribution shifted approximately 4 eV lower relative to ReLeaSE-RF, but with greater variance (1.74 eV). Nonetheless, both ReLeaSE models yield excitation energy profiles that resemble the broader natural distribution observed in PubChemQC, likely reflecting a reduced bias toward specific chromophore-like substructures during generation.

To assess if models tend to generate molecules by using certain scaffolds more frequently than others, we conducted an analysis to quantify the scaffold diversity by applying the methods developed by Kruger et al. This technique systematically fragments molecules into characteristic scaffolds by breaking ring systems. It allows a direct evaluation of scaffold reuse and diversity across different generative outcomes. For consistency with Kruger et al., we maintained Kruger’s default fragmentation rules, focusing on nonring single bonds disconnections. From a design perspective, a trade-off exists between maximizing the number of unique scaffolds and limiting over-representation of a few scaffolds. Here, “scaffold recycling” denotes overuse of the same scaffold across many outputs; operationally, higher counts in the “>10” reuse bin indicate over-representation. Models like MolDQN-RF achieve a more balanced compromise, whereas ReLeaSE models prioritize nonredundancy at the expense of scaffold richness. In fact, MolFinder variants generated the largest number of unique scaffolds compared to MolDQN and ReLeaSE models. They also showed a non-negligible bias toward certain scaffolds. MolDQN-MLP and ReLeaSE variants however generated fewer total scaffolds (146, 136 and 174), but importantly, their distributions are almost entirely (84.2, 91.9 and 91.4%) concentrated in the single-use bin. These results imply that MolFinder models, while achieving broader scaffold coverage, are more prone to reusing a subset of favored scaffolds, MoldDQN and ReLeaSE, although less diverse overall, maintain a cleaner one-fragment–one-molecule mapping, minimizing partial scaffold collapse. In Figure , the tight clustering of excitation energies for the MolFinder variants arises primarily from the target-centered penalty in the objective function and is not attributable to any single scaffold family. By contrast, the oscillator-strength distribution shows that MolFinder-RF shifts probability mass toward higher values compared to MolFinder-MLP; as corroborated by Figure , molecules in this region contain the recurrently used, highly conjugated scaffolds.

Chemical Validity

SMILES-validity alone is an insufficient proxy for chemical validity. A syntactically valid SMILES representation can encode a molecular graph that is chemically inaccurate and such discrepancies, if exist, can be captured via cheminformatics-level sanitization. Recent findings by Schoenmaker et al. indicate that invalid outputs from some of the generative models ranges between 4 to an extreme 89%, with different models having different error-type distributions. We applied a series of chemical consistency checks (including valence validation, aromaticity assignment, and kekulization) to the shortlisted set (top 50 per model; 300 molecules total) after property prediction and selection, and excluded any failures from downstream analyses.

Out of 300 molecules analyzed, five invalid structures were detected, all originating from the ReLeaSE model family (four of them by ReLeaSe-RF implementation). After property prediction, each model’s outputs were ranked by a composite score (score = f norm × ε norm with min–max normalization within model) that increases with both oscillator strength and excitation energy. We then selected the top 50 per model (300 total) for downstream inspection. Only explicit valence violations were encounteredno syntax-related errors (e.g., unmatched ring closures, incorrect bond symbols) or aromaticity inconsistencies were observed. These included configurations such as oxygen atoms exceeding a valence of 2, nitrogen atoms with valence 4 in a nonquaternary context, and halogens (Cl, Br) forming more than one bond, violating standard valence rules (See Figure ). Notably, MolDQN and MolFinder modelsacross RF and MLP implementationsproduced 100% chemically valid outputs.

Molecular Complexity

The structural complexity of molecules is a fundamental property that influences their synthetic feasibility, biological activity, and functional diversity. While complexity is often invoked intuitively by chemists, quantifying it rigorously remains a nontrivial challenge. To systematically assess and compare the complexity of the molecules generated by our models, we incorporated four widely recognized metrics rooted in graph-theoretical and topological principles: the Bertz complexity index, the Barone complexity, the Böttcher complexity, and the spacial complexity score (SPS). Each score captures distinct but complementary aspects of molecular architecturebranching, cyclicity, stereochemical density, and conformational richnessthus providing a multifaceted view of molecular complexity. To enable direct comparison across these heterogeneous scales, we normalized all scores to a common [0, 1000] range, where higher values correspond to greater structural complexity.

The Bertz index emphasizes connectivity and symmetry within the molecular graph, penalizing local redundancies and favoring branching and ring systems. Barone complexity follows a similar logic but reweights contributions based on atom types, highlighting chemically distinct substructures. Böttcher’s metric, built from an information-theoretic perspective, further refines complexity by incorporating the diversity of local atomic environments, stereochemistry, and microstructural asymmetries. The spacial complexity score, recently introduced by Waldmann and co-workers, shifts focus toward three-dimensional aspects such as stereocenter content, hybridization states, and ring topologies, emphasizing biologically meaningful structural features while minimizing dependence on molecular size.

Figure reveals consistent trends across metrics: MolFinder models produce molecules with substantially higher structural complexity, as evidenced by elevated median scores, particularly under Bertz and Barone indices. This reflects MolFinder’s ability to navigate chemical space toward densely connected, heavily branched, and polycyclic structures. Notably, this trend extends beyond two-dimensional graph features: in the Böttcher and spacial complexity scores, which are sensitive to conformational and stereochemical richness, MolFinder again tends to generate molecules occupying more conformationally and topologically intricate regions of chemical space. In contrast, MolDQN and ReLeaSE models exhibit lower and more narrowly distributed complexity scores across all metrics. This suggests that their molecular outputs are comparatively simpler, often favoring linear or minimally branched structures with fewer rings or stereocenters. Such behavior is consistent with the known limitations of reinforcement learning models, which tend to prioritize accessible transformations and may underexplore topologically rich chemical space unless explicitly biased otherwise. Cross-comparison of Figures and indicates an intuitive anticorrelation: models with higher structural complexity (e.g., MolFinder) tend to exhibit lower synthetic accessibility on SCScore, RAScore, and SYBA. SAScore shows weaker separation because it emphasizes fragment frequency and size; molecules built from common chromophore motifs yield moderate SAScore values even when their overall topological and 3D complexity is high.

Structure Generation and Excited-States Properties

Prior to QM calculations, we generated 3D structures from SMILES. Most molecules succeeded; a small fraction failed for two primary reasons. (i) Force-field parametrization failure (12 cases): molecules passed RDKit valence sanitization but contained unsupported atom types or bonding patterns (e.g., hypervalent sulfur/phosphorus), for which UFF lacks parameters, leading to precondition errors during minimization (a coverage gap, not “unrealistic chemistry”); (ii) Conformer embedding failure (9 cases): no reasonable 3D conformer could be placed by ETKDG despite valid inputs, typically in molecules with highly strained ring systems, disconnected fragments, or tautomeric instability. We attempted fallback strategies (alternative ETKDG settings and defaults). Molecules that failed both the initial attempt and fallbacks were excluded from QM. Representative examples for each class are provided in the Supporting Information Figure S5 to indicate concrete areas where future generative models could improve.

In the final screening stage, a systematical computational investigation was performed to evaluate the photophysical properties of generated molecules. Geometries were optimized using the XTB2 − method to obtain reliable ground-state conformers, followed by excited-state calculations employing the simplified Tamm–Dancoff approximation (sTDA) as implemented in ORCA 6.0.1. We selected def2-SVP (def2 family) for RI/JK compatibility and a balanced polarized split-valence description; calculations used CAM-B3LYP with RIJCOSX and TightSCF to obtain vertical excitation energies and oscillator strengths. Screening criteria were established to select molecules with excitation energies in the range of 1.5–6.0 eV and oscillator strengths cutoff of 0.20 (slightly lower value than PubChemQC median, that is around 0.30), chosen pragmatically to prioritize transitions with appreciable intensity for final selection. In addition, to ensure strong electronic transitions, only molecules whose dominant orbital contribution exhibited an absolute transition coefficient greater than 0.9 were retained. All six successful candidates presented in Table are novel, as verified against the PubChem database, and demonstrate promising electronic excitation characteristics that meet the target criteria.

Conclusion

This work presents a novel strategy for fluorescence-oriented molecular generation that repositions existing generative models within a chemically constrained, property-aware framework. Rather than pursuing an exhaustive benchmark across models, our aim was to demonstrate that even with a modest, fragment-curated training set built on chromophore-inspired AIS tokens, meaningful photophysical outcomes can be achieved. By aligning the chemical space with fluorescence-relevant motifs and embedding multiobjective scoring into generation, we enabled diverse generative modelseach with their own representational strengthsto operate effectively within a fluorescence-specific design space.

Importantly, the pipeline’s downstream validationincluding excited-state QM screening, synthetic accessibility, and molecular complexity filteringconfirmed that the selected molecules are both optically promising and structurally novel relative to existing databases. This validation step was essential, as predicted properties from machine learning models can be misleading for chemically strained or exotic structures, where excitation energies or oscillator strengths may be estimated wrongly. We note as limitations that experimental synthesis and photophysical measurements were not performed in this screening study, and that systematic benchmarking against published experimental values was not undertaken due to our exclusive focus on novel candidates; both remain priorities for future work. We also note that, spectral line widths were not incorporated to objective function, as such data are absent from the PubChemQC-derived training labels and require vibronic or solvent broadening analyses beyond the vertical-excitation framework used here.

Our results emphasize that model complexity alone is not the limiting factor; rather, careful data set design, chemically aligned representations, and multistage filtering are key to successful discovery. This study thus opens a path for repurposing general-purpose generative models toward specialized molecular design tasks through targeted data engineering and postgeneration refinement.
