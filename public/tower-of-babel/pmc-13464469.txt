Synaptic zinc plasticity shapes adaptive and maladaptive cortical plasticity following cochlear injury
Authors: Manoj Kumar, Cassandra Linnertz, Brandon Bizup, Wenyuan Huang, Huiwang Ai, Thanos Tzounopoulos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cochlear damage triggers compensatory primary auditory cortex (A1) plasticity that amplifies responses to residual sensory inputs, thereby contributing to the restoration of both cortical responsiveness to sound and perceptual sound detection threshold. However, this adaptation can become maladaptive, producing neuronal hyperactivity that contributes to tinnitus and hyperacusis. The neuromodulatory mechanisms governing these adaptive and maladaptive changes remain unknown. Here, we demonstrate that noise-induced cochlear injury triggers bidirectional synaptic zinc signaling plasticity that potentiates activity in excitatory principal neurons and parvalbumin-expressing interneurons, while suppressing activity in somatostatin-expressing interneurons. These cell-type-specific effects of synaptic zinc plasticity contribute to restoring A1 responsiveness to sound and perceptual detection thresholds, while being necessary for neural hyperactivity. Together, our findings establish synaptic zinc as a pivotal neuromodulator that shapes both adaptive and maladaptive cortical plasticity and identify a promising therapeutic target for improving perceptual recovery after cochlear damage and mitigating tinnitus and hyperacusis.

INTRODUCTION

Noise-induced hearing loss (NIHL) is a major public health concern in modern societies where exposure to loud environments is pervasive. Over 48 million adults in the United States and 430 million people worldwide are affected, and the World Health Organization estimates that more than one billion individuals are at risk due to excessive noise exposure (1). NIHL is the leading cause of tinnitus and hyperacusis (2), highly prevalent disorders that significantly impair quality of life and impose an annual economic burden of $150 billion in the U.S. Current interventions—hearing aids and cochlear implants—offer limited benefit: hearing aids perform poorly in noisy settings (3), and cochlear implants require prolonged rehabilitation (4) and fail to fully restore hearing. A critical barrier to developing effective treatments is the incomplete understanding of the neural mechanisms underlying NIHL and its associated perceptual disorders.

Exposure to loud noise damages cochlear structures, including inner and outer hair cells (IHCs, OHCs), spiral ganglion neurons (SGNs), and synapses between IHCs and SGN dendrites (5). This peripheral damage reduces sensory input to the brain, triggering compensatory plasticity throughout the central auditory pathway (6–11), with the primary auditory cortex (A1) exhibiting the most robust changes (7–9, 12). In A1, principal neurons (PNs) increase responsiveness to spared inputs, restoring sound-evoked responses and thresholds, elevating spontaneous firing, and reorganizing frequency tuning toward less damaged cochlear regions (8, 9). These adaptations involve cell-type-specific changes (7): recovery of parvalbumin-expressing interneurons (PVs) stabilizes PN activity; reduced somatostatin-expressing interneuron (SOM) activity permits PN and PV recovery; and recovered vasoactive intestinal peptide-expressing interneuron (VIP) activity enables PN and PV recovery by inhibiting SOMs. Such plasticity likely improves perceptual detection thresholds (8, 13), particularly in quiet environments (13), but fails to restore features encoded by precise spike timing, such as speech or modulated noise (8). Moreover, it can lead to maladaptive neural hyperactivity underlying tinnitus and hyperacusis (9, 11, 14). Despite its importance, the neuromodulatory mechanisms driving this cell-type-specific cortical plasticity remain unknown. Elucidating these mechanisms will advance our understanding of sensory cortical adaptative and maladaptive transitions after peripheral damage (15, 16) and help identify targets for therapies that enhance recovery while mitigating tinnitus and hyperacusis.

Here, we investigate whether synaptic zinc signaling mediates this plasticity. Synaptic zinc is a major neuromodulator of neurotransmission and sensory processing (17–19). The vesicular zinc transporter ZnT3, which loads zinc into synaptic vesicles, is frequently co-localized with the glutamate transporter VGLUT1 on glutamatergic vesicles (20); and vesicular (synaptic) zinc is co-released with glutamate to fine tune glutamatergic neurotransmission, mainly via NMDA and AMPA receptor modulation (21–25). Moreover, RNAscope studies have demonstrated that the Slc30a3 gene encoding the ZnT3 protein is expressed in GABAergic PV and SOM neurons (26), suggesting that synaptic zinc may also be released from SOM and PV neurons to modulate GABAergic synaptic transmission. In fact, ex vivo acute brain slice electrophysiology experiments have shown that endogenous ZnT3-dependent synaptic zinc release enhances SOM-mediated GABAergic inhibitory postsynaptic currents onto PVs and PNs (27). In sensory processing, synaptic zinc signaling fine tunes neuronal gain (28), sensory stimulus discrimination (29, 30), and sensory stimulus adaptation that enables efficient control of neural processing and behavior, including deviant stimulus detection and adaptation to different background noise statistics (31–33). Importantly, synaptic zinc signaling undergoes bidirectional experience-dependent plasticity (23, 34–37), suggesting a potential role in cortical sensory processing and behavioral adaptation after NIHL. To test this, we combined cell-type-specific longitudinal two-photon Ca2+ imaging (2PCI) in awake mice, in vivo zinc imaging using a genetically encoded zinc sensor, operant behavioral assays, and pharmacological and genetic approaches to manipulate cortical zinc signaling in mice. Our results demonstrate that noise-induced plasticity in cortical synaptic zinc signaling shapes cortical and perceptual plasticity by exerting cell-type-specific control of neural activity.

RESULTS

Recovery of A1 sound-evoked neural response amplitude and perceptual threshold after NIHL

To investigate the neuromodulatory mechanisms underlying A1 neural and perceptual plasticity after noise trauma, we used a mouse model of NIHL in which awake mice were bilaterally exposed to 8–16 kHz narrow-band noise at 100 dB SPL for 2 hours (Fig. 1, A and B). Cochlear damage was assessed by measuring auditory brainstem responses (ABRs) before noise exposure (NE) and at 1 and 10 days after NE (Fig. 1, A to C). ABRs comprise five waves reflecting activity from the auditory nerve (AN) to the inferior colliculus (Fig. 1C). Wave I represents the AN activity in response to the sound stimulus and serves as a reliable indicator of hearing threshold, defined as the lowest sound intensity eliciting a significant Wave I response. Consistent with previous findings (7), NE caused sustained elevation of ABR thresholds for at least 10 days (Fig. 1D, red). Wave I amplitudes across sound intensities (Fig. 1E) and gain, calculated as the average Wave I amplitude change per 10 dB SPL step above threshold, remained significantly reduced (Fig. 1F, red). Sham-exposed (SE) mice showed no changes (Fig. 1, D and F, gray). These results confirm that our NE protocol induces NIHL that reduces the sensory input from the cochlea to the brain for at least 10 days after noise trauma.

To examine compensatory cortical plasticity after NE, we performed longitudinal in vivo wide-field Ca2+ imaging in awake mice to measure A1 PN sound-evoked responses before and after NE (Fig. 1G). To target A1 PNs, we injected the auditory cortex (AC) with AAV9-CaMKII-GCaMP6f, which drives GCaMP6f expression under the CaMKII promoter (Fig. 1G). Twelve to sixteen days post-injection, to identify A1, we presented low-frequency tones (5–6 kHz, 40–60 dB SPL) and imaged sound-evoked changes in transcranial GCaMP fluorescence. Because A1 and the anterior auditory field (AAF) exhibit mirror-reversed tonotopic gradients, these stimuli elicited activity in two distinct regions of the auditory cortex corresponding to the low-frequency domains of A1 and AAF (28) (Fig. 1G). After locating A1, we presented trains of broadband sounds (6–64 kHz, 100 ms, 30–80 dB SPL), and we recorded A1 PN responses before NE and at 1 and 10 days after NE (Fig. 1H). One day after NE, A1 PN response thresholds were elevated, like ABR thresholds (Fig. 1I), but unlike ABR thresholds, PN thresholds recovered by day 10 (Fig. 1I). Response amplitudes decreased at day 1 but exceeded pre-NE levels (hyperactivity) by day 10 after NE (Fig. 1J). Likewise, while AN gain remained reduced for 10 days, A1 PN gain increased and persisted (Fig. 1K). No changes were observed in SE mice (fig. S1, A to C). These findings, consistent with previous studies (7–9, 12, 13, 38, 39), indicate that despite persistent peripheral deficits, cortical sound-evoked responses recover and even become hyperactive after NE.

Next, we asked whether cortical recovery or hyperactivity of sound-evoked responses correlates with perceptual hearing threshold recovery after NE. We used an operant auditory avoidance task in which mice crossed a shuttle box upon hearing a 6-s noise burst (40) (Fig. 1L and Materials and Methods). Crossings during noise trials were scored as Hits, and crossings during silent trials as False Alarms (FAs). After reaching training criteria (Hits >80%, FAs < 20%), mice were tested with noise bursts of varying intensities (20–80 dB SPL). We calculated sound-detection rate (Hit% – FA%) and defined perceptual threshold as the sound level yielding 50% detection (Fig. 1M). Thresholds were measured before NE and at 1- and 10-days after NE. One day post-NE, perceptual thresholds increased significantly, like ABR and PN thresholds (Fig. 1N). By day 10, perceptual thresholds fully recovered to pre-NE levels, unlike ABR thresholds but like PN thresholds (Fig. 1, M and N). SE mice showed no changes (fig. S1D). No change in FA rate was observed in either sham- or noise-exposed mice (fig. S1E), suggesting that the observed shift in perceptual thresholds was not due to a change in FA rate. These results indicate that NE initially impairs both peripheral and perceptual thresholds, but despite persistent peripheral damage, cortical and perceptual thresholds recover within 10 days.

Chelation of extracellular cortical zinc impairs recovery of A1 sound-evoked responses and perceptual thresholds after NIHL

To test whether cortical zinc signaling contributes to neural and perceptual plasticity after NE, we chronically disrupted zinc signaling in the AC. Starting one day before NE and continuing through 10 days post-exposure, we infused the high-affinity extracellular zinc chelator ZX1 (21, 41) into the right AC via an implanted cannula connected to an osmotic pump (Fig. 2, A to C; Materials and Methods). Mice also received AAV-CaMKII-GCaMP6f for selective imaging of putative PNs. After imaging pre-ZX1 A1 responses to broadband sounds, mice were anesthetized, and an osmotic pump filled with either 500 μM ZX1 and 50 μM Alexa-594 in ACSF (ZX1) or just 50 μM Alexa-594 in ACSF (vehicle) was implanted subcutaneously (Fig. 2C). Alexa-594 was added (red-dye) to indirectly track the infusion of the ZX1 into the AC (Fig. 2D, Discussion). The following day, we started our longitudinal in vivo wide-field Ca2+ imaging to compare sound-evoked responses in pre- and post-NE in ZX1- vs. vehicle-infused mice (Fig. 2, E to K). In pre-NE, ZX1 infusion reduced baseline sound-evoked activity compared to vehicle, confirming that AC zinc enhances auditory responses (28, 42) (fig. S2, A and B). After NE, vehicle-infused mice showed robust recovery and hyperactivity of A1 responses by day 10, whereas ZX1-infused mice exhibited only partial recovery and never exceeded pre-NE levels (Fig. 2, I and J), thus showing no hyperactivity. Response thresholds were unaffected (Fig. 2H), but ZX1 significantly reduced response gain at day 1 after NE and throughout the 10-day period after NE (Fig. 2K). In SE mice, ZX1 had no effect (fig. S2, C and D). These findings indicate that chronic removal of cortical zinc attenuates A1 PN recovery and prevents hyperactivity, implicating extracellular zinc signaling in cortical adaptive and maladaptive neural plasticity after NIHL.

Because recovery of neural responses parallels perceptual threshold recovery (8, 13) (Fig. 1N), we assessed perceptual sound detection thresholds in ZX1- vs. vehicle-infused mice after NE (Fig. 2, L to P). Because cortical zinc was removed unilaterally from the right AC, to minimize the involvement of the left AC, we plugged the right ear during behavioral testing sessions (Fig. 2L). ZX1 infusion did not affect perceptual thresholds before NE (Fig. 2, M and N), but significantly elevated thresholds compared to vehicle at 10 days post-NE (Fig. 2, O and P) without affecting FA rate (fig. S2E), indicating reduced perceptual recovery. ZX1 did not alter ABR thresholds or subcortical responsiveness, as shown by unchanged ABR wave IV/I and V/I ratios (fig. S2, F to H), suggesting its effects were cortical rather than peripheral or subcortical, but we cannot exclude thalamic involvement. In SE mice, neither ZX1 nor vehicle affected perceptual thresholds (fig. S2, I to K). Together, these findings demonstrate that extracellular cortical zinc signaling contributes to A1 neural plasticity and perceptual threshold recovery after NIHL (“Limitations of this experiment and interpretation of the ZX1 results” in Discussion).

Genetic deletion of ZnT3 in AC neurons reduces recovery of A1 responses and perceptual thresholds after NIHL

ZX1 infusion removes both tonic and vesicular zinc, the latter requiring the vesicular transporter ZnT3 (17, 21). To determine the specific contribution of ZnT3-dependent synaptic zinc signaling to A1 neuronal and perceptual plasticity after NE—and to rule out potential non-specific effects of ZX1—we genetically eliminated ZnT3 from AC neurons using a dual-recombinase transgenic ZnT3 Cre/Rox mouse line (ZnT3-CreERT2+/+/Rox+/+-mCherry; Fig. 3, A and B), which we extensively tested and validated in our previous publications (36, 42). This mouse enables temporal, region-, and cell-type-specific ZnT3 deletion in adult mice (42). Namely, the Cre-lox recombinase system allows for the tamoxifen-inducible expression of exogenous genes or knockout of floxed genes in ZnT3-expressing neurons, while the DreO-rox recombinase system allows for brain region- and neuronal subtype-specific deletion of ZnT3 in adult mice (42) (Fig. 3B). Here, to investigate the effects of genetic elimination of ZnT3 from AC neurons, we only used the DreO-rox recombinase system; so, tamoxifen was not used in our study.

Using ZnT3 DreO-rox recombinase system, we first tested whether deletion of ZnT3 specifically in the AC reliably reduces sound-evoked extracellular synaptic zinc levels. To test this, we performed in vivo wide-field Zn2+ imaging in awake mice using FRISZ-TM, a genetically encoded far-red and extracellular transmembrane localized zinc sensor that has been validated in our previous publication (43, 44). Namely, FRISZ-TM displays a substantial zinc-specific turn-on response compared to other divalent cations (43, 44), including copper, calcium and magnesium, and selectively detects sound-evoked extracellular synaptic zinc in A1 (43, 44). For this experiment, we injected AAV-Syn-DreO into the right AC of ZnT3-CreERT2+/+/Rox+/+-mCherry mice to direct DreO expression in AC neurons (Fig. 3C, left). To image sound-evoked FRISZ-TM responses from AC neurons, we also injected the AAV9-hSynp-FRISZ-TM (Fig. 3C, left). These mice lacking ZnT3 from AC will be called ZnT3 cKO mice (Fig. 3C, left). For the negative control, we also employed ZnT3-CreERT2+/+/Rox+/+-mCherry mice that were now injected with AAV9-hSynp-FRISZ-TM and AAV9-Flex-TdTomato (Fig. 3C, right). We will call these mice ZnT3 WT mice (Fig. 3C, right). We then performed A1 wide-field FRISZ-TM imaging in awake mice to compare the sound-evoked extracellular zinc levels in response to broadband sounds of different intensities between ZnT3 WT and cKO mice (Fig. 3D). We found that the sound-evoked FRISZ-TM responses were significantly reduced in ZnT3 cKO compared to WT mice (Fig. 3D), supporting effective reduction of ZnT3-dependent sound-evoked extracellular zinc levels in ZnT3 cKO mice.

Next, to investigate the effects of genetic elimination of ZnT3 from AC neuron on A1 neuronal plasticity, we performed longitudinal 2PCI of L2/3 PNs in ZnT3 cKO and WT mice (Fig. 3, E and F). To image sound-evoked responses from A1 PNs, we injected the AAV9-CaMKII-GCaMP8m under the CaMKII promoter to direct its expression in putative A1 PNs. We performed longitudinal 2PCI in awake mice before NE and at 1-, 3-, and 10-days post-NE (Fig. 3, E to G). Pre-NE responses were averaged across two consecutive days (7). In ZnT3 cKO mice, we tracked 566 PNs (387 sound-responsive, d′ ≥ 0, Materials and Methods) across 10 days post-NE; in WT mice, 300 sound-responsive out of 387 tracked PNs were analyzed. Both groups showed elevated thresholds at day 1 post-NE, but by day 10, thresholds remained significantly higher in cKO compared to WT mice (Fig. 3H). Also, cKO mice showed higher distribution of PNs with elevated thresholds compared to WT mice (Fig. 3I) WT mice exhibited full recovery of response amplitudes and even hyperactivity by day 10, consistent with prior reports, whereas cKO mice showed only partial recovery and never exceeded pre-NE levels (Fig. 3J). Similarly, response gain increased markedly in WT mice, but this increase was attenuated in cKO mice, with a reduced shift in cumulative distribution toward higher gain (Fig. 3, K and L). No significant differences were observed in SE mice (fig. S3, A to D). Together, these findings demonstrate that ZnT3-dependent synaptic zinc signaling contributes to A1 L2/3 PN recovery and is needed for hyperactivity after NE. As such, these results highlight a cortical neuromodulatory mechanism that shapes A1 adaptive and maladaptive plasticity following NIHL.

To determine whether ZnT3-dependent synaptic zinc signaling influences perceptual recovery after NE, we assessed perceptual thresholds in mice with bilateral ZnT3 deletion in AC (ZnT3 cKO) vs. controls (ZnT3 WT). ZnT3 cKO mice were generated by bilateral AAV-Syn-DreO injections into AC, while WT controls received AAV-Flex-TdTomato (Fig. 3M). Before NE, perceptual thresholds did not differ between groups, indicating that ZnT3 elimination does not affect normal hearing (Fig. 3M). However, at 1- and 10-days post-NE, ZnT3 cKO mice exhibited significantly elevated perceptual thresholds compared to WT (Fig. 3, N and O), without affecting FA rates (fig. S3E), demonstrating that ZnT3-dependent cortical zinc signaling contributes to perceptual recovery after NIHL.

In SE mice, perceptual thresholds were similar between ZnT3 cKO and WT (fig. S3, F and G). Furthermore, ABR thresholds and ABR wave IV/I and V/I ratios were not different between ZnT3 cKO and WT either before or after NE and SE (fig. S3, H to K), suggesting that the observed perceptual deficits are not due to peripheral or subcortical changes, at least up to the inferior colliculus.

Overall, ZnT3 deletion reduced perceptual recovery after NE, supporting a causal link between zinc-dependent cortical plasticity and perceptual restoration. Importantly, while blocking synaptic zinc signaling allowed partial recovery of corticoneural responses and perceptual thresholds, it prevented hyperactivity. These findings suggest that targeting zinc signaling could partially preserve beneficial plasticity—such as improved perceptual thresholds—while preventing pathological hyperactivity associated with tinnitus and hyperacusis (Discussion).

Time-dependent changes in synaptic zinc and ZnT3 levels after NIHL

Experience-dependent modulation of synaptic zinc has been reported in visual and somatosensory cortices (34, 35, 45). In the auditory system NE increases synaptic zinc levels in the cochlea (36) but decreases them in the dorsal cochlear nucleus (23, 37). We therefore asked whether A1 synaptic zinc levels exhibit plasticity consistent with their role in corticoneural and perceptual recovery after NIHL. To test this, we performed in vivo wide-field Zn2+ imaging in awake mice using FRISZ-TM (Fig. 4, A and B, and Materials and Methods). FRISZ-TM was expressed in AC neurons via AAV9-hsynp-FRISZ-TM injection. We monitored zinc dynamics in response to trains of broadband sounds at 1- and 10-days post-NE (Fig. 4, C to F). At day 1, sound-evoked FRISZ-TM responses were reduced in NE compared to SE mice (Fig. 4, C and D), indicating decreased extracellular synaptic zinc signaling. By day 10, responses were significantly enhanced in NE mice relative to SE controls (Fig. 4, E and F). These findings reveal that NE induces dynamic, time-dependent plasticity in A1 extracellular—likely synaptic (43)—zinc levels.

To determine whether changes in synaptic zinc signaling after NE are linked to alterations in ZnT3, the vesicular zinc transporter, we quantified ZnT3 expression levels in the AC (Fig. 4, G to J). Because commercially available ZnT3 antibodies lacked specificity (at least in our hands), we used a validated knock-in mouse line (ZnT3-HA) in which an HA epitope tag is inserted at the C terminus of the endogenous Slc30a3 gene encoding the ZnT3 protein (36). HA immunostaining was performed on AC sections collected at 1- and 10-days post-NE. At day 1, ZnT3-HA expression did not differ between NE and SE mice (Fig. 4, G and H), suggesting that reduced synaptic zinc signaling at this timepoint (Fig. 4D) reflects decreased zinc release rather than reduced ZnT3 protein. In contrast, by day 10, ZnT3-HA expression was significantly increased in NE mice (Fig. 4, I and J), consistent with enhanced synaptic zinc signaling (Fig. 4F). These findings indicate that increased synaptic zinc signaling 10 days after NIHL is at least partly mediated by ZnT3 upregulation. Overall, NE induces time-dependent synaptic zinc plasticity that correlates with corticoneural plasticity and perceptual recovery after NIHL.

Time-dependent synaptic zinc plasticity modulates cell-type-specific sound-evoked responses after NIHL

Because we observed reduced extracellular sound-evoked synaptic zinc levels 1 day after NIHL (Fig. 4D) but enhanced levels at 10 days after NIHL, we next asked whether this time-dependent changes in zinc levels contribute to the observed changes in the sound-evoked activity of A1 PNs. To answer this question, we removed extracellular zinc in A1 via acute ZX1 infusion at 1 and 10 days after NE and SE (Fig. 5, A and B). We used 2PCI to assess individual PN responses to broadband sounds (20–80 dB SPL) before and after ZX1 (Fig. 5, C to K). Consistent with prior studies (28, 29, 42), ZX1 reduced sound-evoked responses in SE mice (Fig. 5E), confirming that extracellular zinc enhances PN activity. However, at both 1- and 10-days post-NE, ZX1 exerted a stronger inhibitory effect than in SE mice (Fig. 5, F to K). To quantify this effect, we plotted control vs. ZX1 response amplitudes and fitted linear regressions (Fig. 5, L to N). Slopes were significantly reduced at 1- and 10-days post-NE compared to SE, with unchanged intercepts (Fig. 5, O and P), indicating enhanced multiplicative gain control by zinc plasticity after NE. The increased multiplicative effect at day 1 likely reflects elevated tonic zinc, given the reduced sound-evoked synaptic zinc signaling at this time-point (Fig. 4D and Discussion); whereas the effect at day 10 aligns with increased synaptic zinc signaling (Fig. 4F). Because synaptic zinc enhances PN responses (28) (fig. S2A), these findings suggest that time-dependent zinc plasticity contributes to PN recovery of sound-evoked amplitudes after NIHL.

Previous studies suggest that reduced sound-evoked SOM responses after NE are associated with enhanced PN activity (7), but the underlying mechanism remains unclear. We tested whether synaptic zinc plasticity contributes to this reduction. In Pre-NE mice, ZX1 increased SOM responses to 30–50 dB SPL sounds (Fig. 6, C and D), consistent with prior reports (28). At day 1 post-NE, SOM neurons showed no sound-evoked responses at any intensity (30–80 dB SPL), regardless of ZX1 application (Fig. 6E). By day 10, ZX1 robustly enhanced SOM responses across nearly all intensities (Fig. 6, C and F). Although subtractive zinc effects were slightly reduced at lower sound intensities (Fig. 6J), we observed a strong divisive effect of zinc signaling after NE across intensities (Fig. 6, G to J). Together, these findings suggest that increased synaptic zinc signaling after NE suppresses SOM responses, thereby disinhibiting PNs and promoting neural recovery following NIHL.

Enhanced PV activity after NE is thought to balance PN hyperactivity (7), but the underlying mechanism remains unclear. We tested whether synaptic zinc plasticity contributes to this effect by performing experiments in PV-Cre mice, similar to those shown in Fig. 6 (Fig. 7, A and B). In Pre-NE mice, ZX1 increased PV responses to sound (Fig. 7, C, D, G, and J), consistent with prior reports (28, 29). At 1-day post-NE, ZX1 produced an even greater enhancement (Fig. 7, C, E, H, and J), indicating an amplified divisive effect of zinc signaling on PVs. Strikingly, at 10 days post-NE, ZX1 reduced PV responses (Fig. 7, C, F, I, and J), suggesting that elevated synaptic zinc levels (Fig. 4, F and J) exert a multiplicative effect at this later phase. Given that zinc strongly inhibits SOMs at day 10 (Fig. 6, F and I), this suppression likely disinhibits PVs, contributing to their heightened activity. Together, these findings support a model in which synaptic zinc plasticity exerts time-dependent, cell-type-specific effects on A1 L2/3 circuitry after NIHL: early zinc-mediated PV inhibition facilitates PN recovery via disinhibition, while later zinc-mediated SOM inhibition sustains PN and PV hyperactivity (7).

DISCUSSION

Extensive research demonstrates that neuromodulators are key drivers of cortical neuroplasticity, shaping sensory perception and behavior (39, 46). In A1, cholinergic signaling facilitates auditory learning (47, 48), dopaminergic signaling supports reward-based learning (49), serotonergic signaling gates critical-period plasticity (50), and norepinephrine enhances arousal and attentional modulation of auditory processing (51). Synaptic zinc signaling, in turn, modulates synaptic transmission and cortical sensory processing and adaptation. Despite these insights, the neuromodulatory mechanisms underlying compensatory cortical plasticity following peripheral damage have remained unclear. In this study, we identify a critical role for synaptic zinc signaling in promoting recovery in corticoneural sound responses and perceptual thresholds, while being necessary for neural hyperactivity. Understanding how neuromodulators influence corticoneural and perceptual responses following hearing loss will provide essential insight into auditory recovery mechanisms and inform therapeutic strategies aimed at restoring perception and mitigating perceptual hearing disorders.

We demonstrate that noise-induced plasticity of cortical synaptic zinc signaling is a key driver of both A1 L2/3 PN recovery, hyperactivity and restoration of perceptual sound-detection thresholds after NIHL. Using the genetically encoded zinc sensor FRISZ-TM (43), we observed a biphasic pattern: an initial reduction in sound-evoked synaptic zinc responses one day after NIHL (Fig. 4D), followed by a robust increase that exceeded control levels by ten days after NIHL (Fig. 4F). This late-phase upregulation of synaptic zinc signaling likely enhances the suppression of SOMs (Fig. 6, F and I), thereby disinhibiting PNs (Fig. 5, K and O) and PVs (Fig. 7, F and J), ultimately supporting recovery and permitting hyperactivity of sound-evoked responses.

How could noise-induced zinc plasticity shape sound-evoked SOM, PN and PV activity after NIHL? Previous studies show that synaptic zinc inhibits glutamatergic transmission at PN → PN, PN → PV, and PN → SOM synapses (42) in the AC of normal-hearing mice. Thus, elevated synaptic zinc after NIHL would likely strengthen zinc-mediated inhibition at PN → SOM synapses, reducing SOM sound-evoked activity—a hypothesis that remains to be tested. In addition, synaptic zinc enhances GABAergic transmission at SOM → PN and SOM → PV synapses (27), suggesting that changes in zinc levels after NIHL could also modulate inhibitory circuits. Future studies that isolate zinc’s effects on glutamatergic and GABAergic synapses across L2/3 and other layers will be essential to define the synaptic basis of zinc-driven modulation of PN, PV, and SOM activity following NIHL.

Pharmacological or genetic disruption of cortical synaptic zinc signaling from adult animals further support that noise-induced cortical synaptic zinc signaling and its plasticity contribute to the recovery of corticoneural and perceptual thresholds following NIHL (Figs. 2 and 3). While previous studies have shown that A1 plasticity correlates with perceptual recovery after peripheral damage (8, 13), our findings provide direct evidence that disrupting cortical synaptic signaling in A1 impairs this recovery (Fig. 3). As such, our findings provide direct support that A1 plasticity is critical for perceptual recovery after NIHL.

ZX1 was delivered to the AC via chronic cannulas to chelate synaptic zinc (Fig. 2). Because ZX1 diffusion cannot be measured directly, we estimated its spread indirectly by monitoring the spread of Alexa-594, which was co-infused with ZX1, as reported previously (28, 33). Alexa-594 diffused over an average area of 2.1 ± 0.1 mm2, corresponding to a radius of 0.8 ± 0.2 mm (28, 33). Assuming similar diffusion kinetics for ZX1 and Alexa-594, these measurements suggest that ZX1 was largely confined to cortical areas (28, 33). Consistent with this, Fig. 2D shows that the ZX1 spread extended beyond A1, suggesting that cortical fields outside A1 may also have contributed to the observed effects. Future studies will determine the extent of these contributions. Nevertheless, these results — together with Fig. 3, where cortical zinc was eliminated genetically — converge on a critical role for cortical zinc signaling in neural and perceptual plasticity following noise-induced cochlear trauma.

While our experiments suggest that elevated synaptic zinc signaling 10 days after NIHL likely contributes to increased sound-evoked activity in A1, they do not address either whether zinc signaling is necessary for the induction of A1 neural and perceptual plasticity, or whether transient ZX1 application produces lasting effects on neural hyperactivity. Future studies should therefore examine whether brief ZX1 infusion during noise exposure alone is sufficient to block the induction of A1 plasticity, and whether halting ZX1 infusion at different time points prior to imaging reverses neural hyperactivity.

Auditory processing and perception rely on both subcortical and higher-order cortical circuits (6, 10, 12, 52–56), which may also contribute to A1 neuroplasticity and perceptual recovery after NIHL. However, in our experiments, neither ZX1 infusion nor genetic deletion of ZnT3 altered subcortical responsiveness, as evidenced by unchanged ABR thresholds and wave IV/I and V/I ratios (fig. S2, F to H; fig. S3, H to K). These findings indicate that the effects of cortical synaptic zinc removal on corticoneural and perceptual recovery are not mediated through subcortical pathways, at least up to the level of the inferior colliculus. Although our results do not exclude contributions from subcortical or higher-order cortical areas, they underscore the critical role of cortical synaptic zinc signaling in A1 neural and perceptual recovery after NIHL. Importantly, because A1 hypersensitivity to sound is linked to perceptual hypersensitivity to sound and tinnitus (57, 58), and elimination of cortical zinc signaling blocked this neural hyperactivity, we propose—though not directly tested here—that synaptic zinc signaling may also contribute to the development of hyperacusis and/or tinnitus following NIHL.

Although our 2PCI experiments focused on A1 L2/3 PNs, noise-induced synaptic zinc signaling is likely to affect deep-layer PNs, including L5 extratelencephalic (ET) neurons and L6 corticothalamic (CT) neurons, which also show cell-type-specific noise-induced plasticity (59) and are modulated by synaptic zinc signaling (22, 31) in normal hearing mice. CTs primarily target thalamus and play a key role in shaping both the gain and the temporal dynamics of cortical sensory responses (60). In A1, CT activation is critical for biasing sound perception, enhancing detection at the expense of discrimination, or vice versa (40). ETs project to the thalamus and several other subcortical regions, including the brainstem, midbrain, striatum, and amygdala, where they influence sound-evoked responses and contribute to top-down modulation of auditory perception (61). Importantly, both ETs and CTs show noise-induced, cell-type-specific plasticity in their intrinsic properties, synaptic activity, and responses to sound (59, 62), which may contribute to perceptual recovery after NIHL (11, 40, 61, 63, 64). In our future studies, we will explore the role of synaptic zinc signaling in shaping ET and CT plasticity following NIHL.

ZnT3 transporters are the primary source of extracellular, labile, or synaptic zinc throughout sensory pathways, from peripheral to central structures (17). Although synaptic zinc levels in these areas change with experience and peripheral damage, these changes do not always correlate directly with ZnT3 expression. For example, optic nerve injury in the visual system increases ZnT3 immunostaining, suggesting that elevated ZnT3 contributes to higher synaptic zinc levels (65). Similarly, in the auditory system, noise-induced cochlear damage raises synaptic zinc and ZnT3 expression (36). In contrast, in the somatosensory system, whisker stimulation decreases synaptic zinc, while whisker plucking increases it—yet ZnT3 protein and mRNA remain unchanged despite increased zinc and synapse density (35, 45, 66–68). Consistent with these observations, sound-evoked zinc responses were reduced at day 1 after NIHL without changes in ZnT3 expression, but by day 10 both zinc responses and ZnT3 expression were elevated (Fig. 4). Because FRISZ-TM detects sound-evoked changes in extracellular synaptic zinc, and ZX1 produced stronger effects on PN and PV responses at day 1 after NIHL (Figs. 5 and 7), these results suggest that tonic zinc levels may be elevated at this early timepoint, influencing PN responses. The absence of ZnT3 changes at day 1 suggests that other transporters mediate the zinc dynamics underlying enhanced tonic zinc. Zinc homeostasis is regulated by 24 transporters: ZIP1–14 (SLC39) family members import zinc into the cytoplasm, while ZnT1–10 (SLC30) family members export zinc into intracellular compartments (e.g., ZnT3 into synaptic vesicles) or out of the cell (e.g., ZnT1 at the plasma membrane). Together, these findings point to complex involvement of both transporter families in regulating tonic and synaptic zinc after NIHL, and future studies will identify the specific transporters and mechanisms underlying these dynamic changes.

Because synaptic zinc signaling is present in all sensory cortices (17), our findings may reveal generalizable mechanisms of cortical plasticity following peripheral organ damage. For example, peripheral auditory nerve damage alters cortical neural oscillations and disrupts excitation–inhibition balance, potentially contributing to autism-like behaviors (69). Moreover, peripheral damage may lead to increased cortical synchrony and elevated spontaneous gamma oscillations associated with tinnitus (70), which is also associated with cognitive deficits (71). Importantly, therapeutic strategies that target brain plasticity offer promising avenues for treating disorders associated with sensory deficits. Recent studies highlight the potential of cell-type-specific manipulation of cortical interneurons to enhance perceptual recovery after peripheral damage. For example, in the visual cortex, exogenous SOM activation accelerates recovery after monocular deprivation (72), while in the AC, continuous PV activation reverses noise-induced deficits in gap detection (73), and brief 40 Hz PV stimulation restores normal loudness perception after NIHL (58).

Because FRISZ-TM responses can be affected by expression levels that may vary across animals, extracellular synaptic zinc levels cannot be accurately quantified. We plan to address this in future studies by adapting our sensors for fluorescence lifetime imaging microscopy (FLIM) (74), which would provide quantitative, probe concentration-independent readouts of synaptic zinc levels. Nevertheless, our current FRISZ-TM findings are supported by complementary assays and are consistent with previous studies. Specifically, reduced FRISZ-TM responses in ZnT3 cKO mice (Fig. 3D) align with prior confirmation of successful ZnT3 deletion (42), supporting reduced synaptic zinc in these mice. Enhanced FRISZ-TM responses at day 10 after NIHL (Fig. 4F) are further corroborated by elevated ZnT3 expression (Fig. 4I) and stronger ZX1 effects on A1 PN sound-evoked responses (Fig. 5), collectively supporting enhanced sound-evoked zinc signaling in A1 at this timepoint.

Our results identify cortical synaptic zinc signaling as a molecular target for therapeutic intervention in hearing loss and related disorders. Notably, inhibition of ZnT3 and synaptic zinc signaling permits partial restoration of corticoneural responses and perceptual thresholds while completely suppressing hyperactivity underlying tinnitus and hyperacusis. These findings underscore the therapeutic potential of targeting zinc transporters and zinc signaling to harness adaptive plasticity—such as improved perceptual thresholds—while mitigating maladaptive hyperactivity implicated in tinnitus and hyperacusis.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of Pittsburgh. The approved Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) protocol numbers that were used for this study were #26048358 and #23114004. Mice were housed under a 12 hour light/dark cycle at 65–75°F and 40–50% humidity. For experiments shown in Figs. 1, 2, 4, and 5 male and female C57BL/6 J mice were used. For experiments shown in Figs. 3, 6, and 7 male and female ZnT3-CreERT2+/+/Rox+/+-mCherry, SOM-Cre, and PV-Cre mice were used, respectively. For wide-field imaging and two-photon calcium imaging (2PCI), mice were injected with AAVs at postnatal day (P) 49–56, noise- or sham-exposed at P63–70, and underwent ABR, imaging, and behavioral testing between P60–84. For ZnT3 protein quantification (Fig. 4), ZnT3-HA mice were NE or SE at P63–70, and histology was performed between P64–84.

Speaker calibration

Acoustic stimuli were calibrated using preamplifier-connected microphones (1/8“ Type 4138-A-015 and 1/4” Type 4954-B, Brüel & Kjær) and a certified 1 kHz, 94 dB SPL reference speaker (Type 4231, Brüel & Kjær). The microphone was positioned at the mouse ear location. Pure tones and broadband stimuli were delivered at specific input voltages and resulting sound pressure levels were recorded. Voltage inputs required for desired dB SPL were determined using the 1 kHz, 94 dB SPL reference tone.

Noise exposure

Unanesthetized and unrestrained mice were NE or SE between P63–70 for all our experiments. Mice were placed in a 5 × 4-inch acoustically transparent box and bilaterally exposed to an octae-band noise (8–16 kHz) at 100 dB SPL for 2 hours, delivered from a calibrated speaker. Sham-exposed mice were placed in the same box for 2 hours under identical conditions, but without noise presentation. All exposures (SE and NE) were conducted between 4:00 and 7:00 p.m.

C57BL/6 J mice exhibit age-dependent hearing loss; however, all experiments were conducted in 8–12-week-old mice, an age range without detectable hearing deficits. Age-matched sham-exposed mice served as controls for all time points and manipulations. Sham-exposed C57BL/6 J and ZnT3-CreERT2+/+/Rox+/+-mCherry mice showed no changes in ABRs, cortical responses, or perceptual thresholds (figs. S1 and S3), confirming the absence of age-related hearing loss. Differences observed in ZX1 vs. Vehicle (Figs. 2, 5, 6, and 7) and AAV-DreO vs. AAV-TdTomato (Fig. 3) comparisons are attributable solely to experimental treatments, as age and genetic background were matched across groups.

Behavioral training and testing

Behavioral training and testing were performed in a shuttle-cage (14“ W x 7” D x 12” H) bisected into two virtual zones. The shuttle-cage was placed within a sound- and light-attenuation chamber and was equipped with 8 poled shocking floor, calibrated speaker, and mouse position sensors (Coulbourn Instruments). Sound-stimuli were generated using a programable tone/noise generator (A12–33, Coulbourn Instruments) and delivered via calibrated multi-field speaker (MF1, Coulbourn Instruments) hung in the middle of the shuttle cage to provide a homogenous sound field. Foot-shock signals were generated using a programable animal shocker (H13-17A, Coulbourn Instruments). Presentation of sound-stimuli, foot-shock signals and mouse position detection were performed using scripts programmed in GRAPHIC STATE 4 (Coulbourn Instruments).

On the day of training and testing, mice were given at least 5 min to acclimate to the shuttle-cage before beginning the training or testing session. Six to seven-week-old C57BL/6 J mice were initially trained to cross from one side of the shuttle-box to another side to terminate a 200 μA foot-shock. Foot-shock was terminated upon crossing to the other side of the shuttle-box or 10 seconds, whichever occurred first. Seven to eight blocks of 10-foot-shock trials were performed during a training session for two days. Next, mice were trained to cross from one side of the shuttle-box to the opposite side following a sound-stimuli (50 ms noise-burst, 6-sec long train with a repetition rate of 2.5 Hz at 70 SPL, with a randomized intertrial interval of 30–40 sec), to avoid a mild foot-shock (200–400 μA). A successful crossing during the noise-bursts trial was called Hit, whereas crossing during a random 6-sec long silent window was called False-Alarm (FA). Seven to eight blocks of 10 sound-stimuli trials were performed during a training session every day for 4–6 days. Mice were trained every day until their behavioral performance plateaued at Hits% > 80 and FA% < 20. During the behavioral testing sessions, we presented noise-bursts at various sound intensity levels (20–80 dB SPL) in random order. Each sound level was presented at least 10 times with a randomized intertrial interval of 30–40 sec. We measured the Hit and FA rates at individual sound-level and calculated the sound-detection rate (Hit% - FA%). To quantify the sound-detection threshold, we plotted the sound-detection rate against the sound-levels, and the sound-level with a 50% sound-detection rate was defined as the sound-detection threshold.

Auditory brainstem responses

ABR thresholds and wave I amplitudes were measured in mice (P62–84) under isoflurane anesthesia at ∼37°C using an RZ6 processor (Tucker-Davis Technologies), as previously described (7). Subdermal electrodes recorded responses to broadband clicks (1 ms duration, 10–80 dB SPL in 10 dB steps) presented at 18.56 clicks/s via a calibrated MF1 speaker and probe tube inserted into the ear canal. Each stimulus was repeated 512 times, and responses were averaged after bandpass filtering (300–3000 Hz). ABR threshold was defined as the lowest intensity producing a wave I amplitude ≥3 SD above baseline noise, measured from responses at 0 dB SPL. Wave I amplitude was quantified as peak-to-trough voltage. ABR thresholds and wave I amplitudes were measured in mice (P62–84) under isoflurane anesthesia (1.5% in O2) at ∼37°C using an RZ6 processor (Tucker-Davis Technologies), as previously described in Kumar et al. (7). Although isoflurane is known to elevate ABR thresholds in rodents (75), all ABR recordings in this study were performed under identical anesthetic conditions across experimental groups (e.g., NE vs. SE mice). Therefore, any potential elevation of ABR thresholds due to isoflurane is unlikely to affect the interpretation of our comparisons between groups.

Adeno-associated virus injections for in vivo imaging

To image sound-evoked activity in A1 PNs, male and female C57BL/6 J and ZnT3-CreERT2+/+/Rox+/+-mCherry mice (P49–56) were injected with AAV9-CamKIIa-GCaMP6f-WPRE or AAV9-CamKIIa-jGCaMP8m-WPRE into the right AC, as previously described (7, 27–29). For imaging PV and SOM interneurons, male and female PV-Cre and SOM-Cre mice received AAV9-CAG-Flex-GCaMP6f-WPRE injections. To image synaptic zinc responses, C57BL/6 J mice were injected with AAV9-hSyn-FRISZ-TM, as described previously (43). Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (induction: 3% in O2; maintenance: 1.5% in O2) where O2 flow rate was maintained at 0.8–1.0 liter/min and secured in a stereotaxic frame (Kopf Instruments). Body temperature was maintained at ∼37°C using a heating pad, and eyes were protected with ophthalmic ointment. Lidocaine (1%) was injected under the scalp, and a midline incision exposed the skull. A craniotomy (∼0.4 mm diameter) was made over the temporal cortex (∼4 mm lateral to lambda). Using a micromanipulator (Kopf), a glass micropipette containing AAV was inserted 0.5–0.7 mm below the dura, and ∼500 nl of viral solution was injected over 5 min. The scalp was closed with cyanoacrylate adhesive. Postoperative care included carprofen (5 mg/kg, Henry Schein Animal Health) for analgesia and monitoring for stress or pain. If signs of distress were observed, mice received hydrogel (ClearH2O) and ketoprofen until recovery.

Animal preparation for acute in vivo wide-field imaging

Twelve to sixteen days after AAV injection, mice (P60–80) were prepared for in vivo calcium imaging (27–29). Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (induction: 3% in O2; maintenance: 1.5% in O2) and positioned in a custom head holder. Core temperature was maintained at ∼37°C using a heating pad, and eyes were protected with ophthalmic ointment. Lidocaine (1%) was injected under the scalp, and a ∼1.5 cm incision was made over the right temporal cortex. The head was rotated ∼45° in the coronal plane to align the pial surface of the right AC with the imaging plane of the upright microscope. The skull was secured to the holder using dental acrylic (Lang) and cyanoacrylate adhesive. A tube (25 mL syringe barrel or SM1 tube, Thorlabs) was placed around the body to minimize movement. A dental acrylic reservoir was built to hold warm (37°C) ACSF over the exposed skull. ACSF contained (in mM): 130 NaCl, 3 KCl, 2.4 CaCl2, 1.3 MgCl2, 20 NaHCO3, 3 HEPES, and 10 D-glucose (pH 7.25–7.35; ∼300 mOsm). For improved optical access, lidocaine–epinephrine (2% lidocaine, 1:100,000 w/v epinephrine) was injected into the temporal muscle, and a small portion of muscle was retracted. For experiments in Figs 5, 6, and 7, to infuse ZX1 into the A1, under anesthesia, a craniotomy was made over A1. A glass micropipette containing ACSF with 100 μM ZX1 was inserted at the craniotomy edge using a micromanipulator. Mice recovered from isoflurane for 60 min before imaging. Imaging was performed in a sound- and light-attenuating chamber containing the microscope and a calibrated speaker (ES1, Tucker-Davis Technologies). After baseline recordings, ZX1 (100 μM) was infused at 30 nl/min for 20 min, then at 9 nl/min, and responses were re-measured.

In vivo wide-field imaging and analysis

We performed transcranial imaging to measure sound-evoked activity in specific neuronal populations in awake mice (P60–80). Isoflurane was discontinued, and imaging began after a 60 min recovery period (27–29). Sounds were delivered from a calibrated free-field speaker (ES1, ED1 driver, Tucker-Davis Technologies) positioned 10 cm from the left ear, controlled by a digital-to-analog converter (USB-6229, National Instruments; 250 kHz output rate). Sound waveforms and hardware synchronization were managed using Ephus (76). To localize A1, we presented 6 or 32 kHz tones (50 dB SPL) while illuminating the skull with a blue LED (490 nm; M490L2, Thorlabs). Green GCaMP6f fluorescence was imaged using epifluorescence optics (eGFP filter set, U-N41017, Olympus), a 4x objective (Olympus), and a cooled CCD camera (Rolera, Q-Imaging). Images were acquired at 174 × 130 pixels (4x spatial binning; each pixel = 171.1 μm2) at 20 Hz. Fluorescence changes (ΔF/F) were computed by normalizing sound-evoked ΔF to baseline fluorescence (F), where F is the average fluorescence of 1 s preceding the sound onset (for each pixel in the movie). A two-dimensional low-pass Butterworth filter was applied to each frame, and ΔF/F images were generated by averaging 10 consecutive frames (0.5 s) starting at stimulus offset. After A1 localization, we presented broadband sounds (6–64 kHz, 100 ms duration) at 30–80 dB SPL in 5 dB steps from the calibrated speaker and imaged sound-evoked ΔF/F responses. Each stimulus was presented 8–10 times in pseudo-random order.

For in vivo wide-field Ca2+ imaging analysis, a region of interest (ROI; 150–200 μm by 150–200 μm) over A1 was selected to quantify sound-evoked responses to broadband sounds (6–64 kHz, 100 ms). Fluorescence intensity from all ROI pixels was averaged per frame, and ΔF was normalized to baseline fluorescence (F) to yield ΔF/F. Responses from 8–10 presentations of the same sound level were averaged. GCaMP6f response amplitude was defined as the peak within a 50 ms window occurring within 1 s of sound onset. Response threshold was the lowest sound level eliciting a significant fluorescence increase (≥2 SD above baseline F). Response gain was calculated as the slope of response amplitude versus sound level, expressed as the average ΔF/F% change per 5 dB SPL step starting at threshold (8, 13, 77). To assess ZX1 effects on gain type (multiplicative/divisive or additive/subtractive), sound-evoked amplitudes under ZX1 were plotted against control amplitudes, and slope and y-intercept were obtained from linear regression (28). Fits with correlation coefficient r < 0.80 were excluded. Control data in Figs. 6 and 7 include SOM and PV responses from Kumar et al. (7). These data were used to assess the ZX1 vs. control comparisons. The ZX1 experiment was performed in the same control mice as in the previous publication (7), but neither the ZX1 data nor these comparisons were included in the previous publication (7).

For in vivo wide-field Zn2+ imaging analysis, FRISZ-TM zinc sensors exhibit two components: a fast motion-related transient (<0.3 s post-stimulus) and a slower (>0.3 s) synaptic zinc response. To attenuate motion artifacts, we applied a 4th-order, 5 Hz low-pass Butterworth filter and temporally averaged 10 consecutive frames (500 ms) after stimulus onset. FRISZ-TM response amplitude was defined as the peak of the filtered ΔF/F occurring between 300 ms and 2 s after sound onset.

Longitudinal in vivo wide field and 2PCI

Following AAV injections into the AC, a 3 mm cranial glass window was implanted over AC in mice (P49–56), as described previously (77). The temporal muscle was retracted to expose the A1 (∼4–4.5 mm lateral to the midline). The skull was thinned and a ∼ 3 mm by 3 mm section was removed using a 3 mm biopsy punch, leaving the dura intact. A 5 mm glass coverslip was placed over the craniotomy, sealed with silicone elastomer (Kwik-Sil), and reinforced with dental cement. A metal head plate was affixed with dental cement for head fixation during imaging. Mice were head-fixed under a 2P microscope and acclimated for 30–40 min while broadband and pure-tone sounds were passively presented. The following day, after A1 localization, chronic wide-field imaging and 2P imaging of layer 2/3 neurons (175–225 μm below pia) were performed in awake mice. Imaging used a galvanometer-based 2P microscope (Scientifica) controlled by ScanImage 3.8, with a mode-locked infrared laser (940 nm; MaiTai HP, Newport) and a 40x, 0.8 NA objective (Olympus). Green and red fluorescence were simultaneously collected using two PMTs behind emission filters (FF01–593/40, FF03–525/50; Semrock) and a dichroic splitter (Di02-R561; Semrock). Images were acquired at 5 Hz over a 145 by 145 μm field (256 × 256 pixels). Sound stimuli consisted of broadband trains (6–64 kHz, 100 ms) presented at 20–80 dB SPL in 10 dB steps, pseudo-randomized with 5 s interstimulus intervals. Each 2P imaging session lasted 20–30 min, after which mice were returned to their home cage. To track longitudinal changes, the same neurons were imaged for 10 days post SE or NE using blood vessel architecture, depth, and neighboring cell positions for identification (77). Pre-exposure sessions included two days of recordings, and average responses across both days were used as baseline.

2PCI analysis

Images were analyzed post hoc using a custom program, and open-source routines, written using Python 3.5 and MATLAB 2007b, 2011b, 2013a, and 2018a as described previously (33). Before extracting ΔF/F, we used the NoRMCorre software to correct motion artifacts from individual tiff movies (78). Next, using FISSA, a neuropil decontamination toolbox for Ca2+ imaging signals (79), we selected ROIs around the soma of each L2/3 neuron from the temporal average of all tiff movies from a single recording session. Fluorescence values were extracted from each ROI for each frame, and the mean for each cell was computed. FISSA gave us two vectors of fluorescence values for the somatic and the neuropil. We weighted the neuropil vector by 0.8 as described previously (7, 33). The weighted neuropil vector was subtracted from the somatic vector to produce a corrected vector of fluorescence values. These FISSA corrected fluorescence (F) values from each sound trial were then converted to ΔF/F values by using baseline fluorescence measured 1 sec before each sound onset. We then averaged the ΔF/F values from each sound-trail (5–8 trials) to get mean ΔF/F from each neuron. To identify the sound-responsive neurons, we used a tone sensitivity index, d-prime (d’), from preexposure sessions as described previously (33). Briefly, we presented trains of pure tones at interstimulus intervals of 3 s in pseudo-random order that spanned in the range of 4–40 kHz frequencies (500-ms long) in 0.20-octave increments at 30–80 dB SPL in 10 dB SPL steps. For each neuron, we calculated the average response amplitude from responses at and immediately adjacent to the frequency/level combination eliciting the maximum response (preferred frequency, the average of 5 values if the maximum response is observed at dB < 80, 4 values if the maximum response is observed at 80 dB). We then averaged the same number of values selected at random frequency/level locations of the frequency response area (FRA). We took the difference between these averages and iterated this process 1000 times. The tone sensitivity index, d-prime (d’), was calculated as the average of the iterated differences, and the neurons with d’ ≥ 0 only were analyzed further. We then used these sound-responsive cells to assess sound-evoked activities, such as response threshold, amplitude, and gain. The sound-evoked responses were measured for 1 s after the sound onset and were defined as significant responses if the sound-evoked changes in ΔF/F were larger than the mean + 2 standard deviations (SDs) of the baseline fluorescence measured before the sound onset. Peak fluorescence signals during the 1-s period after the sound presentation were quantified as the sound-evoked response amplitude. The response gain was defined as the average change in the fluorescence signals (ΔF/F%) per 10 dB SPL step starting from response threshold (8, 13, 77).

Chronic cannula and osmotic pump implantation

Chronic cannulas were implanted into the right AC to continuously remove local and extracellular zinc via ZX1 infusion. Custom-made cannulas, fused to 3–5 cm vinyl tubing (from Alzet osmotic pumps), were prepared using 30-gauge needles. On the day of surgery, following AAV injections and craniotomy (∼3 mm), the cannulas were implanted medial to A1 (29). The connected vinyl tubing was routed subcutaneously over the mouse’s back. The dura was covered with Kwik-Sil (WPI), and a cranial glass window was implanted as described above (Fig. 2B). Eighteen to twenty-two days post-surgery, in vivo wide-field imaging of A1 was performed. After recording baseline responses, mice were anesthetized, and an Alzet osmotic pump (DURECT, model #1002) filled with 500 μM ZX1 and 50 μM Alexa-594 in ACSF (vehicle) was connected to the tubing and implanted subcutaneously (Fig. 2C). The pump continuously infused ZX1 or vehicle into the AC at ∼4 nl/min. Intracortical spread of ZX1 into A1 was confirmed by red fluorescence signals of Alexa-594. One day after pump implantation, A1 sound-evoked responses were remeasured in the presence of ZX1 or vehicle. Mice were then NE or SE, and imaging was repeated at 1-, 3-, and 10-days after exposure.

Immunohistochemistry

For all immunohistochemistry experiments, mice at P77–84 were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane transcardially perfused with ice-cold 1× PBS, followed by 4% paraformaldehyde in 1× PBS (pH 7.4). Brains were extracted and postfixed for 2 hours in 4% paraformaldehyde. Brains were then submerged sequentially in 15% sucrose in 1× PBS for 24 hours and 30% sucrose in 1× PBS until they no longer floated in solution (typically 24 hours). Brains were then embedded and frozen in Tissue-Tek optimum cutting temperature compound (OCT) and sliced on a cryostat to a thickness of 50 μm into 1x PBS with 0.01% sodium azide.

For ZnT3-HA expression levels, ZnT3-HA mice (36) were NE or SE (P63–70) and histology experiments were performed at P64-P84. Tissue was collected and stained as above, except the slices were instead treated with primary antibodies against HA tag (1:1000 rabbit anti-HA tag, 3724S, Cell Signaling Technologies) and the corresponding secondary antibodies (1:1000 goat anti-rabbit-Alexa Fluor 555, A32732, Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Confocal image acquisition and analysis for immunohistochemistry

Confocal fluorescent imaging was performed on a Leica Stellaris 5 confocal microscope using a 20X objective and using the program LASX (Leica microsystems). Areas of interest were identified visually initially using a 10× air objective. Once the target regions were determined, using a 20× air objective, Z-stacks were collected (0.5-μm steps, 1 zoom, pinhole dimensions: 1.2 airy units). Fields of view with 10% x-y overlap were captured to encompass the area of interest. Auditory cortex boundaries were estimated based on the location of the rhinal fissure and the hippocampus. AC layers were estimated based on DAPI density. For comparisons between NE and SE ZnT3-HA mice, fluorescence intensity analysis function of LASX was used in each ROI.

Statistics and reproducibility

For statistical comparisons between two independent groups that passed the Shapiro–Wilk normality test, we used unpaired t-tests. Otherwise, we used the Mann–Whitney rank-sum test, Kruskal-Wallis one-way analysis of variance, or Friedman test for non-normally distributed data. For comparisons between multiple groups having within-subject factors, a repeated measures two-way ANOVA test was used, Bonferroni corrections were used for multiple two-sample post hoc comparisons among sample groups; the significance level (α = 0.05) of the test was corrected via scaling by the reciprocal of the number of comparisons. Greenhouse-Geisser correction was used when the assumption of sphericity was violated. A permutation test (80) was used for two sample comparisons. Samples for which 5000 of 100,000 random permutations of the data resulted in mean differences greater than the observed difference in sample means were considered significant (P = 0.05). Significance levels are denoted as *P < 0.05. All the statistical tests were two-sided. The details of statistical tests are described in the figure legends. Group data are presented as mean ± SEM. Sample sizes were not predetermined by statistical methods but were based on those commonly used in the field. The images shown in the Figs. 1G, 2C, 2D, 3F, 4B, 4G, 4I, 5C, 5F, and 5I are representative images that were repeated as many times as shown in the corresponding summary graphs.

Rigor and transparency

All experiments, including behavioral assays, in vivo imaging, and histological analyses were conducted under blinded conditions to minimize bias. Experimenters remained unaware of the exposure group (NE vs. SE), treatment condition (ZX1 vs. vehicle), and viral injections (AAV-DreO [cKO] vs. AAV-TdTomato [WT]) until after the analyses were completed.
