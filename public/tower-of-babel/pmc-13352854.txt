Chronic fatigue syndrome in nursing practice: a concept analysis
Authors: Yazhuo Gao, Qianyin Zhu, Min Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNurses are essential in chronic fatigue syndrome (CFS) management, serving as care coordinators and patient advocates, but currently lack a unified guiding framework. CFS affects about 0.89% of the global population based on CDC-1994 criteria, with higher prevalence in women. Since its initial definition in 1988, over 25 diagnostic criteria have been introduced, leading to ongoing confusion about core symptoms and diagnosis. This inconsistency poses significant challenges for nurses, including difficulties in patient identification, inconsistent assessment approaches, and lack of standardized care pathways. A clear theoretical model is needed to improve nursing assessments, intervention planning, and care standardization.MethodsA concept analysis was conducted using the Walker and Avant’s eight-step method. Eight databases were systematically searched for literature from January 1988 to December 2025. Studies on CFS definitions, diagnostic criteria, or conceptual frameworks were included if they addressed key attributes, antecedents, consequences, or measurement methods. Two graduate students and professors independently performed two-stage screening and data extraction. Multiple discussion rounds ensured analytical rigor.ResultsSixty-eight studies were included. CFS antecedents included infections, immune dysregulation, genetics, and stress. Five core attributes were identified as defining features of CFS in the reviewed literature: persistent severe fatigue, post-exertional malaise (PEM), non-restorative sleep, cognitive impairment and/or orthostatic intolerance, and multisystem involvement. Consequences involved disability, poor quality of life, psychological distress, social isolation, and economic burden. Empirical referents included diagnostic criteria, symptom scales, and functional measures.ConclusionsThis study proposed a nursing-oriented conceptual framework for CFS based on the reviewed literature, identifying five core features that may help distinguish it from general fatigue. The framework suggests plausible directions for nursing practice, including prioritizing PEM assessments, promoting energy management and pacing as potential interventions, and considering nurses as care coordinators in multidisciplinary teams. It may also contribute to CFS-focused nursing education and the development of assessment instruments. Given shared pathophysiological features reported in the literature, the framework may offer a conceptual basis for application to post-infectious fatigue conditions such as long COVID, though this transferability requires empirical validation in specific populations and clinical contexts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04749-y.Keywords: Chronic fatigue syndrome, Post-exertional malaise, Nursing, Concept analysis, Walker and Avant’s method

Chronic fatigue syndrome (CFS) is a chronic disease characterized by severe fatigue as its core manifestation. Since it was officially named by the Centres for Disease Control and Prevention in 1988, its conceptual definition and disease boundaries have remained controversial [1–2]. CFS and myalgic encephalomyelitis (ME) are often used interchangeably or combined as ME/CFS in clinical research and the literature; however, they differ in their conceptual origins, pathophysiological orientation, and diagnostic criteria. To ensure consistency, this study uses CFS as the primary term while preserving the original terminology used in cited literature. CFS concept ambiguity has long plagued clinical practice and academic research [3–5]. Since the first case definition, 25 sets of definitions or diagnostic criteria have been developed based on aetiology, pathophysiology, and exclusionary diseases [2, 6–7]. Significant differences exist across standards in core symptom definitions, course requirements, and exclusion criteria, hindering comparisons between research results, creating clinical practice inconsistencies, and restricting the standardized development of nursing practices [8–11].

The disease burden of CFS cannot be ignored, affecting approximately 0.89% of the global population based on CDC-1994 criteria [12]. Patients with CFS generally face problems such as reduced daily activities and impaired social function, and are often stigmatized due to the “invisibility” of the disease [13–15]. Patients with severe CFS experience profound suffering stemming from multiple interconnected sources: illness-related losses, social misunderstanding, and inadequate recognition within the medical system. Central to this suffering is the concept of disease-related loss, which encompasses the multidimensional deprivations patients endure as a consequence of their condition. These losses span several domains, including: (1) the disruption of normal daily life and established routines; (2) the erosion of previously held roles and interpersonal relationships; (3) the diminishment of social support from family and friends; (4) the relinquishment of leisure and recreational pursuits; (5) the depletion of material resources; (6) the fragmentation of personal identity and sense of self; and (7) the decline in functional capacity and independence [16]. In recent years, with the increasing number of COVID-19 patients, CFS has received unprecedented attention. Studies have shown that there is a significant overlap in the symptoms of the two diseases [17–18], which prompts the academic community to re-examine the pathological nature and conceptual boundaries of CFS [19–20]. However, the diagnosis and treatment of CFS still face great challenges due to the lack of specific biomarkers and standardized diagnostic tools [21–22].

Nurses play a pivotal role in the identification, assessment, and management of patients with CFS. Care priorities for patients with CFS include symptom validation, patient education, advocacy, therapeutic relationship building, energy management guidance, and care coordination. However, nurses face significant challenges due to conceptual ambiguity: difficulty in distinguishing CFS from other fatigue-related conditions, lack of standardized assessment protocols, and uncertainty in intervention planning. Nurses are ideal CFS care coordinators who can help patients access community resources such as occupational therapy and district nursing services [23–24]. Therefore, it is important to clarify the essential attributes of CFS through concept analysis to improve the disease cognition and nursing ability of nurses. This study adopted a concept analysis method proposed by Walker and Avant [25] to systematically determine the conceptual connotation and development context of CFS, aiming to clarify its defining characteristics, antecedents, consequences, and empirical reference indicators, as well as to construct a conceptual framework that can guide nursing practice for the provision of a theoretical basis for nursing practice, education, and research.

Methods

Concept analysis is an effective means to examine the basic elements of a concept, helping clarify ambiguous concepts and producing valuable research tools. To enhance methodological rigor and transparency, a scoping review informed by the JBI methodology for scoping reviews [26] and reported in accordance with the PRISMA Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) [27] was conducted to systematically retrieve and synthesize literature for this concept analysis. The scoping review included five key stages: (1) establishing the analytical framework based on Walker and Avant’s eight-step method; (2) developing a comprehensive search strategy across eight databases; (3) dual-independent screening and eligibility assessment; (4) standardized data extraction of concept definitions, attributes, antecedents, consequences, and empirical referents; and (5) evidence synthesis to operationalize the concept analysis. The advantages of a concept analysis are that it is systematic and organized, easy for other researchers to replicate, and tailored to nursing concepts to ensure relevance and practicability. Walker and Avant’s concept analysis method [25] is a simplified version of Wilson’s original concept analysis [28]. It consists of eight steps: concept selection; determining the purpose of the analysis; identifying all applications of the concept; identifying defining features; identifying typical cases; identifying relevant cases, borderline cases, and opposite cases; identifying antecedents and consequences; and defining empirical reference indicators.

Concept selection and analysis purpose

Conceptual ambiguity in CFS directly affects the reliability, validity, and comparability of nursing research findings. Establishing clear definitions via concept analysis facilitates the development and validation of measurement tools and improves research generalizability. CFS has multiple definitions and naming disputes, requiring systematic differentiation between chronic fatigue as a symptom and CFS as a disease entity. Clarifying its core attributes through concept analysis provides a conceptual basis for nursing theory development. Although CFS is a chronic, multisystem, debilitating disease requiring multidisciplinary care, patients are often excluded from necessary disability and social support services [29]. CFS nursing involves multidisciplinary professionals, with primary care clinicians typically playing a key coordinating role in healthcare, social care, and education support [24]. Clarifying CFS’s defining attributes, antecedents, consequences, and experience indicators helps caregivers accurately identify patient needs and develop individualized care plans.

Data sources

This study’s search period was from January 1988 to December 2025. January 1988 was selected as the start date because it marked the Centres for Disease Control and Prevention’s first formal CFS definition and the academic establishment of this concept; while December 2025 aligned with the search execution time that includes the latest diagnostic criteria updates and clinical intervention studies. A scoping search was conducted first, followed by an empirical literature retrieval from CNKI, Wanfang, SinoMed, PubMed, WoS, Embase, Cochrane Library, and CINAHL. Search keywords included chronic fatigue syndrome, myalgic encephalomyelitis, ME/CFS, systemic exertion intolerance disease, post-exertional malaise, and concept analysis. The complete database-specific search strategies, together with the field tags used and the number of records retrieved, are presented in Supplementary Table S1.

Inclusion criteria were: ① Focus on CFS concept definitions, connotation/extension, diagnostic criteria evolution, conceptual framework construction, or interdisciplinary cognitive differences; ② Cover definition characteristics, antecedents, consequences, or measurement methods; ③ Study types: peer-reviewed academic articles, systematic reviews, concept analysis papers, or authoritative diagnostic guidelines/consensus; ④ Concept-related studies on human CFS patients or healthy controls; ⑤ Language: Chinese or English. Eligibility was assessed via title and abstract review, and additional studies were identified by manually searching reference lists of retrieved articles.

Two trained graduate students served as independent reviewers, with a senior professor proficient in both Chinese and English acting as adjudicator for unresolved disagreements. Prior to the literature search, the researchers developed a search strategy and analysis plan. Following data extraction, the analytical process was guided by Walker and Avant’s framework [25]: extracted data on disease mechanisms and triggers were categorized as antecedents; recurring symptom features consistently identified across diagnostic criteria and empirical studies were synthesized into defining attributes; reported patient outcomes and disease impacts were classified as consequences; and validated assessment tools and diagnostic instruments were organized as empirical referents. Each included study was independently mapped to these analytical categories by the two reviewers, with disagreements resolved through discussion or, when unresolved, by the senior adjudicator.

Consistent with the methodological orientation of Walker and Avant’s concept analysis approach and the JBI scoping review framework [25, 26], the analytical aim of this study was to map the conceptual landscape of CFS rather than to synthesise effect estimates. Given that the included corpus spanned diagnostic guidelines, consensus criteria, systematic reviews, empirical primary studies, narrative reviews, and commentaries, sources were classified into three source-of-evidence tiers to inform the interpretive weight given during synthesis. Tier 1 comprised authoritative diagnostic guidelines, consensus criteria, and systematic reviews or meta-analyses; Tier 2 comprised empirical primary research, including quantitative, qualitative, and mixed-methods studies; and Tier 3 comprised narrative reviews, editorials, commentaries, and perspective articles. The mapping of all 68 included studies to the main analytical categories is presented in Supplementary Table S2.

Concept definition of CFS

The origin and historical evolution of the CFS concept

The ME term predates CFS. From the 1930s to 1950s, multiple epidemics facilitated its conceptual formation. Dr. Ramsay formally coined the term in 1956 and operationalized it in 1986–1988 as a disease characterized by muscle fatigability, whereby muscles fail to fully recover their strength after exertion [30–37]. The modern CFS concept emerged from the 1984 outbreak. In 1988, a Centres for Disease Control and Prevention expert group led by Holmes et al. formally defined CFS to better describe the syndrome [38–42] and moved beyond a single viral infection aetiology [43–47]. However, this definition lacked specificity in its symptom criteria and exclusion requirements, which led to inconsistent applications across clinical and research settings. Notably, the broad inclusion criteria risked misclassifying individuals whose fatigue was primarily attributable to psychiatric conditions (e.g., major depressive disorder) as CFS patients, thereby compromising the homogeneity of study populations and hindering etiological research [38–39, 43, 46]. Fukuda et al. thus revised this definition in 1994, and this became the most widely used global CFS diagnostic criteria [6, 42, 48–51]. In 2003, the Canadian Consensus Criteria (CCC) adopted ME/CFS, incorporating post-exertional malaise (PEM) as a core mandatory symptom for the first time. In 2011, the Myalgic Encephalomyelitis International Consensus Criteria (ME-ICC) used a more pathomics-based approach, requiring ≥ 50% reduction in premorbid activity and eliminating the 6-month waiting period [30, 44, 52–55]. In 2015, the Institute of Medicine (IOM) proposed replacing ME/CFS with Systemic Exercise Intolerance Disease (SEID), citing CFS’s vagueness and negative impact on patient care. However, SEID has not been widely accepted, and ME/CFS remains the most common diagnostic terminology used to date [1, 35, 56–60].

Uses and applications of the concept

The CFS concept serves three interrelated fields: clinical diagnosis, scientific research, and epidemiological investigation. Clinically, medical definitions of diseases must simultaneously provide researchers with a relatively homogeneous patient population and clinicians with a basis for individualized diagnosis, prognosis evaluation, and treatment [61]. Due to the lack of specific detection methods and biomarkers, CFS diagnosis relies on clinical case definitions [22, 48, 51, 62, 63]; it is estimated that up to 90% of ME/CFS patients remain undiagnosed, with 29% of diagnosed cases waiting five years or longer [64]. For scientific research, standardized case definitions are essential for study comparability and reproducibility [32, 40, 41]. White argued that the many failures to replicate CFS findings may stem precisely from the condition’s heterogeneous nature and the persistent difficulty in classifying it, noting that attempts to validate the numerous published criterion-based definitions have consistently ended in failure. Differences in symptom profiles and dysfunction severity exist among patients identified by different case definitions, making cross-laboratory comparisons inherently difficult; misclassification may significantly underestimate or overestimate associations with related conditions or their effects [65, 66]. Differences in symptoms and dysfunction severity exist among patients identified by different case definitions, making cross-laboratory result comparisons difficult; misclassification may significantly undervalue or overvalue associated diseases or effects [67–69]. A survey conducted by MEAction found that 95% of clinicians and researchers consider a uniform definition critical to enable cross-study result comparability [56, 70, 71]. In epidemiology, diagnostic criteria choice significantly impacts prevalence estimates: using Holmes’ 1988 criteria, which employed more restrictive diagnostic requirements, prevalence estimates were consistently lower than with later definitions; physician-based surveillance studies reported rates of approximately 4–10 per 100,000 (0.004%-0.010%), while community-based studies using Holmes criteria ranged from 0 to 0.3% (median ~ 0.05%) [40, 69, 72]; and Using Fukuda’s 1994 criteria, community-based prevalence estimates range from approximately 0.1% to 2.5%, with a pooled estimate of ~ 0.68%-1% across systematic reviews, though figures vary substantially by study design and population [12, 49, 63, 68, 73]. ME/CFS affects millions globally, ranging from 836,000 to 2.5 million U.S. adults [66]. Females are disproportionately affected, with female-to-male ratios ranging from 1.5:1 to 4:1 across studies, and peak incidence in the 10–19 and 30–39 age groups [1, 3].

Definition of the concept in the literature

Holmes definition

The first CFS work case definition, proposed by Holmes et al. in 1988, included: (1) report of persistent or recurrent, debilitating fatigue for 6 months or more that is not relieved by bed rest; (2) report of at least 8 out of 11 secondary symptoms (mild fever, sore throat, painful lymph nodes, unexplained muscle weakness, muscle pain, prolonged fatigue after exercise, headaches, migratory joint pain, neuropsychological complaints, sleep disturbances, and acute symptom onset); and (3) report that daily functioning is at least 50% impaired from pre-illness levels. The definition also included exclusion criteria for malignancies, autoimmune diseases, chronic infectious diseases, psychiatric disorders, neuromuscular disorders, endocrine disorders, and drug dependencies that might explain fatigue [39–41, 43, 44, 46].

Fukuda definition

In 1994, Fukuda et al. defined CFS as: (1) clinically assessed, unexplained, persistent, or recurrent chronic fatigue lasting 6 months or more with new or definite episodes; is not the result of persistent exertion; not substantially relieved by rest; and leads to a substantial reduction in previous levels of occupational, educational, social, or personal activity; (2) concomitant presence of 4 or more out of 8 symptoms (memory/attention problems, sore throat, tender lymph nodes, muscle pain, joint pain, headaches, lack of recovery from sleep, PEM) [44, 47–51, 55]. Given that only 4 out of 8 symptoms are required, some individuals who meet the diagnostic criteria may not have core symptoms such as PEM [32, 74, 75].

CCC definition

The 2003 CCC stipulates that the following symptoms must be present: (1) unexplained chronic physical or mental fatigue lasting for at least 6 months; (2) PEM requiring at least 24 h of recovery; (3) significant pain; (4) sleep dysfunction; and (5) two neurological or cognitive symptoms. In addition, individuals must report symptoms from two of the three categories of autonomic manifestations, neuroendocrine manifestations, and immune manifestations [44, 52, 53, 55]. The CCC selected cases with fewer psychiatric comorbidities and more severe physical dysfunction compared to the Fukuda criteria [31, 55, 69].

ME-ICC definition

In 2011, ME-ICC defined ME as an acquired neurological disease with complex systemic dysfunction, which is characterized by pathological disorders of the nervous, immune, and endocrine systems, as well as disorders of cellular energy metabolism and ion transport [35, 36, 44, 52]. Patients must report: (1) post-exercise neuroimmune exhaustion; (2) symptoms of neurological dysfunction, with at least one symptom from three of the four neurological dysfunction domains; (3) symptoms of immune, gastrointestinal, and genitourinary disorders, with at least one symptom from three of the five categories; (4) at least one energy production/transport disorder symptom. ME-ICC completely removes the 6-month waiting period requirement, emphasizing that early diagnosis may reveal new insights into the early stages of disease pathogenesis [76–78].

IOM definition

The clinical case definition proposed by the IOM Committee in 2015 requires the following symptoms: (1) a substantial reduction or impairment in the ability to participate in occupational, educational, social, or personal activities at pre-illness levels for more than 6 months, accompanied by extremely severe fatigue that is new or has a definite onset time, is not the result of persistent overexertion, and does not significantly relieve with rest; (2) PEM; (3) sleep without recovery; (4) at least one cognitive impairment or orthostatic intolerance. The above symptoms must be present and cause significant functional impairment during at least half the time, and the presence of other medical conditions should not prevent the diagnosis unless all symptoms are explained by these conditions [1, 32, 56, 58, 59, 64]. Maksoud et al. demonstrated the validity of these diagnostic criteria in clinical practice [79]. Through factor analysis and empirical methods, such as data mining, researchers have confirmed fatigue, discomfort, neural cognitive problems after labour, and non-restorative sleep as the most common core areas of the disease [31, 68, 74, 75, 80].

Differential diagnosis and disease differentiation

Although the clinical symptoms of CFS showed overlap, the aetiology, diagnosis standards, and core characteristics differed. Of the 68 studies included in this analysis, 8 (11.8%) were used to clarify differential diagnosis and disease definitions. Clarifying these distinctions is essential for accurate diagnoses and targeted treatments, as detailed in Fig. 2.

Commonalities and differences between CFS and similar concepts

CFS and ME

ME and CFS show symptomatic overlap, but their concept origin, pathological guidance, and diagnostic criteria differ. The core of ME is a multisystem dysfunction caused by inflammation of the nervous system, and has been classified as a disease of the nervous system by WHO since 1969[4,30–32,35−37,78]. CFS initially focused on the core manifestation of “unexplained chronic fatigue”, and the diagnostic framework focused on a combination of symptoms rather than pathological mechanisms [38, 53]. There are significant differences in the diagnostic criteria between ME and CFS. Hence, while there is some overlap between the two, ME and CFS essentially define two different diseases. Some patients and researchers oppose the simple equivalence of the two, and advocate for a precise definition based on differences in pathological mechanisms [75, 78, 81].

CFS and fibromyalgia (FM)

CFS with FM are common chronic functional diseases, both showing overlap in clinical manifestations and being highly vulnerable to clinical confusion, despite the clear characteristics and pathological mechanisms. The core driving symptom of CFS is disabling fatigue, with PEM being a hallmark manifestation, which is the delayed deterioration of symptoms after physical or cognitive activities, accompanied by fluctuations in multi-system functions [1, 44, 82]. On the other hand, the core characteristic of FM is generalized body pain (without a direct link to fatigue). Historically, the 1990 American College of Rheumatology (ACR) classification criteria required the identification of at least 11 out of 18 designated tender points on physical examination as a hallmark diagnostic feature [3, 82, 83]. However, subsequent revisions, notably the 2010/2011 ACR preliminary diagnostic criteria and the 2016 modifications, replaced the tender point count with a symptom-based approach employing the Widespread Pain Index (WPI) and the Symptom Severity Scale (SSS), reflecting a shift toward recognizing FM as a centralized pain condition rather than a peripheral tender point disorder [84]. The tender point criterion is therefore presented here for historical context and to illustrate the traditional basis for differentiating FM from CFS, rather than as a current diagnostic standard. Pathophysiological studies further support the distinction between the two. CFS patients are characterized by neuroimmune disorders and mitochondrial energy metabolism disorders, while FM patients are characterized by central nervous system hyperalgesia and hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis dysfunctions [85, 86].

CFS and long COVID

Since the COVID-19 pandemic, long COVID and its association with CFS have become a research hotspot. The two conditions exhibit highly similar symptom profiles and biological abnormalities, suggesting the presence of common pathophysiological mechanisms [19–20, 87]. But the two are not identical, as is reflected in the core differences between aetiology triggers and symptoms lineage. Long COVID diagnosis includes a history of SARS-CoV-2 infection, with clear symptoms associated with infection time, usually persisting 3 months after infection [17]. Although the aetiology of CFS is related to infection, it is not strictly dependent on infection. In some patients, it can be triggered by stress and environmental factors [1, 20, 30]. The spectrum of symptoms of SARS-CoV-2 is broader. In addition to CFS-related symptoms, it is often accompanied by persistent respiratory symptoms such as cough and dyspnoea, cardiovascular sequelae such as myocarditis and hypertension, and specific manifestations such as olfactory/taste disorders [88]. The symptoms of CFS are more focused on neuroimmune and energy metabolism dysfunction, and lack of specific damage to the respiratory and cardiovascular systems [1].

CFS and idiopathic chronic fatigue (ICF)

ICF refers to 6 months or more of unexplained chronic fatigue. Despite patients having symptoms of fatigue, these do not meet the diagnostic criteria for CFS. ICF is defined as a clinical diagnosis of mental and physical fatigue, no known pathological association, and does not satisfy at least four of the eight standard CFS symptoms [49, 88]. In a cardiopulmonary exercise testing study, CFS patients showed decreased exercise capacity when tested the next day, whereas ICF patients showed improvement, with responses more similar to those of sedentary and healthy controls. This finding suggests essential physiological differences between the two [89]. Furthermore, at the molecular level, skeletal muscle mitochondrial dysregulation has been reported as a potential contributor to ICF in older adults, with reduced mitochondrial content and impaired biogenesis signaling observed in ICF individuals compared with non-fatigued controls [90], providing additional evidence of distinct pathophysiological underpinnings between ICF and CFS. Distinguishing between CFS and chronic fatigue or ICF is very important in clinical practice, because CFS is a completely different pathological disease [91–94].

Conceptual definitions used in this study

Based on the above, CFS was defined as a complex multisystem disease lasting more than six months, characterized by severe fatigue that cannot be relieved by rest, accompanied by exertional discomfort, non-recovery sleep, cognitive impairment, and/or orthostatic intolerance, which significantly affects the patient’s daily function and quality of life and cannot be explained by other known diseases.

Defining characteristics

Persistent severe fatigue

This feature is characterized by a duration of six months or more, new onset or definite onset time, inability to be significantly relieved by rest, and resulting in a significant decline in mobility. The severity of fatigue in ME/CFS patients has been systematically quantified, where cognitive fatigue and physical fatigue are higher than mental fatigue, and adolescent patients have slightly higher fatigue than adults [91]. This persistent severe fatigue is different from ordinary fatigue after exertion. It is characterized by the inability to recover even after adequate rest, and has a substantial impact on daily activities. The multidimensional characteristics of fatigue have also been reported in the included literature. Fatigue in CFS patients involves physical, cognitive, and mental dimensions, and there are complex interactions among these [2, 53, 54, 95].

PEM

This feature is characterized by the worsening of symptoms after physical or mental activity, often with a delay of 24–48 h, lasting days to weeks, and with a significantly prolonged recovery time. PEM has been increasingly recognized as a core symptom of CFS in modern diagnostic criteria, and it is listed as a necessary condition for the diagnosis of CFS by ICC [96], IOM criteria, and British National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) guidelines. The included literature suggests that the probability of PEM in CFS patients is approximately 10.4 times higher than that in healthy people [97]. The clinical manifestations of PEM have the following characteristics: trigger factors are diverse (physical or mental fatigue can be induced), symptoms are complex (aggravated fatigue with cognitive difficulties, etc.), time characteristics are obvious (onset can be delayed, recovery takes 6–12 days), and is out of proportion to precipitating factors [98]. One study reported that 90% of patients experienced PEM after physical/cognitive fatigue and emotional distress, with an average of 7 ± 2.8 symptoms per episode, and 84% of patients lasting for more than 24 h [99]. High levels of PEM were frequently described as being associated with more symptoms, psychological distress, and social burden.

Non-restorative sleep

This feature is characterized by feeling tired after sleep, poor sleep quality, and inability to recover energy, which is an almost universal symptom of CFS. It is characterized by feeling unrested despite adequate or even prolonged sleep, daytime sleepiness, need for naps, difficulty falling asleep, and difficulty maintaining sleep [100, 101]. The reviewed literature suggests that CFS patients may have prolonged bed rest time, sleep latency, and decreased sleep efficiency [102]. However, there is inconsistency between the abnormal degree of objective sleep parameters and the subjective report of sleep disorders and fatigue in CFS patients, suggesting that there may be abnormal microscopic sleep structures not yet captured by traditional polysomnography [100]. Some studies have also suggested that changes in heart rate variability and abnormal autonomic nervous system activity during sleep may be involved in the mechanism of sleep disorders in CFS. Namely, these are manifested as increases in heart rate variability, heart rate, and the aggravation of orthostatic intolerance [101].

Cognitive impairment and/or orthostatic intolerance

85% of CFS patients report cognitive dysfunctions, which are subjectively described as “brain fog”, and manifest as delayed thinking, difficulty in concentration, confusion, and forgetfulness [103, 104]. Neuropsychological studies have suggested that CFS patients may be primarily impaired in visuospatial immediate memory, reading speed, graphic action ability, and episodic memory [105]. Decreased cerebral blood flow and abnormal neurovascular coupling have been reported in neuroimaging studies of CFS patients [106]. Some studies have reported increased neuroinflammatory markers in the cingulate gyrus, hippocampus, amygdala, and other regions, which were described as positively correlated with the severity of cognitive impairments [107]. Orthostatic Intolerance refers to the body’s inability to effectively regulate blood pressure and heart rate while standing, resulting in symptoms such as dizziness, rapid heartbeat, fatigue, and syncope, which improve when lying down [108, 109]. Common types of orthostatic intolerance include postural tachycardia syndrome, neurally mediated syncope, and orthostatic hypotension [110]. 32.5% of orthostatic intolerance patients present with postural tachycardia syndrome or orthostatic hypotension [111]. Attenuated vagal baroreceptor reflexes and enhanced sympathetic vasomotor tone have been reported, which may contribute to loss of heart rate control and blood pressure instability [112]. ME/CFS and the sequelae of COVID-19 show similar patterns of autonomic dysfunction characterized by decreased heart rate variability and baroreceptor dysfunction [113].

Multisystem involvement

Multisystem involvement is characterized by the dysfunction of nervous, immune, cardiovascular, endocrine, and other systems. Neuroimaging studies have suggested that abnormalities are most commonly observed in the frontal cortex, with notable hypofunction in the insular and thalamic regions. Abnormalities in these key network hubs have been proposed to disrupt connectivity to the limbic system and may contribute to the hallmark symptoms of ME/CFS [114]. Abnormally low levels of catecholamines and other neurotransmitters have been reported in the cerebrospinal fluid of CFS patients, which were described as associated with decreased motor performance and worse cognitive symptoms [115]. In terms of immunity, immune checkpoints such as CTLA-4 and PD-1 are up-regulated, and CD8 + T cell dysfunction is correlated with symptom severity [85]. Mitochondrial dysfunction has been frequently described as a key mechanism underlying abnormal energy metabolism in CFS. Phosphor-31 magnetic resonance spectroscopy studies have reported that the mitochondrial metabolic rate and ATP synthesis rate of CFS patients appear to be decreased, which manifested as a rapid depletion and slow regeneration of ATP, the slow oxidative metabolism of fuel substrates, and a surge in skeletal muscle oxidative stress [86]. Gut microbiome studies have reported that CFS patients may have reduced levels of butyrate-producing bacteria, and it has been hypothesized that butyrate deficiency may trigger immune activation and neuroinflammation [116]. Patients with short-term (< 4 years) CFS have more significant dysbiosis of the gut microbiota, while patient with long-term (> 10 years) CFS show some dysbiosis recovery, but more serious metabolic and clinical abnormalities [117].

Case analysis

The following cases were hypothetically constructed based on clinical presentations commonly described in the literature and clinical experiences, in accordance with Walker and Avant’s approach to concept analysis [25].

Typical case

Ms. Zhang, a 35-year-old teacher, developed persistent fatigue after a viral infection two years ago. Her main complaint was that she did not feel well rested after sleeping 8–9 h every night, requiring multiple naps during the day. The fatigue persisted for more than two years, and at present, she can only manage teaching responsibilities for half of the day. The fatigue was significantly aggravated after normal work or light exercise, accompanied by headaches and muscle soreness, which usually appeared 24 h after the activity and lasted for several days. The evaluation found that Ms. Zhang also had significant brain fog, which manifested as memory loss, poor concentration, slow thinking speed, and difficulty finding words; After standing for more than 10 min, her heart rate increased, and dizziness and blurred vision occurred, which were relieved when laying down. There were also several immune symptoms, such as a recurrent sore throat and tender lymph nodes, as well as neuroendocrine manifestations such as chills and abnormal temperature regulation. After excluding thyroid disease, anaemia, and autoimmune diseases, Ms. Zhang was diagnosed with CFS. This situation met all the defining features of CFS and was a typical case.

Borderline case

Mr Wang was a 42-year-old information technology engineer. He went to the hospital due to fatigue and sleep problems caused by long-term overtime work. His chief complaint was fatigue for about 8 months, decreased work efficiency, and poor sleep quality. However, fatigue was significantly improved after a holiday, and fatigue after work could be recovered on the same or next day without obvious PEM. His basic cognitive function was normal and there was no obvious brain fog. There was no orthostatic intolerance or multi-system symptoms. This condition was only partially consistent with the defining characteristics of CFS, but more consistent with job burnout or ICF. This was therefore a borderline case.

Opposite case

Ms. Chen, a 28-year-old, complained of persistent fatigue, lethargy, weight gain, and chills. Laboratory tests showed a significant increase in TSH and a decrease in FT4, and the patient was diagnosed with primary hypothyroidism. The symptoms of fatigue gradually improved after thyroid hormone replacement therapy. This case had a clear cause of fatigue which was relieved by targeted treatment, and did not meet the “cannot be explained by other known diseases ”diagnostic principle of CFS. Hence, this is an opposite case.

Related case

Ms Liu, a 45-year-old, was diagnosed with long COVID because she continued to have multiple symptoms three months after being infected with COVID-19. Her chief complaint was persistent fatigue, discomfort after exertion, tiredness after sleep, and decreased concentration and memory. However, this was also accompanied by persistent cough, chest tightness, and hyposmia, which were specific symptoms of COVID-19. Long COVID represents a related case, as it shares substantial overlap with CFS but is a broader concept encompassing a wider variety of persistent symptoms with multi-organ system involvement.

Antecedents and consequences

Antecedents

Based on the reviewed literature, the occurrence of CFS appears to be the result of multiple factors, involving complex interactions at multiple levels including infection triggers, immune dysregulation, genetic susceptibility, stress events, and environmental factors. Infection triggers were frequently described in the reviewed literature as the most commonly reported antecedent causes of CFS, with about 50–80% of patients reporting that their illness began with an acute infection [1, 18–20, 95]. Epstein-Barr virus, human herpesvirus 6, enterovirus, and SARS-CoV-2 have all been reported as potential triggers, and these infections have been proposed to trigger a sustained immune response potentially leading to chronic inflammation and neuroimmune dysfunction [34, 50]. However, not all infected persons will develop CFS, suggesting that infection may be a necessary but not sufficient condition, acting in conjunction with other predisposing factors.

Abnormalities of the immune system have been frequently reported in the literature and are proposed to play a central role in the pathogenesis of CFS. Patients have been reported to show immunological features such as elevated levels of proinflammatory cytokines, decreased natural killer cell function, and reduced T cell numbers [118]. An in-depth phenotyping study reported evidence of a persistent immune response, which manifested as the upregulation of immune checkpoints such as CTLA-4 and PD-1, suggesting a decrease in effector T cells and their impaired differentiation, proliferation, and function [85, 95, 115]. In addition, familial aggregation suggests the involvement of genetic susceptibility [1, 68]. Severe physiological or psychological stress may induce or aggravate immune dysregulation by activating the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis and sympathetic nervous system [20, 30, 53]. Gut microbiome imbalances, especially reduced levels of butyrate-producing bacteria, have also been suggested as potentially involved in CFS pathogenesis [116, 117].

Consequences

The consequences of CFS include multi-dimensional impacts on the individual, family, society, and health care system. At an individual level, functional disabilities and significant declines in quality of life are the most immediate consequences. About 75% of patients are unable to maintain full-time employment, and 25% are severely ill and bedridden or housebound for extended periods [1, 69, 89, 95]. A total of 97% of CFS patients reported problems performing usual activities, 83% reported impaired mobility, and 64% reported self-care difficulties [13]. This not only affected their career development and economic income, but also profoundly changed their life trajectory. The chronic and uncertainty of this disease brings a significant psychological burden, and is often associated with depression and anxiety. However, the included literature suggests that these psychological symptoms are more likely secondary consequences of the disease, resulting from the lack of social understanding, rather than a primary cause of CFS.

At a social level, restricted activities lead to a significant reduction in social activities, resulting in social isolation [1, 10, 14]. Because of the long-standing lack of clear biomarkers, patients often face questions, misunderstandings, and feelings of stigma from health care professionals, employers, and families [15, 16, 57]. At an economic level, the annual economic loss is estimated at US $17–24 billion in America alone [30, 79]. Furthermore, up to 90% of patients are undiagnosed, and 29% of diagnosed patients waited 5 years or more to receive an accurate diagnosis [64], and there is currently no Food and Drug Administration-approved specific treatment, which places higher requirements on medical resource allocation and staff training [119].

Conceptual framework of CFS

Empirical referents

In Walker and Avant’s concept analysis framework, empirical referents are defined as observable phenomena or measurable indicators that demonstrate the occurrence of a concept in the real world [25]. They provide the operational bridge between abstract defining attributes and clinical observation. It is important to note that the instruments presented in this section function as empirical referents insofar as they operationalize the defining attributes identified in this analysis. Such instruments make these abstract attributes measurable and observable in clinical and research settings. However, their inclusion as empirical referents within this concept analysis does not imply diagnostic equivalence. No single instrument below should be interpreted as a validated stand-alone diagnostic tool for CFS on the basis of this analysis alone.

DePaul symptom questionnaire (DSQ) and PEM subscale

Developed by Professor Leonard Jason’s team at the DePaul University in 2010, the DSQ assesses ME/CFS symptom severity and frequency, providing specific assessment guidance for clinicians and researchers. The latest version includes 79 symptom items, with participants rating each symptom’s frequency and severity over the past 6 months, such as fatigue, exercise-related discomfort, sleep disturbance, pain, neurocognitive, autonomic, neuroendocrine, immune, and genitourinary symptoms. Widely used in screening and symptom monitoring [120, 121], it has good reliability and validity: a test-retest reliability with Pearson or Kappa coefficient ≥ 0.70, an eight-factor structure via exploratory factor analysis with each factor showing good internal consistency [122], and supported content, construct, predictive, and discriminant validity that effectively distinguishes ME/CFS patients from healthy controls and other chronic disease patients [123]. Based on this questionnaire, Cotler et al. developed a PEM subscale to assess CFS’s core symptoms, recommended by National Institute of Neurological Disease and Stroke (NINDS) and CDC. It comprises 5 core items with Cronbach’s α = 0.84 and test-retest Kappa = 0.74–0.88, and 5 supplementary items, correctly identifying PEM in 97% of CFS patients and distinguishing them from multiple sclerosis patients [124]. Jason and Chee further developed a more concise yet comprehensive assessment tool [125].

Bell chronic fatigue immune dysfunction syndrome (CFIDS) disability scale

The Bell CFIDS disability scale developed by New York physician Dr. David Bell for The Physician’s Guide to CFS [126] assesses ME/CFS patients’ overall function on a 0-100 scale (10-point intervals). It is simple to use, and reflects daily activity ability and disease severity. Although it was developed from clinical experience, its simplicity and practicality have led to widespread use, with good content validity. Recent studies validated its clinical utility by combining it with the SF-36 and Chalder fatigue scale for functional status assessments [127]. This scale is included in the ME/CFS Common Data Element Tool library [128].

Chalder fatigue scale (CFS-11)

Developed in 1993 by Professor Trudie Chalder’s group at King’s College London, this is a patient-rated scale to measure the severity of physical and mental fatigue and is now widely used in ME/CFS research [129, 130]. A number of cross-cultural validation studies have confirmed its good psychometric properties [131–133]. The scale contains 11 items and is scored by a 4-point Likert scale. The physical fatigue component includes 7 items: fatigue, need for more rest, sleepiness, difficulty getting started, lack of energy, decreased muscle strength, and weakness. Mental fatigue includes difficulty concentrating, speaking incorrectly, finding words, and memory problems. However, some researchers have pointed out that the scale may have a ceiling effect in CFS populations, which limits its sensitivity to symptom deterioration [134].

Functional capacity scale (FUNCAP)

FUNCAP is a functional assessment tool for CFS developed by Norwegian and British researchers in 2024. The design is based on extensive patient feedback [135]. The questionnaire has excellent psychometric properties. There are two versions of the questionnaire, FUNCAP55 for diagnosis and disability assessment, and FUNCAP27 for clinical follow-up and research. The eight functional domains covered by the questionnaire include personal hygiene/basic functions, walking/mobility, keeping upright, family activities, communication, outside activities, response to light and sound, and concentration.

Non-disease-specific instruments and objective indicators

Beyond the disease-specific instruments described above, the reviewed literature also frequently employed generic and objective referents. The 36-Item Short Form Health Survey (SF-36) was the most commonly used measure of health-related quality of life, applied in approximately one-third of the included studies, and is often combined with fatigue-specific scales to capture the functional impact of CFS. Two-day cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) has been used as an objective referent for post-exertional malaise, while neuroimaging (MRI/fMRI/PET/SPECT/MRS) and candidate biomarkers (pro-inflammatory cytokines, natural-killer-cell cytotoxicity, cortisol) have been explored as physiological correlates of the defining attributes. None of these instruments has yet achieved stand-alone diagnostic status for CFS. In the Walker and Avant framework, they are regarded as empirical referents, meaning observable indicators that operationalize the defining attributes, rather than validated diagnostic tools.

Operational definition of CFS

Based on Walker and Avant’s concept analysis methodology [25] and the defining attributes, antecedents, consequences, and empirical referents synthesized in this study, the following operational definition of CFS for nursing practice is proposed. This definition is derived directly from the five defining attributes identified in this analysis.

CFS is a complex, multisystem, chronic clinical condition that can be operationally identified in nursing practice when all of the following criteria are met: (1) persistent severe fatigue, new in onset or having a definite onset time, lasting at least six months, not substantially relieved by rest, and resulting in a significant reduction from pre-illness levels of occupational, educational, social, or personal functioning; (2) PEM, a disproportionate worsening of symptoms following physical, cognitive, or emotional exertion, typically delayed 24–48 h in onset and prolonged in recovery; (3) non-restorative sleep, feeling unrefreshed despite adequate or even prolonged sleep duration; (4) cognitive impairment (e.g., deficits in attention, processing speed, or working memory) and/or orthostatic intolerance (e.g., dizziness, tachycardia, or syncope upon standing); (5) multisystem involvement, evidenced by concurrent neurological, immunological, neuroendocrine, or autonomic manifestations. Additionally, symptoms cannot be fully explained by other identifiable medical, psychiatric, or substance-related conditions after standard clinical evaluation. This operational definition is intended to support nursing identification, assessment, and research informed by the present concept analysis, and is not proposed as a substitute for established clinical diagnostic criteria.

Discussion

It should be noted at the outset that concept analysis, as a theoretical method, does not test or evaluate the effectiveness of clinical interventions. Rather, by clarifying the defining attributes, antecedents, and consequences of a concept, it infers priorities for assessment and care that can subsequently guide empirical investigation. This concept analysis synthesized the existing literature to clarify the defining attributes of CFS, highlighting its frequently described multisystemic and functional impairment features. The shift of PEM from an optional to a necessary diagnostic criterion reflects a fundamental change in recognizing CFS as a neuroimmune disease with unique pathophysiological mechanisms [136]. This study systematically summarized the characteristics of PEM identified in the reviewed literature, including diverse triggers, complex symptoms, unique temporal features, and disproportion to the triggers. This conceptual clarification has direct relevance for setting priorities in nursing assessment and care planning. Based on these findings, several plausible directions for nursing practice can be inferred. From the perspective of conceptual clarification rather than intervention testing, it is plausible that energy management centred on pacing strategies could serve as a key focus for nursing assessment and patient education in CFS. Supporting evidence from intervention studies suggests that pacing may effectively reduce fatigue and psychological distress and improve physical function [137]. However, evaluating intervention effectiveness was not the primary objective of this concept analysis, and this evidence is provided for contextual background rather than as a finding of the present study. Based on the conceptual understanding of PEM characteristics identified herein, it is plausible that nurses could prioritize assessing patients’ energy boundaries, facilitating task breakdown strategies, and supporting patients to maintain activity and symptom diaries. These actions would assist in identifying energy limits, managing tasks, and detecting triggers of PEM. Although conceptually grounded, these potential nursing priorities require further empirical validation in clinical settings. The energy envelope theory has been proposed as a relevant theoretical framework for understanding energy management in CFS [138]. Consistent with the scope of concept analysis, which identifies conceptual priorities rather than tests interventions, the attributes identified in this analysis suggest that future nursing research could explore the comparative effects of different pacing education approaches and the application of digital health technologies in PEM monitoring and prevention. This conceptual evolution indicates that nursing assessments may benefit from prioritizing the monitoring of PEM over a sole focus on fatigue.

Conceptual heterogeneity from multiple diagnostic criteria remains an ongoing challenge in CFS research. This discrepancy directly impacts clinical decision-making, with different criteria potentially diagnosing or excluding the same patient. The conceptual heterogeneity identified in this analysis suggests that, from a nursing perspective, flexible assessment approaches may be warranted-comprehensively evaluating the spectra of patient symptoms and functional status, and using diagnostic criteria as a reference rather than a rigid checklist. Detailed nursing records of specific symptoms and functional levels may facilitate inter-agency information exchange and longitudinal follow-up. Future nursing research should explore more clinically applicable assessment tools. This study also highlighted multisystemic symptom characteristics as a core attribute of CFS, underscoring the need for multidisciplinary, collaborative care for CFS patients. However, fragmented healthcare systems often fail to meet these comprehensive needs. The multisystem involvement identified as a core attribute of CFS in this analysis supports the hypothesis that nurses, with their comprehensive assessment and interprofessional communication skills, could serve as effective care coordinators [23]. This proposed role encompasses the management of specialty consultations/referrals, the integration of health information, care continuity, and patient advocacy. Drawing on international models such as the UK NICE guidelines [139], the conceptual framework developed in this study provides a theoretical basis that could inform the gradual establishment of CFS-specific service networks in China, including the training of specialist nurses and the development of community and home care services. However, translating these conceptual insights into policy recommendations would require further evidence from implementation studies. Nursing professionals could potentially contribute to policy-making and social advocacy to reduce disease stigma.

Long COVID onset has resulted in a current focus on chronic illnesses in the medical field, thereby promoting researchers to explore the biomedical mechanisms of CFS [140]. The two diseases share pathological mechanisms such as immune disorders and mitochondrial dysfunction, and thus may generate comparative research findings [19, 81, 141]. This brings new opportunities and challenges to nursing practice: more solid biomedical evidence helps nurses confirm the authenticity of patient symptoms and promote the establishment of therapeutic nurse-patient relationships; Many patients feel marginalized by the health care system [16]. In these instances, nurses can be critical in rebuilding patient trust. The high overlap between the two conditions implies that the nursing experience has transferable value across diseases. Future nursing research should systematically compare the similarities and differences in the care needs of patients with long COVID and CFS, and develop evidence-based nursing interventions suitable for this disease spectrum.

At present, there is no comprehensive assessment tool that fully meets all the defining characteristics of CFS, nor is there a localized scale that has been validated in the Chinese population. The defining attributes and empirical referents identified in this concept analysis highlight this gap: the absence of comprehensive assessment tools that capture the multisystem nature of CFS as conceptualized in this framework. With the development of digital health technologies, real-time monitoring, and ecological transient assessment methods may overcome these limitations. Future nursing research should focus on developing nurse-specific tools that integrate multi-system dysfunction assessments, verifying the applicability of existing international scales to the Chinese population, exploring symptom monitoring programs assisted by wearable devices, and establishing a sensitive indicator system to evaluate nursing practice.

Limitations

This concept analysis has several inherent limitations. To begin with, included studies exhibited substantial conceptual and clinical heterogeneity due to divergent diagnostic criteria, which vary in symptom requirements and thresholds, leading to heterogeneous patient cohorts that may undermine the stability and generalizability of identified concept attributes. The lack of a universally accepted CFS diagnostic gold standard remains a fundamental challenge, underscoring the imperative for an internationally unified diagnostic framework. A second limitation relates to the diverse evidence sources synthesized, including original research, systematic reviews, concept analysis papers, consensus documents, and clinical guidelines. These sources differ significantly in methodological rigor and evidence hierarchy, potentially introducing variability in the strength and consistency of evidence supporting the proposed framework. To partially mitigate this concern, all 68 included sources were classified into three evidence tiers to inform the interpretive weight given during synthesis; nevertheless, future studies should further refine this weighting scheme when empirically validating the proposed attributes. Additionally, while the literature search covered eight major Chinese and English databases, language restriction may have introduced bias. Relevant studies in other languages and non-indexed sources were not systematically retrieved, potentially limiting the cultural and clinical diversity of integrated perspectives. Finally, this study relied exclusively on existing literature without the direct involvement of clinical experts or patients, which limits the extent to which the proposed framework reflects current clinical realities and lived patient experience. To consolidate the translational pathway of this work, we recommend two complementary next steps: (1) a multi-round Delphi consensus study with an international, multidisciplinary panel of CFS specialists, nurses, and methodological experts to appraise and refine the five defining attributes and the operational definition; and (2) a patient-participatory research design (e.g., PPI panels or co-design workshops) to ensure the framework is grounded in lived experience. Together, these approaches would empirically validate the operational definition in real-world nursing practice.

Conclusions

This study applied Walker and Avant’s concept analysis to propose a nursing-oriented conceptual framework for CFS by identifying five defining attributes: persistent severe fatigue, PEM, non-restorative sleep, cognitive impairment and/or orthostatic intolerance, and multisystem involvement, as well as their antecedents, consequences, and empirical referents. Based on this conceptual clarification, the framework may inform nursing practice by supporting PEM-focused assessment and energy management strategies, contribute to CFS-specific curriculum development in nursing education, and highlight directions for future research, including the validation of assessment instruments and the evaluation of pacing interventions. Given the pathophysiological overlap between CFS and post-infectious fatigue conditions such as long COVID reported in the literature, the framework may also offer a conceptual basis for cross-condition comparison, though this potential application requires further empirical testing. It should be noted that these implications are derived from conceptual analysis rather than empirical intervention studies, and require validation across diverse populations and clinical contexts.
