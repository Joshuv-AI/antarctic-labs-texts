Police as de facto first responders in trauma systems in low- and middle-income countries: Training, governance, and the impact of insecurity
Authors: Vukosi Baloyi, Emmanuel M Ndaba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryTrauma remains a leading cause of death globally, with the greatest burden borne by low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where prehospital systems are often underdeveloped, fragmented, or constrained by insecurity. In these settings, injured patients frequently reach care through informal pathways, and police officers commonly function as de facto first responders—providing initial assistance, coordinating evacuation, and, in some cases, directly transporting patients to hospitals.Despite their central role, police involvement in trauma care is rarely formalized or integrated into trauma system frameworks. Evidence from LMICs shows that police and other lay responders often lack formal training in trauma first aid, yet targeted, low-cost training programs can significantly improve knowledge, confidence, and willingness to provide care. In parallel, increasing violence and insecurity—such as in the Western Cape, South Africa—have further embedded police within prehospital care pathways.This article argues that the key limitation is not the absence of responders, but the absence of systems that define, train, equip, and govern their roles. We propose a pragmatic, scalable framework for integrating police into LMIC trauma systems, emphasizing defined roles, competency-based training, essential equipment, governance, and data integration to improve early trauma care and system resilience.Keywords: Accidental Injuries; Accidents, Traffic; Health Care Quality, Access, And Evaluation; policy

Introduction

Trauma remains a leading cause of death and disability globally, accounting for millions of deaths annually, with over 90% occurring in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).1,3 Despite this disproportionate burden, trauma system development in LMICs has lagged behind that of high-income countries (HICs), particularly in the prehospital phase of care.1 2 4 5 Mature trauma systems typically assume the availability of rapid ambulance response, trained paramedics, reliable dispatch systems, and safe scene access.4 6 In many LMIC settings, however, these assumptions do not reflect operational reality.4 5 7

Ambulance services in LMICs are often under-resourced, unevenly distributed, and subject to prolonged response times, particularly in densely populated urban areas and informal settlements.5 7 As a result, injured patients frequently reach healthcare facilities via non-ambulance pathways, including private vehicles, taxis, community members, or police transport.3,58 9 These alternative pathways are not aberrations but adaptive responses to structural limitations within health systems.4 9 Consequently, the functional trauma system—the system that actually delivers patients to care—differs substantially from formal system designs and policy descriptions.1 2 4

Police officers are uniquely positioned within this functional trauma system because they are commonly dispatched to road traffic collisions and scenes of interpersonal violence, arrive early, possess authority to secure environments, and have access to vehicles capable of rapid transport.410,12 In many LMIC contexts, police therefore become the first official point of contact for injured patients and may provide initial assistance, coordinate care, or directly transport patients to hospital.410,12 Yet, despite their centrality, police roles in trauma care are rarely formalized, trained, or governed within national or regional trauma system frameworks, and often occur in the context of limited first aid knowledge and training.10

This disconnect between practice and policy creates both risk and opportunity. Untrained or unsupported police involvement may lead to inconsistent care, unsafe patient handling, delayed hemorrhage control, or medico-legal uncertainty. Conversely, failure to acknowledge police as trauma system actors represents a missed opportunity to strengthen early care using an already-present workforce, particularly given evidence that targeted first-responder training can significantly improve trauma care knowledge and capability in resource-constrained settings.11 12 The WHO has long emphasized that, in settings without mature emergency medical services (EMSs), improving outcomes depends on training and equipping those who are already first on scene, including police and other lay responders.6

In parallel, increasing insecurity in many LMIC urban environments has further reshaped trauma system function. Violence against EMS personnel has emerged as a critical threat, forcing ambulances to delay entry, stage responses, or require police escort, thereby embedding law enforcement more deeply into prehospital care pathways.13,15 The Western Cape, South Africa, exemplifies this intersection of trauma burden, violence, and system adaptation, where “red zone” policies and police-escorted responses have become operational necessities.

This article provides a perspective on police as an integral component of LMIC trauma systems—not through deliberate task shifting, but as a pragmatic adaptation to constrained EMS capacity, high injury burden, and, in some settings, profound insecurity.1,47 13 Accordingly, the critical question for trauma system planners is not whether police should be involved, but how their existing role can be made safer, more effective, and accountable.

Training and capability of police in LMICs

Evidence from LMICs suggests that police officers frequently encounter injured patients but often have limited formal training in trauma first aid. Commonly reported knowledge gaps include hemorrhage control, airway positioning, spinal precautions, and recognition of shock, whereas fear of causing harm and uncertainty regarding medico-legal responsibility further limit effective intervention.10

Interventional studies from LMICs, including work from Tanzania and other LMIC settings, demonstrate that these limitations are modifiable. Short, structured first-responder training programs for police have been shown to significantly improve knowledge, confidence, and reported willingness to provide care.11 12 These programs typically focus on post-crash first aid, bleeding control, safe patient handling, and rapid transfer, and are designed to be feasible, scalable, and comparatively low cost in resource-constrained settings.11 12

Despite these gains, sustainability challenges persist. High personnel turnover, lack of refresher training, absence of standardized equipment, and weak integration into national or provincial trauma frameworks undermine long-term impact. Without formal system endorsement and ongoing governance, improvements in police first-response capability remain fragile and unevenly sustained.10 11

Police involvement: risk, necessity, or opportunity?

Concerns regarding police participation in medical care are legitimate. Police are not healthcare providers, and untrained intervention carries the risk of inappropriate handling, delayed definitive care, or harm. However, available evidence suggests that in LMICs, police involvement is already occurring at scale, often in the absence of alternatives.10 11 Ignoring this reality may perpetuate preventable morbidity and mortality, particularly from time-critical conditions such as uncontrolled hemorrhage.

Importantly, available evidence indicates that police can acquire and retain basic trauma response skills when training is appropriately designed and contextually relevant.11 12 Short, focused training programs emphasizing hemorrhage control, safe patient positioning, and rapid transfer have demonstrated improvements in knowledge, confidence, and willingness to act. These findings align with broader evidence supporting the effectiveness of training lay responders in LMIC trauma systems.12 16 17

Structure over cadre: lessons from high-income trauma systems and implications for LMIC settings

Comparisons with HIC police models illustrate that the primary differentiator is not the cadre itself, but the surrounding system. In HICs, police hemorrhage-control initiatives and first-response models are embedded within governance frameworks that define scope of practice, mandate training, provide equipment, and integrate responders into dispatch and handover pathways.18 19 These structural elements, rather than professional background alone, enable safe and effective police participation.

Importantly, evidence from HIC trauma systems demonstrates that police involvement in prehospital care—particularly as a mode of rapid transport for penetrating trauma—can improve outcomes when embedded within organized systems. Systematic review evidence and guideline recommendations from the Eastern Association for the Surgery of Trauma indicate that police transport, in select urban settings with short transport times, is associated with comparable or improved survival outcomes relative to traditional EMS transport, particularly for penetrating injuries requiring rapid access to definitive care.20 21 These findings reinforce that non-traditional responders can play a meaningful role in improving time-to-definitive care when integrated into coordinated trauma systems.

LMIC settings lack many of the structural supports required for coordinated prehospital care, resulting in variability and fragility in early trauma response. In these contexts, formal EMSs are frequently absent or underdeveloped, and police officers often function as de facto first responders, providing initial assistance, coordinating evacuation, and directly transporting injured patients to hospital.10 However, this role is typically undertaken in the absence of adequate training, equipment, or system-level support. Qualitative evidence demonstrates that such responders frequently operate with limited knowledge of basic trauma care principles, perform potentially harmful or suboptimal interventions, and work within resource-constrained and high-risk environments, all of which contribute to poor patient outcomes.10

At the same time, emerging evidence suggests that this gap is not insurmountable. In LMIC settings where lay responders—including police and community members—constitute the primary prehospital workforce, targeted, context-specific training interventions have been shown to significantly improve trauma care knowledge and capability. A short, structured training program for first responders in Guatemala resulted in substantial improvements in core emergency care competencies across domains such as hemorrhage control, airway management, and transport, despite its brief duration and low-resource design.22 This supports the concept that non-medical responders can be rapidly upskilled to deliver effective early trauma care when appropriately trained.

Together, these findings highlight a critical distinction: the limitation in LMIC prehospital care is not the absence of responders, but the absence of systems that define, train, equip, and integrate them. The foundational principles underpinning high-income trauma systems—clear role definition, competency-based training, equipment provision, coordinated transport pathways, and robust data capture—remain transferable and adaptable to resource-constrained environments, as reflected in global trauma system frameworks.18 21 23 24 In this context, the challenge is not whether police and other lay responders should be involved in early trauma care, but how to structure that involvement to maximize benefit and minimizing harm.

The Western Cape experience, South Africa

Insecurity as a trauma-system determinant

In the Western Cape, particularly in high-risk areas such as Philippi and Hanover Park, violence against EMS personnel has necessitated fundamental adaptations in prehospital care delivery. Attacks on EMS crews escalated significantly from around 2015, prompting formal recognition at a provincial level and the introduction of “red zone” policies, whereby high-risk areas were designated and access to scenes became contingent on prior security clearance and police escort.13 25 Recurrent threats—including hijackings, robberies, stoning of ambulances, and exposure to crossfire—have since shaped EMS operational protocols, shifting response models away from conventional dispatch toward security-driven systems.13

In parallel, system-level adaptations have emerged beyond traditional EMS structures. The Philippi Project represents a community-based ambulance model developed in response to both violence and broader system strain, illustrating how frontline providers adapted care delivery within constrained and insecure environments.25 These developments highlight that EMS evolution in the Western Cape has been driven not only by clinical need, but by social and security pressures.

Operationally, EMS responses in red zones are contingent on prior scene security. Ambulances are required to stage at predetermined safe locations—typically police stations—and await law enforcement escort before entering the scene.14 This model reflects a shift from a purely patient-centered response paradigm to a security-contingent system, in which access to care is mediated by real-time threat assessment and interagency coordination.

System adaptation and impact on care delivery

Although these adaptations are necessary to ensure provider safety, they have measurable consequences for trauma care delivery. Quantitative evidence from the Western Cape demonstrates that red zone protocols are associated with substantial delays, with time to definitive care increasing by up to 46% and total call-to-facility time increasing by more than 40 min compared with non-red zone settings.14 Importantly, standard EMS performance metrics are also affected, as “arrival on scene” is frequently recorded at the police station rather than the patient location, potentially underestimating true response delays.14

These delays are compounded by operational constraints, including limited police availability for escort, prolonged coordination at dispatch level, and reduced on-scene time due to safety concerns. Furthermore, system-wide effects have been observed, with increasing response and mission times across the broader EMS network, suggesting that red zone operations may contribute to strain beyond the affected areas.14 More broadly, these findings reinforce that violence is not simply an operational challenge but a key determinant of trauma system performance in South Africa.26

Evidence from other LMIC settings reinforces that these patterns are not unique to South Africa. In Ghana, emergency medical technicians frequently experience both physical and non-physical violence during high-stress emergency scenarios, including road traffic accidents, political gatherings, and domestic emergencies, with patients’ relatives and bystanders identified as the primary perpetrators.15 These findings demonstrate that violence is not incidental but structurally linked to the nature of emergency response environments, contributing to delays, operational disruption, and compromised care delivery.

Safety interventions, limitations, and implications for system design

Current safety strategies in the Western Cape include staged response, police escort, minimization of on-scene time, and rapid patient extraction. However, qualitative evidence suggests that these measures do not fully mitigate risk, with EMS personnel continuing to report significant exposure to violence and persistent fear whereas on duty.13 In addition, frontline providers report inadequate preparation for operating in hostile environments and limited effectiveness of existing psychological support structures.13

These findings highlight important limitations of current approaches. First, reliance on police escort introduces delays and is constrained by competing demands on law enforcement resources.14 Second, the prioritization of rapid extraction over comprehensive on-scene care may affect the quality of early trauma management. Third, the absence of structured integration between EMS and law enforcement limits system efficiency and consistency. Framing violence within a broader trauma systems perspective underscores the need to address insecurity as a core system variable rather than an external constraint.26

Importantly, evidence from LMIC settings further supports the need for formalized security integration within prehospital systems. In Ghana, recommendations include the incorporation of structured safety measures such as requiring police presence prior to dispatch in potentially violent environments, alongside strengthened training, legal protections, and interagency coordination.15 These recommendations align closely with the operational realities observed in the Western Cape, reinforcing that police involvement is not incidental but a necessary component of safe emergency care delivery in high-risk settings.

Addressing these challenges requires a broader systems-based response. Recommended strategies include enhanced EMS–police coordination, targeted hostile-environment training for responders, provision of appropriate protective equipment, strengthening of workforce capacity, and community-level interventions aimed at reducing violence against emergency personnel.13 25

Collectively, the Western Cape experience demonstrates that in insecure environments, trauma systems are fundamentally security-dependent systems, where timely access to care is contingent not only on medical capability but on the ability to safely reach the patient. In this context, police are not merely adjunct responders but essential enablers of care, reinforcing the need to formally integrate their role within trauma system design.25 26

Toward pragmatic integration rather than idealized systems

Formalizing police roles within trauma systems does not imply the medicalization of policing. Rather, it acknowledges existing practice and seeks to reduce harm and maximize benefit. A pragmatic, scalable approach should prioritize a core set of high-impact competencies—such as hemorrhage control, basic airway positioning, and rapid transport—supported by clearly defined scopes of practice, essential equipment, and governance frameworks that ensure accountability and protect both patients and police officers.6 17 24 A conceptual framework for the pragmatic integration of police into LMIC trauma systems is shown in figure 1.

To enable scalability, these interventions must be standardized, low-cost, and adaptable across diverse LMIC contexts. Short, competency-based training programs, simple equipment bundles, and integration into existing dispatch and referral pathways represent feasible entry points for system strengthening without requiring fully developed EMSs infrastructure.12 17

Crucially, integration must be accompanied by robust data systems. Inclusion of police-transport and police-first-aid variables in trauma registries would allow objective assessment of impact, guide quality improvement, and inform evidence-based policy decisions.6 7 In parallel, hospitals in LMIC settings should prioritize routine collection and reporting of data on mode of transport to emergency care—including police, private, and informal pathways—and evaluate how these influence time-to-definitive care and patient outcomes. Without such data, debates regarding police involvement risk remaining speculative and ideologically driven.

Research and policy implications

This analysis identifies several priorities for future research and policy. First, the burden and characteristics of police-transported trauma must be quantified. Second, outcomes associated with police-delivered first response should be rigorously evaluated, including mortality, time-to-definitive care, and complication rates. Third, the cost-effectiveness and scalability of training and equipping police as first responders require formal assessment.5 9 12 17

In insecure environments, research should additionally examine how security-contingent models—such as escort-based EMSs responses—affect access to care, system efficiency, and patient outcomes.13,15 These settings provide important natural experiments for understanding how trauma systems function under conditions of constraint and risk.

From a policy perspective, LMIC trauma systems must move beyond idealized, ambulance-centric models that may be unattainable in high-violence or resource-limited contexts. Recognizing police as legitimate, although limited, trauma system actors represents a necessary evolution toward resilient, context-appropriate systems.6 24

Conclusion

Police already occupy a central position within many LMIC trauma systems, particularly where EMSs capacity is limited or security risks are high. The Western Cape exemplifies how insecurity accelerates this integration, embedding law enforcement within prehospital care pathways.

Rather than viewing police involvement as an ad hoc workaround, trauma system planners should recognize it as a structural reality. Formalizing this role through targeted training, essential equipment, defined governance structures, and integrated data systems offers a pragmatic and scalable pathway to improving early trauma care.

Such an approach does not replace the long-term goal of developing comprehensive EMSs systems but provides an immediately actionable strategy to reduce preventable mortality and strengthen system resilience in the interim.
