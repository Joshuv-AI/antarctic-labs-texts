Spatiotemporally reprogrammed L-arginine metabolic nanoregulator potentiates immunotherapy
Authors: Qian Cheng, Xiaolei Shi, Qushuhua Qin, Yuzhe Chen, Jiawei Wang, Lin Wang, Zheng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
L-arginine (L-Arg) deprivation in the tumor microenvironment (TME) drives effector T cell dysfunction and immunotherapy resistance. However, simply supplementing L-Arg can be counterproductive, as tumor cells and immunosuppressive myeloid cells act as dominant consumers, co-opting the nutrient to promote tumor progression. To break this detrimental cycle without fueling protumoral networks, we develop a near-infrared (NIR)-triggered nanoregulator (hPFL@Lipo) to simultaneously alleviate intratumoral L-Arg deficiency and redirect its metabolism to support antitumor immunity. This nanoregulator was constructed through coordination-driven self-assembly to co-load and stabilize L-Arg and Fe3+ within hollow Prussian blue (hPB) nanoparticles, followed by lipid membrane encapsulation for enhanced systemic stability. Under NIR irradiation, hPFL@Lipo releases Fe3+ and L-Arg while generating localized hyperthermia. Fe3+ repolarizes M2-like macrophages toward an M1 phenotype, thereby increasing the intratumoral M1-to-M2 ratio. The photothermal effect induces immunogenic tumor cell death, which promotes the infiltration of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells. Concurrently, the released L-Arg supplements the local pool, while thermal ablation reduces the overall cellular burden within the tumor, thereby alleviating arginine local depletion. Together, this strategy resolves the tumor–immune conflict over L-Arg by remodeling the intratumoral landscape of L-Arg consumers in favor of antitumor effector cells, thereby reprogramming the net metabolism of the tumor from a tumor-promoting to a tumor-suppressing state and achieving potent synergy with αPD-1 therapy.

Introduction

Cancer immunotherapy, which harnesses the immune system to recognize and eliminate tumor cells, has been widely employed for diverse malignant tumors [1,2]. In particular, immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy involves monoclonal antibodies that block PD-1/PD-L1 interactions, reactivating T cell-mediated antitumor immunity and achieving revolutionary advances in oncology [3,4]. However, ICB monotherapy exhibits limited clinical response rates across multiple cancer types, particularly in solid tumors [3,5]. A key contributing factor is the immunosuppressive nutrient deprivation within the tumor microenvironment (TME) [[6], [7], [8]], which critically impairs effector functions of cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) and undermines the metabolic fitness required for sustained antitumor immunity, thereby establishing a feedforward loop of immune dysfunction. The competitive consumption and metabolism of nutrients between tumor cells and immune cells within TME constitute a pivotal determinant of immune regulation, dictating the magnitude and polarization of immune responses [7,[9], [10], [11], [12]].

As a pivotal immunometabolic hub, L-arginine (L-Arg) metabolism orchestrates the functional fates of immune cells and profoundly influences tumor progression, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance [8,13]. Specifically, L-Arg availability is indispensable for T cell receptor zeta (TCRζ, CD247) chain expression, clonal expansion, and cytokine production in T lymphocytes [8,14,15]. Furthermore, L-Arg serves as an essential substrate for M1 macrophages, fueling the production of nitric oxide (NO), which mediates both direct tumor killing and enhanced T cell responses [14]. However, TME imposes dual barriers to L-Arg availability for effector immune cells: compromised vascular perfusion restricts its supply [13,16], while malignant and immunosuppressive cells competitively deplete the limited pool [17,18]. Consequently, antitumor immune cells within the TME persist in a state of L-Arg deprivation, leading to attenuated effector functions and a general dysfunctional state [17,18]. Alleviating L-Arg deprivation within TME is critical to rescuing immunotherapeutic efficacy [16,[19], [20], [21]]. However, strategies to supplement tumoral L-Arg face delivery challenges. The high hydrophilicity of free L-Arg causes poor encapsulation and premature leakage from nanocarriers [19,22], while the use of engineered bacteria for in situ biosynthesis presents practical and regulatory hurdles [[23], [24], [25]]. Therefore, it is imperative to develop advanced delivery platforms that enable spatiotemporally controlled L-Arg delivery while ensuring a high safety profile.

More critically, L-Arg exhibits a dual regulatory role in tumor progression within TME. Before employing L-Arg as an adjunctive therapeutic agent, its metabolic niche must be critically evaluated [17,26]. The intercellular metabolic competition for L-Arg among tumor cells, immunosuppressive cells, and effector immune cells dictates its impact on tumor growth [17,27]. Specifically, when effector immune cells are competitively outcompeted, exogenous L-Arg supplementation may fuel tumor progression. Typically, in many solid tumors, particularly metastatic lesions, high infiltration of M2 macrophages overexpressing arginase 1 (ARG1) redirects L-Arg catabolism toward polyamine synthesis, enhancing their immunosuppressive phenotype [14,28]. Moreover, metabolically reprogrammed tumor cells consume a large amount of L-Arg to accelerate proliferation. However, the limited intratumoral infiltration and functional exhaustion of T cells impose a metabolic constraint, rendering them less competitive in L-Arg acquisition [29,30]. Therefore, effective use of L-Arg as an adjunctive therapy requires strategies to not only replenish L-Arg but also reprogram the cellular composition of the TME to establish an immune-favorable L-Arg metabolic niche.

To overcome this dual challenge, we engineer a photoactivatable nanoregulator (hPFL@Lipo) (Scheme 1). First, the hollow Prussian blue (hPB) serves as a photothermal agent, exerting photothermal treatment (PTT) to rapidly ablate a portion of the tumor tissue upon near-infrared (NIR) irradiation. This tumor debulking effect directly reduces the competitive pressure for L-Arg uptake by cells within the TME. Concurrently, PTT-induced immunogenic cell death (ICD) releases tumor antigens and danger signals, which recruit dendritic cells (DCs) and promote their maturation, thereby driving the infiltration and activation of CD8+ T cells. Second, Fe3+ ions co-loaded into hPB with L-Arg via coordination-driven self-assembly are released upon NIR irradiation to repolarize M2 macrophages toward an M1 phenotype. This redirection shifts L-Arg metabolic flux toward immunostimulatory nitric oxide (NO) production. Notably, the reduction in tumor mass following photothermal ablation, together with the decrease in immunosuppressive cells, creates a metabolic window in which the subsequent NIR-triggered L-Arg release can more effectively fuel antitumor immune cells. Finally, the lipid bilayer encapsulation prevents premature leakage of hydrophilic L-Arg during systemic circulation, enabling spatiotemporally controlled release at the tumor site. Thus, our strategy not only delivers L-Arg in a targeted manner but also actively reshapes the cellular composition and functional state of the TME. Specifically, it increases M1 macrophages and tumor-specific effector T cells while decreasing tumor mass and immunosuppressive M2 macrophages, thereby fundamentally reprogramming the net metabolic balance of L-Arg in favor of antitumor immunity. Results and discussion

Impact of L-Arg Supplementation on anti-PD-1 immunotherapy

Evidence indicates that L-Arg depletion in TME is one of the key factors contributing to tumor immune evasion [17,18]. Therefore, we hypothesized that L-Arg supplementation would enhance the efficacy of ICB in colorectal cancer. To test this hypothesis, we initially established a subcutaneous CT26-tumor-bearing mouse model and treated the mice with an anti-PD-1 (αPD-1) antibody alone or in combination with L-Arg (Fig. 1a). Unexpectedly, L-Arg failed to augment the antitumor effects of αPD-1, as evidenced by comparable tumor volumes (Fig. 1b–S1a). Flow cytometric analysis of tumor-infiltrating T cells demonstrated no significant increase in the percentages of CD4+ or CD8+ T cells following L-Arg administration (Fig. 1c–e). Likewise, L-Arg supplementation did not appreciably alter intratumoral TNF-α and IFN-γ levels (Fig. 1f and g), indicating that these key cytokine-mediated effector functions of CD8+ T cells were not potentiated. Additionally, the frequencies of M1-and M2-like macrophages (Fig. 1h–S1b-f) and the intratumoral concentration of IL-10 also showed no significant change (Fig. S1g). Taken together, in the CT26 tumor model studied here, supplementation with free L-Arg did not significantly enhance the antitumor efficacy of αPD-1 therapy. We propose that this outcome results from a dual competitive pressure within the TME: the low expression of Argininosuccinate Synthase 1 (ASS1) in CT26 tumor cells heightens their dependence on extracellular L-Arg (Fig. S2), while concomitant L-Arg consumption by immunosuppressive cell populations further intensifies metabolic competition [31,32]. To examine whether this phenomenon is relevant in human colorectal cancer, we performed a bioinformatics analysis. Using The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database, we compared the expression profiles of genes associated with L-Arg metabolism between colorectal cancer tissues and normal tissues. Our analysis revealed an upregulation of gene expression encoding proteins involved in L-Arg transport (SLC7A1 and SLC7A6; Fig. 1i and S3a), binding (RBM39; Fig. 1j), and catabolism (ARG2; Fig. S3b) within colorectal cancer, when compared to normal tissues. Subsequently, we utilized the UALCAN website to conduct expression analysis of proteins associated with L-Arg metabolism using the CPTAC (Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium) module [33,34]. The results showed elevated total protein expression levels of SLC7A1 and RBM39 in colorectal cancer tissues compared to normal tissues (Fig. 1k and l). Moreover, the abundance of phosphorylated RBM39 isoform NP_001310351.1 at residues Ser97 and Ser136 was significantly higher in tumor tissues compared to normal tissues (Fig. S3c–d). These data indicate that colorectal cancer progression is accompanied by L-Arg metabolic reprogramming. Importantly, this metabolic reprogramming correlates with impaired antitumor immunity. Analysis of the TCGA colon adenocarcinoma (COAD) cohort revealed that expression of “RBM39/SLC7A6” was negatively correlated with CD8+ T cell infiltration (Fig. S4a–b), while the expression of “RBM39/SLC7A1/SLC7A6” was inversely associated with central memory CD8+ T cell infiltration (Fig. S4c–d). These correlations suggest that enhanced tumor cell-intrinsic L-Arg metabolism may contribute to an immunosuppressive TME by limiting L-Arg availability for T cells. Furthermore, we found that colorectal cancer tissues harbor abundant immunosuppressive cells, particularly ARG1-high M2-type macrophages, further exacerbating the detrimental competition for L-Arg (Fig. 1m). Together, these data reveal a dual competitive pressure in the colorectal TME: tumor cells and immunosuppressive cells collectively establish an L-Arg-depleted microenvironment. Based on these analyses of clinical datasets, we propose that the limited efficacy of free L-Arg supplementation in our CT26 model may reflect this broader clinical pattern. Therefore, we speculate that inhibiting the “detrimental” consumption of L-Arg within the TME may be a prerequisite for unlocking the therapeutic potential of L-Arg in tumor immunotherapy.

To investigate whether partially relieving the immunosuppressive pathway of L-Arg consumption could unlock the therapeutic potential of L-Arg, CT26 tumor-bearing BALB/c mice were treated with arginase inhibition (2(S)-Amino-6-boronohexanoic acid, ABH) with or without L-Arg supplementation (Fig. 1n). Arginase converts L-Arg to L-ornithine and urea and is pivotal in the competition for L-Arg by M2 macrophages. Compared with the PBS control, ABH monotherapy significantly increased the intratumoral M1/M2 macrophage ratio (Fig. 1p–r, S5c-d), with limited effect on CD8+ T-cell infiltration (Fig. 1s–S5e). This suggests that pharmacological inhibition of arginase can shift macrophage polarization away from an immunosuppressive M2 phenotype, potentially initiating the rebalancing of L-Arg metabolism toward a more immune-permissive state. Critically, this metabolic rebalancing enabled a synergistic therapeutic response. Whereas ABH alone produced only modest tumor growth inhibition relative to PBS (≈48.2%), the combination of ABH + L-Arg resulted in substantially stronger suppression (≈89.2%; Fig. 1o–S5a-b). Notably, compared with ABH monotherapy, the ABH + L-Arg combination increased intratumoral CD8+ T-cell infiltration by approximately 2.5-fold (Fig. 1s–S5e). Collectively, these results indicate that partial inhibition of immunosuppressive L-Arg catabolism unlocks the therapeutic potential of L-Arg.

However, translating this strategy via systemic arginase inhibition is challenging due to the risk of impairing hepatic ureagenesis, a critical pathway for ammonia detoxification [35]. To overcome this limitation, we turned to iron-based agents as a targeted alternative. Fe3+ was selected for its dual role: as a modulator of macrophage metabolism, capable of repolarizing them toward an M1 phenotype and downregulating arginase expression [[36], [37], [38]]; and for its excellent coordination chemistry [39,40], which promotes stable co-loading with L-Arg into nanoparticles for tumor-selective delivery. We found that combining intratumoral FeCl3 with L-Arg synergistically suppressed tumor growth (Fig. S6a–b). By the end of treatment, tumors in the combination group were 54.7% smaller than those receiving FeCl3 alone (Fig. S6b–c). This enhanced efficacy was underpinned by a remodeled immune microenvironment (Fig. S7a–f). Critically, the therapeutic synergy was abolished in macrophage-depleted mice (Fig. S8a–b), confirming that Fe3+ acts primarily by modulating macrophages to unlock the therapeutic potential of L-Arg.

Research has reported that photothermal therapy (PTT) induces immunogenic cell death (ICD) in tumor cells, ultimately promoting the expansion and intratumor infiltration of CD8+ T cells [[41], [42], [43], [44]]. This effect is driven by the release of tumor antigens and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), which promote DC maturation and the resultant upregulation of cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL)-recruiting chemokines [45]. Although PTT may transiently deplete some resident immune cells, the ablated tumor compartment is rapidly repopulated by newly recruited and activated immune populations, ultimately resulting in a net increase in tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells. Leveraging this immunomodulatory capacity, we examined whether PTT could potentiate L-Arg-driven antitumor immunity (Fig. 1t). Initially, PTT alone or in combination with L-Arg induced near-complete tumor ablation by day 4 post-irradiation. However, during the subsequent monitoring window, L-Arg supplementation substantially suppressed tumor regrowth (Fig. 1u), with final tumor volumes in the PTT + L-Arg group being 73.1% smaller than those in the PTT-only group (Fig. S9a–b). Flow cytometric analysis revealed that, compared to PTT alone, the combination of PTT with L-Arg resulted in a 2.14-fold increase in intratumoral CD4+ T cells, a 2.83-fold increase in CD8+ T cells, and a 33.7% increase in M1-like macrophages, while concurrently reducing M2-like macrophages by 26.6%. (Fig. 1v–y, S9c-f). Mechanistically, PTT both alleviates competitive L-Arg pressure through tumor debulking and, via ICD, recruits CD8+ T cells that preferentially utilize supplemented L-Arg, amplifying antitumor immunity beyond thermal ablation alone.

Guided by these results, we present a tumor-targeted nanosystem (hPFL@Lipo) that integrates photothermal nanocarriers (hollow Prussian Blue, hPB) with L-Arg and macrophage reprogramming agent Fe3+ for co-delivery. The platform is designed to replenish intratumoral L-Arg while redirecting its metabolism toward an immunostimulatory axis, thereby potentiating colorectal-cancer immunotherapy.

Construction and Characterization of hPFL@Lipo

The synthetic procedure of hPFL@Lipo was schematically illustrated in Fig. 2a. We employed hPB as photothermal carriers, owing to their superior biocompatibility (FDA-approved status), high surface-to-volume ratio, and exceptional Fe3+ affinity [46,47]. First, hPB was synthesized through hydrochloric acid etching of pristine Prussian Blue nanoparticles (PB; Fig. S10a–b) [48]. The resulting hPB exhibited a well-defined cubic morphology with distinct hollow structures (shell thickness: ∼28.2 nm) and a uniform particle size distribution (247.2 ± 10.7 nm), as confirmed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Fig. 2b–S10c). The hPFL nanoparticles were subsequently synthesized by depositing Fe3+-L-Arg complexes onto hPB. This process was driven by the coordination affinity between Fe3+, L-Arg, and the hPB framework, yielding the composite material. TEM and SEM images showed that hPFL retained cubic architecture, yet exhibited increased and heterogeneous internal contrast, together with some irregular protrusions on the surface (Fig. 2c–S10d). This morphology arises from the localized deposition of the Fe3+-L-Arg coordination complex both inside the cavity and on the surfaces of the hPB framework. In addition, a few ultrasmall, irregularly shaped nanoparticles with contrast identical to the protrusions and internal deposits are observed outside hPFL, corresponding to the small amount of excess Fe3+-L-Arg complexes that failed to be incorporated into the hPB framework. To further clarify the crystal structure of the hPB framework after loading, X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was performed. The diffraction pattern of hPFL retained the characteristic peaks of the hPB core at identical 2θ positions, while exhibiting a slight decrease in peak intensity (Fig. S11a). This reduction is likely attributable to the presence of amorphous surface species formed from the loaded Fe3+ and L-Arg, which partially attenuate the X-ray signal from the crystalline hPB core, consistent with the morphological observations. To elucidate the interactions among Fe3+, L-Arg, and hPB, we conducted X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analyses to compare the chemical states of constituent elements in hPB and hPFL nanoparticles. XPS survey spectra confirmed the presence of C, N, O, and Fe elements in both systems (Fig. S12a–b). Deconvolution of the high-resolution N 1s spectrum for hPFL revealed four peaks. The three components at 397.0 eV, 399.0 eV, and 401.6 eV are characteristic of the PB framework. The new peak at 399.7 eV is attributed to the amino nitrogen of the incorporated L-Arg, confirming its successful introduction into hPFL (Fig. 2d). Furthermore, deconvolution of the Fe 2p spectrum for hPFL revealed a significant reduction in the Fe2+/Fe3+ ratio, along with the appearance of an additional peak at 713.08 eV (Fe3+ 2p3/2) (Fig. 2e). This new peak is attributed to Fe3+ species coordinated to L-Arg, where the interaction with the carboxylate and amino groups induces a chemical shift to higher binding energy relative to the cyano-coordinated Fe3+ 2p3/2 species within the hPB framework. The C 1s spectrum of hPFL exhibited an additional peak at a higher binding energy (288.18 eV) than that of carbonyl carbon peak (-O-C=O, derived from PVP) (Fig. 2f), derived from the decrease in electron density of carbonyl carbon atoms in L-Arg's carboxyl groups (-O-C=O) upon Fe3+ coordination. Collectively, these results conclusively demonstrate the successful loading of Fe3+-L-Arg complexes onto hPB through coordination bonds, ensuring stable immobilization of Fe3+ and L-Arg on the nanoplatform. Furthermore, Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy analysis revealed a significant enhancement in the absorption peak at 1640 cm−1 corresponding to C=O stretching vibrations in hPFL compared with hPB, further confirming successful L-Arg loading (Fig. S12c). Vis-NIR absorption spectra demonstrated that hPFL retained the characteristic absorption of hPB around 750 nm, while exhibiting markedly intensified absorption at ∼400 nm, attributable to Fe3+-L-Arg coordination complexes (Fig. 2g).

To mitigate premature leakage of Fe3+ and L-Arg during systemic circulation, we implemented a lipid bilayer encapsulation strategy, yielding a stabilized nanoformulation designated as hPFL@Lipo (Fig. 2a). Post-encapsulation characterization of hPFL@Lipo revealed an increase in hydrodynamic diameter and a reversal of zeta potential from positive to negative values compared to uncoated hPFL (Fig. 2h and i). The TEM image of hPFL@Lipo revealed a low-electron-density layer on the outer surface of nanoparticles, consistent with the presence of a lipid layer (Fig. S12d). In addition, compared with hPFL, hPFL@Lipo exhibited significantly reduced cargo leakage, with L-Arg release rate decreasing by 71.6%, over 12 h in aqueous medium (Fig. 2j). hPFL@Lipo demonstrated superior dispersibility and stability under physiologically relevant conditions, a characteristic that contributed to the extension of circulation time in vivo (Fig. 2k, Fig. S13). hPFL@Lipo also exhibited superior photothermal conversion capacity (Fig. 2l), even over multiple lasers on/off cycles (Fig. S14a). Notably, the photothermal performance of hPFL surpassed that of hPB, likely due to the incorporation of Fe3+ and L-Arg, which modifies the electronic structure of the framework and enhances non-radiative relaxation of photoexcited states. The concentration of hPFL@Lipo and laser power density positively correlated with heating rates and maximum temperatures (Fig. S14b–c). Notably, upon NIR irradiation, hPFL@Lipo rapidly released the majority of its Fe3+ and L-Arg cargo (Fig. 2m–n, S14d), driven by the photothermal effect that disrupts the liposomal structure and cleaves the coordination bonds between Fe3+ and L-Arg. Moreover, acidic conditions also promoted the release of both components (Fig. S15a–b). This dual responsiveness to light and pH enables spatiotemporally controlled drug release within the acidic tumor microenvironment upon NIR stimulation.

Immunomodulatory functions of hPFL@Lipo in vitro

M2 macrophages are highly enriched in colorectal TME (Fig. 1m), where arginase-mediated metabolism predominantly converts L-Arg into ornithine and urea. Ornithine subsequently serves as a polyamine precursor that fuels tumor cell proliferation [49]. Reprogramming these macrophages toward an M1 phenotype represents a strategic intervention that redirects L-Arg metabolism toward tumor-suppressive NO production pathways. To dissect the respective contributions of Fe3+ and L-Arg to this process, we treated pre-polarized M2 macrophages with PBS, L-Arg alone, Fe3+ alone, or L-Arg + Fe3+. We found that L-Arg alone had no discernible effect on polarization, whereas Fe3+ alone skewed macrophages toward an M1 phenotype, as indicated by increased CD86 and iNOS and decreased Arg-1 expression (Fig. S16a–f). To further examine the underlying metabolic shift, we measured urea and NO levels as readouts of arginase and iNOS pathway activities, respectively. Consistent with the polarization data, Fe3+ treatment increased NO production and reduced urea levels compared to PBS (Fig. S16g–h), indicating a metabolic redirection from the arginase pathway toward the iNOS pathway. Notably, the addition of L-Arg to Fe3+ further enhanced NO production relative to Fe3+ alone, suggesting that L-Arg serves as a substrate that amplifies iNOS-mediated metabolic flux once the pathway is engaged.

We confirmed that neither hPFL@Lipo nor hPFL@Lipo with brief sub-ablative photo-irradiation (1.5 W/cm2 for 40 s; denoted as hPFL@Lipo + l) induced detectable cytotoxicity in RAW 264.7 macrophages (relative viability >91.7%; Fig. S17a). This brief photo-irradiation protocol was designed to avoid hyperthermia-mediated cell death while triggering the release of L-Arg and Fe3+. We then treated M2-polarized RAW 264.7 macrophages with hPFL@Lipo in vitro. Flow cytometry revealed that hPFL@Lipo alone moderately increased M1 markers, elevating CD86+ cells from 10.3 % (PBS) to 17.7% (Fig. 3a and b), and iNOS+ cells from 16.6% (PBS) to 23.6% (Fig. 3c–S17b), likely due to minor Fe3+ leakage during co-culture. Strikingly, hPFL@Lipo + l significantly enhanced this repolarization effect, raising CD86+ cells to 33.8% and iNOS+ cells to 52.0%, attributed to rapid photo-triggered Fe3+ liberation. These changes were accompanied by corresponding decreases in M2 markers Arg-1 (Fig. S17c–d). Notably, addition of the iron chelator deferoxamine (DFO; Fig. S18a–d) substantially attenuated the phenotypic shifts induced by hPFL@Lipo + l treatment, demonstrating that chelation of released Fe3+ largely abrogated the immunomodulatory activity. These results indicate that Fe3+ release from the nanoparticles is a key driver of M1 polarization in macrophages. To evaluate the ability of hPFL@Lipo to reprogram M2 macrophage L-Arg metabolism toward an immunostimulatory phenotype, we quantified the production of the immune effector molecule NO. Compared to PBS group, hPFL@Lipo treatment increased both intracellular and extracellular NO levels in RAW 264.7 macrophages (Fig. 3d–S19a-c). Notably, photo-irradiation further enhanced this effect of hPFL@Lipo, resulting in a 137.7% increase in intracellular NO (Fig. 3d–S19a-c), and a 38.2% increase in extracellular NO compared to hPFL@Lipo treatment alone (Fig. 3e). Consistent with these findings, hPFL@Lipo + l treatment reduced the production of urea in M2-skewed macrophages by 21.7% (Fig. S19d). To validate the universality of this immunomodulatory mechanism, we employed a human macrophage model. The human monocytic leukemia cell line THP-1 was first differentiated and polarized into M2-like macrophages. Consistent with our findings in murine RAW 264.7 cells, upon NIR irradiation (l), hPFL@Lipo significantly drove the repolarization of THP-1-derived M2 macrophages toward an M1 phenotype, as evidenced by a marked increase in the surface expression of the M1 marker CD86 (Fig. S20b–c). To further evaluate macrophage responses under conditions that better model therapeutic irradiation, M2-polarized RAW 264.7 macrophages were exposed to a longer irradiation protocol (1.5 W/cm2, 3 min; denoted as L). While this longer irradiation reduced total macrophage numbers, the remaining population exhibited a pronounced shift toward an M1-dominant phenotype, with the M1/M2 ratio increasing approximately 2.55-fold in the hPFL@Lipo + L group compared to PBS controls (Fig. S21a–e). This confirms that Fe3+ release from hPFL@Lipo remains effective in driving macrophage polarization toward an M1-dominant phenotype even under therapeutic-grade photothermal conditions. Subsequently, we systematically evaluated the dual photothermal bioeffects of hPFL@Lipo in vitro: tumor cell ablation and T-cell immunomodulation (Fig. 3g). Upon longer photoirradiation (1.5 W/cm2, 3 min; L), hPFL@Lipo induced hyperthermic tumor cell death in murine CT26 cells, suppressing viability by 60.5% at 44 mg mL−1 (Fig. 3h). The extent of cell death was further characterized by Annexin V/PI co-staining, which revealed 12.4% apoptosis and 62.0% necrosis in hPFL@Lipo + L group (Fig. S22). In addition, under the same administration dose and irradiation conditions, hPFL@Lipo + L induced stronger tumor cell suppression than hPB@Lipo + L (Fig. 3h–S22), which was mainly attributed to the higher photothermal conversion efficiency of hPFL@Lipo (Fig. 2l). To validate these findings in human cells, we extended the analyses to human colorectal carcinoma HCT116 cells (Fig. S23a). Critically, hPFL@Lipo + L induced ICD in both murine CT26 and human HCT116 cells, evidenced by substantial release of high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) and adenosine triphosphate (ATP) (Fig. 3i–j, Fig. S23b–c). To delineate the contributions of PTT, Fe3+, and L-Arg to dendritic cell (DCs) activation, we co-incubated CT26 tumor cells pretreated with various formulations with bone marrow-derived DCs (BMDCs) for 48 h (Fig. S24a–b). As expected, tumor cells pretreated with hPB@Lipo + L induced substantial DC maturation, confirming that ICD triggered by PTT alone is sufficient to activate DCs. The full formulation, hPFL@Lipo + L, further enhanced DC maturation, attributable to its superior photothermal conversion efficiency and a minor additional immunogenic stimulus from Fe3+-associated processes such as ferroptosis. Notably, pretreatment with Fe3+ alone or L-Arg + Fe3+ resulted in only weak DC maturation. Consistent with these findings, hPFL@Lipo + L treatment led to a 73.6% increase in mature DCs (CD11c+CD80+CD86+) compared to the hPFL@Lipo group (Fig. 3k and l), highlighting that the potent DC maturation induced by hPFL@Lipo is primarily attributable to photothermal-triggered ICD. Consistently, DCs in hPFL@Lipo + L group exhibited a pro-inflammatory cytokine profile, characterized by 1.50-fold and 1.71-fold upregulation of TNF-α and IL-12 secretion, respectively, while immunosuppressive cytokine IL-10 decreased by 57.3% (Fig. 3m–S25a-c). To assess the downstream T-cell response primed by DCs maturation, we co-cultured aforementioned DCs with T cells. CFSE dilution assays demonstrated 3.42-fold greater T-cell proliferation in the hPFL@Lipo + L group compared to PBS controls (Fig. 3n and o). Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) analysis further confirmed that hPFL@Lipo + L pretreatment promoted T-cell effector function, as evidenced by at least a 2.58-fold increase in the secretion levels of the antitumor cytokines IFN-γ and TNF-α compared to other groups (Fig. 3p–S25d). Flow cytometric analysis of T-cell surface markers further validated that hPFL@Lipo + L pretreatment activated DCs-mediated T-cell priming, with a 44.0% upregulation of CD69 (an early activation marker) and a 1.79-fold upregulation of CD25 (a key regulator of T-cell proliferation and mid-stage activation marker; Fig. S26a–d). Collectively, these results suggest that hPFL@Lipo-mediated PTT has the potential to promote intratumoral T-cell infiltration and activation while reducing tumor cellularity. This, in turn, orchestrates a metabolic shift towards beneficial L-Arg utilization within TME.

To investigate the direct immunomodulatory role of L-Arg, we assessed its impact on T-cells in vitro. Pre-activated T cells were treated with free L-Arg or with hPFL@Lipo followed by NIR irradiation (hPFL@Lipo + l). Both direct L-Arg supplementation and NIR-triggered L-Arg release from hPFL@Lipo augmented the activation state and effector function of pre-activated T cells, as evidenced by the upregulation of CD25 and a substantial increase in IFN-γ secretion (Fig. 3q–s). A key finding was that L-Arg induced a markedly stronger proliferative response in pre-activated T cells than in naive cells (7.85-fold versus 2.47-fold; Fig. 3t–S27), directly demonstrating their heightened metabolic dependency on this amino acid. Collectively, these data demonstrate that L-Arg availability is a critical limiter of T-cell expansion and cytokine production and establish that the controlled release afforded by hPFL@Lipo can effectively meet this demand.

Tumor-targeting performance and biosafety assessment of hPFL@Lipo

Furthermore, in vivo blood biochemistry and routine hematological tests revealed no significant differences in all key parameters between hPFL@Lipo-treated mice and PBS-treated controls, with no indications of hepatic or renal damage or other hematological abnormalities (Fig. S28b). Antitumor efficiency and immunomodulatory functions of hPFL@Lipo in vivo

Following tumor accumulation, we evaluated the photothermal conversion efficacy of hPFL@Lipo in vivo. Twelve hours post intravenous administration of hPFL@Lipo to CT26 tumor-bearing mice, localized irradiation resulted in a rapid temperature increase in tumor tissue to 48 °C within 4 min, sufficient to induce irreversible tumor cell death (Fig. 4d–S30b). To assess therapeutic outcomes, hPFL@Lipo (7.5 mg kg−1) was intravenously administered to CT26 subcutaneous tumor-bearing mice (Fig. 4e). All treatments were well-tolerated, as evidenced by stable body weights (Fig. 4f) and no significant histopathological abnormalities in major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) upon H&E staining (Fig. S31), collectively supporting (along with the data in Fig. S28) the favorable biosafety profile of hPFL@Lipo. Treatment with hPFL@Lipo alone significantly suppressed tumor growth compared to the PBS control, leading to a 46.0% reduction in tumor volume and extending the median survival time from 20 to 26 days (Fig. 4g–h, S32a-b). Furthermore, the combination with NIR irradiation (hPFL@Lipo + L) dramatically enhanced antitumor efficacy, which was evidenced by a 98.1% inhibition of tumor growth, a 95.6% reduction in tumor weight, and a 100% survival rate compared to PBS group until the end of the study. To further investigate the immunomodulatory capacity of hPFL@Lipo, we characterized the tumor immune microenvironment. Flow cytometric analysis revealed that hPFL@Lipo + L therapy markedly skewed macrophage polarization toward an anti-tumor phenotype, evidenced by a 2.36-fold increase in CD86+ M1 macrophages, 2.12-fold increase in iNOS+ M1 macrophages, and a concurrent 62.1% reduction in Arg-1+ M2 macrophages relative to the PBS control (Fig. 4i–j, S33a-d). Similar trends were observed in the hPFL@Lipo group, and the differences between hPFL@Lipo + L and hPFL@Lipo alone were not statistically significant. This is likely because, even in the absence of laser triggering, Fe3+ and L-Arg can be slowly released from hPFL@Lipo in the weakly acidic tumor microenvironment through dissociation of the coordination bonds. Notably, intratumoral NO levels were elevated by 23.3% in the hPFL@Lipo + L group and by 13% in the hPFL@Lipo group compared to PBS group (Fig. 4k–S33e), an effect we attributed to hPFL@Lipo-mediated macrophage M1 polarization and localized L-Arg delivery. Furthermore, hPFL@Lipo + L treatment significantly promoted DCs maturation in tumor-draining lymph nodes (TDLNs), showing a 65.8% increase compared to the PBS group and a 39.9% increase compared to hPFL@Lipo treatment alone (Fig. 4l–S34a). This enhancement is likely attributable to ICD induced by PTT, which may facilitate antigen presentation and subsequent immune activation. Correspondingly, we observed substantial infiltration of cytotoxic T cells (CD3+CD8+) and activated cytotoxic T cells (CD3+CD8+IFN-γ+) in tumors from the hPFL@Lipo + L group, with frequencies reaching 3.75-fold and 1.71-fold higher, respectively, than those in the PBS control group (Fig. 4m–o, S34b-c). Importantly, both T-cell subsets were significantly more abundant in the hPFL@Lipo + L group than in the hPFL@Lipo-alone group (Fig. 4m-o). In addition, we also evaluated the impact of hPFL@Lipo treatment on key immunosuppressive populations. Notably, hPFL@Lipo + L significantly reduced the proportion of Foxp3+ regulatory T cells (Tregs) among CD4+ T cells (Fig. S35a–b), while both hPFL@Lipo and hPFL@Lipo + L markedly decreased the frequency of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) compared to the PBS control group (Fig. S35c–d). These results indicate that hPFL@Lipo + L not only enhances antitumor effector immunity but also alleviates immunosuppressive components within the TME, collectively shifting the TME toward an antitumor immune microenvironment.

To evaluate the metabolic modulation induced by hPFL@Lipo, we measured NO, spermidine, and urea levels in both serum and tumor tissues at 24 h and 48 h after treatment. In tumor tissues, NO levels were significantly elevated in both the hPFL@Lipo and hPFL@Lipo + L groups (Fig. S36a), confirming successful redirection of L-Arg metabolism toward the iNOS-mediated pathway. In contrast, intratumoral spermidine and urea remained unchanged (Fig. S36b and c), which can be attributed to the combined effects of reduced polyamine-producing cells, release of pre-existing metabolites from dying cells, and infiltration of newly recruited immune cells that contribute to the tissue pool. In serum, urea levels were significantly increased in the hPFL@Lipo + L group (Fig. S36f), consistent with acute tumor debulking and release of nitrogenous waste, whereas NO and spermidine showed no notable changes (Fig. S36d and e), likely due to their localized production and rapid clearance. Collectively, these results provide direct metabolic evidence that hPFL@Lipo + L reprograms intratumoral L-Arg flux toward an antitumor immune-supporting state, accompanied by transient systemic changes.

To further investigate the impact of hPFL@Lipo + L on TME, we performed transcriptomic sequencing of tumor tissues, identifying a total of 33,097 genes. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed pronounced segregation between the hPFL@Lipo + L and PBS groups within the global transcriptional landscape, indicating substantial reprogramming of the TME (Fig. 4p). Differential expression analysis using DESeq2 identified 569 differentially expressed genes (DEGs; |log2FC| > 1, FDR-adjusted p-value <0.05), comprising 363 upregulated and 206 downregulated genes, between the hPFL@Lipo + L and PBS groups (Fig. S37a and b). Notably, the transcriptional alterations in hPFL@Lipo + L group were more pronounced than those observed in hPFL@Lipo group (Fig. S37a–c). Gene expression profiling revealed upregulation of genes associated with antigen processing and presentation, T-cell activation and differentiation, TCR signaling, and cytokine production in the hPFL@Lipo + L group compared to the PBS group (Fig. 4q–r, S38). Independent qRT-PCR validation of these representative genes (B2m, Tap1, Ifng, Zap70, Cd3e, Tbx21) from the above-mentioned pathways confirmed their significant upregulation in the hPFL@Lipo + L group (Fig. S39a–f), robustly corroborating the transcriptomic findings. Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes pathway (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis of DEGs between hPFL@Lipo + L and PBS groups revealed activation of the IL-17 signaling pathway (which facilitates immune cell recruitment and enhances effector T cell functions) and the C-type lectin receptor (CLR) signaling pathway (which regulates macrophage polarization, DCs maturation, and antigen presentation efficiency) (Fig. 4s). Collectively, these results indicate that hPFL@Lipo + L treatment effectively reprograms macrophage polarization and promotes intratumoral infiltration and activation of cytotoxic T cells, thereby remodeling the immunosuppressive TME into an immunostimulatory niche.

To assess the durability of antitumor immunity, we performed a 60-day rechallenge experiment. Mice that achieved complete tumor regression after hPFL@Lipo + L or hPB@Lipo + L treatment were rechallenged with CT26 tumor cells on day 60 (Fig. S40a). Notably, 71.4% of mice in the hPFL@Lipo + L group remained tumor-free after rechallenge, whereas only modest protection was observed in the hPB@Lipo + L group (Fig. S40b, S41a-c). Flow cytometric analysis of splenocytes revealed a significantly elevated frequency of effector memory T cells (TEM; CD44+CD62L−CD8+) in the hPFL@Lipo + L group (Fig. S41d–e), confirming the establishment of durable long-term immune memory.

Furthermore, to validate the universality of our approach, we extended the evaluation to the 4T1 murine breast tumor model, a highly aggressive and poorly immunogenic tumor model. As shown in Fig. S42a–c, hPFL@Lipo + L treatment significantly suppressed 4T1 tumor growth compared to control groups, as evidenced by tumor growth curves, representative photographs of excised tumors, and endpoint tumor weights. In addition, hPFL@Lipo + L therapy significantly prolonged the survival of 4T1 tumor-bearing mice (Fig. S42d). Consistent with these antitumor effects, hPFL@Lipo + L treatment also markedly activated the tumor immune microenvironment, as indicated by increased infiltration of M1-polarized macrophages and CD8+ T cells (Fig. S43a–d). These results demonstrate that the therapeutic efficacy of hPFL@Lipo + L is not limited to CT26 colorectal tumor but extends to other tumor types, further supporting the broad applicability of this immunometabolic modulation strategy.

Synergistic antitumor efficacy of hPFL@Lipo with αPD-1 therapy

Having established that hPFL@Lipo monotherapy remodels the immunosuppressive TME and enables targeted L-Arg delivery, both critical for effective immune checkpoint blockade, we reasoned that such a remodeled microenvironment might exhibit enhanced sensitivity to immune checkpoint blockade. We therefore next explored whether combining a sub-therapeutic dose of hPFL@Lipo (3.75 mg kg−1, half of the therapeutic dose in Fig. 4) with αPD-1 could yield synergistic therapeutic outcomes (Fig. 5a). Throughout the course of the antitumor treatment, there was a slight increase in the body weight of mice across all groups (Fig. S28c), suggesting a good biocompatibility for all formulated treatments. Tumor growth curves revealed that the combination of hPFL@Lipo + L with αPD-1 therapy achieved a 64.1% greater inhibition of tumor growth compared to hPFL@Lipo + L therapy (Fig. 5b). This enhancement was markedly superior to the 33.6% improvement observed upon the addition of αPD-1 to hPB@Lipo + L. Consistent with these findings, the average weight of excised tumors in the hPFL@Lipo + L + αPD-1 group was 65.0% lower than that in the hPFL@Lipo + L group (Fig. 5c and d). This margin of improvement exceeded the 22.6% reduction attained with the hPB@Lipo + L + αPD-1 combination versus hPB@Lipo + L therapy. In addition, survival analysis showed that the hPFL@Lipo + L + αPD-1 group exhibited prolonged survival compared to the PBS control (Fig. S44a). Histopathological assessment further supported the enhanced efficacy. To investigate the underlying mechanisms, we assessed the alterations in tumor immune microenvironment. Flow cytometric analysis revealed that hPFL@Lipo + L + αPD-1 treatment most significantly enhanced immune activation, marked by a 1.98-fold increase in total CD8+ T cell infiltration and a 1.16-fold increase in activated (IFN-γ+) CD8+ T cells compared to the hPB@Lipo + L + αPD-1 group (Fig. 5g–j). To further characterize the functional status of CD8+ T cells, we analyzed their proliferative capacity and cytotoxic potential. Both hPFL@Lipo + L and the combination treatment increased the proportions of Ki-67+CD8+ and Granzyme B+ CD8+ T cells relative to the PBS control. Notably, the combination therapy further enhanced the proportion of Ki-67+ CD8+ T cells compared to hPFL@Lipo + L alone, and Granzyme B+ CD8+ T cells also showed a further increase in the combination group (Fig. S44b–e). Furthermore, the hPFL@Lipo + L + αPD-1 treatment promoted a more favorable macrophage polarization profile, increasing the M1 macrophage proportion by 46.3% while reducing the M2 population by 37.3% relative to the hPB@Lipo + L + αPD-1 group (Fig. 5k–l, S45a-b). Consistent with these cellular changes, ELISA results indicated that the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interleukin-12 (IL-12), were highest in the hPFL@Lipo + L + αPD-1 group among all treatments (Fig. 5m and n). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that hPFL@Lipo + L effectively synergizes with αPD-1 to remodel the immune microenvironment and potentiate a robust antitumor response, thereby achieving enhanced tumor suppression.

Abscopal and Anti-metastatic effect of hPFL@Lipo combined with αPD-1 therapy

Subsequently, we evaluated the ability of the hPFL@Lipo + L-based treatment to elicit a systemic anti-tumor immune response in the context of αPD-1 combination therapy. The abscopal effect, a phenomenon wherein local therapy induces regression of non-irradiated distal tumors, serves as a key indicator of systemic antitumor immunity [50]. To assess this effect, we established a bilateral CT26 tumor model by inoculating tumor cells subcutaneously into both flanks of mice, with the right tumor designated as the primary (treated) lesion and the left as the distal (untreated) tumor (Fig. 6a). All groups received systemic αPD-1 treatment in addition to their designated local therapies. Monitoring of distal tumor growth revealed that both hPB@Lipo + L and hPFL@Lipo + L local treatments effectively inhibited distal tumor growth (Fig. 6b). Importantly, the inhibition achieved by hPFL@Lipo + L was 65.5% higher than that of hPB@Lipo + L. Consistent with the growth inhibition, the average weight of excised distal tumors in the hPFL@Lipo + L group decreased by 43.0% compared with the control group and by 30% compared with the hPB@Lipo + L group (Fig. 6c and d). Flow cytometric analysis of TDLNs from distal lesions revealed that hPFL@Lipo + L treatment increased the proportion of mature DCs (CD80+CD86+) to 1.67-fold that of the control group and 1.54-fold that of the hPB@Lipo + L group (Fig. 6e–S46). Furthermore, the proportion of CD8+ T cells infiltrating the distal tumors in the hPFL@Lipo + L group was 2.65 times that of controls and 1.48 times that of hPB@Lipo + L group (Fig. 6f and g). Liver metastasis is a major cause of mortality in colorectal cancer patients [51]. To address this critical issue, we subsequently investigated the anti-metastatic effect of hPFL@Lipo-mediated αPD-1 combination therapy. Mice bearing subcutaneous CT26 tumors were divided into three experimental groups: control, surgery, and hPFL@Lipo + L. Subsequently, intrasplenic injection of CT26 cells was performed, after which all mice received αPD-1 treatment (Fig. 6h). Longitudinal monitoring revealed abnormal body weight gain and abdominal distension in the control and surgery groups, whereas hPFL@Lipo + L-treated mice maintained normal physiological parameters throughout the study (Fig. 6i–j, S47a). On day 16 post-intrasplenic injection, the excised liver and spleen weights, which were used as auxiliary indicators of metastatic burden [[52], [53], [54]], were markedly higher in the control and surgery groups than in the hPFL@Lipo + L group (Fig. 6k–S47c). Macroscopic imaging and H&E staining revealed the presence of obvious metastatic lesions in the livers and spleens of the control and surgery groups, with the surgery group showing smaller metastatic areas than the control group, likely due to pre-existing tumor antigen exposure (Fig. 6l–o, S47b, d). Remarkably, no detectable metastatic lesions were found in the livers and spleens of hPFL@Lipo + L-treated mice. Flow cytometric analysis revealed a 79.8% increase in splenic central memory T cell proportions (CD44+CD62L+) in the hPFL@Lipo + L group compared to the surgical group (Fig. 6p and q). These results collectively demonstrate that hPFL@Lipo + L therapy synergizes with αPD-1 to induce long-term immune memory, effectively suppressing metastatic progression.

Conclusion

Evidence indicates that severe L-Arg deprivation faced by antitumor effector cells constitutes a key pathological mechanism underlying resistance to immunotherapy [13,55]. In murine models of colorectal cancer, we observed that oral L-Arg supplementation alone provided limited benefit to αPD-1 therapy. This limited efficacy can be attributed not only to insufficient elevation of intratumoral L-Arg levels but, more critically, to the unaddressed metabolic competition for L-Arg among tumor cells, immunosuppressive cells, and effector immune cells, a competition which ultimately dictates the net outcome of L-Arg supplementation on tumor progression.

Our bioinformatic analysis revealed heightened L-Arg metabolism in colorectal tumor tissues, indicative of intense nutrient competition within TME. The overwhelming numerical superiority of M2 macrophages and tumor cells over antitumor immune cells severely limits the benefit that the latter can derive from exogenous L-Arg supplementation. We demonstrated two distinct but complementary approaches to reconfigure this suppressive metabolic landscape. In the first approach, by weakening the metabolic advantage of M2 macrophages with iron ions (Fe3+), L-Arg supplementation markedly enhanced anti-tumor immunity and tumor growth suppression. In the second, PTT acted synergistically with L-Arg by directly reducing tumor burden while promoting T-cell infiltration. Collectively, our findings demonstrate that both Fe3+ and PTT can reconfigure the tumor ecosystem by establishing an immunostimulatory L-Arg metabolic niche, thereby unlocking the potential of L-Arg to potentiate antitumor immunity.

Building on these insights, we engineered a photo-responsive nano-modulator, hPFL@Lipo, capable of spatiotemporally controlled L-Arg release, concurrent Fe3+ delivery, and localized PTT under NIR irradiation. Experimental validation confirmed that photoactivated hPFL@Lipo effectively reverses the immunosuppressive microenvironment and enhances the infiltration and activation of cytotoxic T cells. In combination with αPD-1, hPFL@Lipo plus irradiation induces a robust systemic antitumor immune response and immunologic memory, leading to synergistic inhibition of both primary and distal tumor growth. This study introduces a therapeutic strategy termed metabolic niche reprogramming and provides an effective nanotechnological platform, offering potential methodological avenues for overcoming tumor immune resistance in tumor immunotherapy.

Preparation of hPB nanoparticles

K3[Fe(CN)6]·3H2O (1.1 g), PVP (24 g), HCl solution (12 M, 2.67 mL), and 320 mL ddH2O were mixed in a flask under magnetic stirring for about 30 min. The mixed liquid was placed in an oil bath at 80 °C for 20 h. The 60 nm seeds were obtained after centrifugation (12,000 rpm, 5 min) and washing the residue with ddH2O three times. Then, K3[Fe(CN)6]·3H2O (1.1 g), PVP K30 (24 g), HCl solution (12 M, 0.267 mL), the obtained 60 nm PB seeds (80 mg), and 320 mL ddH2O were mixed in a flask, and the rest steps are as above to obtain 120 nm PB NPs. HCl (20 mL, 1 M), PB NPs (20 mg), and PVP K30 (100 mg) were mixed and sealed in a Teflon-lined autoclave and kept at 140 °C for 4 h. The precipitation was collected by centrifugation (12,000 rpm, 5 min) and washed with ddH2O twice. Finally, the obtained hPB nanoparticles were dissolved in ddH2O for future use.

Preparation of hPFL nanoparticles

20 mg of hPB was dispersed in FeCl3·6H2O ethanol solution (2 mg mL−1, 5 mL) and the mixture was stirred at room temperature for 20 min. Subsequently, an aqueous solution of L-Arg (100 mg mL−1, 2 mL), pre-adjusted to pH 7, was added, and the mixture was stirred at 45 °C for 15 min. Following this, FeCl3·6H2O solution in ethanol (15 mg mL−1, 6 mL) was added dropwise to the above solution via a syringe pump over a period of 1 h under stirring at 50 °C, and the stirring was continued for another 30 min. The precipitation was collected by centrifugation (12,000 rpm, 5 min) to obtain hPFL nanoparticles. The nanoregulator was constructed via coordination-driven self-assembly, wherein Fe3+ ions coordinate with the carboxylate and amino groups of L-Arg, leading to the in-situ formation of a metal–organic complex within the hollow cavities of hPB. This cooperative interplay between exogenous Fe3+, endogenous Fe3+ from the hPB framework, and L-Arg drives the formation of a stable, high-payload composite.

Preparation of hPFL@Lipo

hPFL@Lipo was obtained by a two-step approach. Firstly, 5 mg of hPFL (1 mg mL−1 in ethanol) and 2 mL of DOPA solution (2 mg mL−1 in chloroform) were mixed under ultrasonication for 20 min. The mixture was centrifuged to remove free DOPA and then redispersed in chloroform. For liposome coating, nanoparticles obtained above were mixed with a lipid blend of DPPC, cholesterol, and DSPE-PEG2000 (at a molar ratio of 4:4:2) in chloroform. After overnight stirring, the organic solvent was then removed by rotary evaporation to form a thin lipid film, which was subsequently hydrated with ddH2O to yield the final hPFL@Lipo nanoparticles for further use.

The loading efficiencies of L-Arg and Fe3+ assessment

To digest the sample, 100 μg of hPFL was incubated in 990 μL of hydrochloric acid. The mixture was then centrifuged at 11,000 rpm for 5 min. The concentration of L-Arg in the supernatant was quantified using the naphthol-diethyl hydrazine method. Specifically, the indicator aqueous solution consisting of NaOH (40 mg mL−1), 1-naphthol-propanol (80 mg mL−1), and 2,3-Butanedione-propanol (0.5 μL mL−1) was first prepared. 130 μL of the indicator solution and 20 μL of the supernatant were mixed and incubated for 20 min. The absorbance of the mixture was detected at 540 nm by a microplate reader. A calibration curve was prepared in the concentration range of 0 to 200 mg L−1 using L-Arg as the standard.

The loading efficiency of Fe3+ was quantified by inductively coupled plasma (ICP) analysis. Equal amounts of empty hPB nanoparticles (before loading) and hPFL nanoparticles (after loading) were separately digested with concentrated hydrochloric acid, and the iron content in each digest was measured. The Fe3+ loading content was calculated as the difference in iron content between hPFL and hPB nanoparticles.

L-Arg leakage prevention assay

Samples were prepared by dispersing hPFL (0.6 mg) and hPFL@Lipo (with equivalent hPFL content, 0.6 mg) in 1 mL of ddH2O, respectively. The solutions were loaded into dialysis bags and immersed in 5 mL of ddH2O. At indicated time points, 1 mL of the external aqueous solution was withdrawn and replaced with an equal volume of ddH2O. The amount of L-Arg released was quantified using the naphthol-diethyl hydrazine method above.

Stability assay

hPFL@Lipo was dispersed in RPMI 1640 medium containing 10% FBS and stood at 37 °C. Hydrodynamic diameter of hPFL@Lipo was measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS) at different time points. For visual stability assessment, hPFL@Lipo was dispersed in PBS, and RPMI 1640 containing 10% FBS. Digital photographs were taken at indicated time points to document aggregation or precipitation.

Measurement of photothermal performance

All concentrations of hPB@Lipo and hPFL@Lipo nanoparticles are expressed in terms of their Prussian blue content throughout the study (including in vitro cellular assays and in vivo animal experiments).

The pure solution of PBS and dispersions of hPB@Lipo, hPFL@Lipo (100 mg L−1) were irradiated with 808 nm NIR irradiation (1 W cm−2). For comparison, different concentrations (10, 25, and 50 mg L−1) of hPFL@Lipo (1 W cm−2) and 100 mg L−1 of hPFL@Lipo (2 W cm−2) were also irradiated. The change of temperature was monitored by a thermocouple probe for 600 s. The photothermal stability of hPFL@Lipo under 808 nm NIR irradiation was also evaluated under 4 cycles of heating/cooling processes.

NIR-triggered Fe3+ release in vitro

hPFL@Lipo (with an equivalent hPFL content of 1 mg) was suspended in 0.5 mL of ddH2O at pH 5.5 and irradiated with 808 nm NIR irradiation (1.5 W cm−2). The concentration of Fe3+ in the supernatant was detected by the 1,10-Phenanthroline method. Specifically, 100 μL of ascorbic acid solution at pH 5.5 was added to reduce any Fe3+ to Fe2+, then 100 μL of 0.1% (w/v) 1,10-phenanthroline solution at pH 5.5 was added. The absorbance of the mixture was measured at 510 nm using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer. A calibration curve was prepared in the concentration range of 0 to 200 mg L−1 using FeCl3·6H2O as the standard.

NIR-triggered L-Arg release in vitro

hPFL@Lipo (with an equivalent hPFL content of 4 mg) was suspended in 1.0 mL of ddH2O and irradiated with 808 nm NIR irradiation (1.5 W cm−2). At the indicated time points, the nanoparticle suspension was subjected to centrifugation, and the supernatant was collected. The concentration of L-Arg in the supernatant was analyzed by the naphthol-diethyl hydrazine method mentioned above. Subsequent treatments were performed as described for RAW 264.7 cells.

Cell culture

RAW 264.7 cells and HCT116 cells were cultured in DMEM containing 10% FBS and 1% antibiotics. Cytotoxic T lymphocyte-2 (CTLL-2) cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10 % FBS and 100 U/mL recombinant mouse IL-2. All cells were incubated at 37 °C, 5% CO2.

Macrophage polarization investigation in vitro

M2-skewed RAW 264.7 cells were incubated with L-Arg (20 mg L−1) and hPFL@Lipo (100 mg L−1) for 24 h. In the hPFL@Lipo + l group, the cells were subjected to 808 nm NIR irradiation (1.5 W cm−2) for 40 s. For M1 phenotype macrophage (CD86+) detection, the cells were collected and stained with FITC-anti-F4/80, APC-anti-CD11b, PE-anti-CD86 for 30 min. For M1 phenotype macrophage (iNOS+) and M2 phenotype macrophage (Arg-1+) detection, after being stained with FITC-anti-F4/80 and APC-anti-CD11b, the cells were fixed and permeabilized, followed by the instructions of Fixation/Permeabilization Solution Kit. Then the cells were washed and stained with PE-anti-iNOS or PE-Arg-1 for 30 min. Finally, cells were resuspended in PBS and analyzed by FACS. To elucidate the mechanism of macrophage polarization, M2-skewed RAW 264.7 cells were incubated for 24 h under conditions mimicking the tumor microenvironment (pH 6.0, 50 μM H2O2) with one of the following: FeCl3·6H2O (30 μM), or hPFL@Lipo (50 mg L−1), followed by 808 nm NIR irradiation (1.5 W cm−2, 40 s). Each treatment was also performed in the presence or absence of deferoxamine (DFO, 100 μM).

To evaluate human macrophage polarization, Human THP-1 monocytes were differentiated into M0 macrophages with PMA (200 ng mL−1, 48 h) and then polarized to an M2 phenotype using recombinant human IL-4 (20 ng mL−1, 48 h). In parallel, murine RAW 264.7 macrophages were polarized to an M2 state using recombinant murine IL-4 at the same concentration and duration.

NO detection in vitro

The generation of NO in RAW264.7 cells was determined using fluorescence microscope under conditions mimicking the tumor microenvironment (pH 6.0, 50 μM H2O2). M2-skewed RAW264.7 cells were cultured with L-Arg (20 mg L−1) and hPFL@Lipo (100 mg L−1) for 4 h. For the hPFL@Lipo + l group, after 2 h of co-culture, cells were subjected to 808 nm NIR irradiation (1.5 W cm−2) for 40 s. 6 h later, cells were stained with DAF-FM DA and DAPI following the manufacturer's instructions, followed by fluorescence microscope observation. The supernatant was collected for NO detection by the Griess Reagent kit, based on the manufacturer's procedures.

Assessment of cell viability in the CT26-M2 TAM Co-culture transwell system

To assess macrophage-mediated cytotoxicity, a transwell co-culture system was employed. Briefly, 3 × 106 of M2-skewed RAW 264.7 cells were seeded in the upper compartment of the transwell system, and 1 × 106 of CT26 cells were seeded in the bottom chamber of the transwell system (24-well plate, 0.4 μm-sized microporous membrane) and incubated for 24 h. The medium in the upper chamber was replaced with medium containing L-Arg (20 mg L−1), hPB@Lipo (100 mg L−1), and hPFL@Lipo (100 mg L−1). The hPFL@Lipo + l group was subjected to 808 nm NIR irradiation for 40 s. After a 24-h incubation, the cytotoxic effect of macrophages on CT26 cells was evaluated by MTT assay in the bottom chamber.

To exclude direct nanoparticle-mediated effects, a parallel control was performed under identical conditions but without macrophages in the upper chamber. Only CT26 cells were seeded in the lower chamber and treated with the same regimens described above.

In vitro toxicity of hPFL@Lipo

For toxicity against CT26 cells detection, CT26 cells were seeded in a 96-well plate overnight. The medium was replaced with fresh medium containing gradient concentrations of hPB@Lipo and hPFL@Lipo. After 4 h, CT26 cells were subjected to different treatments: no light irradiation and 808 nm NIR irradiation (1.5 W cm−2) for 3 min, followed by a 24-h incubation. Cell viability was then assessed using the MTT assay. For toxicity against RAW 264.7 cells detection, RAW 264.7 cells were seeded in a 96-well plate overnight. The medium was replaced with fresh medium containing gradient concentrations of hPFL@Lipo. After 4 h, RAW 264.7 cells were subjected to different treatments: no light irradiation and 808 nm NIR irradiation (1.5 W cm−2) irradiation for 40 s, followed by a 24-h incubation. Cell viability was then assessed using the MTT assay.

Detection of immunogenic cell death (ICD) markers in vitro

CT26 cells were seeded in a 24-well plate and cultured with hPFL@Lipo (50 mg L−1) for 12 h. Cells in the irradiated group were subjected to 808 nm NIR irradiation (1.5 W cm−2) for 3 min, and the non-irradiated group was not treated. 5 h later, the culture supernatant was collected and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min. The release of HMGB1 and the secretion of ATP were detected using HMGB1 and ATP detection kits, respectively, following the manufacturer's instructions.

Maturation of bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) in vitro

BMDCs were isolated from the bone marrow of C57BL/6 mice and cultured in 1640 medium containing GM-CSF (20 ng mL−1) and IL-4 (10 ng mL−1). CT26 cells were seeded in a 24-well plate and cultured with hPFL@Lipo (50 mg L−1) for 4 h. Cells in the irradiated group were subjected to 808 nm NIR irradiation (1.5 W cm−2) for 3 min, and the non-irradiated group was not treated. The original medium was then replaced with fresh medium, and BMDCs were added for co-culture. After 20 h, cells were collected and stained with FITC-anti-CD11c, BV421-anti-CD80, and PE-anti-CD86 antibodies, and analyzed using flow cytometry. Concurrently, the culture supernatant was collected to analyze the secretion of TNF-α, IL-12, and IL-10.

Tumor-DC-T cell Co-culture system

Splenic lymphocytes (SPLCs) were extracted from healthy mice and stained with CFSE following the manufacturer's instructions. BMDCs were co-cultured with pre-treated CT26 for 20 h to stimulate the maturation of BMDCs according to the aforementioned method. Then the CFSE-SPLCs were added to the co-culture system. 4 days later, the suspended cells were harvested and analyzed for T cell (CTLL-2) proliferation and CD69 and CD25 expression using flow cytometry. Additionally, the cell supernatant was collected to detect the secretion of IFN-γ and TNF-α by ELISA.

Immunomodulatory effects of L-Arg on T-cell in vitro

For T-cell functional assays, CTLL-2 cells were collected after pre-activation in the tumor-DC-T cell co-culture system described above.

To assess T-cell activation state and effector function detection, these pre-activated T cells were then cultured in medium containing either free L-Arg (20 mg L−1) or hPFL@Lipo (100 mg L−1). The hPFL@Lipo + l group were then subjected to 808 nm NIR irradiation (1.5 W cm−2, 40 s) to trigger L-Arg release. After a 3-day incubation, CTLL-2 cells were harvested for flow cytometric analysis of CD25 expression, while the culture supernatants were collected for IFN-γ levels detection by ELISA.

For proliferation analysis, pre-activated (collected from the same co-culture system) and naive T cells were labeled with CFSE, resuspended in fresh RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with L-Arg (50 mg L−1), and cultured separately.

Tumor targeting in vivo

During the synthesis of liposomes, DiR was incorporated into the mixed lipids to obtain DiR-labeled hPFL@Lipo (DiR-hPFL@Lipo). CT26 tumor-bearing mouse model was established, and when the tumor volume reached approximately 100 mm3, DiR-hPFL@Lipo was administered intravenously via tail vein (5 mg kg−1). At the indicated time points (1 h, 2 h, 9 h, 24 h, 33 h, and 48 h), mice were imaged using an in vivo small animal imaging system (In-Vivo FX PRO, Bruker, Germany). After 48 h post-injection, tumors and major tissues (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were obtained to measure fluorescence ex vivo.

To further assess the accumulation of hPFL@Lipo in tumors, CT26 tumor-bearing mice with a tumor volume of approximately 200 mm3 were intravenously administered hPFL@Lipo (5 mg kg−1). At the indicated time points (0, 8, and 24 h), the mice were euthanized, and the tumor tissues were collected and weighed. Subsequently, the tumor tissues were minced into small pieces and digested with nitric acid under heating. The accumulation of iron in the tumor tissues was quantitatively analyzed by ICP-OES.

In vivo pharmacokinetic study of hPFL@Lipo

Cy5.5-labeled hPFL@Lipo was intravenously injected into CT26 tumor-bearing mice at a dose of 10 mg kg−1. Blood samples (10 μL) were collected via the tail vein at predetermined time points, diluted in 40 μL of water, and stored protected from light at 4 °C until analysis. Fluorescence intensity (excitation/emission: 675/700 nm) was measured using a fluorescence spectrophotometer and converted to percentage of injected dose (%ID) based on a standard curve prepared in naive mouse blood.

Photothermal imaging in vivo

A CT26 tumor-bearing mouse model was established, and when the tumor volume reached approximately 100 mm3, hPFL@Lipo and PBS were administered intravenously via tail vein (5 mg kg−1). 12 h later, the mice were subjected to 808 nm NIR irradiation (1.5 W cm−2) at indicated time points, and the intratumoral temperature was recorded using a thermal infrared imaging instrument.

Biocompatibility of hPFL@Lipo in vivo

Following intravenous injection of hPFL@Lipo via the tail vein, blood samples were collected from healthy mice at the 3-day time point. Subsequently, these samples were analyzed using an MNCHIP POINTCARE Blood Biochemistry Analyzer and an MC-6200VET Auto Hematology Analyzer. Blood from untreated mice served as the control.

Hemolysis assay

Fresh blood from healthy BALB/c mice was centrifuged (3000 rpm, 10 min) in anticoagulated tubes, which were then washed several times with PBS to obtain red blood cells (RBCs). Then equal amounts of RBCs were dispersed in PBS containing a range of concentrations of hPFL@Lipo (from 0 to 300 mg L−1). In addition, RBCs dispersed in PBS and 1% Triton X-100 were set as the negative and positive control, respectively. After incubation for 4 h at 37 °C with shaking at 300 rpm, the RBCs were centrifuged and supernatant absorbance was determined at 540 nm. The percentage of hemolysis was calculated as the following formula: hemolysis rate (%) = (absorbance of sample - absorbance of negative control)/(absorbance of positive control - absorbance of negative control) × 100.

Animal models

Mice were obtained from Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. All animal studies were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the Animal Experiment Center of Huazhong University of Science and Technology, Wuhan, China. All mice were housed in the specific pathogen-free (SPF) animal room of Tong Ji Medical College (Wuhan, China). All animal experiments were performed according to the guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals (ethics committee of Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology; 4233).

Subcutaneous tumor model

5 × 105 CT26 cells or 4T1 cells were subcutaneously injected into the right flank of 6-week-old female BALB/c mice. When the tumor reached approximately 100 mm3, all mice were given to different treatments. The tumor size was measured using calipers, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula: V = (width2 × length)/2. Mouse body weight was also recorded.

For L-Arg supplementation in vivo, mice were treated with 0.3% L-Arg in drinking water. Mice in αPD-1 group were intravenously injected with αPD-1 (1.5 mg kg−1). ABH group mice were treated with plain drinking water and intraperitoneally injected with 200 μg of ABH per mouse every other day, for a total of 3 injections. Mice in PTT group were intravenously injected with PB nanoparticles (5 mg kg−1) and subjected to 808 nm NIR irradiation (1.5 W cm−2) for 4 min, 24 h post injection. Mice in Fe3+ group received intratumoral injections of 50 μL of 5 mM FeCl3 ·6H2O every 3 days, for a total of 3 injections. ELISA was employed to detect the levels of TNF-α and IFN-γ in tumor homogenates according to the manufacturer's instructions.

For the antitumor efficiency of hPFL@lipo detection, mice were randomly divided into 3 groups (PBS, hPFL@Lipo, hPFL@Lipo + L). 100 μL of PBS or hPFL@Lipo (7.5 mg kg−1) was administered intravenously via tail vein. For the irradiated groups, the tumor tissue was subjected to 808 nm NIR irradiation (1.5 W cm−2) for 4 min 24 h post-injection. When the tumor volume exceeded 2000 mm3, the animals were sacrificed, the survival rate in each group was recorded, and the major tissues (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were also collected for H&E staining.

For evaluation of long-term antitumor immune memory, mice were divided into three groups: untreated, hPB@Lipo + L, and hPFL@Lipo + L. Mice in the hPB@Lipo + L and hPFL@Lipo + L groups were first inoculated subcutaneously with CT26 tumor cells (5 × 105). On day 7 post-inoculation, the mice received intratumoral injection of hPB@Lipo (10 mg kg−1) or hPFL@Lipo (7.5 mg kg−1), followed by 808 nm NIR irradiation (1.5 W cm−2, 5 min) to achieve complete tumor regression. The untreated group received neither tumor inoculation nor treatment during this phase. On day 60 after the initial treatment, all mice were rechallenged with CT26 tumor cells (5 × 105) on the contralateral side, and tumor growth was monitored. At the end of the observation period, mice were euthanized, and splenocytes were collected for flow cytometric analysis of effector memory T cells (CD44+CD62L−CD8+).

For antitumor efficiency detection of hPFL@lipo combined with αPD-1, mice were randomly divided into 6 groups (PBS, hPFL@Lipo, hPB@Lipo + L, hPFL@Lipo + L, hPB@Lipo + L + αPD-1, hPFL@Lipo + L + αPD-1). 100 μL of PBS, hPB@Lipo, or hPFL@Lipo (3.75 mg kg−1) were administered intravenously via tail vein and αPD-1 (1.5 mg kg−1) was administered intraperitoneally. For the irradiated groups, the tumor tissue was subjected to 808 nm NIR irradiation (1.5 W cm−2) for 4 min 24 h post-injection. Tumor tissues were collected, photographed, weighed, and sectioned for Ki67 staining, H&E staining, and NO detection. Flow cytometry was employed to detect tumor-infiltrating M1 macrophages and CD8+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, IFN-γ+ T cells, M1 macrophages, and M2 macrophages according to the methods described below. ELISA was employed to detect the levels of IL-12 and TNF-α in tumor homogenates according to the manufacturer's instructions.

For flow cytometry in vivo, tumor tissues collected from mice were minced with scissors and homogenized into single cells with 1 mg mL−1 collagenase IV, 0.1 mg mL−1 hyaluronidase, and 0.1 mg mL−1 DNase I in DMEM for 1.5 h at 37 °C. Then the cells were filtered through a 70 μm nylon filter strainer, lysed with red blood cell lysate, blocked with anti-CD16/32 antibody for 15 min at 4 °C, and adjusted to a 100 μL solution containing 1 × 106 cells in a vial. For M1 and M2 phenotype macrophage detection, the cells were treated following the protocols mentioned above. For CD8+ and CD4+ T cells detection, the cells were stained with FITC-anti-CD3, PE-anti-CD4, and APC-anti-CD8 antibodies. For IFN-γ+ T cells detection, the cells were stained with FITC-anti-CD3, APC-anti-CD8, and PE-anti granzyme B. After that, the cells were washed with PBS followed by flow cytometry.

Abscopal effect detection model

8 × 105 CT26 cells were injected subcutaneously into the right flank of BALB/c mice to serve as the primary tumor, and 2 × 105 CT26 cells were injected subcutaneously into the left flank to serve as the distal tumor. When the volume of the right tumor reached approximately 100 mm3, Mice were randomly divided into 3 groups (n = 5): PBS, hPB@Lipo + L + αPD-1, hPFL@Lipo + L + αPD-1. 100 μL of PBS, hPFL@Lipo, or hPB@Lipo (5 mg kg−1) were administered intravenously via tail vein on Day 1. For the irradiated groups, the right tumor tissue was irradiated with 808 nm NIR irradiation (1.5 W cm−2, 4 min) 12 h post injection. αPD-1 (1.5 mg kg−1) was injected intravenously on Day 2. The size of the left tumor was measured every two days. On Day 14, mice were sacrificed, the distal tumors were collected, photographed, and weighed, and the draining lymph nodes were collected. DCs and CD8+ T cells infiltrated in the lymph nodes were analyzed by flow cytometry.

Liver metastasis model

On day −8, 5 × 105 CT26 cells were subcutaneously inoculated into the right flank of 6-week-old female BALB/c mice. When the tumor volume reached approximately 100 mm3 (designated as day −3), the tumor-bearing mice were randomly divided into two groups. One group underwent surgical resection of the subcutaneous tumors, while the other group received an intravenous injection of hPFL@Lipo (5 mg kg−1), followed by 808 nm NIR irradiation (1.5 W cm−2) for 4 min at 12 h post-injection. αPD-1 (1.5 mg kg−1) was injected intravenously on day −2.

On day 0, a CRC liver metastasis model was established using three categories of mice: healthy tumor-free mice, tumor-free mice that had survived after surgical resection, and mice treated with hPFL@Lipo plus 808 nm NIR irradiation. Briefly, the mice were anesthetized and a small laparotomy was performed to expose the spleen. A suspension of 5 × 105 CT26 cells in 100 μL was intrasplenically injected. The injection site was gently compressed with a cotton swab for 5 min to ensure adequate cell delivery into the hepatic portal circulation. The abdominal incision was then sutured and disinfected with iodine.

The body weight of the mice was recorded every 4 days. On day 16, all mice were euthanized. The abdominal circumference was measured, and the livers and spleens were collected. The excised livers and spleens were photographed and weighed. Tissue sections were prepared for H&E staining, and the relative metastatic area in both the liver and spleen was quantified from the H&E-stained sections. Furthermore, splenic tissues were digested into single-cell suspensions stained with FITC-anti-CD8, APC-anti-CD62L, and PE-anti-CD44 for the analysis of T central memory (Tcm) cells by flow cytometry.

FACS analysis of antitumor immune response in vivo

Tumor tissues from mice in each group were minced with scissors and incubated with a digestion enzyme mixture (1 mg mL−1 Collagenase IV, 0.1 mg mL−1 Hyaluronidase, 0.1 mg mL−1 DNase I) at 37 °C for 1.5 h. Subsequently, the samples were filtered through a 70 μm nylon mesh, red blood cells were lysed with red blood cell lysis buffer, and non-specific binding was blocked with anti-CD16/32 antibody at 4 °C for 15 min. The samples were then adjusted to a 100 μL solution containing 1 × 106 cells in a vial. For the detection of M1 macrophages, cells were stained with FITC-anti-F4/80, PE-anti-CD11b, and APC-anti-CD86. For the detection of M2 macrophages, after being stained with FITC-anti-F4/80 and PE-anti-CD11b, the cells were fixed and permeabilized followed by the instructions of Fixation/Permeabilization Solution Kit. Then the cells were washed and stained with APC-anti-CD206 for 30 min. For the detection of CD8+ T cells, cells were stained with FITC-anti-CD3, PE-anti-CD4, and APC-anti-CD8 for 30 min. For the detection of CD8+ IFN-γ+ T cells, cells were stained with FITC-anti-CD3, PE-anti-IFN-γ, and APC-anti-CD8 for 30 min. Finally, cells were resuspended in PBS and analyzed by FACS.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± s.d. The difference between the two groups were performed by the Student's t-test. The significances among groups were detected by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001 was considered statistically significant.
