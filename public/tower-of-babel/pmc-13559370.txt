Intraoperative nerve monitoring remains associated with reduced recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy after robot-assisted minimally invasive esophagectomy: response to the editorial commentary
Authors: Takashi Shigeno, Keisuke Okuno, Kazuya Yamaguchi, Taichi Ogo, Kenro Kawada, Hisashi Fujiwara, Kiyohide Fushimi, Yusuke Kinugasa
Published: 2026

We sincerely thank Dr. Lammes and colleagues for their thoughtful and insightful Editorial Commentary on our recent article, “Intraoperative Recurrent Laryngeal Nerve Monitoring for Esophagectomy: A National Cohort Study” (1). We greatly appreciate their careful evaluation of our work and their constructive discussion regarding the evolving role of intraoperative nerve monitoring (IONM) to reduce recurrent laryngeal nerve (RLN) palsy, one of the most clinically significant complications following minimally invasive esophagectomy (MIE) with three-field lymphadenectomy.

As highlighted in the commentary, meticulous lymph node dissection along the RLN is essential for achieving optimal oncological outcomes in patients with esophageal cancer. However, such procedures inherently carry the risk of RLN palsy which may subsequently lead to aspiration pneumonia and respiratory morbidity. In this context, we believe our nationwide cohort study provides important real-world evidence supporting the potential clinical utility of IONM in esophageal cancer surgery.

In their commentary, the authors acknowledged the potential value of IONM in reducing postoperative RLN palsy and respiratory complications while appropriately highlighting several important limitations of our study, including the lack of detailed information regarding patient selection, IONM implementation, and the inherent limitations of retrospective database analyses. We fully agree that these issues should be carefully considered when interpreting our findings. Our study was conducted using the Japanese nationwide Diagnosis Procedure Combination (DPC) database (2-5). DPC database is an administrative claims database derived from a nationwide reimbursement system and enables large-scale multicenter analyses that reflect real-world clinical practice. However, it does not contain detailed information regarding IONM implementation, including the monitoring modality (intermittent or continuous), device specifications, standardized monitoring protocols, intraoperative loss of signal, surgeon experience, or institutional learning curves. Therefore, our study was not designed to determine the optimal technical application of IONM itself. Furthermore, patient selection criteria for IONM could not be adequately addressed. Although we performed propensity score matching to reduce measured confounding, residual selection bias may still have remained. Nevertheless, our study included 4,855 patients from 348 institutions and demonstrated that IONM use during MIE was associated with lower incidences of postoperative RLN palsy and respiratory complications. We believe that these findings provide meaningful evidence regarding the potential clinical utility of IONM in routine surgical practice.

Importantly, the commentary raised a particularly relevant question regarding whether the advancement of robot-assisted minimally invasive esophagectomy (RAMIE), which offers improved visualization and dexterity, may reduce the baseline risk of RLN palsy (6) and thereby diminish the additional value of IONM. To address this concern, we performed an additional subgroup analysis limited to patients who underwent RAMIE. Because restricting the analysis to robotic cases substantially reduced the sample size and limited patient retention after matching, we adopted inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) rather than propensity score matching. After weighting, excellent covariate balance was achieved (all weighted standardized differences <0.1). Table 1 shows that, even within the robotic subgroup, IONM remained associated with a significantly lower incidence of postoperative RLN palsy [average treatment effect on the treated (ATET), −8.96%; 95% confidence interval (CI): −12.25 to −5.67]. Similarly, IONM was also associated with a significantly reduced incidence of postoperative respiratory complications (ATET, −6.52%; 95% CI: −12.07 to −0.97). These findings suggest that although robotic technology may reduce the technical difficulty of dissection and lower the baseline risk of nerve palsy, IONM may still provide additional clinical value in procedures requiring meticulous dissection around the RLN. This may be particularly relevant in technically demanding situations, including three-field lymphadenectomy and patients undergoing extensive upper mediastinal or cervical dissection.

At the same time, our findings should not be interpreted as supporting routine use of IONM in all esophagectomy procedures. Rather, we consider IONM to be one possible strategy for RLN preservation, whose optimal indications remain to be established. As noted by Dr. Lammes et al., future high-quality prospective studies should further clarify the interaction between IONM and RAMIE, compare intermittent and continuous monitoring approaches, and identify patient populations most likely to benefit. Standardization of monitoring protocols and accumulation of experience across high-volume centers will be essential for further validating its clinical utility.

Once again, we sincerely thank Dr. Lammes and colleagues for their constructive comments and valuable insights on our work. We hope that this discussion contributes to further improvement in surgical safety and reduction of RLN-related morbidity in esophageal cancer surgery.
