Resting energy expenditure based on equation estimation can predict renal outcomes in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus and biopsy-proven diabetic kidney disease
Authors: Xiang Xiao, Shuming Ji, Junlin Zhang, Deying Kang, Fang Liu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsThe aim of this study was to investigate the relationship between resting energy expenditure (REE) based on equation estimation and renal outcomes in patients with diabetes kidney disease (DKD).MethodsA total of 124 patients were enrolled from a retrospective cohort of Type 2 Diabetes mellitus (T2DM) patients with biopsy-proven DKD. Renal outcome defined as End-Stage Renal Disease (ESRD). To compare the predictive ability of different REE estimation equations on ESRD. Patients’ REE was assessed according to the estimating equation with the best predictive power, and then the relationship between REE and ESRD risk was fitted using a restricted cubic spline curve (RCS) plot and REE cutoff values were obtained. Grouping using cutoff values, and ultimately evaluate the relationship between REE and the risk of ESRD using a Multivariate Cox regression model.ResultsThe strongest predictive validity for renal outcomes was the NDCKD-equation. The patients were divided into the higher-REE group (n = 78) and the lower-REE group (n = 46), based on the cutoff value. During the follow-up, 30 of 124 patients (24.2%) proceeded to ESRD. Multivariate Cox regression models showed that the risk of ESRD in patients with lower REE was 6.08 times increased compared with that in those with higher REE (HR = 6.08; 95% CI, 1.28–28.80, p = 0.023).ConclusionThese findings suggested that the lower REE was an independent risk factor for unfavorable renal outcomes in patients with DKD.Keywords: Diabetic kidney disease, resting energy expenditure, outcome assessment, risk factor

Introduction

According to the International Diabetes Federation (IDF), the number of adults with diabetes worldwide is projected to exceed 537 million by 2021, and this number is expected to increase to 783 million by 2045 [1]. Diabetic kidney disease (DKD) serves as a primary microvascular complication of diabetes and has emerged as a leading cause of chronic kidney disease (CKD) and end-stage renal disease (ESRD), causing a significant burden on global society and economy [2–4].

Currently, the primary strategy for managing DKD revolves around the modulation of risk factors, including glycemic control, blood pressure management, lipid control, and dietary protein restriction, to achieve the stated goals. Additionally, the use of renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system inhibitors (RAASi), glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRA), and sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) has shown renoprotective effects. Despite the availability of more options, a significant number of patients still progress to ESRD [1]. Therefore, there is an urgent requirement to deeply explore the pathogenesis of DKD to identify more potent intervention targets.

Resting energy expenditure (REE), commonly known as basal metabolic rate (BMR), is the amount of energy the body consumes while at rest after a period of fasting for more than 2 h, in a supine position, and at a suitable temperature for 30 min. This energy expenditure is essential for the maintenance of cellular and organ functions, as well as for sustaining the body’s level of alertness [5]. It is widely acknowledged that REE is determined by biochemical reactions occurring at the subcellular and cellular levels [5]. A growing number of studies have focused on the REE of CKD and T2DM [6–8]. Increased REE frequently observed in patients with T2DM [9] and T2DM with CKD [10], while decreased in CKD without diabetes [11]. Most of the studies have concerned the establishment of energy equations and assessment of their accuracy in CKD [12–15]. Although numerous studies has been done, it is still unclear whether there are any relationships between REE and outcomes of DKD.

REE is increasingly considered to be significantly associated with the outcomes of the disease. A study showed that higher REE was associated with higher mortality and cardiovascular death in ESRD patients on continuous ambulatory peritoneal dialysis (CAPD) [16]. However, there are two different conclusions from studies in the field of oncology. Some studies suggested that lower REE was better for the prognosis of patients with tumors [17], while others indicated that higher REE was worse [18]. Furthermore, existing therapeutic strategies for DKD, including low-protein diet and SGLT2i, have demonstrated enhancements in REE, which has been demonstrated in studies [19–21]. However, the correlation between REE and DKD remains unclear.

Therefore, the objective of this retrospective cohort study was to assess the predictive value of REE based on the energy equation as a noninvasive biomarker for renal outcomes in patients with T2DM and biopsy-proven DKD.

Methods

Study participants

T2DM patients with biopsy-confirmed DKD between April 2009 and December 2019 were included in this retrospective cohort study. The diagnosis and classification of T2DM adhere to the standards set by the American Diabetes Association (ADA) [22], while DKD diagnosis follows the criteria defined by the Renal Pathology Society (RPS) in 2010 [23]. Inclusion criteria: (1) aged 18 years or older, (2) diagnosed with T2DM, and (3) confirmed diagnosis of DKD through renal biopsy. Exclusion criteria: (1) a malignant tumor, (2) other coexisting glomerular diseases, (3) chronic inflammatory disease include metabolic dysfunction associated steatohepatitis (MASH) and autoimmune diseases, (4) active infectious chronic liver disease (a. Active Hepatitis B, b. Active Hepatitis C, c.Liver Cirrhosis; meet any of the above), (5) chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), (6) e-GFR <15mL/min/1.73 m2 or undergoing dialysis, or (7) incomplete data. The study was in compliance with the ethical principles outlined in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent revisions. Prior to hospitalization, all individual patients provided written informed consent.

Clinical and pathologic characteristics

After the renal biopsy was performed, clinical and pathologic data were collected from the electronic medical record. Subsequent follow-ups of these patients were performed 2–4 times per year based on the patient’s condition. Renal outcome was defined as ESRD, which means the requirement for renal replacement therapy (hemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis or kidney transplant) or/and an e-GFR <15 mL/min/1.73 m2. The e-GFR was estimated from the CKD-EPI equation, a creatinine-based calculation developed by the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaborative. The REE was calculated by Harris-Benedict equation (HARRIS) [24], Mifflin-St Jeor equation [25], WHO/FAO/UNU (WHO) equation [26], eREE-CKD (CKD) equation [13], and ND-CKD (NDCKD) equation [14], respectively. (Table S1) Histological aberrations were assessed in accordance with the RPS criteria [23]. The thickness of glomerular basement membrane (GBM) in samples designated for electron microscopy (EM) examination using Haas’ direct measurement/arithmetic mean methodology [27].

Statistical analysis

The continuous variables were described as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), and categorical data were presented as frequency combined with percentages. The time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (time-ROC) curve and time-dependent concordance index (time-C-index) were used to evaluate the prognostic accuracy of different energy calculation equations. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) was used to calculate the cutoff value. To determine the significance of variations in characteristics between two groups, statistical tests such as t-tests, Mann-Whitney U tests, and chi-square tests were used. Additionally, Pearson or Spearman correlation analysis was conducted to explore the correlations between REE and clinical and pathological characteristics. The renal survival curves were evaluated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. To decrease bias induced by age and e-GFR on renal outcomes, we categorized patients by age into elderly (age ≥65 years) and non-elderly (age <65 years) and classified them by e-GFR into the CKD stable stage group (CKD stages 1–3a, eGFR ≥45 mL/min/1.73m2) and CKD rapid progression group (CKD stage 3b-5, eGFR <45 mL/min/1.73m2). Furthermore, the impact of REE on renal outcomes was analyzed using Cox proportional hazard models. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 and R version 4.1.3, incorporating the survival, time-ROC, time-C-index, and survminer packages. Statistically significant was defined as a two-sided p-value <0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 124 patients were enrolled from a retrospective cohort of T2DM patients with biopsy-proven DKD (Figure 1). The baseline characteristics of the 124 patients are shown in Table 1. Men account for 85.5%. The mean age was 50.13 years. The median follow-up time was 27 months (12–42 months). The median diabetes duration was 96 months (36–132 months). The average serum creatinine was 1.44 ± 0.68 mg/dl. The median e-GFR was 60.43 mL/min/1.73m2 (46.02–94.03 mL/min/1.73m2). The median of proteinuria was 2.17 g/d (0.88–4.19g/d). As for the pathological characteristics, the glomerular class lesions were classified into categories I, IIa, IIb, III, and IV, accounting for 8.06% (10), 23.39% (29), 20.16% (25), 34.68% (43), and 13.71% (17), respectively. Among the cohort of 124 patients, for whom GBM thickness measurements were accessible, the average GBM thickness was determined to be 851.33 ± 381.07 nm. 77.42% (96) were administered RAASi. The WHO equation estimated the highest REE (1679.01 kcal/day (1441.345–1775.82) kcal/day), while the HARRIS equation estimated the lowest REE (755.43 ± 187.40 kcal/day).

Comparative efficacy of diverse prediction equations for ESRD

The Area Under Curve (AUC) was used to assess the ability of REE equations to predict the risk of ESRD (Figure 2). The NDCKD equation had the strongest predictive power for renal outcome (Figure 3A, B). It presented an average AUC value of 0.761 and a cutoff value of 1464.51 kcal/day (Figure 3C, D). The risk of ESRD was decreased with increasing REE (Figure 3D).

Subsequently, patients were divided into the higher-REE group (REE >1464.51 kcal/day, n = 78) and the lower-REE group (REE ≤1464.51 kcal/day, n = 46). DKD patients in the low REE group showed older age and significantly higher levels of serum creatinine, proteinuria, and systolic blood pressure (SBP) (All p < 0.05). Additionally, individuals within the lower REE exhibited lower levels of e-GFR, hemoglobin, coupled with more severe glomerular damage and interstitial inflammation than the higher REE group (All p < 0.05). Moreover, there was a higher proportion of female in the group with lower REE (p < 0.05) (Table 1).

Clinical and pathological features associated with REE

As shown in (Table 2), REE was not associated with the incidence of diabetic retinopathy, hypertension, duration of diabetes, HbA1c, total cholesterol, or GBM thickness. REE showed a negatively correlated with both age (r = −0.353, p < 0.001) and serum creatinine (r = −0.375, p < 0.001), whilst demonstrating a positive correlation with BMI (r = 0.663, p < 0.001), e-GFR (r = 0.591, p < 0.001), serum albumin (r = 0.274, p = 0.002), triglycerides (r = 0.197, p = 0.029), and hemoglobin (r = 0.638, p < 0.001). It showed that the patients with younger age and lower creatinine had higher REE, whereas the patients with higher BMI, e-GFR, serum albumin, triglycerides, and hemoglobin had higher REE. Regarding pathological characteristics, REE was significantly negatively correlated with glomerular class (r = −0.241, p = 0.008), interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy (IFTA) (r = −0.374, p < 0.001), interstitial inflammation (r = −0.360, p = 0.001), and arteriolar hyalinosis (r = −0.268, p = 0.004). It showed that REE is lower in DKD patients with more severe pathological changes (Table 2).

Risks of progression to ESRD

During the follow-up period, 22 of 46 (47.82%) patients with lower REE progressed to ESRD, whereas only 8 of 78 (10.26%) with higher REE values progressed to ESRD. The five-year renal survival rates in the low REE group were 52.17% (24 out of 46) and 93.59% (73 out of 78) in the higher REE groups. The Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed that the patients with higher REE had better renal outcomes (Log-rank p < 0.001) (Figure 4). Additional analysis by subgrouping patients based on different age and e-GFR using Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated that among patients aged ≥60 years and those aged <60 years, those with higher REE had a significantly lower risk of ESRD. Similarly, in subgroups with an e-GFR ≥45 mL/min/1.73m2 and <45 mL/min/1.73m2, patients with higher REE also exhibited a lower risk of ESRD (Figure 5).

Univariate Cox analyses demonstrated hemoglobin, serum albumin, urinary protein, serum creatinine, glomerular classification, IFTA, interstitial inflammation, arteriolar hyalinosis, and renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system inhibitors (RAASi) utilization were risk factors for ESRD progression (All p < 0.05). After adjustment for baseline age, gender, BMI, hypertension, duration of DM, e-GFR, proteinuria, hemoglobin, serum albumin, glomerular class, IFTA, and RAASi use (in model 3), remained an independent risk factor for progression of DKD to ESRD. In comparison to the higher REE group, the risk of ESRD in patients with lower REE was 6.08 times increased compared with that in those with higher REE (HR = 6.08; 95% CI, 1.28-28.80, p = 0.023). Furthermore, lower serum albumin and RAASi not used were also independent risk factors for progression of DKD to ESRD. (Table S2, Figure 6)

Discussion

In this study, it was shown that REE assessed based on the REE estimation equation can be used to predict the risk of ESRD in patients with DKD. As REE increased, the risk of ESRD in DKD patients decreased.

Patients with DKD are often advised to strict dietary restrictions, including low protein and low-fat diets to slow down the deterioration of renal function, as well as low carbohydrate diets to maintain optimal blood glucose levels. Adhering to these principles inevitably leads to a significant increase in the prevalence of protein-energy wasting (PEW). However, the evidence demonstrates a close association between PEW and the incidence, mortality, and decline in quality of life among patients with CKD [28]. The nutritional consensus statements advocate for an energy intake of 25-35 kcal/kg/d for patients with CKD [29]. Despite this, a multitude of studies have showed that the energy intake of patients with CKD [30] and T2DM [31] are seriously insufficient. However, individually characterizing energy intake for each patient can be a huge challenge. To address this issue, it was advisable to measure REE via indirect calorimetry (IC) [32] in order to more accurately estimate the total energy consumption. However, IC measurements require specialized equipment, skilled personnel, and optimal environmental conditions, making it unsuitable for widespread dissemination. In situations where direct calorimetry was not feasible, the use of energy prediction equations was considered a reasonable approach [29]. In order to establish a straightforward, noninvasive, and cost-effective predictive marker, we used the energy equation to calculate REE in our study.

We evaluated REE in patients with DKD using five different REE estimation equations. Of the five equations we chose, the eREE-CKD equation and the ND-CKD equation were the most recently published [13,14]. The advantage of these two equations is that diabetes and e-GFR are included as crucial factors in the estimation of REE [13,14]. Therefore, the use of these two equations to assess REE in patients with DKD is more accurate than other equations. In our findings, by evaluating the predictive efficiency of several equations, we found that the ND-CKD equation was the best predictor of renal outcomes. Therefore, we used the ND-CKD equation as the primary focus for studying the correlation between REE and renal outcomes in DKD patients.

Our findings indicated that calculation of REE using the ND-CKD equation had the strongest predictive value for predicting renal prognosis in patients with DKD. The equation includes e-GFR, but it does not imply that e-GFR can replace REE. There is a fundamental distinction between the two, as e-GFR serves as a measure of renal function, while REE, as a comprehensive measure of metabolism, requires the integration of factors such as age, gender, weight, and e-GFR. Furthermore, our results indicated that equations that do not include e-GFR, such as the CKD equation, still possess commendable predictive ability for renal outcomes, highlighting the distinction between the two. Additionally, our subgroup analysis of patients with different ages and e-GFR levels demonstrated that patients with lower REE, regardless of age or e-GFR, had a higher risk of ESRD.

Previous studies have consistently demonstrated that REE is influenced by various factors, including age, gender, and body mass index [33]. Our observational findings were consistent with the correlation between REE and BMI, demonstrating a positive association. Furthermore, males generally had higher values than females. Upon investigating the relationship between REE and nutritional status in middle-aged and elderly hemodialysis patients, they discovered a positive correlation between REE and serum albumin levels [34], and our study comes to the same result. N Vaisman et al. [35] demonstrated that correcting anemia in patients with congestive heart failure (CHF) leads to an increase in REE, and our study also showed that the level of hemoglobin and REE showed a positive correlation. Prior investigations proposed a decrease in REE among CKD patients [11], a phenomenon linked to the deterioration of renal function [36]. Additionally, we observed a positive correlation between REE and serum creatinine levels, while eGFR showed a negative correlation. Furthermore, we investigated the relationship between REE and the pathologic characteristics of the patients. A negative correlation was found between glomerulopathy, IFTA, interstitial inflammation, arteriolar hyalinization and REE. Therefore, we propose that a decrease in REE be associated to deteriorating renal function and/or more severe renal pathological changes.

Some studies have suggested that higher REE might be beneficial to the prognosis of the disease [18]. This is the same as our study. It is well known that the low protein diet can delay the progression of kidney disease [37]. Two studies found that the low protein diet could increase REE, the effect may be due to the diet, keto analogs, or transamination reaction [19,20]. It suggests that the mechanism of the low protein diet delaying the progression of kidney disease may be realized by increasing REE. In addition, a study found that SGLT2 inhibitors may have therapeutic potential for prediabetic obesity by upregulating energy expenditure and reducing the insulin-to-glucagon ratio and the activation of the AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK)/sirtuin 1 (SIRT1) pathway in the liver and adipose tissue [21]. Another study reported that IL-27 signaling promotes adipocyte thermogenesis and energy expenditure, which provides a new therapeutic target for obesity treatment [38]. To summarize, these studies suggest that the regulation of REE may become a new promising target for DKD treatment.

However, it is unknown exactly why REE is associated with the progression of DKD. Firstly, REE serves as an indicator of the body’s basal metabolism, which is influenced by organ function and cellular activity. A decline in e-GFR signifies irreversible death of more renal unit working cells, leading to decreased metabolism in these dying cells. Consequently, the overall basal metabolism of the kidney decreases, thereby affecting the overall REE [5]. Secondly, previous reports have demonstrated alterations in energy metabolism-related genes in type 2 diabetes patients, which are closely associated with mitochondrial function. Therefore, REE levels may partly reflect the mitochondrial function of patients and thus be linked to the occurrence and progression of DKD [39,40]. However, the mechanisms by which REE operates in the development of the disease remain unclear, necessitating further research.

Of course, there are some limitations in this study. Firstly, our study was a retrospective study, so there were some inevitable selection biases. In fact, the patients were biopsy-proven diabetic kidney disease, and the sample size was insufficient. Secondly, the measurement of REE was based on the energy equation, which was less accurate than RRE measurement by indirect calorimetry. Meanwhile, the ND-CKD equation shows a decrease in the ability of long-term prediction, which may be related to the predictive ability of a baseline marker value may become weaker as the target time gets further from the baseline [41]. Finally, we did not control therapeutic interventions which could be confounders to the results. The patients included in this study generally have more severe proteinuria, so the research results may be more suitable for patients with more severe proteinuria.

In conclusion, we need to pay more attention to the REE of DKD patients, which is closely related to PEW and renal outcomes. REE can be used to screen for patients with DKD at high risk of incidence of ESRD.
