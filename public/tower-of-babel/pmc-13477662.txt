Screening of Metabolic Reprogramming-Related Diagnostic Biomarkers and Analysis of Immune Heterogeneity in Atopic Dermatitis Based on Multi-Cohort Validation
Authors: Bingwei Zhi, Xin Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAtopic dermatitis (AD), often called eczema, is a long-lasting skin condition that causes severe itching and distress for many people. While we know it involves the immune system and the skin barrier, we still do not fully understand how changes in how skin cells process energy affect the disease. We wanted to look into these energy processes to find better ways to help people living with eczema and see how their experiences differ from people with other skin conditions like psoriasis.MethodsWe gathered and analyzed genetic data shared by patients and healthy volunteers across multiple study groups. Instead of looking at thousands of genes at once, we focused on groups of genes that metabolic reprogramming-related. We used computer models to see if specific genes could help identify people with eczema. We also studied how these genes relate to the immune cells present in the skin, and used this information to see if we could group patients into different molecular types.ResultsWe discovered two specific genes, called RRM2 and HMMR, that are highly active in people living with eczema. These two genes successfully helped us distinguish people with eczema from both healthy individuals and people living with psoriasis. Patients with higher activity in these genes also showed a stronger presence of active immune cells in their skin. By looking at these patterns, we found that people with eczema generally fall into one of two distinct groups, each having its own unique immune features.ConclusionThis study identifies RRM2 and HMMR as promising candidate biomarkers associated with metabolic reprogramming in AD and reveals distinct immune heterogeneity and potential molecular subtypes, offering a preliminary framework for further exploration of subtype-guided strategies in AD. If further validated, these findings may assist in differential diagnosis and guide subtype‑specific therapeutic strategies for patients with AD.Keywords: atopic dermatitis, metabolic reprogramming, immune infiltration, diagnostic biomarkers, multi - cohort validation, RRM2, HMMR

Introduction

Atopic dermatitis (AD) is a relapsing, chronic, non-communicable inflammatory skin disease featuring persistent pruritus.1 Predominantly affecting the pediatric population,2,3 AD imposes a substantial physical, psychological, and socioeconomic burden. The pathogenesis of the disease arises from a complex interplay of multiple factors under a background of genetic susceptibility, including epidermal barrier dysfunction, immune dysregulation—primarily driven by Type 2 inflammation—and microbial dysbiosis.4,5 Notably, epidermal barrier defects resulting from loss-of-function mutations in the FLG gene, which encodes filaggrin, constitute a fundamental pathological cornerstone.6 Furthermore, the excessive release of cytokines such as IL-4, IL-13, and the pruritogenic factor IL-31, mediated by Th2 cells, drives a vicious cycle of persistent inflammation and pruritus.7 Although the clinical manifestations of AD evolve across different age groups, intractable pruritus and sleep disturbances remain hallmark features throughout all stages, severely impairing patients’ quality of life and psychosocial functioning.8 Additionally, patients often present with clinically significant symptoms of anxiety or depression.9 While basic skin care and standardized topical corticosteroids or calcineurin inhibitors remain first-line interventions,10 targeted biologics and JAK inhibitors have emerged as pivotal systemic therapies capable of modifying the disease course in moderate-to-severe cases.11,12 Consequently, a profound understanding of the molecular heterogeneity, multidimensional pathogenesis, and clinical phenotypes of AD is imperative for the development of precision medicine and the improvement of long-term patient prognoses.

Metabolic reprogramming refers to the adaptive process by which cells modify the flux and rate of metabolic pathways to shift from homeostatic patterns to pathological or compensatory states that meet specific functional demands under fluctuating microenvironments, such as hypoxia, nutrient deprivation, or pathological conditions.13 Originally identified as a hallmark of malignancy, metabolic reprogramming is exemplified by the Warburg effect, wherein tumor cells prioritize aerobic glycolysis over oxidative phosphorylation to procure the energy and biosynthetic precursors necessary for rapid proliferation.14 Recent advancements have increasingly demonstrated that metabolic reprogramming is not exclusive to oncology but also plays a pivotal pathogenic role in various chronic inflammatory diseases,15 with AD emerging as a prominent area of investigation. In the epidermis of AD patients, keratinocytes undergo a metabolic shift toward enhanced peroxisomal β-oxidation and anaerobic glycolysis.16 This metabolic recalibration not only fuels keratinocyte proliferation but also profoundly alters epidermal lipid composition and skin barrier integrity. Furthermore, metabolic reprogramming—characterized by branched-chain amino acid depletion and dysregulated tryptophan metabolism—serves as a central bridge in AD pathogenesis by modulating mTOR-mediated Th2 immune responses.17 Specifically, studies have elucidated that lipid metabolic disturbances mediated by the CD36-SREBP1 axis exacerbate AD inflammation in the context of obesity by upregulating thymic stromal lymphopoietin.18 Consequently, targeting metabolic reprogramming is gaining traction as a novel therapeutic frontier in the management of AD.

Notably, the pathological features of AD—such as persistent inflammation and excessive keratinocyte proliferation—are closely linked to alterations in cellular energy metabolism.19 To sustain rapid mitosis, activated cells must fundamentally remodel their biosynthetic and glycolytic networks through metabolic reprogramming.20 Among our candidate proteins, RRM2 (ribonucleotide reductase regulatory subunit M2) and HMMR (hyaluronan-mediated motility receptor) serve as key molecular bridges at this interface. RRM2 directly regulates nucleotide metabolism by catalyzing dNTP synthesis, which is essential for DNA replication.21 HMMR is the major receptor for hyaluronan—a component that is highly remodeled in AD—and activates downstream PI3K/Akt and MAPK signaling pathways, thereby promoting aerobic glycolysis and biomass production.22 Thus, RRM2 and HMMR are not non-specific participants in cell division or systemic inflammation, but rather targeted metabolic adaptors driven by the AD microenvironment. Crucially, this energetic recalibration is intrinsically coupled with pathological tissue remodeling, as emerging evidence highlights that errors in metabolic regulation can impair normal skin regeneration and aberrant wound repair pathways. Recent regenerative studies have also highlighted the interaction between tissue repair pathways, angiogenesis, and cellular signaling in skin regeneration, suggesting that mechanisms involved in tissue remodeling may share biological links with metabolic regulation and inflammatory responses.23

In this study, we aimed to identify metabolic reprogramming-related diagnostic biomarkers for AD and to explore their association with immune heterogeneity. By integrating multi-cohort transcriptomic data, we screened key metabolic genes, evaluated their diagnostic performance, and further characterized AD subtypes based on their expression. Our results reveal new aspects of metabolic-immune interactions in AD and identify potential molecular candidates for precise diagnosis and subtype-guided therapy.

Materials and Methods

Data Download and Processing

Transcriptomic datasets for Atopic Dermatitis (AD) and healthy controls were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/), including GSE32924, GSE120721, GSE36842, GSE6012, GSE107361, GSE16161, and GSE121212 (Table 1). For microarray datasets, probe IDs were mapped to gene symbols using the AnnoProbe package. To handle the multi-probe mapping issue, the expression value of each gene was represented by the probe with the median intensity to ensure robust quantification. For datasets with raw counts or pre-normalized values (GSE6012, GSE121212), a log2(count + 1) transformation was applied to internalize the variance. Although SVA correction successfully addressed the major technical differences across studies, some minor residual non‑biological batch effects may still exist due to differences in microarray platforms and sample processing among the five integrated cohorts. A total of 1804 metabolic reprogramming-related genes (MRGs) were obtained by retrieving and deduplicating genes from seven metabolic superpathways annotated by Reactome pathways from a previous study (Supplementary Table 1).24

Multi-Cohort Integration and Batch Correction

To expand the sample size for subtype identification, five datasets (GSE32924, GSE120721, GSE16161, GSE107361, and GSE121212) were integrated. We only retained genes that were commonly expressed across all five cohorts. To eliminate technical variations (batch effects) while preserving critical biological signals, the ComBat algorithm from the sva R package was implemented. A model matrix including the biological group information (AD vs Control) was incorporated into the ComBat function to ensure that disease-related heterogeneities were not over-corrected. The efficacy of the batch removal was visually inspected and validated using Principal Component Analysis (PCA) via the FactoMineR and factoextra packages before and after the procedure.

Weighted Gene Co-Expression Network Analysis (WGCNA)

Using WGCNA, hierarchical clustering was performed on the top 25% of most variable genes in the training set. A soft threshold of β = 10 (R2 = 0.85) grouped co-expressed genes into modules. The top 25% of genes were selected to remove background noise from low‑variance genes and reduce the computational burden. Pearson correlation linked each module to sample traits, identifying modules significantly associated with disease.

Differential Expression Analysis

To identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs), the limma package was used on the training set, comparing AD and control groups. After normalizing the expression matrix, a linear model was fitted for each gene, and empirical Bayesian adjustment was applied to variance estimates to ensure statistical stability. Genes with |log2(fold change)| > 1 and an adjusted P-value < 0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed. The Benjamini-Hochberg method controlled the false discovery rate. Through this process, DEGs associated with AD were successfully identified.

Construction of Protein-Protein Interaction (PPI) Networks and Functional Enrichment Analysis

A PPI network with high-confidence interactions (score > 0.7) was built via STRING, visualized in Cytoscape (v3.10), and key modules were identified using MCODE. The sub-network extraction was performed using standardized parameters: Degree Cutoff = 2, Node Score Cutoff = 0.2, K-Core = 2, and Max. Depth = 100. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were then conducted with the clusterProfiler R package.

Identification and Validation of Diagnostic Biomarkers

The pROC package was used for Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (ROC) analysis. The diagnostic performance was quantified by the Area Under the Curve (AUC) and its 95% Confidence Interval (CI). The optimal diagnostic threshold (cutoff) was determined by maximizing the Youden Index (sensitivity + specificity - 1). Subsequently, a nomogram-based diagnostic model was built via the rmda package, and its reliability along with clinical utility were evaluated using calibration plots and Decision Curve Analysis (DCA). To confirm the robustness of our results, the diagnostic framework underwent external validation with test sets. Expression boxplots were also plotted to compare diagnostic gene levels between AD and normal samples.

Immune Infiltration Analysis

Immune infiltration was assessed using the MCP-counter algorithm. The GSVA package performed Single Sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) to calculate immune cell scores, comparing immune-related functions and cell infiltration between AD and control groups. Additionally, correlations between AD diagnostic genes and both immune cells and immune functions were analyzed.

Single-Gene Enrichment Analysis

The “c5.go.v2024.1.Hs.symbols” and “c2.cp.kegg_legacy.v2024.1.Hs.symbols.gmt” subsets were downloaded from MSigDB (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/index.jsp), and enrichment analysis of the feature genes was performed using the GSEA package.

Subtype Identification

Using consensus clustering with diagnostic biomarker expression, AD samples were stratified into two distinct subtypes. Principal component analysis (PCA) and differential expression analysis validated the subtype distinction. Immune infiltration patterns specific to each subtype were characterized by ssGSEA and MCP-counter. Differential expression between subtypes was then analyzed, and GO/KEGG enrichment was performed.

Building a ceRNA Network Centered on Diagnostic Genes

Data were retrieved from the miRTarBase database (https://mirtarbase.cuhk.edu.cn/). miRNAs targeting the feature genes were identified, and lncRNAs regulating these miRNAs were subsequently screened. A ceRNA regulatory network (mRNA-miRNA-lncRNA) was then constructed using Cytoscape.

Statistical Analysis

R version 4.4.1 was used for all statistical analyses. Data quality was strictly controlled by removing genes with low expression levels (rowSums < 1). Intergroup differences were examined using the Wilcoxon test, and correlations between two variables were calculated with Spearman and Pearson methods. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05, with P-values categorized as: ****P < 0.0001; ***P < 0.001; **P < 0.01; *P < 0.05; ns, P > 0.05.

Results

Module Identification by WGCNA and Analysis of DEGs

For WGCNA, the top 25% of genes with the highest variance in the training set (GSE107361) were selected. A soft threshold of β = 10 (R2 = 0.85) was applied (Figure 1A). Strongly correlated modules were merged, resulting in 12 modules and a clustering dendrogram (Figure 1B). The module-trait correlation heatmap revealed that the Pink module had a highly significant positive correlation with AD (cor = 0.8, p < 0.0001) (Figure 1C). Module membership in the Pink module was significantly positively correlated with gene significance for AD, indicating that its core genes are strongly associated with the disease phenotype (Figure 1D). The volcano plot showed DEGs between AD and control groups, identifying 530 upregulated and 1032 downregulated genes (Figure 1E).

Screening of Candidate Genes and Functional Enrichment Analysis

The intersection of significantly upregulated genes in AD and genes in the AD-associated Pink module identified by WGCNA analysis yielded 90 AD-related genes (Figure 2A). A high-confidence PPI network (confidence > 0.7) for these genes was constructed using the STRING database and visualized with Cytoscape (Figure 2B). The MCODE plugin identified key module genes, yielding 44 candidate genes (Figure 2C and D). Enrichment analysis was then performed on these candidates. GO analysis indicated significant enrichment in biological processes and pathways including cell cycle and chromosome segregation. KEGG analysis revealed significant enrichment in pathways such as cell cycle, oocyte meiosis, and the p53 signaling pathway (Figure 2E).

Screening and Evaluation of the Diagnostic Value of Feature Genes

The intersection of AD-associated candidate genes with MRGs yielded two genes (RRM2 and HMMR) as diagnostic-related metabolic signature genes (Figure 3A). In the training set, RRM2 achieved an AUC of 0.90 (95% CI: 0.82–0.98), with a sensitivity of 0.79 and a specificity of 0.92 at the optimal cutoff; HMMR achieved an AUC of 0.92 (95% CI: 0.84–1.00), with a sensitivity of 0.93 and a specificity of 0.90 (Figure 3B). Furthermore, to evaluate the discriminatory ability and disease specificity of the identified metabolic signature genes, independent validation was performed comparing AD with psoriasis. RRM2 achieved an AUC of 0.75 (95% CI: 0.60–0.89) in distinguishing AD from psoriasis, while HMMR achieved an AUC of 0.69 (95% CI: 0.55–0.84) (Figure 3C). The results showed that RRM2 and HMMR could effectively distinguish AD from psoriasis, suggesting that these genes represent AD-specific metabolic reprogramming rather than a general inflammatory response. This suggests their potential as promising candidate biomarkers that may assist in differential diagnosis, pending further validation in prospective clinical cohorts. Across four external validation cohorts, the AUC values for the signature genes were all above 0.7, confirming their good diagnostic performance, stability, and reliability (Figure 3D). These genes were also significantly highly expressed in AD samples (Figure 3E). A nomogram incorporating the two signature genes was built to predict patient risk (Figure 3F). The calibration curve showed strong agreement between predicted risk and actual disease prevalence (Figure 3G). DCA indicated that the model’s predicted high-risk group was highly consistent with the actual number of affected individuals, highlighting its clinical relevance (Figure 3H).

Immune Infiltration Analysis

MCP-counter results indicated significant increases in T cells, myeloid dendritic cells, neutrophils, and monocytic lineage, and decreases in NK cells, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells in AD compared to controls (Figure 4A). ssGSEA immune function scores revealed that, except for mast cells (downregulated), most immune-related pathways and cell types—such as DCs, aDCs, Th1, Th2, Tfh, and inflammation-promoting pathways—were significantly higher in the AD group (Figure 4B). Correlation analysis showed positive associations of RRM2 and HMMR with monocytic lineage, myeloid dendritic cells, and T cells, and negative associations with endothelial cells (Figure 4C). Furthermore, the signature genes RRM2 and HMMR were significantly positively correlated with most immune function scores (eg, aDCs, DCs, Th1/Th2 cells, and inflammation-promoting pathways), and only significantly negatively correlated with mast cells (Figure 4D).

HMMR was significantly enriched in chromosome segregation, nuclear division, and spindle microtubule movement according to GSEA (Figure 5A). Additionally, HMMR was closely associated with the cell cycle, NOD-like receptor signaling, and T cell receptor signaling pathways (Figure 5B). RRM2 was significantly upregulated in GO terms such as mitosis, spindle formation, and chromosome segregation (Figure 5C). Analysis of AD Subtypes Associated with Signature Genes

Due to the limited number of AD patient samples in the training set, we merged the training set (GSE107361) with the validation sets (GSE32924, GSE120721, GSE16161, GSE121212) and performed subtype analysis on 97 AD patient samples (Figure 6A). Based on the expression of the two AD signature genes, clustering analysis identified two AD-associated disease subtypes: Cluster 1 contained 39 samples, and Cluster 2 contained 58 samples (Figure 6B–D). PCA results showed a clear separation between the two clusters (Cluster 1 and Cluster 2) along the first principal component axis (Figure 6E). Furthermore, the expression levels of both HMMR and RRM2 were elevated in Cluster 2 (Figure 6F). MCP-counter algorithm assessment revealed that the infiltration proportions of monocytic lineage and neutrophils were significantly higher in Cluster 2 than in Cluster 1 (Figure 6G). ssGSEA score analysis showed that Cluster 2 had significantly higher scores in multiple immune indicators, including inflammation-promoting, dendritic cells (DCs/aDCs/pDCs), B cells, and cytolytic activity, compared with Cluster 1 (Figure 6H). Differential expression analysis was performed between the subtypes (|log2FC| > 0.585, p.adj < 0.05). A total of 82 upregulated genes were identified in Cluster 1, and 258 upregulated genes in Cluster 2 (Figure 6I). Enrichment analysis was subsequently performed on these upregulated genes. The upregulated genes in Cluster 1 were mainly enriched in muscle system processes and muscle contraction, as well as processes such as transmembrane transport. In terms of KEGG pathways, they were primarily involved in circadian rhythm, cadherin signaling, and related xenobiotic and drug metabolism pathways (Figure 6J). The upregulated genes in Cluster 2 were enriched in biological processes including chromosome segregation, organelle fission, and mitosis. KEGG pathway analysis revealed that these genes were highly significantly enriched in cell cycle and DNA replication pathways (Figure 6K).

The constructed ceRNA (lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA) regulatory network comprises a total of 36 lncRNAs, 52 miRNAs, and targeted signature genes (Figure 7). Discussion

In this study, we systematically explored the link between metabolic reprogramming and immune heterogeneity in AD using multi-cohort transcriptomic data. Although metabolic dysregulation is increasingly recognized in chronic inflammatory diseases, its role in AD—particularly regarding diagnostic biomarkers and immune crosstalk—remains poorly defined. By integrating WGCNA, differential expression, and PPI network analyses, we aimed to uncover metabolism reprogramming related molecular signatures and their potential influence on the AD immune microenvironment.

In this study, RRM2 and HMMR were identified as two metabolic reprogramming-related genes with significant diagnostic value for AD. Through multi-cohort validation, these genes demonstrated high accuracy in distinguishing AD from healthy controls and even from psoriasis—a critical differential diagnosis. These findings highlight their potential as robust clinical biomarkers. RRM2 is a rate-limiting enzyme in dNTP synthesis, essential for DNA replication and repair, and also participates in inflammatory responses and immune regulation.21 Previous studies have identified RRM2 as a potential diagnostic biomarker for AD, and its upregulated expression is closely associated with macrophage infiltration, suggesting that it may participate in the chronic inflammatory process of AD by modulating innate immune responses.25 Furthermore, RRM2 is highly expressed in both lesional skin tissues and peripheral blood of AD patients. It exhibits positive correlations with multiple inflammatory signaling pathways, including IL-17, JAK-STAT, NF-κB, TNF-α, chemokines, and MAPK, thereby promoting the production of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, which drive immune cell recruitment and inflammatory amplification effects.26 In summary, RRM2 is a robust metabolic reprogramming-related diagnostic biomarker for AD, highlighting the metabolic-immune crosstalk in this disease. HMMR, as one of the major receptors for hyaluronan (HA), is significantly upregulated under inflammatory conditions and participates in regulating keratinocyte migration and tissue remodelling.27 In atopic dermatitis (AD), HA metabolism undergoes substantial remodelling: the expression of hyaluronan synthase 3 (HAS3) is elevated in keratinocytes, leading to abnormal accumulation of low-molecular-weight HA (LMW-HA) in the epidermal intercellular spaces, and LMW-HA serves as a high-affinity ligand for HMMR.28 HMMR cooperates with CD44 to mediate HA-dependent signalling, activating downstream PI3K/Akt and MAPK/ERK pathways that promote cell survival, proliferation, and migration.22 In cancer studies, this pathway has been shown to enhance glucose uptake, glycolytic flux, and biosynthesis through mTORC1, driving aerobic glycolysis and biomass production.29 Given that AD pathology is characterised by keratinocyte hyperproliferation and persistent inflammation, we speculate that HMMR may promote metabolic reprogramming in AD through a similar PI3K/Akt–mTOR axis, thereby providing the energy and biosynthetic precursors required for rapidly proliferating keratinocytes.

Our immune infiltration analysis revealed significant alterations in the immune landscape of AD patients. Specifically, the proportions of T cells, myeloid dendritic cells, neutrophils, and monocytic lineage were markedly increased in AD lesions. Elevated scores were observed in most immune-related pathways and cell types, including dendritic cells, Th1 cells, Th2 cells, Tfh cells, and inflammation-promoting pathways. AD is a classic Th2-dominant skin disease, whose role is not limited to inflammatory amplification but also deeply involved in skin barrier disruption and the maintenance of chronic immune cycles.26 Highly activated Th2 cells produce IL-4, IL-13, IL-5, and IL-31, driving type 2 immune deviation, directly inhibiting skin barrier protein expression, and inducing the itch-scratch cycle, thereby forming a vicious cycle of inflammation and barrier disruption.30 Dendritic cells play a crucial role in the pathogenesis of AD. As professional antigen-presenting cells, they bridge innate and adaptive immunity.31 In AD, dendritic cells drive Th2 cell polarization via the TSLP-OX40L axis, produce IL-1α and chemokines to amplify inflammatory responses, and, due to defective IFN-α production and impaired sensing of Staphylococcus aureus, contribute to both immune dysregulation and skin barrier disruption.32 Studies have shown that in AD, monocytes infiltrate the dermis and differentiate into antigen-presenting macrophages, produce pro-inflammatory cytokines, and synergize with eosinophils to amplify inflammation, serving as key drivers bridging innate and adaptive immunity.33 In summary, the coordinated actions of Th2 cells, dendritic cells, and monocytes/macrophages form a complex immune network that sustains inflammation and barrier disruption in AD. However, it is critical to note that these immune infiltration patterns are derived from computational deconvolutions and represent tight correlational networks rather than experimental causality. While these statistical associations are highly suggestive, they reflect a synchronized microenvironmental state rather than a proven mechanistic driving force.

The enrichment analysis of candidate genes revealed that the metabolic reprogramming-related diagnostic markers (RRM2 and HMMR) are significantly enriched in biological processes such as the cell cycle, chromosome segregation, spindle microtubule movement, DNA replication, and the p53 signaling pathway. In atopic dermatitis, the cell cycle not only reflects abnormal proliferation and differentiation disorders of keratinocytes but also serves as a core molecular event linking skin barrier disruption and immune microenvironment dysregulation.34 Studies have shown that the p53 signaling pathway is significantly activated in AD lesions, and p53-mediated cell cycle regulation and DNA damage response are involved in the abnormal proliferation of keratinocytes and the disruption of the epidermal barrier in atopic dermatitis.35 Additionally, after stimulation with Th2-type cytokines, keratinocytes in the AD model exhibited increased oxidative stress levels, with significantly higher H2O2-induced DNA damage compared to normal controls. Notably, this damage could be rescued by inhibiting NADPH oxidase.36 Although the enriched terms were predominantly associated with cell cycle, chromosome segregation, and DNA replication, these findings are consistent with the metabolic functions of the two genes. RRM2, as the rate‑limiting enzyme for dNTP synthesis, directly links nucleotide metabolism to DNA replication, while HMMR promotes aerobic glycolysis via PI3K/Akt signalling. Therefore, the observed cell‑cycle enrichment reflects the metabolic demands of hyperproliferation in AD.

We acknowledge that molecular subtyping typically employs broader gene sets; however, selecting strictly RRM2 and HMMR carries a distinct rational advantage. These two genes represent the rigorous intersection between AD-specific modules and core metabolic reprogramming pathways, functioning as key executive nodes of immunometabolic crosstalk. As demonstrated by our results, consensus clustering directed by these two functional hubs successfully partitioned AD patients into two sub-populations with sharply contrasting immune landscapes and metabolic signatures. This confirms that a focused, pathway-anchored signature can effectively capture the core biological heterogeneity of AD without introducing non-specific transcriptomic noise.

Based on the current study, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the identification of the metabolic reprogramming-related diagnostic biomarkers RRM2 and HMMR was derived solely from transcriptomic data of public cohorts without protein-level or experimental functional validation; therefore, future studies should incorporate immunohistochemistry, Western blotting, and in vivo or in vitro assays to confirm their biological roles in AD pathogenesis. Second, immune infiltration analyses relied on computational algorithms such as MCPcounter and ssGSEA, which provide estimated rather than experimentally validated immune cell abundances, highlighting the need for single-cell RNA sequencing and multiplex immunohistochemistry to precisely map immune heterogeneity in AD lesions. Third, the constructed ceRNA network was based on database predictions without experimental verification, so dual-luciferase reporter assays and RNA interference experiments are essential to validate these regulatory axes. Fourth, the study focused exclusively on two metabolic genes, potentially overlooking other metabolic pathways involved in AD heterogeneity, suggesting that broader metabolomic and transcriptomic integration—including lipidomics and flux analysis—should be pursued to enable more comprehensive patient subtyping. Sixth, all data were cross-sectional, which limits causal inference regarding metabolic-immune crosstalk and disease progression, indicating that longitudinal cohort studies and time-series animal models are necessary to establish temporal dynamics and causality. Finally, despite the use of SVA correction, the integration of multiple GEO datasets may introduce residual batch effects. Although PCA confirmed the overall effectiveness of batch adjustment, we cannot completely exclude the possibility that technical variation influences our subtype classification results, particularly given the modest sample sizes of some individual cohorts. In summary, rigorous multi-omics validation spanning protein-level assays, single-cell resolutions, and direct metabolomic profiling is fundamentally required before these candidates can be translated into real-world clinical practice.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study systematically explores the role of metabolic reprogramming in AD by integrating multi-cohort transcriptomic data and highlights its close association with immune heterogeneity. Our findings provide novel insights into the metabolic-immune crosstalk underlying AD pathogenesis and offer a foundation for identifying potential molecular targets. The proposed diagnostic framework and subtype classification based on metabolic signatures may facilitate precision diagnosis and subtype-specific management of AD. Further experimental and mechanistic studies are warranted to translate these findings into clinical applications and to deepen the understanding of metabolic regulation in inflammatory skin disorders.
