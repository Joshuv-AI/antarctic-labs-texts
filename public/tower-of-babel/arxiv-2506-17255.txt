UltraSketchLLM: Saliency-Driven Sketching for Ultra-Low Bit LLM Compression
Sunan Zou*†, Ziyun Zhang*†, Xueting Sun*‡, Guojie Luo*†

Abstract
The rapid growth of large language models (LLMs) has outpaced the memory constraints of edge devices, necessitating extreme weight compression beyond the 1-bit limit. While quantization reduces model size, it is fundamentally limited to 1 bit per weight. Existing multiple-to-one compression methods either rely on mapping tables (inducing memory overhead) or incur severe accuracy degradation due to random weight grouping. We introduce UltraSketchLLM, an index-free, sketch-based framework that achieves ultra-low bit compression (down to 0.5 bits per weight) while preserving model performance. UltraSketchLLM leverages data sketching, a sub-linear representation technique from streaming applications, to map multiple weights to single values with bounded error. Our approach integrates an underestimate AbsMaxMin sketch to minimize relative errors for small weights, importance-aware space allocation to prioritize salient weights, and a straight-through estimator for compression-aware finetuning. Experiments on Llama-3.2-1B demonstrate up to 0.5-bit compression with competitive perplexity, alongside tolerable latency overhead. UltraSketchLLM offers a practical solution for deploying LLMs in resource-constrained environments.

Introduction

The rapid advancement of large language models (LLMs) has unlocked remarkable capabilities in natural language processing, yet their growing scale presents significant hurdles for deployment on resource-constrained scenarios.
As the need for privacy-preserving, low-latency, and cost-effective inference intensifies, compressing LLMs to operate within tight memory budgets has emerged as a pressing research challenge.
UltraSketchLLM targets compressing weight to the extreme while maintaining tolerable performance and latency, as in Figure
1(a)
.

Model weights compression maps original weights to ones with smaller space occupation.
Existing weight compression methods have made notable progress in reducing model size and can be classified to one-to-one mapping and multiple-to one mapping, as in Figure
1(b)
.
One-to-one mapping such as quantization
Lin et al. 2024
cannot break the one bit per weight limit, restricting their ability to leverage redundancy across weights for deeper compression.
The induced hybrid-bit and irregular pruning sparsity
Cheng et al. 2024
also hampers inference efficiency on hardware.
Multiple-to-one mapping strategies, such as hashing-based compression
Chen et al. 2015
, address this by grouping weights to share a single representation, potentially achieving sub-1-bit compression.
Yet, these methods suffer from significant drawbacks: random weight grouping in hashing leads to large approximation errors and performance drops, while index-based approaches
Zhang et al. 2024
impose memory overhead from mapping tables, undermining their utility in memory-scarce settings.

To tackle these challenges, we introduce UltraSketchLLM, an innovative index-free, multiple-to-one mapping framework for ultra-low bit LLM weight compression.
UltraSketchLLM harnesses data sketching, a technique that approximates large datasets with sub-linear memory and bounded error.
However, designing a sketch algorithm fitting the LLM weight compression problem is non-trivial.
We profile LLM weights features to exploit the possible sketch algorithm and have the following findings as in Figure
2
:
1) Sketch preserves more weights untouched than quantization but induces larger relative error and causes severe model degradation.
2) Some weights are much more tolerable to sketch compression than salient weights.

Based on the above findings, our method employs a novel underestimate AbsMaxMin sketch to minimize relative errors, particularly for small weights critical to model fidelity.
We enhance this with importance-aware space allocation, dedicating more sketch resources to salient weights.
The nature of index-free mapping theoretically constrains the information each weight can carry.
Even with a delicate sketch algorithm design, directly applying sketch compression to a pre-trained model can cause significant quality degradation.
Compression-aware finetuning is needed to mitigate this perturbation, and previous works
Desai et al. 2022
;
Zhang et al. 2024
leverage aggregated gradient to update the mapped weights, limiting the degree of freedom.
UltraSketchLLM integrate a straight-through estimator (STE)-based finetuning approach to adapt the model to compression.
Combined with a multi-row sketch design, UltraSketchLLM enhances the expressiveness of the compressed model, as in Figure
4
.

To summarize, our key contributions are threefold:
1) Index-free ultra-low bit compression: UltraSketchLLM achieves compression rates as low as 0.5 bits per weight without index tables, drastically cutting memory demands for edge-device inference.
2) Underestimate sketch design: The mutlei-row AbsMaxMin sketch design reduces relative errors and offers more mapping choices to reduce hash collision caused perturbation.
3) Importance-aware and finetuning synergy: Combining saliency-driven resource allocation with STE-based finetuning, UltraSketchLLM sustains strong performance at extreme compression levels.
Experiments in Section
4
validate the feasibility of UltraSketchLLM, providing an ultra-low bit compression solution with comparable performance and latency overhead.

2.1
Transformer-Based LLMs and Compression Techniques

Modern large language models (LLMs) leverage Transformer architectures
Vaswani et al. 2017
, which rely on two primary components: self-attention (Attn) and feed-forward networks (FFN).
The self-attention mechanism is defined as:

\text{Atten}(X)=\text{Softmax}\left(\frac{XW^{Q}(XW^{K})^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_{k}}}\right)XW^{V}\cdot W^{O},

(1)

where
X
is the input and
W^{Q}
,
W^{K}
,
W^{V}
, and
W^{O}
are projection matrices for queries, keys, and values, respectively.
Similarly, FFN is computed as:

\text{FFN}(X)=\left(\text{Activation}(XW^{Gate})\odot XW^{Up}\right)W^{Down}

(2)

with
W^{Gate}
,
W^{Up}
, and
W^{Down}
as projection matrices.
These matrices constitute the majority of trainable parameters in LLMs, particularly in models with multi-head attention or mixture-of-experts designs.
Consequently, compressing these weights is essential to reducing memory demands, especially for inference on resource-constrained devices like edge hardware.

Several compression methods have been explored to address this challenge.
They map weights with large storage into representations with less storage, and Figure
1(b)
classifies them into three types.
One-to-one mapping represents each original weight with an exclusive representation.
Scalar quantization works explore the weight distribution
Huang et al. 2024
, importance
Yao et al. 2021
, and relevance to activation
Xiao et al. 2023
to reduce the mapped representation storage.
However practical and efficient, at least 1 bit per weight is needed to preserve its independent representation.

Multiple-to-one mapping methods group weights to share representations, enabling sub-1-bit compression.
However, random grouping
Desai et al. 2022
;
Chen et al. 2015
can increase errors, and index-based
Zhang et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2024
methods introduce memory overhead from mapping tables.
Therefore, existing index-free methods concentrate on the pre-training scenario
Desai et al. 2022
;
Chen et al. 2015
and consider compressing caused perturbation during training.

Dong et al. 2024
explores the sparsity of weights and uses random binary weights to represent unimportant blocks.

Zhang et al. 2024
uses a sketch-like method for model compression, which replaces hash mapping with clustering to reduce error while maintaining an index table.
These works use gradient aggregation to update grouped parameters, constraining the degree of freedom.
UltraSketchLLM uses a straight-through estimator with a multi-row sketch structure to enhance the expressiveness of the compressed model.
Besides, existing index-free multiple-to-one works ignore the importance of the discrepancy between weights, significantly influencing the compression effect.

Characteristics of LLM Weights

Instead of compressing all LLM weights uniformly, a hybrid compression rate scenario is more promising with the following findings:
Weights vary significantly in their sensitivity to compression-induced perturbations.
We compress different layers’ weight matrix to its
\frac{1}{8}
size using the sketch algorithm in Section

while maintaining other rows untouched.
The profiling result in Figure

shows that compressing different layers degrades the perplexity from

to

, while the original model has a perplexity of
10.3
.
Previous works on hybrid quantization provide similar results
Zhang and Shrivastava 2024
;
Huang et al. 2024
.
This feature allows us to maintain the integrity of crucial weights and aggressively compress others.

In addition, the relative error induced by compression techniques strongly influences the model’s ability.
Figure
2(b)
compares the perplexity of compressing Llama-3.2-1B model with 4-bit quantization and
1/4

Sketch methods preserve more weights untouched (15.29%) than quantization (3.95%), while facing greater model quality degradation due to much larger relative and sign error.
This phenomenon also exists in scalar quantization methods like
Gong et al. 2024
.
Our proposed UltraSketchLLM leverages data sketching to utilize these features, introducing an AbsMaxMin sketch that reduces relative errors for small weights, combined with importance-aware space allocation.
This approach avoids index overhead and maintains accuracy at ultra-low bit rates.

2.3
Data Sketch Techniques

Data sketching is a sub-linear memory compression technique approximating large datasets with controlled error bounds
Cormode and Muthukrishnan 2005
.
It maintains a
M\times N
sketch state (matrix) as in Figure
4(c)
and follows a select-update-retrieve process:
Select: Hash function maps data to a set of locations in the matrix.
Update: Application-specific rules update these locations based on input data and the current sketch state.
Retrieve: Original data or statistics are approximated by getting bonded values from the sketch state and interpreting them with specific rules.
Substantial memory reduction for data estimation makes it superior in streaming applications like heavy hitters, cardinality
Zou et al. 2024
, and frequency distributions
Cao et al. 2024
.

Jiang et al. 2018
and
Gui et al. 2023
also apply this technique in gradient compression for distributed training.

The expectation of approximation error induced by the data sketch is theoretically bounded.
The upper bound is tightly related to the space assigned for the sketch state, as
E((\text{Error}))\propto Mem(Sketch)
.
A larger sketch state generally leads to a higher approximation estimation and vice versa.
The agile error control helps UltraSketchLLM adopt importance-aware differentiated weights compression rate for different rows of LLM weights.
Data sketch compression can achieve a different compression ratio by assigning various sketch matrix sizes.
Deployment of data sketch in weights compression for low memory utilization is a parameter-sharing method in nature.
This method is also orthogonal to scalar quantization, which provides significant compression potential.

3
Design of UltraSketchLLM

Figure 3:
Illustration of inference procedure (one layer) and memory footprint in UltraSketchLLM.

3.1
Inference with Index-Free Compression

UltraSketchLLM leverages the data sketch to implement a multiple-to-one mapping to compress model weights.
Model weights are represented and stored by sketch states instead of the original weight matrices.
Each weight is mapped to an element in the sketch state, while each sketch element may correspond to multiple weights.
UltraSketchLLM adopts a layer-by-layer computation during inference.
Sketch states stay permanently in the memory, while weights for calculation are decompressed and released dynamically.
Sketch states take much smaller space than the original weight matrices, requiring less memory to load a model.
Full weights of a particular layer are active only during inference calculation on that layer.
Peak memory usage can be reduced from
\sum_{i}^{n}{Mem(Layer_{i})}
to
\sum_{i}^{n}{Mem(Sketch_{i})}+Max(Mem(Layer_{i}))
using sketch, where
n
is the number of layers and
Layer_{i}
,
Sketch_{i}
are storage by weight matrix and sketch state of the
i_{th}
layer, respectively.

The forward pass of each layer in UltraSketchLLM has the following stages: decompression, computation, and memory release, as in Figure
3
.
Decompression stage retrieves weights in their original shape for calculation from sketch states.
The hash function first calculates corresponding indices in sketch states for all weights.
Then, intermediate results are retrieved by the above indices in a batch.
Interpreting these intermediate results based on specific sketch rules results in the decompressed weights.
The computation stage follows the original inference step, using input activation and decompressed weights.
Finally, the memory release stage frees the memory used in this layer, and the next layer inference continues this procedure.

3.2
AbsMaxMin Sketch Design for Error Reduction

(a)

(b)

(c)

Figure 4:
(a) Aggregated gradient estimation from previous works; (b) Straight-through estimator for finetuning in UltraSketchLLM; (c) Multi-row AbsMaxMin sketch design.

Hash-based compressing methods group unrelated weights together and represent them in less space.
Unlike scalar and vector quantization, the error induced by sketch compression is not controlled by the original weight and may induce a relative error larger than 1.
Simultaneously, a sign error may arise during this procedure.
When infected by the same amount of error, weights with larger absolute values are more resistant to perturbations.
Approximating a small weight with a large one is more likely to cause mode degradation than the inverse situation
Huang et al. 2024
.
Therefore, UltraSketchLLM adopts an underestimate sketch, AbsMaxMin sketch, to reduce relative error induced by compression, avoiding mapping weights with small absolute value mapped to large ones.

Sketch algorithm design contains two primary components: sketch state and update/interpret rules.
A sketch state
S
is maintained to support these operations, and its size can be allocated based on the accuracy needed.

S
in AbsMaxMin is initialized as
inf
, and each state stores the corresponding weights with minimum absolute value.
Previous index-free multiple-to-one mapping adopts a single mapping, and weights have only one chance of falling into a particular shared parameter, as in Figure
4(a)
.
To induce multiple choices and create variety during mapping, UltraSketchLLM incorporates a multi-row mapping method as in Figure
4(c)
.
Each row of
S
equips an independent hash function
H_{i}
.
The independence of hash functions reduces the collision between weights.
It also creates different collision patterns, namely, the weight may collide with different counterparts, offering more possibilities to group weights with similar values together.

Sketch state maintenance comprises three stages: selection, update, and retrieval.
Select stage in AbsMaxMin maps an item to one or several elements in the sketch state, which can be formulated by:

I(I_{0},I_{1},...,I_{n})=H(Addr(w))

(3)

, where
w
is original weight and
H=(H_{0},H_{1},...,H_{n})
are independent hash functions.

Addr(w)
is the memory address or bias of the corresponding weight, also a natural index free of storage.
The update stage in the AbsMaxMin sketch replaces the value in sketch states by the weight being processed only when its absolute value is smaller than the current sketch state.
Otherwise, sketch states remain unchanged.
This process is calculated by:

S[i,I_{i}]=(Abs(S[i,I_{i}])>Abs(X))\ ?\ X:S[i,I_{i}]

(4)

We adopt a
Max
operation during the interpret stage to leverage the variety in multi-row sketch and increase the possibility of mapping a weight to a close value.
During inference, decompressing a weight can be calculated by

w^{\prime}=Max_{i}^{n}(S[i][H_{i}(Addr(w))])

(5)

Given fixed sketch storage, the number of rows and columns of sketch state is a trade-off, and row number in UltraSketchLLM is empirically set to 3.

AbsMaxMin, as an underestimate sketch, either preserves the original value or represents it with a smaller absolute value.
Therefore, the error is controlled by the upper bound of the minimum value of sketch states.
The proposed AbsMaxMin sketch ensures that the minimum value is higher than a targeted threshold with a high probability, as formulated by the following equation:

P(s\geq\Phi^{-1}(1-p^{\frac{1}{n}}))\geq p,

(6)

where
s
is the sketch state,
\Phi
is the cumulative distribution function of model weights,
p
is the target probability, and
n
is the number of conflicting elements, related to allocated sketch size and the number of model weights.
The detailed proof is in Appendix
B
.

3.3
STE-Based Finetuning with Multi-Row Sketch

Figure 5:
Multi row mapping with Straight-through Estimator (right) induces less relative error than single row with aggregated gradients (left).

Multiple-to-one weight mapping still causes non-negligible relative and sign errors even with the AbsMaxMin sketch above.
Therefore, we apply compression-aware finetuning to mitigate model quality degradation.
The compression procedure becomes trainable with STE
Bengio et al. 2013
and fixing random seed in hash functions.
Previous multiple-to-one mapping works use mapped parameters as the only trainable parameters
Zhang et al. 2024
;
Desai et al. 2022
, as in Figure
4(a)
.
Weights are grouped only once, and the interpreted weights during the forward pass are fixed to the mapping result, limiting the degree of freedom.
Once mapped, values, no matter how large or small, have to crystallize into a compromised medium value after convergence.

Notably, the multi-row sketch can offer multiple mapping choices on different rows with the same compression ratio.
Unlike single-row designs, this diversity reduces errors from weights clashing in the same sketch states.
UltraSketchLLM only fixes the mapping method while preserving the original trainable parameter size during finetuning.
The bonding between weights and sketch states may vary during finetuning, as the
Min
and
Max
operations in the AbsMaxMin sketch consider not only weight mapping but also updated values in sketch states, providing another dimension of freedom.

Figure
5
illustrates the superiority of the proposed method (multi-row mapping) over existing ones (single-row mapping).
A set of weights is mapped to a compressed representation with the same index and a reduce function (Min).
Initial mapping shows a significant error after compression for both methods.
Finetuning using aggregated gradient only updates the shared parameters, comprising conflict values by mediating them after finetuning.
On the contrary, weights with similar absolute values tend to group together after finetuning using STE and multiple mapping choices by multi-row design.
This process forms an adaptive mapping method, effectively reducing relative error.

The index-free mapping procedure induces a discrete operation.
A gradient estimation method is needed to update trainable parameters.
Previous works estimate the gradient of trainable parameters by aggregating gradients from corresponding original weights
Zhang et al. 2024
;
Desai et al. 2022
, as in Figure
4(a)
.
UltraSketchLLM keeps the trainable parameter size untouched and uses a Straight-Through Estimator to estimate the gradient with compressing perturbation.
Figure
4(b)
illustrates its basic flow during finetuning.
Each original weight (a trainable parameter) is first compressed using the AbsMaxMin sketch, updating the sketch states.
Then, weights are retrieved from sketch states waiting for computation, which forms a fake-compress procedure.
This procedure does not track the gradient, and the gradient from the preceding computation part estimates each weight gradient of the whole projection operation using the sketch.
The fake compression induces a perturbation, and the estimated gradient contains loss information caused by such a perturbation.

3.4
Salient-Weight-Aware Sketch Space Allocation

Profiling results in Section
2.1
and previous works show that weights vary in their tolerance of perturbation by compression.
Assigning more space to preserve salient weights while applying a high compression rate to other weights is straightforward.
The estimation error of sketch methods is tightly related to the assigned sketch state space.
Thus, UltraSketchLLM allocates sketch state size elastically based on importance metrics.
Each sketch with a different state size compresses sets of mutually disjoint weights.

The granularity of weight sets should fulfill the importance metric and sketch design requirements.
The former has been widely discussed in literature.
The latter is related to the question: How many elements can a sketch state compress effectively?
A large sketch state will result in inefficient retrieval due to random memory accesses with poor locality.
A small sketch state cannot distribute and hash weights evenly, harming the compression effect.
Empirical practices in stream processing deploy sketch with state size ranging from
2^{10}
to
2^{16}

Chiosa et al. 2021
.
In the weight compression problem, sketch state size is also limited by the final compressed weight size.

Combining all these constraints, we choose two granularities for sketch space allocation: row-wise and layer-wise.
For the row-wise granularity, each row of the weight matrix corresponds to an independent AbsMaxMin sketch instance, with a weight size of
1e4
to
1e5
.
For the layer-wise granularity, each projection weight matrix is compressed by an AbsMaxMin sketch instance.
Each sketch instance is assigned a state space based on its importance metrics.
UltraSketchLLM adopts the expected value of the squared activations as importance metrics
Kawrakow 2024
.
For each row of the weight matrix (input dimension), the importance
I_{j}
is given by:

I_{j}=\mathbb{E}[a_{j}^{2}]=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}a_{k,j}^{2}

(7)

where
N
is the sampled data size,
a_{k,j}
denotes the
j
-th activation component for the
k
-th sample, and
a_{j}
is the
j
-th component of the input activation vector
\mathbf{a}
.

\mathbb{E}[\cdot]
represents the expectation over a set of training data.
Given importance
I_{j}
, we assign
{I_{j}}/\sum_{i}^{n}I_{i}\times Mem(Sketch)
as the space of sketch state for each weight matrix row.

Mem(Sketch)
is the target storage by all sketch states in a layer.
The layer-wise importance is estimated by the mean of row importance.
Similar to row-wise, each layer is assigned a space in proportion to its importance.

A
discusses other optional influence methods and their effect on UltraSketchLLM

3.5
Implementation and Integration with Quantization

UltraSketchLLM uses a hash-based natural index to compress model weights, trading more computation for less space occupation.
The hash calculation and random memory access during LLM inference increase latency.
To reduce this overhead and increase finetuning efficiency, we implement the
AbsMin
Scatter operator on the GPU to implement the AbsMaxMin sketch and modify the linear layer correspondingly.
For the finetuning procedure, we implement a fake compression flow.
Firstly, sketch states are initialized to
inf
, and independent hash functions calculate sketch state indices of weights in a batch.
Then we use the
AbsMin
scatter operation, inputting the above indices and the original weight values to simulate the sketch state update.
Finally, memory accesses in batch decompress the weights from the sketch state.
The inference procedure only includes the batch memory accesses and sends retrieved weights in their original shape for the subsequent calculation.
We also modify the parameter storing method to keep only sketch states in memory.

The proposed sketch-based compression method is orthogonal to scalar quantization works.
We combine UltraSketchLLM with 4-bit and 8-bit quantization to compress LLM weights to the extreme.
Experiments show this combination induces only slightly more perturbation than sketch-based compression alone.

5
Limitation and Future Work

Though UltraSketchLLM achieves 0.5 bit per weight compression, it still faces the challenge of irregular memory accesses in weight retrieval.
The hash-based decompression process introduces a latency overhead.
Although our tests indicate this overhead remains reasonable, optimizing it through hardware acceleration could enhance UltraSketchLLM ’s suitability for time-sensitive use cases.
Besides, model quality degradation even after finetuning requires a more delicate sketch algorithm design, as we find some sketch states are not mapped to weights even at a high compression rate.
Our experiments primarily focus on the Llama-3.2-1B model, a relatively small LLM due to the experimental environment limit.
Future studies should evaluate UltraSketchLLM on larger models and across diverse datasets and tasks to confirm the framework’s robustness.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we introduced UltraSketchLLM, a framework designed to compress large language models (LLMs) to ultra-low bit representations using data sketching techniques.
This approach tackles the pressing challenge of deploying LLMs with limited memory.
By integrating an underestimated AbsMaxMin sketch with saliency-aware resource allocation and a straight-through estimator (STE)-based finetuning strategy, UltraSketchLLM achieves remarkable compression rates (down to 0.5 bits per weight) while preserving competitive model performance.
Our experimental evaluation on the Llama-3.2-1B model showcases UltraSketchLLM’s ability to reduce memory usage by up to 75%, surpassing state-of-the-art quantization techniques.

Appendix A
Optional Importance Metrics Choices

Various importance metrics have been proposed recently to support efficient quantization and compression techniques.
Loss-based metrics evaluate parameter importance by estimating their impact on the model’s loss function, often using gradient information or higher-order derivatives.
HAWQ
Dong et al. 2019
and its proceeding works leverage the hessian metric (second-order derivatives) as an importance metric.
AWQ
Lin et al. 2024
identifies the importance of weight by considering activation.
Regularization metrics encourage sparsity or small weights during training, facilitating pruning by identifying less important parameters
Biswas et al. 2025
.
The selection of these methods needs to consider computation cost, effect, granularity, and other factors.

We adopt an implementation in Llama.cpp
Kawrakow 2024
as an important metric as its granularity fits the sketch algorithm and has competing effects against SOTA methods.
It calculates the activation by the following process:
Suppose a tensor row is represented as:

\mathbf{w}=[w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{d}]

And an activation column is represented as:

\mathbf{a}=[a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{d}]

The dot product of
\mathbf{w}
and
\mathbf{a}
produces one entry in the activation passed to the next layer:

y=\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{a}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}w_{j}a_{j}

Suppose the quantized version of
\mathbf{w}
is:

\mathbf{q}=[q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{d}]

The quantized values should minimize the error in the dot product due to quantization. The error function can be written as:

F=\left[\sum_{j=1}^{d}(q_{j}-w_{j})a_{j}\right]^{2}

(8)

Define
r_{j}=q_{j}-w_{j}
, then:

F=\left[\sum_{j}a_{j}r_{j}\right]^{2}

Since there are multiple activations, we can consider the expectation of the error:

\mathbb{E}[F]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{j}a_{j}r_{j}\right)^{2}\right]

(9)

If the activations are not strongly correlated (i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[a_{i}a_{j}]\approx 0
for
i\neq j
), the second term can be neglected, yielding:

\mathbb{E}[F]\approx\sum_{j}\mathbb{E}[a_{j}^{2}]r_{j}^{2}=\sum_{j}\langle a_{j}^{2}\rangle(q_{j}-w_{j})^{2}

(10)

Minimizing the weighted mean square error
\sum_{j}\langle a_{j}^{2}\rangle(q_{j}-w_{j})^{2}
is equivalent to minimizing
\mathbb{E}[F]
. Therefore, the expectation
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{a^{2}}]
can be used as an importance matrix to quantize
\mathbf{w}
.

Apart from existing methods, we also identify two optional importance metrics from the point of view of sketch compression: top-K elements and perturbation when compressing certain parts of model weights.
The former is a special magnitude-based metric, and the latter is a loss-based method.
In the Top-K metric, UltraSketchLLM considers weights with top-k large absolute values as important ones, stores them independently, and keeps their value untouched.
This has dual effects on the AbsMaxMin sketch in UltraSketchLLM.
AbsMaxMin is an underestimate sketch, so removing values naturally reduces the induced relative error.
Besides, large values are outliers and generally significantly impact the model quality.
However, storing Top-K in a specific structure requires a mark for each weight.
This constraint limits the under-1-bit compression.

Unlike pruning or quantization methods, UltraSketchLLM needs a larger granularity due to sketch efficiency considerations.
Grouping 128 or 256 weights and evaluating their importance is popular today.
Sketch incorporates a hash function trying to map weights evenly to sketch states.
A mismatch between sketch state size and weights to be compressed will ruin the even mapping here.
Therefore, sketch usually leverages a
2^{10}
to
2^{16}
compression granularity.

Taking perturbation as an important metric when compressing a specific part of the model weights is a straightforward and effective way to evaluate the influence of compressing each layer using UltraSketchLLM.
Applying sketch compression directly on LLMs without finetuning can result in severe model degradation, as the profiling results in Figure 2a.
However, infeasible the results are, the degradation extent can serve as an important metric for the subsequent finetuning procedure.
We have the following findings based on the profiling results on Llama-3.2-1B model:
1) Front-end layers are more important than back-end layers.
Compressing front-end layers (0-2) causes perplexity degradation to almost
6000
.
However, only compressing layers in the back can produce even more perplexity under
11
, almost the same as the original model.
This may be because front-end layers capture the internal relation among input sentences, while later ones are in charge of reasoning
Geva et al. 2020
.
Therefore, missing the original relation will cause severe quality degradation.
2) Attention layers are more tolerable than feed-forward network layers.
This may be due to feed-forward networks storing the knowledge.
Disturbing the knowledge, especially in the front-end layers, will destroy the ability of LLMs after pre-training.

Leveraging the importance of the sketch state space allocation is also a question.
In the experimental results of row-wise importance metrics, UltraSketchLLM directly applies the relative importance to allocate the corresponding sketch state space.
This causes significant heterogeneity and may cause relatively bad memory locality.
It might be a good idea to classify importance metric values into several categories and assign a sketch state space based on each category.
Hybrid-precision quantization methods generally assign weight bits based on importance.
It is possible to refer to the granularity in these methods to control the number of categories.

As discovered by previous works
Cui and Wang 2024
, important or salient weights are rare but influence the final inference output greatly.
This is in line with our findings, as Figure 2a shows that only one layer causes much more quality degradation than the other counterparts.
In the experiment, we tried compressing common layers 50 times while preserving six important layers with a
75\%
to
100\%
compression ratio.
The total compression ratio is around 0.125.
Experiments show that such a method obtains a perplexity of around
35
, much higher than compressing all the layers uniformly to 0.125.
This shows the effectiveness of importance-aware compression and also UltraSketchLLM’s superiority over existing quantization methods, which can only achieve up to
32\times
compression.

Proof of AbsMaxMin Sketch Error Bound

In this section, we prove that the error of the AbsMaxMin sketch can be controlled within an acceptable range. This is demonstrated by establishing that the estimated value in the AbsMaxMin sketch possesses a lower bound that holds with high probability.

Let the size of the AbsMaxMin sketch be
m
, and the number of elements mapped to the sketch be
k
, with each element mapped exactly once. For any bucket, the number of elements mapped to the bucket follows
n\sim\text{Binomial}(k,\frac{1}{m})
, where
Binomial
denotes the binomial distribution. Let the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of the model’s parameter distribution be denoted by
\Phi
, and the value of the bucket (i.e., the minimum value among the elements mapped to this bucket) be
s
.

We aim to determine a lower bound
L
such that
P(s\geq L)=p
. Since the probability that each element mapped to the bucket is at least
L
is
1-\Phi(L)
, it follows that:

p=P(s\geq L)=(1-\Phi(L))^{n}.

(11)

Solving for
L
, we obtain
L=\Phi^{-1}\left(1-p^{\frac{1}{s}}\right)
. Consequently, this implies:

P\left(s\geq\Phi^{-1}\left(1-p^{\frac{1}{n}}\right)\right)\geq p.

Appendix D
Further Discussion on Limitations

D.1
Sketch Algorithm Shortcomings

Sketch algorithms provide an effective and efficient way to estimate statistical data for streaming applications.
However, it was not originally designed for a large amount of data compression, and thus, it has the following shortcomings, even though the delicately designed AbsMaxMin has tried to mitigate some of them.

Sketch states are randomly bound to weights, and uneven allocation may happen, especially during low compression rates.
Table
3
shows the unoccupied sketch states size during LLM compression under different compression rates.
Even at a high compression rate (
1/8
), some sketch states are still utilized, while others may be mapped to multiple weights.
This phenomenon not only wastes the assigned space but also harms the compression quality as more weights face hash conflicts.
As the input to UltraSketchLLM sketch hash functions is static (natural memory address), we plan to explore better hash functions fitting for this scenario.

Besides, less than 1-bit compression can carry theoretically constrained information, and UltraSketchLLM tends to lose bit information.
However, sign information is critical in preserving the model quality.
Bitnet
Wang et al. 2023
achieves 1 bit weight by weight representation with only negative and positive values.
UltraSketchLLM plans to adopt hierarchical sketch design to mitigate this problem.
Similarly, relative error is not controlled by the weight itself; namely, the relative error can be larger than 1.
In the future, we plan to leverage a grouping technique to mitigate this shortcoming.

D.2
Incapability of Computation Compression and Memory Locality

UltraSketchLLM effectively compresses the weight parameters in projections, reducing the peak memory footprint needed during inference.
However, the computation is not reduced and is conducted using either the original or the quantized types.
The stored KV cache and activation are preserved in the original format.
Both the memory needed and the latency increase as the token is generated.
Sketch also faces poor memory locality as hash mapping makes each weight correspond to random addresses.
UltraSketchLLM will explore the proper granularity of sketch states and put them in a local memory to reduce memory access overhead.
