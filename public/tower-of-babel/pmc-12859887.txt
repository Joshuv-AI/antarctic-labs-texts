Effect of chlorhexidine vs. saline for oral care on oral microbiota and prognosis of elderly mechanically ventilated patients in the intensive care unit: a single-center, single-blind, randomized controlled trial
Authors: Changcui Qiu, Lulu Tang, Zhuo Hang, Ke Wei, Sheng Wang, Xiaoping Zhu, Qixing Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe role of chlorhexidine in oral care for mechanically ventilated elderly patients remains debated, particularly regarding its impact on oral microbiota and clinical outcomes.ObjectiveTo compare oral microbiota composition and prognosis among elderly mechanically ventilated patients receiving oral care with either chlorhexidine solution or normal saline.MethodsSixty intubated patients (≥ 65 years) were randomized to receive oral care with 0.12% chlorhexidine (intervention group) or 0.9% saline (control group) three times daily during mechanical ventilation. All patients underwent standardized oral hygiene during mechanical ventilation. Oral samples were analyzed by 16 S rRNA sequencing, with outcomes compared to oral health and clinical data. Secondary outcomes included dental plaque index, ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP), 28-day mortality, patient outcomes, and oral complications.ResultsOne week after intervention, the chlorhexidine group exhibited a significant decrease in oral bacterial diversity (Simpson index, P = 0.037). Despite this, no significant differences were observed in clinical infection symptoms-defined as fever, purulent airway secretions, elevated white blood cell count, or increased C-reactive protein levels requiring evaluation by the attending physician. All participants received standard ICU antimicrobial prophylaxis or therapy according to institutional protocols; no additional systemic antibiotics were initiated solely for oral or respiratory infections. There were no significant differences between groups in ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) incidence (P = 1.000), duration of mechanical ventilation (P = 0.063), ICU stay (P = 0.191), 28-day mortality (P = 0.82), or rates of complications and plaque formation. The chlorhexidine group also showed a decrease in dominant microbial taxa and an increase in specific anaerobic bacteria.ConclusionChlorhexidine temporarily altered oral microbiota composition without improving short-term clinical outcomes. Although no infections increased, its potential impact on microbial balance warrants caution, and larger studies are needed to validate these findings.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov ID NCT06691841. Background

The oral microbiome is the second most diverse microbiome in the human body, after the gut microbiome. It creates a symbiotic microbiome, that regulates various physiological processes in the host [1, 2]. Mechanical ventilation, as a form of non-biological stress, can induce structural changes in the oral microbiota mediated by factors, such as aspiration, body position alterations, high oxygen inhalation leading to O2 toxicity in hypoxic patients, impaired airway clearance, and ventilator-induced lung injury. These factors, together with aspiration risk, oxygen toxicity, impaired airway clearance, and ventilator-induced injury, may collectively contribute to reduced oral microbiota diversity and increase the susceptibility of mechanically ventilated patients to adverse outcomes, including pneumonia [3, 4]. Studies have shown that the occurrence of oral microbiota dysbiosis is age-related, and oral pathogens increases with an individual’s age. This dysbiosis has been linked to various systemic diseases [5]. However, few studies have investigated changes in oral microbiota among elderly patients undergoing mechanical ventilation, and there is no standard evaluation criteria for assessing the impact of oral care measures on oral microbiome. Furthermore, patients in intensive care units (ICUs) receiving mechanical ventilation are generally susceptible to the risk of developing oral infections, hospital-acquired pneumonia, and other systemic infections [6]. Oral colonization is one of the most critical risk factors for ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) [7].

Chlorhexidine, a broad-spectrum antimicrobial agent, is extensively used for oral care in mechanically ventilated patients. However, the 2022 SHEA guidelines [8] recommend brushing as the preferred method of oral care but advises against the use of Chlorhexidine, challenging the prevailing knowledge regarding the application of oral care protocols in these patients. Accumulating evidence shows that chlorhexidine can induce adverse side effects, including tooth staining, taste alteration, and oral mucosal irritation, particularly when used chronically or at higher concentrations [9]. Moreover, other scholars have shown that chlorhexidine mouthwash at low concentrations (e.g., 0.05%) did not significantly reduce the incidence of aspiration pneumonia, suggesting that its preventive efficacy remains uncertain [10]. Despite this, many medical institutions still use Chlorhexidine for oral care to prevent VAP and microbial colonization [11]. Some studies have shown that [12] the risk of developing VAP did not vary significantly between patients using Chlorhexidine combined with brushing and those using Chlorhexidine alone, suggesting that the use of chlorhexidine oral care is controversial. Saline, as a traditional oral care solution, has always been accepted in various clinical institutions [13].

Besides these negative effects, other bioactive compounds have been studied that are based on the idea of altering the oral microbiota without fully changing it. Recent studies have examined paraprobiotics and postbiotics, which are non-viable byproducts or metabolites of bacteria, in terms of their safety and efficacy for oral health to maintain microbial homeostasis and maintain the integrity of the mucosa. The oral formulations as an oral mousse or gel containing such agents have been shown to be safe and potentially effective for individuals who may require intubation [14].

Therefore, this study aimed to compare the effects of two commonly used oral care solutions − 0.12% chlorhexidine gluconate and 0.9% normal saline - on oral microbiota composition and short-term clinical outcomes in elderly patients undergoing mechanical ventilation. The primary outcome was oral microbiome diversity assessed by 16 S rRNA gene sequencing, while secondary outcomes included dental plaque index, ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) incidence, 28-day mortality, and oral complications.

Materials and methods

Study design

A randomized controlled trial (Fig. 1) was adopted in this study, employing a parallel design. The protocol of this study was approved by Ethics Committee of Shanghai Tenth People’s Hospital (Approval Number: 22K204), and registered at clinical trials (# NCT06691841).

Flow chart diagram showing the scheme of study

Study settings, participants and recruitment

The participants were admitted to the Intensive Care Unit of the Tenth People’s Hospital affiliated with Tongji University and received orotracheal intubation from September 2023 to February 2024.

Inclusion criteria comprised age ≥ 65 years and Completeness of clinical data, while the exclusion criteria were as: Patients with abnormal coagulation function, severe respiratory burns, oral surgery, or pulmonary and systemic infections; Patients who are expected to require mechanical ventilation via endotracheal intubation for no more than 5 days.

Elimination criteria of this study were as: Failure to adhere to the prescribed protocol; Incomplete data; Patients expected to die or to be extubated with anticipated death within 48 h; Patients who voluntarily discontinued the treatment during the study period. All patients voluntarily agreed to participate and written informed consent was obtained from their families.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated with reference to a previous preliminary study [15], with the effect size calculated as follows: d = 0.8; α = 0.05; power = 0.8. The minimum sample size for each group was 26, with an anticipated dropout/withdrawal rate of 10–20%. Finally, 30 participants were deemed suitable for each group.

In total, we screened 532 patients and randomized 126 of them (63 in each group) and performed analyses. The study design adhered to the guidelines specified in the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) statement (Fig. 1). Of those participants, there were 83 participants that completed sampling at 48 h and 60 that completed at 5 days. There were a total of 34 due to extubation, 19 died before Day 5, and 13 participants had withdrawal of consent. Analyses were eventually completed on all available complete cases, and intention-to-treat imputation did not occur, as biological endpoints were specific to full specimens sets.

Randomisation, allocation concealment, and blinding

Using a computer-generated random number table, patients were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the experimental group or control group. The randomization sequence was prepared by an independent research assistant who was not involved in participant recruitment, group allocation, intervention implementation, or data collection. Allocation concealment was maintained using opaque, consecutively numbered envelopes. Outcome assessors remained blinded to group assignments. Due to the nature of the intervention, blinding of nurses and participants was not possible.

Intervention methods

In current clinical practice, oral care solutions for mechanically ventilated patients are applied at a concentration of 0.12%. Therefore, we applied 0.12% chlorhexidine gluconate solution (in the Chlorhexidine group), while a concentration of 0.9% normal saline (control group). In both groups, similar oral care was given, which was applied at 6:00, 14:00, and 20:00 daily. The oral care method [16] involves a combination of rinsing and brushing: the operator holds a syringe in one hand for rinsing and a negative pressure suction toothbrush in the other hand for brushing and suctioning. The procedure involves brushing the teeth, cheeks, tongue surface, sublingual area, hard palate, and the surface of the tracheal tube in sequence, from the opposite side to the near side, while performing oral, nasal, airway, and subglottic suctioning as needed.

Data collection and outcomes

After endotracheal intubation, the patient was immediately examined, and the corresponding oral care solution was administered after enrolment until the endotracheal tube was removed. Oral secretions were obtained through 16sRNA testing before oral care was given at 0 h, 2 days, and 5 days post-orotracheal intubation. Oral secretions were collected as follows: before oral care, approximately 3 ml of 0.9% saline was injected into the patient’s mouth, and approximately 3–5 ml of oral secretions was collected using the sputum specimen collection method. Disposable sterile gloves were utilized during the sampling process to prevent contamination. Next, the samples were immediately sealed and stored in sterile centrifuge tubes and transported to the laboratory in a mobile refrigerator and frozen at -80 °C until DNA extraction. During specimen collection, relevant clinical data of patients were collected. Sample collection and data collection were performed by two trained research assistants.

Before the conducting the oral care portion, trained nursing staff conducted a brief oral evaluation to document the oral health status for each patient at baseline (e.g., dental condition [natural teeth present or absent], visible dental plaque, gingival inflammation, and condition of mucosa). Exclusion criteria for patients included patients with severe pre-existing oral infection, recent oral surgery, or significant tooth loss, per the study’s inclusion/exclusion criteria. Baseline oral health characteristics were similar across groups.

Primary outcome indicators: The oral microbiota composition were compared between the two groups at different time points. Secondary outcome indicators: ① The distribution and composition of oral microbiota in both groups was examined; ② the 28-day mortality rate of patients; ③ Occurrence of oral complications: The incidence of complications such as oral mucosal bleeding, oral inflammation, oral ulcers, fungal infections, herpes virus infections, tongue base retraction, laryngeal edema, and granulomas in elderly mechanically ventilated patients at was recorded at 0 h, 2 days, and 5 days of intervention. The patients’ diagnoses were based on blood routine tests, histopathological examinations, smear tests, or fungal cultures; ④ Dental Plaque Index [17]: We performed visual inspection and adopted the probe method to score the mesial buccal, mid-buccal, distal buccal, and lingual surfaces of the teeth in elderly patients receiving mechanical ventilation. A score of 0 indicated no plaque in the marginal area, 1 indicated a thin layer of plaque in the marginal area, 2 indicated a moderate amount of plaque on the marginal or adjacent surfaces, and 3 indicated the presence of a large amount of soft deposits in the gingival sulcus, marginal, and adjacent surfaces. Dental Plaque Index = sum of all tooth plaque scores / total number of teeth examined.

Data analysis

Bar charts displaying the relative abundance of various microbial genera were created using R software. Raw sequencing data were processed using QIIME version 1.9.1.

Low-quality reads were removed using Trimmomatic (version 0.36) under the following quality control thresholds: minimum average Phred score ≥ 20 in a 50 bp sliding window, and any read shorter than 200 bp after trimming was discarded. Chimeric sequences were identified and filtered using UCHIME. Sequences were clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at 97% sequence similarity using the UCLUST algorithm, and sequences were taxonomically classified against the SILVA 138 reference database. To maintain consistency between samples, sequences were randomly rarefied to the lowest sequencing depth prior to further diversity analysis. Alpha diversity indices (Chao1, Shannon, Simpson, and ACE) and beta diversity (Bray-Curtis, unweighted Unifrac and weighted Unifrac) were calculated with QIIME and visualized in R software (version 3.5.3).

Principal coordinate analysis methods in QIIME were applied to analyze Bray-Curtis, unweighted UniFrac, and weighted UniFrac distance matrices to measure beta diversity. Linear discriminant analysis (LDA) effect size analysis was employed to identify differentially abundant oral microbiota between the two groups, using a logarithmic LDA score > 3.0 as the threshold. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was adopted to calculate differences in alpha diversity indices, oral microbiota relative abundance, and BugBase-predicted phenotypes between LJD and NS.

To corroborate compositional comparisons, we also conducted differential abundance testing using the Analysis of Composition of Microbiomes (ANCOM) method, an additional differential abundance method that is non-parametric in nature and deals with both sparsity and compositionality, resulting in a more robust detectability of taxa with significant differences between groups over time. We found that the ANCOM results were consistent with the LDA effect size analysis and showed no large taxa demonstrating a likely differential abundance between the groups.

Categorical data were presented as n(%), and differences between groups were compared using Pearson’s chi-square test, corrected chi-square test, or Fisher’s exact test based on applicable conditions. The Shapiro-Wilk test was utilized to conduct normality testing for continuous data. If the data did not follow a normal distribution but had a kurtosis absolute value < 10 and a skewness absolute value < 3, the variable was considered acceptable as normally distributed and was expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. Differences between two independent samples were compared using an independent samples t-test if homogeneity of variance was met, or the independent samples t’ test was applied if the criterion was not met. Differences in related samples (repeated measurement data) between two groups were analyzed using paired t-tests, and comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using one-way repeated measures analysis of variance. For multiple comparisons, the LSD test was employed. Two-way repeated measures analysis of variance was used to analyze interaction effects within and between groups.Survival analysis was conducted using Kaplan–Meier curves, with p-values obtained through the log-rank test. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS 29.0 and R software (version 3.5.3). A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study population

A total of 532 elderly patients on mechanical ventilation were screened, of which 385 (72.37%) were excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria. Twenty-one patients (3.95%) refused to participate. Ultimately, 126 patients were included in the initial randomization; however, only participants who completed sampling at each time point (n = 83 at 48 h; n = 60 at day 5) were included in the corresponding analyses. No partial datasets from withdrawn or incomplete cases were analyzed. The study procedure is presented in Fig. S1.

The average age of patients in the intervention group was 74.10 ± 6.82 years, while that of patients in the control group was 75.50 ± 8.47 years. Notably, there were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of general demographic data such as age, gender, and APACHE II scores (Table 1).

Effects of different oral care solutions on primary outcomes

Comparison of oral microbiota differences in elderly patients with mechanical ventilation

A total of 779 OTUs were identified in this study. The number of OTUs per sample ranged from 12 to 407, with an average of 117. Regarding the representative sequences of the 779 OTUs, 21 phyla, 48 classes, 103 orders, 165 families, 607 species, and 343 genera were identified. The top 5 phyla at the phylum level included Firmicutes, Actinobacteria, Bacteroidetes, Proteobacteria, and Fusobacteria, all of which are dominant oral bacteria. The top 5 genera included Corynebacterium, Streptococcus, Prevotella, Enterococcus, and Mycoplasma. A comparison of the top 5 colonies in each group is shown in Appendix Table 1.

Relative abundance and core microbiota of oral microbiota in elderly patients on mechanical ventilation

This research article was designed to compare the oral microbiota diversity of the two groups of elderly patients on the ventilator pre-intervention, 2-days after the intervention, and 5-days after the intervention. The assessment of alpha diversity was conducted utilizing the Shannon and Simpson indices primarily, which represents richness and evenness of community diversity. Other indices (Chao1 and ACE) were used for cross-validation, but are not reported for interpretation clarity and focus.

Beyond the overall between-groups comparisons, we performed paired comparisons for each time-point (NPVa1 vs. PVa1, NPVa2 vs. PVa2, NPVa3 vs. PVa3). The control group (saline) showed relative stability in alpha diversity throughout the time-period observed while fluoroquinolone exposure produced a transient period of decreased diversity at 2-days intervention (P = 0.037, Simpson index) and partial recovery at 5-days. These findings indicate fluoroquinolone exposure modestly altered oral microbial balance during the initial period of MVS.

The analysis revealed no differences in oral microbiota Chao index (p = 0.19), Shannon index (p = 0.079), and ace index (p = 0.24) between the two groups (Fig. 2a, b,d). However, there was a significant difference in the Simpson index (Fig. 2c, p < 0.05). In addition, we measured beta diversity, which describes the differences in taxonomic composition between samples, including elements, such as the weighted Bray-Curtis matrix, unweighted phylogenetic UniFrac matrix, and weighted phylogenetic UniFrac dissimilarity matrix. It was observed that samples belonging to the same group generally clustered together, while samples from different groups showed separation (Fig. 3a-c). The results indicated that samples from the same group and different groups were distinctly separated (Fig. 4a-c), suggesting that different oral care solutions did not exert distinct effects on oral microbiota diversity. These results also suggest that intubation in critically ill patients did not significantly affect the oral microbiota diversity following oral care with two different oral care solutions.

Effects of different oral care solutions on secondary outcomes

Comparison of 28-day mortality rate in elderly mechanically ventilated patients

The 28-day mortality rate for elderly mechanically ventilated patients using saline as an oral care solution was 43.33%, while for those using chlorhexidine as an oral care solution, it was 36.67%. There was no statistically significant difference between the two groups (p = 0.82)(Fig. 5).

Discussion

This study compared the effects of Chlorhexidine and saline as oral care solutions on the oral microbiome and clinical outcomes of elderly patients receiving mechanical ventilation in the ICU. The results indicated that the top five phyla in this group of patients included Firmicutes, Actinobacteria, Bacteroidetes, Proteobacteria, and Fusobacteria. At the genus level, the top five groups were Corynebacterium, Streptococcus, Prevotella, Enterococcus, and Mycoplasma. In the Chlorhexidine group, the oral bacterial diversity increased within one week of oral care use; however, there were no significant changes in clinical infection symptoms d. Compared with the control group, the Chlorhexidine group exhibited a significant reduction in major dominant bacterial groups and a significant increase in some anaerobic bacteria. The observed increase in anaerobic bacteria following chlorhexidine exposure may reflect a selective antimicrobial effect on aerobic commensals, which allows the anaerobes to transiently expand. Chlorhexidine’s broad-spectrum biocidal action disrupts bacterial membranes but can disproportionately affect oxygen-tolerant species, hence modifying ecological balance within the oral cavity. Previous studies similarly report that repeated chlorhexidine exposure might reduce beneficial aerobic flora, such as Streptococcus, while permitting the relative enrichment of anaerobic genera, including Prevotella and Fusobacterium. This compositional shift did not result in clinical infection; however, the alteration in anaerobic abundance does suggest long-term ecological consequences that require further investigation in elderly ventilated patients. Notably, there was no significant difference between the two groups in terms of mechanical ventilation duration, ICU stay duration, patient outcomes, incidence of VAP, 28-day mortality rate, occurrence of complications, or dental plaque condition.

Moreover, we explored the changes in the oral microbiota of elderly patients undergoing mechanical ventilation using 16 S rRNA gene sequencing technology. At the phylum level, bacteria such as Proteobacteria, Bacteroidetes, Actinobacteria, Firmicutes, and Fusobacteria were detected, which are similar to the microbiota profile of normal elderly patients [18], consistent with findings from previous studies [19]. The Chao1 and ACE indices are obtained by calculating the number of different OTUs in the community, and reflect the richness of species in the community. The Shannon index and Simpson index represent community diversity, with higher values correlating with increased diversity. In this study, we found no significant differences in the Chao, Shannon, and ACE indices of oral microbiota between the two groups. However, the Simpson index was significantly different between the two groups. The Simpson index reflects the contribution of dominant species to the overall diversity and the species distribution status of the entire ecosystem. If a particular species dominates the ecosystem, the Simpson index tends to be relatively high. Therefore, the Simpson index provides a more robust indicator for assessing the impact of dominant species on overall diversity. After administration of Chlorhexidine oral care in elderly patients undergoing mechanical ventilation, the dominant bacterial species decreased, accompanied with a reduction of diversity, which suggests that Chlorhexidine may exert antibacterial effects in the short term. A previous study [20] suggested that the concept that Chlorhexidine oral care can prevent ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) might be biased, however, evidence supporting this is lacking. Further analysis showed that the use of different oral care solutions did not alter microbiota diversity at different time points, providing more intuitive evidence. Furthermore, we compared the abundance of the top five microorganisms at the genus level in the two groups and found no difference in the abundance of Halomonas, Streptococcus, Prevotella, Enterococcus, and Mycoplasma. Despite having a smaller microbial population compared to the gut, the human oral cavity exhibits remarkable microbial diversity, hosting more than 700 types of bacteria, fungi, and viruses. These microorganisms interact closely within the oral environment, playing a crucial role in maintaining oral health and significantly impacting overall systemic health [21].

Secondly, we found no significant difference in mechanical ventilation time, ICU hospitalization time, and patient outcomes among patients who received two types of oral care solutions. In the comparison of the 28-day mortality rate between the two groups, no significant difference between the two groups. This is consistent with the results of most previous studies. For instance, Tran et al. [22], [23], showed that Chlorhexidine can shorten mechanical ventilation time or reduce ICU hospitalization time. In ICU patients undergoing mechanical ventilation, discontinuation of Chlorhexidine may not affect the ICU mortality or ICU extubation time [24]. However, a study [25] suggest that daily tooth brushing in patients receiving mechanical ventilation may decrease the ICU mortality rates, shorter mechanical ventilation times, and shorter ICU hospitalization times. This demonstrates that the oral care method may have a greater impact on ICU mortality, mechanical ventilation time, and ICU hospitalization time in patients undergoing mechanical ventilation patients than the choice of oral care solution.

Furthermore, the efficacy of Chlorhexidine in preventing VAP may be dependent on its concentration, formulation, and frequency of use [25]. Here, we found no significant difference in the incidence of VAP among elderly patients on mechanical ventilation using different oral care solutions. However, most current studies suggest that Chlorhexidine mouthwash or gel may reduce the incidence of ventilator-associated pneumonia in critically ill patients [26–28]. The inconsistency observed in the results may vary depending on the method of oral care. In this study, we utilized tooth brushing as a method of oral care, which can potentially reduce the incidence of VAP through mechanical means [29]. Our findings suggest that consistent tooth brushing policies and programs should be adopted, as it is more effective than the choice of oral care solution.

Even with some variation in study results, 0.12% chlorhexidine digluconate is still generally accepted as an effective adjunct therapy for the reduction of oropharyngeal colonization of respiratory pathogens-a crucial factor preceding VAP. Several clinical trials and meta-analyses of clinical trials documented that routine use of a 0.12% chlorhexidine infection rate with few adverse effects including mucosal irritation, altered taste, and/or tooth staining. While our study failed to find statistically significant differences between groups in short-term infection outcomes, it is important to consider our results within the context of established evidence of chlorhexidine as a potential preventive measure when used appropriately and in conjunction with mechanical oral cleaning.

Finally, it has been shown that compared to non-mechanically ventilated patients, mechanically ventilated patients have poorer oral health and are more prone to oral complications [30]. Therefore, strategies for eliminating microorganisms and dental plaque from the oral cavity of patients can prevent the risk of complications such as VAP. Indeed, comprehensive oral care interventions provide better outcomes than conventional care methods in maintaining oral cleanliness and removing plaque and microorganisms [11]. In the present study, we found no significant difference in the effects of two different oral care solutions on the plaque index and oral complications [31]. Thus, we speculate that this is likely because comprehensive oral care intervention can maintain good oral hygiene and minimize the proliferation of bacteria in the oral cavity. The method of oral care may have a greater impact on plaque removal than the choice of oral care solution. In a study by Ozden et al. [32] there was no significant difference in oral mucosal integrity between groups that used saline, sodium bicarbonate, and 0.2% chlorhexidine for oral care, which is consistent with our findings. However, Ozdemir’s study [33] found significant differences in the occurrence of oral complications among different concentrations of Chlorhexidine, highlighting the need for further research to explore the application of multiple oral care solutions.

Research has shown that oral microbiota imbalance may be influenced by age [34]. Decreased immunity, tooth damage, and reduced saliva production, contribute to imbalance in oral microbiota. However, few studies have explored the impact of different oral care solutions on the oral microbiota among elderly patients undergoing mechanical ventilation. Here, we applied 16 S rRNA gene sequencing technology to analyze the abundance and core microbiota of the oral microbiota in patients after receiving mechanical ventilation, laying a firm foundation for future research.

This study had a number of limitations. First, the small sample size and short follow-up time reduced statistical power and limited the assessment of long-term effects of chlorhexidine on oral microbiota and clinical outcomes. A post-hoc power analysis determined that 35% power to detect between-group differences at α = 0.05 was available based upon the observed effect sizes. Second, unmeasured confounders, such as diet and concomitant medications, may affect microbial diversity. Furthermore, as this was a single-center trial in an older ICU cohort, generalizability is limited. Future studies should test this in multicenter investigations with a larger cohort that is more representative of patients in the ICU, with longer follow-up, and incorporating metagenomic or metabolomic analyses. Finally, research should explore the use of natural or bio derived oral care agents including plant extracts, postbiotics, and antimicrobial peptides to reduce pathogenic colonization, while preserving commensal microbiota in ventilated patients.

Conclusions

Chlorhexidine may change the oral microbiota in older adults on mechanical ventilation. There was no immediate increase in clinical infections. The potential for chlorhexidine to cause dysbiosis should keep clinicians aware of risks. Intentional incorporation of evidence-based oral care protocols that combine chlorhexidine with probiotics or other microbiota-preserving approaches as routines can improve clinical safety and reduce risk of infections. The initial findings from this study are preliminary and exploratory and should be viewed as hypothesis-generating. Larger, multicenter studies with longer follow-up and validation of observational approaches are needed to enhance understanding of findings and to inform appropriate oral care for mechanically ventilated older adults.
