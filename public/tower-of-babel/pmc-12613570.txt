Clinical features, microbiological characteristics, and treatment outcomes of fungal keratitis in a tertiary referral hospital in Eastern China
Authors: Jianfeng Yu, Qin Zhang, Kai Fan, Jiawei Luo, Pengfei Li, Chengyao Zheng, Pingping Liu, Huaijin Guan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFungal keratitis (FK) is a serious corneal disease that was considered as the main cause of vision loss in developing countries. Early diagnosis and timely treatment of FK can significantly strengthen the curative effect and decrease the risk of the blindness. The fungal spectrum varies and is closely associated with climate and geographic location. The clinical characteristics of FK in eastern China are limited. We explored the risk factors, fungal species, characteristics of in vivo confocal microscopy (IVCM), and treatment of FK in a tertiary referral hospital in eastern China.MethodsThis retrospective study encompassed a thorough analysis of clinical data, IVCM images, and fungal cultures in FK between January 2021 and May 2024. The clinical profiles, microbial characteristics, and therapeutic outcomes of these patients were meticulously analyzed.ResultsA total of 174 patients were diagnosed with FK. Seven fungal species were identified in 104 culture-positive cases. Fusarium was the most common species (49.04%), followed by Alternaria (23.08%) and Aspergillus (15.38%). 166 (95.4%) patients showed evidence of mycelia or spores in IVCM images. Diverse fungal strains typically showed varied culture characteristics and IVCM expressions. 42 (24.14%) patients were successfully treated with topical medicine alone, and 132 (75.86%) patients needed additional surgical therapies, including intrastromal injections of voriconazole (38.51%), conjunctival pedicle flap surgery (8.05%), lamellar keratoplasty (22.41%), penetrating keratoplasty (4.02%), and enucleation (2.87%). Visual acuity improved in 104 patients (59.77%), but remained unchanged in 36 (20.69%) patients and decreased in 34 patients (19.54%) following active treatment.Conclusions fusariumwas the most frequently isolated species, followed by Alternaria and Aspergillus.

Introduction

Fungal keratitis (FK) is a highly prevalent infectious disease of the cornea, which can lead to severe visual impairment [1]. FK typically develops following minor ocular trauma, especially plant-related injuries [2]. An estimated one million FK cases occur globally each year [2]. Approximately 8–11% of FK patients undergo enucleation, leading to an annual loss of 84,143–115,697 eyeballs [3]. The incidence of FK is increasing significantly, particularly with the rise in contact lens use [3].

Patients with FK frequently experience symptoms, including redness, pain, photophobia, and vision impairment, accompanied by clinical signs such as conjunctival hyperemia, chemosis, corneal infiltration, toothed or feathery margins, satellite infiltrates, endothelial plaques, anterior chamber inflammation, and hypopyon [2]. The gold standard for microbiological diagnosis of FK remains the fungal culture of corneal scrapings, despite its limitation of prolonged processing times [4]. In vivo confocal microscopy (IVCM) provides non-invasive, immediate and visual images of potential fungal pathogens in the living cornea. However, IVCM is unable to accurately identify the specific fungal species [5].

It is widely recognized that early diagnosis and timely treatment of FK can significantly strengthen the curative effect, reduce the occurrence of ocular complications, and lower the risk of the blindness. This study aims to investigate the epidemiology, diagnostic accuracy, and treatment outcomes of FK in a tertiary referral hospital in eastern China, addressing a critical gap in regional data.

Materials and methods

Patients

From January 2021 to May 2024, the clinical records of FK patients at the Affiliated Hospital of Nantong University were reviewed retrospectively. This study was approved by the ethics committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Nantong University and conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was a retrospective study, and informed consent was waived. The need for informed consent was waived by the ethics committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Nantong University.

Enrolled patients underwent anterior segment photography, IVCM, corneal scraping, and microbiological culture. The inclusion criteria included: (1) hyphae or spores observed in corneal smears or detected using IVCM; (2) positive fungal identification via microbial cultivation. The following conditions are excluded: (1) bacterial keratitis and other types of keratitis; (2) fungal endophthalmitis without keratitis; (3) any co-infections were ruled out. Relational data, including demographics, risk factors, clinical characteristics, fungal species, visual acuity, management, complications and therapeutic outcomes, were collected for further analysis.

IVCM

Fungal hyphae or spores were detected using a HRT3 with Rostock Corneal Module (Heidelberg, Germany). All the operations were performed by the trained technicians. Each corneal ulcer area was scanned following the previous standard procedure [6]. Briefly, a new IVCM Tomocap (Heidelberg, Germany) was placed over the microscope objective. 0.5% proparacaine (Alcon, TX, United States) was used for topical anesthesia. The volume scan mode, incorporating a z-stack of 40 images, was used for IVCM examination. Each image had 384 × 384 pixels covering an area of 400 μm×400 μm. Abundant branching highly reflective linear structures indicated a filamentous fungal infection. Longated particles or pseudomycelium indicated the yeast infection. IVCM images were captured for further analysis. The length, width and branching angles of hyphae were evaluated using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Corneal smear microscopy

Following anesthesia with 0.5% proparacaine, corneal scrapings were obtained from the margin and base of the ulcer using a 21-gauge needle. The corneal scrapings were evenly applied to the slides, and then stained with Giemsa for 15 min after methanol fixation. The slides were observed under a microscope after washing and drying.

Microbiological cultivation and diagnosis

Corneal scrapings were placed on blood agar and Sabourand medium for microbiological culture. The media were cultured under aerobic conditions. The Sabouraud medium were incubated at 27 ◦C for 3 weeks. The blood agar plates were incubated at 37 ◦C for 7 days. Microscopic analysis using lactophenol cotton blue staining were used to identify the fungal species. Culture was considered positive when the same fungal species displayed semiconfluent growth at the site of inoculation on at least one medium. The characteristics of fungal species were observed under a microscope.

Treatment procedures

FK patients were treated hourly with 5% natamycin (North China Pharmaceutical CO., Hebei, China), and 1% voriconazole (Pfizer, NY, USA) eye drops. With significant improvement in the condition, eye drops were administrated once every two hours for 4–8 weeks. Additionally, 1% atropine and 0.1% compound tropicamide eye drops were administrated three times a day. Patients with corneal ulcers larger than 6 mm in diameter or extending into the final one-third of the stroma underwent a subconjunctival injection of 0.5 mL voriconazole 1%. When the corneal ulcer was not effectively controlled by topical drug therapy, surgical treatment was performed. Corneal lesion excision combined with intrastromal injection of voriconazole (50 µg/0.1 mL) was used for deep recalcitrant FK. If the corneal ulcer continues to deteriorate, lamellar keratoplasty or penetrating keratoplasty was performed. Conjunctival flap covering surgery was performed if a high risk of corneal perforation was indicated, including the posterior corneal ulcer involving the posterior one-third corneal stroma. Lamellar keratoplasty was considered when the lesion was confined to the anterior 3/4 of the stroma, when the lesion diameter exceeded 6 mm, or when the condition had worsened over a period of more than 2 weeks. The indications for penetrating keratoplasty included progression of the corneal ulcer despite appropriate medical treatment, as well as impending corneal perforation or actual perforation greater than 2 mm in diameter. Further adjustment of the therapeutic schedule and addition of oral antifungal medicine were administrated based on the progression of FK and the results of fungal culture.

Therapeutic evaluation

The treatment process and complications were documented in details. FK healing was defined as the resolution of corneal ulcers, complete re-epithelialization, and absence of hyphae. Throughout the therapeutic process, the severe complications, including corneal perforation, loss of vision, and FK recurrence, were monitored. All patients were followed up at one week, two weeks, and one month post-discharge. The follow-up period is 3 months.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS 20.0 version (IBM-SPSS, IL, USA). The data were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Visual acuity was converted into logMAR for further analysis. The visual acuity levels of counting fingers, hand movement, perception to light and no perception to light were quantified as 1.9, 2.3, 2.8 and 3.0 logMAR, respectively [1]. Chi-square test was used for species distribution. The Paired t-test was used to determine significant visual improvement. T test was used for other continuous variables. The correlation between variables was assessed using Pearson’s test. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was employed to illustrate the risk of complications over time. p < 0.05 was deemed to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Demographic information and risk factors

Generally, a total of 174 FK patients were enrolled from January 2021 to May 2024. The mean age of the patients was 64.68 ± 9.24 years (range: 39–89 years). There were 101 males (58.05%) and 73 females (41.95%) in the study. All patients had unilateral infections. The number of rural patients (145, 83.33%) was significantly higher than that of urban patients (29, 16.67%). Ocular trauma in 94 patients (54.02%) was the primary risk factor. Among the patients with ocular trauma, 59 (62.77%) patients were male, and 35 (37.23%) patients were female. Among the trauma-related patients, 59 patients (62.77%) reported vegetative injuries, 29 patients (30.85%) reported non-vegetative mechanical injuries, and 6 patients (6.38%) reported chemical or thermal injuries (Table 1). Among the cases with vegetative trauma, 34 (57.63%) males and 25 (42.37%) females were included.

Findings of causative organisms

Corneal scrapings from FK patients were collected for microbiological cultivation. Direct microscopic examination of the Giemsa-stained corneal smears showed that 89.66% of FK cases were hyphae-positive. Fungal cultures were positive in 59.77% cases. A total of seven fungal species were identified. Among the causative fungi, the filamentous fungi were the primary pathogenic species, accounting for 96.15% (100 of 104). Fusarium (51 of 104, 49.04%) was the most frequently isolated fungal pathogen, followed by Alternaria (24 of 104, 23.08%), Aspergillus (16 of 104, 15.38%), Scedosporium apiospermum (7 of 104, 6.73%), Candida (4 of 104, 3.85%), Curvularia (1 of 104, 0.96%) and Paecilomyces lilacinu (1 of 104, 0.96%), respectively. Fusarium (49.04%) was significantly more common than Alternaria (23.08%) and Aspergillus (15.38%) (p < 0.001). All patients were infected with a single fungal pathogen. In vivo confocal microscopic characteristics

Among culture-positive cases, hyphae or spores were detected in 166 patients using IVCM, and the positive incidence was 95.4%. Diverse fungi displayed distinct characteristics in IVCM images. Filamentous fungi exhibited highly reflective, linear, and branching structures with various reflectance and lengths. Fusarium keratitis showed a large number of highly reflective hyphae with 238 ± 79.19 μm in length, 2.7 ± 0.97 μm in width, and branching angles at 67.42° ± 6.1° (Fig. 2A). The IVCM images of Alternaria keratitis showed highly reflective filamentous structures with 149.33 ± 75.74 μm in length, 2.7 ± 0.89 μm in width, and branching angles at 61.48° ± 8.16° (Fig. 2B). The IVCM images of Aspergillus keratitis showed high-contrast hyphae with 162.76 ± 66.44 μm in length, 3.87 ± 2.24 μm in width, and branching angles at 60.69° ± 10.9° (Fig. 2C). The Scedosporium apiospermum filaments presented with 197.9 ± 57.6 μm in length, 2.65 ± 0.95 μm in width, and branching angles at 52.44° ± 16.62° (Fig. 2D). Curvularia hyphae showed short and thick structures with 33.36 ± 9.91 μm in length and 3.18 ± 1.47 μm in width, but no obvious branching angles in IVCM images (Fig. 2E). The IVCM images of Candida displayed abundant pseudomycelium and few reflective Candida spores (Fig. 2F).

Medical and surgical treatment outcome

All patients underwent topical antifungal treatment, including 5% natamycin eye drops (66, 37.93%) and 1% voriconazole (168, 96.55%). A total of 42 (24.14%) patients were successfully treated with topical medical management alone. Overall, 132 (75.86%) patients with deep or large corneal lesions required additional surgical therapies to control the infection and/or complications. Corneal debridement combined with intrastromal injections of voriconazole (67, 38.51%) was the most commonly performed surgery, followed by conjunctival pedicle flap surgery (14, 8.05%, Fig. 3A), lamellar keratoplasty (39, 22.41%, Fig. 3B), and penetrating keratoplasty (7, 4.02%, Fig. 3C). 28 (16.09%) patients developed severe complications during the treatment, including corneal perforation, FK recurrence, graft rejection, and panophthalmitis. 5 patients (5, 2.87%) underwent enucleation. Regarding the time for seeking appropriate medical attention in the hospital, we observed the following trends: Patients who received only drug treatment had a mean time of (8.76 ± 5.1) days. In contrast, those who underwent operative treatment had a significantly longer mean time of (13.9 ± 7.87) days. There is a significant statistical difference (P < 0.001). A larger infiltrate size at presentation significantly predicted worse visual acuity (p = 0.001).

Discussion

FK is a common clinical infection that was considered as the main cause of vision loss in developing countries [7]. Generally, the prevalence rates of FK in developing countries with tropical and subtropical climates are much higher than those in developed countries with cold or temperate climates [8]. Previous studies have demonstrated that the causative fungi of FK vary depending on several factors, including geographical regions, climates, and urbanization [9]. Climatic differences within countries are closely associated with regional variations. Owing to the vast territory of China, the climate exhibits marked diversity across different locations. Epidemiological investigations have reported that the prevalence of FK was 26.8–57.6% among infectious keratitis in China [10–13]. It is essential to figure out the profile of causative fungi in different climates, although there have been some retrospective studies on FK in China [12, 14, 15]. However, there are few reports associated with the clinical characteristics of FK in eastern China, despite its clinical significance. In the study, we explored the risk factors, fungal species, IVCM characteristics, and treatment of FK in a tertiary referral hospital in eastern China.

As we know, ocular trauma is the major risk factor for FK in China [8]. FK frequently occurs in agricultural workers who accidentally suffer from ocular vegetative injuries [8]. Our findings showed that 83.33% of FK patients came from rural areas. Corneal trauma, particularly plant-related injuries, is the predominant risk factor for FK. In contrast, in developed countries, ocular surface diseases are more commonly associated with FK [1]. Previous literatures have reported that contact lens wear was a significant risk factor for FK in developed countries [3, 12, 16]. However, no FK cases caused by contact lens are included in the study. This is because most Chinese farmers in this study do not commonly use contact lenses. Our findings highlight a notable difference in the etiology of FK between our study population and those reported in developed countries. For the farmers who do wear contact lenses, it remains crucial to maintain proper hygiene and remain vigilant about the potential risks associated with FK.

Early identification of the causative pathogens is crucial for the effective management of FK [17]. It has been reported that over 70 different fungal species can be involved in developing FK [18, 19]. Previous studies have demonstrated that over 95% of FK cases were caused by filamentous fungi, with Fusarium and Aspergillus being the predominant pathogens [3]. In line with recent research conducted in China, our study also found that Fusarium species were the most frequently identified causative agents, accounting for 49.04% of the cases [12, 15]. In contrast to other studies [2, 12, 13, 15, 16, 20], Alternaria accounted for 23.08% of the cases, surpassing the proportion of Aspergillus; however, no statistically significant difference was observed. Alternaria, a ubiquitous dematiaceous filamentous fungus, is commonly found in soil, plants, and decay. Several studies reported varying prevalence rates of Alternaria in ocular infections, ranging from 3.3% to 10.4%, with significant geographic variation. Notably, higher incidence rates are consistently observed in warm and humid regions [21]. The tertiary referral hospital is situated in a subtropical monsoon climate near the Yangtze River estuary, where the air is exceptionally humid. This unique environmental setting may significantly influence the distribution of fungal species. Among those agricultural workers, occupational exposure is a significant risk factor due to their frequent contact with contaminated soil and plant debris.

Until now, the microbiological culture of corneal samples has remained the gold standard for diagnosing FK, as it enables the definitive identification of specific fungal species [22]. However, fungal culture requires a slow turnaround time and necessitates specialized laboratory facilities [4]. In this study, the positive rate of fungal culture was 59.77%, which was slightly higher than that reported in northern China (51.6%) [12], but lower than the rate observed in central China (74.12%) [15]. Overall, FK is characterized by nonspecific clinical features, a variable positive rate of microbial culture, and an extended time required for fungal culture [23]. These factors can lead to delays in diagnosis and appropriate treatment. In this context, smear microscopy of corneal scrapings and IVCM play crucial roles in the diagnosis of FK [23].

Direct smear microscopy serves as a fast, low cost and accurate method to identify fungal structures, including hyphae or candida forms in FK [22]. A positive examination of fungal hyphae in corneal tissue is highly indicative of FK. There are various smear staining techniques used for identifying fungal microorganisms in the diagnosis of FK, including potassium hydroxide (KOH) wet mount, Giemsa stain, Gram stain, and lactophenol cotton blue [23, 24]. However, the accurate recognition of fungal structures and the identification of fungal species depend on the observer’s experience [23, 24]. Our findings show that the sensitivity of Giemsa-stained smear microscopy was 89.66%, which is in line with earlier reports [23]. IVCM is a novel optical microscopy technique used for detecting potential fungal pathogens with high sensitivity and specificity [25, 26]. Among all cases diagnosed with FK in the study, 166 (95.4%) cases exhibited positive findings of hyphae or spores in IVCM images. Compared to microbial culture results in vitro, IVCM may offer a more convenient and effective approach for observing the morphological characteristics of causative fungi. Previous studies have reported that the branching angles of filamentous fungi in IVCM images could be used to distinguish Fusarium and Aspergillus [27]. However, recent reports found no significant difference in the branching angles between Fusarium and Aspergillus, which is consistent with our findings [25, 28]. Nonetheless, IVCM examination is emerging as a valuable approach for the early detection of fungal hyphae in FK. In addition, IVCM allowed for real-time, dynamic tracking of FK treatment. Throughout treatment, regular examination using IVCM could provide direct visualization of fungal hyphae within the corneal tissue. Persistent or newly emerging hyphal growth on IVCM images indicated the need to intensify therapeutic measures or adjust the treatment plan. In the study, the indications for the eradication of hyphae included the following clinical characteristics: (1) the absence of fungal hyphae, debris, and inflammatory cells on IVCM imaging, and (2) complete resolution of the corneal ulcer with full re-epithelialization.

The management of antifungal medications is the primary treatment for FK. Natamycin is the only antifungal drug approved by the US-FDA for treating ocular fungal infections [17]. Currently, topical natamycin has been recognized as the first-line choice for FK [29]. However, natamycin has difficulty in penetrating the corneal stroma, and covers the ulcer surface which may impair the absorption of other drugs. Approximately one quarter of those natamycin-treated FK cases continue to deteriorate to corneal perforation and even intractable endophthalmitis [30, 31]. The unsatisfactory therapeutic effect of 5% natamycin eye drops may be attributed to several factors, including poor ocular penetration, reduced sensitivity of the causative pathogens to the drug, and the severity of the condition. In clinical practice, combined treatment has generally been recommended, especially for patients with deep corneal lesions. Voriconazole is the second-best topical drug available for the treatment of FK [29]. Voriconazole has a broad antifungal spectrum [32], and is considered as a good substitute for natamycin-resistant FK cases [33, 34]. A total of 42 (24.14%) cases were successfully treated with topical administration of natamycin and voriconazole. Additionally, an adjuvant therapy with intrastromal voriconazole seems to be a safe and effective approach for moderate-to-severe FK that responds poorly to conventional antifungals [35–37]. Voriconazole injections along the margins of corneal lesions can form a barrage surrounding the fungal infiltration [38]. In the study, corneal debridement combined with intrastromal injections of voriconazole (99, 56.9%) was the most frequently performed surgery. Conjunctival flap surgery is an effective alternative approach for refractory FK when keratoplasty is unavailable [39]. Among 14 cases who underwent conjunctival flap surgery, 13 (92.86%) patients successfully recovered, and only one patient with treatment failure underwent enucleation. Therapeutic keratoplasty, including anterior lamellar keratoplasty and penetrating keratoplasty, is the best treatment strategy for intractable FK or large perforations [40]. In the study, 39 (22.41%) patients underwent lamellar keratoplasty, and 7 patients (4.02%) received penetrating keratoplasty. It is vitally important to identify the higher risk of corneal perforation and timely perform appropriate therapeutic management. All those 46 cases with uncontrolled infections or large perforations were successfully treated with keratoplasty.

Conclusions

In the tertiary referral hospital in Eastern China, Fusarium is the most commonly isolated fungal species, followed by Alternaria and Aspergillus. The prevalence of Alternaria is significantly increased compared to most areas in China. Plant-related ocular trauma is the main risk factor for FK. Outdoor workers need appropriate eye protection and appropriate medical attention for any ocular injuries. IVCM has demonstrated high sensitivity in detecting fungal hyphae, supporting its role in the early diagnosis of FK. Antifungal therapy combined with surgery is an effective strategy for difficult to treat FK. Rapid diagnosis, accurate assessment of corneal infections, and appropriate treatment determine the prognosis for vision.
