Problem

Recall that a plane polygon is said to be
simple

Problem A.
.

Given an integer
n\geq 1

n

e(n)
– it can have?

This sequence (first proposed by the author) has been already assigned a number A375986 in the OEIS

as the
Politrigon sequence
. For
n=1
, there is nothing to prove. For
n=2
, the answer is straightforward. The union of two triangles, if a simple
M
-gon, can have any number of sides in the range
3\leq M\leq 10
, and also
M=12
. The latter is mostly familiar from a pleasingly symmetric hexagram known as
the star of David
. Starting from
n=3
, the problem becomes increasingly tricky. In order to prove that
e(3)=22
, one needs not-so-short case-by-case analysis. The three initial examples (

) are presented in Fig.
1

e(n)
-gon (the union of
n

For
n\geq 4
, the exact value is unknown. Sure, meticulous and tedious case-by-case analysis is still possible. Aesthetically, this is completely unsatisfactory. Some other method (if it exists) should be looked for. The more productive approach is to perform a wide and dense search (human - computer interaction), applying many
ad hoc
improvements on the way. In doing search, we fix two vertices of one triangle, letting all the remaining float freely. This gives
6n-4
degrees of freedom. That is, a subset of a
(6n-4)
th dimensional space over which maximization should be performed. Such dense coverage is hardly doable for larger
n
, unless several theoretical simplifications are contrived.

For the starters, we will prove the lower bound by simply providing a construction.

Proposition
.

For
n\geq 4
,
e(n)\geq 11n-11
.

If (by some Prophesy of the Grand Oracle) one new beforehand that the inequality is in fact equality (even without having all means to prove it), the problem would lose some of its allure. But it does not! (A spoiler:
e(7)\geq 67
,
e(8)\geq 79
). It can happen, though, that
11n
is still the correct order of growth. Yet, the possibility that it is faster than that is not implausible.

Indeed, going much ahead of ourselves, let us write down the known values and bounds for the sequence
e(n)
, putting edge count increases in between:

\displaystyle 3\,\,_{9}\,12\,\,_{10}\,22\,\,_{11}\,33\,\,_{11}\,\,44\,\,_{11}\,55\,\,_{12}\,67\,\,_{12}\,79.

If one managed to chisel a construction, say, for
n=11

e(11)\geq 79+12+12+13
, this would heavily point to the fact that the growth of
e(n)
, contrary to intuition, is in fact quadratic!

Pushing beyond

Can we get beyond
11n-11
for larger
n
? All attempts to tackle this problem convinces the solver that numeric approach should come first. As mentioned, in order to understand the core of this problem, a dense and wide search needs to be performed. Since
6n-4

Problem A’.
.

Given an integer
n\geq 1
. Suppose,
3n
points-angles (labelled
1
through
3n
in an increasing order) are chosen on the unit circle. Assume, the
i

1\leq i\leq n
) is formed by vertices
i,n+i,2n+i

c(n)
– of sides it can have.

Thus, we not only limit the geometry of triangles (all their circumcircles are identical), but also impose a strict cyclic order on their vertices.

Obviously,
c(n)\leq e(n)
. Since the Problem A’ has
n-1
degree of freedom, it is hardly believable that

can match
e(n)
for larger

. However, concrete examples (extracted from wide search) show that the sequence

stars as
3,12,22
, and

is achievable for

. In other words, nothing worse that

itself.

Canonical specimen from the species

Now, imagine that for certain
n
we have found the exact value of
e(n)
. How among all unions of
n
triangles, if the latter is a simple
e(n)
-gon, one extracts the unique specimen (or, at least, a finite number of competing ones) which optimizes the construction? By “optimal” we mean the “most pleasing” one. Mathematically speaking, we are looking for a polygon which is as close to a disc as possible. If we were talking about all simple
M
-gons, majority of mathematicians would agree that “optimal” in this case should mean “regular”.

Therefore, to strictly define “optimality”, one needs to distinguish a regular
M
-gon from all
M
-gons by some simple criteria. The first thing which comes to mind is isoperimetric property: among all
M
-gons with a fixed perimeter, a regular
M
-gon has the maximal area (see
bogosel
for the mice geometric proof).

Unfortunately, the isoperimetric property does not distinguish regular hexagram from all unions of two triangles with
12
sides. Indeed, consider two intersecting unit equilateral triangles with parallel sides (see Fig.
9
). Denote side lengths of an inner hexagon by
a,C,b,A,c,B
(in that order).

Figure 9:
Two equilateral triangles

By a simple calculation,
a+b+c=1
,
a=A
,
b=B
,
c=C
. The perimeter of the union is thus always
4
. On the other hand, the area of the union is equal to
\frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}(1+a^{2}+b^{2}+c^{2})
. The maximum is achieved when
(a,b,c)
approaches
(1,0,0)
. Even worse, the desired point
(a,b,c)=(\frac{1}{3},\frac{1}{3},\frac{1}{3})
gives a minimum for an area rather than a maximum. The isoperimetric property, consequently, works heavily against the hexagram.

Yet, the property itself can be salvaged due to the following observation. With respect to a homothety, an area is a quadratic function whereas a perimeter is linear. So what we actually compare to an area is not a perimeter
per se
but rather its square. Since the sum of squares is a simpler function than the square of a sum, this hints to the following formulation.

Problem B.
.

Let
M=e(n)
. Suppose an intersection of
n
triangles is a simple
M
-gon
\mathcal{P}
with side lengths equal to
\ell_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq M
. Find the one which realizes the maximum of the function

\displaystyle\vartheta=\frac{S(\mathcal{P})}{L(\mathcal{P})},\,S(\mathcal{P})=\text{area of polygon }\mathcal{P},\,L(\mathcal{P})=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{M}\ell_{i}^{2}.

If we were dealing with all
M
-gons, regular
M
-gon is optimal again.
