A serial 4-year cross-sectional study of dyslipidemia on Pamirs Plateau, the roof of the world
Authors: Yisi Liu, Yue Shu, Xinyu Wang, Bin Li, Dai Su, Chaoji Huangfu, Zhu Huang, Yuxin Wang, Shuqian Zhang, Taiwei Wang, Chaoyue Gao, Lin Luo, Zexinyao Ren, Rui Wang, Yue Gao, Wei Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground Dyslipidemia is a major cardiovascular risk factor; however, disease patterns—particularly lipid profiles—remain understudied in high-altitude populations. On the Pamirs Plateau (> 4000 m), no relevant epidemiological studies have been conducted to date. This study investigates the disease patterns in this region, with an emphasis on dyslipidemia epidemiology.MethodsWe conducted a serial cross-sectional study (2021–2024) using annual health examination data from Tashkurgan County, Pamirs Plateau. Adults aged ≥ 18 years residing ≥ 1 year were included. We first examined the overall disease patterns, with a focus on dyslipidemia. Age-standardized prevalence was calculated and stratified by sex and ethnicity. Subsequently, lipid profile distributions and temporal trends were analyzed. To place these findings in a global context, LDL-C levels were compared with populations from plains and other high-altitude regions. Finally, potential risk factors were identified using multivariate logistic regression and machine learning models.ResultsAmong a representative subset (24.37%) of the Pamirs Plateau population, dyslipidemia was the most prevalent condition, followed by hypertension, sinus bradycardia, and fatty liver. The prevalence of dyslipidemia ranged from 25.24% to 37.64%. LDL-C levels were lower than those in plains and other high-altitude regions. Ethnic disparities were evident: the minority population (predominantly Tajik) maintained stable, favorable lipid profiles across ages, while non-minorities exhibited pronounced age-related fluctuations. Age 70 emerged as a potential inflection point at which sex-related differences in LDL-C levels reversed. Male, non-minority, older age, diabetes, frequent alcohol consumption, and higher education were significantly associated with dyslipidemia. Unexpectedly, improved living conditions (range hood use [OR: 1.839, 95% CI: 1.673–2.021], natural gas use [OR: 1.273, 95% CI: 1.152–1.406], tap water access [OR: 1.315, 95% CI: 1.204–1.436]) were linked to a higher risk, a “modernization paradox”. Additionally, distinct temporal fluctuations were observed: LDL-C levels sharply declined in 2022 before rebounding, coinciding with the COVID-19 lockdown period.ConclusionsThis first comprehensive analysis from “the roof of the world” reveals unique lipid patterns, including low LDL-C levels, pandemic-era fluctuations, ethnic disparities, unique age-sex patterns, and a paradoxical association between improved living conditions and increased dyslipidemia risk. These insights should inform context-specific lipid management strategies for this and similar vulnerable high-altitude populations globally.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-025-04526-9.Keywords: Dyslipidemia, The Pamirs Plateau, Tashkurgan county, Living conditions, Cross-sectional study

Background

Dyslipidemia is a major modifiable risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD) [1], increasing the risk of CVD by fivefold [2]. While numerous epidemiological studies have documented dyslipidemia prevalence globally, the prevalence and patterns of dyslipidemia vary considerably across geographic regions and ethnic populations [3–6].

Currently, epidemiological research has predominantly concentrated in low- to mid-altitude regions with ready access to healthcare and research resources, where prevalence typically ranges from 35.40 to 60.30% [7–10]. By contrast, data from high-altitude areas—often socioeconomically disadvantaged and medically underserved—remains limited. Globally, more than 400 million people reside in high-altitude regions above 1500 m [11], where physiological stressors, primarily chronic hypobaric hypoxia, induce complex physiological adaptations. However, the impact of these adaptations on lipid profiles among long-term high-altitude residents remains incompletely understood, with studies showing inconsistent results, potentially due to variations in population genetics and altitude levels [12–14]. A study conducted in the Jiarong Tibetan area demonstrated a U-shaped relationship between lipid levels and altitude across three groups (1500–2399 m, 2400–3299 m, and ≥ 3300 m) [15]. A study from Peru reported conflicting results, indicating a positive association between high altitude and both hypertriglyceridemia and low high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) levels [16]. In contrast, other studies have shown that populations residing at high altitudes (≥ 3500 m) tend to have a lower prevalence of dyslipidemia compared to those living below 3500 m [17]. Similarly, research from Ecuador indicated that adults living at high altitudes (2758–2787 m) had a lower likelihood of hypercholesterolemia than those living at sea level [18]. Notably, epidemiological health data, particularly concerning dyslipidemia, from populations inhabiting extreme environments, such as those from the Pamirs Plateau—“the roof of the world”—home to around 98,000 residents living at an average altitude exceeding 4000 m, are virtually non-existent [19]. Addressing this paucity of information is essential for understanding health patterns and developing appropriate public health strategies for these unique and often underserved populations.

Furthermore, high-altitude environments often harbor ethnically diverse population [20], adding another layer of complexity. The Pamirs Plateau is predominantly inhabited by Tajik populations, alongside Uyghur, Kazakh, Kyrgyz, and Han ethnicities [21]. Different ethnic groups may possess distinct genetic backgrounds influencing metabolic pathways, diverse cultural practices shaping diet and lifestyle, and potentially divergent physiological adaptations acquired over generations of residence in the shared hypoxic environment [22]. The interplay between chronic high-altitude exposure and specific ethnic heritage in modulating lipid metabolism remains poorly understood.

To address this knowledge gap, we analyzed health examination data collected from adult residents of Tashkurgan County, situated on the Pamirs Plateau, between 2021 and 2024. Its population is predominantly of Tajik ethnicity, co-residing with smaller proportions of other groups (Uyghur, Kazakh, Kyrgyz, and Han individuals). Previous studies on lipid profiles in ethnic minorities have primarily focused on populations such as the Uyghur and Tibetan [23, 24], evidence concerning the Tajik population is extremely limited. Additionally, this region is undergoing socioeconomic transition, from traditional agriculture and animal husbandry to increasing modernization, potentially altering lifestyle patterns and related health outcomes [25]. This unique confluence of extreme altitude, specific ethnic composition, and evolving environmental and lifestyle factors makes Tashkurgan County an invaluable, yet understudied, “natural laboratory” for exploring how these factors interact to influence dyslipidemia risk.

This study has three primary aims: (1) to characterize disease patterns over a 4-year period in Pamirs Plateau residents, with an emphasis on the prevalence and temporal trends (2021–2024) of dyslipidemia; (2) to describe the lipid profiles and examine variations according to age, sex, and ethnicity; and (3) to identify the risk factors associated with dyslipidemia in this specific high-altitude, multi-ethnic setting, considering demographic, clinical, and importantly, household environmental factors reflecting living conditions. To our knowledge, this is the first large-scale epidemiological investigation on disease patterns and lipid profiles within this unique high-altitude population on the Pamirs Plateau. By examining temporal trends across recent years, exploring potentially novel environmental risk factors alongside traditional ones, and enabling direct ethnic comparisons within a shared high-altitude context, this study offers valuable insights into the complex interplay among altitude, ethnicity, lifestyle modernization, and lipid metabolism. These findings may inform targeted prevention strategies for this and similar vulnerable populations globally.

Methods

Study design and participants

This serial cross-sectional study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [26]. We analyzed health examination data collected over four consecutive years (2021–2024) from Tashkurgan County to evaluate temporal trends in the prevalence and risk factors of dyslipidemia. This high-altitude region (an average elevation exceeding 4000 m) is primarily inhabited by individuals engaged in agriculture and animal husbandry, with a relatively stable demographic structure. The primary ethnic groups include minority groups (Tajik, Uyghur, Kazakh, Kyrgyz, and other ethnic groups) and non-minority (Han populations). Individuals were eligible for inclusion if they were aged ≥ 18 years and had resided in the region for at least 1 year. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) pregnant or breastfeeding women; (2) presence of severe chronic conditions, including end-stage renal disease, advanced cancer, and severe liver disease (representing a small proportion of the study population, 0.63–0.73%); (3) diagnosis of mental health disorders; and (4) missing key variables (as detailed in Additional file 1: Table S1).

The study received approval from the Ethics Committee of the Academy of Beijing Institute of Radiation Medicine, with the approval number AF/SC-08/02.153. Informed consent was waived as the data were derived from routine community health checkups.

Data collection

Survey questionnaire

A standardized questionnaire was administered by trained interviewers proficient in both the local language and Mandarin at designated community health centers. The questionnaire collected comprehensive data on socio-demographic characteristics, medical history, family history of diseases, living environment, and health-related behaviors (as detailed in Additional file 1: Table S2).

Physical measurements

Physical examinations were conducted in accordance with a standardized protocol. Anthropometric measurements, including height, weight, and waist circumference, were obtained in the morning after an overnight fast, with participants standing barefoot and without outerwear. Blood pressure was measured in a seated position after at least 5 min of rest using a validated automated sphygmomanometer. Two blood pressure readings were taken at an interval of at least 3 min, and the average value was recorded. If the right arm was unsuitable for measurement, the left arm was used instead.

Laboratory measurements

Fasting venous blood samples were collected from all participants following standardized procedures. Biochemical parameters, including fasting plasma glucose, serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), albumin (Alb), serum creatinine (SCr), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), were measured in a certified laboratory. All laboratory analyses adhered to rigorous quality control measures to ensure accuracy and comparability.

Definition of study variables

Outcome variables

According to the 2023 Chinese guideline for lipid management [27], high TC was defined as TC ≥ 5.2 mmol/L, high TG as TG ≥ 1.7 mmol/L, low HDL-C as HDL-C < 1.0 mmol/L, and high LDL-C as LDL-C ≥ 3.4 mmol/L. Participants were classified as having dyslipidemia if they met any of the following conditions: (1) high TG, (2) low HDL-C, or (3) high LDL-C. TC was not included in the diagnosis of dyslipidemia, as it is primarily determined by HDL-C and LDL-C levels [28]. The definitions remained consistent throughout the study period. Variable assignments are detailed in Additional file 1: Table S1.

Predictor variables

A total of 28 predictor variables were selected from the 2021 baseline survey, categorized as follows: demographic characteristics (such as sex, age, ethnicity); medical history (hypertension, diabetes); family history (hypertension, diabetes); living environment (kitchen ventilation systems, fuel types, sources of drinking water, livestock barn, toilet); physical health indicators (body mass index (BMI), waist circumference); health behaviors (such as physical activity, dietary habits, smoke status); liver function indicator (ALT, AST, Alb); and kidney function indicator (SCr, BUN).

Ethnicity was categorized as non-minority (Han population) and minority (including Tajik, Uyghur, Kazakh, Kyrgyz, and other ethnic groups). Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg [29], or self-reported history of hypertension. Diabetes was defined as fasting plasma glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L [30] or self-reported history of diabetes. Family history of hypertension or diabetes was defined as having at least one first-degree relative diagnosed with the respective condition. BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height (m2) and categorized as follows: underweight (< 18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (18.5–23.9 kg/m2), overweight (24.0–27.9 kg/m2), and obesity (≥ 28.0 kg/m2) [31]. Central obesity was defined as waist circumference ≥ 90 cm in men and ≥ 85 cm in women [32]. Physical activity was categorized into four levels: never (no participation in moderate- or high-intensity activity); occasional (less than 150 min/week of moderate- or less than 75 min/week of high-intensity activity, but not entirely inactive); frequent (≥ 150 min/week of moderate- or ≥ 75 min/week of high-intensity activity); and daily (almost daily participation in physical activity, with at least 30 min of moderate-intensity exercise per day). Dietary habits were classified as balanced or unbalanced. A balanced diet was characterized by a pattern based primarily on cereals and tubers, with adequate intake of vegetables, fruits, dairy, and moderate animal-source foods, while limiting salt, oil, and added sugar. An unbalanced diet was defined as a dietary pattern primarily based on either meat or plant-based foods with limited variety, or characterized by high consumption of salty, oily, or sugary foods. Smoke status was categorized as current smoking (smoking at least one cigarette per day in the past 12 months) or non-smoking. Drink status was classified as never (no alcohol intake in the past 12 months), occasional (≤ 2 times/month), and frequent (≥ 3 times/month).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as means and standard deviations (SDs), while categorical variables are summarized as frequencies and percentages. Group differences in continuous variables were assessed using multivariate linear regression, adjusting for variables with a P-value < 0.2 in univariate analysis. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square (χ2) test or univariate logistic regression. Bonferroni correction applied during multiple group comparisons. Given the small proportion of non-minority participants, stratified bootstrap resampling (1000 iterations) was performed for ethnic subgroup analyses to assess the robustness of observed patterns. Variables with a P-value < 0.2 in univariate analysis were included in multivariate logistic regression using a backward stepwise approach to identify risk factors for dyslipidemia, with results presented as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Age-standardized prevalence, with the 18–29 and ≥ 90 years groups combined, and other groups divided into 10-year intervals, was estimated via direct standardization using the 2021 population as the reference. Specifically, age-standardized prevalence was calculated by multiplying the age-specific prevalence by the corresponding weights of the standard population, summing these products, and dividing by the total population of the standard population (in this study, the 2021 population) [33–35].

Additionally, five machine learning models were employed to evaluate the relative importance ranking of all variables for dyslipidemia. To mitigate overfitting, the hold-out method was applied, randomly dividing the dataset into a training set (n = 46,914) and a test set (n = 20,106) at a 7:3 ratio before any data preprocessing [36]. Given the binary nature of the outcome variables, supervised learning algorithms were used to predict the likelihood of dyslipidemia [37]. The machine learning models implemented included logistic regression, logistic regression with least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regularization (Lasso regression), random forest (RF), gradient-boosted decision tree (GBDT), and support vector machine (detailed in Additional file 1: Table S3). In the test set, model performance was evaluated using the area under the curve (AUC) value, sensitivity (Eq. 1), specificity (Eq. 2), accuracy (Eq. 3), positive predictive value (PPV) (Eq. 4), and negative predictive value (NPV) (Eq. 5). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were utilized to determine the optimal classification threshold, with AUC ≥ 0.7 considered an adequate level of performance [36]. The DeLong test was conducted to compare AUC differences between models [38]. Variable importance was assessed in the GBDT and RF models.

True positives (TP) and true negatives (TN) represent correctly classified cases of dyslipidemia and non-dyslipidemia, respectively, while false positives (FP) and false negatives (FN) indicate misclassified cases. To minimize random bias and enhance robustness, we additionally applied fivefold cross-validation in our machine learning analysis. A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 29.) and R Studio.

Results

Baseline characteristics of study participants

A total of 16,715 (8227 men and 8488 women), 17,535 (8781 men and 8754 women), 15,020 (7312 men and 7708 women), and 17,749 (8743 men and 9006 women) participants were included in 2021, 2022, 2023, and 2024, respectively. Among these, 8398 participants were enrolled annually. Over the 4-year period, a cumulative total of 23,880 participants were enrolled, accounting for 58.25% of long-term residents in Tashkurgan County and 24.37% of the population on the Pamirs Plateau (“the roof of the world”) [19, 39]. The mean (SD) age of participants was 42.1 (14.6), 43.7 (14.7), 43.5 (13.3), and 43.9 (14.6) years, respectively. The study population was predominantly composed of minorities, with the Tajik group being the largest. The proportion of non-minority residents (Han population) remained consistently low, ranging from 2.25% to 3.70%, reflecting the actual and stable ethnic composition of the region. The baseline characteristics of participants stratified by dyslipidemia, high TC, high TG, low HDL-C, and high LDL-C were summarized in Table 1 and Additional file 1: Tables S4–7.

Overview of diseases in Pamirs Plateau

A serial cross-sectional analysis identified dyslipidemia, hypertension, sinus bradycardia, and fatty liver disease as the most prevalent conditions in this high-altitude region (Fig. 1A–D). The prevalence of dyslipidemia declined markedly to 25.24% in 2022, followed by consecutive annual increases of 32.65% in 2023 and 12.43% in 2024. A similar trend was observed for hypertension, which decreased by 10.56% from 2021 to 2023 before rebounding to 21.72% in 2024. We further categorized diseases into seven systems for analysis and found that endocrine, digestive, and circulatory system diseases were the most prevalent in this region, while respiratory, reproductive, and nervous system diseases were relatively uncommon (Additional file 1: Fig. S1). Additionally, a subgroup analysis based on ethnicity revealed that ethnic minorities exhibited a higher prevalence of circulatory, respiratory, and reproductive diseases, whereas non-minority individuals had a higher prevalence of endocrine, digestive, and nervous diseases (Additional file 1: Fig. S1). Notably, the prevalence of respiratory diseases demonstrated a consistent annual increase, peaking in 2024 at 10.48% in the minority group and 4.05% in the non-minority group. In contrast, endocrine system diseases showed a sharp decline in 2022, followed by a gradual resurgence in subsequent years (Additional file 1: Fig. S1).

Temporal and demographic variations in dyslipidemia prevalence and lipid profile

From 2021 to 2024, the overall prevalence of dyslipidemia in our population was 33.80% (8071 cases out of 23,880 screened individuals), ranging from 25.24% to 37.64% (Fig. 2A). A noticeable decline was observed in 2022, with the prevalence of dyslipidemia dropping to 25.24%, high TC to 10.07%, and high LDL-C to 6.07%, followed by a sharp increase in subsequent years. Meanwhile, high TG prevalence demonstrated a consistent upward trend, while low HDL-C prevalence steadily declined (Fig. 2A). Age-standardized prevalence (Fig. 2B) and annual percentage changes (APC, Fig. 2C) reflected similar trends, with high LDL-C prevalence increasing sharply from 2022 to 2023, before returning to baseline levels in 2024. Analysis of dyslipidemia subtype composition further corroborated these findings (Additional file 1: Fig. S2). Importantly, longitudinal analysis of 8,398 participants enrolled annually from 2021 to 2024 demonstrated consistent temporal patterns (Fig. 2D), likely due to the stable health status of the cohort and consistent demographic characteristics over the years, further supporting the reliability and robustness of our serial cross-sectional analysis. Sex and ethnic stratification revealed similar temporal trends with the overall population, with dyslipidemia prevalence consistently higher in males than females, and in non-minority than minority populations (Fig. 2E–H). Further comparison with other chronic diseases showed that while the prevalence of hypertension, coronary heart disease, and stroke remained stable over the 4 years, diabetes exhibited an upward trend, mirroring the pattern of dyslipidemia after 2022. Notably, the dip in dyslipidemia prevalence observed in 2022 was unique to this condition, as no such variation was seen in the other chronic diseases (Fig. 2I).

Mean lipid levels throughout the 4-year period mirrored the prevalence trends, with TG and HDL-C levels remaining relatively stable, while TC and LDL-C levels exhibited a marked decrease in 2022, in both serial cross-sectional analysis of the entire cohort and longitudinal analysis of the 8398 participants enrolled annually (Fig. 2J, K). Sex stratification demonstrated that men had higher TG and LDL-C levels but lower HDL-C levels compared to women (Fig. 2L–N). Similarly, ethnic stratification revealed higher TG and LDL-C levels and lower HDL-C levels in non-minority individuals compared to minority populations. Bootstrap resampling analysis (1000 iterations) confirmed these patterns, despite the relatively small sample size in the non-minority group (Fig. 2O–Q).

Age-stratified analysis of the 2024 data revealed distinct lipid trajectories across demographic groups. Men reached peak TG and LDL-C levels at 40–49 years, whereas women exhibited delayed peaks, with LDL-C peaking at 50–59 years and TG at 80–89 years. Interestingly, age 70 represented a potential transition point for sex differences. Before this age, women maintained more favorable lipid profiles than men, while after 70, this pattern showed a tendency to reverse, though differences were not statistically significant (Additional file 1: Fig. S3A–C). Analysis of 2021 to 2023 data showed similar patterns (Additional file 1: Fig. S3D–L).

Ethnic stratification revealed notable differences in lipid profiles across age groups. HDL-C levels were comparable between minority and non-minority populations, while significant ethnic disparities were observed in TG and LDL-C profiles. Non-minority individuals exhibited substantially higher TG levels than minority individuals until ages 60–69, after which the relationship inverted. Minority populations maintained remarkably stable lipid levels across the lifespan, while non-minority groups demonstrated age-related fluctuations, with TG levels peaking at ages 40–49 before declining rapidly, and LDL-C exhibited bimodal peaks at ages 40–49 and 70–79 (Additional file 1: Fig. S4A–C). Bootstrap resampling analysis (1000 iterations) confirmed these age-related patterns. Nevertheless, the interpretation of age-related lipid patterns in the non-minority group should be considered with caution due to the limited sample size, although this composition accurately reflects the true demographic structure of Tashkurgan County, where minorities constitute over 93.44% of the population according to official census data [40].

Global comparison of lipid profiles

To contextualize our findings within the global epidemiological landscape, we reanalyzed the data using the LDL-C threshold of 3.0 mmol/L, as recommended by the 2019 ESC/EAS guidelines [41]. The results showed consistent trends for high LDL-C across different diagnostic thresholds (Fig. 3A–C). We then compared LDL-C levels from our study with those from both plain and high-altitude regions in China and abroad (Fig. 3D). LDL-C was selected for comparison as it is identified in the ESC guidelines as the primary lipid marker contributing to CVD [41]. Despite similar sex distributions, our population appears to be younger than comparison cohorts (Additional file 1: Table S8). However, age-stratified analysis revealed consistently lower LDL-C levels across all age groups in this high-altitude population compared to those reported in both plain and other high-altitude regions [42–46] (Fig. 3D). Despite these differences, the temporal distribution of LDL-C peaks in our population aligned with international trends, with peaks occurring at 40–49 years in men and 50–59 years in women [43]. Interestingly, the age-related trajectory of LDL-C in this population diverged from patterns observed in other Chinese cohorts. While previous domestic studies have indicated that LDL-C levels tend to increase with age [44, 45], our population exhibited clear mid-life peaks followed by subsequent declines. Collectively, these distinctive lipid profiles observed in our study population suggest complex interactions between ethnic background, altitude, and potentially other environmental factors influencing lipid metabolism.

Multivariate analysis

To explore the risk factors associated with dyslipidemia, a univariate analysis was conducted, revealing a particularly interesting phenomenon: individuals residing in better environmental conditions (e.g., access to range hood, tap water, natural gas, absence of livestock barn, and availability of sanitary toilet) exhibited poorer lipid profiles, a trend that remained consistently significant throughout the 4-year study period (Table 1 and Additional file 1: Tables S4–7). In addition, individuals with dyslipidemia were more likely to be male, older, and have a higher level of education. They also exhibited a greater prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, and central obesity. Furthermore, they were more likely to be overweight or obese, and present with elevated ALT and AST levels. We extended our analysis to high TC, high TG, low HDL-C, and high LDL-C, revealing consistent findings, reinforcing the associations observed (Additional file 1: Tables S4–7). The multivariate analysis validated the findings. After adjusting for potential confounders, individuals with access to range hood (OR: 1.839, 95% CI: 1.673–2.021, P < 0.001), natural gas (OR: 1.273, 95% CI: 1.152–1.406, P < 0.001), tap water (OR: 1.315, 95% CI: 1.204–1.436, P < 0.001), no livestock barn (OR: 1.148, 95% CI: 1.063–1.239, P < 0.001), and sanitary toilet (OR: 1.182, 95% CI: 1.091–1.280, P < 0.001) (the highest OR over the 4-year period) are more likely to have dyslipidemia (Fig. 4A–D). Similar associations were observed in the multivariate analysis for high TC, high TG, low HDL-C, and high LDL-C across different diagnostic threshold (Additional file 1: Fig. S5–9A–D).

Subsequently, to explore the role of non-clinical variables in dyslipidemia, we excluded clinical variables (including disease history and family history of hypertension and diabetes, BMI, central obesity, and liver and kidney function indicators) and re-conducted the multivariate logistic regression analysis. The results indicated that, except for the relatively unstable effect of no livestock barn, environmental factors are significant and stable risk factors for high LDL-C and dyslipidemia (Fig. 5A, B). In addition, alcohol consumption, higher education level, and aging were consistently identified as risk factors, whereas females are stable protective factors (Fig. 5A, B). Similar findings were observed when the same analysis was applied to high TG and low HDL-C (Additional file 1: Fig. S10A, B).

Machine learning

To further evaluate the relative importance of variables for dyslipidemia, we employed five machine learning models to identify the key contributors, thereby supplementing the findings of the multivariate analysis. To avoid excluding potentially important clinical or biological variables, we incorporated all variables in machine learning. Among them, the RF model (AUC: 0.722) and the GBDT model (AUC: 0.711) demonstrated superior performance, significantly outperforming the other three models, including support vector machine (AUC: 0.664), Lasso regression (AUC: 0.645), and logistic regression (AUC: 0.644) (Table 2). The optimal classification thresholds for logistic regression, Lasso regression, RF, GBDT, and support vector machine were 0.339, 0.344, 0.364, 0.350, and 0.296, respectively. We conducted a comprehensive evaluation of AUC, accuracy, PPV, specificity, sensitivity, and NPV, with the GBDT model exhibiting superior performance, consistently ranking within the top two across all metrics, followed by the RF model. Similar findings were observed for high TG, low HDL-C, and high LDL-C (Additional file 1: Table S9). We therefore employed the GBDT and RF models to assess the importance rankings of variables. Environmental factors consistently ranked among the top five non-clinical predictors of dyslipidemia (Fig. 6A, B). Similar analyses for low HDL-C and high LDL-C yielded comparable results, with environmental factors ranked among the top six for high TG (Additional file 1: Fig. S11A–F). Additionally, to mitigate potential bias from the 7:3 train/test split, we performed a fivefold cross-validation. The results were consistent: RF and GBDT models showed superior performance, with environmental factors consistently ranking high among non-clinical variables (Additional file 1: Fig. S12, 13) (Additional file 1: Table S10).

Association between living environmental factors and dyslipidemia

Integrating the findings from multivariate logistic regression and machine learning analyses, which suggested an association between environmental factors and dyslipidemia, we constructed three multivariate models to further examine this relationship (Table 3). In the crude model, which included only environmental variables, improved living conditions were associated with an increased risk of dyslipidemia. Specifically, the presence of a range hood in the kitchen was linked to a higher risk (OR: 2.033, 95% CI: 1.863–2.219, P < 0.001), as was the use of tap water as a primary water source (OR: 1.457, 95% CI: 1.347–1.575, P < 0.001) and natural gas (OR: 1.238, 95% CI: 1.143–1.342, P < 0.001) (the highest OR over the 4-year period). The associations of livestock barn absence and sanitary toilet availability with dyslipidemia risk were inconsistent. In both the partially adjusted model (adjusting for sex and ethnicity) and the fully adjusted model (adjusting for all significant variables identified in the univariate analysis), the associations between environmental factors and dyslipidemia remained largely unchanged. Although a slight attenuation was observed in the fully adjusted model, the associations remained statistically significant. We conducted a similar analysis for high TG, low HDL-C, and high LDL-C. While the effects of individual environmental factors exhibited some variability, the overall association pattern remained consistent with the primary findings (Additional file 1: Tables S11–13).

Discussion

Over four consecutive years of cross-sectional analyses, our study provides the first epidemiological insights into disease patterns in an understudied population residing on “the roof of the world”—the Pamirs Plateau (with an average elevation exceeding 4000 m). Our findings reveal that dyslipidemia is the most prevalent condition in this high-altitude region, characterized by distinct lipid profiles. Our cohort showed lower LDL-C levels than those reported in both plain regions and other high-altitude areas, with significant ethnic disparities, unique age-sex patterns, and a striking paradoxical association between improved household environmental conditions and increased dyslipidemia risk—a phenomenon we term the “modernization paradox” in this context. Furthermore, we observed distinct temporal fluctuations, particularly a sharp decline and subsequent rebound in LDL-C levels coinciding with the period surrounding the major COVID-19 lockdown.

Consistent with global patterns [47], dyslipidemia emerged as the most prevalent condition in our Pamirs Plateau population. However, this region’s lipid profile demonstrates distinctive characteristics, with LDL-C levels relatively lower than those of other populations [43–45]. This phenomenon likely reflects long-term physiological adaptations to chronic hypobaric hypoxia in high-altitude residents [48], as well as unique gut microbiome compositions [49], that influence lipid metabolism. Importantly, LDL-C levels in our cohort were lower than those reported in other high-altitude regions [42, 46]. This suggests factors beyond altitude alone are at play. Our ethnic stratification provides compelling evidence: The minority group, primarily Tajik, consistently demonstrated a lower prevalence of dyslipidemia and more favorable lipid profiles than their non-minority population (Han Chinese) neighbors. This disparity may stem from genetic variants affecting lipid metabolism that may differ across ethnic groups [50]. The Tajik population, having resided in high-altitude environments for generations, may have undergone selection for genetic variants that confer more favorable lipid profiles under hypoxic conditions. Dietary patterns may also play a role. The minority population traditionally consumes more meat, milk, and dairy products (associated with reduced dyslipidemia risk [51]), whereas Han individuals who have migrated to the region may retain dietary habits higher in processed foods and refined carbohydrates [52]. Particularly noteworthy was our finding that the minority population exhibited relatively stable lipid levels with age, whereas the non-minority population showed pronounced fluctuations. Bootstrap resampling analysis confirmed the robustness of these patterns despite the smaller sample size in the non-minority group. These patterns might reflect different metabolic responses to aging between ethnic groups [53].

Sex differences in our cohort partially align with established patterns [43], with males showing higher dyslipidemia prevalence than females. The age-related peaks for TG and LDL-C (men: 40–49 years; women: LDL-C 50–59 years, TG later) mirror international findings [43] likely reflecting behavioral differences including alcohol consumption and dietary patterns [54]. Our identification of age 70 is a potential transition point. Before this age, women maintained more favorable lipid profiles than men, consistent with the cardioprotective effects of estrogen during reproductive years [55]. However, this advantage showed a tendency to reverse after age 70, though without statistical significance. This contrasts with studies from low-altitude regions reporting transitions at ages 50–60 [56–58]. The delayed pattern may be related to the extreme altitude exposure (> 4000 m), which has been reported to induce metabolic adaptations that affect lipid metabolism [59]. Additionally, our predominantly Tajik population may harbor genetic variants modifying hormonal responses to aging. Whether this represents genuine altitude-related modification of post-menopausal metabolic changes [60] or ethnic-specific aging patterns requires validation in other extreme-altitude populations with diverse ethnic compositions.

Our most unexpected finding was the robust association between improved environmental conditions and a higher risk of dyslipidemia. This association persisted after multivariate adjustment for demographic and clinical confounders. These “modernization paradox” observations align with the “nutrition transition” model proposed by Popkin, where populations undergoing socioeconomic development often experience temporary increases in cardiometabolic disorders due to shifts in dietary and activity patterns, followed by health improvements as health systems and literacy evolve [61]. Our findings suggest that Tashkurgan County in the Pamirs Plateau may currently be in such a transitional phase, where living conditions have improved ahead of health behaviors and knowledge [62]. Nonetheless, with only initial data on behavior available, the underlying mechanisms remain speculative. Evidence from other high-altitude populations suggests plausible pathways. In transitioning Tibetan communities, dietary patterns are shifting from a “traditional” diet—rich in whole grains, dairy, and vegetables—to a “modern” diet high in sugar, sodium, and unsaturated fats [63]. Additionally, economic development-driven mechanization may contribute to reduced physical activity [64, 65]. Similarly, mechanization and urbanization in other high-altitude regions have been linked to reduced physical activity and increased processed food access [66, 67]. We hypothesize that similar lifestyle shifts may explain the observed associations in our population. These findings underscore the importance of the integration of health literacy programs to enable the high-altitude, multi-ethnic population to effectively adapt to the health challenges associated with urbanization.

The temporal trend in dyslipidemia prevalence from 2021 to 2024—marked by a notable decline in 2022 followed by a sharp rebound—coincided with the implementation and subsequent relaxation of COVID-19 restrictions, raising the possibility of pandemic-related influences on lipid metabolism. However, without direct and detailed behavioral data from our study population, the mechanisms underlying this pattern remain speculative. We hypothesize that during lockdown, reduced dining out and increased consumption of home-cooked meals might have contributed to lower LDL-C levels [68]. Similarly, potential alterations in physical activity patterns, known to influence lipid metabolism [69], could have played a role. The subsequent sharp rebound in 2023 could potentially represent a compensatory phenomenon following restriction lifting, as has been observed in other contexts following periods of deprivation or restriction [70, 71]. Individuals in post-lockdown reverted to pre-pandemic routines, increased consumption of processed foods, and reduced physical activity. Similar post-COVID-19 pandemic rebounds in cardiometabolic risk factors have been documented in other populations [72, 73].

Beyond environmental, ethnic, sex, and age-related factors, our multivariate models consistently identified several additional risk factors for dyslipidemia, including diabetes, overweight or obese, excessive alcohol consumption, and higher educational attainment. These findings are consistent with existing evidence and further reinforce the robustness of our results [74–77]. Moreover, in the machine learning analysis, continuous variables ranked highly in importance, likely due to their richer information content compared to categorical variables [78].

This study has several strengths. First, the use of a large and representative cohort across 4 years provides robust data on a geographically and culturally unique population. With coverage of 58.25% of the long-term residents of Tashkurgan County and 24.37% of the long-term residents of the Pamirs Plateau [19, 39], our findings reflect stable rather than temporary migration effects. Second, its focus on a unique, understudied high-altitude population from “the roof of the world” in the Pamirs Plateau fills an important knowledge gap. Third, our comprehensive assessment of environmental factors revealed novel associations that might have been overlooked in most conventional analyses focused solely on clinical risk factors. These findings contribute to our understanding of how socio-environmental transitions influence cardiometabolic health. Fourth, the combination of conventional statistical analyses with machine learning approaches strengthens the reliability of our findings.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences, especially regarding the environment–lifestyle–dyslipidemia relationship, despite the longitudinal analysis of 8398 participants who participated annually. Second, although we assessed participants’ diet, physical activity, and other behavioral factors and adjusted for potential confounders, the data on behavior were not sufficiently detailed, so residual confounding from unmeasured factors cannot be excluded. Consequently, the findings on the “modernization paradox” and the observed 2022 temporal patterns should be interpreted with caution, and the underlying mechanisms warrant further investigation. Future studies incorporating more detailed lifestyle assessments and pandemic-specific behavioral data are needed. Third, reliance on self-reported data introduces potential recall and social desirability bias. Fourth, the interpretation of detailed age-related lipid patterns in the non-minority group should be considered with caution due to the limited sample size, although bootstrap resampling analysis confirmed these age-related patterns. Fifth, considering the differences in age composition between studies, the lower LDL-C levels observed in this study compared to those in plain and other high-altitude regions warrant further investigation. Finally, the machine learning models demonstrated moderate predictive performance, which may limit their clinical utility. However, their primary role in this study was to provide variable importance rankings, offering complementary insights to the multivariate regression analyses. Future research could focus on improving model performance and validating its applicability in larger, multi-center cohorts.

Overall, our findings carry significant public health implications for the Pamirs Plateau region and potentially other high-altitude communities, particularly those undergoing socioeconomic transition. The distinctive lipid profiles and ethnic disparities observed suggest that it is crucial to develop culturally appropriate dyslipidemia screening and health promotion strategies in this multi-ethnic region [79]. Increased awareness and screening efforts should focus on non-minority individuals and males, considering both traditional and environment-related lifestyle risk factors. Future research should also investigate the genetic backgrounds of Tajik and other local ethnic groups to better understand ethnicity-related differences in lipid metabolism. Furthermore, the “modernization paradox” highlights the need for public health education on diet, physical activity, and other lifestyle factors that address potential adverse health consequences of improved living conditions.

Conclusions

In this study, we provided epidemiological data of Tashkurgan County, situated on the Pamirs Plateau, "the roof of the world", and revealed that dyslipidemia is one of the most prevalent health conditions in this unique high-altitude, multi-ethnic setting. This population exhibits distinctive lipid profiles characterized by notably lower LDL-C levels compared to those in both plain regions and other high-altitude populations, with significant variations across ethnicity, age, and sex. Most strikingly, we identified a paradoxical association between improved living conditions and increased dyslipidemia risk. Collectively, our findings contribute valuable population-based data on an understudied population and illuminate the complex relationships between altitude, ethnicity, modernization, and dyslipidemia. These insights should inform context-specific screening, prevention, and intervention strategies that address the unique challenges and opportunities for cardiometabolic health promotion in this high-altitude region of the Pamirs Plateau. As modernization continues, understanding and mitigating its potential adverse health consequences becomes increasingly important for safeguarding population health.
