Surface sums for lattice Yang–Mills in the large- N N limit
Jacopo Borga, Sky Cao, Jasper Shogren-Knaak

Abstract
We give a sum over weighted planar surfaces formula for Wilson loop expectations in the large- N N limit of strongly coupled lattice Yang–Mills theory, in any dimension. The weights of each surface are simple and expressed in terms of products of signed Catalan numbers. In establishing our results, the main novelty is to convert a recursive relation for Wilson loop expectations, known as the master loop equation , into a new peeling exploration of the planar surfaces. This exploration reveals hidden cancellations within the sums, enabling a deeper understanding of the structure of the planar surfaces. We view our results as a continuation of the program initiated in [ CPS23 ] to understand Yang–Mills theories via surfaces and as a refinement of the string trajectories point-of-view developed in [ Cha19a ] .

2.1.3
The orientation of embedded maps

Recall from Definition
2.1
that lattice embeddings send blue and yellow faces to
unoriented
edges and plaquettes respectively. Further, recall that lattice Yang–Mills theory is defined on a lattice
\Lambda
where all the plaquettes and the edges are oriented. Thus, to have these maps be sensible objects in lattice Yang–Mills they must “inherit” the orientation of the lattice. Indeed, the maps considered in
[

(Theorem

Before doing this, we highlight a trivial but subtle aspect: to determine if a plaquette on the lattice is positively or negatively oriented, we considered a prior orientation of each plaquette (for instance, in two dimensions, this prior orientation is the natural clockwise orientation of each plaquette). We must also fix such a prior orientation on the embedded maps (recall their definition from Definition
2.1
).

Prior orientation of an embedded map
. The prior orientation of the edges on the boundary of each internal yellow face is defined to be their clockwise orientation.
12
12

12

Note that this is a natural definition since each internal yellow face is isomorphically mapped to a plaquette, which has a prior clockwise orientation (at least in dimension two).
Note that since the maps we are considering have bipartite faces, this imposes that all the edges on the boundary of the blue faces are counter-clockwise oriented; and this further imposes that the edges on the boundary of each external yellow face are also clockwise oriented. We stress that our maps or maps with boundary are always embedded in an orientable surface (recall Section
2.1.1
) and so the clockwise/counter-clockwise orientation of the boundary of a face is well-defined.

as the operations are defined for a specific location instead of all locations of an edge. This leads to a more general master loop equation.

Now we can state our surface sum formula for the large-

Theorem 3.4

(
\mathrm{U}(\infty)

There exists a number

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\phi_{\Lambda_{N},N,\upbeta}(s)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\phi(\ell_{i}),

where

\phi(\ell)=\sum_{K:\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\to\mathbb{N}}\phi^{K}(\ell),\quad\text{with}\quad\phi^{K}(\ell)=\sum_{M\in\mathcal{N}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{M}(\ell,K)}\upbeta^{\area(M)}w_{\infty}(M),

and the infinite sum

is absolutely convergent.

Remark 3.5
.

One might naturally wonder whether the non-separability condition in the definition of

is essential. We will show in Appendix

that in dimension two, even for a plaquette

We need to clarify how we define

Here

is such that there exists

, and

While this definition may seem somewhat ad-hoc, as there are many ways to define

3.2
The master loop equation in the large-
N
limit

We can now state the master loop equation for the large-
N
limit of
\mathrm{U}(N)
Wilson loop expectations.

Theorem 3.6

(
\mathrm{U}(\infty)
master loop equation for fixed plaquette assignment and location
)
.

Fix a non-null loop
\ell
and a plaquette assignment
K:\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\to\mathbb{N}
. Let
\mathbf{e}
be a specific edge of the loop
\ell
. Suppose that the edge
\mathbf{e}
is a copy of the lattice edge
e\in E_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
. Then

\displaystyle\phi^{K}(\ell)=

\displaystyle\sum_{\{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\}\in\mathbb{S}_{-}(\mathbf{e},\ell)}\sum_{K_{1}+K_{2}=K}\phi^{K_{1}}(\ell_{1})\phi^{K_{2}}(\ell_{2})-\sum_{\{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\}\in\mathbb{S}_{+}(\mathbf{e},\ell)}\sum_{K_{1}+K_{2}=K}\phi^{K_{1}}(\ell_{1})\phi^{K_{2}}(\ell_{2})

\displaystyle+\upbeta\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}(e^{-1},K)}\phi^{K\setminus p}(\ell\ominus_{\mathbf{e}}p)-\upbeta\sum_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}(e,K)}\phi^{K\setminus q}(\ell\oplus_{\mathbf{e}}q),

where
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}(e,K)
denotes the collection of plaquettes in
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
containing
e
as one of the four boundary edges (with the correct orientation) and such that
K(p)\geq 1
.

We also used the (more compact) notation
\sum_{K_{1}+K_{2}=K}
to indicate the sum
\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}K_{1},K_{2}:\\
K_{1}+K_{2}=K\end{subarray}}
, where
K_{1}+K_{2}=K
means that
K_{1}(p)+K_{2}(p)=K(p)
for all
p\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
.

Remark 3.7
.

Note as
\phi^{K}(\ell)=0
if
(\ell,K)
is not balanced the sum
\sum_{K_{1}+K_{2}=K}\phi^{K_{1}}(\ell_{1})\phi^{K_{2}}(\ell_{2})
only considers decompositions of
K
such that
(\ell_{1},K_{1})
and
(\ell_{2},K_{2})
are both balanced.

The above master loop equation will be our fundamental tool to prove Theorem
3.4
and explicitly compute Wilson loop expectations in dimension two in
[
BCSK24
]
.

We point out that the master loop equations introduced in Theorem
3.6
is more general than the ones previously presented in the literature as it is both for a fixed location in the loop and for a fixed plaquette assignment

. This seemingly subtle difference is crucial for the proofs of the results in
[

]
. Moreover, one can easily recover the more classical (but weaker) form of the master loop equation presented in the literature; as stated in the next corollary.

Corollary 3.8

(
\mathrm{U}(\infty)
master loop equation for fixed location
)
.

Fix a non-null loop
\ell
and a specific edge
\mathbf{e}
of
\ell
. Let
\upbeta_{0}(d)
be as in the statement of Theorem
3.4
. Then for all
|\upbeta|\leq\upbeta_{0}(d)
,

\displaystyle\phi(\ell)=

\displaystyle\sum_{\{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\}\in\mathbb{S}_{+}(\mathbf{e},\ell)}\phi(\ell_{1})\phi(\ell_{2})-\sum_{\{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\}\in\mathbb{S}_{-}(\mathbf{e},\ell)}\phi(\ell_{1})\phi(\ell_{2})

\displaystyle+\upbeta\sum_{\ell^{\prime}\in\mathbb{D}_{-}(\mathbf{e},\ell)}\phi(\ell^{\prime})-\upbeta\sum_{\ell^{\prime}\in\mathbb{D}_{+}(\mathbf{e},\ell)}\phi(\ell^{\prime}).

Remark 3.9
.

We stress that the master loop equation in Corollary

requires the additional assumption that

, while the master loop equation in Theorem

holds for all

3.3
Open problems

We give here a list of open problems that we think might be interesting to explore:

1.

Theorem
3.4
is shown for
\upbeta
small. In our forthcoming companion paper
[
BCSK24
]
, we will prove that in 2D, the Wilson loop expectation
\phi(p)
of a plaquette

is exactly
\upbeta
(the analogous result has been proved for
\mathrm{S}\mathrm{O}(N)
lattice Yang–Mills in
[
BG18

). Since we have that

for all

and

, Theorem

can hold only if

. Thus, it would be interesting to understand the value
\upbeta^{*}
at which the conclusion of Theorem

first fails to hold. Related results from the paper
[
HP00
]
would seem to imply that, at least in 2D,

, and it would be nice to confirm whether this is true. Moreover, it would be interesting to discover the right limiting expression for
\phi_{\Lambda_{N},N,\upbeta}(s)
when

.

2.

Theorem
3.4
determines the large-
N
behavior of the Wilson loop expectations
\phi_{\Lambda_{N},N,\upbeta}(s)
. It would be interesting to establish a
1/N
-expansion of these quantities, that is, to show that (at least for
\upbeta
small enough) for any string

\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{2k}\left(\phi_{\Lambda_{N},N,\upbeta}(s)-\phi_{0}(s)-\frac{1}{N^{2}}\phi_{2}(s)-\dots-\frac{1}{N^{2k}}\phi_{2k}(s)\right)=0,

where for all
h\in\mathbb{N}
,

\phi_{2h}(s):=\sum_{K:\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\to\mathbb{N}}\phi^{K}_{2h}(s),\quad\text{with}\quad\phi^{K}_{2h}(s)=\sum_{M\in\mathcal{M}_{2h}(s,K)}\upbeta^{\area(M)}w_{\infty}(M).

As for the proof of Theorem
3.4
, the first step would be to guess the set
\mathcal{M}_{2h}(s,K)
of maps and the type of weight
w_{\infty}(\cdot)
one should consider in the sums

\phi^{K}_{2h}(s)=\sum_{M\in\mathcal{M}_{2h}(s,K)}\upbeta^{\area(M)}w_{\infty}(M).

One natural guess (see also Appendix
A
for more explanations) would be to consider (non-separable) maps with generalized Euler characteristic equal to
2h
and the same signed Catalan weights as in the planar case. Unfortunately, this guess seems to be incorrect and various similar adaptations all turn out to be incorrect. We plan to revisit this problem in the future.

3.

Theorem
3.4
give us a simple sum over weighted planar surfaces. This can be naturally interpreted as a finite (recall that the sum is absolutely convergent) signed measure on the set of non-separable planar embedded maps. Is it possible to define a notion of scaling limit for this maps? Whould the limiting measure be a signed measure on the space of Liouville quantum gravity surfaces? The latter are the scaling limit of many natural models or random planar maps
[
She22
]
.

4.

Our “peeling exploration” from Section
5.2
can be naturally interpreted as a signed-Markov chain on the space of non-separable planar embedded maps. Would this Markov chain help in answering the question in the previous item? Can one compute the transition signed measures of this chain?

5.

In this paper we considered lattice Yang–Mills with the
Wilson
action, but it seems very natural to also consider the large-
N
limit of lattice Yang–Mills with the
Villain/heat-kernel
action, in the same spirit as
[
PPSY23
]
. This should yield a different surface sum formula that might work in a larger regime of the

parameter. We plan to explore this question in future work.

Two fundamental tools: pinchings and backtrack cancellations

In this section we introduce two preliminary tools that will be used later to establish Theorems

and

4.1
Pinchings and surface cancellations

In this short section we introduce an important operation on the blue faces of embedded maps, called the pinching operation. Throughout the paper, it will be repeatedly used to get certain surface cancellations (recall Definition
1.2
).

We start with a few definitions. We say that a face of an embedded map has
disjoint vertices
if none of its vertices are identified; see the left-hand side of Figure
8
for an example.

For a balanced pair
(s,K)
, consider an embedded map
M\in\mathcal{M}(s,K)
, as introduced in Definition
2.3
. Fix a blue face
B
of
M
with disjoint vertices and let
v
be a vertex of
B
.
Recall from Observation
2.2
that the vertices of
M
are bipartite. Given any vertex
u\neq v
on
B
in the same partite class as
v
, let
\pp_{u,v}
denote the
pinching operation
that pinches the vertices
u
and
v
together inside
B
. More precisely, one draws a line from
u
to
v
through the interior of
B
and contracts this line to a point splitting
B
into two blue faces
\pp_{u,v}(B)
that share a vertex (the vertex corresponding to
u
and
v
). See the right-hand side of Figure
8
for an example. Note we define pinchings for vertices in the same partite class because this ensures that the resulting two new blue faces have both even degree.

We denote by
\AF(B,v)
the set of all pairs of blue faces obtained from pinching the vertex
v
with another vertex of the blue face
B
in the same partite class. Similarly, we let
\AM(M,B,v)
denote the set of all maps that can be obtained from
M
by the same type of pinchings.

Figure 8
:

Left:
An example of a blue face with disjoint vertices and of another blue face with non disjoint vertices.

Right:
An example of the pinching operation
\pp_{u,v}(B)
for a blue face
B
having 8 vertices.

We have the following surface cancellation result.

Lemma 4.1

(
single vertex pinching cancellations
)
.

Fix a blue face
B
with disjoint vertices and a vertex
v
of
B
. Then

w_{\infty}(B)+\sum_{B^{\prime}\in\AF(B,v)}w_{\infty}(B^{\prime})=0,

(15)

where if
B^{\prime}
is the pair of faces
\{B^{\prime}_{1},B^{\prime}_{2}\}
, then
w_{\infty}(B^{\prime}):=w_{\infty}(B^{\prime}_{1})\cdot w_{\infty}(B^{\prime}_{2})
.

As a consequence, for an embedded map
M\in\mathcal{M}(s,K)
and a vertex
v
on a blue face
B
of
M
with disjoint vertices, we have that

\displaystyle w_{\infty}(M)+\sum_{M^{\prime}\in\AM(M,B,v)}w_{\infty}(M^{\prime})=0.

(16)

Proof.

Let
C=\prod_{f\in\BF(M)\setminus B}w_{\deg(f)/2}
where
\BF(M)
denotes the set of blue faces of
M
. Note that since the embedded maps in
\AM(M,B,v)
are obtained from
M
by only modifying
B
, we have from (
13
) that

w_{\infty}(M)=C\cdot w_{\infty}(B)

and

\sum_{M^{\prime}\in\AM(M,B,v)}w_{\infty}(M^{\prime})=C\cdot\sum_{B^{\prime}\in\AF(B,v)}w_{\infty}(B^{\prime}).

Hence we only need to prove (
15
) to complete the proof of the lemma.
Assume that
B
has degree
2k
. Then we can write

\displaystyle w_{\infty}(B)=w_{\deg(B)/2}=(-1)^{k-1}\cat(k-1).

Now recall the following recursive property of Catalan numbers

\cat(k)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\cat(i-1)\cat(k-i).

(17)

This lets us write

\displaystyle\ w_{\infty}(B)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k-1}\cat(k-1)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k-1}\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\cat(i-1)\cat(k-1-i)

\displaystyle=-(-1)^{k-2}\sum_{j=0}^{k-2}\cat(j)\cat(k-j)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{h=1}^{k-1}(-1)^{h-1}\cat(h-1)(-1)^{k-h-1}\cat(k-h-1)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{B^{\prime}\in\AF(B,v)}w_{\infty}(B^{\prime}_{1})w_{\infty}(B^{\prime}_{2}).

This is enough to complete the proof.
∎

4.2
Backtrack cancellations

We turn to our second tool. One fundamental property in lattice Yang–Mills theory is the following one: if a matrix configuration assigns to an oriented edge the matrix
Q
, then it assigns the matrix
Q^{-1}
to the opposite orientation of the same edge. This has the immediate consequence that if
\ell
has a backtrack, i.e.

\ell=\pi_{1}\,\mathbf{e}\,\mathbf{e}^{-1}\,\pi_{2},

where
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
are two paths of edges,
\mathbf{e}
corresponds to the oriented edge
e\in E_{\Lambda}
, and
\mathbf{e}^{-1}
corresponds to
e^{-1}
,
then

\phi_{\Lambda,N,\upbeta}(\pi_{1}\,\mathbf{e}\,\mathbf{e}^{-1}\,\pi_{2})=\phi_{\Lambda,N,\upbeta}(\pi_{1}\,\pi_{2}).

(18)

Obviously, the same property must be satisfied by the limit
\phi(\ell)=\sum_{K:\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\to\mathbb{N}}\phi^{K}(\ell)
in Theorem
3.4
.

In order to prove that
\phi_{\Lambda,N,\upbeta}
converges to
\phi
in the large-
N
limit, we will need to show that
\phi
has the property in (
18
) before establishing Theorem
3.4
; see the proof of Lemma
5.6
in Section
5.1.2
for further details. Hence we establish this result here. In fact, we will actually show a stronger result, that this property holds for
\phi^{K}
.

Lemma 4.2

(
backtrack cancellations
)
.

Suppose that
\ell=\pi_{1}\,\mathbf{e}\,\mathbf{e}^{-1}\,\pi_{2}
is a loop with backtrack
\mathbf{e}\,\mathbf{e}^{-1}
and
(\ell,K)
is a balanced pair. Then

\phi^{K}(\pi_{1}\,\mathbf{e}\,\mathbf{e}^{-1}\,\pi_{2})=\phi^{K}(\pi_{1}\,\pi_{2}),

where
\phi^{K}
has been introduced in the statement of Theorem
3.4
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{e}\,\mathbf{e}^{-1}}
denote the set of embedded maps in
\mathcal{N}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{M}(\ell,K)
such that
\mathbf{e}
and
\mathbf{e}^{-1}
are together in a blue 2-gon and
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathbf{e}\,\mathbf{e}^{-1}}
denote the complement of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{e}\,\mathbf{e}^{-1}}
in
\mathcal{N}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{M}(\ell,K)
. Further, let
\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{\text{same}}_{\mathbf{e}\,\mathbf{e}^{-1}}
denote the maps in
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathbf{e}\,\mathbf{e}^{-1}}
where
\mathbf{e}
and
\mathbf{e}^{-1}
are in the same blue face and
\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{\text{split}}_{\mathbf{e}\,\mathbf{e}^{-1}}
denote the maps where
\mathbf{e}
and
\mathbf{e}^{-1}
are in two separate blue faces. With this decomposition of
\mathcal{N}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{M}(\ell,K)
we have that

\displaystyle\phi^{K}(\ell)

\displaystyle=\upbeta^{\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}}K(p)}\left(\sum_{M\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{e}\,\mathbf{e}^{-1}}}w_{\infty}(M)+\sum_{M\in\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{\text{same}}_{\mathbf{e}\,\mathbf{e}^{-1}}}w_{\infty}(M)+\sum_{M\in\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{\text{split}}_{\mathbf{e}\,\mathbf{e}^{-1}}}w_{\infty}(M)\right)

\displaystyle=\phi^{K}(\pi_{1}\,\pi_{2})+\upbeta^{\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}}K(p)}\left(\sum_{M\in\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{\text{same}}_{\mathbf{e}\,\mathbf{e}^{-1}}}w_{\infty}(M)+\sum_{M\in\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{\text{split}}_{\mathbf{e}\,\mathbf{e}^{-1}}}w_{\infty}(M)\right),

where to get the last equality we used that each map
M\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{e}\,\mathbf{e}^{-1}}
corresponds bijectively to the map in
\mathcal{N}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{M}(\pi_{1}\,\pi_{2},K)
obtained by removing the blue 2-gon containing
\mathbf{e}
and
\mathbf{e}^{-1}

\mathbb{R}^{I_{K_{*},M_{*}}}
. We remark that we will only every apply the norm to elements of
\Theta_{K_{*},M_{*}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{I_{K_{*},M_{*}}}
, however our terminology of “norm” is only accurate when viewed as a function defined on
\mathbb{R}^{I_{K_{*},M_{*}}}
, since this is a vector space while
\Theta_{K_{*},M_{*}}
is only an affine space.

For
\gamma\in(0,\infty)
, let

(D_{\gamma}f)(\delta)\penalty\ :=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\in\mathcal{S}_{o}\\
\delta(s)\leq\delta\end{subarray}}\sum_{K:(s,K)\in I_{K_{*},M_{*}}}\gamma^{\area(K)}|f(s,K)|,\qquad\forall f\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{K_{*},M_{*}}},\delta\in\Delta_{+}.

(31)

For
\lambda\in(0,1/2)
,
\gamma\in(0,\infty)
, define the norm

\|f\|_{\lambda,\gamma}\penalty\ :=\sum_{\delta\in\Delta_{+}}\lambda^{\iota(\delta)}(D_{\gamma}f)(\delta),\qquad\forall f\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{K_{*},M_{*}}}.

(32)

Here, even if
\|\cdot\|_{\lambda,\gamma}
does not satisfy all the mathematical properties of a norm (it may be infinite), we will still refer to it as one, because this is really how we think about it.

Note that the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\lambda,\gamma}
also depends on
K_{*},M_{*}
, but we keep this dependence implicit. In the end, the motivation for this norm is because it satisfies the estimate in Lemma
5.11
, which we will get to later.

Remark 5.9

(
comments on the norm
)
.

One can think of the norm (
32
) as a mixed
\ell^{1}
and
\ell^{\infty}
norm. Intuitively, one should think of
\lambda
as a small parameter and
\gamma
as a large parameter greater than 1 (the latter will only be true when
\upbeta
is small enough – see the proof of Proposition
5.14
), so that

\|f\|_{\lambda,\gamma}\leq C\quad\text{ implies that}\quad|f(s,K)|\leq C\lambda^{-\iota(s)}\gamma^{-\area(K)},

i.e.
f
decays exponentially in
\area(K)
and grows at most exponentially in
\iota(s)
(a quantity which should be thought of as a proxy for the length of
s
).

Perhaps a more natural definition of the norm would have been to use
\lambda^{|\delta|}
instead of
\lambda^{\iota(\delta)}
in (
32
), i.e. to just weight by the total length. However, this norm turns out to be too weak to close the ensuing contraction mapping argument. The problem is that if
s^{\prime}
is a positive splitting of

, then it could be the case that
|s^{\prime}|=|s|
, while it is always the case that
\iota(\delta(s^{\prime}))\leq\iota(\delta(s))-1
as we will show in Lemma
5.13
. The latter estimate allows us to gain a crucial factor of
\lambda
(which recall we are thinking of as small).

The reason we take finite parameters
K_{*},M_{*}
is so that we have the following soft estimate.

Lemma 5.10
.

Let
K_{*}:\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}

\phi^{K}(s)

and

, we have that

where we recall that the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\lambda,\gamma}
is defined using the implicit parameters
K_{*}
and
M_{*}
, which we fixed.

We claim that there are only finitely many pairs

such that

To see this, first we observe that if

for all

. This follows because in this case,

is nonzero, then one of its edges must be contained in a plaquette

for which

for which this holds and such that

This concludes the proof of our claim.

Thus, it follows that there is some constant

such that

where we used that
\lambda<1/2
and (

∎

Remark 5.12
.

Consider

and

and

First notice

and

A.3
Backtrack erasure

While the above discussion shows that we need to enforce more conditions then the ones found in Section
A.1
, it does not illuminate why we want to only sum over non-separable embedded maps (recall their definition from Definition
3.2
). It turns out that the non-separable condition emerges if one looks for conditions to ensure that the result in Lemma
4.2
holds, i.e. the ability to erase backtracks.

More precisely, if
\phi_{\text{ans}}(\ell)
is the Wilson loop expectation in the large-

and

, and

To understand what is needed for such an invariance, let’s first parse through what the condition in (

and

are in a blue 2-gon and the rest of the map is precisely
