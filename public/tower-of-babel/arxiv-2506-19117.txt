PrITTI: Primitive-based Generation of Controllable and Editable 3D Semantic Scenes
Christina Ourania Tze, Daniel Dauner, Yiyi Liao, Dzmitry Tsishkou, Andreas Geiger

Abstract
Large-scale 3D semantic scene generation has predominantly relied on voxel-based representations, which are memory-intensive, bound by fixed resolutions, and challenging to edit. In contrast, primitives represent semantic entities using compact, coarse 3D structures that are easy to manipulate and compose, making them an ideal representation for this task. In this paper, we introduce PrITTI, a latent diffusion-based framework that leverages primitives as the main foundational elements for generating compositional, controllable, and editable 3D semantic scene layouts. Our method adopts a hybrid representation, modeling ground surfaces in a rasterized format while encoding objects as vectorized 3D primitives. This decomposition is also reflected in a structured latent representation that enables flexible scene manipulation of ground and object components. To overcome the orientation ambiguities in conventional encoding methods, we introduce a stable Cholesky-based parameterization that jointly encodes object size and orientation. Experiments on the KITTI-360 dataset show that PrITTI outperforms a voxel-based baseline in generation quality, while reducing memory requirements by up to 3 × 3\times . In addition, PrITTI enables direct instance-level manipulation of objects in the scene and supports a range of downstream applications, including scene inpainting, outpainting, and photo-realistic street-view synthesis.

Introduction

Primitives have long served in the computer vision community as a parsimonious and compositional representation, capable of capturing complex 3D geometry using only a small number of low-dimensional parameters
[
55
,
4
,
6
,
3
,
49
]
. In the deep learning era, they have been successfully applied to tasks such as shape abstraction
[
62
,
81
,
30
,
42
]
and scene parsing
[
18
]
, where the goal is to decompose input observations into semantically meaningful object instances. Yet, despite their representational strengths, primitives remain largely underexplored in generative settings. In particular, no prior work has leveraged them as the fundamental units for synthesizing large-scale, 3D urban scenes with abstract, semantic structure. Instead, most existing approaches for this task rely on voxel grids
[
29
,
79
]
or hierarchical voxel structures
[
53
,
35
]
to represent 3D environments.

Crucially, the extent to which large-scale scenes can be generated, controlled, and edited heavily depends on the choice of the underlying representation. An ideal representation must be memory-efficient, compositional, and allow for localized, intuitive edits. Primitives naturally satisfy these requirements, offering a compelling alternative for scalable 3D semantic scene synthesis. In contrast, voxel grids exhibit several limitations that make them ill-suited for this task.
First
, due to cubic scaling with resolution, they are
memory-inefficient and prohibitively expensive for large-scale environments.
Second
, they impose a fixed spatial resolution, limiting flexibility in scene detail. For instance, tall elements of a scene, like buildings or trees, may not be fully represented within this restricted spatial domain, leading to truncated or distorted visual appearances.
Third
, voxel grids are difficult to manipulate. Even a simple operation, such as translating a vehicle, requires identifying and updating all voxels associated with that object and then filling in the vacated region. This process is complicated by ambiguous boundaries between objects and background (e.g., car and road), as individual voxels may span both and hinder clear semantic distinctions.

In this work, we are the first to adopt coarse 3D bounding primitives for generating controllable and editable 3D semantic scenes. Although many recent works
[
75
,
36
,
2
,
51
,
73
,
74
,
8
]
rely on abstract semantic layouts, these are typically hand-crafted or pre-defined inputs, rather than being generated. Our method, named PrITTI, leverages the primitive annotations from the KITTI-360 dataset
[
32
]
, which include 3D ellipsoids (e.g., trees), cuboids (e.g., buildings, vehicles), and extruded 2D polygons of varying shapes (e.g., road, sidewalk). While cuboids and ellipsoids can be consistently parameterized, the arbitrary geometry of polygons makes them difficult to represent uniformly. To address this, we use a hybrid representation: ground elements are rasterized, while objects are modeled as parameterized primitives (cuboids and ellipsoids). Training proceeds in two stages: a variational autoencoder (VAE)
[
26
]
first compresses input 3D layouts into a compact 2D latent space, followed by a latent diffusion model (LDM)
[
56
]
trained on these representations to enable controllable generation of novel scenes. Despite using only discrete primitive density labels for supervision, our LDM supports downstream tasks such as scene inpainting and outpainting without fine-tuning (see Fig.
1
). This is achieved via a latent manipulation mechanism
[
38
]
that allows localized edits while preserving global structure. A key component of our approach is its disentangled latent structure, where ground and object elements are encoded in two separate channel groups. This design further facilitates applications like ground-conditioned object generation with no extra supervision required. Additionally, our instance-level primitive representation supports intuitive editing operations including translation, scaling, and rotation. Finally, we introduce a Cholesky-based
[
10
]
parameterization of 3D object orientation and size that offers greater numerical stability than standard quaternion-based encodings, as evidenced by our experiments.

To summarize, our method introduces: (1) the first primitive-based formulation for large-scale 3D urban scene generation at an abstract, semantic level, (2) a disentangled latent representation that enables controllable manipulation, and (3) a stable encoding of 3D object orientation and size based on Cholesky decomposition. Experiments demonstrate that, even after voxelizing our primitive scenes for evaluation, PrITTI achieves competitive reconstruction performance compared to a voxel-based baseline despite requiring significantly less memory. Moreover, it delivers superior generation quality and diversity while offering greater controllability and editability.

3
Method

We introduce PrITTI, a latent diffusion-based framework for controllable and editable generation of 3D primitive-based semantic scene layouts. An overview of our approach is illustrated in Fig.
2
.

3.1
3D Semantic Scene Layout Representation

An input 3D semantic scene layout
\mathcal{S}
consists of 3D cuboids and ellipsoids depicting common urban objects, along with upwards extruded polygons denoting ground surfaces. Given the distinct nature of object and ground annotations, we adopt different representations for each in our method:

Ground Raster Maps.
Ground annotations are provided for five semantic classes (road, sidewalk, parking, terrain, ground) as 2D polygons that have been upwards extruded into 3D, each with a different shape and number of vertices (see Fig.
2
). The irregular and variable structure of these polygons makes it challenging to parameterize them in a consistent way that is suitable for learning. To address this, we perform ray casting on the extruded 3D surfaces of each semantic class in gravity direction to generate ground raster maps. These maps consist of bird’s eye view (BEV) height maps
\mathbf{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times 5}
and binary occupancy masks
\mathbf{B}\in\{0,1\}^{H\times W\times 5}
, providing a convenient representation well-suited for standard convolutional architectures.

Object primitives.
We group objects into 11 categories including: vegetation cuboid (
\mathcal{VC}
), vegetation ellipsoid (
\mathcal{VE}
), vehicle big (
\mathcal{VB}
), vehicle small (
\mathcal{VS}
), two wheelers (
\mathcal{TW}
), human (
\mathcal{H}
), construction big (
\mathcal{CB}
), construction small (
\mathcal{CS}
), pole (
\mathcal{P}
), traffic control (
\mathcal{TC}
), and object (
\mathcal{O}
). Each category is annotated in the form of bounding primitives: 3D cuboids are used across all categories except
\mathcal{VE}
, which are represented as 3D ellipsoids. We parameterize each object primitive by a 9-dimensional feature vector
\mathbf{f}_{i}\in[0,1]^{9}
consisting of the normalized 3D center location
\mathbf{t}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and 6D Cholesky parameters
\mathbf{c}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{6}
. The latter jointly encode orientation and size information via the lower-triangular Cholesky decomposition
[
10
]
of a positive-definite scatter matrix representing the object’s spatial extent in 3D. This representation removes orientation ambiguities caused by rotational symmetries.
Further details, including the list of semantic classes grouped under each category and the derivation of Cholesky parameters, are provided in the supplementary material.

3.3
).

Overall, we represent a 3D scene layout
\mathcal{S}
as a set
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}=\{\mathbf{H},\mathbf{B},\mathbf{F}\}
, where
\mathbf{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times 9}
contains the feature vectors for all object primitives in the layout, and
M
denotes their total number.

3.2
Layout Variational Autoencoder

To enable generation via a LDM, we train a layout variational autoencoder (LVAE) in the first stage to encode 3D semantic scene layouts into a compact 2D latent space. The LVAE has two jointly trained branches: one for rasterized ground inputs and one for vectorized object primitives.

Encoding.
Our architecture includes two encoder modules, each designed to handle a different type of input modality. The convolutional
ground encoder

[
56
]

\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{G}}

encodes the normalized ground raster maps into a latent representation
\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{G}}\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times w\times c}
, where
h=H/2^{d}
,
w=W/2^{d}
,
d\in\mathbb{R}
is the downsampling factor, and
c
denotes the number of latent channels. The
object encoder

\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{O}}
processes the feature vectors of object primitives. To facilitate fixed-size set prediction during decoding, we first pad each layout to a constant number of primitives per-category, determined from dataset statistics. Each object is then represented by its feature vector
\mathbf{f}_{i}
, augmented with a 10-dimensional learnable category embedding and a binary variable
p_{i}\in\{0,1\}
to distinguish real from padded entries. This yields features
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 20}
, where
N
is the fixed number of object primitives across all categories. These features are projected to a higher-dimensional space and processed by a Transformer encoder
[
63
]
to model inter-primitive relationships. The resulting embeddings are projected into a learnable 2D latent grid matching the spatial resolution of the ground latent. For that, we employ a scatter-mean operation, where each feature vector is assigned to a grid cell based on the normalized 2D center location of its corresponding object primitive. If multiple features are mapped to the same cell, they are aggregated by averaging, preserving spatial structure in the latent space.
The resulting grid serves as the object latent representation,
\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{O}}\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times w\times c}
, and is channel-wise concatenated with
\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{G}}
to construct a joint layout latent
\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{L}}=[\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{G}};\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{O}}]\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times w\times 2c}
suitable for diffusion modeling.

Decoding.
During decoding, a single latent code is sampled from the joint distribution and then split across the channel dimension into ground and object components. The
ground decoder

\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{G}}
processes the ground-specific latent
\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{G}}
through a convolutional architecture
[
56
]
to reconstruct the input raster maps. For object primitives, we employ an
object decoder

\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{O}}
that builds on the DETR
[
7
]
Transformer. The object-specific latent
\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{O}}
is patchified into a sequence of
h^{\prime}\times w^{\prime}
latent tokens, which are linearly projected and augmented with positional embeddings. The resulting tokens serve as keys and values in the decoder’s cross-attention layers. For each object category,
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{O}}
uses a fixed number of learnable
object queries
, yielding
\boldsymbol{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d_{L}}
, which are then transformed into output embeddings. The output embeddings are processed by per-category Feed Forward Networks (FFN), each predicting the 3D center location
\mathbf{\hat{t}}_{i}
, Cholesky parameters
\mathbf{\hat{c}}_{i}
, and an existence probability
\hat{p}_{i}
for every object primitive in that category. This setup
enables the model to consistently predict a fixed-size set of
N
primitives per layout.

Training.
Our training objective for the first stage combines losses from raster- and vector-based inputs, along with a KL divergence term computed over the joint latent. For
ground raster maps
, we apply two loss terms: an L1 loss between predicted and ground-truth height maps, averaged over occupied pixels, and a binary cross-entropy (BCE) loss between predicted and ground-truth occupancy masks. For
object primitives
, we first match predicted and ground-truth instances within each category using the Hungarian algorithm
[
27
]
, which minimizes a pairwise cost consisting of (i) BCE on existence probabilities, (ii) L1 loss on center locations, and (iii) L1 loss on Cholesky parameters. After matching,
the corresponding losses are computed for each object category, normalized by the number of real ground-truth instances inside the batch, and then averaged across the batch.

Layout Reconstruction.
To recover a 3D semantic scene layout, we first merge the predicted height maps for all ground classes into a unified height representation and then convert it into a triangle mesh via extrusion.
For object primitives, we first discard those with predicted existence probability lower than a threshold and then place the remaining ones into the scene layout according to their predicted 3D center locations and Cholesky parameters; further details are provided in the supplementary material. As with the ground layout, object primitives are converted into triangle meshes for visualization purposes.

3.3
Diffusion Transformer and Applications

The second stage of our pipeline trains a LDM
[
56
]
on the joint latent representations of our pre-trained LVAE. By leveraging a latent manipulation strategy inspired by RePaint
[
38
]
, our diffusion model supports practical downstream applications without additional fine-tuning.

Training.
We derive layout latent codes
\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{L}}
for all training samples using the frozen LVAE. These codes are used to train a diffusion model
\epsilon_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{L}}^{t},t,y)
, where
y
is a scene-level conditioning label controlling vegetation density (low, medium, high). While our experiments focus on vegetation, this conditioning scheme is general and can be applied to other object categories as well.
Following DiT
[
48
]
, we adopt a Transformer-based backbone that operates on sequences of noisy latent patches and incorporates conditioning via adaLN-Zero blocks
[
50
]
. The model is trained to predict Gaussian noise using a standard mean squared error (MSE) loss. After training, latent codes are sampled through the learned reverse diffusion process and decoded into novel 3D scene layouts.

Scene Inpainting.
RePaint
[
38
]
introduces a modified diffusion sampling strategy that synchronizes the denoising of unknown pixels with the noised version of known pixels, achieving impressive image inpainting results. We adopt this synchronization process in the 2D latent space of a pre-trained DiT model to support 3D scene layout editing, which is complementary to the object-level editing supported by our instance-level primitive representation. Additional implementation details are provided in the supplementary material. By properly setting a binary latent mask we can edit a given layout in diverse ways.
For example, we can inpaint its left half while keeping the right half fixed (see Fig.
1
). Another compelling use case, enabled by our disentangled latent structure, is ground-conditioned object generation, where we fix the ground channels and inpaint only the object channels, allowing new primitives to be synthesized in accordance with the existing ground layout.

Scene Outpainting.
We extend a 3D scene layout block beyond its original spatial extent by applying a sliding-window approach in 2D latent space that leverages the same latent manipulation strategy used for inpainting. To preserve spatial consistency across block boundaries, each new block is designed to partially overlap (typically by 50%) with its previously generated neighboring block. The input scene is first extended in parallel along the four cardinal directions, and then a second pass completes the remaining corner regions. This outpainting strategy can be applied iteratively to generate arbitrarily large 3D scenes.

Experiments

Dataset.
We conduct our experiments on the KITTI-360 dataset
[
32
]
, as it is the only real-world urban dataset with dense 3D bounding primitive annotations for large-scale outdoor environments. We group the 37 KITTI-360 classes into 16 categories by removing rare labels and merging the rest based on semantic and scale similarity, as we found scale consistency important for primitive reconstruction. For each ego-vehicle pose, we limit the corresponding 3D scene layout to a
64m\times 64m
field of view, extending
64m
forward and
32m
laterally on both sides. To minimize spatial overlap between training and test data,
we thoroughly split the dataset as detailed in the supplementary material. Additionally, layouts for which annotations are incomplete (e.g., at the end or beginning of a sequence) are discarded,
resulting in 61,913 training poses and 1,233 test poses.

Baseline.
Being the first primitive-based method for large-scale 3D semantic scene generation, we compare against a representative voxel-based baseline, SemCity
[
29
]
. This comparison allows for a detailed analysis of the differences between the two representations across various aspects.
Similar to PrITTI, SemCity adopts a two-stage training strategy: first, an autoencoder (AE) is trained based on a 3D-CNN encoder, an MLP decoder, and triplanes as the intermediate latent representation; second, a diffusion model is trained on the learned latent triplane space. For training SemCity, we follow their original setup and voxelize our primitive scenes into a

Implementation Details.
We implement our raster autoencoder and diffusion model using the standard VAE
[
56
]
and DiT
[
48
]
architectures provided by the Diffusers library
[
67
]
. In practice, we use ground raster maps with a spatial resolution of
H=W=256
and joint latents of dimensionality
h=w=32
and
c^{\prime}=2c=64
. Our object encoder consists of six Transformer encoder layers, while the object decoder comprises six Transformer decoder layers and processes a fixed number of
N=514
learnable object queries, determined from dataset statistics. Our LVAE, containing 93.1M trainable parameters, was trained on a single A100 GPU for 3 days with a batch size of 32. During this time period, our model completed 300 epochs, while SemCity, trained on the same GPU, reached only epoch 18. For training the diffusion model, we use the DiT-B architecture (130M parameters) with a
2\times 2
patch size, a batch size of 256, and DDPM
[
22
]
noise scheduling. The model is trained for 3 days on 4 A100 GPUs with 1000 timesteps, classifier-free guidance scale of 4.0 and linear variance schedule. During inference, we apply 250 denoising steps. For inpainting and outpainting, we set the roll-back step
J=10
and number of resampling times
R=10
.

4.1
3D Semantic Scene Reconstruction and Representation Efficiency

Metrics.
To evaluate
3D scene reconstruction
from the LVAE, we compute standard voxel-based metrics
[
29
]
, including Intersection-over-Union (IoU) and mean IoU, by voxelizing our reconstructed primitive scenes. In addition, our method supports evaluation using standard 3D object detection metrics
[
5
]
that are tailored for object primitives. Specifically, we compute the mean
AP_{3D}
across all object categories excluding
\mathcal{VE}
and over a range of IoU3D
[
52
]
thresholds
\tau\in\{0.05,\ldots,0.50\}
, as well as at
single thresholds
\tau=0.25
and
0.50
(
AP_{3D}^{25}
,
AP_{3D}^{50}
). For ground raster maps, evaluation is performed using
IoU for binary masks and MSE for height maps reconstruction.

Primitives vs. Voxels.
SemCity represents scenes using fixed-resolution voxel grids, while our method combines ground raster maps with 3D object primitives, resulting in a significantly more compact and memory-efficient representation. As shown in Fig.
3
, our method requires only 2.52 MB per scene (2.50 MB for rasters and just 0.02 MB for primitives) compared to 8.00 MB for SemCity at its highest resolution (
256^{2}\times 32
), which is over
3\times
larger. By default, during AE training, SemCity’s decoder is evaluated on a subsampled set of 400K points, rather than the full voxel grid. While this was sufficient in their setup, where training was performed on voxelized LiDAR scans that only partially capture scene geometry (e.g., building outlines), we found it limiting when training on our voxelized dataset, which preserves complete volumetric geometry. Although full grid evaluation was infeasible due to GPU memory constraints, increasing the query points count to 1M significantly improves reconstruction performance, raising mIoU from 70.52 to 93.81. We refer to this configuration as SemCity (Base).
To reduce memory requirements, we also train SemCity at lower resolutions (
64^{2}\times 8
and
128^{2}\times 16
), where evaluating all grid points is feasible, and then upsample the outputs to match the highest target resolution.
While this reduces per-scene memory requirement to 0.12 MB and 1.00 MB, respectively, it considerably lowers mIoU to
50.52 and 68.98, both falling below our method’s 70.27. This highlights a clear trade-off between memory requirements and reconstruction quality in voxel-based approaches. In contrast, our representation is resolution-independent and achieves comparable reconstruction quality with minimal memory requirements, despite using a much more compact latent space and being subject to approximation errors from voxelizing our scenes for evaluation. A qualitative comparison in Fig.
3b
shows that SemCity can occasionally produce incomplete objects due to missing query points in those regions, which mistakenly leads to empty voxels. The figure also illustrates how fixed-resolution grids can introduce artifacts like vertical clipping as seen in a truncated vegetation primitive.

Ablations.
We present autoencoder design and object parameterization variants in Fig.
4

(i) Cholesky vs. quaternions:
We compare our Cholesky-based encoding against a quaternion-based alternative using synthetic data, where each sample contains a single, zero-centered vehicle primitive with random scale and yaw. We vary the training set size while keeping the test set fixed across all settings (see supplementary material for more details). As shown in Fig.
4b
, our parameterization consistently achieves higher mean IoU3D and greater stability. In contrast, the quaternion-based encoding plateaus and even degrades with more data, likely due to the sign ambiguity causing loss discontinuities under standard regression objectives.
(ii) Latent split:
In this ablation, both decoders operate on the joint latent
\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{L}}
instead of their respective components,
\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{G}}
and
\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{O}}
. This modification degrades reconstruction performance across all metrics: AP3D drops from 62.11 to 53.78, MSE increases from 0.0075 to 0.0355, and IoU falls from 0.9996 to 0.9970, likely because the combined features make it harder for each decoder to focus on its relevant ground or object information.
(iii) Joint vs. separate training:
We compare joint training of ground and object branches in a shared latent space against training them independently. In the default setting, a single latent code is sampled from the joint distribution and then split during decoding. In the ablated variant, we train two separate VAEs with no shared latent or joint optimization. While separate training slightly improves height map reconstruction (MSE: 0.0059 vs. 0.0075), joint training yields better primitive reconstruction (AP3D: 62.11 vs. 60.28), likely due to improved semantic alignment between branches, allowing the object decoder to place objects more accurately based on scene context (e.g., cars on road).

5
Conclusion

We presented PrITTI, the first method for generating large-scale 3D semantic scenes in urban environments using vectorized object primitives and rasterized ground surfaces. Our latent diffusion model produces high-quality, diverse, and controllable layouts, and further supports downstream tasks like scene inpainting and outpainting without additional fine-tuning. Compared to a voxel-based baseline, PrITTI achieves similar reconstruction quality with over
3\times
lower memory requirements and significantly better generative performance. Our instance-level primitive representation enables intuitive object-level edits such as translation and scaling, which remain challenging to perform with voxel grids. These results establish primitives as a compact, scalable, and well-suited representation for compositional 3D semantic scene generation.

Limitations.
While PrITTI demonstrates strong results on KITTI-360, our evaluation is currently limited to this dataset due to the scarcity of large-scale outdoor scenes with dense primitive annotations. Additionally, the coarse nature of our primitives limits geometric detail. Future work could explore finer or deformable shapes to better capture complex structures (e.g., ground surfaces)
[
47
,
1
]
. Finally, our method is limited to static scenes and does not yet model real-world dynamics essential for autonomous driving simulations and adversarial scenarios generation
[
19
]
.

Dataset Construction.

As a first step, we analyze dataset statistics and discard infrequent labels including tunnel, bridge, train, stop, and caravan. Among the remaining labels, five correspond to ground-related classes (road, terrain, sidewalk, parking, ground), which we treat as separate ground categories. The rest are grouped into 11 object categories based on semantic and physical scale similarity, the latter being important for accurate primitive reconstruction. Moreover, the provided test poses are distributed sequentially within limited portions of each sequence, covering only a narrow spatial extent. B
Methodological Details

In this section, we provide detailed information regarding the representations, methodologies, and architectural components used in our framework.

B.1
Cholesky Parameters

Standard quaternion-based encodings of 3D orientation suffer from sign ambiguity: both
q
and
-q
represent the same rotation but differ numerically. This introduces discontinuities in the loss landscape when optimizing with standard regression objectives. To address this, we propose a Cholesky-based parameterization that jointly encodes the orientation and size of an object in a continuous 6D representation.
To derive this representation, we first construct a matrix
\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
, referred to as the object’s
scatter matrix
, which captures its spatial extent in 3D. Let
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}>0
denote the object’s scaling factors along its principal axes, and let
\mathbf{v}_{1},\mathbf{v}_{2},\mathbf{v}_{3}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
be the orthonormal column vectors of the object’s rotation matrix. These form the orthonormal basis matrix
\mathbf{V}=[\mathbf{v}_{1}\ \mathbf{v}_{2}\ \mathbf{v}_{3}]
and the diagonal scale matrix
\mathbf{\Lambda}=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3})
, from which we construct the scatter matrix as:

\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{V}\mathbf{\Lambda}\mathbf{V}^{\top}=\sum_{j=1}^{3}\lambda_{j}\mathbf{v}_{j}\mathbf{v}_{j}^{\top}

(1)

We then apply the Cholesky decomposition
[
10
]
to
\mathbf{S}
, yielding
\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^{\top}
, where
\mathbf{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
is a lower-triangular matrix. The Cholesky parameters
\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{6}
consist of the non-zero entries of
\mathbf{L}
.

Our Cholesky-based parameterization removes orientation ambiguities by operating on a sign-invariant scatter matrix
\mathbf{S}
. Specifically,
\mathbf{S}
is invariant under orientation sign flips
\mathbf{v}_{j}\mapsto-\mathbf{v}_{j}
since the outer product
\mathbf{v}_{j}\mathbf{v}_{j}^{\top}
remains unchanged. Additionally, we can show that
\mathbf{S}
is symmetric and positive-definite:

Symmetric.

\mathbf{S}^{\top}=(\mathbf{V}\mathbf{\Lambda}\mathbf{V}^{\top})^{\top}=(\mathbf{V}^{\top})^{\top}\mathbf{\Lambda}^{\top}\mathbf{V}^{\top}=\mathbf{V}\mathbf{\Lambda}\mathbf{V}^{\top}=\mathbf{S}

(2)

since
(\mathbf{V}^{\top})^{\top}=\mathbf{V}
and
\mathbf{\Lambda}
is diagonal (and hence symmetric).

Positive-definite.

Because
\mathbf{V}
is orthonormal (i.e.,
\mathbf{V}^{\top}=\mathbf{V}^{-1}
),
\mathbf{S}
is similar to
\mathbf{\Lambda}
:

\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{V}\mathbf{\Lambda}\mathbf{V}^{-1}

(3)

and thus shares the same eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}>0
. Therefore,
\mathbf{S}
is positive-definite.

These properties ensure the existence of a
unique
Cholesky decomposition
[
10
]
, where
\mathbf{L}
is a lower-triangular matrix with strictly positive diagonal entries. This yields a unique 6-dimensional representation
\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{6}
, well-suited for regression-based learning.

Given the Cholesky parameters
\mathbf{c}
, we reshape them into a lower-triangular matrix
\mathbf{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
and recover the scatter matrix as
\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^{\top}
. By performing eigendecomposition of
\mathbf{S}
, we obtain its eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}
and eigenvectors
\mathbf{v}_{1},\mathbf{v}_{2},\mathbf{v}_{3}
. These define the object’s scaling and rotation matrices as
\mathbf{\Lambda}=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3})
and
\mathbf{R}=[\mathbf{v}_{1}\ \mathbf{v}_{2}\ \mathbf{v}_{3}]
, respectively.

B.2
Layout Variational Autoencoder

In the first training stage, a Layout Variational Autoencoder (LVAE) learns to compress 3D semantic scene layouts into compact 2D latent representations suitable for latent diffusion modeling.

B.2.1
Architecture

The LVAE consists of separate encoder-decoder pairs for ground and object modalities. We describe each component in detail below.

Ground Encoder.
Our ground encoder
\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{G}}
is composed of stacked downsampling blocks that progressively reduce spatial resolution while increasing channel dimensionality. Each block contains a residual unit followed by a strided convolution. The residual units consist of two convolutional layers with group normalization and SiLU activation. At the bottleneck, a self-attention layer captures global context, while additional residual blocks focus on local features. The encoder takes as input the channel-wise concatenated ground raster maps,
[\mathbf{H};\mathbf{B}]\in\mathbb{R}^{256\times 256\times 10}
, and produces a latent grid of shape
32\times 32\times 64
. This grid is split along the channel dimension into two chunks representing the mean
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathcal{G}}\in\mathbb{R}^{32\times 32\times 32}
and variance
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\mathcal{G}}\in\mathbb{R}^{32\times 32\times 32}
of the ground latent representation.

Object Encoder.
Our object encoder
\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{O}}
operates on a fixed-size set of object primitives, each represented by a 20-dimensional feature vector. The input vectors are first projected to a 512-dimensional embedding space and processed by a 6-layer Transformer encoder with 8 attention heads and a feedforward dimension of 1024. A linear projection layer then reduces the output dimensionality to 64. Using a scatter-mean operation, the resulting features are spatially arranged into a latent grid of shape
32\times 32\times 64
, which is split along the channel dimension into the mean
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathcal{O}}\in\mathbb{R}^{32\times 32\times 32}
and variance
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\mathcal{O}}\in\mathbb{R}^{32\times 32\times 32}
of the object latent representation.

Joint Layout Latent.
We form joint mean and variance tensors,
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathcal{L}},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\mathcal{L}}\in\mathbb{R}^{32\times 32\times 64}
, by concatenating the corresponding ground and object components along the channel axis. A joint layout latent representation
\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{L}}\in\mathbb{R}^{32\times 32\times 64}
is then sampled using the reparameterization trick. This design preserves a disentangled latent structure that can be easily separated into ground and object latents,
\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{G}},\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{O}}\in\mathbb{R}^{32\times 32\times 32}
. We found this beneficial for improving reconstruction quality and enabling applications such as generation of novel object primitives conditioned on a given ground layout.

Ground Decoder.
The ground decoder
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{G}}
first processes the ground latent representation
\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{G}}
through a self-attention layer followed by two residual units. The resulting features are then progressively upsampled to the original spatial resolution
256\times 256
through a series of upsampling blocks. Each block consists of two residual units and a transposed convolution. As spatial resolution increases, the number of channels is gradually reduced. A final convolutional layer maps the output to 10 channels, matching the dimensionality of the concatenated ground raster maps,
[\hat{\mathbf{H}};\hat{\mathbf{B}}]\in\mathbb{R}^{256\times 256\times 10}
.

Object Decoder.
The object decoder
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{O}}
follows a DETR-style
[
7
]
Transformer architecture and uses
N
learnable queries, representing the total number of predicted primitives across all object categories. It comprises 6 Transformer decoder layers with 8 attention heads, a feedforward dimension of 1024, and no dropout. The queries interact with the object latent representation
\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{O}}
through cross-attention. Specifically, the latent is first patchified with a patch size of 1 resulting in a sequence of
32\times 32
tokens, which are augmented with sine positional embeddings. The resulting query embeddings of dimensionality 512 are grouped by object category and passed through dedicated feedforward networks (FFN) to predict the corresponding object primitive parameters.

Prediction Heads.
We use a separate prediction head for each object category, composed of a 3-layer FFN with a hidden size of 1024. The FFN outputs a 9-dimensional vector for each primitive encoding
its normalized 3D center location and 6D Cholesky parameters. A probability of existence is predicted by an additional linear layer.

B.2.2
Training

The first-stage training loss comprises three components, detailed below.

Ground Loss.
We supervise the reconstruction of input raster maps using two losses: a binary cross-entropy (BCE) loss for occupancy masks, and an L1 loss for height maps averaged over occupied pixels. Let
\mathbf{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times 5}
and
\mathbf{B}\in\{0,1\}^{H\times W\times 5}
denote the ground-truth height maps and occupancy masks, respectively, and let
\hat{\mathbf{H}}
and
\hat{\mathbf{B}}
be their predicted counterparts. The ground loss is given by:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{ground}}=\lambda_{\mathcal{G}}^{\text{occ.}}\cdot\mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}}(\hat{\mathbf{B}},\mathbf{B})+\lambda_{\mathcal{G}}^{\text{height}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sum\mathbf{B}}\left\|(\hat{\mathbf{H}}-\mathbf{H})\odot\mathbf{B}\right\|_{1}

(4)

where
\odot
denotes element-wise multiplication. The weight terms
\lambda_{\mathcal{G}}^{\text{occ.}},\lambda_{\mathcal{G}}^{\text{height}}
control the relative contributions of the two losses and are set to 1 and 9, respectively.

Object Loss.

Our object decoder
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{O}}
predicts a fixed number of
N^{c}
primitives for each object category
c
(see Table
2
), resulting in
N=\sum_{c=1}^{11}N^{c}
total predictions per scene. Since these predictions are unordered, we first establish one-to-one correspondences by computing a bipartite matching between predicted and ground-truth primitives for each category independently. Specifically, let
\mathcal{O}^{c}=\{\mathbf{o}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N^{c}}
and
\hat{\mathcal{O}}^{c}=\{\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N^{c}}
denote the sets of ground-truth and predicted primitives, respectively, for category
c
. Each primitive is represented as
\mathbf{o}_{i}=(\mathbf{t}_{i},\mathbf{c}_{i},p_{i})
, where
\mathbf{t}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is the 3D center location,
\mathbf{c}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{6}
are the Cholesky parameters, and
p_{i}\in\{0,1\}
is a binary variable distinguishing existent primitives
(p_{i}=1)
from padded placeholders
(p_{i}=0)
. We apply the Hungarian algorithm
[
27
]
to find the optimal assignment
\hat{\sigma}^{c}
minimizing the total cost:

\hat{\sigma}^{c}=\arg\min_{\sigma^{c}\in\mathcal{P}_{N^{c}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N^{c}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{match}}(\mathbf{o}_{i},\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{\sigma(i)})

(5)

where
\mathcal{P}_{N^{c}}
denotes the set of all permutations of
N^{c}
elements, and the pairwise matching cost
\mathcal{L}_{\text{match}}
is defined as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{match}}(\mathbf{o}_{i},\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{\sigma(i)})

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\mathcal{O}}^{\text{prob.}}\cdot\mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}}(p_{i},\hat{p}_{\sigma(i)})

(6)

\displaystyle+\mathds{1}_{[p_{i}>0]}\cdot\Big(\lambda_{\mathcal{O}}^{\text{center}}\cdot\|\mathbf{t}_{i}-\hat{\mathbf{t}}_{\sigma(i)}\|_{1}+\lambda_{\mathcal{O}}^{\text{Chol.}}\cdot\|\mathbf{c}_{i}-\hat{\mathbf{c}}_{\sigma(i)}\|_{1}\Big)

After matching, we compute the object loss over all categories using the optimal assignments:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{object}}=\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N^{c}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{match}}(\mathbf{o}_{i},\hat{\mathbf{o}}_{\hat{\sigma}^{c}(i)})\right)

(7)

where
\mathcal{C}
denotes the set of all object categories. Each loss term in Eq. (
7
) is normalized by the number of real (i.e., non-padded) ground-truth primitives in the corresponding sample. The normalized losses are then averaged across the batch.

The weights
\lambda_{\mathcal{O}}^{\text{prob.}},\lambda_{\mathcal{O}}^{\text{center}}
and
\lambda_{\mathcal{O}}^{\text{Chol.}}
are configured differently for bipartite matching and loss computation. For
matching
, we use fixed values across all object categories:
\lambda_{\mathcal{O}}^{\text{prob.}}=6
,
\lambda_{\mathcal{O}}^{\text{center}}=3
, and
\lambda_{\mathcal{O}}^{\text{Chol.}}=3
. Here, we prioritize the BCE term to avoid pairing low-confidence predictions with real ground-truth instances. The center and Cholesky terms are given equal weight, reflecting their shared importance for accurate geometric alignment. During
loss computation
, we use different weights for object categories based on their fixed number of predicted primitives. Specifically, categories are grouped into low-, medium-, and high-count ranges, with higher weights assigned to higher-count groups. This adjustment accounts for the fact that individual losses are normalized by the number of real ground-truth primitives in each sample. Without it, high-count categories, which typically contain more real instances per sample, would contribute less to the overall loss, making them harder for the model to learn. For each group, we set the center
\lambda_{\mathcal{O}}^{\text{center}}
and Cholesky
\lambda_{\mathcal{O}}^{\text{Chol.}}
weights such that their corresponding losses are of similar magnitude. This ensures that both geometric components contribute equally during training. We assign a smaller weight
\lambda_{\mathcal{O}}^{\text{prob.}}
to the BCE loss compared to
\lambda_{\mathcal{O}}^{\text{center}}
and
\lambda_{\mathcal{O}}^{\text{Chol.}}
, since accurate existence prediction is already encouraged by the earlier matching step. Increasing this weight would unnecessarily emphasize the BCE term, reducing the influence of the geometric components in the overall loss. Table

B.2.4
Layout Reconstruction

To reconstruct a 3D scene layout, we follow distinct procedures for ground surfaces and objects, reflecting their different representations. For
ground surfaces
, we first merge the individual height maps of all ground categories into a unified composite map. When multiple categories overlap at the same location, we retain the one with the highest elevation value. We then construct a triangle mesh by projecting each occupied pixel to 3D space and connecting adjacent pixels in a
2\times 2
grid to form two triangles per cell. This creates a continuous surface that faithfully represents the reconstructed ground geometry. For
objects
, we first recover their rotation matrix
\hat{\mathbf{R}}
and scaling matrix
\hat{\mathbf{\Lambda}}
from the predicted Cholesky parameters
\hat{\mathbf{c}}\in\mathbb{R}^{6}
, as described in Sec.
B.1
. Next, we construct a full transformation matrix
\hat{\mathbf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{4\times 4}
by combining the recovered orientation and scale with the predicted 3D center location
\hat{\mathbf{t}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
:

\hat{\mathbf{T}}=\begin{bmatrix}\hat{\mathbf{R}}\hat{\mathbf{\Lambda}}&\hat{\mathbf{t}}\\
\mathbf{0}^{\top}&1\end{bmatrix}

(10)

This transformation is applied to a unit-sized, zero-centered 3D cuboid or ellipsoid (depending on the object category) to place the predicted primitive within the reconstructed 3D scene layout.

\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{L}}\in\mathbb{R}^{32\times 32\times 64}
.

B.3.2
Training

Given an initial latent
\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{L}}^{0}
, we define a
forward process
that gradually corrupts it by adding small amount of Gaussian noise over time, producing a sequence of increasingly noisy latents
\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{L}}^{1},\ldots,\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{L}}^{T}
. The amount of noise introduced at each step is controlled by a variance schedule
\{\beta_{t}\in(0,1)\}_{t=1}^{T}
. The joint distribution over all timesteps can be expressed as:

\displaystyle q(\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{L}}^{1:T}\mid\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{L}}^{0})

\displaystyle=\prod_{t=1}^{T}q(\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{L}}^{t}\mid\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{L}}^{t-1})

(11)

\displaystyle q(\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{L}}^{t}\mid\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{L}}^{t-1})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{L}}^{t};\sqrt{1-\beta_{t}}\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{L}}^{t-1},\beta_{t}\mathbf{I})

(12)

A convenient property of the above process is that the noisy latent
\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{L}}^{t}
at any timestep
t
can be directly sampled in a single step from the following distribution:

q(\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{L}}^{t}\mid\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{L}}^{0})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{L}}^{t};\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{L}}^{0},(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t})\mathbf{I})

(13)

where
\alpha_{t}=1-\beta_{t}
, and
\bar{\alpha}_{t}=\prod_{s=1}^{t}\alpha_{s}
.

To generate novel latents, we learn a
reverse process
that denoises from pure noise
\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{L}}^{T}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I})
back to a clean latent
\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{L}}^{0}
. The joint distribution of this denoising process is given by:

\displaystyle p_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{L}}^{0:T})=p(\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{L}}^{T})\prod_{t=1}^{T}p_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{L}}^{t-1}\mid\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{L}}^{t})

(14)

\displaystyle p_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{L}}^{t-1}\mid\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{L}}^{t})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{L}}^{t-1};\mu_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{L}}^{t},t),\Sigma_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{L}}^{t},t))

(15)

where each transition is modeled as a Gaussian with learnable mean
\mu_{\theta}
and covariance
\Sigma_{\theta}
, both estimated by a neural denoising network with parameters
\theta
. In practice, we follow prior work
[
22
]
and set
\Sigma_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{L}}^{t},t)=\sigma_{t}^{2}\mathbf{I}=\frac{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\beta_{t}\mathbf{I}
to untrained time dependent constants. Moreover, we represent the mean
\mu_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{L}}^{t},t)
as:

\displaystyle\mu_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{L}}^{t},t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}}\left(\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{L}}^{t}-\frac{\beta_{t}}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}}\,\epsilon_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{L}}^{t},t)\right)

(16)

where
\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{L}}^{t}=\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\,\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{L}}^{0}+\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
and
\epsilon_{\theta}
is a neural network that predicts
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
from
\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{L}}^{t}
. For training this network, we adopt the simplified objective introduced in
[
22
]
:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{simple}}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{L}}^{0},\boldsymbol{\epsilon},t}\left[\left\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}-\epsilon_{\theta}\left(\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\,\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{L}}^{0}+\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon},t\right)\right\|^{2}\right]

(17)

In our framework, we condition the denoising network on additional input
y
, which controls the density of certain primitives in the scene (e.g., vegetation). The network then becomes
\epsilon_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{L}}^{t},t,y)
, and the training objective is modified as:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{LDM}}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{L}}^{0},\boldsymbol{\epsilon},t,y}\left[\left\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}-\epsilon_{\theta}\left(\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\,\mathbf{z}_{\mathcal{L}}^{0}+\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon},t,y\right)\right\|^{2}\right]

(18)

B.3.4
Applications

By leveraging a latent manipulation strategy inspired by RePaint
[
38
]
, our diffusion model supports practical downstream tasks without fine-tuning.

Scene Inpainting.
RePaint proposed a modified diffusion-based sampling strategy that enables image inpainting using a binary mask to specify known and unknown regions. The core idea is to leverage a pre-trained denoising diffusion model as a generative prior and synchronize the denoising of unknown pixels with the noised version of known pixels. In our method, we extend this strategy to operate in the 2D latent space of a pre-trained LDM, enabling localized edits of 3D scene layouts. For clarity, we drop the subscript
\mathcal{L}
and denote the noisy layout latent at timestep
t
as
\mathbf{z}_{t}
. Given an initial latent
\mathbf{z}_{0}
, the modified denoising step becomes:

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}_{t-1}

\displaystyle=(1-m)\odot\mathbf{z}_{t-1}^{\text{known}}+m\odot\mathbf{z}_{t-1}^{\text{unknown}}

(19)

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}_{t-1}^{\text{known}}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\mathbf{z}_{0},(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t})\mathbf{I})

(20)

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}_{t-1}^{\text{unknown}}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_{t},t,y),\sigma_{t}^{2}\mathbf{I})

(21)

where
m
is a binary latent mask identifying the regions to be edited,
\odot
is the element-wise product, and
\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_{t},t,y),\sigma_{t}^{2}\mathbf{I})
represents the learned reverse process of our pre-trained diffusion model. By properly setting the mask
m
, we can edit an original scene in diverse ways.

Scene Outpainting.

Scene outpainting aims to extend a 3D scene layout beyond its initial spatial extent. We achieve this by leveraging the same latent manipulation strategy used for inpainting, combined with a sliding-window approach to ensure spatial consistency. Specifically, each newly generated layout block is designed to partially overlap (by 50%) with its preceding block. In each outpainting step, the initial latent
\mathbf{z}_{0}
is formed by copying values from the overlapping area and zeroing out the rest. The binary latent mask
m
is set to one in the non-overlapping region and zero elsewhere, indicating the region to be synthesized.

C
Experimental Details

In this section, we detail the synthetic dataset and evaluation setup used to compare our Cholesky-based encoding against a quaternion-based alternative. In particular, for this ablation study, we construct synthetic datasets of increasing size, where each sample contains a single zero-centered vehicle primitive with randomly sampled scales and yaw orientations. The training sets consist of 1.2K, 2.5K, 5K, and 10K samples, with each larger set including all samples from the smaller ones. A shared test set of 2K samples is used for evaluation. To reduce variance due to dataset randomness, we repeat the dataset generation with five different random seeds. For each seed and training size, both encoding variants are trained for 20 epochs, and we calculate the mean IoU3D on the shared test set. Final results are obtained by averaging IoU3D across seeds for each dataset size.

Precision and Recall Metrics.
For evaluating our generative models, among others, we use the precision and recall metrics proposed by Kynkäänniemi et al.
[
28
]
. Given feature sets
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{r}
and
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{g}
extracted from real and generated samples, respectively, a binary function
f(\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\Phi})
determines whether a feature vector
\boldsymbol{\phi}
lies within the estimated manifold of a reference set
\boldsymbol{\Phi}\in\{\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{r},\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{g}\}
:

f(\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\Phi})=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }\|\boldsymbol{\phi}-\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime}\|_{2}\leq\|\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime}-\text{NN}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\Phi})\|_{2}\text{ for at least one }\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime}\in\boldsymbol{\Phi}\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(22)

where
\text{NN}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\Phi})
denotes the
k
th
nearest neighbor
of
\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\prime}
from the set
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
. Using this function, the precision and recall metrics are computed as:

\displaystyle\text{precision}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{r},\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{g})=\frac{1}{|\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{g}|}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\phi}_{g}\in\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{g}}f(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{g},\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{r})

(23)

\displaystyle\text{recall}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{r},\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{g})=\frac{1}{|\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{r}|}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\phi}_{r}\in\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{r}}f(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{r},\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{g})

(24)

Intuitively, precision measures the proportion of generated samples that appear realistic, while recall reflects how well these samples cover the real data manifold. Following
[
28
]
, we use an equal number of real and generated samples, i.e.,
|\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{r}|=|\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{g}|
. However, we found that precision and recall are highly sensitive to several factors including:
(i)
implementation details,
(ii)
the diversity of real samples,
(iii)
the number of evaluation samples, and
(iv)
the neighborhood size
k
used for manifold estimation (see Eq.
22
). For
(i)
, we follow
[
48
]
, which forms the basis of our DiT models, and use the evaluation suite from
[
15
]
. To address
(ii)
, we implement two distinct strategies for sampling real poses. The first, presented in the main paper, enforces a minimum spatial distance threshold (in meters) between neighboring poses. The second uses a farthest point sampling (FPS)-inspired approach, where poses are selected sequentially to maximize their distance from the previously chosen ones. The number of evaluation samples in
(iii)
is either explicitly specified or implicitly determined. In the threshold-based strategy, it is inferred automatically from the distance constraint. In contrast, for the FPS approach, we explicitly control the evaluation size, experimenting with 1K, 5K, and 10K real sampled poses. In both strategies, we additionally vary the neighborhood size
k
in
(iv)
(specifically,
k=3
and
k=5
) to assess its influence on the resulting generative performance. Fig.
9
presents additional quantitative comparisons of precision and recall between our method and SemCity under these different evaluation configurations.

For both sampling strategies, we observe a consistent decrease in precision as the number of evaluation samples increases. In the distance-based strategy, this corresponds to smaller threshold values (e.g., 1m yields 32,203 samples, whereas 10m yields 3,855). This behavior can be explained through Eq.
22
: as more real samples are added, the hyperspheres defined by their
k
th
nearest neighbors become smaller and more densely packed. Consequently, even if a generated sample appears realistic, it becomes more difficult to fall within the required distance of any real sample’s hypersphere. In contrast, recall remains relatively stable across varying sample counts. This is likely because denser sampling refines the real manifold but does not change its overall extent. Additionally, we observe that both precision and recall improve as the neighborhood size
k
increases. This follows intuitively from the definition of the binary function
f(\cdot,\cdot)
in Eq.
22
, where a larger
k
results in larger hyperspheres for each sample, loosening the inclusion criterion.

E
Limitations

Despite its promising results, our approach comes with certain limitations. First, it is evaluated only on KITTI-360
[
32
]
, as this remains the sole real-world dataset providing dense primitive-level annotations. Moreover, as discussed in the main paper, our current framework is limited to static scenes and represents objects using coarse 3D primitives, which reduces geometric fidelity. In addition, ground elements are modeled as rasterized 2D representations and subsequently extruded into 3D. However, this extrusion process is inherently constrained by the resolution of the input raster maps, which may lead to less detailed ground surfaces. Furthermore, accurately reconstructing fine-grained attachments (e.g., lamps or traffic lights mounted on poles) and small, densely arranged structures (e.g., fences) remains quite challenging. Such geometric inaccuracies can be learned by the autoencoder and propagated to the diffusion model. This occasionally results in generated scenes with floating or disconnected objects. Finally, another limitation arises in outpainting settings, particularly when the conditioning block used in our sliding-window approach contains large empty regions along the extrapolation direction. In such cases, the diffusion model is afforded greater generative freedom, which can lead to completions that are weakly connected or semantically inconsistent with the rest of the scene. Fig.
10
illustrates two failure cases of our method.

F
Potential Societal Impacts

Our work targets 3D semantic scene generation for urban environments using primitive-based representations. Traditionally, creating such 3D environments requires expert knowledge and significant manual effort, making it costly and time-consuming. Additionally, reliance on existing layout data, which remain scarce particularly for complex outdoor scenes, limits diversity and can lead to repetitive scene compositions. In contrast, our approach enables the generation of diverse and realistic 3D semantic scenes. This has the potential to benefit a range of applications, including simulation, robotics, and autonomous driving. By supporting controllable and interpretable 3D generation, our method can contribute to safer and more scalable testing environments as well as improved scene understanding for embodied agents. However, like all generative models, our framework could be misused if applied to construct or manipulate 3D spatial environments in ways that misrepresent reality. For example, synthetic scenes could be used to simulate favorable outcomes in urban planning proposals or test surveillance systems under unrealistic conditions. Moreover, our primitive-based representation simplifies real-world geometry, which may lead to misleading conclusions in downstream tasks if over-relied upon. This risk, however, does not undermine its value in practical applications. As with existing modeling and simulation tools, effective use relies on a clear understanding of the method’s assumptions and limitations.
