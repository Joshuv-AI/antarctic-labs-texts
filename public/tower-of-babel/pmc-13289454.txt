Background

Appropriate infection prevention and control (IPC) practices constitutes a cornerstone of patient safety and quality care across all healthcare settings [1]. Healthcare-associated infections (HAIs) is considered a big challenge for healthcare system at different levels, including outcome, resources, and cost of care [2]. This happens despite the belief that up to 70% of HAIs can be potentially prevented through effective IPC strategies [3].

Emergency departments presents a distinctive challenge for IPC implementation due to its operational characteristics, diverse patient population, and complex dynamic environment, shared rooms, and unknown carrier status [4]. Several guidelines highlighted the appropriate IPC practices relevant to the emergency department [4, 5]. These include hand hygiene, use of personal protective equipment, aseptic techniques, environmental cleaning, and other IPC measures aiming to prevent transmission of infection at an emergency setting [6, 7]. Despite the availability of IPC guidelines for emergency setting, the compliance is not always optimal [8]. Several factors can potentially influence the compliance of healthcare workers (HCWs) with IPC practices. These include organizational factors such structure, policies, resources, and overcrowding and HCW factors such as awareness, beliefs, skills, and training [9–11]. These factors are usually targeted in intervention programs aiming to improve the compliance of HCWs with IPC practices [12–14].

Despite the critical role of IPC in emergency care and the persistent challenges in achieving optimal compliance, comprehensive studies assessing different IPC practices in emergency settings are limited, locally [15] and internationally [7]. Most of the studies focus on hand hygiene compliance and environmental factors [16, 17]. The objective of the current study was to assess the compliance with a number of IPC practices among a group of HCWs working in a large emergency department. Additionally, to evaluate variability in compliance by profession, unit, shift, and pandemic time.

Methods

Design

An observational cross-sectional study was conducted at the emergency departments between 2018 and 2023. The study obtained the required ethical approvals from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at King Abdullah International Medical Research Centre (KAIMRC), Riyadh, Saudi Arabia.

Setting

This study was conducted at the Emergency Departments of two hospitals of the Ministry of National Guard Health Affairs, Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, Riyadh; King Abdulaziz Medical City (KAMC-R) and King Abdullah Specialized Children Hospital (KASCH-R). The two hospitals are 1700-bed tertiary care facility serving approximately 1.38 million individuals affiliated with the Saudi National Guard, including soldiers, employees, and their families. KAMC-R was serving mainly adult patients but also pediatric cardiac patients while KASCH-R was serving pediatric patients but also adult oncology/hematology patients. The facility has been accredited by the Joint Commission International (JCI). The Emergency Departments are huge with over 250 active beds totals, accommodating more than 260,000 visits annually, averaging over 700 patients daily. The patients were assigned to one of the six units based on the Canadian Triage and Acuity Scale (CATS), which is divided into five levels [18]. The triage itself was done in the rapid assessment and management unit. Levels 1 and 2 were assigned to resuscitation, level 3 was assigned to acute care units, levels 4 and 5 were assigned to urgent care unit. Those who are waiting admission bed were assigned to acute medical unit and respiratory patients of all levels were assigned to flu unit.

Participants

Healthcare workers working at the emergency department were included in the study. There were no exclusions based on professional categories or duration of service. Administrative staff without clinical duties were excluded from the study.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated based on an expected compliance rate of 50%, which is associated with largest sample size. A total 384 observation opportunities would be required to detect this compliance rate with a 5% confidence interval, 80% power, and 95% significance level. Much larger sample was were collected to allow stratification of the compliance by unit and healthcare professionals.

Data collection tool

Data were gathered using a standardized IPC observation form to evaluate compliance rates across different standard IPC practices. HCW-specific practices are IPC practices monitored for individual HCWs during the observation session. These included performing hand hygiene when indicated, wearing appropriate personal protective equipment (PPE) according to anticipated risk/exposure, appropriate donning and doffing of PPE, and cleaning and disinfection of medical equipment in-between patients. Unit-specific practices are IPC practices monitored for the unit as a whole during the observation session. These included adherence to isolation precautions (all suspected/confirmed cases of epidemiologically important pathogens are appropriately isolated with appropriate signages), proper disposal of sharps (immediately after use in appropriate containers which are not overfilled), proper waste management (as per hospital policy), proper housekeeping of environmental surfaces (proper contact time and dilution of disinfectants), proper patient sitter practices (wearing PPE as per hospital policy and restricted in case of patients with positive acute respiratory infection, ARI), and ARI competence as assessed by overall IPC practices done by the unit staff for patients with positive ARI (by discussion and interrogation when applicable). Hand hygiene compliance was defined as performing hand rubbing (with alcohol-based formulation) or hand washing (with soap and water) during one of the World Health Organization (WHO) five-moment HH opportunities [19]. These include before patient contact, before an aseptic task, after exposure to body fluids, after patient contact, and after contact with patient surroundings [19].

Data collection

It was done during observation sessions. Sessions were limited to no more than 10 min to mitigate the Hawthorne effect, where the awareness of being observed might alter behavior [20]. Sessions were chosen conveniently during morning and afternoon shifts in both weekdays and weekends to get a better representation of the compliance rates. The four HCW practices were monitored for all HCWs observed during the session while the six-unit practices were done once when applicable. Professional categories monitored included physicians, nurses, and other HCWs. The later included technician, therapists, dietician, students, social worker, and any other health-related profession involved in patient care. Observers were either experienced IPC professionals or medical students who received standardized training on IPC guidelines prior to starting data collection. This included a session about universal precautions, isolation precautions, and related IPC policies. Additionally, students’ data collection was instantly monitored by an experienced IPC professionals before independent data collection. and Observers were wearing hospital attire to be granted access to healthcare areas/services. Observers were collecting data from all units without staff assignments for specific units.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Compliance was calculated by dividing the sum of compliant opportunities over the overall opportunities observed. Compliance was stratified by IPC practices, professional categories, healthcare units, shift work, and pandemic time. Compliance rates across strata were compared using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. The sample size calculation was done using Open Epi version 2.2. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all two-tailed tests. SPSS (Version 27.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp) was used for all statistical analyses.

Results

Table 1 presents an overview of observed IPC practices across professional categories and healthcare units. Out of 123,947 HCW-specific practices observed, 85,542 (69.0%) were compliant and out of 41,650 unit-specific practices observed, 38,355 (92.1%) were compliant. The details of the compliance rates and their 95% confidence intervals are shown in Supplementary Table 1. There was high variability between different practices as regards the number of observations. For example, hand hygiene was the highest among all the ten practices to contribute data (31.4%), followed by proper use of PPE (19.9%) and proper donning and doffing of PPE (15.2%). ARI competence was the lowest among all the ten practices to contribute data (1.2%), followed by adherence of patient sitters (3.3%) and proper housekeeping (4.3%).

Table 2 presents compliance data for various IPC practices across different units. The data showed generally high compliance specially with unit-specific practices. For example, the compliance of all unit-specific practices (with the exception of ARI competence) was similarly high (> 90%) in all units. On the other hand, there were significantly variable levels of compliance with key HCW-specific and unit-specific practices across the six units. The variability between units was highest in the compliance with patient sitters’ policy, which ranged between 52.5% compliance in flue unit and 92.1% in urgent care center (39.6% differences between units). The variability between units was also relatively high in the compliance with proper use of PPE (14.4% differences), hand hygiene (13.8%), and disinfection of medical equipment (12.1%). With the big number of observations examined, between-unit differences were significant in all practices with the exception of ARI competence.

Compliance with appropriate infection control practices by healthcare unit

Table 3 shows the shift-specific compliance with appropriate IPC practices, comparing AM and PM shifts. There were no shift differences in the compliance of four practices; Both shifts had similarly high (> 95%) compliance rates in ARI competence while both shifts had similarly moderate compliance rates in proper use of PPE (> 70%), proper donning and doffing of PPE (> 70%), and disinfection of medical equipment (> 60%). Notably, the other six practices showed a marginal lead of one of the two shifts, mainly PM shift. For example, AM marginal compliance lead was observed in hand hygiene (1.2% difference, p = 0.008) while PM marginal compliance lead was observed in adherence to isolation precautions, proper disposal of sharps, proper waste management, and proper house-keeping (1% to 6% difference, p < 0.01). Supplementary Table 2 shows the differences in compliance by hospital. KASCH-R has consistently higher compliance in all monitored practices than KAMC-R.

Compliance with appropriate infection control practices by shift

Table 4 shows the differences in compliance in relation to the COVID-19 pandemics. In both HCWs-specific practices and unit-specific practices, the compliance was highest during the COVID-19 pandemic (2020–2021). Post-pandemic compliance was lower than both pandemic and pre-pandemic years. For example, hand hygiene compliance was 64.7% before the pandemic, 75.0% during the pandemic, and 46.1% after the pandemic (p < 0.001). Similarly, adherence to isolation precautions was 97.0% before the pandemic, 98.9% during the pandemic, and 73.6% after the pandemic (p < 0.001).

Discussion

This study assessed multiple IPC best practices across different emergency units at a tertiary care hospital in Riyadh. The low compliance of disinfection of medical equipment in this study (61%), has been recognized as challenge in both emergency department and other hospital locations [14, 21]. Medical equipment usually harbor infectious pathogens and the success of cleaning is difficult to assess through regular observation [22]. The reasons of low compliance include limited awareness and training about the appropriate cleaning methods and/or cleaning agent as well as unclear responsibility [6]. Enhanced cleaning and disinfection of medical equipment using a multimodal cleaning bundle focusing on cleaning time, auditing, and feedback was associated with one-third reduction of HAIs rates [14]. Additionally, implementation of device bundles for central line, ventilator, and urinary catheters were associated with better adherence and reduced infection/mortality [13]. Unlike disinfection of medical equipment, the compliance with environmental cleaning in the current study including proper housekeeping and proper waste management were very high (≥ 95%). This may be related to the difference in the behavior of responsible personnel specifically, HCWs specially nurses for medical equipment and housekeeping staff for environmental surfaces and wastes.

The current findings showed 67% compliance of hand hygiene. The true rate could be even lower as hand hygiene compliance is frequently overestimated when HCWs recognize being observed. Hand hygiene is widely recognized as one of the most effective measures of IPC programs, yet compliance rates in emergency departments are typically low, 30% to 55% [7, 16, 23]. Overcrowding and limited time allocated per patient assessment is believed to be a major drive factor for low compliance of hand hygiene [16, 17]. Multimodal interventions including education, monitoring and feedback, campaigns, and cues were shown to effectively improved hand hygiene compliance in emergency setting [12]. The low compliance with patients’ sitter policy in this study (64%) was not unexpected. It was reported that less than 20% of patients’ sitters in Saudi hospitals are aware of appropriate IPC standards they should follow [24]. Unlike the Western studies [25], the presence of patients’ sitters in the emergency department in this study is deeply influenced by socio-cultural factors [24] and my reflect the fact that many of the patients in the emergency department of our hospital stay more than one day in one or more of the emergency units [26].

As expected, nurses in the current study were much more compliant with implementing IPC practices than other professions. The results were so robust that it was replicated in the four HCWs-specific practices in all units. The higher nurse compliance is well-known in hand hygiene [27, 28] and to less extent other IPC practices [10, 29]. It has been shown that several factors can influence the compliance with IPC practices including standard precautions among nurses [10] and physicians [11]. These include personal factors such as self-efficacy, awareness and beliefs and organizational factors such as safety culture, policies, and reinforcement [10, 11]. Targeting these factors are pivotal in any program aiming to improve the compliance with IPC best practices among HCWs of different professions [12, 13].

The current findings showed more than 10% differences in the overall compliance of IPC practices between the different emergency units. The notably lower compliance observed in the rapid assessment and management unit may be related to overcrowding and rapid turnover, as almost all emergency patients were seen in this unit before assigned to other units through the triage process. It has been reported that overcrowding in the emergency department is completing with the adherence to ICP standards, specially hand hygiene [6, 16, 17]. Some units such as flu unit may have experienced higher seasonal variability in the number of admissions than other units. On the other hand, the higher compliance observed in urgent care center may be related to the lower acuity of patients served. Additionally, this unit was allowed to send patients to clinics or home if the number of patients rises beyond the capacity of the unit. Interestingly, compliance with patient sitters’ policy which was the lowest among all unit-specific practices had the highest variability between units. This may further indicate the need to review the patient sitters’ policy which is inadequately and inconsistently implemented by various units. On the other hand, the higher compliance of all monitored IPC practices in KASCH-R compared with KAMC-R may be related to the type of served patients; KASCH-R was serving more vulnerable patients (mainly pediatric patients and to less extent adult oncology/hematology patients) than KAMC-R. Additionally, KASCH-R has a new spacious building with a relatively lower crowding index than KAMC-R.

The current findings showed minor differences in compliance of IPC practices by shift. The differences ranged between 1% and 6%, with an overall marginal lead of evening/night shift by less than 1%. Although the differences were minor, six out of ten practices showed significant differences by shift, mainly due to large number of opportunities observed (~ 165 thousand). Hand hygiene in this study was slightly better in day shift while proper waste management and house-keeping was slightly better in night shift. Literature showed mixed findings about the role of shift on hand hygiene compliance and environmental cleaning [27, 30]. A review article showed that six out of ten studies that examined hand hygiene and shift showed no associations, three showed positive associations with day shift, and one showed an association with night shift [27]. The cleanliness of the emergency department was shown to be better in the day shift before but not after an improving project that involve adding an additional cleaning manpower [30].

The current findings showed higher compliance of IPC practices during the COVID-19 pandemic. This finding is not surprising as the pandemic was associated with strong emotional factors, higher awareness, better resources, organizational support, and enforced implementation that collectively resulted in improving the compliance of IPC practices [31, 32]. On the other hand, the reduced levels of compliance after COVID-19 pandemic are alarming and underscore the challenges of maintaining the pandemic-associated momentum in IPC through the post-pandemic era.

The current study has several advantages; examination of large number of opportunities, involving ten IPC practices across six emergency units of different patient and acuity levels. This allowed to stratify the data per profession, unit, and shift. Nevertheless, few limitations should be acknowledged. The single center experience may limit the generalization of the findings. The cross-sectional design does not proved causation. The observational nature of data collection and the convenient sampling may have resulted in some degree of bias, likely overestimation of compliance by the Hawthorne effect [33]. However, the possibility of bias was reduced by shortening the observation sessions and collecting data during morning and afternoon shifts in both weekdays and weekends. There was considerable variability in the number of observations monitored across IPC practices. However, this unlikely to affect the validity of the study findings as the smallest number of observations monitored for a single IPC practice was close to 2000, which is practically several times higher than the required sample size. The large sample size resulted in higher probability of statistical significance. Therefore, both values/differences and significance were used in the interpretation.

Conclusions

we are reporting 69% compliance in HCW-specific practices and 92% compliance unit-specific practices across different emergency units at a large tertiary care hospital. Nurses across all units had much better compliance than other professions. There were > 10% differences in the compliance across the units, with higher compliance in mainly pediatric compared with adult units. The differences in the compliance across the units may be related to crowding level, acuity levels, and seasonal variability in the number of admissions. The compliance of IPC practices was highest during the COVID-19 pandemic but was not maintained thereafter. The findings underscore the importance of intervention program that focus on improving disinfection of medical equipment, hand hygiene, and adherence of patient sitters.
