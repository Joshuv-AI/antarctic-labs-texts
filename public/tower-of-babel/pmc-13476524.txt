DNAJC12 p.Asp44Gly associated with mild hyperphenylalaninemia and migraine-like headaches: Structural and deep learning analyses
Authors: Jie Zhang, Binghe Xiao, Zhiliang Wang, Xiang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveDNAJC12 encodes a J-domain co-chaperone involved in the function of aromatic amino acid hydroxylases, and its deficiency is associated with hyperphenylalaninemia (HPA) and monoamine neurotransmitter deficiency. We assessed a DNAJC12 c.131A > G (p.Asp44Gly, D44G) variant identified in a 19-year-old man who presented with mild HPA, recurrent migraine-like headaches, and phenylketonuria-like white matter changes, along with four reported clinically relevant missense variants (A62E, R72P, H102Q, and W103C), using an integrated deep learning (DL)-based approach.MethodsDL-based and conventional prediction algorithms were integrated to evaluate the clinical and genetic findings, together with protein structural and pathogenic impact. Analyses incorporated AlphaFold2, AlphaMissense, REVEL, PolyPhen-2, and ThermoMPNN. Principal component analysis (PCA) was used to characterize overall, position-level, and substitution-level pathogenicity metrics. Pathogenicity classification followed American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics and ClinGen Sequence Variant Interpretation standards.ResultsThe J-domain was confidently modeled, and Asp44 was located within the conserved His-Pro-Asp motif. DNAJC12 missense variants may affect predicted side-chain interactions and residue-contact patterns with thermodynamic changes. PCA of aggregated pathogenicity metrics indicated that the predicted deleteriousness of p.Asp44Gly was consistent with stronger position-specific constraint. D44G was classified as a variant of uncertain significance (PM1, PM2_Supporting, and PP3_Supporting).ConclusionOur findings provide additional clinical and genetic evidence relevant to DNAJC12-related disease and support a potential functional effect of D44G, but do not establish its pathogenicity yet. DNAJC12 should be included in the genetic evaluation of patients with unexplained HPA with neurological manifestations, and D44G warrants further functional validation.

Introduction

Hyperphenylalaninemia (HPA) is a common biochemical phenotype of inherited amino acid metabolism disorders, with broad clinical manifestations ranging from asymptomatic presentation to severe neurological disease [1], [2]. Pathogenic variants in phenylalanine hydroxylase (PAH) or in genes involved in tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4) metabolism are major causes of HPA [3]. In recent years, pathogenic DNAJC12 variants have been recognized as a rare cause of HPA in patients without detectable defects in PAH or BH4-pathway genes [2].

DNAJC12, a member of the HSP40/DnaJ family, is abundantly expressed in the brain and functions as a co-factor for the heat shock 70 kDa protein (Hsp70) family [4], [5]. It contains a conserved J-domain with the characteristic His-Pro-Asp (HPD) motif, which stimulates adenosine triphosphate (ATP) hydrolysis by Hsp70 to maintain cellular proteostasis and assist ATP-dependent cellular processes [6]. DNAJC12 also serves as a co-chaperone for aromatic amino acid hydroxylases (AAAHs), including PAH, tyrosine hydroxylase (TH), and tryptophan hydroxylase (TPH), and is thought to contribute to their activity and protein homeostasis [2], [7]. These enzymes mediate critical hydroxylation reactions in phenylalanine (Phe) and monoamine metabolism: PAH converts Phe to tyrosine (Tyr), TH converts Tyr to L-DOPA, and TPH converts tryptophan to 5-hydroxytryptophan; L-DOPA and 5-hydroxytryptophan are then further converted to dopamine and serotonin, respectively [7]. Accordingly, pathogenic variants in DNAJC12 cause DNAJC12 disease, a neurometabolic disorder characterized by HPA, dopamine and serotonin deficiency, consistent with impaired functional activity of AAAHs [2], [8].

The structural and functional integrity of DNAJC12 is essential for maintaining neurometabolic homeostasis. The DNAJC12 gene, located on chromosome 10q21.3, consists of five exons and can be alternatively spliced to produce two transcripts [2]. These include the experimentally validated isoform a (198 amino acids) and a predicted isoform b (107 amino acids), though the cellular expression of the latter has not been experimentally confirmed [5]. Structurally, the N-terminal half of isoform a has been characterized by NMR spectroscopy (PDB 2CTQ), revealing a 13-residue N-terminal tail, a canonical J-domain (residues 14–79), and a linker helix (residues 87–100) [9]. The J-domain of DNAJC12 adopts a well-defined structure consisting of a characteristic tetra-helical fold [10]. Disruption of the structural integrity of this domain is expected to impair DNAJC12 function, potentially by reducing protein stability or altering interactions with Hsp70 or client proteins [9]. In contrast, the C-terminal half (residues 101–198) lacks experimental resolution and currently relies on the AlphaFold model. Tai et al. have shown that this region contains a highly conserved octapeptide RKFRNYEI (residues 191–198), which is proposed to participate in TH recognition and mediate TH binding [9]. Notably, pathogenic or likely pathogenic missense variants previously reported in DNAJC12 are located within or near functionally important regions. For example, p.Ala62Glu [11] and p.Arg72Pro [12] are located in the J-domain, whereas p.His102Gln [13] and p.Trp103Cys [14] lie near the junction between the N-terminal region and the C-terminal region. This distribution suggests that amino acid substitutions in functionally relevant regions are more likely to impair DNAJC12 function and may be associated with HPA and related neurological phenotypes. Therefore, systematic pathogenicity assessment of DNAJC12 missense variants, particularly those located in key functional regions, is of considerable importance.

Genetic evaluation is essential for identifying causative variants and supporting diagnosis and treatment in Mendelian disorders [15]. However, in DNAJC12 disease, the pathogenicity of missense variants is often difficult to determine because many variants lack direct functional evidence and standard classification frameworks alone may be insufficient for confident interpretation [14]. Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning have improved protein structure modeling and the prediction of the effects of amino acid substitutions [16], [17]. These methods have become useful adjuncts for the evaluation of missense variants by providing complementary information on structural context and predicted functional effect [18].

In this study, we integrated AI-based protein structural modeling and pathogenicity prediction algorithms as complementary tools to evaluate a variant of clinical interest, p.Asp44Gly, together with four previously reported DNAJC12 missense variants (p.Ala62Glu, p.Arg72Pro, p.His102Gln, and p.Trp103Cys) within American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) framework. This integrative approach may help refine the interpretation of rare DNAJC12 missense variants in patients with neurometabolic phenotypes.

Materials and methods

Data collection and ethics statement

Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood and subjected to whole-exome sequencing (WES). The candidate variant was subsequently validated by Sanger sequencing (Supplementary Data). Four additional clinically significant missense variants in DNAJC12 were identified through a review of the published literature. Missense variants reported in the absence of phenotypic data were not included in the subsequent analyses. The wild-type DNAJC12 sequence (Q9UKB3; accessed on 5 December 2025) was obtained from UniProtKB (https://www.uniprot.org/). This sequence was then used as the template to generate the following mutant amino acid sequences: p.Asp44Gly, p.Ala62Glu, p.Arg72Pro, p.His102Gln, and p.Trp103Cys. Clinical data were collected after written informed consent was obtained from the patient and his parents, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

AlphaFold2 protein structural prediction

AlphaFold2 (AF2) was used to predict the structures of the DNAJC12 variants. Model quality was assessed using AF2 confidence metrics, including the predicted local distance difference test (pLDDT) and the predicted template modeling (pTM) score [19]. The pLDDT score ranges from 0 to 100, with higher values indicating higher confidence in the model [20]. The pTM is a global confidence metric provided by AF2 that estimates the expected TM-score between the predicted structure and the unknown true structure [21]. Models were generated using the ColabFold implementation of AF2 (v1.5.5) with default settings and MMseqs2-based multiple sequence alignment generation [22]. Separate 1–99 models were generated for wild-type, D44G, A62E, and R72P. Because H102Q and W103C are located outside residues 1–99, they were not included in the separate 1–99 model pTM comparison. In addition, for descriptive pLDDT comparison, residues 1–99 were extracted from each full-length model, including H102Q and W103C.

Protein structure alignment

Structural alignment was performed in ChimeraX (University of California, San Francisco) using the MatchMaker tool with default parameters based on the Needleman-Wunsch algorithm and BLOSUM62 substitution matrix [23], [24]. Protein structural similarity was evaluated using two complementary metrics: the TM-score (https://zhanggroup.org/TM-score/; obtained on 2 January 2026) and the root mean square deviation (RMSD). The TM-score employs a length-dependent distance parameter to normalize deviations across Cα atoms. The RMSD measures the average distance between superimposed Cα atoms in structurally equivalent regions after optimal alignment [25]. A TM-score ≤ 0.17 indicates structurally unrelated proteins, while a TM-score ≥ 0.45 suggests similar topological arrangements [26], [27].

In silico pathogenicity prediction and ACMG classification

To assess the potential pathogenicity of the DNAJC12 variants (p.Asp44Gly, p.Ala62Glu, p.Arg72Pro, p.His102Gln, and p.Trp103Cys), we utilized three computational prediction tools: AlphaMissense (AM), PolyPhen-2, and ThermoMPNN [28], [29], [30] (accessed on 20 December 2025). The mutant DNAJC12 amino acid sequences were used as input for both AM and PolyPhen-2 to retrieve pathogenicity scores. The predicted wild-type DNAJC12 protein structure file in PDB format was loaded. Using this structure, the ThermoMPNN deep neural network predicted the change in thermodynamic stability (ΔΔG°) for all possible missense mutations (n = 3762) in DNAJC12. The pathogenicity classification of p.Asp44Gly followed ACMG criteria [31], with PM2 applied at Supporting strength and PP3_Supporting assigned using the REVEL score in accordance with relevant ClinGen Sequence Variant Interpretation (SVI) recommendations (https://www.clinicalgenome.org/working-groups/sequence-variant-interpretation).

Evolutionary conservation analysis

Amino acid sequences of DNAJC12 orthologs across multiple species were retrieved from UniProtKB. Filtered sequences were aligned using CLUSTAL Omega (CLUSTAL O; v1.2.4) [32]. Evolutionary conservation scores for amino acid positions, calculated by CLUSTAL O, were extracted via Jalview for further analysis [33].

Dimension reduction analysis

We employed multidimensional scaling (MDS) to analyze global relationships based on pairwise comparisons. Pairwise comparison data were symmetrized and converted into a dissimilarity matrix, which was then mapped into a low-dimensional space for visualization and analysis [34]. The quality of the MDS mapping was evaluated using the scaled stress index [35]. Metrics exhibiting higher similarity formed spatial clusters in the low-dimensional output. We integrated the pathogenic metrics of the DNAJC12 variants by principal component analysis (PCA). PCA transformed the combined pathogenic metrics into orthogonal principal components, with the first two dimensions visualized to reveal distribution patterns. The cumulative explained variance of these dimensions was reported to quantify representation fidelity [36].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses and visualization were produced using Python version 3.10.6. Data manipulation tasks were handled with Pandas. Core numerical computations and statistical analyses were performed using SciPy and NumPy. Dimensionality reduction techniques (MDS, PCA) were implemented utilizing Scikit-learn. Visualization of results was accomplished through seaborn and Matplotlib. Continuous variable normality was assessed via the Shapiro–Wilk test. Non-normally distributed continuous variables (per Shapiro–Wilk) underwent the Mann–Whitney U test. Variable associations were evaluated using Pearson correlation (normally distributed continuous variables) or Spearman correlation (ordinal/non-normal continuous variables). The Clinical AI Research checklist was also incorporated into this study [37].

Results

Case report

A 19-year-old man, born to non-consanguineous parents, presented with a 2-year history of recurrent migraine-like headaches. The attacks occurred approximately once every 2 months and were preceded by bilateral visual aura with flashing lights, lasting approximately 30 min. This was followed by pulsating pain in the left or right temporo-occipital region, accompanied by nausea, photophobia, and phonophobia. The headache was rated 7 on a 10-point visual analog scale (VAS), lasted approximately 4–5 h, and improved with ibuprofen or sleep.

His past medical history was notable for mild motor developmental delay, easy fatigability, and poor attention during childhood. However, his academic performance and social functioning were largely preserved. Cognitive screening at presentation was within normal limits (Mini-Mental State Examination, 29/30; Montreal Cognitive Assessment, 27/30). There was no family history of neurological or metabolic disease. General physical and neurological examinations were unremarkable. Cranial nerves, sensory testing, cerebellar function, pyramidal signs, gait, and posture were all normal. Electromyography showed no evidence of peripheral neuropathy or myopathic change.

Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed diffuse, symmetric T2-weighted fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) hyperintensities in the bilateral centrum semiovale and periventricular white matter, with no obvious cortical involvement (Fig. 1A, B). The same regions were hyperintense on diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), with slightly decreased signal on the corresponding apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) maps (Fig. 1C–F). Metabolic screening by tandem mass spectrometry at age 17 years showed an elevated fasting plasma Phe level of 395.45 μmol/L (reference range, 20–120 μmol/L), a Tyr level of 33.14 μmol/L (reference range, 9.0–81.4 μmol/L), and an increased Phe-to-Tyr ratio of 11.93 (reference range, 0.28–1.80). Repeat fasting plasma testing at age 19 years, without intervening treatment, showed a persistently elevated Phe level of 280.74 μmol/L (reference range, 20–120 μmol/L), a Tyr level of 80.80 μmol/L (reference range, 20–100 μmol/L), and an increased Phe-to-Tyr ratio of 3.48 (reference range, 0.5–2.0). Urinary organic acid analyses by gas chromatography–mass spectrometry were normal on both occasions. Newborn screening records were unavailable, so it could not be determined whether HPA had been present during the neonatal period.

WES identified an apparently homozygous DNAJC12 variant, c.131 A > G (p.Asp44Gly; Fig. S1A, B). In addition, variant filtering identified no candidate rare coding variants in PAH or major BH4-related genes (Supplementary Data). The mean target-region depth was 140.84×, with 99.78% target-region coverage and 98.37% of target bases covered at ≥20×. The variant allele fraction was 100%, supporting an apparently homozygous state, and Sanger sequencing of the proband further showed only the mutant peak at this position (Fig. S1C). Moreover, this missense variant is extremely rare in the general population (allele frequency 0.000003098; gnomAD version 4.1.0). No informative allele-frequency data for this variant were available in ChinaMAP beta version (March 2020).

At the time of writing, 56 patients with DNAJC12 disease have been reported in the literature [8]. Among them, only one published patient carried p.Asp44Gly, in compound heterozygosity with c.410_413del; however, the independent role of p.Asp44Gly was not evaluated separately in that report. Five patients with DNAJC12 disease carrying pathogenic or likely pathogenic missense variants other than p.Asp44Gly have been reported, involving p.Ala62Glu, p.Arg72Pro, p.His102Gln, and p.Trp103Cys. Protein structure prediction and comparison

AF2 was used to generate full-length structural models of the wild-type protein and five clinically relevant missense variants (D44G, A62E, R72P, H102Q, and W103C; Fig. S2). Model confidence metrics are summarized in Table 1. The median pLDDT values for the full-length protein models ranged from 62.39 to 69.75, with interquartile ranges (IQRs) of 45.60–55.61. For the local regions surrounding each variant, we measured the modeling confidence of the corresponding domain containing the mutation. The median pLDDT values ranged from 91.00 to 92.56 for the J-domain variants of D44G, A62E, and R72P, with IQRs of 4.44–6.00, and from 44.59 to 46.72 for the non-J-domain variants H102Q and W103C, with IQRs of 28.30–31.34. For residues 1–99 modeled with AF2 (Fig. S2), the median pLDDT values ranged from 80.19 to 90.88, with IQRs of 6.50–13.75. The pTM values were lower for the full-length models (0.45–0.46) than for the separate 1–99 models of D44G, A62E, and R72P (0.61–0.64), indicating higher global model confidence for these shorter N-terminal constructs. These results suggest that the separate N-terminal 1–99 models provide a high confidence basis for interpreting J-domain protein structure, whereas analyses involving more C-terminal regions are limited by lower model confidence.

Predicted local structural context of DNAJC12 variants

To explore the predicted structural consequences of missense variation, we compared AF2-predicted local conformations around each substituted residue. The DNAJC12 J-domain is a known functional region [39], and three of the five variants (D44G, A62E, and R72P) are located within this high-confidence segment. Representative local structural views are shown in Fig. 2A–E. In the AF2 model, the D44G substitution removed the aspartate side chain and was predicted to alter the local residue geometry. This model showed changes in local hydrogen-bonding geometry with downstream residues, including a modest increase in predicted hydrogen-bond distance, and reduced van der Waals contacts with the upstream residue (Fig. 2A). The A62E substitution replaced hydrophobic alanine with a larger, negatively charged glutamate, which may introduce local steric crowding near K58. The negatively charged Glu62 may form an attractive electrostatic interaction with the positively charged K58, potentially promoting K58 reorientation and altering surrounding van der Waals contacts and local hydrogen-bonding networks (Fig. 2B). The H102Q and W103C substitutions lie outside the best-resolved region of the protein and therefore require more cautious interpretation. In the AF2 models, H102Q altered the predicted distance between residue 102 and T99, potentially affecting local hydrogen-bonding. Moreover, glutamine with its long amide-containing side chain may alter van der Waals interactions with the nearby V105 (Fig. 2D). W103C removed the bulky indole side chain of tryptophan and was predicted to alter local packing and hydrogen-bonding interactions, with a possible effect on helical continuity in this region (Fig. 2E). Because this region outside the J-domain has lower prediction confidence, these structural inferences should be regarded as provisional.

Global structural comparison of DNAJC12 variant models and wild type

Pairwise structural comparison showed moderate predicted similarity between full-length variant models and the wild-type model, with TM-scores ranging from 0.52 to 0.69. In contrast, the N-terminal 1–99 region showed higher structural similarity across models (TM-scores 0.87–0.93), with D44G and A62E closer to wild type (WT) than R72P (Table S2).

To further compare these models, we performed MDS based on TM-score and RMSD. In the full-length analysis, the WT and five variant models showed broad separation in both the TM-score-based and RMSD-based projections (Fig. 2F, G). Among the five variants, D44G was positioned closest to the wild-type model in the RMSD-based projection.

We then repeated the analysis using AF2 models of the 1–99 truncation, including WT, D44G, A62E, and R72P. These models also showed separation in MDS space, although the clustering pattern differed depending on whether TM-score or RMSD was used (Fig. 2H–I). Overall, these analyses suggested distinct predicted structural relationships among the variants at the full-length and N-terminal levels.

Pathogenicity assessment of DNAJC12 variants

We next assessed the predicted pathogenicity of five clinically relevant DNAJC12 variants (D44G, A62E, R72P, H102Q, and W103C) by integrating pathogenicity predictions, structural analysis, evolutionary conservation, population allele frequency, and predicted stability change (ΔΔG°) (Fig. 3A, B; Table 1). ThermoMPNN predicted variable destabilizing effects across these five missense variants, with ΔΔG° values ranging from 0.0512 to 3.4950 kcal/mol. For D44G, the BLOSUM62 score for the Asp-to-Gly substitution was −1 (Table 1). Multiple sequence alignment showed that Asp44 is highly conserved across species (Table 1; Fig. S3). In addition, AM, PolyPhen-2, ThermoMPNN, and REVEL yielded concordant predictions of a deleterious effect for this variant (Table 1). No known post-translational modification site was identified at this residue (Fig. S4).

The D44G variant is extremely rare in population databases, as noted above. It is located within the highly conserved HPD motif, a functionally critical region of J-domain proteins [6]. Based on its location in a critical functional domain without reported benign variation (PM1), extremely low population frequency (PM2_Supporting), and REVEL-based computational evidence (PP3_Supporting), D44G was classified as a VUS according to ACMG criteria and relevant ClinGen SVI recommendations (Tables S4 and S5).

Amino acid-position-level analysis of predicted pathogenicity

In line with ACMG recommendations to seek additional evidence for improving VUS classifications, we further assessed these DNAJC12 variants by examining position-specific pathogenicity profiles across the protein sequence. We calculated the mean AM, PolyPhen-2, and BLOSUM62 scores and mean ΔΔG° values for all possible missense substitutions at each residue, together with conservation and pLDDT values (Fig. S5A-K). For visualization of regional trends, these positional profiles were smoothed using a Savitzky–Golay filter [40] (Fig. S5F-K). Positions 44 and 62 exhibited similar patterns across several metrics, including relatively high AM and PolyPhen-2 scores and strong evolutionary conservation. Compared with positions 102 and 103, position 44 was distinguished by higher ΔΔG° and pLDDT values, although both metrics showed slightly lower values than at position 72. In contrast, the BLOSUM62 score was lower at position 44 than at the other four positions.

PCA based on amino acid-position-level pathogenicity metrics showed a distribution pattern broadly comparable to that of the variant-level PCA (Fig. 3B, C), with the five residue positions exhibiting trends generally consistent with their corresponding missense variants. These findings suggest that position-specific properties may contribute to the predicted deleterious profiles of DNAJC12 missense variants.

Substitution-specific patterns of predicted pathogenicity

To examine substitution-specific patterns independently of residue position, we analyzed the mean AM score across a human proteome-wide dataset of 216,175,350 missense substitutions, together with the mean AM, PolyPhen-2, and BLOSUM62 scores and mean ΔΔG° values for all possible missense substitutions in DNAJC12 (n = 3762) (Fig. 3A; Fig. S5L-N). Correlation analyses across the overall, positional, and substitution-level metrics showed generally good agreement (Fig. S5L-N). At the substitution level, the AM pattern observed in DNAJC12 was strongly concordant with the human proteome-wide pattern (Fig. S5N; Spearman's correlation coefficient = 0.88).

PCA further indicated that the D–G substitution identified in our patient occupied a relatively intermediate position, similar to H–Q and R–P, whereas W-C exhibited a more distinct pathogenic profile. The A–E substitution was positioned closer to the benign-associated region (Fig. 3D).

Taken together, these findings suggest that the pathogenic impact of missense variation in DNAJC12 is shaped by both the intrinsic properties of the amino acid substitution and the positional context of the affected residue. For D44G, the predicted deleterious effect may reflect the critical role of residue 44 rather than the specific amino acid substitution, consistent with the location of Asp44 within the functionally critical HPD motif.

Discussion

This report describes a young adult with mild HPA carrying an apparently homozygous DNAJC12 p.Asp44Gly variant. A notable feature of this case was the predominance of recurrent migraine-like headaches rather than the more typical developmental delay, dystonia, parkinsonism, or cognitive decline reported in patients with DNAJC12-related disease [8]. Headache has rarely been highlighted in this disorder, and migraine-like symptoms have been only occasionally documented [14]. Although causality cannot be established from a single case, a biological link is plausible because DNAJC12 acts as a co-chaperone for AAAHs and may influence monoamine homeostasis, a pathway long implicated in migraine biology [2], [41]. The diffuse white matter abnormalities observed in our patient may be related to chronic HPA. Similar MRI patterns have been reported in other PKU-related disorders [42], [43], in which elevated Phe is thought to impair oligodendrocyte biology and myelin formation, possibly by interfering with cholesterol and other lipid synthesis in the brain [44], [45]. However, the mechanisms underlying white matter changes in DNAJC12-related HPA remain unclear, and further study is needed to determine whether the same process accounts for the imaging findings in this disorder.

Marked clinical heterogeneity across age groups is another notable feature of reported DNAJC12 disease. Deng et al. highlighted an age-dependent phenotypic pattern, with predominantly neurodevelopmental manifestations in the first decade and a shift toward parkinsonism-dystonia in the second [2]. However, our patient presented in late adolescence without parkinsonism or major cognitive impairment, further suggesting phenotypic heterogeneity in DNAJC12-related presentations. In Deng et al., 22 of 52 published patients (42.3%) were considered neurologically asymptomatic, but this figure should be interpreted cautiously because a detailed neuropsychological assessment was not performed systematically, and subtle deficits may have been missed [2]. A recent DNAJC12 knockout mouse study showed that biallelic DNAJC12 variants can lead to markedly reduced or absent DNAJC12 protein expression, decreased PAH activity, and impaired monoamine homeostasis [46]. From a clinical standpoint, these findings support early consideration of DNAJC12-related disease and, when feasible, targeted biochemical evaluation of monoamine metabolism, including cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) monoamine neurotransmitter and metabolite profiles, such as dopamine, serotonin, HVA, 5-HIAA, and the HVA/5-HIAA ratio [12].

Recent advances in AI-assisted structural prediction have improved the interpretation of missense variants by linking sequence change to putative effects on protein structure and function [47], [48]. Here, we integrated in silico pathogenicity prediction, evolutionary conservation analysis, and AF2-based structural modeling to assess five clinically relevant DNAJC12 variants: D44G, A62E, R72P, H102Q, and W103C. These complementary approaches suggested potentially deleterious effects for these variants, although the strength and nature of evidence differed across sites. D44G, A62E, and R72P warrant particular attention, as all three localize to the J-domain, where structural interpretation is relatively high, and show broadly concordant findings across conservation analysis, in silico pathogenicity prediction, and local structural modeling. By contrast, H102Q and W103C are located in a region of lower structural confidence, so the corresponding mechanistic interpretations are necessarily more tentative.

Among the variants examined here, R72P has the strongest existing mechanistic support. Anikster et al. reported that the homozygous c.215G > C (p.Arg72Pro) variant perturbs the intradomain hydrogen-bonding network of the J-domain, leading to reduced structural stability and impaired interaction with Hsp70 [12]. Consistent with these findings, the recent inability to obtain sufficient soluble R72P protein for purification is compatible with impaired protein solubility and possible structural instability [9]. In contrast, A62E remains mechanistically underexplored [11]. Nevertheless, both A62E and R72P are located within the J-domain and have been associated with a similar clinical pattern in published cases [8]. Our structural modeling suggests that A62E may likewise affect the local J-domain context, potentially influencing Hsp70 co-chaperone function. This interpretation remains hypothetical and will require direct functional validation.

The His42-Pro43-Asp44 (HPD) tripeptide is a highly conserved motif located between helices II and III of the DNAJC12 J-domain. Although it is not a major determinant of J-domain folding, the HPD motif is required for J-domain-mediated stimulation of Hsp70 ATPase activity, suggesting that its strong conservation primarily reflects its essential role in interactions with Hsp70 family proteins [9], [10]. Prior studies have shown that replacement of the His residue by glutamine abolishes the DnaJ–DnaK (a member of the family of Hsp70 proteins) interaction, and that substitutions affecting Pro or Asp can similarly impair J-protein function, thereby disrupting Hsp70-dependent protein folding and transport. Given that Asp44 is part of the conserved HPD motif, the potential effect of D44G may be attributable to impaired J-domain co-chaperone function rather than to global destabilization of the DNAJC12 protein. The DNAJC12 c.131 A > G variant was previously reported in compound heterozygosity with c.410_413del, but its independent contribution remained unclear [8]. In the present case, the apparently homozygous D44G variant was classified as a VUS under the ACMG framework. Although benign missense variation in DNAJC12 appears relatively limited in ClinVar (Table S3) and missense variation is a reported disease mechanism in DNAJC12-related disease, the available evidence was insufficient to support application of PP2 [31]. Similarly, apparent homozygosity without parental confirmation or other supporting evidence is insufficient to meet a moderate pathogenic criterion (PM3, version 1.0). Therefore, the available evidence does not currently support classification of D44G as likely pathogenic. Parental confirmation of true homozygosity or functional evidence demonstrating impaired Hsp70 interaction or co-chaperone activity would provide additional evidence for reassessing its classification.

Beyond the canonical J-domain core, H102Q and W103C are located at the interface between the C-terminal linker-helix and a predicted disordered region, where structural and mechanistic interpretations remain limited. Although these residues are spatially adjacent, variants at these positions have been reported in patients with different clinical presentations, potentially related to differences in amino acid chemistry and structural roles [49]. H102Q is a relatively conservative substitution and may be less disruptive to the local structural context, potentially contributing to a milder phenotype, although this interpretation remains speculative [50]. By contrast, W103C may be more disruptive because it replaces a bulky aromatic residue with a much smaller side chain [51]. In addition, one study showed that the W103C variant fails to support PAH folding properly, supporting the pathogenicity of this DNAJC12 variant [14]. These findings suggest that the specific physicochemical contributions of individual residues may influence mutational severity and clinical trajectory [52].

This study has several limitations. First, it is based on a single case, which limits causal inference between the DNAJC12 missense variant and the headache-dominant phenotype. Second, although WES and Sanger sequencing identified the variant as apparently homozygous, parental samples were unavailable for segregation analysis, and neither a deletion in trans nor uniparental disomy could be excluded. In the absence of segregation confirmation and functional evidence, c.131 A > G (p.Asp44Gly) therefore remains classified as a VUS. Third, AF2-based structural interpretation is limited in flexible, disordered, or interaction-dependent regions and cannot directly establish mutation-associated structural or functional changes in hydrogen bonding, thermodynamic stability, protein dynamics, chaperone-mediated interactions, or cellular function. Finally, CSF monoamine studies and longitudinal neuropsychological follow-up were not available. Despite these limitations, the combined clinical, metabolic, genetic, and computational findings suggest the potential clinical relevance of the identified DNAJC12 variant in this patient, although its significance remains uncertain.

Conclusion

In summary, we reported the DNAJC12 p.Asp44Gly variant in an apparently homozygous state in a patient with mild hyperphenylalaninemia and migraine-like headaches. The variant was classified as a variant of uncertain significance (PM1, PM2_Supporting, and PP3_Supporting). Our findings highlight the importance of considering both the physicochemical properties of the amino acid substitution and the functional role of the affected residue when interpreting missense variants. Further experimental investigation is needed for exact functional impact of D44G. These findings support inclusion of DNAJC12 in the diagnostic evaluation of patients with unexplained mild HPA, particularly in the presence of subtle neurological or neuropsychiatric features or white matter abnormalities on neuroimaging.
