Hair Products Marketed to Adolescents on Social Media: A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Natalia V. Chalupczak, Matilde Iorizzo, Shari R. Lipner
Published: 2025

Introduction

Social media usage is practically ubiquitous among adolescents and hair products are frequently promoted on these platforms. However, marketing strategies or risk messaging targeted toward young audiences are understudied. Therefore, we analyzed social media content related to hair products promoted toward adolescents to help inform dermatologists in counseling patients.

Methods

Data were extracted from the first 100 publicly accessible TikTok and 50 Instagram posts using hashtags “#teenhaircare,” “#tweenhaircare,” and “#hairtok” (10/05/2025–10/15/2025). Inclusion criteria were English-language posts ≤2 years describing hair products, routines, or advice targeting adolescents or adolescent parents (aged 9–18). Posts were coded for promoter type, marketing strategy, product category, claims, hair type, and ingredients. All posts were reviewed and coded by a single investigator (N.V.C.) using a predefined coding framework. The coauthors (M.I. and S.R.L) independently reviewed all coded data for accuracy and any discrepancies were resolved by consensus.

Results

Average views per post exceeded 51 million. Influencers accounted for 44.7% (n = 67) of all creators, followed by other sources (33.3%; n = 50) and brands (19.3%; n = 29) (Table 1). Colorful packaging (50.7%; n = 76), “natural/clean” labeling (27.3%; n = 41), and use of cartoon logos (11.3%; n = 17) were the most common marketing strategies. Key claims included “glowy/shiny hair” (34%; n = 51), “damage repair” (26.7%; n = 40) and “age-appropriate” (24.7%; n = 37). Cost per promoted product was USD 7–300 (mean USD 34.52). Eighty-two percent (n = 123) of products contained fragrances (30%; n = 45), sulfates (25.3%; n = 38), silicones (13.3%; n = 20), and parabens (5.3%; n = 8). Only four posts (2.6%) were created by dermatologists. Most videos targeted teens (53.3%; n = 80), and only 6% (n = 9) of posts were geared toward afro-ethnic hair.

Discussion

We found that influencer marketing dominated adolescent hair products with almost no physician oversight. Previous studies have demonstrated that adolescents are highly susceptible to influencer messaging. For example, a survey-based study [1] of 1,612 adolescents reported that influencers had a significant impact on behavior (p < 0.05). Our findings of limited dermatologist-generated content align with previous literature. For example, in a cross-sectional analysis [2] of Instagram hashtags related to top dermatologic diagnoses, only 5% of dermatologic-focused posts were created by board-certified dermatologists.

We also found that the vast majority of products promoted to adolescents contained common allergens or irritants, which is concerning for the potential to cause irritant or allergic contact dermatitis. Related to this concern, in a systematic review [3] including 11,593 children, allergens with the highest prevalence included cocamidopropyl betaine (5.5%), fragrance (4.8%), and methylisothiazolinone (4.3%), which are found in many hair products. In addition, in a cross-sectional study [4] of 1,179 children’s cosmetic products, 53.8% contained fragrances, the most common allergen identified in our analysis.

Study limitations include restriction to English-language posts and potential algorithm bias in content sampling. The cross-sectional design does not allow for temporal analysis of marketing trends.

In sum, we found that hair products marketed to adolescents were mostly promoted by influencers with almost no dermatologist involvement. In addition, a significant proportion of promoted products contained irritants or allergens that could cause contact dermatitis. Therefore, we recommend that physicians inquire about hair-care products in adolescent patients especially when there is concern for contact dermatitis. It has become necessary for dermatologists to engage in digital education and advocate for improved regulatory oversight of influencer marketing to ensure safe and age-appropriate cosmetic practices in adolescents.

Statement of Ethics

This study used publicly accessible social media data and did not involve human subjects. Therefore, ethics approval and informed consent were not required per institutional guidelines.
