Pan-cancer analysis of single-cell profiles with polygenic signals reveals genetic influences on tumor immunity across cancers
Authors: Chunyu Deng, Ka Li, Yunlong Ma, Jingjing Li, Yu Li, Mu Su, Yijun Zhou, Yaru Zhang, Jianzhong Su, Yan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInherited genetic variation can weaken the ability of the immune system to detect and eliminate malignant cells, limiting the effectiveness of cancer immunotherapy. However, how germline polymorphisms shape the tumor immune microenvironment across cancers remains unclear. Here, we present a polygenic analysis framework that integrates single-cell RNA sequencing with GWAS summary statistics across 14 cancer types to identify genes, immune cells, and functional programs linked to cancer risk. We identify three major genetic modules associated with T cells, B cells, and myeloid cells, and show that the T-cell module is strongly linked to cytotoxic and regulatory T cells, particularly in melanoma and breast cancer. We also identify trait-related genes, including CST7, that are associated with cytokine signaling and antigen presentation. In addition, we develop a deep-learning model that predicts immunotherapy response from both tissue and blood samples, supporting the potential of integrated germline and immune features as predictive biomarkers. Together, these findings provide a framework for understanding how inherited variation shapes tumor immunity and may guide biomarker development for cancer immunotherapy. Subject terms: Cancer microenvironment, Computational models

Introduction

Germline genetic variations in immune-related genes significantly influence cancer development and immunotherapy outcomes, as evidenced by their roles in immune deficiencies and immunosenescence1,2. In recent years, substantial progress has been made in the clinical application of immune checkpoint blockade immunotherapy, yet many cancer patients fail to benefit from this approach3. Predictors of immunotherapy responsiveness have predominantly been centered on intrinsic characteristics4, such as somatic genetic alterations5, mutational load6, and microsatellite instability7. However, the role of germline genetic variation in shaping cancer immune responsiveness, as inferred from genome-wide association studies (GWAS), remains poorly characterized8,9. Emerging evidence indicates that host-intrinsic immune regulatory variation, reflected in germline immune eQTLs and polygenic architectures, modulates baseline immune-cell differentiation, activation thresholds, and cytokine responsiveness, thereby contributing to inter-individual heterogeneity in tumor immune microenvironments (TIME) and immunotherapy outcomes10,11.

Previous studies have systematically identified numerous associations between germline genetic polymorphisms and immune cell infiltration12. Bedognetti et al. demonstrated the significant influence of genetic background on the composition and functional orientation of the TIME using The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) data. They found high heritability in estimates of NK cell and T cell subset infiltration, and interferon signaling pathways10. Recent research has confirmed that eQTLs associated with TIME are concentrated in active transcriptional regions and correlate with gene expression in specific immune cell subsets, such as macrophages and dendritic cells13. The immunogenicity of tumors displays considerable heterogeneity among individuals with the same cancer type and across different cancer types14. However, it remains unclear which immune cell states, pathways, and genes are most strongly influenced by germline variation across cancers, and how this variability relates to immunotherapy.

In this study, we present a scPagwas-based polygenic analysis framework for the tumor immune microenvironment (scPagwas-based TIME pipeline), which integrates GWAS summary statistics with single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data to characterize trait-associated immune cell states. However, the original scPagwas method faces significant challenges when applied to tumor GWAS data, as it was originally designed for general genomic analyses and relies on broad KEGG pathways. These pathways are insufficient to capture the complex dynamics of the TIME and lack specificity for immune-related genetic factors. To address these limitations, we refine the original approach by integrating tumor hallmark meta-programs and immune-specific eQTL data, thereby improving the identification of genetic signals within the TIME.

Using this framework, we systematically assess trait-relevant genes, cell types, and meta-programs across diverse cancer types. We also develop a deep-learning model that utilizes cancer-related cell types and genes to evaluate how germline genetic components influence responses to immunotherapy, particularly immune checkpoint blockade. Together, these analyses provide a framework for understanding how germline genetic background shapes the TIME across cancers and may support the identification of potential targets and predictive markers for cancer immunotherapy.

Results

Overview of scPagwas-based TIME pipeline for tumors

We hypothesized that germline variants associated with cancer susceptibility influence the transcriptional activity of specific immune cell states within the TIME. To test this, we developed a scPagwas-based TIME pipeline that integrates tumor-specific GWAS data with scRNA-seq profiles to identify the immune cell types, gene programs, and genes most strongly modulated by inherited variation.

The scPagwas-based TIME pipeline takes four inputs (Fig. 1a): (i) scRNA-seq expression profiles from tumor-infiltrating immune cells, (ii) cancer-specific GWAS summary statistics FinnGen15, (iii) immune eQTL-derived SNP–gene links from OneK1K16, and (iv) predefined functional gene programs (curated pathways). Building upon the polygenic regression framework of scPagwas17, the pipeline converts the normalized gene-by-cell matrix into pathway activity scores and subsequently into genetically informed pathway activity scores, enabling quantification of GWAS-linked pathway activity at single-cell resolution. Based on these genetic pathway activity scores, the scPagwas-based TIME pipeline identifies trait-related genes (TRGs), trait-related cell types (TRCs), trait-related meta-programs (TRPs), and a trait-related score (TRS) with a significance measure for each cell. Trait-relevant cell types are those in which genetic associations with tumor GWAS variants reveal either cell-intrinsic or microenvironmental mechanisms contributing to cancer susceptibility. Importantly, the pipeline incorporates a modified procedure for deriving trait-relevant genes and meta-programs, including enhanced correlation sampling and gene-level significance evaluation, which extend beyond the original scPagwas workflow.

GWAS summary statistics for 14 cancers were obtained from FinnGen15 and GWAS Catalog databases20 (Fig. 1a (2),Table S3). To capture regulatory effects, we integrated 4,077,555 peripheral blood immune eQTLs from OneK1K16 with cancer GWAS data (Fig. 1a (3)). This strategy was motivated by evidence that cis-eQTLs improve gene-trait discovery21 and are partly conserved across immune states, including tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes22–24.

Our study utilized 113 meta-programs related to tumor25 as the core computational units for scPagwas-based TIME pipeline (Fig. 1a (4)). These tumor hallmark meta-programs, linked to immune cells, enable comprehensive analysis of transcriptional intratumor heterogeneity across large scRNA-seq tumor datasets25 (Supplementary Information). To further enhance the meta-programs, we incorporated immune cell markers, including cell-type-specific and context-dependent eQTLs, which are significantly enriched in genetic variants associated with immune diseases26.

TRGs were prioritized by correlating gene expression with genetic pathway activity scores across cell types. To reduce the influence of skewed distributions and outliers that commonly affect scRNA-seq data27 (Fig. S4), we combined repeated subsampling-based correlation analysis with non-parametric differential expression testing. Using a data-driven threshold of the top 500 TRGs, selected because performance plateaued at this range and most cancers contained more than 500 significant TRGs (Fig. S5a,b; Methods), we calculated TRS and corresponding cell-level p values using the scPagwas enrichment procedure. Significant TRCs were then identified by aggregating cell-level significance, whereas significant TRPs were determined by differential analysis of the genetic-pathway-activity-score matrix. Together, these analyses established the scPagwas-based TIME pipeline as a framework for identifying trait-relevant immune-cell interactions at single-cell resolution.

Application of the scPagwas-based TIME pipeline to diffuse large B-cell lymphoma

To evaluate whether the scPagwas-based TIME pipeline preferentially detects eQTL-driven cell states rather than nonspecific transcriptomic perturbations, we established a simulation-based benchmark using replicated raw UMI count matrices from a melanoma immune-cell atlas (Fig. 2a, b). A high-confidence eQTL gene set was defined by intersecting TRGs with significant T-cell cis-eQTL signals from OneK1K (FDR < 0.05) and CD8 + T-cell eQTL genes from eQTLGen. As controls, we selected non-eQTL genes with matched basal expression levels. Perturbations were then introduced by downregulating TRGs in cytotoxic T cells and by inducing either two-fold eQTL-gene upregulation or random-gene upregulation in macrophages (Fig. 2c). Under this framework, the pipeline detected macrophages with TRG upregulation with high sensitivity (89.8%; Fig. 2d), whereas sensitivity remained low for cytotoxic T cells with TRG downregulation (1.8%) and for macrophages with random-gene upregulation (2.8%). These results demonstrate that the pipeline is highly sensitive to cell states influenced by germline eQTL signals, while showing minimal sensitivity to unrelated or randomly perturbed transcriptional changes.

We next applied the scPagwas-based TIME pipeline to diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), an aggressive B-cell malignancy with known somatic and germline alterations affecting B-cell receptor signaling, antigen presentation, and immune regulation28,29. However, conventional enrichment tools like LD-score segmentation (LDSC-SEG) and MAGMA tend to inappropriately assign disease-associated signals to myeloid lineages, frequently ranking them above B cells30. To mitigate this issue and to focus the analysis on biologically relevant targets, we restricted downstream evaluation to B-cell populations when examining DLBCL-associated genetic signals (Supplementary Information).

We integrated DLBCL GWAS data with two independent scRNA-seq datasets to examine the ability of the scPagwas-based TIME analysis pipeline to distinguish immune cell populations and tumor-associated B-cell states. The scRNA-seq datasets included: (1) normal PBMCs (Fig. 2e; 97,039 cells from 23 individuals), and (2) B-cell-specific data, including de novo DLBCL (4 patients), follicular lymphoma (FL; 3 patients), and pediatric tonsils (1 patient) (Fig. 2h; 9915 cells). When applied to PBMC data, the pipeline achieved clear separation between B cells and other immune cell types, as quantified by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, and showed higher area under the curve (AUC) values compared with scPagwas and scDRS (AUC = 0.942 vs. 0.746 and 0.591, respectively; Fig. 2f, g). Similarly, the pipeline outperformed the comparison methods in distinguishing DLBCL tumor B cells from non-DLBCL B cells, including normal and follicular lymphoma B cells (AUC = 0.921 vs. 0.555 and 0.544; Fig. 2i, j). Together, these analyses illustrated that the scPagwas-based TIME pipeline could consistently capture trait-associated B-cell states across distinct biological contexts.

To further evaluate the scPagwas-based TIME analysis pipeline for linking polygenic signals with TIME features, we applied the same analytical framework to PBMC and DLBCL-related datasets and compared the resulting cell-type associations across methods (Fig. S5d). In PBMC data, the scPagwas-based TIME pipeline identified disease-associated B cells (P = 2.0 × 10-4) and dendritic cells (P = 1.0 × 10-4). In addition, when applied to tumor and normal B-cell populations, the pipeline successfully highlighted DLBCL tumor cells (P = 0.0451). Extending the workflow to GWAS data from B-cell lymphoma, follicular lymphoma, and lymphoid leukemia further supported its biological specificity. In follicular lymphoma, the pipeline identified significant enrichment in Pre-B cells (Figure S5f; adjusted p < 0.001), consistent with disrupted early B-cell development28,31 (Table S4). In lymphoid leukemia, associations were observed with early erythroid and myeloid progenitor cells, suggesting effects on leukemic stem cell biology and differentiation pathways32.

Pathway-level benchmarking further distinguished the scPagwas-based TIME pipeline from comparison methods. Compared with scPagwas and MAGMA, the pipeline showed stronger enrichment for immune-related pathways, including granulocyte activation and antigen presentation, whereas scPagwas emphasized more general cellular processes such as protein translation, and MAGMA did not identify significant pathway enrichment (Figs. 2k; S5e; Table S5). These results support the ability of the pipeline to connect genetic variants with immune-cell-specific processes in the tumor microenvironment.

To evaluate the stability of the scPagwas-based TIME analysis pipeline under biological noise, we performed sensitivity analyses by introducing Gaussian perturbations to both GWAS summary statistics and scRNA-seq data. Perturbation at the single-cell level had minimal impact on gene prioritization (Spearman ρ = 0.80– ≤ 0.5), with signal degradation occurring beyond this level. This pattern is consistent with the pipeline’s reliance on a relatively small subset (~5%) of SNPs linked to cis-regulated meta-program genes. Correlation remained higher under low-noise conditions because low-level noise mainly affects non-informative SNPs, whereas higher noise levels are more likely to disrupt germline signals (Fig. S5c). Together, these analyses indicated that the pipeline is stable to typical single-cell measurement noise, while remaining sensitive to biologically meaningful germline variation.

Immune-genetic modules across cancers

To elucidate the immune features associated with germline genetics, we summarized key results obtained using the scPagwas-based TIME analysis pipeline across 14 cancer types. This included significant TRCs (Fig. 3a; Table S6) and the top five TRPs (Table S7). Our results indicated that different cancers exhibited varying impacts on immune cells, underscoring the genetic diversity in tumor development at the cellular level. Furthermore, the top five TRPs for each tumor type predominantly consisted of immune-specific meta-programs, suggesting that host genetics primarily influence immune cells through immune-related pathways33,34. These findings underscored the complexity of tumor biology and the necessity of considering immune cell diversity when exploring genetic links in cancer.

We defined trait-related cell populations (TRCs) as those whose transcriptional profiles are significantly enriched with cancer-related polygenic signals, representing a genetic-functional link with the TIME. To contextualize the TRC results within the broader methodological landscape, we performed a comparative analysis using methods such as scPagwas17, Fusion35, Rolypoly36, scDRS37, and scLinker38 across all tumors. Under identical statistical thresholds, several alternative approaches yielded sparse or no significant immune associations in multiple cancer types, whereas the scPagwas-based TIME pipeline consistently identified trait-associated immune cell populations (Fig. S6; S7a; Supplementary Data 1). Additionally, extensive GWAS data for breast and lung cancers derived from different samples showed significant positive correlations with our initial findings (Fig. 3b). Further validation of the method was carried out by applying the scPagwas-based TIME pipeline to two independent liver cancer scRNA-seq datasets: GSE140228 (CD45⁺ immune cells profiled from multiple anatomical sites)39 and GSE151530 (pre-/post-treatment tumor biopsies)40. Both datasets showed that the pipeline consistently identified myeloid-associated cell populations as the trait-relevant cell type, demonstrating the robustness and reproducibility of our method across different datasets (Fig. S7b).

In this study, we identified three cancer-related modules with specific genetic correlations to immune cell types (Fig. 3c). Module 1, encompassing thyroid, lung, melanoma, breast, gastric, and endometrial cancers, was significantly correlated with T lymphocyte-related cell types. Module 2 included pancreatic, colorectal, and esophageal cancers, which showed a notable genetic correlation with B and plasma cells. Module 3 comprised head and neck, prostate, kidney, ovarian, and liver cancers, demonstrating significant genetic correlation with myeloid lineage-related cell types, including dendritic cells, macrophages/monocytes, and mast cells. These modules are designated as TRC-T, TRC-B, and TRC-Myeloid, respectively.

These TRC modules reflect germline-linked regulatory influences on immune pathways rather than the immune cell composition captured in scRNA-seq datasets. Because the pipeline infers trait-cell relationships through polygenic effects on pathway-level activity, the resulting modules capture inherited immune regulatory tendencies specific to each cancer type. For instance, although macrophages represent a larger fraction of infiltrating immune cells in breast cancer datasets, breast cancer consistently maps to the TRC-T module (Fig. 3a; S3a), highlighting that its genetic architecture aligns more strongly with T-cell-related pathways than with cell-type abundance alone.

This systematic classification elucidates the genetic mechanisms underlying distinct cancer types. For instance, our results revealed a genetic correlation between lung cancer and T cells, consistent with studies showing that T-cell-related genetic polymorphisms can predict treatment efficacy in early-stage non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients41. We also found a strong genetic link between pancreatic cancer and B/plasma cells, aligning with research suggesting that elevated B cell levels are associated with a favorable prognosis and may influence immunotherapy outcomes for pancreatic cancer42. Additionally, our study identified a significant genetic association between liver cancer and macrophages, corroborating previous findings that tumor-associated macrophages play a crucial role in supporting liver cancer stem cells, particularly in early-stage tumors43. The classification of tumor types based on TRC elucidated the heterogeneity of tumors and their immune system interactions, offering additional insights into the genetic landscape and immunological characteristics of various cancer types.

Characterization of TRGs and TRPs across cancer modules

To compare TRGs across cancer types, we ranked genes using weighted Pearson correlation coefficients that incorporated both genetic coefficients and gene expression levels (Fig. 4a; Supplementary Data 2; Methods). Clustering of the resulting similarity matrix identified substantial overlap in TRGs across cancer modules (Fig. 4b). The TRC-T module showed conserved gene expression patterns, particularly in melanoma and breast cancer. The TRC-B module highlighted shared B/plasma-cell profiles in pancreatic and colorectal cancers, whereas the TRC-Myeloid module showed broader similarity across multiple cancers.

Enrichment analysis revealed distinct functional features across the three modules (Fig. 4c; Supplementary Data 3). In the TRC-T module, TRGs were significantly enriched in antigen presentation and immune recognition pathways, including interleukin-2 production and MHC protein binding44,45. Gene Ontology (GO) and Reactome analyses further supported enrichment in lymphocyte-related and T-cell receptor pathways (Fig. S8; Supplementary Data 4). In contrast, the TRC-B module was enriched in broader cellular-function pathways, particularly in pancreatic and colorectal cancers. In the TRC-Myeloid module, liver and kidney cancers showed enrichment in antimicrobial defense pathways, while chemokine production and macrophage activation were enriched across all TRC-Myeloid cancers. Survival analysis showed significant associations with prognosis in most TRC-T tumors (including melanoma, lung, and breast cancers) and TRC-Myeloid tumors (including kidney, liver, and head and neck cancers), but not in TRC-B cancers (Fig. 4d).

To characterize trait-related meta-programs (TRPs) across cancers, we analyzed the top five TRPs associated with the genetic predisposition of each cancer type (Table S7). Most cancers showed distinct TRP enrichment patterns (Figs. 5a, S9). Within the TRC-T module, melanoma and breast cancer shared several top-ranked TRPs, including CD4-Cytotoxic and CD8-Cytotoxic (Fig. 5b (1), S9). These shared TRPs were strongly associated with prognosis in melanoma and showed more limited prognostic associations in breast cancer. At the same time, cancers within the same module still retained distinct sets of genetically associated TRPs (Fig. 5b). In the TRC-B module, MP21-Respiration emerged as a significant genetic contributor in esophageal and colorectal cancers, whereas MP3-Cell Cycle (HMG-rich) did not (Fig. 5b (2)). This pattern was consistent with previous studies suggesting that chronic inflammatory responses to tissue damage can generate cytokine-rich microenvironments that promote sustained proliferation and tumor development in colorectal cancer, with similar mechanisms potentially relevant to esophageal cancer46.

In the TRC-Myeloid module, MP3-Cell cycle (HMG-rich) was the predominant TRP in kidney cancer and was significantly associated with prognosis (Fig. 5b (3)). We next examined whether these module-level genetic patterns were reflected in bulk immune features from TCGA. Leukocyte Fraction differed significantly across modules, with the TRC-T module showing the highest levels, followed by TRC-B and TRC-Myeloid (Fig. 5c). In parallel, cancers in the TRC-T module showed significantly higher objective response rates (ORR) to immunotherapy than cancers in the TRC-Myeloid module (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Data 6). Because tumor mutational burden (TMB) is an established biomarker of immunotherapy response6,47, we further compared TMB across modules and found that TRC-T cancers, particularly melanoma and lung cancer, had the highest TMB levels (Fig. 5e). TRC-T cancers also showed elevated immune escape dN/dS score relative to other cancers (Fig. 5f)48 Together, these results indicated that cancers in the TRC-T module were characterized by stronger immunotherapy-associated features than cancers in the other modules.

Experimental validation of cross-cancer TRGs in the TRC-T module

To experimentally evaluate the functional relevance of TRGs identified across TRC-T modules, we selected candidate genes showing consistent genetic associations in melanoma, breast cancer, and other TRC-T-associated malignancies (Supplementary Data 7; Methods). Although comprehensive experimental validation of all SNP–gene interactions remains impractical, we prioritized five genes—CST7, IL32, NDUFB1, CCL4, and ALOX5AP—on the basis of their strong genetic associations and predicted involvement in T-cell activation. These genes were identified through a systematic filtering strategy integrating T-cell cis-eQTL and GWAS data. Consistent with this prioritization framework, TRC-T-derived TRGs were enriched for immune eQTL-associated genes reported by Pagadala et al. and Sears et al.49 (Fisher’s exact test, P = 1.4 × 10−4; Fig. S11), supporting the biological coherence of the selected candidates. As shown in Fig. 6a, top-ranked genes typically harbor multiple significant cis-eQTLs and are strongly implicated in cancer-related biology. Notably, SNPs in CST7 (for example, rs11550623) show robust colocalization between breast cancer risk loci and CD8 + T-cell eQTLs (Fig. 6b). We further performed literature mining to identify genes with high computational relevance (≥0.20) but relatively limited prior investigation (<1000 citations), thereby focusing on promising yet underexplored candidates (Fig. 6c). Experimental experts excluded GZMA and CD8A from validation because these genes are already well-established T-cell markers with extensive functional evidence.

To assess whether these genes contribute to activated T-cell responses, we performed short hairpin RNA (shRNA)-mediated knockdown experiments in stimulated T cells. Individual gene knockdown reduced expression of the corresponding targets (Fig. 7d), and pooled multi-shRNA transduction also decreased expression of the indicated genes (Fig. 7e). Multi-gene perturbation was associated with reduced T-cell proliferation (Fig. 7f) and lower expression and secretion of IFNG/TNFA and IFN-γ/TNF-α, respectively (Fig. 7g, h). In addition, CST7 knockdown alone was accompanied by reduced cytokine output, supporting a potential role for this gene in modulating T-cell effector function. Together, these experiments provide orthogonal support for the functional relevance of prioritized TRGs in activated T cells.

Collectively, our top-ranked TRGs showed strong cis-regulatory signals in T cells, colocalization with cancer GWAS loci, and experimental associations with T-cell activation-related phenotypes. These findings supported the biological plausibility of the prioritized TRGs and suggested that they may contribute to shaping tumor–immune interactions across cancers.

Melanoma heritability estimates and immunotherapy response prediction

Melanoma is a prototypical cancer type in the TRC-T module and exhibits a statistically significant survival advantage linked to TRG expression (Fig. 4d). To further characterize its immune-genetic features, we systematically analyzed the key trait-related cell types (TRCs), trait-related genes (TRGs), and trait-related meta-programs (TRPs). Among cells within the top 5% of the genetic pathway activity score distribution, cytotoxic T-cell populations were strongly enriched, particularly Tem/Trm.cyt (38.4%) and Tem/Temra.cyt (23.6%) (Fig. 8a). Notably, previous studies have identified Tem/Temra.cyt as a strong indicator of immunotherapy response50. Consistent with this cytotoxic phenotype, TRG analysis showed that the top 10 TRGs, especially Cystatin 7 (CST7), displayed strong positive correlations with the genetic pathway activity score (Fig. 8b; Pearson correlation coefficient > 0.1). CST7 has been reported as a marker of acute inflammation in humans51. Moreover, all top 10 TRGs were significantly associated with survival (Fig. 8c; p < 0.001). TRP analysis showed that cytotoxic T-cell–related programs, particularly CD4_Cytotoxic and CD8_Cytotoxic, had the strongest genetic contributions in melanoma, and Tem/Trm.cyt accounted for the largest proportion of MP-CD4/CD8_Cytotoxic cells (Fig. 8d, e). Together, these findings indicated that cytotoxic functional programs are under specific genetic influence in cytotoxic lymphocyte populations, consistent with their central role in recognizing and eliminating tumor cells.

To further investigate the genetic mechanisms underlying melanoma, we examined whether melanoma-associated TRGs were linked to TIME-related eQTL SNPs reported in public resources12 (Table S9). We identified five TIME-related SNPs associated with melanoma-relevant TRGs. Among these, rs679147 and rs596002 were linked to CTSW, highlighting a potentially important role for this locus in cancer immunogenetics52 (Fig S12a). Another variant, rs1736919, was associated with HLA-F, a member of the HLA gene family that contributes to major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I) molecules involved in the initiation of immune responses. Although the precise function of HLA-F remains incompletely understood, it has been proposed to participate in immune tolerance and immune evasion during fetal development53,54. In addition, rs9357006 and rs9379860 were linked to BTN3A. Previous studies have shown that dysfunctional BTN3A, together with dysregulated immune checkpoints and type I/II interferon signaling, contributes to defective crosstalk between plasmacytoid dendritic cells and γδ T cells in melanoma, thereby affecting clinical outcome55. These observations suggested that germline variants linked to TRGs may participate in key biological processes governing tumor–immune interactions in melanoma.

To explore the translational potential of TRGs in immunotherapy, we next used germline genetic features to construct a predictive model based on scRNA-seq data obtained from pretreatment melanoma tumors (Table S10). Because TRG effects are likely to involve complex non-linear interactions, we implemented a multilayer perceptron (MLP) architecture, which has shown strong performance in previous single-cell prediction tasks involving multi-gene regulatory relationships56,57. This MLP-based framework enabled the model to capture high-dimensional interaction patterns among TRGs and to predict immunotherapy response. Specifically, we focused on Tem/Trm.cyt and Tem/Temra.cyt populations (Fig. 8f). The model was trained using 508 TRGs as input features and 3,838 TRCs derived from pretreatment tumor samples, with cells grouped by patient and split into non-overlapping training and test sets.

The melanoma TRG-based prediction model achieved an AUC of 0.92, indicating strong predictive performance and exceeding that of MAGMA (AUC = 0.75) and FUSION (AUC = 0.75), two commonly used methods for identifying genetically associated genes (Fig. 8g). Among all features, HLA-J emerged as the most important TRG (Fig. 8h). HLA molecules play a central role in immunotherapy by presenting peptide antigens that trigger immune responses against tumor cells53, and they have also been recognized as prognostic markers of treatment response and survival58. Thus, the prominence of HLA-J in our model was consistent with the established role of HLA molecules in shaping immunotherapy response41. Pretreatment blood samples from patients with melanoma were also used to create a model for predicting response to immunotherapy (Fig. 8i). Our findings showed that even when using blood samples, the model could effectively predict immunotherapy response based on TRGs (Fig. 8j).

Breast cancer heritability estimates and immunotherapy response prediction

Breast cancer was another major cancer type within the TRC-T module and clustered closely with melanoma (Fig. 4b). Although TRGs in breast cancer were associated with improved survival (Fig. 4d), breast cancer showed a weaker response to immunotherapy than melanoma (Fig. 9f). Among cells within the top 5% of the genetic pathway activity score distribution, breast cancer was enriched for Tem/Trm.cyt, NK, and Treg cells (Fig. 9a). Among TRGs, CST7 showed the strongest genetic correlation and was significantly associated with survival benefit (Fig. 9b, c). CST7 has also been implicated in immune regulation and breast cancer progression59.

TRP analysis identified MP-CD4_T_reg as the dominant genetically associated meta-program in breast cancer (Fig. 9d). Treg cells showed the highest abundance in MP-CD4_T_reg, whereas Tem/Trm.cyt cells were the most abundant in MP-CD4-Cytotoxic and were also highly abundant in MP-CD4_T_reg (Fig. 9e). Single-cell tumor data further showed a higher proportion of Treg cells in breast cancer than in melanoma, whereas the proportion of Tem/Trm.cyt cells varied less between the two cancers (Fig. 9g). In addition, breast cancer showed a significant increase in genetically significant Treg cells (Fig. 9h) and reduced white blood cell infiltration in TCGA data relative to melanoma (Fig. 9i). Thus, despite sharing the TRC-T classification, breast cancer exhibited significantly lower objective response rates (p < 0.05) together with stronger Treg dominance, consistent with an immunosuppressive microenvironment linked to poor immunotherapy response60.

Analysis of TIME-related eQTL SNPs in breast cancer identified 14 SNP–TRG associations (Fig S12b). These included rs67689854 in RPL13 and rs12576996/rs3903072 in CFL1. Previous studies have linked disrupted RPL13 expression and translation to breast cancer metastasis and identified CFL1 as an important factor in cancer invasion61,62. Several of these SNPs have also been functionally connected to melanoma13 (Table S11), and the CTSW-associated variants rs679147 and rs596002 were shared between the two cancers, suggesting convergent immune-genetic regulation. Breast cancer eQTL-associated TRGs also prominently included HLA-B and HLA-C, whose allelic variation may influence antigen presentation and immunotherapy response41,63,64.

To evaluate the predictive value of breast cancer TRGs for immunotherapy response, we developed a deep-learning model using 477 TRGs and 16,407 Tem/Trm.cyt cells, following the same strategy used for melanoma (Fig. 9j). The model achieved an AUC of 0.99 (Table S12), outperforming two comparison methods (Fig. 9k; MAGMA = 0.86; FUSION = 0.88). Among all features, HLA-B emerged as a particularly important TRG (Fig. 9l; Supplementary Data 8), further supporting the relevance of HLA class I variation in immunotherapy response41. We then applied the model to pretreatment blood-derived scRNA-seq data from patients with breast cancer (Fig. 9m). Even in this setting, tumor-derived TRGs retained predictive utility for immunotherapy response (Fig. 9n; AUC = 0.94). Overall, TRG-based features derived from the scPagwas-based TIME pipeline showed strong predictive value in both tissue and blood samples.

Given the availability of subtype-specific GWAS data for breast cancer (FinnGen; HER2-negative: n = 15,663; ER-negative: n = 3092; ER-positive: n = 4263), we next examined subtype heterogeneity by integrating these GWAS data with scRNA-seq profiles from GSE161529 (421,761 cells). Distinct immune-genetic patterns were observed across ER + , ER−, and HER2− tumors (Fig. S13; Table S13). In ER+ tumors, NK cells were the dominant trait-associated population (FDR = 6.12 × 10⁻⁵), with NKG7 as the top TRG (Fig. S13a,b), consistent with their cytotoxic immune surveillance role65. In contrast, ER− tumors showed broad immune-genetic associations across nearly all cell types, consistent with a broadly dysregulated inflammatory microenvironment66. HER2− tumors showed significant enrichment of mast cells (FDR = 3.09 × 10⁻⁹), potentially reflecting VEGF-related angiogenic activity67.

Notably, Treg cells were genetically significant across all three subtypes (ER+: FDR = 0.003; ER−: FDR = 0.002; HER2−: FDR = 0.002). In ER+ tumors, this pattern may be related to suppression of NK-cell activity68, whereas in TNBC, Treg infiltration has been associated with checkpoint inhibitor resistance69. Together, these results indicated that elevated Treg-associated genetic signals are shared across breast cancer subtypes, while the surrounding immune architecture differs by subtype, supporting the need for subtype-specific immunotherapy strategies70.

Discussion

Using a scPagwas-based TIME pipeline, we integrated eQTLs, GWAS, scRNA-seq tumor data, and tumor immune-related meta-programs to identify trait-relevant cells (TRCs), genes (TRGs), and programs (TRPs). This framework captured tumor heterogeneity across 14 cancer types and grouped them into three immune-genetic modules: TRC-T, TRC-B, and TRC-Myeloid. We further examined shared and distinct immune-genetic features between breast cancer and melanoma and used TRCs and TRGs to build a deep-learning model for predicting immunotherapy response from tissue and blood samples.

Although cancers could be classified into three modules, tumors within the same module showed distinct meta-program contributions, highlighting differences in immune-genetic architecture across tumor origins71. For example, within TRC-T, the dominant TRP in lung cancer was CD4-Naive, whereas melanoma and breast cancer were characterized by CD4/CD8-Cytotoxic and CD4-Treg programs, respectively. These meta-programs were also associated with favorable prognosis, supporting important roles for immune-related programs and their linked polymorphisms in tumor progression and treatment response70,72,73. Consistently, TRC-T tumors showed higher objective response rates, and their TRGs were mainly involved in cytokine production, antigen presentation, and immune recognition, processes central to T-cell-mediated antitumor immunity74,75.

The scPagwas-based TIME pipeline reduces millions of variants to a ranked set of high-confidence TRGs, enabling focused experimental validation. Although comprehensive validation of all polygenic predictions remains impractical, integration of eQTL, GWAS, and immune-function information supports the pipeline’s ability to identify functionally relevant immune-related targets. Validation of CST7, IL32, NDUFB1, CCL4, and ALOX5AP further supports its utility in identifying genes involved in T-cell function in TRC-T cancers.

While colocalization is widely used to prioritize causal variants, it may be less sensitive for complex polygenic traits. Some validated TRGs identified by our pipeline, such as CCL4, showed significant cumulative pathway-level effects but weak colocalization with GWAS signals at individual loci (Fig. S17). This discrepancy likely reflects the diffuse nature of polygenic immune regulation in cancer, which locus-based approaches may miss. By aggregating subtle effects across pathways and meta-programs, the pipeline captures biologically relevant but genetically distributed immune regulators.

Autoimmune GWAS provide a potentially informative resource because they capture germline variants involved in T-cell activation, cytokine signaling, and interferon pathways linked to tumor immunity. Prior studies have shown that autoimmune risk variants overlap with SNPs influencing intratumoral interferon signaling76 and that large-scale autoimmune GWAS have guided the discovery of immune checkpoints later advanced into clinical testing77,78. However, our Mendelian randomization analysis detected no significant causal effect of autoimmune genetic liability on cancer risk across 14 tumor types (Fig. S14), suggesting that autoimmune GWAS do not add substantial cancer-specific germline information beyond cancer-derived GWAS used in our pipeline. These results support prioritizing cancer GWAS for etiologic specificity, while supporting autoimmune traits as a complementary resource for future cross-trait immunogenetic studies.

Analysis of melanoma and breast cancer within the TRC-T module revealed shared TRGs and TRPs across both cancer types. We also identified the common TIME-associated gene CTSW and the functional SNPs rs679147 and rs596002 in both cancers. This finding is consistent with epidemiological evidence showing frequent co-occurrence of melanoma and breast cancer79,80. These cancers have been described as second primary cancers, and UK Biobank data support an increased overall breast cancer risk in patients with melanoma80. Subtype stratification further helps clarify immune evasion mechanisms and may inform personalized immunotherapy. In triple-negative breast cancer, Treg-associated signatures may paradoxically coexist with increased immunotherapy responsiveness, possibly because of compensatory activation of cytotoxic immune cells81–85.

Despite these shared features, melanoma and breast cancer showed distinct immune-genetic profiles. In melanoma, MP-CD4_Cytotoxicity was mainly associated with Tem/Trm.cyt and Tem/Temra.cyt, whereas breast cancer showed stronger genetic associations with Treg cells and MP-CD4-Treg. Breast cancer also exhibited lower immunotherapy responsiveness than melanoma (Fig. 9f), consistent with prior studies linking breast cancer to Treg-dominated immunosuppression and melanoma to cytotoxic lymphocyte activity and better response to immune checkpoint blockade86,87. Treg cells are key immunosuppressive components of the TIME88,89, and our subtype analyses further support their consistent genetic association across breast cancer subtypes. At the same time, breast cancer included HLA class genes involved in antigen processing, suggesting that altered antigen presentation may also contribute to reduced immune checkpoint blockade efficacy86,87.

We further developed an MLP deep-learning model to predict immunotherapy response using TRCs and TRGs from melanoma and breast cancer. HLA class genes, particularly HLA-B in breast cancer, may contribute to differential response by affecting immune recognition of tumor cells, consistent with prior evidence that HLA variation influences the efficacy of HLA-dependent immunotherapy90,91. Cross-validation using TRGs from both cancers showed strong predictive performance (Fig. S15). The model also retained predictive utility in additional cancer types and in blood-derived data (Fig. S16), supporting the broader use of genetically informed cell states and genes for immunotherapy prediction.

Several limitations should be considered. First, our analyses relied mainly on European-ancestry GWAS and eQTL resources, and most public scRNA-seq datasets lacked ancestry annotations. As a result, ancestry mismatch cannot be excluded and may limit cross-population generalizability. Second, bulk-level GWAS and eQTL analyses inherently average across heterogeneous tumor populations, obscuring subtype- or clone-specific signals. Third, while the scPagwas-based TIME analysis pipeline efficiently prioritizes candidate immune-interactive genes at scale, comprehensive experimental validation remains beyond the scope of this study. Future work should integrate single-cell and spatial transcriptomic data, as well as large-scale CRISPR perturbation assays, to systematically bridge computational prediction with functional validation.

Methods

scRNA-seq data collection

A total of 35 scRNA-seq published datasets19,92–96, which included 712 samples from GEO and ArrayExpress (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/biostudies/arrayexpress). We initially compiled a list of potentially relevant studies by conducting a PubMed search, and subsequently maintained its relevance through an ongoing literature review (Table S1). Datasets were required to provide count-based expression matrices and at least 15,000 genes to ensure integration robustness. The final cohort spanned 14 cancer types (e.g., head/neck, lung, gastric) and included six pre-immune checkpoint blockade therapy datasets97–102 covering tumor/blood cells (Table S10).

Preprocess scRNA-seq data for each cancer

To maximize gene preservation, we performed cancer-specific rather than pan-cancer integration of scRNA-seq datasets. All preprocessing, integration, and downstream analyses were performed separately for each cancer type. scRNA-seq datasets belonging to different cancers were never merged into a single object, ensuring that immune-cell variation and batch correction were handled strictly within each cancer. Each tumor cohort was preprocessed following best practices (sc-best-practices.org), including Scanpy-based103 data merging with zero-imputation for missing genes. After PCA-based batch effect assessment, we applied BBKNN correction when needed. Quality control involved filtering low-quality cells via median absolute deviations thresholds104: 5 median absolute deviations for total counts/genes and 3 median absolute deviations for mitochondrial content (>8% excluded). Data normalization employed Scran’s pooled size factors with log-transformed CPM. Subsequent dimensionality reduction used the top 30 BBKNN-corrected principal components for kNN-graph construction (k = 15), Louvain clustering (resolution=1), and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) visualization.

Cell-type annotation was performed using CellTypist18 for each cancer type. CellTypist employs logistic regression classifiers optimized via stochastic gradient descent. Following normalization (10,000 counts/cell) and log1p transformation, automated annotation utilized the Pan_Fetal_Human reference model, with each cluster assigned its predominant cell type based on prediction scores. Non-immune clusters (epithelial/endothelial/fibroblast) were systematically excluded, prompting iterative dimensionality reduction and re-annotation. Verification involved UMAP cluster distinctness assessment and manual validation through Wilcoxon rank-sum testing of differentially expressed genes (rank_genes_groups function), cross-referenced against established immune markers from literature19,95,105 to ensure annotation fidelity prior to downstream analysis.

Data Integration and Quality Control

To ensure high-quality and biologically relevant data integration, we implemented the following quality control measures:

We selected primary, untreated tumor samples for scRNA-seq, ensuring the data originated from heterogeneous but biologically relevant tumor microenvironments. Datasets with fewer than 15,000 genes were excluded to retain the maximum number of relevant features. Additionally, datasets from scRNA-seq immune subsets were excluded to focus on tumor-associated cells, in line with our goal of studying the broader tumor microenvironment. To mitigate batch effects, we applied the BBKNN method, which is particularly suited for datasets with moderate sample sizes, as in our study. Although deep learning-based batch correction methods are effective at larger scales, they often reduce the number of retained genes, which is undesirable for our multi-gene regression analysis. The BBKNN method preserved all relevant features while effectively addressing batch-related biases. We validated the effectiveness of batch effect removal by calculating the Silhouette score, which measures clustering consistency. A Silhouette score below 0.5 indicates that data points are clustered according to biological features rather than batch labels. In our analysis, the Silhouette scores for all cancer datasets were close to 0, confirming that batch effects were effectively removed and that the data integration was biologically driven.

GWAS summary statistics

The GWAS summary statistics for the investigated solid tumors (Table S3) were obtained from FinnGen (https://www.finngen.fi/en) and GWAS Catalog (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas/). In cases where multiple datasets were available for a specific cancer phenotype, we gave precedence to the dataset with the largest sample size. When the differences in sample size were negligible, we chose the most recently published data. For the subtypes in FinnGen, broader cancer classifications were favored. Significantly, although GWAS data reflect population-level genetic characteristics, scRNA-seq data are derived from individual patient cohorts. Considering the shared disease contexts and population backgrounds, we applied the GWAS findings to scRNA-seq data on the basis of their broad applicability.

Meta-programs of cancer hallmarks and immune cells

We selected a set of 113 meta-programs representing cancer hallmarks and immune-cell states. This gene list of meta-programs was derived from the research conducted by Tirosh et al.25 and Ota et al.26 The meta-program gene set used in this study was derived from two major categories of meta-programs: 41 consensus meta-programs identified across multiple tumor types, representing both general cellular processes (for example, cell cycle and stress response) and lineage-associated programs, which we mapped to the 11 hallmarks of transcriptional intratumor heterogeneity, as well as functionally annotated meta-programs from non-malignant TIME cell types, including CD8 T cells (12 meta-programs), CD4 T cells (10 meta-programs), macrophages (13 meta-programs), and B cells (12 meta-programs). To further expand immune-cell representation, we also incorporated the gene set reported by Ota et al., which included 28 distinct immune cell subsets derived from 337 patients with ten categories of immune-mediated diseases and 79 healthy volunteers.

Immune cell eQTL data and construction of a high-confidence T-cell cis-eQTL set

Immune cell expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) data were obtained from the OneK1K database (hg38; https://onek1k.org/), which comprises scRNA-seq profiles of 1.27 million peripheral blood mononuclear cells from 982 donors. For the scPagwas-based TIME pipeline, immune-related eQTLs were first filtered at P < 0.05. Variants retained for downstream analysis were further restricted to SNPs present in both the eQTL and cancer GWAS datasets and with a minor allele frequency (MAF) > 1%.

For the T-cell-focused analysis, single-cell eQTL data were restricted to six CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T-cell clusters in OneK1K, and only associations with Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 were retained. To define a high-confidence T-cell cis-eQTL gene set, SNP-to-gene links from OneK1K were intersected with the top 50 trait-related genes (TRGs) ranked by the pipeline in the TRC-T module. SNP–gene pairs supported by both resources were retained as high-confidence T-cell cis-eQTLs, yielding 36 genes and covering 72% of the top-50 TRGs.

For each retained SNP–gene pair, the Spearman correlation coefficient (ρ) reported in OneK1K was recorded. Absolute correlation values (|ρ | ) were used as effect-size estimates, and |ρ | ≥ 0.15 was considered evidence of relatively robust allele-dependent expression.

Genome-wide Manhattan-style plots were generated using ggplot2 by concatenating chromosomal coordinates on the x-axis and plotting |ρ| values on the y-axis to visualize cis-eQTL effect magnitudes for each gene. Heat maps showing per-gene |ρ| distributions, gene–cancer association scores across five tumor types, and an integrated score were generated using pheatmap (v1.0.12). Genes were ordered in descending order of the integrated score to prioritize candidate loci for downstream functional validation.

Optimization calculation process and obtaining TRGs

In the pipeline analysis, the procedure followed the standard approach outlined in scPagwas21, with key modifications made for the calculation of TRGs. First, the genetically associated pathway activity scores derived from scPagwas were used to randomly select 10,000 samples of genetic pathway activity scores and single-cell gene expression data for correlation analysis. This random sampling process was repeated 1000 times to enhance the robustness of the results. All correlation results from the random samplings were integrated and averaged to calculate gene correlation scores, a method designed to minimize the impact of outliers and improve the reliability of the associations. To account for the non-linear distribution of the data during correlation calculations, we compared the differential gene expression between the top 5% of samples, based on genetic pathway activity score, and the remaining samples (Fig. S4). This analysis yielded statistically significant p-values for each gene. The Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) and corresponding p-values were then used to assess the relationship between genes and genetic pathway activity score, with genes exhibiting a PCC greater than 0.1 and a p-value less than 0.05 being deemed genetically significant. To rank genes based on their overall association, a weighted PCC was calculated for each gene. Genes were subsequently sorted according to this weighted PCC, ensuring that the trait-relevant scores were comparable across different cancer types. Finally, the top 500 genes with the strongest genetic correlations were selected to calculate the genetic TRS score and p-value for each cell, using the standard procedure from scPagwas.

Determine the optimal quantity of genes for calculating TRS

To evaluate the reliability of calculating TRS scores using the top 500 genes, we performed a sensitivity analysis on validated PBMC and DLBCL tumor B-cell datasets. We iteratively calculated TRS scores by increasing the gene count from 50 to 1,000 (at intervals of 50) and monitored the resulting changes in the scPagwas-based TIME pipeline’s performance via AUC metrics. Gene ranking was restricted to those with a weighted PCC greater than 0, as negative weighted PCC values represent an inverse correlation irrelevant to our ranking objective. By quantifying the number of genes with a positive weighted PCC across all cancer types, we observed that this count consistently exceeded 500. These findings empirically support the use of the top 500 genes as a robust and appropriate parameter for TRS calculations across diverse cancer datasets.

Gene ontology enrichment analysis

We performed over-representation analysis (ORA) for biological process terms using WebGestaltR (v0.4.6) to characterize significant genes identified by three different methods: scPagwas, MAGMA, and the scPagwas-based TIME pipeline. For each approach, we defined significant genes using method-specific thresholds (adjusted p-value ≤ 0.05 for pipeline and MAGMA, PCC ≥ 0.1 for scPagwas). The analysis was conducted against the non-redundant Gene Ontology Biological Processes database (2023-01 release) using human gene symbols as identifiers. A reference set of 18,988 protein-coding genes from the Human Genome Nomenclature Committee served as background. Significant terms were selected using Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction (p < 0.05) and visualized through horizontal bar plots depicting -log10(FDR) values for the top 10 enriched terms, generated using ggplot2 (v3.4.2) with default parameters. All analyses were conducted in R (v4.2.2) using standard statistical packages from CRAN and Bioconductor.

scPagwas-based TIME pipeline for the analysis of diffuse large B-Cell Lymphoma

Simulation framework on tumor-immune single-cell data. To evaluate the scPagwas-based TIME analysis pipeline under controlled conditions, we generated an in-silico tumor-immune scRNA-seq dataset by re-sampling raw UMI counts from the melanoma datasets. The positive genes were defined by intersecting the top 500 TRGs with cis-eQTL genes detected in OneK1K T-cell subsets (FDR < 0.05) and in the eQTLGen CD8⁺ cohort, yielding a set of 317 TRGs. For each TRG, we selected one control gene with baseline expression levels falling between the 10th and 90th percentiles of the TRG distribution. Together with an equally sized set of matched control genes, this process produced three gene panels of identical size and comparable abundance. Cytotoxic T cells were randomly divided into a reference group and a downregulated group, in which TRG expression levels were scaled by a factor of 10⁻³. Macrophage/monocyte cells were categorized into three groups: (i) an unmodified reference group, (ii) a TRG-up group with two-fold amplification of all TRGs, and (iii) a random-up group with two-fold amplification of the random-gene panel.

Validation of DLBCL as the gold standard. To validate the effectiveness of the pipeline in cancer analysis, we applied GWAS data for DLBCL to two scRNA-seq datasets: one consisting of normal peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and the other comprising B cells from de novo DLBCL, follicular lymphoma, and pediatric tonsils. In our analysis, we identified B cells in the first dataset, normal PBMCs, and tumor B cells of DLBCL in the second dataset as positive cells, and all others as negative cells. Next, we computed the AUC values using these identifications and the TRS scores to assess the accuracy of identifying positive cells. Furthermore, we compared the pipeline results with those obtained using the original scPagwas and scDRS methods.

Comparison of cell type identification results with other methods. We selected four methods to compare the pipeline with alternative approaches: Fusion35, Rolypoly36, scDRS37, and scLinker38. Fusion and MAGMA techniques primarily employ the top 500 genetically significant genes derived from these methods to compute single-cell enrichment scores based on scDRS. Subsequently, they assessed the significance of cell types. We compared cell-type association results across cancer types to assess consistency between the pipeline and alternative methods; greater concordance was interpreted as support for the robustness of the pipeline.

Comparative analysis of association results from independent GWAS datasets. Furthermore, we performed additional analyses using the GWAS data to validate our findings. Specifically, we acquired GWAS results from two tumor datasets, Breast Cancer (ieu-b-4810) and Lung Cancer (ieu-b-4954), comprising a substantial number of samples exceeding ten thousand. These two datasets were selected based on the premise that larger sample sizes in the GWAS data were associated with greater precision of the results. The statistical significance of the association between the genetic TRS scores for the same tumor in these two GWAS datasets was evaluated using Pearson’s correlation analysis.

Identification of cancer-specific TRPs

To determine the genetic associations of MP for each cancer, we classified cells into high genetic association cells (top 5% based on genetic pathway activity score ranking) and control cells. We used FindAllMarkers in the Seurat package based on the MPs×Cells score matrix, followed by the Wilcoxon rank-sum test to compute significantly different meta-programs as TRPs. The resulting p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg correction method. The top five meta-programs for each cell type were obtained using this process.

Noise assessment and evaluation

To evaluate the robustness of the scPagwas-based TIME pipeline to data noise, we systematically introduced controlled perturbations to both GWAS summary statistics and scRNA-seq data. For GWAS data, Gaussian noise scaled by standard errors (noise levels k = 0.1-1.0 in 0.1 increments) was added to effect size estimates (β). For single-cell data, Poisson-approximated noise was applied to non-zero UMI counts followed by standard renormalization. The pipeline was re-executed with identical parameters on each perturbed dataset, and robustness was quantified via Pearson correlation (ρ) between original and noisy gene-level Pathway-Cell Correlation scores across all noise levels.

Analysis of TCGA data

Tumor immune-related data were downloaded from TCGA, including lymphocyte infiltration data, TMB-related data, and immune cell infiltration data calculated using Cibersort from the official website of the Genomic Data Commons (GDC) at https://gdc.cancer.gov/about-data/publications/panimmune. Discrepancies in lymphocyte proportions among different cancer types were calculated based on the lymphocyte infiltration data. We retrieved TCGA OS survival-associated information from https://tcga.xenahubs.net/ and performed survival analysis on all genes across diverse cancer types. Overall survival associations were evaluated using univariable Cox proportional hazards regression, implemented with the R survival package. For each gene, a Cox model of OS time and event status was fitted with gene expression as the independent variable, and p-values were extracted for downstream interpretation. We further validated the significance of survival-associated TRGs using a hypergeometric test. Furthermore, Cox regression analysis was used to compute the survival analysis results for the top five TRPs for each specific cancer type.

Text-mining-based literature evidence

Polygenic regression model in scPagwas

In scPagwas, a polygenic regression framework was developed to integrate GWAS summary statistics with pathway activities derived from scRNA-seq. SNPs are first mapped to nearby genes and then grouped into pathways based on curated gene sets (e.g., KEGG). The model assumes that SNPs within each pathway share a common variance component (σ²), which reflects the contribution of that pathway to the overall trait heritability. scPagwas extends this concept by allowing the variance σ² to depend on cell-specific pathway activity scores, thereby linking genetic effects to cellular functional states. To estimate these relationships efficiently, scPagwas employs a method-of-moments approach, fitting the observed and expected variances of SNP effects across pathways. This yields pathway-level coefficients that quantify how genetic effects are modulated by cell-specific pathway activities.

Finally, the genetically associated pathway activity score for each cell is computed as a weighted sum of these coefficients across all pathways, providing a quantitative measure of the cell’s genetic relevance to the studied trait.

Identification of trait-relevant genes and cells in scPagwas

To identify disease- or trait-associated genes and cell populations, scPagwas correlates each gene’s expression profile with the genetically associated pathway activity score across all single cells. Genes showing strong positive correlations are defined as trait-relevant genes (TRGs), representing those most likely influenced by genetic variants.

For each cell, scPagwas computes a trait-relevant score (TRS) by averaging the expression levels of TRGs, thereby quantifying the degree to which a cell exhibits a genetically enriched transcriptional program. To correct for variation in sequencing depth and gene detection, a background control set of genes with matched expression levels is generated, and its mean score is subtracted from the raw TRS, ensuring that enrichment reflects genuine genetic associations rather than technical bias.

Cells with significantly higher TRS values are then considered trait-relevant, and their distributions across annotated cell types are evaluated to pinpoint specific cell populations enriched for genetic risk signals. This framework enables a robust, noise-resistant estimation of how disease-related genetic effects manifest at both the gene and single-cell levels.

scDRS analysis for identifying trait-associated cell types

To independently validate trait–cell type associations, we employed the scDRS (single-cell Disease Relevance Score) framework, which integrates GWAS summary statistics with scRNA-seq data to identify cell populations enriched for genetically implicated genes.

Gene-level association inference using MAGMA: For each GWAS dataset, we first mapped SNPs to nearby genes using MAGMA v1.10, applying a symmetric window of ±10 kb around each gene boundary based on the NCBI37.3 reference genome. Input files were generated from the cleaned GWAS summary statistics, containing SNP identifiers, p-values, and sample sizes. MAGMA computed per-gene association p-values using the European panel from the 1000 Genomes Phase 3 dataset as the LD reference. The resulting gene-level summary statistics were annotated with HGNC gene symbols and stored as input for the scDRS pipeline.

Calculation of scDRS scores: For each cancer type, we used the processed scRNA-seq data and the corresponding MAGMA output file as inputs to the scDRS_pipeline.py script. Genes were ranked by significance, and the top 1000 trait-associated genes were selected for each GWAS. For every cell, scDRS computed a disease relevance score (DRS) by aggregating the expression of these top genes and comparing it to randomly sampled control gene sets matched for mean expression and variance (n = 200 controls per run). This normalization procedure ensures that high DRS values represent genuine enrichment of genetically associated gene expression rather than technical or expression-level artifacts.

Cell-type enrichment analysis: Cells were grouped by their annotated cell type, and the distribution of DRS values was evaluated to identify cell populations significantly enriched for trait-relevant expression patterns. The results were summarized as cell-type-level enrichment p-values and effect sizes, reflecting the relative contribution of each immune or stromal subset to disease-associated genetic signals.

This two-step MAGMA + scDRS approach provided an independent and complementary validation of the cell-type associations detected by scPagwas and scPagwas-based TIME pipeline.

Similarity and enrichment analysis for TRGs

In this study, we analyzed the similarity between TRGs in pan-cancer by calculating the Jaccard coefficients of pairwise cancer TRGs. Subsequently, cluster analysis was performed on the resulting coefficient matrix. Genes showing a PCC > 0 and a p-value < 0.01 were considered TRGs. To investigate the functional genes associated with different cancer TRGs, we used the WebGestaltR package (https://github.com/bzhanglab/WebGestaltR) for gene enrichment analysis. The ORA method used the Reactome and GO databases for enrichment analysis. A minimum of 5 and a maximum of 100 genes were defined during this process. Statistical significance was determined using the FDR method (Benjamini-Hochberg correction), with an FDR threshold set at 0.05 and a topThr value of 100.

Identification of TRC-T-associated experimental genes

We focused on identifying genes with significant cross-cancer genetic associations, based on the integration of data from five cancers (breast cancer, melanoma, non-small cell lung cancer, thyroid cancer, and gastric cancer). The steps for selecting candidate genes were as follows:

Weighted Pearson Correlation Coefficient (PCC) Calculation: We first calculated the weighted PCC for each gene across all five cancers, integrating the correlation data into a unified table. For genes missing from certain cancer datasets, we filled the missing PCC values with zeros.

Min-Max Normalization: To ensure comparability across cancer datasets, we performed Min-Max normalization on the PCC values for each cancer, addressing potential biases caused by differences in data scaling.

Average Score Calculation: We computed the mean of the normalized PCC values across all cancers for each gene, generating an average score that serves as a comprehensive indicator of cross-cancer genetic association.

Candidate Gene Selection: Based on the average scores, we ranked the top 50 genes and selected them as potential candidates for experimental validation.

Redundancy Removal: To reduce experimental complexity, we performed correlation analysis to eliminate highly redundant genes. Specifically, we calculated the Pearson correlation coefficients between the normalized PCC values of all candidate genes, removing pairs with a correlation absolute value greater than 0.9. We retained the genes with higher average scores, ensuring that the final list of candidate genes was biologically independent.

Expert Review: After filtering, immunology experts manually selected five genes from the top candidates for experimental validation. These genes were chosen based on their potential role in immune response and tumor immunity, as they showed significant cross-cancer associations in our analysis.

Experimental validation strategy

Primary human T-cell isolation and activation: Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from healthy donor blood by Ficoll-Plus (Solarbio) density-gradient centrifugation. CD3 + T cells were then negatively selected using the BeaverBeads™ Human CD3 + T Cell Isolation Kit (Beaver, 71101) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Purified CD3 + T cells (>95% purity) were activated with anti-CD3/CD28-coated magnetic beads (Beaver, 71102) at a 1:1 bead-to-cell ratio and cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, 11875101) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, 10099141), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin. Recombinant human IL-2 (30 U/mL; Procell, PC0103) was added 48 h after stimulation to support expansion. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2.

Lentiviral shRNA-mediated gene knockdown: Short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) targeting CST7, IL32, NDUFB1, CCL4, and ALOX5AP were designed using BLOCK-iT™ RNAi Designer (Thermo Fisher) and cloned into the pLKO.1-puro vector (Addgene, 10878). The corresponding oligonucleotide sequences are listed in Table S8. A non-targeting shRNA cloned into the same pLKO.1-puro backbone was used as the control. Lentiviral particles were generated by co-transfecting HEK293T cells (ATCC, CRL-3216) with the shRNA expression plasmid, the packaging plasmid pCMV-gag-pol (Addgene, 14887), and the envelope plasmid pCMV-VSV-G (Addgene, 8454) using ExFect™ Transfection Reagent (Vazyme, T101-01). Viral supernatants were collected at 48 h and 72 h after transfection, filtered through 0.45 μm membranes, and concentrated by ultracentrifugation (25,000 × g, 4 °C, 2 h). Activated CD3 + T cells were transduced with lentiviral particles at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10 in the presence of 8 μg/mL polybrene (Sigma, TR-1003). For combinatorial knockdown experiments, equal amounts of individual shRNA lentiviruses were pooled before transduction. Transduced cells were selected with puromycin (2 μg/mL; Sigma, P8833) for 48 h before downstream assays.

Quantitative real-time PCR: Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol™ Reagent (Invitrogen, 15596026) and reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the ReverTra Ace™ cDNA Synthesis Kit (Toyobo, TRT-101). Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed on a QuantStudio™ 5 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) using SYBR™ Premix Ex Taq™ (Takara, RR420A). Primer sequences for CST7, IL32, NDUFB1, CCL4, ALOX5AP, IL2RA, IL2, IFNG, and TNFA, together with the endogenous control 18S rRNA. Relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2^-ΔΔCt method.

Cytokine secretion and proliferation assays: Culture supernatants from stimulated or transduced T cells were collected at the indicated time points. Secreted IFN-γ and TNF-α were quantified using Human IFN-γ ELISA MAX™ Deluxe (BioLegend, 430104) and Human TNF-α ELISA MAX™ Deluxe (BioLegend, 430204) kits according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a Tecan Spark® microplate reader (Tecan, 30086377), and cytokine concentrations were calculated from standard curves generated with recombinant proteins. T-cell proliferation was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Dojindo, CK04). Transduced T cells were seeded into 96-well plates at 5 × 10^4 cells per well. After 48 h, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm, and proliferation values were normalized to day 0 controls.

Replication and controls: Experiments shown in Fig. 7 were performed using primary CD3 + T cells independently isolated from two healthy donors, which were treated as biological replicates (N = 2). Within each donor-derived experiment, technical replicates (n = 3–4, depending on assay format) were used. For qPCR, technical replicates were averaged before calculating relative expression with the 2^-ΔΔCt method using 18S rRNA as the endogenous control. For ELISA and CCK-8 assays, replicate wells were measured and averaged prior to downstream analysis.

Predictive analysis of immune therapy response employing a deep learning model

We constructed deep learning models by utilizing pre-immunotherapy scRNA-seq data derived from melanoma and breast cancer (tissue and blood samples). The response classification was carried out according to the RECIST/iRECIST criteria, where Responders were defined as those with Complete Response or Partial Response, and Non-responders included those with Stable Disease, Progressive Disease, or Unevaluable responses. An identical preprocessing approach was applied to the renal cell carcinoma (tissue and blood) and lung cancer (tissue) datasets. TRCs and TRGs, such as the Tem/Trm.cyt and Tem/Temra.cyt populations, were exploited as biological features. To prevent data leakage arising from shared genetic background among cells from the same individual, we performed subject-level data partitioning, assigning all cells from each participant exclusively to either the training or testing set. This strategy ensures independent model evaluation and prevents inflation of predictive performance due to within-individual correlations, and it provides a reliable foundation for implementing a Dynamic Multi-Layer Perceptron with a modified architecture. This architecture consisted of dual hidden layers (with 256 and then 128 nodes), an Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.001, and a binary output layer. The performance of the model was evaluated through ROC analysis of the response probability predictions in comparison to the clinical labels.

Our MLP model employs backpropagation to compute loss function gradients for parameter optimization, determining gene-specific weights within the genetic architecture. To rigorously evaluate feature contributions, we implemented stepwise TRG inclusion, systematically increasing gene sets while quantifying their impact on predictive performance via AUC analysis.

Statistics and reproducibility

All statistical analyses were performed using R and Python unless otherwise specified. All tests were two-sided unless otherwise indicated. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Where multiple comparisons were performed, P values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate procedure. Exact statistical tests used for each analysis are described in the corresponding Methods subsections, figure legends, or supplementary tables, as appropriate.

For scRNA-seq-based analyses, preprocessing, integration, and downstream analyses were performed separately for each cancer type to avoid cross-cancer batch confounding. Individual cells that passed quality-control filtering were used as analytical units for pathway activity scoring, trait-related score calculation, and visualization. Biologically independent samples referred to distinct patient samples or independent published datasets, depending on the analysis. Trait-related genes were prioritized by combining repeated subsampling-based correlation analysis with differential expression testing, and major findings were further examined across independent datasets where available. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size because this study primarily analyzed publicly available scRNA-seq, GWAS, and eQTL datasets generated by the original studies. No data were excluded unless they failed the predefined quality-control criteria described above.

For comparative and enrichment analyses, including identification of trait-related cell types, trait-related genes, trait-related meta-programs, pathway enrichment, and cross-cancer module comparisons, the same analysis framework and thresholds were applied consistently across datasets. Multiple-hypothesis correction was performed where appropriate. Computational reproducibility was supported by repeated subsampling during trait-related gene prioritization, robustness analyses under data perturbation, and validation in independent datasets. For predictive modeling of immunotherapy response, all cells from the same individual were assigned exclusively to either the training or testing set to prevent subject-level information leakage and to ensure independent model evaluation. Model performance was assessed by receiver operating characteristic analysis and area under the curve.

For experimental validation, primary CD3 + T cells independently isolated from two healthy donors were treated as biologically independent replicates (N = 2). Within each donor-derived experiment, technical replicates (n = 3–4, depending on assay format) were included and averaged before downstream analysis; technical replicates were not treated as biologically independent replicates for statistical inference. For qPCR assays, technical replicates were averaged before relative expression values were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method with 18S rRNA as the endogenous control. For ELISA and CCK-8 assays, replicate wells were averaged before plotting and interpretation. The experiments shown in Fig. 7 were repeated across the two independent donors and showed similar overall trends. For data presentations in which formal statistical testing was not applied, including descriptive visualizations and workflow summaries, reproducibility was supported by consistency across repeated computational analyses, independent datasets, or independent donor-derived experiments, as applicable.

Ancestry control

All datasets used in this study—including GWAS summary statistics, eQTL data, LD reference panels, and weight files—were derived exclusively from European ancestry cohorts. The GWAS data had already been corrected for population stratification in their original analyses, and the LD matrices were computed using the 1000 Genomes Project European reference panel, ensuring ancestry consistency across all components of the polygenic regression model.
