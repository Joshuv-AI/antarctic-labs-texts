Shortcuts and Identifiability in Concept-based Models from a Neuro-Symbolic Lens
Samuele BortolottiDISI, University of Trento, Italy name.surname@unitn.it Emanuele Marconato DISI, University of Trento, ItalyDI, University of Pisa, Italy name.surname@unitn.it Paolo MorettinDISI, University of Trento, Italy name.surname@unitn.it Andrea PasseriniDISI, University of Trento, Italy name.surname@unitn.it Stefano TesoCIMeC & DISI, University of Trento, Italy name.surname@unitn.it

Abstract
Concept-based Models are neural networks that learn a concept extractor to map inputs to high-level concepts and an inference layer to translate these into predictions. Ensuring these modules produce interpretable concepts and behave reliably in out-of-distribution is crucial, yet the conditions for achieving this remain unclear. We study this problem by establishing a novel connection between Concept-based Models and reasoning shortcuts (RSs), a common issue where models achieve high accuracy by learning low-quality concepts, even when the inference layer is fixed and provided upfront. Specifically, we first extend RSs to the more complex setting of Concept-based Models and then derive theoretical conditions for identifying both the concepts and the inference layer. Our empirical results highlight the impact of reasoning shortcuts and show that existing methods, even when combined with multiple natural mitigation strategies, often fail to meet these conditions in practice.

Introduction

Concept-based Models (CBMs) are a broad class of self-explainable classifiers
[
Alvarez-Melis and Jaakkola 2018
,
Koh et al. 2020
,
Espinosa Zarlenga et al. 2022
,
Marconato et al. 2022
,
Taeb et al. 2022
]
designed for high performance and
ante-hoc
interpretability.
Learning a CBM involves solving two conjoint problems:
acquiring high-level
concepts
describing the input (e.g., an image) and
an
inference layer
that predicts a label from them.
In many applications, it is essential that these two elements are “high quality”, in the sense that:

i
) the concepts should be
interpretable
, as failure in doing so compromises understanding
[
Schwalbe 2022
,
Poeta et al. 2023
]
and steerability
[
Teso et al. 2023
,
Gupta and Narayanan 2024
]
, both key selling point of CBMs; and

ii
) the concepts and inference layer should behave well also
out of distribution
(OOD), e.g., they should not pick up spurious correlations between the input, the concepts and the output
[
Geirhos et al. 2020
,
Bahadori and Heckerman 2021
,
Stammer et al. 2021
]
.

It is natural to ask under what conditions CBMs can acquire such “high-quality” concepts and inference layers.
While existing studies focus on concept quality
[
Mahinpei et al. 2021
,
Zarlenga et al. 2023
,
Marconato et al. 2023a
]
, they neglect the role of the inference layer altogether.
In contrast, we cast the question in terms of whether it is possible to
identify
from data concepts and inference layers with the intended semantics, defined formally in
Section

3.1
.
We proceed to answer this question by building a novel connection with
reasoning shortcuts
(RSs), a well-known issue in Neuro-Symbolic (NeSy) AI whereby models achieve high accuracy by learning low-quality concepts
even if the inference layer is fixed

[
Chang et al. 2020
,
Marconato et al. 2023b
,
Wang et al. 2023
,
Yang et al. 2024a
]
.
E.g., a NeSy model for autonomous driving whose inference layer encodes the traffic laws might confuse pedestrians with red lights, as both entail the same prediction (the car has to stop)
[
Bortolotti et al. 2024
]
.

We generalize RSs to CBMs in which
the inference layer is learned
, and supervision on the concepts may be absent.
Our analysis shows that shortcut solutions can be exponentially many (indeed, we count them explicitly in
Section

B.2
) and that, in general, maximum likelihood training is insufficient for attaining intended semantics.
This negatively affects interpretability – as the learned concepts cannot be easily mapped to the ground-truth annotations – and OOD behavior.
On the positive side, we also specify conditions under which (under suitable assumptions) CBMs
cannot
be fooled by RSs, proving that
the ground-truth concepts and inference layer can be identified
(see
Theorem

3.8
).

Our numerical simulations with regular and NeSy CBMs on several learning tasks suggest CBMs can be severely impacted by reasoning shortcuts in practice, as expected, and also that unfortunately the benefits of popular mitigation strategies do not carry over to this more challenging problem.
These results cast doubts on the ability of these models to identify concepts and inference layers with the intended semantics unless appropriately nudged.

Contributions
: In summary, we:

•

Generalize reasoning shortcuts to the challenging case of CBMs whose inference layer is learned.

•

Study conditions for maximum likelihood training to
identify
high quality concepts
and
inference layers.

•

Present empirical evidence that proven mitigations fail in this challenging problem.

Preliminaries

Concept-based Models
(CBMs) first map the input
\bm{\mathrm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
into
k
discrete concepts
\bm{\mathrm{c}}=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{k})\in\mathcal{C}
via a neural backbone,
and
then infer labels
\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}
from this using a white-box layer, e.g., a linear layer.
This setup makes it easy to figure out what concepts are most responsible for any prediction, yielding a form of
ante-hoc
concept-based interpretability.
Several architectures follow this recipe, including approaches
for
converting black-box neural network classifiers
into CBMs
[
Yuksekgonul et al. 2022
,
Wang et al. 2024
,
Dominici et al. 2024
,
Marcinkevičs et al. 2024
]
.

A key issue is how to ensure the concepts are interpretable.
Some CBMs rely on
concept annotations

[
Koh et al. 2020
,
Sawada and Nakamura 2022
,
Espinosa Zarlenga et al. 2022
,
Marconato et al. 2022
,
Kim et al. 2023
,
Debot et al. 2024
]
.
These are however expensive to obtain, prompting researchers to replace them with (potentially unreliable
[
Huang et al. 2024
,
Sun et al. 2024
]
) annotations obtained from foundation models
[
Oikarinen et al. 2022
,
Yang et al. 2023
,
Srivastava et al. 2024
]
or
unsupervised
concept discovery
[
Alvarez-Melis and Jaakkola 2018
,
Chen et al. 2019
,
Taeb et al. 2022
,
Schrodi et al. 2024
]
.

Neuro-Symbolic CBMs
(NeSy-CBMs) specialize CBMs to tasks in which the prediction
\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}
ought to comply with known safety or structural constraints. These are supplied as a formal specification – a logic formula
\mathsf{K}
,
aka

knowledge
– tying together the prediction
\bm{\mathrm{y}}
and the concepts
\bm{\mathrm{c}}
.
In NeSy-CBMs, the inference step is a
symbolic reasoning layer
that steers
[
Lippi and Frasconi 2009
,
Diligenti et al. 2012
,
Donadello et al. 2017
,
Xu et al. 2018
]
or guarantees
[
Manhaeve et al. 2018
,
Giunchiglia and Lukasiewicz 2020
,
Hoernle et al. 2022
,
Ahmed et al. 2022
]
the labels and concepts to be logically consistent according to
\mathsf{K}
.
Throughout, we will consider the following example task:

Example 1

(
MNIST-SumParity
)
.

Given two MNIST digits
[
LeCun 1998
]
, we wish to predict whether their sum is even or odd.
The numerical values of the two digits can be modelled as concepts
\bm{\mathrm{C}}\in\{0,\ldots,9\}^{2}
, and the inference layer is entirely determined by the prior knowledge:

\mathsf{K}=((y=1)\Leftrightarrow\text{$(C_{1}+C_{2})$ is odd})
.
This specifies that the label
y\in\{0,1\}
ought to be consistent with the predicted concepts
\bm{\mathrm{C}}
.
See
Fig.

1
for an illustration.

Like CBMs, NeSy-CBMs are usually trained via
maximum likelihood
and gradient descent, but without concept supervision.
The reasoning layer is typically imbued with fuzzy
[
Zadeh 1988
]
or probabilistic
[
De Raedt and Kimmig 2015
]
logic semantics to ensure differentiability.
Many NeSy-CBMs require
\mathsf{K}
to be provided upfront, as in
Example

1
, hence their inference layer has no learnable parameters. Starting with
Section

3
, we will instead consider NeSy-CBMs that – just like regular CBMs –
learn the inference layer

[
Wang et al. 2019
,
Liu et al. 2023
,
Daniele et al. 2023
,
Tang and Ellis 2023
,
Wüst et al. 2024
]
.

2.1
Reasoning Shortcuts

Before discussing reasoning shortcuts, we need to establish a clear relationship between concepts, inputs, and labels.
The RS literature does so by assuming the following
data generation process

[
Marconato et al. 2023b
,
Yang et al. 2024a
,
Umili et al. 2024
]
: each input
\bm{\mathrm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the result of sampling
k

ground-truth concepts

\bm{\mathrm{g}}=(g_{1},\ldots,g_{k})\in\mathcal{G}
(e.g., in
MNIST-SumParity
two numerical digits) from an unobserved distribution
p^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{G}})
and then
\bm{\mathrm{x}}
itself (e.g., two corresponding MNIST images) from the conditional distribution
p^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{X}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{g}})
.
1
1

1

This distribution subsumes stylistic factors, e.g., calligraphy.

Labels
\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}
are sampled from the conditional distribution of
\bm{\mathrm{g}}
given by

p^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{Y}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{g}};\mathsf{K})
consistently with
\mathsf{K}
(e.g.,
y=1
if and only if
g_{1}+g_{2}
is odd).
The ground-truth distribution is thus:

p^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{X}},\bm{\mathrm{Y}})=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\mathrm{g}}\sim p^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{G}})}[p^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{X}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{g}})p^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{Y}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{g}};\mathsf{K})]

(1)

Intuitively, a
reasoning shortcut
(RS) occurs when a NeSy-CBM with
fixed
knowledge
\mathsf{K}
attains high or even perfect label accuracy by learning concepts
\bm{\mathrm{C}}
that differ from the ground-truth ones
\bm{\mathrm{G}}

[
Marconato et al. 2023b
]
. As commonly done, we work in the setting where
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{C}
.

Example 2
.

In
MNIST-SumParity
, a NeSy model can achieve perfect accuracy by mapping each pair of MNIST images
\bm{\mathrm{x}}=(\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{1},\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{2})
to the corresponding ground-truth digits, that is,
\bm{\mathrm{c}}=(g_{1},g_{2})
.
However, it would achieve the same accuracy if it were to map it to
\bm{\mathrm{c}}=(g_{1}\ \mathrm{mod}\ 2,g_{2}\ \mathrm{mod}\ 2)
, as doing so leaves the parity of the sum unchanged, see
Fig.

1
.
Hence, a NeSy model cannot distinguish between the two based on label likelihood alone during training.

RSs
by definition
yield good labels in-distribution, yet they compromise out-of-distribution (OOD) performance.
For instance, in autonomous driving tasks, NeSy-CBMs can confuse the concepts of “pedestrian” and “red light”, leading to poor decisions for OOD decisions where the distinction matters
[
Bortolotti et al. 2024
]
.
The meaning of concepts affected by RSs is unclear, affecting understanding
[
Schwalbe 2022
]
, intervenability
[
Shin et al. 2023
,
Espinosa Zarlenga et al. 2024
,
Steinmann et al. 2024b
]
, debugging
[
Lertvittayakumjorn et al. 2020
,
Stammer et al. 2021
]

and down-stream applications that hinge on concepts being high-quality, like NeSy formal verification
[
Xie et al. 2022
,
Zaid et al. 2023
,
Morettin et al. 2024
]
.
Unfortunately, existing works on RSs do not apply to CBMs and NeSy-CBMs where the inference layer is learned.

3
Reasoning Shortcuts in CBMs

Given a finite set
\mathcal{S}
, we indicate with
\Delta_{\mathcal{S}}\subset[0,1]^{|\mathcal{S}|}
the simplex of probability distributions
P(S)
over items in
\mathcal{S}
. Notice that the set of its vertices
\mathsf{Vert}(\Delta_{\mathcal{S}})
contains all point mass distributions
\mathbbm{1}\!\{S=s\}
for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
.

CBMs as pairs of functions
. CBMs and NeSy-CBMs differ in how they implement the inference layer, hence to bridge them we employ the following unified formalism.
Any CBM can be viewed as a pair of learnable functions implementing the concept extractor and the inference layer, respectively, cf.
Fig.

2
.
Formally, the former is a function
\bm{\mathrm{f}}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}
mapping inputs
\bm{\mathrm{x}}
to a conditional distribution
p(\bm{\mathrm{C}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{x}})
over the concepts, however it can be better understood as a function
\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}:\mathcal{G}\to\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}
taking ground-truth concepts
\bm{\mathrm{g}}
as input instead, and defined as:

\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}(\bm{\mathrm{g}})=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\mathrm{x}}\sim p^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{X}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{g}})}[\bm{\mathrm{f}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})]

(2)

On the other hand, the inference layer is a function
\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}:\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}\to\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}
mapping the concept distribution output by the concept extractor into a label distribution
p(\bm{\mathrm{Y}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{f}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))
.
For clarity, we also define
\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}:\mathcal{C}\to\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}
, which is identical to the former except it works with concept
values
rather than
distributions
, that is:

\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}(\bm{\mathrm{c}}):=\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}(\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}\})

(3)

Hence, a CBM entails both a pair
(\bm{\mathrm{f}},\bm{\mathrm{\omega}})\in\mathcal{F}\times\Omega
and a pair
(\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}},\bm{\mathrm{\beta}})\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B}
.
2
2

2

We work in the non-parametric setting, hence
\mathcal{F}
and
\Omega
contain all learnable concept extractors
\bm{\mathrm{f}}
and inference layers
\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}
, and similarly
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
contain all learnable maps
\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}
and
\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}
.

Later on, we will make use of the fact that
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
are
simplices

[
Morton 2013
,
Montúfar et al. 2014
]
, i.e., each
\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}\in\mathcal{A}
(resp.
\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}\in\mathcal{B}
) can be written as a convex combination of vertices
\mathsf{Vert}(\mathcal{A})
(resp.
\mathsf{Vert}(\mathcal{B})
).

As mentioned in
Section

2
, supplying prior knowledge
\mathsf{K}
to a NeSy-CBM is equivalent to
fixing the inference layer
to a corresponding function
\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}^{*}
(and
\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{*}
).
Note that whereas
\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}^{*}
changes based on how reasoning is implemented – e.g., fuzzy vs. probabilistic logic –
\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{*}
does not, as both kinds of reasoning layers behave identically when input any point-mass concept distribution
\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}\}
.

Standard assumptions
. The maps
\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}
and
\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}
are especially useful for analysis provided the following two standard assumptions about how data are distributed:

Assumption 3.1

(Invertibility)
.

The ground-truth distribution
p^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{G}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{X}})
is induced by a function

\bm{\mathrm{f}}^{*}:\bm{\mathrm{x}}\mapsto\bm{\mathrm{g}}
, i.e.,
p^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{G}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{X}})=\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{G}}=\bm{\mathrm{f}}^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{X}})\}
.

This means that the ground-truth concepts
\bm{\mathrm{g}}
can always be unambiguously recovered for all inputs
\bm{\mathrm{x}}
by a sufficiently expressive concept extractor; the
\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}
it induces is the identity, indicated as
\mathrm{id}(\bm{\mathrm{g}}):=\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{g}}\}\in\mathsf{Vert}(\mathcal{A})
.

Assumption 3.2

(Deterministic knowledge)
.

The ground-truth distribution
p^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{Y}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{G}};\mathsf{K})
is induced by the knowledge via a map
\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{*}\in\mathsf{Vert}(\mathcal{B})
, such that

p^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{Y}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{G}};\mathsf{K})=\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{g}})
.

This ensures that the labels
\bm{\mathrm{y}}
can be predicted without any ambiguity from the ground-truth concepts
\bm{\mathrm{g}}
.
Both assumptions apply in several tasks, see
[
Bortolotti et al. 2024
]
.
The maximum log-likelihood objective is then written as:

\max_{(\bm{\mathrm{f}},\bm{\mathrm{\omega}})\in\mathcal{F}\times\Omega}\mathbb{E}_{(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{y}})\sim p^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{X}},\bm{\mathrm{Y}})}\log(\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}\circ\bm{\mathrm{f}})(\bm{\mathrm{x}})

(4)

Here,
\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}
is the conditional probability of the ground-truth labels
\bm{\mathrm{y}}
.
Notice that under the above assumptions CBMs attaining maximum likelihood perfectly model the ground-truth data distribution
p^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{X}},\bm{\mathrm{Y}})
, see
Lemma

C.1
.

3.1
Intended Semantics and Joint Reasoning Shortcuts

We posit that a CBM
(\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}},\bm{\mathrm{\beta}})\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B}
has “high-quality” concepts and inference layer if it satisfies two desiderata:

•

Disentanglement
: each learned concept
C_{i}
should be equal to a single ground-truth concept
G_{j}
up to an invertible transformation;

•

Generalization
: the combination of
\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}
and
\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}
must always yield correct predictions.

In our setting, without concept supervision and prior knowledge, the learned concepts are
anonymous
and users have to figure out which is which in a
post-hoc
fashion, e.g., by aligning them to dense annotations
[
Koh et al. 2020
,
Espinosa Zarlenga et al. 2022
,
Daniele et al. 2023
,
Tang and Ellis 2023
]
. Doing so is also a prerequisite for understanding the learned inference layer
[
Wüst et al. 2024
,
Daniele et al. 2023
,
Espinosa Zarlenga et al. 2022
,
Espinosa Zarlenga et al. 2024
]
. The benefit of
disentanglement
is that when it holds the mapping between
\bm{\mathrm{G}}
and
\bm{\mathrm{C}}
can be recovered
exactly
using Hungarian matching
[
Kuhn 1955
]
; otherwise it is arbitrarily difficult to recover, hindering interpretability.
This links CBMs with many works that treat model representations’
identifiability
in Independent Component Analysis and Causal Representation Learning
[
Khemakhem et al. 2020b
,
Gresele et al. 2021
,
Lippe et al. 2023
,
Lachapelle et al. 2023
,
von Kügelgen et al. 2024
]
, where disentanglement plays a central role.

Generalization
is equally important, as it entails that CBMs generalize beyond the support of training data and yields sensible OOD behavior, i.e., output the same predictions that would be obtained by using the ground-truth pair.
Since we want
\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}
to be disentangled, this implies, in turn, a specific form for the map
\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}
, as shown by the next definition, which formalizes these desiderata:

Definition 3.3

(Intended Semantics)
.

A CBM
(\bm{\mathrm{f}},\bm{\mathrm{\omega}})\in\mathcal{F}\times\Omega
entailing a pair
(\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}},\bm{\mathrm{\beta}})\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B}
possesses the intended semantics
if there exists a permutation
\pi:[k]\to[k]
and
k
element-wise invertible functions
\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{k}
such that:

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}(\bm{\mathrm{g}})

\displaystyle=(\bm{\mathrm{\psi}}\circ\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi}\circ\mathrm{id})(\bm{\mathrm{g}})

(5)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}(\bm{\mathrm{c}})

\displaystyle=(\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{*}\circ\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi}^{-1}\circ\bm{\mathrm{\psi}}^{-1})(\bm{\mathrm{c}})

(6)

Here,
\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi}:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C}
is the permutation matrix induced by
\pi
and
\bm{\mathrm{\psi}}(\bm{\mathrm{c}}):=(\psi_{1}(c_{1}),\ldots,\psi_{k}(c_{k}))
. In this case, we say that
(\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}},\bm{\mathrm{\beta}})
is equivalent to
(\mathrm{id},\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{*})
, i.e.,
(\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}},\bm{\mathrm{\beta}})\sim(\mathrm{id},\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{*})
.

That is, intended semantics holds if the learned concepts
\bm{\mathrm{C}}
match the ground-truth concepts
\bm{\mathrm{G}}
modulo simple invertible transformations – like reordering and negation (
Eq.

5
) – and the inference layer
undoes
these transformations before applying the “right” inference layer
\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{*}
(
Eq.

6
).
This also means that CBMs satisfying them are
equivalent
– in a very precise sense – to the ground-truth pair
(\mathrm{id},\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{*})
.
A similar equivalence relation was analyzed for continuous representations in energy-based models, including supervised classifiers, by
Khemakhem et al. 2020b
. In
Lemma

C.4
, we prove that models with the intended semantics yield the same predictions of the ground-truth pair for all
\bm{\mathrm{g}}\in\mathcal{G}
.

Training a (NeSy) CBM via maximum likelihood does not guarantee it will embody intended semantics.
We denote these failure cases as
joint reasoning shortcuts
(JRSs):

Definition 3.4

(Joint Reasoning Shortcut)
.

Take a CBM
(\bm{\mathrm{f}},\bm{\mathrm{\omega}})\in\mathcal{F}\times\Omega
that attains optimal log-likelihood (
Eq.

4
). The pair
(\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}},\bm{\mathrm{\beta}})
entailed by it (
Eqs.

2
and
3
) is a JRSs if it does not possess the intended semantics
(
Definition

3.3
), i.e.,
(\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}},\bm{\mathrm{\beta}})\not\sim(\mathrm{id},\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{*})
.

JRSs can take different forms.
First, even if the learned inference layer
\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}
matches (modulo
\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi}
and
\bm{\mathrm{\psi}}
) the ground-truth one
\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{*}
,
\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}
might not match (also modulo
\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi}
and
\bm{\mathrm{\psi}}
) the identity. This is analogous to regular RSs in NeSy CBMs (
Section

2
), in that the learned concepts do not reflect the ground-truth ones: while this is sufficient for high in-distribution performance (the training likelihood is in fact optimal), it may yield poor OOD behavior.
Second, even if
\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}
matches the identity, the inference layer
\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}
might not match the ground-truth one
\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{*}
. In our experiments
Section

5
, we observe that this often stems from
simplicity bias

[
Yang et al. 2024b
]
, i.e., the CBM’s inference layer tends to acquire specialized functions
\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}
that are much simpler than
\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{*}
, leading to erroneous predictions OOD.
Finally, neither the inference layer
\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}
nor the map
\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}
might match the ground-truth, opening the door to additional failure modes. Consider the following example:

Example 3
.

Consider a
MNIST-SumParity
problem where the training set consists of all possible unique (even, even), (odd, odd), and (odd, even) pairs of MNIST digits,
as in
Fig.

1
. A CBM trained on this data would achieve perfect accuracy by learning a JRS that extracts the parity of each input digit, yielding two binary concepts in
\{0,1\}
, and computes the difference between these two concepts. This JRS involves
much simpler concepts and knowledge
compared to the ground-truth ones. It also mispredicts all OOD pairs where the even digits come before odd digits.

3.2
Theoretical Analysis

We proceed to determine how many
deterministic
JRSs
(\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}},\bm{\mathrm{\beta}})\in\mathsf{Vert}(\mathcal{A})\times\mathsf{Vert}(\mathcal{B})
a learning task admits, and then show in
Theorem

3.8
that this number determines the presence or absence of general (non-deterministic) JRSs.

Theorem 3.5

(Informal)
.

Under
3.1
and
3.2
, the number of deterministic JRSs
(\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}},\bm{\mathrm{\beta}})
is:

\displaystyle\sum_{(\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}},\bm{\mathrm{\beta}})}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bigwedge_{\bm{\mathrm{g}}\in\mathrm{supp}(\bm{\mathrm{G}})}(\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}\circ\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}})(\bm{\mathrm{g}})=\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{g}})\}-C[\mathcal{G}]

(7)

where the sum runs over
\mathsf{Vert}(\mathcal{A})\times\mathsf{Vert}(\mathcal{B})
,
and
C[\mathcal{G}]
is the total number of pairs with the intended semantics.

All proofs can be found in
Appendix

C
.
Intuitively, the first count runs over all deterministic pairs
(\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}},\bm{\mathrm{\beta}})
that achieve maximum likelihood on the training set (i.e., the predicted labels
(\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}\circ\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}})(\bm{\mathrm{g}})
matches the ground-truth one
\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{g}})
for all ground-truth concepts
\bm{\mathrm{g}}
appearing in the data), while the second term subtracts the pairs
(\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}},\bm{\mathrm{\beta}})
that possess the intended semantics as per
Definition

3.3
.
Whenever the count is larger than zero, a CBM trained via maximum likelihood
can
learn a deterministic JRS.

As a sanity check, we show that if prior knowledge
\mathsf{K}
is provided – fixing the inference layer to
\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{*}
– the number of deterministic JRSs in fact matches that of RSs, as expected:

Corollary 3.6
.

Consider a fixed
\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{*}\in\mathsf{Vert}(\mathcal{B})
.
Under
3.1
and
3.2
, the number of deterministic JRSs
(\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}},\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{*})\in\mathsf{Vert}(\mathcal{A})\times\mathsf{Vert}(\mathcal{B})
is:

\textstyle\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}\in\mathsf{Vert}(\mathcal{A})}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bigwedge_{\bm{\mathrm{g}}\in\mathrm{supp}(\bm{\mathrm{G}})}(\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{*}\circ\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}})(\bm{\mathrm{g}})=\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{g}})\}\textstyle-1

(8)

This matches the count for deterministic RSs in
[
Marconato et al. 2023b
]
.

A natural consequence of
Corollary

3.6
that whenever
|\mathsf{Vert}(\mathcal{B})|>1
the number of deterministic JRSs in
Eq.

7
can be substantially
larger
than the number of deterministic RSs
Eq.

8
.
For instance, in
MNIST-SumParity
with digits restricted to the range
[0,4]
there exist exactly
64
RSs but about
100

thousand
JRSs, and the gap increases as we extend the range
[0,N]
.
We provide an in-depth analysis in
Section

B.2
.

Next, we show that whenever the number of deterministic JRSs in
Eq.

7
is zero, there exist
no JRSs at all
, including non-deterministic ones.
This however only applies to CBMs that satisfy the following natural assumption:

Assumption 3.7

(Extremality)
.

The inference layer
\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}\in\Omega
satisfies extremality if, for all
\lambda\in(0,1)
and for all
\bm{\mathrm{c}}\neq\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}
such that
\argmax_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}}\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}(\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}\})_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}\neq\argmax_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}}\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}(\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{\prime}\})_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}
, it holds:

\displaystyle\max_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}}\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}(\lambda\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}\}+(1-\lambda)\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{\prime}\})_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}

\displaystyle\leq\max\left(\max_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}}\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}(\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}\})_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}},\max_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}}\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}(\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{\prime}\})_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}\right)

Intuitively, a CBM satisfies this assumption if its inference layer
\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}
is “peaked”: for any two concept vectors
\bm{\mathrm{c}}
and
\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{\prime}
yielding distinct predictions, the label probability output by
\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}
for any mixture distribution thereof is no larger than the label probability that it associates to
\bm{\mathrm{c}}
and
\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{\prime}
. That is, distributing probability mass across concepts never nets an advantage in terms of label likelihood.
While this assumption does not hold for general CBMs, we show in
Appendix

E
that it holds for popular architectures, including most of those that we experiment with.
Under
3.7
, the next result holds:

Theorem 3.8

(Identifiability)
.

Under
3.1
and
3.2
,
if

the number of deterministic JRSs (
Eq.

7
) is zero

then

every CBM
(\bm{\mathrm{f}},\bm{\mathrm{\omega}})\in\mathcal{F}\times\Omega

satisfying
3.7

that attains maximum log-likelihood (
Eq.

4
)
possesses the intended semantics (
Definition

3.3
).
That is, the pair
(\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}},\bm{\mathrm{\beta}})\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B}
entailed by each such CBM is equivalent to the ground-truth pair
(\mathrm{id},\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{*})
, i.e.,
(\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}},\bm{\mathrm{\beta}})\sim(\mathrm{id},\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{*})
.

4
Practical Solutions

By
Theorem

3.8
, getting rid of
deterministic
JRSs from a task prevents CBMs trained via maximum log-likelihood to learn JRS altogether.
Next, we discuss strategies for achieving this goal and will assess them empirically in
Section

5
.
An in-depth technical analysis is left to
Appendix

D
.

4.1
Supervised strategies

The most direct strategies for controlling the semantics of learned CBMs rely on supervision.

Concept supervision
is the go-to solution for improving concept quality in CBMs
[
Koh et al. 2020
,
Chen et al. 2020b
,
Espinosa Zarlenga et al. 2022
,
Marconato et al. 2022
]
and NeSy-CBMs
[
Bortolotti et al. 2024
,
Yang et al. 2024a
]
.
Supplying full concept supervision for all ground-truth concepts
\bm{\mathrm{G}}
provably prevents learning
\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}
’s that do not match the identity.
However, this does not translate into guarantees on the inference layer
\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}
, at least in tasks affected by confounding factors such as spurious correlations among concepts
[
Stammer et al. 2021
]
.
E.g., if the
i
-th and
j
-th ground-truth concepts are strongly correlated, the inference layer
\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}
can interchangeably use either, regardless of what
\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{*}
does.

Another option is employing
knowledge distillation

[
Hinton 2015
]
, that is, supplying supervision of the form
(\bm{\mathrm{g}},\bm{\mathrm{y}})
to encourage the learned knowledge
\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}
to be close to
\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{*}
for the supervised
\bm{\mathrm{g}}
’s.
This supervision is available for free provided concept supervision is available, but can also be obtained separately, e.g., by interactively collecting user interventions
[
Shin et al. 2023
,
Espinosa Zarlenga et al. 2024
,
Steinmann et al. 2024b
]
.
This strategy cannot avoid
all
JRSs because even if
\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}=\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{*}
, the CBM may suffer from regular RSs (i.e.,
\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}
does not match
\mathrm{id}
).

Another option is to fit a CBM on
multiple tasks

[
Caruana 1997
]
sharing concepts.
It is known that a NeSy-CBM attaining optimal likelihood on multiple NeSy tasks with different prior knowledges is also optimal for the
single
NeSy task obtained by conjoining those knowledges
[
Marconato et al. 2023b
]
: this more constrained knowledge better steers the semantics of the learned concepts, ruling out potential JRSs.
Naturally, collecting a sufficiently large number of diverse tasks using the same concepts can be highly non-trivial, depending on the application.

4.2
Unsupervised strategies

Many popular strategies for improving concept quality in (NeSy) CBMs are unsupervised.
A cheap but effective one when dealing with multiple inputs is to
process inputs individually
, preventing mixing between their concepts. E.g., In
MNIST-SumParity
one can apply the same digit extractor to each digit separately, while for images with multiple objects one can first segment them (e.g., with YoloV11
[
Jocher et al. 2023
]
) and then process the resulting bounding boxes individually. This is extremely helpful for reducing, and possibly overcoming, RSs
[
Marconato et al. 2023b
]
and frequently used in practice
[
Manhaeve et al. 2018
,
van Krieken et al. 2022
,
Daniele et al. 2023
,
Tang and Ellis 2023
]
.
We apply it in all our experiments.

Both supervised
[
Marconato et al. 2022
]
and unsupervised
[
Alvarez-Melis and Jaakkola 2018
,
Li et al. 2018
]
CBMs may employ a
reconstruction
penalty
[
Baldi 2012
,
Kingma 2013
]
to encourage learning informative concepts.
Reconstruction can help prevent collapsing distinct ground-truth concepts into single learned concepts, e.g., in
MNIST-SumParity
the odd digits cannot be collapsed together without impairing reconstruction.
3
3

3

This is provably the case
context-style separation
i.e., assuming the concepts do not depend on the stylistic factor of the input like calligraphy
[
Marconato et al. 2023b
, Proposition 6]
.

Alternatively, one can employ
entropy maximization

[
Manhaeve et al. 2021
]
to spread concept activations evenly across the bottleneck.
This can be viewed as a less targeted but more efficient alternative to reconstruction, which becomes impractical for higher dimensional inputs.
Another popular option is
contrastive learning

[
Chen et al. 2020a
]
, in that augmentations render learned concepts more robust to style variations
[
Von Kügelgen et al. 2021
]
, e.g., for MNIST-based tasks it helps to cluster distinct digits in representation space
[
Sansone and Manhaeve 2023
]
.
Unsupervised strategies all implicitly counteract
simplicity bias
whereby
\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}
ends up collapsing.

Models.
We evaluate several (also NeSy) CBMs.

[

Computing

Concept collapse

We measure the quality of the learned inference layer

6
Related Work

Shortcuts in machine learning
. Shortcuts occur when machine learning models solve a prediction task by using features that correlate with but do not cause the desired output, leading to poor OOD behavior
[
Geirhos et al. 2020
,
Teso et al. 2023
,
Ye et al. 2024
,
Steinmann et al. 2024a
]
. The shortcuts literature however focuses on black-box models rather than CBMs.
Existing studies on the semantics of CBM concepts
[
Margeloiu et al. 2021
,
Mahinpei et al. 2021
,
Havasi et al. 2022
,
Raman et al. 2023
]
focus on individual failures but propose no well-defined, formal notion of intended semantics. One exception is
[
Marconato et al. 2023a
]
which however, like other works, ignores the role of the inference layer.
We build on known results on reasoning shortcuts
[
Li et al. 2023
,
Marconato et al. 2023b
,
Wang et al. 2023
,
Umili et al. 2024
]
which are restricted to NeSy-CBMs in which the prior knowledge is given and fixed. Our analysis upgrades these results to general CBMs. Furthermore, our simulations indicate that strategies that are effective for CBMs and RSs no longer work for JRSs.
At the same time, while NeSy approaches that learn the prior knowledge and concepts jointly are increasingly popular
[
Wang et al. 2019
,
Yang et al. 2020
,
Liu et al. 2023
,
Daniele et al. 2023
,
Tang and Ellis 2023
,
Wüst et al. 2024
]
, existing work neglects the issue of shortcuts in this setting. Our work partially fills this gap.

Relation to identifiability
. Works in Independent Component Analysis and Causal Representation Learning focus on the identifiability of representations up to an equivalence relation, typically up to permutations and rescaling
[
Khemakhem et al. 2020a
,
Gresele et al. 2020
,
von Kügelgen et al. 2024
]
or more general transformations
[
Roeder et al. 2021
,
Buchholz et al. 2022
]
.
The equivalence relation introduced along with intended semantics (
Definition

3.3
) establishes a specific connection between the maps
\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}
and
\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}
. This aligns with existing works that aim to identify representations and linear inference layers using supervised labels
[
Lachapelle et al. 2023
,
Fumero et al. 2023
,
Bing et al. 2023
]
.

Taeb et al. 2022
studied the identifiability of a VAE-based CBM with continuous-valued concepts. In contrast, we provide an analysis of a broader class of (NeSy) CBMs commonly used in practice.
Moreover, while prior works primarily focus on identifying continuous-valued representations, we examine the identifiability of
discrete
concepts
and
of the inference layer.

[
Rajendran et al. 2024
]
explores the identifiability of (potentially discrete) concepts linearly encoded in model representations but is limited to a multi-environment setting, not including supervised labels. Our results differ in that we show how concepts can be identified in CBMs by circumventing joint reasoning shortcuts.

7
Conclusion

We study the issue of learning CBMs and NeSy-CBMs with high-quality quality concepts and inference layers.
To this end, we formalize intended semantics and joint reasoning shortcuts (JRSs), show how reducing the number
deterministic
JRSs to zero provably prevents
all
JRSs, yielding identifiability, and investigate natural mitigation strategies.
Numerical simulations indicate that JRSs are very frequent and impactful, and that the only (partially) beneficial mitigation is contrastive learning.
Our analysis aims to pave the way to the design of effective solutions and therefore more robust and interpretable CBMs.

In future work, we plan to extend our theory to concept learning in general neural nets and large language models. Moreover, we will take a closer look at more complex mitigations such as techniques for smartly constraining the inference layer
[
Liu et al. 2023
,
Wüst et al. 2024
]
, debiasing
[
Bahadori and Heckerman 2021
]
, and interaction with human experts
[
Teso et al. 2023
]
.

A.1.1
MNIST-Add

MNIST-Add

[
Manhaeve et al. 2018
]
consists of pairs of
MNIST
digits
[
LeCun 1998
]
, ranging from
0
to
9
, and the goal is to predict their sum. The prior knowledge used for generating the labels is simply the rule of addition:
\mathsf{K}=(Y=C_{1}+C_{2})
. This task does not admit RSs nor JRSs when digits are processed separately. Below we report four examples:

(9)

All splits cover all possible pairs of concepts, from
(0,0)
to
(9,9)
, i.e., there is no sampling bias. Overall,
MNIST-Add
has
42,000
training examples,
12,000
validation examples, and
6,000
test examples.

A.2
MNIST-SumParity

Similarly, in
MNIST-SumParity
the goal is to predict the
parity
of the sum, i.e., the prior knowledge is the rule:
\mathsf{K}=(Y=(C_{1}+C_{2})\ \mathrm{mod}\ 2)
. This task is more challenging for our purposes, as it is affected by both RSs and JRSs, cf.
Fig.

1
. Below we report four examples:

\{((\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\includegraphics[width]{figures/mnist-0.png}},\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\includegraphics[width]{figures/mnist-1.png}}),1),\ ((\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\includegraphics[width]{figures/mnist-5.png}},\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\includegraphics[width]{figures/mnist-8.png}}),1),\ ((\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\includegraphics[width]{figures/mnist-6.png}},\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\includegraphics[width]{figures/mnist-6.png}}),0),\ ((\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\includegraphics[width]{figures/mnist-4.png}},\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\includegraphics[width]{figures/mnist-1.png}}),1)\}

(10)

We consider two variants: like
MNIST-Add
, in
MNIST-SumParity
proper the training and test splits contain all possible combinations of digits, while in
biased

MNIST-SumParity
the training set contains (even, even), (odd, odd) and (odd, even) pairs, while the test set contains (even, odd) pairs. This fools CBMs into learning an incorrect inference layer implementing subtraction instead of addition-and-parity, as illustrated in
Fig.

1
. Both variants have the same number of training, validation, and test examples as
MNIST-Add
.

Preprocessing
. All CBMs process objects separately. Following
[
Shindo et al. 2023
]
, we fine-tune a pretrained
YoloV11
model
[
Jocher et al. 2023
]
(for
10
epochs, random seed
13
) on a subset of the training set’s bounding boxes, and use it to predict bounding boxes for all images. For each training and test image, we concatenate the regions in the bounding boxes, obtaining a list
2
to
4
images which plays the role of input for our CBMs.

Each object has
8\times 3\times 2\times 2=96
possible configurations of concept values, and we handle between
2
and
4
objects, hence the inference layer has to model
96^{2}+96^{3}+96^{4}
distinct possibilities. Due to the astronomical number of possibilities (which would entail a huge truth table/inference layer for
DSL
and related CBMs), we split the inference layer in two: we first predict individual concepts, and then we aggregate them (into, e.g.,
large-cube
and
small-yellow-sphere
) and use these for prediction. Despite this simplification,
Clevr
still induces JRSs in learned CBMs.

A.4
Metrics

For all models, we report the metrics averaged over
five random seeds
.

Label quality
. We measure the macro average
F_{1}
-score and Accuracy, to account for class imbalance.

Concept quality
. We compute concept quality via Accuracy and
F_{1}
-score. However, the order of ground-truth and predicted concepts might might differ, e.g., in
Clevr

G_{1}
might represent whether “color = red”, while
C_{1}
whether “color = blue”. Therefore, in order to compute these metrics we have to first align them using the Hungarian matching algorithm using concept-wise Pearson correlation on the test data
[
Kuhn 1955
]
. The algorithm computes the correlation matrix
R
between
\bm{\mathrm{g}}
and
\bm{\mathrm{c}}
, i.e.,
R_{ij}=\mathrm{corr}(G_{i},C_{j})
where
\mathrm{corr}
is the correlation coefficient, and then infers a permutation
\pi
between concept values (e.g., mapping ground-truth color “red” to predicted value “blue”) that maximizes the above objective. The metrics are computed by comparing the reordered ground-truth annotations with the predicted concepts.

Inference layer quality
. The same permutation is also used to assess knowledge quality. Specifically, we apply it to reorder the ground-truth concepts and then feed these to the learned inference layer, evaluating the accuracy and
F_{1}
score of the resulting predictions.

For
MNIST-Add
and
MNIST-SumParity
, we conducted an exhaustive evaluation since the number of possible combinations to supervise is
100
. The results in
Table

1
and
Table

2
evaluate the knowledge exhaustively, whereas in
Table

4
, we separately assess the knowledge of in-distribution and out-of-distribution combinations.

In
Clevr
, as discussed in
Section

A.3
, there are too many combinations of concept values to possibly evaluate them all. Therefore, both supervision and evaluation are performed by randomly sampling
100
combinations of ground-truth concepts.

Details of
CBNM
. For
Clevr
, we used a standard linear layer. Since this is not expressive enough for
MNIST-Add
and
MNIST-SumParity
, we replaced it with an interpretable second-degree polynomial layer, i.e., it associates weights to
combinations of two predicted digits
rather than individual digits. We also include a small entropy term, which empirically facilitates data fit when little or no concept supervision is available.

Details of
SENN
.
SENN

[
Alvarez-Melis and Jaakkola 2018
]
are unsupervised concept-based models consisting of a neural network that produces a distribution over concepts, a neural network that assigns a score to those concepts, and a decoder. The final prediction is made by computing the dot product between the predicted concepts and their predicted scores.
SENN
by default includes a reconstruction penalty, a robustness loss, and a sparsity term.

In our implementation, we focus only on the reconstruction penalty. The robustness loss, which aims to make the relevance scores Lipschitz continuous, requires computing Jacobians, making the training of those models infeasible in terms of computational time and resources. Additionally, we did not implement the sparsity term, as it conflicts with the quality of concepts we aim to study.

Details of
bears
∗
.
bears
∗
combines
DSL

[
Daniele et al. 2023
]
and BEARS
[
Marconato et al. 2024
]
.
BEARS is a NeSy architectures based on deep ensembles
[
Lakshminarayanan et al. 2017
]
, designed to improve the calibration of NeSy models in the presence of RSs and provide uncertainty estimation for concepts that may be affected by such RSs and it assumes to be given prior knowledge.

Since in our setup the inference layer is learned, we built an ensemble with one learnable inference layer as in
DSL
and multiple concept extractors. The first element of the ensemble learns both the concepts and the inference layer. Then, we freeze it and train the other concept extractors using the frozen inference layer for prediction. In this sense,
bears
∗
provides awareness of the reasoning shortcuts present in the knowledge learned by the first model.

A.6
Mitigation Strategies

Concept supervision
. When evaluating mitigation strategies, concept supervision for
MNIST-SumParity
was incrementally supplied for two more concepts at each step, following this order: 3, 4, 1, 0, 2, 8, 9, 5, 6, 7. For
Clevr
, supervision was specifically provided for sizes, shapes, materials, and colors, in this order.

Knowledge distillation
. This was implemented using a cross-entropy on the inference layer. For
DSL
and
DPL
, ground-truth concepts were fed into the learned truth table, expecting the correct label. Similarly, for
CBNM
s, which use a linear layer, the same approach was applied. In evaluating the mitigation strategies, we supervised all possible combinations for
MNIST-Add
, whereas for
Clevr
, we supervised a maximum of
500
randomly sampled combinations.

Entropy maximization
. The entropy regularization term follows the implementation in
[
Marconato et al. 2023b
]
. The loss is defined as
1-\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}H_{m_{i}}[p_{\theta}(c_{i})]

where
p_{\theta}(C)
represents the marginal distribution over the concepts, and
H_{m_{i}}
is the normalized Shannon entropy over
m_{i}
possible values of the distribution.

Reconstruction
. The reconstruction penalty is the same as in
[
Marconato et al. 2023b
]
. In this case, the concept extractor outputs both concepts
\bm{\mathrm{c}}
and style variables
\bm{\mathrm{z}}
, i.e., it implements a distribution
p_{\theta}(c,z|x)=p_{\theta}(c|x)\cdot p_{\theta}(z|x)
. The reconstruction penalty is defined as:
-\mathbb{E}_{(c,z)\sim p_{\theta}(c,z|x)}\left[\log p_{\psi}(x|c,z)\right]
where
p_{\psi}(x|c,z)
represents the decoder network.

Contrastive loss
. We implemented contrastive learning using the InfoNCE loss, comparing the predicted
concept distribution
of the current batch with that of its respective augmentations. Following the recommendations of
Chen et al. 2020a
, we applied the following augmentations to each batch: random cropping (20 pixels for
MNIST
and 125 pixels for
Clevr
), followed by resizing, color jittering, and Gaussian blur.

Appendix C
Theoretical Material

In this section, we start by summarizing the prerequisite material. We begin by recalling a central Lemma proven in
[
Marconato et al. 2023b
]
that allows us to write the condition for likelihood-optimal models in terms of
\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}
.
To ease of comparison,
let
p(\bm{\mathrm{C}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{X}}):=\bm{\mathrm{f}}(\bm{\mathrm{X}})
and
p(\bm{\mathrm{Y}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{X}}):=(\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}\circ\bm{\mathrm{f}})(\bm{\mathrm{X}})
denote the conditional distributions defined by the CBM
(\bm{\mathrm{f}},\bm{\mathrm{\omega}})\in\mathcal{F}\times\Omega
, and:

p(\bm{\mathrm{Y}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{G}}):=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\mathrm{x}}\sim p^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{X}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{G}})}[p(\bm{\mathrm{Y}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{x}})]

(11)

Also, we consider the following joint distributions:

p^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{X}},\bm{\mathrm{Y}})=p^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{Y}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{X}})p^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{X}}),\quad p(\bm{\mathrm{X}},\bm{\mathrm{Y}})=p(\bm{\mathrm{Y}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{X}})p^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{X}})

(12)

Lemma C.1

(
[
Marconato et al. 2023b
]
)
.

It holds that:
(i) The true risk of
p
can be upper bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\textstyle\mathbb{E}_{(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{y}})\sim p^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{X}},\bm{\mathrm{Y}})}[\log p(\bm{\mathrm{y}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{x}})]

\displaystyle=-\mathsf{KL}[p^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{X}};\bm{\mathrm{Y}})\ \|\ p(\bm{\mathrm{X}},\bm{\mathrm{Y}})]-H[p^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{X}},\bm{\mathrm{Y}})]]

(13)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\mathrm{g}}\sim p(\bm{\mathrm{G}})}\big(-\mathsf{KL}[p^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{Y}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{g}};\mathsf{K})\ \|\ p(\bm{\mathrm{Y}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{g}})]-H[p^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{Y}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{g}};\mathsf{K})]\big)

(14)

where

\mathsf{KL}
is the Kullback-Leibler divergence and H is the Shannon entropy. Moreover, under
3.1
and
3.2
,
p(\bm{\mathrm{Y}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{X}};\mathsf{K})
is an optimum of the LHS of
Eq.

14
if and only if
p(\bm{\mathrm{Y}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{G}})
is an optimum of the RHS.
(ii) There exists a bijection between the deterministic concept distributions
p(\bm{\mathrm{C}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{X}})
that, for each
\bm{\mathrm{g}}\in\mathrm{supp}(\bm{\mathrm{G}})
, are constant over the support of
p^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{X}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{g}})
apart for zero-measure subspaces
\mathcal{X}^{0}\subset\mathsf{supp}{(p^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{X}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{g}}))}

and the deterministic distributions of the form
p(\bm{\mathrm{C}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{G}})
.

Here, point (i) connects the optima of the likelihood
p(\bm{\mathrm{Y}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{X}})
and the optima of
p(\bm{\mathrm{Y}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{G}})
. This implies that, under
3.1
and
3.2
, knowing the optima of
p(\bm{\mathrm{Y}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{G}})
informs us about optimal CBMs
p(\bm{\mathrm{Y}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{X}})
.
Notice that, to a single
\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}(\bm{\mathrm{G}})=p(\bm{\mathrm{C}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{G}})
might correspond to multiple choices of
\bm{\mathrm{f}}\in\mathcal{F}
. The choice is unique if, by point (ii), we consider deterministic
p(\bm{\mathrm{C}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{G}})
showing that they are into one-to-one correspondence with
p(\bm{\mathrm{C}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{X}})
that are almost constant in the support.

Limiting to those
\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}
’s in point (ii), we can find a simple way to describe optima for
p(\bm{\mathrm{Y}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{G}})
.

Lemma C.2

(Deterministic optima of the likelihood)
.

Under
3.1
and
3.2
,
for all CBMs
(\bm{\mathrm{f}},\bm{\mathrm{\omega}})\in\mathcal{F}\times\Omega
where
\bm{\mathrm{f}}:\bm{\mathrm{x}}\mapsto\bm{\mathrm{c}}
is constant over the support of
p^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{X}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{g}})
apart for a zero-measure subspace
\mathcal{X}^{0}\subset\mathsf{supp}{(p^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{X}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{g}}))}
, for all
\bm{\mathrm{g}}\in\mathcal{G}
, it holds that the optima of the likelihood for
p(\bm{\mathrm{Y}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{G}})
are obtained when:

\forall\bm{\mathrm{g}}\in\mathsf{supp}(p^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{G}})),\;(\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}\circ\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}})(\bm{\mathrm{g}})=\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{g}})

(15)

where
\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}(\bm{\mathrm{g}}):=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\mathrm{x}}\sim p^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{X}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{g}})}[\bm{\mathrm{f}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})]\in\mathsf{Vert}(\mathcal{A})
and
\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}(\bm{\mathrm{c}}):=\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}{(\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}\})}\in\mathsf{Vert}(\mathcal{B})
.

Proof.

Notice that by
Lemma

C.1
, it holds that
\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}\in\mathsf{Vert}(\mathcal{A})
is a deterministic conditional distribution.
Since
\bm{\mathrm{f}}:\bm{\mathrm{x}}\to\bm{\mathrm{c}}
is constant over all
\bm{\mathrm{x}}\sim p^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{X}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{g}})
for a fixed
\bm{\mathrm{g}}\in\mathcal{G}
,
it holds that
\bm{\mathrm{f}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})=\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}(\bm{\mathrm{g}})

apart for some
\bm{\mathrm{x}}
in a measure-zero subspace
\mathcal{X}^{0}\subset\mathsf{supp}{(p^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{X}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{g}}))}
,
therefore we get:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\mathrm{x}}\sim p^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{X}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{g}})}[\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}(\bm{\mathrm{f}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))]

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}(\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}(\bm{\mathrm{g}}))

(16)

\displaystyle=(\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}\circ\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}})(\bm{\mathrm{g}})

(17)

where in the last line we substituted
\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}
with
\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}
since
\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}
is deterministic. For
p(\bm{\mathrm{Y}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{G}})
, the necessary and sufficient condition for maximum log-likelihood is:

\forall\bm{\mathrm{g}}\in\mathsf{supp}(p^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{G}})),\;p(\bm{\mathrm{Y}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{g}})=\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{g}})

(18)

and substituting
Eq.

17
we get:

\forall\bm{\mathrm{g}}\in\mathsf{supp}(p^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{G}})),\;(\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}\circ\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}})(\bm{\mathrm{g}})=\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{g}})

(19)

showing the claim.
∎

C.1
Equivalence relation given by Definition
3.3

We start by proving that
Definition

3.3
defines an equivalence relation.

Proposition C.3
.

Definition

3.3
defines an equivalence relation (denoted as
\sim
) between couples
(\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}},\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}),(\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}^{\prime},\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B}
, where
(\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}},\bm{\mathrm{\beta}})\sim(\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}^{\prime},\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{\prime})
if there exist a permutation
\pi:[k]\to[k]
and
k
element-wise invertible functions
\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{k}
such that:

\displaystyle\forall\bm{\mathrm{g}}\in\mathcal{G},\;\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}(\bm{\mathrm{g}})=(\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi}\circ\bm{\mathrm{\psi}}\circ\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}^{\prime})(\bm{\mathrm{g}})

(20)

\displaystyle\forall\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in\mathcal{C},\;\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}(\bm{\mathrm{c}})=(\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{\prime}\circ\bm{\mathrm{\psi}}^{-1}\circ\bm{\mathrm{P}}^{-1}_{\pi})(\bm{\mathrm{c}})

(21)

where
\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi}:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C}
is the permutation matrix induced by
\pi
and
\bm{\mathrm{\psi}}(\bm{\mathrm{c}}):=(\psi_{1}(c_{1}),\ldots,\psi_{k}(c_{k}))
.

Proof.

To the aim of the proof, it is useful to analyze how
\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi}
and
\bm{\mathrm{\psi}}
relate. Consider for any
\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in\mathcal{C}
:

\displaystyle(\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi}\circ\bm{\mathrm{\psi}})(\bm{\mathrm{c}})

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi}(\psi_{1}(c_{1}),\ldots,\psi_{k}(c_{k}))

(22)

\displaystyle=(\psi_{\pi(1)}(c_{\pi(1)}),\ldots,\psi_{\pi(k)}(c_{\pi(k)}))

(23)

Denoting with
\bm{\mathrm{\psi}}_{\pi}(\bm{\mathrm{c}}):=(\psi_{\pi(1)}(c_{1}),\ldots,\psi_{\pi(k)}(c_{k}))
, we have that:

(\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi}\circ\bm{\mathrm{\psi}})(\bm{\mathrm{c}})=(\bm{\mathrm{\psi}}_{\pi}\circ\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi})(\bm{\mathrm{c}})

(24)

From this expression, notice it holds for all
\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in\mathcal{C}
:

\displaystyle(\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi}\circ\bm{\mathrm{\psi}})(\bm{\mathrm{c}})

\displaystyle=(\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi}\circ\bm{\mathrm{\psi}}\circ\bm{\mathrm{P}}^{-1}_{\pi}\circ\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi})(\bm{\mathrm{c}})

(25)

\displaystyle(\bm{\mathrm{\psi}}_{\pi}\circ\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi})(\bm{\mathrm{c}})

\displaystyle=\big((\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi}\circ\bm{\mathrm{\psi}}\circ\bm{\mathrm{P}}^{-1}_{\pi})\circ\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi}\big)(\bm{\mathrm{c}})

(26)

so that we can equivalently write
\bm{\mathrm{\psi}}_{\pi}:=\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi}\circ\bm{\mathrm{\psi}}\circ\bm{\mathrm{P}}^{-1}_{\pi}
.

Reflexivity
. This follows by choosing
\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi}=\mathrm{id}
and similarly
\psi_{i}=\mathrm{id}
for all
i\in[k]
.

Symmetry
. We have to prove that
(\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}},\bm{\mathrm{\beta}})\sim(\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}^{\prime},\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{\prime})\implies(\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}^{\prime},\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{\prime})\sim(\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}},\bm{\mathrm{\beta}})
. To this end, we start by taking the expression for
\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}
in terms of
\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}^{\prime}
. Consider for any
\bm{\mathrm{g}}\in\mathcal{G}
:

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}(\bm{\mathrm{g}})

\displaystyle=(\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi}\circ\bm{\mathrm{\psi}}\circ\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}^{\prime})(\bm{\mathrm{g}})

(27)

\displaystyle=(\bm{\mathrm{\psi}}_{\pi}\circ\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi}\circ\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}^{\prime})(\bm{\mathrm{g}})

(28)

where in the last step we used
Eq.

24
. By first inverting
\bm{\mathrm{\psi}}_{\pi}
and then
\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi}
, we obtain that:

\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}^{\prime}(\bm{\mathrm{g}})=(\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi}^{-1}\circ\bm{\mathrm{\psi}}_{\pi}^{-1}\circ\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}})(\bm{\mathrm{g}})

(29)

showing the reflexivity of
\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}
. With similar steps, we can show that a similar relation also holds for
\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{\prime}
, that is for all
\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in\mathcal{C}
:

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{\prime}(\bm{\mathrm{c}})=(\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}\circ\bm{\mathrm{\psi}}_{\pi}\circ\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi})(\bm{\mathrm{c}})

(30)

Transitivity
. We have to prove that if
(\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}},\bm{\mathrm{\beta}})\sim(\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}^{\prime},\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{\prime})
and
(\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}^{\prime},\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{\prime})\sim(\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}^{\dagger},\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{\dagger})
then also
(\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}},\bm{\mathrm{\beta}})\sim(\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}^{\dagger},\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{\dagger})
. We start from the expression of
\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}
and
\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}^{\prime}
, where we have that
\forall\bm{\mathrm{g}}\in\mathcal{G}
:

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}(\bm{\mathrm{g}})

\displaystyle=(\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi}\circ\bm{\mathrm{\psi}}\circ\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}^{\prime})(\bm{\mathrm{g}})

(31)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}^{\prime}(\bm{\mathrm{g}})

\displaystyle=(\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi^{\prime}}\circ\bm{\mathrm{\psi}}^{\prime}\circ\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}^{\dagger})(\bm{\mathrm{g}})

(32)

We proceed by substituting the expression of
\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}^{\prime}
in
\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}
to obtain:

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}(\bm{\mathrm{g}})

\displaystyle=(\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi}\circ\bm{\mathrm{\psi}}\circ\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi^{\prime}}\circ\bm{\mathrm{\psi}}^{\prime}\circ\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}^{\dagger})(\bm{\mathrm{g}})

(33)

\displaystyle=(\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi}\circ\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi^{\prime}}\circ\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi^{\prime}}^{-1}\circ\bm{\mathrm{\psi}}\circ\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi^{\prime}}\circ\bm{\mathrm{\psi}}^{\prime}\circ\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}^{\dagger})(\bm{\mathrm{g}})

(Composing
\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi}
with the identity
\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi^{\prime}}\circ\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi^{\prime}}^{-1}
)

\displaystyle=(\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi}\circ\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi^{\prime}}\circ\bm{\mathrm{\psi}}_{{\pi^{\prime}}^{-1}}\circ\bm{\mathrm{\psi}}^{\prime}\circ\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}^{\dagger})(\bm{\mathrm{g}})

(Using that
\bm{\mathrm{\psi}}_{{\pi^{\prime}}^{-1}}:=\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi^{\prime}}^{-1}\circ\bm{\mathrm{\psi}}\circ\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi^{\prime}}
.)

\displaystyle=(\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi^{\dagger}}\circ\bm{\mathrm{\psi}}^{\dagger}\circ\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}^{\dagger})(\bm{\mathrm{g}})

(34)

where we defined
\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi^{\dagger}}:=\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi}\circ\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi^{\prime}}
and
\bm{\mathrm{\psi}}^{\dagger}:=\bm{\mathrm{\psi}}_{{\pi^{\prime}}^{-1}}\circ\bm{\mathrm{\psi}}^{\prime}
.
With similar steps, we obtain that
\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}
can be related to
\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{\dagger}
as:

\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}(\bm{\mathrm{c}})=(\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{\dagger}\circ{\bm{\mathrm{\psi}}^{\dagger}}^{-1}\circ\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi^{\dagger}}^{-1})(\bm{\mathrm{c}})

(35)

for all
\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in\mathcal{C}
. This shows the equivalence relation of
Definition

3.3
.
∎

We now prove how solutions with intended semantics (
Definition

3.3
) relate to the optima of the log-likelihood:

Lemma C.4

(Intended semantics entails optimal models)
.

If a pair
(\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}},\bm{\mathrm{\beta}})\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B}
possesses the intended semantics (
Definition

3.3
), it holds:

\forall\bm{\mathrm{g}}\in\mathcal{G},\;(\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}\circ\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}})(\bm{\mathrm{g}})=\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{g}})

(36)

Proof.

We make use of the fact that by
Definition

3.3
it holds:

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}(\bm{\mathrm{g}})

\displaystyle=(\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi}\circ\bm{\mathrm{\psi}}\circ\mathrm{id})(\bm{\mathrm{g}})

(37)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}(\bm{\mathrm{c}})

\displaystyle=(\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{*}\circ\bm{\mathrm{\psi}}^{-1}\circ\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi}^{-1})(\bm{\mathrm{c}})

(38)

Therefore, combining
Eqs.

37
and
38
we get for all
\bm{\mathrm{g}}\in\mathcal{G}
:

\displaystyle(\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}\circ\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}})(\bm{\mathrm{g}})

\displaystyle=(\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{*}\circ\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi}^{-1}\circ\bm{\mathrm{\psi}}^{-1}\circ\bm{\mathrm{\psi}}\circ\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi})(\bm{\mathrm{g}})

(39)

\displaystyle=(\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{*}\circ\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi}^{-1}\circ\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\pi})(\bm{\mathrm{g}})

(40)

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{g}})

(41)

yielding the claim.
∎

C.2
Proof of Theorem
3.5
and Corollary
3.6

Theorem C.5
.

Let
s\mathcal{G}:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}\{|\mathcal{G}_{i}|\}
be the set of cardinalities of each concept
G_{i}\subseteq\bm{\mathrm{G}}
and
ms\mathcal{G}
denote the multi-set

ms\mathcal{G}:=\{(|\mathcal{G}_{i}|,m(|\mathcal{G}_{i}|),\;i\in[k]\}
, where
m(\bullet)
denotes the multiplicity of each element of
s\mathcal{G}
.
Under
3.1
and
3.2
, the number of deterministic JRSs
(\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}},\bm{\mathrm{\beta}})\in\mathsf{Vert}(\mathcal{A})\times\mathsf{Vert}(\mathcal{B})
amounts to:

\displaystyle\sum_{(\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}},\bm{\mathrm{\beta}})\in\mathsf{Vert}(\mathcal{A})\times\mathsf{Vert}(\mathcal{B})}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bigwedge_{\bm{\mathrm{g}}\in\mathrm{supp}(\bm{\mathrm{G}})}(\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}\circ\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}})(\bm{\mathrm{g}})=\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{g}})\}-C[\mathcal{G}]

(42)

where
C[\mathcal{G}]
is the total number of pairs with the intended semantics, given by

C[\mathcal{G}]:=\prod_{\xi\in s\mathcal{G}}m(\xi)!\times\prod_{i=1}^{k}|\mathcal{G}_{i}|!
.

Proof.

Since we are considering pairs
(\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}},\bm{\mathrm{\beta}})\in\mathsf{Vert}(\mathcal{A})\times\mathsf{Vert}(\mathcal{B})
, we can stick to the fact that
\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}
defines a function
\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}:\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{C}
and that, similarly,
\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}
defines a function
\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{Y}
. In this case,
\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}\circ\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}:\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{Y}
is a map from ground-truth concepts to labels.
We start by
Definition

3.3
and consider the pairs that attain maximum likelihood by
Lemma

C.2
:

\forall\bm{\mathrm{g}}\in\mathsf{supp}(p^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{G}})),\;(\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}\circ\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}})(\bm{\mathrm{g}})=\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{g}})

(43)

where, since
\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}\in\mathsf{Vert}(\mathcal{A})
and is deterministic, we can substitute
\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}
with
\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}
. Since both
\mathsf{Vert}(\mathcal{A})
and
\mathsf{Vert}(\mathcal{B})
are countable, we can convert this to a total count of pairs that attain maximum likelihood:

\sum_{(\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}},\bm{\mathrm{\beta}})\in\mathsf{Vert}(\mathcal{A})\times\mathsf{Vert}(\mathcal{B})}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bigwedge_{\bm{\mathrm{g}}\in\mathsf{supp}(p^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{G}}))}(\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}\circ\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}})(\bm{\mathrm{g}})=\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{g}})\}

(44)

where, for each pair
(\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}},\bm{\mathrm{\beta}})\in\mathsf{Vert}(\mathcal{A})\times\mathsf{Vert}(\mathcal{B})
the sum increases by one if the condition of
Eq.

43
is satisfied.

Among these optimal pairs, there are those possessing the intended semantics by
Lemma

C.4
, which consists of possible permutations of the concepts and element-wise invertible transformations of the concept values. We start by evaluating the number of possible element-wise invertible transformations for each subset of concepts
\mathcal{G}_{i}\subset\mathcal{G}
.

Each concept
c_{i}
has values in the subset
\mathcal{G}_{i}
, for a total of
|\mathcal{G}_{i}|
values. The overall number of possible invertible transformations is then given by the number of possible permutations, resulting in a total of
|\mathcal{G}_{i}|!
maps.
This means that the total number of element-wise invertible transformations is given by:

\prod_{i=1}^{k}|\mathcal{G}_{i}|!

(45)

Next, we consider what permutation of the concepts are possible. To this end, consider the set and the multi-set comprising all different
\mathcal{G}_{i}
cardinalities given by:

s\mathcal{G}:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}\{|\mathcal{G}_{i}|\},\quad ms\mathcal{G}:=\{(|\mathcal{G}_{i}|,m(|\mathcal{G}_{i}|):\;i\in[k]\}

(46)

where
m(\bullet)
counts how many repetitions are present.
When the multi-set contains repeated numbers, i.e., when different
c_{i}
and

The count reduction was studied in

Marconato et al. 2023b
]

D.3
Knowledge distillation

We consider the case where samples
(\bm{\mathrm{g}},\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{g}}))
are used to distill the ground-truth knowledge. Since by
3.2
the ground-truth inference layer is deterministic, we consider the following objective for distillation. Given a subset

the original log-likelihood objective is complemented with the following cross-entropy:

\textstyle\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{g}}\in\mathcal{G}^{K}}\log\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}(\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{g}}\})_{\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{g}})}

(65)

i.e., the
\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{g}})

\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}
is increased for each
\bm{\mathrm{g}}\in\mathcal{G}^{K}

Turned to a constraint on
\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}
this can be written as follows:

\textstyle\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bigwedge_{\bm{\mathrm{g}}\in\mathcal{G}^{K}}\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}(\bm{\mathrm{g}})=\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{g}})\}

(66)

When
\mathcal{G}^{K}=\mathcal{G}
it necessarily holds that
\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}\equiv\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{*}

D.4
Reconstruction penalty

When focusing on reconstructing the input from the bottleneck, the probability distributions
p(\bm{\mathrm{C}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{X}})
may not carry enough information to completely determine the input. In fact, if
\bm{\mathrm{X}}
depends also on stylistic variables
\bm{\mathrm{S}}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
, it becomes impossible solely from
\bm{\mathrm{G}}
to determine the input.
This means that training a decoder only passing
\bm{\mathrm{c}}\sim p(\bm{\mathrm{C}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{X}})
would not convey enough information and would reduce the benefits of the reconstruction term.

Marconato et al. 2023b
have studied this setting and proposed to also include additional variables
\bm{\mathrm{Z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
in the bottleneck to overcome this problem.
The encoder/decoder architecture works as follows: first the input
\bm{\mathrm{x}}
is encoded into
p(\bm{\mathrm{C}},\bm{\mathrm{Z}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{x}})
by the model and after sampling
(\bm{\mathrm{c}},\bm{\mathrm{z}})\sim p(\bm{\mathrm{C}},\bm{\mathrm{Z}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{x}})
the decoder
\bm{\mathrm{d}}
reconstruct the an input image
\hat{}\bm{\mathrm{x}}=\bm{\mathrm{d}}(\bm{\mathrm{c}},\bm{\mathrm{z}})
.
Following, under the assumption of
content-style separation
, i.e., both the encoder and the decoder process independently the concept and the stylistic variables, it holds that the constraint fr maps
\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}\in\mathsf{Vert}(\mathcal{A})
given by the reconstruction penalty result in:

\textstyle\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bigwedge_{\bm{\mathrm{g}},\bm{\mathrm{g}}^{\prime}\in\mathsf{supp}(\bm{\mathrm{G}}):\bm{\mathrm{g}}\neq\bm{\mathrm{g}}^{\prime}}\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}(\bm{\mathrm{g}})\neq\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}(\bm{\mathrm{g}}^{\prime})\}

(67)

The proof for this can be found in
[
Marconato et al. 2023b
, Proposition 6]
. Notice that, with full support over
\mathcal{G}
, the only such maps
\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}
must not confuse one concept vector for another, i.e., at most there are at most
|\mathcal{G}|!
different valid
\bm{\mathrm{\alpha}}
’s that are essentially a bijection from
\mathcal{G}
to
\mathcal{C}
.

(80)

where the equality holds if and only if
\argmax_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}}\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}(\bm{\mathrm{c}})_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}=\argmax_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}}\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}(\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{\prime})_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}
and
\max_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}}\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}(\bm{\mathrm{c}})_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}=\max_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}}\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}(\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{\prime})_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}
.
This proves that probabilistic logic methods satisfy
3.7
.

Concept Bottleneck Models
. We consider
CBNM
s implementing a linear layer as
\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}:\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}\to\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}
, as customary
[
Koh et al. 2020
]
. We consider the general case where the linear layer consists of a matrix
\bm{\mathrm{W}}\in\mathbb{R}^{a\times b}
with
a:=|\mathcal{G}|
rows and
b:=|\mathcal{Y}|
columns. In this setting, a probability distribution
p(\bm{\mathrm{C}})\in\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}
corresponds to the input vector that is passed to the inference layer
\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}
, implemented by the linear layer
\bm{\mathrm{W}}
and a softmax operator.
We denote with
\bm{\mathrm{w}}^{y}_{c}:=\bm{\mathrm{W}}_{c,y}
, corresponding to the
(c,y)
component of the matrix.
The index
c
(resp.
y
) also correspond to the concept vector
\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in\mathcal{C}
(resp.
\bm{\mathrm{y}}
), associated to it, e.g.,
\bm{\mathrm{c}}=(0,1)^{\top}\in\{0,1\}^{2}
corresponds to
c=1
. Following, we just use
\bm{\mathrm{c}}
and
\bm{\mathrm{y}}
to denote their corresponding index of the concepts and of the labels.
Provided a concept probability vector
\bm{\mathrm{p}}\in\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}
, with components
\bm{\mathrm{p}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}}=p(\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}})
, the conditional distribution over labels is given by the softmax operator:

\displaystyle p(\bm{\mathrm{y}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{c}})

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}(\bm{\mathrm{p}})_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}

(81)

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp\big(\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in\mathcal{C}}\bm{\mathrm{W}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}},\bm{\mathrm{y}}}\bm{\mathrm{p}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}}\big)}{\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}}\exp\big(\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in\mathcal{C}}\bm{\mathrm{W}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}},\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{\prime}}\bm{\mathrm{p}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}}\big)}

(82)

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp\big(\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in\mathcal{C}}\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}\bm{\mathrm{p}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}}\big)}{\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}}\exp\big(\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in\mathcal{C}}\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{\prime}}\bm{\mathrm{p}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}}\big)}

(83)

In general, for any random choice of weights
\bm{\mathrm{W}}
, the
CBNM
may not satisfy
3.7
.
Notice also that,
because of the form of
\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}
, the
CBNM
cannot express deterministic
\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}\in\mathcal{B}
.
We consider the particular case associated with optimal models expressing an almost deterministic conditional distribution
\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}\in{\mathcal{B}}
, where a subset of weights
\bm{\mathrm{w}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}}
is extremely high or low.
Notice that this is relevant for models in the context of
3.2
, where
\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}^{*}\in\mathsf{Vert}(\mathcal{B})
.
We can see that in order for this to happen, we need the function
\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}
to be peaked on concept vectors
\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in\mathcal{C}
. This can happen only when the magnitude of one
\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}
is much higher than other
\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{\prime}}
. Hence, we formulate the following condition for
CBNM
:

Definition E.1
.

Consider a
CBNM

(\bm{\mathrm{f}},\bm{\mathrm{\omega}})\in\mathcal{F}\times\Omega
with weights
\bm{\mathrm{W}}=\{\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}\}
and
M>|\mathcal{Y}-1|
.
We say that the
CBNM
is
\log(M)
-deterministic at the vertices if for all
\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in\mathcal{C}
, there exists
\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}
such that:

\displaystyle\forall\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{\prime}\neq\bm{\mathrm{y}},\;\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}-\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{\prime}}\geq\log M

(84)

\displaystyle\forall\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{\prime}\neq\bm{\mathrm{y}}\neq\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{\prime\prime},\;|\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{\prime}}-\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{\prime\prime}}|\leq\log M

(85)

To see why this is helpful, we show that this constrained version of
CBNM
can flexibly approximate deterministic
\bm{\mathrm{\beta}}
’s:

Proposition E.2
.

Consider a
\log(M)
-deterministic
CBNM

(\bm{\mathrm{f}},\bm{\mathrm{\omega}})\in\mathcal{F}\times\Omega
. It holds:

\forall\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in\mathcal{C},\;\max_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}}\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}(\bm{\mathrm{c}})_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}\geq\frac{1}{1+(|\mathcal{Y}|-1)/M}

(86)

In particular, it holds that:

\forall\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in\mathcal{C},\;\lim_{M\to+\infty}\max_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}(\bm{\mathrm{c}})_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}=1

(87)

Proof.

For
\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in\mathcal{C}
, let
\bm{\mathrm{y}}=\argmax_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{\prime}}\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}(\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}\})_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{\prime}}
. We consider the expression:

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}(\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}\})

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}}{\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\exp\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{\prime}}}

(88)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{\prime}\neq\bm{\mathrm{y}}}\exp(-\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}+\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{\prime}})}

(89)

We now make use of the fact that the
CBNM
is
\log M
-deterministic and use
Eq.

84
to get:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{\prime}\neq\bm{\mathrm{y}}}\exp(-\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}+\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{\prime}})}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{1+\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{\prime}\neq\bm{\mathrm{y}}}\exp-\log M}

(90)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{\prime}\neq\bm{\mathrm{y}}}\frac{1}{M}}

(91)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+(|\mathcal{Y}-1|)/{M}}

(92)

giving overall that:

\max_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}}\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}(\bm{\mathrm{c}})_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}\geq\frac{1}{1+(|\mathcal{Y}|-1)/M}

(93)

Now, consider the limit for large
M\in\mathbb{R}
:

\lim_{M\to+\infty}\max_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}}\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}(\bm{\mathrm{c}})_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}\geq\lim_{M\to+\infty}\frac{1}{1+(|\mathcal{Y}|-1)/M}=1

(94)

This concludes the proof.
∎

The last point of
Proposition

E.2
shows a viable way to get peakaed label distributions from a
\log(M)
-deterministic
CBNM
. This limit guaranteed that such constrained
CBNM
s can get closer to achieve optimal likelihood.
We are now ready to prove that a
\log(M)
-deterministic
CBNM
respect
3.7
.

Proposition E.3
.

A
CBNM

(\bm{\mathrm{f}},\bm{\mathrm{\omega}})\in\mathcal{F}\times\Omega
that is
\log(M)
-deterministic (
Definition

E.1
)
satisfies
3.7
, i.e., for all
\lambda\in(0,1)

and for all
\bm{\mathrm{c}}\neq\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{\prime}
such that

\argmax_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}}\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}(\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}\})_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}\neq\argmax_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}}\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}(\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{\prime}\})_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}
, it holds
:

\max_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}}\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}(\lambda\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+(1-\lambda)\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}<\max\big(\max_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}}\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}(\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\})_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}},\max_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}}\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}(\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}\big)

(95)

Proof.

Let
\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}:=\argmax_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}}\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}(\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\})_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}
and
\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{2}:=\argmax_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}}\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}(\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}
and
\lambda_{1}:=\lambda
and
\lambda_{2}:=1-\lambda
, with
\lambda\in(0,1)
and
\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}\neq\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{2}
.
The proof is divided in two steps.

We first check that (1) the values taken by
\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}(\lambda\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+(1-\lambda)\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{i}}
, for
i\in\{1,2\},
are lower than those taken at the extremes.
The explicit expression for
\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}(\cdot)_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}}
is given by:

\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}(\lambda_{1}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+\lambda_{2}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}}=\frac{e^{\lambda_{1}\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}}+\lambda_{2}\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}}}}{\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}}e^{\lambda_{1}\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}+\lambda_{2}\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}}}

(96)

We take the derivative over
\lambda
, using that
\partial\lambda_{1}/\partial\lambda=1
and
\partial\lambda_{2}/\partial\lambda=-1
. Let
Z(\lambda):=\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}}\exp({\lambda_{1}\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}+\lambda_{2}\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}})
. We first evaluate the derivative of this expression:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial Z(\lambda)}{\partial\lambda}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}e^{\lambda_{1}\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}+\lambda_{2}\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}}

(97)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}}e^{\lambda_{1}\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}+\lambda_{2}\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}}(\partial_{\lambda}\lambda_{1}\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}+\partial_{\lambda}\lambda_{2}\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}})

(98)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}}e^{\lambda_{1}\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}+\lambda_{2}\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}}(\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}-\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}})

(99)

\displaystyle=Z(\lambda)\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}}\frac{e^{\lambda_{1}\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}+\lambda_{2}\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}}}{Z(\lambda)}(\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}-\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}})

(100)

\displaystyle=Z(\lambda)\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}}(\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}-\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}})\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}(\lambda_{1}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+\lambda_{2}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}

(101)

Using this result we get:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}

\displaystyle\frac{e^{\lambda_{1}\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}}+\lambda_{2}\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{2}}}}{Z(\lambda)}=\frac{{\partial_{\lambda}e^{\lambda_{1}\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}}+\lambda_{2}\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}}}}Z(\lambda)-e^{\lambda_{1}\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}}+\lambda_{2}\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}}}\partial_{\lambda}Z(\lambda)}{Z(\lambda)^{2}}

(102)

\displaystyle=\frac{{e^{\lambda_{1}\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}}+\lambda_{2}\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}}}(\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}}-\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}})}Z(\lambda)-e^{\lambda_{1}\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}}+\lambda_{2}\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}}}Z(\lambda)\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}}(\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}-\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}})\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}(\lambda_{1}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+\lambda_{2}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}}{Z(\lambda)^{2}}

(103)

\displaystyle=\frac{{e^{\lambda_{1}\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}}+\lambda_{2}\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}}}(\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}}-\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}})}-e^{\lambda_{1}\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}}+\lambda_{2}\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}}}\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}}(\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}-\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}})\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}(\lambda_{1}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+\lambda_{2}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}}{Z(\lambda)}

(104)

\displaystyle=\frac{{e^{\lambda_{1}\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}}+\lambda_{2}\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}}}\big((\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}}-\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}})}-\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}}(\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}-\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}})\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}(\lambda_{1}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+\lambda_{2}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}\big)}{Z(\lambda)}

(105)

For this expression we study the sign of the derivative. Notice that we can focus only on the following term since the others are always positive

(\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}}-\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}})-\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}}(\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}-\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}})\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}(\lambda_{1}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+\lambda_{2}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}

(106)

From this expression, we can make use of the fact that in general, for a scalar function
f(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
, we have
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}[f(\bm{\mathrm{X}})]\leq\max_{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}f(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
and consider the following:

\displaystyle(\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}}-\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}})-\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}}(\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}-\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}})\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}(\lambda_{1}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+\lambda_{2}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}

\displaystyle\geq(\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}}-\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}})-\max_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}}(\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}-\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}})

(107)

\displaystyle=(\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}}-\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}})-(\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}}-\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}})

(108)

\displaystyle=0

(109)

where in the second line we used that the maximum is given by
\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}
. Therefore, the derivative is always increasing in the
[0,1]
interval, meaning that:

\forall\lambda\in[0,1],\;\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}(\lambda_{1}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+\lambda_{2}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}}\leq\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}(\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\})_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}}

(110)

where the equality holds if an only if
\lambda=1
. Similarly, the derivative for
\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}(\cdot)_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{2}}
gives:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}(\lambda_{1}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+\lambda_{2}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{2}}}{\partial\lambda}

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{{e^{\lambda_{1}\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{2}}+\lambda_{2}\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{2}}}\big((\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{2}}-\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{2}})}-\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}}(\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}-\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}})\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}(\lambda_{1}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+\lambda_{2}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}\big)}{Z(\lambda)}

(111)

By using the fact that
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}[f(\bm{\mathrm{X}})]\geq\min_{\bm{\mathrm{x}}}f(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
for a scalar function
f(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
, we obtain:

\displaystyle(\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{2}}-\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{2}})-\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}}(\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}-\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}})\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}(\lambda_{1}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+\lambda_{2}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}

\displaystyle\leq(\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{2}}-\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{2}})-\min_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}}(\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}-\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}})

(112)

\displaystyle=(\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{2}}-\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{2}})-(\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{2}}-\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{2}})

(113)

\displaystyle=0

(114)

where in the second line we used that the minimum is given by
\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{2}
. Hence, the derivative is always decreasing for
\lambda\in[0,1]
and it holds:

\forall\lambda\in[0,1],\;\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}(\lambda_{1}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+\lambda_{2}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{2}}\leq\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}(\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{2}}

(115)

where the equality holds if and only if
\lambda=0
.

(2) Now, we check the same holds when choosing another element
\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{\prime}\neq\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1},\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{2}
. To this end, we consider the following expressions:

\frac{p(\bm{\mathrm{y}}\mid\lambda_{1}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+\lambda_{2}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})}{p(\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}\mid\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1})},\quad\frac{p(\bm{\mathrm{y}}\mid\lambda_{1}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+\lambda_{2}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})}{p(\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{2}\mid\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2})}

(116)

where
p(\bm{\mathrm{y}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{p}}):=\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}(\bm{\mathrm{p}})_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}
.
Consider the first expression. We can rewrite it as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{p(\bm{\mathrm{y}}\mid\lambda_{1}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+\lambda_{2}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})}{p(\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}\mid\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1})}

\displaystyle=\frac{p(\bm{\mathrm{y}}\mid\lambda_{1}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+\lambda_{2}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})}{p(\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}\mid\lambda_{1}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+\lambda_{2}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})}\frac{p(\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}\mid\lambda_{1}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+\lambda_{2}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})}{p(\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}\mid\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1})}

(117)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{p(\bm{\mathrm{y}}\mid\lambda_{1}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+\lambda_{2}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})}{p(\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}\mid\lambda_{1}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+\lambda_{2}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})}

(118)

where in the last line we made use of the fact that the second fraction in the right-hand side of the first line is always
\leq 1
. Similarly we have that:

\displaystyle\frac{p(\bm{\mathrm{y}}\mid\lambda_{1}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+\lambda_{2}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})}{p(\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{2}\mid\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{p(\bm{\mathrm{y}}\mid\lambda_{1}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+\lambda_{2}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})}{p(\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{2}\mid\lambda_{1}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+\lambda_{2}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})}

(119)

We proceed to substituting explicitly the expression for
p(\bm{\mathrm{y}}\mid\lambda_{1}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+\lambda_{2}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}
into the upper bound of
Eq.

118
:

\displaystyle\frac{p(\bm{\mathrm{y}}\mid\lambda_{1}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+\lambda_{2}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})}{p(\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}\mid\lambda_{1}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+\lambda_{2}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{\lambda_{1}\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}+\lambda_{2}\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}}}{e^{\lambda_{1}\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}}+\lambda_{2}\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}}}}\frac{Z(\lambda)}{Z(\lambda)}

(120)

\displaystyle=\exp\big(\lambda_{1}(\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}-\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}})+\lambda_{2}(\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}-\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}})\big)

(121)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\big(-\lambda_{1}\log(M)+\lambda_{2}(\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}-\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}})\big)

(Substituting the bound from
Eq.

84
)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\big(-\lambda_{1}\log(M)+\lambda_{2}\log(M)\big)

(Substituting the bound from
Eq.

85
)

\displaystyle=M^{\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1}}

(122)

With similar steps and substitutions we get that:

\frac{p(\bm{\mathrm{y}}\mid\lambda_{1}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+\lambda_{2}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})}{p(\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{2}\mid\lambda_{1}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+\lambda_{2}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})}\leq M^{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}}

(123)

Taking the product of
Eq.

122
and
Eq.

123
we obtain:

\displaystyle\frac{p(\bm{\mathrm{y}}\mid\lambda_{1}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+\lambda_{2}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})}{p(\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}\mid\lambda_{1}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+\lambda_{2}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})}

\displaystyle\cdot\frac{p(\bm{\mathrm{y}}\mid\lambda_{1}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+\lambda_{2}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})}{p(\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{2}\mid\lambda_{1}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+\lambda_{2}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})}

\displaystyle\leq M^{\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1}}M^{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}}

(124)

\displaystyle=1

(125)

Notice also that:

\displaystyle\frac{p(\bm{\mathrm{y}}\mid\lambda_{1}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+\lambda_{2}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})}{p(\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}\mid\lambda_{1}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+\lambda_{2}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})}

\displaystyle\cdot\frac{p(\bm{\mathrm{y}}\mid\lambda_{1}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+\lambda_{2}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})}{p(\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{2}\mid\lambda_{1}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+\lambda_{2}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})}

\displaystyle\hskip-75.0001pt\geq\min\left(\frac{p(\bm{\mathrm{y}}\mid\lambda_{1}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+\lambda_{2}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})^{2}}{p(\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}\mid\lambda_{1}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+\lambda_{2}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})^{2}},\frac{p(\bm{\mathrm{y}}\mid\lambda_{1}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+\lambda_{2}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})^{2}}{p(\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{2}\mid\lambda_{1}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+\lambda_{2}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})^{2}}\right)

(126)

which in turn means that:

\displaystyle\min\left(\frac{p(\bm{\mathrm{y}}\mid\lambda_{1}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+\lambda_{2}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})^{2}}{p(\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}\mid\lambda_{1}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+\lambda_{2}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})^{2}},\frac{p(\bm{\mathrm{y}}\mid\lambda_{1}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+\lambda_{2}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})^{2}}{p(\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{2}\mid\lambda_{1}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+\lambda_{2}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})^{2}}\right)\leq 1

(127)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\min\left(\frac{p(\bm{\mathrm{y}}\mid\lambda_{1}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+\lambda_{2}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})}{p(\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}\mid\lambda_{1}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+\lambda_{2}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})},\frac{p(\bm{\mathrm{y}}\mid\lambda_{1}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+\lambda_{2}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})}{p(\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{2}\mid\lambda_{1}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+\lambda_{2}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})}\right)\leq 1

(128)

This last expression is in line with the condition of
3.7
, showing that, for all
\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}
, either
\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}(\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\})_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}}
or
\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}(\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{2}}
are greater or equal to

\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}(\lambda\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+(1-\lambda)\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}
.
Combining step (1) and (2), we obtain that
3.7
holds and that:

\max_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}}\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}(\lambda\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\}+(1-\lambda)\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}\leq\max\big(\max_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}}\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}(\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\})_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}},\max_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}}\bm{\mathrm{\omega}}(\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\})_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}\big)

(129)

where equality holds if and only if
\lambda\in\{0,1\}
.
∎

E.2

3.7

We investigate experimentally whether the models used in our evaluation satisfy
3.7
. We proved in
Section

E.1
that the
DSL
inference layer reduces to Probabilistic Logic methods and only consider
DPL
and
DPL
∗

CBNM
we conduct a separate investigation.

We first consider the case where the prior knowledge
\mathsf{K}

DPL

3.7

We now turn to
DPL

∗

(\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1},\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2})\in[100]^{2}

3.7
is satisfied. See
Fig.

11

Finally,
we evaluate explicitly whether a trained

3.7

150

3.7
is satisfied for the
95\%
of possible couples
