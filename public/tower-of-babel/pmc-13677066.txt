AI-bioinformatics synergy for precision medicine in brain diseases: integrating radiomics and multi-omics data
Authors: Bekir Altas, Polymnia Georgiou, Anna Onisiforou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe convergence of artificial intelligence (AI) and bioinformatics is reshaping the landscape of precision medicine in brain diseases, particularly in neurological diseases and brain tumors. Despite advances in both fields, the combined application of radiomics and multi-omics fusion remains underutilized, even though integrative frameworks are essential for addressing the complexity and heterogeneity of imaging and molecular data. This review examines how integrative AI-bioinformatics frameworks enable the fusion of radiomics with genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and other omics modalities to more comprehensively model disease complexity. Leveraging deep learning, graph neural networks, and advanced representation learning, these multimodal systems facilitate biomarker discovery, patient stratification, and personalized treatment strategies. We critically evaluate key challenges including data heterogeneity, limited interpretability, and barriers to clinical validation, and highlight emerging solutions aimed at enhancing reproducibility and translational readiness. Case studies in gliomas, Alzheimer’s disease, and multiple sclerosis illustrate the clinical utility of unified imaging-omics architectures. Ultimately, this review positions radiomics and multi-omics integration as a foundational approach for the next generation of precision medicine in brain diseases by bridging the gap between computational innovation and clinical application.

Introduction

Precision medicine seeks to tailor diagnosis, prevention, and treatment to each patient’s unique molecular, phenotypic, and clinical profile. This need is especially acute in brain diseases, such as gliomas, Alzheimer’s disease (AD), and multiple sclerosis (MS), which exhibit profound heterogeneity in mechanisms, progression, and treatment response. Yet, traditional clinical paradigms still rely on standardized protocols and population-level averages that often fail to capture patient-specific biology.

A major limitation in diagnosing brain disorders is the insufficient specificity of neuroimaging alone. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is essential for visualizing brain structure and pathology but cannot fully resolve the molecular heterogeneity driving many diseases. Gliomas, for example, still require invasive biopsy for definitive diagnosis and molecular subtyping, as structural or functional MRI cannot reliably characterize their molecular features [1]. In practice, distinguishing brain inflammation from grade II glioma remains difficult because both may appear as similar mass-like lesions on conventional MRI [2], delaying treatment and risking unnecessary interventions.

Neurodegenerative diseases face similar diagnostic challenges, as structural imaging often reveals overlapping patterns across conditions. For example, AD can resemble vascular dementia (VaD) [3], and many patients present with mixed dementia (MixD), combining features of AD and subcortical vascular dementia (SVaD). These cases frequently show white matter and frontal lobe abnormalities that conventional MRI cannot attribute to a single cause [4]. This ambiguity underscores the need for integrative approaches that combine imaging with clinical, cognitive, and molecular data to improve diagnostic accuracy. Likewise, MS can mimic other inflammatory or demyelinating diseases, including neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD), acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM), and cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD), leading to diagnostic uncertainty that often requires invasive tests, extensive clinical evaluation, or repeated imaging, ultimately delaying timely therapy [5, 6].

Multimodal data integration, combining imaging, multi-omics (e.g. genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics), and clinical data, offers a powerful path to personalized medicine in brain disorders. Traditional omics analyses rely on group-level comparisons that obscure individual variability essential for accurate diagnosis and treatment, and even advanced single-cell or spatial omics approaches are rarely deployed at an individualized, clinically actionable scale. Consequently, therapies may be prescribed without considering patient-specific molecular profiles. In AD, transcriptomic studies reveal sex-specific hippocampal gene expression, indicating divergent mechanisms and potentially different therapeutic responses [7]. In MS, molecular features vary across both patients and disease stages [8]. These findings underscore the need for integrative, personalized diagnostics that reflect biological diversity and disease trajectory, advancing toward sex-aware and stage-specific precision medicine.

Artificial intelligence (AI) and advanced bioinformatics are increasingly central to addressing these gaps. Approaches such as multimodal deep learning, graph neural networks, and generative AI (GenAI) enable joint modeling of complex, heterogeneous data types [9]. Metadata-conditioned generative models, such as BrainSynth, can synthesize anatomically plausible MRI scans reflecting biological variation, such as age and sex, improving model training and addressing data imbalance [10]. Integrated models can also predict subtypes, identify biomarkers, and support non-invasive molecular diagnostics. For instance, the MINDSETS study showed that combining imaging with cognitive, clinical, and genetic data markedly improves the differentiation of AD from VaD [11].

The convergence of AI and bioinformatics thus represents a key enabler of precision medicine in brain diseases [11, 12]. AI models complex, nonlinear patterns in high-dimensional data, while bioinformatics curates and interprets large-scale molecular datasets. Together, they support the integration of imaging-derived radiomics with genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and clinical variables, enabling comprehensive, individualized disease models. However, their combined potential for radiomics and multi-omics fusion remains underutilized, especially in neuro-oncology and neurological disease research. This review addresses this translational gap by demonstrating how synergistic AI–bioinformatics approaches can unify diverse data types to produce clinically actionable insights. By outlining the current landscape and practical implementation pathways, we aim to support the development of robust, interpretable, and scalable tools for diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment personalization.

Building on this objective, the review examines the evolving integration of AI and bioinformatics in radiomics- and multi-omics–driven precision medicine for the brain (Fig. 1). We summarize the state of radiogenomics and omics-based analyses across neurological diseases, survey AI methods for multimodal data fusion in gliomas, AD, and MS, and discuss translational challenges, including data heterogeneity, interpretability, and clinical deployment, while proposing strategies to accelerate the adoption of integrated AI–bioinformatics pipelines in brain diagnostics and therapeutics.

Overview of the AI–bioinformatics precision medicine pipeline

The development of AI-enabled precision medicine for brain diseases involves a sequence of interconnected analytical stages that transform heterogeneous molecular, imaging, and clinical data into clinically actionable insights. This workflow spans multimodal data acquisition, data harmonization, bioinformatics analysis, AI-driven multimodal integration, predictive modeling, and clinical translation, with each stage contributing to the robustness, interpretability, and translational potential of the overall framework (Fig. 1).

Multimodal data collection

The workflow begins with the acquisition of complementary patient data, including genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, metabolomics, radiomics derived from MRI, Positron Emission Tomography (PET), or Computed Tomography (CT) imaging, and longitudinal clinical information (Fig. 1A). Each modality captures distinct biological information, ranging from molecular alterations to anatomical and functional brain changes. Because no single modality fully characterizes complex brain diseases, including gliomas and neurological diseases such as AD and MS, integrating these heterogeneous data sources provides a more comprehensive representation of disease biology.

Data preprocessing and harmonization

Before multimodal integration, each data modality requires preprocessing to minimize technical variability and ensure compatibility across datasets (Fig. 1B). Imaging data undergo quality control, normalization, segmentation, and feature extraction, whereas omics datasets require normalization, batch-effect correction, and feature filtering. Harmonization methods, including ComBat and NeuroCombat, are particularly important in multicenter studies because they reduce scanner- and site-specific variability while preserving biologically meaningful signals. Dimensionality reduction and feature alignment further facilitate downstream multimodal integration.

Bioinformatics analysis

The molecular datasets subsequently undergo bioinformatics processing to extract biologically meaningful information (Fig. 1C). Typical analyses include differential gene expression, pathway enrichment, multi-omics network modeling, multi-omics integration, and biomarker discovery. These analyses transform high-dimensional molecular measurements into interpretable biological features that can later be integrated with neuroimaging and clinical variables.

AI-based multimodal integration

Following preprocessing, AI methods integrate imaging, molecular, and clinical information into unified patient representations (Fig. 1D). Depending on the research objective and data characteristics, integration may employ early, intermediate, or late fusion strategies, graph neural networks, transformer architectures, similarity-based methods, or generative models. These approaches capture complementary information across modalities while modeling complex nonlinear biological relationships.

Model training, validation, and performance evaluation

Integrated representations are subsequently used to train predictive models (Fig. 1E). Appropriate training, validation, and test splits, together with cross-validation and external validation cohorts, are essential for obtaining reliable performance estimates. Evaluation should include discrimination metrics, calibration, robustness across sites, bias assessment, uncertainty quantification, and model explainability to facilitate clinical translation.

Predictive outputs

The trained models generate clinically relevant outputs, including disease classification, molecular subtype prediction, prognosis, risk stratification, treatment response prediction, and biomarker identification (Fig. 1F). These outputs bridge computational analyses and individualized clinical decision-making.

Clinical translation

Finally, predictive outputs are incorporated into AI-assisted precision medicine workflows that support diagnosis, prognosis, treatment selection, and longitudinal disease monitoring for disorders such as gliomas, AD, and MS (Fig. 1G). Successful clinical implementation depends on regulatory compliance, external validation, interpretability, and seamless integration into existing healthcare infrastructure.

Radiomics and multi-omics in brain disorders

Radiomics involves the systematic extraction of high-dimensional quantitative features from clinical images (MRI, CT, PET) using computational algorithms. Unlike traditional visual interpretation, radiomics produces large, mineable datasets that support biomarker discovery, disease characterization, and longitudinal assessment of treatment response, enabling precision, patient-specific care [13, 14]. Radiomic features fall into four main classes: (i) statistical features, including first-order histogram metrics (mean, median, entropy, skewness, kurtosis) and higher-order texture features derived the Gray-Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM), Gray-Level Run-Length Matrix (GLRLM), Gray-Level Size Zone Matrix (GLSZM), Neighborhood Gray-Tone Difference Matrix (NGTDM), and Gray-Level Distance Zone Matrix (GLDZM); (ii) model-based features, which use autoregressive and fractal analyses to capture texture regularity; (iii) transform-based features, using Fourier transforms, Gabor filters, and wavelets to represent frequency and multiscale patterns; and (iv) shape-based features, characterizing morphology through measures such as volume, surface area, sphericity, compactness, and elongation. Detailed methodological descriptions are available in [13, 15–18].

Multi-omics refers to the integrated analysis of multiple molecular layers spanning the central dogma of biology, from DNA to RNA to proteins and metabolites. Genomics identifies inherited and somatic variants, epigenomics captures regulatory modifications such as DNA methylation and histone marks, and transcriptomics measures mRNA and noncoding RNA expression to reveal dynamic gene activity. Proteomics assesses protein abundance and posttranslational states, while metabolomics and lipidomics quantify small molecules and lipid species that reflect cellular physiology. These datasets range from high-resolution single-cell and spatial omics that capture cell-type and microenvironmental signals to lower-resolution bulk and targeted omics that provide averaged or pathway-focused tissue-level profiles.

Biomedical imaging captures anatomical and functional outcomes of underlying molecular processes but cannot directly measure them, while omics provides molecular detail without spatial or phenotypic context. For example, bulk transcriptomics identifies dysregulated genes in MS without revealing affected central nervous system (CNS) regions, and genomic sequencing detects glioma mutations without indicating their anatomical distribution or most aggressive areas. Radiogenomics bridges this gap by linking imaging features to molecular and genetic alterations, enabling non-invasive molecular characterization and mechanistic insight. Over the past decade, it has become a key integrative approach for understanding how molecular changes shape imaging patterns and for predicting molecular subtypes, supporting precision diagnostics and personalized therapy in brain diseases [19–21].

AI methods for multi-modal integration

AI provides the computational foundation for integrating radiomics, multi-omics, and clinical data in precision medicine for brain diseases. The following sections review multimodal integration frameworks that have been developed or applied to neuro-oncology and neurological diseases, highlighting their methodological principles and their utility for biomarker discovery, disease subtyping, prognosis, and treatment response prediction.

Similarity- and graph-based fusion methods

Similarity Network Fusion (SNF), introduced by Wang et al. (2014), is one of the most widely used multimodal integration frameworks for precision medicine applications. By integrating patient-similarity graphs constructed separately from each modality, such as gene expression, methylation, and miRNA, SNF iteratively fuses complementary information into a unified network [22]. In brain diseases, SNF has been successfully applied to neurodegenerative disorders [23–25] and neuro-oncology, where it improves disease subtype discovery, identifies clinically meaningful patient groups that single-modality or simple concatenation approaches often miss, and enhances the prediction of clinically relevant outcomes through the integration of heterogeneous imaging and molecular data.

A major challenge in multimodal integration for brain diseases is missing data, particularly in neurodegenerative cohorts where entire modalities may be absent (for example, MRI and genomics are available, but PET or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) proteomics are missing); miss-SNF addresses this limitation by reconstructing missing similarity networks through a nonlinear message-passing strategy, enabling fusion even when complete modalities are missing [26]. This method has demonstrated robust clustering and prediction performance across The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) cancers and AD datasets, recovering clinically meaningful subgroups despite substantial modality dropout [26].

Deep learning approaches for multimodal integration

Deep learning architectures have become central to multimodal integration in precision medicine for brain diseases because they can capture complex, nonlinear relationships among heterogeneous imaging, molecular, and clinical data. These approaches support clinically relevant tasks, including disease classification, radiogenomic analysis, biomarker discovery, prognosis, and treatment response prediction across gliomas, AD, and MS. Deep learning approaches for multimodal integration can be broadly categorized into several classes, each designed to address specific challenges in combining diverse data types such as imaging, omics, and clinical variables. These include: (i) fusion-based architectures, (ii) attention-based architectures [e.g. transformers with cross-modal attention or hypernetwork-based fusion leveraging convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and multilayer perceptrons (MLPs)], (iii) generative architectures [e.g. multimodal variational autoencoders (mmVAEs), generative adversarial networks (GANs), and diffusion models], (iv) graph-based architectures [e.g. graph neural networks (GNNs) and heterogeneous graph networks], and (v) hybrid architectures that combine multiple model families (e.g. CNN + transformer or GNN + transformer designs).

Fusion architectures

Fusion architectures constitute a fundamental strategy for integrating imaging, multi-omics, and clinical information in brain disease precision medicine. The choice of fusion architecture determines how complementary information from different modalities is combined and ultimately influences model performance, interpretability, and robustness. Common strategies include early, intermediate, and late fusion. Early fusion concatenates modality features at the input stage into a single feature vector. Intermediate fusion processes each modality with its own architecture (e.g. CNNs for MRI, MLPs for omics) and merges their latent representations at deeper layers. Late fusion trains separate models per modality and combines their predictions through voting, averaging, or learned weighting, keeping modalities independent until the final step. Attention-based/transformer architectures

Attention-based architectures, particularly transformers, have emerged as powerful multimodal integration frameworks for precision medicine in brain diseases by enabling explicit, learnable cross-modal reasoning through self-attention and, in some cases, dynamic parameter generation via hypernetworks [38, 39]. Unlike fixed fusion strategies, these models adaptively weight the relevance of features from each modality, learning task-specific relationships between imaging, omics, and clinical data [38, 40]. This enables the identification of imaging features associated with specific molecular alterations and, conversely, molecular pathways that explain observed imaging phenotypes, supporting applications such as radiogenomic analysis, disease subtyping, and biomarker discovery in gliomas [41].

In cross-modal transformer models, MRI is encoded into image tokens using a Vision Transformer (ViT) or CNN, while omics and clinical data are encoded into corresponding token sets via MLPs or transformers [42]. Self-attention and cross-attention layers then learn adaptive weights that determine how imaging, omics, and clinical tokens influence one another [43, 44]. Multi-head attention allows different heads to attend to distinct representation subspaces [44], which, in multimodal neuroimaging applications, may capture distinct cross-modal relationships, such as associations between regional atrophy and inflammatory pathways or cognitive decline. These mechanisms enable the integration of heterogeneous data without requiring explicitly predefined modality relationships.

Attention-based and transformer architectures, which enable rich representation learning and cross-modal reasoning through attention mechanism [42, 45], have outperformed single-modality baselines and simpler fusion schemes in several brain disease applications by capturing complex, nonlinear imaging–omics–clinical interactions. They are also highly modular, allowing the incorporation of additional modalities, clinical covariates, or longitudinal time points by adding tokens to the sequence. However, these advantages come with important limitations. Cross-modal transformers incur high computational cost (quadratic in sequence length), demand substantial training data, especially for 3D MRI, and are prone to overfitting in small clinical cohorts without strong regularization or transfer learning [46, 47].

Hypernetwork-based fusion provides an efficient alternative. Here, a meta-network that processes tabular clinical and omics data (typically via MLPs) dynamically generates the weights of a primary CNN processing imaging data [39, 48]. This allows non-imaging context to modulate how MRI is interpreted. For example, in brain-age prediction, a hypernetwork receiving age, sex, and genotype can generate convolutional filters tailored to age- and genotype-specific aging patterns, enabling a single CNN backbone to specialize across patient subgroups [48, 49]. Hypernetworks therefore offer (i) context-aware image interpretation, (ii) better computational efficiency than full transformers, (iii) parameter-efficient adaptation (no separate model per subgroup), (iv) reduced data requirements, and (v) straightforward integration of tabular/omics data without designing token-level attention over thousands of genes. Their drawbacks include the absence of explicit attention matrices (weaker cross-modal interpretability), sensitivity to noisy covariates (which can destabilize the CNN), and the need for careful feature selection, regularization, and architectural design [39].

Cross-modal transformers are particularly suitable when maximal predictive performance and explicit modelling of imaging–omics–clinical relationships are required, whereas hypernetwork-based fusion offers an attractive alternative for computationally efficient multimodal integration in brain disease precision medicine [43, 50–52].

Generative models

Generative architectures, such as mmVAEs, GANs, and diffusion models, differ from fusion-based approaches by learning the underlying probability distributions that generate imaging and omics patterns in healthy and diseased brains. They operate in latent spaces that capture essential structure in high-dimensional data while filtering noise. Shared latent spaces map imaging, omics, and clinical data into unified representations that encode cross-modal biological commonalities. mmVAEs provide a probabilistic framework that learns shared disease-related factors while separating modality-specific noise, an approach demonstrated in general multi-omics applications as well as in unimodal MRI-based normative modeling in AD [53, 54]. Advanced mmVAE models support cross-modal imputation (predicting omics from imaging and vice versa) and normative modeling, enabling individualized quantification of deviations from healthy aging using multimodal MRI [55].

GANs consist of a generator (G) that synthesizes realistic data samples and a discriminator (D) that distinguishes real from synthetic data [56]. GANs have been widely applied for data augmentation, synthetic image generation, and domain adaptation in medical imaging [57–59]. However, standard GANs offer limited control over the type of data they generate; as a result, their outputs may be unpredictable, untailored to specific clinical needs, and limited in their ability to address class imbalance, especially when rare disease subtypes cannot be synthesized reliably. To overcome this limitation in precision medicine applications, conditional GANs (cGANs) extend GANs by conditioning both the generator and discriminator on auxiliary information such as class labels, continuous attributes, or structured clinical and molecular data. This conditioning allows targeted synthesis of specific disease subtypes or patient profiles using features such as molecular signatures, imaging phenotypes, demographics (e.g. age, sex, disease stage), or treatment-response categories. Consequently, cGANs can: (i) mitigate data scarcity in rare subgroups, (ii) address class imbalance without discarding information, (iii) support privacy-preserving data sharing, (iv) support hypothesis generation through “what-if” simulations that may provide mechanistic insight, and (v) facilitate cross-site model generalization by generating synthetic data that may reduce the need to share real patient data.

Diffusion models represent a more recent generative paradigm, learning to reverse a gradual noising process through iterative denoising steps [60, 61]. Compared with GANs, diffusion models can reduce susceptibility to mode collapse, exhibit more stable training, and can generate high-fidelity and diverse samples. Recent studies demonstrate their promise for imaging–omics synthesis and high-fidelity biomedical data generation [62]. Their primary limitations include high computational cost (due to repeated denoising iterations), slow inference, and architectural complexity requiring careful training optimization.

Graph neural networks for imaging–omics

Biological systems are inherently network-structured, yet conventional machine-learning models such as CNNs and MLPs treat omics features as independent, exchangeable variables. This neglects the underlying topology of biological regulation. In reality, molecular processes are organized into structured networks, including gene regulatory networks (GRNs) in which transcription factors regulate downstream genes, protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks in which proteins assemble into signaling cascades and complexes, and metabolic pathways where enzymes catalyze sequential biochemical reactions [73].

GNNs and heterogeneous graph networks (HGNs) address these limitations by framing multimodal integration as a topological learning problem, rather than a feature-independence problem [74–77]. In GNN frameworks for precision medicine in brain diseases, data are represented as graphs in which nodes correspond to patients, brain regions, genes, or proteins, and edges encode meaningful relationships such as structural or functional connectivity between brain regions and, at the molecular level, gene co-expression, transcription-factor binding, PPI edges, or enzymatic dependencies [78].

A representative application of graph-based multi-omics integration is MOGANet, which uses per-view graph convolutional networks (GCNs) to extract structured features from individual omics modalities and a hierarchical attention-based fusion mechanism (HAFM) to capture relationships within and between modalities [79]. MOGANet was evaluated on three TCGA cancer datasets, including lower grade glioma (LGG), and outperformed traditional and state-of-the-art methods in classification tasks. Functional enrichment analysis further supported the biological relevance of the identified biomarkers [79].

GNNs offer several advantages for imaging–omics integration. They provide biological interpretability by aggregating information at the pathway or module level, enabling models to reflect known molecular organization. Their graph-based structure can reduce the effective dimensionality by imposing topological constraints on the feature space rather than treating each variable independently. Additionally, GNNs support transfer learning because many biological networks, such as pathways or PPI modules, are conserved across diseases and cohorts. Finally, GNNs can accommodate missing information through graph-based message passing, allowing representations of nodes with incomplete features to incorporate information from connected neighbors, which may be particularly valuable in incomplete or heterogeneous clinical datasets.

HGNs provide additional benefits when multiple biological entity types coexist, as is typical in brain-disease precision medicine, where imaging regions, genes, proteins, cell types, and pathways interact through distinct mechanisms. Standard GNNs may be limited in their ability to model such hierarchical, multi-relational systems, whereas HGNs explicitly encode multiple node and edge types [80]. Recent biomedical knowledge graph frameworks further extend this paradigm through path-based reasoning, enabling interpretable link prediction and prioritization of drug–disease associations, including candidate therapeutic indications for AD [81]. This may support biologically grounded reasoning, such as identifying pathway-level disruptions associated with specific regional imaging abnormalities in particular patient subgroups [80].

Although multimodal AI frameworks share the common goal of integrating imaging, molecular, and clinical information, their implementation varies according to disease-specific biological characteristics, data availability, and clinical objectives. The following sections illustrate how these frameworks have been applied in gliomas, AD, and MS to support diagnosis, molecular characterization, prognosis, and treatment response.

Gliomas

Gliomas represent ~81% of malignant primary brain tumors, with glioblastoma (GBM) accounting for nearly 45% of cases and exhibiting a 5-year survival rate of only ~5% despite advances in therapy [103, 104]. Current standard treatment, including maximal safe resection followed by radiotherapy and temozolomide, improves survival modestly but is limited by the infiltrative growth pattern of gliomas, which leads to recurrence and functional morbidity [105, 106]. Early and accurate diagnosis remains a critical unmet need. Patients often present with nonspecific neurological symptoms, and early-stage or low-grade gliomas may be subtle or non-enhancing on MRI [107]. Although MRI remains the diagnostic gold standard [108], overlapping radiological features between tumor types frequently require invasive biopsy for definitive classification [109, 110]. Management challenges persist intraoperatively, where distinguishing tumor from functionally critical tissue is difficult [111], and during longitudinal follow-up, where pseudoprogression may mimic recurrence, complicating treatment decisions [112]. These limitations highlight the need for non-invasive, multimodal diagnostic strategies integrating imaging with molecular biomarkers to improve early detection, tumor classification, and personalized therapy planning [113].

Despite rapid progress in generative AI and increased availability of medical data, current AI diagnostic tools remain constrained by limited generalizability. Many oncology models are trained on monomodal, small, or demographically biased datasets, reducing their clinical reliability and hindering deployment [114, 115]. For brain tumors, these limitations are compounded by pronounced intratumoral heterogeneity, complex brain anatomy, and a scarcity of large, curated multimodal datasets. Integrating imaging, genomic, and clinical data across institutions is difficult due to heterogeneous file formats, inconsistent imaging protocols, sparse labels, and unstructured clinical narratives, all of which impede interoperability. Moreover, data scarcity for rare tumor subtypes, privacy constraints, and incomplete validation of synthetic data generation reduce model robustness [116]. Critical components of trustworthy AI, such as uncertainty calibration, external validation, and bias assessment across sex, age, and imaging site, remain underdeveloped, limiting clinician confidence [117]. Only a small number of systems incorporate interpretable, clinician-facing interfaces, underscoring the need for generative AI frameworks capable of integrating multimodal knowledge, producing validated synthetic data, and delivering transparent, uncertainty-aware predictions for precision neuro-oncology.

Recent radiogenomic studies further demonstrate the potential of multimodal AI to non-invasively infer tumor biology. Ahanger et al. integrated radiomic features from multi-sequence MRI (T1, T1-contrast, FLAIR, T2) with genomic pathway annotations from TCGA and CPTAC to predict deregulation of key oncogenic signaling pathways in GBM [92]. Using machine learning models trained on BraTS radiomics, they achieved AUCs of 0.7 (RTK–RAS), 0.8 (PI3K), and 0.75 (TP53), showing that MRI-derived radiomic signatures contain informative patterns linked to oncogenic signaling pathway activity [92]. Kazerooni et al. analyzed multiparametric MRI and targeted sequencing from 357 IDH–wildtype GBMs, identifying distinct radiogenomic and spatial signatures associated with key mutations such as EGFR, PTEN, TP53, and NF1 [98]. Their machine and deep learning models demonstrated that tumor location and imaging characteristics reflect underlying oncogenic drivers and molecular heterogeneity, offering clinically accessible biomarkers for non-invasive tumor profiling and improved patient stratification [98].

Beyond algorithmic advances, platform-level tools are emerging to support the clinical use of radiomics in glioma. Kundal et al. introduced RadGLO, an interactive platform that integrates radiomic features from multi-institutional MRI datasets (TCGA, UCSF, UPENN) to enable grade-wise analysis, gene–feature correlation, and personalized survival prediction, incorporating modules for risk stratification and region-specific tumor volume quantification [93].

Alzheimer’s disease

AD is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder and the leading cause of dementia, affecting over 55 million people worldwide, with no effective therapies [118]. Structural MRI (sMRI) remains a cornerstone of the clinical assessment in AD due to its wide availability and utility in visualizing brain atrophy [119]. In parallel, genomic approaches, such as genome-wide association studies (GWAS) and transcriptomic profiling, have advanced understanding of AD’s molecular mechanisms [7, 120]. However, most studies have historically examined imaging and omics data in isolation, limiting insight into how these biological layers interact to shape disease progression.

Growing evidence underscores the importance of multimodal integration for characterizing AD and prodromal stages such as MCI. Traditional classifications (e.g. amnestic versus non-amnestic, early versus late MCI) capture some clinical variability but fail to represent the molecular and imaging heterogeneity underlying disease trajectories [121, 122]. Single-modality approaches relying solely on CSF biomarkers, neuropsychological scores, or imaging often overlook key biological interactions and provide limited predictive power [123–128]. In contrast, multimodal frameworks integrating MRI, PET, genomics, transcriptomics, or CSF proteomics have revealed robust subgroups with distinct cognitive trajectories, neurodegeneration patterns, and molecular pathways.

In recent years, large-scale initiatives such as the Accelerating Medicines Partnership–Alzheimer’s Disease (AMP-AD) [90, 91] and the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) [88, 89] have catalyzed this shift by generating open-access, deeply phenotyped, multi-omics and neuroimaging datasets. These resources now enable the development and rigorous validation of multimodal AI and bioinformatics methods for subtype discovery, progression modeling, and biomarker identification. Building on these datasets, several integrative multimodal frameworks have emerged. Wu et al. introduced a federated Genotype-Expression-Imaging Data Integration (GEIDI) model that jointly analyzes sMRI, gene expression, and single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP)-level genotypes using ADNI data [100]. GEIDI outperformed standard eQTL methods, revealing genotype-dependent transcriptomic influences on brain structure and offering a personalized approach for biomarker discovery and therapeutic targeting [100]. Similarly, an ADNI-based study using SNF and spectral clustering integrated sMRI, CSF peptidomic/proteomic profiles, and clinical data to identify two biologically distinct mild cognitive impairment (MCI) subtypes [23]. One subgroup exhibited neuronal resilience and minimal atrophy, while the other showed AD-like neurodegeneration and faster clinical conversion [23].

Additionally, SNF-based studies have uncovered genetic and transcriptomic subtypes that differ in atrophy patterns, AD conversion time, and enriched biological pathways [24]. Multi-omics SNF analyses have uncovered molecularly distinct AD subtypes with unique biological signatures and progression trajectories [25]. A stage-wise deep learning model integrating MRI, PET, and SNP data has also shown strong performance. Zhou et al. demonstrated that hierarchical fusion of modality-specific and joint latent features significantly improves accuracy in distinguishing AD, MCI, and cognitively normal aging compared with single-modality approaches [34].

Beyond these integrative pipelines, unsupervised approaches such as deep or generalized canonical correlation analysis have identified imaging-driven MCI subtypes aligned with specific genetic variants [129]. Supervised multimodal models, including multi-kernel learning [130] and heterogeneous mixture learning [131], further improve the prediction of MCI-to-AD conversion by jointly modeling imaging, CSF, and genetic features. Collectively, these multimodal studies highlight how integrating imaging with omics enables more accurate subtyping, improved prognosis, and deeper mechanistic understanding of early AD.

Recent work has also applied multimodal deep generative models to capture patient-specific abnormalities. Kumar et al. introduced an mmVAE that jointly models volumetric features from T1-weighted (cortical and subcortical) and T2-weighted (hippocampal) MRI to learn a normative distribution of healthy brain morphology and quantify individualized deviation maps in AD [55]. Their approach showed greater sensitivity to disease stage and stronger associations with cognition than unimodal models, highlighting the value of multimodal normative modeling for precision phenotyping.

Multiple sclerosis

MS is the leading non-traumatic cause of neurological disability in young and middle-aged adults (18–40 years) [132], affecting more than 2.8 million people globally [133]. The disease is highly heterogeneous in clinical presentation, lesion pathology, and treatment response. Diagnosis relies primarily on MRI evidence of dissemination in space and time, supported by clinical assessment and CSF findings as defined by the McDonald criteria [134]. MS follows four principal clinical courses. Clinically isolated syndrome (CIS) is the first demyelinating episode that suggests the onset of MS [135]. Relapsing–remitting MS (RRMS) involves episodic relapses followed by periods of partial or full recovery. Secondary progressive MS (SPMS) is defined by gradual, sustained disability accumulation after an initial RRMS phase. Primary progressive MS (PPMS) involves continuous neurological worsening from disease onset without clear relapses or remissions.

Although MRI is central to MS diagnosis and monitoring, conventional T1, T2, and FLAIR sequences capture only part of the disease spectrum [136, 137]. They detect macroscopic white-matter lesions but poorly reflect microstructural injury, cortical demyelination, diffuse inflammation, or gray-matter neurodegeneration [136, 137]. The weak correlation between lesion burden and clinical disability, the “clinico-radiological paradox,” underscores these limitations [138, 139]. MRI also lacks sensitivity for early neurodegenerative changes that predict long-term outcomes and cannot distinguish biologically distinct lesion types such as active, smoldering, or remyelinating plaques. Clinically, these gaps create major unmet needs, including accurate early diagnosis and differentiation from mimics such as NMOSD, biomarkers predicting CIS-to-RRMS conversion, early identification of the transition to SPMS, and individualized prediction of treatment response. There remains a further need to quantify processes that MRI only indirectly reflects, including axonal loss, mitochondrial dysfunction, microglial activation, and remyelination failure.

Multi-omics technologies provide molecular-level insight into MS heterogeneity and help address gaps left by imaging alone. Genomic studies have identified more than 200 risk loci, implicating dysregulated immune pathways, HLA-associated susceptibility, and genetic influences on oligodendrocyte biology [140, 141]. Epigenomic profiling shows abnormal DNA methylation and chromatin remodeling across T cells, B cells, and glial populations [142–145]. Transcriptomic and single-cell analyses reveal distinct immune cell states, including pathogenic Th1/Th17 populations and disease-associated microglia enriched in progressive MS [146–149]. CSF proteomics captures dynamic inflammatory and neurodegenerative signals, such as complement activation and axonal injury markers like neurofilament light chain [150–152]. Metabolomic studies highlight alterations in lipid metabolism, mitochondrial stress, and oxidative injury that contribute to demyelination and energy failure [153–158]. These complementary layers enable a more granular view of MS biology and open the door to identifying molecular subtypes that cannot be resolved by imaging alone.

Integrating MRI with multi-omics using AI and machine learning offers a powerful opportunity to overcome current diagnostic and prognostic limitations in MS. When combined with genomic or transcriptomic data, AI models can improve diagnostic accuracy, including differentiation from other inflammatory disorders and prediction of CIS conversion to clinically definite MS. Multimodal approaches integrating imaging, CSF proteomics, and immune signatures have shown promising performance in forecasting disability progression and transition to SPMS, and may outperform MRI-only models [94, 95, 159] . AI-driven big-data integration further supports the prediction and reclassification of MS subtypes [94, 96, 97]. Radiogenomic analyses can additionally reveal lesion-specific molecular profiles linked to remyelination capacity, chronic inflammation, or cortical involvement [12, 160–163], offering mechanistic insights into why some patients undergo rapid neurodegeneration despite modest lesion burden.

In the therapeutic domain, multimodal AI models that integrate MRI trajectories with peripheral gene expression or CSF biomarkers show potential for predicting treatment response to disease-modifying therapies, including anti-CD20 agents and S1P receptor modulators [94, 96, 97, 102, 164]. This precision-medicine approach may help identify suitable therapies for individual patients earlier and reduce the trial-and-error common in current practice. Integrative AI methods that link imaging networks with molecular datasets may also help identify the pathways underlying radiological changes, revealing potential targets for neuroprotection and remyelination [12, 21, 94, 96, 97, 160–163].

Data heterogeneity and integration complexity

The integration of multimodal data, imaging, multi-omics, and clinical records, is essential for precision medicine in brain diseases but is challenged by fundamental differences in data scale, structure, and semantics. MRI is spatially dense and volumetric, omics data are tabular and high-dimensional, and clinical data may be longitudinal, categorical, or free-text. This mismatch, along with the tens of thousands of features typical of omics layers, creates dimensional imbalance that hinders integration and interpretability. The problem is amplified in brain-disease studies, where sample sizes are often limited, especially in glioma subtypes, early-stage AD, and progressive MS, restricting statistical power and generalizability. Even after modality-specific preprocessing, inconsistencies in data distributions can persist, introducing artificial correlations or noise in downstream analyses [165]. Temporal misalignment between imaging acquisition and biospecimen collection further complicates unified analytical frameworks, as does the frequent occurrence of missing modalities for individual subjects [166]. These challenges intensify in multicenter collaborations, where batch effects, scanner differences, and protocol variations can obscure true biological signals.

Missing data remain a major challenge in multimodal brain disease studies because many patients lack one or more data modalities, such as MRI, PET, CSF biomarkers, or multi-omics profiles, reducing sample size and potentially introducing selection bias. Naïve exclusion of incomplete cases decreases statistical power and may impair model generalizability. To address this issue, approaches including statistical imputation, matrix completion, multimodal autoencoders, graph-based methods, and missing-modality learning have been developed to reconstruct absent information or enable models to operate with incomplete inputs. The recently developed miss-SNF framework extends SNF to datasets with completely missing modalities by reconstructing missing patient similarities through a nonlinear message-passing strategy. When entire data modalities were artificially removed for subsets of patients, miss-SNF achieved AD diagnostic performance comparable to that obtained using complete multimodal datasets, demonstrating its ability to mitigate the impact of missing modalities on predictive performance [26]. Similarly, a missing-data extension of the Subtype and Stage Inference (SuStaIn) algorithm enabled the integration of MRI, PET, cerebrospinal fluid biomarkers, and cognitive assessments from the ADNI despite incomplete multimodal data, identifying five clinically meaningful AD subtypes while substantially increasing the number of individuals available for analysis [167].

From a clinical perspective, missing-modality robustness is particularly important when a modality cannot be acquired rather than being randomly absent. For example, CSF biomarkers may be unavailable when lumbar puncture is contraindicated or declined, while PET or molecular profiling may not be accessible in all clinical settings. Multimodal models should, therefore, support flexible inference from different combinations of available modalities rather than require a fixed complete input set. Such flexible deployment is technically feasible; for example, UroFusion-X retained more than 90% of its predictive performance under substantial modality dropout in a multicenter cancer cohort [168], although comparable validation is still needed in brain diseases. However, preservation of predictive performance does not by itself justify replacing an invasive diagnostic test. There is no universal performance threshold for such substitution, rather, model performance should be evaluated in the intended population and clinical setting and against the diagnostic standard it is intended to replace [169, 170]. Evaluation should consider predictive performance, generalizability, and clinical usefulness, with external validation across clinically relevant modality combinations before reduced-modality models are used for clinical decision-making [169, 170].

Harmonization techniques such as ComBat, originally developed for genomics, have been successfully adapted to neuroimaging [171, 172]. In large multi-site cortical-thickness studies, ComBat reduces scanner-related variance, improves reproducibility, and increases statistical power when linking imaging features to clinical covariates [172], enabling more reliable analyses of brain development, aging, and disease trajectories. ComBat has also been extended to magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS), where it mitigated vendor and site effects for metabolites such as GABA+ and glutamate, revealing age- and sex-related associations previously obscured by site variability [173]. It has also proved effective for harmonizing volume- and surface-based MRI features. A Gaussian Process Classifier study across three research sites confirmed that ComBat reduces site-related variability while preserving biological associations, even on independent datasets, though performance varied by tissue type, highlighting tissue-specific considerations for future applications [174].

Other approaches for multi-site harmonization include linear mixed-effects models and deep learning methods such as domain adaptation and GAN-based harmonization [175, 176], though these typically require large datasets and may lack interpretability. NeuroCombat provides a more efficient and interpretable alternative by using regression with empirical Bayes to remove scanner effects while preserving covariate-related signals [177]. LongCombat extends this framework to longitudinal data by accounting for within-subject correlations [178, 179], and both methods have shown improved statistical power and cross-site reliability in diffusion MRI studies.

Using the B-Q MINDED dataset of repeated scans from healthy adults across two 3T scanners, a recent study demonstrated that NeuroCombat and LongCombat substantially reduced scanner-induced variability in diffusion metrics such as fractional anisotropy, mean diffusivity, and kurtosis measures, bringing inter-scanner variability close to scan–rescan levels [180]. Tailoring harmonization to the research context, whether ROI-based or voxel-wise, further improved alignment, underscoring the importance of harmonization in large, multi-site neuroimaging studies [180].

Challenging the large-cohort misconception: machine learning in small-n, high-p settings

The rapid adoption of AI in biomedical research has transformed expectations for data-driven discovery and precision medicine. In recent years, advances in biomedical AI have been increasingly driven by deep learning architectures, including CNNs, transformers, and foundation models, which typically achieve optimal performance when trained on very large datasets [181]. Consequently, there is a growing perception that robust machine-learning analyses invariably require large patient cohorts. While this assumption is often valid for data-intensive deep-learning applications, it should not be generalized to all machine-learning methodologies.

This distinction is particularly important for integrated multi-omics studies. Unlike imaging-based AI applications that can exploit large publicly available repositories, studies integrating clinical, imaging, and multiple omics modalities from the same patients are frequently constrained by intrinsically limited cohort sizes. Prospective clinical trials, longitudinal biomarker studies, precision medicine investigations, and many rare or heterogeneous brain diseases require the collection of multiple high-dimensional molecular and clinical datasets from each participant, making large, well-characterized cohorts difficult to assemble. Consequently, these studies are inherently characterized by a small-n, high-p data structure, in which the number of measured molecular features substantially exceeds the number of available patients [182].

Importantly, intrinsically limited cohort size does not preclude the application of machine learning. Rather, it requires methodologies specifically designed for high-dimensional, low-sample settings [183]. Regularized statistical learning, ensemble methods, and informed machine-learning approaches that integrate prior biological knowledge have shown considerable promise for improving robustness, generalization, and predictive performance in high-dimensional biomedical datasets with limited sample sizes, demonstrating that effective machine learning relies on methodologies tailored to the characteristics of the available data [184, 185]. For example, the COMET framework leverages transfer learning from large electronic health record datasets to improve prediction in substantially smaller multimodal omics cohorts, demonstrating how external knowledge can compensate for limited sample availability while preserving biological and clinical relevance [186]. Similarly, pretrained molecular representations, biological priors, multimodal data integration, and domain-adaptation strategies further illustrate that successful machine learning depends not only on absolute sample size but also on selecting methodologies appropriate for the available data, study design, and biological question.

This challenge is particularly evident in brain diseases. While AD has benefited from large, extensively characterized multimodal resources such as ADNI and related international consortia, comparable datasets integrating matched clinical, imaging, and multi-omics data remain considerably more limited for diseases such as MS and glioblastoma. As a result, many translational studies in these disorders are necessarily conducted using intrinsically small-n, high-p cohorts. The central challenge is therefore not whether machine learning can be applied, but rather how to identify analytical strategies that are appropriate for these data characteristics and ensure that their performance is evaluated using methodological criteria suitable for limited-cohort studies rather than expectations established for large-scale deep-learning applications.

Despite these advances, methodological guidance remains fragmented. Most published studies introduce individual algorithms or task-specific analytical solutions without providing investigators with a coherent framework for selecting appropriate machine-learning strategies according to sample size, data modality, biological objective, and available external resources. Future progress in precision medicine therefore requires a shift from viewing limited cohort size as a barrier to recognizing it as a methodological design problem. Rather than asking whether machine learning can be applied to integrated multi-omics studies with intrinsically small patient cohorts, the more appropriate question is which machine-learning methodologies are most suitable for the available data and how they should be designed, validated, and transparently reported to generate reliable, reproducible, and clinically meaningful biological insights.

Synthetic data generation to address data scarcity and imbalance in brain diseases

Synthetic data, artificially generated datasets that retain the statistical structure of real multimodal brain data, offer a powerful solution to challenges in brain disease research, including small or imbalanced cohorts, missing modalities, and General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)-driven restrictions on cross-border data sharing [187, 188]. Generative models, such as GANs, VAEs, and diffusion models, can produce realistic neuroimaging, omics, and clinical data that capture essential biological and structural patterns while enabling flexible augmentation of underrepresented patient groups.

Different GAN architectures address specific modality needs in brain disease research. Deep convolutional GANs (DCGANs) generate realistic neuroimaging (MRI, lesion maps) [189], while cGANs synthesize disease-specific abnormalities, including AD-related patterns [68]. In neuro-oncology, a cGAN–CNN trained on tumor MRI achieved an accuracy of 0.93–0.97, highlighting the potential value of this approach for glioma classification [190]. Cycle-consistent GANs (CycleGANs) support unpaired cross-modality translation (e.g. MRI↔CT) [191] and, combined with diffusion models, can produce synthetic tumor-bearing MRI data that improve unsupervised tumor detection and segmentation across BraTS2020 and external datasets [192]. For clinical tabular data, tabular GANs (TGANs), conditional tabular GANs (CTGANs), and TT-GAN models generate realistic synthetic electronic health records and multimodal patient profiles to augment small or imbalanced cohorts [193]. GANs also generate synthetic electroencephalography (EEG) time series that can enhance performance in brain–computer interface, emotion recognition, and seizure prediction tasks, underscoring their potential utility for low-resource neurophysiological data [194].

Synthetic omics data are especially valuable in brain disease research, where multi-omics data, particularly from single-cell and spatial omics, remain costly, technically demanding, and inconsistently available across cohorts. However, synthetic data generation for single-cell and bulk omics is less widely applied than synthetic generation for imaging. Conditional single-cell GANs (cscGANs) can generate realistically scRNA-seq profiles by learning nonlinear gene–gene dependencies across cell types, enabling augmentation of sparse populations, improving marker detection, strengthening classifier robustness, and supporting method evaluation when biosamples are limited [195]. Variational autoencoder approaches also contribute to synthetic omics generation. ACTIVA, an introspective VAE conditioned on cell type, produces realistic scRNA-seq profiles and can expand rare subpopulations on demand [196]. GAN-based models for bulk RNA-seq, such as Min-Max GAN, can replicate high-dimensional expression profiles and improve downstream classification when used for augmentation [197]. OmicsGAN extends synthetic generation to integrated multi-omics by jointly modeling two omics layers and their biological interaction network (such as miRNA–mRNA regulation), generating coherent synthetic datasets that have demonstrated improved predictive performance relative to the original data [198].

While synthetic omics generation has been demonstrated in cancer and generic omics contexts, published studies explicitly generating synthetic multi-omics profiles for major brain diseases (e.g. AD, MS, glioma), appear to be limited, underscoring a significant research gap in this domain. Generative frameworks, such as multiDGD, provide a potential strategy for addressing this gap by jointly modeling multiple omics modalities within a shared representation while accounting for sample covariates [199]. Adapting such frameworks to brain diseases could enable the generation of biologically coherent multimodal profiles while preserving relationships across molecular layers. This remains particularly challenging in brain diseases because multimodal cohorts are often small, longitudinal sampling is limited, and molecular measurements may originate from different tissues or biological compartments, complicating biologically coherent cross-modal generation.

The utility of synthetic data depends on rigorous validation to ensure that generated samples preserve the statistical, biological, and clinical characteristics of real-world datasets without introducing artifacts or bias. This is particularly important for synthetic omics, where batch effects and other technical variations may be learned and reproduced as apparent biological signals, while excessive correction may remove genuine biological variation [200]. Validation should include assessment of image realism, distributional fidelity, preservation of clinically relevant features, downstream model performance, and evaluation on independent real-world cohorts to determine whether synthetic augmentation improves predictive accuracy without compromising generalizability [201]. Evidence from medical imaging studies indicates that supplementing real datasets with synthetic images can improve both predictive performance and external generalizability when rigorously validated across independent cohorts [202]. In multicenter studies, synthetic data should be evaluated across institutions, imaging platforms, and patient populations to confirm robustness and avoid propagating site-specific biases. Consequently, synthetic datasets are best used to augment rather than replace real patient data, particularly for rare diseases and underrepresented patient subgroups [201].

Model interpretability and clinical transparency

A major barrier to clinical adoption of AI in brain diseases is the limited interpretability of many models, especially deep neural networks that function as “black boxes.” Clinicians require transparent and biologically plausible explanations for predictions, whether identifying disease subtypes, extracting imaging biomarkers, or integrating multi-omics profiles, without which even high-performing models face skepticism and limited clinical utility.

These concerns are heightened by persistent challenges in brain-disease diagnosis and management: (i) conventional neuroimaging often fails to reliably distinguish between subtypes, such as gliomas versus inflammatory lesions or AD versus vascular and mixed dementias; (ii) linking imaging features to histopathological or molecular signatures remains limited; (iii) predictive models for treatment planning and disease trajectory estimation are underdeveloped; and (iv) reliable tools for forecasting treatment response, complications, and long-term outcomes are scarce.

Explainable AI (XAI) is increasingly used to address these limitations by making multimodal predictions understandable to clinicians [203]. While most clinical XAI tools rely on saliency maps, recent work emphasizes broader forms of explanation, including case-based reasoning, textual rationales, and auxiliary cues—to better support clinical interpretation and communication [204]. Regulatory frameworks, including GDPR and the EU AI Act, emphasize transparency for high-risk medical AI systems [205], further underscoring the importance of XAI.

Recent advances demonstrate progress in brain-disease applications. In dementia detection, an unsupervised XAI framework combined neuroanatomical morphology with CNN-derived relevance maps and produced rule-based, example-based, and textual explanations that aligned with clinician judgment [206]. Similarly, Leandrou et al. developed an explainable XGBoost model based on MR radiomic features from the hippocampus and entorhinal cortex, using SHAP to identify the features driving diagnostic classification among normal controls, MCI, and AD. The model achieved high diagnostic performance while providing clinically interpretable explanations of disease-related imaging biomarkers [207]. Furthermore, graph theory-based models combined with SHAP have been used to explain brain connectivity alterations in AD, identifying key regions such as the hippocampus, amygdala, posterior cingulate, and precuneus that contribute to disease classification, thereby enhancing the transparency of network-based AI models [208].

In MS, SHAP-based attribution enabled transparent prediction of patient-specific disability trajectories from baseline MRI [209]. In neuro-oncology, integrating Grad-CAM, SHAP, and LIME within an explainable CNN allowed the model to focus on biologically relevant tumor regions while reducing architectural complexity and achieving high accuracy (99% on seen data; 95% on unseen data) [210]. Similarly, SHAP has been applied to radiomics-based prediction of overall survival in glioblastoma, enabling clinicians to identify the radiomic features, feature interactions, and patient-specific risk profiles driving model predictions, thereby improving the interpretability of personalized treatment planning [211]. Explainable deep-learning frameworks combining LIME, Grad-CAM, and SHAP have also demonstrated improved brain tumor detection while maintaining high fidelity, stability, and consistency across independent datasets [212]. Beyond neuroimaging, explainable AI has also been integrated into with multi-omics frameworks. For example, the PRISM-ML pipeline combined interpretable machine learning, multi-omics systems biology, and electronic health record data mining to identify patient-specific biomarkers, stratify molecular subtypes, and prioritize drug repurposing candidates for AD, demonstrating how XAI can improve the biological interpretability and clinical utility of multi-omics precision medicine models [213]. Together, these approaches highlight XAI’s growing potential to make multimodal AI clinically interpretable and actionable.

Reproducibility challenges

Reproducibility remains a major barrier to the adoption of AI–bioinformatics pipelines in brain disease research, where multimodal models must integrate heterogeneous imaging, omics, and clinical datasets. Irreproducibility arises from multiple sources, including data variability, model stochasticity, preprocessing inconsistencies, and hardware-dependent computational differences [214]. Model-level non-determinism further exacerbates instability. Deep neural networks commonly used in radiogenomics and multimodal fusion rely on stochastic optimization (e.g. SGD), random weight initialization, dropout, and GPU-accelerated operations, all of which can cause the same model and codebase to produce different results across runs, a phenomenon well documented in biomedical AI research [214]. Even when using the same overarching methodology, slight variations in parameter settings, preprocessing choices, or feature extraction procedures can lead to markedly different outputs, further undermining model stability and comparability across studies. Such sensitivity highlights the need for rigorous protocol standardization and transparent reporting to ensure that multimodal AI models for brain diseases behave reproducibly across datasets, sites, and computational environments.

Digital twin models for personalized disease simulation

Digital twins (DTs) are computational replicas of individual patients that maintain a bidirectional connection with real-world clinical data, enabling continuous updates and real-time simulation [215]. In the brain, DTs offer a framework for integrating multimodal information, including neuroimaging, electrophysiology, molecular profiles, and clinical trajectories, to predict future health outcomes and treatment response. By providing a virtual replica of a patient’s brain and neural systems, DTs may allow clinicians to simulate therapeutic interventions, anticipate disease evolution, and evaluate potential outcomes before applying them in practice [216]. Through in silico experimentation, DTs can be used to test therapeutic strategies, anticipate complications, and personalize intervention timing [216].

Emerging applications demonstrate the growing relevance of DTs in brain diseases. An AD digital-twin model (DADD) showed that personalized digital biomarkers derived from non-invasive EEG can reconstruct patient-specific neurodegeneration and enable preclinical AD detection [217]. The model predicted CSF biomarker positivity with 88% accuracy and future cognitive decline with 87% accuracy, outperforming standard EEG biomarkers [217]. In MS, digital twin methods have been used to estimate individualized brain atrophy trajectories by comparing patient MRI-derived neurodegeneration with a hypothetical healthy-aging twin, revealing that pathological thalamic atrophy may begin 5–6 years prior to clinical symptom onset and uncovering distinct early- versus simultaneous-onset subgroups [218].

Digital twins are also emerging in neuro-oncology. A data-driven DT framework for high-grade gliomas initializes mechanistic tumor-growth models using population priors and then personalizes them through Bayesian calibration with patient-specific MRI, enabling multi-objective optimization of radiotherapy under uncertainty [219]. Compared with standard-of-care dosing, these individualized regimens improved modeled time-to-progression and allowed substantial reductions in radiation exposure without compromising modeled tumor control [219].

Despite their potential, several challenges must be addressed before DTs can support routine clinical decision-making. Validation is particularly challenging for intervention-oriented DTs because outcomes under alternative treatments cannot be directly observed for the same patient. Accordingly, validation should extend beyond retrospective predictive accuracy to assess calibration, uncertainty, robustness, updating stability, counterfactual consistency, and the clinical consequences of DT-guided decisions [220]. Clinical implementation also depends on robust data and computational infrastructure capable of integrating and updating heterogeneous patient information [221]. However, healthcare DTs remain at an early stage of development, with data availability, technical infrastructure, model uncertainty, reproducibility, and prospective clinical validation representing important barriers to broader translation [221]. Prospective studies are therefore needed to determine whether DT-guided decisions improve patient outcomes compared with standard care. Finally, DTs that provide patient-specific diagnostic or therapeutic recommendations may require regulation as medical-device software, with evidence of safety and clinical performance and mechanisms for monitoring model performance and updates after deployment.

Sex and gender bias in AI models and big data

Sex is a fundamental biological variable influencing disease incidence, molecular mechanisms, progression, and treatment response across brain disorders. However, many AI and bioinformatics models are trained on sex-imbalanced datasets or without sex-stratified analyses, leading to systematic performance biases and reduced generalizability [222]. Compounding this issue, many biomedical AI systems may lack adequate mechanisms to detect or correct sex and gender bias, risking suboptimal or inequitable outcomes that conflict with the goals of precision medicine [222]. Such disparities are well documented; for example, a multi-institutional study found that machine-learning models consistently underdiagnosed female patients and produced lower AUROC values for women despite comparable clinical utility across thresholds [223]. In neuroimaging, sex differences are pronounced enough that deep learning models trained on diffusion MRI can classify sex with AUCs of 0.92–0.98, reflecting robust microstructural white-matter distinctions beyond macroscopic anatomy [224].

Although often conflated, “sex” and “gender” represent distinct constructs, sex reflects biological attributes, whereas gender encompasses sociocultural identity, behaviors, and structural factors that influence diagnostic pathways and data availability. Many datasets fail to distinguish these dimensions, making it difficult to disentangle biological from societal influences and hindering effective bias detection and mitigation [225].

Machine-learning systems also inherit sociocultural and structural inequities embedded in historical clinical practice and policy, and without explicit mitigation, they can perpetuate or amplify these biases [226]. This is especially consequential in brain diseases, where incidence and progression differ substantially between men and women. For instance, glioblastomas occur more frequently in men, whereas women with lower-grade gliomas often show better survival outcomes [227, 228], while AD and MS are more prevalent in women [229, 230]. These epidemiological and biological patterns underscore the need for AI models that explicitly account for sex as both a confounding and mechanistic variable, particularly in multimodal frameworks integrating imaging, genomics, and clinical data.

To mitigate these biases, AI models should incorporate sex as both a biological variable and a potential effect modifier throughout model development and validation. Recommended strategies include balanced recruitment of male and female participants, sex-stratified analyses, fairness-aware learning algorithms, reweighting or resampling techniques to address class imbalance, and evaluation of model performance separately for each sex. In multimodal frameworks, incorporating sex-specific molecular, imaging, and clinical features may improve biological fidelity while reducing prediction bias. External validation across diverse populations and transparent reporting of sex-disaggregated performance metrics are also essential to ensure equitable and clinically reliable AI systems for precision medicine [222].

Regulatory considerations for clinical translation

Beyond technical performance, the successful clinical deployment of AI models for brain disease diagnosis and prognosis requires compliance with evolving regulatory frameworks governing medical devices, data protection, and clinical validation. Within the European Union, AI-enabled clinical decision support systems are increasingly regulated under the Medical Device Regulation (MDR) and the recently adopted Artificial Intelligence Act (AI Act), which classifies many AI-enabled medical devices as high-risk systems requiring rigorous conformity assessment, risk management, transparency, human oversight, and post-market monitoring before clinical implementation [231]. These regulatory frameworks seek to ensure that AI systems remain safe, effective, and trustworthy throughout their lifecycle while balancing innovation with patient safety [232].

The GDPR further establishes strict requirements for the collection, storage, sharing, and processing of patient data, emphasizing data minimization, transparency, informed consent, privacy protection, and safeguards for automated decision-making. Compliance with GDPR is particularly important for multimodal AI models, which integrate highly sensitive neuroimaging, genomic, and clinical data across multiple institutions, making robust data governance and secure data-sharing frameworks essential for collaborative precision medicine [231].

AI systems intended for clinical decision support may be regulated as medical-device software, including Software as a Medical Device (SaMD), depending on their intended purpose and jurisdiction. Regulatory agencies, including the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the European Medicines Agency (EMA), require evidence appropriate to the device and its intended use before AI-based tools can be implemented in routine healthcare. Such evidence may include external multicenter validation, prospective clinical studies, assessment of robustness across diverse patient populations and imaging platforms, continuous post-market performance monitoring, and controlled management of model updates throughout the AI lifecycle [232]. Furthermore, explainability, bias assessment, cybersecurity, and comprehensive documentation of model development and validation are becoming important considerations in the regulation and governance of trustworthy AI systems. Addressing these regulatory requirements will be critical for translating multimodal radiomics–multi-omics AI frameworks from research environments into routine precision medicine for brain diseases.

Clinical validation and translational readiness

Robust clinical validation is essential for translating multimodal AI–bioinformatics frameworks into routine care. Despite rapid methodological progress, most models remain at low technology readiness levels due to limited external validation, scarce prospective testing, and heterogeneous clinical workflows. Multimodal systems combining radiomics, genomics, transcriptomics, and clinical data must show reproducibility across scanners, sites, acquisition protocols, and patient populations. Cross-cohort evaluation, using independent datasets, harmonization, and federated validation, is critical for demonstrating generalizability. Clinical readiness further requires evidence of meaningful endpoints, including improved diagnostic accuracy, earlier detection, actionable stratification, and enhanced treatment planning. Prospective and adaptive trials, as well as digital twin-based simulations, can strengthen validation by assessing individualized predictions and treatment-response forecasting.

Several multimodal AI frameworks have demonstrated encouraging translational performance through external or multicenter validation. For example, the GEIDI framework was evaluated using data from ADNI, integrating structural MRI, gene expression, and genotype information to identify personalized imaging–genetic associations to identify personalized imaging–genetic associations for AD biomarker discovery [121]. Similarly, a multimodal SNF framework applied to 972 ADNI participants integrated cognitive, genetic, and MRI data to identify clinically meaningful mild cognitive impairment subtypes, achieving ~90% diagnostic accuracy and validating subtype predictions using longitudinal cognitive assessments, fluid biomarkers, neuroimaging features, dementia diagnosis, and pseudo-trajectory analysis [164]. In neuro-oncology, multicenter validation has also been demonstrated using the Unified Multimodal Brain Imaging Foundation (UMBIF) model, which was pretrained on 51 029 routine brain MRI examinations collected across multiple institutions and subsequently validated on independent multicenter datasets for post-treatment outcome prediction and molecular profiling of gliomas. The model predicted clinically relevant biomarkers, including IDH mutation, MGMT promoter methylation, and 1p/19q codeletion, highlighting the potential of foundation models to support non-invasive molecular diagnostics and clinical decision-making [163]. Despite these encouraging findings, most multimodal AI systems remain at the retrospective validation stage, with relatively few undergoing prospective multicenter evaluation or routine clinical deployment [233].

Regulatory translation adds further complexity. Compliance with MDR, FDA guidance, and Good Machine Learning Practice (GMLP) principles requires appropriate transparency, documented model provenance, explainability, continuous monitoring, and management of data drift. Uncertainty quantification, calibration, and post-market surveillance are important considerations for clinical and regulatory acceptance. Seamless integration also requires interoperable deployment architectures aligned with HL7/FHIR, PACS/RIS, and hospital cybersecurity standards.

Successful translation requires moving beyond technical interoperability to address the operational realities of clinical workflows. Emerging approaches combining multimodal AI with agentic systems may support clinical integration by enabling AI systems to retrieve and interpret information across heterogeneous environments, including electronic health records, imaging systems, and laboratory databases [234]. Agentic AI may further support clinical decision-making and treatment planning by integrating diverse data sources and providing context-aware outputs, although its deployment introduces ethical, privacy, regulatory, and governance challenges that require interdisciplinary collaboration [235]. However, effective clinical implementation also depends on ensuring that these systems integrate with existing healthcare infrastructure and workflows without creating additional complexity for clinical users.

The design of clinician-facing interfaces is also important for effective implementation, with deployed prediction models using approaches such as non-interruptive dashboards, worklists, order-set linkage, and interruptive alerts. Successful implementation requires alignment with clinical workflows and has been supported by stakeholder co-design, clinician training, appropriate threshold selection, and performance monitoring. Adoption may otherwise be limited by workflow misalignment, alert fatigue, lack of transparency, and insufficient governance, emphasizing the importance of integrating AI in ways that support rather than disrupt clinical decision-making [236].

Ultimately, translational readiness depends on models that are reliable, interpretable, and embedded into multidisciplinary workflows. Close co-validation with clinicians, radiologists, and molecular tumor boards, together with integration into decision-support systems and assessment of real-world utility, is crucial for moving multimodal AI from experimental research to clinically trusted precision-medicine tools.

Implementation pathways and future directions

Successful translation of multimodal AI–bioinformatics frameworks requires aligned ethical, technical, and organizational progress. Core principles—transparency, fairness, accountability, privacy, and human oversight—must guide deployment, with strict adherence to GDPR, MDR, and institutional governance. Standardizing imaging protocols, metadata, and multimodal ontologies will improve interoperability and support privacy-preserving approaches such as federated learning.

Bias mitigation remains crucial. Systematic auditing of sex, ancestry, age, and socioeconomic biases, combined with synthetic augmentation, fairness-aware algorithms, and participatory design, is needed to ensure equitable performance. Close collaboration with clinicians, radiologists, scientists, and patients, and the use of interpretable visual analytics tools with uncertainty quantification, will be essential for real-world adoption. Knowledge-based components can further strengthen interpretability and regulatory acceptability.

Future research should focus on several technical and translational priorities. Multimodal models need to better integrate asynchronous longitudinal data collected at different clinical time points and connect molecular alterations with imaging phenotypes and clinical outcomes across biological scales. Brain foundation models should move beyond generic fine-tuning through modality-specific representations, including tokenization strategies for neuroimaging and electrophysiological data, and parameter-efficient fine-tuning approaches that enable adaptation to small disease-specific or rare-variant cohorts. Causal AI represents another important direction, with structural causal models and counterfactual inference providing a framework for estimating how individual patients might respond under alternative treatments rather than simply predicting outcomes from observed associations. Finally, the transition from retrospective validation to prospective evaluation could begin with non-interventional “shadow-mode” deployment, in which AI predictions are generated within clinical workflows without influencing patient care, followed by prospective multicenter and adaptive clinical trials that evaluate whether AI-guided decisions improve patient outcomes.

Economic evaluation is critical for the clinical translation of multimodal AI. Evidence across healthcare suggests that AI can improve cost-effectiveness by increasing diagnostic accuracy, improving quality-adjusted life years, reducing unnecessary procedures, and optimizing resource use [237]; however, evidence specific to multimodal AI in brain diseases remains limited. Current economic evidence also remains immature: only 28% of health economic evaluations of medical AI included implementation costs and 57% included operational costs, suggesting that reported cost-effectiveness may not reflect the full costs of AI deployment [238]. Future studies should therefore compare multimodal AI with standard care while accounting for implementation and operational costs and assessing value through clinically meaningful outcomes, including avoided invasive procedures, earlier diagnosis, improved treatment selection, healthcare resource use, and quality-adjusted life years.

Conclusion

The integration of artificial intelligence and bioinformatics is reshaping precision medicine in brain diseases, enabling unified analysis of radiomic, multi-omic, and clinical data that extends beyond the capabilities of any single modality. Across gliomas, AD, and MS, multimodal AI models have demonstrated potential to improve diagnostic accuracy, identify biological subtypes, and support individualized disease forecasting. Deep learning, graph neural networks, similarity-based fusion, and generative models together provide a powerful toolkit for capturing the complex, multiscale biology of the human brain.

Yet, translation to clinical practice remains constrained by data heterogeneity, limited reproducibility, and challenges in interpretability, regulation, and workflow integration. The current evidence base is also uneven across the diseases reviewed, with AD particularly well represented because of large, extensively characterized resources such as ADNI and AMP-AD, while comparable multimodal datasets remain more limited for MS and glioma. This imbalance may lead to AI models that are optimized for AD-specific data and patterns but generalize poorly to other brain diseases, limiting the development of transferable AI approaches across different disease contexts. Harmonization methods, synthetic data generation, digital twins, and sex-informed modeling offer a path toward more reliable, equitable, and clinically credible systems but must be validated with rigor and transparency.

Ultimately, realizing the promise of multimodal AI will require sustained investment in harmonized datasets, reproducible modeling pipelines, and prospective, real-world evaluation. By linking imaging-derived phenotypes with molecular and clinical profiles, AI–bioinformatics pipelines provide a path toward earlier detection, refined patient stratification, and genuinely personalized therapeutic strategies for complex brain diseases.
