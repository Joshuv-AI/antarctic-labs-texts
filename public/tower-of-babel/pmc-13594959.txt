Multi-omics analyses reveal steroid hormone and antioxidant pathways regulating sperm storage capacity in female Muscovy ducks (Cairina moschata)
Authors: Zhou Wu, Xiaohuan Chao, Zhiqiang Chang, Liming Lan, Xue Rong Ma, Weijian Zhu, Aijun Liu, Weiming Liang, Xiquan Zhang, Cheng Fang, Congliang Ji, Qingbin Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe duration of fertility in poultry is directly influenced by sperm store ability within specialized sperm storage tubules (SSTs) of the female uterovaginal junction (UVJ). Improving the duration of fertility reduces artificial insemination frequency, influencing animal welfare, and production costs. Here, we investigated physiological and multi-omic factors underlying sperm storage capacity in female Muscovy ducks (Cairina moschata). Phenotypes were collected from approximately 1,500 ducks for three generations, with focus on the peak and late laying stages. High and low sperm storage individuals were classified for histological evaluation and multi-omics analyses (transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics).Results show sperm storage capacity significantly declines with age, associated with ciliated cell integrity in the UVJ microenvironment. Multi-omics revealed enrichment in pathways, including steroid hormone biosynthesis, glutathione metabolism, and tryptophan metabolism. Metabolites such as cholesterol and progesterone were identified as potential biomarkers. Down-regulation of LIMA1 and CYP2R1, along with altered functional pathways, suggests coordinated regulation of antioxidation balance, immune response, and steroid synthesis within the UVJ microenvironment. Our findings provide a multi-omics framework for better understanding fertility duration and offer biomarker candidates and pathways for future genetic improvement in poultry reproduction.Keywords: Muscovy duck, Duration of fertility, Sperm storage, UVJ

Introduction

In avian species, ovulation and sperm arrival at fertilization site generally do not occur simultaneously. To address this and ensure continuous production of fertile eggs, the female reproductive tract has evolved the ability to store sperm while maintaining their viability (Sasanami et al., 2013; Assersohn et al., 2024). This sustained duration that females can lay fertile egg is referred to as the duration of fertility (DF), which is directly determined by sperm storage capacity of female individuals (Luo et al., 2024). Specifically, females utilize specialized sperm storage tubules (SSTs) located at the uterovaginal junction (UVJ) of the oviduct to reserve sperm, ensuring sperm availability at the time of ovulation. This mechanism also serves as a form of post-copulatory sexual selection. Notably, the precise synchronization between ovulation and sperm arrival is crucial for successful fertilization.

The sperm storage traits in female poultry refer to their capacity to retain viable sperm in the oviduct, therefore enabling the continuous production of ferti lized eggs. These traits are closely associated with the duration of fertility, which is an important economic trait in poultry production, especially with the widespread of artificial insemination (AI) technologies (Wang et al., 2024). As the poultry industry continues to expand, the extensive use of AI has created an urgent need to improve the sperm storage ability of female birds to improve efficiency of AI and production (Sasanami et al., 2013). Nowadays commercial poultry have been selected for consistent and high fertility, however variations in sperm storage duration still can be observed, which requires further investigation into the complexity of this trait and to improve it.

Muscovy ducks (Cairina moschata) are highly valued in the poultry farming due to their fast growth rate, high feed conversion efficiency, excellent meat quality, large body size, with economic value for both large-scale industry and small-scale farmers (Arias-Sosa and Rojas, 2021; Rufino et al., 2025). Despite the economic importance of sperm storage capacity in Muscovy ducks, the genetic and molecular mechanisms regulating the sperm storage trait remain poorly understood. Previous studies have suggested that the molecular structure of the oviduct and sex hormones may play potential roles in regulating sperm storage in various poultry species (Yang et al., 2020; Adetula et al., 2018, 2023; Chai et al., 2024a; b). However, conventional genomic approaches often fail to comprehensively elucidate the complex regulatory networks involved in this physiological process. Multi-omics integrative analysis provides a novel perspective for identifying key regulatory pathways underlying complex traits such as sperm storage capacity, which may involve the immune response within the microenvironment, steroid and hormone biosynthesis and antioxidant defense system. To disentangle the molecular mechanisms underlying sperm storage capacity in female Muscovy ducks, we recorded related phenotypes such as the Number of Fertilized Eggs (NFE) and Maximum Number of continuously produced Fertilized Eggs (MNFE) as indicators to assess the duration of fertility. Phenotypic data were collected during both the peak and late laying periods to compare the influence of laying age and to screen individuals with high or low sperm storage capacity. The vital tissue uterovaginal junction (UVJ) were collected for transcriptomic, and proteomics sequencing, while the serum was used for untargeted metabolomics analysis. The primary objective of this study was to identify differential genes, metabolites, and proteins associated with sperm storage capacities, thereby expand our understanding on the molecular regulatory network influencing sperm storage in Muscovy ducks.

Materials and methods

Experimental animals and study design

In order to better understand the genetic characteristics of duration of fertility, in this study, we measured sperm storage capacity traits in approximately 1,500 female Muscovy ducks. The Muscovy duck population was obtained from the breeding population of the breeding farm at the Waterfowl Division for Wen’s Group, and maintained under uniform housing conditions (ethics agreement: SYXK-2024-0136), the breed and age of studied individuals were controlled to control variation. During the trial period, fresh semen was collected from Muscovy drakes and assessed for quality under a microscope. Semen from selected drakes was then pooled, and diluted prior to use. Artificial insemination (AI) was performed on female ducks on two consecutive days at around the same time each afternoon. The insemination dose was standardized across all birds. Egg collection began on the morning of the third day following insemination. In case of soft-shelled eggs and cracked eggs, we opened them to determine fertilization status, which was recorded accordingly. The experiment was carried out at two laying stages: peak (33–35 weeks) and late (48–50 weeks) stages. Prior to the trial, all females underwent 15 to 18 days sperm depletion period to eliminate residual sperm from the reproductive tract. Each experimental trial lasted 12 days, during which eggs were collected, stored and transported to the hatchery. At the end of the trial, all eggs were fumigated for disinfection and set for incubation. Candling was performed on day 30 of incubation, and fertilization status was recorded. A total of 1,459 and 1,468 female Muscovy ducks recorded were at the peak and late laying stages, respectively. We subsequently excluded individuals with incomplete or unreliable records for further analyses.

Phenotyping and sample collection

We collected phenotypes for the two trial periods, and calculated indicators including (1) Effective Duration of fertility (ED): the number of days between AI and the last viable embryo before the first unhatched egg; (2) Maximum Duration of fertility (MD): the number of days between AI and the final viable embryo; (3) Maximum Number of continuously produced Fertilized Eggs (MNFE): highest sustained count of fertilized eggs within trial period; (4) Fertility duration day (FDD): the number of days from the day after AI to the last viable embryo before two cumulative unhatched eggs, minus any unhatched eggs (0 or 1) before two cumulative unhatched eggs; (5) Number of Fertilized Eggs (NFE): the number of viable embryos during the trial period. In addition to that we also calculated Individual Fertility Rate (IFR) during the trial period, and the Total Egg Production number (TEP). We then calculated the repeatability for the phenotypes (exception for IFR, was derived as an overall frequency from fertility data), and performed further statistical test between the two stages to compare the effect of laying stages.

Moreover, in order to compare the selection on DF trait across generation, we additionally looked into the record of last two generations (G9 and G10) and compared the overall phenotypes, providing extra evidence for determining the interval between AI. More than 1,500 female ducks were assessed per generation, before excluding individuals failed the quality control of data records. Due to data integrity considerations, significant test was performed exclusively between G10 and G11 (the studied cohort), while G9 data were presented to represent of the general characteristics and baseline of the population.

Grouping of high and low sperm storage capacity

We further selected individuals and classified them into high and low sperm storage capacity groups. The criteria were, individuals with TEP, NFE and FDD equal 12 were high sperm storage capacity (HSS) group; and individuals with TEP = 12, NFE ranging 4 to 6 and FDD ranging 4 to 6 were considered low sperm storage capacity (LSS) group. UVJ tissues and serum samples were collected immediately following the completion of the 12-day fertility assessment trial, after all egg records were finalized. This sampling time point captures the signatures associated with the maintenance phase of sperm storage, as opposed to the phase immediately following insemination.

Oil Red O staining

We employed multiple methods to observe the histological difference between HSS and LSS groups, including Hematoxylin-Eosin staining, Oil Red O staining, and electron microscopy (SEM/TEM) were used to observe the UVJ and SST structures.

Samples were taken out from low-temperature storage and allowed to gradually equilibrate to room temperature. The samples were then fixed using a designated fixative solution for 15 minutes, followed by thorough rinsing under running water and subsequent air-drying. The Oil Red O staining solution was prepared by mixing the dye with deionized water, and were filtered twice to obtain staining solution. Samples were immersed in the staining solution and incubated for the recommended duration under light-protected conditions, after which rapid differentiation was carried out using a series of isopropanol solutions (Sinoreaget, Shanghai) of decreasing concentrations to achieve clear staining. For subsequent washing and counterstaining, the samples were rinsed with water to remove excess stain, followed by secondary staining with hematoxylin (Servicebio, Wuhan). After staining, graded washing was performed to ensure uniform and distinct staining. The samples were briefly treated in a bluing solution (Servicebio, Wuhan), then given a final rinse with water. Finally, the samples were mounted using a mounting medium (Servicebio, Wuhan) for further analysis.

Hematoxylin-Eosin staining

Following standard tissue processing procedures, tissue samples were collected, fixed, and sectioned for Hematoxylin-Eosin (HE) staining. We used the Hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) HD Permanent Staining Kit (Sinoreaget, Shanghai, G1076-500ML) and followed manufacturer’s suggestions. In short, for paraffin-embedded sections, deparaffinization was performed by immersing the sections in dewaxing solutions (Servicebio, Wuhan), followed by sequential immersion in anhydrous ethanol and 75% ethanol (Sinoreaget, Shanghai), and rinsed with water. For frozen sections, samples were removed from −20 °C storage, allowed to bring back to room temperature, fixed with fixative solution (Sinoreaget, Shanghai) for 15 minutes, and thoroughly rinsed with water. Prior to staining, sections were pretreated for 1 minute. For hematoxylin staining, sections were stained for 3 to 5 minutes, then sequentially treated with water, differentiation solution, rinsed with water, and subjected to bluing treatment. For eosin staining, sections were dehydrated in 95% ethanol for 1 minute, followed by rapid staining for 15 seconds. During dehydration and mounting, sections were processed using anhydrous ethanol, n-butanol (Sinoreaget, Shanghai), xylene solutions (Sinoreaget, Shanghai), and finally mounted with neutral balsam. Finally, sections were examined under a microscope, and images were captured and analyzed to evaluate staining quality and tissue structure, which ensures high-quality tissue sections.

Scanning electron microscopy and transmission electron microscopy

As for scanning electron microscope (SEM), samples were precisely excised from fresh tissue to minimize mechanical damage. Dissection was completed within 1 to 3 minutes, and the sample size was limited to less than 3 mm². The samples were then gently rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). After preliminary processing, samples were immersed in fixative for 2 hours, then transferred to 4 °C for storage. The initially fixed samples were washed three times with 0.1 M phosphate buffer (PB, pH 7.4) to remove excess fixative. Subsequently, samples were post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide (Ted Pella Inc, Redding) for 1 to 2 hours. Then, samples were washed three times with phosphate buffer. Samples were then dehydrated through a graded series of ethanol concentrations from 30% to 100%, 15 minutes per step, followed by treatment with isoamyl acetate for 15 minutes to gradually remove moisture. Critical point drying was performed to avoid structural changes caused by water evaporation. Dried samples were mounted on conductive carbon tape and sputter-coated with gold for 30 seconds to enhance conductivity. The prepared samples were examined under a SEM, and high-resolution images were captured for to investigate the microstructure and characteristics of the samples.

As for transmission electron microscopy (TEM), the sample preparation was similar, with the following modifications. Tissue samples were trimmed to approximately 1 mm³ and fixed in TEM-specific fixative at 4 °C. Following post-fixation in 1% osmium tetroxide mixed with 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4), samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series followed by 100% acetone (Sinoreaget, Shanghai), then infiltrated and embedded in 812 resins (SPI, catalog number: 90529-77-4). After polymerization, ultrathin sections (60–80 nm) were cut, double-stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and finally observed under a TEM.

RNA-Sequencing of utero-vaginal junction (UVJ)

RNA-sequencing was performed for 8 UVJ samples, 4 for each HSS and LSS group. This sample size is consistent with previous studies in poultry reproductive tracts and provides sufficient statistical power when combined with appropriate correction and control procedures for false positive. The selection was constrained by the phenotypic criteria for HSS and LSS groups and the availability of extreme phenotype individuals from the population. RNA quality and integrity was evaluated using agarose gel electrophoresis, NanoPhotometer spectrophotometer, Qubit 2.0 fluorometer and Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. For library construction, mRNA was enriched by poly(A) tail capture using Oligo(dT) magnetic beads. Sequencing was performed on a NovaSeq 6000 platform to generate around 48 million reads per sample (approximately 7.2 Gb per sample). Raw reads underwent quality control, were assembled into transcripts using Trinity, and were further processed with Corset to remove redundancy and obtain Unigene sequences. Reads containing ambiguous bases and low-quality bases (Phred ≤ 5 for more than 50% of the read) were removed. High-quality reads were aligned to non-redundant Unigene sequences for gene expression quantification, followed by differentially expressed genes detection and functional annotation and enrichment analysis. We used StringTie to assemble novel transcripts and to annotate with further function. After filtering out the low-quality reads (mapping quality < 10, unpaired and multiple mapped reads), we used featurecounts of subread for gene quantification. Subsequently, differentially expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (Love et al., 2014), with suggested steps. After read count normalization, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were detected by comparing the HSS to the LSS using |log2(FoldChange)| ≥ 1 and adjusted P value ≤ 0.05, where P value was corrected using Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) FDR method.

4D- data-independent acquisition (DIA) proteomics sequencing

Frozen UVJ samples were ground to powder in liquid nitrogen to preserve protein integrity. We selected a total of eight samples for proteomic analyses, four samples each group. The powdered samples were lysed in a buffer containing 7 M urea, 2 M thiourea, 4% SDS, 40 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.5), 1 mM PMSF, and 2 mM EDTA to achieve comprehensive protein extraction and denaturation. Following incubation on ice, 10 mM DTT was added and samples were sonicated to reduce disulfide bonds. After centrifugation at 13,000 g for 20 minutes at 4 °C, the supernatant containing proteins was collected. Proteins were purified via acetone precipitation, reduced with DTT at 56 °C, alkylated with iodoacetamide, and quantified using the BCA method. For trypsin digestion, 100 µg of protein per sample was digested overnight at 37 °C with trypsin at a 1:50 ratio. The resulting peptides were desalted using C18 cartridges, vacuum-dried, and reconstituted in 0.1% formic acid prior to LC-MS/MS analysis.

Peptide separation was conducted using a nano-flow liquid chromatography (LC) system with a 60-minute gradient, and mass spectrometry (MS) analysis was performed on a timsTOF Pro2 instrument in positive ion mode using ddaPASEF and diaPASEF acquisition modes. DIA-NN software (version 1.8.1) was employed for DIA (Data-Independent Acquisition) database searching and protein quantification using a Libraryfree approach against a species-specific UniProt database (including 28,714 sequences). Deep learning-based spectral library prediction and match-between-runs (MBR) functionality were enabled, and identifications were filtered at a 1% false discovery rate (FDR) at both the peptide and protein levels. Protein quantification was performed using the MaxLFQ algorithm, the intensity values of each protein across different samples were extracted and subjected to within-sample normalization to obtain the relative quantitative values (R) of proteins across samples. Differentially expressed proteins between groups were identified using t-test with BH adjustment for multiple testing.

Metabolomics analysis on serum

Serum samples were first vortexed for 10 seconds to ensure homogeneity, and 50 µL of each sample was transferred to centrifuge tubes. Extraction solution (300 µL of 20% acetonitrile in methanol) was added, and the mixture was vortexed for 3 minutes. Following centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 minutes at 4 °C, 200 µL of supernatant was transferred to a new centrifuge tube and stabilized at −20 °C, followed by an additional centrifugation for 3 minutes. Data acquisition was performed using an ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography system (Shimadzu) coupled to a Triple TOF-6600 mass spectrometer. Separation was achieved on an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 C18 column with a mobile phase consisting of 0.1% formic acid in water (phase A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (phase B) at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min. The column temperature was maintained at 40 °C, and the input volume was 2 µL.

Raw mass spectrometry data were processed, and peak extraction and retention time correction were performed using ProteoWizard and XCMS software. Peak areas were normalized using support vector regression (SVR), and peaks with a missing rate exceeding 50% were excluded. Metabolite identification was carried out using an in-house database, integrated public databases, an AI-predicted library, and the metDNA method. Quality control (QC) samples, prepared by pooling aliquots of all samples, were used to assess consistency with one QC sample every six runs. Processed data were analyzed using CD 3.1 software for peak alignment, peak extraction, and filtering based on mass-to-charge ratio and retention time. Molecular formulas were predicted from precursor and fragment ions, and metabolites were identified by matching against mzCloud, mzVault, and Masslist databases. Raw quantitative data were normalized to obtain metabolite identification and quantification. Subsequent functional annotation was performed using the KEGG, HMDB, and LIPID Maps databases. Data were processed using metaX software, and partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) was applied to calculate variable importance in projection (VIP) values for each metabolite. Metabolites with VIP > 1 and P < 0.05 were considered significantly differential metabolites. Metabolic pathway enrichment analysis was then conducted using the MBRole 2.0 online tool (https://csbg.cnb.csic.es/mbrole2/) based on the KEGG database, with statistically significant threshold of P < 0.05.

Integrated multi-omics analysis was performed in a stepwise manner based on eight samples, including four individuals in each group. On the basis of differential analyses, pairwise integration was first conducted between each pair of omics datasets by overlapping DEGs, DEPs, and DAMs and mapping them simultaneously to KEGG pathways to clarify the relationships between genes, proteins and metabolites. We first performed this between each pair of analyses for a specific perspective, then we comprehensively integrated all omics analyses to obtain a more complete profile of differences between groups. We calculated the correlations between the genes, compounds and metabolites and visualized the results by bar plots and bubble plots based on the KEGG pathways commonly enriched by datasets, showing the numbers of DEGs, DEPs in each pathway. Moreover, correlation-based integration was performed by calculating Pearson correlation coefficients between differentially abundant metabolites and differentially expressed genes or proteins to identify enriched pathways among these correlated molecules. Additionally, Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCA) was applied to visualize the correlation structure among genes, proteins, and metabolites within pathways of special interest. This multi-layered approach allowed us to identify commonly regulated pathways and capture both direct and indirect associations.

Measurement of serum progesterone concentration

For serum progesterone concentration measurement, 40 venous blood samples (2 mL each) were collected from female ducks with high and low sperm storage capacity using EDTA-free vacuum blood collection tubes. The samples were stored at room temperature until blood coagulation and serum separation. Subsequently, the samples were centrifuged at 3,500 rpm for 5 minutes, and the resulting serum was transferred. An enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) was performed including blank control wells, standard curve wells, and sample wells. Diluent (40 μL) was added to each sample well, followed by 10 μL of diluted serum (resulting in a 5-fold dilution). Enzyme-labeled antibody (100 μL) was added to all wells except the blank wells. After sealing, the plate was incubated at 37 °C for 60 minutes. A 20-fold diluted wash solution was prepared to wash the wells, then blotted dry. Chromogenic substrates A and B (50 μL each) were added to each well, mixed, and incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 15 minutes. Stop solution (50 μL) was added to terminate the reaction. Optical density (OD) was measured at a wavelength of 450 nm using a spectrophotometer, with the blank well used for zero calibration, and all measurements were completed within 15 minutes after adding the stop solution.

Results

Overview of phenotypic statistics

In this study, we focused on the traits that are indicators of sperm storage capacity, and investigated the phenotypic, histological, and multi-omic differences between the high and low sperm storage capacity in female Muscovy ducks. The phenotypic traits related to sperm storage capacity were measured, including the ED, MD, MNFE, FDD, TEP, NFE and IFR (Table S1, see Methods for description). In general, mean values for all traits were higher during the peak laying stage (33–35 weeks) than the late laying stage (48–50 weeks), but only IFR showed a statistical significance between the two stages (P < 0.01). Frequency distribution histograms for each phenotypic trait were generated to compare the two trail stages (Fig. S1A). We noted that median values for all phenotypes shift leftward with increasing laying age, indicating a decline in sperm storage capacity. Coefficients of variation for all traits exceed 20%, with several traits such as the NFE, MNFE, and FDD close to 40%, suggesting considerable phenotypic variation in sperm storage capacity within the population. The phenotypic correlation for the peak stage (33–35 weeks) (Fig. 1A) demonstrated that NFE shows positive correlation with FDD, MNFE, MD and ED (correlation coefficients: 0.78, 0.85, 0.68, and 0.78, respectively. Similarly, during the late stage (48-50 weeks), NFE shows positive correlations with FDD, MNFE, MD and ED (correlation coefficients: 0.60, 0.85, 0.68, and 0.80, respectively. Notably, ED and DM exhibit strongest positive correlations in both stages (0.92 and 0.89, respectively). The individual fertility rate in the two stages is shown in Fig. 1B. The correlation coefficients between the NFE and other phenotypic traits remained relatively stable across stages, suggesting that for practical production the FE trait can serve as a representative indicator for selection on sperm storage capacity in Muscovy ducks. To verify this, we compared the phenotypic records across generations (Fig. 1C), and showed that the G10 and G11 show significantly difference (P value < 0.001) in the TEP, NFE, and FDD, and (P value < 0.05) in MD and MNFE, with no significant difference in EN.

Regarding the artificial insemination (AI) interval, cross‑generation fertility rate curves demonstrated that employing a 5‑day AI interval enables a fertility rate above 90% (Fig. S1B). Furthermore, the differences observed between generations suggest that genetic selection for this trait could further extend the AI interval. In addition, repeatability was estimated based on phenotypic data and is shown in Table S2. Results show that the repeatability values for estimated phenotypes are relatively low, ranging from 0.01 to 0.26. This suggests that these phenotypes can be largely influenced by environmental factors and further implies low heritability, indicating that while improving sperm storage traits through selection may be challenging, thus more efforts are needed for potential genetic gain.

When look into the difference in serum progesterone concentration measured in HSS and LSS female Muscovy ducks. A highly significant difference (P < 0.001) was observed between the two groups (Fig. 1D).

Histological examination of the utero-vaginal junction (UVJ) and sperm storage tubules (SST)

HE staining and Oil Red O staining results showed, the luminal surface of the sperm storage tubules (SSTs) is lined by a simple columnar epithelium consist of tall prismatic cells, with a small number of goblet cells scattered throughout (Fig. 2AB). In vertical section, the cells appear tall columnar, and the apical surface exhibits densely arranged microvilli, forming a brush border or striated border visible under light microscopy. These suggest that the epithelial cells of the SSTs possess absorptive and secretory functions. Oil Red O staining confirmed the presence of lipid droplets within the SSTs, suggesting active lipid synthesis and transport that may support sperm survival (Fig. 2C). Further sample assessment can be found in Fig. S2.

As for SEM and TEM examination (Fig. 2D), TEM revealed that for LSS and HSS samples, ciliated cells in the UVJ exhibited edema or swelling, membrane disruption, and organelle swelling, while cilia remained structurally intact with normal “9 + 2″ microtubule organization. Mitochondria, rough endoplasmic reticulum, goblet cells and tight junctions were observed. Moreover, SEM results showed abundant ciliated cells distributed across the endometrium. Taken together, compared to the HSS sample, the LSS group tend to have fewer ciliated cells and exhibited more subtle cellular damage, suggesting that sperm storage trait may be associated with the number and structural integrity of ciliated cells.

Differentially expressed genes between the high and low sperm storage capacity

In total, we generated around 578 million reads which were processed for overall expression profile assessment and differentially expressed gene detection. Biological replicates showed high consistency across all samples, although clustering within groups was not always clear, but we observed separation between groups, which may indicate expression differences between high and low sperm storage capacity groups and within group variation (Fig. S3). A total of 2,679 differentially expressed genes (DEGs, cutoff: P value ≤ 0.05, |log2FoldChange| ≥ 1) were identified, including 1,436 upregulated and 1,243 downregulated genes (Fig. 3AB, Table S3), demonstrating significant differences in gene expression associated with sperm storage capacity, and their subcellular localization. For instance, LIMA1 encodes LIM Domain And Actin Binding 1, a cytoskeleton-associated protein that inhibits actin filament depolymerization and cross-links filaments in bundles, which is involved in membrane ruffling, steroid metabolic process and cholesterol homeostasis (Duethorn et al., 2022).

GO enrichment analysis was performed to further elucidate the functional annotations of DEGs. Several terms that may associate with sperm storage function were identified (Fig. 3C). Key biological processes such as “translation”, “peptide biosynthetic process”, and “protein transport” indicate active protein production and trafficking, which are essential for maintaining sperm viability during prolonged storage. Cellular components including “ribosome”, “Golgi apparatus”, and “endomembrane system” reflect the cellular function required for protein synthesis, modification, and secretion within a tissue. Molecular functions like “structural molecule activity”, “ligase activity”, and “cofactor binding” further support the metabolic and structural demands of sperm storage environments. Collectively, these GO terms suggest that the UVJ tissue involved in sperm storage possess high translational activity, secretory capacity, and well‑developed membrane trafficking systems to sustain sperm function and viability. The upregulated genes identified in HSS relative to LSS were significantly enriched in biological processes (BP) related to “translation” and “peptide biosynthesis”, cellular components (CC) associated with “ribosomes” and “ribonucleoprotein complexes”, and molecular functions (MF) involving “structural constituent of ribosome” and “structural molecule activity”, and “peptide transport” (Fig. S4A). Whereas, downregulated genes were enriched in terms related to “protein transport”, “endomembrane system”, “protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum”, and “peptidase complex components”, in addition to various metabolic processes and associated membrane structures (Fig. S4B). In addition, genes involved in sperm maturation was also found to be differentially expressed, such as gene encoding keratin, type II cytoskeletal 5 (K2C5) and ADAM Metallopeptidase Domain 28 (ADAM28). K2C5 participates in sperm maturation, research in mice have showed that it is essential for spermatogenesis and male fertility (Kitakaze et al., 2025). ADAM28, a member of the ADAM family, is highly expressed in the epididymis and contributes to sperm maturation as well as sperm egg interaction during fertilization. ADAM28 also mediates cell adhesion and proteolysis in lymphocyte migration and regulates the ectodomain shedding of lymphocyte surface proteins, revealing its diverse biological functions beyond reproductive regulation (Howard et al., 2000; Zhao et al., 2025). We have visualized the expression levels of key candidate genes between the LSS and HSS groups to demonstrate the differential patterns across the groups (Fig. S4C).

Among the KEGG pathways enriched in the DEGs, several are potentially associated with sperm storage function (Fig. 3D). Upregulated pathways such as “ribosome”, “mTOR signaling”, and “RNA degradation” suggest active protein synthesis and RNA turnover mechanisms essential for maintaining sperm viability over extended periods. “Glycerophospholipid metabolism” and “endocytosis” point to dynamic membrane remodeling and nutrient uptake, while “regulation of actin cytoskeleton” and “Wnt signaling” support structural integrity and cellular organization. Conversely, downregulated pathways including “lysosome”, “calcium signaling”, and “cytokine‑cytokine receptor interaction” may reflect a protective environment that suppresses sperm degradation, and minimizes immune responses against stored sperm. Together, these pathway patterns depict a specialized cellular state optimized for long‑term sperm preservation and controlled release.

Proteomics demonstrates differentially expressed proteins enriched in cilium, lipid transport and immune response

In proteomic analysis of the UVJ, a total of 102,838 peptides and 8,666 proteins were identified, of which 8,665 were quantified. PCA plot shows that the HSS and LSS groups were distinctly separated into independent clusters, with samples within each group exhibiting a high level of similarity (Fig. S5AB). The number of proteins and peptides, the length of peptides and missed cleavages altogether demonstrated that overall data quality is ensured, which allows the subsequent analyses (Fig. S5CD). The majority of identified peptides ranged from 7 to 20 amino acids in length, consistent with typical tryptic digestion and mass spectrometry fragmentation patterns, and this distribution met the general quality control standards.

A total of 741 differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were identified, including 502 upregulated and 239 downregulated proteins (Fig. 4AB). Subcellular localization analysis showed that the majority of DEPs were localized to the cytoplasm (28.34%), followed by the nucleus (27.13%), and the number of up-regulated proteins in these categories are much higher than the down-regulated ones (Fig. 4CD). GO functional annotation yielded 628 enriched terms (Fig. 4E), with BP accounting for 40.61%, MF 37.74%, and CC 21.18%. Key GO terms included “cilium assembly” (P value = 1.96 × 10-18), “9 + 2 motile cilium”, “ATP-dependent microtubule motor activity, minus-end-directed”, “spermatogenesis”, “lipid transporter activity”, “calcium ion binding”, and immune-related processes such as “adaptive immune response”, and “antigen processing and presentation”. The repeatability of metabolite extraction and detection was assessed by overlaying total ion current (TIC) chromatograms of quality control (QC) samples, which showed good consistency (Fig. S7). Integration of transcriptome, proteome, and metabolome in sperm storage capacity

In this study, transcriptomic, metabolomic, and proteomic data were integrated to preliminarily explore the potential correlations between DEGs, DAMs, and DEPs. First of all, transcript information was matched and integrated with identified protein, visualized by a Venn diagram (Fig. 6AB). A total of 37 DEGs and DEPs showed a one-to-one correspondence. The Scatter plot for the high and low groups revealed a certain degree of correlation between transcript and corresponding protein expression levels (Fig. S9A). Correlation analysis was performed on the fold changes of proteins or genes identified in both groups, indicating that differential expression of genes and proteins showed a generally correlated pattern. Integrated GO enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment in BP related to “proteolysis”, “immune response”, and “actin cytoskeleton organization”; in CC associated with “cytoskeleton”, “Golgi membrane”, and “plasma membrane”; and in MF including “calcium ion binding” and “oxidoreductase activity”. KEGG pathway analysis (Fig. 6C) further showed significant enrichment in “motor proteins”, “phagosome”, “purine metabolism” and “Intestinal immune network for IgA production”, “glycerophospholipid metabolism”, and “steroid hormone biosynthesis”, suggesting the key roles in the molecular regulation of sperm storage played by DEPs and DEGs (Table S6).

Subsequently, integrated transcriptome and metabolome analysis revealed clear clustering patterns between DEGs and DAMs, indicating strong correlations between them (Fig. S9B). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis identified common enrichment in “purine metabolism”, “biosynthesis of cofactors”, “nucleotide metabolism”, “glycerophospholipid metabolism”, “glycolysis/gluconeogenesis”, “steroid biosynthesis”, and several other metabolic pathways, suggesting coordinated regulation at both transcriptional and metabolic levels (Fig. 6D). Based on pathway enrichment results and prior knowledge, we focused on biological processes including glucose metabolism, hormone synthesis, lipid metabolism, and amino acid synthesis and metabolism. For correlation analysis between DAMS and DEGs, screening criteria were set as Pearson correlation coefficient > 0.80 and P-value < 0.05, we identified a list of 27 DAMs and 157 DEGs identified across 36 related metabolic pathways (Table S7). Key DEGs were associated with glutathione metabolism, steroid biosynthesis, glycolysis, and hormone synthesis, including glutathione reductase, sterol 14α-demethylase, glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase, and estradiol 17-dehydrogenase. Corresponding DAMs included cholesterol, tryptophan, valine, and xanthosine, suggesting coordinated regulation of redox balance, energy metabolism, and steroid hormone pathways.

Furthermore, we focused on integrated pathway mapping of DEPs and DAMs (Fig. S9C), which reveals co-enrichment in key metabolic pathways, including “glycolysis/gluconeogenesis”, “steroid biosynthesis”, “purine and pyrimidine metabolism”, “tryptophan metabolism”, “glycerophospholipid metabolism”, and “nucleotide metabolism” (Fig. 6E). These commonly enriched pathways were prioritized for further interpretation, suggesting their involvement in the regulation of sperm storage capacity. We further focused on DEPs and DAMs with strong correlations (Pearson correlation > 0.80, P < 0.05). These correlated molecules were primarily enriched in pathways related to “glutathione metabolism”, “nucleotide metabolism”, “purine metabolism”, “glycerophospholipid metabolism”, “pyruvate metabolism”, “cofactor biosynthesis”, and “glycerolipid metabolism” (Table S8), highlighting the regulation of redox balance, energy metabolism, and lipid dynamics in sperm storage.

By integrating investigation among DEGs, DEPs and DAMs, we identified 32 enriched KEGG pathways that were commonly enriched across three datasets, such as “glutathione metabolism”, “steroid biosynthesis”, and “tryptophan metabolism” (Fig. 6F). We further focused on the Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCA) for the KEGG pathways that are of special interest, and visualized by four quadrants graphs. Discussion

In this study, we integrated phenotypic measurements with transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic data to investigate sperm storage in Muscovy ducks. Repeatability estimates were low (0.01–0.26), meaning single measurements poorly reflect true storage capacity. Currently, no standardized evaluation system exists for sperm storage traits in poultry, making it difficult to define individual sperm storage capacity (Wen et al., 2022). Accurately characterizing this capacity is therefore a critical first step toward genetic improvement. Previous studies have developed several phenotypic indicators based on egg production data, including ED, MD, MNFE, FDD, and NFE (Kabir et al., 2026). Our study further demonstrated the population variation that some hens produce fertilized eggs continuously, while others do so irregularly, highlighting the need for a comprehensive indicator that captures sperm storage capacity in Muscovy ducks. In general, FDD provides a comprehensive measure across the entire experimental period, and when combined with the NFE, ensures that both persistence and laying performance are accounted for. The correlation between the NFE and FDD decline with the age of laying, suggesting it is reasonable to include both as complimentary indicators for the phenotype. Although the low repeatability implies low heritability, the NFE shows high phenotypic correlations with other sperm storage traits (r = 0.68 to 0.85) and remains relatively stable across the two laying stages, making it a reliable indirect selection criterion to improve breeding efficiency. From an egg production perspective, the daily fertility rate curves demonstrated that performing artificial insemination every 5 days maintained a fertility rate above 90%. This strategy could be further refined by tailoring the insemination interval to the physiological status of the flock, applying a 5‑day interval during the peak laying period and possibly adjusting it during the end of lay to balance reproductive performance with production costs.

Histological observations revealed ciliary damage and cellular disorganization in the UVJ of LSS capacity ducks, the multi‑omics data further pointed to functional enrichment in cilia structure, steroid metabolism pathways, and immunity.

Previous studies established that the physiological basis for sperm storage in birds lies in the presence of numerous sperm storage tubules (SSTs) in the uterovaginal junction (UVJ) of the reproductive tract, along with specialized structures that continuously to support prolonged sperm storage until fertilization. With TEM and SEM assessment of ultrastructure, we confirmed that abundant cilia on the endometrial surface of the UVJ, with secretory epithelial cells scattered among them. This was further supported by functional enrichment in proteomic findings, where the “motor proteins”, “cilium assembly”, “microtubule-based movement” and “extracellular region” are highlighted for the DEPs. These observations are in line with studies in the same structure of the avian oviduct (Waqas et al., 2016). Ciliated cells facilitate mucosal movement and promote sperm transport, whereas secretory cells produce different small molecules that maintain the physiological activity of the reproductive tract (Avilés et al., 2010). Ultrastructural studies of the chicken reproductive tract have not fully addressed sperm–epithelial interactions, however, one study reported that sperm heads penetrate deeply into the cilia of oviductal epithelial cells, with no lysosomes observed around the cilia, suggesting that ciliated cells may play a supportive role in sperm storage (Chen et al., 2015). More recently, ciliated cells have been shown to possess secretory capabilities similar to those of secretory cells (Waqas et al., 2015). In llama, ciliated cells have been reported to predominate in the uterotubal epithelium (Apichela et al., 2009). In turkeys, the apical surface of SST epithelial cells have been reported to secrete microvesicular bodies and that, when sperm are present, sperm heads make intimate contact with microvilli, with plasma membrane exchange occurring via secretory vesicles (Bakst and Bauchan, 2015). These previous studies indicate that both secretory and ciliated epithelial cells in the female reproductive tracts contribute to sperm storage through their secretory activities, with ciliated cells additionally providing motility that may interact with sperm flagella to facilitate sperm release. Although the avian female reproductive systems are highly similar, whether SST epithelial cells in Muscovy duck function similarly as those in turkeys or other studied avian requires further investigation. These previous findings and our results together highlight the importance of molecular structure and function of the ciliated cell in determining the sperm storage capacity.

When further investigating into the microenvironment of UVJ and SSTs, we highlight the important process of lipid synthesis and composition within. We showed the active regulation of lipid secretion in SST within UVJ, supporting by DEGs that are involved in lipid and steroid synthesis or metabolism, such as LIMA1, CYP51A1, HMGCS1, PANK3 and NAT8B. For instance, LIMA1 exhibits protein-protein interaction with ZFP41-22, a putative DNA-binding regulatory protein associated with meiosis in human spermatogenesis (Horisawa-Takada et al., 2021). CYP51A1 and HMGCS1, both encode key enzyme in steroid biosynthesis, and PANK3, which controls coenzyme A (CoA) biosynthesis for fatty acid oxidation and synthesis. Reduced expression of these genes suggests modulated lipid metabolism in the UVJ microenvironment of HSS birds. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that independent validation of key candidate genes and metabolites using methods such as immunohistochemistry was important to further understand functions and regulatory networks. Future studies should incorporate validation approaches to confirm the functional roles of candidate molecules in regulating sperm storage capacity.

Integrated investigation across transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic results identified three commonly enriched pathways, “steroid hormone biosynthesis” (Fig. S10), “steroid biosynthesis”, and “primary bile acid biosynthesis”. Cholesterol was the common metabolite identified across these pathways. In the steroid biosynthesis pathway, cholesterol was upregulated in the low sperm storage capacity group, while several differentially expressed genes, including CYP51, LBR, MESO1, ERG25, CYP2R1, and DHCR7 were downregulated. And DEPs such as FDFT1, DHCR24, and DWF1 were also shown to exhibit decreasing trends. Cholesterol, a lipid molecule of the steroid family, serves as a critical component of cell membranes, modulating membrane fluidity and stability, and acts as a precursor for numerous bioactive molecules, including cortisol, aldosterone, and sex hormones. In the primary bile acid biosynthesis pathway, cholesterol was upregulated, accompanied by upregulation of cytochrome P450 family 46 subfamily A member 1 (CYP46A1), potentially influencing bile acid synthesis. This may suggest the important role CYP46A1 plays in lipid metabolism that may regulate the microenvironment of the sperm storage tubules. However, bile acids are primarily synthesized from cholesterol in the liver and released into the small intestine to regulate lipid metabolism, their presence and function in the avian reproductive tract have not been well established, and their specific role in sperm storage requires further investigation. In the steroid hormone biosynthesis pathway, SRD5A1 was down-regulated in the high sperm storage capacity group. SRD5A1 encodes an enzyme that converts testosterone to the potent androgen 5α-dihydrotestosterone (DHT), and also converts progesterone or corticosterone to their 5α-reduced metabolites. Interestingly, we showed that serum progesterone concentration was significantly higher in the HSS group compared to the LSS ones. This may explain the pattern with the down-regulation of SRD5A1 in the low-capacity group and the associated reduction in progesterone metabolism. Within the steroid biosynthesis pathway, CYP2R1, a key enzyme in vitamin D metabolism that converts vitamin D to the active ligand for the vitamin D receptor. Its expression was down-regulated in the HSS group. Studies in quail have shown that dietary supplementation with 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D₃] prolongs sperm storage duration (Lucio et al., 2017). In human reproductive research, it has been reported to significantly increase intracellular calcium concentration and sperm motility in mature spermatozoa (Blomberg Jensen et al., 2011, 2012). Additionally, 25-hydroxyvitamin D exhibits antioxidant properties, thus supplementation of semen extenders with 25-hydroxyvitamin D could extend sperm viability in partridges (Goodarzi et al., 2019). These are in line with the results of the present study. Together, these support the hypothesis that low-capacity ducks have reduced conversion of cholesterol to sex hormones and vitamin D metabolites, leading to lower level of 5α-dihydrotestosterone and 25-hydroxyvitamin D. Therefore, we propose using cholesterol, progesterone, and 25(OH)D₃ as potential biomarkers of sperm storage.

Phosphatidylcholine (PC), a major phospholipid component of cell membranes, was identified as a key metabolite in the integrated analysis. The phospholipase A2 group IVA (PLA2G4A), which hydrolyzes membrane phospholipids to release arachidonic acid and other bioactive lipids, was less abundant in the HSS group, whereas Lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC) was less abundant in the high-capacity group. PC is an essential component of sperm cell membranes. Spermatozoa within SSTs require energy exchange with the surrounding epithelium to maintain basic metabolism, and substantial energy consumption occurs during sperm activation and transport to the fertilization site. As a critical membrane component, PC is actively involved in sperm membrane fluidity, energy supply, acrosomal reactions and sperm motility. Furthermore, PC exhibits antioxidant properties that may protect sperm from oxidative stress during storage in the female reproductive tract, potentially extending sperm survival. In dogs, significant differences in sperm plasma membrane composition were observed between high- and low-motility populations, with PC, identified as one of the motility markers (Lucio et al., 2017). Further studies show PC treatments have improved acrosomal responses in human sperm and prevented enzymatic degradation of enzymes in turkey semen storage. Studies on turkey sperm preservation have demonstrated that supplementation with PC in extenders allows exogenous phospholipids to incorporate into the sperm plasma membrane, counteracting the detrimental effects of lipid peroxidation and enzymatic degradation during storage (Long and Conn, 2012). Studies on human male infertility have reported higher PC levels in infertile groups (Correnti et al., 2023), which may explain the higher abundance we found in LSS compared to HSS group within the positive channel. Previous studies have demonstrated the double-edged sword functions of lipid in chicken sperm storage, that excessive secretion may be detrimental (Wen et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2021b; a). Unlike cholesterol, PC showed a more complex pattern with opposite direction, pointing to complex lipid dynamics across storage groups.

Across the multi-omic analyses, the “Glutathione metabolism” pathway (Fig. S11) was also identified significant, the higher abundance of trypanothione and the increased expression of GGT7, GPX4, and GSTA3 in the UVJ tissues may suggest the antioxidant protection during the storage. It is widely acknowledged glutathione, trypanothione and glutathione peroxidase are crucial antioxidant to protect sperm from oxidative stress across organisms (O’Flaherty, 2015). The superfamily of GSH transferases (GSTs) plays a key antioxidant role, primarily by mediating the formation of mixed disulfides with surface exposed cysteines, thereby protecting proteins from oxidative damage under cellular stress (Tossounian et al., 2024). Because their function, supplementation of semen extenders with glutathione and glutathione transferase has been commonly used during cryopreservation which significantly prolongs sperm viability (Llavanera et al., 2020; Zou et al., 2021). These suggest that the observed gene, protein or metabolite changes in the glutathione metabolic pathway may influence sperm storage duration within the female reproductive tracts, and both glutathione and trypanothione and their derivatives may serve as biomarkers associated with sperm storage capacity. To further confirm the functional significance of glutathione metabolism in sperm storage, we note that direct measurements of oxidative stress markers (e.g., ROS levels, lipid peroxidation, the GSH/GSSG ratio) will be necessary in future studies.

As for immune response related process, tryptophan metabolism was enriched in the integrated multi-omics analyses. We detected tryptophan and its metabolites to be downregulated in the HSS group, such as l-Tryptophan. Tryptophan, an essential amino acid, is critical for protein synthesis and plays important roles in immune function. l-tryptophan serves as a precursor for serotonin, a neurotransmitter that acts as a signaling molecule between the nervous and immune systems, influencing T cell activation and differentiation by modulating dendritic cell maturation and function (Li et al., 2011; Herr et al., 2017; Seo and Kwon, 2023; Xu et al., 2025). The kynurenine pathway is a major route of tryptophan metabolism, its metabolites have been shown to promote regulatory T cell differentiation and function, contributing to immune tolerance and preventing autoimmune responses. Previous studies showed that tryptophan metabolites supplementation of the extender yielded a positive effect on sperm motility in various species to prolong sperm motility, such as in short-term sperm storage of trout and in buffalo (Kocabaş et al., 2019; Ahmed et al., 2020).The gut microbiota coordinately modulates diverse physiological functions of tryptophan metabolites to sustain immune homeostasis when confronting exogenous and xenogeneic antigens, revealing mechanistic similarities between sperm physiology and regulated immune responses in the female reproductive tract with different sperm storage capacities. Moreover, genes encoding Lysozyme G (LYG), T-cell immunomodulatory protein (TIP), and Serpin Family B Member 6 (SPB6) are consistently down-regulated in the HSS ducks. Taken together, the immune-related signatures in the UVJ microenvironment of female individuals support a state of immune suppression and tolerance promotion supported by change of expression of innate immune genes, enrichment of metabolites and pathways known to promote immune tolerance, and a non-inflammatory immune environment. This is consistent with the hypothesis that the female reproductive tract establishes a tolerogenic environment to facilitate prolonged sperm survival while preventing pathogen invasion.

Finally, integrated multi-omics analyses further revealed common enrichment in “nitrogen metabolism” and “arginine biosynthesis pathways”, with glutamine–fructose-6-phosphate aminotransferase (GFPT1) significantly downregulated in the HSS capacity group. Glutamine, a non-essential amino acid, plays a critical role in nitrogen balance and metabolism. Glutamine transports nitrogen atoms in the blood, providing a nitrogen source for the synthesis of non-essential amino acids, nucleotides, and other nitrogen-containing compounds. Glutamine can be converted via glutaminase reactions to glutamate, which then participates in arginine biosynthesis. Nitric oxide synthase (NOS) enzymes convert l-arginine to nitric oxide (NO) and l-citrulline. In the human reproductive tract, NO has been shown to stimulate sperm motility by activating sGC, leading to cGMP synthesis and activation of cGMP-dependent protein kinase (Machado-Oliveira et al., 2008). It was suggested that NO enhances sperm viability and fertility in rooster semen stored at low temperatures (Lee et al., 2025). The importance of optimal NO levels on sperm motility have also been reported in boar studies (Jovicić et al., 2018; Staicu et al., 2019). Although the in vivo presence of NO and NOS in the avian reproductive tract has not been established, current evidence suggests that NO, together with calcium and progesterone, may modulate sperm flagellar activity to enhance sperm motility within the female reproductive tract (Lefièvre et al., 2009). This is consistent with the findings of different progesterone levels we observed and significant enrichment of calcium signaling pathways in gene expression profile and proteomic analyses (e.g., PDE1C, AT2C2, and Troponin C1). Moreover, glutamine is also closely associated with sperm quality maintenance, redox homeostasis, and maturation. Taken together, these observations suggest that glutamine may influence arginine biosynthesis and subsequent NO production, thereby affect sperm motility and ultimately contribute to differences in sperm storage capacity. While the general biological pathways identified are fundamental processes that may be conserved across avian species, the specific molecular signatures and biomarkers may be context-dependent.

In summary, this study provides a comprehensive understanding for evaluating sperm storage in Muscovy ducks by combining Number of Fertilized Eggs (NFE) and Maximum Duration of fertility (MD). We identified critical differentially expressed genes and proteins between individuals with high and low sperm storage capacity, as well as potential biochemical markers, including progesterone and cholesterol. Additionally, we identified regulatory and molecular pathways involving lipid metabolism, immune response, antioxidant processes and sperm development. The biochemical microenvironment of the UVJ is vital for sperm storage. For instance, phosphatidylcholine may protect sperm from oxidative stress in complex dynamics. Furthermore, the enrichment of the glutathione metabolism and tryptophan metabolism pathways indicates a sophisticated antioxidant defense and immunoregulatory mechanism in the female tracts, which maintains sperm viability over extended periods. These findings provide both a valuable basis for genetic improvement and practical recommendations for artificial insemination management in poultry production.
