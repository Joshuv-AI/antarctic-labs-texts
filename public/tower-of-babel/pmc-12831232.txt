Technology readiness of patients and its association with intention to use nursing robots: latent profile analysis
Authors: Wenyi Wang, Xinyun Pan, Yan Yan, Xuchun Ye
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRobotic technology is transforming healthcare by delivering more precision, convenience, and efficiency, as seen in applications like blood collection robots. However, the full potential of such innovations hinges critically on patient acceptance. To systematically understand the drivers of intention to use, the concept of technological readiness—an individual’s stable propensity to embrace new technologies—provides a valuable lens. Nevertheless, the demographic profile of technological readiness and its specific relationship with the intention to use nursing robots remain underexplored.ObjectiveThis study aimed to identify profiles of technology readiness among patients, analyze the factors influencing these profiles, and investigate the relationship between different profiles and the intention to use blood collection robots.MethodsIn this study, data on technology readiness and intention to use were collected from 331 patients between December 2024 and February 2025 through a cross-sectional survey. Latent profile analysis (LPA) was employed to assess population heterogeneity in patients’ technology readiness, whereas logistic regression analysis was applied to identify factors associated with the intention to use blood collection robots.ResultsPotential profiling revealed three distinct technology readiness populations: conservative avoiders (47.7%), ambivalent adopters (18.5%), and active adopters (33.8%). The results of the three-profile classification are related to the intention to use blood collection robots. The results clearly show that the intention to use the blood collection robot by patients belonging to conservative avoiders (profile 1) was weaker and statistically significant than both active adopters (profile 3) and ambivalent adopters (profile 2). While profile 2 has a high intention to use, it also has a high level of insecurity and discomfort. Interestingly, the group of patients with low education levels is more likely to be attributed to active adopters. The results of the multiple regression analysis on patients’ intention to use revealed significant differences in education level, experience with robots, and potential profiles.ConclusionsThis study reveals the complex psychological characteristics of patient groups when encountering new technologies and their potential relationship with intention to use nursing robots. This suggests that clinical practice should account for patient heterogeneity from standardized procedures to a patient-centered approach. Targeted measures should be proposed to enhance the efficiency of technology implementation and improve patient experience.Keywords: Technology readiness index, Nursing robots, Intention to use, Latent profile analysis

Introduction

Driven by global population aging, uneven distribution of healthcare resources, and the information technology revolution, robots are quietly transforming the global healthcare paradigm [1]. In 2014, the WHO and the World Bank calculated a global shortage of 9 million nurses and midwives. They predict that this will reduce to 7.6 million by 2030, but it will have a disproportionate impact on regions such as Africa [2]. Robotics holds immense potential to enhance care efficiency, improve patient experiences, and alleviate the workload of healthcare professionals [3]. Since the last century, research and development of nursing robots has been underway worldwide. Japan’s Paro robot, launched in 2004, provides emotional support and interaction for the elderly, children, and other individuals with special needs. It is now widely used in various care facilities, playing a particularly significant role in dementia care [4]. The third edition of the U.S. robotics roadmap, From the Internet to Robotics, released in 2016 [5], identified the demand for nursing robots as essential for promoting health, enabling independent living, and enhancing quality of life. It emphasized leveraging the advantages of human-robot collaboration to improve care efficiency and reduce healthcare costs.

Among various nursing robotics applications, automated blood collection robots stand out as a typical and clinically vital embodiment. The blood collection robot is to identify blood vessels through infrared or ultrasonic technology, and then Artificial Intelligence (AI) algorithms will intelligently and precisely determine the position, angle, and depth of blood collection for patients’ puncture according to the obtained images, so as to realize the automation, intelligence, and precision of blood collection. Such robotic systems align with the evolving landscape of precision medicine and digital health [6]. It has been used in some hospitals in Shanghai, Jiaxing, and Nanjing. The automated blood collection robot can partially replace the function of nurses, reduce the repetitive and physical labor of nurses, and, at the same time, reduce the occupational hazards of nurses, such as needlestick injuries and blood contamination. On the other hand, the automated blood collection robot can effectively reduce the queuing time of patients, and its success rate of puncture reaches up to 94.3% [7].

While the technical merits of robots are evident, their successful implementation relies heavily on patient acceptance [8]. Therefore, understanding patients’ intention to use such robots and the factors associated with them is crucial. Existing research indicates that different groups exhibit inconsistent attitudes and intentions toward using various types of robots [9–11]. Moreover, most studies rely on theoretical frameworks such as the Technology Acceptance Model, emphasizing the critical role of cognitive evaluations like perceived usefulness and ease of use [12–14]. However, blood collection robot technology has reached a relatively mature stage, with its functional efficacy already proven [7]. What sets it apart is its novel and invasive nature of interaction. For such robots, differences in their intended use cannot be fully explained by the technology itself or the superiority of traditional models. To bridge this gap, this study introduces the construct of technological readiness.

The technology readiness index (TRI) 2.0 is a robust instrument that has been used to measure technology adoption, specifically to predict technology-related behavioral intention as well as actual behaviors [15, 16]. Based on Parasuraman’s research, technology readiness can be defined as an individual’s stable psychological tendency to adopt and use emerging technologies in achieving life and career goals. This construct contains interacting psychological facilitators and constraints that together constitute personality traits that predict technology use behavior [17]. Technological readiness offers a new perspective on individual differences in acceptance. Furthermore, this study aims to capture patient heterogeneity through latent profile analysis, providing additional relevant insights into the variation in intention to use blood collection robots.

The present study

This study focuses on the relationship between population heterogeneity in technology readiness and intention to use blood collection robots, and explores the effects of sociodemographic factors on population heterogeneity in technology readiness and intention to use blood collection robots.

Specifically, the first aim was to characterize patients’ TRI stratification using latent profile analysis (a person-centered statistical method that investigates unobserved subgroups based on commonalities) [18]. Empirical studies reveal distinct typologies: US research delineated five clusters, including explorers, pioneers, skeptics, hesitators, and avoiders. Contrastingly, South Korean investigations identified a tripartite classification comprising explorers, laggards, and pioneers [19]. African studies in the South African context uncovered three user segments that partially overlapped with the pioneers, paranoids, and explorers [20]. Meanwhile, Chilean research expanded the taxonomy to five distinct groups: pioneers, hesitators, avoiders, explorers, and skeptics [21].

Although previous studies have demonstrated the population heterogeneity of technological readiness, its specific profile structure and distribution may be influenced by the particular technological context. This study focuses on patients’ experiences with nursing robots in healthcare settings. When confronted with emerging robotic technologies, patients’ technological beliefs may be more complex and contradictory. Therefore, we propose the following research question and hypotheses:

Research question

What potential profile structure does the technological readiness of patients encountering blood collection robots exhibit?

Hypothesis 1

There are multiple types of latent profiles of Chinese patients’ TRI for the blood collection robot.

Regarding the impact of demographic variables on the profile of technological readiness, existing research findings are inconsistent. The Korean study found that laggards were more likely to be female and older users (i.e., over 40 years of age) [19]; Ramírez-Correa, P et al. found that age, gender, and education all had a significant effect on clustering in a study of technology readiness stratification in Chile [21]. Based on previous research, our second aim was to characterize demographic differences based on age, gender, education, income, physical health status, and experience with the robot. Research questions and hypotheses are as follows:

Research question

Which factors (such as age, gender, and prior technology usage experience, etc.) can predict a patient’s assignment to a specific technology readiness profile?

Hypothesis 2

Women (a) and older individuals (b) would be relatively conservative in their technology readiness categories.

Regarding the current status of blood collection robots, the relationship between other demographic factors and varying profiles of technological readiness may be complex. We explore this as one of our research questions without making any assumptions. The influence of demographic and social factors on intention to use is complex [22, 23]. It is moderated by specific cultural contexts, types of technology, or sample characteristics. Based on this, we propose the following research question :

Research question

Which factors (such as age, gender, and prior experience with technology, etc.) can predict patients’ intention to use blood collection robots?

TRI captures an individual’s technology adaptation profile through a four-dimensional structure: optimism, innovativeness, discomfort, and insecurity. Optimism reflects a positive outlook on technology. Innovativeness serves as the intrinsic motivation for individuals to become technology pioneers. Optimism and innovativeness exert significant influence on e-health literacy [24], actual usage behavior [25, 26], and expectations regarding technology [27, 28]. Discomfort stems from a lack of self-efficacy in technological mastery, often manifested as anxiety about losing control in technological interactions. This dimension has a negative impact on technology diffusion [29] and e-health literacy [24]. Insecurity involves questioning the reliability of the technology and being alert to potential negative impacts. Insecurity is a key factor distinguishing user acceptance behavior [30, 31].

The four-dimensional structure of technology readiness shows bipolar characteristics: optimism and innovativeness orientation constitute facilitators of technology adoption, while discomfort and risk perception form inhibitors. The relationship between technological readiness group heterogeneity and intention to use robots remains an area requiring further exploration. The third objective was to explore the relationship between technology readiness categories and behavioral intention to use automated blood collection robots. If distinct profiles of technological readiness exist, these groups—representing differences in underlying technological beliefs—should exhibit systematic variations in their decision-making processes and ultimate behaviors when confronted with robotics. Therefore, we propose the following research question and hypotheses:

Research question

Is there a significant difference in intention to use blood collection robots among patients with varying profiles of technological readiness?

Hypothesis 3

Different technology readiness categories have different levels of intention to use the blood collection robots.

Methods

Design

A three-month cross-sectional survey study was conducted from December 2024 to February 2025 in several tertiary hospitals in the Yangtze River Delta region. Convenience sampling was used to complete the survey. Face-to-face questionnaires were administered by uniformly trained researchers to ensure that residents correctly understood the care robots.

Participants

We recruited patients of several tertiary hospitals in the Yangtze River Delta region to participate in the questionnaire survey. Inclusion criteria were as follows: at least 18 years old, demand for blood collection, have good cognitive and verbal abilities, give informed consent, and volunteer for this study. Respondents with mental illness or serious illnesses with unstable conditions were excluded. 360 people participated in the questionnaire, of which 29 had incomplete responses, resulting in a final sample size of 331.

Measures

Data were collected through paper-based questionnaires that included demographic characteristics, the technology readiness index 2.0, and indicators of acceptance of automated blood collection robots.

Demographic Characteristics. Patient demographic characteristics, including age, gender, education, income, self-report physical health status, and experience with robots, were used in the analysis.

The technology readiness index 2.0 (TRI2.0). It was compiled by A. Parasuraman in 2000 and updated and simplified from the original version by A. Parasuraman and C. L. Colby in 2015 [15]. We acquired the authorization of the scale and translated it into Chinese. The scale is used to assess an individual’s readiness to adopt and utilize new technologies and has 16 items with four dimensions: optimism, innovativeness, discomfort, and insecurity. It uses a 5-point Likert scale (strongly disagree = 1, strongly agree = 5), with a higher total score representing a higher tendency for users to accept and use new technologies. In this study, the Cronbach’s α for optimism, innovativeness, discomfort, and insecurity were 0.908, 0.825, 0.803, and 0.859, respectively. The overall Cronbach’s α for the scale was 0.712.

Questionnaire on intention to use automated blood collection robots. The intention to use an automated blood collection robot questionnaire was adapted from questionnaires already established in the existing literature [32] and revised to fit the context of blood collection robot use. It was assessed by two items (“I would like to use an automated blood collection robot” and “I plan to continue to use an automated blood collection robot in the future”) and answered on a 5-point Likert-type scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Cronbach’s α of the tool was 0.900 in a previous study at the time of development [32] and 0.891 in this study.

Data analysis

Latent profile analysis (LPA) was conducted using maximum likelihood estimation to identify unobserved classes with similar characteristics by analyzing the response patterns of the 16 items of the TRI2.0 [20]. Model fit assessment started with single-category models, gradually increasing the number of potential categories until the explanatory power of the model was no longer significantly increased. The model with the best data fit was determined according to statistical criteria, model parsimony, and clinical interpretability. The optimal model selection integrates the following metrics: information indices such as Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), and sample-size-adjusted Bayesian information criterion (aBIC) (the lowest value is preferred), the significance levels of the Lo-Mendell-Rubin likelihood ratio test (LMR-LRT) and bootstrap likelihood ratio test (BLRT) (p < 0.050), and the entropy value (range 0–1, higher is better) reflecting the classification accuracy. Final profile visualization was achieved by plotting line plots of item means for each potential category.

Descriptive statistics were used for demographic profiles using means and standard deviations or frequencies and percentages. After determining the optimal latent profile model, chi-square tests, non-parametric tests, and logistic regression analysis were performed, and further explored (1) the association of demographic variables with intention to use automated blood collection robots (2) the relationship between technical readiness profiles and intention to use automated blood collection robots.

Results

LPA and TRI groups

Latent model indices with 1 to 4 profile solutions were evaluated using statistical assessments and theoretical considerations. Based on the analysis, a 3-profile solution consistent with Hypothesis 1 (Table 1) was selected. The significant decrease in AIC, BIC, and aBIC from Profile 1 to Profile 4 suggests a good balance between model fit and complexity [33]. Since the LMR-LRT values were not significant in the four-profile model (p = 0.340), the first three-profile model was compared. Compared to the two-profile model, the three-profile model had lower information criterion metrics (AIC, BIC, and aBIC) and all significant LR tests (LMR-LRT p = 0.020 and BLRT p < 0.001), with an entropy value of 0.902 (indicating strong classification) and more than 5% of the sample for each profile (to ensure each category has sufficient sample size to guarantee the stability and reliability of the analysis results). Considering the above reasons, practical significance, and interpretability, the 3-profile model was chosen as the final fitted model.

Discussion

This study identified population heterogeneity in technology readiness among patients and explored the relationship between the three-category model and intention to use the blood collection robot. The relationships between sociodemographic variables on population heterogeneity in technology readiness and sociodemographic variables on intention to use the blood collection robot were also explored.

Multiple profiles of technology readiness

This study used LPA to identify a three-category model of technology readiness in patients: conservative avoiders (47.7%), ambivalent adopters (18.5%), and active adopters (33.8%). The results of this study differ slightly from previous studies of technology readiness user segmentation. Five profile types were identified in studies of the general public in America and Chile [21]: pioneers, hesitators, explorers, avoiders, and skeptics. However, among 356 participants using exercise wearables in South Korea, three groups of users [19]: explorers (high motivation, low inhibition), laggards (low motivation, high inhibition), and pioneers (high motivation, high inhibition) were explored. Possible reasons for the differences are that, first, our study was conducted in a hospital environment using robots. Currently, robotics is rapidly evolving and impacting the public with ever-changing thinking [34], so the technology readiness stratification of patients who are in technologically advanced hospital environments may differ. Second, the U.S. and Chilean studies did large-sample national public surveys, whereas our study and the Korean study targeted specific groups, and our study focused on patients in areas with higher levels of technological development.

According to the stratified ranking of the dimensions of technology readiness, the low motivation and high inhibition type of avoiders were missing in our study compared to the U.S. study. Another reason may stem from cultural differences between Eastern and Western views of technology [35, 36]. Overall, more than half of patients remain conservative avoiders or ambivalent adopters, exhibiting high levels of insecurity and discomfort toward new technologies. Therefore, the design and development of robotic systems should account for the specific needs of different patient groups. Safety features should be enhanced, and timely, accurate communication with users should be maintained.

Relationship between three-profile technology readiness and patients’ intention to use the blood collection robot

The stratification of technological readiness categories is one of the factors associated with patients’ intention to use blood collection robots, and Hypothesis 3 was verified. Profile 3, with a high level of optimism and innovativeness and a low level of insecurity and discomfort, accounted for 33.8% of the total population and was the most willing to try the blood collection robot (median intention to use is 5.0). This is consistent with the findings of Lee et al. [24] which suggested that the optimism and innovativeness dimensions of patient technology readiness significantly influence eHealth literacy and further have an impact on the adoption and deployment of eHealth devices. Profile 1 is the category with the lowest optimism-innovativeness dimension score and intermediate level of insecurity-discomfort dimension among the three groups, accounting for 47.7% of the total, and was the least willing to try the blood collection robot (median intention to use is 2.0).

Profile 2 is the high-optimism-innovativeness group, which has the highest insecurity and discomfort in the three-category model, accounting for 18.5% of the total. Interestingly, despite experiencing higher levels of discomfort and insecurity toward new technologies, participants still demonstrated a relatively high intention to use blood collection robots, with a median score of 4.0. Patient identity may explain this phenomenon. Overall, patients are positive about the application of new technologies, such as AI in healthcare [37]. For patients, new technologies often bring new hope for treatment. However, patients may have a higher sense of insecurity due to the special characteristics of their physical health [38]. What’s more, robotics is still in its early stages of development [39]. Given the specific needs of such patients, human-machine interaction safety must be considered concurrently during design and development. At the same time, we should also provide patients with adequate explanations and sufficient information support.

Demographic factors in technology readiness classification and intention to use blood collection robots

The results of the three-profile demographic differences did not support Hypotheses 2a and 2b. Previous studies have found that females and older users are more likely to be in the technology readiness lagging group [19], which was not found in this study. In this study, only the education level was associated with the technology readiness category. In particular, we found that those with low education levels (primary and below) were more likely to belong to profile 3 (active adopters) than those with high education levels (master’s degree or above). That is, people with high education levels are more likely to have a conservative-avoidant attitude toward new technologies. The level of education showed the same trend in the intention to use blood collection robots, i.e., those with higher education levels were rather more conservative in their intention to use blood collection robots. Robotic blood collection is an invasive process and produces physical contact with the patient, making it susceptible to a higher perception of risk. Highly educated people often have better information acquisition and analytical skills [40], and may be more proactively aware of the potential risks of robotic blood collection (e.g., equipment malfunction, risk of infection, lack of “human touch” care). Even if the probability of these risks is low, their perception of risk may be higher. Highly educated people can draw on high health literacy to understand medical information and make informed decisions [41], but it can also bring additional cognitive load and anxiety.

Based on this, during the rollout of invasive blood collection robotics, healthcare professionals and technology companies should provide detailed, evidence-based risk-benefit information, emphasizing how the technology complements rather than replaces the role of the healthcare provider, respects patient preferences, and provides alternative options.

Additionally, our research found that extensive experience with robots is associated with an increased intention to use blood collection robots. It has been shown that usage experience reduces perceived risk through three mechanisms: information acquisition (e.g., product knowledge accumulation), trust enhancement (e.g., brand loyalty), and behavioral habituation (e.g., repeat purchase) [42]. Actual robot use experience validates the safety of blood collection robots through physical practice (e.g., pain level, accuracy of blood collection), and dissolves the catastrophizing imagery of technological unknowns. In addition, haptic interaction driven embodied learning [43] reconfigures the user’s emotional representation of the robot. Body memories of experienced users (e.g., robotic arm pressure sensation) are translated into positive affective associations, transforming the technology from a ‘cold tool’ to a ‘collaborative partner’.

Limitations

Despite the contributions, the current study has some limitations. First, the cross-sectional design of the data does not allow for the inference of causality, and further experimental research is needed to determine whether the factors identified in the current study represent explanatory causal mechanisms for blood collection robot use intentions.

In addition, only patients of three tertiary hospitals in the Yangtze River Delta region were selected for data collection in this study. The overall higher economic and educational level in the Yangtze River Delta region and the greater exposure to and use of new technologies may have influenced the results of the study. It is important to emphasize that the findings and conclusions of this study are primarily applicable to the specific sampled population and the context under investigation. Caution should be exercised when generalizing these results to broader populations or different healthcare settings, as factors such as cultural background, healthcare system differences, and levels of technological infrastructure may influence the outcomes. Subsequently, consideration can be given to selecting a wider geographical distribution of the population for the survey to expand the sample size and sample representativeness.

Furthermore, this study primarily relies on self-report scales to measure technical readiness and intention to use. Although these scales have been validated with good reliability and validity, this approach has certain limitations. First, respondents’ answers are subjective and may not fully reflect their objective behavior. More importantly, these measurements may be subject to social desirability bias. Although this study has made efforts to control such bias through methods like anonymous surveys, its potential impact should still be taken into consideration.

Finally, the assessment of participants’ physical health status relied on a self-reported, composite measure based on patients’ recall of their medical visits, number of chronic conditions, and subjective well-being. While practical for data collection, this approach lacks the objectivity and precision of standardized instruments, such as activities of daily living (ADL) scales, and may be subject to recall bias.

Conclusion and implication

This study identified three types of technological readiness among patients and their associated factors, as well as factors related to intention to use the blood collection robot. Notably, it was found that patient groups with different levels of technological readiness exhibited varying intentions to use the blood collection robot. The results suggest that in today’s world of rapid technological development, we need to pay attention to the specific needs of different patient groups. While driving technological innovation, we should balance it with humanistic values and advance new technologies in a step-by-step manner. Moreover, we need to give our users enough information so that they can fully and objectively understand robotics. In addition, this study expands the application of technology readiness in healthcare.
