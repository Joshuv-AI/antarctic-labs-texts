Tumor-associated endothelial cells in tumor immune escape and immunotherapy: multifaceted roles and treatment approaches
Authors: Tianyu Zheng, Xinran Yu, Caihong Yu, Wangting Xu, Zhuoyang Fan, Yongjie Zhou, Changyu Li, Juncheng Wan, Chaoqiao Jin, Xuran Jin, Wen Zhang, Zhiping Yan, Peng Luo, Bufu Tang, Xudong Qu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe tumor microenvironment (TME) is a key factor influencing carcinogenesis, disease progression, and therapeutic outcomes. Tumor-associated endothelial cells (TECs), integral components of the TME, form the structural and functional foundation of the vasculature, while also modulating tumor immune responses. TECs regulate immune cell activity and distribution through multiple molecular mechanisms, thereby influencing tumor immunity. Recent evidence highlights TEC-targeted interventions as a promising strategy to enhance immunotherapy efficacy. This review examines the mechanisms through which TECs drive tumorigenesis, progression, and metastasis, and explores the therapeutic potential of TEC-targeted approaches in cancer treatment. It also provides a systematic analysis of TECs’ impact on the immune microenvironment, assessing the potential of TEC-directed therapies to overcome chemoresistance and immunotherapy resistance, thus offering new perspectives for drug development targeting TECs. Finally, the review critically evaluates potential molecular targets within TECs and recent advancements in genetic research for cancer therapy, emphasizing key research priorities and pressing clinical challenges that require urgent focus.Keywords: Tumor-associated endothelial cells (TECs), Tumor microenvironment (TME), Immunosuppressive mechanisms, Endothelial heterogeneity, TEC-targeted therapeutics

Introduction

Malignant neoplasms consist of both neoplastic cells and the tumor microenvironment (TME), a complex biological niche that includes various cellular populations (such as immune and stromal components), extracellular matrix (ECM), vasculature, lymphatic vessels, and soluble factors like cytokines and molecular mediators. The TME plays a critical role in neoplastic transformation, disease progression, and metastasis. The bidirectional interactions and intricate molecular crosstalk between neoplastic cells, immune cells, and stromal elements are key regulators of tumor development and progression [1]. Endothelial cells (ECs), located within the stromal compartment of the TME, are central to both angiogenesis and vascular homeostasis [2]. Tumor-associated ECs (TECs), however, exhibit distinct alterations in phenotype, gene expression, and functional properties compared to their normal counterparts [3].

ECs, which form the lining of blood vessels, are crucial for maintaining vascular homeostasis and hemodynamic stability. Pathological angiogenesis in tumors is marked by abnormal, excessive vessel formation [4]. Substantial evidence shows that malignant progression heavily relies on this dysregulated angiogenesis. Neovascularization ensures the delivery of essential nutrients, oxygen, and growth factors to rapidly growing tumors, thus supporting tumor growth and enabling metastatic spread [5]. The vasculature also influences tumor immunity through multiple mechanisms: (1) ECs recruit and traffic immune cells in a manner that is often immunosuppressive, through intravascular secretory factors; (2) endothelial dysfunction disrupts T cell activity and promotes their exclusion; and (3) structural and biochemical abnormalities in the vasculature create barriers that impede lymphocyte migration and infiltration [6]. Folkman’s seminal hypothesis in 1971 established that tumor growth is angiogenesis-driven, suggesting that anti-angiogenic therapies (AATs) could suppress malignant progression [7]. Such therapies can modulate TEC-mediated effects, potentially enhancing the efficacy of immunotherapy [8]. Vascular normalization, which involves transforming aberrant, hyperpermeable tumor vasculature into structurally and functionally competent vessels through EC reprogramming (such as upregulation of quiescence factors like Nr2F2 and a shift from glycolysis to oxidative phosphorylation via PFKFB3 inhibition), has emerged as a promising strategy in cancer therapy [9]. Despite robust anti-tumor effects observed in preclinical models, AATs have shown limited clinical success. EC heterogeneity within and between tumors complicates therapeutic efficacy. The lack of standardized EC classification systems and uniform single-cell analytical methods further hampers meaningful cross-tumor comparisons of EC phenotypes. Thus, there is an urgent need to better understand EC functions in human pathology to drive clinical innovation and targeted therapies for pathological angiogenesis in cancer.

Elucidating the specific functions of TECs facilitates the identification of druggable targets, potentially improving the efficacy of cancer treatment strategies. During the early stages of tumor development, immune cells residing in the TME perform essential surveillance roles in tumor detection and elimination [10]. Immune-targeted therapies have shown significant clinical benefit in cancer treatment. TECs interact dynamically with immune checkpoint molecules, thereby modulating therapeutic responses to anti-tumor treatments [11]. In hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), the packing fraction (a measure of cellular density and spatial crowding) and the distribution of immune cell populations within the TME play a critical role in disease progression and treatment outcomes [12]. Clinical data suggest that inhibiting xCT (SLC7A11), a key cystine/glutamate antiporter involved in cellular antioxidant defense, enhances treatment efficacy in a synergistic manner [13, 14]. Disrupting SLC7A11-mediated tumor survival, which supports a pro-angiogenic and immunosuppressive microenvironment, not only induces immunogenic tumor cell death but also promotes tumor vascular normalization. This normalization enhances immune cell infiltration into the tumor. Consequently, targeting SLC7A11 in combination with radiofrequency ablation (RFA) synergizes to boost anti-tumor immune responses, presenting a promising therapeutic strategy for HCC treatment [14–16]. TECs are critical regulators of immune cell trafficking and function, orchestrating the initiation, activation, and suppression of effector immune responses through complex molecular mechanisms that remain incompletely understood [17]. Recent immunotherapy research emphasizes the importance of stromal paracrine signaling networks in effective tumor targeting, highlighting the need to disrupt pro-tumoral pathways while enhancing tumor-suppressive mechanisms [18]. Furthermore, TECs have emerged as promising targets for chronotherapeutic strategies in cancer treatment [19], where drug delivery is timed to capitalize on circadian rhythms in vascular function, optimizing therapeutic efficacy and minimizing toxicity.

The interaction between neoplastic cells and the endothelium is crucial in mediating metastatic progression through complex networks of adhesion molecules and chemokine signaling. Both endothelial and neoplastic cells secrete various molecular mediators that coordinate the dynamics of the TME, presenting novel opportunities for therapeutic intervention [20]. Neoplastic and ECs exhibit significant phenotypic plasticity, allowing them to adapt to environmental signals [21]. Upon breaching the epithelial barrier, invasive tumor cells establish bidirectional communication with adjacent ECs, initiating the secretion of cytokines and mitogens that influence TME dynamics [22]. TECs act as primary mediators of tumor-associated angiogenesis, orchestrating the formation of new blood vessels. During hematogenous dissemination, malignant cells undergo intravasation, utilizing the systemic vasculature for distant organ colonization. The metastatic cascade culminates in the formation of viable micrometastatic foci at secondary sites. Newly formed vessels exhibit distinct transcriptional profiles compared to pre-existing vessels, providing an opportunity for targeted therapeutic strategies aimed at overcoming drug resistance [23]. The transcriptome of nascent vessels contributes to their chaotic structure and poor perfusion, which acts as a physical barrier to effective drug delivery. Conversely, the transcriptome of mature vessels upregulates pro-survival and drug-efflux pathways, fostering a resilient, therapy-resistant phenotype. Targeting these distinct transcriptional signatures can address these barriers. Additionally, malignant cells can reprogram ECs to facilitate extravasation through mechanisms that differ from physiological processes. Understanding the molecular mechanisms governing endothelial responses during tumor cell extravasation offers valuable therapeutic opportunities for developing anti-metastatic interventions [24].

This review consolidates the current understanding of EC functions as key regulators within the TME. It systematically examines the complex interplay between endothelial and neoplastic cells, their roles in tumor initiation, progression, and metastasis, and highlights the significant heterogeneity of the endothelial compartment. Particular emphasis is placed on endothelial-immune cell interactions and their implications for immunotherapeutic strategies, including TEC-targeted therapies, immune checkpoint inhibition, anti-angiogenic approaches, and combination immunomodulatory treatments. Additionally, this review outlines emerging research priorities and identifies critical knowledge gaps that require further exploration [25].

Regulatory mechanisms of endothelial cells in neoplastic transformation and progression

Structural organization and cellular heterogeneity of tumor-associated endothelial cells

Neoplastic cells reside within a complex TME, which is a critical component of tumor biology. The TME represents a highly dynamic and non-equilibrium ecological system composed of neoplastic cells, immune components, stromal elements, bioactive molecules (such as cytokines, chemokines, and mitogenic ligands), vasculature, and ECM components. The stromal compartment of the TME is primarily composed of vascular ECs and cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), both of which play essential regulatory roles [26, 27]. The immune landscape within the TME includes infiltrating macrophages, lymphocytes, dendritic cells, and mast cells, which secrete various mediators that support tumor growth, angiogenesis, and immune evasion [28]. Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), a well-characterized immune cell population, primarily exhibit immunosuppressive and pro-angiogenic properties [29]. Within this complex ecosystem, TECs function as a central hub, mediating bidirectional communication between immune cells and other TME components, thus establishing a network of reciprocal interactions [30]. Cancer-associated morbidity and mortality largely result from two key biological processes: uncontrolled neoplastic proliferation and metastatic spread to distant organs. Tumor-driven angiogenesis orchestrates these processes by promoting neovascular branching and vessel sprouting, creating a nutrient and oxygen supply system that also facilitates metastatic dissemination through the bloodstream. This highly coordinated process requires complex molecular crosstalk between neoplastic and ECs [31]. Malignant progression is driven by the activation and reprogramming of proximal stromal elements within the TME [32, 33]. Invasive tumor cells breach the epithelial barrier and establish bidirectional communication with adjacent ECs, triggering the release of signaling molecules that modify the TME [34]. In contrast to normal ECs (NECs), TECs display distinct alterations in their structural architecture, molecular signatures, and functional capabilities. TECs exhibit increased proliferative capacity and possess mechanisms that induce immune tolerance, thereby facilitating cancer progression and dissemination. Thus, the TME acts not as a passive environment but as an active orchestrator of cancer expansion and survival.

The major features of TECs

ECs possess diverse functional roles that extend beyond their traditional functions, directly contributing to tumor metastasis. These cells mediate tumor cell entry into circulation, a critical step in the metastatic cascade. ECs are also a major source of CAFs through endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndMT), which directly influences tumor development. The heterogeneity of CAFs significantly affects cancer cell motility and invasiveness through multiple molecular mechanisms. Consequently, ECs contribute to metastatic development both through direct pathways and secondary effects [35]. Within the intricate architecture of the TME, ECs orchestrate processes such as vascular homeostasis, barrier function, and angiogenesis, while also modulating anti-tumor immunity. Emerging evidence suggests that EC metabolism may represent a promising strategy to overcome resistance to conventional AATs [36]. Understanding the phenotypic and functional diversity of TECs will enhance our comprehension of their multifaceted roles within the TME, enabling the identification of novel therapeutic targets and ultimately improving clinical outcomes in cancer treatment.

Mechanistic contributions of TECs to cancer development and advancement

TEC-mediated tumor vascularization

Tumor angiogenesis is a critical prerequisite for neoplastic growth and metastatic spread. As illustrated in Fig. 2, angiogenic factors stimulate the formation of new blood vessels to support tumor progression. The tumor vasculature serves a dual role: sustaining tumor growth by delivering oxygen and nutrients, while simultaneously providing pathways for metastatic dissemination [37]. TECs, with stem cell-like properties, engage in complex interactions with neoplastic cells to drive neovascularization, facilitating tumor progression, metastatic spread, and contributing to therapeutic resistance [38, 39]. During neovascular development, ECs establish sophisticated communication networks with pro-angiogenic cellular components through mechanisms such as intercellular adhesion complexes, specialized junctional structures, and paracrine signaling networks involving cytokines and metabolic mediators [40]. ECs act as key regulators of cancer cell behavior, promoting invasive and migratory phenotypes through various molecular pathways [41].

Molecular Mechanisms of Pathological Angiogenesis in the Tumor Microenvironment. Angiogenic initiation is triggered by multiple stimuli, including hypoxia and spatiotemporal gradients of blood vessel growth promoters (secreted by neoplastic cells), leading to the specification of tip and stalk cell phenotypes from quiescent vessels. VEGF-VEGFR2 signaling cascade activation induces endothelial cell activation, concurrent with pericyte dissociation and MMP-mediated deterioration of the basement membrane and extracellular matrix (ECM) components. Specialized tip cells acquire a motile phenotype and initiate directional migration. Proliferating stalk cells support vessel elongation, facilitating the extension of nascent vascular sprouts throughout the TME

The establishment of tumor-associated vasculature, driven by pathological angiogenesis, is a hallmark of cancer that supports both tumor growth and systemic dissemination. The angiogenic switch in tumors is often triggered by oncogenic activation, leading to the production of pro-angiogenic factors. In the early stages, tumors may remain in a quiescent state until they reach a critical volume threshold, determined by the limits of oxygen diffusion and ensuing hypoxic stress. At this point, neovascularization is induced, enabling clonal expansion and providing routes for metastatic spread. Research on intercellular signaling networks shows that newly formed ECs help establish an immunosuppressive microenvironment that supports ongoing angiogenesis [42]. ECs, as the inner lining of blood vessels, form a highly specialized boundary between the circulation and surrounding tissues [43, 44]. Despite their structural simplicity, ECs perform critical functions in vascular homeostasis, cellular trafficking, immune regulation, and hemostasis [45]. TECs exhibit distinct phenotypic and functional differences compared to NECs. Figure 3 highlights the key biological distinctions between these cell types. TECs are characterized by aberrant vascular architecture, including disorganized networks, irregular dilation, impaired barrier function, and poor perfusion—traits that contribute to tumor invasion and metastatic progression [41]. Reduced pericyte coverage and weakened endothelial junction integrity result in compromised vascular barriers, creating a microenvironment marked by hypoxia, nutrient deprivation, acidosis, and chronic inflammation [46]. This pathological vasculature not only sustains tumor growth but also facilitates the escape of metastatic cells [47]. Therapeutic strategies targeting TECs represent a pioneering approach to TME-directed interventions, offering an alternative to conventional anti-tumor therapies [48]. Thus, targeting vascular normalization holds promise as a strategy to disrupt metastatic spread and improve therapeutic outcomes [49].

Fundamental distinctions exist between NECs and TECs at multiple biological levels. TECs originate through diverse mechanisms, including direct reprogramming of neoplastic cells, cancer stem cells (CSCs), and vascular progenitor cells (VPCs). Additionally, TEC formation may occur through cellular fusion events between NECs and either malignant cells or VPCs. TECs demonstrate the capacity to incorporate tumor-derived apoptotic bodies and exosomes, which may serve as vehicles for oncogenic material transfer [50]. The tumor microenvironment, rich in growth factors and cytokines, can promote genomic instability in TECs, resulting in phenotypic and functional alterations. Physiological vasculature exhibits a well-organized endothelial monolayer, continuous basement membrane, and comprehensive pericyte coverage. NECs display characteristic metabolic profiles, including enhanced glycolytic flux, active pentose phosphate pathway utilization, serine biosynthesis, and glutamine metabolism for biomolecular synthesis. In contrast, tumor vessels manifest architectural abnormalities, including aberrant TEC organization, compromised basement membrane integrity, and deficient pericyte coverage. These structural alterations culminate in compromised perfusion and enhanced permeability, facilitating tumor cell intravasation

Angiogenic regulation is controlled by the dynamic balance between vessel-forming inhibitors and promoters, with the vascular transition phase serving as a critical threshold in this molecular equilibrium [51]. The upregulation of pro-angiogenic signaling is primarily driven by pathophysiological conditions, particularly hypoxia, which arises due to increased tissue mass, impaired vascular function, and vessel occlusion. Tumor-associated angiogenesis is characterized by a persistent disruption of angiogenic homeostasis, where pro-angiogenic mediators consistently outweigh their inhibitory counterparts [7]. This molecular imbalance leads to uncontrolled vessel formation, driving EC transition from quiescence to an activated, migratory, and invasive phenotype [52]. In the TME, hypoxia acts as a key regulator of cancer progression, stimulating the production of pro-angiogenic factors, particularly VEGF, which triggers the angiogenic switch [53]. TECs exhibit enhanced resistance to hypoxia-induced cell death compared to NECs. In the metabolically stressed TME, characterized by oxygen and nutrient deprivation, increased autophagic activity in the vasculature emerges as a critical adaptive response, enabling ECs to meet heightened bioenergetic demands and establish paracrine communication with stromal components. ECs are essential mediators of nutrient and oxygen delivery to the tumor parenchyma. Autophagy plays a key role in endothelial responses to metabolic stress, maintaining redox homeostasis and preserving endothelial plasticity. The destabilized vascular barrier in malformed tumor vasculature, caused by disrupted intercellular junctions due to hyperactivated VEGF signaling, closely involves autophagic and lysosomal pathways in tumor angiogenesis [47]. Hypoxic conditions in ECs not only enhance autophagic processes but also promote the stabilization of HIF-α, leading to increased VEGF expression and heightened glycolytic metabolism, thus further driving angiogenesis and vascular remodeling. These angiogenic mediators are produced by both neoplastic cells and stromal components of the TME [47, 54]. Consequently, therapeutic strategies targeting intratumoral oxygen deprivation represent promising approaches for anti-cancer interventions [55, 56]. VEGF is the primary pro-angiogenic mediator, and the activation of the VEGF/VEGFR signaling axis by neoplastic cells under hypoxic conditions is a key mechanism driving tumor vascularization [57]. As illustrated in Fig. 4, VEGF promotes tumor vascularization by exerting pro-angiogenic effects. The endothelial barrier plays a pivotal role in tumor cell dissemination between primary and metastatic sites. Transendothelial migration (TEM), the process by which tumor cells traverse the endothelial barrier, is a critical step in both intravasation and extravasation. Hypoxia acts as a key regulator of TEM and serves as a pivotal initiator of angiogenic responses. Molecular alterations, such as proto-oncogene activation and tumor suppressor inactivation, further contribute to the derepression of VEGF promoters in neoplastic cells. VEGF mediates paracrine activation of ECs, inducing migratory and proliferative responses that drive angiogenesis [58]. VEGF expression is widespread across various malignancies and serves as a central mediator of tumor-associated angiogenesis. VEGF signaling remodels the TME through multiple mechanisms, enhancing tumor cell motility, survival, and intravasation, while also promoting metastatic potential and vascular permeability [59]. The tumor vasculature critically regulates immune cell trafficking and function, influencing immune surveillance. Vascular normalization strategies, which improve tumor perfusion, offer therapeutic benefits by addressing key pathological features such as genomic instability, metastatic potential, and impaired drug delivery. Enhanced vascular function and perfusion alleviate tumor hypoxia and optimize therapeutic agent delivery, ultimately improving treatment outcomes [47, 60]. Thus, vascular dysfunction, rather than vessel density, may serve as a more reliable prognostic marker for disease progression and metastatic potential.

VEGF-Mediated Molecular Cascade in Pathological Angiogenesis: From Initiation to Vessel Maturation. The progression of tumor angiogenesis follows a highly coordinated sequence of molecular and cellular events: ① When a quiescent tumor goes through vascular niche establishment, hypoxia-induced signaling orchestrates the biosynthesis of pro-angiogenic effectors, predominantly VEGF, which subsequently activates reversibly dormant ECs in adjacent vessels. ② The activated endothelium initiates the secretion of proteolytic enzymes that systematically degenerate the surrounding extracellular matrix (ECM), establishing permissive conditions for vessel sprouting. ③ Specialized endothelial tip cells emerge and orchestrate the directional migration of ECs along VEGF gradients through precisely regulated chemotactic responses. ④ Concurrent endothelial cell proliferation ensures sufficient cellular supply for nascent vessel formation and sprouting progression. ⑤ Following vascular sprout anastomosis and the start of blood flow, the initially immature and hyperpermeable vessels undergo a maturation process characterized by endothelial cell differentiation, de novo ECM deposition, and pericyte recruitment and coverage. This temporally regulated process, while initially creating vessels that facilitate tumor cell intravasation and metastatic dissemination due to their compromised barrier function, ultimately results in the establishment of a more structured circulatory network. The entire sequence is dynamically modulated by VEGF signaling, matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), adhesion molecules, and guidance factors, collectively establishing the complex vascular architecture characteristic of tumor progression

Tumor-endothelial cell crosstalk during cancer cell invasion and migration

Metastatic disease remains the primary cause of cancer-related mortality. Tumor progression and metastatic dissemination are now understood to depend not only on intrinsic neoplastic cell properties but also on complex interactions with the TME, particularly the aberrant stromal components [61]. Within the TME, ECs undergo phenotypic changes, acquiring characteristics that facilitate malignant progression. Pathological angiogenesis is a key prerequisite for tumor progression, driven by multiple molecular signals and tumor-derived factors. The strength of this pro-angiogenic signaling is further amplified by intricate crosstalk and compensation among key pathways, such as the dynamic interplay between VEGFR and FGFR, which ensures sustained activation of shared downstream effectors like PI3K/AKT. This network flexibility, characterized by functional redundancy and common signaling hubs, enables the tumor vasculature to adapt and persist despite targeted therapies, thus promoting disease progression. The tumor vasculature serves dual purposes: supporting tumor growth by delivering nutrients and oxygen while simultaneously providing pathways for metastatic dissemination. TECs exhibit significant phenotypic changes compared to NECs. Structural abnormalities in tumor vessels, particularly the compromised integrity of endothelial tight junctions due to VEGF signaling activation, facilitate tumor cell intravasation. The immature vessel architecture, marked by insufficient smooth muscle cell and pericyte coverage, further promotes TEM of tumor cells [62]. CXCR7, highly expressed in tumor vasculature, is a key mediator of tumor progression and metastasis, promoting angiogenesis, enhancing tumor cell adhesion to the endothelium, and creating an immunosuppressive microenvironment. Beyond passively supporting metastasis, TECs actively influence tumor cell behavior through the expression of angiocrine factors. Elevated expression of these factors in metastatic compared to non-metastatic tumors suggests a regulatory role for TECs in modulating tumor cell phenotypes [63]. A complex bidirectional communication network exists between TECs and neoplastic cells, where TECs actively drive neoplastic progression. TECs can reprogram quiescent tumor cells into more aggressive phenotypes, characterized by increased tumorigenicity, extravasation capacity, and chemoresistance [64, 65]. Notch signaling within ECs is crucial for tumor development and stem cell maintenance [66–69], promoting tumor cell migration, metastasis, and neutrophil infiltration [70]. TECs not only produce pro-tumorigenic factors but also downregulate tumor suppressors. This proximal signaling between endothelial and tumor cells modulates the expression of both tumor-stimulating and tumor-suppressing factors, orchestrating key aspects of tumorigenesis. Cancer cells secrete various factors that influence metabolism, inter-organ communication, and disease progression. These tumor-derived factors circulate systemically, interacting with the vast endothelial surface [71]. Furthermore, EC signaling promotes systemic metabolic disturbances in cancer, contributing to tumor-associated cachexia and revealing new insights into vascular metabolism in disease progression [72]. Sequential Events in Hepatocellular Carcinoma Lung Metastasis: From Primary Tumor to Metastatic Colonization. The metastatic cascade of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) to the lungs proceeds through multiple coordinated stages: ① Neoplastic cells, predominantly originating in the perifalciform region of the hepatic left lobe, undergo invasive transformation and actively disrupt the tight junctional complexes of hepatic vein endothelial cells, thereby facilitating their entry into the bloodstream (intravasation). ② Upon entering the circulation, tumor cells must simultaneously achieve multiple survival requirements, including immune evasion and maintenance of viability, before executing successful extravasation through the vascular wall; these cells may then either enter a dormant state or initiate proliferation through precisely regulated adhesion-dependent mechanisms. ③ The successfully extravasated tumor cells proceed to establish metastatic foci within the pulmonary parenchyma, subsequently inducing angiogenesis to establish a dedicated blood supply, which is critical to sustained proliferation and continued enlargement of the spreading lesion. This temporally coordinated process involves complex molecular events, including endothelial barrier modulation, expression of selective adhesion receptors, and secretion of pro-angiogenic factors, all of which are orchestrated through intricate exchanges among neoplastic cells, endothelial cells, immune components, and stromal elements contained in the metastatic microenvironment

Immunoregulatory functions of tumor-associated endothelial cells

Heterogeneity and function of immune cell populations in the TME

The tumor immune landscape comprises a diverse array of cellular populations, including natural killer (NK) cells, regulatory T cells (Tregs), TAMs, myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and other immune components. Neoplastic lesions exhibit significant heterogeneity in the spatial organization, composition, and activation status of immune infiltrates, which profoundly affect disease progression and therapeutic response to immunological interventions. A comprehensive understanding of immune cell diversity and their functional roles within the TME holds the potential to drive the development of more targeted immunotherapeutic strategies, ultimately improving patient outcomes [73].

Bidirectional signaling networks between tumor-associated endothelial cells and macrophages

TAMs are pivotal mediators of innate immunity within the TME, with a significant abundance that can constitute over 50% of the tumor volume in some cancers. These cells serve as master regulators of tumorigenesis and angiogenesis [74, 75]. TAMs originate from various sources, including tissue-resident populations and peripheral reservoirs such as the bone marrow (BM) and spleen, and they orchestrate a range of tumor-supporting functions throughout cancer development—from initiation and progression to metastatic spread, therapeutic response, and recurrence [76–78]. Extensive research has highlighted their distinct roles in tumor progression [79], with the majority of TAMs arising from recruited circulating monocytes. Notably, monocytes from cancer patients exhibit unique characteristics compared to those from healthy individuals, such as altered subpopulation distribution, transcriptional profiles, and metabolic signatures—features indicative of tumor-specific adaptations [80–84]. Tumors actively create a supportive microenvironment marked by dysregulated angiogenesis and TAM infiltration [85], where TAMs contribute to neoplastic progression through mechanisms including immune suppression, angiogenic stimulation, stromal remodeling, and direct cellular interactions [86, 87]. Within the TME, TAMs promote tumor survival, growth, and migration through the release of cytokines and trophic factors that inhibit apoptosis—critical for invasion and circulatory entry [88]. Cancer cells, in turn, reprogram TAMs into an immunosuppressive, anti-inflammatory phenotype by secreting various factors, including development promoters, vascular-modulating compounds, and matrix-modifying mediators [88–90]. Notably, TECs cooperate with TAMs to enhance metastatic potential by modulating endothelial permeability and facilitating the adhesion and infiltration of circulating tumor cells [91]. TAMs further exert immunosuppressive functions by preventing NK cells and T cells from attacking cancer cells during tumor progression and post-therapeutic recovery [89]. These macrophages demonstrate remarkable plasticity, with their functional state being dynamically regulated by cancer cell-derived signals, intratumoral localization, and interactions with other TME components—factors that vary across tumor types and respond to regional and systemic metabolic and endocrine modulators [92]. The adaptation of TAMs to specific intratumoral niches is a key determinant of their plasticity and heterogeneity [93], further influenced by their ontogenic origins [94].

Clinical and experimental evidence has demonstrated that increased TAM infiltration is significantly associated with poor prognosis and reduced overall survival across various cancer types [95–97]. Angiogenesis and metastasis are fundamental hallmarks of cancer, and given that TAMs critically regulate both processes, extensive analyses of clinical specimens have established robust correlations between elevated TAM density (particularly M2-polarized TAMs) and adverse clinical outcomes, including higher histological grade, enhanced tumor proliferation, increased vascular density, greater Treg infiltration, and poorer clinical results [98–100]. The recruitment and differentiation of TAMs are orchestrated by a variety of molecular mediators, such as interleukin-3 (IL-3) from T cells, colony-stimulating factor-1 (CSF-1) from tumor and stromal cells, granulocyte-macrophage (GM)-CSF from stromal and immune cells, and CCL-2 from tumor cells, fibroblasts, and ECs [101]. While CSF-1 overexpression in tumors promotes macrophage mobilization and neoplastic progression, some studies suggest that certain macrophage populations may have tumor-suppressive effects [102, 103], such as inflammatory macrophages that recruit immune cells through chemokine production [104]. Nonetheless, the prevailing evidence from disease models and clinical trials supports the cancer-promoting role of TAMs. TAMs exert their pro-tumoral effects through multiple mechanisms. They create a mutation-prone microenvironment and activate key transcription factors like STAT3 and NF-κB [105] by generating a range of inflammogenic modulators, including EGFR family ligands, TNF, IL-6, IFN-γ, proteases, reactive oxygen species (ROS), and reactive nitrogen species [106]. This inflammatory response, now recognized as the seventh hallmark of cancer, plays a pivotal role in tumor evolution. Direct cell-cell contact between M2 macrophages and neoplastic cells enhances tumor cell proliferation via STAT3 activation, while similar interactions contribute to chemoresistance in myeloma [107, 108]. Furthermore, TAMs promote therapeutic resistance by inducing epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT), which transforms neoplastic cells and develops a pro-inflammatory tumor-associated stroma, driving invasive behavior. This process is influenced by endogenous factors, including signaling pathway activation, transcription factor regulation, and epigenetic modifications, which are modulated by extrinsic factors such as tumor-stroma interactions [109]. Mechanistically, TAM-induced EMT enhances tumor cell motility and reduces therapy responsiveness. The bidirectional interactions between TAMs and neoplastic cells significantly influence the clonal propagation, persistence, and neoplastic potential of cancer stem cells (CSCs). In addition to their roles in stimulating cancer cells, vascularization, and tumor dissemination, TAMs play a critical role in mediating immunosuppression.

TAMs that are preferentially localized within hypoxic tumor regions exhibit elevated VEGF-A expression [110]. This spatial distribution is regulated by the downregulation of Nrp1 in TAMs as they enter hypoxic niches, leading to their retention within these microenvironmental domains [111]. The molecular response to hypoxia involves HIF-1α activation in TAMs, triggering VEGF-A production. Notably, the hypoxic TME fosters macrophage polarization toward an M2 phenotype, significantly amplifying pro-angiogenic responses [112]. In addition to VEGF-mediated effects, TAMs orchestrate angiogenesis through multiple mechanisms. They secrete matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and release thymidine phosphorylase (TP) and urokinase-type plasminogen activator (uPA), all of which enhance EC migration, vascular invasion, and ECM remodeling [113]. TAMs further promote angiogenesis by stimulating the generation of angiogenic factors via pro-inflammatory mediators, particularly interleukin-1 (IL-1) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) [114]. Additionally, TAM-derived YKL-40 activates MAPK signaling in ECs, leading to the upregulation of VEGF receptors (VEGFR-1 and VEGFR-2), thereby further potentiating the angiogenic cascade [115].

Endothelial cell-mediated immune inhibition in the TME

Cancer cells display a unique metabolic phenotype, characterized by an enhanced ability to acquire and utilize nutrients from nutrient-depleted environments, thereby supporting cellular viability, proliferation, and biomass accumulation. This metabolic adaptation, distinct from normal cellular metabolism, underpins the development and maintenance of cancerous properties, establishing metabolic reprogramming as a hallmark of cancer [116]. Neoplastic cells systematically alter metabolic flux through various pathways to meet elevated bioenergetic and biosynthetic demands while managing oxidative stress—key processes critical for proliferation and survival [117]. The role of metabolic reprogramming in coordinating immune responses is increasingly recognized [118]. Changes in immune cell metabolism significantly affect their functional properties. During inflammatory responses, T cells require substantial energy and biomolecular resources to support expansion, proliferation, and immune effector activity, necessitating considerable metabolic adaptation. Similar metabolic flexibility is observed in ECs, which engage with and regulate immune cell function [119]. This metabolic remodeling in both immune cells and ECs derives energy from the degradation of three primary biomass sources—glucose, fatty acids, and amino acids—primarily through glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation [120]. TECs are critical sentinel components and sophisticated immunological regulators within the TME, orchestrating tissue-specific and vessel-type-specific immunoregulatory functions. These cells perform multiple coordinated functions, including regulating leukocyte migration, modulating leukocyte activation states, and controlling antigen presentation. TECs exhibit altered immunological phenotypes, with diminished antigen presentation capabilities and reduced immune cell recruitment, fostering an immune-inhibitory microenvironment that facilitates tumor immune escape. This functional adaptation enables tumors to establish and maintain immunological sanctuaries, promoting cancer progression and therapeutic resistance [121].

Exosomes are specialized, membrane-bound vesicles secreted into the interstitial microenvironment through multivesicular bodies (MVBs) by various cell types, including neoplastic cells. These nanoscale vesicles carry a complex cargo of proteins, messenger RNA (mRNA), microRNA (miRNA), transcription factors, lipids, and other bioactive molecules [122]. Emerging evidence shows that neoplastic cells systematically release exosomes into their local environment, where they perform critical functions, such as regulating tumor growth and progression, orchestrating angiogenic responses, facilitating immune evasion, and promoting tumor invasion and metastatic spread [123]. Thus, exosomes serve as key mediators of both homotypic crosstalk between cancer cells and heterotypic signaling within the TME.

Under physiological conditions, ECs play a pivotal role in immune surveillance by regulating immune cell trafficking within surrounding tissues. In response to inflammatory stimuli, activated ECs orchestrate the recruitment and directional migration of effector leukocytes into the TME. As key mediators of T cell recruitment and activation, ECs are integral components of immune system regulation [119]. The molecular mechanisms underlying TEC-dependent immune modulation are outlined in Fig. 6. Through selective secretion of targeted mediators, ECs can differentially modulate the proliferation of distinct T cell subpopulations. These T cell subsets perform diverse immunological functions, providing both soluble and contact-dependent signals that reciprocally regulate EC function and survival. Emerging evidence suggests that ECs modulate immune cell metabolism, facilitating their transition from quiescence to activation. The metabolic state of ECs serves as a key determinant of endothelial phenotype and functional behavior [120]. Rapidly proliferating tumors create metabolic constraints within the microenvironment, resulting in critical deficiencies of glucose and amino acids essential for T cell activity [124–126]. In response, T cells undergo metabolic reprogramming to achieve their effector state, characterized by enhanced glycolysis, glucose uptake, glutamine oxidation, and oxygen consumption [127]. However, glucose deprivation within the TME impairs these energy-intensive processes, forcing CD4 + T cells to adopt a quiescent, energy-conserving phenotype, ultimately leading to functional impairment.

Neoplastic cells have evolved sophisticated metabolic adaptations, providing survival advantages through enhanced energy production and anabolic processes, while simultaneously engineering a microenvironment that compromises immune cell metabolism and anti-tumor responses. TECs exhibit distinct metabolic characteristics, including overexpression of GLUT transporters, which indicates increased glucose uptake and glycolytic activity. Compared to NECs, TECs demonstrate significantly elevated glycolytic rates, with glycolysis serving as the primary ATP source. TECs also upregulate glucose utilization for nucleotide biosynthesis, a metabolic adaptation driven by altered enzymatic expression and increased production of pro-angiogenic factors (particularly VEGF) and pro-glycolytic factors, particularly in the hypoxic TME [128]. Therapeutic strategies targeting EC metabolism should aim to normalize TEC glycolytic flux to physiological levels rather than induce TEC death, as the latter could lead to adverse effects, toxicity, and potentially increase metastatic risk. Beyond glycolysis, TECs exhibit metabolic plasticity, maintaining functional mitochondria and actively participating in nutrient supplementation to support stressed cancer cells. In the nutrient-limited TME, characterized by hypoxia, hypoglycemia, and compromised perfusion, TECs upregulate autophagy as an adaptive response. This autophagic activation allows TECs to utilize glycogen as an energy reserve, supporting their survival under challenging conditions. The genetic and metabolic reprogramming of ECs offers a promising strategy to reverse the immunosuppressive vascular microenvironment and overcome immune evasion, paving the way for a novel vascular-targeting paradigm in next-generation cancer therapeutics.

Molecular mechanisms governing TEC-CAF interactions

Cancer-associated fibroblasts: origins and subtypes

Fibroblasts are the primary multifunctional stromal cells in connective tissue, responsible for orchestrating ECM deposition, basement membrane assembly, epithelial differentiation, immune modulation, and tissue homeostasis maintenance [129]. CAFs share characteristics with activated fibroblasts during inflammatory responses [130]. Within the TME, CAFs exhibit abnormal accumulation and significant heterogeneity in their cellular origins, phenotypic states, and functional properties [131]. CAFs originate from various cellular precursors, including tissue-resident fibroblasts, pericytes, ECs, stellate cells, BM-derived mesenchymal cells, and adipocytes, reflecting their complex developmental origins [132–134]. The transformation of fibroblasts into CAFs is driven by a complex network of signaling molecules, cytokines, and miRNAs within the tissue microenvironment, which collectively regulate cellular responses through multiple signaling pathways [135]. CAFs secrete a diverse range of signaling molecules, including VEGF, FGF, and platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), all of which contribute to tumor progression [136]. Additionally, CAFs produce transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), a key mediator in EMT [137]. This multifaceted functionality highlights the complex roles of fibroblasts in cancer biology. CAFs maintain close interactions with neoplastic cells throughout all stages of cancer progression, with their structural and functional contributions increasingly recognized as critical determinants of disease evolution. By generating growth factors, chemokines, and ECM components, CAFs regulate vasculogenic responses in ECs and pericytes, making them critical factors in tumor malignancy and promising therapeutic targets [132].

Cancer-associated fibroblasts in tumor progression

CAFs, as a critical component of the TME, promote tumor cell growth by releasing mitogenic growth factors [138]. The role of CAFs in facilitating tumor growth is illustrated in Fig. 7. CAFs are central to the activation of the nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) pro-inflammatory signaling pathway, which promotes cancer progression and neovascularization. CAF-derived TGF-β induces EMT, a key process for tumor invasion and metastasis. Beyond their involvement in multifactorial crosstalk between neoplastic cells and the TME, CAFs provide essential structural support to the tumor-supportive microenvironment. They synthesize ECM proteins, which contribute to the desmoplastic interaction at tumor margins and within the tumor mass, thus protecting cancer cells from immune surveillance and chemotherapeutic agents. Upon accumulation in the TME, CAFs are activated by local growth factors and cytokines. In certain solid tumors with pronounced desmoplastic reactions, fibroblasts can comprise up to 80% of the tumor volume [139]. The pleiotropic functions of CAFs help orchestrate the TME and regulate tumor-stromal interactions through direct cell-cell contact, the release of multiple mediators, ECM remodeling, and the enhancement of epithelial cell malignancy [140, 141]. CAFs facilitate hypoxia-driven tumor angiogenesis and represent a significant barrier to the penetration of therapeutic agents and nanomedicines into tumor tissue, thereby modulating tumor response and therapeutic efficacy [142]. Emerging evidence suggests that CAFs contribute to the exclusion of cytotoxic T cells from tumors and inhibit anti-tumor immune responses [143]. In several malignancies, an increased CAF density within the TME correlates with poor clinical outcomes [144, 145]. CAF-secreted TGF-β and stromal cell-derived factor-1 (SDF-1) promote the formation of capillary-like structures by interacting with TGF-β receptor 1 (TGF-βr1) and C-X-C chemokine receptor type 4 (CXCR-4) on neoplastic cells, facilitating EC migration, vessel formation, and angiogenesis [146, 147]. ECM remodeling is another crucial function of CAFs and a characteristic feature of the fibrogenic response in numerous cancers. Stimulated fibroblasts are a major source of ECM-degrading proteases, including MMPs, zinc-dependent endopeptidases that promote neoplastic cell invasion through the ECM [148–150].

The mechanisms underlying CAF-mediated tumor progression are diverse and complex. Normal fibroblasts undergo transdifferentiation into CAFs in response to multiple stimuli, including therapy-induced DNA damage, intercellular and cell-matrix contact signaling, physiological stress, inflammatory signals, ECM alterations, and receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) ligand activation. Once activated, these CAFs exert profound effects within the tumor microenvironment. CAFs facilitate tumor progression through diverse mechanisms, including the secretion of pro-tumorigenic cytokines, dynamic ECM remodeling, enhancement of angiogenesis, immunosuppression, metabolic reprogramming via provision of substrates (e.g., lactate, amino acids, fatty acids) to tumor cells, and regulation of therapeutic responses

Therapeutic strategies targeting tumor endothelial cells: current status and future perspectives

Pharmacologic agents and mechanisms

Ideal cancer therapies should selectively eliminate malignant cells while sparing normal cells. Conventional chemotherapies exert their anti-tumor effects through various mechanisms, including direct cytotoxicity, cell cycle arrest, and suppression of metastatic dissemination [151]. Key therapeutic targets include adhesion molecules, signaling molecules, and chemokines that regulate cancer cell-EC interactions [152–154]. Regulatory molecules governing angiogenesis and invasion are promising targets for anti-cancer therapies [155, 156]. The incorporation of bevacizumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), into standard chemotherapy regimens significantly improves overall survival in patients with colorectal and lung cancers [157]. Sunitinib, a small-molecule receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitor, also plays a role in anti-cancer therapy [158]. Multi-targeted tyrosine kinase inhibitors, including sunitinib, sorafenib, and pazopanib, target VEGFR1-3, PDGF receptor (PDGFR), and c-Kit [159]. Biological properties and therapeutic implications in anti-angiogenic treatment

Tumor progression critically depends on neovascularization—the formation of new blood vessels from the existing vasculature [160]. This process involves a dynamic equilibrium between pro-angiogenic and anti-angiogenic factors within the tissue niche. VEGF-A is a key pro-angiogenic factor, making anti-VEGF therapies a promising area of study. Such therapies can induce unanticipated vascular normalization, ECM remodeling, and improved vascular perfusion [161, 162]. Src homology 2 domain-containing protein tyrosine phosphatase 2 (SHP2), encoded by PTPN11, is ubiquitously expressed across various cell types, including vascular cells. SHP2 plays a critical role in mediating growth factor and cytokine signaling, with its expression often upregulated or mutated in tumors, contributing to its oncogenic potential through dysregulated activity [163–165]. SHP2 directly interacts with VEGFR2, establishing it as a key regulator of VEGF-VEGFR2 signaling [166], making SHP2 in TECs an emerging target for AATs [167]. In addition to its effects on angiogenesis, anti-VEGF therapy modulates the tumor immunological microenvironment by reducing immunosuppressive cell populations, including Tregs and MDSCs [168, 169]. The combination of angiogenesis inhibitors with immunotherapy and radiotherapy has shown significant therapeutic efficacy in advanced solid tumors [170], as demonstrated by the approval of the atezolizumab-bevacizumab regimen in HCC and the pembrolizumab-lenvatinib combination in endometrial carcinoma. Table 3 summarizes the ongoing clinical trials investigating the combination of anti-angiogenesis therapy and immunotherapy. Despite their potential, AATs present significant clinical challenges, including adverse effects such as hypertension, hand-foot syndrome, proteinuria, and thyroid dysfunction, which are attributed to their impact on normal vasculature. Additionally, the development of drug resistance often requires modifications to the treatment regimen [171–173]. Prolonged AAT can induce tumor hypoxia, enhancing invasiveness [174], as hypoxic conditions stimulate further angiogenesis via the mobilization of BM-derived endothelial progenitor cells [175]. Furthermore, although tumors may initially respond to AATs, these treatments can paradoxically promote disease recurrence and metastatic progression [176]. AAT is therefore a double-edged sword, offering potential benefits such as tumor regression and extended survival, but also posing clinically significant risks due to its adverse effects [171, 173, 177]. A fundamental challenge in AAT lies in its narrow therapeutic window, where optimal efficacy requires precise dosing to achieve vessel normalization without exacerbating hypoxia, while also managing the toxicities associated with effects on healthy vasculature [178]. Critically, this combination approach has become a cornerstone of modern cancer treatment, as evidenced by its adoption as the standard first-line regimen for advanced cervical cancer following the positive results of NCT03635567, which demonstrated the significant survival benefit of adding pembrolizumab and bevacizumab to chemotherapy.

Regulation of cancer-associated fibroblast development

CAFs play a complex dual role in tumor progression and metastatic dissemination, exhibiting both tumor-promoting and tumor-suppressing properties. They orchestrate immunosuppression within the TME by secreting a range of immunomodulatory factors, including interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), interleukin-6 (IL-6), C-C motif chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), which collectively regulate the trafficking and function of cytotoxic T lymphocytes, NK cells, and macrophages [179–181]. The paracrine signaling network between CAFs and immune cells facilitates the differentiation of immunosuppressive myeloid populations and modulates macrophage recruitment to the tumor site [182–184]. The CAF secretome, which includes chemokines such as C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 9 (CXCL9), CXCL10, and SDF-1, regulates T cell recruitment and activation [185]. Beyond secretome-mediated immunomodulation, CAF-modified ECM plays a critical role in regulating immune cell infiltration by establishing physical barriers and influencing the accessibility of adhesion molecules, thereby controlling immune cell access to tumor cells [186, 187].

Current challenges and limitations in endothelial cell-targeted cancer therapy

Phenotypic and functional heterogeneity

Emerging evidence demonstrates that TEC subtype heterogeneity correlates with tumor malignancy grade. TECs exhibit phenotypic characteristics influenced by tumor-derived factors, such as growth factors, chemokines, and miRNAs secreted by the parent tumors. These phenotypes show dynamic plasticity in response to the TME, disease progression stage, and metastatic status. Identifying TEC-specific markers reflecting vascular heterogeneity could enable more precise patient stratification for AATs [188]. In contrast to NECs, TECs display distinct functional subpopulations with significant phenotypic variation. Single-cell transcriptomic analyses have identified two predominant TEC subpopulations: pro-angiogenic and immunomodulatory phenotypes. The pro-angiogenic subpopulation shows elevated expression of VEGF and angiopoietin-2 (ANGPT2), activating VEGFR2/ERK signaling pathways that drive aberrant angiogenesis, resulting in dysfunctional vascular architecture and insufficient perfusion. The immunomodulatory subpopulation upregulates immune checkpoint molecules, such as programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) and indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase 1 (IDO1), while secreting chemokines like CCL2 and CSF1 to recruit TAMs and Tregs, thereby promoting an immunosuppressive microenvironment. Spatial transcriptomic analyses reveal distinct anatomical distributions of these subpopulations: pro-angiogenic TECs predominantly reside within the tumor core, while immunomodulatory TECs concentrate at the invasive margins [189].

Delta-like ligand 4 (Dll4), a member of the Notch ligand family primarily expressed in ECs, plays a key role in vascular lumen formation in both physiological and pathological contexts. The Dll4/Notch signaling pathway, operating downstream of VEGF signaling, governs vascular remodeling during embryonic development [190–192]. Mechanistically, endothelial Dll4 binds to Notch1 receptors on adjacent cells, triggering γ-secretase-mediated proteolytic cleavage of the Notch intracellular domain (NICD). The NICD then translocates to the nucleus, where it forms a transcriptional complex with recombination signal binding protein Jκ (RBP-Jκ) to regulate target gene expression. While Dll4 expression is detected in various malignant cell types [193], its most robust and sustained expression occurs in tumor vasculature. Preclinical studies demonstrate that inhibiting Dll4 through genetic deletion, localized Dll4/Notch antagonist overexpression, systemic administration of soluble inhibitors, or DNA vaccination significantly suppresses tumor growth, even in VEGF inhibitor-resistant malignancies [194–199]. Dll4/Notch pathway inhibition paradoxically induces endothelial hyperproliferation and increased vascular density, but also causes excessive vessel branching, defective lumen formation, and impaired mural cell recruitment. Despite the increase in vascular density, this abnormal vasculature is functionally inadequate, failing to properly perfuse the tumor, ultimately creating an antitumoral environment. These vascular abnormalities, which mirror those commonly seen in human cancers, suggest that elevated Dll4 expression in tumor vasculature may act as a host defense mechanism by reducing endothelial sensitivity to VEGF [200], thus limiting tumor vascularization and hematogenous dissemination [201].

Immune evasion is a core hallmark of primary malignancies. Within the TME, malignant cells evade immunosurveillance by expressing immune checkpoint molecules, particularly programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) and its ligand (PD-L1), which modulate the activity of infiltrating cytotoxic lymphocytes, including CD8 + T cells and NK cells [202]. The PD-L1/PD-1 axis suppresses the cytotoxic functions of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, thereby promoting immune escape and facilitating tumor progression [203]. Elevated PD-L1 expression, observed across a range of human malignancies [204], correlates with poor clinical outcomes [205, 206]. Notably, this immunosuppressive mechanism extends beyond cancer cells to the tumor vasculature. Specifically, TECs also express PD-L1, acting as a critical barrier to T-cell trafficking and function. The regulation of PD-L1 expression within the TME involves multiple mechanisms—transcriptional, post-transcriptional, and post-translational—triggered by inflammatory mediators and oncogenic signaling pathways [203]. Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes exhibit significantly higher PD-1 expression compared to their counterparts in normal tissues across various tumor types. The overexpression of both PD-L1 and PD-1 in the TME is a key mechanism of immune evasion, contributing to tumor progression and adverse clinical outcomes. Consequently, targeting the PD-L1/PD-1 axis has emerged as a promising immunotherapeutic strategy, demonstrating significant anti-tumor efficacy across multiple cancer types [207]. Disruption of PD-L1/PD-1 signaling restores cytotoxic lymphocyte function, thereby reducing tumor immune tolerance [203].

Tumor-associated endothelial cells in treatment resistance

Table 4 summarizes several immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) approved or under clinical investigation by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration. The immune checkpoint pathway, comprising cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4), PD-1, and its ligand PD-L1, serves as a crucial negative regulator of T cell-mediated immune responses, allowing cancer cells to evade immune surveillance [223]. Anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) restore T cell functionality by blocking inhibitory signals mediated by PD-1 ligands (PD-L1 and PD-L2), which are primarily expressed on malignant and tumor-associated cells, including TECs [224, 225]. ICIs, a class of mAbs targeting the PD-1/PD-L1 axis or CTLA-4, represent a major advancement in cancer immunotherapy. These inhibitors effectively attenuate inhibitory T cell activation signals, allowing tumor-reactive T cells to overcome regulatory mechanisms and generate potent anti-tumor responses [226]. While anti-PD-1 therapy has significantly improved 5-year survival rates compared to conventional treatments [227], durable responses are observed in only a subset of patients, with approximately 87% of PD-1/PD-L1-targeted malignancies exhibiting resistance to treatment [228]. Resistance to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade may be either primary (lack of initial response) or acquired (disease progression following initial response). A key mechanism of both resistance forms is the tumor’s ability to exploit alternative immunosuppressive pathways to circumvent checkpoint blockade [207]. Conventional cytotoxic chemotherapy enhances anti-tumor immunity through two mechanisms: the secretion of tumor-associated antigens and the selective reduction of immunosuppressive cell populations [229, 230]. The intricate interplay between anti-tumor immunity and angiogenesis is a fundamental aspect of cancer biology, as these processes are highly interdependent and considered established hallmarks of cancer [231, 232]. These normally regulated physiological mechanisms are often co-opted during neoplastic progression, promoting tumor development and metastatic spread. The therapeutic effectiveness of PD-1/PD-L1 blockade is critically dependent on efficient lymphocyte trafficking from lymphoid organs to tumor sites via the vasculature [232, 233]. Consequently, resistance to PD-1/PD-L1 suppression is often associated with inadequate baseline T cell infiltration into the TME [234–236]. TECs play a central role in regulating vascular architecture and function within the TME. They actively interact with circulating immune cells, modulating immune cell trafficking and anti-tumor immune responses. Mechanistically, TECs suppress pro-inflammatory cytokine production by CD8 + T cells, and PD-L1 deficiency on TECs results in reduced suppression and apoptosis of tumor-infiltrating CD8 + T cells, ultimately enhancing tumor growth inhibition in experimental models. These findings suggest that TECs regulate tumor antigen-specific CD8 + T cell responses through the PD-1/PD-L1 axis, selectively promoting immunosuppressive CD4 + T cell development and facilitating tumor immune evasion [237]. Recent evidence also indicates that targeting ECs, particularly through anti-VEGF therapies, synergistically enhances ICI efficacy, highlighting vascular normalization as a promising strategy to overcome treatment resistance.

Conclusion and outlook

The TME comprises a diverse array of cellular constituents, including immune and stromal cells, as well as non-cellular elements such as the ECM, vasculature, lymphatics, cytokines, and bioactive mediators [238]. TECs act as central regulators within the TME, orchestrating tumorigenesis, progression, and metastatic dissemination by dynamically modulating tumor angiogenesis and immune microenvironment homeostasis. This review elucidates the multi-dimensional regulatory mechanisms of TECs at various levels: at the molecular level, TECs regulate tumor cell proliferation and microenvironmental remodeling through three key mechanisms: signaling pathway competition (e.g., dynamic regulation of the PI3K/AKT pathway by VEGFR and FGFR), functional redundancy (e.g., complementary activation of ANG-Tie2 and VEGF-VEGFR), and shared downstream signaling networks (e.g., NF-κB mediating both IL-6 and VEGF expression) [239–242]. At the functional level, TECs serve dual regulatory roles: promoting neovascularization through the secretion of angiogenic factors, notably VEGF, while also facilitating tumor metastasis via the release of specific cytokines (IL-6, IL-8) and chemokines (CXCL12) [243]. Notably, TECs and immune cells establish a sophisticated bidirectional regulatory network—immune cells influence angiogenesis efficiency by regulating TEC function, while TECs shape the immunosuppressive microenvironment by inhibiting antigen presentation, recruiting Tregs, and employing other mechanisms. These findings highlight the pivotal role of TECs in tumor progression and provide a theoretical foundation for targeting TECs as an anti-tumor strategy.

TECs significantly regulate the TME through intricate bidirectional signaling networks. On one hand, TEC-mediated metabolic remodeling (e.g., enhanced glycolysis and glutamine metabolism) drives lactate accumulation, leading to TME acidification. This acidic microenvironment not only supports the bioenergetic needs of TEC proliferation but also suppresses effector T cell function and potentiates the immunosuppressive activities of MDSCs. On the other hand, TECs and immune cells establish a reciprocal regulatory mechanism—immune cells (e.g., TAMs) modulate TEC activity via molecules such as miR-21-5p carried by exosomes, while TECs reciprocally suppress immune responses through immune checkpoint pathways such as PD-L1/PD-1. These mechanistic insights highlight the importance of targeting the integrated regulatory network of the TME for effective microenvironment remodeling.

Currently, the combination of neovascularization-suppressing agents (e.g., bevacizumab) and ICIs has become a standard therapeutic approach. Ongoing research is advancing in three innovative directions: first, the development of novel bispecific antibodies targeting VEGFR2/PD-L1; second, exploring metabolic reprogramming interventions, particularly lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA) inhibitors; and third, the innovation of chimeric antigen receptor-engineered TECs (CAR-TECs) as a therapeutic strategy [244]. Extensive clinical evidence demonstrates that this vascular-immune combination approach significantly enhances objective response rates and progression-free survival in patients with solid tumors, ushering in a new era of vascular-immune synergistic therapy. Thus, EC-targeted therapies represent a promising frontier for therapeutic innovation and offer novel opportunities for personalized treatment strategies.

Despite significant progress in TEC-targeted anti-tumor therapies for solid tumors, three critical challenges persist in clinical applications. First, the molecular mechanisms underlying TEC subpopulation heterogeneity (e.g., pro-angiogenic/immunomodulatory phenotypes) remain incompletely understood. For example, in HCC, the limited understanding of molecular heterogeneity in hepatocarcinogenesis and complex tumor-TME interactions continues to hinder therapeutic efficacy and patient outcomes [245–247]. Second, current anti-angiogenic agents show suboptimal targeting efficiency against metabolic-immune coupling mechanisms (e.g., lactate-PD-L1 co-regulation), which may exacerbate tumor hypoxia and promote EMT-mediated chemoresistance. Third, existing therapeutic strategies lack precise intervention approaches and validated biomarker-guided protocols to address the multi-dimensional interactions within the TEC-immune axis, such as antigen presentation suppression and chemokine gradient alterations.

In conclusion, TECs offer compelling therapeutic targets, presenting a paradigm-shifting opportunity to overcome current treatment limitations. Future research should focus on three key areas: First, the systematic characterization of TEC subpopulation heterogeneity using integrated spatial transcriptomics, spatial metabolomics, and single-cell multi-omics analyses, alongside organoid chip platforms. Second, the development of innovative therapeutic modalities, including VEGFR2/PD-L1 bispecific antibodies and smart-responsive nanodrug delivery systems [248]. Third, the application of artificial intelligence modeling to predict TEC-immune cell interaction networks, combined with TEC molecular subtype-guided umbrella trials—designed to efficiently test multiple targeted therapies in parallel by matching them to specific TEC subtypes—will optimize personalized combination therapies. By thoroughly elucidating TECs’ central role and compensatory mechanisms within the TME, these strategies aim to shift therapeutic approaches from conventional “vascular inhibition” to “vascular-immune synergistic remodeling,” offering novel solutions to combat tumor drug resistance and metastatic progression.
