An all-in-one theranostic platform for enhanced TACE: self-developing embolization with dual-targeted arsenic trioxide delivery
Authors: Tianwen Yuan, Zhong Qi, Xiaojun Zhang, Kai Yang, Ying Xu, Jun Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains a leading cause of cancer mortality. For patients with advanced, unresectable disease, transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) is a recommended first-line locoregional therapy. However, its efficacy is constrained by the inherent limitations of conventional embolic materials—such as non-target embolization, vessel recanalization, and incomplete distal occlusion—and by the low intra-tumoral delivery efficiency of chemotherapeutic agents. Furthermore, the high prevalence of p53 mutations (~ 30%) in HCC drives tumorigenesis and chemoresistance, creating a critical therapeutic gap, especially within the hypoxic tumor microenvironment. To address these multifaceted challenges, we developed an innovative theranostic platform, CHSA/NAT. This system combines a radiopaque, thermosensitive hydrogel (NAT) for precise, image-guided vascular occlusion with CD44-targeted, glutathione (GSH)-responsive chitosan/hyaluronic acid nanomicelles (CHSA), which enable the smart delivery of arsenic trioxide (ATO)—a potent agent with p53-mutant corrective activity. In vitro, ATO demonstrated superior cytotoxicity over doxorubicin against multiple HCC cells under hypoxia. In a p53-mutant PLC/PRF/5 subcutaneous model, CHSA-ATO achieved a tumor inhibition rate of 84.6%, significantly outperforming free ATO and doxorubicin, and effectively modulated apoptotic proteins (downregulating mutant p53/Bcl-2, upregulating Bax). Crucially, superselective administration of CHSA/NAT enabled successful embolization with clear radiological visualization in rat hepatic and renal arteries, demonstrating its precise image‑guided delivery and embolization capability. Most importantly, in an orthotopic rat HCC model, the combined treatment exhibited powerful synergistic efficacy, attaining a remarkable 96% tumor suppression rate and significantly prolonging survival, with a favorable safety profile. This work presents a novel “embolization‑imaging‑targeted chemotherapy” strategy that simultaneously overcomes the key limitations of conventional TACE and ATO delivery, offering a promising and translatable nanoplatform for the effective treatment of advanced HCC.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04382-6.Keywords: Nanomicelles, Arsenic trioxide, Thermosensitive hydrogel, Targeting, Transarterial chemoembolization

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) ranks as the sixth most common cancer globally and the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality [1, 2]. The majority of patients are diagnosed at an intermediate or advanced stage, thereby losing the opportunity for curative interventions such as surgical resection or liver transplantation [3, 4]. For these patients with unresectable disease, or those presenting with vascular invasion, extrahepatic spread, transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) has become one of the most frequently employed non-surgical treatment modalities [5, 6]. The principle of TACE involves the selective embolization of tumor-feeding arteries to block blood supply, concurrently delivering high concentrations of chemotherapeutic agents locally. Consequently, TACE is recommended as a first-line treatment for advanced HCC by both the European Association for the Study of the Liver (EASL) and the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD), demonstrating particular efficacy in hypervascular tumors [7, 8].

Despite its widespread clinical application, the therapeutic efficacy of TACE remains significantly limited. The recent CHANCE001 retrospective cohort study involving Chinese patients with advanced HCC reported an objective response rate (ORR) of merely 32% for TACE monotherapy [9]. These limitations are likely attributable to the local pathological alterations induced by embolization and the inherent constraints of the embolic materials themselves. On one hand, TACE exacerbates the hypoxic and acidic tumor microenvironment, triggering vascular structural disarray and molecular-level changes [10]. This may not only enhance the malignant potential of residual tumor cells but also increase the risks of the tumor metastasis and drug resistance [11]. On the other hand, currently used embolic agents (e.g., lipiodol or polymer microspheres) in conventional TACE (cTACE) or drug-eluting bead TACE (DEB-TACE) are associated with issues such as vessel recanalization, non-target embolization, inability to occlude distal microvasculature, and still poor utilization efficiency of drugs [12]. Notably, studies indicate no substantial difference in progression-free survival between patients receiving cTACE and those undergoing DEB-TACE [13, 14]. Therefore, there remains considerable scope for improving the efficacy of TACE. There is an urgent need for the development of novel embolic materials and combination therapeutic strategies with the capability to achieve complete vascular occlusion and enable targeted drug delivery.

Thermosensitive hydrogels, characterized by adequate mechanical strength and injectability, represent a promising class of tumor embolic agents [15]. Their thermosensitive phase transition property allows them to exist in a sol state below the phase transition temperature. In this state, they can be smoothly injected through an interventional microcatheter into the tumor-feeding arteries. At the target embolization site, body temperature triggers their transformation into a gel state. The resulting robust mechanical strength enables the gel to withstand hemodynamic pressure, forming a stable and durable embolic structure. As a unique embolic material, hydrogels possess the potential to achieve complete embolization of both large vessels and small microvasculature. This characteristic effectively combines the advantage of microspheres in occluding larger arteries with the capability of iodized oil to block smaller vessels [16]. Compared to Poloxamer 407, poly(N-isopropylacrylamide) (PNIPA) offers the advantage of a lower critical solution temperature (LCST) that can be readily tuned by adjusting the monomer ratio [17, 18]. The thermosensitive nanogel synthesized by Zhou et al. [19], based on poly (N-isopropylacrylamide-co-butyl methacrylate) and loaded with apatinib, demonstrated favorable antitumor efficacy. Upon body temperature triggering, it achieved effective embolization in the vasculature of rabbit VX2 liver tumors and enabled localized sustained drug release. Furthermore, the development of gels with radiopaque functionality facilitates intraoperative real-time imaging, enabling precise guidance during transarterial catheter procedures. This integrated capability addresses the “blind” nature of traditional non-visualizable hydrogels while incorporating the imaging advantages of iodinated embolic agents [20]. Specifically, the designed PNIPA-based materials help prevent non-target embolization and confirm complete tumor vasculature occlusion under image guidance, thereby enhancing the accuracy and safety of the interventional therapy.

Mutations in the p53 gene, which occur at an average frequency of 30% in HCC, represent the most common genetic alteration in this malignancy [21]. These mutations lead to loss of p53 tumor-suppressive function, thereby promoting tumorigenesis and metastasis [22]. Among them, the p53-R249S mutation—resulting from an arginine-to-serine substitution at codon 249—represents a distinct molecular hallmark of HCC [23]. This mutation is the sole hotspot alteration in HCC that is strongly associated with chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection and dietary exposure to aflatoxin B1 (AFB1). Notably, p53-R249S accounts for approximately 26% of all p53 mutations reported in HCC, yet it remains exceedingly rare (less than 2%) in other cancer types [24]. Despite its prevalence and clinical significance, the development of effective therapies specifically targeting p53-R249S HCC, particularly those that rely on advanced drug delivery strategies, remains a major challenge. Arsenic trioxide (ATO), a drug with established efficacy in acute promyelocytic leukemia, has garnered increasing attention for its potential in solid tumor treatment [25]. ATO exerts antitumor effects through multiple mechanisms, including inducing tumor cell differentiation and apoptosis, inhibiting tumor cell proliferation, and exerting anti-angiogenic effects [26–28]. Furthermore, it has been reported that ATO restores p53 function through protein structure stabilization [29]. However, its characteristics as a water-soluble inorganic salt result in a short in vivo half-life and poor targeting. The high dosage required to achieve effective therapeutic concentrations may cause severe toxic side effects [30, 31]. Utilizing nanotechnology to construct ATO delivery systems represents an effective strategy to address this challenge [32, 33]. For instance, Song et al. [34]. investigated multifunctional chitosan derivatives, which subsequently loaded ATO, enhancing tumor accumulation and reducing systemic toxicity. Glutathione (GSH) is critical for redox homeostasis and chemoresistance in cancer. Intracellular GSH levels in tumor cells reach millimolar concentrations, whereas extracellular GSH remains in the micromolar range, making GSH an attractive trigger for tumor-specific drug release [35]. Arsenic (III) readily forms stable As–S bonds with thiol groups, which dissociate under reducing conditions [36]. Thus, the GSH-rich tumor microenvironment provides a rational basis for designing nanodrugs that enable controlled arsenic release via GSH-triggered As–S bond cleavage.

Building upon this foundation, this study aims to design and construct an GSH integrated “embolization-imaging-targeted chemotherapy” theranostic platform: a self-developing thermosensitive hydrogel loaded with ATO-targeted nanomicelles serving as a novel TACE therapeutic platform (Scheme 1). This system utilizes a PEGylated iodine -labeled PNIPA thermosensitive polymer (NAT) as the embolic matrix, ensuring precise body temperature-triggered embolization with inherent imaging capability. Concurrently, ATO is encapsulated within chitosan/hyaluronic acid nanomicelles (CHSA) that are responsive to the tumor microenvironment (e.g., high glutathione levels) and capable of targeting CD44 receptors. The material properties, in vitro antitumor efficacy, in vivo embolization performance, antitumor effect on p53‑mutant subcutaneous model and rat orthotopic HCC model were further evaluated. This design leverages the synergistic effect of hepatic artery embolization and targeted chemotherapy to achieve durable and visible vascular occlusion via the thermosensitive hydrogel, while utilizing intelligent nanomicelles to enhance ATO accumulation within tumor tissue, thereby augmenting the antitumor efficacy and reducing systemic toxicity.

Methods

Synthesis of the self-developing thermosensitive polymer

Synthesis of water-soluble iodinated contrast agents

2,3,5-Triiodobenzoic acid (TIBA, 1.5 g, 3 mmol) and allyl poly (ethylene glycol) (APEG, 4.8 g, 2 mmol) were accurately weighed and dissolved in 25 mL of anhydrous N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF). 4-Dimethylaminopyridine (DMAP, 0.0549 g, 0.45 mmol) was added, and the mixture was maintained under an ice bath for 12 h. Subsequently, 1,3-dicyclohexylcarbodiimide (DCC, 0.7737 g, 3.75 mmol) was introduced, and the reaction was allowed to proceed at room temperature for an additional 18 h under nitrogen protection. After completion, the reaction mixture was cooled in an ice bath and filtered. The filtrate was dialyzed against double-distilled water (DD water) for 48 h using a dialysis bag (MWCO of 1 kDa), followed by lyophilization to obtain APEG-TIBA, denoted as AT.

Synthesis of self-developing thermosensitive polymer

The self-developing thermosensitive polymer was synthesized via emulsion polymerization. Purified N-isopropylacrylamide (NIPA), AT, sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), and N, N’-methylenebisacrylamide (MBA) were placed in a flask and dissolved in water. Potassium persulfate (KPS) was added to initiate the polymerization, and the reaction continued at 70 °C under a nitrogen atmosphere for 4.5 h, yielding a polymer dispersion. This dispersion was placed in a dialysis bag (MWCO of 8–14 kDa) for dialysis. Finally, the self-developing thermosensitive polymer NIPA-APEG-TIBA (abbreviated as NAT) was obtained after lyophilization.

A single-factor study was conducted by preparing polymers with varying NIPA: AT molar ratios (20:1, 10:1, 5:1) and NIPA: MBA molar ratios (24:1, 16:1, 8:1). The phase transition behavior of each formulation was evaluated to determine the optimal composition. The modification degree of the TIBA in both AT and NAT was determined by UV spectrophotometry (Supplementary Materials).

Preparation of ATO-loaded nanomicelles

Thiolated hyaluronic acid (HA-SH) was first synthesized, and its thiol content was determined using Ellman’s assay, as described in the Supplementary Materials. HA-S-ATO (abbreviated as HSA) was prepared through a conjugation reaction mediated by reduced glutathione (GSH). Briefly, HA-SH (50 mg) was dissolved in 10 mL of DD water and pretreated with an appropriate volume of GSH solution at room temperature for 2 h. Subsequently, an aqueous solution of ATO (5 mg/mL) was added to the mixture. The pH was adjusted to 5–6, and the reaction was allowed to proceed under stirring at room temperature for 3 h. The crude product was purified by dialysis against deionized water at 4 °C for 2 days, followed by lyophilization to obtain HSA as a solid.

The drug-loaded nanomicelles were prepared via electrostatic complexation. Under vigorous stirring, a 0.1 M acetic acid solution containing HSA (1.5 mg/mL) was added dropwise into a chitosan (CS) solution (0.025%, w/v). Stirring was continued until a faint bluish opalescence appeared, indicating the formation of micelles. The resulting mixture was dialyzed against deionized water for 2 days and then lyophilized to obtain the drug-loaded nanomicelles, designated as CHSA. The arsenic loading efficiency in CHSA was quantified using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS), as detailed in the Supplementary Materials.

Blank nanomicelles (CS-HA-SH, abbreviated as CHS) were prepared using the same procedure without the addition of ATO. The nanomicelles were labeled with FITC (F-CHSA) for fluorescence tracing (Supplementary Materials).

Preparation of the composite embolic agent

The drug-loaded composite embolic agent was prepared by electrostatically incorporating CHSA nanomicelles into the self-developed thermosensitive polymer (NAT). Briefly, CHSA was dissolved in PBS (pH 7.4) and then slowly added dropwise into the NAT solution at room temperature. The mixture was subsequently stirred for an additional 4 h to obtain the final formulation, designated as CHSA/NAT. The blank control embolic agent (CHS/NAT) was prepared using an identical procedure, with CHS nanomicelles substituting for CHSA.

Characterization of physical and chemical properties

Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) Spectroscopy: the chemical structures of APEG, TIBA, NIPA, and the synthesized AT, NAT were confirmed by FTIR spectroscopy (Nicolet, Nexus 470, USA) across the wavenumber range of 4000 –500 cm− 1.

Proton Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (1H NMR) Spectroscopy: The molecular structures of AT, NAT, HA-SH and HSA were verified by 1H NMR spectroscopy (Bruker, Avance III 400 MHz, Switzerland).

Elemental Analysis: Elemental analysis of the lyophilized HA-SH product was conducted using energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy mapping (EDS-Mapping, TESCAN, the Czech Republic).

Morphological Characterization: The morphology of NAT was observed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, TESCAN, the Czech Republic). The morphology of CHSA was examined using transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEM-2100, Japan).

Particle Size and Zeta Potential: The hydrodynamic diameter, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential of CHSA were measured using a dynamic light scattering analyzer (DLS, Brookhaven, 90Plus PALS, USA).

Stability Assessment: CHSA was stored at 4 °C. At predetermined time points, the particle size and size distribution were measured by (DLS. Additionally, photographic documentation of the sample appearance was taken on day 0 and day 14 for visual comparison.

Phase transition behavior and embolization performance

The sol-gel transition was assessed via the test tube inversion method. A 3% (w/v) solution of NAT or CHSA/NAT was placed in a 37 °C water bath, and the time required for gelation was recorded. Embolization performance was evaluated using a custom-made vascular catheter model. A super selective catheter connected to a soft tube was placed in a 37 °C water bath. One milliliter of the sample solution, stained with bromophenol blue, was slowly injected into the catheter to observe the embolization within the tube.

Rheological properties

The rheological properties of the post-phase transition hydrogel were measured at 37 °C using DHR-2 rheometer (TA, USA). Tests included time sweep, strain sweep, frequency sweep, and apparent viscosity measurements.

In Vitro release

To investigate the drug release behavior and GSH-responsive characteristics of CHSA and CHSA/NAT, their release profiles were studied in PBS (pH 7.4) containing different concentrations of GSH (0 µM, 20 µM, 1 mM, and 10 mM) using the dialysis bag method. The release of free ATO in PBS served as the control. Specifically, an appropriate amount of CHSA and CHSA/NAT was dispersed in PBS and placed inside a dialysis bag (MWCO of 3500 Da). At predetermined time intervals, 1 mL of the dialysate was sampled and replaced with an equal volume of fresh PBS medium. The arsenic content in the dialysate was determined by ICP-MS to calculate the cumulative release rate. Release curves were plotted to systematically evaluate the drug release behavior of the different formulations.

Hemolysis assay

A hemolysis assay was performed to assess the hemocompatibility of the materials. Whole blood was collected from healthy New Zealand rabbits via the marginal ear vein and centrifuged to obtain erythrocytes. A 2% (v/v) red blood cell (RBC) suspension was prepared in PBS (pH 7.4). For the experimental groups, NAT solutions were diluted in PBS to a series of gradient concentrations. Deionized water and PBS served as the positive and negative controls, respectively. One milliliter of each sample solution was mixed thoroughly with an equal volume of the RBC suspension. The mixtures were incubated at 37 °C with gentle shaking for 2 h, followed by centrifugation. The clarity of the supernatant was observed, and its absorbance was recorded using a UV‑1800PC spectrophotometer over the full wavelength range. Morphological changes of the erythrocytes were also examined under an optical microscope.

Cell safety evaluation

The cytocompatibility of NAT and CHS was evaluated in HepG2, PLC/PRF/5, and N1S1 cell lines. HepG2 and PLC/PRF/5 cells were seeded into 96‑well plates at a density of 3 × 104 cells/mL, while N1S1 suspension cells were seeded directly at 1 × 105 cells/mL. After 24 h of culture, cells were treated with serial concentrations (1‑1000 µg/mL) of NAT or CHS for 24 h and 48 h. Cell viability was assessed using the MTT assay, and absorbance was measured at 490 nm with a microplate reader (BioTek 800 TS).

In Vitro antitumor activity evaluation

The cytotoxicity of ATO and doxorubicin (Dox, as a positive control) were evaluated using the MTT assay. HepG2, PLC/PRF/5, and N1S1 cells were seeded in 96-well plates and allowed to adhere for 24 h. Cells were then exposed to serially diluted concentrations of each drug under either normoxic (21% O2) or hypoxic (1% O2) conditions for 24 h. Following incubation, MTT reagent was added, and the absorbance was measured at 490 nm. The IC50 values were calculated from dose-response curves.The cytotoxic effects of CHSA and CHSA/NAT were similarly assessed against the three tumor cell lines under both normoxic and hypoxic conditions using the MTT assay. To further evaluate the pro-apoptotic effects in p53-mutant cells, flow cytometry was performed using a TUNEL kit (Supplementary Materials).

Cellular uptake study

Internalization of FITC‑labeled CHSA (F‑CHSA) was assessed in HepG2, PLC/PRF/5, and N1S1 cells under normoxic (21% O2) or hypoxic (1% O2) conditions. Cells were treated with free FITC or F‑CHSA for 4 h, with or without pre‑incubation with excess free HA (100 µg/mL) for 1 h in the competition group. Cellular uptake was quantitatively evaluated by flow cytometry (BD FACSCanto II), and fluorescence distribution was visualized by fluorescence microscopy (Nikon Eclipse Ti‑E) after fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde and DAPI counterstaining.

Antitumor efficacy in a subcutaneous xenograft model

An ectopic tumor model was established by subcutaneously inoculating PLC/PRF/5 cells (5 × 106) into the right flank of Balb/c nude mice. When tumor volumes reached approximately 100 mm3, mice were randomly divided into four groups for intravenous administration of saline, Dox, ATO, or CHSA every two days for a total of five doses. Tumor volumes and body weights were measured every other day. At the experimental endpoint, mice were euthanized, and tumors were excised, weighed, and processed for subsequent analysis. The protein expression levels of p53, Bcl-2, and Bax in tumor tissues were determined by standard Western blotting. β-Actin was used as an internal loading control, and band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software.

Vascular occlusion capacity

The vascular occlusion capacity was evaluated using a rabbit ear vein model and a rat femoral/subcutaneous vein model. In the rabbit model, New Zealand white rabbits were anesthetized and CHSA/NAT (1 mL) was injected into the marginal ear vein. Vascular occlusion was assessed 14 days post-injection. In the rat model, Sprague-Dawley rats were anesthetized and CHSA/NAT (1 mL) was injected into the exposed subcutaneous vein and the femoral vein, respectively. Occlusion was evaluated 2 h after injection.

Rat hepatic and renal artery embolization and imaging

An orthotopic hepatocellular carcinoma model was established in SD rats according to a previously reported method [37]. The specific procedures were described in the Supplementary Materials. Hepatic and renal artery embolization was performed under the guidance of digital subtraction angiography (DSA, UNIQ FD20, Netherlands) via carotid artery catheterization. The specific procedures were as follows: Tumor-bearing rats were anesthetized and placed in a supine position. The common carotid artery was exposed and cannulated. A guidewire was inserted and advanced under real-time imaging guidance to selectively position the catheter tip in the hepatic artery and renal artery, respectively. Subsequently, 1 mL of CHSA/NAT suspension was slowly injected through the catheter into each target artery. Following the injection, the catheter was withdrawn, and the surgical incision was sutured closed. The embolization process and radiopacity of the agent were monitored in real-time using DSA. Following the procedure, the animals were euthanized at designated time points. The liver and kidneys were subsequently harvested and grossly examined for embolization-related changes.

Therapeutic efficacy of TACE in hepatocellular carcinoma

Different formulations were administered via the hepatic artery through carotid artery catheterization to systematically evaluate their therapeutic effects on the rat orthotopic hepatocellular carcinoma model. N1S1 orthotopic HCC-bearing rats were randomly divided into five groups (n = 4): control (normal saline), ATO (5 mg/kg), CHSA, NAT, and CHSA/NAT groups. All groups received a 1 mL infusion of the respective formulation via the hepatic artery. Tumor progression was regularly monitored postoperatively using MRI (uMR 9.4T, United Imaging Life Science Instrument, China). In addition, body weights of the rats were recorded every two days. At the 14-day endpoint, the animals were euthanized. The tumors were then excised, and their size and weight were measured to calculate the tumor inhibition rate. Tumor tissues were fixed, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned for histopathological evaluation. The sections were subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining for morphological observation and TUNEL staining for apoptosis detection. Quantitative analysis of the staining results was performed using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software.

Survival study and safety evaluation

A survival study was conducted to evaluate the long-term therapeutic efficacy. Tumor-bearing rats were treated with different formulations (normal saline, ATO, CHSA, NAT, and CHSA/NAT). The physiological condition of the rats was monitored. Meanwhile, the survival time of the tumor-bearing rats following treatment with the different formulations was recorded, and Kaplan-Meier survival curves were plotted for statistical comparison among groups. Systemic safety was further assessed by serum biochemistry and histopathological analyses (Supplementary Materials).

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical comparisons between groups were performed using one-way or two-way ANOVA followed by appropriate post-hoc tests. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Optimization and characterization of the self-developing thermosensitive polymer

2,3,5-Triiodobenzoic acid (TIBA), which possesses a high iodine density, was selected as the core motif to impart intrinsic radiopacity. Its inherent hydrophobicity, however, necessitates chemical modification for practical biomedical use, a principle exemplified by the clinical success of non-ionic, hydrophilic analogs such as iohexol. To achieve this requisite hydrophilicity and polymerizability, a pegylation strategy was employed. TIBA was conjugated with allyl poly (ethylene glycol) (APEG) via esterification. This reaction yielded the water-soluble and polymerizable derivative APEG-TIBA (abbreviated as AT), thereby transforming the hydrophobic iodine-rich unit into a functional monomer suitable for subsequent copolymerization. Subsequently, AT and N-isopropylacrylamide (NIPA) were copolymerized through emulsion polymerization, yielding the intelligent polymer NAT, which integrates temperature responsiveness with inherent radiopacity (Fig. 1A and B).

Optimization and characterization of NAT. (A) Schematic diagram and (B) synthetic route of NAT preparation. (C) Phase transition behavior at different NIPA: MBA molar ratios. (D) Standard curve of TIBA. (E) FT-IR spectra of APEG, TIBA, AT, NIPA, and NAT. Two-dimensional contour plot of phase transition temperature. (F) ¹H NMR spectrum of AT and (G) NAT

The composition of the thermoresponsive copolymer NAT, specifically the molar feed ratio of the hydrophilic iodinated monomer AT to NIPA and the crosslinker N, N’-methylenebisacrylamide (MBA) to NIPA, critically determines its physicochemical properties. The lower critical solution temperature (LCST) of poly-(NIPA) homopolymer is approximately 32 °C. Incorporation of the hydrophilic AT segment systematically elevated the LCST of the resulting copolymer to 33.5 °C, 35 °C, and 36 °C for NIPA: AT molar ratios of 20:1, 10:1, and 5:1, respectively. This effect is attributed to the enhanced overall hydrophilicity imparted by the PEG chains of AT, which raises the LCST and thereby attenuates the phase transition degree at a given temperature. These results indicate a direct trade-off: while a higher AT content is essential for greater radiopacity, it concurrently increases the LCST. The volumetric change during the phase transition of the polymers demonstrated a non-monotonic dependence on the ratio of the crosslinker MBA, exhibiting an initial increase followed by a decrease (Fig. 1C). It is plausible that this trend arises from distinct structural and dynamic regimes of the polymer network at different crosslinking densities. At moderate crosslinking (16:1), the network is sufficiently cohesive to enable cooperative chain collapse during dehydration, maximizing volume change. However, at excessively high MBA concentrations (8:1), the resulting densely crosslinked network severely restricts segmental mobility, kinetically hindering large-scale collapse and thus diminishing the transition amplitude. Based on a comprehensive evaluation balancing radiopacity for imaging and phase transition properties for thermal responsiveness, the optimal formulation was determined to be a NIPA: AT molar ratio of 5:1 combined with a NIPA: MBA molar ratio of 16:1.

The modification degree of the developing agent in the polymer prepared from the optimal formulation was determined by UV spectroscopy. Based on the characteristic absorption of TIBA at 330 nm (Figure S1) and its good linear relationship in the concentration range of 0.156–0.625 mg/mL (Fig. 1D), the TIBA modification degrees in AT and NAT were calculated to be 22.74% and 4.19%, respectively, confirming the successful incorporation of the developing unit into the polymer backbone. FTIR analysis (Fig. 1E) showed that the C = O stretching vibration peak of the TIBA carboxyl group at 1720 cm− 1 in AT shifted to 1600 cm− 1 and 1400 cm− 1 (symmetric stretching vibration of -COO−), confirming the formation of an ester bond. The NAT spectrum simultaneously exhibited characteristic absorption peaks at 2890 cm− 1 (methyl/methylene), 1640 cm− 1 (amide I band), and 1580 cm− 1 (aromatic ring), and the miscellaneous peaks for carbon-carbon double bonds in the 1500–1700 cm− 1 region disappeared, indicating complete polymerization. NMR spectra further verified the chemical structures of the materials. Compared to APEG (Figure S2A) and TIBA (Figure S2B), the characteristic peaks at δ 13.8 ppm (-COOH) and 2.8 ppm (-OH) disappeared in the AT spectrum (Fig. 1F), while the signals corresponding to the APEG double-bond hydrogens and TIBA aromatic hydrogens were retained, indicating the successful esterification reaction. In the NAT spectrum (Fig. 1G), the terminal methyl group of NIPA (Figure S2C) and the characteristic peaks of AT remained, while the double-bond hydrogen signals in the δ 5.0-6.5 ppm range completely disappeared, proving copolymer formation via free radical polymerization.

Preparation and characterization of ATO nanomicelles

To address the limitations of ATO in hepatocellular carcinoma treatment, such as high systemic toxicity and poor targeting efficacy, a GSH-responsive nanocarrier system was designed based on the tumor microenvironment characterized by high glutathione (GSH) levels and CD44 receptor overexpression. ATO was covalently grafted onto thiolated hyaluronic acid (HA-SH) via arsenic-sulfur bonds, followed by electrostatic self-assembly with chitosan (CS) to construct drug-loaded nanomicelles (CHSA) (Fig. 2A, B). 1H NMR spectroscopy (Fig. 2C) revealed characteristic peaks of the thiolating agent MEA at δ 2.4–2.7 ppm in HA-SH compared to native HA, confirming successful thiol modification. EDS analysis (Fig. 2D) further demonstrated uniform sulfur distribution, providing strong evidence for the successful synthesis of HA-SH.

(E) Zeta potential values of individual components and final product. (F) TEM image and (G) Particle size distribution of CHSA, data are expressed as mean ± SD (n = 3). (H) Visual appearance and (I) size changes of CHSA during storage

Thiol quantification via Ellman’s assay (Figure S3) indicated a total thiol substitution degree of 46.1% ± 3.1% in HA-SH. The substitution degree of arsenic (As) on CHSA was determined to be approximately 3.5% by ICP-MS analysis, confirming the successful loading of ATO onto HA via arsenic-sulfur bonds (Figure S4).

Zeta potential analysis (Fig. 2E) revealed a positive surface charge of 28.5 mV for CHSA, in contrast to the negative charges of HA (-28.3 mV) and HAS (-15.1 mV). This charge reversal confirms the successful formation of a CS coating on the nanomicelles. Furthermore, the resultant positive charge of CHSA facilitates its subsequent electrostatic complexation with the negatively charged thermosensitive polymer NAT. TEM imaging (Fig. 2F) and dynamic light scattering (Fig. 2G) showed that CHSA formed uniformly dispersed spherical nanoparticles with a hydrodynamic diameter of 249.72 ± 3.51 nm and a PDI of 0.107 ± 0.017. The stability evaluation demonstrated that after storage at 4 °C for 14 days, the CHSA nanosuspension showed no significant changes in visual appearance, particle size or PDI (Fig. 2H, I). These observations indicate good homogeneity and stability of the CHSA formulation. The results collectively confirm that the prepared nanoparticles successfully encapsulated As and possessed excellent storage stability.

Preparation and characterization of the composite embolic agent

The ATO-loaded nanomicelles (CHSA) were integrated with the self-developing thermosensitive polymer (NAT) via electrostatic interaction, successfully constructing a composite embolic system (CHSA/NAT) with controlled drug release capability (Fig. 3A). SEM observation showed that NAT prepared from the optimal formulation formed a typical porous three-dimensional network structure after phase transition, consistent with the morphological characteristics of thermosensitive hydrogels, which established the structural foundation for its embolization performance and drug-loading capacity.

Preparation and characterization of CHSA/NAT. (A) Schematic diagram of CHSA/NAT synthesis. (B) In vitro phase transition behavior and catheter delivery/embolization performance of NAT and CHSA/NAT. (C) Rheological characterization: Oscillatory time sweep, Strain amplitude sweep, Shear viscosity, Frequency sweep. (D) In vitro release profiles of CHSA and CHSA/NAT

At room temperature (25 °C), the system existed as a translucent sol with excellent fluidity and injectability, allowing smooth administration through a syringe. Upon heating to 37 °C, the system underwent phase transition within 45 ± 5 s, during which water molecules were expelled from the gel network, leading to contraction and formation of a solid gel (Fig. 3B). In vitro embolization experiments demonstrated that CHSA/NAT remained in the sol state during catheter delivery and rapidly transformed into a stable embolic mass upon entering the simulated vascular environment. Notably, the incorporation of CHSA did not compromise the thermosensitive embolization properties of the hydrogel, confirming the clinical potential of the composite system.

Rheological tests comprehensively characterized the gel properties of CHSA/NAT (Fig. 3C). Time sweep measurements showed that the storage modulus (G′) consistently exceeded the loss modulus (G″) after phase transition, indicating a typical gel state. Frequency sweep analysis confirmed that G′ remained higher than G″ within the 0.01–10 Hz range, demonstrating stable gel structure. Strain sweep results revealed that the material-maintained gel characteristics at low strains but underwent gel-sol transition at high strains, consistent with typical hydrogel mechanical behavior. Moreover, shear viscosity tests indicated significant shear-thinning behavior, ensuring favorable injectability during administration while forming stable emboli in vivo.

To evaluate the GSH-responsive release behavior of the nanocarriers, the release profiles of ATO from both CHSA nanomicelles alone and the CHSA/NAT integrated system under varying GSH concentrations (0 µM, 20 µM, 1 mM, and 10 mM) were evaluated. As shown in Fig. 3D, minimal ATO release was observed for both formulations at 0 µM and 20 µM GSH. In contrast, release was markedly accelerated at 1 mM GSH, with the fastest release rate achieved at 10 mM GSH. It is well established that GSH plays a critical role in maintaining redox homeostasis, with intracellular GSH levels in tumor cells significantly elevated compared to normal cells [38]. Specifically, the GSH concentration in the extracellular matrix of normal tissues and body fluids ranges from 2 to 20 µM, while intracellular GSH levels can reach 2–10 mM, with typical values of approximately 1–2 mM in most cells [39]. Collectively, these findings confirm that ATO release from CHSA-based formulations is specifically triggered by elevated GSH levels via cleavage of As–S bonds.

Biocompatibility of embolic and carrier materials

The cytotoxicity of the hydrogel embolic system on different hepatocellular carcinoma cells was investigated using the MTT assay. Figure 4A and B show the cell viability of HepG2, PLC/PRF/5, and N1S1 cells after 24 h and 48 h of incubation with various concentrations of the materials. The three cell lines exhibited similar trends: cell viability slightly decreased with increasing NAT concentration and prolonged incubation time, which may be attributed to the presence of TIBA. However, cell viability remained above 80% even at the highest concentration, indicating no significant cytotoxicity within the tested dose range. These results collectively demonstrate that neither the embolic material nor the carrier material exhibits significant toxic or inhibitory effects on cells, confirming their safety for the intended applications.

As shown in Fig. 4C, compared to the positive control (Water), the supernatant of the negative control (PBS, pH 7.4) and experimental groups remained clear and transparent, visually indicating no erythrocyte rupture. Full-wavelength UV scanning of the supernatant (Fig. 4D) revealed strong absorption at 541 nm and 575 nm only in the positive control, while no absorption was detected in the negative control or embolic system groups. Microscopic observation (Fig. 4E) confirmed that erythrocytes in the positive control group were ruptured, whereas those treated with NAT maintained intact morphology without shrinkage or rupture, similar to the negative control. These results demonstrate that the embolic system possesses favorable hemocompatibility and is unlikely to cause significant hemolysis after arterial administration and embolization.

In Vitro targeting efficiency and antitumor activity of the composite embolic system

In conventional TACE, a key limitation is the non-targeted distribution and insufficient local concentration of chemotherapeutic agents within the tumor, which ultimately compromises therapeutic efficacy. To address this, we developed a targeted nanodelivery system by loading ATO into HA-modified nanomicelles. The active targeting capability of this system, conferred by affinity of HA for CD44 receptors overexpressed on hepatocellular carcinoma cells [40–42], was subsequently evaluated across three liver cancer cell lines (HepG2, PLC/PRF/5, and N1S1 As shown in Fig. 5A, compared to the weak fluorescence signal in the free FITC group, F‑CHSA demonstrated significantly enhanced fluorescence under both normoxic and hypoxic conditions in three hepatocellular carcinoma cell models, confirming that the nanomicelles achieve effective active targeting delivery through specific binding between HA and overexpressed CD44 receptors on tumor cells. This receptor‑mediated uptake was further validated by HA competition assays, in which pre‑incubation with excess free HA markedly reduced the fluorescence intensity across all three cell lines (Fig. 5A and S6). Consistently, quantitative flow cytometry analysis revealed significantly higher fluorescence intensity in the F‑CHSA group, which was substantially decreased upon HA competition (Figure S7), collectively confirming the CD44‑dependent internalization of CHSA.

Cellular uptake and in vitro therapeutic effect of CHSA/NAT. (A) Representative fluorescence images of HepG2 and PLC/PRF/5 cells treated with free FITC, F‑CHSA, or HA competition (free HA pre‑incubation + F‑CHSA) under normoxic and hypoxic conditions. Nuclei were stained with DAPI (blue). Cell viability after 24 h incubation with CHSA and CHSA/NAT under normoxic conditions in (B) HepG2, (C) PLC/PRF/5, and (D) N1S1 cells. Cell viability after 24 h incubation with CHSA and CHSA/NAT under hypoxic conditions in (E) HepG2, (F) PLC/PRF/5, and (G) N1S1 cells

The intrinsic hypoxia characteristic of solid tumors is further exacerbated by blood flow blockage after TACE therapy [10]. Since the hypoxic environment may affect chemotherapy efficacy, it is important to investigate the inhibitory effects of chemotherapeutic agents under hypoxic conditions. Using the clinically chemotherapeutic agent doxorubicin (DOX) as a reference, we specifically evaluated the antitumor advantages of ATO under hypoxic conditions. Experimental data (Figure S5) revealed that ATO exhibited superior inhibitory effects over DOX on all three hepatocellular carcinoma cell lines in hypoxic environments: the IC50 for HepG2 cells was about 52.0 µg/mL, while cell viability remained above 60% even at 200 µg/mL DOX; the IC50 for PLC/PRF/5 cells significantly decreased to approximately 3.3 µg/mL, markedly lower than that for DOX (about 15.9 µg/mL); the IC50 for N1S1 cells was about 8.8 µg/mL, with much smaller variation than DOX (about 16.2 µg/mL). These results fully demonstrate the significant therapeutic advantage of ATO in the hypoxic microenvironment associated with TACE therapy.

Further evaluation of the antitumor efficacy of the drug-loaded system (Figs. 5B-G) showed that, compared to free ATO, CHSA exhibited stronger inhibitory effects on all three tumor cell lines under both normoxic and hypoxic conditions, attributable to its active targeting capability that promotes drug uptake efficiency. The CHSA/NAT group showed slightly increased IC50 values, indicating that the hydrogel matrix prolongs drug action time through sustained release. Although this characteristic reduces short-term cell inhibition rates, it contributes to achieving more prolonged therapeutic effects.

To further validate the pro-apoptotic effects, flow cytometry was performed using a TUNEL kit in p53-mutant PLC/PRF/5 cells. As shown in Figure S8, the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) results revealed that, under normoxic conditions, Dox and ATO induced comparable levels of apoptosis. In contrast, under hypoxic conditions, ATO induced significantly stronger pro-apoptotic effects compared to Dox (p < 0.01), demonstrating the superior therapeutic advantage of ATO in the hypoxic microenvironment. Notably, the CHSA group exhibited even more pronounced apoptotic induction under hypoxic conditions (p < 0.001), indicating that nanoformulation further enhances the pro-apoptotic efficacy. These findings are consistent with the MTT results and underscore the superior antitumor advantage of CHSA, particularly in the hypoxic tumor microenvironment.

In Vivo antitumor efficacy of CHSA in an ectopic xenograft model

The antitumor efficacy was evaluated in nude mice bearing subcutaneous PLC/PRF/5 xenografts (Fig. 6A). Tumor growth in the control group was rapid, all treatment groups exhibited significant growth inhibition compared to the control (Fig. 6B). Notably, the CHSA treatment group exhibited superior antitumor efficacy over free ATO and Dox, achieving a final tumor inhibition rate of 84.6%, significantly higher than the free ATO group (72.1%) and the Dox group (37.7%) (Fig. 6C). This superior in vivo efficacy is attributed to the dual-targeting design of CHSA, which integrates biological targeting (via the HA for CD44 receptor-mediated uptake) and tumor-microenvironment-triggered release (via the GSH-sensitive As-S bonds enabling tumor-specific ATO release). This combination synergistically enhances tumor-specific drug accumulation. Furthermore, body weight monitoring revealed no significant changes across groups, indicating minimal systemic toxicity and good biocompatibility of the formulations (Fig. 6D).

Antitumor Efficacy of CHSA in an Ectopic Xenograft Model. (A) Schematic diagram of the anti-tumor therapeutic effect of CHSA in tumor-bearing mice. (B) Tumor growth curves of individual mice after different treatments. (C) Tumor weight, representative images, and tumor volume of mice after different treatments. (D) Body weight variations of mice in different treatment groups. (E, F) Expression levels of p53, Bcl, and Bax in tumor tissues detected by Western blot and quantitative analysis

To decipher the molecular basis for this enhanced efficacy, we analyzed the expression of key apoptosis-regulating proteins in the excised tumors. Western blot analysis revealed a treatment-specific modulation of the p53-Bcl-2/Bax axis (Fig. 6E and F and S14). In the Dox group, the expression level of the mutant p53 protein remained largely unchanged, consistent with its known mechanism of action being largely p53-independent. In contrast, both ATO and CHSA treatment led to a significant reduction in mutant p53 levels. This downregulation was markedly more pronounced in the CHSA group, where p53 expression was decreased to approximately 30% of the control level. A parallel trend was observed for the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2, which was substantially suppressed in the CHSA-treated tumors. Conversely, the expression of the pro-apoptotic effector Bax exhibited an inverse correlation, with the CHSA group showing the most robust induction. The p53-R249S mutation, which accounts for approximately 26% of reported p53 mutations in HCC [24], was selected as the genetic basis for tumor modeling in this study. Accordingly, the PLC/PRF/5 cell line harboring the R249S mutation was employed to establish the in vivo tumor model. This mutant p53 protein predominantly accumulates in the cytoplasm [43]. Although transcriptionally dysfunctional in canonical tumor-suppressive pathways, this mutant p53 not only loses its tumor-suppressive function but also acquires oncogenic properties that promote cell survival, primarily through upregulating anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 family proteins and downregulating pro-apoptotic factors such as Bax. These results suggest that ATO, particularly when delivered using HA-targeted CHSA nanomicelles, effectively counteracts the tumor-promoting effects in p53-mutant HCC models and results in superior tumor growth inhibition.

Embolization and radiopacity of the composite embolic system

Figure 7A illustrates the procedure for establishing the orthotopic hepatocellular carcinoma model in rats and performing TACE. To establish the orthotopic liver cancer model, N1S1 cells were injected into the left lobe of the liver. At 14 days post-inoculation, distinct tumor tissue was visible in the left hepatic lobe (Fig. 7B), with dissected tumor volumes reaching up to 200 cm3 (Fig. 7C), confirming successful model establishment. Isolation of the rat carotid artery is shown in Fig. 7D. Catheterization of the rat carotid artery was performed using DSA equipment, followed by TACE procedure after successful catheterization (Fig. 7E).

Embolization and radiopacity effects of CHSA/NAT. Establishment of the rat orthotopic HCC model and surgical embolization procedures: (A) Schematic diagram; (B) Surface and (C) cross-sectional views of orthotopic liver tumor; (D) Rat carotid artery; (E) Hepatic artery embolization in HCC model. (F) Embolization effects of drug-loaded self-developing thermosensitive hydrogel in rabbit ear vein, rat subcutaneous vein, and femoral vein vessels. Radiopacity results before and after (G) liver and (H) kidney artery embolization. Post-embolization anatomical view of the (I) liver and (J) kidney

The embolic performance of CHSA/NAT was systematically evaluated using different vascular models (Fig. 7F). In the rabbit ear vein model, compared to the pre-embolization state, the main trunk of the auricular vein showed no significant blood flow 14 days post-embolization. Notably, extensive necrosis was observed in the distal branches of the vessel, indicating that CHSA/NAT induced effective and durable vascular occlusion in this model. In rat models, CHSA/NAT also demonstrated robust occlusive capability. For the subcutaneous vein, the injected vessel segment remained translucent with no observable blood flow after the designated period, confirming complete occlusion. For the larger-diameter femoral vein, although this vessel is considerably wider than the subcutaneous vein, CHSA/NAT was still capable of achieving complete occlusion, as evidenced by the absence of blood flow. Collectively, these results across multiple models demonstrate that upon intravenous injection, CHSA/NAT undergoes a sol-to-gel phase transition at body temperature. This in situ gelation effectively blocks blood vessels, confirming its potential as an effective embolic agent.

The intrinsic radiopacity and embolic efficacy of CHSA/NAT were evaluated in real-time using digital subtraction angiography (DSA) in tumor-bearing rats. For hepatic artery embolization, DSA images before and after embolization are presented in Figs. 7G, respectively. Prior to the procedure, the hepatic arterial vasculature was clearly visualized using an iodinated contrast agent (iohexol). During the injection of CHSA/NAT itself, the material provided sufficient intrinsic contrast for real-time visualization without the need for additional contrast medium, confirming its excellent radiopacity. Post-embolization, blood flow was markedly absent in the distal hepatic arteries, with only residual perfusion observable near the portal vein region, indicating successful occlusion of the targeted vessels. Macroscopic examination of the explanted liver revealed significant morphological changes on the surface of the left lobe, corresponding to the embolized area (Fig. 7I).

The embolic effect on the renal artery was similarly assessed. DSA images obtained before and after the injection of CHSA/NAT confirmed effective occlusion of the targeted renal artery (Fig. 7H). Gross examination of the explanted kidneys revealed a marked difference (Fig. 7J): the embolized kidney (left) exhibited a pale gray color indicative of complete ischemia, contrasting sharply with the normal, well-perfused appearance of the contralateral non-embolized kidney (right). The immediate embolization efficacy in the hepatic artery was further evaluated by in vivo imaging at 1 h and 12 h post-embolization. As shown in Figure S9, the free Dir group exhibited rapid signal decay at the tumor site, with fluorescence almost completely diminished by 12 h. In contrast, the Dir-CHSA/NAT group showed strong and localized fluorescence in the liver tumor region at both time points, indicating successful vascular occlusion and favorable retention of the material at the target site. These results demonstrate that the material achieves rapid embolization upon injection while maintaining sustained retention and durable occlusion effects. These findings establish that CHSA/NAT possesses effective intrinsic radiopacity for procedural guidance and induces durable, complete vascular occlusion in both hepatic and renal arterial models, validating its potential as an imageable embolic agent.

In Vivo therapeutic efficacy of the composite embolic system

To evaluate the therapeutic effect of CHSA/NAT in TACE treatment for hepatocellular carcinoma, an orthotopic liver cancer model was successfully established in SD rats, with tumor growth monitored by MRI (Fig. 8A). MRI results showed that on day 14 post-treatment, the tumor volume in the CHSA/NAT group was significantly smaller than that in the control group, indicating substantial therapeutic efficacy (Fig. 8B). Further gross examination and tumor analysis (Fig. 8C–E) revealed significantly smaller tumors in all treatment groups compared to the control group at 14 days post-surgery. The tumor inhibition rates for free ATO, CHSA, NAT, and CHSA/NAT were 79.06% ± 8.67%, 88.37% ± 2.49%, 69.42% ± 11.00%, and 96.35% ± 0.92%, respectively. Compared to free ATO, CHSA significantly enhanced drug uptake by tumor cells, thereby improving antitumor efficacy. Notably, the CHSA/NAT group exhibited the most pronounced tumor suppression, suggesting that the drug-loaded hydrogel not only blocks tumor blood supply but also enhances ATO accumulation in tumor cells through GSH-responsive release and active targeting mechanisms, achieving synergistic antitumor effects via combined embolization and chemotherapy.

In vivo therapeutic efficacy of CHSA/NAT. (A) Treatment schedule. (B) MRI of tumor-bearing rats before treatment and on day 14. (C) Gross appearance, (D) tumor weight, and (E) tumor inhibition rate of excised tumors from each group after 14-day treatment. (F) H&E staining and TUNEL staining of tumor tissues from each group after 14 days of treatment

Figure 8F shows H&E staining and TUNEL staining results of tumor tissues from different groups at 14 days post-treatment. H&E staining revealed densely arranged tumor cells with intact nuclear morphology in the control group, representing typical tumor tissue characteristics. All treatment groups exhibited varying degrees of tumor tissue damage, characterized by nuclear pyknosis, fragmentation, and reduced cell density, with the most significant pathological changes observed in the CHSA/NAT group. TUNEL staining further validated therapeutic efficacy from the perspective of apoptosis. Compared to the control group, the free ATO group showed apparent fluorescence signals, indicating ATO-induced tumor cell apoptosis. The CHSA group exhibited further enhanced fluorescence intensity, demonstrating promoted intracellular ATO accumulation through GSH responsiveness and active targeting. Notably, the CHSA/NAT group displayed the strongest apoptotic fluorescence signals, indicating that the drug-loaded hydrogel combined with targeted chemotherapy maximizes tumor cell apoptosis, highlighting synergistic therapeutic advantages.

To further assess the long-term efficacy, a survival analysis was performed. The results showed that all rats in the control group died within 25 days, while the median survival in the CHSA/NAT group was extended to 43 days, further verifying the therapeutic efficacy and clinical potential of this drug-loaded hydrogel system (Figure S10). Furthermore, no significant reduction in body weight was observed in any group during the treatment period, suggesting good biocompatibility of CHSA/NAT (Figure S11). Systemic safety of the embolic material was further assessed through serum biochemistry and histopathological analysis of major organs. As shown in Figure S12 and S13, AST and ALT levels exhibited mild, transient elevations at days 3 and 7 post‑embolization, which returned to baseline by day 14, indicating reversible hepatic effects. Histological examination of the liver revealed no evidence of cholestasis, significant necrosis, or other pathological abnormalities. CREA and UREA levels remained within normal ranges throughout the observation period, suggesting preserved renal function. H&E staining of the heart, spleen, lungs, and kidneys showed no notable pathological alterations. Collectively, these findings provide robust evidence for the systemic biocompatibility and safety of CHSA/NAT.

In summary, CHSA/NAT synergistically enhances antitumor efficacy and significantly prolongs survival in tumor-bearing rats through combined vascular embolization, tumor microenvironment-responsive drug release, and targeted delivery, providing a promising novel strategy for TACE treatment of hepatocellular carcinoma.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study successfully developed an all‑in‑one theranostic platform (CHSA/NAT) based on a self‑developing thermosensitive hydrogel loaded with dual‑targeting arsenic trioxide nanomicelles, realizing an integrated “embolization–imaging–targeted chemotherapy” strategy for HCC. The platform features an optimized iodinated polymer that undergoes rapid sol–gel transition at body temperature for intelligent, image‑guided vascular occlusion, while the GSH‑sensitive HA‑based nanomicelles enable CD44‑mediated active targeting and GSH ‑triggered drug release. The system exhibited excellent biocompatibility and hemocompatibility in vitro. In PLC/PRF/5 subcutaneous HCC model, CHSA treatment achieved 84.6% tumor inhibition, significantly outperforming free ATO and doxorubicin, and mechanistically downregulated mutant p53 and Bcl‑2 while upregulating Bax. In an orthotopic rat HCC model, CHSA/NAT delivered via superselective catheterization provided clear real‑time radiographic visualization and effective embolization, resulting in a remarkable 96.35% tumor inhibition rate and a significant extension of animal survival. In summary, we present a novel, image‑guided locoregional therapeutic strategy characterized by self‑developed embolization, GSH‑sensitive/CD44- targeting, and mutant‑p53‑pathway modulation, effectively overcoming the limitations of conventional TACE and showing high clinical translation promise.
