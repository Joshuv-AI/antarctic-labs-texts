Stage-specific accumulation of characteristic aroma compounds in Lichuan black tea manufactured from ‘Echa 10’
Authors: Minlan Wang, Yan Yang, Xiaojie Dong, Yingke Ma, Qiuqin Zhao, Kun Huang, Ainong Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study systematically investigated aroma dynamics across five consecutive processing stages (fresh leaves, withering, rolling, fermentation, drying) of Lichuan black tea (LCBT), a geographically indicated specialty tea from Enshi, China. Using headspace solid-phase microextraction coupled with gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (HS-SPME-GC–MS), and multivariate statistical analysis, 81 volatile compounds were identified across all samples, with alcohols (25) and terpenoids (21) representing the two most abundant classes. Free volatiles peaked during rolling and fermentation, with stage-specific markers in fresh leaves and drying. Bound volatiles showed distinct accumulation patterns, with alcohols and aldehydes enriched at withering and rolling, ketones at rolling and drying, and acids declining continuously. Key aroma-active compounds followed stage-dependent patterns coupled with enzymatic and thermal reactions. Fermentation and drying selectively modulated volatile biosynthesis and release, ultimately shaping LCBT's signature floral and fruity aroma. The new fundamental insights into the process-aroma correlation mechanism were proposed during its manufacturing.

Introduction

Tea, one of the world's three most consumed non-alcoholic beverages (Zhai, Zhang, Granvogl, Ho, & Wan, 2022). Tea is traditionally classified into six categories based on processing technology: green, black, oolong, yellow, white, and dark tea (Peng et al., 2022). Among these, black tea is the only fully fermented type (Zheng, Li, Xiang, & Liang, 2016). It is favored by consumers for its distinctive flavor and multiple health benefits, including cardiovascular protection, anti-obesity effects, blood pressure and glucose regulation, and modulation of intestinal flora (Bhattacharya, Dasgupta, Paul, Mukhopadhyay, & Bhattacharjee, 2025; Chen et al., 2022; Ren et al., 2023).

Beyond its health benefits, the distinctive aroma of black tea is a key driver of consumer preference. Aroma characteristics are influenced by factors such as tea cultivar and processing techniques (Kang et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2025). During black tea processing, aroma compounds are particularly susceptible to oxidative catalysis by polyphenol oxidases and hydrolysis by hydrolases, which directly affect the aromatic quality of the final product (Zhang et al., 2020; Lu et al., 2024). Fresh tea leaves contain a variety of non-volatile precursors, such as glycosidically bound volatiles, that contribute little to aroma directly. Bound volatiles, primarily in the form of glycosides, are considered important aroma reservoirs in tea leaves. Their hydrolysis by endogenous β-glucosidase during processing releases free aglycones, which directly contribute to the overall aroma profile (Zhou et al., 2026). However, most previous studies have either focused exclusively on free volatiles or analyzed bound forms only in the final product, overlooking their dynamic precursor-to-product transitions throughout the entire processing chain. During processing, these precursors undergo enzymatic and thermal transformations to generate a wide array of volatile compounds. Therefore, simultaneous analysis of both free and bound volatiles is essential for a comprehensive understanding of aroma formation during black tea processing. To date, over 700 volatile compounds have been identified in tea leaves and infusions, although only a subset contributes significantly to characteristic aroma profiles (Wang et al., 2025).

Lichuan black tea (LCBT) is a premium Chinese congou black tea produced in Enshi, Hubei Province, within the high-quality tea region of the Wuling Mountain area. It is manufactured from one bud, one bud-with-one-leaf, or one bud-with-two-leaves of the local cultivar ‘Echa 10’ through sequential steps of withering, rolling, fermentation, and drying. As the predominant cultivar used for LCBT production in the Enshi region, ‘Echa 10’ is known for its high yield (accounting for over 70% of the local cultivation area) and good stress tolerance, yet its aroma formation dynamics during manufacture processing have rarely been characterized. The resulting tea is characterized by a tight, uniform appearance, a bright red liquor, a floral honey-like aroma, and a mellow, sweet taste (Qin et al., 2023; Su et al., 2025). Existing studies have mostly focused on the overall quality of LCBT or other traditional black tea cultivars. Systematic research on the dynamic changes of volatile compounds-particularly the conversion between free and bound forms-throughout the entire processing chain of ‘Echa 10’ remains limited (Su et al., 2026). However, in large-scale commercial manufacturing, the aroma quality of LCBT frequently suffers from pronounced batch-to-batch heterogeneity, which is particularly prominent in the intensity and long-term stability of its signature floral and fruity sensory characteristics. This quality inconsistency originates essentially from the empiricism of current prevailing processing protocols, in which critical core units including withering, fermentation and drying are mostly operated based on subjective artisan experience rather than objective chemical indicator monitoring. It is widely recognized that the formation and interconversion between free and bound volatiles are highly sensitive to subtle fluctuations in processing parameters, whereas the dynamic evolution pattern of full volatile profiles across the entire processing chain has still not been systematically documented for this unique tea cultivar. Furthermore, the explicit precursor-product relationships between bound volatile pools and their free aroma-active counterparts remain largely uncharacterized. Such lack of mechanistic insights into the underlying volatile formation and release pathways has become a critical bottleneck restricting the development of targeted processing optimization strategies, which are urgently required to stabilize and purposefully enhance the floral-fruity aroma quality of LCBT in real industrial production scenarios (Li et al., 2025; Qin et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024).

The present study was designed to systematically characterize the dynamic variation profiles of both free and glycosidically bound volatile fractions throughout the complete manufacturing processing chain of LCBT (cv. ‘Echa 10’), including all five core processing stages of fresh leaves, withering, rolling, fermentation, and final drying. Headspace solid-phase microextraction combined with gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (HS-SPME-GC–MS) was employed for aroma compound identification and quantification. Multivariate statistical analyses, including principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA), were performed to screen for differential volatile compounds and odor activity values (OAVs) were calculated to evaluate their aroma contribution. The outcomes of this work not only bridge the existing knowledge gap in the dynamic evolution characteristics of free and bound volatiles throughout the full LCBT manufacturing workflow, but also provide actionable theoretical guidance for industrial process parameter optimization, standardized quality control, and the subsequent development of year-identified geographical indication LCBT products with stable characteristic aroma profiles.

Materials and methods

Sample preparation

Sample preparation during tea processing

Fresh tea leaves (FL) (Camellia sinensis cv. ‘Echa 10’) were harvested at an altitude of 900 m in Wendou Town, Banqiao Township, Enshi Prefecture, Hubei Province, China, with the exact sampling location georeferenced to GPS coordinates 29.9306° N, 108.6512° E. The harvested fresh tea leaves were processed sequentially following the standard LCBT manufacturing workflow: withering (WI), rolling (RO), fermentation (FE), and drying (DR). The full detailed processing protocol is illustrated in Fig. 1. Uniform tea shoots with one bud and one adjacent leaf were manually plucked as FL raw material. FL were spread evenly on a withering trough for 16 h at a spreading thickness of 1–2 cm, under ambient temperature maintained at 16–24 °C. The pre-withered leaves were then rolled for 2 h at 18–20 °C using a standardized tea rolling machine (Model 6CR-35, rotation speed 48–52 r/min, Hunan Jiu-nong wang Electromechanical Equipment Co., Ltd., China). The rolled leaf pellets were transferred to fermentation trays and fermented for 3 h under controlled conditions of 25–30 °C, and covered with a sterile damp cloth to sustain a high-humidity microenvironment throughout the fermentation process. Finally, the fermented leaves were dried in a tea drying machine (Model JY-6CHZ-9B, rated power 15 kW, Fujian Jia-you Tea Machinery Intelligent Technology Co., Ltd., China) at a programmed temperature range of 80–120 °C for a total duration of 2 h. All collected samples were immediately freeze-dried, then sealed and stored at −18 °C in darkness until subsequent volatile compound analysis.Fig. 1The changes in the appearance of Echa 10 tea leaves during processing.Fig. 1

The changes in the appearance of Echa 10 tea leaves during processing.

Preparation of tea soup and extraction of free volatile

Tea leaves (10.00 g) were weighed into a stoppered flask, to which 200 g of boiling water was added. Following a 5-min infusion, the mixture was filtered, and the filtrate (tea liquor) was collected in a stoppered flask, cooled to ambient temperature, and stored until analysis. Thereafter, 5.00 mL of the tea liquor was placed into a 20 mL headspace vial containing 1.5000 g of NaCl and 15 μL of internal standard solution (cyclohexanone, 1.024 g/L in ethanol). A magnetic stir bar was introduced, and the vial was hermetically sealed before GC–MS analysis.

Isolation bound volatile substances

Referring to our previous work (Ma et al., 2025) and preliminary tests results. The extraction of bound volatile compounds sequentially involved adsorption, water washing, diethyl ether/pentane rinsing, and methanol washing. Following extraction, the collected eluate was hydrolyzed with cellulase (Liang et al., 2020; Wang, Ren, et al., 2025) at 38 °C for 48 h to release the bound volatile compounds prior to analysis.

In order to maintain the relative standard deviation (RSD) within 10.0%, all experiments were performed on each sample in at least triplicate under identical conditions.

Chemicals and reagent

All chemicals and reagents used in this study were of analytical grade. Pentane, dichloromethane, methanol, ethyl acetate, diethyl ether, sodium chloride, citric acid, and disodium hydrogen phosphate were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Cyclohexanone, n-alkanes (C7-C30), cellulose (50 U/mg), Amberlite XAD-2 resin, and other reference standards were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich Chemical Co., Ltd. (St. Louis, MO, USA).

Analysis and identification of volatile substances

Qualitative analysis

The volatile substances were collected and analyzed by HS-SPME-GC–MS in full scan mode in order to ensure all volatile substances could be detected, following our previous work (Feng et al., 2022; Ma et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2022) The sample was equilibrated for 40 min at 50 °C before the SPME (Supelco, Bellefonte, PA) fiber was inserted into the vial. The volatile substances were extracted by a fiber coated with divinylbenzene/Carboxen/poly-dimethylsiloxane (DVB/CAR/PDMS) (50/30 μm thickness; Supelco, Bellefonte, PA) and were extracted for 15 min at 50 °C. Volatile compounds were analyzed by GC–MS using an Agilent 7890 A GC system coupled with an Agilent 5975C mass selective detector (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Chromatographic separation was achieved on a DB-5MS fused silica capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm i.d., 0.25 μm film thickness; J&W Scientific, Folsom, CA, USA) with helium (≥99.999%) as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1.0 mL min−1. The injector temperature was 250 °C, and 1 μL of each sample was injected in spitless mode. The oven temperature program was as follows: initial temperature 40 °C held for 4 min, increased to 260 °C at 5 °C min−1, then to 280 °C at 15 °C min−1, and held for 1 min. The GC–MS transfer line temperature was 280 °C. The mass spectrometer was operated in electron ionization (EI) mode at 70 eV, with the ion source temperature of 230 °C and a quadrupole temperature of 150 °C. The detector voltage was 1753 V. Mass spectra were acquired in full-scan mode over the m/z range 20–400 at a scan rate of 1 scan s−1.

Qualitative analysis of volatile compounds was conducted by comparing the acquired mass spectra with the NIST 20 library and the Wiley Registry 12th (Wiley 275) library. The results were further validated using linear retention indices (RIs), which were calculated based on the retention times of C₇-C₃₀ n-alkanes, which were run under identical chromatographic conditions. For certain compounds, identification was achieved by analyzing reference standards.

Quantitative analysis

Semi-quantification was performed by the internal standard method with cyclohexanone (in ethanol as the internal standard. OAVs calculation

OAVs were calculated as the ratio of the mass concentration of each volatile compound to its corresponding odor threshold in water. Specifically, the OAVs for a given compound was determined using the following equation:(2)OAV=ciOTiwhere ci is the mass concentration of the compound (μg/L), and OTi is its odor threshold in water (μg/L). The OTi values were taken from the odor & flavor detection thresholds in water database (https://www.leffingwell.com/odourthre.htm), and odor descriptions were obtained from literature and flavornet (https://www.flavornet.org/).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted in triplicate, and results are expressed as mean values ± relative standard deviations (RSDs) and RSDs within 10.0%, with means rounded to two decimal places and RSDs rounded to two significant digits. Data were preliminarily organized using Microsoft Excel. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed using SPSS 23 software, followed by principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) using SIMCA 14.1 software. Graphs were generated using Origin 2024, GraphPad Prism 10.1.2, and Microsoft Excel 2019.

Results and discussion

Tea samples from five processing steps

LCBC tea samples at five processing stages (FL, WI, RO, FE, and DR) differed markedly in color and morphology shown in Fig. 1. During manufacturing, the leaf color gradually shifted from green to reddish-brown and finally to black, concomitant with a morphological change from flat to curled and twisted leaves.

Variation volatile compounds during processing

To investigate the dynamic variations of free and bound volatile compounds during the entire processing of LCBC from ‘Echa 10’, samples collected from five distinct processing stages were analyzed by HS-SPME-GC/MS as shown in Table S1. A total of 81 volatile compounds were identified and classified into eight categories based on their chemical structures: alcohols (25), aldehydes (9), ketones (7), esters (7), phenols (4), terpenoids (21), acids (3), and others (5). Notably, the quantities of these eight categories represent the combined totals of free and bound volatile compounds.

Distribution of free and bound volatile compounds during tea processing revealed by Venn diagram analysis

Venn diagram analysis was performed to identify common and stage-specific volatile compounds across the five processing stages, FL, WI, RO, FE and DR for both free and bound fractions shown in Fig. 2.Fig. 2The Venn diagrams of free (A) and bound (B) volatile compounds during LCBT processing.Fig. 2

The Venn diagrams of free (A) and bound (B) volatile compounds during LCBT processing.

To free volatile compounds, the distribution of compounds categories at the five stages are presented in Fig. 2(A). Forty-four free volatile compounds were common to all five processing stages, demonstrating their stability throughout processing and their role as the core of the free aroma profile. Few stage-specific free volatiles were observed: one in fresh leaves, three in dried leaves, and none in withered, rolled, or fermented leaves. Cosmene, a conjugated polyene monoterpene that has been identified exclusively in FL, serves as a characteristic volatile marker for unprocessed fresh tea materials. Owing to the consecutive conjugated double bonds in its molecular structure, cosmene exhibits high chemical reactivity. It is hypothesized that once fresh tea shoots are plucked and detached from the mother plant, cosmene is rapidly depleted during the early stage of withering. As tea processing proceeds, cosmene is continuously consumed without significant replenishment from endogenous biosynthetic pathways, and is ultimately completely eliminated by the end of the withering period. This suggests that cosmene is a primary volatile metabolite endogenously synthesized in living tea tissues. The exclusive appearance of (E)-3-hexen-1-ol, dehydro-linalool, and α-terpineol only after the drying stage can also be fully rationalized from a mechanistic perspective. The formation of (E)-3-hexen-1-ol is attributed to heat-induced, putatively non-enzymatic release from its bound precursors under the acidic, high-temperature conditions during drying, which is consistent with findings published in previous black tea processing research (Zheng et al., 2016).

Dehydro-linalool is confirmed as a thermal dehydration product of linalool, the dominant monoterpene alcohol that accumulates in earlier processing stages, and this conversion is greatly promoted by the elevated temperatures applied in the drying step. Finally, α-terpineol is most likely generated via acid-catalyzed hydration and rearrangement of terpene hydrocarbons, or through further conversion of other terpene alcohols under the same thermal conditions. Notably, although the total number of quantified free volatiles increased significantly from 49 (sampled immediately after withering) to 57 (sampled after rolling), no new stage-specific volatile compounds were detected in this transition, indicating that the observed increase in volatile count derives from compounds that are already shared across adjacent processing stages, rather than from de novo generation of entirely unique metabolites.

Venn diagram analysis of bound volatile compounds (Fig. 2B) identified 31 compounds shared across all five processing stages, constituting a stable core of bound aroma precursors. Stage-specific compounds comprised three detected exclusively in FL (germacrene D, (E)-β-farnesene, and (+)-δ-cadinene) and two restricted to dried leaves (β-pinene and 2,6-dimethylnaphthalene), with none observed in WI, RO, or FE leaves. All stage-specific compounds were present solely in bound forms. The three fresh-leaf-specific compounds are presumably endogenous bound volatiles that undergo enzymatic hydrolysis or thermal conversion during the initial processing stages. In contrast, the two drying-specific compounds likely represent heat-induced bound products generated via thermal rearrangement of existing precursors or de novo glycoside formation during drying, both of which require elevated temperatures (Ma et al., 2024).

These findings demonstrate that alterations in volatile compound profiles during processing are manifested not only in total species counts but also in the dynamic interplay between core and stage-specific components. While free volatiles are predominantly composed of common constituents with minimal stage-specific contributions, bound volatiles display pronounced stage specificity during withering, fermentation, and drying, pointing to distinct transformation and generation mechanisms among different aroma precursor classes.

Dynamic changes of free and bound volatile components during tea manufacture processing

The relative content percentage distribution and total concentration dynamics of volatile compounds classified into alcohols, aldehydes, ketones, esters, phenolics, terpenes, organic acids, and other minor categories across all manufacture processing stages of LCBT are visualized in Fig. 3. The proportional distribution of relative content for each chemical class is specifically illustrated in Fig. 3(A), from which the two key observations below can be drawn: (1) Alcohols and aldehydes share highly consistent enrichment profiles across the entire processing workflow. Their free forms predominantly accumulate in the RO and FE stages, where these two stages together account for 50% of total detected alcohols and 59% of total detected aldehydes, respectively. This observation strongly indicates that the physical tissue damage and triggered enzymatic oxidation occurring during withering and rolling substantially drive the massive accumulation of free alcohols and aldehydes in processed tea. (2) Ketones show a unique late-stage enrichment pattern during processing. The bound ketones sampled at the RO-b and DR-b stages jointly account for more than 60% of the total bound ketone pool, demonstrating that ketone formation and massive accumulation occur primarily after the rolling step. This trend is most plausibly explained by oxidative degradation of polyunsaturated fatty acids and a series of heat-induced reactions that take place in later processing stages (Ren et al., 2023; Shahidi & Hossain, 2022). (3) Free-form organic acids occupy a notably high proportion of the total free volatile pool. The maximum relative abundance of free acids is detected in FL-f (52%), followed by FE-f (20%) and WI-f (18%). The continuous decreasing trend of the relative proportion of organic acids across successive processing stages clearly demonstrates that these acids are primarily endogenous components synthesized in fresh tea leaves, and are subsequently progressively diluted or transformed along the downstream processing chain. (4) Phenolics and terpenes are mainly contributed by WI-f and RO-f. WI-f and RO-f contribute 50% of total phenolics and 47% of total terpenes. This confirms that the withering and rolling stages are critical regulatory nodes for the transformation and release of bound aroma precursors that generate phenolic and terpene volatiles (Chen et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2025). (5) The proportion of esters and others are also observed. Esters mainly originate from WI-b (19%) and RO-f (16%), reflecting the activity of esterification reactions during the middle processing stages. As clearly illustrated in Fig. 3(A), different chemical classes of volatile compounds exhibit distinct stage-dependent distribution patterns across the full manufacturing workflow of LCBT. Alcohols and aldehydes are predominantly released in the early processing stages, i.e., WI and RO, whereas ketones are markedly enriched at the late processing stage of DR. Free organic acids are primarily endogenous metabolites originating from fresh tea leaves, while phenolic volatiles and terpenoids show their highest contribution during the fermentation stage. Esters are preferentially detected in their bound fraction. These well-defined, non-overlapping distribution profiles collectively demonstrate that core processing operations including physical tissue disruption, enzymatic oxidation, and high-temperature thermal treatment exert divergent regulatory effects on the formation, release and biotransformation of different aroma components (Chen et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2024).

To intuitively visualize the dynamic accumulation profiles of volatile compounds across all manufacture processing stages, the concentrations of each chemical class were plotted as line graphs in Fig. 3(B). The sub-figure layout follows a structured grouping rule: a1 and a2 are dedicated to free volatiles, while b1 and b2 correspond to bound volatiles. Within each subset, a1 and b1 present the variation trends of the dominant volatile classes (alcohols, aldehydes, esters and terpenes), whereas a2 and b2 illustrate the dynamic changes of the minor classes (ketones, phenolics, organic acids, and the unclassified “others” fraction). With the unique exception of organic acids, the total content of free volatile compounds consistently reached its maximum value at the RO and FE stages, following a general trend of first increasing and then decreasing over processing. This overall dynamic consequently resulted in a lower total volatile content in the final finished LCBT product than that measured in the initial fresh leaf material. In comparison, the total concentration of bound volatile compounds followed a far more complex, non-monotonic variation pattern across the whole processing workflow.

1) The contents changes of alcohols, aldehydes, esters, and terpenes.

The trends of free alcohols, aldehydes, esters, and terpenes were generally consistent, as shown in Fig. 3(B, a1). The total contents of these compounds basically peaked between the RO and FE stages. Among the free compounds, alcohols and terpenes were relatively abundant. Specifically, the total contents of alcohols at the RO and FE stages were 116,124 μg/L and 114,591 μg/L, respectively, while those of terpenes at the RO and FE stages were 23,539 μg/L and 18,248 μg/L, respectively, which were significantly higher than those in the other processing stages. These observations strongly indicate that the consecutive processing operations spanning the rolling and fermentation stages establish a highly conducive biochemical and physical environment for the massive accumulation of free alcohols and terpenoids, which can be mechanistically attributed to the activated enzymatic hydrolysis of endogenous bound aroma precursors, as well as the physical disruption-triggered release of pre-synthesized volatiles from ruptured cellular compartments (Chen et al., 2022; Chen, Li, et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2019). Although the types and total amounts of free volatile compounds increased markedly in the early processing stages, the subsequent continuous enzymatic oxidation, heat-induced reactions, and volatile losses collectively led to a net decrease in total content. Consequently, the total volatile content in the finished black tea was lower than that in the fresh leaves. This observed downward trend is most plausibly attributed to the combined action of multiple mutually interactive factors, including intensive physical tissue disruption during rolling, successive enzymatic hydrolysis and oxidative degradation, as well as thermal evaporation driven by elevated temperatures during the drying stage. Nevertheless, the precise relative quantitative contribution of each individual factor cannot be dissected from the current dataset and remains to be systematically determined in subsequent investigations.

As illustrated in Fig. 3(B, b1), the concentrations of bound alcohols, aldehydes and terpenoids remained at relatively stable levels with negligible fluctuations across the FL, WI and RO stages, but underwent sharp, statistically significant alterations after the FE and DR operations. This result confirms that the fermentation and drying stages exerted far more pronounced regulatory effects on the release of these four classes of bound aroma compounds. Notably, the content of bound esters reached a distinct peak of 21,703 μg/L at the WI stage, demonstrating that the maximum biosynthesis and release of ester compounds occur specifically during WI process.

2) The contents changes of ketones, phenolics, acids, and others.

The changes in total contents of free ketones, phenolics, acids, and others are shown in Fig. 3(B, a2). The content of free acids was relatively high in FL, approximately 2959 μg/L. It decreased rapidly to about 1062 μg/L upon entering the WI stage and remained at this level through the RO and FE stages, reaching its lowest value at the DR stage. This indicates that fresh leaves contained a certain amount of these substances, but they were largely transformed or lost during the WI stage. Previous studies have shown that the activities of several key aroma-forming enzymes in black tea processing, such as β-glucosidase (β-GC), lipoxygenase (LOX), and alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH), reach their maximum values during the withering stage (Gong et al., 2022). These enzymes are not only responsible for the hydrolysis of glycosidic precursors but also deeply involved in the biosynthesis of terpenoids. The content of free acids decreased sharply after drying: low-boiling-point acids volatilized at high temperatures, while thermally unstable acids underwent pyrolysis, decomposing into small molecular compounds such as aldehydes and ketones (Saikia & Mahanta, 2002). The changes in total contents of ketones, phenolics, and other compounds followed similar trends to those of alcohols and other classes described above, but their total contents were much lower than those of alcohols.

The contents of bound ketones, phenolics, acids, and others are presented in Fig. 3(B, b2). The content of bound ketones was relatively low in fresh leaves (approximately 220 μg/L), showed an increasing trend during processing, and then decreased to 144 μg/L after drying. The contents of phenolics, acids, and others were even lower than those of ketones and remained relatively stable throughout the process.

Overall, the total content and composition of volatile compounds in LCBT showed clear stage-dependent variations during processing. The WI and RO stages were the most critical for the generation and release of volatile compounds, as evidenced by the peak total content of free compounds at these two stages, which aligns with previous studies (Li et al., 2025). The observed dynamic variations in alcohol, aldehyde, ester and ketone profiles during processing are tentatively ascribed to cascaded enzymatic and thermochemical reactions activated across the manufacturing workflow, which collectively facilitate the development of the floral, fruity and sweet aroma signature characteristic of finished black tea. Further targeted investigations integrating enzymatic activity characterization and precursor tracing experiments are therefore required to systematically elucidate the inherent mechanistic basis of the aforementioned aroma compound transformation processes.

Dynamic transformation between free and bound aroma compounds

To elucidate the dynamic interconversion between free volatiles and bound aroma precursors during tea manufacture processing, the ratio variations of seven classes of free-to-bound volatile compounds across the manufacturing stages are illustrated in Fig. 3C. The results revealed highly concordant variation patterns among alcohols, aldehydes, terpenes, and phenols. The free-to-bound ratios of these four compound classes increased progressively from the fresh leaf stage, peaked during withering, and subsequently declined through rolling, fermentation, and drying. This pattern is primarily attributed to the gradual activation of endogenous glycosidases during the slow dehydration characteristic of withering, which catalyzes the hydrolysis of bound aroma precursors, thereby converting volatile components from their bound to free forms. Upon entering the rolling stage, extensive cellular disruption facilitates the substantial volatilization and oxidative transformation of free aroma compounds, driving the continuous decrease in the free-to-bound ratio. In marked contrast to these four classes, esters exhibited a delayed accumulation pattern: their free-to-bound ratio remained consistently low from the fresh leaf through withering stages, only reaching a peak during fermentation. This delayed response arises because esters are rarely generated via the glycoside hydrolysis pathway; instead, they are predominantly synthesized through esterification reactions between alcohols and organic acids during the middle and late stages of processing. Ketones, conversely, maintained low free-to-bound ratios throughout the entire manufacturing process, exhibiting only minor fluctuations.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that withering constitutes the critical stage for the release of free terpenes, alcohols, aldehydes, and phenols, whereas fermentation predominantly governs ester biosynthesis. Throughout the complete black tea manufacturing process, distinct classes of volatile compounds exhibit independent critical generation windows, reflecting category-specific metabolic and chemical transformation mechanisms.

OPLS-DA

To further remove orthogonal systematic noise uncorrelated to inter-group metabolic variations, and to accurately pinpoint the core volatile metabolites that drive the distinct separation of LCBT samples from different processing stages, a supervised OPLS-DA model was constructed based on the full volatile metabolomic dataset covering all five key processing stages. The OPLS-DA score plot (Fig. 4B) presents the results. The model exhibited a good fit (R2(X) = 0.983, R2(Y) = 0.945) and robust predictive ability (Q2 = 0.9), with no overfitting as confirmed by 200 permutation tests. As shown in the score plot, the first predictive component (PC1, 62.4%) and the orthogonal component (PC2, 16.6%) collectively explained 79.0% of the total variance, thus fully reflecting the core differences in volatile components among samples. In total, 18 volatile compounds with a variable importance in projection (VIP) score > 1 were prioritized and identified as key differential metabolites, which act as the principal contributors to the characteristic aroma profile formation of LCBT. The subsequent unsupervised hierarchical clustering analysis further independently validated the robustness and reliability of the sample classification results obtained from the OPLS-DA model.

Cluster analysis and Heatmap

To directly visualize the relative abundance distribution of volatile compounds in free and bound metabolite fractions across the five key LCBT manufacture processing stages, a two-way hierarchical clustering heatmap encoded by continuous color gradient was generated. Prior to statistical analysis, the raw concentration dataset of all detected volatile components was subjected to log10 transformation to eliminate the interference of extreme outlier values, and to ensure that both high-abundance and low-abundance metabolites received equivalent weighting during subsequent clustering. The final output heatmap is presented in Fig. 5, where dark red color corresponds to compound concentrations above the global mean of the full dataset, with color intensity positively correlated to the actual concentration - a deeper red indicates a higher relative abundance level. The cluster analysis of FL, WI, RO, FE and DR.

The unsupervised two-way clustering embedded in the heatmap unambiguously partitioned all tested samples into two fully separated clusters corresponding to the free volatile fraction group and the bound volatile fraction group. The statistically distinct, obvious separation between the metabolite profiles of free and bound fractions provides solid evidence that fundamental compositional differences in volatile components exist between the two chemical forms. The degree of enzymatic release (or non-release) of bound aroma precursors originally stored in the bound fraction is demonstrated to exert a decisive regulatory effect on the final integrated aroma profile of processed LCBT. In both the free and bound states, across the five processing stages, FL and WI formed one subgroup, while RO and FE formed another. This suggests that the early stage of wilting is dominated by physical water loss and limited enzymatic reactions, with no substantial aroma transformation having yet occurred. In contrast, RO and FE were tightly clustered together, indicating that rolling disrupts cell structures, bringing enzymes into contact with substrates and rapidly initiating the fermentation process, such that the volatile profile of rolled samples already closely resembles that of the final fermented product.

In the free state, the wilting, rolling, and fermentation samples showed significant enrichment of several key volatile compounds, including geraniol, methyl salicylate, myrcene, linalool, (E)-linalool oxide, and (E)-2-hexenal. Most of these compounds originate from the hydrolysis of glycosidic precursors or the oxidative degradation of fatty acids. Notably, the samples after wilting and rolling were densely distributed with those after fermentation, indicating that some oxidation reactions had already commenced after wilting. Traditionally, intense oxidation is believed to occur mainly during rolling and fermentation (Aaqil et al., 2023; Abudureheman, Yu, Fang, & Zhang, 2022). However, the heatmap in this study reveals that a fermentation-like volatile profile already appears at the wilting stage. This may be due to water loss from leaves during wilting, which increases cell membrane permeability and elevates the activities of polyphenol oxidase (PPO) and peroxidase (POD), thereby initiating low-level oxidation earlier. Alternatively, some volatile compounds such as aldehydes and alcohols may already be generated via the lipoxygenase pathway during wilting.

The key aroma-active compounds

The OAVs of individual volatile compounds were calculated according to Eq. (2) and are summarized in Table 1. Among the 18 differential metabolites identified by OPLS-DA with VIP scores exceeding 1, only five exhibited OAVs greater than 1 and were consequently designated as key aroma-active compounds namely cedrol, benzaldehyde, phenylacetaldehyde, decanal, and β-damascenone. The remaining 13 compounds, despite their statistical significance in sample discrimination, demonstrated limited sensory contributions. This discrepancy can be attributed to the following three factors. First, certain compounds (e.g., benzyl alcohol, α-terpineol, and (Z)-3-hexen-1-ol) exhibit relatively high odor thresholds in water, rendering their actual concentrations insufficient to elicit olfactory perception despite their statistical significance (VIP > 1) (Wu et al., 2016; Yuan, He, Qian, Zheng, & Qian, 2016; Zhu et al., 2018). Second, some hydrophobic compounds (e.g., 2,4-di-tert-butylphenol and dibutyl phthalate) are susceptible to matrix binding by tea polyphenols through hydrophobic interactions, thereby diminishing their headspace volatility and perceptual efficacy. Third, for several compounds (e.g., cadalene, α-muurolol, edulane, and (Z)-citral), odor threshold values were unavailable in the literature, precluding OAVs calculation. Collectively, these findings underscore a critical distinction: a VIP score > 1 reflects statistically significant intergroup differences, whereas an OAVs >1 indicates sensory relevance. The former functions as a chemometric screening tool, while the latter serves as a sensory-oriented prioritization criterion (Castro-Vázquez, Díaz-Maroto, & Pérez-Coello, 2007; Javed et al., 2018; Noguerol-Pato, González-Álvarez, González-Barreiro, Cancho-Grande, & Simal-Gándara, 2012; Tronchoni et al., 2018). The changes in the content of these compounds during different manufacture processing stages and their contribution to the aroma may be important factors in the formation and evolution of the characteristic aroma of LCBT. Among the five selected key aroma substances, β-damascenone has an extremely low aroma threshold (approximately 0.002 μg/L) and contributes significantly to the floral, fruity, and sweet aromas of the finished tea (Wu et al., 2016). Benzaldehyde presents sweet, fruity, toasted, almond, aromatic, and caramel notes, while phenylacetaldehyde presents floral and rose notes (Javed et al., 2018). Both are generated in large quantities via the amino acid degradation pathway during the withering and rolling stages (Shi et al., 2019; Zhao et al., 2020). Decanal, as a fatty aldehyde, presents sweet, citrus, and green notes and has a greater influence on the aroma of fresh leaves and fermented tea samples (Javed et al., 2018) Cedrol imparts a woody, cooling sensation that forms a complementary and balanced aroma, together contributing to the complex and harmonious aroma profile of LCBT (Chen et al., 2015). During the drying stage, its content decreases due to thermal effects. The combined action of these five compounds jointly shapes the unique aroma quality of LCBT.

It should be noted that the odor thresholds (OTs) used for OAVs calculation in the present study were retrieved from publicly available aqueous solution databases, rather than being experimentally determined in the real complex LCBT beverage matrix. Abundant endogenous components in tea infusion including tea polyphenols and free amino acids are capable of binding volatile aroma molecules through hydrogen bonding interactions, which would elevate their actual in-mouth perception threshold and inevitably lead to moderate overestimation of the OAVs values reported in this work. Nevertheless, as all samples underwent identical processing, relative OAVs rankings and comparative trends remain valid for differential metabolite screening. Furthermore, the additive OAVs model cannot account for synergistic or antagonistic interactions among co-existing aroma compounds. Therefore, OAVs serves as a preliminary screening tool, and sensory recombination studies are warranted to confirm the actual contributions of these compounds to the overall aroma profile of LCBT.

Aroma characteristics of LCBT

To visually compare aroma profiles across LCBT processing stages, six aroma categories were established based on volatile compound OAVs and their sensory descriptors including sweet, woody, fruity, green, floral, and chemical. OAVs of all compounds with values ≥1 were log-transformed to generate radar plots (Fig. 6A and B). Marked differences were observed among samples across the six categories. FL samples exhibited the lowest free fraction scores across all categories, whereas FE samples showed elevated scores in both free and bound fractions. By contrast, DR samples in the free fraction displayed reduced scores across all categories, with particularly pronounced decreases in green and chemical notes, suggesting that drying substantially modulates tea aroma development. Potential change mechanisms of key aroma compounds

The proposed biosynthetic pathways of the key aroma compounds identified in this study were inferred from literature reports (Yang, Zheng, Yu, & Sun, 2019; Zhou, He, & Zhu, 2023) and the stage-specific volatile profiles observed across the five processing stages (Fig. 7). These pathways are hypothesized from the detected compounds and their accumulation patterns; however, direct enzymatic or transcriptomic evidence was not obtained in this study, and further molecular validation is warranted. As illustrated in Fig. 7, tea aroma compounds are primarily generated through glycoside hydrolysis, the shikimate pathway, and the mevalonate (MVA) pathway. In Camellia sinensis, glycolysis produces phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP), a pivotal intermediate that feeds into multiple downstream biosynthetic routes, ultimately yielding diverse volatile constituents through sequential enzymatic transformations.Fig. 7The potential generation mechanisms of key aroma compounds.Fig. 7

The potential generation mechanisms of key aroma compounds.

In pathway (1), PEP enters the shikimate pathway to yield shikimic acid, which undergoes sequential enzymatic conversions to phenylalanine and cinnamic acid. Cinnamic acid then serves as the precursor for benzaldehyde (No.27) and phenylacetaldehyde (No.28), which are subsequently reduced to their corresponding alcohols, benzyl alcohol (No.4) and phenylethyl alcohol (No.10), respectively. The results showed that the levels of benzaldehyde and phenylacetaldehyde increased significantly during WI, RO, and FE stages. This increase is hypothesized to arise from activated phenylalanine metabolism and the promotional effects of polyphenol oxidation products (Lai et al., 2026; Shahidi & Hossain, 2022; Shi et al., 2019). During withering, water loss is proposed to increase cell permeability, thereby facilitating the enzymatic conversion of phenylalanine by aromatic L-amino acid decarboxylase and phenylalanine ammonia-lyase into precursors of benzaldehyde and phenylacetaldehyde; however, this mechanism awaits direct enzymatic validation.

In pathway (2), pyruvate derived from PEP enters three distinct biosynthetic routes. In route (a), pyruvate is converted to geranyl diphosphate (GPP). GPP is hydrolyzed to yield nerol (No. 19) and, following cyclization and subsequent hydrolysis, α-terpineol (No. 17). Enzymatic conversion of GPP further yields geraniol (No. 20) and linalool (No. 8), the latter being subsequently oxidized to linalool oxide (furanoid) (No. 7). GPP is also converted to geranylgeranyl diphosphate (GGPP), which upon enzymatic cleavage yields β-carotene. β-Carotene is subsequently degraded to β-damascenone (No. 35) and ionone (No. 40). The results confirmed that β-damascenone increased significantly during rolling and fermentation (Table 1; Fig. 4). This increase is hypothesized to result from physical disruption and altered biochemical conditions during processing, which facilitate enzymatic oxidation and non-enzymatic transformation of carotenoid precursors (Huang et al., 2009; Liu, Shen, et al., 2025). Water loss during withering is further proposed to increase cell permeability, thereby enabling carotenoid access to carotenoid cleavage dioxygenases (CCDs) to initiate the reaction; however, this mechanism awaits direct enzymatic validation. In route (b), pyruvate is oxidized to acetyl-CoA, which is subsequently converted to fatty acids and ultimately to hexanal (No. 26) and decanal (No. 30). The data showed that decanal decreased during withering (Fig. 4). This decrease is hypothesized to reflect volatilization and oxidation to decanoic acid during withering, when cell structures remain relatively intact (Wang et al., 2023). In route (c), pyruvate is converted to isopentenyl diphosphate (IPP) via the mevalonate (MVA) pathway. IPP is subsequently transformed into farnesyl diphosphate (FPP), ultimately yielding sesquiterpenes. Notably, FPP is converted to cedrene (No. 68) by terpene synthases (TPSs), which is subsequently chemically transformed to cedrol (No. 23) (Zhou et al., 2024).

In LCBT, the dominant volatile substances across all processing stages are classified as alcohols, aldehydes, ketones, esters, terpenoids and phenolic derivatives, whose concentrations follow distinct stage-dependent dynamic profiles in response to different manufacturing operations. The results of the present study confirm that the formation of aroma-related volatile metabolites in Camellia sinensis is driven by the synergistic combination of endogenous de novo biosynthesis and post-synthesis chemical interconversion. Meanwhile, during the high-moisture fermentation stage and subsequent high-temperature thermal processing, these core volatile components undergo extensive mutual transformation via a series of complex cascaded chemical reactions.

Conclusions

This study systematically characterized the stage-specific dynamic changes of free and bound volatile compounds from ‘Echa 10’ during LCBT manufacture processing using SPME-GC–MS. The total volatile content peaked during withering and rolling, subsequently declining through fermentation and drying to levels below those of FL. The key aroma compounds were generated through enzymatic and thermally driven reactions.

OPLS-DA effectively discriminated the five processing stages, identifying 18 compounds with VIP > 1 as key differential metabolite. Hierarchical clustering clearly separated free and bound fractions while reflecting processing-stage progression; the close clustering of rolling and fermentation samples indicates that oxidative reactions are initiated prior to formal fermentation. Five compounds including cedrol, benzaldehyde, phenylacetaldehyde, decanal, and β-damascenone were identified as key aroma-active contributors (VIP ≥ 1, p < 0.05, OAV ≥ 1). Among these, β-damascenone exerted the greatest sensory impact owing to its extremely low odor threshold. Its accumulation during rolling and fermentation was attributed to carotenoid degradation. The increases in benzaldehyde and phenylacetaldehyde during withering, rolling, and fermentation were linked to phenylalanine metabolism, whereas decanal exhibited a dynamic pattern characterized by a decrease during withering, a peak during fermentation, and a sharp decline during drying. The odor thresholds employed herein were derived from aqueous databases; future studies should utilize matrix-specific values to enhance OAV accuracy.

OAVs-based aroma category analysis revealed that fermentation samples exhibited the highest intensities of floral, fruity, and sweet notes, whereas drying substantially diminished green and chemical notes. Collectively, the five key aroma compounds define the complex and balanced aroma profile of LCBT: β-damascenone, benzaldehyde, and phenylacetaldehyde contribute floral-fruity-sweet characteristics, while cedrol imparts woody-cooling nuances and decanal adds citrus-green notes. These findings provide mechanistic insights into aroma formation during black tea processing and establish a theoretical foundation for quality control and process optimization.
