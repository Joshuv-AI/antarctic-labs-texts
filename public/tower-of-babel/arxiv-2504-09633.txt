The speed of random walks on semigroups
Guy Blachar, Be’eri Greenfeld

Abstract
. We construct, for each real number 0 ≤ α ≤ 1 0\leq\alpha\leq 1 , a random walk on a finitely generated semigroup whose speed exponent is α \alpha . We further show that the speed function of a random walk on a finitely generated semigroup can be arbitrarily slow, yet tending to infinity. These phenomena demonstrate a sharp contrast from the group-theoretic setting. On the other hand, we show that the distance of a random walk on a finitely generated semigroup from its starting position is infinitely often larger than a non-constant universal lower bound, excluding a certain degenerate case.

1.
Introduction

Random walks have proven to be essential in the study of many algebraic, geometric and probabilistic properties of finitely generated groups, such as amenability, growth, and metric embeddings of groups
[
3
,
4
,
10
,
16
,
18
,
20
]
. A key ingredient in the study of these properties is to quantify the “drift” of the random walk from its starting point, and investigate how properties of the group as those mentioned above reflect in the asymptotic behavior of the random walk.

One such quantity which has been explored in these studies is the
speed
(also known as the
rate of escape
) of the random walk. Let
\Gamma
be a finitely generated group with a finite symmetric generating set
T
, and let
\{R_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
be a finitely supported symmetric random walk on
\Gamma
such that the support of
R_{1}
generates
\Gamma
as a semigroup. The speed function of
\{R_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
is defined as the function
n\mapsto\mathbb{E}\left|R_{n}\right|
, where
\left|\cdot\right|
denotes the word metric on
\Gamma
with respect to
S
. We further define the
speed exponent
of the random walk as
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\log_{n}\mathbb{E}\left|R_{n}\right|
.

Consider the following two test cases. If
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}
then the speed function of any symmetric random walk over it is
\asymp\sqrt{n}
(the ‘diffusive’ case), as follows from the Central Limit Theorem. In fact, Hebisch–Saloff-Coste
[
14
]
proved that for any nilpotent group,
\mathbb{E}|R_{n}|\asymp\sqrt{n}
(see
[
19
]
for certain extensions).
On the other extreme, if
\Gamma=\mathbb{F}_{d}
is a non-abelian free group then the speed function of any random walk on it is
\asymp n
(the ‘ballistic’ case). This generalizes to simple random walks on any non-amenable group. Indeed, Kesten’s theorem
[
16
]
shows that the spectral radius of such random walks is smaller than
1
, which in particular implies that the speed function grows linearly.

The following “inverse problem” thus naturally arises: Which functions can be (asymptotically) realized as speed functions of random walks on finitely generated groups? Specifically, which real numbers occur as speed exponents of random walks on finitely generated groups? These fundamental problems are attributed in
[
2
]
to Vershik (and reformulated by Peres).

The speed function of a random walk on
\Gamma
is bounded if and only if
\Gamma
is finite. Lee–Peres
[
17
]
(also attributed there to Erschler and Virág) showed that if
\Gamma
is infinite then
\mathbb{E}|R_{n}|\gtrsim\sqrt{n}
; in fact, they proved it for random walks on any amenable transitive graph. Hence the speed exponent of a random walk on any infinite group lies in the interval
[\frac{1}{2},1]
.
Erschler
[
11
,
8
,
9
]
showed that the numbers
1-2^{-i},\ i=1,2,\dots
occur as speed exponents of random walks on iterated wreath products of
\mathbb{Z}
; realized functions oscillating between two different speed exponents as speed functions; and constructed sub-linear speed functions that are arbitrarily close to linear.
Next, Amir–Virág realized any ‘sufficiently regular’ function
f
such that
n^{3/4}\lesssim f(n)\lesssim n^{1-\varepsilon}
for some
\varepsilon>0
as the speed function over a suitable finitely generated group, extending the known spectrum of speed exponents to
[\frac{3}{4},1]
; see also
[
1
]
for a joint control on the speed and entropy functions.
Finally, Brieussel–Zheng
[
6
]
realized any sufficiently regular function
\sqrt{n}\lesssim f(n)\lesssim n
as the speed function over some finitely generated group, thereby identifying the spectrum of possible speed exponents as the full interval
[\frac{1}{2},1]
. For more on random walks on discrete groups see the survey
[
20
]
.

In this paper, we focus on random walks on
semigroups
. Such random walks have been studied in
[
12
,
13
,
15
]
. Our main goal in the paper is to prove that the spectrum of speed exponents for random walks on semigroups is the full interval
[0,1]
, in a vast contrast to the group-theoretic setting:

Theorem A

(Arbitrary speed exponents)
.

Let
\alpha\in[0,1]
. Then there exists a finitely generated semigroup
S
and a simple random walk
\{R_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
on
S
such that

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\log_{n}\mathbb{E}|R_{n}|=\alpha.

We also show that, again in contrast with the group-theoretic case, there is no universal gap between constant speed functions and arbitrary increasing function; namely, there is no semigroup analog of the aforementioned result of Lee–Peres:

Theorem B

(Arbitrarily slow speed functions)
.

Let
\omega
be an arbitrarily slow function such that
\omega(n)\xrightarrow{n\rightarrow\infty}\infty
. Then there exists a finitely generated semigroup
S
and a simple random walk
\{R_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
on
S
such that

\mathbb{E}|R_{n}|\leq\omega(n)\ \ \text{for infinitely many}\ n

while
\mathbb{E}|R_{n}|\xrightarrow{n\rightarrow\infty}\infty
.

Although semigroups are more algebraically flexible than groups, there are some fundamental challenges in constructing semigroups whose speed functions are significantly different from those of groups, as we now demonstrate. First, if a semigroup
S
contains a finite one-sided ideal then the speed function is bounded (Proposition
8.1
). This eliminates many natural constructions of semigroups, including monomial semigroups and generally semigroups with zero, so
S
must be far from a pathological semigroup and, in some sense ‘closer’ to a group. However, if
S
surjects onto an infinite group then, by
[
17
]
, its speed function is
\gtrsim\sqrt{n}
. So, in some sense,
S
must also be ‘orthogonal’ to groups. Moreover, if there is a semigroup homomorphism,
S\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
with infinite image, then its speed is
\asymp n
. This observation eliminates, in particular, the use of graded semigroups.

Finally, we show that while the speed functions of random walks on semigroups are quite flexible, the rate at which such random walks escape from their starting points – namely, the distribution of the random variables
\{|R_{n}|\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
itself – cannot be completely arbitrary. This follows from a more general result on random walks on directed graphs.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a rooted directed graph, i.e., a directed graph with a root
o\in V
such that there is a path from
o
to each vertex in
G
. We define the
rooted ball spread
of
G
as the function

F_{G}(n)=\min_{v\in V}\max_{w:\operatorname{dist}(v,w)\leq n}\operatorname{dist}(o,w).

When
G
is undirected (i.e.,
E
is symmetric), then
F_{G}(n)\geq\frac{n}{2}
(see Remark
7.3
). If
G
has a finite strongly connected component, then
F_{G}
is bounded (Proposition
7.4
).
We assume that all the outdegrees in
G
are finite. If the outdegrees of vertices in
G
are uniformly bounded then
F_{G}(n)\gtrsim\log n
, see Corollary
7.5
.

We now consider a simple random walk
\{R_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
on
G
starting from the root
o
.

Theorem C
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a rooted directed graph without finite strongly connected components, where all outdegrees are bounded by
d\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
\{R_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
be a simple random walk on
G
. Then, almost surely,

\operatorname{dist}(o,R_{n})\geq F_{G}(\log_{d}n+\log_{d}\log_{d}n)

for infinitely many values of
n
.

For random walks on semigroups, this theorem translates to:

Corollary D
.

Let
S
be a
d
-generated semigroup with no finite right ideals. Let
\{R_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
be a simple (right) random walk on
S
. Then, almost surely,

|R_{n}|\geq F_{S}(\log_{d}n+\log_{d}\log_{d}n)

for infinitely many values of
n
.

We show that this bound is tight in Example
7.8
.

2.
Preliminaries on semigroups and random walks

2.1.
Cayley graphs

Let
S
be a finitely generated semigroup, with a finite generating set
T
. The
Cayley graph
of
S
with respect to
T
is the directed graph
\operatorname{Cay}(S,T)=(V,E)
, with vertex set
V=S
and edges
E=\left\{(a,at)\mid a\in S,t\in T\right\}
. Any Cayley graph is a rooted directed graph, with root
1
; in other words, there is a path from
1
to any
a\in S
.

While a Cayley graph
\operatorname{Cay}(S,T)
of a finitely generated semigroup
S
need not be vertex-transitive, it still carries some symmetries. Indeed, the outdegrees of all vertices is the same, and equals the number of generators
\left|T\right|
. Furthermore,
S
acts on
\operatorname{Cay}(S,T)
via multiplication from the left on the vertices. This resembles the action of a group on its Cayley graphs by translations, however here distinct vertices may be mapped to the same vertex. Note, however, that the indegrees of the vertices need not be even finite, and the Cayley graph may contain loops and double edges.

The ideal structure of
S
can also be read from its Cayley graphs. Right ideals of
S
correspond to closed subsets in
\operatorname{Cay}(S,T)
, i.e., sets of vertices with no outgoing edge from the set to its complement. Principal right ideals of
S
are the sets obtained by taking the minimal strongly connected subsets containing a given vertex. Therefore, strongly connected components of the Cayley graph are principal right ideals (while the reverse claim is not true in general). Left ideals of
S
correspond to sets of vertices which are closed under the action of
S
by translations.

2.2.
Random walks

Let
(G,o)
be a rooted directed graph which is locally finite, i.e., all outdegrees are finite. A
simple random walk
on
(G,o)
is a sequence of random variables
\left\{R_{n}\right\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
, such that
R_{0}=o
, and
R_{n}
is chosen uniformly from the neighbors of
R_{n-1}
, independently between the different steps of the random walk.

We define the
speed
of a simple random walk
R_{n}
on
(G,o)
as the function
\ell_{G}(n)=\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{dist}(o,R_{n})]
, where
\operatorname{dist}(o,v)
denotes the distance of the vertex
v\in V
from the root
o
. Note that
\operatorname{dist}(o,v)<\infty
for all
v\in V
, hence
\ell_{G}(n)<\infty
for all
n
.

Given a finitely generated semigroup
S
with a finite generating set
T
, a
simple random walk
on
S
(with respect to
T
) is a simple random walk on
\operatorname{Cay}(S,T)
. This can be rewritten as a sequence of random variables
R_{n}=X_{1}\cdots X_{n}
, where
X_{1},X_{2},\dots
are i.i.d. random variables distributed uniformly on
T
. When clear from the context, we do not emphasize the generating set. The distance can then be interpreted as

\left|a\right|\coloneqq\operatorname{dist}(1,a)=\inf\left\{k\geq 0\mid\exists t_{1},\dots,t_{k}\in T:a=t_{1}\cdots t_{k}\right\}

for all
a\in S
.

2.3.
Presentations of semigroups

Let
\Sigma=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{d}\}
be a finite alphabet. The free semigroup
\Sigma^{*}
consists of all finite strings over the alphabet
\Sigma
.
Every finitely generated semigroup can be described as a quotient of the free semigroup on some finite alphabet, modulo a congruence
1
1

1

In universal algebra, a congruence on an algebraic structure
A
is a substructure of
A\times A
that is also an equivalence relation.
, which, in practice, will be given by a set of relations of the form
u=w
where
u,w\in\Sigma^{*}
. For example, the semigroup
S=\left<x_{1},x_{2}|x_{1}^{2}=x_{1},x_{2}^{2}=x_{2},x_{1}x_{2}x_{1}=x_{2}x_{1}x_{2}\right>
is given as a quotient of the free semigroup generated by
x_{1},x_{2}
modulo the congruence generated by the relations
x_{1}^{2}=x_{1},x_{2}^{2}=x_{2},x_{1}x_{2}x_{1}=x_{2}x_{1}x_{2}
; that is, the largest semigroup generated by two elements satisfying these relations.

Endow the alphabet
\Sigma
with some total order, say,
x_{1}<\cdots<x_{d}
. This induces a (lexicographic) total order on the free semigroup
\Sigma^{*}
as follows:
u<w
if either
|u|<|w|
or if
u=u_{1}\cdots u_{n},\ w=w_{1}\cdots w_{n}
where
u_{1},\dots,u_{,}w_{1},\dots,w_{n}\in\Sigma
and for the first
i
such that
u_{i}\neq w_{i}
, we have
u_{i}<w_{i}
with respect to the order on
\Sigma
.

Given a system of relations
u_{1}=w_{1},u_{2}=w_{2},\dots
in the free semigroup
\Sigma^{*}
with
u_{1}<w_{1},u_{2}<w_{2},\dots
, consider the
reduction procedure

w_{1}\rightarrow u_{1},w_{2}\rightarrow u_{2},\dots
, which can be thought of as an algorithm replacing every element in
\Sigma^{*}
containing some
w_{i}
as a subword, say,
v=v_{0}w_{i}v_{1}
, by the same word with
w_{i}
replaced by
u_{i}
, namely,
v_{0}u_{i}v_{1}
. This algorithm is not deterministic, as we can have multiple occurrences of one or more
w_{i}
’s in
v
; some of these occurrences can even overlap. This situation is called an
ambiguity
. We say that an ambiguity is
resolvable
if the two different reductions can be further reduced to yield the same irreducible word – that is, a word that cannot be further reduced by any of the reduction rules.

Bergman’s ‘Diamond Lemma’
[
5
]
asserts that, if all ambiguities are resolvable, then the elements of the associated quotient semigroup are in one to one bijection with all irreducible words in the free semigroup. It is worth noting that Bergman’s Diamond Lemma in fact applies in a much more general setting, of reduction rules associated with defining relations of associative algebras; however, we focus here on the semigroup-theoretic version for conciseness.

For example, the defining relations of the semigroup
S

(indeed, putting
x_{1}<x_{2}
we have that
x_{1}x_{2}x_{1}<x_{2}x_{1}x_{2}
). There is an ambiguity in the word
x_{1}^{3}
, since the word
x_{1}^{2}
, which can be reduced by
r_{1}
, appears in it twice; however, both applications of
r_{1}
yield
x_{1}^{2}
, which can be further reduced to the irreducible word
x_{1}
. A less trivial ambiguity is in the word
x_{2}^{2}x_{1}x_{2}
, which can be reduced to both
x_{2}x_{1}x_{2}
(by
r_{2}
) and to
x_{2}x_{1}x_{2}x_{1}
(by
r_{3}
). However,
x_{2}x_{1}x_{2}
can be further reduced to the irreducible
x_{1}x_{2}x_{1}
by
r_{3}

x_{2}x_{1}x_{2}x_{1}

x_{1}x_{2}x_{1}^{2}

r_{3}

4.
Speed estimates

Let
R_{n}=X_{1}\cdots X_{n}
be a simple random walk on
\mathcal{S}_{\vec{m}}
with respect to the generating set
\left\{x,y\right\}
. In this section, we provide estimates for the speed
\mathbb{E}\left|R_{n}\right|
of
R_{n}
. For convenience, we write the reduced form of
R_{n}
as

(2)

R_{n}=y^{N_{0}(R_{n})}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\pi_{k}(R_{n})\right)x^{\delta(R_{n})}y^{N_{\infty}(R_{n})},

where
\pi_{k}(R_{n})=xy^{j_{k,1}}\cdots xy^{j_{k,N_{k}(R_{n})}}
is an element such that

m_{k}\leq j_{k,1},\dots,j_{k,N_{k}(R_{n})}<m_{k+1},

or
\pi_{k}(R_{n})=1
if no subword of the form
xy^{j}x
for
m_{k}\leq j<m_{k+1}
appears in the reduced form of
R_{n}
. Also,
\delta(R_{n})
is the indicator of whether there is some
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
N_{k}(R_{n})>0
. Under these notations,

(3)

\left|R_{n}\right|=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left|\pi_{k}(R_{n})\right|+N_{0}(R_{n})+N_{\infty}(R_{n})+\delta(R_{n}).

Remark 4.1
.

It will be useful to extend
R_{n}
to an infinite product, and consider
R_{\infty}=X_{1}X_{2}\cdots
as an infinite word in
x,y
. We note that the reduced form of
R_{\infty}
is almost surely a well-defined infinite word. Indeed, we may write
R_{\infty}=Y_{1}Y_{2}\cdots
, where
Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots
are i.i.d. random variables with distribution
\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}=y^{j}x)=\frac{1}{2^{j+1}}
for all
j=0,1,\dots
. The reduced form of
R_{\infty}
is then given by replacing each occurrence of
x^{2}
by
x
, and removing
Y_{t}
from the product if there appears
Y_{i}
which absorbs it. Since almost surely the length of
Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots
will not be bounded, the prefixes of the infinite word
Y_{1}Y_{2}\cdots
after applying these reductions stabilize, and thus the reduced form of
R_{\infty}
is well-defined.

We further note that, if the reduced form of
R_{\infty}
exists, then
\pi_{k}(R_{n})
is a subword of
\pi_{k}(R_{\infty})
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\left\{0\right\}
, since they are achieved by applying the same reduction steps.

To establish an upper bound for the speed of
R_{n}
, we begin with estimating its prefix and suffix of
y
’s, i.e.,
N_{0}(R_{n})
and
N_{\infty}(R_{n})
.

Proposition 4.2
.

For
k\in\left\{0,\infty\right\}
we have
\mathbb{E}[N_{k}(R_{n})]\leq 1
.

Proof.

Indeed,
N_{0}(R_{n})
counts the number of
y
’s that appear in
R_{n}
before the first time an
x
appears in
R_{n}
. This is bounded by a geometric random variable with parameter
\frac{1}{2}
supported on
\left\{0,1,\dots\right\}
, and thus
\mathbb{E}[N_{0}(R_{n})]\leq 1
. The calculation for
N_{\infty}(R_{n})
is the same in reverse.
∎

The next two propositions consider
\mathbb{E}\left|\pi_{k}(R_{n})\right|
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Proposition
4.3
is tight for small values of
k
, when we expect
\pi_{k}(R_{n})
to appear entirely as a subword of
\pi_{k}(R_{\infty})
(and, likely, both will be equal). In this case we estimate
\mathbb{E}\left|\pi_{k}(R_{n})\right|
by estimating
\mathbb{E}\left|\pi_{k}(R_{\infty})\right|
. On the contrary, Proposition
4.4
is suitable for large values of
k
, when there is a high probability that no word of the form
xy^{j}x
for
m_{k}\leq j<m_{k+1}
appears in
R_{n}
.

Proposition 4.3
.

For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
we have
\mathbb{E}\left|\pi_{k}(R_{\infty})\right|\leq(m_{k}+2)2^{m_{k+1}-m_{k}}
.

Proof.

Write
R_{\infty}=Y_{1}Y_{2}\cdots
as in Remark
4.1
, and define the random variable
\tau=\inf\left\{i\geq 1\mid\left|Y_{i}\right|\geq m_{k+1}+1\right\}
. Then

\left|\pi_{k}(R_{\infty})\right|=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau-1}\left|Y_{i}\right|\boldsymbol{1}_{\left\{\left|Y_{i}\right|\geq m_{k}+1\right\}}.

Since
Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots
are independent and
\mathbb{P}(\left|Y_{i}\right|\geq m_{k+1}+1)=2^{-m_{k+1}}
for all
i
, the random variable
\tau
is a geometric random variable with parameter
2^{-m_{k+1}}
. Furthermore, note that
\left\{\tau=t\right\}=\bigcap_{i=1}^{t-1}\left\{\left|Y_{i}\right|\leq m_{k+1}\right\}\cap\left\{\left|Y_{t}\right|\geq m_{k+1}+1\right\}
, so the random variables
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{m}
are independent and identically distributed also after conditioning by
\left\{\tau=m\right\}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left|\pi_{k}(R_{\infty})\right|

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{\tau-1}\left|Y_{i}\right|\boldsymbol{1}_{\left\{\left|Y_{i}\right|\geq m_{k}+1\right\}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}\left|Y_{i}\right|\boldsymbol{1}_{\left\{\left|Y_{i}\right|\geq m_{k}+1\right\}}\mid\tau=t\right]\mathbb{P}(\tau=t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}(t-1)\mathbb{E}\left[\left|Y_{1}\right|\boldsymbol{1}_{\left\{\left|Y_{1}\right|\geq m_{k}+1\right\}}\mid\left|Y_{1}\right|\leq m_{k+1}\right]\mathbb{P}(\tau=t)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left|Y_{1}\right|\boldsymbol{1}_{\left\{\left|Y_{1}\right|\geq m_{k}+1\right\}}\mid\left|Y_{1}\right|\leq m_{k+1}\right](\mathbb{E}[\tau]-1)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left|Y_{1}\right|\mid m_{k}+1\leq\left|Y_{1}\right|\leq m_{k+1}\right]\mathbb{P}\left(\left|Y_{1}\right|\geq m_{k}+1\right)(\mathbb{E}[\tau]-1)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left|Y_{1}\right|\mid m_{k}+1\leq\left|Y_{1}\right|\leq m_{k+1}\right]\mathbb{P}\left(\left|Y_{1}\right|\geq m_{k}+1\right)(2^{m_{k+1}}-1).

Next, note that
\left|Y_{1}\right|
has a geometric distribution with parameter
\frac{1}{2}
, and thus
\mathbb{P}\left(\left|Y_{1}\right|\geq m_{k}+1\right)=2^{-m_{k}}
and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|Y_{1}\right|\mid m_{k}+1\leq\left|Y_{1}\right|\leq m_{k+1}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=m_{k}+1}^{m_{k+1}}i\,\mathbb{P}(\left|Y_{1}\right|=i\mid m_{k}+1\leq\left|Y_{1}\right|\leq m_{k+1})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{-m_{k}}-2^{-m_{k+1}}}\sum_{i=m_{k}+1}^{m_{k+1}}i2^{-i-1}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{m_{k}+1}\sum_{i=m_{k}+1}^{\infty}i2^{-i-1}=m_{k}+2.

Combining everything, we have

\mathbb{E}\left|\pi_{k}(R_{\infty})\right|\leq(m_{k}+2)2^{m_{k+1}-m_{k}},

as required.
∎

Proposition 4.4
.

For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
we have
\mathbb{E}\left|\pi_{k}(R_{n})\right|\leq\frac{m_{k}+2}{2^{m_{k}+1}}n
.

Proof.

Consider again
R_{\infty}=X_{1}X_{2}\cdots
as an infinite word in the generators
x,y
, and
R_{n}
as its prefix consisting of the first
n
letters. For each
1\leq t\leq n
, let
W_{t}
the number of
y
’s that appear from position
t+1
until the first
x
. Then

\left|\pi_{k}(R_{n})\right|\leq\sum_{t=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{1}_{\left\{X_{t}=x\right\}}\boldsymbol{1}_{\left\{m_{k}\leq W_{t}<m_{k+1}\right\}}(W_{t}+1).

Indeed, the right hand side sums the lengths of all appearances of
xy^{j}x
for
m_{k}\leq j<m_{k+1}
in
R_{n}
before applying any reductions. Therefore

\mathbb{E}\left|\pi_{k}(R_{n})\right|\leq\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[W_{t}+1\mid X_{t}=x,m_{k}\leq W_{t}<m_{k+1}]\mathbb{P}(X_{t}=x,m_{k}\leq W_{t}<m_{k+1}).

By the Markov property of random walks,
W_{t}
is independent of
X_{t}
, and thus

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left|\pi_{k}(R_{n})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[W_{t}+1\mid m_{k}\leq W_{t}<m_{k+1}]\mathbb{P}(X_{t}=x)\mathbb{P}(m_{k}\leq W_{t}<m_{k+1})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2^{m_{k}+1}}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[W_{t}+1\mid m_{k}\leq W_{t}<m_{k+1}].

Note that
W_{t}+1
is again a geometric random variable with parameter
\frac{1}{2}
. In a similar manner to the proof of Proposition
4.3
,
\mathbb{E}[W_{t}+1\mid m_{k}\leq W_{t}<m_{k+1}]\leq m_{k}+1
for all
t
. Therefore we conclude that

\mathbb{E}\left|\pi_{k}(R_{n})\right|\leq\frac{m_{k}+2}{2^{m_{k}+1}}n.

∎

Together, we reach the following upper bound:

Corollary 4.5
.

\mathbb{E}\left|R_{n}\right|\leq\sum_{k:m_{k}\leq n-2}\frac{m_{k}+2}{2^{m_{k}}}\min\left\{2^{m_{k+1}},n\right\}+3.

Proof.

By (
3
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left|R_{n}\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left|\pi_{k}(R_{n})\right|+\mathbb{E}[N_{0}(R_{n})]+\mathbb{E}[N_{\infty}(R_{n})]+\mathbb{E}[\delta(R_{n})]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left|R_{n}\right|=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left|\pi_{k}(R_{n})\right|+3.

Note also that
N_{k}(R_{n})=0
for any
k
with
m_{k}>n-2
, since then
xy^{j}x
is a word of length
j+2>n
for any
m_{k}\leq j<m_{k+1}
, and thus cannot be a subword of
R_{n}
. It is therefore enough to prove that
\mathbb{E}\left|\pi_{k}(R_{n})\right|\leq\frac{m_{k}+2}{2^{m_{k}}}\min\left\{2^{m_{k+1}},n\right\}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
with
m_{k}\leq n-2
. Indeed, note first that
\pi_{k}(R_{n})
is a subword of
\pi_{k}(R_{\infty})
and both are reduced, hence by Proposition
4.3
we have

\mathbb{E}\left|\pi_{k}(R_{n})\right|\leq\mathbb{E}\left|\pi_{k}(R_{\infty})\right|\leq(m_{k}+2)2^{m_{k+1}-m_{k}}.

The fact that
\mathbb{E}\left|\pi_{k}(R_{n})\right|\leq\frac{m_{k}+2}{2^{m_{k}+1}}n
follows from Proposition
4.4
.
∎

For the lower bound on the speed of
R_{n}
, we focus on bounding
\mathbb{E}\left|\pi_{k}(R_{n})\right|
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
from below. We do this by estimating the number of subwords
xy^{j}x
for
m_{k}\leq j<m_{k+1}
of an appropriate prefix of
R_{n}
.

Proposition 4.6
.

For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
m_{k}\leq n-2
, we have

\mathbb{E}[N_{k}(R_{n})]\geq\frac{\min\left\{2^{m_{k+1}},n\right\}}{2^{m_{k}+3}}.

Proof.

Write
R_{n}=X_{1}\cdots X_{n}
. For any
1\leq i\leq 2^{m_{k+1}}
, let
A_{i}
denote the event that there is some
m_{k}\leq j<m_{k+1}
such that
X_{i}\cdots X_{i+j+1}=xy^{j}x
, and that
y^{m_{k+1}}
is not a subword of
X_{1}\cdots X_{i-1}
. We first claim that
N_{k}(R_{n})\geq\sum_{i=1}^{\min\left\{2^{m_{k+1}-1},n\right\}}\boldsymbol{1}_{A_{i}}
. Indeed,
N_{k}(R_{n})
counts the appearances of subwords of the form
xy^{j}x
for
m_{k}\leq j<m_{k+1}
in the reduced form of
R_{n}
, and the event
A_{i}
precisely means that such a subword starts at position
i
. Therefore

\mathbb{E}[N_{k}(R_{n})]\geq\sum_{i=1}^{\min\left\{2^{m_{k+1}-1},n\right\}}\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{1}_{A_{i}}]=\sum_{i=1}^{\min\left\{2^{m_{k+1}-1},n\right\}}\mathbb{P}(A_{i}).

Next, we write
A_{i}=B_{i}\cap C_{i}
, where
B_{i}
is the event that
xy^{m_{k+1}}
is not a subword of
X_{1}\cdots X_{i-1}
, and
C_{i}
is the event that
X_{i}\cdots X_{i+j+1}=xy^{j}x
for some
m_{k}\leq j<m_{k+1}
. Conditioned on
X_{i}=x
, the events
B_{i}
and
C_{i}
are independent. Indeed, conditioned on
X_{i}=x
, the event
B_{i}
depends only on
X_{1},\cdots,X_{i-1}
, and
C_{i}
depends only on
X_{i+1},\cdots,X_{n}
. Therefore, for any
1\leq i\leq n-m_{k}-1
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(A_{i})=\mathbb{P}(B_{i}\cap C_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{P}(B_{i}\cap C_{i}\mid X_{i}=x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{P}(B_{i}\mid X_{i}=x)\mathbb{P}(C_{i}\mid X_{i}=x).

We claim that
\mathbb{P}(B_{i}\mid X_{i}=x)\geq\frac{1}{2}
. Indeed, let
M
denote the number of appearances of
y^{m_{k+1}}
in
X_{1}\cdots X_{i-1}
. At any given position, the probability that
xy^{m_{k+1}}
starts at that position is
2^{-m_{k+1}-1}
, and thus
\mathbb{E}[M]\leq(i-1)2^{-m_{k+1}-1}\leq\frac{1}{2}
. By Markov’s inequality,
\mathbb{P}(M\geq 1\mid X_{i}=x)\leq\frac{1}{2}
, and thus
\mathbb{P}(B_{i}\mid X_{i}=x)\geq\frac{1}{2}
. Finally, note that
\mathbb{P}(C_{i}\mid X_{i}=x)\geq\mathbb{P}(X_{i+1}\cdots X_{i+m_{k}}=y^{m_{k}})\geq 2^{-m_{k}}
.

Combining all inequalities, we have

\mathbb{P}(A_{i})\geq 2^{-m_{k}-2}\,,

and thus

\mathbb{E}[N_{k}(R_{n})]\geq\sum_{i=1}^{\min\left\{2^{m_{k+1}-1},n\right\}}\mathbb{P}(A_{i})\geq\frac{\min\left\{2^{m_{k+1}},n\right\}}{2^{m_{k}+3}},

completing the proof.
∎

Combining all values of
k
, we get:

Corollary 4.7
.

\mathbb{E}\left|R_{n}\right|\geq\sum_{k:m_{k}\leq n-2}\frac{m_{k}+1}{2^{m_{k}+3}}\min\left\{2^{m_{k+1}},n\right\}.

Proof.

By (
3
), it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left|R_{n}\right|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left|\pi_{k}(R_{n})\right|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(m_{k}+1)\mathbb{E}[N_{k}(R_{n})]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{k:m_{k}\leq n-2}(m_{k}+1)\mathbb{E}[N_{k}(R_{n})],

and the claim now follows from Proposition
4.6
.
∎

Our upper and lower bounds on the speed of
R_{n}
are summarized in this corollary:

Corollary 4.8
.

We have

\sum_{k:m_{k}\leq n-2}\frac{m_{k}+1}{2^{m_{k}+3}}\min\left\{2^{m_{k+1}},n\right\}\leq\mathbb{E}\left|R_{n}\right|\leq\sum_{k:m_{k}\leq n-2}\frac{m_{k}+2}{2^{m_{k}}}\min\left\{2^{m_{k+1}},n\right\}+3.

5.
Realization of speed functions

Given a sequence
\vec{m}=(m_{1},m_{2},\dots)
(with
m_{1}=1
)
, denote:

\gamma_{\vec{m}}(n)=\sum_{k:\ m_{k+1}\leq\log_{2}n}m_{k}2^{m_{k+1}-m_{k}}.

Lemma 5.1
.

Suppose that, for some constants
\beta\geq 1
and
\delta>0
we have that
m_{i}\leq\beta m_{i-1}+\delta
for all
i>1
. Then

C_{1}\gamma_{\vec{m}}(n)\leq\mathbb{E}|R_{n}|\leq C_{2}\left(\gamma_{\vec{m}}(n)+n^{\frac{\beta-1}{\beta}}\log_{2}n\right)

for some positive constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
.

Proof.

By Corollary
4.8
,

\mathbb{E}|R_{n}|\geq\sum_{k:\,m_{k+1}\leq\log_{2}n}(m_{k}+1)2^{m_{k+1}-m_{k}-3}\geq\frac{1}{8}\gamma_{\vec{m}}(n).

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{m_{k}+2}{2^{m_{k}}}\min\left\{2^{m_{k+1}},n\right\}\leq

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k:\ m_{k+1}\leq\log_{2}n}(m_{k}+2)2^{m_{k+1}-m_{k}}+\sum_{k:\ m_{k+1}>\log_{2}n}\frac{(m_{k}+2)n}{2^{m_{k}}}

\displaystyle\leq 3\gamma_{\vec{m}}(n)+3n\sum_{k:\ m_{k+1}>\log_{2}n}\frac{m_{k}}{2^{m_{k}}}

and

\displaystyle\sum_{k:\ m_{k+1}>\log_{2}n}\frac{m_{k}}{2^{m_{k}}}\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in\mathbb{Z}:\\
r\geq\frac{1}{\beta}\log_{2}n-\frac{\delta}{\beta}\end{subarray}}\frac{r}{2^{r}}.

Fix
N>1
. Let
g(t)=\sum_{r=N}^{\infty}rt^{r}=t\left(\frac{t^{N}}{1-t}\right)^{\prime}=\frac{t^{N}}{(1-t)^{2}}(N(1-t)+t)
. Therefore
g(1/2)\leq N2^{-N-2}
, so, taking
N=\lceil\frac{1}{\beta}\log_{2}n-\frac{\delta}{\beta}\rceil
,

\displaystyle\sum_{k:\ m_{k+1}>\log_{2}n}\frac{m_{k}}{2^{m_{k}}}\leq\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\log_{2}n-\frac{\delta}{\beta}\right)2^{-\frac{1}{\beta}\log_{2}n+\frac{\delta}{\beta}}\leq Cn^{-\frac{1}{\beta}}\log_{2}n

for
C=2^{\frac{\delta}{\beta}}\cdot\frac{1}{\beta}
.
Hence:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{m_{k}+2}{2^{m_{k}}}\min\left\{2^{m_{k+1}},n\right\}\leq 3\gamma_{\vec{m}}(n)+3n\cdot Cn^{-\frac{1}{\beta}}\log_{2}n.

Collecting pieces, using Corollary
4.8
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8}\gamma_{\vec{m}}(n)\leq\mathbb{E}|R_{n}|\leq 3\gamma_{\vec{m}}(n)+3Cn^{1-\frac{1}{\beta}}\log_{2}n+3,

and the claim follow.
∎

This lemma allows us to understand the slowest rate of speed functions our construction yields:

Example 5.2
.

Take the sequence
m_{k}=k
. Then

\gamma_{\vec{m}}(n)=\sum_{k=1}^{\left\lfloor\log_{2}n\right\rfloor-1}2k=\binom{\left\lfloor\log_{2}n\right\rfloor}{2}.

We may therefore apply Lemma
5.1
with
\beta=1
and
\delta=1
, and deduce that the speed of the random walk
R_{n}
on the semigroup
\mathcal{S}_{\vec{m}}
(corresponding to the sequence
m_{k}=k
) satisfies
\mathbb{E}\left|R_{n}\right|\asymp\log^{2}n
.

5.1.
Arbitrary speed exponents

We can now prove:

Theorem 5.3

(Theorem A)
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Then there exists a finitely generated semigroup such that, for a simple random walk supported on a finite generating set of it,

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\log_{n}\mathbb{E}|R_{n}|=\alpha.

Proof.

Fix a real number
\alpha\in(0,1)
and let
\beta>1
be such that
\frac{\beta-1}{\beta}=\alpha
(namely,
\beta=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}
). For each
i\geq 1
, let
m_{i}=1+\lfloor\beta\rfloor+\cdots+\lfloor\beta^{i-1}\rfloor
.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
and let
t
be such that
\log_{2}N\in[m_{t},m_{t+1})
. Then

\gamma_{\vec{m}}(N)=\sum_{k:\ m_{k+1}\leq\log_{2}N}m_{k}2^{m_{k+1}-m_{k}}=\sum_{k=1}^{t-1}m_{k}2^{m_{k+1}-m_{k}}=\gamma_{\vec{m}}(2^{m_{t}}).

Let us focus on
n=2^{m_{t}}
for
t\gg_{\beta}0
. Then

\displaystyle\frac{\beta}{\beta-1}\cdot\beta^{t-1}-2t\leq\frac{\beta^{t}-1}{\beta-1}-t

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 1+\lfloor\beta\rfloor+\cdots+\lfloor\beta^{t-1}\rfloor

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\beta^{t}-1}{\beta-1}\leq\frac{\beta}{\beta-1}\cdot\beta^{t-1}.

Notice that

\log_{2}\log_{2}n\geq\log_{2}\lfloor\beta^{t-1}\rfloor\geq(t-1)\log_{2}\beta-1\geq ct

for some
c>0
depending only on
\beta
(recall that
t\gg_{\beta}0
). Hence

\log_{2}n=\frac{\beta}{\beta-1}\cdot\beta^{t-1}+\Delta,\ \ \ \text{where}\ \ \ \Delta\leq 2t\leq\frac{2}{c}\log_{2}\log_{2}n.

It follows that

\displaystyle\gamma_{\vec{m}}(n)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle m_{t-1}2^{m_{t}-m_{t-1}}\geq 2^{\lfloor\beta^{t-1}\rfloor}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}2^{\beta^{t-1}}\geq\frac{1}{2}2^{\frac{\beta-1}{\beta}\log_{2}n-\frac{2}{c}\log_{2}\log_{2}n}\geq n^{\frac{\beta-1}{\beta}}\cdot\log_{2}^{-\frac{3}{c}}n

(recall that
t\gg_{\beta}0
).
On the other hand,

\displaystyle\gamma_{\vec{m}}(N)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma_{\vec{m}}(2^{m_{t}})=\sum_{k=1}^{t-1}m_{k}2^{m_{k+1}-m_{k}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle m_{t-1}(2^{m_{2}-m_{1}}+\cdots+2^{m_{t}-m_{t-1}})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2m_{t-1}2^{m_{t}-m_{t-1}}\leq 2\log_{2}N\cdot 2^{\beta^{t-1}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\log_{2}N\cdot 2^{\frac{\beta-1}{\beta}m_{t}+\frac{2(\beta-1)}{\beta}t}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\log_{2}^{1+\frac{2(\beta-1)}{c\beta}}N\cdot N^{\frac{\beta-1}{\beta}}.

It follows that

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\log_{n}\gamma_{\vec{m}}(n)=\frac{\beta-1}{\beta}=\alpha.

Finally, by Lemma
5.1
,

\displaystyle\alpha=\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\log_{n}\gamma_{\vec{m}}(n)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\log_{n}\mathbb{E}|R_{n}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\max\Big\{\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\log_{n}\gamma_{\vec{m}}(n),\frac{\beta-1}{\beta}\Big\}=\alpha.

The proof is completed.
∎

6.
Arbitrarily slow speed functions

In this section, we prove Theorem B. Namely, we show that there is no gap between constant and non-constant speed functions of random walks on semigroups. To do this, we analyze the speed of random walks on a certain quotient of
\mathcal{S}_{\vec{m}}
.

The quotient we consider is the following:

Construction 6.1
.

Fix an increasing sequence
\vec{m}=\left\{m_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
of positive integers such that
m_{1}=1
. We use the notations
\prec
and
\preceq
as in Construction
3.1
. We also write
j\sim j^{\prime}
if
j\preceq j^{\prime}\preceq j
, i.e., if
m_{k}\leq j,j^{\prime}<m_{k+1}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
. We define the semigroup

\overline{\mathcal{S}}_{\vec{m}}=\left\langle x,y\mid x^{2}=x,\forall i,j\geq 1:xy^{j}xy^{j^{\prime}}x=xy^{j}x\text{ if }j^{\prime}\preceq j\right\rangle.

The difference between
\mathcal{S}_{\vec{m}}
and
\overline{\mathcal{S}}_{\vec{m}}
comes when considering the product of
xy^{j}x
with
xy^{j^{\prime}}x
when
j\sim j^{\prime}
. Indeed, in
\mathcal{S}_{\vec{m}}
, the subsemigroup
\left\langle xy^{m_{k}}x,\dots,xy^{m_{k+1}-1}x\right\rangle
is free for all
k
, whereas the same semigroup in
\overline{\mathcal{S}}_{\vec{m}}
satisfies the semigroup law
ab=a
.

We provide the following upper bound for the speed of a simple random walk on
\overline{\mathcal{S}}_{\vec{m}}
:

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
R_{n}
be a simple random walk on
\overline{\mathcal{S}}_{\vec{m}}
with respect to the generating set
\left\{x,y\right\}
. Then

c_{0}\sum_{k:m_{k}\leq\log_{2}n}(m_{k}+1)\leq\mathbb{E}\left|R_{n}\right|\leq\sum_{k:m_{k}\leq n-2}(m_{k}+2)\min\left\{1,\frac{n}{2^{m_{k}}}\right\}+3.

The proof of the proposition follows similar lines as the calculations in Section
4
. We first note that, as in Proposition
3.2
, every element
a\in\overline{\mathcal{S}}_{\vec{m}}
has a reduced form

a=y^{j_{0}}xy^{j_{1}}x\cdots y^{j_{t-1}}xy^{j_{t}},

where
j_{0},j_{t}\geq 0
,
j_{1},\dots,j_{t-1}\geq 0
, and
j_{1}\prec j_{2}\prec\cdots\prec j_{t-1}
(rather than
\preceq
in
\mathcal{S}_{\vec{m}}
). We write the reduced form of the simple random walk
R_{n}
as

(4)

R_{n}=y^{N_{0}(R_{n})}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\overline{\pi}_{k}(R_{n})\right)x^{\delta(R_{n})}y^{N_{\infty}(R_{n})},

where
\overline{\pi}_{k}(R_{n})=xy^{j}
is an element such that
m_{k}\leq j<m_{k+1}
, or
\overline{\pi}_{k}(R_{n})=1
if no subword of the form
xy^{j}x
for
m_{k}\leq j<m_{k+1}
appears in the reduced form of
R_{n}
. Also,
\delta(R_{n})
is the indicator of whether there is some
k
such that
\overline{\pi}_{k}(R_{n})\neq 1
. We have again

\left|R_{n}\right|=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left|\overline{\pi}_{k}(R_{n})\right|+N_{0}(R_{n})+N_{\infty}(R_{n})+\delta(R_{n}),

so

(5)

\mathbb{E}\left|R_{n}\right|=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left|\overline{\pi}_{k}(R_{n})\right|+\mathbb{E}[N_{0}(R_{n})]+\mathbb{E}[N_{\infty}(R_{n})]+\mathbb{E}[\delta(R_{n})].

Proposition
4.2
is the same for random walks on
\mathcal{S}_{\vec{m}}
and
\overline{\mathcal{S}}_{\vec{m}}
, so we focus on
\mathbb{E}\left|\overline{\pi}_{k}(R_{n})\right|
.

Proposition 6.3
.

For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
we have
\mathbb{E}\left|\overline{\pi}_{k}(R_{n})\right|\leq m_{k}+2
.

Proof.

Similarly to Proposition
4.3
, we may extend
R_{n}
to an infinite word
R_{\infty}
, which has a well-defined reduced form, and thus
\mathbb{E}\left|\overline{\pi}_{k}(R_{n})\right|\leq\mathbb{E}\left|\overline{\pi}_{k}(R_{\infty})\right|
. Write
R_{\infty}=Y_{1}Y_{2}\cdots
, where
Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots
are i.i.d. random variables with distribution
\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}=y^{j}x)=2^{-j-1}
, and let
\tau=\inf\left\{j\mid m_{k}<\left|Y_{j}\right|\leq m_{k}+1,\forall i<j:\left|Y_{i}\right|\leq m_{k}\right\}
(which might be infinite). Then

\left|\overline{\pi}_{k}(R_{\infty})\right|=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left|Y_{j}\right|\boldsymbol{1}_{\left\{\tau=j\right\}}.

Therefore

\mathbb{E}\left|\overline{\pi}_{k}(R_{\infty})\right|=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|Y_{j}\right|\boldsymbol{1}_{\left\{\tau=j\right\}}\right]=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|Y_{j}\right|\mid\tau=j\right]\mathbb{P}(\tau=j).

By the independence of
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{j}
,

\mathbb{E}\left|\overline{\pi}_{k}(R_{\infty})\right|=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|Y_{j}\right|\mid m_{k}+1\leq\left|Y_{j}\right|\leq m_{k+1}\right]\mathbb{P}(\tau=j).

We saw in the proof of Proposition
4.3
that
\mathbb{E}\left[\left|Y_{j}\right|\mid m_{k}+1\leq\left|Y_{j}\right|\leq m_{k+1}\right]\leq m_{k}+2
, and thus

\mathbb{E}\left|\overline{\pi}_{k}(R_{\infty})\right|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(m_{k}+2)\mathbb{P}(\tau=j)\leq m_{k}+2.

∎

Proposition 6.4
.

There exists a universal constant
c_{0}>0
such that, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
m_{k}\leq\log_{2}n
, we have
\mathbb{P}(\overline{\pi}_{k}(R_{n})\neq 1)\geq c_{0}
.

Proof.

Consider
R_{n}=X_{1}\cdots X_{n}
as a word of length
n
. Then
\overline{\pi}_{k}(R_{n})=1
if and only if no subword of the form
xy^{j}x
for
m_{k}\leq j<m_{k+1}
appears before the first appearance of
xy^{m_{k+1}}
in
R_{n}
. Write
m=\min\left\{n,2^{m_{k}}\right\}
.
By Proposition
A.1
, the probability that
xy^{m_{k}}
appears as a subword in
X_{1}\cdots X_{m}
is bounded from below by some absolute constant
c>0
. After the first appearance of
xy^{m_{k}}
, the probability that the next letter is
x
is
\frac{1}{2}
. This shows that the probability that
xy^{m_{k}}x
appears before any
xy^{j}
for
j\geq m_{k+1}
is bounded from below by
\frac{1}{2}c
, proving the proposition.
∎

We can now prove the speed estimates on
R_{n}
.

Proof of Proposition
6.2
.

Combining Proposition
6.3
and Proposition
4.4
, which is still valid since
\overline{\mathcal{S}}_{\vec{m}}
is a quotient of
\mathcal{S}_{\vec{m}}
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left|\overline{\pi}_{k}(R_{n})\right|\leq(m_{k}+2)\min\left\{1,\frac{n}{2^{m_{k}}}\right\}

for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
m_{k}\leq n-2
. The claimed upper bound now follows from (
5
).

For the lower bound, note that

\mathbb{E}\left|\overline{\pi}_{k}(R_{n})\right|\geq(m_{k}+1)\mathbb{P}(\overline{\pi}_{k}(R_{n})\neq 1)\geq c_{0}(m_{k}+1)

for all
k
with
m_{k}\leq\log_{2}n
, where the last inequality follows from Proposition
6.4
. Therefore, by (
5
),

\mathbb{E}\left|R_{n}\right|\geq\sum_{k:m_{k}\leq\log_{2}n}\mathbb{E}\left|\overline{\pi}_{k}(R_{n})\right|\geq c_{0}\sum_{k:m_{k}\leq\log_{2}n}(m_{k}+1)

as required.
∎

We return to the proof of Theorem B. The key observation is that the upper bound of Proposition
6.2
is constant on any interval of the form
[2^{m_{k}},m_{k+1}+1)
. Therefore, if the sequence
\left\{m_{k}\right\}
grows very fast, the speed of
R_{n}
will grow slowly.

Proof of Theorem B.

Let
\omega\colon\mathbb{N}\to[1,\infty)
be a function such that
\omega(n)\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}\infty
. We construct a sequence
\vec{m}=\left\{m_{k}\right\}
inductively as follows: we set
m_{1}=1
, and for each
k\geq 2
we let
m_{k}>2^{m_{k-1}}
be the first integer such that

\omega(m_{k})>\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}(m_{i}+2)+3.

Consider the random walk
R_{n}
on
\overline{\mathcal{S}}_{\vec{m}}
. By Proposition
6.2
,
\mathbb{E}\left|R_{n}\right|
is not bounded, and for all
k\geq 2
we have

\mathbb{E}\left|R_{m_{k}}\right|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}(m_{i}+2)\min\left\{1,\frac{m_{k}}{2^{m_{i}}}\right\}+3=\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}(m_{i}+2)+3<\omega(m_{k})

proving the theorem.
∎

7.
A lower bound on the distance of random walks

As another angle to study the rate of escape of random walks on semigroups, we ask how close can they be to their starting point. We show that almost surely there is a subsequence of the path of the random walk which “diverges to infinity”, provided that the semigroup does not contain finite right ideals. The main result of this section is proved for the more general case of random walks on rooted directed graphs, and we rephrase it in the language of random walks on semigroups at the end of the section.

Let
(G,o)
be a rooted directed graph, and let
R_{n}
be a simple random walk on
G
. If
G
contains a finite strongly connected component, then there is a positive probability that
R_{n}
will reach that component after finitely many steps, and then
R_{n}
will be trapped there from that time onwards. To detect such components, we introduce the following function:

Definition 7.1
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a rooted directed graph with root
o
. We define

F_{G}(v,n)=\max_{w\in B_{G}(v,n)}\operatorname{dist}(o,w)

for each
v\in V
, and the
rooted ball spread
of
G
as

F_{G}(n)=\min_{v\in V}F_{G}(n,v).

In words,
F_{G}(n)\geq r
if any ball
B_{G}(v,n)
of radius
n
in
G
contains a vertex
w
of distance at least
r
from the root
o
.

We now describe some properties of the function
F_{G}
.

Remark 7.2
.

(1)

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have
F_{G}(n)\leq F_{G}(o,n)\leq n
.

(2)

For any
v\in V
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have
F_{G}(v,n)\geq\operatorname{dist}(o,v)
.

Remark 7.3
.

If
G=(V,E)
is undirected, then
F_{G}(n)\geq\frac{n}{2}
for all
n
. Indeed, we will show that
F_{G}(v,n)\geq\frac{n}{2}
for all
v\in V
. On the one hand, if
\operatorname{dist}(o,v)\geq\frac{n}{2}
, then
F_{G}(v,n)\geq\operatorname{dist}(o,v)\geq\frac{n}{2}
. On the other hand, if
\operatorname{dist}(o,v)<\frac{n}{2}
, take
v^{\prime}\in V
such that
\operatorname{dist}(o,v^{\prime})=\left\lceil\frac{n}{2}\right\rceil
. Therefore
\operatorname{dist}(v,v^{\prime})\leq n
, and so
F_{G}(v,n)\geq\operatorname{dist}(o,v)\geq\frac{n}{2}
.

We remark that if
G
has no dead ends, then
F_{G}(n)=n
, since we can then choose the vertex
v^{\prime}
so that
\operatorname{dist}(v,v^{\prime})=\operatorname{dist}(o,v)+\operatorname{dist}(v,v^{\prime})
. For this reason, for undirected
G
we have
F_{G}(n)=n
for all
n
if and only
G
has no dead ends.

Note that this argument clearly fails for directed graphs, since if a directed graph contains a dead end, there might be no way of going back to the root. However, as mentioned above, we will now show that the function
F_{G}
detects finite strongly connected components in directed graphs:

Proposition 7.4
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a rooted directed graph with root
o
.

(1)

If
G
has a finite strongly connected component, then
F_{G}
is bounded.

(2)

If
G
has no finite strongly connected components, then
F_{G}(\left|B_{G}(o,n-1)\right|)\geq n
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

(1)

Assume that
G
has a finite strongly connected component
A
. Fix
v\in A
, and take
R>0
such that
A\subseteq B_{G}(o,R)
. Then

F_{G}(n)\leq F_{G}(v,n)\leq\max_{w\in A}\operatorname{dist}(o,w)\leq R

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, showing that
F_{G}
is bounded.

(2)

Assume that
G
has no finite strongly connected components, and take
v\in V
. We claim that
F_{G}(v,\left|B_{G}(o,n-1)\right|)\geq n
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Indeed, if
\operatorname{dist}(o,v)\geq n
, then

F_{G}(v,\left|B_{G}(o,n-1)\right|)\geq F_{G}(v,n)\geq n

since
\left|B_{G}(o,n-1)\right|\geq n
.

Suppose therefore that
\operatorname{dist}(o,v)<n
. Since
v
does not lie in a finite strongly connected component, there is a path
v_{0}=v,v_{1},\dots,v_{k}
such that
\operatorname{dist}(o,v_{k})=n
. We may assume that the path is simple, i.e.
v_{i}\neq v_{j}
for
i\neq j
, and that
k
is the minimal index such that
\operatorname{dist}(o,v_{k})=n
. Therefore
v_{0},\dots,v_{k-1}
are distinct elements in
B_{G}(o,n-1)
, and thus
k\leq\left|B_{G}(o,n-1)\right|
. It follows that

F_{G}(v,\left|B_{G}(o,n-1)\right|)\geq F_{G}(v,k)\geq\operatorname{dist}(o,v_{k})=n,

concluding the proof.∎

Corollary 7.5
.

Suppose that
G=(V,E)
has no finite strongly connected components. Suppose further that the
\deg^{+}(v)\leq d
for all
v\in V
. Then
F_{G}(n)\geq\log_{d}n-1
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

Since
\deg^{+}(v)\leq d
for all
v\in V
, it follows that
\left|B_{G}(o,R-1)\right|\leq\sum_{i=0}^{R-1}d^{i}\leq d^{R}
for all
R\geq 0
. Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and take
R
so that
d^{R}\leq n<d^{R+1}
. Then

F_{G}(n)\geq F_{G}(d^{R})\geq F_{G}(B_{G}(o,R-1))\geq R\geq\log_{d}n-1

as required.
∎

We now return to studying random walks, and prove Theorem C. I.e., we give a lower bound for the distance of the random walk from the root in terms of the function
F_{G}
.

Theorem 7.6
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a rooted directed graph without finite strongly connected components, such that
\deg^{+}(v)\leq d
for all
v\in V
. Let
R_{n}
be a simple random walk on
G
. Then, almost surely,
\operatorname{dist}(o,R_{t})\geq F(\log_{d}t+\log_{d}\log_{d}t)
for infinitely many values of
t
.

Proof.

We first replace
G
by the directed graph
G^{\prime}
obtained from
G
by adding loops for any vertex
v\in V
, until
\deg^{+}_{G^{\prime}}(v)=d
. We will prove the theorem for the simple random walk
R^{\prime}_{n}
on
G^{\prime}
, which will also prove the theorem for
R_{n}
. Indeed, note first that
F_{G}=F_{G^{\prime}}
, since we only added loops. We couple
R_{n}
with
R^{\prime}_{n}
as follows: sample
R^{\prime}_{0},R^{\prime}_{1},\dots
as a simple random walk on
G^{\prime}
, and then define
R_{n}
by taking the sequence
R^{\prime}_{0},R^{\prime}_{1},\dots
and removing any transition where the random walk
R^{\prime}_{n}
used a loop that we added from
G
to
G^{\prime}
. Suppose that for some
t\geq 1
we have
\operatorname{dist}(o,R^{\prime}_{t})\geq F_{G^{\prime}}(\log_{d}t+\log_{d}\log_{d}t)
; then, by the coupling, there is some
s\leq t
such that
R_{s}=R^{\prime}_{t}
, and thus

\operatorname{dist}(o,R_{s})=\operatorname{dist}(o,R^{\prime}_{t})\geq F_{G^{\prime}}(\log_{d}t+\log_{d}\log_{d}t)\geq F_{G}(\log_{d}s+\log_{d}\log_{d}s).

We therefore focus on the random walk
R^{\prime}_{n}
on
G^{\prime}
. Color the edges of
G^{\prime}
by elements of
\Sigma=\left\{x_{1},\dots,x_{d}\right\}
, such that from each vertex there is exactly one outgoing edge of each color. Then, any vertex
v\in V
and a word
\xi\in\Sigma^{*}
define a path in
G^{\prime}
, by starting from
\xi
and choosing the edges according to the letters of
\xi
. The random walk
R^{\prime}_{n}
then corresponds to sampling a random word
W_{n}=Y_{1}\cdots Y_{n}
in
\Sigma^{n}
.

For each
n\geq 1
, write
t_{n}=d^{n}
and
k_{n}=n+\log_{d}n
, and define the event

A_{n}=\left\{\exists t:t-{n-1}+k_{n}\leq t<t_{n}:\operatorname{dist}(o,R^{\prime}_{t})\geq F_{G^{\prime}}(k_{n})\right\}.

We will prove that
\mathbb{P}(A_{n}\mid R^{\prime}_{d^{n-1}}=v)\gtrsim\frac{1}{n}
for all
n\geq 1
and
v\in V
.

We define a function
\alpha_{n,v}\colon\Sigma^{*}\to\Sigma^{k_{n}}
as follows: given
\xi\in\Sigma^{*}
, write by
w\in V
the end vertex after starting a random walk from
v
and following the steps of
\xi
. By the definition of
F_{G^{\prime}}
, there is a path
w=w_{0},\dots,w_{k}
for
k\leq k_{n}
such that
\operatorname{dist}(w,w_{k})=F_{G^{\prime}}(k_{n})
. We then define
\alpha_{n,v}(w)
to be some word of length
k_{n}
, such that its first
k
letters correspond to the path
w_{0},\dots,w_{k}
.

By Proposition
A.1
,

\mathbb{P}(\exists t_{n-1}+k_{n}\leq t<t_{n}:Y_{t-k_{n}+1}\cdots Y_{t}=\alpha_{n,v}(Y_{t_{n-1}+1},\dots,Y_{t-k_{n}})\mid R^{\prime}_{t_{n-1}}=v)\gtrsim\frac{d^{n}}{d^{k_{n}}}=\frac{1}{n}.

We note that if
R^{\prime}_{t_{n-1}}=v
and
Y_{t-k_{n}+1}\cdots Y_{t}=\alpha_{n,v}(Y_{t_{n-1}+1},\dots,Y_{t-k_{n}})
for some
t_{n-1}+k_{n}\leq t<t_{n}
, then by definition of
\alpha_{n,v}
we have
\operatorname{dist}(o,R^{\prime}_{t})\geq F_{G^{\prime}}(k_{n})
; therefore we also have

\mathbb{P}(A_{n}\mid R^{\prime}_{t_{n-1}}=v)\gtrsim\frac{1}{n}.

for all
v\in V
.

The events
A_{1},A_{2},\dots
are not independent, so we cannot apply the Borel-Cantelli lemma directly; however, the above is enough to follow the proof of the lemma. Indeed, we first note that by the strong Markov property for the random walk
R^{\prime}_{n}
,

\mathbb{P}(A_{n}^{c}\mid A_{N}^{c},\dots,A_{n-1}^{c})\lesssim 1-\frac{1}{n}

for all
1\leq N\leq n
. This holds because
A_{N}^{c},\dots,A_{n-1}^{c}
depend only on
R^{\prime}_{d^{N-1}},\dots,R^{\prime}_{d^{n-1}}
, while we proved that
\mathbb{P}(A_{n}^{c}\mid R^{\prime}_{d^{n-1}}=v)\gtrsim 1-\frac{1}{n}
regardless of the value of
v\in V
.
Next, for any
N\geq 1
we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{n=N}^{\infty}A_{n}^{c}\right)=\prod_{n=N}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(A_{n}^{c}\mid A_{N}^{c},\dots,A_{n-1}^{c})\lesssim\prod_{n=N}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)=0,

where the last equality holds since
\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n}=\infty
. Therefore

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}((\limsup A_{n})^{c})=\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{N=1}^{\infty}\bigcap_{n=N}^{\infty}A_{n}^{c}\right)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{n=N}^{\infty}A_{n}^{c}\right)=0.

This shows that almost surely, the events
\left\{A_{n}\right\}
hold infinitely often. Therefore, almost surely, there are infinitely many values of
t
such that
\operatorname{dist}(o,R^{\prime}_{t})\geq F_{G}(\log_{d}t+\log_{d}\log_{d}t)
, which proves the same for
R_{n}
as explained at the beginning of the proof.
∎

As random walks on semigroups are a special case of random walks on graphs, we first rephrase Theorem
7.6
for random walks on semigroups. For a finitely generated semigroup
S
, we denote by
F_{S}
the function
F
of its Cayley graph.

Corollary 7.7
.

Let
S
be a
d
-generated semigroup with no finite right ideals, and let
R_{n}
be a simple random walk on
S
. Then, almost surely,
\left|R_{n}\right|\geq F_{S}(\log_{d}n+\log_{d}\log_{d}n)
for infinitely many values of
n
.

While this bound is clearly not optimal for most semigroups, there are examples for which this is tight.

Example 7.8
.

Consider the semigroup
S=\left\langle x,y\mid xy=y^{2}=y\right\rangle
, and let
R_{n}
be a simple random walk on
S
. We first note that by the diamond lemma, any element of
S
can be written uniquely either as
x^{m}
or as
yx^{m}
for some integer
m\geq 0
. Therefore
F_{S}(n,a)=\left|a\right|+n
for all
a\in S
, since
\left|ax^{n}\right|=\left|a\right|+n
, and thus
F_{S}(n)=n
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Corollary
7.7
shows that, almost surely,
\left|R_{n}\right|\geq\log_{d}n+\log_{d}\log_{d}n
for infinitely many values of
n
. We will show that this is optimal for the random walk
R_{n}
on
S
.

Consider the random variables
\left\{\left|R_{n}\right|\right\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
. We have
R_{0}=0
,
R_{1}=1
, and for all
n\geq 1
we have
\mathbb{P}(R_{n+1}=R_{n}+1)=\mathbb{P}(R_{n+1}=1)=\frac{1}{2}
. We can therefore describe the distribution of
\left|R_{n}\right|
as follows: given an infinite sequence of independent tosses of a fair coin,
\left|R_{n}\right|
denotes the length of the current run of the tosses. By
[
7
]
, for any
\varepsilon>0
we have
\left|R_{n}\right|\leq\log_{2}n+(1+\varepsilon)\log_{2}\log_{2}n
for all but finitely many values of
n
.

8.
Bounded speed

We finish the paper by considering the case where
\mathbb{E}\left|R_{n}\right|
is bounded independently of
n
. This is the case, of course, if
S
is finite, since then
\left|R_{n}\right|
is itself bounded. To study this case, we utilize ideas from
[
15
]
.

Proposition 8.1
.

Let
S
be a finitely generated semigroup, and let
R_{n}
be a simple random walk on
S
. If
S
contains a finite one-sided ideal, then
\mathbb{E}\left|R_{n}\right|
is bounded.

Proof.

Write
R_{n}=X_{1}\cdots X_{n}
, where
X_{1},X_{2},\dots
are i.i.d. random variables distributed uniformly on the generating set of
S
. We prove the claim for right ideals; the claim for left ideals follows from a similar argument.

Let
I\leq S
be a finite right ideal of
S
, i.e.,
IS=I
, and write
M=\max_{a\in I}\left|a\right|
. Fix some
a_{0}\in I
, and write
m=\left|a_{0}\right|
. Define the stopping time

\tau=\inf\left\{n\mid X_{n-m+1}\cdots X_{n}=a_{0}\right\}.

It is a classical fact that
\mathbb{E}[\tau]<\infty
. We include a short proof here for completeness. We note that
\mathbb{P}(\tau>n)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\forall j\leq\frac{n}{m}:X_{m(j-1)+1}\cdots X_{mj}=a\right)\leq\left(1-\frac{1}{d^{m}}\right)^{\left\lfloor\frac{n}{m}\right\rfloor}
. Then

\mathbb{E}\left|R_{n}\right|=\mathbb{E}\left[\left|R_{n}\right|\mid\tau\leq n\right]\mathbb{P}(\tau\leq n)+\mathbb{E}\left[\left|R_{n}\right|\mid\tau>n\right]\mathbb{P}(\tau>n)\leq M+n\left(1-\frac{1}{d^{m}}\right)^{\left\lfloor\frac{n}{m}\right\rfloor}

is bounded, since the last term tends to
0
as
n
tends to
\infty
.

This proves that if
S
contains a finite right ideal, then
\mathbb{E}\left|R_{n}\right|
is bounded, completing the proof.
∎

Remark 8.2
.

If
S
contains a finite right ideal
I
, then
R_{n}
is almost surely trapped in a finite set. Indeed, the proof of Proposition
8.1
shows that
\mathbb{P}(\exists n:R_{n}\in I)=1
, and since
I
is a right ideal
R_{n}\in I
implies
R_{m}\in I
for all
m\geq n
. However, this is not the case when
S
contains a finite left ideal but no finite right ideals, as demonstrated in Example
8.4
.

In some cases, Proposition
8.1
gives a complete characterization of the cases where the speed is bounded, as demonstrated in the following proposition. Recall that an inverse semigroup is a semigroup in which for every element
x
there exists a unique element
y
(‘weak inverse’) such that
xyx=x,yxy=y
.

Proposition 8.3
.

Let
S
be a finitely generated commutative semigroup or an inverse semigroup, and let
R_{n}
be a simple random walk on
S
. If
\mathbb{E}\left|R_{n}\right|
is bounded, then
S
contains a finite two-sided ideal.

Proof.

Suppose that
\mathbb{E}\left|R_{n}\right|\leq M
for all
n
. By Markov’s inequality,

\mathbb{P}(\left|R_{n}\right|\leq 2M)\geq\frac{1}{2},

for all
n
, and thus there exists
a\in B_{S}(1,2M)
such that
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(R_{n}=a)>0
. This shows that the random walk
R_{n}
is
positive recurrent
. By
[
15
, Corollary 3.2]
, the Cesàro sums
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mu^{j}
converge to a probability measure
\pi
supported on a completely simple minimal ideal
K
of
S
with a finite group factor in its Rees representation. In other words,
K=E\times G\times F
where
G
is a finite group,
E
and
F
are semigroups, and the multiplication is given by

(e,g,f)(e^{\prime},g^{\prime},f^{\prime})=(e,g\phi(f,e^{\prime})g^{\prime},f^{\prime})

for some function
\phi\colon F\times E\to G
. But if
S
is either a commutative semigroup or an inverse semigroup, we must have
E=F=\left\{1\right\}
, so
K=G
is a finite ideal of
S
.

Indeed, if
S
is commutative then for any
e,e^{\prime}\in E,f,f^{\prime}\in F
we have
(e,\phi(f,e^{\prime}),f^{\prime})=(e,1,f)(e^{\prime},1,f^{\prime})=(e^{\prime},1,f^{\prime})(e,1,f)=(e^{\prime},\phi(f^{\prime},e),f)
so
E,F
are trivial.
Furthermore, given
e\in E,f\in F
, for every
e^{\prime}\in E,f^{\prime}\in F
we have that
(e^{\prime},\phi(f,e^{\prime})^{-1}\phi(f^{\prime},e)^{-1},f^{\prime})
is a weak inverse of
(e,1,f)
, so
S
cannot be an inverse semigroup unless
E,F
are trivial.
∎

We conclude by giving an example of a semigroup for which the speed is bounded, while the random walk itself is not trapped in a finite subset almost surely.

Example 8.4
.

Consider the semigroup
S=\left\langle x,y\mid xy=y^{2}=y\right\rangle
of Example
7.8
, and let
R_{n}
be a simple random walk on
S
. Note that
\mathbb{E}\left|R_{n}\right|
is bounded, since
y
generates a finite left ideal
Sy=\left\{y\right\}
. However, as we saw before, the distance
\left|R_{n}\right|
itself is almost surely unbounded.

Appendix A
Appearances of subwords

The study of the number of appearances of subwords in a random word is a well-studied subject. In this section we consider the appearance of subwords in a random word, where the subwords change with the position and depend on the prefix of the word.

Proposition A.1
.

Let
\Sigma=\left\{x_{1},\dots,x_{d}\right\}
be a finite alphabet, and let
X=X_{1}\cdots X_{n}\in\Sigma^{n}
be a random word of length
n
in
\Sigma
. Fix
k\leq\frac{n}{2}
, and let
g\colon\Sigma^{*}\to\Sigma^{k}
be a function. Then

\mathbb{P}(\exists 1\leq j\leq n-k:X_{j+1}\cdots X_{j+k}=g(X_{1}\cdots X_{j}))\geq\frac{1}{4(\frac{d^{k}}{n}+1)}.

Proof.

Let
N=\sum_{j=1}^{n-k}I_{j}
, where
I_{j}=\boldsymbol{1}_{\left\{X_{j+1}\cdots X_{j+k}=g(X_{1}\cdots X_{j})\right\}}
. We will compute the first two moments of
N
, and apply the Paley–Zigmund inequality to achieve the desired lower bound.

To compute
\mathbb{E}[N]
, note that

\mathbb{E}[I_{j}]=\mathbb{P}(X_{j+1}\cdots X_{j+k}=g(X_{1}\cdots X_{j}))=\frac{1}{d^{k}},

and thus

\mathbb{E}[N]=\sum_{j=1}^{n-k}\mathbb{E}[I_{j}]=\frac{n-k}{d^{k}}\geq\frac{n}{2d^{k}}.

For the second moment, let
1\leq j<j^{\prime}\leq n-k
. If
j^{\prime}-j\geq k
, then

\mathbb{E}[I_{j}I_{j^{\prime}}]=\mathbb{P}(X_{j+1}\cdots X_{j+k}=g(X_{1}\cdots X_{j}),X_{j^{\prime}+1}\cdots X_{j^{\prime}+k}=g(X_{1}\cdots X_{j^{\prime}}))=\frac{1}{d^{2k}},

since the equalities are independent. If
j^{\prime}-j<k
, there is overlap between
X_{j+1}\cdots X_{j+k}
and
X_{j^{\prime}+1}\cdots X_{j^{\prime}+k}
of size
k-(j^{\prime}-j)
, and thus

\mathbb{E}[I_{j}I_{j^{\prime}}]=\mathbb{P}(X_{j+1}\cdots X_{j+k}=g(X_{1}\cdots X_{j}),X_{j^{\prime}+1}\cdots X_{j^{\prime}+k}=g(X_{1}\cdots X_{j^{\prime}}))\leq\frac{1}{d^{2k-(j^{\prime}-j)}}

(where the last inequality is an equality if and only if
g(X_{1}\cdots X_{j})
and
g(X_{1}\cdots X_{j^{\prime}})
agree on the overlap, otherwise the left hand side is
0
). Therefore

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[N^{2}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n-k}\mathbb{E}[I_{j}]+2\sum_{1\leq j<j^{\prime}\leq n-k}\mathbb{E}[I_{j}I_{j^{\prime}}]

\displaystyle=\frac{n-k}{d^{k}}+2\sum_{r=1}^{k-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n-k-r}\mathbb{E}[I_{j}I_{j+r}]+2\sum_{j^{\prime}-j\geq k}\mathbb{E}[I_{j}I_{j^{\prime}}]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n-k}{d^{k}}+2\sum_{r=1}^{k-1}\frac{n-k}{d^{2k-r}}+\frac{(n-k)^{2}}{d^{2k}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n-k}{d^{k}}+\frac{2}{d^{k}(d-1)}+\frac{(n-k)^{2}}{d^{2k}}\leq\frac{n}{d^{k}}+\frac{n^{2}}{d^{2k}}.

By the Paley–Zygmund inequality,

\mathbb{P}(N>0)\geq\frac{\mathbb{E}[N]^{2}}{\mathbb{E}[N^{2}]}\geq\frac{\frac{n^{2}}{4d^{2k}}}{\frac{n}{d^{k}}+\frac{n^{2}}{d^{2k}}}\geq\frac{1}{4(\frac{d^{k}}{n}+1)}

completing the proof.
∎
