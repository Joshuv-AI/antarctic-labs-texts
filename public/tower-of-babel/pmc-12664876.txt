Comprehensive and In-Depth Molecular and Pathway Studies of the Hippocampus in Alzheimer’s Disease
Authors: Farnaz Tayani Pour, Fatemeh Saadatpour, Ali Salari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlzheimer’s disease (AD) still lacks a conclusive treatment, largely due to an incomplete understanding of the molecular mechanisms involved. To enhance our knowledge of AD pathogenesis and identify potential therapeutic targets, this study integrates differential gene expression analysis, pathway enrichment, hub gene discovery, protein–protein interaction (PPI) clustering, and transcription factor/protein kinase regulation into a single, cohesive pipeline. This comprehensive systems-level approach moves beyond single-gene analyses to offer a broader, mechanistically focused insight into AD biology. Using RNA-seq data from the CA1 region of the hippocampus—a subregion selectively affected in early AD—we identified 1,104 differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Among the enriched pathways, “7-alpha-hydroxycholesterol” was upregulated, while “vacuolar organization” was downregulated in AD samples. Furthermore, five novel hub genes (MRPS7, RPL5, GFM1, RAD51, and ASPM) were identified within the PPI network. The first three—MRPS7, RPL5, and GFM1—along with ACO2 and MT-ATP6, are potentially linked to hereditary forms of AD due to their roles in mitochondrial function. We also discovered four collaborative clusters within the network that notably associated with the “inflammatory response”, “7-alpha-hydroxycholesterol”, “Mitochondrial dysfunction” and “Oxidative phosphorylation” pathways, making them promising candidates for therapeutic and diagnostic investigation due their behavioral information members. Additionally, we identified ten transcription factors (GATA2, CHD1, THRA, IRF7, ZBTB48, POLE4, ZNF219, SLC2A4RG, NR1D1, and RXRA) and one protein kinase (PRKCZ) as potential regulatory elements in AD. This study broadens our understanding of Alzheimer’s disease by identifying five candidate hub genes, two functional PPI clusters, two signaling pathways, and eleven regulatory proteins, thereby laying the groundwork for future therapeutic and diagnostic developments in molecular AD research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10571-025-01630-5.Keywords: Comprehensive molecular study, Alzheimer’s disease, Hub genes, PPI network, Transcriptome data, Transcription factors

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease is a chronic, progressive neurodegenerative condition, accounting for 60–80% of all dementia cases globally (Association 2019). AD has become a significant health burden due to the aging population, with an estimated 152 million cases by 2050 (Patterson 2018). Clinically, AD is characterized by progressive memory loss, cognitive impairment, and deficiencies in judgment, abstraction, language, and attention (Anand et al. 2014). Pathogenic features of AD include the formation of intracellular neurofibrillary tangles, extracellular β-amyloid plaques, and neuronal loss and destruction (Selkoe 2001). Current pharmaceutical treatments, such as donepezil, galantamine, rivastigmine, and memantine, provide only temporary symptomatic relief (Corbett et al. 2012; Kishi et al. 2017). Despite extensive research, no effective therapeutic solutions have been developed, highlighting the need to identify new molecular targets for diagnosis and treatment of AD.

Previous studies have used protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis to uncover AD-related genes. Raj et al. (2012) identified four genes associated with AD through PPI network analysis (Raj et al. 2012). Functional analyses using the KEGG and Gene Ontology (GO) databases, alongside high-throughput data from GEO microarray datasets, have been employed in various studies. Gui et al. identified four hub gene candidates for potential AD treatment through PPI network analysis of differentially expressed genes (Gui et al. 2021). In 2021, Zhang et al. identified six biomarkers via RNA-seq data analysis, focusing on co-expression genes and the PPI network (Zhang et al. 2021a, 2021b). Furthermore, in 2022, Liting Song et al. discovered several therapeutic pathways in AD, including cytokine-mediated signaling pathways, through PPI network and sub-network analyses (Song et al. 2022).

In the present study, we conducted RNA-sequencing analysis of hippocampal tissue gene expression to identify potential genes and key pathways in Alzheimer’s disease. Our study offers novel insights into the hub genes and pathways and regulatory mechanisms at both transcriptional and post-translational levels in AD, aiming to deepen the understanding of molecular targets for future therapeutic development.

Materials and Methods

Source of Differential Expressed Genes

In this study, we aimed to identify the primary molecular players involved in Alzheimer’s disease pathogenesis. To achieve this, we analyzed the complete transcriptional data from the CA1 region, the largest part of the hippocampal brain tissue, collected from both Alzheimer’s patients and healthy individuals using bulk RNA sequencing techniques. This data was sourced from the GSE184942 accession in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (Fig. 1A). Paired-end reads were aligned to the hg38 reference genome using the Hisat2 aligner, version 2.1.0 (Kim et al. 2019). Gene counting was performed with the HTSeq tool using default parameters (Anders et al. 2015), and differentially expressed genes were identified using the DESeq2 package in the R programming environment (Love et al. 2014). In addition, DEGs were selected based on a raw p-value ≤ 0.05 and a log2 fold change ≥ + 1 for up-regulated genes or log2 fold change ≤ − 1 for down-regulated genes. Although DESeq2 also provides adjusted p-values using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction, applying this stricter threshold in our dataset (n = 8 samples) resulted in very few significant DEGs, limiting the feasibility of downstream functional and network analyses. Therefore, for this exploratory and hypothesis-generating study, we used the raw p-value threshold to retain biologically informative genes, while acknowledging the need for future validation in larger datasets. Moreover, Volcano plots were utilized to visualize the distribution of DEGs and to facilitate comparisons of gene expression levels across different sites.

Pathway Enrichment Analysis

Understanding how genes contribute to particular signaling pathways and biological processes (BP) is the goal of this section. KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes), Reactome, Wikipathways, BioPlanet, Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB), and the UK Biobank project are among the six signaling pathway databases we used to do this.

KEGG is a group of databases that include details on disease, medications, chemicals, biological processes, and genomes. From data processing in genomics, metagenomics, metabolomics, and other omics investigations to modeling and simulation in systems biology and translational research in drug development, it is extensively utilized in bioinformatics research and teaching (Kanehisa And Goto 2000). Reactome provides species-specific pathways, each backed up by references to relevant research (Jassal et al. 2020), whereas Wikipathways focuses on certain biochemical pathways, such as signaling, metabolic, and regulatory pathways (Martens et al. 2021). A detailed, integrated route database, BioPlanet includes 1,658 human pathways from hand selected, publically accessible sources that have undergone rigorous redundancy and consistency cross-evaluation (Huang et al. 2019). In addition, MSigDB is renowned for its vast gene set database and is often used for gene set enrichment analysis (Liberzon et al. 2015). A prospective cohort research, the UK Biobank project gathers comprehensive genetic and phenotypic data from over 500,000 people in the UK who are between the ages of 40 and 69 when they are recruited (Bycroft et al. 2018).

A seventh BP database, which identifies biological processes necessary for an organism’s existence and capacity to interact with its surroundings, was used to assess the DEGs in addition to signaling pathways. Multiple chemical reactions or events that are involved in the upkeep and modification of living things make up biological processes.

The up- and down-regulated genes were entered independently into an online functional annotation tool called Enrichr (https://amp.pharm.mssm.edu/Enrichr) in order to conduct these analyses. This program offered a thorough comprehension of the six databases’ signaling pathways as well as the BP Gene Ontology of genes that were up- and down-regulated. BP, KEGG, MSigDB, BioPlanet, Wikipathways, UK Biobank GWAS, and Reactome enrichment pathways were examined using a P-value of 0.05 as the cutoff criterion. The Rich Factor, which is the ratio of the DEGs to the total number of genes identified in the related pathway, was used to score the results. A greater level of enrichment was indicated by a larger Rich Factor (Fig. 1C).

Construction PPIs Network and Clusters

The protein-protein interaction network of differentially expressed genes was visualized using the Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes and Proteins (STRING) database (https://string-db.org) with a confidence score threshold of ≤ 0.4 to predict interaction patterns of DEGs in Alzheimer’s disease patients. Currently, STRING includes 24.6 million proteins from 5090 different species (Szklarczyk et al. 2019).

Protein nodes that did not interact with other proteins were removed from the PPI network. In the PPI network, genes were represented as nodes, and edges represented the interactions between them (Fig. 1E). The degree of connectivity for each node, indicating the number of interactions of the corresponding gene, was examined using the CentiScaPe plugin (Scardoni et al. 2009) within the Cytoscape environment. Fifteen nodes with high degrees of connectivity were designated as hub genes (Fig. 1F). To determine which functions or signaling pathways were affected by these hub genes, we constructed individual hub networks based on their interactions (Fig. 1G). Each member of these hub networks was then evaluated using the KEGG and MSigDB pathway databases and the BP Gene Ontology database by Adjusted p-value ≤ 0.05 in the Enrichr web tool (Fig. 1H).

In addition, significant subnetworks within the PPI network were identified. The advanced settings for the plugin were as follows: degree cutoff = 2, node score cutoff = 0.2, and K-core = 2. A subnetwork with a score of 5 was selected as the primary significant cluster (Fig. 1I). This process helped us understand which specific pathways or functions were enriched within the identified gene clusters (Fig. 1I).

Upstream & Downstream Regulatory Factors Evaluation from DEGs

Identifying the transcription factors (TFs) that drive changes in gene expression is crucial for understanding the underlying gene regulatory mechanisms in Alzheimer’s disease. To achieve this, we analyzed transcriptional regulatory factors using eight TF databases: ChEA3, ChEA_2022, TRRUST_Transcription_Factors_2019, TF_Perturbations_Followed_by_Expression, Enrichr_Submissions_TF-Gene_Coocurrence, TRANSFAC_and_JASPAR_PWMs, TF-LOF_Expression_from_GEO, and ARCHS4_TFs_Coexp. Additionally, we used ARCHS4_Kinases_Coexp for investigating kinase involvement in our AD DEGs.

ChEA3 (ChIP-X Enrichment Analysis 3) is a transcription factor enrichment analysis tool that ranks transcription factors (TFs) associated with user-submitted gene sets. The ChEA3 background database comprises a collection of gene set libraries sourced from various origins. These include TF-gene co-occurrence derived from crowd-sourced gene lists, TF-gene co-expression from RNA-seq studies, and TF-target relationships from ChIP-seq experiments, among others. By combining enrichment results from these diverse sources, ChEA3 generates a composite rank that surpasses the predictive accuracy of rankings based on individual libraries, effectively identifying the proper upstream TFs (Keenan et al. 2019).

The ARCHS4_Kinases_Coexp database was constructed from 187,946 RNA-seq samples available through ARCHS4 from the GEO database, including 103,083 samples from mice and 84,863 from humans. This database enabled us to investigate another crucial regulatory player at the post-translational level. It provides information on protein kinases that are co-expressed with upregulated genes, which we utilized in our study (Lachmann et al. 2018). These databases are accessible through Enrichr’s web-based tools (Kuleshov et al. 2016).

Finally, the results were compared with both up- and down-regulated genes to identify differentially expressed TFs (DE-TFs) and Protein kinases (DE-PKs), which are pivotal regulators of DEGs in AD (Fig. 1D, J). We conducted upstream and downstream analysis on each cluster member to determine which regulatory factors influenced each cluster, discovering relationships between certain TFs and protein kinases within our clusters (Fig. 1J). The positive results were then integrated into clusters based on their targets to reveal the DE-TFs and DE-PKs, which compared with the overexpressed genes (Fig. 1K). The DE-TFs and DE-PKs network (Fig. 1L) represents a type of cluster. Moreover, to identify potentially shared TFs or protein kinases across our clusters, we merged the four clusters together (Fig. 1M). Subsequently, to gain a deeper understanding of the function of each cluster, BP and pathway enrichment analyses were performed using the Enrichr tool (Fig. 1N).

Independent Validation Using Public Datasets

To strengthen the reliability of our candidate genes, we performed validation using two independent GEO RNA-seq datasets (GSE64810, GSE68719) (Dumitriu et al. 2016; Labadorf et al. 2015) and one Microarrays dataset (PMID14769913) (Blalock et al. 2004) along with the GTEx database. For these analyses, we used three datasets and the GTEx database to validate our final candidate genes. Adjusted p-values were calculated using the Benjamini-Hochberg method, and only genes with FDR ≤ 0.05 were considered significantly validated. In addition, Expression profiles in normal hippocampal tissues were retrieved from the GTEx portal (v8) to confirm physiological relevance (DeLuca et al. 2012).

We found 1,104 differentially expressed genes with 430 up-regulated and 674 down-regulated in the AD hippocampus compared to the healthy hippocampus using a log2 fold change cutoff of −1 and + 1 and a significant criterion of p-value < 0.05 (Supplementary Table 2). Furthermore, a Volcano plot was used to examine the distribution pattern of these DEGs (Fig. 2A).

Upstream and Downstream Regulatory Factors Evaluation from the DEGs

The ChEA3 and ARCHS4_TFs_Coexp databases were used to analyze upstream regulatory actors for up-regulated genes. Four transcription factors (TFs) were identified using the ChEA3 database: SIX5, ZNF219, THRA, and SLC2A4RG. Furthermore, NR1D1 was shown to be a co-expressed TF that is co-expressed with the THRA gene by the ARCHS4_TFs_Coexp database.

The ARCHS4_Kinases_Coexp database was used for the downstream analysis in order to discover post-translational regulators. The findings showed that the only co-expressed protein kinase that was overexpressed in the current investigation was PRKCZ. Like NR1D1, PRKCZ was likewise co-expressed with THRA. Thus, in AD hippocampus DEGs, NR1D1 and PRKCZ were both recognized as putative transcriptional and post-translational regulators. The down-regulated genes did not contain any notable kinases or TFs.

Pathway Enrichment Analysis for DEGs

The roles of the up- and down-regulated genes were ascertained using six distinct databases, including one biological process (BP) database and five signaling pathway databases. 22 signaling pathways for the overexpressed genes were found by the study (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Tables 3, and Supplementary Table 4). For example, MSigDB disclosed xenobiotic metabolism, while the KEGG database emphasized the citrate cycle (TCA cycle). Furthermore, OPRL1 -> Ion Channels had the richest factor, but MYOC was linked to cell adhesion in glaucoma according to the Elsevier database (Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 3). Bile Acid and Salt Synthesis Via 27-Hydroxycholesterol and Bile Acid and Salt Synthesis Via 7Alpha-Hydroxycholesterol were found in the Reactome database. Furthermore, oxysterols produced from cholesterol were identified by the Wikipathway database as a significant signaling pathway in the hippocampus that was altered during the AD process.

In the small intestine, bile acids and bile salts are essential for the breakdown and absorption of lipids. A number of metabolic routes are involved in their formation, including one from 7α-hydroxycholesterol and another from the intermediate 27-hydroxycholesterol (Russell 2003). A sequence of enzymatic processes converts cholesterol into bile acids. Bile acid production occurs via two main pathways: Traditional (Neutral) Route: This is the main route, which starts with the production of 7α-hydroxycholesterol and converts cholesterol to cholic acid and chenodeoxycholic acid. Alternative (Acidic) process: 27-hydroxycholesterol starts this process, which mostly produces chenodeoxycholic acid. The predominant method for bile acid synthesis is the traditional pathway via 7α-hydroxycholesterol, which produces cholic acid and chenodeoxycholic acid, the two main bile acids. The emulsification and absorption of dietary lipids depend on these bile acids. Bile acids’ function in the digestive process is further enhanced by their conjugation to bile salts (Russell 2003).

The formation of bile acids, especially chenodeoxycholic acid, requires the alternate pathway via 27-hydroxycholesterol. The proper digestion and absorption of dietary fats, as well as the preservation of cholesterol homeostasis, depend heavily on this system. These bile acids’ ability to act in the digestive system is further improved by their conjugation to bile salts (Norlin and Wikvall 2007). The greatest rich factor for overexpressed genes was found to be the positive regulation of low-density lipoprotein receptor activity, which was represented by 43 biological processes in the BP database (Fig. 2C and Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 4).

The study of downregulated genes identified 23 pathways from the five pathway databases. The KEGG database indicated that the rich factors for the metabolism of estrogens and androgens were 0.4 and 0.25, respectively, and for IL5R -> STAT Signaling. The Reactome database found genes with mutations linked to vascular dementia with a rich factor of 0.4. With a rich factor of 0.25, the Elsevier pathway database indicated both NAD Biosynthesis II (from tryptophan) and IL9R -> STAT Signaling. The BioPlanet database discovered interleukin-9 signaling using a rich factor criterion of 0.2. With a rich factor of 0.01 for down-regulated genes, the Wikipathway database found SWI/SNF and BRM/BAF in the chromatin remodeling pathway (Fig. 2D and Supplementary Tables 5 and Supplementary Fig. 2). Furthermore, 26 biological processes were identified by the gene ontology database for down-regulated genes; the negative regulation of synapse formation had the highest rich factor of 0.4 (Fig. 2E and Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 6).

PPIs-Network and Clusters Construction

Construction of the PPI Network

The PPI network was built using the STRING database. Each edge was given an edge-weight score in order to determine the network’s interaction confidence. Each edge in the PPI network was given a confidence value that represented the likelihood that the interaction was physiologically relevant. Only interactions with a confidence level higher than 0.4 were analyzed in order to guarantee interaction reliability and minimize false positives. 483 nodes and 1074 edges were found in the PPI data for DEGs in Alzheimer’s disease when compared to healthy samples (Fig. 1E and Fig. 4A).

In KEGG, STAT1 is linked to inflammatory bowel disease, the JAK-STAT signaling pathway, the cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, and the chemokine signaling pathway (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Table 8). Additionally, in MSigDB (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Table 9), it affects IL-6/JAK/STAT3 signaling, inflammatory response, interferon-alpha response, and interferon-gamma response; in the BP database (Supplementary Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table 7), it affects cellular response to cytokine stimulus and cellular response to different interferons (alpha, beta). According to KEGG, RPS29 affects ribosomes (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Table 8); in the BP database, it affects translation, cellular response to interferon-beta, and ribosomal function (Supplementary Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table 7).

In KEGG, WNT1 is linked to the TGF-beta and Hippo signaling pathways (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Table 8); in MSigDB, it is linked to TNF-alpha signaling through NF-kB pathways (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Table 9). According to the BP database, the last hub gene, MT-ATP6, is involved in the control of cell aging, mitochondrial translation, and mitochondrial ATP generation in conjunction with electron transport (Supplementary Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table 7). However, the KEGG and MSigDB databases did not provide any particular findings for MT-ATP6 (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Table 8).

Upstream and Downstream Regulatory Factors from Clusters

Sub-network clustering was extracted from the PPI network using the MCODE plugin in the Cytoscape environment (Fig. 4A). Four clusters were found that scored five or above (Fig. 4B). Several transcription factor and protein kinase databases were utilized to find regulatory players for each cluster member. We determined which regulators were shared with DEGs, found a sizable number of TFs and protein kinases for each cluster, and described them as DE-TFs or DE-PKs, respectively. Clusters based on the study were created by combining all of the regulators (Fig. 4C).

Nine TFs (EBF3, IRF7, ZBTB48, GABPA, CHD1, GATA2, STAT1, THRA, and POLE4) were linked to Cluster 1, whereas POLE4 belonged to Cluster 2 (Fig. 4Cc1). Using post-translational phosphorylation pathways, Cluster 2 is regulated by 13 TFs (GABPA, RXRA, MBD4, EMX2, ZNF90, GRHL2, STAT1, CHD1, THRA, GATA2, ZBTB48, EBF3, and IRF7) and three protein kinases (SRPK1, AURKA, and BUB1B). Cluster 2 comprised all protein kinases except SRPK1 (Fig. 4Cc2). In addition, 11 TFs were found in Cluster 3: RXRA, which is a member of Cluster 3, GATA2, MBD3, STAT1, SIX5, GRHL2, ZNF219, ZNF710, ZNF740, THRA, and FOXO6. One phosphorylated regulating component in Cluster 3 was ARAF protein kinase (Fig. 4Cc3). Four members of Cluster 4 may be controlled by just three TFs (GATA2, STAT1, and CHD1), all of which were down-regulated with the exception of GATA2 (Fig. 4Cc4).

The findings show that the four clusters share a number of TFs and protein kinases. A combined network of 60 nodes and 249 edges was produced by integrating the four groups based on shared genes in order to find these common components (Fig. 4D). Notably, the combined network showed links between the clusters, including the one mediated by ZBTB48, which had two connections with Cluster 2 and five interactions with Cluster 1. In a similar vein, EBF3 has three connections in Cluster 2 and two in Cluster (1) In Cluster 1, IRF7 had three contacts, while in Cluster 2, it had two connections. With three and two interactions, respectively, GABPA served as a TF between Clusters 1 and (2) Between Cluster 1 and Cluster 2, these four TFs—ZBTB48, EBF3, IRF7, and GABPA—acted as regulatory molecules. As an inducer of RAD51 in Cluster 2 and FABP4 in Cluster 3, GRHL2 was important.

All 10 members of Cluster 2, including POLE4 TF and the protein kinases BUB1B and AURKA, were stimulated by RAD51. Two TFs were found to be RAD51 inducers: ZNF90 and MBD4. As a representation of Cluster 2’s capacity to control POLE4, RAD51’s function seems to be more significant than that of other Cluster 2 members. It is also a crucial candidate for more research since TF stimulated RPL35 and RPL22 in Cluster 1, demonstrating how Cluster 2 might control Cluster 1 through POLE4, which is regulated by RAD51 (Fig. 4E). Furthermore, GRHL2 was shown to be a bridge protein between Clusters 2 and 3 due to its downregulation, indicating a potential involvement in preventing AD. Moreover, CHD1 which is down-regulated in Clusters 1, 2, and 4, is another important TF. In order to promote RXRA in Cluster 3, overexpressed TF THRA may control these clusters by timely activation. Additionally, THRA in Cluster 2 activated CDC45 and ASPM (Fig. 4D).

Cluster 3 was triggered by six TFs (ZNF710, MBD3, SIX5, FOXO6, ZNF740, and ZNF219) through twelve interactions. Cluster 3 showed co-expression of ARAF protein kinase, RXRA, and MED16 in that order (Fig. 4D). Additionally, only Cluster 2 members interacted with four TFs (ZNF90, EMX2, DEK, and MBD4), and four Cluster 2 members co-expressed SRPK1 protein kinase (Fig. 4D). GATA2 and STAT1 also had interactions with each of the four clusters. Twelve interactions involving STAT1: two with Cluster 3, one with Cluster 4, one with Cluster 2, and eight with Cluster 1. Twelve contacts were also seen in sequence for GATA2: three with Cluster 1, five with Cluster 2, two with Cluster 3, and two with Cluster 4 (Fig. 4D).

Pathway Enrichment Analysis from Clusters

Although the regulators of these four clusters have been discovered, it is still unknown how important they are in Alzheimer’s disease. Six functional databases were used to assess each cluster in order to remedy this. Six databases were used to assess the results for each cluster (Supplementary Fig. 5 and Supplementary Table 10). For Clusters 2 and 3, the Cell Cycle pathway was important for neuron cell proliferation, according to KEGG (Fig. 5A). Along with DNA replication, G1 to S cell cycle regulation, and the electron transport chain routes, Wikipathway also identified the cell cycle pathway for Cluster 2. Reports of cytoplasmic ribosomal proteins for Cluster 1 suggested that they supported the translation machinery, which Cluster 4 also suggested. Cluster 3’s prion disease pathway was identified as being associated with neurodegenerative disease brought on by prion protein aggregates in neural cells (Fig. 5B).

Cluster 2’s E2F target, DNA repair, Myc targets v1, mitotic spindle, and G2-M checkpoint pathways were all represented by different functions in the MSigDB database. Important for AD processes, Cluster 3 contained the p53 pathway, IL-2 and STAT5 Signaling Pathways, Adipogenesis, Bile Acid Metabolism, UV Response DNA, and Interferon Gamma Response. Cluster 2 showed E2F-mediated control of cell cycle and DNA replication pathways, whereas Cluster 3 showed E2F-mediated control of adipogenesis and cancer pathways. In the BioPlanet database, Cluster 1’s primary route was translation (Fig. 5E).

Many of the functions from each cluster were represented in the BP database; some of these functions were shared by Clusters 4 and 1, including translation, gene expression, mitochondrial translational elongation, cellular protein metabolic process, peptide biosynthesis process, and mitochondrial translation. This implies that these clusters work closely together in AD processes, which has important ramifications for therapeutic targeting. Clusters 1, 2, and 4 shared cellular macromolecule biosynthesis mechanisms, suggesting cooperation in this crucial mechanism. The control of cell division, a crucial aspect of cell cycle activity in Cluster 2, was the sole biological process that Clusters 2 and 3 shared, suggesting cooperation with Cluster 3 (Fig. 5C).

Using information from six databases, an integrated study of functional pathways was carried out to investigate common pathways among the four groups. Three databases—BioPlanet, BP, and Reactome—reported translations for both Clusters 1 and 4. Clusters 1 and 4 were shown to have subfamilies of the translation pathway, including Mitochondrial Initiation, Elongation, and Termination. Additionally, ribosome function was reported for KEGG Clusters 1 and 4 (Fig. 5G and Supplementary Table 10).

Clusters 2 and 3 in BP and BioPlanet, respectively, shared the integrated breast cancer pathway and the control of cell division. BioPlanet and MSigDB identified the Oxidative Phosphorylation route in Cluster 2, and MSigDB reported it in Cluster 4. In BP and BioPlanet, mitochondrial gene expression was shown to differ across Clusters 1 and 2. Clusters 1 and 3 from Reactome and BioPlanet reflected pathways related to metabolism and developmental biology. According to BP, Clusters 1, 2, and 4 contained the biosynthesis of biological macromolecules (Fig. 5F).

Validation of Candidate Genes Across Independent Datasets

We used the GTEx database and three more GEO datasets to validate the differential expression of important candidate genes found in our original RNA-seq dataset. Two of them were collected using the RNA-seq approach from a collection of neurodegenerative diseases, and one was taken from AD microarrays. Four NR1D1, THRA, IRF7, and SLC2A4RG were elevated with FDR < 0.05 in the microarray dataset. Additionally, eight highly up-regulated genes were identified in the two RNA-seq datasets from the collection of neurodegenerative diseases: GFM1, SLC2A4RG, NR1D1, PRKCZ, RXRA, GATA2, POLE4, and ZNF219. Out of these eight genes, GFM1 is a hub gene, and the other genes are those that are implicated in the roles of regulatory factors in the current study.

In addition, GTEx analysis showed recurring expression patterns in the hippocampus and other brain regions. PRKCZ (16:69), NR1D1 (15:15), ZBTB48 (9:5), THRA (70:37), IRF7 (5:9), POLE4 (25:4), RXRA (16:57), ZNF219 (14:14), SLC2A4RG (12:97), GATA2 (1:7), GFM1 (8:6), MRPS7 (18:71), CHD1 (3:2), RPL5 (295:5), RAD51 (0.1), and ASPM (0.03) are a few examples. Their expression in the hippocampus is supported by these data, which are relevant to our findings. Our candidate genes’ biological resilience is further supported by these validations, which go beyond the original cohort.

Discussion

Since Alzheimer’s disease is a neurodegenerative disease that cannot be cured, prevention and therapy are still major and unsolved issues. Memantine, galantamine, rivastigmine, donepezil, and other FDA-approved anti-AD medications can help reduce the clinical symptoms of AD. Nevertheless, AD cannot be cured by these drugs; they merely impede the disease’s development (Corbett et al. 2012; Kishi et al. 2017; Tan et al. 2014).

Although our study only uses one RNA-seq dataset with eight samples, we acknowledge that this method may cause questions about the robustness and generalizability of our results. It is crucial to remember, nevertheless, that using a single dataset in RNA-seq studies with comparable sample sizes is a common and generally recognized approach in the scientific community. A research that was published in PLOS ONE, for instance, successfully used a similar methodology, proving the feasibility and dependability of this kind of approach (Hozhabri et al. 2022).

Potential Limitations and Mitigations

Sample Size and Dataset Selection

The use of a single publicly accessible RNA-seq dataset (GSE184942), which only included eight hippocampus tissue samples (4 AD and 4 control), is one of the study’s main limitations. This small sample size raises the possibility of both statistical and technical variability and could not adequately represent the biological heterogeneity of Alzheimer’s disease. Samples were carefully chosen to reflect the population of interest in order to lessen this, and normalization techniques were strictly followed in order to minimize technical noise. Additionally, the results were cross-validated for internal consistency after downstream studies, such as pathway enrichment, hub gene identification, and network clustering, were carried out using commonly used tools and databases.

Statistical Considerations and DEG Selection

The statistical handling of variable gene expression is another drawback. Applying a strong FDR cutoff (adjusted p < 0.05) to our tiny dataset resulted in a significant loss of signal, lowering the number of DEGs to less than 30 genes, even though DESeq2 gives adjusted p-values using the Benjamini-Hochberg technique for multiple testing correction. The range of systems biology investigations that followed would have been severely constrained as a result. Therefore, we decided to apply a criterion of log2 fold change ≥ + 1 or < −1 and raw p-value ≤ 0.05, which is a frequently recognized approach in exploratory or hypothesis-generating investigations, particularly when dealing with small-sample RNA-seq data. We gave priority on maintaining potentially significant biological signals for additional research, even though this strategy may raise the possibility of false positives.

To improve the reliability of our results and overcome the drawbacks of depending on uncorrected p-values in a small-sample dataset, we used the GTEx database and three more GEO datasets in an independent validation approach. In particular, the repeatability of candidate genes that were first discovered with nominal p-values (raw p ≤ 0.05, |log2FC| ≥ 1) was reexamined across these distinct transcriptome cohorts. In all three datasets, the genes were reexamined using a strict false discovery rate criterion (FDR ≤ 0.05). An extra layer of contextual validity was added by confirming the physiological expression of these genes in non-diseased hippocampus regions by analyzing expression profiles from GTEx brain tissues. By using a two-pronged strategy—biological cross-reference with normal tissue expression and statistical correction using FDR in external datasets—we were able to reduce false positives and improve our list of potential genes. In the end, our integrated method validates exploratory transcriptome findings within limited datasets in a unique way while supporting the repeatability, cross-cohort relevance, and biological plausibility of our chosen molecular targets.

Functions of DEGs

Three databases—BioPlanet, BP, and Elsevier—provided functional findings of down-regulated genes for Interleukin-9 signaling. Two members, STAT1 and IL9, were included, and a rich factor value of 0.2 was used (Supplementary Tables 5 and 6). According to earlier research, interleukin-9 signaling, which is regulated by the STAT protein pathway, is strongly linked to cell proliferation and anti-apoptotic processes (Bauer et al. 1998).

Furthermore, our study discovered that the cellular response to interferon-beta, which was shown to slow down brain shrinkage in 2011 (Filippi et al. 2011), was down-regulated (Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 6). Vacuole organization control, a crucial step in neurotransmitter transport from the synaptic environment, is another function exemplified by down-regulated genes (Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 6). Additionally, AD is characterized by a down-regulation of the proteasomal protein catabolic process, which is a major mechanism for protein breakdown in the presence of tau and amyloid accumulation (Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 6). Recent research indicates that down-regulated genes are closely connected to Alzheimer’s disease and are tied to certain neuronal processes.

Moreover, the current study of overexpressed genes also revealed that the OPRL1-> Ion Channel pathways, which are linked to neurobehavioral features like stress responses, anxiety behavior, learning and memory, locomotor activity, and inflammatory and immune responses, were up-regulated pathways (Ruggeri et al. 2018; Der-Avakian et al. 2017).

due to their functions, these pathways could be associated with AD patients. While several studies have proposed a connection between cholesterol and AD (Loera-Valencia et al. 2021; Solomon et al. 2007), in hippocampal AD samples, the synthesis of bile acids and bile salts through “27-hydroxycholesterol” is fueled by overexpressed genes (Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 3). This relationship results in the overexpression of the “27-hydroxycholesterol” pathway (Russell 2000), which converts cholesterol into oxysterols, bile acids, and bile salts. Both the “27-hydroxycholesterol” and “24-hydroxycholesterol” pathways have been identified as markers of AD (Wang et al. 2016).

Cholestenoic acid is produced from cholesterol because of its high density (Goldstein et al. 2006). An increase in the synthesis of amyloid-beta by neural cells is also linked to attempts to reduce cholesterol in the brain (Ghribi et al. 2006). By transforming cholesterol into bile acid and oxysterol molecules, this process successfully lowers cholesterol levels and aids in maintaining homeostasis. Furthermore, if cholesterol levels continue to drop, the RXR heterodimer may attach to oxysterol and bile acid to form a complex that could aid in controlling them (Repa et al. 2000).

As a consequence, this study highlights the “27-hydroxycholesterol” pathway as a significant cholesterol regulating mechanism, particularly in associated with AD samples. The author suggests that the breakdown of phospholipid neural membranes and apoptotic cell death may be caused by the upregulation of the “27-hydroxycholesterol” pathway, which is brought on by a marked drop in cholesterol levels in hippocampus AD samples. Another study has shown that this mechanism is associated with impaired synapse function and cognitive impairment in adulthood (Loera-Valencia et al. 2021).

Additionally, this study found that the synthesis of bile acids and bile salts via the “7-alpha-hydroxycholesterol” route is an up-regulated function linked to cholesterol metabolism in AD. This discovery raises the possibility of a future study topic in patients with hippocampus AD.

Regulators from DEGs

Additionally, among the overexpressed genes, six regulatory actors were identified; five of them, SIX5, ZNF219, THRA, SLC2A4RG, and NR1D1, were transcription factor regulators, and one was a protein kinase regulator. By controlling cluster 3 as a TF to activate two genes, NCOA3 and RXRA, which are known for their functions in cholesterol metabolism, SIX homeobox 5 (SIX5) is emphasized. The induction of 86 overexpressed genes as TFs in this investigation supported this regulation. Notably, early research studies have identified SIX5 as a key transcription factor in Parkinson’s and Alzheimer’s disorders (Kelly et al. 2020; Dharshini et al. 2019).

It was also discovered that 24 genes were overexpressed by Zinc Finger Protein 219 (ZNF219). Like SIX5, it controls RXRA and NCOA3, which were also found to be tau regulators in an early investigation (Barrachina and Ferrer 2009). There is evidence linking AD patients to Thyroid Hormone Receptor Alpha (THRA) (Goumidi et al. 2011). Its function as a potential regulatory actor was highlighted by this study, which found that it was a TF regulator for 27 overexpressed genes in three clusters. THRA, for instance, caused NDUFA1 in cluster 2, RXRA and NCOA3 in cluster 3, and RPS26, RPL30, and RPL35 in cluster 1. According to this study, THRA is positioned as a prominent potential regulator since it activates 27 overexpressed genes in hippocampus AD patients throughout different clusters.

The TF regulator SLC2A4RG was found to have 21 inductions in overexpressed genes, including important genes like the main hub gene MRPS7, the protein kinase regulator PRKCZ, the cluster and cholesterol metabolism regulator RXRA, the AD regulator SIX5, and the candidate therapy gene KIF1C. SLC2A4RG has been linked to Huntington’s disease and multiple sclerosis (MS) in the past (Tian et al. 2020), and this work found that it is also linked to AD and TF induction, suggesting that it may be a promising target for treatment.

due to its involvement in metabolism, inflammation, redox homeostasis, and circadian rhythm pathways, the nuclear receptor subfamily 1 group D member 1 (NR1D1) has been shown to be down-regulated in Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS) and has been identified as a TF involved in 13 co-expressed interactions, including KIF1C, THRA, and PRKCZ, which may be relevant in Alzheimer’s disease patients (Killoy et al. 2022).

This study found that 13 overexpressed genes co-expressed the protein kinase Protein Kinase C Zeta Type (PRKCZ), which has been connected to AD functions (Mao et al. 2020), This suggests that PRKCZ is related to AD. These results highlight the need for additional research on PRKCZ, with a particular emphasis on these 13 connections in patients with hippocampus AD.

Hub Genes of Protein-Protein Interaction Network

The investigators explored into the possible connections between these genes after determining the roles of each gene in the lists of genes that were up- and down-regulated. This was accomplished by building a protein-protein interaction network with 1074 edges and 483 nodes utilizing information from the STRING database. In order to determine the key genes in this network, the authors carefully examined every connection for every node and arranged them according to degree. In the end, they discovered that twelve hub genes connected with at least 15 nodes (Table 1).

The main hub gene in this investigation was mitochondrial ribosomal protein S7 (MRPS7), which was increased and participated in 23 PPI network interactions (Table 1). The involvement of members of cluster 1 in translation processes and cytoplasmic ribosomal proteins were revealed by their sequence identification. Only small or large ribosomal proteins are altered in this cluster, which includes eight ribosomal components (RPL5, RPL35, RPL22, GFM1, RPL22L1, RPL30, RPS26, and RPS29) (Fig. 4Bc1). MRPS7 was determined to be the reference gene for the brain’s metencephalon area by Park et al. in 2013 (Park et al. 2013). Furthermore, MRPS7 was found to be one of the seven hub genes linked to osteosarcoma in a 2020 study by Liu et al. According to Liu, MRPS7 may be connected to osteosarcoma development (Liu et al. 2020). Chen et al. found that blocking mitochondrial translation was a selective and efficient way to target osteosarcoma in another investigation (Chen et al. 2019). Given its role in ribosomal proteins, which are essential to the translation mechanism, and the results of this study showing that it is involved in both translation function and ribosomal and oxidative phosphorylation pathways, as well as being up-regulated in AD samples from the hippocampus, MRPS7 may be a promising target for slowing the progression of AD in patients.

Additionally, it has been shown that MRPS7 has a role in mitochondrial protein synthesis, which is essential for the generation of cellular energy. A common characteristic of many neurodegenerative disorders is mitochondrial dysfunction. For instance, Parkinson’s disease (PD), of which mitochondrial dysfunction is a known cause. Defects in mitochondrial protein synthesis have been found to worsen dopaminergic neuron degeneration (Gopisetty and Thangarajan 2016). Furthermore, mitochondrial abnormalities are also seen in Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS), which shares similarities with AD and PD. Degeneration of motor neurons may result from disruption in mitochondrial protein synthesis, possibly involving MRPS7 (Ganetzky and Falk 2021).

Repairing damaged DNA requires a protein that is encoded by the RAD51 gene. The RAD51 protein binding to the DNA break site and encasing it in a protein sheath is a crucial first step in the repair process. To aid in DNA repair, RAD51 interacts with a wide range of other proteins, such as BRCA1 and BRCA2, in the nucleus of different kinds of healthy cells. By directing RAD51 to locations of DNA damage within the nucleus, the BRCA2 protein controls its function. The precise link between these two proteins is unclear, despite indications that BRCA1 activates RAD51 in response to DNA damage. Together, these three proteins aid in DNA repair, which stabilizes a cell’s genetic material (Cousineau et al. 2005; Pellegrini et al. 2002). RAD51 is also thought to have a role in movement control mechanisms, while its involvement in nervous system development is still unclear (Peng and Charron 2013; Klar 2014). This study discovered that the hippocampus regions of AD patients have down-regulated levels of the BRCA1, BRCA2, and RAD51 genes. BRCA1 and BRCA2 down-regulation, however, did not produce statistically significant p-values. In particular, RAD51 was a member of cluster 2 (Fig. 4Bc2) and emerged as a hub gene with 23 connections (Table 1). Homologous recombination, DNA damage response, ATR signaling, E2F targets, DNA repair, and G2-M checkpoints were among the mechanisms engaged in this cluster (Fig. 5Bc2, Dc2).

Moreover, it was found that RAD51 is co-expressed with eight members of cluster 2 and is controlled by three transcription factors: ZNF90, MBD4, and GRHL2. All three transcription factors, with the exception of MBD4, exhibit down-regulation in the hippocampus of AD patients (Fig. 4E). RAD51’s function in DNA repair is essential for avoiding apoptosis, which causes neuronal death. It is crucial to look into this gene candidate and find the regulatory elements that might prevent it from being expressed in AD samples.

Additionally, RAD51 is essential for the homologous recombination repair of double-strand breaks in DNA, which preserves genomic stability. Therefore, DNA repair mechanisms, such as RAD51-mediated repair, are essential in Huntington’s disease (HD). Neurons may accumulate DNA damage as a result of mutations that hinder DNA repair (Mollica et al. 2016). Defective DNA repair pathways are another characteristic of Ataxia Telangiectasia (A-T). A typical characteristic of A-T is oxidative stress-induced DNA damage, which RAD51 helps to repair (Qiu et al. 2022).

Two different protein translational systems are found in eukaryotes: one that functions in the mitochondria and the other in the cytoplasm. Mutations in this system cause mitochondrial DNA maintenance to be disrupted and the respiratory chain-oxidative phosphorylation system to break down, both of which are essential for maintaining mitochondrial function, including the respiratory cell system (Shen et al. 2020). In this study, the GFM1 hub gene’s oxidative phosphorylation pathway was identified as a shared pathway in clusters 2 and 4 (Figs. 3B and 5G). One of the mitochondrial translation elongation factors is encoded by GFM1 (Shen et al. 2020). According to data from the MSigDB database, GFM1 belongs to cluster 1 and is linked to ribosome and mitochondrial translation processes, such as initiation, termination, and elongation. Furthermore, STAT1 transcription factors regulate GFM1, which was found to be a hub gene in our study with 21 interactions (Fig. 4D). GFM1’s overexpression in the AD hippocampus and its association with cluster 1, which stands for Mitochondrial Translation, emphasize its significance in the mitochondrial environment’s oxidative phosphorylation process, which has a significant effect on cell survival.

GFM1 also plays a crucial role in mitochondrial translation elongation, which impacts energy production and mitochondrial function. Thus, mitochondrial malfunction is linked to the severe neurological condition known as Leigh Syndrome. This disease has been associated with mutations in genes related to mitochondrial translation, such as GFM1 (Fukumura et al. 2015). Defects in mitochondrial protein synthesis, possibly involving GFM1, are also important in Mitochondrial Encephalopathy, Lactic Acidosis, and Stroke-like Episodes (MELAS), another mitochondrial disorder (Ameele et al. 2022).

Furthermore, RPL5 is involved in the cytosolic and mitochondrial environments of the ribosome’s major component 60 S. Three transcription factors (TFs) were found to be targets of RPL5 within cluster 1 in this investigation: EBF3, IRF7, and ZBTB48. In 2018, Ben et al. reported that RPL5 was a new biomarker for rhabdoid tumors (Ren et al. 2018). Our analysis reveals lower expression exclusively in the hippocampus region, maybe restricted to a specific hippocampus location, whereas the majority of research has concentrated on RPL5 overexpression in malignancies and has not found any changed expression in AD samples.

RPL5 is also a component of the ribosomal machinery, which is necessary for the synthesis of proteins. Numerous disorders can result from abnormalities in ribosomal activity. Because ribosomes are essential for neural growth and function, mutations in RPL5 have neurological consequences, even though Diamond-Blackfan Anemia (DBA) is largely a hematological disorder (Quarello et al. 2010). Furthermore, healthy ribosome function is essential for brain development in neurodevelopmental disorders. Developmental delays and intellectual problems have been associated with mutations in ribosomal proteins, such as RPL5 (Hetman And Slomnicki 2019).

An aberrant spindle protein homolog (ASPM) is shared by all species, including humans, mice, Drosophila, and C. elegans (Bond et al. 2002). Variants of the faulty ASPM gene are linked to autosomal recessive primary microcephaly (Kaindl et al. 2010), impacting the size of the head and brain in particular. In embryonic neuroblasts, the ASPM gene’s protein product is essential for controlling neurogenesis and ensuring appropriate mitotic spindle function (Kouprina et al. 2005). Additionally, ASPM has been connected to the evolution of brain size in humans (Zhang 2003; Mekel-Bobrov et al. 2005), possibly acting as a neurological underpinning for the theory known as “brain reserve,” which holds that cognitive decline might be prevented by having a larger brain (Steffener and Stern 2012; Whitwell 2010) (Katzman et al. 1988). Additionally, Kim et al. have documented the connection between ASPM and brain growth (Kim et al. 2011). The effect of ASPM mutations on hippocampus volume and surface was examined by Passemard et al., who found that mutated ASPM samples had smaller size and surface area (Passemard et al. 2016). As a hub gene with 18 links in the PPI network, ASPM is shown to be down-regulated in AD patients’ hippocampal regions and participates in mitotic spindle processes in conjunction with RAD51. These findings strongly imply that ASPM plays a crucial role in AD mechanisms. Future studies should examine the precise molecular mechanisms influencing the hippocampus region in AD and the regulatory factors of ASPM, which include two protein kinases, BUB1B and AURKA, and seven TFs: RXRA, DEK, MBD4, ZNF90, RAD51, STAT1, and CHD1 (Fig. 4Cc2).

Additionally, ASPM is well-known for its function in controlling brain growth and neurogenesis. This gene is susceptible to mutations that cause microcephaly. Thus, ASPM mutations are a well-known cause of primary microcephaly, which is typified by intellectual impairment and a markedly smaller brain (Kumar et al. 2004). A wider role in brain development and function beyond microcephaly is also shown by new research that links mutations in ASPM to autism spectrum disorders (ASD) (Wu et al. 2018).

Previous research has identified a number of other hub genes, including ACO2, STAT1, RPS29, WNT1, RPL35, MDH1, and MT-ATP6. In AD hippocampus samples, ACO2 is elevated with 17 interactions. In an AD mouse model, Chen et al. suggested a treatment strategy that targets proteins involved in mitochondrial energy metabolism, such as ACO2 (Chen et al. 2021). Immunological reactions are mediated by STAT1 (signal transducer and activator of transcription 1), which upregulates genes in response to type I, II, or III interferons. STAT1 is essential for regulating the immune response because it generates dimers that attach to particular promoter regions (Katze et al. 2002). Along with IFNAR1, IL9, and IL1R1 genes, STAT1 is a hub gene with 17 contacts in the PPI network that exhibits down-expression in the AD hippocampus (Supplementary Fig. 3G). The significance of STAT1 in AD is highlighted by its function as a regulator for each of the four clusters identified in this investigation, especially cluster 1 (Fig. 4D, E). It is significantly suppressed with the course of AD and is linked to the pathophysiology of AD and Tau protein (Kitamura et al. 1997; Zhang et al. 2021a, 2021b). Preliminary research has confirmed the findings of this study, indicating that more possibilities may be taken into consideration for further investigation.

A hub gene with 16 connections, RPS29 is a ribosomal protein in cluster 1 that performs ribosomal functions (Table 1, Supplementary Fig. 3H). It is implicated in the ribosome pathway, translation, and the cellular response to interferon-beta and is markedly overexpressed in AD samples. TFs GATA2, STAT1, CHD1, GABPA, and ZBTB48 control RPS29 (Fig. 4Cc1). In a 2021 investigation, Huiwen Gui et colleagues. identified RPS29 as a hub gene for AD (Gui et al. 2021), With 16 connections, WNT1 is connected to important signaling pathways, such as neuron formation and TGF-beta, Hippo, and TNF-alpha signaling through NF-kB pathways. Our discovery of WNT1 as a gene implicated in hippocampus AD is corroborated by Macyczko’s 2023 study (Macyczko et al. 2023). Additionally, cluster 1’s ribosome and translation pathway is home to PL35, another hub gene with 16 connections. TFs such THRA, EBF3, IRF7, CHD1, STAT1, and POLE4 (Fig. 4Cc1), which connect clusters 1 and 2, regulate it. In 2021, Wang et al. documented overexpression of RPL35 in AD (Wang And Li 2021). Our findings were further supported by Wang et al.‘s (2017) identification of MDH1, a hub gene with 16 connections, as a hub gene in AD.

MT-ATP6 (mitochondrially encoded ATP synthase membrane subunit 6) is involved in the last stage of oxidative phosphorylation in the electron transport chain. Proton movement across the mitochondrial membrane is facilitated by this enzyme. This analysis identified MT-ATP6 as a hub gene with 15 interactions. It is overrepresented in AD hippocampus data and is linked to mitochondrial translation, ATP generation, and cell aging regulation. Numerous neurological disorders are associated with mutations in MT-ATP6 and related genes that impact mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (Weidling And Swerdlow 2020; Lunnon et al. 2017; Coskun et al. 2012). According to this analysis, MT-ATP6 is a hub gene associated with other AD genes (MRPS7, GFM1, RPL5, MTIF3, and COX15), indicating that future research should look more closely at these relationships (Supplementary Fig. 3L) (Stendel et al. 2020).

Furthermore, a number of investigations involving AD patients have identified mitochondrial abnormalities as an early indicator of AD (Sharma et al. 2021). Impaired mitochondria are one of the first indications of AD. The five hub genes that have been identified—MRPS7, GFM1, RPL5, ACO2, and MT-ATP6—have roles in mitochondrial processes and may be able to connect compensatory mitochondrial functioning with hereditary AD. Further inquiry into these hub genes is important to understand their roles in hereditary links and mitochondrial functions in AD.

Functions of Candidate Clusters

In this investigation, information from seven functional databases was used to analyze the functionalities of the four clusters that were found (Supplementary Fig. 5 and Supplementary Table 10). The neurodegenerative disease amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, which causes the progressive loss of motor neurons that govern voluntary muscles, is notably linked to cluster 2’s motor neuron disease pathway (Feldman et al. 2022). The electron transport chain (OXPHOS system in mitochondria) and oxidative phosphorylation are two other pathways that are part of this cluster. These pathways were discovered by Kawamata et al. in 2017 (Kawamata And Manfredi 2017) and were identified by down-regulated genes (Kawamata And Manfredi 2017).

In Cluster 2, two genes, NADH dehydrogenase 1 alpha subcomplex subunit 1 (NDUFA1) and Dynactin subunit 1 (DCTN1), were found to be less active. Mutations in NDUFA1 cause mitochondrial complex I deficiency (MT-C1D), leading to a disorder of the mitochondrial respiratory chain with a wide range of clinical manifestations, from lethal neonatal disease to adult-onset neurodegenerative disorders (Supplementary Fig. 6 A) (Huttula et al. 2022). Furthermore, DCTN1 interacts with microtubules, cytoplasmic dynein, and dynein cargo adaptors (Cianfrocco et al. 2015). Moreover, the development of neurodegenerative disease including Parkinson’s and Alzheimer’s has been connected to mutations in DCTN1 (Hori et al. 2021; Vilariño-Güell et al. 2009) (Supplementary Fig. 6B). Additionally, cluster 2 contains the cytochrome c oxidase assembly protein COX15 homolog (COX15), which functions as a COX assembly factor, and the NDUFA1 oxidative phosphorylation pathway. Defects in the human electron transport chain are frequently caused by COX deficiency. The hereditary risk for Alzheimer’s disease has been found to be influenced by variations in the COX15 gene (Vitali et al. 2009) (Supplementary Fig. 6 C). However, the KEGG, BioPlanet, and MSigDB databases classified both COX15 and NDUFA1 as part of the oxidative phosphorylation signaling route, while the KEGG and BioPlanet databases listed DCTN1 as part of the Huntington’s disease pathway. Furthermore, the Lissencephaly gene (LIS1) was found in the BP database’s axo-dendritic transport and synapse construction pathways as well as BioPlanet’s neuronal migration and development pathways. These genes’ interactions within the PPI network offer important new information about other relationships related to the AD process. These interactions, in turn, demonstrated connections between the hub gene MT-ATP6 and COX15 and NDUFA1. ACO2, another hub gene, is likewise linked to COX15. Thus, the down-expressed transmembrane protein 126B (TMEM126B), a component of the mitochondrial complex I assembly complex, is associated with NDUFA1 (Heide et al. 2012). Complex I deficiency-induced mitochondrial respiratory chain dysfunction can result in a range of clinical symptoms, from adult-onset neurodegenerative disease to fatal neonatal disease (Giachin et al. 2016). Kinesin-like proteins (KIFC1) were discovered to be overexpressed in this investigation and associated with COX15, constituting cluster 2. In an Alzheimer’s model, RT-PCR revealed overexpression of KIF1C, which is linked to the microtubule motor, after amyloid-β injection (Arora et al. 2020; Xin et al. 2006).

These findings imply a link between the molecular processes studied and AD toxicity. All togethers, the genes in cluster 2, as well as their pathways and interactions, make up a substantial portion of the AD network found in this investigation and present encouraging avenues for further AD treatment research.

In Cluster 3 members, One important therapeutic strategy for neurodegenerative disease such as Alzheimer’s is the PPAR signaling pathway, which is connected to an anti-inflammatory mechanism in microglial cells (Yu et al. 2021). The potential of PPAR as a therapeutic target for neurodegenerative disease was highlighted by a study that discovered that PPAR overexpression inhibits α-secretase (BACE1), the crucial gene that releases amyloid-β in brain cells (Wójtowicz et al. 2020). Cluster 3 members in this study had the PPAR signaling pathway, with greatly down-expressed fatty acid binding protein 4 (FABP4) and overexpressed RXRA transcription factors. FABP4, which is mostly expressed in macrophages and adipocytes, serves as a fatty acid transporter protein and indirectly contributes to inflammatory processes linked to the phagocytic processes of tau and amyloid-β accumulation by macrophages. FABP4 may be used to treat AD by inducing its expression. Furthermore, cluster 3 examined the prion disease pathway, which was corroborated by Condello et al.‘s 2020 research on the connection between prions and other neurodegenerative disease. These disorders have similarities while having different pathogenic molecules (prion, tau, amyloid-β, and α-synuclein), which may indicate possible treatment strategies (Condello et al. 2020; Kellett And Hooper 2009). Using information from the KEGG, MSigDB, BP, and Reactome databases, cluster 3 examined bile acid metabolism, secretion, and transport—all of which are strongly linked to AD (Supplementary Fig. 6D and 6E). Furthermore, this made cluster 3 much more significant, particularly given the role played by the “7-alpha-hydroxycholesterol” and “27-hydroxycholesterol” pathways. The functional information gleaned from cluster 3 emphasizes how important these gene connections are to comprehending the molecular behavior of AD in subsequent research. As a result, cluster 3 members and their connections present themselves as viable subjects for additional AD research (Supplementary Table 10).

In Cluster 4 members the UK Biobank GWAS v1 database was also used to identify dementia and Alzheimer’s disease, with a particular emphasis on the highly overexpressed Mitochondrial Ribosomal Protein L39 (MRPL39) (5.2 log2FC). In the trisomic mmu16 mouse model, MRPL39 overexpression is correlated with a decrease in brain volume (Uguagliati 2020). Additionally, four hub genes—MRPS7, GFM1, RPL5, and RPS29—that showed interactions with MRPL39 were identified by analyzing the interactions of MRPL39 in the PPI network. In AD patients, other cluster 4 members were linked to mitochondrial ribosome function, which is important (Supplementary Fig. 6 F). Santamaría Vicario et al. (2016) showed a decrease in MTIF3, which is likewise linked to mitochondrial ribosome activity, in Parkinson’s disease (Santamaría Vicario et al. 2016). Given its resemblance to the neurodegenerative categories of Parkinson’s disease, MTIF3 may be linked to AD. Additional research on the AD subtype of neurodegenerative disease could focus on MTIF3. In order, GATB has not been shown in AD samples before, despite being described as a critical response to heat stress with a downregulated value (Luo et al. 2022), GATB is also a potential for further research to uncover its function in AD hippocampus samples due to its notable downregulation and interaction with critical cluster 4 member MRPL39, which suggests involvement in brain size reduction (Supplementary Fig. 6 F).

Regulators from Clusters

Through the identification of transcription factors (TFs) from many databases, the authors investigated regulatory factors within these clusters. The findings showed that a number of protein kinases and TFs served as regulatory factors for every cluster. While some TFs were implicated in more than one cluster, others only regulated one. For example, clusters 2 and 1 were regulated by four TFs: EBF3 and GABPA were downregulated, whereas ZBTB48 and IRF7 were overexpressed (Fig. 4E). Clusters 2 and 3 may also be controlled by the downregulated TF GRHL2. Clusters 3 and 1 were discovered to be influenced by THRA, another important regulatory component.

By taking part in retinoic acid-mediated gene activation, the nuclear receptor known as retinoid X receptor alpha (RXRA) mediates the biological effects of retinoids (Zhou et al. 2021). Functional enrichment analysis revealed that RXRA affects beta-amyloid and contributes to the synthesis of bile acids and bile salts through “27-Hydroxycholesterol” (Griffiths et al. 2019), It is also linked to pyruvate metabolism and the citric acid cycle (TCA cycle), both of which are critical in neurodegenerative diseases such as Parkinson’s and Alzheimer’s (Garabadu et al. 2019). RXRA is a member of cluster 3 and was found to be an overexpressed TF that activates clusters 2 and 3 (Fig. 4D). Moreover, it has been previously documented that RXRA mutations affect patients with Alzheimer’s disease (Kölsch et al. 2009). The dual regulatory role of RXRA was found in two clusters in our study (Fig. 4D), indicating that it may be useful for future investigations into the molecular landscape of hippocampus Alzheimer’s disease.

In a similar vein, the overexpressed TF POLE4 belongs to cluster 2 but activates clusters 1 and 2. Furthermore, many eukaryotic creatures share a chromatin remodeling protein called Chromodomain-Helicase DNA-binding 1 (CHD1) (Clapier et al. 2017). CHD1 possesses a DNA-binding domain, an ATPase catalytic domain, and two tandem chromodomains. Patients with Alzheimer’s disease have been found to have it as a TF (Potashkin et al. 2019). By affecting important oxidative, ribosomal, and translation processes, especially in clusters 1 and 4, CHD1 was downregulated in this study and was found to regulate clusters 1, 2, and 4. This suggests that CHD1 may be able to reduce the pathogenic phenotype of Alzheimer’s disease. Additionally, our investigation found that GATA Binding Protein 2 (GATA2) was an overexpressed regulatory component that activated all clusters (Fig. 4E). Genes essential for the development of embryos, self-renewal, upkeep, and operation of stem cells that create blood, lymphatic systems, and other tissues are regulated by GATA2 (Lugus et al. 2007). GATA2 has been identified as a regulatory TF in earlier research (Rahman et al. 2019, 2020). Alzheimer’s disease emphasizes the GABAergic synapse pathway, which is controlled by GATA2 and GATA1 (Abyadeh et al. 2022). Given that GATA2 regulates all four clusters, it may be possible to target it therapeutically in people with Alzheimer’s disease.

Potential Therapeutic Implications of Identified DEGs and Clusters

Hub Genes as Therapeutic Targets

Within the protein-protein interaction network of AD samples, the investigation discovered five new hub genes: MRPS7, RAD51, GFM1, RPL5, and ASPM (Table 1). Because of their essential functions in the network, these hub genes are important and may play a significant influence in the pathophysiology of the disease. The function of MRPS7 in the production of mitochondrial proteins (Supplementary Table 7) (Gopisetty And Thangarajan 2016), One known factor contributing to AD pathogenesis is mitochondrial dysfunction, which may be lessened by targeting MRPS7 (Giudice et al. 2022). Furthermore, DNA repair pathways include RAD51 (Supplementary Table 7) (Laurini et al. 2020). Increasing its activity could shield neuronal cells against DNA damage buildup, which is a defining feature of neurodegenerative disorders. Moreover, GFM1 is essential for the elongation of mitochondrial translation (Supplementary Table 7). Altering GFM1 activity may enhance neuronal energy production and mitochondrial function, hence delaying neurodegeneration. Additionally, Ribosomal Protein L5 (RPL5): RPL5 participates in protein synthesis as a ribosome component (Supplementary Table 7). Stabilizing ribosome activity through therapeutic approaches may guarantee appropriate protein synthesis and stop the generation of abnormal proteins associated with AD. ASPM’s function in neurogenesis is well-established (Supplementary Table 7). Improving ASPM function may encourage neuronal repair and regeneration, providing a means of reversing AD’s neuronal loss.

Collaborative Clusters and Therapeutic Pathways

Four cooperative clusters were found in the PPI network, indicating gene groups that collaborate and may have a shared impact on AD pathogenesis. Clusters 1 and 4 in particular are involved in translation processes within the mitochondria. Along with the “7-alpha-hydroxycholesterol” and “27-hydroxycholesterol” pathways, Cluster 3 is also in charge of the “Inflammatory Response” pathways. Thus, with regard to NDUFA1 and COX15 members, the protein complex in Cluster 2 also indicates their role in “oxidative phosphorylation” and “mitochondrial dysfunction.”

Given their significant contributions to the development of AD, these signaling pathways may also be viable targets for therapy. Protein interactions inside each cluster may provide useful information for this purpose. As a result, the four clusters cooperate through a number of distinct proteins, either through the same pathways (e.g., mitochondrial translation in Clusters 1 and 4) or through pathways that have been crucial to the development of AD (e.g., oxidative phosphorylation and mitochondrial dysfunction in Cluster 2). The current study identified the 7-alpha-hydroxycholesterol signaling pathway as a potential pathway for cluster 3.

Transcription Factors and Protein Kinases as Regulators

In AD samples, the study found one protein kinase (PRKCZ) and 10 transcription factors (GATA2, CHD1, THRA, IRF7, ZBTB48, POLE4, ZNF219, SLC2A4RG, NR1D1, and RXRA) to be regulatory factors. An additional layer of possible treatment targets is provided by these regulatory molecules. For example, the expression of several downstream genes implicated in AD pathogenesis may be modulated by transcription factors that target upstream regulators of the DEGs. Nuclear receptors such as NR1D1 and RXRA, for example, control gene expression in response to inflammatory and metabolic cues. In AD patients, medications that either activate or inhibit these TFs may aid in the restoration of normal gene expression patterns. Furthermore, the Protein Kinase (PRKCZ) participates in signal transduction pathways that control a number of biological functions, such as metabolism, apoptosis, and cell survival. By adjusting PRKCZ activity, abnormal signaling pathways in AD may be corrected, thereby enhancing neuronal survival and function (Infante et al. 2022).

Pathway-Based Therapeutic Strategie1s

The citrate cycle (TCA cycle), bile acid production, and xenobiotic metabolism are among the important pathways that are changed in AD, according to the pathway enrichment study. These routes shed light on AD’s metabolic abnormalities. Changes in cholesterol metabolism in AD may be indicated by the overexpression of bile acid production pathways. Treatments that alter the synthesis or function of bile acids may aid in controlling cholesterol and lowering the risk of AD (Russell 2003). Furthermore, neurons’ ability to produce energy is impacted by the dysregulation of the citrate cycle (TCA cycle). Enhancing mitochondrial activity and TCA cycle efficiency through therapeutic interventions may improve neuronal energy metabolism and slow the course of AD (Tai et al. 2023). The detoxification of foreign chemicals is a necessary step in the xenobiotic metabolism pathway. Improving neurons’ ability to detoxify could shield them from environmental pollutants that fuel AD pathogenesis (Satyanarayana 2014).

In conclusion, our work thoroughly assessed differentially expressed genes in order to offer fresh perspectives on the genes and pathways underlying Alzheimer’s disease associated with the hippocampal region.

Conclusions

The purpose of this study was to explore Alzheimer’s disease by examining differentially expressed genes, hub gene candidates, potential clusters and their interactions, as well as upstream (TFs) and downstream (protein kinase) regulatory factors. We observed that two cholesterol-related pathways—27-hydroxycholesterol and 7-alpha-hydroxycholesterol—were up-regulated, while down-regulated pathways such as vacuolar organization regulation and negative regulation of proteasomal protein catabolic process appeared to be relevant to AD processes.

Several signaling pathways identified in this study may play roles in AD progression. For example, clusters 1 and 4 were enriched for “Mitochondrial Translation” pathways, while cluster 2 was related to “Oxidative phosphorylation” and “Mitochondrial dysfunction.” Cluster 3 contained members involved in the cholesterol metabolism pathways “7-alpha-hydroxycholesterol” and “27-hydroxycholesterol.”

Five hub gene candidates (MRPS7, RPL5, GFM1, RAD51, and ASPM) were identified as potentially associated with AD in hippocampal tissue. Among these, MRPS7, RPL5, GFM1, ACO2, and MT-ATP6 are mitochondrial-related genes that may link hereditary factors to AD pathogenesis through impaired mitochondrial function and energy metabolism.

Additionally, 11 candidate regulatory molecules (10 transcription factors GATA2, CHD1, THRA, IRF7, ZBTB48, POLE4, ZNF219, SLC2A4RG, NR1D1 and RXRA and one protein kinase PRKCZ) were identified that may influence the expression of DEGs and functional clusters. Notably, NR1D1 was predicted to regulate PRKCZ at the transcriptional level, while PRKCZ may act at the post-translational level.

Taken together, these findings provide preliminary insights into genes, pathways, and regulatory mechanisms that may underlie Alzheimer’s disease. While these results broaden our understanding of AD at the molecular level, they should be interpreted cautiously due to the limited dataset size and require further experimental validation in independent cohorts. Future studies with larger sample sizes and experimental confirmation will be essential to assess the therapeutic or diagnostic potential of the identified candidates.
