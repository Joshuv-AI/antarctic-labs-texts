What illness taught me about care: Delay, certainty and dignity in oral cancer
Authors: Neil Shah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Clinicians who develop cancer within their own specialty occupy a rare position, holding both the knowledge of the expert and the vulnerability of the patient. This article reflects on what that experience revealed about cancer care, not its biology or technique, but the conditions in which it is received. Three elements consistently shaped the experience of illness: how quickly care was delivered, how clearly it was explained, and how respectfully the patient was treated. These are described as delay, certainty, and dignity. Delay influences not only clinical outcomes but also psychological burden and trust. Certainty relates to communication, expectation-setting, and the patient’s understanding of their trajectory through care. Dignity encompasses the preservation of identity, autonomy, and respect during treatment. Even when clinical decisions are appropriate, failures in these domains can carry significant cost. The article proposes that these three elements form a simple, transferable framework for understanding and improving patient experience across cancer pathways. Attention to delay, certainty and dignity may offer a practical means of aligning technical excellence with humane care.

For most of my career, I diagnosed and treated patients with oral cancer. I examined lesions, performed biopsies, planned resections and explained pathology results. I had delivered difficult diagnoses many times.

I never expected to become the patient myself.

The lesion on my tongue did not seem alarming at first. I examined it, reasoned with it and reassured myself it was probably inflammatory. Even as it persisted, I interpreted it generously – the kind of quiet rationalisation clinicians are perhaps especially skilled at making for themselves.

Then one area began to change.

The surface became irregular. A rolled edge appeared. It no longer felt familiar. I arranged a biopsy within two weeks. Eighteen days later, I underwent surgery for stage III oral cancer.

I still remember the phone call.ʻNeil, I’m really sorry. It’s cancer.’

I had made that call to patients myself many times before. This time I was sitting on the other side of the consultation.

What followed – major surgery, six weeks of radiotherapy, profound fatigue and, during recovery, another cancer diagnosis – changed not only my understanding of illness, but my understanding of care itself.

Delay

Before becoming ill myself, I understood delay largely as a systems problem – referral pathways, waiting lists, access to biopsy and diagnostic intervals. I had spent years working on early diagnosis initiatives in oral cancer, including developing a consultant-led tele-triage pathway intended to reduce unnecessary delay.

Living through cancer made me realise something more uncomfortable.

Delay is also profoundly human.

Even with specialist knowledge, I delayed. Not dramatically. Not negligently. But in the ordinary, recognisable way many patients do. I explained the lesion to myself. I monitored it. I reassured myself. I waited.

That experience changed the way I think about diagnostic delay entirely.

Patients do not delay simply because they are uninformed. Human beings delay because uncertainty is psychologically easier to live with than the possibility of cancer. Clinicians are not immune to that instinct. In some ways, knowledge can even strengthen it.

There is good evidence that diagnostic and treatment delay worsens outcomes in oral cancer.1, 2 But delay is also an emotional experience – a period in which uncertainty expands and time distorts. Waiting is not a neutral interval in care. It is part of the experience of illness itself.

During my own treatment, I became increasingly aware that patients are often left alone with uncertainty between appointments – a period where communication, continuity and expectation-setting become critically important. Even brief contact, or a clearly identified route back into the service, can reduce the sense of abandonment that patients sometimes experience while waiting. Patients cope better when they understand where they are in the pathway, what may change and when they should seek help. Even brief moments of orientation can restore a sense of control.

I also realised how important it is for waiting to feel purposeful rather than passive. Prehabilitation advice, symptom self-management guidance and simple preparation checklists can help patients feel involved in their own care rather than suspended outside it.

These are small interventions. They do not make delay acceptable. But they reduce the harm it causes.

Certainty

My surgeon delivered the news of my diagnosis with exceptional professionalism and kindness. I knew the team, the pathway and the likely treatment trajectory. The waiting was still difficult, but I understood what was happening and why.

The contrast came later.

During recovery from radiotherapy, I received another cancer diagnosis. Suddenly I found myself outside my own area of expertise, navigating an unfamiliar pathway with no mental map of what came next. I was no longer the surgeon who understood the sequence of investigations, MDT discussions and treatment decisions. I was simply a patient waiting for results.

In many ways, that period felt harder than the original cancer diagnosis.

Not necessarily because the situation itself was worse, but because I had lost orientation. I realised how psychologically important it is for patients to understand where they are within a pathway, even when outcomes remain uncertain.

Uncertainty without structure is its own kind of suffering.

Certainty, in this context, does not mean false reassurance or unrealistic optimism. It means clarity. Patients cope better when they know what the next step is, when results are expected and who to contact if things change.

Something as simple as:ʻThe next decision will be X. You will hear from us by Y. If you do not, contact Z.’

Dignity

Head and neck cancer affects some of the most personal aspects of human identity – speech, swallowing, appearance and social interaction. These are not abstract functions. They shape how we exist in the world and how we are recognised by others.

The fatigue after radiotherapy was so profound that, for a period, I could barely get out of bed. There were moments where the effort of existing felt total. I had treated patients through radiotherapy for years, but I do not think I fully understood the depth of exhaustion until I experienced it myself.

What carried me through that period – alongside family and friends – was the quality of interaction with the people around me.

How examinations were conducted.

Whether questions were welcomed.

Whether uncertainty was acknowledged honestly.

Whether I was still addressed as a person rather than a diagnosis.

These moments are rarely captured in outcome measures or survival curves. Yet they profoundly shape how patients experience illness and whether they feel their identity and personhood have been preserved throughout treatment.4

Dignity is not an optional extra in care. It is part of what patients remember long after technical details have faded.

What this means for dentistry

For many patients with oral cancer, the first meaningful clinical encounter occurs in primary dental care. The Triad of Care therefore begins long before the specialist clinic.

A second look at the lateral tongue or floor of mouth takes seconds. A clear and honest conversation about a suspicious lesion – delivered with certainty about the next step and respect for the patient’s dignity in that moment – can change the trajectory of someone’s life.

Early action costs little.

Waiting costs everything.5

Reflection

Returning to clinical practice after cancer changed the way I speak to patients.

I explain waiting periods more explicitly now. I outline the next step before patients feel the need to ask. I acknowledge uncertainty rather than moving past it too quickly. I understand more clearly that patients are not simply experiencing disease. They are experiencing delay, uncertainty, vulnerability and the emotional weight of waiting.

The Triad of Care is not a formal research framework. It is a lens, shaped by lived experience, through which clinicians might reflect on what patients most need from us when they are at their most vulnerable.

I once understood oral cancer primarily through pathology, imaging, surgery and treatment planning.

Becoming the patient taught me that illness is experienced very differently from the inside.

Delay, certainty and dignity are not peripheral to care.

For patients, they are often what care feels like.
